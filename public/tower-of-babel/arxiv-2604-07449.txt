Quantum Fluctuations and Newton-Cartan Geometry for Non-Relativistic de Sitter space Preprint:
Matthias Harksen, Diego Hidalgo, and Watse Sybesma

Abstract
We study a non-relativistic realisation of two-dimensional de Sitter gravity both from its boundary and bulk description. On the boundary side, we analyse the Schwarzian-type boundary action associated with non-relativistic de Sitter gravity and evaluate its one-loop partition function in order to compute its quantum fluctuations. Rather than relying on the coadjoint-orbit construction, we derive the path integral measure directly from the action using the Ostrogradsky formalism. We find a temperature-dependent prefactor scaling as T 2 T^{2} , of which the power agrees with the counting of the four global symmetry generators present. On the bulk side, we construct the corresponding torsionless Newton-Cartan geometry and show that it satisfies the equations of motion of a non-relativistic JT-like action and uplift the geometry to a three-dimensional Lorentzian geometry.

The boundary description of non-relativistic de Sitter space

2.2
Isometries of the Galilean de Sitter action

In order to understand the structure of the path integral and, in particular, the origin of zero modes in the one-loop analysis, we first determine the global symmetries of the boundary action. Having reviewed the derivation of the Galilean de Sitter action in equation (
12
), we recall that it is given by

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{EdS-G}}=\int dt\Bigg[c_{0}\left(v^{\prime 2}-2v^{\prime\prime}+\left(\frac{v^{\prime\prime}}{v^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\right)+c_{1}\left(s^{\prime\prime}+s^{\prime}\left(v^{\prime}-\frac{v^{\prime\prime}}{v^{\prime}}\right)\right)\Bigg]\,.

(19)

We now observe that the action is invariant under the finite transformations

\displaystyle s(t)

\displaystyle\mapsto s(t)+a+be^{-v(t)}\,,\hskip 28.45274ptv(t)\mapsto-\log\!\big(c+de^{-v(t)}\big)\,,

(20)

where
a,b,c,d
are arbitrary constants. The corresponding infinitesimal isometries which leave the action invariant are given by

\displaystyle\delta s

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{a}+\epsilon_{b}e^{-v(t)}\,,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta v=\epsilon_{c}+\epsilon_{d}\,e^{v(t)}\,,

(21)

where
\{\epsilon_{a},\epsilon_{b},\epsilon_{c},\epsilon_{d}\}
are infinitesimal parameters, which will be used to construct the corresponding Noether charges. These four independent transformations generate the global part of the warped Virasoro symmetry algebra (
17
) and will later be associated with the non-Gaussian sector of the path integral. Because the action contains higher-derivative terms, the corresponding Noether charges must be constructed using the generalised Ostrogradsky formalism. We compute the generalised Ostrogradsky momenta as

\displaystyle p^{(s)}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial L}{\partial s^{\prime}}-\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial s^{\prime\prime}}\right)=2c_{1}\left(v^{\prime}-\frac{v^{\prime\prime}}{v^{\prime}}\right)\,,

(22)

\displaystyle p^{(v)}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial L}{\partial v^{\prime}}-\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial v^{\prime\prime}}\right)=2c_{1}s^{\prime}+2c_{0}v^{\prime}+\frac{2}{v^{\prime}}\left[c_{1}s^{\prime\prime}+c_{0}\left(\frac{{v^{\prime\prime}}^{2}}{{v^{\prime}}^{2}}-\frac{v^{\prime\prime\prime}}{v^{\prime}}\right)\right]\,,

(23)

\displaystyle p^{(s)}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial L}{\partial s^{\prime\prime}}=2c_{1}\,,

(24)

\displaystyle p^{(v)}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial L}{\partial v^{\prime\prime}}=-\frac{2}{v^{\prime}}\left[c_{1}s^{\prime}+c_{0}\left(v^{\prime}-\frac{v^{\prime\prime}}{v^{\prime}}\right)\right]\,.

(25)

From these, we may compute the four associated charges, obtaining

\displaystyle Q[\epsilon]=\int dt\Big[p^{(s)}_{1}\,\delta s+p^{(v)}_{1}\,\delta v+p^{(s)}_{2}\,\delta s^{\prime}+p^{(v)}_{2}\,\delta v^{\prime}\Big]\,,

(26)

where
\delta s
and
\delta v
are the infinitesimal symmetry variations given in (
21
). Substituting these variations into the charge formula yields

\displaystyle Q_{a}

\displaystyle=Q[\epsilon_{a}]=2c_{1}\int dt\left(v^{\prime}-\frac{v^{\prime\prime}}{v^{\prime}}\right)\,,

(27)

\displaystyle Q_{b}

\displaystyle=Q[\epsilon_{b}]=-2c_{1}\int dt\frac{v^{\prime\prime}}{v^{\prime}}e^{-v(t)}\,,

(28)

\displaystyle Q_{c}

\displaystyle=Q[\epsilon_{c}]=2\int\frac{dt}{v^{\prime}}\left[c_{1}s^{\prime\prime}+c_{0}\left(\frac{{v^{\prime\prime}}^{2}}{{v^{\prime}}^{2}}-\frac{v^{\prime\prime\prime}}{v^{\prime}}\right)\right]\,,

(29)

\displaystyle Q_{d}

\displaystyle=Q[\epsilon_{d}]=2\int\frac{dt\,e^{v(t)}}{v^{\prime}}\left[c_{1}s^{\prime\prime}+c_{0}\left(v^{\prime\prime}+\frac{{v^{\prime\prime}}^{2}}{{v^{\prime}}^{2}}-\frac{v^{\prime\prime\prime}}{v^{\prime}}\right)\right]\,.

(30)

Evaluating the Poisson brackets of the charges directly, we find that
Q_{a},Q_{b},Q_{c},Q_{d}
realise the conformal Galilean algebra in (
4
). More explicitly, the non-vanishing brackets are

\displaystyle\{Q_{c},Q_{b}\}=-Q_{b}\,,\hskip 28.45274pt\{Q_{c},Q_{d}\}=Q_{d}\,,\hskip 28.45274pt\{Q_{d},Q_{b}\}=Q_{a}\,.

(31)

Identifying
Q_{c}=\mathcal{D}
,
Q_{b}=\mathcal{H}
,
Q_{d}=\mathcal{K}
, and
Q_{a}=2\mathcal{Z}
, we recover the algebra (

As we will see in the next sections, these symmetries are directly responsible for the presence of zero modes in the quadratic fluctuation analysis, which in turn require a separate treatment in the path integral.

2.3
Equations of motion and saddle point

We now determine the classical saddle of the action and analyse quadratic fluctuations around it, which will form the basis of the one-loop computation in the next section. To this end, we decompose the fields into background configurations and fluctuations

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt-\frac{2c_{1}\bar{s}^{\prime}\bar{v}^{\prime\prime}}{\bar{v}^{\prime 3}}\delta v^{\prime 2}+\frac{3c_{0}\bar{v}^{\prime\prime 2}}{\bar{v}^{\prime 4}}\delta v^{\prime 2}+\frac{2c_{1}\bar{s}^{\prime}}{\bar{v}^{\prime 2}}\,\delta v^{\prime}\,\delta v^{\prime\prime}-\frac{4c_{0}\bar{v}^{\prime\prime}}{\bar{v}^{\prime 3}}\,\delta v^{\prime}\,\delta v^{\prime\prime}\Bigg]\,.

(39)

To fully account for the quadratic fluctuations, we must evaluate the path integral over the fluctuation modes, which we will do in the next section. In addition, the path integral measure is fixed as the Pfaffian of the associated symplectic form, defined as

\Omega=\int^{\beta}_{0}dt\Big(dp_{v}\wedge dv+dp_{s}\wedge ds\Big)\,.

(40)

There the generalized momenta
p_{v}
and
p_{s}
are defined, using the Ostrogradsky formalism, through

p_{w}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{\prime}}-\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w^{\prime\prime}}\right)\,,

(41)

with
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(w,w^{\prime},w^{\prime\prime})
the Lagrangian corresponding to the action in (
19
), which depends at most on second derivatives, and where
w
denotes either
v
or
s
. Applying this to the quadratic fluctuation action, we obtain the corresponding generalised Ostrogradsky momenta

\displaystyle p_{\delta s}

\displaystyle=\frac{2c_{1}}{\bar{v}^{\prime}}\left(\left(\bar{v}^{\prime}-\tfrac{\bar{v}^{\prime\prime}}{\bar{v}^{\prime}}\right)\delta v^{\prime}-\delta v^{\prime\prime}\right)\,,

(42)

\displaystyle p_{\delta v}

\displaystyle=\frac{2c_{1}}{\bar{v}^{\prime}}\left(\bar{v}^{\prime}\delta s^{\prime}+\delta s^{\prime\prime}-\tfrac{\bar{s}^{\prime\prime}}{\bar{v}^{\prime}}\delta v^{\prime}\right)+\frac{2c_{0}}{\bar{v}^{\prime 2}}\left(\left(\bar{v}^{\prime 2}-\tfrac{3\bar{v}^{\prime\prime 2}}{\bar{v}^{\prime 2}}+\tfrac{2\bar{v}^{\prime\prime\prime}}{\bar{v}^{\prime}}\right)\delta v^{\prime}+\tfrac{2\bar{v}^{\prime\prime}}{\bar{v}^{\prime}}\delta v^{\prime\prime}-\delta v^{\prime\prime\prime}\right)\,.

(43)

Using these expressions, one finds that the symplectic form is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{EdS-G}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=2c_{1}\int\frac{dt}{\bar{v}^{\prime}}\left[2\bar{v}^{\prime}d\delta v^{\prime}\wedge d\delta s+d\delta v^{\prime}\wedge d\delta s^{\prime}+d\delta s^{\prime\prime}\wedge d\delta v-\tfrac{\bar{s}^{\prime\prime}}{\bar{v}^{\prime}}d\delta v^{\prime}\wedge d\delta v\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+2c_{0}\int\frac{dt}{\bar{v}^{\prime 2}}\left[\bar{v}^{\prime 2}d\delta v^{\prime}\wedge d\delta v-d\delta v^{\prime\prime\prime}\wedge d\delta v+d\delta v^{\prime\prime}\wedge d\delta v^{\prime}\right]\,.

(44)

We are primarily interested in evaluating these expressions on the saddle (
38
), namely the quadratic action (
2.3
) and the symplectic form (
2.3
). Substituting the saddle (
38
) into these expressions, we obtain

\displaystyle I^{(0)}_{\mathrm{EdS-G}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi^{2}c_{0}}{\beta}\,,

(45)

\displaystyle I^{(2)}_{\mathrm{EdS-G}}

\displaystyle=2c_{1}\int dt\left[\delta s^{\prime}\delta v^{\prime}+i\tfrac{\beta}{2\pi}\delta v^{\prime}\delta s^{\prime\prime}\right]+c_{0}\int dt\left[{\delta v^{\prime}}^{2}-\left(\tfrac{\beta}{2\pi}\right)^{2}{\delta v^{\prime\prime}}^{2}\right]\,,

(46)

\displaystyle\Omega^{(2)}_{\mathrm{EdS-G}}

\displaystyle=4c_{1}\int dt\left[d\delta v^{\prime}\wedge d\delta s+i\tfrac{\beta}{2\pi}d\delta s^{\prime\prime}\wedge d\delta v\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt2c_{0}\int dt\left[d\delta v^{\prime}\wedge d\delta v+\left(\tfrac{\beta}{2\pi}\right)^{2}d\delta v^{\prime\prime\prime}\wedge d\delta v\right]\,,

(47)

where
I^{(1)}_{\mathrm{EdS-G}}=0
by the equations of motion.

2.4
Evaluating the one-loop path integral

In this section, we evaluate the one-loop contribution to the boundary path integral for the Galilean de Sitter gravity theory. We will do this in the boundary description.

Exactly
equating a gravitational path integral to the path integral of a quantum mechanical system in one dimension – its boundary description – is highly non-trivial, but well-studied in JT gravity
Stanford:2017thb
.
This can be understood in the following way: since the dilaton in JT gravity acts as a Lagrange multiplier, one can perform integration over the dilaton at the level of the path integral, which fixes the curvature of the geometry and only leaves boundary fluctuations. We will return later to the bulk formulation of non-relativistic JT gravity in Section
3
, where we will argue that an analogous mechanism is at work for Galilean de Sitter gravity, which can be cast into a JT-like action formulation with a dilaton that again acts as a Lagrange multiplier and may be integrated out. The relevant path integral we want to solve becomes

\mathcal{Z}_{\text{EdS-G}}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}[\mathcal{D}s]\,[\mathcal{D}v]\,e^{-I_{\text{EdS-G}}[s,v]}\,.

(48)

Given the boundary equations we impose, the space
\mathcal{M}
we integrate over consists of all configurations of
s
and
v
subject to the boundary conditions (
33
), and with EdS-G symmetry modded out. Plugging in the fluctuations, this yields

\mathcal{Z}_{\text{EdS-G}}=e^{-I^{(0)}_{\text{EdS-G}}[\overline{s},\overline{v}]}\int_{\mathcal{M}}[\mathcal{D}\delta s]\,[\mathcal{D}\delta v]\,e^{-I^{(2)}_{\text{EdS-G}}[\delta s,\delta v]}\,.

(49)

As we have seen, the theory (
19
) possesses a set of global symmetries generated by the EdS-G algebra (
2
).
These will play a crucial role in evaluating the path integral, as they give rise to zero modes.
The evaluation of the path integral proceeds in three steps.
First, we expand the fluctuations in Fourier modes, which diagonalise the quadratic action.
Second, we evaluate the resulting Gaussian integrals for the non-zero modes.
Third, we treat the zero modes separately, as they are associated with the global symmetries identified in Section
2.2
.

To implement the first step, we expand the fluctuations in Fourier modes as follows

\delta v(t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big(v_{n}^{(R)}+iv_{n}^{(I)}\Big)\,e^{\frac{2\pi}{\beta}int}\,,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta s(t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big(s_{n}^{(R)}+is_{n}^{(I)}\Big)\,e^{\frac{2\pi}{\beta}int}\,,

(50)

where we will require the real (
R
) and imaginary parts (
I
) to satisfy

w_{n}=w_{n}^{(R)}+iw_{n}^{(I)}\,,\quad w_{-n}^{(R)}=w_{n}^{(R)}\,,\quad w_{-n}^{(I)}=-w_{n}^{(I)}\,,

(51)

and
w
is a placeholder for
s
and
v
. Plugging the Fourier expansion into the second-order on-shell action (
46
) and symplectic form (
47
) yields

\displaystyle I^{(2)}_{\mathrm{EdS-G}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\beta}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big(2c_{1}n^{2}(n-1)\delta s_{n}\delta v_{-n}+c_{0}n^{2}(n^{2}-1)\delta v_{n}\delta v_{-n}\Big)\,,

(52)

\displaystyle\Omega^{(2)}_{\mathrm{EdS-G}}

\displaystyle=4\pi i\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big(2c_{1}n(n-1)d\delta v_{n}\wedge d\delta s_{-n}-c_{0}n(n^{2}-1)d\delta v_{n}\wedge d\delta v_{-n}\Big)\,.

(53)

We have used
\int^{\beta}_{0}dte^{i(m-n)t}=\beta\,\delta_{mn}
.
Using the real and imaginary parts of the Fourier coefficients, we may rewrite the expressions entirely in terms of positive modes, in which case we find

\displaystyle I^{(2)}_{\mathrm{EdS-G}}

\displaystyle=\frac{16\pi^{2}}{\beta}c_{1}\delta s_{-1}\delta v_{1}-\frac{8\pi^{2}}{\beta}c_{0}\sum_{n\geq 2}n^{2}(n^{2}-1)\left[\delta v_{n}^{(R)}\delta v_{n}^{(R)}+\delta v_{n}^{(I)}\delta v_{n}^{(I)}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\frac{16\pi^{2}}{\beta}c_{1}\sum_{n\geq 2}n^{2}\left[\delta s_{n}^{(R)}\delta v_{n}^{(R)}+\delta s_{n}^{(I)}\delta v_{n}^{(I)}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt-\frac{16\pi^{2}i}{\beta}c_{1}\sum_{n\geq 2}n^{3}\left[\delta s_{n}^{(R)}\delta v_{n}^{(I)}-\delta s_{n}^{(I)}\delta v_{n}^{(R)}\right]\,.

(54)

For the symplectic form, we get that

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{EdS-G}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=16\pi ic_{1}d\delta v_{-1}\wedge d\delta s_{1}+16\pi ic_{1}\sum_{n\geq 2}n^{2}\left[d\delta v_{n}^{(R)}\wedge d\delta s_{n}^{(R)}+d\delta v_{n}^{(I)}\wedge d\delta s_{n}^{(I)}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-16\pi c_{1}\sum_{n\geq 2}n\left[d\delta v_{n}^{(I)}\wedge d\delta s_{n}^{(R)}-d\delta v_{n}^{(R)}\wedge d\delta s_{n}^{(I)}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt+32\pi c_{0}\sum_{n\geq 2}n(n^{2}-1)d\delta v_{n}^{(I)}\wedge d\delta v_{n}^{(R)}\,.

(55)

We have explicitly separated the
n=1
mode, as it requires special treatment and is associated with the global symmetries identified in Section
2.2
. For
n\geq 2
, the quadratic fluctuation integral is Gaussian and can therefore be evaluated using

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{T}A_{n}x}=\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{4}}{\det A_{n}}}\,.

(56)

For each mode
n\geq 2
, the quadratic action may be written in the form
\frac{1}{2}x^{T}A_{n}x
, where the fluctuation vector is

x=\big(\delta v_{n}^{(R)},\delta v_{n}^{(I)},\delta s_{n}^{(R)},\delta s_{n}^{(I)}\big)
. For
n\geq 2
, the matrix
A_{n}
takes the form

\displaystyle A_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{16\pi^{2}n^{2}}{\beta}\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}(n^{2}-1)&0&2c_{1}&2ic_{1}n\\
0&c_{0}(n^{2}-1)&-2ic_{1}n&2c_{1}\\
2c_{1}&2ic_{1}n&0&0\\
-2ic_{1}n&2c_{1}&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(57)

The corresponding symplectic form may likewise for
n\geq 2
be represented by an
4\times 4
antisymmetric matrix
\Omega_{n}

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}=16\pi i\begin{pmatrix}0&-2ic_{0}n(n^{2}-1)&c_{1}n^{2}&-c_{1}in\\
2ic_{0}n(n^{2}-1)&0&c_{1}in&c_{1}n^{2}\\
-c_{1}n^{2}&-c_{1}in&0&0\\
c_{1}in&-c_{1}n^{2}&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(58)

From these expressions, we obtain

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{4}}{\det A_{n}}}=\frac{\beta^{2}}{256\pi^{2}c_{1}^{2}n^{4}(n^{2}-1)}\,,\hskip 28.45274pt\mathrm{Pf}(\Omega_{n})=256\pi^{2}c_{1}^{2}n^{2}(n^{2}-1)\,.

(59)

Hence we find

\displaystyle Z(\beta)=e^{\frac{4\pi^{2}c_{0}}{\beta}}\mathcal{B}(\beta)\prod_{n\geq 2}\mathrm{Pf}(\Omega_{n})\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{4}}{\det A_{n}}}=e^{\frac{4\pi^{2}c_{0}}{\beta}}\mathcal{B}(\beta)\prod_{n\geq 2}\frac{\beta^{2}}{n^{2}}\,.

(60)

Before detailing
\mathcal{B}(\beta)
, we evaluate the infinite product using zeta-function regularisation.
This relies on the analytic continuation of the Riemann zeta function,

\displaystyle\zeta(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{-s},\qquad\zeta^{\prime}(s)=-\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{-s}\log n\,.

(61)

Applying this to the product in (
60
), we obtain

\displaystyle\prod_{n\geq 2}\frac{\beta^{2}}{n^{2}}

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigg(\sum_{n\ge 2}\bigl(2\log\beta-2\log n\bigr)\Bigg)=\exp\Big(2\log(\beta)\,(\zeta(0)-1)+2\zeta'(0)\Big)=\frac{1}{2\pi\beta^{3}}\,,

(62)

where we used the regularized values
\zeta(0)=-\frac{1}{2}
and
\zeta^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)
. The non-Gaussian contribution from the
n=1
modes is captured by
\mathcal{B}(\beta)
, which we compute as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\beta)

\displaystyle=16\pi i\,c_{1}\Bigg(\iint d\delta v_{1}^{(R)}d\delta s_{1}^{(R)}+\iint d\delta v_{1}^{(I)}d\delta s_{1}^{(I)}+i\iint d\delta v_{1}^{(R)}d\delta s_{1}^{(I)}-i\iint d\delta v_{1}^{(I)}d\delta s_{1}^{(R)}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt\times\exp\Bigg(
\frac{16\pi^2}{\beta} c_1
\Big(
\delta s_1^{(R)} \delta v_1^{(R)}
+
\delta s_1^{(I)} \delta v_1^{(I)}
-
i \left( \delta s_1^{(I)} \delta v_1^{(R)} - \delta s_1^{(R)} \delta v_1^{(I)} \right)
\Big)
\Bigg)=4\beta\,,

(63)

where in order to evaluate the preceding integral we introduced a regulator
-i\epsilon\absolutevalue{v_1}^{2}
, with
\mathrm{Im}(\epsilon)<0

to ensure convergence. The integral is first computed at
finite
\epsilon
, after which the
\epsilon\to 0
limit is taken,
noting that the final result is regulator-independent.

Collecting all contributions, we obtain the final expression for the partition function

\mathcal{Z}_{\text{EdS-G}}(\beta)=\frac{2}{\pi\beta^{2}}\exp\Bigg(
\frac{4\pi^2 c_{0}}{\beta}
\Bigg)\,.

(64)

This corresponds to the main result of this section. The overall scaling
\mathcal{Z}_{\text{EdS-G}}(\beta)\sim\beta^{-2}
is consistent with the general expectation that each global symmetry generator contributes a factor of
\beta^{-1/2}
. Since the EdS-G algebra possesses four generators, this results in the observed
\beta^{-2}
behaviour. This is a characteristic feature of Schwarzian-type theories, in which exponential growth arises from the classical saddle, and the quantum fluctuations provide a temperature-dependent prefactor determined by the number of global symmetry generators.

Performing an inverse Laplace transform, we obtain the density of states (dos) as a function of the energy
E
,

\mathcal{D}_{\text{dos}}(E)=\frac{\sqrt{E}}{\pi^{2}\sqrt{c_{0}}}\,I_{1}\!\left(4\pi\sqrt{c_{0}E}\right)\,,

(65)

where
I_{1}
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind. In particular, for small energies
E\to 0
, this behaves as

\mathcal{D}_{\text{dos}}(E)=\frac{2}{\pi}E+\mathcal{O}(E^{2})\,,

(66)

which shows that the density of states vanishes linearly at low energies. The linear behaviour at low energies also reflects how quantum fluctuations resolve the naive semiclassical gap in the spectrum and indicate the presence of low-energy states in the quantum theory.

3
A bulk Newton-Cartan description of Galilean de Sitter

In the previous section, we developed a boundary description of non-relativistic de Sitter space and evaluated its one-loop contribution. The goal of this section is to complement that analysis by constructing the corresponding bulk description and elucidating its geometric structure.
A particular novelty is that we work out a non-relativistic equivalent of JT gravity and show that the geometry corresponding to EdS-G satisfies its equations of motion.
We begin in Section
3.1
by deriving the two-dimensional Newton–Cartan geometry associated with the bulk gauge connection. In Section
3.2
we show that this geometry realises the EdS-G algebra (
2
) as its isometry algebra, and in Section
3.3
we provide a relativistic uplift to three dimensions. We then turn in Section
3.4
to a complementary second-order description, showing that the same Newton–Cartan geometry arises from a non-relativistic JT-type action. Finally, in Section
3.5
we compare this second-order formulation with the underlying first-order gauge-theoretic description. Taken together, these results establish a bulk description whose geometric and symmetry structures precisely match those of the boundary dynamics studied in Section
2
.

3.1
Bulk Newton–Cartan geometry from the gauge connection

To construct a bulk description of Galilean de Sitter gravity, we work in a two-dimensional Newton–Cartan framework. Newton–Cartan geometry, originally introduced by Élie Cartan
Cartan:1923
;
Cartan:1924
, provides a geometric formulation of non-relativistic gravity analogous to the role played by Riemannian geometry in general relativity. In particular, it is the natural geometric language for theories with Galilean symmetry. We refer to
Hartong:2022lsy
for an excellent modern review of non-relativistic gravity, including its history, formulation, and recent developments.

In two dimensions, the Newton–Cartan data consist of a temporal one-form
\tau_{\mu}
, a spatial vielbein
e_{\mu}
together with its inverse
e^{\mu}
, and a
U(1)
mass gauge field
m_{\mu}
. More generally, Newton–Cartan geometry may also be torsionful, depending on whether the clock form
\tau_{\mu}
is closed. Such torsionful geometries play an important role in more general non-relativistic settings and can account for effects such as non-relativistic gravitational lensing
Hansen:2019vqf
.

Our starting point is the boundary gauge field derived in Section
2
. In the gauge (
13
), it takes the form

\displaystyle a=\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{L}(t)\mathcal{K}+\mathcal{T}(t)\mathcal{D}\,.

(67)

To reinterpret this in Newton–Cartan variables, we pass from the basis
\{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{K},\mathcal{Z}\}
to the basis adapted to the EdS-G algebra,

\displaystyle H=\frac{1}{\ell}\mathcal{D}\,,\qquad P=\frac{1}{2\ell}\bigl(\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{K}\bigr)\,,\qquad G=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{K}\bigr)\,,\qquad M=\frac{1}{\ell}\mathcal{Z}\,.

(68)

We then introduce the radial group element

\displaystyle b(r)=e^{-r\mathcal{H}}\,,

(69)

and construct the bulk connection in radial gauge according to the permissible gauge transformation

\displaystyle A(t,r)=b^{-1}(r)db(r)+b^{-1}(r)a(t)b(r)\,.

(70)

Besides that, expanding the one-form
A
in the extended Galilean basis (
19
) as

\displaystyle A(t,r)=\tau H+eP+\omega G+mM\,,

(71)

allows us to read off the Newton–Cartan fields directly from the coefficients of the generators, obtaining

\displaystyle\tau_{\mu}dx^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\ell\,\mathcal{T}(t)\,dt\,,

(72)

\displaystyle e_{\mu}dx^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\ell\Bigl(1+\mathcal{L}(t)+r\mathcal{T}(t)\Bigr)dt-\ell\,dr\,,

(73)

\displaystyle m_{\mu}dx^{\mu}

\displaystyle=2\ell r\mathcal{L}(t)\,dt\,,

(74)

\displaystyle\omega_{\mu}dx^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(1-\mathcal{L}(t)+r\mathcal{T}(t)\Bigr)dt-dr\,.

(75)

These gauge fields define the two-dimensional Newton–Cartan geometry associated with the bulk gauge connection
A(t,r)
.
It is convenient to introduce the inverse temporal vector
v^{\mu}
and the inverse spatial vielbein
e^{\mu}
, which together with
\tau_{\mu}
and
e_{\mu}
satisfy the following orthogonality and completeness relations of Newton–Cartan geometry

\displaystyle v^{\mu}\tau_{\mu}

\displaystyle=1\,,

\displaystyle\tau_{\mu}e^{\mu}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle e_{\mu}v^{\mu}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle e_{\mu}e^{\mu}

\displaystyle=1\,,

\displaystyle\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}

\displaystyle=v^{\mu}\tau_{\nu}+e^{\mu}e_{\nu}\,,

(76)

which we can solve for the contravariant objects, yielding

\displaystyle v^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell\mathcal{T}(t)}\Bigl(\partial_{t}+\bigl(1+\mathcal{L}(t)+r\mathcal{T}(t)\bigr)\partial_{r}\Bigr)\,,\hskip 14.22636pte^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}=-\frac{1}{\ell}\,\partial_{r}\,.

(77)

They allow us to construct the degenerate spatial metric and its inverse,

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}=e_{\mu}e_{\nu}\,,\qquad h^{\mu\nu}=e^{\mu}e^{\nu}\,.

(78)

For the general connection above, the spatial metric becomes

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}=\ell^{2}\Bigl(1+\mathcal{L}(t)+r\mathcal{T}(t)\Bigr)^{2}dt^{2}-2\ell^{2}\Bigl(1+\mathcal{L}(t)+r\mathcal{T}(t)\Bigr)dt\,dr+\ell^{2}dr^{2}\,,

(79)

and
h^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}=\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\partial_{r}^{2}
which are required to satisfy the following completeness relation

\displaystyle h^{\mu\nu}\tau_{\nu}=0\,,\qquad h_{\mu\nu}v^{\nu}=0\,,\qquad h^{\mu\rho}h_{\rho\nu}=\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}-v^{\mu}\tau_{\nu}\,.

(80)

This completes the Newton–Cartan data associated with the bulk gauge connection.
In the next subsection, we show that this geometry realises the EdS-G algebra as its isometry algebra.

3.2
Isometries of the Newton–Cartan geometry

In this subsection we restrict the geometry to constant values of
\mathcal{L}(t)=\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
\mathcal{T}(t)=\mathcal{T}_{0}
, for simplicity. The Newton–Cartan geometry we consider, therefore, takes the form

\displaystyle\tau_{\mu}dx^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\ell\mathcal{T}_{0}\,dt\,,

(81)

\displaystyle e_{\mu}dx^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\ell\Bigl(1+\mathcal{L}_{0}+r\mathcal{T}_{0}\Bigr)dt-\ell\,dr\,,

(82)

\displaystyle m_{\mu}dx^{\mu}

\displaystyle=2\ell r\mathcal{L}_{0}\,dt\,.

(83)

What makes the above truly a Newton–Cartan geometry is the fact that, in addition to the Galilean boost-invariant quantities
h^{\mu\nu}
and
\tau_{\mu}
, one can construct the following boost-invariant combinations
Bergshoeff:2014uea
;
Hartong:2014oma
;
Hartong:2024lqb

\displaystyle\hat{v}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=v^{\mu}-h^{\mu\nu}m_{\nu}\,,

(84)

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=h_{\mu\nu}-\tau_{\mu}m_{\nu}-\tau_{\nu}m_{\mu}\,.

(85)

The presence of the gauge field
m_{\mu}
captures the Bargmann symmetry of this two-dimensional model. Let us emphasise this point clearly by examining the isometries. Given the Killing vector
\xi
, the objects
\{h^{\mu\nu},\tau_{\mu}\}
transform as usual spacetime tensors by a Lie derivative, namely

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi}h^{\mu\nu}=0\,,\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\tau_{\mu}=0\,.

(86)

Notice that these objects of the Newton-Cartan geometry are Galilean boost-invariant, so their transformation laws do not exhibit any gauge parameter. For the rest of the tensors built upon from these, it is possible to group both diffeomorphisms
\xi
and gauge symmetries
\lambda
in one symmetry transformation
\delta_{\epsilon}\equiv\mathcal{L}_{\xi}+\delta_{\lambda}
, generating the following symmetry transformations for the remaining tensors

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi}v^{\mu}=h^{\mu\nu}\lambda_{\nu}\,,\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{L}_{\xi}h_{\mu\nu}=2\lambda_{(\mu}\tau_{\nu)}\,\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{L}_{\xi}m_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}\sigma+\lambda_{\mu}\,,

(87)

where
\sigma
parametrises the
U(1)
gauge parameter of
m_{\mu}
while
\lambda_{\mu}
denotes the Galilei boost parameters. Notice that we used the completeness relations (
76
) to derive these expressions and they also show that the combinations introduced in (
84
) are indeed boost invariant.

Let us now turn to working out the symmetries. Given the Newton-Cartan geometry in (
81
), we insert the ansatz

\displaystyle\xi=\xi^{t}(t,r)\partial_{t}+\xi^{r}(t,r)\partial_{r}\,,\qquad\lambda_{\mu}=\lambda_{\mu}(t,r)\,,\qquad\sigma=\sigma(t,r)\,,

(88)

into (
86
)–(
87
) and find a coupled system of partial differential equations. The first two constraints,
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}h^{\mu\nu}=0
and
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\tau_{\mu}=0
from (
86
) together imply that

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\xi^{t}=0\,,\qquad\partial_{r}\xi^{r}=0\,,\qquad\partial_{t}\xi^{t}=0\,,

(89)

so these already imply that
\xi^{t}=\mathrm{const}
and
\xi^{r}=\xi^{r}(t)
, while the remaining conditions in (
87
) determine the gauge parameters
\lambda_{\mu}
and
\sigma
in terms of
\xi^{\mu}
. Solving the full system leads to

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{0}}{\mathcal{T}_{0}}\partial_{t}+\Big(b_{1}\cosh(\mathcal{T}_0 t)+b_{2}\sinh(\mathcal{T}_0t)\Big)\,\partial_{r}\,,

(90)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=\ell\left(b_{1}-b_{2}\right)(\cosh(\mathcal{T}_0 t)-\sinh(\mathcal{T}_0 t))\bigg(\left(\mathcal{T}_{0}r+\mathcal{L}_{0}+1\right)\partial_{t}-\partial_{r}\bigg)\,,

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=b_{3}+\frac{\ell}{\mathcal{T}_{0}}\bigg((b_{1}+b_{2})\mathcal{L}_{0}e^{\mathcal{T}_{0}t}+(b_{1}-b_{2})(\mathcal{T}_{0}r+1)e^{-\mathcal{T}_{0}t}\bigg)\,,

where
b_{0},b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}
are integration constants. To define the extended algebra we take the tuples
(\xi_{i},\sigma_{i})
for
i=0,1,2,3
where for each
i
we set all
b_{j}=0
except
b_{i}
. Now, we define the commutator by

\displaystyle\commutator{(\xi_i, \sigma_i)}{(\xi_j, \sigma_j)}:=\bigg(\commutator{\xi_i}{\xi_j},\xi_{i}(\sigma_{j})-\xi_{j}(\sigma_{i})\bigg)\,.

(91)

This bracket arises from combining diffeomorphisms with
U(1)
gauge transformations and corresponds to the semi-direct product structure of spacetime and the Bargmann symmetries. Thus, we find (with some suitable rescaling of the constants
b_{i}
) that

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=(\xi_{0},\sigma_{0})=\left(\frac{1}{\ell\mathcal{T}_{0}}\partial_{t},0\right)\,,

(92)

3.4
Non-relativistic Jackiw-Teitelboim gravity from an action principle

Having identified the bulk Newton–Cartan geometry associated with the gauge connection, we now ask whether the same structure can be obtained from a second-order action principle. In this subsection, we show that this is indeed the case: the geometry constructed above arises as a solution of a non-relativistic JT-type model formulated directly in Newton–Cartan variables. This provides a complementary second-order description of the bulk theory and sets the stage for the comparison with the first-order gauge-theoretic formulation in Section

. The corresponding action, proposed in

An equivalent form of the action, which is simply a rewriting of (

\displaystyle I[\tau,e,m,\phi]=\kappa\int d^{2}x\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\phi\left(\partial_{\mu}\omega_{\nu}-\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\tau_{\mu}e_{\nu}\right)\,.

3.5
The relationship between the first and second order formulations

Having shown that the Newton–Cartan geometry introduced above arises from a second-order non-relativistic JT-type action, we now compare this description with the first-order gauge theory formulation underlying the boundary construction. Our goal is to show how the bulk fields appearing in the Newton–Cartan description are related to the variables of the first-order formalism, and to clarify in what sense the second-order equations reproduce only part of the dynamics of the full first-order theory.

The BF action principle for Galilean de Sitter gravity in the first-order formalism is
Gomis:2000bd

\displaystyle I=\mu\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Bigg(\phi R(G)+\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\Big(\eta R(M)+\zeta R(H)-\rho R(P)\Big)\Bigg)\,.

(107)

with
\mu
being a coupling constant, and the two-form curvatures given by

\displaystyle R(H)=d\tau\,,\hskip 14.22636ptR(P)=de+\omega\tau\,,\hskip 14.22636ptR(G)=d\omega-\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\tau e\,,\hskip 14.22636ptR(M)=dm+\omega e\,.

(108)

Here
\{\eta,\zeta,\rho,\phi\}
are scalar fields, while
\{\tau,e,\omega,m\}
are one-forms. In this formalism,
\omega_{\mu}
is still a dynamical field. A general variation of (
107
) produces the boundary term

\Theta=\mu\int_{\partial\mathcal{M}}\Bigg(\phi\delta\omega+\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\Big(\eta\delta m+\zeta\delta\tau-\rho\delta e\Big)\Bigg)\,.

(109)

To obtain a well-defined variational principle, we impose boundary conditions at the asymptotic de Sitter boundary, such that the scalar fields are proportional to the radial components of the gauge fields,

\displaystyle\eta=k\,\tau_{r}\,,\hskip 14.22636pt\rho=k\,e_{r}\,,\hskip 14.22636pt\phi=k\,\omega_{r}\,,\hskip 14.22636pt\zeta=k\,m_{r}\,,

(110a)

with

a constant. We conclude that the action must then in addition to (
107
) be supplemented by the boundary term

I_{\partial}=-\frac{k}{2}\mu\int dr\,\Bigg(\omega_{r}^{2}+\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\Big(m_{r}^{2}+\tau_{r}^{2}-e_{r}^{2}\Big)\Bigg)\,.

(111)

Now that the variational principle is well defined, variation of the action (
107
) with respect to
\{\eta,\rho,\phi,\zeta\}
imposes the flatness constraints

\displaystyle R(H)=0\,,\qquad R(P)=0\,,\qquad R(G)=0\,,\qquad R(M)=0\,,

(112)

for the curvatures defined in (
108
). One may solve algebraically these flatness constraints in (
112
) to determine the spin connection
\omega
in terms of the other fields
m,\tau
and
e
finding

\displaystyle\omega_{\mu}=\frac{1}{E}\,\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\bigl(\partial_{\alpha}(m_{\beta})\,\tau_{\mu}-\partial_{\alpha}(e_{\beta})\,e_{\mu}\bigr)\,,

(113)

where
E=-\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\tau_{\mu}e_{\nu}
. Note that this was precisely the form we used to define the second-order formulation in (
98

\{\tau,e,\omega,m\}
gives

\displaystyle d\eta=0\,,\hskip 11.38092ptd\rho+\omega\eta-\tau\phi=0\,,\hskip 11.38092ptd\phi-\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}(\tau\rho-e\eta)=0\,,\hskip 11.38092ptd\zeta+\omega\rho-e\phi=0\,.

(114a)

Solving the curvature constraints (
108

110

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell\,\mathcal{T}(t)\,dt\,,

(115a)

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell\,\big(-dr+\left(r\mathcal{T}(t)+1+\mathcal{L}(t)\right)\,dt\big)\,,

(115b)

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-dr+\big(r\mathcal{T}(t)+1-\mathcal{L}(t)\big)\,dt\,,

(115c)

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\ell r\mathcal{L}(t)dt\,,

(115d)

4
Discussion

In this paper, we considered the two-dimensional Galilean version of de Sitter space, whose symmetries are captured by the extended de Sitter-Galilei (EdS-G) algebra. We studied aspects of both its bulk and boundary realisation.

On the boundary side, we employed a Schwarzian-like action to compute one-loop contributions. We report that these contributions capture quantum fluctuations that dominate the low-energy density of states, and at low temperatures, we found the partition function to be dominated by
\mathcal{Z}_{\text{EdS-G}}(\beta)\sim\beta^{-2}
. Here, this
2
correctly reflects the factors of
1/2
that are added corresponding to the four generators of the EdS-G algebra. To arrive at this result, we derived the symplectic form entering the path integral measure directly from the effective action by introducing Ostrogradsky canonical momenta. To the best of our knowledge, this action-based derivation of the symplectic form has not been emphasised in the literature. We also found that the logarithmic contribution is not accompanied by an imaginary phase, in agreement with corresponding two-dimensional relativistic computations
Maldacena:2019cbz
;
Cotler:2019nbi
. This contrasts with the relativistic result from four dimensions
Blacker:2025zca
;
Maulik:2025phe
, which may be attributed to the intrinsically two-dimensional nature of the present computation.
It should also be pointed out that the central charge
c_{1}
drops out of our final result – this is potentially a side-effect of choosing our vacuum in (
38
). We furthermore assumed, in our computation, that the answer is one-loop exact. In light of the close relation to the warped Schwarzian theory
Afshar:2019tvp
, this appears plausible, although it certainly warrants further investigation. An exciting direction for future work would be to construct a SYK-like realisation of this model. A possible source of inspiration in this direction is
Afshar:2019axx
, where related ideas were explored in the context of flat space holography and the complex SYK model and its corresponding Carrollian counterpart.

On the bulk side, we show that once the EdS-G algebra is geometrised in a Newton-Cartan language, one can construct a JT-like action whose geometry solves the equations of motion. We note in particular that, upon restricting to the torsionless sector with
d\tau=0
, the equation obtained by varying the JT-like action with respect to
\tau_{\mu}
involves only first derivatives. It would be interesting to understand whether this JT-like action reflects a deeper Lagrange-multiplier role for
\tau_{\mu}
, or whether it is simply a feature of the torsionless truncation. We also leave a careful Hamiltonian analysis of this action for future discussion.
A natural next step for future study is to investigate bulk fluctuations around the background studied here, along lines similar to recent analyses of logarithmic contributions and Lichnerowicz-type zero modes in near-extremal black holes
Maulik:2024dwq
;
Blacker:2025zca
;
Maulik:2025phe
;
PandoZayas:2026vbg
. The goal would be to use these zero modes to verify our prediction regarding the exponent factor of
\beta^{-2}
entering the partition function. Finally, we think this setup may also aid the exploration of thermal gravitational systems in a non-Lorentzian context.

We want to end by emphasising that, in this paper, we have contributed to paving the way for extending holographic duality in low dimensions beyond relativistic symmetries. In conjunction, we also believe our results contribute to the development of tools for further unravelling the mysteries of de Sitter spacetime.

Appendix A
Ostrogradsky symplectic form for the Schwarzian theory

In this appendix, we show that the symplectic structure of the Schwarzian theory
Witten:1987ty
;
Alekseev:1988ce
;
Stanford:2017thb
;
Saad:2019lba
can be recovered directly from Ostrogradsky variables.

We begin with the Schwarzian theory

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{Sch}}[f]=-\int d\tau\left(\mathrm{Sch}(f,\tau)+\frac{1}{2}{f^{\prime}}^{2}\right)\,.

(121)

We now expand to second order with
f=\bar{f}+\delta f
deriving the

\displaystyle I^{(1)}_{\mathrm{Sch}}

\displaystyle=\int d\tau\,\partial_{\tau}\left(\bar{f}^{\prime}-\frac{\bar{f}^{\prime\prime 2}}{\bar{f}^{\prime 3}}+\frac{\bar{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}}{\bar{f}^{\prime 2}}\right)\delta f\,,

(122)

\displaystyle I^{(2)}_{\mathrm{Sch}}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\tau}{\bar{f}^{\prime 2}}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\bar{f}^{\prime 2}\delta f^{\prime 2}+\frac{3}{2}\frac{\bar{f}^{\prime\prime 2}}{\bar{f}^{\prime 2}}\delta f^{\prime 2}-2\frac{\bar{f}^{\prime\prime}}{\bar{f}^{\prime}}\delta f^{\prime}\delta f^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\delta f^{\prime\prime 2}\right]\,.

(123)

Now, we compute the Ostrogradsky conjugate momenta to
\delta f
to find

\displaystyle p_{\delta f}=\frac{\partial L^{(2)}_{\mathrm{Sch}}}{\partial(\delta f^{\prime})}-\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(\frac{\partial L^{(2)}_{\mathrm{Sch}}}{\partial(\delta f^{\prime\prime})}\right)=\frac{2}{\bar{f^{\prime}}^{2}}\left[\mathrm{Sch}\left(\tanh\tfrac{\bar{f}}{2},\tau\right)\delta f^{\prime}+\tfrac{\bar{f}^{\prime\prime}}{{\bar{f}^{\prime}}}\delta f^{\prime\prime}-\tfrac{1}{2}\delta f^{\prime\prime\prime}\right]\,.

(124)

Hence, the symplectic form which we will use to compute the measure is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{Sch}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,dP_{\delta f}\wedge d(\delta f)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\beta}\frac{2\,d\tau}{\bar{f}^{\prime 2}}\left[\mathrm{Sch}\!\left(\tanh\frac{\bar{f}}{2},\tau\right)d\delta f^{\prime}\wedge d\delta f+\frac{\bar{f}^{\prime\prime}}{\bar{f}^{\prime}}d\delta f^{\prime\prime}\wedge d\delta f-\frac{1}{2}d\delta f^{\prime\prime\prime}\wedge d\delta f\right].

(125)

The last term can be integrated by parts to find

\displaystyle\Omega^{(2)}_{\mathrm{Sch}}=\int_{0}^{\beta}\frac{d\tau}{\bar{f}^{\prime 2}}\left[d\delta f^{\prime\prime}\wedge d\delta f^{\prime}+2\,\mathrm{Sch}(\tanh\tfrac{\bar{f}}{2},\tau)d\delta f^{\prime}\wedge d\delta f\right]\,.

(126)

Once evaluated on the classical solution
\bar{f}(\tau)=\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\tau
this gives the same as equation (114) of
Saad:2019lba
and equation (2.8) of
Stanford:2017thb

(131)

\displaystyle I_{n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}D_{n}&0&-iE_{n}&nE_{n}\\
0&D_{n}&-nE_{n}&-iE_{n}\\

(132)

For modes with
n\geq 2
the quadratic fluctuation integral is a standard Gaussian.
The case
n=1
is special, since the corresponding integral is not Gaussian and the symplectic form is degenerate, and must therefore be treated separately.
We denote its contribution by

(133)

\displaystyle=\frac{32\pi^{4}}{3\beta^{2}}n^{2}(n^{2}-1)(ck-24\kappa^{2})\,,

(134)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\det I_{n}^{(2)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{32\pi^{6}}{3\beta^{4}}n^{4}(n^{2}-1)(ck-24\kappa^{2})\,.

(135)

The remaining contribution comes from the
n=1

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}(\beta)=2\pi i\left[k\int d\tilde{\sigma}_{1}d\tilde{\sigma}_{1}^{*}+4i\kappa\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{\beta}\right)^{2}\int d\epsilon_{1}d\tilde{\sigma}_{1}^{*}\right]e^{\frac{4\pi^{2}i}{\beta^{2}}\left[\frac{k\beta}{2}\absolutevalue{\tilde{\sigma}_1}^{2}-4\pi i\kappa\epsilon_{1}^{*}\tilde{\sigma}_{1}\right]}=2\beta\,.

The first term gives

Appendix C

Appendix D
Equations of motion for the non-relativistic JT action

In this appendix, we present the equations of motion for the second-order non-relativistic JT action written entirely in terms of lower-index Newton–Cartan variables. This formulation is equivalent to the one presented in the main text, but its variation is somewhat simpler since the action is expressed directly in terms of the one-forms
\tau_{\mu}
,
e_{\mu}
, and
m_{\mu}
. We have explicitly verified that the resulting equations of motion are equivalent to those obtained from the formulation used in the main text.

The action is

I[\tau,e,m,\phi]=\kappa\,\int d^{2}x\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\phi\,\Big(\partial_{\mu}\omega_{\nu}-\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\tau_{\mu}e_{\nu}\Big)\,,

(146)

where the spin connection is given by

\omega_{\mu}=\frac{1}{E}\,\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\Big(\partial_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\tau_{\mu}-\partial_{\alpha}e_{\beta}e_{\mu}\Big)\,,

(147)

and
E=-\epsilon^{\nu\rho}\tau_{\nu}e_{\rho}
. If one furthermore wishes to impose the torsionless condition
d\tau=0
, this may be implemented by introducing a Lagrange multiplier
\zeta
and adding the term

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{\ell^{2}}\int d^{2}x\,\zeta\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\tau_{\nu}

(148)

to the action. Variation with respect to
\zeta
then imposes
\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\tau_{\nu}=0
, which is equivalent to
d\tau=0
. We now vary the action with respect to the dynamical fields. For simplicity, boundary terms arising from integrations by parts will be omitted.

\displaystyle\delta\phi:

\displaystyle\qquad\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\Big(\partial_{\mu}\omega_{\nu}-\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\tau_{\mu}e_{\nu}\Big)=0\,,

\displaystyle\delta m_{\beta}:

\displaystyle\qquad\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\alpha}\Bigg(\frac{1}{E}\partial_{\mu}\phi\tau_{\nu}\Bigg)=0\,,

(149)

\displaystyle\delta\tau_{\mu}:

\displaystyle\qquad\Big(\epsilon^{\alpha\nu}\epsilon^{\mu\beta}e_{\beta}\omega_{\alpha}+\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\Big)\partial_{\nu}\phi-\frac{E}{\ell^{2}}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}e_{\nu}\phi=0\,,

\displaystyle\delta e_{\mu}:

\displaystyle\qquad\Bigg(\epsilon^{\beta\nu}\epsilon^{\alpha\mu}\tau_{\alpha}\omega_{\beta}-\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}e_{\beta}\Bigg)\partial_{\nu}\phi+E\epsilon^{\beta\nu}\epsilon^{\alpha\mu}\partial_{\alpha}\Bigg(\frac{1}{E}\partial_{\nu}\phi\,e_{\beta}\Bigg)+\frac{E}{\ell^{2}}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\tau_{\nu}\phi=0\,.

These equations are equivalent to (
100
) obtained from the formulation presented in the main text.
