Predisposing factors and histopathological changes in duodenogastric reflux: a retrospective case-control study
Authors: Ümit Karabulut, Ramazan Yolaçan, Berat Ebik, Ahmet Yavuz, Mustafa Zanyar Akkuzu, Ferhat Bacaksiz, Çiğdem Budak Ece, Feyzullah Uçmak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuodenogastric reflux (DGR), defined as the retrograde flow of duodenal contents into the stomach, has an incompletely understood pathophysiology. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between DGR and clinical, endoscopic, histopathological, and laboratory parameters in dyspeptic patients, and to assess its association with Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection, intestinal metaplasia, and potential risk factors.MethodThis retrospective case-control study included 524 patients who underwent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy between January 2017 and December 2022. A total of 287 patients with endoscopic signs of DGR such as bile pooling, mucosal edema, or hyperemia were classified as the DGR group, while 237 patients without these findings served as controls. Clinical, biochemical, endoscopic, and histopathological data were compared, and risk factors were analyzed using multivariate logistic regression.FindingsChemical gastropathy was significantly more frequent in the DGR group (p < 0.001). There were no significant differences regarding intestinal metaplasia or H. pylori positivity (p > 0.05). The DGR group showed higher total bilirubin and lower albumin levels (p < 0.05). Cholecystectomy and cholelithiasis were identified as independent strong risk factors (p < 0.001), while age contributed modestly; gender and H. pylori infection showed no significant association.ConclusionsDGR is a multifactorial condition associated with gastric mucosal injury and systemic biomarker changes. A history of cholecystectomy or gallstones markedly increases DGR risk. Its independent role in intestinal metaplasia remains uncertain, warranting further prospective research.Keywords: Duodenogastric reflux, Chemical gastropathy, Cholecystectomy, Intestinal metaplasia, Helicobacter pylori

Introduction

DGR is a gastrointestinal condition defined by the backward flow of duodenal contents into the stomach, the pathophysiology of which is not fully understood. Normally, the pyloric sphincter prevents bile from entering the stomach. However, surgeries such as cholecystectomy or pyloroplasty may compromise this function and lead to DGR, exposing the gastric mucosa to bile acids and pancreatic enzymes [1–4].

The mechanism of DGR is multifactorial. Following cholecystectomy, the loss of the gallbladder’s reservoir function, changes in upper gastrointestinal motility, and disruption of neurohormonal balance may increase the likelihood of DGR. Pyloric dysfunction and impaired coordination between the stomach and duodenum can further promote reflux [4, 5]. Recent studies have emphasized that duodenogastric reflux is a complex condition influenced by multiple physiological and clinical factors, and a better understanding of its underlying mechanisms and associated risk factors is essential for improving its diagnosis and management [6, 7].

Prolonged DGR may induce mucosal inflammation, foveolar hyperplasia, and parietal cell loss. More critically, it may contribute to intestinal metaplasia, a recognized premalignant condition. Inflammation-related mechanisms are known to play a key role in carcinogenesis through their effects on cellular proliferation, apoptosis, and angiogenesis. In this context, angiogenesis within the tumor microenvironment is also considered an important contributor to the progression of premalignant changes [8, 9]. Likewise, H. pylori infection is associated with inflammation, atrophy, and metaplasia. Some studies suggest a synergistic effect of DGR and H. pylori, but the nature of this relationship remains unclear [5].

This study aims to evaluate whether, in addition to known surgical factors, clinical and demographic characteristics contribute to DGR development. It also investigates whether DGR, like H. pylori, is a risk factor for intestinal metaplasia, based on endoscopic and histopathological findings.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective case-control study included 524 patients who presented to the Gastroenterology Outpatient Clinic of Dicle University Faculty of Medicine with dyspeptic complaints and underwent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy between January 2017 and December 2022. Only patients with fully accessible records were included.

Patients with chronic liver disease were excluded to minimize confounding effects, as such conditions can independently alter serum bilirubin and albumin levels, potentially biasing the assessment of their association with duodenogastric reflux.

Duodenogastric reflux (DGR) was defined based on the presence of visible bile in the gastric lumen during endoscopy. To distinguish pathological reflux from physiological bile presence, only cases with prominent and persistent bile accumulation were considered as DGR. Accompanying mucosal changes such as erythema or edema were considered supportive findings; however, since these changes may also be observed in other gastric conditions, they were not used as independent diagnostic criteria but were evaluated in conjunction with the presence of bile reflux. Due to the retrospective design of the study, objective diagnostic methods such as Bilitec monitoring, 24-hour pH monitoring, or scintigraphy could not be applied. All endoscopic procedures were performed after overnight fasting according to the standard institutional protocol and were evaluated by experienced endoscopists.

Based on these criteria, 287 patients with endoscopic evidence of bile reflux constituted the DGR group, while 237 patients without these findings served as the control group.

Histopathological evaluation

Biopsy samples from the antrum and corpus were obtained from all patients. Antral biopsies were taken 2–3 cm from the pylorus, from both lesser and greater curvatures. In addition, corpus biopsies were obtained from the lesser and greater curvatures of the gastric body, in accordance with the Updated Sydney System (Houston update) recommendations [10]. Specimens were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and stained with Hematoxylin–Eosin (H&E), Alcian Blue (pH 2.5), and Periodic Acid–Schiff (PAS).

Each parameter was graded on a four-point scale (none, mild, moderate, severe) according to the Updated Sydney System (Houston update). Chemical gastropathy and reflux-related changes were also reported. Chemical gastropathy was defined based on characteristic histological features, including foveolar hyperplasia, mucosal edema, smooth muscle fibers in the lamina propria, vascular congestion, and minimal inflammatory infiltrate. It was differentiated from other types of gastritis by the relative absence of significant inflammatory cell infiltration and the predominance of reactive epithelial changes.

Due to the retrospective design of the study, a standardized grading system for chemical gastropathy was not available; therefore, this lesion was evaluated descriptively based on established histopathological criteria. Intestinal metaplasia was evaluated according to the biopsy site (antrum and corpus).

Histopathological evaluations were performed by experienced gastrointestinal pathologists using routine pathology reports.

Statistical evaluation and clinical comparisons

Patient groups were compared based on DGR presence regarding demographics, labs, endoscopy, and histopathology. Factors like cholecystectomy, gallstones, and intestinal metaplasia risk were analyzed. Etiological and clinical contributors to DGR were statistically assessed.

Statistical analysis

Normality of data was assessed using Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Shapiro-Wilk tests, and distribution measures (variation, skewness, kurtosis). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD; categorical variables as percentages. Between-group comparisons used Independent Samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, depending on distribution.

Chi-square test evaluated associations between DGR and endoscopic/histopathological parameters, including cholecystectomy and cholelithiasis. Binary logistic regression analyzed independent predictors of DGR, with results reported as OR, Exp(B), and 95% CI. Model fit was assessed via Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using SPSS 24.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

A total of 524 patients were included in the study. Duodenogastric reflux (DGR) findings were identified in 287 patients (54.7%), whereas no DGR findings were observed in 237 patients (45.3%). The mean age was 49.7 ± 17.8 years in the DGR group and 52.9 ± 15.1 years in the control group. This difference was statistically significant (p = 0.030). There was no statistically significant difference between the groups in terms of gender distribution (p = 0.081) (Table 1).

Endoscopic and histopathological findings

Endoscopically, the prevalence of gastric ulcers did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.629). Duodenal ulcers were observed in 18.8% of the case group and 13.5% of the control group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.064).

Histopathological examination revealed that chemical gastropathy was present in 40.5% of the DGR group and 23.7% of the control group, representing a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of intestinal metaplasia and H. pylori positivity (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

When laboratory findings were evaluated, total bilirubin levels were significantly higher in the case group compared to the control group (p < 0.001). In addition, serum albumin levels were found to be significantly lower in the case group (p = 0.003) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the clinical, histopathological, and laboratory characteristics of patients with endoscopically diagnosed duodenogastric reflux (DGR) presenting with dyspeptic symptoms. Findings show that DGR causes not only localized gastric mucosal changes but also systemic biomarker alterations. Its link with gallbladder diseases highlights its clinical relevance. These results suggest DGR has a broader, multifactorial pathophysiology involving both local and systemic factors. Unlike many studies, our research emphasizes that age and anatomical changes contribute to DGR, rather than a single cause.

In our study, the mean age of DGR patients was significantly lower than that of the control group. Regression analysis identified age as an independent risk factor. Thus, DGR should be considered clinically relevant not only in older but also in middle-aged adults. Reduced visceral sensitivity with age may lead to fewer GI symptoms in elderly individuals, resulting in asymptomatic cases and delayed diagnosis. As with H. pylori, some patients may not show clear symptoms, making diagnosis based solely on presentation potentially misleading. Although patients with DGR appeared younger in the descriptive analysis, multivariate regression analysis evaluates the independent effect of age after adjustment for other covariates included in the model. Therefore, the association observed in the regression model may reflect the effect of age after controlling for potential confounding factors such as gallstone disease history.

Although literature suggests a greater predisposition to gallstones and functional gastrointestinal disorders in women, our study found no significant association between gender and DGR.

We observed a significantly higher rate of chemical gastropathy in DGR patients, suggesting that retrograde bile flow induces specific mucosal damage through chemical irritation. When deconjugated bile acids and compounds like lysolecithin mix with gastric acid, they may cause lesions such as foveolar hyperplasia, interstitial edema, capillary dilation, and parietal cell damage [11]. These features reflect chemical gastropathy and disruption of gastric mucosal defense. Our findings suggest DGR can be identified not only clinically but also histopathologically, highlighting the value of endoscopic biopsies.

The relationship between DGR and H. pylori remains unclear [12]. Some studies suggest bile acids suppress H. pylori and promote ulcer healing via gastric alkalinization [13]. Akbaş et al. found lower H. pylori prevalence in bile gastritis [14]. In our study, H. pylori positivity was slightly higher in the DGR group, but not significantly so, suggesting an indirect, complex relationship likely influenced by host factors and pH, rather than a direct link.

Although the relationship between DGR and H. pylori remains unclear, clinical observations suggest a bidirectional interaction. Some studies report improvement in DGR following H. pylori eradication [13], indicating that while both can independently cause mucosal inflammation, their coexistence may intensify mucosal damage. Literature shows that primary bile reflux and H. pylori gastritis differ histologically, but their coexistence is more often linked to premalignant changes like intestinal metaplasia and atrophic gastritis [15]. DGR—particularly post-cholecystectomy—has also been associated with increased risks of gastric cancer and intestinal metaplasia [16, 17], suggesting a possible role in carcinogenesis.

A study conducted by Chen et al. in 2021 identified advanced age and cholelithiasis as significant risk factors for intestinal metaplasia in patients with endoscopically confirmed bile reflux [18]. Similarly, a large Japanese study with 2,283 patients found a strong association between high bile reflux and metaplasia, independent of H. pylori infection [19], indicating bile acids may directly influence mucosal cell differentiation.

Supporting this, a prospective study by Gündoğdu et al. (n = 136) found higher H. pylori prevalence and more frequent antral metaplasia in bile reflux patients [20]. They suggested the antrum’s anatomical exposure to acid and bile increases its vulnerability. This emphasizes regional variation in gastric mucosal response. However, some studies found no significant correlation; for instance, Akbaş et al. reported similar atrophy and metaplasia rates in bile reflux-positive and -negative patients [14].

Our findings are consistent with such results, showing no statistically significant difference in the frequency of intestinal metaplasia between the DGR and non-DGR groups. This discrepancy may be attributed to factors such as reflux duration, its chemical composition, the duration of mucosal exposure, and individual genetic or environmental differences. Moreover, not only the presence of reflux but also the severity of coexisting H. pylori infection, the local immune response, and accompanying comorbidities should be considered in the development of metaplasia.

Laboratory data showed statistically significant differences in total bilirubin and albumin levels between the DGR and control groups. However, it should be noted that most of these values remained within normal reference ranges. Therefore, the clinical significance of these biochemical differences should be interpreted with caution. The slightly higher bilirubin levels observed in the DGR group may reflect underlying biliary conditions or alterations in biliary physiology rather than a direct effect of DGR, whereas lower albumin levels could be associated with mild inflammatory processes, nutritional status, or other systemic factors. Given the retrospective cross-sectional design of the study, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship between DGR and systemic biochemical changes. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify whether these laboratory differences have meaningful clinical implications.

Multivariate analysis confirmed that cholelithiasis and cholecystectomy are strong, independent risk factors for DGR. Continuous bile flow and impaired gastric function post-cholecystectomy may promote reflux and mucosal injury, highlighting the need for clinical vigilance in such patients.

This study has several limitations. Due to its retrospective design, objective diagnostic methods such as Bilitec monitoring, 24-hour pH monitoring, or hepatobiliary scintigraphy were not available, and the diagnosis of DGR was based on endoscopic findings, which represents a major limitation of the study. In addition, the severity of DGR could not be consistently graded because standardized endoscopic severity data were not available for all patients. Potential confounding factors such as medication use and dietary habits could also not be fully evaluated. Furthermore, information regarding smoking status, alcohol consumption, metabolic factors such as diabetes and BMI, and other motility-related conditions was not consistently available in the medical records and therefore could not be included in the regression analysis.

Therefore, prospective studies using objective diagnostic methods and standardized grading systems are needed to further clarify the relationship between DGR, histopathological changes, and systemic biomarkers. Furthermore, long-term prospective and longitudinal studies are warranted to better elucidate the potential role of DGR in the development of premalignant lesions and gastric cancer.

Conclusions

DGR development is significantly influenced by cholelithiasis and cholecystectomy. The coexistence of DGR and H. pylori may exert a dual toxic effect on the gastric mucosa, potentially resulting in more severe damage. Our study demonstrates a strong association between DGR and chemical gastropathy. However, in terms of premalignant changes such as intestinal metaplasia, no significant association between DGR and intestinal metaplasia was observed in our study, and DGR alone may not be a sufficient determinant. While some studies suggest that bile reflux combined with H. pylori infection synergistically increases the risk of metaplasia and atrophy, our study did not find a significant effect of this combination on metaplasia rates. Prospective, functional, and longitudinal studies are needed in the future to better understand the long-term complications and malignant transformation potential of DGR.
