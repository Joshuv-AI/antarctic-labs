CAPO: Counterfactual Credit Assignment in Sequential Cooperative Teams
Shripad Deshmukh, Jayakumar Subramanian, Raghavendra Addanki, Nikos Vlassis

Abstract
In cooperative teams where agents act in a fixed order and share a single team reward, it is hard to know how much each agent contributed, and harder still when agents are updated one at a time because data collected earlier no longer reflects the new policies. We introduce the Sequential Aristocrat Utility (SeqAU), the unique per-agent learning signal that maximizes the individual learnability of each agent’s action, extending the classical framework of Wolpert and Tumer 2002 to this sequential setting. From SeqAU we derive CAPO (Counterfactual Advantage Policy Optimization), a critic-free policy-gradient algorithm. CAPO fits a per-agent reward decomposition from group rewards and computes the per-agent advantage in closed form plus a handful of forward passes through the current policy, requiring no extra environment calls beyond the initial batch. We give analytic bias and variance bounds and validate them on a controlled sequential bandit, where CAPO’s advantage over standard baselines grows with the team size. The framework is general; multi-LLM pipelines are a natural deployment target.



1
Introduction

Cooperative multi-agent systems arise in settings ranging from
classical multi-agent reinforcement learning
(
Vlassis 2007
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)

to modern pipelines composed of large language models and
tool-using agents
(
Liu et al. 2025
;
Zhao et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2026
)
. A
common challenge in these systems is credit assignment: a team of
agents receives only one team-level reward, and attributing this
shared signal to individual agents is under-determined. Classical
approaches handle this through centralized critics, whose input
space grows exponentially in the team
size
(
Foerster et al. 2018
)
; through factored value functions,
which introduce modeling
constraints
(
Sunehag et al. 2018
;
Rashid et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Peng et al. 2021
)
;
or through cumulative importance-sampling corrections, whose
variance grows exponentially in the number of agents already
updated
(
Kuba et al. 2022
)
. The sequential cooperative setting,
in which agents act in a fixed order and are updated one at a time
within a batch, brings a further difficulty: updating an upstream
agent shifts the distribution over downstream agents’ behavior, so
rollouts collected under the pre-update policy are off-policy for
subsequent updates within the same batch.

Contributions.

•

Sequential Aristocrat Utility (Theorem
1
).

We extend the opacity-learnability framework of
Wolpert and Tumer 2002

from simultaneous to sequential cooperative teams, and identify the
unique prefix-dependent baseline that maximizes per-agent
learnability; subtracting it from the team reward gives the

Sequential Aristocrat Utility
(SeqAU). This characterization
and the upstream-cancellation identity
(Appendix
B
) it implies are
estimator-independent and apply to any sequential cooperative team.

•

CAPO algorithm.

We instantiate SeqAU as
CAPO
(Counterfactual Advantage
Policy Optimization), a critic-free policy-gradient algorithm with
three ingredients: an additive reward
decomposition
(
Guestrin et al. 2002
)

fit by a least-squares ridge estimator, an upstream-cancellation
identity that reduces the direct per-agent effect to a single-agent
marginal, and fictitious sampling for the indirect effect (drawing
counterfactual continuations from the current joint policy).

•

Closed-form bias and variance analysis
(Theorems
2
–
4
).

CAPO’s per-agent advantage bias is linear in the non-additivity
residual; its variance adapts to inter-agent coupling
(
K
-independent in the factored-policy limit,
O(K-k)
worst
case), compared to the inherent
\Theta(K)
of shared-baseline
estimators like MA-GRPO
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
and
O(\exp K)
of
cumulative-IS estimators like HAPPO
(
Kuba et al. 2022
)
and
HA-GRPO; the policy-gradient MSE has no importance-sampling-ratio
inflation. To our knowledge, such a closed-form bias-and-variance
analysis of per-agent advantages is not available for
decomposed-critic methods in the multi-agent literature.

•

Empirical validation.

On a controlled sequential-bandit testbed, per-agent advantage MSE
scales as the theory predicts, CAPO’s end-to-end regret advantage
over MA-GRPO, HA-GRPO, and C3
(
Chen et al. 2026
)
grows with team
size, and an ablation confirms the indirect-effect correction is
necessary precisely when sequential-update non-stationarity is
present.

We develop the framework in a contextual-bandit setting with
sequential execution, where closed-form ground-truth advantages
and exact variance calculations allow the theoretical claims to be
tested cleanly. Extension to multi-agent language-model
pipelines
(
Liu et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2026
;
Zhao et al. 2025
)
, where
sequential execution is native, is left to follow-up work.

2
Setup

We consider a cooperative team of
K
agents acting in a contextual
bandit
(
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
)
, each agent with action set of size
A
. A context

x\sim\mathcal{D}
is drawn, and the agents act in a fixed order

1,2,\ldots,K
. Each agent
k
observes the prefix of upstream
actions
a_{<k}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1})
and samples its own
action
a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
from

\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k},x;\theta_{k})
. We write

a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{K})
for the joint action and

a_{>k}=(a_{k+1},\ldots,a_{K})
for the suffix. The joint policy
factorizes autoregressively,

\pi(a\mid x)=\prod_{k}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid a_{<k},x)
, and we denote
the induced downstream policy given a prefix-plus-focal action

a_{\leq k}
by

\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{\leq k})=\prod_{j>k}\pi_{j}(\cdot\mid a_{<j})
.
The joint action produces a scalar team reward

R(a,x)\in[-R_{\max},R_{\max}]
with conditional mean

f(a,x)=\mathbb{E}[R\mid a,x]
, and the team objective is

V(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}}[R]
. For brevity we suppress

x
throughout; every expression should be read pointwise in
x
,
and the theoretical results hold pointwise and therefore in
expectation over
\mathcal{D}
.

Execution order versus update order.

Two orderings recur throughout the paper. The
execution
order

1,2,\ldots,K
is fixed by the environment or task and
determines which agents’ actions are visible to which, defining
the prefix
a_{<k}
and suffix
a_{>k}
for each agent. The

update order
is the sequence in which agents’ policies are
updated within a single outer training iteration. When updates are
sequential, by the time it is agent
k
’s turn to be updated the
agents earlier in the update order have already moved from
\mu

to the new policy
\pi
, so the data collected under
\mu
is now
off-policy for agent
k
, leading to a non-stationarity issue.

3
Opacity, Learnability, and the Sequential Aristocrat Utility

Following
Wolpert and Tumer 2002
, let the
opacity
of agent

k
’s utility
U_{k}
at action
a_{k}
be the variance of
U_{k}
over
the randomness of all other agents’ actions, given
a_{k}
and any
information
\mathcal{I}
on which
U_{k}
is allowed to depend. Low
opacity means that agent
k
sees its own action’s effect on
U_{k}

without confounding from teammates’ choices; high opacity means the
signal is drowned in teammate noise. A utility is
factored

with respect to the team reward if the argmax over agent
k
’s
actions of
U_{k}
coincides with the argmax of the team reward,
holding all other agents fixed (Appendix
A
gives the
formal definition). Factoredness ensures that optimizing
U_{k}
does
not mislead the team; low opacity ensures that optimizing it is
statistically tractable. For simultaneously-acting teams,

Wolpert and Tumer 2002
showed that the
Aristocrat Utility

(AU),

U_{k}^{\mathrm{AU}}(a)\;=\;R(a)-\mathbb{E}_{a_{k}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{k}}[R(a_{-k},a_{k}^{\prime})],

is factored and has minimum opacity among all factored utilities
whose baseline depends on
a_{-k}
alone.

Sequential learnability.

In the sequential setting, agent
k
’s information set naturally
extends to include the upstream prefix
a_{<k}
. We consider
prefix-conditional utilities of the form
U_{k}(a)=R(a)-b_{k}(a_{<k})
,
with baseline
b_{k}
any function of
a_{<k}
, and define the
suffix-averaged form
of any
utility
U
,

\bar{U}_{k}(a_{k};a_{<k})\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{a_{>k}\sim\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{\leq k})}[U(a)].

For a reference Markov kernel

\rho(\cdot\mid a_{<k})\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{k})
and moves

a_{k}^{1},a_{k}^{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
, the
sequential learnability

of
U_{k}
is the signal-to-noise ratio

\Lambda_{k}^{\mathrm{seq}}(U_{k};\rho,a_{k}^{1},a_{k}^{2})\;=\;\frac{\mathbb{E}_{a_{<k}}\!\bigl[\bar{U}_{k}(a_{k}^{1};a_{<k})-\bar{U}_{k}(a_{k}^{2};a_{<k})\bigr]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{a_{<k},\,a_{k}\sim\rho(\cdot\mid a_{<k})}\!\bigl[\mathrm{Var}_{(a_{<k},a_{>k})\mid a_{k}}[U_{k}]\bigr]}},

(1)

whose numerator measures how much the suffix-averaged utility shifts
with the focal action, averaged over prefixes, and whose denominator
measures how much
U_{k}
fluctuates under teammate behavior for a
fixed focal action; both expectations and the inner variance are
pointwise in
a_{<k}
. The reference kernel is a degree of freedom;
the natural choice, used throughout, is

\rho(\cdot\mid a_{<k})=\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k})
.

Theorem 1

(Sequential Aristocrat Utility)
.

Within the class of sequential difference utilities

\{R-D(a_{<k}):D:\prod_{j<k}\mathcal{A}_{j}\to\mathbb{R}\}
, all of
which are factored with respect to
R
, the sequential learnability

\Lambda_{k}^{\mathrm{seq}}
is maximized for every prefix
a_{<k}
and
every pair
(a_{k}^{1},a_{k}^{2})
, uniquely, by the baseline

D^{*}(a_{<k})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{a_{k}^{\prime}\sim\rho(\cdot\mid a_{<k}),\;a_{>k}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k},a_{k}^{\prime})}\!\bigl[R(a_{k}^{\prime},a_{<k},a_{>k}^{\prime})\bigr].

(2)

The resulting
Sequential Aristocrat Utility
is

g_{k}^{\mathrm{SeqAU}}(a)=R(a)-D^{*}(a_{<k})
. Factoredness of the
search class, uniqueness, and the full proof are in
Appendix
A
.

Counterfactual advantage.

Setting
\rho(\cdot\mid a_{<k})=\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k})

in (
2
) gives the SeqAU-induced counterfactual advantage

A_{k}^{\mathrm{SeqAU}}(a_{\leq k})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[R\mid a_{\leq k}]\;-\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[R\mid a_{<k}],

(3)

the quantity that COMA
(
Foerster et al. 2018
)
estimates via a
learned centralized critic in the simultaneous-action setting. The
main contribution of this section is Theorem
1
, which
establishes that the baseline subtracted in (
3
),

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[R\mid a_{<k}]
, is not merely
a
natural choice in
the sequential setting but the
unique

learnability-maximizing one among prefix-conditional baselines, with
no structural assumption on the team reward.
Section
4
turns (
3
) into a tractable
estimator via an additive decomposition of
R
and an
upstream-cancellation identity, recovering a direct/indirect form
that collapses to Wolpert-Tumer’s Aristocrat Utility in the factored-policy
limit.

4
CAPO: Counterfactual Advantage Policy Optimization

4.1
The estimation problem and three failure modes

Within an outer iteration, write
\pi^{(0)}=\mu
for the
rollout-collection joint policy and, for
k\geq 1
,
\pi^{(k)}
for
the joint policy after agent
k
has been updated; the joint policy
at the moment of agent
k
’s update is therefore
\pi^{(k-1)}
, which
we call the
current joint policy
for that update. The SeqAU
target (
3
) requires two conditional expectations of the
team reward under
\pi^{(k-1)}
, neither directly available. Under
sequential updates,
\pi^{(k-1)}
differs from
\mu
by exactly the
upstream agents that have already moved, so the
\mu
-rollouts in the
batch are not a direct match for either expectation. Estimating the
SeqAU advantage therefore requires a structured reward-attribution
estimator; three lines of prior work attempt this, and each fails
in a specific way that motivates one ingredient of CAPO.

Centralized critic (COMA, DOP).

COMA
(
Foerster et al. 2018
)
learns
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[R\mid a]
as a
centralized
Q
-critic on the joint action and marginalizes the
focal action at
O(|\mathcal{A}|^{K})
cost; DOP
(
Wang et al. 2021
)

reduces this via an additively factored critic. Both target
simultaneous execution and do not address sequential-update drift.

Cumulative importance sampling (HA-GRPO).

HA-GRPO
(
Kuba et al. 2022
)
reweights the shared trajectory
advantage by the cumulative prefix

\prod_{j<k}\pi_{j}^{(j)}/\mu_{j}
, whose variance grows exponentially
in the number of agents already updated.

Replay-based counterfactual (C3).

C3
(
Chen et al. 2026
)
replays the downstream pipeline at
alternative focal actions under actual environment dynamics,
paying one reward evaluation per replay. It assumes nothing about
the reward structure but cannot scale when reward evaluation is
expensive.

The three CAPO moves answer these in turn: an additive reward
decomposition that makes per-agent attribution closed-form
(Section
4.2
), an upstream-cancellation identity
that simplifies the SeqAU target to two cheap pieces
(Section
4.3
), and fictitious sampling that
estimates the indirect effect under the current joint policy

\pi^{(k-1)}
without importance weights or replay
(Section
4.4
).

4.2
Additive reward model for closed-form per-agent attribution

The first failure mode (centralized-critic blowup) suggests trading
generality for tractability. Following a long line of work on
additive or factored team-reward decompositions in cooperative
multi-agent RL
(
Guestrin et al. 2002
;
Kok and Vlassis 2006
;
Sunehag et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
, we model the expected team reward as

f(a)\;=\;\mathbb{E}[R\mid a]\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\varphi_{k}(a_{k}).

(4)

This structure allows per-agent attribution to be recovered in
closed form from a single linear regression rather than a learned
critic. (Section
5
bounds the advantage bias incurred when
the assumption is violated.) We estimate the per-agent components
by regressing the team reward onto agent-action indicator features.
Each joint action is encoded as
\psi(a)\in\{0,1\}^{d}
with

d=KA
, the concatenation of per-agent one-hot vectors. Stacking
the
N
rollouts of a batch into a feature matrix

\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
with reward vector
r\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the
ridge-regression estimator

\hat{\varphi}\;=\;\bigl(\lambda I+\Psi^{\top}\Psi\bigr)^{-1}\Psi^{\top}r

(5)

returns per-agent components whose concatenation

\hat{\varphi}=(\hat{\varphi}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\varphi}_{K})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

identifies the best additive approximation to
f
under
\mu
. The
solve is
O(d^{3})
, requires no gradient steps, and reuses the same

N
rollouts the policy-gradient update would consume anyway. (At

\lambda=0
, this reduces to the slate
pseudoinverse
(
Swaminathan et al. 2017
)
, which targets the same
additive linear structure for slate off-policy evaluation; we add
the ridge regularizer to supply coverage where some per-agent
actions are rare under
\mu
.) Per-coordinate variance of

\hat{\varphi}
is bounded independently of
K

(Theorem
3
), which is what makes per-agent attribution
cheap for arbitrarily large teams.

4.3
Upstream cancellation: focal advantage reduces to direct + indirect

Plugging the additive approximation
\hat{f}(a)=\sum_{k}\hat{\varphi}_{k}(a_{k})

into the SeqAU advantage (
3
) produces a sum over all

K
agents in both conditional expectations

\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}[\hat{f}\mid a_{\leq k}]
and

\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}[\hat{f}\mid a_{<k}]
. Under the autoregressive
factorization of
\pi^{(k-1)}
, contributions from agents

upstream
of
k
depend only on the prefix
a_{<k}
and appear
identically in both terms; they cancel exactly in the difference and
need not be estimated. What remains is a
clean two-piece advantage:

\hat{A}_{k}^{\mathrm{LS}}(a_{\leq k})\;=\;\underbrace{\hat{\varphi}_{k}(a_{k})-\mathbb{E}_{a_{k}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k})}[\hat{\varphi}_{k}(a_{k}^{\prime})]}_{D_{k}:\text{direct effect (closed form)}}\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{j>k}\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{>k}\mid a_{\leq k}}[\hat{\varphi}_{j}]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{>k}\mid a_{<k}}[\hat{\varphi}_{j}]\bigr)}_{I_{k}:\text{indirect effect}}.

(6)

Direct effect.

D_{k}
depends only on
\hat{\varphi}_{k}
and the current marginal

\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k})
and is closed-form. In the
factored-policy limit where
I_{k}
vanishes,
D_{k}
alone is the
per-agent advantage that DOP
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
obtains in the
simultaneous-execution setting via critic-side cancellation,
attained here reward-side.

Indirect effect.

I_{k}
captures how agent
k
’s action shifts the conditional
distribution over downstream agents’ actions, and through them the
reward contributions
\hat{\varphi}_{j}
for
j>k
. This term is the
new content of the sequential setting; estimating it is the subject
of Section
4.4
. The full algebraic derivation
of (
6
) is in
Appendix
B
.

4.4
Fictitious sampling for the indirect effect

The indirect-effect expectations in (
6
)
require conditional distributions over downstream actions under

\pi^{(k-1)}
, which the
\mu
-rollouts cannot supply. CAPO
addresses this by drawing fictitious continuations from

\pi^{(k-1)}
conditioned on each rollout’s
\mu
-realized prefix,
rather than reweighting the
\mu
-rollouts (the HA-GRPO approach,
with exponential IS-variance cost) or re-executing the downstream
pipeline in the real environment (the C3 approach, with one reward
evaluation per replay). For each rollout
n
, we generate two
sample sets:

\tilde{a}_{>k}^{(n,\ell)}\;\sim\;\pi_{>k}^{(k-1)}\!\bigl(\cdot\,\big|\,a_{\leq k}^{(n)}\bigr),\qquad\ell=1,\ldots,L,

(7)

\bigl(\tilde{a}_{k}^{(n,\ell)},\tilde{a}_{>k}^{\prime(n,\ell)}\bigr)\;\sim\;\pi_{k}^{(k-1)}\!\bigl(\cdot\,\big|\,a_{<k}^{(n)}\bigr)\cdot\pi_{>k}^{(k-1)}\!\bigl(\cdot\,\big|\,a_{<k}^{(n)},\tilde{a}_{k}^{(n,\ell)}\bigr),\qquad\ell=1,\ldots,L,

(8)

and form the per-rollout Monte Carlo estimate of the SeqAU
advantage:

\hat{A}_{k}^{(n)}\;=\;\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\Bigl[\hat{f}\bigl(a_{\leq k}^{(n)},\tilde{a}_{>k}^{(n,\ell)}\bigr)\;-\;\hat{f}\bigl(a_{<k}^{(n)},\tilde{a}_{k}^{(n,\ell)},\tilde{a}_{>k}^{\prime(n,\ell)}\bigr)\Bigr].

(9)

The first term holds the prefix-plus-focal-action at its realized
value and averages over downstream completions drawn from

\pi_{>k}^{(k-1)}(\cdot\mid a_{\leq k}^{(n)})
; the second
integrates the focal action out under

\pi_{k}^{(k-1)}(\cdot\mid a_{<k}^{(n)})
and then draws downstream
completions from

\pi_{>k}^{(k-1)}(\cdot\mid a_{<k}^{(n)},\tilde{a}_{k}^{(n,\ell)})
.
Their difference is exactly
D_{k}+I_{k}

from (
6
), with the direct effect computed
in closed form and the indirect effect estimated by the fictitious
draws. The draws require only policy forward passes, not reward
evaluations or environment interactions; evaluating
\hat{f}
is a
table lookup of dimension
K
.

The fictitious estimator avoids both pathologies. It does not
reweight, so no IS variance accumulates with
k
; the effective
sample size is
L
for every agent, independent of position within
the team. It does not replay, so no reward evaluations are spent
beyond the initial batch. The cost asymmetry against C3 is
structural in regimes where reward evaluation dominates (external
verifiers, code executors, graders, human raters); the cost
asymmetry against HA-GRPO is statistical, with no exponential-in-
k

variance entering the gradient (Theorem
4
).

We note that the prefixes
a_{<k}^{(n)}
and
a_{\leq k}^{(n)}

in (
9
) are those realized under the
rollout-collection policy
\mu
; only the downstream continuation
(and the counterfactual focal action in (
8
))
are drawn from
\pi^{(k-1)}
. The outer average over
n
therefore
integrates the advantage against the
\mu
-prefix distribution
rather than the
\pi_{<k}^{(k-1)}
-prefix distribution. Under
sequential updates this introduces a prefix-drift bias already
captured by Theorem
2
through the per-agent
total-variation drift
\bar{\delta}=\max_{j}\|\pi_{j}-\mu_{j}\|_{1}
; we
accept it in exchange for avoiding a cumulative
importance-sampling prefix.

5
Theoretical Properties

We characterize the statistical properties of the CAPO advantage
estimator (
6
). Let

\varepsilon(a)=\hat{f}(a)-f(a)
denote the residual of the
additive approximation, so that
\|\varepsilon\|_{\infty}
measures
the worst-case non-additivity. Let

G_{\mu}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\psi(a)\psi(a)^{\top}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be the
population Gram matrix of the indicator features with smallest
eigenvalue
\kappa_{\mu}=\lambda_{\min}(G_{\mu})
. We assume

\kappa_{\mu}>0
, that is, every per-agent action has positive
probability under the marginal of
\mu
; the ridge regularizer

\lambda>0
supplies the coverage otherwise. All rewards are
bounded,
|R(a)|\leq R_{\max}
. Proofs are given in
Appendix
C
.

Advantage bias under non-additive rewards.

Theorem 2

(Advantage bias)
.

Let
\Delta_{k}=\hat{A}_{k}^{\mathrm{LS}}-A_{k}^{\mathrm{SeqAU}}

denote the CAPO advantage bias, where
\hat{A}_{k}^{\mathrm{LS}}
is
computed from
\hat{\varphi}
fit on
\mu
-rollouts. For any joint
action
(a_{<k},a_{k})
,

|\Delta_{k}(a_{\leq k})|\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\geq k}\mid a_{<k}}[\delta_{k}^{\varepsilon}(a_{-k})]\;+\;\|\varepsilon\|_{\infty}\cdot\max_{a_{k},a_{k}^{\prime}}\!\bigl\|\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k},a_{k})-\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k},a_{k}^{\prime})\bigr\|_{1},

logging policies.

Bias has two channels. The first vanishes when the additive residual
is insensitive to the focal action; the second vanishes when the
joint policy is factored and downstream agents do not see the focal
action. In a GRPO loop with

7
Related Work

Cooperative MARL credit assignment.

The opacity-learnability framing that motivates SeqAU originates
with
Wolpert and Tumer 2002
. COMA
(
Foerster et al. 2018
)

operationalized the AU counterfactual through a centralized critic
at
O(|\mathcal{A}|^{K})
marginalization cost. Closer in spirit,
Dr.Reinforce
(
Castellini et al. 2025
)
combines policy gradients
with aristocrat-utility difference rewards computed from the
environment reward, but targets simultaneous-execution Dec-POMDPs
and has no analog for the indirect effect that arises only under
sequential execution. In the factored-policy limit, CAPO’s direct
effect reduces to Dr.Reinforce’s aristocrat utility under additive
rewards. Additive
or factored team-reward decomposition in cooperative multi-agent
RL dates back to
Guestrin et al. 2002

and
Kok and Vlassis 2006
; the decomposed-critic family
moves this principle from the reward to the value function, with
DOP
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
assuming an additively factored
Q
and
VDN
(
Sunehag et al. 2018
)
, QMIX
(
Rashid et al. 2018
)
,
FACMAC
(
Peng et al. 2021
)
, and DAE
(
Li et al. 2022
)
using
related value-side factorizations. All assume
simultaneous

decentralized execution in Dec-POMDPs, with no mechanism for one
agent’s action to enter another agent’s input, so the indirect
effect that characterizes the sequential setting is identically zero
by construction. CAPO inherits this additive-reward decomposition principle but
applies it to the reward rather than the value function,
identifying per-agent components by closed-form regression rather
than by gradient-based critic learning.

Sequential cooperative credit assignment.

The sequential prior work is comparatively sparse.
HAPPO
(
Kuba et al. 2022
)
performs sequential per-agent updates
with a cumulative importance-sampling prefix, paying the
exponential-in-
k
variance cost analyzed in
Section
5
; HA-GRPO is its critic-free GRPO variant.
SeCA
(
Zang et al. 2023
)
imposes a virtual agent ordering and
learns a counterfactual centralized
Q
-critic conditional on the
prefix of preceding agents’ actions, but the ordering is virtual
(execution remains simultaneous and downstream agents do not
actually condition on upstream actions), and the centralized
Q

enumerates discrete actions, intractable for LLM token sequences.
Seq-MADAC
(
Lu et al. 2025
)
uses a HAPPO-style sequential
decomposition for hyperparameter configuration without a
counterfactual baseline. Most relevant conceptually is

Triantafyllou et al. 2025
, who formalize the decomposition of
the total counterfactual effect of an agent’s action in sequential
multi-agent MDPs into an agent-specific effect (propagating through
subsequent agents’ actions, analogous to CAPO’s indirect effect)
and a state-specific effect (propagating through state transitions).
Their estimator is a Shapley-value post-hoc attribution; CAPO turns
the same direct-versus-indirect decomposition into a tractable
policy-gradient estimator under the additive-reward assumption. Our work uses learnability maximization baseline which is in contrast with the optimal variance-reduction baseline
(
Kuba et al. 2021
)
for multi-agent settings.

Critic-free policy gradients for multi-agent LLMs.

A recent line adapts GRPO
(
DeepSeek-AI 2025
)
’s critic-free
within-group baseline to multi-agent LLM pipelines, with two
families. Shared-baseline methods (MA-GRPO
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
,
AT-GRPO
(
Zhao et al. 2025
)
, M-GRPO
(
Hong et al. 2025
)
) assign every
agent the same trajectory or hierarchy-level advantage; HA-GRPO
adds HAPPO’s cumulative importance-sampling prefix to correct
sequential-update drift. Counterfactual methods estimate per-agent
credit by intervening on upstream actions and observing downstream
outputs. The two closest concurrent comparisons are
C3
(
Chen et al. 2026
)
, which replays the downstream pipeline at
alternative focal actions and reads actual environment rewards via
a leave-one-out baseline (capturing the fixed-prefix counterfactual,
but freezing downstream continuations to the rollout-collection
distribution rather than the current joint policy, and paying one
full reward evaluation per replay), and CCPO
(
Li et al. 2026b
)
,
which estimates counterfactual credit via topology-specific
shortcuts (for example, “removing” the reasoner in a
reasoner–solver pipeline by resampling the solver from the empty
prompt); these shortcuts are tightly tied to the pipeline structure
and do not extend to general sequential chains. Shapley-style
alternatives such as SHARP
(
Li et al. 2026a
)
and Lazy
Agents
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
use multi-step causal attribution at
correspondingly higher per-step cost; single-agent hindsight-credit
work
(
Tan et al. 2026
)
is in the same wave but a different setting.
CAPO’s distinguishing combination against this line is critic-free,
reward-side (closed-form ridge regression rather than learned
Q

or environment replay), uses current-policy fictitious continuations
(rather than frozen-prefix replay), and is generic across sequential
chains (no topology assumption beyond fixed execution order).

8
Conclusion

We have presented CAPO, a critic-free per-agent policy gradient for
cooperative multi-agent teams under sequential execution. On the
theoretical side, we extended the opacity-learnability framework
of
Wolpert and Tumer 2002
to the sequential setting and identified
the unique learnability-maximizing prefix-conditional baseline,
which subtracted from the team reward gives the Sequential
Aristocrat Utility (SeqAU). On the
algorithmic side, CAPO estimates the SeqAU advantage in closed form
through an additive reward decomposition, an upstream-cancellation
identity, and fictitious sampling for the indirect effect. The
resulting estimator admits analytic bias and variance bounds, which
we validate on a controlled sequential-bandit testbed.

Several directions remain open. The additivity assumption is exact
when rewards separate across agents and gracefully lossy otherwise;
heavily interaction-dependent rewards are a regime where a richer
estimator may be preferable. Extension beyond one-step bandits to
full sequential MDPs, and end-to-end evaluation on multi-LLM
pipelines, are natural follow-ups.

A.1
Factoredness

Definition 5

(Factoredness)
.

A per-agent utility
g_{k}
is
factored with respect to the team
reward
R
if, for all
a_{k}^{1},a_{k}^{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
and all
prefixes
a_{<k}
,

\mathrm{sgn}\!\bigl[\bar{g}_{k}(a_{k}^{1};a_{<k})-\bar{g}_{k}(a_{k}^{2};a_{<k})\bigr]\;=\;\mathrm{sgn}\!\bigl[\bar{R}_{k}(a_{k}^{1};a_{<k})-\bar{R}_{k}(a_{k}^{2};a_{<k})\bigr],

where

\bar{U}_{k}(a_{k};a_{<k})=\mathbb{E}_{a_{>k}\sim\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{\leq k})}[U(a)]

denotes the suffix-averaged form of any utility
U
.

Factoredness aligns per-agent utility with team reward: every move
that improves the suffix-averaged
g_{k}
at agent
k
also improves
the suffix-averaged team reward, and conversely. A utility satisfying
factoredness is therefore a valid target for agent
k
to optimize in
place of
R
.

A.2
Sequential difference utilities are factored

Proposition 6
.

For any function
D:\prod_{j<k}\mathcal{A}_{j}\to\mathbb{R}
, the utility

g_{k}(a)=R(a)-D(a_{<k})
satisfies the factoredness condition of
Definition
5
.

Proof.

The suffix-averaged form is

\bar{g}_{k}(a_{k};a_{<k})=\bar{R}_{k}(a_{k};a_{<k})-D(a_{<k})
. Since

D(a_{<k})
does not depend on
a_{k}
, it cancels in the difference

\bar{g}_{k}(a_{k}^{1};a_{<k})-\bar{g}_{k}(a_{k}^{2};a_{<k})=\bar{R}_{k}(a_{k}^{1};a_{<k})-\bar{R}_{k}(a_{k}^{2};a_{<k})
,
and the signs coincide. ∎∎

Proposition
6
justifies restricting the SeqAU
search to sequential difference utilities: every such utility is a
valid optimization target, so the question Theorem
1

answers is which baseline
D
within this class maximizes
learnability.

A.3
Proof of Theorem
1

We substitute the utility

g_{k}(a)=R(a)-D(a_{<k})
into the sequential learnability
expression (
1
) and minimize the denominator
while showing the numerator is independent of
D
.

Numerator.

For any
a_{k}
,

\bar{g}_{k}(a_{k};a_{<k})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{a_{>k}\sim\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{\leq k})}\!\bigl[R(a)-D(a_{<k})\bigr]\;=\;\bar{R}_{k}(a_{k};a_{<k})-D(a_{<k}).

Since
D(a_{<k})
does not depend on
a_{k}
, it cancels in the
difference

\bar{g}_{k}(a_{k}^{1};a_{<k})-\bar{g}_{k}(a_{k}^{2};a_{<k})=\bar{R}_{k}(a_{k}^{1};a_{<k})-\bar{R}_{k}(a_{k}^{2};a_{<k})
,
and the numerator of (
1
) is independent of

.

Denominator.

By the law of total variance, conditioning on
a_{<k}
,

\mathrm{Var}_{(a_{<k},a_{>k})\mid a_{k}}[g_{k}]\;=\;\mathrm{Var}_{a_{<k}}\!\bigl[\bar{g}_{k}(a_{k};a_{<k})\bigr]\;+\;\mathbb{E}_{a_{<k}}\!\bigl[\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{>k}\mid a_{\leq k}}[g_{k}]\bigr].

The inner variance in the second term is taken over
a_{>k}
for
fixed
a_{\leq k}
. Since
D(a_{<k})
is constant under this
conditioning, the second term equals

\mathbb{E}_{a_{<k}}[\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{>k}\mid a_{\leq k}}[R]]
and does not depend
on
D
. Maximizing learnability therefore reduces to minimizing the
first term,

\mathrm{Var}_{a_{<k}}[\bar{R}_{k}(a_{k};a_{<k})-D(a_{<k})]
,
which is invariant to adding an
a_{k}
-independent constant to
D
.

Taking the joint expectation
\mathbb{E}_{a_{<k},\,a_{k}\sim\rho(\cdot\mid a_{<k})}

in the denominator of the learnability expression and applying

\mathrm{Var}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}[Y])^{2}
, the minimization reduces to the
quadratic loss

\min_{D}\;\mathbb{E}_{a_{<k}}\mathbb{E}_{a_{k}\sim\rho(\cdot\mid a_{<k})}\!\bigl[(\bar{R}_{k}(a_{k};a_{<k})-D(a_{<k}))^{2}\bigr].

Pointwise in
a_{<k}
, the minimizer of this loss is the conditional
expectation of
\bar{R}_{k}(a_{k};a_{<k})
under
\rho(\cdot\mid a_{<k})
,

D^{*}(a_{<k})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{a_{k}\sim\rho(\cdot\mid a_{<k})}\!\bigl[\bar{R}_{k}(a_{k};a_{<k})\bigr]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{a_{k}^{\prime}\sim\rho(\cdot\mid a_{<k}),\;a_{>k}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k},a_{k}^{\prime})}\!\bigl[R(a_{k}^{\prime},a_{<k},a_{>k}^{\prime})\bigr],

which is independent of the choice of
(a_{k}^{1},a_{k}^{2})
and therefore
maximizes
\Lambda_{k}^{\mathrm{seq}}
at every prefix and every move
pair simultaneously. This completes the proof. ∎

Appendix B
Derivation of the upstream-cancellation identity

We derive the direct/indirect decomposition of the CAPO advantage
stated in Eq. (
6
). Substitute the additive
model
\hat{f}(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{\varphi}_{j}(a_{j})
into the
SeqAU counterfactual advantage

A_{k}^{\mathrm{LS}}(a_{\leq k})=Q_{k}^{\mathrm{LS}}(a_{\leq k})-V_{k}^{\mathrm{LS}}(a_{<k})
of (
3
). Expanding
Q_{k}
under
additivity and splitting the sum over
j
into upstream (
j<k
),
focal (
j=k
), and downstream (
j>k
):

\displaystyle Q_{k}^{\mathrm{LS}}(a_{\leq k})

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}\!\Bigl[\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{\varphi}_{j}(a_{j})\;\Big|\;a_{\leq k}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\;=\;\underbrace{\sum_{j<k}\hat{\varphi}_{j}(a_{j})}_{\text{upstream}}\;+\;\hat{\varphi}_{k}(a_{k})\;+\;\sum_{j>k}\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}\!\bigl[\hat{\varphi}_{j}(a_{j})\mid a_{\leq k}\bigr],

(10)

where the upstream terms exit the expectation because each

\hat{\varphi}_{j}
with
j<k
is a function of
a_{j}
alone (under
additivity) and therefore a deterministic function of the prefix

a_{<k}
that
Q_{k}
already conditions on. Similarly expanding

V_{k}
:

\displaystyle V_{k}^{\mathrm{LS}}(a_{<k})

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}\!\Bigl[\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{\varphi}_{j}(a_{j})\;\Big|\;a_{<k}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\;=\;\underbrace{\sum_{j<k}\hat{\varphi}_{j}(a_{j})}_{\text{same upstream}}\;+\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}\!\bigl[\hat{\varphi}_{k}(a_{k})\mid a_{<k}\bigr]\;+\;\sum_{j>k}\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}\!\bigl[\hat{\varphi}_{j}(a_{j})\mid a_{<k}\bigr].

(11)

The upstream sum
\sum_{j<k}\hat{\varphi}_{j}(a_{j})
is identical in
Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
): both
conditional expectations are taken under the same joint policy

\pi^{(k-1)}
and both condition on the same prefix
a_{<k}
; the
upstream terms depend only on
a_{<k}
and are therefore deterministic
constants under both conditionings. Subtracting (
11
)
from (
10
), the upstream contributions cancel
identically, yielding

A_{k}^{\mathrm{LS}}(a_{\leq k})\;=\;\underbrace{\hat{\varphi}_{k}(a_{k})\;-\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}[\hat{\varphi}_{k}(a_{k})\mid a_{<k}]}_{D_{k}}

\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{j>k}\!\Bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}[\hat{\varphi}_{j}(a_{j})\mid a_{\leq k}]\;-\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}[\hat{\varphi}_{j}(a_{j})\mid a_{<k}]\Bigr)}_{I_{k}}.

The conditional expectation in
D_{k}
is taken over
a_{k}
alone (since

\hat{\varphi}_{k}
depends only on
a_{k}
), and under the autoregressive
factorization of
\pi^{(k-1)}
this is the marginal expectation

\mathbb{E}_{a_{k}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k})}[\hat{\varphi}_{k}(a_{k}^{\prime})]
, giving
the form of Eq. (
6
). ∎

C.1
Proof of Theorem
2
(general bias bound)

Let
\hat{Q}_{k}(a_{\leq k})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}[\hat{f}\mid a_{\leq k}]

and
\hat{V}_{k}(a_{<k})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}[\hat{f}\mid a_{<k}]
, and let

Q_{k},V_{k}
denote the corresponding quantities for the true
conditional mean
f
. By definition,

\hat{A}_{k}^{\mathrm{LS}}=\hat{Q}_{k}-\hat{V}_{k}
and

A_{k}=Q_{k}-V_{k}
, so

\Delta_{k}(a_{\leq k})\;=\;\bigl(\hat{Q}_{k}(a_{\leq k})-Q_{k}(a_{\leq k})\bigr)\;-\;\bigl(\hat{V}_{k}(a_{<k})-V_{k}(a_{<k})\bigr).

Using linearity of expectation and
\hat{f}-f=\varepsilon
,

\hat{Q}_{k}(a_{\leq k})-Q_{k}(a_{\leq k})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{>k}\mid a_{\leq k}}\!\bigl[\varepsilon(a)\bigr],\qquad\hat{V}_{k}(a_{<k})-V_{k}(a_{<k})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\geq k}\mid a_{<k}}\!\bigl[\varepsilon(a)\bigr].

Expanding the
V_{k}
-residual by conditioning on
a_{k}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\geq k}\mid a_{<k}}[\varepsilon]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{a_{k}\sim\pi_{k}}\!\bigl[\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{>k}\mid a_{\leq k}}[\varepsilon]\bigr],

so

\Delta_{k}(a_{\leq k})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{>k}\mid a_{\leq k}}[\varepsilon]\;-\;\mathbb{E}_{a_{k}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{k},\,a_{>k}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k},a_{k}^{\prime})}[\varepsilon].

Write this as a difference of conditional expectations of a single
random quantity,
\varepsilon(a)
, under two different distributions
over
(a_{k},a_{>k})
: one fixes
a_{k}
at its observed value and
averages
a_{>k}
under
\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{\leq k})
; the other
averages
a_{k}
under
\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k})
and, conditional on

a_{k}
,
a_{>k}
under
\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k},a_{k})
. Decomposing
by the tower property,

\Delta_{k}(a_{\leq k})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{a_{k}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{k}}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{>k}\mid a_{\leq k}}[\varepsilon(a_{<k},a_{k},a_{>k})]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{>k}\mid a_{<k},a_{k}^{\prime}}[\varepsilon(a_{<k},a_{k}^{\prime},a_{>k}^{\prime})]\right].

Adding and subtracting

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{>k}\mid a_{<k},a_{k}^{\prime}}[\varepsilon(a_{<k},a_{k},a_{>k})]

inside the bracket splits the integrand into two channels. The first
channel,

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{>k}\mid a_{\leq k}}[\varepsilon(a_{<k},a_{k},a_{>k})]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{>k}\mid a_{<k},a_{k}^{\prime}}[\varepsilon(a_{<k},a_{k},a_{>k})],

captures how the downstream-conditioning distribution changes with
the focal action; it is bounded by

\|\varepsilon\|_{\infty}\cdot\|\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k},a_{k})-\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k},a_{k}^{\prime})\|_{1}
. The second channel,

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{>k}\mid a_{<k},a_{k}^{\prime}}[\varepsilon(a_{<k},a_{k},a_{>k})-\varepsilon(a_{<k},a_{k}^{\prime},a_{>k})],

captures the sensitivity of the residual to the focal action; it is
bounded in absolute value by

\delta_{k}^{\varepsilon}(a_{-k})=\max_{a_{k},a_{k}^{\prime}}|\varepsilon(a_{<k},a_{k},a_{>k})-\varepsilon(a_{<k},a_{k}^{\prime},a_{>k})|
.
Taking absolute values and the
\pi_{k}
expectation,

|\Delta_{k}(a_{\leq k})|\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\geq k}\mid a_{<k}}[\delta_{k}^{\varepsilon}(a_{-k})]\;+\;\|\varepsilon\|_{\infty}\cdot\max_{a_{k},a_{k}^{\prime}}\|\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k},a_{k})-\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k},a_{k}^{\prime})\|_{1},

which is the statement of Theorem
2
. ∎

C.2
Specialization under factored policies

Under a factored joint policy
\pi(a)=\prod_{k}\pi_{k}(a_{k})
, the
downstream conditional distribution is independent of the focal
action:
\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k},a_{k})=\pi_{>k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k})=\prod_{j>k}\pi_{j}(\cdot)
. Hence the second channel of the
bound in Theorem
2
vanishes identically. From the first
channel, using the factored structure,

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\geq k}\mid a_{<k}}[\delta_{k}^{\varepsilon}(a_{-k})]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{a_{j}\sim\pi_{j},\,j\neq k}\!\bigl[\delta_{k}^{\varepsilon}(a_{-k})\bigr].

The residual
\varepsilon(a)=\hat{f}(a)-f(a)
is
computed from
\hat{\varphi}
fit on
\mu
-rollouts, and the advantage
we want is under
\pi
-rollouts. When
\mu_{j}\neq\pi_{j}
, the
ridge-fit
\hat{f}
differs between the two measures only through the
Gram matrix; under factored rollouts the mismatch propagates as

\bigl|\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{-k}}[\varepsilon(a_{<k},a_{k},a_{>k})]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{-k}}[\varepsilon(a_{<k},a_{k},a_{>k})]\bigr|\;\leq\;\|\varepsilon\|_{\infty}\cdot\sum_{j\neq k}\|\pi_{j}-\mu_{j}\|_{1}.

This is the standard bound for changing measures under a bounded
integrand. Substituting and applying the triangle inequality,

|\Delta_{k}(a_{k})|\;\leq\;2\|\varepsilon\|_{\infty}\cdot\sum_{j\neq k}\|\pi_{j}-\mu_{j}\|_{1}\;\leq\;2(K-1)\bar{\delta}\|\varepsilon\|_{\infty},

using
\bar{\delta}=\max_{j}\|\pi_{j}-\mu_{j}\|_{1}
, which is the
specialization claimed in the second half of Theorem
2
.
∎

C.3
Proof of Theorem
3
(advantage variance)

Under the additive reward
f(a)=\sum_{k}\varphi_{k}^{*}(a_{k})
, the
ridge-regression solution can be written as

\hat{\varphi}\;=\;(\lambda I+\Psi^{\top}\Psi)^{-1}\Psi^{\top}r.

We work at the population level, replacing the sample Gram matrix

\Psi^{\top}\Psi
by
NG_{\mu}
; the fluctuations of
\Psi^{\top}\Psi

around
NG_{\mu}
contribute an
O(1/\sqrt{N})
correction that does
not affect the
K
-scaling statement.

At the population level, write
r=\Psi\varphi^{*}+\eta
where

\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
has i.i.d. entries with

\mathbb{E}[\eta_{i}\mid\Psi]=0
and
\mathrm{Var}(\eta_{i})\leq R_{\max}^{2}
(the
additive assumption makes
\Psi\varphi^{*}
the exact conditional
mean). Then

\hat{\varphi}-\varphi^{*}\;=\;(\lambda I+NG_{\mu})^{-1}\bigl(\Psi^{\top}\eta-\lambda\varphi^{*}\bigr),

and the ridge bias term vanishes in the full-coverage regime relative
to the variance. For the variance contribution,

\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\varphi})\;\preceq\;(\lambda I+NG_{\mu})^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}[\Psi^{\top}\eta\eta^{\top}\Psi]\,(\lambda I+NG_{\mu})^{-1}\;\preceq\;\frac{R_{\max}^{2}}{\lambda+N\kappa_{\mu}}\,I,

where we used
\mathbb{E}[\Psi^{\top}\eta\eta^{\top}\Psi]\preceq R_{\max}^{2}\cdot\Psi^{\top}\Psi
(by the variance bound on
\eta
) and

(\lambda I+NG_{\mu})^{-1}\preceq(\lambda+N\kappa_{\mu})^{-1}I
.

The direct-effect component

D_{k}(a_{\leq k})=\hat{\varphi}_{k}(a_{k})-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid a_{<k})}[\hat{\varphi}_{k}]
is a linear functional

w_{D}^{\top}\hat{\varphi}
whose coefficient vector
w_{D}
has one
indicator entry of weight
1
for
\hat{\varphi}_{k}(a_{k})
and a
probability vector of
\ell_{2}
norm at most
1
on the marginal
expectation, so
\|w_{D}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2
. Hence

\mathrm{Var}\!\bigl(D_{k}(a_{\leq k})\bigr)\;=\;w_{D}^{\top}\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\varphi})w_{D}\;\leq\;\|w_{D}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\frac{R_{\max}^{2}}{\lambda+N\kappa_{\mu}}\;\leq\;\frac{2R_{\max}^{2}}{\lambda+N\kappa_{\mu}},

independent of
K
. For the full advantage

\hat{A}_{k}^{\mathrm{LS}}=D_{k}+I_{k}
under exact indirect-effect
evaluation, the indirect effect adds further coordinates to the
linear functional. For each
j>k
, the coefficient on

\hat{\varphi}_{j}(a_{j})
is

P_{\pi_{>k}}(a_{j}\mid a_{\leq k})-P_{\pi_{>k}}(a_{j}\mid a_{<k})
,
whose squared sum over
a_{j}
is bounded by

\|P(\cdot\mid a_{\leq k})-P(\cdot\mid a_{<k})\|_{1}\cdot\|P(\cdot\mid a_{\leq k})-P(\cdot\mid a_{<k})\|_{\infty}\leq 2
.
Summing over
j>k
contributes at most
2(K-k)
to
\|w\|_{2}^{2}
,
giving the worst-case bound

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{A}_{k}^{\mathrm{LS}})\leq(2+2(K-k))R_{\max}^{2}/(\lambda+N\kappa_{\mu})
. In the factored-policy limit, the
indirect-effect coefficients all vanish and the
K
-independent
direct-effect bound applies to the full advantage. ∎

C.4
Proof of Theorem
4
(gradient MSE)

Let
g_{k}^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}[A_{k}\,s_{k}]
be the true gradient and

\hat{g}_{k}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell}\hat{A}_{k}^{\mathrm{LS},(\ell)}s_{k}^{(\ell)}

the CAPO estimator. Decompose

\hat{g}_{k}-g_{k}^{*}\;=\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell}(A_{k}s_{k})^{(\ell)}-g_{k}^{*}}_{E_{\text{var}}}\;+\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell}\Delta_{k}^{(\ell)}s_{k}^{(\ell)}}_{E_{\text{bias}}}.

The first term is the standard REINFORCE estimation error of a
bounded random variable and has

\mathbb{E}\|E_{\text{var}}\|^{2}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{L}\mathrm{Var}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}(A_{k}s_{k}).

The second term is a sum of
L
i.i.d. bounded quantities; its
second moment is

\mathbb{E}\|E_{\text{bias}}\|^{2}\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}[\Delta_{k}^{2}\|s_{k}\|^{2}]\;\leq\;B_{k}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}[\|s_{k}\|^{2}],

using
|\Delta_{k}|\leq B_{k}
. Applying
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}

to
\hat{g}_{k}-g_{k}^{*}=E_{\text{var}}+E_{\text{bias}}
,

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{g}_{k}-g_{k}^{*}\|^{2}\;\leq\;\frac{2}{L}\mathrm{Var}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}(A_{k}s_{k})\;+\;2B_{k}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(k-1)}}[\|s_{k}\|^{2}],

which is the bound of Theorem
4
. Crucially, the
fictitious rollouts are drawn from
\pi^{(k-1)}
directly rather than
reweighted from
\mu
, so no cumulative importance-sampling prefix
enters either term. ∎

Appendix E
Non-stationarity parametrization in the optimization experiment

The non-stationarity parameter
\rho
used in
Section
6.3
controls how strongly each agent’s
conditional policy depends on its upstream action, which in turn
determines the severity of the distribution shift over downstream
behavior when an upstream agent’s policy is updated. Concretely, the
initial joint policy is drawn as follows. For each agent
k\geq 1

and each upstream-action value
c\in\mathcal{A}_{k-1}
, we first
sample a base conditional distribution

\pi^{\mathrm{base}}_{k}(\cdot\mid c)\sim\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\mathbf{1})

over
\mathcal{A}_{k}
, and then concentrate it toward the diagonal by
the exponential tilt

\pi_{k}(a\mid c)\;\propto\;\pi^{\mathrm{base}}_{k}(a\mid c)\cdot\exp\bigl(\rho\cdot\mathbf{1}[a=c]\bigr).

(12)

At
\rho=0
the tilt is trivial and

\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid c)=\pi^{\mathrm{base}}_{k}(\cdot\mid c)
, which is
approximately uniform. As
\rho
grows, mass concentrates on the
action
a=c
, so agent
k
’s behavior becomes increasingly dictated
by agent
k-1
’s action. This does
not
change what optima the
team reward admits—
\varphi_{k}
and
g_{k\ell}
are drawn
independently of
\rho
—but it determines how much the distribution
over downstream behavior shifts when an upstream agent’s policy is
updated during training. Agent 0, which has no upstream conditioning
variable, is drawn uniformly from the simplex regardless of
\rho
.
Across the three values
\rho\in\{0,1,2\}
that we sweep in
Section
6.3
, and the four values

\rho\in\{0,5,10,20\}
swept in the direct-effect ablation of
Section
6.4
, the diagonal mass of the initial
conditional policies increases from near
1/|\mathcal{A}|

(near-uniform) to near
1
(near-deterministic), spanning the range
from mild to severe sequential-update non-stationarity.
