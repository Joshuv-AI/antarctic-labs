Glial heterogeneity shapes region-specific angiogenesis and neurovascular integrity in the CNS
Authors: Minghe Shen, Zhidan Li, Guojiao Huang, Yue Zhang, Siyue Wen, Fengru Guo, Yingkun Guo, Liguo Zhang, Xuelian He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proper function of the central nervous system (CNS) requires adequate blood supply to both gray and white matter, yet the mechanisms governing the region-specific angiogenesis remain poorly understood. Through analyzing glial cell profiles combined with genetic ablation approaches, we uncover distinct pro-angiogenic programs in astrocytic and oligodendroglial lineages that differentially regulate gray and white matter vascularization. Astrocyte depletion selectively disrupted gray matter angiogenesis, whereas oligodendrocyte lineage loss caused white matter hypovascularization. Gray matter astrocytes are selectively enriched for the hypoxia-inducible factor HIF1α and promote vascular growth via VEGF-dependent signaling, while the oligodendrocyte-enriched chromatin-remodeler CHD8 directly activated pro-angiogenic genes, including Tgfa and Sema5a, to promote white matter vascularization. Conditional Chd8 deletion recapitulated white matter vascular defects and visual dysfunction without neuronal loss. These findings define lineage-specific mechanisms of CNS angiogenesis and establish astrocytes and oligodendrocytes as distinct drivers of region-specific vascular development, providing a conceptual framework for targeted therapies in neurovascular and neurodevelopmental disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Proper brain function depends on the precise coordination between neural and vascular networks to ensure adequate oxygen and metabolic supply, as reflected by regional differences in capillary density and flow dynamics (1). For example, capillary density is higher in gray matter (GM) than in white matter (WM) (2, 3). While recent studies indicate regional dependence in cerebral angiogenesis (4), the mechanisms underlying these differences remain incompletely understood. Emerging evidence further indicates that regional heterogeneity in the physiological properties of glial cells parallels the topographic variation observed in vascular supply and capillary density across brain regions (5–7). However, whether glial cells actively orchestrate the distinct vessel density and branching patterns observed across central nervous system (CNS) regions (e.g., GM versus WM), and whether angiogenesis is governed by a universal or region-specific program, remain unresolved.

Recent studies suggest that glial cells, particularly astrocytes and oligodendrocyte lineage cells, are central regulators of CNS angiogenesis (8–10). Astrocytes secrete pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF and WNT7A, while oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) express paracrine cues that influence vessel growth (8, 11–13). Yet, prior work has viewed glial regulation of angiogenesis as largely uniform, without addressing the cell type-specific and regionally diverse nature of glial populations across the CNS during development. Notably, a recent study reported that astrocytes selectively promote neovascularization in gray matter lesions during tissue repair, but not at white matter injury sites, suggesting region-specific functional heterogeneity between GM and WM astrocytes (14). It remains poorly understood whether GM and WM glia employ distinct molecular programs to direct vascular formation or patterning, and how these programs are coordinated during postnatal development. Given the pronounced regional heterogeneity of glial populations (15–17), we hypothesized that the molecular programs governing angiogenesis are region-specific.

In this study, we addressed these knowledge gaps using single-cell transcriptomic profiling, lineage-specific genetic ablation, and molecular interrogation of transcriptional networks. We found that astrocytic and oligodendroglial lineages exhibit discrete pro-angiogenic signatures that govern region-specific vascularization. Astrocyte depletion selectively impaired GM angiogenesis, whereas loss of oligodendrocyte lineage cells caused profound WM hypovascularization. We showed that GM astrocytes were enriched for the angiogenesis regulator HIF1α (11–13) and drive gray matter vascular growth via VEGF-dependent signaling, while the oligodendrocyte-specific chromatin remodeler CHD8 directly activated pro-angiogenic genes such as Tgfa and Sema5a, controlling WM vascular development.

Our findings reveal a previously unrecognized division of labor among glial lineages in regulating CNS angiogenesis, establishing astrocytes and oligodendrocytes as distinct, region-specific drivers of vascular growth. By uncovering lineage-specific transcriptional programs that couple neuroglial differentiation to vascular patterning, our work provides a mechanistic framework for understanding how neurovascular diversity arises and how its disruption may contribute to white matter ischemia, neurodevelopmental, and neurodegenerative disorders.

RESULTS

Astrocytic lineage and oligodendroglial lineages have distinct pro-angiogenic molecular signatures

The cerebral microvasculature undergoes rapid and widespread expansion in the first postnatal month (18, 19).To systematically evaluate the degree of changes in the postnatal microvasculature in the control cortex and corpus callosum, we mainly focused our analysis on the motor cortex and corpus callosum over the developmental windows ranging from P3 to P40 (fig. S1, A to C). Co-immunostaining with the endothelial cell (EC) marker CD31 and proliferation marker Ki67 in control mice revealing distinct spatiotemporal patterns of endothelial cell proliferation (fig. S1D). Cortical EC proliferation peaked sharply at P10 before declining to near-zero by P30, whereas corpus callosum proliferation peaked earlier (∼P7). This proliferative activity temporally correlated with region-specific increases in vessel density: cortical branching rose markedly from P10 to P21 and stabilized between P30 and P40, while an earlier increase occurred in the corpus callosum (P7–P14), plateauing by P21 (fig. S1E). These dynamics coincide with enhanced gliogenesis during the first postnatal month (20–22), suggesting a potential functional interplay between glial lineage expansion and sustained endothelial proliferation and vascular branching.

Many of the growth factors known to influence cerebral angiogenesis are also expressed by glial cells and influence brain development and neural function (9, 10). To investigate whether angiogenesis-associated genes are differentially expressed in glial cells during early postnatal development, we reanalyzed a published single-cell atlas from the neocortex of postnatal day (P) 5–P6 hGFAP-GFP mice, which express GFP under the control of the human glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) promoter (23). Cell clustering was performed based on previously annotated and characterized cell populations (Fig. 1A and fig. S2). By systematically mapping the expression of mRNAs encoding canonical angiogenic factors and core signaling components across glial populations, we found that Vegfb is expressed by all these cells, consistent with previous reports. Astrocytes predominantly express mRNAs encoding VEGFα, ANGPT1, WNT7a, and FGF1, whereas OPCs express mRNAs encoding PDGFα, Dll4, TGFα, and semaphorins (Fig. 1, B and C), reflecting cell type-specific angiogenic regulation.

Decreased astrocyte density is associated with GM hypovascularization

The differential expression of angiogenesis-associated genes between astrocyte and oligodendrocyte lineages prompted us to investigate whether these lineages also differ in their ability to support vascular development. Radial glial are known to orchestrate neurogenesis, control angiogenesis, and stabilize the nascent brain vascular network (24, 25). We generated a mouse line expressing diphtheria toxin fragment A (DTA) under the control of the human glial fibrillary acidic protein (hGFAP) promoter to deplete brains of radial glia. In these hGFAP-Cre:DTA mice, we observed neonatal cerebral hemorrhage and widespread cortical thinning in all mutants (fig. S3, A and B). CD31 staining revealed dramatic defects in the cortical vascular network (fig. S3B). Moreover, BLBP+ radial glial, GS+ astrocytes, and PDGFRα+ OPCs were lost throughout the brain (fig. S3, C and D).

We also developed a model in which DTA expression is inducible in radial glia. In these mice, referred to as GFAP-iDTA mice hereafter, DTA expression is driven by the tamoxifen (TAM) inducible hGFAP promoter. TAM was given at P3 to activate Cre recombinase-driven DTA expression (Fig. 1D). At P11, these mice also exhibited decreased cortical thickness and a pronounced reduction in BLBP+ radial glia, OLIG2+ oligodendrocytes, and glutamine synthetase (GS)-positive astrocytes across both the cortex and corpus callosum, which coincided with a severe decrease in vessel density (Fig. 1E and fig. S3, E and F).

We did not observe similar vascular defects in the corpus callosum when tamoxifen treatment was initiated at P7 (Fig. 1, F and G), despite a significant reduction of CD31+ vessels in the cortex (Fig. 1G). When DTA expression was induced at P7, the density of PDGFRα-H2B-GFP+ oligodendrocytes was also not significantly affected, although GS+ astrocyte numbers were reduced in both the cortex and corpus callosum (Fig. 1H and fig. S4A), indicating that oligodendrocyte fate specification was largely spared following GFAP+ cell ablation from P7 onward. Before P7, GFAP was predominantly expressed in radial glial cells. These cells act as the primary source for generation of astrocytes and OPCs, a process that is largely completed by P7. After P7, GFAP+ cells are mainly astrocytes. Therefore, ablating GFAP+ cells before P7 likely disrupts vascular regulatory mechanisms mediated by radial glia, whereas ablation after P7 primarily affects astrocytes and specifically impairs GM angiogenesis.

In the brain, angiogenesis begins around embryonic day 9.5 and continues until approximately P21 (22). To more accurately assess the role of astrocytes in regulating angiogenesis, we employed another mouse line that inducibly expresses DTA in astrocytes under the control of the Aldh1l1 promoter (referred to as Aldh1l1-iDTA mice). Aldh1l1-CreERT and hGFAP-CreERT show comparable recombination efficiency in astrocytes (fig. S4C). However, ablation of Aldh1l1+ cells prior to P7 resulted in unexpected lethality within 24 hours after TAM administration. We attribute this to the broader expression pattern of Aldh1l1 in essential peripheral tissues outside the CNS (26), whereas GFAP expression is largely restricted to the central nervous system (26, 27). We therefore administered TAM to Aldh1l1-iDTA mice starting at P10 or P14, in addition to GFAP-iDTA mice treated from P14. Both lines display extensive loss of SOX9+ astrocytes in the brain, with vascular defects in the cortex but no obvious effects on vessel development in the corpus callosum (Fig. 1I and fig. S4, B and D to H). When astrocyte depletion was initiated at P21, immunohistochemical analysis revealed markedly diminished perivascular coverage of AQP4+ astrocytes, yet cortical CD31+ vascular density remained comparable to controls (fig. S5A). These findings demonstrate that astrocytes are essential for developmental angiogenesis in GM but not in WM.

To determine the potential mechanisms underlying the vascular phenotype, we examined whether the defects result from reduced EC proliferation or vessel regression. By analyzing the distribution of Ki67+CD31+ cells in the brain of control and GFAP-iDTA mice treated with TAM starting at P7 or P14, we found that the number of Ki67+ cells among CD31+ ECs decreased in the cortex, but not in the corpus callosum of GFAP-iDTA mice (fig. S5, B to D). At P14, when vessel pruning begun (28), we found no quantitative change in the ratio of collagen IV (Col IV) to CD31 in the cortex between control and GFAP-iDTA mice treated with TAM starting at either time (fig. S5, E and F), suggesting the vessel defects following astrocyte depletion are therefore likely due to reduced EC proliferation but not regression of vessels already formed. In addition, astrocyte depletion led to the presence of TER119+ cells outside CD31+ areas and triggered widespread microglia activation (fig. S6, A to D), prompting us to assess whether inflammation contributes to worsened the vessel growth beyond the loss of vessel-stabilizing and angiogenic signals from astrocytes and oligodendrocyte lineage cells, we pharmacologically depleted activated microglia in GFAP-iDTA mice using PLX3397 (29). As shown in fig. S6, E to G, PLX3397 profoundly reduced the CD31+ EC proliferation throughout the brains of GFAP-iDTA mice without affecting vessel regression, indicating that microglial activation did not further exacerbate the impairment of angiogenesis.

Ablation of astrocytes impairs vascular progression in the postnatal retina

Astrocytes appear during a phase in development in which vessel sprouting is still taking place (22). Therefore, ablating GFAP+ cells during certain developmental time windows does not allow precise dissection of the respective contributions of radial glia and astrocytes to angiogenesis and cannot tell us whether astrocytes control angiogenesis in GM. Use of the retina as a model can circumvent these limitations. Unlike the forebrain, in which astrocytes and OPCs both originate from postnatal radial glia, these two lineages in the visual system arise from distinct anatomical regions and separate progenitor pools (30, 31). Retinal astrocytes are generated by the optic disc progenitor zone and scaffold retinal vascular network formation (32) (Fig. 2A and fig. S7A). This distinct origin allows clear differentiation between astrocyte- and OPC-mediated angiogenic effects, avoiding confounding contributions from radial glia across time points. We therefore employed the retina–optic nerve system to assess whether astrocytes specifically regulate GM angiogenesis.

Retinal astrocytes labeled by hGFAP-CreERT-mediated tdTomato expression co-localized with SOX9+ cells, were close to CD31+ vessels, and were exclusively localized to the retinal nerve fiber layer (fig. S7B). This layer is a region that contains only vasculature, astrocytes, and the axons of retinal ganglion cells extending toward the optic nerve head. In hGFAP-GFP mice at P7, we detected hGFAP-GFP+/SOX9+ astrocytes throughout retinal and optic nerve tissues (Fig. 2B and fig. S7C), validating the hGFAP-CreERT mouse model as a suitable tool for investigating astrocyte-mediated angiogenesis in the visual system. When we administered tamoxifen for 5 days to GFAP-iDTA mice starting at P3 or P7, there was a significant reduction in CD31+ and Lectin+ vascular density across all retinal layers compared with controls (Fig. 2, C to G, and fig. S7, D and E). Similarly, tamoxifen-induced astrocyte ablation beginning at P14, when blood vessels reach the retinal periphery the onset of vascular development in the ML layer also occurs, resulted in widespread decreases in CD31+ and Lectin+ vascular density across the ML and DL layers (fig. S7, F and G). In the optic nerve, the numbers of SOX9+ and GFAP+ astrocytes were substantially reduced in GFAP-iDTA mice compared with the control upon tamoxifen treatment (fig. S8), indicating efficient astrocyte depletion. The numbers of CD31+ blood vessels were comparable between controls and astrocyte-depleted mice under both treatment regimens (fig. S8). These results demonstrate that astrocyte depletion impairs retinal vascular development without affecting the optic nerve vasculature.

Ablation of OLIG2+ progenitors results in defective WM angiogenesis

We next evaluated whether the oligodendrocyte lineage is responsible for WM angiogenesis. During early brain development in wild-type mice and in mice carrying the hGFAP-GFP reporter, OLIG2 was transiently expressed in BLBP+ radial glia and hGFAP-GFP+ astrocytic progenitors before P7 (fig. S9, A to D). By contrast, its expression declined in WM astrocytes after P7, becoming largely restricted to PDGFRα+ OPCs, as shown by analysis of mice carrying a PDGFRα-H2B-GFP reporter (Fig. 3A and fig. S9E).

Since both primitive and mature OPCs express OLIG2, we utilized Olig2-TK mice (Fig. 3B). In this mouse line, a suicide gene is knocked in at the Olig2 locus, and ganciclovir (GCV) treatment specifically eliminates actively dividing OLIG2+ cells, allowing selective depletion of OLIG2+ progenitor cells while preserving differentiated oligodendrocytes (33). When GCV was administered to Olig2-TK mice from P1 to P6, we observed dramatic reductions in GS and OLIG2 staining throughout the brain compared to controls (fig. S9, F to H). There were also considerable reductions in Lectin+ vascular density throughout WM and GM as well as cortical thinning in the Olig2-TK mice (fig. S9, F and G). This widespread angiogenesis deficit was similar to the phenotype observed in both GFAP-iDTA mice after tamoxifen-induced ablation at P3 (Fig. 1E and fig. S3, E and F) and in hGFAP-Cre:DTA mice (fig. S3, A and B).

Administration of GCV for 5 days beginning at P7, when PDGFRα-H2B-GFP+/OLIG2+ OPCs are widely distributed across the brain, caused a significant reduction in OLIG2+ cells in both the corpus callosum and cortex of Olig2-TK mice compared to controls by P14 (Fig. 3, A to C). At P14, the CD31+ vessel density was reduced in corpus callosum, but vessel density was not significantly reduced in the cortex (Fig. 3D). Proliferated OLIG2+ cell depletion beginning at P7 does not affect development of SOX9+ and GS+ astrocytes (fig. S10A). The GCV administration for 10 days beginning at P14 induced a global reduction in PDGFRα+ OPCs and led to a dramatic decrease in CD31+ and Lectin+ vessel that remained restricted to the corpus callosum (fig. S10, B to E). We also observed that significant WM-selective vascular reduction phenotypes in both oligodendrocyte lineage expressing Olig2-CreERT:DTA (referred to as Olig2-iDTA) mice and PDGFRα-CreERT:DTA (referred to as Pα-iDTA) mice following tamoxifen-induced cell ablation (Fig. 3, E to G, and fig. S10, F to H). The GCV administration in young adult Olig2-TK mice failed to affect vascular density (fig. S11A). Together, these results indicate that the oligodendrocyte lineage plays an essential role in corpus callosum angiogenesis.

Following OPC removal, we observed a reduction in CD31+ cell proliferation that was restricted to the corpus callosum, with no alteration in vascular regression (fig. S11, B to F). Unlike the effects observed after astrocyte depletion, ablation of OLIG2+ cells did not induce vascular leakage or IBA1+ microglia over-activation (fig. S11G). PLX3397 treatment also exacerbated impaired angiogenesis in both white and gray matter of Olig2-TK mice, without affecting vascular regression (fig. S11, H to J), suggesting that the angiogenic deficit resulting from glial cell loss is independent of microglial activation or disruption of the vascular barrier.

Brain-derived OLIG2+ progenitors promote optic nerve angiogenesis

During development, OPCs originate from the ventricular zone of the third ventricle and migrate into the optic nerve towards the retina (30, 31). We found that the OLIG2+ oligodendrocyte lineage in the optic nerve is not locally derived: Our data indicate that these cells are the descendants of glia progenitors that arise from the third ventricle during late embryogenesis. These cells, which express BLBP and SOX9, migrate via the optic chiasm into the optic nerve at birth (fig. S12, A and B). Notably, even though the optic nerve had abundant GFAP+ astrocyte progenitor cells at P0.5, CD31+ vessels were only sporadically observed within the optic nerve and were surrounded by OLIG2+/SOX9+ cells (Fig. 4A and fig. S12C). At P7, OLIG2+ cells had migrated toward the optic nerve head, and CD31+ vascular density was increased (Fig. 4B and fig. S12D). These observations suggest a potential role of OLIG2+ cells in angiogenesis. Interestingly, OLIG2+ cells in the optic nerve migrate in a vascular-independent fashion (Fig. 4B), differing from the previously reported vascular-guided migration of OPCs in the forebrain (34).

As expected, administration of GCV to Olig2-TK mice or TAM to Pα-iDTA mice for 5 days beginning at P7 did not affect retinal vascular development. In both strains, vessel growth appeared normal, with a nearly complete primitive vascular network observed in the retina (Fig. 4, C and D). In contrast, both Olig2-TK and Pα-iDTA mice exhibited severe defects in vessel development within the optic nerve, which correlated with a marked reduction in OLIG2+ and PDGFRα+ cells, respectively (Fig. 4, E to H, and fig. S12, E and F). Furthermore, when GCV treatment of Olig2-TK mice was initiated at P14, OLIG2+ cells were efficiently depleted and angiogenesis in the optic nerve was significantly impaired (fig. S12G). Collectively, these genetic ablation studies reveal distinct glial-vascular regulatory mechanisms: Radial glial cells function as neural progenitors to coordinately regulate angiogenesis in both GM and WM domains, astrocytes specifically govern GM angiogenesis, and OPCs selectively control WM vascularization.

CHD8 expressed by oligodendrocytes is necessary for WM angiogenesis

The developing CNS parenchyma experiences physiological hypoxia (35). HIF1α promotes CNS angiogenesis to enable neural cells to adapt to low oxygen conditions (12, 13). To determine whether HIF1α is responsible for region-specific angiogenesis, we re-clustered transcriptomic data from astrocytes (23) and identified WM and GM astrocytes using established markers (fig. S13A). WM astrocytes are enriched in expression of Vim, Gfap, Fos, Dbi, and Jun, whereas GM astrocytes have elevated expression of Aldh1l1, Slc1a3, Fgfr3, and Vegfa (fig. S13B). Notably, Hif1a was expressed in GM astrocytes and at lower levels in WM astrocytes and OPCs (fig. S13C), and this corresponded to higher levels of nuclear HIF1α accumulation in GM astrocytes (fig. S13, D and E). Expression of HIF1α was also detected in hGFAP-GFP+ astrocyte in mouse retinal (fig. S13F). Furthermore, pro-angiogenic factors secreted by astrocytes such as Vegfa, Angpt1, and Angptl4 were expressed in GM astrocytes but were observed at low or undetectable levels in WM astrocytes (fig. S13C).

Given the low expression of HIF1α in OPCs, to identify transcriptional regulators responsible for oligodendrocyte lineage–mediated WM angiogenesis, we performed a comprehensive differential expression analysis of transcriptional networks across oligodendrocyte lineages relative to astrocytes (Fig. 5A). In primitive and mature OPCs, we detected expression of previously described transcriptional regulators of oligodendrocyte fate commitment and differentiation including Olig2, Chd8, Sox10, Yy1, Tcf3, Rbpj, Ncor2, and Nkx2–2 as well as previously unreported transcriptional regulators such as Mta2 and Nelfe (Fig. 5B and fig. S13, G and H). Olig2 and Chd8 were top-ranked candidates by P value. To further explore the role of OLIG2 in angiogenesis, we bred mice with a floxed Olig2 allele with PDGFRα-CreERT and with CNP-Cre mice. PDGFRα-CreERT mediates gene deletion in early-stage OPCs, and CNP-Cre results in deletion in late-stage OPCs. Neither deletion paradigm significantly altered CD31+ vascular density in GM or WM (Fig. 5C and fig. S14A), demonstrating that oligodendrocyte-lineage OLIG2 is dispensable for angiogenic regulation.

Chd8, one of the highest-risk susceptibility genes for autism spectrum disorder (ASD) (36, 37), is required for CNS myelination (38), and emerging evidence that implicates neurovascular dysregulation in ASD pathogenesis (39, 40) suggests that CHD8 may mediate angiogenic regulation. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated CHD8 expression in BLBP+ glial progenitors and PDGFRα+ OPCs within the third ventricular zone (fig. S14B). In the optic nerve, CHD8 was enriched in OLIG2+ oligodendroglial lineage cells but was undetectable in GFAP+ astrocytes (fig. S14C). These observations are consistent with the previous report that CHD8 has a glial cell-type-specific expression pattern restricted to OPC populations (38). Strikingly, in contrast to the ubiquitous expression of OLIG2 observed in juvenile cortical OPCs, CHD8 was expressed in a distinct deep-to-superficial cortical gradient with strong preferential accumulation in corpus callosum OPCs and low abundance or absence in superficial cortical OPCs (fig. S14, D and E). Thus, CHD8 is preferentially expressed in WM OPCs.

To investigate whether CHD8 drives pro-angiogenic gene expression, we identified the gene targets of CHD8 in OPCs. By comparing the genomic binding profiles of CHD8 and OLIG2, we found that CHD8-targeted sites (approximately 19,104 peaks) were distinct from those of OLIG2 in OPCs (Fig. 5D). CHD8-specific target genes are overrepresented in glial cell development processes and functionally converge on vascular developmental processes including the Wnt signaling pathway, VEGF receptor binding, and sprouting angiogenesis (Fig. 5E). Known pro-angiogenic genes were highly enriched in the group of CHD8-bound genes, whereas OLIG2 did not bind these regions (Fig. 5F), suggest that pro-angiogenic genes are specifically targeted by CHD8 in OPCs. One of these pro-angiogenic CHD8 target genes, Sema5a, is, like Chd8, associated with ASD susceptibility (41–43). Differential gene expression analysis of transcriptomics data (38) from OPCs isolated from P7 brain tissues of control and OPC-specific Chd8 knockout mice revealed significant downregulation of pro-angiogenic genes following Chd8 deletion (Fig. 5G). Consistently, qRT-PCR analysis of O4+ OPCs from P7 cortices confirmed reduced expression of Pdgfa, Sema5a, Tgfa, Vegfa, Wnt7a and Wnt7b in Chd8 knockout mice (fig. S14F). Together, these findings demonstrate that CHD8, unlike other oligodendroglial transcription factors, functions as a transcriptional activator that directly initiates pro-angiogenic gene programs in the WM oligodendrocyte lineage.

Oligodendroglial CHD8 specifically drives postnatal WM angiogenesis

To investigate the role of CHD8 in angiogenesis during brain development, we selectively ablated Chd8 floxed alleles in glial progenitors and primitive OPCs by generating heterozygous Olig1-Cre; Chd8fl/+ mice and homozygous Olig1-Cre; Chd8fl/fl mice (Fig. 6A). Due to the lethality of Chd8 complete knockout, subsequent analyses were performed on Olig1-Cre; Chd8fl/+ (Chd8Het cKO) mice. We performed immune-electron microscopy analyses in Chd8Het cKO mice and their corresponding controls and observed direct physical contacts between OLIG2+CHD8+ OPCs and adjacent blood vessels in control mice (Fig. 6B and fig. S15, A and B), suggesting that CHD8-dependent downstream effects in oligodendrocyte lineage cells may be mediated, at least in part, through direct interactions with the vasculature. A significant reduction of CHD8 in the nuclei of perivascular OPCs in Chd8Het cKO mice confirming the knockout efficiency (Fig. 6B). Importantly, we found that neither OPC nor astrocyte development were perturbed in these Chd8Het-knockout mice (Fig. 6, C and D, and fig. S15C), but there was a substantial reduction in CD31+ vessels specifically within the corpus callosum of these mice (Fig. 6E). A similar decrease was also observed in the optic nerve (Fig. 6F). In addition, we observed a similar vascular phenotype in the WM (including the corpus callosum and optic nerve) of mice in which Chd8 was deleted using NG2-CreERT (NG2-CreERT; Chd8fl/fl), an inducible Cre line with well-established OPC specificity (20, 44) (Fig. 6, G to I). Loss of Chd8 in OPCs did not alter the development of PDGFRα+ OPCs or GFAP+ astrocytes (fig. S15, E to H). Notably, deletion of Chd8 in either glial progenitors or OPCs had no effect on retinal vascular development (fig. S15, D to F and I). These observations demonstrate a regionally specific requirement for oligodendrocyte-encoded Chd8 in the control of WM angiogenesis.

Mice with oligodendrocyte lineage-specifically deficient in CHD8 have neurological dysfunction

Loss-of-function variants of the Chd8 gene are associated with a high penetrance of ASD and other neurological disorders (46). However, how loss of Chd8 in oligodendrocytes influences ASD pathogenesis is unclear. Adequate angiogenesis is critical for neuronal survival and normal neural function. We found that, although there are fewer blood vessels and a clear myelination defect in the optic nerves of Chd8Het-knockout mice, the density of RBPMS+ retinal ganglion cells did not differ, and the integrity of retinal ganglion cell axons are not obviously altered (fig. S17, A to C). The Chd8Het-knockout mice have visual dysfunction and reduced visual evoked potential latency, but we did not detect significant effects on amplitude of the response to visual stimulation (fig. S17, D and E). We speculate that the neurological dysfunction resulting from Chd8 loss in oligodendrocytes is primarily attributable to the loss of myelination rather than to neuronal or axonal death caused by vascular defects.

Ischemia, hypomyelination, and axonal pathology often co-occur with WM degeneration (47). However, we did not observe axonal degeneration in Chd8Het-knockout mice. Whether there is a causal relationship among these pathologies is debated. To determine whether WM ischemia and dysmyelination inevitably lead to axonal degeneration, we analyzed Olig2-TK mice treated with GCV to deplete OLIG2+ progenitor cells. No significant abnormalities in retinal ganglion cell density were detected in these mice (fig. S17F). Electron microscopy analysis of Olig2-TK mice treated with GCV beginning at P14 revealed intact optic nerve axons but markedly impaired myelination, particularly in small-diameter axons, as evidenced by an increased g-ratio (fig. S17G). Corresponding with these structural findings, the mice displayed visual impairment characterized by reduced signal transmission efficiency upon OLIG2+ cell loss, but unchanged amplitude of visually evoked cortical responses, suggesting preserved neuronal integrity (fig. S17, H and I).

As our data indicate that differences in vascular supply and remodeling potentially contribute to distinct pathological responses, we investigated how defective angiogenesis in GM induced by astrocyte loss affects neuronal function. TAM administered to GFAP-iDTA mice starting at either P7 or P21 led to significant decreases in RBPMS+ retinal ganglion cells, an increased number of Caspase-3+/RBPMS+ retinal ganglion cells relative to controls, and degeneration of RGC axons in the optic nerve (fig. S18, A to D), indicating that astrocytes are critical for postnatal retinal ganglion cell survival. As expected, GFAP-iDTA mice have impaired optomotor responses (fig. S18E), and the astrocyte ablation led to significantly reduced amplitude and prolonged latency in visual evoked potentials compared to control mice (fig. S18, F and G). These data suggest that astrocyte loss–induced vascular and angiogenic defects critically impair retinal ganglion cell survival and visual function, establishing astrocytes as essential regulators of postnatal neuronal viability and circuit integrity.

DISCUSSION

Region-specific regulation of CNS angiogenesis

The central nervous system exhibits striking regional differences in vascular density and architecture that are essential for neuronal activity, metabolic homeostasis, and circuit integrity (5, 48, 49). Yet, the mechanisms establishing such region-specific angiogenesis have remained poorly defined. Our study reveals that astrocytic and oligodendroglial lineages employ distinct transcriptional programs to drive vascular growth in gray and white matter, respectively, thereby uncovering a lineage-based framework for neurovascular specialization.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis showed that glial populations segregate into two discrete pro-angiogenic signatures, with astrocytes enriched in VEGF-, WNT-, and ANGPT-dependent genes, and oligodendroglial lineages expressing PDGF-, TGFα-, and semaphorin-related regulators. These data, together with lineage-specific ablation experiments, establish that astrocytes selectively govern angiogenesis in gray matter (GM), whereas oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) are indispensable for white matter (WM) vascularization. This functional specialization suggests that glial heterogeneity, long appreciated in neurophysiological contexts, extends to vascular patterning, thereby coordinating local metabolic demands with cellular identity.

Astrocytes have long been implicated in angiogenesis during development (32, 50); however, prior studies have not addressed whether angiogenic competence varies across astrocyte subtypes or brain regions. By leveraging the distinct developmental origins of retinal and optic nerve astrocytes, we show that angiogenic function is restricted to specific astrocytic subsets rather than being a universal glial property. We further show that Vegfa, Angpt1, and Angptl4 are preferentially expressed in GM astrocytes, establishing a molecular basis for astrocyte-driven angiogenesis in metabolically demanding regions. A recent study showed that astrocytes differentially contribute to angiogenesis following stroke in white versus gray matter. Specifically, cortical astrocytes, but not white matter astrocytes, activate a pro-angiogenic transcriptional program, a process that underlies a key repair mechanism in injured gray matter (14). These findings indicate that astrocyte-mediated regional angiogenesis contributes to both developmental vascularization and adult vascular regeneration.

Astrocyte and OPC loss impairs angiogenesis but not vasculature regression

During development, EC proliferation declines markedly and vessel regression initiates by P14, yet sustained proliferation persists. Depletion of astrocytes or oligodendrocyte precursor cells at P14 via multiple genetic models reduced vascular proliferation, indicating that these cells drive the continued increase in vascular density from P14 to P21. Astrocyte-derived vascular endothelial growth factor has been reported to stabilize developing retinal vessels (51), a recent study showed that vessel regression was not initiated by the retraction of astrocytic end feet (52), which supports our observation that the removal of astrocytes at P14 and P21 does not affect vascular stability. In addition, the role of OPCs in vascular stability remains unclear. A study found that OPCs frequently accumulate around damaged vessels under ischemic conditions (53). Thus, whether OPCs participate in vessel regression during chronic hypoperfusion requires further investigation.

In this study, microglial depletion using PLX3397 inhibited endothelial cell proliferation without promoting vessel regression, leading to a significant reduction in vascular density. However, whether this PLX3397-induced decrease in vascular density is dependent on microglia remains unclear due to potential off-target effects of the inhibitor (54, 55). PLX3397 has been reported to have affinity for c-Kit, which is expressed in endothelial cells (56), suggesting that it may also directly target these cells.

Oligodendrocyte lineage heterogeneity and vascular regulation

Oligodendrocyte lineage cells were once considered a uniform population dedicated to myelination, yet accumulating evidence points to marked transcriptional and functional diversity across CNS regions (57, 58). Our data extend this concept by identifying OPCs as key orchestrators of white matter–specific angiogenesis. Using genetic ablation and developmental mapping, we found that OPC loss results in profound vascular deficits in the corpus callosum and optic nerve, while sparing gray matter vasculature.

The optic nerve serves as an ideal model for studying glial precursor cells and OPC migration, as it contains only axons and glial cells and no neuronal cell bodies. Unexpectedly, we observed that OLIG2+ OPCs in the P0 optic nerve migrate and initiate vascularization independently of pre-existing vessels, suggesting that glial progenitors can pattern the vasculature rather than simply following endothelial cues. This observation challenges classical models of endothelial-led angiogenesis in the brain and spinal cord (34), suggesting that glial lineages can act as a primary vascular organizer in the developing CNS.

Oligodendrocyte-lineage-expressing CHD8 as a regulator of white matter angiogenesis

Through comparative transcriptomic analysis, we identified two key oligodendrocyte lineage regulators, OLIG2 and CHD8, among the top differentially expressed transcription factors. OLIG2, a canonical marker for the oligodendrocyte lineage, is crucial throughout its development (59, 60). However, it does not appear to promote angiogenesis directly, as genome-wide occupancy analyses show no activation of pro-angiogenic genes by OLIG2, and its knockout does not result in vascular phenotypes. In contrast, CHD8, despite its known role in oligodendrocyte development (38), emerged as a previously unrecognized regulator of OPC-mediated vascular development. Interestingly, this chromatin remodeler and high-confidence ASD risk gene (36, 37) was enriched in WM OPCs, where it directly bound and activated pro-angiogenic targets such as Tgfa and Sema5a. Conditional deletion of Chd8 in glial progenitors or OPCs impaired WM vascularization without altering OPC or astrocyte development, demonstrating a cell-autonomous requirement for CHD8 in white matter angiogenesis.

Although NG2 is expressed in pericytes, CHD8 expression is barely detectable in pericytes at the developmental stages, suggesting that CHD8 is unlikely to play a major functional role in this cell population under our experimental conditions. In contrast, we observed robust CHD8 expression in oligodendrocyte lineage cells, consistent with previous reports (38). While we cannot formally exclude a potential contribution from pericytes, the differential expression pattern strongly supports the conclusion that the impaired angiogenesis phenotype observed in Chd8 iKO mice primarily results from CHD8 loss in oligodendrocyte lineage cells. A pericyte-specific genetic approach will be required to definitively address this question in future studies.

Importantly, the function of CHD8 in white matter angiogenesis is distinct from the HIF1α-mediated angiogenic program observed in astrocytes (13), suggesting that astrocytes and OPCs utilize separate transcriptional pathways to achieve region-specific vascularization. Unlike the effects of astrocyte ablation, which lead to neuronal loss and hypovascularity in GM, Chd8 deletion primarily disrupts vascular integrity in WM without overt neuronal degeneration, which contrasts with the effects of astrocyte depletion.

Implications for neurodevelopmental and neurovascular disorders

Our findings have broad implications for understanding neurodevelopmental pathologies characterized by white matter dysfunction and vascular abnormalities. Developmental neurovascular dysregulation has been increasingly linked to ASD and other cognitive disorders (39, 40). We discovered that CHD8 expression in OPCs is necessary for postnatal brain angiogenesis. Moreover, we showed that Sema5a, which is reduced in autistic brains and recognized as a potential ASD susceptibility gene (41, 42), is a CHD8 target gene. SEMA5A promotes angiogenesis by enhancing endothelial cell proliferation and suppressing apoptosis (43). The CHD8–SEMA5A axis identified here provides a mechanistic connection between genetic susceptibility and neurovascular development, suggesting that impaired OPC-driven angiogenesis could contribute to circuit-level abnormalities in ASD.

The distinct roles of astrocytes and OPCs in regulating GM and WM angiogenesis offer new opportunities for regionally targeted therapeutic strategies. Enhancing astrocyte-mediated HIF1α signaling may promote vascular repair in ischemic gray matter, whereas modulation of CHD8-dependent transcriptional programs in OPCs could restore vascular and myelin integrity in white matter lesions. Together, these findings establish glial heterogeneity as a central principle of CNS vascular organization, revealing that region-specific angiogenesis arises from lineage-restricted transcriptional networks. By linking glial diversity to vascular patterning, our work provides a conceptual and mechanistic framework for exploring how neurovascular coupling is built, maintained, and disrupted across the developing and diseased brain.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All animal use and study protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Sichuan University (WCSUH-2026-012). Animals were housed in a vivarium with a 12-hour light/dark cycle with free access to normal chow food and water. Mice of either sex on either a mixed C57BL/6. All studies complied with all relevant animal use guidelines and ethical regulations. Mice homozygous for floxed alleles of Chd8 (38)were crossed with Olig1-Cre+/− mice to generate Chd8 cKO (Olig1- Cre+/−; Chd8f/f) mice. hGFAP-GFP mice (The Jackson Laboratory, 003257) and tdTomato reporter (The Jackson Laboratory, 007914) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. DTA mice (The Jackson Laboratory, 009669) were crossed with hGFAP-CreERT mice (The Jackson Laboratory,012849) or Aldh1l1-CreERT (The Jackson Laboratory, 031008) to generate hGFAP-CreERT:DTA and Aldh1l1-CreERT:DTA mutant mice, respectively.

Administration of GCV

For GCV treatment, Olig2-TK mice were administrated with GCV (50 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection once daily at the indicated period. Tissues were harvested at indicated stages and subjected to immunostaining.

Tamoxifen and drug administration

Tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich, T5648) was dissolved to stock concentration of 5 and 20 mg/ml in ethanol and sunflower seed oil (1:9, v/v). For neonatal pups (P3), 20 μl (5 mg/ml) of tamoxifen was administered once daily at the indicated period. For mice older than P7, tamoxifen (100 mg/kg) was administered by intraperitoneal injection once daily at the indicated period.

Immunohistochemistry

Cryosections (12-mm thick) or vibratome sections (50-mm thick) were permeabilized and blocked in blocking buffer (0.3% Triton X-100 and 5% normal donkey serum in PBS) for 1 hour at room temperature and overlaid with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C.Antibodies used in the study were: rabbit anti-OLIG2 (Novus Biologicals, NBP1–28667), rat anti-PDGFRα (BD Bioscience, 558774), rabbit anti-CHD8 (Abcam, ab84527), rat anti-GFAP (Invitrogen, #13–0300), rat anti-CD31 (BD Pharmingen, 553370), goat anti-CD31 (R&D System, BAF3628), rat anti-CD31 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc18916), rabbit anti-SOX9 (Abcam, ab185966), guinea pig anti-RBPMS (Sigma-Aldrich, ABN1376), rabbit anti-GS (Abcam, ab176562), goat anti-Aldh1l1 (Thermo Fisher, 600–101-HB6), rabbit anti-GFP (Invitrogen, #A-11122), rabbit anti- HIF 1α(Proteintech, 20960–1-AP), rat anti- TER119 (Thermo Fisher, 14–5921-81), rabbit anti- Collagen IV (Bio-rad, 2150–0150). After washing with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS, cells or brain sections were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to Cy2, Cy3 or Cy5 (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories) for 2 hours at room temperature, stained in DAPI for 5 min, washed in PBS and mounted with Fluoromount-G (SouthernBiotech). Cell counting was carried out in a double-blind manner.

Immuno-transmission electron microscopy (Immuno-TEM)

Immuno-TEM was performed as previously described (61, 62). After fixation and embedding, ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were prepared. Double immunogold labeling was performed by co-incubating sections with anti-OLIG2 and anti-CHD8 antisera (1:50 each), followed by detection with 35-nm gold-conjugated rabbit anti-guinea pig secondary antibodies for OLIG2 and 10-nm gold-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies for CHD8. Grids were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and sections were imaged using a HITACHI HT7800 transmission electron microscope at 30 kV.

Identification of transcriptional regulators

Single cells expression data used in this study was retrieved from (23). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between oligodendrocyte lineage (pri-OPC/OPC) and astrocyte were identified using the FindMarkers function in Seurat, applying significance thresholds of P value < 0.01 and logFC > 0.2. To investigate the transcription regulators regulating these DEGs, mouse factor-target gene interaction data in GMT format were retrieved from the TFlink database (63). Then, over-representation analysis (ORA) of the DEGs against this interaction network was performed using the clusterProfiler package (64).

Optomotor response

For measuring the visual acuity of mice, the optomotor reflex-based spatial frequency threshold test was used. Prior to testing, mice underwent 10-minute habituation in the experimental arena. During testing, freely-moving mice were placed on a central platform surrounded by four monitors displaying moving vertical sine-wave gratings (spatial frequency: 0.15–0.60 cycles/degree in 0.05 increments; constant 12°/s velocity, 100% contrast). Positive tracking was defined when there was a movement of the head (motor response) toward the direction of the bars or rotation of the body in the direction consistent with the stimulus. Each eye was tested separately using oppositely-rotating gratings to determine the maximum resolvable spatial frequency.

Visual evoked potential (VEPs) recording and analysis

Prior to testing, animals were placed in a dark environment for a minimum of four hours to allow for dark adaptation. All subsequent procedures were conducted under dim red light to preserve the adapted state throughout the VEPs recording. Adult mice were anesthetized with 1% isoflurane delivered at a flow rate of 0.4 liter/min and secured in a stereotaxic instrument. Ophthalmic ointment was applied to maintain corneal hydration throughout the surgery. Subsequently, the scalp was depilated, the skin over the skull was surgically removed, and the underlying connective tissue was carefully cleared to expose the cranial surface. Holes were drilled using a dental drill over the bilateral primary visual (V1) cortices (anterior-posterior [AP]: −3.63 mm; medial-lateral [ML]: 2.5 mm for the left hemisphere and 3.0 mm for the right hemisphere) and over the cerebellum for the reference electrode. Custom-made electrodes were then implanted into the corresponding sites. LEDs were positioned on either side of the eyes, and flicker stimulation at 1 Hz (4 ms duration) was delivered using the TDT RZ2 system. Local field potentials (LFPs) from the primary visual cortex (V1) were recorded during stimulation using the same system. LFP signals were sampled at 3 kHz and bandpass filtered between 1 and 300 Hz. VEPs were analyzed offline using MATLAB (2024b). LFP signals were segmented into epochs spanning from −100 ms pre-stimulus to 300 ms post-stimulus, with baseline correction performed using the pre-stimulus interval. All preprocessing steps were conducted using the EEGLAB toolbox (EEGLAB 2025). The characteristic VEP components, N1 and P2, were identified using a custom MATLAB script with ginput-based manual selection, and their latencies and amplitudes were subsequently quantified. These components were averaged across 180 stimulus sweeps.

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from O4+ OPCs isolated from P7 cortices of mutant mice and their littermate controls, using the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was generated with iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad). qRT-PCR was performed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Model: 4376600) using SYBR Green chemistry. For normalization, Gapdh was used as the internal reference control in qRT-PCR experiments. qRT-PCR primers are listed in table S1.

Statistical analysis

All analyses in this research were performed using Microsoft Excel, GraphPad Prism 9.00 (https://www.graphpad.com). Normality was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were analyzed by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test (for two groups) or ordinary one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test (for multiple groups), depending on the experimental design. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The specific statistical test used is indicated in the corresponding figure legend. All data are presented as mean ± SEM, with individual data points plotted, and the significance levels are indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and not significant (n.s.). Quantifications were performed using at least three individual experiments. The specific numbers (n) for individual experiments are listed in the figure legends. The n value was defined as the number of experiments that were repeated independently with similar results. For each animal, quantification was averaged from three sections per brain region. No animals or data points were excluded from the analyses. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes, but our sample sizes were similar to those generally employed in the field. No randomization was used to collect all the data, but data were quantified with blinding. Representative images and results are shown from immunostaining.
