Executive Summary
Strategic Context and Research Rationale
The rapid expansion of utility-scale photovoltaic (PV) solar energy facilities is a cornerstone of global
decarbonization, yet it necessitates a rigorous, data-driven understanding of avian-solar interactions to
mitigate ecological risks. Traditional monitoring remains heavily dependent on manual carcass searches,
a method fundamentally constrained by high operational costs, searcher efficiency biases, and
significant safety risks for personnel in extreme environments. Furthermore, manual surveys are
inherently reactive and provide only a snapshot in time; they fail to capture the behavioral context of
avian usage and cannot provide the long-term continuous data stream required for comprehensive
environmental impact assessments. Over the course of this project, we collected more than 19,000
hours of daytime video across five diverse operational solar sites—Desert Southwest (DS1, DS2 in
Arizona), Midwest (MW1, MW3 in Illinois), and Northeast (NE1 in Massachusetts)—proving that the
sheer scale of data required for modern regulatory auditing makes manual review commercially and
operationally impractical. To support sustainable solar expansion, there is a strategic imperative to
transition toward non-invasive, automated monitoring systems. This framework utilizes an edge-
computing, machine-vision architecture designed to detect and classify complex avian interactions—
specifically fly-throughs, perching, and collisions—in near-real time. By leveraging high-definition
cameras and localized AI processing, the system enables the collection of high-volume, high-velocity
data previously inaccessible to site operators. The following technical breakdown details the multi-
phased pipeline designed to translate raw pixels into actionable ecological intelligence.

The Four-Stage Machine Vision Pipeline
Deploying sophisticated AI at the "edge" on the NVIDIA Jetson Xavier NX embedded system requires a
highly optimized computational strategy. To maintain a processing speed of 30 frames per second (1/30
of a second per frame)—a requirement for near-real-time performance—we engineered a multi-phased
modeling pipeline. This sequential approach manages limited onsite power and processing resources by
filtering irrelevant data early and scheduling computationally intensive tasks, such as deep behavioral
classification, for nighttime off-peak hours.

Sequential Framework Architecture – Model Components
 • MODT (Moving Object Detection and Tracking) – Employs background subtraction (MOG2) and
 Kalman Filters to isolate movement and generate object "tracks;" filters high-volume data
 efficiently which makes it essential for isolating objects of interest from static infrastructure and
 background noise.
 • MobileNetV3 (Object Classification) – Rapid binary classification (bird vs. non-bird) using image-
 by-image classifier with majority rule or transfer learning; discards non-avian tracks (insects,
 clouds, vegetation) within 1/30th of a second to balance edge computing and sufficient frame
 count.
 • Metadata-only Bi-directional Long Short-Term Memory (Metadata-only BiLSTM; Collision
 Detection) – One-dimensional analysis of metadata (x/y coordinates and speed); provides near-
 real-time collision alerting by processing lightweight metadata instead of heavy image
 sequences during peak daylight.

 iii

 • Fusion BiLSTM (Activity Classification) – Combines 2D track images with 1D metadata for deep
 behavioral analysis; scheduled for nighttime execution to manage GPU load; provides the deep
 context (e.g., FlyThrough vs. PerchOnPanel) required for ecological reporting.

This tiered architecture ensures the system remains responsive to high-priority events like collisions
while maintaining the depth of analysis required to differentiate subtle behavioral patterns without
overloading the edge hardware.

Performance Benchmarks
The transition to automated environmental monitoring requires a foundation of data-driven trust. Our
core models were subjected to rigorous benchmarking against large-scale datasets and validated against
specific physical parameters to ensure reliability.
 • Detection Range Capabilities: The system demonstrated reliable effective detection ranges
 mapped to specific avian dimensions: Large birds (~110 cm) at 400 m, Medium birds (~50 cm) at
 200 m, and Small birds (~25 cm) at 60 m.
 • Object Classification Reliability: The MobileNetV3 model achieved 94.9% track-level accuracy,
 effectively distinguishing birds from common false triggers such as wind-driven vegetation or
 aircraft.
 • Activity Classification Granularity: Using a "Top 3" accuracy approach, the system reached a
 94.7% success rate. This metric is vital because single tracks often contain multiple sequential
 behaviors (e.g., a fly-through ending in a perch); the Top 3 approach ensures these multifaceted
 interactions are captured.
 • Collision Detection Efficacy: The Meta BiLSTM model achieved 76.7% accuracy. Critically, this
 deep learning approach outperformed traditional machine learning models, such as SVM,
 Adaboost, and Random Forest, which capped at 71.4%, justifying the necessity of the BiLSTM
 architecture for time-series metadata.
These laboratory-validated benchmarks confirm the system’s readiness for the unpredictable
environmental conditions of operational PV facilities.

Field Validation and Operational Outcomes
Field validation serves as the ultimate stress test for an AI system's environmental robustness. Trials
conducted at the Argonne National Laboratory and operational PV sites processed a dataset of 2,712
tracks to assess real-world utility. The system achieved a bird detection accuracy of 94.3%, significantly
exceeding the initial project target of 80%. Activity classification agreement rates between the AI and
human analysts reached 74.1% (Top 1) and 84.2% (Top 2). However, field validation also identified a
23.5% false-negative rate in collision detection during simulations using plush decoys (swallow and
mallard sizes). Technical analysis indicated that these misses occurred when the MODT algorithm lost
the object's trajectory during the post-collision phase, preventing the Meta BiLSTM from receiving a
complete motion path. Resolving this tracking continuity gap is a primary objective for the transition
from version 1.0 to 1.1, ensuring even rare impact events are captured with higher fidelity.

Future Research and Strategic Roadmap
Iterative development is essential to ensure monitoring technologies keep pace with the complexities of
solar infrastructure and avian ecology. Our roadmap for the next generation of this technology is
defined by three strategic pillars:
 1. Hardware & Environmental Expansion: Integration of co-deployment of thermal or infrared
 imaging to enable 24-hour monitoring and expanding software compatibility to hardware
 platforms beyond the current SCC system.
 2. Data Persistence Strategies: Moving from real-time deletion toward selective visual data
 retention as onsite connectivity improves, allowing for robust post-event auditing and
 verification.
 3. Model Generalization: Refining the collision model by incorporating real-world observations to
 supplement current simulated decoy data, reducing false negatives and enhancing
 generalizability across diverse species. This machine-vision framework represents a significant
 leap forward in automated environmental oversight. By allowing operators to proactively learn
 about potential risks and implement preventative measures, this technology serves as the
 cornerstone for bird-compatible, sustainable PV solar energy development and ecologically
 mindful facility siting.

1. Introduction
Utility-scale photovoltaic (PV) solar energy facilities play an important role in meeting ever-increasing
electricity demands. As deployment continues, facility owners, regulators, researchers, and conservation
organizations need reliable information on how birds interact with solar energy facility infrastructure so
that potential ecological risks can be assessed and managed. Thus, accurate understanding of
interactions between birds and solar infrastructure is important for continued deployment of utility-
scale solar energy facilities.

Existing monitoring approaches rely largely on periodic carcass searches. To estimate adverse impacts of
PV solar facilities on birds, more specifically bird mortality inside the PV facility footprints, manual
searches for bird carcasses between the rows of solar panels by trained surveyors has been the most
scientifically defensible method to date (Huso et al. 2016; Kosciuch et al. 2021). More recently, trained
dogs are also used to detect bird carcasses at solar facilities (Boland et al. 2026). Although these
methods remain important, the major limitations of field surveying are (1) its inability to determine the
causes of fatality in many cases, (2) its infeasibility to continuously monitor bird activities over extended
periods of time (e.g., bird migratory season), and (3) the difficulty of observing bird activities or usage
without altering their behaviors. Field surveys are also subject to errors associated with searcher
efficiency and carcass removal by predation and require excessive man hours that are costly and can
present health and safety concerns for observers working outdoors during certain times of year in
certain regions. These concerns also apply to the use of trained dogs to some extent. Automated
monitoring of bird activities using video could provide a practical and safe solution. However, the key
challenge of such an approach is extracting useful information, including negative (e.g., bird collisions
and strandings), neutral (e.g., safe flights), and positive activities (e.g., nesting and foraging), from such
high volume and high velocity data in a timely manner that would allow delivery of pertinent
information to users.

Automated video monitoring offers a practical way to supplement conventional field surveys by
continuously recording bird activity at PV facilities and capturing interactions that may otherwise go
undetected. However, continuous video collection produces large volumes of high-velocity data, making
manual review impractical. Applying machine learning could enable automated methods capable of
detecting birds, distinguishing them from other moving objects, and classifying their interactions with
solar infrastructure for timely extraction of useful information.

Existing studies of automated bird monitoring using machine learning have mainly focused on only
object detection and classification to address important questions in avian ecology. Bird counting

studies use both aerial imagery (Abd-Elrahman et al., 2005; Boudaoud et al., 2019; Weinstein et al.,
2022) and on-ground photos (Akçay et al., 2020; Spoorthy et al., 2001; Wang et al., 2023;) to map bird
populations' movement trends as helpful indicators of variation in habitat quality. Species identification
studies also use still imagery from multiple sources to resolve the difficulties in extracting the fine-
grained features required to distinguish the presence of different bird species to aid in assessment and
further understanding of the biodiversity of ecosystems (Bhandari et al., 2014; Branson et al., 2014;
Ghosh and Islam, 2018; Mao et al., 2021). Studies of active bird detection and deterrence in agricultural
environments use sensors in combination with video cameras to actuate physically harmless,
environmentally friendly repellants designed to deter flocks of birds from causing damage to crops in
near-real-time (Garcia et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2019; Marcoň et al., 2021; Roihan et al., 2020; Srividya et
al., 2021); while collision avoidance studies use video as well to dynamically identify and intervene with
potential bird-turbine impacts at both inland and offshore windfarms (Salkanović et al., 2019;
Gradolewski et al., 2021; Duerr et al., 2023; A. Rioperez and de la Puente, 2010; May et al., 2012) as well
as bird-aircraft strikes in low-altitude airspace (Jo et al., 2019, Xu and Shi, 2014; Tian et al., 2017).

Few studies have utilized machine learning for the purpose of observing and classifying bird activities.
Bodewes (2010) proposed a process for the annotation of data obtained from attaching GPS loggers to
Lesser black-backed gulls (Larus fuscus) as well as the implementation of a supervised learning approach
to classifying the birds' behavior to substantiate hypotheses regarding their use of foraging areas
(Bodewes 2010). Morbale (2022) proposed the design and execution of a pipeline to build and train
several machine learning models based on accelerometer sensor data from the same dataset on Lesser
black-backed gulls used by Bodewes to facilitate live classification of their activities through
microcontroller-based embedded systems (Morbale, 2022). Conners et al. (2021) evaluated the utility of
Hidden Markov Models to classify major movement activities from magnetometer and accelerometer
data collected from four species of free-ranging albatross as a case study (Conners et al., 2021). Of these
studies, all are based on data obtained directly from bird-borne sensors.

The goal of this project was to develop a technology for automated monitoring of avian-solar
interactions, more specifically fly-through, perching, and collisions, using a machine learning approach in
conjunction with a high-definition edge-computing camera. This project produced rigorously tested
machine learning models that are executed on these cameras. The purpose of this technology
development was to improve the ability to collect a large volume of accurate data on avian-solar
interactions in order to facilitate understanding of solar-related avian impacts. Argonne developed this
technology for PV solar energy facilities. At the end of this project, the technology was ready for
systematic field trials at solar energy facilities. By enabling long-duration, automated monitoring, the
resulting technology is intended to provide facility operators, agencies, and researchers with a higher-
volume of more timely information on avian-solar interactions than can be obtained from periodic field
surveys alone in real-world environments.

3.1. Video Recordings
The primary data for our technology development were video recordings of bird activities collected at
operational PV solar facilities using the SCC system. We assembled a total of 13 systems that could
continuously record videos over extended period of times. Each system consisted of a SCC and
associated electronics that enable continuous 5-minute video segments to be written to an external USB
hard drive from dawn until dusk (Figure 1). We installed one or two camera systems per selected PV
facility and recorded videos of bird activities from September 2019 through August 2024. Although
video recording at each facility during this period was not continuous due to maintenance, severe
weather events, and limited personnel availability, we collected more than 19,000 hours of daytime
videos.

Because bird collisions at PV solar facilities have been estimated to occur at approximately 2.5 birds per
megawatt per year (Kosciuch et al., 2020), naturally occurring collisions were unlikely to be captured
frequently in video recordings. To compensate for the limited likelihood of collision observations for
model development and evaluation, we collected videos of simulated bird collisions at two PV facilities.
The simulations used plush bird decoys representing small birds similar in size to a swallow (L 7 in × W 9

 5

in × H 2 in) and larger birds similar in size to a female mallard duck (L 13 in × W 19 in × H 5 in). A small
weight was attached to each decoy to improve stability during flight. Decoys were thrown one at a time
toward solar panels from an elevated platform in front of a panel row and/or from the rooftop of an
adjacent two-story building. Simulated collisions were recorded at varying camera distances and angles,
and under different times of day and seasonal conditions. Using this approach, we collected videos
containing approximately 230 simulated bird collisions with solar panels.

3.2. Training Data
Object classes and bird activity classes used for this project are summarized in Table 1. The object-class
annotation scheme included 11 classes that were expected to occur in videos collected at operational
PV sites: Bird, Cable, Panel, Plant, Car, Human, OtherAnimal, Insect, Aircraft, Other,
and Unknown. These classes were selected to represent the primary biological objects, facility
infrastructure, vegetation, maintenance activity, and moving background objects that could appear
within or near the facility footprint. Although all 11 object classes were annotated for training-data
development, the latest object classification model was designed to produce a binary output—bird or
not bird—as described in Section 4.3.

The bird activity classification scheme included seven classes: FlyOverAbove,
FlyOverReflection, FlyThrough, PerchOnPanel, LandOnGround,
PerchInBackground, and Collision (Table 1). These activity classes describe high-level, discrete
movements observed in the camera field of view rather than behaviors with specific ecological
interpretations. The FlyOverReflection class was included in reducing false-negative bird
detections caused by bird reflections on panel surfaces.

Training data were generated from the video recordings using a combination of an automated method
and human interpretation (Hamada et al. 2026). The moving object detection and tracking (MODT)
algorithm described in Section 4. was used to identify video segments containing potential birds and
other moving objects and to output time-sequence images of each moving object, referred to as
“tracks”, together with associated metadata. Trained human interpreters reviewed the MODT-
generated tracks and assigned an object class to each track. When a track contained a bird, interpreters
also assigned bird activity classes. If a single bird track contained more than one activity type, all
observed activity types were documented. For example, a bird that descended from high flight, moved
between panels, and then perched on a panel was documented as FlyOverAbove, FlyThrough,
and PerchOnPanel.

All interpretations underwent quality-control review before inclusion in the final dataset (Hamada et al.
2026). Each object interpretation was independently reviewed, and observations were removed if the
interpreter disagreed with the original interpretation. Uncertain cases were sent to an additional
interpreter for final determination. A similar review process was used for bird activity interpretation to
improve dataset consistency; bird observations were ultimately confirmed by at least four interpreters,
and in some cases as many as six. Using this procedure, custom training data were created from more
than 7,500 hours of video recordings, representing approximately 40% of the video collected at PV solar
facilities at the time of model development. The final training dataset included more than 10,000 bird
tracks, 5,000 insect tracks, and 500 aircraft tracks. In addition, nearly 14,000 plant tracks were
annotated. We applied augmentation techniques to compensate for imbalance in the training data upon
developing machine learning models. Further details of training-data preparation were reported in
Hamada et al. 2026.

4. Software – Machine Learning Models
4.1. Requirements
To guide the design of machine-learning architectures for monitoring avian-solar interactions, we
focused on three requirements. First, the models need to detect birds at varying distances from the
camera, including both nearby birds and far away birds that appear as small objects in the background.
Second, the models need to use the unique information available in video data, particularly temporal
patterns, such as image sequence and movement rate, in addition to the spatial and optical information
available in individual images in order to be accurate for small and/or far away birds. Third, the models
need to generate predictions in near-real-time on the embedded camera processor. Recognizing birds
from a single image can be challenging for both image-classification models and human interpreters,
especially when birds are far from the camera and difficult to distinguish from other objects. To address

 7

this challenge, we selected model architectures capable of analyzing image sequences from the same
moving object over time rather than relying on individual frames alone.

Our architecture selection focused on neural-network-based machine learning, which reflected the
current state of the art for image classification at the time. However, classifying video or image
sequences differs from classifying individual images because the model must account for temporal
information as well as spatial features. Therefore, the selected architectures needed to learn patterns
that vary across both space and time. Candidate architecture types capable of incorporating time-
varying information included Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs; Schmidt 2019), Long Short-Term
Memory (LSTM) networks (Schmidhuber and Hochreiter, 1997), 3D Convolutional Neural Networks
(CNNs; Ji et al., 2012; Wang, 2023), and more recent architectures such as Inflated 3D CNNs (I3D;
Carreira and Zisserman, 2017), Two-Stream I3D (Carreira and Zisserman, 2017), and Separable 3D CNNs
(S3D-G; Howard et al., 2017), each with varying levels of documented accuracy.

Because the models must run in near-real-time on the embedded camera processor, architecture
selection was also constrained by execution speed. In particular, model complexity and the number of
parameters had to be balanced against the limited computational resources available on the camera. To
meet these requirements, we developed three separate machine-learning classification models, each
designed for a specific task in predicting bird interactions with PV solar energy facility infrastructure.
These models are executed after moving objects have been detected and tracked in the video using the
method described below.

Given the complexity of detecting birds in varying distances and classifying their activities, we opted to
develop four models that are executed in sequence to optimize performances for our avian-solar
interaction monitoring software—moving object detection and tracking (MODT), object classification,
bird collision detection, and bird activity classification models. Each of the four models is designed to
accomplish a unique task. The MODT model (or algorithm) detects and tracks objects moving in the
camera field of view and extracts images of those objects from video to create tracks. The object
classification model classifies those tracks into 11 classes—Bird, Cable, Panel, Plant, Car,
Human, OtherAnimal, Insect, Aircraft, Other, and Unknown (Table 1). The collision
detection model determines whether the bird collides with solar panels or other infrastructure. The
activity classification model classifies non-collision bird tracks into six activity categories
FlyOverAbove, FlyOverReflection, FlyThrough, PerchOnPanel, LandOnGround, and
PerchInBackground (Table 1).

4.2. Moving Object Detection and Tracking Algorithm
The moving object detection and tracking (MODT) algorithm is the first component of the avian-solar
interactions monitoring software. Its purpose is to identify portions of video containing birds or other
moving objects and convert those detections into track-level inputs for the downstream classification
models. The algorithm draws a bounding box around each detected object and tracks it until the object
stops moving or leaves the field of view. As the object moves through the scene, MODT crops each full-
frame image into the bounding box, producing a sequence of cropped track images centered around the
moving object. For each cropped image, the algorithm also records the object's x/y coordinates, area,
and relative speed. These images and metadata are saved as a time sequence with a unique identifier,
together defining a complete object track.

 8

Because the video footage generally has a relatively static background, MODT uses background
subtraction, a common computer-vision technique, to separate moving foreground objects from the
static scene (Cheung and Kamath, 2003). Background subtraction estimates the scene elements that
remain consistent across a sequence of frames and subtracts that background from subsequent frames,
leaving only the portions of the image that have changed. We selected the Mixture of Gaussians 2
(MOG2) background subtraction algorithm for this implementation because it is robust to changes in
background appearance and lighting conditions and is supported in the OpenCV library (Cheung and
Kamath, 2003; Zivkovic, 2004; Zivkovic and Van Der Heijden, 2006).

After detecting a moving object, MODT predicts how that object is likely to move in the next frame using
a Kalman Filter. It then characterizes prior movement using a temporal moving average of the distance
traveled between frames and the variance in that movement; together, these define an allowable range
for the object's predicted location in the subsequent frame. If a newly detected object falls within the
predicted range of a previously detected object, it is associated with that object and the corresponding
track is extended. If the detection does not meet the track-continuity criteria, it is instead treated as a
new moving object that may form a separate track in later frames.

Through this detection-and-association process, MODT groups images of the same moving object into a
single track. Each track consists of a time sequence of cropped still images centered on the moving
object, along with metadata for each image: the object's x/y coordinates relative to the upper-left
corner of the full video frame, its relative speed (pixel displacement per 1/30 second between frames),
and its relative size (number of pixels occupied within the frame).

PV solar facility videos contain many background features that can produce irrelevant detections, such
as wind-driven vegetation, moving power lines, and glare on panel surfaces. To reduce these false
detections, MODT screens object movement patterns and rejects tracks that do not resemble the
relatively smooth flight paths typical of birds. We also required each retained track to contain at least 10
frames, which improves tracking continuity and reduces short-lived false detections. Because birds can
make complex movements that might otherwise interrupt a track, the algorithm allows a track to
continue for several frames without a new detection before terminating it. Within the full avian
monitoring pipeline, MODT supplies the track images and metadata used by the object classification,
collision detection, and activity classification models described in Section 3.2.

MODT was also used to generate the training data described in Hamada et al. (2026). We designed the
algorithm to detect all moving objects in a video, regardless of object type, while minimizing false
negatives—that is, MODT is intentionally built to over-detect moving objects. This design choice has two
benefits: (1) human interpreters do not need to review tens of thousands of hours of video footage to
extract observations of birds, insects, and other wildlife, since non-target objects are filtered out
downstream by the object classification model, and (2) over-detection minimizes the risk of missing true
biological objects.

4.3. Moving Object Classification Model
The moving object classification model uses the track images produced by the MODT algorithm to
determine whether each detected moving object is a bird or a non-bird object. We evaluated two
approaches for this task: an image-by-image classification approach and a track-based classification
approach. The image-by-image approach applies a CNN-based classifier to individual images within a
MODT-generated track and then assigns a single label to the full track using majority rule. This approach

 9

is computationally efficient for video processing because it relies on standard image-classification
architectures. We trained three transfer-learning models using neural networks pretrained on ImageNet
and then retrained with our project-specific training data: MobileNetV2 (Sandler et al., 2018),
MobileNetV3 (Howard et al., 2019), and ResNet101V2 (He et al., 2016). For the final edge-computing
implementation, we selected a MobileNetV3 Small CNN model. During execution, the model classifies
every fourth image in each MODT-generated track as bird or not bird, and the full track is classified using
majority rule (Figure 3).

Figure 3 Concept of an image-by-image object classification model. Evey fourth image in a track is
considered for object classification. A singe object class for the track is determined based on a majority
rule.

The track-based approach uses the full sequence of images in a MODT-generated track as input to a
multiple instance learning (MIL) model (Figure 4). MIL is a supervised learning framework in which
individual training examples are organized into sets, and the label is assigned to the set as a whole
rather than to each individual instance (Carbonneau et al., 2018). This structure is well suited to MODT
outputs because each track naturally contains multiple images of the same moving object observed over
time. However, like many sequence-based object-classification architectures, the MIL model requires a
fixed-length set of images as input. Because our tracks are variable in length, we chose to sample track
images to create fixed length inputs. We also included an attention mechanism in the final MIL
architecture so that the model could assign different levels of importance to different images or image
regions within a track. Although developed for a different application area, MIL-based approaches have
achieved high accuracy in medical imaging, where multiple images of the same anatomical region are
analyzed together to identify abnormalities (Quellec et al., 2017).

4.4. Activity Classification Model
The bird activity classification model is executed after the object classification model identifies a MODT-
generated track as a bird. Its purpose is to classify the bird’s activity using information from the full track
rather than from a single image. Because individual images often do not contain enough visual
information to distinguish among activity classes, the model uses the time sequence of track images
together with the metadata generated by the MODT algorithm. These metadata include the bird’s x/y
coordinates, relative speed, and area, which describe the bird’s movement through the camera field of
view. We therefore focused on model architectures capable of learning both visual patterns in the image
sequence and temporal patterns in the associated movement data. We chose architectures based on
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, a type of recurrent neural network designed to learn order-
dependent information across sequential data (Schmidhuber and Hochreiter, 1997).

The activity classification model utilizes motion-path information (x/y coordinates) that provides
important contextual information for differentiating bird activities. At the macro scale, a bird’s
movement can be represented by its path across the full camera field of view. Representative motion-
path images for each activity class are shown in Figure 5. Activities involving continuous flight, such as
FlyOverAbove and FlyThrough, generally produce relatively long and smooth lateral paths.
However, their vertical position in the field of view differs: FlyOverAbove typically appears higher in
the image, whereas FlyThrough appears closer to the panel array. In contrast, LandOnGround

 11

often shows movement toward the lower portion of the field of view. These trajectory differences
indicate that x/y coordinates over time are useful features for distinguishing among bird activity classes.

The bird activity classification model used a Fusion BiLSTM architecture (Figure 6) (Wang et al. 2024),
which combines the metadata-based BiLSTM branch (one-dimensional time-series metadata including
speed and x/y coordinates) with the track-image BiLSTM branch (two-dimensional track-image
sequences). This design allows the model to use both macro-scale motion and local visual information.
The metadata branch captures how the bird moves across the full field of view, helping distinguish
activities that occur in different parts of the scene. The image branch captures local changes in the bird’s
appearance and body position, helping distinguish activities that may occur in similar regions of the
image. For example, a bird flying high over the panels and a bird perching on a background object may
both appear in the upper portion of the field of view, but their image sequences can differ in body
position and motion. By combining these complementary inputs, the Fusion BiLSTM model was designed
to improve activity classification for bird tracks identified by the object classification model. This model
was developed to classify bird tracks into six activity classes: FlyOverAbove,
FlyOverReflection, FlyThrough, PerchOnPanel, LandOnGround, and
PerchInBackground.

4.5. Collision Detection Model
The collision detection model is executed after the object classification model identifies a MODT-
generated track as a bird. Its purpose is to detect and report potential bird collisions with solar panels.
Executing this model inside the camera allows bird collision detection in near-real-time so that collision
outcomes, such as fatality, injury, or apparent no harm, can be evaluated more accurately. Although
collisions could be treated as one of the bird activity classes, preliminary testing suggested that the full
activity classification model would be too slow for near-real-time execution because it uses track-image
sequences as two-dimensional inputs. To support faster execution, we developed a separate Meta
BiLSTM model specifically for collision detection (Figure 7). This model uses only one-dimensional MODT
metadata—motion path, x/y coordinates, and speed—as input rather than the track images used by the
activity classification model. This design was supported by preliminary analysis of the simulated collision
data, which showed that surrogate-bird collision tracks had motion-path features that were distinct
from other activity classes. These collision tracks typically formed an up-and-down arc that ended in
rapid deceleration at the panel, followed by sliding down the panel, falling to the ground, or both
(Figure 5f).

Because naturally occurring bird collisions with solar panels are rare in the available video dataset, the
collision detection model was trained using simulated collision data collected with bird surrogates. This
approach allowed us to develop and evaluate a near-real-time collision detection model despite the

 13

limited availability of real-world collision events. However, model performance may not fully represent
performance on real bird collisions if the movement patterns of actual collisions differ substantially from
those produced by the simulated bird surrogates. Even with this limitation, the simulated-collision
model provides a useful foundation for developing and refining collision detection methods as real-
world collision data become available.

5. Model Execution Timeline
To execute the avian-solar interactions monitoring software on an onsite camera with limited
computational resources, the four machine-learning models—MODT, object classification, collision
detection, and activity classification—are executed in a staged sequence based on operational need
(Table 2). During daytime operation, the MODT algorithm continuously detects and tracks moving
objects in the live video feed. When MODT generates a track, the MobileNetV3 model evaluates the
track image sequence and predicts whether the object is a bird. Tracks classified as non-birds are
discarded, while tracks classified as birds are passed to the Meta BiLSTM model for near-real-time
collision detection. At the same time, the track images and metadata for bird-classified objects are
saved to the camera’s internal storage for later processing. During nighttime operation, when
computational demand is lower, the more intensive Fusion BiLSTM model uses the saved bird-track data
to classify non-collision bird activities. After all model execution is complete, the system logs the

6.3. Activity Classification Model
As noted earlier, a single bird track can include more than one activity type. For example, a bird may
begin by flying high above the panels, then descend toward the array, fly through the panel rows, and
ultimately perch on a panel (Figure 12). Although the final activity or desired activity classification may
be PerchOnPanel, this track also contains portions corresponding to FlyOverAbove and
FlyThrough. Therefore, a model prediction of any of these three activity classes could reasonably be
considered consistent with the observed activities. Because the neural-network models developed for
this project produce only one activity prediction per track, we used a “Top 3” accuracy approach to
better account for tracks containing multiple activities. Under the standard “Top 1” approach, only the
activity class with the highest predicted probability is counted. For “Top 2” accuracy, a classification is
considered correct if the labeled activity appears among the two highest-probability predictions.
Similarly, for “Top 3” accuracy, the labeled activity can appear among the three highest-probability
predictions. This approach accounts for cases in which several plausible activity classes receive high
predicted probabilities because the bird performs multiple activities within a single track.

7. Validation
To evaluate the operational viability of our avian-solar interaction monitoring software for near-real-
time deployment, we field-tested all four models on the SCC edge computing device over an extended
deployment period at an active solar facility. Each model is strategically integrated into a specific phase
of the sequential processing pipeline:

 • MODT Algorithm: Detects moving objects within the live video stream in real time.
 • MobileNetV3 Model: Filters these detections to differentiate birds from other moving objects.
 • Metadata-only BiLSTM Model: Analyzes the filtered tracks during daytime operations to detect
 immediate bird-panel collisions in near-real time.
 • Fusion BiLSTM Model: Evaluates complex bird activities off-peak during nighttime hours when
 computational demands are lower (Table 6).

Field validation for the object and bird activity classification models was conducted at the Argonne
National Laboratory photovoltaic (PV) solar facility. During this period, all detected motion tracks were
archived on the camera’s internal storage. Following data retrieval, an independent analyst reviewed
the MODT algorithm outputs to manually classify the object type for each track. For tracks verified as
avian, the analyst further categorized the specific bird activity. Finally, the accuracy of the model
predictions was validated by calculating the rate of agreement between the automated model outputs
and the analyst’s independent classifications.

are flying high above the panels and then descend to land on the ground between them in many tracks.
There was also an improvement in FlyThrough misclassified as FlyOverReflection, which was a
misclassification also seen in the model training because a bird flying through the panels and reflection
of a bird flying over panels may appear in a comparable region in the frame.

Bird activity classification accuracy of 74.1% (Top 1 metric) and 84.2% (Top 2 metric) both demonstrated
by the field validation exceeded the 70% accuracy target for our version 1.0 software.

7.2. Collision Detection Model
To validate the Metadata-only BiLSTM model for collision detection, we conducted field collision
simulations on November 10–11, 2023, at an operational solar site. During validation, the MODT
algorithm, MobileNetV3 object classification model, and Metadata-only BiLSTM collision detection
model ran concurrently in near-real-time. We evaluated 34 simulated collision events spanning multiple
surrogate bird types and impact trajectories. To quantify false positive detections, we combined these
simulation collision tracks with non-collision tracks collected between April 19 and May 14, 2024
(originally gathered during field validation of the Fusion BiLSTM activity classification model). For

 24

operational consistency, we executed the Metadata-only BiLSTM model off-camera across this
combined dataset. Because the edge-computing and off-camera implementations run the identical
detection model, this offline approach yields performance results directly equivalent to conventional
field validation.

The Metadata-only BiLSTM collision detection model achieved an 86.7% overall accuracy, with a 13.1%
false-positive rate (219 tracks) and a 23.5% false-negative rate (8 tracks) (Figure 15). Because bird
collisions with solar panels are rare, minimizing false negatives is critical for accurately estimating
collision frequencies at PV facilities. Therefore, despite the model's reasonably high overall accuracy, the
24.5% false-negative rate exceeds acceptable thresholds and highlights the need for further refinement.
The collision detection accuracy of 86.7% demonstrated by the field validation exceeded the 70%
accuracy target for our version 1.0 software. In the meantime, reduction of false negatives would
increase accuracy of collision detection and provide much-needed data in understanding avian-solar
fatality.

Examples of successful and unsuccessful collision detection by the Metadata-only BiLSTM model during
field validation are shown in Figure 16. In Figure 16a, the bird decoy struck the panel, slid down and off
the bottom of the panel, and landed on the balcony. In this example, the MODT algorithm properly
extracted the track, and the MobileNetV3 model correctly classified the object as a Bird, while the

the Metadata-only BiLSTM model, the latter lacked sufficient information to accurately determine it as
Collision. This result indicates that the primary driver of false negatives in collision detection was
this inability of the MODT model to consistently track full motion trajectories from the pre- to post-
event phases of a collision incident.

8. Summary
To enable sufficient data collection to improve an understanding of the effects of PV solar energy
facilities to birds, we developed a machine-vision technology that monitors daytime bird activities at PV
facilities continuously over an extended period of time, such as an entire bird migratory season. Building
a portable machine-vision based, edge-AI system that maximizes accuracy at large distances required
trade-offs in model architectures used and software execution pipelines. To overcome this challenge, we
implemented four strategies: (1) creating a multi-phased modeling software pipeline (2) creating
machine learning models that use sequences of images and metadata to perform classification
(3) creating models complex enough to achieve high accuracy but not so complex as to take a long time
to compute, and (4) utilizing computer vision and machine learning libraries optimized for fast execution
on GPUs.

Our final software package for near-real-time avian-solar interactions monitoring consists of four
machine learning models (version 1.0):
 1. MODT algorithm utilizing background subtraction for object detection and tracking,
 2. MobileNetV3 object classification model with transfer learning using non-crop-and-scale
 images as input for object classification
 3. Metadata-only BiLSTM collision detection model using x/y coordinates and speed as input for
 collision detection and

 27

 4. Fusion BiLSTM activity classification model using images, x/y coordinates, and speed as input
 for activity classification.

To work with the camera’s limited internal computational resources, we divided the model execution
pipeline into phases to allow for time-sensitive tasks to perform in near-real-time, such as object
detection and tracking, object classification, and collision detection, and postponing activity
classification for off-hours. This process also allowed for grouping sequences of data of moving objects
over time first using the MODT algorithm that can later be given to the Fusion BiLSTM model for activity
classification. Our findings showed that performing object and activity classification on a sequence of
data (i.e., images and metadata) of the same object over time has higher accuracy than classification on
individual data points. We developed custom neural network architectures for object, activity and
collision classification that were both efficient and had high accuracy using these object track data. We
also used the NVIDIA TensorRT framework to execute our machine learning models quickly on the
camera's NVIDIA Xavier NX processor, and we used several GPU-enabled OpenCV libraries for machine
vision optimization. This required re-writing portions of our code as well as model optimization and
porting.

For near real-time bird activity monitoring, our software presented in this report is intended to be
executed on the SCC system. Alternatively, the software can be executed independently of the camera
using video recordings instead of a live camera feed. This method allows for video recordings capturing
bird activities to be preserved for validation and further analyses. Our bird monitoring technology was
primarily intended for the PV facilities owners and operators who would want to understand bird
activities at their facilities to proactively learn about potential risks to birds and implement preventive
measures and to apply the knowledge for ecologically mindful siting of their future PV solar energy
projects. We also envision our technology being used by researchers for biological/ecological studies in
the context of large-scale PV solar energy development, including birds’ attraction to and avoidance of
PV solar facilities, behavioral patterns across seasons and regions, and birds’ adaptation to PV facilities
over time.

We believe that combining observations from our AI camera technology — which continuously monitors
solar facilities to detect birds and classify their activities — with data from traditional bird monitoring
methods, such as field surveys (e.g., carcass, nest, and point-count surveys), motion-activated camera
traps, and acoustic recorders, will drive substantial advances in our understanding of how solar energy
facilities affect birds, both in terms of risks and potential benefits. This integrated approach could help
address questions such as rates of bird mortality across facilities and regions, the primary causes of bird
fatality within PV facility footprints, and the potential benefits PV facilities offer to birds. Answering
these questions stands to offer considerable value to the solar industry and scientific community alike in
supporting bird-compatible, sustainable energy development.

As with any emerging monitoring technology, our system has areas where we see clear opportunities for
future development. Currently, our technology is compatible for daytime hours only and with the
specific hardware (SCC) described in this study. Expanding to nighttime monitoring (e.g., through
thermal or infrared imaging) and broader hardware compatibility are natural next steps as the
technology matures. Similarly, our near-real-time system does not currently retain visual data, a design
choice driven by data storage and wireless connectivity constraints common to the remote locations of
many large-scale PV solar facilities; as storage and connectivity infrastructure improve, retaining more
visual data will become increasingly feasible and would further strengthen the system's analytical value.
