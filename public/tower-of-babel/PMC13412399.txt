Modelling Future Evolution of the Antarctic Ice Sheet With Explainable Machine Learning
Authors: Paula Maddigan, Bach Hoai Nguyen, Peter Yu Feng Siew, Bing Xue, Nicholas Golledge, Mengjie Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The accelerating effects of climate change, particularly rising sea levels resulting from melting land ice, require accurate predictions of ice sheet dynamics. Traditional numerical models, such as the Parallel Ice Sheet Model (PISM), are widely used; however, they rely on simplifying assumptions and are computationally intensive, limiting their ability to make long‐term predictions. This study addresses these limitations by employing machine learning techniques to emulate ice sheet dynamics, offering a faster, more efficient alternative to traditional numerical models. Using data generated by PISM for the Antarctic ice sheet spanning 2015 to 2100, we develop proof‐of‐concept machine learning models that predict key ice properties—ice mask, ice thickness, and ice velocity—based on climate inputs. Our models tackle both spatial and temporal challenges by incorporating the ice properties of neighbouring cells and past temporal values, thereby improving predictive accuracy. Additionally, explainable AI (XAI) techniques are applied to enhance trust and explainability. Our best‐performing model, a Random Forest with an expanding window, achieves an average RMSE of 3.36 for thickness and 65.6 for velocity and misclassifies on average only 3 grid points per year for the ice mask out of 2257, demonstrating good accuracy comparable to that of the original simulator at a fraction of the computational cost. This approach not only improves the computational efficiency of scenario modelling but also contributes to the broader field of climate science by enabling more frequent and extensive analyses of climate change impacts on Antarctic ice sheets.

Introduction

Climate change is leading to a concerning rise in sea levels (Golledge 2020; Levy et al. 2023; Hanna et al. 2024), with the melting of land ice being a primary contributor (Edwards et al. 2021). Since the 1990s, Greenland and Antarctic ice loss have contributed about 21 mm to global mean sea level rise (Otosaka et al. 2022). In the future, Antarctic ice loss is expected to be the largest contributor, reaching up to 34 cm rise by 2100 and several metres by 2300 under the high‐emission Shared Socioeconomic Pathway 5–8.5 (Fox‐Kemper et al. 2021). It is essential to gain insight into the future impacts of climate variability and global warming on ice sheets to develop effective strategies to mitigate the impacts of ongoing ice loss. Policymakers need accurate and efficient projections of ice loss to develop adaptation plans to minimise the social and economic impacts of sea‐level rise, such as relocating assets and communities and building flood‐resistant infrastructure in low‐lying coastal areas (Magnan et al. 2022). Considerable research has focused on developing numerical models to simulate ice sheets (Goelzer et al. 2020; Seroussi et al. 2024). However, most require assumptions to simplify complex physical processes. Furthermore, the high computational cost makes it challenging for existing numerical models to produce large numbers of long‐term (multi‐millennial) predictions necessary for robust uncertainty quantification.

To address this issue, we develop a proof‐of‐concept 2D machine learning (ML) emulator to simulate the spatial evolution of ice sheet thickness and flow. ML identifies complex patterns, offering an alternative to conventional numerical models that rely on intricate physical computations. By training models on historical data using advanced algorithms, ML approaches can provide timely predictions without compromising accuracy. This capability is particularly valuable for scenario‐based ensemble modelling, offering a faster alternative to traditional methods. Previous studies have explored the development of statistical emulators to better understand the future contributions of Antarctic ice loss to global sea‐level rise (Berdahl et al. 2020; Lowry et al. 2021; Edwards et al. 2021; DeConto et al. 2021). However, an explainable AI (XAI) emulator that captures the spatial–temporal variability of both the thickness and the dynamic changes of the Antarctic ice sheet has not yet been achieved.

The primary goal of this study is to emulate a low‐resolution version of a previously published simulation (Golledge et al. 2015) that used the Parallel Ice Sheet Model (PISM) (Bueler and Brown 2009). The aim is to achieve greater efficiency than the numerical model while making fewer assumptions. To accomplish this, we develop ML models using PISM‐generated ice sheet data for Antarctica spanning 86 years (2015 to 2100). The computational domain is composed of equidimensional (x, y) cells, each with associated coordinates. For each year in the dataset, every cell has three climate variables: air temperature, ocean temperature, and precipitation. We aim to predict three outputs: ice mask, ice thickness, and ice velocity. Further details on the three outputs are presented in Section 3.1 and summarised in Table 1. Related Work

Existing studies present a range of approaches to simulating the complex dynamics of the Antarctic ice sheet, from empirical models to more complex physical models. Empirical statistical models (Rahmstorf 2007; Vermeer and Rahmstorf 2009; Levermann et al. 2013; Schaeffer et al. 2012) use historical data and observed trends with linear relationships to predict future changes. While relatively simple and easy to implement, their heavy reliance on historical data means that these models may not adequately capture complex non‐linear interactions and the long‐term trends associated with future climate change. Physical models such as the PISM (Bueler and Brown 2009; Winkelmann et al. 2011), the Ice Sheet System Model (ISSM) (Larour et al. 2012), and the Community Ice Sheet Model (CISM) (Lipscomb et al. 2019) 1 are extensively used for understanding the response of the ice sheet to external factors. They encapsulate the physics of ice dynamics and their interactions with environmental indicators, enabling long‐term predictions and accommodating complex non‐linear relationships. Their design allows for scenario modelling, an important tool for analysing the impact of climate change in Antarctica.

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have led to ML solutions that efficiently process large, high‐dimensional datasets and capture complex relationships. A proof‐of‐concept study (Hu et al. 2021) developed a multi‐layer perceptron (MLP) model for surface melt over the Larsen Ice Shelf, outperforming simulations from the regional climate model RACMO2. Deep learning methods such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Physics‐Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) have become popular in climate science, capturing spatio‐temporal patterns and building physical constraints into the learning process. For example, CNNs have been used to emulate complex climate fields, while PINNs have been developed into general purpose libraries, such as PINNICLE v1.0, to solve PDE‐constrained problems (Cheng et al. 2025). Similar ideas have also been explored in related areas, such as accelerating subglacial hydrology with neural networks (Verjans and Robel 2024) and blending ML with dynamical climate forecasts (Slater et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2025). By comparison, ice sheet emulation has received much less attention, partly due to the unique challenges posed by long timescales and strong memory effects, in which past changes in thickness, flow, and grounding‐line position strongly influence future behaviour. Some recent studies have taken steps in this direction: Jouvet and Cordonnier (2023) focused on the ice‐flow solver component of the simulator and showed that CNNs can approximate higher order ice dynamics, effectively emulating the computationally expensive Glen–Stokes model used to determine ice velocity. Chawla and Holmes (2025) demonstrated how PINNs can model thickness evolution under the shallow ice approximation by approximating the partial differential equations used to describe grounded ice thickness, thereby focusing on the thickness evolution equation component. These studies are promising but focus mainly on the ice dynamics (i.e., ice flow) of small‐scale ice caps and mountain glaciers, rather than on the variability of the entire ice sheet, which includes both ice dynamics and surface mass balance processes. Furthermore, these studies retain certain physical components within their ice‐flow simulations, making them hybrid models. In contrast, our emulator is fully data‐driven, simulating changes across the entire Antarctic ice sheet to provide a more complete, comprehensive, and efficient tool for long‐term Antarctic ice sheet projections.

Table 2 compares the three approaches. Physical models are well established and provide high interpretability, but their high computational cost limits their use in ensemble simulations and rapid scenario assessments. Hybrid approaches combine physics with AI models, improving flexibility and efficiency but introducing additional complexity and remaining computationally intensive. Data‐driven approaches use ML to learn models from the data and offer advantages in efficiency and scalability. Once trained, they can generate predictions across large spatial and temporal domains quickly, achieving accuracy comparable to that of traditional models, provided that the training data are representative. While their interpretability is not as straightforward as that of physics‐based approaches, it can be improved using statistical analyses and diagnostic tools. In this work, we propose a data‐driven approach that combines AI's efficiency with explainability methods. Our approach provides transparency in predictions by offering insights into the decision‐making process. We incorporate spatial and temporal features to capture complex ice dynamics, while balancing computational efficiency, accuracy, and explainability.

Methodology

The goal of this work is to emulate the computationally intensive numerical PISM (Winkelmann et al. 2011) using efficient ML techniques.

Dataset

We first use PISM to generate an ice sheet dataset spanning 86 years, from 2015 to 2100 (Golledge et al. 2015). The simulation evolves at a rate of 1.3 model years per processor hour across two processors. For each year, outputs are written on a 51 × 51 grid (2601 grid points per year). At each grid point, we record three climate variables—precipitation, air temperature, and ocean temperature—and three ice sheet properties, ice mask, ice thickness, and ice velocity. Figure 1 shows an example output for the year 2024. We create a tabular dataset in which each grid point for each year corresponds to a row. The task of our ML model is to predict the three ice sheet properties (targets) at each grid point (X, Y) over time, using the climate variables and historical data. Table 1 describes the dataset.

Proposed ML Models

The goal of our study is to predict annual ice mask, thickness, and velocity measurements using historical data, precipitation, air, and ocean temperatures. Our research presents a solution of explainable spatio‐temporal forecasting models that incorporate exogenous variables to predict multiple target values. We evaluate three ensemble algorithms: Random Forest (Breiman 2001), CatBoost (Prokhorenkova et al. 2018), and XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin 2016).

We chose these algorithms for their ability to handle complex, noisy, and skewed data, as well as for mitigating overfitting through techniques such as bagging, boosting, and regularised objective functions. Notably, they can tolerate outliers, extreme values, and zero‐inflated data without relying on data scaling, making them well‐suited to our dataset, which exhibits these characteristics.

Random Forest constructs multiple decision trees using bagging from random bootstrap samples, while CatBoost uses gradient boosting on symmetrical trees for faster execution. XGBoost employs a similar gradient boosting framework with a regularised objective function and stands out for its execution speed. All three algorithms can generate feature importance scores, making them valuable for explaining model decisions.

Our solution comprises three models: a classification model to predict the ice mask, and two regression models for ice thickness and ice velocity. We propose that all models use the same set of input features.

Feature Engineering

In addition to using the climate variable features listed in Table 1, we engineer new features to capture the time‐series aspect of the data, the spatial characteristics, and the variable correlations. For the time‐series element, we add values from previous short‐time points (lags) and average values from previous long‐time points to smooth historical data. Similar approaches have been discussed in previous studies (Kämäräinen et al. 2023; Leites et al. 2024; Maddigan and Susnjak 2022). To capture spatial characteristics, we introduce spatial grid features that represent the average values of neighbouring coordinate points within a 3 × 3 grid surrounding each data point. We select only the nearest neighbours to capture spatial correlation at short distances, as it diminishes with increasing distance. In Antarctica, localised transitions near grounding lines and glaciers can exhibit considerable spatial and temporal variability (Miles et al. 2022; Bamber and Aspinall 2013), with notable datasets deriving measurements from the mean of neighbouring cells (Pritchard et al. 2025; Dotto et al. 2021). Therefore, given the low resolution of our dataset, the immediate nearest neighbours are expected to be the most informative. A 3 × 3 neighbourhood also provides a simple and computationally efficient feature that can be generalised across our models. To empirically support this selection, we report the RMSE for larger neighbourhoods. Specifically, we extend the neighbourhood by 2 grid points in each direction yielding a 5 × 5 grid and extend by 3 grid points in each direction yielding a 7 × 7 grid. In addition, we remove the grid feature entirely.

Using the climate values (precipitation, air temperature, and ocean temperature) and the historical target values (mask, thickness, and velocity), we create the following engineered features for predicting target values:

Through experimentation, we determined that using 4 years of lag values and 10‐year rolling averages provided sufficient predictive accuracy without increasing feature space or training time. Rolling features require data from the previous 10 years; hence, the prediction horizon of our model spans from 2025 through to 2100, a total of 76 years.

Recursive (Multi‐Step) Forecasting

In this scenario, we generate forecasts for multiple time periods ahead without updating the training set with actual values. Instead, we use the previously predicted values to retrain the model. For example, when forecasting 2026, we use the predicted 2025 values from the previous cycle. With recursive forecasting, residual errors compound over time. Therefore, we apply both the expanding window and the moving window approaches to our best‐performing algorithm, which is selected based on its one‐step ahead forecasting performance.

Both one‐step ahead and recursive forecasting scenarios retrain the models each year, which can be computationally intensive. Thus, we also explore the concept of periodic retraining for our best‐performing algorithm, where models are retrained at fixed intervals to improve efficiency. Performance Metrics

Ice Mask Classification Metrics

The precision of a class is the fraction of grid points correctly classified as belonging to the class, out of all the grid points predicted to belong to it. For each ice mask class m∈{2,3,4}, we can define the precision as

(1) Precisionm=TPmTPm+FPm where TPm and FPm represent the true positives and the false positives of the class m.

The recall of a class is the fraction of grid points predicted to belong to the class out of all the grid points that actually belong to this class. For each ice mask class m∈{2,3,4}, we can define the recall as

(2) Recallm=TPmTPm+FNm where FNm represents the false negatives of the class m.

The F1 score of a class is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, balancing the two.

(3) F1m=2×precision×recallprecision+recall

Thickness and Velocity Regression Metrics

To evaluate the accuracy of the thickness and velocity model algorithms, we report the root‐mean‐square error (RMSE), a widely used metric of model performance. RMSE provides a measure of the difference between actual and predicted values, with larger deviations being penalised more heavily than with other metrics. The RMSE for the velocity and thickness models, used to predict 2257 grid points each year, can be expressed as

(4) RMSE=12257∑i=12257(vi−v^i)2 where vi and v^i represent the actual velocity value and the predicted velocity value, respectively.

The Rank Metric

To reduce the impact of extreme values on model evaluation, we also rank algorithms by RMSE for each year (Makridakis and Hibon 1995) and then average the ranks across the 76‐year test period. We report both average metrics and ranks (lower is better).

Forecast Validity

We use the Diebold–Mariano test (Diebold and Mariano 1995) for the thickness and velocity models to determine if each model's predictions are statistically significantly different from the benchmark and competing algorithms. This test evaluates pairwise differences in forecast accuracy at each grid point over 76 years. It calculates the loss differential between the two forecasts and then evaluates a test statistic to check if the expected value of the loss differential is zero.

Model Explainability

To ensure transparency and trust in our models, we use explainability techniques to provide insights into how our models reach their conclusions. We use two approaches with our best‐performing model: (1) Random Forest's built‐in feature importance 2 based on the Gini index (an impurity‐based measure) and (2) SHAP (based on game theory) (Lundberg and Lee 2017) to obtain both global and local insights. These methods help identify which features drive ice sheet predictions, thereby increasing our understanding of ice dynamics in our models.

The thickness model with a 3 × 3 grid performed equally well with the 5 × 5 grid (RMSE 3.36) but showed higher RMSE with a 7 × 7 grid and without any grid features (3.41 and 3.39, respectively). These findings support our decision to use a 3 × 3 grid, which aligns with previous literature studies (refer Section 3.4) suggesting that nearest neighbouring cells are the most informative in areas of Antarctica with considerable spatial and temporal variability, particularly given the low resolution of our dataset.

The improvement in velocity performance with a shorter training horizon may reflect non‐stationarity or concept drift in the underlying climate data, in which recent observations are more relevant for predicting near‐future dynamics.

Random Forest Feature Importance

We provide insight into the decision‐making process of our Random Forest model with an expanding training window by analysing its feature importance scores.

Ice Mask Prediction

Figure 8 explains the mask model predictions using the Random Forest impurity‐based feature importance. We calculate variable importance values for each feature and plot the results across the 76 test sets (2025–2100). The top sub‐figure of Figure 8 shows the top five important variables for predicting the ice mask: the previous year's ice thickness, the 10‐year rolling average of ice mask, and ice masks from 1 to 2 years prior. These results explain delayed responses in ice dynamics, where the ice mask from up to 3 years prior has a strong effect because ice flow, accumulation, and melting do not cause immediate changes but unfold over multiple years. The top five variables are engineered features, which indicate that the model benefits significantly from temporal dependencies in the data.

Feature importance in predicting ice mask.

The importance of input variables is illustrated in the bottom sub‐figure of Figure 8. Air temperature is the largest contributor, with ocean temperature and precipitation appearing similar. The air temperature's direct and immediate effect on surface melting makes short‐term variations more influential than rolling or grid averages. In contrast, rolling features (long‐term averages) are more valuable for ocean temperature and precipitation, capturing long‐term climate trends and lagged effects.

Unfortunately, our model lacks features to capture the mechanisms driving this decline, such as ice shelf breakup dynamics, grounding‐line migration, or delayed ice‐ocean interactions, leading to incorrect predictions that continue to increase.

Conclusions and Future Work

In this study, we developed an explainable ML framework to emulate an example simulation using the PISM, significantly improving computational efficiency while capturing key ice sheet dynamics. Our analysis demonstrated that Random Forests effectively model ice sheet evolution, with past ice thickness, rolling climate features, and spatial neighbourhood information playing crucial roles. By applying impurity‐based feature importance and SHAP‐based analysis, we provided insights into how past ice conditions and long‐term climate trends influence predictions. Given the gradual nature of ice sheet changes, our results show that historical ice conditions remain dominant predictors, as ice mass loss, accumulation, and flow adjust over decades rather than instantaneously. The comparison between one‐step ahead and recursive forecasting further underscores the challenge of accumulated errors over time, emphasising the need for correction mechanisms in long‐term projections. Quantitatively, the Random Forest emulator reproduced ice mask classifications with an average error of only three cells per year, predicted thickness with an average RMSE of 3.36 m, and velocity with an average RMSE of 65.6 m/year across 2257 grid points. These results confirm that our data‐driven approach achieves reliable accuracy while being more than an order of magnitude faster than traditional simulations. This highlights the importance of temporal dependencies, reflecting the inherently slow response of ice sheets to climate forcing.

In future, we plan to extend our framework in several directions. We will test the emulator across different ice sheet models to evaluate its consistency and robustness and explore ways to improve generalisability using domain adaptation or transfer learning. We also aim to develop strategies for controlling forecast errors over longer timescales, for example, through adaptive correction schemes that balance accuracy with computational cost. Finally, we plan to build on our feature engineering by using approaches such as Genetic Programming to develop more advanced spatial descriptors that capture directional and long‐range effects, helping approximate processes such as fracture and grounding‐line retreat while maintaining physical consistency.
