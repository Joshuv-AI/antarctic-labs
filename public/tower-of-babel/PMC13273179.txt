Highly stretchable and conformal bioinspired kirigami structural epidermal electrodes for ECG recording during daily activities
Authors: Ziying Wang, Ze Chen, Jiameng Li, Dagong Jia, Xiaoming Hou, Zhangyi Li, Haojun Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The conformal adaptation of epidermal electrodes to the skin is crucial for achieving stable electrocardiogram (ECG) signal monitoring during exercise. Here, we present a bioinspired kirigami-structured epidermal electrode (BKSEE) specifically designed for dynamic human–machine interfaces. The BKSEE consists of three distinct layers: a copper electrode featuring a bioinspired kirigami structure (BKS), an adhesive film made of polyethylene glycol (PEG) and polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), and a PDMS anti-adhesion film. The BKSEE overcomes the limitations of existing static conformal designs for metal electrodes. It simultaneously offers low stiffness for adaptation of skin, the combined advantages of a high filling area and stable dynamic contact, as well as excellent deformability, favorable microscopic conformability, and reliable adhesion. By meeting the multidimensional requirements for skin-conformal fitting, it can collect ECG signals in real time and stably during daily activities, and demonstrates remarkable resistance to sweat and motion artifacts.

With the advancement of personalized and remote healthcare monitoring technologies1,2, such as electrocardiograms (ECG)3,4, electroencephalograms (EEG)5, and electromyograms (EMG)6–8, the integration of electronic devices with irregular, flexible surfaces has become increasingly crucial for a variety of emerging applications, including wearable electronics9–11, soft robotics12,13, and human–machine interfaces14,15. Epidermal electrodes, a class of wearable electronic devices, are designed to adhere to the irregular, soft surfaces of biological tissues to measure physiological electrical signals. This necessitates that epidermal electrodes exhibit highly precise and reliable monitoring capabilities. However, achieving this goal requires overcoming the challenge of noise interference generated by limb movements during daily activities16. These issues typically stem from the conformal adaptation of human–machine interfaces. For instance, the tightness of adhesion between epidermal electrodes and the skin can significantly influence the accuracy of contact impedance characterization6. Additionally, inadequate contact between the epidermal electrodes and the skin can result in relative movement at the human–machine interface, causing unpredictable variations in motion artifacts17,18.

To address these challenges, both domestic and international research efforts have concentrated on refining electrode materials and optimizing electrode structures. These electrodes exhibit exceptional adhesion strength, low elastic modulus, remarkable ductility, and outstanding flexibility, allowing them to form highly conformal interfaces with the irregular surfaces of biological tissues. For example, epidermal electrodes made from organic materials (e.g., poly(3,4- ethylenedioxythiophene): poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT: PSS)19–21, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)22,23, polyacrylamide (PAM) and Ecoflex24–26, etc.), exhibit the ability to form highly adherent and conformable interfaces with biological tissues. While these materials are widely regarded as promising electrode candidates, they often exhibit insufficient electronic performance or limited stability in humid environments27. As a result, traditional rigid conductive materials like copper and gold have been extensively used. However, their minimal stretchability prevents them from forming highly conformal interfaces with the skin. The interface structures that have evolved over billions of years in biological systems provide natural templates for adapting artificial devices to dynamic surfaces. This biomimetic approach has been validated in the field of electrodes, as demonstrated by deformable structures such as wrinkled28–30, waved31, or serpentine structures32. While these structures enhance macroscopic stretchability, they all fall short in providing microscopic conformability33,34.

Here, inspired by the biomimetic structures of snake abdomens and armadillo skin, and leveraging the cold processing capabilities and high precision of femtosecond laser technology35–37, the BKSEE was designed and fabricated. Through topological optimization, the BKSEE maintains a stable contact area even under dynamic stretching conditions. Notably, its mechanical properties provide the electrode with the dual advantages of a large fill area and excellent dynamic contact stability. This solution systematically fulfills the multidimensional core requirements for conformal adaptation of epidermal electrodes in wearable biosensing applications. This solution systematically addresses the multidimensional core requirements for conformal adaptation. The BKS copper electrode was subjected to simulation and experimental analysis, and its mechanical adaptation to the skin was evaluated in terms of deformability and micro-compliance. To ensure strong and reliable adhesion, we integrated the BKS copper electrode with an adhesion layer composed of PEG and PDMS blended in precise ratios. When attached to the human chest, the epidermal electrode enables stable and reliable real-time ECG signal recording. Compared to previously reported fractal-structured and mesh-structured epidermal electrodes, BKSEE exhibits lower noise levels during dynamic recordings38–41. Notably, during daily activities such as walking and cycling, it accurately captures heart rate measurements.

Design of skin conformal and motion-adaptive BKSEE

BKSEE is a single-lead wearable epidermal electrode, which exhibits excellent resistance to sweat and motion interference in Fig. 1a. It can monitor ECG signals from the left side of the human chest. BKSEE consists of a PDMS anti-adhesion layer, a PEG & PDMS adhesion layer, a copper electrode layer, a copper interconnect layer, and a polyimide (PI) insulating layer, as illustrated in Fig. 1b. The Janus patch, composed of an anti-adhesion layer and an adhesion layer, exhibits reliable adhesion properties in both dry and humid environments. Surprisingly, BKSEE has good interfacial adhesion under dynamic conditions. In order to enhance the stretchability and microscopic conformability of BKSEE, the copper electrode layer is engineered with a bioinspired kirigami structure. The conformal adaptation between epidermal electrodes and the three-dimensional dynamic surface of skin is the key to realizing the long-term stable monitoring of ECG signals. The three-dimensional movement of pectoral muscles sets the significance of researching the mechanical properties of epidermal electrodes. Such complexity of muscle movement requires the electrode to be able to capture the deformation of muscles in a three-dimensional way. Figure 1c illustrates the movement of pectoral muscles during two stages, contraction and expansion. During the contraction phase, skin covering the pectoral muscles is relaxed in the x–y plane and exhibits protrusion in the z-axis. Instead, during the expansion phase, skin covering the pectoral muscles is stretched in the x–y plane and relaxed in the z-axis. Therefore, rigid copper foils attached to the skin of the chest require exceptional flexibility and stretchability. Although the design of wrinkled and serpentine structures can improve the stretchability of copper foils, they cannot offer a combination of a large active surface of the electrode and excellent microscopic conformability due to out-of-plane buckling42–44.

To overcome this, we drew inspiration from bionics to design a kirigami structure with deformable properties for copper foil. The design was primarily inspired by two biological structures: the armadillo shell and the ventral scales of snakes. The armadillo shell consists of an interwoven structure of rigid mineralized tissue and flexible collagen fibers45. When subjected to external impacts, the highly rigid mineralized layer undergoes minimal deformation, while the interwoven, extremely flexible collagen fibers deform through bending and stretching. This ensures that the mineralized layer maintains its overall planar shape. The ventral scales of snakes exhibit “V” shaped structures at the ends of their hexagonal scale pattern, which open or close when the snake’s belly stretches or compresses. This behavior closely resembles the deformation mechanism of kirigami structures46,47. Based on these observations, we designed the copper foil body as an array of 1 mm-diameter disks, mimicking the armadillo’s rigid mineralized structure, as illustrated in Figs. 1dⅰ and S3a. Simultaneously, we developed a flexible, as illustrated in Fig. 1dⅱ, expandable connecting joint inspired by the hexagonal pattern of the snake’s belly (Fig. S3b–d). This joint functions like the flexible collagen fiber tissue within the armadillo’s carapace, enabling flexible connections between disk units and facilitating overall deformation. Figure 1e illustrates the deformation mechanism of the BKS copper electrode in both the x–y plane and the z-axis.

Structural and mechanical properties of the BKS copper electrode

The mechanical properties of copper electrodes with flexible joints are influenced by the design of the flexible joint structures. In order to achieve optimal adaptation of the mechanical properties of the flexible joint to the skin, including elastic modulus and strain range, three distinct kirigami flexible joint structures based on span length, named Kirigami1, Kirigami2, and Kirigami3, were designed. Furthermore, considering the out-of-plane buckling influenced by the ratio of line width to thickness, as well as the machining precision of the available equipment, the line widths of these three types of flexible joints are all set to 0.04 mm. Firstly, finite-element simulations of uniaxial tension were conducted to analyze the magnitude and distribution of three different flexible joint structures. The results, presented in Fig. 2a, reveal that under 15% uniaxial tension, strain in all three structures is primarily concentrated along the “V” lines of the structure. Notably, Kirigami2 exhibits a maximum true strain of just 0.365, significantly lower than the true strain values observed for both Kirigami1 and Kirigami3. Next, we performed tensile tests to investigate the relationship between stress and strain for three different flexible joint structures. The results, shown in Fig. 2b, indicate that Kirigami1 exhibits a maximum strain range of 32.93%, which falls within the strain range of human skin. However, when the strain reaches 26.82%, the structure not only experiences its peak stress of 573.42 MPa, significantly higher than the maximum stress recorded for Kirigami2 (457.92 MPa) and Kirigami3 (449.65 MPa), but also begins to crack. Kirigami2, on the other hand, achieves a maximum strain range of 26.35%, significantly higher than Kirigami3 19.24%. Considering the stress–strain characteristics of the three structures and the strain range of human skin, the results indicate that Kirigami2 is the optimal design. Furthermore, the microscopic conformability of Kirigami2 was evaluated by using a scanning electron microscope (SEM). To verify that the electrodes did not experience out-of-plane buckling during the entire elastic deformation stage, we utilized the irreversible nature of their plastic deformation as counter-evidence. Figure 2d shows that tearing occurs only at the rigid tile connections throughout the tensile fracture process of the flexible hinge. Furthermore, its maximum tensile strain is ~27%, which fails to meet the 30% strain requirement necessary for skin applications48,49. To reduce the tensile stiffness of the copper electrode and expand its strain range, we drew inspiration from the deformation of the “V” line in the hexagonal pattern. Specifically, we replaced the Kirigami2 flexible joints in sections ①–④ of Fig. S4 with arc-shaped flexible joints and removed the excess Kirigami2 flexible joints. As shown in Fig. 2e, the BKS copper electrode comprising rigid tiles, Kirigami2 flexible joints, and arc-shaped flexible joints not only exhibits exceptional mechanical properties but also achieves an active surface area of up to 32.66%.

As illustrated in Fig. 3c, the stress–strain curve of the BKS copper electrode demonstrates significant nonlinearity, which arises from the interplay of two distinct types of flexible joints. When the strain is below 30%, the tensile stiffness of the BKS copper electrode is a mere 3.8 × 10-³ N mm-¹, demonstrating exceptional flexibility. At this stage, its shape is primarily governed by the rotational deformation of the less rigid arc-shaped flexible joints, while the more rigid Kirigami2 flexible joints remain undeformed. When the strain exceeds 30%, the rotational deformation of the arc-shaped flexible joints reaches its limit, causing the Kirigami2 flexible joints to gradually experience stretching deformation. The tensile stiffness increases sharply until the strain reaches 53.82%, at which point gradual cracking begins to occur. Figure 3e shows the images of the BKS copper electrode before and after tensile fracture, while Fig. 3d illustrates the force curve when the electrode is bent to 130°. As the bending radius decreases, the bending force gradually increases, ultimately reaching a maximum of just 0.018 N, which exhibits the electrode’s exceptional flexibility.

Based on simulations and testing, the results indicate that when the strain is below 30%, the deformation of the BKS copper electrode is primarily driven by reversible elastic deformation at the flexible joints, while the rigid tiles remain virtually strain-free. This characteristic ensures stable and reliable conformal interfaces. In addition, the mechanical properties of the BKS copper electrode can be adapted to the skin, including strain range, tensile stiffness, and bending stiffness.

Additionally, to prove the safety for multiple uses, we evaluated the electronic properties of the Kirigami flexible joints and the BKS copper electrode. The resistance of Kirigami2 flexible joints exhibited <1% change when subjected to elongation at a rate of 0.1 mm min−1 (Fig. S7). Furthermore, electrical measurements conducted under bending, stretching, and 1000 cycles at 30% strain confirmed the reliability of the BKS copper electrode, as illustrated in Fig. 3f–h.

Design and mechanical properties of the Janus adhesive patch

Due to the thickness of the BKS copper electrodes, relying solely on native adhesion mechanisms, such as van der Waals and electrostatic forces50,51, is insufficient to ensure secure bio-attachment. Therefore, we designed a Janus adhesive patch composed of PDMS and PEG, which possesses a low elastic modulus and high tensile strength, to ensure sufficient adhesion strength for the device.

The degree of cross-linking during the curing process of PDMS is influenced by the extent of the reaction between the vinyl groups and hydrosiloxane50. When a specific amount of PEG is incorporated into the PDMS precursor and subsequently cured, the resulting solution forms a low crosslinked network due to PEG’s impact on the catalytic activity of platinum51 (Fig. S8). Benefiting from the abundant free molecular and pendant chains within the network, these chains form stable bonding interfaces with the skin surface through multiple non-covalent interactions, such as physical entanglement and hydrogen bonding (Fig. S9). This endows the material with outstanding skin adhesion properties. In this study, to fabricate adhesive films with exceptional mechanical properties, we mixed the curing agent and PEG in five distinct ratios: 1:9, 2:8, 3:7, 4:6, and 5:5. The mechanical properties of the resulting films were then evaluated. Given that the films prepared with a 5:5 ratio are virtually tack-free, we focused our analysis on the first four ratios. Fig. S10 shows the adhesion tests of the films with a 1:9 ratio. The results revealed that these films not only exhibit poor adhesion properties but also tend to leave residues on the skin’s surface, which interfered with stable adhesion at the interface. Figure 4a–c illustrates the adhesion tests of films prepared with 2:8, 3:7, and 4:6 ratios, respectively. The results indicate that the 2:8 ratio films exhibited superior adhesion performance in peel, shear, and pull-off tests under both wet and dry conditions, compared to the other ratios. To further validate the sweat resistance of the adhesive layer, we conducted underwater adhesion strength tests on pig skin (Video 1). The results showed a maximum peel force of 3.5 kPa, confirming reliable adhesion in highly humid environments. Additionally, external force peel tests on the adhesive layer integrated onto PDMS films (Fig. S11) demonstrated that the skin-contacting portion remained firmly adhered throughout the peeling process, with no expansion of interfacial gaps or film slippage observed. Next, we evaluated the stretch properties of films with ratios of 2:8, 3:7, and 4:6. Figure S13 illustrates the variation in elongation at break across these different ratios, showing a decreasing trend as the PEG content decreases. Similarly, Fig. S14 illustrates the changes in modulus, revealing an increasing trend with lower PEG content. When one side of the adhesive film is bonded to the BKS copper electrode, the opposite side must remain non-adhesive to prevent unintended attachment. To achieve this, we integrated a fully cross-linked PDMS film, only 100 μm thick, on this side. The final Janus adhesive patch is shown in Fig. S15.

Finally, we integrated the BKS copper electrode with a Janus adhesive patch and evaluated its ability to conform to the dynamic surfaces of the skin. The electrode patches, fabricated through this integrated process, exhibited exceptional flexibility and stretchability, as shown in Fig. 4d, ensuring a snug fit. Initially, both patches adhered well to the surface of the arm. Even when the skin was compressed or distorted, they maintained a secure and stable fit, demonstrating outstanding deformability and adaptability to motion, as shown in Fig. 4e. When applied to finger joints, the patches provided strong adhesion and seamlessly adapted to dynamic hand movements, such as extension and grip, as illustrated in Fig. 4f.

Conclusions

Here, we reported a BKS copper electrode that integrates rigid tiles, kirigami flexible joints, and circular flexible joints. When integration with a Janus adhesive patch, the resulting epidermal electrodes demonstrate exceptional conformity to dynamic skin. This is achieved through the strategic incorporation of two types of flexible joints that connect the rigid tiles. On one hand, the BKSEE enables reversible elastic deformation within a strain range of 30%, ensuring excellent microscopic conformability and electrical stability, with resistance variation remaining below 1%. On the other hand, it exhibits remarkable mechanical deformability of up to 53.82% and an ultra-low tensile stiffness of 3.8 × 10⁻³ N mm-¹. Additionally, it achieves an active surface area of 32.66%, which is 10.83% greater than that of fractal designs with the same line width. Additionally, Table S1 compares the mechanical properties of BKSEE with those of electrodes featuring different structures. The results demonstrate that BKSEE exhibits superior overall mechanical performance, particularly its exceptional conformability to skin surfaces. Furthermore, by integrating the copper electrode layer with the adhesion layer, the BKS copper electrode achieves exceptional wet and dry adhesion performance along with outstanding adhesion stability.

Leveraging their exceptional skin adhesion, high mechanical deformability, and durability, the BKSEE can seamlessly adapt to dynamic body movements. This capability enables real-time, stable ECG recording during daily activities while exhibiting excellent resistance to sweat and motion artifacts. Therefore, we anticipate that the bioinspired kirigami design will play a crucial role in advancing the integration of electronic devices with irregular and flexible surfaces.

Materials and methods

Materials

PDMS base liquid (DOWSIL™ 184 Silicone Elastomer Base) and curing agent (DOWSIL™ 184 Silicone Elastomer Curing Agent) were obtained from Dow Corning. PVA, Ethanol, PEG (Mw = 400) were obtained from Kaimat (Tianjin) Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. Flexible Copper Clad Laminate (FCCL, PI coated with copper foil) and copper foil were obtained from Metal Materials (Hefei) Trading Co., Ltd. Water-soluble tape was obtained from Aquasol Corporation.

Equipment

The patterns of copper interconnects and copper electrodes were prepared by picosecond laser (YLCF3030UP, Wuhan Yuanlu Optoelectronics Technology Co., Ltd, China). The pattern of the janus adhesive substrate was prepared by a laser marking machine (BJ-E30W, Liaocheng Baiju Laser Equipment Co., Ltd, China). The kirigami flexible joints were visualized by field emission scanning electron (SIGMA, Zeiss AG, Germany; MIRA 3 LMH, Tescan AG, Czech Republic). The tensile properties and adhesive properties were measured by a universal testing machine (TY-2, Jinan Hanson Precision Instruments Co., Ltd., China). The resistance was measured by an electrochemical workstation (CHI760E, Shanghai Chenhua Instrument Co., Ltd., China).

Fabrication of copper interconnects and copper electrodes

Figure S1a and b illustrate the fabrication process of copper interconnects and copper electrodes. Firstly, a PVA precursor was prepared by dissolving a precise amount of PVA powder in deionized water, followed by thorough stirring. The glass substrate was then cleaned and secured for spin-coating. The well-mixed PVA solution was spin-coated onto the cleaned glass substrate at 800 rpm and subsequently cured at 80 °C, forming an adhesive layer. Next, a copper foil and a flexible copper clad laminate (FCCL) were adhered to separate glass substrates in a flat configuration. Finally, femtosecond laser technology was employed to fabricate copper interconnects on the FCCL and electrode layer on the copper foil. The laser power is set to 1200 W. The pulse width is specified as 85 fs, with the pulse energy typically controlled at 300 μJ. The scanning speed is set to 500 mm/s, and the spot diameter is 5 μm.

Preparation of PDMS anti-adhesion layer

As illustrated in Fig. S1c, the base liquid and curing agent of PDMS were mixed in a beaker at a 10:1 ratio and thoroughly stirred. The well-mixed precursor was then degassed under vacuum for one hour to eliminate air bubbles. Next, the degassed solution was spin-coated onto a cleaned glass substrate at 600 rpm and cured in an oven at 120 °C for 10 min. Finally, the PDMS anti-adhesion layer was fabricated.

Preparation of PEG and PDMS adhesion layer

Firstly, the curing agent and PEG were mixed in five different ratios: 1:9, 2:8, 3:7, 4:6, and 5:5. Each mixture was then combined with the PDMS base solution in a 1:10 ratio and thoroughly stirred. After degassing under vacuum for one hour, the precursor was spin-coated onto the PDMS anti-adhesion film on a glass substrate at 600 rpm and cured in an oven at 80 °C for 60 min, resulting in the formation of the PEG and PDMS bonding layer. The preparation process is shown in Fig. S1d.

Transfer printing of copper interconnects and copper electrodes

Firstly, the glass substrates with copper interconnects and electrodes were immersed in water to dissolve the PVA adhesive. Next, water-soluble tape was applied to the surfaces of the copper interconnects and electrodes, facilitating their transfer from the glass substrate to the tape. The water-soluble tapes, now holding the copper interconnects and electrodes, were then adhered to the surface of the PEG and PDMS adhesion film at the desired locations. Subsequently, the assembly was submerged in water to remove the water-soluble tapes. The copper interconnects and electrodes were then soldered together. Finally, the Janus adhesive substrate was fabricated through laser patterning according to the specified layout, and any excess material was carefully removed. The preparation process is shown in Fig. S1e.

Finite element simulation

The magnitude and distribution of strain from the flexible joint structure and the electrode were calculated, respectively, by using ABAQUS software. We analyzed a part of the model to reduce the simulation calculation time. Through uniaxial tensile testing, we measured the elastic modulus of copper foil to be 51,975.60 MPa, with a yield stress of 154.44 MPa (plastic strain of 1.18 × 10⁻⁵ mm) and Poisson’s ratio of 0.35. Both the flexible joint and the electrode layer were set to a thickness of 10 μm. Next, we fully fixed the left end of the flexible joint to constrain its six degrees of freedom, applying a 15% horizontal displacement load at the right end. For the electrode layer, one end was fixed to prevent lateral displacement during deformation, while the other end was subjected to a uniaxial tensile load with 30% displacement and a bending load with a 130° bending angle.

Measurement of mechanical characterization

Tensile and bending measurements for the copper foil, flexible joints, BKS copper electrodes, and PEG and PDMS adhesion layer were conducted using a universal testing machine. The samples were secured in the machine’s jaws and then stretched at a predetermined speed (copper material: 1 mm min−1, organic material: 10 mm min−1) and bent at a 1 mm min−1 speed. An electrochemical workstation was connected to the flexible joints and BKS copper electrodes to record the resistance in real time.

Measurement of the work of adhesion

To evaluate and calculate adhesion strength, adhesive films were affixed to polyimide (PI) substrates (15 mm × 50 mm) and glass substrates (30 mm × 30 mm), respectively. Custom fixtures were fabricated using 3D printing technology. Prior to adhesion testing, the skin was cleaned with a solution of deionized water and ethanol. For wet skin testing, water was sprayed onto the surface, and any excess droplets were removed. For the peel tests, the PI substrate with adhesive films was applied to the skin on the arm, with one end secured in the machine’s jaw. The substrate was then peeled away vertically at a speed of 2 mm min−1 until complete detachment occurred. For the shear tests, the procedure was similar to that of the peel tests, except that the substrate remained parallel to the direction of velocity of the jaw. For the pull-off tests, the glass substrate with the adhesive film was applied to the skin on the arm and removed using a custom fixture at a speed of 1 mm min−1 until full separation. For the underwater pull-off tests, the glass substrate with the adhesive film was fixed in the upper machine jaw, while the pigskin-covered glass substrate was positioned at the bottom of a tank. The entire force measurement system was then submerged in water prior to commencing the pull-off tests.

The ECG measurement

For the measurements, signals were recorded by connecting an Arduino Uno and an ADS1292R Development Board. The potential detected was delivered to the Development Board through cables. A USB cable was connected to a laptop, and the signals were recorded by the software.
