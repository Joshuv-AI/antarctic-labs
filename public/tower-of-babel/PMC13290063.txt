Real-World Patient Characteristics, Mutational Landscape, and Outcomes in Advanced/Metastatic HER2-Mutant Non–Small Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Christine M. Lovly, Christina Baik, Misako Nagasaka, Tejas Patil, Sonia S. Maruti, Stephen Stanhope, Neslihan A. Kaya, Stephan Herbertz, Beth Nordstrom, Kathryn Evans, Xiuning Le
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEThis observational study assessed the real-world characteristics, treatments, and outcomes of US patients with HER2-mutant advanced non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) overall and according to HER2 mutation type (tyrosine kinase domain [TKD] and non-TKD).METHODSDeidentified data were extracted for patients with advanced/metastatic NSCLC from the Flatiron Health-Foundation Medicine NSCLC Clinico-Genomic Database. Patients with oncogenic HER2 mutations were included. The primary objectives were to assess the prevalence of HER2 mutations and coaberrations, treatment patterns, and real-world overall survival (OS).RESULTSOverall, 559/14,768 (3.8%) patients had HER2 mutations; 262 (1.8%) were oncogenic. Patients with oncogenic TKD mutations (n = 197) were more frequently younger, female, and never-smokers than those with oncogenic non-TKD mutations (n = 65) and had fewer oncogenic coaberrations. Among patients with oncogenic HER2 mutations who underwent first-line treatment (n = 193), most received platinum-based chemoimmunotherapy (30.5%) or chemotherapy alone (27.9%); 119 patients (61.7%) received second-line treatment. Median OS after first and second lines of treatment was 13.5 months (95% CI, 11.6 to 16.9) and 11.1 months (95% CI, 9.2 to 13.6), respectively. Median OS with first-line platinum-based chemoimmunotherapy was 21.1 (95% CI, 12.2 to NA) and 11.7 months (95% CI, 8.3 to NA) in patients with TKD/non-TKD mutations, respectively, and median OS with platinum-based chemotherapy alone was 9.1 (95% CI, 5.7 to 16.0) and 17.3 (95% CI, 13.6 to NA) months, respectively.CONCLUSIONNSCLC patients with oncogenic TKD HER2 mutations had different characteristics and genetic features than patients with non-TKD mutations. Real-world outcomes with first- and second-line standard-of-care treatment were suboptimal, highlighting the need for new treatment options for patients with advanced HER2-mutant NSCLC.

INTRODUCTION

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2 also known as ErbB2), encoded by the ERBB2/HER2 gene (hereafter referred to as HER2), is a member of the ErbB family of receptor tyrosine kinases and is an important oncogenic driver.1-3 In non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), HER2 mutations are reported in approximately 2%-4% of cases4-6; the most prevalent mutations are exon 20 insertions within the tyrosine kinase domain (TKD).7 Although HER2 mutation testing in NSCLC is recommended,8 it is often not performed,9,10 possibly reflecting a paucity of HER2-directed treatments in this setting. At the time of writing, no such agents are approved for first-line (1L) treatment of HER2-mutant NSCLC and patients generally receive 1L chemotherapy with or without immunotherapy.11,12 Three HER2-directed or HER2-targeted agents are available for previously treated advanced/metastatic HER2-mutant NSCLC. The HER2-directed antibody-drug conjugate, trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd),13,14 received accelerated approval in the United States in 2022. The HER2-selective tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) zongertinib15,16 and the HER2/EGFR TKI sevabertinib17,18 have recently received accelerated approval in the United States based on the Beamion LUNG-1 and SOHO-01 trials, respectively.15,17 Zongertinib is also approved in China (conditional) and Japan for patients with previously treated advanced/metastatic HER2-mutant NSCLC.

Several real-world evidence (RWE) studies have evaluated HER2-mutant NSCLC.4,6,11,19-30 However, some lacked detailed genomic profiling, assessment of clinical outcomes according to line of treatment (LOT), and survival data. Also, although some studies indicate that type of HER2 mutation can influence sensitivity to treatment7,22,31; few data exist regarding disease characteristics and outcomes in patients with non-TKD or non–exon 20 TKD mutations.20-22,26 Other evidence gaps include tumor mutational burden (TMB) and comutation profiles. Here, we report the results of an RWE study to better characterize patients with advanced/metastatic HER2-mutant NSCLC in real-world clinical practice.

METHODS

Study Design

This was a noninterventional/observational cohort study of patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC with HER2 mutations. Patients were diagnosed between January 1, 2015, and June 30, 2022. To allow for ≥1 year of follow-up, data cutoff was June 30, 2023 (Fig 1A).

Patient selection and study design. (A) Study design schematic. (B) Flowchart of cohort selection. *For 1L and 2L cohorts, the index date was defined as date of LOT initiation. 1L, first-line; 2L, second-line; a/m, advanced/metastatic; HER2, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2; NSCLC, non–small cell lung cancer; onc, oncogenic mutation; TKD, tyrosine kinase domain.

The primary data source was the Flatiron Health-Foundation Medicine Clinico-Genomic Database (FH-FMI CGDB).32 FH-FMI CGDB contains processed, deidentified longitudinal electronic medical records data from approximately 280 US cancer clinics. Data are linked to the Foundation Medicine FoundationCore genomic profiling database via deidentified, deterministic matching.32,33 OncoKB was used to annotate oncogenic function.34

This study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practice guidelines. Institutional review board approval was obtained from the WCG Institutional Review Board,35 with a waiver of informed consent for use of deidentified data. Reporting was undertaken according to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidance.

Patient Selection Criteria and Cohorts

The NSCLC cohort comprised adults with histologically/cytologically confirmed stage (IIIB-IVB) NSCLC with a record of treatment or visit ≤90 days after diagnosis. American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage was defined at diagnosis according to the treating physician.

The HER2-mutant cohort comprised patients with ≥1 nonsynonymous HER2 mutation. A subgroup of these patients was identified with oncogenic or likely oncogenic HER2 mutations as per OncoKB (henceforth referred to as the HER2-oncogenic [onc] mutant cohort) and was the main focus of these analyses, to be more clinically relevant. If more than one blood or solid tissue NGS assay result was available, the test closest to index date (the mapped NGS test) was used to determine HER2 status.

The 1L cohort comprised patients in the HER2-onc mutant cohort who initiated 1L treatment between January 1, 2015, and June 30, 2022, and had undergone NGS before or within 1 month of 1L initiation. The 2L cohort comprised patients in the 1L cohort who subsequently initiated 2L treatment during the same period.

Additional cohorts were also analyzed (secondary objectives). The HER2 wild-type cohort comprised NSCLC cohort patients without HER2 mutations or amplification. The HER2-amplified cohort comprised NSCLC cohort patients without HER2 mutations but with HER2 amplification (≥6 copy-number alterations [solid tissue NGS]).

Variables

Variables included number and type of LOT, patient characteristics, and biomarker status. We also evaluated comutations (nonsynonymous, regardless of pathogenicity) from the mapped NGS, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) status, and TMB.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were the prevalence of oncogenic HER2 mutations and coaberrations, treatment patterns, and overall survival (OS) in patients with HER2-mutant NSCLC. Secondary outcomes included progression-free survival (PFS), time to treatment discontinuation (TTD), and time to next treatment (TTNT). OS and PFS outcomes have been previously validated.36,37

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive analyses were performed in the HER2-mutant cohort and the HER2-onc mutant cohort, overall and grouped according to TKD (exons 18-21) or non-TKD mutations and by treatment (LOT and regimens). 95% CIs for prevalence were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method. OS, PFS, TTD, and TTNT were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. The frequency of genetic co-aberrations in the TKD and non-TKD groups were compared using the Fisher's exact test. For indication-based cohorts the index date was the date of diagnosis of NSCLC. For 1L and 2L cohorts, the index date was the date of LOT initiation. OS was defined as the time from index date to date of death and left truncation was corrected for using NGS report date as the delayed entry date. Specimen date was used for sensitivity analyses. For patients with no record of death, OS was censored at the date of last record before study end. PFS was defined as the time from the index date to the first documented disease progression or death, per treating clinician. For patients with no record of disease progression or death, PFS was censored at the latest date when progression was assessed before the end of the study period. TTD was defined as the time from start of LOT to end of LOT, death, or the end of the study period. TTNT was defined as the difference between subsequent LOT start dates. For patients with no record of further treatment or death, TTD and TTNT were censored at the end of the study period. This was a descriptive study; no formal hypothesis testing was performed. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.1 or higher (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

To assess generalizability of the HER2-mutant cohort genomic data from the FH-FMI CGDB, deidentified genomic data from 61,717 patients with NSCLC (lung adenocarcinoma) were obtained using FoundationInsights, a web-based platform for interrogation of the broader FoundationCore database.38

RESULTS

Patient Disposition

A total of 14,768 adult patients in the FH-FMI CGDB were diagnosed with advanced/metastatic NSCLC (comprising the NSCLC cohort, Fig 1B). Of these, 559 (3.8%) patients had HER2 mutations (HER2-mutant cohort) and 262 (1.8%) had oncogenic HER2 mutations (HER2-onc mutant cohort). Patient characteristics in the HER2-mutant cohort, HER2-amplified cohort, and HER2 wild-type cohort are shown in the Data Supplement (Table S1).

HER2 Mutation Landscape: Frequency and Types of HER2 Mutations

To characterize the mutational landscape of the HER2-mutant cohort (589 mutations across 559 patients; 24 had multiple variants of whom four patients had a combination of two oncogenic/likely oncogenic HER2 mutations per OncoKB annotation), we plotted the distribution of mutation types and their oncogenic function (Fig 2A). The most frequently detected variants that had known oncogenic function per OncoKB annotation were A775_G776insYVMA (also known as Y772_A775DUP, 18.5%), S310F (4.1%), G776>VC (3.7%), and S310Y (2.0%; Table 1, Fig 2A). TKD mutations were more often oncogenic or likely oncogenic than non-TKD mutations (Fig 2B). Among 243 (41.3%) TKD mutations, of which 74.9% occurred in exon 20, 184 (75.7%) were classified as oncogenic, 14 (5.8%) as likely oncogenic, 39 (16.0%) as unknown, 5 (2.1%) as inconclusive, and 1 (0.4%) as resistant. Although there were more non-TKD mutations in this general cohort, most non-TKD mutations (73.4%) had unknown oncogenic status. Among 346 (58.7%) non-TKD mutations, 46 (13.3%) were classified as oncogenic, 21 (6.1%) as likely oncogenic, 254 (73.4%) as unknown, 15 (4.3%) as inconclusive, and 10 (2.9%) as likely neutral. Details of observed HER2 mutations are reported in the Data Supplement (Table S2).

Comutations and Amplifications in Patients With Oncogenic HER2-Mutant NSCLC

For all subsequent analyses of patient characteristics and outcomes, we focused on patients with oncogenic or likely oncogenic mutations (HER2-onc mutant cohort) to ensure greater clinical relevance. In this cohort (n = 262), patients with TKD and non-TKD mutations appeared to have different tumor genetics. Concurrent HER2 amplification was more common in patients with TKD mutations (12.7%) than in those with non-TKD mutations (3.1%; P = .03; Fig 2C). Patients with TKD mutations had significantly fewer comutations than patients with non-TKD mutations EGFR (3.6% v 18.5%; P = .0003), KRAS (4.6% v 15.4%; P = .01), and PIK3CA mutations (3.6% v 12.3%; P = .01; Table 2; Fig 2D); TMB ≥10 mutations/megabase for TKD patients was 18.3% versus 62.2% for non–TKD-mutant patients (P < .0001; Fig 2E). Specific KRAS and EGFR variants are described in the Data Supplement (Table S3).

Characteristics of Patients With Oncogenic HER2-Mutant NSCLC

Patients with oncogenic TKD and non-TKD mutations appeared to have different demographic and clinical characteristics. Patients with TKD mutations appeared to be younger (<65 years at diagnosis: TKD/non-TKD: 40.6%/26.2%), with a higher proportion of females (64.0%/52.3%), never-smokers (51.8%/21.5%), and tumors with nonsquamous histology (97.0%/75.4%) than patients with non-TKD mutations (Table 2; Fig 2E).

Patients in the HER2-amplified and HER2 wild-type groups more closely resembled the non-TKD mutation group than the TKD group, with numerically comparable proportions of females, never-smokers, and tumors with squamous histology or high TMB (Fig. 2E, Data Supplement, Table S1). The frequency of brain metastases was numerically comparable across cohorts (21.3%, 15.4%, 22.3%, and 17.0% in the HER2-onc TKD, HER2-onc non-TKD, HER2-amplified, and HER2 wild-type cohorts, respectively).

Among patients in the HER2-onc mutant cohort, most mapped NGS tests used tissue (n = 202, 77.1%) rather than blood samples (n = 60, 22.9%). Most patients (n = 235, 89.7%) had records from only one NGS test. For most patients, samples were taken around the time of diagnosis (Data Supplement, Fig S2). FMI assays and bait sets used are shown in the Data Supplement (Table S4).

Among patients in the HER2-onc mutant cohort, 211 (80.5%) were treated at community centers, 33 (12.6%) at academic-only centers, and 18 (6.9%) at combined academic and community centers. Most patients (n = 211, 80.5%) received systemic treatment for advanced disease; 40.8% of these patients received one LOT, 28.4% two LOTs, and 30.8% three or more total LOTs over the study period.

1L Treatment in Patients With Oncogenic HER2 Mutations

Of patients in the HER2-onc mutant cohort, 193 (73.7%) patients had a record of treatment or visit ≤90 days after diagnosis and were thus included in the 1L cohort (Table 2; patient flow is shown in Fig 1B). Of these, 142 (73.6%) patients had a TKD mutation and 51 (26.4%) patients had a non-TKD mutation. Median time from diagnosis to treatment initiation was 1.1 months (interquartile range, 0.8-1.8) with no difference between the TKD and non-TKD groups. Most patients received platinum-based chemotherapy, with (n = 59, 30.5%) or without (n = 54, 27.9%) immunotherapy. Treatment patterns were generally numerically comparable among TKD and non-TKD patients (Table 3).

2L Treatment in Patients With Oncogenic HER2 Mutations

Of 119 patients included in the 2L cohort, 92 (77.3%) had TKD HER2 mutations and 27 (22.7%) had non-TKD HER2 mutations. The most common 2L treatment was immunotherapy with or without non–platinum-based chemotherapy/vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitors (n = 43, 36.0%), most commonly nivolumab (n = 21, 17.6%; Table 3). Most patients who previously received 1L platinum-based chemotherapy with immunotherapy switched to a different class of 2L treatment (Data Supplement, Table S6).

Median OS from the start of 2L treatment was 11.1 months (95% CI, 9.2 to 13.6; Fig 3C) overall, 10.8 months (95% CI, 9.1 to 15.4) in patients with TKD mutations (n = 92), and 12.9 months (95% CI, 8.4 to 32.8) in patients with non-TKD mutations (n = 27). Among patients who previously received 1L platinum-based chemotherapy, median OS was 10.1 months (95% CI, 7.9 to 23.5) in those with TKD mutations (n = 58) and 13.6 months (95% CI, 9.8 to NA) in those with non-TKD mutations (n = 18). Corresponding median OS estimates with delayed entry correction applied based on specimen date are shown in the Data Supplement (Table S5). Overall, median PFS from the start of 2L treatment was approximately 3-4 months across subgroups (Fig 3D). PFS values were within the range of TTD and TTNT values (Data Supplement, Fig S3B).

Patients With EGFR Mutations, KRAS Mutations, or ALK Rearrangements

Tumors with oncogenic HER2 mutations and co-occurring alterations in EGFR, KRAS, or ALK may represent a biologically distinct subgroup from HER2-mutant tumors without coaberrations. As expected, more patients in this group (n = 28) received TKIs in a 1L setting than in the full cohort (Data Supplement, Table S7). We conducted sensitivity analyses of 1L and 2L outcomes excluding patients with EGFR, KRAS, or ALK alterations, but did not see appreciable differences in outcomes compared with the whole cohort results, potentially due to small numbers (Data Supplement, Table S8).

DISCUSSION

This is one of the largest real-world studies to date to evaluate patients with HER2-mutant NSCLC. Of 14,768 patients with advanced/metastatic NSCLC, 3.8% had HER2 mutations and 1.8% harbored oncogenic or likely oncogenic variants. Despite evolving assays during the study period, these prevalence estimates align with prior reports (1.2%-5.1%).6,19,23,24,26,28,39-42 For example, a recent analysis of the Guardant360 database in the United States identified HER2 mutations in 1,719 of 33,080 (5.2%) patients with NSCLC, with 634 (1.9%) classified as oncogenic.6 Likewise, in our study, a high proportion of identified HER2 variants had unknown oncogenic function. Most of these variants occurred outside of the TKD and require structural and functional analysis to ascertain their significance.43 However, most (approximately 80%) TKD mutations had oncogenic or likely oncogenic function, reflecting their clinical relevance.

Unlike most real-world studies, we systematically assessed HER2 mutations for oncogenicity using OncoKB. Patients with oncogenic or likely oncogenic TKD and non-TKD mutations appeared to have distinct characteristics and tumor genetics. Consistent with prior reports,6 patients with oncogenic HER2 TKD mutations were more frequently younger, female, and never-smokers than typical for NSCLC as a whole, and almost exclusively had tumors with nonsquamous histology. Oncogenic comutations were less frequent with HER2 TKD than non-TKD mutations, warranting further investigation.

Most patients in the HER2-onc mutant cohort received 1L platinum-based chemotherapy (alone or in combination), consistent with other studies across geographic regions.4,23,39 Median OS was 13.5 months in the 1L cohort and was numerically comparable in patients with TKD and non-TKD mutations. Median OS with 1L treatment was largely consistent with other US studies,23 including a previous FH-FMI CGDB study (median OS 13.2 months).11 Median PFS (5.4 months) was also comparable with previous reports (approximately 6-7 months).39,44-46

Of note, in patients with HER2 TKD mutations, median OS with first-line chemoimmunotherapy was numerically longer than with chemotherapy alone and vice versa in patients with HER2 non-TKD mutations. These findings are of uncertain significance and may reflect a chance event and/or small patient numbers (confidence intervals overlap). Alternatively, underlying clinicogenomic differences, including a higher frequency of co-occurring oncogenic alterations associated with limited responsiveness to immunotherapy in the non-TKD HER2 mutation subgroup, may partly explain the observations. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating only and require confirmation in larger cohorts.

In the 2L cohort, treatment modalities were heterogeneous, with the most common being immune checkpoint inhibitors with or without VEGF inhibitors. Reflecting the time frame of this study, very few (5%) patients received T-DXd. Median OS was <1 year and median PFS was 3.7 months, underscoring the historical unmet need. The accelerated approval of T-DXd in 202247 and recent accelerated approvals of zongertinib and sevabertinib represent significant progress and warrant further studies of outcomes in patients with HER2-mutant NSCLC in real-world clinical practice. Also, with the availability of new treatments, more research is required regarding optimal therapy in patients with specific mutation types. Furthermore, given that most biomarker testing was conducted close to the time of diagnosis, it will be important to consider retesting later during the disease course, given the availability of second-line treatments.

Regarding limitations, the 2023 cutoff of the study precluded meaningful analyses of patients treated with T-DXd or zongertinib. Indeed, to date, real-world data with these agents is scarce, reflecting the timing of regulatory approvals, although one recent study of 35 patients treated with T-DXd was published, with findings broadly consistent with clinical trials.48 This paucity of data underscores the need for future real-world analyses. Another potential limitation of this study is that the FH-FMI CGDB is limited to patients with genetic testing who have electronic medical record information. However, comparisons with other US databases, such as the SEER Program, indicate that patients with NSCLC in the FH-FMI CGDB are largely representative of NSCLC as a whole.49 Moreover, our analyses of the broader FMI database indicated similar trends. Although this was a large study overall, interpretation of outcomes in some subgroups was limited by small sample sizes and further studies are required. Certain patient characteristics, such as disease stage at 1L or 2L initiation and comorbidities, were not captured in the database. This study was chiefly epidemiologic in nature; therefore, comutations were not analyzed in detail, and intrapatient comparisons of tumor and blood samples were not undertaken. Although previous investigations support the use of real-world estimates of progression and mortality,37,50 this study was not designed to formally compare OS or other outcomes between subgroups. Real-world PFS was based on physician assessment and is thus collected differently than in some clinical trials.36 However, we found that PFS was closely aligned with TTD and TTNT, suggesting our PFS estimates were reliable. Future studies should include formal adjusted comparisons of OS between subgroups, including by HER2 mutation type.

In conclusion, HER2-mutant lung cancer comprises a clinically relevant molecular subset of NSCLC. Oncogenic or likely oncogenic HER2 mutations may occur both within and outside the TKD, necessitating a careful analysis of variants and a thoughtful approach to the development of targeted therapies. Patients with HER2-mutant NSCLC have traditionally been treated with first-line chemotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy, with clear room for improvement in outcomes. With the emergence of new HER2-targeted therapies, future real-world studies will be critical to understand their impact on outcomes and to optimize treatment sequencing for patients with HER2-mutant NSCLC.
