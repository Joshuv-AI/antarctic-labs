Investigating the relationship between nurse managers’ leadership style and predicted nurses’ turnover
Authors: Kawthar Alsaleh, Taibah Mohammed Ali, Fatimah Alhalafi, Faten Ali, Mashael Alfotais, Olfat Salem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimNurse turnover presents a significant challenge globally, impacting healthcare costs, patient outcomes and quality of care. This profound issue is very alarming in Saudi Arabia, necessitating an urgent need for effective strategies to mitigate such challenges. Effective management and leadership styles have emerged as pivotal determinants influencing nurse turnover and the intention to leave the profession. This research seeks to explore the relationship between the impact of three distinct leadership styles, transformational, transactional and passive-avoidant leadership, as implemented by nurse managers and the anticipated turnover among nurses, as well as investigate the relationship between the demographic characteristics and anticipated nurse turnover.MethodsThis study used a quantitative cross-sectional correlational design. Using a convenience sampling method, nurses from a tertiary governmental hospital in Makkah Province, Saudi Arabia, were included. The self-administered questionnaire consists of participant demographic inquiries, the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire and the Anticipated Turnover Scale (ATS).ResultsThe study involved a total of 192 nurses. The study analysis showed a significant positive relationship between passive-avoidant leadership and the predicted nurse turnover. The proximity in mean scores of both transactional and transformational leadership styles indicated a relatively balanced prevalence of the two leadership styles among nurse managers, whereas the passive-avoidant leadership style was the least prevalent leadership style. Markedly, the data indicated that 77.6% of the participants had high ATS scores, signalling high turnover intentions. The results also revealed that two demographic variables, nationality and qualification level, have a statistically significant relationship with anticipated turnover.ConclusionThe investigation concluded that the leadership style implied by nurse managers has a significant relationship with the predicted turnover level of nurses. This study suggests fostering the growth of transformational leaders while discouraging the passive-avoidant style. Implementing targeted leadership development programmes can empower nurse managers to adopt effective leadership approaches and ultimately mitigate turnover rates.

Introduction

Turnover is one of the significant problems facing the nursing profession.1 Nursing intentions for turnover lead to absenteeism and nursing shortage, which in turn results in low quality of care.2 An effective leadership style is a central point that can combat the constantly challenging problem of nurses’ turnover.3 Turnover among nurses is substantially increasing across the globe.4–6 The rate of turnover among nurses ranges between 12% and 34%.7 Consequently, this brings about additional financial losses for healthcare organisations.8 Though there is no ideal workplace, leaders who influence and motivate their followers can bring outstanding outcomes. Leaders may exhibit diverse leadership styles depending on the situations arising. With respect to the healthcare field, the presence of a good leader to guide and resolve conflicts and internal problems, along with maintaining the stability of followers, is of utmost importance to achieve the overall organisational goal.9 10 Effective leadership in the healthcare sector is a vital factor that strengthens the integration and quality of care.11

Leadership is the process and ability to positively influence others’ attitudes and values through motivation and guidance directed towards attaining a common goal.12 Leaders should encourage employees and assign tasks to teams for goal attainment.13 The leadership style defines and shapes a leader’s approach to organisational challenges. The leadership styles highlighted in this study are transformational, transactional and passive-avoidant leadership. Leaders with a transformational leadership style can lead and guide a positive change through mobilising, motivating and empowering their followers. These leaders challenge their followers to go beyond the expectations and status quo, and they take into consideration individual and inspirational needs.14–17 Whereas transactional leadership is an exchange-based leadership that promotes and encourages subordinates’ compliance through rewards or punishments. The followers are motivated and reinforced by an exchange of values designed to fulfil the contractual obligations.12 16 18 In the context of passive-avoidant leadership, leaders adopt a completely hands-off approach, abstaining from critical decision-making and avoiding active leadership roles. They refrain from intervening until problems reach a significant threshold, becoming too apparent to ignore. This approach to leadership is marked by a lack of proactiveness, as leaders tend to delay involvement until issues escalate, potentially hindering timely resolution and contributing to an environment where challenges are addressed only when they reach a critical point of urgency.14 The definition of turnover and its method of measurement vary widely among organisations and researchers, and thereby this inconsistency brought on various turnover rates and difficulties in comparing studies. In this present study, nurse turnover is described as nurses leaving or transferring from the formally defined position.19–21

Leadership plays an integral role in the daily interactions with staff nurses, and this affects their work outcomes and well-being.22 23 The improvement and optimisation of healthcare systems necessitate effective and strategic leadership.23 A previous study24 revealed that 88% demonstrated a strong correlation between leadership style and nurses’ job satisfaction, with the transformational leadership style showing the highest number of positive associations. In addition, a recent systematic review underscores the critical role of nursing leadership in enhancing nurse performance, which identified numerous motivating factors across six domains, including leadership styles and practices.25 Other findings from studies revealed that transformational, authentic and humble leadership styles, along with perceived organisational support, significantly reduce nurses’ intention to leave their jobs.26–28

Prior studies have underscored the alarming issue of nurse turnover worldwide,4–6 as well as in Saudi Arabia,29 30 emphasising the urgent need for effective strategies to mitigate this challenge. Leadership styles have emerged as pivotal determinants influencing nurse turnover and the intention to leave the profession.26 Leader-member exchange theory provides a compelling lens for a better understanding of the relationship between nurse managers’ leadership styles and the turnover intentions of nursing staff. This theory emphasises the quality of the relationship between leaders and their followers, suggesting that high-quality exchanges foster trust, respect and mutual obligation and are predictive of their work performance. Such dynamics can resonate strongly in healthcare settings, where the interactions between nurse managers and staff are critical to workplace outcomes.31–34 Further investigation into the potential role of managers’ leadership styles and their extended effects on turnover is crucial. The findings in the literature highlight the critical role that leadership plays in shaping the nursing profession and decreasing nurses’ intention for turnover.26 28 35 However, it is noteworthy that only a limited number of studies have explicitly addressed and highlighted the critical gap in understanding the specific leadership styles as key predictors of the concerning turnover intention in the Saudi healthcare context.25 36–38 This study is not only pivotal for advancing the nursing profession but is also directly relevant to addressing nursing shortages that hinder the achievement of the Saudi Vision 2030.39–41 Given the multitude of challenges faced by nurses, focusing on leadership styles becomes a critical factor in establishing a positive work environment that fosters nurse empowerment. Consequently, yielding a reduction in nursing shortages, burnout and turnover rates, while also enhancing the quality of patient care. The conduct of research on the effectiveness of different leadership styles helps in gaining a deeper understanding of the specific type of leadership that most effectively reduces the extensive turnover problem. Providing such a base of knowledge will also empower nursing managers to evolve and develop into effective leaders who promote staff nurses’ satisfaction and deliver optimal patient care.11 Hence, this research seeks to explore the relationship between the impact of three distinct leadership styles, transformational, transactional and passive-avoidant leadership, as implemented by nurse managers and the anticipated turnover among nurses, as well as to investigate the relationship between demographic characteristics and anticipated nurse turnover.

Methods

Research design and participants

This study used a quantitative cross-sectional and correlational design. Using a convenience sampling method, 192 nurses from a government hospital in Makkah Province, Saudi Arabia, were included. This convenience sampling approach involved the selection of study participants based on their accessibility and willingness to participate. The selection criteria for this study included employed nurses who are actively engaged in direct patient care, regardless of their academic qualification level. Nurses in managerial or preceptor roles whose primary duties do not involve direct patient care were excluded. The sampling frame consisted of 382 individuals. A confidence level of 95% was chosen, with a margin of error set at 5%. By using these criteria, the sample size for the study was determined to be 192.

Data collection instruments

The data collection for this study was carried out using an online survey platform (QuestionPro). Nurses who met the eligibility criteria and agreed to participate were invited through a paper-based invitation, which included a quick response (QR) code to access the online questionnaire. The disseminated invitation contained a statement on the purpose of the study along with clear instructions for completing the questionnaire. The invitations disseminated yielded a response rate of 71.1%, corresponding to 50.3% of the total eligible nurses. The study questionnaire included a clear and easily understandable statement outlining the purpose of the study, details about participation and a consent form. The survey consisted of demographic questions to gather participant information, the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ-5X),14 17 and the Anticipated Turnover Scale (ATS).42 43 The data collection process took place over a 3-week period, starting on 15 April 2023.

The first instrument used in the study is the MLQ (also known as MLQ-5X). MLQ-5X is a psychological questionnaire created by Bass and Avolio14 17 to assess a broad spectrum of leadership styles: passive-avoidant leaders, transitional leaders and transformational leaders. The rater form of the MLQ-5X consists of 45 items about leadership styles and nine outcome items, which measure the perceived satisfaction outcomes and the leader’s effectiveness. The MLQ-5X has been validated across various cultures and can serve as a means of differentiating between effective and ineffective leaders across all levels of an organisation.44 45

The second instrument is the ATS, which was developed by Hinshaw and Atwood.42 43 ATS aims to assess nurses’ intentions and willingness to leave their existing jobs, either through internal turnover (leaving the current position and acquiring a different position within the same organisation) or external turnover (leaving the current organisation that a person works for). The ATS consists of 12 Likert scale items with responding choices ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). To avoid acquiescence bias, ATS includes six items that were reversely scored. The entire variation of the results can be anywhere between 12 and 84 points, with a higher score indicating a greater anticipation of turnover. The ATS has shown excellent reliability and construct validity across studies.46 47 Responses indicating a mean score exceeding 3.5 were considered indicative of an intention for turnover.48

Data analysis

Initially, the study data were imported into the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences V.29, where they were cleaned and analysed. Both descriptive and inferential statistics were used for data management and analysis. The descriptive statistics included frequency, percentage, mean and SD. The inferential statistics consisted of a t-test and analysis of variance (ANOVA), along with linear regression analysis. These analyses were used to answer the objectives of this research, investigating the effects of the nurse managers’ leadership style on the anticipated nurse turnover and the demographic factors that might contribute to nurses’ intentions to leave the profession. The level of significance was set at a two-tailed p value of 0.05.

Results

Participants’ demographic characteristics and anticipated turnover

192 participants were included in the study sample, with the majority being female, accounting for 176 participants (91.7%). As for the age distribution, the largest group consisted of 70 participants (36.5%) who were between the ages of 31 and 35. The participants displayed a diverse range of nationalities, with the majority consisting of Saudi nationals (n=80, 41.7%). When considering marital status, the largest group was composed of married individuals (n=98, 51%). In terms of the educational qualification level, the majority of participants held a bachelor’s degree (n=156, 81.3%). Work experience among participants varied, with the largest group comprising 94 participants (49%) who had 8 years or more of work experience. Regarding the distribution of hospital departments among the participants, 56 nurses (29.2%) were working in the intensive care unit, 52 nurses (27.1%) in the emergency department and 45 nurses (23.4%) in the medical department, with the remaining nurses working in various other departments. The results revealed that two demographic variables, specifically nationality and qualification level, demonstrated a statistically significant relationship with anticipated turnover (p<0.05). Discussion

Nursing managers hold the responsibility to ensure that nurses effectively fulfil their duties and are actively involved in the process. Nurse turnover is a significant and prominent issue within healthcare systems, with various contributing factors.49 In alignment with the substantial attention given to the influence of leadership on employee turnover in the existing literature, the results of this research indicate that perceived leadership styles of nurse managers exhibited a statistically significant relationship with nurse turnover intention. In this study, it was observed that the passive-avoidant leadership style emerged as a notable and significant predictor of nurses’ turnover intention, demonstrating an increase in the likelihood of nurses intending to leave their positions. Markedly, our findings also revealed a non-significant negative correlational relationship between transformational leadership and nurse turnover, suggesting a potential mitigating effect. It is also essential to acknowledge that while the associations between the transformational leadership style and turnover intention did not reach statistical significance in our study, the negative coefficient value hints at a possible influence on nurse turnover. This indicates that larger studies could yield better insights into the effects and associated factors of transformational leadership on turnover intentions, enhancing our understanding of its role in promoting nurse retention within healthcare organisations. These findings contrast with the results of the study conducted by Sulaiman et al,35 where transformational leadership was found to have a statistically significant reduction in the predicted nurse turnover. This negative relationship suggests that the inspiring qualities typically associated with transformational leadership may act as protective factors against turnover intentions among nurses. Transformational leaders, known for their ability to motivate and inspire, may create a positive work environment, foster professional development and enhance job satisfaction, thereby reducing the likelihood of nurses considering leaving their roles.10 29 On the contrary, the finding of the study conducted by Smama'h et al50 reported an observed lack of significant correlation between turnover intention and various leadership styles. The disparity between the studies emphasises the nuanced nature of leadership effects on workforce outcomes, emphasising the need for further exploration and nuanced understanding of the intricate dynamics that contribute to turnover intentions in organisational settings.

As transformational leadership was the only leadership style that demonstrated a negative trend with nurse turnover intentions, the findings of this study emphasise the importance of fostering transformational leadership. In addition, the findings of this study reinforce the existing literature suggesting that transformational leadership positively impacts employee performance and enhances psychological empowerment, leading to greater well-being and increased commitment to their organisations.51 52 This enhanced commitment is crucial for the healthcare industry as it enhances the quality of care and patient safety, along with promoting a strong drive to achieve goals, improve staff retention and performance, boost motivation and job satisfaction and reduce turnover, burnout and absenteeism.53–56

Innovation has long been acknowledged as a critical factor in both the success of companies and the economic advancement of nations.37 In line with Saudi Vision 2030, which prioritises innovation as a key driver of economic diversification and development, transformational leadership plays a critical role in fostering an innovative culture within organisations.40 57 By adopting transformational leadership, institutions create supportive environments that not only enhance innovation but also reduce nurse turnover, aligning with the Saudi Vision 2030’s goals to address nurse shortages and improve healthcare quality.41

According to the examination of leadership styles among nurse managers, the transactional leadership style emerged as the most commonly perceived leadership approach. Interestingly, the transformational leadership style is very closely followed. The proximity in mean scores of both transactional and transformational leadership styles indicated a relatively balanced prevalence of the two leadership styles among nurse managers. It is noteworthy that the passive-avoidant leadership style received the lowest mean score, implying a less prevalent perception of this leadership approach among the surveyed nurses. Such results were also observed in previous studies.35 38 41 58–60 These findings further reinforce previous research by emphasising the need for a well-defined roadmap and targeted initiatives to encourage transformational leadership skills within the nursing profession and to support the development of effective nursing leaders.27 38 61–63

Markedly, the data indicated that 77.6% of the participants had high ATS scores, signalling high turnover intentions. This result suggests a substantial proportion of the sample expressed a desire to leave their current positions. These results are alarming and underscore the utmost importance of developing interventions that help mitigate this issue. Comparing our findings with the existing literature reveals considerable variations. In a study by Almalki et al,64 about 40.4% of respondent nurses had the intention to leave their current employment. Conversely, the study conducted by Kaddourah et al65 showed that around 94% of participants were willing to leave their current job. These disparities emphasise the nuanced nature of turnover intentions and the need for a comprehensive understanding of contributing factors.

Moreover, this study discovered a significant result of predicted turnover in connection to nursing professionals with a master’s degree and above when compared with those with a bachelor’s degree. This comes in congruence with the research conducted by Suliman et al,66 as both investigations revealed that greater levels of education were associated with an increased probability of nurses leaving their positions. Such findings imply that nurses with higher degrees may have greater motives and plans for future career changes and co-occurring factors that contribute to their elevated turnover intentions. Additionally, the study findings showed a significant relationship between nationality and turnover intentions. Particularly, Saudi nurses are more inclined towards turnover intentions than their peers from India and Sudan. These results align with those by Alanazi et al67 and Albougami et al,68 which were conducted in Saudi Arabia, wherein their investigation revealed a noteworthy correlation between nationality and intentions to leave. Nevertheless, distinctions arise in the specifications among the specific nationalities, contributing to the variations in turnover intentions.

Limitation

A limitation of this study is its confinement to a single hospital, and the resultant restricted sample size may impede the extension of findings to a more diverse population of healthcare settings. Moreover, the use of a non-probability convenience sampling method introduces a potential for bias, thereby further constraining the generalisability of the results to a broader population. Future research should address these limitations by conducting multicentre studies with probability sampling methods and involving a larger number of participants, which would enable a more representative sample. Such studies would also account for the varying organisational and contextual factors across different settings that could act as confounding variables influencing turnover rates. Another limitation is the lack of longitudinal data, which would have allowed for the examination of how nursing leadership styles impact nursing outcomes over an extended period. Addressing this aspect in future studies is crucial for gaining a deeper understanding of the long-term impact of leadership approaches on nursing outcomes, such as job satisfaction and patient care quality, thereby contributing positively to the nursing profession as a whole.

Conclusions

Regarding the prediction of nurses’ turnover to the leadership styles of nurse managers, the leadership style implied by nurse managers had a significant relationship with the predicted turnover level of the nurses. This study suggests fostering the growth of transformational leaders while discouraging the passive-avoidant style. This study provides a deeper understanding of how leadership dynamics impact turnover and workplace stability, carrying valuable theoretical and practical insights for both the healthcare industry and academic research. Healthcare organisations and policymakers aiming to enhance retention strategies should focus on the implementation of targeted leadership development programmes that empower nurse managers to adopt effective leadership approaches, which can eventually mitigate turnover rates. These programmes should include training modules on effective communication, emotional intelligence and team building to empower nurse managers in adopting transformational practices. By prioritising such leadership development initiatives, organisations can enhance nurse retention and improve patient care outcomes through increased stability and continuity within nursing teams.

Additionally, this study underscores the importance of ongoing research that investigates turnover intention among nurses and leadership styles. As the healthcare landscape evolves, it emphases the dynamic nature of effective leadership in the face of changing healthcare environments and nursing workforce needs. Future researchers should incorporate longitudinal and multi-institutional analyses to further validate the findings related to leadership styles and nurse turnover intentions. Investigating the interplay between leadership styles, turnover intention and other factors such as job satisfaction and organisational culture could also provide deeper insights into the dynamics affecting nurse retention.
