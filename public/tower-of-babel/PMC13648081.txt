Synthesis and Characterization
of Zinc Complexes with
Diethyl 2‐Hydroxyazulene-1,3-dicarboxylate as a Ligand

Tatsuya Iwashina, Ryoga Yamamoto, Takuya Sagawa, Tomohiro Imai, Kazuki Yamamoto, Takahiro Gunji

Abstract

The reaction of diethyl 2-hydroxyazulene-1,3-dicarboxylate
(DEHA) with zinc species (Zn­(OAc)2 and ZnCl2) in the presence of various alkali metal carbonates (M2CO3) was investigated. The resulting complexes
were characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, Fourier-transform
infrared spectroscopy, elemental analysis, mass spectrometry, single-crystal
X-ray structural analysis, and UV–vis and fluorescence spectroscopy.
The reaction between DEHA and Zn­(OAc)2 in
the presence of Rb2CO3 or K2CO3 as bases afforded Zn-DEHA-Rb or Zn-DEHA-K, respectively, where three DEHA anions were coordinated
to Zn2+, with an alkali metal ion as the counter cation.
On the other hand, the reaction between DEHA and Zn­(OAc)2 in the presence of Na2CO3 afforded Zn-DEHA-Na-1, in which four DEHA anions were
coordinated to two Zn2+ and Na+, with two acetoxy
ions present. The reaction between DEHA and ZnCl2 in the presence of Na2CO3 provided Zn-DEHA-Na-2, where three DEHA anions were coordinated
to Zn2+, with Na+ as the countercation. The
zinc complexes exhibited S1 emission despite differences
in their structures. Furthermore, the synthesized complexes were mixed
with poly­(methyl methacrylate) to form composite films.

Introduction

Azulene is a unique molecule,
blue in color, and possesses a simple
structure. Azulene is known to exhibit
a large dipole moment of 1.08 D and to
display fluorescence from the second excited state in violation of
Kasha’s rule. Its color tone, electronic state, and solubility can be modulated
by introducing substituents into the azulene framework and forming
derivatives.

In recent years, several studies have been reported
that exploit
the unique properties of azulenes in material science.
−

 For example, Yamaguchi et al. developed
four isomers of terazulene and applied them as organic field-effect
transistor (OFET) materials, thereby enabling control of hole mobility.

Coordination polymers containing azulene moieties are expected
to exhibit properties not observed in azulenes alone because of interactions
between azulene units and metal species. For example, Sun et al. reported that a cobalt-coordinated polymer coated
on gold rods showed a high rectification effect via the metal-to-ligand
transfer transition (MLCT) from Co2+ to azulenes. Furthermore,
Barman et al. reported that zinc-based
metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) containing azulene derivatives
as ligands showed high hydrogen storage capacities owing to the polarity
of azulenes. Although coordination polymers containing azulene moieties
are expected to exhibit unique properties, the number of reported
studies remains limited due to the lack of systematic investigations
of metal complexes employing azulenes as ligands, which constitute
the fundamental structural units of coordination polymers.

In
our previous work, the synthesis and characterization of metal
complexes employing diethyl 2-aminoazulene-1,3-dicarboxylate (DEAA) and diethyl 2-hydroxyazulene-1,3-dicarboxylate
(DEHA) as ligands were investigated
(Scheme S1). DEAA formed stable
complexes with only Pd2+, whereas DEHA formed
stable complexes with many transition metal ions, including Pd2+, Co2+, Ni2+, Cu2+, and
Zn2+, in the presence of Cs2CO3 (Figure S1). The Ni2+, Co2+, and Zn2+ complexes with DEHA formed coordination
polymers containing Cs+. Lanthanide complexes with DEHA reported by Zhang and Petoud contained K+ ions. Furthermore, the crystal structures
of salen and phenol derivatives complexes are known to depend on the type of
alkali metal ion. Finelli et al. reported that alkali metal complexes
utilizing salen-type ligands form discrete molecular structures in
the single-crystal state when the metal ion is Li+ or Na+, whereas they form one-dimensional polymeric structures when
the metal ion is K+, Rb+, or Cs+. Boyle et al. reported that alkali metal complexes
with phenol-derivative ligands primarily form discrete molecular structures
such as cubes or squares in the single-crystal state for Li+ and Na+, while primarily forming polymeric structures
for K+, Rb+, and Cs+. Based on these findings, it is known that alkali
metal complexes are more likely to form polymeric structures in the
single-crystal state as one moves down the periodic table. Therefore,
in this study, the structural change in the Zn-DEHA complexes
was studied by varying the alkali metal carbonate used, as shown in .

Experimental Section

Characterization

1H and 13C­{1H} nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra
were acquired using a JNM-ECZ400S spectrometer (JEOL, Japan; 1H at 399 MHz and 13C­{1H} at 100 MHz)
at 22 ± 5 °C. Chemical shifts of 1H and 13C­{1H} NMR spectra were referenced to the residual
proton and carbon signals of the corresponding deuterated solvents,
respectively. Fourier-transform infrared
(FTIR) spectroscopy was performed using an FT/IR-6100 spectrometer
(JASCO, Japan) equipped with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR)
accessory (ZnSe prism, JASCO ATR PRO 0450-S). Mass spectrometry was
performed using a JMS-T100CS AccuTOF CS instrument (JEOL, Japan) with
electrospray ionization (ESI). Elemental analyses were performed using
a UNICUBE elemental analyzer (Elementar, Germany). Thermogravimetric-differential
thermal analysis (TG-DTA) was conducted using a TG-DTA 2000SE instrument
(Netszch, Japan). The samples were heated to 1000 °C at a rate
of 10 °C/min under airflow. The decomposition temperatures were
visually recorded using an SMP3 melting point apparatus (Stuart, U.K.).
Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) absorption spectra were
recorded using a V-670 UV–Vis–NIR spectrophotometer
(JASCO, Tokyo, Japan). Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analyses were
performed using an XtaLAB Synergy-S diffractometer (RIGAKU, Japan)
equipped with a monochromatic Mo–Kα radiation source
(λ = 0.7107 Å). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDX)
was performed using an SU9000 field-emission scanning electron microscope
(Hitachi High-Technologies, Japan) after the samples were coated with
gold. Molecular weight estimation from diffusion coefficients obtained
by diffusion ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) NMR was performed according
to a previously reported method. The
deposition numbers were CCDC 2535569 for Zn-DEHA-Na-1 and 2535572 for Zn-DEHA-Na-2. These data can be obtained
free of charge at http://www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk/conts/retrieving.html or from the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre, 12 Union Road,

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Solubility of Zinc Complexes
with DEHA

Zn2+ was selected as the transition
metal ion because it has no unpaired electrons and is suitable for
the NMR analysis. Zn–DEHA complexes were synthesized
by reacting DEHA with Zn­(OAc)2 or ZnCl2 in the presence of alkali metal carbonate, such as Cs2CO3, Rb2CO3, K2CO3, or Na2CO3, at a molar ratio
of 2:1:3. The elemental compositions of Zn-DEHA-Rb and Zn-DEHA-K were analyzed by EDX. These analyses confirmed the
incorporation of rubidium and potassium cations into Zn-DEHA-Rb and Zn-DEHA-K, respectively (Figures S1 and S2). The solubilities of Zn–DEHA complexes are listed in . All Zn–DEHA complexes were soluble
in chloroform and dichloromethane and insoluble in hexane and EtOH.
Only Zn-DEHA-Rb was insoluble in acetone. For comparison,
the solubility of Na-DEHA is also included in ; unlike the zinc
complexes, Na-DEHA was soluble only in THF. Notably,
these complexes did not decompose upon washing with water and exhibited
resistance to hydrolysis.

Structure of Zn-DEHA-Na-1 and Zn-DEHA-Na-2

The crystallographic data and selected
bond distances obtained from single-crystal X-ray diffraction are
summarized in and . The acetoxy ions incorporated in Zn-DEHA-Na-1 were derived from Zn­(OAc)2. Single
crystals suitable for X-ray analysis could not be obtained for Zn-DEHA-Rb or Zn-DEHA-K. Based on EDX, elemental
analysis, and mass spectrometry, the chemical formulas of Zn-DEHA-Rb and Zn-DEHA-K were determined to be identical. As a
result, the compositions of Zn-DEHA-Rb and Zn-DEHA-K were determined to be [ZnM­(OC10H5(COOC2H5)2)3] (M = Rb (Zn-DEHA-Rb) and K (Zn-DEHA-K)) similar to that of Zn-DEHA-Cs. The structure of Zn-DEHA-Na-1 comprises four DEHA ligands and two acetoxy ions coordinated to both zinc
and sodium ions (). The Zn center was five-coordinate, whereas the sodium ion was
six-coordinate. Two crystallographically distinct conformations were
observed within the Zn-DEHA-Na-1 crystal, with dihedral
angles of 167° (Zn-DEHA-Na-1 (a)) and 178°
(Zn-DEHA-Na-1 (b)). The Zn–O and Na–O bond
lengths were 1.993–2.059 and 2.386–2.485 Å, respectively.
τ5 values were 0.05 and 0.18, respectively. It was
suggested that Zn formed square pyramidal shape rather than trigonal
bipyramidal shape in both structures. In the solid-state packing, an alternating arrangement of the two
conformations generated a one-dimensional structure, which was supported
by π–π stacking interactions (3.395 Å). These
observations suggest that the coexistence of these two conformations
facilitated the formation of the one-dimensional assembly.

Zn-DEHA-Na-2 consisted of three DEHA ligands
coordinated to zinc and sodium ions, similar to Zn-DEHA-Cs (). This
structural difference from Zn-DEHA-Na-1 reflects the
use of ZnCl2 as the zinc source, which does not introduce
acetoxy ion. Two THF-d
8 molecules were
present per Zn-DEHA-Na-2 unit as crystallization molecules.
Both zinc and sodium were six-coordinate. The Zn–O and Na–O
bond lengths were 2.027–2.090 and 2.325–2.443 Å,
respectively, and the average dihedral angle was 171°. τ6 value was 0.24. It was suggested that Zn formed octahedron
shape rather than trigonal prismatic shape. On the other hand, τ6 values of Zn-DEHA-Cs was 0.13 and the structure around Zn formed octahedron similarly
to Zn-DEHA-Na-2. In the packing structure, π–π
stacking interactions were observed (3.367 Å). However, unlike Zn-DEHA-Cs, Zn-DEHA-Na-2 did not form a one-dimensional
coordination polymer, which may be attributed to the difference in
ionic radii between the sodium and cesium ions. For (troponato)­sodium­(I)
hydride (Na­(Tp)·H2O), the Na–O distances were
2.326 and 2.318 Å (CCDC: 1217301). For (2,4-pentanedionato)­sodium­(I) (Na­(acac)), the Na–O distances
were 2.367 and 2.447 Å (CCDC: 119051). For hexa­(2,4-pentanedionato)­trizinc­(II) (Zn3(acac)6), the Zn–O distances were 1.971–2.167 Å
(CCDC: 1313743). Accordingly, the Na–O
and Zn–O bond lengths in Zn-DEHA-Na-1 and Zn-DEHA-Na-2 are comparable to those of Na­(Tp)·H2O, Na­(acac), and Zn3(acac)6.

Spectra of DEHA Complexes

The Zn-DEHA complexes were characterized by 1H NMR and FTIR spectroscopy. The 1H NMR spectra of the
Zn-DEHA complexes are shown in , together with the spectrum of Na-DEHA for comparison. For all DEHA complexes, the signal
attributable to the hydroxy proton of DEHA (11.5 ppm)
was absent, consistent with complexation. In addition, the signals
of the azulenyl moieties shifted to higher magnetic fields relative
to those of DEHA, as reported for Pd-DEHA and Zn-DEHA-Cs. The ethoxy-group
quartet signals of Zn-DEHA-Cs, Zn-DEHA-Rb, Zn-DEHA-K, and Na-DEHA also shifted upfield,
whereas those of Zn-DEHA-Na-1 and Zn-DEHA-Na-2 shifted downfield. These results indicate that, among complexes
with the same metal-to-ligand ratio (Zn-DEHA-Cs, Zn-DEHA-Rb, Zn-DEHA-K, and Zn-DEHA-Na-2), Cs+, Rb+, and K+ behave as relatively
electron-rich cations, whereas Na+ behaves as a relatively
electron-deficient cation. For sodium-containing complexes (Zn-DEHA-Na-1, Zn-DEHA-Na-2, and Na-DEHA), the direction and magnitude of the ethoxy quartet shift depended
on whether zinc or acetoxy ions were present. DOSY NMR was performed
to further investigate the solution-state structures. The molecular
weights of DEHA and metal-DEHA complexes
estimated from the diffusion coefficients are summarized in . Based on the molecular
weights estimated in THF-d
8, Zn-DEHA-Cs, Zn-DEHA-Rb, Zn-DEHA-Na-1, and Na-DEHA were inferred to form oligomeric species, whereas Zn-DEHA-K and Zn-DEHA-Na-2 were inferred to be predominantly
monomeric. These results suggest that the upfield shift observed in Zn-DEHA-Cs and Zn-DEHA-Rb can be attributed to
the synergistic effect of electron-rich Cs+ and Rb+ ions and oligomerization. Na-DEHA was estimated
to exist as a monomer in DMSO-d
6.

The FTIR spectra of DEHA and DEHA complexes
are shown in , and the corresponding assignments are summarized in . The absorption band of DEHA at 1673 cm–1 was assigned to υ­(CO)
vibration of free CO groups. The absorption bands of the Zn-DEHA complexes at 1666–1683 cm–1 were
assigned to the υ­(CO) vibration of CO groups
coordinated to alkali metal ions. In the single-crystal structures,
the CO bond lengths for carbonyl groups coordinated to Na
were 1.203–1.222 Å in Zn-DEHA-Na-1 and 1.212–1.222
Å in Zn-DEHA-Na-2; the corresponding distances for
carbonyl groups coordinated to Cs were 1.198–1.214 Å in Zn-DEHA-Cs. These values did not substantially deviate from
those of free CO bonds (1.211 Å in DEHA and
1.217 Å in Pd-DEHA). Accordingly, only minor differences
were observed in the FTIR spectra between the υ­(CO)
bands of free CO groups and those of CO groups coordinated
to alkali metal ions. Consistent with this interpretation, Na-DEHA exhibited a band at 1660 cm–1, which was assigned
to υ­(CO) of CO coordinated to the sodium ion.
In contrast, the absorption bands of the Zn-DEHA complexes
at 1590–1606 cm–1 were assigned to υ­(CO)
of CO coordinated to the zinc ion.

The UV–Vis spectra of DEHA and
the Zn-DEHA complexes are shown in . The absorption band of DEHA at 430 nm, assigned to the S0 → S1 transition,
red-shifted to 455–459 nm for the Zn-DEHA complexes.
The absorption band of DEHA at 363 nm, assigned to the
S0 → S2 transition, red-shifted to 393–404

The fluorescence spectra of DEHA and
the corresponding
complexes are shown in . In our previous work, the fluorescence
of DEHA was assigned to the S2 emission. In
contrast, all zinc complexes exhibited S1 emission, as
observed for Zn-DEHA-Cs, regardless of structural differences
and types of alkali metal ions. The reason for this behavior was further
examined using Time-Dependent Density Functional Theory (TD-DFT) calculations.
Due to the high computational cost associated with the large size
of the zinc complexes, TD-DFT calculations were not performed on the
full systems. Therefore, Na-DEHA was selected as a simplified
model to investigate the excited-state properties and it displays
S1 emission similar to that of the zinc complexes. Furthermore,
benzo­[a]­azulene, which
exhibits S2 emission, was used as a reference system to
compare a heterometallacycle with a fused benzene ring. Recently,
Dunlop et al. attributed the S2 emission of azulene to excited-state aromaticity. Specifically,
they proposed that low aromaticity in S1 promotes thermal
deactivation, whereas higher aromaticity in S2 favors radiative
decay. We applied this framework to our system. Aromaticity was evaluated
according to the harmonic oscillator model of aromaticity (HOMA),
using the optimized structure from the TD-DFT calculation. The S0, S1, and S2 structures of azulene, DEHA, Na-DEHA, and benzo­[a]­azulene
were optimized using DFT at the B3LYP/6–31g+ (d,p) level. The
optimized structures of DEHA and Na-DEHA are shown in Figures S3 and S4, respectively.
In DEHA, the carbonyl group not engaged in hydrogen bonding
was rotated by 24° relative to the azulene ring in the S1 structure. In Na-DEHA, the carbonyl group not
coordinated to the sodium ion was rotated by 44° relative to
the azulene ring in the S1 and S2 structures.
The TD-DFT-derived HOMA values for S0, S1, and
S2 in azulene, DEHA, Na-DEHA,
and benzo­[a]­azulene are summarized in . For azulene and DEHA, the HOMA values decreased from 0.96 to 0.87 and from 0.89 to 0.78,
respectively, when going from S2 to S1, consistent
with their S2 emission. Conversely, Na-DEHA and benzo­[a]­azulene showed no substantial change
in the HOMA values between S2 and S1, which
accounts for the S1 emission of Na-DEHA but
does not explain the S2 emission of benzo­[a]­azulene. To explain the S2 emission of benzo­[a]­azulene, the S2–S1 energy
gap was considered. In azulene derivatives, fluorescence is generally
dominated by S2 emission when the S2–S1 gap exceeds 10,000 cm–1, whereas both S2 and S1 emissions can coexist when the S2–S1 gap is 9000–10,000 cm–1. When the S2–S1 gap is below 9000 cm–1, S1 emissions
dominates. The S2–S1 gaps of azulene, DEHA, Na-DEHA,
and benzo­[a]­azulene calculated by TD-DFT are summarized
in . The S2–S1 gap of benzo­[a]­azulene
was 10,015 cm–1, thereby supporting its S2 emission. Conversely, the S2–S1 gap
of Na-DEHA was calculated to be 0 cm–1, well below 9000 cm–1. Thus, the S1 emission of Na-DEHA can be explained by both the small
change in excited-state aromaticity and the small S2–S1 gap. Collectively, these results suggest that complexation
shifts DEHA fluorescence from S2 to S1 emission by minimizing differences in aromaticity between
S2 and S1 and by reducing the S2–S1 gap to below 9000 cm–1. However, it should
be noted that the S1 emission of these complexes could
not be distinguished from the S1 and S2 emissions
based on the calculation results.

PMMA Composite Films Containing DEHA Complexes

PMMA composite films containing 0.1, 1, and 3
wt % of Zn-DEHA-Rb, Zn-DEHA-K, Zn-DEHA-Na-1, Zn-DEHA-Na-2, and Na-DEHA were prepared
for application as dyes dispersed in polymers. The appearance and
UV–Vis spectra of the PMMA composite films are shown in Figure S5 and Figures S6–S10, respectively.
In the UV–vis spectra, only the PMMA composite films containing Zn-DEHA-K exhibited a high transmittance of approximately
90% at loadings up to 3 wt %, whereas the transmittance of films containing
other complexes decreased with increasing complex content. The dispersion
estimated from the UV–vis spectra showed a weaker correlation
with the solubility of the metal-DEHA complexes in THF
than that reported in previous studies. This weaker correlation is thought to result from the loss of stabilization
by THF coordination during drying in the composite film formation
process. In the FTIR spectra, no shifts were observed in the PMMA carbonyl band around 1730 cm–1 (Figure S11). The composite films exhibited
improved thermal stability, as reflected by generally higher 20% weight-loss
temperatures (T
d20) compared with pure
PMMA (Figures S12–S16). T
d20 values of PMMA-metal-DEHA complexes
are summarized in . This improvement is thought to be due to the interaction between
PMMA and metal-DEHA. However, the absence of significant
shifts in the IR spectra (Figure S11) and
the lack of substantial improvement in T
d10
 suggest that there is no major interaction
between PMMA and metal-DEHA.

Conclusion

The reactions between DEHA and zinc species (Zn­(OAc)2 and ZnCl2) in the presence of alkali metal carbonates
(Rb2CO3, K2CO3, and Na2CO3) were investigated. The reaction of DEHA with Zn­(OAc)2 in the presence of Rb2CO3 or K2CO3 afforded [ZnM­(OC10H5(COOC2H5)2)3] (M = Rb (Zn-DEHA-Rb) and K (Zn-DEHA-K)). In contrast, the reaction of DEHA with Zn­(OAc)2 and Na2CO3 yielded [Zn2Na2(OC10H5(COOC2H5)2)4(CH3COO)2] (Zn-DEHA-Na-1). In Zn-DEHA-Na-1, the acetoxy ion
derived from Zn­(OAc)2 was present, as confirmed by 1H NMR, 13C NMR, elemental analysis, and single-crystal
X-ray diffraction. The single-crystal structure revealed two conformations
that differed in the azulene-ring angle, enabling π–π
interactions. When DEHA was reacted with ZnCl2 and Na2CO3, [ZnNa­(OC10H5(COOC2H5)2)3] (Zn-DEHA-Na-2) was formed. Unlike Zn-DEHA-Na-1, Zn-DEHA-Na-2 contained no acetoxy ions. Moreover, Zn-DEHA-Na-2 did not form a coordination polymer, in contrast
to Zn–DEHA–Cs, which may be attributable
to the difference in ionic radii between Cs+ and Na+. In the 1H NMR spectra, the signals of the azulene
moiety in all Zn-DEHA complexes shifted upfield relative
to those of DEHA. The ethoxy-group resonances shifted
either upfield or downfield depending on the alkali metal ion. In
the FTIR spectra, the υ­(CO) band to CO groups
coordinated to the zinc ion shifted to lower wavenumbers relative
to the υ­(CO) band associated with hydrogen-bonded carbonyl
groups. Furthermore, the carbonyl stretching frequencies showed no
significant change between the υ­(CO) band of free CO
groups and that of CO groups coordinated to alkali metal ions.
In the fluorescence spectra, the S2 emission of DEHA shifted to S1 emission upon complexation with zinc and
sodium ions. This shift was attributed to the small difference in
aromaticity between S1 and S2 and the small
S2–S1 gap. PMMA-metal-DEHA composite films were prepared, and their heat resistance was evaluated
by TG-DTA. The results showed improved heat resistance compared with
pure PMMA. These results suggest that the Zn-DEHA complexes
can be used as building blocks for functional materials such as MOFs
incorporating Zn-DEHA units.
