Evaluating robotic surgery skills decay and maintenance of proficiency in a high-fidelity simulated environment
Authors: Ricardo E. Nunez-Rocha, Samy Castillo-Flores, Andres A. Abreu, Roberto Ramirez, Morgan Pettigrew, Sofia Garces-Palacios, Amr Al Abbas, Angela A. Guzzetta, Daniel J. Scott, Herbert J. Zeh, Ganesh Sankaranarayanan, Patricio M. Polanco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSkill retention and decay are critical in robotic surgical simulation training. While performance decay has been studied in virtual reality platforms, its effects in high-fidelity biotissue drills remain underexplored. We evaluated both the impact of training breaks and the benefits of continued practice beyond proficiency among general surgery residents performing simulated robotic bowel anastomoses.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 132 h of robotic simulation from 45 residents who reached proficiency (OSATS ≥ 28) on a high-fidelity bowel anastomosis drill. Two cohorts were included: a skill decay group (n = 30) who returned ≥ 1 month after achieving proficiency, and a continued training group (n = 15) who completed ≥ 3 additional sessions post-proficiency. OSATS scores and task times were compared between initial and follow-up sessions. Skill decay was defined as a ≥ 2-point OSATS drop or ≥ 6-min time increase. Statistical tests included paired comparisons, ROC analysis, and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsIn the skill decay group, the median interval was 91 days (IQR 63–134). Task time increased from 30.5 to 34.5 min (p = 0.03), while OSATS declined from 30 to 28 (p = 0.001). By 6 months, OSATS scores dropped 17.6% and time worsened 19.3%. ROC curves identified 84 days (AUC 0.76) for technical decay and 188 days (AUC 0.89) for efficiency loss. Higher baseline OSATS predicted decline (OR 2.72; p = 0.02). In the continued training group, OSATS improved from 29.5 to 34 (p < 0.001), and task time decreased from 30.5 to 25.5 min (p = 0.01).ConclusionRobotic simulation performance deteriorates within 3–6 months of inactivity. Conversely, continued deliberate practice beyond proficiency yields measurable gains. Structured refreshers near the 3-month mark and extended practice should be integrated into robotic curricula to sustain readiness.

The rapid expansion of robotic surgery has reshaped the surgical landscape. Between 2012—2018, the proportion of robotic procedures increased eight-fold nationally, surpassing laparoscopic volumes in several procedures, despite variability in outcomes and absence of standardized training pathways [1, 2]. Although this reflects growing interest, adoption has outpaced the establishment of a globally accepted robotic curricula. As a result, concerns remain about operative readiness when surgeons adopt robotic platforms without validated educational frameworks [2, 3].

In response, simulation-based curricula have been developed to address gaps in robotic training. Virtual reality (VR), and inanimate drills provide a safe environment to develop technical proficiency without patient risk [4, 7]. At our institution, we validated and implemented a comprehensive training program comprising a 19-drill VR curriculum and three inanimate drills, followed by high-fidelity, procedure-specific bio-tissue drills, with increasing console autonomy as residents advance in their training [5, 8, 9]. This structured curriculum reflects a broader shift towards objective, proficiency-based progression over traditional time-based models [7, 10].

Beyond training, simulation performance may also predict intraoperative proficiency. In a recent study, residents with higher Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS) scores during robotic side-to-side bowel anastomosis simulation were more likely to have shorter operative time and patients experienced fewer complications [10]. These findings underscore the value of simulation not only as an educational tool but also as a mechanism to enhance surgical performance and optimize patient outcomes.

Despite these benefits, questions remain about how well-acquired skills are retained over time and how to best maintain them. Prior studies have suggested that robotic performance decay can occur within 4 weeks of inactivity, and trainees often require multiple attempts to achieve and maintain mastery [6, 11]. Additionally, many continue practicing even after meeting established proficiency benchmarks, indicating an ongoing need for skill reinforcement [6]. However, the potential benefits of continued training beyond proficiency, as well as the timeframe in which skill decay begins after training gaps, have yet to be evaluated in robotic simulation-based education.

Considering these gaps, we aimed to evaluate both skill decay after training gaps and performance gains with continued practice after proficiency in our curriculum. Specifically, our objective was to determine whether prolonged gaps in training led to measurable deterioration in OSATS scores and time to task completion in a high-fidelity model of robotic intestinal anastomoses. A secondary objective was to define whether continued deliberate practice after achieving proficiency, resulted in further improvements in technical performance.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This retrospective study evaluated simulation performance among general surgery residents enrolled in our structured robotic training curriculum. This study was reviewed and deemed exempt by the University of Texas Southwestern Institutional Review Board. The primary objectives were to assess skill retention following training gaps and to examine performance gains with continued deliberate practice beyond proficiency. Eligible participants included PGY-2 to PGY-5 residents who had completed the initial steps of the curriculum and demonstrated proficiency in a high-fidelity side-to-side bowel anastomosis drill using the Da Vinci Xi Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical) (Fig. 1), a technically complex and clinically relevant task chosen for its reproducibility and objective scoring. All simulation sessions were recorded and conducted in a standardized simulation environment in the UT Southwestern Simulation Center.Fig. 1Robotic side-to-side bowel anastomosis biotissue drill

Robotic side-to-side bowel anastomosis biotissue drill

Proficiency criteria and assessment tools

To ensure consistency, a benchmark of proficiency was established prior to the study. Residents were considered proficient when they achieved a minimum score of 28 or higher on the OSATS score. This score has been previously validated through consensus among expert faculty based on established benchmarks in surgical simulation literature and prior validation work within our institution [10, 12]. Additionally, procedural efficiency was assessed using time to task completion, recorded in minutes from the beginning of the task to the final suture cut.

Group assignment and timepoints of interest

Residents were categorized into two separate cohorts depending on their pattern of participation following proficiency (Fig. 2). The first cohort, referred to as the ‘skill decay group,’ included residents who, after achieving proficiency, experienced a training gap of ≥ 1 month without simulation training and later returned for one additional session within 12 months. In this group, the first timepoint (Time 1) was defined as the resident’s passing attempt, which was the first session in which they reached the OSATS threshold of ≥ 28. The second timepoint (Time 2) represented their return session after the training gap. The second cohort, named as the “training beyond proficiency group”, included residents who continued practicing for ≥ 3 sessions regularly after reaching proficiency (Fig. 2). Time 1 was again defined as the passing attempt, and Time 2 was the resident’s last available training session.Fig. 2Flow chart of training groups

Flow chart of training groups

Outcome measures and threshold definitions

The primary outcomes assessed were changes in technical performance (OSATS) and changes in efficiency (time to task completion). Both OSATS and time to task completion change were calculated using complementary approaches: actual percentage change, defined as ((Value₂ − Value₁) / Value₁) × 100, and normalized change (Observed Change − Minimum Change) / (Maximum Change − Minimum Change) × 100. For OSATS, higher normalized change values represent greater skill retention or improvement, while lower values reflect decline. For time, lower normalized change values reflect greater efficiency (i.e., faster performance), while higher values indicate worsening efficiency (i.e., slower performance).

To determine if the observed changes represented significant skill decay, we established thresholds based on a combination of three analytic approaches: distributional change, variability, and the Reliable Change Index (RCI). For OSATS, a reduction of ≥ 2 points between Time 1 and Time 2 were defined as a significant decline in performance. This was selected based on a median decline of 2 points across the cohort, standard deviation approximation, and an RCI estimate of 2.3 points, all of which support a ≥ 2-point cutoff. For efficiency, a ≥ 6-min increase in time to task completion was designated as a significant decline in procedural efficiency.

Statistical analysis

After verifying distributional assumptions using the Shapiro–Wilk test, paired comparisons were performed using paired t-tests for normally distributed data or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-parametric data. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to determine the time between drills cutoffs for significant decay. To determine which factors were associated with significant decline, multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted. Inter-rater reliability between graders was calculated using the Pearson’s correlation coefficient. All analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.3.1), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 132 h of video simulation were reviewed, and 45 residents were included in the analysis: 30 in the skill decay group and 15 in the training beyond proficiency group. In the skill decay group, 43.3% of residents identified as female and 56.7% as male. Half of the residents in this group had more than 90 days between their proficiency and return session. The median time to task completion increased from 30.5 min (IQR: 26–33) to 34.5 min (IQR: 30.5–39.5) upon return (p = 0.03) (Fig. 3A). Likewise, the median OSATS score at the time of proficiency was 30 (IQR: 29–31.8), which declined to 28 (IQR: 26.2–29) on their return attempt (p = 0.001) (Fig. 3B). Moreover, residents completed a median of 6 additional attempts in the training beyond proficiency group after reaching the passing benchmark. The median time to task completion decreased from 30.5 min (IQR: 28–33) to 25.5 min (IQR: 24.25–27.75; p = 0.01) (Fig. 3C), and the median OSATS score improved from 29.5 (IQR: 28.2–31.7) to 34 (IQR: 33–35; p = 0.001) (Fig. 3D). The intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.80 (95% CI: 0.76—0.84, p < 0.001) among the OSATS scores assessed by the two graders.Fig. 3Skill decay group: A Median time to task completion increase and B Median OSATS score decay. Skill decay and efficiency loss over time

Residents in the skill decay group experienced progressive declines in both technical performance and efficiency (Table 1). At the 3-month mark, OSATS scores declined by 11.7%, while time to task completion remained virtually unchanged (0.1%), suggesting early technical decay without immediate functional impact. However, after 3 months, OSATS scores had declined by 17.6% from baseline, and task completion time increased by 19.3% between 3 and 6 months, indicating growing deterioration in both performance and efficiency (Table 1). Efficiency continued to worsen over time, with time increases of 43.4% at 9 months and 48.2% at 12 months, highlighting substantial functional decay. Multivariable analysis

Logistic regression was performed to identify predictors of skills decay. For performance decline (OSATS drop ≥ 2 points), the only significant predictor was higher baseline OSATS score (OR 2.72; 95% CI: 1.28–8.40; p = 0.02), indicating that residents who initially performed at a higher level were more likely to experience measurable decline (Fig. 6). For efficiency loss (≥ 6-min increase), time between drills (OR 1.01 95% CI: 1.00–1.02; p = 0.04) was predictive and baseline time showed a trend toward predicting efficiency loss (OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.29–0.87, p = 0.05), though it did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Predictors of performance and efficiency decline after proficiency achievement

Median number of repetitions after proficiency was 5 (3—9). Residents who continued training after reaching proficiency demonstrated consistent improvement in both technical performance and task efficiency (Table 2). By 3 months, OSATS scores had improved by 10.5%. At 6 months, OSATS gains reached 17.1%, with time remaining stable. Discussion

This study evaluated skill decay and performance improvement patterns among general surgery residents participating in a structured robotic simulation curriculum focused on bowel anastomosis. We examined two distinct trainee trajectories: residents returning to simulation after a period of inactivity following proficiency, and those who deliberately continued training beyond proficiency. Our findings show that technical performance begins to decline after more than three months of inactivity, while efficiency loss becomes evident in less than six months. In contrast, continued deliberate practice beyond proficiency was associated with significant and measurable improvements in both technical performance and efficiency, offering important insights into how robotic simulation curricula may be optimized to improve retention.

This observed skill decay aligns with existing literature. Jenison et al. [1] successfully showed that robotic surgical skills significantly degrade within four weeks of inactivity, with retraining unable to fully restore baseline performance in inanimate drills in newly trained surgeons. In contrast, while a systematic review by Higgins et al. [13] also found that simulation-based medical education yields relatively high retention at short intervals, more substantial degradation occurs beyond a 90-day timeframe in multiple simulated scenarios. Our ROC analysis identified a threshold of 84 days (3 months) for performance decay and 188 days (6 months) for efficiency loss. These findings are consistent with several prior studies. For instance, Varley et al. [14] showed that laparoscopic skills were retained at four weeks but declined significantly by 12 weeks of inactivity, despite prior proficiency acquisition. Uribe et al. [15] also observed that all trainees who returned within 60 days retained skills, while those with a 90-day gap showed performance decline, though recoverable with a single session, suggesting that 90 days may be a critical threshold for skill retention in surgical simulation.

Additionally, our multivariable analysis identified predictors of decay. A higher baseline OSATS score remained the only significant predictor of a ≥ 2-point decline (OR: 2.72; p = 0.02), suggesting that high performers, having less margin for improvement, are more prone to measurable drop-off. Moreover, for a ≥ 6-min time increase, only days between drills was predictive (OR: 1.01; p = 0.04), reinforcing that prolonged intervals adversely affect performance. From a cognitive science perspective, this aligns with the well-established spacing effect, where learning is better retained when refreshed at optimal intervals [16]. Further support comes from Liu et al., who compared 3- and 6-month retention in robotic training. They found that participants in the 6-month group had significantly worse time to complete tasks, lower scores, and higher penalty scores in their initial return, similar to our ROC curves findings. These differences highlight the impact of longer gaps from the platform and suggested that retraining may be necessary [17].

Our subdomain-level analysis further revealed that OSATS components requiring higher-order integration were most vulnerable to decay. In contrast, those reflecting more fundamental psychomotor control, such as respect for tissue and time and motion, remained stable. This is supported by findings from Orlando et al. [18], who demonstrated that, even after 11—20 weeks gaps, medical students retained robotic simulation skills related to tissue handling and economy of motion significantly better than laparoscopic equivalents. These insights support a shift from rigid time-based retraining schedules to personalized re-engagement strategies, with competency-based refreshers focused on complex domains [18].

In parallel, we also found that continued deliberate practice beyond proficiency yields measurable performance enhancement. Residents who pursued extended simulation training improved across nearly all OSATS subdomains, indicating that extended practice fosters not just retention but higher-order performance. This is in line with findings by Kolozsvari et al. [19], who assessed retention in VR laparoscopy one month after simulation training and observed that training beyond proficiency on a simple task led to a higher starting point and faster acquisition of a secondary more complex skill. Although the benefits of training beyond proficiency did not further improve retention beyond baseline, early performance gains were evident [19]. More compelling evidence comes from Stefanidis et al. [20], who conducted a randomized trial demonstrating that training to automaticity, beyond standard proficiency, resulted in superior performance, fewer errors, and more durable retention in laparoscopic surgery [20]. Together, these findings support simulation as a platform for ongoing skill improvement. They also highlight the need for longitudinal curricula with structured refreshers and opportunities for training beyond proficiency.

Our findings carry important implications for simulation-based surgical education. Most notably, post-proficiency refreshers as an evidence-based intervention may help to mitigate early performance decline. Standard curricula that assess proficiency only once and allow prolonged gaps before re-engagement may overlook this critical window. Instead, simulation programs should incorporate scheduled reassessments, particularly for tasks involving high cognitive load or complex psychomotor demands. These refreshers can be brief, competency-based, and targeted toward domains most vulnerable to decay. Additionally, these targeted refreshers should be focused on the OSATS subdomains most susceptible to decline. As robotic surgery continues to expand within surgical training, aligning curriculum structure with these evidence-based timeframes for skill retention will be essential to support long-term surgical readiness and minimize skill decay.

While this study offers meaningful contributions to the field of simulation-based surgical education by incorporating objective thresholds to define skill decay, several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a single-institution study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit generalizability. Additionally, variability in resident training level, untracked surgical exposure in the operating room and outside the simulation laboratory, and differences in individual motivation to continue practicing may have introduced confounding factors, as differences in clinical rotations and operative experience could influence technical performance independently of simulation training. Future multicenter studies with standardized exposure tracking will be needed to validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that both technical performance and procedural efficiency in robotic simulation decline with time in the absence of continued practice. A significant deterioration in technical skill was observed as early as three months after achieving proficiency, while losses in efficiency became evident by six months. In contrast, residents who continued practicing beyond proficiency achieved measurable gains in both skill quality and task completion time, reinforcing the benefits of deliberate practice beyond proficiency. Collectively, these findings highlight the need for simulation-based curricula to include structured refreshers around the 3-month mark and sustained opportunities for advanced training to promote long-term skill retention.
