Executive Summary
In a collaborative project between Siemens Gamesa Renewable Energy and the National
Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), a cost-effective method to measure dynamic main shaft
torque and the influence of non-torque loads was applied to the gearbox of the Gamesa G97 2-
megawatt (MW) wind turbine at the NREL Flatirons Campus and verified through a field test
campaign that took place in 2023. The method involved the installation of fiber-optic sensors on
the ring gear of the gearbox. Additionally, traditional strain gauges and a rotary encoder were
installed on the main shaft to measure mechanical torque and orthogonal bending moments. We
compare the measured total main shaft bending moments from the field test campaign to those
predicted by an updated OpenFAST 1 model of the G97 wind turbine for similar environmental
conditions. We also assessed the sensitivity of the predicted bending moment to varying levels of
turbulence intensity, shear exponent, and yaw error.

This report outlines the steps taken to update, verify, and analyze the G97 model in OpenFAST
v3.5.2 (Jonkman et al. 2024). The original G97 files, dated from 2011, were likely used in
OpenFAST’s predecessor, FAST, and therefore needed extensive reorganization. Further, the
original G97 model lacked a controller that worked with 64-bit operating systems, so we
incorporated a controller designed in ROSCO. 2 Several proprietary specifications documents
were referenced to fill gaps in the G97 model, and any missing parameters were noted. Lastly,
the turbine was tested through various turbulent wind profiles generated with TurbSim. 3

OpenFAST models the main shaft as an unsupported beam that connects the rotor to the gearbox.
The results presented in this report required transformation, filtering, and processing to replicate
the configuration of the G97 2-MW turbine used in the field test campaign. Statistically, the
OpenFAST main shaft bending moment is similar to the collected field test data, with the caveat
that the simulation’s data range is quite large. Additionally, time series signals show that the
OpenFAST results and field test data are comparable despite the noted modeling assumptions.
Overall, the findings presented in this report show decent agreement between an OpenFAST
model and real-world turbine data and address any uncovered differences.

Section 1 highlights the importance of drivetrain reliability, describes the field test campaign,
and introduces the open-source simulation tools used for the analysis. Section 2 describes the
simulation modeling tools and how the G97 OpenFAST model was updated. Section 3 describes
our approach to derive main shaft bending moments. Section 4 presents the OpenFAST results
and compares them to data collected in the field test campaign. Finally, Section 5 presents
conclusions, reiterates key takeaways, and suggests future work.

1 Background
Drivetrain reliability, including gearboxes, remains a significant contributor to existing wind
turbine operations and maintenance costs (Veers et al. 2023). Up until 2009, the annual gearbox
failure rate averaged between 5% and 10% (Lantz 2013). Although much research, investment,
and industry effort have paid dividends and reduced the average annual replacement rate to 2.5%
for the U.S. land-based fleet (Pulikollu 2023), room for improvement remains (Haus et al. 2024).
At the same time, gearbox torque density (input mechanical torque per unit mass) has increased
such that some designs now have torque densities higher than 200 newton-meters per kilogram
(Nejad et al. 2022). The rapid pace at which torque density requirements have risen has led to
increasingly complex and lightweight designs. At the same time, these new turbines must
comply with stricter noise regulations and respond to more prominent dynamic interactions.
Altogether, new reliability challenges arise. Accurate knowledge of the rotor mechanical torque
and the influences from non-torque loads are essential to the reliability of these modern, highly
loaded gearbox designs, so they can be maintained or even improved upon while torque density
is increased and other design requirements are met (Gutierrez-Santiago et al. 2024, 2025).
Additionally, when rotor mechanical torque is measured in-service, better estimations of the
remaining useful life of the gearbox and drivetrain are possible (Keller 2024).

Generally, it is not practical nor economical to have sensors that provide detailed in-service
torque and non-torque measurements. It is possible to estimate the mechanical torque from the
electric currents in the generator, but this requires assumptions about the variable gearbox,
generator, and power converter efficiencies. This information is not available in high-load,
damaging events like emergency braking and low-voltage ride-through when the generator
disconnects from the grid (Keller 2024; Gutierrez-Santiago et al. 2025). The conventional
method to measure rotor mechanical torque and non-torque loads is based on bonding strain
gauges to the main shaft. In full Wheatstone bridge configurations, the strain gauges convert the
main shaft torsional and bending deformations to changes in electrical resistance while
minimizing sensitivity to temperature and off-axis loads. However, providing the excitation
voltage to the strain gauges and transferring the resulting signals from the rotating main shaft to a
stationary data logging system requires dedicated instrumentation and is typically not amenable
to wider in-service installation (Gutierrez-Santiago et al. 2024, 2025).

In a collaborative project between Siemens Gamesa Renewable Energy and the National
Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), a cost-effective method to measure dynamic main shaft
torque and the influence of non-torque loads was applied to the gearbox of the Gamesa G97
2-megawatt (MW) wind turbine at the NREL Flatirons Campus and verified through a field
measurement campaign (Gutierrez-Santiago et al. 2024, 2025). The method involves installing
fiber-optic sensors on the ring gear of the gearbox, from which measurements of ring gear
deformations can be correlated to rotor mechanical torque (Gutierrez-Santiago et al. 2022a,
2025), and the influence of rotor non-torque loads on the load-sharing behavior of the gearbox
can be assessed (Gutierrez-Santiago et al. 2022b, 2024). Traditional strain gauges and a rotary
encoder were also installed on the main shaft to measure mechanical torque and orthogonal
bending moments. In normal power production conditions, the torque measurements from both
systems can be correlated to the electrical torque estimated by the turbine controller; however,
the best and only correlation of the orthogonal bending moments is to the rotor overhung weight

 1

moment during idling conditions (Gutierrez-Santiago et al. 2024, 2025). This correlation requires
assumptions regarding the exact weight and distribution of the blade mass, location of the hub
and rotor center of gravity, and stiffness characteristics of the main bearing(s) and gearbox
torque arm elastomeric mounts. Additionally, a wiring error in the Wheatstone bridges for the
orthogonal bending moments led to concerns about their accuracy. For these reasons, an effort
was undertaken by the authors to compare the measured bending moments from the field test
campaign to those predicted by an OpenFAST model of the G97 wind turbine. This report
describes the results of that effort. Beyond the comparison of measured and modeled main shaft
bending moments described in this paper, the bending moments themselves directly influence the
loads that the main bearings and gearbox must support. These loads play a critical role in main
bearing and gearbox design and reliability (Bergua Archeli et al. 2021; Nejad et al. 2022;
Brasseur et al. 2024; Gutierrez-Santiago et al. 2024, 2025).

1.1 Field Test Campaign
The field measurement campaign was conducted from April 25 to July 31, 2023. The rotor
mechanical torque measured by the fiber-optic strain gauges was evaluated and compared to the
main shaft strain gauges (Gutierrez-Santiago et al. 2025), and influences of rotor non-torque
loads on the gearbox load-sharing were also assessed (Gutierrez-Santiago et al. 2024). However,
neither work directly assessed the measured orthogonal bending moments on the main shaft
because of the uncertainty of the measurements described earlier. The remainder of this
subsection will describe the Gamesa G97 wind turbine and the main shaft bending moment strain
gauges.

1.1.1 Gamesa G97 2-MW Wind Turbine
The wind turbine used in this campaign was the Gamesa G97 located at the NREL Flatirons
Campus, shown in Figure 1. It is a three-blade upwind configuration with a rotor diameter of 97
meters (m), a power output of 2 MW, and a swept area of 7,390 m2.

The G97 has a four-point-mount drivetrain, in which two separate spherical roller bearings
support the main shaft and transfer all rotor non-torque loads to the main frame. The main shaft
then transmits nearly all the torque to the gearbox, which is composed of a planetary stage with
three equally spaced planets and a floating sun pinion followed by two parallel stages with
helical gearing (Gutierrez-Santiago et al. 2024, 2025).

NREL installed and commissioned a Class III G97 turbine in 2011 then replaced it with a Class
II G97 turbine in 2013. 4 During its lifetime, Gamesa owned and operated the turbine. The
sensing systems and G97 turbine itself were decommissioned in August and September 2023,
respectively. The following subsections summarize the instrumentation pertinent to this report. A
full description of the instrumentation package was previously published by Gutierrez-Santiago
et al. (2024, 2025).

1.1.2 Main Shaft Bending Moment and Meteorological Instrumentation
Two full Wheatstone bridges consisting of four LEA-06-W250B-350 strain gauges each were
spot-welded on the G97 main shaft as shown in Figure 1 to measure main shaft orthogonal
bending moments. The bridges were located approximately halfway between the two main
bearings. A battery-powered V-Link transmitter system was used to transmit the bridge outputs
to a base station where they were recorded in units of volts (V). A CEV58M-1600 absolute
rotary shaft encoder was installed to measure absolute rotor and main shaft azimuth angle, with
the zero-position set when the blade labeled as “A” was pointing down. A second set of sensors
was previously installed on the NREL M4/site 4.4 meteorological tower located near the wind

1.2 Simulation Tools
The following subsections describe the open-source software packages that NREL maintains to
simulate turbines. Because of its proprietary data, the G97 OpenFAST model is not open source
and is therefore maintained on a private repository. Contact the authors for more information
about the G97 turbine model used in this report.

1.2.1 OpenFAST
OpenFAST (https://github.com/OpenFAST/openfast) is an open-source simulation framework to
model land-based and offshore wind turbines. Built on FAST v8, it was released in 2017 and is
actively maintained by an NREL team. The version used in this report is v3.5.2, released on
April 11, 2024. OpenFAST is a multiphysics, multi-fidelity tool for simulating the coupled
dynamic response of wind turbines (NREL 2025). Its framework contains computational
modules for aerodynamics, hydrodynamics (for offshore structures), controls and electrical
system dynamics, and structural dynamics. Users can specify myriad simulation settings or
turbine attributes to analyze a wide range of wind turbine configurations. Table 2 summarizes the
modules used for this analysis, and a more in-depth explanation can be found in (NREL 2025).

1.2.2 TurbSim
TurbSim (https://github.com/old-NWTC/TurbSim) is a code package that generates stochastic
turbulent wind profiles based on numerical meteorological conditions. This report uses TurbSim
v3.5.2, the same version as OpenFAST. According to the TurbSim User’s Guide (Kelley and
Jonkman 2007), it “numerically simulates time series of three-dimensional wind velocity vectors
at points in a vertical rectangular grid” and is used “to provide a numerical simulation of a full-
field flow that contains bursts of coherent turbulence that reflect the proper spatiotemporal
turbulent velocity field relationships seen in instabilities with nocturnal boundary layer flows.”
TurbSim generates wind profiles in several file formats that OpenFAST can read as inputs. For
this analysis we specified .bts files, which is a binary time series file.

Several key parameters are user-specified to instruct TurbSim how to generate a desired wind
profile. Table 3 summarizes the parameters used to define the turbulence intensity (TI).
IEC_WindType, specifies the wind type which is set to normal turbulence model (NTM).
IECturbc, or turbulence characteristic, may have values of “C,” “B,” “A,” or “A+,” all of which
correspond to the standard IEC 61400-1:2019 turbulence characteristics, with “A+” being the
most turbulent. Conversely, IECturbc can also have a numerical value between 0 and 100 as
whole-number percentages, representing the ratio between the standard deviation of the wind
speed and the mean wind speed at hub height. URef represents the mean wind speed at hub
height. The horizontal flow angle, HFlowAng, represents the wind direction relative to the rotor.
Lastly, PLExp is the vertical shear power law exponent and can have a numerical value between
-1.0 and 1.0, where the IEC standard is 0.2 for land-based wind turbines.

 Table 3. TurbSim Parameters

 Parameter Description Units

 URef Mean (total) velocity at the reference height m/s
 HFlowAng Horizontal mean flow (skew) angle deg

 m/s = meters per second, deg = degrees

1.2.3 ROSCO
NREL’s Reference Open-Source Controller (ROSCO) (Abbas et al. 2022) was developed to
provide a modular control system architecture with a Fortran-based software structure that
follows the bladed-style dynamic library link (DLL) interface. In addition to a compiled DLL
file, ROSCO also generates an input file (DISCON.in) that resembles OpenFAST’s various user-
specified input files. ROSCO provides a reference turbine controller with industry-standard
functionality and can be tuned for a variety of turbine models. At the time of this analysis,
ROSCO 2.4.1 included yaw controller, individual pitch controller, and variable speed controllers
to suit a turbine through all operational wind conditions. According to its documentation, it
parses OpenFAST inputs and outputs and interfaces with the ServoDyn module.

2.1 Coordinate Systems
The following subsections define the coordinate systems and naming conventions used in
OpenFAST. Note that Figures 3–5 show a two-blade downwind turbine in which the positive x-
direction points downwind and out of the rotor, but the coordinate frames also apply to a three-
blade upwind turbine in which the positive x-direction still points downwind but out of the back
of the nacelle. OpenFAST’s naming convention appends a single letter to indicate in which
coordinate system an output resides.

2.1.1 Nacelle/Yaw Coordinate System
The nacelle/yaw (n) coordinate system is a rotational frame and has its origin at the center of the
top of the tower. It rotates with respect to the tower top/base-plate coordinate system. The
parameters NacCMxn, NacCMyn, and NacCMzn describe the nacelle center of mass (CM), shaft
orientation, and hub position in this coordinate frame. Figure 3 shows the xn, yn, and zn axis
directions.

2.1.2 Shaft Coordinate System
The shaft (s) coordinate system is a fixed frame that does not rotate with the rotor. It does,
however, turn with the tower and yaw with the nacelle. The shaft bending moment at the hub is
expressed in this coordinate system. Figure 4 shows the xs, ys, and zs axis directions.

2.1.3 Hub Coordinate System
The hub (h) coordinate system is a rotational frame with its origin at the apex of the rotor or the
plane of a non-coned rotor. The hub coordinate system rotates with the rotor. All aerodynamic
forces and moments act in the hub coordinate system. Figure 5 shows the directions of the xh, yh,
and zh axes.

2.2 Updating OpenFAST Input Files
The original G97 FAST model files were from 2011, which means they were likely developed to
suit FAST v7 or earlier. Since then, OpenFAST has had three major releases and is not
compatible with the original G97 files. Updating the G97 began by assessing what files,
variables, and data existed and comparing them to a wind turbine model that is known to be
compatible with OpenFAST v3.5.2. For this analysis, we used the International Energy Agency
Wind Technology Collaboration Programme (IEA Wind) 22-MW reference wind turbine
(RWT) 5 and used its files as a new framework to map the current G97 parameters. The new G97
files follow a similar naming convention to the IEA Wind 22-MW RWT, where each file name
includes the turbine origin/owner, turbine rating, rotor radius, and filename. For example, GWT-

main shaft bending moment is accounted for in our postprocessing methodology described in
Section 3.1. However, any potential difference in blade stiffness characteristics between the

3 Methodology
This section describes a simple drivetrain analytical model used to determine the main shaft
bending moments from aerodynamic loads generated in OpenFAST. Because OpenFAST
drivetrain modeling capabilities are relatively limited, extra postprocessing steps were required
to emulate main bearings and estimate the local bending moments along the main shaft.

3.1 Main Shaft Bending Moment Diagram
In its simplest form, the drivetrain can be considered as a simply supported, rigid beam in
bending with point forces and masses representing rotor aerodynamic forces and moments, rotor
and gearbox weight, and main bearing reactions. A free-body diagram of the drivetrain is shown
in Figure 9, referenced to the same nacelle (n) and shaft (s) coordinate systems as previously
described in Figure 3 and Figure 4. All forces and moments shown are illustrated acting on the
main shaft.

3.2 Using OpenFAST to Analyze the Main Shaft
The following subsections describe the postprocessing approach that we used to incorporate
OpenFAST results to determine the main shaft bending moments in Equations (3) and (4). A
verification of the approach is first described in this section, in which the main shaft bending
moment at the FB determined from Equation (4) is compared to the moment at the same location
directly computed by OpenFAST. The verification enables confident comparison of OpenFAST
simulation results combined with Equation (3) to the field test campaign data described in
Section 4, along with some initial insights into the influences of environmental modeling
parameters on the main shaft bending moment.

 19

3.2.1 Filtering Output Data
Despite our efforts to update the G97 OpenFAST model and develop and tune a ROSCO
controller as described in Sections 2.2 and 2.3, the OpenFAST simulations resulted in rotor
loads—including torque and moments—that included dynamics from other structural modes.
When plotting the time series signals, there appeared to be a vibration or oscillation in addition
to the base signal. We postulated that due to missing information or improperly tuned
parameters, such as blade, tower, or drivetrain stiffness, some modes in this multi-physics model
may be resonating. Appendix A illustrates the extent of the resonance that accompanies the load
signals. We applied two different filtering methods to compare our data, even though some
models may not have been optimally tuned.

 • We used a fast Fourier transformation and an inverse fast Fourier transformation to apply
 a low-pass filter using the rotor 1P frequency as the cutoff frequency to isolate the main
 shaft dynamics from other structural frequencies. We used the average rotor speed to
 determine the rotor 1P frequency in hertz and found that this approach could be applied
 to any wind condition. Appendix Figures A2 to A5 illustrate the unfiltered load outputs
 and the data filtering processes.
 • We calculated the per-revolution average of each unfiltered load output to compare them
 to the field campaign test data and reduced the number of points, similar to a load
 revolution distribution (LRD) analysis used to summarize gearbox loads (Guiterrez-
 Santiago et al. 2025). The LRD strategy was applied in a similar manner to the measured
 G97 bending moments described in Section 4.1.

3.2.2 Verifying Front Bearing Calculation
Because OpenFAST v3.5.2 does not model any main bearings or other drivetrain supports, as
described earlier in Section 3.1, we knew there would be no OpenFAST signal to directly
compare the field test campaign data to. However, other works have implemented novel methods
to analyze drivetrains in OpenFAST (Krathe et al. 2025), which were added in later versions of
OpenFAST v4.0.0. OpenFAST v3.5.2 does provide two outputs named LSSGagMys and
LSSGagMzs, which stand for low-speed shaft gauge moment about the y-axis (and z-axis) in the
shaft coordinate system. This output serves as a virtual strain gauge that predicts the bending
moment at a desired location along the main shaft. We utilized the parameter ShftGagL, which
stands for shaft gauge length to measure the bending moment 1.566 m aft of the rotor apex to
emulate the FB location, as shown in Figure 9, for comparison to Equation (4), which is
calculated from the rotor weight and OpenFAST rotor loads.

Figure 11 compares the time series of the total predicted bending moment at the G97 FB from
LSSGagM (blue) and our calculation (dark-green dashed) from Equation (4) for a wind profile
with an average wind speed of 17 m/s with 14% TI. Additionally, the LRD of the FB bending
moment, or per-revolution average (white/black triangle), is shown, and it traces the 1P filtered
signal. The predicted main shaft bending moment at the FB is often near the bending moment at
the FB in idling conditions of 552 kN-m. However, it can be as high as 1,900 kN-m for two brief
periods, or more than 3 times the static moment. Although this may seem extreme, previous
mechanical load testing on a 1.5-MW wind turbine in the same range of TI commonly measured
maximum main shaft bending moments at the measurement location of approximately 4 times
the static moment (Santos and van Dam 2015).

modeled, and therefore using LSSGagM to predict bending moment downwind of the FB is not

transform and filter the OpenFAST rotor aerodynamic loads is sound. Furthermore, the plot
shows that the LRD strategy produces the same trend as the 1P filtered data using fewer points.
Thus, the combination of processing OpenFAST rotor aerodynamic loads with Equation (3) and
determining the per-revolution average should be a reliable approach to compare the measured
main shaft bending moments from the field test campaign to those derived from OpenFAST.

3.3 Sensitivity Analysis
We devised three conditions to serve as a sensitivity analysis to assess their effect on the main
shaft bending moment. We tested the OpenFAST G97 model with a range of TI, vertical shear,
and yaw error values. The goal was to observe how these conditions individually impact the
main shaft bending moment at the measurement location and discuss any noteworthy trends.
Another goal was to cover a range of conditions such that we encapsulated the range of bending
moments measured during the field test campaign.

During the G97 field test campaign, only those operations that had a minimum of 150 kW of
electrical power were processed and analyzed. This resulted in 837 10-minute time series, or
139.5 hours of data (Gutierrez-Santiago et al. 2024, 2025). Figure 12 shows the range of TI and

 21

mean of the absolute value of the yaw error based on these 10-minute time series. Although TI
over 25% and even up to 40% was observed in a few cases at lower wind speeds, TI was more
commonly under 20%, especially at higher wind speeds. Yaw errors were commonly less than
±10 degrees, with a few 10-minute periods as high as ±20 degrees. Examining further, individual
LRD averages for yaw error were by far most commonly within ±10 degrees; however,
occasional extremes up to ±60 degrees were observed in the LRD averages.

4 Results and Discussion
This section begins with a statistical examination of the main shaft bending moment at the
measurement location for the different range of conditions outlined in Section 3.3. After
examining the individual effects of TI, shear exponent, and yaw error with the G97 OpenFAST
model, we compared selected simulation sets to the G97 field test campaign data.

4.1 Sensitivity Analysis
Figure 14 shows an example statistical result for the average per-revolution main shaft bending
moment at the measurement location from the combination of OpenFAST rotor loads with
Equation (3) over the full range of simulated mean wind speeds. Two cases are represented in the
plot. This first case is for 12% TI with no vertical shear and no yaw error, as listed in Table 10.
The color bar indicates the number of occurrences. For comparison, the second case consisting of
the white/black squares show the per-revolution averaged results for purely steady winds (i.e., TI
= 0%). The steady winds result in a nearly constant main shaft bending moment around
420 kN-m, which is consistent with the bending moment in static conditions described in
Section 3.1. Typical bending moments with 12% TI are similar to those in steady winds;
however, the range of moments increases with wind speed. For some rotor revolutions in
Region 3 above the rated wind speed of 11 m/s, the bending moment can be as low as 100 kN-m
or more than 800 kN-m, the latter of which is over twice as much as in static conditions or in
steady winds.

4.1.1 Turbulence Intensity
The TI sensitivity analysis shows how the OpenFAST G97 model responds to time-varying
changes in horizontal wind speed. We simulated 12%, 14%, 16%, and 24% TI conditions as

 24

listed in Table 10, which are expected values at the Flatirons Campus and, as shown in
Figure 12, well within the measured field test campaign data. Figure 15 includes plots that
correspond to each TI value with color bars that indicate the number of occurrences. Every wind
profile had 0 vertical shear and 0 yaw error to distribute the thrust loads more evenly across the
rotor area and isolate the effects of TI. As expected, the variance in bending moment increases
with both wind speed and TI. At low wind speeds and low TI, the density of points is much
tighter around the moment in steady winds of 420 kN-m. Conversely, high wind speeds with
high TI have a sparser density of points from the assumed uniform wind speed distribution. For
the 12% TI case, the main shaft bending moment at the measurement location commonly ranges
between 200 and 800 kN-m. Greater variances in horizontal wind speeds at higher wind speeds
causes much larger maximum bending moments on the shaft. In the most extreme case at 24% TI
and the highest wind speeds, the moment can be as high as 1,500 kN-m. This is almost 4 times
the static moment at the same location, similar to the ratio demonstrated earlier in Figure 11 and
prior testing on a 1.5-MW turbine with similar TI values (Santos and van Dam 2015).

4.1.2 Shear Exponent
The shear exponent sensitivity analysis shows how the main shaft bending moments change
based on the wind speed’s vertical distribution across the rotor area. We simulated shear
exponent values, PLExp, of -0.3, -0.15, 0.15, and 0.3 as listed in Table 10, whereas the IEC

 25

standard for PLExp is 0.2. All cases include a TI of 12%. Figure 16 includes plots that
correspond to each shear exponent value with color bars that indicate the number of occurrences.
In a positive shear case, the higher wind speeds apply more thrust on the upper half of the rotor,
causing a large positive aerodynamic pitching moment. That pitching moment counters the
moment due to the rotor and hub weight. A PLExp of 0.15 reduces the average main shaft
bending moment by approximately 200 kN-m and reduces the range of bending moments, as
shown in the bottom-left plot. Conversely, extreme negative shears increase the bending moment
in the shaft, as depicted in the top two plots. When combined with the effect of 12% TI,
maximum values of 1,400 kN-m are reached for a shear exponent of -0.3, similar to the 24% TI
case alone described in the previous section.

 Each plot contains the per-revolution averaged data from 5 minutes of simulation data over eight different wind

4.1.3 Yaw Error
The yaw error sensitivity analysis shows how the loads on the main shaft change with nacelle
angle with respect to incoming wind direction. We simulated yaw errors of -20, -10, 10, and 20
degrees, which, as shown in Figure 12, were consistent with yaw errors recorded during the field
test campaign. Figure 17 includes plots that correspond to each yaw error value with color bars
that indicate the number of occurrences. Note that negative yaw error means incoming winds are
from the right, and positive yaw error means incoming winds are from the left when facing
upwind. The main shaft bending moments for negative yaw errors are slightly higher in Region 2

 26

than positive yaw errors. A wake-steering study showed that a low-speed shaft bending moment
of the lead turbine increased with negative inflow angles and decreased with positive inflow
angles in Region 2 conditions (Thedin et al. 2024). Additionally, a report assessing component
loads attribute trend of the gravity loading being relieved by aerodynamic loading (Damiani et al.
2018). Our results show that in Region 3, the trend appears to flip, where positive yaw error
increases bending moment and negative yaw error decreases bending moment in the main shaft.
We suspect that this load reversal may be related to the controller. In Region 3 the controller
attempts to maximize power output by pitching the blades, which changes the flap-wise (or
edgewise) loads. This trend calls for future investigation.

4.1.4 Summary
Thus far, we have discussed the TI, shear exponent, and yaw error sensitivity analyses primarily
as separate conditions, because OpenFAST allows us to isolate them. Figure 18 combines the
results from Sections 4.1.1, 4.1.2, and 4.1.3 and is plotted in the same fashion. What we observe
is that when these results are combined, the range of bending moments is rather large. By
superimposing these results, we recognize that we are suggesting that the conditions in our
analysis are mutually exclusive. However, we know that is not the case because as we previously
showed, some conditions may increase or decrease bending moment. Therefore, if we simulated
every permutation of TI, shear exponent, and yaw error, we may predict a slightly different

 27

distribution. We postulate that some outliers may become more extreme and at the same time,
the density of points may increase around the average bending moment. Regardless of the model
limitations or environmental conditions, this plot shows that the average predicted main shaft
moment from OpenFAST was roughly 400 to 420 kN-m, which is relatively close to the
395 kN-m static moment approximation made in Section 3.

The range in predicted bending moment from OpenFAST was quite large as shown in Figure 19.
So, we visually compared the field test campaign data with our results from Figure 15 to

test campaign was estimated to be 0.11, as show in Figure 13. Figure 19 shows a side-by-side

during the field test campaign because the G97 data do not align with our negative shear (-0.3, -

in Figure 12. The 12% TI and shear exponent of 0.15 case is anything but an extreme wind
profile, yet the main shaft bending loads can be nearly twice as much as the static load. Further,
this shows how significant the role of TI is with respect to main shaft bending moment.

5 Conclusions
Updating the original G97 OpenFAST files was a straightforward, yet arduous task. Since
OpenFAST is continuously improving, turbine models often need to be manually updated to
ensure compatibility. Ideally, a future addition to OpenFAST will be to emulate multipoint
drivetrain support systems, such as main bearings and torque arms, to more accurately simulate
main shaft and drivetrain loads. While the G97 2-MW turbine installed at the Flatirons Campus
has since been decommissioned, our analysis yielded some positive results. We showed that the
processed main shaft strain data from the G97 field test campaign (Gutierrez-Santiago et al.
2024, 2025), with an average static moment of 400 kN-m and a range of 100 to 600 kN-m, are
within the boundaries of the OpenFAST simulations assuming 12% TI, 0.15 shear exponent, and
low yaw error conditions. This coherence between simulation data and field test data indicates
that despite a wiring error in the Wheatstone bridge, the processing methods applied to the
measured main shaft strain data likely minimized the impacts of the reported wiring issue during
the field test campaign. A caveat to our sensitivity analysis is that conditions with high wind
speeds and high TI yielded a large variance in main shaft bending moment. Therefore, the
boundary that encompassed all the OpenFAST results was much larger than the boundary that
encompassed all the field test data. Although extreme values of TI, shear exponent, and/or yaw
error resulted in main shaft bending moments up to 4 times the static moment, as shown by the
OpenFAST simulations, bending moments in excess of 3 to 4 times the static moments were also
observed in a separate Flatirons Campus field test. Overall, statistics and 10-minute averages of
field test measurements seem to have similar trends to corresponding simulation results. A
valuable future work would be to use v4.0.0 or later of OpenFAST that incorporates the novel
drivetrain model (Krathe et al. 2025) and compare our results. Further, we would like to compile
OpenFAST to be compatible with the 32-bit proprietary controller for comparison. Lastly, we
would also like to execute additional OpenFAST simulations by using TurbSim to replicate wind
profiles recorded during the field test campaign.

Figure A1 illustrates some high-level results of an OpenFAST simulation with 17 m/s wind
speed, 14% TI, 0 shear, and 0 yaw error. The plots, from top to bottom, show the wind speed,

signals. Observe that for outputs like wind speed, torque, and OOP deflection, these signal
include other structural dynamics, whereas outputs like rotor speed and blade pitch, which
include rotational inertia or controller effects, have smoother signals. Outputs that might be
subject to structural modes or deflection that may be improperly tuned could be a culprit or
OpenFAST is simply accounting for other structural modes. All OpenFAST simulations had a
disabled drivetrain flag, DrTrDOF, such that the drivetrain was assumed to be a rigid body for
torsional loads during this analysis. Note that with the best available specifications for the G97
turbine, we observed that adding torsional stiffness and damping did not reduce the excitations.
We found that the 1P and LRD filtering strategies effectively isolated the drivetrain dynamics
from the other structural frequencies.
