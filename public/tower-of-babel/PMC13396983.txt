Frontline Healthcare Professionals' Perceptions of the Duty to Care During the COVID-19 Pandemic: A Multi-Method Study in Mozambique
Authors: Ângela Alface, Graça Salomé, Khátia Munguambe, Troy D. Moon, Esperança Sevene, Elizabeth Heitman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
COVID-19 intensified ethical tensions between clinicians' duty to care and self-protection amid PPE shortages. We explored frontline healthcare professionals' perspectives in Maputo, Mozambique. Semi-structured interviews with healthcare professionals at four hospitals (April–June 2022) were recorded in Portuguese, transcribed, and thematically analyzed. Fixed-response items were captured in REDCap and summarized descriptively. Fifty-three respondents described varied perspectives on duty. Nearly half affirmed unconditional obligations grounded in professional ethics. Many framed duty as contingent on available PPE and institutional support; 8% were unsure. Themes included irregular PPE distribution, unequal training, and reliance on informal information, shaping perceived risk, preparedness, and responsibility. Quantitative summaries echoed these patterns: only 58% reported receiving training, and as PPE re-supply declined many felt exposed. Sustaining duty to care in resource-constrained settings during an outbreak requires reliable PPE provision, equitable and timely training, and trustworthy communication systems to protect workers and strengthen health-system resilience. Background

During the height of the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021), healthcare professionals across the globe played a crucial role in the response, often facing serious risks to their own personal safety [1-4]. Risks to healthcare professionals were especially high in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), as the pandemic placed strain on the resources of health systems, and access to personal protective equipment (PPE) was often limited [5]. Studies from other LMICs, such as Pakistan, have demonstrated that pre-pandemic PPE training significantly impacted healthcare professionals' safety during the crisis response [6]. But reports abounded in both the scientific literature and in popular news that in many low-resource environments even the implementation of such simple measures as providing face masks to coughing patients, or ensuring available water for handwashing, could pose challenges [7, 8]. Recommended physical distancing in primary-level hospitals was difficult due to overcrowding and inadequate space and ventilation [9]. Consequently, a high mortality rate from COVID-19 was anticipated among healthcare professionals and their families [10, 11].

Mozambique, a Portuguese-speaking country in southeastern Africa, is ranked 182nd out of 193 countries on the United Nations Development Program's (UNDP) Human Development index (HDI) for 2023–2024 [12]. During non-pandemic times Mozambique faces significant challenges to the delivery of quality health services, and the country is marked by shortages of both human and material resources [13]. To the extent that it could, the Mozambican Ministry of Health (Ministério de Saúde, MISAU) tried to promote globally-recommended mitigation measures among the general public, including hand washing, use of masks, physical distancing, and, once vaccines became available, widespread vaccination. In addition, the Mozambican National Health System (NHS) developed a National Preparedness and Response Plan for COVID-19, which resulted in guidelines and standards of conduct based on international standards, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) [14, 15]. This plan consisted of providing the NHS with sufficient resources to guarantee a stock of medicines, laboratory reagents, disinfectants, medical devices, and PPE to support the response to COVID-19. More specifically, the plan included the acquisition of furniture and equipment; medicines; medical-surgical material to equip the intensive care units (ICUs); and a plan to train national, provincial and district-level clinicians, maintenance staff, and support staff for a rapid response to the COVID-19 outbreak. Finally, the plan described additional support that included guaranteeing food supplies for health workers and patients; psychosocial support for patients, family members and healthcare personnel; and telemedicine options to limit the need for patients to visit the health facility [16].

Despite the existence of these plans, by August 2022, roughly 4,200/38,000 (11%) healthcare professionals in Mozambique had become infected with the virus [17, 18]. This significant risk raises an urgent ethical question: What is a healthcare professional's obligation to provide care to the sick within health systems that may not be able to provide them resources for self-protection?

The extent to which healthcare professionals are obligated to put themselves at risk in delivering care is not a new question and remains controversial. The ability to count on healthcare professionals' service is essential to public health planning for epidemics. Responses to this ethical tension are often driven by apprehension about contracting an incurable disease or the fear of passing it on to a loved one. Historical evidence suggests that since Hippocratic times, doctors have frequently chosen to avoid patients with deadly epidemic diseases [19]. More recently, similar behavior was seen in response to the initial cases of HIV/AIDS, as well as during the 2013–2016 West African Ebola epidemic [20]. In situations involving infectious diseases, the duty to care entails caring for patients even when there is some degree of risk in doing so. As McDougall and colleagues discuss, healthcare professionals are only human, and their ethical obligation to work is neither unlimited nor absolute [21]. Moreover, healthcare professionals are themselves a vital resource, essential for delivering patient care, and must be used wisely. The American Medical Association's (AMA) Code of Ethics provides a nuanced perspective: “The ethical obligation to provide care remains constant, even when physicians face risks beyond the norm to their own safety, health, or life. However, physicians are encouraged to balance the immediate benefits to individual patients with their ability to continue providing care in the future” [22]. During a healthcare emergency, patients may suffer increased harm if healthcare personnel are physically or mentally incapable of fulfilling their duties, leading to a reduction in the available workforce [1, 23-25].

The ethical issues and tensions that arose concerning the duties of healthcare professionals amid the COVID-19 pandemic have been the focus of studies from high-income countries [26-28]. However, little has been published related to healthcare professionals in low-resource settings. Our study aimed to address this gap and evaluate the perceived ethical challenges en-countered by frontline healthcare professionals in Mozambique in carrying out their professional duties during the COVID-19 pandemic. Our primary objective was to learn how healthcare professionals assessed their experiences working during the pandemic, their sense of risk as related to the materials and resources available to them, and their perceptions of the conditions of their health facilities to respond to the pandemic. Further, we sought to identify healthcare professionals' perceptions of the ethical tensions that were created during the pandemic through balancing their own well-being against their duty to care for patients with either confirmed or suspected COVID-19.

Methodology

Study Design

We conducted a cross-sectional, observational study using a multi-method approach, with a qualitative emphasis. Data were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire that included both qualitative questions, exploring respondents' perspectives, and quantitative questions capturing demographic and other numerical results. This design allowed for a comprehensive understanding of the study topic by integrating qualitative insights with contextual quantitative data. Interviews were conducted between April 1 and July 31, 2022.

Study Setting and Study Population

The study population consisted of frontline healthcare professionals directly involved in the care of COVID-19 patients in four hospitals in Maputo Province, Mozambique: Machava General Hospital, Matola Provincial Hospital, Matola II Health Center, and Machava II Health Center (Figure 1). These four hospitals were chosen as they were the four facilities in Maputo Province whose emergency departments experienced the highest volume of COVID-19 patients at the time the study began.

Selection of study participants was intentional and based on job roles, with a focus on physicians/clinical officers, nurses and other service assistants involved in direct patient care. Clinical officers or non-physician clinicians are independent healthcare professionals with 18-24 months of training who frequently function as the main care provider with support from a physician [29]. Service assistants represent a cadre of lay healthcare professionals who provide support to nurses, patient transport, as well as general cleaning of the facilities (including medical waste) [30]. Inclusion criteria required the participants to have been in their position for a minimum of 2 years. In order to reach saturation, we sought to interview five physicians/clinical officers, five nurses, and five service assistants at each of the four facilities for a total of 60 participants.

Study Tools and Procedures

We developed a semi-structured questionnaire that utilized both fixed response and open-ended questions. Participants were asked for their sociodemographic information as well as their perceptions of existing NHS plans to protect healthcare providers during the pandemic. Additionally, questions were included to explore participants' perceptions of the tension between their own well-being and professional duty to care (Table 1).

Data Processing and Management

Interviews were conducted in Portuguese, in person, and audio recorded, with responses to the fixed-response questions registered on a paper data collection form. Following completion of the interview, quantitative data from the data collection form were entered into an electronic database developed in REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture). Additionally, the audio recordings were transcribed, word for word, in Portuguese and a copy of the transcription stored in REDCap. Weekly data quality checks were performed to ensure the consistency and completeness of the transcriptions. After analysis was completed, selected quotes intended for dissemination at international conferences and for publication were translated into English by study team members fluent in both Portuguese and English. The English translations were crafted to convey the original meaning of the quotes, with final wording decisions made through team review and consensus.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize respondents' sociodemographic characteristics using frequencies and proportions. Quantitative data were analyzed in Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS)V20. We showed variables as counts and column percentages by role. We compared groups with Pearson's chi-square (or Fisher's exact test when needed) and reported effect size (Cramer's V). α = 0.05. Interview transcriptions were coded and analyzed by content in Microsoft Excel. We generated coding reports and summarized data according to organized themes.

Conceptual Framework

We analyzed interview data using Thematic Analysis, following a six-step process of familiarization with the transcripts, generation of initial codes, grouping codes into categories, development of candidate themes, refinement of themes through continuous comparison, and final definition and naming of themes. To situate these themes in a normative context, we applied the Ethical Duty to Care framework, which distinguishes absolute, conditional, and absent duties in relation to professional obligation, reciprocity, and risk [31-35]. This dual approach ensured both a systematic analysis of respondents' accounts and a theoretically grounded interpretation of how healthcare professionals navigated duty to care during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Results

Participant Profile and Contextual Framing

We interviewed a total of 53 healthcare professionals from across our four study facilities (Table 2), including physicians/clinical officers (30%), nurses (38%), and service assistants (32%). Most had over 5 years of experience (57%), offering grounded insights into ethical and emotional challenges during the pandemic. The sample was predominantly female (81%), with many in stable relationships (66%). This diversity of roles and lived experiences enriched the qualitative exploration of duty of care, institutional trust, and moral resilience in low-resource healthcare settings.

Strengthening the Frontline: COVID-19 Training and Knowledge Gaps Among Healthcare Professionals

In order to support countries' preparedness to respond to the COVID-19 pandemic, the WHO recommended the development and provision of training programs focused on infection prevention and control (IPC) measures; worker protection and safety; disease prevention and rapid response; and screening, diagnosis and case management [10, 15]. In alignment with these recommendations, Mozambique's National COVID-19 Pandemic Response Plan (2021) outlined a structured training framework developed by MISAU to provide healthcare professionals and public health professionals with current knowledge on epidemiological trends, IPC practices, and patient management protocols, all crucial for reducing COVID-19 transmission in healthcare settings and the broader community [16]. With this in mind, we questioned respondents about any COVID-19 specific training that they had received, with only 31 (58%) reporting that they had received any formal training. Of those that had received training, nurses were the most frequent 17/20 (85%) (Table 3), followed by physicians/clinical officers 7/16 (44%), and then service assistants 7/17 (41%). Beyond formal programs, 48 (91%) of respondents reported that they had sought information through informal channels, primarily television and social media, highlighting the reliance on non-institutional sources for pandemic knowledge. Among the training received, key topics included General information about COVID-19 disease (24%), Mitigation strategies for COVID-19 prevention (23%), Infection control and biosafety in the workplace (20%), Treatments for COVID-19 (18%), and Ethical considerations during COVID-19 (13%).

Perceptions of Professional Duty and Availability of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) Supply and Use

According to MISAU guidelines, Mozambique's national health system was expected to supply frontline healthcare professionals with a complete package of PPE, including surgical and N95 masks, caps, coveralls, gloves, shoe covers, protective glasses or face shields, and gowns. While nearly all healthcare professionals acknowledged receiving PPE at some stage (Table 4), they observed that consistency lapsed as the pandemic progressed. Initially, PPE was distributed daily, offering a sense of protection and preparedness. However, as the pandemic progressed, distribution intervals were stretched, first to weekly and eventually to only once a month. In reality, only a small fraction of professionals reported having guaranteed access to PPE at the beginning of each shift throughout the pandemic, emphasizing the growing mismatch between policy expectations and frontline experience.

Thirty-three respondents (64%) reported that re-supply of PPE was insufficient, often leading to the reuse of coveralls, gloves, or masks meant for single use. One participant noted:

As for PPE, we must reuse the coveralls. It is very annoying, because it's not mine, I don't even know who it belonged to.

Others emphasized that even basic materials, such as disinfectant alcohol, were sometimes lacking.

There was a moment when we were lacking even disinfectant alcohol itself.

The perceived lack of adequate protection was closely tied to feelings of vulnerability. Nearly half of respondents (47%) stated they did not feel safe with the limited PPE provided (Table 5), particularly given the expectation of its prolonged use. As one participant described:

At the time when the pandemic was at its peak, we had a great lack of PPE… whoever could acquire it had to rely on other colleagues.

Frontline workers also described how shortages forced them to adopt improvised practices that they felt were unsafe, such as working with only one pair of gloves or reusing gowns after meals. As one respondent recalled:

I needed to eat so I had to wear those coveralls and then take them off, go eat, then put the same gown back on.

Some respondents contrasted their experiences with practices observed in other countries, underscoring a perception of unequal standards:

From the experiences I have had in reference hospitals in other countries… it was clear that you could not wear PPE more than once.

Unprepared and Under-Resourced: Critical Gaps in Healthcare Facility Readiness During the COVID-19 Pandemic

Most respondents (75%) said their facilities could not handle COVID-19 care or ensure workers' safety. Respondents pointed to missing basic infrastructure for infection control. As one clinician put it:

We don't have rooms for disinfection, we don't have rooms for changing, disposal…we always had to improvise buckets.

Patient flow was also unsafe:

Patients with cough mixed in with others…COVID patients were placed in the chronic disease ward…there should have been a place a little farther away.

Respondents also reported gaps in medicines and essential equipment:

There was a time when there was a shortage of azithromycin, which we used a lot with our COVID patients.

Creating even a basic ICU remained problematic:

We don't have enough humidifiers, articulated beds…We don't have enough oxygen masks, so we borrow them.

What emerges is a picture of healthcare delivery characterized by improvised responses to infrastructure gaps, dangerous physical layouts, and ongoing stockouts, circumstances that systematically undermined both operational capacity and staff safety.

Perceived Ethical Gaps and Discriminatory Practices in COVID-19 Patient Care

Most participants (75%) reported not witnessing questionable or unethical conduct during COVID-19, however, among the 13 (25%) who did (Table 6), concerns converged on refusal to provide care and perceived discrimination against suspected or confirmed cases. Fear, amplified by high mortality and gaps in equipment often led to healthcare professionals' hesitation to perform procedures or approach unstable patients:

As demand was high…we experienced many deaths…professionals were understandably fearful…I believe they also were afraid.

There were colleagues who were afraid to approach (a patient) …it's really COVID, I'm afraid.

This sometimes translated into minimal-contact assessments and delayed basic assistance, even when PPE was available, such as:

They were afraid of touching the patient…alleging lack of gloves…only a visual examination…it felt like discrimination.

The patient asked for water. No health professional came… because of fear… (Interviewer: But did they have PPE?) Yes…This happened several times.

While reports of unethical behavior were few, some respondents described avoidance rooted in fear and perceived discrimination, highlighting potential inequities in care during surges (Table 7).

Healthcare Professionals' Perceptions of the Balance Between Their Duty to Care and Personnel Well-Being

Nearly half of respondents (47%) affirmed a duty to care even without PPE, framing the duty as part of their professional identity:

As a healthcare professional…I have an obligation to care for whomever the patient may be…I am a doctor, and I do not treat illnesses. I treat patients.

It was a choice I made…you end up doing things you shouldn't do…but it's my obligation.

In parallel, many emphasized protecting themselves and their families. Roughly one-third (30%) refused to deliver care without PPE because of the risk of exposure:

I have to be well to be able to take care of others…I first need to take care of myself, being protected.

Without PPE…I could pass this disease on to my family…we have to have complete PPE and then we can care.

Another 15% adopted a conditional stance, that duty applies only when minimum protections are ensured:

My mission is to save lives…if the necessary conditions are provided…Not having PPE makes it complicated, for my own protection…I have to find the necessary materials to work.

Not without PPE…it's an obligation, I don't have to treat that patient without PPE, but I also have to let it go.

Four respondents (8%) expressed mixed views, reflecting between duty and safety:

At some point, I did feel obligated…but if we didn't have materials…no.

Providing care without protective equipment? It's complicated.

Distributions were similar across age and sex, with no statistically significant differences (age χ2(4) = 2.01, p = 0.734; sex χ2(2) = 0.848, p = 0.654; Tables 8-9).

Discussion

Understanding how healthcare professionals view the ethical challenges related to the duty to care during a healthcare emergency, such as a pandemic, is crucial for effectively planning for and managing delivery of care. In recent years, this issue has sparked significant discussion in high-income countries [5, 6, 9, 20, 33, 36]. But despite recent efforts to bring their voices into the conversation [9, 20, 37] in resource-limited settings like Mozambique, much work remains to be done in fully addressing and integrating the perspectives of healthcare professionals into planning. In this study, we explored for the first time the conflicts that frontline healthcare professionals en-countered in providing care in Mozambique during the COVID-19 pandemic, integrating thematic analysis and the Ethical Duty to Care framework to interpret these contested ethical decisions.

As anticipated, our thematic analysis revealed diverse opinions among healthcare professionals in Mozambique regarding the duty to care during the COVID-19 pandemic. Nearly half of respondents affirmed an absolute obligation to provide care, often citing adherence to a professional code of ethics as justification. In contrast, another third of respondents expressed that they were not obligated to provide care, particularly in situations where PPE was inadequate, reflecting an outcome-oriented duty to care view that prioritized prevention of broader harm. Self-protection was a pre-requisite for continuing practice, rather than withdrawal from duty.

A significant minority (15%) adopted what our thematic analysis identified as a conditional duty, representing a character-based ethics approach within the duty to care framework that balances professional obligations with reasonable safety limits. A smaller subset (8%) held contingent positions, expressing uncertainty as they weighed obligations to patients against risk of exposure and institutional support. Taken together, these patterns depict a context-calibrated duty that shifted with the availability of PPE, perceived risk, and confidence in protection within the facility.

These findings align with other studies, which also report that healthcare professional´ views range from a belief in an unlimited duty to care to a preference for clearly defined boundaries [4, 5, 21, 36, 37] While one respondent expressed a strong commitment to saving lives, that individual also acknowledged the risks of working without proper protection. Another described adapting to resource constraints by seeking materials from other departments, demonstrating resilience but also exposing the pressure and unsustainability of such measures during a highly contagious pandemic. These healthcare professionals demonstrated the need for essential conditions, such as adequate PPE, to fulfill their professional obligations.

In general, this spectrum of perspectives reflects the complex ethical and practical challenges that healthcare professionals faced during the pandemic. As distribution intervals for PPE stretched from daily to weekly to monthly, the ethical positioning of many professionals shifted from unconditional to conditional duty frameworks. This temporal dimension demonstrates how duty to care is not static but dynamically negotiated based on changing resource availability and institutional support.

This juxtaposition of factors in perceived professional responsibility is a very difficult dilemma to address, and one that likely every healthcare professional has faced, at least theoretically, at some point in their careers. The AMA's first Code of Medical Ethics, published in 1847, addressed the issue of personal risk during epidemics, stating that “When pestilence prevails, it is [physicians'] duty to face the danger and continue their labors for the alleviation of suffering, even at the jeopardy of their own lives” [38]. The response of healthcare professionals to recent outbreaks of Influenza, Ebola, and COVID-19 have challenged this absolutist point of view with counter arguments for competing duties to oneself and to one's family members [20, 32, 39, 40]. Over time, the AMA softened its stance and now holds that “because of their commitment to care for the sick and injured, individual physicians have an obligation to provide urgent medical care during disasters. This ethical obligation holds even in the face of greater than usual risks to their own safety, health, or life. The physician workforce, however, is not an unlimited resource, therefore, when participating in disaster responses, physicians should balance immediate benefits to individual patients with ability to care for patients in the future” [20, 22].

Recent articles on the duty to care seldom emphasize the responsibility of institutions to ensuring the safety of their health workforce [33, 41, 42]. In contrast, one meta-analysis of healthcare professionals' willingness to work during pandemics identified perceived personal safety and an increased sense of preparedness as just some of the factors associated with an increased willingness to work [43].

Our findings on COVID-19 training and informational resources among Mozambican healthcare professionals highlight both strengths and gaps in pandemic preparedness. Only 58% of our respondents reported receiving formal training on COVID-19, revealing a gap in the reach and implementation of such programs that could potentially compromise effective response. The disparity in training frequency across professional roles also draws attention to inequalities in healthcare preparedness. Concerned about the distribution of responses across professional categories, our analysis revealed interesting patterns. Nurses, who received the highest rates of formal training (85%), were more likely to express positions of conditional duty, possibly reflecting their deeper understanding of infection control principles. In contrast, physicians and clinical officers, who reported lower rates of training (44%), showed more varied ethical positioning, suggesting that knowledge and preparedness significantly influence ethical reasoning about their professional obligations. This finding has important implications for the duty to care framework, suggesting that ethical obligations cannot be divorced from practical knowledge and institutional support. Healthcare professionals cannot be expected to fulfill duties for which they have not been adequately prepared or equipped [6].

With respect to respondents' sources of information about COVID-19, the thematic analysis revealed a heavy reliance on informal channels, 91% of respondents reported using television and social media as a primary source of information. This reliance on public rather than professional resources has important implications for ethical decision-making. The duty to care framework presumes that healthcare professionals have access to reliable, evidence-based information to guide their ethical choices. However, when formal training is limited (only 58% received formal COVID-19 training) and ethics content is minimal (13% received ethics training), healthcare professionals are left to navigate complex moral terrain with insufficient institutional support. These findings suggest that the duty to care framework should be expanded to encompass institutional responsibilities for ensuring not only material resources but also ethical guidance and decision-making support during public health crises [7, 8, 20, 26].

The limited availability of PPE and the limited ability of the healthcare facilities themselves to permit health workers to maintain recommended distances with patients with suspected/confirmed COVID-19 significantly impacted our study participants' sense of safety and impacted their provision of high-quality, compassionate care during the pandemic. As described by our respondents, fewer than 10% felt they had consistent and adequate quantities of PPE throughout the pandemic and at every shift. Many described this limitation as creating a sense of frustration and an increased sense of anxiety and fear for their own safety. While Mozambique frequently produces well-designed national plans and guidelines for its healthcare workforce, its extreme lack of resources means that, these plans' implementation and follow through can often be difficult to achieve.

Healthcare professionals in our study highlighted stressful and emotionally challenging experiences during the pandemic. For example, many (25%) described scenarios in which their colleagues provided questionable or unethical care, most likely out of fear and a lack of the PPE and cleaning supplies they needed to feel adequately protected. Frontline healthcare professionals working in both disasters and pandemics are generally most concerned about the welfare of their families first, and themselves second [28, 44, 45]. Our findings highlight how systematic shortfalls and failures can lead to individual compromises that the duty to care framework identifies as in-justices. When healthcare professionals lack adequate protection, their ability to provide equitable care becomes compromised, creating ethical tensions between self-preservation and professional obligations [46-49].

Our integration of thematic analysis with the ethical duty to care framework reveals that traditional duty to care frameworks developed in resource-rich settings may not adequately address the ethical complexities faced by healthcare professionals in LMICs. The findings suggest the value of ethics frameworks tailored to local resources and system realities, instead of relying on universal assumptions. In resource-limited context, this approach highlights that the duty to care framework built on reciprocal obligations between healthcare professionals and institutions becomes crucial, when institutions fail to meet basic duties, it is legitimate to question individual ethical responsibilities. Thematic findings indicate that ethical decisions are made within systems of constraint and support, highlighting the need to move beyond individual-level ethics to approaches that acknowledge collective and institutional responsibility.

Addressing practical applications of these findings, our research suggests that ensuring reliable, consistent availability of PPE represents not merely a logistical challenge but an ethical imperative that enables healthcare professionals to fulfill their professional duties safely. The identified gap in ethics training, with only 13% of respondents reporting that they received such preparation, indicates that expanding the content of professional ethics education could significantly help healthcare professionals navigate complex ethical decisions during future crises. This instruction should be coupled with institutional support systems that provide clear protocols for ethical decision-making during resource scarcity, including explicit guidelines for when and how professional duties may be legitimately constrained without compromising professional integrity.

Given the global nature of pandemic threats, our findings also underscore the importance of international cooperation, where high-income countries and international organizations should support systematic capacity building in LMICs. This support must encompass not only material resources but also the development and dissemination of ethical guidance specifically adapted to resource-constrained environments, ensuring that healthcare professionals worldwide have both the tools and moral support necessary to navigate future health emergencies effectively.

This study presents several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design captured perspectives at a single point in time, although we asked respondents to reflect on their experiences throughout the pandemic. Future longitudinal studies could better capture the temporal evolution of perceptions of duty. Second, the relatively small sample size, drawn from a limited number of healthcare facilities within Maputo Province, constrains the generalizability of findings to other regions of Mozambique or comparable low-resource settings, where resource constraints may be even more severe. Finally, while our sample included diverse professional roles, larger studies could enable more detailed analysis of role-specific ethical positioning. Logistical constraints also affected data collection, as adherence to strict COVID-19 safety protocols, including physical distancing and PPE use, staff shortages and high patient volumes, particularly in primary care centers, limited the availability of some healthcare professionals to participate. As a result, interviews had to be rescheduled, extending the data collection period and potentially influencing participant responses. To address the shortage of qualified physicians in certain hospitals, general clinical officers with comparable clinical responsibilities were included, which may have introduced additional variability in perspectives. These limitations underscore the need for future research with larger, more geographically diverse samples to deepen understanding of healthcare professionals' experiences in resource-constrained environments.

Conclusion

This study sheds light on the complex ethical and practical challenges faced by healthcare professionals in Mozambique during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly regarding their duty to care in a resource-constrained setting. The findings highlight diverse perspectives, ranging from an absolute obligation to care to a conditional sense of duty shaped by personal safety concerns and resource availability. These insights reveal a pragmatic yet committed approach among healthcare professionals, emphasizing the critical need for systemic improvements in resource allocation, training, and institutional support to better prepare for future health crises.

To strengthen preparedness, systems must ensure reliable PPE at the start of each shift, equitable access to PPE, staffing, and training (role-appropriate, low-bandwidth online and blended training), and clear, consistent guidance. Although limited in scope and generalizability, the findings emphasize the urgent need for investment in healthcare infrastructure and policies that prioritize the safety and preparedness of frontline workers. Doing so will not only bolster their ability to respond effectively to emergencies but also foster a more resilient and ethical healthcare system.
