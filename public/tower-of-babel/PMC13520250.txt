Multifactorial analysis of allergen-specific IgE sensitization in children with allergic diseases in Suzhou, China
Authors: Hongyan Zhang, Lijun Zhou, Boyi Chen, Wenna Qiu, Yuanyuan Zhang, Hong Zhu, Hongying Wang, Yuan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAllergic diseases represent a major public health challenge among children. Allergen sensitization patterns may be influenced by factors such as age, gender, season, and disease phenotype. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the associations of these factors with allergen sensitization patterns in children.MethodsWe studied 2,434 symptomatic children who underwent allergen testing at a district-level hospital in Suzhou, China, using the ImmunoCAP Phadia 250 system. Seventeen common airborne allergens (AAs) and food allergens (FAs) were assessed. The primary definition of sensitization was sIgE ≥ 0.35 IU/mL, whereas robustness analyses were additionally performed at thresholds of ≥ 0.70 and ≥ 3.50 IU/mL. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate associations with age, gender, and season, with false-discovery rate correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.ResultsPositivity rates varied systematically according to the sIgE threshold. As thresholds increased from grade ≥ 1 to ≥ 2 and ≥ 3, absolute positivity rates decreased as expected, whereas the direction of age-, gender-, and season-related patterns remained consistent. AA sensitization was more common than FA sensitization, with Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus and Dermatophagoides farinae representing the predominant sensitizers, whereas milk and egg were the leading food allergens. With increasing age, AA sensitization increased whereas FA sensitization declined; these patterns persisted after multivariable adjustment and at higher positivity thresholds. Males showed higher odds of sensitization, particularly for mite allergens. Seasonal variation was most evident for mite allergens, with higher positivity during summer–autumn than winter, broadly paralleling local humidity patterns. Across disease phenotypes, AA sensitization was more common in asthma and rhinitis, whereas FA sensitization was relatively more common in atopic dermatitis and the food-allergy group; mixed diseases showed a higher overall sensitization burden.ConclusionsIn this single-center referred cohort, the findings describe allergen test positivity among clinically tested children rather than population-level prevalence. Age, gender, season, and disease phenotype were associated with distinct sensitization patterns, supporting greater attention to mite-focused assessment and environmental control from school age onward, particularly during humid seasons, as well as milk and egg evaluation during early childhood. The overall findings remained consistent at stricter sIgE positivity thresholds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-07166-x.

Introduction

Allergic diseases have emerged as a major public health challenge worldwide, with prevalence rates exhibiting a significant upward trajectory over the past few decades [1]. Current estimates suggest that food allergies affect 1% to 10% of the global population. The prevalence of atopic dermatitis is approximately 8%, with a lifetime prevalence reaching up to 20% [2]. In a study investigating allergies among adolescents in southern Sweden, 32% of all children reported having at least one allergic disease [3]. Among them, 67% reported a single allergic condition and 33% reported multiple allergic diseases [3]. Moreover, marked geographical variation exists in the prevalence of food allergy among school-age children across Europe [4]. In China, several studies have analyzed the prevalence and distribution of both airborne allergens (AAs) and food allergens (FAs), revealing considerable regional diversity in sensitization patterns [5–7]. Global reports consistently indicate increasing incidence rates of allergic rhinitis, asthma, eczema, and food allergies among children and adolescents, significantly affecting quality of life and overall health [8–10]. This increasing burden is particularly evident in southeastern China, a region characterized by a warm, humid climate and high population density. Rapid industrialization and urbanization in this area have been accompanied by a marked increase in allergic disease prevalence. Despite the growing public health burden posed by allergic conditions, the epidemiological characteristics of allergens in this region remain insufficiently characterized [10].

The pathogenesis of allergic diseases is driven by complex interactions between genetic predisposition and environmental exposures, with environmental allergens, including AAs and FAs, serving as major triggers [11–13]. In southeastern China, the combination of high humidity and elevated temperatures provides favorable conditions for the proliferation of allergens, particularly dust mites and molds. Concurrently, shifting dietary patterns have progressively increased children’s and adolescents’ exposure to FAs, including milk, eggs, shrimp, and crab [14]. Furthermore, improved economic conditions and lifestyle changes have led to increasingly diverse clinical presentations of allergic diseases. For example, food allergies are notably prevalent among preschool children, while school-age children and adolescents are more frequently affected by AAs. Despite the growing burden of allergic diseases, most previous studies have focused on individual disease types or specific allergens, with relatively few comprehensive multifactorial analyses incorporating age, gender, and seasonal variation [15].

Southeastern China is characterized by unique geographical and environmental conditions that may increase allergen exposure. The region’s warm and humid climate, rapid urbanization, and dense population collectively contribute to increased allergen exposure to both AAs and FAs. Additionally, as one of China’s economically developed regions, changes in dietary patterns may have increased children’s exposure to a broader range of potential FAs. Nevertheless, epidemiological data on allergen sensitization across different age and gender groups in this region remain limited. Furthermore, relatively few studies have comprehensively evaluated multiple contributing factors simultaneously, making it difficult to establish evidence-based approaches for clinical management and preventive strategies.

To address these gaps, we conducted a single-center cross-sectional study of children with allergic diseases who underwent allergen testing at a district-level hospital in Suzhou, China. The aims of this study were to: (i) characterize sensitization to common AAs and FAs; (ii) evaluate the associations of age, gender, and season with allergen sensitization; and (iii) describe sensitization patterns across different clinical diagnoses, including rhinitis, asthma, atopic dermatitis, urticaria, conjunctivitis, food allergy, and mixed diseases. Because this cohort consisted of referred symptomatic children, our findings reflect allergen test positivity among clinically tested children rather than population-level prevalence. By integrating multiple determinants within a single analytical framework, we aimed to provide clinically relevant and context-specific evidence to support early diagnosis, individualized management, and season-specific prevention strategies in Suzhou and, cautiously, in climatically similar settings.

Materials and methods

Inclusion of population

We identified 2,434 children who underwent allergen testing at the Children’s Hospital of Wujiang District, Suzhou between September 2022 and September 2024. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Wujiang District, Suzhou (approval No. 2025006). Physician-confirmed diagnoses extracted from electronic medical records were categorized as asthma, allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis (dermatology-confirmed), urticaria, allergic conjunctivitis, and food allergy. “Mixed diseases” denoted ≥ 2 physician diagnoses among these categories and was treated as a separate category in analyses. Diagnostic criteria followed the Chinese Expert Consensus on Diagnosis and Management of Allergic Diseases in Children, which incorporates relevant international and national guidelines for asthma, allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis, urticaria, allergic conjunctivitis, and food allergy, including GINA (Global Initiative for Asthma) and ARIA (Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma) where applicable [16–18].

Determination method

The ImmunoCAP Phadia 250 allergen detection system, together with the corresponding specific IgE (sIgE) assay kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), was used for allergen testing. A total of 9 common AAs and 8 FAs were assessed in each patient. The AAs included Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus (Der p), Dermatophagoides farinae (Der f), cockroach, cat dander, dog dander, mold mixture, tree pollen mixture, grass pollen mixture, and Aspergillus fumigatus. The FAs included milk, peanut, egg white, wheat, soybean, shrimp, crab, and a nut mixture. In addition to single allergens, composite outcomes included FAs, AAs, and overall allergen sensitization (positivity to at least one food and/or airborne allergen). Venous blood samples (3 mL) were collected during outpatient visits. Samples were centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min to separate serum, and serum sIgE levels were measured using fluorescence immunoassay. Quality control procedures and testing protocols were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The interpretation and classification of sIgE levels followed the World Allergy Organization (WAO) criteria [6] as follows: Grade 0 < 0.35 IU/mL (1 kUA/L ≈ 1 IU/mL); Grade 1 ≥ 0.35 and < 0.7 IU/mL; Grade 2 ≥ 0.7 and < 3.5 IU/mL; Grade 3 ≥ 3.5 and < 17.5 IU/mL; Grade 4 ≥ 17.5 and < 50 IU/mL; Grade 5 ≥ 50 and < 100 IU/mL; Grade 6 ≥ 100 IU/mL. The primary outcome was sensitization, defined as sIgE ≥ 0.35 IU/mL, consistent with current diagnostic practice and the established clinical use of sIgE cut-offs [19]. To assess robustness, all key analyses were repeated at higher positivity thresholds (≥ 0.70 and ≥ 3.50 IU/mL) [20].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, whereas continuous sIgE concentrations were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR). Differences in proportions between groups (e.g., age-, gender-, and season-specific positivity) were evaluated with χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests when expected cell counts were inadequate, and exact two-sided P values were reported. Associations between covariates and allergen sensitization (including single allergens and the composite outcomes of AAs, FAs, and overall sensitization) were assessed using logistic regression models, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. Univariable models included age (modeled as a continuous variable per 1-year increase), gender, or season separately. Multivariable models jointly adjusted for age (continuous), gender, season with winter as the reference category, and disease classification. For descriptive purposes, age was additionally categorized into five groups (< 1, 1–3, 3–6, 6–12, and 12–18 years), using infants as the reference group; these analyses are presented in the Supplementary Materials. Additional multivariable logistic models included pre-specified interaction terms for age × disease and gender × age. Given multiple comparisons across allergens and covariates, multiplicity was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery rate (FDR), with BH-adjusted q values reported in the main tables (q(BH)). Unless otherwise specified, statistical significance was defined as q < 0.05, whereas raw P values were also presented for transparency. To aid interpretation of seasonal patterns, monthly meteorological indicators for Suzhou during the study period (mean relative humidity and temperature) were summarized and considered descriptively when discussing season-related findings. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version: 4.0.6; packages including stats, broom, and ggplot2), with SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0 used for result verification and figure cross-checking.

Results

General characteristics

From 2022 to 2024, a total of 2,434 children were enrolled, including 1,415 males (58.1%) and 1,019 females (41.9%). The mean age was 6.33 ± 3.13 years (range: 1 month to 16 years), and the median age (IQR) was 6.0 (4.0–8.0) years (Table 1). By age group, the cohort included < 1 year (n = 51; 2.1%), 1–3 years (n = 370; 15.2%), 3–6 years (n = 977; 40.1%), 6–12 years (n = 936; 38.5%), and 12–18 years (n = 100; 4.1%). By season of testing, spring (March–May) accounted for 825 children (33.9%), summer (June–August) for 751 (30.9%), autumn (September–November) for 477 (19.6%), and winter (December–February) for 381 (15.7%). Among clinical diagnoses, mixed diseases (defined as ≥ 2 physician-confirmed diagnoses) represented the largest subgroup (n = 869; 35.7%), followed by rhinitis (n = 659; 27.1%), atopic dermatitis (n = 427; 17.5%), asthma (n = 291; 12.0%), urticaria (n = 109; 4.5%), food allergy (n = 53; 2.2%), and conjunctivitis (n = 26; 1.1%).

Allergen sensitization profile

Median sIgE levels and positivity rates according to age group, gender, and season are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Across the cohort, AAs predominated over FAs. Der p and Der f were consistently the most common sensitizers, followed by molds. Pet dander allergens (cat and dog), pollens (grass and tree mixtures), cockroach, and most FAs (milk, egg, peanut/nuts, wheat, soybean, shrimp, and crab) showed comparatively lower positivity rates (Fig. 1A). Analysis of sIgE grade distributions showed that mite allergens accounted for a larger proportion of higher-grade sensitization than other AAs or FAs, whereas molds, pet dander allergens, pollens, and most FAs were predominantly associated with lower-grade sensitization (Fig. 1B). Across age groups, AA positivity peaked between 3 and 12 years of age, whereas FA positivity was more common in younger children and gradually declined with age; overall sensitization generally followed the pattern observed for AAs (Fig. 1C). By gender, males showed higher positivity than females for AAs, FAs, and overall sensitization, with the largest absolute difference observed for AAs. Nevertheless, the rank order of individual allergens was broadly similar between genders (Fig. 1D). The primary analysis and all figures used grade 1 as the positivity threshold. Sensitivity analyses using higher thresholds (grade 2 and grade 3) demonstrated similar qualitative trends with the expected attenuation in positivity rates and are presented in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Table S2 and Figure S1).

Age distribution of allergen sensitization spectrum

Across the five age groups, AA sensitization became progressively more common with increasing age, whereas FA sensitization was more common in early childhood and subsequently declined (Table 2). For composite outcomes, AA sensitization increased from 33.5% in children aged 1–3 years to 59.5% in those aged 3–6 years and 69.2% in those aged 6–12 years (q = 8.19 × 10⁻⁴¹). In contrast, FA sensitization decreased from 67.8% at 1–3 years to 49.1% at 3–6 years and 31.0% at 6–12 years (q = 1.32 × 10⁻³⁵). Overall sensitization increased through early school age and then plateaued (74.1% at 3–6 years and 73.7% at 6–12 years) but did not remain significant after FDR correction (q = 0.064).

Allergen sensitization by age group

At the single-allergen level, Der p and Der f showed strong age-related trends and showed the highest sensitization rates in older children (q < 0.05). Mold sensitization also increased with age, rising from 12.7% at 1–3 years to approximately 31–32% at ≥ 6 years (q = 2.47 × 10⁻¹⁴). More modest but statistically significant increases were observed for cat dander (q = 3.93 × 10⁻⁶), dog dander (q = 1.09 × 10⁻⁵), grass pollen mixture (q = 0.003), tree pollen mixture (q = 0.003), cockroach (q = 1.09 × 10⁻⁵), and crab (q = 1.09 × 10⁻⁵). In contrast, several FAs showed the highest positivity rates in younger children. Milk sensitization declined from 61.1% at 1–3 years to 35.9% at 3–6 years and 15.2% at 6–12 years (q = 6.74 × 10⁻⁶⁶), and egg sensitization showed a similar pattern (q = 4.92 × 10⁻⁴³). Nut sensitization showed the highest positivity at 1–3 years and decreased thereafter (q = 0.001), whereas peanut (q = 0.298), wheat (q = 0.072), and soybean (q = 0.193) did not remain significant after FDR correction. Similar directional trends were observed at higher positivity thresholds, with most age-related associations persisting at grade ≥ 2 and a subset remaining significant at grade ≥ 3 (Supplementary Table S3).

Gender distribution of allergen sensitization spectrum

Stratified by gender, males showed higher sensitization rates than females across all composite outcomes (Table 3), including AAs (61.7% vs. 54.2%, q = 0.002), FAs (46.4% vs. 41.1%, q = 0.026), and overall sensitization (75.9% vs. 70.1%, q = 0.008). At the single-allergen level, male predominance was most pronounced for mite allergens, including Der p (49.6% vs. 39.6%, q = 2.74 × 10⁻⁵) and Der f (50.0% vs. 41.9%, q = 8.63 × 10⁻⁴). More modest but statistically significant differences after FDR correction were also observed for cockroach (5.7% vs. 3.0%, q = 0.011), shrimp (7.6% vs. 4.7%, q = 0.017), soybean (6.2% vs. 3.4%, q = 0.011), and grass pollen mixture (8.1% vs. 5.5%, q = 0.040). For other allergens, including milk, egg, cat/dog dander, tree pollen mixture, nuts, wheat, peanut, mold, and crab, gender differences were modest and did not remain significant after FDR correction. Sensitivity analyses using higher positivity thresholds (grade 2 and grade 3) showed similar directional trends, although fewer allergens remained significant. Male–female differences persisted for AAs and overall sensitization, and the associations were primarily driven by mite sensitization, with threshold-dependent associations observed for a limited subset of additional allergens (Supplementary Table S4).

Seasonal distribution of allergen sensitization spectrum

Seasonal variation was most evident for mite sensitization (Table 4). Der p positivity was 43.0% in spring, 47.4% in summer, and 50.9% in autumn, compared with 39.9% in winter (q = 0.034). Der f showed a similar pattern, with positivity rates of 44.5%, 47.7%, and 53.0% during spring–autumn, respectively, compared with 41.2% in winter (q = 0.034). Similarly, the composite AA sensitization rate was higher in summer and autumn (61.8% and 61.6%, respectively) than in winter (52.2%; q = 0.039). Other allergens and the FA composite did not remain significant after FDR correction for seasonal differences (all q > 0.05), nor did overall sensitization (q = 0.181).

Allergen sensitization by season

Sensitivity analyses across different positivity thresholds showed a graded pattern (Supplementary Table S5). At grade 1, seasonal associations were limited to mite allergens and the AA composite (q = 0.034 and q = 0.039, respectively). At grade 2, significance narrowed to Der p and Der f (both q = 0.030), whereas AAs, FAs, and overall sensitization remained non-significant. At grade 3, seasonal associations became more pronounced: AAs and overall sensitization were both higher outside winter (both q = 0.005), with mite allergens remaining the principal contributors (Der p: q = 0.020; Der f: q = 0.040).

Across the study period, monthly relative humidity increased from late spring, peaked during summer and early autumn, and declined in winter, whereas temperature followed the expected seasonal rise-and-fall pattern (Supplementary Figure S2A–B). This meteorological pattern paralleled the observed allergen sensitization patterns: Der p and Der f positivity peaked during summer–autumn and was lowest in winter, while the AA composite showed a similar seasonal pattern; in contrast, FAs varied only modestly across seasons. Exploratory analyses suggested that monthly mite sensitization tracked humidity more closely than temperature (Supplementary Figure S2C–D), consistent with the proliferation of mites under warm and humid conditions.

Associations of age, gender, and season with allergen sensitization

In univariable models (Supplementary Table S6; Figures S3–S5), the odds of AA sensitization increased with age, particularly for mite allergens. Der p showed an OR of 1.178 (95% CI: 1.146–1.211; q = 7.83 × 10⁻³¹), whereas Der f showed an OR of 1.174 (95% CI: 1.143–1.207; q = 4.72 × 10⁻³⁰). In contrast, FA sensitization declined with age (FAs: OR = 0.840; 95% CI: 0.816–0.864; q = 1.66 × 10⁻³²), particularly for milk (OR = 0.769; 95% CI: 0.742–0.796; q = 1.13 × 10⁻⁴⁷) and egg (OR = 0.781; 95% CI: 0.753–0.809; q = 1.55 × 10⁻⁴¹). Males showed higher odds than females for several AAs, particularly mite allergens (Der p: OR = 1.388; 95% CI: 1.181–1.633; q = 7.42 × 10⁻⁴; Der f: OR = 1.499; 95% CI: 1.274–1.765; q = 2.33 × 10⁻⁵) and cockroach (OR = 1.910; 95% CI: 1.265–2.955; q = 0.009). Relative to winter, autumn was associated with higher mite sensitization (Der p: OR = 1.611; 95% CI: 1.229–2.117; q = 0.035; Der f: OR = 1.565; 95% CI: 1.192–2.057; q = 0.039), whereas associations for spring versus winter were not significant after FDR correction. Age was modeled as a continuous covariate to preserve statistical information and minimize small-cell bias. When age was categorized into bands (< 1, 1–6, 6–12, and 12–18 years), sparse cells—especially among infants/adolescents and at higher sIgE thresholds—occasionally resulted in quasi-complete or complete separation and unstable estimates (e.g., inflated ORs and non-finite CIs). Therefore, age-band analyses are presented descriptively only (Supplementary Table S7).

After multivariable adjustment for age, gender, and season, these associations persisted and remained strongest for mite allergens (Supplementary Table S8; Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Per-year increases remained significant for AAs and key mite species (AAs: OR = 1.204; 95% CI: 1.169–1.240; q = 7.87 × 10⁻³⁴; Der p: OR = 1.198; 95% CI: 1.164–1.233; q = 2.05 × 10⁻³⁴; Der f: OR = 1.194; 95% CI: 1.161–1.229; q = 1.28 × 10⁻³³), whereas FAs continued to decline with age (FAs: OR = 0.842; 95% CI: 0.818–0.866; q = 1.65 × 10⁻³¹; milk: OR = 0.770; 95% CI: 0.743–0.797; q = 1.91 × 10⁻⁴⁶; egg: OR = 0.782; 95% CI: 0.754–0.810; q = 1.42 × 10⁻⁴⁰). Male gender was independently associated with higher AA sensitization (OR = 1.432; 95% CI: 1.205–1.703; q = 3.21 × 10⁻⁴), primarily driven by mite allergens (Der f: OR = 1.585; 95% CI: 1.335–1.884; q = 3.29 × 10⁻⁶; Der p: OR = 1.457; 95% CI: 1.227–1.730; q = 1.74 × 10⁻⁴) and cockroach (OR = 1.993; 95% CI: 1.313–3.099; q = 0.008). Seasonally, AA sensitization was higher in summer (OR = 1.733; 95% CI: 1.278–2.354; q = 0.025), whereas mite sensitization remained elevated in autumn compared with winter (Der p: OR = 1.656; 95% CI: 1.233–2.229; q = 0.025; Der f: OR = 1.617; 95% CI: 1.203–2.177; q = 0.030); associations for spring did not remain significant after FDR correction.

Robustness analyses using stricter positivity thresholds (grade 2 and grade 3) yielded similar qualitative findings. Age-related increases in AA sensitization—particularly for Der p and Der f—persisted or became more pronounced, whereas male–female differences in AA sensitization remained comparable to those observed at grade 1. Seasonal associations remained most evident for mite allergens (summer/autumn > winter). In contrast, associations for several FAs with low positivity rates became attenuated at higher thresholds, with only a subset (e.g., milk and egg at grade 2) retaining significant age-related declines. Overall, higher sIgE thresholds strengthened associations for core AAs while reducing weaker associations involving FAs or pollens (Supplementary Table S8).

Distribution of allergens in different diseases

Across clinical diagnoses, overall sensitization rates were high, ranging from 55.0% in urticaria to approximately 80% in asthma (80.1%) and rhinitis (80.0%), and remained elevated in atopic dermatitis (69.1%), mixed diseases (71.2%), and conjunctivitis (76.9%) (Table 5). AA sensitization was more common than FA sensitization in most disease categories: asthma (68.0% vs. 48.8%), rhinitis (67.5% vs. 48.7%), atopic dermatitis (51.5% vs. 41.7%), conjunctivitis (61.5% vs. 38.5%), and mixed diseases (55.9% vs. 41.4%). Exceptions were observed in urticaria (FAs 37.6% vs. AAs 34.9%) and the food-allergy group (FAs 45.3% vs. AAs 41.5%). Within AAs, Der p and Der f were the leading sensitizers across all diagnostic groups, including asthma (57.7% and 56.7%, respectively), rhinitis (55.1% and 53.3%), atopic dermatitis (40.7% and 39.3%), conjunctivitis (46.2% and 46.2%), the food-allergy group (32.1% and 32.1%), and mixed diseases (42.9% and 42.2%). Mold sensitization was also relatively common, particularly in asthma (33.3%), rhinitis (33.8%), and mixed diseases (25.0%). Pet dander allergens (cat/dog) and pollens (grass/tree mixtures) were less frequent and showed broadly similar distributions across disease phenotypes (typically ~ 4%–12%). Among FAs, milk and egg were the most common FAs in all diagnostic groups (milk: 27%–34%; egg: 21%–31%), whereas peanut, shellfish (shrimp/crab), soybean, and wheat sensitization were less frequent (generally ≤ 12%). Overall, mite allergens accounted for a large proportion of sensitization in respiratory and mixed disease phenotypes, whereas milk and egg dominated the food allergen sensitization pattern across diagnostic categories.

Associations of age and gender with sensitization within diagnostic categories

At the single-allergen level, Der f sensitization increased with age across respiratory and mixed disease phenotypes (all q ≤ 10⁻⁵), with additional positive associations observed for cockroach, cat/dog dander, and tree pollen mixture in selected diagnoses. In contrast, age was inversely associated with egg and milk sensitization across several disease categories, as well as with wheat and nut sensitization in atopic dermatitis and mixed diseases. Estimates for conjunctivitis were imprecise because of the small sample size.

Gender-related associations were generally modest. Most diagnoses showed no significant gender association after FDR correction. The clearest association was observed in rhinitis, in which males had higher odds of shrimp sensitization (OR = 3.345; 95% CI: 1.690–7.267; q = 0.015). More modest male-predominant associations limited to specific diagnoses were observed for selected AAs (e.g., cat/dog dander and cockroach in rhinitis), although these associations did not persist consistently across disease categories.

To evaluate effect modification, interaction terms for age × disease and gender × age were included in the main models. After Benjamini–Hochberg correction, the gender × disease interaction remained significant for wheat, soybean, crab, AAs, and overall sensitization (q ≤ 0.05), whereas the gender × age interaction was not significant (all q ≥ 0.05). These interaction terms did not materially alter the primary age- and gender-related associations; therefore, the main results are presented without interaction terms (Supplementary Table S10).

Discussion

In this single-center cohort of children referred for allergy testing, AA sensitization was more common than FA sensitization across most disease categories, with Der p and Der f consistently representing the most common sensitizers. These mite allergens accounted for a large proportion of sensitization in respiratory phenotypes (asthma and rhinitis) and remained common in atopic dermatitis and mixed diseases, whereas milk and egg were the predominant FAs across diagnostic groups. Age, gender, and season all influenced sensitization patterns: AA sensitization increased with age, whereas FA sensitization declined; males showed higher odds of sensitization, particularly for mite allergens; and mite sensitization was more common in summer–autumn than winter, broadly paralleling local humidity patterns. These findings extend prior reports from other regions that identified mite allergens as dominant airborne sensitizers and milk/egg as the most common FAs in children, while additionally providing multivariable, FDR-controlled estimates and robustness analyses using stricter sIgE positivity thresholds (grade 2/3) [6]. Overall, these findings suggest a region-specific sensitization profile with potential implications for diagnostic interpretation and environmental management strategies in clinical practice.

Age-related patterns of airborne and food allergen sensitization

Age emerged as the dominant determinant of allergen sensitization [20]. Across the cohort, AA sensitization increased steadily with age—most prominently for Der p and Der f—whereas FA sensitization declined from early childhood onward, particularly for milk and egg. These associations were consistent in both univariable and multivariable analyses and remained evident at stricter sIgE positivity thresholds, supporting the robustness of the findings. Notably, crustacean sensitization (shrimp and crab) demonstrated an age-related increase in our cohort, with lower positivity rates in preschool children and higher rates during school age. These patterns persisted after adjustment for gender and season and across major disease categories, particularly asthma and rhinitis. Consistent with Liu et al. [21], our data suggest a peak in crustacean-specific IgE sensitization during later childhood and adolescence. However, this finding differed from that reported by Zeng et al. [22], indicating potential heterogeneity related to geographic setting, sampling framework, or analytical thresholds. Similar regional differences in seafood sensitization have also been reported previously. One possible explanation may involve cross-reactive tropomyosin shared among house dust mite, cockroach, shrimp, and crab allergens, whereby increasing AA sensitization with age could contribute to crustacean-specific IgE positivity in the absence of primary food allergy [23, 24]. However, because component-resolved diagnostics and oral food challenge testing were not performed in this study, true food allergy could not be reliably distinguished from cross-reactivity. In contrast, wheat sensitization showed heterogeneous patterns across disease categories and did not remain significant after FDR correction in the primary age-band analyses; therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

These trends are biologically plausible. Cumulative exposure to airborne allergens and airway mucosal maturation may favor mite sensitization with increasing age, whereas maturation of epithelial barriers and the development of oral tolerance may contribute to the decline in milk and egg sensitization later in childhood [13, 25]. From a clinical perspective, our findings may help guide age-specific allergen assessment strategies. In early childhood, evaluation of milk and egg sensitization may be particularly relevant, whereas in school-age children, greater attention may be warranted for AAs, especially mite allergens, together with environmental mitigation measures (e.g., bedding encasements and humidity control). In mite-sensitized children with crustacean-specific IgE positivity, differentiation between cross-reactivity and true food allergy should rely on comprehensive clinical evaluation, including clinical history, in vivo testing (e.g., skin prick or prick-by-prick testing), oral food challenge when appropriate, and component-resolved diagnostics [26–28].

Gender differences in airborne and food allergen sensitization

In our cohort, males showed higher sensitization rates for overall sensitization as well as for both AAs and FAs. Sensitization to mite allergens, particularly Der p and Der f, was significantly more common in males than females, and several FAs (e.g., shrimp and soybean) also demonstrated male predominance. These findings are consistent with previous reports describing male predominance in childhood allergen sensitization despite differences in allergen panels and study populations [29, 30]. Prior studies on sex-related differences in allergic diseases have shown that prepubertal boys tend to exhibit higher IgE sensitization rates and allergic manifestations, whereas postpubertal females more commonly develop allergic symptoms, suggesting an age-related shift potentially influenced by sex hormone-related mechanisms and environmental exposures [31]. Nevertheless, the underlying biological mechanisms remain incompletely understood, and the cross-sectional design of our study does not permit causal inference.

From a clinical perspective, our findings may help inform individualized assessment strategies for allergen sensitization. In particular, boys presenting with asthma or rhinitis may warrant closer evaluation for mite sensitization and consideration of environmental mitigation measures. However, FA testing should remain primarily guided by clinical history rather than gender alone in order to avoid unnecessary testing [32].

Seasonal patterns of airborne and food allergen sensitization

Seasonal variation in sensitization was most evident for AAs, particularly mite allergens. Sensitization to Der p and Der f was more common during summer–autumn and lowest in winter, whereas FA sensitization showed relatively limited seasonal variation. Monthly humidity and temperature increased from late spring through early autumn and declined during winter, paralleling the seasonal peak in mite sensitization and suggesting a humidity-associated pattern of airborne sensitization [33, 34]. These findings are consistent with recent multicenter studies in Chinese children showing that Der p and Der f sensitization begins in late spring and remains elevated through autumn, whereas multiple pollen allergens show summer–autumn peaks with marked regional variation (e.g., mite sensitization predominating in eastern monsoon regions, while several tree and weed pollens are more common in arid northwestern regions). In addition, multi-pollen sensitization has been associated with greater symptom burden. Together, these observations suggest that local climate conditions and local allergen ecology may influence the timing and magnitude of seasonal sensitization patterns, with mite sensitization appearing to track humidity more closely than temperature [11, 35, 36].

From a clinical perspective, our findings may help inform season-specific environmental management strategies, particularly for children with asthma or rhinitis. Increased attention to mite-control measures before and during summer–autumn (e.g., humidity control, mattress and pillow encasements, and bedroom cleaning) may be beneficial in regions with humid climates. In areas with pronounced seasonal pollen peaks, incorporating pollen forecasting and broader pollen panels into allergen assessment may further support counseling and the timing of preventive interventions. Nevertheless, the emphasis on mite-control strategies should be interpreted in the context of local climatic and environmental conditions.

Allergen sensitization patterns across disease phenotypes

Across diagnostic categories, sensitization patterns showed distinct phenotype-specific distributions. In respiratory phenotypes (rhinitis and asthma), AAs—particularly mite allergens such as Der p and Der f—were more common and demonstrated clear age-related increases, consistent with cumulative exposure to AAs and airway maturation [37, 38]. In atopic dermatitis and the food-allergy group, FAs, particularly milk and egg, represented the predominant sensitization pattern during early childhood, consistent with epithelial barrier dysfunction and the subsequent development of oral tolerance. Mixed diseases were associated with a higher overall sensitization burden, suggesting a tendency toward multisensitization rather than a single-trigger phenotype [39].

These findings may help inform phenotype-oriented allergen assessment strategies. In respiratory phenotypes, greater emphasis may be warranted for evaluation of mite sensitization and environmental control measures, whereas milk and egg assessment may be particularly relevant in early-childhood atopic dermatitis or food-allergy presentations. Broader allergen panels may also be useful in children with mixed diseases because of the higher frequency of multisensitization. When crustacean-specific IgE is detected in mite-sensitized children, differentiation between cross-reactivity and true food allergy should rely on comprehensive clinical evaluation, including clinical history, in vivo testing, oral food challenge when appropriate, and component-resolved diagnostics [23]. Finally, findings from small subgroups (e.g., conjunctivitis or FAs with low positivity rates within individual diagnoses) should be interpreted cautiously, as sparse strata may yield unstable estimates; therefore, overall phenotype-consistent patterns are likely to be more informative for clinical interpretation.

Potential clinical implications

The multifactorial analyses in this study may provide clinically relevant insights for individualized allergen assessment and management according to age, gender, season, and disease phenotype. In infants and young children, evaluation of FAs, particularly milk and egg, may be especially relevant, whereas school-age children and adolescents may benefit from greater attention to AAs, particularly mite allergens such as Der p and Der f [40]. Season-specific allergen exposure control strategies may also be beneficial, particularly during periods associated with increased mite sensitization. Because males in our cohort showed higher sensitization rates—especially among children with asthma or rhinitis—closer attention to AA sensitization and environmental mitigation strategies may be warranted in selected clinical settings [41]. In children with mixed allergic diseases, broader allergen assessment may help identify multisensitization patterns. Furthermore, given the potential influence of climate change on allergen exposure and sensitization patterns, continued monitoring and region-specific studies may help improve understanding of evolving allergic disease trends and support future preventive strategies.

Limitations of the study

This study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single district-level hospital in Suzhou, southeastern China, and included symptomatic children referred for clinical allergen testing. Therefore, the reported findings reflect allergen test positivity among clinically tested children and may not be generalizable to other regions or broader pediatric populations. Second, the hospital-based referred cohort may introduce selection bias and limit representativeness relative to the general pediatric population. Third, although multivariable models adjusted for age, gender, season, and disease classification, several potentially important confounding factors—including parental atopy, environmental tobacco smoke exposure, household humidity or mold exposure, socioeconomic status, and dietary patterns—were not systematically recorded and therefore could not be controlled. Fourth, sensitization was primarily defined using sIgE ≥ 0.35 IU/mL (grade 1). Although this threshold facilitates comparison with previous studies, grade-1 sensitization may have limited clinical relevance, and sIgE positivity does not necessarily indicate clinically confirmed allergy. Although sensitivity analyses were additionally performed using stricter thresholds (grade 2–3), oral food challenges, skin prick testing, and component-resolved diagnostics were not available to confirm clinical allergy or distinguish co-sensitization from cross-reactivity (e.g., shrimp–mite tropomyosin cross-reactivity). Fifth, all assays were performed using a single testing platform, and inter-assay variability, pre-analytical factors, and batch effects may have influenced quantitative sIgE measurements. Sixth, because of the cross-sectional study design, causal relationships between allergen exposure, sensitization, and disease outcomes cannot be inferred; longitudinal multicenter studies are needed to better characterize sensitization trajectories and clinical outcomes over time. In addition, sparse cells in certain subgroup analyses, particularly among infants/adolescents and at higher sIgE thresholds, may have resulted in unstable odds-ratio estimates with wide confidence intervals. Seventh, the study focused on 17 common airborne and food allergens; therefore, less common or emerging allergens were not evaluated, limiting coverage of the local allergen exposure spectrum. Eighth, “mixed diseases” were defined as ≥ 2 physician-confirmed diagnoses among the prespecified categories and were analyzed as a single composite group. Detailed combination-specific sensitization analyses (e.g., asthma + rhinitis) were not performed and may warrant further investigation in larger multicenter cohorts.

Future studies incorporating multicenter cohorts, longitudinal designs, broader allergen panels, and additional sociodemographic and environmental variables may help improve the robustness and generalizability of these findings.
