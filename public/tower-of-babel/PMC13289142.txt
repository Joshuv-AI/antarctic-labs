Trends in the use of adult-specific preference-weighted health-related quality of life instruments in clinical trials over the past 50 years: a protocol for a meta-research study using deep learning-based natural language processing and large language models
Authors: Sarun Srikhom, Nancy Devlin, Nhung Nghiem, Sandra Nolte, Vu Vo, An Tran-Duy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundHealth technology assessment bodies increasingly emphasise the importance of preference-weighted health-related quality of life (HRQoL) evidence. However, such measures are often absent in clinical trial publications. It is not yet clear how frequently clinical trials have incorporated these measures over the past five decades, how the use of preference-weighted HRQoL instruments has evolved over time, and how trends differ across disease areas, countries and global regions. This study aims to (1) assess changes over time in the proportions of clinical trials using each preference-weighted HRQoL instrument in adults, and (2) model secular trends in the adoption of these instruments across disease areas, countries and regions. The study will provide a comprehensive, systematic assessment of the use of preference-weighted HRQoL instruments in clinical trials since 1976 and develop a scalable approach for large-scale evidence synthesis.MethodsWe will identify clinical trials involving humans published in English since 1976 through systematic searches of MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library and Web of Science. We will focus on generic preference-weighted HRQoL instruments for adults, including EQ-5D-3L, EQ-5D-5L, Short Form 6 Dimensions, 12-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-12), Health Utility Index 2, Health Utility Index 3, Assessment of Quality of Life (AQoL) series (AQoL-4D, AQoL-6D, AQoL-7D, AQoL-8D), Quality of Well-Being Scale (QWB), QWB Self-Administered (QWB-SA), 15D and Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) with the Preference Scoring System (PROPr). Screening and data extraction will be automated using natural language processing (NLP) pipeline or large language models (LLMs). To determine the most accurate approach, we will benchmark NLP and LLM performance against a manually curated reference dataset of 5000 randomly sampled articles reviewed independently by three reviewers. Model performance will be evaluated using classification metrics including accuracy, recall and F1-score. Annual counts and proportions of trials using each instrument will be calculated, stratified by disease area, country and region. Trends will be modelled using basis-splines (B-splines) with 2 or 3 degrees of freedom and Bayesian spline regression to estimate secular changes in both absolute numbers and proportions of instrument use over time.Ethics and disseminationThis study uses only published literature and does not involve human participants or individual-level data. All results will be reported in aggregate form, with no identifiable information. Formal ethics approval is therefore not required. Findings will be disseminated via peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations, and aggregated data and analysis code will be made publicly available to support transparency and reproducibility.Keywords: Quality of Life, Clinical Trial, Natural Language Processing, Machine Learning, Artificial Intelligence, Systematic Review

Introduction

Following the early model of health technology assessment (HTA) in the USA in the 1970s, several countries in Europe such as Sweden, the Netherlands and the UK quickly established independent, public bodies to provide scientific assessment of health interventions to healthcare decision makers.1 Major aspects to be assessed in HTA include safety, efficacy, patient-reported outcomes (PROs), real-world effectiveness,2 cost and cost-effectiveness.3 Since the late 1980s, increasing interest in HTA has been observed globally owing to the continually soaring cost of healthcare.4 Notable HTA organisations such as the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), Canada’s Drug Agency (CDA; formerly known as CADTH), the Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits Advisory Committee and the Swedish Council on Technology Assessment in Health Care were established, providing standardised and regularly updated guidelines on methods and reporting for HTA submissions. Many high-income and middle-income countries now formally require sponsors to provide economic evaluation in submissions to the national HTA agencies who make recommendations on whether a new technology should be reimbursed.5,7 Notably, HTA bodies continue to be established to support emerging healthcare systems globally, including in regions such as Asia, the Middle East and Africa.8,10 The most widely recommended metric used to inform reimbursement decisions has been incremental cost per quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained, where QALY is a generic measure of health outcomes that captures benefits of a healthcare intervention in both quality and quantity.11 Despite ongoing debate about QALYs, particularly in the USA,12 it remains the cornerstone of outcomes measurement in economic evaluation of healthcare.

With the widespread use of QALYs in health economic evaluations, methods for measuring health utility, a metric needed for the calculation of QALYs, have evolved substantially over the past decades. Utility, in the context of QALYs, refers to a summary of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) on a scale anchored at 1 (full health) and 0 (dead), which is used to quality-weight life years.13 These utilities can be obtained for the specific health states of relevance using stated preference methods.14 Methods that have been commonly used to directly measure these preferences include rating scale, the standard gamble and the time trade-off, and more recently, various forms of discrete choice experiments.13 15 Because use of these techniques is complex and time-consuming, a common alternative indirect approach is to measure the health states of patients using standard generic measures of PRO, for which utilities are already available. These utilities, for all health states described by the PRO, are generated by studies using stated preference methods. Several generic preference-weighted PROs have been developed to quantify HRQoL for economic evaluations. Among these, widely used systems include the Health Utility Index (HUI),16 17 the Short Form 6 Dimensions (SF-6D)18 and the EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L.19 The HUI, developed in Canada, provides a multi-attribute health status classification system, including a seven-dimension (HUI-2) system16 and an eight-dimension (HUI-3) system.17 The SF-6D, derived from the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) and 12-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-12), converts general HRQoL measures into a utility score based on patient responses.18 The EQ-5D, developed by the EuroQol Group, consists of five dimensions, including mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression, initially with three problem levels in each dimension (EQ-5D-3L)20 and later expanded to include a five-level (EQ-5D-5L) system.21 Many HTA agencies reference these instruments, with systematic reviews highlighting their widespread adoption in economic evaluations.22 Additionally, other preference-weighted HRQoL instruments were also developed and used in specific geographic regions, for example the 15D in Finland23 and the Assessment of Quality of Life (AQoL) in Australia.24 Another less frequently used instrument is the Quality of Well-Being Scale (QWB), a preference-weighted HRQoL questionnaire designed to measure overall status of well-being in four domains, including physical activities, social activities, mobility and symptom/problem complexes.25 26

Especially over the last 20 years, instruments based on item response theory (IRT), most notably the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS),27 have also been developed. PROMIS encompasses a large range of physical, mental and social health domains, with the majority measured via an item banking approach. That is, a pool of items that are hypothesised to measure the same underlying latent construct are calibrated to an IRT model, which ensures that all items are measured on the same metric. Items of the same item bank can be administered in the form of established short forms, customised short forms (ie, individual selection of specific items) or via computerised adaptive testing, that is, an approach where the PRO assessment is tailored to the individual respondent. In addition, PROMIS also offers profiles where various short forms are combined. For example, the PROMIS-29 Profile is a collection of 4-item short forms covering seven health domains, that is, physical function, depression, anxiety, fatigue, sleep disturbance, ability to participate in social roles and activities, and pain interference, as well as a numeric rating scale to assess pain intensity. In addition to the PROMIS-29 Profile, eight further PROMIS profiles with different collections of short forms and items have been developed. Some of these profiles, such as PROMIS-43 and PROMIS-57, include the same seven domains as PROMIS-29, but use longer short forms, comprising six and eight items per domain respectively.28 Valuation studies have been conducted in the USA to produce preference weights (known as PROMIS Preference Scoring System or ‘PROPr’) for particular sets of health states in the PROMIS and for all health states in the PROMIS-29 Profile,29,31 enabling the calculation of health utility for these versions of PROMIS.32

Despite the importance of capturing patient preferences for health outcomes and increasingly clear guidance from HTA bodies about the evidence they require on HRQoL, preference-weighted PROs are often omitted from published clinical trials. This may reflect many factors, such as less importance placed on HRQoL and QALYs in the past, limited awareness of clinical trial designers, a tendency to focus primarily on clinical endpoints and the fact that some important healthcare markets do not consider QALY evidence in their decision-making processes (eg, the USA33 and Germany).34 Also, in some disease areas, studies that measure health utility are limited. For example, only one study using a preference-weighted instrument, the SF-36, to measure HRQoL in patients with extremity sarcoma was identified in a 2023 systematic review,35 suggesting limited familiarity and uptake of preference-weighted HRQoL instruments in this field. Identifying such gaps can support the development of strategies to encourage the use of such instruments during the study design phase. In designing studies that aim to measure health utility, the availability of a wide range of PROs with differences in domains, number of items or validations for different disease areas can make the choice of an appropriate instrument difficult. Although all preference-weighted HRQoL instruments can be used to estimate health utilities and generate QALYs, different instruments may produce different utilities for the same health state because their descriptive systems differ in the number of questions and response categories used to describe health. For example, whereas the EQ-5D-5L has five questions covering five health domains, the AQoL-8D has 35 questions and includes additional domains such as sleep and social functioning. Differences in value sets (ie, the collections of utilities assigned to all possible health states for a given instrument and country) and in the range of utilities that an instrument can produce may also contribute to variation in utility estimates. These differences raise questions about the interchangeability of QALY estimates across studies using different instruments. This heterogeneity in measurement approaches highlights the importance of systematically documenting which preference-weighted HRQoL instruments are used in clinical trials to inform HTA bodies about the comparability and synthesis (eg, meta-analysis) of preference-weighted HRQoL and QALY data.

It is currently unclear to what extent clinical trials conducted over the past decades have included health utility as an outcome measure, the extent to which the use of instruments has changed, and how their use in different disease areas, countries and geographic regions (eg, America, Europe and Asia) has changed over time. Although a few systematic reviews have summarised the use of HRQoL instruments, these have focused only on specific disease areas and/or geographical regions over a shorter period, and included only clinical trials with HRQoL instruments being reported in the titles or abstracts or being indexed in other fields that could be identified from the search (ie, these reviews were not designed to capture the proportion of all clinical trials that used an instrument).36 37

Understanding the secular trend in the use of preference-weighted HRQoL instruments in different disease areas, countries and geographic regions over a long time period will signal shifts in instrument adoption and the extent to which emerging instruments influence the landscape of health utility measurement. This will be useful to inform recommendations in HTA guidelines and provide opportunities to better understand the practical reasons behind instrument selection. The latter is important to inform improvements in existing instruments, support the development of new instruments, and to identify clinical areas where action is required to promote and support the incorporation of health utility measures in clinical trials. This study aims to examine secular trends in the use of preference-weighted instruments to measure HRQoL in adults in clinical studies published over the past 50 years. We focus only on preference-weighted PROs developed to measure HRQoL. Although preference-weighted HRQoL instruments for children and adolescents such as EQ-5D-Y38 and Child Health Utility 9 Dimensions (CHU-9D)39 have been developed, challenges remain in the development of utilities for child HRQoL, and the use of paediatric PROs is still emerging as a field. Specific objectives of the present study are: (1) to assess the changes over time in the proportions of published clinical trials that used each preference-weighted instrument to measure HRQoL in adults, and (2) to model the secular trends in the use of preference-weighted HRQoL instruments in different disease areas, countries and geographic regions.

Methods and analysis

Eligibility criteria

We will examine all clinical trials involving humans and published in English since 1976, the year when the first preference-weighted HRQoL instrument (ie, the QWB) was introduced.25 Eligible studies must be available in full text. We will exclude non-human studies, non-English studies, protocols and abstracts only.

Search strategies

Searches will be conducted in MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science to identify relevant clinical trials. The search strategy will be based on the following terms and their variants: ‘clinical trial’, ‘clinical study’, ‘medical study’, ‘controlled trial’, ‘randomised trial’ and ‘RCT’. We will apply filters for (1) studies involving human participants, (2) studies in medicine or healthcare, (3) studies published in English, and (4) studies without the term ‘protocol’ in their titles. We will include all records identified from the search for screening and data extraction. The search results will be imported into the reference management tool EndNote (Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA, USA) to collect the PDF files using the ‘Find Full Text’ feature for all studies within institutional library subscriptions. For articles outside the subscription, library services will be used to retrieve the remaining full texts.

Screening and data extraction

A preliminary search in MEDLINE alone suggests that over the past 50 years, more than 260 000 clinical studies involving humans have been published in full text in English. Manually screening and extracting data from such a large volume of studies is impractical. To address this, we will automate the process using natural language processing (NLP) techniques or large language models (LLMs). To determine the most accurate automated approach for screening and extracting, we will compare the performance of our NLP pipeline and several LLMs against a reference standard derived from manually extracted data on a subset of articles.

The following types of information will be extracted from each article: (1) journal name; (2) year of publication; (3) years during which the study was conducted; (4) country of corresponding author; (5) country where the study was conducted; and (6) whether the study used a specific preference-weighted instrument to directly measure HRQoL.

This study will focus only on generic preference-weighted HRQoL instruments, specifically: EQ-5D-3L,20 EQ-5D-5L,21 SF-6D,18 SF-12,40 HUI-2,16 HUI-3,17 AQoL-8D,41 AQoL-7D,42 AQoL-6D,43 AQoL-4D,44 QWB,25 QWB-Self-Administered (QWB-SA),45 15D23 and PROMIS with PROPr.27

To inform model training in NLP pipeline and refine prompts for a LLM, we will systematically document the full range of terminology and reporting patterns used to either mention instruments only or to describe HRQoL measurement using specific instruments. We will also identify common reporting patterns and typical locations within articles where considered study characteristics are presented, including journal information, publication year, study period, country of corresponding author and country of study conduct. To achieve this, we will randomly select articles identified through the search strategies and manually review them until approximately 100 articles referencing each preference-weighted HRQoL instrument are obtained. As individual articles may report multiple instruments, a single article can contribute to multiple article-instrument instances. Each article-instrument instance will be classified as either ‘mention only’ or ‘actual use’ for the corresponding instrument. This process will be performed independently by three reviewers, with disagreements resolved through discussion and, where necessary, consultation with a fourth reviewer.

Natural language processing approach

We will develop a tailored NLP pipeline designed specifically to identify and classify the use of preference-weighted HRQoL instruments. This approach emphasises precision, reproducibility and transparency in processing a large volume of studies, while incorporating specific rules, patterns and insights that come from the HRQoL research domain. To implement the NLP approach, we will use GROBID (GeneRation Of BIbliographic Data)46 to automatically convert the PDF files into XML documents, which will be used as input for the NLP programme. XML structure is used to preserve the logical structure of scientific articles, including section headings, paragraphs and references, which supports accurate text segmentation and rule-based information extraction. Since parsing the PDF corpus is computationally intensive, a high-performance, multi-core server will be used to accelerate this process.

An important aspect of developing the NLP pipeline is ensuring they can distinguish between documents that merely mention preference-weighted HRQoL instruments and those that actually use them for HRQoL measurement. Using the terminology and reporting patterns identified during manual review, we will construct two complementary resources within the NLP pipeline.

First, we will develop annotated corpora that capture the terminology and phrasing used to describe the use of individual preference-weighted HRQoL instruments. These corpora will systematically document variations in how each preference-weighted instrument is mentioned (eg, the instrument name appears in introduction or background), referenced (eg, the instrument is cited but not necessarily used in the study) or reported (eg, the instrument is described in methods or results) in academic articles. They will primarily support the training of classification models designed to distinguish between ‘mention only’ and ‘actual use’ and to detect specific instruments.

Second, we will develop a structured set of annotated reporting patterns to facilitate the extraction of key study characteristics mentioned earlier (eg, journal information and publication year). These reporting patterns are conceptually distinct from the classification corpora and will be used directly in the rule-based NLP component for information extraction rather than for model training. A rule-based NLP approach will then be applied to identify and extract these study characteristics using structured textual cues derived from bibliographic metadata, author affiliations and relevant sections of the full text. In this context, structured textual cues refer to predictable, rule-governed patterns in text that signal specific types of information and can be reliably identified using rule-based methods, such as section headings, journal name and author affiliation formats containing countries.

In parallel, the NLP pipeline will be configured to detect document segments that report HRQoL measurement and, in particular, the use of preference-weighted HRQoL instruments. To filter parts of articles containing specific terminology, we will first perform section-level text segmentation to divide full-text articles into structured sections (eg, Methods, Results and References). Following segmentation, we will implement the same rule-based approach using pre-defined patterns. This method is particularly effective for structured sections of academic articles, such as Methods and Results, where terminology is typically consistent. The rules will scan each section for exact or pattern-based matches of known instrument names (eg, ‘EQ-5D-3L’, ‘SF-6D’, ‘HUI-3’ and ‘AQoL-8D’) and their text variants, in close proximity (eg, less than 10 words) to utility-related terms such as ‘utility’, ‘QALY’, ‘preference-weighted’, ‘tariff’, ‘value set’, ‘time trade-off’, or ‘standard gamble’. Boolean logic will be used to combine inclusion and exclusion patterns; for instance, cases where instrument names appear without any utility-related term will be excluded. Section-level filtering will further ensure that matches are only retained from relevant parts of the text (eg, Methods and Results sections) while excluding mentions in references or acknowledgements.

For each corpus created for each instrument, we will train and evaluate deep learning-based sequence classification models that are capable of processing long textual inputs and capturing contextual word dependencies. We distinguish between two high-level categories of NLP approaches: traditional bag-of-words representations combined with shallow machine learning classifiers (eg, support vector machines and naïve Bayes) and deep learning models. Traditional bag-of-words approaches discard word order and treat documents as unordered collections of words. This can be inadequate for scientific articles, where sentence structure and word ordering contribute substantially to meaning. For example, the statements “we used EQ-5D-3L instead of SF-6D” and “we used SF-6D instead of EQ-5D-3L” contain identical words but describe opposite methodological choices. We will therefore use deep learning models, which preserve word order and are better suited to processing long, complex and structured documents such as academic articles. To implement deep learning models in NLP, we will use deep learning architectures, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs),47 recurrent neural networks (RNNs)47 and transformer architectures.48 Before training these models, the text will be tokenised, meaning that raw text is split into smaller units called tokens (ie, words or subwords). Then, each document is represented as a sequence of token embeddings, where each embedding is a numerical vector that encodes an individual token as numerical input for the model. In this study, we will prioritise context-aware embeddings, which generate different vector representations for the same token depending on the surrounding text. This approach is deemed suitable for full-text scientific articles, where terminology is context-dependent and methodological descriptions may vary across studies. CNNs produce feature maps that capture local patterns in text, RNNs produce hidden state sequences that capture sequential dependencies and transformer architectures generate contextual embeddings that incorporate long-range dependencies. For each model type, the learned representations in the form of numerical vectors will be passed into a task-specific fully connected neural network layer, followed by an activation function (eg, sigmoid), to transform these learned representations into probability scores for classification.

At the document level, models will classify each identified preference-weighted HRQoL instrument into ‘no mention’, ‘mention only’, or ‘actual use’, distinguishing between mere mentions and confirmed applications of specific instruments. Prediction probabilities will also be generated along with the classification outputs of models to quantify prediction confidence.

Large language model approach

In addition to our custom NLP pipeline, we will consider LLMs since this approach has distinct advantages for the task. As previously mentioned, the NLP approach adopts a modular strategy, combining rule-based and neural networks to build an end-to-end system optimised for this specific task. In contrast, LLMs like GPT-4 (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA)49 excel at contextual understanding and can perform classification tasks, such as distinguishing between mentions and actual uses of instruments, with minimal fine-tuning or even in zero-shot settings.50,52 Additionally, LLMs have demonstrated high accuracy and adaptability across a wide range of information extraction tasks.53 54 However, their use is accompanied by several challenges. LLMs require substantial computational resources,55 carefully designed prompt engineering56 and third-party tools for specific tasks such as full-text PDF parsing, each of which may introduce errors or inconsistencies.50 Additionally, these models are susceptible to hallucinations, that is, generating content not present in the source text.50 To evaluate performance, we will compare the result from the manual screening of the sub-sample of articles against those of selected LLMs, including (but not limited to) GPT-5,57 Claude 4 (Anthropic PBC, San Francisco, CA, USA)58 and Llama 4 (Meta Platforms, Inc., Menlo Park, CA, USA)59 using a range of confidence-level metrics (see ‘Evaluation of model performance’ section).

To ensure optimal performance of the LLMs, we will develop an overall task-specific prompt template for all LLMs based on established prompt engineering strategies in medical NLP.60 This template will include techniques to enhance clarity, relevance and model alignment with domain-specific tasks. The template will define the expected document structure and outputs, assign the model a domain-specific persona (eg, a health economics researcher) and provide concise contextual information to clarify the classification task.60 61

Before providing studies as input to LLMs, the context will be provided in the form of brief background details to help the model understand the scope of the problem.60 61 We will begin with a set of core prompts and conduct a single round of controlled refinement to improve clarity, consistency and alignment with the study objectives. To implement this process, the prompts will be constructed using the above-mentioned template that includes the following elements:

Task instruction: a statement directing the model to perform two tasks: (a) extract key study characteristics from the document or relevant sections, including journal name, year of publication, years during which the study was conducted, country of the corresponding author and country where the study was conducted; and (b) determine whether a preference-weighted HRQoL instrument was actually used for HRQoL measurement in a given document or section.

Text inputs to the LLMs will consist of either full articles or document segments (eg, Methods section) parsed into the markdown format using Markdrop, an open source Python package.62 The markdown format is used to provide a clean and readable text format aligned with how LLMs are typically trained to process text, while preserving the structural organisation of the source documents. Depending on the input length limitations and computational resource availability of the model, either full-section inputs or paragraph-level chunk inputs with overlapping windows will be used to preserve continuity. The prompt template will wrap these inputs, maintaining the same structural cues to ensure clarity and consistency.

The use of article content with commercial LLM services may be subject to publisher licensing terms, institutional policies and evolving text-mining and data-mining requirements. Because our analysis requires processing of full articles, we will minimise the risk of data retention and reuse of article text using the following approaches. For GPT-based and Claude models, we will use Spark AI,63 a secure environment provided by the University of Melbourne (Melbourne, Victoria, Australia) that prevents users’ data from being exposed to external systems. Spark AI requires user-specific credentials, encrypts data in transit and stores data securely in user-specific accounts. For LLMs that are not incorporated in Spark AI, we will use enterprise application programming interface (API)-based services rather than consumer-facing interfaces, as these services provide stronger data governance controls and do not use submitted inputs for model training by default. Where possible, we will disable options for training and data use, and we will also consider locally deployable models (eg, Llama-based models) to further reduce external transfer of article text.

Initial prompt versions will be evaluated using a validation set of manually classified articles, which are the same set used in the NLP approach. Model outputs will be assessed not only for accuracy but also for confidence, consistency and formatting stability. Findings from these evaluations will guide the prompt refinement, which may involve clarifying task instructions, adjusting example phrasing (ie, providing artificially constructed sample inputs and outputs) or removing irrelevant cues. Misclassifications and hallucinations will be documented to identify ambiguities in task interpretation. Based on this evaluation, a single round of prompt refinement will be conducted. This refinement process is designed solely to enhance task understanding and instruction clarity, rather than to retrain or optimise the model using validation outcomes, thereby minimising the risk of data leakage.

The resulting refined prompts will be treated as the final prompt set for broader application and subsequent evaluation. Model-specific refinements will be limited to minimal technical adjustments necessary for compatibility, such as input formatting modifications to meet token limits or interface requirements.

Evaluation of model performance

From the search results, a random sample of 5000 articles will be selected to evaluate model performance, ensuring no overlap with articles used for model training in NLP pipeline and for prompt refinement of LLMs. This sample will be processed using the NLP pipeline, the LLMs and manual screening by three independent reviewers. For the latter, as in the previous manual review to inform development of the NLP pipeline and LLM prompts, any discrepancies in the results among the three reviewers will be resolved through discussion and, where necessary, consultation with a fourth reviewer. Special attention will be paid to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the manually extracted data, as it serves as the reference standard for subsequent analyses.

For each model and each type of information, we will calculate standard classification metrics, referred to as confidence-level metrics, including accuracy, recall and F1-score, using results from the manual review as the reference standard.64 In this context, accuracy refers to the proportion of the sampled articles with correct classification by the model (ie, the number of articles that is correctly classified by the model as containing or not containing a specific type of information divided by the total number of sampled articles). Precision is defined as the proportion of articles identified by a model as containing a specific type of information that truly contains that type of information (ie, the number of articles with a specific type of information correctly identified by the model divided by the number flagged by the model). Recall represents the proportion of actual positives that are correctly identified by the model (ie, the number of articles with a specific type of information correctly identified by the model divided by the true number of articles with that type of information). The F1-score, that is, the harmonic mean of precision and recall, summarises the balance between these two metrics with F1=(2 ×Precision ×Recall) ÷(Precision+Recall). For all these metrics, the closer the values are to 1, the better the model performance.

Given the potential class imbalance between mentions and true use of HRQoL instruments, the F1-score will serve as the primary metric, while accuracy will be reported as complementary indicators of performance. We will also calculate inter-rater reliability using Cohen’s kappa coefficient65 between manual reviewers and each model to assess reliability. Cohen’s kappa coefficient quantifies the degree of agreement beyond chance, with values close to 1 indicating almost perfect agreement, values near 0 reflecting agreement no better than random chance, and intermediate values corresponding to varying levels of consistency. This will be used to evaluate how closely the model outputs align with the judgments of human (ie, the reviewers), not only in terms of raw accuracy but also in terms of reproducibility and reliability. The model with the highest overall F1-score, supported by consistent performance across the other metrics, will be selected to process the remaining articles.

Outputs from the selected model

The outputs from the selected model will be organised into a structured dataset, with each eligible clinical study annotated for: (1) use of a preference-weighted HRQoL instrument, classified as ‘no use’, ‘mention only’ and ‘actual use’ and linked to the specific instrument; (2) name of the instrument (if actual use); (3) study period; (4) year of publication; (5) country where the study was conducted; and (6) journal name.

Where a clinical study discusses or uses multiple preference-weighted HRQoL instruments, each instrument will be independently classified and recorded. This approach allows studies mentioning or using multiple HRQoL instruments to be captured systematically.

Post-extraction assessment

If a preference-weighted HRQoL instrument was used in a clinical trial, it will be counted for each year within the study period. If the study period is not reported, the year of publication will be used. For a clinical trial that was conducted across many countries, the instrument used in that clinical trial will be counted for each of the participating countries. If the country of the study is not reported, the country of the corresponding author will be used. We will map the journal names to specific research areas (eg, cardiology, oncology and general medicine) using the Scopus Source list66 that contains all journal names and the corresponding subject areas.

Data analysis

We will assess the performance of NLP pipeline and LLMs in large-scale classification, focusing on trade-offs between transparency, contextual adaptability, computational resource requirements and cost of use. These outcomes will provide the empirical foundation for modelling secular trends and for evaluating the practical feasibility of applying different AI-based approaches to large-scale evidence synthesis.

To explore the patterns of instrument use over time, we will compute annual counts and proportions over the past five decades. These will be broken down by instrument type, disease area, country and geographic region. Trends will be presented in two ways: number of studies using each instrument and the proportion of clinical trials that used a specific instrument in a specific calendar year. This dual approach will allow us to capture both the scale and relative uptake of instruments across different contexts and time periods.

We will use basis-splines (B-splines)67 68 with 2 or 3 degrees of freedom and Bayesian spline regression67 69 to model trends of changes in the number and proportion of preference-weighted HRQoL instrument use over time. To select the best-fitting model for determining the trend, we will apply the smallest deviance information criterion which is a trade-off between model fit and complexity.70 To compare the trends in use between instruments and the trends in use for a specific instrument between research areas, countries and geographic regions, we will calculate the Bayes factor71 for each fitted model. The Bayes factor indicates the strength of evidence for change in the prevalence of an instrument relative to no change at all. We will use R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) for the statistical analysis.

Since individual studies may discuss or use multiple preference-weighted HRQoL instruments, each instrument identified within a study contributes a separate record to the analysis dataset. Consequently, the number of instrument records may exceed the number of studies. In the descriptive analysis, we will also report: (a) the total number of studies screened; (b) the total number of instrument records generated (ie, study-instrument combinations); (c) the frequency distribution of studies by number of instruments mentioned or used; and (d) patterns of instrument co-use (ie, which instruments are frequently discussed or used together). This stratification will provide insights into trends in multi-instrument use over time and whether certain instruments are commonly used in combination.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study. The protocol is based exclusively on analysis of published literature and does not involve primary data collection or direct patient participation.

Study period

The study is expected to commence in December 2026 and conclude in December 2027.

Discussion

Although a few studies have examined trends in the use of an individual preference-weighted HRQoL instrument, such as the EQ-5D72 73 or the SF-6D, there has been no comprehensive investigation into the usage trends of a wide range of instruments, assessed both in absolute and relative terms, across all published clinical trials over an extended period. We propose a study that systematically examines the use of all available preference-weighted HRQoL instruments in all clinical trials published over the past 50 years. While this study is limited to adults for now, we will propose a follow-up study in the future with a focus on the use of instruments for children and adolescents. Conducting a study of this scale using a conventional systematic review approach presents significant challenges due to time and resource constraints. In the present protocol, we have introduced innovative, state-of-the-art methodologies designed to overcome these limitations and to generate robust, scalable evidence. The study is the first of its kind to explore secular trends of PRO measure use in HTA evaluations over the past five decades, a task enabled by the use of artificial intelligence that would have been infeasible in the past.

Several studies have demonstrated that the EQ-5D family of questionnaires have been the dominant instruments for measuring HRQoL in specific research areas. However, in the past decade, there has been growing interest in newer preference-weighted HRQoL instruments that may have more favourable attributes in specific clinical areas and which extend beyond health to include broader aspects of well-being, such as the EQ Health and Wellbeing (EQ-HWB),74 the PROMIS27 and the ICEpop CAPability measure for Adults (ICECAP-A).75 Understanding a potential shift in the use of preference-weighted HRQoL instruments is essential for improving existing tools or developing more robust instruments. Furthermore, our study will identify clinical areas where preference-weighted HRQoL instruments have been rarely used or not used at all, identifying important evidence gaps and suggesting priorities for policy decisions in HTA and healthcare aimed at promoting their adoption.

In addition to presenting the main findings, we will provide a flexible framework that can be adapted to identify studies that mention, cite and/or use any PRO instrument. This study will also establish an innovative method that can be applied to future research with different focuses, for example, including other types of studies beyond clinical trials or concentrating on specific disease areas or a subgroup of patients. If our approach proves to be viable and provides high-quality extraction tables, it will set a new standard for systematic evidence review in our area and beyond.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis is restricted to English-language publications, which may lead to the under-representation of trials published in other languages and may affect the generalisability of global usage trends. Second, the study focuses exclusively on adult populations and therefore does not capture the use of preference-weighted HRQoL instruments in children and adolescents. Third, the protocol assesses only major LLMs, which may limit the completeness and accuracy of automated identification and extraction of instruments. Consequently, some instruments reported in the literature may be missed or misclassified, and findings may not fully reflect the performance of alternative NLP approaches or emerging models.

Ethics and dissemination

This study is based exclusively on published literature and does not involve human participants or access to individual-level data. All findings will be reported in aggregate form, with no personal or identifiable information included. Therefore, formal ethics approval is not required. Results will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presentation at academic conferences. Aggregated data and analysis code will be made publicly available to enhance transparency and reproducibility.
