1 Introduction
This report details our FY26 experimental investigation of UC (uranium monocarbide, also known as
uranium methanide). We characterized UC’s rate of oxidation at ambient conditions and performed
quasistatic X-ray diffraction (XRD) experiments in diamond anvil cells (DAC) at high pressures (P ).
These DAC experiments extended to higher P than any previous work on UC and revealed new in-
formation about its phase diagram and compressibility. Complementary first-principles calculations
on high-P UC are currently ongoing and will be described in a separate report.
 UC has much in common with UN (uranium mononitride), which we investigated in FY25
[1, 2]. Like UN, UC crystallizes in a nominally face-centered-cubic (FCC, space group F m3̄m)
crystal structure, and is known to have at least one transition to a high-P phase around 27 GPa
[3]. Unlike UN, UC readily oxidizes in air, and this difficulty has limited previous research into
UC’s properties. The only previous observation of the high-P phase inferred it was "pseudo-body-
centered orthorhombic" but was not able to determine its space group [3]. A recent computational
study proposed the high-P phase may belong to the orthorhombic space group P mmn [4]. For
convenience, this report will refer to the nominally FCC phase of UC (i.e., the phase which is stable
at ambient P ) as α-UC, and the orthorhombic high-P phase as β-UC.
 This report is organized as follows: Section 2 describes the sample synthesis and characterization
at ambient conditions, Section 3 describes our high-P experiments, and Section 4 details the inter-
pretation of the outcome of our experiments in terms of phase assemblage and unit cell parameters
as a function of P .

2 Synthesis and characterization
UC powder was fabricated using a carbothermic reduction (CTR) method [5]. UO2 and graphite
powders were milled in a C:O ratio of 1.2, then added to a ZrO2 jar and milled in a high energy ball
mill for 45 minutes. After milling, the powders were pressed into pellets and placed on tungsten
trays, which were transferred to a Centorr furnace for CTR at 2073 K. After CTR, a portion of the
powders was sealed under an inert atmosphere for use in DAC samples, and another portion was
exposed to air to monitor the rate of oxidation.
 The chemistry of the UC powder after 0, 1, and 5 hours of oxidation was evaluated using a
LECO C844 combustion analyzer to determine O content and a Bruker D2 X-ray diffractometer to
determine phase purity (Table 2-1). LECO data were calibrated with a WC standard (LECO corp.
501–123), and each sample was measured in triplicate using approximately 0.1 g of material in an
Al2 O3 crucible with Sn and Cu accelerant (for C analysis) or an Sn vial with a C crucible and no
accelerant (for O analysis). XRD data were collected with Cu K-α radiation over a diffraction angle
range of 10 − 90◦ , and were analyzed via Rietveld refinement with X’Pert Highscore [6].
 Rietveld analysis detected the major FCC α-UC phase, as well as minor uranium dicarbide
(UC2 , also known as uranium acetylide) with the tetragonal I4/mmm structure [7]. No oxide
impurity phase was detectable until after 1 hour of oxidation, and the quantity of oxide was very
small (<1 wt%) even after 5 hours. Combustion analysis did detect a small amount of O even
in the starting material. Assuming the worst-case scenario (i.e., that all O was hosted in the UC
lattice), the nominal UC phase would be an oxycarbide with a stoichiometry of UC0.902 O0.098 .
Additional uptake of O caused the UC unit cell volume (V ) to decrease by ≈ 0.5% after 1 hour of
exposure to air, but the 0-hour initial V matches literature values for UC within 0.3% [8, 9, 10, 11,
12]. We determined that the starting composition was sufficiently pure to proceed with the DAC
experiments. Since DAC sample preparation involves air exposures of typically less than 1 hour, no
major changes to the as-synthesized composition were expected.

3 Experimental methods
To create DAC samples, the powdered UC starting material was unsealed and pressed into sample
flakes approximately 10 µm thick and 60 µm in diameter. These were loaded into sample chambers
consisting of 90 µm diameter holes drilled into Re gaskets pre-indented to 30 µm thickness. Similarly
sized Cu flakes and 20 µm diameter ruby spheres were also loaded to serve as in situ P standards.
The gaskets were mounted on symmetric-type DACs modified for radioactive containment with
200 µm diameter anvil culets and were gas loaded with Ne to serve as a hydrostatic P -transmitting
medium. Fluorescence from the ruby spheres was intended to verify cell closure, but no signal
was observed due to a spectrometer malfunction, leading to a large range of initial P . Three DAC
experiments were prepared. Experiment 1 suffered a gasket collapse during Ne loading. Experiments
2 and 3 were loaded at initial P of 1 and 25 GPa, respectively.
 The DAC samples were taken to Advanced Photon Source synchrotron at Argonne National
Laboratory (Sector 16, administered by HPCAT) for angle-dispersive XRD experiments. Each
sample was isothermally compressed at ambient temperature using an inflating gas membrane [13].
Nominal synchrotron beam size was 1x1 µm2 full-width at half of the maximum intensity, beam
energy was 29.2 keV (λ = 0.42459 Å), and a 50 µm diameter pinhole was used to reduce beam tails.
The distance from the sample to the Pilatus3 X 2M X-ray detector was 349.5 mm, as calibrated by
a CeO2 diffraction standard. The exposure time for each diffraction pattern was 0.1 seconds.
 Diffraction images were integrated in Dioptas [14] over a diffraction angle range of 5-21° and
analyzed with whole-pattern Le Bail refinement in GSAS-II [15]. The refined parameters were: the
instrumental background function, the unit cell dimensions, the relative phase abundances, and the
microstrain broadening of each phase. The Le Bail method treats the height of each peak as a
free parameter and does not use structure factors to constrain relative diffraction intensities [16].
While this introduces additional degrees of freedom that can confound peak indexing [17], the Le
Bail method is often necessary in cases where there are crystallographic perferred orientations or
multiple coexisting phases with similar lattice spacings. In these cases, which include the high-P
transition in UC (see Section 4.1), other algorithms (such as Rietveld refinement) tend to converge
on unphysical solutions.
 Sample P was determined based on the refined V of Cu in each pattern and the equation of
state parameters of [18]. Since both Cu and UC were present at the same X-ray focal region and
our Ne pressure transmitting medium cannot sustain large P gradients (i.e., ≈ 0.006 GPa/µm at 50
GPa [19]), we are confident that the stress state of the two phases are consistent with each other.
UC unit cells were refined in either FCC (F m3̄m), orthorhombic (P mmn), or hexagonal (R3̄m)
symmetry, with the latter then being converted into the non-standard face-centered rhombohedral
(FCR) setting using the following equations:

4 Results and Discussion

4.1 Crystal structures
Figure 4-1 shows a selection of integrated and refined XRD data from our DAC experiments. We
did not observe evidence of UO2 or UC2 in these data. As noted in Section 2, α-UC at ambient
P is described well by the conventional FCC structure. This is also true for our DAC data where
P < 15 GPa. However, over the range 15–25 GPa, the first two principal diffraction features (at
Q ≈ 2.2, 2.6 Å3 ) broaden and new shoulder peaks appear (Q ≈ 2.1, 2.3, 2.7 Å3 ). These changes
likely indicate the incipient crystallization of a separate β-UC phase. Although the new phase’s
intensity is too low in this region to be confident in its V or symmetry, we have chosen to treat
it as P mmn and include it in our refinements. Both P mmn and Immm have been proposed for
β-UC, but we prefer P mmn because the reflection at Q ≈ 1.4 Å3 should not occur with the Immm
structure, and P mmm is more stable according to density functional theory (DFT) calculations
[4, 21]. Including β-UC in the 15–25 GPa refinements avoids confounding the V of the coexisting
α-UC; Olsen et al. [3], on the other hand, treated all data up to 28 GPa as a single phase and
noticed an anomalous increase in V at 15 GPa (i.e., where we first observe β-UC).
 Between 28 and 33 GPa, UC undergoes a structural transition where the orthorhombic β-
UC increases in intensity until it dominates the phase assemblage. Over this same range, α-UC
diminishes in intensity, rapidly decreases in V , and its first diffraction peak (Q ≈ 2.3 Å3 ) broadens
and splits into a doublet. This doublet is inconsistent with the phase’s nominally cubic symmetry,
but is expected in a FCR structure. FCR is a non-standard setting of the R3̄m space group which
increases the size of the unit cell and uses a change of basis (Equations 1–2) to align the atomic
positions with those of FCC. FCR can therefore be thought of as a distorted FCC structure where
the sides of the unit cell remain equal in length (a = b = c) but the angles between the sides become
acute (α = β = γ < 90◦ ). For clarity, we will refer to the α angle as θ to avoid confusion with
α-UC. The high-P phase of UN [3] has FCR symmetry, and FCR was recently found to apply to the
low-P phase as well, with the phase transition representing a decrease of θ from ≈ 90◦ to ≈ 86◦ [2].
We can extend this interpretation from UN to UC: if α-UC at <28 GPa is FCR with θ ≈ 90◦ , then
the transition region represents a degree-two (i.e., continuous) transition to θ ≈ 89◦ , resulting in
the separation of the doublet peaks. We will refer to this modified high-P structure as α′ -UC. Note
that FCR with θ ≈ 90◦ is almost indistinguishable from FCC and does not significantly influence
the determination of V in α-UC; we appeal to it because using FCR for both α and α′ is more
parsimonious than appealing to a distinct FCC α phase.
 The beginning of our transition region at 28 GPa matches the α-UC disappearance P observed
by Olsen et al. [3]. In our data, however, α′ -UC persists alongside the dominant β phase up to
our highest measured P . This wide-ranging phase coexistence is unusual; there can be only one
energetically stable equilibrium phase for a given stoichiometry at a given set of conditions, and in
most experiments the low-P phase disappears within a few GPa of the high-P phase crystallizing.
It could be that the total energy of the FCR and P mmn structures are similar enough in the high-P
region that kinetic energy barriers are sufficient to inhibit total conversion of the former, despite the
latter being slightly stabler. The use of a methanol/ethanol P -transmitting medium by Olsen et al.
(which can sustain P gradients > 10× that of Ne [19]) might have led to total α → β conversion in
that case since larger deviatoric stresses can catalyze phase transitions [22]. Nonetheless, replication
of this phase coexistence should be a priority for future studies.

istence of multiple phases makes it difficult to positively identify each diffraction feature, especially
at higher diffraction angles. Specifically, there is a small reflection near Q ≈ 2.0 Å3 (d ≈ 3.1 Å)
that appears when β-UC becomes dominant, but whose position is not attributable to β-UC or any
other material in our samples (see the crosses in Figure 4-1). This reflection could indicate that
β-UC is actually less symmetrical than P mmn. It is even conceivable that all the UC peaks in the
"high-P region" are attributable to a single phase with even lower (presumably monoclinic) sym-
metry. Follow-up computational work will search for other candidate UC structures to determine
whether either of these scenarios are reasonable [21].

5 Conclusions
We studied the high-P behavior of UC with DAC XRD measurements. Despite the susceptibility
of UC to oxidation, we were able to synthesize and load a pristine sample of α-UC with a crystal
structure and V0 consistent with previous measurements. Starting at ∼15 GPa, orthorhombic β-UC
begins to crystallize. We find that the β phase is likely P mmn in symmetry. Accounting for this
coexisting phase improved the quality of the α-UC EoS fit; our α-UC K0 is higher than previous
experimental values but consistent with recent ab-initio calculations (≈180 GPA). Around 30 GPa,
β-UC becomes the dominant phase and α-UC transforms into a modified α′ structure. Since α′ -UC
appears to be FCR with lattice angle θ ≈ 89◦ , we favor the interpretation that α-UC is also FCR
with θ ≈ 90◦ , though it is hard to distinguish between this case and FCC α-UC with θ = 90◦ . Both
the α′ and β phases persisted up to 80 GPa. The range of compression for these phases was too low
for a high-fidelity EoS fit, but both phases appear to have ≈ 10% larger K0 than α-UC, meaning
that UC becomes less compressible during the high-P transition. Our observation of coexistence of
α′ and β-UC over a large P range is unusual and warrants further investigation. Forthcoming DFT
calculations should shed more light on the equilibrium structure at high P .
