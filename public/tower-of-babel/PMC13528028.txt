Epigenetic entropy, socioeconomic differences, and health and lifespan in the Women’s Health Initiative
Authors: Khyobeni Mozhui, Athena Starlard-Davenport, Yangbo Sun, Aladdin H. Shadyab, Ramon Casanova, Fridtjof Thomas, Robert B. Wallace, Jay H. Fowke, Karen C. Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAging is marked by changes in DNA methylation (DNAm) including an increase in randomness or entropy in the epigenome. Here, we computed epigenetic entropy using DNAm data from the Women’s Health Initiative (WHI) ancillary study AS315 (n = 2192), and tested for replication in WHI ancillary studies, BA23 (n = 1989) and AS311 (n = 868). We investigated how different metrics of epigenetic stochasticity relate to demographic variables, health, and mortality risk, and how these compare to machine-learning based models of aging such as the DunedinPACE.ResultsEpigenetic entropy had robust and consistent negative associations with income and education in the WHI. Notably, low income was related to increased stochasticity and higher methylation in DNA regions that are targeted by the polycomb proteins—key regulators of cellular memory and embryonic development. While higher income was associated with reduced entropy scores and lower DunedinPACE in White women, this effect of income was diminished in Black and Hispanic women, and on average, Black and Hispanic women had relatively higher stochastic variability. Painting a complex picture, our analysis of the polycomb regions in a small cohort of children showed significant positive associations with parental income. Additionally, in the WHI, higher DunedinPACE predicted higher mortality risk, while the maintenance of methylation at enhancer regions was associated with improved survival.ConclusionsThese findings indicate close ties between social and economic factors and aspects of epigenetic aging, and highlight the polycomb targeted CpGs as potential sensors of the social environment and related exposures across demographic groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13148-026-02180-3.

Background

Epigenetic clocks such as the Horvath clock, PhenoAge, DunedinPACE, and many others are machine-learning (ML) based predictive biomarkers that estimate the biological age or the rate of aging [1–4]. The underlying biomolecular data is DNA methylation (DNAm)—the methylation status of cytosine residues that are adjacent to guanine residues (CpG sites). These epigenetic marks can be measured from readily accessible biofluids such as blood or saliva and are informative of whether a person is aging faster or slower relative to their chronological age. The first-generation clocks (e.g., Horvath, Hannum) were trained to be accurate predictors of chronological age [1, 2]. The second-generation clocks (e.g., PhenoAge, GrimAge) were trained on broader health parameters and better assess overall health, disease states, and life expectancy [3, 5, 6]. The recent DunedinPACE model was developed by tracking longitudinal changes in health traits from age-matched individuals over decades, and is reported to directly measure the pace-of-aging [4].

Development of these ML-based clocks starts with a training phase during which the algorithms select a set of CpGs that are assigned specific weights. The final computed value (be it the predicted age, mortality, or rate of aging) is based on the methylation levels at distinct CpGs. The preselected CpGs are typically unique for each model, and the reasons behind why the algorithm selected those specific sites and assigned those numeric weights remain obscure [7–9]. Due to this opacity, the biological basis of these clocks, and the extent to which they represent stochastic versus programmatic processes of aging remain largely undefined [10–12]. There are however some common themes shared across the DNAm clocks that hint at the underlying biology. For instance, relative to the genome-wide background, DNA sequences in bivalent chromatin states and bound by the polycomb repressive complex (particularly PRC2) are consistently overrepresented among both the first- and second-generation clocks, and recent work by Moqri et al. has proposed the PRC2-AgeIndex, defined as the average methylation at the PRC2 targeted CpGs, as a universal biomarker of aging [3, 6, 13]. These are evolutionarily conserved sequences and regulated by the polycomb group proteins, which play critical roles in gene expression regulation and establishment of cellular memory during embryonic development and cell fate determination [14]. A common feature of aging is for CpGs in gene promoters, particularly those targeted by the polycomb complex, to drift from low to relatively higher methylation states [15]. Concurrently, sites that have high methylation at birth begin to lose methylation with aging, and this shifting landscape of the methylome is implicated in cancers and other health risks [16]. These patterns of change are closely related to epigenetic entropy, a term borrowed from Shannon’s Information theory that refers to the level of randomness that can be computed from DNAm data either at the global genome-wide or at regional scales [16–18]. The epigenetic shifts and the accumulation of stochastic variability likely contribute to some of the signals captured by the epigenetic clocks [9, 16, 18, 19].

The Women’s Health Initiative (WHI) is a long-term prospective study of postmenopausal women that began recruitment in 1993 [20–22]. Previous studies of the first- and second-generation DNAm clocks in the WHI have shown that a higher rate of epigenetic aging predicts shorter lifespan, and are associated with lung cancer [23], insomnia and immune aging [24], cognitive impairment and dementia [25], diabetes related traits, and cardiovascular health [26–29]. Notably, the PhenoAge clock is strongly associated with social disparities—WHI participants with lower education exhibited more advanced epigenetic aging, and accelerated PhenoAge partly explained the disparity in life expectancy between racial and ethnic groups [29]. This is consistent with the growing evidence that these epigenetic biomarkers of aging are strongly influenced by the larger social and environmental context. In the United States, recent studies have shown that the DunedinPACE detects more rapid aging among Black participants and among participants with low education and income [30, 31]. This sensitivity to social factors should not be surprising since variables such as education, poverty, and experiences of societal biases have profound impacts on health, stress, and mental and emotional well-being. In fact, the close ties between the epigenetic models of aging and social variables support the “weathering hypothesis”, which proposes that the chronic exposure to socioeconomic disadvantage and related stressors lead to accelerated aging and higher disease burden among minority groups [29–32].

However, a major caveat is that all these ML-based epigenetic biomarkers were trained in cohorts that were predominantly, or in the case of the DunedinPACE, almost exclusively of European ancestry [4, 33]. As a result, the estimated metrics of biological aging are calibrated to the characteristics of the initial training cohort, and this will very likely introduce algorithmic biases that limit full generalizability to other populations. This implies that some of the population differences reported by previous studies could be confounded by the inconsistent performance of these models in different populations.

In the present work, we use the WHI datasets to compute training-independent estimates of epigenetic entropy and CpG methylation variability. Our goal is not to develop another predictive model but to examine how these fundamental features of the methylome relate to social and health variables. The only subsetting of CpGs we performed was based on biologically informed chromatin states. We start by examining correlations between chronological age and different measures of epigenetic entropy or drift and gross chromatin states. We then examine whether the non-ML readouts will be as sensitive to socioeconomic disparities and predictive of life expectancy. Numerous previous studies have extensively examined the first- and second-generation clocks in the WHI [3, 23–29, 34], and here we use only the newer DunedinPACE model as a training-based reference for comparison. We present evidence that simple measures of epigenetic stochasticity and mean methylation at the polycomb sites are associated with socioeconomic disparities. We also recapitulate the strong sensitivity of the DunedinPACE to the social environment and its association with health and lifespan. Furthermore, we uncover intriguing connections between methylation maintenance at active enhancer regions and lifespan.

Epigenetic stochasticity and how it relates to aging and chromatin states

The density plots of the methylation beta-values across the ~ 450 K CpGs show the expected bimodal distribution with most CpGs at beta-values close to 0 (i.e., most cells are unmethylated at that CpG) or 1 (most cells are methylated at that CpG) (Fig. 1a). With aging, cells drift away from their initial methylation states [15], and this results in an increase in entropy and a shift towards a beta = 0.5 (“hemi-methylated” state). We computed the methylome-wide entropy using all CpGs that had complete data in all participants. To illustrate the “landscape erosion” towards a hemi-methylated and presumably more random state, Fig. 1a displays 5 participants with Shannon entropy > 0.85, and 5 with entropy < 0.4. In general, the bivalent chromatin states (TssBiv, BivFlnk, and EnhBiv; see Table 2 for expansions and descriptions of these standard acronyms) are characterized by low mean methylation and tend to gain methylation with aging (i.e., positive correlates of age; e.g., Fig. 1e) and plot to the top left quadrant of Fig. 1d.[42] The bivalent states are the binding sites for PRC2, and mean methylation of these regions also had the highest positive r with entropy, which indicates that the drift towards higher methylation (what we refer to as positive drift) at PRC2 bound CpGs contributes to an increase in epigenetic discordance (Table 2). In contrast, CpGs in regions characterized by high methylation generally lose methylation with aging (we refer to this as negative drift) and plot to the lower right quadrant (e.g., the actively transcribed Tx and TxWk, and the enhancer regions Enh and EnhG; e.g., Fig. 1f). These inverse correlates of age generally tended to show inverse correlations with entropy (Table 2). CpGs that flank actively transcribed genes (TxFlnk) have mean methylation beta-value close to ~ 0.5 and had the strongest negative correlation with entropy. Collectively, the results show that positive drift in hypomethylated sites (e.g., TssA, TssBiv, etc.) contribute to higher entropy, while methylation maintenance at CpGs in hemi- and hypermethylated sites (TxFlnk, ReprPCWk and Quies), which generally undergo negative drift, contribute to lower entropy.

Since the bivalent states had the highest positive correlation with age and entropy, we pooled these three chromatin regions and examined levels of stochastic epimutations (SEpiM). As these CpGs gain methylation with aging, we implemented an outlier detection approach [43, 44] that counted for positive outliers. We refer to the log10 positive outlier counts as BivSEpiM, and this measure of epigenetic stochasticity due to positive drift showed a stronger positive correlation with chronological age (Fig. 1g).

For comparison, we selected two of the first-generation clocks (Horvath and Hannum), and PhenoAge as an example of a second-generation clock, and the DunedinPACE. Additionally, we computed the PRC2-AgeIndex using low methylation CpGs with high PRC2 binding signal [13]. After regressing on age, higher age-adjusted stochasticity measured by BivSEpiM showed strong correlations with age-acceleration measured by the Horvath and Hannum clocks, and only modest correlations with PhenoAge and DunedinPACE (Fig. 1h). Age-adjusted entropy and the mean methylation of bivalent CpGs (we refer to this as BivCpGAvg) showed positive correlations only with the first-generation chronological clocks but not with PhenoAge or DunedinPACE (Fig. 1h). The age-adjusted residuals of BivCpGAvg and PRC2-AgeIndex had near perfect correlation, and this is expected as both values are based on the average methylation at CpGs that have high PRC2 binding signal. Taken together, the data indicates that the accumulation of stochasticity, particularly in bivalent sites partly contribute to the signals of age-acceleration captured by the 1st generation clocks, which were trained primarily on chronological age, but have weaker relations with the biomarkers of aging that were trained on health-related variables.

Epigenetic stochasticity and social and health variables at baseline

We treated the age-regressed residuals of the DNAm readouts (i.e., age-deviation) and the DunedinPACE as outcome variables and examined associations with baseline health and social variables. Note that the DunedinPACE is also known to have a moderate but significant positive correlation with age [4], and in AS315, age and DunedinPACE have a Pearson r of 0.08 (p = 0.0003). Therefore, we regressed out the effect of chronological age for all the readouts. For rigor, we applied multiple regression models in the AS315 that included a sensitivity analysis; we then tested for replication in BA23 and AS311. Regarding the interpretation of age-deviation, as all the DNAm readouts listed in Table 3 increase in value with aging, a positive age-deviation is interpreted as higher, and a negative interpreted as slower rate of change with aging. Model 1 was a multivariable regression with race/ethnicity, socioeconomic index (SE index, a combination of self-reported income and education), smoking status, body mass index (BMI), and total weekly recreation energy expenditure (TEXPWK; MET-hours/week) as predictors. To make the magnitudes of effects comparable, Fig. 2a displays the standardized regression estimates. For the three non-training-based readouts (i.e., entropy, BivSEpiM, and BivCpGAvg), the most consistent association was with SE index (Table 3). SE index also had significant negative association with DunedinPACE (Fig. 2a; unstandardized regression estimates in Data S1.). A unit increase in SE index resulted in − 0.004 lower entropy, − 0.02 lower BivSEpiM, − 0.001 lower BivCpGAvg, and − 0.005 slower DunedinPACE after adjustment for chronological age (Table 3). We note that the bivalent readouts as well as the DunedinPACE showed higher aging rates in all other race/ethnicity groups relative to White participants. We computed these same readouts in BA23, and in the much smaller AS311. After excluding participants who were also part of AS315, the negative associations between SE index and bivalent readouts and entropy were consistent in both datasets (Fig S2 and Data S2, S3). For example, in BA23, a unit increase in SE index resulted in − 0.005 lower entropy, − 0.009 lower BivSEpiM, -0.001 lower beta BivCpGAvg, and a − 0.009 slower DunedinPACE after adjustment for chronological age (Table 3). To illustrate the gradational effect of income on these measures of aging, we display bar plots of the non-training epigenetic readouts and the DunedinPACE (all adjusted for chronological age due to the correlation with age) by self-reported income for AS315 and BA23 (Fig. 2b, c). The distribution and variance are shown as violin and box plots in Fig S3. The potential slower aging effect of high income appears particularly pronounced in the White population, whereas, for the Black participants, most of the DNAm readouts had positive age-adjusted residual values even among the highest income group (Fig. 2b, c).

In Model 2, we included additional baseline health variables: history of hysterectomy, diabetes, CVD, hypertension, and cancer (Fig. 2d; Fig S2). As sensitivity analysis, we repeated Model 2 in AS315 in a subset of participants with DNA collected at the initial screening visit (see Methods for details) (Data S1). In Model 3, we included all the variables in Model 2, plus chronological age, alcohol intake and the healthy eating index (2010 HEI) score to account for differences in dietary intake (Data S1–S3). These models were implemented to verify that the negative association with SE index is robust. Given the similarity with BivCpGAvg, PRC2-AgeIndex confirmed a significant negative association with SE index in both AS315 and BA23 (Data S1-S2). All these showed that the non-training based DNAm readouts, and the DunedinPACE have negative associations with SE index. Entropy and the bivalent readouts were not significantly associated with baseline health variables aside from slightly higher entropy for women who had undergone hysterectomy in AS315 and BA23. In contrast, DunedinPACE had a strong association with the baseline health and lifestyle variables—smoking, higher BMI, and histories of diabetes or hypertension were associated with a higher pace of aging, while higher total weekly energy expenditure from recreational activity and higher HEI score slowed the pace of aging. In AS315, BivCpGAvg showed an unexpected significant negative regression estimate for past smokers relative to never smokers, but no difference between never and current smokers was observed; however, this was not replicated in BA23 or AS311.

Socioeconomics and mean methylation at the chromatin states

To examine if the association with SE index was specific to the polycomb and bivalent CpGs, we performed the Model 3 regression with the age-adjusted mean methylation values for the remaining 12 chromatin states in the AS315 and BA23 datasets. Next, we tested whether the measures of epigenetic stochasticity and mean methylation at chromatin states predicted life expectancy. Testing association with parental income in a young cohort

Since the bivalent chromatin states had the strongest association with SE index, we used a publicly available buccal epithelial DNAm data to examine whether BivCpGAvg and BivSEpiM will also be related to income in a young cohort. The data was a subset of the Peers and Wellness Study (PAWS) and consisted of 9–13 year-old participants (94 females, 84 males). [45] We applied a multivariable regression with participant and family characteristics as predictor variables (Fig. 3c; Table S4). After adjusting for genetic ancestry, sex had the strongest effect on BivCpGAvg (females estimated to have 0.004 higher BivCpGAvg; p = 5.6E-07). This was followed by parental income (computed as income-per-dependent; estimate = 0.002, p = 0.001) and then belonging to a minority family (minorities estimated to have 0.003 higher beta-value, p = 0.01). For BivSEpiM, income showed only modest evidence of association (p = 0.06; Table S4). The direction of association with income is opposite to that seen in the WHI (i.e., higher parental income associated with higher mean DNAm and higher stochasticity at the PRC2 bound CpGs). To test if the association with BivCpGAvg is consistent between the sexes, we repeated the analysis in males and females separately (Table S5). The positive association with income was slightly stronger in females (1 unit increase in income-per-dependent associated with 0.0025 higher mean beta-value; p = 0.02), but also consistent in males (1 unit increase in income-per-dependent associated with 0.0016 higher mean beta-value; p = 0.09).

We repeated the same regression model for the mean methylation at the chromatin states as the dependent variables. As in the WHI, ReprPC had a significant association with income after Bonferroni correction, but again, in the inverse direction with higher parental income associated with higher mean methylation (Fig. 3d; Table S6). Modest associations were also detected for TssA and TssAFlnk. When sexes were analyzed separately, the link between ReprPC and income became non-significant but the direction of effect remained the same in both males and females (Table S6).

Discussion

To summarize, we have described the large-scale methylation features among postmenopausal women and confirmed the typical pattern for CpGs in low methylation sites to gain, and CpGs in high methylation sites to lose methylation with aging. In particular, the stochasticity and mean methylation in the bivalent and PRC2 targeted CpGs were consistently associated with SE index and varied between self-reported race/ethnicity in the WHI. The lowest age-adjusted entropy and bivalent methylation levels were among the White and Hispanic women at the highest income grade, while the highest age-adjusted entropy and methylation levels were among the low-income minority groups. Since the increase in DNAm entropy and methylation averages will partly reflect the increase in cellular heterogeneity with aging, we also attempted to account for blood cell heterogeneity by including the DNAm-based estimated proportions of blood cell types, and in doing so, the link between epigenetic entropy and bivalent methylation with SE index became stronger (see Data S1–S3). However, the measures of epigenetic entropy and drift, and average methylation by chromatin states showed no significant associations with health variables. Among the chromatin states, we did find evidence that methylation maintenance at enhancer CpGs could be associated with a lower risk of death. The DunedinPACE on the other hand, was significantly associated with SE index and baseline health variables such as BMI, smoking, and energy expenditure, and was predictive of all-cause mortality.

Relating DNAm entropy to the chromatin states

Of the DNAm readouts, the most “global” measurement we computed was the methylome-wide entropy that was defined using the full set of available CpGs. This value estimates the overall randomness and is related to the phenomenon of “epigenetic drift”, the increase in stochasticity and variance in DNAm with aging [16]. Among the WHI participants, there was an increase in global entropy albeit only with a modest positive Pearson r with age. This modest linear fit is consistent with that reported in Sziraki et al. (see their supplemental Figs. 15 and 16) [19], who also used data generated on the same array platform. Additionally, the WHI is an older cohort (50–83 year-olds), and there is some evidence that the relationship between epigenetic aging and chronological age is not perfectly linear and that it begins to plateau among older individuals [9, 46]. Our goal was to assess the overall change in the epigenome rather than develop an accurate predictor of age, and for this reason, we opted for this more global measure without pre-selection of CpGs.

Segregating the CpGs by their predicted chromatin states and computing the average methylation levels was another very broad stroke approach. We applied this data reduction to determine the general patterns by which chromatin states contribute to the increase in epigenetic entropy. The strongest positive correlates of entropy were the methylation levels at the bivalent chromatin domains and regions bound by the polycomb complex (TssBiv, BivFlnk, EnhBiv, ReprPC). The mean methylation computed from the PRC2 enriched bivalent CpGs is very similar to the PRC2-AgeIndex biomarker, which is also computed from CpGs with high binding signals for the PRC2 core subunits, EZH2 and SUZ12 [13]. These sites mark repressed states with low average methylation that undergo age-dependent positive drift. The bivalent and polycomb target sites are highly conserved regulatory regions, and are crucially involved in embryonic development and in maintaining cellular identity and function [14]. Similar to the bivalent and polycomb domains, CpGs at active promoters (TssA) also typically have low methylation levels, and in the WHI, the mean methylation at TssA was also positively correlated with entropy.

Chromatin states as potential sensors of the social environment

In the WHI, the chromatin states that had significant positive correlations with entropy were all negatively associated with SE index (i.e., higher SE index related to lower methylation at the bivalent regions, the repressed polycomb ReprPC, and TssA). In contrast, the chromatin state with the strongest negative correlation with entropy was TxFlnk, which marks CpGs located at 3’ and 5’ ends of transcribed genes, and this was the only chromatin domain that showed a positive association with SE index in the WHI. Given the inverse association with the bivalent states, lower SE index was also linked with higher PRC2-AgeIndex and higher levels of stochasticity as measured by BivSEpiM. Here, stochastic epimutation simply refers to the counts of outlier CpGs and this is another way of quantifying the levels of epigenetic discordance [43, 44]. Our findings in the WHI are consistent with previous work by Fiorito et al.[43] Theirs was a multi-cohort study of mostly adult male and female participants of European ancestry that encompassed a wide age-range. They used education level as a proxy of socioeconomic position and found that methylome-wide stochastic epimutation was higher among those with lower education. [43] The work by Fiorito also found that the regions with high levels of stochasticity were enriched in CpGs targeted by PRC2 complex. Taken together, our results suggest that at least in older adults, higher socioeconomic status may help in maintenance of the overall methylome topology by potentially mitigating the accrual of methylation at low methylation sites bound by the polycomb repressor, while preventing the loss of methylation from CpGs that are in hemi- or hypermethylated states.

In our analysis of DNAm data from children (9–13 year olds) [45], among the variables we included in the model, parental income was the second most significant factor associated with BivCpGAvg. However, unlike the inverse link with SE index in the WHI, higher parental income was associated with higher mean methylation at the bivalent CpGs. Higher parental income also tended to associate with higher bivalent stochasticity. Average methylation at ReprPC and TssA also had positive associations with parental income. The polycomb targeted bivalent CpGs play dual roles in developmental programming and aging. During early development, gains in methylation at polycomb CpGs mark cellular differentiation and lineage commitment. [47] Methylation gains at polycomb CpGs also regulate timing of puberty, a process that is highly sensitive to nutritional states and environmental stressors. [48] Later in life, methylation and stochasticity at these CpGs increase with aging, a process that is also highly sensitive to stressors and socioenvironmental exposures. [13, 15] Due to this, higher methylation at polycomb domains holds different significance at different life stages. While higher methylation is an indicator of aging among adults, higher methylation in adolescence may indicate growth and developmental acquisition. Indeed, the reversal in the direction of association with socioeconomics in the WHI versus PAWS may reflect the sensitivity of the polycomb and bivalent CpGs to environmental conditions and highlights the complexity of how social variables relate to the epigenome across stages of life.

The results we present here, and the previous study by Fiorito et al., provide evidence linking the polycomb regions and the build-up of stochasticity to important social variables. In this regard, we must note that both education and income are readily recordable variables that are part of an extremely complex social construct of one's status in society and referred to as socioeconomic status [49]. SE index and parental income therefore serve only as global proxies that tag along with a multitude of unmeasured variables such as differences in access to health care and education, food and nutrition options, neighborhood and housing conditions, environmental pollutants and toxins, chronic stress, etc. These exposures—the social and environmental determinants of health—exert a strong influence on interrelated biological processes such as the stress and glucocorticoid pathway, composition of circulating immune cells and inflammatory state, metabolic health, etc. [50–52] The large-scale non-training based readouts we computed may therefore show the cumulative effects of these social and environmental factors. Based on our observations, we posit that CpGs that undergo positive epigenetic drift with aging, particularly those in the bivalent and polycomb regions, are sensitive to extrinsic cues and may serve as potential sensors of the complex social environment in humans. This is an extension of the conserved function that the polycomb genes play in sensing environmental cues such as light, nutrients and the cellular microenvironment in several different organisms [53, 54].

Relating the epigenetic features to health and lifespan

Socioeconomic factors have significant effects on health traits and life expectancy, and this has also been shown in the WHI [55, 56]. However, the measures of epigenetic entropy and methylation at the bivalent sites showed no associations with the baseline health variables such as BMI, history of diseases, smoking, and active energy expenditure, and did not predict all-cause mortality in the WHI. One possibility is that these gross readouts from the methylome that represent the cumulative and slow accrual of changes with aging simply lack the sensitivity to serve as predictors of specific health states and lifestyle conditions. Socioeconomic factors impact health via complex pathways, and while the global DNAm entropy, BivSEpiM and BivCpGAvg may reflect the socio-environmental exposures, these exposure signals may have weaker associations with the highly variable and multifactorial health outcomes and lifespan. This limited ability to predict health and disease is also true for the first-generation epigenetic clocks, which were trained on chronological age, [6] and our comparison (see Fig. 1h) showed that the bivalent readouts and entropy are more strongly correlated with the age-acceleration measured by the Hannum and Horvath clocks, and less so with the more health informative PhenoAge and DunedinPACE.

Another possibility is that the changes in the bivalent chromatin states and higher stochasticity and entropy may represent adaptive or reparative changes. [57] In other words, some of the features of epigenetic aging may help buffer against health challenges. This alternative explanation challenges the assumption that all biomarkers that track with aging are necessarily indicators of decline in health and function. Mitteldorf refers to forms of epigenetic aging that may signal an increase in repair mechanisms with aging as “Type 2” epigenetic changes [12], and such changes will have complex associations with health outcomes especially in a highly heterogeneous and varied population such as the WHI.

In contrast to the global entropy and bivalent readouts, the widely used epigenetic clocks and pace-of-aging biomarkers are derived from extensive training algorithms. For the initial training, the DunedinPACE included blood biomarkers that are related to health and correlated with aging (e.g., C-reactive protein, blood cell counts, albumin, cholesterol), waist-hip ratio, dental health, lung function, cardiorespiratory fitness, etc. Notably, the DunedinPACE was trained on the New Zealand (NZ) based Dunedin Longitudinal Cohort, which consists predominantly of European ancestry participants, with only a very small minority of participants identifying as indigenous people of New Zealand [4, 58]. These training-based clocks and biomarkers therefore likely tap into CpGs that may be more directly involved in disease pathways that are strongly influenced by the social environment. It is quite remarkable that a biomarker panel of aging developed on a NZ cohort is potentially informative of the societal structures in the United States. The DunedinPACE revealed a potential slower pace of aging among the higher income White women, but the effect of income was not as pronounced among the minority women. Except for the small group of self-identified Asians on the high-income scale, all other minority groups had positive pace of aging according to the DunedinPACE. While the training population could partly contribute to the differences between race/ethnic groups, there is growing evidence that DunedinPACE captures the effects of adversity and negative experiences that is independent of genetic ancestry [59]. Having a higher pace of aging as captured by the DunedinPACE likely has important health consequences, and in both AS315 and BA23, higher DunedinPACE predicted shorter lifespan independent of SE index. Overall, our results are in agreement with the growing evidence that DunedinPACE is a sensitive biomarker of the age-accelerating effects of social inequities and discrimination in a wide age range, and in both males and females [30, 31, 60–62].

Intriguingly, when it came to predicting survival time, the general trend was for higher methylation at active chromatin states (e.g., TssA, TssAFlnk, EnhG, and Enh) to be linked to lower risk of all-cause mortality, and this reached statistical significance for Enh. Except for TssA, which was uncorrelated with chronological age, all these chromatin states tended to have a negative correlation with aging, and this was strongest for Enh. Enhancers are important regulatory elements, and the ENCODE 15-states model divides them into three categories based on histone marks: bivalent enhancers in repressed sites (EnhBiv) that have low methylation, and enhancers in genic (EnhG) and non-genic (Enh) sites [38]. Both Enh and EnhG mark actively transcribed regions, and other studies have also shown that these have higher methylation and lose methylation with aging. [63–65] Our results suggest that being able to maintain higher methylation levels at these enhancer CpGs may extend lifespan. To our knowledge, this is the first time the global average methylation of Enh CpGs has been associated with all-cause mortality in humans. There is however evidence from mouse studies that interventions such as caloric restriction, which extends lifespan, suppress the age associated methylation loss at enhancer CpGs. [63] Furthermore, in humans, aberrant methylation at enhancer CpGs have been implicated in cancer progression and metastasis [66]. The work by Cole et al. [66] also showed that higher methylation at the enhancer CpG of the metastasis gene KIT increased survival time among cancer patients. This direction of association is therefore in agreement with our observation.

Limitations

A limitation of the present work is that we only examined time to all-cause mortality but did not perform a more detailed analysis to test whether these DNAm readouts predict time to adjudicated diseases or disease-related deaths. Given the links between enhancer methylation and cancer progression and mortality, this will be particularly relevant in follow-up studies. We also acknowledge that the results we present are mostly based on older postmenopausal women (age 50–79 at baseline), and the significant associations between the bivalent and polycomb sites and SE index may represent the effects of lifelong exposures to social inequities and weathering. In the smaller sub-cohort of young children from the PAWS study, the bivalent CpGs and ReprPC were also associated with parental income but indicated a much more complex picture as higher income was related to higher stochasticity and methylation. The results also suggested that the link with income is more prominent in females than in males. However, a major limitation is the very small sample size of PAWS, especially when subsetted by sex. Additionally, the participants were 9–13 year-olds and this is a time of significant hormonal changes and we did not have data on important variables such menarche and BMI. Further work is needed to examine the polycomb targeted CpGs as potential sensors of the social environment and the nature of changes across the age spectrum, and how these may relate to health and aging. The link between enhancers and lifespan also needs further replication. To facilitate such follow-up studies, we have provided all the computational codes, and these can be easily computed from existing datasets and can be adapted to the newer DNAm microarray datasets.

Conclusions

We have introduced alternative approaches to quantify aspects of epigenetic aging that are based on entropy, stochasticity, and simple methylation averages at defined chromatin states. These large-scale methylation features relate not only to chronological age but also to socioenvironmental variables and potentially to lifespan. Our findings indicate that the broader social context and experiences are reflected in methylation levels and in the buildup of epigenetic stochasticity at polycomb-regulated DNA sequences. Taken together, the results suggest that methylation changes at the polycomb domains, which are key regulators of developmental timing and maturation, also serve as a molecular interface through which the social milieu and individual experiences become embedded in biological aging.

Methods

WHI DNA methylation datasets and sample inclusion

The WHI is a multicenter long-term prospective study of postmenopausal women that began in 1993. [20–22] Women between the ages 50–79 were recruited in 1993–1998. Ancillary studies have generated genomic data, and the present work uses the genome-wide DNA methylation dataset by the three ancillary studies: (1) WHI-EMPC/AS315, [28, 35] (2) BA23 [23, 36], and (3) AS311. These 3 datasets have been previously analyzed jointly as part of large meta-studies, and detailed descriptions can be found in these publications. [3, 67–69] All three datasets measured blood DNA methylation using the Illumina Infinium Methylation 450 K BeadChip (Illumina Inc.).

The EMPC study (Epigenetic Mechanisms of PM-mediated CVD Risk; AS315) randomly selected 2200 participants from within the Clinical Trial (CT) arm and measured DNA methylation from blood collected at time of eligibility screening visit (SV) for the majority, or at annual visit 3 or 6, and with repeated measurements for a subset of the samples [35]. We used only the first sampling for the present work, and after excluding eight samples with low methylation calls, the primary analyses were done for 2192 AS315 participants measured either at SV (64%), annual visit 3 (29%), or annual visit 6 (7%) in the CT arm. The statistical analysis for AS315 was repeated in the 1396 SV samples after excluding the annual visit samples (Fig S1). BA23 was designed as a case/control study of risk for future CHD. We received epigenetic data for 2107 BA23 participants collected at SV prior to randomization to a study arm in the clinical trials (CT) or enrollment in the observational study (OS); after excluding samples that overlap with WHI-AS315, we performed the analyses in 1989 participants. AS311 is a matched case–control study of bladder cancer, and like BA23, used blood DNA collected at SV and prior to disease diagnosis. We received 882 array data; after excluding an array with too many missing values (~ 262,970 CpGs had NA values) and samples that overlap with AS315, we performed analyses in 868 participants.

Computing entropy, chromatin annotations, and estimates of biological aging

All analyses were done using the processed data that were provided in NetCDF format by the WHI. For uniformity, we used the beta-mixture quantile (BMIQ) normalized data, which applied the same data quality control and processing steps implemented by WHI-EMPC [35]. The R package “ncdf4” was used to interface with the data matrices [70]. Before any downstream analyses, we first counted how many CpGs had missing values per sample, and excluded one sample from AS311, and eight samples from BA23 as those had unusually high missingness.

For methylome-wide discretized entropy calculation, we used the same method described in a prior study. [18] The optimal number of bins was estimated to be 50 using the Freedman-Diaconis rule [71]. The R codes used for each step are in supplemental file Data S4. We then used the R package “entropy” [72, 73] to compute the discretized entropy value for each participant and this was scaled to a number between 0–1. Example density plots for the beta-values were generated in R with ggplot2 [74] for ten participants of AS315; these were all non-Hispanic Whites and five had low entropy, and five had high entropy.

To annotate for chromatin states, we downloaded the archived REMCChromHMM file from the Zhou github page [41, 75]. This contains the 15-states chromHMM consensus chromatin annotations for each of the ~ 480,000 CpGs in the 450 K platform [38]. These annotations were matched by CpG probe IDs to the WHI BMIQ data matrices, and average methylation levels for each chromatin state was computed for each participant. As an alternate measure of randomness in the epigenetic data, we counted CpGs that were outliers in the three bivalent states to compute epigenetic stochasticity: bivalent/poised transcription start site (TssBiv), flanking bivalent TSS (BivFlnk), and bivalent enhancer (EnhBiv). While the entropy measures the shift in the genome-wide methylome landscape for each participant and is not relative to the study cohort, the outlier counts are estimates of how many CpGs are outliers for an individual relative to the study cohort. We adapted the outlier detection method described in Gentilini but counted only the positive outliers as these sites gain methylation with aging and a higher beta-value is presumed to represent a more aged methylome, i.e., beta-values over 3-times the interquartile range (IQR) of the 3rd quartile (75% or Q3) [43, 44]. The codes used to compute the bivalent stochastic epimutations (BivSEpiM) are in Data S4. In brief, we retained only CpGs located within the bivalent states, and for each CpG, computed the value = Q3 + 3*IQR. The CpG beta-value matrix was then converted to a 0 (when beta-value is ≤ Q3 + 3*IQR) or 1 (when beta-value is > Q3 + 3*IQR), and the positive outlier CpGs were counted for each participant, and the counts then log10 transformed. For PRC2-AgeIndex, we used the ENCODE H1 human ChIP-Seq data experiments ENCSR000ASY for EZH2, and ENCSR000ATS for SUZ12, and referred to the python codes provided by Moqri et al. and translated into R codes [13]. The detailed R scripts and data sources are described in Data S4.We selected CpG with methylation beta ≤ 0.3, and with high PRC2 signal and computed the average DNAm across these CpGs for each participant.

We used a few training-based clocks as reference. For the Horvath and Hannum, we used the pre-computed clocks that were shared by the WHI. Specifically, we used the linear transformed Horvath and Hannum DNAm age predictions [1, 2]. We computed the PhenoAge (aka, Levine2018) and the DunedinPACE using the dnaMethyAge R package [3, 4, 76, 77].

Statistical analyses

Pearson correlations were used for bivariate comparisons of chronological age with the DNAm readouts. As most of the DNAm readouts, and the DunedinPACE also show varying levels of correlation with chronological age, we used the residuals to make the values independent of chronological age. We used the linear regression lm() function in R to test associations between the age-residuals of the DNAm readouts and baseline variables. These baseline variables included BMI computed from weight and height at baseline (kg/m2), medical history at baseline, and personal habits including smoking status (0 = never smoked; 1 = past smoker; and 2 = present smoker), and recreational energy expenditure computed as measured by the TEXPWK variable (MET-hours/week). TEXPWK was computed from questionnaire-based reports of recreational physical activity that included walking, mild, moderate and strenuous physical activity in kcal/week/kg [78]. Among the demographic variables, WHI collected self-reported education and income at time of screening and are presented on an ascending scale from 1–11 for education, and 1–8 for income (higher numbers represent higher education or income). We derived a single variable, SE index as the average of the two after excluding unreported or missing data.

We applied a simple model, Model 1: lm(yi ~ race/ethnicity + SE index + Smoking + BMI + TEXPWK), where yi is each of the DNAm readouts fitted independently. We limited the regression analyses to participants who identified as non-Hispanic White (White), non-Hispanic Black (Black), Hispanic, and Asian or Pacific Islander (Asian/PI) in AS315, and White, Black and Hispanic for BA23 and AS311. An expanded version of Model 1 (Model 1b) included blood cell proportions (lymphocytes, monocytes, granulocytes) that were indirectly estimated from the DNAm data. Model 2 was the same as Model 1 and included additional medical history terms collected at baseline: hysterectomy, diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), hypertension, and cancer ever. The DNAm data for BA23 and AS311 were from baseline SV, and majority of the AS315 blood DNA was from the SV prior to assignment of a study arm (n = 1396). Model 2 was repeated for AS315 after excluding the 633 blood DNA that were sampled at visit year 3, and 163 at visit year 4. Model 3 included all the variables in Model 2, plus chronological age, the alcohol intake variable, and 2010 HEI total score [79, 80]. The lm() R codes and details on the variables are provided in Data S4. For methylome-wide entropy, BivCpGAvg and BivSEpiM, we applied a nominal statistical threshold of p ≤ 0.05 to declare significance, followed by different regression models and sensitivity analyses in AS315 to examine consistency, and then tested for replicability of effects in BA23 and AS311. For the mean methylation at the 12 other chromatin states, we applied a Bonferroni corrected alpha ≤ 0.05 (p = 0.004) in AS315 and then tested for replicability in BA23.

We performed Cox regressions to test predictive value of the DNAm readouts using the 2022 versions of All Discovered Deaths, and Adjudicated Outcomes. This was done using the survival R package [81, 82]. For all-cause mortality, we used the variable DEATHALL (all discovered deaths including from National Death Index) in conjunction with ENDFOLLOWALLDY (days enrollment to end of follow up including uncensored death). Covariates for Cox regression were baseline variables age, smoking status, BMI, diabetes, cancer, SE index, hormone therapy arm, dietary modification arm, alcohol intake, 2010 HEI total score, and with race/ethnicity in the strata() term. The same Cox model was repeated in the three race/ethnicity groups separately. The coxph() R codes are provided in Data S4.

Testing association in a younger cohort

The full set of DNAm beta-values and metadata were downloaded from NCBI’s Gene Expression Omnibus dataset GSE94734 [45]. This data was also generated on the same 450 K BeadChip, and we computed BivCpGAvg, BivSEpiM, and mean methylation at the chromatin states using the R codes in Data S4. We then used these DNAm readouts as outcome variables, and parental income as the primary predictor, and adjusted for age and gender of participant, reported minority status, adversity score, parental education and genetic ancestry as cofactors. The regression models are provided under Tables S4 and S5.
