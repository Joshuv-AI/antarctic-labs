Abstract
This paper aims to argue that, under typical developmental conditions, syntactic productivity is grounded in embodied
cognition, emerging primarily through the vocalization of human praxis within a causally structured world. The core
claim is that the mapping from action structure to clausal structure follows a specific pattern: causal graph motifs in
motor planning map onto predicate-argument templates in perisylvian networks via learned alignment. This mapping is
operationalized by specifying how motor planning circuits scaffold combinatorial encoding in LIFG and LSTG. Nonaction semantics, including complementation, quantification, and abstract concepts, emerge through metaphorical
extension and conventionalization of action-based templates. While alternative pathways exist, such as statistical
learning from text, embodied praxis represents the typical ontogenetic pathway. The paper generates falsifiable
developmental predictions, addresses cross-linguistic typology and clinical dissociations, and outlines implications for
cognitive psychiatry. This perspective grounds intelligibility in the synchronization between body, action, memory, and
objective causality.
Keywords: Cognitive Science; Linguistics; Psychomotor Performance; Language Development; Mirror Neurons; Sign
Language; Causality.

1. Introduction
In his influential work Kant and the Foundations of Analytic Philosophy, Robert Hanna identifies a convergence
between Humboldt, Chomsky, and Kant. All three recognize that human minds are fundamentally productive rather than merely
receptive. From finite means, the mind generates infinite outputs. This productive capacity represents a decisive break with
behaviorism and empiricism.
This convergence, however, carries a significant risk. By identifying a common thread, one risks obscuring crucial
distinctions between phenomena that operate at fundamentally different ontological and neurophysiological levels. The
productivity of generative grammar, the productivity of Kantian categories, and the productivity of spatiotemporal forms each
possess distinct origins and functions. Conflating them under a single rubric of internal productivity obscures rather than
illuminates their nature.
This paper aims to argue that, under typical developmental conditions, syntactic productivity is grounded in embodied
cognition, emerging primarily through the vocalization of human praxis within a causally structured world. Rather than proposing
that all syntactic competence derives exclusively from motor praxis, we treat this as the predominant, though not exclusive,
ontogenetic pathway, since alternative developmental routes to syntactic competence may exist. From this perspective, the syntax
of language mirrors the syntax of action because both instantiate a common underlying organizational structure. Accordingly,
cognition is conceptualized as being grounded in the dynamic integration of the body, action, memory, and objective causality.

2. Methodology
A qualitative study (Risemberg et al., 2026a; Pereira et al., 2018) was conducted, taking the form of descriptive and
reflective research (Risemberg et al., 2026b).
This is a theoretical and integrative article; no empirical data were collected. The present framework was developed
through a non-systematic, targeted review and synthesis of primary and secondary sources spanning four bodies of literature: (1)
classical and contemporary philosophy of mind, particularly Kant's transcendental categories and Robert Hanna's comparative
analysis of Kant, Humboldt, and Chomsky; (2) generative and usage-based linguistic theory, including debates between nativist
and constructivist accounts of syntactic acquisition; (3) developmental and cognitive neuroscience literature on motor planning,
mirror neuron systems, hierarchical predictive coding, and the neurobiology of sign language; and (4) embodied cognition
research, including conceptual metaphor theory, consensus perspectives on situated and embodied language acquisition (Reggin
et al., 2023), and critiques of strong embodied cognition.

Sources were selected for theoretical relevance rather than through a systematic or exhaustive search protocol, consistent
with the conventions of hypothesis-generating and theoretical-synthesis articles in cognitive science and philosophy of mind.
Priority was given to foundational texts defining the theoretical positions under discussion (Chomsky, Humboldt, Hanna, Lakoff
and Johnson) and to recent empirical and review literature (2021-2025) capable of constraining or informing the proposed model.
The theoretical construction proceeded in three steps. First, the productivity arguments advanced by Hanna, Chomsky,
and Humboldt were examined to identify points of convergence and the risk of conflating ontologically distinct forms of
productivity. Second, a schematic correspondence was proposed between the hierarchical organization of goal-directed motor
action and the combinatorial structure of clauses, informed by neuroscientific evidence on motor planning, perisylvian language
networks, and predictive coding. Third, this correspondence was used to derive a set of falsifiable developmental,
neurostimulation, and lesion-based predictions, formulated with heuristic effect-size benchmarks to facilitate future empirical
testing. Clinical dissociations (aphasia-apraxia), cross-linguistic typology, and critiques of embodied cognition were then
examined as boundary conditions against which the model's explanatory scope and limitations were assessed.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1 Productivity in Humboldt and Chomsky
A central insight shared by Humboldt and Chomsky is that children routinely generate linguistic expressions they have
never previously encountered. Explaining this generative capacity remains a fundamental challenge for theories of language
acquisition (Hauser et al., 2002). Usage-based and constructivist frameworks, most prominently advanced by Tomasello and
Goldberg, provide a compelling alternative to strong nativist accounts. Rather than positing an innate, domain-specific
grammatical architecture, these approaches propose that children gradually construct grammatical competence through intentionreading, statistical learning (Saffran, Aslin, & Newport, 1996), and pattern abstraction, progressing from concrete, item-based
constructions to increasingly productive linguistic representations.
A simple illustration comes from verb morphology. After exposure to a limited set of past-tense forms, such as I left, I
ran, and I felt, children readily produce novel inflected forms that they have never explicitly encountered. This productive
capacity reflects the ability to extract abstract regularities from finite linguistic input and generalize them to new lexical items
and contexts. Within the present framework, this capacity need not be attributed to an autonomous grammatical mechanism.
Instead, it may reflect the brain's ability to identify, stabilize, and flexibly recombine structured regularities embedded in both
linguistic experience and goal-directed human action through domain-general processes of statistical learning and distributional
inference.
Large language models present an important counterpoint. LLMs acquire sophisticated syntax from text-only training,
without the developmental scaffolding provided by praxis. This does not falsify the claim that embodied praxis is the typical
ontogenetic pathway. Rather, it suggests that syntax, once abstracted and conventionalized in a language community, can be
learned through purely statistical means. LLMs represent an alternative, non-typical pathway to syntactic competence.
3.2 Operationalizing the Action-to-Syntax Mapping
The central proposal of this framework is that language acquisition relies on a systematic mapping between the
hierarchical organization of goal-directed action and the combinatorial structure of clauses. Specifically, recurrent causal motifs
in motor planning are hypothesized to provide the scaffolding from which predicate-argument structures emerge during
development (Glenberg & Kaschak, 2002). At a schematic level, this relationship can be represented as follows:
Motor planning: Agent → Action → Patient → Instrument → Goal → Result
Clausal structure: Subject → Predicate → Object → Adjunct → Complement → Modifier

This correspondence should not be interpreted as a one-to-one translation between motor and linguistic representations.
Rather, it reflects a developmentally acquired alignment between action schemas and grammatical templates. Through repeated
experience, hierarchical representations generated by motor planning systems become progressively associated with the
combinatorial structures encoded by the language network. Within this framework, the left inferior frontal gyrus contributes
primarily to the representation of hierarchical structure, whereas the left superior temporal gyrus supports the semantic content
integrated within these structures.
The proposed neural mechanism can be formalized in terms of hierarchical predictive representations (Friston, 2010;
Pezzulo, Parr, & Friston, 2022). Premotor and supplementary motor cortices generate prospective action schemas organized
according to nested causal relations, in which distal goals are recursively decomposed into subgoals and ultimately into
elementary motor acts, each associated with predicted sensory consequences (Kim, Chua, & Hu, 2025; Tsay et al., 2024).
Linguistic composition exhibits a comparable hierarchical organization, with clauses decomposing into phrases, phrases into
words, and words into morphemes. Across development, repeated co-occurrence of action and language enables the brain to
learn stable correspondences between these two hierarchical domains through statistical learning. These mappings are proposed
to emerge from the coordinated activity of motor and perisylvian networks, with cerebellar learning mechanisms contributing to
the calibration and refinement of the underlying distributed representations (Pulvermüller, 2005; Rizzolatti & Craighero, 2004;
Carriere, Tomasello, & Pulvermüller, 2025).
This framework yields a set of empirically testable predictions. First, transient disruption of motor planning circuits, for
example through transcranial magnetic stimulation targeting the supplementary motor area, should selectively impair the
processing of syntactically complex sentences describing novel goal-directed actions, while exerting comparatively weaker
effects on the comprehension of abstract, non-action-related sentences. Second, developmental variation in fine motor control
should predict subsequent variation in syntactic complexity, even after controlling for vocabulary size and general cognitive
ability. Third, focal lesions involving motor planning networks should be associated with selective impairments in hierarchical
syntactic operations, such as recursive embedding and the processing of nested dependencies, while relatively preserving other
aspects of linguistic competence, including lexical-semantic knowledge and basic sentence comprehension.
3.3 Addressing Non-Action Semantics and Abstract Concepts
A central challenge for any praxiological account of language concerns the emergence of abstract semantics. If syntax
is grounded in the hierarchical organization of action, how do concepts such as belief, obligation, possibility, number, and time
develop beyond the domain of overt motor behavior?
The present framework proposes that abstract semantic systems emerge through the progressive metaphorical extension
and conventionalization of action-based representational structures. As argued by Lakoff and Johnson, abstract concepts are
systematically structured by embodied experience through conceptual metaphor. Domains such as time, causation, knowledge,
and mental states are routinely understood in terms of more concrete sensorimotor experiences, allowing action-derived
representational schemas to be generalized well beyond their original domain (Garello, Ferroni, Gallese, Ardizzi, & Cuccio,
2024).
Within this framework, complementation and subordination arise from the recursive organization of action planning
itself. Goal-directed behavior is inherently hierarchical: distal goals are achieved through nested sequences of subgoals, each of
which may require its own internally structured action plan. Linguistic embedding reflects this organizational principle by
allowing clauses to be recursively incorporated within other clauses. During development, children repeatedly experience
hierarchical action organization before mastering analogous syntactic structures, enabling language to externalize preexisting
patterns of nested causal organization.

Quantification may similarly originate from the iterative structure of action. Goal-directed behaviors such as reaching,
grasping, and object manipulation consist of repeated sensorimotor cycles through which children acquire stable representations
of numerosity and cardinality. Number words subsequently provide symbolic labels for distinctions that are first established
through recurrent interactions with discrete objects and events.
Function words can likewise be interpreted as linguistic expressions of relational structures initially experienced through
embodied interaction. Prepositions encode spatial relations that emerge from navigation and object manipulation; articles
grammaticalize distinctions in reference, familiarity, and identifiability established during repeated interactions with objects; and
auxiliary verbs encode aspectual, temporal, and modal properties of action, including distinctions between completed and
ongoing events, as well as between realized, possible, and necessary actions.
Importantly, this framework does not claim that all semantic knowledge is reducible to motor representations. Rather,
it proposes that motor planning provides the initial compositional architecture upon which language is constructed. Once this
hierarchical combinatorial system has emerged, semantic representations can progressively expand beyond the motor domain
through conceptual metaphor, abstraction, cultural transmission, and linguistic conventionalization, ultimately supporting the
full range of human conceptual thought.
3.4 Addressing Cross-Linguistic Typology
A comprehensive theory of syntax must account for the remarkable diversity of grammatical systems observed across
languages. At first glance, a praxiological account based on action structure might appear difficult to reconcile with typological
phenomena such as ergative alignment, extensive applicative systems, noun incorporation, polysynthesis, or relatively free word
order.
The present framework accommodates this diversity by treating grammatical constructions as language-specific
conventionalizations that selectively profile different aspects of a common underlying action schema. Cross-linguistic variation
therefore reflects differences in how languages encode and highlight components of goal-directed events rather than differences
in the fundamental organization of praxis itself. From this perspective, ergative-absolutive systems foreground the affected
participant and the causal organization of events, whereas nominative-accusative systems privilege the initiating agent. Likewise,
noun incorporation and polysynthetic morphology represent highly integrated linguistic encodings of actions and their
participants, paralleling the unified representation of objects, goals, and motor sequences characteristic of skilled action planning.
Variability in constituent order can be understood as alternative strategies for organizing information structure and discourse,
while preserving the same underlying relational architecture among event participants.
Signed languages provide particularly compelling evidence for the proposed action-language relationship. Although
they rely on the visual-manual rather than the auditory-vocal modality, natural signed languages exhibit the full range of
hierarchical and recursive syntactic organization found in spoken languages. Neuroimaging and lesion studies further
demonstrate that sign language processing recruits largely the same left-lateralized perisylvian network that supports spoken
language, despite the use of different sensory and motor effectors (Emmorey, 2021). These findings suggest that syntactic
organization is not intrinsically linked to speech production, but instead depends on domain-general mechanisms for the
hierarchical organization of motor representations. Within the present framework, signed languages therefore constitute strong
evidence that syntax emerges from the structured organization of praxis rather than from vocalization itself.
3.5 Three Qualitatively Distinct Levels of Productivity
Although Hanna's framework interprets productive cognition as manifestations of a single underlying principle, the
present account argues that greater explanatory precision is achieved by distinguishing three qualitatively different levels of
organization.

Level 1: Generative Grammar. This level concerns the linguistic expression of thought through speech or sign. Its
representational foundation lies in the externalization of hierarchically organized praxiological structures, enabling the
productive generation of an effectively unbounded number of utterances from a finite set of lexical and combinatorial resources.
Its neurobiological implementation is hypothesized to depend primarily on distributed interactions among perisylvian language
regions, motor planning systems, sensory cortices appropriate to the linguistic modality, mirror neuron networks, and
phonological or visuospatial working memory.
Level 2: Kantian Categories. This level concerns the formal organization of cognition itself. Rather than expressing action, it
involves the mind's capacity to represent, relate, and evaluate its own cognitive operations. Functions such as unification,
differentiation, causal attribution, and inferential integration belong to this level. Its neural basis is expected to involve prefrontal
executive systems, metacognitive networks, and large-scale association networks that integrate information across multiple
cognitive domains.
Level 3: Spatiotemporal Forms of Experience. This level corresponds to the embodied structure of primary sensibility. Its
foundation lies in proprioceptive, vestibular, interoceptive, and sensorimotor experience, together with the temporal continuity
provided by memory. These systems organize the body's initial engagement with the physical world, supplying the experiential
framework within which both action and cognition develop. Candidate neural substrates include somatosensory cortices,
cerebellar circuits, hippocampal memory systems, and distributed sensorimotor networks.
These three levels should not be regarded as different expressions of a single computational mechanism. Rather, they
represent distinct but interacting forms of organization. Generative grammar externalizes the hierarchical structure of praxis into
language; the Kantian categories provide the formal principles through which cognition organizes and evaluates experience; and
the spatiotemporal forms constitute the embodied conditions that make both action and cognition possible. Together, they define
complementary dimensions of human cognition without collapsing into a single explanatory level.
3.6 Clinical Dissociations and Motor Independence
A critical challenge for any praxiological account of language is to explain the well-established clinical dissociation
between aphasia and apraxia (Whiteside, Dyson, Cowell, & Varley, 2015). Neuropsychological evidence has documented double
dissociations between disorders of language and disorders of motor planning, indicating that although these capacities share
important developmental and evolutionary relationships, they are supported by partially distinct neural systems in the mature
brain.
The present framework accommodates these findings through a developmental scaffolding hypothesis. During language
acquisition, motor planning systems are proposed to provide the representational substrate from which hierarchical linguistic
structures emerge. With increasing experience, however, these representations become progressively consolidated within
specialized perisylvian language networks, particularly those involving the left inferior frontal and left superior temporal cortices.
As a result, damage to these regions can produce aphasia while relatively sparing domain-general motor planning, whereas
lesions affecting premotor and motor programming systems may impair praxis without substantially disrupting the abstract
syntactic representations encoded within the language network.
Evidence from neurostimulation and lesion studies further supports this distinction. Disruption of motor cortical regions
often produces measurable effects on the processing of action-related semantics, yet exerts comparatively limited influence on
core combinatorial operations or hierarchical syntax. Such findings are consistent with the view that mature linguistic
representations retain functional links to motor systems while achieving a substantial degree of computational autonomy.

This interpretation also addresses prominent critiques of strong embodied cognition, particularly those advanced by
Mahon and Caramazza. Their work has demonstrated that motor activation during language processing is neither universal nor
sufficient to explain linguistic competence, and that experimental task demands can substantially influence estimates of motor
involvement. The developmental scaffolding hypothesis is fully compatible with these observations. It does not propose that
motor representations remain constitutive of all linguistic processing throughout life. Rather, it argues that motor planning plays
a privileged role during the emergence of syntactic competence, after which increasingly specialized language networks support
linguistic processing with progressively less dependence on the systems that initially scaffolded their development. This
developmental transition provides a principled explanation for why motor involvement is often most evident during language
acquisition, novel learning, and the processing of unfamiliar action-related material, while being less consistently required for
fluent, automatized language use in adulthood.

3.7 Developmental Predictions and Measurement Strategies
The vocalized praxis framework generates a series of empirically testable predictions that can be evaluated using
developmental, neurostimulation, and longitudinal methodologies.
Prediction 1: Fine Motor Milestones and Syntactic Emergence. Developmental trajectories of fine motor control should
systematically predict the emergence of increasingly complex syntactic constructions. Specifically, mastery of multi-step object
manipulation and sequential motor planning is expected to precede, and significantly correlate with, the acquisition of
hierarchical syntactic structures, including relative clauses, complement clauses, and ditransitive constructions, even after
controlling for overall cognitive development. This hypothesis can be tested by relating standardized measures of fine motor
ability, such as the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, to indices of syntactic complexity, including mean length
of utterance, clausal subordination, and syntactic diversity. A moderate-to-large association is predicted (e.g., r > 0.40).
Prediction 2: TMS Disruption of Motor Planning. Transient disruption of motor planning networks, for example through
transcranial magnetic stimulation targeting the supplementary motor area or dorsal premotor cortex, should selectively impair
the processing of syntactically complex sentences describing novel goal-directed actions. In contrast, comprehension of
canonical sentence structures and abstract, non-action-related sentences should be comparatively preserved. This prediction
follows from the hypothesis that action-based representations remain particularly relevant when linguistic processing requires
the construction of unfamiliar hierarchical event structures. The expected effect would be moderate for action-related syntactic
processing (approximately d > 0.60) and minimal for abstract sentence comprehension (d < 0.20).
Prediction 3: Motor Delays and Language Outcomes. Individual differences in early motor development should prospectively
predict later syntactic competence, even after accounting for vocabulary size, nonverbal intelligence, socioeconomic status, and
language exposure. Longitudinal cohort studies should therefore reveal that early motor planning abilities explain unique
variance in subsequent syntactic development beyond established cognitive and environmental predictors. A moderate
prospective association is anticipated (e.g., r > 0.35).
Importantly, these quantitative effect sizes should be regarded as heuristic estimates rather than definitive predictions.
Their primary purpose is to provide benchmarks that facilitate prospective hypothesis testing and power calculations. The central
claim of the present framework is therefore not the precise magnitude of these associations, but the existence of selective and
reproducible relationships between the development of hierarchical motor planning and the emergence of hierarchical syntactic
competence.

4. Implications for Cognitive Psychiatry and Intervention
If syntactic competence is developmentally scaffolded by praxis, language disorders should not be conceptualized solely
as impairments of isolated linguistic mechanisms. Rather, their assessment should also consider the integrity of the motor,
praxiological, and sensorimotor systems that may contribute to the emergence of linguistic structure. Under this framework,
children with primary deficits in motor planning or skilled action may be at increased risk for associated impairments in syntactic
development, even when broader cognitive abilities are relatively preserved.
This perspective also has important implications for intervention. If hierarchical action provides developmental support
for the acquisition of syntax, therapies that systematically engage goal-directed action, object manipulation, and participation in
causally structured activities may enhance language development when integrated with conventional speech and language
interventions. The present framework therefore predicts that randomized controlled trials comparing combined praxiologicallinguistic interventions with language-focused therapy alone could provide a direct empirical test of the developmental
scaffolding hypothesis.
The framework further suggests that motor-related measures may offer valuable developmental biomarkers.
Quantitative analyses of speech motor control and vocal production, together with objective assessments of fine motor
performance and motor planning, may provide complementary indicators of the maturation of embodied language systems. A
lifespan perspective is equally important, as the influence of praxiological scaffolding is expected to vary across developmental
stages, with the strongest effects occurring during periods of heightened neuroplasticity and early language acquisition.
More broadly, this framework offers cognitive psychiatry an integrative theoretical model that bridges developmental
neuroscience, neuropsychiatry, embodied cognition, and situated linguistics. Rather than viewing language as the product of an
encapsulated cognitive module, it conceptualizes linguistic competence as the outcome of progressively specialized neural
systems that emerge through the coordinated development of body, action, memory, and the causal structure of experience.

5. Limitations and Open Questions
Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the account does not fully explain how abstract, non-action
semantics emerge from praxis. While metaphorical extension is proposed as a mechanism, this requires further development and
empirical support. Second, the predictions are specific to typical development; alternative pathways, such as statistical learning
from text as in LLMs, clearly exist. Third, the neural mechanisms linking motor planning to linguistic composition require more
detailed specification and empirical validation. Fourth, important aspects of language remain unaddressed, including pragmatics,
discourse structure, and language change. These represent important areas for future work.

6. Conclusion
Hanna's synthesis of Kant, Humboldt, and Chomsky correctly emphasizes the active, constructive nature of human
cognition, yet it also risks obscuring important distinctions among the forms of productivity they each sought to explain. The
present framework argues that, in typical human development, syntactic productivity emerges through the motor expression of
hierarchically organized praxis grounded in embodied interaction with a causally structured world. On this view, language does
not originate from an autonomous grammatical faculty alone, but from the progressive mapping of action representations onto
linguistic structures during development. This reinterpretation situates human intelligibility within the coordinated dynamics of
body, action, memory, and objective causality, thereby integrating insights from classical philosophy with contemporary
developmental neuroscience, embodied cognition, and cognitive psychiatry.

Figure 3. Internal hierarchical organization of intelligent action from sensorimotor experience to structured language.

This figure illustrates the proposed developmental sequence through which intelligent action progressively acquires
symbolic complexity. Reflex analogies strengthen interaction patterns that are stabilized through temporal gradient memory and
organized into internal action schemas. Mirror neuron mechanisms contribute to motor simulation and social understanding,
supporting the emergence of speech syntax based on praxiological representations (Agent-Verb-Object structure). Finally,
structured language arises as a stable symbolic system exhibiting generative productivity from finite cognitive structures. The
model integrates concepts from developmental psychology, cognitive neuroscience, embodied cognition, and generative
linguistics into a unified explanatory framework.

This figure contrasts Hanna's concept of homogeneous internal productivity with the present theoretical proposal, which
distinguishes three qualitatively different but functionally interconnected levels of cognitive organization: (1) Generative
Grammar, responsible for linguistic productivity grounded in embodied praxis; (2) Kantian Categories, representing higher-order
conceptual organization and reflective understanding; and (3) Space-Time, corresponding to embodied sensorimotor experience
and neurophysiological integration. These levels are coordinated through continuous body-action-memory synchronization
interacting with the objective causal structure of the world. The central contribution of the model is the reinterpretation of
cognition as an embodied, dynamic, and hierarchically organized process rather than a single homogeneous computational
system.
