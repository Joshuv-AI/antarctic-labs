Microscale bioturbation as a first order control on distal prodelta to offshore mudrock reservoirs
Authors: Korhan Ayranci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMud-dominated reservoirs exhibit significant heterogeneity driven by depositional processes and post-depositional modification, yet the role of micro-scale bioturbation remains poorly constrained. This study focuses on the impact of diminutive trace fossils (Multina and Phycosiphon) within event beds of the Lower Silurian Qusaiba Member (Qalibah Formation, Saudi Arabia) using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), petrography, and QEMSCAN analyses. In the studied rocks, these diminutive trace fossils form dense, shallow-tier networks restricted to the upper portions of thin event beds and constitutes at least 1% of the sediment volume. Micro-CT imaging reveals complex three-dimensional burrow architectures that disrupt primary sedimentary fabrics and generate pronounced micro-scale heterogeneity. Mineralogical analyses indicate systematic differences between burrow fills and host sediment, including redistribution of clay minerals and localized enrichment of diagenetic phases such as pyrite. Consequently, the trace fossil producers modified sediment composition through selective feeding and induced early diagenetic processes. The findings demonstrate that micro-scale bioturbation is a fundamental component of mudrock fabric, potentially exerting a first-order control on porosity, permeability, and geomechanical properties. Introduction

Mud-dominated unconventional reservoirs display vertical and horizontal reservoir heterogeneities due to a wide range of parameters such as organic content, lithology and bioturbation contrast1–3. One of the most common ways of introducing such heterogeneity is the presence of frequent event beds (e.g., turbidites and hyperpycnites). Event deposition in muddy successions influences (1) sedimentation rate causing dilution of total organic matter content, (2) oxygen content at the sediment–water interface, and (3) geochemical composition of the mudrocks. Although, these factors can affect the reservoir characterization, very little is known about the bioturbation characteristics of such event beds4 and their possible effects in micro-scale heterogeneity.

Event beds are common in relatively deep-water settings contributing to increased sedimentation rates5–8 and they have been recorded in various unconventional reservoirs9–11. It is challenging to recognize their presence in cores, outcrops, and well logs due to relatively subtle grain size variation within these deposits, indiscernible sedimentary structures like normal grading, and limited contrasts in petrophysical properties relative to the surrounding background sedimentation. These events can reach far distances from the source or river mouth5,12, and they display various internal characteristics depending on the proximity to the source. In an event bed deposited close to the source, some sedimentary structures such as normal and inverse graded beds can be used to identify their presence, but in deep-water domains, distal to source recognition is challenging8,13,14. Bioturbation may further alter the primary physical sedimentary structures after the deposition of event beds5,7,15,16.

Bottom water chemistry can also be greatly influenced by these event beds. They can introduce short-lived or long-lived oxygen and salinity fluctuations, and benthic food availability8,10,17,18. Among these, variation in oxygen content and elevated organic matter availability during or right after the events can attract r-selective organisms which normally do not colonize in these settings under stressed conditions (e.g., anoxia)8,19. Such behavior, also known as opportunist behavior, might result in intense and shallow-tier, monospecific bioturbation (e.g., Phycosiphon)8,11,20–22.

Bioturbation exerts significant influence on reservoir porosity, permeability, mineralogy and mechanical behavior depending on the burrow size, burrow infill, burrow lining, etc.4,23–27. While these affects are relatively well documented in conventional reservoirs, probably due to presence of relatively large trace fossils, their role remains poorly understood in unconventional systems, particularly where bioturbation may subtly modify pore architecture, influence hydraulic flow pathways, alter mineralogy and mechanical behavior28,29.

It should be noted that most marine sedimentary deposits, such as deltaic and shoreface settings, display some degree of bioturbation, unless they exert extreme stress conditions on bioturbators, and they are generally represented by various trace fossil assemblages30–32. Given that different trace fossils can be represented by various characteristics, such as different infill lithology and lining, it may be difficult to analyze their combined effects on sedimentary deposits. However, deposits with a low-diverse ichnofauna allow to have a better understanding of the effects of individual trace fossils on certain deposits or certain stratigraphic surfaces24,33. This, in the future, may allow to construct a generalized model to test various deposits (e.g., siltstone vs carbonates) and effects of various trace fossils (e.g., sand-filled vs mud-walled).

The main aim of this study is to assess the impact of Multina and Phycosiphon, common trace fossils in deep-water settings22,34–36 and unconventional reservoirs, and to characterize the mineralogical composition of the burrow fill and surrounding matrix using micro-CT imaging and geochemical analyses. The occurrence of shallow-tier opportunistic Multina and Phycosiphon within event beds interpreted as hyperconcentrated-flow deposits of the world-renowned Qusaiba Member of the Qalibah Formation is investigated herein.

Geological setting

The Qusaiba Member was deposited during the early Silurian (middle to late Llandoverian; 443–433 Ma) as a major post-glacial deposit37,38. It is predominantly represented by mudstones with rare sandstone alternation close to the top of the member39 (Fig. 1). It has been documented to be deposited in an epicontinental, passive continental margin, developed after the Late Ordovician glaciation38,40,41. The Qusaiba Member is widely regarded as one of the most prospective unconventional gas reservoirs in the Middle East as it represents the main source rock for most of the Paleozoic units, particularly around the Arabian Peninsula38,42,43. It is age equivalent to several other shale basins in the Middle East, including, the Mudawwara Formation (Jordan), Sahmah Formation (Oman), Abba Formation (Iraq), Dadas Formation (Turkey), Ghakum Formation (Iran), and Akkas Formation (Iraq)40,44,45. The strong influence of ongoing deglacial eustatic sea level rise lead to widespread black shale deposition resulting in organic matter preservation. This member has been documented to represent relatively calm depositional environments and conditions including offshore to delta toe with oxygenated to anoxic conditions38–40,44.

The Qusaiba Member is conformably overlained by the sandstone-dominated Sharawra Member which has been interpreted to represent prodelta deposits44. Turbidity current and storm deposits are well documented in the Sharawra Member and, given that the boundary is gradational, it is expected to observe similar event deposits in the upper section of the Qusaiba Member.

Comparable event beds have been documented from several mud-dominated successions worldwide, including the Horn River Group (Canada)9, Vaca Muerta Formation (Argentina)7,46, Barnett Shale (USA)47, and Bazhenov Formation (Russia)48 suggesting that episodic event bed deposition is a common process in fine-grained unconventional reservoir systems.

Results

The Qusaiba Member in the Qassim Region is predominantly composed of thick successions of massive to finely laminated mudstones (Figs. 1, 2). Toward the upper part of the member, these mudstones locally incorporate lenses and thin layers of very fine-grained (silty and sandy) sediments that exhibit weak current-generated sedimentary structures, including low-amplitude current ripples (Fig. 3). Bioturbation intensity throughout the succession is generally low (0–30%) and is characterized by low-diversity ichnoassemblages dominated by horizontal trace fossils, including Planolites, Gordia, Phycosiphon, Multina as well as mantle and swirl structures (i.e., navitextura49).

The present study focuses on a subset of light-grey mudstone beds exhibiting massive fabrics to weakly developed grading, including both normal and inverse grading. Individual beds are thin, ranging from approximately 1 to 5 cm in thickness (Fig. 2), and are laterally continuous at the outcrop scale. Bioturbation within these beds remains sparse (1–30% bioturbated). The ichnofabric is predominated by assemblages of diminutive Phycosiphon, and Multina with rare occurrences of navitextura (Fig. 1B, C). Due to the small dimensions and variable preservation, definitive distinction between Phycosiphon and Multina is difficult. Therefore, the term “Phycosiphon-like trace fossils” is adopted throughout the manuscript.

Trace fossils are preferentially expressed on weathered surfaces, where Phycosiphon-like trace fossils occur as dark-grey tube infill, that contrasts sharply with the lighter-colored mudstone matrix. Individual trace fossils are predominantly horizontal to oblique in orientation and display hook-shaped morphologies (Fig. 2). The trace-fossil assemblage is interpreted as shallow-tier and is restricted to the uppermost 1–2 cm of individual beds. No discernible halos or spreiten structures were observed in hand specimens (Fig. 2).

Petrographical analysis provide additional insight into the internal architecture of the Phycosiphon-like trace fossils and their relationship with the surrounding matrix. Thin-section observations from both analyzed samples indicate that the Phycosiphon-like trace fossils and host matrix is composed predominantly of clay-sized material, with only rare and sparsely distributed silt-sized grains (Figs. 4, 5). The mudstone matrix is characterized by a dispersed distribution of iron-rich opaque minerals, which are locally abundant and impart a heterogeneous appearance to the background fabric. In contrast, the infill of Phycosiphon-like trace fossils is generally depleted in iron-rich opaque minerals relative to the surrounding matrix, resulting in a subtle but consistent petrographic distinction between trace fossils and host sediment (Figs. 4, 5). Individual trace fossils are narrow, with typical widths of less than 1 mm, whereas their lengths vary considerably, locally extending to several millimeters. This variability reflects the complex morphology of Phycosiphon-like trace fossils and their oblique to horizontal orientation within the sediment. Several Phycosiphon-like trace fossils specimens exhibit well-developed spreite structures in thin sections, expressed as darker color laminated zones enriched in iron-bearing minerals (e.g., green arrows in Figs. 4C, D, 5D). Outside of these spreite zones, iron-rich minerals are generally absent or only weakly developed within the burrow fill, reinforcing the compositional contrast between the trace fossil and the surrounding matrix. Occasionally, clumped aggregates or isolated grains of pyrite were observed within Phycosiphon-like trace fossils (e.g., Fig. 5B, C).

Five subzones within the thin sections were selected for detailed mineralogical mapping and quantitative analysis in order to evaluate the influence of Phycosiphon-like trace fossils on mineral distribution and bulk geochemical composition (A1, A2, B1, B2, and B3; Figs. 4, 5). These subzones were chosen to include both trace fossil interiors and adjacent host matrix, allowing direct comparison between bioturbated and non-bioturbated sediment. Quantitative mineralogical analyses across all subzones indicate that the clay mineral assemblage is dominated by chlorite and kaolinite, with average abundances of approximately 40% and 32%, respectively, followed by subordinate illite averaging around 10% (Table 1; Fig. 6). Other minerals collectively account for less than 10% of the total mineralogical composition in each subsample (Table 1). Overall, the five analyzed subzones display broadly comparable bulk mineralogical proportions, with only minor inter-sample variability.

Despite the relatively uniform bulk mineralogy among subzones, mineralogical mapping reveals a pronounced compositional contrast between Phycosiphon-like trace fossil infills and the surrounding mudstone matrix (Fig. 7). In all examined subzones, Phycosiphon-like trace fossils are characterized by a marked depletion in chlorite relative to the host sediment, accompanied by enrichment in kaolinite and illite (Fig. 7). This systematic mineralogical differentiation suggests that Phycosiphon-like trace fossils exert a significant control on the redistribution and/or alteration of clay minerals at the microscale. In addition to clay mineral variability, several Phycosiphon-like trace fossil specimens exhibit preferential concentrations of pyrite, occurring either as scattered individual grains or small clusters within the burrow fills (e.g., subzones B1 and B3; Fig. 7). Collectively, these results demonstrate that, although bulk mineralogical compositions remain relatively consistent at the thin-section scale, Phycosiphon-like trace fossils significantly modifies mineral distributions at the microscale. This highlights the important role of bioturbation in influencing sediment geochemistry and early diagenetic processes in fine-grained depositional systems.

3D visualization of burrows provides vital insights into burrow morphology, dimensions, internal structures, volume and burrow network. Micro-CT scanning results of the studied sample display complex burrow network architecture of the Phycosiphon-like trace fossils and distinct density differences between the burrow networks and the matrix (Fig. 8). The scanned sample displays three distinct layers: bottom layer, top layer, and event bed interval (Fig. 8). Both top and bottom layers are massive mudrock units with no bioturbational structures (Fig. 8) while the event bed layer clearly shows predominantly Phycosiphon-like trace fossils (Fig. 8). Micro-CT imaging reveals the trace fossils as distinct light-colored structures that contrast sharply with the surrounding darker matrix. These burrows display oblique, straight to sinusoidal burrow morphologies (Supplementary Video S1). Some of the burrows display well-preserved spreiten (green arrow; Fig. 9) which is difficult to identify in hand samples and thin sections (Figs. 4, 5). In order to calculate the volume of Phycosiphon-like trace fossils, the event bed interval was isolated from the bottom and top layer and analyzed separately. The event bed interval is approximately 15.92 cm3 and the isolated Phycosiphon-like trace fossils volume is approximately 0.10 cm3. Therefore, the Phycosiphon-like trace fossils volume is about 1% of the event bed interval (Fig. 10 and Table 2). It should be noted that the segmentation procedure was performed conservatively to minimize the inclusion of matrix material within the segmented trace-fossil volume. However, accurate discrimination between trace-fossil infill and the surrounding matrix is inherently challenging because both phases exhibit similar attenuation characteristics and only limited grayscale contrast in the micro-CT dataset. Segmentation was therefore based on grayscale thresholding, with trace fossils primarily identified within the range of 42,000–65,440, whereas the surrounding matrix generally exhibited lower grayscale values. Despite careful threshold selection and manual refinement, some trace-fossil structures were likely excluded from the final segmentation due to overlap in grayscale values between the burrow infill and matrix (Fig. 10C). Lowering the threshold value (e.g., 41,500) results in the inclusion of additional more complete trace-fossils in certain parts of the sample; however, it also leads to the incorporation of matrix material in other areas where grayscale values overlap (Fig. 10D). This trade-off highlights the difficulty of applying a single threshold to a low-contrast dataset and introduces uncertainty into volumetric calculations. Manual segmentation tests indicate that the total trace-fossil volume is likely greater than that obtained through threshold-based segmentation alone (Fig. 10E), suggesting that the reported volumetric estimates are conservative. However, applying detailed manual segmentation throughout the entire micro-CT dataset would be extremely time-consuming and impractical given the large number of slices. Consequently, the reported volumetric estimate is the minimum bioturbation volume, and should be regarded as conservative. Future studies employing machine-learning-based segmentation approaches may improve phase discrimination and provide more accurate quantification of trace-fossil volumes in low-contrast mudstone successions.

Discussion

Multiple lines of sedimentological and ichnological evidence support the interpretation of the studied intervals as event beds, potentially deposited by turbidity currents and/or hyperpycnal flows. First, the overall bioturbation intensity within the Qusaiba Member is consistently very low and sporadic, indicating that background depositional conditions were generally unfavorable for sustained benthic colonization. The event bed intervals also show low bioturbation intensity (1–30% bioturbated). The principal environmental stressors in distal prodelta to offshore depositional settings that commonly result in sparse bioturbation include reduced oxygen availability, high and episodic sedimentation rates, variations in substrate consistency, limited benthic food supply, and salinity fluctuations5,7,50,51. The Qusaiba Member has been widely interpreted as representing distal delta-front deposits38,44 and is characterized by grey color sediments with relatively low total organic carbon (TOC) contents52. The low TOC values and grey color sediments53 suggest that bottom-water oxygenation was likely sufficient to promote efficient degradation of organic matter, thereby arguing against persistent dysoxic to anoxic conditions as the primary control on reduced bioturbation intensity. In addition, the inferred distal delta-front setting implies periodic organic matter delivery by fluvial input suggesting that benthic food limitation was also unlikely to be the dominant stress factor. Consequently, oxygen depletion and organic matter scarcity are considered as secondary controls on the observed sparse bioturbation.

Instead, sedimentological and ichnological evidence points toward high sedimentation rates and frequent event-driven deposition as the primary controls on the sparse bioturbation observed. In the upper Qusaiba Member, the presence of transported and fragmented shell material, lensoidal sandstone beds, and navitextura (typical of soft-to-soupy sediments49) indicate episodic depositional events and pronounced variations in substrate consistency. These features are consistent with rapid deposition from sediment-laden flows, potentially including hyperpycnal flows, leading to the formation of fluid-rich deposits and soft- to soupy-ground conditions54–56. The ichnofabric further supports this interpretation: the dominance of shallow-tier Phycosiphon-like trace fossils reflects an opportunistic colonization strategy, whereby trace makers rapidly exploited newly deposited event beds, likely in response to short-lived increases in organic matter availability following depositional events8,21. Such opportunistic, low-diversity ichnoassemblages are characteristic of mud-dominated, event-influenced environments subjected to repeated physical disturbance and unstable substrates. Comparable sedimentological features and bioturbation patterns have been documented from similar mud-prone successions elsewhere (Fig. 2E, F)9,11.

The detailed QEMSCAN results indicate that intervals characterized by Phycosiphon-like trace fossils are associated with geochemical modifications compared to non-bioturbated or weakly bioturbated matrix (Fig. 7). This demonstrates that Phycosiphon-like trace fossils are not simply an ichnological overprint on primary sedimentary structures, but an active factor on geochemical character of the mudrocks possibly during early diagenesis. The close association between Phycosiphon-like trace fossils and changes in mineralogical signatures suggests that sediment processing by the trace maker plays a role in controlling geochemical heterogeneity at the centimeter to millimeter scale. The QEMSCAN-derived mineral abundances presented herein should be regarded as semi-quantitative estimates obtained through automated mineralogical mapping. These data are primarily intended to highlight relative mineralogical differences between trace-fossil fills and the surrounding matrix rather than to provide precise absolute mineral concentrations. Consequently, the reported values should be interpreted within the context of the analytical resolution and inherent uncertainties associated with automated mineral identification in fine-grained mudstone samples.

Both Multina and Phycosiphon has been reported to be produced by deposit-feeding organisms that selectively consume fine-grained, organic-rich sediment and backfills the burrow with processed material34,50,57,58. This selective feeding behavior results in redistribution of clay minerals, organic matter, and associated elements within the sediment (in the studied case, the top 1–2 cm). Consequently, burrow fills may display geochemical compositions that differ from the surrounding matrix, reflecting biological fractionation rather than changes in sediment supply or water-column chemistry59. Further diagenetic studies can reveal the alteration of clay minerals between each other (e.g., kaolinite and illite). It should be noted that such diagenetic influence, may alter petrophysical characteristics of the mudrocks. For example, kaolinite and illite have been documented to affect the porosity in shale reservoirs60–62. Therefore, Phycosiphon-like trace fossils may have direct impact (positive or negative) on reservoir characterization, similar to other relatively larger trace fossils24,25,63. The localized enrichment of pyrite within these trace fossils is interpreted to reflect early diagenetic mineral precipitation facilitated by organic matter accumulation and reduced redox conditions within the burrow microenvironment.

Recent advances in rock and sediment imaging techniques have significantly improved our understanding of the behavior and morphology of burrowing organisms64. However, most of these approaches have focused on macro-scale trace fossils, whereas investigations of micro-scale trace fossils remain comparatively limited25,65,66. Micro-CT scanning offers a powerful, non-destructive means to resolve small-scale biogenic structures that are not detectable using conventional medical CT systems, particularly in fine-grained sedimentary rocks. This capability is especially relevant in mud-dominated depositional environments, where grazing represents the dominant feeding strategy, resulting in a prevalence of horizontally oriented trace fossils50. While such horizontal traces are readily identifiable on bedding-plane surfaces, their recognition in core or outcrop cross-sections is often challenging, particularly when dealing with diminutive structures. Given that trace fossils can control or influence rock characteristics (e.g., porosity, permeability and mechanical properties) in conventional reservoirs, they theoretically have the possibility to play a vital role in unconventional reservoir characterization. The results presented here are consistent with these previous studies in demonstrating that trace fossils can form highly interconnected three-dimensional networks65 that significantly modify sediment fabric.

Micro-CT scan analysis of the Qusaiba Member event beds reveals that trace fossils, predominantly Multina and Phycosiphon, can constitute at least 1% of the sediment volume (Fig. 10). Although this volumetric proportion appears relatively small, it likely represents a minimum estimate because segmentation was intentionally performed conservatively to minimize the inclusion of matrix material, and the actual trace-fossil volume may therefore be higher. Nevertheless, even relatively low trace-fossil abundances may be significant in unconventional reservoir systems, where fluid storage and flow are strongly controlled by heterogeneities at the millimeter- to micron-scale. Consequently, trace-fossil volumes of only a few percent may exert a disproportionate influence on reservoir quality and performance by modifying pore connectivity and permeability pathways28.

The Phycosiphon-like trace fossils appear brighter in the micro-CT scans, indicating a higher X-ray attenuation relative to the surrounding matrix. Although this observation may initially suggest that the infill is denser than the matrix, the mineralogical data indicate otherwise. The matrix is predominantly composed of chlorite, whereas the Phycosiphon-like trace fossil infills are dominated by kaolinite and illite. Given that chlorite generally has a density comparable to or slightly higher than that of kaolinite and illite67, the enhanced contrast observed in the micro-CT images is unlikely to result solely from differences in mineral density. Instead, the brighter appearance of the trace fossils is more plausibly attributed to the densely packed nature of the infilling material, which increases bulk density and X-ray attenuation relative to the surrounding matrix. This interpretation suggests that the micro-CT contrast primarily reflects variations in sediment packing rather than intrinsic differences in mineral composition.

The dense presence of Phycosiphon-like trace fossils generate a highly interconnected network of reworked sediment, locally disrupting primary sedimentary structures and introducing pronounced micro-scale heterogeneity. At this scale, biogenic structures can either enhance or reduce effective porosity and permeability, contingent on the extent of sediment mixing, compaction, and subsequent diagenetic modification. In fine-grained, organic-rich successions such as the Qusaiba Member, this heterogeneity is likely to exert significant control over fluid storage, flow pathways, and geomechanical behavior. Accordingly, the presence of abundant diminutive trace fossils, detectable exclusively through high-resolution micro-CT imaging, has important implications for unconventional reservoir characterization, where subtle variations in sedimentary fabric may impose first-order controls on reservoir quality and stimulation efficiency.

Implications

The trace fossils and micro-scale sedimentary fabrics documented in this study are generally below the resolution of conventional wireline logs and therefore cannot be directly identified in most subsurface datasets. Furthermore, the subtle grain-size variations and sedimentary structures characteristic of the studied event beds are unlikely to produce diagnostic log responses. Nevertheless, the results have important implications for reservoir characterization. The recognition that event beds may host concentrated biogenic reworking and localized mineralogical heterogeneity provides a process-based framework for interpreting fine-scale variability within the Qusaiba Member. Although individual event beds cannot be mapped using conventional logs, intervals interpreted as event beds from core calibration may be incorporated into geological and reservoir models as discrete facies characterized by distinct sedimentary fabrics, bioturbation intensities, and mineralogical compositions. Such facies may influence porosity distribution, pore connectivity, geomechanical properties, and hydrocarbon storage capacity, thereby contributing to reservoir heterogeneity at scales relevant to unconventional resource development.

Conclusions

Event beds in mud-dominated distal prodelta and offshore successions, such as the Qusaiba Member, play an important role in generating sedimentary heterogeneity through rapid deposition, substrate disruption, and opportunistic colonization by trace-making organisms. Micro-scale bioturbation, exemplified by shallow-tier Multina and Phycosiphon, constitutes a significant volumetric fraction of these event beds, forming complex burrow networks that locally modify primary sedimentary structures and create pronounced micro-scale heterogeneity. Petrographic and geochemical analyses demonstrate that Multina and Phycosiphon actively redistributes clay minerals, influencing early diagenetic processes and potentially altering porosity, permeability, and mechanical behavior. High-resolution micro-CT imaging reveals the detailed morphology and volumetric significance of these diminutive trace fossils, highlighting their importance as first-order controls on reservoir quality. Collectively, these results underscore that micro-scale bioturbation is not a minor textural feature but a fundamental component of distal prodelta to offshore mudrocks, with critical implications for characterization and modeling of unconventional reservoirs.

Materials and methods

The test samples in this study includes thin beds of muddy units including Phycosiphon-rich intervals at their top (Fig. 2). The samples were collected from the upper most section of the Qusaiba Member outcrops of the Qalibah Formation located in the Qassim Region of central Saudi Arabia (Fig. 1). The outcrop is approximately 35 m, it is a well-exposed succession, and it covers the mud-prone Qusaiba Member at the bottom and the sand-rich Sharawra Member on top. The beds displaying Phycosiphon are located between 7 and 10 m, with bed thickness range from 3 to 6 cm (Figs. 2, 3). Two samples were selected for thin section and geochemical analysis while one sample was selected for micro-CT scanning analysis. The studied rock samples and thin sections specimens are curated in the Geosciences Department, College of Petroleum Engineering and Geosciences, King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals (KFUPM), Dhahran, Saudi Arabia. The analyzed materials are catalogued under sample identifiers OC1Phy26001.

Micro-CT analysis

A representative sample measuring 5.5 × 3.5 × 3 cm was analyzed using X-ray micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) with an Xradia 620 Versa system in King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals. The scan was conducted at an accelerating voltage of 160 kV and a power setting of 25 W. A manufacturer-specified HE6 filter was applied during acquisition to optimize beam quality and image contrast. The total scan duration was 4 h, 16 min, and 23 s. The reconstructed dataset was obtained with a voxel resolution of 29.3 μm, corresponding to a slice spacing of 29.3934 μm, enabling detailed three-dimensional visualization of the sample’s internal structure. The reconstructed micro-CT images were then processed using Dragonfly software to segment and differentiate trace fossils from the surrounding matrix. This workflow enabled the isolation of ichnological features and allowed quantitative analysis of their three-dimensional volumetric distribution within the sample. Trace fossils were segmented from the surrounding matrix using grayscale thresholding in Dragonfly. The reconstructed dataset exhibited grayscale values ranging from 0 to 65,440.

QEMSCAN analysis

A total of 2 thin sections were examined to quantify surface mineralogy using Quantitative Evaluation of Minerals by SCANning electron microscopy (QEMSCAN), operating at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and a beam current of 10 nA (± 0.05 nA). Field image scans covered an area of 0.5 cm2 with a spatial resolution defined by a point spacing of 5 μm. Further methodological details on QEMSCAN analysis can be found in previous studies68,69.
