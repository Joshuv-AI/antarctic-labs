Solving Convex-Concave Problems with 𝒪 ~ ( ϵ − 4 / ( 3 p + 1 ) ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-4/(3p+1)}) p p th-Order Oracle Complexity *
Lesi Chen, Xinliang Zhang, Chengchang Liu, Junru Li, Luo Luo, Jingzhao Zhang

Abstract
When the objective has Lipschitz continuous p p th-order derivatives, it is known that convex-concave minimax problems can be solved with 𝒪 ( ϵ − 2 / ( p + 1 ) ) \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2/(p+1)}) p p th-order oracle calls. This complexity upper bound was speculated to be optimal as it is achieved by a natural generalization of the optimal first-order method. In this work, we show an improved upper bound of 𝒪 ~ ( ϵ − 4 / ( 3 p + 1 ) ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-4/(3p+1)}) by applying the Monteiro-Svaiter acceleration. We also establish a lower complexity bound of Ω ( ϵ − 2 / ( 3 p − 1 ) ) \Omega(\epsilon^{-2/(3p-1)}) , suggesting a gap still exists for p ≥ 2 p\geq 2 .

Introduction

We study smooth convex-concave minimax optimization problems over convex compact sets
{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}
:

\min_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),

(1)

where the objective
f
has
L_{p}
-Lipschitz continuous
p
th-order derivatives.

This problem naturally arises from many applications, including finding a Nash equilibrium of a two-player zero-sum game
[
53
,
33
,
24
]
, solving the Lagrangian function in constrained optimization
[
45
,
50
]
, and many machine learning problems such as adversarial training
[
56
]
, AUC maximization
[
55
]
, and distributionally robust optimization
[
9
]
.

A fundamental result for Problem (
1
) is the minimax theorem, which was first proved for bilinear problems by
53
, and extended to general convex-concave functions by
51
. The minimax theorem guarantees the existence of a saddle point
({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*})
such that

f({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*})=\min_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\min_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}).

Given the existence of the saddle point, a natural question is:
“What is the complexity of finding such a point?” We study this question under the oracle model
[
35
]
. This model restricts algorithms to access oracles that contain local information about the objectives, where the
p
th-order oracle returns the tuple
(f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),\nabla f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),\cdots,\nabla^{p}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
that gives the function value and derivatives up to order
p
at the query point
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
.
The order
p
naturally gives a division of different algorithm classes.
For example, first-order methods purely use gradient information; second-order methods (also known as Newton-type methods) jointly use gradient and Hessian information; and tensor methods
(p\geq 2)
leverage higher-order derivatives information.
A central problem in optimization theory research is to accurately characterize the gains provided by higher-order oracles
[
16
,
41
,
42
]
.

The complexity of first-order methods for solving minimax optimization is well understood.
In his seminal work,

25
proposed the celebrated extragradient (EG) method and proved it can find an
\epsilon
-solution to Problem (
1
) in
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\nicefrac{{L_{1}}}{{\epsilon}}\right)
iterations, where each iteration calls two first-order oracles.

46
proposed the optimistic gradient (OG) method which has the same convergence guarantee but only requires a single first-order call every iteration.
34
,
37
further proposed methods for more general spaces known as mirrox-prox (MP) and dual extrapolation (DE), respectively.
34
also proved a lower bound to show that the

\Omega(\nicefrac{{L_{1}}}{{\epsilon}})
complexity is the limit of all first-order algorithms, and recent works
[
57
,
45
]
further refined the diameter dependency in the lower bound.

Compared with the theory of first-order methods, we have less knowledge of higher-order minimax optimization.
In their seminal work,
31
proposed the
second-order generalization of EG, which can provably find an
\epsilon
-solution with
{\mathcal{O}}((\nicefrac{{L_{2}}}{{\epsilon}})^{2/3})
second-order calls.
Recently,

6
generalized this method to non-Euclidean setup and to
p
th order, achieving the
{\mathcal{O}}((\nicefrac{{L_{p}}}{{\epsilon}})^{2/(p+1)})
upper complexity bound of
p
th-order oracle calls.

18
,
1
simplified the proof of the
p
th-order EG by viewing the
p
th-order tensor step as an oracle.

30
provided alternative analyses from the
control-theoretic perspective.
Besides the high-order EG algorithm, numerous other algorithms have also been proposed, including high-order OG
[
23
,
22
]
, high-order DE
[
29
]
, and high-order reduced-gradient methods
[
43
]
.
All of these methods have the same
{\mathcal{O}}((\nicefrac{{L_{p}}}{{\epsilon}})^{2/(p+1)})
upper complexity bound of
p
th-order oracle calls, which is speculated to be optimal in
[
1
,
29
]
.

The main contribution of this paper is to break the commonly conjectured

\Omega((\nicefrac{{L_{p}}}{{\epsilon}})^{2/(p+1)})
barrier by proving a better upper complexity bound of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}((\nicefrac{{L_{p}}}{{\epsilon}})^{4/(3p+1)})
for objectives with
L_{p}
-Lipschitz continuous
p
th-order derivatives. Our new result is achieved by applying the high-order momentum acceleration technique
[
32
]
to both variables
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
, and reducing solving the original Problem (
1
) to solving
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left((\nicefrac{{\gamma}}{{\epsilon}})^{4/(3p+1)}\right)
proximal minimax subproblems

\min_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})+\frac{\gamma}{p+1}\|{\bm{x}}-\bar{\bm{x}}\|^{p+1}-\frac{\gamma}{p+1}\|{\bm{y}}-\bar{\bm{y}}\|^{p+1},

where
(\bar{\bm{x}},\bar{\bm{y}})
is the proximal center. If we take
\gamma=L_{p}
, the above subproblem has a constant condition number for
p
th-order methods. Then an
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left((\nicefrac{{L_{p}}}{{\epsilon}})^{4/(3p+1)}\right)
upper complexity bound can easily be achieved by applying any existing convergent
p
th-order method to solve the above subproblems.

Finally, we complement our upper bound by proving a lower complexity bound showing that any
p
th-order algorithm can not find an
\epsilon
-solution in less than
\Omega((\nicefrac{{L_{p}}}{{\epsilon}})^{2/(3p-1)})
oracle calls. Our lower bound is the first non-trivial lower bound for high-order minimax optimization under general conditions:
Existing lower bounds either require the diameter to scale with
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\epsilon}}

[
1
,
29
]
, or is subsumed by the
\Omega((\nicefrac{{1}}{{\epsilon}})^{2/(3p+1)})
bound for standard minimization problems
[
3
]
(see the discussion in Section
5.1
).
Our lower bound matches the upper bound when
p=1
, but a gap still exists when
p\geq 2
.
We hope our results can be an initial step for future works to further fill the gap.

Additional Related Works

Apart from the aforementioned related works in minimax optimization, we also review closely relevant studies on high-order convex optimization. In a seminal work,
36
proposed the Cubic Regularized Newton (CRN) method with the
{\mathcal{O}}((\nicefrac{{L_{2}}}{{\epsilon}})^{1/2})
second-order oracle complexity, which initiated a series of subsequent works analyzing the complexity of second- and higher-order algorithms.

38
proposed the accelerated CRN (ACRN) method that achieves the improved

{\mathcal{O}}((\nicefrac{{L_{2}}}{{\epsilon}})^{1/3})
complexity.

32
introduced a novel and intricate acceleration framework, under which they proposed a method with
{\mathcal{O}}((\nicefrac{{L_{2}}}{{\epsilon}})^{2/7}\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\epsilon}}))
second-order oracle complexity.

40

gave a second-order implementation of third-order tensor step and
generalized the ACRN method to
p
th order, achieving the
{\mathcal{O}}((\nicefrac{{L_{p}}}{{\epsilon}})^{1/(p+1)})
upper bound.
Later, three independent groups
[
19
,
4
,
17
]
generalized the Monteiro-Svaiter accelerated method to
p
th order and achieved the
{\mathcal{O}}((\nicefrac{{L_{p}}}{{\epsilon}})^{2/(3p+1)}\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\epsilon}}))
upper bound, which matches the
\Omega((\nicefrac{{L_{p}}}{{\epsilon}})^{2/(3p+1)})
lower bound established by
3
up to only logarithmic factors. Recently,
8
,
26
independently proposed different novel enhancements to remove the
\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\epsilon}})
factor caused by the line search subroutine in the Monteiro-Svaiter acceleration, achieving the optimal
{\mathcal{O}}((\nicefrac{{L_{p}}}{{\epsilon}})^{2/(3p+1)})
oracle complexity.

10
analyzed both the upper and lower bounds for the limiting behavior of the
p
th-order Monteiro-Svaiter acceleration when
p\rightarrow\infty
, which can be used to improve the complexity of many related optimization problems
[
11
,
13
,
9
]
.

Notations

We use
\|\,\cdot\,\|
to denote the Euclidean norm for vectors and the spectral norm for matrices. We hide logarithmic factors in the notation
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\,\cdot\,)
.
For a set
{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we let
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{Z}}({\bm{z}})=\left\{{\bm{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\langle{\bm{v}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}-{\bm{z}}\rangle\leq 0,\forall{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}}\right\}
be the normal cone of
{\mathcal{Z}}
at point
{\bm{z}}
. We also let
{\rm Proj}_{{\mathcal{Z}}}(\bar{\bm{z}})=\arg\min_{{\bm{z}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|{\bm{z}}-\bar{\bm{z}}\|
be the projection operator of
{\bm{z}}
onto the set
{\mathcal{Z}}
.
To simplify notations, we let
{\bm{z}}=({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
and define the operator on
{\bm{z}}
as

{\bm{F}}=(\nabla_{x}f,-\nabla_{y}f)^{\top}
. Let
{\bm{T}}
and
{\bm{M}}
be two order
k
tensors, their inner product is defined as
\langle{\bm{T}},{\bm{M}}\rangle=\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}}{\bm{T}}_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}}{\bm{M}}_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}}
.
Notation
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product. For a
k
order tensor
{\bm{T}}
, we define its spectral norm as

\|{\bm{T}}\|=\sup_{{\bm{v}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{v}}_{k}}\left\{\langle{\bm{T}},{\bm{v}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{v}}_{k}\rangle:\|{\bm{v}}_{i}\|\leq 1,1\leq i\leq k\right\}.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we describe the formal problem setup. We begin with assumptions made in our analyses.
First, we assume that the domain is bounded and that the function is convex-concave.

Assumption 2.1

(Bounded domain)
.

We assume that both the sets
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are convex and compact with diameters
D_{\mathcal{X}}:=\sup_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\|<+\infty
and
D_{\mathcal{Y}}:=\sup_{{\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\|<+\infty
.
We also define
{\mathcal{Z}}={\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
and
D_{\mathcal{Z}}:=\sup_{{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\|
.

Assumption 2.2

(Convex-concave functions)
.

We assume that
f(\,\cdot\,{\bm{y}})
is convex for any fixed
{\bm{y}}
and
f({\bm{x}},\,\cdot\,)
is concave for any fixed
{\bm{x}}
.

Recall that
{\bm{z}}=({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
and that
{\bm{F}}=(\nabla_{x}f,-\nabla_{y}f)^{\top}
. The convex-concavity assumption is also equivalent to assuming the monotonicity of the operator
{\bm{F}}
:

\langle{\bm{F}}({\bm{z}})-{\bm{F}}({\bm{z}}^{\prime}),{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}}.

Third, we assume that the function has the following smoothness properties. The first one is standard for analyses of first-order algorithms. The last one provides a guarantee for higher-order methods.

Assumption 2.3

(Gradient Lipschitzness)
.

We suppose the gradient of
f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
is
L_{1}
-Lipschitz continuous for some
L_{1}>0
:

\|\nabla f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-\nabla f({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\|\leq L_{1}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|,\quad\forall{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}},~{\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}.

Assumption 2.4

(
p
th-order Derivative Lipschitzness)
.

We suppose
\nabla^{p}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
is
L_{p}
-Lipschitz continuous for some
L_{p}>0
:

\|\nabla^{p}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-\nabla^{p}f({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\|\leq L_{p}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|,\quad\forall{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}},~{\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}.

We notice that previous
p
th-order EG method
[
31
,
6
]
only require Assumption
2.2
and
2.4
, but unnecessarily require Assumption
2.1
and
2.3
. In the following, we discuss that our two additional assumptions only incur mild complexity cost.

Remark 2.1

(Discussion on Assumption
2.1
)
.

Although the
p
th-order EG also works under the unconstrained setting, the convergence rates will depend on
\|{\bm{z}}_{0}-{\bm{z}}^{*}\|
. When we know
\bar{D}_{\mathcal{Z}}
is an upper bound for
\|{\bm{z}}_{0}-{\bm{z}}^{*}\|
, we can simply run our algorithm on the constraint set
\{{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\|{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}_{0}\|\leq\bar{D}_{\mathcal{Z}}\}
, where
d=d_{x}+d_{y}
. When we underestimate
\bar{D}_{\mathcal{Z}}
, we can run the algorithm with
\bar{D}_{\mathcal{Z}}
doubled each time until we find an
\epsilon
-solution, which only causes an additional
\log(\|{\bm{z}}_{0}-{\bm{z}}^{*}\|/\bar{D}_{\mathcal{Z}})
factor in the total complexity.

Remark 2.2

(Discussion on Assumption
2.3
)
.

Although the additional Assumption
2.3
makes our algorithm slightly more restricted than the accelerated algorithms such as
p
th-order EG, the upper complexity bound of our algorithm will only have a mild logarithmic dependency on
L_{1}
. Moreover, Assumption
2.3
can be derived from Assumption
2.4
when the domains
{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}}
are compact (Assumption
2.1
).

2.1
High-Order Oracle

We then introduce the oracle for measuring complexity. Following
35
, we restrict the scope of this paper to algorithms that use the
p
th-order oracles defined below.

Definition 2.1

(
p
th-order oracle)
.

Given a query point
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
, the
p
th-order oracle returns the following derivative information

\nabla^{(0,\cdots,p)}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}):=(f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),\nabla f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),\cdots,\nabla^{p}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})).

To leverage this
p
th-order oracle, a common way is to take a tensor step

[
16
,
6
]
that is formally defined as follows.
In this work, we only consider the case that
p
is a constant and leave the study of limiting behavior when
p\rightarrow\infty

[
11
]
as future work.

Definition 2.2

(
p
th-order tensor step)
.

For an input point
\bar{\bm{z}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
and a monotone operator
{\bm{F}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
the
p
th-order tensor step outputs
{\bm{z}}={\mathcal{T}}_{p}(\bar{\bm{z}};{\bm{F}},M)
such that

\left\langle\bar{\bm{F}}_{p-1}({\bm{z}};\bar{\bm{z}})+\frac{M}{p!}\|{\bm{z}}-\bar{\bm{z}}\|^{p-1}({\bm{z}}-\bar{\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}^{\prime}-{\bm{z}}\right\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}},

(2)

where
\bar{\bm{F}}_{p-1}({\bm{z}};\bar{\bm{z}}):=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\frac{1}{i!}\nabla^{k}{\bm{F}}(\bar{\bm{z}})[{\bm{z}}-\bar{\bm{z}}]^{k}
is the
(p-1)
th-order Taylor expansion of
{\bm{F}}
.

The algorithm proposed in this work jointly uses the above
p
th-order tensor step with
{\bm{F}}=(\nabla_{x}f,-\nabla_{y}f)^{\top}
,
{\bm{F}}=\nabla_{x}f
, and
{\bm{F}}=-\nabla_{y}f
in different iterates.
When
p=1
, the above tensor step is exactly the (projected) gradient step; When
p=2
, it corresponds to the cubic regularized Newton step
[
36
]
and
can be efficiently solved via binary search
[
31
]
; In general (
p\geq 2
), it corresponds to solving a strongly monotone variational inequality when setting
M>L_{p}
, which can be solved by the interior point method
[
48
,
47
]
or the cutting plane method
[
20
]
.

2.2
Optimality Criterion

A natural goal for the optimality of a solution
z
in Problem (
1
) is the duality gap
[
37
]
:

{\rm Gap}_{f}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}):=\max_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})-\min_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}}f({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}).

(3)

In this work, we instead adopt a criterion that is stronger than the
widely used duality gap, defined as follows.

Definition 2.3

(
7
)
.

We say
{\bm{z}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
is an
\epsilon
-solution to Problem (
1
) if

r_{\bm{F}}^{\rm tan}({\bm{z}}):=\min_{{\bm{c}}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{Z}}({\bm{z}})}\|{\bm{F}}({\bm{z}})+{\bm{c}}\|\leq\epsilon,\quad{\rm where}~~{\bm{F}}=(\nabla_{x}f,-\nabla_{y}f)^{\top}.

The above criterion is known as the tangent residual, which is introduced by
7
to generalize the gradient norm from unconstrained to constrained settings.
It is lower-bounded by the duality gap, as formally stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Under Assumption
2.2
, we have that

{\rm Gap}_{f}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\leq D_{\mathcal{Z}}\min_{{\bm{c}}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathcal{Z}}}}\|{\bm{F}}({\bm{z}})+{\bm{c}}\|,\quad\forall{\bm{z}}=({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}.

Proof.

It follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the convex-concavity of function
f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
: For
{\bm{c}}_{m}=\arg\min_{{\bm{c}}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathcal{Z}}}}\|{\bm{F}}({\bm{z}})+{\bm{c}}\|
, we have that

\displaystyle{\rm Gap}_{f}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\max_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})-\min_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}}f({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\langle\nabla_{y}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),{\bm{y}}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}\rangle+\min_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\langle\nabla_{x}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\langle{\bm{F}}({\bm{z}}^{\prime}),{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\rangle\leq\langle{\bm{F}}({\bm{z}})+{\bm{c}}_{m},{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{Z}}\|{\bm{F}}({\bm{z}})+{\bm{c}}\|.

∎

To find a point with a small tangent residual, we exploit smoothness and bound the tangent residual by the distance to the optimal solution in the following.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let the operator
{\bm{F}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be monotone and
L
-Lipschitz continuous. For any
{\bm{z}}^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
r_{\bm{F}}^{\rm tan}({\bm{z}}^{*})=0
, one gradient step
{\bm{z}}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{Z}}(\bar{\bm{z}}-\eta{\bm{F}}(\bar{\bm{z}}))
ensures

r_{{\bm{F}}}^{\rm tan}({\bm{z}})\leq\left\|\frac{\bar{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}}{\eta}+{\bm{F}}({\bm{z}})-{\bm{F}}(\bar{z})\right\|\leq\left(\frac{1}{\eta}+L\right)(2+\eta L)\|\bar{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{*}\|.

Consequently, setting
\eta=1/L
yields
r_{{\bm{F}}}^{\rm tan}({\bm{z}})\leq 6L\|\bar{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{*}\|
.

Proof.

From the update
{\bm{z}}={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{Z}}(\bar{\bm{z}}-\eta{\bm{F}}(\bar{\bm{z}}))
we know that

\frac{\bar{\bm{z}}-\eta{\bm{F}}(\bar{\bm{z}})-{\bm{z}}}{\eta}=\frac{\bar{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}}{\eta}-{\bm{F}}(\bar{\bm{z}})\in{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{Z}}({\bm{z}}).

Therefore, we can upper-bound the tangent residual as follows.

\displaystyle r_{{\bm{F}}}^{\rm tan}({\bm{z}})

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(\bar{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}})/\eta+{\bm{F}}({\bm{z}})-{\bm{F}}(\bar{z})\right\|\leq\left(1/\eta+L\right)\|{\bm{z}}-\bar{\bm{z}}\|

\displaystyle=\left(1/\eta+L\right)\left\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{Z}}(\bar{\bm{z}}-\eta{\bm{F}}(\bar{\bm{z}}))-\bar{\bm{z}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left(1/\eta+L\right)\left\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{Z}}(\bar{\bm{z}}-\eta{\bm{F}}(\bar{\bm{z}}))-{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{Z}}({\bm{z}}^{*}-\eta{\bm{F}}({\bm{z}}^{*}))+{\bm{z}}^{*}-\bar{\bm{z}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(1/\eta+L\right)(2+\eta L)\|\bar{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{*}\|,

where the last line uses the non-expansiveness of the projection operator, the
L
-Lipschitz continuity of operator
{\bm{F}}
, and the triangle inequality.
∎

2.3
Uniform Convexity

Finally, we introduce uniform convexity, which defines a natural function class that
(p-1)
th-order tensor methods enjoy a linear convergence rate
[
17
]
. This function class provides an important tool in our analyses.

Definition 2.4
.

A function
h({\bm{z}}):{\mathcal{Z}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is

p
th-order

\mu
-uniformly convex for some
\mu>0
if

h({\bm{z}})\geq h({\bm{z}}^{\prime})+\langle\nabla h({\bm{z}}^{\prime}),{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{p}\|{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\|^{p},\quad\forall{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}}.

We say
h({\bm{z}})
is
p
th-order
\mu
-uniformly concave if
-h({\bm{z}})
is
p
th-order
\mu
-uniformly convex.

In the following, we recall some properties of uniformly convex functions, which are also useful to derive the main results of this paper.

Lemma 2.3

(Section 4.2.2,
39
)
.

Let
h({\bm{z}}):{\mathcal{Z}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be
p
th-order
\mu
-uniformly convex. Then for any
{\bm{z}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
and
{\bm{z}}^{*}=\arg\min_{{\bm{z}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}h({\bm{z}})
, we have that

\frac{p-1}{p}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\|\nabla h({\bm{z}})\|^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\geq h({\bm{z}})-h({\bm{z}}^{*})\geq\frac{\mu}{p}\|{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{*}\|^{p},\quad\forall{\bm{z}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}.

A typical example of uniformly convex functions is the power function

d_{p}({\bm{z}})=\|{\bm{z}}\|^{p}/p
, whose properties are listed in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

The function
d_{p}({\bm{z}})=\|{\bm{z}}\|^{p}/p
with
p\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
satisfies the following:

1.

It is
p
th-order
\left(1/2\right)^{p-2}
-uniformly convex;

2.

It has
(p-1)!
-Lipschitz continuous
p
th-order derivatives.

Proof.

See
[
39
, Lemma 4.2.3]
for item 1, and
[
49
, Theorem 7.1]
for item 2, respectively.

∎

Next, let us define uniformly-convex-uniformly-concave functions.

Definition 2.5
.

We say
f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
is
p
th-order
\mu_{x}
-uniformly-convex-
\mu_{y}
-uniformly-concave if
f({\bm{x}},\,\cdot\,)
is
p
th-order
\mu_{x}

The following lemma shows that the gradient operator of a

-uniformly-convex-
\mu
-uniformly-concave function is
({2\mu}/{p})

Lemma 2.5
.

If
f:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a
p
th-order
\mu
-uniformly-convex-
\mu
-uniformly concave function, then its gradient operator is
({2\mu}/{p})
-uniformly monotone, i.e.,

\langle{\bm{g}}_{1}-{\bm{g}}_{2},{\bm{z}}_{1}-{\bm{z}}_{2}\rangle\geq\frac{2\mu}{p}\|{\bm{z}}_{1}-{\bm{z}}_{2}\|^{p},\quad\forall{\bm{g}}_{1}\in{\bm{A}}({\bm{z}}_{1}),{\bm{g}}_{2}\in{\bm{A}}({\bm{z}}_{2}),~~\forall{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2}\in{\mathcal{Z}},

(4)

{\bm{A}}({\bm{z}})={\bm{F}}({\bm{z}})+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{Z}}({\bm{z}})
and
{\bm{F}}=(\nabla_{x}f,-\nabla_{y}f)^{\top}
.

Proof.

For any
({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{2}),({\bm{x}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{2})\in{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
, we have that

\displaystyle f({\bm{x}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{1})-f({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{1})

\displaystyle\geq\langle\nabla_{x}f({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{1})+{\bm{u}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}-{\bm{x}}_{1}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{p}\|{\bm{x}}_{2}-{\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{p},~~{\bm{u}}_{1}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}({\bm{x}}_{1});

\displaystyle f({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{2})-f({\bm{x}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{2})

\displaystyle\geq\langle\nabla_{x}f({\bm{x}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{2})+{\bm{u}}_{2},{\bm{x}}_{1}-{\bm{x}}_{2}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{p}\|{\bm{x}}_{2}-{\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{p},~~{\bm{u}}_{2}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}({\bm{x}}_{2});

\displaystyle-f({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{2})+f({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{1})

\displaystyle\geq\langle-\nabla_{y}f({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{1})+{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{2}-{\bm{y}}_{1}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{p}\|{\bm{y}}_{2}-{\bm{y}}_{1}\|^{p},~~{\bm{v}}_{1}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}({\bm{y}}_{1});

\displaystyle-f({\bm{x}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{1})+f({\bm{x}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{2})

\displaystyle\geq\langle-\nabla_{y}f({\bm{x}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{2})+{\bm{v}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{1}-{\bm{y}}_{2}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{p}\|{\bm{y}}_{2}-{\bm{y}}_{1}\|^{p},~~{\bm{v}}_{2}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}({\bm{y}}_{2}).

Summing up the above four inequalities yields Eq. (

∎

4.1
A Uniformly-Convex-Uniformly-Concave Surrogate

Instead of directly optimizing the original objective, our accelerated algorithm optimized the following uniformly-convex-uniformly-concave surrogate function

Lemma 4.1
.

Under Assumption

and

5
The
\Omega(\epsilon^{-2/(3p-1)})
Lower Bound

Our upper bound in the previous section suggests that existing rates are not optimal.
In this section, we provide lower bounds for the following algorithm class, which is a natural generalization of the
p
th-order algorithms from convex minimization
[
41

44
]
to convex-concave minimax problems.
The assumption of
{\bm{z}}_{0}=({\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{y}}_{0})=({\bm{0}},{\bm{0}})

f({\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}_{0})

Definition 5.1

(

.

We define the

that starts with
{\bm{z}}_{0}=({\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{y}}_{0})=({\bm{0}},{\bm{0}})

and then takes one of the following three options:

(A)

Generate

and
{\bm{y}}_{t+1}={\bm{y}}_{t}

{\rm Gap}_{f}({\bm{x}}_{T},{\bm{y}}_{T})\geq\frac{L_{p}T}{\beta^{p+1}2^{p-1}(p+1)!}=\Omega\left(\frac{L_{p}D_{\mathcal{Z}}^{p+1}}{T^{(3p+1)/2}}\right),

which degenerates to the same
\Omega(\epsilon^{-2/(3p+1)})
lower bound derived from convex minimization
[
3
]
. The cause of this degeneration is the asymmetry of the diameters
D_{\bar{\mathcal{X}}}
and
D_{\bar{\mathcal{Y}}}
in the construction of Theorem
5.1
, since the rescale factor
\beta
has to depend on their maximum rather than the minimum, to remain the same Lipschitz constant of the
p
th-order derivatives.

5.2
Our Lower Bound

In this section, we give a new lower bound construction with symmetric diameters in
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
to fix the aforementioned issue in
[
1
,
29
]
.
We construct the unscaled hard instance
f:\bar{\mathcal{X}}\times\bar{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
as

\bar{f}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\frac{L_{p}}{2^{p+1}p!}\left({\bm{y}}_{[1]}(1-{\bm{x}}_{[1]})^{p}+\sum_{i=1}^{T}{\bm{y}}_{[i+1]}({\bm{x}}_{[i]}-{\bm{x}}_{[i+1]})^{p}\right),

(14)

where the associated constraint sets
\bar{\mathcal{X}}
and
\bar{\mathcal{Y}}
are

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}:0\leq{\bm{x}}_{[T+1]}\leq{\bm{x}}_{[T]}\leq{\bm{x}}_{[1]}\leq 1\},

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}:0\leq{\bm{y}}_{[i]}\leq 1,~~\forall i=1,\cdots,T+1\}.

We summarize the properties of our construction in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

The function
\bar{f}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
defined in Eq. (
14
) satisfies the following properties.

1.

It is convex-concave
and has
L_{p}
-Lipschitz continuous
p
th-order derivatives on
\bar{\mathcal{X}}\times\bar{\mathcal{Y}}
.

2.

For any algorithm
{\mathcal{M}}
in Definition
5.1
running on
\bar{f}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
within
t
iterations, we have

{\rm supp}({\bm{x}}_{t})\subseteq\{1,2,\cdots,t\}\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm supp}({\bm{y}}_{t})\subseteq\{1,2,\cdots,t\}.

(15)

3.

Let
\bar{\bm{F}}=(\nabla_{x}\bar{f},-\nabla_{y}\bar{f})^{\top}
. For any
{\bm{z}}=({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in\bar{\mathcal{X}}\times\bar{\mathcal{Y}}
with
{\bm{x}}_{[T+1]}={\bm{y}}_{[T+1]}=0
,

r_{\bar{\bm{F}}}^{\rm tan}({\bm{z}})\geq\frac{{\rm Gap}_{\bar{f}}({\bm{z}})}{D_{\bar{\mathcal{Z}}}}\geq\frac{D_{\bar{\mathcal{Z}}}^{p}}{2^{(p+1)/2}(T+1)^{(3p-1)/2}},

where
D_{\bar{\mathcal{Z}}}=\sqrt{2(T+1)}
is the diameter of constraint set
\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
C
for the proof.
∎

Now, to give a lower bound for optimizing a convex-concave function
f:{\mathcal{Z}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
with
L_{p}
-Lipschitz continuous
p
th-order derivatives, such that
{\mathcal{Z}}
has a diameter no more than
D_{\mathcal{Z}}
, we only need to rescale the above hard instance as well as its domain as

f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\bar{f}(\beta{\bm{x}},\beta{\bm{y}})/\beta^{p+1},\quad{\mathcal{Z}}=\bar{\mathcal{Z}}/\beta,\quad{\rm where}~~\beta={D_{\bar{\mathcal{Z}}}}/{D_{{\mathcal{Z}}}}.

(16)

Finally, we obtain the following lower bound.

Theorem 5.2
.

The hard instance
f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
defined in Eq. (
16
) satisfies the following.

1.

It is convex-concave and has
L_{p}
-Lipschitz continuous
p
th-order derivatives on
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
.

2.

Let
{\bm{F}}=(\nabla_{x}f,-\nabla_{y}f)^{\top}
. For any
T\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
and any sequence
\{{\bm{z}}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
generated by a
p
th-order algorithm
{\mathcal{M}}
in Definition
5.1
, we have that

r^{\rm tan}_{{\bm{F}}}({\bm{z}}_{T})=\Omega\left(\frac{L_{p}D_{{\mathcal{Z}}}^{p}}{(T+1)^{(3p-1)/2}}\right).

(17)

In other words, the number of
p
th-order oracle calls for the algorithm class in Definition
5.1
to find an
\epsilon
-solution is lower bounded by
\Omega((\nicefrac{{D_{{\mathcal{Z}}}^{p}L_{p}}}{{\epsilon}})^{2/(3p-1)})
.

Proof.

First, item 1 directly follows from Lemma
5.1
(item 1) and the scaling in
Eq. (
16
). Second, from Lemma
5.1
(item 2), we know that the sequence

generated by
{\mathcal{M}}
satisfies
({\bm{x}}_{T})_{[T+1]}=({\bm{y}}_{T})_{[T+1]}=0
, and thus Eq. (
17
) follows from Lemma
5.1
(item 3).
∎

The theorem shows an
\Omega(\epsilon^{-2/(3p-1)})
lower bound holds for all
p
th-order algorithms in Definition
5.1
.
Note that our hard instance also has
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
-Lipschitz continuous
q
th-order derivatives for all
q=1,\cdots,p
, which means the
lower-order Lipschitzness does not change the lower bound.

When
p=1
, our lower bound is tight as the
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-1})
upper bound can be achieved by the EG method
[
25
]
. When
p\geq 2
, our lower bound still has a gap between the
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-4/(3p+1)})
upper bound established in this work.
We conjecture that our current lower bound is possibly not tight, but improving it looks challenging as we find it difficult to come up with a convex-concave function with
p
th-order derivatives besides the function
x^{p}y
we used in Eq. (
14
).
We hope our result can be an initial step towards proving a tighter lower bound in the future.

6
Conclusion and Future Works

In this paper, we propose a
p
th-order algorithm Minimax-AIPE for convex-concave minimax problems. Our theoretical result shows our proposed method achieves an
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-4/(3p+1)})
upper complexity bound and thus improves the best-known
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2/(p+1)})
result.
We also establish an
\Omega(\epsilon^{-2/(3p-1)})
lower bound for all
p
th-order algorithms.

One important future direction is to further close the gap between our upper and lower bounds. Other possible directions include shaving the logarithmic factor in the upper bound by extending the existing technique from
p=1

[
27
,
12
]
to all
p\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
, and using our framework to accelerate other methods such as stochastic algorithms
[
54
,
58
,
52
]
and quasi-Newton algorithms
[
21
]
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad\|\tilde{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{*}(\bar{\bm{x}})\|\leq\|\tilde{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{*}(\hat{\bm{y}};\bar{\bm{x}})\|+\|{\bm{x}}^{*}(\hat{\bm{y}};\bar{\bm{x}})-{\bm{x}}^{*}(\bar{\bm{x}})\|\\

(47)

Combining

, Eq. (
44

Appendix C
Proof of Lemma
5.1

Proof.

We prove the lemma by verifying each property one by one.

1.

It is apparent that both the sets
\bar{\mathcal{X}}
and
\bar{\mathcal{Y}}
are convex. We only need to show that
\bar{f}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
is convex-concave in
\bar{\mathcal{X}}\times\bar{\mathcal{Y}}
. Let

{\bm{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&\\
1&-1&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&1&-1\end{bmatrix}

(53)

and
{\bm{e}}_{1}=(1,0,\cdots,0)^{\top}
. Let
\bar{g}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\sum_{i=1}^{T+1}{\bm{y}}_{[i]}{\bm{x}}_{[i]}^{p}
. This function is convex-concave on the domain
{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{+}
. Since
\bar{f}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=(L_{p}/(2^{p+1}p!))\bar{g}({\bm{B}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{e}}_{1},{\bm{y}})
and the affine mapping is a convexity–preserving operation, we know that
\bar{f}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
is also convex-concave on the domain
\{{\bm{x}}:{\bm{B}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{e}}_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{+}\}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{+}
. Next, we show that
\bar{f}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
has
L_{p}
-Lipschitz continuous
p
th-order derivatives.
It is easy to show that

\|\nabla^{p}\bar{g}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-\nabla^{p}\bar{g}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\|\leq 2p!(\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\|+\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\|),\quad\forall{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}.

Since the matrix
{\bm{B}}
in Eq. (
53
) satisfies
\|{\bm{B}}\|\leq 2
, we further have that

\|\nabla^{p}\bar{f}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-\nabla^{p}\bar{f}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\|\leq L_{p}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\|+\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\|),\quad\forall{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}.

2.

We prove the property in Eq. (
15
) by induction. We let

{\mathbb{R}}_{t}^{T+1}=\{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}:{\bm{x}}_{[i]}=0,~~i=t+1,\cdots,T+1\}.

Suppose
({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{t})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{t}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{t}
, our goal is to show that
({\bm{x}}_{t+1},{\bm{y}}_{t+1})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{t+1}\cap\bar{\mathcal{X}})\times({\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{t+1}\cap\bar{\mathcal{Y}})
when the algorithm lies in the class of Definition
5.1
. Note that our hard instance in Eq. (
14
) is constructed such that
for any
q\in\{1,\cdots,p\}
we have

\bar{\bm{F}}^{q-1}(\bar{\bm{z}}_{t})[{\bm{h}}]^{q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{t+1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{t+1},\quad\forall{\bm{h}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1},

Next, we consider the different cases when the algorithm takes different options.

Option (A).
We have
{\bm{y}}_{t+1}={\bm{y}}_{t}
and
{\bm{x}}_{t+1}
satisfies that

\left\langle\sum_{k=1}^{q}\frac{1}{k!}(\nabla_{x})^{k}\bar{f}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t},\bar{\bm{y}}_{t})+\frac{M_{t}}{q!}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}-\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}\|({\bm{x}}_{t+1}-\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}),{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{t+1}\right\rangle\geq 0,~~\forall{\bm{x}}\in\bar{\mathcal{X}}.

In this case, we can prove that
{\bm{x}}_{t+1}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{t}^{T+1}
. Otherwise,
{\bm{x}}_{t+1}\notin{\mathbb{R}}_{t}^{T+1}
, and there must exist an index
j\in\{t+1,\cdots,T+1\}
such that
({\bm{x}}_{t+1})_{[j]}>0
. Then, letting
{\bm{x}}={\bm{x}}_{t+1}-({\bm{x}}_{t+1})_{[j]}{\bm{e}}_{j}
in the above inequality leads to a contradiction.

Option (B).
We have
{\bm{x}}_{t+1}={\bm{x}}_{t}
and
{\bm{y}}_{t+1}
satisfies that

\left\langle\sum_{k=1}^{q}\frac{1}{k!}(-1)^{k}(\nabla_{y})^{k}\bar{f}(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t},\bar{\bm{y}}_{t})+\frac{M_{t}}{q!}\|{\bm{y}}_{t+1}-\bar{\bm{y}}_{t}\|({\bm{y}}_{t+1}-\bar{\bm{y}}_{t}),{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}_{t+1}\right\rangle\geq 0,~~\forall{\bm{y}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Y}}.

In this case, we can prove that
{\bm{y}}_{t+1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{t+1}
. Otherwise
{\bm{y}}_{t+1}\notin{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{t+1}
, there must exist an index
j\in\{t+2,\cdots,T+1\}
such that
({\bm{y}}_{t+1})_{[j]}>0
. Define
j_{m}=\{\max j:({\bm{y}}_{t+1})_{[j]}>0,~j=t+2,\cdots T+1\}
. Then letting
{\bm{y}}={\bm{y}}_{t+1}-({\bm{y}}_{t+1})_{[j_{m}]}{\bm{e}}_{j_{m}}
in the above inequality also leads to a contradiction.

Option (C).
We have
{\bm{z}}_{t+1}=({\bm{x}}_{t+1},{\bm{y}}_{t+1})
satisfies that

\left\langle\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\frac{1}{k!}\nabla^{k}\bar{\bm{F}}(\bar{\bm{z}}_{t})+\frac{M_{t}}{q!}\|{\bm{z}}_{t+1}-\bar{\bm{z}}_{t}\|({\bm{z}}_{t+1}-\bar{\bm{z}}_{t}),{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}_{t+1}\right\rangle\geq 0,~~\forall{\bm{z}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Z}}.

We can mimic the analysis in option (A) for variable
{\bm{x}}
and the analysis in option ((B) for variable
{\bm{y}}
separately to show that
({\bm{x}}_{t+1},{\bm{y}}_{t+1})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{t+1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{t+1}
.

Combining all the above three cases proves the induction that
({\bm{x}}_{t+1},{\bm{y}}_{t+1})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{t+1}\cap{\mathcal{X}})\times({\mathbb{R}}^{T+1}_{t+1}\cap{\mathcal{Y}})
. And the induction base is trivially satisfied as
({\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{y}}_{0})=({\bm{0}},{\bm{0}})
.

3.

For any
{\bm{z}}=({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in\bar{\mathcal{X}}\times\bar{\mathcal{Y}}
with
{\bm{x}}_{[T+1]}={\bm{y}}_{[T+1]}=0
, we can use Lemma
2.1
to give a lower bound of the tangent residual via duality gap as follows:

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{Z}}r_{\bar{\bm{F}}}^{\rm tan}({\bm{z}})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L_{p}}{2^{p+1}p!}\left(\max_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\bar{f}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})-\min_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\bar{f}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L_{p}}{2^{p+1}p!}\left(\bar{f}({\bm{x}},{\bm{1}})-\bar{f}({\bm{1}},{\bm{y}})\right)=\frac{L_{p}}{2^{p+1}p!}\bar{f}({\bm{x}}_{T},{\bm{1}})

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{p}}{2^{p+1}p!}\left(\left(1-{\bm{x}}_{[1]}\right)^{p}+\sum_{i=1}^{T-1}\left({\bm{x}}_{[i]}-{\bm{x}}_{[i+1]}\right)^{p}+\left({\bm{x}}_{[T]}\right)^{p}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L_{p}(T+1)}{2^{p+1}p!}\left(\frac{1}{T+1}\left(1-{\bm{x}}_{[1]}+\sum_{i=1}^{T-1}\left({\bm{x}}_{[i]}-{\bm{x}}_{[i+1]}\right)+{\bm{x}}_{[T]}\right)\right)^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{p}}{2^{p+1}p!(T+1)^{p-1}}=\frac{L_{p}\left(D_{\bar{\mathcal{Z}}}/\sqrt{2}\right)^{p+1}}{2^{p+1}p!(T+1)^{(3p-1)/2}},

where the last inequality uses the convexity of
x^{p}
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and the last equality uses the fact that
D_{\bar{\mathcal{X}}}=D_{\bar{\mathcal{Y}}}=\sqrt{T+1}
and
D_{\bar{\mathcal{Z}}}=\sqrt{2(T+1)}
.

∎
