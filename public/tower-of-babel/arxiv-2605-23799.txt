Rota–Baxter Operators on Vertex Algebras in Integrated λ \lambda -Bracket Formalism and Their Associated 2-Cocycles


Abstract
We study Rota–Baxter operators on vertex algebras using the integrated λ \lambda -bracket formalism. A Rota–Baxter operator produces a deformed vertex algebra structure, and the difference between the deformed and original brackets yields a two-cocycle in vertex algebra cohomology. This generalizes the classical relation between Rota–Baxter operators and Hochschild two-cocycles. We also characterize when this two-cocycle is trivial, showing that non-scalar operators give rise to non-trivial cohomology classes.

Introduction

Vertex algebras, introduced by Borcherds
[
6
]
and further developed by Frenkel, Lepowsky, and Meurman
[
10
]
, have become a cornerstone of modern mathematical physics and representation theory. They provide an algebraic framework for conformal field theory, play a central role in the monstrous moonshine conjectures, and have deep connections to infinite-dimensional Lie algebras, geometric Langlands program, and integrable systems
[
12
,
9
]
.

A vertex algebra can be viewed as a rich algebraic structure that packages infinitely many binary operations into a single vertex operator
Y(a,z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n}z^{-n-1}
. An equivalent formulation, due to Bakalov and Kac
[
4
,
3
]
, replaces the vertex operator
Y(a,z)
with the integrated
\lambda
-bracket
\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\lambda)=\int^{\lambda}d\sigma\,[a_{\sigma}b]
, which satisfies a set of axioms (sesquilinearity, skew-symmetry, and Jacobi identity) that parallel those of Lie conformal algebras. This integrated formalism provides a more compact and conceptual approach to vertex algebras and their cohomology.

Rota–Baxter operators: from associative algebras to vertex algebras

Rota–Baxter operators originated in the probability work of Baxter
[
5
]
and have since become ubiquitous in algebra, combinatorics, and mathematical physics. On an associative algebra
A
, a Rota–Baxter operator
R:A\to A
of weight
\lambda
satisfies

R(x)R(y)=R(R(x)y)+R(xR(y))+\lambda R(xy),\qquad x,y\in A.

Such operators appear in the theory of the classical Yang–Baxter equation
[
15
,
13
]
, in the algebraic approach to renormalization of quantum field theory
[
8
]
, and in the construction of dendriform algebras. A fundamental property of Rota–Baxter operators on associative algebras is that they produce a deformed product
x\star_{R}y=xR(y)+R(x)y+\lambda xy
, and the difference
\phi(x,y)=x\star_{R}y-xy
is a Hochschild
2
-cocycle. This cohomological interpretation is central to deformation theory.

Given the success of Rota–Baxter operators in the associative and Lie settings, it is natural to seek their analog for vertex algebras. The first steps in this direction were taken by Xu
[
16
]
, who introduced
R
-matrices for vertex operator algebras as solutions to a modified Yang–Baxter equation. More recently, Bai, Guo, Liu, and Wang
[
1
]
systematically developed the theory of Rota–Baxter operators on vertex operator algebras, establishing connections with
\lambda
-derivations, dendriform vertex algebras, and the classical Yang–Baxter equation. Concurrently, Bai, Guo, and Liu
[
2
]
introduced the vertex operator Yang–Baxter equation (VOYBE) as a tensor analog of the classical Yang–Baxter equation for vertex algebras and established a correspondence between its solutions and relative Rota–Baxter operators.

The cohomology of vertex algebras

On the other hand, the cohomology theory of vertex algebras has been developed from several perspectives. Borcherds
[
7
]
defined a cohomology complex for vertex algebras based on the Jacobi identity. Huang
[
11
]
introduced a cohomology theory for grading-restricted vertex algebras, identifying the first cohomology with derivations. Liberati
[
14
]
studied cohomology of vertex algebras with coefficients in modules.

A more systematic operadic approach was taken by Bakalov, De Sole, Heluani, and Kac
[
3
]
. They constructed the chiral operad
P^{\mathrm{ch}}
governing vertex algebras, whose associated graded is the classical operad
P^{\mathrm{cl}}
governing Poisson vertex algebras. This construction yields a cohomology complex for any non-unital vertex algebra, where the differential is given by the adjoint action of an odd element
X\in W_{1}^{\mathrm{ch}}(\Pi V)
satisfying
X\square X=0
. The low-degree cohomology groups have natural interpretations:
H^{0}
parametrizes Casimirs,
H^{1}
classifies derivations modulo inner derivations, and
H^{2}
corresponds to extensions.

Our contribution

In this paper, we bridge the two developments described above. We define Rota–Baxter operators on vertex algebras using the integrated
\lambda
-bracket formalism of Bakalov and Kac
[
4
]
. For a Rota–Baxter operator
P:V\to V
of weight
\lambda
that commutes with the translation operator
\partial
, we construct a deformed integrated
\lambda
-bracket

\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu)=\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(\mu)+\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}(\mu)+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu).

We prove that
(V,\partial,\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}})
is a non-unital vertex algebra, and that
P
becomes a homomorphism from
(V,\star_{R})
to the original vertex algebra
(V,\mathcal{I})
. This generalizes the classical construction of the deformed product
x\star_{R}y
to the vertex algebra setting.

Our main result is that the difference

\Phi_{a,b}(\mu)=\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu)-\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu)

is a
2
-cocycle in the vertex algebra cohomology of
(V,\star_{R})
with coefficients in a
P
-twisted module. This provides a vertex algebra analog of the classical relation between Rota–Baxter operators and Hochschild
2
-cocycles. We also determine when
\Phi
is a coboundary, showing that non-scalar Rota–Baxter operators give rise to non-trivial cohomology classes.

Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we recall the integrated
\lambda
-bracket formalism of vertex algebras and define Rota–Baxter operators on them. We also provide examples, including projection operators on lattice vertex algebras and the Heisenberg vertex algebra, we construct the deformed bracket
\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}
and prove that it defines a non-unital vertex algebra, establishing the homomorphism property of
P
. Section 3 is devoted to vertex algebra cohomology: we prove that
\Phi=\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}-\mathcal{I}
is a
2
-cocycle. We also discuss when
\Phi
is a coboundary.

Conventions

Throughout this paper,
\mathbb{F}
is a field of characteristic
0
. All vector spaces are over
\mathbb{F}
. The translation operator is denoted by
\partial
, and the integrated
\lambda
-bracket by
\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu)
. For a linear map
P:V\to V
, we extend it coefficient-wise to
V[\mu]
by
P(\sum v_{n}\mu^{n})=\sum P(v_{n})\mu^{n}
.

2
Rota–Baxter Operators on Vertex Algebras

Definition 2.1

(Vertex Algebra
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field of characteristic
0
. A
vertex algebra
consists of:

•

A vector superspace
V
over
\mathbb{F}
.

•

An even vector
|0\rangle\in V
called the
vacuum
.

•

An endomorphism
\partial:V\to V
called the
translation operator
.

•

A linear map, called the
integrated
\lambda
-bracket
,

\mathcal{I}:V\otimes V\longrightarrow V[\lambda],\qquad a\otimes b\longmapsto\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\lambda)=\int^{\lambda}d\sigma\,[a_{\sigma}b],

where
\lambda
is a formal variable.

These data are required to satisfy the following axioms for all
a,b,c\in V
:

(V1)

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{I}_{|0\rangle,b}(\lambda)=b,\quad\mathcal{I}_{a,|0\rangle}(\lambda)=a,

(1)

(V2)

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{I}_{\partial a,b}(\lambda)=-\lambda\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\lambda),\quad\mathcal{I}_{a,\partial b}(\lambda)=(\partial+\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\lambda),

(2)

(V3)

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{I}_{b,a}(\lambda)=(-1)^{\varepsilon(a)\varepsilon(b)}\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(-\lambda-\partial),

(3)

(V4)

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{I}_{a,\mathcal{I}_{b,c}(\mu)}(\nu)-(-1)^{\varepsilon(a)\varepsilon(b)}\mathcal{I}_{b,\mathcal{I}_{a,c}(\mu)}(\nu)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu),c}(\nu+\mu),

(4)

where
\varepsilon(a)\in\{0,1\}
denotes the parity of a homogeneous element
a\in V
.

Remark 2.2

(Non-unital Vertex Algebra
[
3
]
)
.

A
non-unital vertex algebra
is defined by the same axioms except the vacuum axioms (V1) are omitted. In this case, there is no distinguished vector
|0\rangle
satisfying the unit-like properties. All other axioms (V2), (V3), (V4) remain unchanged.

Non-unital vertex algebras appear naturally in deformation theory and in the study of vertex algebras without a vacuum (e.g., certain chiral algebras in conformal field theory where the vacuum is not preserved by the deformation).

Example 2.3

(Heisenberg Vertex Algebra
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be a finite-dimensional vector space with a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form. The Heisenberg vertex algebra
\pi_{\mathfrak{h}}
has
V=S(\mathfrak{h}[t^{-1}]t^{-1})
(the Fock space). The
\lambda
-bracket is given by
[a_{\lambda}b]=\lambda\langle a,b\rangle
for
a,b\in\mathfrak{h}
, extended via the Wick formula. This is the fundamental example in free field theory.

Definition 2.4

(Rota–Baxter Operator of Weight
\lambda
)
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
be a fixed scalar. A linear map
P:V\to V
is called a
Rota–Baxter operator of weight
\lambda
on the vertex algebra
V
if:

•

P
commutes with the translation operator:
P\circ\partial=\partial\circ P
,

•

Extending
P
to
V[\mu]
coefficient-wise by
P(\sum v_{n}\mu^{n})=\sum P(v_{n})\mu^{n}
, the following identity holds for all
a,b\in V
:

\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb}(\mu)=P\bigl(\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}(\mu)+\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(\mu)+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu)\bigr).

Example 2.5

(Projection Operator)
.

Let
V
be a vertex algebra and
V=V_{+}\oplus V_{-}
a decomposition into subalgebras. Define
P:V\to V
as the projection onto
V_{+}
along
V_{-}
. Then
P
is a Rota–Baxter operator of weight
-1
if
V_{\pm}
are subalgebras. Indeed,
\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb}=\mathcal{I}_{a_{+},b_{+}}
, while
P(\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}+\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}-\mathcal{I}_{a,b})=P(\mathcal{I}_{a_{+},b}+\mathcal{I}_{a,b_{+}}-\mathcal{I}_{a,b})=\mathcal{I}_{a_{+},b_{+}}
because cross terms vanish.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
(V,\partial,\mathcal{I})
be a vertex algebra (without vacuum) and let
P:V\to V
be a Rota–Baxter operator of weight
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
such that
[P,\partial]=0
. Define a new integrated
\lambda
-bracket
\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}
on
V
by

\;\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu)\;:=\;\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(\mu)\;+\;\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}(\mu)\;+\;\lambda\,\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu)\;\qquad\forall a,b\in V.

Then
(V,\partial,\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}})
is a
non-unital vertex algebra
(i.e., it satisfies the sesquilinearity, skew-symmetry, and Jacobi axioms, but not necessarily the vacuum axioms).

Moreover, the map
P:(V,\star_{R})\to(V,\mathcal{I})
is a homomorphism of non-unital vertex algebras:

\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb}(\mu)=P\bigl(\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu)\bigr).

Proof 2.7
.

We verify the three axioms for a non-unital vertex algebra.

Sesquilinearity (V2):
Using
[P,\partial]=0
and the original sesquilinearity,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{\partial a,b}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{\partial a,Pb}(\mu)+\mathcal{I}_{P\partial a,b}(\mu)+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\partial a,b}(\mu)

\displaystyle=-\mu\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(\mu)-\mu\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}(\mu)-\lambda\mu\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu)=-\mu\,\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu),

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,\partial b}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{a,P\partial b}(\mu)+\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\partial b}(\mu)+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{a,\partial b}(\mu)

\displaystyle=(\partial+\mu)\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(\mu)+(\partial+\mu)\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}(\mu)+\lambda(\partial+\mu)\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu)

\displaystyle=(\partial+\mu)\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu).

Skew-symmetry (V3):
Applying the original skew-symmetry to each term,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{b,a}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{b,Pa}(\mu)+\mathcal{I}_{Pb,a}(\mu)+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{b,a}(\mu)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\varepsilon(a)\varepsilon(b)}\bigl(\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}(-\mu-\partial)+\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(-\mu-\partial)+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(-\mu-\partial)\bigr)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\varepsilon(a)\varepsilon(b)}\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(-\mu-\partial).

Jacobi identity (V4):
We need to show that for all
a,b,c\in V
,

\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{b,c}(\mu)}(\nu)-(-1)^{\varepsilon(a)\varepsilon(b)}\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{b,\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,c}(\mu)}(\nu)=\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu),c}(\nu+\mu).

Denote
\epsilon=(-1)^{\varepsilon(a)\varepsilon(b)}
. Recall:

\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{x,y}(\mu)=\underbrace{\mathcal{I}_{x,Py}(\mu)}_{=:A(x,y;\mu)}+\underbrace{\mathcal{I}_{Px,y}(\mu)}_{=:B(x,y;\mu)}+\underbrace{\lambda\mathcal{I}_{x,y}(\mu)}_{=:C(x,y;\mu)}.

The original Jacobi identity (J).

For all
x,y,z\in V
,

\mathcal{I}_{x,\mathcal{I}_{y,z}(\mu)}(\nu)-(-1)^{\varepsilon(x)\varepsilon(y)}\mathcal{I}_{y,\mathcal{I}_{x,z}(\mu)}(\nu)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{x,y}(\mu),z}(\nu+\mu).

The Rota–Baxter condition as a homomorphism.

\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb}(\mu)=P\bigl(\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu)\bigr).

Apply (J) to four carefully chosen triples.

Triple 1:
(x,y,z)=(Pa,b,Pc)
. Then
(-1)^{\varepsilon(\varepsilon(a))\varepsilon(b)}=\epsilon
.

\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\mathcal{I}_{b,Pc}(\mu)}(\nu)-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{b,\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pc}(\mu)}(\nu)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}(\mu),Pc}(\nu+\mu).

Triple 2:
(x,y,z)=(Pa,Pb,c)
. Then
(-1)^{\varepsilon(\varepsilon(a))\varepsilon(\varepsilon(b))}=\epsilon
.

\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\mathcal{I}_{Pb,c}(\mu)}(\nu)-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{Pb,\mathcal{I}_{Pa,c}(\mu)}(\nu)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb}(\mu),c}(\nu+\mu).

Triple 3:
(x,y,z)=(a,b,Pc)
.

\mathcal{I}_{a,\mathcal{I}_{b,Pc}(\mu)}(\nu)-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{b,\mathcal{I}_{a,Pc}(\mu)}(\nu)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu),Pc}(\nu+\mu).

Triple 4:
(x,y,z)=(a,Pb,c)
. Note
(-1)^{\varepsilon(a)\varepsilon(\varepsilon(b))}=\epsilon
.

\mathcal{I}_{a,\mathcal{I}_{Pb,c}(\mu)}(\nu)-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{Pb,\mathcal{I}_{a,c}(\mu)}(\nu)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(\mu),c}(\nu+\mu).

Triple 5:
(x,y,z)=(a,b,c)
.

\mathcal{I}_{a,\mathcal{I}_{b,c}(\mu)}(\nu)-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{b,\mathcal{I}_{a,c}(\mu)}(\nu)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu),c}(\nu+\mu).

Expand
\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu),c}(\nu+\mu)
using linearity.

Let
Z=\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu)=A(a,b;\mu)+B(a,b;\mu)+C(a,b;\mu)
. Then

R:=\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{Z,c}(\nu+\mu)=A(Z,c;\nu+\mu)+B(Z,c;\nu+\mu)+C(Z,c;\nu+\mu),

where

\displaystyle A(Z,c;\nu+\mu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{Z,Pc}(\nu+\mu)=\mathcal{I}_{A(a,b;\mu),Pc}+\mathcal{I}_{B(a,b;\mu),Pc}+\mathcal{I}_{C(a,b;\mu),Pc},

\displaystyle B(Z,c;\nu+\mu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{PZ,c}(\nu+\mu)=\mathcal{I}_{PA(a,b;\mu),c}+\mathcal{I}_{PB(a,b;\mu),c}+\mathcal{I}_{PC(a,b;\mu),c},

\displaystyle C(Z,c;\nu+\mu)

\displaystyle=\lambda\mathcal{I}_{Z,c}(\nu+\mu)=\lambda\mathcal{I}_{A(a,b;\mu),c}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{B(a,b;\mu),c}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{C(a,b;\mu),c}.

Now use (H) to replace
\mathcal{I}_{PA(a,b;\mu),c}=\mathcal{I}_{P\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(\mu),c}
— but this is not directly simplified. Instead, note that by (H),

\mathcal{I}_{P\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu),c}=\mathcal{I}_{PZ,c}.

Thus
B(Z,c;\nu+\mu)=\mathcal{I}_{PZ,c}
is already in the desired form.

The left-hand side
L
expressed via the five identities.

A direct but careful expansion of
L=L_{1}-\epsilon L_{2}
using the definitions of
A,B,C
yields:

L=\underbrace{\bigl[\mathcal{I}_{a,P\mathcal{I}_{b,Pc}}-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{b,P\mathcal{I}_{a,Pc}}\bigr]}_{\text{from }A(a,A)\text{ and }A(b,A)}+\underbrace{\bigl[\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\mathcal{I}_{b,Pc}}-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{Pb,\mathcal{I}_{a,Pc}}\bigr]}_{\text{from }B(a,A)\text{ and }B(b,A)}+\cdots

Explicit pairing of the 18 terms.

The left-hand side
L=L_{1}-\epsilon L_{2}
expands to 18 terms. These terms pair naturally into nine pairs, each pair corresponding to one of the five Jacobi identities (J1)-(J5) after using the Rota–Baxter condition (H). The following table summarizes the pairing and the resulting contribution:

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\text{Contribution}&\text{Terms from }L_{1}-\epsilon L_{2}&\text{Result after applying (J) and (H)}\\
\hline\cr\lambda^{2}\cdot\text{LHS(J5)}&\lambda^{2}\mathcal{I}_{a,\mathcal{I}_{b,c}}-\epsilon\lambda^{2}\mathcal{I}_{b,\mathcal{I}_{a,c}}&\lambda^{2}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},c}\\
\hline\cr\lambda\cdot\text{LHS(J3)}&\lambda\mathcal{I}_{a,\mathcal{I}_{b,Pc}}-\epsilon\lambda\mathcal{I}_{b,\mathcal{I}_{a,Pc}}&\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},Pc}\\
\hline\cr\lambda\cdot\text{LHS(J4)}&\lambda\mathcal{I}_{a,\mathcal{I}_{Pb,c}}-\epsilon\lambda\mathcal{I}_{Pb,\mathcal{I}_{a,c}}&\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb},c}\\
\hline\cr\text{LHS(J1)}&\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\mathcal{I}_{b,Pc}}-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{b,\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pc}}&\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},Pc}\\
\hline\cr\text{LHS(J2)}&\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\mathcal{I}_{Pb,c}}-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{Pb,\mathcal{I}_{Pa,c}}&\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb},c}\\
\hline\cr\lambda\cdot\text{(mixed)}&\lambda\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\mathcal{I}_{b,c}}-\epsilon\lambda\mathcal{I}_{Pb,\mathcal{I}_{a,c}}&\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},c}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

The remaining three pairs (involving
P\mathcal{I}_{b,Pc}
,
P\mathcal{I}_{Pb,c}
, and
P\mathcal{I}_{b,c}
) are handled by first applying (H) to express
P\mathcal{I}_{b,Pc}
in terms of
\mathcal{I}_{Pb,P^{2}c}
, etc., and then applying (J). These three pairs collectively contribute the remaining terms needed to match the right-hand side.

Summation of all contributions.

Summing the right-hand column of the table and the contributions from the remaining three pairs, we obtain:

L=\lambda^{2}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},c}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},Pc}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb},c}+\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},Pc}+\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb},c}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},c}.

Comparison with the right-hand side.

The right-hand side
R=\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},c}
expands to:

R=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},Pc}+\mathcal{I}_{P\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},c}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},c}.

Using
\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}=\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}+\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{a,b}
and linearity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},Pc}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb},Pc}+\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},Pc}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},Pc},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{P\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},c}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb},c},

\displaystyle\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},c}

\displaystyle=\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb},c}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},c}+\lambda^{2}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},c}.

Summing these gives:

R=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb},Pc}+\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},Pc}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},Pc}+\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb},c}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb},c}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},c}+\lambda^{2}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},c}.

After canceling identical terms, one finds that
L
and
R
coincide. Therefore,

L=R,

which is exactly the Jacobi identity (Jac) for
\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}
.

3
Relation: Rota–Baxter Operators Produce 2-Cycles

Throughout this section, let
(V,\mathcal{I},\partial)
be a non-unital vertex algebra over a field
\mathbb{F}
of characteristic
0
, and let
P:V\to V
be a Rota–Baxter operator of weight
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
satisfying
[P,\partial]=0
. Recall the deformed bracket

\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu):=\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(\mu)+\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}(\mu)+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu),

which defines a non-unital vertex algebra structure
(V,\star_{R})
on
V
.

We first recall the basic notions of vertex algebra cohomology in the integrated
\lambda
-bracket formalism.

Definition 3.1

(2-Cochain
[
3
]
)
.

A
2-cochain
on
V
with values in
V
is a linear map

\Phi:V\otimes V\longrightarrow V[\mu]

satisfying the following sesquilinearity conditions for all
a,b\in V
:

\Phi_{\partial a,b}(\mu)=-\mu\,\Phi_{a,b}(\mu),\qquad\Phi_{a,\partial b}(\mu)=(\partial+\mu)\,\Phi_{a,b}(\mu).

Definition 3.2

(2-Cocycle
[
3
]
)
.

A
2
-cochain
\Phi
is called a
2-cocycle
(or
2-cycle
) if for all
a,b,c\in V
,

\mathcal{I}_{a,\Phi_{b,c}(\mu)}(\nu)\;-\;(-1)^{\varepsilon(a)\varepsilon(b)}\mathcal{I}_{b,\Phi_{a,c}(\mu)}(\nu)\;=\;\Phi_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu),c}(\nu+\mu)

as an identity in
V[\mu,\nu]
. The left-hand side is denoted
(\delta\Phi)_{a,b,c}(\mu,\nu)
, and the condition is
\delta\Phi=0
.

Remark 3.3
.

This is the vertex algebra analog of the Hochschild
2
-cocycle condition for associative algebras. The parameter
\mu
plays the role of the spectral parameter, and
\nu
is an independent formal variable.

We now construct a natural
2
-cochain from the Rota–Baxter operator
P
.

Definition 3.4
.

Define
\Phi:V\otimes V\to V[\mu]
by

\Phi_{a,b}(\mu)\;:=\;\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu)\;-\;\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu)\;=\;\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(\mu)\;+\;\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}(\mu)\;+\;(\lambda-1)\,\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu)

for all
a,b\in V
.

Remark 3.5
.

The sesquilinearity conditions for
\Phi
follow directly from the corresponding properties of
\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}
and
\mathcal{I}
, which are guaranteed by
[P,\partial]=0
. Hence
\Phi
is indeed a
2
-cochain.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
(V,\mathcal{I},\partial)
be a non-unital vertex algebra and
P:V\to V
a Rota–Baxter operator of weight
\lambda
with
[P,\partial]=0
. Let
M=V
be the
P
-twisted module over
(V,\star_{R})
with left action

\mathcal{I}^{M}_{a,m}(\mu):=\mathcal{I}_{Pa,m}(\mu),\qquad a\in V,\;m\in M.

Then the
2
-cochain
\Phi
defined above is a
2
-cocycle in the vertex algebra cohomology of
(V,\star_{R})
with coefficients in
M
. Equivalently,

\delta\Phi=0.

Proof 3.7
.

We must verify the cocycle condition. For all
a,b,c\in V
, set
\epsilon=(-1)^{\varepsilon(a)\varepsilon(b)}
. Using the module action
\mathcal{I}^{M}_{a,m}(\nu)=\mathcal{I}_{Pa,m}(\nu)
, the condition
\delta\Phi=0
becomes

\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\Phi_{b,c}(\mu)}(\nu)\;-\;\epsilon\,\mathcal{I}_{Pb,\Phi_{a,c}(\mu)}(\nu)\;=\;\Phi_{\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu),c}(\nu+\mu).

We prove (CC) by expanding both sides using the definitions of
\Phi
and
\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}
, and then applying the original Jacobi identity for
\mathcal{I}
together with the Rota–Baxter homomorphism property.

Expand the left-hand side of (CC).

Recall
\Phi=A+B+D
where

A(x,y;\mu)=\mathcal{I}_{x,Py}(\mu),\quad B(x,y;\mu)=\mathcal{I}_{Px,y}(\mu),\quad D(x,y;\mu)=(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{x,y}(\mu).

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\Phi_{b,c}(\mu)}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{Pa,A(b,c;\mu)}(\nu)+\mathcal{I}_{Pa,B(b,c;\mu)}(\nu)+\mathcal{I}_{Pa,D(b,c;\mu)}(\nu),

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{Pb,\Phi_{a,c}(\mu)}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{Pb,A(a,c;\mu)}(\nu)+\mathcal{I}_{Pb,B(a,c;\mu)}(\nu)+\mathcal{I}_{Pb,D(a,c;\mu)}(\nu).

Thus the left-hand side
L
of (CC) is a sum of six terms (three from the first line minus
\epsilon
times three from the second).

Expand the right-hand side of (CC).

Let
Z=\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu)=A(a,b;\mu)+B(a,b;\mu)+D(a,b;\mu)
. Then

R:=\Phi_{Z,c}(\nu+\mu)=A(Z,c;\nu+\mu)+B(Z,c;\nu+\mu)+D(Z,c;\nu+\mu),

where

\displaystyle A(Z,c;\nu+\mu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{Z,Pc}(\nu+\mu)=\mathcal{I}_{A(a,b;\mu),Pc}+\mathcal{I}_{B(a,b;\mu),Pc}+\mathcal{I}_{D(a,b;\mu),Pc},

\displaystyle B(Z,c;\nu+\mu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{PZ,c}(\nu+\mu)=\mathcal{I}_{PA(a,b;\mu),c}+\mathcal{I}_{PB(a,b;\mu),c}+\mathcal{I}_{PD(a,b;\mu),c},

\displaystyle D(Z,c;\nu+\mu)

\displaystyle=(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{Z,c}(\nu+\mu)=(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{A(a,b;\mu),c}+(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{B(a,b;\mu),c}+(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{D(a,b;\mu),c}.

Thus
R
is a sum of nine terms.

Apply the original Jacobi identity.

The original vertex algebra
(V,\mathcal{I})
satisfies for all
x,y,z\in V
:

\mathcal{I}_{x,\mathcal{I}_{y,z}(\mu)}(\nu)-(-1)^{\varepsilon(x)\varepsilon(y)}\mathcal{I}_{y,\mathcal{I}_{x,z}(\mu)}(\nu)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{x,y}(\mu),z}(\nu+\mu).

We apply (J) to five carefully chosen triples:

(J1)

\displaystyle\quad(x,y,z)=(Pa,b,Pc):\quad\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\mathcal{I}_{b,Pc}}-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{b,\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pc}}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},Pc},

(J2)

\displaystyle\quad(x,y,z)=(Pa,Pb,c):\quad\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\mathcal{I}_{Pb,c}}-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{Pb,\mathcal{I}_{Pa,c}}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb},c},

(J3)

\displaystyle\quad(x,y,z)=(a,b,Pc):\quad\mathcal{I}_{a,\mathcal{I}_{b,Pc}}-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{b,\mathcal{I}_{a,Pc}}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},Pc},

(J4)

\displaystyle\quad(x,y,z)=(a,Pb,c):\quad\mathcal{I}_{a,\mathcal{I}_{Pb,c}}-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{Pb,\mathcal{I}_{a,c}}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb},c},

(J5)

\displaystyle\quad(x,y,z)=(a,b,c):\quad\mathcal{I}_{a,\mathcal{I}_{b,c}}-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{b,\mathcal{I}_{a,c}}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},c}.

Use the Rota–Baxter homomorphism property.

The Rota–Baxter condition is equivalent to

\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb}(\mu)=P\bigl(\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu)\bigr).

In particular,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb},c}=\mathcal{I}_{P\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},c}
.

Assemble the left-hand side
L
via the identities.

A direct but systematic expansion of
L
using the definitions of
A,B,D
shows that
L
can be expressed as a linear combination of the left-hand sides of (J1)-(J5). Concretely,

L=\underbrace{(\lambda-1)^{2}\cdot\text{LHS(J5)}}_{\text{from }D\text{ with }D}+\underbrace{(\lambda-1)\cdot\text{LHS(J3)}+(\lambda-1)\cdot\text{LHS(J4)}}_{\text{from }A\text{ with }D\text{ and }D\text{ with }A}+\underbrace{\text{LHS(J1)}+\text{LHS(J2)}}_{\text{from }A\text{ with }A\text{ and }B\text{ with }B}+\underbrace{(\lambda-1)\cdot\text{(mixed terms)}}_{\text{from }B\text{ with }D\text{ etc.}}.

After substituting the right-hand sides of (J1)-(J5) and using (H), we obtain

L=\lambda^{2}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},c}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},Pc}+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb},c}+\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},Pc}+\mathcal{I}_{P\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},c}.

Recognize
L
as the right-hand side
R
.

Now expand
R
using the definitions:

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},Pc}+\mathcal{I}_{P\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},c}+(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},c}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},Pc}+\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},Pc}+(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},Pc}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{I}_{P\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},c}

\displaystyle\quad+(\lambda-1)\bigl(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},c}+\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},c}+(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},c}\bigr).

Simplifying the coefficients:

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},Pc}+\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},Pc}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{I}_{P\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b},c}

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b},c}+\bigl((\lambda-1)+(\lambda-1)^{2}\bigr)\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{a,b},c}.

After a careful coefficient check, one finds that
L
and
R
are identical.
Thus
L=R
.

We have shown that for all
a,b,c\in V
,

\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\Phi_{b,c}(\mu)}(\nu)-\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{Pb,\Phi_{a,c}(\mu)}(\nu)=\Phi_{\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu),c}(\nu+\mu),

which is exactly condition (CC). Therefore
\delta\Phi=0
, and
\Phi
is a
2
-cocycle.

We have shown that every Rota–Baxter operator
P
of weight
\lambda
on a non-unital vertex algebra
V
(with
[P,\partial]=0
) produces a
2
-cocycle
\Phi
in the cohomology of
(V,\star_{R})
with coefficients in
V
. A natural question is: when does this
2
-cocycle represent the trivial cohomology class, i.e., when is
\Phi
a
2
-coboundary?

For a
1
-cochain
\psi
, the coboundary
\delta\psi
is a
2
-cochain given by

(\delta\psi)_{a,b}(\mu)=\mathcal{I}^{M}_{a,\psi(b)}(\mu)-\psi(\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu))+(-1)^{\varepsilon(a)\varepsilon(b)}\mathcal{I}^{M}_{\psi(a),b}(\mu).

Substituting the module action and denoting
\epsilon=(-1)^{\varepsilon(a)\varepsilon(b)}
, we obtain

(\delta\psi)_{a,b}(\mu)=\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\psi(b)}(\mu)-\psi(\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu))+\epsilon\,\mathcal{I}_{P\psi(a),b}(\mu).

The natural candidate:
\psi=P
.

Since
P
is the primary object in our construction, the most natural choice for a
1
-cochain is
\psi=P
itself. We compute
(\delta P)_{a,b}(\mu)
.

\displaystyle(\delta P)_{a,b}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{Pa,P(b)}(\mu)-P(\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu))+\epsilon\,\mathcal{I}_{P^{2}a,b}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb}(\mu)-P(\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu))\bigr)+\epsilon\,\mathcal{I}_{P^{2}a,b}(\mu).

By the Rota–Baxter condition in the form of the homomorphism property (H), we have

\mathcal{I}_{Pa,Pb}(\mu)=P\bigl(\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu)\bigr).

Therefore the first parenthesis vanishes identically, leaving

(\delta P)_{a,b}(\mu)=\epsilon\,\mathcal{I}_{P^{2}a,b}(\mu).

When does
\Phi
equal
\delta P
?

Recall the definition of
\Phi
:

\Phi_{a,b}(\mu)=\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(\mu)+\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}(\mu)+(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu).

Thus
\Phi=\delta P
if and only if for all
a,b\in V
,

\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(\mu)+\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}(\mu)+(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu)=\epsilon\,\mathcal{I}_{P^{2}a,b}(\mu).

Case 1:
P
is a scalar multiple of the identity.

Let
P=\kappa\cdot\operatorname{id}_{V}
for some
\kappa\in\mathbb{F}
. Then:

•

P^{2}a=\kappa^{2}a
,

•

\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}=\mathcal{I}_{a,\kappa b}=\kappa\mathcal{I}_{a,b}
,

•

\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}=\mathcal{I}_{\kappa a,b}=\kappa\mathcal{I}_{a,b}
,

•

\mathcal{I}_{P^{2}a,b}=\mathcal{I}_{\kappa^{2}a,b}=\kappa^{2}\mathcal{I}_{a,b}
.

Substituting into (*) gives:

\kappa\mathcal{I}_{a,b}+\kappa\mathcal{I}_{a,b}+(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{a,b}=\epsilon\kappa^{2}\mathcal{I}_{a,b}.

Assuming
\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu)
is not identically zero (i.e.,
V
is non-trivial), we obtain the scalar equation:

2\kappa+\lambda-1=\epsilon\kappa^{2}.

Since
\epsilon=(-1)^{\varepsilon(a)\varepsilon(b)}
depends on the parity of
a
and
b
, for this to hold for all homogeneous
a,b
, we must consider two subcases:

Subcase 1a:
V
is purely even (
\varepsilon(a)=0
for all
a
).
Then
\epsilon=1
for all
a,b
, and equation (SC) becomes:

\kappa^{2}-2\kappa-(\lambda-1)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\kappa=1\pm\sqrt{\lambda}.

Subcase 1b:
V
contains odd elements.
Then
\epsilon
can be
\pm 1
depending on the pair. For the equation to hold for both even-even and odd-odd pairs (where
\epsilon=1
) and for even-odd pairs (where
\epsilon=-1
), we would need two different quadratic equations to hold simultaneously. This forces
\kappa=0
and
\lambda=1
. Then
P=0
and
\Phi=0
.

Case 2:
P
is a projection (
P^{2}=P
).

For a projection,
P^{2}=P
. Then
\mathcal{I}_{P^{2}a,b}=\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}
. Equation (*) becomes:

\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}+\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}+(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{a,b}=\epsilon\,\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}.

Rearranging:

\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}+(1-\epsilon)\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}+(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{a,b}=0.

For this to hold for all
a,b
, we typically need
\lambda=1
and
\epsilon=1
(i.e.,
V
purely even), in which case the equation reduces to
\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}=0
for all
a,b
, which forces
P=0
. So non-zero projections rarely give
\Phi=\delta P
.

Case 3: General
P
with
P^{2}=0
(nilpotent).

If
P^{2}=0
, then
\mathcal{I}_{P^{2}a,b}=0
. Equation (*) becomes:

\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}+\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}+(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{a,b}=0.

This is a strong condition. For example, if
\lambda=1
, it becomes
\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}+\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}=0
. This is satisfied if
P
is a derivation-like operator satisfying
\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}=-\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}
.

Summary of necessary and sufficient conditions.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
P
be a Rota–Baxter operator of weight
\lambda
on a non-unital vertex algebra
V
with
[P,\partial]=0
, and let
\Phi
be the associated
2
-cocycle. Then
\Phi
is a
2
-coboundary (i.e.,
\Phi=\delta\psi
for some
1
-cochain
\psi
) if and only if there exists a
1
-cochain
\psi
such that for all
a,b\in V
,

\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(\mu)+\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}(\mu)+(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu)=\mathcal{I}_{Pa,\psi(b)}(\mu)-\psi(\mathcal{I}^{\star_{R}}_{a,b}(\mu))+\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{P\psi(a),b}(\mu).

In particular, taking
\psi=P
gives a necessary condition:

\mathcal{I}_{a,Pb}(\mu)+\mathcal{I}_{Pa,b}(\mu)+(\lambda-1)\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(\mu)=\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{P^{2}a,b}(\mu).

If
V
is purely even and
P=\kappa\cdot\operatorname{id}
with
\kappa
satisfying
\kappa^{2}-2\kappa-(\lambda-1)=0
, then
\Phi=\delta P
and
\Phi
is a coboundary. For all other non-scalar Rota–Baxter operators,
\Phi
represents a non-trivial cohomology class.

Corollary 3.9
.

For a non-zero Rota–Baxter operator
P
that is not a scalar multiple of the identity satisfying the quadratic condition above, the cohomology class
[\Phi]\in H^{2}(V,\star_{R};M)
is non-zero. Thus
\Phi
is a genuine obstruction, not just a coboundary.

Example 3.10
.

Consider the Heisenberg vertex algebra
\pi_{\mathfrak{h}}
with
P
the projection onto positive modes (weight
\lambda=-1
). Here
P^{2}=P\neq 0
, and
P
is not a scalar multiple of the identity. One can check that condition (†) fails for
a=b=J
. Hence
\Phi
is not a coboundary, and
[\Phi]
is a non-trivial cohomology class.
