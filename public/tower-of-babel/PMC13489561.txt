Do the Modified Mediterranean and Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension Dietary Patterns Interact with Psychological Disorders to Affect Sleep Quality and Quantity? Yazd Health Study-Taghzieh Mardom-e-Yazd Study
Authors: Saeed Sherafatmanesh, Farzan Madadizadeh, Mahdieh Hosseinzadeh, Mohammad Mohammadi, Masoud Mirzaei, Hossein Khosravi-Boroujeni, Amin Salehi-Abargouei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The separate relationship between dietary food intake and psychiatric illnesses on individuals’ sleep has been discovered in previous investigations. However, it is yet unknown how these variables combine to affect the quantity and quality of sleep. Therefore, the purpose of the present cross-sectional study was to assess the possible interaction between Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) and modified Mediterranean (mMED) dietary patterns (DPs) and psychological ailments (PAs) on sleep quality and duration among Iranian adults, Yazd, Iran.Materials and Methods:Baseline data from a large Iranian cohort study (Yazd Health Study (YaHS)-Taghzieh Mardom-e-Yazd (TAMYZ)) were used in the current investigation. A validated food frequency questionnaire was utilized to obtain participants’ dietary consumption. The adherence level of the study DPs was calculated according to the earlier scientific literature. The depression, anxiety, and stress scale-21 questionnaire was employed to assess PAs. Additionally, gathering information regarding the individuals’ sleep quality and duration was done via a validated nocturnal sleep questionnaire and a short version of the Pittsburgh questionnaire. Finally, the multivariable adjusted binary logistic regression test was used to examine the interaction between DPs and PAs on sleep quality and duration.Results:Participants with the greatest adherence to the mMED DP and with severe depression had significantly lower odds of “low sleep quality” than those without depression signs and with the lowest adherence to the diet (P for interaction: 0.039).Conclusion:A mMED DP might help those with severe depression by improving sleep quality. Prospective investigations are needed to confirm this finding.

Introduction

“Sleep disorder” refers to a wide range of unpleasant symptoms, such as insomnia, hypersomnia, insufficient sleep, excessive daytime drowsiness, and circadian rhythm disruption.[1] It is known as a decisive aspect of unhealthy lifestyle behavior, which affects 5% to 40% of people in developed countries.[2] Apart from the negative physiological outcomes of sleep disorder, such as diabetes[3] and hypertension,[4] it is bidirectionally accompanied by a spectrum of deleterious effects on a person’s psychiatric state, including depression, anxiety, and psychological stress.[15] Based on findings of a large cohort investigation, the scientists revealed that depression and anxiety disorders are substantially connected to poor sleep habits.[6] While sleeping approximately 6 to 8 h/night is considered an appropriate duration for human psychological stability,[7] it has been documented that 30%–80% of people with psychological ailments (PAs) experience short sleep times within the critical phase of their disease.[8] Since these illnesses have exhibited detrimental effects on human general health, proper investigations regarding modifiable mediators seem to be a high priority.[9]

Dietary food intake, as an important modifiable lifestyle factor, has long been considered to be independently associated with the risk of both PAs and sleep disturbances.[710] For instance, anxiety and depression have been linked to the dietary consumption of omega-3 fatty acids,[11] vitamin B6,[12] iron,[13] fish,[14] and vegetables.[15] On the other hand, it has been found that those with sleep difficulties are more prone to consume energy-dense meals, sugar, caffeine, confectionery foods, and fewer portions of vegetables, fish, whole grains, and beans.[71617] Globally, the Mediterranean diet and the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) are known as the most prominent high-quality priori dietary patterns (DPs).[18] The numerous physiological health advantages related to both of these DPs have been established in earlier investigations.[1920212223] Moreover, there is convincing evidence suggesting that higher adherence to the modified Mediterranean (mMED) and DASH DPs is associated with a reduced probability of PAs.[24252627] Concurrently, it has been distinctly reported that adherence to these well-known priori DPs was linked to the population’s sleep quality and duration.[2829]

Given the importance of bidirectional associations between stress, anxiety, and depression with sleep status, as well as the substantial role of dietary food intakes on sleep status and the occurrence of PAs, we assumed that there might be a possible interaction between mMED and DASH DPs and PAs on sleep quality and quantity. Therefore, the present investigation aimed to study this hypothesis in a large sample of adults living in Yazd, Iran.

Methods and Materials

Study setting and participants

The current cross-sectional investigation was conducted on 9962 individuals aged 20-70 who participated in the recruitment phase of the Yazd Health Study (YaHS), a large prospective cohort study in Yazd, Iran. The exclusion criteria in the present study were as follows: participants with incomplete sleep duration data or who failed to respond to more than 70 Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) questions (n = 850), unfathomable energy intake (<800 kcal/d or >6000 kcal/d) (n = 864), reported sleeping time of less than 3 h or more than 12 h each night (n = 250), pregnancy (n = 104), starting drug therapy for clinical symptoms associated with PAs (n = 91), and history of chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancer (n = 1719). Overall, 6084 adults were eligible for the study analysis. All individuals in the YaHS and TAMYZ studies were informed about the study purposes and signed the written consent form. Additionally, the procedure of this study was approved by the ethics committee of Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences, Yazd, Iran (ethics code: IR.SSU.SPH.REC.1400.111).

Evaluation of dietary food intake

All data regarding the dietary intake of YaHS participants were obtained by skilled assessors via a validated semiquantitative FFQ[30] (also known as Taghzieh Mardom-e-Yazd [TAMYZ] in Persian). The YaHS-TAMYZ study’s methodology is covered in detail in other research.[31] Respondents were requested to indicate a) their typical frequency of consumption of each food item during the last 12 months and b) a general portion size for each food item based on five options of Iranians’ typical serving sizes. Finally, the gathered information was then converted to grams per day via Iranian household measures.[32] The assessment of the energy and nutrient intakes was carried out using the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) food composition database.[33]

Adherence to the modified Mediterranean diet

In the current investigation, the mMED diet score was determined for each subject based on an earlier study by Trichopoulou et al.[34] The adherence score of this DP was calculated based on the median intake of nine food items, including fruits, vegetables, legumes, nuts, whole grains, seafood, red meat, dairy products, and the ratio of monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) to saturated fatty acids (SFAs). A score of 0 was assigned to consumption amounts greater than the median for meat and dairy products, and a score of 1 was given to those who consumed these food groups less than the median amount. However, a reverse scoring process was employed for the items, including whole grains, vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, seafood, and MUFA to SFA ratio. In total, the mMED diet adherence score ranged from 0 to 9.

Adherence to the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension diet

DASH DP scoring was performed according to the scale that was discovered by Fung et al.[35] The DP adherence score was measured by considering eight target dietary food groups, including fruits, vegetables, nuts and legumes, low-fat dairy products, whole grains, sodium, sweetened beverages, and red and processed meats. The participants were divided into quintiles based on the consumption amount of each of the mentioned food items and received a specific score from 1 to 5. Overall, the higher intake of fruits, vegetables, nuts and legumes, low-fat dairy products, and whole grains, along with less intake of sodium, sweetened beverages, and red and processed meat were assigned a greater score. Finally, the DASH adherence score for each individual was measured by summing the scores, which were between 8 and 40.

Sleep assessment

Sleep quantity

The routine sleep duration of the study adults was obtained via a self-reported nocturnal sleep questionnaire, which divided the participants into tertiles (top tertile: >8 h/night, lowest tertile: <5 h/night) according to their responses. Then, based on a previous experiment regarding the relationship between diet and sleep,[36] we classified sleep duration as a) insufficient sleep (<5 h vs. ≥5 h), b) short sleep duration (<5 h vs. 5-8 h), and c) long sleep duration (>8 h vs. 5–8 h).

Sleep quality

All information regarding participants’ sleep quality was evaluated using a validated Iranian short version of the Pittsburgh questionnaire with a score range of 0–11.[37] According to its specific scoring process,[38] the sleep quality was classified into four main features: (I) delay in falling sleep, (II) sleep disorders, (III) use of medication for sleep, and (IV) sleep quantity.

Assessment of psychological profile

The validated questionnaire of the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) was utilized to assess the indicators of study PAs.[39] The DASS-21 consists of seven questions for depression, anxiety, and stress disorders, which are scored for each question from 0 (did not relate to me at all – never) to 3 (exactly represented my experience over the last week). Thus, it can be expected that a higher score summation reflects the advancement level of PAs. Depending on the participants’ responses, they were divided into five categories: “no illness,” “mild,” “moderate,” “severe,” and “extremely severe”.[40] Finally, all participants were assigned to two main groups as follows: those with “no or mild or moderate PAs indicators” and those with “severe and extremely severe PAs indicators.”

Evaluating other study variables

A self-administered questionnaire was employed to obtain the participants’ demographic data, such as age, sex, job status, education level, smoking status, marital state, homeownership status, history of chronic diseases (including high cholesterol levels, hypertension, asthma, Alzheimer’s disease, thyroid dysfunction, osteoporosis, lung disease, arthritis, hepatitis, renal failure, kidney stone, fatty liver, epilepsy, chronic headache, rheumatic arthritis, psychiatric disorder, and multiple sclerosis), and average time of watching television (TV)/movie and mobile use per day. Body weight (kg) was calculated via an accurate body analyzer (Omron BF511, Omron Inc., Nagoya, Japan) with minimum coverage and no shoes. Height (cm) was measured in a standard position without shoes via a nonstretchable tape to the nearest 0.5 cm. Body mass index (BMI) was measured as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m2). Furthermore, the short version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ)[41] was used for assessing the individuals’ physical activity.

Statistical analysis

Based on the score of adherence to each of the investigated DPs, participants were divided into quintiles in the DASH and tertiles in the mMED DPs and were defined as a qualitative variable in subsequent analyses. The histogram and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test were utilized to evaluate the normal distribution of the variables. Analysis of variance was used to compare continuous variables, and the Chi-square test was employed to compare categorical variables across the levels of adherence to the DASH and mMED DPs. Additionally, analysis of covariance with Bonferroni correction was used to compare age-, sex-, and energy-adjusted dietary food groups and nutrient intakes across quintiles of DASH and tertiles of mMED diet scores. Moreover, the interaction between the quintiles of DASH and tertiles of mMED DPs and PAs on sleep quality and quantity was assessed using a multivariable adjusted binary logistic regression.

The total statistical examinations regarding the interaction assessment were adjusted for sex, age, BMI, house ownership status, education status, job status, occupation, smoking status, marital status, chronic diseases, total energy intake, physical activity, duration of mobile phone use, duration of TV/movie watching, and tea and coffee consumption. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (version 22.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). P < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Results

A total of 6084 people between the ages of 20 and 70 were enrolled in the current investigation when the qualifying criteria were fulfilled (3097 men and 2987 women).

General characteristics

According to the quintiles of DASH and tertiles of mMED diet scores, the participants’ general characteristics are presented in Table 1. The highest adherence of DASH diet was significantly associated with higher education state, home ownership, and lower amounts of physical activity, watching television and movies, and depression score (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the top tertile of mMED DP was more likely to be male, with higher weight and education, home ownership, and lower rates of smoking, sleep time, anxiety, and depression scores (P < 0.05).

According to Table 3, severe stress was associated with higher odds of “short sleep duration” (P = 0.019, odds ratio [OR] = 2.27, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.14-4.52), “long sleep duration” (P = 0.015, OR = 2.36, 95% CI: 1.18–4.71), and “insufficient sleep” (P = 0.042, OR = 2.01, 95% CI: 1.02–3.93) in participants with the lowest adherence to the DASH DP. Moreover, severe stress was related to the upper chance of “short sleep duration” (P = 0.034, OR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.06-4.1) and “insufficient sleep” (P = 0.047, OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.01–3.82) in individuals in the lowest tertile of the mMED DP.

Based on Table 4, it was detected that severe anxiety was associated with a higher chance of “sleep disorders” in participants with the lowest adherence to DASH (P < 0.001, OR = 11.48, 95% CI: 5.23–25.19) and mMED (P < 0.001, OR = 13.24, 95% CI: 6.6–26.52) DPs. Moreover, severe depression was observed to be accompanied by higher odds of “sleep disorders” in those with the lowest adherence to the DASH (P < 0.001, OR = 9.66, 95% CI: 3.89–24.00) and mMED (P < 0.001, OR = 9.1, 95% CI: 3.93–21.09) DPs. Moreover, severe stress was associated with a higher risk for “sleep disorders” in individuals with the lowest adherence to DASH (P < 0.001, OR = 8.88, 95% CI: 3.60-21.92) and mMED DPs (P < 0.001, OR = 7.02, 95% CI: 2.94-16.79). Furthermore, it was shown that severe anxiety, depression, and stress were significantly related to the higher chances of “had to use medication for sleep” and “delay in falling asleep” in participants with the lowest adherence to each of the study DPs (all P < 0.05).

As Table 5 depicts, it was discovered that severe anxiety, depression, and stress were connected to a higher risk of “low sleep quality” in participants with the lowest adherence to each DASH and mMED DPs (all P < 0.05). However, participants without signs of severe anxiety, depression, and stress who were in the top quintile of the DASH DP were significantly associated with lower odds of “low sleep quality” (all P < 0.05). A reduced risk of “low sleep quality” was seen in severe depression participants with the highest adherence to the mMED DP (P = 0.039, OR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.05–0.92). In addition, it was detected that those in the highest adherence to the mMED DP and with severe depression had a significantly lower risk of “low sleep quality” in comparison with individuals without depression and with the lowest adherence to the diet (P for interaction: 0.039). No other interaction was found between the study DPs and PAs to affect sleep quality and duration.

Discussion

Findings of the present population-based cross-sectional investigation revealed an interaction between higher adherence to the mMED DP and severe depression on the risk of participants’ “low sleep quality.” To our knowledge, this is the first effort to evaluate the interaction between a priori DPs and PAs on human sleep indicators.

There are convincing reports in relation to the bidirectional link between dietary eating components and sleep. In 2021, a systematic review investigation reported a favorable association between healthy food habits and improved sleep quality. Moreover, the researchers suggested that consumption of processed and free-sugar-rich foods may result in sleep difficulties.[42] Additionally, it has been documented that the consumption of specific nutrients may stimulate various hormonal signaling pathways, leading to alterations in sleep quality and duration.[4344] On the other hand, it has been confirmed that unfavorable sleep quality, together with impaired sleep duration, can affect dietary eating behavior.[28] According to the findings of a systematic review and meta-analysis study, sleep restriction may influence diet by increasing the consumption of energy from macronutrients.[45]

Over the past few decades, DASH and Mediterranean diet styles have become widely accepted DPs as a result of their convincing proof of their health advantages.[4647] According to earlier investigations, a positive association has been established between these popular healthy vegetable-rich DPs and suitable sleep quality.[282948] In 2017, an investigation on older adults showed that higher adherence to a Mediterranean DP was linked to greater sleep quality, coupled with a decreased likelihood of variations in sleep duration.[29] Moreover, Godos et al. described a higher sleep quality following a Mediterranean DP in Italian adults.[48] Likewise, in 2020, Liang et al. suggested the health advantages of the DASH DP in relation to sleep duration and quality.[28] In general, the total dietary intake of protein, fiber, magnesium, and potassium is accepted as a number of well-known beneficial nutrients in both DASH and Mediterranean DPs, which have been assumed to impact sleep parameters.[4849] One cross-sectional investigation has indicated a direct association between the amount of dietary fiber intake and sleep quality indicators.[50] Similarly, a positive relationship has been found regarding the dietary intake of magnesium and sleep duration in both young and older women.[51] It appeared that magnesium may act as a regulatory factor in the circadian cycle. It may also promote sleep through the synthesis of melatonin.[5253] In addition, the Mediterranean diet comprises seafood, nuts, and seeds, which can increase the availability of tryptophan and thus enhance serotonin synthesis as a promoter agent for sleep.[5455]

On the other hand, mutual relations have been discovered between PAs and sleep parameters.[256] In one study, the researchers observed higher symptoms of depression and anxiety in suboptimal sleepers (those sleeping less than 6 or 8.5 or more h/night) compared to subjects reporting an average of 6 h or <8.5 h/night.[57] Meanwhile, diet as a modifiable lifestyle behavior may have an impact on processes in the human brain that contribute to the development of PAs, such as synaptic plasticity, membrane fluidity, and the regulation of neurotransmitter production.[58] It has been established that a higher adherence to the Mediterranean DP is related to mental and psychological profile well-being.[48] Given that inflammation and oxidative stress have been suggested to be potential factors in the development of depression and psychological stress, the high content of antioxidant agents and beneficial dietary fats such as omega-3 fatty acids or virgin olive oil in Mediterranean DP may support the useful effects of this DP.[24] In addition, improvements in other possible risk factors, including insulin sensitivity, vascular inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and metabolic syndrome, have been suggested through a high adherence to the Mediterranean DP, which in turn may diminish the risk of PAs.[4859]

The strengths of the present investigation include the analysis of an extensive population and the adjustments for a broad spectrum of sociodemographic and clinical data. However, it is important to consider the main limitations of the investigation. The cross-sectional design of this project precludes the inference of causation and temporal effect. In addition, biases, including measurement error, misclassification, recall, and social desirability, are all possibilities when using FFQs.

Conclusion

The observations of the current investigation disclosed a reverse interaction between the highest adherence to the mMED DP and severe depression on the risk of “low sleep quality.” Further prospective and experimental design studies are required to elucidate the rectitude of our results.
