Spiral volumetric modulated arc therapy enhances peak-to-valley dose contrast for 3D-GRID radiotherapy compared to VMAT and tomotherapy
Authors: Wu Weiwei, Zhiwei Liu, Li Weizhi, Li Zhenghuan, Dai Zhitao, Wang Chuangao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThree-dimensional GRID radiotherapy for bulky tumours requires high peak dose while effectively preserving a low-dose intratumoural valley, but this remains challenging with current linac-based techniques. Spiral volumetric modulated arc therapy (sVMAT) is an integrated delivery technique with synchronized longitudinal couch motion and gantry rotation that generates a spiral/helical beam trajectory, combining key advantages of volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) and helical tomotherapy (TOMO). This study evaluated the dosimetric feasibility of sVMAT for single-fraction 3D-GRID radiotherapy and compared it with conventional VMAT and TOMO.MethodsTwenty patients with bulky tumours were retrospectively replanned. Cylindrical GRID peak structures with a diameter of 1.0 cm were generated within the GTV using a uniform 0.5–1.0 cm retraction from the GTV boundary and a 1.0 cm clearance from adjacent OARs; cylinders violating these spatial constraints were automatically truncated or excised. A single fraction of 18 Gy was prescribed to the virtual GRID peaks only, while dose to the intervening non-peak GTV valley was minimized as low as reasonably achievable rather than intentionally prescribed. For each patient, three plans were individually generated: VMAT, TOMO and sVMAT. sVMAT plans were created on the NeuRT Aurora platform using DeepPlan with synchronized gantry rotation and bidirectional couch translation. Dosimetric comparisons included gradient index (GI), peak-to-valley dose ratio using two definitions (PVDR1=D10%/D90% and PVDR2=D2%/D50%), and OAR sparing. Delivery efficiency was evaluated using monitor units (MU) and beam-on time.ResultssVMAT showed the strongest overall dosimetric performance. sVMAT achieved the steepest dose gradient, with a lower GI than VMAT and TOMO (8.86 ± 2.00 vs. 14.93 ± 5.18 and 15.07 ± 5.97; both p < 0.001). PVDR1 was numerically highest with sVMAT (5.137 ± 2.325), compared with VMAT (4.692 ± 2.213) and TOMO (3.963 ± 2.288). PVDR2 was also highest with sVMAT (2.975 ± 0.470 vs. 2.604 ± 0.464 and 2.575 ± 0.567, respectively). All three techniques met single-fraction OAR constraints without clinically meaningful differences. Both MU and beam-on time for sVMAT were intermediate between VMAT and TOMO, with VMAT showing the lowest values and TOMO the highest; mean beam-on time was 1181 ± 512 s for sVMAT, versus 307 ± 30 s for VMAT and 1896 ± 1224 s for TOMO.ConclusionFor cylindrical 3D-GRID radiotherapy, sVMAT provided superior dose confinement and enhanced peak-to-valley dose contrast, while maintaining OAR sparing comparable to VMAT and TOMO. These findings support sVMAT as a promising platform for modern high-contrast 3D-GRID radiotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16337-z.Keywords: Spatially fractionated radiotherapy, 3D-GRID, sVMAT, Volumetric modulated arc therapy, Helical tomotherapy

Introduction

Spatially fractionated radiotherapy (SFRT) has been employed for decades as an effective modality to debulk massive, locally advanced, and radioresistant tumors [1–3]. Historically, conventional 2D GRID radiotherapy delivered highly inhomogeneous doses using collimated fields with cerrobend-alloy or brass physical blocks [4]. These blocks restricted radiation into a checkerboard or honeycomb pattern, generating localized high-dose “peaks” through open portions and low-dose “valleys” beneath shielded areas. The peak to valley dose ratio (PVDR) is widely recognized as a critical parameter governing the therapeutic efficacy of SFRT [5, 6]. This extreme spatial dose modulation bypasses normal tissue tolerance and triggers biological responses including tumor hypoxia relief, endothelial apoptosis, and systemic anti-tumor immunity [7].

Despite its clinical efficacy, traditional 2D GRID therapy utilizing physical blocks or static multileaf collimator (MLC) patterns lacked 3D conformality. For large, deep-seated tumors, this approach often resulted in unacceptable normal tissue toxicity due to large entrance and exit dose tracks extending beyond the target. Consequently, modern SFRT has evolved into sophisticated 3D-GRID and Lattice Radiotherapy (LRT) [8, 9]. By leveraging MLC-based virtual GRID blocks (VGB) and advanced treatment planning systems (TPS), ablative doses can now be precisely confined to highly modulated sub-volumes entirely within the gross tumor volume (GTV).

Modern 3D-GRID aims to replicate classic standalone GRID by prescribing an ablative dose to virtual cylinders while intentionally sparing the surrounding tumor valley [10–15]. However, recreating the absolute “cold valley” of a physical block using linear accelerators remains a formidable technical challenge. While volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) and Helical Tomotherapy (TOMO) have been utilized for 3D-GRID [16, 17], they face inherent physical limitations. VMAT offers rapid delivery but suffers from cumulative MLC transmission leakage when covering massive targets with a single isocenter, which artificially elevates the valley baseline [18]. Conversely, TOMO accommodates extended targets, although dynamic jaw technology can reduce the longitudinal dose-tail effect at the cranial and caudal target edges, residual longitudinal dose spread may still occur in highly modulated plans, leading to unwanted spillage [19].

Recently, a novel radiotherapy platform, the NeuRT Aurora (Neusoft IntelliRay Technology, Shenyang, China), was developed to enable VMAT delivery with simultaneous longitudinal couch movement. This system features a 6-MV flattening-filter-free (FFF) linac mounted on a ring gantry for continuous rotation, paired with a 122-leaf dual-layer MLC for dynamic beam shaping [20]. This design conceptually and mathematically integrates the helical delivery pattern of TOMO with the modulation flexibility of VMAT, generating a spiral beam trajectory termed spiral volumetric modulated arc therapy (sVMAT). By incorporating a moving couch into VMAT delivery, the NeuRT Aurora system substantially expands the degrees of freedom for plan optimization. This study is the first to systematically compare sVMAT against VMAT and TOMO for single-fraction 18 Gy 3D-GRID, testing whether its spiral trajectory and dual-layer MLC can overcome the valley-elevation limitations of existing techniques. By systematically comparing its dosimetric performance against conventional VMAT and TOMO, the primary objective is to determine whether sVMAT can overcome existing physical bottlenecks to achieve steeper dose gradients, maintain a colder valley, and maximize the PVDR, while ensuring strict organ-at-risk (OAR) sparing.

Methods

Patient selection and target delineation

We retrospectively enrolled 20 patients with bulky tumors previously treated at our institution. Tumor sites included the shoulder (n = 1), chest (n = 2), lung (n = 4), abdomen (n = 3), liver (n = 5), and pelvis (n = 5). Utilizing simulation CT scans fused with diagnostic MR/PET images, senior radiation oncologists delineated the GTV and adjacent OARs. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Ganzhou Cancer Hospital (Approval No. 2025-38). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective and observational nature of the dosimetric analysis.

GRID generation

The high-dose GRID targets were automatically generated using the smART Vision Pro V1.3.0 software (Shenying Medical Technology Co., Ltd., China). Through Boolean intersection operations, cylindrical GRID peak structures with a diameter of 1.0 cm were generated and strictly confined within the GTV. Specifically, the cylinders were generated with a uniform 1.0 cm inward retraction from the GTV boundary and mandated to maintain a 1.0 cm spatial clearance from all adjacent OARs. Cylinders violating these spatial constraints were automatically truncated or excised. Following automated generation, treating physicians manually reviewed and adjusted the structures to ensure a strict safety margin between complex OARs and the ablative regions (Fig. 1). Although spherical vertices are often used in lattice radiotherapy [21], 1.0 cm cylindrical GRID peaks were adopted to approximate classic physical-block and virtual GRID-block geometry. To minimize geometric bias, identical cylinder structures were used for VMAT, TOMO, and sVMAT plans in each patient. As detailed in Table 1, the GTV for the enrolled cohort ranged from 151.3 to 2019.8 cc (mean: 662.6 ± 585.0 cc), and the total volume of the generated GRID cylinders ranged from 2.6 to 52.3 cc (mean: 14.6 ± 14.3 cc). Consequently, the percentage of GTV occupied by GRID cylinders (GRID/GTV ratio) was geometrically constrained to a mean of 2.2 ± 0.5. This standardization provided a consistent geometric baseline for dosimetric comparison across platforms.

Treatment planning

A standard single-fraction 3D-GRID strategy was implemented to reflect classic SFRT practices. An ablative dose of 18 Gy was prescribed exclusively to the virtual GRID cylinders (D95%>18 Gy) in a single fraction, while the non-peak GTV valley region was not assigned a prescription dose and was minimized as low as reasonably achievable. VMAT, TOMO, and sVMAT plans were generated using the same clinical planning goals and priority hierarchy. Due to differing optimizer architectures across the three systems, platform-specific settings were iteratively adjusted to achieve the best possible plan for each technique. For VMAT and sVMAT, NTOs were enabled and tuned alongside platform-specific avoidance and dose-falloff controls. Inverse optimization across all platforms strictly prioritized OAR sparing while maintaining prescribed GRID peak coverage, no robust optimization was performed.

VMAT plans

Plans were generated using the Eclipse treatment planning system TPS (v15.6, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) for a VitalBeam linac. Utilizing a 6-MV flattening-filter-free (FFF) beam with a maximum dose rate of 1400 MU/min and enabled jaw tracking, 3 to 5 full or partial arcs were deployed depending on GTV geometry. To maximize beam intersection at the cylindrical targets and enhance modulation, collimator angles were distributed evenly starting from 00 with 450 increments [22]. Final dose calculation was performed using the clinically commissioned Acuros XB (AXB) algorithm.

TOMO plans

Plans were optimized using the Accuray Precision TPS (v3.5.0.3, Accuray, Madison, WI, USA) for a TOMO C system. A 6-MV FFF beam (850 MU/min) was used with a 2.5 cm dynamic jaw width, a modulation factor of 4.0–4.5, and a pitch of 0.12. Optimization was performed under the “High” resolution setting. Iterative optimization was conducted to satisfy OAR constraints, maintain GRID peak coverage, and minimize dose to the intervening GTV valley region. Final dose calculation was performed using the clinically commissioned convolution-superposition algorithm.

sVMAT plans

Plans were designed for the NeuRT Aurora accelerator (Neusoft IntelliRay Technology, Shenyang, China) using the DeepPlan TPS (v1.3, Wisdom Tech., Hefei, China). Modulation was achieved via continuous bidirectional couch translation synchronized with gantry rotation. The plan structure comprised “couch-in” arcs (collimator 00, 2 rotations) and “couch-out” arcs (collimator 900, 6–8 rotations). This asymmetric couch-in/couch-out arrangement was used to increase modulation freedom under the restricted longitudinal field width, with the couch-out arcs providing additional modulation for valley-dose suppression and OAR sparing. The final number of couch-out rotations was individualized according to target length and complexity until GRID peak coverage, OAR constraints, and valley-dose objectives were simultaneously satisfied. To optimize modulation efficiency, the maximum field width in the longitudinal direction was strictly restricted to 8.0 cm. The DeepOPT direct aperture optimization (DAO) algorithm was employed. Final dose calculation was performed using a Monte Carlo algorithm.

Dosimetric and delivery parameter analysis

Dose-volume histogram (DVH) data for the GRID, GTV and OARs were extracted for quantitative analysis.

Target dosimetric evaluation

For the peak-dose GRID targets, the evaluated metrics included D5% (Gy), Dmax (Gy), V110%, Dmean(Gy), conformity index (CI), and gradient index (GI). For the valley-dose GTV, the metrics included Dmean, D2%(Gy), D10%(Gy), D50% (Gy), D90%(Gy), and D98%(Gy). To rigorously quantify the spatial dose heterogeneity fundamental to SFRT, the PVDR was calculated using two distinct definitions: PVDR1 (the ratio of D10% to D90% within the GTV) [23], and PVDR2 (the ratio of D2% to D50% within the GTV) [24]. The GI assessing dose fall-off outside the GRID, was calculated as shown in Eq. 1 [25] (where V50% and V100% are the volumes receiving 50% and 100% of the prescription dose, respectively). Lower GI values indicate a steeper dose gradient. The ablative volume fraction (AVF) was defined as the percentage of the GTV volume (VGTV, cm3) receiving 100% of the prescription dose (V100%, cm3), reflecting the focus of the radiotherapy dose, calculated as shown in Eq. 2 [26, 27].

OAR analysis

OAR evaluation was based on functional subunit architecture. Near-maximum dose (D0.01CC) were recorded for serial organs (spinal cord, esophagus, etc.), whereas mean doses (Dmean) were evaluated for parallel organs (heart, liver, lungs, kidneys). OARs dose constraints adhered strictly to AAPM TG-101 recommendations and Timmerman criteria for 1-fraction SBRT [28, 29].

Delivery efficiency assessment

Delivery efficiency was assessed using total MUs and beam-on time. For VMAT, beam-on time was obtained from actual delivery on the clinical VitalBeam linac. For TOMO and sVMAT, beam-on time was calculated by the corresponding TPS from the finalized deliverable plans, rather than measured from physical delivery. Linear regression analyses were performed to correlate these efficiency parameters with the GTV volume.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (v25.0, IBM Corp., USA), with the Shapiro-Wilk test determining the distribution of continuous variables. The statistical workflow was re-examined after peer review to ensure consistency with the paired, within-patient, three-technique design. Normally distributed metrics (mean ± SD) were compared using repeated-measures ANOVA, and sphericity violations detected by Mauchly’s test were addressed via Greenhouse-Geisser correction. Non-normal data (median [IQR]) were evaluated using the Friedman test. To control the family-wise error rate, significant main effects were followed by Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons, with statistical significance defined as a two-sided P < 0.05.

Results

GRID dose metrics

Under the 18 Gy single-fraction regimen, sVMAT delivered the highest GRID peak doses (D2%: 21.44 ± 0.98 Gy; Dmax: 23.52 ± 0.95 Gy) and the largest target coverage (V110%: 59.18 ± 7.90%), significantly exceeding VMAT (all p < 0.001) while remaining statistically comparable to TOMO (p > 0.05). This indicates vastly superior dose gradient control compared to both VMAT (GI: 14.93 ± 5.18) and TOMO (GI: 15.07 ± 5.97) (p < 0.001). GTV dose metrics

For the GTV valley region, where no intentional dose was prescribed, sVMAT consistently demonstrated the strongest ability to maintain a “cold” valley bed. Specifically, sVMAT achieved the lowest D90% (2.84 ± 1.45 Gy) and D95% (1.68 ± 1.41 Gy) within the GTV compared to TOMO (p < 0.05), reflecting superior valley sparing. Consequently, the spatial dose heterogeneity, parameterized by PVDR1 and PVDR2 was highest for sVMAT. The PVDR2 for sVMAT reached 2.975 ± 0.470, significantly outperforming both VMAT and TOMO (p < 0.001), confirming an enhanced peak-to-valley contrast. Furthermore, the AVF remained highest with sVMAT (4.429 ± 0.917%), significantly exceeding the other two conventional platforms (p < 0.001).

OAR dose metrics

All three platforms successfully met the predefined single-fraction OAR dose constraints for single-fraction SBRT without clinically meaningful differences (Table 3). The mean dose (Dmean) to the lungs for sVMAT was 1.71 ± 1.09 Gy, which was comparable to VMAT (1.51 ± 1.02 Gy) and TOMO (1.67 ± 0.99 Gy) (P = 0.083). Similarly, Dmean of the heart, liver minus GTV, and kidneys showed no significant differences across the three platforms (all P > 0.05).

Delivery Efficiency

Delivery efficiency differed markedly across the three planning platforms (Table 2). VMAT showed the shortest beam-on time (307 ± 30 s) and the lowest MU consumption (5437 ± 991). TOMO showed the longest estimated beam-on time (1896 ± 1224 s) and the highest MU values (26796 ± 17304). sVMAT occupied an intermediate position, with an estimated beam-on time of 1181 ± 512 s. Linear regression analyses (Fig. 3) revealed that sVMAT beam-on time exhibited a strong positive correlation with GTV volume, reflecting its volume-dependent scalability, whereas VMAT showed minimal volume dependence due to its single-isocenter, large-field capability.

Discussion

SFRT exploits extreme spatial dose modulation to overcome radioresistance in massive tumors [30], yet the clinical efficacy of this technique is fundamentally limited by the delivery system’s ability to sculpt high-magnitude peaks without flooding the surrounding healthy tissue. The primary challenge lies in maximizing the PVDR while strictly confining the ablative dose. This study demonstrates that sVMAT is technically feasible for 3D-GRID. It also outperforms VMAT and TOMO in valley coldness, dose gradient, and PVDR.

Dosimetric analysis confirms that sVMAT converts its modulation flexibility into superior target coverage and spatial confinement at 18 Gy. Generating a high dose peak is clinically insufficient if the radiation heavily spills into the valley. In our setup, no intentional dose was prescribed to the GTV valley to mimic the traditional physical-block GRID effect. Under this condition, maintaining an ideal ‘cold’ valley bed is a rigorous test of a linac’s physical limitations. VMAT struggles with this due to cumulative transmission leakage across large target volumes treated with a single isocenter, which artificially elevates the valley baseline. Conversely, sVMAT resolved this bottleneck. By leveraging a 5 mm dual-layer MLC with a remarkably low leaf transmission rate of 0.5%, paired with a spiral trajectory, sVMAT recorded a valley D95% of only 1.68 ± 1.41 Gy. This demonstrates that sVMAT can closely mimic the “cold valley” characteristic of conventional 2D physical blocks, but with modern 3D conformality.

The quantification of spatial heterogeneity warrants critical examination. Historically, PVDR1 (D10%/D90%) has been used to assess GRID plans. However, in standalone 3D-GRID plans without intentional valley dosing, D90% may approach very low values, making PVDR1 sensitive to denominator instability and minor cold-spot variations. In addition, D10% may be influenced by dose spill from the high-dose peaks into the surrounding valley region, particularly in plans with a larger GI and slower dose fall-off [31]. Therefore, PVDR1 may overestimate peak-to-valley contrast when peak-dose spillage elevates the upper portion of the GTV dose-volume histogram. To complement PVDR1, PVDR2 (D2%/D50%) was used as an additional DVH-based descriptor of spatial dose contrast. Although Fig. 4 alone does not visually demonstrate uniformly tighter dispersion for PVDR2 across all platforms, the numerical results in Table 2 showed lower standard deviations and coefficients of variation for PVDR2 than for PVDR1. For example, in TOMO plans, PVDR1 was 3.963 ± 2.288, whereas PVDR2 was 2.575 ± 0.567. Therefore, PVDR2 was interpreted as a complementary, less denominator-unstable descriptor of peak-to-valley contrast, while PVDR1 was retained to maintain comparability with previous GRID studies. Using this complementary metric, sVMAT achieved the highest PVDR2 among the three techniques (2.975 ± 0.470). Together with its lower GI (8.86 ± 2.00), this finding suggests that sVMAT may provide improved spatial confinement and peak-to-valley separation compared with VMAT and TOMO in the present 3D-GRID planning setting.

The physical mechanisms underlying these dosimetric differences warrant specific attention. Covering bulky tumors with a single isocenter reduces the modulation capacity of a standard VMAT MLC [18]. With MLC leaf transmission typically ranging from 1.5% to 2.0% [32, 33], prolonged operation at modulation limits leads to cumulative leakage that gradually elevates the baseline valley dose and compromises peak-to-valley contrast. This limitation compromises dose fall-off and elevates the GI within the GTV. Furthermore, TOMO theoretically accommodates extended targets using its continuous couch motion. Yet, the constant longitudinal field width used in fixed-jaw TOMO delivery is associated with a craniocaudal dose-tail effect, which can be reduced but not completely eliminated by dynamic jaw technology [34]. In addition, the binary MLC has a leaf width of 6.25 mm at isocenter, which is larger than that of many conventional MLCs. This, combined with the high MUs typical of TOMO plans, may increase low-dose spread and limit its ability to maintain sharply separated peak and valley regions in 3D-GRlD planning. These physical constraints limit its dosimetric performance in SFRT.

In contrast, sVMAT physically resolves both bottlenecks by integrating the continuous spiral trajectory of TOMO with the dynamic dual-layer MLC of VMAT. The dual-layer MLC configuration features a 5 mm leaf width with a remarkably low leaf transmission rate of 0.5% [35]. This minimizes the cumulative leakage that typically elevates valley doses. Furthermore, our sVMAT planning protocol employed alternating collimator angles (0° and 90°) between the couch-in and couch-out arcs. Coupled with a strictly limited maximum field width of 8.0 cm in the couch moving direction, this kinematic setup maximizes modulation freedom. It effectively restricts beam divergence, sharpens the penumbra around the GRID cylinders, and largely eliminates the dose tail effect inherent to TOMO based radiotherapy.

Delivery efficiency remains a critical consideration for clinical implementation. VMAT showed the shortest beam-on time at approximately 307 s, whereas sVMAT required an intermediate duration of approximately 1180 s and TOMO required the longest duration of approximately 1896 s. It should be noted that VMAT beam-on time was measured during actual linac delivery, whereas TOMO and sVMAT beam-on times were calculated by the corresponding TPS from the finalized plans. The strong correlation between sVMAT beam-on time and target volume (R²=0.824) indicates that, although sVMAT is more efficient than TOMO, it does not achieve the rapid throughput of conventional VMAT arcs. Clinically, this prolonged beam-on time raises important concerns regarding intrafraction motion, particularly because even minor geometric shifts during delivery may blur the 18-Gy ablative peaks into the intended valley regions or displace high-dose subvolumes toward adjacent OARs. Therefore, clinical implementation of sVMAT for 3D-GRID should incorporate advanced motion-management strategies, including surface-guided radiotherapy, 4DCT-based motion assessment, respiratory gating, breath-hold, tumour tracking, and robust patient immobilization. Robust or motion-encompassing optimization may further improve the stability of the spatial dose pattern, although such approaches were not implemented in the present study.

We constrained the GRID/GTV volume ratio to a uniform approximately 2.2% across all plans to isolate platform-specific dosimetric effects, although the optimal ratio remains biologically undefined [36]. This geometric normalization served as a critical baseline for internal validity, ensuring that the observed dosimetric superiorities of sVMAT, particularly regarding GI and PVDR, were attributable intrinsically to the mechanical and modulatory capabilities of the delivery platform rather than confounding variations in target volume fractions. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged, Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, different clinically commissioned dose engines were used across platforms, which may have introduced algorithm-dependent differences in calculated dose, particularly in heterogeneous anatomy and highly modulated GRID peak regions. Thus, the results should be interpreted as a comparison of complete clinical planning systems rather than beam trajectories alone. Second, TOMO and sVMAT plans were not physically delivered in this retrospective planning study. Although all sVMAT plans were generated within the deliverable parameter space of the TPS and accelerator model, future end-to-end phantom measurements, patient-specific QA, and logfile analyses are required before clinical implementation. Finally, other highly conformal delivery techniques may provide comparable or complementary advantages. CyberKnife-based robotic delivery and non-coplanar dynamic trajectory radiotherapy can generate steep dose gradients and offer advanced motion-management capabilities. Recent DTRT studies have demonstrated the feasibility of non-isocentric dynamic trajectories and respiratory gating [37, 38], suggesting that these approaches should be included in future SFRT platform-comparison studies.

Conclusion

The integration of continuous longitudinal couch motion with volumetric modulated arc therapy establishes sVMAT as a feasible and dosimetrically superior platform for classic single-fraction 3D-GRID radiotherapy. By leveraging a dynamic dual-layer MLC within a spiral delivery trajectory, sVMAT effectively circumvents the modulation constraints inherent to single-isocenter VMAT and the longitudinal dose spillage characteristic of TOMO. When prescribing an ablative 18 Gy dose without intentional valley dosing, sVMAT consistently delivered the steepest dose gradients, the lowest valley baseline, and the maximal peak-to-valley contrast (D2%/D50%). Although the technique necessitates an intermediate delivery duration compared to standard VMAT, this temporal investment yields a decisive gain in spatial confinement. sVMAT is a technologically distinct and clinically promising platform for 3D-GRID in bulky, radioresistant tumors, warranting prospective validation.
