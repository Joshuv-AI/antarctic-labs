Neurological complications in microgravity and long duration spaceflight
Authors: Giselle Coelho, Alejandro Rabinstein, W David Freeman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-duration spaceflight produces structural, functional, and hemodynamic brain changes driven by microgravity, radiation, elevated CO2, and isolation. Consequences include Spaceflight-Associated Neuro-Ocular Syndrome, vestibular imbalance, orthostatic intolerance, and cognitive disturbance. We consolidate current evidence, present a cerebrovascular physiologic framework, and discuss emerging countermeasures—including lower body negative pressure, artificial gravity, advanced neuromonitoring, and synthetic torpor—needed to safeguard neurological health on exploration-class missions.Subject terms: Engineering, Neurology, Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

The brain is responsible for the most crucial and tightly calibrated functions in the human body. It is also the most susceptible to changes in the conditions of the internal and external environment1–3. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that the brain will be particularly affected by the vastly different conditions to be encountered in deep space1–3.

Structural and functional brain changes have been documented in astronauts returning from missions in the International Space Station (ISS), but still little is known about the potential consequences of more prolonged and pronounced exposure to the risk factors that could produce neurological complications during deep space travel3–7. This article will review those risk factors, the brain changes that have been noted after exposure to microgravity, the neurological problems that may be expected during long space travel, and what preventive strategies might help diminish their impact.

Risk factors for neurological complications in microgravity

The main risk factors for neurological complications in deep space are anticipated to be microgravity, cosmic radiation, increased CO2, confinement and social isolation, and disruption of the circadian rhythm (Fig. 4)2–5.

Among these factors, microgravity has been the most studied – though our knowledge of the consequences of prolonged exposure to microgravity remains rudimentary. As discussed in the next section, morphological and connectivity changes have been observed with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and functional MRI (fMRI) in astronauts returning from missions at the ISS, particularly those lasting 6 months or more1–4. These brain changes are thought to be primarily related to cephalic fluid shifts because of the absence of the usual fluctuations in intracranial pressure that we experience on Earth with changes in body position (i.e., lower intracranial pressure in erect compared with supine position)5. Yet, several other mechanisms may also be at play, including vestibular adaptation and reweighing of sensorimotor networks in response to weightlessness and deprivation of proprioceptive inputs6.

Less is known about the effects of cosmic radiation because no human has yet been exposed to the type and intensity of radiation present in deep space. It is estimated that astronauts on a Mars mission may be exposed to radiation levels up to ten times higher than those experienced during missions on the International Space Station7. Cosmic radiation includes high-charge, high-energy (HZE) particles with high linear energy transfer, which produce complex biological damage not seen with terrestrial radiation sources8. These limitations significantly constrain risk prediction for long-duration missions and highlight uncertainties in modeling radiation-induced neurological injury7,9,10.

In the ISS the concentration of CO2 averages 0.5% (vs 0.04% on Earth)11 Though this concentration is much lower than the levels seen in individuals who exhibit symptoms of acute narcosis, persistent exposure to even low degrees of hypercapnia could still be detrimental. Furthermore, it has been suggested that the sensitivity to CO2-related symptoms (such as headache and mental fogging) may be exacerbated during space missions, perhaps because of an interaction with the effects of microgravity12. A ground analog study using 6° head-down tilt strict bed rest for 30 days combined with elevated ambient CO2 has shown widespread changes in functional connectivity (as assessed by serial resting state fMRI) across vestibular, somatosensory, motor and visual centers, with associated reductions in sensorimotor performance12.

Prolonged confinement and social isolation can negatively affect mental health. In a high-fidelity ground experiment designed to simulate the conditions expected to occur in a mission to Mars, 6 participants were kept confined for 520 days in a 550 m³ pressurized chamber with interconnected habitable modules. There were major differences among the participants in measures of stress, fatigue and depression; some developed severe and protracted psychological symptoms while others tolerated the experiment without negative psychological consequences13. Sleep disruption and alterations in the circadian rhythm were common, and they correlated with the degree of individual sedentariness14 Sleep problems, particularly insomnia, are very prevalent during ISS missions and astronauts report frequent use of sleep-inducing medications15.

Cerebral changes in microgravity and deep space

Multiple studies have reported morphological and functional brain changes using pre- and post-spaceflight neuroimaging2,4,16–22. Structural and connectivity alterations have also been documented in studies with brain analogs23. While the findings across these studies have been variable (due at least in part to differences in measurement techniques)3, they demonstrate that long-duration spaceflights can provoke microstructural, macrostructural and functional modifications in the brain, primarily from prolonged exposure to microgravity.

Structural changes

Cephalad movement of body fluids is thought to play a large role in accumulation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the head. Measurement of cerebral blood flow (CBF) changes in microgravity remains poorly understood with relation to intracranial pressure (ICP) changes well described physiologically on Earth (Fig. 1). Transcranial Doppler (TCD) is an easy, compact way to measure CBF non-invasively using ultrasound over the temporal bone area of the human skull and it can provide non-invasive information about brain compliance/elastance via waveform and pulsatility and resistance indices (Fig. 2).

Increased volume of the lateral and third ventricles is probably the most consistent finding across studies1,2,4,17,18,24. Cephalad fluid redistribution because of the lack of intracranial pressure fluctuations normally induced by gravity is most likely the major factor responsible for the increase in cerebrospinal fluid volume that occurs over the course of long-duration spaceflight25,26. Concomitant upward brain shift with twisting of the cerebral aqueduct (that communicates the third with the fourth ventricles) may also contribute to the accumulation of fluid in the lateral and third ventricles, with relative preservation of the volume of the fourth ventricle1,17,18. Ventricular size improves after return to Earth but may remain abnormal even after 12 months4. Fluid redistribution would also explain the observed increase in free water content in frontopolar, temporal and occipital regions4,17 which has been accompanied by alterations in water diffusivity along and across white matter pathways documented by diffusion tensor imaging (Fig. 3)16,17,22.

Functional changes

Neuroimaging studies longitudinally evaluating gray and white matter changes have produced more variable results. Transient focal gray matter volume changes (decrease in orbitofrontal and temporal regions and increase in basal ganglia, pre- and post-central gyri and supplementary motor cortex) have been noted in the context vertical brain shift and increased ventricular volumes19,22,25. Reversible changes in white matter have also been reported, such as increased white matter volume in cerebellar and corticospinal tracts (which control motor function), the superior longitudinal fasciculus (integrally involved in visual processing), and vestibular pathways (that regulate balance)16,22. Complex alterations in connectivity across various brain regions have been described with serial fMRI, but understanding the significance of these findings will demand further investigation20,27 (Figs. 4 and 5).

It is still unclear whether these structural and functional brain changes represent adaptive or maladaptive responses. Some of these neuroimaging findings have been shown to be associated with neurological symptoms or impairment in neurological functions. For instance, astronauts with greater cerebral changes on MRI had poorer postural control and worse cognitive performance upon return to Earth in the minority of studies that included those clinical assessments16,17. Most, if not all, of the documented cerebral changes appear to be reversible based on available evidence. However, whether permanent alterations in brain morphology or connections could happen with much longer spaceflights remains unknown. Beyond ventricular enlargement and upward brain shift, long-duration spaceflight appears to affect other compartments involved in brain fluid homeostasis. Recent MRI studies demonstrate changes in visible perivascular spaces, suggesting altered glymphatic and perivascular fluid clearance in microgravity21. White matter alterations are also more anatomically specific than previously recognized, with diffusion-based analyses identifying changes in sensorimotor, cerebellar, and association tracts after long-duration missions22. These findings indicate that structural brain adaptation extends beyond cerebrospinal fluid redistribution to include remodeling of white matter architecture and fluid transport pathways, which may influence both neurologic function and post-flight recovery.

Functional and physiologic adaptations further expand the spectrum of cerebral changes in spaceflight. Vestibular and cerebellar systems undergo sensory reweighting in microgravity, with altered neural responses and connectivity patterns28,29. In parallel, autonomic dysfunction and altered cerebrovascular regulation may impair cerebral autoregulation and contribute to orthostatic intolerance29,30. Radiation exposure represents an additional concern, as experimental models demonstrate that high-energy particle irradiation can induce persistent cognitive deficits through hippocampal and cortical injury9. Emerging data from commercial spaceflight missions such as Inspiration 4 suggest that even short-duration missions may produce measurable neurovestibular and physiologic changes, although detailed neuroimaging correlates remain limited31. Together, these mechanisms highlight that neurological risk during long-duration spaceflight is multifactorial, involving structural, functional, hemodynamic, and radiation-related processes.

Spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome (SANS)

Cerebral changes on neuroimaging have been observed in individuals with spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome (SANS)17,32. This ophthalmological complication causes loss of near vision (hyperopic shift) and occurs quite frequently during prolonged space missions33,34. Ocular morphological correlates include optic disc edema, choroidal folds and nerve layer infarcts on funduscopic examination, and posterior globe flattening on orbital imaging (MRI, optical coherence tomography or ultrasound)33,34. While vision typically recovers after weeks or months, the ocular changes sometimes subsist35. The presence of optic disc edema and the association of visual changes with vertical brain shift on MRI initially led to the hypothesis that SANS was caused by intracranial hypertension. Yet, astronauts with symptoms of SANS do not have other features of intracranial hypertension (such as headaches, nausea, diplopia or transient visual obscurations) and the ocular findings of SANS are frankly asymmetric, when not strictly unilateral. Moreover, a recent study showed only a modest association between greater peripapillary retinal thickness (indicative of edema) and larger lateral ventricle volume on brain MRI (and indirect measure of increased cerebrospinal fluid pressure) and no association with white matter or total cerebral volumes36. Thus, while the precise pathophysiology of SANS remains unclear, available evidence suggests that SANS is more likely related to a process of intraocular CSF compartmentalization with increase in the local pressure around the optic nerve, rather than intracranial hypertension34. More recent reviews and methodology studies support a multifactorial pathophysiology and highlight the need for standardized ocular measurements in spaceflight research37,38.

Hemodynamic changes

Lastly, cerebral hemodynamic changes also occur during long-duration spaceflight. Most notably, stagnant and retrograde blood flow in the internal jugular vein have been shown by ultrasound studies conducted aboard the ISS; one astronaut even had thrombosis of the internal jugular vein39,40. Therefore, venous hypertension with its accompanying effects on glymphatic drainage41 may contribute to the pathogenesis of neurological complications in deep space.

Brain and neurologic equations

Understanding the quantitative relationships governing cerebrovascular physiology is essential for predicting and monitoring the neurological effects of microgravity described above. These relationships provide a mechanistic framework linking systemic cardiovascular adaptations, intracranial pressure dynamics, and cerebral blood flow regulation. The following equations therefore serve as a physiological foundation for interpreting neuroimaging findings, guiding real-time monitoring strategies, and informing the development of targeted countermeasures for maintaining cerebral perfusion and metabolic homeostasis during long-duration spaceflight.

There are at least 4 important equations for cerebrovascular physiology relevant to Earth and microgravity physiology undergoing research (Fig. 5). The first equation (Eq. 1) is Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP), which is equal to the Pulse Pressure (PP) added to the Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP). While MAP has different algebraic forms, we find that the simplified one below (Eq. 1) is easiest to remember. Pulse Pressure (Eq. 2) is the Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP) minus the Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP). Cerebral Perfusion Pressure (Eq. 3) therefore is the forward pressure (MAP) minus the intracranial backpressure (ICP), which is relevant given the cephalad fluid shifts intracranially and potential effects on CPP and CBF. Cerebral Blood Flow (CBF) (Eq. 4) is similar to Ohm’s law (Eq. 5, where I = current or CBF, V is CPP or voltage difference, and R = Resistance is CVR or CerebroVascular Resistance). Validation of Eq. 4 has been performed using non-invasive TCD (CBF) in normal subjects (auto-adjusting CVR variable) and using multimodal software in brain-injured subjects which shows CVR becomes a constant, and thus CPP linearly dependent on MAP. Oxygen Extraction Fraction (OEF) of brain tissue (Eq. 5) is proportional to the Cerebral Metabolic Consumption of Oxygen (CMRO2) divided by the CBF times the O2-carrying capacity of arterial blood (CaO2) (Eq. 6). These equations are important especially for the brain’s metabolic or metabotropic considerations on CBF and energy consumption. For example, the brain’s CMRO2 and OEF (O2 metabolism) are linearly related to core body temperature. For longer spaceflight missions into deeper space (e.g., Mars and beyond) considerations for reducing and controlling body and brain metabolism or torpor will become increasingly relevant. Equations

Given the cardiovascular adaptations to microgravity, including reduced plasma volume and altered autonomic tone, MAP (Eq. 1) becomes a key upstream determinant of cerebral perfusion under these shifting conditions.

Because PP reflects arterial compliance and stroke volume, changes observed during spaceflight—such as vascular remodeling and decreased preload—may directly influence PP and thus indirectly affect MAP and downstream cerebral hemodynamics.

Given the cephalad fluid shifts described above, the CPP equation (Eq. 3) becomes particularly relevant because even modest increases in ICP may reduce effective cerebral perfusion despite preserved or mildly altered MAP.

In the context of altered autoregulation in microgravity or disease states, Eq. 4 highlights how impaired CVR adaptability may render CBF increasingly pressure-passive and dependent on fluctuations in CPP.

As microgravity and prolonged spaceflight may alter both cerebral metabolism and perfusion, Eq. 5 underscores the compensatory role of OEF in maintaining oxygen delivery when CBF is reduced or redistributed.

Given spaceflight-associated changes such as “space anemia” and shifts in oxygenation dynamics, Eq. 6 becomes critical for interpreting how systemic adaptations influence cerebral oxygen delivery and metabolic balance.

Expected neurological complications

In addition to the visual impairment related to SANS (an ophthalmological rather than a neurological complication), astronauts returning from spaceflight missions often have diminished postural balance, reduced manual dexterity, and impaired reaction times and executive function16,17,42. Balance problems are related to vestibular dysfunction, which can be transiently severe. Changes in various cerebral areas known to contribute to balance control through connections with the peripheral vestibular system have been noted after prolonged spaceflight and the degree of these changes correlates with the severity and duration of the postural imbalance upon return to Earth16,20. Of note, these central changes may be adaptations to the weightless environment: decreased vestibular input may serve to ameliorate symptoms of space motion sickness during the spaceflight. Yet, once back on Earth this reduction in vestibular afference to the brain causes imbalance and demands reliance on compensatory proprioceptive and visual sensory inputs until readjustment to gravity occurs. Early imbalance in returning astronauts is exacerbated because proprioceptive function also needs time to undergo reprogramming after prolonged exposure to microgravity (deprivation of proprioceptive inputs occurs in the absence of gravity)43. Meanwhile, judgment of subjective visual vertical is also temporarily degraded after long-duration space missions44.

Postural imbalance only becomes a problem once space travelers are back on Earth. The same cannot be said of the cognitive and behavioral problems that may manifest themselves during spaceflight. Unfortunately, we do not have enough information about cognitive function during space missions. However, it is unlikely that the impaired performance on tasks of spatial working memory, dual motor tasks, and reaction time tests observed in post-flight assessments16,17 would not also be present during the spaceflight. Some of the neuroimaging findings discussed in the previous section would be expected to have consequences in fine motor and higher cortical functions. A battery of neurocognitive tests (called Cognition) was specifically developed and validated for use in the high-performing cohort of astronauts45. As information on the results of these cognitive testing becomes available, we will better understand the magnitude of the problem.

Any cognitive impairment during long-duration missions will be magnified by behavioral problems that may develop because of confinement and isolation and by the deleterious consequences of insomnia and disruptions of the sleep-wake cycle15,45. Executive dysfunction is common in individuals with sleep disorders (such as insomnia)46 and depression47. Thus, multiple factors could contribute to impair the execution of complex cognitive tasks vital to the success of long-duration space missions.

Preventive Strategies: Monitoring and Countermeasures

Preventive strategies for health maintenance in space can be designed to address specific complications (such as the efforts underway to reduce the risks of SANS) or to ameliorate the effects of the main potential mechanisms (e.g., microgravity or radiation). Given our insufficient knowledge on what health problems can be encountered during long sojourns into deep space, the latter strategy appears particularly appealing. Table 3 provides a high-level summary of NASA countermeasures to these major areas of neurological space challenges.

In addition to systemic countermeasures, neurological-specific strategies are increasingly recognized as essential components of risk mitigation in long-duration spaceflight. Vestibular rehabilitation and sensorimotor re-adaptation protocols, including pre-flight adaptation training and post-flight recalibration exercises, may accelerate recovery of balance and spatial orientation. Cognitive countermeasures should incorporate structured neurocognitive monitoring and training using validated tools such as NASA’s Cognition battery, enabling early detection of performance decline and targeted interventions. Neuroprotective strategies are also under investigation, including the use of antioxidants and anti-inflammatory agents to mitigate radiation-induced central nervous system injury, particularly in the context of exposure to high-energy particles.

Advanced neurological monitoring will play a central role in future missions. Techniques such as transcranial Doppler (TCD) allow real-time assessment of cerebral hemodynamics and autoregulation, while electroencephalography (EEG) can provide continuous evaluation of sleep architecture and cognitive states. Measurement of optic nerve sheath diameter offers a non-invasive surrogate for intracranial pressure monitoring, which may be particularly relevant for early detection of SANS-related changes. In parallel, targeted intracranial pressure management strategies, including lower body negative pressure, venous outflow optimization, and potential pharmacologic modulation of CSF dynamics may help mitigate cephalad fluid shifts and their neurological consequences.

Within the NASA Human Research Program framework, these interventions align with a risk-based model that categorizes neurological risks across domains including sensorimotor performance, cognitive function, and neuro-ocular health, emphasizing integrated monitoring and countermeasure development across mission phases48–52.

Negative pressure applied to the lower body has been proposed to diminish the effects of microgravity on fluid distribution. Early data appear promising. In studies relying on head-down tilt to simulate microgravity, the application of lower body negative pressure induced reductions in estimated intracranial pressure (by non-invasive measures)53 and optic nerve sheath diameter54. Application of moderate lower body negative pressure (30 minutes of −25 mmHg) during spaceflight has been shown to reduce jugular vein volume (making it comparable to the volume found in preflight supine position), with associated increases in middle cerebral vein velocity and portal vein cross-sectional area40. However, these measurements were only obtained twice during the spaceflight and it is unknown whether frequent application of lower body negative pressure can afford persistent beneficial effects on venous volume and pressure.

Another possible countermeasure against the deleterious effects of prolonged exposure to microgravity could be the use of centrifuge-generated artificial gravity. While multiple potential beneficial effects from the application of artificial gravity have been posited55,56 its implementation in deep space represents an enormous engineering challenge. Gradual adaptation to the rotation of the centrifuge would be necessary to prevent severe motion sickness57

Inducing synthetic torpor through hypothermia is theoretically a very appealing alternative. In a state of torpor, metabolic demands can be considerably reduced: brain oxygen consumption declines by 10% for each degree Celsius below normal (e.g., down to 50% of baseline at 32°C)58 Furthermore, based on data from hibernating animals, it has been estimated that humans could reach a minimum metabolic rate of 10%–20% of baseline even while maintaining body temperatures slightly above 30°C59. Natural hibernators can go into torpor for months without any major loss of body mass or bone density, despite consuming no food. Torpor protects them from tissue damage by suppressing mechanisms of autophagy and apoptosis60 Notably, torpor also generates resistance to radiation injury61 To make things even more ideal, space travelers undergoing torpor could be kept upright under moderate lower body negative pressure to minimize cephalad fluid shifts. The big problem is that we do not know how to induce synthetic torpor in humans safely or how to unlock our genetic code to allow for the occurrence of natural torpor. Clinical experience with induction of hypothermia (for instance, in survivors of cardiac arrest) is not very informative: those patients require mechanical ventilation, sedation to prevent shivering, artificial nutrition, and very close monitoring to prevent serious complications. The engineering aspects of synthetic torpor induction can be handled62 but the biology requires much more investigation59.

Shielding of the spacecraft can theoretically protect humans from cosmic radiation. However, shielding has drawbacks because of its added weight and the risk of provoking spallation (fragmentation of particle radiation) that could potentially worsen the effects of cosmic radiation exposure10. Future advances in engineering might increase the efficiency of shielding, but it is unlikely that shielding alone will be sufficient to eliminate the health risks related to exposure to cosmic radiation during interplanetary missions.

Close monitoring of physiologic parameters will be essential to detect both anticipated and unexpected health changes during long-duration spaceflight. Current capabilities aboard the International Space Station include ultrasound-based vascular assessments, optical coherence tomography for evaluation of SANS, and ambulatory physiologic monitoring such as Holter electrocardiography. These tools reflect ongoing efforts within NASA’s Human Research Program to develop compact, reliable monitoring systems suitable for spaceflight, although significant limitations remain for deep-space missions where real-time ground support is not feasible40,48–51.

Lastly, we need to learn how to screen and select those individuals at the lowest risk for health problems in deep space. Experience with SANS17 and long confinement13 have already offered proof that there is great variability in the susceptibility to the medical complications that can be encountered during prolonged spaceflight. Genetic, metabolic, endocrine, and behavioral factors may need to be considered in the future to identify ideal candidates for interplanetary missions.

Conclusion

Although our knowledge of the neurological complications in deep space is just incipient, there is already evidence that long-duration spaceflight can produce changes in brain function and morphology. In turn, these changes can result in symptoms that could compromise the physical and mental performance of space travelers. Therefore, much more research needs to be conducted to identify ways to mitigate the risks of the various environmental factors that may imperil brain health in deep space.
