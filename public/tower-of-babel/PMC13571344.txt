Coronary Embolization of an Avulsed Radial Arterial Wall Fragment During Transradial PCI
Authors: Raid Faraj, Sebastien Hess, Xavier Lamit, Eric Quiring
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransradial access is the preferred approach for coronary interventions, but vascular complications may rarely lead to unexpected coronary events.Case SummaryA 50-year-old woman with cardiovascular risk factors presented with exertional symptoms and a positive stress echocardiography result in the anterior territory. Coronary angiography via right radial access revealed a significant mid–left anterior descending coronary artery lesion. During ad hoc percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), difficult catheter advancement due to radial resistance and spasm required escalation to a long guide sheath. After guidewire placement, angiography revealed a new hazy filling defect at the proximal left anterior descending coronary artery–left circumflex coronary artery bifurcation, associated with chest pain and ST-segment elevation. Thromboaspiration restored coronary flow, retrieving a 3-cm vascular fragment. Histopathology confirmed an avulsed radial arterial wall fragment containing the 3 layers of the vascular wall. Upper limb imaging showed preserved radial flow.DiscussionThis case illustrates coronary embolization secondary to radial arterial wall injury during transradial PCI.Take-Home MessagesDifficult catheter advancement during transradial PCI may cause radial arterial wall avulsion with subsequent coronary embolization. Unexpected angiographic filling defects should prompt consideration of nonthrombotic embolic mechanisms and rapid bailout strategies.

History Presentation

A 50-year-old woman presented with exertional malaise and was referred for coronary angiography after a positive stress echocardiography result demonstrating anterior ischemia. At admission, she was asymptomatic. Past Medical History

The patient had a history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and active tobacco use, without known coronary artery disease or previous cardiovascular events. She was treated with aspirin 75 mg daily, atorvastatin 40 mg daily, and a fixed-dose combination of perindopril (5 mg) and amlodipine (5 mg).

Management

In view of the patient's symptoms, the concordant anterior ischemia on stress echocardiography, and the angiographic finding of a significant mid-LAD stenosis, ad hoc percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) was performed after informed consent was obtained. The patient received intravenous aspirin 250 mg, clopidogrel 300 mg, and unfractionated heparin 100 IU/kg. Diagnostic coronary angiography had initially been performed through a 5-F right radial introducer sheath (Radifocus Introducer II, Terumo). The sheath was then exchanged over a guidewire for a 6-F sheath. Advancement of a 6-F extra backup 3.5 Launcher guiding catheter (Medtronic) was met with marked resistance despite intra-arterial nitrates (200 μg of nitroglycerin) and additional sedation. A telescoping attempt using a 4-F Judkins Right diagnostic catheter was unsuccessful, and the left radial pulse was deemed inadequate for crossover. Therefore, a 6-F, 45-cm Prelude Roadster guide sheath (Merit Medical) was advanced with mild resistance, after which the extra backup guiding catheter could be delivered easily and the left main selectively intubated. The LAD was wired with a SION blue coronary guidewire, and the diagonal branch was wired for protection with a second SION blue guidewire (Asahi Intecc). The first contrast injections then demonstrated a long hazy radiolucent structure straddling the proximal LAD and proximal left circumflex coronary artery (LCx), accompanied by acute chest pain and ST-segment elevation in the anterior and lateral leads (Figure 3, Video 2). The activated clotting time was within the therapeutic range. Tirofiban was initiated, and aspiration was first attempted using an Eliminate aspiration catheter (Terumo) in the LCx after wiring the vessel. No material was retrieved initially, and flow became absent in the mid-distal LCx, whereas the radiolucent image disappeared from the LAD (Figure 4, Video 3). A second aspiration attempt using an Export AP aspiration catheter (Medtronic) restored normal LCx flow and resulted in complete disappearance of the angiographic image (Figure 5, Video 4). Discussion

Transradial access is now the preferred route for coronary angiography and PCI because it reduces bleeding and vascular complications compared with femoral access in both acute coronary syndromes and stable ischemic heart disease. However, its safety profile should not obscure the fact that transradial catheterization can produce acute radial arterial wall injury, including endothelial denudation, intimal tears, medial dissection, spasm, hematoma, and radial artery occlusion. Such injury may be further promoted by repeated catheter exchanges, particularly when performed in small-caliber or reactive arteries, increasing mechanical stress on the vascular wall. Optical coherence tomography studies have shown that acute radial arterial wall injury after transradial procedures is not rare, and contemporary series still report radial artery occlusion in roughly the single-digit percentage range, with spasm, prolonged sheath dwell time, crossover, and hematoma among the major risk factors.1,2

The present case is clinically important because it moves beyond the usual spectrum of local radial artery complications and documents a downstream coronary embolic event caused by avulsed vascular tissue. That mechanism is exceptionally uncommon. In a focused search, we identified only a limited number of directly relevant published coronary cases in which avulsed vascular tissue fragments were implicated as the embolic material during a transradial coronary procedure. In a report by Klaudel et al3, a patient with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction had right coronary occlusion by an avulsed radial artery fragment delivered through the guide catheter; the investigators described it as the first case in which a radial artery fragment avulsed by the catheter was carried to the right coronary artery and successfully aspirated.

Other published reports strengthen the plausibility of the mechanism even when coronary embolization did not occur. Radial artery avulsion or stripping has been described as a rare but recognized complication of transradial procedures, usually related to severe spasm, small-caliber or tortuous arteries, catheter-tip trauma, difficult introduction or withdrawal, or forceful manipulation.4,5

The present case nevertheless adds several important elements to the literature. First, the embolic event occurred during ad hoc PCI, not merely during diagnostic angiography. Second, the trigger was not simple catheter exchange alone but a sequence of escalating resistance, radial spasm, unsuccessful catheter advancement, and subsequent use of a long guide sheath. Third, the angiographic presentation as a long hazy bifurcation filling defect involving the proximal LAD and proximal LCx created an immediate differential diagnosis with thrombus and dissection. Other potential procedural causes of abrupt coronary filling defects should also be considered in this setting, including catheter-related thrombus formation, coronary air embolism, hydrophilic coating embolization from interventional devices, or other forms of iatrogenic foreign-body embolization. Finally, the diagnosis was strengthened by histopathologic confirmation of vascular tissue.

From a practice standpoint, this case reinforces 3 teaching points. The first is prevention. Marked resistance during catheter or sheath advancement should not be normalized during transradial PCI. Persistent resistance despite vasodilators and adequate sedation should prompt immediate reassessment of wire position, artery caliber/tortuosity, and the threshold for changing strategy or converting access rather than continuing forceful manipulation. The second is recognition. When a new angiographic filling defect appears abruptly after difficult transradial manipulation, especially with chest pain and ST-segment changes despite therapeutic anticoagulation, operators should broaden the differential beyond thrombus alone and consider embolization of arterial wall tissue or other iatrogenic material.6 In this setting, the morphology and timing of the angiographic abnormality are important clues. The third is management. Current guidelines do not recommend routine aspiration thrombectomy, but this case illustrates that selective bailout aspiration can be both diagnostic and therapeutic when an acute iatrogenic embolic obstruction is suspected. Here, aspiration restored flow and retrieved the culprit material, avoiding unnecessary stenting of the proximal bifurcation lesion.

Conclusions

This case represents more than a rare complication report. Its educational value lies in reminding interventionalists that difficult transradial manipulation can generate not only local access-site injury but also embolic coronary complications from avulsed arterial wall tissue. Early suspicion, rapid aspiration-based bailout, and a low threshold to abandon a hostile radial route are the main practical lessons.
