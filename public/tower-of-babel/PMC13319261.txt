Genetics of Cerebrotendinous Xanthomatosis
Authors: Jennifer Hanson, Penelope E. Bonnen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX) is rare, autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in CYP27A1, which encodes sterile 27 hydroxylase, a key enzyme in bile acid biosynthesis. Enzyme deficiency results in reduced cholic and chenodeoxycholic acid synthesis with accumulation of cholestanol, bile acid intermediates, and bile alcohols, producing a progressive multisystem disorder characterized by chronic diarrhea, juvenile-onset cataracts, tendons xanthomas, and neurological dysfunction. Although CTX typically begins in childhood, diagnosis is frequently delayed until adulthood, limiting the benefit of effective disease modifying therapy with chenodeoxycholic acid. Since the identification of CYP27A1, more than 200 pathogenic variants have been reported, including canonical loss of function alleles and missense variants with variable residual enzyme activity. In this review, we summarize the medical genetics, population genetics and genotype phenotype relationships of CTX. We highlight insights from large population databases that refine global incidence estimates, revealing population-specific enrichment of pathogenic variants and persistent underdiagnosis. We further review functional and clinical data demonstrating that stratification of CYP27A1 variants by functional effect, complete loss of function versus hypomorph alleles, correlates with clinical severity and biochemical phenotype. Finally, we discuss emerging advances in biochemical testing and newborn screening strategies that offer the potential for presymptomatic diagnosis, enabling timely initiation of therapy and prevention of irreversible disease manifestations. Integrating molecular, functional, and population genetic data provides a framework for improved variant interpretation, earlier diagnosis, and optimized therapeutic intervention in CTX.

Introduction

Cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX) is an inborn error of metabolism, the hallmark features of which include chronic diarrhea, juvenile-onset cataracts, tendinous xanthomas, and progressive neurological dysfunction (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man [OMIM 213700]). CTX is an autosomal recessive genetic condition caused by variants in CYP27A1, which codes for the sterol 27-hydroxylase enzyme that functions in bile acid synthesis. Biallelic pathogenic variants in this gene result in decreased cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid synthesis and consequently cause elevations of cholestanol, bile acid intermediates, and bile alcohols. Diagnosis can be made with clinical diagnostic testing of plasma cholestanol, bile acid intermediate, and bile alcohol levels; urinary bile alcohol levels; and/or genetic testing of CYP27A1. Despite a pediatric age of onset, CTX diagnosis is often not made until the third or fourth decade of life owing to the multisystem clinical presentation and the emergence of features over decades [1, 2]. Treatment with the bile acid chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) is available that significantly slows progression of disease and when started early, can prevent the major clinical problems associated with this disease, underscoring the importance of early diagnosis [1, 3–7]. Understanding the genetics of CTX can help facilitate diagnosis. Here, we discuss the medical genetics, population genetics, and genotype phenotype correlations of CYP27A1.

Medical Genetics of CTX

The gene that encodes the sterol 27-hydroxylase enzyme, CYP27A1, was cloned in 1991 [8] and shortly thereafter the first pathogenic variants that cause CTX were identified [9]. This first report of pathogenic variants in CYP27A1 described two unrelated patients, each of whom were presumed homozygous for a different variant. The reference sequence for CYP27A1 has changed over time and so has the variant nomenclature. The first two pathogenic variants reported in CYP27A1 were: NM_000784.1:c.1456C>T (p.Arg446Cys) now known as NM_000784.4:c.1435C>T (p.Arg479Cys) and NM_000784.1:c.1204c>T (p.Arg362Cys), which is now reported as NM_000784.4:c.1183C>T (p.Arg395Cys). Expression of each of these missense variants in cultured cells showed reduced CYP27A1 protein levels and significantly diminished enzyme activity for p.(Arg479Cys) and no enzyme activity for p.(Arg395Cys) [9].

The gene structure of CYP27A1 was subsequently delineated as comprising nine exons and eight introns [10], which we now know span approximately 33 Kb (hg38 2:218781749-218815293) (Fig. 1). The CYP27A1 complementary DNA (cDNA) has 1596 nucleotides, and the protein includes 532 amino acids (NM_000784.4(CYP27A1)). This same study reported additional pathogenic variants in CYP27A1 that were the first frameshift and splice variants reported in patients with CTX (NM_000784.4: c.819del (p.Asp273Glufs*13) and NM_000784.4: c.845-1G>A(splice)) [10]. These two variants were demonstrated to be loss-of-function alleles through analysis of mRNA in patient fibroblasts; no stable sterol 27-hydroxylase mRNA was detected in patients’ cells [10].Fig. 1Pathogenic variants in CYP27A1 cause CTX. Since the cloning of CYP27A1, over 200 variants have been identified in individuals with CTX; pathogenic variants are present in every exon of CYP27A1 (Fig. 1). ClinVar currently lists 151 loss-of-function (LOF) variants for CYP27A1 (66 frameshift, 49 nonsense, 36 canonical splice site variants) as pathogenic or likely pathogenic. ClinVar contains 25 missense variants in CYP27A1 categorized as pathogenic or likely pathogenic for CTX. However, we identified 54 missense variants in literature reported as causing CTX; some of these 54 missense variants are not listed in ClinVar at all and others are in ClinVar listed as variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Under American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) variant classification guidelines for clinical interpretation of variants, LOF variants can be presumed to be likely pathogenic even if that specific variant has not been reported in a prior patient as causing disease; missense variants are not presumed to be likely pathogenic without additional supporting evidence. As a result, there are many missense VUS and almost no LOF VUS for CYP27A1. The only frameshift and nonsense variants that are not presumed to be LOF variants in ClinVar are frameshift and nonsense variants that occur in the last ten amino acids of the protein; because they occur in the very end of the protein, it is uncertain what effect they may have on protein function.

Population Genetics of CTX

Understanding the frequency that disease-causing variants have in a population can facilitate identification of patients as well as shed light on the frequency of disease. We queried one of the largest population genetic databases (gnomAD 4.0.0, N = 807,000, January 2024) for all pathogenic variants in CYP27A1. Analysis of these data across four global populations, African/African American N = 37,545, Admixed American N = 30,019, Asian N = 67,994, and European N = 622,057, shed light on the frequency of the variants that cause CTX across the world. A total of 126 LOF variants in CYP27A1 were identified in gnomAD; of the 54 missense variants published in literature as causing CTX, 43 were found in gnomAD. The allele frequency of all pathogenic variants combined was 0.0018 in African/African American, Admixed American, and European populations (Fig. 2). The highest frequency pathogenic variant in the African/African American population was c.379C>T (p.Arg127Trp), which had an allele frequency of 0.0002. This variant has been well documented in patients with CTX; however, a detailed search was not able to find even a single published patient with CTX who was identified as Black or African who had this variant, indicating that there is likely an underdiagnosis of CTX in this population in particular. The highest frequency variant in Admixed Americans was c.1183C>T (p.Arg395Cys), 0.0005, which was one of the first variants published in CTX and has been identified in several patients with CTX. The European population’s highest frequency variant was also c.1183C>T (p.Arg395Cys), and it had the same allele frequency in Europeans as in Admixed Americans, 0.0005. The Asian population had the highest frequency of pathogenic variants. This appears to be due to multiple higher frequency variants. For example, the highest frequency variant in the Asian population is in a canonical splice site, c.1184+1G>A; with a frequency of 0.0008, it has a significantly higher frequency than the highest frequency variants in the other populations. In addition, the well-documented pathogenic variant c.1214G>A (p.Arg405Gln) appears in the Asian population at a frequency of 0.0005. There is yet another variant, c.1415G>C (p.Gly472Ala), which has a frequency of 0.0007. This missense variant is reported in fewer patients with CTX than other pathogenic variants; however, its Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion score (CADD) score = 29, which is well within the range of pathogenicity for CYP27A1.

CTX has been underdiagnosed, likely owing to the complex constellation of clinical features progressing over decades. Moreover, underdiagnosis leads to an incomplete ascertainment of variants that cause disease. To overcome these challenges, we innovated the use of combining clinical genetics, population genetics, and bioinformatics to identify pathogenic variants and calculate incidence of a monogenic disorder [11]. We conducted a bioinformatics assessment of the variants that were reported in literature to cause CTX and leveraged population genetics of those pathogenic variants and the variants present in population-based database (ExAC, N = 60,000) that were predicted to be pathogenic on the basis of the bioinformatic features of the variants present in patients with CTX. We showed that most disease-causing variants had similar frequencies in global populations. The greater power of the gnomAD 4.0 database with 800,000 individuals was leveraged to calculate the incidence of CTX across global populations using all CYP27A1 LOF variants in the database plus the published pathogenic missense variants present in the database. The missense variants included as pathogenic in this study were identified in patients with confirmed elevations in cholestanol, clinical manifestations of CTX, and complete CYP27A1 genotype comprising variants that had evidence supporting pathogenicity according to ACMG variant interpretation guidelines. Under this model, the incidence for African/African American, Admixed American, and European populations was the same: 1 in 308,000 (Fig. 2). The Asian population incidence was significantly higher at 1 in 80,000. Incidence estimates derived using Hardy–Weinberg-based approaches are influenced by incomplete ascertainment of all pathogenic variants, variant pathogenicity assignment, incomplete and inaccurate reporting of pathogenic variants in medical literature, and population allele frequency estimates, which may vary with the size and composition of reference datasets. Together, these factors affect population genetics-based disease-incidence estimates and it is important to interpret these results within the framework of the available data.

The incidence of CTX has been reported to be high in certain population isolates including Jewish populations and Druze [10, 12–17]. There appears to be CYP27A1 variants that have risen to high frequency in these groups, resulting in more cases of CTX. The variant that appears in the Druze population is c.355delC (p.Arg119Glyfs*24) [15]. Druze is a religious faith practiced by approximately 1 million individuals, most of whom live in Syria, Lebanon, and Israel. This variant was identified in a Druze village in Israel. This variant is present in just 2 out of 152,000 chromosomes in gnomAD. Those two individuals were in the gnomAD Middle Eastern population, which is consistent with the Druze geographic origins.

There are a handful of variants that are found in high frequency in Jewish populations [10, 12–14, 16, 17]. A high frequency of CTX was reported in Israel prior to the cloning of the gene [12]. As soon as CYP27A1 was cloned, pathogenic variants were identified in Jewish individuals. The first of these was c.819del (p.Asp273Glufs*13), which was identified in Moroccan Sephardic Jews [10]. This variant is present in 1 allele out of 1,461,890 in gnomAD, making it exceedingly rare. In the same study, c.845-1G>A(splice), was reported in a Moroccan Sephardic Jewish patients with CTX; this variant is also rare in gnomAD at a frequency of 0.000025. In contrast, a CYP27A1 variant was identified in an Algerian Sephardic Jew with CTX, c.1016C>T (p.Thr339Met) [16], and this variant is present in gnomAD in African/African American, Admixed American, Asian, and European populations, indicating it is present across global populations. Reports of genetic variants in Ashkenazi Jews lagged behind those for other Jewish populations, but analyses of the Ashkenazi Jewish population in gnomAD (N = 14,800) showed that CTX has a high frequency in the Ashkenazi population with a gene carrier rate of 0.002 [17]. gnomAD contained three pathogenic CYP27A1 variants in the Ashkenazi Jewish population, including one, c.1321C>T (p.Pro441Ser), that appears only in Ashkenazi Jewish. These data placed the incidence of CTX in Ashkenazim at 1 in 342,000, which is in line with other global populations.

The island population of Sardinia, Italy, was reported to have a “recurrent mutation” when it was found in two purportedly unrelated families in Sardinia [18]. Two individuals with CTX were described, both of whom were members of families with reported consanguinity. No additional cases of CTX on the island of Sardinia have been reported in medical literature, leaving open the question of whether this island experiences a high prevalence of CTX. The pathogenic variant observed in these families, c.1435C>T (p.Arg479Cys), is also present in the population database gnomAD, predominantly in the European population as well as in African/African American, Admixed American, and Asian populations.

Functional Genotype and Clinical Phenotype Association in CTX

Genotype–phenotype associations in CTX have historically not been detected. However, there are challenges to conducting a sufficiently statistically powered study in rare disease, and the clinical presentation (phenotype) of diseases like CTX that progress across decades and involve multiple organ systems can be difficult to capture fully and consistently. Clinical diagnostic criteria refer to cardinal features of CTX: cataracts, tendon xanthomas, cerebellar signs, pyramidal signs, peripheral neuropathy, and chronic diarrhea [2, 19, 20]. However, many studies report that CTX’s clinical presentation is highly variable across individuals, even within families, and that no genotype–phenotype relationships exist for CTX [2, 21–24]. While reports that no genotype–phenotype association exists for CTX, no genotype–phenotype studies have been identified that were conducted using a robust sample size or statistical analysis. As is often the case in rare disease, there does not appear to be a sufficient number of individuals with the same genotype for powerful statistical analysis. For example, Verrips et al. [21] conducted a comparison across individuals who were homozygous for a CYP27A1 pathogenic variant; in 79 individuals who had a homozygous genotype, there were 23 different variants. While variant-specific associations have been reported, these findings are difficult to interpret owing to small cohorts, population differences in variant allele frequency, and limited longitudinal data [25].

Systematic Approach Identifies Functional Genotype—Clinical Phenotype Association

To bring a systematic approach to the clinical description of individuals with CTX, we applied information theory analysis to data from 218 individuals with CTX to quantitatively assess clinical variability [26]. Across 12 clinical features, phenotypes were highly consistent within families, with only 4 of 83 families showing notable variability. The progressive, multisystem nature of CTX over decades contributes to perceived variability, but standardizing terminology and comparing features by age decade revealed a more uniform clinical presentation [26].

One way of improving statistical power in genotype–phenotype studies is to bin genotypes on the basis of functional effects as opposed to testing every pathogenic variant or genotype independently. In the context of a disease like CTX, which is caused by loss of sufficient activity of the CYP27A1 encoded protein, sterol 27-hydroxylase, a function-based paradigm involves binning together pathogenic variants that results in complete loss of enzyme function (LOF) and separately binning together those that retain some residual enzyme function (hypomorphs). Several studies have tested and reported the resulting effect of pathogenic CYP27A1 variants on mRNA, protein, or enzyme activity. It has been documented that CYP27A1 nonsense, splice, or frameshift variants have no enzyme activity, mRNA, or protein [10, 15, 16, 27–32]. Notably, there are several missense variants that have been demonstrated to be LOF variants [9, 28, 29, 33–43]. Pathogenic hypomorphs have also been reported for CYP27A1; missense variants in CYP27A1 that cause CTX have been demonstrated to have full or partial protein levels alongside partial enzyme activity [9, 38–41, 44, 45].

This function-based genotype paradigm was coupled with the information theory analysis of clinical data to query if there was a genotype–phenotype association for CTX [26]. This study compared the clinical presentation of individuals who possess two LOF CYP27A1 variants versus individuals who possess two hypomorph missense variants. Individuals with two LOF variants had significantly greater clinical burden than those with two pathogenic missense variants (p = 0.0001) [26]. This study revealed that statistically powered analyses coupled with systematic assessment of phenotype identifies a consistent pattern of clinical presentation and increase in disease burden that aligns with the functional consequences of pathogenic variants in CYP27A1.

More Sensitive Biochemical Testing Identifies Milder CTX Associated with CYP27A1 Hypomorphs

As an inborn error of metabolism, the gold standard for diagnosis of CTX is biochemical testing. 5α-Cholestanol has historically been the most commonly used biochemical marker. No genotype–phenotype association was reported between the genotype of a person with CTX and the cholestanol level of a person with CTX, and cholestanol is not utilized as a quantitative marker for the clinical severity of disease. In recent years clinical genetic testing has surged and new biochemical markers have been developed for testing of CTX [45–48]. These markers appear to be more sensitive than cholestanol and their use has led to reports of individuals with CTX who have elevations in these markers while having normal or slightly above-normal cholestanol [45, 49–56]. These individuals were noted generally to have a milder clinical course of CTX.

The genotypes of all individuals in literature reported to have a genetically confirmed diagnosis of CTX and normal or high-normal cholestanol levels when not taking medication (CDCA/cholic acid) (N = 10) were examined [45, 49–57]. All ten individuals were reported to have tendon xanthomas, without which they most likely would not have been diagnosed with CTX [57]. The second most common features were dementia and pyramidal signs, with 30% (3/10) displaying these features. The genotypes of these ten individuals showed that none had an LOF genotype [57]. Three individuals were homozygous for two hypomorphs. The remaining individuals had a variant for which there was no functional data. While more data on milder cases of CTX continue to emerge, critical analyses of these cases in the context of the larger CTX patient population can be leveraged to detect findings relevant to diagnosis, management, and prognosis of CTX.

Summary

Since the cloning of CYP27A1, the gene that is responsible for CTX, over 200 pathogenic variants have been identified in individuals with CTX. CTX occurs across all major global populations as well as some populations that have experienced population bottlenecks, with a frequency ranging from 1 in 80,000 to 1 in 340,000. CYP27A1 harbors canonical loss-of-function variants as well as missense variants that result in complete loss of enzyme function. In addition, other pathogenic missense variants retain some residual enzyme activity and are hypomorphs. Genotype–phenotype analyses based on functional genotype—loss of function versus hypomorph—revealed a phenotype–functional genotype association for CTX.
