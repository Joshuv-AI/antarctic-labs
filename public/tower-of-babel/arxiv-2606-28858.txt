Axions on de Sitter space


Abstract
We study a massless minimally coupled compact scalar, or axion, on global D D -dimensional de Sitter space (dS D ). From a Lorentzian perspective, we quantise the theory canonically, determine the quantum dS charges, and find that the axion zero mode supplies a quantum-mechanical factor beyond the oscillator Fock space ℱ osc \mathcal{F}_{\text{osc}} . The full Hilbert space is ℋ = L 2 ​ ( S 1 ) ⊗ ℱ osc \mathcal{H}=L^{2}(S^{1})\otimes\mathcal{F}_{\text{osc}} , with the integer quantum-mechanical momentum on L 2 ​ ( S 1 ) L^{2}(S^{1}) identified with the conserved U ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{U}(1) shift charge. The relevant single-particle unitary irreducible representation (UIR) of the dS group, SO ⁡ ( D , 1 ) \mathrm{SO}(D,1) , captures the oscillator sector, but misses the zero mode. We find that the neutral zero-particle state is dS -invariant and normalisable. Charged zero-particle states are normalisable, but only SO ⁡ ( D ) \mathrm{SO}(D) invariant. This implies that geodesic observers related by dS boosts do not agree on the number of particles in a charged sector, an effect absent in QFTs equipped only with the standard Bunch–Davies vacuum. We further compute field-strength Wightman two-point functions in charged sectors and find that they are Hadamard. For non-zero charge they are not dS -invariant at finite global times, but they are asymptotically so at early and late times. We complement this analysis with a Euclidean perspective. The ordinary D D -sphere path integral, 𝒵 S D \mathcal{Z}_{S^{D}} , written in terms of Harish-Chandra characters, has access only to the neutral sector. Charged sectors require vertex-operator insertions, and summing over them gives a decorated sphere path integral, 𝒵 ^ S D = 𝒵 QM ​ 𝒵 S D \widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{S^{D}}=\mathcal{Z}_{\text{QM}}\,\mathcal{Z}_{S^{D}} that captures the entire Hilbert space, with 𝒵 QM \mathcal{Z}_{\text{QM}} denoting the partition function of a quantum rotor at a dimension-dependent effective temperature. Finally, in dS 3 , we use the duality between an axion and a photon to translate our results to electromagnetism. The oscillator sector relates to the photon UIR, while the axion zero mode gives rise to magnetic monopole sectors, which are allowed by the topology of global dS 3 .

The early universe is believed to have passed through a period of rapid accelerated expansion, known as inflation

In the present paper, we study the massless minimally coupled scalar on

and we report a variety of interesting results.

To set the stage, we temporarily leave de Sitter and define the compact scalar on a general
D
-dimensional spacetime,

. By compact scalar, also called an axion or axion-like field, we mean a map from
D

\displaystyle\phi:X_{D}\rightarrow S^{1}~.

(1.1)

Equivalently, it is an ordinary real scalar field

\displaystyle\phi(x)\sim\phi(x)+2\pi~.

(1.2)

At the level of our analysis,

We conventionally take the scalar to be dimensionless, and the radius of the target-space circle to be 1. It participates in a quantum theory with action

\displaystyle S[\phi]=\int\differential[D]{x}\,\sqrt{-g}\ \quantity(-\frac{f^2}{2}\,g^{\mu\nu}\pd_\mu\phi\, \pd_\nu\phi- v(\phi))~,

(1.3)

[
26

1.2

In the free case,

\displaystyle\phi(x)\mapstochar\rightarrow\phi(x)+\alpha~,\qquad 0\leqslant\alpha<2\pi~,

(1.4)

Locally,

\partial_{\mu}\phi(x)
and higher derivatives, together with vertex operators,

\displaystyle V_{n}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{in\phi(x)}~.

(1.5)

Note that the vertex operators are only gauge-invariant when

Another defining property of the compact scalar theory is that, in contrast to the non-compact scalar, it allows for quantised winding. If spacetime contains a one-cycle, the scalar field can wind around its target-space circle. In differential form language, with
\differential{\phi}=\differential{x}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\phi

\displaystyle\int_{C}\differential{\phi}\in 2\pi\,\mathbb{Z}~,

(1.6)

{\text{dS}}_{2}

has no non-trivial one-cycles. However, this feature returns in other geometries with positive cosmological constant such as (extremal) Nariai black holes.

The many faces of axions

Before turning to the quantum theory of axions in de Sitter, it is useful to spell out where such fields come from. A common mechanism for compact scalars comes from gauge fields wrapped on compact cycles

Bosonisation.

In two dimensions, fermionic theories can often be written in bosonic variables. Formally, this is done by gauging the fermion number symmetry

[

,

\displaystyle S[\phi]=-\frac{f^{2}}{2}\int\differential[D]{x}\sqrt{-g}\;g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\,\partial_{\nu}\phi~.

2.1
Classical modes

The field equation that follows from

\displaystyle\Box\,\phi=0~.

We are interested in the set of mode solutions of this equation that can be obtained by analytically continuing the spherical harmonics on

In global coordinates, eq.

\displaystyle\quantity(
-\frac{\pd^2}{\pd t^2}
-(D-1)\tanh t\,\frac{\pd}{\pd t}
+\frac{\tilde{\Box}}{\cosh^2t}
)\phi(t,\boldsymbol{\theta})=0~,

(2.5)

where
\widetilde{\Box}
is the Laplacian on the unit

\displaystyle\int_{S^{D-1}}\differential{\!\vol}_{D-1}\,Y_{\ell\boldsymbol{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{*}~Y_{\ell^{\prime\prime}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime\prime}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime\prime}}~.

(2.7)

In the above,

is the Laplace-Beltrami operator on

The multi-index

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{D-2},\ldots,m_{1})~,\qquad\ell\geqslant m_{D-2}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant m_{2}\geqslant|m_{1}|~.

Here the entries before the last one are non-negative integers and

. For

. The number of allowed

\displaystyle N_{\ell,D}=\frac{(2\ell+D-2)(\ell+D-3)!}{\ell!(D-2)!}~,

. The constant harmonic is

\ell\geqslant 1

2.2
Canonical quantisation

The momentum density conjugate to
\phi
, determined from the action functional, is

\displaystyle\Pi(t,\boldsymbol{\theta})=\kappa_{D}~\sqrt{\widetilde{g}}~\cosh^{D-1}t\,~\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi(t,\boldsymbol{\theta})~.

(2.26)

We decompose the quantum field
\hat{\phi}
into its zero-mode
\hat{\phi}_{0}
and oscillator
\hat{\varphi}
parts, as

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}(t,\boldsymbol{\theta})=\hat{\phi}_{0}(t,\boldsymbol{\theta})+\hat{\varphi}(t,\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(2.27)

At equal times, the canonical commutation relation is

\displaystyle\commutator{\hat{\phi}(t,\vec{\theta})}{\hat{\Pi}(t,\vec{\theta}')}=i\,\delta(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})~,

(2.28)

where
\delta(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})
is defined by
\int_{S^{D-1}}\differential[D-1]{\theta'}\,\delta(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})f(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})=f(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\differential[D-1]{\theta'}

The spatially constant part of

\displaystyle\hat{p}=\int_{S^{D-1}}d^{D-1}\theta\,~\hat{\Pi}(t,\boldsymbol{\theta})=\kappa_{D}\opbraces{\volume}_{D-1}\cosh^{D-1}t\,~\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\phi}_{0}(t)~.

(2.29)

It can be readily shown that
\partial\hat{p}/\partial t=0
, as a direct consequence of the field equations.

The zero-mode field operator is

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{0}(t)=\hat{q}+\frac{\hat{p}}{\kappa_{D}}\,f_{D}(t)~,\qquad\commutator{\hat{q}}{\hat{p}}=i~,\qquad\hat{q}\sim\hat{q}+2\pi~.

(2.30)

\hat{\phi}

is an angle. Therefore, the zero-mode operator acts on the quantum-mechanical Hilbert space of the particle on the circle. As a result, the “angular momentum” operator
\hat{p}
has integer spectrum:

\displaystyle\hat{p}\ket{n}_{{\text{QM}}}=n\ket{n}_{{\text{QM}}}~,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}~,

(2.31)

where the subscript

These states span the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions on a circle,

Strictly speaking,

The full Hilbert space.

The oscillator vacuum, i.e. the standard Fock vacuum for the

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{\ell\boldsymbol{m}}\ket{0}=0~,\qquad\ell\geqslant 1~.

(2.40)

However, the full Hilbert space is spanned by states of the form

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}_{\text{QM}}\otimes\hat{a}_{\ell_{1}\boldsymbol{m}_{1}}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\ell_{2}\boldsymbol{m}_{2}}^{\dagger}\cdots\ket{0}~,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=L^{2}\quantity\big(S^1)\otimes\mathcal{F}_{\text{osc}}~.

(2.42)

It is clear that this Hilbert space harbours infinitely many “zero-particle” states, namely

\displaystyle\ket{\Omega_n}\coloneqq\ket{n}_{\text{QM}}\otimes\ket{0}~,\quad\mbox{with}\quad n=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots

(2.43)

and any linear combination thereof. Each

is an eigenstate of

(see
equation

However, there is another reasonable requirement that a candidate vacuum must satisfy: it must preserve dS isometries.
12
12

12

(2.45)

In contrast to the momentum eigenstates,

2.3
\mathrm{SO}(D,1)
UIRs in the space of classical modes

One can infer the transformation properties of states under spacetime isometries in a variety of ways. A commonly employed method is to calculate a correlation function, over some state, of operators whose behaviour under isometries is known. The transformation of the correlation function then reveals information about the properties of the state.

Here, we consider a more direct approach. First, we discuss the dS transformations of the classical Bunch–Davies modes. We then determine the quantum dS charges and consider their action directly on the QFT Hilbert space.

2.4
Classical dS charges

The energy-momentum tensor of

\displaystyle{T^{\mu\nu}}=\kappa_{D}R_{{\text{dS}}}^{2}\quantity( \partial^{\mu}\phi\, \partial^{\nu}\phi- \frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu} \partial^{\alpha}\phi\, \pd_{\alpha}\phi)~,

Hence, the two-point function in a charged “zero-particle” state restores full
\mathrm{SO}(D,1)
invariance at very late (and very early) times.

2.8
Comments on spontaneous symmetry breaking

As detailed in the introduction, the theory we consider arises in many cases as a theory of Goldstones, which would indicate spontaneous symmetry breaking (SSB). This is a delicate subject in de Sitter for two reasons. First, due to the compactness of its spatial slices at finite global time, and second, due to enhancement of infrared effects similarly to flat space QFT below the critical dimension
[
108
,
61
,
38
,
51
]
. A caveat to this is SSB of
(D-1)
-form symmetries, where the theory develops special states known as “(p)universes”
[
75
,
113
,
8
,
118
,
89
]
, allowing SSB in compact volume or low dimensions. This possibility was recently explored in
{\text{dS}}_{2}

[
4
]
.

In our case, the theory we consider does not exhibit spontaneous symmetry breaking (SSB) of its
\mathrm{U}(1)
shift symmetry
[
108
]
, unless it couples to discrete gauge fields
[
4
]
. However, let us note some qualitative similarities with SSB in flat space. At finite times, field-strength two-point functions
equation

2.89
are not
dS
-invariant, but at late (and early) times they are. So, the states
\ket{\Omega_{n\neq 0}}
, although not
dS
-invariant themselves, give rise to
dS
-invariant two-point functions at very late times.

Similarly, one can readily understand that two-point functions in the Fourier dual basis,
\ket*{\tilde{\Omega}_\vartheta}
, of zero-particle states
equation

2.45
, are also
dS
-invariant at late times. Moreover, these states are mapped to one another with the action of the symmetry operator
\mathrm{e}^{i\alpha\hat{p}}
, as in
equation

2.46
. Since all
\ket*{\tilde{\Omega}_\vartheta}
states are indistinguishable at this level, choosing any one of them would indicate SSB of the
\mathrm{U}(1)
shift symmetry. Of course, this is not truly the case, as none of these states is a
dS
-invariant vacuum, and there is no obvious reason (e.g. cluster decomposition) to pick such a state.

Furthermore, there is no clear order parameter. In flat space the natural order parameters would be vertex operators,
V_{n}(x)=\;\text{\scalebox{1.2}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip 0.0pt\hbox{}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{0.60277pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-1.65831pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}}}{\mathrm{e}^{in\phi(x)}}\text{\scalebox{1.2}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip 0.0pt\hbox{}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{0.60277pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-1.65831pt}{\hbox{.}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}}}\;
. A non-vanishing neutral two-point function thereof at large separation would indicate SSB. Here, vertex operator two-point functions decay very rapidly at late times:

\displaystyle\matrixelement{\Omega_0}{\hat{V}_{-n}(t,\vec{\theta}) \hat{V}_n\qty(t,\vec{\theta}')}{\Omega_0}\sim\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha\,n^{2}\,\absolutevalue{t}}~,

(2.93)

for some positive constant
\alpha
, with two-point functions in other
n\neq 0
zero-particle states behaving similarly. This behaviour can be inferred, for instance, from
[
108
]
or
equation

4.21
. We find ourselves in the delicate situation where the would-be order parameter has diluted completely at late times and we lose access to it, but the available observables (e.g. field-strength two-point functions) at late times behave in a manner compatible with symmetry breaking. Barring
(D-1)
-form symmetries, this is likely as close as one can get to SSB in
dS

Monopole sector.

To reproduce the duality of electromagnetism with the compact massless scalar theory at the quantum level, the corresponding Hilbert spaces should come out isomorphic. How the oscillator sector matches was made clear above. Let us now see how the zero-mode sector of the massless compact scalar manifests itself as a monopole sector from the electromagnetic viewpoint.

Because global
{\text{dS}}_{3}
contains a non-trivial 2-cycle — its spatial slice,
S^{2}
— the field strength
\hat{F}_{\mu\nu}
is not simply expressed as the exterior derivative of a (globally well-defined) gauge potential. In particular, since
\hat{F}_{\mu\nu}
is a closed two-form on global
{\text{dS}}_{3}

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{\mu\nu}=\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mu\nu}+\hat{F}^{({\text{osc}})}_{\mu\nu}=\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mu\nu}+2\partial_{[\mu}\hat{A}_{\nu]}~.

(3.11)

Here
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mu\nu}
is the harmonic piece of the field strength, satisfying

\partial_{[\mu}\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\rho\sigma]}=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla^{\mu}\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mu\nu}=0~,

(3.12)

off-shell. The operator
\hat{A}_{\mu}
is the standard globally well-defined oscillator gauge-potential one-form.

The duality
equation

1.12

D=3
and written in Lorentzian signature, reads:

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{\mu\nu}=2\pi f^{2}\,\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\,\partial^{\rho}\hat{\phi}~.

(3.13)

The oscillator piece,
\hat{\varphi}
, of the scalar field is directly mapped to
\hat{F}^{({\text{osc}})}_{\mu\nu}
. This is encoded, for instance in
equation

2.56

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mu\nu}=2\pi f^{2}\,\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\,\partial^{\rho}\hat{\phi}_{0}(t)~.

(3.14)

Utilising this, it follows that the harmonic field strength has vanishing electric component,
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{ti}=0
, while its spatial components are:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{ij}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\pi\,\hat{p}}{R_{{\text{dS}}}^{2}\,\opbraces{\volume}_{2}}\frac{\epsilon_{tij}}{\cosh^{2}{t}}=-\frac{2\pi\,\hat{p}}{R_{{\text{dS}}}^{2}\,\opbraces{\volume}_{2}}\widetilde{\epsilon}_{ij}~.

(3.15)

This corresponds to the magnetic field of a monopole:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{B}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{t\mu\nu}\hat{\mathcal{F}}^{\mu\nu}=-\frac{2\pi\,\hat{p}}{R_{{\text{dS}}}^{2}\,\opbraces{\volume}_{2}\,\cosh^{2}{t}}=-\frac{\hat{p}}{2R_{{\text{dS}}}^{2}\cosh^{2}{t}}~.

(3.16)

In fact, expressing

\displaystyle\nabla^{\mu}\quantity(\star{\hat{F}}^{(\t{osc})})_{\mu}=\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{B}}}{\partial t}~.

(3.18)

Hence

is a magnetic current sourcing Maxwell’s equations for the oscillator part.

From the above discussion, it follows that the operator
\hat{p}
is exactly the monopole charge. Its eigenvalues are quantised due to Dirac quantisation.
Its conjugate,
\hat{q}

5
Outlook

In this work we studied axions on a fixed de Sitter spacetime. While there are many questions we left unanswered, the most interesting one is arguably to let spacetime itself fluctuate. Coupled to gravity, axions are known to source new saddles, such as Euclidean axion-wormholes
[
70
]
. In fact, such wormholes are not semiclassical in the axion frame
[
129
]
, necessitating a quantum account of the axion dynamics. Recent work suggests that axions also support new saddles in spacetimes with positive cosmological constant
[
3
,
64
]
. These saddles have rich topology, making them natural testbeds for revisiting the questions we answered here on
{\text{dS}}_{D}
and
S^{D}
.

As a first approximation to the gravitational path integral with
\Lambda>0
, one can study one-loop contributions around all such saddles, see
[
84
]
for a recent account of some. It seems likely that, although the empty sphere path integral is blind to the charged sectors,
subleading saddles are sensitive to them (whenever such charged sectors are allowed by the saddle topology). Indeed, the “kettleball” saddles of
[
3
]
have topology
S^{1}\times S^{D-1}
, and therefore probe the shift-symmetry sectors. Interestingly, they also have access to the dual
(D-2)
-form symmetry associated with the conservation of higher-dimensional vortices, an effect present neither in Lorentzian
{\text{dS}}_{D}
nor in the leading Euclidean sphere saddle. This programme would give a concrete handle on the gravitational path integral in spacetimes with positive cosmological constant. A related approach appears in
[
14
]
, in which the dynamics around new instantons made available in Einstein–Maxwell theory
[
39
]
is investigated. The interplay between additional fields and couplings, and the emergence of new Euclidean saddles
32
32

32

Whether such saddles admit a Lorentzian interpretation is an interesting question in its own right.

deserves further investigation.
