Fragile Thoughts: How Large Language Models Handle Chain-of-Thought Perturbations
Ashwath Vaithinathan Aravindan, Mayank Kejriwal

Abstract
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting has emerged as a foundational technique for eliciting reasoning from Large Language Models (LLMs), yet the robustness of this approach to corruptions in intermediate reasoning steps remains poorly understood. This paper presents a comprehensive empirical evaluation of LLM robustness to a structured taxonomy of 5 CoT perturbation types: MathError, UnitConversion, Sycophancy, SkippedSteps, and ExtraSteps . We evaluate 13 models spanning three orders of magnitude in parameter count, testing their ability to complete mathematical reasoning tasks despite perturbations injected at different points in the reasoning chain. Our key findings reveal heterogeneous vulnerability patterns: MathError perturbations produce the most severe degradation in small models (50-60% accuracy loss) but show strong scaling benefits; UnitConversion remains challenging across all scales (20-30% loss even for largest models); ExtraSteps incur minimal accuracy degradation (0-6%) regardless of scale; Sycophancy produces modest effects (7% loss for small models); and SkippedSteps cause intermediate damage (15% loss). Scaling relationships follow power-law patterns, with model size serving as a protective factor against some perturbations but offering limited defense against dimensional reasoning tasks. These findings have direct implications for deploying LLMs in multi-stage reasoning pipelines and underscore the necessity of task-specific robustness assessments and mitigation strategies. Introduction

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting has become an important technique for eliciting complex reasoning from Large Language Models (LLMs).
20
demonstrated that providing intermediate reasoning steps dramatically improves performance on mathematical problem-solving and multi-step reasoning tasks. Building on this,
9
showed that even zero-shot CoT prompting, using simple phrases such as “Let’s think step by step”, can unlock latent reasoning capabilities in LLMs without annotated examples. This success has established CoT as a
de facto
standard in prompting strategies for reasoning-intensive applications.

Yet this success raises a fundamental question: to what extent are LLMs genuinely performing step-by-step logical reasoning, and to what extent are they exploiting surface-level patterns learned during training
16
;
1
? When a model produces a correct final answer following a CoT trajectory, does it verify the consistency of intermediate steps, or does it simply correlate reasoning text with expected outputs
2
? This distinction carries immediate practical implications for high-stakes applications such as finance
4
, medicine
13
, and scientific discovery
11
, where understanding whether models achieve accuracy through robust reasoning or through brittle pattern matching is essential for safe deployment.

Recent empirical work has exposed concerning fragility in CoT reasoning.
5
demonstrated that single-character typographical errors significantly degrade accuracy on mathematical benchmarks.
14
showed that semantically adversarial perturbations to code-reasoning problems reduce accuracy by over 42%.
26
identified a “snowball” effect where errors early in reasoning chains amplify through subsequent steps. These findings collectively highlight the brittleness of LLM reasoning to input corruptions. However, existing studies focus narrowly on specific perturbation types (typos, code-level attacks) or isolated models, leaving unanswered the question of how diverse, reasoning-specific corruptions affect multiple model families across different scales and architectures.

In real-world deployment, reasoning chains may be incomplete, contain computational errors, or originate from upstream systems of varying quality. Assessing how LLMs handle such realistic corruptions is essential for building trustworthy multi-stage reasoning pipelines. Yet no prior work has systematically evaluated a broad range of models against a comprehensive, structured taxonomy of reasoning-specific perturbations.

To fill this gap, we present the first systematic evaluation of LLM robustness to CoT perturbations. Our contributions are:

1.

A structured perturbation taxonomy
comprising 5 reasoning-specific types: mathematical errors, unit conversion, sycophancy, skipped steps, and extra steps;

2.

A broad empirical evaluation
across 13 language models spanning three orders of magnitude in parameter count, revealing how robustness scales with model size and varies across perturbation types;

3.

Quantitative characterization
of differential scaling relationships, showing that robustness improvements are heterogeneous: steep for mathematical errors, shallow for sycophancy and skipped steps, and absent for redundant information.

Related Work

Input robustness.
Several studies have examined LLM sensitivity to corrupted inputs.
16
found that generative LLMs are surprisingly robust to common noise types such as spelling errors and OCR artifacts, yet
1
showed that models trained on clean data produce unpredictable outputs under even minor perturbations like single typos. At enterprise scale,
2
demonstrated that prompt formatting or word-order variations can degrade performance by up to 40 percentage points, with smaller models sometimes outperforming larger ones on consistency metrics. Our work shifts focus from surface-level input noise to perturbations within intermediate reasoning steps, a distinct failure regime.

Chain-of-thought reasoning and its fragility.
CoT prompting
20
;
9
has become the standard approach for eliciting multi-step reasoning, yet questions persist about whether CoT chains reflect genuine reasoning or exploit spurious patterns.
17
found evidence of “Clever Hans” behavior, and benchmarks such as ProcessBench
25
and DeltaBench
7
reveal that correct final answers often mask significant internal reasoning errors. On the adversarial side,
5
showed that single-character typos degrade accuracy substantially,
21
introduced backdoor attacks on reasoning chains, and
14
;
22
demonstrated that CoT structure can be exploited in code generation.
26
identified a snowball effect in which small errors amplify through subsequent steps and proposed adversarial fine-tuning as a countermeasure.

Mitigation and bias.
Training-based approaches
23
;
6
enable self-verification through supervised and reinforcement learning, with
24
showing that late-chain errors are disproportionately harmful. Detection mechanisms such as Chain-of-Scrutiny
10
and SafeChain
8
target backdoor attacks, while prompt engineering strategies
18
;
15
aim to minimize perturbation impact. Orthogonally,
12
and
19
uncovered systematic authority and bandwagon biases in CoT reasoning, showing that even incorrect expert endorsements increase both error rates and model confidence.

Unlike prior work, which typically examines isolated perturbation types (typos, backdoors) or individual models, we systematically evaluate a structured taxonomy of five reasoning-specific perturbation types across 13 models spanning three orders of magnitude in scale, characterizing both accuracy impact and qualitative error-handling strategies.

3
Methodology

In this section, we describe the construction of our perturbed dataset and the experimental framework used to evaluate model robustness.

3.1
Dataset and Task Formulation

We utilize the GSM8k dataset
3
, a collection of high-quality grade school math word problems, with a single integer as the final answer. Our evaluation focuses on a partial-trace completion task: given a question
q
from the dataset and a partial reasoning trace
\tau
consisting of the first few steps of the full solution, the model is tasked with generating the remaining steps and the final answer. We systematically introduce perturbations of different types into this partial trace to produce a corrupted trace
\tau^{\prime}
, allowing us to study how models recognize and respond to errors injected at specific points in the reasoning chain.

3.2
Perturbation Types

We introduced 5 distinct types of perturbations into the CoT solutions. These modifications range from logical errors to noise, designed to test different facets of model reasoning.

1.

A random intermediate equation is modified to yield an incorrect result (e.g.,
3+4=8
). This tests the model’s ability to detect and correct (or be misled by) explicit calculation failures.

3.3
Evaluation Framework

We evaluate the robustness of 13 language models across diverse providers and parameter scales shown in Table
1
. For each problem, we evaluate the model’s completion given two different partial solutions: the correct CoT and the perturbed CoT. The primary metric for evaluation is accuracy on the completion task. To further analyze the impact of perturbations, we measure the difference in accuracy between clean and perturbed conditions for each model. This allows us to quantify robustness across the different models and identifies which models are most susceptible to specific perturbation types. Formally, we define the robustness of a model
M
to a perturbation type
p
as follows: given a problem
q
with a clean reasoning trace
\tau
and a final answer
a
, the model’s robustness is characterized by comparing its completion accuracy on the clean trace
\text{Acc}_{M}(q,\tau)
against its accuracy on the perturbed trace
\text{Acc}_{M}(q,\tau^{\prime})
, where
\tau^{\prime}=\text{perturb}_{p}(\tau)
. The
degradation
caused by perturbation
p
is:

\Delta\text{Acc}_{p}(M)=\text{Acc}_{M}(q,\tau)-\text{Acc}_{M}(q,\tau^{\prime}).

Two examples are discussed that illustrate how unit conversion perturbations expose different failure modes in model reasoning. In Example
4.2.1
, Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.5 demonstrates
unit error correction behavior
: despite the perturbed partial solution presenting a mathematically correct intermediate result but in different units (10,800 seconds instead of 180 minutes), the model explicitly recognizes this inconsistency, recomputes the correct value, and delivers the correct final answer. This exemplifies the robustness of larger models to reasoning inconsistencies. By contrast, in Example
4.2.2
, OpenAI GPT 4o-mini exhibits
unit error propagation
: the model accepts the perturbed intermediate value, builds upon it, and transparently converts the erroneous result further, ultimately reporting 9,600 seconds as the final answer. Remarkably, OpenAI GPT 4o-mini never questions the semantic inconsistency (the problem asks for minutes but the solution provides seconds), suggesting that semantic coherence tracking remains orthogonal to arithmetic competence. Where mathematical errors often elicit explicit verification or backtracking, unit errors frequently cascade silently, making them a particularly insidious form of intermediate perturbation.

It is worth noting that unit conversion results should be interpreted with caution, as many questions do not explicitly specify the units in which the answer must be provided. For instance, questions like
"How long will it take?"
do not specify whether the answer should be given in days, hours, minutes, or seconds. However, models that correctly handle unit consistency tend to maintain alignment with the units provided in the problem statement, providing their final answers in the same units as those given in the question. This suggests that robust models use explicit unit grounding from the problem text as a guiding principle, rather than assuming arbitrary unit conversions are acceptable.

5
Discussion & Limitations

Our findings reveal that LLM robustness to CoT perturbations is neither uniform nor simply a function of scale. The five perturbation types expose fundamentally different failure modes with distinct scaling behaviors. MathError shows the strongest size-dependent protection (slope
-0.170
), with catastrophic degradation in small models (50–60%) but only 5–10% loss above 500B parameters, suggesting that larger models develop qualitatively different arithmetic verification mechanisms. UnitConversion, by contrast, remains challenging at all scales (20–30% loss even for the largest models), indicating that dimensional reasoning may require explicit architectural support or training signal that general language modeling does not provide. ExtraSteps incurs virtually no cost regardless of scale (slope
-0.001
), implying that robustness to redundant information is a property established in right from the low parameter scale and not further improved through scaling. Sycophancy and SkippedSteps occupy a middle ground with similar moderate slopes (
\approx-0.031
) but different baselines: sycophancy produces the smallest absolute drops (
\leq
7% for small models), while skipped steps cause moderate degradation (
\approx
15%), reflecting a partial but incomplete capacity to reconstruct missing reasoning.

These patterns carry direct implications for deploying LLMs in multi-stage reasoning pipelines, where intermediate steps may originate from upstream systems of varying quality. Mathematical pipelines require external numerical verification, as models cannot reliably detect or correct embedded arithmetic errors. Dimensional tracking tasks like unit conversion, coordinate systems and measurement uncertainty should not be delegated without external constraint enforcement, given consistent vulnerability across all scales. The tolerance for extra steps suggests that providing verbose or redundant reasoning paths does not significantly harm performance, offering a practical mitigation strategy. However, models do not reliably filter false authority signals, so user-facing reasoning interfaces should not depend on self-correction of misinformation embedded in prompts.

This study, while comprehensive in scope, operates within several important constraints that shape its findings and point toward future directions. Firstly, all experiments use relatively short reasoning chains (3–10 steps) from GSM8K, a dataset of moderate mathematical difficulty. It remains unclear whether the observed scaling relationships persist under longer chains, harder problems, or perturbations injected deeper within extended reasoning sequences. Additionally, while our 13 models span diverse training paradigms, including supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning-based post-training for reasoning, we do not isolate training methodology as an independent variable. A dedicated comparison of identically-scaled models trained via different paradigms would clarify whether specific perturbation types can be addressed through targeted training rather than scale alone.

6
Conclusion

This work addresses a critical gap in LLM reasoning robustness by systematically evaluating 13 models across 5 perturbation types spanning three orders of magnitude. Our primary contributions are: (1) a structured perturbation taxonomy grounded in real-world reasoning failures; (2) the first broad empirical evaluation of LLM robustness across diverse model families and scales; and (3) quantitative characterization of how model size protects against different reasoning corruptions. The findings challenge the assumption that scaling alone ensures robustness. While mathematical error detection improves dramatically with size (50-60% loss in 3B models vs. 5-10% in 100B+ models), unit conversion remains difficult even in the largest models (20-30% loss), and extra steps incur minimal cost regardless of architecture. These results have immediate practical implications: organizations deploying LLMs in multi-stage reasoning systems must design task-specific validation mechanisms rather than rely on scale alone. Mathematical pipelines require numerical verification, dimensional tasks require external constraint enforcement, and reasoning chains should be validated for consistency and completeness. The heterogeneous vulnerability landscape suggests that trustworthy LLM reasoning requires not just scale but also architectural innovations, targeted training procedures for robustness, and externally-enforced verification for high-stakes applications. This work provides the empirical foundation for prioritizing such efforts.
