The Power of Second Order Methods for Sequence Preconditioning
Annie Marsden, Elad Hazan

Abstract
Sequence prediction methods for dynamical systems with long memory, i.e. marginally stable systems, typically achieve regret that grows polynomially with the hidden dimension of the underlying generative model. Universal Sequence Preconditioning (USP) 16 is a method that compresses any sequence which comes from a linear dynamical system into a “preconditioned” sequence which requires exponentially shorter memory for accurate prediction. However, the preconditioned sequence yields exponentially larger diameters and gradients, hindering USP from unlocking optimal regret bounds. Inspired by the minimum description length principle, we show that the Vovk-Azoury-Warmuth (VAW) algorithm is naturally matched to the USP regime. Indeed, it takes advantage of the memory compression while remaining robust to the exponential explosion of the diameter. We prove that combining USP with VAW achieves astoundingly strong results: for any marginally-stable linear dynamical system, this algorithm achieves polylogarithmic regret 𝒪 ⁡ ( log 3 ⁡ T ) \mathcal{O}\left(\log^{3}T\right) even in the presence of asymmetric hidden transition matrices. Finally, we extend the applicability of USP beyond bounded-spectrum systems by providing new complex-analytic bounds on Chebyshev polynomials, allowing for systems with constant complex arguments.



1
Introduction

The central challenge in sequence prediction is the simultaneous presence of long memory and high hidden dimension of the underlying generative process.
Universal Sequence Preconditioning (USP)
16
addresses this challenge by transforming the sequence into one that can be predicted with short memory, without sacrificing predictive accuracy. USP is “universal” because its transformation is signal-agnostic, and should provide a clear benefit to any downstream learning algorithm.

This sounds too good to be true. However, previous work
16
showed that such reductions are possible for signals that arise from certain linear dynamical systems. The method they develop is to convolve the original signal with the coefficients of the Chebyshev polynomial. This construction achieves an exponential compression of memory, reducing the effective memory to be logarithmic in sequence length (or horizon).
However, this comes at a cost: the Chebyshev coefficients grow exponentially large with the degree, resulting in large optimal diameter.
This creates a mismatch with first-order learning methods, which scale linearly with optimal diameter, yielding suboptimal regret.

In this work, we resolve this tension using second order online learning methods. At a high level, the effectiveness of a preconditioner is governed by two quantities: the effective memory of the transformed representation (number of parameters) and the magnitude of the optimal diameter. In the setting of USP, we can think of the preconditioned signal as a compressed representation of the original signal. The compressed representation requires a history which is linear in the degree of the Chebyshev polynomial, however the coefficient magnitude grows very large. This evokes the Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle
18
;
8
, which seeks to minimize the combined cost of a model’s structural complexity and the bit-length required to represent its parameters. For polynomials, the minimum description length principle suggests that the most efficient representation balances the degree of the polynomial and the logarithm of the coefficients magnitude.

Inspired by this principle, we observe that the regret of the Vovk–Azoury–Warmuth (VAW) forecaster
1
;
22
matches this balance: it grows linearly with the effective memory and only logarithmically with the the optimal diameter. Applying VAW to USP results in an astoundingly strong algorithm– its regret is polylogarithmic with respect to the horizon and the hidden dimension, and applies to systems with asymmetric transition matrices. This greatly improves upon the original regret bound
\mathcal{O}(T^{11/13})
of
16
which only applies to systems with eigenvalues with vanishing complex component.

Moreover, by proving new complex-analytic bounds for monic Chebyshev polynomials, we weaken the original restriction for USP that the argument of the complex eigenvalues vanish at the rate
o(1/\log^{2}T)
. We can now tolerate complex eigenvalues with constant argument.

1.1
Our Results

We resolve the fundamental tension between universal sequence preconditioning and the exponentially large gradient norms it induces by using second-order online learning methods. Our main contributions are as follows:

Logarithmic Regret for Sequence Prediction:

We propose a novel algorithm that applies the Vovk-Azoury-Warmuth (VAW) forecaster to raw sequences while concurrently learning the preconditioning coefficients. We obtain cumulative prediction error guarantee of
\mathcal{O}\left(Y^{2}\log^{3}(1+TYS)\right)
, where
Y
is the maximum norm of the output,
T
is the horizon, and
S
represents terms specific to the generative linear dynamical system (the norms of the input and output projection matrices and the “condition number” of the diagonalization of the hidden transition matrix). For the formal statement of our main result, see Theorem
2
.

Because the regret of VAW scales only logarithmically with the norm of the comparator, it successfully absorbs the exponential parameter blowup inherent in Chebyshev preconditioning. While previous first-order approaches achieved a polynomial cumulative regret bound of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{11/13})
, our second-order VAW approach exponentially improves this to optimal polylogarithmic
\mathcal{O}(\log^{3}(T))
regret.

Extension to Systems with Constant Complex Arguments:

We extend the theoretical guarantees of Universal Sequence Preconditioning (USP) to a strictly broader class of dynamical systems. We establish new, tight complex-analytic bounds for monic Chebyshev polynomials (Lemma
1
), proving that the preconditioner’s approximation error remains exponentially vanishing even when the underlying system’s eigenvalues have a constant complex argument.

1.2
Related Work

Our work is most closely related to the literature on prediction in latent linear dynamical systems, rather than recovery. Early spectral-filtering results demonstrated online competitiveness with the best LDS predictor without explicit system identification, but relied on strong spectral structure like symmetry
11
.
10
later extended spectral filtering to general latent LDSs using a convex relaxation that accommodates phases and no longer requires a symmetric transition matrix. However, in the asymmetric setting their guarantee still has polynomial dependence on the horizon and polynomial dependence on the hidden dimension, so it does not yield a polylogarithmic cumulative prediction error bound in the regime we study. Polynomial in
d
and
T
bounds are also a classical consequence of the Cayley–Hamilton theorem and realization theory; see, e.g.,
13
;
4
.

A second line of work studies
marginally stable
systems more directly.
7
proved no-regret prediction guarantees for marginally stable systems with polynomial system parameter dependence. In the fully observed adversarial setting their bounds remain polynomial in the horizon, while their stronger polylogarithmic-in-
T
results rely either on stochastic assumptions or on a reduction to finite-memory autoregressive prediction. These results aren’t direct comparators since our focus is absolute cumulative prediction error for a noiseless input–output LDS.

There is also a body of work on partially observed prediction through the Kalman filter. SLIP gives polylogarithmic regret for partially observed LDS prediction under stochastic noise, but its guarantee is stated relative to the Kalman predictor and crucially assumes that the closed-loop matrix
A-KC
is diagonalizable with real eigenvalues
17
. Likewise, Tsiamis and Pappas obtain logarithmic regret for online learning of the Kalman filter for nonexplosive systems, including marginally stable ones, again with performance measured against the Kalman predictor
21
. These papers show that polylogarithmic-in-
T
prediction is possible under stronger stochastic/filtering assumptions.

The direct asymmetric comparison closest in spirit to our setting is the recent work of Marsden and Hazan, which handles asymmetric marginally stable LDSs and removes hidden-dimension dependence from the regret bound
15
. However, their horizon dependence remains polynomial, so even after standardizing to the realizable noiseless setting, this line of work falls into the
O(\mathrm{poly}(T),\mathrm{polylog}(d))
regime rather than the polylogarithmic-in-
T
regime.

Finally, there is a large recovery-oriented literature on learning LDS realizations from input–output data, including semiparametric least squares, stochastic subspace identification, multiscale Hankel/SVD methods, and moment-based recovery
19
;
20
;
14
;
2
. These methods can be used to form predictors. However, their guarantees when converted to cumulative prediction error inevitably lead to a polynomial dependence on the hidden dimension. Related high-dimensional identification results with logarithmic dependence on dimension, such as Fattahi’s Markov-parameter learning result, require stronger inherent-stability assumptions and therefore do not directly apply to the marginally stable regime considered here
6
. In short, prior work obtains at most two of the following three properties simultaneously: marginal stability, genuinely asymmetric/complex-spectrum hidden dynamics, and polylogarithmic cumulative prediction error with only polylogarithmic dependence on hidden dimension.

Second-Order Online Learning.

In the realm of online convex optimization, second-order methods are renowned for achieving logarithmic regret for strongly or exponentially concave cost functions. Two of the most prominent algorithms in this space are the Online Newton Step (ONS)
9
and the Vovk–Azoury–Warmuth (VAW) forecaster
22
;
1
. However, standard regret guarantees for ONS depend explicitly on the diameter of the decision set. In the USP setting, where the Chebyshev transformation yields a comparator with an exponentially large magnitude, any diameter-dependent bound becomes vacuous.

Our approach critically relies on the VAW algorithm to circumvent this limitation. A defining property of VAW is that its regret bound is independent of the domain diameter; rather, the magnitude of the comparator enters the bound only logarithmically. This exact property is what allows us to absorb the exponential blowup of the preconditioner’s parameters and achieve tight (polylogarithmically) regret rates of
\mathcal{O}\left(\log^{3}(T)/T\right)
, even when accounting for approximation error.

Minimum Description Length (MDL) and Information Theory.

The connection between online learning, prediction, and data compression is a foundational concept in information theory. The Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle
18
;
8
posits that the best statistical model is the one that maximally compresses the data, balancing the complexity of the model itself against the cost of describing the data given the model. The regret of an online learning algorithm is intimately tied to the redundancy in universal coding
3
. By framing USP through the lens of MDL, we demonstrate that second-order methods achieve an optimal compression scheme for LDS: the effective memory represents the model’s structural complexity, while the logarithmic regret represents an optimal bit-length coding of the exponentially large parameter space.

2
Theoretical Results Overview

For clarity of presentation, we consider a single-input, single-output Linear Dynamical System where
d_{in}=d_{out}=1
. At each time step
t
, we observe an input
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
and an output
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
according to the law:

\displaystyle\textbf{h}_{t}=\textbf{A}\textbf{h}_{t-1}+\textbf{B}u_{t}\qquad\qquad y_{t}=\textbf{C}\textbf{h}_{t}.

(1)

We refer to
A
as the hidden transition matrix. The hidden state
\textbf{h}_{t}
can be factored out giving

\displaystyle y_{t}=\sum_{s=0}^{t}\textbf{C}\textbf{A}^{s}\textbf{B}u_{t-s}.

For
\theta_{s}=\textbf{C}\textbf{A}^{s}\textbf{B}
for
s=0,\dots,T
, the observation is equivalently
y_{t}=\sum_{s=0}^{t}\theta_{s}u_{t-s}
. Therefore in order to predict a linear dynamical system up to horizon
T
, it suffices to learn
T
parameters (one for each value of
\textbf{C}\textbf{A}^{s}\textbf{B}
for
s=0,\dots,T
).

The core insight of Universal Sequence Preconditioning
16
is that the current observation
y_{t}
can be represented, up to negligible error, using only
\mathcal{O}(\log(T))
parameters and history. For history window
n=\lceil\log((T\cdot||\textbf{C}||\cdot||\textbf{B}||)/\delta)\rceil
, let
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
be the coefficients of the
n
-th degree monic Chebyshev polynomial (so
p_{n}^{\text{cheby}}(x)=x^{n}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_{n-j}x^{j}
with implicit
c_{0}=1
).
Algebraic manipulation shows that

y_{t}=-\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}y_{t-i}}_{\aleph_{0}}+\underbrace{\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{s}c_{i}\textbf{C}\textbf{A}^{s-i}\textbf{B}\right)u_{t-s}}_{\aleph_{1}}+\underbrace{\sum_{s=0}^{t-n}\textbf{C}p_{n}^{\text{cheby}}(\textbf{A})\textbf{A}^{s}\textbf{B}u_{t-n-s}}_{\aleph_{2}}.

(2)

Since the monic
n
-th degree Chebyshev polynomial satisfies
\max_{x\in[-1,1]}\lvert p_{n}^{\text{cheby}}(x)\rvert\leq 2^{-(n-1)}
, if
A
’s eigenvalues fall in this range, then the term
\aleph_{2}
becomes negligible for
n
large enough. The authors show that you can extend this property to the complex plane with argument bounded by
1/64n^{2}
instead of just the interval
[-1,1]
, allowing asymmetric hidden transition matrices. In summary, the seminal work shows that by defining parameter,

\theta^{\text{USP}}_{s}:=\sum_{i=0}^{s}c_{i}\textbf{C}\textbf{A}^{s-i}\textbf{B},\text{ with }c_{0}=1,

(3)

then

y_{t}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}y_{t-i}+\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}\theta^{\text{USP}}_{s}u_{t-s}+\epsilon_{t},

(4)

where
\lvert\epsilon_{t}\rvert\leq T\cdot||\textbf{C}||\cdot||\textbf{B}||\cdot\kappa\cdot 2^{-n}
, where
\kappa
is the condition number of the matrix diagonalizing
A
, as long as the complex eigenvalues of
A
have argument bounded by
1/64n^{2}
.

Equation
4
suggests a simple learning algorithm— learn
\theta^{\text{USP}}
via regression. A naive application of first order learning methods gives regret which scales linearly with the norm of this parameter. This is unfortunate since this norm grows exponentially with
n
; from Lemma 3.2 of
16
,
\max_{i=0,\dots,n}\lvert c_{i}\rvert
can grow as large as
2^{0.3n}
. Although
\theta^{\text{USP}}
can have a tiny dimension which is only logarithmic in
T
, its norm does not reflect the reduction in domain. This is where the insight of our work comes in. Inspired by the minimum description length principle, we consider applying a second order learning method to sequence preconditioning. Critically, second order learning methods do not treat the
dimension
and the
norm
of the learnable parameter space as equal citizens in the way that first order methods do.

The error of the preconditioning method has two components: one from approximation and the other from learning/estimation. While Chebyshev polynomials yield optimal exponential decay for approximation error, the learning error—previously relying on first-order methods—can be significantly improved. Specifically, the original USP algorithm uses online gradient descent to learn the parameters of the model which grow exponentially with the degree of the polynomial. We suspect that this tradeoff is necessary, formalized in the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
p_{n}(x)=x^{n}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_{j}x^{j}
denote any monic polynomial. If there exist global constants
C,c>0
such that for all
n\geq 1
,

\max_{x\in[-1,1]}\lvert p_{n}(x)\rvert\leq C\cdot 2^{-cn}
then there must also exist constants
B,b>0
such that for all
n\geq 1
,
\max_{j\in[n-1]}\lvert c_{j}\rvert\geq B2^{bn}
.

In Appendix
B
we prove a weaker statement than the above in which we assume
c=1
. Regardless of the truth of Conjecture
1
, we observe that by using the VAW algorithm we can obtain a regret bound which grows logarithmically with these large coefficients, unlocking tight (up to logarithms) regret bounds.

The second key insight is that we can strengthen the result analyzing the Chebyshev polynomial on the complex plane to guarantee that even if
A
has eigenvalues with constant complex argument, we achieve exponentially vanishing error. We state the details in Section
2.2
.

2.2
Bounding the Chebyshev Polynomial for Constant Arguments

The original USP analysis required the imaginary part of the eigenvalues to shrink as
n
increased. We introduce a tighter bound demonstrating that
M_{n}(z)
remains exponentially small even when evaluated over a constant complex argument.

Lemma 1
.

Let
M_{n}(z)
be the monic Chebyshev polynomial of degree
n
. For any decay constant
p\in(0,1)
, if we assume
|\arg(z)|\leq s
where
s\leq\left(\frac{1-p}{12}\right)^{2}
, then for sufficiently large
n
,
|M_{n}(z)|\leq 2^{-pn}
.

The proof of this lemma requires establishing tight bounds on the complex trigonometric functions and their inverses. The full proof is deferred to Appendix A.

Next, these results are consistent with the main hypothesis of our paper: VAW is much more stable than first-order methods

3.1
Signal Norm and Preconditioning Dynamics

Our theoretical results are impeded by the inability to prove that the preconditioned target signal will have small norm. If we could bound the norm of the preconditioned signal that didn’t grow with the size of the preconditioning coefficients then two important changes could be made to our results: 1) we would not need to learn the autoregressive coefficients and 2) we could just as easily apply Online Newton Step (ONS) to get the state of the art regret bounds. Empirically, however, we see that the preconditioned target signal actually has much smaller norm than the raw signal in many regimes. Figures

Conclusion

Summary

We addressed a fundamental tension in sequence prediction for marginally stable linear dynamical systems: the trade-off between effective memory compression and the exponential explosion of parameter magnitudes. By interpreting Universal Sequence Preconditioning (USP) through the lens of the Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle, we established that second-order online learning methods—specifically the Vovk-Azoury-Warmuth (VAW) forecaster—are uniquely equipped to absorb the parameter blowup induced by Chebyshev preconditioning. Our theoretical framework yields near-optimal guarantees. By shifting the learning burden from the parameter norm to the effective dimension, we exponentially improved the cumulative prediction regret from the
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{11/13})
bounds of prior first-order approaches to
\mathcal{O}(\text{polylog}(T))
, completely eliminating polynomial dependencies on both the time horizon and the hidden state dimension. Furthermore, we introduced novel complex-analytic bounds for monic Chebyshev polynomials, successfully extending the theoretical viability of USP to a strictly broader class of dynamical systems with constant complex arguments. Empirically, our evaluations robustly corroborate these theoretical insights. The Chebyshev-preconditioned VAW algorithm consistently outperforms both first-order methods and adaptive-polynomial approaches, particularly in adverse regimes characterized by large time horizons, high-dimensional hidden states, and highly correlated input sequences.

Limitations

Our theoretically analyzed VAW algorithm requires learning the autoregressive coefficients. This is a slight departure from the spirit of USP since an offline transformation to the target sequence makes it easier to learn whereas in our case we must learn this transformation. However, it is also deeply connected to USP since its proof heavily relies on construction of the benchmark
\textbf{x}^{*}
, which is the Chebyshev preconditioned solution.
Moreover, empirical evidence suggests this requirement may be an artifact of our proof technique. Another limitation of this work is the practicality of the VAW algorithm. It requires a much larger memory footprint than the first order methods. The memory footprint grows poly-logarithmically with horizon length.

Future Directions

Promising directions for future work include extending these second-order preconditioning frameworks to non-linear dynamical systems, perhaps by using the recent breakthrough of
5
. Ultimately, our results demonstrate that when appropriately paired with second-order optimization, universal preconditioning unlocks fundamentally new capabilities for scalable, highly accurate sequence prediction.

Lemma 2
.

For any
z\in\mathbb{C}
, if
|Im(z)|\leq t
then
|\cos(z)|\leq\cosh(t)
.

Proof.

Let
z=x+iy
where
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. We use the identity for the magnitude of the complex cosine function:
|\cos(x+iy)|^{2}=\cos^{2}(x)+\sinh^{2}(y)
. Since
\cos^{2}(x)\leq 1
we have
|\cos(z)|^{2}\leq 1+\sinh^{2}(y)
. Using the identity
\cosh^{2}(y)-\sinh^{2}(y)=1
, we can substitute
1+\sinh^{2}(y)=\cosh^{2}(y)
:

|\cos(z)|^{2}\leq\cosh^{2}(y)\text{}

Taking the square root of both sides gives
|\cos(z)|\leq\cosh(y)
. Since
\cosh(y)
is an even function and increasing for
y\geq 0
and we are given that
|y|=|Im(z)|\leq t
it follows that
|\cos(z)|\leq\cosh(t)
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|z|\leq 1
and
|\arg(z)|\leq s<\pi/2
. Then
|Im(\arccos(z))|\leq 8\sqrt{s}
.

Proof.

Let
z=re^{i\theta}
with
0\leq r\leq 1
and
|\theta|\leq s
. Recall the series expansion for the imaginary part of
\arccos(z)
:

Im(\arccos(z))=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}r^{2k+1}\sin((2k+1)\theta)\text{}

where
a_{k}
are the Taylor coefficients. Using the inequality
|\sin(x)|\leq\min(|x|,1)
and
|r|\leq 1
we split the sum at an index
K
:

|Im(\arccos(z))|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{K}a_{k}(2k+1)|\theta|+\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}a_{k}\text{}

Using the known bounds for the coefficients
a_{k}(2k+1)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}
(for
k\geq 1
) and
a_{k}\leq\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}
, and substituting
|\theta|\leq s
:

|Im(\arccos(z))|\leq s\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}+\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}\text{}

Using rigorous integral bounds,
\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{-1/2}\leq 2\sqrt{K}
and
\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}k^{-3/2}\leq 2/\sqrt{K}
. Including the
k=0
term explicitly yields a bounded sum:

|Im(\arccos(z))|\leq 4\left(s\sqrt{K}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\right)\text{}

To minimize this expression, we choose
K\approx 1/s
. Substituting this back gives
4(s\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}+\sqrt{s})=4(2\sqrt{s})=8\sqrt{s}
. Thus,
|Im(\arccos(z))|\leq 8\sqrt{s}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma 1.

The monic Chebyshev polynomial is given by
M_{n}(z)=2^{-(n-1)}T_{n}(z)=2^{-(n-1)}\cos(n\arccos(z))
. Let
w=\arccos(z)
. By Lemma 3,
|Im(w)|\leq 8\sqrt{s}
. Consequently, the imaginary part of the argument to the cosine is bounded by:

|Im(nw)|=n|Im(w)|\leq 8n\sqrt{s}\text{}

Applying Lemma 2 with
t=8n\sqrt{s}
, we have
|T_{n}(z)|=|\cos(nw)|\leq\cosh(8n\sqrt{s})
.
Using
\cosh(x)\leq e^{x}
for
x\geq 0
, in base 2,
e^{x}=2^{x\log_{2}e}
. Thus:
|T_{n}(z)|\leq 2^{(8n\sqrt{s})\log_{2}e}
.
Substituting this back into the expression for
|M_{n}(z)|
:

|M_{n}(z)|\leq 2^{-n}\cdot 2^{8n\sqrt{s}\log_{2}e}=2^{-n(1-8\sqrt{s}\log_{2}e)}\text{}

We desire
|M_{n}(z)|\leq 2^{-pn}
. This condition is satisfied if:

1-8\sqrt{s}\log_{2}e\geq p\implies 8\sqrt{s}\log_{2}e\leq 1-p\text{}

Solving for
s
:

\sqrt{s}\leq\frac{1-p}{8\log_{2}e}\implies s\leq\left(\frac{1-p}{8\log_{2}e}\right)^{2}\text{}

Using
\log_{2}e\approx 1.44
the denominator is approximately 11.54.
∎

Appendix B
Coefficient growth of extremal polynomials

The following lemma shows that any monic polynomial that ensures all values in
[-1,1]
are exponentially small, must have exponentially large coefficients.

Theorem 3

(Coefficient growth for flat monic polynomials)
.

Fix
M>0
. Let
p(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}x^{j},a_{n}=1
, be a monic polynomial of degree
n
. If

\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|p(x)|\leq M2^{1-n},

then

\max_{0\leq j\leq n}|a_{j}|=\frac{1}{n}2^{\Omega(n)}

In particular, at least one coefficient of
p
is exponentially large in
n
.

Proof.

We split the proof into two parts. The first part is the conceptual reduction,
and the second part is the technical no-cancellation estimate.

Part I: Reduction to a no-cancellation statement.

Write
p
in the Chebyshev basis:

p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}T_{k}(x),

where
T_{k}
is the
k
th Chebyshev polynomial. Since

T_{n}(x)=2^{n-1}x^{n}+\text{lower-degree terms},

and
p
is monic, the coefficient of
T_{n}
must be

b_{n}=2^{1-n}.

The remaining Chebyshev coefficients are controlled by the assumption that

p
is small on
[-1,1]
. Indeed, using
T_{k}(\cos\theta)=\cos(k\theta)
and
orthogonality on
[0,\pi]
,

\displaystyle|b_{k}|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}p(\cos\theta)\cos(k\theta)\,d\theta\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left|p(\cos\theta)\right|\left|\cos(k\theta)\right|d\theta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left|p(\cos\theta)\right|d\theta

\displaystyle\leq 2\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|p(x)|\leq 2M2^{1-n}=4M2^{-n}.

Thus, after scaling by
2^{n-1}
, the polynomial

q(x):=2^{n-1}p(x)

has the form

q(x)=T_{n}(x)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}T_{k}(x),\qquad|c_{k}|\leq 2M.

So the whole issue is the following: can the bounded lower-degree Chebyshev
terms cancel the coefficient growth of
T_{n}
? The answer is no. This is the
content of the next lemma.

Lemma 4

(No cancellation, asymptotic form)
.

Fix
M>0
. Suppose

q(x)=T_{n}(x)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}T_{k}(x),\qquad|c_{k}|\leq 2M.

Write

q(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}d_{j}x^{j}.

Then

\max_{0\leq j\leq n}|d_{j}|\geq\frac{1}{n+1}2^{(1+\Omega_{M}(1))n}.

Equivalently, the coefficients of
q
grow like
(2+\Omega_{M}(1))^{n}/n
.

Proof.

Choose
\alpha>1
sufficiently large depending only on
M
, and set

y:=\frac{\alpha-\alpha^{-1}}{2}.

For a polynomial
r(x)=\sum_{j}r_{j}x^{j}
, define

\|r\|_{y}:=\sum_{j}|r_{j}|y^{j}.

We use the standard Chebyshev coefficient estimate

\|T_{k}\|_{y}=\frac{\alpha^{k}+(-1)^{k}\alpha^{-k}}{2}=\Theta(\alpha^{k}).

Thus

\|T_{n}\|_{y}=\Omega(\alpha^{n}),\qquad\|T_{k}\|_{y}\leq O(\alpha^{k}).

Therefore

\displaystyle\|q\|_{y}

\displaystyle\geq\|T_{n}\|_{y}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|c_{k}|\|T_{k}\|_{y}

\displaystyle\geq\Omega(\alpha^{n})-O_{M}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha^{k}\right).

Since

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha^{k}\leq\frac{\alpha^{n}}{\alpha-1},

choosing
\alpha
large enough depending on
M
gives

\|q\|_{y}\geq\Omega_{M}(\alpha^{n}).

On the other hand, if

D:=\max_{0\leq j\leq n}|d_{j}|,

then

\|q\|_{y}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}|d_{j}|y^{j}\leq D(n+1)y^{n}.

Hence

D\geq\frac{1}{n+1}\Omega_{M}\left(\left(\frac{\alpha}{y}\right)^{n}\right).

Finally,

\frac{\alpha}{y}=\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{\alpha^{2}-1}>2.

Therefore

D\geq\frac{1}{n+1}2^{(1+\Omega_{M}(1))n}.

∎

∎
