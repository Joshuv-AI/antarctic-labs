EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report assesses the financial impact of design changes throughout a project’s lifecycle, demonstrating how
timing of design modifications can significantly influence total costs from a vendor perspective. It demonstrates
the process of considering the costs associated with design and redesign of a physical protection system (PPS) at
the various stages through which a commercial vendor must pass on the way to deployment of a small modular
reactor (SMR). The study used a hypothetical SMR facility for the comparative analysis.
This report presents a comprehensive security-by-design (SeBD) evaluation for advanced and small modular
reactors (ASMRs), utilizing a hypothetical four-unit SMR Facility (SMRF) to analyze PPS efficacy and
associated lifecycle costs. This study models and evaluates several physical security postures ranging from an
offsite response force relying on local law enforcement to various onsite response configurations utilizing blast-
and-bullet-resistant enclosure (BBRE) towers, BBRE blisters on the buildings, and dedicated security floors. Each
design iteration was evaluated in path analysis tools and tabletop exercises to ensure the overall effectiveness of
each iteration against a hypothetical design basis threat (DBT). The baseline analysis demonstrated that offsite
response strategies, even with enhanced physical delay features, remain vulnerable to radiological sabotage
scenarios. In contrast, onsite response strategies utilizing facility-integrated defensive positions and reinforced
security infrastructure proved effective in neutralizing all adversary scenarios.
The financial analysis demonstrates that while highly advanced onsite configurations, such as a dedicated security
floor, require substantial upfront capital investments, they ultimately yield a significant lifetime cost savings (as
much as 30% for the parameters used in this study) compared to offsite response alternatives. The analysis also
demonstrates that implementing these structural changes late in the design phase (post-seismic analysis) triggers
extensive design rework, escalating regulatory fees and engineering costs. This highlights the critical importance
of collaborative, early-stage SeBD engineering to deliver both a physically secure and financially viable nuclear
facility

1. INTRODUCTION
Security-by-Design (SeBD) has been discussed in numerous applications for advanced and small modular reactors
(ASMRs) due to its use as a tool to effectively right-size security for nuclear deployment and its potential for
upfront and long-term cost savings driven by the inherent resilience of the resulting security system against
defined threats. SeBD attempts to integrate security features into the design of nuclear facilities from the earliest
design stages, spanning the full lifetime of the facility from commissioning and operations through
decommissioning. Key elements considered in SeBD include, but are not limited to:
 • Reducing the consequences of a malicious act
 • Reducing the category of nuclear material
 • Reducing the potential radiological impacts of a malicious act
 • Integrating security features into facility layout, design, and construction
 • Increasing wall thickness to protect against ballistics, uncrewed aerial systems (UAS), and other threats
 • Siting safety structures, systems, and components (SSCs) below grade
 • Integrating detection, delay, and response into the facility layout
 • Integrating safety features into the facility design with security in mind
 • Reducing the overall number of potential targets to be protected
These elements should be incorporated during the design stage of an ASMR facility. Implementing SeBD can help
reduce the potential consequences of a malicious act, improve security resiliency against evolving threats, and
lower both upfront and long-term costs for implementing security systems. However, one area rarely addressed in
SeBD is the impact of altering a facility's design or adding security measures at different stages during the design
process, which might increase overall design costs, delay ASMR design schedules, and affect deployment
timelines.
This project aims to address these concerns for ASMR vendors. It will accomplish this by developing design
modifications based on collaboration between safety and security experts, identifying security measures that
impact facility construction, and evaluating design changes made to support facility operations. Each
modification is evaluated to determine upfront security technology procurement costs, long-term operation and
maintenance (O&M) requirements, and the engineering labor hours needed to implement these changes both
before and after the completion of a seismic analysis. The seismic analysis was selected as the design milestone,
or breakpoint, because of the significant time and labor hours required to complete seismic evaluations for an
ASMR facility. A hypothetical small modular reactor facility will be used to demonstrate these principles and
generate these analytical results.

 1.1. ECONOMIC EVALUATIONS
This report presents various financial analyses evaluating the impact of design changes on overall project costs
from the perspective of a design vendor. This section documents the methodology, assumptions, and references
used to determine the financial impacts of the changes described throughout this report. Two types of financial
analyses were conducted:

3

 1. Design and Redesign Costs: An evaluation of the engineering costs for the initial design and subsequent
 redesigns, including typical contractual requirements and liabilities imposed on the design vendor for
 executing those redesigns.
 2. Equipment Lifecycle Costs: An evaluation of the procurement and long-term maintenance costs for the
 equipment required to implement the design changes.

 1.2. COST IMPACT OF DESIGN AND REDISGN FROM SECURITY CHANGES
This section describes the purpose, scope and methodology of the cost impact on the design vendor resulting
from the design changes described in the remainder of this document.

1.2.1. Purpose and Scope
This analysis estimates the cost impact of design changes introduced at different phases of the design
certification process. It is assumed that the design vendor bears the costs of any design changes and their
downstream consequences, including the engineering and project management labor required to develop and
process the changes. Additionally, the design vendor is liable for certain construction expenses resulting from
modifications made to an approved Design Certification Document (DCD). Please note that this specific cost
allocation is utilized in this analysis to illustrate downstream effects and should not be interpreted as a typical
cost-sharing agreement or as representative of Engineering, Procurement, and Construction (EPC) best practices.
The scope of this evaluation is strictly limited to costs attributable to the design vendor. Consequently, this
analysis excludes the following:
 • Procurement and material costs: Covered under the methodology described in Section 1.3.
 • Construction labor and field costs: These are borne by the owner or EPC contractor and are outside the
 vendor-attributable allocation.
 • Owner’s costs and financing costs
 • Inspections, Tests, Analyses, and Acceptance Criteria (ITAAC) stage costs
These excluded expenses fall outside the scope of design vendor liabilities for the phases and contract parameters
defined in this project.
This cost assessment is structured around two distinct operational phases: initial design and final design. Initial
design is defined in this analysis as the period before the commencement of structural seismic calculations. At this
stage, the reactor plant exists in a hypothetical state with less than 30% design completion. SSCs can be added or
removed with minimal, if any, rework because the design vendor has not yet completed the rigorous analyses
required to prove regulatory compliance. The second phase involves introducing modifications after major
structural analyses or equipment locations have been determined and completed.
 • Phase 1 is defined as the period before seismic analyses are functionally complete. During this phase, the
 Design Certification Application (DCA) has not yet been submitted, and large-scale engineering analyses
 remain incomplete. This corresponds to approximately 30% completion of the design.
 • Phase 2 represents the period in which significant engineering evaluations have been initiated or
 finalized. This report considers Phase 2 to begin when civil, structural, and seismic analyses are well
 underway, and it extends to include the initial construction phase of the facility. This corresponds to 70–
 100% completion of the design.

Each design and design change in this assessment is divided into two categories, forward engineering and rework.
Forward engineering refers to the new analysis, design, and documentation efforts intrinsic to the change itself.
Rework is the cost of revisiting, revising, and re-qualifying engineering work products that have already been
completed. This cost arises when a change is introduced after the relevant analysis has been completed.
This analysis shows that costs for design changes made after the work is complete are two-to-six times higher
than those made before the work is finished. One example of this is highlighted in the U.S. Department of
Energy’s Pathway to Commercial Liftoff: Advanced Nuclear program, which identifies several root causes for the
cost and schedule overruns at Vogtle Units 3 and 4.2 These include incomplete design, inadequately detailed
project schedules, insufficient quality assurance practices, poor risk assessment, and a shortage of experienced
labor. The issue of incomplete designs is functionally similar to design rework, which this report will assess for cost
implications. It is reasonable to conclude that changing designs during construction will lead to additional project
costs.
This report also considers the costs incurred by the design vendor if the DCD must be amended or changed, or if
the NRC needs to review these changes. The NuScale press release cited at the beginning of this section
documented 115,000 hours of NRC review for Phase 1 of the review process. Another NuScale press release
documented a total of 250,000 hours spent on NRC review for the DCA.3 This equates to a total NRC review cost
of approximately $80 million, based on the NRC hourly rate from 10 CFR 170.20.4 This assessment will use the
ratio of hours spent by design vendor resources to total hours for the project to estimate additional NRC review
hours.
This report assumes that approximately 30% of a project's cost occurs before significant engineering
documentation is signed off. The 30%/70% ratio is loosely based on AACE International’s design maturity
framework concept through preliminary design.5

2. HYPOTHETICAL FACILITY DESIGN
The SMRF is designed around four independent integral pressurized water reactors (iPWRs) that operate at a low
power density, producing a combined electrical output of 196 MWe (with each module rated at 140 MWth and
capable of generating 49 MWe). Each module utilizes 39 fuel assemblies arranged in a 17×17 rod bundle
containing uranium fuel enriched to 5% U-235. To optimize safety and environmental protection, all four reactors
and the shared spent fuel pool are situated below grade at the basement level of the central reactor building,
relying on a robust natural circulation primary cooling system that operates passively upon shutdown.
Structurally and operationally, the facility comprises several specialized buildings engineered to support
generation and maintenance. The central hub is the reactor building, flanked by four individual turbine buildings,
an office building (which houses the control room below grade), a nuclear receiving building, and a non-nuclear
receiving building. The plant employs a dry cooling system utilizing dry cooling radiators to cool the reactors and
the spent fuel pool without requiring continuous access to natural water resources. This configuration significantly
reduces long-term operating costs and eliminates thermal pollution in local waterways. Figure 1 and Figure 2
provide an overview of this hypothetical facility.

 2.1. TARGETS AND TARGET SETS
The hypothetical facility analyzed in this example utilizes passive cooling supported by a Reactor Cavity Cooling
System (RCCS) and a Reactor Air Cavity Cooling System (RACCS). Figure 3 outlines the failure logic (fault tree) for
an individual reactor at the SMRF. Each of the four reactors within the facility has an identical failure profile, which
can occur via the pathways illustrated in Figure 3.

 Figure 3. SMRF Radiological Sabotage Fault Tree

As illustrated in the fault tree, a reactor is vulnerable to sabotage if three independent safety systems are
rendered nonfunctional.
The first system that must be disabled is the RCCS. For the RCCS to fail, both redundant RCCS tanks must fail. An
individual tank can fail either by being drained or by severing the outlet line connecting the tank to the reactor
cavity.
The second system that must fail is the RACCS. This system utilizes ambient atmospheric air to remove heat from
the reactor vessel via passive natural convection. RACCS failure can be caused by blocking the air inlets or outlets,
or by severing the internal venting ducts that channel air into the cavity.
The final system that must fail is the reactor emergency cooling system (RECS). This system utilizes an active,
powered mechanical pump to exchange heat from reactor coolant with secondary cooling water adjacent to the
spent fuel pool. Cooling water drawn from the pool is pumped through the reactor core, and the heated effluent is
routed to exterior condensers for heat rejection before being returned to the pool. The RECS can be disabled
either by a failure of the dedicated diesel generator (which deprives the pump of electrical power) or by a failure of
the condenser (due to a loss of heat-rejection capability or loss of coolant inventory along the condenser line).

3. INITIAL PHYSICAL PROTECTION SYSTEM DESIGN - OFFSITE
 RESPONSE FORCE
This project utilizes a design-and-evaluation cycle to assess various combinations of safety and SeBD
modifications for a small modular reactor facility. These evaluations are based on altering the physical location of
safety features, modifying the response force strategy, adding detection and delay features, and implementing
structural design changes. Ultimately, the analysis identifies the cost-effectiveness of each design decision
relative to the specific milestone at which the change is integrated into the overall SMR facility design lifecycle.

 3.1. INTRUSION DETECTION SYSTEM
To facilitate this analysis, a baseline physical protection system (PPS) was developed to serve as the foundation
for the facility's security design. The initial PPS was designed to rely on an offsite response force. The primary
design requirement for this configuration is that the offsite response force be capable of reaching any target
within the facility within 20 minutes. A 20-minute response time is exceptionally rapid for offsite response forces,
particularly when relying on local law enforcement agencies (LLEAs).
The facility perimeter is secured by two 8-foot-tall chain-link fences; the inner fence is topped with razor wire. The
outer fence functions as a nuisance barrier, while the inner fence defines the facility’s Protected Area (PA)
boundary. The 30-foot-wide space between these fences serves as the isolation zone. This isolation zone is
equipped with two independent lines of detection: dual-stack bistatic microwave sensors and six-beam active
infrared sensors. Because the ECP roof extends into the isolation zone, it is secured with front- and rear-facing
fences and monitored by a monostatic microwave sensor and a six-beam active infrared sensor. Additionally, the
perimeter is equipped with bi‑spectral closed-circuit television (CCTV) cameras to facilitate assessment of alarm
causes. The layout of the exterior intrusion detection system (IDS) is illustrated in Figure 5.

 Figure 5. Site Boundary and PPS

The initial PPS design did not include structural modifications to the facility; however, it did include the
installation of interior IDS. This interior IDS configuration used balanced magnetic switches (BMS) on all facility
entry doors and passive infrared (PIR) sensors along the transit pathways to target locations. Figure 6 illustrates
these interior IDS capabilities.

 3.2. DELAY FEATURES
To facilitate an offsite response strategy at the SMRF, numerous delay barriers of varying materials and
dimensions were integrated into the design. A fundamental requirement when relying on an offsite response
force is to ensure adversaries are delayed long enough to allow the force to arrive and deploy effectively.
Starting at the perimeter, a two‑fence perimeter intrusion detection and assessment system (PIDAS) constitutes
the first physical barrier an adversary must contend with. Both fences are 8‑foot‑tall chain‑link structures; the
inner fence is topped with razor wire.
The next barrier layer comprises the facility’s exterior walls and doors. The walls are constructed of reinforced
concrete, with thicknesses that vary by structural area. The turbine buildings and reactor hall have walls 2 meters
thick; the exterior walls of the nuclear receiving building are 0.5 meters thick; and the administrative building
walls, which house the CAS, are 0.75 meters thick.
Exterior personnel entryways are fitted with standard steel security doors that include interior crash bars. The
receiving building also incorporates two large roll‑up doors to accommodate oversized shipments, and the
turbine‑building entrances feature interior security vestibules to provide an additional layer of delay upon
adversary entry (Figure 10).

total adversary task time required to complete a malicious act.

3.3.5. Adversary Attack Scenario – Tabletop Evaluation
A SME-developed attack scenario assumed a six-member adversary team attacking from the north side; the team

the adversaries crossed the PIDAS, which triggered a local response dispatch 15 seconds later. The estimated
offsite response time was approximately 20 minutes.
The adversary team completed the inner PIDAS breach between 17 and 27 seconds, allowing all six members to
cross the isolation zone and reach the outside of the north turbine building at 32 seconds. Once at the building,
one adversary executed a series of breaches to gain access to Turbine Building 4 between 56 seconds and 1
minute 37 seconds. The team then breached the final shark cage into the turbine hall by 5 minutes 12 seconds and

took defensive positions to hold off the incoming response force. From that position, one adversary began placing

4. DESIGN FOR ONSITE RESPONSE
The remaining scenarios evaluated in this analysis focus on various onsite response force strategies. For this
design stage, it is assumed the facility design begins from the original baseline described previously. The first
configuration comprises four responders deployed in elevated blast-and-ballistic-rated enclosure (BBRE) towers
positioned at the corners of the PA. In these scenarios, the towers house the BBREs at a height of approximately
50 feet above grade, providing a protected platform for observation and engagement. While modeled after
existing technologies, these BBREs represent a conceptual design rather than a proprietary system from any
single physical security manufacturer. The BBREs are equipped with sliding weapon ports that allow responders to
maintain surveillance and rapidly open a direct line of fire when necessary.
Following these initial scenarios, alternative onsite response designs were evaluated, specifically incorporating
blisters and dedicated gun ports. Blisters are structurally integrated BBREs, typically mounted in elevated
positions along the facility envelope. Equipped with similar engagement ports, they provide opportunities for
observation and defense extending outward from the facility, down the exterior skin of the building, and
internally into high-consequence areas.
The physical layout of facility-integrated blisters can take two primary forms:
 • Access-Limited Blisters: positions built into the corners of the facility and accessible only by localized
 stairwells or structural catwalks that restrict rapid access.
 • Dedicated Security Floor: a continuous, secure mezzanine level within the facility that provides protected
 access to both the blisters and internal gun ports. This layout provides overlapping fields of fire that cover
 critical target locations across the entire facility.
Both architectural layouts were evaluated across different scenarios.
In contrast to blisters, standard gun ports are integrated directly into the walls at key tactical locations throughout
the facility. Because they do not protrude from the wall face, they do not facilitate engagement along the
building’s exterior skin. Across the remaining scenarios, the modeled onsite response configurations evolved
through various combinations of towers, blisters, and distributed gun ports.

During the design process, the project team evaluated the strategic placement and angular orientation of the

Ensuring comprehensive FOVs and minimizing adversary angles of attack reduces the number of armed

Facility changes were made to relocate the RACCS inside the reactor building and to move all interior doors within
the alcove connecting the turbine buildings to their exterior faces. These changes were evaluated without

5. OPTIMIZED PHYSICAL PROTECTION SYSTEM
The analysis of an offsite response strategy reveals the significant challenges that must be overcome to
adequately protect a site against a well-equipped, determined adversary with an objective of radiological
sabotage. The integration of delay features into a facility design introduces high logistical and financial burdens,
and these efforts are still insufficient to mitigate the potential for adversaries to acquire positions of advantage
that allow them to effectively engage arriving offsite responders with a high likelihood of defeating them and
completing their sabotage objective. Following SME evaluation of the offsite response scenario, focus shifted to
varying levels of onsite response capabilities.
Initially, a more traditional onsite response posture was evaluated, with responders posted in towers located in
the four corners of the PA. This strategy has several advantages, including positioning responders in protected,
elevated positions with overlapping fields of view and fields of fire; providing good line-of-sight for responders
along the perimeter as well as along the majority of the facility; and protecting responders from many forms of
small-arms attacks while allowing them to still engage threats. Through scenario analysis, it is clear that some of
these advantages can be negated or overcome by various adversarial load outs and tactics. To mitigate this,
another response strategy investigated was the addition of towers at other locations around the PA. This
increases the number of responders who are able to engage a threat and also provides greater overlap of FOVs
and FOFs toward the perimeter and back toward the facility.
Perhaps the greatest weakness of these response strategies is the limited ability for the response to engage an
adversary who has managed to defeat exterior defenses and gain access to the interior of the facility.
Encountering this result in several scenarios, another response strategy was considered with responders posted in
BBRE blisters in the upper corners of the facility. In some cases, these blisters were incorporated alongside the
tower response strategy, allowing for a layered approach with greater defense-in-depth.
Critically, the blisters allowed responders to engage internally as well as externally to the facility. The blisters
themselves could be implemented using a graded approach. In some cases, the blisters were accessible only by
stairs leading directly to each. While the responders had the ability to engage outside and within the facility, this
setup was still somewhat lacking in flexibility. The design that provided the greatest degree of freedom for the
responders was the incorporation of a dedicated security floor on the top of the facility with blisters located in the
corners and with gun ports integrated in both exterior and interior walls across the security floor. The gun ports
provided flexibility for outward engagement should an adversary use a strategy of suppressive fire against the
blisters. Additionally, the interior gun ports offered effective overwatch of target locations while providing a
significant level of protection for the responders using them.

6. OVERVIEW OF FACILITY DESIGN CHANGES
Facility design changes considered during this tabletop analysis included modifications to ingress and egress and
the addition of delay barriers along critical paths to target locations. The analysis initially assumed a response
configuration that relied on local law enforcement for an offsite response to alarms. For this analysis, the offsite
response time was defined as 20 minutes from alarm to arrival at facility target locations; actual response times
will vary by locale and can be significantly longer in some areas. This offsite-response strategy required costly
facility modifications to provide substantial delay between the site perimeter and target locations. Examples
include adding shark cages at multiple exterior and interior facility entrances and constructing a 3-foot-thick
concrete barrier around the emergency core-cooling tanks in the turbine buildings.
The offsite-response scenario results indicated that, even with extensive delay features, no credible offsite
strategy existed to regain control of the facility before adversaries defined under this DBT could accomplish their
sabotage objectives. Given those results, the evaluation focus shifted to onsite response strategies.
The initial onsite posture considered BBREs sited on towers at each corner of the PA, with one responder, armed
with a 5.56-mm rifle, posted in each tower. In this configuration, responders are largely confined to their towers
because moving outside exposes them for extended periods; this tower-centric strategy proved ineffective across
the tested scenarios.
Ingress and egress were adapted in several ways based on scenario results. First, shark cages were installed at all
personnel and vehicle portals leading into the facility. These turbine-grating person-traps impose significant delay
on adversaries regardless of their breach method and can be designed to force attackers into exposed positions
during entry attempts. Second, several doors were relocated or added for safety and security; entrances to
Turbine Buildings 2 and 3 were moved to the south sides to remove sheltered alcoves in the facility center that
previously allowed adversaries to stage breaches without harassment from external responders. Finally, doors
were added between each turbine building and the reactor building on the first below-grade level to satisfy safety
requirements for personnel egress during emergencies.

7. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Scenario analysis is a productive way to evaluate PPS design and response strategies to determine effective
protection of nuclear facilities and assets. As with any assessment for which models are used, the accuracy of the
results would suffer if the model employed did not adequately reflect the conditions of the system being
evaluated. When these evaluations have been conducted, significant time and effort has been put in to ensure an
effective and valuable assessment can be achieved.
A wide range of scenarios were evaluated using Scribe3D© to demonstrate the benefits of SeBD methodology to
both increase the likelihood of defending a small modular reactor site against a determined and well-equipped
adversary and also reduce the costs associated with constructing and operating such a site. The model used for
this evaluation was the hypothetical Small Modular Reactor Facility (SMRF). In order to meet the defense needs of
the facility, various changes to the baseline design were evaluated. These changes ranged from large-scale,
structural adaptation (e.g., movement of RACCS towers from an external location to an internal one or
construction of a dedicated security floor) to smaller, less obtrusive changes (e.g., changing the location of
personnel ingress/egress or adding shark cages to doors along critical pathways). The financial and logistical
burden of each of these hypothetical changes can be significantly reduced by incorporating them early in the
design process. The later a change occurs in the development timeline, the higher the cost will be.
The scenario analysis considered offsite and onsite response strategies. Based on the results of this analysis as
well as previous studies, an offsite response is unlikely to be an effective or financially advantageous option. For
such a strategy, facilities must be designed with extensive passive and active delay features to provide time for an
offsite response team to muster and arrive in time to interrupt the activities of an adversary. Additionally,
contracting costs for offsite responders typically meet or exceed the cost of a dedicated, onsite response force.

Table 29 suggests that for security by design, simpler may be better. What this data does not account for is the
robustness of the design if DBT methods and characteristics change; additionally, it does not account for a
demonstration of a defensible defense-in-depth. Design 2 exhibits the lowest defense-in-depth, with all armed
responders located within exterior towers. Design 4 shows a robust design, with multiple BBRE locations across
the security floor for both interior and exterior engagement. However, for a 40-year period, the addition of a
single armed responder may account for an estimated $29 million in cost.

Table 29 should be used as a study to inform the reader on the benefits of SeBD, and the importance of early
consideration of security within the nuclear design process. Continued PPS design iterations can challenge the
PPS to reduce the possibility of SSC or response position design changes and retrofits late in the design process,
and result in a more robust and flexible PPS design.
