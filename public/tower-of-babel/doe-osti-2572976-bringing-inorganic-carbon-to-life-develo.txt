1.A. To develop an artificial carbon monoxide dehydrogenase (CODH) enzyme using a
semisynthetic protein-catalyst construct.

 The [NiFe] carbon monoxide dehydrogenase (CODH) enzymes interconvert CO2 and CO with
high rates and efficiency, effectively performing a biological analog of the water-gas shift reaction
under mild conditions and using protons and electrons rather than hydrogen gas. This enzyme is
critical for primary energy conversion in the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway, the dominant metabolic
pathway in diverse anaerobic bacteria and archaea. The active sites of these proteins feature a
unique [4Fe-4S-Ni] cluster, bound to the protein via five cysteine residues and a single histidine.
A number of conserved residues in the secondary sphere are found to be essential for activity.
Despite years of ongoing effort to understand and characterize these enzymes, many questions
remain about the mechanism of CO2 activation by the [NiFe] CODHs, including fundamental
unknowns such as relevant metal oxidation states, site(s) of substrate binding, interaction
between the primary and secondary coordination spheres, and structures of intermediates.
Moreover, significant experimental challenges are found in working with the native enzyme,
including pronounced oxygen sensitivity, low expression yields, and large molecular weights, all
of which hinder mechanistic investigations and prevent application of CODH to effect large-scale
CO2 conversion.
 To address many of these basic mechanistic questions and gain a molecular-level
understanding of the requirements for efficient and selective CO2 reduction to CO, we proposed
the development of protein-based mimics of CODH that are highly functional, operate in water,

and use a nickel center as the active-site catalytic metal. We pursued two approaches to
accomplish this goal. In the first, azurin (Az), a robust, naturally-occurring metalloprotein, was
used as a scaffold into which a small-molecule catalyst, nickel cyclam (cyclam = 1,4,8,11-
tetraazacyclotetradecane), and derivatives were to be installed in a site-selective fashion. In the
second approach, model CODH enzymes were to be constructed using a site-differentiated
ferredoxin scaffold, post-translationally incorporating nickel into one corner of the cubane to
reproduce the redox-active core of CODH (Figure 1). Structural, spectroscopic, functional, and
mechanistic investigations were to be pursued to identify critical elements required for high levels
of activity and determine guiding principles for synthetic catalyst design.

1.B. To repurpose an existing metalloprotein active site as a mimic of acetyl coenzyme A
synthase (ACS), capable of catalyzing carbon-carbon bond formation and acyl group
transfer reactions

 Acetyl coenzyme-A synthase (ACS) is another critical component of the Wood-Ljungdahl
pathway, performing reversible coupling reactions between CO and a methyl group to form acetyl-
CoA. This energy-rich cofactor plays an important role in downstream biological processes.
Thought to be the oldest mechanism for fixing CO2 into cellular carbon and implicated in the
chemoautotrophic theory of the origins of life, the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway remains a dominant
metabolic process in acetogens, methanogens, and other classes of anaerobic organisms. The
reaction catalyzed by ACS generates a new carbon-carbon bond under net redox-neutral
conditions, with relevance to production of liquid fuels from simple C1 reagents. Because of the
potential applications of ACS-type chemistry, understanding the native enzyme is an important
objective; however, as with CODH, little is known about the mechanism by which acetyl-CoA is
synthesized from its C1 constituents.
 The active site of ACS contains a [4Fe-4S] cluster bound to a binuclear nickel center (Figure
1). Within the multimetallic site, only one of the nickel ions is thought to be involved in catalysis;
the role of the additional metals remains unclear. Moreover, the pertinent oxidation states of the
catalytically relevant nickel center remain debated, with experimental evidence supporting and
against both proposed mechanisms. To better characterize the landscape of accessible chemical
reactivity at a biological nickel center, we will develop a functional model of ACS within the active
site of the azurin protein scaffold. Building from preliminary results indicating nickel-substituted
azurin (NiAz) can access a NiI state that is suggested to closely resemble the geometric and
electronic structure of a key intermediate in ACS, we will develop reactivity for substrate binding
and conversion. Structural and mechanistic insight will be provided by an array of spectroscopic,
analytical, and computational tools to address long-debated questions about the mechanism of
ACS and shed insight into critical elements for effective C-C bond coupling reactions.

2. What was accomplished under these goals?

2.A. To develop an artificial carbon monoxide dehydrogenase (CODH) enzyme using a
semisynthetic protein-catalyst construct

 Over this project, we have shown that the synthetic complex nickel cyclam ([1]) binds to
histidine residues in Az in a selective and stable fashion to generate semisynthetic Az-[1]
constructs that show increased activity and selectivity for CO2 reduction relative to free [1]
(Schneider, C.R., and Shafaat, H.S., Chem. Commun., 2016, 52, 9889-9892). A ruthenium
chromophore covalently attached to the surface of the protein further enhances activity, enabling
direct, light-initiated catalysis that shows complete selectivity for CO2 reduction to CO (Schneider,
C. R.; Manesis, A. C.; Stevenson, M. J.; Shafaat, H. S. Chem. Commun. 2018, 54, 4681–4684).
We have demonstrated that buffer is intimately involved with catalysis (Schneider, C. R.; Lewis,

L. C.; Shafaat, H. S. Dalton Trans. 2019, 48, 15810–15821) and probed a trapped intermediate
using spectroelectrochemical methods (Behnke, S. L.; Manesis, A. C.; Shafaat, H. S. Dalton
Trans. 2018, 47, 15206–15216). These studies enumerated the many contributing factors needed
to achieve selective CO2 reduction to CO, laying a foundation for replicating the CODH system
with higher fidelity.
 Building from this body of work, we extended our studies focused on understanding structure-
function relationships in enzymatic CO2 reduction to a multimetallic protein-based model that
exhibits greater structural fidelity to the native enzyme. Towards this end, we began pursuing
incorporation of nickel centers into the site-differentiated [4Fe-4S] cluster of Pyrococcus furiosus.
These scaffolds were previously indicated in work by Michael Johnson, Jose Moura, and
coworkers to support the generation of a site-differentiated cofactor in the [4Fe-4S] cluster site.
Recognizing the potential structural similarity between the nickel-bound cofactor and the core of
the active site in CODH, we began to probe accessible reactivity of the [Ni-3Fe-4S] Fd (Lewis,
L.C.; Shafaat, H.S. Inorg. Chem. 2021, 60 (18), 13869-13875). These initial studies laid a strong
foundation for our continued study of CODH models under the Physical Biosciences program
supported by award DE-SC0024869.

2.B. To repurpose an existing metalloprotein active site as a mimic of acetyl coenzyme A
synthase (ACS), capable of catalyzing carbon-carbon bond formation and acyl group
transfer reactions.

 The organometallic carbon-carbon coupling catalyzed by ACS represents one of the most
ancient mechanisms for carbon-carbon bond formation in Nature, and likely can be considered
as the root of all complex biological macromolecules. Thus, understanding, and ultimately
replicating, this process is of utmost importance in this global environment.
 Through our work on this project towards these goals, we have established the potential of
nickel-substituted azurin to act as a spectroscopic model for the proximal nickel center in ACS,
demonstrating that NiIAz has a similar reduction potential and spectroscopic features as a key
intermediate in ACS (Manesis, A. C.; Shafaat, H. S. Inorg. Chem. 2015, 54, 7959–7967). We have
shown that introducing a substrate binding channel into this model system confers the ability to
bind CO and a -CH3 group, giving rise to protein-based models for the NiFeC state of ACS
(Manesis, A. C.; O’Connor, M. J.; Schneider, C. R.; Shafaat, H. S. J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2017, 139,
10328–10338) as well as the as-yet unresolved but obligatory NiIII-CH3 state (Manesis, A. C.;
Musselman, B. W.; Keegan, B. C.; Shearer, J.; Lehnert, N.; Shafaat, H. S. Inorg. Chem. 2019, 58,
8969–8982). We used pulsed EPR spectroscopy in conjunction with density functional theory
calculations to reveal that the EPR-active S = ½ Ni-CH3 state features a d9, NiI center bound to a
cationic methyl ligand, rather than the canonical d7, NiIII species expected from traditional
organometallic teachings (Kisgeropoulos, E. C.; Manesis, A. C.; Shafaat, H. S. J. Am. Chem.
Soc. 2021, 143, 849–867). This system thus represents the first definitive example of a
biochemical inverted ligand field. Finally, we demonstrated that this model NiAz system is capable
of performing selective thioester synthesis from biochemically relevant one-carbon precursors
(Manesis, A. C.; Yerbulekova, A.; Shearer, J.; Shafaat, H. S. Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. U. S. A. 2022,
119, e2123022119). This study established the chemical competence of a mononuclear nickel
center in a biochemical environment to carry out ACS-like chemistry, advancing our understanding
of primordial carbon-carbon coupling processes (Figure 2). The research pursued through this
project laid an essential foundation for ongoing research on ACS models, currently supported
under the Physical Biosciences program supported by award DE-SC0024869.

3. What opportunities for training and professional development has the project provided?

Seven graduate students (five female, two male), five undergraduate students (three female, two
male), and one female high school student have been involved in research supported by this
grant. These scientists have received extensive training across diverse areas of chemistry as a
result of this experience, including biochemical protein expression and characterization, air-free
techniques, optical, vibrational, and magnetic spectroscopy, time-resolved spectroscopic
techniques, density functional theory calculations, solution- and gas-phase assays, and scientific
communication. A brief description of each of their contributions is given below.

Graduate students
Dr. Anastasia Manesis (Ph.D. Fall 2018, now Asst. Prof. at University of Illinois, Urbana-
Champaign): Anastasia was responsible for developing and driving essentially all of the initial
work on the 2nd project, developing NiAz as a model for ACS. Anastasia was the first author on
five publications related to this work and has given oral presentations at multiple scientific
meetings, including 1 national ACS meeting, a Gordon Research Seminar, and the 2018 Penn
State Symposium on Metallobiochemistry.

Dr. Camille Schneider (Ph.D. Summer 2019, now Principal Scientist at Abbott): Camille was
responsible for developing and driving all of the work on the 1st project, development of a
semisynthetic CODH using nickel cyclam in azurin. Camille was also responsible for the
systematic investigation of buffer effects on the solution-phase activity of nickel cyclam. Camille
was the first author on two publications related to this work and has given oral presentations at
multiple scientific meetings, including the 2nd International Solar Fuels conference, the Solar Fuels
“Young” Symposium, and the 2016 Ohio Inorganic Weekend.

Dr. Shelby Behnke (Ph.D. Fall 2019, now employed by Intel Corporation): Shelby was the principal
researcher behind the spectroelectrochemical investigations of nickel cyclam using optical,
resonance Raman, and EPR techniques. Shelby gave multiple poster presentations at local and
regional meetings as well as the Gordon Research Conference on Inorganic Reaction
Mechanisms; she also gave an oral presentation in the associated Gordon Research Seminar.
