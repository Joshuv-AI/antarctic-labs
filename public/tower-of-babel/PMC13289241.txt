Early versus delayed EKOS thrombolysis in intermediate-high risk pulmonary embolism: a retrospective multicentre analysis
Authors: Julia Schulten-Baumer, Abdelrahman Elhakim, Peter Radke, Andreas Schuchert, Björn Stöcker, Niklas Schofer, Matthias Mezger, Elias Rawish, Thomas Stiermaier, Ingo Eitel, Tobias Schmidt, Christian Frerker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltrasound-assisted catheter-directed thrombolysis using the EkoSonic Endovascular System (EKOS) is an established treatment for intermediate high-risk pulmonary embolism (IHR-PE). However, the optimal timing of treatment initiation remains uncertain. Early right ventricular (RV) unloading may improve outcomes by reducing RV pressure overload and preventing haemodynamic deterioration.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective multicentre analysis of patients with IHR-PE treated with EKOS at three tertiary centres. Patients were stratified by time from CT-confirmed diagnosis to EKOS initiation: early (<12 hours) versus delayed (>12 hours). Echocardiographic parameters included RV/left ventricular (LV) ratio, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), LV ejection fraction (LVEF) and systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP). Secondary outcomes were intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay, complications, bleeding events and in-hospital mortality. 3-month follow-up was available in two centres.ResultsOverall, 152 patients were included (early n=106; delayed n=46) in the study. Baseline characteristics were largely comparable, although dyspnoea New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II–III was more frequent in the early group (NYHA III: 73% vs 46%, p=0.001). Both groups showed significant improvements in RV/LV ratio, TAPSE, sPAP and LVEF (all p<0.001). Early treatment resulted in numerically greater RV functional improvement (Early Δ Discharge (D)-Admission (A) TAPSE early 4.86±5.14 mm vs delayed Δ D-A 3.5±5 mm), without statistically significant differences between groups. At 3-month follow-up, RV recovery measured by TAPSE was comparable. Early EKOS therapy was associated with a significantly shorter ICU stay (median 24 hours vs 45.5 hours, p=0.033), fewer PE-related complications (p=0.03), while procedure-related complications and in-hospital mortality did not differ.ConclusionsIn IHR-PE, EKOS initiation within 12 hours is associated with a significantly shorter ICU stay and significantly less PE-related complications. These findings support early EKOS treatment strategies and warrant confirmation in prospective studies.

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is the third most common cause of cardiovascular death worldwide.1 Over the past decade, catheter-directed therapies (CDTs) have emerged as important treatment options that enable more rapid and targeted thrombus reduction. CDT can be broadly classified into catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDTL) and catheter-directed thrombectomy.

The EkoSonic Endovascular System (EKOS) developed by Boston Scientific represents the most widely used CDTL and is based on ultrasound-assisted thrombolysis (USAT). High-frequency ultrasound waves facilitate fibrin strand separation within the thrombus, enhancing penetration and effectiveness of the locally and continuously administered thrombolytic agent.2 Several studies have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of USAT, which has contributed to the widespread clinical use.36 The recently published prospective Higher-risk Pulmonary Embolism Thrombolysis (HI-PEITHO) trial demonstrated a significant reduction in early clinical deterioration without an increase in major bleeding and with no observed intracranial haemorrhage, further supporting the role of catheter-based reperfusion strategies in intermediate high-risk PE (IHR-PE).7

Despite the availability of multiple catheter-based treatment options, the optimal timing of CDT remains unclear. Current guidelines recommend CDT primarily in cases of haemodynamic deterioration despite anticoagulation in IHR-PE.6 Consequently, decisions regarding treatment escalation and timing are largely made by the Pulmonary Embolism Response Team (PERT).6 Unlike acute myocardial infarction or ischaemic stroke, no specific time targets for CDT have been established in PE management.

Against this background, the present study aimed to evaluate whether differences in safety and efficacy exist between early USAT performed within 12 hours and delayed intervention initiated after 12 hours of diagnosis in patients with IHR-PE.

Methods

Patient population

This retrospective multicentre analysis included consecutive patients with IHR-PE who underwent EKOS-lysis at three German centres. The participating institutions were the University Hospital Schleswig Holstein (UKSH) in Lübeck, the Friedrich-Ebert-Hospital in Neumünster and the Schön Clinic in Neustadt. The study period extended from August 2020 to December 2023 (figure 1).

All patients were diagnosed using a combination of clinical assessment, laboratory testing including troponin, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and D-dimers, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and CT pulmonary angiography. IHR-PE was defined in accordance with European Society of Cardiology (ESC) recommendations requiring a simplified Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index >1, evidence of right ventricular (RV) dysfunction and elevated cardiac troponin levels in the absence of haemodynamic compromise.6

Once the diagnosis was established the case was promptly reviewed by the PERT. This multidisciplinary team included an interventional cardiologist, an intensive care specialist, a radiologist and an emergency medicine physician. Therapeutic decisions were made collaboratively within the PERT using the same clinical decision-making approach across all participating centres, while taking the individual clinical situation into account.

Based on this assessment the team recommended either immediate initiation of EKOS therapy or primary anticoagulation with continuous application of unfractionated heparin (UFH) with close surveillance.

Patients were classified according to the time interval between CT imaging and initiation of EKOS-therapy. One group included patients treated with a CT-to-EKOS time <12 hours while the second group consisted of patients treated after >12 hours. As no guideline-recommended time threshold for CT-to-EKOS lysis exists, a 12 hours time interval was selected in accordance with prior observational study.8

We deliberately selected CT-to-EKOS time instead of door-to-EKOS time, as patients with PE often present with non-specific symptoms and were admitted for alternative working diagnoses, with PE being identified later in the clinical course. CT-based timing therefore more accurately captures the interval of postdiagnostic decision making, during which treatment strategy is determined by the PERT, rather than diagnostic delay.

Patients without IHR-PE were excluded from the analysis.

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study.

EKOS-lysis was performed by experienced interventionalists according to institutional standards. The most common chosen access site was femoral vein followed by veins of cubital fossa.

Following the procedure, patients were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for close monitoring while receiving a continuous infusion of recombinant tissue plasminogen activator, with a total dose of 6 mg administered at a rate of 1 mg/hour. 6 hours after the procedure, TTE was conducted to assess improvement in RV overload and to exclude pericardial effusion. The EKOS catheters were then removed, followed by application of a compression bandage for 6–8 hours.

Patients were routinely transferred to a standard ward after lysis and switched from UFH to non-vitamin K oral anticoagulant. Another TTE was performed prior to discharge to assess improvement of RV overload.

Follow-up (FU) was performed at 3 months at UKSH Lübeck and Schön Clinic Neustadt, including repeat TTE to assess RV dysfunction.

Efficacy measures

Efficacy outcomes comprised improvements in the RV to left ventricular (LV) ratio (RV/LV ratio), systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP) and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) and the TAPSE/sPAP ratio as a surrogate marker of RV pulmonary artery (RV-PA) coupling, as measured by TTE.

Safety measures

Safety outcomes included all complications as well as all-cause mortality. Complications were grouped into procedure-related and PE-related complications.

Procedure-related complications were defined as adverse events occurring within 24 hours after EKOS catheter placement and considered directly attributable to the intervention, including access site complications, catheter-related adverse events and bleeding events occurring in direct timely association with the procedure.

PE-related complications were defined as adverse events attributable to the underlying PE or its medical management rather than the catheter intervention itself, including haemodynamic deterioration, stroke and complications occurring during anticoagulation treatment. Complications were additionally classified as bleeding or non-bleeding events.

Non-bleeding complications include all in-hospital adverse events not related to bleeding. Bleeding complications were further differentiated into procedure-related and non-procedure-related events. Procedure-related bleeding was defined as bleeding occurring within 24 hours after EKOS catheter placement and considered directly attributable to the intervention, including access site and diffuse bleeding. Non-procedure-related bleeding events were defined as bleeding complications occurring during hospitalisation without direct association with catheter placement or the thrombolysis. Severity of bleeding complications was graded according to the Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) and Global Use of Streptokinase and Tissue Plasminogen Activator for Occluded Coronary Arteries (GUSTO) classification.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (V.29.0.1.0; IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed data were compared using the Student’s t-test, while non-normally distributed data were analysed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD or median (IQR), depending on distribution. Within-group comparisons between repeated echocardiographic measurements were performed using paired statistical testing, applying paired t tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests depending on data distribution. To address baseline differences in LV ejection fraction (LVEF), an additional analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusting for baseline LVEF was performed to assess intergroup differences in discharge LVEF.

Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test depending on sample size and are presented as absolute numbers and percentages.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics, clinical presentation and laboratory findings

We included 152 patients with IHR-PE (female 51.9%; age 65 years (57–73); median CT-to-EKOS time 3.2 hours (1.4–16.0)). EKOS was initiated within 12 hours in 106 patients (69.7%; CT-to-EKOS 2.0 hours (1.1–3.3)) and after more than 12 hours in 46 patients (30.3%; CT-to-EKOS 23.3 hours (17.7–36.0)). Baseline characteristics, comorbidities and VTE related risk factors were comparable between groups (see table 1). Clinical presentation differed between groups. Advanced dyspnoea was more frequent in the early group. New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III predominated with early EKOS, whereas NYHA class II was more common in the delayed group (p=0.001). Other symptoms and oxygen demand were similar. Vital signs at presentation were comparable (see table 2). Laboratory findings were largely similar. Troponin, NT-proBNP, C-reactive protein and renal function did not differ. D-dimer concentrations were higher in the early group (13 (9–20) vs 9 (7–14) mg/L Fibrinogen Equivalent Units (FEU); p=0.002) and fibrinogen concentrations (4.2±1.0 vs 3.7±1.1 g/L; p=0.03) were higher in the delayed group (see table 3).

Efficacy measurements

At admission, CT derived RV/LV ratio was similar between groups. LVEF was significantly lower in the delayed group (p=0.003). Additional ANCOVA adjusting for baseline LVEF confirmed no significant difference in discharge LVEF between the early and delayed treatment groups after adjustment for baseline values (p=0.608). RV/LV ratio, TAPSE, sPAP and the RV end-diastolic pressure (RVEDd) did not differ (see table 5). From admission to discharge, both groups showed significant improvement in RV function, reflected by a significant improvement in RV/LV ratio, TAPSE, sPAP and RVEDd (all p<0.001), with sustained improvement at FU (see table 6). The TAPSE/sPAP ratio improved from 0.38 (0.3–0.5) at admission to 0.7 (0.5–0.9) at discharge and 0.9 (0.7–1.2) at FU, without significant differences between early and delayed treatment groups.

Complications and mortality

Overall complications occurred in 15.1% with no difference between both groups (see table 7). PE-related complications occurred significantly more commonly in delayed group (1 (0.9%) early group vs 4 (8.7%) delayed group; p=0.030).

Impact of timing on ICU stay

A finding of the present study is the significantly longer ICU stay in patients with IHR-PE treated with delayed CDTL. These findings are consistent with the findings of Zhang et al, who likewise reported a significant shorter ICU-stay in patients treated with CDT within 12 hours.8 Prolonged ICU stay has consistently been associated with higher rates of nosocomial complications, including pneumonia and bloodstream infections, and represents a major driver of in-hospital costs.9 Recent data from PERT registry suggests that ICU utilisation differs substantially across advanced treatment strategies, with USAT being associated with 100% ICU admission, whereas using catheter-directed thrombectomy devices and anticoagulation alone show lower ICU utilisation. Differences in revenue-per-case between CDT reported in the registry study may be influenced by variation in ICU utilisation, particularly the lower need for prolonged ICU after mechanical thrombectomy.10 These findings highlight ICU stay as a key determinant of resource utilisation in contemporary IHR-PE management.10

From an economic perspective, shortening ICU stay through earlier USAT initiation may therefore represent a relevant strategy to reduce overall treatment costs without compromising procedural safety or clinical outcomes.

Importantly, in our cohort, the reduction in ICU stay observed with early CDTL was not accompanied by higher complication rates or increased in-hospital mortality. This supports the interpretation that earlier intervention improves treatment efficiency rather than reflecting premature de-escalation of care. Taken together, these findings suggest that optimisation of CDTL timing after CT diagnosis may have relevant clinical and economic implications while maintaining a favourable safety profile.

Efficacy of EKOS reflected by echocardiographic recovery

The echocardiographic findings indicate that EKOS provides effective and sustained unloading of the RV in IHR-PE. The consistent improvement of RV/LV ratio, TAPSE, sPAP and RVEDd from admission to discharge and further at FU suggests rapid reversal of RV pressure overload. These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating effective RV recovery following EKOS.35

Although with no statistical significance, differences in improvement of echocardiographic parameters were numerically greater during early EKOS group, favouring early EKOS initiation. In a small study by Edla et al, early EKOS (<24 hours) was compared with delayed EKOS (>24 hours), and a greater improvement in pulmonary artery pressure was observed in the early-treatment group.11 12 However, data regarding optimal timing of EKOS is scarce.

In addition, the TAPSE/sPAP ratio, as a surrogate marker of RV-PA coupling, improved in both treatment groups from admission to discharge and FU, without significant intergroup differences. Previous studies have demonstrated that impaired RV PA coupling is associated with adverse outcomes in acute PE and may provide superior prognostic information compared with isolated assessment of TAPSE or sPAP alone. A TAPSE/sPAP ratio in the range of 0.3–0.4 has been associated with RV PA uncoupling and increased mortality risk in acute PE.13 14 In our cohort, the median TAPSE/sPAP ratio at admission was 0.38, indicating relevant baseline RV PA uncoupling, with marked improvement to 0.7 at discharge and 0.9 at FU, suggesting substantial recovery of RV haemodynamic function after EKOS therapy.

In our cohort, the LVEF was significantly higher during early group at admission and at discharge. However, the magnitude of LVEF improvement over time did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that the observed differences should not be interpreted as a treatment-related effect. In addition, changes in LVEF in the setting of acute PE should be interpreted with caution, as they may at least partly reflect haemodynamic recovery following RV unloading and reversal of interventricular septal shift rather than true recovery of intrinsic LV systolic function.

Safety outcomes including complications and mortality

The safety outcomes of the present study indicate comparable complication rates between early and delayed EKOS, particularly with regard to procedure associated adverse events. The similar incidence of procedural complications suggests that earlier, as well as delayed EKOS initiation does not increase intervention related risk and supports the technical safety of the procedure independent of timing.

PE-related complications were statistically significantly more frequent in the delayed EKOS group. These events occurred predominantly in the later course of hospitalisation and were mainly driven by gastrointestinal bleeding while on anticoagulation.

We assume that the longer preinterventional exposure to UFH in the delayed group, together with the prolonged ICU-stay and the frequent periods of over-anticoagulation and under-anticoagulation, may have increased the susceptibility to bleeding complications.15 While gastrointestinal bleeding was numerically more common in the delayed group, this difference did not reach statistical significance, most likely due to the low absolute number of events and the resulting limited statistical power.

Overall, these findings indicate that early EKOS preserves a favourable safety profile, with no increase in procedure-related complications and no signal for excess bleeding or mortality.

CT-to-EKOS time

An important finding of the present study is the marked variability in CT-to-EKOS time. This finding needs to be interpreted in the context of real-world clinical decision-making in IHR-PE. As these patients often presented clinically stable despite a recognised risk of early haemodynamic deterioration, an initial strategy of anticoagulation with close surveillance may have preceded treatment escalation in selected cases, particularly when the multidisciplinary PERT decision favoured initial conservative management with subsequent escalation to EKOS following clinical deterioration. At the same time, as this study reflects real-world multicentre clinical practice, delayed treatment initiation may also have been influenced by multidisciplinary reassessment or practical and logistical factors within routine care.15 This variability likely reflects the complexity of contemporary IHR-PE management rather than a single uniform cause of delay.

This highlights the clinical challenge of identifying the optimal timing for treatment escalation in IHR-PE, a condition characterised by a dynamic and potentially unpredictable clinical course that may be underestimated in routine practice.15 16 Our findings suggest that treatment escalation in IHR-PE may still be frequently deferred despite the recognised risk of early clinical deterioration in this patient population. Together with the preserved safety profile observed in both groups and the significantly lower rate of PE-related complications in the early treatment group, these findings support consideration of a more proactive CDT approach in carefully selected patients with IHR-PE. This interpretation is further supported by the recently published HI-PEITHO trial, which demonstrated a reduction in early clinical deterioration without an increase in major bleeding, reinforcing the role of EKOS-based reperfusion strategies in this patient population.7 17

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and non-randomised design precludes causal inference and is susceptible to residual confounding despite comparable baseline characteristics. Treatment timing was determined by clinical judgement within PERT structures and may therefore reflect unmeasured factors influencing both intervention timing and outcomes. Symptom onset-to-CT and symptom onset-to-EKOS intervals could not be reliably determined due to the retrospective multicentre design and the often non-specific clinical presentation of PE, which may limit interpretation of the temporal relationship between symptom onset and EKOS initiation. Serial measurements of cardiac biomarkers such as troponin and NT-proBNP were not routinely performed across all participating centres as part of standard clinical care and could therefore not be reliably assessed in this retrospective multicentre analysis.

Selection bias cannot be excluded, as patients treated early may have differed in clinical trajectory or organisational factors not fully captured in the available data. The relatively smaller size of the delayed treatment group limits statistical power, particularly for safety and mortality endpoints.

Finally, although this was a multicentre study, the results reflect experience from high-volume centres with established EKOS and PERT pathways, which may limit generalisability to other settings.

Conclusion

In patients with IHR-PE, EKOS therapy is associated with rapid and sustained improvement of RV function and a favourable safety profile. Early initiation within 12 hours after CT diagnosis is associated with a shorter ICU stay, lower PE-related complications and comparable mortality rates. These findings support early EKOS initiation, while prospective randomised studies are required for confirmation.
