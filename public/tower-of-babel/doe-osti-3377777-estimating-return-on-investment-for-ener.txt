 Common Terms
 To ensure clear and consistent understanding of key return on investment (ROI) concepts, this
 report introduces commonly used terms as they apply specifically to calculating ROI. Although
 many of these terms are used broadly across various industries, the definitions and applications
 here align with their intended use in this report and in evaluating the ROI of technical assistance
 (TA).

 Baseline data: The data that are collected or that already exist at the beginning of the
 assessment. These data may be used as reference throughout the study and for final comparisons
 to determine impact and attribution. Typically, these data are collected as level 4 or level 3 in a
 Kirkpatrick, Phillips’, or similarly structured ROI framework, further described in Section 2
   (Dadd 2016).

 Benefits: Tangible and intangible impacts that are deemed positive outputs and outcomes
 resulting from a program. Many sources do not define benefits, making direct comparison across
 programs challenging.

 Chain of impact: Demonstration of the link between each level of evaluation to provide
 evidence of attribution. See also definition of “levels” (ROI Institute 2025; Dadd 2013).

 Deliverables: Measurable outputs and outcomes, both tangible and intangible, required for the
 completion of a project. Deliverables are identified before a project begins or are agreed upon in
 early stages (Rahman et al. 2024).

 Framework: A conceptual approach that defines the metrics and analysis to be used in
 evaluating a program. A framework provides a foundation and guidance for maintaining
 consistency during analysis. This is distinct from a logic model, which is the theoretical basis for
 attributing impact, and methodology, which identifies the processes for determining impact.

 Impact: The long-term result of a TA program. Impact is a less direct measurement than
 outcomes—impact is measured over a longer time frame and may interact with external factors,
 making attribution of results more challenging.

 Indicators: Measurable elements that capture conditions or changes and are combined into a
 metric to assess a program or intervention. Indicators encompass any value that may be assessed
 as a part of a ROI analysis, including inputs, process measures, outputs, and outcomes
 (Kotamraju and Mettille III 2012).

 Inputs: Any resources, including human, financial, and physical, used to support a program.
 This may include hours committed, economic costs of a program, audience numbers, the degree
 of activity, or other resources (Kotamraju and Mettille III 2012; Phillips and Phillips 2009).

 Levels: The hierarchy of data used to inform data collection and create a chain of impact. Lower
 levels are typically easier to collect and are important to show chain of impact, though alone do
 not show results. Higher levels are more difficult to collect but have more power to show results.
 Recommended practice incorporates data collection at all levels associated with the evaluation
 framework (Phillips and Phillips 2009).

 v

 Logic model: Visual representation of a program, mapping the problem a program intends to
 solve, the resources required to administer it, and the targeted outputs, outcomes, and impacts
 expected to result from the program (Dadd 2013; Scott et al. 2024).

 Methodology: The processes used for collecting data, calculating metrics, and determining
 impact. The definition of metrics and their inputs is a key part of a methodology.

 Metric: A measure of the effectiveness of a program in meeting its stated goals. Metrics
 typically combine multiple indicators to inform a composite score or value. ROI is an example of
 a metric, as are TA recipient perception of effectiveness and improved organizational capacity or
 performance.

 Monetization: The process of assigning a monetary value to an indicator. Monetary values are
 then considered in the calculation of ROI metric.

 Outcome: Result or effect of a program (Scott et al. 2024). Outcomes describe the overall
 quality and the impact of the program to participants and society. Outcomes may be quantitative
 or qualitative, such as job placement and retention rates, or improved reputation. (Kotamraju and
 Mettille III 2012). Outcomes result from outputs and, in some cases, may be associated with and
 referred to as benefits.

 Outputs: The direct observable mechanics of a program (Scott et al. 2024). An output may be a
 quantifiable value, such as the number of degrees produced or the number of students graduating.
 Outputs are distinct from process measures (Kotamraju and Mettille III 2012).

 Process measure: The means used to deliver programs, such as training, learning assessments,
 and technology use (Kotamraju and Mettille III 2012).

 Steps: The order of operations in which a ROI evaluation is completed. Steps differ from levels,
 which represent hierarchal data categories (Phillips and Phillips 2009).

 Executive Summary
 The U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE) Office of State and Community Energy Programs
 (SCEP) engaged the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) to assess the return on
 investment (ROI) of technical assistance (TA) programs. TA programs are often provided to
 strengthen an entity’s ability to address a need or achieve a goal or outcome by providing
 targeted information or resources. They may support community-led energy planning, build
 institutional capacity, help inform community investment decisions, or mitigate risk for dollars
 DOE is responsible for disbursing to communities (U.S. Government Accountability Office
 2020; Scott et al. 2022; Congress.gov, 2023). TA impacts, however, have historically been
 challenging to evaluate quantitatively because outputs and outcomes resulting from programs
 can be intangible, long term, and difficult to monetize (e.g. assign a monetary value). This study
 develops a potential framework to integrate ROI analysis into the design and evaluation of
 energy TA programs. The purpose is not to assign a singular financial value to TA but to create a
 rigorous, adaptable approach that captures both tangible and intangible impacts, enabling TA
 practitioners to assess performance, improve data collection practices, and communicate
 monetized value.

 The NLR team reviewed more than 40 studies across five commonly used evaluation
 frameworks relevant to applying ROI to TA programs: Kirkpatrick, Phillips, abdi, Social Return
 on Investment (SROI), and Participatory Social Return on Investment (PSROI). Each has
 strengths and limitations in its application to TA. The Kirkpatrick framework serves as a
 precursor to common ROI calculations and provides an early structure to consider program
 success. The Phillips framework builds on the Kirkpatrick framework, incorporating a planning
 process and a formula to consider monetary values. The abdi framework extends this work by
 incorporating intangible, qualitative outputs and outcomes as a part of ROI. The SROI and
 PSROI frameworks explicitly integrate social and environmental value and stakeholder input
 into the planning process and are more data and resource intensive. Several of these frameworks
 also offer strategies to incorporate proxies for intangible results.

 This report outlines findings from the literature review and synthesizes considerations from these
 frameworks into a hybrid approach that aligns practically with the available data and structure of
 energy TA programs. The approach, described in Section 5 and shown in Figure ES-1, integrates
 the five-level evaluation hierarchy of Phillips’ framework, the proactive planning and baseline
 assessment of SROI, and incorporates intangible outputs and outcomes as a part of the ROI from
 the abdi framework. Although the process of implementation may vary depending on program
 goals and structure, this approach enables the consideration of qualitative data for which direct
 monetization is not possible, use of proxies where possible, and emphasizes planning for ROI
 from program inception.

 Through review of the evaluation frameworks, the team prioritized practices that can be applied
 to evaluating ROI for TA programs. Effective ROI calculations for TA require intentional
 integration of ROI-related indicators into program design, with a focus on identifying all
 potential resulting outputs and outcomes, the data points needed to attribute those results to the
 program, and a strategy for data collection before, during, and after program engagement. This
 strategy and the potential outputs and outcomes may be represented through a theory of change
 or a logic model, agreed upon by engaged stakeholders to ensure target outputs and outcomes are
 specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART) for the program.

 Even with strong conceptual frameworks, application of ROI to TA programs is resource and
 time intensive and faces inherent limitations. The targeted impacts from a program might not be
 realized until years after a program concludes, making attribution of results to the program
 difficult. TA outcomes are often intangible, such as building capacity or performing analyses to
 inform decisions—value that may not be easily quantified or monetized. Valuation approaches
 will also vary because of differences in a program’s target outputs and outcomes, limiting the
 comparability of programs. Despite these constraints, integrating ROI planning into TA design
 can offer potential complementary benefits. ROI evaluations encourage more rigorous planning
 and data collection, have the potential to improve the attribution of program results, and facilitate
 learning between programs. This approach can be used to strategically complement, but not
 replace, other forms of program evaluation when data and resource limitations can be overcome.

 To demonstrate feasibility of the approach, NLR applied the proposed ROI framework to the
 Communities Local Energy Action Program (Communities LEAP), a SCEP program supporting
 community-driven energy planning. Although the program’s pilot phase (2021–2024) was not
 designed with ROI evaluation in mind, it offered data to validate components of the framework.
 The team evaluated both the program and a specific community with available quantifiable data.
 Resulting ROI calculations are not representative of the full program but demonstrate that a
 structured ROI framework could be applied to outputs and outcomes resulting from TA to
 quantify potential and actual impacts when designed and resourced with this framework in mind.

 Key challenges and benefits should be considered when implementing a ROI evaluation. A ROI
 evaluation framework is not universally applicable, nor is it intended to replace other evaluation
 approaches. When it is planned and applied using recommended practices, ROI can provide
 meaningful insight into how well a TA program achieved its intended goals. If deemed
 appropriate, ROI can enable TA practitioners to define SMART metrics, establish baseline data
 for comparison, and determine data collection needs and strategies during the planning process.
 Quantitative and qualitative measures are both collected and considered in ROI calculations.
 Recommended practice is for practitioners to quantify and monetize values directly or through a
 proxy. Importantly though, the absence of monetizable values does not represent a lack of value.

 Ultimately, the value of TA extends beyond measurable financial returns, and ROI calculations
 alone do not represent program impact. This study offers a practical, replicable approach for
 calculating ROI for energy TA to quantify and communicate that value—combining economic
 rigor with the recognition of social and institutional benefits that define successful TA programs.
 For programs that choose to invest the time and resources needed to calculate ROI and embed
 principles into program design, practitioners can demonstrate tangible and intangible returns on
 federal investments that advance energy systems nationwide.

 1 Background and Introduction
 The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Office of State and Community Energy Programs
 (SCEP) requested the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) assess return on investment
 (ROI) of technical assistance (TA) programs funded by the office to enable deeper understanding
 of program impact. SCEP invests in “place-based strategies that accelerate the development of
 innovative energy technologies to bolster communities, create jobs, save money, and strengthen
 energy independence and resiliency” (U.S. Department of Energy n.d.[b]). This effort aimed to
 gain a better understanding of the value delivered by TA programs funded by SCEP and ensure
 programmatic outcomes are aligned with DOE goals.

 Although there is no consistent definition of TA across DOE, generally, TA is provided to
 strengthen an entity’s ability to address a need or achieve a goal or outcome by providing
 targeted information or resources. TA is often structured as an individualized and tailored
 approach, developed collaboratively to meet the recipient’s needs, and may support community-
 led energy planning, build institutional capacity, help inform community investment decisions,
 or mitigate risk for dollars DOE is responsible for disbursing to communities (U.S. Government
 Accountability Office 2020; Scott et al. 2022; Congress.gov, 2023).

 NLR serves as a TA program manager and direct provider to state, local, and Tribal governments
 and organizations. In this role, NLR supports SCEP programs, including administering and
 providing TA to states, communities, and Tribal Nations, developing tools and performing
 analyses in support of planning efforts, and conducting related research to bolster the impact of
 SCEP’s programs. Drawing on experience with the SCEP TA portfolio, the NLR team
 synthesized published ROI literature and offers a methodology and considerations for evaluating
 the ROI for TA programs going forward.

 The NLR team applied, to the extent possible, the resulting ROI framework to the Communities
 Local Energy Action Program (Communities LEAP), a recent SCEP-funded TA program.
 Communities LEAP supports locally driven energy planning and development to create long-
 term economic benefits. Through the program, state and local governments and Tribes partner
 with community-based organizations and TA providers to identify energy-related priorities,
 challenges, and opportunities. Each group of partnering organizations, or coalition, receives
 customized in-depth TA—primarily from NLR—to explore one or more pathways, such as
 adding generation capacity, improving energy efficiency, supporting energy planning, and
 strengthening community resilience. The program helps participants access analysis and
 resources that inform decision-making and lay the groundwork for future energy-related
 projects. This report applies the ROI framework to the data available from the pilot phase of
 Communities LEAP, implemented between 2021 and 2024 (U.S. Department of Energy
 2021). This approach may be applicable as an evaluation method for other TA programs in the
 future when considered as a part of program development.

 In addition to this effort on ROI for TA programs, NLR is conducting related, parallel research
 efforts to evaluate, align, and standardize TA impact metrics and evaluation. Although these
 efforts are in progress and results are not yet available to inform this report, the NLR research

 1.1.1 Metrics Framework for Technical Assistance
 The DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy (EERE) identified a growing need
 for coordinated and consistent evaluation strategies for federally funded energy-related TA
 programs. EERE identified a need to address the following complexities:

 • Individuals and organizations developing and implementing DOE assistance programs need a
 shared way of communicating about their work, both internally and externally.
 • Lack of a standard framework has made it difficult to meaningfully distinguish between
 programs and between methods of measuring success in a consistent and coherent way.
 • Crosscutting program coordination, collaborative learning, and best practice sharing has
 historically occurred on an ad-hoc basis.
 In support of EERE, NLR aims to address these challenges by providing a framework that
 enables better, more consistent assessment efforts across DOE TA programs to help programs
 succeed at meeting their objectives. Specifically, NLR aims to:

 1. Document approaches and best practices in DOE TA program metrics and evaluation
 efforts to date
 2. Review the literature of approaches and best practices of metrics and evaluation efforts
 beyond the DOE portfolio
 3. Develop a measurement and evaluation framework that can be tailored for use within
 various EERE-funded TA programs
 4. Develop a measurement and evaluation framework that can look across a portfolio of
 EERE-funded TA programs to better understand and evaluate impact at the portfolio
 scale.

 1.1.2 Preliminary Exploration of Generalized Metrics for Technical Assistance
 Programs
 In addition to the metrics framework strategy, EERE also identified a growing need for a set of
 standardized metrics that would be broadly applicable to TA programs. Using information and
 findings from the simultaneous efforts Metrics Framework for Technical Assistance and Return
 on Investment for State and Community Energy Technical Assistance Programs, NLR aims to
 identify a set of metrics that are broadly applicable to a given subset of TA. Although not all
 metrics identified may be relevant to all TA programs, all TA programs can leverage a subset of
 those metrics that are relevant and achievable for more consistent evaluation.

 2 Literature Review
 Various frameworks can be used to measure the impact of specific programs. ROI is a specific
 metric that can be used within an evaluation framework to assess whether the benefits of a
 program outweigh its costs, typically expressed as a percentage using the following formula
   (Dadd 2016):

 ROI = [(Realized Monetary Benefits – Actual Costs) / Actual Costs] × 100

 ROI is traditionally employed in a business context across a variety of sectors where
 measurement of quantifiable outputs or outcomes can be directly compared to performance. For
 a business, the data used to calculate ROI are largely available internally, such as sales volume,
 product or service quality, or customer complaints. TA, however, involves supporting external
 stakeholders to meet their own goals such as developing a community-led energy plan, building
 institutional capacity, informing investment decisions, or mitigating risk. The data points,
 outputs, and outcomes associated with TA may not be as easily available, measurable,
 quantifiable, or monetizable as in business contexts.

 The NLR team reviewed more than 40 sources to understand how ROI has been employed in TA
 or similar programs in other sectors, including health care, education, and finance. Although
 frameworks for ROI analysis are used by these sectors to determine if the benefits outweigh the
 costs of certain investments, there are limited documented examples of ROI calculations
 performed for TA. Results from this literature review are summarized in this section and inform
 the approach in Section 6 for a ROI methodology to evaluate SCEP TA programs.

 The scope of this literature review focused on frameworks most relevant to TA evaluations and
 those that formed the foundation of those frameworks—it is not intended to be a comprehensive
 scan of all ROI publications. From the literature, the NLR team identified five ROI frameworks
 that informed the approach and data points needed: Kirkpatrick, Phillips, abdi, Social Return on
 Investment (SROI), and Participatory Social Return on Investment (PSROI). Each of these
 frameworks, the data points needed to perform the calculations, and methods for calculating are
 described in further detail in the following sections.

 2.1 Development of Return on Investment Frameworks
 An evaluation framework outlines the conceptual approach, including the type of metrics and
 analysis to be used to evaluate a program. ROI is one of many metrics to evaluate program
 effectiveness and requires the development of an evaluation framework to ensure appropriate
 integration into TA program design. Metrics comprise multiple indicators, “the basic raw
 material used in program evaluation,” (Kotamraju and Mettille III 2012, 6). Indicators include all
 elements that may be measured as part of a ROI evaluation, including inputs, outputs, process
 measures, outcomes, and impacts. A description of metrics and indicators is provided in Table 1.
 Inputs include resources used to support the program, such as funding, staffing, or physical
 resources. Process measures are the means used to deliver a program, such as delivered training
 or learning assessments, and are determined as a part of program development and strategy to
 guide program evaluation. Outputs are the direct observable mechanics of the program, such as
 the number of technical reports, community workshops, or results of participant surveys.
 Outcomes are “the result(s) or effect(s) of an intervention” from the TA provided (Scott et al.

 3

 2024). Indicators may be classified as internal or external. Internal indicators are controlled or
 measured within a program and may include quantified data such as TA hours delivered or
 participant satisfaction surveys. External indicators are those influenced, but not fully controlled,
 by the program, such as jobs created or energy saved as a result of participation (Kotamraju and
 Mettille III 2012).

 To perform a comprehensive ROI analysis, evaluators identify clear target outputs and outcomes
 and perform a baseline assessment to provide a point against which to measure change. When
 target outputs, outcomes, and baseline assessments are established at the beginning of a project,
 teams are able to meaningfully measure progress and potential impact. Challenges with the
 attribution of results to a program are considered a part of program design. This report discusses
 approaches for isolating common outputs and outcomes to ensure accurate measurement, while
 noting that TA practitioners may not always be able to clearly isolate qualitative, external
 outputs and outcomes resulting from programs (Kotamraju and Mettille III 2012).

 ROI analysis can support evaluation in a range of industries and be performed using
 representative and complex data points, but all calculations fundamentally compare the
 relationship between a set of costs and a set of benefits (Phillips and Phillips 2009).
 Quantification of costs can encompass opportunity costs, time horizon, discount rate,
 monetization, and externalities, and “when any or all of these calculations are omitted, the result
 is invariably the under- or over-estimation of costs and benefits, which in turn skew the
 assessment by skewing the measures” (Kotamraju and Mettille III 2012, 8). Costs can include
 tradeoffs in how funds are spent, the time value of money, and costs borne by other parties.
 Benefits include private and social, internal and external, monetary and nonmonetary, immediate
 and long-term, and tangible and intangible results (2012). When tracking benefits, TA may result
 in actions that would not have otherwise occurred, as well as actions that occur on an accelerated
 timeline, reflecting the time value of money.

 4

 In 1954, Donald Kirkpatrick developed a framework to evaluate learning and development
 programs (Dadd 2016). Since its creation, Kirkpatrick’s four-level framework has been expanded
 for ROI calculations. ROI is represented by a range of data classification levels. For the purpose
 of this report, levels are hierarchal data categories, in which each data level must have defined
 outputs or outcomes and measurements of success (Phillips and Phillips 2009).

 Gathering data at all levels is required to represent the chain of impact. Dadd (2013) explains
 that the “‘chain of impact’ shows the link between each level of outcome to provide credible
 evidence of the impact of the [program]” (6). The chain of impact aims to address a program’s
 ability to attribute outcomes directly to the program’s implementation. Chain of impact is an
 indicator used to demonstrate the extent to which the program has contributed to the outcome.
 The chain of impact is similar to the chain of evidence initially developed by Kirkpatrick’s
 framework (Dadd 2016).

 Two frameworks originating from Kirkpatrick’s framework were used to inform the proposed
 ROI approach for TA: the Phillips five-level framework and the abdi six-level framework. Two
 additional independent frameworks, SROI and PSROI, are also relevant to TA. Figure 1 and
 Figure 2 depict the data levels and step approaches used in these frameworks. Each data level
 must have defined outputs or outcomes and measurements of success (Phillips and Phillips
 2009).

 2.2 Tangible and Intangible Values in ROI Frameworks
 A challenge in calculating ROI is quantifying intangible or long-term impacts at complex data
 levels. In a 2024 literature review to measure and calculate the benefits of government funded
 education research grants, Rahman et al. (2024) assessed potential deliverables and associated
 categories that could help evaluate the value of this work. Figure 3 shows tangible outputs,
 intangible outputs, and intangible outcomes along with the associated deliverable examples,
 many of which are closely aligned with typical outputs and outcomes from community TA
 (2024). Intangible benefits can still be measured but typically take more time and money to
 quantify. Depending on the goals of program evaluation, quantification of intangible benefits
 may not be essential. However, intangible benefits can sometimes be represented through
 proxies, data points that are easier to measure and more obtainable than the intangible values
 they represent (Crawley-Stout et al. 2016). For example, although there may not be a direct
 measurement for employee productivity, project completion rate is a measurable and indicative
 proxy. Further techniques on how to apply proxies are detailed in Section 2.3.4.

 quality TA outputs and outcomes reinforce each other and lead to intended long-term impacts.

 Because of challenges in quantifying intangible benefits in economic ROI calculations,
 quantitative outcomes from ROI calculations alone do not holistically represent program
 outcomes and impact. When reporting ROI, studies commonly include both tangible (easily
 quantifiable benefits) and intangible (qualitative benefits) that extend beyond calculable
 monetary data (Rahman et al. 2024). A review of TA program evaluations noted “quantitative
 data were reported more frequently than qualitative data, 51% and 22%, respectively” with 26%
 of studies reporting both data types (Scott et al. 2022, 6). Furthermore, 42% of data reported

 8

 were subjective, such as surveys to rate TA outcomes, versus 21% of data as objective, such as
 number of community visits (2022). Even when subjective data are represented in quantitative
 terms, this can still be challenging to monetize because of the need for a proxy. The case study
 from Jayani and Yamazaki (2012), explored in Section 3.3.2, provides an example of monetizing
 ROI with subjective survey data and a financial multiplier.

 2.3 Return on Investment Frameworks
 2.3.1 Kirkpatrick Four-Level Framework
 As a framework to evaluate learning, Donald Kirkpatrick developed the precursor to common
 ROI frameworks, which analyzes reaction, learning, behavior, and results (Dadd 2016). As
 shown in Figure 4, Level 1 (Reaction) gauges the extent to which an individual finds the
 program engaging, relevant, and/or satisfying. Level 2 (Learning) examines to what extent an
 individual gains knowledge, skills, attitude, confidence, and commitment as the program
 intended, often measured with a pre- and post-test. Level 3 (Behavior) gauges to what extent an
 individual applies the program’s learned material once they have returned to their environment.
 Kirkpatrick recommends that an “accountability and support” package be developed and
 implemented as a method to encourage application of the learned behavior. Level 4 (Results)
 analyzes to what extent an outcome results from the program. Level 4 is useful in evaluating the
 overall effectiveness of the program in achieving the original intended impact. Control groups
 are used to understand and measure progress in levels 2–4. It is also recommended to identify
 “leading indicators” that signal whether the program is successful (Kirkpatrick Partners n.d.).

 Figure 4. Kirkpatrick’s four-level framework.
 Source: Kirkpatrick Partners n.d.

 Kirkpatrick’s framework features a chain of evidence, which includes quantitative facts and
 figures, as well as qualitative stories and testimonies that can highlight the value obtained during
 the program (Kirkpatrick Partners n.d.). Each level is evaluated chronologically and included in
 the chain of evidence (Dadd 2016). The chain of evidence approach has been adapted by the
 Phillips and abdi frameworks into the chain of impact used to show linkage between a program
 and intended outputs and outcomes (Dadd 2013), described in the following sections.

 9

 The Kirkpatrick framework assumes training impacts the results and does not consider other
 potentially contributing factors or considerations of attribution. Results, or Level 4 of
 Kirkpatrick’s framework, include program outcomes and “the cost of evaluation should be
 considered in relation to the potential benefits.” This level does not, however, include ROI
 analysis (Dadd 2016).

 2.3.2 Phillips’ Five-Level Framework
 Phillips’ five-level ROI framework is one of the most commonly used approaches for estimating
 ROI (Dadd 2016). Phillips’ framework was developed in the 1970s and expands on Kirkpatrick’s
 four-level framework. Phillips adds a fifth level of valuation to assess the organization’s impact
 against the costs of the project, represented as a percentage. Phillips’ framework incorporates
 additional techniques in the calculation of ROI to better understand and represent outcomes,
 including control groups, trend line analysis, forecasting, and estimations. This framework also
 provided 12 guidelines for evaluation, included in Appendix A (Dadd 2013). Originally designed
 for corporate applications and “widely used in evaluating human capital [programs] within
 organizations,” more than 21 application types of Phillips’ framework are listed by the ROI
 Institute, including training and development, education, health services, project management,
 information technology, and engineering solutions (Dadd 2016).

 Phillips’ framework includes five evaluation levels, and an optional level 0, summarized in
 Figure 1:

 0. Level 0 is included to measure inputs and indicators ahead of program implementation.
 1. Level 1 evaluates “Reaction, Satisfaction & Planned Actions,” and measures learners’
 satisfaction and reaction to a given project and their implementation plans.
 2. Level 2 evaluates “Learning” by measuring the learners’ understanding of the project.
 3. Level 3 examines the “Application & Implementation” of the implementation plan, which
 may include the use of knowledge and skills on the job.
 4. Level 4 evaluates the “Business Impact,” or how organizations impact indicators are
 changed as a result.
 5. Level 5 calculates ROI, comparing the monetary benefits against the monetary costs of
 the project (Dadd 2016).
 As indicated by the inclusion of Level 0 into Phillips’ framework, data collection is critical to
 ROI. The methods for selecting and collecting data must align with what can feasibly be
 collected and what is needed to inform the ROI calculation. Phillips (2007) lists the following as
 potential methods of data collection: surveys, questionnaires, tests, on-the-job observations,
 interviews, focus groups, action plans and assignments, performance contracts, and business
 performance monitoring. Business impact data are then isolated to pinpoint direct study impacts,
 and isolated data are converted to monetary values. Phillips emphasizes the importance of a
 strong planning process, including identifying inputs and target outputs and outcomes,
 performing a baseline assessment to measure progress against, and planning data collection
 methods and points to inform ROI evaluation during program development (Phillips 2007).

 When planning programs with the intent to evaluate ROI, isolating the program’s effects will
 increase accuracy of ROI evaluations (Phillips 2007). Control groups, trend lines, forecasting
 models, and feedback from participants, supervisors, managers, experts, and customers are all

 10

 techniques to isolate learning and development impacts that could be incorporated in the
 planning and implementation process. This approach also supports improved techniques to
 address attribution, or the ability to credit an action to a specific result (Phillips 2007). Gathering
 data systemically to create a chain of impact also isolates impact outcomes (Dadd 2016). The
 chain of impact identifies outputs and outcomes at each level followed by data collection and
 measurement for each, supporting the ability to attribute those impacts to the program (Dadd
 2016). To identify a chain of impact, it is necessary to define “clear SMART (Specific,
 Measurable, Attainable, Realistic and Timely) [outputs and outcomes] at each level and then
 collecting measurement data” as evidence to demonstrate linkage (Dadd 2016, 30).

 A complete ROI assessment using Phillips’ framework results in a monetary value represented
 by a percentage ROI. Two primary data points are required for this calculation: program costs
 and net program benefits. Converting collected data into monetary values “requires a value to be
 placed on each unit of data connected with the program” (Phillips 2007, 12). Case studies
 reviewed for this report analyze techniques to convert data of program costs and net benefits to
 monetary values. Some common techniques to assign value to originally nonmonetized outputs
 and outcomes include using financial data from industry databases, historical costs, and estimates
 from participants and managers (Phillips 2007). Abundance of qualitative data, such as job
 satisfaction, can make this already challenging step more difficult, as additional work is needed
 to convert qualitative data to quantitative values (Phillips 2007). To calculate program costs, a
 “conservative approach is to include all [program] costs so that the total is fully loaded,”
 including the cost of the ROI assessment to ensure the evaluation is comprehensive (Phillips
 2007). Following a complete ROI assessment, final results are reported. This report discusses
 case studies of ROI monetizing techniques and the challenges in completing a ROI analysis for
 an existing TA program in Section 3.3.

 2.3.3 abdi Six-Level Framework
 The organization abdi ltd., based in the United Kingdom, designed the abdi six-level framework,
 summarized in Table 2, which builds off the Kirkpatrick and Phillips’ frameworks “by refining
 the levels…and adding a sixth [level], implementing project tools to facilitate a more disciplined
 planning stage, and focusing on stakeholder engagement and accountability” (Dadd 2013, 6). To
 address hard-to-quantify outcomes excluded from Phillips’ framework, the sixth level of the abdi
 framework considers nonmonetized outcomes, also referred to as intangibles, that have not been,
 or cannot be, converted to monetary values. The evaluation of intangibles occurs after
 calculating ROI values. In Phillips’ framework, intangible benefits are captured as a type of data,
 but in the abdi framework, intangibles are considered as a sixth step in the evaluation process
 following ROI calculation (Dadd 2016). Although intangible outputs and outcomes are not
 monetized, they are still considered relevant to program results and are qualitatively included as
 a representation of ROI overall.

 Case studies implementing abdi discuss challenges in data collection, determining attribution,
 and calculating monetary values. Determining attribution is not required in level 2 and 3 data
 collection, though it is a critical step in ROI analyses. To address these challenges, reports reflect
 common use of assumptions and estimates for determining attribution, rather than quantitative
 data (Dadd 2016). Data collection is particularly difficult for learning and development programs
 and TA programs because of challenges in data access, quality, and type with few data points

 represented as monetary values, since outputs and outcomes from these programs are not
 typically financially based.

 Some challenges to measuring ROI include monetizing a program’s inputs and outputs.
 Although Phillips’ framework is widely accepted for assessing ROI, it has limitations for

 creating a sixth outcome level and reports intangible outputs along with percent ROI. Dadd notes

 rather, including intangible outcomes more comprehensively represents the entire scope of a
 program’s impact and indicates success in alignment with identified outputs or outcomes (Dadd
 2016).

 2.3.4 Social Return on Investment
 SROI measures the “value of social benefits in relation to the relative cost of achieving those
 benefits.” (Millar & Hall, 2013) SROI value encompasses social, economic, and environmental
 value and examines the importance of these factors to impacted people or organizations to
 measure change (Nicholls et al. 2012). Although SROI can provide a monetary value beyond
 financial evaluations used in other ROI approaches, similar to the adbi framework, SROI does

 12

 not require a monetary value to be applied to all impacts. Instead, SROI values can be used to
 calculate “extra financial” values (Rahman et al. 2024).

 The SROI framework is designed to translate SROI value into monetary value through
 representative proxies. These proxies may be easy to source, such as cost savings or change in
 income, whereas others may be more challenging. Commonly used techniques to identify those
 proxies include stated preference, contingent valuation, revealed preference, and hedonic pricing.
 Stated preference and contingent valuation techniques ask individuals to put a numeric value on
 an item to represent their willingness-to-pay as a proxy. Revealed preference evaluates publicly
 available data on related activities, such as government data for household spending, to represent
 similar values. Hedonic pricing examines the market pricing of a given good or service in
 comparison to others (Nicholls et al. 2012).

 Once translated, outcomes from SROI can result in a cost-benefit ratio that evaluates the
 investment compared to SROI value outcomes. SROI can be used “to analyze the effects on
 stakeholders, identify opportunities to improve performance and optimize investment[s]”
 (Rahman et al. 2024, 106). The purpose of SROI is to account for social, environmental, and
 economic outputs and outcomes in monetary values to represent changes in value to impacted
 people or organizations.

 SROI can be conducted as either forecast or evaluative. Forecast SROI is primarily an estimation
 of value that will be created by the program. This framework can be used for planning, showing
 how investment can maximize impact, and identifying factors to measure once a program is
 implemented. Forecast SROI can also be used to provide the framework by which to collect
 outputs and outcomes needed for evaluative SROI. It is recommended to begin using the SROI
 framework with a forecast value, as this method can inform the data points and collection
 systems needed for a full analysis following program implementation. Evaluative SROI is
 performed following a program’s conclusion to assess the program's actual outputs and
 outcomes. SROI can be used by organizations and individuals, such as nonprofits, social
 enterprises, private businesses, funders, and policymakers.

 To successfully conduct a SROI, an organization needs to engage relevant stakeholders
 throughout the SROI process. As examples, stakeholders may include funders, staff, and
 beneficiaries. SROI casts a wide net, aiming to include stakeholders at every stage in the SROI
 process, with an explicit attempt to include those who may not have historically engaged on the
 decision. Quantitative data on inputs, outputs, and outcomes that reflect priorities of stakeholders
 are also required, along with data representative of a scenario in which the program did not
 occur. The data from this scenario can be used for comparison to identify what changes would
 have happened even without support of the program. Last, outcomes that can be easily assigned
 monetary value, such as job creation, as well as subjective outcomes, such as feeling safer, are
 considered (New Philanthropy Capital 2010).

 SROI inputs include anything a stakeholder contributes to a program, often represented as money
 or time. Time, such as volunteer hours, can be converted to financial value based on what work
 would be paid. For example, if an individual volunteered 3 hours of teaching in a location where
 teachers are paid $20/hour, the input would be $60. Outputs may be described in the same units
 as inputs and generally include the result of the activity. If the purpose of a program is to

 13

 establish and continue a volunteer-led reading program for children, an outcome may be the
 daily reading sessions that are established as a result. Outcomes may be long term, potentially
 represented as an increase in children’s reading proficiency over time, or more books checked
 out at the local library during a defined interval (Nicholls et al. 2012).

 2.3.5 Participatory Social Return on Investment
 Developed in 2011 by Abrar Chaudhury, PSROI is a five-step framework that connects
 economic analysis with adaptation prioritization and planning. PSROI does not propose a new
 approach but builds on existing SROI methods with an added participatory focus. Though
 described as a distinct process, PSROI cannot be conducted without incorporating the steps noted
 in a SROI analysis. This framework is used primarily for multistakeholder planning by using
 community-level participation to support identification of adaptation strategies (Chaudhury
 2012). A PSROI analysis is conducted over five steps, represented in Figure 6.

 The first step in the PSROI framework is to identify stakeholders. Stakeholders encompass a
 larger range compared to SROI because PSROI may include anyone who may be relevant to the
 given project from participating community representatives at any given level, involved during
 the framework’s implementation, impact mapping, or with an interest in the outcomes of the
 framework (Sova et al. 2012). The second step is to convene these stakeholders in a participatory
 process such as a workshop, interviews, or community mapping, in which challenges, strategies,
 and plans can be developed. The final product of step 2 can be a proposed solution or next steps
 for addressing the problem in question. Step 3 engages experts and partners to further refine the
 plan developed in step 2.

 Step 4 calculates the cost-benefit analysis via the PSROI ratio, using the same formula as the
 SROI ratio, to calculate the monetary value of the economic, social, and environmental benefit
 during this step. Step 5 validates the baseline results. The intent of steps 4 and 5 are (1) to
 capture perspectives from the local stakeholders who were involved in the program’s process and
 then (2) to compare these perspectives with the baseline PSROI. One method of capturing these
 perspectives is via interview. The goal of this interview is to validate the findings from step 4
 and assumptions included in the baseline, along with any additional benefits and technical or
 market assumptions that arise from the community. The baseline PSROI ratio is then compared
 with a PSROI ratio that has been recalculated based on primary field data to identify gaps in
 which the stakeholder and market perceptions diverge. The participatory elements are key
 differentiating factors of PSROI and are more typically used to examine specific adaptation
 strategies (Chaudhury et al. 2016). Figure 5 visually represents the fourth and fifth steps of the
 PSROI process.

 3 Considerations for Performing ROI Calculations
 As part of the literature review, the NLR team identified considerations for how ROI frameworks
 could be applied to TA programs. This section includes an exploration of the theory of change
 and logic models, monetization approaches for key outputs and outcomes, and case studies that
 informed the approach. The theory of change has been applied by energy TA and other industries
 to uncover monetary and nonmonetary values most important to stakeholders. Monetization
 approaches help to provide an economic value to outcomes that are traditionally nonmonetary,
 and case studies serve as examples to inform the approach described in Section 5.

 3.1 Theory of Change and Logic Models
 An adjacent approach for measuring value created in TA is the theory of change. Although not
 necessarily measuring monetary values, a theory of change is “a description … of how and why
 a desired change is expected to happen in a particular context. It is focused [on] what a program
 does (its activities or interventions) and how [these lead] to desired goals being achieved” (The
 Center for Theory of Change 2025). This approach, represented visually as a logic model,
 defines program goals up front and measures change made toward those goals with defined
 outputs and outcomes that represent progress, are measurable, and are attributable to the
 program. Logic models are created during program development in partnership with practitioners
 and funders. Collaborative development ensures the logic model represents funder goals and that
 it is possible to measure and achieve progress in implementation (Woodhouse et al. 2013).

 To leverage this approach, a recommended practice is to define all elements of the theory of
 change before designing the TA program: “Pre-specifying the level of change needed to achieve
 a precondition makes it easier to design the components of the intervention to achieve that
 target” (De Silva et al. 2014, 6). Incorporating logic model development allows the program
 team to identify the inputs, or the activities that will be performed, and determine how those will
 lead to the short-, medium-, and long-term outputs and outcomes the team envisions will be
 achieved. By incorporating theory of change considerations in planning, “design and
 implementation issues are brought center-stage from the start” (De Silva et al. 2014, 9). A theory
 of change is designed to measure change in performance for identified metrics, so it cannot be
 completed without first performing a baseline assessment to measure progress (Woodhouse et al.
 2013).

 To inform the theory of change development, it necessary to understand TA needs.
 Recommended approaches include evaluating the “scale and scope of current program offerings,
 service area coverage, and services provided,” and performing surveys and focus groups to tailor
 programs to recipient needs (Miran 2013). An example of the results of a survey conducted to
 inform TA services offered is shown in Table 3. Data from these efforts can help practitioners
 design strategically targeted support to each recipient and allow the program team to better
 identify targets and potential impacts based on engagement (Miran 2013).

 The application of theory of change and development of individual logic models is both industry
 and program specific and may occur at different stages of program implementation. For
 comparison, in the private health care sector, logic models are leveraged as an evaluation
 mechanism because of the available internal, quantifiable data. Use of logic models has been
 growing in the public health sector because of increasing pressures to optimize programs. Public
 health data can be abundant, and tools and templates have helped to streamline logic model
 development and ROI evaluation. However, health care ROI studies still note the difficulties and
 subjectivity of monetizing intangible outputs and outcomes for comprehensive ROI evaluation,
 as well as limited access to the financial expertise needed to perform ROI calculations (Crawley-
   Stout et al. 2016; Rein et al. 2012).

 The health care sector has been successful at introducing logic models and targeted program
 goals early into projects for long-term evaluation, because the sector already uses control groups
 (Crawley-Stout et al. 2016; Rein et al. 2012). In a study on participants implementing health
 information technology programs, although some of the evaluators had access to health
 economists, others relied on external data sources or experts for planning ROI calculations,
 resulting in a wide range of results that over- or underestimated ROI (Rein et al. 2012). The
 growing use of ROI in health care, and TA-related case studies emphasizing parallel challenges,
 makes lessons learned from the health care industry helpful to consider in evaluating ROI in TA.
 A case study of how a logic model has been employed to measure success and progress for the
 DOE Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project (ETIPP) is included in Appendix E.

 3.2 Monetizing Technical Assistance Outputs and Outcomes
 In the energy sector, researchers aimed to evaluate and quantify assumptions that “energy
 efficiency policies are beneficial because they induce investments that pay for themselves”
 (Fowlie et al. 2015, 1). This study leveraged principles from multiple frameworks to calculate

 17

 the ROI of a subset of energy efficiency TA support programs funded by DOE. Notably, it found
 that the ROI for these programs, evaluated based on their uptake between 2011 and 2014, was
 −9.5% annually. The study considered both monetizable outputs in energy savings resulting from
 weatherization improvements and monetized intangible results, including a reduction in
 environmental damages and a reduction in the average cost per ton of avoided carbon. The
 misalignment of these results and original expectations that influence investment is an assumed
 uptake of planning efforts: “An important factor leading to negative returns on investment is the
 incomplete realization of projected energy savings … the projected savings are roughly 4 times
 the quasi-experimental estimates” (Fowlie et al. 2015, 34).

 External factors influencing whether energy efficiency and other TA recommendations are
 implemented include lack of access to funding or contractors or vendors that can implement
 recommendations in a community (Gravert et al. 2024). This report does not attempt to predict
 these factors but acknowledges external factors will impact the uptake of energy planning efforts.
 The assessment by Fowlie et al. (2015) exclusively evaluated energy efficiency measure uptake
 as a result of participation in TA programs and is only one study that does not necessarily
 represent uptake rates of outcomes resulting from all TA programs. It is, however, the only study
 the NLR team identified in the literature that analyzed the long-term uptake rate of energy
 planning that could be attributed to TA programs. The quantified uptake finding provides a
 reasonable assumption on the potential uptake rate of energy plans resulting from TA,
 conservatively at 25%, which is used for application of the proposed ROI approach to
 Communities LEAP. Long-term evaluation of uptake rates is an opportunity for future study.

 Monetizing individual energy planning efforts and consistently estimating energy savings
 nationally vary by communities as well. As a part of this study, the NLR team reviewed
 monetized outputs and outcomes that were pursued in energy planning activities through
 Communities LEAP. Although available values were not always aligned with data needs for ROI
 calculation, specific outputs and outcomes relevant to the case study are referenced in Section 5.
 The full list of monetized values is included in Appendix D.

 3.3 Example ROI Applications
 To examine how ROI calculations are applied, the team reviewed two case studies. The first
 evaluates New York State Energy Research and Development Authority (NYSERDA) energy
 efficiency TA programs. This case study was one of the few examples that evaluates TA focused
 on energy, and ROI is heavily context and industry dependent. Although the case is dated, the
 team examined the approach for relevant metrics, including the qualitative data leveraged and the
 methods for doing so over an extended period. The second evaluates an auditor training program
 from Indonesia’s Directorate General of Taxes. This case study did not focus on energy TA but
 demonstrates methods for assessing a training program using Phillips’ framework and
 incorporating financial proxies for qualitative data. The study also details lessons learned and
 challenges associated with data collection. The team considered these studies in the development
 of the approach in Section 5.

 3.3.1 NYSERDA Energy Efficiency Technical Assistance
 The NYSERDA evaluation of two energy-related TA programs, FlexTech and the Technical
 Assistance Program, serves as an example for how ROI has been calculated for energy-related

 18

 TA programs (Hogan et al. 2003). The programs offered cost-sharing to connect NYSERDA
 customers to tailored TA to improve energy efficiency and energy cost savings. In this analysis,
 researchers used telephone surveys to evaluate the combined cost-effectiveness of TA provided
 from 1992 to 2000. Pre-survey data from existing files included the completed project
 information, participant type, investment in the TA project, and overall work completed.
 Telephone surveys were used to establish a database for results reported by TA customers
 surveyed. The survey asked quantifiable questions to understand outputs and outcomes the
 recipient directly attributed to their participation in the program (Hogan et al. 2003).

 A total of 216 customers responded to the survey, of which “144 (66%) implemented
 recommendations” given by the TA (Hogan et al. 2003, 2). The evaluation measured energy
 savings from the participants (annual), monetary savings from the participants (annual), capital
 spent by the participant on energy efficiency improvements (single cost), and the overall payback
 (calculated and averaged based on participant type). NYSERDA reported cost-effectiveness
 through customer financial investment, financial energy savings, electricity and natural gas
 savings, and emissions reduction.

 The study found that for every $1 spent on TA, customers made a one-time investment of $18 on
 energy efficiency improvements and received $5 in annual energy savings. An important
 nonmonetized benefit noted in the study identified additional stimulation in New York’s energy
 economy, improving the “economic viability of New York’s businesses and institutions and
 reduce[d] New York’s dependency on energy imports” (Hogan et al. 2003, 3). The ROI results
 also indicate the programs’ approximate annual investment of $5 million could “result in $90
 million in energy-related capital improvements and $25 million per year in energy savings”
   (Hogan et al. 2003, 4).

 Although the telephone survey methods may not be applicable to data gathering approaches
 today, the NYSERDA case study conducted ROI evaluations of energy-based TA. The study
 also provides examples of an approach using qualitative survey data and subjective feedback to
 account for attribution (i.e., whether the customer action was a direct result of the TA). The
 report does not include in-depth methods for monetizing the feedback and completing the ROI
 calculation. However, the clear outcomes implemented from the TA and the ability to calculate
 energy saving to a financial value offer more tangible evaluation outcomes than many other TA
 programs reviewed for this report.

 3.3.2 A Return on Investment Approach to Training Evaluation in Public Sector
 Indonesia’s Directorate General of Taxes auditor training program offers a ROI case study that
 calculates monetary benefits with adjustments from intangible benefits. In this evaluation, Jayani
 and Yamazaki measure increased self-confidence from a 2011 training program based on survey
 data and an application of Phillips’ five-level framework to determine if the benefits of training
 exceeded the cost (2012). Although performance records are the most reliable metric for Phillips’
 framework, current performance records were not suitable for analysis because of the tax
 auditors’ workload structure. The study relied on data collected through questionnaires and
 internal data, modeled from other studies.

 Jayani and Yamazaki used methods defined by Phillips (2003) to evaluate ROI with survey-
 based data. Participants assessed the impact of training programs on themselves under the

 19

 assumption that “because the participant is the center of performance improvements, they may
 have very accurate input about performance improvements” (Jayani and Yamazaki 2012, 20).
 Questionnaires included both open- and closed-ended questions. Of the 427 auditors who took
 the intermediate training, the questionnaire response rate was about 26.5%. To monetize the ROI
 indicators, both the cost of running the training program and employee’s salaries were needed.
 The evaluation makes multiple assumptions, including that participants can provide an accurate
 analysis of training program impacts, an approach argued against by some practitioners, and that
 when participants saved time completing an audit, they then used this time productively.

 Phillips’ levels 2–4 data were collected for 8 weeks, using chain of impact to address attribution.
 The three performance improvement metrics of job performance, time savings, and quality (error
 reduction) fall within level 4 of Phillips’ framework and aimed to isolate the effects of training.
 Increased performance was based on participant survey feedback and perception of performance
 after the training. Audit completion time was a measurement of how long individuals took to
 complete audits, and quality of audits were measured through error reductions. To complete the
 ROI calculation, audit completion time and error reduction measurements assume participants
 use this extra time in a productive way and do more work on the same salary.

 The survey asked respondents about their confidence level after training, collected in parallel
 with the quality (error reduction) metric to align credibility of self-reported improvements and
 adjust for potential errors. In practice, these metrics were combined to represent an adjusted level
 of improvement: “If after the training, the respondent stated 80% of improvements but with only
 70% level of confidence, then the adjusted performance improvement should be 80% × 70% that
 is 56%” (Jayani and Yamazaki 2012, 23). The three performance improvements, adjusted for
 confidence and measured against salary as the financial proxy, were then used to calculate
 realized monetary benefits in level 5, the ROI cost-benefit analysis. Participants were asked the
 percent of skills, learned from the training program, that they use at work, which was then
 converted to “percentage of salary spent on the tasks using the acquired skills … The resulting
 figure is then multiplied by three components of productivity gained from three dimensions of
 performance improvements” (Jayani and Yamazaki 2012, 24). A 1% increase in performance
 improvement metric was weighted as a benefit of 1% gain in salary. Figure 7 shows the logic
 behind calculating a total productivity gain based on salary.

 Detail on the cost of developing the training program as well as some other relevant supporting

 monetizing subjective questionnaire data and offers lessons learned to consider in calculating
 ROI for similar training-based programs. The use of salary is a key example of using proxy data,
 which is valuable in public sector ROI analyses when outputs and outcomes are not directly
 correlated to financial values. Jayani and Yamazaki (2012) note that collecting adequate data was
 the most challenging part of the ROI calculations and that “it is far easier to collect the data when
 ROI evaluation and data collection methods have been considered during training program
 design and development” (30).

 Jayani and Yamazaki (2012) received a large range in feedback of performance metrics,
 representing disagreement on the impact of the program—not easily conveyed through a ROI
 value. Participants had a performance improvement ranging from 5% to 100%, a time-savings
 improvement of 5% to 90%, and a quality improvement of 6% to 90%. Prior to data collection
 for the ROI analyses, the study did not collect baseline data, so it is not known if this range of
 employee feedback is attributable to the training program or the result of differences in
 participant baseline.

 4 Discussion
 Calculating ROI is a complex, resource- and time-intensive process requiring substantial
 planning, engaged stakeholders, and significant data collection, even with multiple widely used
 frameworks available. Developing programs with timelines, control groups, SMART metrics,
 and data types that fit into ROI evaluation frameworks can require shifts in TA program design
 and budget (Katz 2015). ROI has not been widely applied to TA because of the mismatch
 between typical TA goals and monetizable data needed for ROI calculations as well as
 challenges in attributing long-term results to a TA program.

 ROI calculations in isolation do not provide a holistic representation of all value generated by a
 TA program. Assessing the extent to which an output or outcome can be attributed to a specific
 input is dependent on the level of sophistication during data collection and can be impacted by
 the disconnect between a researching entity, TA implementer, and TA recipient. Inputs or
 measures of a program with nonmonetizable or intangible outcomes, such as teaching or
 learning, may often be excluded from a ROI, as collecting data for these factors can prove too
 costly (Kotamraju and Mettille III 2012). Rahman et al. (2024) describe observations regarding
 why government research funding, representing similar outputs and outcomes to TA programs, is
 not sufficiently evaluated with economic ROI calculations alone:

 1. Most ROI studies on research grants focus exclusively on the annual gain from
 previous investments.
 2. Educational research expenditures are known as “public goods” whose market
 value is, by concept, difficult to quantify.
 3. Considering the success of the retrospective study, there is almost no established
 formula to measure educational research impact, and those that exist use a variety
 of non-identical attributes. (Rahman et al. 2024, 101)
 One way to address the challenges emphasized in ROI frameworks is to design projects that
 embed planning for ROI evaluation from the onset. By incorporating the intent to calculate ROI
 as a part of program design, the program team must perform a baseline assessment to generate a
 value upon which to compare results. This also allows the team to identify SMART metrics,
 define proxies for nonmonetary values, determine data collection methods and points necessary,
 and collect representative data. Evaluating ROI after a project is completed limits the ability to
 collect data, leading to increased difficulty in monetizing outputs and outcomes, which
 ultimately reduces the usefulness of the ROI framework (Rahman et al. 2024).

 Another challenge that arises during ROI evaluation is the bias that may occur depending on the
 parties involved in the assessment. Bias may arise when the practitioner is the evaluator of their
 own work, or where a third party evaluates a program without full context. Practitioners who are
 not fully invested or involved in their program may not be able to fully understand the issues
 they are attempting to address (Baum et al. 2010). In one case, a health care TA program trained
 their clinicians to assess ROI, and it was found that clinicians were incentivized to demonstrate a
 positive performance through the ROI result (Crawley-Stout et al. 2016). Approaches to mitigate
 the challenge of evaluation bias include involving stakeholders in the design of the evaluation
 and including a third-party evaluator during the program development stage.

 22

 Calculating ROI shortly after completing a TA program may not sufficiently consider the long-
 term goals and values of TA; however, calculating a long-term ROI makes attributing outcomes
 to a TA program challenging. Analyzing ROI throughout a program and shortly after its
 completion limits the number of outside factors and entities that may influence measured
 outcomes. Higher-level data, required for comprehensive ROI calculation, are more likely for
 medium and long-term outcomes. Collecting data to show chain of impact is one method to
 addressing this challenge (Dadd 2016). ETIPP’s TA logic model in Appendix E reflects this
 relationship, as the measurable outcomes required for a ROI analysis are not expected to occur
 until 3-5+ years after the TA work concludes.

 ROI frameworks have specific limitations as well. The Kirkpatrick framework has received
 criticism for not considering a monetized ROI value, other potentially influencing factors that
 could impact attribution of results, or nonmonetizable outputs and outcomes (Dadd 2016).
 Phillips’ framework was developed to assess economic evaluations in ROI in response to
 industry demands identifying this gap in the Kirkpatrick framework. Although Phillips’
 framework is an accepted approach and relatively straightforward to implement with planning
 and design, it does not consider nonmonetizable outputs and outcomes, many of the primary
 outputs and outcomes from TA programs. The abdi framework, while incorporating
 nonmonetizable outputs and outcomes and adding a step to engage stakeholders, does not
 emphasize detailed planning as recommended in SROI and PSROI before program
 implementation.

 SROI was designed to address some of the gaps in considering nonmonetizable outcomes and
 planning efforts but is dependent on thorough and robust data collection and monitoring. The
 SROI framework uses factors that are open to interpretation and biases, such as applying
 financial values to social benefits, and has received criticism on the reproducibility of results,
 data integrity, and comparability of approaches across programs or organizations (Arvidson et al.
 2010; New Philanthropy Capital 2010; Gair 2009). PSROI is also highly context dependent and
 designed to be adapted for each use case, also raising criticisms for reproducibility, integrity, and
 comparability (Arvidson et al. 2010).

 Despite these challenges and limitations, for some TA programs the costs associated with
 rigorous ROI assessments may be warranted and could ultimately lend lessons for other
 programs.

 5 Potential Approach for Calculating ROI in TA
 Programs
 The NLR team drew from existing ROI frameworks and related examples to develop an
 approach potentially applicable to energy TA programs for collecting and analyzing financial
 impacts through ROI. The NLR team recognizes that TA programs often produce intangible and
 nonmonetary outputs and outcomes, require longer timelines for achieving those outputs and
 outcomes, and involve nuanced outputs and outcomes that may not be fully represented in a
 single ROI value. Still, ROI may be a reasonable complement to other forms of evaluation for
 some programs, and the NLR team proposes the following framework for use in those contexts.

 This approach leverages the foundation of the five levels applied in Phillips’ framework, the
 SROI forecast method to inform program development through baseline assessments and
 planning data collection strategies before implementation, and the abdi framework’s application
 of proxies for intangible outputs and outcomes. Process steps for implementing this process are
 detailed in Figure 8, though implementation of these steps will vary depending on program goals
 and structure. Financial analysis is the core foundation of these frameworks, which are accepted
 across industries to measure ROI. These frameworks allow for the incorporation of nuanced and
 intangible outputs and outcomes that result from TA programs. This approach has been applied,
 as possible, to Communities LEAP, as described in Section 6.

 6.2.2 Limitations of Applying ROI Methodology to the City of Duluth
 The approach detailed in Section 5 was informed by a literature review of ROI frameworks and
 case studies and recommends incorporating ROI evaluation and data collection into program
 design. Because Communities LEAP was not designed to collect the requisite data and the
 application of the ROI calculation was made after program completion, assumptions were made
 to conduct this valuation, which is incomplete. This case study should instead be considered an
 example of procedure and possibilities rather than a precise measurement of value of
 Communities LEAP, Duluth’s success, or of energy planning TA overall.

 Key limitations to this analysis include:

 • The program costs used for the ROI calculation do not include the cost to the local partners
 to participate in the TA program nor the cost of implementing TA findings—namely, energy
 efficiency and EV charging station installation.
 • Outcomes resulting from TA findings, such as energy efficiency retrofits or increased
 adoption of PEVs, are prospective estimates, and actual uptake will likely differ.
 • The 25% uptake assumption is not specific to Communities LEAP and may be significantly
 different in practice. Quantifying actual uptake would require additional resources for long-
 term data collection.
 • The prospective ROI blends the community-based program costs and benefits of additional
 secured funding with household accrued savings from energy efficiency and PEV adoption.
 • The ROI value is reported as a one-time value in 2030. Estimating benefits as annual values
 in 2030 excludes ramp rates or lifetime benefits of either energy efficiency or PEVs.
 • The estimated benefits do not include the time value of money, or savings that occur from
 accelerating the adoption timeline for energy efficiency and EV charging station installation.
 • The PEV analysis had limited available baseline data.
 • PEV savings use proxy data for the transportation analysis provided by the TA. Inclusion of
 EV charging station costs and benefits would present a more complete ROI.

 ROI analyses require extensive data, are time consuming, and must be incorporated int a
 program’s design. ROI was not a planned metric for Communities LEAP, so this preliminary
 attempt offers an example of how it could be used to evaluate TA. Notably, the 630% ROI value
 only includes direct TA costs, and prospective benefits from additional investment in energy
 efficiency and EV charging installation would require significant additional funding. Further, the
 ROI should not be interpreted as causational—for example, that a $500,000 TA investment will
 result in energy efficient retrofits equaling $2 million in savings. Rather, a TA investment can
 support a community to develop plans to achieve realized energy savings long term. The ROI of
 67%, based on securing additional funding, does not include the variety of outcomes from energy
 planning TA, which could vary significantly across communities.

 34

 7 Conclusion
 ROI can be a useful metric for understanding the impact of a program, but it is not a complete
 representation of value nor is it designed to be applicable to all contexts and projects. This study
 found it can be adapted and planned to incorporate in TA programs in certain circumstances. It is
 a time-consuming analysis requiring in-depth data collection and purposeful planning, so a
 recommended practice is to embed it into program design and calculate it selectively. For
 example, Phillips and Phillips (2009) recommend calculating ROI for 5%–10% of programs and
 capping the cost of implementing ROI at 3%–5% of a project budget. Figure 8, included in
 Section 5, outlines the proposed approach for considering ROI in TA.

 All ROI frameworks reviewed for this study emphasize the importance of considering ROI when
 designing a project, including indicators, objectives, control groups, and time frames. A
 guideline to support successful implementation is to structure inputs, outputs, and outcomes as
 SMART and ensure data collection aligns with the measurements needed (Katz 2015). All costs
 should also be tracked throughout the program, including the cost of the ROI evaluation, to
 appropriately represent the project investment (Phillips 2007). When data are identified to
 include in a ROI calculation, data must be collectible and attributable to program participation,
 and collected throughout the planning, implementation, and evaluation process to measure
 progress. The most accurate ROI value would quantify as many outputs as possible, though
 intangible outputs and outcomes may not always be quantifiable, and reporting intangibles
 without a monetary value may be necessary. Additionally, because data collection may not be
 possible long-term and limited research has been done on the uptake rate of energy planning, an
 area for future research could be to comprehensively evaluate uptake rates resulting from a range
 of energy TA programs to better account for potential impact. Even when a program impact can
 be monetized, demonstrating evidence of attribution is important to strengthen the accuracy of
 ROI results. As discussed, Phillips (2007) recommends isolating data points to be measured
 using techniques such as control groups, participant estimates of improvement, or direct
 customer feedback. Even with these approaches, isolating the effect of a TA program is
 challenging. Data to consider in the calculation may not be readily available, no two
 communities are alike, and the length of time required for anticipated outputs and outcomes
 often extends beyond the period of program engagement.

 The Communities LEAP pilot phase was not designed with the intent to calculate ROI, and
 quantifiable outputs and outcomes or associated proxies were not specifically collected for use in
 monetary analysis. Long-term TA outcomes, such as savings from efficiency measures, are more
 easily monetizable compared to short-term outcomes, such as knowledge transfer or continuation
 of planning phases. However, attribution of long-term outcomes is more difficult to quantify, and
 this ROI analysis occurred before long-term outcomes can be realized. Satisfaction and
 perception are primary success indicators of Communities LEAP, but these align most
 closely with level 1 data—data necessary but insufficient for a complete ROI analysis.

 The value of Communities LEAP is primarily reflected through the program’s intangible
 outcomes and outputs, benefits highlighted by the abdi framework as equally important to
 consider in a program’s value, even if not monetized. Communities LEAP is refining its
 evaluation framework to clearly reflect impact areas and correlated data collection strategies, and

 35

 although the new framework is not an implementation of the ROI approach introduced in this
 report, data collected have the potential to inform more robust monetary analyses in the future.

 The approach detailed in Section 5 could be considered for future ROI evaluation of TA
 programs to systematize TA program impact analysis. The application of this approach has the
 potential to more clearly quantify tangible and intangible outputs if considered from the start,
 even if outputs and outcomes from TA remain nonmonetizable. A lack of a monetizable value
 does not inherently mean a program lacks value—instead, it is an opportunity to define and
 represent values that matter most to all stakeholders and recipients of a program.

 Appendix A. Detailed Approach
 The proposed approach shown in Figure A-1 is a step-by-step process that incorporates the
 designation of data levels used to inform the focus of each phase of data collection. The data
 levels identified are similar to those used by abdi and Phillips; level 5 data are nonmonetized
 impact outcomes, and level 6 is the return on investment (ROI) analyses. This approach does not
 argue against abdi’s designation of nonmonetized impact outcomes but rather considers that data
 collection for both monetizable and nonmonetizable outputs and outcomes for energy planning
 TA are measured concurrently. The planning phase of this approach designates outputs and
 outcomes that cannot be monetized in advance, enabling the measurement of those benefits
 before a monetized ROI can be calculated.

 The approach emphasizes the project planning phase, using steps from the Social Return on
 Investment (SROI) framework such as establishing scope, identifying stakeholders, and mapping
 outcomes. Establishing scope includes, as described in the SROI framework, determining what is
 being measured, what outputs and outcomes they align to, and how these data are collected. The
 scope designated is used to drive decisions throughout the ROI process. A case study analyzed in
 Appendix E depicts methods that could be used to create a foundation for ROI analysis.

 Following data collection, after implementation of the program, evaluators isolate program
 effects and validate results. The use of semistructured interviews to validate results was
 incorporated from Participatory Social Return on Investment (PSROI) step 5. Participant attitude
 is an indicator of TA programs, and although challenging to monetize, survey and interview-
 based data can inform TA success and support outcome attribution. The Jayani and Yamazaki
 case study (Section 3.3.2) is one example in how participant confidence and self-rated
 improvement can account for attribution and be incorporated into a monetary ROI.

 The use of proxies to monetize outputs and outcomes, as described in the abdi framework, is
 necessary to quantify some outputs and outcomes resulting from TA. Appendix C lists peer-
 reviewed data that could be used in monetizing results. This list is not a comprehensive review of
 all data that could be used to monetize outputs and outcomes but includes some data that were
 considered in this report and represents the existence of this data for other evaluators. Proxies for
 monetization are identified during planning. Where possible, practitioners should account for the
 time value of costs and benefits, such as through inclusion of opportunity costs, discount rates,
 and accelerated timelines resulting from TA.

 After a ROI analysis is complete, both monetized and nonmonetized outputs and outcomes are
 reported. Outputs and outcomes from TA include results such as building networks and
 transferring knowledge, the value of which is beyond what can be represented through monetary
 value (Rahman et al. 2024). Resulting successes from these outputs and outcomes can be
 communicated by tracking event participation and attendance, contacts shared, reports published,
 and survey results that indicate learnings. These types of results are then incorporated into the
 ROI narrative when a TA program is evaluated.

 Appendix B. Supplementary ROI Principles
 The following are supplementary considerations for performing ROI calculations. This report
 outlines the time- and resource-intensive nature of calculating ROI, and these principles may
 help future evaluators.

 The Phillips framework is guided by the following 12 implementation principles (Phillips and
   Phillips 2009, 15):

 • When conducting a higher-level evaluation, collect data at lower levels.
 • When planning a higher level evaluation, the previous level of evaluation is not required to
 be comprehensive.
 • When collecting and analyzing data, use only the most credible sources.
 • When analyzing data, select the most conservative alternatives for calculations.
 • Use at least one method to isolate the effects of the program or project.
 • If no improvement data are available for a population or from a specific source, assume that
 no improvement has occurred.
 • Adjust estimates of improvements for the potential error of the estimates.
 • Avoid use of extreme data items and unsupported claims when calculating ROI calculations.
 • Use only the first year of annual benefits in the ROI analysis of short-term solutions.
 • Fully load all costs of the solution, project, or program when analyzing ROI.
 • Intangible measures are defined as measures that are purposely not converted to monetary
 values.
 • Communicate the results of the ROI Methodology to all key stakeholders.
 The SROI framework is based on seven principles that underpin how SROI should be applied.
 SROI principles include (Nicholls et al. 2012):

 1. Involve stakeholders.
 2. Understand what changes.
 3. Value the things that matter.
 4. Only include what is material.
 5. Do not over-claim.
 6. Be transparent.
 7. Verify the result.
 According to SROI guidelines, a SROI report should be consistent, concise, and transparent. It
 should include information about the organization, including stakeholders and activities, a scope
 of the analysis, executive summary, methods of data collection, and assumptions and limitations.
 Supporting information may include an impact map, case studies, a sensitivity analysis, and
 rationale for decision-making, such as an audit trail to demonstrate justification for decisions. A
 typical report could be broken into five sections:

 1. Scope and stakeholders
 2. Outcomes and evidence
 3. Impact
 4. Social return calculation
 5. Audit trail.

 D.1 Supporting Data for Plug-In Vehicle Calculation Assumptions
 To monetize benefits of the transportation analysis, the NLR team assumed the societal cost
 savings of using plug-in vehicles (PEVs) would be a financially positive outcome. On a national
 scale, the per-vehicle cost savings of PEVs “is more than 2.5 times greater than the combined
 capital investment in charging infrastructure and grid upgrades” (Wood et al. 2024). Although
 valuing the installation of PEV chargers would more directly reflect outcome of the TA analysis,
 the entities bearing costs and receiving benefits are not known, and there is no standard
 methodology to value the benefits of PEV chargers. Owners of PEVs benefit from the cost
 savings and generally bear purchase and maintenance costs, making PEVs an easier proxy for
 monetizing the value of the TA analysis. Therefore, benefits associated with household PEV
 adoption were used in ROI calculations. The following assumptions were made to monetize
 adoption of PEVs and associated savings:

 51

 • The baseline year for the PEV study was 2022. No data are available on growth in PEVs
 between the time of the PEV study and the time of the ROI analysis.
 • Duluth has the same number of PEVs per capita in 2022 as the state of Minnesota.
 • PEV lifetime financial savings are assumed to be evenly distributed across a vehicle’s life.
 • The average lifetime of PEVs is 15 years.
 • All PEVs in Duluth are assumed to remain in Duluth, excluding impacts of out-migration.
 • Estimated annual savings apply to the percentage of PEVs expected in 2030, and this
 analysis does not account for the period of growth needed to achieve this ratio.
 • The capital investment required for building charging infrastructure and investing in PEVs is
 not considered.
 • Of the growth, and therefore the cost savings, of PEVs, 25% is directly attributable to
 outcomes of the transportation analyses provided by the TA.
 • Although cost savings of PEVs are actualized as individual benefits, savings are considered
 in aggregate as a program benefit in this analysis.
 • The cost savings of PEVs could not be realized without the installation of charging stations
 described in the transportation analysis. Insufficient data are available to directly monetize
 charging station impact.
 Online databases were used to set the baseline of PEVs in Duluth in 2022. First, the team
 converted Minnesota’s total PEVs in 2022 (11,900) to a per-capita value (0.0021) (U.S.
 Department of Energy n.d.-a and World Population Review 2025). This per-capita value was
 then applied to the city of Duluth’s population to estimate the total number of PEVs in Duluth
 before the TA program, estimated at 200 PEVs.

 • The estimated increased adoption of PEVs is based on theoretical outcomes.

 The methods used to monetize outcomes of the transportation analysis reflect the challenges and

 quantified and monetized but are also limited as a direct representation of the intended outcome.

 ROI framework through using databases and proxies to monetize TA outcomes.

 Appendix E. Application of the Theory of Change to
 the Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project
 To provide a complementary example using some of the concepts explored in this report, this
 appendix describes the use of the theory of change for the DOE Energy Technology Innovation
 Partnership Project (ETIPP). ETIPP is an DOE-funded community-led TA program providing
 tailored support to coastal, remote, and island communities. Through this project, teams of
 community stakeholders are partnered with national laboratory technical experts and Regional
 Partner organizations to help address their specific energy challenges. Competitively selected
 communities work through one of two support tracks: developing a Strategic Energy Plan or
 performing in-depth analysis through a Deep Dive TA Project. This program began in 2021,
 offering 5 years of lessons learned, metrics available over time, and methodologies applied to
 measure success.

 Although initial indicators gathered for ETIPP were valuable in understanding outputs from the
 work, the TA team was interested in understanding outputs and outcomes that better represented
 the value of the program. The TA team developed a logic model that represented the program
 theory and structure, activities, inputs, outputs, and short-, mid-, and long-term outcomes that
 would represent value to stakeholders (Figure E-1). The logic model assisted ETIPP in
 identifying key performance indicators that were needed to collect and evaluate whether each of
 these indicators was achieved and how those indicators informed the associated metrics.

 This logic model was developed considering lessons learned, feedback from community
 stakeholders that received support on the project, support from TA practitioners, and funders to
 ensure agreement on achievable outputs and outcomes. The interviews included questions on the
 program’s process, experience, and satisfaction. Although this analysis of ETIPP does not reflect
 a monetary return, the logic model displays direct outputs and outcomes that can be expected
 when communities receive TA through ETIPP and are representative of the outputs and
 outcomes practitioners, recipients, and funders consider a success. Additionally, the logic model
 reflects the time-based challenge of evaluating TA, as long-term outcomes are not realized until
 at least 5 years after TA (Figure E-1).

 Overall, this detailed implementation plan and the comprehensive approach to indicator

 attribution for long-term outcomes rather than estimating or assuming attribution after the fact.

 indicators such as satisfaction, and incorporating quantitative representations of qualitative

 meetings, the number of knowledge or capacity-building events hosted, and the number of
 attendees into quarterly data tracked by regional partners.
