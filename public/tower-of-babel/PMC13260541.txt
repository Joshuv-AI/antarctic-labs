Numerical investigation of thermal performance in unsteady Casson hybrid nanofluid flow across rotating disks under variable Darcy resistance and Joule heating

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe findings of this study have significant implications for a wide range of engineering applications that include cooling of rotating disk surfaces, the optimization of chemical reactors, and the design of filtration systems, where the synergistic use of variable porous media and hybrid nanofluids offers a pathway to enhanced performance. Keeping these substantial applications in perspective, the present study examines the optimization of thermal transport in an unsteady Casson hybrid nanofluid flowing through a variable porous medium between two rotating disks. The flow is influenced by inertial effects, Joule heating, viscous dissipation, and time-dependent heat generation and absorption effects. The modeled equations are solved through the Homotopy Analysis Method (HAM) in dimensionless form. It is deduced in this investigation that axial velocity augments with growth in stretching parameter of the lower disk , Reynolds number and suction factor while declines with augmentation in stretching parameter of the upper disk . Radial velocity augments on the interval and it declines on the range with growth in the stretching parameter of the lower disk . Tangential velocity augments with growth in suction parameter and angular velocity at the lower disk , while it declines with growth in Reynolds numbe r. Thermal profiles augment with growth in Eckert number , heat source parameter , suction parameter r and stretching parameter of lower and upper disks . A close agreement is observed between the HAM solutions and the numerical Runge-Kutta shooting results, confirming the accuracy, reliability, and validity of the HAM-based solutions. For variations in Reynolds number in the range the percentage error in between HAM and Runge-Kutta shooting results are 0.0%, 2.4 × 10− 5%, 2.2 × 10− 5% and 1.5 × 10− 5%. Introduction

Nanofluid flow explains one of the most significant advancements in fluid thermal engineering over the past two decades, involving the suspension of nanometer-sized particles (typically 1–100 nm) in conventional base fluids. These nanoparticles fundamentally alter the thermophysical properties of the base fluid. The occurrence of these nanoparticles enhances thermal conductivity as revealed by Choi [1] through several mechanisms, like the formation of liquid layers at the particle-fluid interface and particle clustering, which collectively contribute to superior heat transfer characteristics compared to conventional fluids. Rathore et al. [2] discussed the impression of nanoparticles on nanofluid flow on a curved surface. Nanofluid flow behavior is governed by complex interactions between nanoparticles and the base fluid. The enhanced thermal properties of nanofluids make them particularly valuable in applications requiring efficient heat dissipation, including electronic cooling systems, automotive radiators, heat exchangers, and solar energy collectors [3]. Rafique et al. [4] examined computationally magnetized nanofluid flow on an inclined cylinder with variable thermal conductance. Hybrid nanofluid flow characterizes an evolutionary advancement in thermal fluid science, wherein two or more distinct types of nanoparticles are simultaneously suspended in a base fluid to achieve synergistic improvements in thermophysical properties beyond those attainable with single-nanoparticle nanofluids. This innovative class of heat transfer fluids combines materials with complementary characteristics, such as metallic nanoparticles with excellent thermal conductivity alongside metal oxide nanoparticles with superior chemical stability, or carbon-based nano-materials with unique structural properties alongside ceramic particles with high temperature resistance [5]. Choudhary et al. [6] studied magnetohydrodynamics hybrid nanofluid flow on a nonlinear stretchable and permeable surface with the effects of gyrotactic microorganisms. Khan et al. [7] discussed the impressions machine learning approach on the thermal efficacy of magnetohydrodynamics radiative hybrid nanoliquid flow on a surface with heat generation effects. The flow behavior of hybrid nanofluids exhibits greater complexity than conventional nanofluids due to interactions between different nanoparticle types, which can include synergistic thermal enhancement, modified particle-particle interactions, and altered flow dynamics. The selection of appropriate nanoparticle combinations, such as alumina-copper, silver-graphene, or carbon nanotube-iron oxide hybrids, depends on the specific application requirements and desired thermophysical properties. Rafique et al. [8] discussed the production of entropy and fluid flow for a hybrid nanofluid on a nonlinear radiative sheet with slip constraints.

Casson fluid flow is a distinct class of non-Newtonian fluid behavior that has garnered substantial attention in both theoretical fluid mechanics and practical industrial applications due to its unique rheological characteristics. Originally developed to model the flow of printing ink containing pigment particles in a linseed oil-varnish vehicle. Algehyne et al. [9] discussed the thermal distribution for magnetized Casson nanofluid flow on an exponentially extendable sheet with the effects of chemical reactivity. The Casson constitutive equation has since been successfully applied to describe the flow behavior of numerous complex fluids, including blood, chocolate melts, tomato paste, honey, various food products, drilling muds, and polymer solutions [10]. Kavya et al. [11] studied mass and thermal transference forecasting for nanofluid flow on a surface by implementing the machine learning approach. Islam et al. [12] examined a theoretical overview and modelling for Casson fluid flow on a stretchable surface with varying viscosity and boundary constraints of a sinusoidal pattern. The fundamental distinction of Casson fluids lies in their yield stress behavior, meaning they require a finite applied stress, known as the yield stress, to initiate flow; below this critical stress threshold, the fluid maintains a rigid, solid-like structure, while above it, the material begins to deform and flow with a characteristic shear-thinning behaviour. Abbas et al. [13] studied magnetohydrodynamic Casson fluid flow with fractional order on a surface with the effects of thermal radiations. Jayaprakash et al. [14] studied the thermal transference for Casson fluid flow on an aligned flat surface with leading-edge slip boundary constraints. This yield stress phenomenon arises from the formation of internal structural networks or particle aggregates that resist deformation until sufficient external force disrupts these interactions, allowing relative motion between fluid elements. In biomedical engineering, Casson fluid models have proven particularly valuable for hemodynamic studies, as human blood exhibits pronounced yield stress behavior at low shear rates due to red blood cell aggregation and rouleaux formation, enabling more accurate predictions of blood flow through arteries, capillaries, and medical devices [15]. Industrial applications of Casson fluid flow analysis span diverse sectors, including food processing, where understanding chocolate flow during molding and coating operations ensures product quality, petroleum engineering, where drilling mud rheology affects wellbore stability and cuttings transport, and chemical manufacturing, where polymer solution behavior influences processing efficiency. Mahmood et al. [16] discussed the thermal transference process for Casson fluid flow on a radiative movable surface with convective and chemically reactive effects.

Fluid flow through variable porous media represents a sophisticated and highly relevant area of fluid mechanics that addresses the complex reality of natural and engineered porous systems where permeability, porosity, and other structural properties exhibit spatial or temporal variations rather than remaining uniform throughout the medium. Lone et al. [17] studied magnetized fluid flow through two extendable surfaces with variable porosity effects on the surface. Unlike idealized homogeneous porous media models that assume constant properties throughout the domain, variable porous media acknowledge that real-world porous structures, including geological formations, biological tissues, industrial filters, and composite materials [18]. Choudhary et al. [19] examined the shape analysis of aluminium oxide nanoparticles on water flow over a permeable surface using the impressions of slip constraints. The study by Khan et al. [20] quantitatively examined Maxwell fluid behavior with dual diffusion effects within a varying permeability gap between two disks, highlighting the influence of porous variations on flow characteristics. This spatial variability fundamentally alters flow patterns, creating preferential pathways where permeability is higher while inducing stagnation or reduced velocity in low-permeability regions, leading to phenomena such as channelling, dispersion, and non-uniform residence time distributions that critically influence transport processes Alqarni et al. [21]. Waseem et al. [22] studied statistically and mathematically the Casson fluid flow with effects of activation energy on an exponentially elongating surface with Darcy regime. In hydrogeology, understanding variable porous media flow is essential for predicting contaminant transport in aquifers with heterogeneous geological structures, designing effective groundwater remediation strategies, and managing water. Petroleum reservoir engineering similarly relies heavily on variable porosity and permeability models to simulate oil and gas recovery from naturally heterogeneous formations, optimize well placement, and design enhanced oil recovery techniques that account for preferential flow paths and bypassed oil zones. Mumtaz et al. [23] investigated nanofluid flow with thermo-rheological characteristics through a variable porous space between two aligned extendable surfaces, examining how temperature-dependent viscosity and thermal conductivity influence velocity and heat transfer profiles. Industrial applications extend to designing advanced filtration systems with graded porosity for improved particle capture efficiency, optimizing catalytic converters where flow distribution through variable porous substrates affects conversion rates, and developing biomedical scaffolds with controlled pore architectures to guide tissue growth and nutrient transport [24].

Unsteady fluid flow, also commonly known as transient or time-dependent flow, represents a fundamental class of fluid motion wherein the velocity, pressure, density, and other flow properties at any given point in the flow field exhibit temporal variations, distinguishing it fundamentally from steady-state flows where conditions remain invariant with time. This time-dependent behavior arises from a multitude of physical mechanisms, including time-varying boundary conditions like oscillating surfaces or moving boundaries. Mehmood and Rehman [25] investigated the accurate prediction of nonlinear convective fluid flow over a surface by employing a machine learning framework, demonstrating its effectiveness in capturing complex flow behavior and enhancing predictive accuracy compared to traditional numerical techniques. Unsteady pressure gradients imposed by external forcing, flow instabilities that develop spontaneously even under constant boundary conditions, or the inherent transient nature of starting and stopping processes in engineering systems, as [26]. Engineering applications involving unsteady flow are equally pervasive, encompassing the start-up and shutdown of pumps and turbines, aerodynamic response of aircraft wings to gusts or manoeuvring, flow-induced vibrations in heat exchanger tubes, and offshore structures [27–30]. In nature, unsteady flows are ubiquitous and include phenomena such as pulsatile blood flow through the circulatory system driven by cardiac contractions, tidal flows in coastal regions responding to gravitational forcing, atmospheric flows influenced by diurnal heating cycles, and the complex wake dynamics behind bluff bodies characterized by periodic vortex shedding. Bilal et al. [31] studied time-dependent nanofluid flow on a Riga surface with experimental validation using micro-rotational effects on the flow system. Heat transfer in unsteady flows exhibits corresponding temporal variations, with thermal layers at the borderline developing and responding to changing flow conditions, leading to time-varying Nusselt numbers and thermal transport rates [32]. These flows have critical implications in applications ranging from electronic cooling during transient power loads to thermal management of pulsed power systems and biomedical therapies involving time-dependent heating protocols. Mahmood et al. [33] discussed magnetized time-based nanofluid flow with nonlinear radiative effects on a surface with the use of slip constraints. Jubair et al. [34] examined a computational solution for nanofluid flow with the impression of chemical reactions on an inclined cylinder/plate. Mahmood et al. [35] studied the comparative analysis of magnetized stagnant point flow for nanofluid flow on a stretchable sheet with dissipative effects. Many such works can be seen in Refs [36–40]. The analysis of unsteady flows requires careful consideration of dimensionless parameters, which characterize the relative importance of local acceleration to convective acceleration, and the reduced frequency, which quantifies the unsteadiness level in oscillatory flows relative to convective timescales.

Applications in industry

This research work has substantial industrial applications in diverse engineering fields where efficient heat transfer is essential. It is particularly relevant to the design and optimization of cooling systems in rotating machinery like turbines, compressors, and disk reactors, where enhanced thermal conductivity of hybrid nanofluids improves heat dissipation and system reliability. In energy systems, including thermal and nuclear power plants as well as magnetohydrodynamic generators, the study aids in managing thermal loads by incorporating Joule heating effects and flow through porous structures modeled via Darcy resistance. The findings are also applicable in biomedical engineering, especially in bio-reactors and drug delivery systems, where the Casson fluid model represents blood-like behavior. Furthermore, the research supports advancements in electronics cooling, where compact and high-performance devices require efficient thermal regulation, and in chemical and process industries involving rotating disk reactors and porous catalytic beds for improved reaction rates and temperature control. Additional applications extend to metallurgical processes, like casting and metal forming under electromagnetic heating, and to filtration and oil recovery systems, where understanding flow through porous media is crucial. Solution method

To solve Eqs. (9–11) using the boundary conditions given in Eq. (12), we shall use the Homotopy Analysis Method (HAM). It is a powerful and versatile semi-analytical technique for solving nonlinear differential equations that has gained substantial recognition in the applied mathematics and engineering communities since its introduction by Liao [44, 45]. This method is fundamentally rooted in the concept of homotopy from topology, which describes a continuous deformation between two functions, and it provides a sophisticated framework for transforming a complicated nonlinear problem into an infinite series of linear sub-problems that can be solved systematically. One of the most distinctive and powerful features distinguishing HAM from other perturbation-based or series expansion methods is its ability to provide great flexibility through the so-called convergence-control parameter, denoted as ħ, which offers a simple yet elegant way to adjust and control the convergence region and rate of the series solution. Unlike traditional perturbation methods that rely on the existence of small or large physical parameters, HAM remains valid regardless of the presence or magnitude of such parameters, making it applicable to strongly nonlinear problems where conventional perturbation techniques would fail. To satisfy the principle of mass conservation within the incompressible flow between the disks, this increased radial outflow near the lower disk is compensated by a stronger downward axial flow from the central region. Essentially, the more the lower disk stretches, the more fluid it demands to be drawn axially toward it from the gap to feed the radial ejection, thereby accelerating the axial velocity component. This intensified downward flow effectively short-circuits the radial flow in the upper region, drawing fluid downward before it can travel very far radially, thereby reducing the radial velocity component in the region .

Effects of suction parameter (S) on velocity

The impression of the section, parameter (S) on velocity is examined in Fig. (9). From this graphical view, it is revealed that with growth in the factor (S) there is an augmentation in . The observed augmentation in with an intensification in (S) is a direct consequence of the mass conservation principle and the enhanced removal of fluid mass from the flow domain inside the gap of two disks. Physically, strengthening the suction condition at the disk surfaces (typically by drawing fluid into the porous boundaries) creates a stronger sink effect. To replenish the fluid being extracted through the walls, the flow must accelerate in the axial direction, particularly in the core region between the disks. This increased axial velocity is a compensatory mechanism to satisfy continuity, ensuring that the volumetric flow rate lost to suction is balanced by a higher through-flow velocity. Furthermore, the presence of hybrid nanoparticles, which generally increase the fluid’s thermal conductivity and alter its momentum diffusivity, influences the boundary layer characteristics, but the primary driver for the axial velocity augmentation remains the intensified mass removal at the boundaries, which directly injects the axial pressure gradient and the resultant velocity field.

Effects of angular velocity at lower disk on velocity

The impression of angular velocity at the lower disk on tangential velocity is examined in Fig. (10). From this graphical view, it is revealed that with growth in the factor there is an augmentation in . The augmentation in with an intensification in is fundamentally attributed to the enhanced transmission of rotational kinetic energy from the lower disk boundary to the adjacent fluid layers. As the lower disk rotates faster, the no-slip condition injects fluid particles in immediate contact with the disk surface, which acquire a proportionally higher tangential momentum. This momentum is then transferred radially outward and axially upward through the inter-disk gap via the combined action of fluid viscosity and inertial effects, often referred to as the radial pumping. The presence of hybrid nanoparticles modifies the effective viscosity and momentum diffusivity of the base fluid, but the underlying mechanism remains governed by the rotating disk acting as a centrifugal fan; the higher the angular velocity of the lower disk , the greater the centrifugal force imparted on the fluid, resulting in a stronger tangential velocity field throughout the flow domain.

Effects of reynolds number Re on velocity

The impression of Reynolds number (Re) on tangential velocity is examined in Fig. (11). From this graphical view, it is revealed that with growth in the factor (Re) there is a reduction in . The observed reduction in with an escalation in (Re) is a physically consistent outcome of the changing dominance between inertial and viscous forces within the rotating flow system. As inertia intensifies, the radial pressure gradient driving the secondary flow becomes more prominent, causing the fluid to be ejected outward more aggressively near the rotating disks. To satisfy mass conservation, this augmented radial outflow necessitates a stronger axial inflow toward the disks from the gap. This enhanced axial convection transports low-tangential-momentum fluid from the upper disk downward into the mid-region and toward the rotating lower disk. Consequently, while the angular velocity of the disk remains constant, the increased Reynolds number promotes a more vigorous secondary circulation that effectively dilutes the rotational momentum, leading to a thinner tangential boundary layer and a net suppression of tangential velocity throughout the gap.

Conclusions

The present study examines the optimization of thermal transport in an unsteady Casson hybrid nanofluid flowing through a variable porous medium between two rotating disks. The flow is influenced by inertial effects, Joule heating, viscous dissipation, and time-dependent heat generation and absorption effects. The combined influence of these effects, together with thermal radiation, nanoparticle concentration gradients, and gyrotactic microorganism transport, creates a sophisticated coupled nonlinear system. The modeled equations have been solved through the Homotopy Analysis Method (HAM) in dimensionless form.
