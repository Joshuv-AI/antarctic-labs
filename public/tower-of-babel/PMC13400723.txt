Unique properties of light-induced changes of variable chlorophyll fluorescence in purified PSII core complexes of Thermosynechococcus vestitus
Authors: Ulrich Schreiber, Ivan Bobkov, Marc Nowaczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA new type of kinetic chlorophyll fluorometer (Klughammer et al. 2024, Photosynth Res 161:151–176) which enables measuring the changes of rel. fluorescence yield during application of saturating single-turnover 15µs flashes (ST-kinetics, STK) simultaneously with the PAM relaxation kinetics induced by the same flash, was applied on purified PSII core complexes (PSII CC) isolated from Thermosynechococcus vestitus (Lambertz et al. 2023, Biochim Biophys Acta 1864:148953). While the main text concentrates on purified PSII dimers, under Supplementary Materials also comparative measurements with purified monomers and heterodimers are presented. Freshly solubilized, non-preilluminated samples display extremely high ratios of maximal/minimal fluorescence yields up to 26, corresponding to Fv/Fm = 0.96. Both PAM and STK techniques reveal period-4 oscillations in presence of 2 µM dichlorobenzoquinone (DCBQ). Period-2 oscillations are observed in the PAM relaxation kinetics after relaxation of donor-side dependent quenching (DQ). Maximal fluorescence yields in STK responses are lowered with respect to the corresponding PAM responses by High Intensity Quenching (HIQ), consisting of DQ and carotenoid triplet quenching (TQ). The DQ observed in presence of 20µM DCMU lowers flash-induced Fv by about 30%, relaxing with a half-time of about 40µs, as revealed by double flash STK measurements. When a train of ST with 5s dark intervals is applied in the absence of artificial acceptors, up to ST#3 both STK and PAM measurements show about 30% quenching (DQ) with respect to maximal fluorescence yield (Fm). Suppression of DQ occurs in a train of additional ST, reflected in a multi-step further rise to Fm. This “terminal rise” is inhibited by 20 µM DCMU and by 2µM DCBQ, suggesting that redox-reactions involving reduced QB are involved in this phenomenon. It is proposed that double reduced QB is either protonated, so that after release of PQH2 the PSII acceptor side is inactivated, or reoxidized by the donor side (via cyclic PSII, PSII-CEF). Based on this rationale a simple scheme is presented to explain the phenomenon. A similar “terminal rise” is observed using the PAM technique upon illumination by strong continuous light. It is suggested that the “terminal rise” in PSII CC and the “thermal” I1-I2 phase in vivo may involve the same pathways of DQ suppression, the molecular steps of which remain to be clarified by further research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11120-026-01228-y.Keywords: PS II core complexes, PS II dimers, MULTI-COLOR-PAM II fluorometer, Single turnover flashes, PAM technique, STK technique, High Intensity Quenching (HIQ), Donor-side dependent quenching (DQ), Period-2 oscillations, Period-4 oscillations, Polyphasic fluorescence rise, PSII-CEF

Introduction

Photosystem II (PSII) is a large membrane protein complex normally embedded in the thylakoid membrane together with photosystem I (PSI), the cytochrome b6f complex and the ATP-ase. The main redox cofactors of PSII are coordinated by the central D1 and D2 subunits (Umena et al. 2011). Absorbed light energy is transferred via the antenna proteins CP43 and CP47 to the reaction center (RC) pigment molecules (commonly called “P680”) (for a review, see Müh and Zouni 2020). Excitation energy is transformed into chemically stabilized energy by charge separation at the RC, consisting in the reduction of the bound plastoquinone QA by the primary donor P680 via pheophytin, followed by a cascade of secondary reactions of charge stabilization at PSII acceptor and donor sides in the time range of ns to ms. QA− reduces the mobile plastoquinone QB in the so-called QB pocket at the D1 protein. P680+ is rapidly re-reduced by the adjacent tyrosine Z (YZ), which in turn is reduced by the oxygen-evolving complex (OEC) (Vinyard et al. 2013; Shevela et al. 2023). The catalytic cycle of the OEC involves the states S0-S4, with the index indicating the number of positive charges (Kok et al. 1970). H2O is split and molecular O2 released after the light-induced transition from S3 to S4, followed by the return to the S0 state in the dark.

PSII forms a homodimer with a molecular mass of ~ 500 kDa (Boekema et al. 1995), with each monomer composed of at least 20 protein subunits and numerous cofactors, including chlorophylls, quinones, carotenoids, lipids, bicarbonate, non-heme iron and the unique Mn4CaO5 cluster (Shen 2015; Cox et al. 2020; Zabret et al. 2021). When isolated from their natural surroundings in the thylakoid membrane, purified PSII core complexes (PSII CC) still have a fully functional OEC, but lack the natural secondary acceptor pool of free plastoquinone (PQ), so that artificial electron acceptors have to be added to investigate the properties of light induced O2 evolution in PSII CC. In presence of 1 mM dichloro-benzoquinone (DCBQ) plus 5 mM ferricyanide very high rates up to 6000 µmol O2 (mg Ch. h)−1 were observed in strong continuous light (Lambertz et al. 2023), demonstrating high integrity of the preparation, which with minor modifications was also used in the present fluorescence study.

The fluorescence properties of PSII CC are best studied in the absence of artificial electron acceptors, like DCBQ, as oxidized quinones are known to quench Chl fluorescence (Amesz and Fork 1967). Furthermore, measurements in the absence of artificial electron acceptors are of particular interest, because they allow to investigate a unique state of PSII CC, in which forward electron is blocked after full reduction of QB, but cyclic PSII (PSII-CEF) is still possible, in contrast to the situation in presence of DCMU, where reduction of QB and cyclic PSII are blocked. In PSII CC, this state can be reproducibly induced by a train of saturating flashes.

Considering that PSII CC preparations have been available for at least 25 years (see e.g. Dekker et al. 1988; Sugiura and Inoue 1999), it is astonishing that up to now no dedicated study on the fluorescence properties of PSII CC in the absence of artificial acceptors (and of DCMU) has been published. In retrospect it appears that this may be due to the fact that the fluorescence responses of PSII CC in the absence of artificial electron acceptors are extremely sensitive to preillumination (including by weak ambient light), strongly affecting the reproducibility of the responses.While under in vivo conditions, reliable measurements of Chl fluorescence are easy and can be readily carried out even by non-experts using standard equipment, for reliable and reproducible measurements on PSII CC some expertise and advanced fluorometric equipment are required.

Magyar et al. (2018) observed remarkable fluorescence changes of PSII CC isolated from Thermosynechococcus vulcanus in presence of DCMU (and absence of artificial acceptors) induced by a train of saturating single turnover flashes. In these experiments, the first flash increased fluorescence yield (F1) just to about 35% of the maximal fluorescence yield that is induced by the following 10–12 flashes. Interestingly, in order to observe a fluorescence increase beyond F1, the “half-waiting time” had to be sufficiently long (in the range of several hundred µs to 1–2 ms). Based on these and other data (i.e., Sipka et al. 2019, 2021), Garab and co-workers developed the idea that the multi-flash induced increase beyond F1 reflects “the gradual formation of light-adapted charge-separated states (PSIIL) with significantly increased stability of charges compared to the closed state of PSII (PSIIC) that is elicited by a single flash” (Garab et al. 2023). If this kind of basic heterogeneity of Fv were generally true, this would seriously question the validity of Fv/Fm as a proxy for the maximal photochemical quantum yield of PSII (Kitajima and Butler 1975; Björkman and Demmig 1987). Notably, Garab and co-workers not only questioned the validity of Fv/Fm in PSII CC, but generally in all photosynthetic organisms. Their findings and far-reaching conclusions provided the impetus for the detailed investigation of the fluorescence properties of PSII conducted in the present study.

Here, a new type of kinetic chlorophyll fluorometer (Klughammer et al. 2024) was employed, which enables measuring the changes of rel. fluorescence yield during application of saturating single-turnover 15µs flashes (ST-kinetics, STK) simultaneously with the PAM relaxation kinetics induced by the same flash. In this way very detailed information on electron transfer reactions at donor and acceptor sides of PSII CC can be obtained, even in the absence of artificial electron acceptors. In particular, the combination of PAM and STK techniques allows to differentiate between fluorescence changes caused by changes in the redox state of QA (control by photochemical quenching) or by changes in the S-states of the OEC (controlled by donor-side dependent quenching, DQ) (Klughammer et al. 2024). At the end of an STK recording photochemical quenching is always fully suppressed, so that fluorescence yield is controlled by non-photochemical quenching mechanisms only.

While it has been known for more than 50 years, that the maximal rel. fluorescence yield is not only quenched when PSII reaction centers are open, but also when PSII is closed and fluorescence is measured in the presence of the higher S-states S2 and S3 (Delosme 1971; Joliot and Joliot 1973; Schreiber and Neubauer 1987), up to now it has been a matter of debate, to what extent changes of DQ play a role in dark-light induction kinetics. Regarding the polyphasic rise kinetics upon onset of saturating continuous light (O-I1-I2-Fm, Neubauer and Schreiber 1987; or O-J-I-P, Strasser et al. 1995), there is general consensus that the initial photochemical phase, O-I1 or O-J, reflects the reduction of QA, whereas the ensuing “thermal” phases (I1-I2-Fm or J-I-P) have been discussed controversially (for an extensive review see Stirbet and Govindjee 2012). While in intact cells, induction of the I1-I2 or J-I phases is known to parallel the reduction of the PQ-pool, the involved molecular mechanism of excitation quenching has not yet been definitely clarified.

By lacking the secondary PQ-pool, purified PSII CC in the absence of artificial electron acceptors are unique in becoming blocked in forward electron transport as soon as in a sequence of saturating single turnover flashes (ST) 3 electrons are accumulated in the primary QAQB acceptor pool. In the present study we took adavantage of this property to investigate the nature of the intriguing fluorescence quenching that persists after closure of PSII, but can be eliminated by further illumination.

Materials and methods

Photosynthetic material and sample preparation

The experiments were carried out at room temperature (22–24 °C) using freshly diluted, non-preilluminated samples of purified PSII core complexes, which were isolated from Thermosynechococcus vestitus, as outlined in Lambertz et al. (2023). Protein purification was strictly performed under dim green light conditions. In brief, thylakoid membranes were resuspended in 80 ml of buffer B (20 mM MES pH 6.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM CaCl2, 0.5 M mannitol) and homogenized in a Dounce homogenizer twice before adding 0.05% (w/v) of of n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (β -DDM) and homogenizing again.The suspension was centrifuged in a JA 25.50 (Beckman Coultiers) rotor at 22,000 x g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in buffer A (buffer B without addition of mannitol) supplied with 1.2% (w/v) of β -DDM and 0.5% (w/v) sodium cholate. The final chlorophyll concentration was adjusted to 1 mg Chl/ml. Extraction reaction was performed for 30 min in the dark at 20 °C in a rotary oven and the extract thereafter centrifuged in Beckmann Type 45 Ti Fixed-Angle Titanium Rotor at 102,800 rcf, 4 °C for 1 h. The resulting green supernatant was filtered before given onto pre-equilibrated (buffer B with addition of 0.03% (w/v) β -DDM) StrepTactin-XT4Flow affinity column. Bound protein was washed to prevent unspecific binding and then detergent exchange for 2,2-didecylpropane-1,3-bis-β-D-maltopyranoside (LMNG) was performed based on work of Hauer et al. (2015). The exchange was carried out by 1 h and 1 ml/min gradient wash (0-100%) of the sample against buffer B with addition of 0.05% (w/v) LMNG and subsequent gradient wash against detergent-free buffer B. The sample then was eluted with buffer B containing 0.05% (w/v) LMNG and 50 mM biotin. Using Amicon spin concentrators (Merck) with a 100 kDa cut-off peak fractions were washed with buffer B containing 0.002% (w/v) LMNG and concentrated to ~ 1 mg Chl/ml before applying onto ion-exchange column UnoQ6 (BioRad) to allow separation of monomeric and dimeric fractions of isolated PSII complexes. For successful separation gradient elution (10–53%) with elution buffer (buffer B with addition of 0.002% (w/v) LMNG and 100 mM MgSO4) was used and resulting peaks were separately washed and concentrated in Amicon spin concentrators with a 100 kDa cut-off. Final samples were aliquoted and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -70 °C before being sent on dry ice to the first author, where they were stored at -30 °C in the dark until use.

While all experiments presented in the main text were carried out with dimeric preparations, in Supplementary Materials S1, S6 and S7 also comparative data obtained with monomeric and heterodimeric preparations are presented.

For obtaining meaningful, reproducible data it proved essential that samples did not see any light before measuring the light-induced fluorescence responses. During sample preparation, filling of the cuvette and fluorescence measurements, the dark room was illuminated by a single 540 nm LED, the irradiance of which at the sample level did not exceed 0.001 µmol 540 nm quanta m−2s− 1. The Optical Unit of the Multi-Color-PAM II Fluorometer was covered by a black cloth. After dilution/solubilization of the frozen PSII CC aliquots in the reaction buffer (20 mM MES pH 6.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 30 mM CaCl2, 1 M betaine), in the absence of artificial electron acceptors the core complexes behave like “quantum counters”, with even minute amounts of light leading to slowly reversible increases of fluorescence yield.

Final chlorophyll concentration was 1 µg Chl/ml in all experiments. The samples (volume 1.3 ml) were continuously stirred within the 10 × 10 mm cuvette with the help of a small magnetic “flea”. During Fast Kinetics recordings stirring was automatically interrupted for a couple of seconds, controlled by the PamWin-4 user software.

For comparison with the ST-induced PAM relaxation kinetics of PSII CC samples, under Supplementary Materials S1 an analogous measurement with intact cells of Synechococcus leopoliensis (SAG 1402-1) is presented. Synechococcus leopoliensis (former Anacystis nidulans) was grown photoautotrophically in BG11 medium under artificial light (warm white LED) at 30 °C.

All data in the individual figures were obtained using fresh, non-preilluminated samples, presented without replicate measurements (i.e. N = 1).

Experimental set-up

Fluorescence was measured with a Multi-Color-PAM II Chlorophyll Fluorometer (Heinz Walz GmbH, Effeltrich, Germany). Details of the experimental set-up were outlined in preceding publications of Schreiber et al. (2012) and Klughammer et al. (2024). This measuring system provides an exceptionally powerful so-called “ST-lamp”, based on a chip-on-board array of 22 power-LEDs (440–450 nm), in addition to the standard Multi-Color-Emitter unit. While this lamp normally is applied for generating saturating single turnover flashes (ST) with widths in the µs time range, in the present study it is also used for generating multiple turnover pulses of extremely strong blue light (up to 39000 µmol m−2s− 1) with a width of 600ms. Such high quantum flux intensities are required for the separation of photochemical and thermal phases in the polyphasic rise kinetics of PSII CC, due to the lack of a light harvesting antenna system.

Rationale of combined PAM and ST-kinetics measurements

The rationale of combined PAM and ST-kinetics (STK) measurements already was explained in some detail in Supplementary Materials S3 and S5 of Klughammer et al. (2024). In brief, with the Multi-Color-PAM II fluorometer it is possible to carry out simultaneous STK and PAM measurements by triggering a saturating ST that is generated by the new, exceptionally powerful ST-lamp at a defined point of time in a PAM Relaxation Kinetics recording (“embedded STK”). In this way, the time dependent effect of an ST generated by the ST-lamp can be assessed with the help of repetitively applied, weak PAM ML pulses. This is similar, but more informative, than the classical pump-and-probe approach, where single weak probe flashes are applied to determine the fluorescence yield at defined dark-times following a pump flash. Using the repetitively applied 1µs PAM ML pulses, the whole time course of the relaxation kinetics is obtained in one run with a single measurement.

In the present study, a saturating 15µs ST was routinely triggered simultaneously with the start of a PAM Relaxation Kinetics recording (i.e. at t = 0) for assessment of the QA− reoxidation kinetics that follow ST-induced QA reduction. The effective PSII absorption cross-section of PSII CC is relatively small, due to the lack of a light-harvesting antenna system. Therefore, ST flashes provided by the standard PAM Emitter Unit are not strong enough to saturate QA reduction in PSII CC, even at their maximal width of 50µs. 15µs flashes of the separate ST-lamp are saturating, when applied at maximal intensity, which amounts to 1.3 mol 440 nm quanta m−2s− 1.

The two separate fluorescence responses (PAM Relaxation Kinetics and STK) can be quantitatively compared by normalizing the initial fluorescence yields (i.e. before ST-induced formation of QA−). This normalization has proven particularly important to assess the extent of non-photochemical quenching caused by the ST. The ST-induced increase of fluorescence yield measured during the ST (in the present study at the end of 15µs ST) generally is substantially lower than that assessed after some time delay by weak pulses of PAM measuring light, ML. This lowering of fluorescence yield is due to so-called High Intensity Quenching, HIQ, which has been shown to consist of carotenoid triplet quenching, TQ, and donor-side dependent quenching, DQ, both of which develop during ST-application (Klughammer et al. 2024) and relax after the ST, displaying different relaxation kinetics. In PSII CC, TQ is distinctly smaller than in whole cells, because of the relatively small absorption cross section of the core complexes, which limits the exciton density generated by the ST. Furthermore, ST-induced TQ relaxes in air within a few µs, whereas the relaxation of DQ, the rate of which depends on the S-state, displays half-times in the range of tens of µs to ms.

Principle of flash-profile correction

The principle of ST-profile correction was outlined in some detail under Materials and methods of Klughammer et al. (2024) in the section on “ST flash properties”. In the context of the present study this correction is particularly important with respect to reliable determination of the initial fluorescence yield, Fi, of the measured STK. When measured with a fresh non-preilluminated sample, Fi is equivalent to the minimal relative fluorescence yield, F0, which is characteristic for a sample with the primary stable PSII acceptor QA being fully oxidized. After ST-profile correction the F0STK and F0PAM signals describe the same state of PSII and, hence, can be normalized to the same value of 1, for quantitative comparison of the amplitudes of light induced PAM and STK responses and assessment of HIQ, which lowers FSTK with respect to FPAM.

Results and interpretations

Single flash induced changes of fluorescence yield in the absence of artificial electron acceptors

As outlined under Materials and methods (section on “Photosynthetic materials and sample preparation”) PSII CC samples in the absence of artificial electron acceptors have to be kept in strict darkness before measuring chlorophyll fluorescence responses, in order to avoid uncontrolled, slowly reversible or even irreversible increases of the initial fluorescence yield, Fi. When this prerequisite is fullfilled, as was the case in the measurements of Fig. 1, the applied dimer preparation displays outstandingly large values of up to Fm/F0 ~ 20, corresponding to Fv/Fm ~ 0.95. To our knowledge these are the highest values ever measured with any type of photosynthetically active samples. High values of Fm/F0 and Fv/Fm are generally considered to be indicative of high extents of sample integrity and efficiency with which light energy is converted into chemically fixed energy by charge separation in PSII. In this context, however, it has to be kept in mind, that high Fv/Fm values can correspond to high quantum yields of photosynthetic electron transport only, if also the integrity of secondary structural and functional components is assured.

In Fig. 1 panels a and b the PAM response of a freshly prepared, non-preilluminated PSII dimer sample to a saturating 15µs ST applied at t = 0, F(PAM), is presented, using linear and logarithmic time scales, respectively. Maximal fluorescence yield, Fpeak, is observed at 175 µs after ST-triggering. In the relaxation kinetics within the displayed 10ms time range a rapid and a slow phase can be visually distinguished. Notably, there is a substantial fraction of ST-induced Fv, that does not relax within 10 ms. In previous analogous experiments with algae and chloroplasts, this fraction was characterized to reflect “inactive PSII”, i.e. PSII reaction centers in which the formed QA− is not reoxidized by QB (Lavergne and Leci 1993). When PSII is embedded in the thylakoid membrane, this fraction may be visualized to consist of a dynamically changing population of PSII in which the QB binding site is temporarily vacant, resulting in a stationary state of binding and unbinding of PQ. In purified PSII CC such a dynamically changing state is prevented by the lack of a PQ pool. This principal difference may be expected to result in systematic differences between the dark regeneration characteristics of PSII CC compared to PSII in vivo. In algae and cyanobacteria at room temperature usually dark-light Chl fluorescence induction kinetics can be readily reproduced using 3–5 min dark intervals between consecutive measurements. In this case, not only the QA− of active but also that of inactive PSII is reoxidized. In contrast, the QA− formed in “inactive PSII” of PSII CC (i.e. with the QB binding site being vacant) cannot be reoxidized by forward electron flow, due to the lack of freely diffusible PQ. However, there may be partial reoxidation via recombination (or back transfer of electrons involving still unknown pathways) with positive charges stored at the PSII donor side.

In this form of presentation it is apparent that PAM signal processing does not set in before about 90µs after start of the recording. During this time period the photodiode detector is gated in order to avoid flash induced artifacts. With other words, the PAM measurement is “blind” for any ST induced changes of fluorescence yield occurring during the ST and within 75µs after the 15µsST. As will be shown below (in Figs. 4 and 5), this “information gap” can be filled by the ST kinetics (STK) measuring technique, which can be applied simultaneously with PAM measurements.

In Fig. 1b, the amplitude of the fluorescence peak, Fpeak, at175 µs after the ST, amounts to 16.7 Fo units. Although this may be considered an exceptionally high value, the here measured Fpeak does not yet constitute true maximal fluorescence yield, Fm, which is defined for a state in which all photochemical and non-photochemical quenching is completely suppressed. The observed Fpeak plateau is the result of two overlapping opposite signal changes, namely the increase due to relaxation of donor-side dependent quenching, DQ, and the decrease caused by electron transfer from QA− to QB. At 175µs after the ST, these two opposite signal changes just happen to compensate each other. This means that Fpeak is lowered with respect to the true maximal fluorescence yield, Fm, in a twofold manner: First, by the negative ∆F that is caused by QA− oxidation during the 175µs dark interval and second, by not taking into account the positive ∆F which results from relaxation of DQ at dark times longer than 175µs and thus is hidden in the dominating decline of fluorescence yield. As will be demonstrated below in Fig. 2, the true maximal fluorescence yield after application of one saturating ST can be assessed in the presence of DCMU, which inhibits QA− reoxidation by QB.

Figure 1c shows the changes of relative fluorescence yield during application of the saturating 15µs ST, the ST Kinetics (STK), which were measured immediately before the PAM responses to this particular ST displayed in panels a and b. As both types of data were normalized at F0 = 1, the amplitudes of F(STK) and F(PAM) are directly comparable. Notably, maximal fluorescence yield reached during the 15µs ST, F15µsSTK, is lower than Fpeak by a factor of ~ 1.7. The difference is distinctly larger than in measurements with intact cells of Chlorella (Klughammer et al. 2024). As already outlined under Materials and methods (section on “Rationale of combined PAM and ST-kinetics measurements”), the flash-induced lowering of fluorescence yield is due to High Intensity Quenching, HIQ, which has been characterized to reflect a combination of carotenoid triplet quenching, TQ, and donor-side-dependent quenching, DQ (Klughammer et al. 2024). Obviously, in the 160µs darktime between the 15µs ST and the Fpeak at 175µs a large part of this HIQ is relaxed.

In Fig. 2 the ST relaxation kinetics measured by the PAM technique in presence and absence of 20 µM DCMU are compared. The depicted experiment was carried out with a dimer sample that previously had been stored for 7 months at -30 °C. Compared with the dimer sample in the experiment of Fig. 1, which had been stored for just 1 month at -30 °C, this sample displays somewhat less Fv/F0, but very similar relaxation kinetics. Almost total inhibition of QA− reoxidation by 20 µM DCMU is apparent, which is indicative of a high extent of integrity of the core complex at the site of competitive binding of QB and DCMU to the D1 protein. As expected, maximal fluorescence, Fm, obtained in presence of DCMU is higher than the Fpeak of the control. In presence of DCMU the post-ST rise of fluorescence yield in the dark extends to 1ms. In view of the fact that QA− formation is quasi-instantaneous and terminated at the end of the 15µs ST, this fluorescence rise must reflect HIQ relaxation. As the lifetime of TQ in air amounts to a few microseconds only, the dark rise > 0.1ms may be considered a final phase of DQ relaxation. As will be shown below (section on “Twin STK recordings for assessment of HIQ relaxation”) the complete relaxation kinetics can be reliably assessed with µs resolution by Twin STK measurements.

Assuming that the initial state of the PSII donor side is the same with and without DCMU present, also the same DQ relaxation kinetics should apply. This means that in the initial part (up to about 1ms) of the commonly measured flash-induced decay kinetics (as e.g. displayed in Fig. 1a, b) the apparent rate of QA− reoxidation is generally slowed down by DQ relaxation. Furthermore, it is obvious that standard PAM measurements are “blind” for any decay component faster than 100µs. In practice, these findings bear on the quantification of various decay components in terms of amplitudes and time constants by exponential fit analysis. The user software of the Multicolor-PAM-II applied in the present study (PamWin-4) provides an exponential fit routine for analysing decay curves, with which we have analysed the data of Fig. 1a, b. In Fig. 3 the outcome of a fit using two exponentials and a constant quasi-irreversible component is shown. The PSII monomers represent a population of active, intact PSII complexes that are roughly half the molecular mass of PSII dimers, despite being identical in their protein subunit composition. In contrast, PSII heterodimers are likely composed of one active monomer bound to an inactive monomer that lacks both extrinsic subunits and a mature OEC. This was demonstrated by Lambertz et al. (2023) who showed that heterodimers exhibit only half the oxygen evolution activity of the dimeric PSII population. This is attributed to a lower manganese content, which is primarily explained by the inactive monomer’s association with the Psb27 assembly factor.

Twin STK recordings for assessment of DQ relaxation

As outlined in the preceding section, for technical reasons PAM measurements are not suited to provide quantitative information on the changes of fluorescence yield during the first 75µs after a saturating flash. However, in principle this “information gap” can be filled by double flash measurements using the separate ST lamp of the Multi-Color-PAM II fluorometer (twin STK recordings). Details on this measuring technique were communicated in Klughammer et al. (2024) (under Materials and methods in the section on “Twin flashes for measuring dark relaxation, assessment of HIQ and determination of FmST”). Briefly, the first ST (ST#1, at full intensity) saturates QA reduction, at the same time unavoidably generating “High Intensity Quenching”, HIQ. With the help of the second ST (ST#2, at 20% of maximal intensity) which is applied after variable dark time, tD, the overlapping relaxation kinetics of HIQ and of QA− reoxidation are assessed. When the latter is prevented by DCMU, selectively HIQ relaxation is measured. HIQ consists of rapidly decaying carotenoid triplet quenching, TQ (half-time in air about 2µs) and donor side dependent quenching, DQ (half-time dependent on initial S-state distribution of the oxygen evolving complex, OEC).

A typical twin STK recording with a control sample of purified dimers is presented in Fig. 4 for tD = 5µs between ST#1 and ST#2. During the first 15µs ST, which is applied with maximal flash intensity, fluorescence yield increases to the level denoted as F15µsSTK#1. The second ST just serves the purpose of determining the increase of relative fluorescence yield after a defined dark time, tD. In the given example, it is applied with a width of 5µs and 20% of maximal flash intensity. The presented data are corrected for the ST on/off profiles and for the different intensities of ST#1 and ST#2 (for further details, see Fig. 4 and accompanying text in Klughammer et al. 2024). The initial fluorescence yield of the second ST kinetics, FiSTK#2, can be readily assessed and compared with the value observed at the end of the first ST kinetics, F15µsSTK#1. The difference is a measure of HIQ relaxation during the first 5µs of darkness.

Analogous measurements were carried out using purified dimers with tD values ranging from 2µs to 700µs in the absence (Control) and presence of 20 µM DCMU. The results are shown in Fig. 5 panels a and b, with linear and logarithmic time scales, respectively. Each data point (resulting from a measurement analogous to the one depicted in Fig. 4) was obtained using a fresh non-preilluminated sample. Up to tD = 15µs the FiSTK#2 values +/- DCMU are close to equal. From tD = 20µs upwards, photochemical quenching resulting from QA− reoxidation develops in the control.

The STK data of Fig. 5 can be directly compared with the PAM data presented in Fig. 2. The former provide unique information on HIQ relaxation during the first 100µs following a saturating ST.

The HIQ relaxation kinetics in the presence of DCMU display an overall half time of about 40µs. In principle, this half-time applies to the convolute of TQ and DQ relaxation (Klughammer et al. 2024). However, as the half-time of TQ relaxation in air amounts to a few µs only, it may be assumed that the overall relaxation kinetics are dominated by DQ relaxation. The data suggest that DQ initially lowers ST-induced maximal fluorescence yield by about 30%. If it is assumed, that under the given conditions the dimer samples initially were in the S1 state of the oxygen evolving complex (OEC), DQ relaxation at tD >10µs would reflect the post-ST dark transition S1 YZ+ QA− → S2 YZ QA−. The here measured overall half-time of this transition amounts to about 40µs, which is significantly lower than for S1-S2 advancement in flash-induced O2 yields reported by Dau and Haumann (2007): 70µs for S1-S2 and 30µs for S0-S1.

As to the mechanism of light-induced DQ, it appears likely that P680+ in equilibrium with YZ+ is the actual quencher (Butler 1972; Klughammer et al. 2024):

S1 YZ P680 QA → (flash) S1 YZ+ P680 QA− ←→ S1 YZ P680+ QA− → (dark) S2 YZ P680 QA−.

Fluorescence changes induced by sequences of ST, as measured via STK and PAM relaxation kinetics

In the past, experiments applying repetitive flash illumination have played an important role in the elucidation of the mechanisms of electron transport at PSII donor and acceptor sides (Joliot et al. 1969; Delosme 1971; Joliot and Joliot (1973; Velthuys and Amesz 1974; Bowes and Crofts 1980; Laasch et al. 1993, Vermaas et al. 1984; Messinger and Renger 1993; Shinkarev et al. 1997; Ananyev and Dismukes 2005; Shevela et al. 2007). Very recently, Klughammer et al. (2024) introduced a new type of chlorophyll fluorometer (Multi-Color-PAM II) which has proven well suited for the assessment of changes that are induced by sequences (or trains) of flashes. In particular, period-4 oscillations of fluorescence yield are indicative of the periodically changing S-states of the OEC, which so far have been primarily studied via the yield of flash-induced oxygen evolution. This new measuring system is unique in providing two complementary types of simultaneously measured chlorophyll fluorescence information, PAM relaxation kinetics and ST kinetics (STK), as already demonstrated above in the experiment of Fig. 1. Here, for the first time this measuring system is applied in the study of repetitive flash-induced fluorescence changes of purified PSII CC preparations. In this application, attention has to be paid to the peculiar state of the PSII acceptor side, which lacks the natural secondary electron acceptor pool, into which normally after QB= protonation PQH2 is released and from where free PQ rebinds to PSII. So far, it is not known what happens when in the absence of artificial electron acceptors the secondary intrinsic electron acceptor QB becomes double reduced. If it becomes protonated and released from its binding site into the QB-pocket and/or into the detergent belt of the micelle, the QB binding site would be vacant, resulting in “inactive PSII”. Alternatively, in view of recent work of the Dismukes group (Ananyev et al. 2016; Gates et al. 2023; Zournas et al. 2023) also a backtransfer of electrons from the acceptor to the donor side may be envisaged, when forward electron transport is not possible. In this case, PSII would remain active, carrying out cyclic electron transport (PSII-CEF). These aspects will be dealt with in the Concluding Discussion (section on The “terminal” rise: A tentative interpretation).

For an understanding of the data in Fig. 6 and S2, it is important to recall that FiSTK#1 is identical to the F0 of the non-preilluminated sample, whereas FiSTK#2 constitutes the initial fluorescence yield measured 5s after ST#1. Generally, the increased value of FiSTK#n with respect to FiSTK#n−1 reflects the extent of quasi-irreversible closure of PSII reaction centers caused by the preceding flash, ST#n-1. This means, that when in the plots the ST number is even, the increment of FiSTK is caused by an uneven flash number and vice versa. Quasi-irreversible closure of PSII reaction centers (i.e. when QA− is not reoxidized within 5s dark time) may be tentatively explained by formation of fractions of PSII CC with the acceptor states QA−QB= or QA−QBH2 or QA−QB (vacant).

As apparent in Fig. 6 and Fig.S2_1a, up to ST#7 the ST-induced increments of FiSTK#n are larger with even compared to uneven ST numbers.The observed binary oscillation pattern agrees with the assumption that in non-preilluminated samples the state QAQB prevails, which after ST#1 forms QAQB− in equilibrium with QA− QB. The latter is responsible for the increase of FiSTK#2 with respect to FiSTK#1. During ST#2 the acceptor state QA− QB− in equilibrium with QA QB= is formed. Interestingly, as revealed by the relatively small increase of FiSTK#3 with respect to FiSTK#2, part of the formed QB= appears to be reoxidized during the 5s dark interval.

In the bottom panel of Fig. 6 the increase of FiSTK reflects the ST induced suppression of photochemical quenching, i.e. the decreasing “openness” of PSII (5s after the preceding ST), whereas the increase of F15µsSTK indicates the ST induced suppression of non-photochemical quenching. This interpretation relies on the assumption that QA is fully reduced at the end of each ST, so that photochemical energy conversion and the associated photochemical fluorescence quenching are completely blocked when F15µsSTK is assessed. It is already apparent by visual inspection that the increases of FiSTK and F15µsSTK observed with increasing ST number are similar. In view of the distinctly different quenching mechanisms involved, this is a surprising finding. Actually, as shown in Fig.S2_1b, for ST numbers 7–15, i.e. after cessation of the binary oscillation (displayed in panel a), the correlation between the increases in F15µsSTK and FiSTK is very close (R2 = 0.9995), strongly suggesting a common cause, the nature of which remains to be elucidated. In this context, simultaneously measured PAM responses may provide complementary information. As already demonstrated above in Fig. 1, the applied measuring system allows to record the PAM relaxation kinetics that are induced by the very ST, the STK of which displays the characteristic fluorescence levels FiSTK and F15µsSTK. After normalization of F0, the amplitudes of the PAM and STK responses can be quantitatively compared. In panel a of Fig. 9 the new term “terminal rise” is introduced for the biphasic fluorescence increase that is induced in an ST sequence with ST#>3, reflecting stepwise ST-induced suppression of DQ. Comparison with the data in Figs. 6 and 8 shows that this rise happens in parallel with a very similar increase of initial fluorescence yield, Fi, measured 5s after the ST, which reflects stepwise ST-induced suppression of photochemical quenching. Fi is always distinctly lower than F150µs and F5ms, which means that even in the absence of a secondary acceptor pool, a substantial part of ST-induced QA− can be reoxidized within 5s, but this part decreases with increasing ST number. The oxidized part is re-reduced upon charge separation in the next flash, causing full suppression of photochemical quenching and formation of DQ, with the latter being manifested in the F15µsSTK and F150µsPAM signals. With TQ relaxing within a few µs, the PAM signal measured 135µs after the flash should be predominantly affected by DQ. Hence, the “terminal rise” can be considered to reflect the stepwise suppression of the ST-induced DQ, suggesting a stepwise transformation of the OEC states S2 + S3 into S0 + S1.Under the given conditions, the Fv involved in this transformation amounts to 28.5% of overall Fv.

These unique experimental features must be considered when attempting to understand the molecular mechanisms underlying the “terminal rise” phenomenon. Such an attempt will be made in the Concluding Discussion, where it is proposed that in the absence of natural or artificial acceptor pools, double reduced QB can either be protonated and released in form of PQH2 into the detergent belt (increasing the population of “inactive PSII”) or backreact in a bivalent reaction with positive charges at the donor side. Based on these assumptions, a tentative simple scheme will be presented that can explain parallel increases of Fi and of maximal fluorescence yield induced by the same flashes.

Additional information in favor of bivalent backreactions with positive charges stored in the S states of the OEC, is presented under Supplementary Materials S3, where the ST-induced PAM relaxation kinetics of Fig. 7 are deconvoluted into two exponentials and one quasi-irreversible component, with the plots vs. flash-number displaying binary oscillations.

Suppression of the “terminal rise” of fluorescence yield by DCBQ and DCMU

Figure 10 shows that the terminal rise of fluorescence yield in a sequence of ST, which was characterized above by the experiments in Figs. 6, 7, 8 and 9, is not only suppressed by 2 µM DCBQ (panel a), but also in the presence 20 µM DCMU (panel b). Thanks to F0 normalization, the signal amplitudes in Fig. 10 can be directly compared with those in Figs. 7, 8 and 9, where in the absence of additions F150µs is somewhat larger than 17 F0 units.

In the presence of 2 µM DCBQ, instead of the terminal rise phenomenon, a damped period-4 oscillation is observed both in F15µsSTK and F150µsPAM (Fig. 10a). The oscillation pattern is very similar to that observed in vivo (see e.g. the oscillation of the QA− state in Fig. 10a of Klughammer et al. 2024). Obviously, the artificial electron acceptor DCBQ can substitute for the natural PQ acceptor pool.

Fluorescence measurements in the presence of DCBQ are generally complicated by the fact that its oxidized form does not only quench Chl fluorescence photochemically, but also non-photochemically in a concentration dependent manner (Amesz and Fork 1967). Some details on the concentration dependencies of photochemical and non-photochemical quenching of fluorescence in PSII CC are presented in Supplementary Materials S4. In purified dimers at a Chl concentration of 1 µg/ml the non-photochemical quenching by 2 µM DCBQ is negligibly low, whereas at the same concentration the photochemical quenching already is saturated, provided sufficient incubation time is allowed. The data in Fig. 10a were measured in the presence of 2 µM DCBQ after 2 h incubation.

Screenshots of the original PAM relaxation kinetics, on which the PAM data in Fig. 10 are based, are presented in Supplementary Materials S5.

In presence of 20 µM DCMU (Fig. 10b) the amplitude of F150µs (PAM) remains below 14 F0 units throughout the whole train of 15 consecutive ST. The terminal rise is completely suppressed. There is a small increase induced by ST#2, which is diminished when a longer dark time for DQ relaxation is allowed, with fluorescence yield being measured at 1ms. In this context, the data presented above in Fig. 4 are important, which in the presence of DCMU show significant DQ relaxation in the 150µs to 1ms time range. While the terminal rise is also missing in the plot of F15µs(STK) vs. ST number, there is a pronounced increase induced by ST#2, which is about two times larger than in the control (see Fig. 9a). This observation suggests that the increase of F15µs(STK) induced by ST#2 and the terminal rise have different mechanistic causes.

The effects of DCBQ and DCMU indicate that the development of the terminal rise of fluorescence yield requires the accumulation of the secondary PSII acceptor QB in its reduced form, which is prevented by both DCBQ and DCMU.

Polyphasic fluorescence rise induced by continuous light in purified dimers

In intact cells, the increase of Chl fluorescence yield induced by strong continuous light is polyphasic, displaying the characteristic fluorescence levels F0, I1, I2 and Fm (Neubauer and Schreiber 1987) or O, J, I and P (Strasser et al. 1995). While there has been general consensus that the F0-I1 transient reflects the photochemical reduction of QA, the mechanism of the ensuing “thermal phase” is still discussed controversially. The following measurements with purified dimers bear on the interpretation of the thermal phase.

The F0-I1 rise reflects the light induced closure of PSII by reduction of the primary acceptor QA. This is the photochemical phase of the polyphasic rise kinetics. The half-rise time of F0-I1 is proportional to quantum flux density and the effective, wavelength dependent absorption cross section of PSII. At the applied actinic intensity, in the given preparation of PSII dimers it amounts to 415µs. For comparison, in a dilute suspension of Chlorella vulgaris the same half-rise time was observed at 5.5 times lower intensity of 440 nm light (not shown in the figures), which reflects the correspondingly larger antenna system which efficiently absorbs 440 nm light. On the other hand, in Synechococcus leopoliensis, where the light harvesting phycobilisome does not contribute much to 440 nm absorption, use of the same intensity as with the dimers results in a half-rise time of 466µs, i.e. similarly as in the PSII dimers.

As shown in Fig. 11b, after a dip phase at 2-3ms there is a secondary rise in fluorescence yield beyond the I1 level which consists of two sub-phases. This secondary rise strongly reminds of the “thermal” I1 – I2 phase observed in intact chloroplasts and cells (for chloroplasts, see e.g. Figure 3 in Neubauer and Schreiber 1987 and for Chlorella Fig. 3a in Schreiber and Klughammer 2021). In this context, it is important to remind that the fluorescence rise beyond the I2 level observed in vivo was shown to reflect variable fluorescence of PSI (Schreiber and Klughammer 2021), so that it cannot possibly contribute to the polyphasic rise measured in purified PSII CC. Hence, the I2 – P phase is missing in PSII CC or, with other words, the “classical” levels of I2, P and Fm (or J and P) defined in the literature are identical in PSII CC. In the following, the maximal level measured in PSII CC will be denoted by Fm.

The question arises whether the I1 – Fm phase induced by strong continuous light may have the same cause as the “terminal rise” of fluorescence yield induced in a sequence of ST, as characterized in the experiments of Figs. 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10 above. As e.g. apparent in Fig. 9, also the “terminal rise” displays two sub-phases, which supports the notion of a common cause. Furthermore, comparison of the data in Figs. 3 and 11 reveals that the maximal fluorescence yield induced by a single ST, FmST, is practically equal to I1 in the polyphasic rise induced by strong continuous light. While QA is fully reduced in both cases, further charge separations are required to exhaust the intrinsic acceptor pool, which above was shown to be a prerequisite for the terminal rise phenomenon.

For special applications, the Multi-Color-PAM II allows (as an exception) to substantially increase the intensity of continuous actinic illumination beyond the maximal intensity provided by the standard actinic light source. In this way, the notoriously small optical absorption cross-section of PSII CC can be partially compensated. For this purpose, a multiple turnover pulse of extremely strong light of the separate “ST lamp” can be triggered, which normally serves for ST generation only. The parallel increase of I1 and development of a dip phase at extremely high actinic intensities bears on the interpretation of the ensuing thermal rise phase. In view of the information obtained above in the experiments using saturating ST, it is clear that the Fo – I1 rise in PSII dimers is the complex result of at least 4 overlapping events:

The first turnover of PSII charge separation, resulting in an increase of fluorescence yield due to QA- formation, the rate of which is proportional to actinic intensity, and a concomitant decrease of fluorescence yield by DQ;

The first turnover of PSII charge separation, resulting in an increase of fluorescence yield due to QA- formation, the rate of which is proportional to actinic intensity, and a concomitant decrease of fluorescence yield by DQ;

S state advancement (reduction of oxidized YZ by the OEC) resulting in relaxation of DQ and a concomitant increase of fluorescence yield;

S state advancement (reduction of oxidized YZ by the OEC) resulting in relaxation of DQ and a concomitant increase of fluorescence yield;

QA- reoxidation by QB, leading to a decline of fluorescence yield;

QA- reoxidation by QB, leading to a decline of fluorescence yield;

a second turnover of PSII charge separation in that fraction of PSII in which QA- has become reoxidized by QB, with the combination of (3) and (4) stabilizing QA dependent fluorescence yield, but due to the concomitantly formed DQ resulting in an overall decline of fluorescence yield; furthermore, DQ is known to increase with the number of positive charges stored in the OEC and, hence, is larger than in the first turnover.

a second turnover of PSII charge separation in that fraction of PSII in which QA- has become reoxidized by QB, with the combination of (3) and (4) stabilizing QA dependent fluorescence yield, but due to the concomitantly formed DQ resulting in an overall decline of fluorescence yield; furthermore, DQ is known to increase with the number of positive charges stored in the OEC and, hence, is larger than in the first turnover.

These considerations suggest that, provided that sufficiently high actinic intensity is applied, in purified dimers the DQ after the first turnover is mostly relaxed before the second turnover occurs. In this case, the F0 – I1 transient is close to reflecting a single turnover and the I1 level approaches the maximal fluorescence yield, Fm. The dip phase reflects the DQ that is caused by the second charge separation. The same DQ is also formed at lower actinic intensities, but then hidden in the fluorescence rise. At its lowest point, a maximal fraction of PSII in high S-states (S2 + S3) coexists with the acceptor state QA−QB=. It appears reasonable to assume, that the ensuing thermal rise of fluorescence yield reflects the reduction (i.e. deactivation) of S2 + S3 by the same kind of backreaction as was proposed above to cause the “terminal rise” in a train of ST.This assumption is supported by the fact that the thermal phase is inhibited by 20 µM DCMU (not in the figures). Obviously, when the accumulation of electrons on QB is prevented, a major pathway for S-state deactivation is blocked, at least in purified PSII CC dimers.

Concluding discussion

The presented results demonstrate that purified PSII CC are ideally suited for investigating the relationship between Chl fluorescence yield and PSII activity. Solubilized at just 1 µg Chl/ml these preparations provide optically thin, transparent samples that yield robust signals and large light induced changes of relative fluorescence yield. However, in the course of the present study it has become clear that for obtaining reliable and reproducible results much more experimental skill and attention are required than in the case of fluorescence measurements on non-fractionated photosynthetically active samples, like cyanobacteria, algae or leaves.

“F0 problem” in the absence of artificial electron acceptors

Particular attention has to be paid to F0 measurements on PSII CC in the absence of artificial electron acceptors, when very weak preillumination unintentionally may lead to uncontrolled reduction of the intrinsic QAQB acceptor pool, accompanied by increases of the “dark” fluorescence yield, FO, and corresponding accumulation of positive charges at the PSII donor side. Actually, this problem may explain why in the few previous communications on the Chl fluorescence properties of PSII CC, the influence of F0 on the presented data was eliminated by plotting ratios of variable fluorescence only, like (F-F0)/(Fm-F0) (i.e., Magyar et al. 2018, 2023; Sipka et al. 2019; Zabret et al. 2021; Lambertz et al. 2023). While this is a suitable way to get rid of erratic changes of F0, important information on the dark state and integrity of PSII is lost. For example, if PSII for some reason in one sample is partially blocked and, hence, displays higher F0 than a second, normal sample, the light-induced fluorescence changes of the two samples in terms of (F-F0)/(Fm-F0) can be the same. In the present study, correct values of F0 have proven of particular importance, not only for the determination of reliable values of Fm/F0 and Fv/Fm, but also for the possibility to quantitatively compare the amplitudes of flash-induced changes of relative fluorescence yield measured simultaneously by the PAM and STK techniques. For this purpose, both sets of original data were normalized to a common F0 = 1. In the case of STK measurements, assessment of F0 is particular challenging, as it requires sub-µs time resolution. The applied new measuring system (Klughammer et al. 2024) is unique in allowing to correct for distortion by the rising-edge flash profile of the initial fluorescence changes.

Unprecedentedly high values of Fm/F0 and Fv/F0 in our purified PSII CC

The investigated preparations of highly purified PSII CC isolated from Thermosynechococcus vestitus (Lambertz et al. 2023) generally display exceptionally high values of Fm/F0 > 10, corresponding to Fv/Fm>0.9. Notably, this is even true for the Fv that is induced by a single saturating 15µs flash (see e.g. data in Fig. 3, where in purified PSII dimers FmST/F0 = 19 was determined, corresponding to Fv/Fm = 0.947). A decisive prerequisite for such high values is that the sample is freshly solubilized and has not seen any light before application of the first flash, with which the relative fluorescence yield FmST is induced. When this prerequisite is fulfilled, the quantum yield with which an absorbed photon results in a stabilized PSII charge separation is remarkably close to 1. As demonstrated in the Slow Kinetics recordings under Supplementary Materials S4, in the absence of artificial electron acceptors the extremely low quantum flux density of 0.01 µmol 440 nm quanta m−2s− 1 is high enough to induce within 150 s illumination a 36% increase of F0. Considering that in the given preparation a full turnover of PSII occurs within about 600 µs at a quantum flux density of 6500 µmol 440 nm quanta m−2s− 1 (see Fig. 11a), this means that at 0.01 µmol 440 nm quanta m−2s− 1 it takes about 360s for every PSII to receive a light quantum. When, as in the given case, even at such low PSII turnover rates a slow increase of fluorescence yield is observed, this reflects a high extent of irreversibility of the underlying light induced reactions.

To our knowledge, the maximal values of Fm/F0 and Fv/Fm reported here represent the highest recorded to date for any photosynthetically active sample. This underscores the exceptional structural and functional integrity of our purified complexes. However, a fair comparison with other PSII CC preparations described in the literature is not possible, if, as mentioned above, previously published fluorescence data were presented in units of (F-F0)/(Fm-F0). In the particular case of Magyar et al. (2023), besides plots of (F-F0)/(Fm-F0), in their Fig. 2 also some F0-normalized data of DCMU-treated PSII CC of Thermosynechococcus vulcanus at -80 °C are presented. Notably, whereas in our preparations in the absence of any additions the first ST at room temperature induces F/F0> 10, in Fig. 2 of Magyar et al. (2023) the first ST induces F1/F0 < 2 only. It is not clear, to what extent this obvious lack of ST-induced Fv is due to the sub-freezing temperature or to the presence of DCMU or to particular properties of the PSII CC samples or to pretreatment of the samples, e.g. with respect to preillumination.

The experimental observation that a single saturating 15µs flash can induce FmST/F0 = 19, corresponding to Fv/Fm=0.947 (see Fig. 3), casts considerable doubt on the idea advanced by Sipka et al. (2021) that generally a large part of Fv not only requires the light induced reduction of QA (formation of closed PSII reaction centers, PSIIC), but that “additional illumination gradually transforms PSIIC into a light-adapted charge-separated state (PSIIL)”. Obviously, in our PSII CC preparation most of Fv can be induced by a single ST. It remains to be clarified what exactly is the cause of the strongly differing results of Garab and co-workers. The development of the PSIIL idea mainly was based on measurements of flash-induced increments of fluorescence yield using similar purified PSII CC dimers isolated from thermophilic cyanobacteria as in the present study. Hence, in principle similar results leading to similar conclusions should be expected. In reality, however, the results differ considerably, calling for a thorough investigation into the underlying reasons – an important task for future research.

In this context, we would like to draw attention to some Fv/Fm data that were measured in 2021 on a similar dimeric PSII CC preparation as used in the present study and so far were just published in form of a preprint (Zabret and Nowaczyk 2021). There was a disquieting discrepancy between surprisingly low values of Fv/Fm ~ 0.3 and the known exceptionally high rates of oxygen evolution in this preparation (Zabret et al. 2021). In hindsight, it now appears that in the applied samples F0 was substantially increased by preillumination. If we assume that the true Fv/Fm of the applied preparation amounted to 0.9, this means that 70% of all PSII were blocked by unintentional preillumination, so that the remaining light induced changes of fluorescence yield should be considered to reflect the properties of a selection/population of PSII CC which displays a relatively high rate/capacity of QA− reoxidation. This example illustrates the importance of measuring fluorescence responses with non-preilluminated samples, the initial dark-state of which is controlled by appropriate F0 measurements. When for measuring oxygen evolution artificial electron acceptors are added to the same preparation, effective PSII quantum yield, of course, is not affected by preillumination.

Principal problem of correct assessment of Fm using an ST-protocol

In a given photosynthetically active sample, the F0 and Fm states are intimately linked by the definition that the quantum yield of non-photochemical dissipation of excitation energy is the same upon assessment of F0 (with QA being fully oxidized) and assessment of Fm (after quantitative reduction of QA) (Kitajima and Butler 1975). While in the case of F0 an unambiguous value can be obtained by straight forward procedures, using PAM and STK techniques (see e.g. Figure 1), assessment of Fm is complicated by the fact that light-induced QA reduction unavoidably is accompanied by a transient increase of non-photochemical energy dissipation (Joliot and Joliot 1973; Schreiber and Neubauer 1987). The more the latter is relaxed/reversed at the moment of Fm determination, the higher (and more correct) is the obtained value, provided full QA reduction is maintained. In our STK measurements, when F15µsSTK is determined at the end of a saturating 15µs ST, no time is given for relaxation and, hence, maximal fluorescence yield is lowered by full-size non-photochemical energy dissipation, for which the term High Intensity Quenching, HIQ, was introduced (Schreiber et al. 2019) and which was shown to consist of carotenoid triplet quenching, TQ, and donor-side dependent quenching, DQ (Klughammer et al. 2024). In contrast, in our PAM measurements, the initial post-ST data points are collected after 75–150 µs dark time, during which all of TQ and part of DQ are relaxed. These features explain the large difference between the maximal fluorescence signals assessed by the two techniques, as apparent in the data of Fig. 1 and also Fig. 9.

As shown in Fig. 2, when rapid reoxidation of QA− is prevented by DCMU, DQ relaxation leads to a post-ST dark rise of fluorescence yield measured by the PAM technique in the 0.1-1 ms time range. A similar rise in presence of DCMU previously was detected in spinach chloroplasts using a pump-and-probe measuring technique (Schreiber and Krieger 1996). In view of the data in Figs. 1 and 2 it is clear that in the absence of DCMU the true ST induced maximal fluorescence yield is higher than Fpeak (Fig. 1b). The data in Fig. 3 demonstrate that FmST can be estimated by extrapolation of the PAM relaxation kinetics using a standard fitting routine provided by the PamWin-4 user program of the Multi-Color-PAM II fluorometer.

The twin-ST data of Figs. 4 and 5 describe the complete post-ST HIQ relaxation kinetics in dimeric PSII CC, both in the absence and presence of DCMU. Considering that for each data point a fresh non-preilluminated sample had to be used, these STK measurements were much more time/material-consuming and labor-intensive than the analogous PAM measurements in Fig. 2. This “investment”, however, paid off by unique new information on the rate of charge stabilization at the PSII donor side in the given dimeric PSII CC preparation. As TQ in air relaxes within a few µs, the relaxation kinetics in presence of DCMU may be assumed to mainly reflect DQ relaxation. Hence, if under the given conditions the dimer samples initially were in the S1 state of the oxygen evolving complex (OEC), DQ relaxation would mainly reflect the transition S1 YZ+ QA− → S2 YZ QA−. For this transition a half-time of 70 µs was reported (Dau and Haumann 2007), which is distinctly larger than the 40 µs estimated from the experiment of Fig. 5. At the present stage, it cannot be decided whether the observed smaller value is due to the contribution of TQ relaxation or whether it possibly reflects a significant amount of S0 in the initial S-state distribution. The half-time for advancement from S0 to S1 was reported to amount to 30 µs (Dau and Haumann 2007). In any case, the data demonstrate the potential of twin-ST measurements for obtaining quantitative information on PSII donor-side properties. It is clear that this potential can be exploited only with the help of extensive further measurements, which would be beyond the scope of the present communication.

Assuming that in the experiment of Figs. 4–5 in presence of DCMU at 1 ms the state S2 YZ QA− is formed, the then observed maximal fluorescence yield, FmST, does not necessarily constitute the maximal fluorescence yield which was defined at the beginning of the present section. For this to be true, at FmST the rate of non-photochemical energy dissipation should be equal to that effective upon assessment of F0 (in a non-preilluminated sample, with QA fully oxidized) (Kitajima and Butler 1975). In this context, it has to be considered that all fluorescence measurements, in contrast to absorbance measurements, have an “active” character, in the sense that the incident measuring light (ML) is not only absorbed, but also interacts functionally with the photosynthetically active sample. Based on this interaction, fluorescence can provide unique information not only on the relative “openness” of PSII, but also on non-photochemical processes that compete with photochemical energy conversion. Even when QA is fully reduced, absorbed light energy (including the ML, the fluorescence response of which is measured) can transiently form the radical pair P680+Pheo−. The reversible radical pair model (Schatz et al. 1987) predicts that reversible trapping can be accompanied by more or less loss of excitation energy, reflected by “non-photochemical” fluorescence quenching, the extent of which depends on the time constants of nonradiative decay of the radical pair (in the singlet state) to the ground state and of spin dephasing (resulting in the triplet state) (see also Schreiber and Krieger 1996). Theoretically, ST-induced S-state advancement from S1 to S2 results in a lowering of FmST with respect to the “true” Fm, as per definition the latter applies to the S1 state of the PSII donor side and charge separation in the S2 state involves more energy dissipation than in the S1 state (Joliot and Joliot 1973).

FSTK and FPAM of dimeric PSII CC upon illumination by trains of saturating ST

Measuring the fluorescence responses induced by trains of saturating ST is particularly rewarding in the case of PSII CC on the simple practical grounds that a unique wealth of information can be obtained within a couple of minutes using just one fresh, non-preilluminated sample. In the present study generally 15 ST were applied with 5 s dark intervals between consecutive flashes, resulting in 15 individual STK and PAM relaxation recordings (Figs. 6 and 7, respectively, see also Supplementary Materials S2 and S3). Studying PSII CC, much longer dark intervals can be applied than in analogous in vivo measurements (e.g. 0.1 s in the experiment of Fig. 10 of Klughammer et al. 2024), because the rate of S-state deactivation is rather slow in PSII CC (not explicitly investigated here; but see Fig. 10a). The kinds of information obtained by the STK and PAM measurements are complementary: While F15µsSTK in the presence of 100% QA− is strongly quenched by HIQ, HIQ disappears with increasing dark time between the ST and measurement of FPAM, which in parallel with QA reoxidation becomes increasingly controlled by photochemical quenching. The latter aspect is apparent in the plot of the F5msPAM values in Fig. 8, which display a pronounced period-2 oscillation.

In the study of the relationship between relative fluorescence yield and the state of PSII CC, measurements in the absence of any additions are of particular interest. While no inhibitors are known that selectively block electron transport beyond QB, in PSII CC this inhibition occurs quite naturally after ST#3, resulting in a state of the PSII acceptor side that in vivo can be obtained by strong continuous illumination only. Theoretically, when the PSII acceptor pool is restricted to the intrinsic acceptors QA and QB, three saturating ST should lead to the formation of QA−QB= (or QA−QBH2 or QA−QB (vacant) + PQH2) and maximal fluorescence yield. In reality, however, as documented in Figs. 6, 7, 8 and 9, the fluorescence yield induced by ST#3 is not maximal and a characteristic biphasic further increase is observed with ST#4–15. For this increase, which equally is observed in FSTK (Fig. 6) and FPAM (Figs. 7, 8 and 9), we have introduced the descriptive term “terminal rise”. The occurrence of this rise in F15µsSTK strongly suggests that it does not involve a decrease of photochemical quenching. With other words, all F15µsSTK data points in the experiment of Fig. 6 go along with QA being fully reduced, so that the suppression of a non-photochemical quenching mechanism must be involved. Notably, the terminal rise of F15µsSTK in Fig. 6 is close to proportional to that of the initial fluorescence yield, Fi (see also Supplementary Materials S2). This increase of Fi indicates that with increasing ST number the population of PSII increases, in which QA− is not reoxidized after 5s dark time. Based on the observed stepwise parallel suppression of non-photochemical quenching (manifested in F15µsSTK) and photochemical quenching (manifested in Fi) in the following section a plausible interpretation of the reactions causing the “terminal rise” will be presented.

As shown under Supplementary Materials S6, a very similar terminal rise of F15µsSTK in a train of saturating ST is also observed in purified PSII heterodimers and monomers. More experiments will be required to reproduce the subtle differences between these three preparations and, if confirmed, to draw conclusions on the underlying causes.

The “terminal rise”: a tentative interpretation

For understanding the “terminal rise” phenomenon the peculiar state of the PSII acceptor side in the absence of artificial acceptors is of central importance. Theoretically, when QB becomes double reduced, it may become protonated and released from its binding site into the detergent belt of the micelle. In this case, due to the lack of free PQ, the binding site would become vacant and PSII inactivated at the acceptor side. If this would happen in all PSII CC, maximal fluorecence yield would be reached after 3 flashes and there would be no “terminal rise” phenomenon. Alternatively, similarly as proposed by the Dismukes group (Ananyev et al. 2016; Gates et al. 2023; Zournas et al. 2023) a backtransfer of electrons from double reduced QB to the donor-side may be envisaged, whenever forward electron flow is not possible (PSII-CEF) In this case, S3 would be reduced to S1, which would explain the suppression of DQ. But, as QB becomes reoxidized, PSII would remain active, so that no suppression of photochemical quenching and of the concomitant increase of Fi would be expected. In reality, however, there is a stepwise parallel suppression of non-photochemical quenching and photochemical quenching.

The observed parallel suppression of non-photochemical quenching (manifested in F15µsSTK ) and photochemical quenching (manifested in Fi) can be explained, when one fraction of PSII CC undergoes the backreaction, whereas another, complementary fraction becomes inactivated at the acceptor side, as outlined by the following scheme, assuming the initial state S1QAQB.

Fraction 1: S1QAQB → (ST#1) S2QAQB− → (ST#2) S3QAQB= → (backtransfer) S1QAQB → (loop).

Fraction 2: S1QAQB → (ST#1) S2QAQB− → (ST#2) S3QAQB= → (protonation & PQH2 release) S3QAQB(vacant) → (ST#3) S0QA−QB(vacant).

Up to formation of S3QAQB= both fractions of PSII CC undergo the same reactions. Thereafter a fixed fraction of PSII CC cycles the electrons back (PSII-CEF), returning to the initial state (forming a loop). In the rest, QB= becomes protonated and the formed PQH2 released. In fraction 2 PSII becomes inactivated at the acceptor side, with the donor side in state S3 (resulting state S3QA−QB(vacant). Hence, the following ST#3 induces the high-fluorescence state S0QA−QB(vacant), which does not participate in further ST-induced reactions. The population of S1QAQB formed in fraction 1 after electron backtransfer, again splits into two fractions, with one undergoing backtransfer and the other one protonation etc. In this way, with each loop the population of low-fluorescence active PSII-CEF decreases and that of high-fluorescence inactive PSII increases.

While in principle, this simple scheme may explain the stepwise increase of fluorescence yield, both in Fi and in F15µsSTK, in reality a higher complexity should be expected, due to possible involvements of QC, cytb559, non-heme iron, YD etc. It also cannot be excluded that in some centers a free PQ is in the detergent belt. These aspects have to be clarified by future experiments and in silico modelling, not only involving fluorescence, but absorbance and EPR measurements as well.

Suppression of the “terminal rise” by 2 µM DCBQ and by 20 µM DCMU

In presence of 2 µM DCBQ both PAM and STK techniques reveal a damped period-4 oscillation when dimeric PSII CC are illuminated by a train of ST (Fig. 10a). Thanks to F0 normalization, the F15µsSTK plot in presence of DCBQ can be quantitatively compared with that in Figs. 6 and 9 (absence of DCBQ). Obviously, the artificial acceptor eliminates the terminal rise of fluorescence yield, which is replaced by a period-4 oscillation. This oscillation reflects the periodical changes of non-photochemical quenching caused by the higher S-states. A pronounced period-4 oscillation is also observed when fluorescence yield is measured at 150µs by the PAM technique, i.e. 135µs after ST-off (F150µsPAM), with the amplitude of the oscillation being distinctly larger than in F15µsSTK. On first sight, the latter is surprising, as a large part of the oscillating DQ should be relaxed in the 135µs dark time. The apparent discrepancy, however, can be resolved by recalling that F15µsSTK is not only quenched by DQ, but also by TQ, whereas F150µsPAM is not affected by TQ. To our knowledge, in Fig. 10a for the first time pronounced period-4 oscillations of fluorescence yield in PSII CC are presented. These oscillations contain a wealth of detailed information on the reactions at the PSII donor side, further discussion of which, however, would be beyond the scope of the present communication.

The data in Fig. 10b demonstrate a close to complete suppression of the “terminal rise” in dimeric PSII CC by 20 µM DCMU, as revealed by both STK and PAM measuring techniques. Together with the effect of DCBQ shown in Fig. 10a, this finding strongly supports the scheme presented in the preceding section.

Measurements analogous to those shown in Fig. 10b were also carried out with monomeric and heteromeric PSII CC preparations in the presence of DCMU, as shown under Supplementary Materials S7. As it appears, in contrast to the dimers, suppression of the terminal rise by 20 µM DCMU is not complete in the monomers and largely incomplete in the heterodimers. We also noticed that in aged dimer preparations (kept for one day at room temperature in the dark) even 40 µM DCMU did not fully suppress the terminal rise (not in the figures). Further research will be required to check on the plausible conclusion that in the investigated preparations the QB binding site of monomeric and heterodimeric PSII CC is modified with respect to that of the dimers, so that the efficiency of DCMU binding is affected. In this context, it may be mentioned that the ST-driven terminal rise phenomenon has not been observed in intact Chlorella cells, neither in the presence nor in the absence of DCMU (Schreiber 2024).

Garab and co-workers have extensively reported on a phenomenon very similar to the “terminal rise” investigated in the present study (Magyar et al. 2018, 2023; Sipka et al. 2019, 2021; Garab 2025), with the important difference that the fluorescence rise described in their work was measured in the presence of 40 µM DCMU. Not considering any potential inefficiency of DCMU binding, these authors interpreted the stepwise increase of fluorescence yield to reflect a hypothetical ST-induced transformation of closed PSII (PSIIC) into “light adapted” closed PSII (PSIIL), suggesting that a light induced conformational change is responsible for a large part of Fv. Unfortunately, it was assumed that the conclusions drawn from measurements with PSII CC isolated from Thermosynechococcus vulcanus and with spinach thylakoids at sub-freezing temperatures can be generalized, thus generally questioning the mainstream notion that Fv/Fm can serve as a proxy of PSII quantum yield in all photosynthetically active organisms, including intact leaves. The results of the present study cast serious doubt on the conclusions of Garab and co-workers. Further considerations dealing with important prerequisites for meaningful Fv/Fm determinations are presented in the below section on “Fv/Fm and the quantum yield of PSII”.

Polyphasic fluorescence rise induced by strong continuous light

Phenomenologically, the polyphasic rise kinetics of dimeric PSII CC presented in Figs. 11 and 12 are very similar to those measured in intact spinach chloroplasts in presence of methyl viologen (see Fig. 9B in Neubauer and Schreiber 1987), where methyl viologen served the purpose to suppress the so-called I2-P phase, which recently was shown to reflect variable fluorescence yield of PSI (Schreiber and Klughammer 2021). As the I2-P phase is missing in purified PSII CC, I2, P and Fm levels are synonymous. At the I1 level, which is determined by a saturating ST 2ms after onset of continuous illumination, QA is fully reduced and the ensuing “thermal” fluorescence rise reflects the relaxation of the very DQ that was generated during the charge-separations leading to I1. Based on the data presented in Figs. 6, 7, 8 and 9, it can be concluded that in PSII CC, the “terminal rise” of fluorescence yield observed in a train of saturating ST and the “thermal” phase (I1-Fm) induced by strong continuous illumination reflect the same underlying process. Undoubtedly, the same microstates of PSII are formed upon illumination with strong continuous light as in a series of ST and, once QB has become double reduced, in both cases two possibilities remain: Protonation of QB= followed by inactivation of the acceptor side or PSII-CEF. When only part of PSII undergoes CEF, with every cycle the population of “inactive PSII” will increase, until in the end practically all PSII will be in the state S0QA−QB(vacant), characterized by maximal fluorescence yield, Fm.

In view of the very similar phenomenology of the I1-Fm fluorescence rise in PSII CC and the “thermal” I1-I2 phase in non-fractionated samples, such as chloroplasts, algae, cyanobacteria and leaves, the question arises whether also I1-I2 possibly could be explained by a combination of backcycling of electrons from QB= to the donor side (PSII-CEF) and transient PSII inactivation (when after protonation of QB= and release of PQH2 the QB binding site remains vacant). Such explanation would be in line with the generally accepted understanding that the “thermal” phase parallels accumulation of PQH2. Further experiments are required for a definite answer to this question.

Fv/Fm and the quantum yield of PSII

Presumably the most outstanding result of the present study are the surprisingly high values of Fm/F0 in the order of 20 (corresponding to Fv/Fm = 0.95) which are observed in the absence of artificial acceptors in our dimeric PSII CC preparations. In this context, related findings from time resolved fluorescence measurements are important. Van Amerongen and Croce (2013) reconstructed picosecond fluorescence kinetics of PSII CC (isolated from Thermosynechococcus) measured by Miloslavina et al. (2006) and van der Weij-de Wit et al. (2011), concluding that “charge separation occurs with an average time constant τ below 100 ps, leading to the relatively fast disappearance of the (fluorescence) signal. Since this time constant is much shorter than the normal excited-state lifetime of chlorophyll (typically several ns), this guarantees a high quantum efficiency of charge separation (ϕCS). The corresponding value is above 0.95, using the well-known relation ϕCS = 1 – τ/τChl (Croce and van Amerongen 2011), where τChl is the average lifetime of the excited Chl in the absence of charge separation.” Hence, there is close agreement between the Fv/Fm values determined by the PAM technique and by sophisticated time-resolved fluorescence techniques. As already mentioned above, besides the aspect of exceptionally high functional integrity of our PSII CC, a plausible reason for the surprisingly high Fv/Fm values is the lack of LHCII and the short distance between quanta absorption and trapping.

For reliable determination of Fv/Fm as a proxy of the photochemical quantum yield of PSII, not only F0, but also Fm have to be correctly determined. The results of our measurements using PSII CC in the absence of artificial acceptors demonstrate that this can be a difficult task, depending on the type and state of the investigated sample. While for the determination of F0 it is sufficient to ensure that QA is fully oxidized, the correct determination of Fm is significantly more demanding. This is because it must not only be ensured that QA is fully reduced, but also that the S2 and S3 states, which are inevitably formed at the PSII donor side during light-induced reduction of the PSII acceptor pool, are converted back to the original S0 and S1 states. Theoretically, Fv/Fm is a measure of PSII quantum yield only, when there is no change of the rate constant for non-radiative energy dissipation between assessment of F0 and Fm (Kitajima and Butler 1975). In common experiments with intact leaves or algae this is not a problem, as after proper dark acclimation the reactions downstream PSI are dark-inactivated, so that a strong multiple-turnover light pulse (so-called Saturation Pulse, SP) can flood the whole electron transport chain with electrons that also find their way to the PSII donor side. In this way, reduction of the PQ-pool is functionally coupled with the suppression of DQ, so that the true Fm can be reached. There are, however, conditions under which DQ cannot be readily suppressed. In the case of samples with damaged OEC, not only the re-reduction of oxidized YZ is slowed down, leading to increased DQ, but also SP-induced flooding of the acceptor pools does not work anymore. An example using heat-treated spinach chloroplasts was given in the experiment of Figs. 6 and 7 in Schreiber and Neubauer (1987). Similarly, even if the OEC is not damaged, at sub-freezing temperatures re-reduction of oxidized YZ is slowed down and electron flow beyond QA is suppressed. As shown in Figs. 5 and 6 in Neubauer and Schreiber (1987), this leads to suppression of the “thermal phases” of the polyphasic fluorescence rise upon onset of strong continuous illumination. In this case, suppression of DQ is “frozen in” and correct determination of the PSII quantum yield via Fv/Fm is not possible.

Very recently, Konyukhov et al. (2026) reexamined the Kitajima and Butler (1975) model for relating Chl fluorescence to the quantum yield of energy conversion in PSII. Based on experimental data from the literature, these authors came to the conclusion that “if the Fv/Fm measurements are made properly”, the value of photochemical quantum yield can indeed be estimated. While we fully agree with this conclusion, we note that the idea of a pivotal role of light driven DQ suppression for correct Fm determination is not considered in this communication. This idea is supported by our measurements using PSII CC and agrees with previous measurements carried out with the same experimental set-up using intact Chlorella cells (Klughammer et al. 2024).
