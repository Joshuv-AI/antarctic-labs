A model of free sialic acid storage disorder reveals hypomyelinating leukodystrophy and Purkinje cell degeneration
Authors: Mary E Hackbarth, Mahin S Hossain, Marya S Sabir, Petcharat Leoyklang, Heidi Dorward, John Douglas Burke, Gene Elliot, Lisa Garrett, Patricia Zerfas, Danielle A Springer, Laura Pollard, Stephen Wincovitch, Marjan Huizing, William A Gahl, May Christine V Malicdan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFree sialic acid storage disorder (FSASD) is a rare, neurodegenerative, lysosomal storage disease caused by biallelic variants in SLC17A5, which encodes the lysosomal sialic acid exporter, sialin. Although clinical severity varies, all affected individuals exhibit progressive neurological decline and characteristic lysosomal and urinary accumulation of free sialic acid. No US Food and Drug Administration-approved therapies currently exist, and the molecular underpinnings of central nervous system (CNS) dysfunction remain poorly defined. To model the most prevalent FSASD-associated allele, we generated a Slc17a5R39C mouse model carrying the pathogenic p.Arg39Cys variant using CRISPR/Cpf1. Homozygous Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice recapitulated key features of FSASD, including biochemical accumulation of free sialic acid, ataxia, tremors, seizures and shortened lifespan. Neuropathological analyses revealed widespread CNS hypomyelination, with relatively preserved peripheral nervous system myelination, while transcriptomic profiling showed broad suppression of myelin-associated genes and a cerebellum-specific inflammatory signature. This was accompanied by pronounced astrogliosis, progressive Purkinje cell degeneration and elevated neurofilament proteins, including serum neurofilament light polypeptide. Altogether, the Slc17a5R39C/R39C model of FSASD provides critical insights into disease pathogenesis, highlights region-specific neurodegenerative vulnerabilities, and establishes a robust preclinical platform for mechanistic investigation and therapeutic development.Keywords: Rare disorders, Sialic acid, Sialin, Myelin development, Purkinje cell loss

INTRODUCTION

Free sialic acid storage disorder (FSASD) is an autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disease caused by biallelic variants in SLC17A5 (Aula et al., 1979; Verheijen et al., 1999). This gene encodes sialin, a transmembrane symporter responsible for lysosomal egress of free sialic acid released from glycoconjugates and other acidic hexoses. FSASD encompasses a spectrum of clinical severity, ranging from the mild Salla disease to severe infantile forms (Verheijen et al., 1999; Huizing et al., 2021). This variability reflects a well-established genotype–phenotype correlation, wherein hypomorphic alleles, such as the common Finnish founder variant p.Arg39Cys, are associated with the milder form of the disease. Patients homozygous for this allele present with early-onset, slowly progressive neurological symptoms including intellectual disability, hypotonia, spasticity, seizures and ataxia, yet survive into adulthood with limited or no visceral organ involvement [Salla disease; Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) #604369] (Huizing et al., 2021; Aula et al., 2000). In contrast, individuals with null or severely deleterious alleles develop multi-organ pathology (infantile free sialic acid storage disorder; OMIM #269920) and die within the first few years of life (Huizing et al., 2021). The disorder's rarity and the nonspecific neurological features of FSASD make diagnostic delays common, also suggesting significant underdiagnosis in clinical populations (Huizing et al., 2021; Wreden et al., 2005; Adams et al., 1993).

Despite the identification of the fundamental role of sialin in central nervous system (CNS) integrity, no US Food and Drug Administration-approved therapies or clinical trials currently exist for FSASD (Huizing et al., 2021). The absence of genotype-representative models has hindered mechanistic understanding and therapeutic development. A previously generated Slc17a5-null mouse (Slc17a5Tm1Lex) recapitulated key neurologic phenotypes such as seizures and myelin defects, but its severe early lethality limited utility for studying disease progression and evaluating the timing and efficacy of therapies (Prolo et al., 2009; Stroobants et al., 2017).

To overcome these limitations, we generated a knock-in mouse model carrying the orthologous p.Arg39Cys variant, Slc17a5R39C/R39C, mirroring the most prevalent missense variant in patients with FSASD (Verheijen et al., 1999; Huizing et al., 2021; Aula et al., 2000). This model was designed to reflect the milder human disease course while offering sufficient lifespan to dissect spatiotemporal disease mechanisms and assess translational outcome measures.

Here, we present a comprehensive phenotypic, biochemical, transcriptomic and histological characterization of the Slc17a5R39C/R39C mouse model. We show that this model recapitulates core clinical features of FSASD, including CNS hypomyelination, motor dysfunction and elevated free sialic acid. Notably, we also identify cerebellar Purkinje cell vacuolization and degeneration, reactive astrogliosis and regional neuroinflammatory signatures. These findings establish the Slc17a5R39C/R39C mouse as a robust and tractable system for modeling FSASD pathophysiology and for evaluating future therapeutic interventions.

RESULTS

Generation and phenotypic characterization of Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice

We used CRISPR/Cpf1 to knock-in the orthologous p.Arg39Cys variant in the Slc17a5 murine locus (Fig. 1A; p.R39C). Sanger sequencing confirmed successful generation of the p.Arg39Cys variant (Fig. 1B). Gross morphological examination of Slc17a5R39C/R39C homozygous mice at 3 months revealed reduced body size and kyphosis compared to wild-type (WT) littermates (Fig. 1C), which was observable as early as postnatal week 3. Heterozygous Slc17a5R39C/+ in-crosses resulted in a Mendelian distribution of genotypes at birth and exhibited no perinatal lethality. In general, ∼65% of Slc17a5R39C/R39C homozygous mice (also referred to as R39C mice) survived at 90 days of age, with fewer than 25% surviving beyond 9 months (Fig. 1D). The median age of death for Slc17a5R39C/R39C homozygous mice was 134 days (4.5 months) or 157 days (5.2 months) for males or females, respectively (Table S1). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that lifespan reduction was independent of sex in mutants (Table S1). Approximately 25% of Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice were found dead in their cage or reached humane endpoint at ∼1.5 months of age owing to severe hindlimb paralysis, repeated seizures, respiratory distress, and/or lethargy and moribund appearance. Because seizure activity was spontaneous and transient, observations of these events are anecdotal and were not systematically quantified. Peripheral tissue histopathology of the liver, kidney, spleen and heart was generally unremarkable at both 3.5 and 9.5 months of age (Fig. S1). Heterozygous littermates were unaffected and indistinguishable from WT littermates throughout their lifetime.

To determine the molecular consequences of the Slc17a5 p.Arg39Cys substitution, we assessed the expression of Slc17a5 mRNA using two TaqMan gene expression assays, i.e. one at the exon 2-3 boundary upstream of the (c.115C>T) Arg39Cys variant and one at the exon 10-11 boundary downstream of the variant. Whole-brain extracts of homozygous and WT animals exhibited equivalent levels of Slc17a5 expression (Fig. 1E).

Free and total sialic acid was measured via liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS-MS). Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice exhibited significantly increased free sialic acid concentrations in serum (P<0.0053), plasma (P<0.0008) and urine (P<0.0001) that were approximately four, six and nine times those of WT littermates, respectively (Fig. 1F-H). Free sialic acid was significantly increased in urine (P<0.0115) and serum (P<0.0013), but not in plasma, of the Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice at this timepoint (Fig. 1I-K).

Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice exhibit motor coordination deficits and gait abnormalities

To assess the impact of the p.Arg39Cys variant on motor function, we subjected mice to a battery of neuromotor tests at 3 months of age. Initially, the mice were assessed by a broad observational screening platform – SmithKline Beecham, Harwell, Imperial College, Royal London Hospital, Phenotype Assessment (SHIRPA) (Lalonde et al., 2021) – in which Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice exhibited impaired coordination, tremor and dragging tail/belly while walking. A novel comprehensive ataxia screen was then designed to quantify the pronounced neurologic and ataxic phenotype. This assessment comprised eight tests, including ledge test, hindlimb clasping, gait, kyphosis, stance score, ataxia score, ladder traversal and horizontal beam. Each mouse was scored on each test, and a summative ataxia score was calculated for each animal (see Supplementary Materials and Methods for detailed testing procedures and scoring paradigm). The Ataxia Screen revealed dramatically elevated composite ataxia scores in Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2A).

Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice also exhibited significantly worse performance than that of WT littermates when challenged by RotaRod (Fig. 2B; P<0.0001), inverted grid (Fig. 2C; P<0.0001), negative geotaxis (Fig. 2D; P<0.0051) and wire hang (Fig. 2E; P<0.0001) assessments. On grip strength assessment, peak forelimb and hindlimb tension (Fig. 2F,G) appeared unaffected. Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice exhibited a clumsy, stumbling gait, compared to that of WT littermates. Detailed gait analysis, derived from footprint ink patterns, showed that, despite a normal stance width (Fig. 2H), stride length (Fig. 2I; P<0.0001) was reduced, and significant alterations in step angle (Fig. 2J; P<0.0001) and paw angle (Fig. 2K; P<0.0001) were quantified, consistent with the observed broad-based, irregular gait. These findings are apparent in representative footprint ink patterns (Fig. 2L), which show reduced stance uniformity and coordination in Slc17a5R39C/R39C compared to WT mice. Collectively, these data reveal motor decline, strongly implicating cerebellar and/or spinal circuit dysfunction.

Brain morphology and cytoarchitecture in Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice

To assess the impact of the p.Arg39Cys variant on brain morphology, Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was performed on sagittal brain sections at 3 months of age. Although gross brain (Fig. 3A) and spinal cord (Fig. S2Ai,ii) structure appeared preserved, detailed examination of cerebellar architecture revealed regional abnormalities in Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice (Fig. 3A), which were consistent across anterior and posterior lobules, suggesting widespread cerebellar involvement. Specifically, compared to WT controls, the Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice exhibited marked reduction in Purkinje cell density, especially in lobules I/II; sparse Purkinje cells were apparent within lobule X (Fig. 3B). Nissl staining of adjacent sections revealed no gross abnormalities in cortical or cerebellar cytoarchitecture (Fig. 3C). These results suggest a specific vulnerability of cerebellar Purkinje neurons to sialin dysfunction, occurring in the context of otherwise preserved neuronal organization.

Hypomyelination is prevalent in the central, but not peripheral, nervous system at 3 months

Given the prominent finding of hypomyelination in individuals with FSASD, we next evaluated whether white matter abnormalities were also present in the CNS. Luxol Fast Blue (LFB) staining of sagittal brain and spinal cord sections from 3-month-old Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice revealed reduced myelin density throughout the brain (Fig. 4Aii), particularly in the corpus callosum (Fig. 4Aiii,v) and cerebellar white matter (Fig. 4Aiv,vi), and both dorsal and lateral columns of the spinal cord (Fig. S2Av,vi), compared to that in WT littermates. To further assess myelination, we performed immunofluorescence (IF) for proteolipid protein (PLP), a major constituent of CNS myelin. In Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice, PLP signal intensity was diminished in the cortex, corpus callosum, cerebellum (Fig. 4Bii,iv,vi) and spinal cord (Fig. S2Bii) compared to that in WT littermates (Fig. 4i,iii,v; Fig. S2Bi), reinforcing the presence of CNS hypomyelination. PLP expression in Slc17a5R39C/R39C brains (Fig. 4) and spinal cords (Fig. S2B-F) was prevalent within demarcated axonal tracts within the white matter but failed to distribute evenly throughout cortical areas and spinal cord gray matter. In contrast, PLP antigenicity in WT brains appeared more diffuse and distributed as thin floss-like filaments rather than punctate tracts (Fig. 4B; Fig. S2B). Quantification of PLP-positive signal supported trends visually observed in microscopy imaging. Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice demonstrated significantly reduced integrated density of PLP IF in the corpus callosum (Fig. S3A; P<0.0001), cerebellar white matter (Fig. S3C; P<0.0001) and major white matter tracts of the spinal cord (Fig. S2C; P<0.0001), consistent with reduced overall myelin protein abundance. In parallel, Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice also exhibited significantly reduced percentage area occupied by PLP-positive signal in the same regions (Fig. S2D and Fig. S3B,D; P<0.0001), further demonstrating that a smaller proportion of tissue contained detectable myelin in mutant mice than in WT littermates.

To further quantify the dramatic differences observed via immunohistochemistry, we performed immunoblotting for myelin basic protein (MBP), a key structural component of compact myelin, in brain hemispheres (Fig. 5A) and spinal cord tissue (Fig. 5E) from 3-month-old WT and Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice. Consistent with our histological findings, densitometric analysis confirmed significant reduction of MBP signal in the left hemisphere (Fig. 5C; P<0.0001) and spinal cord (Fig. 5G; P<0.0001) of mutant mice compared to that in WT littermates, supporting observations of widespread CNS hypomyelination at the protein level. To evaluate whether myelination deficits were secondary to axonal loss, we probed for neurofilament light polypeptide (NFL; also known as NEFL), a cytoskeletal protein enriched in axons (Fig. 5B,F). Interestingly, NFL levels were increased in the brains of Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice compared to those in WT littermates (Fig. 5D; P<0.0275), and significant differences in expression were seen especially within the spinal cord (Fig. 5H; P<0.0012). Although brain lysates appeared to have a single predominant NFL isoform (Fig. 5B), spinal cord lysates from both genotypes demonstrated immunoreactivity of multiple bands around the expected NFL molecular mass of 68 kDa (Fig. 5F). Irrespective of size, the intensities of all expressed bands in the spinal cord were greater in Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice than in WT controls (Fig. 5F). Although densitometric analyses in Fig. 5 correspond to the representative western blots displayed, independent repeat experiments performed with additional biological replicates and on separate blots consistently demonstrated the same genotype-dependent differences in MBP and NFL expression.

To assess ultrastructural consequences of these findings, we examined myelin morphology via transmission electron microscopy (TEM). In WT animals, compact and uniformly wrapped myelin sheaths were observed in the cervical spinal cord, optic nerve and sciatic nerve. In contrast, Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice displayed disorganization of myelin lamellae in the cervical spinal cord and optic nerve (Fig. S4A,B). Despite the presence of myelin, affected axons exhibited irregular compaction and decompaction of the myelin sheath. Axonal swellings, often filled with dense accumulations of vesicular or granular material, were frequently observed in both the optic nerve and spinal cord of mutant mice and could be suggestive of impaired axonal transport or axon–glial dysregulation (Marrone et al., 2022; Duncan et al., 2021). In contrast, peripheral myelinated fibers in the sciatic nerve retained largely intact architecture, although subtle abnormalities such as myelin infoldings and occasional axoplasmic degeneration were observed (Fig. S4C), suggesting that CNS regions are disproportionately affected. Of note, no quantitative measurements were done.

Transcriptomic profiling of Slc17a5R39C/R39C brain highlights global disruption of myelination pathways and regional transcriptomic signatures

To elucidate the molecular underpinnings of these white matter changes, we conducted bulk RNA sequencing on three brain regions: left hemisphere, right cerebellum and right forebrain. Principal component analysis and unsupervised clustering demonstrated clear segregation by both genotype and brain region (Fig. S5A-C), confirming robust transcriptomic alterations. Differential expression analysis revealed 792 significantly dysregulated genes in the hemisphere (Fig. 6A), 688 in the cerebellum (Fig. 6B) and 101 in the forebrain (Fig. S6A). Although some differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were shared between regions, most were spatially confined, emphasizing the heterogeneity of transcriptomic disruption (Fig. S7). Among the most dysregulated transcripts across regions was the myelin-associated gene Opalin (Fig. 6A,B; Fig. S6A), a central nervous system-specific gene encoding an oligodendrocytic myelin paranodal and inner loop protein (Teng et al., 2024), which was profoundly downregulated in both hemisphere and cerebellum.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment of DEGs revealed consistent downregulation of oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelination programs in all three regions, including terms related to myelination, axon ensheathment and oligodendrocyte differentiation (hemisphere, Fig. S8A; cerebellum, Fig. S9A; forebrain, Fig. S6B). In the cerebellum, upregulated transcripts were strongly enriched for immune-related GO terms (Fig. S9A), and cell-type enrichment analysis showed elevated microglial signatures (Fig. S9B) compared to hemisphere (Fig. S8B) and forebrain (Fig. S6C) regions, suggesting a spatially confined neuroimmune response.

Focusing on canonical myelination genes, several including Mag, Mog and Mal were consistently downregulated in both the hemisphere and cerebellum (Fig. S10). Notably, Mbp was the most abundantly expressed gene across the dataset (Fig. S5C), yet showed marked reduction in Slc17a5R39C/R39C brain. Together, these data demonstrate that the Slc17a5 R39C variant disrupts oligodendrocyte gene expression and induces regional glial reactivity, particularly within the cerebellum, thereby offering mechanistic insight into the spatially selective white matter pathology observed in this model. The full dataset of myelination gene expression results is available in Table S2.

Cell-type composition varies by brain region with modest genotype-associated effects

To examine whether the Slc17a5 p.R39C mutation influences the cellular composition of the brain in the knock-in mouse model, we performed cell-type deconvolution using the MuSiC algorithm and Zeisel Brain Data resource as the single-cell reference dataset.

Cellular composition varied by region and genotype (Fig. 6C; Figs S12 and S13). The cerebellum exhibited lower neuronal proportions and higher astrocyte/ependymal and endothelial contributions relative to forebrain, which demonstrated the highest neuronal representation (Fig. 6C; Fig. S13). When stratified by genotype, knock-in mouse cerebellum demonstrated increased microglia, slightly elevated astrocyte/ependymal and decreased oligodendrocyte proportions relative to WT mice (Fig. 6C). Altogether, these results indicate that brain region is a significant driver of cellular composition, while the p.R39C mutation is associated with modest changes in glial cell proportions.

Purkinje cell vacuolization precedes degeneration in Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice

To assess the onset and progression of Purkinje cell loss, we performed IF staining for calbindin D-28K, a Purkinje neuron-specific marker, on sagittal cerebellar sections from Slc17a5R39C/R39C and WT mice at postnatal day (P)21 and 3 months of age (Fig. 7A). Most Purkinje cell development occurs within the first three postnatal weeks of life in mice, at which time dendrites are formed and synaptic connections are established (Sotelo and Dusart, 2009; Dusart and Flamant, 2012). These cells reach maturity by P21; therefore, this intermediate timepoint was chosen to assess Purkinje cell morphology and counts. At P21, Purkinje cell density and organization were largely preserved in mutant mice, although subtle regional differences were noted (Fig. 7A). By 3 months, however, Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice exhibited a dramatic reduction in calbindin D-28K-positive Purkinje neurons compared to WT, with loss most pronounced in anterior (lobules I-V) and central (VI-VII) lobules, and relative preservation in the nodular zone (lobule X) (Fig. 7B). This anterior to posterior gradient is typically seen in cerebellar disorders (Sarna and Hawkes, 2003; Sarna et al., 2003) involving progressive, regionally extensive degeneration. Quantification of Purkinje cell linear density confirmed a significant reduction in 3-month-old mutant mice compared to that in age-matched WT mice (Fig. 7C; P<0.0001). No significant differences were observed at P21, indicating that Purkinje neuron degeneration emerges between weaning and early adulthood.

At higher magnification, Purkinje cells from Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice at P21 already demonstrated profound vacuolization, with many vacuoles measuring over 4-5 μm in diameter (Fig. 7B, arrowheads). Although lobule X consistently had the highest linear density of Purkinje cells at 3 months of age, these straggler cells were distinctively vacuolated.

To assess whether cerebellar damage is associated with neuroaxonal degeneration that can be detected on a systemic level, we measured levels of NFL, one of three obligate components of the axonal cytoskeleton, in the serum. NFL is released into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood following neuroaxonal injury and is therefore a sensitive biomarker of axonal injury across a broad spectrum of neurodegenerative diseases (Khalil et al., 2018). Although NFL levels were modestly elevated in mutant mice at 1 month of age, a significant increase in these levels was observed by 3 months (Fig. 7D), supporting ongoing neurodegeneration in the Slc17a5R39C/R39C model.

Widespread astrogliosis in Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice at 3 months

Because the cerebellum-specific inflammatory transcriptional profile suggested regionally selective reactive astrogliosis, a hallmark of neuroinflammatory injury, we next sought to directly evaluate the extent of astrocytic reactivity in the Slc17a5R39C/R39C model. We performed IF staining for GFAP, a canonical marker of reactive astrocytes, across sagittal brain (Fig. 8Ai,Bi) and cervical spinal cord sections (Fig. S2B). Compared to WT littermates, Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice exhibited widespread and markedly elevated GFAP immunoreactivity throughout the brain (Fig. 8Bi). Higher-magnification images revealed increased astrocytic labeling in multiple regions of the Slc17a5R39C/R39C brain, including the cortex (Fig. 8Bii), hippocampus (Fig. 8Biii), cerebellar white matter (Fig. 8Biv) and molecular layer of the cerebellum (Fig. 8Bv). In the hippocampus of Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice, strong GFAP signal was localized to the CA1 and dentate gyrus regions, indicative of focal gliosis. In the cerebellum, pronounced astrocytic hypertrophy and intense GFAP staining were observed in both the molecular and granule cell layers, particularly surrounding Purkinje cell bodies and dendritic arbors, and in the deep cerebellar nuclei, suggesting localized injury and glial reactivity in this vulnerable region. Despite qualitatively apparent greater GFAP positivity in Slc17a5R39C/R39C tissue, quantification of IF demonstrated increased integrated density and percentage area of GFAP-positive signal only in the cerebellar molecular layer (Fig. 8I,J; P<0.0001), with no significant differences seen between genotypes in the corpus callosum (Fig. 8C,D), hippocampal CA1 region (Fig. 8E,F) or cerebellar white matter (Fig. 8G,H).

Interestingly, although there were no visible differences in the staining patterns or fluorescence intensities of GFAP in the cervical spinal cords of Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice compared to those of WT mice (Fig. S2B), quantitative assessments determined Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice as having mildly increased integrated density of GFAP IF (Fig. S2E; P<0.05) and increased percentage area of tissue occupied by GFAP-positive signal (Fig. S2F; P<0.01) compared to WT mice. These findings confirmed that the p.Arg39Cys mutation induces robust and regionally patterned, rather than global, reactive astrogliosis. This astrogliosis agrees with transcriptomic evidence of neuroinflammation and is consistent with the broader neurodegenerative phenotype observed in this FSASD mouse model.

DISCUSSION

We report the generation and characterization of a novel CRISPR/Cpf1-engineered knock-in mouse model of FSASD, harboring the Slc17a5 p.Arg39Cys (R39C; Slc17a5R39C/R39C) homozygous variant. This model reflects the variant most commonly associated with the mildest clinical form of FSASD, also known as Salla disease, and recapitulates key biochemical, behavioral and neuropathological features of the human condition.

The Slc17a5R39C/R39C mouse showed significantly elevated levels of free sialic acid in urine, serum and plasma, confirming lysosomal export impairment consistent with sialin dysfunction. Notably, these mice exhibited no overt pathology in peripheral organs – such as the liver, kidney, spleen or heart – highlighting a relevant distinction from the previously reported Slc17a5Tm1Lex model (Stroobants et al., 2017). This is consistent with the CNS-predominant nature of mild FSASD (Salla disease) and further supports the construct validity of the model. Neurologically, Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice displayed motor impairments – including tremors, ataxia and seizures – accompanied by coordination deficits across multiple behavioral paradigms. Among these, the neurobehavioral battery (ataxia screen) proved to be a particularly efficient and reproducible readout of disease progression and may serve as a reliable outcome measure in future therapeutic studies. Grip strength assessment revealed that Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice had normal strength in both forepaws and hindpaws, supporting that the gait abnormalities may result from a neurological etiology rather than muscular degeneration.

Our neuropathological analysis revealed a striking CNS-specific hypomyelination phenotype, corroborating clinical findings in FSASD patients (Barmherzig et al., 2017; Haataja et al., 1994; Sonninen et al., 1999; Biancheri et al., 2004; Linnankivi et al., 2003) and prior sialin knockout mouse models (Prolo et al., 2009; Stroobants et al., 2017). Myelin deficits were especially pronounced in the cerebellum, cerebral white matter, corpus callosum and spinal cord, while the peripheral nervous system was relatively spared. Notably, myelin ultrastructure in the optic nerve and cervical spinal cord of Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice revealed poorly compacted sheaths, vesicular axonal swellings and disrupted lamellae, which are hallmarks of impaired myelin maintenance (Steyer et al., 2020). However, axonal content appeared largely intact, suggesting a primarily oligodendrocyte-driven pathology.

Although the exact timing of hypomyelination onset was not assessed in this study, previous work suggests that sialin-deficient mice exhibit delayed myelination within the first two postnatal weeks (Prolo et al., 2009). This timeline coincides with a period of rapid oligodendrocyte maturation and the production and deposition of myelin in all major CNS white matter tracts. Thus, the presence of intact, but hypomyelinated, CNS tracts in Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice offers a tractable platform for testing remyelination strategies during this window of developmental plasticity, perhaps through recently emerging pro-myelinating small molecule drugs (Al Jaf et al., 2024), gene complementation or gene-editing strategies (Harb et al., 2023). Such therapeutic maneuvers, with appropriately targeted constructs and vectors, could have discernible, positive impacts on myelination (Jeon et al., 2024), especially that which occurs postnatally in mice since oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPC) differentiation begins within the first week of life (Nishiyama et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2023).

Transcriptomic profiling further supported disruption of myelin biology, revealing consistent downregulation of genes involved in myelination and oligodendrocyte differentiation, including Mbp, Mag, Mog and Mal, as well as Opalin, one of the most significantly dysregulated transcripts across brain regions. GO analysis indicated the presence of defects in oligodendrocyte maturation and axon ensheathment. These findings suggest that sialin deficiency impairs oligodendroglial function through both cell-autonomous and potentially non-cell-autonomous mechanisms, such as defective neuron–glia signaling or aberrant axon–glial interactions (Duncan et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2023).

To better contextualize these findings, we compared this phenotype with other lysosomal and neurodegenerative disorders. The Slc17a5R39C/R39C model is notable not because FSASD uniquely involves cerebellar or white matter pathology, but because it combines selective cerebellar neuronal vulnerability with widespread CNS myelin/oligodendrocyte disruption. Similar to Niemann–Pick type C (NPC), mutant mice exhibit Purkinje cell degeneration with an anterior-to-posterior gradient (Elrick et al., 2010). However, unlike NPC, for which microglial activation is an early and dominant feature (Cougnoux et al., 2020), our transcriptomic data highlight a strong and consistent suppression of myelination-related pathways across brain regions, with a more regionally restricted inflammatory signature.

Other lysosomal disorders further underscore this distinction. GM1 gangliosidosis is characterized primarily by neuronal and metabolic pathway alterations (Liu et al., 2024), while multiple sulfatase deficiency presents as a severe multisystem disease with astrocyte-driven neurodegeneration (Settembre et al., 2007). In metachromatic leukodystrophy, Arsa-null mice display gliosis and storage pathology but lack robust CNS demyelination (Hess et al., 1996). In contrast, the Slc17a5R39C/R39C model demonstrates CNS-predominant myelin involvement with relative preservation of peripheral nerve myelination, consistent with prior sialin-deficient models (Prolo et al., 2009; Stroobants et al., 2017).

Comparison with other disorders of sialic acid metabolism further highlights the specificity of this phenotype. GNE myopathy primarily affects skeletal muscle (Carrillo et al., 2018; Malicdan et al., 2007), whereas sialidosis involves altered lysosomal exocytosis and neuroimmune dysregulation (d'Azzo et al., 2015; Fremuth et al., 2025), and sialuria reflects dysregulated sialic acid synthesis rather than lysosomal storage (Martinez et al., 2019). Together, these comparisons support the conclusion that FSASD is characterized by a combination of CNS-restricted hypomyelination, cerebellar vulnerability and regionally accentuated neuroinflammation, distinguishing it from other lysosomal disorders.

The cerebellum emerged as a region of heightened vulnerability. Transcriptomic signatures revealed enriched neuroimmune activation and increased expression of microglial and astrocytic gene programs. These patterns were validated histologically by the presence of robust GFAP-positive astrogliosis, with hypertrophic astrocytes concentrated around vulnerable Purkinje neurons. Calbindin D-28K staining revealed progressive Purkinje cell degeneration in the Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice, with early vacuolization detectable at P21 and pronounced cell loss by 3 months. The anterior-to-posterior gradient of degeneration parallels what is observed in other cerebellar ataxias and lysosomal disorders such as NPC (Walterfang et al., 2020; Cougnoux et al., 2018; Baudry et al., 2003; Kunkel et al., 2023; Ko et al., 2005), and suggests a convergent vulnerability of Purkinje cells to lysosomal stress and demyelination.

Whether Purkinje cell loss results from intrinsic sialin deficiency or from secondary processes, such as hypomyelination (Kunkel et al., 2023), astrocyte reactivity or neuroimmune dysregulation, remains an open question. Future studies leveraging cell type-specific knockouts and early developmental transcriptomics will be crucial for dissecting these mechanisms. Still, the absence of frank neuronal loss at P21 implies a potential therapeutic window during which Purkinje cell integrity could be preserved with timely intervention.

Neurofilament findings provide an additional lens on axon–glia interactions in this model. Neurofilament proteins, including NFL, NFM (also known as NEFM) and NFH (also known as NEFH),­ are critically dependent on autophagy–lysosome pathways for degradation (Rao et al., 2023). NFL elevation in whole hemisphere and spinal cord lysates of Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice suggests impaired neurofilament turnover, a process that can be tightly linked to autophagy–lysosome function. Although these assays do not resolve the precise anatomical origin of the NFL changes, the more pronounced increase in NFL in spinal cord relative to that in brain hemisphere is consistent with the high metabolic and structural demands of long spinal axons. Differential abundance of NFL-immunoreactive peptides of varying molecular masses, especially in Slc17a5R39C/R39C spinal cord lysates, raises further questions about the role of post-translational modifications on the stability and persistence of neurofilament proteins in this model, as has been reported in other neurodegenerative disorders (Wang et al., 2001; Liu et al., 2011).

Additionally, the more observable increased NFL expression in Slc17a5R39C/R39C spinal cord relative to that in the left-brain hemisphere likely reflects heightened regional vulnerability of spinal motor pathways. Although neurofilament pathology and defects in axonal transport are well documented in motor neuron disease (Liu et al., 2011), the spinal cord, in general, also has greater demands for neurofilament content and turnover than the brain owing to its composition of long, heavily myelinated axons. Importantly, serum NFL was elevated in mutant mice by 1-3 months, in line with ongoing axonal injury and cytoskeletal dysregulation. Together with the Purkinje cell pathology, these findings support the view that FSASD involves both myelin-specific and broader axonal vulnerabilities, even if the precise regional sources of NFL accumulation remain to be clarified.

Interestingly, our results demonstrated normal gross morphology and myelination of the sciatic nerve. However, peripheral dysmyelination, a clinical correlate of polyneuropathy, has been reported in Salla disease in the literature with variable neurologic presentations (Varho et al., 2000, 2002). Given these clinical features and the indisputable link between sialin function and CNS myelination, further studies are warranted to investigate what role sialin serves in the peripheral nervous system. Comprehensive analysis of peripheral nerves and neuromuscular function may uncover additional disease mechanistic clues.

The survival of Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice into adulthood provides a unique opportunity for longitudinal and interventional studies. Future work will leverage this window to test therapeutic strategies such as gene therapy or small-molecule approaches, particularly during early postnatal stages when myelination is still ongoing.

The Slc17a5R39C/R39C model provides a valuable platform for dissecting the molecular mechanisms of FSASD and for evaluating therapeutic strategies targeting sialin restoration, oligodendrocyte precursor maturation, remyelination, neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation. Notably, compared with other lysosomal storage disease models, this model demonstrates a pronounced cerebellar inflammatory signature with microglial enrichment and reduced oligodendrocyte-associated transcripts, supporting a central role for glial dysfunction in disease pathogenesis. These features may have broader relevance to other leukodystrophies and lysosomal disorders characterized by disrupted glial–neuronal crosstalk and white matter vulnerability.

In sum, the Slc17a5R39C/R39C model is distinguished by the combination of CNS-restricted hypomyelination, selective cerebellar vulnerability and regionally accentuated neuroinflammation, providing a framework to study FSASD pathogenesis that is not fully recapitulated in other lysosomal disease models.

Limitations

Although the Slc17a5R39C/R39C mouse model recapitulates multiple aspects of the human FSASD phenotype and provides a valuable platform for mechanistic and translational studies, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the precise temporal dynamics of disease onset and progression remain incompletely defined. Although we observed Purkinje cell vacuolization by P21 and degeneration by 3 months, it is unclear whether gliosis, hypomyelination or transcriptional alterations precede or follow these events. Longitudinal studies with earlier timepoints, including transcriptomic and proteomic profiling before overt pathology, will be essential to establish causality and define therapeutic windows.

Second, the mechanisms underlying CNS-selective hypomyelination remain to be fully elucidated. Our findings suggest oligodendrocyte maturation defects, yet the specific cell-intrinsic or -extrinsic drivers, such as axon–glia signaling deficits or lysosomal dysfunction, require further exploration. In particular, conditional knockouts targeting Slc17a5 in oligodendrocytes or Purkinje neurons could help resolve the relative contributions of each cell type to CNS pathology (Elrick et al., 2010; Wan et al., 2020). Additionally, CNS myelination was only assessed at a single timepoint, at which NFL levels (on immunoblots and in serum) were already significantly elevated. Further investigation of early postnatal timepoints, including P7-P14, when OPC differentiation and myelination are actively occurring (Prolo et al., 2009), are warranted to determine whether hypomyelination precedes or occurs independently of axonal loss.

Third, although qualitative histological assessment suggested increased GFAP staining in mutant brains, these differences were not consistently captured by regional fluorescence quantification. This likely reflects a combination of limited sampling, regional variability in astrogliosis, and the possibility that differences in staining distribution or intensity were heterogeneous and not fully resolved by the quantitative approach used.

Lastly, this model was maintained on a B6;S129 hybrid background, which appeared to influence both survival and phenotypic severity. In this cohort, ∼25% of homozygous mutant mice developed a severe neurological phenotype with early mortality (∼1.5-2 months), whereas another subset survived beyond 9 months with milder disease. This variability suggests that genetic background modifies disease expression, consistent with prior observations in other lysosomal disorders such as NPC. To address this, the model has been backcrossed onto a C57BL/6 background for future studies, which will improve interpretability and reduce background-related variability.

Taken together, these limitations highlight important areas for future investigation, particularly in defining temporal disease dynamics, cell type-specific mechanisms and the contribution of genetic modifiers.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Constructs and generation of Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice

Mutant 129S;B6J-GE(Slc17a5)R39C (Slc17a5R39C/R39C) mice were generated by utilizing CRISPR/Cpf1 genome editing to create the p.Arg39Cys (c.115C>T+c.117G>C) missense variant in exon 2 of the murine Slc17a5 gene (NM_172773) (Fig. 1A,B).

A guide RNA (5′-AGCTCCAGTGTGCTGCTCTGCTC-3′) was designed and synthesized to introduce a double-stranded break between nucleotides 111 and 115 of exon 2 (Integrated DNA Technologies). A single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotide (ssODN) was designed as donor template to introduce the c.115C>T and c.117G>C nucleotide substitutions, converting the WT arginine (CGG) codon to a cysteine (TGC) codon (ATCATATAATTTAAAGTTTTCTCTCCATCTTAATTTCAGCTCCAGTGTGCTGCTCTGCT TGCTACAACTTAGCGATTTTGGCGTTCTGTGGTTTCTTCGTTCTCTATGCCTTACGGGTGA). The donor sequence also included synonymous substitutions to prevent re-cutting and facilitate genotyping.

Pronuclear injections were performed using standard procedures. Fertilized eggs were collected from superovulated 129S1/SvImJ (The Jackson Laboratory) females ∼9 h after mating with C57BL/6J males (The Jackson Laboratory). Co-injection of Cpf1 mRNA, guide RNA and the ssODN donor template was performed into the male pronucleus of zygotes using a capillary needle with a 1-2 μm opening (pulled using a Sutter P-1000 micropipette puller). Injections were carried out using a FemtoJet 4i microinjector (Eppendorf SE, Germany) under continuous flow. Upon confirmation of pronuclear swelling, the needle was withdrawn through the cytoplasm, likely resulting in a small amount of additional RNA delivery to the cytosol. Injected embryos were then surgically transferred into pseudo-pregnant CD-1 recipient females.

Nine injection sessions yielded 111 pups, with one found dead prior to genotyping. PCR and Sanger sequencing identified 82 WT pups, of which 26 had off-target or undesired edits, including 15 with a 5 bp deletion (del5), two mosaic founders (WT/del5/R39C) and one compound heterozygous mouse (del5/R39C), which was used to establish the colony. Founder mice were bred to C57BL/6;129S hybrid mice. Breeding pairs of heterozygous mice (Slc17a5R39C/+) were intercrossed to generate homozygous (Slc17a5R39C/R39C) mice and WT (Slc17a5+/+) littermates for phenotypic and molecular characterization.

Animal husbandry and procedures

All mice had water and food available ad libitum and were maintained under a standard 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. All procedures were performed according to National Institutes of Health guidelines, and in accordance with the Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC) guidelines in an approved facility. All procedures were approved by the National Human Genome Research Institute (NHGRI) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) under protocol G-14-5 (Mouse Models for Disorders of Sialylation).

Genotyping

2-mm tail snip biopsies were collected from 12- to 16-day-old mice, and genomic DNA was extracted using an Extract-N-Amp Tissue PCR Kit (XNAT2-1KT, Sigma-Aldrich), as per the manufacturer's protocol. Genotyping was performed by one of two methods, both of which were used to detect the c.115C>T+c.117G>C missense variants. The first utilized PCR amplification using primers listed in Table S3 followed by Sanger sequencing. The second utilized a custom Taqman SNP genotyping assay.

Sialic acid measurement

Blood and urine samples were collected in tandem, when possible, and constituted a terminal procedure. Mice were anesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol (Avertin), and peritoneal cavity was disrupted to access organs. Up to 1.5 ml whole blood was collected from the abdominal aorta of sacrificed animals using a heparin-treated hypodermic needle and syringe. Blood samples were collected on ice and microcentrifuged to separate components. Supernatant serum was collected for analysis. Urine was collected from bladders or anesthetized animals using a hypodermic needle and syringe. Urine samples were collected on ice and microcentrifuged to separate debris. Supernatant urine was collected in a clean tube for analysis. Samples were sent to Greenwood Genetic Center (Greenwood, SC, USA) for analysis. Sialic acid was measured via LC-MS-MS. For urine sialic acid quantification, total and free sialic acid were normalized to creatinine levels.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR

3-month-old R39C homozygote animals presenting with ataxia and WT littermates were anesthetized with tribromoethanol. Left lateral liver lobes as well as whole brains were collected and flash frozen on dry ice to preserve mRNA. RNA was isolated from frozen tissues using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74104) and RNAse-free DNase Set (Qiagen, 79656) according to the manufacturer's protocols. RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using a High-Capacity RN-to-cDNA™ Kit (Applied Biosystems, 4387406) on a C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad). Quantitative PCR was performed using TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, 436914), and TaqMan Gene Expression Assays for Slc17a5 and two normalization controls (Rn18s and Actb) on a 7500 Fast Real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Two TaqMan assays were utilized: one upstream of the p.Arg39Cys variant location across the exon 2-3 boundary, the other downstream of the variant encompassing the exon 10-11 boundary.

Mouse behavioral analysis

Slc17a5R39C/R39C and WT littermates were assessed at 3 months of age for neuromuscular phenotyping. The testing cohort included 13 homozygotes (three female, ten male) and 14 WT (five female, nine male). Animals were allowed to acclimatize to housing conditions for 1 week before any testing was carried out. The following tests were performed and detailed in the Supplementary Materials and Methods: neuromuscular assessment battery test (ataxia screen), RotaRod evaluation, wire hang, negative geotaxis, inverted grid, grip strength and footprint ink test (gait assessment).

Visceral organ histology

WT and Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice of both sexes were anesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol (Avertin) and euthanized by disruption of the descending aorta after opening the peritoneal cavity.

For analysis at 3.5 months of age, the left lateral lobe of the liver, left kidney and intact heart were collected. Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight, processed and embedded in paraffin. Transverse and coronal 5 μm paraffin sections were cut, mounted on glass slides, and stained with H&E or periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) using standard histology procedures.

For analysis at 9.5 months of age, the left lateral lobe of the liver and spleen were collected, fixed in 4% PFA overnight and dehydrated by immersion in serial 15% to 30% sucrose solutions. Tissues were then embedded into optimal cutting temperature (OCT) medium and frozen at −80°C. Transverse frozen sections of 10 μm thickness were cut, mounted on glass slides, and stained with PAS or Oil Red O using standard histology procedures. Tissue processing, embedding, sectioning and histological staining described above were performed by Histoserv, Inc (Germantown, MD, USA).

X-ray imaging

High-resolution radiographs were obtained using a Faxitron® cabinet X-ray system (ultra-high resolution model; Faxitron, Tucson, AZ, USA) housed at NHGRI. WT and Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice were anesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol (Avertin) prior to imaging and positioned on a transparent plastic support within the X-ray chamber. Exposure parameters were optimized for adult mice, typically using 35 kVp and 10-15 s exposure time at 3× magnification. Images were captured on FujiFilm® computer radiography image plates (CR XL-1, FujiFilm, Tokyo, Japan), which were subsequently processed and digitized using the FujiFilm® CR console. Image contrast and gradation were adjusted as needed using built-in QA tools, and final radiographs were exported in high-resolution format for analysis.

TEM

Mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol and euthanized by disruption of the descending aorta as described above. Samples were collected from the optic nerve, thoracic spinal cord and sciatic nerve. All tissues were cut to 1 mm3 and fixed (2% glutaraldehyde/1% PFA in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, pH 7.4) for 48 h at 4°C before being washed with cacodylate buffer three times. Tissues were then fixed with 1% OsO4 for 2 h, washed with 0.1 M cacodylate buffer three times, washed once with water and placed in 1% uranyl acetate for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were subsequently serially dehydrated in ethanol and propylene oxide and embedded in EMBed 812 resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA). Thin sections, ∼80 nm, were obtained by utilizing an ultracut-UCT ultramicrotome (Leica, Deerfield, IL, USA), after which they were placed onto 300 mesh copper grids and stained with saturated uranyl acetate in 50% methanol followed by lead citrate. The grids were viewed in a JEM-1200EXII electron microscope (JEOL Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) at 80 kV, and images were recorded with a XR611M, mid-mounted, 10.5 M pixel, CCD camera (Advanced Microscopy Techniques Corp., Danvers, MA, USA).

CNS histology and IF

Mixed-sex mice of both mutant and WT genotypes were euthanized at either 3 weeks (P21) or 3 months (P90) of age with tribromoethanol and intracardially perfused with 10 ml 1× PBS followed by 10 ml 4% PFA. The brain and cervical spinal cord were removed and fixed in 4% PFA at 4°C for 48 h. Spinal cords, which were still in bone, were decalcified overnight using a formic acid-based solution. Both tissues were then processed, embedded in paraffin, cut into 5 μm-thick midsagittal or cross-sections, respectively, and mounted on glass slides.

Tissue processing, embedding, sectioning and histological staining were performed by Histoserv, Inc. (Germantown, MD, USA). For histological analysis, brain and spinal cord sections were stained for H&E, Nissl and LFB using standard histology protocols.

For IF, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized in Hemo-De Xylene Substitute (Electron Microscopy Services, Hatfield, PA, USA), rehydrated and then subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval in 0.01 M sodium citrate, pH 6.4, for 15 min. Sections were blocked and permeabilized [4% donkey serum/2% Triton X-100/1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS] for 1 h at room temperature followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in 1× PBS. The next day, slides were washed twice in PBS-T (0.2% BSA/0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS), once in 1× PBS, and then incubated with the appropriate Alexa Fluor (AF)-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Slides were again rinsed in PBS-T followed by 1× PBS as described above, mounted with VECTASHIELD Antifade Mounting Medium DAPI (H-1200-10, Vector Laboratories) and sealed with clear nail polish. Information regarding the antibodies used in this section and their dilutions is available in Table S3.

Light microscopy

24-bit images of visceral organ histology were obtained using a Zeiss Axio Scan.Z1 Slide Scanner (Zeiss Microscopy, Jena, Germany) equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 40×/0.95 Korr M27 objective scaled at 0.110 μm×0.110 μm (per pixel).

36-bit images of CNS histology slides were obtained using an AxioObserver.Z1/7 microscope (Zeiss Microscopy). Whole brain overviews were obtained using an EC Plan-Neofluar 10×/0.30 NA M27 objective at 0.345 μm×0.345 μm scaling (per pixel), whereas higher-magnification regions of interest (ROIs; such as cerebellar lobules or cortical areas) were imaged using a Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.40 NA oil DIC objective at 0.055 μm×0.055 μm scaling (per pixel). Whole cervical spinal cord cross-sections were imaged using an EC Plan-Neofluar 40×/1.30 NA oil objective at 0.086 μm×0.086 μm scaling (per pixel). All images had 10% overlap between adjacent tiles. ZEN Blue v3.5.093.00002 software was used to stitch images with the following parameters: fuse tiles/no shading correction/7% minimal overlap/7% maximal shift. For each stain, image acquisition settings were kept the same regardless of genotype or timepoint.

Widefield microscopy

Midsagittal brain and cervical spinal cord sections from mixed-sex mice of both genotypes (Slc17a5R39C/R39C and WT) stained for PLP or GFAP were imaged using a widefield system (Nikon Instruments Inc., Melville, NY, USA) mounted on an inverted TiE microscope with a Nikon Plan Fluor 40×/1.30 NA oil (immersion oil type A, 1.518 reference index) objective lens. All images were acquired using a pco.edge sCMOS 4.2 camera with 1×1 binning, 16-bit, and a 2000 pixel×2000 pixel imaging field (each tile; with 5% overlap). The number of tiles (∼600 for brain and 50 for spinal cord) needed to capture the entire brain or spinal cord was calculated. The z-stack range was set to 10 μm with a step size of 0.3 μm, totaling 35 steps. AF488 Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) (40 ms), AF633 Goat anti-Chicken IgG (H+L) (70 ms) and DAPI (60 ms) excitations were collected in emission filters 525/36 nm, 705/72 nm and 455/50 nm, respectively. All images were collected using the same settings. Extended depth of focus (EDF) and shading corrections were applied (shading during acquisition and during the stitching process) in Nikon's Elements software package version 5.21.03 for acquisition and 6.10.02 for processing. The final processing step was to convert the EDF processed/stitched/shading corrected Nikon file (∼35 to 500 GBs) to an Imaris file format. The stitched file was then converted using Imaris file converter, which further reduced the file size (∼1 to 15 GBs) and visualized using Imaris 10.1.0 software (Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK). Information regarding the antibodies used in this section and their dilutions is available in Table S3.

IF intensity quantification

EDF and shading corrected images acquired in .nd2 format were converted to OME-TIFF files using NIS-Elements Viewer and subsequently imported into Fiji/ImageJ for analysis. Quantification of GFAP+ (AF633) and PLP+ (AF488) tissue regions was performed in the cerebellar molecular layer as well as in anatomically defined CNS white matter regions, including spinal cord white matter tracts, cerebellar white matter (deep cerebellar nuclei) and the corpus callosum. Three to four sections per genotype were analyzed for each tissue, and any sections with apparent tears or missing tissue were excluded entirely from the analysis. For each anatomical region, a manually defined signal threshold was established and then applied consistently to all WT and Slc17a5R39C/R39C tissue sections analyzed within that region. This region-specific thresholding approach was utilized to account for differences in signal characteristics across distinct CNS white matter structures while maintaining a uniform analysis strategy within each region. Following thresholding, ten randomly selected ROIs (100×100 μm) were placed within the relevant anatomical region of each tissue section. Within each ROI, Fiji/ImageJ was used to measure area, mean intensity, area fraction (percentage area with positive staining), mean gray value, integrated density and raw integrated density; however, only integrated density and area fraction (percentage area) results are presented. These measurements were collected from threshold-defined signal within each ROI and used for quantitative comparison of IF staining between genotypes.

Cerebellar Purkinje cell analysis and quantification

Midsagittal brain sections from mixed-sex mice of both genotypes (Slc17a5R39C/R39C and WT) aged 21 days or 3 months were stained for calbindin D-28K and imaged on an AxioObserver.Z1/7 microscope (Zeiss Microscopy). An initial whole-brain overview was generated by using DAPI fluorescence to detect the whole tissue with an EC Plan-Neofluar 10×/0.30 NA M27 objective. This image was then placed in the ZEN advanced tile viewer (Zeiss image acquisition software), at which point a manual contour was drawn around the entire cerebellum (region of interest). AF555, which corresponded to the calbindin D-28K primary antibody, was set as the reference channel. Support points were randomly distributed across the tissue, focused, and the whole 14-bit cerebellum image was obtained using an EC Plan-Neofluar 40×/1.30 NA oil objective and scaled at 0.086 μm×0.086 μm (per pixel). ZEN Blue v3.5.093.00002 (Zeiss Microscopy) was used to stitch images with the following parameters: fuse tiles/no shading correction/7% minimal overlap/7% maximal shift. For each stain, image acquisition settings were kept the same regardless of genotype, sex or timepoint.

Linear density of cerebellar Purkinje cells at P21 and 3 months, presented as number of cells/mm, was determined by dividing the total number of counted Purkinje cells by the total length of the Purkinje cell layer on 40× whole-cerebellum images stained for calbindin D-28K as described above. For manual Purkinje cell counts, all CZI files were converted to TIFF and opened on Fiji ImageJ software, using which an investigator marked cell bodies of all calbindin D-28K-positive Purkinje cell somas while unaware of experimental groups. To validate these counts, a semi-automatic analysis was conducted using the ZEN Blue Image Analysis Module's analysis wizard to obtain the same thresholding, automated segmentation and region filtering to identify and count the Purkinje cell bodies. Quality control was completed through interactive analyses. Numbers of cell bodies were recorded before and following quality control. On sections with non-specific staining, regions with signal outside the granular Purkinje cell layer were removed during interactive analysis. All sections were analyzed using the same Image Analysis settings except for two slides from a 3-month-old WT mouse in which there was high background. The setting for this section was adapted to automatically find and count the Purkinje cells.

For high-resolution images of cerebellar Purkinje cells, overviews of the cerebellum were first obtained using a Plan-Neofluar 10×/0.30 NA objective using the tiling and position modules on a Zeiss 880 laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss Microscopy). Positions at the Purkinje–granule cell interface from each of the ten cerebellar lobes were manually marked on each tiled whole-cerebellum overview so that Purkinje cell somas and their dendrites could be visualized. Z-series (24 slices) were then automatically collected from each marked position using a 63×/1.4 NA oil DIC M27 objective. Series were all collected using the same pinhole settings; however, images were taken using slightly different gain settings as detector sensitivity was optimized to show the quality of the Purkinje cells. Image processing was completed using ZEN Blue v3.5.093.00002 (Zeiss Microscopy). Orthogonal projections were made using the weighted average method to show details throughout the cell body of the Purkinje cells.

Information regarding the anti-calbindin D-28K antibody used in this section and its dilution is available in Table S3.

Western blotting

Mixed-sex mice of both genotypes (Slc17a5R39C/R39C and WT) were anesthetized at 3 months of age with a terminal dose of 1.25% tribromoethanol and intracardially perfused with 10 ml 1× PBS. The brain was rapidly dissected, bilateral olfactory bulbs were removed, and the remaining tissue (composed of the forebrain, cerebellum and brainstem) was separated into left and right hemispheres. The entire spinal cord was collected from the spinal column. Tissues were then snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and cryo-pulverized in TT1 Extra Thick tissueTUBEs (Covaris, 520007) on dry ice with a CP01 cryoPREP Manual Dry Pulverizer (Covaris, 500230). In a new microcentrifuge tube, 30-50 mg cryopulverized tissue was homogenized on ice in RIPA Buffer (Millipore Sigma, R0278) supplemented with Halt Protease Inhibitor 100X Cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 78425) using a Pellet Pestle Cordless Motor (Millipore Sigma, Z359971) equipped with disposable pellet pestles. After tissues were effectively disrupted, they were incubated on ice for 20 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 g (maximum speed) for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected in a fresh microcentrifuge tube, the pellet was disposed, and protein concentration was quantified using a Micro BCA™ Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23235). 10 μg protein lysate for the left hemisphere and for whole spinal cord were loaded into Novex™ Tris-Glycine Mini Protein Gels, 4-20% (Thermo Fisher Scientific, XP04120BOX) and run in Novex™ Tris-Glycine SDS Running Buffer (10×) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, LC2675) at 130 V for 1.5 h. Proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane using an iBlot2 device (Invitrogen). Membranes were incubated in Intercept® (PBS) Blocking Buffer (Licor, 927-70001) for 1 h at room temperature followed by primary antibodies diluted in Licor Blocking Buffer overnight at 4°C. Vinculin was used as a loading control. Membranes were washed three times in PBS-Tween (0.1% Tween 20 in 1× PBS), once in 1× PBS, and then incubated with secondary antibodies diluted in Licor blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature. After washing in PBS-Tween followed by 1× PBS as described above, protein bands were detected using an Odyssey infrared fluorescence system (version 3.0; LI-COR Biosciences). Protein quantification and intensity comparisons were performed using Empiria Studio® Software v2.3.0.154 (LI-COR Biosciences). Molecular masses of probed antibodies were as follows: MBP, rat (14-21.5 kDa); NFL, mouse (68 kDa); vinculin, mouse (124 kDa).

More information regarding the antibodies used in this section and their dilutions is available in Table S3.

Serum NFL quantification

Mixed-sex mice of both genotypes (Slc17a5R39C/R39C and WT) were anesthetized as described above. Prior to intracardiac perfusion, the peritoneal cavity was accessed with a midline incision, and a 23 g hypodermic needle with syringe was inserted into the inferior vena cava to collect 400-600 μl of blood per mouse. The blood was transferred to a microcentrifuge tube, allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, centrifuged at 4°C for 25 min at 1300 g, and supernatant serum was isolated and stored for further analysis. Serum concentrations of NFL were measured using an NF-light v2 Advantage kit from Quanterix (Billerica, MA, USA). Quanterix technology utilizes enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA) and SimoaTM bead technology to provide ultrasensitive measurements of biomarkers in the CSF, blood, plasma or serum on the SR-X platform. NFL levels were assayed according to the Quanterix kit instructions. All assays were run in duplicate for each biological sample. Each plate had calibrators run in triplicate and two internal controls run in duplicate. Results are presented in pg/ml.

Bulk RNA sequencing-based transcriptomic profiling

Brain tissues were collected from four WT and four Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice (see Table S1 for mouse details) following PBS perfusion. The left hemisphere, right forebrain and right cerebellum were dissected, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and transferred to dry ice before storage at −80°C. Samples were subsequently shipped to Azenta Life Sciences Inc. (Burlington, MA, USA) for bulk RNA sequencing.

Total RNA was isolated using an RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA concentration was measured using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent TapeStation. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina, in combination with the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs). In brief, polyadenylated RNA was captured using oligo(dT) beads and fragmented at 94°C for 15 min. First- and second-strand cDNA synthesis followed, after which the cDNA fragments were end repaired, A tailed and ligated to universal adapters. Libraries were indexed and amplified by limited-cycle PCR. Library quality was verified on the Agilent TapeStation and quantified using both the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer and qPCR (KAPA Biosystems). Libraries were clustered on a single flowcell lane and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 (or equivalent) platform using 2×150 bp paired-end reads. Image processing and base calling were conducted using the instrument's control software. Raw BCL files were converted to FASTQ format and demultiplexed using Illumina's bcl2fastq v2.17, allowing for a single mismatch in index recognition.

Sequencing reads were aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome (GRCm38) using HISAT2 (Kim et al., 2019), and gene-level counts were generated using the DESeq2 package (v1.41.2) in R (v4.3.0) (Love et al., 2014). Count matrices were imported into R for quality control and downstream analysis. A regularized log (rlog) transformation was applied to normalize the data for visualization and clustering. Principal component analysis and hierarchical clustering were conducted to evaluate sample relationships. DEGs were identified using a threshold of adjusted P-value<0.0001 and |log2 fold change|≥1.2. Volcano plots were generated using the EnhancedVolcano R package (v1.19.0). Myelination genes were curated from Chavarria-Siles et al. (2016). Functional enrichment of DEGs was assessed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.9.0), incorporating GO biological processes terms and cell-type signature gene sets. Visualization of results was performed using ggplot2 and pheatmap in R. All raw data and accompanying metadata were submitted to Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; accession number GSE328153).

Cell-type deconvolution of bulk RNA-sequencing data

To estimate the cellular composition in the bulk RNA-sequencing dataset, computational deconvolution was performed using the MuSiC algorithm (Wang et al., 2019). Briefly, bulk RNA-sequencing count matrices were generated from mouse brain tissue samples collected from WT and Slc17a5 p.R39C homozygous knock-in mice. The samples represented three brain regions, including the right cerebellum, right forebrain and left hemisphere. The Zeisel Brain Data resource was utilized as the single-cell RNA-sequencing reference (Zeisel et al., 2015). The cells in the reference dataset were assigned to five broad cell populations: astrocytes/ependymal cells, endothelial/mural cells, microglia, neurons and oligodendrocytes. Gene identifiers were then harmonized between the bulk and single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets, and the music_prop MuSiC function was applied to estimate cell-type proportions in each bulk RNA-sequencing sample relative to the reference. The resulting estimates were utilized for downstream visualizations (see Fig. 6C and Figs S11-S15). Statistical analyses included Wilcoxon rank-sum test for genotype-based comparisons, Kruskal–Wallis test for brain region-based comparisons and Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the unpaired two-tailed t-test, or, when appropriate, a mixed-effects analysis with multiple comparisons, to compare quantitative data obtained from Slc17a5R39C/R39C mice to those from age-matched WT littermate mice (GraphPad Prism 10 software). For all experiments, data are presented as the mean±s.e.m., with P<0.05 considered statistically significant for individual comparisons. Statistical significance P-value thresholds were as follows: *P<0.05, **P<0.01, ***P<0.001 and ****P<0.0001.
