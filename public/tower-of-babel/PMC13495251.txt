Real-time prediction of trauma-induced coagulopathy using an inverted transformer (trauma-former): a methodological feasibility and simulation study based on the ADEMP framework
Authors: Xiaolei Huang, Wenliang Chen, Guan Wei, Wenjia Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrauma-induced coagulopathy (TIC) is a leading cause of preventable mortality following severe injury, yet prehospital recognition is fundamentally constrained by the limited sensitivity of static vital-sign metrics. Although fifth-generation (5G) wireless technology theoretically enables continuous biosignal transmission, robust computational frameworks for streaming risk prediction remain underdeveloped and largely unvalidated. To address this methodological gap, this study presents Trauma-Former, an inverted Transformer (iTransformer) architecture for real-time TIC prediction, trained and validated on high-fidelity synthetic physiological data within a rigorous in-silico engineering assessment framework.MethodsAn Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process generated 1 Hz vital-sign waveforms (heart rate [HR], systolic and diastolic blood pressure [SBP/DBP], SpO₂) for 1,240 simulated 30-minute transport episodes (50% TIC prevalence), structured according to the ADEMP framework. Trauma-Former embeds 60-second histories as independent variable tokens, applying self-attention across physiological streams to model inter-variable coupling. Models were benchmarked against an expanded set including a linear trend-based logistic regression (LR-trend), a 1D convolutional neural network (1D-CNN), a bidirectional gated recurrent unit (GRU), LSTM, XGBoost, PatchTST, Informer, and the shock index. An independent test set with 25% TIC prevalence assessed performance under clinically representative event rates. A sensitivity analysis incorporating binary missingness indicators probed the missing-completely-at-random (MCAR) assumption. Monte Carlo standard errors (MCSE) quantified simulation uncertainty throughout.ResultsOn the balanced development set, Trauma-Former achieved an AUROC of 0.939 (95% CI 0.92–0.95; MCSE = 0.003) and an AUPRC of 0.88. Critically, the LR-trend model achieved an AUROC of 0.917 (95% CI 0.89–0.94), confirming that the synthetic task is predominantly solvable by detecting monotonic vital-sign drifts; the incremental contribution of iTransformer’s cross-variable attention is consequently modest (AUROC gap: 0.022). Under a realistic 25% prevalence setting, AUROC remained 0.931 but positive predictive value (PPV) collapsed from 0.89 to 0.48—a 46% relative reduction—indicating that fewer than half of all model-generated alerts would correspond to true TIC events in a representative prehospital cohort. Adding binary missingness indicators yielded only a marginal PPV improvement of 0.02 under 30% MCAR data loss. The model demonstrated resilience to 30% packet loss and 4G-equivalent latency jitter with less than 5% AUROC degradation.ConclusionsTrauma-Former provides a reproducible, open-source synthetic testbed for prehospital AI development. All reported metrics represent upper-bound estimates derived under deliberately simplified linear conditions. The severe PPV collapse under realistic prevalence identifies alarm fatigue as the paramount translational barrier and underscores that rigorous external validation on real-world prehospital data is the absolute prerequisite for any clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-026-02909-x.Keywords: Trauma-induced coagulopathy, Inverted transformer, Synthetic data, In-silico engineering assessment, Simulated 5G, Real-time prediction, PPV collapse, ADEMP framework, Simulation study

Background

Uncontrolled hemorrhage remains the leading preventable cause of death after severe trauma, and its lethality is greatly amplified by the rapid development of trauma-induced coagulopathy (TIC) [1–3]. TIC represents an endogenous failure of hemostasis triggered by tissue injury, hypoperfusion, endothelial glycocalyx shedding, and dysregulation of coagulation pathways [4]. Recent mechanistic insights have highlighted the role of shock-induced endotheliopathy (SHINE) and the massive release of thrombomodulin, which rapidly destabilize cardiovascular homeostasis [5, 6].Epidemiological data indicate that more than one-third of severely injured patients already exhibit coagulopathy upon emergency department arrival, a condition independently associated with a four-fold increase in mortality [7–9]. The established ‘golden hour’ paradigm underscores the critical importance of early intervention [10]; however, prehospital recognition of TIC remains severely limited [11].

Current prehospital practice relies on intermittent vital-sign measurements and rule-based scores such as the shock index (heart rate / systolic blood pressure) [12]. While relatively specific, these static metrics lack sensitivity for compensated shock—the period during which vasoconstriction maintains near-normal blood pressure despite ongoing intravascular volume loss and microcirculatory collapse [13, 14]. By the time hypotension becomes overt and the shock index crosses conventional thresholds, the coagulopathic cascade may have already reached an irreversible stage. Early resuscitation with fibrinogen and plasma has been shown to mitigate organ injury and prevent progression, underscoring the need for timely prediction [15, 16]. The physiological signatures of compensated shock—subtle narrowing of pulse pressure, progressive tachycardia, and declining heart rate variability—are not captured by discrete measurements [17]. These trends evolve over tens of seconds to minutes and require continuous, high-frequency (≥ 1 Hz) monitoring to be reliably detected [18, 19].Streaming such waveform data from a moving ambulance presents substantial technical challenges. Legacy 4G networks exhibit severe latency jitter (> 100 ms) and packet loss rates exceeding 1% in congested urban environments [20], rendering streaming inference unreliable.

The emergence of fifth-generation (5G) ultra-reliable low-latency communication (URLLC) addresses this infrastructure gap. Under optimal deployment conditions, dedicated 5G network slices can provide near-deterministic latency and high reliability [21, 22]; however, real-world 5G performance varies substantially with geographic location, network load, and infrastructure maturity [23]. The latency profiles simulated in this study should therefore be regarded as optimistic reference benchmarks rather than as representative of general prehospital deployments. Proof-of-concept demonstrations suggest that 5G-enabled connectivity could reduce prehospital data transmission delays [24, 25], though clinical outcome evidence remains limited. While conceptually inspired by the ‘digital twin’ paradigm [26, 27], our current work is explicitly positioned as an in-silico engineering assessment: a purely simulated computational framework with no deployed hardware or clinical intervention. The transition from raw data streams to actionable clinical predictions requires a computational framework capable of modeling complex, non-linear, and asynchronous temporal dependencies inherent in trauma physiology [28].

Deep learning approaches for medical time series have traditionally relied on recurrent neural networks (RNNs) such as long short-term memory (LSTM) networks [29–31]. Although effective for short sequences, RNNs suffer from vanishing gradients and struggle to capture long-range temporal dependencies [32]. The Transformer architecture, built on parallelized self-attention mechanisms, has achieved state-of-the-art performance across numerous sequence modeling tasks [33] and has been adapted for healthcare applications including early sepsis prediction [34, 35] and ECG anomaly detection [36]. Recent studies confirm that Transformer models are increasingly displacing RNNs in biomedical time-series analysis [37–39]. The inverted Transformer (iTransformer) proposed by Liu et al. [40] treats the entire historical sequence of each variable as an independent token, applying self-attention across variables to model dynamic inter-variable coupling. This ‘variate-as-token’ strategy is conceptually appealing for detecting decompensation, where the breakdown of normal coupling between heart rate and pulse pressure is a hallmark of hemorrhagic shock [41] Investigations into why basic Transformers fail in medical time-series tasks have further highlighted the necessity of cross-variable attention mechanisms [42].

Related work

Several studies have explored machine learning for early identification of TIC or massive transfusion. Marsden et al. [43] developed a Bayesian network model using manually recorded prehospital vital signs and reported an AUROC of 0.87 (95% CI 0.79–0.95) in their final clinical evaluation. Xiong et al. [7]compared ten machine learning models on a multicenter trauma registry of 13,235 patients, achieving an AUROC of 0.91 (Random Forest) in external validation. Du et al. [44] used random forest models to predict postoperative TIC with an AUROC of 0.82. All of these studies, however, relied on sparse, non-continuous data and did not exploit the temporal dynamics of physiological waveforms. Morid et al. [29] noted that LSTM remains the most common architecture for healthcare time series but is increasingly challenged by Transformer-based models. Tang et al. [34] demonstrated that a Transformer model could predict sepsis onset from ICU vital signs with higher accuracy than LSTM. To our knowledge, no published study has applied an inverted Transformer architecture to continuous prehospital vital signs or systematically benchmarked it against contemporary time-series models in the context of TIC prediction [45].

Study contribution and positioning

This study is a methodological feasibility investigation. We do not claim clinical validation, nor do we suggest that the reported performance metrics are generalizable to real-world clinical settings. Our specific aims are to: (1) design and publicly release a physiologically informed synthetic data generator producing realistic 1 Hz vital-sign trajectories; (2) implement and open-source Trauma-Former with complete training and evaluation pipelines; (3) rigorously benchmark Trauma-Former against eight baselines (LR-trend, 1D-CNN, GRU, LSTM, PatchTST [46], Informer [47], XGBoost, and shock index) using patient-level cross-validation on a large synthetic cohort, adhering to the ADEMP framework [48, 49] and reporting Monte Carlo standard errors [50]; (4) quantitatively validate synthetic data fidelity using the multivariate Hellinger distance [51]; (5) quantify the impact of disease prevalence on predictive values—specifically the collapse of PPV from 0.89 to 0.48 when moving from a balanced development set to a 25% prevalence test set—as the primary translational finding of this study; (6) conduct a sensitivity analysis of missing data mechanisms comparing standard masking against binary missingness indicators; and (7) discuss risk of bias in alignment with PROBAST + AI [52] and algorithmic fairness as recommended by TRIPOD + AI [53]. By releasing all code and simulation tools, we aim to establish a reproducible, privacy-preserving benchmark [54, 55] that can accelerate the development and external validation of deep learning-based prehospital decision support systems [56].

Methods

Ethical statement

This study used exclusively synthetic data generated by mathematical models and did not involve any human participants, medical records, or identifiable health information. No institutional review board approval was required according to the policy of the authors’ institution and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Synthetic physiological data generator

A stochastic simulator based on the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process—a mean-reverting stochastic differential equation widely used to model physiological time series [57]—was constructed to generate continuous 1 Hz waveforms for four vital signs: HR, SBP, DBP, and SpO₂. All simulator parameters were derived from physiologically plausible ranges and assumptions in the trauma literature [4, 12, 13]; no individual patient data were used. For TIC-positive trajectories, linear drift was superimposed on HR (positive drift) and SBP (negative drift) after 5 min of simulated transport, reflecting progressive hemodynamic deterioration during compensated shock. DBP was modeled as a linear function of SBP with added noise; pulse pressure narrowed progressively in the TIC group. Sensor noise (Gaussian) and brief motion artifacts were added to increase realism.

We acknowledge that this generative model is a deliberate and critical simplification. Real TIC progression is non-linear and often characterized by abrupt, cascading decompensation rather than steady linear drift. This ‘phenomenological’ approach directly simulates observable physiological trends rather than modeling the underlying mechanistic coupling between cardiovascular variables. This design choice provides a transparent, controllable test environment for isolating the effects of network impairments from biological complexity, and systematically biases the study towards producing upper-bound estimates of model discrimination. All reported performance metrics should be interpreted within this idealized context. The complete parameter set and OU process equations are provided in Additional file 1.

A total of 1,240 independent patient transport episodes, each lasting 30 min, were generated for model development and internal validation, with a balanced class split (50%; n = 620 TIC, 620 control) [58]. The cohort size was selected based on a power analysis: with an expected AUROC of 0.90 and TIC prevalence of 50%, 1,240 episodes provide > 90% power to detect a 0.05 AUROC difference at a two-sided α = 0.05 [59, 60]. To evaluate performance under clinically representative event rates (typically 16–34%) [61–63], an additional independent test cohort of 1,000 episodes with 25% TIC prevalence (n = 250 TIC, 750 control) was generated with all other parameters held constant. This evaluation under realistic prevalence was treated as the most clinically informative benchmark of model utility. Synthetic data fidelity was validated using the multivariate Hellinger distance , where p and q are the probability density functions of the empirical and theoretical distributions, respectively [51]. Detailed fidelity validation plots (KDE comparisons, cross-variable correlation error heatmaps, individual trajectory visualizations) are provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Simulation design: ADEMP framework

The design and reporting of this simulation study strictly adhered to the ADEMP (Aims, Data-generating mechanisms, Estimands, Methods, and Performance measures) framework [48]. The primary aim was to evaluate the discriminative ability of the iTransformer for real-time TIC detection and to compare its performance against established baselines under controlled synthetic conditions. A secondary aim was to quantify the impact of disease prevalence on PPV as the primary translational benchmark. The estimand was the dynamic probability of TIC onset within a 30-minute prehospital window, conditional on the observed 60-second history of vital signs. Performance measures included AUROC (primary), AUPRC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV (with emphasis on behavior under realistic prevalence), NPV, F1-score, Brier score, calibration metrics, and decision curve analysis. Monte Carlo standard errors were computed for all metrics.

System architecture and in-silico engineering feasibility assessment

This is a purely simulated network experiment; no actual 5G hardware was deployed or tested. A three-layer architecture was emulated mathematically. The edge layer (simulated ambulance gateway) buffers 60-second windows, performs Z-score normalization, and applies linear interpolation for isolated missing points (≤ 5 seconds). The transmission layer was modeled under two configurations: (i) 5G URLLC, modeled as a Gamma distribution (shape = 2.0, scale = 4.0) yielding a median latency of 8 ms (IQR 6–11 ms) [21, 22]; and (ii) 4G LTE, modeled as a lognormal distribution (µ = ln(40), σ = 0.1) with a median of 40 ms [20, 64]. Our simulated latency profiles correspond to near-optimal conditions; published field measurements at vehicular speeds document substantially higher and more variable latencies. Our resilience analysis therefore provides a necessary but insufficient ‘best-case’ stress test. The cloud layer (virtualized GPU server with Redis TimeSeries database) runs inference and alerting logic. The overall system architecture and simulated network environment are shown in Fig. 2.

Data preprocessing and missing data handling

Each 60-second window was normalized to zero mean and unit variance using statistics computed from the training folds. For gaps ≤ 5 s, linear interpolation was applied. For longer gaps (up to 30 s), missing time steps were padded with zeros, and corresponding positions were masked in the self-attention layers. The iTransformer inherently supports masking: by supplying an attention mask that omits padded positions, the model attends only to observed data.

We acknowledge a critical limitation. In real prehospital settings, prolonged signal loss is often missing not at random (MNAR)—it may result from severe agitation, cardiovascular collapse, or cardiac arrest. The absence of signal itself carries prognostic information, and zero-padding with masking discards this information. In alignment with the PROBAST + AI framework [52], we explicitly acknowledge that our masking mechanism assumes missingness is completely at random (MCAR).

Sensitivity analysis for missing data mechanism

To partially assess the impact of the MCAR assumption, we conducted a supplementary experiment in which binary missingness indicators (1 if missing, 0 if observed) were appended as additional features to the input tensor before the masking step, allowing the model to exploit the presence or absence of a signal as a potential prognostic cue. Under the 25% prevalence test set with 30% random missingness, this modification yielded an AUROC of 0.926 (95% CI 0.91–0.94) and a PPV of 0.50, compared with 0.931 and 0.48 under standard masking alone. The modest PPV improvement of 0.02 confirms that, within the constraints of this MCAR simulation, the prognostic information carried by missingness patterns per se is limited. This experiment does not capture true MNAR scenarios (e.g., signal loss during cardiovascular collapse), which require fundamentally different architectural solutions such as learnable temporal sparse memory [65]. Results are presented in Supplementary Figure S2.

Baseline models

Trauma-Former was compared against an expanded set of baselines probing different aspects of the prediction task. The shock index (HR/SBP) was computed at the final time step of each window (static threshold > 1.0). LR-trend: to directly test the detectability of linear trends in the synthetic data, we implemented a logistic regression model using slope, intercept, and R² from linear regressions fitted independently to each 60-second window for each vital sign (12 features total; L2-regularized, C = 1.0), trained with the same patient-level cross-validation. This baseline directly quantifies the extent to which the TIC prediction task reduces to detecting monotonic vital-sign trends. 1D-CNN: a standard temporal convolutional network with two convolutional layers (32 and 64 filters, kernel size 3) followed by max pooling and a fully connected head (128 units, dropout 0.2). GRU: a two-layer bidirectional gated recurrent unit with 64 hidden units per layer and a sigmoid output, included as a temporally-aware recurrent baseline to complement the LSTM comparison. LSTM: two bidirectional layers with 64 hidden units, followed by a dense sigmoid layer. XGBoost: gradient-boosted trees using 20 summary features (mean, slope, minimum, maximum, variance for each vital sign); note that XGBoost was provided only 20 hand-crafted features versus the full 60-second waveform used by deep learning models. PatchTST and Informer: recent efficient Transformer variants adapted for classification. All deep learning baselines were implemented in PyTorch 2.1 and trained with identical optimization settings (AdamW, learning rate 1 × 10⁻⁴, batch size 64, early stopping with patience of 10 epochs).

Experimental design and validation strategy

Patient-level 5-fold cross-validation was performed on the development cohort of 1,240 independent episodes [65]. All windows originating from the same synthetic patient were confined to a single fold, preventing temporal leakage. Performance metrics were averaged across five folds, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained via percentile bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) at the patient level. MCSE = , where K = 5 is the number of cross-validation folds, following Koehler et al. [50]. Bayesian hyperparameter optimization (50 iterations) was conducted for Trauma-Former over , , ,dropout ∈ [0.1, 0.5], learning rate ∈ [10⁻⁵, 10⁻³], and batch size∈{32,64,128}. Detailed hyperparameter configurations for all models, including the search protocol and baseline settings, are provided in Additional file 2.

Performance metrics and statistical analysis

The primary metric was AUROC. Secondary metrics included AUPRC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, F1-score, Brier score, calibration slope and intercept, and decision curve analysis. Early warning time (EWT) was defined as the interval between the first moment the model output exceeded 0.80 for three consecutive minutes and the simulated hospital arrival time (t = 30 min). Robustness tests added Gaussian noise (SNR from 20 dB to 5 dB), randomly masked 10–50% of time steps, and simulated transient HR sensor failures (dropout for 5–30 s), each repeated five times with different random seeds. All statistical analyses were performed in Python 3.10 using scikit-learn, SciPy, and custom scripts.

Open science and reproducibility

The complete source code, including the synthetic data simulator, all model implementations, training and evaluation scripts, hyperparameter search, and figure generation code, is publicly available at: https://github.com/DoctorLin1990/trauma-former. The repository is version-controlled, contains a detailed README, and specifies the exact Python environment (requirements.txt). All random seeds are fixed to ensure exact reproducibility. Upon acceptance, the complete codebase will be deposited in Zenodo with a DOI for long-term accessibility.

Predictive performance on balanced development cohort

Table 2 presents the cross-validated performance of all models on the balanced development cohort. Under these class-balanced (50% TIC) and linearly-structured conditions, Trauma-Former achieved the highest AUROC (0.939; 95% CI 0.92–0.95; MCSE = 0.003) and AUPRC (0.88). Critically, the LR-trend model—which merely fits a linear regression to each 60-second window—achieved an AUROC of 0.917 (95% CI 0.89–0.94; MCSE = 0.004), only 0.022 below Trauma-Former. This confirms that the synthetic task is predominantly solvable by detecting monotonic vital-sign drifts, and that the performance gap between the iTransformer and a simple linear trend detector is modest. The remaining gap is attributable to the model’s explicit modeling of cross-variable interactions. The GRU (AUROC 0.854) and 1D-CNN (0.868) baselines provide fairer comparators than the MLP-only ablation and further contextualize the incremental gain of the iTransformer architecture. Performance under realistic disease prevalence: the ppv collapse

The most clinically consequential finding of this study is the severe degradation of positive predictive value under realistic TIC prevalence, and this result is foregrounded accordingly. On the independent test set with 25% TIC prevalence, Trauma-Former maintained high discriminative ability (AUROC 0.931; 95% CI 0.91–0.95; MCSE = 0.004). However, as is mathematically expected from Bayes’ theorem [66], PPV fell sharply from 0.89 (balanced development set) to 0.48—a 46% relative reduction. This means that in a prehospital population with realistic TIC frequency, approximately one in two model-generated alerts would correspond to a non-TIC patient, a level of false-positive signaling that would likely undermine clinical utility and contribute to alarm fatigue [67]. Decision curve analysis [68] on this test set (Fig. 3D) showed that Trauma-Former still provided positive net benefit, but the range of threshold probabilities with clinically meaningful net benefit narrowed to approximately 0.20–0.60. These results underscore that AUROC alone is an insufficient and potentially misleading indicator of clinical utility in low-prevalence settings [69].

Sensitivity analysis with missingness indicators. Under the same 25% prevalence test set with 30% MCAR missingness, adding binary missingness indicators to the input tensor yielded an AUROC of 0.926 and a PPV of 0.50, compared with 0.931 and 0.48 under standard masking alone (Supplementary Figure S2). The marginal PPV improvement of 0.02 confirms that, within the constraints of this simulation, the prognostic information carried by the pattern of signal absence per se is limited under MCAR conditions.

Early warning lead time

With the optimized alert rule (probability ≥ 0.80 sustained for ≥ 3 consecutive minutes), Trauma-Former generated its first alert at a median of 18.1 min (IQR 13.4–22.3) before simulated hospital arrival in TIC-positive episodes of the development cohort. The detection rate was 91.5% (567/620); the false-positive rate among control episodes was 8.3% (51/620). On the 25% prevalence test set, the PPV of the alert rule decreased to 0.46, corresponding to more than one false alert for every true positive. Sensitivity analyses on the persistence requirement (Supplementary Figure S4) showed that reducing persistence to 1 min increased lead time to 21.4 min but raised the FPR to 15.1%; increasing persistence to 5 min reduced FPR to 5.9% but shortened lead time to 14.6 min. The 3-minute rule offered a pragmatic compromise.

Robustness to data imperfections and simulated network conditions

Algorithmic robustness to signal artifacts and missingness

Trauma-Former maintained AUROC > 0.90 under additive Gaussian noise down to SNR = 10 dB, and with up to 30% randomly missing time steps (MCAR). Performance degradation was significantly smaller than that of LSTM across all noise levels. During simulated transient HR sensor failure (dropout up to 30 s), Trauma-Former’s AUROC remained above 0.92, indicating effective use of correlated signals through the cross-variable attention mechanism. System-level tolerance to simulated network latency

This is a purely simulated network experiment; no actual 5G hardware was tested. Under the simulated 5G URLLC profile, the median end-to-end latency was 37.2 ms (IQR 34.5–41.2 ms), well within the 100 ms target. The end-to-end latency breakdown is shown in Fig. 4A. Under the 4G profile, median latency increased to 42.1 ms (IQR 37.8–47.3 ms), with approximately 1.1% of packets exceeding the 50 ms deadline; these packets were masked and model AUROC was unaffected (0.938 vs. 0.939). Our results demonstrate algorithmic robustness to worst-case simulated packet loss patterns but do not provide evidence of equivalent performance in actual prehospital deployments.

Self-attention weights were extracted from the final encoder layer for all TIC-positive windows. This pattern was absent in control episodes. This finding should be interpreted as a model validity confirmation rather than a novel discovery. The elevated attention weight between HR and pulse pressure directly mirrors the linear drift programmed into the synthetic data generator, confirming that the model correctly identifies the predefined coupling between rising heart rate and narrowing pulse pressure. It does not constitute the discovery of novel pathophysiology. A more convincing demonstration of genuine interpretability would require showing that the attention pattern changes appropriately when the generative model is altered (e.g., using non-linear dynamics), or that the model correctly ignores clinically irrelevant noise injected into one channel. These experiments are identified as directions for future work.

Ablation and sensitivity analyses

Replacing the inverted embedding with standard time-step tokenization reduced AUROC to 0.893 (Δ = −0.046). Removing the self-attention module entirely (MLP-only classifier on flattened window) reduced AUROC to 0.837 (Δ = −0.102). Notably, the GRU and 1D-CNN baselines—which serve as fairer recurrent and convolutional comparators than the MLP-only ablation—achieved AUROCs of 0.854 and 0.868, respectively, further contextualizing the incremental gain of the iTransformer architecture. A lookback window of 60 s yielded the highest AUROC (0.939); 30 s resulted in 0.904, and 120 s provided no additional gain (0.932). Discussion

The paramount finding: PPV collapse and alarm fatigue

The most clinically consequential finding of this study is not the high AUROC achieved under idealized conditions, but the stark collapse of PPV—from 0.89 to 0.48—when the model was evaluated under a realistic TIC prevalence of 25%. This finding is deliberately foregrounded because it directly challenges the common practice of evaluating predictive models under artificially balanced conditions. Even a model with high sensitivity (0.89) and specificity (0.86) will generate a substantial number of false-positive alarms when the event rate is low. In the TIC context, where true prevalence in unselected trauma populations is estimated between 16% and 34%, our data suggest that approximately one in two automated alerts would be false, placing the model squarely within the ‘alarm fatigue zone’—a well-recognized patient safety hazard in clinical monitoring environments [70, 71].

This finding fundamentally challenges the notion that AUROC alone is a sufficient metric of clinical utility. Any prospective deployment must incorporate explicit strategies to mitigate alarm fatigue [72], including site-specific threshold calibration informed by local TIC prevalence, secondary verification mechanisms (e.g., paramedic confirmation, trend confirmation over longer windows), or the integration of additional data streams (e.g., point-of-care lactate, end-tidal CO₂) to augment PPV. The decision curve analysis provides a framework for such calibration, demonstrating that net benefit narrows substantially at 25% prevalence compared with balanced conditions.

Methodological contributions and separation of claims

The value of this work must be carefully separated into two distinct contributions. First, the open-source pipeline and reproducible benchmark have high and immediate methodological value: by releasing all components, we address the scarcity of publicly available high-resolution trauma datasets and the absence of standardized benchmarks for fair model comparison. Any research group can reproduce our experiments, test alternative architectures, and adapt the framework to their own simulated or real datasets.

Second, the performance metrics reported herein (AUROC 0.939, lead time 18 min) are effectively non-generalizable to real-world clinical settings. They represent upper-bound estimates derived from a deliberately simplified, linearly-structured synthetic data generator. This point warrants strong emphasis: the high AUROC values should not, under any circumstances, be cited as evidence of clinical diagnostic accuracy. The LR-trend baseline—achieving AUROC 0.917 using only simple linear regression features—demonstrates that the primary driver of high performance is the detectability of monotonic vital-sign trends. The incremental contribution of the iTransformer’s explicit cross-variable attention is approximately 0.022 AUROC points: modest but consistent, attributable to its modeling of inter-variable coupling. The open-source tools have high and immediate value; the performance numbers do not.

Value of this simulation study despite its deliberate simplicity

The deliberately simplified generative model enables a controlled stress test in which algorithmic performance and network effects (latency jitter, packet loss) can be studied in isolation from the uncontrolled confounding variables inherent in real electronic health records (e.g., undocumented fluid boluses, medication administration, measurement artifacts). This design choice explicitly trades physiological realism for experimental control, an appropriate trade-off for design-phase validation of a computational pipeline.

Synthetic data that preserve only aggregate statistical properties eliminate re-identification risk [54] and can be freely shared [73], enabling independent replication without ethical or regulatory barriers. This study also integrates multiple contemporary methodological standards—ADEMP, PROBAST + AI, TRIPOD + AI, rigorous MCSE quantification, and systematic missing data sensitivity analysis—within a single, coherent evaluation pipeline. As regulatory bodies increasingly recognize the role of computational modeling and simulation in medical device evaluation [74], a transparent, reproducible framework of this type serves as a template for application to future, more realistic synthetic data generators and, ultimately, to real-world clinical datasets [75].

Limitations and future work

Simplified generative model

This is a synthetic-data study employing a deliberately simplified linear drift process. Any competent trend-detection algorithm can achieve high AUROC under these conditions, as our LR-trend baseline (AUROC 0.917) conclusively demonstrates. The reported performance metrics should therefore be interpreted strictly as upper-bound estimates unlikely to be replicated in real clinical settings. Future work must incorporate more realistic pathophysiological dynamics, such as those generated by mechanistic ordinary differential equation models of coagulation kinetics, or by generative adversarial networks trained on real waveform repositories [59, 76]. This limitation is further substantiated by the results of our supplementary stress-test experiment (Additional file 3 and Supplementary Figure S6), conducted as a post-hoc exploration. When challenged with synthetic data incorporating covert, non-linear decompensation (power-law drift with randomized onset) and amplified physiological noise, under 30% missing data handled via spline interpolation, baseline models degraded substantially (1D-CNN: AUROC 0.790; GRU: AUROC 0.703). This preliminary experiment foreshadows the performance reductions expected when models trained on simple linear assumptions confront more biologically complex dynamics, and serves as a concrete target for future methodological refinements. We emphasize that this supplementary experiment was conducted post-hoc and its parameters were not pre-registered; results should therefore be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive.

Single prevalence evaluation

The realistic prevalence evaluation was conducted at a single prevalence point (25%). A more comprehensive evaluation would systematically vary prevalence across the full clinically plausible range (10%–30%) to map the complete prevalence–PPV relationship.

Missing data handling

Our masking-based approach assumes MCAR and discards the prognostic information carried by the pattern of signal loss itself. Our missingness indicator sensitivity analysis demonstrated only a marginal PPV improvement (0.48 → 0.50), confirming limited incremental benefit under MCAR conditions but not capturing true MNAR scenarios. Future work should integrate learnable temporal sparse memory or attention mechanisms specifically designed to model informative signal absence.

Simulated network latency

The network assessment was purely simulated and provides no guarantee of real-world 5G or 4G field performance. Our simulated latency profiles correspond to near-optimal conditions; published empirical measurements at vehicular speeds have documented substantially higher and more variable latencies [77]. Future work should incorporate empirical network traces from actual ambulance deployments, alongside model compression techniques (knowledge distillation, INT8 quantization) for resource-limited edge gateways.

Algorithmic fairness

This simulation omitted sociodemographic heterogeneity (age, sex, ethnicity), which inherently influences cardiovascular compensatory reserves and baseline physiological parameters [78]. The model cannot currently be audited for algorithmic bias across marginalized subpopulations [79, 80]. Future transitions to real-world electronic health records must integrate explicit de-biasing techniques and stratify performance evaluations by demographic groups [81].

Clinical implementation

This study does not address the clinical implementation challenges of prehospital decision support, including display design, alert recipient designation, triggered action protocols, or multi-center independent validation. Integration of additional data streams (end-tidal CO₂, point-of-care lactate, multimodal fusion) and telemedical support capabilities are identified as promising directions for future work.

Risk of bias assessment (PROBAST + AI)

Applying the PROBAST + AI framework reveals high risk of bias across multiple domains [82]. Regarding participants and data sources: the synthetic data are generated from a deterministic process perfectly matching the model’s assumptions, with no real-world heterogeneity, measurement error, or confounding; applicability to clinical populations is severely limited. Regarding predictors: the four vital signs are cleanly defined and free of missing data in the training set, unlike real prehospital predictors that suffer from inconsistent sampling [83], undocumented sensor malfunctions, and fluid boluses that alter the physiological trajectory. Regarding outcome: TIC is defined based on a deterministic drift threshold; real TIC diagnosis requires viscoelastic testing and is subject to inter-rater variability and time-dependent treatment effects. Regarding analysis: the absence of any real-world external validation renders the model’s generalizability completely unknown [84].

Regarding applicability (PROBAST + AI Applicability domain), high concern is rated across all three dimensions. Participants: the synthetic cohort was generated from a population-free mathematical model and is not representative of any real prehospital trauma population with respect to demographics, injury mechanism, injury severity distribution, comorbidity burden, or prehospital treatment. Predictors: continuous 1 Hz high-fidelity waveforms are not routinely available in current prehospital practice, and the clean, artifact-limited signals used here do not reflect true field conditions. Outcome: the TIC label is derived from a programmed physiological threshold, which does not map onto any standardized clinical or laboratory definition (e.g., INR, TEG/ROTEM criteria) used in real trauma care. These high applicability concerns reinforce that the present model cannot be considered ready for clinical use without extensive external validation.

We clearly acknowledge these high risks of bias. Crucially, the two dimensions of this work must be evaluated separately: the open-source code and simulation tools have high and immediate methodological value as a development platform, whereas the reported performance numbers do not constitute evidence of real-world performance and should not be extrapolated beyond the specific conditions of this simulation. External validation on independently collected real-world prehospital data is the absolute prerequisite for any clinical translation [85].

Conclusions

We have presented Trauma-Former, a real-time prediction framework for trauma-induced coagulopathy based on an inverted Transformer architecture, validated exclusively on high-fidelity synthetic physiological data within a rigorous, transparent, and fully reproducible simulation framework. The primary finding of this study is not the high AUROC (0.939) observed under idealized linear conditions—a result closely approximated by a simple linear trend detector (LR-trend, AUROC 0.917)—but rather the stark degradation of PPV from 0.89 to 0.48 when the model was tested under a realistic disease prevalence of 25%. This PPV collapse identifies alarm fatigue as the paramount translational barrier for prehospital AI deployment and underscores the fallacy of evaluating clinical utility on the basis of discriminative metrics alone.

This work is unambiguously a methodological feasibility study; it does not claim, imply, or approach clinical readiness. Its contributions are threefold: (1) the establishment of a rigorous, transparent, and fully open-source synthetic testbed for prehospital AI development that integrates contemporary methodological frameworks (ADEMP, TRIPOD + AI, PROBAST + AI) into a single reproducible pipeline; (2) the precise quantification of how class imbalance affects PPV in the context of continuous vital-sign monitoring, providing a concrete, data-driven warning against the premature deployment of predictive models in low-prevalence settings; and (3) the demonstration, through the deliberate inclusion of a simple LR-trend baseline, that the detection task on this linear dataset is predominantly one of trend identification, thereby calibrating expectations about the incremental contribution of sophisticated deep learning architectures. We invite the research community to use, critique, and extend this framework. Prospective external validation on real-world prehospital data, with explicit attention to site-specific disease prevalence, algorithmic fairness across demographic subgroups, and systematic alarm fatigue mitigation strategies, is the non-negotiable next step before any clinical application can be contemplated. Notably, a post-hoc non-linear stress test (Supplementary Material S3) confirmed that all evaluated models degrade substantially—with Trauma-Former AUROC falling from 0.939 to 0.815—when the linear drift assumption is replaced by more biologically complex dynamics, further underscoring the upper-bound nature of all primary analysis metrics and the necessity of more realistic generative models in future work.
