Leadless AV Synchronous Pacing in a Patient With Ebstein’s Anomaly and Prior Glenn Shunt
Authors: Dingxin Qin, Natasha Vedage, Jacqueline Danik, Sihong Huang, Doreen DeFaria Yeh, Christopher P Learn, Ada C Stefanescu Schmidt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeadless pacing offers an important alternative to transvenous systems in patients with complex congenital heart disease and limited venous access.Case SummaryWe report the case of a 29-year-old woman with Ebstein anomaly, hypoplastic right ventricle, prior bidirectional Glenn shunt, multiple septal defect repairs, and transcatheter pulmonic valve replacement who presented with symptomatic paroxysmal atrioventricular block. Multi-Modality Imaging Guided Leadless Pacemaker Implant

ASD = atrial septal defect; AV = atrioventricular; EAM = electroanatomical mapping; ICE = intracardiac echocardiography; TEE = transesophageal echocardiography; TPVR = transcatheter pulmonic valve replacement; VSD = ventricular septal defect.

Permanent pacing in adults with complex congenital heart disease (CHD) is often challenging because of distorted anatomy, surgically altered venous pathways, and prosthetic valves. These challenges are particularly pronounced in Ebstein's anomaly with cavopulmonary palliation, where apical tricuspid leaflet displacement, atrialized right ventricle (RV), and Glenn shunt physiology complicate transvenous lead placement and increase risk of valvular injury and lead instability. Although leadless pacemaker systems have shown feasibility in multicenter CHD series,1,2 experience in patients with complex CHD remains limited. We report successful Micra AV implantation guided by transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and 3-dimensional electroanatomic mapping (EAM) to optimize device positioning and avoid tricuspid valve interaction in a patient with Ebstein's anomaly, hypoplastic RV, and prior Glenn shunt.

History of Presentation

A 29-year-old woman with complex CHD, including Ebstein's anomaly, tricuspid valve hypoplasia, hypoplastic RV, pulmonary valve stenosis, conoventricular ventricular septal defect (VSD), atrial septal defect (ASD), Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome, status post bidirectional Glenn shunt, ASD closure, VSD closure, and transcatheter pulmonic valve replacement (TPVR) (Table 1, Figure 1), presented with symptomatic bradycardia associated with progressive exertional dyspnea and lightheadedness. Electrocardiography and ambulatory rhythm monitoring demonstrated baseline sinus rhythm with first-degree atrioventricular (AV) block and intermittent 2:1 AV block with exertion.

Past Medical History

The patient had undergone multiple previous cardiac interventions (Table 1), including pulmonary valvuloplasty and an electrophysiology study at age 1 demonstrating a low-risk accessory pathway not requiring ablation, surgical closure of an ASD and conoventricular VSD with concomitant bidirectional Glenn shunt creation at age 3, repeat VSD closure for residual shunting in 2001, and, most recently, TPVR with an Alterra pre-stent and 29-mm Sapien S3 valve for severe pulmonic regurgitation in 2023.

Differential Diagnosis

On presentation, initial laboratory evaluation including Lyme serologies and thyroid function testing, did not identify a reversible cause of her conduction disease. Her baseline electrocardiogram showed a borderline prolonged PR interval and incomplete right bundle branch block. The AV block was therefore attributed to progressive His-Purkinje disease related to her complex CHD and previous surgical repairs, potentially compounded by loss of antegrade conduction through the preexisting accessory pathway.

Management

Cardiac imaging demonstrated a patent Glenn shunt characterized by a superior vena cava–to–right pulmonary artery connection (Figure 1). Imaging also confirmed Ebstein's anomaly with apical displacement of the septal tricuspid leaflet (Figure 1), preserved biventricular size and systolic function, mild tricuspid regurgitation, and previous TPVR with mild pulmonary regurgitation. A multidisciplinary conference was convened to review the case, and permanent pacing was recommended. Given her complex congenital anatomy, implantation of a Micra AV leadless pacemaker (Medtronic) was favored, as this approach allows delivery via femoral venous access, avoids transvenous leads, and offers AV synchronous pacing within a compact device profile (26 mm in length). Considering the anatomic challenges, the procedure was performed under combined TEE and EAM guidance to optimize device positioning and procedural safety.

The patient was brought to the electrophysiology laboratory for implantation of a leadless pacemaker. General anesthesia was induced, and a TEE probe was inserted without difficulty. Bilateral femoral venous access was obtained; a 25-F Micra delivery sheath was advanced through the right femoral vein. Left femoral venous accesses were obtained for insertion of a mapping catheter (PENTARAY® NAV, Johnson & Johnson MedTech) and an intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) (SOUNDSTAR®, J&J MedTech) catheter to allow imaging integration with the EAM system (CARTO 3, J&J MedTech).

Following systemic heparinization, EAM of the RV was performed with particular attention to delineation of the RV septum and true RV apex. No significant septal low-voltage areas were identified. The Micra AV delivery catheter was advanced through the sheath and positioned in the RV under fluoroscopic and TEE guidance. The delivery system was carefully maneuvered to the anteroseptal RV. The mapping catheter was positioned in the same region to correlate fluoroscopic and EAM landmarks (Figure 2). TEE confirmed no significant interaction with the septal leaflet; contrast injection through the delivery catheter confirmed satisfactory positioning. The Micra AV pacemaker was deployed in standard fashion. Optimal device position and fixation were achieved on the first deployment attempt (Figure 3). Final device measurements confirmed stable pacing parameters (R-wave 10 mV, impedance 610 Ω, threshold 0.7 V @0.24 ms). Postprocedural TEE demonstrated preserved mobility of all tricuspid valve leaflets, unchanged mild tricuspid regurgitation, and no pericardial effusion.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At 3-month follow-up, device performance remained stable, and the patient reported marked symptomatic improvement, particularly after optimization of atrial sensing to enable consistent AV synchronous pacing.

Discussion

This case demonstrates the successful implantation of an AV synchronous leadless pacemaker in a young patient with Ebstein's anomaly, hypoplastic RV, prior bidirectional Glenn shunt, multiple septal defect repairs, and TPVR, using comprehensive multimodality preprocedural and intraprocedural imaging guidance. Building on emerging multicenter experience supporting the feasibility and safety of leadless pacemaker implantation across a spectrum of CHD substrates,1,2 this case further illustrates a reproducible strategy that integrates existing leadless pacing platforms, multimodality imaging, and EAM to optimize procedural safety and efficacy in complex patients with CHD.

Leadless pacing has emerged as an important alternative to transvenous systems in patients with complex CHD, in whom abnormal venous pathways, intracardiac shunts, prior surgical palliation, and distorted chamber geometry often make conventional lead placement challenging or undesirable.1,2 In addition to technical barriers, transvenous leads in this population carry heightened long-term risks, including venous occlusion, paradoxical embolism, lead–valve interaction, and infection. Leadless systems eliminate the need for intravascular leads and surgical pockets, thereby offering a more durable pacing strategy. In formulating the leadless pacing strategy, device selection was guided by patient-specific anatomic considerations. In the present case, due to the small RV size and the presence of atrialized septal tricuspid valve leaflet, the Micra AV system was chosen as a more anatomically suitable solution while still preserving AV synchronous pacing.

Advanced imaging and mapping integration were critical to procedural success. Preprocedural multimodality imaging provided comprehensive anatomic assessment and was instrumental in procedural planning, particularly in defining RV size, septal orientation, and tricuspid valve anatomy. Intraprocedural TEE provided real-time visualization of tricuspid valve anatomy, allowing careful navigation of the delivery catheter across the valve while avoiding interaction with the apically displaced septal leaflet. ICE further facilitated visualization of intracavitary structures and allowed real-time integration of the ultrasound imaging with the EAM system. EAM allowed for reconstruction of the RV anatomy and provided detailed information on the electrical properties of the local ventricular tissue. Although a recent report described CARTO UNIVU (Johnson & Johnson MedTech) fusion imaging to assist Micra implantation in Ebstein’s anomaly,3 this case highlights a different strategy, leveraging EAM with a multipolar mapping catheter to define the RV septum and guide precise device positioning. The combined use of fluoroscopy, TEE, ICE, and EAM allowed precise localization of a safe anteroseptal implant site and likely contributed to successful first-pass deployment with stable electrical parameters.

There was a recent report of implant of a leadless pacemaker through retrograde route via the right internal jugular vein–superior vena cava–Glenn shunt down the main pulmonary artery across the pulmonary valve.4 This strategy is not feasible in the present case because of the presence of a prosthetic pulmonary valve. Transfemoral delivery with multimodality imaging guidance was favored. Meanwhile, as leadless pacing continues to evolve toward more physiologic and modular systems, leadless cardiac resynchronization platforms and conduction system pacing approach may soon be available to improve outcomes in complex CHD substrates. In younger patients with CHD, durable device management remains an important challenge; advances in device retrievability and emerging technologies such as piezoelectric energy harvesting may further extend device longevity and reduce cumulative hardware burden.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that AV synchronous leadless pacing can be safely and effectively achieved in patients with highly complex CHD, including Ebstein's anomaly, hypoplastic RV, prior Glenn shunt, and TPVR. Careful preprocedural planning, individualized device selection, and the integration of multimodality imaging were critical to optimize device implantation. This experience expands the growing body of evidence supporting leadless pacing in surgically palliated congenital substrates and highlights the value of advanced imaging and mapping to overcome anatomic constraints. As leadless technologies continue to evolve toward physiologic pacing, modular systems, and improved lifecycle management, their role in CHD populations will likely broaden further.
