The neuronal network of small cell lung cancer: a narrative review of the role of the nervous system in the pathogenesis and progression
Authors: Mateusz Gap, Filip Gajewski, Weronika Pająk, Joanna Pec, Jakub Kleinrok, Grzegorz Kurec, Paweł Krawczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveSmall cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a highly aggressive cancer with a poor prognosis, in which increasing attention is focused on the role of the nervous system as an active regulator of cancer initiation, progression and metastasis. The aim of this study is to provide an integrated focus on SCLC review of the mechanisms of neuronal regulation of this cancer, as well as a critical analysis of their therapeutic potential.MethodsA literature review was conducted in the PubMed database, covering publications from 1st January 2020 to 1st December 2025, using defined keywords relating to SCLC and the nervous system. The analysis included publications that met the criteria relating to the role of the nervous system in the pathogenesis, progression or treatment of SCLC.Key Content and FindingsThe review identifies the multi-level influence of the nervous system on SCLC, including: the formation of functional synapses between cancer cells and neurons, the relevance of vagotomy in the early stages of carcinogenesis, the role of neurotransmitters and their receptors, neuropeptides and neurotrophins in tumour development and their influence on the tumour microenvironment, as well as the interaction of SCLC with astrocytes in brain metastases (including Reelin and SERPINE1). Therapeutic perspectives were also discussed, including the use of ADRB2 blockade, the inhibition of synaptic neurotransmission, and the blockade of the neurotrophin axis.ConclusionsThe findings presented in this study provide a basis for further research into the interactions between SCLC and the nervous system, with a view to developing new targeted treatments in the future.

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is an aggressive cancer that accounts for approximately 14% of all lung cancers, and its predominant risk factor is smoking, which accounts for 95% of cases (1). At the time of initial diagnosis, most patients have distant metastases, mainly to the opposite lung, brain, liver, adrenal glands and bones (2). Metastases to the central nervous system are particularly common, occurring in 10% of patients at the time of diagnosis of SCLC, and in an additional 40–50% of patients developing these metastases later (2).

SCLC originates from pulmonary neuroendocrine cells (PNECs) in which pathologic variants have occurred in the following genes: TP53, RB1, MYC, PTEN, NOTCH and CREBBP (3-8). The neoplasm consists of two main cell lines: neuroendocrine (NE) cells, which exhibit electrical activity and the ability to metastasise, and non-neuroendocrine (non-NE) cells, which do not exhibit the characteristics of NE cells but support their function (9). SCLC divides into three molecular subtypes, dependent on the transcription factors achaete-scute family bHLH transcription factor 1 (ASCL1) (SCLC-A), neuronal differentiation 1 (NEUROD1) (SCLC-N), YAP1 (SCLC-Y) or POU class 2 homeobox 3 (POU2F3) (SCLC-P), among which SCLC-A and SCLC-N subtypes are defined as NE subtypes (9,10). This division may be relevant in research on targeted therapeutic goals (10).

The current standard of frontline of treatment is based on platinum and etoposide chemotherapy, in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors and radiotherapy, including, in selected cases, prophylactic cranial irradiation (2,11). These strategies have led to improved response rates by more than 60%, even in patients with metastatic disease. Nonetheless, in most patients these responses are transient and the cancer is often resistant to second-line treatment, resulting in a low survival rate (2,12). This explains the urgency of developing new, more effective and targeted therapies (2,11).

Latest research has changed the perspective on SCLC—not only as a NE cancer, but also as a cancer that actively uses neuronal signaling to support tumour initiation, proliferation and metastasis (13,14). Sympathetic neurons have been shown to promote SCLC progression through β2-adrenergic receptor (ADRB2)-dependent signaling on tumour cells (15). Furthermore, it has been found that parasympathetic innervation also plays an important role in the initiation and progression of SCLC, but a lesser role in the maintenance of advanced cancer (13). The two studies showed that the activity of the autonomic nervous system was related to the development of cancer cells. Moreover, SCLC cells can establish functional synapses with nerve cells and respond to glutamatergic and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-ergic signals, which directly increases their ability to proliferate and invade (13,16-18). In the context of metastatic disease, especially within the central nervous system, SCLC uses interactions with astrocytes to create a microenvironment similar to the early stages of brain development, which promotes the survival of cancer cells (14,19).

These studies also started to clarify therapeutic targets focused on the signaling of neurons with cancer cells in SCLC. Pharmacological inhibition of ADRB2 has been shown to reduce tumor growth and sympathetic innervation in preclinical models of SCLC (15). It has also been found that vagotomy performed at an early stage of SCLC development results in a significant delay in tumour growth and metastasis (13). Additionally, blocking functional potentials or synaptic receptors, including glutamatergic and GABA-ergic signaling, inhibits the proliferation of cancer cells (13,16,17). Although derived from different research models, these data consistently suggest that the nervous system, at various levels, may be targeted for therapeutic intervention in patients with SCLC.

Nevertheless, research on the influence of the nervous system on SCLC is still sparse and disorganized. Previous review articles have focused mainly on general aspects of SCLC biology (20-22) or on cancer-neuron interactions in other tumours (23-25). There is therefore a lack of detailed synthesis of current knowledge regarding the interaction between the nervous system and SCLC cells. The innovative approach of this study consists of filling this knowledge gap and, subsequently, presenting a review of the newest studies concerning the role of the nervous system in the initiation, progression and metastasis of SCLC, and a critical analysis of how these interactions can be exploited in cancer treatment.

The combining, sorting and analysis of this data aim to prove that the neural mechanisms are a key part of SCLC pathogenesis, and not just an accompanying, insignificant feature and provide a potential approach to the development of new, more effective treatments or supplements to existing cancer therapies. Concurrently, significant gaps in knowledge about the interaction between the nervous system and SCLC were emphasized, including a limited number of studies, especially clinical trials, the prevailing role of preclinical models, and an incomplete understanding of which neural pathways are most important in the development of SCLC. Filling these gaps by further targeted research, including translational and clinical studies, will be crucial to determine whether therapies based on interactions between the nervous system and SCLC can feasibly improve treatment outcomes for patients with this exceptionally aggressive cancer. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0523/rc).

Methods

This review was designed as a narrative review with a structured literature search focused on the role of the nervous system in the pathogenesis, progression, and therapeutic targeting of SCLC. Databases were searched for articles published between 1st January 2020 and 1st December 2025 using the following search string: (“small cell lung cancer” OR “small cell lung carcinoma” OR SCLC) AND (“nervous system” OR neuron* OR neural OR innervation OR synap* OR neurotransmitter* OR adrenergic OR cholinergic OR glutamate OR GABA OR astrocyte* OR Schwann OR neurotrophin* OR Reelin OR SERPINE1). Reference lists of relevant reviews and original articles were additionally screened manually to identify further eligible studies.

Eligible publications included original, experimental studies, translational studies, and selected clinical trials directly addressing neural mechanisms in SCLC, including tumour innervation, neurotransmitter signaling, neuron-tumour synapses, neurodevelopmental mimicry, and therapeutic targeting of these pathways. Studies on lung cancer or cancer neuroscience were included only if they provided important mechanistic context for understanding SCLC-specific findings and were clearly identified as supporting evidence, not main evidence. Case reports, publications primarily focused on paraneoplastic neurological syndromes, and studies that only addressed neurological problems related to SCLC treatment were excluded.

Titles, abstracts, and full texts were screened for relevance, and the final synthesis prioritized SCLC-specific mechanistic studies. Because of the heterogeneity of the available evidence and the predominance of preclinical models, a qualitative synthesis was performed rather than a formal systematic review or meta-analysis (Table 1).

The neural network components in SCLC

The first reports of nerve fiber infiltration into tumours date back to the 20th century, when they were interpreted as preserved remnants of normal innervation of the tissue in which the tumour developed. The development of technologies such as electron microscopy and advanced immunohistochemical staining has led to a paradigm shift (26-28). It is now believed that tumour innervation involves the preservation of existing local nerves as well as the active growth of new nerves in the tumour microenvironment. Studies show that cancer cells are capable of producing neurotrophic factors that promote axon growth and increase innervation density (29,30). The exact factors responsible for neurogenesis are discussed in section “Molecular mechanisms in the interaction between SCLC and the nervous system”.

In the context of SCLC, the role of the nervous system in tumour progression and aggressiveness is increasingly emphasised. Studies indicate that NE cells in SCLC are electrically excitable and that the potentials they generate may promote tumour progression. Neuronal activity is also associated with increased energy demand in cells, and its maintenance is supported by non-NE cells. The results of these studies suggest that tumour innervation in SCLC is functional and drives growth and malignancy (9,31).

Savchuk et al. (13) assessed the effect of innervation on SCLC growth. Material was taken from SCLC tumours in which the expression of numerous neurotransmitter receptor genes was found. It was thus demonstrated that SCLC cells are capable of receiving nerve signals. In induced lung tumours in mice, numerous nerve fibers, including parasympathetic, sympathetic and sensory fibers, were found to innervate the tumours. Furthermore, innervation was also found in liver metastases.

Subsequently, it was performed denerving the tumor by unilateral cervical vagotomy (experimental group), while in some mice the procedure was simulated (control group). In mice undergoing unilateral vagotomy at an early stage of tumor development, tumor initiation in the lungs and the appearance of metastases in the liver were noticeably delayed or undetectable, the overall tumor burden was lower, and survival time was prolonged compared to the control group. In contrast, in models in which denervation was performed at a late stage of tumor, no significant effect on lesion size or survival was observed, suggesting that neuromodulatory interventions may be most effective in the early stages of carcinogenesis. The pathomorphological study also compared the innervation of early lesions and the innervation of late-stage tumours. It was shown that, compared to early lesions, advanced tumours have less innervation, which may reflect the changing dependence of SCLC on nerve signals during progression.

The interactions between nerve cells and SCLC were also assessed. First, samples of human brain tissue with SCLC metastases were analysed. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that there were extensive areas within the tumour containing a mixture of axons and tumour cells, and that the tumour cells closest to the axons had a higher proliferation rate, suggesting that there are functional interactions between neurons and SCLC. Intracranial allografts in mice were then examined and, as in human samples, the presence of nerve cells in the tumour mass, especially at its periphery, and a higher proliferation rate of tumour cells in the neuron-rich area of the tumour compared to the neuron-poor area were demonstrated. However, this phenomenon may be related to better blood supply to these tumour fragments and, consequently, better access to oxygen and nutrients.

In a study conducted by Sakthivelu et al. (16) in SCLC and cortical neuron cultures, as well as in an animal model of SCLC transplants to the brain, spatial structures corresponding to synapses were demonstrated by microscopy, and postsynaptic proteins were detected in cancer cells. The functionality of these synapses was evaluated. For this purpose, the retrograde transsynaptic spread of rabies virus (RABV) was traced in SCLC cells in both neuron cultures and SCLC allografts to the brain in mice. Tracking of this virus confirmed that SCLC are capable of forming functional synapses with neurons both in vitro and in vivo. Excitatory and inhibitory neurons were identified, further indicating that SCLC may be innervated by different subtypes of neurons in vivo.

Molecular mechanisms in the interaction between SCLC and the nervous system

An increasing number of studies indicate that the development of SCLC cannot be fully explained exclusively by classic oncogenic mechanisms and immune interactions in the tumor microenvironment (TME). The role of the nervous system as an active regulator of tumor biology is gradually revealed and appears to be crucial (13,31,32). SCLC is a special model of cancer because its NE origin predisposes tumor cells to integrate with neuronal signals at both the molecular and functional levels (14,33).

Currently, it is believed that neuron-tumor communication in SCLC occurs at multiple levels and involves classical neurotransmitter transmission, trophic signaling, vesicular communication, and bioelectrical phenomena. These mechanisms do not operate independently, but form a dynamic network of feedback that influences proliferation, cellular plasticity, and metastatic capacity (19,31).

Neurotransmitters and neuromodulators

Many researchers state that in the TME of lung cancers an intensive communication between the nervous system and cancer cells occurs. Neurotransmitters such as noradrenaline, adrenaline, acetylocholine (ACh), glutamate, GABA and neuropeptides can be released by nerve fibers and cancer cells. They interact with specific receptors, leading to modulation of cancer cell proliferation, angiogenesis and the immune system response in TME (34-37).

Adrenaline and noradrenaline

Adrenaline and noradrenaline are catecholamines, which are released by the adrenal medulla and sympathetic nerve endings mainly in stressful conditions. In the TME, they can interact with the tumor via α and β adrenergic receptors present on both tumor and immune cells. Catecholamines bind to those receptors and stimulate adenylate cyclase activity, furtherly increasing cAMP levels. cAMP activates further pathways such as protein kinase A (PKA), regulating gene transcription, cell survival and inflammatory signaling. In cancer, these signaling pathways promote tumor progression and metastasis by enhancing angiogenesis, inflammation, resistance to apoptosis and immune suppression. Nevertheless, researchers suggest that β-adrenergic receptor antagonists may reduce stress-related tumor progression, leading to better overall survival (38).

In lung cancer, it has been shown that adrenergic signaling induced by adrenaline and noradrenaline can increase the production of proangiogenic factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and extracellular matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2 and MMP-9). Those factors promote angiogenesis and tumor invasion. In addition, catecholamines can modulate the function of immune cells in the TME, leading to increased immunosuppression and facilitating tumor progression (39).

A study by Fnu et al. (15) revealed the contribution of the sympathetic nervous system to the development of SCLC. A significant reduction in the growth of the tumor was noted following chemical denervation of local sympathetic nerves in a mouse model of SCLC. In addition, it was shown that ADRB2 in cancer cells mediated crosstalk with nerve fibres. ADRB2 deletion resulted in reduced tumour growth and increased overall survival, as well as reduced growth of SCLC organoids and xenografts. In addition, ADRB2 was shown to promote cancer progression through activation of PKA, and, therefore, PKA inhibition reduced SCLC growth.

ACh

ACh is an important neurotransmitter of the parasympathetic nervous system. It can also be synthesized by non-nerve cells, including cancer cells and TME cells (40). The presence of a non-neuronal cholinergic system has also been reported in lung cancer. Cancer cells produce ACh and, at the same time, stimulate cholinergic receptors. ACh interacts mainly through nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChR) and muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (mAChR), further activating signaling pathways regulating cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and cell migration. In addition, activation of nicotinic receptors can lead to the stimulation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT pathways, which promote cancer cells growth and survival. In SCLC, it has been reported that cholinergic signaling can function in a paracrine way, supporting cancer cell proliferation and modulating the TME (41-43).

Glutamate and GABA

Glutamate and GABA are the main stimulatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters in the central nervous system. However, their role has also been reported in various cancer pathologies. Glutamatergic receptors, N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) and amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionate (AMPA), have been detected to be expressed in lung cancers. Their activation may lead to increased cancer cell proliferation and activation of signaling pathways connected with cell survival. On the other hand, GABA can modulate cancer cell growth via GABA A and GABA B receptors, further affecting cAMP-dependent signaling pathways and cell cycle regulation. In the TME, these neurotransmitters can be produced by both cancer cells and nervous system cells, which leads to the formation of complex neuro-tumor communication networks (44-47).

A study by Houcek et al. (18) confirmed the ability of SCLC to form functional synapses with glutaminergic and GABA-ergic neurons. For this purpose, the AMPA receptor (AMPAR) antagonist cyanquaxalene (CNQX) was used to divide the cancer cell populations into those sensitive and those insensitive to CNQX. The GABA A receptor antagonist bicuculline was then applied, resulting in a reduction in neuronal activity in both SCLC populations. These conclusions are also confirmed by other studies (13,16,17).

Neuropeptides

Neuropeptides are a crucial group of signaling molecules that are involved in communication between the nervous system and cancer cells. The expression of multiple neuropeptides has been reported in SCLC, including substance P, neuropeptide Y (NPY), and gastrin-releasing peptide (GRP). Substance P acts mainly through the neurokinin receptor neurokinin-1 receptor (NK-1R) and NPY through Y1 and Y5 receptors, whose activation leads to increased cancer cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis. In SCLC, neuropeptides often act through autocrine or paracrine mechanisms, stimulating tumor cell growth and modulating the functioning of the TME (48,49). Trophic factors and growth signaling

Neurotrophins, being a group of growth factors, play a key role in the survival, differentiation, and plasticity of neurons. The most important ones are nerve growth factor (NGF), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), neurothorphin-3, and neurotrophin-4. They can be produced by nerve cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells in the TME (50,51).

In SCLC, neurotrophins can activate Trk family receptors (TrkA, TrkB, TrkC) and the low-affinity receptor [p75 neurotrophin receptor (p75NTR)]. Those actions lead to the activation of signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT, MAPK/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and phospholipase C gamma (PLCγ). Furthermore, they promote cancer cell survival, proliferation, and resistance to apoptosis (52,53). Moreover, higher expression of NGF and other neurotrophins in lung cancer may promote neurogenesis in the tumor and tumor growth by stimulation of proliferation and interaction between cancer cells and nerve fibers in the TME (52,53).

Reelin and SERPINE1 in the interaction between SCLC and astrocytes

The study conducted by Qu et al. is focused on the interconnection between SCLC metastatic cells and the activity of reactive astrocytes. Tumour cells in brain metastases exhibited features of neurons in the initial phases of brain development. These stamps were especially pronounced in microenvironmental interactions between SCLC and active astrocytes. SCLC secreted neurodevelopment-associated molecule Reelin, which induced astrocytes to be recruited to the TME, where astrocytes produce particles such as SERPINE1 that enhance tumour cell survival. Focusing on curtailing this dependency may be a turning point in decreasing risks of brain metastases and difficulties in their treatment. Notwithstanding, Reelin and SERPINE1 had a crucial role in the interaction between SCLC and astrocytes, further factors remain unknown and require continued studies (19).

Therapeutic perspectives based on neuronal mechanisms driving SCLC progression

In light of recent reports, SCLC should be considered not only as an aggressive NE cancer, but also as a tumour that interacts with the nervous system. Its neuronal characteristics may have facilitated the proliferation, migration and metastasis of cancer cells. Hartmann et al. (14) indicated in their work that G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) and other neuronal surface receptors might represent new therapeutic targets. Winkler et al. (54) emphasised that the relationship between SCLC and the nervous system involved direct communication between neurons and cancer cells, as well as indirect interaction through nerve cells influencing the immune system and cells present in the tumour microenvironment. This implied that SCLC could be considered part of a complex neural-neoplastic network, offering new therapeutic opportunities for patients. Yaniv et al. (55) further stressed that the nervous system was a significant component of the SCLC microenvironment. They highlight that there was clinical and preclinical evidence demonstrating the benefits of targeting the nervous system in cancer treatment.

The interaction between tumours and the nervous system shows new therapeutic potential. Goals include inhibiting tumour growth, strengthening the immune system’s response to therapy, preventing metastasis and reducing resistance to treatment (56). Savchuk et al. (13) demonstrated that neuronal activity and innervation drove SCLC growth in both primary tumours and brain metastases. Strategies for achieving this included limitation of tumour innervation, inhibition of neuronal activity and blockade of selected receptors. The authors demonstrated that transecting the vagus nerve inhibited tumour growth and progression. In the case of lung cancer metastasising to the brain, both glutamatergic and GABA-ergic neuronal activity stimulated SCLC growth. In co-culture models, the addition of tetrodotoxin (TTX), glutamate, and GABA-ergic receptor inhibitors stopped tumour proliferation. Sakthivelu et al. (16) also indicated that neuron-SCLC communication, especially glutamate-dependent communication, might be a therapeutic target. Its pharmacological inhibition in combination with chemotherapy appeared promising. The use of medicines targeting nerve-tumor interactions in SCLC remained limited by the preclinical character of most evidence, the risk of systemic consequences, and poor understanding of critical pathways and mediators essential for neural tumor support. The biological heterogeneity of SCLC posed an additional challenge (13,16).

Sympathetic axis modulation appears to be a promising direction. Fnu et al. (15) demonstrated that sympathetic neurons might directly promote SCLC progression. Blocking the ADRB2 and thereby inhibiting adrenergic signaling might limit tumor growth. Garramona et al. (57) suggested that poorer prognoses in patients may be associated with increased sympathetic nervous system activity and higher adrenergic receptor expression. The use of beta-blockers appeared to be an interesting therapeutic concept, particularly as part of combination therapy. Combining these drugs with chemotherapy or immunotherapy might improve treatment outcomes, improve survival rates, reduce the frequency of metastases and limit the development of resistance. However, at this point, clinical data for SCLC and beta-blockers were heterogeneous, indicating a need for further research (56,57).

Modulation of cholinergic signaling also appears to show promising results (56). Cholinergic signaling in lung cancer might function as an autocrine growth axis. Its pharmacological interruption might represent a potential therapeutic approach (58). Nicotine acetylcholine receptors might be another target for anticancer therapy in SCLC (59). Wang et al. (60) pointed to the role of the α7 nAChR in the proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis of lung cancer, thereby promoting disease progression. However, in their paper, Arunrungvichian et al. (61) emphasised that, despite the rationale for α7 nAChR as a target in lung cancer treatment, there was still a lack of approved antagonists. Before they could be used in clinical practice, improvements were needed in terms of selectivity, efficacy and safety profile. Cheng et al. (62) underlined that the activated nicotine/nAChR pathway is connected not only with lung cancer progression but also with the establishment of tumor resistance to treatment. This might have been crucial in the context of potentially sensitizing the tumor to treatment by inhibiting cholinergic transmission.

Another potential therapeutic target is the neurotrophin axis. Kimura et al. (63) found that co-expression of BDNF and tropomyosin receptor kinases B (TrkB) was related with a poorer prognosis in individuals with SCLC. Furthermore, BDNF promoted the migration of cells characterized by high TrkB expression. Meng et al. (64) emphasized that activation of the BDNF/TrkB axis was associated with a more aggressive type of SCLC, characterized by increased growth and increased migration of cancer cells. Blocking TrkB might be a potentially promising treatment strategy to limit tumor growth and invasive potential. However, its clinical applicability might be constrained by potential systemic effects, including neurological damage, and possibly inhibition of antitumor immune responses.

A further therapeutic target may be tumor-associated Schwann cells. Cao et al. (65) indicated that tumor-associated repair-like Schwann cells (TAR-SCs) supported the growth, invasion, and migration of SCLC. However, there were significant translational limitations to this potential therapy, such as the preclinical nature of the data and uncertainty regarding the selectivity and safety of the intervention (65,66).

Discussion

Taken together, the available evidence supports that SCLC should be discussed not only as a NE malignancy, but also as a tumour integrated functionally with the nervous system (13,14). The studies discussed in this review indicate that neural input may influence several key stages of SCLC biology, including tumour initiation, progression, invasive behaviour, and adaptation within the brain metastatic niche (13,15,16,19). Rather than representing a passive feature of the tumour microenvironment, interactions between neurons and tumour appear to form an active regulatory network that may lead to the exceptional aggressiveness of this disease (13,16,19).

At the same time, current evidence base remains limited and unclear. Although recent studies consistently support a functional role of neural signaling in SCLC, most available data come from preclinical models, while prospective clinical validation is still lacking (13,15,16). In addition, the biological heterogeneity of SCLC suggests that the extent of neural dependency may differ between tumour contexts and molecular subtypes, which makes broad therapeutic generalisation premature at this stage (10,13,16). Notwithstanding these suggestions, differences in the neuron-tumor interactions depending on molecular subtypes, or changes in the genome, remain unexplored. Future research into these differences could aid the search for more accurate therapeutic targets focused on neuronal interactions with a specific subtype of SCLC. However, it is also important to remember the intratumour heterogeneity and the plasticity of cancer cells, which could potentially decrease the effectiveness of these future therapies (67). The most solid evidence currently supports a functional role for neuronal activity, synapse formation, and adrenergic signaling in SCLC progression, whereas the therapeutic value of cholinergic, neurotrophin-related, and broader neuromodulatory strategies remains more preliminary.

It is noteworthy to explore whether neuronal interactions differ between NE and non-NE SCLC. A study by Peinado et al. (9) found the presence of nAChRs in NE cells, the activation of which caused depolarisation of these cells. It was also found that the cholinergic innervation density was reduced in advanced tumours. This suggests that cholinergic neurons may play an important role in stimulating NE cells during the tumour initiation stage, and consequently, the NE subtypes of SCLC (SCLC-A, SCLC-N) are probably interacting with cholinergic neurons at least during the early stages of development. Furthermore, a study by Sakthivelu et al. (16) found that the SCLC-A and SCLC-N subtypes may exhibit particular interactions with the glutamatergic system. Ireland et al. (68) found that increased MYC expression may promote the transition from NE to non-NE SCLC, and another study found that denervation of an aggressive SCLC tumour driven by the MYC oncogene did not inhibit its growth (13). Based on these studies, it can be speculated that non-NE SCLC may be less influenced by interactions with neurons. Nevertheless, it still remains unknown whether neural interactions actually differed in any significant way depending on whether the SCLC was of the NE or non-NE subtype. Considering that non-NE subtypes of SCLC account for approximately 30% of cases, their relationship with the nervous system should be further clarified (69).

A study by Ireland et al. (70) suggested that NE cancers such as SCLC may originate from basal cells; it is therefore important to consider whether neuronal interactions with cancer cells occur after NE differentiation during disease progression or at an earlier stage of development. It is known that denervation of a tumour at an early stage inhibits its growth, in the opposite to advanced tumours, so neuronal interactions with cancer cells probably exist at a very early stage of development (13). It was also found that the PNEC cells, which arise from basal cells and give origin to SCLC, with the exclusion of SCLC-P, possess innervation (14,70). Nevertheless, it is not known whether there are any interactions between neurons and the basal cells from which SCLC might originate, and, if such interactions exist, would they promote carcinogenesis. This provides a reason for further, detailed research.

In a study by Umemura et al. (71), SCLC has been classified into five genetic subtypes: the NSCLC subgroup (with genetic alterations associated with NSCLC), the Hotspot subgroup (targetable hotspot mutations common in tumors), the PI3K subgroup (PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway mutations), the MYC subgroup (MYC family amplifications) and the HME subgroup (mutations in the histone-modifying enzymes). A study by Savchuk et al. (13) found that denervation of an aggressive SCLC tumour caused by the MYC oncogene does not inhibit its growth, compared with an SCLC tumour without this mutation at an early stage of development. This suggests that, depending on the genetic subtype or specific mutation, there may be differences in the interactions between cancer cells and neurons. However, there is as yet no study confirming the existence of these differences in SCLC or any other cancer in which neuronal interactions occur. This issue should be explored in future research. Furthermore, there is no study linking the existence of neuron-tumour interactions to any specific genomic alterations, which would seem to be an important issue in view of the study by Li et al. (72), which suggested that the beneficial effects of PCI in SCLC may be limited in some molecular subgroups. Such a study would, in particular, provide a better understanding of the pathomechanism SCLC brain metastases and the differences in the effectiveness of prophylactic cranial irradiation.

From a translational perspective, these findings suggest that neural signaling may represent a meaningful additional therapeutic target in SCLC rather than a replacement for standard, established treatment. Among the most promising directions are modulation of the sympathetic axis through ADRB2 blockade, interference with glutamatergic and GABA-ergic neuron-tumor communication, and targeting of cholinergic or neurotrophin-related pathways. However, the clinical relevance of these strategies remains uncertain because the available evidence is still largely preclinical and, in part, heterogeneous (13,15-17,56,57,63,64).

Future studies should, therefore, focus on translating these observations into clinically relevant settings, including prospective validation in human material, better characterization of tumour signaling, and clarification of whether neural dependency differs across molecular subtypes and treatment contexts (10,13,16). Greater methodological standardisation and improved models of neuron-tumour interactions are necessary to identify which patients may benefit most from neuromodulatory approaches and at what stage of disease such interventions are most likely to be effective (13,16,19).

Conclusions

Evidence from the past years indicates that SCLC is not merely an autonomous mass of tissue, but an active component of a broader neuro-oncological network. Particularly striking are reports that SCLC exhibit intrinsic electrical activity. Under laboratory conditions, the generation of action potentials has been observed—this electrical self-stimulation act as a direct driver for tumour proliferation. The aggressiveness of SCLC stems directly from its ability to form functional connections with glutamatergic and GABA-ergic neurons. The cancer simply ‘taps into’ the body’s existing electrical infrastructure and exploits natural neurotransmission to accelerate its own growth. In this system, the nerve-tumour axis is the primary regulator of disease dynamics. Here we see the dualistic influence of the autonomic nervous system: from early initiation supported by parasympathetic signals, through to advanced progression, where the sympathetic axis (ADRB2) plays a key role. However, understanding this phenomenon requires moving beyond rigid frameworks. A key piece of the puzzle is the mechanisms of neuro-mimicry, which are particularly pronounced in brain metastases, where the Reelin-SERPINE1 axis plays a leading role. The role of TAR-SCs is also crucial here. By releasing exosomes containing miRNA-21-5p, these cells actively promote cancer invasiveness, which is particularly critical in the NE subtypes SCLC-A (with expression of ASCL1 transcription factor) and SCLC-N (with expression of NEUROD1 transcription factor). All these interactions form a tightly integrated feedback system; they determine why SCLC metastasises so rapidly and becomes resistant to treatment.

In light of these findings, pharmacological disruption of nerve-tumour communication is one of the most promising therapeutic approaches. The inclusion of beta-blockers (targeting ADRB2) as therapy is an approach worth implementing in clinical practice. Such a strategy slows tumour growth and—equally importantly—enhances patients’ response to immunotherapy by modulating the microenvironment. Other potential targets include the inhibition of synaptic neurotransmission (AMPA/NMDA antagonists) and the blockade of the nicotinic axis (α7 nAChR). Success here, however, depends on timing: these interventions must be implemented at an early stage, before the tumour achieves full trophic autonomy. Realistically speaking, neuromodulation is a synergistic complement to standard chemotherapy, not a replacement for it.

Despite the optimism, we are still coming up against specific barriers. We lack precise biomimetic models that would accurately reflect human synaptic interactions, as well as non-invasive imaging biomarkers for monitoring neurogenesis within the tumour in vivo. Future research projects will focus on even more precise targeting of neuronal signals, which will allow for better patients’ stratification based on the innervation density of a specific tumour. This approach holds potential that extends beyond oncological control alone. Limiting the neurotropism of a tumour effectively reduces cancer pain. Combined with personalised treatment tailored to a specific molecular subtype, this offers hope of a breakthrough for patients in whom existing regimens have simply failed.
