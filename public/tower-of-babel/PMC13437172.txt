Dental sedation in Japan after the 2026 reimbursement revision: patient safety, access to care, and the role of dental anesthesiologists
Authors: Toru Yamamoto, Hiroko Kanemaru, Shigenobu Kurata, Yutaka Tanaka, Naotaka Kishimoto
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The 2026 revision of Japan’s national health insurance dental fee schedule provides an important opportunity to reconsider the provision of dental sedation. Under the revised schedule, dental anesthesia management fees were reorganized into dental anesthesia management fee 1 (750 points) and dental anesthesia management fee 2 (600 points), intended for management following general anesthesia and sedation, respectively. New reimbursement categories for inhalation or intravenous dental anesthesia were also established [1]. For dental inhalation or intravenous anesthesia (II), the points differ according to the qualifications of the personnel providing care: 2,600 points when performed exclusively by a dentist engaged in anesthesia, 1,700 points when performed exclusively under the supervision of such a dentist, 900 points when performed exclusively by other personnel, and 600 points in all other cases [1]. Until May 2026, the relevant evaluation for dental intravenous sedation/anesthesia was 600 points. From June 1, 2026, when dental intravenous sedation/anesthesia is managed by a dentist engaged in anesthesia under the revised criteria, a total of 3,200 points may be claimed, consisting of 2,600 points for dental intravenous sedation/anesthesia and 600 points for dental anesthesia management fee 2 [1]. Thus, the increase from 600 to 3,200 points explicitly links higher evaluation with involvement of a specialist in dental anesthesia and systemic patient management.

In this Letter, the term “dental anesthesiologists” is used in a broad professional sense to refer to dentists trained in dental anesthesia and perioperative patient management. This term does not necessarily indicate a single reimbursement category or board-certification status. However, when discussing the 2026 fee schedule, the relevant reimbursement category refers to the dentists engaged in anesthesia who meet the criteria defined in the Japanese reimbursement system [1]. This distinction is important because the policy implications depend on who provides sedation, under what conditions, and with what rescue capacity.

This revised reimbursement should not be understood as protecting professional territory, nor as denying sedation performed by other appropriately trained dental professionals. Rather, it should prompt reconsideration of the expertise, systems, education, and collaboration required to provide sedation safely. The value of dental sedation does not lie merely in administering sedatives, but in the clinical judgment required to assess systemic risk, select sedation levels, respond to unintended deep sedation or airway and circulatory changes, and manage recovery.

Dental sedation enables necessary dental treatment for patients who may otherwise have difficulty receiving care, including those with dental fear, severe gag reflex, frailty, systemic disease, or intellectual/developmental disabilities. However, conscious or moderate sedation requires a certain degree of cooperation and understanding. When cooperation or decision-making capacity is limited, the choice among minimal or moderate sedation, deep sedation, and general anesthesia should be based on the patient’s ability to cooperate, treatment invasiveness, systemic risk, and institutional rescue capacity, after providing appropriate explanation to the patients. Written informed consent involving guardians or caregivers must be obtained.

At the same time, dental sedation has risks specific to dentistry. In dental and oral surgical procedures, the operative field and airway often overlap. Blood, saliva, irrigation fluid, mouth props, dental instruments, patient positioning, and the operator’s hands may interfere with airway evaluation and emergency airway rescue. The Japanese Dental Society of Anesthesiology guidelines for intravenous conscious sedation emphasize that safe dental sedation requires preoperative assessment, appropriate monitoring, intraoperative management, postoperative observation, and preparedness for complications [2]. Furthermore, sedation exists on a continuum from conscious sedation to deep sedation and general anesthesia. The Japanese Dental Society of Anesthesiology practical guide for deep sedation emphasizes that deep sedation requires careful patient selection, appropriate personnel and facilities, and management by dentists experienced in general anesthesia and systemic patient management [3].

For these reasons, the 2026 revision should not be interpreted as encouraging indiscriminate expansion of dental sedation. Instead, it creates a new opportunity to connect reimbursement policy with patient safety. The essential question is not who is permitted to administer sedative drugs, but which patients should receive which depth of sedation, in what setting, and with what rescue capacity. International procedural sedation guidelines similarly emphasize preparation for deeper-than-intended sedation and the skills, monitoring systems, and emergency equipment needed to rescue patients from respiratory or cardiovascular compromise [4]. This perspective may also be useful to Asian and international readers, considering how dental sedation systems can be organized according to local resources and healthcare structures.

In this context, the role of dental anesthesiologists becomes increasingly important. They can assess indications for sedation, determine whether sedation or general anesthesia is appropriate, coordinate treatment plans with dental operators, design repeated-treatment strategies for patients with disabilities, educate clinical staff, establish emergency protocols, and collaborate with medical institutions. Their expertise also extends to designing systems that allow regional dental sedation services to function safely.

This perspective is particularly important in special-needs dentistry, geriatric dentistry, and dental care for medically compromised patients. The success of dental sedation should be evaluated not only by whether a single procedure is completed, but also by whether the approach reduces burdens on patients, families, caregivers, and community dental providers while maintaining safety and continuity of care. A recent narrative review described an important role of intravenous sedation in Japanese dental practice for older adults, medically compromised patients, patients with disabilities or with dental anxiety [5]. However, not all dental facilities should be expected to provide the same level of sedation care. A risk-based system is needed in which sedation depth and settings are determined according to patient risk and institutional rescue capacity.

The 2026 reimbursement revision indicates increased social recognition of dental anesthesiologists, while reminding us that such recognition must be accompanied by greater responsibility. Dental anesthesiologists should regard this revision as a call to lead quality improvement, safety management, education, standardization, and regional collaboration in dental sedation. The future of dental sedation will not be determined by reimbursement alone. Rather, it will depend on whether this revision leads to safer, more standardized, and more sustainable systems of care. Japan’s experience may offer one perspective on how policy, specialist training, and patient safety can be connected in dental sedation.
