Recycling-compatible electroplating of enriched tin-119 targets for the production of antimony-119 for targeted Auger electron therapy
Authors: Aivija Grundmane, Caterina F Ramogida, Valery Radchenko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimony-119 (119Sb) is a radionuclide of interest for targeted Auger electron therapy. Isotopically pure 119Sb can be produced by proton irradiation of enriched tin-119 (119Sn) targets in conventional widely available low energy medical cyclotrons. Enriched 119Sn is very costly (~ 20,000 CAD/gram), requiring recycling of the target material to make this production route economically viable. Herein, we investigate three Sn electroplating methods, evaluate the target quality, as well as method compatibility with subsequent 119Sb radiochemical purification steps.ResultsThree electroplating bath compositions (Sn(II) sulfate, Sn(IV) sols, and Sn(IV) chloride) for Sn deposition were tested and successfully implemented. One of the methods, utilizing a Sn(IV) chloride bath solution was able to achieve quantitative electroplating efficiency (98 ± 3% Sn deposited after 72 h), and an overall post-purification recovery of 84 ± 6% Sn, accounting for losses during the dissolution and purification steps. Moreover, this electroplating bath composition yielded the most homogeneous targets, both with respect to its structure, as well as lack of contaminants and the highest weight percentage of surface Sn (88 ± 2 wt%).ConclusionsAll three electroplating baths investigated were able to yield Sn targets of sufficient quality for irradiation, although they showed markedly different surface morphology and surface elemental composition. Sn(IV) chloride bath solution was found to be capable of recycling of 119Sn as recovered from the 119Sb purification step. Furthermore, this method yielded the best results in all aspects, displaying the highest homogeneity in surface morphology and elemental composition, which are desirable characteristics for beam tolerance and radionuclidic purity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41181-026-00468-6.Keywords: Electroplating, Tin, Antimony-119, Targetry, Radiopharmaceuticals, Tin-119, Targeted Auger electron therapy, Radiochemical purification

Background

Antimony-119 (119Sb) has recently become a radionuclide of interest for targeted Auger electron therapy (TAET) (Grundmane et al. 2024; Randhawa et al. 2021; Filosofov et al. 2021; Bernhardt and Forssell-Arons 2001; Thisgaard and Jensen 2008). It has a favourable half-life (t1/2 = 38.19 h) for clinical application and during its decay it emits almost exclusively Auger electrons (23.7 per decay) and conversion electrons, accompanied only by very low energy (< 30 keV) X-rays and gamma rays, resulting in very localized dose deposition offering the potential of TAET on a cellular level (Thisgaard and Jensen 2008; Eckerman and Endo 2008; Bastami et al. 2022; Zwaniga et al. 2025).

There are two main viable routes for the production of 119Sb: (a) direct production by proton bombardment of enriched tin-119 (119Sn) using the 119Sn(p,n)119Sb nuclear reaction and (b) generator production of metastable tellurium-119 m (119mTe) by proton bombardment of natural Sb (natSb) using 121Sb(p,3n)119mTe and 123Sb(p,5n)119mTe nuclear reactions (Thisgaard and Jensen 2009; Thisgaard et al. 2011; Bennett et al. 2019). The advantages of each production route have been discussed elsewhere, with this work focusing on the direct production route from 119Sn (Grundmane et al. 2024). The cross section of the 119Sn(p,n)119Sb nuclear reaction is very favourable at low proton energies, reaching a maximum of ~ 1100 millibarn at 11–12 MeV (Thisgaard and Jensen 2009). As most cyclotrons utilized in nuclear medicine are typically low energy (< 30 MeV), this production route is widely accessible worldwide without the requirement of additional, more sophisticated infrastructure (International Atomic Energy Agency 2026).

The inherent physical and chemical characteristics of the target material must be considered within the wider context of radiopharmaceutical production. At 232 ºC, Sn has a low melting point, requiring good design of both the target and the cooling system to avoid melting the target material during the irradiation. Suitable target systems have been proposed, showing Sn targets able to withstand 180 µA 16 MeV proton beams (Thisgaard et al. 2011). Desirably, Sn is a relatively non-toxic metal, guaranteeing safe radiopharmaceutical composition even if quantitative removal of the target material is not possible during the purification step (Winship 1988). Natural Sn (natSn) is also a plentiful resource—appropriate purity natSn (> 99.999%) for radioantimony (1xxSb) production is readily available at a relatively low cost (~ 20 CAD/gram) reducing the upfront cost of target material for initial proof of concept experimentation focusing on target design, production, and understanding basic radiochemistry of 1xxSb. Given that natSn consists of 10 stable isotopes, there are 7 other Sb radionuclides co-produced, some of which have cumbersome half-lives – 120mSb (t1/2 = 5.76 d), 122Sb (t1/2 = 65.4 h), 124Sb (t1/2 = 60.2 d), inherently limiting the use of 119Sb for pre-clinical study, while others are produced in significant amounts – 116mSb (t1/2 = 1.0 h), 117Sb (t1/2 = 2.8 h), 118mSb (t1/2 = 5 h), requiring extended cooldown duration after irradiation. Thus, for production of radioisotopically pure 119Sb, target enrichment is necessary. However, enrichment of the desired 119Sn target material is very costly, with a gram of 119Sn currently costing upwards of 20,000 CAD (~ 13,000 EUR). Considering that a typical target consists of tens to hundreds of milligrams of target material and is expected to produce nanograms to micrograms of the desired radionuclide, recovery and recycling of the target material is a priority to ensure affordable access to 119Sb based radiopharmaceuticals.

For cyclotron irradiation, solid targets are preferred where possible, to maximize the irradiation yields. Typical design consists of the target material (tens to hundreds of milligrams) deposited within a recess of a thermally conductive metal “coin” target backing material that can easily slot into the solid target holder system of conventional cyclotrons. Although foils may also be irradiated, due to the low melting point of Sn and large mass of target material required for a foil target (500–1000 mg), this strategy is not feasible for 119Sn targets and was not considered further in this work. Several methods of Sn deposition may be considered for target manufacturing. If starting from solid Sn stock material, the low melting point and malleability of Sn can be exploited to melt or press it onto the target backing, respectively. Unfortunately, after irradiation, the target material must be dissolved to liberate the produced 119Sb, which then will be purified and separated from the bulk target material, typically using column chromatography. Since recycling of 119Sn is necessary, quantitative recovery in metallic form following purification chemistry would be needlessly cumbersome, if not impossible. For this reason, electroplating is a highly attractive strategy for Sn target manufacturing.

Extensive reviews outlining the electrochemistry of Sn have been published by others (Walsh and Low 2016; Ashworth et al. 2015; Ammar et al. 1989). Two methods of manufacturing electroplated Sn targets that are currently routinely used for cyclotron production of 1xxSb have been published—Sn(II) sulfate bath and Sn(IV) hydroxide bath (Thisgaard and Jensen 2009; Olson et al. 2024). Both methods can produce targets of sufficient quality for cyclotron irradiations. Furthermore, both methods report suitable recycling strategies for recovering Sn after purification. However, the purification methods paired with the target recycling strategies reported in these publications have significant limitations for further application in radiopharmaceutical synthesis, such as large (> 5 mL) elution volumes, recovery matrices that are not suitable for 1xxSb radiolabeling (e.g. 0.8 M HCl), and/or complicated purification steps limiting method applicability for automation. The limitations of these purification methods, and others are discussed in more detail elsewhere (Randhawa et al. 2021; Grundmane et al. 2025a). Improved purification methods overcoming these issues were devised and recently published, recovering Sn as Sn(IV) chloride in hydrochloric acid (HCl) (Grundmane et al. 2025b). However, neither of the previously published Sn target recycling methods are capable of utilizing Sn in the form it is recovered, as Sn(IV) chloride in excess conc. HCl is not easily converted to Sn(II) sulfate or Sn(IV) hydroxide, prompting the development of a different electroplating approach.

Given the current lack of suitable Sn target material recycling methods capable of recycling Sn as recovered from the recently developed 1xxSb purification radiochemistry, herein, two methods of electroplating Sn were investigated to overcome the current limitations. Sn(IV) sols bath, initially proposed for application of corrosion resistant coatings was adapted from literature, utilizing Sn(IV) chloride pentahydrate as the starting material (Wang et al. 2017). Simple Sn(IV) chloride bath in hydrochloric acid was also investigated, drawing inspiration from Sn coatings of Cu for industrial applications. Sn from both bath compositions, alongside the current gold standard, Sn(II) sulfate bath, were directly electroplated on target backings using a conventional electroplating cell. The methods’ suitability for repeated recycling was evaluated by converting the recovered Sn(IV) HCl solution, after purification, into the corresponding bath solution, if possible. Testing of the trace metal composition throughout the purification process was conducted using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) to identify potential impurities. Target quality was evaluated by cyclotron irradiation, as well as scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis.

Materials and methods

Materials

Silver (Ag, 14 gauge, 0.999 fine silver) from RioGrande (Albuquerque, United States) and copper (Cu, C101 oxygen free electronic grade) from McMaster-Carr (Elmhurst, United States) were used as target backing material, which also doubled as the cathode. Platinum wire (Pt, 0.25 mm, 99.9% trace metals basis) was used as the anode and purchased from MilliporeSigma (Burlington, United States). Diamond polishing compounds (9 µm, 3 m, and 1 µm) were all purchased from Ted Pella (Redding, United States).

The reagents necessary for target production, tin(II) sulfate (SnSO4, ≥ 95%), tin(IV) chloride pentahydrate (SnCl4·5H2O, 98%), tin foil (Sn, 99.9%), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 96%, Suprapur grade), 4-hydroxybenzenesulfonic acid (phenolsulfonic acid, 65 wt. % in H2O), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37 wt. % in H2O, 99.999% trace metals basis), gelatin (from porcine skin, powder, microbiology grade), 2-naphthol (99%), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, contains inhibitor, 30 wt. % in H2O, ACS reagent) were sourced from MilliporeSigma. Ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH, 29%) was acquired from Avantor (Center Valley, United States), ethanol (anhydrous)—from Commercial Alcohols (Brampton, Canada), boric acid (H3BO3, 99.5% ACS reagent grade)—from Aldrich Chemical Company (Milwaukee, United States), DBE-300 resin—from TrisKem (Bruz, France), and acetylacetone (Hacac)—from Fisher Scientific Company (Fair Lawn, United States). Ultrapure water from a Millipore Milli-Q system (18 MΩ·cm) was used throughout.

Instrumental techniques

ICP-MS analysis was conducted at TRIUMF (Vancouver, Canada) using an Agilent 8900 Triple Quadrupole ICP-MS. All samples were prepared in two separate matrices, depending on the analyte of interest: 2% HNO3 for measurements of Al, Ba, Be, Cd, Ca, Cr, Co, Cu, Ga, Fe, Pb, Mg, Mn, Ni, K, Rb, Ag, Na, Sr, Tl V, and Zn, or 1% HCl + 0.5% L-cysteine for measurements of Sb, Au, Hf, Ir, Pd, Pt, Rh, Ru, Te, and Sn. Measurements for Be and Al were acquired in no gas mode. Measurements for all other elements were acquired in Helium mode. A multi-element internal standard containing Bi, Tb, Y, Ge, and Sc was used throughout. Each measured analyte was corrected against the internal standard that was closest in mass. Limit of detection was taken as the lowest measured standard concentration that could be distinguished from the blank.

SEM images and EDX data were obtained at Simon Fraser University (SFU) 4D LABS (Burnaby, Canada) with a Thermo Scientific Quattro S ESEM (Environmental Scanning Electron Microscope, Hillsboro, Oregon USA) equipped with a Thermo Scientific UltraDry 60 mm2 EDX detector. Samples were imaged without conductive coating, using a 20 keV beam at a spot size of 3 (64 pA) or 5 (1.0 nA). EDX maps were acquired with a spot size of 5 (1.0 nA).

The power supply used for electroplating was a BK Precision (Yorba Linda, United States) 9174B DC Power Supply 0–35 V, 3A/35–70 V,1.5A. The electroplating cell was made from acrylic and contained a well 1 cm in diameter (see Supplementary Information Fig. S1).

A 119Sn target was pressed using a 40-ton laboratory pellet press with built-in hydraulic press from Across International (Livingston, United States). A stainless steel disc of the same diameter as the target backing was placed in the press. A hardened steel pellet, with a diameter equivalent to that of the recess (1 cm) was placed on top of the 119Sn surface, followed by another stainless steel disc. The stack was secured in the press by lowering the piston. Then, pressure was applied by gentle operation of the hydraulic pump lever, until slight resistance was felt.

Electroplating

Tin(II) sulfate bath

Sn(II) sulfate bath was prepared as per Olson et al., in batches of 50 mL (Olson et al. 2024). H2SO4 (1.5 mL) and phenolsulfonic acid (1.7 mL) were added to a polypropylene centrifuge tube containing SnSO4 (~ 4.5 g), gelatin (100 ± 10 mg), and 2-naphthol (50 ± 5 mg). The resultant mixture was vortexed. Water (10 mL) was added to the mixture and vigorously vortexed until the gelatin appeared to be dissolved, indicated by foaming. Then, more water was added in increments of 10 mL and vortexed, until reaching a total volume of 50 mL. Aliquots of the bulk solution (1–4 mL) were then used as the electroplating bath solution. Sn targets were electroplated on Ag target backings under constant voltage of 3.0 V using a Pt wire anode.

Tin(IV) sols bath

Preparation of Sn(IV) sols bath was adapted from literature, according to which the following molar ratio at the given concentrations was found to produce the highest quality coatings: 1 mol SnCl4·5H2O (0.475 M): 1.85 mol NH4OH (0.878 M): 0.35 mol Hacac (0.167 M) (Wang et al. 2017). Same as for the Sn(II) sulfate bath, bulk batches of 50 mL were prepared and stored in polypropylene centrifuge tubes. SnCl4·5H2O (~ 8.4 g) was dissolved in a solution of Hacac (857 mL) and ethanol (25 mL), followed by the addition of NH4OH (6.686 mL) and water (25 mL). The resultant mixture was then stirred on a hotplate at 60 °C for 4 h at 500 rpm. Finally, H3BO3 (~ 1.55 g) was added to give a total concentration of 0.5 M H3BO3, and the mixture was further stirred for a few more minutes. Aliquots of the bulk solution (4 mL) were then used as the electroplating bath solution. Sn targets were electroplated on Ag target backings under constant current of 0.0079 A (current density of 1 A/dm2) using a Pt wire anode.

Tin(IV) chloride bath

Sn(IV) chloride bath was prepared to mimic a typical recovery fraction from the purification. Sn foil (50–150 mg) was dissolved in concentrated HCl (2 mL). H2O2 (200 µL) was added to the solution to oxidize the Sn. The resultant solution was allowed to react for no less than 30 min and was often left alone for weeks or even months before proceeding with the next step, representative of use case application, where recovered 119Sn may be stored for a long time before re-use. Right before electroplating, water (2 mL) was added to the solution to decrease its acidity and the solution vortexed. This was then used as the electroplating bath solution right away. Sn targets were electroplated on Cu or Ag target backings under constant current of 0.0079 A (current density of 1 A/dm2) using a Pt wire anode.

Enriched tin(IV)-119 chloride bath

119Sn(IV) chloride bath was prepared as per Sect. “Tin(IV) chloride bath”., with minor differences. Metallic 119Sn (200 mg) was dissolved in concentrated HCl (8 mL). Before electroplating, H2O2 (200 µL) was added to a 2 mL aliquot of the bulk solution to oxidize the Sn for no less than 30 min, as before. Right before electroplating, water (2 mL) was added to the solution to decrease its acidity and the solution vortexed. This was then used as the electroplating bath solution right away. 119Sn targets were electroplated on Ag target backings under constant current of 0.0079 A (current density of 1 A/dm2) using a Pt wire anode.

Tin deposition and recycling

The suitability of the tested electroplating methods for recycling Sn were evaluated in two main aspects. First, electroplating efficiency—per cent recovery of Sn from the different matrix compositions of the three electroplating baths (see Sect. “Electroplating”) with known Sn concentration was evaluated at different timepoints. When the corresponding timepoint was reached, the target was removed from the electroplating set-up, and any moisture and/or unadhered Sn was removed by dabbing the surface of the target with a WypAll L40 towel and exerting percussive force on the target until any remaining Sn was shown to be strongly adhered. Only the mass of the strongly adhered Sn was counted towards the per cent recovery of Sn. Note that for Sn(II) sulfate and Sn(IV) sols baths, the bulk solutions were assumed to have uniform distribution of Sn for this calculation, while for Sn(IV) chloride bath the exact mass of Sn in each bath was known with higher accuracy due to individual bath preparation.

Second, and most important, the overall recycling efficiency—per cent recovery of Sn encompassing the whole recycling process was evaluated. This included dissolving a Sn target of a known mass and conducting a typical separation as per Grundmane et al. (Grundmane et al. 2025b) Briefly, the target was dissolved in 12 M HCl (2–5 mL) under heat (~ 150 °C), followed by the addition of H2O2 to the target solution in a ratio of 1:10 parts by volume. The target solution was allowed to react for 30 min, after which it was directly loaded onto a column containing DBE-300 resin, followed by washing with 12 M HCl. During the load and wash steps, a volume equivalent to that of the target solution (typically 1–3 mL) + first 1 mL of the HCl wash eluted from the column was collected (Fig. 1), containing > 95% of the Sn as Sn(IV) chloride in 12 M HCl. The collected fraction then was either directly added to the electroplating bath, such as for the Sn(IV) chloride bath, which only required the addition of water prior to electroplating, or had to be chemically converted to a form such that the desired electroplating bath composition may be achieved. Several methods of incorporating the recovered Sn(IV) chloride fraction in Sn(IV) sols bath were investigated: utilizing the recovered fraction in place of Sn(IV) chloride pentahydrate directly, pH adjustment to pH 1 by adding excess NH4OH; molar ratio adjustments to ensure a 1 mol SnCl4·5H2O : 1.85 mol NH4OH: 0.35 mol Hacac ratio; recovery of Sn(IV) chloride by (a) boiling off the HCl on a hotplate, (b) boiling off HCl using a condenser setup, (c) removal of excess HCl under airflow, (d) adding ethanol to lower the boiling point, followed by boiling off the solution on a hotplate. No straightforward method of transforming Sn(IV) chloride to Sn(II) sulfate without the introduction of unwanted additives was devised in this work.

Target backing durability

Beam tolerance

Suitability of the Cu and Ag target backings for cyclotron production of 119Sb was tested experimentally, irradiating Sn targets electroplated on either target backing material using the 13 MeV cyclotron (TR13) at TRIUMF. Targets were irradiated for 1 h, starting at 15 A, and increasing the beam current by 5 µA with each subsequent irradiation, up to a maximum of 30 µA (maximum safe operable current on the TR13 for this target type based on its physical characteristics and expected yield). Subsequent irradiations at 30 A increasing the beam time duration were carried out, up to a maximum irradiation time of 4 h.

Chemical resistance

Equally importantly, the target backing material should have high durability with respect to target dissolution, and any material that does dissolve should not interfere with the subsequent purification of 119Sb. Hence, both target backing materials (without Sn) were placed in concentrated HCl and heated for 1 h in a glass beaker to replicate typical target dissolution conditions. Prior to use in this experiment, concentrated HCl was refluxed in the glass beakers and collected for measurement to account for the trace metal contribution from the beakers. The influence of polishing the target backing material with diamond paste prior to electroplating was also simultaneously evaluated by polishing half of the analyzed targets with diamond paste (9 m, followed by 3 µm, and finally 1 m) until a mirror-like surface was achieved.

Another experiment, placing Ag and Cu backed Sn targets (electroplated using the Sn(IV) chloride bath) in concentrated HCl was also conducted, dissolving the Sn using three methods: (1) in a glass beaker at room temperature (~ 20 °C) overnight, (2) in a glass beaker at ~ 200 °C (note that the exact temperature was not measured, and the hotplate reading was taken as true) for 1–2 h (depending on the thickness of Sn target, hotplate used, beaker location on the hotplate, etc.), and (3) placing the target back into the electroplating cell, ensuring that only the recess containing Sn is exposed to the acid. Then, to evaluate the effect of any dissolved target backing material on the separation chemistry, a typical purification was undertaken, collecting the following three fractions: first 2 mL recovered (bulk Sn), latter 9 mL of HCl wash (trace Sn), as well as the elute fraction in ethanol (where the 119Sb would be recovered) (Fig. 1). The elute fraction in ethanol was dried down and reconstituted in a small volume (50 mL) of aqua regia before dilution. All other samples were directly diluted for ICP-MS analysis.

Results

Electroplating

All three electroplating bath compositions (Sn(II) sulfate, Sn(IV) sols, and Sn(IV) chloride) were successfully implemented and resulted in Sn targets of similar visual appearance (Fig. 2) and sufficient quality (thickness, adherence, etc.) for irradiations using the TR13 cyclotron; none of the targets melted during irradiation. All methods yielded targets in the 50–150 mg Sn range after electroplating for 24–72 h. Enriched 119Sn targets were electroplated using the Sn(IV) chloride bath, resulting in more centralized deposits of non-uniform thickness (Fig. 3).

Enriched 119Sn targets electroplated on Ag backings using Sn(IV) chloride bath (~ 50 mg 119Sn, 72 h electroplating duration)

The best Sn electroplating efficiency was observed when using the Sn(IV) chloride bath (98 ± 3%) after 72 h (Table 1). This is in agreement with the results obtained using enriched 119Sn, recovering 193.6 out of 200.0 mg (96.8%) across 4 targets. Although the relationship between the Sn(IV) chloride solution storage duration and electroplating efficiency was not investigated in detail herein, given the high recovery and low standard deviation reported here, despite the wide range in storage duration prior to electroplating (30 min–few months), we did not observe any significant decrease in recovery upon long-term storage of the Sn(IV) chloride solution prior to electroplating. Sn(II) sulfate bath showed comparable but higher variance in recovery (95 ± 19% after 36 h), while Sn(IV) sols bath was found to be the least effective at electrodepositing Sn (60 ± 30% after 48 h), although this may be improved by utilizing longer electroplating time durations or by increasing the current. It should be stressed that the electroplating efficiency of the latter two methods, Sn(II) sulfate and Sn(IV) sols baths, has an additional uncertainty introduced due to the bulk preparation of these solutions—potential non-uniform dispersion of Sn in the bulk solution. Hence, more accurate determination of electroplating efficiency by preparation of individual bath solutions should be undertaken to validate this data with absolute certainty. Furthermore, the choice of maintaining constant voltage in the case of Sn(II) sulfate bath or constant current in the case of Sn(IV) sols and Sn(IV) chloride baths may also affect the electroplating speed and recovery, making direct comparison between the conditions used more difficult. Nonetheless, we chose to utilize constant voltage of 3.0 V for the Sn(II) sulfate bath to replicate the exact literature conditions that are currently the standard. Whereas for the other investigated methods, we sought to further improve the electroplating performance and target quality by applying constant current of 0.0079 A (1 A/dm2), which enables the deposition of more even layer thickness. Currents of 0.0790 A and 0.7900 A (current densities of 10 A/dm2, and 100 A/dm2, respectively) were tested to increase the electroplating speed for the Sn(IV) sols bath but resulted in the Sn being reduced on top of the cathode without adhering to it in any significant amounts (< 10 mg). Although further fine-tuning to increase the electroplating speed may be possible, it was not investigated further here.

Recycling of Sn

For an electroplating method to be compatible with 119Sn recycling from a Sn(IV) chloride solution in 12 M HCl, its bath composition must follow the following criteria: (a) the starting matrix of Sn(IV) chloride in 12 M HCl has to be easily converted to the form of Sn required for this method, (b) the number of steps and other reagents used for this conversion should be kept to a minimum to limit impurity and loss of the enriched material along the way. Sn(II) sulfate bath requires Sn sulfate as the source of Sn, which is not easily prepared from the starting matrix of Sn chloride; therefore, this method is incompatible with 119Sn recycling. Sn(IV) sols bath utilizes solid SnCl4·5H2O as the starting material, which is a similar form to that of the recovered Sn(IV) chloride solution, with the main difference being the excess concentrated HCl. Unfortunately, none of the conditions tested for the matrix conversion for the Sn(IV) sols bath (see Sect. “Tin deposition and recycling”) were able to yield successful electroplating results. Attempts to boil off or remove excess HCl (b.p. 48–108 °C depending on concentration) under airflow resulted in most of the volatile Sn(IV) chloride (b.p. 114 °C) being lost. Although fractional distillation may be possible, we sought to keep the procedure simple and less technically challenging to ensure the end process could be easily implemented widely, thus it was not investigated further. Attempts to alter the bath composition were also unsuccessful. Regardless of the adjustments made to account for the excess HCl present, no electroplating of Sn was achieved, resulting either in Sn being reduced on top of the cathode without adhering to the backing or remaining in solution altogether. Because of the unreliability of this method, further optimization was not pursued.

Overall recycling of Sn was successfully achieved using the Sn(IV) chloride bath (Table 2). However, presence of Cu impurity (introduced from target dissolution) was shown to inhibit electroplating, and a correlation between the mass amount of Cu and per cent Sn recovery was observed (Fig. 4). This is not surprising as Cu(II) chloride is used as a co-catalyst in Wacker process to continuously re-oxidize Pd (reduced form) to Pd(II) chloride (catalyst for the oxidation of ethylene to acetaldehyde), hence it likely plays a similar role in re-oxidizing the Sn as it is being reduced (Nair 2011). In contrast, presence of Ag was shown to have no notable effect on the Sn electroplating process. To validate this experiment, one recycling cycle with enriched 119Sn was conducted, showing 36.6/44.8 mg 119Sn deposited after recovery from the purification, corresponding to 81.7% overall recovery yield.

Surface characterization

Although the deposited Sn appeared similar to the naked eye regardless of the electroplating methodology used, upon closer inspection by SEM, each bath composition resulted in different surface morphology. Targets generated from Sn(IV) chloride bath showed the highest surface homogeneity without significant visible surface contamination or discrete crystal formation (Fig. 5), targets generated from Sn(IV) sols bath showed formation of larger clusters and much different fine structure (Fig. 6), while the targets generated by Sn(II) sulfate bath yielded a ‘barnacle-like’ structure, with significant presence of visible contaminants, particularly fibers (Fig. 7). Compared to targets produced by the Sn(II) sulfate bath, fewer of the fibers were present in targets produced by this Sn(IV) sols bath, while in the Sn(IV) chloride bath only one target contained any fibers at all, indicating that the latter method produces the smoothest targets, with lowest capability of tearing the lint-free napkin used for drying of the target surface after electroplating.

While the target surface morphology may be manipulated after electroplating, the elemental composition cannot. To evaluate this, further EDX analysis was performed, revealing a presence of different contaminants and crystals formed, depending on the bath composition used. Sn(IV) chloride bath resulted in targets with the highest surface homogeneity and percentage of Sn on the target surface (88 ± 2% by weight), with only one target showing identifiable contamination in the form of some napkin fibers (Figs. 11, 12). Sn(IV) sols bath yielded targets with significant surface heterogeneity and even observable formation of discrete oxide and chloride crystal clusters (Figs. 11, 13). Finally, targets electroplated using the Sn(II) sulfate bath contained contaminants which were predominantly carbon-based and could be categorized as either flakes or napkin fibers (Figs. 11, 14). Based on their shape, the flakes are likely undissolved pieces of 2-naphtol and gelatin, which were used as components for the electroplating bath.

Target backing durability

All the tin targets remained intact under all irradiation parameters, up to the maximum beam current of 30 µA and irradiation duration of 4 h, regardless of the target backing material used. Discolouration of the tin surface to a darker grey colour was observed after irradiation, alongside some structural amorphization (Supplementary Information Figs. S3–S4). While the textural changes could be indicative of minor melting beginning to occur, no contamination within the cyclotron or loss of target material was detected, indicating that the targets can safely withstand the tested conditions.

Predictably, the durability of the Ag target backing during the target dissolution process in HCl was shown to be higher than that of Cu. Under the tested conditions ~ 1 mg of Cu was found after dissolution compared to the ~ 0.1 mg of Ag (Fig. 18). Cu target backings also showed presence of additional impurities in Zn, Ni and Cr, that were not present for Ag target backings. Polishing of the target backing surface did not show any significant difference with respect to trace metal impurities.

The method of tin target dissolution showed a significant difference in impurity of Cu after dissolution (Fig. 19). Dissolution in a glass beaker at room temperature overnight showed almost ten times more Cu than overnight dissolution in an electroplating cell or 1 h long dissolution in a glass beaker at elevated heat. Although significant amounts of Cu and Ag were detected after the dissolution process, these impurities were subsequently separated from Sb in the purification process and collected in the same fraction as the bulk Sn target material, with no significant level of Cu or Ag present in the elute regardless of the dissolution method used. In all cases, the amount of Sn found in the elute fraction was low, corresponding to a removal of 99.97 – 99.999% of Sn during the purification process, depending on the dissolution method used.

Screening of other impurities in the elute showed significant presence of several elements (Fig. 20). The amount of impurities present was fairly comparable, regardless of the target dissolution method or target backing material used, with the most notable difference reflected in Cu and Ag contents. One important exception to this was the content of Sb, reporting 1100 ± 600 ng Sb (n = 6) when Ag backed targets were used, compared to 9 ± 8 ng Sb (n = 3) for Cu backed targets under equivalent conditions. To validate this, we repeated the experiment with Ag, observing the same result. Since the experiments with Ag backings were conducted some time after those with Cu backings, we suspect the glass beakers utilized in the experiment were contaminated, giving rise to an artificially high Sb result. Therefore, to further confirm this possibility and to validate this experiment with an Ag backed 119Sn target, we performed a typical separation as per natSn targets, with the exception of using a Teflon beaker for the dissolution step and measured the contents of 119Sn and Sb in the elute fraction. The results showed 2 μg of 119Sn, corresponding to 99.996% removal of 119Sn during the purification and 9 ng of Sb, which agrees with the data acquired with Cu backed natSn targets, therefore indicating likely contamination of the glass beakers rather than a Sb contribution from the Ag backing.

Discussion

Target quality

All three electroplating methods tested were able to successfully deposit Sn with sufficient adherence—no dislodgement was observed upon stress testing. Although none of the targets melted during irradiation on the TR13 cyclotron, the irradiation parameters used in this study were relatively mild. While detailed microstructure analysis is beyond the scope of this work, the structural differences shown as a result of the different electroplating bath composition used may prove to affect the durability of the target material under higher beam currents and/or longer irradiation time durations. We hypothesize that the more homogeneous deposits obtained using the Sn(IV) chloride bath would likely be able to withstand harsher irradiation conditions relatively better than the other two methods tested due to more uniform cooling.

It should be noted that enriched 119Sn targets electroplated using the Sn(IV) chloride bath displayed higher surface heterogeneity compared to natSn deposits. Due to the expensive nature of the enriched material, electroplating bath solutions containing ~ 50 mg 119Sn per solution were prepared and then electroplated for 72 h to ensure maximum depletion of the solution, as opposed to 100–150 mg natSn, which was electroplated for 48–72 h. The combination of the lower mass and increased electroplating time ensured that 96.8% of the 119Sn could be utilized, but also resulted in the solution being depleted faster, hence the excessive current density applied to the depleted solution would have given rise to the hydrogen evolution reaction. The generation of hydrogen causes bubbling, which is evidenced by the generated structural holes in target surface, seen clearly in the SEM images (Fig. 8). Although shorter electroplating durations and/or variable current density approaches may be applied here to improve the target quality, we suggest exploiting the malleable nature of Sn by utilizing a target press. Figure 10 shows high surface heterogeneity after pressing the target, addressing the limitations imposed by structural morphology.

Most importantly, the elemental composition of the target cannot be altered after the electroplating process is finished. The Sn(IV) chloride bath clearly performed the best in this category as well, showing a notable decrease in the level of surface impurities found in the targets electroplated using this method compared to the others (Fig. 11), minimizing the production of undesired radioactive co-products and maximizing the yield of the desired nuclear reaction. The EDX data acquired for 119Sn was generally in agreement with that of natSn, albeit with a slight increase in the amount of Cl found on the target surface, likely due to the same reasons as for the structural differences. The amount of surface Cl was shown to increase even further when using recycled 119Sn recovered from the purification (Fig. 16). Crucially, the utilization of a manual hydraulic press did not introduce any additional surface impurity that could be detected by EDX (Fig. 17), offering no observable downside for the introduction of a pressing step after electroplating.

Tin recycling

Quantitative electrochemical deposition of Sn is essential for the electroplating bath composition to be suitable for the recycling of the expensive 119Sn target material. Although electrochemical deposition of Sn was found to be achievable by all electroplating bath compositions, ultimately, only the Sn(IV) chloride bath was found to be compatible with recycling Sn in a matrix as recovered from the purification. The electroplating efficiency when utilizing the Sn(IV) chloride bath was shown to be near-quantitative after electroplating for 48 h (90 ± 8%, Ag or Cu cathode, n = 13) and quantitative after 72 h (96 ± 4%, Ag or Cu cathode, n = 9) (Table 1). The electroplating efficiency of 119Sn is in agreement with these results, reporting a total of 96.8% of target material deposited after 72 h (Ag cathode, n = 4).

The overall recovery of Sn using this method, accounting for losses during the purification process, was found to be 84 ± 6% (Ag or Cu cathode, n = 9) in the absence of Cu, indicating that some Sn was lost during the target processing (dissolution and purification). In the presence of Cu, the recovery percentage dropped rapidly, and a correlation was found between the amount of Sn recovered and the amount of Cu present (Fig. 4), indicating that Cu is an unsuitable target material when recycling of Sn is required. The overall recycling recovery for 119Sn was found to be 82% (Ag cathode, n = 1), corroborating the results of natSn targets.

Influence of target backing material

No differences were observed in the performance of Cu and Ag backings during irradiation or as the cathode material. However, the interference posed by the dissolved Cu material in subsequent recycling cycles presents Ag as the better target backing material for this application. Furthermore, the cross sections of the 63Cu(p,n)63Zn and 65Cu(p,n)65Zn nuclear reactions are considerably high, producing an estimated maximum (calculated without accounting for the attenuation by Sn) of 100 GBq of 63Zn (t1/2 = 38.49 min), and 40 MBq of 65Zn (t1/2 = 243.93 d) at the end of bombardment under the maximum allowable TR13 irradiation conditions (30 µA, 4 h), compared to the 20 GBq of 107Cd (t1/2 = 6.50 h) and 10 MBq of 109Cd (t1/2 = 461.9 d) using the 107Ag(p,n)107Cd and 109Ag(p,n)109Cd nuclear reaction, considering the Ag backing. Most importantly, the energy of the decay emissions of the longer lived 109Cd is very low (< 90 keV) and thus easily shielded, while the fields produced by the 1115 keV (I = 50.12%) gamma emission of 65Zn are much more cumbersome to deal with. For these reasons, Ag should be chosen as the target backing material of choice, where possible.

Conclusions

We report the implementation and testing of different Sn electroplating bath compositions for cyclotron target manufacturing—Sn(II) sulfate, Sn(IV) sols, and Sn(IV) chloride baths. All three electroplating baths yielded Sn targets of suitable quality for cyclotron irradiation with 13 MeV protons. Upon closer investigation by SEM and EDX analysis, each method resulted in different surface elemental composition and morphology. Targets produced using the Sn(IV) chloride bath performed the best in all aspects, producing the most homogeneous structural deposits and highest surface percentage of Sn (88 ± 2 wt% Sn), compared to formation of discrete Sn clusters and lower surface percentage of Sn for the Sn(II) sulfate (69 ± 7 wt% Sn) and Sn(IV) sols baths (67 ± 6 wt% Sn). The Sn(IV) chloride bath also achieved the highest electroplating efficiency (98 ± 3% Sn deposited after 72 h) compared to that of Sn(II) sulfate (70 ± 20% Sn deposited after 48 h) and Sn(IV) sols baths (60 ± 30% Sn deposited after 48 h), although this may further be optimized for the other methods by increasing electroplating time duration or changing the applied current. Furthermore, Sn(IV) chloride bath was the only method found to be compatible with recycling of Sn from a starting solution of Sn(IV) chloride in 12 M HCl, with an overall post-purification recovery of 84 ± 6% Sn. All experiments conducted with enriched 119Sn were in agreement with those acquired for the natSn(IV) chloride bath, validating the method suitability for the recycling of enriched 119Sn target material. In summary, we report a method of recovering and reusing enriched 119Sn target material with minimal losses, as recovered from the production of 119Sb.
