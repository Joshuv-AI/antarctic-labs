In this issue of the Journal of Investigative Dermatology Innovations, Liu et al (2026) report both reassuring and somewhat concerning insights into acne pathobiology and risk factors.

The reassuring findings come from a thorough replication analysis that was conducted using the SMCGES cohort. They are reassuring in the sense that they confirm previous data, supporting the validity of the authors’ approach as well as, importantly, suggesting that we do not have to rethink our current models of acne pathogenesis (again [van Steensel, 2026]). Liu et al (2026) evaluated 88 previously reported acne-associated SNPs from seven GWAS in 2741 cases from the Chinese subset of the SMCGES and 2235 controls. Two loci were replicated, 1 near TGFB2 and 1 in PNPLA3. TGFB2 is a robust hit that has appeared in multiple GWAS (Van Steensel, 2025), firmly implicating TGF-ß signaling in acne pathogenesis. The risk allele lowers TGFB2 expression (Liu et al, 2026), which is expected to negatively impact tissue remodeling, contributing to comedogenesis (de Vos et al, 2019, 2018; Wilson et al, 2014) as well as scarring (Penn et al, 2012). TGF-ß signaling also regulates sebocyte differentiation and lipogenesis, where activation of the pathway keeps cells in an undifferentiated state (McNairn et al, 2013). Hence, it could be speculated that loss of activity affects sebaceous progenitors in accordance with the comedo-switch hypothesis (Saurat, 2015).

PNPLA3 codes for patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing protein, a triglyceryl lipase that is highly expressed in the sebaceous gland and associated with its mid-late stage differentiation (Düz et al, 2026). The PNPLA3 risk allele (I148M) was previously identified as an acne risk allele (Mitchell et al, 2022). It has been extensively characterized and increases enzyme activity (eg, Wang et al, 2025). It is by now well understood that abnormal lipid metabolism can disrupt sebaceous differentiation (Smith and Thiboutot, 2008). In addition, PNPLA3 has a role in retinoic acid (RA) metabolism (Pirazzi et al, 2014). Its increased activity might interrupt RA signaling, a crucial component of pilosebaceous differentiation and homeostasis. Indeed, acne is associated with reduced RA signaling in the skin (Durgin et al, 2026). RA can counteract Wingless signaling, an excess of which is associated with abnormal differentiation of sebaceous progenitors and comedo formation (Shang et al, 2022). Accordingly, a gain-of-function allele in WNT10A was previously identified as an acne risk factor (Petridis et al, 2018).

So far, not much new under the sun—but now for the novelty. Using the data from the SMCGES, Liu et al (2026) conducted the first-ever gene-environment interaction analysis for acne in an Asian population. Several environmental factors were assessed. These included intake of milk and fast food—for which associations with acne have been previously both claimed and refuted (Dhakal et al, 2025; Melnik and Schmitz, 2008)—but also screen usage time. Although the authors do not explicitly state what prompted this investigation, they might have been inspired by previously published surveys showing that teens—the age group most likely to develop acne— average more than 8 hours of daily screen usage (https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/168509).

Whatever the inspiration might have been, from all the environmental factors assessed in the study cohort, screen usage time had the most significant interaction with previously identified risk loci. This finding is concerning, as a great many people spend (too) much time looking at screens. The association with acne seems difficult to reconcile with our current understanding ; therefore, the variable “screen time” needs unpacking. Is it really a causal factor, or an indicator of lifestyle interacting with the genetic risk factors? Causality might be inferred by noting that chronic exposure to light can disrupt circadian rhythms, suppressing melatonin secretion, which might in turn affect pilosebaceous homeostasis (Lyons et al, 2019). One might also hypothesize that the blue light emitted by computer and phone screens could be a risk factor, but there is presently no evidence to support this notion (Werner et al, 2025).

In agreement with Liu et al (2026) a more straightforward explanation is that screen time is associated with behaviors that affect acne risk. Epidemiological studies have consistently associated screen time with a sedentary lifestyle, obesity, and intake of foods with a high energy density or high glycemic index. Diet in particular is associated with acne, and it has been speculated that this could be through the insulin/IGF1-PI3K-FOXO-protein kinase B-mTORC1 axis (Melnik, 2023). Although this mechanism remains speculative, the energy link is probably real as there is evidence to suggest that metformin can reduce acne severity (Szefler et al, 2024). Consistent with this interpretation, functional annotation (Gene Ontology) of the variants interacting with screen time indicated processes related to inflammatory signaling, lipid metabolism, and responses to oxidative stress (Liu et al, 2026). These are all pathways that are related to metabolic state and environmental exposure.

Before attempting to arrive at a conclusion, it should be noted that the Chinese subset of the SMCGES cohort consists of 6225 young adults (mean age around 25 years), 2345 of whom underwent dermatological assessment (Huang et al, 2025). Considering that half of them were found to have scarring, one might surmise that acne severity in this population is skewed towards moderate/severe. Given the strong influence of genetics on disease severity, environmental factors may be less likely to trigger acne in those that are less burdened with risk alleles.

Thus, the majority of people with acne—specifically those who have mild disease—might not benefit significantly from such lifestyle advice as one might conceive of after reading this paper, at least when it comes to their skin. For those who present with more severe acne, lifestyle modification might be a worthwhile addition to any medication that is prescribed.

Finally, the data presented by Liu et al (2026) underscore the need for continued study of acne pathogenesis. The disease is a clear-cut case of genetic predisposition triggered by environmental factors that are neither sufficiently mapped nor properly understood, such as pollution (Liu et al, 2018). This knowledge gap must be addressed; addressing a patient’s environment and lifestyle is much more feasible than changing their genetics.
