An epidemiological survey of HPV infection among women in Southern Hebei, China
Authors: Heng Xiao, Yanbin Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the genotypes and distribution patterns of human papillomavirus (HPV) infections among women in southern Hebei, China.MethodsCervical specimens were collected from female clinical patients (including both outpatients and inpatients) and health examination participants at Handan Central Hospital between September 2022 and August 2025. HPV genotyping was performed using a real-time fluorescent PCR method with the HybriBio (Chaozhou, China) 23-type HPV nucleic acid genotyping kit (fluorescent PCR), which detects 23 HPV genotypes (16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 68, 6, 11, 42, 43, 44, 53, 66, 73, 81, 82). The study analyzed the positivity rates of each genotype, the proportion and genotype distribution among different age groups and cervical diseases. ResultsAmong 41,045 cervical samples, the HPV positivity rate was 24.20%. The top three most prevalent genotypes were 52, 16, and 58, respectively. The detection rate of high-risk HPV (hrHPV) was 20.22%, while that of low-risk HPV (lrHPV) was 7.40%. The positivity rates were highest among women aged <20 (40.27%), >60 (29.30%), and 51–60 years (28.00%), monotypic infections were predominant across all age groups, and the positivity rates were significantly higher among patients with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia and cervical cancer compared to the general population. ConclusionThis study on HPV genotype distribution among women in the southern Hebei region provides strong evidence for establishing a cervical cancer screening and prevention system in the area, as well as valuable epidemiological data for the development and application of HPV vaccines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-026-03222-1.

Cervical cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors among women. In recent years, its incidence and mortality rates in China have continued to rise, with a trend toward younger patients. Reports indicate that there are approximately 110,000 new cases of cervical cancer and about 59,000 deaths annually in China, posing a serious threat to women’s health [1]. Cervical cancer is also one of the cancers with relatively well-defined causes. Reports indicate that human papillomavirus (HPV) infection can be detected in 99.7% of cervical cancer patients, and its development is closely associated with long-term, recurrent HPV infection [1]. HPV is a circular double-stranded DNA virus with high species and tissue specificity. It primarily infects the stratified squamous epithelium of specific sites on the skin and mucous membranes in humans. Both men and women can be infected, with transmission occurring primarily through sexual activity. Reports indicate that 80% of sexually active women will contract HPV at some point in their lives [2]. To date, over 200 HPV genotypes have been identified. Based on their oncogenic potential, they are classified into two major categories: low-risk HPV (lrHPV) and high-risk HPV (hrHPV). Low-risk types primarily cause benign lesions such as genital warts, while high-risk types are primarily associated with the development of cervical cancer and cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) [3]. Common high-risk HPV types include HPV16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, common low-risk types include HPV6, 11, 42, and 43. In recent years, the prevention and control of HPV infection have become a key focus in the global public health sector. The World Health Organization (WHO), the European Union, and other countries recommend HPV nucleic acid testing as the method of choice for HPV screening [4, 5]. HPV nucleic acid testing offers advantages such as high sensitivity, ease of operation, and high reproducibility, making it particularly suitable for large-scale HPV screening. However, HPV infection exhibits significant racial and regional variations. The predominant infection genotypes, age distribution patterns, and the degree of association with cervical lesions may differ across regions. Therefore, studying HPV infection trends, genotype distribution characteristics, and regional prevalence is of great clinical significance for HPV vaccine development, data monitoring, and the prevention and control of cervical cancer. As a major transportation hub and densely populated city in southern Hebei Province, Handan has yet to have the epidemiological characteristics of HPV infection among women systematically elucidated, and there is a lack of targeted screening and prevention data to support these efforts. This study analyzed HPV testing data from over 40,000 women at Handan Central Hospital over the past three years to systematically investigate the prevalence of HPV infection, genotype distribution, age characteristics, infection patterns, and association with cervical lesions among women in this region. The aim is to provide scientific evidence for formulating regional HPV infection prevention and control strategies and improving the effectiveness of cervical cancer prevention and control. The findings are reported below.

Materials and methods

Source of specimens

Female patients aged 14–86 years who visited Handan Central Hospital for clinical consultations (including both outpatients and inpatients) or for health examinations between September 1, 2022, and August 31, 2025, were selected. They were divided into six experimental groups based on age (under 20 years, 21–30 years, 31–40 years, 41–50 years, 51–60 years, > 60 years).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria:

(1) Female patients aged 14–86 years who underwent cervical HPV testing at Handan Central Hospital between September 1, 2022, and August 31, 2025;

(2) Availability of complete demographic information (age, source of specimen);

(3) Valid HPV genotyping results with interpretable internal control (β-globin Ct ≤ 40).

Exclusion criteria:

(1) Samples with invalid PCR results (β-globin Ct > 40 or no Ct in any channel) after one repeat test;

(2) Incomplete clinical records (e.g., missing age or specimen source information).

Study groups

Participants were divided into two groups based on the reason for HPV testing.

Clinical patient group: women who underwent HPV testing due to gynecological symptoms, abnormal screening results, or confirmed cervical diseases (including both outpatients and inpatients).

Health examination group: asymptomatic women who voluntarily underwent cervical HPV screening during routine health checkups.

The clinical patient group was divided to some diagnostic subgroups: Cervicitis group (diagnosed by clinical examination/colposcopy), CIN/cervical cancer group – (confirmed by histopathology), Other diagnoses (e.g., benign polyps, non-neoplastic conditions, hormonal imbalance, etc.)

HPV positivity rates and multiple infection rates were compared between the two groups using the chi-square test (see Statistical methods).

Sample collection

Cervical specimens were collected using a disposable cervical cell collector (HybriBio, Chaozhou, China) and a disposable sterile sampling swab (Hetai, Guangzhou, China). Patients were instructed to avoid vaginal douching, use of intravaginal medications (e.g., contraceptive creams), and application of acetic acid or iodine solutions for three days prior to sampling. Sexual intercourse was avoided within 24 h before the test.

After exposing the cervix with a speculum, excess secretions were gently wiped away. The cervical brush was inserted into the cervical os and rotated in a single direction for 4–5 rotations. The brush head was then detached and placed into a tube containing cell preservation solution. The brush handle was snapped off, the cap of the collection tube was tightly secured, and the sample was labeled. The collection tube was stored upright at 4 °C and tested within 7 days.

Instruments and reagents

HPV genotyping was performed using SLAN-96P real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR systems ( Hongshi, Shanghai, China; multiple instruments of the same model) with the Chaozhou HybriBio 23-type HPV nucleic acid genotyping kit (HybriBio, Chaozhou, China; fluorescent PCR method). During the three-year study period, multiple production lots of the kit were used (lot numbers available upon request), all following the manufacturer’s instructions. The kit detects 23 HPV genotypes, including 17 high-risk types (HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 73, 82) and 6 low-risk types (HPV 6, 11, 42, 43, 44, 81).

Testing method

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from cervical samples using the lysis buffer supplied with the HybriBio 23-type HPV genotyping kit (HybriBio, Chaozhou, China). Briefly, 1 mL of the cell preservation solution was transferred to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min, after which the supernatant was discarded. The resulting pellet was washed with 500 µL of cell preservation solution, mixed thoroughly, and centrifuged again for 2 min. Following removal of the supernatant, 50 µL of lysis buffer was added, and the mixture was vortexed and then heated in a boiling water bath for 10 min. After a final centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant containing the extracted DNA was collected and used directly for PCR amplification. All extraction steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Reagent preparation

The six PCR master mix tubes (one for each detection well) were thawed at room temperature, mixed by inverting, and briefly centrifuged. DNA polymerase was similarly thawed and centrifuged. For each sample, a reaction mixture was prepared by combining 17.5 µL of PCR master mix and 0.5 µL of DNA polymerase per well.

Sample loading

Two microliters of the extracted sample DNA, negative control (nuclease-free water), or positive control (cloned plasmid DNA containing HPV16, 39, 58, 11, 53 and β-globin) were added to the corresponding PCR reaction wells.

PCR amplification

Amplification was performed on a SLAN-96P real-time PCR system using four fluorescence detection channels (FAM, HEX, ROX, Cy5). The assignment of HPV genotypes to each channel and well is shown in Table 1. The thermal cycling program was: 95 °C for 10 min (1 cycle); 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 60 s (fluorescence data collection), 45 cycles; followed by 38 °C for 5 s (1 cycle).

Result interpretation

HPV genotyping was performed using a real-time PCR assay with the HybriBio 23-type kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The kit provides semi-quantitative detection of 23 HPV genotypes based on Ct value cutoffs as specified below. A run was considered valid only if the negative control showed no Ct in any channel and the positive control showed a Ct ≤ 36 in the corresponding channels. A sample was interpreted as negative when β-globin showed a Ct ≤ 40 in the Cy5 channel and all other channels showed no Ct. A positive result was defined as a Ct value < 40 with a typical S-shaped amplification curve in any channel other than β-globin, indicating positivity for the corresponding genotype(s). Samples with Ct values between 40 and 45 were re-tested; a Ct < 40 on repeat testing was considered positive, otherwise negative. The absence of any Ct in all channels (including β-globin) indicated insufficient sample quality or PCR inhibition, requiring re-sampling.

Quality controls

Each run included a negative and a positive control as described above. The run was accepted only if the negative control showed no amplification and the positive control showed amplification for all six targets (HPV16, 39, 58, 11, 53, β-globin) with Ct ≤ 36, covering all four fluorescence channels. No invalid runs occurred in this study.

To ensure the adequacy of pre-analytical steps, the β-globin gene (co-amplified in the Cy5 channel alongside HPV targets) served as an endogenous internal control for specimen cellularity, DNA extraction efficiency, and the absence of PCR inhibitors. Only samples with a β-globin Ct ≤ 40 (as defined in Sect. 1.6.5) were considered evaluable. Furthermore, all cervical samples were collected and processed under optimized conditions: specimen collection followed the patient preparation instructions (avoidance of douching, vaginal medications, and sexual intercourse prior to sampling), samples were stored at 4 °C and tested within 7 days, and DNA extraction was performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s protocol using the kit-provided lysis buffer. These measures collectively ensured that pre-analytical variables did not compromise the validity of the HPV genotyping results.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 24.0 software. Categorical data are expressed as counts or percentages. Chi-square tests were used to compare HPV positivity rates and multiple infection rates between the clinical patient group and the health examination group. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sample size justification

This was a retrospective study including all consecutive female patients who underwent HPV testing at Handan Central Hospital between September 1, 2022, and August 31, 2025. No a priori sample size calculation was performed. Nevertheless, with a total of 41,045 participants and an observed HPV positivity rate of 24.20%, the 95% confidence interval for the overall prevalence is approximately ± 0.41% (assuming simple random sampling), providing high precision. This sample size far exceeds the typical requirement for regional HPV prevalence surveys (often > 1,000 participants) and ensures adequate statistical power for subgroup analyses.

HPV prevalence and distribution of HPV genotypes

Among 41,045 female patients, 9,931 tested positive for HPV, resulting in a prevalence rate of 24.20%. High-risk HPV (hrHPV) was detected in 20.22% of all women, accounting for 83.6% of all HPV-positive cases. The top three genotypes were 52(4.40%), 16(4.16%), and 58(3.16%). These three types also ranked among the top three in terms of positivity rates across all 23 genotypes. The infection rate for low-risk HPV(lrHPV) types was 7.40%, with the top three genotypes being 42(2.36%), 81(1.83%), and 44(1.49%). The specific distribution is shown in Fig. 1.

Age distribution of HPV infections

Among the 41,045 female patients, screening participation was primarily concentrated in the 31–60 age group. The HPV positivity rate exhibited a U-shaped distribution: the highest rate was in women < 20 years (40.27%), followed by > 60 years (29.30%) and 51–60 years (28.00%). The lowest rates were observed in the 31–40 years group (21.51%). Differences among age groups were statistically significant (χ² = 258.798, P < 0.05) (Table 5). When analyzing HPV infection types by age group, HPV16 was the predominant type in the < 20, 20–30, and > 60 groups (10.92%,4.94%,6.72%, respectively), whereas HPV52 dominated in the 31–40, 41–50, and 51–60 age groups (3.71%, 3.64%, and 5.77%, respectively). The distribution of infections by genotype across different age groups is shown in Supplementary Table S2. These patterns suggest that HPV16 may be more associated with early and late-onset infections, while HPV52 is the main driver in middle adulthood.

Comparison of HPV positivity rates among health examination group, and cervicitis group, cervical intraepithelial neoplasia(CIN) or cervical cancer group

As defined in the Methods section, the HPV positivity rates of the clinical patient group and the health examination group were compared. Among the clinical patient group, we focused on cervicitis and CIN/cervical cancer subgroups as they represent the most common HPV-related diagnoses; other benign conditions (not shown) were not included in this comparison. Further stratification by final diagnosis showed that the HPV positivity rates increased in the following order: the general health screening group, the cervical inflammation group, and the cervical intraepithelial neoplasia/cervical cancer group. The differences in HPV positivity rates among the three groups were highly significant (χ² = 952.313, P < 0.05). Similarly, multiple infection rates increased stepwise from health examination (24.42%) to cervicitis (30.80%) to CIN/cancer (34.89%) (χ² = 26.882, P < 0.05) (Table 7). Regarding genotype distribution (Supplementary Table S3), HPV16 was the most common type in the CIN/cancer group (10.78%), followed by HPV58 (7.01%) and HPV52 (6.54%). In contrast, the health examination group showed a predominance of HPV52 (2.31%), HPV53 (1.68%), and HPV58 (1.53%). The marked elevation of HPV16 in cervical lesions underscores its high oncogenic potential, while HPV52 and 58 are also important contributors in this region.

Discussion

Currently, the role of HPV in the development and progression of cervical cancer is well established. HPV infection is considered a necessary prerequisite for the development of cervical cancer, in particular, persistent infection with high-risk HPV types has been shown to be associated with abnormal proliferation of cervical epithelial cells through mechanisms such as the activation of oncogenes and the suppression of tumor suppressor genes, ultimately contributing to the progression to cervical cancer. As early as 1983, the renowned scholar Zur Hausen proposed that HPV infection might cause cervical cancer [6]. In 1995, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) officially declared cervical HPV infection to be the primary cause of cervical cancer. In 2005, cervical HPV testing was recommended as a method for cervical cancer screening. In 2014, the World Health Organization declared HPV genotyping to be a crucial primary screening tool for cervical cancer. Currently, there are various methods available for HPV genotyping, primarily including real-time quantitative PCR and microfluidic chip technologies. In recent years, methods for HPV detection using urine samples have also been developed [7]. The real-time PCR method selected for this study is currently the most widely used genotyping method. It can detect 23 genotypes, including 17 high-risk HPV types and 6 low-risk HPV types. With its high sensitivity, it serves as a valuable screening method for HPV infection.

Regarding the HPV positivity rate, the rate of HPV infection among women in the southern Hebei province in this study was 24.20%. To provide a detailed comparison with previous work in China, we reviewed HPV prevalence data from multiple regions. Our observed positivity rate (24.20%) is similar to that reported in Chengdu (20.67%) [8], Xiamen (25.24%) [9], Zhengzhou (21.31%) [10], and Chongqing (21.41%) [11], higher than that in Shanghai (18.9%) [1], Beijing (17.28%) [12], and Dongguan (18.3%) [13], but lower than that in Kunming (36.84%) [14]. This broad comparison indicates that HPV prevalence in southern Hebei falls within the mid-range of Chinese cities, with significant regional variability likely attributable to differences in economic development and education level.

With regard to genotype distribution, the predominance of HPV52, HPV16, and HPV58 in our region is consistent with the pattern observed in Beijing and Dongguan [12, 13], but differ from the patterns observed in Chongqing [11], where HPV16, 52, and 58 are the predominant genotypes. This suggests that the distribution of HPV genotypes exhibits distinct regional specificity.

No previous large-scale HPV epidemiological study has been conducted specifically in Handan or southern Hebei Province. Therefore, our findings provide the first baseline data for this region, which will be valuable for future comparative analyses and public health planning.

Besides, regional differences are also observed in terms of the spectrum of carcinogenic risk associated with different genotypes. Research has shown that, the most common HPV genotypes globally among cervical cancer patients are HPV16 and HPV18, however, the combined prevalence and attributable risk of HPV52 and HPV58 in invasive cervical cancer in East Asia are significantly higher than in other regions [15]. In this study, genotypes 52, 16, and 58 had the highest infection rates, this provides a basis for selecting HPV vaccination strategies in the Handan region. Currently, the bivalent and quadrivalent vaccines available in China primarily target HPV types 16 and 18, while the 9-valent vaccine targets types 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58. Based on the findings of this study, promoting the 9-valent vaccine may better align with the prevention and control needs of the Handan region.

This study found that the prevalence of human papillomavirus (HPV) infection among women undergoing health examinations showed a year-on-year downward trend (decreasing from 14.34% to 13.46%), and the general population undergoing health examinations typically better reflects the prevalence trends of HPV infection in the general population. Statistical analysis indicates that the magnitude of this downward trend is not statistically significant. From these data, we can observe that as HPV vaccination becomes more widespread, the overall HPV infection rate is stabilizing, showing only a gradual decline. In contrast, the infection rate among women seeking clinical care has risen year by year (from 27.57% to 29.33%), indicating that as cervical cancer screening becomes more widespread, public awareness of the need for medical consultation has increased, leading to a higher detection rate of HPV-positive cases. However, attention should be paid to strengthening health education, behavioral interventions, and follow-up management for this patient population.

This study found that HPV positivity rates exhibited significant age-related differences, with the highest rate observed in the < 20 age group (40.27%), followed by the > 60 age group (29.30%) and the 51–60 age group (28.00%). The differences in positivity rates across age groups were statistically significant (P < 0.05). This U-shaped distribution pattern is consistent with the results of multiple domestic studies [9]. Women under 20 are in the early stages of sexual activity, they often have low health awareness, and a lack of sex education, weaker cervical epithelial barrier function, all of which contribute to an increased risk of HPV infection. At the same time, the immune system in this age group is relatively active, and some infections may be transient, however, persistent infection with high-risk HPV types still warrants vigilance regarding the development of cervical lesions. The infection rate is higher among women aged 60 and older. This may be related to the onset of menopause in this age group, where declining estrogen levels lead to cervical epithelial atrophy, a reduction in lactobacilli within the vaginal microbiota, an increase in other pathogenic bacteria, and a decline in immune function. Women aged 31–60 constituted the largest group screened in this study, but their positivity rates (21.51%–28.00%) were not the highest. This may be attributed to stronger health awareness and higher screening coverage among women in this age group. However, as this age group is at high risk for cervical lesions, a high screening frequency must be maintained, particularly for women aged 51–60, whose positivity rates are approaching those of the elderly group, necessitating enhanced follow-up management. Based on the aforementioned age distribution characteristics, it is recommended that efforts should be stepped up to provide health education for sexually active women under the age of 20 and to promote screening among women aged 60 and older, while maintaining the routine screening frequency for women aged 31 to 60, in order to facilitate early detection and early intervention.

In this study, HPV-positive cases were predominantly single infections (68.75%), which is consistent with the 60%–70% rate of single infections reported in most domestic studies [1, 10]. Multiple infections accounted for 31.24%, with dual infections being the most common (20.44%) and infections involving triple or more genotypes being the least common (10.80%), this distribution of infection patterns is consistent with that reported in most domestic studies. We also observed a U-shaped distribution of multiple infection rates across different age groups, which is similar to reports in relevant domestic literature [16]. However, the clinical significance of multiple infections remains controversial. Multiple studies have shown [17, 18] that, compared to single HPV infections, multiple HPV infections are more strongly associated with cervical lesions, particularly those involving genotypes such as HPV 16, 52, and 58. Studies by KIM [19] suggest that, compared to single HPV infections, multiple HPV infections are significantly more strongly associated with high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL), and patients with multiple HPV infections tend to have longer-persisting HPV infections. LUO [20] suggest that multiple infections with high-risk HPV may be associated with an increased risk of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia through synergistic effects. However, TONG [21] suggest that multiple high-risk HPV infections may be associated with a higher risk of low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions but not with high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions. This further indicates that the mechanism by which multiple HPV infections lead to cervical lesions remains unclear, and further research is needed. In this study, the rate of multiple infections among cervical cancer patients (34.9%) was higher than that among patients with cervical inflammation (30.8%), suggesting that multiple infections may be associated with the severity of cervical lesions, however, the specific mechanism requires further in-depth analysis with a larger sample size and in conjunction with pathological results.

The results of this study also show that the HPV positivity rate among cervical cancer patients (41.71%) was significantly higher than that among patients with chronic cervicitis (34.83%), while the HPV positivity rate was lowest among women undergoing health examination (13.72%), the differences in HPV positivity rates and multiple infection rates between patients with cervicitis and cervical cancer and those undergoing health examination were statistically significant (P < 0.05). This further confirms that HPV infection is a major risk factor for the onset and progression of cervical lesions. It is worth noting that while the prevalence of HPV infection is lower among women who undergo health examination, it still stands at 13.72%. Therefore, even in the absence of clinical symptoms, women may still be infected with HPV, and routine screening is recommended for early detection. Chronic cervicitis, as a common gynecological inflammatory condition, creates an inflammatory microenvironment that disrupts the cervical mucosal barrier, promoting HPV infection and persistence, thereby forming a vicious cycle of “inflammation-infection-lesion.” Therefore, routine HPV screening should be conducted for patients with chronic cervicitis to detect infections and intervene in a timely manner. The high HPV positivity and multiple infection rates observed in cervical cancer patients further underscore the central role of HPV screening in the early diagnosis and treatment of cervical cancer. For HPV-positive patients, particularly those with multiple high-risk HPV infections, enhanced follow-up monitoring is essential, when necessary, this should be combined with cervical cytology, colposcopy, and pathological biopsy to prevent the missed diagnosis of high-grade lesions. Furthermore, in our study, the prevalence of HPV subtype 16 was significantly higher in patients with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia and cervical cancer compared to the other two groups, it further demonstrating the critical role of HPV 16 in the development of cervical cancer.

In summary, through a systematic analysis of HPV testing data from 41,045 women, this study comprehensively reveals the prevalence of HPV infection, the distribution of genotypes, age-related characteristics, infection patterns, and trends in this region. It provides a scientific basis for the prevention and control of HPV infection, the optimization of screening protocols, and the prevention and treatment of cervical cancer in the Handan region, and is of great significance for reducing the incidence and mortality rates of cervical cancer in the region. Currently, China has made the prevention and control of cervical cancer a priority for women of childbearing age. We should actively promote HPV screening, strengthen health education and awareness campaigns, and enhance women’s awareness of self-care, while also expanding HPV vaccination efforts, particularly among adolescent girls. It is important to note that the 9-valent vaccine does not cover all HPV types, therefore, women who have received the HPV vaccine are still advised to undergo regular cervical cancer screening.

However, our study has certain limitations. We conducted a single-center cross-sectional study with a large sample size (41,045 participants), which is a strength, and there may be some selection bias and information bias related to sampling strategies and data collection. A specific example of this bias is observed in the HPV positivity rate in the < 20 years age group (40.27%), which should be interpreted with caution, as all 293 participants in this subgroup were clinical patients (none were from the asymptomatic health examination group). This selection bias likely overestimates the true prevalence in young women, and future studies should include community-based screening to validate our findings. Besides, some important factors, including socioeconomic status, educational level, lifestyle, and HPV vaccination status, were not included in the analysis. Nevertheless, the large sample size of this study offers a valuable resource for future more in-depth analyses, such as multivariable modeling and time-series forecasting, which we plan to conduct in subsequent studies to further enhance the effectiveness of data presentation and interpretation. Future studies should adopt a multicenter design, expand the sample size (if possible), collect more comprehensive exposure information, and explore whether specific high-risk and low-risk HPV genotype combinations occur more often than expected by chance, as such preferential pairing patterns may provide insights into viral interactions and the risk of cervical lesion progression.
