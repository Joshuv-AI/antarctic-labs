Antibody reactivity to the VAR2CSA DBL5 domain among pregnant women in Northern Ghana
Authors: Richmond Balinia Adda, Sylvester Donne Dassah, Abdul-Rashid Mohammed, Jonah Kulariba, Nsoh Godwin Anabire, James Abugri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPregnancy-associated malaria (PAM), caused predominantly by Plasmodium falciparum, remains a major contributor to maternal and neonatal morbidity in malaria-endemic regions. Placental sequestration of infected erythrocytes is mediated by the parasite protein VAR2CSA, a leading target for PAM vaccine development. However, extensive antigenic polymorphism within VAR2CSA complicates the identification of broadly protective vaccine candidates. Conserved subdomains such as the Duffy Binding–Like 5 (DBL5) domain may contribute to naturally acquired antibody responses during pregnancy. To assess the specificity of DBL5-directed immunity, hepatitis B virus (HBV)-infected pregnant women were included as a non-malarial infectious comparator group. This study evaluated plasma immunoglobulin G (IgG) reactivity to DBL5 and examined its concordance with antibody reactivity to full-length VAR2CSA among pregnant women in Northern Ghana.MethodsPlasma samples from 156 pregnant women were purposively selected from a cross-sectional antenatal cohort and categorised into P. falciparum-infected (n = 60), HBV-infected (n = 60), and co-infected (n = 36) groups. Recombinant DBL5 was expressed in E. coli, purified, and used in indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) to measure plasma IgG reactivity. A subset of samples was tested in parallel against full-length VAR2CSA to evaluate concordance of antibody responses. Multi-tool immunoinformatics analyses were additionally performed to predict B-cell and T-cell epitopes and estimate population coverage.ResultsPlasma IgG reactivity to DBL5 differed significantly across study groups (p < 0.001), with the highest median optical density (OD450) observed among P. falciparum-infected women (1.54), compared with HBV-infected (0.88) and co-infected participants (1.20). DBL5-specific IgG responses demonstrated a strong positive correlation with reactivity to full-length VAR2CSA (Spearman’s ρ = 0.75, p < 0.0001), indicating substantial concordance between recognition of the DBL5 domain and the native placental malaria antigen. Immunoinformatics analyses identified multiple conserved DBL5 regions with strong predicted HLA-binding affinity and broad estimated global population coverage.ConclusionPregnant women exposed to P. falciparum exhibit elevated plasma IgG reactivity to the VAR2CSA DBL5 domain, and these responses closely parallel antibody recognition of full-length VAR2CSA. These findings suggest that DBL5 may represent an immunologically recognisable component of naturally acquired PAM-associated antibody responses. However, given the exploratory cross-sectional design, the observed associations should be interpreted cautiously and warrant further investigation in longitudinal and functional studies evaluating the role of DBL5 in placental malaria immunity and future vaccine research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13785-7.

Introduction

Pregnancy-associated malaria (PAM), caused predominantly by Plasmodium falciparum, remains a major public health challenge in sub-Saharan Africa, where malaria transmission is highly endemic [1]. Pregnant women, particularly primigravidae, are disproportionately affected because they lack pregnancy-specific immunity against placental parasite variants [2]. PAM is characterized by the sequestration of P. falciparum-infected erythrocytes within the intervillous spaces of the placenta through binding of the parasite antigen Variant Surface Antigen 2-Chondroitin Sulfate A (VAR2CSA) to placental chondroitin sulfate A (CSA) [3, 4]. Placental sequestration promotes local inflammation and impaired placental function, contributing to maternal anaemia, fetal growth restriction, low birth weight, preterm delivery, and increased neonatal mortality [2, 4].

Given its central role in placental adhesion, VAR2CSA has become the principal target for PAM vaccine development [5]. However, the large molecular size, structural complexity, and extensive antigenic diversity of VAR2CSA have complicated efforts to develop broadly protective vaccines [6, 7]. Naturally acquired immunity to PAM is mediated by antibodies directed against multiple VAR2CSA domains, including both strain-specific and cross-reactive epitopes [8, 9]. Consequently, increasing attention has focused on identifying relatively conserved VAR2CSA subdomains capable of eliciting broader immune recognition while minimizing the challenges posed by sequence polymorphism.

Among the VAR2CSA domains, the Duffy Binding–Like 5 (DBL5) domain has emerged as a potentially important antigenic target because of its reported immunogenicity and relative sequence conservation across geographically diverse P. falciparum isolates [10, 11]. Tutterrow et al. demonstrated that naturally acquired antibodies targeting multiple VAR2CSA domains, including DBL5, were associated with protection against placental malaria among Cameroonian women living in areas of intense malaria transmission [12]. Similarly, Rotich et al. highlighted DBL5 as a promising alternative vaccine target because of evidence supporting its antigenicity and cross-reactive immune recognition across endemic populations [11]. Experimental studies have further shown that recombinant DBL5 proteins can induce immunoglobulin G (IgG) responses capable of recognizing VAR2CSA-expressing parasites and, in some instances, inhibiting CSA binding in vitro [13, 14]. In addition, DBL5 contains both linear and conformational epitopes recognized during naturally acquired malaria exposure [10, 11]. Collectively, these findings support continued investigation of DBL5 as a conserved component of PAM-directed immune responses.

Although other VAR2CSA domains, including DBL1, DBL2, and DBL3, have also been investigated as vaccine candidates, several exhibit substantial sequence variability and inconsistent cross-reactive antibody recognition across parasite strains [6, 7]. In contrast, DBL5 has demonstrated relatively conserved antigenic regions and reproducible immune recognition in geographically distinct populations, making it a particularly attractive candidate for inclusion in multivalent or epitope-focused PAM vaccine strategies [10, 11].

To better assess the specificity of DBL5-directed immune responses, this study included pregnant women infected with hepatitis B virus (HBV) as a non-malarial infectious comparator group. Chronic HBV infection is highly prevalent in many parts of sub-Saharan Africa and has been associated with altered B-cell and T-cell immune function [15]. Inclusion of an HBV-only group therefore allowed differentiation between malaria-specific antibody responses and generalized immune activation associated with chronic infection during pregnancy. A co-infected group was additionally included to explore whether concurrent HBV infection may influence malaria-associated antibody reactivity.

Northern Ghana experiences perennial malaria transmission, with P. falciparum accounting for the overwhelming majority of malaria infections during pregnancy [16]. In this setting, PAM remains a substantial contributor to maternal and neonatal morbidity despite ongoing malaria control interventions. Understanding naturally acquired immune responses to conserved VAR2CSA domains within such endemic populations may therefore provide important insights for future vaccine development strategies.

In the present study, we investigated plasma IgG reactivity to the VAR2CSA DBL5 domain among pregnant women in Northern Ghana and examined its relationship with antibody responses to full-length VAR2CSA. We further applied a multi-tool immunoinformatics approach to identify conserved B-cell and T-cell epitopes within DBL5 and to estimate predicted population coverage. Together, these analyses were intended as an exploratory assessment of DBL5-directed antibody reactivity and predicted antigenic features, with the aim of generating hypotheses regarding the possible role of DBL5 in naturally acquired PAM-associated immune responses and its potential relevance for future multicomponent vaccine research [17].

Materials and methods

Study setting and population

This study utilised archived plasma samples and associated clinical data obtained from cross-sectional antenatal studies conducted between October 2016 and February 2017 in Northern Ghana. The parent studies investigated the prevalence of malaria and hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection among pregnant women attending their first antenatal care (ANC) visit. Northern Ghana was selected because of its persistently high burden of pregnancy-associated malaria (PAM), substantial HBV prevalence, and consistently high ANC attendance rates, which provide an important setting for investigating naturally acquired immune responses during pregnancy.

Malaria transmission in Northern Ghana is perennial, with seasonal intensification during the rainy season, and Plasmodium falciparum accounts for the vast majority of infections in the region [1, 15, 18]. Previous surveillance studies have reported P. falciparum prevalence exceeding 25% among pregnant women attending ANC clinics in this setting [1]. Despite the implementation of malaria control interventions, including intermittent preventive treatment during pregnancy (IPTp) and insecticide-treated bed nets, PAM remains a significant contributor to maternal anaemia, placental malaria, low birth weight, and adverse neonatal outcomes in Northern Ghana.

Participants were recruited from six public health facilities distributed across the region to ensure broad geographical representation and capture variation in malaria exposure patterns. These facilities included Tamale West Hospital, Tamale Central Hospital, Bilpella Health Centre, Tamale Teaching Hospital, Sankpala Health Centre, and Kosawgu Health Centre (Fig. 1). All participating facilities provide routine antenatal services and serve predominantly rural and peri-urban populations within malaria-endemic communities.

Study participants and sample selection

Plasma samples used in this study were purposively selected from a previously described cohort of 2,258 pregnant women recruited during antenatal care attendance in Northern Ghana [1]. The parent cohort was established to investigate the prevalence of malaria and hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection during pregnancy. Of the original cohort, 187 participants were excluded because of incomplete demographic or clinical records, insufficient plasma volume, or poor sample quality, including visible haemolysis. Consequently, 2,071 archived plasma samples remained available for downstream analyses.

For the present study, a subset of 156 plasma samples was selected based on the availability of clearly defined infection status and adequate plasma volume for parallel enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analyses. The selected sample size reflected the exploratory nature of this investigation and the availability of archived specimens rather than a formal power-based sample size calculation. In addition, because samples were purposively selected from a single archived cohort in Northern Ghana based on plasma availability and completeness of records, the findings may not be fully generalisable to all pregnant women in Northern Ghana or other malaria-endemic settings. Selection focused on establishing well-characterized comparison groups to evaluate antibody reactivity to the VAR2CSA DBL5 domain under different infection conditions.

Pregnant women who were negative for both malaria and HBV were not included in the primary analytical groups because the principal objective of the study was to investigate naturally acquired antibody reactivity to the VAR2CSA DBL5 domain in the context of malaria exposure during pregnancy. Since VAR2CSA-specific immune responses are acquired predominantly following exposure to placental P. falciparum infection, inclusion of malaria-unexposed pregnant women would have contributed limited information regarding DBL5-directed antibody recognition.

The HBV-only group was included as a non-malarial infectious comparator to distinguish malaria-specific antibody responses from generalized immune activation associated with chronic infection during pregnancy. Chronic HBV infection has been associated with altered adaptive immune responses, including modulation of B-cell and T-cell function, making it a biologically relevant comparator in this setting. The co-infection group was additionally included to explore whether concurrent HBV infection may influence malaria-associated antibody reactivity patterns.

Where feasible, the malaria-only and HBV-only groups were frequency-matched by key demographic and obstetric variables, including maternal age and gravidity, to minimise major baseline differences between comparison groups. Matching was performed at the group level rather than through individual pair-matching.

To minimise technical variability, the same positive and negative control samples were included across all ELISA plates, and all assays were performed under standardised experimental conditions using identical antigen coating concentrations, incubation times, and reagent preparations. However, OD₄₅₀ values were not formally normalised to plate-specific control ratios or standard curves, and residual inter-plate variability cannot be completely excluded. Consequently, measured antibody reactivity values should be interpreted as semi-quantitative estimates of relative IgG binding rather than absolute antibody concentrations.

Plasma from non-pregnant, malaria-naive individuals with no history of residence in malaria-endemic regions were obtained from a commercial biorepository (SeraCare Life Sciences, Milford, MA, USA) and used as negative controls for background reactivity assessment in the ELISA assays.

Bioinformatics analysis of DBL5

The DBL5 domain amino acid sequence was retrieved from the P. falciparum 3D7 reference strain (PlasmoDB ID: PF3D7_1335100) in FASTA format and subjected to a multi-tool immunoinformatics pipeline to identify putative antigenic regions.

Linear B-cell epitope prediction. Three complementary tools were used to maximise prediction robustness: (i) BepiPred 2.0 (IEDB), which applies a random forest classifier trained on antibody-antigen structural data, with a threshold score of 0.5 used to define predicted epitopes; (ii) ABCpred, which uses a recurrent neural network to identify B-cell epitope windows of 16 residues, with a threshold of 0.51; and (iii) LBtope, a support vector machine-based predictor trained on experimentally validated linear B-cell epitopes. Only regions predicted by at least two of the three tools were retained for further analysis, to reduce false positives.

T-cell (MHC class I) epitope prediction. Three servers were employed: (i) NetMHCpan 4.1, which predicts peptide–MHC binding using a pan-specific neural network; (ii) the IEDB-recommended MHC class I prediction method, which integrates multiple algorithms; and (iii) SYFPEITHI, a score-based matrix method trained on known MHC ligands. HLA alleles were selected based on global prevalence and representation of common HLA supertypes (A2, A3, B7, B44), ensuring broad population relevance. Peptides with predicted binding affinity IC₅₀ < 200 nM were classified as high-affinity binders. Population coverage for the retained epitope set was estimated using the IEDB Population Coverage Calculation Tool, which integrates HLA allele frequency data across 21 geographic populations.

Conformational B-cell epitope prediction. Structure-based epitope prediction was performed using DiscoTope 2.0 and ElliPro, applied to the AlphaFold2-predicted three-dimensional structure of DBL5. These tools identify discontinuous surface-exposed epitope clusters based on spatial accessibility and protrusion scores.

Recombinant DBL5 protein expression and purification

The gene sequence encoding the Duffy Binding-Like 5 (DBL5) domain (PlasmoDB ID: PF3D7_1335100) was codon-optimized for expression in E. coli, synthesized commercially (GeneArt, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and cloned into a pET-28a(+) expression vector by the provider. This construct incorporated an N-terminal hexahistidine (His-tag) tag to facilitate purification. The recombinant plasmid was subsequently transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) competent cells for protein expression, following established protocols at the West African Centre for Cell Biology of Infectious Pathogens (WACCBIP).

Protein expression was carried out based on methods previously described in (Anabire et al., 2019), with modifications. Briefly, a single transformed colony was used to inoculate a 2 L culture in LB medium. Expression of the recombinant His-tagged DBL5 protein was induced at mid-log phase with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 37 °C for 4 h. Bacterial cells were then harvested by centrifugation.

The cell pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, pH 8.0) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail and lysed on ice using a sonicator (Qsonica). The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 9,000 × g for 45 min at 4 °C to separate the soluble fraction from cell debris.

The recombinant His-tagged DBL5 protein was purified from the soluble supernatant under native conditions using immobilised metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) with Ni-NTA resin (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The resin was washed with wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 300 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, pH 8.0) to remove weakly bound contaminants. The target protein was eluted using an elution buffer containing 250 mM imidazole.

The eluted protein was dialyzed extensively against phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to remove imidazole and exchange the buffer. The purity, molecular weight, and integrity of the purified protein were confirmed by SDS-PAGE. Western blot analysis using a monoclonal anti-His tag antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was performed to confirm protein identity; however, the original blot image is no longer available as the work was conducted as part of an undergraduate project, and the raw data could not be recovered. Protein identity is therefore supported by the SDS-PAGE profile and the confirmed sequence of the expression construct. Although Western blot confirmation using an anti-His or domain-specific antibody was originally performed during the preliminary phase of the project, the raw blot image was not recoverable for inclusion in the present study because the work originated from an undergraduate research project with incomplete archival retention of primary laboratory files. Consequently, confirmation of recombinant DBL5 identity in the current manuscript relies primarily on the expected SDS-PAGE migration profile together with the verified nucleotide sequence of the recombinant expression construct. This represents a methodological limitation because protein identity and purity cannot be confirmed to the same level of rigor typically achieved through orthogonal validation approaches such as immunoblotting, peptide mass fingerprinting, or mass spectrometry-based protein characterisation.

Assessment of antigen–antibody reactivity by indirect ELISA

Plasma IgG reactivity to the recombinant VAR2CSA DBL5 domain was assessed using an indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

A subset of plasma samples (n = 30) was also tested against full-length recombinant VAR2CSA protein (produced in HEK293 cells; provided by Dr. Amlabu, WACCBIP) using the same indirect ELISA protocol described above, with antigen coating at 2 µg/mL.

High-binding 96-well microplates (Nunc MaxiSorp, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Roskilde, Denmark) were coated overnight at 4 °C with 100 µL per well of purified recombinant DBL5 protein at a concentration of 2.0 µg/mL in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). Following antigen coating, plates were washed three times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBS-T) to remove unbound protein.

Non-specific binding sites were blocked by adding 200 µL per well of blocking buffer (5% skim milk in PBS) and incubating for 2 h at room temperature. Plates were then washed three times with PBS-T before sample addition.

Plasma samples were diluted 1:1000 in blocking buffer and added to the plates in duplicate wells. A dilution of 1:1000 was selected following preliminary optimisation experiments to place OD₄₅₀ readings within the linear range of the assay and to minimise non-specific background binding observed at lower dilutions; however, this approach does not normalise for inter-individual differences in total IgG concentration, which may contribute to variability in observed OD values. Plates were incubated for 2 h at room temperature, followed by three washes with PBS-T. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated goat anti-human IgG secondary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), diluted 1:10,000 in blocking buffer, was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature.

After a final washing step, antibody binding was visualized using 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific Waltham, MA, USA). The enzymatic reaction was allowed to proceed for 20 min in the dark and was stopped by adding 25 µL of 1 M sulfuric acid. Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a Varioskan Lux microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Negative control samples, consisting of plasma from non-pregnant, malaria-naive individuals with no history of Plasmodium falciparum or hepatitis B virus infection, were included on every plate to define background reactivity. Blank wells containing all reagents except plasma were used to correct for plate background. OD values presented in the results represent background-subtracted values.

Healthy control OD values were included in all relevant figures to allow direct comparison between malaria-exposed and malaria-naive individuals.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Antibody reactivity data, expressed as optical density at 450 nm (OD450), were initially assessed for distributional assumptions using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test together with visual inspection of quantile–quantile (QQ) plots. Because the data were not normally distributed, non-parametric statistical methods were applied throughout the analysis. Continuous variables are therefore presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), unless otherwise specified.

Comparisons of plasma IgG reactivity to DBL5 among the three study groups (malaria-only, HBV-only, and co-infection) were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Where significant overall differences were identified, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test with Dunn’s adjustment for multiple comparisons. Because matching was performed at the frequency level rather than through individual pair-matching, independent-group statistical analyses remained appropriate.

Associations between plasma IgG reactivity to DBL5 and full-length VAR2CSA were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (Spearman’s rho). Correlation strength was interpreted according to standard guidelines for non-parametric correlation analyses.

For subgroup analyses, antibody reactivity levels were further stratified according to gravidity and maternal age categories where sample sizes permitted meaningful comparisons. DBL5 antibody reactivity was additionally categorized as low, intermediate, or high based on thresholds derived from the OD450 distribution of malaria-naïve healthy control samples. The cutoff for positive seroreactivity was defined as the mean OD450 value of healthy controls plus two standard deviations. Samples with OD450 values exceeding this threshold were classified as DBL5-reactive.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Conservation analysis

To evaluate the sequence conservation of the DBL5 domain and the predicted epitopes, a multiple sequence alignment was performed. The amino acid sequence of the DBL5 domain from the P. falciparum 3D7 reference strain (PlasmoDB ID: PF3D7_1335100) was aligned with homologous VAR2CSA DBL5 sequences from 50 geographically diverse P. falciparum isolates, obtained from the PlasmoDB database. Alignment was conducted using the MAFFT algorithm (v7.490) with default parameters. Sequence conservation was calculated as the percentage identity at each position relative to the 3D7 reference. Regions with ≥ 80% sequence identity across the isolate panel were defined as conserved.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The use of archived plasma samples was approved by the Tamale Teaching Hospital Ethics Review Committee (TTHERC; Approval ID: TTHERC/21/04/16/02), which serves as the Institutional Review Board for research conducted at Tamale Teaching Hospital and affiliated facilities in Northern Ghana. This approval covered both the original parent studies from which samples were derived and the secondary analysis of stored samples for the present sub-study. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant in the original studies.

Data quality assurance

All laboratory procedures, including protein purifications and ELISA, were performed in duplicates to ensure technical reproducibility. Negative and positive controls were included in every ELISA plate to validate assay performance and results.

Results

Participant characteristics

From the original cohort of 2,071 pregnant women with complete clinical data and adequate sample quality, 156 participants were selected for analysis based on confirmed infection status and plasma availability. Detailed demographic and obstetric characteristics of the parent cohort have been reported previously (Anabire et al., 2019). The selected subset was comparable to the parent population, with a median age of 26 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 23–30 years). Approximately 62% of participants were primigravidae, reflecting the study’s focus on women at increased risk of pregnancy-associated malaria.

Expression and characterization of recombinant DBL5 protein

Confirmation of successful recombinant protein expression, solubility, and purity is an essential prerequisite for the validity of ELISA-based antigen–antibody reactivity measurements, as impure or aggregated antigen preparations can lead to non-specific binding and unreliable OD readings.

The Duffy Binding-Like 5 (DBL5) domain of VAR2CSA was successfully expressed as a soluble, hexahistidine-tagged recombinant protein in E. coli and purified by nickel–nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) affinity chromatography. SDS-PAGE analysis revealed a predominant band migrating at approximately 42 kDa (Fig. 3), consistent with the expected size of the DBL5 construct. Protein identity was further supported by the presence of the N-terminal hexahistidine tag incorporated into the construct design, which was confirmed at the cloning stage prior to expression.

The observed molecular weight exceeded the theoretical mass of approximately 38 kDa, a difference likely attributable to the amino acid composition and migration behavior of the protein under denaturing conditions rather than post-translational modification. The expressed construct comprised 353 amino acids, with a predicted extinction coefficient of 77,070 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹.

Plasma IgG reactivity to DBL5 differs by infection status

Plasma IgG reactivity to the recombinant DBL5 domain was assessed by indirect ELISA. Background-subtracted OD₄₅₀ values differed significantly across the three study groups (Kruskal–Wallis test, p < 0.001; Fig. 4A and B).

Women with P. falciparum infection alone exhibited the highest DBL5-specific IgG reactivity (median OD₄₅₀: 1.54; IQR: 1.31–1.72). Participants infected with HBV alone showed substantially lower reactivity (median OD₄₅₀: 0.88; IQR: 0.74–1.02), comparable to values observed in malaria-naive healthy controls. The co-infection group demonstrated intermediate reactivity (median OD₄₅₀: 1.20; IQR: 0.98–1.42). Pairwise comparisons confirmed that DBL5-specific IgG reactivity was significantly higher in the malaria-only group than in both the HBV-only group (p < 0.0001) and the co-infection group (p = 0.003). The difference between the co-infection and HBV-only groups did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.06).

Within the malaria-infected group, DBL5-specific IgG reactivity did not differ significantly by gravidity (primigravid women: median OD₄₅₀ 1.52, IQR 1.28–1.70, n = 37; multigravid women: median OD₄₅₀ 1.58, IQR 1.35–1.75, n = 23; Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.37), although a marginal trend toward higher reactivity in multigravid women was observed, consistent with the gradual accumulation of VAR2CSA-directed immunity over successive pregnancies. Stratification by maternal age category (< 20 years, 20–29 years, and ≥ 30 years) similarly revealed no statistically significant differences in DBL5 reactivity across age groups (Kruskal–Wallis test, p = 0.41). The absence of significant gravidity- and age-related differences in DBL5 reactivity may reflect several factors, including the high P. falciparum transmission intensity in the study setting, where repeated exposure could contribute to detectable domain-specific responses across multiple demographic groups. However, because the analysis was cross-sectional and unadjusted for potential confounding variables, this interpretation remains speculative. Stratified median OD₄₅₀ values across all infection groups, gravidity categories, and age groups are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Predicted B-cell and T-cell epitopes within DBL5

Immunoinformatics analysis identified putative antigenic regions within the DBL5 sequence using a multi-tool approach. Linear B-cell epitope prediction using BepiPred 2.0, ABCpred, and LBtope identified multiple regions with consistent scores across at least two tools, suggesting potential surface-exposed antibody-binding sites distributed along the domain. Only consensus predictions (≥ 2 tools in agreement) were taken forward. T-cell epitope prediction using NetMHCpan 4.1, the IEDB-recommended MHC class I method, and SYFPEITHI identified several peptides with high predicted binding affinity (IC₅₀ < 200 nM) to common HLA supertypes. Estimated global population coverage for the combined epitope set exceeded 95%, including substantial coverage for populations in malaria-endemic regions. Structure-based analysis using DiscoTope 2.0 and ElliPro, applied to the AlphaFold2-predicted DBL5 structure, identified clusters of surface-accessible residues overlapping with several linear epitope regions. These predictions are exploratory and are presented to guide future experimental validation.

Conservation of predicted epitopes

Conservation analysis of the DBL5 domain across 50 diverse P. falciparum isolates revealed several regions of high sequence conservation (≥ 80% identity). The predicted high-affinity MHC class I T-cell epitopes and their conservation are summarized in Table 1. Notably, the majority of the predicted high-affinity T-cell epitopes (4 out of 5) and several linear B-cell epitope regions overlapped with these conserved blocks. For example, the predicted T-cell epitope ‘KYQEWSRKRS’ (positions 260–270) was located within a conserved region with 92% identity across isolates. This overlap suggests that the predicted epitopes are not strain-specific but are potentially targetable across diverse parasite populations.

Association between DBL5 and full-length VAR2CSA antibody reactivity

A positive association was observed between anti-DBL5 and anti-VAR2CSA IgG reactivity within the analysed subset of malaria-infected samples (Spearman’s ρ = 0.74, p < 0.0001; Fig. 4C). Samples with higher VAR2CSA-specific OD₄₅₀ values generally demonstrated higher DBL5-specific reactivity, whereas samples with lower VAR2CSA binding tended to exhibit correspondingly lower DBL5 responses. Although the correlation was substantial, this analysis was based on a relatively small subset of participants (n = 30) and should therefore be interpreted cautiously because the estimated correlation strength may be sensitive to sampling variability and may not fully represent the broader study population. The findings nevertheless suggest partial concordance between DBL5-directed responses and broader VAR2CSA antibody recognition patterns. Agreement between assay readouts was further assessed by Bland–Altman analysis (Fig. 4D), which showed a small mean difference between DBL5 and VAR2CSA OD₄₅₀ values within acceptable limits of agreement.

Discussion

This study investigated naturally acquired antibody reactivity to the VAR2CSA Duffy Binding–Like 5 (DBL5) domain among pregnant women in a malaria-endemic region of Northern Ghana. We observed significantly higher immunoglobulin G (IgG) reactivity to recombinant DBL5 among women with active P. falciparum infection compared with women infected only with hepatitis B virus (HBV). In addition, DBL5-specific antibody responses demonstrated a positive correlation with reactivity to full-length VAR2CSA, suggesting that DBL5 contributes to the broader antigenic profile recognised during naturally acquired pregnancy-associated malaria (PAM) immunity. Together, these findings suggest that DBL5 may represent an immunologically recognisable component of VAR2CSA during naturally acquired PAM exposure and warrant further investigation in future studies evaluating its potential relevance within broader PAM vaccine research frameworks.

DBL5 antibody reactivity and naturally acquired PAM immunity

Protective immunity to PAM develops progressively over successive pregnancies and is mediated largely by antibodies targeting VAR2CSA-expressing infected erythrocytes within the placenta [19, 20]. These antibodies can target epitopes that are shared among geographically diverse parasite isolates [21]. These antibodies can inhibit parasite adhesion to chondroitin sulfate A (CSA), thereby reducing placental parasite accumulation and improving pregnancy outcomes [19, 22, 23]. Although functional adhesion-inhibition assays were not performed in the present study, the elevated DBL5-specific IgG responses observed among malaria-infected women indicate that this domain is immunologically recognised during natural infection. Naturally acquired antibodies against VAR2CSA have been associated with reduced risk of low birth weight and other adverse pregnancy outcomes in malaria-endemic populations [24]. This observation is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that individual VAR2CSA domains can induce antibody responses in naturally exposed populations [7, 12].

DBL5-specific antibody reactivity was detected in both primigravid and multigravid women. The presence of detectable responses among primigravid women suggests that immune recognition of this domain may occur relatively early following placental exposure to P. falciparum. Previous studies have similarly reported that antibodies directed against individual VAR2CSA domains may arise during first pregnancies, even before broader adhesion-inhibitory immunity becomes fully established [8]. While multigravid women generally develop stronger and more functionally effective anti-VAR2CSA responses over time, early acquisition of DBL5-directed antibodies may contribute to the gradual development of PAM-specific immunity.

The absence of statistically significant differences in DBL5 reactivity according to gravidity or maternal age should be interpreted cautiously. Although high malaria transmission intensity within the study setting may plausibly contribute to widespread exposure and detectable domain-specific responses across demographic groups, the present analysis was observational and did not adjust for potentially important confounding variables, including gestational age, intermittent preventive treatment during pregnancy (IPTp) use, parasite density, malaria transmission season, haemoglobin status, or prior exposure history. Consequently, the observed similarities between gravidity and age groups cannot be interpreted as evidence that these factors do not influence DBL5-directed immunity, and the underlying determinants of antibody variability remain uncertain.

Relationship to previous VAR2CSA subunit studies

Several studies have evaluated individual VAR2CSA domains as candidate vaccine antigens, with varying findings regarding sequence conservation, structural accessibility, and induction of cross-reactive antibodies [6–8, 13]. The extensive polymorphism observed across portions of VAR2CSA has complicated the identification of broadly protective vaccine targets [6, 7]. Nevertheless, DBL5 has been reported to contain relatively conserved antigenic regions that are recognised by antibodies from geographically distinct malaria-endemic populations [10, 11].

The positive correlation observed between DBL5-specific IgG responses and antibodies directed against full-length VAR2CSA supports the presence of shared antigenic determinants between the recombinant DBL5 construct and the native placental parasite antigen. This finding is consistent with the concept that naturally acquired immunity to PAM is polyclonal and involves recognition of multiple antigenic regions across the VAR2CSA molecule rather than a single dominant epitope [7, 12]. At the same time, the incomplete concordance between DBL5 and full-length VAR2CSA responses suggests that DBL5 represents only one component of the broader placental malaria antigenic repertoire.

Effects of malaria–HBV co-infection on antibody reactivity

Women with concurrent malaria and HBV infection exhibited intermediate DBL5-specific IgG reactivity compared with women infected with malaria alone. Chronic HBV infection has previously been associated with altered B-cell and T-cell function, including immune exhaustion and impaired cellular activation, which may influence antibody responses to unrelated pathogens [25]. Although the present study was not designed to define the immunological mechanisms underlying co-infection, these findings raise the possibility that chronic HBV infection may modulate malaria-associated humoral responses during pregnancy. However, because the analyses were based on unadjusted cross-sectional group comparisons, alternative explanations related to differences in parasite density, HBV viral activity, maternal immune status, gestational age, nutritional status, or other unmeasured clinical variables cannot be excluded.

The inclusion of an HBV-only comparator group was intended to distinguish malaria-specific antibody recognition from generalised immune activation associated with chronic infection. The substantially lower DBL5 reactivity observed in HBV-only participants supports the specificity of the observed DBL5 responses to malaria exposure rather than nonspecific immune stimulation. These findings further emphasise the importance of considering co-endemic infections when evaluating naturally acquired immunity and vaccine responsiveness in malaria-endemic populations.

Implications for PAM vaccine design and epitope prediction

VAR2CSA remains the principal target for PAM vaccine development because of its essential role in placental sequestration [3]. However, the large size and antigenic diversity of the full-length protein continue to present important challenges for vaccine design [6]. Alternative expression systems, such as Pichia pastoris, have been explored to optimise the production of properly folded VAR2CSA domains for vaccine development [26]. Consequently, increasing attention has focused on identifying conserved VAR2CSA regions capable of eliciting broadly reactive immune responses while reducing strain-specific variability.

In the present study, DBL5 was associated with measurable antibody reactivity during natural infection and showed concordance with full-length VAR2CSA antibody recognition within this cross-sectional dataset. However, the DBL5–VAR2CSA correlation analysis was conducted in only a small subset of participants (n = 30), which may not fully represent the broader study population. Consequently, the observed correlation should be interpreted cautiously and requires replication in larger and independent cohorts to determine the robustness and reproducibility of this relationship. In parallel, immunoinformatics analyses identified several predicted B-cell and T-cell epitopes within DBL5 with broad estimated population coverage. Rather than establishing DBL5 as a vaccine antigen, these exploratory findings warrant further investigation of DBL5 within multivalent or chimeric vaccine research strategies incorporating multiple conserved VAR2CSA regions. Such approaches may improve immune breadth and reduce the impact of antigenic polymorphism. Recent evidence indicates that even a single full-length VAR2CSA variant can purify antibodies that broadly neutralise diverse placental parasite isolates, supporting the feasibility of broadly protective vaccine strategies [27].

Nevertheless, the immunoinformatics analyses presented here remain computational and hypothesis-generating. Experimental validation using peptide-based immunoassays, ELISpot analysis, peptide microarrays, and competition ELISAs will be necessary to confirm recognition of predicted epitopes in naturally exposed populations. In addition, the functional significance of DBL5-specific antibodies remains unresolved. Because inhibition of infected erythrocyte adhesion to CSA is considered a key correlate of protective immunity in PAM, future studies should prioritise functional adhesion-blocking assays to determine whether DBL5-directed antibodies contribute directly to protection against placental malaria.

Overall, this study contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the relevance of conserved VAR2CSA domains in PAM vaccine research. The identification of immunogenic and potentially conserved regions within DBL5 supports continued investigation of this domain within broader multicomponent vaccine development frameworks.

Interpretation of these findings should additionally consider the purposive sampling strategy used in this exploratory study. Participants were selected from an archived antenatal cohort based on the availability of sufficient plasma volume and complete demographic and clinical records, which may have introduced selection bias. Women included in the final analysis may therefore differ systematically from excluded participants with respect to infection severity, immune status, healthcare utilisation, or other unmeasured characteristics. Furthermore, because the study population was derived from a single geographic region with intense malaria transmission, the observed antibody reactivity patterns may not reflect those of pregnant women in other regions of Ghana or in settings with different transmission dynamics. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and not assumed to be broadly generalisable without confirmation in larger and more representative populations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should inform interpretation of the findings. First, antibody reactivity was measured by indirect ELISA using a fixed plasma dilution of 1:1000, which does not normalise for inter-individual differences in total IgG concentration. Quantification of total IgG followed by standardised dilution from a fixed starting concentration would provide a more rigorous comparison and is recommended for future studies. Additionally, although identical positive and negative control samples were included across ELISA plates and assays were performed under standardised laboratory conditions, OD₄₅₀ measurements were not normalised using plate-specific standard curves or reference-based correction methods. Residual inter-plate variability therefore cannot be completely excluded and may have contributed to measurement imprecision. Furthermore, the correlation analysis between DBL5-specific and full-length VAR2CSA-specific antibody reactivity was conducted in only a small subset of malaria-infected participants (n = 30). Because this subset may not be fully representative of the overall study population, the estimated correlation coefficient may be sensitive to sampling variability and should be interpreted as preliminary pending confirmation in larger and independently validated datasets.

Furthermore, no functional inhibition assays were performed. Demonstration that DBL5-specific antibodies can block the binding of P. falciparum-infected erythrocytes to chondroitin sulfate A is required to establish any contribution to protective immunity, and such assays represent an important priority for future work.

Also, raw Western blot data confirming recombinant DBL5 identity were not recoverable because portions of the laboratory work were initially conducted within an undergraduate research framework in which long-term archival preservation of all primary image files was incomplete. As a result, antigen identity in the present study relies principally on the observed SDS-PAGE migration profile together with the verified recombinant construct sequence rather than on orthogonal protein validation methods. Although the observed molecular weight was consistent with the expected DBL5 construct size, the absence of recoverable immunoblot confirmation means that antigen identity and purity cannot be verified to the same standard typically expected for rigorously validated recombinant proteins. Additional confirmatory approaches, including anti-His immunoblotting, peptide mass fingerprinting, liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS), or other proteomic characterisation methods, were not available at the time of the original experiments but would strengthen confidence in antigen identity in future studies. Consequently, the ELISA findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution pending independent replication using more comprehensively validated antigen preparations.

Additionally, the analysis was conducted on a purposively selected subset of archived samples obtained from a single geographic region in Northern Ghana. Inclusion depended on the availability of sufficient plasma volume together with complete demographic and clinical records, which may have introduced selection bias. Participants included in the final analysis may systematically differ from excluded women in ways that could influence antibody reactivity, including differences in infection burden, immune status, antenatal attendance patterns, nutritional status, or other unmeasured clinical characteristics. Consequently, the observed effect sizes may either overestimate or underestimate the true magnitude of DBL5-associated antibody responses within the broader pregnant population. In addition, because the sample was derived from a single malaria-endemic setting and the sample size was determined by archive availability rather than a formal power calculation, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and may not be generalisable to all pregnant women in Northern Ghana or to other endemic regions with different transmission intensities, demographic profiles, or co-infection patterns. Replication in larger, prospectively designed, multicentre studies will therefore be important to assess the robustness and external validity of these findings.

Moreover, the cross-sectional design precludes assessment of the durability of DBL5-directed antibody responses or their relationship with subsequent pregnancy outcomes. Longitudinal studies are required to evaluate the kinetics, persistence, and potential protective correlates of these responses.

Next, all immunoinformatics findings are computational and hypothesis-generating. Predicted B-cell and T-cell epitopes require experimental validation using peptide-based assays, ELISpot analysis, and competition ELISA before functional relevance can be established.

Finally, the analyses were not adjusted within a multivariable modelling framework for several potentially important confounding variables, including gravidity, gestational age, parasite density, intermittent preventive treatment during pregnancy (IPTp) use, malaria transmission season, haemoglobin status, nutritional status, and broader indicators of maternal immune function. These factors may independently influence both malaria exposure and antibody responsiveness during pregnancy. Consequently, the observed differences in DBL5-specific IgG reactivity between study groups cannot be interpreted as independent or causal associations. In particular, the reduced antibody reactivity observed among women with malaria–HBV co-infection may reflect differences in underlying clinical or epidemiological characteristics rather than a direct immunomodulatory effect of HBV infection itself. The absence of multivariable adjustment therefore limits the precision of effect estimates and necessitates cautious interpretation of between-group comparisons within this exploratory cross-sectional study.

Conclusion

Taken together this exploratory study suggests that the VAR2CSA DBL5 domain may be associated with naturally acquired IgG antibody reactivity among Plasmodium falciparum-infected pregnant women in Northern Ghana. Elevated DBL5-specific IgG reactivity was specific to malaria exposure, as evidenced by significantly lower reactivity in HBV-infected women without malaria, and showed substantial concordance with antibody reactivity to full-length VAR2CSA (Spearman’s ρ = 0.74), indicating shared antigenic features between the domain and the native protein. Multi-tool immunoinformatics analyses identified conserved linear and conformational B-cell and T-cell epitopes within DBL5 with broad estimated global population coverage.

While these findings do not establish functional protection or vaccine suitability, they warrant further investigation of DBL5 as a potentially immunologically relevant and antigenically accessible component of VAR2CSA. Future studies should prioritise functional validation of DBL5-specific antibodies through adhesion-inhibition assays, experimental confirmation of predicted epitopes in naturally exposed populations, and evaluation of DBL5-directed immune responses in larger and geographically diverse cohorts.
