Pseudomyogenic hemangioendothelioma of the thoracolumbar spine: A case-based review of a rare cause of vertebral collapse and neurological deficit
Authors: Kaveh Ebrahimzadeh, Mohammad Mirahmadi Eraghi, Farahnaz Bidari Zerehpoosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPseudomyogenic hemangioendothelioma (PMHE) is a rare, intermediate-grade vascular tumor. Spinal involvement with pathological fracture and neurological compromise is extremely rare.Case presentationA 24-year-old male presented with a T12 burst fracture, worsening back pain, bilateral lower-extremity paresthesia, and paraparesis. The tumor was hypointense on T1-weighted sequences and hyperintense on T2-weighted sequences, with compression of the spinal cord. A posterior T12 vertebral column resection with pedicle screw fixation (T9–T11 and L1–L3), laminectomy, and anterior column reconstruction using an expandable mesh cage was performed. Gross total resection was achieved. Histopathological and immunohistochemical findings were consistent with PMHE. The two-year follow-up was uneventful.Clinical discussionThe diagnosis of spinal PMHE is challenging, as it may mimic other lytic bone tumors. Surgical decompression accompanied by circumferential reconstruction remains essential for neurological preservation.ConclusionAggressive spinal PMHE presenting with neurological deficits is rare. Posterior vertebral column resection with reconstruction may contribute to durable local control. Meticulous correlation of imaging, pathological, and clinical findings is required to establish an accurate diagnosis.

Introduction

Pseudomyogenic hemangioendothelioma (PMHE) is a rare soft-tissue vascular tumor that is histologically distinct from epithelioid hemangioendothelioma (Ma et al., 2011). It was originally described as a fibroma-like variant of epithelioid sarcoma (Mirra et al., 1992). The tumor exhibits a marked male predominance and typically affects individuals between 20 and 50 years of age. Despite its occasionally multifocal presentation, PMHE generally carries a relatively favorable long-term prognosis (Hornick and Fletcher, 2011a). Approximately 78% of reported cases occur in the extremities, frequently involving multiple tissue planes, with the dermis and subcutaneous tissue also commonly affected. However, spinal involvement remains exceedingly rare, particularly in the absence of adjacent soft-tissue extension (Yang et al., 2023; Dianat et al., 2019; McGinity et al., 2013; Klag et al., 2019; Miettinen et al., 1999). The authors present an exceptionally rare case of PMHE with clinically dominant spinal involvement and review the relevant literature.

Case presentation

A 24-year-old male initially presented to a primary center with progressive thoracolumbar back pain over the past year. During the initial evaluation, imaging demonstrated a T12 vertebral body wedge fracture with focal thoracolumbar kyphosis and a destructive T12 lesion, resulting in mechanical instability. Whole-body fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG PET/CT) revealed a hypermetabolic compression fracture of T12 with postoperative changes related to cement augmentation and dural involvement. Additionally, FDG-avid lesions were identified, including a lytic lesion in the distal medial condyle of the left humerus and focal hypermetabolic soft-tissue lesions in the patient's left mid-thigh and overlying the coccyx.

Additional lytic lesions were noted in the T7, T9, and L2 vertebrae without significant metabolic activity. An incidental focal area of increased FDG uptake was observed in the sigmoid colon; therefore, the patient was referred for endoscopic correlation. No visceral organ involvement or lymphadenopathy was detected.

The patient presented with a history of resection of a femoral mass and T12 vertebroplasty. Initial histopathology demonstrated epithelioid and spindle cell proliferation with associated inflammatory infiltrates and keratin-positive immunohistochemical staining. The differential diagnosis included keratin-positive giant cell tumor, xanthogranulomatous or epithelial tumors, non-ossifying fibroma, and solid-type aneurysmal bone cyst. As interpretations remained discordant and tissue was insufficient for advanced molecular analysis, a definitive diagnosis could not be established.

The patient presented to our referral center with progressive back pain, bilateral lower-extremity paresthesia, and paraparesis. Neurological examination revealed motor strength of 4/5 in the lower extremities. Repeat imaging demonstrated collapse of the T12 vertebral body, with retropulsed bony and tumoral fragments causing marked spinal canal compromise and thoracic spinal cord compression (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Given the patient's progressive neurological deficit, mechanical instability, and circumferential spinal cord compression, single-stage posterior-only vertebral column resection was selected to allow single-stage circumferential decompression and reconstruction through a single posterior corridor.

The patient was positioned prone under general anesthesia. Posterior exposure was followed by pedicle screw instrumentation from T9–T11 and L1–L3, with posterior spinal fusion performed to establish immediate stability. The procedure was performed under continuous intraoperative neuromonitoring. A wide laminectomy was then carried out, achieving circumferential decompression of the spinal cord.

Using the same posterior approach, a complete posterior vertebral column resection of T12 was performed, and a gross total resection (GTR) under microscopic visualization was achieved. Anterior column reconstruction was subsequently accomplished through the posterior corridor using an expandable mesh cage to restore spinal alignment and stability (Fig. 3).Fig. 3(A) Intraoperative posterior view after complete T12 posterior vertebral column resection demonstrates circumferential decompression and posterior instrumentation from T9–T11 and L1–L3. Pathological findings

Microscopic evaluation of the vertebral mass revealed a hypercellular neoplasm arranged in sheets and short fascicles of spindle or epithelioid cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm, vesicular nuclei, and prominent inflammatory cell infiltration, invading bone and adjacent adipose tissue. Some neoplastic cells exhibited abundant, deeply eosinophilic cytoplasm and eccentric vesicular nuclei, resembling rhabdomyoblasts.

Occasional mitotic figures were noted, along with scattered red blood cell (RBC) extravasation, without anaplasia or necrosis. On immunohistochemistry (IHC), tumor cells were negative for S100, desmin, CD34, and myoD1, and positive for CD31, vimentin, and cytokeratin (CK); INI-1 expression was preserved (Fig. 4). A skin lesion of the thigh near the knee, biopsied a few months earlier and originally reported as a schwannoma, was re-evaluated. Similar neoplastic cells were identified in the dermis, extending into the hypodermal fat. The epidermis was acanthotic without tumor involvement. Histopathological evaluation of the T12 biopsy specimen, supported by immunohistochemical findings, confirmed the diagnosis of multifocal PMHE.

No significant postoperative sensory deficits were noted. The postoperative clinical course was uneventful, and the patient experienced prompt relief of back pain and paraparesis. The patient was discharged in a good general condition on his postoperative day 4.

The case was presented and discussed at the neuro-oncology tumor board. Adjuvant radiotherapy was initially considered as a potential strategy to improve local control. However, given the extent of surgical resection and the absence of histopathological criteria suggestive of dedifferentiation, specifically, low mitotic activity (3 mitoses per 50 high-power fields), a Ki-67 proliferative index of 5%, and no evidence of tumor necrosis, it was decided to proceed with close clinical and radiological follow-up rather than immediate postoperative radiotherapy.

At 2-year follow-up, posterior spinal fixation (PSF) remained stable, and no evidence of local recurrence or disease progression was identified. This study has been reported in accordance with the SCARE guidelines (Kerwan et al., 2025).

Discussion

In 2011, Hornick and Fletcher (2011a) first described PMHE as a rare vascular neoplasm representing an intermediate-grade malignancy, with a tendency for locally aggressive growth across multiple tissue planes. Although PMHE shares histopathologic characteristics with epithelioid sarcoma–like hemangioendothelioma, its classification remains a matter of debate, as it exhibits distinct spindle-cell morphology, consistent CD31 positivity, preserved INI-1 immunoreactivity, and a lack of CD34 expression (Hornick and Fletcher, 2011a; Miettinen et al., 1999).

PMHE most commonly affects young adult males and typically involves the lower extremities. It has also been reported in various regions, including the head, maxillary sinus, oral cavity, thoracic and abdominal wall, breast, and external genital organs (Mirra et al., 1992; Hornick and Fletcher, 2011a; McGinity et al., 2013; Billings et al., 2011; Billings et al., 2003; Tokyol et al., 2005; PENG et al., 2011; Martín Cocilova et al., 2026).

The differential diagnosis remains broad, including osteoblastoma, osteoid osteoma, osteochondroma, giant cell tumor of bone, aneurysmal bone cyst, and hemangioma (Klag et al., 2019). PMHE is characterized by multiple discontinuous lesions that are hyperintense on T2-weighted and STIR images and hypointense on T1-weighted images. Contrast enhancement and/or surrounding edema may also be observed on MRI (Inyang et al., 2016). Some lesions demonstrate an expansile rather than frankly destructive pattern (McGinity et al., 2013). PET imaging may provide limited diagnostic value, as fluorodeoxyglucose uptake is variable (Inyang et al., 2016).

Management and surgical insights

Given the rarity of spinal involvement in PMHE, management decisions are typically guided by principles applied to similar lesions, particularly since standardized guidelines have not yet been established. While prior reports have described vertebroplasty and costotransversectomy, adjuvant radiotherapy or systemic therapy in PMHE has been reported only in selected advanced cases, with long-term outcomes remaining poorly characterized (Dianat et al., 2019; McGinity et al., 2013; Klag et al., 2019; Mubarak et al., 2021; Al-Qaderi and Mansour, 2019).

Banks et al. (2026) described a rare case of metastatic PMHE with a favorable clinical and radiographic response, suggesting a potential role for radiotherapy in unresectable, residual, or disseminated disease, rather than after complete surgical excision. Gabor et al. (2018) reported disease stabilization with sirolimus therapy, supporting targeted systemic therapy in multifocal or progressive PMHE when surgical options are limited. In the absence of standardized treatment protocols, adjuvant therapy decisions should therefore be individualized. Targeted therapies using rapamycin inhibitors, such as everolimus, have shown efficacy in selected patients; however, their long-term efficacy remains poorly understood (Ozeki et al., 2017; Gabor et al., 2018).

Although PMHE-associated metastasis has been rarely reported, the tumor has a high propensity for local recurrence, with rates as high as 58% (Hornick and Fletcher, 2011b). GTR is the preferred therapeutic option, particularly in cases of osseous involvement, given the locally aggressive behavior of these lesions. Preoperative embolization may be indicated to minimize intraoperative bleeding and achieve tumor devascularization prior to spondylectomy in similar vascular tumors (Al-Qaderi and Mansour, 2019).

Since en bloc spondylectomy maximizes tumor resection while minimizing the risk of recurrence, it may be a preferred approach for managing malignant and locally aggressive spinal lesions, such as chondrosarcomas and chordomas (Jones et al., 2018). By preserving oncologic margins and avoiding intralesional disruption, this technique reduces the risk of tumor cell dissemination compared with piecemeal resection approaches, such as vertebrectomy or vertebral column resection. It has also been associated with reduced local recurrence in other intermediate-grade vascular tumors; however, comparative data specific to spinal PMHE remain limited (Lee et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). On the other hand, en bloc resection is associated with a higher chance of morbidity, with an estimated rate of % 35 and % 2 in terms of complication and mortality, respectively (Boriani et al., 2010).

In our case, one-stage posterior vertebral column resection was favored over alternative strategies, including a staged anterior–posterior approach or en bloc spondylectomy, given the patient's acute neurological deterioration. This approach may avoid the morbidity associated with the anterior approach while providing urgent circumferential decompression (Hamzaoglu et al., 2022). The presence of a pathological fracture with retropulsed fragments enables piecemeal resection, which is safer than en bloc resection, which would require mobilization of an unstable, fractured vertebral body. Given the tumor's intermediate-grade biology, the additional morbidity of en bloc resection with an estimated rate of up to 35% complication rate was not justified (Boriani et al., 2010).

Our case suggests that posterior-only vertebral column resection can achieve GTR and meaningful neurological recovery in the setting of acute spinal cord compression. Furthermore, the 2-year follow-up demonstrating sustained clinical remission provides incremental evidence to the limited literature on intermediate-term outcomes following surgical management of spinal PMHE.

PMHE impairing the spine remains poorly understood; future studies with ongoing accrual of cases and long-term follow-up are required to establish standardized management strategies and improve patient outcomes. Given the high risk of local recurrence after surgical resection in PMHE, close follow-up, including serial imaging and adjuvant therapy for positive margins, remains crucial.

Conclusion

PMHE typically follows an indolent clinical course with a low risk of metastasis. Posterior-only vertebral column resection may allow complete resection of the affected vertebral body, with GTR remaining a cornerstone of therapy. A multidisciplinary approach, combined with close postoperative surveillance and adjuvant therapy when indicated, is warranted to optimize surgical outcomes and mitigate the risk of local recurrence.
