An exploratory radiologic index for evaluating diaphragmatic injury risk in left thoracoabdominal penetrating stab wounds: the diaphragmatic penetration index
Authors: Ekrem Cakar, Onur Olgac Karagulle, Yavuz Selim Komek, Mahmut Emin Cicek, Mustafa Ayvazoglu, Ibrahim Taskin Rakici, Mert Mahsuni Sevinc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPenetrating injuries to the left thoracoabdominal (LTA) region pose significant diagnostic challenges due to the diaphragm’s anatomical location. Early diaphragmatic injuries may be clinically silent and radiologically occult, whereas delayed diagnosis can result in visceral herniation and life-threatening complications. No studies have investigated the role of individualized anatomical parameters, specifically the distance from the entry wound to the diaphragm and diaphragm area, in predicting injury risk. Recognizing this gap, we aimed to introduce and assess a novel parameter, the Diaphragmatic Penetration Index (DPI), that incorporates both factors.MethodsThis retrospective study included 67 adult patients with single-entry LTA penetrating trauma who underwent laparoscopic or open surgical exploration between 2010 and 2025. Patients with multiple wounds, blunt trauma, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, missing CT scans, or loss to follow-up were excluded. DPI was defined as the ratio of diaphragm area (DA) (mm²) to entry wound–to–diaphragm distance (EHD) (mm), both measured from preoperative CT using 3D Slicer and Medseg.ai. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was applied to evaluate DPI’s diagnostic performance.ResultsThe mean patient age was 29.37 ± 11.80 years; 94.0% were male. Diaphragmatic injury was intraoperatively confirmed in 37.3% (n = 25) of patients. Median EHD was 82.48 mm, median DA was 32,450 mm², and median DPI was 394.9. ROC analysis demonstrated that EHD alone did not reach statistical significance (AUC 0.567, 95% CI: 0.415–0.716; p = 0.184), while DA (AUC 0.664, 95% CI: 0.530–0.791; p = 0.013) and DPI (AUC 0.654, 95% CI: 0.516–0.787; p = 0.018) achieved statistical significance but only modest discriminatory ability. Pairwise comparison revealed no significant difference between DA and DPI (p = 0.900), indicating that DA is the principal contributor to DPI performance. At the optimal DPI cut-off of 266.96 (Youden’s index), sensitivity was 48.0% and specificity was 83.3%; retrospective simulation showed that applying this threshold prospectively would have spared 35 patients (83.3% of non-injured patients) unnecessary surgical exploration, while 13 diaphragmatic injuries (52.0%) would have remained undetected.ConclusionNeither DA, EHD, nor DPI demonstrated sufficient individual discriminatory performance to reliably predict diaphragmatic injury in penetrating left thoracoabdominal trauma. EHD was not independently associated with injury likelihood, and DPI offered no significant discriminatory advantage over DA alone. These findings suggest that anatomical parameters derived from CT-based measurements are inadequate as standalone triage tools, and that the high false negative rate at any clinically applicable threshold precludes their use as rule-out criteria. The quantification of this limitation is itself a clinically meaningful contribution, establishing a benchmark for future research. Prospective studies incorporating multiparametric models that combine anatomical, clinical, and radiological variables are required before any of these parameters can be considered for clinical adoption.Keywords: Diaphragmatic penetration index, Left thoracoabdominal trauma, Penetrating injury, CT imaging, Diaphragm injury, ROC analysis

Introduction

Penetrating injuries to the left thoracoabdominal (LTA) region pose a significant diagnostic challenge due to the unique anatomical location of the diaphragm. In the early stages following injury, diaphragmatic lacerations may be clinically silent, with no apparent symptoms or radiological findings. However, delayed or missed diagnoses can result in herniation of abdominal viscera into the thoracic cavity, leading to severe complications such as strangulation, respiratory compromise, or even death. Therefore, early detection and timely surgical intervention are critical for reducing morbidity and mortality associated with diaphragmatic injuries.

The diagnosis of diaphragmatic injury in penetrating trauma (PT) remains complex. While computed tomography (CT) is currently regarded as the gold standard for initial evaluation, its sensitivity in detecting small or occult diaphragmatic tears is limited, particularly in the acute setting [1–3]. Several studies have examined the role of various imaging modalities—including CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)—in identifying diaphragmatic injuries; however, most focus on qualitative radiologic findings rather than individualized anatomical parameters [4, 5].

Notably, there are no studies in the literature evaluating the preoperative prediction of diaphragmatic injury based on the distance between the entry wound and the diaphragm, nor has the influence of an individual’s diaphragmatic area been investigated as a potential risk factor. Anatomical variability in diaphragm size may influence the likelihood of injury even in similar wound trajectories. Recognizing this gap, we aimed to introduce and assess a novel parameter that incorporates both factors.

In this study, we propose the Diaphragmatic Penetration Index (DPI), defined as the ratio of diaphragmatic area (DA, mm²) to entry wound–to–diaphragm distance (EHD, mm). We hypothesize that a higher DPI value, reflecting a larger diaphragm area and shorter wound-to-diaphragm distance, is associated with an increased risk of diaphragmatic injury. The selection of DA and EHD as the two constituent components of the DPI was based on anatomical and geometric reasoning rather than prior validated evidence, as no previous study has examined these specific parameters in combination. EHD was selected on the principle that proximity of a skin entry wound to the diaphragm, as determined by the wound trajectory on CT, is the primary geometric determinant of penetration risk; a shorter EHD implies that the penetrating instrument must travel a shorter distance to reach the diaphragm, thereby increasing the probability of injury. DA was incorporated as an individual-specific scaling factor: a larger diaphragmatic surface area reflects a broader anatomical target and is associated with a shorter EHD for any given entry point in the thoracoabdominal zone, while also accounting for inter-individual variability in thoracic dimensions and body habitus. The DPI, expressed as the ratio DA/EHD, was designed to integrate these two geometric parameters into a single, dimensionless index that reflects both the size of the target organ and the proximity of the wound to it. By retrospectively evaluating patients with single-entry penetrating trauma to the LTA region, we aimed to determine whether DPI, alongside with its individual components DA and EHD, can serve as an exploratory, individualized CT-derived parameters that could complement, but not replace, established clinical and intraoperative criteria in the assessment of patients with suspected diaphragmatic injury.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective study was approved by the Istanbul Training and Research Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2024-19, Date: 29th Nov 2024). A total of 67 patients aged 18 years or older who presented with left thoracoabdominal (LTA) single-entry penetrating trauma to the Emergency Department between 2010 and 2025 were included. All patients underwent either laparoscopic or open laparotomy during elective or emergency diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, and diaphragmatic injuries were recorded intraoperatively.

Patients were excluded if they had multiple entry wounds, died prior to surgery, lacked preoperative CT imaging, or were lost to follow-up after hospitalization. Those with a history of blunt trauma or who underwent cardiopulmonary resuscitation were also excluded, as diaphragmatic rupture may occur in these scenarios through different mechanisms. Additionally, patients sustaining gunshot wounds (GSWs) were excluded due to the high-energy transfer and thermal tissue destruction associated with ballistic injuries, which produce injury patterns fundamentally different from those of low-velocity stab wounds. The study population therefore consisted exclusively of patients with penetrating stab wound injuries.

Data collection

Clinical and operative data were retrieved from electronic hospital records and epicrisis notes. The presence or absence of diaphragmatic injury was documented for each case, as well as associated organ injuries and whether tube thoracostomy was required during hospitalization. All radiological measurements were performed by a board-certified radiologist.

CT imaging and measurement protocol

All patients underwent contrast-enhanced CT scanning using a 128-slice Canon Medical System (Tokyo, Japan) or Philips Ingenuity (Amsterdam, Netherlands). Scan parameters were set at 120 kVp, 200–400 mAs, 1 mm slice thickness, with contrast enhancement.

DICOM images were converted to NIfTI format using 3D Slicer 5.6.2 (www.slicer.org). Diaphragm volumes were segmented and diaphragm area measurements were obtained using the Medseg.ai (Oslo, Norway) online platform (Fig. 1). Axial and coronal reformats were generated with Radiant DICOM Viewer (Warsaw, Poland) at 1 mm thickness. Using the 3D cursor, the location of the diaphragmatic injury was identified (Fig. 2). The EHD was measured perpendicularly from the outer skin surface to the diaphragm at the injury site (Fig. 3). All CT measurements including diaphragm area segmentation and EHD calculation, were performed by a single radiologist (I.T.R.) with dedicated experience in thoracoabdominal trauma imaging. Formal inter-observer and intra-observer reliability analyses were not conducted in this retrospective study. The selection of DA as an index component reflects the anatomical variability of the diaphragm across individuals: thoracic cavity dimensions, body habitus, and subcutaneous adipose tissue thickness all influence both the total diaphragm surface area and the spatial relationship between the body surface and the diaphragm. A larger DA indicates a broader thoracic base and is geometrically associated with a shorter EHD for any given entry point in the thoracoabdominal zone; conversely, a narrower or more obese thorax may position the diaphragm considerably further from a surface wound. DA thus serves as an individual-specific anatomical scaling factor that is intrinsic to the DPI formula. Total diaphragm area — encompassing both hemidiaphragms — was measured to preserve anatomical continuity during segmentation and to reflect the full geometric extent of the structure. All CT acquisitions were performed at end-inspiratory breath-hold (inspirium), standardizing the respiratory phase across all measurements and minimizing respiratory-phase-related variability in diaphragm position and area. The 3D cursor is a triplanar navigation feature of the Radint DICOM Viewer: placing a positional marker on any one plane (axial, coronal, or sagittal) automatically highlights the identical anatomical point on the remaining two planes simultaneously. This co-registration allows unambiguous localization of a diaphragmatic defect across all three orthogonal planes and ensures that EHD is measured from the correct anatomical level.

Statistical analysis

All descriptive and primary statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California USA). Descriptive data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables, and as median (minimum–maximum) for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Comparisons between groups were performed using the independent samples t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric data. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, where appropriate. A two-tailed p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To evaluate the exploratory discriminatory utility of EHD, DA, and DPI individually, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted for each parameter separately. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated using bootstrap resampling (2,000 iterations). Statistical significance of each AUC was assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The optimal cut-off value for DPI was determined using Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity − 1), and corresponding sensitivity and specificity values were reported. Bootstrap confidence intervals and pairwise comparisons between AUCs were performed using Python (version 3.x) with the scikit-learn library to assess whether the composite DPI provided statistically significant discriminatory advantage over either individual component.

Results

A total of 67 patients were included in this study (Table 1). The mean age of the patients was 29.37 ± 11.80 years, and the majority were male (94.0%). Regarding the type of surgery, laparoscopy was the most common approach (68.6%, n = 46), followed by laparotomy (25.4%, n = 17). A conversion from laparoscopy to laparotomy was required in 6.0% (n = 4) of cases. In terms of surgical urgency, emergency procedures accounted for 52.2% (n = 35), while elective surgeries were performed in 47.8% (n = 32). Elective procedures were defined as surgical interventions performed in hemodynamically stable patients without an immediate indication for emergency surgery; these patients underwent diagnostic evaluation for occult diaphragmatic injury, typically within 48 h of admission.

Concurrent organ damage was observed in 40.3% (n = 27) of cases, and tube thoracostomy was required in 29.8% (n = 20). Diaphragmatic injury was detected in 37.3% (n = 25) of the patients (Table 1). For diaphragm-related parameters, the median diaphragm distance (EDH) was 82.48 mm (range: 18.5–167.3 mm), and the total diaphragm area (DA) had a median of 32,450 mm² (range: 189.1–323,400.5 mm²). The Diaphragmatic Penetration Index (DPI) had a median value of 394.9 with a range between 2.18 and 3968.10 (Table 1).

ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the exploratory utility of DA, EHD, and DPI as adjunct parameters in the assessment of diaphragmatic injury (Table 2). EHD alone yielded an AUC of 0.567 (95% CI: 0.415–0.716, p = 0.184), with an optimal cut-off of 60.00 mm, a sensitivity of 80.0%, and a specificity of 38.1%, indicating no statistically significant discriminatory ability. DA alone demonstrated a statistically significant but modest AUC of 0.664 (95% CI: 0.530–0.791, p = 0.013), with an optimal cut-off of 37,225.80 mm², a sensitivity of 88.0%, and a specificity of 42.9%. DPI yielded an AUC of 0.654 (95% CI: 0.516–0.787, p = 0.018). These results suggest that DA is the principal contributor to the discriminatory capacity of DPI, whereas EHD alone does not reach statistical significance as an independent parameter. None of the three parameters demonstrated strong discriminatory ability, and all should be regarded as exploratory adjuncts rather than standalone diagnostic tools.

The optimal cut-off value for DPI, determined using Youden’s index, was 266.96, yielding a sensitivity of 48% and a specificity of 83.3% (Table 2). Of the 48 patients with DPI > 266.96, 35 had no diaphragmatic injury at surgery (true negatives) and 13 had a confirmed diaphragmatic injury (false negatives). Applying the DPI cut-off prospectively would have potentially spared 35 patients (35/42: 83.3%) an unnecessary surgical exploration; however, 13 diaphragmatic injuries (52% of all confirmed injuries) would have remained undetected, underscoring the limitations of DPI as a standalone decision-making tool.

The ROC curves (Fig. 4) illustrate the exploratory performance of EHD, DA, and DPI as adjunct parameters in diaphragmatic injury assessment. The overall low-to-moderate AUC values across all three parameters indicate that additional clinical, intraoperative, or radiological variables are likely required to improve risk stratification in this setting (ΔAUC = − 0.010, p = 0.900 for DPI vs. DA; ΔAUC = + 0.088, p = 0.061 for DPI vs. EHD) .

To quantify the potential clinical impact of the DPI threshold, a contingency analysis was performed using the Youden-derived cut-off of 266.96 (Table 3). Of the 25 patients with confirmed diaphragmatic injury, 12 (48.0%) had a DPI ≤ 266.96 (true positives) and 13 (52.0%) had a DPI > 266.96 (false negatives), consistent with the modest sensitivity of 48% observed in the ROC analysis. Of the 42 patients without diaphragmatic injury who nonetheless underwent surgical exploration, 35 (83.3%) had a DPI > 266.96 (true negatives), representing the proportion of patients who might potentially have been spared an unnecessary surgical procedure had the DPI threshold been applied prospectively. The remaining 7 (16.7%) non-injured patients had a DPI ≤ 266.96 (false positives). These figures confirm that while DPI demonstrates acceptable specificity at this threshold, its sensitivity is insufficient to reliably exclude diaphragmatic injury, and the index cannot serve as a standalone criterion for surgery avoidance.

Discussion

Early diagnosis of diaphragm injury, or assessment of suspected injury based upon the known or suspected trajectory of the penetrating instrument, in penetrating trauma in the left thoracoabdominal region due to sharp or stabbing instruments is challenging and may lead to complications such as gastrointestinal herniation. Therefore, the most discussed aspect of this injury is the diagnostic difficulty. In most cases, diaphragmatic damage is not detected during radiological imaging. The findings are non-specific and are generally observed during emergency exploratory surgery conducted for other organ injuries. In the literature, the rate of missed diaphragmatic injuries in conservatively managed patients varies between 12% and 66%, and such injuries may even be missed during laparotomies [6–8].

The thoracoabdominal region is bounded by the 4th intercostal space anteriorly, the 6th intercostal space laterally, the 8th intercostal space posteriorly, and the costal margin inferiorly. The upper limit of the abdominal cavity is formed by the diaphragm, which rises during expiration to the level of the nipple anteriorly and the lower edge of the scapula posteriorly. This corresponds to the 4th, 6th, and 8th intercostal spaces, respectively. Penetrating injuries to the left thoracoabdominal region are more common than on the right side. This is thought to be due to the fact that most people use their right hand, which leads them to target the left side of the opposing person. In the literature, diaphragmatic injuries caused by penetrating trauma are reported to be more frequent than those caused by blunt trauma [9].

In the literature, the average age of patients with diaphragmatic injuries due to penetrating trauma is reported to be 30 years, which is similar to the average age of 29.37 in our study. Similarly, diaphragmatic injuries were predominantly observed in males, consistent with our findings. The study by Corbenlini and colleagues was different from ours in that it also included extra-abdominal organs, such as the lungs and thoracic bones, and reported a 71.4% rate of concomitant organ injuries with diaphragmatic injury. In our study, only abdominal organ injuries were considered, and this rate was 43%. In the study by D’Souza et al., abdominal organ injuries were reported in 77.9% of cases, which is similar to the findings of our study [10, 11].

In our study, laparoscopy was preferred in 68.6% of cases, and conversion to laparotomy was observed in 6%. Elective procedures were performed in 47.8% of the surgeries. In the present study, all surgical explorations were performed via an abdominal approach (laparoscopy or laparotomy); no patient underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) or open thoracotomy. Diaphragmatic injury was reported in 37.3% of cases where surgery was performed. Koganti et al. reported that negative diagnostic laparoscopy was performed in 114 out of 206 patients (55%) admitted after penetrating trauma [12]. In a multi-center study by Mancini et al., a consensus from 24 hospitals in France suggested that in stable patients with diaphragmatic injury diagnosed by CT and concomitant intrathoracic herniation, prompt repair should be performed whenever possible. However, in cases where emergency treatment of associated organ injuries is necessary, the repair of the diaphragm should be performed 48–72 h later. In patients with uncertain diagnosis on CT, laparoscopy is recommended. The same study also highlighted that early repair provides more favorable anatomical conditions, while delayed repair may lead to fibrosis and retraction of the defect’s borders, making surgery more difficult and potentially requiring the use of mesh for diaphragmatic repair [13, 14].

Chest X-rays have poor performance in diagnosing diaphragmatic injuries, with a false negative rate of 48% [15]. However, the presence of air-fluid levels in the thorax or the tip of the nasogastric tube seen within the thoracic cavity on a chest X-ray can be diagnostic [16]. CT is the reference method for diagnosing diaphragmatic injury, and the sensitivity is reported to range between 14% and 71%, while specificity ranges from 76% to 99% [16, 17]. CT findings, such as the “collar sign,” “dependent-viscera sign,” and diaphragm discontinuity, are characteristic of diaphragmatic injuries [13, 18]. In a meta-analysis by Reitano et al. reviewing 15 studies published between 2001 and 2019, contrast-enhanced CT was preferred as the imaging method for detecting diaphragmatic injury. The same study found that false negatives were more common than false positives in CT scans [3]. Stein et al. conducted a study involving 803 patients, analyzing the role of CT in diagnosing diaphragmatic injuries, and reported sensitivity of 94% and specificity of 95.9% [19]. However, the debate regarding whether CT alone is sufficient for detecting diaphragmatic injuries remains unresolved. Literature supports a 40% incidence of missed injuries in the left thoracoabdominal region in penetrating trauma. As a result, diagnostic laparoscopy is recommended as the standard method in these patients. This approach is also supported by the Eastern Trauma Surgery Association as part of their practice guidelines [20].

Recent evidence further supports a selective non-operative management (SNOM) approach in hemodynamically stable patients with left thoracoabdominal penetrating injuries: Niakan et al. demonstrated that CT imaging achieved a sensitivity of 85.7% and specificity of 56.0% for mild diaphragmatic injury detection, concluding that in stable patients with a completely normal CT scan, non-therapeutic laparoscopies may be safely avoided [21]. The DPI is proposed as a quantitative radiologic parameter to support this selective strategy by providing a threshold-based criterion for ruling out diaphragmatic injury.

In our study, 67 patients were evaluated, with the median EHD being 82.48 mm, a median DA of 32,450 mm², and median DPI value of 394.9. Diaphragmatic injury was detected in 37.3% of the cases. According to ROC analysis of individual and composite discriminatory utility of these parameters, EHD alone did not demonstrate a statistically significant association with diaphragmatic injury, indicating that the distance between the entry wound and the diaphragm, considered in isolation, is insufficient as a predictor of injury. DA alone achieved statistical significance with a modest AUC of 0.664, suggesting a weak but meaningful individual association with injury likelihood. DPI yielded a comparable AUC of 0.654, indicating that DA is the principal contributor to the composite index and that combining the two components does not meaningfully improve discrimination over DA alone.

At the Youden-derived optimal cut-off of 266.96, DPI demonstrated a sensitivity of 48.0% and a specificity of 83.3%. The low sensitivity at this threshold precludes its use as a rule-out parameter, as more than half of confirmed diaphragmatic injuries would remain undetected. Conversely, the relatively high specificity suggests that patients with DPI ≤ 266.96 are more likely to harbour a true injury and may warrant surgical exploration. Taken together, these findings indicate that none of the three parameters achieves adequate standalone discriminatory performance, and all should be regarded as exploratory adjunct metrics requiring integration with clinical and intraoperative findings rather than independent decision-making tools.

Notably, the observed direction of association was contrary to our initial hypothesis: injured patients demonstrated a lower median DPI (414.6) compared with non-injured patients (621.3). This unexpected finding may reflect the influence of DA as the dominant component of DPI — injured patients in our cohort had a smaller median DA, which geometrically reduces the DPI value regardless of EHD. This may suggest that a smaller diaphragmatic surface area, rather than proximity of the wound to the diaphragm, was the more influential anatomical determinant in this cohort. The counterintuitive direction of the association further underscores the exploratory nature of the index and the need for prospective validation before any clinical inferences can be drawn.

Recent literature suggests a tendency to avoid diagnostic laparoscopy in penetrating trauma of the left thoracoabdominal region. In this context, DPI may serve as a measurable, quantifiable adjunct for radiologists and surgeons evaluating patients with suspected diaphragmatic injury. However, given that neither DA, EHD, nor DPI demonstrated sufficient standalone discriminatory performance in the present study, DPI should not be interpreted as a decisive triage criterion. Rather, the current findings establish a quantitative basis for understanding the limitations of anatomical CT-based indices, and any future clinical integration of DPI would require combination with established clinical, intraoperative, and imaging findings within a structured multiparametric framework. External validation in independent, prospective cohorts is required before any form of routine adoption can be recommended.

This study has several limitations. The relatively small sample size and single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, diaphragm measurements were derived from preoperative CT imaging, which may be subject to variability related to patient positioning, slice thickness and operator technique. Standardization of measurement protocols will be essential in future studies. Additionally, all surgical explorations in this cohort were performed exclusively via an abdominal approach; the absence of thoracic approaches such as VATS or thoracotomy may have limited the detection of posterior or lateral diaphragmatic injuries, which can sometimes be more readily identified through a thoracic approach. Furthermore, all CT measurements were performed by a single operator without formal inter-observer or intra-observer reliability analysis, which limits the current assessment of measurement reproducibility; structured reliability testing should be included in future prospective validation studies.

Conclusion

Penetrating left thoracoabdominal trauma remains a clinical dilemma in cases where diaphragmatic injury must be reliably excluded to avoid both unnecessary surgical exploration and missed pathology. The present study sought to evaluate whether anatomical laparoscopic parameters, individually and in composite, could provide meaningful discriminatory information in this setting.

Our findings demonstrate that neither EHD, DA, nor DPI achieved adequate standalone discriminatory performance for diaphragmatic injury detection. EHD was not independently associated with injury likelihood, and DPI offered no significant improvement over DA alone. At the Youden-derived optimal cut-off, DPI yielded acceptable specificity but unacceptably low sensitivity, with more than half of confirmed injuries falling above the threshold. These results indicate that DPI cannot function as a rule-out parameter, and that no single anatomical index derived from CT-derived measurement is sufficient to guide surgical decision-making in isolation.

Nevertheless, the systematic quantification of these limitations constitutes a meaningful contribution in itself. By demonstrating the boundaries of anatomical index-based approaches, this study provides a clear benchmark for future research and argues against the premature clinical adoption of such parameters without further validation. Prospective studies incorporating multiparametric models that integrate anatomical measurements with clinical findings, mechanism of injury, and imaging data are needed before any CT-derived index can be recommended as part of a structured diagnostic algorithm. Until such evidence is available, surgical decision-making in penetrating left thoracoabdominal trauma should continue to be guided by established clinical and radiological criteria.
