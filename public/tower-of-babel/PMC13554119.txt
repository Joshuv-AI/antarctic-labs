A comparative study on buzzy device vs digital distraction technique for mitigating pain during venipuncture among hospitalised children: randomised controlled trial
Authors: Benazeera, Priya Reshma Aranha, K Yashaswini, Noothan Laveena Lasrado, Tamiru Alene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPain is a complex and unpleasant phenomenon, particularly in children, wherein frequent use of needle procedures during the process of hospitalisation significantly increases somatic and emotional distress in children. This randomised clinical trial was conducted among children aged 4–12 years admitted to a paediatric ward in Karnataka, India. Participants were randomly allocated to one of three arms: a Buzzy device intervention arm, a computer tablet distraction arm, or a control arm receiving routine care. Pain during venipuncture was assessed using the Faces Pain Scale–Revised (FPS-R), scored on a 0–10 scale. A total of 96 children were randomised, with 32 allocated to each of the Buzzy device, computer tablet distraction, and control arms. Both distraction interventions significantly reduced the FPR-R pain score compared with the control arm. The mean FPS-R score was 0.31 ± 0.74 in the Buzzy device arm and 3.25 ± 1.95 in the computer tablet arm. The between-group differences in pain scores were statistically significant (p < 0.001), with the Buzzy device demonstrating greater effectiveness than the computer tablet distraction. The Buzzy device and distraction techniques significantly minimised FPS-R pain scores in children, with the Buzzy device being the most effective. These findings support the incorporation of such interventions into paediatric clinical practice to enhance patient comfort.Trial Registration: Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2023/04/051864 registered on 21/04/2023).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57395-4.Keywords: Needle, Injection, Hospitalisation, Blood drawing, Procedural painSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Pediatric healthcare practitioners are guided by the ethical tenet of “do no harm,” which emphasises the importance of preventing and relieving avoidable pain in children1–3. Vein puncture procedures are among the most common procedures performed in a hospital setting and are often characterised by pain, fear, and anxiety4,5. These experiences can hinder cooperation and are associated with adverse psychological consequences, such as avoidance of future healthcare encounters6,7. Pain experienced during needle procedures has been recognised as a major hindrance to the utilisation of preventive healthcare services4,8,9. Children with developmental, emotional, or behavioural problems are especially vulnerable to the pain of venipuncture due to difficulties in comprehending medical procedures and expressing their concerns10. Therefore, pain management is a very important aspect of pediatric healthcare. The American Academy of Paediatrics and the American Pain Society recommend the evaluation and treatment of pain, fear, and stress related to minor procedures11. Although pharmacologic strategies, such as topical anaesthetics, are common and effective, their use in busy practice settings may be limited by cost, time, and availability12,13. Furthermore, these strategies may not adequately address the psychological aspects of procedural pain14,15. As a result, there is an increasing need for complementary non-pharmacologic approaches that are safe, cost-effective, and easily incorporated into practice16. These approaches seek to change cognitive, behavioural, and emotional responses to painful stimuli, with preparation, relaxation, parental participation, and distraction showing promise in improving children’s coping with procedures17. Of these, the Buzzy tool is a novel non-pharmacologic approach that is portable, inexpensive, and easy to use, making it amenable to incorporation into practice18,19. A study by Fuchs et al. demonstrated that this tool is effective in reducing pain and anxiety associated with procedures such as venipuncture, immunisation, and intravenous therapy, with high levels of acceptability reported among pediatric patients20. Distraction techniques are also well known for their ability to distract from painful stimuli and reduce anticipatory anxiety. Recent advances in technology have made it possible to use digital distraction through engaging audiovisual media and interactive applications that are developmentally appropriate and readily available in practice settings21,22. However, the effectiveness of both the Buzzy tool and digital distraction has not been adequately compared during venipuncture23. There is evidence that these interventions can improve cooperation, decrease fear, and increase overall procedural satisfaction in children and their caregivers24,25. The collection of venous blood specimens is one of the most common invasive procedures in pediatric settings and remains a significant source of distress for children. The development of evidence on the relative benefits of these interventions may help healthcare professionals choose child-friendly approaches for the management of procedural pain26. The purpose of this study was to compare the effectiveness of the Buzzy tool and computer-based digital distraction in reducing pain associated with venipuncture in hospitalised children. Thus, the hypothesis of this study is:

H1: There is a significant difference in pain scores among children in Arm I, Arm II, and the control arm.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was a triple-arm, parallel-group, randomised controlled trial conducted at a tertiary-level, multispecialty hospital in India. The study followed CONSORT guidelines for randomised controlled trials, and a CONSORT flow diagram (Fig. 1) presents details of participant recruitment, follow-up, and data analysis. The Yenepoya Ethics Committee-1 approved the trial (YEC-1/2021/053; approval date: 17/01/2022) and prospectively registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2023/04/051864; registration date: 21/04/2023). The study adhered to institutional ethical requirements and followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Assent and consent procedures were conducted in accordance with the Indian Council of Medical Research guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians for all participants. Assent was obtained from school-age children (≥ 7 years) using age-appropriate explanations. For preschool children (< 7 years), formal assent was not required; however, verbal willingness and cooperation were assessed and respected. Eligible participants were recruited and assigned to the intervention or control groups after obtaining consent and assent.

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power software for a one-way ANOVA model. Assuming an effect size of 0.40, statistical power of 95%, and a significance level of 0.05, the calculated total sample required was 102 participants, with 34 children allocated to each study group.

Data collection instruments

Data were collected using a structured demographic form and the Faces Pain Scale–Revised (FPS-R). The demographic form captured information about the child (age, sex, educational level, medical diagnosis, and type of venipuncture performed) and the accompanying parent (age, relationship to the child, and previous hospitalisation).

Pain intensity was measured using the Faces Pain Scale–Revised (FPS-R), a validated self-report tool designed for assessing acute pain in pediatric populations. The scale comprises six facial expressions depicting progressively increasing pain severity, ranging from a neutral face (no pain) to an expression indicating extreme discomfort. Children were asked to identify the face that most closely matched the intensity of pain they experienced during the procedure. The corresponding numerical values assigned to the faces are 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10, with 0 representing no pain and 10 the highest level of pain.

Randomisation and allocation concealment

Participants were randomly allocated to one of three study arms (Buzzy device, digital distraction, or control group) using block randomisation. The random allocation sequence was prepared in advance by an independent nursing officer who was not involved in participant recruitment, intervention administration, or outcome assessment. Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE), each containing the group assignment. After obtaining informed consent and assent, the independent nursing officer opened the next envelope in sequence immediately before the venipuncture procedure and assigned the participant to the respective study group, thereby minimising the risk of selection bias.

Due to the nature of the interventions, blinding of participants and the administering researcher was not feasible. However, to minimise assessment bias, pain intensity was measured immediately after the procedure using the validated Faces Pain Scale–Revised (FPS-R), with standardised instructions provided uniformly to all participants. Although outcome assessment relied on children’s self-reports and could not be fully blinded, consistent administration procedures were maintained across all study groups.

Study intervention administration protocol

Participants were allocated to one of three study arms: the Buzzy device arm, digital distraction arm, or control arm (Table 1). In the Buzzy device arm, combined vibration and cold therapy were administered using the Buzzy device, with pre-cooled ice wings applied 5–10 cm proximal to the venipuncture site approximately 30–60 s before the procedure and maintained until completion. In the digital distraction arm, children were provided with age-appropriate audiovisual content on a tablet before and during venipuncture to divert attention from procedural pain. Participants in the control arm received routine ward care and standard verbal reassurance during the procedure. All children were positioned supine during venipuncture, parental presence was permitted throughout the procedure, and pain intensity was assessed immediately after venipuncture using the Faces Pain Scale–Revised (FPS-R).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to summarise the baseline characteristics of study participants. Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations (SD) and compared between groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, with group differences assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Pain outcomes were assessed using the Faces Pain Scale-Revised (FPS-R). Due to the non-normal distribution of FPS-R scores, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied to compare pain scores among the Control, Intervention 1 (Buzzy device), and Intervention 2 (tablet distraction) groups. The effectiveness of the interventions was further evaluated by comparing the proportion of children reporting zero versus non-zero pain scores using Fisher’s exact test. Subgroup analyses were performed for baseline variables that showed heterogeneity across groups. A multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to assess the influence of gender, type of procedure (blood collection vs. intravenous cannulation), hospitalisation status (self vs. family members), and group assignment (control vs. two intervention groups) on Facial Pain Scores (FPS) in children. The regression model demonstrated strong explanatory power, accounting for 84% of the variance in pain scores (R² = 0.84). A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

The outcomes observed in this study are detailed below.

Demographic characteristics of the children

The demographic characteristics of children in the Buzzy device, digital distraction, and control arms are presented in Table 2. The mean age of children in the Buzzy device and digital distraction arms was 9.09 ± 1.51 years, while the control arm had a mean age of 9.44 ± 1.56 years. In the Buzzy device arm, a higher proportion of participants were female (53%), whereas male children constituted the majority in both the digital distraction arm and the control arm (56% each). Regarding educational level, the largest proportion of children in the Buzzy device arm were in 5th grade (37.5%). In the digital distraction arm, the highest proportion of children (18%) were enrolled in the 2nd and 3rd standards, whereas in the control arm, 28.1% were in the 6th standard. Regarding clinical diagnosis, Respiratory Tract Infection (RTI) was the most common diagnosis in the Buzzy device arm (40.6%). In contrast, genitourinary problems were most frequently observed in the digital distraction arm (34.3%) and the control arm (21.8%). The majority of children in all three arms underwent intravenous cannulation. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences among the three groups with respect to age, gender, educational level, and procedure undergone (p > 0.05), indicating that the groups were comparable for these baseline characteristics. However, a significant difference in the distribution of diagnoses was observed among the groups (p < 0.001), suggesting that the groups were not comparable with respect to diagnoses.

Demographic characteristics of the parents

Table 3 reveals that the mean age of mothers in the Buzzy device arm was 31.66 ± 3.00 years, in the digital distraction arm, 32.38 ± 3.88 years, and in the control arm, 33.3 ± 3.88 years. Mothers in all three groups accompanied their children during hospitalisation. A significant proportion of mothers in the Buzzy device arm (56%) and the digital distraction arm (47%) had completed primary education. The majority of mothers were homemakers, accounting for 50% in the Buzzy device arm, 78% in the digital distraction arm, and 75% in the control arm. Statistical analysis showed no significant differences among the three groups in maternal age or previous hospitalisation experience (p > 0.05), indicating that the groups were comparable for these characteristics. However, a significant difference was observed in maternal education level and employment status across the groups (p < 0.05), suggesting that the groups were not comparable with respect to these variables.

Pain level of buzzy, digital distraction and control arms during venipuncture between the study groups

A comparison of pain scores among the buzzy, digital distraction, and control arms revealed statistically significant differences (Table 4). The Kruskal-Wallis test indicated a significant difference in pain scores across the groups (p < 0.001). The control arm reported the highest mean pain score of 8 ± 1.41, while the buzzy arm had the lowest mean pain score of 0.31 ± 0.74. The digital distraction arm showed an intermediate pain level, with a mean score of 3.25 ± 1.95. The median values and interquartile ranges also reflected this trend: the control arm had a median pain score of 8 and an interquartile range (IQR) of 1.5. In contrast, the buzzy arm had a median of 0 and an IQR of 0, indicating minimal or no pain.

Predictive factors associated with pain in study participants

Table 5 shows that gender did not significantly affect facial pain scores, with male children demonstrating slightly higher scores than female children; however, this difference was not statistically significant (β = 0.17, t = 0.56, p = 0.575). Similarly, the type of procedure (blood collection versus intravenous cannulation) did not significantly influence pain scores (β = −0.07, t = − 0.19, p = 0.846). This finding should be interpreted with caution, as only 22–28% of children underwent blood collection, resulting in limited representation for this procedure.

Children accompanied by family members showed slightly higher pain scores compared to those whose parents had previous hospitalisation experience; however, this association was not statistically significant (β = 0.51, t = 1.61, p = 0.11).

In contrast, study group assignment demonstrated a strong and statistically significant effect on pain scores. Children in the Buzzy arm exhibited a significant reduction of 7.77 points in facial pain scores compared to the control arm (β=−7.77, t = − 21.13, p < 0.001). Similarly, children in the digital distraction arm showed a significant reduction of 4.83 points relative to the control group (β = -4.83, t = -13.14, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that both interventions were effective in reducing facial pain scores, with the Buzzy arm demonstrating greater effectiveness than the digital distraction arm. The regression model explained 84% of the variance in facial pain scores (R² = 0.84), indicating strong predictive capability.

Discussion

This study aimed to compare the efficacy of the Buzzy device and digital distraction methods in reducing venipuncture pain in hospitalised children aged 4 to 12 years. The findings demonstrated that both interventions significantly reduced facial pain scores; however, the Buzzy device was more effective at reducing procedural pain. In the present study, the Buzzy device functioned as a gate-control counter-stimulation intervention, combining mechanical vibration and cold stimulation to modulate pain transmission during venipuncture. In contrast, the computer tablet intervention served as a cognitive/digital distraction, diverting the child’s attention from the painful procedure through audiovisual engagement. Demographic analysis indicated that most patients (53%) in the Buzzy group were female and had lower pain ratings. These findings are consistent with previous studies by Aranha (2024), Oh et al. (2023), and Huzmeli et al. (2024), which highlighted the clinical efficacy of the Buzzy device as a gate-control counter-stimulation method for pediatric procedural pain management27–29. Conversely, Anandhukrishnan et al. (2018) reported that male children experienced higher pain levels, suggesting possible gender-based differences in pain perception and expression that require further investigation30.

Maternal presence during hospitalisation, a trend observed in the present study and supported by Altay et al. (2017), underscores the importance of parental support in maintaining children’s emotional and psychological well-being31,32. Mother-child interaction may enhance the effectiveness of cognitive/digital distraction interventions by improving children’s comfort, cooperation, and emotional security during procedures. The comparative effectiveness of non-pharmacological pain management approaches remains an evolving field in pediatric research33,34. The findings of the present study differ from those of Cozzi et al. (2021), who reported no statistically significant difference between the Buzzy device and handheld digital distraction devices in reducing self-reported procedural pain35. However, the present findings align with the growing evidence reported by Merino et al. (2023), demonstrating that both gate-control counter-stimulation methods, such as the Buzzy device, and cognitive/digital distraction techniques are effective in minimising venipuncture pain in children, although effectiveness may vary according to age, healthcare setting, and delivery mode36. Global evidence continues to support the effectiveness of the Buzzy device in diverse healthcare settings. Simoncini et al. (2023) demonstrated its utility as a non-invasive gate-control counter-stimulation tool for pediatric pain reduction37. The present study extends this evidence by demonstrating a greater reduction in pain scores than previously reported. Similarly, Şahin et al. (2022) documented a pain score difference of 2.5 ± 4.7, whereas the present study identified a more conservative, yet clinically meaningful, difference of 0.267 ± 0.68, suggesting that even modest numerical reductions may correspond to substantial improvements in children’s procedural comfort. Despite increasing international use of the Buzzy device, its implementation within the Indian healthcare context remains limited. Socio-cultural factors, healthcare infrastructure, and financial constraints may contribute to its restricted adoption. Additional randomised controlled trials within Indian pediatric settings are necessary to evaluate the clinical applicability of gate-control counter-stimulation approaches compared with conventional pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions. Furthermore, cost-effectiveness analyses are needed to determine the feasibility and sustainability of integrating Buzzy devices into resource-constrained healthcare systems. Qualitative research exploring the experiences and perceptions of children, parents, and healthcare providers regarding both cognitive/digital distraction methods and gate-control counter-stimulation techniques could provide deeper insights into acceptability, usability, and implementation barriers. Such evidence would support the development of culturally appropriate and evidence-based pediatric pain management strategies.

Overall, although both cognitive/digital distraction using computer tablets and gate-control counter-stimulation using the Buzzy device were effective in reducing venipuncture pain, the Buzzy device demonstrated superior efficacy. These findings support the continued evaluation and adaptation of innovative non-pharmacological pain management interventions across diverse healthcare settings, including India. Further mixed-methods research and economic evaluations are warranted to guide clinical practice and policy regarding the broader implementation of such pediatric pain management tools.

Limitation of the study

This randomised controlled trial has several limitations. As the study was conducted in a single tertiary care centre, the findings may not be generalisable to other healthcare settings. Although randomisation was employed, some differences in clinical diagnoses and selected parental characteristics were observed between groups, which may have influenced children’s pain responses. Blinding was not feasible due to the interventions’ visible nature. The lack of blinding in outcome assessment may have introduced performance and reporting bias, potentially leading to overestimation of the intervention effects. Pain was assessed using the Faces Pain Scale–Revised (FPS-R), a validated but subjective self-report tool, and responses may have been influenced by individual developmental, emotional, and situational factors.

The smaller number of children undergoing blood collection limited procedure-specific comparisons. Moreover, only immediate pain outcomes were assessed, without evaluation of long-term effects or cost-effectiveness. The very low pain scores observed in the Buzzy group, although statistically significant, are lower than typically reported in the literature and may reflect contextual factors such as participant selection, controlled study conditions, or response bias. Therefore, the clinical generalisability and replicability of these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future multicentre studies with prospective trial registration, larger and more diverse samples, and long-term follow-up are recommended to strengthen the evidence base.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that both the Buzzy device and digital distraction techniques are effective non-pharmacological interventions for reducing venipuncture-related pain in children. Significant reductions in pain scores were observed in both intervention groups compared with routine care, confirming the effectiveness of distraction-based and sensory modulation strategies in pediatric procedural pain management. Among the interventions evaluated, the Buzzy device, which combines cold application and vibration, proved superior at minimising pain compared with tablet-based digital distraction. These findings highlight the clinical relevance of integrating simple, safe, and child-friendly pain management approaches into routine pediatric care. The use of such interventions can enhance patient comfort, reduce procedural distress, and promote a more positive healthcare experience for children and their families. Given its ease of use, rapid action, and cost-effectiveness, the Buzzy device may be considered a preferred option in resource-constrained settings.
