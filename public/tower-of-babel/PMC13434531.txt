Mental health literacy mediates associations of self rated health and chronic disease status with anxiety and depression in Chinese residents
Authors: Deliang Kong, Jiyuan Zhong, Yong Li, Wenjun Wang, Cuihong Li, Hao Yang, Guoqing Jiang, Jianjun Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnxiety and depression are important mental health problems worldwide, and their comorbid state, anxiety-depression comorbidity (ADC) , has greater symptom severity. With the increasing burden of chronic non-communicable diseases, residents’ physical and mental health problems have shown an overlapping trend. The mediating role of mental health literacy (MHL) between physical health and psychological outcomes warrants further investigation. Based on the national survey initiative, this study conducted a large-scale stratified multistage population survey across all districts and counties of Chongqing, southwest China, and included 36,874 permanent residents. Mental health literacy (MHL) was assessed using the National Mental Health Literacy Questionnaire (NMHLQ). Depression and anxiety were screened using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), respectively. Descriptive analyses were performed to characterize epidemiological features. Multivariable regression analysis was conducted across the three psychological outcome groups of anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity to explore the effects of self-rated health (SRH) and chronic disease on multiple psychological outcomes and the effects after variable adjustment, and subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis were used to test robustness. The Bootstrap resampling method was used to test the mediating effect, and the standardized path coefficients and effect proportions of each mediation model were calculated. The positive screening rates for anxiety, depression, and ADC in the surveyed area were 2.72% (1004/36874), 4.53% (1672/36874), and 2.11% (777/36,874), respectively. The odds of anxiety, depression, and comorbidity among residents with fair or poor SRH were 2.76 (95% CI 2.40–3.17), 3.31 (95% CI 2.97–3.69), and 2.98 (95% CI 2.55–3.49), respectively. The corresponding odds among residents with chronic disease were 1.40 (95% CI 1.21–1.62), 1.51 (95% CI 1.35–1.70), and 1.41 (95% CI 1.19–1.65), respectively. MHL was negatively associated with the three psychological outcomes. After adjustment for relevant covariates, stratified analysis, and sensitivity analysis, the above associations remained robust. Multivariable logistic regression and multi-group mediation effect analysis showed that MHL had an independent protective role and played a 7–8% partial mediating role in the effects of SRH and chronic disease on mental health outcomes. Poor SRH and chronic disease were associated with anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity among residents, and MHL had an independent protective and partial mediating role in these associations. These findings suggest that residents’ MHL should be improved, and integrated interventions combining chronic disease management with psychological screening should be strengthened.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-63540-w.Keywords: Anxiety-depression comorbidity, Self-rated health, Mental health literacy, Chronic disease status, Multivariate regression analysis, Bootstrap mediation effectSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Psychology, Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

Anxiety and depressive disorders have become major mental health problems contributing to the global burden of disease1. Global burden of disease data indicate that more than 1 billion people worldwide live with mental disorders, among which anxiety disorders and depressive disorders are the most common types2. Anxiety and depression not only reduce individuals’ quality of life and social functioning, but also impose a heavy burden on public health systems and socioeconomic development3. In China, a national epidemiological survey showed that the lifetime prevalence of any mental disorder among adults was approximately 16.6%, with depressive disorders and anxiety disorders being the main types of emotional disorders4. Meanwhile, the accelerated process of population aging and the high prevalence of chronic non-communicable diseases have further compounded mental health risks among residents5. Existing studies have shown that residents’ mental health status is closely related to physical health status, subjective health evaluation, and health-related literacy, suggesting the need to explore the mechanisms influencing mental health outcomes from the perspectives of physical health, mental health cognition, and integrated health6,7.

Self-rated health (SRH) is a comprehensive health indicator commonly used in community surveys. It can integrate physical symptoms, functional status, and psychological experience, reflecting individuals’ subjective evaluation of their overall health8. Previous studies have shown that poorer SRH is associated not only with physical outcomes such as mortality, chronic diseases, and functional limitations, but also with depression, anxiety, and their co-occurrence. A cross-lagged analysis based on China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) data showed a bidirectional association between SRH and depressive symptoms, and SRH could predict subsequent depression; follow-up studies from other countries have also suggested that SRH can predict future risks of depression and anxiety9,10. Studies based on samples of older adults and community surveys in China have shown that individuals with co-occurring depression and anxiety have poorer SRH, and those with poorer SRH have higher detection rates of psychological symptoms11,12. In addition, other studies have also suggested that chronic diseases may affect anxiety and depressive states through pathways such as pain, functional limitation, economic pressure, and health uncertainty13,14. In recent years, anxiety-depression comorbidity (ADC) has gradually been regarded as a mental health state with independent public health significance. A systematic review indicated that comorbid levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms may interact with each other15. The Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety (NESDA) cohort study also showed that anxiety disorders and depressive disorders have complex comorbidity patterns, and individuals with comorbidity usually have a greater symptom burden and poorer course characteristics16. Therefore, analyzing anxiety, depression, and ADC as separate outcomes may help explore the risk characteristics of single symptom states and comorbid states within the spectrum of interrelated psychological symptoms, while also improving the precision of the study findings.

Mental health literacy (MHL) refers to the knowledge, attitudes, and abilities that enable individuals to identify, understand, and respond to mental health problems, and is an important psychosocial resource for mental health promotion17. When Jorm et al. proposed the concept of MHL, they emphasized that it includes the ability to recognize mental disorders, knowledge of risk factors, and self-help strategies18. Recent studies by Elshamy et al. and Chua et al. further indicated that insufficient knowledge of mental illness, stigmatizing attitudes, and barriers to service access may affect individuals’ recognition, expression, and help-seeking for psychological distress, suggesting that MHL is reflected not only in knowledge level but also in stigma awareness and help-seeking decision-making ability19,20. Meanwhile, SRH and chronic disease status may affect individuals’ level of MHL. A study by He et al. showed that having chronic disease was an influencing factor for lower MHL attainment among residents, suggesting that the burden of chronic disease may weaken mental health knowledge, attitudes, and coping abilities21. A study by Kim et al. in Korea showed that individuals with poorer subjective health status had a higher likelihood of depression, suggesting a link between poor SRH and insufficient health information comprehension22. Furthermore, MHL may affect anxiety, depression, and ADC. A review study from Australia showed that insufficient recognition of mental health problems and stigmatizing attitudes may hinder timely help-seeking, thereby increasing the risk of persistent or worsening psychological symptoms23. A Chinese population-based survey showed that low MHL was associated with both recent depression and anxiety, and that individuals with co-occurring depression and anxiety had the highest proportion of low MHL24. These studies suggest that poorer SRH and chronic disease may affect MHL through illness experience, functional limitation, and health uncertainty, whereas insufficient MHL may increase the odds of anxiety, depression, and ADC. Therefore, it is necessary to directly examine the relationships among SRH, chronic disease status, MHL, anxiety, depression, and ADC, and to test the mediating role of MHL between physical health and psychological outcomes.

As a municipality directly under the central government in western China, Chongqing has natural and socioeconomic characteristics involving urban, rural, and mountainous areas, and is characterized by the coexistence of urban and rural structures, marked regional development disparities, and accelerated population aging25. These characteristics are similar to the public health realities of rapid urbanization, population aging, and urban–rural health disparities in China, and may also lead to overlapping chronic disease burden, health service accessibility challenges, and mental health risks26. Therefore, Chongqing may provide a certain representative reference for exploring the associations of SRH and chronic disease status with mental health outcomes among Chinese residents. Previous studies have explored the associations of SRH, chronic disease status, and MHL with psychological distress, including anxiety and depression9–14,21–24, but several limitations remain. First, most existing studies have examined anxiety or depression separately, and fewer have included ADC as an independent outcome, making it difficult to reveal the risk characteristics of the comorbid state15,16. Second, previous studies have mostly focused on the effects of SRH, chronic disease, or MHL on mental health, while fewer have directly tested the mediating role of MHL21–24. Third, large-scale multistage sampling evidence from major cities in China and areas with both urban and rural populations remains limited, which is still insufficient to reveal current population mental health outcomes and related mechanisms25–27. Based on this, this study proposed the following hypotheses: first, poorer SRH and chronic disease are associated with increased odds of anxiety, depression, and ADC; second, higher MHL has a protective role in the above mental health outcomes; and third, MHL mediates the associations of SRH and chronic disease status with anxiety, depression, and ADC. Based on the resident mental health promotion survey task under the Healthy China Action framework, this study conducted a large-scale residents’ mental health survey in Chongqing and comprehensively applied multivariable analysis, covariate-adjusted regression analysis, and mediation effect models to systematically evaluate the effects and effect sizes of SRH and chronic disease status on multiple forms of psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, and ADC, and to test the mediating effect of MHL, thereby providing evidence for identifying psychological distress and promoting integrated prevention and control of physical and mental health.

Methods

Study design

The study sample was derived from a survey of residents’ mental health conducted in Chongqing, China, from October to December 2025. This survey implemented sampling across all districts and counties of Chongqing, a municipality directly under the central government in western China. A stratified multistage random sampling method was used to select permanent residents as study participants. According to the sample size estimation method for cross-sectional surveys, the sample size was calculated using the prevalence estimation formula n = Z2α/2p(1 − p)/d228,29. In this study, p = 0.50 was used to obtain a relatively conservative sample size, with an allowable error of d = 0.01, yielding an initial estimated sample size of approximately 9,604 participants. After considering the design effect of multistage sampling (design effect = 2.0), the minimum sample size was approximately 19,208 participants. Further considering an invalid questionnaire or refusal rate of approximately 10%, the minimum required sample size was approximately 21,342 participants. Considering the multistage sampling framework of district/county–townships (subdistricts)–village (community)–household in Chongqing and the balance of survey samples across different levels, this study finally planned to survey 34,440 participants. The specific sampling procedure was as follows:First, based on the list of districts and counties from the Chongqing Statistical Yearbook30, all townships (subdistricts) within each district or county were numbered, and five townships (subdistricts) were randomly selected from each district or county. Second, all villages (communities) within the selected townships (subdistricts) were numbered, and three villages (communities) were randomly selected from each township (subdistrict). Third, 56 households were randomly selected from each selected village (community). Finally, within each selected household, one permanent resident was chosen as the survey respondent using the Kish Selection Table Method, and the survey continued until at least 56 completed questionnaires were obtained. The exclusion criteria were: (I) individuals with severe physical diseases; (II) residents with impaired cognitive function who were unable to complete the survey; and (III) individuals who refused to participate in the survey.

Data collection

Data were collected through a structured electronic questionnaire. The questionnaire login module included the settings of “login ID” and “name” to ensure valid questionnaire completion and to prevent duplicate submissions. Prior to the survey, staff members from the Public Health Management Office of the Chongqing Mental Health Center provided unified training to personnel involved in the survey, including staff from the Health Commission, mental health institutions, health education personnel, and township (subdistrict) and village (community) staff. The training content included the survey background, survey plan, contents and administration methods of the survey instruments, and survey precautions. During the on-site survey phase, trained investigators assisted participants in scanning a QR code or accessing a survey link on their mobile phones to complete the electronic questionnaire for the residents’ mental health survey. For participants who were unable to use mobile devices or had difficulty completing the questionnaire independently, investigators read the original questions verbatim and recorded the responses using the investigators’ mobile devices. The planned sample size for this survey was 34,440 participants. A total of 37,930 residents participated in this survey, meeting the estimated sample size requirement. Referring to previous community surveys and population-based mental health survey studies31,32, and considering that the questionnaire involved demographic information, health status, and scale items, with incomplete consistency in the type and extent of missing information, no imputation was performed for missing information in this study. In addition, only 1,056 questionnaires had incomplete information, logical errors, or low-quality responses, and the effective questionnaire rate reached 97.21%, indicating an overall high validity rate. Therefore, only questionnaires with complete responses were included in the analysis. After excluding questionnaires with incomplete information, logical errors, or low-quality responses, 36,874 valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis, yielding an effective response rate of 97.21%.

Measurements

Demographic characteristics and covariates

The demographic characteristics included address, gender, residence, age, education level, occupation, marital status, monthly personal income (Chinese Yuan, CNY), monthly household income (CNY), smoking, alcohol use, SRH, and chronic disease status. Age was categorized into 0–24, 25–39, 40–54, 55–64, and ≥ 65 years. Education level was categorized as Illiterate or semi-literate, Primary school, Junior middle school, High school/vocational school, and College/university or above. Occupation was categorized as Farmer/worker, Government employee/technical professional, Commercial/service worker, Retired, and Other. Marital status was categorized as Unmarried, Married, and Divorced/widowed/other. Monthly personal income (CNY) was categorized as 0–1499, 1500–2999, 3000–4999, 5000–7999, and ≥ 8000. Monthly household income (CNY) was categorized as 0–2999, 3000–5999, 6000–9999, 10,000–19,999, and ≥ 20,000. Smoking was defined as smoking ≥ 1 cigarette per day for at least 6 months and was categorized as No and Yes. Alcohol use was defined as drinking ≥ 1 time per week for at least 6 months and was categorized as No and Yes33. Considering the need to effectively explore the mental health risks of anxiety, depression, and ADC in the whole population, and considering that although the prevalence of chronic diseases among adolescents and young adults is lower than that among middle-aged and older adults, some chronic diseases and metabolic risk factors are formed or become apparent during adolescence and young adulthood, and these groups may be exposed to chronic disease risk factors such as unhealthy diet, insufficient physical activity, tobacco smoke, and alcohol use, adolescents and young adults were also included in the analysis in this study34.

SRH and chronic disease status

In the National Mental Health Literacy Questionnaire (NMHLQ), SRH was used to evaluate residents’ self-rated health status through the question, “In the past year, how would you rate your health status?”35. Referring to previous studies and relevant community surveys on the classification of SRH, this study categorized SRH into two groups: good (good, relatively good) and fair or poor (average, relatively poor, poor)36. This classification was mainly based on the focus of this study on differences in mental health odds between relatively good health evaluation and impaired or uncertain health status, while also reducing the influence of uneven sample distribution in multiple categories. The chronic diseases included in the NMHLQ comprised 11 categories: hypertension, diabetes, gastroenteritis, rheumatoid arthritis, cerebrovascular diseases (such as stroke, cerebral infarction, cerebral thrombosis, etc.), intervertebral disc disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, ischemic heart disease, cholelithiasis/cholecystitis, peptic ulcer, and malignant tumor. An “other” option was also provided for participants to report additional chronic disease types. Participants who selected or reported any chronic disease and had obtained a physician’s diagnosis were classified as “Yes” for chronic disease; otherwise, they were classified as “No”37.

MHL

MHL was assessed using the NMHLQ. This questionnaire was developed by the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, based on the economic conditions, living status, and mental health characteristics of Chinese residents, and it serves as the primary survey instrument for the Mental Health Promotion Action uniformly regulated by the Office of the Healthy China Action Promotion Committee38. The NMHLQ has good psychometric performance, with a Cronbach’s coefficient of 0.870 and a Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin coefficient of 0.891, indicating good internal consistency and structural validity. The questionnaire can evaluate residents’ MHL from the perspectives of mental health knowledge, mental health attitudes and behaviors, recognition of common mental health problems, and coping abilities21. The NMHLQ consists of three sections: true/false questions, self-assessment items, and case analysis questions, comprising a total of 44 items. The mental health knowledge judgment section includes 20 items, each with three response options (true, false, and do not know). A correct response receives 5 points, whereas an incorrect response or “do not know” receives 0 points, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 100. The self-assessment section includes 8 items, each scored on a scale of 1–4 points, with a total score ranging from 8 to 32, evaluating individual mental health behaviors and attitudes. The case analysis section includes 16 items that assess residents’ recognition of and response to common mental disorders, with a total score ranging from 0 to 40. To be classified as having adequate MHL, each participant was required to simultaneously meet the following three criteria: (I) total score of the judgment section ≥ 80; (II) total score of the self-assessment section ≥ 24; and (III) total score of the case analysis section ≥ 2838.

Anxiety and depression

Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7). The scale includes seven items covering symptoms such as nervousness, inability to control worry, and somatic discomfort. Each item is rated on a 0–3 Likert scale, with a total score ranging from 0 to 21. A total GAD-7 score ≥ 10 was defined as a positive screening result for anxiety39. This cutoff has been validated in multiple studies and is widely used in clinical screening and epidemiological investigations. In this study, the internal consistency of the scale was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.842).

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). This scale includes nine items covering core depressive symptoms such as depressed mood, loss of interest, sleep disturbance, and fatigue. Each item is scored on a 0–3 Likert scale, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 27. A total PHQ-9 score ≥ 10 was defined as a positive screening result for depression40. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity in Chinese populations and is suitable for screening in primary care and community settings. In this study, the internal consistency of the scale was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.837).

ADC was defined as the simultaneous presence of a positive screening result for anxiety on the GAD-7 and a positive screening result for depression on the PHQ-941.

Statistical analysis

Electronic questionnaire data were organized and cleaned using Excel 2019. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0, and the web-based platform SPSSAU (https://spssau.com/index.html), and R software version 4.2.2. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted for participants’ demographic characteristics and MHL status. The chi-square test was used to analyze differences in demographic characteristics among residents who screened positive for anxiety, depression, and ADC. Variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis model to analyze the associated factors and odds estimates for the three outcome patterns of anxiety, depression, and ADC. The results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To assess multicollinearity among variables in the regression models, considering that the models included multicategorical variables such as age, education level, occupation, marital status, monthly personal income, and monthly household income, the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF) was used for diagnosis in this study. A GVIF < 5 is generally considered to indicate no obvious multicollinearity42. Covariate-adjusted multivariable regression analysis was constructed to analyze the associations of SRH and chronic disease status with anxiety, depression, and ADC. Model 1 was a crude model without covariate adjustments. Model 2 adjusted for relevant covariates, including gender, residence, age group, education level, occupation, marital status, monthly personal income (CNY), monthly household income (CNY), alcohol use, and smoking. Model 3 adjusted for gender, residence, age group, education level, occupation, marital status, monthly personal income (CNY), monthly household income (CNY), alcohol use, smoking, and MHL. To further examine the robustness of the associations of SRH, chronic disease status, and MHL with anxiety, depression, and ADC, subgroup analyses were conducted for variables including gender, residence, age group, education level, occupation, marital status, monthly personal income (CNY), monthly household income (CNY), alcohol use, smoking, and MHL. In the sensitivity analysis, monthly personal income was regrouped into three categories: 0–2999 CNY, 3000–4999 CNY, and ≥ 5000 CNY; SRH was reclassified from a binary variable into three categories: good, fair, and poor; and chronic disease was further refined from the binary classification of “yes/no” into specific disease types, to examine the robustness of the results after adjusting the grouping methods of the variables. R software version 4.2.2 was used for covariate adjustment in the models and for forest plot generation.

Mediation analysis was used to test the mediating effect of MHL in the associations of SRH and chronic disease status with anxiety, depression, and ADC after controlling for relevant demographic characteristics and other confounding factors (Fig. 1). The PROCESS macro program (Model 4) was used to conduct Bootstrap mediation effect tests with 5,000 resamples and to calculate 95% confidence intervals. The mediation effect pathways were decomposed into the direct effect (exposure → outcome; mediator → outcome), indirect effect (exposure → mediator → outcome), and total effect (the sum of the direct and indirect effects). The B values and 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (BCI) of multiple mediation models and their pathways were calculated to examine the effect sizes among related factors, and the proportion mediated was calculated using Bootstrap. Before conducting mediation analysis and constructing mediation effect models, Harman’s single-factor test was applied to all variables except individual characteristics to assess common method bias. The results showed that four factors had eigenvalues greater than 1, and the variance explained by the first unrotated factor was 27.53%, which was below the 40% testing criterion, suggesting that the influence of common method bias on the results of this study was relatively limited43. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05.

Results

The demographic characteristics of the participants

Among the 36,874 participants included in the study, the age of residents ranged from 12 to 98 years, with a mean age of 55.87 ± 14.98 years, including 631 adolescents and young adults. The proportions of male and female participants were 51.39% and 48.61%, respectively. The proportions of residents living in Urban and Rural areas were 28.56% and 71.44%, respectively. A total of 55.20% of participants had an education level of Junior middle school or above. Residents with SRH classified as fair/poor accounted for 23.53% (8678/36,874), whereas those with SRH classified as Good accounted for 76.47% (28,196/36,874). The proportions of residents without and with chronic disease were 64.55% (23,804/36,874) and 35.45% (13,070/36,874), respectively. Residents with inadequate MHL accounted for 67.79% (24,998/36,874), whereas those with adequate MHL accounted for 32.21% (11,876/36,874). Regarding mental health outcomes, the positive screening rates for anxiety, depression, and ADC were 2.72% (1004/36,874), 4.53% (1672/36,874), and 2.11% (777/36,874), respectively. Except for alcohol use and smoking, significant differences were observed in the demographic characteristics associated with positive screening results for anxiety, depression, and ADC across gender, residence, age group, education level, occupation, marital status, monthly personal income (CNY), monthly household income (CNY), SRH, chronic disease, and MHL (gender P < 0.05;monthly personal income P < 0.01; all other variables P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Analysis of influencing factors for anxiety, depression, and ADC

Variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariable analysis. The results of influencing factors shown in Fig. 2 indicated that rural residents had higher odds of anxiety, depression, and ADC. With increasing age, the odds of anxiety, depression, and ADC decreased among residents. Compared with the illiterate or semi-literate group, primary school, junior middle school, high school/vocational school, and college/university or above were associated with lower odds of anxiety, depression, and ADC. Compared with farmers or workers, commercial or service workers had lower odds of anxiety and ADC, and retired residents also had lower odds. Compared with the (monthly personal income, CNY) group of 0–1499, the group with income ≥ 8000 had higher odds, whereas the results for monthly household income were not statistically significant. Residents with fair or poor SRH, chronic disease, and inadequate MHL had higher odds of anxiety, depression, and ADC (P < 0.005). The GVIF of each variable was calculated to assess multicollinearity among variables (Supplementary Tables 1–3). The GVIF values ranged from 1.02 to 3.39, which were below the threshold of 5, indicating that there was no significant multicollinearity among variables in the multivariable regression models.

Covariate adjusted logistic regression analysis

To examine the effects of SRH and chronic disease status on anxiety, depression, and ADC, logistic regression models adjusted for covariates were constructed (Table 2, Fig. 3). The results of Model 1 without covariate adjustment showed that residents with fair or poor SRH and chronic disease had higher odds of anxiety, depression, and ADC (P < 0.001). After adjustment for relevant variables, the results of Model 2 and Model 3 showed that residents with fair or poor SRH and chronic disease still had higher odds of anxiety, depression, and ADC (P < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis of influencing factors for anxiety, depression, and ADC

To further examine the robustness of the effect levels across different subgroups, we adjusted for the covariates in Model 3 and conducted stratified analyses according to participant characteristics. Consistent with the results of the influencing factor analysis and covariate-adjusted logistic regression analysis, fair or poor SRH and chronic disease were associated factors for higher odds of anxiety, depression, and ADC. MHL was a protective factor for anxiety, depression, and ADC. Except for a few stratifications, such as the age group of 0–24 years and the (monthly personal income, CNY) group of ≥ 8000, the results were statistically significant in the remaining groups (Figs. 4, 5, 6).

Sensitivity analysis

The results of the sensitivity analysis showed that, after regrouping monthly personal income, the groups of 3000–4999 CNY and ≥ 5000 CNY showed no significant associations with the three types of psychological outcomes. After SRH was classified into three categories, compared with good SRH, fair SRH and poor SRH were associated with higher odds of anxiety, depression, and ADC. Among specific chronic disease types, some chronic disease types may have had no statistical significance because of the small number of stratified variables, but most diseases showed higher odds. These results suggest that the associations of SRH and chronic disease with mental health odds were relatively robust, and that the variable classification methods did not have a significant influence on the stability of the results (Supplementary Table 6).

Mediation analysis

The Bootstrap resampling method was applied to test the mediation effects. After controlling for gender, residence, age group, education level, occupation, marital status, monthly personal income (CNY), monthly household income (CNY), alcohol use, smoking, and other variables, multiple mediation models were constructed with SRH and chronic disease status as independent variables; anxiety, depression, and ADC as dependent variables; and MHL as the mediator. A total of 5,000 resamples were generated (Table 3). The statistical results of the mediation effects showed that SRH was positively associated with anxiety (B = 1.0780, 95% BCI: 0.9512–1.2049), depression (B = 1.2698, 95% BCI: 1.1699 –1.3696), and ADC (B = 1.1428, 95% BCI: 0.9994–1.2862). Chronic disease status was positively associated with anxiety (B = 0.5287, 95% BCI: 0.4027–0.6547), depression (B = 0.6330, 95% BCI: 0.5341–0.7318), and ADC (B = 0.5321, 95% BCI: 0.3894 ~ 0.6747). MHL was negatively associated with anxiety, depression, and ADC across different model pathways. Within each mediation model pathway, SRH was negatively associated with MHL (B = − 0.4724, 95% BCI: − 0.5270 to − 0.4178), and chronic disease status was negatively associated with MHL (B = − 0.2331, 95% BCI: − 0.2794 to − 0.1867) (all P < 0.001).

Discussion

This study was conducted in southwest China, under the framework of the “Mental Health Promotion Action” of the Healthy China Action (2019–2030) and implemented a large-scale stratified multistage population survey. We analyzed residents’ SRH, chronic disease status, MHL, and their associations with anxiety, depression, and ADC. The results showed that the positive screening rates for anxiety (GAD-7 ≥ 10), depression (PHQ-9 ≥ 10), and ADC among residents in Chongqing, Southwest China, were 2.72% (1004/36,874), 4.53% (1672/36,874), and 2.11% (777/36,874), respectively. Relevant studies in China and other countries have also reported the prevalence of anxiety, depression, and ADC in populations. A study by He et al. based on a nationally representative sample of older adults in China showed that the weighted prevalence rates of anxiety symptoms and depressive symptoms were 1.39% and 2.79%, respectively44. A systematic analysis showed that the prevalence of depression in the general population was 11.02%45. The European Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) cohort study showed that the prevalence of depressive symptoms among people aged over 50 years was mostly in the range of 15%–30%46. In this study, the detection rate of ADC was 2.11%, which was lower than the reported prevalence of comorbidity in Southwest China47 and Europe16. The relatively low positive screening rate in this study may be related to the following factors. First, the problems described by the PHQ-9/GAD-7 use frequency to describe anxiety and depressive manifestations that residents may have experienced in the past two weeks, and the daily mental health burden of some residents may not have reached the criterion for positive screening, with limited sensitivity to mild psychological distress48. Second, based on social expectations and concerns about exposing psychological problems, respondents may have tended to choose relatively positive or mild options, leading to underreporting of psychological symptoms49,50. Third, the population size investigated in this study was relatively large. Unlike the higher positive rates observed in high-risk populations and small-scale surveys, the reported positive rate in large-scale surveys of general community residents may be relatively low51.

The analysis of influencing factors for anxiety, depression, and ADC showed differences in mental health outcomes across sociodemographic factors, including residence, education level, occupation, and age. The positive screening rate among rural residents was higher than that among urban residents, which was consistent with the results of related studies52. This study observed a significant decreasing trend in the odds of anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity with increasing education level, and this “education effect” has also been repeatedly confirmed in previous studies53. In addition, with increasing age, the odds of anxiety, depression, and ADC decreased among residents, which was consistent with the lower odds observed among retired individuals. This may be because older adults have less life pressure and lower work burden54, but it may also be related to the low proportion of older adults and retired individuals in this study. However, it should also be noted that some studies on middle-aged and older adults have suggested that the burden of chronic diseases and multimorbidity, functional limitation, social isolation, and cognitive decline in old age may increase the risk of depression or psychological distress55,56. Rural residence, lower education level, and being farmers or workers were associated with higher mental health odds, which may be because these groups have long faced heavy physical burden, insufficient security, and overlapping risks of chronic diseases, while education level and occupation also limit individuals’ ability to obtain, understand, and apply health information27. This suggests that for rural residents and manual workers, mental health services should be strengthened at the grassroots level, the accessibility of medical and psychological service resources should be improved, and the publicity and education of mental health knowledge should be promoted. Previous studies have shown that higher income levels are associated with lower mental health risk. Ettman et al., based on a survey of adults in the United States, found that people with low income and insufficient economic reserves were more likely to have depressive symptoms, suggesting that insufficient economic resources may increase mental health vulnerability57. A population-based survey in China also showed that household income level was associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms, and people with low income or insufficient economic resources had higher mental health risks58. In this study, although the influencing factor analysis showed that the group with monthly personal income above 8000 (CNY) had higher odds of anxiety, depression, and ADC, the results for monthly household income were not statistically significant, and monthly personal income was not statistically significant after adjustment of income grouping, indicating that no significant association between monthly personal income and mental health odds was observed in this study.

This study comprehensively used multivariable analysis, covariate-adjusted regression analysis, subgroup analysis, and sensitivity analysis to systematically evaluate the relationships of SRH, chronic disease status, and MHL with anxiety, depression, and ADC. The results showed that fair or poor SRH and chronic disease were both associated with increased odds of the three psychological outcomes, whereas MHL showed a stable protective role. The results of covariate-adjusted logistic regression showed that, whether in the crude model or after adjustment for covariates, the associations between SRH and anxiety, depression, and comorbidity remained stable. Subgroup analysis showed that, except for a few stratifications, poorer SRH was a factor associated with psychological distress across different populations. This is consistent with the results of previous studies, which showed that SRH was associated with mental health risk59–61. A study of older adults in China also found that SRH had direct or indirect associations with depressive symptoms62. This may be related to the fact that individuals with poorer SRH are more likely to develop negative health perceptions, such as excessive attention to physical discomfort, concerns about disease progression, and uncertainty about future health, and these cognitive characteristics may easily activate persistent psychological stress responses63.

In terms of chronic disease burden, similar to the results for SRH, chronic disease patients showed positive associations with anxiety, depression, and comorbidity. Related studies have shown that chronic diseases and multimorbidity are significantly associated with depressive symptoms accompanied by anxiety or comorbidity. A study in Southwest China also suggested that chronic disease and poorer SRH were associated with the risk of ADC, indicating that “SRH–chronic disease–stress–psychological comorbidity” may form overlapping effects47. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Read et al. showed that multimorbidity was significantly associated with depression64. A Chinese cohort study also showed that multiple non-communicable diseases had greater effects on the risks of stress, anxiety, and depression than a single disease, and psychological symptoms may further increase as the number of diseases increases65. Meanwhile, in this study, the association between chronic disease and ADC remained significant after covariate adjustment, subgroup analysis, and sensitivity analysis. This suggests certain public health significance. In the future, PHQ-9/GAD-7 or brief scale screening for residents’ mental health problems may be incorporated into chronic disease follow-up and primary health management for hypertension, diabetes, and other chronic diseases, so as to carry out early psychological identification and intervention at the primary care level, reduce psychological risk, and improve health benefits66,67.

The results of this study showed that MHL was negatively associated with anxiety, depression, and ADC, and that its protective role for anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity remained stable in most subgroups. This result is consistent with findings from recent studies in China and other countries. A study of Chinese students showed that MHL level was negatively associated with anxiety and depression, and the associations were statistically significant in both male and female student stratifications68. A study among nurses during the COVID-19 pandemic also suggested that mental health promotion strategies helped reduce psychological problems such as anxiety, stress, and depression69. Furthermore, by constructing a series of mediation effect models, this study quantified the proportions of the mediating effect of MHL in the associations of SRH and chronic disease with the three psychological disorders. The results showed that, whether for anxiety, depression, or their comorbidity, MHL accounted for a certain mediating proportion in the related mental health outcomes. Previous studies have shown that individuals with higher MHL are more likely to recognize problems, actively seek support and professional help, and develop relatively healthy lifestyles when facing physical discomfort or psychological distress70. A study by Guo et al. based on a sample of older adults in Xi’an showed that health literacy played a mediating role between chronic disease and psychological distress, suggesting that improving literacy may help reduce the psychological burden related to chronic disease71. Related studies have also shown that health literacy may serve as a mediator in the pathway through which socioeconomic status affects health outcomes72. In addition, related studies have shown that health literacy may play a moderating role in the relationship among health anxiety, emotional regulation, and cyberchondria73. These pieces of evidence suggest that MHL may not only be directly associated with lower psychological distress, but may also partially explain the link between physical health burden and psychological distress by improving disease cognition, health information comprehension, help-seeking behavior, and coping ability. In the future, residents’ MHL may be improved through community health education, media communication, and training of primary healthcare workers. Targeted MHL interventions may also be carried out for middle-aged and older adults, patients with chronic diseases, and people with poor SRH, and health education may be used to improve adherence to chronic disease self-management and health awareness, thereby improving physical health status and reducing psychological distress.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, as part of the national initiative for residents’ mental health promotion, a large-scale stratified multistage population survey was conducted. Standardized instruments were used, and stepwise sampling was implemented according to administrative divisions, which ensured the standardization and coverage of the sampling and survey process and could relatively effectively reflect the status and level of mental health-related factors among regional residents. Second, this study analyzed anxiety, depression, and ADC as psychological distress outcomes, and simultaneously explored the associations of SRH, chronic disease status, and MHL with these multiple psychological outcomes, as well as the mediating role of MHL, which may provide certain evidence for addressing the current insufficiency of integrated physical and mental health research. Third, this study comprehensively applied covariate adjusted regression analysis, subgroup analysis, and mediation analysis. Covariates were adjusted stepwise, and multiple subgroup analyses were performed. In addition, multiple mediation models incorporating different exposure and outcome variables were constructed, which relatively improved the precision and robustness of the findings.

This study has some limitations. First, this survey was led by the health administrative department, and residents may have tended to choose positive answers, which may have led to a certain degree of underestimation of population health risks. Second, SRH, chronic disease, and psychological symptoms were all based on self-reported data and self-rating scales, which may involve concealment of psychological symptoms, inaccurate reporting of chronic diseases, recall bias, and information bias. Future studies may combine electronic health records and clinical diagnostic data for validation. Third, the determination of SRH and chronic disease was based on a small number of items, and more detailed stratification was not conducted for chronic disease types, number, and severity; therefore, the precision of interactions among variables and the effect levels need to be further refined. Fourth, this study was a cross-sectional survey. Although multiple analytical methods, covariate adjustment, and robustness tests were used, the temporal sequence among SRH, chronic disease, MHL, and psychological outcomes could not be determined, and the mediation results should be interpreted as statistical mediation rather than causal mediation. Fifth, the mediating proportion of MHL was approximately 7%–8%, suggesting that its indirect effect was relatively limited, and its transmission role between SRH, chronic disease, and psychological outcomes should not be overinterpreted. In addition, this study did not fully include factors such as life events, sleep quality, physical activity, and healthcare service utilization, and therefore could not further measure and adjust for the effects of related potential factors. Future studies may further verify the related causal relationships through longitudinal cohort studies or intervention studies.

Conclusions

This study conducted a large-scale stratified multistage community resident survey in Chongqing, Southwest China, and found that poorer SRH and chronic disease were associated with anxiety, depression, and ADC in the surveyed area, suggesting that subjective health evaluation and chronic disease burden are associated with the identification of community psychological distress. MHL was stably negatively associated with the three psychological outcomes and played a partial statistical mediating role between SRH, chronic disease status, and psychological outcomes, indicating that it may be an important factor in the association between physical and mental health. These results suggest that primary public health services should promote the coordinated integration of chronic disease management and mental health services, embed anxiety and depression screening and MHL improvement into chronic disease follow-up, elderly health management, and community health education, and strengthen the popularization of mental health knowledge, so as to improve the accessibility and comprehensive benefits of community mental health promotion.
