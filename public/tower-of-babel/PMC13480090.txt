The relationship between social media use, quality of life, and clinical symptoms in schizophrenia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tulay Sati Kirkan, Ece Buyuksandalyaci Tunc, Gamze Demiray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to determine the effect of social media use in schizophrenia patients as well as to compare their quality of life and clinical progress.MethodsA total of 222 schizophrenia patients who have applied to the psychiatry outpatient clinic of our institution have been enrolled in this research. The patients were divided into two groups based on social media usage status and required to fill out the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), Clinical Global Impression-Severity Scale (CGI-S), Social Media Use Disorder Scale (SMDS), Functional Assessment Short Test (FAST) and 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36).ResultsThe age of the patients using social media was significantly lower than the group not using social media (p < 0.05). Social media use was higher in the male sex (p < 0.05). The level of education and income in the group using social media was significantly higher than those without a social media account (p < 0.05). Among social media users (n = 78), the mean SMDS total score was 8.6 ± 13.6, indicating that most users did not meet criteria for social media use disorder, although a subset demonstrated elevated scores suggestive of problematic use. It has been determined that individuals who use social media have lower scores on both the FAST and PANSS (particularly negative symptoms and general psychopathology). The SF – 36 pain, vitality, and mental health scores were not significantly different in groups using and not using social media. The SF – 36 physical function, physical role, general health, social function, and emotional role score in the group using social media was significantly higher (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe results of this study indicate that social media use among individuals with schizophrenia is associated with lower symptom severity (particularly negative symptoms), better functional capacity, and enhanced quality of life, as measured by the PANSS, FAST, and SF-36. Logistic regression analysis confirmed that younger age, male sex, higher education, and lower functional impairment were independently associated with social media use. These findings suggest that social media may serve as a valuable adjunct to conventional psychosocial interventions; however, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and longitudinal studies are warranted.

Introduction

There has been a global increase in the use of social media in the last two decades. According to published articles, the rate of social media use is significantly higher in young people aged between 18 and 29 compared to older individuals. It is reported that the average daily social media use is 2.6 h [1].

Numerous studies reported loss of excessive daily time spent on social media, and the use of multiple social media platforms was associated with both depression and anxiety. One of the other downsides of social media is reducing human interaction and face-to-face communication. Hence, negative online experiences and crowded information flow cause distraction. It is reported that social media use is higher in people with higher education levels, less depressive symptoms, and more social interaction [2]. However, these findings primarily reflect outcomes in the general population. For individuals with schizophrenia, whose core symptoms include social withdrawal and reduced motivation for interpersonal engagement, the role of social media may be fundamentally different [3, 4].

Decreased interest and desire to form close relationships with others is a crucial feature of schizophrenia. In addition, these individuals lack pleasure in any activity (anhedonia). Therefore, social interactions will likely be less enjoyable or rewarding [3]. Additionally, if the negative symptoms dominate the patient, the situation may be more complex.

Contrary to popular belief, it is thought that social media may have more benefits than harm for patients with schizophrenia. Social media aids this population by providing a pleasant and less worrying atmosphere. This could explain why patients with schizophrenia use social media to interact socially [4]. Approximately 33%-71% of schizophrenia patients use the internet to express their knowledge and preferences, write blog entries, establish friends, evaluate their symptoms, and seek help [1]. The usage of social media is vital for those with psychiatric problems. This is especially crucial for young people, given the expansion of social media use and the significant frequency of mental problems within this age group [5]. These platforms help them interact with their friends and socialize with other people [4].

Demographic or clinical variables linked to social media use in schizophrenia, however, are not well supported by data. The ability to communicate ideas and feelings and the availability of social support are two advantages of social media use in relation to mental health. A link with psychological stress, anxiety, and depression is also a significant drawback [6].

Online searches for mental health data are also common among those who have psychotic disorders [5, 7]. The general public accesses information similarly to this [8], and there are parallels in how people use the internet in different contexts, such as when evaluating its benefits and drawbacks [9]. People who suffer from psychotic disorders are also likely to use social media, hoping to build new connections, keep up with ones they already have, and get online support from their peers [10]. The primary obstacles to internet use for those suffering from severe mental illnesses are the costs associated with it, as well as cognitive challenges and skill deficiencies [11].

Social media platforms can reach large numbers of people who are generally difficult to engage with, such as people diagnosed with schizophrenia, allow others to intervene on issues that are difficult to express face to face, including suicidal ideation, and provide an anonymous, accessible and non-judgmental forum for sharing experiences. People with stigmatizing conditions—especially those with psychiatric illnesses—are more likely to utilize the internet for health-related purposes due to its accessibility, confidentiality, and informal nature [12].

From a public health perspective, social media use is considered not merely an individual behavior but an important social determinant influencing mental health. Particularly among vulnerable populations—such as individuals with schizophrenia—social media is regarded as a potential public health tool for the promotion and protection of mental health, the reduction of social exclusion, and the dissemination of digital-based interventions [13, 14].

This study compared the quality of life and clinical history of patients with schizophrenia to shed light on the impact of social media use.

Materials & method

A total of 222 patients who have applied to the psychiatry outpatient clinic of our institution between December 2020 and June 2021, and who were diagnosed with schizophrenia according to DSM-V criteria (American Psychiatric Association 2013) were included in the study. The ethics committee approval was granted on 24/01/2020 with protocol number 80,576,354–050 – 99/322. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed written consent was obtained from all participants or their guardians.

Each psychiatrist examined all individuals and carefully reviewed the eligibility criteria. Patients selected to participate in the study were aged 18–65, literate themselves or through their legal guardian, without neurological disease or mental retardation, and without alcohol or substance abuse. Attention was paid to ensuring that these patients had stable treatment in the last six months and had not received electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) in the last six months, as this could affect social media use due to neurocognitive side effects.

The study defined two groups: social media users (n = 78) and patients without social media accounts (n = 144). There was no difference between the groups in terms of intervention. No laboratory tests were requested from the patients. Interviewers administered a sociodemographic data form, the PANSS (Positive and Negative Symptom Scale), the Social Media Use Disorder Scale (SMDS), the Functional Assessment Short Test (FAST), and the 36-Item Short Form Questionnaire (SF-36) to all patients.

Measures

Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)

Symptom severity was assessed using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), originally developed by Kay et al. [15]. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Kostakoğlu et al. [16].

PANSS is a semi-structured interview scale consisting of 30 items and it offers three rationally derived categories: positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and general symptoms. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (absent) to 7 (extreme). The total score ranges from 30 to 210, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. In the Turkish adaptation study, the internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) and inter-rater reliability of the scale were found to be high, demonstrating its suitability for the Turkish clinical population.

Functional Assessment Short Test (FAST)

To assess the functioning levels of the participants, the Functioning Assessment Short Test (FAST), originally developed by Rosa et al. was used [17]. The Turkish adaptation, validity, and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Aydemir and Uykur [18].

The FAST is a clinician-administered semi-structured interview scale consisting of 24 items. Each item is scored on a scale from 0 to 3 (0 = No difficulty, 1 = Mild difficulty, 2 = Moderate difficulty, 3 = Severe difficulty). The total score of the scale ranges from 0 to 72. The scoring of the scale’s subscales is as follows: questions 1–4 for autonomy, questions 5–9 for occupational functioning, questions 10–14 for cognitive functioning, questions 15,16 for financial matters, interpersonal relationships from 17 to 22, while leisure activities consist of questions 23 and 24 and are scored accordingly. Higher scores indicate lower functioning levels and greater functional impairment. In the Turkish adaptation study conducted by Aydemir and Uykur (2012), the internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach’s alpha) of the scale was found to be 0.96, and the test-retest reliability coefficient was 0.95.

Social Media Use Disorder Scale (SMDS)

Developed by van den Eijnden, Lemmens, and Valkenburg [19], this scale is a unidimensional instrument consisting of 9 items. It is based on the nine diagnostic criteria for Internet Gaming Disorder defined in the DSM-5. The scale was adapted into Turkish by Savcı and Aysan [20]. The Turkish version demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.90) and satisfactory construct validity, preserving the original single-factor structure.

36-Item Short-Form Survey (SF-36)

To evaluate the quality of life of the participants, the 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36), originally developed by Ware and Sherbourne [21], was used. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Koçyiğit et al. [22]. The SF-36 is a self-administered scale consisting of 36 items. Items are scored using a Likert-type scale. Sub-dimension scores range from 0 to 100, where 100 indicates the best possible health status and 0 indicates the worst health status. The scale does not provide a single total score; instead, each sub-dimension is evaluated individually.

In the study by Koçyiğit et al., the Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficients for the sub-dimensions were reported to range between 0.73 and 0.76, indicating that the scale is valid and reliable for the Turkish population.

Clinical Global Impression-Severity Scale (CGI-S)

The clinical severity of the patients was assessed using the Clinical Global Impression-Severity Scale (CGI-S), developed by Guy [23]. The CGI-S is a clinician-rated, single-item tool that evaluates the patient’s current symptom status on a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (normal, not at all ill) to 7 (among the most extremely ill), including intermediate levels of borderline [2], mild [3], moderate [4], marked [5], and severe [6] illness. The Turkish version has been validated for schizophrenia by Aydemir et al. [24].

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0, was used to analyze the data. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum, frequency, and percentage. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the distribution of continuous variables. Quantitative independent variables were analyzed using the independent samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Qualitative independent variables were analyzed using the chi-square test; when the assumptions of the chi-square test were not met, Fisher’s exact test was used. Relationships among continuous variables were evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis, as the assumption of normal distribution was not met. Correlation coefficients among the total scores of PANSS, FAST, and SMDS were calculated. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with social media use, with social media use as the dependent variable and age, sex, educational level, CGI-S score, PANSS total score, FAST total score, and SF-36 social functioning score entered as independent variables. The level of statistical significance was accepted as two-tailed p < 0.05.

The average age of the participants is 42.5 ± 11.1 (min.19-max.71; median:42.0). The age of the patients using social media was significantly lower than the group not using social media (p < 0.05). For the analyses of the FAST and PANSS scales used in the clinical evaluation of patients, the number of categories for certain independent variables—such as participants’ occupation, marital status, and living arrangements—was reduced.

Participants’ FAST scores showed significant differences according to some sociodemographic and clinical variables. No statistically significant differences in FAST scores were found with respect to gender, marital status, or history of suicide attempt (p > 0.05). In contrast, a statistically significant relationship was observed between educational level and FAST scores (p < 0.001). Bonferroni-corrected analyses indicated that FAST scores decreased significantly as educational level increased, and that all educational groups differed from one another. Living arrangement had a significant effect on FAST scores (p = 0.024). Participants living with their families had significantly lower scores compared to other groups (p = 0.004). Similarly, employment status was found to be associated with FAST scores (p < 0.001), with unemployed individuals exhibiting lower FAST scores. Smoking/alcohol use also showed a significant difference in FAST scores (p = 0.019), with higher FAST scores observed among individuals who did not use tobacco or alcohol. A significant relationship was found between CGI-S score and FAST (p < 0.001); individuals with more severe clinical status (CGI-S ≥ 4) had higher FAST scores. Social media use demonstrated one of the strongest associations with FAST scores (p < 0.001), with social media users showing lower FAST scores. Gender did not produce a significant difference in either PANSS total score or its subscales (p = 0.542). However, with respect to marital status, single individuals had significantly higher PANSS total scores than married individuals (p = 0.006). Educational level had a significant effect on both PANSS total score and all subscales (p < 0.001). According to Bonferroni-corrected comparisons (significance threshold p < 0.008), the PANSS total score of the literate only (no formal schooling; i.e., able to read and write but without formal educational attainment) group was significantly higher than those of primary school (p < 0.001), high school (p = 0.002), and university graduates (p < 0.001). Examination of subscales showed that university graduates had lower positive symptom scores than the literate only (no formal schooling) group (p = 0.001). For negative symptoms and general psychopathology subscales, the scores of primary school, high school, and university graduates were significantly lower than those of the literate only (no formal schooling) group. Similarly, employment status was significant with respect to PANSS scores (p = 0.003), with lower PANSS scores observed among unemployed individuals. A statistically significant relationship was found between CGI-S score and PANSS total and all subscale scores (all p < 0.001). Participants with mild/moderate clinical severity had significantly lower scores than those with severe clinical severity, supporting the clinical consistency of the scales. Smoking and alcohol use were particularly associated with the negative symptoms subscale (p = 0.029), with higher negative symptom scores observed in individuals who did not use tobacco or alcohol. Social media use showed significant differences in PANSS total score, negative symptoms, and general psychopathology subscales (p < 0.05). Participants who did not use social media had higher scores on these subscales and on the total PANSS score (Tables 4 and 5).

The SF – 36 pain, vitality, and mental health scores were not significantly different in groups using and not using social media. The SF – 36 physical functions, physical role, general health, social function, and emotional role score in the group using social media was significantly higher (p < 0.05) (Table 6).

To test the direction and strength of the relationships among PANSS, FAST, and SMDS total scores, Spearman correlation analysis was performed. A high-level, positive, and statistically significant correlation was found between PANSS total score and FAST total score (rₛ = 0.751; p < 0.001). This finding indicates that as the severity of psychopathology increases, impairment in functioning increases markedly.

A weak, negative, and statistically significant relationship was observed between PANSS total score and SMDS total score (rₛ = −0.166; p = 0.013), suggesting that higher psychopathology severity is associated with a tendency toward reduced levels of social media use.

The relationship between FAST total score and SMDS total score was moderate, negative, and statistically significant (rₛ = −0.355; p < 0.001). This indicates that as functional impairment increases, social media use decreases, and that functional capacity may be related to the level of engagement with social media.

Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with social media use (Table 7). Social media use was defined as the dependent variable, and age, sex, educational level, CGI-S score, PANSS total score, FAST total score, and SF-36 social functioning score were entered into the model. The overall model was statistically significant [χ² [8] = 101.226; p < 0.001].

In the regression analysis, male sex, college educational level, age, and FAST total score were independently associated with social media use. The odds of social media use were approximately 2.3 times higher in male patients than in female patients [OR = 2.318; 95% CI: 1.014–5.300; p = 0.046]. Patients with a college educational level had approximately 3.9 times higher odds of social media use compared with those who were literate only (no formal schooling) or had completed primary school [OR = 3.869; 95% CI: 1.329–11.267; p = 0.013]. Each one-year increase in age was associated with an approximately 9.3% decrease in the odds of social media use [OR = 0.907; 95% CI: 0.870–0.946; p < 0.001]. Similarly, each one-point increase in FAST total score was associated with an approximately 5.8% decrease in the odds of social media use [OR = 0.942; 95% CI: 0.908–0.977; p = 0.001]. CGI-S score, PANSS total score, and SF-36 social functioning score were not independently associated with social media use.

Discussion

There are many studies in the literature examining how social media use affects individuals [25, 26], and it is seen that these findings can have both positive and negative impacts. Some studies show that social media use positively affects individuals’ subjective well-being [27] and that perceived social support also develops positively because it ensures the continuity of communication between peers [28]. As mentioned before, it is thought that social media may have more benefits than harm for patients with schizophrenia. Social networking is helpful to this group because it offers a less intimidating and stressful atmosphere. This could be the cause of social media use and interaction by schizophrenia patients [4].

However, the potential risks of social media use in this population should not be overlooked. A recent systematic review published in BMC Psychiatry reported that social media use is disproportionately high among individuals with psychiatric disorders involving delusional thinking, and proposed a conceptual model termed ‘Delusion Amplification by Social Media’, suggesting that excessive platform use may contribute to a distorted sense of self and exacerbate psychotic symptoms [29]. Furthermore, the absence of temporal and physical cues in virtual interactions may be particularly challenging for individuals with impaired social cognition, as is common in schizophrenia. Therefore, while social media offers potential benefits, clinicians should remain vigilant regarding its role in symptom exacerbation, and patients should be monitored for online-related paranoid ideation or delusional content.

As age increases, extroversion tends to decrease, and older individuals generally show lower levels of neuroticism compared to younger individuals. Research consistently indicates that younger individuals use social media more frequently than older individuals and are more susceptible to problematic social media use. This pattern may not be solely driven by extroversion; rather, younger individuals may be motivated by a stronger need for social belonging, fear of missing out, and identity formation through digital platforms, all of which can sustain high levels of social media engagement regardless of personality traits [30]. This is particularly relevant for young patients with psychotic disorders, who are more likely to use social media to build new connections, maintain existing relationships, and seek peer support online [10].

In the current study, we have elaborated that higher income was associated with higher social media use in parallel with the general population. Schizophrenia is usually observed in people with lower socioeconomic status, and this may affect the individual’s access to the internet, smartphones and tablets [31].In this study, 83% of the population earned less than the monthly minimum wage. In this context, social media use may serve as a compensatory mechanism for patients who lack close familial bonds or an established social network. Individuals who are single, childless, or living without family support may turn to digital platforms to fulfill unmet social needs, forming virtual communities that partially substitute for absent interpersonal relationships. This compensatory function of social media is particularly relevant in schizophrenia, where social withdrawal and reduced motivation for face-to-face interaction are core features of the illness [32, 33].

The younger the schizophrenia patients were, the more social media usage they used. In addition, as the education level increases, social media use increases. This aligns with earlier research describing how younger age was linked to increased usage of social media and the internet in people with mental health disorders [34, 35]. Likewise, higher levels of education are linked to increased internet use, as well as among those who have schizophrenia [36]. These qualities aligned with the prevailing patterns in the broader community. Higher education makes sense in that it would help people use and comprehend the Internet and gadgets like computers and cellphones, which might lead to more people using social media [37].

Two findings in the present study warrant further contextual interpretation. First, unemployed individuals exhibited lower FAST scores, suggesting better functional capacity compared to employed patients. This seemingly counterintuitive finding may reflect a selection bias inherent to the outpatient setting: patients with more severe symptoms and greater functional impairment are less likely to maintain employment, whereas those who are unemployed despite having relatively preserved functioning may represent individuals who lost their jobs prior to illness onset or due to structural factors rather than clinical severity. This interpretation is consistent with the well-documented relationship between schizophrenia and structural unemployment in low-income populations [31]. Second, higher FAST scores — indicating greater functional impairment — were observed among individuals who did not use tobacco or alcohol. This finding may reflect the fact that patients with more severe negative symptoms, who already exhibit social withdrawal and reduced hedonic drive, are less likely to engage in substance use behaviors, while simultaneously showing greater functional impairment. Thus, the absence of substance use in this context may be a marker of more severe psychopathology rather than a reflection of healthier behavior [38].

Consistent with this compensatory perspective in the current study, we have found that married patients, individuals living with their families and people with children spend less time on social media than others. This is possibly due to family attachment and socialization. Patients with increased feelings of loneliness [39], low self-confidence perception [40], deterioration in sleep quality [41], problems in eating habits [42] and various adverse effects similar to these are prone to use social media more. They have the chance to meet individuals with similar problems, share feelings, and leverage the beneficial interphase of social media.

Webelhorst et al. (2020) stated that psychiatric patients used the internet more than other people for mental health-related purposes [43]. A high percentage of internet use for mental health-related difficulties was discovered in a recent study comparing the use of the internet in patients with schizophrenia and depression [44]. Social media can contribute to the development of interpersonal communication skills by providing a less stressful and controllable interaction space, especially for patients who have difficulty in face-to-face communication [8, 9]. In addition, online interactions with individuals who share similar experiences through social media can strengthen the sense of belonging and increase self-confidence and self-efficacy [7]. Indeed, some studies suggest that social media use can be considered as a factor that supports the social functioning of individuals with schizophrenia and may play a complementary role to clinical interventions [4, 10].

Overall, our study demonstrates a strong association between PANSS and FAST, whereas SMDS shows weaker to moderate inverse relationships with both psychopathology and functioning. This pattern suggests that clinical severity and functional level are reflected in social media use only indirectly and to a limited extent.

From a public health perspective, social media use represents a scalable and low-cost digital approach with the potential to mitigate social isolation and improve access to mental health–related resources among individuals with schizophrenia [14]. In the present study, individuals who reported using social media exhibited lower total PANSS and FAST scores, suggesting an association between social media engagement, reduced symptom severity, and better functional outcomes. These findings align with public health frameworks that recognize digital technologies as complementary tools to conventional mental health services, particularly by enhancing social connectedness, facilitating information exchange, and supporting participation in social interactions within less stigmatizing and more accessible environments. Consequently, social media platforms may contribute to improvements in quality of life at the population level when appropriately integrated into mental health care strategies. Nevertheless, public health approaches also emphasize the necessity of addressing potential risks, including psychological distress and digital inequities, and highlight the importance of embedding social media–based interventions within evidence-informed and equitable mental health policies [13, 14].

These findings suggest that social media use may be indirectly associated with better communicative capacity in individuals with schizophrenia, as reflected by lower negative symptom scores on the PANSS; however, no direct measure of emotional expression was employed in this study, and this inference should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that social media use among individuals with schizophrenia is associated with lower symptom severity, better functional capacity, and enhanced quality of life, as measured by the PANSS, FAST, and SF-36. These findings suggest that, when used appropriately, social media may serve as a valuable adjunct to conventional psychosocial interventions, supporting social connectedness and overall quality of life in this population. Future research should explore structured and guided social media interventions to maximize benefits while minimizing potential risks.

Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, longitudinal follow-up research is particularly warranted to examine the effects of social media use over time, clarify the direction of causality, and assess whether sustained digital engagement contributes to or results from clinical improvement in schizophrenia.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design of the research precludes the establishment of a definitive causal relationship between social media use and clinical improvement. Although patients using social media exhibited better PANSS and FAST scores, it remains unclear whether social media use actively contributes to clinical recovery or if patients with higher functional capacity and milder symptoms are simply more inclined to utilize digital platforms.

Furthermore, the statistically significant differences in education and income levels between the two groups suggest that socioeconomic status may act as a confounding variable. Patients with higher socioeconomic resources may have better access to technology and treatment options, which could independently influence their quality of life and clinical course.

Finally, several clinically relevant variables were not collected in this study, including treatment stage, medication adherence, family support, the number of acute episodes, prior hospitalizations, history of ECT, and medication doses expressed as chlorpromazine equivalents. Future research should incorporate these data and examine their correlations with functional and quality-of-life outcomes.
