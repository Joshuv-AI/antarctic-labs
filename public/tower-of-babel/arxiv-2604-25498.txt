SymphonyGen: 3D Hierarchical Orchestral Generation with Controllable Harmony Skeleton


Abstract
Generating symphonic music requires simultaneously managing high-level structural form and dense, multi-track orchestration. Existing symbolic models often struggle with a “complexity-control imbalance”, in which scaling bottlenecks limit long-term granular steerability. We present SymphonyGen, a 3D hierarchical framework for contemporary cinematic orchestration. SymphonyGen employs a cascading decoder architecture that decomposes the Bar, Track, and Event axes, improving computational efficiency and scalability over conventional 1D or 2D models. We introduce “short-score” conditioning via a beat-quantized multi-voice harmony skeleton, enabling outline control while preserving textural diversity. The model is further refined using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a cross-modal audio-perceptual reward, aligning symbolic output with modern acoustic expectations. Additionally, we implement a dissonance-averse sampling algorithm to suppress unintended tonal clashes during inference. Objective evaluations show that both reinforcement learning and dissonance-averse sampling effectively enhance harmonic cleanliness while maintaining melodic expression. Subjective evaluations demonstrate that SymphonyGen outperforms baselines in musicality and preference for orchestral music generation. Demo page: https://symphonygen.github.io/

Introduction

For centuries, symphonic composition has served as the primary vehicle for expansive musical storytelling through the collective power of the orchestra.
Replicating this complexity, however, remains a formidable challenge for symbolic AI, as it demands the simultaneous management of high-level form and multi-track textures.
Specifically, models are required to capture dependencies across dozens of voices while maintaining a sophisticated, evolving harmonic landscape over long temporal horizons.
Toward this goal, considerable progress has been achieved through the adoption of Transformer architectures

Frameworks such as SymphonyNet

while more recent models like NotaGen
[

leverage large language model (LLM) training paradigms to generate classical scores with impressive fidelity.

However, while these models effectively capture characteristic patterns of target styles, they often treat generation as a monolithic sequence modeling problem that overlooks intermediate structural decisions, such as the use of a piano-reduction sketch.
This neglect of the multi-stage workflows inherent to professional production leaves a critical gap in controllable symphonic creation, which we characterize as a “complexity-control imbalance”.
On one hand, many approaches for orchestral composition or arrangement

,

,
7
,

flatten multi-track scores into unidimensional token streams, leading to scaling bottlenecks that hinder progress in large-scale orchestral generation.
On the other, prevailing control mechanisms
[

,
13
,
21
,
17
,
1
]
remain insufficient for decomposing the task into workflows that support deep human-AI collaboration.
Furthermore, models trained on broad MIDI datasets frequently produce spurious dissonance, failing to achieve the acoustic clarity and aesthetic value required for cinematic-grade orchestral composition.

To bridge these gaps, we present
SymphonyGen
, a hierarchical framework controlled by a multi-voice harmony skeleton for contemporary cinematic orchestration. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that specifically targets symbolic cinematic orchestration rather than general orchestration. Our central contributions are:

•

3D Hierarchical Architecture:
A cascading encoder-decoder system that processes the Bar, Track, and Event axes separately, reducing computational overhead while improving multi-track scalability.

•

Multi-Voice Harmony Skeleton:
A novel “short-score” condition that provides a granular music outline, steering the composition through beat-quantized harmonic and melodic contours.

•

Audio-Perceptual RL:
A reinforcement pipeline utilizing a cross-modal reward. By aligning symbolic output with acoustic perception, this approach mitigates the qualitative limitations of MIDI datasets.

•

Dissonance-Averse Sampling:
A logit-adjustment algorithm that references the harmony skeleton to suppress unintended clashes during inference, especially those between the non-harmonic tones.

Symbolic Orchestral Generation

Automatic orchestration is the process of expanding or transforming musical material into an ensemble version. Early data-driven approaches like AccoMontage

[

Despite these developments, arrangement models are often built on VAE latent spaces to mitigate the scarcity of paired data
[

with 1D Transformer decoders. While effective for short-term transfer, they often lack the structural awareness for long-form symphonic movements. The Seq2Seq approach in
[

]
is closer to our work in its use of explicit conditions to guide arrangement. However, its conditions are mainly analyzed from the input music, while our condition comes from diverse sources (

2.3
Controllable and Structured Music Generation

Fine-grained control is essential for professional AI music. FIGARO
[
15
]
utilized bar-level interpretable features for controllable generation, while Theme Transformer
[
13
]
focused on thematic conditioning. To address phrase structure, researchers have proposed structure-related attention mechanisms
[

and cascaded diffusion models for whole-song generation
[
17

.
Additionally, Text2midi
[

explored generating symbolic music from text captions.

While previous methods offer diverse conditions for steering music generation, they still lack the detailed multi-voice grounding that could greatly enhance control for complex symphonic orchestration. In contrast, our approach more effectively bridges compositional intent with orchestral realization by outlining the harmony skeleton.

The backbone of our model is a series of cascading Transformer encoders and decoders to process one structural level at a time. This hierarchical approach of decomposing the sequence into Bar, Track, and Event levels is designed to manage the high density of symphonic scores. (We define events as the sequence of tokenized musical notes or note attributes within a specific bar-track intersection.)

While 2D models (e.g., NotaGen) divide tokens into patches, our 3D architecture groups notes by bars and tracks. As shown in
Table
1
, the 3D architecture scales quadratically with respect to each axis independently, and substantially reduces inference VRAM peak usage. This decomposition is particularly effective when coupled with a music outline provider such as our harmony skeleton; by delegating global planning to the skeleton, the model circumvents the exhaustive computation typically required to maintain coherence across a flattened representation.

3.2
Harmony Skeleton Analysis

We employ a rule-based analysis of beat-wise harmony skeleton that consists of two steps:

Chord Template Matching.
We select a template chord (any triads or seventh chords) based on the highest cosine similarity of the pitch-class distribution for each beat.

Extension Identification.
We employ dynamic programming to include a maximal set of additional notes as chord extensions, under the constraint of no new minor or major second intervals being introduced.

The chordal notes and the extensions are stretched to beat boundaries to form the harmony skeleton.
Roughly speaking, the harmony skeleton specifies what pitches are allowed to be used in the music as harmonic tones.

We tested the harmony skeleton analysis method on the DCML harmony annotation corpus
[
3
]
, achieving about 85% pitch-class precision and 89% pitch-class recall.

3.3
Encoding and Decoding

The model operates via a consistent process of transformation, where higher-level encoded features are decoded into contexts to guide lower-level decoders (
Figure
2
).

We first process harmony events (grouped by bars) through an event encoder with pooling to produce harmony bar features.
Simultaneously, music events are processed by a shared event encoder to produce track features, while fusing metadata embeddings (bar length, track ID, instrument ID). The track features are processed by a track encoder to produce music bar features.

We implement a
concat-pass-split
mechanism to integrate harmony and music bar features. They are first concatenated along the bar axis, processed by a bar decoder, after which split back into harmony bar context and music bar context. This enables the model to plan for future harmony changes informed by the harmony sequence.

[\mathbf{c}_{hB}\,||\,\mathbf{c}_{B}]=\mathrm{Dec}_{B}([\mathbf{z}_{hB}\,||\,\mathbf{z}_{B}])

(4)

The bar context is right-shifted to protect causality. It is then broadcast and added to the track features, processed by a track decoder to produce track context.

\mathbf{c}_{T}=\mathrm{Dec}_{T}(\mathbf{1}_{T}\otimes\mathrm{Shift}(\mathbf{c}_{B})+\mathbf{z}_{T})

(5)

The harmony bar context is right-shifted, broadcast and added to the harmony event embeddings, processed by a harmony event decoder to produce harmony event context.

\mathbf{c}_{h}=\mathrm{Dec}_{E}(\mathbf{1}_{E}\otimes\mathrm{Shift}(\mathbf{c}_{hB})+\mathbf{x}_{h})

(6)

The harmony event decoder is supervised by prediction of the harmony events during training, while a standalone 1D decoder is used to generate them during inference.

The music events are predicted by a music event decoder, while fusing the track context, metadata embeddings, and the harmony event context through cross-attention.

3.4
Two-Stream Cross-Attention

A flexible cross-attention mechanism for the music event decoder is important for our architecture to maintain structural coherence.
Odd layers cross-attend to the harmony event context of the current bar, while even layers cross-attend to the output hidden states of the preceding layer, retrieved from the previous bar in a track-aligned manner (
Figure
3
).
As the set of active tracks may vary across bars, we utilize an auxiliary
Track-Previous Index Map
to help a track “find itself” in the previous bar even if its index has shifted.
During inference, the track-previous index map is updated as track IDs are predicted.

In an ablation study, the two-stream cross-attention mechanism reduced about 30% of validation loss compared to using either one stream in isolation.

3.5
Tokenization and Metadata Handling

Figure 4
:
Tokenization Compression Scheme.

To balance the ratios of padding and truncation of the 3D music representation, we propose a compressed REMI
[
6
]
tokenization scheme. It utilizes five types of tokens: Pitch, Position, Duration, End-of-Track, and a novel
Legato
. The compression follows three steps (
Figure
4
):

Note Grouping.
Notes at the same position share the same position token. Notes with the same duration at the same position share the same duration token.

Downbeat Pruning.
We treat
[Pos: 0]
as the implicit default position for each bar, omitting the downbeat tokens.

Legato Fusion.
We compress tokens where a note starts immediately after the previous one ends. A
[Legato: d]
token at position
p
serves as a “mini-BPE” that replaces the original
[Dur: d]
and
[Pos: p+d]
tokens.

Currently, our model processes 32 bars of music at a time, where each bar contains up to 32 tracks and each track up to 32 events. For the harmony skeleton, each bar contains up to 64 events.
Using the compressed tokenization, most event sequences in the dataset fit in the respective limit (97% for music, 98% for harmony).
All positions and durations are quantized to a 32nd-note grid.

Unlike standard models that tokenize metadata within the main sequence, SymphonyGen employs separate heads for predicting bar lengths, track IDs, instrument IDs, as well as future extensions of metadata.
The track ID head is also responsible for predicting the End-of-Bar special track.

3.6
Reinforcement Learning via GRPO

After pretraining, the model is refined using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
[
12
]
.
In each GRPO epoch, we sample
K
harmony skeletons. For each skeleton, we generate a group of
G
musical pieces.
GRPO estimates the advantage function by normalizing rewards within each group, allowing the model to efficiently explore diverse orchestration strategies by the same harmony condition.

The reward function is given by CLaMP 3
[
18
]
, a three-way alignment model for text, audio, and music score.
We utilize its audio encoder to embed the audio converted from MIDI outputs by MuseScore 3.6.2.
After pooling, the cosine similarity against the centroid embedding of a reference audio set serves as the reward.

This cross-modal reward has three primary advantages:
(1) it captures high-dimensional orchestral features that are difficult to specify via rule-based rewards;
(2) it bypasses the “symbolic data bottleneck” by utilizing high-quality audio recordings as the training signal;
and (3) it uses a reference set of different modality from the pretraining dataset, thereby reducing plagiarism risks.

3.7
Dissonance-Averse Sampling

To combat the issue of spurious dissonance often found in symbolic models, we implement inference-time dissonance-averse sampling relative to a reference harmony. Since our architecture maintains explicit knowledge of the harmony skeleton that discerns harmonic (
H
) and non-harmonic (
N
) tones at any time step
t
in the entire music, we can distinguish between different types of dissonances.

First, we define a symmetric dissonance matrix
W
, where
W_{ij}\in[0,1]
represents the degree of clash between pitch
i
and pitch
j
.
Then, we compute the following score:

D_{\text{total}}=\lambda_{hn}\sum_{t}H_{t}^{\top}WN_{t}+\lambda_{nn}\sum_{t}\frac{1}{2}N_{t}^{\top}WN_{t}

(7)

where
H_{t}
and
N_{t}
are the normalized occupancy vectors of active harmonic and non-harmonic tones, respectively.

The values in
W
are guided by interval sensory distance as described in the Plomp-Levelt curve
[
11
]
; we set these weights manually, where (for example) minor seconds and tritones receive weights of
1.0
and
0.95
, while perfect fifths receive
0.1
.
To ensure acoustic clarity,
W
can be further modulated by a register-dependent decay function to respect the Low Interval Limit—a principle in orchestration that necessitates wider intervals in lower registers.

During music event decoding, we subtract the logits of pitch tokens by the amount of dissonance each pitch would introduce at that time. The pitch logits are then re-normalized, preserving the total probability mass.

Experimental Setup

4.1
Dataset

We utilize the
SymphonyNet Dataset

[
8
]
, comprising 728 classical and 45,632 contemporary MIDI files. The MIDI files are partitioned into a 90/10 train/val split.

4.2
Training and Inference Details

SymphonyGen is implemented with 33 layers (512 hidden size, 124M parameters), consisting of 8 harmony event decoder layers, 9 music event decoder layers, and 4 layers each for the rest of encoders and decoders. Pre-training was conducted on four NVIDIA H800 GPUs for one day using the AdamW optimizer
[
9
]
(
lr=1\times 10^{-4}
) with cosine annealing.
GRPO was conducted on a single GPU for several hours until the reward reached a plateau (
lr=4\times 10^{-5}
), using
K=16
and
G=32
.
The CLaMP 3 centroid embedding is computed from a reference set of five game cinematic music tracks.

In GRPO rollout and final inference, harmony skeletons are generated via a standalone 12-layer 1D decoder (768 hidden size, 87M parameters, 1536 sequence length). We employ filters rejecting skeletons with low density (
<3
notes/beat), excessive repetition (
>25\%
), or unusual log probability. About 20% of skeletons survive these filters.

We set sampling parameters
(\lambda_{hn},\lambda_{nn})
to
(1,10)
, top-
p
to
0.99
and temperature to
1.0
.
Pitch logits out of the instrument ranges based on statistics of the dataset are masked in music event decoding.
The BPM is set to a constant
120
.

We consider the ability to easily apply diverse skeleton filters to be an inherent advantage of our controllable framework.

5.1
Objective Evaluation

As shown in
Table
2
, the reinforcement learning stage significantly enhances the average CLaMP score and lowers dissonance scores, while preserving melodic movement and ornament.
This suggests that the model is more aligned with modern acoustic expectations.
Dissonance-averse sampling further suppresses the non-harmonic artifacts.

As
\lambda_{hn}
and
\lambda_{nn}
increase, the melodic movement and ornament reach their peak around
(\lambda_{hn},\lambda_{nn})=(1,10)
. Beyond this “sweet spot” (e.g., at
(5,20)
), the model becomes overly mechanical and stagnant, losing the legitimate passing notes where musicality resides. This suggests that mild
H-N
penalties combined with aggressive
N-N
suppression yield the best balance between harmonic cleanliness and melodic expression.

5.2
Subjective Evaluation

5.2.1
Round 1: Orchestral Composition Task

We collected 235 evaluations from 32 participants (10 educated, 22 general). As shown in
Table
3
, SymphonyGen captures most of the highest ratings across both groups.

General Listeners:
Our model achieves a significant lead in Quality, Coherence and Preference within this group.
This suggests that general listeners find SymphonyGen’s contemporary cinematic style more accessible.
Notably, our model surpasses the average ratings of selected excerpts from the dataset, which indicates that general listeners may find the average MIDI data less “polished” than our RL-aligned dissonance-reduced output.

Educated Listeners:
While SymphonyGen remains the preferred model, the performance gap narrows among educated listeners.
This convergence suggests that educated listeners possess a higher cognitive tolerance for complex harmonic tension or dissonance; they are more likely to appreciate the structural intent of a piece even when certain segments are harmonically compromised.

5.2.2
Round 2: Orchestral Arrangement Task

In light of our marginal lead in Orchestral Richness in Round 1, we introduced a composite reward (
\text{CLaMP 3}+0.2\times\tanh(\text{Track Density}/4)
) in the Round 2 test. We also found it necessary to activate the register-dependent decay in
W
to maintain clarity in the bass as track density increased.
We collected 96 evaluations from 16 participants (all educated). The results are shown in
Table
4
.

SymphonyGen outperforms the baseline in all dimensions, with a significant lead in Overall Quality and noticeable lead in Orchestral Richness. This suggests that when augmented with a track density reward, our model is able to achieve richer orchestration while preserving musicality.

5.2.3
Qualitative Feedback

We have gathered comments from our participants to better understand our model’s performance and limitations.

Strength:
Participants more frequently described the style of our model using expressions like “a strong sense of fate”, “storytelling”, or as having evocative imagery. This suggests that our model is more adept at cinematic orchestration and conveying narrative emotions compared to the baselines.

Weakness:
Listeners still occasionally found that the “harmony was strange” or contained “many mistakes”, with others describing specific segments as “noisy” or “messy”. This is likely due to errors in harmony skeleton generation.
In the Round 2 test, some participants noted the orchestration could feel “too full”. This indicates a limitation of manual reward shaping.

6
Conclusion

This paper introduces SymphonyGen, a 3D hierarchical framework designed to master the dual complexities of orchestral composition and arrangement. By decomposing the Bar, Track, and Event axes, we achieve improved computational efficiency and scalability while maintaining structural coherence. Our multi-voice harmony skeleton provides a music outline to steer orchestration with granular precision. By integrating audio-perceptual reinforcement learning and dissonance-averse sampling, SymphonyGen successfully produces cinematic scores that resonate more effectively with general listeners.

This framework represents a substantial step toward professional AI collaborators capable of handling the structural and textural demands of contemporary orchestral music.
Future work could improve by adding voice-leading constraints to the harmony skeleton generation to reduce uncanny horizontal transitions, as well as using diverse reference audio sets to learn structural layering and orchestral breathing more dynamically.

7
Ethics Statement

Our training data consists of publicly available symbolic music datasets and audio references.
While SymphonyGen automates aspects of orchestral composition, it is designed as a collaborative tool to assist composers rather than compete with them. We anticipate that human composers will benefit more from our controllable framework than general users or AI agents.
Users should remain cognizant of potential plagiarism risks inherent in generative models rather than assuming all outputs are entirely novel. Finally, we acknowledge that our “dissonance-averse” mechanism is tuned to specific harmonic traditions and may not reflect the aesthetic values of all musical cultures.
