Redox-regulated in situ-forming hydrogel informed by single-cell transcriptomics for functional restoration of injured vocal folds
Authors: Ming Xiong, Chen-Yu Zou, Lei Zhao, Yan-Lin Jiang, Qian-Jin Li, Ming-Hui Fan, Yu-Ting Song, Zhi-Xue Shen, Ya-Ya Gao, Yi-Jun Dong, Lin-Qiao Tang, Jun Liu, Juan-Juan Hu, Hui Yang, Hui-Qi Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vocal fold (VF) injury, frequently caused by laryngeal microsurgery, leads to fibrosis and phonation impairment. Effective therapies remain limited, partly due to limited understanding of VF injury mechanisms. Using single-cell RNA sequencing of injured human VFs, we identified marked oxidative stress upregulation in epithelial cells, highlighting the need for epithelial protection and redox modulation. Informed by this insight, we developed an in situ-forming redox-regulated hydrogel, compatible with endoscopic spray delivery. It forms a conformal gel on the VF surface within 2 seconds, providing physical shielding and oxidative microenvironment regulation. The hydrogel reduced intracellular and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, activated endogenous protective mechanisms, preserved mitochondrial function, and consequently rescued epithelial cells from oxidative stress-induced apoptosis and proliferation loss. In vivo, the hydrogel accelerated epithelialization and restored functions (vibratory and phonatory). Overall, this study introduces a unique approach for functional VF repair and offers a promising paradigm for biomaterial development guided by pathological microenvironment.

Introduction

Voice is a fundamental medium for human communication, social interaction, and self-expression. The vocal folds, as the primary anatomical structures responsible for phonation, are composed of a delicate layered mucosa. Injury to the vocal folds may result in hoarseness, dysphonia, or even complete voice loss, significantly affecting communication, occupational function, and psychological well-being. In clinical practice, vocal fold injury is frequently caused by laryngeal surgery, particularly transoral microsurgical procedures widely used to remove vocal fold polyps, cysts, and early-stage laryngeal cancers1,2. Lasers employed in laryngeal microsurgery allow for precise lesion removal, but their thermal effects can still cause unintended damage to adjacent tissues, resulting in fibrotic scarring and compromised vocal fold vibration3–5. Despite the increasing use of laryngeal microsurgery, effective strategies to prevent or treat postoperative vocal fold injury remain lacking, leaving patients vulnerable to long-term voice impairment.

Current treatment options for vocal fold injury include surgical excision, drug injection (e.g., corticosteroids, platelet-rich plasma), and voice therapy6,7. Surgical excision removes scar tissue but often causes secondary damage and recurrent risk. Drug injection demonstrates partial efficacy to reduce fibrosis, yet outcomes are frequently suboptimal and complications (e.g., hematoma)8,9. Voice therapy, although a non-invasive alternative, suffers from time-consuming, poor patient adherence, and inconsistent results10,11. In recent years, novel strategies have emerged involving the injection of stem cells, tissue-engineered scaffolds, or functional hydrogels to prevent vocal fold scarring12–17. Although promising for antifibrotic effects, the inherent drawbacks of injection, including secondary injury and challenging precise lamina propria targeting, remain significant. These limitations may reflect an incomplete understanding of vocal fold injury pathophysiology and the lack of biomaterial designs suited to the vocal fold’s anatomical and functional complexity.

Recent advances in omics technologies have opened new avenues for elucidating the molecular basis of vocal fold injury, supporting the development of more targeted and biologically responsive treatment strategies18–20. To investigate the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying vocal fold injury and identify potential therapeutic targets, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on human vocal fold tissue following injury. To our knowledge, no similar scRNA-seq profiling of injured human vocal folds has been reported prior to our study, generating a high-resolution cellular and molecular map of the regenerative microenvironment. Our analysis revealed that epithelial cells emerged as a dominant population, exhibiting markedly greater enrichment of reactive oxygen species (ROS)-related pathways than other cell types, suggesting a cell type specific ROS response central to the pathogenesis of scarring. Given the epithelial layer’s surface location and direct exposure to mechanical and environmental insults, sustained ROS likely contributes to impaired healing and fibrotic transformation. These findings highlight the epithelial oxidative microenvironment as a promising therapeutic target for precision intervention.

Given the intricate structure of the vocal fold tissue, its repair imposes unique demands on the compliance, spatial precision, and biosafety of therapeutic materials. In recent years, in situ-forming hydrogel strategies have emerged as particularly well-suited for vocal fold applications, owing to their ability to rapidly gel on the injury surface without requiring injection21,22. This approach not only facilitates user-friendly administration but also establishes a stable physical barrier over the epithelium, offering sustained protection to the injured region. When combined with local modulation of oxidative stress, such hydrogels hold potential to simultaneously address both structural vulnerability and microenvironmental imbalance, thereby promoting functional restoration of the vocal folds.

Guided by insights from scRNA-seq and clinical delivery considerations, we developed an in situ-forming redox-regulated hydrogel, composed of amino-modified small intestinal submucosa (SIS-ADH), oxidized hyaluronic acid (OHA), and tannic acid (TA). This hydrogel eliminates the need for injection by undergoing in situ gelation directly on the vocal fold surface, enabling convenient application while minimizing the risk of needle-induced tissue injury. Upon application, it forms a conformal barrier that physically protects the injured epithelium from further environmental exposure. In addition, the synergistic effects of SIS and TA reduce oxidative stress by modulating the local redox microenvironment, thereby promoting epithelial regeneration. These combined features provide both structural and redox-responsive protection to facilitate effective vocal fold repair. Building on the scRNA-seq analysis of injured human vocal folds, this study presents a targeted biomaterial strategy through the design of an in situ-forming hydrogel that adheres to the vocal fold surface and modulates oxidative stress. Results and discussion

Epithelial cells exhibit elevated ROS pathway activity in inflamed human vocal folds

To explore the underlying mechanisms and key factors in vocal fold injury, we conducted scRNA-seq to construct a single-cell atlas of normal and injured human vocal fold. The study included two normal vocal fold samples (Normal VF) obtained from patients who underwent total laryngectomy for head and neck cancer without clinical evidence of vocal fold involvement; the primary tumor sites were anatomically distant from the vocal folds. This sampling strategy has been previously validated18,23,24. In addition, four inflamed vocal fold samples (Inflamed VF) were collected from patients undergoing surgery for Reinke’s edema, a non-malignant inflammatory condition. Detailed patient information is provided in Supplementary Data 1. Because access to normal human vocal fold tissue is inherently limited in clinical settings, the scRNA-seq sample size in this study was relatively small, which should be acknowledged as a limitation.

Differential gene expression analysis between the Normal VF and Inflamed VF identified enrichment in ROS-related pathways, as demonstrated by pathway enrichment results (Supplementary Data 2) and the associated differentially expressed genes (Supplementary Data 3). To determine which cell types primarily accounted for the observed differences in ROS pathway activity between normal and inflamed vocal folds, we performed ROS pathway scoring for each cell using a ROS gene set (Regulation of Response to Reactive Oxygen Species; GO:1901031). Pathway scoring further revealed that among all cell types, epithelial cells in the Inflamed VF exhibited the most prominent upregulation of ROS-related gene sets relative to the Normal VF (Fig. 2c). To further assess the robustness of this observation, we additionally performed an integrated analysis by incorporating three normal samples from a publicly available single-cell dataset of normal pharyngeal/laryngeal tissue (GSE206332), as no directly comparable public scRNA-seq dataset of injured vocal fold tissue is currently available. The overall results remained highly consistent with those obtained from our original dataset, further supporting the robustness of the main findings (Supplementary Fig. 2). This suggests the elevated ROS activity in epithelial cells may play key roles in vocal fold injury.

Given the anatomical location of vocal fold epithelial cells at the tissue surface, these cells are directly exposed to external oxidative environments and mechanical stress, making the elevated ROS levels in vocal fold epithelial cells during injury a potentially critical factor influencing tissue repair. On one hand, epithelial cells play a pivotal role in the healing process, where delayed re-epithelialization is poses a major barrier to timely wound closure25,26. On the other hand, excessive accumulation of ROS has been shown to inhibit keratinocyte migration and proliferation, thereby slowing the re-epithelialization process and ultimately leading to delayed tissue repair27–29. Although previous studies have investigated ROS in laryngeal disorders, their reliance on animal models or bulk RNA sequencing limits resolution for defining oxidative responses at single-cell resolution30,31. In contrast, by leveraging scRNA-seq profiling of injured human vocal folds, our study systematically identify epithelial oxidative stress as a hallmark of vocal fold injury, thereby providing both a precise cellular target and a mechanistic rationale for future therapeutic strategies centered on epithelial ROS modulation. Nevertheless, the relatively small sample size and the lack of a fully matched public scRNA-seq dataset of inflamed vocal fold tissue should be recognized as limitations, and further validation in larger and more closely matched cohorts will be needed.

To corroborate our scRNA-seq observations, immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess MMP3 expression, a matrix metalloproteinase known to be upregulated in response to oxidative stress and involved in extracellular matrix remodeling32. Quantitative fluorescence analysis revealed a significantly higher MMP3 signal in inflamed versus normal vocal folds (Fig. 2d). Notably, MMP3 signals were predominantly localized within the epithelial layer, aligning with our transcriptomic data that identified epithelial cells as a major source of ROS-related activation.

Taken together, this study presents scRNA-seq analysis of injured human vocal folds and identifies epithelial oxidative stress as a key pathological feature. By delineating the spatial and cell-type specificity of redox imbalance, our findings provide a mechanistic foundation and therapeutic rationale for targeting epithelial ROS in vocal fold repair. Despite the limitations in sample availability and external validation, the integrated analysis with a public dataset yielded overall consistent results, which further increases confidence in the robustness of these findings.

Synthesis and characterization of SIS-OHA-TA hydrogel

Guided by the pathological insights revealed by scRNA-seq analysis, we designed a redox-regulated in situ-forming hydrogel with antioxidant, bio-adhesive, and self-healing properties. The hydrogel is synthesized via Schiff-base crosslinking between amino-modified small intestinal submucosa (SIS-ADH) and oxidized hyaluronic acid (OHA), while tannic acid (TA) is incorporated to confer potent redox-regulatory activity and tissue adhesion. SIS, namely decellularized porcine small intestinal submucosa, was selected as the primary component owing to its excellent biocompatibility and its ability to promote epithelial regeneration33–35. Histological staining and DNA content (<50 ng mg−1) confirmed effective decellularization of SIS with preservation of the overall matrix structure (Supplementary Fig. 3). To achieve rapid gelation and adhesion on the vocal fold surface, we designed a system in which SIS is crosslinked with OHA via Schiff-base reaction. To improve the efficiency of this reaction, SIS was modified with adipic dihydrazide (ADH), yielding SIS-ADH. The introduced amine groups readily react with the aldehyde groups on OHA, enabling rapid hydrogel formation36,37. In addition, TA, a natural polyphenol with known antioxidant properties, was incorporated to scavenge ROS in epithelial cells. To verify the ADH modification and optimize SIS-ADH preparation, we further compared products synthesized using different ADH-to-SIS mass ratios and quantified free amino groups using an Ortho-phthalaldehyde assay. The amino group content increased as the ADH-to-SIS ratio increased up to 1:1, whereas no obvious further increase was observed at ratios above 1:1, indicating that 1:1 was the optimal ratio for SIS modification (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Given the critical role of SIS bioactivity in promoting tissue regeneration, we further evaluated whether ADH modification altered its biological properties (Supplementary Fig. 4b–d). The content of key growth factors (TGF-β, VEGF, and FGF-2) showed no significant changes after modification. Moreover, circular dichroism spectroscopy and SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed that the primary protein structure of SIS remained largely intact following ADH conjugation. We next optimized the SIS-ADH/OHA volume ratio by comparing hydrogels prepared at 5:1, 7:1, and 9:1. Rheological analysis showed that the 7:1 formulation exhibited the most favorable overall gelation-related performance and was therefore selected for subsequent studies (Supplementary Fig. 5). In addition, different SIS-ADH/OHA ratios showed no obvious differences in epithelial cell viability or migration (Supplementary Fig. 6). To minimize cytotoxicity, the concentration of TA used in the hydrogel was capped at 8 μM, as higher concentrations were found to inhibit cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Notably, the SIS-OHA (denoted as SO) hydrogel instantly formed within 2 seconds at room temperature without requiring UV exposure, catalysts, or other external conditions (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Movie 1). This in situ-forming capability ensures that the hydrogel can be applied directly onto the vocal fold surface during surgery, where it rapidly conforms to the tissue and forms a localized therapeutic barrier. The fast gelation without the need for UV exposure or other external triggers also makes it well-suited for intraoperative application in transoral microlaryngoscopic procedures, which are commonly performed under endoscopic guidance in a moist and anatomically constrained environment. We have summarized recent representative studies on vocal fold repair in terms of their composition, delivery method, and target tissue layer (Supplementary Data 4)12,14,38–40. Importantly, virtually all existing vocal fold repair materials are injected into the lamina propria as bulking agents. In contrast, our hydrogel is designed for endoscopic surface spraying to protect the denuded epithelium. This fundamentally changes the mechanism of VFs repair strategy.

To further characterize its internal structure, we examined the microstructure of the hydrogel using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). SEM revealed that incorporation of TA at 4 μM and 8 μM (denoted as SO + TA4 and SO + TA8, respectively) reduced the porosity of the hydrogel (SO: 48.14 ± 1.71 %, SO + TA4: 37.06 ± 4.28 %, SO + TA8: 36.93 ± 2.84 %), contributing to a denser internal network structure (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 8).

To assess the mechanical and self-healing of hydrogels, we conducted rheological tests. Time-sweep measurements showed that the storage modulus (G’) increased with TA concentration, reaching 5783.7 Pa for SO, 7968.9 Pa for SO + TA4, and 13348.8 Pa for SO + TA8 (Fig. 3d). This TA-dependent increase in G’ indicates enhanced cohesion, attributable to the formation of a hydrogen-bond network between TA and the groups (-NH2, -COOH, and -OH) in the SIS/OHA matrix41,42. Under cyclic strain (1%-300%), all groups alternated between damaged (300% strain, G’ < G”) and recovered (1% strain, G’ > G”) states, confirming dynamic self-healing capacity (Fig. 3e). Additionally, physically severed SO + TA8 hydrogel pieces could be reassembled and manipulated with tweezers after healing, further demonstrating their intrinsic recoverability (Fig. 3f). Additionally, rheological evaluation over 20-50 °C showed that the G’ of SO + TA8 hydrogel remained stable between 20 °C and 37 °C and, even at elevated temperatures, maintained gel integrity (G’ > G”) with a modulus retention of 90.0% (G’ at 50 °C/G’ at 25 °C), outperforming SO and SO + TA4 (Supplementary Fig. 9a). This thermal stability, attributed to the TA-mediated hydrogen-bond network, did not compromise self-healing ability (Supplementary Fig. 9b). This thermostable self-healing behavior is particularly important in the mechanically dynamic environment of the vocal folds, where repeated stress may challenge material stability.

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms of hydrogel formation, XPS analysis was performed to confirm the successful formation of Schiff base linkages between SIS-ADH and OHA. As shown in the N 1 s spectrum, a characteristic C = N peak at 397.4 eV was observed, along with supporting signals in the C 1 s and O 1 s spectra, indicating the presence of imine bonds and validating the designed Schiff base reaction (Fig. 3g). Notably, further characterization revealed additional mechanisms beyond Schiff base formation. Zeta potential measurements showed that amino modification shifted the surface charge of SIS from −4.35 mV to +21.6 mV (SIS-ADH), while OHA remained negatively charged (−27.8 mV), suggesting the existence of electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged components, which contributes to rapid gelation (Fig. 3h). This electrostatic attraction may further promote in situ gelation under physiological conditions, particularly in the moist and anatomically complex environment of the vocal folds, where rapid and trigger-free gelation is essential for clinical usability and adaptability. Moreover, FTIR analysis revealed changes in protein secondary structure following gelation, including an increase in β-sheet content and a decrease in α-helix, β-turn, and unordered structures, indicating a conformational transition toward a more ordered and stable network (Fig. 3i).

These findings indicate that the gelation of the hydrogel is governed not only by the designed Schiff-base crosslinking mechanism, but also by additional molecular interactions such as electrostatic attraction and protein conformational changes (Fig. 3j). This multimodal crosslinking mechanism endows the hydrogel with rapid in situ gelation capability, making it particularly suitable for application on the anatomically complex and moist surface of the vocal folds. Without the need for external light or chemical triggers, the hydrogel can quickly gel and adhere upon contact with tissue, forming a stable physical barrier that meets the clinical demands of vocal fold repair for immediacy, compliance, and surgical applicability.

Bio-adhesion, mechanical compatibility and TA release of hydrogels

The bio-adhesive properties of hydrogels were systematically evaluated to investigate applicability on the surface of the vocal fold. Without TA, the adhesive strength of the SO hydrogel on porcine intestinal mucosa was measured at 23.93 ± 2.06 kPa. Incorporation of TA significantly enhanced adhesion in a concentration-dependent manner, with SO + TA4 and SO + TA8 reaching 39.53 ± 3.23 kPa and 71.27 ± 5.77 kPa, respectively (Fig. 4a). The concentration-dependent increase in adhesive strength may be attributed to two synergistic effects of TA: (i) its phenolic groups form hydrogen bonds and π-π interactions with tissue surface components (interfacial adhesion)43; and (ii) TA acts as a supramolecular crosslinker that increases the cohesion of hydrogel44,45. Furthermore, to evaluate the effect of high humidity on bio-adhesion, we performed lap shear tests after soaking the hydrogel-tissue constructs in PBS for 4 hours. To assess adhesion under mechanical deformation, SO + TA₈ hydrogel were applied to porcine skin and subjected to bending, twisting, and stretching and maintained robust adhesion in all scenarios, demonstrating strong resistance to displacement and excellent interfacial fixation under dynamic conditions (Fig. 4c). This mechanical compliance is particularly relevant for vocal folds, which undergo rapid, multi-directional strain cycles during phonation, requiring materials to maintain adhesion without delamination or mechanical failure.

To evaluate the mechanical compatibility of hydrogels with vocal fold tissue, rheological frequency sweep analysis was first conducted. The results showed that the storage (G’) and loss moduli (G”) of SO + TA8 exhibited viscoelastic behavior closely resembling that of native vocal fold tissue over the physiological frequency range (Fig. 4d). This viscoelastic similarity suggests that SO + TA8 can deform and recover in synchrony with vocal fold movement during phonation, reducing interfacial stress and minimizing long-term mechanical mismatch. Previous study has shown that materials with high stiffness can disrupt the vibratory behavior of the vocal folds and impair acoustic output, underscoring the importance of mechanical compliance in biomaterial design for phonatory tissue repair46. Furthermore, to assess the durability of the hydrogel under repetitive phonation-like conditions, we performed frequency cycling tests alternating from low (1 Hz) to high (100 Hz) frequency. After 80 cycles, the hydrogel maintained its structural integrity without significant changes in its moduli (Fig. 4e), confirming the stability of SO + TA8 hydrogel under repeated vibratory loading. Moreover, the rheological properties of the hydrogels were insensitive to environmental humidity, as no significant differences in G’ or G” were observed between low (32% RH) and high (99% RH) humidity conditions (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 10). In this context, the viscoelastic properties of the in situ-forming hydrogel are particularly advantageous, as they allow the material to accommodate the rapid and dynamic strain/frequency cycles characteristic of vocal fold vibration.

Given its superior viscoelastic performance, SO + TA8 was selected for subsequent testing in an ex vivo rabbit larynx model to evaluate its tissue adaptability and functional behavior during phonation. The hydrogel was applied to excised rabbit larynges and subjected to airflow-induced vibration, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 4g. High-speed imaging confirmed that SO + TA8 remained firmly adhered throughout phonation-like motion without disrupting mucosal wave propagation (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Movie 2). Quantitative analysis further revealed no significant difference in vibratory amplitude or glottal area (Fig. 4i, j) before and after hydrogel application, indicating that SO + TA8 does not interfere with native biomechanical function. This favorable performance may be partly attributed to the biomimetic properties of the hydrogel. The combination of SIS and OHA recapitulates key structural and compositional features of the vocal fold lamina propria, a multilayered extracellular matrix rich in collagen and viscoelastic components such as hyaluronic acid47,48. SIS serves as a collagen-like fibrous scaffold, while OHA, a derivative of hyaluronic acid, imparts desirable viscoelasticity and water retention. This biomimetic composition and structural compatibility likely contribute to the hydrogel’s high conformity to the vocal fold surface without interfering with its native vibratory function.

The release behavior of tannic acid was further comprehensively evaluated. The in vitro release profile of TA from the SO + TA8 hydrogel in PBS (37 °C) showed that TA reached a release plateau at approximately 24 h, with a cumulative release of about 60% of the loaded TA. The presence of H2O2 did not affect this release behavior (Fig. 4k). This sustained release ensures antioxidant function during the critical early phase of wound healing. In vivo, after applying SO + TA8 hydrogel onto the rabbit vocal fold surface, endoscopic observation confirmed that the hydrogel remained well attached for at least 48 h (Fig. 4l). Quantification of TA in the harvested tissues revealed a peak local concentration at approximately 24 h (Fig. 4m). These results indicate rapid TA release via a concentration gradient, providing direct evidence for sustained antioxidant delivery to the epithelium.

Biocompatibility and redox regulation of hydrogel

Biocompatibility is essential for clinical application of hydrogels49. To evaluate the cytocompatibility, live/dead staining and CCK-8 assays were performed on 16HBE cells cultured with hydrogels. After 5 days of co-culture, live/dead staining showed no significant increase in dead cells compared to controls. At 24 and 48 hours, cell viability remained above 90% in all groups (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. 11). In addition, SEM revealed that 16HBE cells were able to adhere and spread on the surfaces of hydrogels, displaying normal epithelial morphology (Supplementary Fig. 12). These results confirm the low cytotoxicity of the hydrogels. Hemocompatibility was assessed via hemolysis testing (Fig. 5c). No obvious hemolysis was observed in any group, including SO, SO + TA4, and SO + TA8, with hemolysis rates below the safety limit of 5%. Based on earlier scRNA-seq analysis that identified elevated ROS levels as a hallmark of epithelial dysfunction in injured vocal folds, we subsequently evaluated the ability of hydrogels to mitigate oxidative stress and restore epithelial cell function. Firstly, DPPH, ABTS and superoxide anion radical scavenging assay showed that the addition of TA significantly enhanced the multi-antioxidant activity of the hydrogel system (Supplementary Fig. 15a–c). Consistently, catalase activity, an enzymatic marker of intracellular ROS neutralization50, was significantly suppressed in 16HBE cells exposed to 100 μM H2O2, but was progressively restored by treatment with hydrogels in a TA concentration-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 15d). These findings demonstrate that the hydrogel not only scavenges extracellular ROS but also alleviates intracellular oxidative stress by supporting endogenous antioxidant defenses.

Having demonstrated the antioxidant capacity at both extra- and intracellular levels, we next examined whether this ROS mitigation translated into functional improvements in epithelial cell behavior under oxidative stress. 16HBE cell viability and proliferative capacity under oxidative stress were firstly evaluated using live/dead staining and CCK-8 assays (Fig. 5d, e and Supplementary Fig. 16). H2O2 exposure markedly increased cell death and suppressed cell proliferation. However, hydrogel treatment significantly improved cell viability and enhanced cell survival under oxidative stress, indicating potent cytoprotection. This protective outcome is likely attributable to the hydrogel’s ROS-scavenging capability, which helps maintain a more favorable microenvironment for epithelial cell survival and growth. To further validate these observations, primary human vocal fold epithelial cells were isolated (Supplementary Fig. 17a) and subjected to CCK-8 assays under H2O2 stimulation with different hydrogel treatments. Consistent with the findings in 16HBE cells, hydrogel treatment similarly improved cell viability in primary epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 17b). In addition to promoting cell proliferation, the hydrogel enhanced epithelial cell migration under oxidative stress, as demonstrated by both transwell and scratch assays (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 18). H2O2 exposure significantly impaired migratory capacity, while treatment with hydrogel partially restored cell migration (Fig. 5g, h), in line with the observed improvements in cell viability. Notably, cells treated with SO + TA8 showed migration and invasion capacities comparable to controls, suggesting that the hydrogel’s ability to preserve epithelial function under oxidative stress conditions. It should be noted that the 16HBE cells used in this study were derived from human bronchial epithelium rather than vocal fold epithelium, which represents a limitation of the present study. Nevertheless, the bronchus is anatomically adjacent to the human vocal folds, and 16HBE cells have been widely used in studies of ROS and mucosal barrier function51–53. In addition, we repeated several key experiments in primary vocal fold epithelial cells, obtaining results comparable to those observed in the 16HBE cell line. However, due to the difficulty in isolating and expanding primary vocal fold epithelial cells, 16HBE cells were still used in most subsequent validation experiments. Unless otherwise specified, all cell-based experiments described in this study were performed using 16HBE cells.

Collectively, the in vivo results suggest that the hydrogel may protect epithelial cell function in oxidative stress environments (Fig. 5i). Given that re-epithelialization is a critical early step in wound healing, such cellular protection may facilitate faster and more complete mucosal regeneration54. These in vitro observations thus provide a cellular basis for the hydrogel’s potential to support vocal fold repair.

Hydrogel attenuates oxidative stress and reduces epithelial cell apoptosis

The above findings showed that oxidative stress impaired epithelial cell proliferation and migration, both of which were alleviated, at least in part, by the hydrogel. We therefore next examined the direct cytoprotective effects of the hydrogel on epithelial cells under oxidative stress.

We first quantified intracellular ROS levels under the different treatment conditions using DCFH-DA staining coupled with flow cytometry (Fig. 6a, b). Compared with the control group, H2O2 treatment markedly increased the proportion of DCFH-DA-positive epithelial cells (Control: 28.70 ± 1.59 %; H2O2: 55.90 ± 5.26 %; P < 0.0001), indicating substantial intracellular ROS accumulation under oxidative stress. The SO hydrogel without TA showed only a limited effect on ROS reduction (SO: 50.43 ± 3.27 %), whereas incorporation of TA markedly enhanced the antioxidant effect of the hydrogel in a dose-dependent manner. Specifically, SO + TA4 reduced the proportion of DCFH-DA-positive cells to 45.27 ± 4.92 %, while SO + TA8 further reduced it to 35.20 ± 0.72 %, with SO + TA8 performing significantly better than SO + TA4 (P = 0.0419). Relative to the H2O2 group, SO + TA8 significantly lowered intracellular ROS levels (P = 0.0003), with no significant difference compared with the Control group (P = 0.2550). To further validate these findings, we performed DCFH-DA staining in both 16HBE cells and primary human vocal fold epithelial cells and observed results consistent with those obtained by flow cytometry (Supplementary Figs. 17c and 19). In both cell types, H2O2 markedly increased intracellular ROS, whereas hydrogel treatment effectively attenuated this increase. We next assessed the effect of the hydrogel on apoptosis in 16HBE cells using Annexin V-FITC/7-AAD staining (Fig. 6c-d). H2O2 treatment markedly increased the apoptotic rate, defined as the sum of early and late apoptotic cells, to 52.37 ± 4.85 %. The SO hydrogel did not significantly reduce apoptosis (49.03 ± 5.77 %; P = 0.9220 versus H2O2), whereas SO + TA8 treatment significantly lowered the apoptotic rate to 15.18 ± 4.99 %. Notably, the apoptotic rate after SO + TA8 treatment was not significantly different from that in the Control group (Control: 27.60 ± 6.19 %; P = 0.0876). We further validated these observations in primary vocal fold epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 17d). TUNEL staining showed that oxidative stress markedly increased apoptosis in primary epithelial cells (Control: 3.66 ± 2.51 %; H2O2: 57.57 ± 5.19 %; P < 0.0001), whereas hydrogel treatment significantly reduced the apoptotic rate in a dose-dependent manner (SO + TA4: 23.80 ± 7.94 %; SO + TA8: 9.76 ± 1.58 %; P = 0.0314). Because timely epithelial coverage of the wound surface is a critical step in re-epithelialization55, the increase in epithelial apoptosis under oxidative stress may contribute, at least in part, to delayed wound closure.

As mitochondrial dysfunction is an important mechanism linking ROS accumulation to apoptosis, we next examined mitochondrial membrane potential using JC-1 staining (Fig. 6e, f). Flow cytometric analysis showed that H2O2 treatment markedly increased green fluorescence, which reflects loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, with the proportion of green-fluorescence-high cells increasing from 1.94 ± 0.70 % in the control group to 38.67 ± 2.85 % in the H2O2 group. Neither the SO hydrogel without TA (29.77 ± 8.26 %) nor the SO + TA4 hydrogel containing a lower concentration of TA (27.77 ± 2.93 %) significantly rescued this change (P = 0.1913 and P = 0.0855 versus H2O2, respectively), whereas SO + TA8 significantly reduced the proportion of green-fluorescence-high cells to 13.42 ± 4.17 %, indicating a marked protective effect against mitochondrial depolarization. Confocal JC-1 imaging yielded comparable results (Fig. 6g, h). Green fluorescence intensity was substantially increased after H2O2 treatment compared with the Control group (Control: 5.14 ± 0.63; H2O2: 18.51 ± 3.39), whereas hydrogel treatment attenuated this change (SO + TA8: 9.90 ± 1.54). These findings indicate that, beyond reducing intracellular ROS accumulation, the hydrogel may also suppress apoptosis by preserving mitochondrial function.

We further assessed 8-OHdG as a marker of oxidative DNA damage (Fig. 6i, j). H2O2 stimulation markedly increased 8-OHdG levels, whereas hydrogel treatment substantially attenuated this effect. This finding further supports the antioxidant protective effect of the hydrogel, suggesting that its activity extends beyond ROS reduction to limiting downstream oxidative molecular damage.

Hydrogel mitigates oxidative stress-induced injury by preserving mitochondrial homeostasis and suppressing mitochondrial apoptotic signaling

Having shown that the hydrogel reduced intracellular ROS accumulation, attenuated apoptosis, and alleviated the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, we next asked whether these protective effects were associated with the maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis and inhibition of mitochondria-dependent apoptotic signaling. MitoSOX staining was used to assess mitochondrial ROS levels (Fig. 7a, b). As expected, H2O2 stimulation markedly increased the red fluorescence signal, indicating excessive mitochondrial ROS accumulation under oxidative stress. In contrast, treatment with the SO + TA8 hydrogel substantially reduced the MitoSOX signal, suggesting that the hydrogel effectively alleviated mitochondrial oxidative stress. Transmission electron microscopy further supported this observation (Fig. 7e): mitochondria in H2O2-treated cells exhibited pronounced ultrastructural damage, whereas this injury was partially rescued following treatment with the SO + TA8 hydrogel. To further determine whether mitochondrial integrity was functionally preserved, we used the outer mitochondrial membrane protein TOM70 as a mitochondrial marker and examined its colocalization with cytochrome c (Fig. 7c-d). Under physiological conditions, cytochrome c is largely retained within mitochondria, whereas its redistribution into the cytoplasm is a hallmark of mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and apoptotic activation56,57. Confocal imaging together with Pearson’s colocalization analysis showed that H2O2 stimulation reduced the colocalization coefficient from 0.70 ± 0.01 in the control group to 0.44 ± 0.02, consistent with impaired mitochondrial integrity and cytochrome c release from mitochondria into the cytoplasm. This change was markedly attenuated by hydrogel treatment and showed a degree of tannic acid concentration dependence (SO + TA4: 0.62 ± 0.01; SO + TA8: 0.70 ± 0.02, P = 0.0113). These results suggest that the hydrogel limits mitochondrial damage and restrains the initiation of mitochondrial apoptotic signaling at the level of cytochrome c mobilization.

Because cytochrome c release is a central event in mitochondrial apoptosis and is tightly regulated by BCL-2 family proteins, we next examined the expression of key apoptosis-related regulators58 (Fig. 7g, h). Western blot analysis showed that oxidative stress increased the levels of the pro-apoptotic proteins BAX and cleaved caspase-3, while reducing the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2. This pattern is consistent with activation of the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, in which BAX/BCL-2 imbalance promotes mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, facilitates cytochrome c release, and ultimately leads to caspase-3 activation. Notably, these changes were substantially reversed following hydrogel treatment. When considered together with the cytochrome c/TOM70 colocalization data, these results support the idea that the hydrogel suppresses the BAX/cytochrome c/caspase-3 axis and thereby mitigates mitochondria-dependent epithelial apoptosis.

Given that the preservation of mitochondrial homeostasis is closely linked to cellular antioxidant capacity, we further assessed the NRF2/HO-1/NQO1 pathway (Fig. 7i–j). Hydrogel treatment increased the expression of NRF2 as well as its downstream antioxidant effectors HO-1 and NQO1, suggesting that activation of endogenous antioxidant defense may contribute to the observed mitochondrial protection. In this context, the hydrogel appears not only to inhibit downstream apoptotic signaling, but also to act upstream by enhancing the cellular response to oxidative stress, thereby limiting ROS-driven mitochondrial injury and the subsequent apoptotic cascade. Taken together, these findings suggest that the hydrogel protects epithelial cells from oxidative stress-induced injury, at least in part, by reinforcing antioxidant defense, preserving mitochondrial homeostasis, and suppressing mitochondria-dependent apoptotic signaling.

Hydrogel accelerates re-epithelialization and guides tissue remodeling in vocal fold repair

To further assess the reparative effect of hydrogel, we established a rabbit model of vocal fold injury induced by electrocautery. This model was designed to mimic key features of thermal injury in clinical laryngeal surgery59. Laryngoscopic images from a human patient and the rabbit model showed gross morphological similarities, supporting the clinical relevance of this model (Supplementary Fig. 20). Representative endoscopic images after injury and following treatment are shown in Supplementary Fig. 21, and a laryngoscopic movie demonstrating vocal fold appearance after injury is provided in Supplementary Movie 3. Representative movies showing post-treatment vocal fold morphology under endoscopic observation are provided in Supplementary Movie 4. Compared to the commercial hyaluronic acid-based hydrogel (Sinus®), the in situ-forming hydrogel exhibited superior adhesive performance and mechanical adaptability to the vocal folds.

Tissue samples were harvested at 1, 4 and 8 weeks for comprehensive evaluation of epithelial regeneration and fibrosis (Fig. 8a). Laryngoscopic imaging was performed at each time point to observe and document the healing process. Compared to the sham group, the control group showed pronounced scarring or atrophy. Sinus® also resulted in varying degrees of scarring. In the SO group, scarring was reduced but still present in some regions. Notably, the SO + TA8-treated group exhibited smooth, scar-free vocal folds across all time points. These observations suggest that SO + TA8 hydrogel markedly reduces scar formation compared to both control and clinically available hyaluronic acid hydrogels (Fig. 8b). Given our earlier findings that the SO + TA8 hydrogel promotes epithelial cell proliferation and migration under oxidative stress, we hypothesized that this property may accelerate epithelialization and tissue repair in vivo. To assess the pro-epithelialization capacity of SO + TA8, we performed H&E staining on vocal folds harvested one week post-injury and quantified epithelial thickness within the wound area (Fig. 8c, g). The results showed that neither the control group nor the Sinus®-treated group achieved complete re-epithelialization by week 1. In contrast, both the SO and SO + TA8 groups exhibited complete epithelial coverage, with the SO + TA8 group displaying the greatest average epithelial thickness, indicative of accelerated epithelial regeneration. At week 4, epithelial thickness was further compared (Supplementary Fig. 22). Subsequently, PCNA immunohistochemical staining and TUNEL apoptosis assays were performed to evaluate epithelial cell proliferation and apoptosis near the repair zone. PCNA (proliferating cell nuclear antigen) is a well-established marker of DNA synthesis and cell proliferation. As shown in Fig. 8d, h, the number of PCNA+ epithelial cells were significantly lower in the control and Sinus® groups compared to the SO and SO + TA8 groups, indicating limited regenerative activity. A similar trend was observed in the TUNEL staining results (Fig. 8e, i), where fewer apoptotic cells were detected in the SO and SO + TA8 groups, suggesting that both hydrogels could attenuate epithelial cell apoptosis. These findings are consistent with our previous in vitro observations, where hydrogel demonstrated anti-apoptotic effects, supporting a unified mechanism of action across both in vitro and in vivo environments.

Masson trichrome staining was performed on tissue harvested at 8 weeks post-injury to evaluate extracellular matrix remodeling and fibrotic response. As shown in Fig. 8f, j, the control and Sinus® groups exhibited dense collagen deposition, indicative of pronounced fibrosis. In contrast, the SO and SO + TA8 groups showed significantly reduced collagen content, with tissue architecture more closely resembling that of uninjured vocal folds. Notably, the SO + TA8 group demonstrated the least collagen accumulation. This may be attributed to the material’ s ability to promote epithelial regeneration by suppressing epithelial cell apoptosis, thereby reducing the sustained activation of the lamina propria extracellular matrix by external stimuli and supporting functional recovery of the vocal folds. We further performed α-SMA and type I collagen staining to evaluate scar formation within the vocal fold lamina propria (Supplementary Fig. 23). The results showed that the control group exhibited high expression levels of both α-SMA and COL-1, indicating more pronounced myofibroblast activation and collagen deposition after injury, consistent with scar formation. Compared with the control group, the commercial material Sinus® group showed no significant differences in the expression of either α-SMA or COL-1. In the SO group, COL-1 expression was significantly lower than that in the control group, whereas the reduction in α-SMA expression did not reach statistical significance. By contrast, the SO + TA8 group showed significantly lower expression of both α-SMA and COL-1 than the control group (α-SMA, P = 0.0187; COL-1, P = 0.0186), indicating a more pronounced therapeutic effect in reducing aberrant lamina propria remodeling and scar formation after injury. Nevertheless, aspiration risk should be considered for this topical vocal fold hydrogel system. Lung H&E staining at one week revealed no obvious pathological abnormalities (Supplementary Fig. 24). In addition, although this animal model captures certain features of the clinical scenario, further studies, including validation in large-animal models, are still needed before translation to human settings.

Functional restoration and RNA-sequencing elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms of hydrogel

At the tissue level, the hydrogel significantly promoted epithelialization at the injury site and facilitated tissue repair. To further determine whether these structural improvements translated into functional restoration, we evaluated vocal fold vibratory and phonatory performance, and explored the underlying molecular mechanisms through transcriptomic analysis. We employed a custom-built ex vivo vibration platform to evaluate the vibratory and phonatory performance of excised rabbit larynges at 8 weeks post-injury (Supplementary Fig. 25). Nitrogen gas was delivered through the isolated larynx to induce vocal fold oscillation and phonation, while high-speed imaging and audio recording systems were used to capture vibratory motion and acoustic output.

To assess treatment effects on vocal fold physiological function, we analyzed glottal gap dynamics and frame-by-frame vibratory amplitude during phonation. The glottal gap refers to the area between the vocal folds during oscillation, with the maximum glottal gap representing the peak opening and the minimum glottal gap reflecting how tightly the folds close during each vibratory cycle60. Optimal glottal opening and complete closure are critical for generating stable and efficient phonation61. Our results showed that both the Control and Sinus® groups exhibited a marked reduction in maximum glottal gap (Control: 2920.00 ± 116.20 pixels; Sinus®: 3033.67 ± 31.63 pixels) and an increase in minimum glottal gap (Control: 857.33 ± 95.85 pixels; Sinus®: 681.00 ± 94.46 pixels), indicating impaired vocal fold vibration (Fig. 9a). In contrast, the SO (Max: 4548.67 ± 463.73 pixels; Min: 128.33 ± 15.50 pixels) and SO + TA8 groups (Max: 9350.67 ± 875.75 pixels; Min: 138.33 ± 21.03 pixels) demonstrated significant improvement in both parameters, suggesting therapeutic benefits in restoring more normal glottal dynamics. Furthermore, the amplitude was determined by the distance between the mid-membranous vocal folds in each frame. The resulting time-amplitude curves showed patterns consistent with glottal gap changes (Fig. 9b, Supplementary Movie 5). The control and Sinus® groups exhibited low, relatively flat waveforms, suggesting stiff, limited oscillation likely due to scarring. In contrast, the SO and SO + TA8 groups displayed more dynamic amplitude fluctuations, with waveform contours resembling those of healthy vocal folds, indicating improved vibratory flexibility following hydrogel intervention. To evaluate phonatory recovery, we performed acoustic analysis. As indicators of voice quality, jitter and shimmer reflect the pitch instability and amplitude variability between successive vibratory cycles, respectively62 Both the Control and Sinus® groups exhibited elevated jitter and shimmer values, indicating impaired phonatory stability. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the hydrogel plays a critical role in enhancing vocal fold vibratory function and phonatory performance. The comprehensive improvements in glottal dynamics, vibratory amplitude, and acoustic parameters suggest that the hydrogel facilitates the restoration of vibratory rhythm and mechanical pliability, ultimately leading to improved voice quality. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that, although the rabbit vocal fold is widely used in scientific research63,64, there are objective anatomical differences between rabbit and human vocal folds. This should be taken into consideration in future clinical translation.

The superior performance of the SO + TA8 group, together with previous evidence that this hydrogel attenuates epithelial apoptosis and ROS damage, suggests that TA modulates the local microenvironment and promotes mucosal flexibility. To further validate this hypothesis and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, we subsequently performed transcriptomic analysis via RNA sequencing (Fig. 9d). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation among the three groups, with the Sham and Control groups distributed at opposing ends of the plot and the SO + TA8 group occupying an intermediate position. These data suggest that SO + TA8 treatment modulates the transcriptomic profile of injured vocal folds toward a pattern resembling healthy tissue (Fig. 9e). We identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among the three groups (Fig. 9f) and performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to investigate the relevant biological pathways. Compared to the Sham group, the “response to reactive oxygen species” pathway and the “apoptosis” pathway were significantly enriched in the Control group, reflecting elevated oxidative stress and cell death after vocal fold injury (Fig. 9g, j). In contrast, both pathways were markedly downregulated in the SO + TA8-treated group (Fig. 9h, k), suggesting that SO + TA8 treatment attenuates oxidative stress and downstream apoptotic responses, thereby contributing to tissue protection. RT-qPCR validation further supported these findings (Fig. 9i, l): ROS-related genes (MMP3, NCF1, CLEC7A) and apoptosis-related genes (FAS, NFKBIA, BIRC3, CASP1) were significantly upregulated in the Control group relative to sham group, but expression levels were substantially reduced following SO + TA8 treatment, approaching those of the sham group.

These findings help explain the superior epithelial coverage, reduced collagen deposition, and improved vibratory performance observed in the SO + TA8 group in vivo, and are consistent with in vitro results demonstrating that the hydrogel modulates ROS levels and reduces epithelial cell apoptosis. This transcriptomic regulation results from the combined properties of the hydrogel, including its in situ-forming capability and redox-regulated function, which together establish a stable physical barrier at the injury site and reshape the local microenvironment to activate intrinsic tissue repair programs.

In conclusion, this study presents, to our knowledge, a previously undescribed scRNA-seq profiling of injured human vocal folds. This identified elevated epithelial ROS levels as a previously unrecognized contributor to impaired vocal fold healing. Uniquely motivated by this disease-mechanism insight, we designed an in situ-forming hydrogel that provides dual protection to the injured vocal fold epithelium via physical shielding (conformal hydrogel layer) and physiological ROS mitigation (TA-mediated redox regulation). The hydrogel rapidly forms without the need for pH adjustment or light activation, conforms to the dynamic vibratory surface of the vocal folds, alleviates oxidative stress and supports endogenous antioxidant defenses. This process ultimately reduces epithelial apoptosis, preserves epithelial architecture, and promotes timely re-epithelialization. In vivo, the hydrogel successfully promoted functional restoration of vocal folds. Importantly, all three components (SIS, OHA, TA) are already US Food and Drug Administration approved65–67, facilitating subsequent safe clinical application. And the endoscopic spraying procedure is compatible with existing laryngeal microsurgery workflows. Thus, this scRNA‑seq‑informed material design offers a promising and clinically accessible therapeutic avenue for vocal fold injury repair.

Methods

Patient and sample information

Two normal vocal fold samples were obtained from patients who underwent total laryngectomy due to head and neck carcinoma unrelated to the vocal folds. Four inflammatory vocal fold samples were collected from patients undergoing surgery for Reinke’s edema, an inflammatory vocal fold condition. Due to ethical constraints, it was not feasible to obtain completely normal vocal fold tissue. However, the patients selected for normal samples had undergone total laryngectomy for head and neck tumors that did not involve the vocal folds, and the primary tumor sites were anatomically distant from the vocal fold region. Informed consent was obtained from all patients, with approval from the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital (Approval number, 2022-800). Detailed diagnostic information and surgical procedures for each patient are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Single-cell capture and sequencing, and data processing

Vocal fold samples (Normal VF and Inflamed VF groups) were processed using the 10× Genomics platform and sequenced on Illumina HiSeq 2500 (~150–200 million reads/sample). Raw data were analyzed with a modified Drop-seq pipeline. After quality control and doublet removal (DoubletFinder), data were normalized, batch-corrected (Harmony), and analyzed using Seurat for PCA, UMAP, clustering, and marker identification. Gene signature scoring (AUCell) and GO enrichment (clusterProfiler) were also performed. To further strengthen the robustness of the normal tissue analysis, three normal laryngeal tissue samples from an external public scRNA-seq dataset (GSE206332) were incorporated into the integrated analysis together with our in-house data. Detailed methods and parameters are provided in Supplementary Information.

Hydrogel preparation

SIS was collected and prepared as described previously68. The degree of decellularization was first evaluated by histology using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, and Victoria blue staining. Residual cellular content was determined by DNA quantification, which was extracted using an Animal Tissues/Cells Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Cat: D1700, Solarbio). For SIS-ADH preparation, lyophilized SIS was ground into powder. To 0.3 g of SIS powder, 70 mg of pepsin and 30 mL of HCl (pH = 2) were added and digested at 25 °C for 72 h. The digested SIS was diluted with 150 mL of pure water, then ADH (Aladdin, A109760) and EDC-HCl (Aladdin, E106172) were added. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h, dialyzed (MW cutoff: 3500 Da; Solarbio, YA1078) against pure water for 72 h, and lyophilized to obtain SIS-ADH. The optimal reaction condition was determined by measuring the free amino content using a commercial kit (Beyotime, S0584S). For OHA preparation, 2 g of hyaluronic acid (Macklin, H909935) was dissolved in 350 mL of pure water, and 1.129 g of sodium periodate was added. The reaction was carried out in the dark at room temperature for 12 h, followed by termination with ethylene glycol. The mixture was stirred for 30 min, dialyzed for 72 h, and lyophilized to obtain OHA. SIS-OHA-TA hydrogel was prepared by dissolving lyophilized SIS-ADH and OHA in TA solution and mixing them together. The ratio of SIS-ADH to OHA was first optimized based on rheological properties, cell viability, and cell migration. The TA concentration was selected according to its cytotoxicity.

Characterization of hydrogel

The surface morphology of SO, SO + TA4 and SO + TA8 hydrogels was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, S-800, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 25 kV after sputter coating with gold. And the porosity (%) was calculated as (porous area/total area) × 100% (n = 3). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Nicolet 6700, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madison, WA, USA) was performed to analyze the cross-linking chemistry and biochemical composition of the hydrogels, with spectra recorded in the range of 400–4000 cm-1 at room temperature. The TA release was evaluated through in vitro and in vivo models. The wet bio-adhesion properties of the materials were evaluated through the lap shear test, pigskin adhesion test and ex vivo rabbit larynx phonation model. Rheological properties were evaluated using a rotational rheometer (Physica MCR302, Anton Paar, Austria) through time-sweep (1% strain; 1 Hz frequency), strain-sweep tests (1% and 300% strain; 1 Hz frequency) and cycling frequency mode (1% strain; 1–100 Hz frequency; 80 cycles). Furthermore, the effects of temperature and humidity on the rheological properties of the hydrogel were evaluated, and detailed methods and parameters are provided in Supplementary Information.

Cell compatibility

The cell compatibility of the SIS-OHA-TA hydrogel was evaluated using CCK-8 and live/dead staining assays. Human bronchial epithelial cells (16HBE) were cultured in complete medium (90% K-SFM, 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin–streptomycin) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were treated with hydrogel extracts, and viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, 40203ES80, Yeasen, China) at 24 and 48 h. In parallel, a Live/Dead Cell Staining Kit (40747ES76, Yeasen, China) was used to monitor cell viability and morphology at days 1, 3, and 5. All assays were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

Blood compatibility

To assess the blood compatibility of the SIS-OHA-TA hydrogel, a hemolysis assay was performed. Rabbit red blood cells were incubated with hydrogels at 37 °C for 1 h. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and Triton X-100 were used as the negative and positive controls, respectively. After centrifugation, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 545 nm, and the hemolysis rate was calculated.

Tissue compatibility

Subcutaneous implantation was performed in rats, and tissue samples were collected at 1, 2, and 4 weeks post-implantation. The implantation sites were photographed, and histological analysis was conducted using H&E staining on local tissues (1 and 2 weeks) and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) at 2 weeks. In addition, a complete blood count was performed to evaluate systemic immune responses.

Establishment of an oxidative stress model in 16HBE

Based on previously reported methods for inducing oxidative stress in 16HBE cells and the results of our preliminary experiments69, a final concentration of 100 μM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) added to the culture medium for 24 h was selected to establish the oxidative stress model. The treatment groups and corresponding conditions were defined as follows: Control group: cells were cultured in complete medium without any additives; H2O2 group: cells were exposed to complete medium supplemented with 100 μM H2O2; SO + H2O2 group: cells were treated with SIS-OHA extract containing 100 μM H2O2; SO + TA4 + H2O2 group: cells were treated with SIS-OHA-4μM TA extract containing 100 μM H2O2; and SO + TA8 + H2O2 group: cells were treated with SIS-OHA-8μM TA extract containing 100 μM H2O2. All treatment groups were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 24 hours, followed by a PBS wash prior to subsequent analyses.

Assessment of epithelial cell function under oxidative stress

To evaluate the effects of hydrogel on epithelial cell behavior under oxidative stress, 16HBE cells were subjected to CCK-8 assay, live/dead staining, transwell migration assay, and scratch wound healing assay. For the CCK-8 assay, cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with different conditioned media for 24 or 48 h. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, 40203ES80, Yeasen, China), and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Live/dead staining was performed using Calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) reagents (40747ES76, Yeasen, China), and fluorescence microscopy was used to visualize viable and non-viable cells. For the transwell assay, cells were seeded into the upper chamber of transwell inserts, and the lower chamber was filled with conditioned medium corresponding to different treatment groups. After incubation, non-migrated cells were removed, and migrated cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and quantified using ImageJ. For the scratch assay, a linear wound was introduced after cells reached confluence in six-well plates. After treatment with corresponding media, images of the wound area were acquired at defined time points (0 h, 12 h, 24 h), and wound closure was analyzed using ImageJ.

Evaluation of the effects of hydrogel on intracellular ROS, apoptosis, and mitochondrial oxidative stress

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were assessed using a DCFH-DA probe (CA410, Solarbio, China) via flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy and 8-OHdG via fluorescence microscopy (Abcam, ab62623, 1:1000). Cell apoptosis was analyzed by flow cytometry using the FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit with 7-AAD (640922, BioLegend, USA). Mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using the JC-1 Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (40706ES60, Yeasen, China) through both flow cytometry and fluorescence imaging. Mitochondrial ROS levels were detected using the MitoSOX™ Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (M36008, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and visualized by confocal microscopy. The expression of mitochondrial proteins (TOM70, Proteintech, 14528-1-AP, 1:1000; cytochrome c, Proteintech, 66264-1-Ig, 1:500) was evaluated by immunofluorescence.Co-expression analysis of TOM70 and cytochrome c was performed using the Coloc 2 plugin (Colocalization) in ImageJ/Fiji, and the Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated to evaluate their correlation. The expression of apoptosis- and oxidative stress-related proteins was further verified by Western blotting (BCL-2, ABclonal, A0208; 1:1000; BAX, ABclonal, A0207, 1:1000; Caspase-3, Abcam, ab32351, 1:1000; Cleaved caspase-3, Abcam, ab2302, 1:100; NRF2, Cell Signaling Technology, 12721 T, 1:1000; HO-1, ABclonal, A1346, 1:1000; NQO1, ABclonal, A1518, 1:1000). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to observe ultrastructural changes in mitochondria. Flow cytometry was performed on a BD FACS Aria III system, while fluorescence imaging was conducted using a Nikon Ti2 inverted fluorescence microscope or a Nikon TiA1-N-STORM super-resolution microscope. Image analysis was carried out using ImageJ software.

Construction of the rabbit vocal fold injury model

The rabbit vocal fold injury model was constructed as previously described59. Briefly, rabbits were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital, and their vocal folds were visualized using a portable laryngoscope. Bilateral vocal fold injuries were induced by applying electrocautery at 30 W for 5 s per side. Following injury, animals were assigned to different treatment groups: the sham group received general anesthesia and laryngoscopic exposure but did not undergo any injury induction or material application; the control group received no intervention; the Sinus® group was treated topically with a commercial hyaluronic acid-based gel Sinus®; the SO group received SIS-OHA hydrogel; and the SO + TA8 group was treated with SO + TA8 hydrogel. Laryngoscopic evaluation was performed at 1, 4, and 8 weeks post-injury to monitor healing progression, and tissue samples were collected at 1, 4, and 8 weeks for further analysis.

Histological staining reagents

Proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) immunohistochemical staining was performed using an anti-PCNA monoclonal antibody (Abcam, ab29) at a dilution of 1:6000 to assess epithelial cell proliferation. Apoptotic cells were detected using a one-step TUNEL in situ apoptosis kit (Elabscience, E-CK-A320), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Collagen I and α-SMA immunohistochemical staining was performed using an anti-COL-1 antibody (Servicebio, GB11022-3) at a dilution of 1:800 and an anti-α-SMA antibody (HUABIO, SY02-64) at a dilution of 1:50 to evaluate lamina propria remodeling and scar formation. To assess the potential aspiration risk posed by the hydrogel, lung tissue samples were harvested from the upper, middle, and lower lobes of the SO + TA8 group at 1 week after repair and subjected to H&E staining.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

One week after vocal fold injury and treatment, rabbit vocal fold tissues were collected for RNA sequencing. The samples were divided into three groups: Sham group: uninjured vocal fold tissue (n = 3), Control group: injured vocal fold tissue without treatment (n = 3), SO + TA8 group: injured vocal fold tissue treated with SO + TA8 hydrogel (n = 3). The excised vocal fold tissues were washed with cold PBS and immediately stored in liquid nitrogen. The quality and quantity of RNA were assessed using a spectrophotometer, and only samples with a RIN value greater than 7.0 were selected for further processing. Polyadenylated RNA was purified and fragmented before being reverse-transcribed into cDNA. The resulting cDNA was then used to construct sequencing libraries, which were sequenced on the Illumina platform to generate paired-end reads.

High-speed video imaging

High-speed video imaging was first employed to evaluate the adhesion of the material under high-frequency vibration. After in-situ adhesion of SO + TA8 onto the vocal fold surface, the larynx was excised and mounted on a custom-built experimental platform. Vocal fold motion was recorded frame by frame, and parameters including vibratory amplitude (defined as the distance between the midpoints of the bilateral vocal folds) and glottal gap (defined as the area between the vocal folds) were quantified to assess oscillatory performance. The commercial product, Sinus®, was used as a control. In addition, vocal fold vibratory function was assessed at 8 weeks post‑repair, with the same parameters (vibratory amplitude and glottal gap) quantified. For acoustic analysis, parameters such as jitter and shimmer were extracted from phonation recordings using Praat software. A stable 3-second segment of each recording was selected, and analysis was conducted using the default settings.

RT-qPCR

For RNA extraction and reverse transcription, rabbit vocal fold tissues collected at one week post-treatment were used. Total RNA was extracted and reverse-transcribed using the Yeason Total RNA Extraction Kit (19221ES50) and the TAKARA Reverse Transcription Kit (RR092A), following the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR mix was prepared using TB Green without ROX (TaKaRa, RR820A), and the data were analyzed using the △△C method. The RT-qPCR amplification protocol was as follows: Hold stage: 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 30 s; PCR stage: 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 34 s; Melt Curve stage: 60 °C for 1 min, 95 °C for 1 s, followed by 4 °C indefinitely (∞). Primer sequences are provided in the Supplementary Data 5.

Statistics

Statistical tests and scRNA-seq data analysis were performed in R version 4.2.3. Other statistical analyses were conducted using Graphpad Prism 9.5. Unless otherwise stated, each data point represents an independent biological replicate. Measurements were obtained from distinct samples unless otherwise stated. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was used. Abbreviation: ns, not significant.
