Persistently Stuck Mitral Mechanical Prosthesis Rescued Successfully With Deflectable Electrophysiology Catheter
Authors: Vidhyakar Rangasamy Balasubramanian, Prem Krishna Anandan, Mohammed Kasim, Ashwin Kannan, Elango Swamiappan, Madhusri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMechanical mitral valve thrombosis is life threatening and traditionally managed with surgery or thrombolysis, both carrying significant risks. Mechanical prosthetic valves are preferable in younger patients because they are more durable than bioprosthetic valves but are more thrombogenic and should be on life-long anticoagulation. Mechanical valve thrombosis is a dreaded complication that can present in asymptomatic status to severe life-threatening cardiogenic shock. Redo surgery or thrombolysis is the only option available, and both are high-risk procedures and are associated with complications such as stroke, and morbidity and mortality in redo valve replacement procedures are high. Percutaneous release of stuck mechanical valves has been attempted as an off-label procedure in such high-risk scenarios as mostly bail-out procedures or bridge to surgery.1,2 We present a case of transcatheter release of a stuck mechanical mitral valve with novel cerebral protection methods and unconventional methods of opening a stuck mechanical valve without major complications.

Case Summary

A 38-year-old woman who had undergone redo mitral valve replacement (MVR) with a 27-mm bileaflet mechanical valve for prosthetic valve pannus ingrowth and prosthetic valve dehiscence in February 2024 was admitted to the emergency department with sudden onset dyspnea, initially classified as NYHA functional class III, progressing rapidly to NYHA functional class IV. She was in cardiogenic shock with a systolic blood pressure of 60 mm Hg and a pulse rate of 120 beats/min. Her 2-dimensional transthoracic echocardiogram showed stuck mitral valve prosthesis with a mean transvalvular gradient of 34 mm Hg, as shown in Figure 1. Transesophageal echocardiography was not performed during the procedure because of the patient's critical condition, including ongoing cardiogenic shock and the urgency of intervention, which precluded the time and stability required for safe transesophageal echocardiography imaging. Fluoroscopy revealed that both mitral valve leaflets were immobile and in a stuck position, as shown in Figure 2 and Video 1. She was immediately started on noradrenaline infusion and was mechanically ventilated. Her international normalized ratio (INR) was 5.0, and her previous anticoagulation records showed INR values ranging from 2 to 3, with minimal variation and good compliance.

Given the prohibitive risk of a third sternotomy and the patient's history of 2 episodes of prosthetic valve thrombosis—each managed with intravenous thrombolysis using streptokinase and alteplase, administered approximately 5 and 3 months before her most recent surgery in February 2024—thrombolysis was not considered. In the current episode, the patient presented with a jammed prosthetic valve approximately 2 months after her last MVR. However, because of a markedly elevated INR of 5.0 and a high risk of hemorrhagic complications, systemic thrombolytic therapy was deemed contraindicated. Redo surgery was also declined by the patient. The heart team decided to attempt percutaneous transcatheter mechanical thrombectomy of the stuck mitral valve with cerebral protection.

The opening angle and travel arc of the mitral prosthesis were provided by the manufacturer Meril (Miltonia Valve), hereafter referred to as the manufacturer. We attempted to measure the middle and lateral apertures by drawing a red line perpendicular to the inner curve of the outer ring and hinge points of both leaflets on a still-frame cine image on fluoroscopy and obtained dimensions of 4 mm for the middle aperture and 8 mm for the lateral aperture. We also attempted balloon dilation with available 5-mm and 8-mm balloons on a prototype mechanical valve provided by the manufacturer.

Vascular access was obtained via both radial arteries, bilateral femoral arteries, and the right femoral vein under ultrasound guidance. The right radial artery was used for placement of a 6-F pigtail catheter at the noncoronary cusp, the right femoral artery was used for placement of a 6-F Emboshield (Abbott) carotid filter in the right internal carotid artery, and the left femoral artery was used for placement of a 7 × 60-mm Mustang over-the-wire balloon in the left carotid artery, as there was only 1 filter available. The left radial artery was used to protect the left vertebrobasilar system with a 6 × 60-mm Mustang balloon, based on Doppler ultrasound measurements of the left carotid and vertebrobasilar arteries. Both balloons were kept inflated at 6 atmospheres (atm) for 180 seconds during the mitral valve balloon dilation and were deflated afterward. The balloons were then slowly withdrawn under aspiration; minimal embolic debris was noted within the catheter during removal, and no neurologic deficits were observed postprocedure. A dedicated cerebral protection device such as the Sentinel was not available in our catheterization laboratory.

Right femoral vein access was used for transseptal puncture with a Brockenbrough needle and an 8-F Mullins sheath to cross the interatrial septum and was exchanged for a deflectable Agilis sheath, which was used to flex toward the prosthetic mitral valve and was stabilized, as shown in Figure 3. A 0.032-inch Terumo wire over a 6-F Judkins right guiding catheter was used to cross the stuck valve through the lower aperture, which was then dilated with an 8 × 140-mm Mustang semicompliant balloon (Figure 4) at 6 atm. The middle and upper apertures could not be crossed because they remained immobile.

We then used a 6-F deflectable Decapolar Mapping catheter and attempted to hit the upper leaflet by flexing and deflexing movement and by pecking movement, which finally was successful as the catheter was able to negotiate across the immobile upper aperture (Figure 5), and both leaflets started moving. Then the upper aperture was crossed with a 0.032-inch Terumo guidewire and dilated with 8 × 140-mm and 10 × 120-mm Mustang balloons (Figure 6) at 6 atm, and the middle aperture was dilated (Figure 7) with a 5 × 60-mm Mustang ballon at 6 atm.

Case Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient’s hemodynamics improved immediately as soon as the valve leaflets were released. Her inotropic support was withdrawn, and she was extubated on the table. Her neurologic status was preserved, digital subtraction angiography of the cerebral vessels revealed no abnormalities, lactate levels showed a decreasing trend. She was started on oral anticoagulation with heparin bridging with a target INR of 3 to 4. She was discharged on day 4 and had a follow-up at 3 and 6 months, with a mean mitral valve gradient of 4 to 5 mm Hg and NYHA functional class I dyspnea. Her prothrombotic work-up was negative.

Why Beyond the Guidelines

Currently, the American College of Cardiology and European Society of Cardiology recommend surgery as a class I recommendation for treating obstructive mechanical valve thrombosis. The American College of Cardiology also suggests thrombolysis equal to surgery in select cases. However, there are no guidelines on the use of transcatheter mechanical thrombectomy for obstructed mechanical valve thromboses. In cases where there is prohibitive surgical risk and contraindication for thrombolysis, transcatheter mechanical thrombectomy should be considered an alternative method and should be performed with a cerebral protection device for better postoperative outcomes and devoid of major complications.

Discussion

The first-line recommended treatment for a stuck prosthetic mitral valve is a redo surgery or slow infusion of thrombolytic agents. But in our case, the patient had already undergone MVR twice, and the last procedure was performed 3 months back; the patient was not willing for another surgery, and the surgeon turned down because of high prohibitive risk as the patient was in cardiogenic shock. Thrombolysis was also not an option as her INR was very high, and she had a high risk of stroke. We used over-the-wire balloons as cerebral protection methods, which are unconventional and not reliable as we had only one filter device, and we did not have a Sentinel device in our catheterization laboratory at that time. Ideally either a Sentinel device or aortic central cerebral protection devices should be used for better forms of cerebral protection. Regarding cerebral protection, we acknowledge the limitations of using over-the-wire Mustang balloons as a substitute for dedicated cerebral protection devices. This off-label method was used because of the unavailability of a Sentinel device or dual filter system in our catheterization laboratory at the time of the procedure.

The EmboShield filter deployed in the right internal carotid artery did not retrieve significant visible embolic debris on removal, suggesting limited embolic burden or effective containment. As for the balloons used in the left carotid and left vertebrobasilar systems, we agree that these are not designed to act as filters and may only provide temporary occlusion. However, after completing the balloon dilation of the mitral valve, the balloons were deflated slowly, and the associated balloon catheters were carefully withdrawn under aspiration. During this process, minimal embolic debris was observed within the balloon lumen, indicating that some degree of debris entrapment and retrieval was achieved. Importantly, the posterior circulation remained neurologically intact, and no clinical or imaging evidence of posterior circulation stroke was observed postprocedure, supporting the notion that our technique was successful in preventing significant embolization. We kept the balloon inflated for 180 seconds during the mechanical balloon dilation and deflated it after the balloon dilation of the stuck mechanical prosthesis. We used novel methods of opening one of the stuck leaflets using a deflectable electrophysiology mapping catheter, which was described by Vihinen et al,3 followed by balloon dilation using the same balloon that we bench tested on a prototype mechanical valve provided by the manufacturer.3,4 The sizing of the balloon for mechanical thrombectomy was decided based on fluoroscopic measurements of the stuck mitral valve by drawing lines perpendicular to the hinge points of both leaflets and the inner edges of both sides of the leaflets on a still image of the cine taken during fluoroscopy of the valve, and we obtained the measurements of both lateral and central apertures as 8 mm and 4 mm, respectively. Ideally contrast computed tomography should have been used to identify pannus versus thrombus before the procedure, which was not possible as the patient was in cardiogenic shock, and we were running out of time and the patient's hemodynamics were fast deteriorating.

Conclusions

In patients with prohibitive risk for redo mitral valve surgery or thrombolysis, transcatheter mechanical thrombectomy may be an alternative procedure, and cerebral protection is crucial to the immediate procedural outcome.
