DNA-contact mutant p53 displaces BRCA2 from chromatin and drives R-loop-associated genome instability
Authors: Fanfan Li, Ke Fang, Shuhan Si, Quanyong Zhang, Menghan Fan, Chenghao Guo, Jie Sun, Zhuoying Xie, Kai Chen, Zhiyong Chen, Zheng Ge, Chengqi Lin, Zhuojuan Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMutations in the tumor suppressor p53 are among the most frequent events in human cancers. Mutant p53 acquires oncogenic activities beyond the loss of tumor suppressive function. However, how different mutation subtypes differ in their contributions to tumorigenesis remains incompletely understood.ResultsThrough pan-cancer TCGA analysis, we find that patients with p53 DNA contact mutations exhibit worse clinical outcomes than those carrying conformational mutations. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that the DNA contact mutation p53-R273H forms aberrant condensates that sequester BRCA2 and displace it from chromatin, leading to R-loop accumulation and genomic instability. We further identify the DEAD-box helicase DDX3X as a critical factor required for BRCA2-dependent R-loop resolution. Pharmacological inhibition of DDX3X with RK-33 synergizes with the PARP inhibitor Olaparib, enhancing therapeutic efficacy in p53 DNA contact mutant cancer cells.ConclusionsOur study establishes a pathogenic axis through which the clinically aggressive p53 DNA contact mutations drive genomic instability by dislodging BRCA2 from chromatin and disrupting R-loop homeostasis. These findings suggest a potential therapeutic avenue for treating cancers harboring high-risk p53 DNA contact mutations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04151-6.

Background

The tumor suppressor p53, often termed the "guardian of the genome", is a master regulator of cellular homeostasis, orchestrating critical processes such as cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and DNA damage repair [1]. Strikingly, TP53, the gene encoding p53, is the most frequently mutated gene in human cancer, with missense mutations accounting for ~ 80% of alterations [2]. These mutations are highly enriched in the DNA-binding domain (DBD) and are categorized into two distinct classes, namely DNA contact mutations (e.g., R273H, R248Q) that eliminate direct DNA contacts and conformational mutations (e.g., R175H, Y220C) that compromise the overall DBD architecture [3, 4].

Emerging evidence indicates that different p53 mutation subtypes promote tumorigenesis through distinct mechanisms [5]. For example, the R175H conformational mutant promotes metastasis by forming a complex with LSD2/BACH1 to activate pro-metastatic genes [6], while the R273H DNA contact mutant disrupts cell adhesion via SQSTM1/p62-mediated protein degradation [7]. These distinct oncogenic mechanisms underlie the observed differences in clinical prognosis and treatment response associated with different p53 mutations, highlighting the need for mutation-specific therapies [8, 9]. Notably, conformational mutations represent more tractable targets for pharmacological reactivation. Drugs like Rezatapopt (PC14586) can reactivate p53 by stabilizing the Y220C pocket [10], while arsenic trioxide (ATO) covalently binds to conformational mutations restoring their function [11]. However, these approaches are ineffective against DNA contact mutations, which represent the most common clinical variants, leaving a critical therapeutic gap. Therefore, deciphering the mechanisms underlying DNA contact mutation-associated cancers is imperative for developing effective targeted therapies.

The tumor suppressor BRCA2 safeguards genome integrity by facilitating DNA repair and protecting replication forks [12]. Importantly, alterations in BRCA2, which block DNA-repair pathways, are prevalent in various cancers [13]. Consequently, a synthetic lethal strategy utilizing poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors has been developed to target tumors with BRCA2 deficiency [14]. While PARP inhibitors demonstrate clinical efficacy, their long-term use is limited by acquired resistance when administered as maintenance therapy in nearly two-thirds of patients [15]. Therefore, identifying additional regulatory factors in BRCA2 deficiency is essential to elucidate its role in genomic instability and develop new treatment approaches. Emerging evidence indicates that BRCA2 deficiency drives pathological accumulation of R-loops, which are DNA-RNA hybrids with a displaced single-stranded DNA strand whose excessive accumulation compromises genomic stability [16]. Several regulators involved in BRCA2-mediated R-loop modulation have been investigated under diverse conditions [17]. For example, we previously demonstrated that ZFP281-BRCA2 prevents R-loop accumulation during DNA replication in mouse ES cells [18]. Nevertheless, how BRCA2 modulates R-loop dynamics remains incompletely understood; in particular, it is unclear whether this regulatory mechanism could be exploited to develop novel targeted strategies combining PARP inhibitors.

Here, we elucidate how p53 DNA contact mutations promote tumorigenesis by disrupting BRCA2 function. We show that mutant p53 (R273H/R248Q) forms aberrant condensates that sequester and displace BRCA2 from chromatin. This loss of BRCA2 chromatin binding leads to R-loop accumulation and compromises homologous recombination repair, thereby rendering cells hypersensitive to the PARP inhibitor Olaparib. We further identify the helicase DDX3X as a key factor in BRCA2-dependent R-loop resolution. Consequently, combined inhibition of PARP and DDX3X synergistically impairs colony formation in p53 DNA contact mutant tumor cells. Our findings define a targetable axis centered on p53 DNA contact mutant-mediated loss of BRCA2 chromatin binding and propose a novel therapeutic strategy for these cancers.

Results

p53 DNA contact mutations induce R-loop formation

To investigate the clinical significance of p53 mutation subtypes, we analyzed TCGA pan-cancer data through cBioPortal [19]. The analysis revealed that the p53-R248 and p53-R273 DNA contact mutations, along with the p53-R175 conformational mutation, were the most frequent p53 missense mutations (Fig. 1A). Strikingly, patients with R248 or R273 mutations exhibited significantly poorer overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) than those with R175 mutations and p53 wild-type (WT) (Fig. 1B). To further delineate the differences between p53 DNA contact and conformational mutations, we compared drug sensitivity profiles from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer database [20] of cell lines harboring p53-R248Q/R273H mutations to those with p53-R175H mutations. Interestingly, compared to cells with conformational mutations, cells with p53 DNA contact mutations exhibited marked differences in sensitivity specifically to inhibitors of TOP1, TOP2, and RNA helicases, agents that can affect R-loop dynamics [21–23] (Fig. 1C). In contrast, no significant differences were observed for inhibitors targeting other key cancer pathways, such as MEK, ERK or EGFR (Fig. 1C) [24, 25]. To validate this hypothesis, we performed shRNA-mediated knockdown of p53 in tumor cell lines harboring p53 DNA contact (SW1463: R248Q; SW620: R273H/P309S; PANC-1: R273H/C) or conformational (SK-BR-3: R175H) mutations. Knockdown of the mutant p53 in the DNA contact mutant lines (SW1463, SW620, and PANC-1) reduced the signal from the S9.6 antibody, which specifically detects DNA-RNA hybrids (R-loops) (Fig. 1D; Additional file 1: Fig. S1). The observed signal was abolished upon RNase H1 overexpression, confirming the specificity of the S9.6 antibody for R-loops (Fig. 1D). To directly compare the effects of p53-R175H and p53-R273H mutations, we employed prime editing [26] to introduce either the p53-R175H or R273H mutation into the endogenous TP53 locus of HCT116 cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A and B). The edited cell lines exhibited comparable proliferation to WT parental cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S2C). Importantly, immunofluorescence and dot blot analyses confirmed that the p53-R273H mutation specifically promoted R-loop formation, while the p53-R175H mutation did not (Fig. 1E; Additional file 1: Fig. S2D). Furthermore, rapid degradation of p53-R273H protein using the degradation tag (dTAG) system [27] substantially reduced R-loop levels (Additional file 1: Fig. S2E and F). Collectively, these results demonstrated that p53-R273H induces R-loop formation.

Previous research has shown that p53 mutations generally lead to a higher level of genomic instability than p53-WT [28]. Correspondingly, Western blot analysis demonstrated that both p53-R175H and p53-R273H mutant cells showed higher levels of γH2AX compared with p53-WT cells (Fig. 1F). Notably, only p53-R273H cells significantly reduced DNA damage after R-loop resolution by RNase H1 (Fig. 1F), suggesting that genomic instability in p53-R273H cells, but not in p53-WT or p53-R175H, was largely attributable to uncontrolled R-loops. Together, these experiments demonstrate that p53-R273H DNA contact mutation leads to R-loop accumulation, thereby promoting genomic instability.

The p53-R273H mutation impairs the chromatin enrichment of BRCA2

Homologous recombination repair (HRR) is a key mechanism for tolerating DNA damage and resolving R-loops [29]. Given the increased DNA damage and R-loop accumulation observed in cells harboring the p53-R273H mutation, we examined its impact on HRR efficiency. Using the direct-repeat GFP assay in HCT116 cell lines [30], we observed that p53-R273H cells showed a reduced capacity, whereas p53-R175H cells exhibited an increase (Fig. 2A). Notably, the reduced HRR observed in BRCA2 knockdown cells verified the reliability of our assay (Fig. 2A; Additional file 1: Fig. S3A). Given that BRCA2 deficiency is known to trigger both R-loop accumulation and HRR defects [17], we hypothesized that p53-R273H might induce genomic instability by interfering with BRCA2. To test this, we performed S9.6 CUT&Tag and BRCA2 ChIP-Seq in HCT116 cell lines. Our S9.6 CUT&Tag data showed strong concordance with published R-loop maps, confirming the reliability of our profiling data (Additional file 1: Fig. S3D). To define the relationship between BRCA2 binding and R-loops, we categorized genomic regions into three groups: co-occupied by BRCA2 and R-loops, BRCA2-only, and R-loop-only (Fig. 2B-D). Analysis of these categories revealed that at sites normally bound by BRCA2, p53-R273H mutation led to a marked decrease in BRCA2 occupancy alongside a significant increase in R-loop levels (Fig. 2B-D; Additional file 1: Fig. S3E and F). This inverse correlation indicates a functional link between BRCA2 displacement and R-loop accumulation at common genomic sites. We confirmed the R-loop increase at specific loci using S9.6 CUT&Tag–qPCR, with RNase H1 treatment as a negative control (Additional file 1: Fig. S3G). Consistently, Western blot analysis showed reduced chromatin-bound BRCA2 specifically in p53-R273H, but not p53-R175H, HCT116 cell lines (Additional file 1: Fig. S3H). Furthermore, depleting p53 increased chromatin-bound BRCA2 levels in cell lines harboring p53 DNA contact mutations (SW1463, SW620 and PANC-1), but not in the p53 conformational mutant cell line SK-BR-3 (Fig. 2E and F).

To test whether this mechanism extends beyond the p53-R273H mutant, we generated HCT116 cells with the p53-R248Q DNA contact mutation (Additional file 1: Fig. S4A). Similar to p53-R273H, the p53-R248Q DNA mutation induced R-loop accumulation and displaced BRCA2 from chromatin (Additional file 1: Fig. S4B and C). BRCA2 deficiency in tumor often confers sensitivity to PARP inhibitors [32]. Accordingly, colony formation assay revealed that p53-R248Q and p53-R273H mutations were more sensitive to Olaparib, a PARP inhibitor (Fig. 2G). These results indicated that p53 DNA contact mutations conferred a BRCAness phenotype [33]. Collectively, these results suggest that p53 DNA contact mutations may impair BRCA2 chromatin binding, thereby contributing to R-loop accumulation and HRR deficiency.

Reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation revealed that both the p53-R175H and p53-R273H mutants interacted more robustly with BRCA2 compared to p53-WT (Fig. 3A and B). p53 can undergo liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) through its DBD, and point mutations within this domain alter its phase behavior [35]. Specifically, the p53-R273H mutant exhibits distinct biophysical properties, including a higher intrinsic aggregation propensity, compared to p53-WT and p53-R175H mutants [36]. Therefore, we investigated whether p53 mutations could sequester BRCA2 via phase separation. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that only p53-R273H co-formed condensates with BRCA2 (Fig. 3D and E). To map the BRCA2 domain required for this co-condensation, we generated a series of mCherry-fused BRCA2 truncations. Cells expressing the BRCA2-F2 fragment (amino acids 271–552) exhibited nuclear fluorescent puncta (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A-C). Notably, the F2 domain closely resembled the region in the N terminus of BRCA2 that exhibits DNA binding activity (amino acids 250–500) [37]. p53-DBD-R273H induced the formation of significantly larger BRCA2-F2 puncta compared to p53-DBD-WT or p53-DBD-R175H (Fig. 3F and G), suggesting that p53-R273H may disrupt BRCA2 function through this domain.

We next reconstituted the p53-BRCA2 interaction in vitro using purified BRCA2-F2-mCherry and p53-DBD-GFP proteins (WT/R175H/R273H) (Additional file 1: Fig. S5D). The p53-DBD-R273H proteins alone aggregated at lower concentrations than p53-DBD-WT or the p53-DBD-R175H proteins (Additional file 1: Fig. S6A). Critically, when mixed with BRCA2-F2, p53-DBD-R273H, but not p53-DBD-WT or p53-DBD-R175H, incorporated BRCA2-F2 into large aberrant aggregates at lower concentrations (Fig. 3H; Additional file 1: Fig. S6B and C). Consistent with this observation, electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) revealed that p53-DBD-R273H specifically inhibited the binding of BRCA2-F2 to R-loop (Fig. 3I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that p53-R273H mediates aberrant co-condensation with BRCA2, directly linking this p53 DNA contact mutant to the loss of BRCA2 at chromatin.

DDX3X facilitates BRCA2-dependent R-loop resolution

To identify regulators of the observed R-loop accumulation, we performed BioID proximity-labeling with p53-WT and p53-R273H, followed by mass spectrometry (Fig. 4A; Additional file 1: Fig. S7A and B; Additional file 2: Table S1). Among the enriched interactors of p53-R273H were known partners such as MCM7, G3BP1, and TOPBP1, validating the approach (Fig. 4A) [28, 38, 39]. Gene ontology analysis highlighted RNA-binding and helicase functions (Additional file 1: Fig. S7C). Notably, the R-loop-resolving helicase DDX3X showed markedly enhanced binding to p53 mutants (Fig. 4A). Given reports of a BRCA2-DDX3X interaction [17, 40], we hypothesized DDX3X is an R-loop regulator within this axis. Overexpression and endogenous co-immunoprecipitation confirmed that both p53-R273H and R175H strengthened the p53-DDX3X interaction (Fig. 4B and C), analogous to the enhanced binding of BRCA2 to p53 mutants described above, whereas the BRCA2-DDX3X interaction itself was not obviously affected by p53 status (Additional file 1: Fig. S7D). We then asked if p53-BRCA2 condensates affect DDX3X. When DDX3X was co-expressed with p53-DBD (WT/R175H/R273H) and BRCA2-F2, p53-DBD-R273H induced the formation of enlarged nuclear puncta containing all three proteins (Additional file 1: Fig. S8A). To determine the functional consequence, we mapped DDX3X binding in HCT116 cells. DDX3X CUT&Tag revealed that p53-R273H reduced DDX3X occupancy specifically at genomic sites co-bound by BRCA2 (Fig. 4E and F; Additional file 1: Fig. S8B). Binding was also reduced at BRCA2-independent sites, possibly due to sequestration into aberrant aggregates. Consistently, depletion of DDX3X increased R-loop levels at sites where DDX3X binds (Fig. 4G and H; Additional file 1: Fig. S8C, D), and dot blot confirmed DDX3X’s role in suppressing R-loops (Additional file 1: Fig. S8E). Moreover, acute degradation of BRCA2 diminished DDX3X chromatin binding (Additional file 1: Fig. S8F), and BRCA2 depletion impaired DDX3X's association with R-loops (Additional file 1: Fig. S8G). Therefore, BRCA2 promotes DDX3X recruitment to chromatin to resolve R-loops. The p53-R273H mutation subverts this process by sequestering both BRCA2 and DDX3X into dysfunctional condensates, thereby compromising R-loop homeostasis.

Targeting the BRCA2-DDX3X-R-loop axis in cancers with p53 DNA contact mutations

Our findings demonstrate that p53-R273H disrupts the BRCA2-DDX3X functional axis, leading to pathological R-loop accumulation. We therefore investigated the clinical relevance of BRCA2 and DDX3X expression in tumors harboring p53 DNA contact mutations. Analysis of TCGA pan-cancer datasets revealed that low BRCA2 expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis specifically in patients with p53-R248/R273 mutations, but not in those with p53-R175 mutations (Fig. 5A). Correspondingly, long-term survivors with p53-R248/R273 mutations exhibited a higher frequency of BRCA2 copy number gains and elevated BRCA2 expression levels (Fig. 5B). For DDX3X, we observed a trend wherein higher expression correlated with better outcomes in the p53 DNA contact mutation cohort (Fig. 5C), and similar to BRCA2, DDX3X copy number gains and overexpression were more prevalent in survivors (Fig. 5D). Given the poor prognosis associated with p53-R248/R273 mutations, we sought to translate our mechanistic insights into therapeutic strategies. Based on our findings that p53 DNA contact mutations confer a "BRCAness" phenotype with sensitivity to PARP inhibition (Fig. 2G), and that DDX3X is integral to the BRCA2-dependent R-loop resolution pathway (Fig. 4), we hypothesized that simultaneously targeting both vulnerabilities could yield enhanced efficacy. We tested the combination of the PARP inhibitor Olaparib and the DDX3X inhibitor RK-33 [41, 42]. Consistent with this, colony formation assays confirmed that p53 DNA contact mutant cells (R248Q, R273H) were particularly vulnerable to RK-33 monotherapy (Fig. 5E, F). Strikingly, the combination of RK-33 and Olaparib synergistically impaired the viability of p53-R273H mutant cells (Fig. 5E, F). Drug synergy analysis using the Highest Single Agent (HSA) model confirmed significant synergistic interaction (HSA synergy score > 10) in p53-R248Q and p53-R273H cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S8H) [43]. Collectively, these findings suggest that targeting the BRCA2-DDX3X-R-loop axis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for cancers harboring p53 DNA contact mutations.

Discussion

Mounting evidence indicates that mutant p53 proteins gain oncogenic function and contribute to genomic instability [44]. A critical unresolved question, however, is how different mutation subtypes drive tumorigenesis. Our study reveals that p53 DNA contact mutants (e.g., R273H, R248Q) induce genomic instability by forming aberrant condensates that sequester BRCA2 and DDX3X from chromatin (Fig. 5G). This sequestration leads to unresolved R-loop accumulation, homologous recombination deficiency, and a “BRCAness” phenotype. Notably, this axis presents a therapeutic vulnerability, as cells harboring these mutations show marked sensitivity to combined PARP and DDX3X inhibition.

Our findings provide a mechanistic explanation for the clinically observed aggressiveness of tumors with p53 DNA contact mutations like R273H [9, 45]. Despite showing enhanced physical interaction with BRCA2—a feature shared with the conformational mutant R175H—only the R273H mutant disrupts BRCA2's chromatin localization. This specificity can be attributed to the unique biophysical properties of the R273H mutant, which exhibits a higher intrinsic aggregation propensity [36], leading to the formation of larger, dysfunctional condensates that effectively sequester BRCA2. This model of mutation-specific dysfunction is supported by emerging literature. For instance, while both p53 DNA contact and conformational mutants show increased binding to TOPBP1, only p53 DNA contact mutants inhibit its recruitment to chromatin [39]. Similarly, murine models show that different p53 mutants (R172H and R245W) can cooperate with the same cofactor (NR5A2) yet drive distinct transcriptional programs and tumor outcomes [46]. Consistent with this aggregation-driven effect, our BioID assay revealed that p53-R273H aggregates may also enhance interactions with additional proteins, mirroring data from polyQ-expanded Huntingtin, which similarly yields an expanded set of aggregate-specific interactors [47]. The link between p53-R273H‑enhanced protein interactions and aberrant aggregation, and its functional consequences, requires further investigation.

Notably, the R-loop dysregulation induced by p53 DNA contact mutants occurs via a gain-of-function mechanism distinct from the role of wild-type p53 in R-loop suppression by preventing aberrant satellite or non-coding RNA transcription [48, 49]. Instead, it directly incapacitates the tumor suppressor BRCA2, whose dysfunction is a known cause of pathological R-loop accumulation [16]. Our prior work identified ZFP281 as a recruiter of BRCA2 to suppress R-loops [18]. Here, we expand this regulatory landscape by characterizing the DEAD-box helicase DDX3X as a critical BRCA2-interacting effector in R-loop resolution. Although DDX3X mutations are linked to genomic instability in cancer [50], and its ability to unwind RNA–DNA hybrids has been noted [51, 52], its functional partnership with BRCA2 has been unclear. Our epistasis analysis places BRCA2 and DDX3X in the same pathway for R-loop homeostasis. Furthermore, we show that BRCA2 facilitates the recruitment of DDX3X to chromatin, and conversely, p53-R273H-induced condensates co-sequester both proteins, dismantling this protective axis. This reveals a unified mechanism for genomic instability in cancers with dysfunction in either p53 (DNA contact mutants), BRCA2, or DDX3X.

Therapeutic strategies have emerged for p53 conformational mutants (e.g., Rezatapopt, ATO) [10, 11], but p53 DNA contact mutations remain a critical unmet need due to structural resistance [4, 53]. Our study addresses this by mapping a targetable synthetic lethal network downstream of these mutants. We demonstrate that p53 DNA contact mutations create a “BRCAness” state, conferring PARP inhibitor sensitivity. Furthermore, by identifying DDX3X as a core component of the same compromised BRCA2 pathway, we reveal a second node of vulnerability. The synergistic efficacy of combining Olaparib with the DDX3X inhibitor RK-33 validates this dual-targeting strategy. Thus, rather than attempting to rescue the dysfunctional p53 DNA contact mutants themselves, we propose that targeting the BRCA2-DDX3X-R-loop axis offers a promising and mutation-specific therapeutic blueprint for these high-risk cancers.

Conclusions

This study suggests a BRCA2–DDX3X–R-loop axis downstream of p53 DNA contact mutants, wherein aberrant condensate formation drives genomic instability and PARP inhibitor hypersensitivity. Moreover, combined PARP and DDX3X inhibition may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for these aggressive cancers.

Methods

Cell culture

HCT116, PANC-1, SK-BR-3, SW620, HeLa and HEK293T cell lines were purchased from American Type Cell Culture (ATCC), SW1463 was purchased from Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) Typical Culture Collection Committee (Shanghai, China) and cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Ex-Cell Bio) and 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin (HyClone) in the presence of 5% CO2 (v/v) at 37 °C. All cell lines used in our study were authenticated by STR profiling and free from mycoplasma contamination.

Antibodies

The antibodies to FLAG (HA601080) and RAD51 (ET1705-9) were obtained from HUABIO. The antibodies to γH2AX (sc-517348), GAPDH (sc-365062) and dsDNA (sc-58749) were obtained from Santa Cruz. The antibodies to H3 (ab1791) and Anti-Streptavidin (ab319103) were obtained from Abcam. The antibody to BRCA2 (A303-434A) for WB was obtained from Bethyl Laboratories. The antibodies to p53 (A10610) and DDX3X (A5637) were obtained from ABclonal. The antibody to HA (M20003) was obtained from Abmart. The antibody to Tubulin (66,031) was obtained from Proteintech. The antibody to BRCA2 for ChIP was generated in house. The S9.6 antibody (ENH001) to R-loop was obtained from Kerafast.

Genome editing for p53 mutant endogenous knock-in

To generate gene-targeted alleles carrying p53 gene alleles with R175H, R248Q and R273H mutations in HCT116 cells, prime editing technology was employed using the PE3 system. Briefly, the pegRNA-p53 spacer sequence and 3′ extension sequence were designed using the prime-editing guide RNAs design tool (http://pegfinder.sidichenlab.org/) (Additional file 2: Table S2) [26]. Following transfection, cells were selected in DMEM containing 2 mg/mL puromycin. After 2 days of selection, surviving cells were expanded to several 10-cm dishes by limiting dilution and maintained in DMEM. Individual clones were picked and cultured in 24-well dishes, and positive clones were identified by PCR followed by sequencing.

Genome editing for dTAG endogenous knock-in

To generate p53-dTAG cells via endogenous knock-in, HCT116 cells were co-transfected with PITCh plasmids (sgRNA cutting and microhomology-containing dTAG repair template plasmid) [27]. After recovering for 2 days without antibiotic selection, cells were subjected to serial dilution and selected with 1 μg/mL puromycin for 10–14 days. Single colonies were picked, expanded, and genotyped by PCR of genomic DNA targeting the integration site. For homozygous knock-in clones, protein degradation efficiency was validated by Western blot analysis after a 3-h treatment with dTAG-13.

Lentivirus-mediated RNAi

Human p53, DDX3X and BRCA2 shRNA constructs were cloned into the pLKO.1 vector. The NonT shRNA construct (SHC002) was purchased from Sigma. Lentiviral particle preparation and infection were performed as previously described [54]. Twenty-four hours after infection, the cells were selected with 2 μg/mL of puromycin for an additional 48 h.

HR reporter assay

HCT116 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected with 800 ng of pDR-GFP (Addgene plasmid #26,475) and 800 ng of pCBASceI (Addgene plasmid #26,477) using a standard transfection reagent to induce a site-specific double-strand break at the I-SceI recognition site within the DR-GFP reporter. To monitor transfection efficiency, 800 ng of pCDNA5-mCherry was co-transfected as a control. 48 h post-transfection, homologous recombination (HR) efficiency was quantified by flow cytometry (FACS), and normalized to the ratio of GFP-positive to mCherry-positive cells.

Auxin-inducible degron (AID) system

BRCA2 CT sgRNA oligos were cloned into pX330. Homology arms flanking the target site in the last exon of BRCA2 were amplified by PCR from human genomic DNA. To fuse the AID cassette to the C-terminus of BRCA2, HCT116 cells with a TIR1-expressing cassette were co-transfected with the pX330 BRCA2 CT sgRNA construct and the homology arms, followed by selection with hygromycin until cell clones were ready to be picked. The clones were screened by PCR, and confirmed by TA cloning and sequencing.

S9.6 immunofluorescence

To detect R-loop foci, cells grown on glass coverslips were pre-extracted with ice-cold 0.2% Triton X-100 in 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 5 min at room temperature (RT), and then fixed with freshly prepared PTMEF buffer (4% paraformaldehyde, 200 mM PIPES, pH 6.8, 200 mM MgCl2, 10 mM EGTA, pH 8.0, 0.2% Triton X-100) for 10 min at RT as previously described [55]. After washing with 1 × PBS, coverslips were blocked with 1% (w/v) BSA in 1 × PBS for 30 min at RT. Next, coverslips were incubated with S9.6 antibody overnight at 4 ℃, subsequently washed with 1 × PBS, and incubated with appropriate Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT.

Immunostaining

For γH2AX and RAD51 staining, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in 1 × PBS. After washing with 1 × PBS, cells were blocked with 1% (w/v) BSA in 1 × PBS for 30 min at RT, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing with 1 × PBS, cells were incubated with appropriate Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT.

Dot blot

Cells were permeabilized with cold cell lysis buffer (5 mM PIPES pH 8.0, 80 mM KCl, 0.5% NP-40), followed by nuclear lysis buffer (1% SDS, 25 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 5 mM EDTA) with proteinase K digestion at 55 °C for 5 h. Then nucleic acids were extracted by phenol–chloroform and precipitated with ethanol. A total of 200 ng of DNA was blotted onto nylon membrane (Roche, 11,417,240,001). After UV crosslinking, the membrane was blocked in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) containing 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 h at RT. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated overnight with the primary antibody. After washing with TBST, the membrane was incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at RT [56].

Chromatin-bound proteins isolation

Cells were harvested, washed with cold 1 × PBS, and were resuspended in buffer A (10 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.34 M sucrose, 10% glycerol, 1 mM DTT) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma). Triton X-100 was added to the cell suspension to a final concentration of 0.1%, followed by incubation on ice for 5 min. Nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation at 1,300 g for 5 min at 4 °C. Nuclei were washed once in buffer A, and then lysed in no-salt buffer B (0.2 mM EGTA, 3 mM EDTA and 1 mM DTT, protease inhibitors as described above) and incubated on ice for 30 min and the insoluble chromatin was collected by centrifugation and the final chromatin pellet was washed with buffer B prior to Western blotting.

ChIP and ChIP-seq library preparation

ChIP assays were performed as described [18]. Briefly, crosslinked chromatin was sonicated into fragments of 200–800 bp using a Bioruptor (Diagenode) in ChIP lysis buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM EGTA, 0.1% Na-Deoxycholate, 0.5% N-lauroylsarcosine) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma). Chromatin extracts were incubated with specific antibody and protein A agarose beads (Smart-Lifesciences, SA01501L) overnight at 4 °C. Immunoprecipitates were washed five times with RIPA buffer (50 mM HEPES–KOH pH 7.5, 500 mM LiCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1.0% NP-40, 0.7% Na-Deoxycholate) and twice with TE. After the final wash, DNA was eluted and reverse-crosslinked at 65 °C. DNA was then purified and used as a template for qPCR or for ChIP-seq library preparation. Libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal DNA Library Prep Kit (Vazyme, ND607) for further next-generation sequencing.

CUT&Tag

CUT&Tag experiments were performed as described previously [57]. Briefly, 1 × 105 cells were harvested, washed with wash buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM spermidine), and immobilized on Con A MagPoly Beads (Smart-Lifesciences, SM041001) by incubation at RT for 10 min. The bead-bound cells were incubated in 50 μL of primary antibody buffer (wash buffer supplemented with 1% BSA, 2 mM EDTA and 0.05% digitonin) containing 1 μg of S9.6 or DDX3X antibody for 2 h at RT. A total of 10 μg of RNase H1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added together with S9.6 as a negative control. Cells were washed three times briefly with 800 μL of dig-wash buffer (wash buffer with 0.05% digitonin) to remove unbound antibodies. Subsequently, cells were incubated with a 1:100 dilution of either an anti-mouse (Sigma, M7023) or anti-rabbit (Sigma, R2655) secondary antibody in 50 μL of dig-wash buffer for 1 h at RT with slow rotation. After washing with dig-wash buffer as above, cells were resuspended in 100 μL of dig-300 buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl and 0.5 mM spermidine, 1% BSA and 0.01% digitonin) containing a 1:100 dilution of pA-Tn5 adapter complex and incubated for 1 h at RT with slow rotation. The pA-Tn5-bound cells were washed three times with 800 μL of dig-300 buffer, followed by tagmentation in 40 μL of tagmentation buffer (10 mM TAPS-NaOH at pH 8.5, 10 mM MgCl2 and 7.5% DMF) for 1 h at 37 °C. After tagmentation, 1.5 μL of 0.5 M EDTA, 0.5 μL of 10% SDS and 0.5 μL of 20 mg/mL proteinase K were added to the reaction mixture, which was then incubated for 1 h at 55 °C to stop reaction and digest proteins. Genomic DNA was extracted and purified using DNA Extract Beads (Cytiva, 65,152,105,050,250). To generate R-loop libraries, purified genomic DNA was amplified with Phanta Flash Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme, P521) using a universal i5 primer and barcoded i7 primer. The library PCR products were extracted and purified with DNA Extract Beads.

Recombinant protein purification

Briefly, expression of BRCA2-F2-mCherry or p53-DBD-GFP (WT/R175H/R273H) in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells was induced with 0.2 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 16 °C overnight. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and stored at −80 °C. To purify the recombinant proteins, cell pellets from 500 mL of culture were resuspended in 40 mL of lysis buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4 pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, and 0.05% Tween 20) supplemented with phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and homogenized using a high-pressure homogenizer. The lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 20,000 g for 1 h at 4 °C. Pre-equilibrated Ni–nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) agarose (1 mL; QIAGEN) was added to the cleared lysates and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The lysates-agarose slurry was washed five times with 10 mL of wash buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4 pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole, and 0.05% Tween 20). Proteins were eluted with 5 mL of elution buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4 pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 250 mM imidazole, and 0.05% Tween 20). The purified recombinant proteins were analyzed by SDS–PAGE with Coomassie blue staining. Recombinant proteins were concentrated and desalted using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters (Merck Millipore) into buffer D (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM DTT).

In vitro droplet assay

Recombinant proteins at specified concentrations were mixed in buffer D supplemented with 10% of the crowding agent PEG-8000. After incubation at RT for 30 min, the mixture was applied to a glass coverslip and imaged using a Zeiss microscope.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

R-loop substrates were prepared by annealing 10 μM of biotin-labeled oAC864 (RNA) with 10 μM of DNA oligo oAC862 (Additional file 2: Table S2) and 10 μM of DNA oligo oAC863 Additional file 2: Table S2) in annealing buffer (1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.6). The mixture was heated to 95 °C for 2 min and then slowly cooled to RT. To assess protein binding, 100 nM of the annealed R-loop substrates were incubated with the indicated concentrations of purified proteins in binding buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.6, 25 mM KCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT) at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction products were separated by electrophoresis on a 4% native PAGE in 0.5 × TBE buffer at 80 V for 60 min at RT. After film transfer and UV fixation, the nylon membrane was blocked for 1 h at RT. Following washes, the membrane was incubated with Streptavidin-HRP conjugate antibody.

BioID proteomics

The BioID procedure was performed as previously described [58]. When cells reached approximately 80% confluency, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 50 μM biotin (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were then incubated with biotin for 16 h. Subsequently, cells were washed twice with 1× PBS and resuspended in lysis buffer (8 M urea, 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were treated with Pierce Universal Nuclease (Vazyme) for 10 min and then sonicated. After ultracentrifugation, the clarified lysates were precleared with Gelatin Sepharose Beads (Smart-Lifesciences) and then incubated with streptavidin-conjugated Sepharose Beads (Sigma-Aldrich) at 4 °C for 4 h. The beads were spun down and washed four more times with wash buffer (8 M urea, 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4). Finally, bound proteins were eluted by resuspending the beads in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate containing 1 mM biotin and subjected to liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analysis.

S9.6 immunoprecipitation

HCT116 cells were washed twice with 1 × PBS and lysed on ice for 30 min in NETN buffer (100 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, and 0.5% NP-40) supplemented with a proteinase inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were sonicated and clarified by centrifugation at 20,000 g for 10 min at 4 ℃. The supernatant was then incubated with either S9.6 antibody or normal mouse IgG at 4 ℃ overnight, followed by incubating with protein A agarose beads for 3 h at 4 ℃. Beads were washed three times with NETN, boiled in 2 × SDS loading buffer and analyzed by SDS–PAGE.

Immunoprecipitations and Western blotting

For transient transfection and co-immunoprecipitation assays, HEK293T cells were transfected with the indicated plasmids. Cells were lysed in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 420 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA and 0.5% NP-40) supplemented with proteinase inhibitors (Sigma), for 30 min at 4 °C. The lysates were sonicated and clarified by centrifugation at 20,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then adjusted to a final NaCl concentration of 150 mM by adding the balance buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA and 0.5% NP-40). The supernatant was then incubated with antibodies and protein A agarose beads overnight at 4 ℃. Subsequently, the beads were washed three times with wash buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA and 0.5% NP-40), and boiled in 2 × SDS loading buffer and analyzed by SDS–PAGE.

Clonogenic assay

For the clonogenic assay, 400 cells per well were seeded into six-well plates and treated as indicated. After 14 days of incubation, colonies were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet dye (Sangon Biotech) dissolved in 2% ethanol. HSA scores were calculated using SynergyFinder [43].

Kaplan–Meier plot analysis

Clinical data were retrieved from the TCGA PanCancer Atlas cohort using the cBioPortal platform. Kaplan–Meier estimates were used to assess overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Patients were stratified into high and low expression groups based on the mRNA expression Z-scores (log RNA-Seq RSEM, relative to diploid normal samples). A Z-score threshold of 3 was applied to define the high BRCA2 expression group, whereas a threshold of −0.2 was used for the high DDX3X expression group.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Data are presented as Mean ± SEM. The statistical tests employed, including one-way ANOVA and Student's t-test, were chosen based on the experimental design and data characteristics unless otherwise specified. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001). Unless otherwise indicated, each experiment was performed with three independent replicates.

Next generation sequencing analysis

For ChIP-seq analysis, adaptors and low-quality reads were removed using Trim Galore v0.6.7 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/) with the parameter ‘‘-q 20’’. Clean reads were aligned to the human hg19 genome using Bowtie2 (v.2.5.1) with default parameters. PCR duplicates were removed by Picard 2.27.4 (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Peak calling was performed with MACS2 (v.2.2.7.1) for BRCA2 peaks with a p-value cutoff of 1e-5. For CUT&Tag analysis, adapters and low-quality reads were trimmed as described for ChIP above and the resulting reads were aligned to human hg19 genome using Bowtie (v.2.5.1) with the options ‘–local –very-sensitive –no-mixed –no-discordant -I 10 -X 700’. For spike-in normalization, the reads were also aligned to the E. coli genome or to spike-in DNA (Vazyme #TD904) using Bowtie2 with the options ‘–local –very-sensitive –no-overlap –no-dovetail –no-mixed –no-discordant -I 10 -X 700’. Duplicated reads were removed with Picard Tools. Peak calling was performed using SEACR with relaxed parameters. Heatmaps and metaplots of ChIP-seq and CUT&Tag signal were generated with deepTools v3.5.1 computeMatrix, plotHeatmap, and plotProfile. Normalized bigwig files and log₂-transformed fold-change track were created using bamCoverage and bigwigCompare from the deepTools v3.5.1.
