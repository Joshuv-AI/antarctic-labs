Counting 3-way contingency tables via quiver semi-invariants
Calin Chindris, Deepanshu Prasad

Abstract
. Let 𝐓 𝐚 , 𝐛 \mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}} be the number of 3 3 -way contingency tables of size m × n × p m\times n\times p with two of its three plane-sum margins fixed by 𝐚 = ( a 1 , … , a m ) ∈ ℕ m \mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m} and 𝐛 = ( b 1 , … , b n ) ∈ ℕ n \mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n} . When p = 1 p=1 , this is the number of m × n m\times n non-negative integer matrices whose row and column sums are fixed by 𝐚 \mathbf{a} and 𝐛 \mathbf{b} . In this paper, we study the numbers 𝐓 𝐚 , 𝐛 \mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}} through the lens of quiver invariant theory. Let 𝒬 m , n p \mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}^{p}_{m,n} be the p p -complete bipartite quiver with m m source vertices, n n sink vertices, and p p arrows from each source to each sink. Let 𝟏 \mathbf{1} denote the dimension vector of 𝒬 m , n p \mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}^{p}_{m,n} that takes value 1 1 at every vertex of 𝒬 m , n p \mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}^{p}_{m,n} , and let θ 𝐚 , 𝐛 \theta_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}} denote the integral weight that assigns a i a_{i} to the i t ​ h i^{th} source vertex and − b j -b_{j} to the j t ​ h j^{th} sink vertex of 𝒬 m , n p \mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}^{p}_{m,n} . We begin by realizing 𝐓 𝐚 , 𝐛 \mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}} as the dimension of the space of semi-invariants associated to ( 𝒬 m , n p , 𝟏 , θ 𝐚 , 𝐛 ) (\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}^{p}_{m,n},\mathbf{1},\theta_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}) . Using this connection and methods from quiver invariant theory, we show that 𝐓 𝐚 , 𝐛 \mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}} is a parabolic Kostka coefficient. In the case p = 1 p=1 , this recovers the formula for the number of the m × n m\times n contingency tables with row and column sums fixed by 𝐚 \mathbf{a} and 𝐛 \mathbf{b} , which in the classical 2 2 -way setting can also be obtained via the Robinson-Schensted-Knuth correspondence.

1.
Introduction

Let
m
,
n
, and
p
be positive integers, and let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
and
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
be two vectors with non-negative integer coefficients such that

N:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
be the number of all
3
-way contingency tables
X=(x_{ijk})_{(i,j,k)\in[m]\times[n]\times[p]}
whose entries
x_{i,j,k}
are non-negative integers such that

(1)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{p}x_{ijk}=a_{i},\forall i\in[m],

and

(2)

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{p}x_{ijk}=b_{j},\forall j\in[n].

In what follows,
K_{\lambda,\operatorname{\mathcal{R}}}
denotes the parabolic Kostka coefficient associated to a partition
\lambda
and a sequence
\operatorname{\mathcal{R}}=\{R_{1},\ldots,R_{s}\}
of rectangular partitions. Thus
K_{\lambda,\operatorname{\mathcal{R}}}
is the multiplicity of the irreducible representation of
\operatorname{GL}(r)
of highest weight
\lambda
in the tensor product of the irreducible representations with highest weights
R_{1},\ldots,R_{s}
. We assume that
\lambda
has at most
r
non-zero parts and that each
R_{i}
has height at most
r
.

Theorem 1.1
.

With the notation as above,

(3)

\mathbf{T}_{a,b}=K_{\lambda,\operatorname{\mathcal{R}}},

where
\lambda=((pN)^{pm})
and
\operatorname{\mathcal{R}}
is the following sequence of rectangular partitions

(4)

\operatorname{\mathcal{R}}=\{\underbrace{(N^{(p-1)m}),\ldots,(N^{(p-1)m})}_{p\text{~times}},(a_{1}^{pm-1}),\ldots,(a_{m}^{pm-1}),(b_{1}),\ldots,(b_{n})\}.

To prove our formula, we first express
\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
as the dimension of a weight space of semi-invariants for a
p
-complete bipartite quiver. We then apply general reduction techniques, such as the Embedding Theorem
2.6
for quiver semi-invariants, to reduce the problem to computing semi-invariants for a star quiver. These, in turn, can be expressed as parabolic Kostka numbers.

We note that when
p=1
,
\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
is the number of
m\times n
non-negative integer matrices whose row and column sums are fixed by
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
. In this case,
(\ref{eqn:main-formula-parab-Kostka})
recovers the classical formula arising from the Robinson–Schensted–Knuth correspondence, but by a quiver-invariant-theoretic argument that does not use the correspondence itself; see Corollary
3.2
for full details.

2.
Weight spaces of quiver semi-invariants: the tools

Throughout, we work over the field
\mathbb{C}
of complex numbers and denote by
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\dots\}
. For a positive integer
L
, we denote by
[L]=\{1,\ldots,L\}
.

A
quiver

\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}=(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1},t,h)
consists of two finite sets
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
(vertices) and
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}
(arrows) together with two maps
t:\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}\to\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
(tail) and
h:\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}\to\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
(head). We represent
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
as a directed graph with set of vertices
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
and directed edges
a:ta\to ha
for every
a\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}
.

A
representation
of
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
is a family
V=(V_{x},V_{a})_{x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0},a\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}}
, where
V_{x}
is a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector space for every
x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
, and
V_{a}:V_{ta}\to V_{ha}
is a
\mathbb{C}
-linear map for every
a\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}
. After fixing bases for the vector spaces
V_{x}
,
x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
, we often think of the linear maps
V_{a}
,
a\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}
, as matrices of appropriate size.

A
morphism

\varphi:V\rightarrow W
between two representations is a collection
(\varphi_{x}))_{x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}
of
\mathbb{C}
-linear maps with
\varphi_{x}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{x},W_{x})
for every
x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
, and such that
\varphi_{ha}\circ V_{a}=W_{a}\circ\varphi_{ta}
for every
a\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}
. The
\mathbb{C}
-vector space of all morphisms from
V
to
W
is denoted by
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}}(V,W)
.

The
dimension vector

\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}V\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}
of a representation
V
is defined by
(\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}V)_{x}:=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{x}
for all
x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
. By a
dimension vector
of
Q
, we simply mean an
\mathbb{N}
-valued function on the set of vertices
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
. We say a dimension vector
\beta
is
sincere
if
\beta_{x}>0
for every
x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
. The simple dimension vector at
x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
, denoted by
e_{x}
, is defined by
e_{x}(y)=\delta_{x,y}
,
\forall y\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
, where
\delta_{x,y}
is the Kronecker symbol.

The
Euler form
(also known as the Ringel form) of
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
is the bilinear form on
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}
defined by

\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle:=\sum_{x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}\alpha_{x}\beta_{x}-\sum_{a\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}}\alpha_{ta}\beta_{ha},\;\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}.

From now on, we assume that all of our quivers are finite, connected, and acyclic. Then, for any integral weight
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}
, there exists a unique
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}
such that
\sigma_{x}=\langle\alpha,e_{x}\rangle
,
\forall x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
.

Let
\beta
be a sincere dimension vector of a quiver
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
, and let us consider the
representation space
of
\beta
-dimensional representations of
Q
,

\operatorname{rep}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta):=\prod_{a\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{\beta_{ha}\times\beta_{ta}}.

The base change group
\operatorname{GL}(\beta):=\prod_{x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}\operatorname{GL}(\beta_{x})
acts on
\operatorname{rep}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)
by simultaneous conjugation,
i.e.
, for
g=(g_{x})_{x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}\in\operatorname{GL}(\beta)
and
W=(W_{a})_{a\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}}\in\operatorname{rep}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)
, we define
g\cdot W\in\operatorname{rep}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)
by

(g\cdot W)_{a}:=g_{ha}\cdot W_{a}\cdot g_{ta}^{-1},\;\forall a\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}.

This action descends to that of the subgroup

\operatorname{SL}(\beta):=\prod_{x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}\operatorname{SL}(\beta_{x}),

giving rise to a highly non-trivial ring of semi-invariants
\operatorname{SI}(Q,\beta):=\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{rep}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)]^{\operatorname{SL}(\beta)}
. (We point out that since
Q
is assumed to be acyclic, the invariant ring
\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{rep}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)]^{\operatorname{GL}(\beta)}
is precisely
\mathbb{C}
.) Since
\operatorname{GL}(\beta)
is linearly reductive and
\operatorname{SL}(\beta)
is its commutator subgroup, we have the weight space decomposition

\operatorname{SI}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)=\bigoplus_{\chi\in X^{*}(\operatorname{GL}(\beta))}\operatorname{SI}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)_{\chi},

where
X^{*}(\operatorname{GL}(\beta))
is the group of rational characters of
\operatorname{GL}(\beta)
and

\operatorname{SI}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)_{\chi}:=\{f\in\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{rep}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)]\mid g\cdot f=\chi(g)f,\,\forall g\in\operatorname{GL}(\beta)\}

is the space of
semi-invariants of weight
\chi
.

Every
integral weight

\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}
defines a character
\chi_{\sigma}
of
\operatorname{GL}(\beta)
by

(5)

\chi_{\sigma}(g):=\prod_{x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}(\det g_{x})^{\sigma_{x}}\qquad\text{for all }g=(g_{x})_{x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}\in\operatorname{GL}(\beta).

Moreover, every character of
\operatorname{GL}(\beta)
is of the form
\chi_{\sigma}
for some integral weight
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}
. If
\beta
is sincere, this gives a one-to-one correspondence, so we may identify the character group with
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}
. In what follows, we write
\operatorname{SI}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)_{\sigma}
for
\operatorname{SI}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)_{\chi_{\sigma}}
.

We state a reduction tool that will come on handy when proving Theorem
1.1
. It allows us to remove a vertex of weight zero, provided its dimension is at least that of the head of the unique outgoing arrow.

Lemma 2.1

(
Removing vertices of zero weight
; see Lemma 4.6 in
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
be a quiver and
v_{0}
a vertex such that near
v_{0}
,
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
looks like:

v_{0}
w
v_{1}
v_{\ell}
\vdots
a_{1}
a_{\ell}
b

Suppose that
\beta
is a dimension vector and
\sigma
is a weight such that

\beta(v_{0})\geq\beta(w)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma(v_{0})=0.

Let
\overline{Q}
be the quiver defined by
\overline{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}}_{0}=\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}\setminus\{v_{0}\}
and

\overline{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}}_{1}=\bigl(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}\setminus\{b,a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}\}\bigr)\,\cup\,\{ba_{1},\dots,ba_{\ell}\}.

If
\overline{\beta}=\beta|_{\overline{Q}}
and
\overline{\sigma}=\sigma|_{\overline{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}}}
are the restrictions

\operatorname{SI}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)_{\sigma}\;\cong\;\operatorname{SI}(\overline{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}},\overline{\beta})_{\overline{\sigma}}.

2.1.
Network flow polytopes

When the dimension vector is equal to one at every vertex of
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
, one can describe the dimensions of the corresponding spaces of semi-invariants in terms of network flows. This description that allows us to express
\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
as dimensions of weight spaces of quiver semi-invariants.

Let
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}
be an integral weight of
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
. The
network flow polytope
associated to
(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\sigma)
is defined by

(6)

\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}:=\left\{(x_{a})_{a\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}}_{\geq 0}\;\middle|\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}\\

ha=x\end{subarray}}x_{a}=\sigma_{x}\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}\right\}.

Lemma 2.3

(
Network flows from quiver semi-invariants
; see Lemma in
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{1}
be the dimension vector that is equal to one at every vertex of
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
, and let
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}

2.2.
Reflection transformations

We will also make use of reflection transformations to establish
(\ref{eqn:main-formula-parab-Kostka})
. Let
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}_{\geq 0}
be a dimension vector,
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}
, an integral weight and a
x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
a vertex. We define
s_{x}\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
to be the quiver obtained from
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
by reversing all arrows that start or end in

. We also define
s_{x}\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}
by

(s_{x}\beta)_{y}=\begin{cases}\beta_{y},&\text{if }x\neq y,\\[4.0pt]
-\beta_{x}+\displaystyle\sum_{\text{edges }x\text{---}z}\beta_{z},&\text{if }x=y,\end{cases}

and
s_{x}\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}
by

(s_{x}\sigma)_{y}=\begin{cases}-\sigma_{x},&\text{if }y=x,\\[4.0pt]
\sigma_{y}+\sigma_{x}b_{xy},&\text{if }x\neq y,\end{cases}

where
b_{xy}
is the number of edges between
x
and
y
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose that
x
is a sink or a source vertex and
s_{x}\beta
is a dimension vector,
i.e.
,
s_{x}\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}
. Then

(7)

\operatorname{SI}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)_{\sigma}\simeq\operatorname{SI}(s_{x}\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},s_{x}\beta)_{s_{x}\sigma}.

2.3.
Quiver exceptional sequences

The notion of a quiver exceptional sequence, which we review below, plays a key role in our computations, since it allows us to reduce the problem to computing the dimensions of weight spaces of semi-invariants for a star quiver. These can then be expressed as parabolic Kostka coefficients.

In what follows, by a
Schur representation

V
of
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
, we mean a representation such that

\dim\operatorname{End}_{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}}(V)=1
, that is,
\operatorname{End}_{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}}(V)=\{(\lambda\mathbf{Id}_{V(x)})_{x\in\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}
.

For two dimension vectors
\alpha
and
\beta
, we define
(\alpha\circ\beta)_{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}}:=\dim\operatorname{SI}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}},\beta)_{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}
. (We drop the subscript
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
whenever
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
is understood from the context.) It follows from the main results in
[
3
]
that
\alpha\circ\beta\neq 0
if and only if
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=0
and
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}}(V,W)=0
for some representations
V
and
W
of dimension vectors
\alpha
and
\beta
, respectively.

Definition 2.5

(
Quiver Exceptional Sequences
)
.

A sequence

\mathcal{E}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N})

of dimension vectors of
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
is said to be a
quiver exceptional sequence
if:

(1)

\langle\varepsilon_{i},\varepsilon_{i}\rangle=1
and
\varepsilon_{i}
is the dimension vector of a Schur representation for all
i\in[N]
;

(2)

\langle\varepsilon_{i},\varepsilon_{j}\rangle\leq 0
and
\varepsilon_{j}\circ\varepsilon_{i}\neq 0
for all
1\leq i<j\leq N
.

To any quiver exceptional sequence
\mathcal{E}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N})
, we associate the quiver
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathcal{E})
with vertices
\{1,\ldots,N\}
and

-\langle\varepsilon_{i},\varepsilon_{j}\rangle

arrows from vertex
i
to vertex
j
for all
1\leq i\neq j\leq N
. Let

(8)

I:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}

be the map defined by

I(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{N}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\gamma_{i}\varepsilon_{i}

for all
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

We are now ready to state Derksen–Weyman’s Embedding Theorem.

Theorem 2.6

(
The Embedding Theorem for Quiver Semi-Invariants
; see
[
4
]
)
.

Let

\mathcal{E}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N})

be a quiver exceptional sequence for
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}
. If
\alpha
and
\beta
are two dimension vectors of
\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathcal{E})
, then

(\alpha\circ\beta)_{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathcal{E})}=(I(\alpha)\circ I(\beta))_{\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}}.

Applying Theorem
2.6
, we obtain that

\dim\operatorname{SI}(\mathbf{\mathcal{Q}}^{p}_{m,n},\mathbf{1})_{\theta_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}}=\dim\operatorname{SI}(\mathcal{T},\widehat{\beta})_{\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}}.

Since
\widehat{\sigma}_{a,b}(x_{0})=0
and
\widehat{\beta}(x_{0})>\widehat{\beta}(y_{0})
, it follows from Theorem
2.1
that

\dim\operatorname{SI}(\mathcal{T},\widehat{\beta})_{\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}}=\dim\operatorname{SI}(\mathcal{S},\beta)_{\sigma_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}}.

The proof now follows.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.1
. For background material relevant to the computations in the proof below, we refer the reader to
[
1
, Section 4]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We know from Proposition
3.1
that

(11)

\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}=\dim\operatorname{SI}(\mathcal{S},\beta)_{\sigma_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}}.

Now, applying
(\ref{eqn:semi-inv-reflections})
at the source vertices
x_{1},\dots,x_{m+p}
, we get that

(12)

\dim\operatorname{SI}(\mathcal{S},\beta)_{\sigma_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}}=\dim\operatorname{SI}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}},\widetilde{\beta})_{\widetilde{\sigma}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}},

where

\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}:
x_{1}
\vdots
x_{m}
x_{m+1}
\vdots
x_{m+p}
z_{0}
y_{1}
\vdots
y_{n}

and

\widetilde{\beta}(x_{1})=\cdots=\widetilde{\beta}(x_{m})=pm-1,\qquad\widetilde{\beta}(x_{m+1})=\cdots=\widetilde{\beta}(x_{m+p})=m(p-1),

\widetilde{\beta}(z_{0})=pm,\qquad\widetilde{\beta}(y_{1})=\cdots=\widetilde{\beta}(y_{n})=1,

and

\widetilde{\sigma}_{a,b}(x_{i})=-a_{i}\qquad(i\in[m]),\qquad\widetilde{\sigma}_{a,b}(x_{m+\ell})=-N\qquad(\ell\in[p]),

\widetilde{\sigma}_{a,b}(z_{0})=pN,\qquad\widetilde{\sigma}_{a,b}(y_{j})=-b_{j}\qquad(j\in[n]).

To find a closed formula for
\dim\operatorname{SI}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}},\widetilde{\beta})_{\widetilde{\sigma}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}}
, we proceed as follows. First, we use Cauchy’s formula to decompose
\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{rep}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}},\widetilde{\beta})]
into a direct sum of irreducible representations of
\operatorname{GL}(\widetilde{\beta})
. Then, we consider the ring of semi-invariants

\operatorname{SI}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}},\widetilde{\beta}):=\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{rep}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}},\widetilde{\beta})]^{\operatorname{SL}(\widetilde{\beta})}

and sort out those semi-invariants of weight
\widetilde{\sigma}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
. For convenience, we write

V_{i}=\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{\beta}(x_{i})},\ i\in[m+p],\qquad V=\mathbb{C}^{pm},\qquad W_{j}=\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{\beta}(y_{j})}=\mathbb{C},\ j\in[n].

Then

\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{rep}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}},\widetilde{\beta})]=\mathbb{C}\Biggl[\prod_{i=1}^{m+p}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(V,V_{i})\times\prod_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(V,W_{j})\Biggr]

=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m+p}S(V\otimes V_{i}^{*})\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}S(V\otimes W_{j}^{*})

=\bigoplus\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m+p}S^{\lambda(i)}V_{i}^{*}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}S^{\mu(j)}W_{j}^{*}\otimes\left(S^{\lambda(1)}V\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{\lambda(m+p)}V\otimes S^{\mu(1)}V\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{\mu(n)}V\right),

where the direct sum is over all partitions
\lambda(i)
and
\mu(j)
with
\ell(\lambda(i))\leq\dim V_{i}
,
\forall i\in[m+p]
, and
\ell(\mu(j))\leq 1
,
\forall j\in[n]
.

Thus,
\operatorname{SI}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}},\widetilde{\beta})
can be written as

\bigoplus\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m+p}\left(S^{\lambda(i)}V_{i}^{*}\right)^{\operatorname{SL}(V_{i})}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\left(S^{\mu(j)}W_{j}^{*}\right)^{\operatorname{SL}(W_{j})}\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m+p}S^{\lambda(i)}V\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}S^{\mu(j)}V\right)^{\operatorname{SL}(V)}.

Sorting out those semi-invariants of weight
\widetilde{\sigma}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
completely determines the partitions
\lambda(i)
and
\mu(j)
. This way we get that
\operatorname{SI}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}},\widetilde{\beta})_{\widetilde{\sigma}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}}
is isomorphic to the weight space of weight
\widetilde{\sigma}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}(z_{0})=pN
that occurs in the weight space decomposition of

\left(\underbrace{S^{(N^{p(m-1)})}V\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{(N^{p(m-1))}V}}_{p\text{ times}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}S^{(a_{i}^{pm-1})}V\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}S^{b_{j}}V\right)^{\operatorname{SL}(V)}.

Thus,
\operatorname{SI}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}},\widetilde{\beta})_{\widetilde{\sigma}_{a,b}}
is isomorphic to

\left(\underbrace{S^{(N^{p(m-1)})}V\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{(N^{p(m-1)})}V}_{p\text{ times}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}S^{(a_{i}^{pm-1})}V\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}S^{b_{j}}V\otimes S^{((pN)^{pm})}V^{*}\right)^{\operatorname{GL}(V)}.

The dimension of this weight space is precisely
K_{\lambda,R}
. This together with
(\ref{eqn:proof-thm1})
and
(\ref{eqn:proof-thm2})
yields the desired formula for
\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

The following formula holds.

(13)

\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}=\sum_{\mu}K_{\mu,\{(a_{1}),\ldots,(a_{m}),\underbrace{(N^{m}),\ldots,(N^{m})}_{p\text{~times}}\}}\cdot K_{\mu,\{(b_{1}),\ldots,(b_{n}),(N^{mp})\}},

where the sum is over all partitions
\mu
with at most
mp
parts. When
p=1
, this recovers the formula for the number of the
m\times n
contingency tables with row and column sums fixed by
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
, as derived from the Robinson-Schenstead-Knuth correspondence.

Proof.

We know from Proposition
3.1
that

\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}=\dim\operatorname{SI}(\mathcal{S},\beta)_{\sigma_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}}.

Using the same strategy as in the proof of Theorem 1.1, we get that
\operatorname{SI}(\mathcal{S},\beta)_{\sigma_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}}
is isomorphic to

\left(S^{a_{1}}V^{*}\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{a_{m}}V^{*}\otimes\underbrace{S^{(N^{m})}V^{*}\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{(N^{m})}V^{*}}_{p\text{ times}}\otimes S^{b_{1}}V\otimes\cdots\otimes S^{b_{n}}V\otimes S^{(N^{mp})}V\right)^{\operatorname{GL}(V)},

where
V=\mathbb{C}^{mp}
. The dimension of this space is precisely the right hand side of
(\ref{eqn:formula-RSK})
.

∎
