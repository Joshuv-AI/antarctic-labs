Drivers and Outcomes of Engagement in Non‐Formal Special Interest Groups Among Japanese Emergency Physicians: An Activity Theory Analysis
Authors: Takeshi Kawaguchi, Osamu Nomura, Kaho Hayakawa, Shigeki Fujitani, Takuya Saiki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesEmergency medicine requires physicians to manage high uncertainty, time constraints, and diverse responsibilities. To sustain their careers, continuous professional development (CPD) and psychological well‐being are crucial. While participation in formal learning is often limited by time and location, autonomous “non‐formal” Special Interest Groups (SIGs) are emerging as flexible learning spaces. This study aimed to address two research questions (RQs): (1) What drives Japanese emergency physicians (EPs) to engage in non‐formal SIGs? (2) What do EPs learn and gain through these activities?MethodsThis qualitative study was grounded in a constructivist paradigm. We conducted semi‐structured interviews with 14 EPs playing central roles within the Emergency Medicine Alliance, a non‐formal SIG in Japan. Data were analyzed using Braun & Clarke's thematic analysis. Engeström's Activity Theory served as the theoretical framework, focusing on interactions between individuals and the collective, as well as systemic “contradictions”.ResultsFor RQ1 (drivers), engagement was driven by interactions among the Subject (desire for professional development), Object (goals for transforming emergency medicine), and Community (a psychologically safe and diverse network). For RQ2 (gains), learning was mediated by Tools (means of facilitating learning and interaction), Division of Labor (transformation of roles and leadership), and Rules (norms supporting activities), leading to Outcomes (professional development and formation of social networks). A primary contradiction between mutual support and social contribution emerged as a central driver of these activities.ConclusionsNon‐formal SIG activities function as a process of expansive learning, in which participants develop new practices through engagement with systemic contradictions. In particular, the tension between the initial goal of “mutual support” and the evolving goal of “social contribution” acted as a fundamental driver. The collaborative resolution of these contradictions appears central to participants' learning. These findings suggest that non‐formal SIGs offer important value for CPD and the development of social capital.

Introduction

Emergency medicine is characterized by uncertainty, variability, and time constraints in acute care, alongside a constant demand for efficiency to manage high patient volumes and ensure safety in practice [1]. Additionally, emergency departments (EDs) provide diverse clinical encounters and are important environments for clinical training [2]. In teaching hospitals, emergency physicians (EPs) must simultaneously deliver patient care and teaching for medical students and residents [3]. These demands require EPs to multitask continuously—managing high patient volumes, addressing operational issues, adapting to rapidly changing ED conditions, and maintaining broad clinical knowledge and skills—resulting in substantial physical and psychological strain [1]. Creating a sustainable learning environment within limited time and resources is therefore a central challenge in EPs' continuous professional development (CPD).

Supporting EPs' CPD is essential for strengthening ED educational systems and, ultimately, delivering high‐quality patient care. One approach to CPD support is participation in special interest groups (SIGs). In medical education, SIGs are defined as “a group composed of students, residents, faculty, and other healthcare professionals who share a common interest in the development and growth of a specific medical area,” and are typically formed for academic or research activities [4]. SIGs can facilitate peer interaction, social networking, and leadership development, thereby supporting CPD [5]. Beyond knowledge and skills acquisition, routine mechanisms through which professionals support one another may influence both CPD and the psychological well‐being of EPs [6, 7]. These mechanisms include both group discussions and one‐to‐one conversations: group formats can foster solidarity through shared experiences and anxieties, whereas one‐to‐one conversations allow individual issues to be addressed while preserving privacy. Group formats have also been reported to help alleviate mental health concerns [8]. In this context, SIGs may serve a dual function—professional development and mutual support—by enabling members to share experiences and emotions and to offer advice and encouragement. For EPs working under high physical and psychological stress, collegial mutual support may be a foundation for sustaining CPD and long‐term careers.

Professional learning is often classified into three types: formal, non‐formal, and informal learning [9]. Formal learning refers to systematic, institution‐based learning; non‐formal learning involves organized activities outside institutions; and informal learning encompasses unorganized learning through daily experiences. Academic subcommittees and certification courses can be considered “formal SIGs” and are an example of formal learning. However, such formats are often constrained by time and location, potentially limiting participation among busy EPs [10]. This study therefore focuses on “non‐formal SIG” activities: organized, physician‐led initiatives outside formal institutional structures that promote more practical and autonomous learning among Japanese EPs.

In Japan, learning opportunities for EPs remain centered on formal arrangements such as academic conferences and training sessions. Although effective for systematic knowledge acquisition, these formats are not always accessible for EPs. EPs work long hours averaging 61 h per week, including overtime [11, 12]. In a specialty requiring continuous 24‐h, 365‐day operation, some physicians must remain on duty even during conferences, creating structural inequities in educational access [10]. Non‐formal SIG activities may offer more flexible learning opportunities. However, within the chaotic and fragmented ED environment, non‐formal learning can be variable in content and instructional quality, difficult to evaluate [13], and highly case‐dependent. Consequently, it often struggles to develop from sporadic information exchange into systematic knowledge creation or meaning‐making [14].

Despite growing interest in SIGs, research on the drivers of EPs' sustained engagement in non‐formal SIGs—and what they gain in terms of learning, information, and social connections—remains limited. To address these gaps, this study aimed to answer the following research questions. RQ1: What are the drivers that enable EPs to engage in non‐formal SIG activities? RQ2: Specifically, what do EPs learn and what information and connections do they gain through non‐formal SIG activities?

Methods

Research Design and Reporting Guidelines

This study was grounded in a constructivist paradigm, focusing on insights gained through interactions within non‐formal SIG activities. We employed a qualitative research design using semi‐structured individual interviews. The study design and reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [15].

Study Context

We conducted this study within the Emergency Medicine Alliance (EMA), a prominent non‐formal SIG for EPs in Japan. Established in 2009 and certified as a Non‐Profit Organization in 2018, EMA aims to contribute to the development of network building, educational support, and the promotion of clinical research in Japanese emergency medicine [16, 17]. As of 2024, the group has 4420 registered online members and 68 staff members. EMA provides a platform for knowledge sharing and peer learning through regular meetups and digital forums.

Theoretical Framework

We adopted Engeström's Activity Theory as the theoretical framework [18], employing a theory‐informed approach to guide the entire research process from data collection to analysis [19]. This model integrates the structure of an individual action—consisting of Subject, Tools, and Object—with the collective activity structure surrounding it—consisting of Rules, Division of Labor, and Community—into a single “activity system,” which produces Outcomes as a result of the transformation of the Object (Figure 1). Non‐formal SIG activities for EPs unfold as collective practices supported by collaboration, norms, and role divisions, while simultaneously serving as learning spaces for individual physicians. Therefore, a framework capable of capturing interactions between individual and collective activity is considered suitable in this context.

Model of the activity system.

Activity Theory uniquely focuses on contradictions arising within the activity system. Contradictions refer to tensions within or between elements of the system; they are not merely barriers but sources of new learning and practice [20]. Primary contradictions arise as tensions between use value and exchange value within a single element of the system, often appearing as interpretive differences arising from diverse perspectives. Secondary contradictions occur between two or more elements of the same activity system. The process of resolving these contradictions and developing new forms of activity is termed expansive learning [21]. In this study, we posited that analyzing the contradictions inherent in non‐formal SIG activities would allow for a deep understanding of the learning and growth processes of EPs. Activity Theory is widely used to analyze practice‐based learning and has recently been applied in medical education [20, 22]. These studies support the validity of Activity Theory for analyzing collaborative learning spaces rooted in practice, such as SIGs.

Study Setting and Participants

The study targeted EPs with key roles in EMA. Potential participants were selected using purposive sampling and subsequently approached via email. Eligibility was restricted to individuals able to provide written informed consent. The sample size was determined based on theoretical saturation; recruitment and data collection continued until no new themes or codes emerged, indicating that the dataset was sufficiently rich to address the research questions. Fourteen participants were ultimately enrolled.

Data Collection

Semi‐structured individual interviews were conducted online using a video conferencing platform (Microsoft Teams; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) between April and June 2025. This format was chosen to ensure privacy and accommodate the participants' irregular shift schedules. The interviews were conducted in Japanese and lasted between 24 and 82 min (median 49 min). All interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim. The interview guide was developed based on the theoretical framework of Activity Theory to systematically explore participants' personal backgrounds, experiences within the SIG, and the perceived impacts of its activities (Table 1).

Research Team and Reflexivity

The interviews were conducted by the first author (TK), an emergency physician (MD) with 16 years of clinical experience. At the time of the study, TK was a Master's student in health professions education. TK is an operational member of EMA, the non‐formal SIG that is the subject of this study, and had pre‐existing professional relationships with 13 of the 14 participants. This “insider researcher” positionality facilitated rapport and deep contextual understanding. To mitigate potential bias arising from these relationships and the author's personal involvement in the group, we maintained a reflexive journal throughout the research process and engaged in continuous collaborative dialogue with the research team. Participants were aware of the researcher's background and the study's objective to explore the significance of their activities.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA) [23, 24]. The analysis followed Braun and Clarke's six phases, adopting a theory‐informed approach where Engeström's Activity Theory served as a deductive framework to enhance rigor and trustworthiness throughout the process. The phases were: (1) familiarization with the data, (2) generating initial codes, and (3) searching for themes, which were primarily performed by the first author (TK); (4) reviewing themes and (5) defining and naming themes, during which the emerging themes were collaboratively refined through reflexive dialogue within the research team and with the supervising medical education researcher (ON); and (6) producing the report. Data management was supported by Microsoft Excel. Results

A total of 14 EPs participated in this study. Their clinical experience ranged from 5 to 24 years (median 16 years), and their involvement in the non‐formal SIG ranged from 3 to 16 years (median 12 years). Regarding their primary affiliations, four were employed at university hospitals or affiliated institutions, nine at community hospitals, and one at a primary care facility.

The analysis identified distinct themes corresponding to the seven elements of the activity system (Subject, Object, Community, Tools, Outcomes, Division of Labor, and Rules) constituting the non‐formal SIG activities of Japanese EPs. The key findings for each research question are detailed below, with related themes and representative quotes summarized in Tables 2 and 3.

RQ1: Drivers of Engagement in Non‐Formal SIG Activities

The forces driving EPs to invest time and effort in non‐formal SIG activities amid their busy schedules involved interactions among the Subject, Object, and Community elements of Activity Theory (Table 2). These forces comprised three aspects: the desire for professional development through its activities (Subject), goals related to transforming emergency medicine (Object), and a psychologically safe and diverse network (Community).

First, a primary driver was the Subject (the participating physicians) and their intrinsic motivation for professional development. Many participants joined to seek motivating learning experiences that were not available at their own institutions. One participant recounted the shock of realizing there were like‐minded peers across the country, which initially drew them to the activity. This desire for capacity building was further reinforced by the unique value of the SIG. In their daily work, showing negative emotions was often suppressed due to the pressure of leadership roles, and opportunities to discuss “earnest and passionate” ideals of emergency medicine were limited. One participant noted:Leaders are somewhat lonely, aren't they? You feel like you can't voice complaints or show weakness. (Participant 1)

Leaders are somewhat lonely, aren't they? You feel like you can't voice complaints or show weakness. (Participant 1)

This highlights the importance of having a space independent of one's position within the hospital. The SIG provided an opportunity to step away from daily pressures, explore expertise, and experience a sense of personal growth. At the same time, participants also faced a “contradiction between the desire for professional development and the burden of activity,” such as the difficulty of balancing work and raising children. The median experience of 16 years suggests participants are at a career stage where workplace leadership responsibilities increase, coinciding with life events such as parenting. Sustained engagement in the SIG despite these challenges suggests that the SIG functions not merely as a place for knowledge acquisition but as a space for mutual support distinct from daily duties, thereby sustaining individual motivation.

Second, the shared Object—goals related to transforming emergency medicine—integrated individual development desires with organizational practice, elevating the activity into a broader endeavor. Participants sought to achieve goals through the SIG that could not be accomplished alone, such as “enhancing the value of emergency care” and “outreach for solving social issues.” One participant emphasized the significance of sending a message to society as a group:We need to present the necessity of EPs to society and the medical field … and that is not something you can do individually. (Participant 2)

We need to present the necessity of EPs to society and the medical field … and that is not something you can do individually. (Participant 2)

Although the mission evolved over time from the initial concept of “using horizontal connections to support EPs who are about to drop out” to a “review of the mission” to fit the current era, this goal remained at the core of the group. The vision that their activities were not just for self‐satisfaction but contributed to the development of Japanese emergency medicine served as a strong motivation.

Third, the Community—a psychologically safe and diverse network—connected individual aspirations with collective goals, enabling sustainable activity. Participants described SIG members as “friends and rivals” who play a motivational role in improving their practice as EPs. This community was characterized by egalitarian relationships transcending institutional and age barriers. The tacit understanding that “all members are equal” enhanced psychological safety. Such relationships provided opportunities to share and resolve anxieties or questions from daily clinical practice and to view one's own practice objectively. Furthermore, the nationwide network formed through the SIG brought tangible benefits to individual careers, such as serving as a foundation for joint research. The sense of growing alongside trusted peers toward a shared goal was a key driver of continued participation.

RQ2: Learning, Information, and Connections

Through non‐formal SIG activities, EPs acquired not only explicit knowledge and skills but also broader outcomes. These included collaboration with other EPs from diverse clinical backgrounds across Japan and different generations, without direct conflicts of interest, as well as the development of social networks supporting career formation. These were mediated by the Tools, Outcomes, Division of Labor, and Rules elements of Activity Theory (Table 3).

Regarding Tools, participants used mailing lists, website content, and study sessions as means to facilitate collaborative learning. Through these tools, participants accessed information on clinical knowledge and hospital administration, particularly regarding “minor emergencies”—non‐life‐threatening conditions such as epistaxis or dislocations. One participant illustrated how tools shared in the SIG were applied to clinical education:The mailing lists and website content are… quite easy to use for bedside teaching for juniors. I use them often, and I think that is one great merit of participating in this team. (Participant 4)

The mailing lists and website content are… quite easy to use for bedside teaching for juniors. I use them often, and I think that is one great merit of participating in this team. (Participant 4)

However, some participants expressed concern that these long‐standing tools might lead to the group becoming “outdated” as times change.

Regarding Outcomes, participants recognized professional development and the formation of social networks. Specifically, interactions with peers nationwide provided a wider viewpoint of their clinical practice and enabled them to standardize the quality of care, leading to improved professional competence and broader perspectives, which contributed to increased self‐efficacy. Additionally, the formation of connections within egalitarian relationships, described as “free of conflicts of interest” and “like friends in the same profession,” was directly linked to career formation. One participant stated:If this group [EMA] didn't exist, I think my career as an emergency physician might have ended. (Participant 7)

If this group [EMA] didn't exist, I think my career as an emergency physician might have ended. (Participant 7)

Another participant highlighted the significance of the social network for academic success:I wouldn't have had the experience of writing my first original article without EMA… Getting a position at a university hospital is quite hard [without such support]. (Participant 12)

I wouldn't have had the experience of writing my first original article without EMA… Getting a position at a university hospital is quite hard [without such support]. (Participant 12)

Furthermore, involvement in organizational management itself became a crucial learning process. Through the Division of Labor, participants experienced diverse roles and leadership transformations, ranging from core members leading operations to general participants. By taking on coordination roles or mentoring younger members during generational transitions, they cultivated leadership and management skills. Finally, regarding Rules, the degree of discretion afforded to participants and the “relative lack of norms” supporting the activity promoted autonomous learning, providing opportunities to foster participants' problem‐solving abilities and creativity.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to qualitatively elucidate how non‐formal SIG activities among Japanese EPs function as a strategy for CPD within the framework of Activity Theory. The analysis revealed that the driving forces enabling EPs to engage in these activities comprise the interaction of three elements: the desire for professional development, goals related to transforming emergency medicine, and a psychologically safe and diverse network. Furthermore, the learning gained through these activities extended beyond the acquisition of professional knowledge and skills to include collaborative experiences with diverse peers and the development of social networks supporting career formation.

Expansive Learning Driven by Contradictions

A key finding is that the sustained learning and development gained through participation in this SIG stems from the process by which members engage with and attempt to resolve multiple “contradictions,” which are the inherent tensions and structural challenges within their activity system.

Our findings indicate that the ongoing questioning of whether the SIG should remain a support group or aim for social transformation appears to be a key factor underpinning sustained engagement. This process identifies a historical primary contradiction (tension within a single element) within the Object of the activity: specifically, the tension between the initial, internal goal of “mutual support to prevent EPs from dropping out” and the newly emerged, external goal of “social contribution through outreach to solve social issues.” These two goals do not merely coexist; they create a tension involving the prioritization of activities and the group's identity [25, 26]. This fundamental conflict serves as a driver for “expansive learning.” In Activity Theory, expansive learning occurs when members do not just adapt to changes, but proactively redefine their group's purpose and collaboratively create new practices to overcome challenges. Furthermore, as the group attempted to resolve this primary tension, they faced secondary contradictions (i.e., practical tensions between different elements of the system). The collaborative effort to resolve these secondary contradictions led to tangible learning outcomes and group evolution.

Three secondary contradictions were identified in this study. First, a tension emerged between Tools and the Subject or Community, specifically regarding legacy communication methods. Asynchronous tools such as mailing lists, while once effective, no longer aligned with the needs of younger generations who prefer immediate communication [27], leading to concerns about the group becoming outdated. Second, a contradiction appeared between the Division of Labor and the Community. As the group expanded, information asymmetry and discrepancies in role awareness arose between core members managing operations and general members. This challenge may require new leadership and organizational restructuring to encourage broader engagement. Finally, a tension was identified between Rules and the Object, specifically involving the conflict between implicit norms and social responsibility. While the implicit rule that “all members are equal” ensures psychological safety [28], it conflicted with the need for clearer decision‐making processes and accountability structures required to enhance the group's social impact.

Thus, the process through which members encounter contradictions, engage with them collectively, and explore new solutions through dialogue constitutes qualitative transformation and learning within the group, transcending individual‐level knowledge acquisition.

Academic Significance

The academic novelty of this study, in comparison to existing research on SIGs and mental health support, is twofold. First, this study suggests that non‐formal SIG activities contribute to the sustainability of professional careers. While extant research on medical SIGs has predominantly documented their impact on medical students' career selection and professional exposure in regions such as Canada and the United States [29, 30, 31, 32, 33], our study extends this scope to practicing EPs with long‐term involvement. Our findings indicate that SIGs function not only as venues for knowledge acquisition but also as spaces for cultivating leadership and management skills through organizational roles and experiences (Division of Labor) and the restructuring of norms (Rules). This suggests that non‐formal SIGs provide social capital—networks and practical knowledge—that enables professionals to continue and develop their careers over the long term. Bourdieu defines social capital as “the aggregate of the actual or potential resources which are linked to possession of a durable network.” [34] In light of this, the social networks among EPs highlighted in this study function as social capital enabling access to resources such as information, advice, and collaborative opportunities, serving as a foundation for individual CPD. Furthermore, practical knowledge shared through such social capital promotes reflection on what it means to be an EP, contributing to the formation of professional identity.

Second, this integrated learning process driven by contradictions offers a new perspective to existing discussions that tend to treat mental health support and educational learning as separate entities. Notably, by analyzing this SIG through the lens of Activity Theory, our data suggest that the seemingly separate elements of “mutual support” and “professional development” are linked via the primary contradiction within the activity system. Conventionally, research on physician burnout has discussed mutual support separately from learning activities [35], while educational research has focused on knowledge and skills. However, our results indicate improvements in self‐efficacy and the formation of social capital as members shared the process of expansive learning to overcome systemic contradictions.

Practical Implications for Professional Education and Support

The identification of a learning process driven by contradictions offers important implications for professional education and mental health support. From an educational perspective, professional development may be supported through processes of conflict and dialogue in non‐formal settings. Additionally, professional self‐efficacy and psychological connectedness with peers are developed through collaborative engagement with contradictions within the activity system.

Two practical implications arise from these findings. First, regarding the value of the process, strategies for professional CPD should be identified not only in formal learning but also in the process of engaging with and attempting to resolve various contradictions in non‐formal activities. Consequently, medical institutions and academic societies should recognize the effectiveness of such autonomous groups or organizations and support their activities by providing protected time or digital infrastructure [36]. Second, regarding support systems, the finding that connectedness and self‐efficacy are fostered through collaborative engagement with contradictions provides a new perspective on alleviating isolation. These findings resonate with prior peer support research during the COVID‐19 pandemic that highlighted how sharing experiences and anxieties can foster solidarity and improve psychological and social functioning [35]. Our study extends this by identifying that such psychological connectedness is fundamentally linked to professional growth when cultivated through the collaborative resolution of systemic contradictions. This approach may be particularly important for supporting mental health in the high‐burden field of emergency medicine [37].

Limitations and Future Research Directions

Finally, we address the limitations of this study and potential directions for future research.

First, this study focused on a single non‐formal SIG organized by and for Japanese EPs. Therefore, the findings may not be directly applicable to EPs in other countries or cultural contexts, as healthcare systems, clinical environments, and cultural norms regarding peer support vary significantly worldwide.

Second, this study is based on the perspectives of core members with high levels of engagement. Perspectives may differ among less engaged or peripheral members. For instance, these members might perceive the time burden differently or have primary motivations for participation that do not fully align with the goals of the core members.

Third, the data represent a snapshot in time based on the interviews, and we did not track the long‐term process of how contradictions are resolved in practice or how the group transforms. Future research should include comparative studies with non‐formal SIGs in other professions and longitudinal studies capturing organizational development over time and the changing nature of contradictions. Such research would further elucidate the mechanisms through which contradictions drive development in organizational learning activities that support CPD and psychological connectedness.

Fourth, regarding the trustworthiness of our findings, we utilized RTA within a constructivist framework [23, 24]. Because RTA relies on the researchers' subjective and interpretative engagement with the data, variation between coders is understood not as an error to be minimized but as an inherent feature of the analytic process. Nevertheless, from the perspective of research paradigms that prioritize reproducibility, such variation may be interpreted as a limitation. Furthermore, we argue that while participants possess “experiential expertise” regarding their own professional lives, researchers possess the “analytic expertise” required to interpret those experiences through the theoretical lens of Activity Theory. Accordingly, the rigor of our analysis was ensured through researcher reflexivity and ongoing collaborative dialogue rather than through member checking or inter‐coder reliability.

Conclusions

This study elucidates the significance of non‐formal SIGs for Japanese EPs through the lens of Activity Theory. The analysis suggests that these activities function as a process of expansive learning, driven by collaborative efforts to resolve systemic contradictions—particularly the tension between the internal need for “mutual support” and the external goal of “social contribution.” By navigating these conflicts, physicians not only acquire practical knowledge but also cultivate the professional self‐efficacy and psychological connectedness necessary for career sustainability. Consequently, non‐formal SIGs provide a vital, autonomous complement to formal education, supporting physicians' professional and career development in the high‐stakes environment of emergency medicine.
