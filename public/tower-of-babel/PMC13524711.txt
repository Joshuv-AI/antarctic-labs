Global Perspectives on Kidney Disease and Dialysis Care: Insights From Patients, Caregivers, and Advocates
Authors: Diana Clynes, Paul T Conway, Erin Kahle, Isabelle Delaney, Michelle Seger, Mallika L Mendu
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Drivers of global dialysis modality use and patient experience vary across domains, including patient education levels, medical professional training, and formal policy priorities.1 For example, use of home dialysis varies significantly across the globe. Our prior research demonstrated attitudes toward and motivations for choosing a certain modality.2 We conducted a global survey to assess patients’ education about dialysis options, patient and caregiver involvement in decision making, and reasons for choosing a specific modality. The study was performed for quality and operational improvement and, therefore, met the senior author’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) criteria for IRB review exemption and waived informed consent.

The American Association of Kidney Patients Center for Patient Research and Education and the International Home Dialysis Roundtable conducted an electronic survey of 967 respondents and caregivers in July and August 2024. The English language survey was sent via email to a listserv maintained by the The American Association of Kidney Patients (AAKP) and posted on social media. The AAKP database comprises kidney patients across all stages, family members, caregivers, and living donors. The survey was developed to include questions related to demographics, screening, treatment knowledge, and patient empowerment based on information collected from AAKP membership. If respondents indicated that they were home dialysis recipients, they received additional questions related to home dialysis.

Survey respondents were 35% women and 63% men (2% not available); 47% were aged between 18 and 24 years, 30% between 35 and 49 years, and 23% over 50 years. Respondents included dialysis care partners (21%), dialysis patients (14%), kidney transplant recipients (10%), transplant care partners (8%), and patients with advanced chronic kidney disease (6%). Owing to the survey’s structure to encourage participation, respondents did not have to disclose their role; therefore, 42% did not indicate their role, (a limitation of this study given impact on stratifying results). Among patients receiving dialysis, 43% received in-center hemodialysis (HD) and 57% received home dialysis. Respondents in Africa (38%) were more likely to be HD patients than those from Asia (24%). The sample was regionally diverse, comprising participants from Africa (42%), Asia (22%), North America (16%), and “other” (6%), encompassing the Caribbean, Central America, Europe, the Middle East, South America, and Oceania; 14% of respondents did not disclose their location.

The survey allowed for multiple responses per respondent; the overall response rate could not be calculated. Fig 1 shows questions by topic and the response rates. The most commonly answered question was about sources of information regarding dialysis; the most common response was “nephrologist” (26%). As depicted in Fig 2, the distribution varied by region, with “nephrologist” leading among respondents in North America (30%) and Asia (37%). In Africa and other global regions, responses were evenly distributed (eg, in Africa, “other doctor” led with 19%).

Regarding advocacy organizations, 334 responses were obtained. Most patients indicated that they strongly agreed (30%) or agreed (37%) that there were advocacy groups in their country to which they belonged or felt connected. Responses across regions remained relatively consistent with the total responses; however, in North America, 12% of respondents indicated that they disagreed with feeling connected to their community, compared with Asia (5%) and Africa (6%). Examining empowerment in the management of disease, across all regions, the majority strongly agreed (46%) or agreed (40%) that they had played an active role in their own or loved one’s treatment plan.

Responses to questions about home dialysis were more limited, reflecting a smaller sample with home dialysis experience. With respect to attitudes and comfort toward home dialysis, 164 responses were obtained. The top responses included support from the community (30%), increased access to training and understanding modifications (29%), and support from medical professionals (24%). There was variation by region, with a higher proportion of patients from North America (35%), indicating the importance of support from the community. Similarly, a higher proportion of patients from Africa (43%) and Asia (32%) indicated the importance of increased access to training and understanding modifications. Among home dialysis patients, 37% chose this modality with support from their family or care partner(s); 12% made the decision on their own but considered their nephrologist’s opinion; and 9% decided as a team with their nephrologist. The full survey is available for review in Fig. S1.

In this survey study, we provide insights into global views on dialysis care. In this limited survey study, patients and caregivers felt connected to advocacy organizations, were empowered to manage their dialysis care, and, among those receiving home dialysis, had a favorable impression of home dialysis; however, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of the survey including a small sample size that may not be fully representative of the global dialysis population as well as questions limited to home dialysis respondents. This exploratory survey illustrates geographic variation in how patients receive information about dialysis care and support needed for home dialysis, which indicates a need to tailor approaches to dialysis modality education based on geography. Region-specific surveys to capture patient experiences are essential for developing targeted policy solutions that support and empower dialysis care, ensuring that dialysis options align with life goals and remain patient-centered.
