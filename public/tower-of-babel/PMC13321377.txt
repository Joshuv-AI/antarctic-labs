A phylogeny-informed mathematical modeling of HPAI H5N1 transmission dynamics and effectiveness of control measures
Authors: Oluwatosin Babasola, Mohamed Bakheet, Christina Næsborg-Nielsen, Sachin Subedi, M H M Mubassir, Justin Bahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHighly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) H5N1 continues to pose a zoonotic risk due to its transmission among wild birds and domestic poultry, with sporadic spillover events to humans. Phylogenetic and surveillance studies indicate that such spillover is driven mainly by interactions among avian hosts rather than by sustained human to human transmission. However, many existing models examine these host groups separately, which limits the understanding of how cross-species interactions influence the spillover risk. To address this gap, we develop a mechanistic model that describes the transmission dynamics within and between wild birds, domestic birds, and humans. Using this framework, reproduction thresholds are derived for each host group, and sensitivity analysis is performed to identify parameters that most strongly influenced the transmission. Numerical simulations examine the effects of inter species interactions on human HPAI H5N1 infection and assess the performance of different control measures. Simulation results show that vaccination with high efficacy and sufficient coverage reduces reproduction thresholds across host groups and decreases human infection, while reduced contact between birds and humans further limits spillover risk. An optimal control formulation is used to evaluate intervention strategies and the results indicate that the combined implementation of environmental sanitation and targeted poultry culling leads to the greatest reduction in transmission and a lower likelihood of human infection. These findings clarify how cross-species interactions shape zoonotic risk and provide a theoretical basis for evaluating control strategies in multi-host systems.Keywords: Bird flu, Control measure, Optimal control, Phylogeny, Sensitivity analysis, Vaccination, Zoonotic transmission

Introduction

Highly pathogenic avian influenza HPAI subtype H5N1 is an avian influenza virus that infects both domestic and wild birds [1], [2]. Sporadic spillover events from avian hosts to humans and other mammals have been reported, and recent HPAI H5N1 strains have infected not only birds but also a range of mammalian species [3], [4], [5], [6]. Multiple cases in domestic and peridomestic mammals have been reported including in cattle, domestic cats, alpacas, wild raccoons, and humans [7], [8]. In the United States, outbreaks have been recorded in several domestic animal populations, with a small number of human infections linked to direct exposure to infected dairy cattle and poultry [9], [10]. According to [11], nearly all human HPAI H5N1 cases prior to 2024 were associated with direct contact with infected poultry, and as of late 2025, there is no human-to-human transmission.

Despite the absence of human-to-human transmission, the potential for viral adaptation remains a concern. Experimental studies show that several HPAI H5N1 strains can bind to human cell receptors under laboratory conditions [12], [13], [14]. These findings, along with observed spillover events, raise concern about the possibility of future human transmission. This potential for adaptation necessitates a detailed examination of transmission pathways and the factors that contribute to human infection risk. In particular, identifying how infections move across host populations is essential for assessing spillover potential and informing the design of effective mitigation strategies.

Several studies have examined the transmission dynamics of HPAI H5N1 and evaluated strategies to limit its spread [3], [15], [16], [17]. Despite these efforts, the virus continues to pose a public health threat with consequences for both global health and the economy [18], [19]. This suggests that existing approaches do not fully capture the mechanisms driving transmission, especially across host species. To improve the existing understanding of the transmission risk and evaluate the effectiveness of control measures, it is necessary to integrate complementary sources of information. In this study, phylogenetic evidence is combined with mathematical modeling to investigate transmission dynamics across host populations.

Mathematical models have proven to be valuable tools for understanding the dynamics of infectious diseases and are essential for examining the risk of HPAI H5N1 transmission among multiple hosts. For instance, [20] developed a deterministic six compartment model that incorporates vaccination and treatment in poultry farms, where poultry is considered as the natural host of the virus. Through numerical simulations, their results showed that outbreak size depends on vaccine availability and treatment rates. Similarly, [21], [22] examined the impact of vaccination in a dynamic population and identified parameters associated with rapid transmission, followed by theoretical analysis of the epidemic process. While these studies provide a useful understanding of transmission mechanisms and control effects, important gaps remain. In particular, many existing models focus primarily on within species transmission, while cross-species interactions receive limited attention. Recent evidence of transmission from wild birds to poultry and from infected mammals to humans highlights the importance of interactions among host populations. Empirical studies identify domestic poultry as a major source of zoonotic infection. However, many models do not explicitly represent these inter host interactions. As a result, quantitative models that simultaneously capture transmission between wild birds, domestic birds, and humans remain limited.

To address this limitation, we develop a multi-host transmission model that links wild birds, domestic birds, and humans through cross-species transmission pathways within a single system. This formulation allows the model to quantify how interactions across host populations influence spillover risk and enables direct comparison of intervention strategies targeting different hosts. Although these control measures have been implemented in practice, their relative effectiveness across host populations and their combined impact on zoonotic transmission remain difficult to assess using observational data alone. This limitation reflects variation in implementation across regions, as well as practical and economic constraints, particularly for large-scale poultry vaccination. The model therefore provides a quantitative basis for comparing intervention strategies under controlled conditions and for identifying combinations that produce the greatest reduction in transmission. In contrast to approaches that consider host populations separately, it provides a systematic method for evaluating the relative impact of interventions applied to wildlife, domestic animals, and humans.

Phylogenetic analysis is incorporated to inform the model structure and guide the selection of biologically parameter ranges. Observed clustering patterns and cross-species transmission links from H5N1 genomic sequence data support the inclusion and relative importance of transmission pathways between host populations. While parameters are not directly estimated from genomic data, this ensures consistency between the assumed transmission structure and observed epidemiological and evolutionary patterns.

Building on this formulation, the model incorporates an optimal control approach to evaluate intervention strategies such as environmental sanitation, vaccination, treatment, and culling. This enables systematic assessment of how control measures applied to different host populations influence transmission dynamics and human infection risk. This work provides a basis for comparing intervention strategies and identifying approaches that reduce zoonotic transmission under the control scenario.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the methodology, which covers model formulation, parameter definitions, and analysis of the basic reproduction number. Section 3 presents the results, where numerical simulations assess the impact of cross-species transmission on human infection and evaluate intervention strategies using optimal control. Section 4 contains the discussion, and Section 5 concludes with key findings and directions for future work.

Methods

Model formulation

We formulated a compartmental model comprising three host populations, each subdivided according to their epidemiological status. The human and domestic bird populations were modeled using the SVEIR compartmental structure, while the wild bird population followed the SIR compartmental structure. In this study, the domestic bird population represents poultry systems, which serve as the primary amplification hosts for HPAI H5N1 transmission, and does not explicitly include cattle or other mammalian domestic hosts. The exposed and vaccinated classes were omitted for wild birds to reflect limited surveillance of latent infection and the impracticality of vaccination in free living bird populations. We denote the susceptible individuals in each host population by Si, where i∈h,d,w. This population represents individuals who are not infected but are at risk of infection following effective contact with an infectious individual. The vaccinated population denoted by Vj,j∈h,d, represents individuals who were susceptible and subsequently received a vaccine to reduce their likelihood of infection following exposure. Individuals in this group are expected to have partial or complete immunity depending on vaccine efficacy. In the model, vaccine effectiveness is represented through a reduction in susceptibility to infection. Vaccinated individuals experience a reduced force of infection proportional to the vaccine efficacy parameter, so that higher efficacy corresponds to a lower probability of infection upon exposure. The impact of vaccination therefore depends on both vaccine efficacy and vaccination coverage within the population.

According to [23], [24], [25], ongoing efforts to develop human vaccines against avian influenza suggest that vaccination strategies may become feasible in the near future. The exposed group, Ej, includes individuals who have been in contact with the virus but are not yet infectious. The infected population, Ii, consists of individuals capable of transmitting the virus through effective contact with susceptible hosts. The recovered population, Ri, includes individuals who have recovered from infection, either through natural immune response or where applicable, treatment. It is important to note that only the human and domestic bird populations include a vaccinated compartment, while it is omitted for the wild bird population. This exclusion is due to the impracticality of vaccinating wild birds on a large scale as noted in [26], [27]. To inform the model structure and parameter selection, phylogenetic analysis was performed using publicly available H5N1 genomic sequences obtained from the GISAID database. A time-resolved phylogenetic tree was reconstructed to examine clustering patterns and cross-species transmission relationships among wild birds, domestic birds, and human isolates. The resulting phylogenetic patterns provide empirical evidence of inter-host transmission and guide the inclusion of cross-species transmission pathways in the model. Phylogenetic evidence indicates clustering patterns consistent with transmission among wild birds, domestic birds, and humans, particularly within clade 2.3.4.4b, where host adaptation and spillover events have been documented [17]. These patterns correspond to the transmission pathways represented in the model. In particular, links between wild bird and domestic bird sequences support the inclusion of αdw, while connections involving human cases are consistent with αhdand αhw.

Although domestic mammals (DM) and wild mammals (WM) were examined in the broader phylogenetic analysis, they were not included as fully coupled host classes in the main model. For domestic mammals, the phylogeny showed evidence of viral circulation within domestic mammal lineages, indicating the relevance of infection in this group. However, domestic mammals appear to represent secondary circulation following introduction from the avian system rather than a primary driver of sustained multi-host transmission. Similarly, wild mammals were also excluded from the main host structure because the available surveillance data were sparse and the phylogenetic reconstruction did not support a clear source or sink role for wild mammals in the observed epidemic. Therefore, including domestic mammals and wild mammals as fully interacting demes would therefore add model complexity without comparable gain in identifiability or biological support.

In addition to informing the model structure, the phylogenetic analysis was used to guide the selection of biologically plausible parameter intervals. The observed clustering patterns and inferred direction of cross-species transmission provide qualitative information on the relative importance of transmission pathways. This information was used to constrain the ranges of the cross-species transmission coefficients αhd, αhw, and αdw, ensuring that the parameter values are consistent with empirically observed transmission patterns and supported by existing epidemiological studies. While phylogenetic analysis provides guidance on model structure and parameter ranges, the transmission coefficients are not directly estimated from genomic sequence data. The basic reproduction number

The basic reproduction number, often denoted as R0, is regarded as a fundamental epidemiological metric that describes the potential for disease spread within a population. This quantifies the pathogen's transmission capacity and represents the average number of secondary infections generated by a single infectious individual when introduced into a fully susceptible population [29], [30]. To characterize the potential for HPAI H5N1 transmission and to inform the analysis of control measures, calculating the basic reproduction number is essential. Model fitting and parameter estimation

In this section, we estimate the model parameter values defined in Eq. (2). We adopted the least squares method for parameter estimation. The least squares approach is a well-established statistical technique that determines the best-fitting curve for the data by minimizing the difference between model predictions and observed data points [36], [37]. Our data fitting was performed using publicly available HPAI H5N1 human case data obtained from the World Health Organization [33]. Some parameters were estimated directly from the human case data, while others were fixed and bounded using values reported in the literature. For parameters related to the domestic bird component, existing literature served as a reference to guide parameter ranges, which were subsequently explored within the model framework. The resulting parameter values are interpreted as approximate quantities that reproduce general temporal patterns rather than precise statistical estimates. The parameter values reported in Table 3 are expressed in units consistent with the time scale of the model. All rate parameters are measured per day, while vaccine efficacy is dimensionless. Recruitment terms represent the inflow into the susceptible classes, and transmission parameters correspond to effective contact rates scaled by population size.

Some values differ across host groups due to differences in biological interpretation and data sources. In particular, the estimated natural death rate for humans reflects an effective rate over the modeling horizon and should be interpreted as a composite removal rate that includes background mortality and other forms of exit from the modeled population. In contrast, bird populations are described using demographic rates obtained from ecological studies, which are not directly comparable to human demographic rates.

Cross-species transmission parameters were estimated through model fitting and constrained within ranges reported in the literature. These quantities represent effective transmission processes rather than direct physical contact alone, which may lead to values that differ from standard epidemiological expectations. All parameters were selected to align with available data and previously reported ranges, while ensuring that the model reproduces observed incidence patterns.

We acknowledge that fitting to cumulative case data can introduce correlation in the residual structure and limit the ability to represent variability in the transmission process, as discussed in [57], [58]. Residual analysis was conducted to assess model fit, and residual plots are provided in the Appendix. These indicate that the model captures the overall temporal trend, with larger deviations observed in the early phase. These studies show that cumulative observations can produce biased estimates because consecutive values are not independent. However, the data used here are sparse and contain extended periods with zero incidence, and the cumulative series reflects this irregular reporting pattern. For this reason, the model was fitted to cumulative counts while recognizing this limitation. To reduce its impact, cumulative data were used only to obtain broad parameter ranges, and fitted values were checked for consistency with those reported in the literature. We also note that a quantitative integration of sequence data into parameter estimation, such as through phylodynamic modeling, is not considered in this study and remains an important direction for future work.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis is used to evaluate how uncertainty in model outputs arises from variation in input parameters. By systematically varying parameter values and examining the resulting changes in model outcomes, this approach identifies the variables that have the greatest influence on system behavior and clarifies the relationship between parameters and outputs.

For the basic reproduction number R0, defined in Eqs. (6–8), the analysis focuses on the parameters α,ω,ψ,τh,γ,σ,μ,δ,cE,and cI. To quantify the strength and direction of the relationship between R0 and each parameter, the partial rank correlation coefficient method was applied. Parameter values were generated using Latin hypercube sampling with 1500 samples and a fixed random seed to ensure reproducibility. Each parameter was sampled from a uniform distribution centered at its baseline value with a variation of ±20%. For parameters with stronger influence on transmission dynamics, the sampling range was extended to ±40% to allow better exploration of their effects. Probability parameters were restricted to the interval [0,1].

The sampled parameter sets were used to evaluate the corresponding values of R0, and the same samples were used to compute partial rank correlation coefficients. Prior to computing PRCC, scatter plots of each parameter against R0were examined. Across the sampled ranges, the relationship between each parameter and R0was monotonic, with no evidence of non-monotonic behavior. The statistical significance of PRCC values was assessed using a two-sided hypothesis test with a significance threshold of 0.05. The results of the sensitivity analysis are presented in Fig. 4.

To clarify this relationship, the threshold condition for transmission control can be expressed in terms of the vaccination rate ω and vaccine efficacy γ. In the model, vaccination does not represent a fixed coverage proportion but determines the equilibrium fraction of protected individuals through the balance between vaccination, waning immunity, and natural mortality. As a result, increasing ωraises the effective protected population, while higher γimproves the level of protection conferred by vaccination.

The condition R0<1is therefore achieved through joint increases in ωand γ, with higher efficacy reducing the vaccination rate required for control. This threshold corresponds to long term decline of infection, but it does not imply an immediate reduction in infection levels over finite time horizons, particularly in the presence of continued cross-species transmission. This motivates further examination of the system dynamics through numerical simulations.

Results

Numerical simulation

We performed numerical simulations of the model described in Eq. (2), using the baseline parameter values provided in Table 3, to assess the impact of cross-species transmission rates on the spread of HPAI H5N1 in humans. The simulation results are presented in Fig. 6.

The results in Fig. 6 show that cross-species transmission plays a major role in disease spread. As the effective transmission rate per contact between species decreases, the number of new infections over time declines. Increasing cross-species transmission leads to a substantial rise in peak human infections. In contrast, removing cross-species transmission results in markedly lower infection levels, with peak values reduced by approximately 80 to 90% relative to scenarios where such transmission is present.

For visual comparability, all panels are presented using a common axis scale. Consequently, scenarios with lower infection levels may appear compressed relative to those with higher transmission intensity. In particular, Fig. 6(d) shows that reduction in cross-species interaction leads to a substantial decrease in human infections, even without direct human-focused interventions. This scenario also produces the lowest infection levels across all cases, indicating that spillover driven pathways dominate human infection dynamics in the model. This reduction is also associated with a corresponding decrease in infection levels within the poultry population, which further limits spillover to humans. These suggest that interventions targeting animal reservoirs and transmission pathways may be more effective than those focused only on humans.

To assess the stability of these results, a local robustness test was performed by varying selected human transmission and disease progression parameters by ±10% around their baseline values and evaluating the resulting change in peak human infection. The analysis shows that peak human infection is most sensitive to αh, although cross-species transmission remains the primary driver of overall infection magnitude. A 10% decrease in αhreduces the peak by 23.51%, while a 10% increase raises it by 28.53%. In contrast, similar perturbations in αhdand αhwchange the peak by less than 1%. The disease induced mortality rate δhproduces modest changes of approximately 3.5%, while the recovery rate σhhas negligible effect on peak infection. Optimal control strategy

In this section, we implement an optimal control approach by introducing four time dependent control variables to evaluate their effectiveness in mitigating HPAI H5N1 transmission. The control variables, denoted by u1t, u2t, u3t, and u4t, are defined as follows:

The optimal control problem is formulated through an objective functional that minimizes infection levels in humans, domestic poultry, and wild birds, together with the costs associated with implementing control measures over a finite time horizon 0T. The functional includes linear terms in the infected populations and quadratic cost terms associated with the control variables u1t, u2t, u3t, and u4t, where weighting parameters determine the balance between reducing infection burden and intervention cost.

The Pontryagin Maximum Principle is applied to derive the Hamiltonian, the adjoint system, and the characterization of the optimal controls. The resulting optimality system consists of the state equations, adjoint equations, and control characterizations, which are solved numerically. The detailed formulation of the optimal control problem is provided in the Appendix, and the outcomes are presented in Figs. 7−9. The control variables are bounded between 0 and 1, and all simulations use baseline parameter values from Table 3.

When control measures are combined, the effect becomes more pronounced. The combination of sanitation and vaccination reduces peak human infection by about 52%, while sanitation with treatment yields a reduction close to 50%. Adding treatment to sanitation and vaccination provides only a marginal additional effect, with total reduction remaining near 53%. Combinations that exclude sanitation, such as vaccination with treatment, achieve reductions of less than 10%. The relatively weaker effect of vaccination in these simulations is consistent with the threshold analysis in Fig. 5, which characterizes conditions for R0<1, whereas Figs. 7−9 describe reductions in prevalence over a finite time horizon under the intervention scenarios.

In the poultry population, control measures have a stronger impact. Culling alone reduces peak infection by about 83%, while combinations that include culling, such as sanitation with culling or vaccination with culling, reduce infection levels by about 85 to 90%. The most effective strategy is the combined use of sanitation, vaccination, and culling, which reduces poultry infection by about 90%.

These results indicate that sanitation is the dominant intervention for reducing human infection, while culling is most effective for controlling infection in the animal reservoir. Strategies that target both environmental transmission and infected animal populations produce the largest reduction in disease burden.

Discussion

This study developed a mathematical model to examine the transmission dynamics of highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 across humans, domestic poultry, and wild birds. The model explicitly represents cross-species transmission pathways, allowing assessment of how interactions between wildlife reservoirs and domestic poultry shape infection risk in humans. Such multi-host systems are central to avian influenza ecology, where wild birds maintain viral circulation while domestic poultry acts as amplification hosts that increase exposure risk at the human interface. This align with existing studies which showcase the role of wild birds in maintaining avian influenza viruses and the importance of interactions between wild and domestic bird populations in shaping transmission pathways [59], [60].

The reproduction number analysis provides threshold conditions for reducing transmission in this multi-host system. The results indicate that achieving reproduction numbers below unity requires sufficiently high vaccine efficacy combined with vaccination coverage above approximately 50 %. This finding is consistent with established epidemiological theory, where both coverage and efficacy jointly determine population level protection. Previous work examining control strategies for avian influenza outbreaks has shown that vaccination effectiveness depends strongly on coverage levels and timing of intervention [61]. However, even when vaccination reduces transmission within domestic poultry populations, continued exposure from wildlife reservoirs may sustain infection risk. The results further demonstrate that when vaccine efficacy is reduced, substantially higher vaccination coverage is required to achieve R0<1, indicating that incomplete vaccination or lower efficacy may limit the effectiveness of vaccination as a standalone control strategy.

Sensitivity analysis identifies cross-species transmission parameters as primary drivers of infection outcomes. In particular, human infections are more strongly influenced by contact with infected domestic poultry than by direct transmission from wild birds. This result is consistent with epidemiological observations showing that most documented human H5N1 infections occur following exposure to infected poultry or contaminated environments rather than through sustained transmission from wild birds [62]. Domestic poultry therefore serves as the main interface through which infection risk is transferred from wildlife reservoirs to humans.

The optimal control analysis further clarifies the effectiveness of intervention strategies. The control problem is defined through an objective functional that minimizes infection prevalence across host populations together with the costs of intervention over a finite time horizon, with linear penalties on infected populations and quadratic costs on control efforts as specified in the Appendix. Under this formulation, environmental sanitation, vaccination, treatment, and poultry culling are represented as time dependent controls bounded between zero and one. The results show that combined interventions produce the greatest reduction in transmission, with environmental sanitation playing a dominant role in reducing human infections, while the combination of sanitation and culling provides the strongest reduction in poultry infections.

The strong effect of environmental sanitation can be explained mechanistically by its impact on the force of infection. Sanitation reduces environmental contamination and indirect transmission pathways, which are major contributors to human exposure in zoonotic systems. In contrast, vaccination and treatment act indirectly by reducing susceptibility or increasing recovery, which may not sufficiently reduce exposure when environmental transmission remains high. Similarly, culling reduces the number of infectious birds and therefore directly lowers transmission within poultry populations. These findings are consistent with outbreak management practices that emphasize biosecurity measures and rapid removal of infected flocks.

Despite these findings, some limitations should be acknowledged. The model assumes constant transmission parameters and does not account for viral evolution. In practice, HPAI H5N1 viruses evolve over time, and genetic changes may alter transmissibility, host adaptation, and spillover risk. Fixed parameter values therefore represent a time averaged approximation of transmission dynamics. The formulation also assumes homogeneous mixing within each host population and does not include spatial heterogeneity or movement processes such as migration of wild birds, which may influence virus spread.

Parameter uncertainty is another limitation. Several quantities were estimated through model fitting using aggregated incidence data and therefore represent effective transmission rates rather than directly observed biological measures. Although these values were informed by the literature, uncertainty may influence model predictions. In addition, the current formulation does not include additional host species and does not incorporate genomic or phylogenetic information that could provide finer resolution of transmission pathways. In this study, the domestic bird population represents poultry systems, although the structure can be adapted to other host species through modification of compartments and transmission terms.

Future work should extend this approach by incorporating evolutionary and spatial processes. Allowing transmission parameters to vary across viral lineages and integrating genomic data with epidemiological models may improve inference of transmission pathways. Further extensions include spatially explicit models, stochastic formulations, and improved parameter estimation using temporally and spatially resolved epidemiological data, along with integration of genomic sequence data for phylogenetic analysis.

Conclusion

This study presents a mathematical model describing the spread of highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 among humans, domestic poultry, and wild birds. By incorporating interactions across these host populations, the model enables quantitative assessment of how wildlife reservoirs and domestic poultry influence zoonotic infection risk. The analysis shows that infection within domestic poultry populations plays a central role in spillover to humans and therefore represents a critical target for control.

The results indicate that sufficiently high vaccination coverage and efficacy can reduce transmission potential within the system. Sensitivity analysis shows that parameters governing cross population transmission strongly influence infection outcomes, which underscores the importance of managing interactions between host groups. The optimal control analysis further demonstrates that coordinated strategies combining environmental sanitation with management of infected poultry populations can substantially reduce disease burden.

These findings support the use of integrated intervention strategies that address both environmental exposure and infection within poultry populations. Models that account for multiple host populations provide a useful tool for evaluating control measures and can contribute to improved preparedness and response to highly pathogenic avian influenza outbreaks.

Sequence data acquisition and phylogenetic analysis approach

H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b genomic sequences were obtained from GISAID [63]. Sequences were restricted to those classified as clade 2.3.4.4b and sampled between 2019 and 2025. Only high quality genomes were retained. Records lacking essential metadata such as host category, sampling date, or geographic origin were excluded. Duplicate sequences, identified by identical strain name, sampling date, and nucleotide sequence, were removed.

To limit bias from uneven surveillance, we used past subsampling approach. Sequences were grouped into host categories and further stratified by sampling year and region. Random sampling without replacement was conducted within each stratum using a fixed upper limit. All sequences were retained in strata with fewer observations than this threshold. This procedure preserved representation across host groups while maintaining temporal and geographic structure.

Multiple sequence alignment was conducted using MAFFT with the L INS i algorithm. Outlier sequences were identified through root to tip distance analysis based on a preliminary phylogenetic reconstruction. Sequences exceeding three standard deviations from the mean root to tip distance or exhibiting clustering patterns inconsistent with associated metadata were excluded. The final dataset comprised 901 sequences.

Maximum likelihood phylogenetic inference was performed using IQ-TREE [64]. The nucleotide substitution model was selected using ModelFinder according to the Bayesian Information Criterion. The selected model corresponded to GTR with empirical base frequencies, a proportion of invariant sites, and gamma distributed rate variation across four categories.

Host states were assigned to all tips based on metadata and reconstructed at internal nodes using a maximum likelihood discrete trait model with equal transition rates. Transmission dynamics between host categories were quantified using transmission count statistics. This approach estimates the number of transitions between host states across the phylogeny while accounting for the inferred evolutionary history.

Model fit diagnostics

Residual analysis was performed using the logarithmic transformation of cumulative human case data. The residuals are defined as the difference between the logarithm of observed cumulative cases and the logarithm of model predicted cumulative cases. The residuals are centered near zero for most of the observation period, indicating that the model captures the overall temporal trend. Larger deviations are observed in early years, which correspond to low case counts. These results reflect known limitations of fitting cumulative data and the simplified structure of the model.
