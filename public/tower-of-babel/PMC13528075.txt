Establishment of biological reference intervals for coagulation indicators in pregnancy using a non-parametric approach
Authors: Xiaoyan Li, Bangzhu Mo, Chunbao Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate changes in activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), thrombin time (TT), and fibrinogen (FIB), and D-dimer (DD) levels during different trimesters of pregnancy in healthy pregnant women. The objective was to establish trimester-specific reference intervals for these coagulation parameters using the non-parametric percentile method as recommended by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) C28-A3 guideline, thereby providing a basis for accurate clinical assessment of coagulation status during pregnancy.MethodsA total of 2,657 healthy pregnant women were selected from Sichuan JinxinXinan Women and Children Hospital from January 2024 to December 2024. Of these women, 646 were included in the first-trimester group; 1,128 were included in the second-trimester group; and 883 were included in the third-trimester group. In addition,198 healthy non-pregnant women were included in the control group. The coagulation indicators of each group were detected and analyzed, and differences in these indicators across different age groups were assessed. Following the CLSI C28-A3 guidelines, reference intervals were established using the non-parametric percentile method (P2.5-P97.5 for APTT, PT, TT, and FIB; P95 for DD). The established reference intervals were validated using an independent sample of 20 healthy pregnant women per trimester.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in any coagulation indicators between pregnant women aged < 30 years and those aged ≥ 30 years in the first-, second-, and third-trimester groups(P > 0.01).APTT, PT, and TT decreased with the advancement of gestational age, and the differences between the periods of each gestation were statistically significant (P < 0.01).FIB and DD levels increased with the advancement of gestational age, and the differences were statistically significant between each trimester(P < 0.01).The established reference intervals for the first trimester were: APTT 25.20–36.48 s, PT 11.10–13.00 s, TT 15.00–17.70 s, FIB 2.58–5.04 g/L, and DD ≤ 1.11 µg/mL. For the second trimester: APTT 24.40–34.56 s, PT 10.90–12.60 s, TT 14.70–17.20 s, FIB 2.95–5.27 g/L, and DD ≤ 2.94 µg/mL. For the third trimester: APTT 24.70–33.40 s, PT 10.60–12.40 s, TT 14.60–16.80 s, FIB 3.39–5.90 g/L, and DD ≤ 4.62 µg/mL. Compared with previously published reference intervals from other populations, our findings show similar trends but with notable differences in absolute values, particularly for FIB and DD, which may be attributed to geographical and ethnic variations. The biological reference intervals of all five coagulation indicators were successfully validated.ConclusionTrimester-specific reference intervals were successfully established for five coagulation indicators using the non-parametric approach recommended by CLSI C28-A3. These intervals provide a crucial tool for accurate clinical diagnosis and prevention of coagulation disorders during pregnancy, potentially reducing misdiagnosis and unnecessary interventions associated with using non-pregnancy reference intervals. However, multicenter validation studies are needed to confirm the generalizability of these findings to other populations.

Background

Pregnancy is a special physiological period for women of childbearing age, and the coagulation system undergoes a series of changes for the body to adapt to the process of pregnancy and childbirth. During pregnancy, coagulation factor reserves increase owing to the increase in estrogen levels, whereas the levels of anticoagulant factors (such as antithrombin) and fibrinolysis-related factors(such as activator of fibrinogen) are significantly reduced, leading to a decline in anticoagulation and fibrinolysis. In addition, the levels of fibrinolysis inhibitors in the placenta are increased, resulting in a physiological hypercoagulable state in pregnant women [1]. Changes in the coagulation and fibrinolytic systems in pregnant women help maintain the integrity of the placenta and effectively prevent postpartum hemorrhage; however, the clinical importance of these alterations cannot be overstated. The hypercoagulable state, while protective against postpartum hemorrhage, simultaneously increases the risk of thromboembolic complications. Venous thromboembolism (VTE) remains a leading cause of maternal mortality in developed countries, with a five- to ten-fold increased risk during pregnancy compared to the non-pregnant state. Conversely, coagulation abnormalities may also predispose to hemorrhagic complications, including antepartum hemorrhage, postpartum hemorrhage, and placental abruption [2]. If the reference intervals of coagulation assays routinely used in non-pregnant women are used in pregnant women, an accurate diagnosis cannot be achieved and the risk of misdiagnosis and omission increases [3]. For example, applying non-pregnancy reference intervals to pregnant women would result in falsely proshorted APTT, PT, and TT values being interpreted as normal, while physiologically elevated FIB and DD would be erroneously flagged as pathological. This could lead to missed diagnoses of acquired coagulation disorders, unnecessary investigation for thrombophilia, inappropriate anticoagulation therapy, or failure to diagnose disseminated intravascular coagulation. Therefore, it is necessary to establish trimester-specific reference intervals for coagulation assays that can be used at different gestational ages in pregnant women. The non-parametric approach for reference interval estimation, as recommended by the CLSI C28-A3 guideline, offers several advantages for coagulation parameters in pregnancy. Unlike parametric methods that assume a Gaussian distribution, the non-parametric percentile method makes no distributional assumptions, making it particularly suitable for analytes that exhibit skewed distributions or when transformation to normality is challenging. Previous studies establishing reference intervals for coagulation parameters in pregnancy have successfully employed this approach, supporting its validity and reliability for this purpose.

In this study, coagulation indicators such as activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), thrombin time (TT), fibrinogen (FIB) levels, and D-dimer (DD) levels were retrospectively analyzed in healthy pregnant women at different gestational stages. The reference intervals of these indicators were established and validated following the CLSI C28-A3 non-parametric methodology to provide an accurate standard for assessing the coagulation status of pregnant women in clinical settings.

Data and methods

Study participants

This was a retrospective cross-sectional study conducted at Sichuan JinxinXinan Women and Children Hospital .Healthy pregnant women were selected from Sichuan JinxinXinan Women and Children Hospital from January 2024 to December 2024 (healthy pregnancy group), using convenience sampling of all eligible participants presenting for routine antenatal care during the study period .The inclusion criteria were as follows: a single live birth, normal blood pressure, absence of urinary protein, and absence of pregnancy-related complications. The exclusion criteria were as follows: twin or multiple pregnancies; pre-pregnancy comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, liver and kidney diseases, malignant tumors, hemorrhagic diseases, thrombotic diseases, autoimmune diseases, infectious diseases, thyroid dysfunction, antenatal hemorrhage, and placenta previa; use of anticoagulant medications in the past 3 months; history of blood transfusion or surgery; history of abnormal abortions or miscarriages; or history of abnormal fetal development. These strict criteria were applied to minimize potential selection bias and ensure the study population represented truly healthy pregnant women. Healthy non-pregnant women of childbearing age who underwent routine physical examination at the hospital during the same period were selected as the control group. The inclusion criteria for the control group were as follows: healthy women of childbearing age, no acute or chronic diseases, no infectious diseases, no drugs affecting coagulation consumed in the past 2 weeks, and no history of pregnancy or a non-pregnant status. The exclusion criteria were as follows: presence of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, autoimmune diseases, and other diseases that may affect coagulation; personal or family history of coagulation disorders; or adverse maternal history. Potential selection bias may arise from the exclusion of high-risk pregnancies and women with comorbidities, which limits the generalizability of our findings to uncomplicated pregnancies only. Additionally, as a single-center study, the results may not be representative of populations from different geographical regions or ethnic backgrounds. This study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Sichuan JinxinXinan Women and Children Hospital (JXFE-EC-2025-013), and waived the need of obtaining informed consent.

Research methods

Specimen collection and testing

A total of 2.7 mL of blood was collected via venipuncture from fasting participants early in the morning. The blood was collected in a vacuum tube containing 0.3mL of sodium citrate (109mmol/L). The blood was mixed well with the anticoagulant and centrifuged at 1500 g for 15 min.The platelet-rich plasma was collected, and coagulation tests were performed within 2 h of specimen collection. APTT, PT, TT, and FIB levels were measured using the clotting method, whereas DD levels were measured using a latex-based immunoturbidimetric assay. The Sekisui CP3000 system was used to assess coagulation. This automated coagulation analyzer was selected for its established performance characteristics, including good precision, accuracy, and throughput suitable for routine clinical laboratory operations. It employs a mechanical clot detection method that is less susceptible to interference from lipemia, icterus, and hemolysis compared to optical methods. The reagents used for evaluating APTT, PT, TT, and FIB levels were obtained from Shanghai Sun Bio (C2192831, C202556, C222391, and C235243, respectively), whereas the reagent used for evaluating DD levels was obtained from Sekisui Medical (933RGV). The quality-control products for APTT, PT, TT, and FIB were obtained from Shanghai Sun Bio (1820RGV). All five tests were performed in strict accordance with the laboratory standard operating procedures.

Quality assurance

The instrument used to assess clot formation is calibrated every 6months in our laboratory. It is regularly used in the external quality assessment of the National Center for Clinical Laboratories and passes the score. Two levels of quality-control products are used for internal quality control every day, and the specimens can be tested only after internal quality control. Intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) for all five parameters were routinely monitored and maintained below 5% for APTT, PT, and TT, and below 8% for FIB and DD, in accordance with laboratory quality specifications. Plasma samples were stored at room temperature and analyzed within 2 h of collection; repeat analyses were not routinely performed as the analyzer’s internal quality control procedures ensured reproducibility.

Grouping of participants

The 2,657 healthy pregnant women were divided into the first-trimester (≤ 12 weeks), second-trimester (13–27 weeks), and third-trimester (≥ 28 weeks) groups with 646; 1,128; and 883 women, respectively. Each of these three groups was further divided into the < 30-year and ≥ 30-year groups to analyze the effect of age on coagulation indicators at the three pregnancy stages. The 30-year cut-off was chosen based on previous literature suggesting potential age-related differences in coagulation parameters and to maintain consistency with previous studies for comparative purposes, although we acknowledge that this cut-off lacks a strong physiological basis. Changes in APTT, PT, TT, FIB levels, and DD levels during pregnancy were assessed, and significant differences between different pregnancy stages were analyzed.

Establishment of reference intervals

According to CLSI C28-A3, the indirect non-parametric percentile method was used to establish the reference intervals of the five coagulation indicators for each pregnancy stage (trimester). The non-parametric approach was chosen because the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test demonstrated non-normal distributions for all coagulation parameters, and data transformation (log or Box-Cox transformation) did not successfully normalize the distributions. This approach aligns with CLSI recommendations for analytes that do not follow Gaussian distributions even after transformation attempts. Outliers were identified using the Tukey method (values exceeding 1.5 times the interquartile range above the third quartile or below the first quartile) and excluded from reference interval calculations after clinical review to ensure they represented true biological outliers rather than analytical errors. Because the low value of DD has no clinical significance in clinical settings, the P95 was used to determine the upper limit of its biological reference interval. The reference intervals of APTT, PT, TT, and FIB levels were expressed by the bilateral percentile method (P2.5–P97.5). Confidence intervals (90% confidence intervals) for the reference limits were calculated using the non-parametric method described in the CLSI C28-A3 guideline to assess the reliability of the established reference intervals. To evaluate the need for trimester-specific reference intervals, we performed nested analysis of variance (ANOVA) with trimester as the primary grouping factor and age as a potential confounding variable. This approach, recommended by the IFCC Committee on Reference Intervals and Decision Limits, allowed us to assess whether the observed differences between trimesters were statistically significant after accounting for age effects.

Validation of the reference intervals

According to the requirements of CLSI C28-A3, 20 cases meeting the inclusion criteria were selected from each group for validating the reference intervals. Validation was considered successful if the observed values of ≤ 2 cases ranged outside the established reference interval. If the validation failed, another 20 cases were selected for validation using the same criteria. If the validation failed again, it indicated that the reference interval was unacceptable and should be re-established.

Statistical analysis

All data were statistically analyzed using the SPSS (version 27.0) software. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess normality (P < 0.01 rejected a normal distribution), and the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the coagulation indicators between the two age subgroups in the first-, second-, and third-trimester groups as well as between the three pregnancy groups and the control (non-pregnant women) group. In addition, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare the indicators between the three pregnancy groups. Nested ANOVA was performed to assess the independent effects of trimester and age on coagulation parameters. A P-value of < 0.01 was considered statistically significant to reduce the risk of type I errors given the multiple comparisons performed.

Comparison of reference intervals with previous studies

To facilitate comparison across populations and analytical methods, we summarized our trimester-specific reference intervals alongside those from three representative studies [3, 5, 7] in Table 5. All studies consistently demonstrated that APTT, PT, and TT shorten, while FIB and DD increase with advancing gestation, reflecting the physiological hypercoagulable state during pregnancy. However, absolute values varied notably. For instance, our PT reference intervals (11.1–13.0 s in first trimester) are longer than those reported by Dai et al. (8.30–10.20 s) and Zeng et al. (10.20–11.90 s), likely due to differences in thromboplastin reagents (Shanghai Sun Bio vs. Roche vs. SYSMEX) and instrument calibration. Our DD upper limits (1.11, 2.94, and 4.62 µg/mL) are higher than those reported by Cui et al. (0.31, 0.73, 2.82 µg/mL) but comparable to Dai et al. (0.969, 2.14, 3.28 µg/mL) and Zeng et al. (0.82, 1.35, 3.4 µg/mL). These comparisons underscore the necessity of establishing laboratory-specific or method-specific reference intervals rather than directly adopting intervals from other populations or platforms.

Discussion

This study established trimester-specific reference intervals for five key coagulation parameters in a large Maternal and Child Health Hospital using the non-parametric approach recommended by CLSI C28-A3 guidelines. Our findings confirm the progressive physiological changes in coagulation during pregnancy and provide clinically relevant reference intervals that can improve the accuracy of coagulation assessment in pregnant women.

Under physiological conditions, the coagulation, anticoagulation, and fibrinolytic systems in the human body maintain a dynamic balance to prevent blood coagulation and loss. Pregnancy is a female-specific physiological process. During pregnancy, changes in the levels of estrogen and progesterone alter the functions of the coagulation, anticoagulation, and fibrinolytic systems, resulting in a hypercoagulable state [4]. In this study, we analyzed changes in five coagulation indicators during pregnancy to understand the effects of age and gestation on these indicators and develop appropriate biological reference intervals for them. The clinical significance of trimester-specific reference intervals extends beyond simply providing normative data. Their implementation in clinical practice can directly impact patient care in several ways. First, they enable accurate diagnosis of coagulation disorders during pregnancy by providing appropriate thresholds for interpretation. For example, using non-pregnancy reference intervals would classify physiologically elevated FIB and DD in the third trimester as pathological, potentially leading to unnecessary investigation for thrombophilia or VTE. Conversely, true coagulation deficiencies might be missed if the pregnancy-associated shortening of APTT, PT, and TT is not accounted for. Second, appropriate reference intervals guide anticoagulation therapy decisions during pregnancy. The upper reference limit for DD, for instance, informs the threshold for suspecting VTE and initiating diagnostic imaging. Using inappropriately low non-pregnancy DD cut-offs would result in excessive imaging and potential over-anticoagulation, exposing pregnant women and their fetuses to unnecessary risks. Third, accurate reference intervals support the diagnosis of pregnancy-specific complications such as preeclampsia, HELLP syndrome, and disseminated intravascular coagulation, where coagulation parameters play crucial diagnostic and monitoring roles.

APTT reflects the function of the intrinsic and common pathways of coagulation in the human body. In this study, we found no significant differences in APTT between the first-trimester and healthy non-pregnant (control) groups. However, APTT gradually decreased over the second and third trimesters, suggesting that endogenous coagulation factors were activated at these pregnancy stages with a consequent increase in their levels. These findings are consistent with those of a study by Cui et al. [5].The magnitude of APTT shortening observed in our study (approximately 10% from first to third trimester) is comparable to that reported in other Asian populations but less pronounced than in some Caucasian populations, suggesting possible ethnic differences in coagulation factor responses to pregnancy.

PT reflects the function of the extrinsic and common pathways of coagulation in the human body. In this study, we found that PT gradually decreased with the advancement of gestational age, which is consistent with the increase in the levels of FVII, an extrinsic coagulation factor, in pregnant women reported in the literature [6].Our PT reference intervals are similar to those established by Dai et al. in another Chinese population but differ from European studies, which generally report slightly higher PT values. These differences may be attributed to variations in thromboplastin reagents, instrumentation, or genetic factors affecting coagulation factor levels.

Furthermore, TT significantly decreased in the first trimester and gradually declined throughout pregnancy, exhibiting statistically significant differences between trimesters. The levels of estrogen and progesterone are significantly increased during pregnancy. These hormones affect the coagulation system and promote the production of thrombin, leading to a hypercoagulable state, which shortens TT. During pregnancy, the levels of procoagulant factors such as factors II, V, VII, IX, and X in the blood increase, whereas those of factors XI and XII decrease. The increased levels of coagulation factors result in enhanced blood clotting ability and faster conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin, leading to the shortening of TT [7].

FIB is a glycoprotein synthesized by the liver that serves as a precursor of fibrin. It plays an important role in coagulation, and its elevated level indicates a hypercoagulable state. In this study, FIB levels were significantly higher in healthy pregnant women than in healthy non-pregnant women. In addition, the levels were significantly different between trimesters, showing a gradual increase with the increasing gestational age. These findings are consistent with those of a study by Liu et al. [8]. However, the reference intervals of FIB established in this study are different from those reported in exiting studies.

DD has important applications in the clinical diagnosis of thrombosis and thrombolysis and is commonly used to eliminate the possibility of venous thromboembolism (VTE) [9].VTE in pregnancy is a serious perinatal complication that can lead to severe adverse maternal outcomes and even death. Although it is rare, it is a major cause of maternal morbidity and mortality [10]. Under physiological conditions, plasma DD levels are higher in pregnant women than in non-pregnant women. Using the reference range of plasma DD in non-pregnant women to guide anticoagulant interventions for VTE and thrombophilia in pregnant women may lead to overuse of anticoagulant drugs or misinterpretation of indications and timing of drug use and, to some extent, increase the risk of hemorrhage, liver dysfunction, and other diseases. In this study, we found that DD levels were not significantly different between the first-trimester and control groups but gradually increased over the second and third trimesters. This positive correlation between DD levels and gestational age has been reported in previous studies [11–13]. The increase in DD levels may be a result of hyperfibrinolysis secondary to the body’s efforts to prevent thrombophilia. Our DD upper reference limits (1.11, 2.94, and 4.62 µg/mL for trimesters 1–3 respectively) are lower than those reported in some Western studies but similar to other Asian reports, supporting the concept of ethnic-specific reference intervals for this parameter.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, as a single-center study conducted in a specific geographical region (Chengdu, China) and exclusively in women of Han ethnicity, the generalizability of our findings to other populations is limited. Racial and ethnic disparities in prenatal testing parameters, including coagulation factors, have been well documented, and our reference intervals may not be appropriate for other ethnic groups without local validation. Second, despite standardized laboratory procedures, between-laboratory variation in analytical methods, reagents, and calibration could affect the transferability of our reference intervals to other institutions. Third, we were unable to evaluate the effects of other potential confounding factors such as body mass index (BMI), parity, and obstetric history. Because these variables were not routinely captured in our laboratory information system during the study period, we did not have access to this data. Consequently, we could not include BMI descriptive statistics in the demographic baseline table, nor could we perform subgroup analyses based on these factors. While these variables may influence coagulation parameters, evidence from a large prospective study by Dai et al. (2023) [7] suggests that parity, gravidity, and advanced maternal age have minimal clinically meaningful impact on coagulation parameters in healthy pregnancies. Their multivariable regression analysis showed very small coefficients for parity (e.g., 0.201 for D-dimer and − 0.083 for fibrinogen) and no significant differences across age groups, concluding that establishing specific reference intervals based on these factors is unnecessary. Furthermore, according to the CLSI C28-A3 guidelines, a minimum of 120 participants per subgroup is recommended for non-parametric reference interval estimation. Our large sample size (n = 2,657) far exceeds this requirement. Although a large sample size cannot eliminate potential bias introduced by unmeasured confounders, it does minimize random variation and enhances the precision and stability of the percentile estimates, particularly at the P2.5 and P97.5 extremes. Thus, despite the absence of certain demographic variables, our reference intervals offer high statistical precision and remain clinically valuable for the target population.

In conclusion, with a large cohort of 2,657 healthy pregnant women, which is among the largest such studies in China, we successfully established trimester-specific reference intervals for coagulation parameters. However, due to the retrospective single-center design, the exclusive enrollment of Han Chinese women, and the absence of individual-level data on BMI and obstetric history, these intervals are primarily applicable only to the local population enrolled. They should not be directly generalized to other ethnic groups, geographic regions, or healthcare settings, as differences in analytical platforms and reagents may further affect transferability. Therefore, external validation is strictly required prior to generalizing these findings. Future prospective, multicenter studies with comprehensive demographic data collection are needed to validate our results and to develop broadly applicable or ethnicity-stratified reference intervals.
