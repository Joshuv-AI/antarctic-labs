Women’s Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice Regarding Oral Short-Acting Contraceptives After Induced Abortion: A Cross-Sectional Study in Beijing, China
Authors: Shan He, Wei Ma, Jie Gao, Juan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbout half of all patients seeking abortion report having a history of prior abortions and are at high risk for repeat abortion. Data about postabortion contraception in China are limited. This study investigated the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) of women undergoing induced abortion regarding postoperative oral short-acting contraceptives.Study DesignThis cross-sectional study enrolled women at Beijing Luhe Hospital, Capital Medical University, from December 2023 to February 2024. The minimal calculated sample size was 385 participants. A self-administered questionnaire was developed to collect demographic information and assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding postabortion oral short-acting contraceptives. Scores >70% of the possible maximum score of each dimension were considered as good knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practice.ResultsAmong 420 women with questionnaires passing quality control, mean knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were 5.60 ± 2.96/12 (46.67%), 31.27 ± 4.54/45 (69.49%), and 21.70 ± 2.94/25 (86.80%). Lower education (junior high school or below) was associated with lower odds of sufficient knowledge and positive attitudes. Higher knowledge scores were associated with higher odds of positive attitude. Higher knowledge and attitude scores and parity >1 were independently associated with higher odds of adequate practice, while age 36–39 was associated with lower odds.ConclusionIn Beijing, women after induced abortions had poor knowledge and attitudes scores, but a proactive intended practice toward short-acting contraception. Specific knowledge items were identified as requiring improvement. Educational interventions should particularly target women with a lower socioeconomic status to reduce repeated induced abortions.

Background

Induced abortion is the intentional medical or surgical termination of pregnancy for any reason.1 Unintended pregnancies account for about 50% of the 208 million pregnancies reported worldwide yearly.1,2 About 40%–60% of unintended pregnancies and about 30% of all pregnancies are reported to end in abortion.1,2 Both medical and surgical options for termination of pregnancy may be offered to patients seeking abortion in the first and second trimesters.1–3 Patient preference will vary. Mortality and other major complications are rare at all gestational ages in countries with access to safe abortion care.3 Postabortion care includes any or all of the following, as needed or desired: follow-up visits, management of regular adverse effects or complications, and contraception services.4 About half of all patients seeking abortion reported having a history of prior abortions and are at high risk for repeat abortion.3 The patients should initiate contraception as soon as possible, as ovulation may resume as early as 8–21 days after abortion.2–5 For patients having surgical abortions, initiating contraception on the day of the procedure should be recommended; all methods, excluding hysteroscopic sterilization, diaphragm, and cervical cap, can be considered.3,4 For patients having medical abortions with mifepristone and misoprostol or with misoprostol alone, hormonal contraception can be started immediately after the first pill of the medical abortion regimen.1,4,6 The use of short-term contraceptives following an abortion can effectively prevent pregnancy and promote endometrial repair. It can help reduce the risk of uterine adhesions and offer enhanced fertility protection, particularly for women desiring future pregnancies.6,7

Self-management after induced abortion is central to reducing the risk of recurrent unintended pregnancy, but some women who undergo induced abortion for medical reasons also need to plan for a desired future pregnancy and may therefore have different contraceptive priorities.1–4 In clinical practice, short-acting oral contraceptives comprise both combined oral contraceptives and progestogen-only pills, which differ in contraindications, safety profile, and counseling requirements, especially in the immediate postabortion period.1–4 Proper knowledge of contraception and pregnancy is necessary to consult the right healthcare professionals promptly regarding postabortion contraception, adopt appropriate health behaviors, and achieve optimal outcomes. Because oral contraceptives are taken at home, without direct supervision, adequate knowledge and favorable attitudes are essential to support proactive and consistent use. Knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) is one of several behavior‑change frameworks that propose different causal architectures (eg, Theory of Planned Behavior, Health Belief Model, and COM‑B) and use structured surveys to generate quantitative and qualitative information about gaps, misconceptions, and misunderstandings on a specific subject in a defined population.8,9 A study in Ethiopia showed that the practice of postabortion contraception was high, at 78%.10 A study in India showed that women admitted for self-induced abortion had a poor KAP of postabortion contraception.11 Another study from India showed that women seeking induced abortion had good knowledge and favorable attitudes toward contraception but poor practice.12

China has undergone rapid demographic and policy changes over recent decades, including the transition from the one‑child policy to a two‑child and subsequently three‑child policy, together with increasing government emphasis on fertility support and reproductive health protection. Within this context, induced abortion remains legally available in accredited medical institutions, but there is growing policy attention to the prevention of unintended pregnancy, reduction of repeated abortions, and provision of standardized postabortion counseling and contraception services. National and expert consensus documents in China have highlighted the importance of postabortion care, including timely initiation of appropriate contraceptive methods to protect endometrial health and preserve future fertility.13,14 No study has characterized Chinese women’s KAP toward short-acting oral contraceptives at the time of induced abortion, which is the most relevant decision window. Access to high‑quality counseling and contraceptive services, as well as women’s understanding and acceptance of postabortion contraception, may vary according to socioeconomic and regional factors. Clarifying women’s knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding short‑acting oral contraceptives in this policy environment is therefore essential to inform targeted educational and service interventions. One study pointed out poor access to and utilization of postabortion care services in Guangzhou (China).15

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the KAP of postabortion women regarding the use of short-acting oral contraceptives. The results could help identify gaps in knowledge that could be targeted by future educational interventions to improve attitudes and practice.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional study enrolled women admitted to the hospital because they were seeking an induced abortion at Beijing Luhe Hospital, Capital Medical University, from December 2023 to February 2024. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Beijing Luhe Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval 2023-LHKY-125-02). All participants signed the informed consent form before completing the questionnaire.

The inclusion criteria were 1) ≥18 years of age and 2) consulting for induced abortion. The exclusion criteria were 1) having already undergone medical abortion, or women with contraindications to oral short-acting contraceptives.

where Z2 is the confidence coefficient, p is the proportion, and d is the margin of error. The sample size is maximized when p = 0.5. A 95% confidence interval involves a Z-value of 1.96. Precision was assumed at 5%. Hence, a minimum of 385 participants was needed.

Questionnaire and Quality Control

The questionnaire design was based on the “Expert Consensus on Promoting Endometrial Repair after Induced Abortion”, “Chinese Expert Consensus on Clinical Application of Compound Oral Contraceptives”, and “Contraceptive Service Standards after Induced Abortion (2018)”. After the initial draft of the questionnaire was designed, it was modified based on the opinions of two experts. The questionnaire was pilot-tested on a small scale (79 participants) using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as the formal survey. The overall, knowledge, attitude, and practice Cronbach’s α (Feldt’s 95% confidence intervals) for the pilot survey were 0.820 (0.759–0.868), 0.833 (0.776–0.849), 0.767 (0.689–0.825), and 0.767 (0.686–0.823), indicating good questionnaire reliability. The Cronbach’s α and item-total correlations are shown in Supplement Tables 1–4. As two items (K11 and K12) were added after pilot testing, the Cronbach’s α and item-total correlations were also analyzed for the questionnaires collected in formal survey. The overall, knowledge, attitude, and practice Cronbach’s α (Feldt’s 95% confidence intervals) for the formal survey were 0.705 (0.665–0.745), 0.813 (0.788–0.838), 0.610 (0.558–0.663), and 0.810 (0.785–0.836), indicating acceptable questionnaire reliability. The Cronbach’s α and item-total correlations are shown in Supplement Tables 5–8.

The final questionnaire was in Chinese and collected information in four dimensions, comprising 41 items (Supplementary materials-Questionnaire). The basic information section included 12 items, the knowledge dimension included 12 items, the attitude dimension included nine items, and the practice dimension included eight items. For the knowledge dimension, 1 point was given for a correct answer and 0 points for an incorrect or unclear answer, with a score range of 0–12 points. The attitude dimension used a 5-point Likert scale, with scores assigned based on the degree of positivity. A2, A3, and A9 were scored from strongly agree (5 points) to strongly disagree (1 point), while the remaining items were reverse-scored, with a score range of 9–45 points. In the practice section, P3 investigated the main reasons for not accepting oral short-acting contraceptives after induced abortion, P4 investigated whether long-acting contraceptives were considered after induced abortion if there were no plans for further pregnancy, and P8 investigated how knowledge about contraception and induced abortion surgery was obtained. The remaining five questions used a 5-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from strongly agree (5 points) to strongly disagree (1 point), with a score range of 5–25 points.

The participants were recruited through convenience sampling through the outpatient clinics when they were admitted for an induced abortion in the gynecology and obstetrics clinic. The researchers invited the patients to participate in the study. If they accepted, they filled out the questionnaire before the abortion by scanning a QR code. The first page of the questionnaire was the consent form, which had to be completed. The data were collected using the Questionnaire Star platform. The researcher informed the participants that the enrollment would not affect clinical treatment and that all questionnaires were completed anonymously. The participants did not receive any education or guidance before completing the questionnaire. The Questionnaire Star platform was configured to require completion of all items, there were no missing data within collected questionnaires.

If any questions arose during the answering process, the research team members were responsible for promptly answering the participants’ questions. After all data collection was completed, the research team members conducted a quality check of the questionnaire. Questionnaires with obvious logical errors or answered with the same option for all items were considered invalid.

Statistical Analysis

Stata 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations and analyzed using Student’s t-test (comparison of two groups) or ANOVA (comparison of more than two groups). Categorical variables were expressed as n (%) and analyzed using the chi-square test. Spearman correlation analysis was used to analyze the correlations between the KAP dimensions. The KAP dimension scores were defined as good knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practice when >70% of the maximum possible score (ie, >8.4 for knowledge, >31.5 for attitude, and >17.5 for practice).8,17–19

Multiple logistic regression was used with knowledge, attitude, and practice scores as dependent variables to analyze the independent risk factors for KAP scores. For the practice dimension, the participants were grouped as “Strongly agree” and “Agree” to the question “After an induced abortion, I will follow the doctor’s advice to take short-acting contraceptives orally.” in one group, and the others in another group. The characteristics of the two groups were analyzed. Variables with P <0.05 in the univariable logistic regression analyses were included in the multiple regression analyses. Age, parity, education, and income were included a priori in all multivariable models on substantive grounds, irrespective of their univariable P values, while other candidate variables continue to follow the previous screening approach. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for all independent variables to assess multicollinearity, with VIF values close to 1 indicating negligible collinearity and values >5 suggesting potentially problematic multicollinearity that may inflate standard errors and destabilize regression estimates. Two-tailed P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Knowledge

The mean knowledge score was 5.60 ± 2.96 on a maximum of 12 (46.67%), indicating poor knowledge. Higher knowledge scores were observed with higher education (P < 0.001) and higher income (P < 0.001) (Table 1). The knowledge item with the highest rate of correct answers was K2 (85.95%; “The more times induced abortion is performed, the closer the intervals, and the larger the gestational age, the more likely the endometrium is to be damaged”). Attitude

The mean attitude score was 31.27 ± 4.54 on a maximum of 45 (69.49%), indicating an unfavorable attitude. Higher attitude scores were observed with younger age (P = 0.017) and higher education (P = 0.002) (Table 1). Supplement Table 10 presents the distribution of the answers to the attitude items and the scores for the individual items.

Practice

The mean practice score was 21.70 ± 2.94 on a maximum of 25 (86.80%), indicating proactive practice. Higher practice scores were observed with higher education (P < 0.001), full-time housewife (P = 0.030), and higher income (P = 0.013) (Table 1). Supplement Table 11 presents the distribution of the answers to the practice items. The main reasons for not accepting short-acting oral contraceptives after surgery were worries about fertility (50.71%) and economic reasons (23.18%) (Figure 2). If there is no plan for future pregnancy, 33.81% would take long-acting contraception after abortion, 20.71% would not, 28.33% were uncertain, and 17.14% would follow the physician’s advice (Figure 3). The most important means of getting knowledge about postabortion contraception included the Internet (55.24%), healthcare providers (44.76%), community lectures (22.62%), and social circles (21.19%) (Figure 4). Discussion

Data about postabortion contraception are lacking in China. This study investigated the KAP of women who underwent induced abortion regarding postoperative oral short-acting contraceptives. The results suggest that women after an induced abortion had poor knowledge and unfavorable attitudes, but proactive practice toward short-acting contraception, and specific knowledge items were identified as requiring improvement. Educational interventions should particularly target women with a lower socioeconomic status.

The decision on contraception and the type of contraception ultimately belongs to the women undergoing an induced abortion. Still, making the most appropriate decision requires proper knowledge of contraception about the advantages, side effects, the risk of pregnancy recurrence, and plans for fertility.1–4 The advantages of short-acting contraception include relatively mild and well-known side effects and the possibility of stopping them at any time to restore fertility. On the other hand, this method requires close adherence to the dosing schedule to minimize the risk of pregnancy or the combined use of a barrier contraceptive method.

In this study, over half of the participants identified the Internet as their main source of information on postabortion contraception, followed closely by healthcare providers, with smaller but non‑negligible contributions from community lectures and social circles. Given the known risk of misinformation in online reproductive health content and the documented inconsistencies in contraceptive knowledge among providers,20–24 these channels represent critical targets for quality improvement. Clinician‑curated online resources on widely used Chinese health platforms (eg, large commercial health portals and mainstream social media health channels), structured provider training on up‑to‑date contraceptive evidence, and standardized peri‑abortion counseling protocols could help improve the accuracy and consistency of information delivered through both digital and clinical pathways. The second most important source of information was physicians. While international studies have documented important gaps in contraceptive knowledge among physicians,22–24 comparable large‑scale surveys among OB‑GYNs in mainland China remain limited, and this represents an important evidence gap for future research. Of course, physicians with inaccurate knowledge will pass on that inaccurate knowledge.

According to the KAP framework, knowledge and attitudes are often conceptualized as important antecedents of health‑related behaviors.8,9 In the present study, knowledge was independently and positively associated with both attitude and practice, and attitude was independently and positively associated with practice. This pattern suggests that strengthening women’s understanding of short‑acting oral contraceptives could, in principle, support more favorable attitudes and better contraceptive behaviors. Basic and accurate information on short‑acting contraceptives (including potential harms and side effects, contraindications, implications for fertility and congenital malformations, and their role in endometrial repair after abortion) should therefore be provided to women undergoing induced abortion. At the same time, the marked discrepancy between poor knowledge and relatively high practice scores may have at least two explanations: women may genuinely be willing to follow physicians’ advice even when they do not fully understand the rationale, but the practice items were framed as “I will follow the doctor’s advice…” and were completed in the clinic before the abortion, so social desirability bias is also a plausible contributor. Similar patterns of poor knowledge but high reported practice have been observed in previous studies,10,12 although the available data remain limited and warrant cautious interpretation. Notably, the two worst‑answered knowledge items (long‑term safety and congenital malformation risk) corresponded closely to the most frequently cited reason for declining short‑acting contraception (worry about future fertility), highlighting a direct and policy‑relevant link between specific misconceptions and contraceptive hesitancy.

Given that the poorest‑answered knowledge items concerned long‑term safety (K12) and congenital malformation risk (K8), and that worry about future fertility was the dominant stated reason for declining short‑acting oral contraceptives, postabortion counseling should explicitly and systematically address these specific misconceptions. Counseling protocols could include simple, evidence‑based explanations of the long‑term safety profile of short‑acting oral contraceptives and their lack of association with fetal congenital malformations, supported by written materials designed for a junior‑high reading level. In addition, clinician‑curated online content delivered through commonly used health platforms and social media, aligned with the messages provided in the clinic, may help correct misinformation encountered on the Internet. Such structured peri‑abortion counseling and tailored patient education are likely to be more effective than generic information in improving understanding and acceptance of short‑acting oral contraceptives, particularly among women with lower education and health literacy.

In the present study, a low socioeconomic status was generally associated with poorer KAP, and participants with a high willingness to take postabortion contraception based on their physician’s recommendations had a higher level of education than participants with a low willingness. This finding is consistent with evidence that general health literacy is associated with socioeconomic status.25 It also highlights that women of a lower socioeconomic status and undergoing induced abortion should be particularly targeted by future educational interventions to improve KAP. Still, a study from Guangzhou highlighted that access to and utilization of postabortion care were limited, especially in disadvantaged women,15 suggesting that postabortion care services themselves should also be improved. The association of older age with lower odds of positive practice could be due to several reasons, including different fertility intentions at higher parity, perceived decline in fertility, method preference shift, cohort and information effects, and socioeconomic and structural confounding. The study design and data collected do not allow for determining the exact reason.

The present sample was drawn from an urban hospital in Beijing, where most participants held urban residence and relatively high educational attainment. In China, structural factors such as the hukou system, urban–rural disparities, and the large migrant population in metropolitan areas can substantially influence access to family planning services, continuity of care, and eligibility for publicly funded counseling and contraception. The Guangzhou study by Wang et al15 highlighted that disadvantaged and migrant women face particular barriers to postabortion care. The relatively high use of the Internet and formal healthcare providers as information sources in our cohort may therefore not be generalizable to women in rural settings or those with unstable hukou status, underscoring the need to tailor interventions to different subgroups within China.

Strengths and Limitations

A strength of this study is that the participants filled out the questionnaire before the abortion; at that time, they had not yet received postoperative counseling about postabortion care, which likely reduced the influence of clinicians’ explanations and immediate postprocedure emotions on their answers.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inference between knowledge, attitudes, and practice; associations observed between KAP dimensions and sociodemographic factors may reflect underlying, unmeasured constructs rather than directional effects. In addition, the study provides only a single “snapshot” of women’s KAP at the time of abortion, and does not capture how their knowledge, attitudes, and practices may evolve after exposure to postabortion counseling, recovery experiences, or subsequent pregnancies; however, these data can serve as a historical baseline for future longitudinal or interventional studies. Second, this was a single-center study in an urban hospital in Beijing, with a relatively modest sample size compared with the broader population of women seeking induced abortion in China. Consequently, some 95% CIs were wide due to the small number of events in certain combined categories. Thus, the findings may not be generalizable to other regions, rural areas, or different levels of medical institutions with distinct service models and patient profiles. Third, the study did not differentiate between women undergoing induced abortion for unplanned pregnancy and those undergoing abortion for medical indications (eg, missed abortion), even though their fertility intentions, perceived risks, and contraceptive needs may differ substantially, which could dilute or mask important subgroup-specific patterns in KAP. Still, women with a previous history of abortion were excluded because they would have received education about contraception, introducing bias. Counseling pathways, timing of contraceptive discussion, and perceived side‑effect profiles may differ between medical and surgical abortion, and women who complete medical abortion before presentation may have a distinct KAP profile that is not captured here. As a result, the findings should be interpreted as most applicable to women managed in surgical settings or at the pre‑initiation stage of medical abortion, and may not generalize to all women undergoing medical abortion in other care pathways. Fourth, the questionnaire was developed by local investigators based on Chinese expert consensus documents and national standards, and although pilot testing showed acceptable reliability, its content and emphasis may reflect local practices, guidelines, and policies; certain items may not fully capture broader cultural, religious, or regional determinants of postabortion contraceptive decision-making. Furthermore, in routine practice, short‑acting oral contraceptives include both combined oral contraceptives and progestogen‑only pills, which have different contraindications, safety profiles, and counseling needs, particularly in the immediate postabortion period,1–4 and the present questionnaire focused on women’s overall KAP toward the use of such short-acting oral contraceptives after abortion, without distinguishing between specific formulations. Fifth, as with all self-administered KAP surveys, responses are vulnerable to social desirability bias, whereby participants provide answers that they perceive as socially acceptable or consistent with medical recommendations rather than their true beliefs or behaviors.26,27 Considering the relatively high practice scores compared with the knowledge and attitude scores, it is possible that some women overreported their willingness to follow physicians’ advice on short-acting contraception, which would lead to an overestimation of “adequate” practice. Furthermore, although the questionnaire underwent a pilot test, the absence of factor analysis and test-retest analysis limited its psychometric evaluation. Sixth, the use of a self-completed online questionnaire requires a minimal level of literacy and familiarity with smartphones or digital tools, so women with very low literacy or limited digital access may be underrepresented, potentially underestimating the true extent of poor knowledge and unfavorable attitudes in more disadvantaged groups. Seventh, because convenience sampling was used, the exact number of women who were invited to participate is unknown; hence, the response rate cannot be calculated.

Conclusions

Women seeking induced abortion at an urban hospital in Beijing had poor knowledge (mean 46.7% of the maximum score) and unfavorable attitudes (69.5%), but reported a high intention to follow doctors’ recommendations regarding short‑acting oral contraceptives after abortion (practice score 86.8%), with this knowledge-practice gap emerging as the key signal of the study. Two specific misconceptions, long‑term safety (K12) and congenital malformation risk (K8), which had the lowest correct response rates, directly mirrored the most frequently cited reason for declining short‑acting contraception, namely worry about future fertility, highlighting concrete targets for educational messaging. Women with junior‑high education or below and those in the lowest income strata consistently showed poorer KAP scores, indicating that they are the subgroup most likely to benefit from tailored educational and counseling interventions. It is also important to note that the practice dimension captured intended behavior (“I will follow the doctor’s advice”) rather than observed adherence, and the findings should be interpreted accordingly.
