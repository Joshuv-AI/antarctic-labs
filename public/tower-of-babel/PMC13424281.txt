Near-Infrared and Red-Light Photobiomodulation for Ocular Aging and Diseases: A Narrative Review
Authors: Ethan Waisberg, Joshua Ong, Luke Yaldo, Rahul Kumar, Wei Wang, Valerio Calabresi, Giulia Coco, Francisco Gonzalez-Lima, Andrew G. Lee, Giuseppe Giannaccare
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Near-infrared (NIR) and red light photobiomodulation (PBM) has gained increasing interest as a non-invasive therapeutic approach for a variety of medical conditions including ocular diseases. The eye represents a particularly suitable target for light-based therapies owing to its optical accessibility and the high mitochondrial demand of various tissues such as the retina and optic nerve. Ocular aging and several ophthalmic disorders are associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation, providing a biological rationale for the use of PBM as a potential adjunctive approach. This narrative review provides an evidence-weighted, indication-specific synthesis of NIR and red-light PBM in ophthalmology, emphasizing device- and protocol-dependence, differences between multiwavelength PBM and repeated low-level red-light therapy, study-design limitations, safety considerations, and clinical translation gaps. A structured literature search was conducted using major scientific databases to identify relevant experimental studies, clinical trials, and observational reports. The review focuses on proposed mitochondria-centered mechanisms of action, including cytochrome c oxidase-mediated signaling, nitric oxide release, reactive oxygen species-driven hormetic responses, and downstream anti-inflammatory effects. Particular emphasis is reserved to dry age-related macular degeneration, for which literature is more relevant, and to dry eye disease owing to meibomian gland dysfunction and myopia, for which a body of evidence is progressively growing. For other ocular indications, data remain preliminary or exploratory. Overall, available data support biological plausibility and suggest possible functional or anatomical signals in selected ocular diseases, but the clinical evidence remains heterogeneous, with substantial variability in devices, wavelengths, dosimetry, treatment protocols, and study design, and remains insufficient to define PBM as established therapy for most indications. However, the clinical evidence remains heterogeneous, with substantial variability in devices, wavelengths, dosimetry, and treatment protocols. Although available studies generally report favorable short-term tolerability, long-term safety and the role of PBM in routine ophthalmic practice remain insufficiently defined. Well-designed, adequately randomized controlled trials with standardized treatment parameters are required to determine efficacy, optimal protocols, and long-term clinical relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-026-01427-9.

Introduction

In recent years, the use of near-infrared (NIR) and red light has rapidly gained attention as a non-invasive approach investigated across several medical conditions, such as wound injuries [1], ischemic stroke [2], ischemic heart injury [3], and androgenic alopecia [4]. The overall effect produced by NIR/red light therapy is referred to as photobiomodulation (PBM), which is believed to promote regeneration and tissue repair by stimulating cellular activity [4]. The aging process is characterized by a reduction in cellular function and activity, which occurs as a result of a deterioration in mitochondrial function. NIR/red light has been proposed as a non-invasive strategy to modulate mitochondrial function in ocular tissues, but mechanistic plausibility should not be equated with established therapeutic benefit. Therefore, careful interpretation of heterogeneous evidence and transparent reporting of dosimetry are essential when translating mechanistic rationale into clinical claims.

Despite the long history of light therapy, evidence of the efficacy of NIR/red therapy in ophthalmic diseases remains limited [5]. Because the literature is heterogeneous and not equally developed across ocular diseases, this review applies an indication-specific, evidence-weighted framework, with particular emphasis on age-related macular degeneration (AMD), dry eye disease (DED), and myopia. These indications were prioritized because they currently represent the ophthalmic settings in which light-based therapy appears the most clinically informative, combining early efficacy signals with particularly relevant target populations, given the high prevalence of dry AMD in aging populations, DED in routine ophthalmic practice, and myopia in children and adolescents. Accordingly, the primary aim of this review is to provide an evidence-weighted overview of the ocular applications of NIR and red-light PBM, distinguishing indications with comparatively more developed clinical evidence from those supported mainly by preclinical or exploratory data. This review is not intended to provide a formal comparative effectiveness analysis against established therapies or to define optimal dosing parameters, which remain insufficiently standardized across studies. Accordingly, the manuscript should be interpreted as a structured narrative synthesis rather than as a systematic review or meta-analysis. Instead, it aims at summarizing biological plausibility, clinical outcomes, device- and protocol-specific variability, safety considerations, and key gaps that should guide future research. The differentiated contribution of this review is therefore not to present PBM as a single uniform therapeutic modality but to organize the field around a clinically usable evidence hierarchy. Multiwavelength PBM for retinal and ocular surface indications was separated from repeated low-level red light (RLRL) used for pediatric myopia, distinguishing device platforms and manufacturers when reported, and evaluating whether outcomes are supported by controlled human data, preliminary uncontrolled observations, or mainly preclinical rationale. By integrating device/protocol reporting, study design, safety considerations, and practical barriers such as treatment burden and generalizability, this review aims at providing a critical map of where PBM is closest to clinical translation and where it remains exploratory.

In this review, “PBM” is used as the umbrella term describing the biological effects induced by non-ionizing light delivered at low irradiances that do not cause tissue ablation or clinically relevant thermal damage. The terms “low-level light therapy” and “low-level laser therapy” (LLLT) are used in literature as legacy descriptors for PBM and may involve either laser (coherent) or light-emitting diode (LED) (non-coherent) sources; here, we preferentially use PBM for consistency, noting LLLT only when it appears in original study descriptions. “Red-light therapy” refers to PBM delivered predominantly in the red range (typically ~620–700 nm), whereas “NIR therapy” refers to PBM delivered in the NIR range (typically ~700-1100 nm). When studies use mixed wavelengths (e.g., red + NIR ± yellow/amber), we specify the wavelength(s) and refer to the intervention as multiwavelength PBM.

In addition, pediatric myopia literature often refers to repeated low-level red-light (RLRL) exposure, typically delivered as brief, home-based sessions (e.g., minutes, twice daily) and discussed under myopia-control frameworks that differ materially from posterior-segment multiwavelength PBM protocols used in dry AMD. We therefore discuss RLRL as a distinct clinical paradigm and avoid mechanistic or protocol extrapolation from PBM to myopia.

Near Infrared/Red Light

Red and near-infrared PBM refers to the therapeutic use of non-ionizing light, generally delivered by low-power lasers or light-emitting diodes (LEDs), at irradiances intended to modulate cellular function without causing tissue ablation or clinically relevant thermal injury [5, 6]. Lasers provide coherent and typically monochromatic light, whereas LEDs are non-coherent sources that can be configured in arrays and may deliver one or more wavelengths over larger treatment areas (Fig. 1) [7]. In ophthalmology, this distinction is particularly relevant because retinal safety depends on wavelength, irradiance, exposure duration, beam geometry, and treatment repetition. Unlike ablative ophthalmic lasers, PBM protocols use lower irradiances and treatment parameters intended to avoid destructive retinal energy delivery, while still modulating cellular function (Fig. 2). Mechanisms of Action

NIR/red light therapy is thought to act through multiple dose-dependent mechanisms, with cytochrome c oxidase representing one of the best-characterized proposed intracellular photoacceptors in the red/NIR range [8, 9]. Experimental studies support absorption of red to NIR light by cytochrome c oxidase and subsequent changes in mitochondrial membrane potential, oxygen consumption, and electron transport chain activity [10–12]. Mitochondrial effects of PBM have also been demonstrated in vivo in human tissues such as skeletal muscle [13] and brain [14], although these studies often use longer wavelengths because of deeper tissue penetration requirements. However, in vitro findings should be translated cautiously because they cannot fully reproduce tissue perfusion, oxygen delivery, and vascular responses occurring in vivo. Beyond mitochondrial respiration, proposed PBM mechanisms include nitric oxide release, transient reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling, and downstream changes in cellular metabolism. Nitric oxide release from cytochrome c oxidase may contribute to vasodilation and tissue oxygenation, with hemodynamic effects demonstrated in vivo using broadband near-infrared spectroscopy [9]. Low-level ROS generation may act as a signaling mechanism and upregulate antioxidant or cytoprotective pathways, whereas higher doses may become pro-oxidant, consistent with the biphasic dose–response known as hormesis [15–17]. Additional downstream effects may involve gene expression, cytoskeletal organization, cellular electrical properties, adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-related signaling, calcium handling, cyclic adenosine monophosphate, apoptosis, and mitogenic pathways [5, 18, 19]. Anti-inflammatory effects have also been proposed, as PBM may modulate mitochondrial activity, cytokine balance, and tissue metabolic environment in experimental settings [20–23]. Nevertheless, some proposed mechanisms, particularly thermal or non-mitochondrial pathways, remain insufficiently supported in vivo. Therefore, mechanistic plausibility should be used to frame clinical hypotheses rather than to infer clinical efficacy. The Role of Mitochondria in the Eye

Mitochondria are essential for ocular bioenergetics, calcium homeostasis, and neuronal survival [24]. Retinal ganglion cells and photoreceptors have particularly high energy demands, and mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in age-related retinal vulnerability and several ophthalmic diseases [24–27]. In experimental models, age-related decline in retinal mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP production, and retinal function has been associated with increased chronic inflammation and photoreceptor loss [27–30]. Within this biological context, red and NIR wavelengths have been proposed to modulate retinal bioenergetics and inflammatory signaling, whereas shorter wavelengths may exert distinct and potentially deleterious effects on cellular stress pathways [27, 31]. Experimental retinal models have reported improved mitochondrial function, ATP production, and cellular survival after red-light exposure [32]. Supportive observations from non-ocular invertebrate models, including Drosophila and bumble bees, provide additional mechanistic context but should not be interpreted as direct ophthalmic evidence [33, 34]. Taken together, these findings provide a biological rationale for investigating ocular PBM but they do not establish clinical efficacy. Method of Literature Search and Evidence Synthesis

A structured literature search was conducted using PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Scopus. Literature included in this narrative review covered publications from 1986 to March 2026, corresponding to the oldest and most recent references included in the manuscript; human clinical ocular PBM studies ranged from 2008 to March 2026. The search strategy combined terms related to the intervention and ocular indications, including “photobiomodulation,” “low-level light therapy,” “low-level laser therapy,” “near-infrared light,” “red light therapy,” “repeated low-level red-light,” “RLRL,” “ophthalmology,” “retina,” “age-related macular degeneration,” “dry eye,” “meibomian gland dysfunction,” “myopia,” “diabetic retinopathy,” “diabetic macular edema,” “retinitis pigmentosa,” “glaucoma,” and “optic neuropathy.” Reference lists of relevant reviews, meta-analyses, and eligible original studies were also screened to identify additional pertinent publications. Eligible studies included peer-reviewed English-language articles reporting mechanistic or preclinical data in ocular models, as well as clinical studies evaluating ocular outcomes in human participants exposed to red or near-infrared PBM or repeated low-level red-light therapy. Human clinical evidence included randomized or sham-controlled trials, prospective interventional studies, retrospective cohort studies, and, when higher-level evidence was unavailable, small case series or case reports. Studies were excluded if they did not report ocular outcomes, did not involve red or near-infrared PBM/RLRL, used ablative or photocoagulative laser interventions rather than PBM, lacked sufficient treatment-related information, were non-peer-reviewed, or were available only as conference abstracts without full-text data. Study selection was performed in a staged manner. Titles and abstracts were first reviewed for relevance to ocular PBM or RLRL, followed by full-text review of potentially eligible articles. For each included study, extracted information included disease indication, study design, population, comparator or sham control when present, device or manufacturer when reported, wavelength(s), dosimetry or treatment schedule, follow-up duration, main efficacy outcomes, safety findings, and key interpretative considerations. Because this manuscript was designed as a structured narrative review rather than a systematic review, no formal protocol was registered, no Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)-style flow diagram was generated, and no formal count of records screened, excluded, and included was produced. Owing to marked heterogeneity across devices, dosimetry, endpoints, follow-up duration, and study designs, findings were synthesized narratively and no quantitative pooling was performed. Although no formal risk-of-bias scoring tool or Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) assessment was applied, evidence was qualitatively appraised across indications according to predefined domains relevant to study quality and clinical applicability, including study design, control or sham use, masking, sample size, follow-up duration, outcome consistency, dosimetry reporting, safety monitoring, funding or manufacturer involvement when reported, and clinical generalizability. Indications were then categorized as having comparatively more developed, limited but emerging, or preliminary/exploratory evidence, without implying formal GRADE ratings. A summary of this qualitative evidence hierarchy and its implications for clinical applicability is presented in Table 3. To reduce selective interpretation, the synthesis distinguished controlled human data from uncontrolled observations, case reports, and preclinical evidence. Negative, neutral, and conflicting studies were included when available. At present, the largest human datasets exist in nonexudative AMD using multiwavelength PBM, with smaller but growing bodies of evidence in DED/MGD and pediatric myopia. Other ophthalmic indications are supported mainly by small cohorts, case reports, short follow-up studies, or preclinical data. Accordingly, each disease-specific section separates biological rationale from human clinical evidence and highlights limitations that may influence interpretation, including device specificity, protocol heterogeneity, incomplete dosimetry reporting, limited masking or sham control, small sample size, and uncertain durability of effect. To facilitate cross-study comparison and reduce reliance on lengthy narrative descriptions, key human studies and protocols are summarized in comparative tables. Supplementary Table S1 in the electronic supplementary material provides a comprehensive overview of human studies across indications, including study design, wavelength/device characteristics, dosimetry, treatment schedule, main findings, and key interpretative considerations. Throughout the manuscript, wording was calibrated to reflect the strength of evidence: controlled human data are distinguished from uncontrolled observations, case reports, and preclinical findings, and clinical claims are avoided when evidence remains preliminary or exploratory. Age-Related Macular Degeneration

AMD is a major cause of irreversible vision loss in older adults and represents one of the most clinically relevant potential applications of ocular PBM. Although prevalence estimates vary according to age, ethnicity, and disease classification, the global prevalence of AMD has been estimated at approximately 8.7% among individuals aged 45–85 years, corresponding to about 196 million affected people in 2020 and a projected 288 million by 2040 [35]. Current management of non-neovascular AMD is mainly based on risk-factor modification, nutritional supplementation in appropriate patients, monitoring for progression, and timely detection of neovascular conversion. Against this background, non-invasive approaches such as PBM have attracted interest as potential adjunctive strategies, although they should not be considered replacements for established monitoring or treatment pathways.

Throughout this section, the term “dry AMD” is used descriptively for non-neovascular AMD, but the cited PBM studies enrolled heterogeneous phenotypes that should not be considered interchangeable. Under current clinical classification, intermediate AMD is characterized by large drusen and/or pigmentary abnormalities associated with at least medium drusen, whereas geographic atrophy represents late dry AMD rather than intermediate disease. Accordingly, studies enrolling eyes with drusen alone, intermediate AMD, or nonfoveal geographic atrophy should be interpreted as addressing different points along the dry AMD spectrum rather than a single uniform population. In developed countries, AMD is a leading cause of blindness [36]. Among ocular PBM indications, non-neovascular AMD has the largest clinical literature, but the available evidence remains heterogeneous and device-specific. The biological rationale is supported by the role of mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation in the retinal pigment epithelium–photoreceptor complex; however, this mechanistic rationale should not be interpreted as proof of clinical efficacy [36]. Early uncontrolled and small prospective studies, including the Ivandic series and the TORPA/TORPA 2 studies, suggested improvements in visual function and, in some cases, drusen-related parameters [37–39]. These findings were clinically interesting but should be regarded as hypothesis generating because of the absence of sham controls, small or heterogeneous cohorts, mixed AMD phenotypes, and device-specific protocols [37–39]. More rigorous evidence has subsequently come from randomized or sham-controlled studies, particularly those evaluating the Valeda Light Delivery System (LumiThera, Inc., Poulsbo, WA, USA), which delivers 590, 660, and 850 nm light, in the Markowitz study and the LIGHTSITE clinical trial program [40–43]. Taken together, these studies suggest a modest short-term functional signal, generally in the range of approximately 4–5 ETDRS letters in selected trial populations, with additional exploratory anatomical signals involving drusen burden and geographic atrophy [40–43]. However, sham-arm improvement, restrictive eligibility criteria, modest effect sizes, and the fact that longer-term data derive from the same trial program limit the strength of clinical inference [40–43]. Not all studies have shown benefit, and findings appear to depend strongly on device platform, AMD phenotype, and treatment protocol. Grewal et al. did not observe significant structural or functional improvement in a more narrowly defined intermediate AMD cohort treated with a handheld 670-nm LED source [44]. Additional studies, including ELECTROLIGHT and DRUSEN, reported short-term functional or drusen-related signals using different PBM platforms, including Valeda and the EYE-LIGHT® device (Espansione Group S.p.A., Bologna, Italy), but these studies differed substantially in design, wavelength configuration, follow-up, and dosimetry reporting [45, 46]. Therefore, AMD literature should not be interpreted as evidence for a single standardized PBM intervention but as a series of device- and protocol-specific datasets. Across studies, the most recurrent anatomical signal has been change in drusen burden, whereas evidence for slowing geographic atrophy remains narrower and less definitive. Drusen regression or stabilization should not yet be treated as a validated surrogate for reduced progression to late AMD. Similarly, the most defensible clinical endpoint at present is modest short-term improvement or stabilization of visual function, rather than restoration of normal vision or established disease modification. Current evidence does not support counseling patients to expect definite visual improvement nor does it support offering PBM routinely to dry AMD eyes with normal visual acuity. The clinical meaning of the LIGHTSITE findings remains debated. Post-publication correspondence and subsequent commentary have raised concerns regarding between-group comparability, sham-arm improvement, dropout timing, protocol details, and interpretation of secondary anatomical outcomes [47–49]. Recent meta-analyses have also urged caution, concluding that the evidence base remains limited by small samples, heterogeneity, and uncertain clinical meaningfulness of statistically significant findings [50, 51]. Overall, PBM in non-neovascular AMD should be framed as an investigational, device- and protocol-specific adjunctive strategy for selected trial-like patients, particularly those with early or intermediate dry AMD and relatively preserved but not normal visual acuity. It should not replace established AMD management, including risk-factor modification, nutritional supplementation when appropriate, monitoring for neovascular conversion, and standard treatment pathways. Further independent sham-controlled trials with standardized endpoints, longer follow-up, and clearer disease-modification outcomes are needed before routine clinical adoption can be recommended.

Dry Eye Disease

DED is a highly prevalent ocular surface disorder and a frequent cause of ocular discomfort, fluctuating vision, and reduced quality of life. Reported prevalence varies widely, ranging from approximately 5% to 50% across studies, depending on diagnostic criteria, population characteristics, and whether symptoms or clinical signs are used to define disease [52]. Evaporative DED owing to meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) is one of the most common clinical subtypes [53]. Current management is typically stepwise and may include patient education, lid hygiene, lubricants, anti-inflammatory topical therapy, thermal approaches, and device-based treatments such as intense pulsed light (IPL) in selected patients. Within this therapeutic landscape, PBM/LLLT has been investigated as a non-invasive adjunctive option, particularly for MGD-related evaporative DED. Clinical evidence for PBM/LLLT in DED is less developed than in AMD but has expanded across randomized, prospective, comparative, and combination-treatment studies, particularly in evaporative DED owing to MGD [54–60]. Overall, the available evidence suggests that PBM/LLLT may improve symptoms and selected tear-film or ocular-surface parameters, including ocular discomfort, tear production, tear-film stability, lipid-layer measures, and meibum quality [54–60]. By contrast, effects on meibomian gland structure, lid margin signs, and longer-term disease modification appear less consistent [54–60]. Current literature can be interpreted according to treatment context. Stand-alone PBM/LLLT studies suggest possible independent clinical activity, with improvements reported in symptoms and selected tear-film parameters [54, 55, 59]. Perioperative and prophylactic settings, such as cataract surgery, suggest that LLLT may also have a role in patients at risk of ocular-surface destabilization, although these findings should not be directly generalized to chronic MGD-related DED [56]. Comparative and combination studies are particularly relevant because PBM/LLLT is often considered within the same device-based treatment landscape as intense pulsed light [57, 58]. In this context, both LLLT alone and IPL + LLLT have been associated with symptomatic or meibomian-related improvements, but combination protocols make it difficult to isolate the specific contribution of PBM alone [57, 58]. Across studies, the most reproducible clinical signals involve patient-reported discomfort, tear-film stability, tear production, and meibum-related parameters [54–60]. These outcomes are clinically relevant because evaporative DED is often driven by tear-film instability and MGD-related lipid-layer dysfunction. However, the available studies remain heterogeneous in terms of device platform, wavelength, treatment schedule, endpoints, masking, and follow-up duration. In addition, several studies have small sample sizes and some lack complete sham control or evaluate PBM as part of combined device-based therapy rather than as monotherapy [54–60]. Therefore, the current evidence supports a potential adjunctive role for PBM/LLLT in selected patients with MGD-related evaporative DED but does not establish it as a standardized or clearly superior alternative to existing therapies. This interpretation is consistent with the recent TFOS DEWS III Management and Therapy Report, which recognized LLLT among the instrumental treatment options discussed in the management of DED, particularly within device-based approaches targeting MGD and tear-film instability [61]. Nevertheless, PBM/LLLT should currently be viewed as an investigational adjunctive device-based approach rather than as a replacement for established management strategies, including patient education, lid hygiene, lubricants, topical anti-inflammatory therapy, thermal approaches, and IPL in selected cases. Larger, well-designed controlled trials with standardized endpoints, longer follow-up, and clearer separation between PBM monotherapy and combination therapy are needed to define clinical effectiveness, durability, optimal treatment protocols, and the positioning of PBM/LLLT within routine DED/MGD management.

Myopia

Myopia is a major and increasing public health concern in children and adolescents. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis estimated that the pooled global prevalence of myopia in children and adolescents increased from 24.32% in 1990–2000 to 35.81% in 2020–2023, with projections suggesting a prevalence of 39.80% by 2050 and more than 740 million affected cases [62]. Spending less time outdoors [63], higher amounts of near-work activity [64], and high levels of educational pressure [65] have been found to be associated with myopia. However, the precise underlying pathophysiology of myopia is still being debated and may also involve genetic and environmental factors. In contrast to posterior-segment PBM, repeated low-level red-light (RLRL) therapy for myopia should be interpreted as a distinct clinical paradigm focused primarily on ocular growth regulation rather than direct mitochondrial rescue. Proposed mechanisms include choroidal responses, modulation of scleral hypoxia and remodeling, retinal signaling, and pathways such as TGFβ/Smad signaling, but these mechanisms remain incompletely defined and should not be extrapolated directly to other ocular PBM indications [66–71]. This distinction is important because RLRL for myopia differs from multiwavelength PBM used in retinal disease not only in target population and treatment setting but also in therapeutic aim: slowing axial elongation rather than attempting to improve or stabilize retinal function. Across current pediatric literature, the most commonly studied RLRL protocol is relatively consistent. Most studies have evaluated home-based red-light exposure at approximately 635–650 nm, delivered for 3 min per session, twice daily, with an interval of at least 4 h between sessions and generally on at least 5 days per week [66–78]. This relative protocol consistency contrasts with the broader heterogeneity observed in other ocular PBM indications and partly explains why myopia literature appears more clinically coherent. Randomized and longitudinal studies have generally reported slower spherical equivalent progression and reduced axial elongation compared with control conditions, while some studies have also described choroidal thickening, improved choroidal perfusion, or axial shortening [66–78]. These findings suggest a reproducible short-to-intermediate-term treatment signal, but the strength of inference remains limited by device specificity, incomplete power reporting, variable adherence monitoring, and differences in follow-up duration. The available evidence should therefore be interpreted as supporting a protocol family rather than a fully optimized treatment strategy. Most published data relate to home-based devices emitting red light in the 635–650-nm range, but the exact device characteristics, power settings, retinal irradiance, adherence verification, and stopping rules are not uniformly reported across studies [66–78]. Direct comparisons of different power settings suggest that more than one setting may be effective over short-term follow-up, but current evidence does not establish a definitively superior power, dose, or discontinuation strategy for routine use [75–78]. Similarly, reports of axial shortening and choroidal perfusion changes are clinically interesting but should be interpreted cautiously because measurement variability, regression effects, and short follow-up may influence these outcomes [76–78]. Several clinically relevant uncertainties remain unresolved. First, durability after treatment discontinuation is still insufficiently characterized. Current data suggest that rebound after cessation may be modest rather than severe, but available follow-up remains limited and does not yet define evidence-based stopping criteria [72, 73]. Second, the optimal age to initiate treatment, the most appropriate duration of therapy, and the clinical threshold for discontinuation are not established. Third, because RLRL is typically delivered at home over months or years, adherence, parental supervision, device-use monitoring, and real-world feasibility are central to clinical effectiveness but remain incompletely addressed in many studies. RLRL therapy should also be interpreted within the broader myopia-control landscape. Established approaches such as low-dose atropine, orthokeratology, and myopic-defocus spectacle or contact lens strategies have more mature evidence bases and longer follow-up in many settings [79–86]. Therefore, although RLRL has shown supportive randomized pediatric data, its long-term positioning relative to established pharmacologic and optical interventions remains to be defined. At present, RLRL may be considered a potentially relevant myopia-control modality but it should not be presented as a replacement for established strategies or as a universally applicable intervention without attention to patient age, baseline risk, adherence, monitoring, and local device availability. Safety data are reassuring but still incomplete. A recent systematic review including more than 1400 children treated with RLRL and follow-up durations of up to 24 months did not identify irreversible visual function loss or permanent ocular structural damage; transient afterimages were the most commonly reported ocular symptom and generally resolved within minutes [87]. Nevertheless, because treatment is delivered repeatedly to children over prolonged periods, future studies should include standardized monitoring of visual acuity, refraction, axial length, retinal and choroidal structure, subjective visual symptoms, adherence, and delayed phototoxicity signals. Taken together, RLRL therapy has shown supportive short-to-intermediate-term pediatric data for myopia control but it remains a distinct, device-specific intervention whose long-term safety, optimal protocol, discontinuation strategy, and clinical positioning require further clarification.

Other Ocular Conditions with Preliminary or Limited Evidence

Because the evidence for these indications is substantially less developed than for AMD, DED/MGD, and myopia, these subsections are intentionally concise and focus on clinical rationale, available PBM evidence, and the main limitations preventing clinical translation. Beyond dry AMD and DED/MGD, ocular PBM has been explored in a range of additional ophthalmic conditions. However, for most of these indications, the evidence base remains limited by small cohorts, short follow-up, case-series design, or predominantly preclinical data. These conditions are therefore best regarded as exploratory or investigational at present.

Normal Ocular Aging

Normal ocular aging is associated with progressive mitochondrial decline, reduced ATP production, and increased oxidative stress, changes that may be particularly relevant in the retina because of its high metabolic demand [88, 89]. In this context, PBM has been explored as a possible strategy to support retinal bioenergetics in otherwise healthy older individuals. Shinhmar et al. [89] reported that 670-nm light improved contrast sensitivity, particularly along the tritan axis, in individuals aged 40 years and older, while another small study in healthy participants over 55 years found an improvement in scotopic thresholds after daily treatment [44]. However, those same data showed no significant differences in visual acuity, rod-intercept time, low-luminance visual acuity, or retinal structure [44, 89]. Taken together, these small human studies suggest possible age-related functional benefit, but the evidence remains preliminary and insufficient for clinical application.

Diabetic Retinopathy

Most of the available support for PBM in diabetic retinal disease remains preclinical. In rodent and cell models, 670-nm light improved visual function and reduced inflammatory, oxidative, and vascular changes associated with diabetic retinopathy, including leukostasis, superoxide generation, capillary degeneration, vascular permeability, and retinal ganglion cell loss [90–93]. These findings support biologic plausibility and suggest that PBM may influence several pathways relevant to diabetic retinal injury. Human evidence, however, remains limited and mixed. Tang et al. [94] reported reduced focal retinal thickening in a very small series of eyes with non-center-involving diabetic macular edema, and Eells et al. [95] described possible benefit in treatment-resistant diabetic macular edema receiving PBM in addition to anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy. By contrast, a phase 2 randomized clinical trial by Kim et al. [96] found no functional or anatomical benefit in center-involved diabetic macular edema with good visual acuity. Taken together, PBM should currently be regarded as investigational in diabetic retinal disease rather than clinically established.

Retinitis Pigmentosa

Human evidence for PBM in retinitis pigmentosa remains very limited. Ivandic and Ivandic [97] reported a single case of apparent visual improvement after 780-nm laser therapy, but isolated observations of this kind should be regarded as hypothesis generating only. More recently, Siqueira et al. [98] evaluated multiwavelength PBM (590, 660, and 850 nm) in a cohort of 12 patients undergoing nine treatment sessions and observed a short-term improvement in best-corrected visual acuity, while visual field parameters showed only a non-significant trend toward improvement. No major structural or functional retinal adverse effects were detected on optical coherence tomography or electroretinography [98]. These findings are of interest, but PBM for retinitis pigmentosa should still be considered investigational pending sham-controlled studies with longer follow-up, standardized dosimetry, and better genotype/phenotype stratification.

Glaucoma

A biologic rationale exists for PBM in glaucoma because retinal ganglion cell vulnerability is closely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, impaired perfusion, and glial dysregulation [24, 31, 99–101]. In this context, translational interest has focused on whether PBM might help preserve retinal ganglion cell survival and function. Preclinical studies provide some support for this possibility: Beirne et al. [102] reported that 670-nm light partially prevented ex vivo dendropathy, and Osborne et al. [31] found reduced retinal damage in a rat model of experimentally elevated intraocular pressure exposed to red light. Human evidence, however, remains extremely limited. Eslami et al. [103] examined a combined intervention including antioxidant-rich diet and laser therapy in six patients with glaucoma, but no significant change in ganglion cell complex (GCC) or retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness was observed. Given the very small sample, lack of a proper control group, and combined-intervention design, these data are insufficient to support clinical use. PBM should therefore currently be regarded as investigational in glaucoma pending adequately powered sham-controlled trials with standardized endpoints and longer follow-up.

Toxic Optic Neuropathy

Toxic optic neuropathies are biologically plausible targets for PBM because toxic injury may disrupt mitochondrial metabolism in the retina and optic nerve [104]. In a rodent model of methanol toxicity, 670-nm light delivered after intoxication significantly improved recovery of retinal function and attenuated retinotoxic damage [105]. These findings suggest that red or NIR light may exert retinoprotective effects in settings of toxic mitochondrial stress. However, no meaningful human evidence is currently available, and PBM remains investigational in this context.

Mitochondrial Eye Diseases

Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) and autosomal dominant optic atrophy (ADOA) are attractive theoretical targets for PBM because mitochondrial dysfunction directly contributes to retinal ganglion cell loss and progressive bilateral visual impairment in both conditions [106, 107]. This makes them among the most conceptually appealing ocular indications for a mitochondria-centered intervention. Preclinical findings provide some biologic support: in a rotenone-induced in vivo model of mitochondrial optic neuropathy, Rojas et al. [108] found that NIR therapy preserved ganglion cell density and retinal nerve fiber layer thickness in a dose-dependent manner, while also increasing superoxide dismutase and cytochrome c oxidase activity. Human evidence, however, remains essentially absent. A US-registered investigational clinical trial of NIR-LED therapy in Leber hereditary optic neuropathy was terminated because participants were unable to maintain fixation sufficiently to generate interpretable efficacy outcomes and no useful clinical conclusions could be drawn [109]. Accordingly, despite strong biologic plausibility, there are currently no relevant human data supporting PBM for Leber hereditary optic neuropathy or autosomal dominant optic atrophy.

Clinical Interpretation Across Indications

From a clinical perspective, current ocular PBM literature should be interpreted according to indication, device platform, and strength of evidence rather than as a single therapeutic category. Non-neovascular AMD currently has the most developed clinical dataset, particularly through multiwavelength PBM studies using the Valeda platform and, more recently, the EYE-LIGHT® device. However, the reported effects remain modest, device-specific, and not yet sufficient to support routine adoption outside carefully selected trial-like populations. DED/MGD represents a different clinical scenario, in which PBM/LLLT is mainly being investigated as an adjunctive office- or device-based intervention targeting symptoms, tear-film instability, and meibomian gland function. The available studies suggest possible short-term improvements in symptoms and selected ocular-surface parameters, but the evidence remains limited by small samples, short follow-up, heterogeneous protocols, and frequent use of combination treatment with IPL, which complicates attribution of benefit to PBM alone. Pediatric myopia should be considered separately from posterior-segment PBM. RLRL therapy has supportive short-to-intermediate-term pediatric data for slowing myopic progression and axial elongation but its clinical positioning remains less mature than established pharmacologic and optical myopia-control strategies. Long-term safety, rebound after cessation, adherence, optimal stopping criteria, and device/power optimization remain unresolved. For other ocular indications, including diabetic retinal disease, retinitis pigmentosa, glaucoma, toxic optic neuropathy, and mitochondrial optic neuropathies, clinical evidence remains preliminary, mixed, or predominantly preclinical. These areas should therefore be regarded as exploratory, with no current basis for routine clinical use outside research settings. Practical Clinical Take-Home Messages

From a practical standpoint, PBM should not currently be approached as a uniform ophthalmic treatment but as a group of device- and protocol-specific interventions with different levels of evidence across indications. Clinicians should first identify the indication, device platform, wavelength configuration, treatment schedule, and level of supporting evidence before discussing PBM with patients. For non-neovascular AMD, PBM may be discussed only as an investigational, device-specific adjunctive approach in selected patients resembling trial populations, particularly those with early or intermediate dry AMD and relatively preserved but not normal visual acuity. It should not replace established AMD management, including risk-factor modification, nutritional supplementation when appropriate, monitoring for neovascular conversion, and standard treatment pathways. For DED/MGD, PBM/LLLT should be interpreted as an investigational adjunctive device-based option rather than as a replacement for established therapies such as education, lid hygiene, lubricants, anti-inflammatory topical treatment, thermal approaches, or IPL. When PBM/LLLT is combined with IPL, clinicians should recognize that the independent contribution of PBM cannot be confidently isolated. For pediatric myopia, RLRL therapy should be considered separately from posterior-segment PBM. Although short-to-intermediate-term pediatric data are supportive, clinical use should be weighed against established myopia-control strategies, including low-dose atropine, orthokeratology, and myopic-defocus optical interventions. Long-term safety, rebound, adherence, stopping criteria, and device/power optimization remain unresolved. Across all indications, PBM should be discussed with patients using cautious language: current evidence supports biological plausibility and selected clinical signals but not broad routine adoption. Practical implementation should include transparent discussion of uncertainty, treatment burden, cost/access considerations, device specificity, and the need for follow-up monitoring of visual function and ocular structure.

Future Directions and Challenges

Preclinical findings regarding the potential use NIR/red-light PBM in ocular tissues are biologically plausible in several models, but translation to humans remains limited by heterogeneous protocols and variable study rigor. Much of the clinical literature consists of small trials, case series, or observational reports with incomplete dosimetry reporting, limited masking/sham control, and short follow-up. Further research is needed to clarify mechanisms in disease-relevant human contexts, define indication-specific protocols, and determine whether observed anatomical or functional changes translate into clinically meaningful benefit.

Variable parameters and characteristics of LED and laser devices further complicate the ability to interpret research surrounding NIR/red light therapy. PBM effects are highly parameter dependent, and the usage of suboptimal parameters may lead to no benefits to be seen. Until further knowledge is obtained regarding dosimetry of NIR/red light, all relevant parameters should be reported in detail in studies. This should include wavelength, power density, fractionation, energy density, source, and contact modality. In addition, several studies vary significantly in number of treatment sessions and the duration of treatment, which can both significantly impact treatment results.

A key translational challenge is dose selection. PBM often follows a biphasic dose–response (hormesis), in which low doses can be stimulatory while higher doses may be neutral or inhibitory, underscoring the importance of defining therapeutic windows rather than assuming “more is better.” Across ophthalmic studies, heterogeneity in wavelength, irradiance (power density), fluence (energy density), exposure duration, duty cycle (continuous versus pulsed), treatment frequency, and cumulative dose limits cross-study comparability and can plausibly contribute to discrepant outcomes. Rigorous reporting of these parameters is essential for interpreting efficacy and reproducing protocols. Safety assessments should explicitly consider ocular light hazards (photochemical and thermal), which depend on wavelength and exposure characteristics. Future trials should incorporate standardized safety monitoring (visual function, optical coherence tomography [OCT] structural assessment, symptom reporting), evaluate dose-response where feasible, and include longer follow-up to detect delayed effects, while ensuring transparent reporting of dosimetry and device parameters.

As with any emerging investigational intervention, it is also important to interpret new studies with a degree of caution. Several clinically prominent studies in this field were supported by device manufacturers, and potential conflicts of interest should therefore be considered when interpreting the evidence. Animal studies should also be interpreted with caution as the mouse retina is fundamentally different than the human retina, with no peak in central cone density [110]. Mice and rats also do not use vision as a primary sensory modality and attempt to avoid light, unlike humans.

Additional exploratory ophthalmic or neuro-ophthalmic applications, such as spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome, may emerge in the future; however, these remain hypothetical at present and are not supported by interventional clinical data.

There is a continued need for adequately powered, well devised, randomized, controlled trials to further examine the efficacy of NIR/red light therapy for ophthalmic diseases. Future studies evaluate PBM as a potential adjunct to established therapies, while carefully monitoring safety, treatment burden, and additive clinical value.

Conclusions

This review highlights that current ocular PBM literature should be interpreted through an indication-specific, device-specific, and evidence-weighted framework rather than as a single homogeneous treatment category. NIR and red-light PBM are biologically plausible approaches in ophthalmology, but the strength of clinical evidence varies substantially across indications. The device landscape is also more coherent than it may initially appear. In dry AMD, the most developed clinical experience is reported using the Valeda platform, whereas in DED owing to MGD the most reported experience involves Espansione systems such as EYE-LIGHT® and MY MASK(-E). In pediatric myopia, the largest body of experience appears to derive from home-based RLRL devices within the Eyerising platform family. This literature should therefore be interpreted as indication specific and device specific rather than as evidence for a single uniform PBM instrument across ophthalmology. Dry AMD currently has the most developed clinical literature, although interpretation of the major trial program remains debated and practical implementation raises issues of treatment burden, access, and likely cost. DED owing to MGD and myopia control represent two additional areas of clinical interest, as emerging therapeutic signals are being observed in highly prevalent target populations that are directly relevant to routine ophthalmic practice and public health. By contrast, for most other ophthalmic conditions, the evidence remains preliminary, short-term, or predominantly preclinical. At present, PBM should be regarded as investigational for most ocular indications rather than as an established clinical therapy. Larger, well-designed sham-controlled trials, standardized dosimetry reporting, independent replication, longer-term follow-up, and clinically meaningful endpoints will be required to determine whether PBM provides durable benefit and to define disease- and device-specific treatment protocols.
