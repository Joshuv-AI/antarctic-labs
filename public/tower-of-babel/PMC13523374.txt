Antifungal effect and mechanism of magnolol against the Alternaria tenuissima causing leaf blight on Schisandra chinensis
Authors: Xiaojie Ding, Chunlei Sun, Jiahui Li, Liting Wu, Zibo Li, Ning Hao, Rujun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeaf blight on Schisandra chinensis caused by Alternaria tenuissima severely impacts its yield and quality. Plant-derived bioactive compounds serve as a key component of biological control systems, offering promising solutions for environmentally friendly disease management through their natural and sustainable properties.ResultsThis study systematically investigated the antifungal activity of magnolol, a key bioactive compound from Magnoliae Officinalis Cortex (“Houpo” in Chinese), against A. tenuissima and elucidated its underlying mechanisms. In vitro assays demonstrated that magnolol dose-dependently inhibited mycelial growth, achieving a 93.4% inhibition rate at 100 µg/mL. The field experiments showed that after treatment with magnolol for 14 days, the control effect of the leaf blight on S. chinensis was 76.8%. Magnolol compromised the cell membrane integrity of A. tenuissima and induced dynamic changes in antioxidant enzyme (CAT, POD, SOD) activities, characterized by an initial increase followed by suppression. Transcriptome analysis revealed that magnolol induced a total of 4,767 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of the A. tenuissima, of which 2,510 were upregulated and 2,257 were downregulated. Notably, DEGs involved in toxin biosynthesis, ATP synthesis and ergosterol biosynthesis exhibited an overall downregulation trend in the magnolol treatment group. Meanwhile, reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism-related genes were markedly disrupted.ConclusionsOverall, magnolol exerts its antifungal effects by disrupting cell membrane integrity, inhibiting key pathways of toxin and energy metabolism, while also disrupting the homeostasis of ROS metabolism. This study provides a theoretical foundation for developing new plant-derived pesticides based on magnolol.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09300-4.Keywords: Plant-derived bioactive compounds, Magnolol, Alternaria tenuissima, Leaf blight on Schisandra chinensis

Introduction

Schisandra chinensis (Turcz.) Baill is a plant of significant medicinal value. The dried, mature fruits of this plant have multiple bioactivities, including hepatoprotective, antioxidant and neuroprotective effects [1, 2]. This makes S. chinensis a vital component of both traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) and modern functional foods [3]. However, S. chinensis is often exposed to the severe threat of leaf blight, primarily caused by Alternaria tenuissima. When the disease occurs, the leaves exhibit scorching and premature defoliation. This reduces the plant’s photosynthetic capacity and stunts its growth. Furthermore, the fruit is exposed to direct sunlight, resulting in sunscald damage. Ultimately, this results in significantly reduced yields and poorer fruit quality, causing severe economic losses for the S. chinensis cultivation industry [4]. Currently, controlling leaf blight on S. chinensis mainly relies on synthetic chemical fungicides such as difenoconazole. However, long-term and excessive use of chemical fungicides not only facilitates the development pathogen drug resistance, but may also result in pesticide residue levels in Chinese herbal medicines exceeding permissible limits, thereby compromising their safety. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new strategies that are highly effective, environmentally friendly and capable of controlling leaf blight on S. chinensis.

Plant-derived bioactive compounds, as a typical biological control method, demonstrate great potential for development [5]. Its unique advantages include renewable resources, excellent environmental compatibility, diverse target mechanisms of action and a low potential to induce pathogen resistance [6]. Medicinal plants are a valuable source of natural bioactive chemicals and harbor a variety of secondary metabolites with novel structures and multifunctional properties, including alkaloids, phenolic acids, flavonoids, terpenoids and saponins [7, 8]. Numerous studies have confirmed that natural products derived from medicinal plants can have a significant inhibitory effect on plant pathogens [9, 10]. Research has found that the methanol extract of Angelica gigas roots exhibits potent control efficacy against rice blast, which is caused by Magnaporthe oryzae [11]. Rhein, an anthraquinone compound extracted from the traditional Chinese medicinal plant rhubarb, is highly effective at inhibiting Phytophthora capsica [12].

Magnolol is a natural compound extracted from the Magnoliae Officinalis Cortex (“Houpo” in Chinese), and is one of its main active ingredients [13]. It has excellent biological activity and is highly lipophilic [14]. Magnolol has long been used for its antioxidant, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective properties [15]. Magnolol exhibits potent antifungal properties, inhibiting the production of mycotoxins and regulating genes involved in the utilization of carbon and nitrogen [16]. Its potential applications in plant disease prevention and control have gradually attracted attention in recent years. Research has shown that magnolol exhibits significant inhibitory effects against grapevine downy mildew [17]. Furthermore, magnolol also demonstrates strong antifungal activity against Rhizoctonia solani by disrupting the plasma membrane and interfere with cell respiratory metabolism [18]. These findings emphasize magnolol’s potential as a broad-spectrum antifungal agent for managing plant diseases.

The aim of this study is to systematically investigate the antifungal activity of magnolol against A. tenuissima, as well as the underlying mechanisms of its action. The antifungal effects of magnolol were preliminarily elucidated by measuring changes in hyphae growth rate, hyphae morphology, cell membrane permeability, and antioxidant enzyme activity at varying magnolol concentrations. RNA-seq analysis further clarified the key differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and associated metabolic pathways through which magnolol regulates A. tenuissima. This study is expected to shed new light on the eco-friendly management of leaf blight on S. chinensis, offering theoretical support for the development of biological control strategies against diseases in TCM plants.

Materials and methods

Medium, plant extracts and strains

A. tenuissima was preserved by the Laboratory of Medicinal Plant Diseases of Shenyang Agricultural University. The potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium was prepared using 200 g of potatoes, 20 g of dextrose, and 18 g of agar to one liter of distilled water. Magnolol (98% purity) was purchased from Newup Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Hubei, China).

Antimicrobial activity of magnolol on mycelial growth

To prepare a stock solution, dissolve 0.1 g of magnolol in 10 mL of anhydrous ethanol. The A. tenuissima plugs (5 mm in diameter) were placed on the center of PDA plates containing magnolol at the following final concentrations: 6.25 µg/mL, 12.50 µg/mL, 25.00 µg/mL, 50.00 µg/mL and 100.00 µg/mL. The diameters of the colonies were measured after incubating them at a constant temperature of 25 °C for 5 days.

Spore germination rate

The impact of magnolol on the spore germination of A. tenuissima was evaluated in order to assess its direct fungicidal activity. The spore suspension of A. tenuissima was adjusted to 2 × 107 cfu/mL, then magnolol was added at final concentrations of 6.25 µg/mL, 12.50 µg/mL, 25.00 µg/mL, 50.00 µg/mL and 100.00 µg/mL. Culture the suspension on a concave glass slide. The spore germination rate was calculated from three independent samples at 12 h after the incubation using a microscope. Count 100 spores each time.

Field experiments

The field experiments were conducted at the S. chinensis cultivation base of Shenyang Agricultural University, where the plants are artificially cultivated. The seeds used for cultivation are preserved by the Laboratory of Medicinal Plant Diseases of Shenyang Agricultural University. As the plants were artificially cultivated rather than collected from the wild, no specific collection permit was required. The cultivation base has historically experienced severe outbreaks of the disease, making it an ideal location for disease control trials. Magnolol (100 µg/mL) was applied at the first sign of leaf blight on S. chinensis. Spraying was carried out at 7 days intervals, for a total of three applications. The investigation was conducted 7 and 14 days after the last spraying treatment was completed. Observation of the morphology

A. tenuissima was inoculated into the center of a PDA plate containing 100 µg/mL of magnolol. After culturing at 25 °C for 5 days, samples were taken from the edges of the colony for morphological observation of the hyphae and spores. Images were captured using an upright biological microscope (Axio ScopeA12400301).

Cell membrane integrity assay

The hyphae of the A. tenuissima were cultivated in potato dextrose (PD) liquid medium. Magnolol was then added at final concentrations of 6.25 µg/mL, 12.50 µg/mL, 25.00 µg/mL, 50.00 µg/mL and 100.00 µg/mL. Treatment without the addition of magnolol was used as the control. Hyphae samples were collected at 0 h, 3 h, 6 h, 9 h and 12 h post-cultivation. The conductivity of each treatment group was measured using a conductivity meter after the mycelium had been rinsed three times with sterile water. Meanwhile, the extracellular DNA content was also measured. In summary, the absorbance values for each treatment group were measured at 260 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer, with each treatment repeated three times. The malondialdehyde (MDA) content were carried out by referring to the kit (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing).

Detection of the antioxidant enzyme activities in A. tenuissima

Follow the procedure outlined in Section Cell membrane integrity assay. Collecting hyphae samples at 0 h, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h and 48 h post-cultivation. Refer to the kit (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing) for the extraction and activity determination of catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD) [4].

Transcriptomics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from A. tenuissima hyphae using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). All RNA samples were treated with DNase prior to mRNA extraction and sequencing. From each sample, 1 µg of total RNA was used as input for library preparation. Each sample corresponded to an independent biological replicate. Poly(A)-enriched mRNA was purified with the NEBNext® Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB, #E7490). The NEBNext® Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, #E7770S) was used to prepare the sequencing libraries. In brief, the mRNA was fragmented and the resulting cDNA synthesized and purified. Then the purified cDNA were washed for end repair and ligated to adapters. The cDNA library was enriched by PCR before being sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. After removing adapter sequences, low-quality bases, ambiguous N bases, and excessively short reads, the clean reads were obtained for downstream analysis [20]. The sequencing data have been deposited in the SRA database at the NCBI under accession number PRJNA1392687.

Real-Time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using the HiScript® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme Biotechnology, Nanjing, China), with 1 µg of total RNA being used for reverse transcription. The 20-fold diluted cDNA was used as the RT-qPCR template. The reaction was performed in a 20 µL volume and the mixture was prepared in a 96 well plate containing: this consisted of 4 µL of cDNA, 10 µL of ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotechnology, Nanjing, China), 0.4 µL of each primer at 10 mM, and 5.2 µL of nuclease-free water. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [21]. RT-qPCR was performed using the StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientiffc, Waltham, MA, USA). The actin gene (XM_018525560.1) was used as internal references in quantitative gene expression assays. All the primers were designed using Primer Premier 5 software (Table S1). All experiments were conducted with three biological replicates, each of which was performed independently.

Statistical analyses

The SPSS Statistics 25 software (IBM Inc., Armonk, USA) was used to analyze the data. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post-hoc tests with Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) were used to assess differences between multiple groups, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Statistical significance between two groups was calculated using two-tailed t-test.

Results

In vitro antifungal activity of magnolol against A. tenuissima

The antifungal activity of magnolol against A. tenuissima was tested by adding various concentrations of magnolol to a PDA medium. It was found that magnolol effectively inhibits the growth of A. tenuissima in a dose-dependent manner, with EC50 values of 6.79 µg/mL. As the concentration of magnolol increased, the diameter of the fungal colonies gradually decreased (Fig. 1A). At a concentration of 100 µg/mL, magnolol demonstrated an impressive antifungal inhibition rate of 93.4% against A. tenuissima (Fig. 1B).

Inhibitory effect of magnolol on the mycelial growth of A. tenuissima. (A) A. tenuissima inoculated on PDA medium containing various concentrations of magnolol. (B) Mycelial growth inhibition rates were determined by SPSS software. Different letters above the columns indicate that there are significant differences among the means (p < 0.05). (C) Morphological structure of hyphae and spores after treatment with magnolol. (D) The spore germination rate of A. tenuissima after treatment with magnolol

Following treatment with magnolol, significant abnormalities were observed in the hyphae morphology of A. tenuissima. Compared with the control group, the treated hyphae displayed morphological features such as swelling, enlargement and curvature. At the same time, the spores of A. tenuissima treated with magnolol exhibited pronounced abnormalities, characterized by swollen spores underdeveloped germination structures (Fig. 1C). Notably, magnolol inhibits the germination rate of A. tenuissima spores in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1D). These results indicate that magnolol exhibits significant antifungal activity against A. tenuissima in vitro.

In vivo fungicidal activities of magnolol against A. tenuissima

The results of field trials suggest that magnolol treatment is highly effective in controlling leaf blight on S. chinensis (Fig. 2A). The disease index in the treated plots decreased significantly compared to the control group after 7 days and 14 days of magnolol application (Fig. 2B). Analysis of control efficacy shows that magnolol significantly inhibits leaf blight on S. chinensis (Fig. 2C). The control effect was 76.8% at 14 days, which was not significantly different to the rate at 7 days (87.9%). This suggests that magnolol treatment could provide long-term control potential of leaf blight on S. chinensis.

In vivo antifungal activities of magnolol against A. tenuissima. (A) Representative photos were taken under magnolol treatment. (B, C) The disease index and control effect of leaf blight on S. chinensis

Effects of magnolol on the cell membrane integrity of A. tenuissima

We measured the relative electrical conductivity of A. tenuissima in order to evaluate the impact of magnolol treatment on cell membrane integrity. The results showed that the relative electrical conductivity of A. tenuissima increased gradually with prolonged magnolol treatment, exhibiting a concentration-dependent effect (Fig. 3A). The content of extracellular nucleic acids also serves as an indicator of cell membrane integrity. Following treatment with various concentrations of magnolol, the levels of extracellular DNA in the mycelium suspension were significantly higher than in the control group (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, intracellular MDA levels in treated cells increased with rising magnolol concentrations (Fig. 3C). The accumulation of MDA indicates significant lipid peroxidation, which may be a key mechanism leading to damage to the membrane system and eventual cell death [22]. These findings demonstrate that magnolol treatment damage the cell membrane of A. tenuissima, causing the leakage of intracellular substances and interfering with normal growth, development and metabolic processes.

Effects of magnolol on A. tenuissima. Analysis of changes in (A) relative electrical conductivity, (B) extracellular DNA content and (C) MDA content treated with various concentrations of magnolol

The effect of magnolol on the antioxidant enzyme activities in A. tenuissima

Treatment with magnolol significantly influenced the activities of antioxidant enzymes in A. tenuissima. Overall, CAT, POD and SOD activities showed initial induction, followed by subsequent inhibition (Fig. 4A-C). During the 12 h treatment period, the activities of CAT, POD and SOD generally increased, indicating that the antioxidant system of A. tenuissima was activated in response to the initial stages of oxidative stress. However, after 24 h of treatment, the activity of all three enzymes generally decreased. This suggests that prolonged treatment gradually inactivated the antioxidant system, resulting in continuous oxidative damage. These results suggest that magnolol may interfere with normal physiological processes in A. tenuissima by disrupting its antioxidant enzyme system and disturbing redox homeostasis.

Effect of magnolol on the activities of CAT (A), POD (B) and SOD (C) in the mycelium of A. tenuissima

Transcriptome analysis

To further clarify the antifungal molecular mechanism of magnolol, a comprehensive analysis of gene expression was conducted between the CK and magnolol groups. After quality control, a total of 40.01 GB clean reads were extracted from the raw reads, with Q30% > 95.13%. And the minimum GC content was 53.15% and the maximum was 54.37% (Table S2). A total of 4,767 DEGs were identified in the magnolol group relative to the control based on P value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 as screening conditions. Among these, 2,510 genes exhibited upregulation, while 2,257 genes showed downregulation (Fig. 5A, B; Table S3).

Analyses of DEGs in A. tenuissima. (A) Analysis of DEGs with a volcano plot. (B) Bar chart showing the counts of DEGs between the CK and magnolol groups

GO and KEGG analyses

A functional enrichment analysis using the Gene Ontology (GO) database revealed the critical pathways that are affected by magnolol. We found that the dominant terms were ‘biological_process’, ‘cellular process’, ‘metabolic process’ and ‘cellular metabolic process’ in biological process (BP). For cellular components (CC), ‘integral component of membrane’, ‘nucleus’, ‘cytoplasm’ and ‘cellular_component’ accounted for the majority. For molecular function (MF), the ‘ATP binding’, ‘oxidoreductase activity’, ‘zinc ion binding’ and ‘transmembrane transporter activity’ were main terms (Fig. 6A, Table S4).

Comprehensive analysis of functional enrichment in A. tenuissima treated with magnolol. (A) GO analyses of DEGs in magnolol vs. CK. (B) KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of DEGs in magnolol vs. CK

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further revealed significant enrichment in metabolic pathways including ‘biosynthesis of amino acids’, ‘cysteine and methionine metabolism’ and ‘Citrate cycle (TCA cycle)’. Concurrently, antioxidant pathways such as ‘Glutathione metabolism’ and ‘peroxisome’ were also markedly regulated (Fig. 6B, Table S4). These findings systematically reveal the molecular mechanism by which magnolol inhibits fungal growth at the transcriptional level, disrupting basal metabolism, redox homeostasis and membrane-associated functions.

Analyses of DEGs in toxin biosynthesis

Toxins are a key factor in the pathogenic process of fungi and are synthesized through the regulation of multiple gene expressions. This study found that multiple genes involved in toxin synthesis showed a consistent downregulation trend in A. tenuissima treated with magnolol. Specifically, the expression levels of all 4 polyketide synthases (PKSs) and 3 short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase (sdrI) DEGs were significantly downregulated. Among the 16 monooxygenase (moxI) DEGs, only 4 DEGs showed upregulation, while the remaining 12 DEGs exhibited downregulation. And of the 13 dioxygenase (doxI) DEGs, 4 DEGs were upregulated and 9 DEGs were downregulated (Fig. 7A). The results suggest that magnolol could reduce the pathogenicity of A. tenuissima by preventing the expression of genes responsible for toxin biosynthesis.

Heat maps showing the expression levels of DEGs in toxin biosynthesis (A), ROS metabolism (B), ATP biosynthesis (C) and ergosterol biosynthesis (D). RT-qPCR was used to detect the DEGs marked by the black circles

Analyses of DEGs in ROS metabolism

Pathogenic fungi typically adapt to external stresses by undergoing metabolic reprogramming involving reactive oxygen species (ROS). Following treatment with magnolol, 43 DEGs associated with ROS metabolism were identified in A. tenuissima. There was significant upregulation of both NADPH oxidase DEGs, which are involved in the production of ROS. Most genes within the ROS scavenging system also showed an upregulation trend, including 5 SODs, 6 CATs, 11 PODs, 11 glutathione S-transferase (GST) and 1 ascorbate oxidase (AOX) DEGs. Furthermore, DEGs associated with the thioredoxin system, such as thioredoxin reductase (TRR) and thioredoxin (TRX), showed differential expression (Fig. 7B). Collectively, magnolol may influence the redox homeostasis of A. tenuissima by regulating the production and scavenging of ROS.

Analyses of DEGs in ATP biosynthesis

ATP synthesis is fundamental to the pathogenic process of pathogenic fungi, providing essential support for key pathological activities such as the formation of infection structures, toxin synthesis and nutrient uptake. A total of 27 DEGs associated with ATP biosynthesis were identified in this study. Further analysis revealed that 26 DEGs showed a significant downregulation trend, primarily involving ATP synthase (ATPase), cytochrome b (Cytb), malate dehydrogenase (MDA), hexokinase (HXK), phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK), citrate synthase (CS), fumarate hydratase (FH), cytochrome c oxidase (COX) and homoaconitase (ACO) (Fig. 7C).

Analyses of DEGs in ergosterol biosynthesis

The ergosterol biosynthesis pathway is essential for maintaining the integrity of fungal cell membranes and ensuring normal physiological functions [23]. A total of 7 DEGs were identified in this pathway following magnolol treatment, including sterol 24-c-methyltransferase (ERG6), acetyl-CoA C-acetyltransferase (ERG10), mevalonate kinase (ERG12), hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA synthase (ERG13), C-14 sterol reductase (ERG24) and C-3 sterol dehydrogenase (ERG26). It is noteworthy that all 7 DEGs exhibited a downregulation trend (Fig. 7D). The above results suggest that magnolol may interfere with the normal synthesis and balance of ergosterol in cell membranes. This would impair the structural integrity and function of A. tenuissima cell membranes, ultimately inhibiting growth.

RT-qPCR validation of transcriptome data

To verify the accuracy of transcriptome sequencing data, 10 DEGs were selected for RT-qPCR analysis. The results showed that the expression of ERG6 (gene-AALT_g6409), PKSs (gene-AALT_g6058), ATPase (gene-AALT_g5441), Cytb (gene-AALT_g4701), Mox (gene-AALT_g705), Dox (gene-AALT_g10185), GST (gene-AALT_g8981), ERG13 (gene-AALT_g4763) were down-regulated in the magnolol group compared with control. In contrast, SOD (gene-AALT_g7469) and POD (gene-AALT_g7269) showed upregulation (Fig. 8A). Furthermore, regression analysis revealed a positive correlation between RNA-seq and RT-qPCR data (Fig. 8B). These gene expression trends are consistent with the transcriptome data, which further validates the reliability of the transcriptome sequencing results.

Discussion

Medicinal plants are subject to stricter limits on pesticide Maximum Residue Limits (MRLs) than ordinary crops. This places even greater demands on disease prevention and control measures to ensure safety. In this context, biological control technology has become an important approach to achieving the safe production of medicinal plants [24]. Plant natural products, and especially the active substances derived from them, are an important component of the biological control system [25]. They possess significant development potential due to their good compatibility with the environment and ease of degradation [26]. The experimental results indicate that the plant-derived active compound magnolol exhibits significant inhibitory effects against the A. tenuissima. Furthermore, magnolol demonstrated excellent control efficacy against leaf blight on S. chinensis in field conditions. These findings emphasize magnolol’s potential as a broad-spectrum antifungal agent for managing diseases in S. chinensis and other plants. Overall, these findings further validate the feasibility and effectiveness of using bioactive compounds derived from plants to control medicinal plant diseases. This provides a theoretical foundation and practical reference for advancing the safe and sustainable production of Chinese medicinal materials.

The cell membrane is a crucial interface that enables cells to exchange substances and convey information with their external environment [27]. Its structure and function directly determine the survival and physiological activity of the cell [28]. Therefore, any damage to the cell membrane can severely disrupt cell function and even cause cell death [29]. Disrupting cell membrane integrity has become one of the key mechanisms for inhibiting pathogens in biological control [30]. This study systematically evaluated the effects of magnolol treatment on the cellular membrane structure of A. tenuissima mycelium, measuring multiple key physiological indicators. The results revealed concentration-dependent increases in the relative electrical conductivity, extracellular DNA content and MDA content of mycelium treated with magnolol. This suggests that magnolol severely damages the integrity of A. tenuissima cell membranes, resulting in the leakage of intracellular nucleic acids and triggering membrane lipid peroxidation [31]. This finding is consistent with the results of previous studies which reported that magnolol can induce cell death by disrupting the structure of the fungal plasma membrane [18]. Furthermore, the excellent lipophilicity of magnolol allows it to readily interact with phospholipid bilayers [32]. This may improve its ability to target and disrupt cell membrane. Ergosterol is an essential component of fungal cell membranes, and the way in which it is produced is a key target for fungicides [23, 33, 34]. This study found that magnolol significantly inhibits the expression of multiple key genes in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway, indicating that its antibacterial action extends beyond physical disruption of cell membranes to include interference with critical biosynthetic processes. Therefore, the disruption of cell membrane integrity can be considered one of the main mechanisms through which magnolol inhibits A. tenuissima.

The Alternaria toxins primarily include alternariol (AOH), alternariol monomethyl ether (AME) and tenuazonic acid (TeA) [35]. The production of these toxins is regulated by several key genes, including PKSs, sdrI, moxI and doxI [16]. In this study, the plant-derived active compound magnolol significantly downregulates the expression of genes associated with toxin synthesis in A. tenuissima. This indicates that magnolol can reduce the pathogen’ s ability to produce toxins by inhibiting the toxin biosynthesis pathway. When exposed to pesticide stress, pathogens activate moderate ROS signaling and efficient antioxidant scavenging systems in order to maintain intracellular redox homeostasis. However, the inhibitory effects of fungicides are significantly enhanced when they effectively disrupt this metabolic equilibrium of ROS. This study found that the metabolism of ROS within A. tenuissima cells was dynamically disrupted, suggesting that magnolol disrupted its own oxidative stress defense system. Disruption to ATP synthesis pathway leads to an insufficient supply of cellular energy, thereby affecting multiple critical physiological processes, including cell division, substance transport and stress responses [36–38]. Transcriptome analysis revealed that magnolol significantly reduces the expression levels of several key genes involved in ATP synthesis of A. tenuissima, including ATPase, CYB, HXK, and CS. This directly impedes the synthesis of ATP within the pathogen’s cells, thereby disrupting energy metabolism and ultimately inhibiting growth and stress tolerance. Notably, our study is the first to demonstrate that magnolol simultaneously targets multiple nodes of the ATP synthesis pathway—including genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation (ATPase, CYB), glycolysis (HXK), and the TCA cycle (CS)—revealing a multi-level disruption strategy. It is worth noting that disruption to the energy supply may exacerbate damage to cell membrane integrity further, forming a vicious cycle with oxidative stress accumulation [18, 39]. This is consistent with the membrane damage and antioxidant enzyme system dysregulation observed in this study.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the antifungal activity of magnolol against A. tenuissima, and elucidated its mechanism of action. In vitro antifungal tests indicate that magnolol significantly inhibits mycelium growth, with an inhibition rate of 93.4% achieved at a concentration of 100 µg/mL. In vivo antifungal tests have confirmed that treatment with magnolol effectively reduces the incidence of disease. It demonstrated 76.8% efficacy in controlling leaf blight after 14 days. Treatment with magnolol disrupts the integrity of A. tenuissima cell membranes and interferes with the fungal antioxidant enzyme system. Transcriptome analysis indicates that treatment with magnolol significantly affects gene expression in A. tenuissima. KEGG enrichment analysis shows that magnolol primarily affects ‘biosynthesis of amino acids’, ‘cysteine and methionine metabolism’, ‘Glutathione metabolism’ and ‘peroxisome’ pathways. We found that following magnolol treatment, DEGs associated with toxin biosynthesis, ATP synthesis and ergosterol biosynthesis pathways exhibited an overall downregulation trend. At the same time, the balance of the pathogen’s ROS metabolism was significantly disrupted. In summary, magnolol exerts its antifungal effects by disrupting the integrity of the cell membrane, inhibiting key toxin and energy metabolism pathways, and disrupting ROS metabolism homeostasis. This study provides a robust theoretical basis for the development of magnolol as a biological fungicide and offers a promising, sustainable approach to preventing and controlling leaf blight in S. chinensis.
