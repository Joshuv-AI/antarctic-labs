Mechanisms of obesogenic adipocyte signaling in mouse 3T3-L1 cells in response to plasma from C57BL/6 mice exposed to traffic-generated emissions and high-fat diet.
Authors: U Suwannasual, AK Lund
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between exposure to traffic-generated air pollutants and the development of metabolic disorders, including obesity. We have reported that exposure to a mixture of vehicle emissions (MVE) promotes increased angiotensin II (Ang II) levels, associated with adipocyte hypertrophy and lipid accumulation in C57BL/6 mice; and this effect is further exacerbated by high-fat (HF) diet consumption. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, mouse adipocyte 3T3-L1 cells were utilized to test the hypothesis that circulating Ang II mediates obesogenic outcomes via the Ang II type 1 (AT1) receptor following MVE exposure. Plasma from three mo-old male C57BL/6 mice, fed either a high-fat (HF) or low-fat (LF) diet and exposed to either filtered-air (FA) or 100 µg PM/m3 MVE for 6 hr/day for 30 days was collected and applied to the cell culture media (1:40 dilution) for 48 hrs. A subset of cells was pretreated with losartan (10−6 mol/l), an AT1 receptor antagonist, for 30 min. 3T3-L1 adipocytes treated with plasma from either HF diet-fed or MVE-exposed animals, regardless of diet, exhibited adipocyte hypertrophy. Plasma from the MVE + HF diet group induced increased adipocyte mRNA expression of the Ang II type 2 (AT2) receptor, angiotensinogen (AGT), glucose transporter-4 (GLUT-4), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) and leptin, compared to either the LF or HF + FA controls; most of these effects were normalized by losartan treatment. These findings indicate that Ang II – AT1 signaling mediate alterations obesogenic responses in 3T3-L1 adipocytes following exposure to traffic-generated pollution, in combination with a HF diet.Keywords: Vehicle emissions, Air Pollution, Adipocyte, 3T3-L1, Angiotensin II, High-fat diet, Obesity

Introduction

Exposure to air pollution is associated with approximately 8.9 million deaths globally each year, representing 7.6% of total annual mortality rates [1]. Of these deaths, an estimated 4.2 million annually are attributed to ambient outdoor air pollution exposures [2]. Although genetics, diet, social, psychological, and lifestyle factors are all established contributors to the pathogenesis of metabolic syndrome and obesity, recent studies have identified a strong correlation between air pollution exposure and metabolic disease, including obesity, in both adults and children [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. Recent statistics indicate that approximately one in eight individuals worldwide are living with obesity, with prevalence doubling in adults and increasing from 8% to 20% among children aged 5 – 19 years, between 1990 and 2022 [8]. Nearly 99% of the global population resides in regions that do not meet WHO air quality guidelines, and air pollution exposure disproportionately affects children and disadvantaged populations [2]. Thus, elucidating the mechanisms by which air pollution exposure contributes to the progression of metabolic diseases such as obesity is of critical importance.

Despite the recent increase in available treatments for obesity, approximately 40% of adults in the United States are currently living with obesity, with the highest prevalence observed in Hispanic and non-Hispanic Black populations [9]. The risk of adult obesity is greater among individuals who experienced obesity in childhood, and both racial and ethnic disparities in prevalence persist [10], [11], [12]. Obesity is of the leading cause of global mortality and is associated with several diseases, including neurologic, cardiovascular, and metabolic disorders [13], [14], [15]. Although correlations between obesity and these associated pathologies are well established, the underlying mechanisms remain under investigation. Laboratory studies characterizing etiology and metabolic abnormalities of obesity frequently utilize both in vivo and in vitro research models. Notably, the 3T3-L1 mouse adipocyte cell line is widely used as a model to investigate adipogenesis [16].

The pathogenesis of obesity involves significant remodeling of adipose tissue, a process referred to as adipocyte differentiation. This differentiation is characterized by increased adipocyte cell size (hypertrophy), cell number (hyperplasia), lipid accumulation, inflammation through release of proinflammatory cytokines, and immune cell infiltration, among other tissue-level changes [17], [18], [19]. Hormones released by adipocytes, such as leptin and adiponectin, are frequently deregulated in obesity. For example, leptin resistance and elevated leptin levels are commonly observed in obese individuals [20]. Glucose homeostasis is also altered in metabolic syndrome and obesity. Reduced expression of the insulin-regulated glucose transporter−4 (GLUT−4) in adipocytes is associated with glucose intolerance, hyperinsulinemia, and insulin resistance in selective knockout mouse models [21], [22]. Adipocyte differentiation is further associated with increased signaling via the transcription factor peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), which is predominately expressed in adipose tissue [23]. Increased PPARγ signaling has been shown to induce adipogenesis, promote lipid storage, and increase inflammation in adipose tissue [24], [25]. Moreover, PPARγ mRNA expression is positively correlated with body mass index and waist circumference in obese patients [26].

Although mechanistic studies remain limited, exposure to air pollution has been shown to induce oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage in adipose tissue, leading to differentiation of white adipose tissue [27]. This disruption of energy balance within adipose tissue following air pollution exposure may contribute to the metabolic abnormalities observed in obesity [28]. Particulate matter (PM) air pollution has been demonstrated to promote proinflammatory signaling and infiltration/activation of immune cells in adipose tissue [29], which have been confirmed in human studies [30]. Moreover, previous research indicates that exposure to near-roadway air pollution is associated with elevated systemic and adipose tissue leptin levels in obese young adults [31].

Previous research in our laboratory demonstrated that inhalation exposure to a mixture of diesel and gasoline vehicle emissions (MVE) increases epididymal fat pad weight, adipocyte hypertrophy, and lipid accumulation in the adipose tissue of male C57BL/6 wild-type mice, effects that are further exacerbated by a high-fat (HF) diet [32]. These changes in the adipose tissue of MVE-exposed animals were associated with increased systemic and adipose tissue-specific renin – angiotensin system (RAS) signaling, including elevated levels of angiotensinogen (AGT) and angiotensin II receptor subtype 1 (AT1) [32]. In classic (endocrine) RAS signaling, the bioactive peptide angiotensin (Ang) II is generated through enzymatic cleavage of the precursor AGT, which is released from the liver; however, Ang II can also be produced locally in the adipose tissue from secreted AGT and enzymes [33]. Importantly, adipose-derived AGT is reported to be elevated in obese patients [34]. Ang II is implicated in obesogenic signaling in adipocytes, through both the AT1 and AT2 receptors, leading to increased lipid accumulation via decreased lipolysis and increased lipogenesis, respectively [35]. Although increased RAS signaling is associated with the pathogenesis of obesity in laboratory models and humans, it remains unclear whether RAS signaling mediates obesogenic outcomes following exposure to inhaled traffic-generated pollutants.

Building on the findings from our laboratory and others, this study investigated the hypothesis that inhalation exposure to traffic-generated air pollution, specifically MVE, promotes adipocyte differentiation through a mechanism involving increased Ang II signaling via the AT1 receptor, with these effects exacerbated by a HF diet. The current study employs the 3T3-L1 mouse adipocyte cell line and plasma derived from mice exposed to MVE with or without a HF diet, to test this hypothesis.

Materials and methods

Exposure study description and plasma collection

Three-month-old male C57BL/6 (C57BL/6Tac; Taconic Biosciences) wild-type mice, were randomly assigned to receive either a high-fat “(HF) Western” diet, 42% calories from fat (TD88137 Custom Research Diet, Harlan Teklad), or regular “low-fat” (LF) mouse chow for 30 d prior to the beginning of inhalation exposures, as previously detailed [32]. Mice were then randomly assigned from each diet group to be exposed by inhalation for 6 hr/d for a period of 30 d to either filtered air (FA, control) or 100 µg PM/m3 mixed vehicle emissions (MVE: mixture of 30 µg PM/m3 gasoline exhaust + 70 µg PM/m3 diesel engine exhaust). The MVE and FA exposure chambers were maintained at a constant temperature (20–24◦C) and relative humidity of 40–60%. Mice had access to water ad libitum; however, food was pulled daily during exposure periods and replenished each day following exposures.

Mixed vehicle exhaust characterization

MVE was generated by combining the emissions from a gasoline engine and a diesel generator system, the composition of which have previously been reported by our laboratory [32], [36]. Briefly, the MVE exposure particulate matter mass concentration and size distribution were characterization daily and showed a median size distribution of ∼60 nm, particle mass of 102.5 ± 20.9 µg/m3, and particle mass size distribution median of ∼ 1 µm over the 30 d exposure. The chemical characteristics of the MVE included a daily average of 14 ± 12 S.D. ppm carbon monoxide and 9 ± 9 S.D. ppm of nitrogen oxides; sulfates, nitrates, and volatile organic compounds were also quantified and have been previously reported [37]. Exhausts from both systems were combined after their primary dilutions into a 2 m3 mixing chamber, where mice were housed four to a cage in standard shoebox cages.

Necropsy

All procedures involving animals were conducted in an AAALC-approved animal exposure facility and were approved by the Institute’s Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Lovelace Biomedical Institute prior to the initiation of the exposure protocols. All procedures involving animals comply with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the US National Institutes of Health (NIH Publication No. 85–23, revised 1996) and ARRIVE guidelines.

Plasma collection

Within 16 hrs of the final exposure, mice were anesthetized with Euthasol (at a dose of 0.1 ml per 30 g weight) and euthanized by exsanguination for necropsy. Specifically, blood was collected via cardiac puncture in a heparinized syringe and then centrifuged at 3800xg for 10 min at 4°C. The plasma supernatant was carefully removed, aliquoted into cryovials, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. All plasma was stored at −80°C until used for the cell culture assays.

3T3-L1 cell culture

Mouse fibroblast preadipocyte 3T3-L1 cells (ATCC, CL−173, passages 2–3) were seeded at 1 × 104 cell/well into a 24 well plate and grown in complete pre-adipocyte growth medium (Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) supplemented with bovine calf serum to a final concentration of 10%, ATCC 30–2002). On day 4, the preadipocyte cells were induced for differentiation using differentiation media (ATCC 30–2002 DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1.0 µM dexamethasone, 0.5 mM IBMX, and 1.0 µg/ml insulin) for 48 hrs. Following differentiation, the adipocytes were grown in complete adipocyte maintenance media (ATCC 30–2002 DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1.0 µg/ml insulin) until cells were fully differentiated, as determined by cell morphology and lipid droplet accumulation. The cells were maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. The media was changed every 2–3 days throughout the experimental protocol, unless otherwise noted for treatment.

Plasma treatment of 3T3-L1 differentiated adipocytes

When the adipocyte cells were fully differentiated, plasma collected from mice in each of the four exposure/diet groups (LF+FA, HF+FA, LF+MVE, HF+MVE; n = 5 individual samples per group, 1:40 plasma dilution per well) was added to the adipocyte media for 48 hrs. This concentration of plasma was chosen to be consistent with previous in vitro studies from our laboratory utilizing plasma from animals to investigate toxicologic mechanisms of circulating factors following exposure to inhaled MVE in different cell types [38], [39]. Cell size, lipid accumulation, and mechanistic endpoint transcript levels were then assessed using the methods described in the following sections.

Assessment of adipocyte outcomes

Following 48 hrs of treatment of the mature adipocytes with plasma (n = 3 individual samples per exposure/diet group), as described above, the adipocytes were digitally imaged via microscopy at 40x to determine cell area, using ImageJ, as previously described by our laboratory [32].

Adipocyte area and lipid quantification

Area was determined in a minimum of 40–50 cells per exposure/diet group. Additionally, Nile Red Staining was used to quantify the cellular lipid accumulation (n = 3 individual samples per exposure/diet group). After the media was removed, the cells were washed with 1X PBS three times and then treated with 100 ng/ml Nile Red (Cat No. 151744, MP Biomedicals, LLC) in 1X PBS for 15 min in the incubator and then rinsed with 1X PBS. The cells were then imaged at 40X (3–4 images per well, 24 well plate), and fluorescence was quantified using Image J.

AT1 receptor antagonist treatment

In a second experiment, the mature adipocytes were pretreated with the AT1-antagonist, Losartan (10−6 mol/l) diluted in media (n = 8 individual samples per group), for 30 min prior to treatment with plasma from each of the animal exposure/diet groups. Following 48 hrs of plasma treatment, the adipocytes were analyzed for cell area and lipid accumulation with Nile Red staining (n = 3 per group), as described above, or RNA was isolated for use in RT-qPCR (n = 5 per group).

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

Gene expression of AGT, AT1, AT2, leptin, PPARγ and GLUT−4 mRNA were analyzed in the adipocytes from all in vitro experiments via real-time RT-qPCR. RNA was isolated from the adipocytes using a RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini kit (Qiagen, Cat #74804), following the manufacturer protocol. qRT-PCR was completed and analyzed in the BIORAD CFX using the appropriate primers (Table 1), as previously described by our laboratory [32], [40]. ΔΔCT results were analyzed (GAPDH controls) in adipocytes were analyzed from an n = 5 wells, each corresponding to an animal (plasma) from each exposure/diet study group.

Fluorescent immunocytochemistry

In a subset of wells (n = 2–3 individual samples per exposure/diet group, at least 5 images per well), perilipin (PLN−1) expression was analyzed via fluorescent immunocytochemistry. Following 48 hrs of plasma treatment, adipocytes were fixed in ice-cold 100% MeOH for 15 mins at −20° C, cells were then rinsed 3 times in 300 ml cool PBS for 5 min and then blocked in 3% BSA/Tween buffer for 60 min. The cells were then incubated with PLN−1 polyclonal antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, Cat# PA1–1051-A488, 1:1000 dilution), overnight at 4°C. The nuclei were then counter stained with DAPI (1:1000) at room temperature for 1 min. Cells were then imaged at 40x on an EVOS XL Core (ThermoFisher Scientific), using the appropriate excitation/emission filters, and analyzed by Image J software.

Global lactate efflux

The lactate efflux was quantified in the media from the cultured adipocytes, following 48 hrs of treatment with plasma (n = 3 individual samples per exposure/diet group), using an L-lactate assay kit (Abcam Cat No. ab65330), following the kit protocol. The amount of lactate was normalized by the protein concentration for each well.

Statistical analysis

Data provided in graphs represent the mean ± SEM for each of the endpoints analyzed. A two-way ANOVA with a post-hoc Holm-Sidak pair-wise analysis was used to determine the statistical relationships between diet, exposure, and interactions between the exposure and diet using Sigma Plot 15.1 software (Systat). A t-test was used to analyze statistical differences within a group for losartan vs. no-losartan treatment using Prism 10.4.0 software (GraphPad). A p ≤ 0.050 was considered statistically significant for all endpoints analyzed.

Results

Assessment of obesogenic outcomes in 3T3-L1 adipocytes following plasma-treatment

To determine if circulating factors in the plasma from mice exposed to MVE ± HF diet promote outcomes associated with adipocyte differentiation, we treated the media of mature 3T3-L1 adipocytes in culture with plasma from each exposure/diet group of our study animals (1:40 dilution). Following 48 hr of plasma treatment, adipocytes were assessed for size (area) and lipid accumulation, via Nile Red staining.

Adipocyte area measurement

Compared to LF + FA diet controls (Fig. 1A), we observed a significant increase in adipocyte size/area in cells treated with plasma from the LF + MVE (Fig. 1B, p = 0.001), HF + FA (Fig. 1C, p = 0.035) and HF + MVE mice (Fig. 1D, p < 0.001), as summarized in Fig. 1E. Furthermore, plasma from the HF + MVE promoted an even further increase in adipocyte cell area compared to the HF + FA group (Fig. 1E, p = 0.004). The F values for factors that drive adipocyte hypertrophy in these analyses are exposure = <0.001, diet = 0.013, and diet x exposure interaction = 0.527.

Since we observed that plasma from MVE-exposed and HF diet animals promoted adipocyte hypertrophy, we next investigated RAS signaling in our 3T3-L1 adipocyte cell cultures, including AGT, AT1, and AT2 receptors via RT-qPCR.

Angiotensinogen mRNA expression

We observed a significant increase in AGT expression in adipocytes treated with plasma from HF + MVE compared to HF + FA (Fig. 5A, p = 0.023). The respective F values are exposure = 0.601, diet = 0.091, diet x exposure interaction = 0.099.

Angiotensin II receptors mRNA expression

Similar results were seen in adipocyte transcript levels of AT2, with significant increases of AT2 mRNA expression in the HF + MVE group compared to the HF + FA group (Fig. 5B, p = 0.005). The F values for adipocyte AT2 mRNA expression are exposure = 0.079, diet = 0.855, and diet x exposure interaction = 0.012. However, there were no observed differences in AT1 receptor expression across any of the exposure or diet groups (Fig. 5C). The respective F values are exposure = 0.646, diet = 0.154, diet x exposure interaction = 0.098.

AT1 receptor antagonist outcomes on RAS transcript expression

Since we had previously observed that AT1 antagonism prevented adipocyte hypertrophy in the cells treated with plasma from HF + MVE animals (Fig. 4), we next analyzed the expression of these same RAS signaling members in the adipocytes pretreated with losartan. Losartan treatment prevented the increase of both AGT (Fig. 5A, p = 0.016 compared to HF+MVE without losartan) and AT2 (Fig. 5B, p = 0.019 compared to HF+MVE without losartan) mRNA in adipocytes treated with plasma from the HF + MVE mice. Additionally, we observed an increase in AT1 receptor mRNA with losartan treatment in the HF + FA groups (Fig. 5C, p = 0.020).

GLUT−4 mRNA expression

Adipocyte GLUT−4 mRNA levels were also elevated in the HF + MVE plasma-treated group, compared to the LF + FA (Fig. 6B, p = 0.018), LF + MVE (Fig. 6B, p = 0.011) and HF + FA (Fig. 6B, p = 0.048). The respective F values are exposure = 0.233, diet = 0.072, diet x exposure interaction = 0.093.

Leptin mRNA expression

Interestingly, we observed a significant decrease in adipocyte leptin mRNA levels in the LF + MVE plasma-treated group, when compared to LF + FA controls (Fig. 6C, p = 0.004). Conversely, there was a significant increase in adipocyte leptin mRNA expression in the HF + MVE plasma treated group compared to both LF + MVE (Fig. 6C, p = 0.002) and HF + FA (Fig. 5C, p = 0.022). The respective F values are exposure = 0.831, diet = 0.76, diet x exposure interaction = 0.004.

Outcomes of AT1 antagonism on expression of adipocyte differentiation markers

Pretreatment with the AT1 antagonist, losartan, prevented the induction of PPARγ mRNA (Fig. 6A, p = 0.015, compared to the HF+MVE without losartan group) and GLUT−4 mRNA (Fig. 6B, p = 0.012, compared to the HF+MVE without losartan group) observed in the HF + MVE plasma treated groups in the 3T3-L1 adipocyte culture. However, we observed no statistical difference in adipocyte leptin mRNA levels with losartan treatment across any of the exposure or diet plasma-treated groups (Fig. 6C, p = 0.813).

Quantification of lactate efflux

As lactate homeostasis is known to play a significant role in lipid metabolism, including through the inhibition of lipolysis [42], [43], we next analyzed lactate levels in the media from the 3T3-L1 adipocytes treated with plasma from each of our exposure and diet groups. While there were some apparent changes in lactate levels in the media of adipocytes treated with plasma from the different exposure and diet groups (Fig. 7), no statistical differences were noted.

Discussion

The toxicological effects of air pollution have been linked to numerous human diseases and are estimated to contribute to one in ten deaths worldwide [44], [45]. In the U.S., exposure to air pollution accounts for nearly 200,000 premature deaths, with 59,000 of those linked to traffic-generated sources of air pollution [46]. Several recent epidemiology studies have shown a clear correlation between exposure to ambient air pollution levels and increased incidence of metabolic dysfunction and obesity in both adult and adolescent populations [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], including traffic-generated pollution [47], [48]. Furthermore, laboratory studies have shown that the combination of a high-fat diet and PM air pollution disrupts glucose and lipid metabolism and promotes insulin resistance in a C57BL/6 wild-type mouse model [49]. In agreement with these findings, our laboratory has previously reported that exposure to MVE promotes adipocyte hypertrophy, increased lipid accumulation, and inflammatory signaling in adipose tissue of C57BL/6 male mice, which were exacerbated in the animals on a HF diet [32]. While these obesogenic outcomes were associated with increased RAS signaling, including the upregulation of the AT1 receptor in adipose tissue of HF diet and MVE-exposed animals (regardless of diet) [32], the mechanistic role of Ang II in the observed outcomes was not investigated.

As we have previously reported that exposure to MVE increases plasma levels of Ang II [39], the goal of the current study was to characterize the role of circulating Ang II in MVE-exposed animals ± HF diet on subacute toxicological responses in adipocytes using an in vitro model. Since Ang II signaling through the AT1 receptor on adipocytes is known to alter lipid metabolism and inflammatory signaling [35], [50], we investigated whether Ang II – AT1 signaling mediates alterations in adipocyte structure or function, focusing on the outcomes associated with adipocyte differentiation in obesity. To do this, we utilized plasma from the previously described animal study [32] and applied it to the differentiated 3T3-L1 adipocyte cells in culture, with a subset of these cells being pretreated with the AT1 receptor antagonist, losartan.

While it is widely accepted that consumption of a HF diet can promote increased white adipose tissue mass and adipocyte hypertrophy, characteristic of an obese phenotype, less is known about the role of inhaled environmental pollutants in contributing to obesogenic outcomes in adipose tissue. A previous study from Si et al. showed that subchronic (8-week) exposure to PM2.5 air pollution in young, healthy female C57BL/6 mice resulted in increased gonadal and subcutaneous white adipose tissue stores, enlarged adipocytes, and altered lipid and fatty acid metabolism signaling in the adipose tissue [51]. Our laboratory has reported similar results in male C57Bl/6 mice, where MVE exposure promotes increased gonadal white adipose tissue weight, adipocyte hypertrophy, and lipid accumulation, which is further exacerbated in animals fed a HF diet [32]. In agreement with these in vivo study findings, the current study shows that plasma from animals on either a HF diet or those exposed to MVE, regardless of diet, promotes an increase in 3T3-L1 adipocyte cell size in culture. Similar increases in adipocyte size were observed in the cells treated with plasma from LF + MVE and HF + FA animals, with an even further induction in cell size in the adipocytes treated with plasma from the HF + MVE animals.

Our laboratory previously reported that MVE exposure in C57BL/6 wild-type male mice promotes increased RAS activity, including renal expression of renin, circulating Ang II, and expression of AGT in adipose tissue, each of which was further induced when coupled with HF diet [32]. In the current study, we also observed a significant increase in 3T3-L1 adipocyte AGT mRNA in the HF + MVE plasma-treated group. Importantly, adipose tissue serves as an alternate source of AGT production, which is converted to Ang II. Adipose-tissue-derived AGT has been demonstrated to be significantly upregulated in obese animal models and in humans, in addition to circulating AGT [52]. Additionally, AT2 receptor mRNA expression was also induced in the adipocytes treated with plasma from the HF + MVE animals, compared to those treated with plasma from HF + FA, but AT1 receptor expression was unchanged across all groups. Notably, both AT1 and AT2 receptor expression were opposite to the findings in the in vivo study [32], where AT1 was observed to be upregulated and there was no change in expression of AT2. These findings suggest a differential response to the expression of Ang II receptors on adipocytes following subacute vs. subchronic exposure to circulating factors. Additionally, these varied responses may be due to varied production of local Ang II or AGT in adipose tissue in the in vivo model vs. that present in cultured adipocyte cells.

To determine the role of plasma Ang II signaling on the observed change in adipocyte size in the HF ± MVE plasma-treated cells, we pretreated a subset of 3T3-L1 adipocyte cells with the AT1 receptor antagonist, losartan. Losartan treatment prevented the induction of adipocyte hypertrophy observed in the LF + MVE, HF + FA, and HF + MVE plasma-treated cells. Collectively, these findings suggest that MVE exposure, regardless of diet, can promote adipocyte hypertrophy through an Ang II – AT1 mediated signaling pathway. At the transcript levels, we observed a decrease in adipocyte AGT and AT2 mRNA expression with losartan treatment in the HF + MVE plasma-exposed cells, suggesting that induction of adipocyte-specific RAS signaling is mediated, at least in part through Ang II – AT1-mediated signaling.

Unlike the results observed in the in vivo animal exposure study [32], there was no increase in lipid accumulation, as assessed through Nile Red staining, in the 3T3-L1 adipocytes treated with plasma from either the MVE-exposed or HF diet animals. Conversely, there was a decrease in lipid accumulation observed in the adipocytes that were treated with plasma from the HF + MVE animals. These outcomes may be due to differences in the initial “subacute” response (e.g., within 48 hrs of treatment) of the adipocytes in culture to plasma treatment from the study animals, compared to the subchronic exposure-mediated outcomes in the animal study (30 days). Specifically, 3T3-L1 adipocytes treated with plasma from the MVE + HF study animals are actively undergoing hypertrophy but have not yet begun to accumulate excess lipids, which is representative of the early phase of remodeling in adipose tissue [53]. The mechanisms involved in adipocyte hypertrophy include induction of factors, such as PPARγ, which are also associated with adipocyte differentiation, prior to the accumulation of triglycerides and enlargement of lipid droplets [54]. As we observed a significant increase in PPARγ transcript in the HF + MVE plasma-treated adipocytes, along with adipocyte enlargement, it is plausible that the subacute response of adipocyte enlargement observed precedes the lipid accumulation. Importantly, increased adipocyte size (hypertrophy) in white adipose tissue is associated with higher levels of inflammation and increased insulin resistance when compared to hyperplasia of adipocytes during adipose remodeling [55]. As such, traffic generated air pollution exposure may serve as an important factor in promoting metabolic dysfunction in adipose tissue.

PLIN−1 is a lipid droplet surface protein found in high abundance in adipocytes, which is involved in the regulation of lipases. Historical in vivo and in vitro laboratory studies revealed that upregulation of PLIN−1 inhibits the stimulation of lipolysis, while depletion of PLIN−1 leads to increased lipolysis [56], [57]. More recent studies support these initial findings, reporting that inflammatory factors, such as TNF-α, promote ubiquitination and degradation of PLIN−1, leading to impaired lipid metabolism [58]. Additionally, PLIN−1 -/- mice display smaller lipid droplets in adipose tissue, decreased adipogenesis, increased circulating FFA, and thus increased fat distribution in other organs, such as the vasculature and liver [59], [60], [61]. However, there are conflicting results of PLIN−1 variants in humans reported in the literature, with both loss and gain of function polymorphism variants showing improved lipid metabolism [62]. In the current study, we did not observe a statistical change in the expression of PLIN−1 in any of our plasma-treated 3T3-L1 adipocytes in culture. These results suggest that circulating factors in MVE or HF diet animal plasma did not promote altered PLIN−1, and thus lipolysis activity, following 48 hrs of treatment. However, further studies are necessary to determine whether longer treatment times with plasma from the study animals alter PLIN−1, lipolysis, and resulting lipid accumulation.

PPARγ expression is associated with both adipocyte differentiation and adipocyte hypertrophy, both of which contribute to adipose tissue mass. In human studies, PPARγ mRNA expression is reported to be upregulated in the serum of obese patients, regardless of diabetes status, with strong correlations in PPARγ mRNA and body mass index, waist-to-hip ratio, and waist circumference [26]. Studies involving PPARγ-deficient heterozygous mice fed a HF diet showed smaller adipocyte size and reduced fat mass, compared to wild-type controls [63], [64]. However, PPARγ3RA/+ mice that are heterozygous for an alanine mutation at three critical DNA binding sites for PPARγ, which ameliorates PPARγ transcriptional activity, show increased adipocyte hypertrophy and insulin resistance when fed a HF diet, compared to controls [65]. Collectively, these findings show that PPARγ transcriptional activity plays an important role in the regulation of adipocyte metabolism and that both PPARγ alleles are required for HF diet-induced obesity. Our results showed that 3T3-L1 adipocytes treated with plasma from wild-type C57BL/6 mice fed a HF diet did not promote PPARγ mRNA expression over the 48 hr treatment period. However, treatment with plasma from HF + MVE wild-type animals did induce PPARγ mRNA expression, which was significantly increased compared to all other groups. Ang II has previously been reported to inhibit PPARγ activity through Bcr kinase in vascular smooth muscle cells [66]. Furthermore, AT1 receptor antagonists have been shown to induce PPARγ transcriptional activity, as partial agonists, in 3T3-L1 adipocytes in culture [67]. Similarly, we observed an induction of PPARγ in our adipocyte cell culture with losartan treatment, but this increase was only statistically significant in the cells that were also treated with plasma from the HF + FA diet animals. Conversely, there was a decrease in PPARγ mRNA levels in the losartan-treated 3T3-L1 adipocytes within the MVE + HF plasma-treated group, suggesting that inhibition of AT1 signaling reduced PPARγ transcript expression in these cells. It is plausible that the difference in our study finding vs. those previously reported is that losartan has less PPARγ agonist activity, compared to the AT1 antagonists used in the Schupp et al., 2004 study (telmisartan and irbesartan) [67]; however, further studies are necessary to determine the mechanisms involved in the observed alterations in PPARγ mRNA expression following treatment with the HF + MVE plasma±AT1 antagonism. Additionally, since we observed an increase in AT2 receptor expression in the 3T3-L1 adipocytes treated with plasma from HF + MVE animals, and AT2 signaling has been associated with the initial stages of adipocyte differentiation, through promoting PPARγ expression and early lipid accumulation [68], it is necessary to elucidate further the role of AT1 vs. AT2 receptor signaling in adipocytes following MVE exposure ± HF diet.

GLUT4, the insulin-sensitive glucose transporter, is the predominant glucose transporter in adipose tissue, and its expression has been reported to be altered in diabetic patients [69]. There is, however, conflicting literature related to GLUT−4 expression in obesity, with some studies reporting no change in GLUT−4 expression, while others find decreased adipocyte expression of GLUT−4 [69], [70]. Similar conflicts exist in the literature from studies utilizing animal models. For example, adipose tissue from obese Zucker rats exhibited increased GLUT−4 mRNA expression, compared to lean age-matched littermates [71]. However, transgenic mice engineered to express the human GLUT4 gene and promoter showed improved insulin sensitivity when fed a HF diet compared to non-transgenic controls [72]. Importantly, in vitro studies in 3T3-L1 adipocytes show that PPARγ knockdown is associated with a reduction in GLUT4 expression, leading to the decline in insulin-stimulated glucose uptake [73]. Additionally, 3T3-L1 adipocytes stimulated by PPARγ agonist, artepillin C, exhibited increased GLUT4 expression [74]. In agreement with these findings, our results revealed that 3T3-L1 adipocytes treated with plasma from HF + MVE animals show upregulated PPARγ mRNA, associated with increased GLUT−4 mRNA levels, compared to the LF + FA controls, suggesting that PPARγ activation may contribute to the increased GLUT−4 transcript expression. It is unclear whether these are adaptive or maladaptive changes in adipocytes treated with plasma from HF + MVE animals, as PPARγ is known to play a dual role in metabolic regulation in adipose tissue, both promoting lipid storage and regulating GLUT4 expression, the latter of which is dependent on ligand activation of PPARγ [73], [74], [75].

In our previous animal exposure study, MVE exposure led to increased fat pad weight, adipocyte hypertrophy, and lipid accumulation, which were associated with elevated adipocyte AT1 receptor expression in wild-type C57BL/6 mice, regardless of diet, compared to LF + FA controls [32]. In the current in vitro study, losartan treatment prevented the induction of PPARγ and GLUT−4 mRNA in HF + MVE plasma treated 3T3-L1 adipocytes. Additionally, as previously discussed, losartan-treatment also attenuated the adipocyte hypertrophy observed with HF + MVE plasma treatment. As Ang II has previously been reported to inhibit insulin-mediated translocation of GLUT−4 [76], confirming a role for Ang II in insulin homeostasis, it is plausible that the inhibition of upregulated Ang II signaling through the AT1 receptor (losartan treatment) contributes to the normalization of GLUT−4 mRNA expression in the HF + MVE plasma-treated adipocytes. However, further studies are required to confirm the roles of Ang II, PPARγ, and other inflammatory factors in GLUT−4 expression in the MVE + HF plasma-treated adipocytes.

Leptin is a hormone primarily produced and secreted by white adipose tissue, which is involved in regulating energy expenditure and appetite suppression. However, during abnormal metabolic conditions, such as those observed in obesity, leptin resistance can occur [77], even though increased fat stores in obesity are typically associated with increased circulating leptin levels [78], [79]. There is conflicting literature across humans and animal models and in vitro studies as to whether leptin is directly associated with adipocyte hypertrophy; however, a recent study showed that leptin concentrations similar to those observed circulating in obese individuals promoted the expression of factors involved in adipogenesis and lipogenesis, such as PPARγ, in 3T3-L1 cells [80]. In the current study, leptin mRNA expression in 3T3-L1 adipocytes exposed to HF + MVE plasma was significantly elevated compared to adipocytes treated with plasma from the LF + MVE and HF + FA animals, but not the LF + FA controls. Importantly, this increase in leptin expression observed in the adipocytes of the HF + MVE group was not normalized with losartan treatment. Since leptin production is regulated by many factors, including insulin, adiponectin, inflammatory factors, and nutrient state (e.g., nutrient excess promotes leptin production) [81], [82], it is plausible that some (or all) of these factors are promoting the increased leptin mRNA levels observed in the 3T3-L1 cells treated with plasma from the HF + MVE group. Furthermore, as we did observe a significant increase in leptin mRNA levels in the adipose tissue of the C57BL/6 mice exposed to HF + MVE diet in our in vivo exposure study, compared to LF + FA controls [32], the current findings suggest that that an elevation in leptin expression may not be part of the initial toxicity response to circulating factors following MVE exposures in the adipose tissue. Interestingly, leptin expression in the 3T3-L1 adipocyte cells treated with LF + MVE plasma was observed to be suppressed (albeit not statistically) compared to LF + FA controls, even though there was a notable increase in adipocyte size. Such findings warrant future mechanistic studies to determine how alterations in leptin regulation may mediate adipose-specific and systemic metabolic dysfunction associated with environmental air pollution exposures.

Lactate is a product of anaerobic glycolysis, and to a lesser degree, aerobic glycolysis, following the conversion of pyruvate by lactate dehydrogenase. In adipocytes, even in the presence of oxygen, a large portion of glucose is converted to lactate and secreted from the cells [83]. Findings from the literature show that adipose-derived lactate promotes an increase in adipose tissue macrophages and proinflammatory signaling [84]. Although we did not quantify glucose uptake in our in vitro studies, we observed an increase in GLUT−4 expression in 3T3-L1 adipocytes treated with plasma from HF + MVE animals. Consequently, we analyzed adipose-derived lactate levels in the media. While some differences in adipocyte-secreted lactate were noted in the 3T3-L1 cells treated with plasma from animals exposed to MVE, regardless of diet, none of the differences quantified were statistically significant due to high intragroup variability, especially in the LF + FA plasma-treated adipocyte media.

While this in vitro study provides novel insight into the mechanisms involved in the subacute toxicological responses of 3T3-L1 adipocytes to circulating factors following exposure to HF±MVE diet, including Ang II, some study limitations that should be noted. First, the current study only assessed adipocyte responses at one timepoint, which was 48 hrs post-treatment with plasma. This 48 hr timepoint was chosen based on previous in vitro analyses that included losartan and plasma treatments in our laboratory [38], [39]; however, at this timepoint we may not be observing early and/or later signaling outcomes in the adipose resulting from circulating factors following MVE exposures. Second, the current study only investigated the mechanistic contribution of Ang II signaling through the AT1 receptor on the obesogenic outcomes observed in the 3T3-L1 adipocytes in culture. Likely, alterations in AT2, as well as many other circulating factors and signaling pathways (e.g., inflammatory, toll-like receptors, hormonal, etc.) contribute to the toxicological responses observed in adipocytes following environmental air pollution exposures. Lastly, utilized the plasma from male mice from our previous exposure studies. Given that the etiology of metabolic syndrome and obesity differs between males and females [85], as does fat distribution and body composition, the mechanistic results observed in these studies cannot be assumed to be representative of outcomes in females. As the global prevalence of obesity is currently higher in females than in males, further research is essential to elucidate signaling pathways mediating the obesogenic outcomes following environmental exposures in females. Moreover, future in vitro and in vivo studies should include time-course assessments of MVE exposure outcomes, ranging from acute to chronic, characterization of additional obesogenic signaling pathways (such as inflammatory cytokines, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and hypoxia-inducible factors), nutrient sensing pathways (such as mTOR), and comprehensive evaluation of metabolic outcomes, such as adipokine signaling and insulin sensitivity. These approaches are necessary to fully characterize the mechanisms underlying obesogenic outcomes in adipose tissue following exposure to traffic-generated air pollution.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings from the current in vitro study, together with the previously reported in vivo exposure study, from which the plasma was collected for this study [32], demonstrate that exposure to MVE combined with a HF diet promotes hypertrophy and induces the expression of genes involved in adipogenesis in differentiated 3T3-L1 adipocyte cells. This process is associated with increased systemic and adipose-specific RAS signaling. Furthermore, inhibition of Ang II-AT1 receptor signaling in 3T3-L1 adipocytes mitigates most of the obesogenic outcomes observed following subacute exposure to plasma from the HF + MVE animals. Collectively, these data highlight the critical role of Ang II signaling, dependent on the AT1 receptor, in metabolic dysfunction and obesity linked to exposure to traffic-generated environmental air pollution. These findings also indicate a potential therapeutic role for Ang II receptor antagonism in early adipocyte remodeling and obesogenic outcomes promoted by these environmental exposures.
