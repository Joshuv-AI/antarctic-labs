1.
Introduction

A central aspect of Ramsey theory is proving
density theorems
which detect patterns inside every dense subset of a given combinatorial structure.
In some cases, the underlying pattern can be interpreted as a metric space and the task is to embed it into every dense subset in a manner that preserves the metric.
For example, Szemerédi’s theorem
[
38
]
states that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\delta<1
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that every subset
\mathcal{D}\subset[N]:=\{1,....,N\}
of size
|\mathcal{D}|>\delta N
contains a
k
-term arithmetic progression.
A
k
-term arithmetic progression is precisely a rescaled isometric copy of the metric space
([k],|\cdot|)
into
([N],|\cdot|)
(where
|\cdot|
denotes the standard metric on ).
Another example is the Furstenberg--Weiss theorem
[
19
]
(see also
[
34
]
) which finds rescaled isometric copies of the binary tree metric of depth
k
inside dense (defined in an appropriate sense) subsets of the binary tree metric of depth
N
.

From this metric viewpoint, it is natural to strengthen or relax the notion of embeddability and investigate how bounds sharpen or loosen, respectively
.
We refer the reader to the works of Dumitrescu
[
13
]
, Fraser–Yu
[
16
,
17
]
, and of Rödl–Sales
[
35
]
who pursued this direction.

In this paper, we study from this viewpoint the following Ramsey question:

Do dense subsets of the Hamming cube contain metric copies of Hamming cubes of lower dimension?

In the bi-Lipschitz distorted setting, we also study the same question for path metrics (complementing results of
[
13
,
16
,
17
,
35
]
) and for tree metrics. See subsection
1.2
for paths and
1.3
for trees.

k,N\in\mathbb{N}
and let
f:(\{0,1\}^{k},||\cdot||_{1})\to(\{0,1\}^{N},||\cdot||_{1})
be a map from the
k

N
-dimensional Hamming cube with their corresponding Hamming metrics
||\cdot||_{1}
. (See subsection

Denote by

– Given

Theorem 1.1

(Main theorem)

For every

\frac{1}{5}\,\frac{2^{k}}{\log k}\,\log\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\bigr)\leqslant Emb(1,\delta,k)\leqslant 2^{2^{k}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}

\dfrac{2^{-k}}{12R}2^{\frac{2^{k}}{Rk}\;\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}\leqslant Emb^{\leqslant R}(1,\delta,k)\leqslant 2^{2^{k}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}

1.1.
Large subsets of the Hamming cube embed poorly into spaces of nonpositive curvature

The subsequent geometric application of Theorem
1.1
was proposed to us by A. Naor
[
32
]
. Following
[
6
]
, given a class of metric spaces
\mathcal{M}
, a finite metric space
(X,d)
, and
\alpha>1
write

R_{\mathcal{M}}(X,\alpha):=\sup\{|\mathcal{D}|:\mathcal{D}\subset X\;\;and\;\;\exists M\in\mathcal{M}\;\;s.t.\;\;c_{M}(\mathcal{D})\leqslant\alpha\}

where
c_{M}(\mathcal{D})
denotes the infimal bi-Lipschitz distortion over all functions
f:\mathcal{D}\to M
.
See
[
4
,
6
,
27
,
29
,
28
]
for motivations for studying this parameter from the perspective of geometry (nonlinear Dvoretzky theorems, geometric measure theory, probability) and computer science (online algorithms, data structures).

Let
\mathrm{POS}
denote the class of metric spaces of
nonnegative Alexandrov curvature
, and let
\mathrm{CAT}(0)
denote the class of metric spaces of
nonpositive Alexandrov curvature
(the relevant standard definitions can be found for example in the monograph the book
[
10
]
).
Bartal–Linial–Mendel–Naor
[
6
]
showed that

(1)

2^{N(1-\frac{C\log\alpha}{\alpha^{2}})}\leqslant R_{\mathrm{POS}}(\{0,1\}^{N},\alpha)\leqslant C2^{N(1-\frac{c}{\alpha^{2}})}\;\;\;for\;all\;\alpha>1,N\in\mathbb{N}.

where
C,c>0
are universal constants.
The works of Gromov
[
20
,
21
]
and Kondo
[
24
]
imply that the method of
[
6
]
for the upper bound fails when
\mathrm{POS}
is replaced by
\mathrm{CAT}(0)
, and Naor asked if one could prove an upper bound on the size of subsets of
\{0,1\}^{N}
which embed into
\mathrm{CAT}(0)
spaces.
In Section
8
we prove

(2)

R_{\mathrm{CAT}(0)}(\{0,1\}^{N},\alpha)\leqslant 2^{N(1-C/\alpha^{4})}\;\;\;for\;all\;\alpha>1,\;and\;N\geqslant c\alpha^{6},

where
C,c>0
are universal constants.

More generally, the upper bound in
[
6
]
restricts the embeddability into any space of nontrivial
Markov type
.

1.2.
Metric embeddings of paths into dense subsets of paths

We consider the
path space

[N]=\{1,...,N\}
whose metric is given by
d(i,j):=|i-j|
and endow it with the uniform probability measure, i.e.
\mu(\mathcal{D}):=|\mathcal{D}|/N
for all
\mathcal{D}\subset[N]
.

Rödl–Sales
[
35
]
prove that for any
\varepsilon>0,r\in\mathbb{N},k\in\mathbb{N}
, and for any
r
-coloring of the path of length
N=O_{r,\varepsilon}(k^{r})
, there is a
(1+\epsilon)
-distorted embedding
f:[k]\to[N]
such that
f([k])
is monochromatic.
Conversely, they show that there exists an
r
-coloring of
[\Omega_{r,\varepsilon}(k^{r})]
which contains no monochromatic
(1+\varepsilon)
-bi-Lipschitz copy of
[k]
.
We prove a density version of this result, also giving a tight dependence on
k
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N},0<\delta<1,\varepsilon\geqslant 0
.

We denote by
Path(1+\varepsilon,\delta,k)
the smallest
N
such that for every subset
\mathcal{D}\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
with
\mu(\mathcal{D})\geqslant\delta
(i.e.
|\mathcal{D}|\geqslant\delta N
) there exists a
(1+\varepsilon)
bi-Lipschitz map
f:[k]\to[N]
such that
f([k])\subset D
.
Bounds on
Path(1,\delta,k)
correspond exactly to bounds on Szemerédi’s theorem, so the following theorem can be viewed as a bi-Lipschitz relaxation of Szemerédi’s theorem:

Theorem 1.3

(metric embeddings of paths into dense subsets of paths)
.

.

For all
k\geqslant 3,0<\delta<1,0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, we have:

e^{\Omega(k\log(1/\delta))}\leqslant Path(1+\varepsilon,\delta,k)\leqslant e^{O(k\varepsilon^{-1}\log(1/\delta))}.

The upper bound is a strengthening of Theorem 6 of Dumitrescu
[
13
]
who showed
Path(1+\varepsilon,\delta,k)=e^{O_{\varepsilon}(k\log k\log(1/\delta))}
(see also Proposition 3.7 of Rödl–Sales
[
35
]
for a different proof and
[
16
,
17
,
35
]
for related results).

The proof of Theorem
1.3
is in Section
6
.
The proof of the upper bound is similar to the proof in
[
13
]
.
We use a porosity argument.
We show that for any interval
I
, if
\mathcal{D}\cap I
has no
(1+\varepsilon)
-distorted copy of
[k]
then
\mathcal{D}\cap I
is disjoint from an interval
I^{\prime}\subset I
of diameter
\asymp\varepsilon|I|/k
.
This is the discrete analogue of a
porous set
in geometric measure theory.
It is well-known that porous sets do not have full Hausdorff dimension (e.g. see Chapter 11 in the book
[
26
]
), and our argument mimics this fact.
The lower bound follows from a Cantor set construction.
We construct
\mathcal{D}\subset[N]
by removing the middle
4N/k
-sized interval
I
of
[N]
at each iteration.
We argue that for any
(1+\varepsilon)
-bi-Lipschitz map
f:[k]\to\mathcal{D}
, the image of
f
must lie entirely in only one of the two subintervals of
[N]-I
, and continue recursively.

1.3.
Metric embeddings of trees into dense subsets of trees

For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
consider the graph of the complete binary tree of depth
N
. We will denote it by
Tree(N)
, and represent each vertex as a string
w
of
0
’s and
1
’s of length
l(w)\in\{0,...,N-1\}
.
The shortest path metric on the vertices of this graph is a
tree metric
which we denote by
d_{T}
.
Following
[
19
,
34
]
, we define a probability measure on the vertices of
Tree(N)
by

\mu(\mathcal{D}):=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{D}}2^{-l(w)}\;\;\;for\;all\;\mathcal{D}\subset Tree(N).

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N},0<\delta<1,\varepsilon\geqslant 0
.
We denote by
Tree(1+\varepsilon,\delta,k)
the smallest
N
such that for every subset
\mathcal{D}\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
with
\mu(\mathcal{D})\geqslant\delta
there exists a
(1+\varepsilon)
bi-Lipschitz map
f:Tree(k)\to Tree(N)
with
f(Tree(N))\subset D
.
The Furstenberg–Weiss theorem states that
Tree(1,k,\delta)<\infty
for all
k\in\mathbb{N},0<\delta<1
, while Pach–Solymosi–Tardos give quantitative bounds on
Tree(1,k,\delta)
using the bounds for Szemerédi’s theorem.
The following theorem concerns the
(1+\varepsilon)
-distorted setting:

Theorem 1.4

(Metric embeddings of trees into dense subsets of trees)
.

For all
k\geqslant 3,0<\delta<1,0<\varepsilon\leqslant 0.01
, we have:

e^{\Omega(k\log(1/\delta))}\leqslant Tree(1+\varepsilon,\delta,k)\leqslant e^{O(k\varepsilon^{-1}\log(1/\delta))}.

The upper bound uses as black boxes the upper bound for path spaces in Theorem
1.3
and the Ramsey theorem for tree replicas of Pach–Solymosi–Tardos (namely Theorem 1 in
[
34
]
), and the rest of the proof is analogous to the proof of the Furstenberg–Weiss theorem in
[
34
]
.
The lower bound uses the same Cantor set construction as in the lower bound of Theorem
1.3
, along with a characterization of path embeddings
[k]\to Tree(N)
of small distortion. For the proof see Section
7
.

1.4.
Discussion on the proofs

1.4.1.
Upper bound for bounded rescaling undistorted Hamming cubes

The map of
\{0,1\}^{k}
inside
\mathcal{D}\subset\{0,1\}^{N}
which we find has a very special form.
Suppose
N=nk
and write
\{0,1\}^{N}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\{0,1\}^{n}
meaning that each string
x\in\{0,1\}^{N}
is expressed as the concatenation of
k
strings of length
n
,
x=(x^{1},x^{2},...,x^{k})
.
Call an embedding
f:\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}^{nk}
an
equitable block copy
whenever there exist
x_{0}^{1},x_{1}^{1},x_{0}^{2},x_{1}^{2},...,x_{0}^{k},x_{1}^{k}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that

||x^{1}_{0}-x^{1}_{1}||_{1}=||x^{2}_{0}-x^{2}_{1}||_{1}=...||x^{k}_{0}-x^{k}_{1}||_{1}

and the embedding
f
is given by:
f(\alpha)=(x_{\alpha_{1}}^{1},x_{\alpha_{2}}^{2},...,x_{\alpha_{k}}^{k})
for all
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{k}
.
It is clear that an equitable block copy is a rescaled isometric embedding
\{0,1\}^{k}
into
\{0,1\}^{N}
with rescaling
r:=||x^{1}_{0}-x^{1}_{1}||_{1}
.

We find the pairs of strings
(x_{0}^{1},x_{1}^{1}),(x_{0}^{2},x_{1}^{2}),...,(x_{0}^{k},x_{1}^{k})
via induction.
Given any
x^{1}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
, we consider the
section
of
\mathcal{D}
at
x^{1}
, i.e.

\mathcal{D}_{x^{1}}:=\{(y^{2},...,y^{k}):(x^{1},y^{2},...,y^{k})\in\mathcal{D}\}.

The goal of the base case of the induction is to find a pair
(x_{0}^{1},x_{1}^{1})\in\{0,1\}^{k}\times\{0,1\}^{k}
such that
||x_{0}^{1}-x_{1}^{1}||_{1}=r
and the density
\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x_{0}^{1}}\cap\mathcal{D}_{x_{1}^{1}})
is as large as possible.
In the next step, we consider
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{D}_{x_{0}^{1}}\cap\mathcal{D}_{x_{1}^{1}}
and find
(x_{0}^{2},x_{1}^{2})\in\{0,1\}^{k}\times\{0,1\}^{k}
such that
||x_{0}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}||_{1}=r
and the density
\mu(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{x_{0}^{2}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{x_{1}^{2}})
is as large as possible.
We continue this process inductively exactly
k
times, and we obtain an equitable block copy.
At each step
i=1,...,k-1
, we show that the density of
\mathcal{D}^{{}^{\prime}...^{\prime}}
is high enough to ensure that we can find the next pair of points
(x_{0}^{i+1},x_{1}^{i+1})
.

1.4.2.
Upper bound for
(1+\varepsilon)
-distorted Hamming cubes

The key difference between
(1+\varepsilon)
-distorted and undistorted copies of Hamming cubes is that the former can be infinitesimally perturbed and still maintain the same geometric structure.

(1+\varepsilon)
-distorted copies are flexible while undistorted copies are rigid.
The upper bound on
Emb(1+\varepsilon,k,\delta)
follows the same inductive scheme as the upper bound on
Emb^{\leqslant 2}(1,k,\delta)
but exploits this flexibility.
There are two sources of error we can tolerate in the search for a block copy.

First, we do not need the identity
||x^{i}_{0}-x^{i}_{1}||_{1}=r
to hold exactly for each
i=1,..,k
. Instead it suffices to ask for

(1-\varepsilon_{1})\dfrac{n}{2}\leqslant||x^{i}_{0}-x^{i}_{1}||_{1}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon_{1})\dfrac{n}{2}\;\;\;for\;each\;i=1,...,k.

We call this error
block error
and denote it by
\varepsilon_{1}
. Basic Hoeffdig bounds show that fixing any
x_{0}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
almost all
x_{1}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
will satisfy the above estimate. See Lemma
4.1
for the precise statement.
Taking only block error into consideration we can form
roughly equitable block copies
which are given by
x_{0}^{1},x_{1}^{1},x_{0}^{2},x_{1}^{2},...,x_{0}^{k},x_{1}^{k}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
satisfying the above estimate.
The formula for the embedding is the same as before:
f(\alpha)=(x_{\alpha_{1}}^{1},x_{\alpha_{2}}^{2},...,x_{\alpha_{n}}^{k})
for all
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{k}
.
We denote such copies whose image lies inside
\mathcal{D}
by

\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}(D)
(see subsection
2.3
for the notation).

The second error arises when we perturb in an arbitrary way the image of each point in a roughly equitable block copy.
Instead of seeking a roughly equitable block copy inside
\mathcal{D}
(i.e. to show
\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}(D)\neq\emptyset
), we seek instead one inside the
\varepsilon_{2}
-neighborhood of the set
\mathcal{D}
(i.e. we show that
\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}(\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon_{2}})\neq\emptyset
).
Here the
\varepsilon_{2}
-neighborhood

\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon_{2}}
denotes the set of all points
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
of Hamming distance
<\varepsilon_{2}n
from the set
D\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
.
We call this error
perturbation error
and denote it by
\varepsilon_{2}
.

It is intuitive but a little tedious to check that when both the block and perturbation error are sufficiently small, then we get a bi-Lipschitz embedding (see Proposition
4.4
).
The key advantage of the perturbation error is that we can exploit the well-known
concentration of measure phenomenon
of the Hamming cube.
Namely, if a subset of the Hamming cube has positive density, then its
\varepsilon_{2}
-neighborhood contains almost all of the points (see Lemma
2.3
).
This fact ensures that the inductive scheme (see Proposition
4.2
) begins with a very high density.

1.4.3.
Lower bounds (finding dense subsets with no cubes)

We use the probabilistic method. We flip
2^{M}
i.i.d. Bernoulli(
\delta
) random variables, one for each
x\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, and set
\mathcal{D}
to be the set of all points
x
whose Bernoulli random variable is
1
.
We get a random subset with expected density
\mathbb{E}\mu(\mathcal{D})=\delta
.
Our goal is to show that
\mathcal{D}
does not contain any undistorted or bounded rescaling undistorted copy of
\{0,1\}^{k}
.

We can bound the probability that
\mathcal{D}
contains a copy because

undistorted copies are rigid
.
We show that every undistorted map
f:\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}^{N}
must be of the form:

f(\alpha)=\sum_{i\in[k]:\alpha_{i}=1}\mathbf{1}_{I_{i}}\;\;\;for\;all\;\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{k}

where
b\in\{0,1\}^{N}
and
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}
are disjoint subsets of
[N]
of equal size. The rescaling factor of the embedding is
r=|I_{1}|=\ldots=|I_{k}|
.

For the lower bound for undistorted copies, we simply apply a union bound over all copies.
For the lower bound for undistorted copies of bounded rescaling, we observe that for each fixed copy, the number of other copies it intersects is small and apply the Lovász local lemma.

2.1.
General notation and conventions

In the rest of the paper we will denote by
Q_{N}=\{0,1\}^{N}
the
N
-dimensional Hamming cube.

Q_{N}
is equipped with the Hamming metric

d_{N}(x,y)=\lVert x-y\rVert_{1}=\left\lvert\left\{i\in[N]:x_{i}\neq y_{i}\right\}\right\rvert,\;\;\;for\;all\;x,y\in Q_{N}.

Given any set
X
by
\lvert X\rvert
we denote its cardinality. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we set
[n]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,n\}
, and we will identify subsets of
[n]
with vectors in
Q_{n}
which we equip with the Hamming distance, i.e. for
x,y\in Q_{n}
their distance is given by
\lVert x-y\rVert=\left\lvert x\bigtriangleup y\right\rvert
. Given any non-negative integer
k
we define
\binom{[n]}{k}\coloneqq\{x\in Q_{n}:|x|=k\}
; moreover, for every
t\geqslant 0
, we set
[n]^{\leqslant t}\coloneqq\{x\in Q_{n}:|x|\leqslant t\}
, and similarly we define the sets
[n]^{<t},[n]^{\geqslant t},[n]^{>t}
. For the purpose of clarity of the presentation we will make use of the following notation: For real numbers
a,b
and
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
we write
a\!\mathrel{\overset{\varepsilon}{\sim}}\!\!b
to denote that
a\in\left(\left(1-\varepsilon\right)b,\left(1+\varepsilon\right)b\right)
.

Throughout the paper the symbol
\mu
denotes the uniform probability measure corresponding to the ambient space under consideration which will always be clear from context, with the binary tree
Tree_{N}
on
N
levels being the only exception (the measure
\mu
for the tree was given in subsection
1.3
). Finally we write
\log
for logarithm in base
2
, and we use
\ln
for the natural logarithm, while the symbols
\mathbb{P}
and
\ave
denote probability and expectation with respect to auxiliary probability spaces.

Recall that the
bi-Lipschitz distortion
of an injective map between two metric spaces
f:(M_{1},d_{1})\to(M_{2},d_{2})
is defined to be:

distortion(f):=\left(\sup_{x_{1}\neq x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}\in M_{1}}\dfrac{d_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})}{d_{2}(f(x_{1}),f(x_{2}))}\right)\cdot\left(\sup_{x_{1}\neq x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}\in M_{1}}\dfrac{d_{2}(f(x_{1}),f(x_{2}))}{d_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})}\right).

We write
c_{M_{2}}(M_{1})
for the infimal distortion over all injective functions
f:M_{1}\to M_{2}
.

Whenever
M_{2}=L_{2}
is the Hilbert space, we write
c_{2}(M_{1}):=c_{M_{2}}(M_{1})
.

2.2.
Basic facts about concentration of measure

Definition 2.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
and
\mu
the uniform probability measure on
Q_{n}
. We define:

(3)

\alpha_{\varepsilon,n}\coloneq\mu\left([n]^{\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\frac{n}{2}}\right)=\mu\left([n]^{\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)\frac{n}{2}}\right).

We will use the following rough bound for the tails of the binomial distribution:

Lemma 2.1

(Hoeffding bounds)
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
it holds that

(4)

\alpha_{\varepsilon,n}\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}n}{2}\right).

Definition 2.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{D}\subseteq Q_{n}
and
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
. The
\varepsilon
-neighborhood
of
\mathcal{D}
is the set:

(5)

\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\{x\in Q_{n}:\exists x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}\text{ such that }\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert\leqslant\varepsilon n\}.

The following well-known fact tells us that if a set is large enough, then its
\varepsilon
-neighborhood has measure close to
1
.

Lemma 2.3

(Concentration of measure phenomenon for the Hamming cube)
.

.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be a positive integer,
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
, and
\mathcal{D}\subseteq Q_{n}
. If
\mu\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\geqslant\alpha_{\varepsilon,n}
then

(6)

\mu\left(\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}\right)\geqslant 1-\alpha_{\varepsilon,n}.

Proof.

See Chapter 6 in the book
[
30
]
or Section 3.1.5 in the book
[
2
]
.
∎

Notation and estimates on roughly equitable block copies

We repeat here the following definition from the proof outline:

Definition 2.4
.

Let
n,N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{D}\subset Q_{n+N}
. For every
x\in Q_{n}
the
section
of
\mathcal{D}
on
x
is the set:

(7)

\mathcal{D}_{x}\coloneq\{y\in Q_{N}:(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}\}.

In Section
4
we use the following notation in the proof of finding roughly equitable block copies.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in Q_{n}
,
\mathcal{D}\in Q_{kn}
and
\varepsilon_{1}\in[0,1]
. We define:

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{n}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{x^{\prime}\in Q_{n}:\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert\!\mathrel{\overset{\varepsilon_{1}}{\sim}}\!\!\frac{n}{2}\right\}

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{\left(x_{0}^{i},x_{1}^{i}\right)_{i\in[k]}\in(Q_{n}\times Q_{n})^{k}:\forall i\in[k]\,\left(x_{0}^{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{n}\left(x_{1}^{i}\right)\right)\right\}

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{\left(x_{0}^{i},x_{1}^{i}\right)_{i\in[k]}\in\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}:\forall\alpha\in Q_{k}\left((x_{\alpha_{i}}^{i})_{i\in[k]}\in\mathcal{D}\right)\right\}

We have the following easy fact about the sizes of
\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{n}\left(x\right)
and
\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}
.

Fact 2.6
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in Q_{n}
and
\varepsilon_{1}\in[0,1]
. Then:

(11)

\displaystyle\mu\left(\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{n}\left(x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mu\left([n]^{\leqslant(1-\varepsilon_{1})\frac{n}{2}}\right)-\mu\left([n]^{\geqslant(1+\varepsilon_{1})\frac{n}{2}}\right)=1-2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}

(12)

\displaystyle\mu\left(\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n})^{k}.

This follows directly from the translation invariance of the measure

: for a fixed

, the map

is a bijection from

to itself, and it sends
\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{n}(x)
to the set of vectors whose Hamming weight lies in

, whose measure is exactly

.

We will also need a natural probability measure on

which we define by:

(13)

\mu^{k,n}_{\varepsilon_{1}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\coloneq\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}\right\rvert}.

3.
Upper bound for the bounded rescaling undistorted copies of cubes

The following lemma gives sufficient conditions which guarantee that in any large enough family of ’large’ events we can find two whose intersection is also ’large’.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(A_{i})_{i\in[m]}
be events in a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)
with
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu(A_{i})\geqslant\delta
.

If

m>2/\delta
,
then
there exist distinct
i,j
such that

\mu(A_{i}\cap A_{j})>\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}.

Proof.

From Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]\geqslant\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}\Bigr)^{2}=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu(A_{i})\Bigr)^{2}\geqslant(m\delta)^{2}.

Expanding the square,

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu(A_{i})+\sum_{i\neq j}\mu(A_{i}\cap A_{j}).

Hence

\sum_{i\neq j}\mu(A_{i}\cap A_{j})\geqslant(m\delta)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu(A_{i})\geqslant m^{2}\delta^{2}-m.

The average over the
m(m-1)
unordered pairs is therefore

\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\mu(A_{i}\cap A_{j})\geqslant\frac{m^{2}\delta^{2}-m}{m(m-1)}=\delta^{2}\,\frac{m\delta-1}{m\delta-\delta}.

The function
g(x)=(x-1)/(x-\delta)
is increasing for
x>\delta
. Since
m\delta>2
by hypothesis,
g(m\delta)\geqslant g(2)=(2-1)/(2-\delta)>1/2
. Consequently the average exceeds
\delta^{2}/2
, so some specific pair must satisfy
\mu(A_{i}\cap A_{j})>\delta^{2}/2
.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(the inductive step)
.

Let
n,N\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\mathcal{D}\subseteq Q_{n}\times Q_{N}
with
\mu(\mathcal{D})\geqslant\delta
.
If
we have

n\geqslant\max\Bigl\{8\ln\!\Bigl(\frac{4}{\delta}\Bigr),\;\frac{8}{\delta}\Bigr\},

then
there exist
x_{0},x_{1}\in Q_{n}
with
\lVert x_{0}-x_{1}\rVert_{1}=2
such that

\mu\bigl(\mathcal{D}_{x_{0}}\cap\mathcal{D}_{x_{1}}\bigr)\geqslant\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}.

Proof.

Let
\mu
denote uniform measure on
Q_{n}
as well. By Hoeffding’s bound,

\mu\Bigl(\bigl\{x:\bigl|\lvert x\rvert-\tfrac{n}{2}\bigr|\geqslant\tfrac{n}{4}\bigr\}\Bigr)=2\alpha_{1/2,n}\leqslant 2\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{n}{8}\Bigr).

Our assumption on
n
guarantees
2\exp(-n/8)\leqslant\delta/2
. Therefore the average section density on the “middle strip”
\{x:|\lvert x\rvert-n/2|<n/4\}
satisfies

\ave_{|\lvert x\rvert-n/2|<n/4}\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x})\geqslant\frac{\delta-(\delta/2)\cdot 1}{1-\delta/2}>\frac{\delta}{2}.

Consequently there exists a layer
\binom{[n]}{j}
(with
j
between
n/4
and
3n/4
) such that

\ave_{x\in\binom{[n]}{j}}\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x})>\frac{\delta}{2}.

Fix such a
j
. For each
(j-1)
-set
y
let

\mathcal{C}_{y}\coloneqq\bigl\{x\in\tbinom{[n]}{j}:y\subset x\bigr\}.

Every
x\in\binom{[n]}{j}
contains exactly
j
different
(j-1)
-subsets, so

\sum_{x\in\binom{[n]}{j}}\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x})=\frac{1}{j}\sum_{y\in\binom{[n]}{j-1}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{y}}\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x}).

Dividing by
\binom{n}{j}
gives

\ave_{x\in\binom{[n]}{j}}\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x})=\ave_{y\in\binom{[n]}{j-1}}\ave_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{y}}\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x}).

Hence there exists a
(j-1)
-set
y
for which

\ave_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{y}}\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x})>\frac{\delta}{2}.

Now
\mathcal{C}_{y}
consists of all
j
-sets containing
y
; its size is
n-j+1\geq n/4
. Moreover, any two distinct elements
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{y}
satisfy
\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert=2
(they differ in exactly one element outside
y
). Because
n/4>2/(\delta/2)=4/\delta
by the hypothesis on
n
, Lemma
3.1
applied to the family
\{\mathcal{D}_{x}:x\in\mathcal{C}_{y}\}
with density
\delta/2
yields
x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{y}
with

\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x_{0}}\cap\mathcal{D}_{x_{1}})>\frac{(\delta/2)^{2}}{2}=\frac{\delta^{2}}{2},

and
\lVert x_{0}-x_{1}\rVert=2
as required.
∎

We now iterate Lemma
3.2
to build an undistorted copy of
Q_{k}
in any large enough
\mathcal{D}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Define
\gamma(\delta)\coloneqq\max\bigl\{8\ln(4/\delta),\,8/\delta\bigr\}
. For
k\geq 1
and
\delta\in(0,1)
set

N(k,\delta)\coloneqq 2+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma\!\Bigl(\frac{\delta^{2^{i-1}}}{2^{\,2^{i-1}-1}}\Bigr).

Every
\mathcal{D}\subseteq Q_{N(k,\delta)}
with
\mu(\mathcal{D})>\delta
contains an isometric copy of
Q_{k}
with rescaling
2
. Consequently,

\Emb(1,\delta,k)\leqslant N(k,\delta)\leqslant 2^{\,2^{k}\log(2/\delta)}.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
k
that if
N\geqslant N(k,\delta)
and
\mu(\mathcal{D})>\delta
, then
\mathcal{D}
contains an isometric copy of
Q_{k}
with rescaling
2
.

Base
k=1
.

Take
n_{1}=\gamma(\delta)
. Since
N(k,\delta)\geqslant n_{1}+2
, we can write
Q_{N}=Q_{n_{1}}\times Q_{N-n_{1}}
with
N-n_{1}\geqslant 2
. Apply Lemma
3.2
to
\mathcal{D}
viewed as a subset of
Q_{n_{1}}\times Q_{N-n_{1}}
; the lemma gives
x_{0},x_{1}\in Q_{n_{1}}
with
\lVert x_{0}-x_{1}\rVert=2
and
\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x_{0}}\cap\mathcal{D}_{x_{1}})>\delta^{2}/2>0
. Hence there exists
y\in Q_{N-n_{1}}
such that
(x_{0},y),(x_{1},y)\in\mathcal{D}
. Mapping
0\mapsto(x_{0},y)
,
1\mapsto(x_{1},y)
provides the desired isometric embedding of
Q_{1}
.

Inductive step.

Assume the statement holds for
k-1
. Set
n_{1}=\gamma(\delta)
and note that

N(k,\delta)-n_{1}=2+\sum_{i=2}^{k}\gamma\!\Bigl(\frac{\delta^{2^{i-1}}}{2^{\,2^{i-1}-1}}\Bigr)=N\!\Bigl(k-1,\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\Bigr).

Write
Q_{N}=Q_{n_{1}}\times Q_{N-n_{1}}
with
N\geqslant N(k,\delta)
. By Lemma
3.2
applied to
\mathcal{D}\subseteq Q_{n_{1}}\times Q_{N-n_{1}}
, there exist
x_{0},x_{1}\in Q_{n_{1}}
with
\lVert x_{0}-x_{1}\rVert=2
and

\mu\bigl(\mathcal{D}_{x_{0}}\cap\mathcal{D}_{x_{1}}\bigr)>\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}.

Define
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}_{x_{0}}\cap\mathcal{D}_{x_{1}}\subseteq Q_{N-n_{1}}
. Since
N-n_{1}\geqslant N(k-1,\delta^{2}/2)
, the inductive hypothesis applied to
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
with density parameter
\delta^{2}/2
yields an isometric embedding
g:Q_{k-1}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
satisfying
\lVert g(\beta)-g(\beta^{\prime})\rVert=2\lVert\beta-\beta^{\prime}\rVert
.

Now define
f:Q_{k}\to\mathcal{D}
by

f(\alpha_{1},\beta)\coloneqq\begin{cases}(x_{0},g(\beta))&\text{if }\alpha_{1}=0,\\[2.0pt]
(x_{1},g(\beta))&\text{if }\alpha_{1}=1,\end{cases}\qquad(\beta\in Q_{k-1}).

Both
(x_{0},g(\beta))
and
(x_{1},g(\beta))
belong to
\mathcal{D}
because
g(\beta)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
. For two vectors
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\beta)
,
\alpha^{\prime}=(\alpha_{1}^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
we have

||f(\alpha)-f(\alpha^{\prime})||_{1}=||x_{\alpha_{1}}-x_{\alpha_{1}^{\prime}}||_{1}+||g(\beta)-g(\beta^{\prime})||_{1}=2|\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{1}^{\prime}|+2||\beta-\beta^{\prime}||_{1}=2||\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}||_{1},

Hence
\lVert f(\alpha)-f(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert=2\lVert\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\rVert
, so
f
is an isometric embedding of
Q_{k}
with rescaling
2
.

Finally, we get a bound on
N(k,\delta)
.
For
\delta\leq 1
we have
\gamma(\delta)\leqslant 8/\delta+8\ln(4/\delta)\leqslant C/\delta
with an absolute constant
C
. Then

N(k,\delta)\leqslant 2+C\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{\,2^{i-1}-1}}{\delta^{2^{i-1}}}\leqslant C^{\prime}\frac{2^{\,2^{k-1}}}{\delta^{\,2^{k-1}}}\leqslant 2^{\,2^{k}\log(2/\delta)},

where the last inequality uses
\delta^{-2^{k-1}}=2^{2^{k-1}\log(1/\delta)}
and absorbs constants into the exponent. This gives the claimed double‑exponential upper bound.
∎

4.
Upper bound for
(1+\varepsilon)
-distorted copies of cubes

We refer the reader to subsection
2.2
for preliminaries in concentration of measure and to subsection
2.3
for notation regarding roughly equitable block copies.

Lemma 4.1

(Counting the number of roughly balanced pairs
(x_{0}^{1},x_{1}^{1})
inside a subset)
.

.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon_{1}\in[0,1]
and
\mathcal{D}\subset Q_{n}
. Then it holds that

\mu^{1,n}_{\varepsilon_{1}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)>\left(1-\frac{2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}}{\mu\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}\right)\left(\mu\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\right)^{2}.

In particular
if

\mu\left(\mathcal{D}\right)>2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}

then

\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

For every
x\in\mathcal{D}
, from Equation (
11
) of Fact
2.6
we have

\mu\bigl(\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{n}\left(x\right)\bigr)=1-2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}.

The set
\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{n}(x)
misses precisely the points
x^{\prime}
with
\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert\leqslant(1-\varepsilon_{1})n/2
or
\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert\geqslant(1+\varepsilon_{1})n/2
; these two tails together have measure
2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}
. Consequently,

\mu\bigl(\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{n}(x)\cap\mathcal{D}\bigr)\geqslant\mu(\mathcal{D})-2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}.

Now we count pairs. Every element of
\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}(\mathcal{D})
is of the form
(x,x^{\prime})
with
x\in\mathcal{D}
and
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{n}(x)
. Hence

\bigl|\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}(\mathcal{D})\bigr|\geqslant\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\bigl|\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{n}(x)\bigr|\geqslant|\mathcal{D}|\cdot\min_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\bigl|\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{n}(x)\bigr|.

Dividing by
|\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}|=(1-2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n})\,2^{2n}
(by (
12
)) gives

\mu^{1,n}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\mathcal{D})\geqslant\frac{|\mathcal{D}|\bigl(\mu(\mathcal{D})-2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}\bigr)2^{n}}{(1-2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n})2^{2n}}=\frac{\mu(\mathcal{D})\bigl(\mu(\mathcal{D})-2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}\bigr)}{1-2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}}.

Since
1-2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}\leqslant 1
, we obtain

\mu^{1,n}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\mathcal{D})>\mu(\mathcal{D})\bigl(\mu(\mathcal{D})-2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}\bigr)=\Bigl(1-\frac{2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}}{\mu(\mathcal{D})}\Bigr)\bigl(\mu(\mathcal{D})\bigr)^{2}.

In particular, if
\mu(\mathcal{D})>2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}
then
\mu^{1,n}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\mathcal{D})>0
, and therefore
\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}(\mathcal{D})\neq\emptyset
.
∎

The next proposition extends the block construction to
k
dimensions by induction. The inductive hypothesis is that under suitable density conditions we can find
k
pairs
(x_{0}^{i},x_{1}^{i})
with
\lVert x_{0}^{i}-x_{1}^{i}\rVert\approx n/2
such that all
2^{k}
concatenations belong to
\mathcal{D}
.

Proposition 4.2

(Finding roughly equitable block copies inside extremely dense subsets)
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
k\leqslant n
,
\varepsilon_{1}\in[\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}},1]
, and
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{kn}
with
\mu\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\delta
.
If
we have

(14)

\delta>\delta_{k}\coloneq\left(2^{k}\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}}\;\;\;and\;\;\;3k<\varepsilon_{1}^{2}n

then

\mathcal{D}
contains a roughly equitable block copy, i.e.
\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
k
that under the stated conditions
\mu^{k,n}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\mathcal{D})>0
, which immediately implies
\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}(\mathcal{D})\neq\emptyset
.

Base case
k=1
:

Lemma
4.1
gives
\mu_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}(\mathcal{D})\geqslant(1-2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}/\delta)\delta^{2}
. Because
\delta>\delta_{1}=2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}
(by (
14
) with
k=1
), the factor in parentheses is positive, so
\mu_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}(\mathcal{D})>0
.

Inductive step:
Assume
k\geqslant 2
and the statement holds for
k-1
. Write
Q_{kn}=Q_{n}\times Q_{(k-1)n}
. For each
x\in Q_{(k-1)n}
let
\mathcal{D}_{x}=\{y\in Q_{n}:(y,x)\in\mathcal{D}\}
be the section of
\mathcal{D}
over
x
. Define, for a pair
(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}
,

V_{(y,y^{\prime})}\coloneq\bigl\{x\in Q_{(k-1)n}:\{y,y^{\prime}\}\subset\mathcal{D}_{x}\bigr\}.

The key observation is the following double‑counting identity:

\sum_{x\in Q_{(k-1)n}}\bigl|\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}(\mathcal{D}_{x})\bigr|=\sum_{(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}}|V_{(y,y^{\prime})}|.

Indeed, the left‑hand side counts triples
(x,y,y^{\prime})
with
x\in Q_{(k-1)n}
and
(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}(\mathcal{D}_{x})
; the right‑hand side counts the same triples grouped by
(y,y^{\prime})
first. Dividing both sides by
|\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}|\cdot 2^{(k-1)n}
yields

(15)

\ave_{x}\mu_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}(\mathcal{D}_{x})=\ave_{(y,y^{\prime})}\mu\bigl(V_{(y,y^{\prime})}\bigr),

where the averages are taken with respect to the uniform measures on
Q_{(k-1)n}
and
\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}
, respectively.

Now we lower‑bound the left‑hand side. For each
x
, Lemma
4.1
gives

\mu_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}(\mathcal{D}_{x})\geqslant f\bigl(\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x})\bigr)\quad\text{with}\quad f(t)\coloneqq\Bigl(1-\frac{2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}}{t}\Bigr)t^{2}\;(t>0).

The function
f
is convex on
(0,\infty)
(its second derivative
f^{\prime\prime}(t)=2+4\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}/t^{3}>0
). Moreover, Jensen’s inequality applied to the convex function
f
together with
\ave_{x}\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x})=\delta
gives

\ave_{x}\mu_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{1,n}(\mathcal{D}_{x})\geqslant f(\delta)=\Bigl(1-\frac{2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}}{\delta}\Bigr)\delta^{2}.

Combining this with (
15
) we obtain

(16)

\ave_{(y,y^{\prime})}\mu\bigl(V_{(y,y^{\prime})}\bigr)\geqslant\Bigl(1-\frac{2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}}{\delta}\Bigr)\delta^{2}.

If the right‑hand side of (
16
) exceeds
\delta_{k-1}
, then there exists a pair
(y,y^{\prime})
for
\mu(V_{(y,y^{\prime})})>\delta_{k-1}
. For such a pair we can apply the inductive hypothesis to the set
V_{(y,y^{\prime})}\subseteq Q_{(k-1)n}
and conclude that
\mathcal{V}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}(\mathcal{D})\neq\emptyset
.

Thus it suffices to verify that

\Bigl(1-\frac{2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}}{\delta_{k}}\Bigr)\delta_{k}^{2}\;>\;\delta_{k-1}.

The following auxiliary claim will help.

Claim 4.3
.

For
\varepsilon_{1}\geqslant 2/\sqrt{n}
and
k\geqslant 2
,

\delta_{k-1}=2^{-1/2^{\,k-2}}\,\delta_{k}^{2},\qquad\frac{2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}}{\delta_{k}}\leqslant(\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n})^{1/2}.

Proof of Claim
4.3
.

The first equality follows directly from the definition of
\delta_{k}
. For the second inequality we need to show
2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}^{1/2}\leqslant\delta_{k}
. Raising both sides to the power
2^{k-1}
gives the equivalent condition

2^{2^{k-1}}\cdot\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}^{\,2^{k-2}}\leqslant 2^{k}\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;2^{2^{k-1}-k}\leqslant\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}^{\,1-2^{k-2}}.

For
k=2
this reads
2^{2-2}=1\leqslant\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}^{0}=1
, which is true. For
k\geqslant 3
note the
1-2^{k-2}<0
, and that the inequality becomes

\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{2^{k-1}-k}{2^{k-2}-1}}.

The right‑hand side is at least
\frac{1}{4}
for
k\geqslant 3
, furthermore by Hoeffding’s bound (Lemma
2.1
),
\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}\leqslant\exp(-\varepsilon_{1}^{2}n/2)
. Now the condition
\varepsilon_{1}\geqslant\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}
yields the desired bound.
∎

Using Claim
4.3
,

\Bigl(1-\frac{2\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}}{\delta_{k}}\Bigr)\delta_{k}^{2}\geqslant\bigl(1-(\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n})^{1/2}\bigr)\delta_{k}^{2}=\bigl(1-(\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n})^{1/2}\bigr)2^{1/2^{k-2}}\delta_{k-1}.

Now again by Hoeffding’s bound and the constraint
3k<\varepsilon_{1}^{2}n
,
we have that
\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leqslant e^{-\frac{3k}{4}}
, hence it is enough to show that:

(17)

\bigl(1-e^{-\frac{3k}{4}}\bigr)2^{1/2^{k-2}}\geqslant 1.

This holds for every
k\geqslant 2
and consequently the product exceeds
\delta_{k-1}
, completing the induction.
∎

The above block construction can be used to bound
\Emb(1+\varepsilon,\delta,k)
but gives us very bad bounds which are of the order of
2^{k}
. The next proposition shows how to exploit the concentration of measure phenomenon and the nice block structure that is guaranteed by Proposition
4.2
to obtain much better bounds.

Proposition 4.4

(Small block error and small perturbation error yield small distortion)
.

.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{D}\subset\{0,1\}^{kn}
. Let
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\in(0,1)
and set

\varepsilon\coloneqq 9\left(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}k\right).

Assume that

\varepsilon\leqslant\frac{1}{4}.

If
we have a roughly equitable block copy inside the
\varepsilon_{2}
-nbhd of
\mathcal{D}
, i.e.
\mathcal{B}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}\bigl(\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon_{2}}\bigr)\neq\emptyset
,

then

\mathcal{D}
contains a
(1+\varepsilon)
-distorted and
r:=\frac{n}{2}\bigl(1-\varepsilon_{1}-4\varepsilon_{2}k\bigr)
-rescaled copy of the Hamming cube
\{0,1\}^{k}
, i.e.
there exists an injective function
f:Q_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
such that for every
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in Q_{k}
it holds that

(18)

r\,\left\lVert\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\right\rVert\;\leqslant\;\left\lVert f\left(\alpha\right)-f\left(\alpha^{\prime}\right)\right\rVert\;\leqslant\;r\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\left\lVert\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\right\rVert.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{B}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}\bigl(\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon_{2}}\bigr)\neq\emptyset
, there exist pairs

(x_{0}^{i},x_{1}^{i})\in Q_{n}\times Q_{n}\quad\text{for each }i\in[k]

such that

(19)

\frac{n}{2}(1-\varepsilon_{1})\;\leqslant\;\lVert x_{0}^{i}-x_{1}^{i}\rVert\;\leqslant\;\frac{n}{2}(1+\varepsilon_{1})\quad\forall i\in[k],

and for every
\alpha=(\alpha_{i})_{i\in[k]}\in Q_{k}
, the concatenated vector

X(\alpha)\coloneqq(x_{\alpha_{i}}^{i})_{i\in[k]}\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon_{2}}.

By the definition of
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon_{2}}
, for each
\alpha\in Q_{k}
there exists
Y(\alpha)\in\mathcal{D}
such that

(20)

\lVert Y(\alpha)-X(\alpha)\rVert\;\leqslant\;\varepsilon_{2}kn.

Fix one such
Y(\alpha)
for each
\alpha
and define
f(\alpha)\coloneqq Y(\alpha)
.

Step 1: Adjacent vertices.

Suppose
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in Q_{k}
differ in exactly one coordinate. Then
X(\alpha)
and
X(\alpha^{\prime})
differ in exactly one block, and by (
19
),

\lVert X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert=\lVert x_{0}^{i}-x_{1}^{i}\rVert\in\left[\frac{n}{2}(1-\varepsilon_{1}),\,\frac{n}{2}(1+\varepsilon_{1})\right].

Using the triangle inequality and (
20
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert f(\alpha)-f(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert f(\alpha)-X(\alpha)\rVert+\lVert X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert+\lVert X(\alpha^{\prime})-f(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon_{2}kn+\frac{n}{2}(1+\varepsilon_{1})+\varepsilon_{2}kn=\frac{n}{2}\bigl(1+\varepsilon_{1}+4\varepsilon_{2}k\bigr),

and similarly

\displaystyle\lVert f(\alpha)-f(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert

\displaystyle\geqslant\lVert X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert-\lVert f(\alpha)-X(\alpha)\rVert-\lVert f(\alpha^{\prime})-X(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{n}{2}(1-\varepsilon_{1})-2\varepsilon_{2}kn=\frac{n}{2}\bigl(1-\varepsilon_{1}-4\varepsilon_{2}k\bigr)=r.

Thus, whenever
\lVert\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\rVert=1
,

(21)

r\;\leqslant\;\lVert f(\alpha)-f(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert\;\leqslant\;\frac{n}{2}\bigl(1+\varepsilon_{1}+4\varepsilon_{2}k\bigr).

Step 2: General pairs.

Let now
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in Q_{k}
be arbitrary and set
d\coloneqq\lVert\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\rVert
. Choose a geodesic path

\alpha=\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1},\dots,\alpha^{d}=\alpha^{\prime}

in the hypercube with consecutive vertices differing in one coordinate. Summing the block‑wise distances along this path gives

(22)

\frac{n}{2}(1-\varepsilon_{1})d\;\leqslant\;\lVert X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert\;\leqslant\;\frac{n}{2}(1+\varepsilon_{1})d.

Applying the triangle inequality together with (
20
) yields

\displaystyle\lVert f(\alpha)-f(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert f(\alpha)-X(\alpha)\rVert+\lVert X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert+\lVert X(\alpha^{\prime})-f(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\varepsilon_{2}kn+\frac{n}{2}(1+\varepsilon_{1})d\leqslant\frac{n}{2}\bigl(1+\varepsilon_{1}+4\varepsilon_{2}k\bigr)d,

because
2\varepsilon_{2}kn=(n/2)\cdot 4\varepsilon_{2}k\leqslant(n/2)\cdot 4\varepsilon_{2}k\,d
(since
d\geq 1
). For the lower bound,

\displaystyle\lVert f(\alpha)-f(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert

\displaystyle\geqslant\lVert X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert-\lVert f(\alpha)-X(\alpha)\rVert-\lVert f(\alpha^{\prime})-X(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{n}{2}(1-\varepsilon_{1})d-2\varepsilon_{2}kn=\frac{n}{2}\bigl((1-\varepsilon_{1})d-4\varepsilon_{2}k\bigr)\geqslant\frac{n}{2}\bigl(1-\varepsilon_{1}-4\varepsilon_{2}k\bigr)d=r\,d,

where the last inequality uses
d\geq 1
to replace
(1-\varepsilon_{1})d-4\varepsilon_{2}k
by
(1-\varepsilon_{1}-4\varepsilon_{2}k)d
.

Thus for all
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in Q_{k}
,

(23)

r\,\lVert\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\rVert\;\leqslant\;\lVert f(\alpha)-f(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert\;\leqslant\;\frac{n}{2}\bigl(1+\varepsilon_{1}+4\varepsilon_{2}k\bigr)\lVert\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\rVert.

Step 3: Bounding the upper factor by
r(1+\varepsilon)
.

Recall that
r=\frac{n}{2}(1-t)
with
t\coloneqq\varepsilon_{1}+4\varepsilon_{2}k
, and the upper factor in (
23
) is
\frac{n}{2}(1+t)
. Set
u\coloneqq\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}k
; then by definition
\varepsilon=9u
. The assumption
\varepsilon\leq 1/4
gives
u\leq 1/36
. Moreover,

t=\varepsilon_{1}+4\varepsilon_{2}k\leqslant\varepsilon_{1}+4(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}k)=5\varepsilon_{1}+4\varepsilon_{2}k\leqslant 4u,

where the last inequality uses
\varepsilon_{1}\leq u
.

We need to show

\frac{1+t}{1-t}\leqslant 1+9u=1+\varepsilon.

Since the right‑hand side is a decreasing function of
t
for fixed
u
, it suffices to check the inequality at the largest possible
t
, namely
t=4u
. Thus we verify that for
0\leq u\leq 1/36
,

1+4u\leqslant(1-4u)(1+9u).

Expanding the right side gives
1+5u-36u^{2}
, so the inequality reduces to

1+4u\leqslant 1+5u-36u^{2}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;0\leqslant u-36u^{2}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;u(1-36u)\geq 0,

which holds precisely when
u\leq 1/36
. Therefore

\frac{n}{2}(1+\varepsilon_{1}+4\varepsilon_{2}k)=\frac{n}{2}(1+t)\leqslant\frac{n}{2}(1-t)(1+9u)=S(1+\varepsilon).

Combining this with (
23
) yields the desired bi‑Lipschitz bounds (
18
). Finally,
r>0
because
t\leq 4u\leq 4/36=1/9
, so
1-t\geq 8/9>0
; consequently
f
is injective (distinct points have positive distance).
∎

Now we put the two propositions together to obtain the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
. Define

(24)

N(\varepsilon,\delta,k)=C\varepsilon^{-2}k^{3}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right),

with
C=4\cdot 18^{2}
. Then for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
N\geqslant N(\varepsilon,\delta,k)
and every
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{N}
with
\mu\left(\mathcal{D}\right)>\delta
there exists a
(1+\varepsilon)
-bi-Lipschitz embedding
f:Q_{k}\to\mathcal{D}
. Consequently,

\Emb(1+\varepsilon,\delta,k)\leqslant C\varepsilon^{-2}k^{3}\log\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr).

Proof.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
N\geqslant N(\varepsilon,\delta,k)
and
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{N}
with
\mu\left(\mathcal{D}\right)>\delta
, first note that we may assume that
\delta<\frac{1}{4}
else we can work on a subset of
\mathcal{D}
. Furthermore note we can assume that
N=nk
for
n=\left\lfloor\frac{N}{k}\right\rfloor\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geqslant C\varepsilon^{-2}k^{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)-1
, this can be done with a standard averaging argument. As a first remark note that the constraint
24
we have imposed on
N
implies that:

(25)

\varepsilon\geqslant\sqrt{\frac{Ck^{3}\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}{N}}.

The goal is to apply Propositions
4.2
and
4.4
. To this end we define
\varepsilon_{1}=\frac{\varepsilon}{18}
and
\varepsilon_{2}=\frac{\varepsilon}{18k}
and note that the following equation is satisfied

(26)

9(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}k)=\varepsilon<\frac{1}{4}.

So we may apply Proposition
4.4
as long as the assumptions for Proposition
4.2
are satisfied. First note that
n\geqslant k
. Furthermore from the assumption that
\delta<\frac{1}{4}

(27)

\varepsilon_{1}^{2}n=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{18^{2}}\left\lfloor\frac{N}{k}\right\rfloor\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{18^{2}}\left(C\varepsilon^{-2}k^{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)-1\right)>7k^{2}>3k.

In particular we also have that:

(28)

\varepsilon_{1}>k\sqrt{\frac{7}{n}}>\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}.

So we only need to verify that:

(29)

\mu\left(\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon_{2}}\right)>\left(2^{k}\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}\right)^{2^{-(k-1)}}.

From (
24
), the definition of
\varepsilon_{2}
, the definition of
\alpha_{\varepsilon_{2},N}
and Hoeffdding’s bound in Equation
4
we have:

(30)

\displaystyle C\varepsilon^{-2}k^{3}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant N\Longleftrightarrow\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}N}{Ck^{3}}

(31)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\delta\geqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{18^{2}k^{2}}\frac{N}{2}\right)^{\frac{2\cdot 18^{2}}{Ck}}\geqslant\alpha_{\varepsilon_{2},N}^{\frac{2\cdot 18^{2}}{Ck}}.

From the definition of
C
we have that
\frac{2\cdot 18^{2}}{Ck}\leqslant 1
hence by Lemma
2.3
and another application of Hoeffdding’s bound we have that:

(32)

\mu\left(\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon_{2}}\right)\geqslant 1-\alpha_{\varepsilon_{2},N}\geqslant 1-\exp\left(-\varepsilon_{2}^{2}\frac{N}{2}\right)=1-\exp\left(-\varepsilon^{2}\frac{N}{2\cdot 18^{2}k^{2}}\right).

Plugin in the constrain
24
for
N
and noting that for
\delta<\frac{1}{4}
, it holds that
\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)>2
we get that:

(33)

\mu\left(\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon_{2}}\right)\geqslant 1-\exp\left(-\varepsilon^{2}\frac{C\varepsilon^{-2}k^{3}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}{2\cdot 18^{2}k^{2}}\right)>1-\exp\left(-4k\right).

So for Equation
29
to hold it is enough to show that:

(34)

1-\exp\left(-4k\right)\geqslant\left(2^{k}\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}\right)^{2^{-(k-1)}}

Taking logarithms and using one more time Hoeffdding’s bound for
\alpha_{\varepsilon_{1},n}
, we have that it is enough to show that:

(35)

\log\left(1-\exp\left(-4k\right)\right)\geqslant\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\left(k-\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\frac{n}{2\ln 2}\right),

we rearrange and have that the conclusion of the theorem will follow if we prove that:

(36)

2^{k-1}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\exp\left(-4k\right)}\right)\leqslant\left(\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\frac{n}{2\ln 2}-k\right)

Now note that from the calculations we made in Equation
27

2k^{2}<\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\frac{n}{2\ln 2}-k
. Furthermore with an application of the inequality
\log(1+x)\leqslant\frac{x}{\ln 2}
for
x\geqslant-1
we have that

\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\exp\left(-4k\right)}\right)=\log\left(1+\frac{\exp\left(-4k\right)}{1-\exp\left(-4k\right)}\right)\leqslant\frac{\exp\left(-4k\right)}{\ln 2\left(1-\exp\left(-4k\right)\right)}.

From the above consideration Equation
36
will follow if:

(37)

2^{k-1}\frac{\exp\left(-4k\right)}{\ln 2\left(1-\exp\left(-4k\right)\right)}\leqslant 2k^{2}.

This holds and the proof is complete.
∎

5.
Lower bounds for undistorted cubes

Proposition 5.1

(Characterization of undistorted copies)
.

Let
k,N\in\mathbb{N}
with
N\geqslant k
and
1\leqslant r\leqslant N
and let
f:Q_{k}\to\{0,1\}^{N}
for which there exists
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in Q_{k}
it holds:

(38)

\lVert f(\alpha)-f(\alpha^{\prime})\rVert=r\lVert\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\rVert.

Then there exists
b\in\{0,1\}^{N}
and
k
-disjoint subsets
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}
of
[N]
, each of size
r
, such that the map
f
is given by:

(39)

f(\alpha)=b+\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{i\in[k]:\alpha_{i}=1}\mathbf{1}_{I_{i}}.

Proof.

Let
f:Q_{k}\to\{0,1\}^{N}
be as in the hypothesis of Proposition
5.1
. Let
0^{k}
be the zero vector in
Q_{k}
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
f(0^{k})=0^{N}
(otherwise translate by
b=f(0^{k})
). For every
i\in[k]
let
e_{i}\in Q_{k}
be the standard basic vector whose
i
-th coordinate is
1
and all others are
0
. For each
\alpha\in Q_{k}
, denote by

A_{\alpha}=\{m\in[N]:f(\alpha)_{m}=1\}

the support of
f(\alpha)
. Define
I_{i}\subset[N]
by

I_{i}=A_{e_{i}}=\left\{m\in[N]:f(e_{i})_{m}=1\right\}.

Note that for every
i\in[k]
we have that
\lVert f(e_{i})\rVert=\lvert A_{e_{i}}\rvert=r
and for every
i\neq j
with
i,j\in[k]
it holds that
\lVert f(e_{i})-f(e_{j})\rVert=2r
. This implies that
I_{i}\cap I_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
. Furthermore,
\lVert f(e_{i}+e_{j})\rVert=r\lVert e_{i}+e_{j}\rVert=2r
.

We compute

r=r\lVert e_{i}+e_{j}-e_{i}\rVert=\lVert f(e_{i}+e_{j})-f(e_{i})\rVert.

On the other hand

\lVert f(e_{i}+e_{j})-f(e_{i})\rVert=\lvert A_{e_{i}+e_{j}}\triangle A_{e_{i}}\rvert=\lvert A_{e_{i}+e_{j}}\rvert+\lvert A_{e_{i}}\rvert-2\lvert A_{e_{i}+e_{j}}\cap A_{e_{i}}\rvert.

Since
\lvert A_{e_{i}+e_{j}}\rvert=2r
and
\lvert A_{e_{i}}\rvert=r
, we deduce that

r=2r+r-2\lvert A_{e_{i}+e_{j}}\cap A_{e_{i}}\rvert\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lvert A_{e_{i}+e_{j}}\cap A_{e_{i}}\rvert=r.

Similarly
\lvert A_{e_{i}+e_{j}}\cap A_{e_{j}}\rvert=r
, so we can compute that

\lVert f(e_{i}+e_{j})-(f(e_{i})+f(e_{j}))\rVert=\lvert A_{e_{i}+e_{j}}\triangle(A_{e_{i}}\cup A_{e_{j}})\rvert=0.

Thus
f(e_{i}+e_{j})=f(e_{i})+f(e_{j})
for all
i\neq j
. Because
i
and
j
were arbitrary and the supports
I_{i}=A_{e_{i}}
are pairwise disjoint, we can now prove by induction on the Hamming weight of
\alpha
that

f(\alpha)=\sum_{i:\alpha_{i}=1}f(e_{i})=\sum_{i:\alpha_{i}=1}\mathbf{1}_{I_{i}}

for all
\alpha\in Q_{k}
, showing that
f
is affine-linear with disjointly supported basis vectors, as claimed.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Proposition
5.1
shows that every isometric copy of
Q_{k}
inside
Q_{N}
(with a fixed scaling
S
) is, up to translation, an affine subspace spanned by
k
disjointly supported vectors of weight
r
. In particular, the number of such copies inside
\{0,1\}^{N}
can be crudely bounded as follows: choose a translation vector
b
(
2^{N}
possibilities) and for each coordinate
m\in[N]
decide whether it is fixed by
b
or belongs to one of the
k
disjoint blocks
I_{i}
; this gives at most
(k+1)^{N}
choices. Hence there are no more than
(2(k+1))^{N}
rescaled isometric copies of
Q_{k}
in
Q_{N}
.
By the same reasoning, there are no more that
2^{N}RN^{Rk}
rescaled isometric copies with rescaling
\leqslant R
(there are
2^{N}
choices for
b
and for each of the
R
possible rescalings there are
\leqslant N^{Rk}
possible sets
I_{1},...,I_{k}
.)

We now use a probabilistic construction to show that
\Emb(1,\delta,k)
must be exponentially large in
k
.

Theorem 5.3

(Lower bound for undistorted copies)
.

Let
k\geq 4
and
0<\delta<1/2
. Then

\Emb(1,\delta,k)\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{5}\,\frac{2^{k}\log\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\bigr)}{\log k}.

Proof.

Let
N
be an integer to be chosen later. Consider a random subset
\mathcal{D}\subseteq Q_{N}
obtained by including each point independently with probability
p\coloneqq 3\delta/2
. Then
\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{D}|=p\,2^{N}
. By the multiplicative Chernoff bound,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(|\mathcal{D}|\leqslant\delta 2^{N}\bigr)=\mathbb{P}\bigl(|\mathcal{D}|\leqslant(1-\tfrac{1}{3})\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{D}|\bigr)\leqslant\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{(\frac{1}{3})^{2}\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{D}|}{2}\Bigr)=\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{\delta 2^{N}}{12}\Bigr).

Let
M
be the collection of all rescaled isometric copies of
Q_{k}
inside
Q_{N}
. By Remark
5.2
,
|M|\leqslant(2(k+1))^{N}
. For a fixed
M\in M
, the probability that
M\subseteq\mathcal{D}
equals
p^{2^{k}}
(because
|M|=2^{k}
and the inclusions are independent).
Taking a union bound,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists M\in M:M\subseteq\mathcal{D}\bigr)\leqslant|M|\,p^{2^{k}}\leqslant\bigl(2(k+1)\bigr)^{N}\bigl(\tfrac{3\delta}{2}\bigr)^{2^{k}}.

Thus the probability that
\mathcal{D}
either has density
<\delta
or contains an rescaled isometric copy of
Q_{k}
is at most

P_{\text{bad}}\coloneqq\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{\delta 2^{N}}{12}\Bigr)+\bigl(2(k+1)\bigr)^{N}\bigl(\tfrac{3\delta}{2}\bigr)^{2^{k}}.

If

, then there exists a specific set
\mathcal{D}
of density
\geqslant\delta
that contains no rescaled isometric copy of
Q_{k}
; this would imply
\Emb(1,\delta,k)>N
.

We now choose
N

. First make the first term
\leqslant 1/4

\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{\delta 2^{N}}{12}\Bigr)\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\frac{\delta 2^{N}}{12}\geqslant\ln 4\;\Longleftrightarrow\;2^{N}\geq\frac{12\ln 4}{\delta}.

This is certainly satisfied if
N\geq\log(1/\delta)+O(1)
. For the second term we require

\bigl(2(k+1)\bigr)^{N}\bigl(\tfrac{3\delta}{2}\bigr)^{2^{k}}\leqslant\frac{1}{4}.

Taking base‑
2
logarithms,

N\log\!\bigl(2(k+1)\bigr)+2^{k}\log\!\bigl(\tfrac{3\delta}{2}\bigr)\leqslant-2.

Since
\log(3\delta/2)=\log(1/\delta)+\log(3/2)
and
\log(1/\delta)>1

\log(3\delta/2)\leqslant\log(1/\delta)-c_{0}
with

. Also

for
k\geq 4
. Thus it suffices that

3N\log k+2^{k}\bigl(\log(1/\delta)-c_{0}\bigr)\leqslant-2.

Rearranging,

N\leqslant\frac{2^{k}\bigl(\log(1/\delta)-c_{0}\bigr)-2}{3\log k}.

Choosing
c_{0}=1
(which is a crude but valid estimate for
\log(2/3)
) and noting that
2^{k}\log(1/\delta)
dominates the constants for
k\geq 4
, we obtain the condition

N\leqslant\frac{2^{k}\log(1/\delta)}{5\log k}.

For any such
N
we have
P_{\text{bad}}<1
, hence there exists a
\delta
-dense subset of
Q_{N}
with no rescaled isometric copy of
Q_{k}
. Therefore
\Emb(1,\delta,k)>N
for all
N
satisfying the above inequality, which yields the claimed lower bound.
∎

6.
Metric embeddings of path spaces into dense subsets of path spaces

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
.

6.1.
Upper bound (via a porosity argument)

.

Throughout the proof we will fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
which is sufficiently large, and
\mathcal{D}\subset[N]
which has density
\delta\in(0,1)
(i.e.
|\mathcal{D}|\geqslant\delta N
). We will assume that there does not exist a
(1+\varepsilon)
bi-Lipscthiz embedding
f:[k]\to\mathcal{D}
, and our goal is to prove an upper bound on
|\mathcal{D}|
.

STEP 1: A single induction step:

Consider the
k
equidistant points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
in the middle thirds subinterval of
N
.

p_{i}:=i\dfrac{N}{3k}+\dfrac{N}{3}\;\;\;for\;all\;i=1,...,k.

For simplicity of notation we will ignore any floor functions in this section.
Consider now the interval centered at
p_{1},...,p_{k}
of small diameter:

I_{i}:=[p_{i}-\dfrac{\varepsilon}{18k}N,p_{i}+\dfrac{\varepsilon}{18k}N]\;\;\;for\;all\;i=1,...,k.

It is routine to check that any map
f:[k]\to[N]
with
f(i)\in I_{i}
for all
i=1,...,k
is a
(1+\varepsilon)
bi-Lipschitz embedding of the path space
[k]
.
By the hypothesis on
\mathcal{D}
this means that for some
i\in[k]
,
I_{i}\cap\mathcal{D}=\emptyset
.
We can therefore write
[N]-I_{i}=I^{0}\cup I^{1}
where
I^{0},I^{1}
are disjoint intervals of diameter
>N/3
. This completes the first step in the induction.

STEP 2: Iterating the inductive step:

We now apply the same argument to
I^{0}\cap\mathcal{D}\subset I^{0}
and
I^{1}\cap\mathcal{D}\subset I^{1}
.
We pick
k
equidistant points on the middle thirds subinterval of
I^{0}
and place intervals of radius
\dfrac{\varepsilon}{18k}|I_{0}|
at each point.
By the hypothesis, one of these intervals is disjoint from
\mathcal{D}
.
This means that
\mathcal{D}\cap I^{0}
lies in the union of two disjoint subintervals
I^{00}
and
I^{01}
each of diameter
>N/9
.

STEP 3: Estimating the total number of iterations:

We apply this argument
M:=\log_{3}(N/3k)
times.
Observe that after
M
iterations, every interval has at least
k
points and therefore we have enough points to pick
p_{1},...,p_{k}
and the intervals
I_{1},...,I_{k}
in the next iteration.

STEP 4: Estimating the size of
\mathcal{D}
:

At the
m^{th}
-iteration for each
m=1,...,M
, we conclude that the set
\mathcal{D}
lies inside the union of
2^{m}
intervals. Denote this union by
U_{m}
.
Because we remove a large subinterval from each interval in the
m^{th}
-iteration, we have
|U_{m+1}|\leqslant(1-\dfrac{\varepsilon}{18k})|U_{m}|
.
We conclude:

|\mathcal{D}|\leqslant|U_{M}|\leqslant\left(1-\dfrac{\varepsilon}{18k}\right)^{M}N=\left(1-\dfrac{\varepsilon}{18k}\right)^{-\log(3k)}N^{\log_{3}(1-\varepsilon/18k)}N\leqslant 2N^{-C\varepsilon/k}N

where
C=18^{-1}/\log(3)
and we used the convexity of the logarithm.
Rearranging the terms in the inequality
\delta\leqslant|S|/N\leqslant 2N^{-C\varepsilon/k}
gives us the promised bound:
N\leqslant e^{Ck\varepsilon^{-1}\log(1/\delta)}
.

6.2.
Lower bound (via a Cantor set construction)

.

STEP 1: Construction of the Cantor set
.

Throughout the proof we will fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
which is sufficiently large, and construct a sequence of finer and finar approximations
[N]=U_{0}\supset U_{1}\supset...\supset U_{M}=:\mathcal{D}
. For each
m=1,...,M
,
U_{m}
will be the disjoint union of
2^{m}
intervals.

At the first step, we divide
[N]=I^{0}\cup I^{mid}\cup I^{1}
where

I^{0}:=[1,N(1/2-2/k)],\;\;\;I^{mid}:=[N(1/2-2/k),N(1/2+2/k)]\;\;\;I^{1}:=[N(1/2-2/k),N],

and set
U_{1}:=I^{0}\cup I^{1}
. In plain words, we remove the middle interval of length
(4/k)N
.

At the second step, we remove the remove the middle interval of length
(4/k)|I^{0}|
from
I^{0}
to obtain
I^{00},I^{01}
and do the same for
I^{1}
and set
U_{2}:=I^{00}\cup I^{01}\cup I^{10}\cup I^{11}
. At the
m^{th}
step, we take each of the
2^{m}
intervals of
U_{m}
and remove in each the middle interval of density
4/k
to obtain
U_{m+1}
.
Observe that each interval in
U_{m}
has diameter
<(2/3)^{m}N
. We iterate this construction
M=\log_{3/2}(N)
steps, which ensures that the
2^{M}
intervals which comprise
U_{M}
are all singletons.

STEP 2: Analysis to forbid an embedding at a singe scale

Claim
: if
f:[k]\to I^{0}\cup I^{1}
has distortion
\leqslant 2
, then either
f([k])\subset I_{0}
or else
f([k])\subset I_{1}
.

Suppose not. Then there exists
i\in[k]
such that
f(i)\in I^{0}
while
f(i+1)\in I^{1}
meaning that
|f(i)-f(i+1)|>4N/k=4N/k|i-(i+1)|
. On the other hand, either
I^{0}
or
I^{1}
, say
I^{0}
has
|f([k])\cap I^{0}|\geqslant k/2
.
By pigeonholing (cover
I^{0}
with
k/2
balls of radius
N/k
each), there exists
j\neq j^{\prime}\in[k]
such that
|f(j)-f(j^{\prime})|\leqslant 2N/k\leqslant 2N/k|j-j^{\prime}|
.
We obtain the distortion bound:

Distortion(f)\geqslant\dfrac{|f(i)-f(i+1)|/|i-(i+1)|}{|f(j)-f(j^{\prime})|/|j-j^{\prime}|}>\dfrac{4N/k}{2N/k}=2

and the claim follows.
Similarly, we can apply this claim to any interval in the Cantor set construction. For every
m=1,...,M
and any
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{m}
if we have an embedding
f:[k]\to I^{\alpha 0}\cup I^{\alpha 1}
with distortion
\leqslant 2
, then either
f([k])\subset I^{\alpha 0}
or else
f([k])\subset I^{\alpha 1}
.

STEP 3: Recursive argument to forbir any embedding:

Suppose that
f:[k]\to\mathcal{D}
has distortion
\leqslant 2
.
Since
\mathcal{D}\subset I^{0}\cup I^{1}
, by the above claim, either
f([k])\subset I^{0}
or else
f([k])\subset I^{1}
. Denote by
I^{\alpha_{1}}
this interval.
By the same reasoning, since
\mathcal{D}\cap I^{\alpha_{1}}\subset I^{\alpha_{1}0}\cup I^{\alpha_{1}1}
, either
f([k])\subset I^{\alpha_{1}0}
or else
f([k])\subset I^{\alpha_{1}1}
. Denote this interval by
I^{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}
.
By iteratively applying the claim
M
-times, we conclude that
f([k])\subset I^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{M}
.
However, by construction
I^{\alpha}
has diameter
<N(2/3)^{M}=1
meaning that
I^{\alpha}
is a singleton.
A
2
-bi-Lipschitz map must be a bijection so we arrive at a contradiction.

STEP 4: Estimating the size of
\mathcal{D}
:

We now prove a lower bound on the size of the Cantor set
\mathcal{D}
.
We have:
N=|U_{0}|
and
|U_{m+1}|=(1-4/k)|U_{m}|
so we get:

\delta N:=|\mathcal{D}|=(1-4/k)^{M}N=N^{1-\log(1-4/k)/\log(3/2)}>N^{1-(2/\log(3/2))(1/k)}

where at the last step we used the numerical inequality
\log(1-x)<-x+x^{2}/2<-x/2
for all
|x|\leqslant 1/2
.
Rearranging the terms, we get:
N\geqslant e^{C^{\prime}k\log(1/\delta)}
where
C^{\prime}=\log(3/2)/2
.

7.
Metric embeddings of binary trees into dense subsets of binary trees

7.1.
Upper bound

We are given
\mathcal{D}\subset Tree(N)
with
\mu(\mathcal{D})\geqslant\delta
.
Set
N^{\prime}:=h^{-1}(\delta)N
where
h:[0,1/2]\to[0,1]
is Shannon’s binary entropy function
h(p):=p\log(1/p)+(1-p)\log(1/(1-p))

We apply Theorem 1 in
[
34
]
, and obtain a tree replica:
f:Tree(N^{\prime})\to\mathcal{D}
.
A
tree replica
is a map
f:Tree(N^{\prime})\to Tree(N)
which satisfies two properties.
First, if
w_{1},w_{2}\in Tree(N^{\prime})
which lie on the same level of the tree

f(w)
and

lie in the same level of the tree
Tree(N)

l(f(w))=l(f(w^{\prime}))

Secondly, for all
w\in Tree(N^{\prime})
with immediate children
w_{0}
and
w_{1}
, if we denote by
f(w)_{0}
and
f(w)_{1}
the immediate children of
f(w)
, then
f(w_{0})
is a descendant of
f(w)_{0}
and
f(w_{1})
is a descendant of
f(w)_{1}
(in vague words, the image of the tree ”branches out” immediately after each point).

Similar to
[
34
]
, we now consider the set of possible levels of
f(Tree(N^{\prime}))\subset\mathcal{D}
.
Call this set
\mathcal{D}_{path}\subset[N]
, and observe that it has density
|\mathcal{D}_{path}|/N=h^{-1}(\delta)
.
By the upper bound of Theorem
1.3
, we can find an
\varepsilon
-distorted copy of the path
[k]
sitting inside

so long as:

N\leqslant e^{O(k\varepsilon^{-1}\log(1/h^{-1}(\delta)))}=e^{O(k\varepsilon^{-1}\log(1/\delta))}.

Finally, we consider only the levels of
Tree(N^{\prime})
which map one of the
k

[k]
in
\mathcal{D}_{path}
.
This gives us a map

and it is routine to check that it is an
\varepsilon
-distorted embedding.

7.2.
Lower bound

We first describe the
\delta
-dense subset of the tree which avoid tree copies.
The description is much easier that the analysis.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{path}\subset[N]
be the Cantor set which was constructed in subsection
6.2
.
We take
\mathcal{D}\subset Tree(N)
to be the set of all points
w\in Tree(N)
whose level

lies in this set
\mathcal{D}_{path}
.
Clearly,
\mu(\mathcal{D})=\mu(\mathcal{D}_{path})
.
The lower bound follows once we show that
Tree(k)
cannot embed into
\mathcal{D}
with small distortion.
In fact, we will show something stronger: the path metric space
[k]
cannot embed into
\mathcal{D}
with small distortion.

Recall that a
geodesic
in a graph is a shortest path between two points (which is always unique in a tree). A geodesic between
w,w^{\prime}\in Tree(N)
has a very special form: the geodesic starts at
w
, travels upward the tree until the least common ancestor
w\vee w^{\prime}
of
w
and
w^{\prime}
is visited, and then moves downwards again to
w^{\prime}
.
We need the following stability lemma:

Lemma 7.1

(Every small distortion path in a tree is close to a geodesic)
.

.

If

f:[k]\to Tree(N)
be a
0.001
-distorted embedding with rescaling
r\in[N]
,

and
\gamma\subset Tree(N)
is the geodesic from
f(1)
to
f(k)
in
Tree(N)
,

Then
every point in the image of
f
is close to
\gamma
:

d_{T}(f(i),\gamma):=\min_{w\in\gamma}d_{T}(f(i),w)\leqslant 0.01r\;\;\;for\;all\;i\in[k].

Proof.

STEP 1: notation for tripods in trees
:

Given any triple of points
w,w^{\prime},w^{\prime\prime}\in Tree(N)
we can form a
tripod
which is the union of all the geodesics
w\to w^{\prime}
,
w^{\prime}\to w^{\prime\prime}
and
w\to w^{\prime\prime}
.
Because of the tree structure, this tripod consists of a
median point

m
such that each of the
3
geodesics must pass through
m
(e.g. the geodesic
w\to w^{\prime}
is the geodesic
w\to m
concatinated by the geodesic
m\to w^{\prime}
).

STEP 2: tripods of
3
consequtive points must be degenerate

For each
i\in[k-2]
consider the points
f(i),f(i+1),f(i+2)
and form their tripod. Denote the median by
g(i+1)
.
We claim that because
f(i),f(i+1),f(i+1)
form a slightly distorted copy of the path of length
3
, this forces
f(i+1)
and
g(i+1)
to be very close to each other. In particular, we have the estimates:

d_{T}(f(i),f(i+2))=d_{T}(f(i),g(i+1))+d_{T}(g(i+1),f(i+2))\geqslant 2r

d_{T}(f(i),f(i+1))=d_{T}(f(i),g(i+1))+d_{T}(g(i+1),f(i+1))\leqslant(1.001)r

d_{T}(f(i+1),f(i+2))=d_{T}(f(i+1),g(i+1))+d_{T}(g(i+1),f(i+2))\leqslant(1.001)r

adding the last two inequalities and subtracking the first, we get:

2d_{T}(f(i+1),g(i+1))\leqslant 0.001r\implies d_{T}(f(i+1),g(i+1))\leqslant 0.01r.

STEP 3: gluing all the tripods together
:

Set
g(1):=f(1)
and
g(k):=f(k)
and
g(i)
is defined to be the median of the tripod formed by the points
\{f(i-1),f(i),f(i+1)\}
for all

.

By construction, for all
i=1,...,k-2
. the geodesic from
g(i)
to
g(i+2)
must pass through the point
g(i+1)
(this is tedious to check but not hard and the reader should convince themselves).
So we can the path
\gamma
from
f(1)=g(1)\to g(2)\to...\to g(k)=f(k)
and observe that is must also be the geodesic from
f(1)
to
f(k)
.
We leave the full details to the reader.
∎

We are now in a position to prove the lower bound.
Suppose
f:[k]\to Tree(N)
is an embedding of distortion
<0.001
.
Then by the above lemma, we take the geodesic from
f(1)
to
f(k)
and conclude that each point in the image, say
f(i)
is
0.01r
-close to a point on the geodesic
g(i)
.
By the remark made earlier, half of the geodesic is level-decreasing and the other half is level increasing.
One of the two pieces must have at least
k/2
of the points
g(1),...,g(k)
.
Without loss of generality say it is the first half of the path, so
g(1),g(2),...,g(k/2)
is level decreasing.
It is a little tedious but not hard to check that the set of levels

forms a

-distorted path of length

in

.
But by the lower bound of Theorem
1.3
this forces

to be large:

N\geqslant Path(0.1,k/2,\delta)=e^{\Omega(k\log(1/\delta))}.

We need to estimate, for all
0<\gamma<1
, the largest value of
p
such that

|\mathcal{D}|\geqslant 2^{(1-\gamma)N}>|Ball(pN)|.

In other words, we need to solve for (or rather estimate)
p\in[0,1]
in the equation
2^{(1-\gamma)N}=|Ball(pN)|

This is a standard calculation from coding theory which we recall next.

STEP 3: Numerics using Shannon’s binary entropy function.

It is well-known (see for instance Corollary 22.9 in
[
23
]
) that

\sum_{s=1}^{pN}\binom{N}{s}\leqslant 2^{h(p)N}\;\;\;for\;all\;0<p<1\;and\;N\in\mathbb{N},

where
h(p):=p\log_{2}1/p+(1-p)\log_{2}1/(1-p)
is Shannon’s binary entropy function (a similar lower bound also holds but we will not need it here).

Our goal is to find
0<p<1
as large as possible such that
h(p)\leqslant 1-\gamma
.

We have the following calculus facts about the function
h(p)
.
One checks that
h(1/2)=1
,
h^{\prime}(1/2)=0
and
h^{\prime\prime}(p)<0
for all
p\in(0,1)
meaning that
h
is convex and has a maximum at
p=1/2
with
1^{st}
order of vanishing and no more.
Since
h
is smooth, from a Taylor expansion at
p=1/2
we obtain:

h(1/2-\beta)=1-\Theta(\beta^{2})\;\;\;\;for\;all\;0<\beta\lesssim 1.

In particular, for some universal constant
C>0
we have

h(1/2-C\sqrt{\gamma})<1-\gamma\;\;\;\;for\;all\;0<\gamma\lesssim 1.

We take
\varepsilon_{3}:=C\sqrt{\gamma}
. Harper’s inequality and the above estimate on
h(p)
give us:
\mu(\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon_{3}})\geqslant 1/2
.

We choose
k
such that
\varepsilon_{3}=1/36k
, meaning that we choose
k\gtrsim 1/\sqrt{\gamma}
.

STEP 4: Finding a roughly equitable block copy inside
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon_{3}}

By the proof of Theorem
4.5
with
\epsilon=1/2
and
\delta=1/2
we get a roughly equitable block copy with block error
\varepsilon_{1}=1/36
and rescaling
r=N/2k(1-\varepsilon_{1}-4\varepsilon_{2}k)
inside the twice-inflated set
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon_{3}+\varepsilon_{2}}
, where
\varepsilon_{2}\asymp 1/36k

Written in the notation of subsection
2.3
, we have:

\mathcal{B}_{\pm\varepsilon_{1}}^{k,n}\bigl(\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}}\bigr)\neq\emptyset
.
Applying Proposition
4.4

with perturbation error
\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}=1/(18k)
and block error
\varepsilon_{1}=1/18
, conclude that
\mathcal{D}
contains a
2
-distorted copy of the Hamming cube
\{0,1\}^{k}
of dimension
k\gtrsim 1/\sqrt{\gamma}
and the proof is complete.
∎

9.
Discussion on related remarks

The
density Hales-Jewett theorem
of Furstenberg-Katznelson
[
18
]
states that for every finite alphabet
\mathcal{A}
and
\delta>0
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that every subset
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{A}^{N}
of density
\mu(\mathcal{D}):=|\mathcal{D}|/|\mathcal{A}|^{N}>\delta
contains a combinatorial line.
A
combinatorial line
is given via
\{l(a):a\in\mathcal{A}\}
where
l
is a
variable word
of length
N
, i.e.
l\in(\mathcal{A}\cup\{v\})^{N}
where
v
is an extra letter which appears at least once in
l
, and
l(i)\in\mathcal{A}^{N}
is the word obtained by substituting
i
in place of the extra letter
v
.

The
multi-dimensional density Hales Jewett theorem
(which follows from the density Hales Jewett theorem) extracts from dense subsets of
\mathcal{A}^{N}
a
combinatorial subspace
which is given by substituting a variable word in multiple variable letters
v_{1},...,v_{k}
(where each variable letter appears at least once).
Observe that combinatorial subspaces do not give metric embeddings
f:\mathcal{A}^{k}\to\mathcal{A}^{N}
because one variable letter in
v_{1},...,v_{k}
might have a large number of frequencies in
l
while another variable letter might have a small number of frequencies in
l
. See the monograph
[
11
]
and the survey
[
12
]
for more on the density Hales-Jewett theorem, its variations, and its history.

Remark 9.1
.

From the density Hales–Jewett theorem,

it follows easily that
Emb(1,\delta,k)<\infty
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\delta<1
.

Proof.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\delta<1
and
N
which will be chosen later.
Let
\mathcal{D}\subset\{0,1\}^{N}
be a subset of density
\mu(\mathcal{D})\geqslant\delta
.
We break a point
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
into
N/k
-many blocks of size
k
, meaning that we view
\mathcal{D}\subset\{0,1\}^{N}=\mathcal{A}^{N/k}
where our alphabet is
\mathcal{A}:=\{0,1\}^{k}
.
Clearly,
\mathcal{D}
is a subset of density
\delta
and
|\mathcal{A}|=2^{k}
.
Let
N^{\prime}
be the dimension from the density Hales–Jewett theorem and choose
N:=kN^{\prime}
.
By the density Hales–Jewett theorem there exists a 1-variable word
l\in(\mathcal{A}\cup\{v\})^{N}
such that
l(\alpha)\in\mathcal{D}
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}^{k}
.
Observe
f:\alpha\mapsto l(\alpha)
gives us a rescaled isometric embedding into
\mathcal{D}
and the rescaling
r
is equal to frequency of the variable letter
v
in the variable word
l
.
∎
