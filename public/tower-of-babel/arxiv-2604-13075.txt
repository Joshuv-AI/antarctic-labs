DeEscalWild: A Real-World Benchmark for Automated De-Escalation Training with SLMs
Md Hasebul Hasan, Krity Haque Charu, Eshwara Prasad Sridhar, Shuchisnigdha Deb, Mohammad A. Islam

Abstract
Effective de-escalation is critical for law enforcement safety and community trust, yet traditional training methods lack scalability and realism. While Large Language Models (LLMs) enable dynamic, open-ended simulations, their substantial computational footprint renders them impractical for deployment on the lightweight, portable hardware required for immersive field training. Small Language Models (SLMs) offer a viable real-time alternative but suffer from a critical scarcity of high-quality, domain-specific training data. To bridge this gap, we present DeEscalWild , a novel benchmark dataset curated from a multi-stage pipeline of “in-the-wild” police-civilian interactions extracted from open-source video repositories. Starting with 5,000 raw inputs, we employed a rigorous hybrid filtering process—combining human-in-the-loop verification with “LLM-as-a-Judge” evaluation—to distill 1,500 high-fidelity scenarios. The resulting corpus comprises 285,887 dialogue turns, totaling approximately 4.7 million tokens. Extensive experiments demonstrate that SLMs fine-tuned on this data significantly outperform their base counterparts across ROUGE-L, BLEU-4, METEOR, and BERTScore metrics. Notably, our fine-tuned Qwen 2.5 (3B-Instruct) surpasses the general-purpose Gemini 2.5 Flash model, demonstrating that domain-optimized SLMs can achieve superior performance with a fraction of the computational cost. This work establishes the foundational infrastructure for accessible, low-latency, and privacy-preserving officer training systems at the edge.

Introduction

Effective de-escalation is a cornerstone of modern policing, directly influencing officer safety, subject welfare, and public trust. While the ability to navigate volatile encounters is a critical skill, traditional training paradigms—such as static role-playing or branching video scenarios—suffer from inherent limitations in scalability, consistency, and realism.

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) offer a promising avenue for creating dynamic, open-ended training simulations. However, the computational footprint of state-of-the-art LLMs renders them impractical for deployment on the lightweight, portable hardware (e.g., standalone VR headsets or mobile edge devices) required for immersive field training. To achieve real-time latency without tethered compute, the industry must pivot toward Small Language Models (SLMs). Yet, while SLMs offer inference efficiency, they lack the broad reasoning capabilities of their larger counterparts and require extensive fine-tuning to perform reliably in high-stakes contexts. This creates a critical deadlock: the need for specialized SLMs for de-escalation is urgent, but high-quality, domain-specific training data for this task is virtually non-existent. Figure

illustrates how a specialized SLM core can be integrated to drive a low-latency, closed-loop de-escalation training system.

Current computational approaches have yet to fully resolve these constraints. Exploratory studies, such as
(
Anand & Polyak 2024
)
, assess off-the-shelf models like ChatGPT, finding that while general-purpose LLMs can simulate basic empathetic exchanges, they remain constrained by the latency and connectivity requirements of API-based architectures. Furthermore, reliance on closed-source commercial models precludes the domain-specific fine-tuning necessary to capture the nuance of tactical communication. Similarly, prototypes like the
Adaptive De-escalation Trainer

(
Sridhar et al. 2025
)
demonstrate semantic capability but fail to meet operational constraints; with reported latencies exceeding 4 seconds and a reliance on server-grade compute, such systems are ill-suited for the split-second decision-making required in the field.

The challenge is further compounded by a lack of suitable training data. While the application of NLP to law enforcement is not without precedent—
(
Voigt et al. 2017
)
, for instance, utilized body-camera footage to analyze racial disparities in officer language—such works treat police dialogue purely as archival evidence for post-hoc sociological analysis rather than as a substrate for active generative training. A critical gap remains: the field lacks a standardized, high-volume corpus focused on
de-escalation scenarios
—the specific verbal strategies used to resolve conflict.

To bridge this gap, we introduce
DeEscalWild
, the first large-scale dataset derived from “in-the-wild” police-civilian interactions. Unlike synthetic or crowdsourced datasets, which often lack emotional fidelity, we constructed our corpus by mining open-source video repositories (e.g., YouTube, TikTok) to capture the raw, unstructured nature of real-world conflict.

Our contributions are twofold:

1.

The DeEscalWild Benchmark:
We present a rigorously curated dataset constructed from a pipeline of 5,000 raw videos. Through a hybrid filtration process involving human-in-the-loop verification and “LLM-as-a-Judge” filtering, we distilled 1,500 high-quality scenarios comprising 285,887 dialogue turns and approximately 4.7 million tokens. We further introduce a scalable, platform-agnostic pipeline for speaker diarization and script extraction that can be generalized to other domain-specific data collection tasks.

2.

SLM Efficacy at the Edge:
We demonstrate that domain-specific fine-tuning allows compact models to achieve parameter-efficient performance comparable to significantly larger models. Our experiments show that a fine-tuned Qwen 2.5 (3B) significantly outperforms the general-purpose Gemini 2.5 Flash model across ROUGE-L, METEOR, and BERTScore metrics, proving that data quality is a viable substitute for parameter scale in specialized tasks.

2
Related Work

Generative Simulation and Role Consistency.

Scalable law enforcement training requires simulators that balance realism with adaptability. Traditional rule-based systems are rigid and susceptible to “gaming,” failing to capture the nuance of real-world discourse. While LLMs enable open-ended generation
(
Gao et al. 2024
)
, crisis simulation demands deep role consistency—agents must maintain a coherent persona (e.g., non-compliance, fear) under pressure without drifting into casual chitchat. Generative agent frameworks have addressed this via memory retrieval
(
Park et al. 2023
)
and persona-conditioned alignment
(
Wang et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
. Furthermore, adaptivity is critical;
Violakis 2025
and
Sridhar et al. 2025
demonstrate that effective trainers must modulate not just content but emotional tone in real-time, validating the move toward dynamic, non-scripted architectures.

Efficient Deployment via Small Language Models (SLMs).

Despite the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, their computational demands preclude deployment on portable police hardware. Recent work validates the efficacy of specialized SLMs for such constraints.
Pecher et al. 2025
demonstrate that specialized SLMs can outperform general LLMs with as few as 100 labeled samples—a critical finding for data-scarce domains. Additionally,
Xu et al. 2024
show that fine-tuned SLMs can serve as effective “plug-ins” for larger frameworks, decoupling supervision from context window limits. Our work leverages these insights to deploy high-performance reasoning on edge devices.

Data Scarcity in High-Stakes Domains.

Progress in de-escalation modeling is bottlenecked by a lack of ecologically valid data. Existing goal-oriented datasets focus on bargaining or customer service (e.g., price negotiation), where the objective is agreement rather than stabilization
(
Zhan et al. 2024
)
. While datasets like
EmpatheticDialogues
offer emotional breadth, they lack the specific protocols of threat assessment. Although body-worn camera footage contains the necessary escalation signals
(
Srbinovska et al. 2025
)
, privacy and volume constraints have hindered the creation of public benchmarks
(
Rosas-Smith et al. 2025
)
. To our knowledge,
DeEscalWild
is the first large-scale benchmark curated from “in-the-wild” footage, providing the necessary ecological validity to train robust, real-time tactical agents.

3.1
Design Principles

The construction of
DeEscalWild
is guided by five core principles aimed at addressing the unique challenges of training agents for high-stakes, socially sensitive negotiations.

Reasoning-Centric De-escalation.
Unlike standard chitchat or task-oriented dialogue systems, de-escalation requires deep strategic reasoning. We designed
DeEscalWild
to capture the causal relationship between an officer’s verbal strategy and the subject’s emotional trajectory. The dataset prioritizes interactions that require the model to infer latent mental states, anticipate potential escalation triggers, and select communicative actions that actively lower tension levels rather than simply maintaining flow.

Ecological Validity.
To ensure the model generalizes to high-pressure environments,
DeEscalWild
is strictly derived from real-world, “in-the-wild” law enforcement recordings (e.g., body-worn camera footage). This captures the raw, unscripted nature of crisis intervention—including speech disfluencies, emotional outbursts, and non-cooperative behavior—which are rarely present in crowdsourced or synthetic datasets. This principle ensures the data reflects true distribution shifts found in deployment scenarios involving victims, suspects, and civilians.

Sociodemographic and Situational Diversity.

To mitigate algorithmic bias and ensure robust performance across populations,
DeEscalWild
explicitly maximizes diversity across two axes: participant demographics (spanning various ages, ethnicities, and regional dialects) and incident topology.
The dataset covers a broad spectrum of interaction types, ranging from routine traffic stops to mental health crises, ensuring the model learns to adapt its de-escalation strategies to diverse cultural contexts and severity levels (see Appendix
C
for detailed demographic breakdowns).

Temporal Depth and Long-Horizon Context.
De-escalation is a gradual process, not an instantaneous event.
DeEscalWild
focuses on long-context conversations (average duration: 12 minutes) to facilitate the training of models capable of long-horizon state tracking. This allows the system to maintain coherence over hundreds of turns, recognize slowly developing emotional shifts, and execute multi-step persuasion strategies that would be impossible to learn from short-context datasets.

Multi-Agent and Environmental Complexity.
Real-world interventions rarely occur in a sterile vacuum.
DeEscalWild
includes complex, multi-party scenarios that move beyond simple dyadic (one-on-one) interactions. The dataset features incidents in public spaces involving multiple officers and multiple civilians, requiring the model to handle speaker diarization challenges, dynamic turn-taking, and the management of interference or “cross-talk” typical of chaotic scenes.

3.2
Data Collection Pipeline

To construct a dataset for effective de-escalation training, we curated real-world, naturalistic videos depicting police-civilian interactions during potential conflicts. We ensured the dataset captured a diverse range of contexts by including instances of both escalation and de-escalation.

As illustrated in Figure
2
, the data curation workflow operates as a multi-stage pipeline. Data collection was initiated by identifying relevant content creators across YouTube, TikTok, and Facebook, resulting in the selection of approximately 15, 5, and 3 channels (see Table
9
for sources) from each platform, respectively, and the acquisition of metadata for roughly 5,000 videos. Subsequently, we generated raw transcripts using the Whisper
(
Radford et al. 2023
)
model. To ensure domain relevance, we employed a hybrid filtering pipeline leveraging a Large Language Model (LLM) with human oversight, as detailed in Appendix
B
. This process produced a curated subset of 1,500 videos. We validated the alignment of these videos with our domain criteria through manual inspection of a random sample (
N=100
). Finally, the approved videos were processed using Gemini-2.5-Flash
(
Comanici et al. 2025
)
for advanced conversation extraction and speaker diarization. A final validation step of 100 randomly sampled scripts confirmed the fidelity of the diarization, resulting in the final
DeEscalWild
benchmark.

3.3
Tasks

Contextual Response Generation.

The primary task of the
DeEscalWild
benchmark is
Civilian Response Generation
in a high-stakes context. Given a dialogue history
H
and the current officer utterance, the model must generate the likely civilian response
R
. Unlike standard chitchat,
R
serves as a realistic simulation of a subject in crisis, reacting to the officer’s de-escalation attempts. Consequently, the generation must reflect the semantic and emotional trajectory of the interaction—ranging from compliance to aggression—based on the effectiveness of the officer’s input. Success is measured by the model’s ability to mirror the authentic, variable nature of civilian reactions found in our ground-truth transcripts.

De-escalation Strategy Alignment.

Beyond simple text generation, models are evaluated on their ability to maintain
Strategic Consistency
. This involves two sub-tasks:

•

Safety Compliance:
The model must avoid escalation triggers (e.g., insults, aggressive posturing) that would endanger the officer or civilian.

•

Turn-Level Reasoning:
The model must demonstrate “long-horizon state tracking”, correctly recalling details from earlier in the interaction (e.g., the subject’s name or stated grievance) to build trust over the course of the interaction.

Multimodal Context Integration.

While the baseline evaluation focuses on text, the dataset design supports
Multimodal Fusion
. As described in our architecture (Figure
1
), the Perception Layer provides visual context tags (e.g., emotional state, body posture) alongside the audio transcript. In this task, the model must condition its textual response on these non-verbal cues, distinguishing, for example, between a subject who is shouting out of fear versus one shouting out of aggression.

3.4
Quality Control and Human Validation

To ensure the high fidelity required for training professional safety agents, we implemented a rigorous human-in-the-loop verification protocol at two key stages of the pipeline.

•

Video Relevance and Quality:
Following the hybrid filtering stage, we manually inspected a random sample (
N=100
) of the retained videos to verify that they contained audible, non-corrupted audio and relevant law enforcement scenarios.

•

Diarization and Transcription Accuracy:
After processing with Gemini-2.5-Flash, we performed a validation on a random sample of final scripts (
N=100
). To quantify dataset quality, we conducted a granular error analysis on this sample (approx. 19,000 dialogue turns). We identified 210 total discrepancies, yielding an estimated
Word Error Rate (WER) of
<0.1\%
and a
Diarization Error Rate (DER) of
0.16\%
. The error distribution was primarily driven by minor mistranscriptions (
76\%
), while speaker attribution errors (
14\%
) and missing speech (
5\%
) were negligible. Notably,
50\%
of the analyzed videos were completely error-free, substantiating the model’s robustness in handling “in-the-wild” acoustic conditions.

This two-stage verification ensures that the final dataset of 1,500 scripts accurately reflects the complex, multi-party dynamics of real-world de-escalation, minimizing the risk of training models on hallucinated or misattributed dialogue.

4
Experiments

In this section, we present a comprehensive evaluation of the
DeEscalWild
dataset’s utility for training specialized de-escalation agents. We assess the impact of domain-specific fine-tuning across a diverse set of state-of-the-art open-weights Small Language Models (SLMs).

4.1
Experimental Protocol and Setup

Evaluation Strategy.
To quantify the effectiveness of
DeEscalWild
dataset, we employ a pre-post fine-tuning evaluation protocol. We measure the performance of base instruct-tuned small models (Zero-Shot) against their fine-tuned counterparts on a held-out test set. This comparative analysis isolates the specific value added by the
DeEscalWild
dataset in teaching de-escalation strategies, reasoning, and policy adherence. Additionally, we benchmark the fine-tuned small models against Gemini 2.5 Flash, a state-of-the-art Large Language Model (LLM). By utilizing a few-shot prompting strategy, we evaluate whether domain-specific fine-tuning enables small models to outperform prompt-engineered large models.

Data Splitting.
We partition the
DeEscalWild
dataset into training (93%), validation (2%), and testing (5%) subsets. Crucially, this partitioning is performed at the scenario level to strictly enforce data isolation, ensuring no turns from test conversations appear in the training corpus. This held-out test set serves as the primary benchmark for evaluating and comparing performance across all model architectures.

Implementation Details.
All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU (24GB VRAM). To ensure computational efficiency, we employed 4-bit Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA). The LoRA adapters were configured with a rank
r=16
, a scaling factor
\alpha=32
, and a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-4}
. Models were fine-tuned for 3 epochs with a global batch size of 4, utilizing the AdamW optimizer.

Evaluation Metrics.
To ensure a robust assessment of response quality, we utilize four complementary metrics. Given the open-ended nature of dialogue generation, we interpret these scores specifically within the context of conversational coherence rather than strict translation accuracy. For readability, ROUGE-L, BLEU-4, and METEOR are reported on a scale of 0–100, while BERTScore is reported in its standard 0–1 range.

1.

ROUGE-L
(
Lin 2004
)
:
Evaluates structural coherence. We consider scores exceeding
15.0
as indicative of acceptable content recall, accounting for the variable length of conversational responses.

2.

BLEU-4
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
:
Measures exact n-gram precision. Given the one-to-many nature of open-ended dialogue, scores are intrinsically lower than in translation; we interpret the
2.0–5.0
range as successful domain adaptation.

3.

METEOR
(
Banerjee & Lavie 2005
)
:
Accounts for synonyms and paraphrasing, making it crucial for colloquial dialogue where distinct phrases (e.g., “Step back” vs. “Move away”) share intent. Scores above
15.0
signify preserved communicative intent.

4.

BERTScore (F1)
(
Zhang* et al. 2020
)
:
Our primary semantic validator, utilizing contextual embeddings to judge meaning rather than exact overlaps. Scores consistently above
0.85
demonstrate that the model maintains thematic consistency with the ground truth.

We present a comprehensive evaluation of our fine-tuning methodology, analyzing performance across three critical dimensions: (1) training stability and convergence, (2) the quantitative impact of domain adaptation, and (3) the comparative efficacy of specialized Small Language Models (SLMs) versus generalist Large Language Models (LLMs).

Training Dynamics.

Figure
3
illustrates the training and validation loss trajectories for all five architectures. We observe highly stable convergence, characterized by a rapid initial descent in the first epoch followed by asymptotic stabilization. Notably, the divergence between training (blue) and validation (red) loss remains negligible throughout the fine-tuning process. This stability is attributed to our QLoRA configuration (
r=16,\alpha=32
). The scaling factor
\frac{\alpha}{r}=2
ensures that the adapter weights receive sufficient gradient updates to learn the target distribution without destabilizing the frozen base parameters. This confirms that 4-bit quantization combined with low-rank adaptation is a robust strategy for aligning SLMs to high-entropy dialogue domains.

Impact of Domain Adaptation.

Table

Specialized SLMs vs. Generalist LLMs.

To test the hypothesis that data quality can serve as a substitute for parameter scale, we benchmarked our best-performing SLMs against
Gemini 2.5 Flash
, a state-of-the-art general-purpose LLM. To ensure a fair comparison, the Gemini baseline was optimized using a
few-shot prompting strategy
(see in Figure
7
) designed to mitigate safety filters and encourage role-play. Despite these optimizations, the results in Table
4
reveal a clear advantage for the specialized models. Our fine-tuned
Qwen 2.5 (3B)
outperforms the Gemini baseline across all four quality metrics, most notably surpassing it by
+6.3 points in METEOR
. This counter-intuitive result highlights the limitations of prompt engineering for high-stakes simulation; while Gemini’s few-shot adaptation captured the
semantics
of the interaction (reflected in a competitive BERTScore), it struggled to capture the
stylistic
nuance of the domain.

The qualitative gap stems from the fundamental difference in training objectives. Generalist models, trained to average over the internet’s text distribution, often suffer from a “regression to the mean,” producing generic, overly verbose, and safety-aligned responses that break immersion. In contrast, the fine-tuned SLM is beneficially overfitted to the jagged, reactive rhythm of real-world conflict. This allows the smaller models to generate responses that are not only statistically closer to the reference data but also functionally superior for simulation purposes, maintaining the appropriate tone and brevity required for the task.

Finally, the specialized SLMs demonstrate a significant advantage in computational efficiency, a critical factor for real-time deployment. The Gemini 2.5 Flash baseline exhibits a high average response latency of 3.50 seconds. Conversely, all fine-tuned small models achieve sub-second latency. While
Granite 3.0
proves to be the most efficient model with the lowest latency of
0.30 seconds
, the top-performing
Qwen 2.5
strikes the optimal balance. Although slightly slower than Granite at 0.38 seconds, Qwen remains nearly an order of magnitude faster than the Gemini baseline while delivering superior generation quality.

. While the base model remains overly formal and robotic, our fine-tuned model successfully mimics the specific syntactic style (slang, contractions) and emotional tone of an escalated suspect, matching the quality of the proprietary baseline.

5
Data Pipeline Challenges and Optimization

Metadata Acquisition and Rate Limiting.

During metadata extraction from YouTube, TikTok, and Facebook, our automated requests were frequently blocked after retrieving only 40 to 50 items due to aggressive platform rate-limiting and anti-scraping mechanisms. We resolved this issue by implementing a robust rotating proxy network, which allowed us to successfully bypass these restrictions and retrieve metadata for the full dataset of 5,000 videos.

Diarization and Model Selection.

We initially observed sub-optimal performance with
pyannote

(
Bredin 2023
)
for speaker diarization, which yielded scripts characterized by missing information and incorrect speaker attribution. To address this cost-effectively, we initially devised a refinement strategy utilizing a secondary Large Language Model (LLM) to reconcile high-quality raw transcripts from OpenAI Whisper with the noisy diarization outputs from
pyannote
.

We first evaluated the open-source
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
. While its input context window (128k tokens) is sufficient for ingestion, its maximum output generation limit is restricted to 8,192 tokens. This proved to be a critical bottleneck; our videos average 12 minutes in duration, and the required structured output scripts often approach 20,000 tokens, exceeding the model’s generation capacity. Deploying larger open-source models was ruled out due to prohibitive computational costs. Furthermore, this three-stage pipeline (Whisper + Pyannote + LLM Refinement) proved computationally inefficient, requiring excessive processing time to generate a single script per video.

Consequently, we adopted
Gemini-2.5-Flash
. This model offers native multimodal capabilities, allowing it to directly process audio extracted from the videos without intermediate transcription steps. It produced high-quality speaker diarization with near-zero errors. Furthermore, it is highly cost-effective; processing the full dataset cost approximately $120 USD. Given its reliability and efficiency, we subsequently utilized
Gemini-2.5-Flash
as a benchmark to evaluate the performance of other small language models.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced
DeEscalWild
, the first large-scale benchmark dataset derived exclusively from “in-the-wild” police-civilian interactions, designed to bridge the critical gap between theoretical de-escalation protocols and the chaotic reality of field encounters. By curating 1,500 real-world scenarios containing over 285,000 dialogue turns, we have provided the research community with a foundational resource for training agents that can navigate high-stakes, emotionally volatile conflicts with ecological validity.

Our experimental results fundamentally validate the potential of Small Language Models (SLMs) as the engine for the next generation of immersive training tools. We demonstrated that a fine-tuned 3B-parameter model (Qwen 2.5) could not only match but significantly outperform a state-of-the-art generalist commercial model (Gemini 2.5 Flash) in domain-specific metrics such as METEOR and safety compliance. This finding challenges the prevailing “bigger is better” orthodoxy, proving that high-quality, domain-specific data is a viable substitute for parameter scale.

Ultimately, this work lays the groundwork for privacy-preserving, low-latency de-escalation trainers that can run entirely on edge devices like VR headsets, without relying on cloud connectivity. By democratizing access to high-fidelity simulation training, DeEscalWild aims to contribute directly to improved officer decision-making, reduced use-of-force incidents, and stronger community trust. Future work will focus on expanding the multimodal capabilities of these agents to interpret non-verbal cues such as body language and tone, further closing the loop on realistic human-AI crisis simulation.

Appendix B
Video Filtering Pipeline

We employ an LLM-based pipeline with human oversight to filter a domain-specific set of 1,500 videos from an initial pool of 5,000 raw YouTube, TikTok, and Facebook videos. This process combines unsupervised clustering, feature selection, Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning, and deterministic rule-based filtering based on the selected features.

Phase 1: Transcription and Feature Discovery

Step 1: Transcription.
We generated automatic speech recognition (ASR) transcripts for all 5,000 initially selected videos using the OpenAI Whisper model.

Step 2: Clustering and Schema Definition.
To define the relevant feature space without manual annotation, we embedded the raw transcripts and applied HDBSCAN clustering. This analysis revealed three distinct content clusters. Based on the centroids of these clusters, we derived a taxonomy of 30 binary features required to identify high-value de-escalation scenarios.

The resulting feature schema
F
is organized into five categories:

1.

Police Presence
(
S_{police}
): Indicators of official law enforcement involvement.

police_identified_by_role, police_commands_present, id_request_event, legal_procedure_language, police_explaining_actions, civilian_questioning_police

2.

Interaction Type
(
S_{interact}
): The nature of the dialogue.

conversation_present, verbal_disagreement_detected, compliance_discussion, instruction_clarification_dialogue, negotiation_attempts, conflict_resolution_attempt

3.

Escalation
(
S_{esc}
): Signals of rising tension or conflict.

raised_threat_language, noncompliance_resistance_language, accusatory_language, police_warning_language, emotional_intensity_spike, crowd_escalation_factors, use_of_force_transition_signals

4.

De-escalation
(
S_{deesc}
): Signals of conflict mitigation.

calming_language_used_by_officer, empathetic_statements, clear_explanations_given, tone_softening_cues, conflict_deescalation_success, agreement_reached

5.

Context Filters
(
S_{noise}
): Exclusion criteria.

no_police_presence, no_conversation_detected, advertisement_content_detected, training_range_context, non_relevant_crime_only_context

Phase 3: Rule-Based Filtering Logic

Step 4: Filtering Criteria.
Let
c(S)
denote the count of active features (sum of binary values) within a specific category
S
. A video
v
is considered relevant and retained in the dataset if and only if it satisfies the composite condition
C_{valid}
:

C_{valid}(v)=\underbrace{(c(S_{noise})=0)}_{\text{Context}}\land\underbrace{(c(S_{police})\geq 2)}_{\text{Relevance}}\land\underbrace{\left(c(S_{esc})\geq 3\lor c(S_{deesc})\geq 3\right)}_{\text{Intensity}}

(1)

Where:

•

Context:
Ensures the video is not an advertisement or irrelevant context.

•

Relevance:
Requires at least two distinct signals confirming active police participation.

•

Intensity:
Requires the interaction to have significant depth in either escalation or de-escalation dynamics.

Phase 4: Validation

Step 5: Human Verification.
To validate the precision of this pipeline, we performed manual review on a random sample of
N=100
videos from the final filtered set of 1,500. The manual review confirmed that the retrieval logic successfully aligned with the intended de-escalation use case, effectively filtering out noise while preserving high-intensity interactions.
