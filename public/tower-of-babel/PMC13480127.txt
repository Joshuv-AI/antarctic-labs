Case Presentation

A 32-year-old man was referred to a pulmonary hypertension (PH) center for progressive exertional dyspnea over 10 months (World Health Organization functional class [WHO FC] III). He had no past medical history, family history of cardiopulmonary disease, or exposure to anorexigens, illicit drugs, or toxins. Physical examination revealed no signs of congestion or hypoperfusion. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with right axis deviation, right bundle branch block, and right ventricular (RV) strain. Laboratory testing demonstrated normal blood counts and renal and hepatic function; results of autoimmune and viral screening and serologies for schistosomiasis were negative. Question 1: What is the leading diagnosis and which investigations should be performed?

In this young patient with no relevant past medical or family history, the presence of progressive exertional dyspnea over several months, together with elevated NT-proBNP levels, raises initial concern for heart failure. The electrocardiographic findings further suggest predominant RV involvement. Taken together, these findings strongly raise the suspicion of PH. In this context, transthoracic echocardiography represents the first-line investigation to assess the probability of PH, evaluate RV function, and identify potential underlying etiologies (ie, left heart disease and congenital heart disease [CHD]). Further investigations are required to exclude specific causes, including pulmonary, thromboembolic, and systemic conditions, as well as perform risk stratification. Diagnostic confirmation and hemodynamic phenotyping should be performed through right heart catheterization (RHC), which remains the gold standard.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed marked right-sided chamber dilatation with RV hypertrophy and preserved systolic function (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion [TAPSE] 23 mm; tricuspid S′ 13 cm/s) (Figure 2). The left ventricle exhibited D-shaped remodeling with preserved systolic function. Moderate functional tricuspid regurgitation was present, with a peak velocity of 4.8 m/s and severely impaired ventriculoarterial coupling (TAPSE/systolic pulmonary artery [PA] pressure [PAP] ratio 0.21). A large secundum atrial septal defect (ASD) and dilated PA were identified. Question 2: Should genetic testing be performed in this setting?

Recent recommendations from the PH Task Force, presented at the 7th World Symposium on PH, support consideration of genetic testing in patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), including those associated with CHD.1 This approach may identify pathogenic variants—such as in BMPR2, SOX17, or TBX4—with potential implications for disease classification, prognosis, and family screening.1

In this context, genetic testing in the present case can be considered appropriate as part of a comprehensive etiological work-up for CHD-associated PH.

Beyond current recommendations, this case also raises broader pathophysiological considerations. It remains incompletely understood why some patients with CHD develop significant pulmonary vascular disease (PVD), whereas others with comparable shunt lesions do not progress to advanced PH. Genetic susceptibility may represent an important modifying factor in this variability.

In our patient, a heterozygous variant of uncertain significance in EIF2AK4 was identified. Although biallelic pathogenic EIF2AK4 variants are classically linked to pulmonary veno-occlusive disease, the clinical relevance of isolated heterozygous variants remains uncertain. Nevertheless, one may hypothesize that such variants could contribute to an exaggerated pulmonary vascular response in susceptible individuals.

Notably, the degree of vascular remodeling and elevation in PVR observed in this case appeared disproportionate to the magnitude of the shunt, raising the possibility that host-specific factors—including incompletely characterized genetic modifiers—may influence individual vulnerability to PVD in CHD.

Although speculative, this perspective underscores the biological heterogeneity underlying CHD-associated PAH and highlights the potential future role of genetics in refining risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making.

Question 3: What is the most appropriate next step in management?

The patient presents with a secundum ASD associated with a significant left-to-right shunt, resulting in RV volume overload and pulmonary overcirculation. Under usual circumstances, the definitive treatment would consist of surgical or percutaneous closure of the defect. However, this case is complicated by the presence of severe precapillary PH with markedly elevated PVR, which can ultimately limit—or even contraindicate—defect closure.

Current European and American guidelines recommend ASD closure in the presence of a significant shunt (Qp:Qs ≥1.5) when PVR is low or deemed reversible.2,3 However, patients with precapillary PH and persistently elevated PVR (>5 WU), as in this case, represent a high-risk group in whom immediate closure may be hazardous, as abrupt elimination of the interatrial shunt may precipitate acute RV failure due to sudden loss of the “pop-off” mechanism and worsening ventriculoarterial uncoupling. Therefore, the most appropriate next step is to initiate PAH-targeted therapy, with the goal of reducing PVR and improving RV-PA coupling, and reassessing operability afterward.

The patient was started on upfront dual PAH-targeted therapy with tadalafil and bosentan. After 4 months, clinical status improved to WHO FC II, and the NT-proBNP level decreased to 898 pg/mL. Repeat RHC showed objective hemodynamic improvement, although PVR remained elevated at 6.39 WU, leaving the patient in the operability “gray zone” (Figure 1).

Question 4: Should ASD closure be pursued in the presence of persistently elevated PVR despite targeted therapy?

Despite clinical and biomarker improvement with dual PAH therapy, the patient continues to exhibit elevated PVR, rendering complete ASD closure high risk. The decision to pursue ASD closure requires careful balancing of competing physiological considerations. On one hand, closure may relieve RV overload and prevent long-term complications related to persistent left-to-right shunting. On the other hand, acute RV failure may occur due to a sudden increase in afterload, particularly in the presence of residual PVD. This clinical scenario reflects a growing population of patients who do not fit neatly within existing guideline frameworks. European guidelines generally advise against closure when PVR remains >5 WU despite medical therapy,2 whereas recent American guidelines acknowledge greater uncertainty, assigning a class IIb recommendation for closure in patients with PVR between 5 and 8 WU, in the absence of Eisenmenger physiology.3 Given the absence of definitive evidence and the presence of differing recommendations, management should remain individualized and guided by a comprehensive, multidisciplinary evaluation integrating hemodynamics, imaging findings, clinical trajectory, and treatment response, in experienced centers.

Accordingly, this case was discussed within a multidisciplinary team including specialists in adult congenital heart disease, PH, and cardiac surgery. After weighing risks and benefits, a “treat-to-repair” strategy was considered appropriate. Factors favoring repair included the patient's young age, large defect size, significant shunt burden, absence of resting cyanosis or Eisenmenger physiology, normal PCWP, and objective response to PAH therapy. Factors arguing against complete closure were the persistently elevated PVR and concern for fixed pulmonary vascular remodeling with postoperative RV afterload mismatch.

Question 5: How should repair be performed in the presence of significant PVD?

Because complete closure remained potentially hazardous, while continued long-term exposure to a large left-to-right shunt was also undesirable, fenestrated surgical closure was selected as a compromise strategy within a “treat-to-repair” approach. Given the very large defect dimensions and the need for a controlled fenestrated repair, surgery was favored over percutaneous closure.

Fenestrated/valved patch closure offers several theoretical advantages in this context. By preserving a controlled interatrial communication, it maintains the “pop-off” mechanism that allows right-to-left decompression during periods of increased pulmonary vascular load, thereby protecting the RV from acute afterload mismatch. This strategy allows transition from chronic right ventricular volume overload to a more controlled afterload adaptation, rather than exposing the RV to an abrupt hemodynamic shift.

Although evidence remains limited to observational series, available data support fenestrated or valved closure as a reasonable option in carefully selected patients after targeted therapy and expert reassessment.4, 5, 6, 7 Although these data are limited by small sample size and short follow-up, they collectively suggest that PVD may retain partial reversibility in selected patients when appropriately unloaded and pharmacologically treated.

Taken together, these considerations support fenestrated closure as a pragmatic and physiologically sound strategy in patients within the operability gray zone, allowing a controlled transition from volume overload to increased afterload while preserving RV safety.

Therefore, a bovine valved patch closure was selected to allow controlled interatrial decompression in the event of acute RV afterload mismatch (Figure 3). The procedure was performed successfully, and the immediate postoperative course was uncomplicated. Dual PAH-targeted therapy was promptly resumed after surgery.

At 14 days, echocardiography showed transient worsening of RV systolic indices and RV-pulmonary arterial coupling, likely reflecting the anticipated early afterload adaptation phase after shunt restriction (Figure 2). At 6-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated marked clinical and hemodynamic improvement, reaching WHO FC I with normalization of NT-proBNP (66 pg/mL). RV function and RV-PA coupling recovered (TAPSE 20 mm; estimated systolic PAP 37 mm Hg; TAPSE/systolic PAP 0.54) (Figure 2). Follow-up RHC confirmed substantial improvement in pulmonary hemodynamics, with a mean PAP of 34 mm Hg, PCWP of 14 mm Hg, right atrial pressure of 6 mm Hg, cardiac index of 3.07 L/min/m2, and PVR of 3.5 WU, with no residual shunt (Qp:Qs 1.01) (Figure 1).

This case should not be interpreted as support for liberal closure in ASD-associated PAH, but rather as an example of physiology-guided repair in a highly selected patient.

Conclusions

This case illustrates that operability in ASD-associated PH cannot be reduced to a single PVR threshold. In carefully selected patients with substantial shunt burden, objective response to targeted therapy, and no Eisenmenger physiology, a treat-to-repair strategy with fenestrated closure may provide a physiology-guided path between 2 undesirable extremes: leaving a large shunt untreated and performing abrupt complete closure in the presence of residual PVD. This case should not be interpreted as support for liberal closure in ASD-associated PH, but rather as an example of physiology-guided repair in a highly selected patient. Such decisions should remain restricted to experienced multidisciplinary centers.
