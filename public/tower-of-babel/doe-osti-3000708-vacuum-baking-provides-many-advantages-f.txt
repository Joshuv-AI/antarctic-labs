 Vacuum-baking provides many advantages for LiH

 S. M. Matt, C. K. Saw, W. McLean, L. N. Dinh
 Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory
 Livermore, CA 94550, USA

SUMMARY
 In vacuum applications, the thermal decomposition of LiOH corrosion on LiH causes
undesirable hydrogen outgassing. The quantity of outgassing is dependent on the thermal stability
of the corrosion layer, which depends on sample treatment. We have measured the decomposition
of LiOH on as-polished LiH and on previously vacuum-baked LiH (i.e., Li2O-covered LiH) which
both experienced moisture re-exposure. The decomposition was performed in ~ 10-6 Torr vacuum
at 343-363 K over many days and was monitored by diffuse-reflectance infrared Fourier transform
(DRIFT) spectroscopy. The results show that the thermal stability of the LiOH formed on
previously vacuum-baked LiH is better than that of LiOH grown on as-polished LiH. Li2O-covered
LiH (i.e., vacuum-baked) delays the onset of hydrogen outgassing until all Li2O is converted to
LiOH (according to Li2O + H2O → 2 LiOH), has a thinner LiOH corrosion layer and better thermal
stability (most likely due to improved crystallinity with prolonged high temperature baking) than
as-polished LiH. The combination of these factors confirms that high-temperature prolonged
vacuum baking of LiH reduces unwanted hydrogen outgassing in vacuum/sealed applications.

INTRODUCTION
 To avoid unwanted hydrogen outgassing due to the thermal decomposition of LiOH grown
on LiH, high temperature vacuum baking can be used to convert LiOH-covered LiH to Li2O-
covered LiH. LiOH grown on as-polished LiH has the main DRIFT signal at ~ 3675 cm-1. After
high temperature vacuum baking followed by limited moisture re-exposure an additional LiOH
signature may become present at lower wavenumber (~ 3528 cm-1). Lower wavenumbers
correspond to lower binding energies and less thermal stability than the main peak at 3675 cm-1.

 This 3528 cm-1 DRIFT peak of LiOH is thought to arise from LiOH very close to the LiH
substrate. This so-called “interfacial LiOH” is likely formed during moisture re-exposure where
H2O diffusion proceeds through crack lines in the Li2O created during the vacuum heating,
subsequent cooling, and moisture re-exposure. Since this interfacial LiOH signature is never

observed in as-polished LiOH, the logical question arose of whether the formation of this
interfacial LiOH with lower thermal stability by the prolonged, high temperature vacuum bake
may cause more unwanted hydrogen outgassing compared to as-polished LiH.

 To elucidate the effects of prolonged high temperature vacuum baking on LiH, we
compared the thermal stabilities of LiOH corrosion layers grown on as-polished LiH and on
previously vacuum-baked LiH (i.e., Li2O-covered LiH) by DRIFT in vacuum at lower
temperatures. In addition, temperature programmed desorption/decomposition (TPD) was also
employed to complement the results from DRIFT investigation.

MATERIALS, EXPERIMENTS, ANALYSIS
 Polycrystalline warmed-pressed salt disks with 12.7 cm in diameter and 1 mm in thickness
were used for these experiments. For as-polished LiH, the as-received disks were polished with
800 (P-2400) and 1200 (P-4000) grit SiC sandpapers in a nitrogen purged glove box at 1
atmosphere with ~ 3–10 molar ppm moisture content. After that, the samples were exposed to
room air (~ 30–40% relative humidity) from a few seconds to a few minutes to form ~ 240-540
nm initial LiOH corrosion layers, as verified by DRIFT (via a high temperature reaction chamber
equipped with CaF2 windows inside a Praying Mantis DRIFT module by Harris Scientific
Products, Inc.).
 In some experiments, the as-polished LiH disks with 240-540 nm initial LiOH corrosion
layers (described in the previous paragraph) were heated to 470-490 K in ~ 10-6 Torr vacuum to
convert LiOH-covered LiH to Li2O-covered LiH. Upon cooling down to room temperature (~ 295
K), the Li2O-covered LiH disks were briefly re-exposed to room air to re-form ~ 240-530 nm
LiOH. These samples are denoted as vacuum-baked LiH with limited moisture re-exposure.

 A typical LiOH thermal stability test involved heating the two sample types inside the
DRIFT reaction chamber at ~ 10-6 Torr vacuum. In-situ DRIFT spectra were collected manually
during the heating ramp (around 30 minutes) to specific temperatures (~ 343 K or 363 K in this
report). Thereafter, a heating program maintained the set point temperature inside the reaction
chamber while DRIFT spectra were recorded at periodic intervals for up to 150 hours.

 The technique of TPD was explained in detail elsewhere.1-2 TPD in a chamber with a base
vacuum of 3 × 10-8 Torr is employed to record any difference in the levels of outgassing on an “as-
is” sample and a previously vacuum-baked sample that was subsequently exposed to ~ 46 ppm of
moisture for 150 minutes. The “as-is” sample was polished as described above but was
subsequently subjected to varying moisture levels ranging from 3 ppm to 300 ppm over many days
of storage, transportation, and handling before TPD to form a significant LiOH corrosion layer,
but the thickness was not measured.

RESULTS & DISCUSSION
 Vacuum baking of LiOH-covered LiH salt essentially performs the reaction: 2 LiOH + heat
+ vacuum → Li2O + H2O. A portion of the released H2O reacts with the very hydrophilic LiH
substrate, resulting in the subsequent reaction: LiH + H2O → Li2O +H2, while the rest of H2O is
removed by the pump. Therefore, both H2 and H2O are detected by mass spectrometry during
vacuum baking of LiOH-covered LiH.1-3 Vacuum heating in the temperature range of 470-483 K
at ~ 10-6 Torr vacuum overnight is sufficient to convert LiOH-covered LiH to Li2O-covered LiH.

 The curve at the bottom of Fig. 2 is the DRIFT spectrum from a LiH sample that underwent
vacuum-baking, then re-exposed to 30 – 35 ppm moisture level for 50 days at 296 K. There are
two observable LiOH DRIFT peaks in Fig. 2: a shallow one at lower wavenumber (~ 3528 cm-1),
and a sharper and more intense one at higher wavenumber (~ 3675 cm-1). For the same species, an
infrared signature at lower wavenumber indicates a lower vibration frequency, and therefore lower
binding energy. Indeed, after vacuum-heating to 308 K for a few hours, the peak at the lower
wavenumber was reduced by a factor of two (see the middle curve in Fig. 2). At 344 K, this
thermally unstable LiOH peak completely disappeared. Fig.2’s inset presents a magnified view of
the evolution of the 3528 cm-1 LiOH signature as a function of increasing temperature in vacuum.
Under the same vacuum-heat treatment, the infrared intensity of the main 3675 cm-1 LiOH peak
decreased very slightly. The 3528 cm-1 LiOH peak from previously vacuum-baked LiH
corresponds to the so-called “interfacial LiOH”.
 Due to stress relaxation during the vacuum-heating conversion of LiOH to Li2O and
depending on the heating rate and initial corrosion layer thickness, crack lines may form in the
corrosion layer. The crack lines allow moisture diffusion during subsequent moisture re-exposure

to form “interfacial LiOH”, which are simply isolated islands of LiOH near the LiH substrate as
pictorially illustrated in Fig. 3.

Fig. 3 Illustration of the locations for LiOH states that give rise to the main 3675 cm-1 and the down-
shifted minor 3528 cm-1 infrared peaks.

 Because of the small and isolated structures of these isolated LiOH islands, interfacial
LiOH has a different infrared vibrational frequency than the main LiOH peak at 3675 cm-1. The
shifting of the vibrational frequency of the interfacial LiOH from 3675 cm-1 down to 3528 cm-1 is
ascribed to the close vicinity of the isolated LiOH islands to the very effective LiH moisture pump,
which weakens the LiOH bonds. The main 3675 cm-1 peak for LiOH formed on LiH at low
temperatures (e.g., room temperature) is further composed of surface/amorphous LiOH and
crystalline LiOH (also called bulk LiOH). Readers who are interested in the sub-division of LiOH
into interfacial, surface/amorphous, and bulk/crystalline LiOH states, are referred to previous
publications.1-3 However, for comparison of the thermal stabilities of LiOH grown on as-polished
and on vacuum-baked LiH, it is sufficient to examine the reduction of the main DRIFT peak at
3675 cm-1 and of the interfacial peak at 3528 cm-1 (if existing) from these samples during mild
heating (343- 363 K) in vacuum.

 From the bottom DRIFT curve at 296 K in Fig. 2, the height ratio of the interfacial DRIFT
peak at 3528 cm-1 to the main DRIFT peak at 3675 cm-1 is 0.026/0.525 or roughly 5%. The
interfacial DRIFT peak at 3528 cm-1 is not always present in all previously vacuum baked LiH
samples following moisture re-exposure. In fact, the height ratio of the interfacial DRIFT peak to
the main DRIFT peak can vary from 0% to 10% depending on such factors as heating rate, surface
finish, and the initial LiOH thickness before the vacuum-baking step.

 In Fig. 4, the comparison of DRIFT spectra recorded at room temperature of as-polished
samples and of those that underwent vacuum-baked with subsequent limited moisture re-exposure
are presented. The plots in the left panel are from an as-polished LiH sample with 0.1 a. u. at 3675
cm-1 (~240 nm LiOH) and another previously vacuum-baked LiH sample with subsequent
moisture re-exposure to 0.1 au at 3675 cm-1. There is no observable interfacial peak at 3528 cm-1

for the previously vacuum-baked sample. This thermally unstable LiOH peak at 3528 cm-1 is also
below DRIFT detection limit (i.e., less than a few nanometers of LiOH thickness) for the plots in
the center and right panels, which contrast the DRIFT spectra of as-polished samples with and
Li2O-covered LiH samples after H2O re-exposure to 0.15 a. u. (360 nm LiOH) and 0.22 a. u. (528
nm LiOH), respectively. This observation simply means that there is not much creation of crack
lines by the slow vacuum heating rate from room temperature to 343-363 K in 30 minutes for the
samples prepared as described in the “MATERIALS, EXPERIMENTS, ANALYSIS” section with
only 0.1-0.22 a. u. of LiOH. As a result, the thermal stabilities corresponding to the samples
presented in Fig. 4 can be obtained directly by recording the changes in DRIFT peak height at
3675 cm-1. The following figures (Fig. 5 – 7) record how the 3675 cm-1 peak decreases as the
sample is exposed to heat and vacuum.

 From Figs. 5-7, it is seen that the stability of LiOH against thermal decomposition under
vacuum (or equivalently in dry inert gas) of previously vacuum-baked LiH is equal or better than
that of as-polished LiH. The set of experiments with 0.1 a. u. LiOH (left panel of Fig. 4 and Fig.
5) exhibits practically equal stabilities between as-polished and vacuum-baked samples, while the
sets of experiments with 0.15 a. u. (center panel of Fig. 4 and Fig. 6) and with 0.22 a. u. (right
panel of Fig. 4 and Fig. 7) manifest improved thermal stability for the vacuum-baked samples
followed by moisture re-exposure. Logically, it seems the thicker the initial LiOH corrosion before
vacuum-bake, the wider the Li2O buffer layer in between the LiH substrate and the reformed LiOH
top corrosion layer (from limited moisture exposure after vacuum-bake). The wider the Li2O layer
between the LiH substrate and the top reformed LiOH, the less effect the hydrophilic LiH substrate
has on weakening the LiOH bonds, hence more thermal stability for reformed LiOH for samples
with thicker initial LiOH before vacuum baking. But the trend for the improvement in thermal
stability does not seem to track monotonically with LiOH thickness, as evidenced in higher thermal
stability for the previously vacuum-baked sample with 0.15 a. u. of LiOH than for the sample with
0.22 a. u. of LiOH. The variation in surface finish/roughness during the manual polishing of the
samples on different days may have affected the observed results to some degree. However, there
is a connection between the improved crystallinity of the previously vacuum-baked samples and
the 20 K lower temperature used in the investigation of the samples with 0.15 a. u. LiOH grown
on as-polished LiH at room temperature has a large amorphous portion, which is less thermally
stable than crystalline LiOH formed at higher temperatures. The prolonged high temperature
vacuum bake not only converts LiOH to Li2O, but also forms more crystalline Li2 O, which then
transforms to more crystalline LiOH upon moisture re-exposure, ensuring a higher thermal
stability for LiOH. The fact that the improved thermal stability for the samples investigated at 343
K is more visibly pronounced than for the samples at 363 K indicates that the enhanced crystallinity
of the reformed LiOH via the high temperature vacuum baking described in this report is only
modest and therefore affects the thermal stability of reformed LiOH more strongly at lower
temperature.

 Nevertheless, we believe there are many advantages in vacuum-baking of LiH samples,
even with subsequent limited moisture re-exposure. As illustrated in Fig. 8, per reaction: LiH +
H2O → LiOH + H2, exposure of as-polished LiH to moisture during long-term storage grows LiOH
and releases H2 (A, B). High temperature vacuum-baking converts LiOH-covered LiH to Li2O-
covered LiH (C). Following limited moisture-exposure, a thinner LiOH corrosion layer is formed
(D) in comparison with that from as-polished LiOH after prolonged storage in moist environment
(B), resulting in less LiOH outgassing potential. In addition, there is no H2 outgassing with further
exposure to moisture until complete consumption of the Li2O thickness created during the vacuum-

bake per the reaction: Li2O + H2O → 2LiOH. Besides, the high temperature vacuum baking
provides two more benefits: creating a Li2O buffer zone between the LiH substrate and the
reformed LiOH and improving the crystallinity of the reformed LiOH. Both increase thermal
stability of reformed LiOH, especially at lower operation temperatures.

Fig. 8 The evolution of as-polished LiH with prolonged moisture exposure, conversion to Li2O-
covered LiH with high temperature vacuum-bake, and re-establishment of a thinner top LiOH layer
following limited moisture re-exposure.

 Note that crack lines after high temperature vacuum baking are omitted in Fig.8 to be
consistent with the absence of the 3528 cm-1 LiOH DRIFT signals for the three samples in this
investigation (see Fig. 4). However, researchers should keep in mind that depending on the initial
LiOH corrosion thickness before vacuum baking and the aggressiveness of the heating schedule,
stress release formation of crack lines in the Li2O may form. With subsequent moisture re-exposure
and enough crack lines, the presence of isolated LiOH islands at the end of the crack line near the
LiH substrate (see Fig. 3) and the associated 3528 cm-1 LiOH DRIFT signals may be expected.
 Another observation is that: unlike the case of previously vacuum-baked sample followed
by limited moisture re-exposure with possible isolated islands of interfacial LiOH near the LiH
substrate (see Fig. 3), the LiOH phase in as-polished sample with subsequent prolonged moisture
exposure at room temperature is a continuous structure, containing not only amorphous and
crystalline but also interfacial states. Although the DRIFT peak at 3528 cm-1 is not observed in the
sample, the interfacial states would still be present. This is so because since these LiOH states are
interconnected, there is no energy down-shifted 3528 cm-1 LiOH peak arising from the distinct
vibrational frequency of isolated LiOH islands surrounded by Li2O near the LiH substrate. In other
words, the interfacial LiOH is there in full-force for LiOH grown on as-polished LiH just without
the down-shifted 3528 cm-1. Even with possible cracks formation in the corrosion layer by the high

temperature vacuum conversion of LiOH-covered LiH to Li2O-covered LiH, the density of the
thermally unstable interfacial LiOH is significantly reduced (or even eliminated) as in the case of
slow heating of thicker LiOH-covered LiH) by the vacuum-bake.

 As pictorially described in Fig. 8, vacuum-baking of an as-is sample with a short
subsequent re-exposure to moisture reforms only a thinner top layer of LiOH, which is separated
from the LiH substrate by a layer of Li2O. The overall benefit of this is the reduction of hydrogen
outgassing potential. In Fig. 9, the outgassing fluxes from TPD experiments for mass 2 and mass
18 of an as-is sample (polished with subsequent exposure to varying moisture levels ranging from
3 ppm to 300 ppm over many days of storage, transportation, and handling) and a vacuum-baked
sample that was subsequently exposed to ~ 46 ppm of moisture for 150 minutes are presented in
the left and right plots, respectively.

Fig. 9 Outgassing fluxes from TPD experiments for mass 2 and mass 18 of an as-is sample
(polished with subsequent exposure to varying moisture levels ranging from 3 ppm to 300 ppm
over many days of storage, transportation, and handling ) and a vacuum-baked sample that was
subsequently exposed to ~ 46 ppm of moisture for 150 minutes are presented in the left and right

 The outgassing fluxes for both mass 18 (from the reaction: 2LiOH → Li2O + H2O) and
mass 2 (some H2O molecules released from the LiOH decomposition were sucked toward the
hydrophilic LiH substrate and react according to: 2LiH + H2O → Li2O + 2H2) were significantly
reduced for the previously vacuum-baked sample. Mass 2 of the as-is sample is seen to be a very
broad peak running from room temperature to around 550 K at 0.025 K/s heat rate, confirming the
abundant existence of interfacial LiOH state without a separate DRIFT peak for the as-is sample,
as discussed in the previous paragraph. The release of mass 2 from the TPD of the previously
vacuum-baked sample consists of 2 peaks: one centers around 365 K at 0.025 K/s heat rate, the
other around 485 K. The mass 2 peak at lower temperature from the previously vacuum-baked

sample corresponds to interfacial LiOH decomposition associated with LiOH islands along crack
lines near the LiH substrate as illustrated in Fig. 3.3 Most H2O released from the LiOH thermal
decomposition in proximity to the LiH substrate was taken up by it, releasing H2 instead of H2O.
The mass 18 spectra at higher temperature in both plots are from thermal decomposition of LiOH
far away from the LiH substrate (surface/amorphous and bulk/crystalline LiOH).3 Since the top
LiOH layer in the previously vacuum-baked sample is much thinner than in the as-is sample, the
surface-to-bulk ratio is higher in the former,4 causing the shift of the surface/bulk LiOH
decomposition peak to lower temperature for this sample. Beyond the main H2O peaks in both
plots, mass 2 signal rises again with no release of mass 18, signaling the beginning of LiH
decomposition in 10-8 Torr high vacuum. Nevertheless, the main effect of vacuum-baking, even
with subsequent limited moisture re-exposures, is the removal of thermally unstable LiOH bonds
near the hydrophilic LiH substrate and formation of a thick Li2O buffer layer in between the LiH
substrate and the top LiOH.
 The total equivalent H2 outgas potential and expected LiOH DRIFT for the samples in Fig.
9 in a closed system application is presented in Fig. 10. The equivalent H2 outgas potential is
obtained by integrating the outgassing fluxes for mass 2 and mass 18 with respect to time. The
formula for the equivalent H2 outgas potential is “Total H2 outgas potential = molecules of mass 2
per unit area + 2 × molecules of mass 18”. It is seen that in the case of vacuum baking with limited
H2O re-exposure associated with Fig. 9, a reduction of the outgas potential by as much as a factor
of 10 is realized.5

CONCLUSION

 We have experimentally compared the thermal stabilities of LiOH on as-polished LiH and
previously vacuum- baked LiH (i.e., Li2O-covered LiH) followed by moisture re-exposure in ~ 10-
6
  Torr vacuum at 343-363 K by diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform (DRIFT). We also
demonstrated the reduction of outgassing potential, especially at lower temperatures, for vacuum
baked samples by TPD experiments. The results presented in this report confirm that there are
clear advantages in the reduction of unwanted outgassing by the conversion of LiOH-covered LiH
to Li2O-covered LiH via high temperature vacuum baking before assembly of LiH samples into
closed vacuum or dry inert gas applications. These advantages include reduced LiOH outgassing
potential, no H2 outgassing even with moisture interaction until complete consumption of the Li2O
thickness formed by the vacuum-bake, and improved crystallinity (hence enhanced thermal
stability) of the reformed LiOH with moisture exposure on previously vacuum-baked LiH.
