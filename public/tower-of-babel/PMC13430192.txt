A newly developed rapid K39-based LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA for high-throughput serological screening of canine leishmaniasis
Authors: Irene Ferrero, Paolo Poletti, Enrica Giachino, Joel Filipe, Alessia Libera Gazzonis, Carolina Allievi, Paola Dall’Ara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Canine leishmaniasis (CanL) is a zoonotic vector-borne disease caused by Leishmania infantum, with dogs acting as the main reservoir for human infection. Reliable serological assessment is essential for disease surveillance and control. This study describes the development and validation of LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA, a new rapid qualitative and semi-quantitative assay for the detection of anti-L. infantum antibodies in canine serum. Cut-off values were established using multiple statistical approaches, including Youden’s index and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The assay was evaluated on 552 canine samples (313 positives, 206 negatives, 33 borderline), using IFAT as the reference serological method. LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA showed excellent agreement with IFAT (Cohen’s kappa of 0.93), achieving a relative sensitivity of 95.2% and a relative specificity of up to 99.5%. Comparative analyses also demonstrated superior diagnostic performance compared with other commercially available ELISA tests. These findings indicate that LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA represents a reliable alternative tool for the serological screening of canine leishmaniasis.

Introduction

Leishmania spp. are protozoan organisms belonging to the family Trypanosomatidae (order Kinetoplastida) transmitted through the bites of phlebotomine sand flies. They are responsible for leishmaniases, a group of zoonotic diseases characterized by heterogeneous epidemiological and clinical manifestations, ranging from self-limiting cutaneous lesions to severe, potentially fatal visceral forms. Leishmaniasis remains a significant public health concern in the Mediterranean basin, the Americas, Western and Southwestern Asia, as well as in East and North Africa. Leishmania spp. can naturally infect a broad spectrum of domestic and wild mammalian species, which may act as parasite reservoirs. While wildlife hosts are generally considered not to play a major direct role in human transmission, canids, particularly the domestic dog (Canis familiaris), represent the most important reservoir, playing a key role in maintaining and transmitting the infection to humans (Martín-Sánchez et al., 2007; Malta et al., 2010; Carrillo et al., 2013; Ribeiro et al., 2018). Leishmania infantum is the principal etiological agent of canine leishmaniasis (CanL), a chronic multisystemic disease (Alvar et al., 2004; Dantas-Torres, 2007; Morales-Yuste et al., 2022). During its life cycle, Leishmania protozoans exist in two distinct morphological forms: promastigotes, which develop in the gut of the insect vector, and amastigotes, which proliferate within host monocytes and macrophages. In the midgut of an infected sand fly, the motile, flagellated promastigotes multiply and, during a subsequent blood meal, are inoculated into the host’s dermis, where they are phagocytized by dermal macrophages or mononuclear phagocytic cells. Here, promastigotes lose their flagellum and differentiate into round-shaped amastigotes, which replicate intracellularly and progressively disseminate by infecting other cells, spreading to multiple tissues (Paltrinieri et al., 2010). In a later blood meal, sand flies may ingest infected macrophages containing amastigotes, thereby transmitting Leishmania parasites to new susceptible hosts, including dogs or humans.

In dogs, Leishmania infection may develop over weeks to several months (mean incubation period of approximately five months), during which the parasite disseminates systemically, leading to a broad spectrum of clinical outcomes ranging from subclinical or asymptomatic infection to disease (WOAH, 2021). Clinical manifestations vary in severity from cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), characterized by skin lesions, to disseminated visceral leishmaniasis (VL), the most severe and progressive form, which is almost fatal if left untreated (Castagnaro et al., 2007; Silva-Moreira et al., 2025). The infection can involve multiple tissues, causing cutaneous and visceral ulcerative lesions, ocular abnormalities, chronic weight loss, muscle atrophy, and nephropathy, which may lead to renal failure. In this case, an excessive antibody response can induce glomerulonephritis, usually associated with the deposition of immune complexes in the kidneys.

Control of canine leishmaniasis remains challenging. Current diagnostic approaches rely on a combination of parasitological, serological and molecular methods (Reimão et al., 2020). Routine laboratory investigations include a complete blood count, serum biochemical analysis, serum protein electrophoresis and urine examination. Although cytological and histopathological examinations may demonstrate the presence of Leishmania parasites within macrophages or affected tissues, these techniques show limited sensitivity and do not permit species-level identification. Parasitological methods remain the most specific diagnostic assays, as they involve the direct isolation and in vitro culture of promastigotes. Molecular techniques, particularly polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based assays, enable the detection of Leishmania DNA and currently represent highly sensitive and specific tools for the assessment of canine leishmaniasis (Castagnaro et al., 2007; Solano-Gallego et al., 2011; Morales-Yuste et al., 2022). Among serological assays available as support for CanL diagnosis, such as indirect hemagglutination assay (IHA) and Western blot, the indirect immunofluorescence antibody test (IFAT) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) are the most widely used in routine practice (Reimão et al., 2020). Among these, IFAT is regarded the reference serological method by the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH, 2021) for estimating the prevalence of CanL (Vulpiani et al., 2011; Travi et al., 2018; Morales-Yuste et al., 2022; Davenport et al., 2024; Mahachi et al., 2024).

The sensitivity and specificity of ELISA vary considerably depending on the antigen employed (Elmahallawy et al., 2014). Numerous Leishmania-derived antigens have been characterized and applied in ELISA and rapid immunochromatographic tests, including crude soluble antigen (CSA) and several recombinant proteins such as rK39, rK9, rK26, rK28, rKRP42, rKE16, rA2, and rKDDR (Bhatia et al., 1999; Boarino et al., 2005; Mohapatra et al., 2010; Farash et al., 2017, 2022; Reimão et al., 2020), as well as the Leishmania-activated C-kinase antigen (LACK) (Ramiro et al., 2003; Kamoun-Essghaier et al., 2005; Fernández et al., 2018). Although these antigens show variable diagnostic performance in patients affected with VL, the rK39 antigen remains the most widely used for both human and canine VL serodiagnosis (Burns et al., 1993; Braz et al., 2002; Scalone et al., 2002; Rosati et al., 2003; Mohapatra et al., 2010; Maia et al., 2012; Reiter-Owona et al., 2016; Reimão et al., 2020).

This study aimed to develop the LeishmaCHECK Ab, a new qualitative and semi-quantitative indirect ELISA test designed for the identification of L. infantum antibodies in dog serum/plasma samples.

Materials and methods

Specimen collection

To establish the new ELISA assay and evaluate its analytical reliability, canine serum and plasma samples from animals presenting with diverse clinical conditions were collected from veterinary clinics located in northern and central Italy, as well as in Greece (Attica region). All samples were tested for anti-Leishmania antibodies using the reference serological method IFAT (Fluo Leishmania kit, Agrolabo S.p.A.) and classified as positive or negative prior to their use in assay development. A sample was considered IFAT-positive when reactive at a dilution of 1:80 or higher. Canine samples were derived from routine diagnostic submissions without pre-selection, and all laboratory analyses were performed under blinded conditions, as laboratory operators had no access to clinical records or previous diagnostic results. Some samples exhibited marked lipemia or hemolysis. Clinical information regarding disease outcome or ongoing pharmacological treatments was available for only a limited number of cases. The overall sample size was consistent with that reported in previously published ELISA validation studies (Zheng et al., 2003; Mei et al., 2012; Righi et al., 2022; Appeltrath et al., 2024).

ELISA plate setting

Nunc PolySorp and Nunc MaxiSorp microplates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were selected based on manufacturer recommendations (Ferrero et al., 2025a, 2025b, 2025c, 2025d) and on supporting evidence from the literature (https://www.genetex.com/upload/media/support/protocols/genetex_ELISA(indirect).pdf), to determine optimal assay performance. Overall, coating and blocking conditions adopted for the development of the new indirect ELISA assay were based on standardized procedures described in the literature (https://www.cusabio.com/protocols/ELISA_Protocol.pdf) and optimized according to internal protocols and technical expertise.

During assay development, plates were coated with different recombinant antigens derived from L. infantum promastigotes and amastigotes. Among these, the fusion protein K9-K26-K39 that contains immunodominant regions of Leishmania K9, K26 and K39 (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA) or K39 antigen (Rekom Biotech, Granada, Spain) at 0.25 μg/ml (Kurkjian et al., 2005; Vaish et al., 2012; Mondal et al., 2019), 0.5 μg/ml (Zijlstra et al., 2001; Saliba et al., 2019) or 1 μg/ml (Silva et al., 2021) with or without LACK antigen (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA). All antigens were coated in carbonate-bicarbonate (CB) buffer (pH 9.6) (Agrolabo S.p.A., Scarmagno, Italy) in accordance with published protocols (https://www.biomol.com/dateien/Bethyl--ELISA-Protocol.pdf) and incubated overnight at 2–8 °C. After a wash step, plates were blocked using two commercial blocking buffers (BB1 and BB2) (Surmodics, Eden Prairie, MN, USA) and allowed to dry at room temperature (RT, 20–25 °C) for one day before being stored at 2–8 °C.

In the finalized ELISA protocol, Nunc MaxiSorp microplates were prepared and coated with 0.5 μg/ml of recombinant L. infantum K39 antigen (Rekom Biotech). In the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA, a positive control (PC), consisting of anti-histidine (His) tag antibody labelled with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), a negative control (NC, sample diluent) and samples, diluted 1:100 in sample diluent composed of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Agrolabo S.p.A., Scarmagno, Italy), were dispensed in 100 μl into each well and incubated for 30 min at RT. During this incubation, specific anti-L. infantum antibodies present in positive samples bound to the immobilized antigen, forming antigen-antibody complexes. Following an initial washing step to remove unbound material, 100 μl of HRP-conjugated anti-dog IgG antibody (Bethyl Laboratories, Montgomery, TX, USA) diluted 1:80,000 was added to each well and incubated for 15 min at RT. After a second wash cycle, 100 μl of the chromogenic substrate 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB; Surmodics) was added and incubated for 10 min at RT in the dark, allowing color development. The enzymatic reaction was stopped by the addition of 100 μl of stop solution (0.2 M sulfuric acid; Agrolabo S.p.A.), and the optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm by using an ELISA microplate reader (Multiskan SkyHigh, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The assay run was considered valid when the OD value of the NC was below 0.1 and that of PC exceeded 0.6, within the limits described in the literature (Yuan et al., 2023; Appeltrath et al., 2024).

Validation of LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA

The diagnostic performance of LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA was assessed relative to IFAT (Fluo Leishmania kit, Agrolabo S.p.A.), the reference serological assay based on slides coated with whole Leishmania parasites. A total of 552 samples were tested by IFAT, using a titre of 1:80 as the diagnostic cut-off.

Two analytical approaches were applied to the borderline samples to evaluate the impact of these specimens. First, borderline samples were excluded to compare only clearly positive and negative animals. Secondly, they were classified as negative, following previous studies that considered dogs seropositive only at IFAT titres ≥ 1:160, as a titre of 1:80 may represent non-specific reactivity or antibody levels insufficient to reliably indicate infection (Oliva et al., 2014; Gizzarelli et al., 2020; Maurelli et al., 2020; Morales-Yuste et al., 2022; Mahachi et al., 2024; Santoro and Vellusi, 2015).

Briefly, IFAT was performed by incubating 20 μl of samples diluted 1:80 and appropriate controls on antigen-coated substrate slides in a humid chamber for 30 min at 37 °C. Following a washing step, 20 μl of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labelled anti-dog IgG antibody conjugate was added to each well, and the slides were incubated again for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After washing and application of the mounting medium, coverslips were placed and slides were examined under a fluorescence microscope equipped with a FITC filter. A sample was considered negative when Leishmania parasites appeared greyish-red, whereas positive samples were characterized by a distinct yellow-green fluorescence localized on the parasite membrane and flagellar region.

Positive samples were further subjected to two-fold serial dilutions up to 1:640 to determine their antibody titre defined as the highest dilution yielding a positive fluorescence signal.

ELISA false-negative results were assigned to samples with IFAT titres above 1:80, in line with previous reports (Oliva et al., 2014; Anfossi et al., 2018; Stensvold et al., 2019; Gizzarelli et al., 2020; Maurelli et al., 2020; Morales-Yuste et al., 2022; Mahachi et al., 2024; Santoro and Vellusi, 2015).

Regarding controls, for PC, a minimum OD of 0.6 was set for test validity (Yuan et al., 2023; Appeltrath et al., 2024), consistent with prior ELISA studies (Raditic et al., 2011; Valero et al., 2012; Sirikajornpan et al., 2021). The sample diluent was used as NC, considering the 0.3 OD as threshold (Yuan et al., 2023; Appeltrath et al., 2024) as described in both research and commercial ELISA assays using PBS-based solutions (Hagler and Naranjo, 1994; Niedrig et al., 1999; Peng et al., 2013; EuroMAbNet, 2023; https://www.immunoreagents.com/protocols/view/2).

Determination of ELISA cut-off

Multiple approaches were employed to determine the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA cut-off. In preliminary experiments, the cut-off was calculated as the mean OD of negative samples plus three times the standard deviation (Lardeux et al., 2016; Khatri et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021). Samples with OD values within ±10% of the cut-off were classified as borderline, following the approach used in other commercial ELISA assays (ABIN997000, DRG Instruments GmbH; Antibodies online, 2023; DRG Instruments, 2023). Cut-off was established by analyzing 132 samples (66 positives and 66 negatives) previously used for ELISA validation. According to various methodologies, optimal cut-off value was defined as the minimum difference between sensitivity and specificity (Mazzu-Nascimento et al., 2017) or as a value between the maximum OD of negative samples and the minimum OD of positive samples (Lardeux et al., 2016). Furthermore, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and Youden’s index (J) ranging from 0 (ineffective test) to 1 (perfectly effective test), were used to identify the cut-off that maximized the distinction between true- and false positives. Finally, considering the amounts of true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives obtained from the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA against IFAT as reference assay, all diagnostic parameters including the accuracy, relative sensitivity and specificity, positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV, respectively) were determined at the selected cut-off value. The 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were computed for sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV.

Evaluation of reproducibility and precision

The accuracy of the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA was evaluated by assessing variability within a single or different assay runs (intra- and inter-assay precision). Tests were conducted on different days under comparable conditions following the WOAH guidelines (WOAH, 2023). To account for potential differences in manual technique, assays were performed by two independent operators. Positive and negative samples were analysed in eight replicates across three separate batches, yielding a total of 24 measurements per sample, in accordance with International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) Q2(R1) recommendations, which require a minimum of nine determinations (at least three replicates under three distinct conditions) (FDA, 2021). For each sample, the mean, minimum and maximum OD values, standard deviation (SD), and percentage coefficient of variation (%CV) were calculated.

Comparison with commercial tests

The performance of the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA was evaluated in comparison with two commercially available ELISA assays commonly reported in the literature. In one study, 370 canine serum/plasma samples (152 IFAT-positive, 186 IFAT-negative and 32 IFAT-borderline) were tested using Leiscan ELISA or CIVTEST® Canis Leishmania (HIPRA, Amer, Spain) (Rodríguez-Cortès et al., 2013; Solano-Gallego et al., 2014). In a second study, 84 samples (42 IFAT-positive, 40 IFAT-negative and 2 IFAT-borderline) were assessed using INgezim Leishmania ELISA (Gold Standard Diagnostics, Madrid, Spain) (Risueño et al., 2012; Ledesma et al., 2017; Páez et al., 2025). All assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Evaluation of serological cross-reactivity

Serum samples previously confirmed positive for Ehrlichia canis antibodies by both IFAT (Fluo Ehrlichia kit, Agrolabo S.p.A.) and ELISA (EhrlichiaCHECK Ab ELISA, Agrolabo S.p.A.), were tested for anti-Leishmania antibodies by LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA to assess potential serological cross-reactivity between the two pathogens.

Determination of ELISA kit expiry

In the absence of specific regulations for stability testing of veterinary diagnostic kits, the ASTM F1980-21 Standard Guide for Accelerated Aging of Sterile Medical Device Packages (ASTM International, 2021), which uses the Arrhenius equation to estimate device degradation under accelerated conditions, was followed. Accelerated stability was conducted at 37 °C for 6 weeks, corresponding to 18 months of real-time stability at 2–8 °C, as calculated using online tools (https://keystonecompliance.com/aat-calculator/; https://pkgcompliance.com/resource/accelerated-aging-calculator/). For both accelerated and real-time stability studies, initial testing was performed at baseline (T0), followed by weekly assays during the first six weeks. Thereafter, kits stored at 2–8 °C were tested every three months up to 18 months. For each sample and control, the percentage of remaining activity (%RA) was calculated as the ratio of the OD at a given time point to that at T0, defining 70% as the minimum acceptable activity (Lexmond et al., 2011).

Statistical analysis

During comparison studies statistical analyses were performed using a t-test, with P-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Cohen’s d was calculated to quantify the strength of the relationship (or effect size) with thresholds of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, moderate and large effects, respectively. Additionally, Cohen’s kappa (κ) coefficient was used to assess the degree of agreement beyond chance. Moreover, a simple linear regression model was used to explain the variation between the observations and the predicted values.

Results

Preliminary assays on Nunc PolySorp and MaxiSorp microplates

Preliminary assays were performed on Nunc PolySorp and Nunc MaxiSorp plates under different coating conditions and conjugate dilutions (1:30,000 and 1:80,000). Across all tested conditions, the NC and IFAT-negative sera consistently showed low OD values, whereas the PC and IFAT-positive sera displayed clear reactivity. No nonspecific signal was detected in uncoated wells, and the conjugate alone produced OD values comparable to the NC, confirming minimal background reactivity (Supplementary Table S1).

On PolySorp plates, assay performance was influenced by both antigen composition and blocking buffer. Among IFAT-negative samples, statistically significantly lower OD values were observed with K9-K26-K39 than with K9-K26-K39 plus LACK when BB1 was used, whereas no differences were detected with BB2. Furthermore, BB2 significantly reduced nonspecific reactivity compared with BB1 when K9-K26-K39 plus LACK was employed, indicating more effective blocking (Supplementary Table S2). No significant differences were observed between K39 alone, K39 plus LACK, and K9-K39-K26, suggesting that the addition of LACK did not improve assay performance when K39 was used as the coating antigen (Supplementary Table S2). The PC produced strong signals (OD > 1.5) only with K9-K39-K26 plus LACK or K39 coated with 0.5 μg/ml, whereas lower K39 coating concentrations generated suboptimal OD values (Supplementary Table S1).

Subsequent optimization focused on K39-coated MaxiSorp plates blocked with BB2. Antigen concentrations of 0.5 and 1 μg/ml and conjugate dilutions of 1:30,000 and 1:80,000 provided comparable discrimination between positive and negative samples without detectable nonspecific reactivity. However, the 1:80,000 conjugate dilution yielded improved signal intensity in positive sera. Evaluation on a larger panel of 101 sera (77 IFAT-negative and 24 IFAT-positive) showed that MaxiSorp plates produced significantly higher OD values in positive samples (mean OD ± SD of 2.130 ± 1.063) than PolySorp plates (mean OD ± SD of 1.450 ± 0.780; Cohen’s d = 0.303) and eliminated the two false-negative results observed with PolySorp (Supplementary Table S2). Consequently, relative sensitivity and specificity compared with IFAT reached 100% on MaxiSorp plates (Cohen’s kappa of 1.000, excellent agreement), whereas PolySorp plates achieved 91.7% sensitivity and 100% specificity (Cohen’s kappa of 0.944, excellent agreement) (Table 1). The NC formed by sample diluent and tested with 1:80,000 conjugate dilution showed OD values in the range of negative samples. Regarding the PC, a dilution of 1:300,000 provided the strong reactivity (OD values roughly 2.7) with minimal background, while lower dilutions frequently generated saturated or overflow signals (Supplementary Table S1). Comparable results were obtained with K39 concentrations of 0.5 and 1 μg/ml under different incubation conditions. Based on these findings, the final ELISA format was established using MaxiSorp plates coated with K39 as the sole antigen, BB2 blocking buffer, a conjugate dilution of 1:80,000 and incubation times of 30/15/10 min, as these conditions provided the best balance between signal intensity and low background reactivity.

Cut-off determination

The cut-off of the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA was determined using 132 samples (66 IFAT-positive and 66 IFAT-negative; Supplementary Table S4), considering IFAT as the reference test. According to previous studies, an appropriate cut-off may lie between the maximum OD of negative samples (Max OD–) and the minimum OD of positive samples (Min OD+) (Lardeux et al., 2016). In this dataset, Max OD− was 0.450 and Min OD+ was 0.318. Moreover, considering sensitivity and specificity curves (Mazzu-Nascimento et al., 2017), OD values between 0.360 and 0.450 corresponded to their intersection, and both parameters reached 98.5% (Supplementary Table S4). In addition, according to the ROC curve analysis, the optimal cut-off was OD 0.460, representing the best balance between sensitivity and specificity (Fig. 1) thus confirming the previous analysis. Similarly, the maximum Youden’s index (J = 0.985) corresponded to OD values between 0.460 and 0.570 (Fig. 2).Fig. 1Cut-off determination by ROC curve.Fig. 2Cut-off determination by Youden’s index.

Reproducibility assessment

Reproducibility assay was conducted on controls, two positive (IFAT titres of 1:80–1:160 and > 1:640) and two negative samples (Supplementary Table S6). For ELISA assays, the acceptable limits for the coefficient of variation (%CV) are generally below 10% for intra-assay precision and between 15% and 20% for inter-assay one. Evaluation of serological cross-reactivity

Co-infections involving ehrlichiosis, babesiosis, and leishmaniasis are relatively frequent, particularly in tropical, subtropical, and temperate regions, where hematophagous vectors are widely distributed (Krawczak et al., 2015). In addition, previous studies have reported serological cross-reactivity between antibodies against Ehrlichia canis and Babesia canis and Leishmania spp. in commonly used diagnostic assays (Mancianti et al., 1996; Mettler et al., 2005; Ferreira et al., 2007). Thus, given the availability of E. canis-positive samples, the potential serological cross-reactivity between anti-Ehrlichia canis and L. infantum antibodies was evaluated. A total of 46 canine samples, previously confirmed positive for E. canis antibodies by both IFAT (Fluo Ehrlichia kit, Agrolabo S.p.A.) and ELISA (EhrlichiaCHECK Ab ELISA, Agrolabo S.p.A.), were subsequently tested using the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA. As all samples yielded negative results, no evidence of cross-reactivity was observed between anti-E. canis antibodies and the Leishmania K39 antigen (Supplementary Table S8).

In the accelerated stability study, the PC, NC and five samples (two negative and three positive) were evaluated. All controls and samples showed percentage of remaining activity (%RA) values above 70% at each time point (Supplementary Table S9). These results indicate that kit reagents remain stable for up to 6 weeks at 37 °C, supporting an estimated shelf life of 18 months at 2–8 °C, pending confirmation through long-term stability studies.

Discussion

Serological detection of antibodies against L. infantum is crucial for confirming infection, assessing clinical disease and monitoring public health interventions. Ideally, the diagnostic sensitivity of a serological assay should be evaluated using animals with parasitologically confirmed infection, whereas specificity should be assessed in dogs whose uninfected status has been verified through longitudinal follow-up and consistently negative test results (Medeiros et al., 2021). However, such well-defined reference populations are often difficult to obtain under field conditions, and the absence of a perfect reference standard remains a common challenge in studies evaluating diagnostic performance. Among the available serological techniques, IFAT and ELISA are the most widely used serological methods for clinical evaluation and research on canine L. infantum infection. Although IFAT, based on whole promastigotes, is generally considered the reference serological method (WOAH, 2021; Travi et al., 2018; Mahachi et al., 2024), it has several limitations, including the need for specialized equipment and experienced personnel for result interpretation. Furthermore, IFAT may exhibit cross-reactivity with other trypanosomatids, particularly Trypanosoma cruzi and potentially with other Leishmania species (Troncarelli et al., 2009). Consequently, relying exclusively on IFAT as the reference method may introduce bias when assessing the diagnostic performance of novel assays. The performance of the K39-based LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA was evaluated not only in comparison with IFAT but also against other commercially available ELISA assays. This approach provided an additional and more robust assessment of assay performance, reducing dependence on a single reference test and allowing a broader evaluation of agreement among currently available serological methods.

The classification of samples with borderline IFAT titres (1:80) represents a critical issue in seroepidemiological studies of CanL, as it can influence estimates of seroprevalence and diagnostic performance. To assess the impact of these samples, two approaches were evaluated. First, borderline samples were excluded to maximize epidemiological homogeneity between clearly seropositive and seronegative dogs. Secondly, they were classified as negative, consistent with previous studies that considered dogs seropositive only at IFAT titres ≥ 1:160 (Oliva et al., 2014; Gizzarelli et al., 2020; Maurelli et al., 2020; Morales-Yuste et al., 2022; Mahachi et al., 2024; Santoro and Vellusi, 2015). Under this assumption, a titre of 1:80 may indicate non-specific reactivity, early exposure to Leishmania, or antibody levels insufficient for a reliable assessment of infection status. In the absence of compatible clinical signs or additional confirmatory evidence, such dogs should not necessarily be considered infected. Regardless of the analytical approach adopted, LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA based on K39 antigen achieved a high relative sensitivity (95.2%) and specificity up to 99.5% together with excellent agreement with IFAT (Cohen’s kappa of 0.93). These findings indicate a strong ability to detect anti-Leishmania antibodies while minimizing false-positive results, a particularly valuable characteristic in endemic areas where confirmatory testing is often required.

The performance of LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA was evaluated against two commercially available ELISA assays, INgezim Leishmania (GSD) and Leiscan (HIPRA). Both assays are based on crude immunodominant L. infantum antigens. However, they differ in their detection systems. In INgezim Leishmania assay, an HRP-conjugated monoclonal anti-canine IgG antibody is employed, whereas in Leiscan, HRP-labelled protein A is used for IgG detection (Páez et al., 2025). Despite these methodological differences, all ELISA assays produced both false-positive and false-negative results. Notably, the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA generated the lowest proportion of false-positive reactions. Direct comparison among LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA, Leiscan, and IFAT further confirmed this observation.

The present study demonstrated that both LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA and Leiscan are highly reliable serological tools for the detection of anti-Leishmania antibodies, exhibiting diagnostic performances comparable to IFAT. When IFAT borderline samples (titre 1:80) were excluded, the minimal discrepancies observed between the two ELISA assays and the excellent agreement values (Cohen’s kappa > 0.93) indicated that they may be used interchangeably or complementarily in clinical screening protocols.

Consistent with previous findings (Santoro and Vellusi, 2015), both Leiscan and LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA failed to identify a limited number of positive samples with IFAT titres of ≤ 1:160, 1:320, and ≥ 1:640, resulting in false-negative classifications. Conversely, inclusion of IFAT borderline samples led to a slight reduction in specificity for both ELISA platforms, attributable to a modest increase in false-positive results. Nevertheless, both assays maintained excellent performance compared with IFAT, with sensitivities exceeding 94%, overall accuracies above 95%, and excellent inter-test agreement (Cohen’s kappa > 0.91). These results highlight the robustness and reliability of both ELISA systems, even when applied to clinically ambiguous cases or samples characterized by low antibody titres.

In CanL, the humoral response is typically associated with high antibody titres, whereas low antibody levels are characteristic of subclinical infections or dogs that have been exposed but remain uninfected (Travi et al., 2018). Elevated anti-K39 antibody levels may indicate an active VL or disseminated infection (Reiter-Owona et al., 2016). The detection of false positives and false negatives complicates the establishment of a precise correlation between IFAT antibody titres and ELISA results. Despite this issue, a strong correlation between LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA results and Leiscan-predicted IFAT titres (coefficient of determination R2 of 0.979) was determined, supporting the use of LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA also as a reliable semi-quantitative assay for the serological screening of canine leishmaniosis.

Unlike Leiscan, the INgezim Leishmania kit exhibited more pronounced performance variability depending on the reference method used for comparison. When IFAT borderline samples were excluded, INgezim Leishmania assay showed substantially lower diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 66.7% and an overall accuracy of 79.3%, achieving only moderate agreement with IFAT (Cohen’s kappa = 0.588). In contrast, under the same conditions, the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA demonstrated superior diagnostic performance, reaching a sensitivity of 76.2% and a specificity of 100%, together with good concordance with IFAT. These findings indicate that, although the INgezim Leishmania kit maintains acceptable specificity, its higher proportion of false-negative results limits its sensitivity as a screening tool when compared with the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA. The inclusion of IFAT borderline samples did not adversely affect the diagnostic performance of either assay. On the contrary, both ELISA platforms displayed remarkable stability across performance metrics. Nevertheless, when compared with IFAT, the INgezim Leishmania assay continued to show lower sensitivity than LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA (66.7 vs 76.2%) and a higher number of false-negative results (14 vs 10 cases). Of the 14 false-negative samples identified by the INgezim Leishmania assay, four were also classified as negative by Leiscan, whereas ten were likewise negative using the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA. Notably, four of these ten samples were obtained from dogs undergoing anti-Leishmania treatment, a factor that may have influenced antibody detection. Overall, these findings confirm that, while both assays exhibit high specificity, the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA provides a more sensitive and robust diagnostic approach, regardless of whether IFAT borderline samples are included in the analysis.

The superior diagnostic performance observed for LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA may be partially explained by the characteristics of the recombinant antigens selected for assay development. ELISA-based immunoassay performance largely depends on antigen quality. In this study, we used the recombinant fusion protein K9-K26-K39 together with LACK and K39, individually or in combination. LACK is a conserved Leishmania protein and a component of the NeoLeish® vaccine, representing an immunological target against L. infantum (Alonso et al., 2023). Kinesin-derived antigens K9, K26, and K39 have been widely used for VL serodiagnosis in humans and dogs (Boarino et al., 2005) and are involved in essential cellular processes in Leishmania (Gerald et al., 2007). Among these, K26 is an amastigote-upregulated hydrophilic protein containing 11 repeats of a 14-amino acid motif (Bhatia et al., 1999; Mohapatra et al., 2010; Travi et al., 2018). K39 is also predominantly expressed in amastigotes during VL (Rosati et al., 2003; Alvar et al., 2004; Silva et al., 2021) and includes a conserved 39-amino acid tandem repeat shared within the L. donovani complex (Burns et al., 1993; Badaro et al., 1996; Gerald et al., 2007; Dhom-Lemos et al., 2019). These repetitive regions enhance antibody recognition and induce strong humoral responses (Dhom-Lemos et al., 2019; Silva et al., 2021). In addition, K39 showed high sensitivity and specificity in ELISA-based serodiagnosis of human and canine VL (Burns et al., 1993; Braz et al., 2002; Scalone et al., 2002; Rosati et al., 2003; Mohapatra et al., 2010; Maia et al., 2012; Reimão et al., 2020), particularly in symptomatic cases (Badaro et al., 1996; Bhatia et al., 1999; Coelho et al., 2012). However, its sensitivity decreases in asymptomatic infections, as also observed for IFAT (Mettler et al., 2005). Based on its overall superior diagnostic performance, K39 was ultimately selected as the antigen of choice for the present study.

Compared to IFAT, the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA produced only one false-positive result. While some studies have reported reactivity of antibodies against Ehrlichia canis and Babesia canis with Leishmania spp. in common serological tests (Mancianti et al., 1996; Mettler et al., 2005; Ferreira et al., 2007), others found no cross-reactivity in IFAT, ELISA or rK39-based rapid tests in co-infections with these tick-borne pathogens (Krawczak et al., 2015). Consistently, our analysis of E. canis-positive samples showed no cross-reactivity with the Leishmania K39 antigen. Moreover, the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA does not differentiate between infections caused by L. infantum and the closely related L. donovani, both members of the L. donovani complex. However, L. donovani predominantly infects humans, whereas L. infantum is more pathogenic in dogs and is the primary agent of CanL (Konno et al., 2022).

False-negative ELISA results were defined as samples with IFAT titres > 1:80, consistent with previous studies (Anfossi et al., 2018; Stensvold et al., 2019; Mahachi et al., 2024). Among the positive samples included in this study, the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA yielded 15 false-negative results, with IFAT titres ranging from 1:80–1:160 to > 1:640. Given their limited frequency, these discordant findings are unlikely to reflect a systematic limitation of the assay. Rather, individual variability in antibody specificity or binding affinity may contribute to this discrepancy. Notably, four samples (no. 17, no. 72, no. 124, and no. 132) yielded false-negative results not only with LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA but also with the Leiscan and INgezim Leishmania assays.

Several explanations may account for false-negative serological results. The diagnostic performance of serological assays is influenced by the infecting Leishmania species, host immune response and assay sensitivity (Romero et al., 2005; Reiter-Owona et al., 2016). In cross-sectional studies such as the present one, some infected animals may not yet have undergone seroconversion at the time of sampling. Consequently, false-negative results may occur during the early stages of infection, when antibody levels remain below the detection threshold of serological assays. In the absence of parasitological confirmation and longitudinal follow-up, the true infection status of individual dogs cannot be established with complete certainty (Paltrinieri et al., 2010).

In addition, antibody levels may decline following successful treatment. Studies in both dogs and humans with VL have documented reductions in anti-Leishmania antibodies, including anti-K39 antibodies, after clinically effective therapy (Kumar et al., 2001; Reiter-Owona et al., 2016; Solano-Gallego et al., 2016). Clinical information was available for six of the fifteen false-negative dogs. Four animals (no. 191, no. 193, no. 195, and no. 196) received anti-Leishmania treatment, one dog (no. 194) was vaccinated, and one (no. 197) was asymptomatic. Importantly, all six samples were also negative with the INgezim Leishmania ELISA despite IFAT titres ranging from 1:80–1:160 to > 1:640. These findings support the hypothesis that reduced antibody detectability, rather than assay failure, may explain at least some of the discordant results.

The lower sensitivity of serological assays in asymptomatic infections should also be considered. ELISAs based on total, soluble or recombinant Leishmania antigens frequently fail to identify a proportion of asymptomatic infected dogs (Carvalho et al., 2002; Porrozzi et al., 2007; Rosenzweig et al., 2008; Coelho et al., 2012). Although K39-based ELISAs generally achieve high sensitivity in dogs with established clinical disease, they may yield negative results in asymptomatic animals with parasitologically confirmed infection. Similar observations have been reported in humans, where anti-K39 antibodies are most strongly associated with active VL or with subclinical infections that subsequently progress to overt disease (Badaro et al., 1996; Alvar et al., 2004).

A further consideration relates to the clinical form of leishmaniasis. Serological testing is generally less sensitive in CL than in VL, as antibody titres are typically lower due to the predominance of a cell-mediated (Th1) immune response over a humoral (Th2) response (del Giudice et al., 1998; Lombardo et al., 2014; Reiter-Owona et al., 2016; Villanueva-Saz et al., 2025). Consistent with this, most CL samples may result negative by K39-based ELISA despite detectable IFAT titres (Reiter-Owona et al., 2016).

Finally, a prozone (Hook) effect cannot be completely excluded. In VL patients with strong humoral responses, excess antibodies may interfere with antigen-antibody complex formation, leading to falsely negative serological results (Butch, 2000; Madariaga et al., 2006). Although prozone-like phenomena have been described in several immunoassays (Mathai et al., 2020; Mina et al., 2024), this mechanism was not specifically investigated in the present study. Therefore, its contribution to the observed false-negative results remains speculative and warrants further evaluation through serial dilution testing of discordant samples. In any case, when clinical signs are indicative of disease, negative results should be retested or confirmed using an alternative diagnostic assay. Therefore, serology should be interpreted in combination with clinical and molecular findings.

Moreover, LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA accuracy was supported by reproducibility analyses, which showed intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation within acceptable ranges (Lexmond et al., 2011; Conrad, 2023).

The present study was designed as a cross-sectional evaluation of assay performance in a heterogeneous population of dogs representative of routine diagnostic submissions, thereby reflecting real-world clinical conditions. Although this protocol does not allow for a longitudinal assessment of infectious status, and the lack of parasitological confirmation or comprehensive clinical data limits the definitive classification of the infection, the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA proved to be a useful tool for serological screening of anti-Leishmania antibodies in dogs. Additionally, most samples were analysed under blinded conditions, as recommended to minimize bias in diagnostic evaluations (Ribeiro et al., 2019), with clinical information available for only a limited subset of samples.

However, as samples were derived from routine clinical submissions rather than randomized selection, future prospective studies incorporating randomized sample selection, longitudinal follow-up, and fully blinded testing procedures would provide further evidence regarding the diagnostic accuracy, reproducibility, and field applicability of the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA. Although no nonspecific binding was observed, potential interference from drugs or endogenous compounds in inflamed blood cannot be completely excluded and warrants further investigation.

Regarding ELISA kit controls, because positive biological controls are difficult to standardize and may introduce batch-to-batch variability, only commercially available non-infectious reagents were included in the kit. Since the recombinant antigens carried a His-tag and no commercial antibodies against the immobilized molecules were available, an anti-His HRP antibody was selected as a positive control, as previously described (Zentgraf et al., 1995; Lindner et al., 1997; Bornhorst and Falke, 2000; Debeljak et al., 2006; He et al., 2022). Our results confirmed the suitability of using both the PC, consisting of the anti-His HRP antibody, and the NC as sample diluent.

The ELISA kit must be stored at a temperature between 2 °C and 8 °C to preserve its stability from the time of manufacture until use. Accelerated stability testing, typically carried out at 37 °C, is the most appropriate approach for simulating product degradation by increasing the rate of chemical and physical reactions in biological reagents. This method allows the shelf life of the product to be established more quickly (Cannell et al., 1985; Campa et al., 2021). During transport or storage, various unforeseen events may occur, such as breaks in the cold chain, which can negatively affect product quality and performance. Stability studies can be performed at defined temperatures that replicate the environmental conditions of the regions or countries where the product is to be distributed (Mechkovski, 1994; Bajaj et al., 2012). Unlike pharmaceutical preparations and veterinary medicinal products, where established stability testing guidelines are available (González-González et al., 2022; EMA, 2024), there is no dedicated legislative or regulatory framework governing the estimation of shelf life for veterinary diagnostic kits, such as ELISA or immunochromatographic assays. In this study, the Standard Guide ASTM F1980-21 (ASTM International, 2021), which applies the Arrhenius equation to predict medical device degradation under accelerated conditions, was followed to estimate the corresponding real-time shelf life. Our results showed that the kit remains stable for six weeks at 37 °C and can be stored for up to 18 months at 2–8 °C, evidence supported by in-house experience in developing ELISA tests (Ferrero et al., 2025a, 2025b, 2025c, 2025d). The accelerated stability assessment is adequate to support the commercialisation of the developed product and indicates that it remains suitable for use even if the cold chain is interrupted for up to six weeks.

Conclusions

The control of leishmaniasis remains challenging in both human and canine populations. The diagnosis of CanL should always integrate clinical history and presenting signs. Within this framework, the LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA represents a rapid and reliable screening tool, providing accurate qualitative and semi-quantitative results in about one hour, without the need for thermostatic equipment. Positive results are highly suggestive of active infection and should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical and laboratory findings. However, false-negative outcomes may occur in asymptomatic or subclinically infected animals, in cases of CL or due to the prozone effect. Moreover, pharmacological treatment may reduce anti-K39 antibody levels, potentially affecting test sensitivity. The stable performance also in samples with ambiguous serological status or low antibody titers highlights the diagnostic robustness, while its potential utility for post-treatment monitoring warrants further investigation. Collectively, these findings support the use of LeishmaCHECK Ab ELISA as a practical, robust and reliable alternative to IFAT for routine serological screening of canine leishmaniosis.
