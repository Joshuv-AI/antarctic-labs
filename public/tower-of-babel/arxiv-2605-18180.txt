Canonical Regularisation of Wide Feature-Learning Neural Networks
George Whittle, Pranav Vaidhyanathan, Juliusz Ziomek, Natalia Ares, Maike A. OsborneDepartment of Engineering ScienceUniversity of Oxford

Abstract
Wide neural networks in the feature-learning regime drive modern deep learning, and yet they remain far less studied than their kernel-regime counterparts. We consider a critical yet under-explored difference between these two regimes: the regulariser and prior implied by gradient flow training. This canonical regularisation property is well-studied in kernel regime networks — of all the infinite global minima, gradient flow selects exactly the vanishing ridge solution — and underpins the celebrated NN-GP correspondence, precisely allowing the modelling of noise during training. However, we prove ridge regularisation biases gradient flow in feature-learning regime networks, even in the infinitesimal limit of vanishing regularisation. Over training, ridge distorts the inductive bias of the network, with a particular damage done to pretrained networks where the implicit prior is informative. We resolve this by axiomatising the canonical regulariser as a regime-agnostic function-space energy and lift, which uniquely identifies ridge in the kernel regime, and crucially generalises to the feature-learning regime. By studying the Riemannian geometry of feature-learning networks, we derive geodesic ridge from our framework, generalising ridge to the feature-learning regime. Correspondingly, we prove the canonical function-space prior is a Riemannian Gibbs Process , generalising the more familiar Gaussian Process. As a practical contribution, we propose arc ridge as a minimax-robust, scalable surrogate to geodesic ridge, revealing a deep relationship between early stopping and canonical regularisation across learning regimes. Finally, we demonstrate the consequences of our theory empirically on both image processing and NLP transfer-learning problems.

This paper confronts one such case—

The kernel-regime picture is well understood. An overparametrised model has an entire continuum of zero-loss interpolators, yet gradient flow selects a single, well-defined point from this continuum: the unique vanishing-regularisation limit of

Anchored ridge equals the squared Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) norm of the change in network output under the NTK over training, so the same penalty governs both parameter and function space, and the corresponding implicit prior over functions is Gaussian—the celebrated Neural Network–Gaussian Process correspondence. From this single equivalence flow virtually all the analytical conveniences of kernel-regime networks: principled treatment of label noise, optimal shrinkage, calibrated uncertainty, predictable transfer behaviour. We argue that the gap is geometric. Behind every overparametrised network lie three natural parameter-space objects (Section
3.1
): the
flow orbit
, the region of parameter space gradient flow can ever explore from a given initialisation; the

, the canonical

output fibres
, directions transverse to the flow manifold along which predictions on training points do not change, but along which predictions at unseen inputs can change drastically. In the kernel regime these collapse: orbit, flow manifold, and the affine plane
\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathrm{Im}(J^{\top})
coincide, the trajectory is confined to it, and the fibres meet it orthogonally everywhere. Anchored ridge, the squared chord from
\bm{\theta}_{0}
to
\bm{\theta}

1

Anchored ridge, still measured along Euclidean chords, no longer respects this curvature: at the gradient-flow limit its gradient acquires a component along the output fibres, pulling parameters along directions invisible to the training loss but consequential at test points. The resulting bias is silent at the loss level, persists in the limit as regularisation shrinks to zero, and is what we prove formally (Theorem
3.1
).

The same geometry suggests the cure. The intrinsic counterpart of squared Euclidean distance on a curved manifold is squared geodesic distance, and the corresponding intrinsic counterpart of anchored ridge is
geodesic ridge
(Proposition
4.1
): squared geodesic distance from initialisation along the flow manifold, plus an analogous on-fibre term. Its gradient at the gradient-flow limit lies tangent to the flow manifold along the realised flow direction (Figure
1
, left, dashed orange), and through the standard MAP correspondence it yields a function-space prior that generalises the NTK Gaussian Process to curved geometry: the
Riemannian Gibbs Process
. In the kernel regime the manifold flattens, geodesics straighten into chords, and both objects reduce to anchored ridge and the NTK GP. The progression standard ridge
\to
anchored ridge
\to
geodesic ridge thus captures a sequence of geometric corrections: one Euclidean for initialisation, then one Riemannian for manifold curvature.

Geodesic distances are computationally inaccessible at scale. Our final contribution identifies a tractable upper bound that retains the alignment property:
arc ridge
, the squared total path length of the training trajectory itself. Arc ridge requires no manifold solvers and no second-order information; it is a single scalar accumulated over training updates. It nevertheless inherits the no-bias property of geodesic ridge along the realised gradient-flow direction, sandwiches geodesic ridge from above against an anchored-ridge lower bound, sits at the minimax-optimal point within this sandwich, and reduces formally to early stopping under exact gradient flow. The combination places it within reach of any standard training pipeline.

3
Ridge Biases Gradient Flow in the Feature Learning Regime

The first core theoretical contribution of this work is an exposition of the feature-learning regime as a
pathology
of both anchored and standard ridge. To understand this, we must first understand the
geometry
underlying wide networks, and how it differs between the two regimes.

3.1
Parameter-space Geometry

We begin by illuminating the parameter-space geometry of any overparametrised network satisfying Assumption
2.1
, restricting ourselves to the region
U
for complete rigour. This geometry is best understood as a collection of lower-dimensional subsets, which collectively span all of
U
. The first of these is the horizontal orbit generated by the directions available to gradient flow:

Definition 3.1

(Flow orbit)
.

Let

\mathcal{H}_{\bm{\theta}}:=\operatorname{Im}J(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\subset T_{\bm{\theta}}\Theta

denote the horizontal distribution induced by the model-output Jacobian. The
flow orbit

through
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, denoted
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\subseteq\Theta
, is the
reachable set of the horizontal control system

\dot{\bm{\theta}}(t)=J(\bm{\theta}(t))^{\top}\bm{u}(t),\qquad\bm{u}\in L^{2}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{n}),

namely

\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right):=\left\{\bm{\theta}(T):T>0,\ \bm{u}\in L^{2}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{n}),\ \dot{\bm{\theta}}(t)=J(\bm{\theta}(t))^{\top}\bm{u}(t),\ \bm{\theta}(0)=\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\}.

The flow orbit is the fundamental control-theoretic object. In general it is a sub-Riemannian reachable set as its tangent directions are generated by the Lie closure of
\mathcal{H}
, and therefore its dimension can exceed
n
. Thus the orbit itself should not be identified with function space. In order to move from function-space quantities back to parameter space, we fix a gauge, selecting a single representative from the flow orbit for each output value. This gives us our second fundamental geometric object:

Definition 3.2

(Gauge-fixed flow manifold and representative map)
.

Let

\bm{\theta}^{\star}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\phi_{\mathrm{gf}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},t\right)

be the gradient-flow interpolator from
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. The
gauge-fixed flow manifold

through
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is the basin sheet

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right):=\operatorname{cc}_{\gamma_{\mathrm{gf}}}\left\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right):\lim_{t\to\infty}\phi_{\mathrm{gf}}\left(\bm{\theta},t\right)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\phi_{\mathrm{gf}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},t\right)\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\,

where
\operatorname{cc}_{\gamma_{\mathrm{gf}}}
denotes the connected component containing
the realized gradient-flow trajectory

\gamma_{\mathrm{gf}}:=\{\phi_{\mathrm{gf}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},t\right):t\in[0,\infty)\}.

Under Assumption
2.1
,
\Theta^{\star}\cap U
is normally hyperbolic, and thus there exists an open neighbourhood
V
of
\Theta^{\star}\cap U
in which
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
is an embedded submanifold by the stable manifold theorem. For this neighbourhood, we define

C_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0}):=g\bigl(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\cap V\bigr)\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{n}.

The canonical representative map is

g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right):=\left(g\big|_{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\cap V}\right)^{-1}(\bm{c}),\qquad\bm{c}\in C_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0}).

Our final geometric object spans the remainder of parameter-space:

Definition 3.3

(Output fibre)
.

The
output fibre
through
\bm{\theta}
, denoted
\Theta\left(\bm{c}\right)\subset\Theta

\Theta\left(\bm{c}\right):=g^{-1}\left(\bm{c}\right)=\bigl\{\,\bm{\theta}:g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\bm{c}\bigr\}.

Intuitively,
\Theta\left(\bm{c}\right)
represents the collection of vertical directions in parameter space which do not affect training predictions

, but can have a marked effect on the network’s predictions away from the training inputs

U

\Theta\left(Y\right)=\Theta^{\star}\cap U

3.2
The failure of ridge in the feature-learning regime

Equipped with our geometric understanding of parameter-space, exposing the failure of both standard and anchored ridge in the feature learning regime is straightforward. The feature-learning regime is characterised by
curved
geometry — features evolve over training and
J
changes meaningfully with
\bm{\theta}
— thus
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
curves meaningfully between
\bm{\theta}_{0}
and
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
. As illustrated in Figure
1
, this stands in stark contrast to the geometry of the kernel regime where
J
is constant on
U
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\equiv\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=\left\{\bm{\theta}_{0}+J^{\top}\bm{u}:\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}
is an affine subspace.

Now consider the interaction of these geometries with the anchored ridge regulariser,
\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
. The contribution to gradient flow training of this regulariser is
-2\left(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
, e.g. the chord from
\bm{\theta}_{0}
to
\bm{\theta}
. In the kernel regime, this always lies within the tangent space of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
, and thus anchored ridge can never induce parameter movement off-manifold — it is
compatible
with gradient flow (Proposition
C.10
). This geometric alignment is precisely what causes the vanishing regularisation property underpinning the NN-GP correspondence, and as shown by
33
, allows us to explicitly model observation noise in the training set through an MAP interpretation of training.

In the feature-learning regime, however, curvature of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
causes this gradient to generically have a component lying in off-manifold fibre directions. The implications of this are significant: the regularisation gradient causes parameter displacements invisible to the loss function and thus uncontrollable by the training set, but which can drastically change behaviour away from the training set by destroying the network’s covariance structure and inductive bias imbued by initialisation. We can formally describe this pathology by its bias to gradient flow in the infinitesimal limit:

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider a wide feature-learning regime network satisfying Assumption
2.1
. Let
\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{\lambda\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\theta}\right\rVert^{2}}:=\arg\min\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)+\lambda\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
. Then

\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\theta}\right\rVert^{2}}:=\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{\lambda\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\theta}\right\rVert^{2}}\neq\bm{\theta}^{\star},

where
\bm{\theta}^{\star}:=\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\phi_{\mathrm{gf}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},t\right)
is the time-limit of unregularised gradient flow.

An identical argument proves failure of standard ridge in both kernel
and
feature-learning regimes. Figure
2
illustrates this pathology exactly. Intuitively, the limiting dynamics has two timescales. On the fast timescale, small-
\lambda
regularised trajectories stay close to the unregularised one, seemingly converging to
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
. However, any
\lambda>0
gives rise to a slow timescale on which the uncontrolled regularisation gradient induces drift fibre directions, biasing the time-limit. For larger
\lambda
— necessary in problems with little data or noisy targets — this pathology becomes immediate, yielding significant divergence from the unregularised trajectory.

While not always a bad thing in practice—this effect is precisely what causes weight decay-induced
grokking

[
36
,
44
]
and underpins the adaptive kernel representer theorem of
24
—in cases where the feature geometry is well-suited to the task a priori (i.e. pretrained networks), it can harm generalisation by replacing useful inductive bias with drifted features purely fitting noise
[
23
]
. We expose this pathology empirically in Section
5
, which confirms a massive degradation of test performance under both standard and anchored ridge at high regularisation strengths.

4
Canonical Regularisation in the Feature-learning Regime

Despite its pathology in the feature-learning regime, it is clear that anchored ridge’s harmony with gradient flow in the kernel regime is quite special. Indeed, this result implicitly underpins the NN-GP correspondence and Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory as a whole
[
17
]
. It is thus reasonable to describe anchored ridge as the
canonical regulariser
of kernel-regime networks, corresponding to the
function-space canonical prior
NTK Gaussian Process. We thus set out to derive an equivalent regulariser for the feature-learning regime.

Such a task requires us to return to regime-agnostic first-principles and axioms, ensuring unique identification of anchored ridge in the kernel regime as a validation of our framework. We propose a horizontal function-space energy formulation (with respect to the local Fisher metric
K(\bm{\theta})
) as a suitably fundamental object underpinning such networks in the context of gradient flow training:

Definition 4.1

(Canonical function-space energy, prior, and regulariser)
.

For some overparametrised network initialised at
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, the
canonical function-space energy
at
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}
is the minimum control-theoretic energy required to transport
g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
from
g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
to
\bm{c}
under gradient flow dynamics:

\displaystyle E(\bm{c}):=

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{u}(t)}\int_{0}^{1}\bm{u}(t)^{\top}K(t)\,\;\bm{u}(t)\,dt\quad

subject to

\displaystyle\quad\dot{\bm{\theta}}=J\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)^{\top}\bm{u},\ K(t)=J\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)J\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)^{\top},\;\bm{\theta}(0)=\bm{\theta}_{0},\;\bm{\theta}(1)=g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right).

For kernel-regime networks, the minimum energy control is the constant
\bm{u}^{\star}(t)=K^{-1}\left(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\right)
, recovering the standard squared RKHS norm
E(\bm{c})=\left(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\right)^{\top}K^{-1}\left(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\right)\equiv\left\lVert\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{RKHS}}^{2}
(Proposition
C.10
(c)).

The canonical function-space prior arises from
E(\bm{c})
straightforwardly as a Gibbs distribution:

Definition 4.2

(Canonical function-space prior)
.

The canonical function-space prior induced by
E
is a
Riemannian Gibbs Process
, with prediction density on the training set

p(\bm{c}\mid X)\propto\exp\!\left(-\beta E(\bm{c})\right).

The temperature
\beta
is the sole degree of freedom under
E
(due to scale invariance of gradient flow), and thus takes the role of a global prior scale.

Applying this Riemannian Gibbs Process prior to kernel-regime networks yields the familiar NTK GP, i.e.
\mathcal{GP}\left(f\left(\cdot;\bm{\theta}_{0}\right),(2\beta)^{-1}k_{\mathrm{NTK}}(\cdot,\cdot^{\prime})\right)
, with
p(\bm{c}\mid X)\propto\exp\!\left(-\beta\left\lVert\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{RKHS}}^{2})\right)
(Corollary
C.3
). Kolmogorov consistency over arbitrary finite test inputs at the fixed training set follows from a parametric pushforward (Proposition
C.15
); consistency
across
training subsets remains an open problem (Remark
C.5
).

Completing our generalisation requires one final task: a unique lift of the low-dimensional function-space energy to high-dimensional parameter-space. Reasonable axioms underpin this lift. Namely, we require the lift to be a) additive to the function-space energy
E(\bm{c})
associated with the training predictions
g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\bm{c}
at
\bm{\theta}
(thereby forcing the lift to geometrically correspond only to
\Theta\left(\bm{c}\right)
, b) quadratic in the ambient Euclidean metric of parameter-space, and c) uniformly
0
at the gauge-fixed representative
g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)
. This uniquely gives us the
canonical lift
(Theorem
C.6
):

Definition 4.3

(Canonical lift)
.

The
canonical lift
of
E(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
to parameter-space is

R(\bm{\theta}):=E(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right))\;+\;\inf_{\psi}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\dot{\psi}(t)\right\rVert^{2}dt,

where the infimum is over piecewise-
C^{1}
paths
\psi:[0,1]\to\Theta\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)\subset\Theta
with
\psi(0)=g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)
,
\psi(1)=\bm{\theta}
, and
g\left(\psi(t)\right)=g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\;\forall\;t\in[0,1]
.

Verifying this lift in the kernel regime uniquely points to anchored ridge: We have that
E(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right))=\left\lVert\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{RKHS}}^{2}\equiv\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{flow}}\right\rVert^{2}
where
\Delta\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{flow}}
is the projection of
\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}
onto
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
. Similarly, the lifted term is exactly
\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{fibre}}\right\rVert^{2}
, where
\Delta\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{fibre}}
is the projection of
\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}
onto
\Theta\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)
. As in the kernel regime these two sets are orthogonal and affine, the Pythagorean identity yields
R(\bm{\theta})=\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{flow}}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{fibre}}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
, uniquely recovering anchored ridge (Corollary
C.2
; Proposition
C.10
(d)).

Framework in hand, we now present the canonical regulariser of the feature-learning regime:

Proposition 4.1

(Geodesic ridge)
.

The canonical regulariser of feature-learning regime networks is
geodesic ridge
, defined as

d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{0}\right):=d^{2}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right),\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)+d^{2}_{\mathrm{fibre}}\left(\bm{\theta},g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)\right),

where
d^{2}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right),\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\equiv E(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
is the minimum horizontal distance in parameter-space from
\bm{\theta}_{0}
to the the gauge-fixed representative
g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)
, and
d^{2}_{\mathrm{fibre}}\left(\bm{\theta},g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)\right)
is the geodesic distance from the gauge-fixed representative to
\bm{\theta}
along the output fibre
\Theta\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)
.

While generally irreducible beyond this form, geodesic ridge is theoretically complete:

Theorem 4.2

(Canonical regulariser characterisation of geodesic ridge)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
:

(a)

Unique minimiser on each fibre.
For every
\bm{c}\in C_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
,
g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)
is the unique minimiser of
d^{2}\left(\cdot,\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
over
\Theta\left(\bm{c}\right)\cap V
.

(b)

Trajectory-level minimality.
Once the gradient flow trajectory
\bm{\theta}(t)
enters
V
,
\bm{\theta}(t)=\bm{\theta}^{\prime}(g\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right))
and
d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta}(t),\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=E(g\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right))
. In particular every intermediate trajectory point is the minimum-cost representative of its output vector.

(c)

Unbiased limit property.

\lim\limits_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)+\lambda d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=\bm{\theta}^{\star}
.

These properties are exemplified in Figure

. We have thus generalised anchored ridge to the feature-learning regime, theoretically alleviating all issues associated with anchored ridge and thus allowing for principled modelling of observation noise and uncertainty in the feature-learning regime.

4.1
Arc Ridge: A Minimax-Robust Upper Bound to Geodesic Ridge

Geodesic ridge, while theoretically complete, is intractable at scale. However, two observations combine to yield a practical surrogate. First, under exact gradient flow trajectories remain on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
by construction, so the fibre term in Definition
4.1
vanishes. Second, geodesic ridge is upper-bounded by the squared length of
any
on-manifold path,
including the realised training path
:

Definition 4.4

(Squared path length regulariser (arc ridge))
.

For a training trajectory
\gamma:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the
squared path length regulariser
, or
arc ridge
, is

L^{2}(\gamma)\;:=\;\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}(t)\right\rVert\,dt\right)^{2}.

L^{2}(\gamma)
depends only on the image of
\gamma
as a subset of
\Theta
, not on its time-parametrisation.

Theorem 4.3

(Squared path length upper-bounds the canonical regulariser)
.

Let
\gamma_{\text{train}}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
be any piecewise-
C^{1}
path with
\gamma_{\text{train}}(0)=\bm{\theta}_{0}
and
\gamma_{\text{train}}(T)=\bm{\theta}
. Then

d^{2}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}\right)\;\leq\;L^{2}(\gamma_{\text{train}});

under exact gradient flow (where the fibre term vanishes),
d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\leq L^{2}(\gamma_{\text{train}})
. The endpoint gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L^{2}
lies along
\dot{\gamma}_{\text{train}}(T^{-})\in\mathrm{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top})\cap T_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
(in particular
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L^{2}\in\ker N(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
), so regularisation by
L^{2}
introduces no component along the output fibres or indeed orthogonal to the realised gradient flow direction. Proof deferred to the appendix.

The bound follows from the definition of geodesic distance as an infimum of arc lengths chained with the energy–length identity (Proposition
C.9
); proof deferred to the appendix. Anchored ridge trivially lower-bounds geodesic ridge on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
as the solution to a relaxation of the same defining problem (Proposition
C.13
); the resulting sandwich and the shared vanishing-regularisation limit of all three regularisers at
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
are recorded in Corollary
C.4
.

In the absence of tractability of the canonical regulariser, it is principled to instead solve a minimax-robust relaxation of MAP training, choosing the regulariser that minimises worst-case regret within the sandwich
\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\leq d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\leq L^{2}(\gamma_{\text{train}})
. This directly motivates arc ridge regularisation. Furthermore, arc ridge requires storing only the accumulated arc length—a single float—since the regularisation gradient direction is fully determined by the current data gradient. It is therefore applicable at scale, and is used for most of our empirical results.

Arc ridge has a further theoretical property: it connects to early stopping, since regularisation by accumulated path length behaves like a time-based stopping criterion and, given an estimate of observation noise, obviates the need for a validation set (Proposition
F.4
). The relationship between ridge regularisation and early stopping is well-studied, and in agreement with our theory, early stopping generalises trivially to feature-learning regime networks
[
11
,
50
]
, unlike anchored ridge.

Our theory predicts that at high regularisation strengths, the pathological gradient component of standard and anchored ridge will cause significant degradation of the prior imbued by pretraining, manifesting as very poor test performance. 6
Conclusion and Limitations

We proved that the implicit prior of a wide network is governed not by any norm on parameters, but by the geometry of the trajectories gradient flow can trace. We showed that while in the kernel regime anchored ridge is geometrically aligned with training, in the feature-learning regime training geometry curves, causing the regularisation gradient of anchored (and standard) ridge to pathologically degrade the implicit prior at initialisation — a critical failure mode in transfer learning, for example. We proposed a correction to this by identifying two axioms uniquely identifying anchored in the kernel regime, and applied these to the curved geometry of feature-learning to obtain a principled framework not only yielding
geodesic ridge
as a generalisation of anchored ridge to the feature-learning regime, but also revealing the implicit prior here as a Riemannian Gibbs process. For practical unbiased regularisation at scale, we proposed
arc ridge
a minimax-robust surrogate to true geodesic ridge, which is cheap to compute at scale and reveals deep links to early stopping. Finally, we validated the predictions empirically: regularisers that respect the geometry of training preserve the implicit prior; those that ignore it do not.

Several directions remain open. Empirically, tighter scalable approximations to geodesic ridge, broader validation across architectures and tasks, and rigorous verification of Assumption
2.1
in concrete settings are immediate next steps. Theoretically, full Kolmogorov consistency of the Riemannian Gibbs Process across training subsets, analysis of the flow manifold in the
\mu
P infinite-width limit, and extensions to non-interpolating regimes
[
40
,
27
]
and preconditioned optimisers such as Adam
[
22
]
or Muon
[
19
]
all remain open.

Appendix B
Extended Related Work

Implicit bias and minimum-norm interpolation.

The connection between gradient flow and minimum-norm solutions in overparametrised linear models is classical
[
3
]
: the gradient flow limit is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse solution, which is also the
\ell_{2}
-minimum-norm interpolator. The same identity holds in kernel regression and in NTK-regime neural networks
[
17
,
25
,
10
]
: the gradient flow limit is the minimum-RKHS-norm interpolator, whose Gibbs distribution is the Gaussian Process prior
\mathcal{N}(g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right),K)

[
37
,
13
,
33
]
. The
kernel vs. rich
dichotomy of
43
delineates when networks behave like linear models (kernel regime) vs. when feature learning occurs. Our contribution is to identify the
energy
underlying this selection—not merely the norm—and to generalise it to the feature-learning regime. Theorem
3.1
shows that, beyond the kernel regime, the min-Euclidean-norm interpolator and the gradient flow limit diverge; the energy-based view moves beyond the implicit-bias framing to a positive characterisation of the canonical function-space prior.

Implicit bias in classification and structured models.

The implicit bias of gradient descent has been characterised in several other settings. For classification with the logistic loss on linearly separable data, gradient descent converges in direction to the max-margin (hard-SVM) solution
[
40
,
18
]
; for homogeneous networks this extends to a KKT-based characterisation of the margin
[
27
]
. For matrix factorisation, gradient descent induces an implicit nuclear-norm penalty
[
12
]
, and for wide two-layer networks trained with the logistic loss a related max-margin structure emerges
[
7
]
. These results characterise the
direction
of implicit bias in specific architectures or losses; our framework instead characterises the
function-space energy
underlying gradient flow selection and provides a geometric generalisation that applies across architectures and losses within the feature-learning regime.

Feature-learning theory.

The mean-field limit
[
6
,
29
,
38
]
describes the infinite-particle dynamics of two-layer networks in a regime where the Jacobian varies substantially, complementing the NTK picture. The Tensor Programs framework of
46
provides a unified characterisation of wide networks via a dynamical dichotomy between kernel and feature-learning regimes, with
\mu P
scaling
[
45
]
as the canonical parameterisation enabling stable feature learning. Recent work has analysed what gradient descent actually
learns
in the feature-learning regime:
8
prove that gradient descent can learn representations unavailable to fixed-kernel methods, and
24
characterise the adaptive kernel that emerges at the feature-learning infinite-width limit. Our work complements these results by characterising the
regulariser
that is canonical for the dynamics induced by gradient flow in this regime, rather than describing what those dynamics converge to.

Weight decay beyond RKHS regularisation.

Weight decay has been studied as a driver of phenomena beyond its interpretation as an RKHS prior. The grokking phenomenon—delayed generalisation long after data loss reaches zero—was first documented by
36
and has been given a provable analysis in the setting of ridge regression by
44
. The effect of weight decay on the features learned by neural networks, and its connection to the adaptive kernel at the feature-learning limit, is analysed by
24
. Theorem
3.1
and Remark
C.4
provide a complementary theoretical perspective: in the feature-learning regime, both standard ridge and anchored ridge select interpolating solutions that differ systematically from the gradient flow limit. Their practical effect is therefore
solution-set modification
rather than
prior specification
, and the benefits observed in practice may arise partly from this solution-selection property rather than from Bayesian regularisation.

Geometry-aware optimisers.

Natural gradient descent
[
2
]
preconditions the gradient by the inverse Fisher information matrix, which in the supervised learning setting equals
K\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{-1}

[
28
,
32
]
. Under this preconditioning, function-space dynamics become isotropic and training trajectories approximate geodesics on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)

[
51
,
5
]
; several wide-network approximations reproduce this isotropic behaviour
[
20
]
. Recent optimisers implement related principles at scale
[
19
,
4
]
. Our work is complementary: rather than proposing a new optimiser, we characterise the
regulariser
canonical for the geometry induced by gradient flow; preconditioning the gradient flow by the natural-gradient (NTK) metric is a direct route to tightening our path-length bound, but we leave its full development to future work.

G(\bm{c})\;=\;K\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)^{-1}\;+\;N(\bm{c})^{\top}N(\bm{c}),

so
G\succeq K^{-1}
pointwise, with equality
G(\bm{c})=K\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)^{-1}
if and only if
T_{g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=\mathrm{Im}(J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)^{\top})
(the kernel-regime alignment). Moreover, along the realised gradient flow trajectory
\bm{\theta}(t)=g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}(t)\right)
, the velocity
\dot{\bm{c}}(t)
satisfies
N(\bm{c}(t))\,\dot{\bm{c}}(t)=0
, so the trajectory’s
G
-energy reduces to
\dot{\bm{c}}^{\top}K\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)^{-1}\dot{\bm{c}}
.

C.3
Uniqueness of the canonical kernel-regime energy and lift

The kernel-regime canonical function-space energy
E(\bm{c})=(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))^{\top}K^{-1}(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))
of Definition
4.1
is the unique continuous output-space energy compatible with the natural geometry of the flow manifold; “naturality” alone — the property that vanishing-regularisation MAP estimation recovers the gradient flow limit — does not single it out. We make these statements formal here.

Theorem C.4

(Output-space energy uniqueness)
.

Let
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric positive definite. Suppose
\rho_{G}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
is continuous, satisfies
\rho_{G}(\bm{0})=0
, is nontrivial, and obeys

(i)

(
G^{-1}
-isometry invariance)
\rho_{G}(U\bm{c})=\rho_{G}(\bm{c})
for every
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
U^{\top}G^{-1}U=G^{-1}
;

(ii)

(orthogonal additivity)
\bm{u}^{\top}G^{-1}\bm{v}=0\implies\rho_{G}(\bm{u}+\bm{v})=\rho_{G}(\bm{u})+\rho_{G}(\bm{v})
.

Then
\rho_{G}(\bm{c})=\alpha\,\bm{c}^{\top}G^{-1}\bm{c}
for some constant
\alpha>0
.

In words, axiom (i) asks that
\rho_{G}
depends only on the
G^{-1}
-geometry of the displacement, not on the basis we describe it in (the analogue of rotational invariance for the standard inner product); axiom (ii) asks that
G^{-1}
-orthogonal displacements contribute additively (the function-space analogue of the Pythagorean identity). Together they leave a single scale degree of freedom, absorbed into the prior temperature
\beta
. Applied with
G=K^{-1}
, this characterises the kernel-regime canonical energy of Definition
4.1
uniquely. The corresponding parameter-space lift inherits this uniqueness: in the kernel regime, the orthogonal decomposition
\mathbb{R}^{m}=\mathrm{Im}(J^{\top})\oplus\ker J
coincides with the flow-tangent / fibre decomposition globally, and the squared ambient distance along
\Theta\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)
is the unique fibre extension respecting orthogonal additivity in this decomposition.

Remark C.1

(Both axioms of Theorem
C.4
are individually necessary)
.

Dropping axiom (i) (isometry invariance), the functional
\rho_{G}(\bm{c}):=(\bm{c}_{1}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)_{1})^{2}
is continuous, vanishes at the origin, is non-trivial, and is orthogonally additive (every basis-aligned splitting of
\bm{c}
trivially satisfies the additivity hypothesis); but it depends on the chosen first coordinate and is not of the form
\alpha\,\bm{c}^{\top}G^{-1}\bm{c}
. Dropping axiom (ii) (orthogonal additivity), the functional
\rho_{G}(\bm{c}):=\big(\bm{c}^{\top}G^{-1}\bm{c}\big)^{2}
is continuous,
G^{-1}
-isometry invariant, and non-trivial; but
G^{-1}
-orthogonal displacements satisfy
\rho_{G}(\bm{u}+\bm{v})=(s+t)^{2}\neq s^{2}+t^{2}=\rho_{G}(\bm{u})+\rho_{G}(\bm{v})
for
s,t>0
, so axiom (ii) fails. Hence both axioms are individually necessary for the uniqueness conclusion.

Proposition C.5

(No-go for naturality alone in the kernel regime)
.

Let
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
be the gradient-flow interpolator from
\bm{\theta}_{0}
in a kernel-regime model with constant Jacobian
J\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
K=JJ^{\top}\succ 0
. Consider the two-parameter family of trajectory-blind quadratic regularisers

\mathcal{F}\;:=\;\Bigl\{\,R_{a,B}(\bm{\theta})\;:=\;a\,\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\;+\;\bigl(J(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0})\bigr)^{\top}B\,\bigl(J(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0})\bigr)\;:\;a>0,\ B\in\mathrm{Sym}^{+}_{n}\,\Bigr\},

where
\mathrm{Sym}^{+}_{n}
denotes the cone of symmetric positive semi-definite
n\times n
matrices. Distinct
(a,B)
give distinct regularisers in
\mathcal{F}
(their Hessians
2(aI_{m}+J^{\top}BJ)
differ), and distinct elements of
\mathcal{F}
correspond to distinct Gaussian MAP parameter priors
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\theta}_{0},\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}(aI_{m}+J^{\top}BJ)^{-1})
. Yet every element of
\mathcal{F}
attains the same vanishing-regularisation limit:

\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\,\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}}\bigl[\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)+\lambda R_{a,B}(\bm{\theta})\bigr]\;=\;\bm{\theta}^{\star}\qquad\forall(a,B)\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathrm{Sym}^{+}_{n}.

The vanishing-regularisation property (“naturality”) therefore does
not
pin out anchored ridge — only the special case
B=0
does, and singling it out within
\mathcal{F}
requires the additional orthogonal-additivity axiom of Theorem
C.4
.

Theorem C.6

(Uniqueness of the canonical lift)
.

Fix the canonical function-space energy
E
of Definition
4.1
. Among all functionals
R:\Theta\to[0,\infty)
satisfying

(a)

(additivity to
E
)
R(\bm{\theta})=E(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right))+S(\bm{\theta})
for some
S:\Theta\to[0,\infty)
depending only on the on-fibre displacement of
\bm{\theta}
from
g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)
;

(b)

(Euclidean fibre quadratic) the restriction of
S
to each fibre
\Theta\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)\cap V
is a quadratic in the ambient Euclidean metric inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{m}
;

(c)

(vanishing at the gauge-fixed representative)
S(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right))=0
,

the unique solution (up to a positive scale) is

R(\bm{\theta})\;=\;E(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right))\;+\;\inf_{\psi}\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\dot{\psi}(t)\right\rVert^{2}\,dt\;=\;E(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right))\;+\;d^{2}_{\mathrm{fibre}}\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right),\bm{\theta}\right),

where the infimum runs over piecewise-
C^{1}
paths
\psi:[0,1]\to\Theta\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)
with
\psi(0)=g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)
,
\psi(1)=\bm{\theta}
. In the kernel regime this reduces to anchored ridge; in the feature-learning regime it is geodesic ridge of Proposition
4.1
.

Theorem C.7

(Combined uniqueness of geodesic ridge as the canonical regulariser)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the only functional
R:\Theta\to[0,\infty)
on a neighbourhood of
\Theta^{\star}
which simultaneously satisfies the function-space-energy axioms of Theorem
C.4
(with
G=K^{-1}
at
\bm{\theta}_{0}
) on the output channel
g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
, the lift axioms (a)–(c) of Theorem
C.6
on the parameter-space channel, and the vanishing-regularisation property
\arg\min(\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\cdot\right),Y\right)+\lambda R)\to\bm{\theta}^{\star}
as
\lambda\downarrow 0
, is, up to a single positive scale,

R(\bm{\theta})\;=\;d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\;=\;d^{2}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)\right)+d^{2}_{\mathrm{fibre}}\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right),\bm{\theta}\right),

i.e. geodesic ridge. In the kernel regime this collapses to anchored ridge; the corresponding function-space prior is uniquely the Riemannian Gibbs Process of Definition
4.2
, collapsing to the NTK Gaussian process under linear
J
.

C.4
Geometry of the flow manifold and output fibres

Proposition C.8

(Output fibres are smooth submanifolds)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, for every
\bm{c}\in g(U)
the output fibre
\Theta\left(\bm{c}\right)\cap U
is an embedded
C^{\infty}
submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
of codimension
n
.

Remark C.2

(Path-connectivity of output fibres)
.

In the overparametrised regime
m\gg n
, the connected component of
\Theta\left(\bm{c}\right)\cap V
containing
g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)
is path-connected via the implicit-function-theorem chart on
\Theta\left(\bm{c}\right)
over
V

[
26
]
, so the fibre distance
d_{\mathrm{fibre}}\left(\bm{\theta},g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)=\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right\rVert
in Definition
4.3
is finite.

Corollary C.1

(Unique fibre–flow intersection in
V
)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
C.2
, for every
\bm{c}\in C_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
the intersection
(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\cap V)\cap\Theta\left(\bm{c}\right)
consists of exactly one point, which we denote
g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)
. In particular
\bm{\theta}^{\star}=\bm{\theta}^{\prime}(Y)
, and the
\omega
-limit
\omega(\bm{\theta}_{0})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\phi_{\mathrm{gf}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},t\right)=\bm{\theta}^{\star}
.

Proposition C.9

(Constant-speed optimality)
.

Among piecewise-
C^{1}
paths
\gamma:[0,1]\to(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right),\,\text{ambient Euclidean})
with fixed endpoints, the squared-energy
\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}(t)\right\rVert^{2}\,dt
is minimised by constant-speed parametrisations of length-minimising paths, and at the minimiser the energy equals the squared arc length:
E(\gamma_{\mathrm{opt}})=L^{2}(\gamma_{\mathrm{opt}})
.

Remark C.3

(Existence and non-uniqueness of minimising geodesics)
.

On the smooth Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\cap V,g_{\text{ambient}})
near
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
, geodesic completeness on a compact sublevel set of the squared distance from
\bm{\theta}_{0}
follows from local properness of
g|_{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\cap V}
(Lemma
D.7
); the Hopf–Rinow theorem
[
9
, Ch. 7, Thm. 2.8]
then yields a length-minimising geodesic between any two endpoints in the same component. Minimisers need not be unique: conjugate points and non-trivial topology of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\cap V
can create multiple equal-length shortest paths. The gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}E(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
is correspondingly a set-valued quantity in general; any continuously tracked minimising geodesic yields a valid subgradient selection, and each element of this subdifferential is a valid descent direction for
E
. The squared path-length regulariser of Definition
4.4
circumvents this non-uniqueness by tracking a specific (training-induced) horizontal path on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
, with gradient given by Proposition
F.3
.

Remark C.4

(Standard ridge fails doubly in feature-learning networks)
.

Standard ridge,
R_{\mathrm{std}}(\bm{\theta})=\left\lVert\bm{\theta}\right\rVert^{2}
, fails in feature-learning networks for two independent reasons.

First
, it ignores the initialisation: for any
\bm{\theta}_{0}\neq\bm{0}
, minimising
\left\lVert\bm{\theta}\right\rVert^{2}
rather than
\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
already biases solutions away from the gradient flow limit
in the kernel regime
, where anchored ridge is the correct canonical regulariser
[
13
,
33
]
. For linear models, anchored ridge is the classical Tikhonov regulariser
[
41
]
centred at the initial estimate
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, equivalent to MAP estimation under the Gaussian parameter prior
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\theta}_{0},\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}I)

[
34
]
; ridge regression
[
16
]
corresponds to the special case
\bm{\theta}_{0}=\bm{0}
.

Second
, even for networks with zero initialisation,
R_{\mathrm{std}}=R_{\mathrm{ctr}}|_{\bm{\theta}_{0}=\bm{0}}
, so Theorem
3.1
applies directly: whenever
J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
is non-constant along the training trajectory, the minimum-Euclidean-norm interpolator centred at the origin diverges from
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
.
In summary, anchored ridge is the correct target in the kernel regime but is itself insufficient in the feature-learning regime, where only the canonical curved lift of Definition
4.3
selects the gradient flow solution.

C.5
Kernel-regime reduction of the canonical objects

Proposition C.10

(Kernel-regime reduction)
.

Suppose the model has constant Jacobian
J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\equiv J
on
U
, with
K=JJ^{\top}\succ 0
on the corresponding NTK Gram matrix. Then the canonical objects of Section
4
reduce as follows:

(a)

Affine flow manifold:

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\cap U=\big(\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathrm{Im}(J^{\top})\big)\cap U
;

(b)

Closed-form gauge-fixed representative:

g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)=\bm{\theta}_{0}+J^{\top}K^{-1}(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))
;

(c)

RKHS canonical energy:

E(\bm{c})=(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))^{\top}K^{-1}(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))=\left\lVert\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{RKHS}}
;

(d)

Pythagorean lift:
the canonical lift of Definition
4.3
satisfies
R(\bm{\theta})=\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
(anchored ridge);

(e)

Geodesic distance:

d^{2}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}\right)=\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
for every
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
, and the metric gap operator
N(\bm{c})
of Theorem
C.3
vanishes identically;

(f)

NTK Gaussian Process prior:
the Riemannian Gibbs Process of Definition
4.2
reduces to
g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}(g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right),(2\beta)^{-1}K)
, equivalently
f\left(\cdot;\bm{\theta}\right)\sim\mathcal{GP}(f\left(\cdot;\bm{\theta}_{0}\right),(2\beta)^{-1}k_{\mathrm{NTK}})
.

Corollary C.2

(Anchored ridge is the canonical lift in the kernel regime)
.

Under the hypotheses of Proposition
C.10
, geodesic ridge of Proposition
4.1
coincides with anchored ridge:
d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
for every
\bm{\theta}\in U
.

Corollary C.3

(Riemannian Gibbs Process is the NTK Gaussian Process in the kernel regime)
.

Under the hypotheses of Proposition
C.10
, the canonical function-space prior of Definition
4.2
coincides with the NTK Gaussian Process posterior
\mathcal{N}(g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right),(2\beta)^{-1}K)
on the training-output coordinates and pushes forward, under evaluation at any finite test inputs
X_{\mathrm{te}}\subset\mathcal{X}
, to a Gaussian on
\mathcal{Y}^{|X_{\mathrm{te}}|}
with the standard NTK-GP mean and covariance.

C.6
Failure of non-canonical regularisation in the feature-learning regime

The bias of anchored ridge identified by Theorem
3.1
is not specific to the chord
\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
: every
trajectory-blind
quadratic regulariser whose curvature-correcting tensor is independent of training history exhibits the same pathology, and every regulariser that depends only on outputs cannot disambiguate the gauge-fixed representative from any other point on the same fibre.

Proposition C.11

(Static-anchor regularisers bias gradient flow)
.

Let
M\in\mathrm{Sym}^{+}_{m}
and
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be
independent of the gradient-flow trajectory
(functions of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
only). Define
R_{M,\bm{v}}(\bm{\theta}):=(\bm{\theta}-\bm{v})^{\top}M\,(\bm{\theta}-\bm{v})
and
\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{M,\bm{v}}:=\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}}[\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)+\lambda R_{M,\bm{v}}(\bm{\theta})]
. Then
\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{M,\bm{v}}
is characterised by
M(\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{M,\bm{v}}-\bm{v})\in\mathrm{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{M,\bm{v}}\right)^{\top})
and, under Assumption
2.1
, satisfies
\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{M,\bm{v}}=\bm{\theta}^{\star}
if and only if

M\,(\bm{\theta}^{\star}-\bm{v})\in\mathrm{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}^{\star}\right)^{\top}).

In particular, whenever the gradient-flow displacement
\bm{\theta}^{\star}-\bm{\theta}_{0}
has a non-trivial component in
\ker(J\left(\bm{\theta}^{\star}\right))
(the generic feature-learning regime under non-constant
J\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)
), no choice of static
(M,\bm{v})
can satisfy this condition, so every trajectory-blind quadratic biases the gradient-flow limit.

Proposition C.12

(Output-norm regularisers fail to select the gauge-fixed representative)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be continuous with
f(g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))=0
and
f
strictly convex on
C_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
. The pure output-shaped regulariser
R(\bm{\theta}):=f(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
satisfies, for every
\lambda>0
,

\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}}\bigl[\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)+\lambda R(\bm{\theta})\bigr]\;=\;\Theta\left(\bm{c}_{\lambda}\right)\cap U,

i.e. the entire output fibre at the optimal output value
\bm{c}_{\lambda}
is selected. The vanishing-regularisation limit
\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\arg\min
is therefore
\Theta^{\star}\cap U
, not the singleton
\{\bm{\theta}^{\star}\}
. A non-trivial fibre extension is therefore necessary in the canonical lift of Definition
4.3
; pure output-energy is structurally insufficient to select the gradient-flow limit.

Proposition C.13

(anchored ridge lower-bounds the canonical regulariser)
.

For every
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
,

\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\;\leq\;d^{2}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}\right)\;=\;d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{0}\right).

The inequality is an instance of the relaxation principle: anchored ridge is the minimum of
\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}(t)\right\rVert^{2}\,dt
over paths from
\bm{\theta}_{0}
to
\bm{\theta}

without
the horizontal constraint
\dot{\gamma}\in\operatorname{Im}(J\left(\gamma\right)^{\top})
, whereas the canonical regulariser is the minimum of the same functional
with
the horizontal constraint imposed. Dropping a constraint can only decrease the minimum.

Corollary C.4

(Sandwich bound and shared vanishing-regularisation limit)
.

For every
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
reached by a piecewise-
C^{1}
horizontal training path
\gamma_{\mathrm{train}}
from
\bm{\theta}_{0}
,

\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\;\leq\;d^{2}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}\right)\;=\;d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\;\leq\;L^{2}(\gamma_{\mathrm{train}}).

Each of the three regularisers (anchored ridge
\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
, geodesic ridge
d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
, arc ridge
L^{2}(\gamma_{\mathrm{train}})
) attains the same vanishing-regularisation limit when the corresponding regularised objective is minimised under exact gradient flow:
\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\arg\min(\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\cdot\right),Y\right)+\lambda R)=\bm{\theta}^{\star}
for
R\in\{\left\lVert\cdot-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}|_{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)},\,d^{2}\left(\cdot,\bm{\theta}_{0}\right),\,L^{2}(\gamma_{\mathrm{train}})\}
. The three regularisers therefore agree at the gradient-flow limit but disagree away from it; arc ridge is the minimax-optimal point of this sandwich (Proposition
F.2
).

Proposition C.14

(Properness of the Riemannian Gibbs Process)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the partition function of the Riemannian Gibbs Process prior of Definition
4.2
is finite:

Z:=\int_{C_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})}\exp\!\big(-\beta E(\bm{c})\big)\,d\bm{c}\;\leq\;\left(\frac{\pi\,C^{2}}{\beta}\right)^{n/2},

where
C
is the upper Jacobian bound of Assumption
2.1
. The Riemannian Gibbs Process is therefore a proper probability measure on
C_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
.

Proposition C.15

(Kolmogorov consistency over arbitrary finite test inputs)
.

Fix a training set
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
, an initialisation
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, and a temperature
\beta>0
. Define the parameter-space prior
\pi_{\beta}
on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\cap V
by

\pi_{\beta}(d\bm{\theta})\;=\;Z_{\beta}^{-1}\,\exp\!\big(-\beta\,d^{2}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}\right)\big)\,d\mathrm{vol}_{g_{\text{ambient}}}(\bm{\theta}),

where
d\mathrm{vol}_{g_{\text{ambient}}}
is the Riemannian volume measure induced by the ambient Euclidean metric. For any finite test set
X_{\mathrm{te}}=(X_{n+1},\dots,X_{n+\ell})\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, let
\Phi_{X_{\mathrm{te}}}:\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\cap V\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
be the evaluation map
\bm{\theta}\mapsto(f\left(X_{n+1};\bm{\theta}\right),\dots,f\left(X_{n+\ell};\bm{\theta}\right))
. The push-forward family

\mu_{X_{\mathrm{te}}}\;:=\;(\Phi_{X_{\mathrm{te}}})_{\sharp}\pi_{\beta},\qquad X_{\mathrm{te}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\text{ finite},

is Kolmogorov consistent: for any pair
X_{\mathrm{te}}\supseteq X_{\mathrm{te}}^{\prime}
, the marginal of
\mu_{X_{\mathrm{te}}}
on the coordinates indexed by
X_{\mathrm{te}}^{\prime}
equals
\mu_{X_{\mathrm{te}}^{\prime}}
. By the Kolmogorov extension theorem, the family
\{\mu_{X_{\mathrm{te}}}\}
defines a unique stochastic process on
\mathcal{X}
at the fixed training set
(X,\bm{\theta}_{0},\beta)
. (Consistency
across
training subsets remains an open problem.)

Remark C.5

(Status of the Riemannian Gibbs Process as a Bayesian prior)
.

Properness.
Under Assumption

In the kernel regime, where

Kolmogorov consistency.
Verification of Kolmogorov consistency for the projective family

across input subsets remains open; however, the parametric structure buys us consistency over arbitrary finite test inputs at any fixed training set: any parameter prior on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\cap V
— naturally the Riemannian Gibbs measure

yields a Kolmogorov-consistent family of finite-dimensional marginals

,

,

.

D.1
Gradient flow basics

Lemma D.1

(Gradient formula)
.

For any loss with output gradient

Lemma D.2

(Lyapunov decrease)
.

Along any gradient flow solution

Proof.

∎

Lemma D.3

(Critical points are interpolators)
.

Under Assumptions

and A.3,

Lemma D.6

(Trajectory length bound)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
and A.3 (with
\mathcal{L}\left(\cdot,Y\right)

\mu

M

where
\mathcal{L}^{\star}:=\min_{\bm{c}}\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{c},Y\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(Y,Y\right)
and

is a uniform upper bound on the largest singular value of

on

\mu=M=2

.

Proof.

Write
h(\bm{c}):=\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{c},Y\right)
and
r(t):=g\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)-Y
. By A.3,
h
is
\mu
-strongly convex with minimiser
Y
, giving
\left\lVert\nabla h(\bm{c})\right\rVert\geq\mu\left\lVert r\right\rVert
and
\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}^{\star}\geq\tfrac{\mu}{2}\left\lVert r\right\rVert^{2}
;
M

\left\lVert\nabla h(\bm{c})\right\rVert\leq M\left\lVert r\right\rVert

and hence

on its compact closure. Then

∎

D.4
Geometric identities

Lemma D.8

(Infinitesimal orthogonality)
.

At every
\bm{\theta}
with full-rank
J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
,
\operatorname{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top})\perp\ker J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
in the ambient Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\mathbb{R}^{m}=\operatorname{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top})\oplus\ker J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
as an orthogonal decomposition.

Proof.

If
v\in\operatorname{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top})
and
w\in\ker J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
, write
v=J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top}u
; then
v^{\top}w=u^{\top}J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)w=0
. Dimension count:
\dim\operatorname{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top})=\operatorname{rank}J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=n
and
\dim\ker J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=m-n
, summing to
m
.
∎

Lemma D.9

(Geodesic length dominates ambient chord)
.

For any embedded
C^{1}
submanifold
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and any two points
p,q\in M
in the same path component, the intrinsic geodesic distance
d_{M}\left(p,q\right)
(defined as infimum of arc lengths of
C^{1}
curves in
M
) satisfies
d_{M}\left(p,q\right)\geq\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert
.

Proof.

For any
C^{1}
curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
with
\gamma(0)=p
,
\gamma(1)=q
,
\operatorname{Length}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}(t)\right\rVert\,dt\geq\left\lVert\int_{0}^{1}\dot{\gamma}(t)\,dt\right\rVert=\left\lVert q-p\right\rVert
by the triangle inequality for integrals. Taking the infimum over
\gamma
preserves the inequality.
∎

Lemma D.10

(Arc-length endpoint gradient)
.

Let
\gamma:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be piecewise-
C^{1}
with
\dot{\gamma}(T^{-})\neq 0
. Then the arc length
s(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}(t)\right\rVert\,dt
is Fréchet-differentiable with respect to the endpoint
\gamma(T)
, with

\frac{\partial s}{\partial\gamma(T)}\;=\;\frac{\dot{\gamma}(T^{-})}{\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}(T^{-})\right\rVert}.

Equivalently, by the envelope theorem applied to the arc-length functional
[
30
]
: the gradient of the arc length with respect to the endpoint equals the terminal unit tangent vector. For a geodesic, this coincides with the terminal momentum (cotangent vector in the Hamiltonian formulation).

Proof.

Fix
h\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\delta>0
. Consider the comparison path
\tilde{\gamma}
that agrees with
\gamma
on
[0,T-\delta]
and connects
\gamma(T-\delta)
linearly to
\gamma(T)+\varepsilon h
on
[T-\delta,T]
. The arc length of the final segment is

\bigl\|\dot{\gamma}(T^{-})\delta+\varepsilon h\bigr\|\;=\;\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}(T^{-})\right\rVert\delta\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\left\langle\frac{\dot{\gamma}(T^{-})}{\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}(T^{-})\right\rVert^{2}},h\right\rangle+O\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\right)\right)^{1/2},

so
\Delta s=s_{\varepsilon}-s=\varepsilon\langle\dot{\gamma}(T^{-})/\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}(T^{-})\right\rVert,h\rangle+O(\varepsilon^{2}/\delta+\varepsilon\delta)
. Setting
\delta=\sqrt{\varepsilon}
gives
\Delta s=\varepsilon\langle\dot{\gamma}(T^{-})/\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}(T^{-})\right\rVert,h\rangle+O(\varepsilon^{3/2})
, confirming the stated Fréchet derivative.
∎

D.5
Optimisation lemma

Lemma D.11

(Vanishing-regularisation selection principle)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}:\Theta\to[0,\infty)
be continuous with non-empty
\arg\min\mathcal{L}
, and let
R:\Theta\to[0,\infty]
be continuous. Assume that for each
\lambda>0
the regularised problem admits a unique minimiser
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}\in\arg\min(\mathcal{L}+\lambda R)
, and that every sequence
\{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda_{k}}\}
with
\lambda_{k}\downarrow 0
has a convergent subsequence. Then every limit point of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}
as
\lambda\downarrow 0
lies in
\arg\min\mathcal{L}
and, among
\arg\min\mathcal{L}
, minimises
R
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{\bm{\theta}}\in\arg\min\mathcal{L}
and set
\mathcal{L}^{\star}:=\min\mathcal{L}
. By optimality,
\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda})+\lambda R(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda})\leq\mathcal{L}^{\star}+\lambda R(\bar{\bm{\theta}})
, so
\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda})\leq\mathcal{L}^{\star}+\lambda R(\bar{\bm{\theta}})
and
R(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda})\leq(\mathcal{L}^{\star}-\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}))/\lambda+R(\bar{\bm{\theta}})\leq R(\bar{\bm{\theta}})
. Taking
\lambda\downarrow 0
along a convergent subsequence with limit
\bm{\theta}^{\infty}
, continuity yields
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{\infty})\leq\mathcal{L}^{\star}
, so
\bm{\theta}^{\infty}\in\arg\min\mathcal{L}
. Passing to the limit in
R(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda})\leq R(\bar{\bm{\theta}})
gives
R(\bm{\theta}^{\infty})\leq R(\bar{\bm{\theta}})
for every
\bar{\bm{\theta}}\in\arg\min\mathcal{L}
; hence
\bm{\theta}^{\infty}
minimises
R
over
\arg\min\mathcal{L}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

Step 1:
\rho_{K}
is a function of

The group

acts transitively on each level set

, kernel specialisation),
\bm{\theta}^{\star}=\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{lin}}

\bm{\theta}^{\star}
for every

The map

E.4
Proof of Proposition
C.10
(kernel-regime reduction)

Proof of Proposition
C.10
.

Throughout,
J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\equiv J
is constant on
U
and
K=JJ^{\top}\succ 0
.

(a) Affine flow manifold.
Constant
J
makes the parameter dynamics
\dot{\bm{\theta}}=J^{\top}\bm{u}
confined to
\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathrm{Im}(J^{\top})
for any
L^{2}
control
\bm{u}
; hence
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\subseteq\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathrm{Im}(J^{\top})
. The reverse inclusion follows from constant-control reachability: every point
\bm{\theta}_{0}+J^{\top}\bm{a}
is hit by
\bm{u}(t)\equiv\bm{a}/T
at time
T
. The basin sheet
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
then equals the connected component of
\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathrm{Im}(J^{\top})
containing the trajectory, which (by linearity of the GF dynamics on this affine subspace) is the whole subspace intersected with
U
.

(b) Closed-form gauge-fixed representative.
On the affine flow manifold,
g(\bm{\theta})=g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)+J(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0})
. Setting
g(\bm{\theta})=\bm{c}
and requiring
\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\mathrm{Im}(J^{\top})
gives
\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}=J^{\top}\bm{a}
with
JJ^{\top}\bm{a}=K\bm{a}=\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
, so
\bm{a}=K^{-1}(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))
and
g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)=\bm{\theta}_{0}+J^{\top}K^{-1}(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))
.

(c) RKHS canonical energy.
The differential
Dg|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)=J^{\top}K^{-1}
is constant. The pullback metric on
C_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
is therefore
G=(J^{\top}K^{-1})^{\top}(J^{\top}K^{-1})=K^{-1}JJ^{\top}K^{-1}=K^{-1}
, constant in
\bm{c}
. Geodesics of a constant Riemannian metric are constant-speed straight lines (Definition: a curve with vanishing covariant acceleration on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},K^{-1})
is exactly an affine line
[
9
, Ch. 2, Prop. 3.5]
). The constant-speed straight line from
g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
to
\bm{c}
has
G
-energy
\int_{0}^{1}(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))^{\top}K^{-1}(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))\,dt=(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))^{\top}K^{-1}(\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))=\left\lVert\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{RKHS}}
.

(d) Pythagorean lift.
By Lemma
D.8
,
\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}
decomposes orthogonally as
(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)-\bm{\theta}_{0})+(\bm{\theta}-g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right))
with components in
\mathrm{Im}(J^{\top})
and
\ker J
respectively; the Pythagorean identity gives
\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}
. Using (b),
g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)-\bm{\theta}_{0}=J^{\top}K^{-1}(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))
, whose squared Euclidean norm equals
(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))^{\top}K^{-1}(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right))=E(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
from (c). The path-infimum on the fibre
\Theta\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)\cap V
from
g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)
to
\bm{\theta}
is realised by the ambient-Euclidean straight line, of squared length
\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}
. Hence the canonical lift
R(\bm{\theta})=E(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right))+\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
, anchored ridge.

(e) Geodesic distance and vanishing metric gap.
For any
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
, the geodesic from
\bm{\theta}_{0}
to
\bm{\theta}
on the affine
(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right),g_{\mathrm{ambient}})
is the ambient straight line, of squared length
\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
. By Theorem
C.3
, the metric gap operator
N(\bm{c})=(I-P_{g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)})\,Dg|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)
vanishes precisely when
\mathrm{Im}\,Dg|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\subseteq\mathrm{Im}(J^{\top})
. Constant
J
gives
Dg|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)=J^{\top}K^{-1}
, whose image is
\mathrm{Im}(J^{\top})
, so
N\equiv 0
on all of
C_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
.

(f) NTK Gaussian Process prior.
The Riemannian Gibbs measure of Definition
4.2
on
C_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
has density
p(\bm{c})\propto\exp(-\beta\,\left\lVert\bm{c}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\right\rVert^{2}_{K^{-1}})
by (c), which is the Gaussian density
\mathcal{N}(g\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right),(2\beta)^{-1}K)
. The corresponding parametric pushforward through
f(\cdot;\bm{\theta})
at any test inputs
X_{\mathrm{te}}=(X_{n+1},\dots,X_{n+\ell})
is, in the constant-Jacobian regime, the Gaussian
\mathcal{N}\big(f\left(X_{\mathrm{te}};\bm{\theta}_{0}\right),(2\beta)^{-1}\,J_{\mathrm{te}}J^{\top}(JJ^{\top})^{-1}J\,J_{\mathrm{te}}^{\top}\big)
with
J_{\mathrm{te}}
the Jacobian at the test inputs. The covariance kernel
k(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f\left(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)^{\top}\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f\left(\bm{x}^{\prime};\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
pre- and post-conditioned through the training set agrees with the conventional NTK posterior (
37
, Eq. 2.41); collecting all test inputs yields the full Gaussian process
\mathcal{GP}(f\left(\cdot;\bm{\theta}_{0}\right),(2\beta)^{-1}k_{\mathrm{NTK}})
.

E.8
Proof of Theorem
C.3
(metric-gap decomposition)

Proof of Theorem
C.3
.

Write
g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right):=(g|_{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\cap V})^{-1}(\bm{c})
and
A(\bm{c}):=Dg|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)
. Since
g\circ\bm{\theta}^{\prime}=\mathrm{id}
on
C_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
, differentiating gives
J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)\,A(\bm{c})=I_{n}
.

Decomposition.
Let
P_{\bm{\theta}}:=J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top}K\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{-1}J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
, the orthogonal projector onto
\mathrm{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top})
in the ambient inner product (Lemma
D.8
). Decompose

A(\bm{c})=P_{g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)}A(\bm{c})+(I-P_{g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)})A(\bm{c})=:A_{\parallel}(\bm{c})+N(\bm{c}).

Apply
J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)
:
J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)N(\bm{c})=J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)(I-P_{g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)})A(\bm{c})=(J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)-J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right))A(\bm{c})=0
(using
J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)P_{\bm{\theta}}=J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
). Hence
N(\bm{c})
ranges in
\ker J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)
. Also
J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)A_{\parallel}(\bm{c})=J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)A(\bm{c})=I_{n}
, giving
J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)^{\top}K\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)^{-1}J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)A(\bm{c})/K\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)^{-1}=I_{n}
; equivalently
A_{\parallel}(\bm{c})=J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)^{\top}K\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)^{-1}
.

Pythagorean identity.
Since
A_{\parallel}(\bm{c})\in\mathrm{Im}(J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)^{\top})
and
N(\bm{c})\in\ker J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)
, the columns are pairwise ambient-orthogonal (Lemma
D.8
); the cross terms in
A^{\top}A
vanish, giving

G(\bm{c})=A(\bm{c})^{\top}A(\bm{c})=A_{\parallel}(\bm{c})^{\top}A_{\parallel}(\bm{c})+N(\bm{c})^{\top}N(\bm{c})=K\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)^{-1}+N(\bm{c})^{\top}N(\bm{c}),

where the last equality uses
A_{\parallel}^{\top}A_{\parallel}=K\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{-1}J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top}K\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{-1}=K\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{-1}
. Positivity of
N^{\top}N
gives
G\succeq K^{-1}
, with equality iff
N(\bm{c})=0
iff
\mathrm{Im}\,A(\bm{c})\subseteq\mathrm{Im}(J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)^{\top})
, equivalently
T_{g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=\mathrm{Im}(J\left(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)\right)^{\top})
.

Trajectory directions lie in
\ker N
.
Along the realised gradient flow trajectory
\bm{\theta}(t)=\bm{\theta}^{\prime}(\bm{c}(t))
, the velocity
\dot{\bm{\theta}}(t)=-J\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)^{\top}\nabla h(g\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right))\in\mathrm{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)^{\top})
(by gradient flow A.3 and chain rule). Differentiating
g\circ\bm{\theta}^{\prime}=\mathrm{id}
at
\bm{c}(t)
gives
A(\bm{c}(t))\,\dot{\bm{c}}(t)=\dot{\bm{\theta}}(t)\in\mathrm{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)^{\top})
. Decomposing
A\,\dot{\bm{c}}=A_{\parallel}\dot{\bm{c}}+N\dot{\bm{c}}
with
A_{\parallel}\dot{\bm{c}}\in\mathrm{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)^{\top})
and
N\dot{\bm{c}}\in\ker J\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)
, and noting that the sum lies in
\mathrm{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)^{\top})
, the orthogonal decomposition forces
N(\bm{c}(t))\,\dot{\bm{c}}(t)=0
. Hence the trajectory’s
G
-energy reduces to
\dot{\bm{c}}^{\top}A_{\parallel}^{\top}A_{\parallel}\dot{\bm{c}}=\dot{\bm{c}}^{\top}K\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)^{-1}\dot{\bm{c}}
.

In the kernel regime, Proposition

.

Proof of Proposition

(constant-speed optimality)

Proof of Proposition

.

Proof of Theorem

Identifying
\bm{\theta}_{\text{minnorm}}

The bias has a direct geometric interpretation: the anchored ridge regularisation gradient

that pushes parameters along

E.21
Proof of Proposition
C.14
(Riemannian Gibbs Process properness)

Proof of Proposition
C.14
.

By Theorem
C.2
,
g|_{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\cap V}
is a
C^{\infty}
diffeomorphism onto
C_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The pullback metric on
C_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
is
G(\bm{c})=Dg|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)^{\top}Dg|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right)=K(g|^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left(\bm{c}\right))^{-1}+N(\bm{c})^{\top}N(\bm{c})
by Theorem
C.3
. The squared geodesic distance
E(\bm{c})
of Definition
4.1
is the corresponding infimum over piecewise-
C^{1}
paths.

Step 1 (two-sided spectral bound on
G
).
Assumption
2.1
gives
c\leq\sigma_{\min}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right))\leq\sigma_{\max}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right))\leq C
on
V
, hence
c^{2}I_{n}\preceq K(\bm{\theta})\preceq C^{2}I_{n}
and

for

Appendix F
Detailed Analysis of Arc Ridge Regularisation

We provide detailed derivations justifying the squared path-length regulariser
R=L^{2}(\gamma_{\mathrm{train}})=s(T)^{2}
, where
s(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\dot{\bm{\theta}}(t)\right\rVert\,dt
is the cumulative Euclidean arc length of the training path. Throughout, we work in effective-parameter-space (parameters scaled by
\mu P
widths as in Section
4
) and suppress the subscript
eff
for readability.

F.1
Detailed Derivation of the Upper Bound

We expand the proof of Theorem
4.3
and clarify when the bound is tight.

Proposition F.1

(Upper bound via constant-speed optimality, expanded)
.

Let
\gamma:[0,T]\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
be any piecewise-
C^{1}
horizontal path from
\bm{\theta}_{0}
to
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
. Then

d^{2}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}\right)\;\leq\;L^{2}(\gamma)\;=\;\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}(t)\right\rVert\,dt\right)^{2}.

Equality holds if and only if
\gamma
is a geodesic on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
(traversed at any constant or non-constant speed).

Proof.

Step 1: Geodesic distance as infimum of arc lengths.
The geodesic distance
d_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}\right)
equals the infimum of arc lengths
L(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}(t)\right\rVert\,dt
over all piecewise-
C^{1}
horizontal paths
\gamma
from
\bm{\theta}_{0}
to
\bm{\theta}
; this is the standard definition of Riemannian distance with the ambient Euclidean metric restricted to
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
. Therefore, for any admissible path
\gamma
,

d_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}\right)\;\leq\;L(\gamma)\;\leq\;L(\gamma_{\mathrm{train}}),

where the second inequality holds because
\gamma_{\mathrm{train}}
is itself an admissible horizontal path. Squaring both sides gives
d^{2}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}\right)\leq L^{2}(\gamma_{\mathrm{train}})
.

Step 2: Tightness condition.
Equality
d^{2}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}\right)=L^{2}(\gamma)
holds if and only if
L(\gamma)=d_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}\right)
, i.e.
\gamma
is a length-minimising path. By Proposition
C.9
, among all paths of fixed total arc length, the minimum energy
E(\gamma)=\int\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}\right\rVert^{2}\,dt
is achieved by the constant-speed reparametrisation; length-minimising paths are exactly the geodesics, regardless of their time-parametrisation. Since
L^{2}(\gamma)=(\text{arc length})^{2}
depends only on the set-theoretic image of
\gamma
, equality holds for any time-parametrisation of a geodesic.

Step 3: Inclusion of the fibre term.
When
\bm{\theta}(T)
lies exactly on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
(as guaranteed by Corollary
C.1
under exact gradient flow), the canonical regulariser satisfies
d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta}(T),\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=d^{2}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}(T)\right)
, and the bound applies directly. In the discrete-step setting,
\bm{\theta}(T)
may deviate slightly off the manifold, contributing a non-negative fibre term
d^{2}_{\mathrm{fibre}}\left(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}(g\left(\bm{\theta}(T)\right)),\bm{\theta}(T)\right)
; the path length still upper-bounds the total canonical regulariser since it upper-bounds the flow component alone.
∎

F.2
Minimax Robust Motivation

Propositions
C.13
and
F.1
establish the sandwich

\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\;\leq\;d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\;\leq\;L^{2}(\gamma_{\mathrm{train}})\;=:\;s(T)^{2},

which holds for all
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
at any time
T
. The canonical regulariser
d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
is intractable, but at each training step
t
the accumulated path length
s(t)
is known exactly.

Proposition F.2

(Minimax robust regulariser)
.

Fix
\lambda>0
and let the unknown regulariser value
r^{\star}=d^{2}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
be known only to lie in the interval
[r_{\mathrm{lo}},r_{\mathrm{hi}}]=[\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2},\,s(T)^{2}]
(from the sandwich). The solution to the minimax problem

\min_{\bm{\theta}}\max_{r\in[r_{\mathrm{lo}},\,r_{\mathrm{hi}}]}\bigl[\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)+\lambda r\bigr]

uses the worst-case regulariser
r=r_{\mathrm{hi}}=s(T)^{2}
and is therefore equivalent to minimising the path-length regularised objective
\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)+\lambda s(T)^{2}
. Squared path length is thus the minimax-optimal practical regulariser within the sandwich.

Proof.

For any fixed
\bm{\theta}
, the map
r\mapsto\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)+\lambda r
is linear and strictly increasing in
r
(since
\lambda>0
). Therefore,
\max_{r\in[r_{\mathrm{lo}},r_{\mathrm{hi}}]}[\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)+\lambda r]=\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)+\lambda r_{\mathrm{hi}}=\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)+\lambda s(T)^{2}
, attained at
r=s(T)^{2}
. Minimising over
\bm{\theta}
gives the stated equivalence.
∎

The choice
\lambda=\sigma^{2}/n
—where
\sigma^{2}
is the observation noise variance and
n
is the number of training points—has a Bayesian interpretation as the ratio of the likelihood precision to the prior precision in MAP inference. Under a Gaussian likelihood with noise
\sigma
(i.e., the SSE setting of A.3 paired with
\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)
as
-\log p(Y|g\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
up to constants), this
\lambda
makes the regularisation gradient dimensionally consistent with the data gradient (both scale as
1/n
in the large-data limit), and the stopping criterion derived in Section
F.4
below requires only an estimate of
\sigma^{2}
rather than a validation set. For other strictly convex losses, the analogous coupling reads
\lambda\propto\beta/(\text{likelihood precision})
, with the prior scale
\beta
of Definition
4.1
substituted for the Gaussian-specific factor
1/(2\sigma^{2})
.

F.3
Gradient Under Standard Gradient Flow

We derive the regularisation gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}s(T)^{2}
in the case of unmodified gradient flow.

Proposition F.3

(Path-length regularisation gradient)
.

Under gradient flow
\dot{\bm{\theta}}(t)=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)
, the gradient of the squared path-length regulariser
R(T)=s(T)^{2}
with respect to the endpoint
\bm{\theta}(T)
is

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}(T)}R\;=\;2s(T)\cdot\frac{\dot{\bm{\theta}}(T)}{\left\lVert\dot{\bm{\theta}}(T)\right\rVert}\;=\;-\frac{2s(T)}{\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\right\rVert}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\Big|_{\bm{\theta}(T)}.

This gradient lies in
\operatorname{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}(T)\right)^{\top})
, has magnitude
2s(T)
, and is anti-parallel to the data gradient.

Proof.

Write
s(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\dot{\bm{\theta}}(t)\right\rVert\,dt
. By Lemma
D.10
,
\partial s/\partial\bm{\theta}(T)=\dot{\bm{\theta}}(T)/\left\lVert\dot{\bm{\theta}}(T)\right\rVert
, the unit tangent at the endpoint. Applying the chain rule:
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}(T)}R=2s(T)\cdot\partial s/\partial\bm{\theta}(T)=2s(T)\,\dot{\bm{\theta}}(T)/\left\lVert\dot{\bm{\theta}}(T)\right\rVert
. Under gradient flow
\dot{\bm{\theta}}=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}
, so
\dot{\bm{\theta}}/\left\lVert\dot{\bm{\theta}}\right\rVert=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}/\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\right\rVert
. Since gradient flow satisfies
\dot{\bm{\theta}}=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}=J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top}\bm{u}
for some
\bm{u}
(Lemma
D.1
), we have
\dot{\bm{\theta}}\in\operatorname{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top})
; hence the gradient lies in
\operatorname{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top})=T_{\bm{\theta}(T)}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{flow}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
and has no fibre component, as claimed by Theorem
4.3
.
∎

Two properties of this gradient deserve emphasis:

•

Monotonically increasing magnitude
:
s(T)
grows throughout training, so
\left\lVert\nabla R\right\rVert=2s(T)
increases over time regardless of whether the loss is decreasing.

•

No fibre bias
:
\nabla R\in\operatorname{Im}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top})
, so regularisation by
\lambda R
introduces no gradient component in
\ker(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
; it does not push
\bm{\theta}
off the flow manifold.

F.4
Formal Equivalence to Early Stopping Under Gradient Flow

Proposition F.4

(Path-length regularisation is equivalent to early stopping)
.

Assume additionally that
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,Y)
is strictly convex in model outputs (i.e.
\bm{c}\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\bm{c},Y)
is strictly convex on
\mathcal{Y}^{n}
; this holds for all standard losses including SSE, softmax cross-entropy, and logistic loss). Under gradient flow with regularised objective
\mathcal{L}\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}\right),Y\right)+\lambda R
, where
R=s(T)^{2}
, the training trajectory
\{\bm{\theta}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is identical (as a set-valued path in parameter-space) to the unregularised gradient flow trajectory. (In particular,
s(T)
is the arc length of the path-image, which depends only on the path geometry and not on its time parametrisation; it is therefore the same quantity whether computed along the regularised or unregularised trajectory.) The dynamics converge at the unique stopping time
T^{\star}
satisfying

2\lambda s(T^{\star})=\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\bigl(\bm{\theta}(T^{\star})\bigr)\right\rVert,

(1)

at which the total gradient is zero. Moreover,
T^{\star}<\infty
whenever
\lambda>0
.

Proof.

By Proposition
F.3
, the total regularised gradient is

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bigl[\mathcal{L}+\lambda R\bigr]\;=\;\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}-\frac{2\lambda s(T)}{\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\right\rVert}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\;=\;\left(1-\frac{2\lambda s(T)}{\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\right\rVert}\right)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}.

As long as
2\lambda s(t)<\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}(t))\right\rVert
, the factor in parentheses is positive, so
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}[\mathcal{L}+\lambda R]
is a positive scalar multiple of
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}
. Gradient flow in the direction
-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}[\mathcal{L}+\lambda R]
is therefore identical in direction (though not in speed) to unregularised gradient flow in direction
-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}
; the trajectory is the same path.

To show
T^{\star}
exists and is finite:
s(t)
is non-decreasing and tends to
\infty
along a trajectory that does not converge (since
\left\lVert\dot{\bm{\theta}}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\right\rVert
is bounded away from zero until convergence). By A.3,
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})=0
if and only if
g\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=Y
(Lemma
D.3
); under Assumption
2.1
, the Grönwall bound in Lemma
D.6
gives
\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}(t))\right\rVert=\left\lVert J\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right)^{\top}\nabla h(g\left(\bm{\theta}(t)\right))\right\rVert\leq\sigma_{\max}M\left\lVert r(t)\right\rVert\to 0
exponentially (with
h:=\mathcal{L}\left(\cdot,Y\right)
). Therefore the continuous function
h(t)=2\lambda s(t)-\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}(t))\right\rVert
satisfies
h(0)<0
and
h(t)\to+\infty
; by the intermediate value theorem there exists
T^{\star}
with
h(T^{\star})=0
, i.e. condition (
1
) holds.
∎

Comparison with classical early stopping.

Proposition
F.4
shows that path-length regularisation is a
continuous-time, gradient-based
analogue of early stopping:

(i)

Same trajectory
: the model visits the same sequence of parameter values as unregularised training, only stopping earlier.

(ii)

No validation set
: the stopping condition (
1
) depends only on training quantities—the cumulative arc length
s(T)
, the current data-loss gradient magnitude, and
\lambda
. Classical early stopping requires a held-out validation set.

(iii)

Interpretable via noise
: setting
\lambda=\sigma^{2}/n
links the stopping criterion to the observation noise
\sigma^{2}
: training stops at
T^{\star}
with
s(T^{\star})=\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}(T^{\star}))\right\rVert\cdot n/(2\sigma^{2})
. An estimate of
\sigma^{2}
from the likelihood model or held-out noise is sufficient.

(iv)

Grokking avoidance
: the path length
s(t)
is monotone; as training approaches the interpolating manifold,
\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\right\rVert\to 0
while
s
continues to grow, ensuring that
T^{\star}
lies strictly before full interpolation for any
\lambda>0
.

We report test performance from the designated evaluation dataset

Yelp Review

For this experiment, we use the DistilBERT
[
39
]
encoder base architecture, again with a MuReadout regression head using default Pytorch Linear initialisation (kaiming uniform). We transfer learn from pretraining again with with no weight freezing, ensuring
\mu P
via per-layer learning rate scaling. We train each regulariser setup over 2 seeds (due to excess runtime) over a number of epochs determined using the patience mechanism, that is, we train for a base 20 epochs, then subsequently whenever the final epoch exhibits the best validation loss we train for a further three epochs, again due to the excessive runtime preventing substantial overtraining. We again train with a base learning rate of 0.003 (this time decreasing to 0.0001 for the largest regularisation strength) and momentum of 0.9. We further process the training dataset by first transforming it to a valid regression target by normalising the scores (1 star to 5 star) to the interval [0, 1] by affine transformation, transforming to the reals via logit transform (clipping scores at 0.01 and 0.99 to prevent infinite targets), then corruption by with a per-datapoint fixed (for a given training run) additive isotropic 0-mean Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 0.1 (unitless).

Appendix H
Empirical Verification of Assumption
2.1

We probe Assumption
2.1
(uniform Jacobian conditioning) directly along gradient-flow trajectories. Recall the assumption claims the existence of constants
0<c\leq C<\infty
such that
c\leq\sigma_{\min}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
and
\sigma_{\max}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right))\leq C
for all
\bm{\theta}\in U
, with
U
an open neighbourhood of
\Theta^{\star}
traversed by the trajectory. Equivalently, the NTK Gram
K\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)J\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top}
has
\sigma_{\min}^{2}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right))\leq\lambda_{\min}(K\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
and
\lambda_{\max}(K\left(\bm{\theta}\right))\leq\sigma_{\max}^{2}(J\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
, so monitoring
\lambda_{\min}(K\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
and
\lambda_{\max}(K\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
along training is a direct empirical proxy.

Setup.

We train a
\mu P
-parameterised MLP with two hidden layers and GELU activations
[
45
,
49
]
on the 1-D regression toy dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{16}
with
x_{i}\in[-1,1]
uniformly spaced,
y_{i}=\sin(\tfrac{\pi}{2}x_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
for
\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
with
\sigma
small. Training uses full-batch gradient descent (a discrete-time approximation of gradient flow) with no regularisation (Ridgeless), at hidden widths
d_{\mathrm{model}}\in\{64,256,1024,4096,16384\}
across multiple seeds. At each epoch we record
\lambda_{\min}(K\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
,
\lambda_{\max}(K\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
, and the training loss; epochwise quantities are aggregated across seeds with shaded confidence regions denoting standard error.

Results.

Figure
4
shows the three quantities. Two qualitative observations support Assumption
2.1
:

(i)

the minimum NTK eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}(K\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
remains bounded away from
0
throughout training, with stable properties across width and growth to a stable maximum across training;

(ii)

the maximum NTK eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}(K\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
remains finite throughout training, with width-dependent magnitude that stabilises as
d_{\mathrm{model}}\to\infty
;

(iii)

the training loss decreases monotonically, consistent with exponential convergence of gradient flow to an interpolator under Assumption
2.1
(Proposition
C.1
).

The combination of (i) a strict positive lower bound on
\lambda_{\min}(K\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
along the trajectory and (ii) a uniform upper bound on
\lambda_{\max}(K\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
is exactly the spectral condition required by Assumption
2.1
; (iii) verifies the trajectory remains in
U
until convergence. Together these provide preliminary empirical evidence that Assumption
2.1
holds across widths in the practical
\mu P
regime, complementing the theoretical justification in Appendix
A
(A.4) and Remark
A.1
. A more comprehensive verification across architectures and tasks remains a natural avenue for follow-up work.
