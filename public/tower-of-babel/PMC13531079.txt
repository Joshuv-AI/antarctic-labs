Percutaneous Retrieval of a Migrated Iliac Vein Stent Traversing the Tricuspid Valve

History of Presentation

A 38-year-old patient with a history of venous insufficiency presented to an outside emergency department 1 day after undergoing left common iliac vein (LCIV) stenting with an 18 × 80–mm self-expanding venous stent. The patient reported new-onset palpitations, dyspnea, and presyncopal symptoms. On examination, she was afebrile and hemodynamically stable (blood pressure 122/74 mm Hg; heart rate 78 beats/min; oxygen saturation 96% on room air; respiratory rate 22 breaths/min). Telemetry demonstrated frequent premature ventricular complexes and short runs of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia.Take-Home Messages•Intracardiac venous stent migration, though rare, may present with arrhythmia or acute tricuspid regurgitation.•A multidisciplinary percutaneous approach can achieve safe, complete retrieval and prevent the need for open surgery.•Early transition to a dual-snare technique with deflectable sheaths should be considered when a single-snare attempt risks injury to the tricuspid apparatus.

Past Medical History

The patient’s history included chronic venous insufficiency status post multiple interventions (bilateral great saphenous vein radiofrequency ablation, left anterior accessory saphenous vein polidocanol injectable foam (Boston Scientific Corp), and right short saphenous ablation) and a provoked pulmonary embolism after cesarean delivery.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis after recent iliac vein stenting included, but was not limited to, stent thrombosis with pulmonary embolism or embolization of the stent itself.

Investigations

Chest radiography revealed a metallic density overlying the cardiac silhouette (Figure 1), concerning for an embolized stent. Computed tomography angiography confirmed a metallic stent extending across the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle (RV) without pericardial effusion. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated the stent traversing the tricuspid apparatus and revealed severe tricuspid regurgitation with preserved biventricular systolic function (Figure 2, Video 1).Figure 1Chest RadiographChest radiograph demonstrating intracardiac migration of the iliac venous stent.Figure 2Transthoracic Echocardiogram(A and B) Transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrating the migrated iliac venous stent extending from the right atrium to the right ventricle, traversing the tricuspid valve, with color Doppler demonstrating severe tricuspid regurgitation. Management

After a multidisciplinary heart team discussion, we opted to attempt percutaneous stent retrieval to avoid a sternotomy in a young patient. Alternatives included, but were not limited to, introducing a transcatheter heart valve within the stent, though this would not address tricuspid regurgitation outside the stent, and may worsen ectopy. If only partial retrieval were feasible, the residual stent could be secured (“jailed”) within the inferior vena cava using a larger stent or via femoral cutdown. Finally, open surgical extraction via sternotomy was also considered. The patient’s nonsustained ventricular tachycardia was treated with β-blockers, allowing more time for procedural planning.

Ultrasound-guided venous access was obtained via bilateral femoral and jugular veins under general anesthesia. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated the TTE findings (Figure 3, Videos 2 to 4).Figure 3Transesophageal Echocardiograms(A to D) Transesophageal echocardiograms demonstrating the migrated iliac venous stent and severe tricuspid regurgitation.

Using fluoroscopic and TEE guidance, the right atrial end of the migrated stent was snared via the right common femoral vein using a 12-F deflectable FlexCath Advantage steerable sheath (Medtronic) with a 35-mm Goose Neck snare (Medtronic) (Video 5). Of note, a basket snare compatible with a 12-F sheath was not compatible with a 12-F deflectable sheath. A JR4 diagnostic catheter (Medtronic) within the deflectable sheath improved the ability to navigate the snare within the right atrium (RA). Stent orientation and its spatial relationship to the tricuspid valve apparatus were determined using a combination of fluoroscopy and real-time 3-dimensional TEE. TEE allowed direct visualization of how the stent was seated across the tricuspid annulus, its proximity to the leaflets and posterior commissure, and the degree of subvalvular involvement. Rapid ventricular pacing was not required, as the stent remained relatively stable without significant cyclic displacement during the cardiac cycle. Without using significant force for fear of disrupting the tricuspid apparatus or causing a pericardial effusion, an attempt was made to retract the migrated stent into the inferior vena cava–cavoatrial junction with a single snare; however, the acute stent-traction angle resulted in posterior commissural displacement of the stent (Videos 6 and 7, Supplemental Figure 1).

A 17-F Agilis NxT steerable sheath (Abbott EP) was then introduced via left femoral venous access. Using TEE guidance, the deflectable sheath was safely navigated around the stent and through the tricuspid valve, avoiding the subvalvular apparatus. TEE guided a dual-snare technique, facilitating snaring the desired stent tine in the RA and in the RV, achieving the most favorable traction vector with minimal tension on the tricuspid valve (Video 8, Supplemental Figure 2). Therefore, we were able to slide out of the posterior commissure without any interaction with the tricuspid subvalvular apparatus (Figure 4). The stent was then ensheathed within a 26-F GORE DrySeal (W.L. Gore & Associates) introducer and removed (Figure 5). We performed intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) of the left iliofemoral vein to understand the mechanism of stent migration and the risks and benefits of repeat stenting. IVUS revealed asymmetric narrowing of the LCIV consistent with possible May-Thurner syndrome. Although the stent was oversized to the minimal luminal diameter, we postulated that the focality of the narrowing (short landing zone), along with the degree of distensibility without obvious fibrosis in the LCIV, may have led to stent migration.

Postretrieval TEE confirmed that there was no significant injury to the tricuspid valve apparatus and only mild residual tricuspid regurgitation (Figure 7). No angiographic complication was visualized on completion venography. Hemostasis was achieved using suture-mediated closure devices and figure-of-eight reinforcement. Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was extubated immediately after the procedure. Repeat TTE the following day demonstrated normal right ventricular function, with only trace to mild tricuspid regurgitation. The patient was discharged home the next day in stable condition.

Discussion

Intracardiac venous stent migration is an extremely rare but potentially catastrophic complication.1 Migration to the RA or RV may precipitate acute valvular dysfunction, arrhythmia, or perforation. A systematic review spanning 25 years identified only 52 reported venous stent migration events in total, most of which involved noncardiac locations or smaller-caliber stents.1,2 Among those, successful endovascular retrieval from the heart was uncommon, and none were described once the stent had traversed the tricuspid annulus, causing significant tricuspid regurgitation. Given the rarity of this complication, current societal guidelines and position statements do not provide specific recommendations for the management of intracardiac venous stent migration, reflecting the paucity of evidence and collective clinical inexperience worldwide.

Historically, open surgical extraction is considered definitive treatment, but with advances in transcatheter technologies, percutaneous retrieval is a feasible alternative in carefully selected cases by an experienced interventional, surgical, and imaging team.

Procedural planning should include surgical standby, multimodality imaging, and readiness to manage acute valvulopathy or perforation. In this case, TEE guidance facilitated a dual-snare strategy to extract the stent en bloc while minimizing manipulation within the tricuspid apparatus. Large-bore sheaths enabled controlled vascular removal. The success of this complex retrieval underscores the importance of seamless collaboration between interventional, imaging, anesthesia, and surgical teams.

The use of IVUS contributed meaningfully at multiple stages of planning. First, in cases where complete percutaneous retrieval appears uncertain, IVUS allows sizing of the inferior vena cava to determine whether a strategy of stent-jailing the migrated stent may be feasible. Second, after successful retrieval, IVUS evaluation of the native iliac vein may characterize true luminal dimensions, wall morphology (distensibility vs fibrosis), residual extrinsic compression, informing the mechanism of stent migration, and whether and how restenting could be approached.

Although it is not unreasonable to attempt a single-snare technique of the proximal edge of the stent alone, our case highlights the importance of quickly changing the strategy (dual-snare technique) when the stent is not easily removed before damaging the tricuspid valve, RA, or RV and when multimodal imaging suggests that the stent is not likely to release without damaging surrounding structures. In addition, the use of deflectable sheaths—instead of standard sheaths—and angled catheters significantly improved our ability to accurately navigate and reach the stent within the RA, as well as manipulate catheters around the stent without interacting with the tricuspid apparatus and retroflex the tip of the sheath around the stent and snare the desired tine to pull the stent in a manner that facilitated retrieval and avoided damaging the tricuspid apparatus. For example, TEE demonstrated that the stent was wedged into the posteroseptal commissure of the tricuspid valve. With our combination of deflectable sheaths and catheters, we were able to snare the desired tine to guide the stent out of the commissure without damaging the tricuspid apparatus. Snaring the stent at the proximal and distal ends also improved coaxial alignment for successful ensheathing of the stent.

Conclusions

Percutaneous retrieval of a migrated iliac venous stent traversing the tricuspid valve is a feasible alternative in carefully selected cases by an experienced interventional, surgical, and imaging team. Careful access planning, TEE guidance, and a dual-snare technique allowed complete removal while preserving valvular function.
