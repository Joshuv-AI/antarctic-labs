Comparative efficacy of an 8-week core stability program versus foot-ankle strengthening on pain, function, and distal structural parameters in individuals with knee osteoarthritis: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Shaikh Nabi Bukhsh Nazir, Amna Asim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is a common musculoskeletal condition linked to aberrant joint loading, pain, and reduced function. While effective rehabilitation should target both local knee problems and the entire kinetic chain, direct comparisons between proximal (core stability) and distal (foot-ankle) interventions are limited. This randomized controlled trial evaluated the efficacy of an 8-week core stability exercise (CSE) program versus foot-ankle strengthening (FAS) in individuals with knee OA. One hundred participants (aged ≥ 40 years, Kellgren-Lawrence grade II–III) were randomly assigned to either CSE (n = 50) or FAS (n = 50). Both groups performed conventional knee exercises three times weekly for 8 weeks, supplemented by their respective group-specific training. The primary outcome was resting pain intensity evaluated via a visual analogue scale (VAS). Secondary outcomes included the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) subscales, functional performance tests (40-m fast-paced walk, 30-second chair stand, and 11-step stair climb), and distal structural measures (Achilles tendon thickness and navicular/foot ratio). Assessments were conducted at baseline and after 8 weeks, with between-group differences analyzed using ANCOVA adjusted for baseline values. The CSE group showed significantly greater improvements across all outcomes compared to the FAS group. Between-group differences exceeded validated minimal clinically important differences (MCID) for resting pain and all KOOS subscales, met MCID thresholds for the walk and chair-stand tests, and safely surpassed minimal detectable changes (MDC) for the stair-climb test and distal structural parameters; large effect sizes favored core stability training. These findings indicate that proximal core stabilization added to conventional knee exercises provides superior short-term improvements in functional mobility and distal structural surrogates compared to foot-ankle strengthening combined with conventional knee exercises in knee OA rehabilitation. Crucially, these static distal structural modifications are exploratory, likely reflecting localized soft-tissue fluid shifts rather than permanent anatomical remodeling, and demand future validation via advanced diagnostic imaging.Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov ID= NCT06766877 (registered 9 January 2025).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-64385-z.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent musculoskeletal disorder that contributes to chronic pain, functional impairment, and diminished quality of life1. The condition arises from a combination of aberrant joint loading, compensatory movement strategies, and degenerative changes, highlighting the need for a multifaceted rehabilitation approach that addresses both local impairments and kinetic chain dysfunction2. Although abnormal joint mechanics influence structural disease progression, contemporary models recognize knee OA as a complex, multifactorial disorder3. Joint degradation is closely linked to localized low-grade chronic inflammation, progressive periarticular muscle weakness, and altered neurophysiological pain processing4. Notably, loading alterations do not occur in isolation; they interact with distinct inter-joint coordination variations that together shape a patient’s overall pain severity and functional limitations5. Accordingly, a comprehensive rehabilitation strategy must employ targeted physical exercises to simultaneously address these interconnected muscular, functional, and biomechanical pathways6.

A growing body of evidence highlights the importance of considering not only the knee itself but also the biomechanical contributions of both the proximal and distal structures6,7.The lumbo-pelvic-hip complex acts as a proximal stabilizer that is central to the transmission of forces and dynamic positioning during weight-bearing activities8,9. Weakness or delayed activation of key muscles (e.g., Gluteus Medius) can increase the knee valgus moments and medial compartment stress, which are implicated in the development of knee OA10,11.Core stability exercises that involve the deep abdominal and paraspinal muscles enhance the stability of the intra-abdominal pressure, reduce compensatory pelvic tilting, and promote optimal patellofemoral tracking during dynamic activities, such as ascending stairs or sit-to-stand activities12–14.

Distally, the foot-ankle complex functions as a mobile adaptor and rigid gait lever, and abnormalities, such as excessive foot pronation, often due to weak intrinsic foot muscles, result in excessive tibial internal rotation and dynamic knee valgus15–18. Plantarflexor strengthening has been shown to support eccentric control during mid-stance, thereby reducing the peak of ground reaction forces and enhancing shock absorption19–21.Additionally, targeted intrinsic foot exercises support the medial longitudinal arch, stabilize the Windlass mechanism during terminal stance, and subsequently can reduce compensatory tibial rotation and medial knee stress22–24. A decrease in Achilles tendon thickness may have been associated with improved midfoot alignment and ankle range of motion24. The theoretical framework for evaluating Achilles tendon thickness (ATT) as a secondary endpoint extends on established kinetic chain associations connecting proximal alignment to distal anthropometric metrics as a means to reduce load on medial tibiofemoral Knee OA24–26.Altered lumbo-pelvic positioning can influence lower-extremity tracking and load distribution, downstream affecting the foot-ankle complex25,26. Because ATT correlates cross-sectionally with foot alignment (navicular/foot ratio) and localized ankle mobility in knee osteoarthritis cohorts, macrostructural monitoring via calipers provides a cost-effective surrogate to track multi-joint kinetic adjustments24. This structural endpoint serves to test whether modifying the proximal foundation yields concurrent anthropometric shifts in downstream soft-tissue complexes.

Although proximal (core/lumbo-pelvic) and distal (foot-ankle) interventions each have theoretical support and have been studied separately in individuals with knee osteoarthritis25,26, direct head-to-head comparisons within a standardized framework remain scarce, especially those that evaluate structural outcomes such as Achilles tendon thickness and medial longitudinal arch integrity.

To address this gap, the present study conducted a randomized controlled trial comparing two 24-session supervised exercise programs (core stability exercises versus foot-ankle strengthening exercises) both added to the same conventional knee exercise program in individuals with knee OA. While proximal and distal interventions have been explored independently or across heterogeneous rehabilitation designs, there remains a paucity of clinical trials directly evaluating the effects of proximal core stability versus distal foot-ankle strengthening within a singular, standardized conventional knee exercise framework while simultaneously assessing patient-reported outcomes, functional performance, and specific distal structural parameters such as Achilles tendon morphology and navicular/foot ratio. The primary outcome was VAS pain intensity, selected because it provides a highly responsive, distinct measurement of resting pain intensity that is sensitive to short-term changes over an 8-week period without the confounding influence of functional tasks. Secondary outcomes included the multidimensional KOOS subscales to track activity-induced symptoms, functional performance measures, and structural parameters (Achilles tendon thickness and navicular/foot ratio). This trial is likely to inform general clinical priority within orthopedic physical therapy by providing empirical evidence on the comparative efficacy of proximal versus distal exercise components when added to a standard knee rehabilitation framework. However, because participants were not stratified by specific baseline deficits—such as core weakness, flatfoot posture, or hip strength—these findings reflect group-level averages and do not guide individualized prescription for specific biomechanical phenotypes.

Methodology

This was a prospective, assessor-blinded, two-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted in an outpatient orthopedic physical therapy clinic. The study adhered to the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines and was registered prospectively (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06766877; registry record available at https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06766877)27. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Baqai Medical University (IRB No: BMU-EC/2–2023/02) on June 3, 2023. Initial preliminary pilot data collection commenced in July 2023 to test protocol feasibility; however, the definitive, double-arm RCT protocol, including the outcomes measures, was formally registered and prospectively locked on January 9, 2025, prior to enrolling the first participant of the definitive cohort. No pilot data was integrated into the final analysis. Written informed consent was provided by all participants.

Participants

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were: participants were aged 40 years or older, diagnosed with knee OA per the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) clinical criteria, and presented with predominantly medial compartment tibiofemoral involvement classified as Kellgren-Lawrence (K/L) grade II or III on radiological assessment. Participants were recruited through a standard procedure following direct diagnostic referral from physiatrists, orthopedic surgeons, and general physicians The study sample encompassed individuals with both unilateral and bilateral knee osteoarthritis. For participants presenting with bilateral involvement, the most symptomatic knee—defined as the knee with the higher self-reported baseline pain intensity—was selected as the index knee for all subsequent clinical and structural evaluations. The study excluded people with post-traumatic osteoarthritis, lower limb sensorimotor dysfunction, uncontrolled blood pressure, malaise, weight loss, fever, lumbar spine pathology with radicular lower limb pain (sciatica), mechanical low back pain causing functional limitations, a history of spinal surgery, the use of assistive walking devices, a baseline reading of less than 4.0 cm on the 0–10 cm resting VAS score, and severe joint deformity. Participants with a BMI greater than 30 kg/m² were excluded to minimize confounding mechanical loading variations during the gross macroscopic tracking of Achilles tendon thickness and foot alignment metrics, as excessive adipose tissue can introduce substantial measurement artifacts on digital caliper and manual arch assessments.

Recruitment and settings

Participants were recruited through direct referrals and from outpatient departments of Baqai Medical University and Rabia Moon Hospital. The first participant of the definitive, prospectively locked trial cohort was enrolled on January 10, 2025. Recruitment continued until February 15, 2026, when the last participant was randomized. All participants completed the 8-week intervention, and the final follow-up assessment took place on April 30, 2026, at the Physiotherapy Department, Baqai Medical University, Karachi, Pakistan.

Intervention protocols

Interventions

The 8-week intervention included a standardized conventional knee exercise program (warm-up, dynamic mobilization, and isometric strengthening) Table 1, with additional group-specific components: core stability exercises in one group (Table 2) and foot-ankle strengthening exercises in the other (Table 3), following established protocols for knee osteoarthritis rehabilitation and foot-ankle training in lower limb conditions25,26,28.

Sessions were conducted three times per week on alternate days, each lasting approximately 40 min. TiDier checklist was used to report the interventions in this study29. To maximize dosage equivalence across both study arms, total therapist contact time was strictly matched at 120 min per week per participant, and both groups dedicated the initial 15–20 min of every session to the exact same multi-joint conventional knee exercise protocol (Table 1). The baseline parameters of this shared conventional program remained entirely unchanged in isometric strengthening exercises during weeks 4–8. While repetitions and sets were progressively built up during the initial 3 weeks to establish a baseline tolerance, no further progression or changes in intensity, volume, or contraction duration were introduced for the conventional exercises from week 4 to week 8. Instead, training loads were kept strictly static to ensure that all cumulative progression stresses were isolated solely within the group-specific training variables. Group-specific progression loads were standardized via automated criteria, allowing an identical volume ceiling increase of up to 20% in hold duration or repetitions each week, provided no biomechanical compensation or pain flare-ups occurred. However, session-by-session subjective intensity via Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scales was not formally quantified, and the groups differed inherently in functional multi-joint task exposure due to the distinct anatomical nature of the interventions. Exercises were standardized for intensity, resistance, repetitions, sets, duration, and progression (Tables 1, 2 and 3; Appendix A, Tables A1–A4). To maximize treatment consistency and eliminate inter-operator variability, all exercise sessions were delivered and supervised exclusively by a single clinician—the Principal Investigator (PI). The PI is a senior physical therapist with 10 years of extensive experience spanning clinical hospital practice and academic orthopedic physical therapy teaching. This single-provider design structurally eliminated potential threats to internal validity arising from inter-therapist protocol drift or variation in delivery style. Protocol standardization and treatment fidelity were strictly maintained in real time using session-by-session compliance checklists to verify, monitor, and log the exact execution of all prescribed sets, repetitions, and progression steps for every participant.

Group-specific protocols

CSE (Core Stability Group): Performed the conventional knee exercise program plus core stability exercises targeting the transverse abdominis, lumbar multifidus, rectus abdominis, external oblique, gluteus maximus, gluteus medius, and vastus medialis obliquus26–28. The program included abdominal drawing-in maneuver (maintained throughout), back bridge, quadruped arm lift, lateral step-up/step-down, unilateral bridge, and side bridge (isotonic and isometric variants). Exercises were individualized and progressed weekly based on tolerance and absence of compensation (up to 20% increase in repetitions/hold time or addition of one set)26–28. All unilateral exercises were performed bilaterally.

FAS (Foot-Ankle Group): Performed the conventional knee exercise program plus an 8-week foot-ankle strengthening protocol adapted from Dantas et al. study25. The program was divided into warm-up and cool-down (all weeks), isolated strengthening of intrinsic and extrinsic foot-ankle muscles (weeks 1–4), and functional strengthening with gait training (weeks 5–8)25. Exercises included elastic-band resisted ankle movements, toe grasping and abduction tasks, short-foot exercises, plantar arch raises, forefoot stance, and gait variations with open/closed toes. Progression was individualized according to performance, tolerance, and absence of compensatory patterns (increasing band resistance, object difficulty, or hold duration)25.

All exercises were standardized to ensure safety, adherence, and reproducibility25,30, while participants were requested to maintain their usual daily activities and avoid initiating new treatments or exercise programs during the study period.

Safety and adverse event monitoring

Adverse events (AEs) were monitored throughout the 8-week trial period in compliance with CONSORT-Harms guidelines. A non-serious AE was defined as any unfavorable, unintended symptom, sign, or transient muscle/joint soreness that emerged or worsened during the trial but did not require advanced medical intervention or disrupt the protocol. A Serious Adverse Event (SAE) was explicitly defined as any untoward medical occurrence that resulted in death, was life-threatening, required inpatient hospitalization or prolongation of existing hospitalization, or resulted in persistent or significant disability.

Throughout the trial, all participants were actively monitored by the attending physical therapist during every training circuit and were verbally screened at the start of each session regarding their recovery and physical status. AEs were solicited proactively at the beginning of each of the 24 supervised sessions using a standardized, open-ended script: ‘Have you experienced any new pain, swelling, or health changes since your last session?’ Any reported event was recorded in a dedicated safety log utilizing a standardized form adapted from the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) adverse event guidelines, documenting its duration, severity, and management. All AEs were independently assessed by a senior physical therapist for their causal relationship to the intervention protocols. A formal safety protocol was in place requiring immediate session termination and referral to a specialist in the event of any SAE or severe AE. Both active physical therapy groups demonstrated an excellent safety profile, with zero non-serious AEs and zero SAEs or training-related injuries reported over the full duration of the trial (0% AE rate).

Outcomes

Outcomes were assessed at baseline and at the end of the 8-week intervention. In case of bilateral knee OA, most painful knee was assessed for the reading of outcome measures.

Primary outcomes

Visual analogue scale

In this study, pain intensity was evaluated at rest using a standard Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) converted to a 0 to 10 point scale (measured in centimeters), where 0 indicates ‘no pain’ and 10 indicates ‘worst imaginable pain.’ Pain levels were categorized into four corresponding clinical grades: no pain (0 to 0.4 cm), mild pain (0.5 to 4.4 cm), moderate pain (4.5 to 7.4 cm), and severe pain (7.5 to 10.0 cm). The established, validated between-group MCID for pain on this 10-point scale in knee osteoarthritis populations ranges from 0.84 to 0.9 points (or cm)31,32.

Secondary outcomes

Foot alignment was evaluated under a semi-weight-bearing condition, with the participant bearing equal weight through both lower limbs30.

Navicular/foot ratio

The navicular/foot ratio—utilized as a surrogate marker for medial longitudinal arch integrity—was calculated as the quotient of the height of the navicular tuberosity divided by the total length of the foot. Measurement of navicular height was done at the point of contact between the navicular tuberosity with the ground, and the total length of the foot was measured between the calcaneus and the second toe. Both parameters were recorded in raw millimeters (mm). The ratio was expressed as a dimensionless decimal fraction (navicular height in mm/foot length in mm). To eliminate measurement bias, the outcome assessor was completely blinded to group allocations and performed all calculations independently using standardized positioning scripts, ensuring that the structural tracking remained highly insulated from expectation errors. This approach was chosen in order to adapt to the differences in the structure of arches caused by the difference in foot length. It has high inter-tester reliability, with an Intraclass Correlation Coefficient ICC of 0.98, with a minimal detectable change (MDC) of 0.01933.

Achilles tendon thickness (ATT)

Achilles tendon thickness (ATT) was measured with the help of a digital caliper (Digital Calipers by Adoric), which is a non-expensive instrument that displays the results in inches and millimeters. Participants were positioned prone on an examination table with both feet hanging freely over the edge of bed to maintain a passive, neutral ankle position (approximately 90° of flexion). The measurement site was established at a level 5 cm proximal to the most distal palpable insertion of the Achilles tendon on the calcaneus, roughly corresponding horizontally with the level of the medial malleolus. This method has demonstrated excellent intrarater (ICC₃,₁ = 0.96) and interrater (ICC₃,₁ = 0.98) reliability in patients with knee osteoarthritis, with MDC of 0.88 mm34. The concurrent validity of using calipers for this parameter has been confirmed against diagnostic ultrasound, showing a strong correlation (r = 0.847), (p = 0.002) and high reliability (ICC = 0.838) for tracking side-to-side thickness variations (Elbaz et al., 2016)60. While ultrasound remains the gold standard for visualizing internal tissue pathology, digital calipers provide a valid, cost-effective alternative for clinical anthropometric tracking. It is critical to note that caliper measurements represent a gross macroscopic assessment of the distal region, encompassing the tendon proper, overlying skin, subcutaneous tissue, and surrounding fluid structures (such as the paratenon and retrocalcaneal bursa). Unlike diagnostic ultrasound, calipers cannot isolate independent internal tissue borders. To maintain terminological consistency with validating trials (Elbaz et al., 2016), we retain the operational definition of ‘Achilles tendon thickness’ (ATT). However, we explicitly clarify that this caliper-derived metric indexes the gross posterior ankle soft tissue complex—co-registering skinfold depth, subcutaneous fat, retrocalcaneal fluid bursae, and the tendon proper—and should not be interpreted as an isolated, histological assessment of tendinous tissue borders alone.

Knee injury and osteoarthritis outcome Score (KOOS)

The Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) is a 42-item, patient self-administered patient outcome measure designed to measure knee health in five subscales (Pain, Symptoms, Activities of Daily Living (ADL), Sport and Recreation and knee-related Quality of Life). The subscales have a range of 0 (extreme knee problems) to 100 (no problems), which gives a more detailed understanding of the functional limitations and quality of life of a patient. It takes approximately 10–15 min to fill in the KOOS which is commonly used in knee OA in Urdu language35. For the KOOS questionnaire, the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) values were 12.1 for symptoms, 13.2 for quality of life, 11.3 for pain, 10.2 for activities of daily living (ADL), and 15.5 for the sports and recreation subscale36.

40-m fast-paced walk test

This assessment was performed first in the functional testing sequence to minimize the confounding effects of residual lower-limb muscle fatigue. It was conducted in a quiet, dedicated 10-meter unobstructed clinic hallway, with participants wearing standard comfortable athletic flat shoes without custom orthotics. After familiarizing themselves with the procedure, participants performed the test as per instruction. Assessors delivered the standardized verbal instruction script: “When I say go, walk down the 10-meter path as quickly as you safely can without running, turn around the marker, and continue back until you have completed 4 lengths (totaling 40 meters). I will stop the timer during your turns.“. No proactive verbal encouragement or pacing prompts were provided during the test to prevent performance bias. Timing was captured via a digital stopwatch by an independent examiner who was strictly blinded to group allocations and positioned in an isolated testing area. The minimal clinical important difference - the smallest detectable difference that a patient considers meaningful, is generally considered to be an increase of 0.2 to 0.3 m/s according to Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) guidelines37,38.

30 s chair stand test

This test was administered second in the functional circuit, precisely 5 min after the completion of the walk test to ensure adequate metabolic recovery. A standard armless chair with a seat height of 43 cm stabilized against a wall, and participants wore their standard athletic flat shoes. After familiarizing themselves with the procedure, participants performed the test as per instruction. The standardized instruction script read to each participant was: “Sit in the center of the chair with your feet flat on the floor and your arms crossed securely over your chest. When I say go, rise to a full standing position and sit back down completely as many times as you can in 30 seconds.“. The examiner stated the remaining time only at the 15-second mark but otherwise provided no proactive verbal encouragement. The counting of complete repetitions was tracked by the same blinded outcome assessor, and repetitions were only finalized if the participant reached full extension at the top and completely returned to the seated position before the 30-second mark. The minimally clinically important difference (MCID) is usually two to three repetitions37,38.

11-step stair climb test

This assessment was performed third and last in the functional performance circuit, exactly 5 min following the chair stand test. The test utilized a standardized indoor flight of 11 consecutive steps featuring a step height of 15 cm, a depth of 28 cm, and continuous side handrails. Use of the handrail was restricted to emergency balance recovery only; active pulling or pushing on the rail for ascending or descending propulsion was strictly prohibited, and no assistive devices were permitted. After familiarizing themselves with the procedure, participants performed the test as per instruction. The assessor delivered the following standardized script: “When I say go, ascend these 11 steps as quickly and safely as possible, turn around at the top landing, and immediately descend back to the starting floor.“. The assessor stood slightly behind the participant for safety but provided no external verbal prompts or pacing encouragement. Stopwatch timing, spanning from the word “Go” until both feet returned flat to the starting floor, was collected by the blinded examiner. A change greater than 2.33 s (the minimal detectable change) indicates statistically significant functional improvement beyond measurement error37,38.

Sample size

We performed an a priori sample size calculation using G*Power for an independent t-test based on the primary outcome, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) resting pain intensity. Assuming an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.60, an alpha of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, the analysis yielded a required total sample size of 44 participants26. To account for potential attrition, the final sample size was set to 100 participants (50 per group). Because the baseline-to-post-intervention correlation (r) was unknown at the planning stage, the independent t-test framework served as a conservative benchmark. The primary analysis uses ANCOVA to control for baseline scores, which reduces error variance and increases statistical power relative to a t-test. Consequently, this t-test-based sample size represents a highly conservative threshold; the study remains robustly powered to detect the specified effect size without inflating the risk of Type II error.

Randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding

The participants were randomized with a ratio of 1:1 by the use of a computer-generated randomization sheet that was created by an independent statistician to avoid unequal distribution of the subjects to the core stabilization and foot/ankle strengthening treatments. It was not predictable in group assignments and equally allocated the same number in both groups. The concealment of allocation was done by means of sequentially numbered, sealed and opaque envelopes that were not opened until the participants completed the baseline assessment. This step safeguards against selection bias whereby the researchers would know or manipulate the assignment of the group before enrolling.

Blinding was implemented to the fullest extent possible given the nature of the exercise-based interventions. The outcome assessor was blinded to group assignments during all data collection to eliminate measurement bias. To maintain this blinding, follow-up assessments were conducted in a separate room away from the intervention area. Participants were instructed verbally and through written reminders before each session not to disclose their exercise assignments or discuss their physical therapy routines with the assessor. The assessor also used standardized instruction scripts to guide participants through functional tests, minimizing unscripted conversation that could inadvertently reveal group allocation.

Baseline knee radiographs were independently evaluated and graded according to the Kellgren–Lawrence (K/L) criteria by an experienced consultant radiologist who was completely blinded to the study objectives, hypotheses, and group allocation. To minimize expectation bias, participants were explicitly informed during the consent process that the trial was a head-to-head comparison of two active physical therapy protocols, without indicating which intervention was hypothesized to be superior. The two exercise environments were kept structurally separate to prevent cross-contamination and peer discussion of group assignments.

Because the interventions involved visibly distinct exercises (core stability vs. foot/ankle), blinding of participants and therapists was not feasible. To mitigate this limitation, therapists followed standardized exercise scripts, and participants remained unaware of the study hypotheses to reduce the potential for expectation bias. Data analysts were independent of the data collection process and remained completely blinded to group assignments during the initial stages of statistical analysis. To prevent data-driven results and analytical bias, group codes were masked as ‘Group A’ and ‘Group B’ throughout the primary statistical computations and were only unblinded after the completion of the pre-specified analysis plan.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0). Separate one-way analyses of covariance (ANCOVA) were performed for each dependent variable to examine post-intervention between-group differences, with the corresponding baseline score entered as a covariate and group as a fixed factor. Assumptions of residual normality, homogeneity of variance and regression slopes, and absence of influential outliers (Cook’s distance) were assessed for all 11 dependent variables and were met (see Supplementary Table), supporting the use of this statistical approach. Effect sizes are reported as partial η².

For the primary outcome (VAS pain intensity), statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Between-group differences were evaluated using ANCOVA; p-values for secondary outcomes were interpreted against a post- hoc Bonferroni-adjusted threshold. Due to the fully supervised clinic setting, real-time data monitoring, and immediate verification of all completed forms, the final dataset contained no missing questionnaire items, incomplete functional tests, or invalid structural measurements (0% missing data).

Results

One hundred patients were randomized to the two intervention groups (50 patients each). There were 50 patients in each group, and there were no follow-up losses or discontinuations in either group. Therefore, the final analysis (without exclusions) involved all 100 randomized patients (Fig. 1). The 100% adherence and retention rate was achieved through rigid clinical oversight, including immediate telephonic follow-ups to reschedule missed appointments within the active week, flexible alternate-day session availability, and full financial coverage of all supervised clinic visits. Safety logs demonstrated that no severe adverse events, falls, or cardiovascular complications occurred during the trial. No participant withdrew from the study due to pain or safety concerns.

Discussion

In this randomized controlled trial involving individuals with Kellgren-Lawrence grade II–III knee osteoarthritis, a core stability exercise program added to conventional knee exercises was compared with an equivalent foot-ankle strengthening program supplemented by the same conventional exercises. The core stability group demonstrated greater improvements across multiple outcome domains, including pain, patient-reported function, performance-based mobility, and distal structural parameters. Between-group differences were moderate to large in magnitude, exceeding established minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs) for patient-reported pain, KOOS subscales, and functional strength, while surpassing minimal detectable changes (MDCs) for objective structural and stair-climbing parameters. Distal changes—such as reduced Achilles tendon thickness and improved foot alignment—were significantly greater in the core stability group, even though the foot-ankle group received a protocol specifically designed to target these structures. Although these findings are consistent with the theoretical framework of the proximal kinetic chain, which posits that lumbo-pelvic control can influence lower-extremity function12,39, the present data cannot confirm dynamic kinetic chain modifications. Thus, these proximal-distal relationships remain speculative within the scope of this study2,40.

The core stability group showed a greater reduction in resting pain on the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), with an adjusted between-group mean difference of 1.20 points (95% CI: 0.77 to 1.64), exceeding the MCID range of 0.84 to 0.9. Similar pain improvements following trunk neuromuscular training in knee osteoarthritis have been reported, albeit with different effect sizes28. These reductions align with meta-analytic evidence demonstrating a small-to-moderate association between specific gait kinetic variables (e.g., peak knee adduction moment) and pain intensity in medial knee osteoarthritis, with body mass index moderating this relationship41. Whether core activation influences pain through descending inhibitory mechanisms, altered sensorimotor integration, or improved biomechanical efficiency remains unresolved.

The core stability group exhibited greater improvements across all Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) subscales, with changes exceeding MCID thresholds and corresponding to partial η² values ranging from 0.347 to 0.553. Performance-based measures showed parallel improvements: 40-meter fast-paced walking speed increased by 0.22 m/s, 30-second chair-stand repetitions increased by 2.97, and 11-step stair-climb time decreased by 2.41 s. These findings extend those of a single-arm trial that reported significant gains in gait speed, reductions in external knee adduction moment, and large KOOS improvements following six weeks of core stabilization training in individuals with knee osteoarthritis14. The observed between-group improvements cleanly surpassed validated MCID thresholds for all subjective KOOS metrics and resting pain. For performance-based measures, the 30-second chair stand test met its MCID threshold (achieving a −2.97 repetition difference against an MCID of 2–3), and the 40-m walk test mean change of 0.22 m/s successfully entered its target MCID range (0.2 to 0.3 m/s). Conversely, because a universally accepted MCID is lacking for the remaining metrics, statistically significant structural and agility changes were confirmed by demonstrating that the 11-step stair climb test (2.41 s), navicular/foot ratio (−0.094), and Achilles tendon thickness (4.82 mm) all significantly surpassed their established MDC values (2.33 s, 0.019, and 0.88 mm, respectively).

These results support the hypothesis that enhanced proximal control can improve lower-extremity function during tasks requiring multi-joint coordination and trunk stability. Previous systematic reviews have reported positive balance and mobility outcomes following core stability interventions in post-arthroplasty populations; however, heterogeneity in comparator interventions and outcome assessments has limited synthesis42. Notably, performance-based tasks in the present study were not blinded to participants or therapists, introducing potential effort-dependent effects that cannot be entirely excluded.

Compared with the foot-ankle strengthening (FAS) group, the core stability exercise (CSE) group demonstrated a mean reduction in Achilles tendon thickness (ATT) of 4.82 mm (95% CI: 4.43 to 5.52; partial η² = 0.862) and an increase in the navicular-to-foot ratio of 0.094 (95% CI: 0.082 to 0.107; partial η² = 0.703). These changes occurred despite the FAS group receiving a protocol previously shown to improve local foot strength and arch support.

These findings are consistent with the biomechanical interdependence of the lower limb kinetic chain. The relationship between ATT, navicular-to-foot ratio, and ankle range of motion offers insights into these systemic adaptations43. Foot posture influences lower leg muscle mass and can alter muscle properties such as tone and stiffness44. Patients with medial tibiofemoral knee OA often present with more pronated foot posture and higher calcaneal valgus angles—factors associated with changes in varus/valgus alignment, knee adduction moment, and limb or trunk positioning, all of which increase medial compartment loading45–47. These interconnected factors are well documented in the biomechanical context of knee OA4849. Systematic review and meta-analysis evidence further supports this interpretation, showing that higher baseline peak knee adduction moment and the presence of varus thrust during gait are associated with increased odds of medial tibiofemoral OA structural progression50.

Increased ATT in knee OA is understood as a potential protective adaptation to altered biomechanics, redistributing forces to minimize discomfort in the affected knee24,51. The rationale for tracking ATT in this study was that proximal kinetic chain improvements might unload overstressed distal structures, with macroscopic changes captured by calipers. The observed parallel reduction in ATT following CSE may reflect mitigation of this protective distal adaptation. However, the 4.82 mm reduction in ATT over eight weeks is highly counterintuitive and unlikely to represent definitive physiological or structural matrix remodeling, which typically requires months of progressive loading. This same physiological timeline constraint applies to the observed anthropometric shifts in the navicular/foot ratio, which cannot be attributed to definitive structural or anatomical changes in osseous or ligamentous alignment over a short 8-week window. Because an 8-week intervention is fundamentally insufficient to induce permanent structural remodeling of the tendon matrix in proximal and distal soft-tissue structures, the rapid reduction in ATT observed in the CSE group must be critically interpreted as a functional byproduct of improved top-down neuromuscular coordination. We hypothesize that the reduction in caliper-measured ATT may partly reflect shifts in local soft-tissue fluid or retrocalcaneal bursal volume, rather than surrogate tendinous matrix remodeling. However, without diagnostic ultrasound or MRI, this interpretation remains speculative, and changes in subcutaneous adipose tissue cannot be ruled out. Because true tendinous matrix remodeling requires months of progressive, heavy loading, a structural reduction of this magnitude over 8 weeks is physiologically implausible for the tendon. Therefore, the observed adaptation may reflect changes in the composite soft tissue structures captured by the caliper. We hypothesize that proximal core stabilization unloads the lower-extremity kinetic chain, prompting a reduction in localized low-grade edema, shifting microvascular fluid volumes, or resolving chronic retrocalcaneal bursal swelling. This composite soft tissue translation explains the macroscopic caliper shifts without violating established physiological timelines for tendon adaptation. While the change in navicular/foot ratio exceeded the minimal detectable change (MDC = 0.019)—indicating a genuine anthropometric shift rather than measurement error—the underlying pathway remains speculative. In the absence of dynamic tracking, this finding should be interpreted as a short-term correlation requiring replication rather than definitive evidence of structural tissue adaptation.

The observed improvement in navicular/foot ratio may also be explained by a bidirectional association between knee symptoms and foot alignment52,53. Improved pain and knee function in the CSE group may have facilitated more normalized gait patterns, reducing compensatory mechanisms such as lateral trunk sway. This, in turn, has likely decreased excessive foot pronation and altered load distribution on the knee, ultimately improving medial longitudinal arch structure. The magnitude of the between-group difference (−0.094) exceeds the MDC of 0.019, reinforcing that this represents a statistically significant arch adaptation rather than measurement bias. Because evaluations were performed by a strictly blinded assessor, this structural change suggests a potential top-down biomechanical adaptation. The exact physiological or biomechanical mechanism driving the change in the navicular/foot ratio remains unknown and highly speculative, as this study did not capture dynamic kinematics or muscle activation patterns. The observed anthropometric shift may represent a functional adaptation, but it must not be interpreted as definitive structural evidence of arch remodeling.

One speculative interpretation is that improved lumbo-pelvic stability indirectly alters load distribution on the Achilles tendon and medial longitudinal arch. However, because localized kinematic variables were not monitored, this pathway remains hypothetical. Although associations between proximal neuromuscular characteristics and distal alignment have been linked to knee osteoarthritis, the current findings are consistent with previous studies11. Nevertheless, this study did not capture definitive biomechanical parameters—such as external knee adduction moment, dynamic knee valgus angles, or plantar pressure during gait—and therefore cannot establish a direct causal pathway or confirm definitive alterations in lower-limb mechanics. The absence of 3D motion analysis and kinetic force-plate measurements precludes direct conclusions regarding joint loading. Replication using concurrent kinematic and neuromuscular measures is necessary to identify underlying mechanisms.

Conversely, ankle-foot strengthening induced local changes in ankle tendon characteristics, consistent with the notion that localized distal loading can produce tissue-specific alterations54,55,56. The modest changes observed in the FAS group do not preclude the possibility that distal training may be effective in other clinical settings or with different protocol specifications.

The overall pattern of between-group differences is largely comparable to previous research that separately compared proximal- and distal-focused interventions in knee OA. However, the confounding effects of differential participant expectations cannot be ruled out. Core stability exercises involve large-muscle, multi-joint movements and may have been perceived by participants as more comprehensive and therapeutic than isolated foot-ankle strengthening. If the CSE cohort maintained higher baseline recovery expectations, this psychological bias could have inflated subjective self-reported outcomes and driven greater physical effort during functional tests. Because this study did not capture dynamic joint moments, surface electromyography, or plantar pressure mapping, a definitive causal pathway linking proximal stabilization to distal alignment cannot be verified. Consequently, these findings should be viewed as preliminary and descriptive.

The modest improvements in the FAS group, particularly in distal structural outcomes, may be explained by several factors. Unlike Dantas et al. (2023),who reported that the same foot-ankle protocol was superior to usual care for pain and function, the present study used a core stability program as the comparator25. Rather than concluding that the 8-week duration was uniquely insufficient for the FAS group, the modest improvements in distal metrics within this cohort must be contextualized by the mechanical nature of the exercises57,58. Unlike the multi-joint, large-muscle trunk movements in the CSE group that effectively un-loaded the entire lower extremity, the localized and isolated nature of the FAS exercises may have inadvertently concentrated mechanical stresses directly onto the already compromised distal structures of these knee OA patients. Therefore, the lack of structural changes in the FAS group is not a reflection of an inadequate timeframe but rather indicates that isolated distal strengthening under standard gravity conditions is less effective at mitigating the systemic, top-down kinetic chain pathomechanics that drive medial compartment knee osteoarthritis loading. Finally, the absence of credibility assessment means that non-specific treatment effects cannot be ruled out as contributors to the better outcomes in the CSE group59. Together, these factors likely account for the relatively smaller improvements seen in the FAS group.

Several limitations should be considered. First, Achilles tendon thickness was measured using digital calipers, a method inherently less precise than diagnostic ultrasound imaging. Although digital calipers offer a cost-effective surrogate for clinical anthropometric tracking, they provide only a gross macroscopic assessment that encompasses the tendon proper, overlying skin, subcutaneous tissue, and surrounding fluid. They cannot isolate internal tissue boundaries or differentiate internal tissue health, such as structural tendinous disorganization or peritendinous fluid accumulation. Consequently, the observed short-term changes should be interpreted with caution; they remain an unverified hypothesis that may reflect shifts in localized fluid or bursal volume rather than true matrix remodeling, while potential structural variations in the overlying subcutaneous adipose tissue remain an unmeasured confounder. Second, although outcome assessors were blinded, participants and supervising therapists could not be blinded to treatment allocation, and the study lacked a formal treatment credibility assessment. Furthermore, all exercise interventions were delivered and supervised exclusively by a single lead therapist to ensure strict protocol fidelity. While this single-provider framework successfully eliminated inter-rater variability in treatment delivery, it inherently introduced the potential for performance bias. Because the treating therapist was also the lead researcher and aware of the study hypotheses, they may have inadvertently or subconsciously influenced participant encouragement, tactical cueing, or effort across the two groups. Because the exercise programs were visibly distinct, participants in the core stability exercise group may have perceived their intervention as more comprehensive, introducing expectation and effort biases that cannot be quantified and that may have contributed to the superior outcomes observed in that cohort. Third, the complete absence of direct biomechanical measurements and objective laboratory metrics—such as three-dimensional motion analysis, dynamic joint moments, surface electromyography (sEMG), or plantar pressure force-plate mapping—is a primary limitation that constrains the conclusions. Without these direct mechanistic outcomes, the proposed kinetic-chain adjustments invoked to explain differences between the proximal and distal interventions remain entirely speculative, descriptive, and unverified. Fourth, the study relied solely on indirect structural measures, specifically the navicular/foot ratio and digital caliper tendon tracking. These parameters are static anthropometric surrogates and lack the dynamic granularity needed to explain complex, multi-joint loading adjustments during functional gait or weight-bearing tasks. Fifth, because knee osteoarthritis rehabilitation aims for sustained long-term improvement, the eight-week endpoint precludes any conclusions about the durability of the observed benefits. Sixth, the eligibility criteria (BMI > 30 kg/m², VAS < 4, no severe deformity or assistive device use) limit generalizability to populations with more pronounced obesity or more advanced disease. Seventh, all sessions were supervised by therapists; adherence, fidelity, and outcomes may therefore not generalize to real-world or home-based settings. Furthermore, although intensive monitoring yielded a perfect retention rate (0% dropout), this ideal compliance reflects a controlled university clinic environment and may not be achievable in routine practice. Finally, the absence of a true control group prevents any determination of absolute effectiveness relative to natural history or non-specific care effects.

Strengths of this trial include prospective registration, CONSORT-compliant reporting, concealed allocation, assessor blinding, complete follow-up, and sufficient statistical power for between-group comparisons. Future trials should incorporate longer-term follow-up with imaging-based structural outcomes, randomization stratified by baseline kinetic chain deficit profiles to test precision-medicine hypotheses, active comparator groups matched for credibility and expectancy, cost-effectiveness analyses, and pragmatic implementation studies. The effects of combined proximal-distal protocols in additive or synergistic combinations are also warrant investigation. External validity would be enhanced in future studies through objective adherence monitoring and inclusion of diverse socioeconomic groups.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present findings suggest that, in patients with Knee OA, core stability exercises added to conventional knee exercises produce greater short-term improvements in pain, patient-reported function, and performance-based mobility compared with foot-ankle strengthening added to conventional knee exercises. Crucially, while changes were observed in static distal anthropometric surrogates, these structural findings are highly unexpected and remain mechanistically unverified within the scope of this study. Because these short-term modifications likely reflect localized fluid shifts rather than definitive physiological tissue remodeling, they must be interpreted with caution. Future robust replication trials are required to evaluate these proximal-distal interactions using objective diagnostic imaging, three-dimensional gait analysis, and dynamic neuromuscular measurements. Finally, future research should explore stratified approaches based on baseline kinetic-chain deficits and evaluate the long-term durability of combined proximal-distal protocols.
