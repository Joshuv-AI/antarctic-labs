Introduction

In this paper we consider the following Dirichlet problem with a small
parameter
\varepsilon>0
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\varepsilon\Delta_{p}u&=a(x)|u|^{q-2}u-b(x)|u|^{\gamma-2}u,&&x\in\Omega,\\
u&=0,&&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

Here

\Delta_{p}u=\operatorname{div}\!\left(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\right)

is the
p
-Laplacian,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a bounded connected
domain with
C^{1}
boundary, and
1<p<q<\gamma<p^{*}
, where

p^{*}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{pN}{N-p},&\text{if }p<N,\\[4.0pt]
+\infty,&\text{if }p\geq N.\end{cases}

We assume that

a,b\in L^{\infty}(\Omega),\qquad a(x)\geq 0,\qquad b(x)\geq\sigma_{b}>0\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega,

(1.2)

where
\sigma_{b}>0
is a constant. Throughout the paper we also assume that

a\not\equiv 0
.

A function
u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
is called a
weak solution
of
problem (
1.1
) if it is a critical point of the energy functional

\Phi_{\varepsilon}(u)=\frac{\varepsilon}{p}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}\,dx-\frac{1}{q}\int_{\Omega}a|u|^{q}\,dx+\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{\Omega}b|u|^{\gamma}\,dx.

(1.3)

Here
W^{1,p}_{0}:=W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
is the closure of

C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
in the Sobolev space
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
.
A weak solution
u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
is called a
ground state

of
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
if

\Phi_{\varepsilon}(u)\leq\Phi_{\varepsilon}(w)

for every weak solution
w\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
of problem (
1.1
).

Problem (
1.1
) is singularly perturbed: as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, the
differential order disappears in the limit, and boundary layers may form near

\partial\Omega
. Already in the one-dimensional case
p=2
,
a=b=1
,
the problem becomes

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\varepsilon u^{\prime\prime}(x)&=u^{q-1}(x)-u^{\gamma-1}(x),&&x\in(0,1),\\
u(0)&=u(1)=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.4)

The formal method of matched asymptotic expansions
(see, for instance,
[
16
,
26
]
) leads to the approximation

u(x)\sim U\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)+U\!\left(\frac{1-x}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)-1,\qquad 0\leq x\leq 1,

where the boundary-layer profile
U
is determined by

-U^{\prime\prime}=U^{q-1}-U^{\gamma-1},\qquad U(0)=0,\qquad U(+\infty)=1.

This profile admits the integral representation

\int_{0}^{U(\xi)}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{2\left(\frac{t^{\gamma}}{\gamma}-\frac{t^{q}}{q}+\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)}}=\xi.

The approximation is consistent with the boundary conditions in the leading
order: in the interior of the interval, both arguments

x/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
and
(1-x)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
are large, so that

U\approx 1
and
u(x)\approx 1
, whereas near the endpoints the
corresponding boundary-layer profile provides the transition to the zero
boundary value.

Extending such a description to multidimensional domains, especially in the
presence of non-symmetric geometry or variable coefficients, is a substantial
mathematical difficulty. At the same time, singularly perturbed nonlinear
boundary value problems form a central topic in modern elliptic theory because
they combine delicate asymptotic analysis with a wide range of applications.
They arise, for instance, in population dynamics, models of infection spread,
control theory, mechanics of materials with memory, nonlinear optics,
physiological models, and predator–prey systems; see, for example,

[
23
,
25
,
29
,
33
]
.

A large part of the mathematical literature on singularly perturbed elliptic
problems is devoted to concentration phenomena. In this direction one usually
studies families of solutions which, as the perturbation parameter tends to
zero, develop sharply localized profiles: interior or boundary spikes,
spike-layer solutions, multi-peak solutions, or bubble-type solutions. The
main questions concern the existence of such concentrating solutions, the
number and location of their concentration points, the influence of the
geometry of
\Omega
, and the role of the coefficients and lower-order terms
in selecting the limiting profiles. This circle of problems has been
extensively studied for semilinear and quasilinear elliptic equations and is
closely related to the analysis of concentration near critical points of
auxiliary potentials, mean-curvature type quantities, or other effective
variational landscapes; see, among many others,

[
2
,
3
,
5
,
11
,
15
,
24
,
27
,
31
,
22
]
.

The problem considered in the present paper has a different emphasis. Rather
than constructing spike or bubble solutions and describing the fine geometry
of their concentration sets, we study the asymptotic behaviour of ground
states for a competing-superlinear Dirichlet problem with small diffusion.
In this setting the formal limiting equation is no longer an elliptic boundary
value problem, but a pointwise algebraic balance between the two nonlinear
terms. Our main result shows that, under the positivity assumption on
a
,
ground states converge to the explicit profile

\bar{u}_{0}(x)=\left(\frac{a(x)}{b(x)}\right)^{1/(\gamma-q)},

with convergence in measure and strong convergence in
L^{r}(\Omega)
for

1\leq r<\gamma
. Thus the singular limit is described not by localization at
isolated points, but by convergence to a spatially distributed equilibrium
profile determined by the variable coefficients.

A further difficulty is caused by the loss of differential order in the
singular limit. Formally setting
\varepsilon=0
reduces the elliptic
problem to the algebraic equation

a(x)|u|^{q-2}u-b(x)|u|^{\gamma-2}u=0,

so that the boundary condition is no longer encoded in the limiting equation.
Moreover, this equation does not select a unique limit. In the nonnegative
class, for example, every function of the form

u(x)=\bar{u}_{0}(x)\chi_{E}(x),\qquad\bar{u}_{0}(x)=\left(\frac{a(x)}{b(x)}\right)^{1/(\gamma-q)},

where
E\subset\Omega
is measurable, is a formal solution of the limiting
equation. Thus the limit problem has a continuum of possible solutions.

For this reason, the main asymptotic issue is not merely to identify the
algebraic balance between the nonlinearities, but to determine which of the
many possible limiting profiles is selected by ground states of the original
elliptic problem. The result proved below shows that, under the positivity
assumption on
a
, the selected profile is the full positive branch

\bar{u}_{0}
. This also explains why the natural convergence statement is
formulated first in measure and then in
L^{r}(\Omega)
,
1\leq r<\gamma
,
rather than as a classical convergence of solutions of elliptic boundary value
problems.

In the present paper, problem (
1.1
) is studied by means of the
nonlinear Rayleigh quotient method developed in

[
18
,
21
]
. Applying this method to the parameter

\varepsilon
leads to the nonlinear generalized Rayleigh quotient

\Upsilon(u):=\frac{\left(\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}a|u|^{q}\,dx\right)^{\frac{\gamma-p}{\gamma-q}}}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}\,dx\left(\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}b|u|^{\gamma}\,dx\right)^{\frac{q-p}{\gamma-q}}}.

Since in the general setting we assume only
a\geq 0
, there may exist
nonzero functions
u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

\int_{\Omega}a|u|^{q}\,dx=0.

Thus the extremal quantities are naturally defined on the set

\mathcal{D}:=\left\{u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}:\int_{\Omega}a|u|^{q}\,dx>0\right\}.

In what follows,
\Upsilon
is considered on
\mathcal{D}
.

Two extremal parameter values are associated with this quotient:

\varepsilon^{*}=c_{p,q,\gamma}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{D}}\Upsilon(u),\qquad\varepsilon_{e}^{*}=c_{e,p,q,\gamma}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{D}}\Upsilon(u),

where

c_{p,q,\gamma}=\frac{\gamma-q}{\gamma-p}\left(\frac{q-p}{\gamma-p}\right)^{\frac{q-p}{\gamma-q}},\qquad c_{e,p,q,\gamma}=\frac{p(\gamma-q)}{q(\gamma-p)}\left(\frac{\gamma(q-p)}{q(\gamma-p)}\right)^{\frac{q-p}{\gamma-q}}.

(1.5)

Moreover,

0<\varepsilon_{e}^{*}<\varepsilon^{*}<+\infty.

The first main result describes existence and nonexistence of positive
solutions of problem (
1.1
) depending on the parameter

\varepsilon
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded connected domain with

C^{1}
boundary, let
1<p<q<\gamma<p^{*}
, and assume that
(
1.2
) holds. Then the following assertions hold.

(1)

If
\varepsilon>\varepsilon^{*}
, then problem (
1.1
) has no
nontrivial weak solutions in
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
.

(2)

For every
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{e}^{*}
, problem (
1.1
)
has a positive weak solution

u_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega).

Moreover,

(i)

u_{\varepsilon}
is a ground state;

(ii)

\Phi_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})<0,\qquad\left.\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(tu_{\varepsilon})\right|_{t=1}>0;

(iii)

u_{\varepsilon}\in C^{1,\kappa}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)

for some
\kappa\in(0,1)
.

(3)

For every
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{e}^{*}
, problem (
1.1
)
has a second positive weak solution

v_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega).

In addition,

\Phi_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})>0,\qquad v_{\varepsilon}\in C^{1,\kappa}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)

for some
\kappa\in(0,1)
.

Remark 1.1
.

In Theorem
1.1
, regularity is asserted only locally in the
interior of the domain. This is because, for a
C^{1}
boundary, one
cannot in general expect global
C^{1,\kappa}
-regularity of solutions
up to
\partial\Omega
.

Corollary 1.1

(The classical case
p=2
)
.

Let
p=2
, let
\partial\Omega
be of class
C^{2,\alpha}
, and
let
a,b\in C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, with
(
1.2
) satisfied. Then the positive weak solutions

u_{\varepsilon}
and
v_{\varepsilon}
obtained in
Theorem
1.1
are classical solutions of problem (
1.1
).
In particular,

u_{\varepsilon},\ v_{\varepsilon}\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\Omega}).

The next result describes the asymptotic behaviour of ground states as

\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded connected domain with

C^{1}
boundary, let
1<p<q<\gamma<p^{*}
, assume that
(
1.2
) holds, and suppose that there exists a constant

\sigma_{a}>0
such that

a(x)\geq\sigma_{a}\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega.

Let
u_{\varepsilon}
be any family of positive ground states of
problem (
1.1
),
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{e}^{*}
. Then, as

\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
,

u_{\varepsilon}\to\bar{u}_{0}\quad\text{in measure in }\Omega,

where

\bar{u}_{0}(x)=\left(\frac{a(x)}{b(x)}\right)^{1/(\gamma-q)}\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega.

(1.6)

Moreover,

u_{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}_{0}\to 0\quad\text{strongly in }L^{r}(\Omega),\qquad 1\leq r<\gamma,

(1.7)

and

u_{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}_{0}\rightharpoonup 0\quad\text{weakly in }L^{r}(\Omega),\qquad 1<r\leq\gamma.

The limit function
\bar{u}_{0}
satisfies the limiting equation

a(x)\bar{u}_{0}^{q-1}(x)-b(x)\bar{u}_{0}^{\gamma-1}(x)=0\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega.

. Dashed lines indicate the

By the simple scaling
u\mapsto tu
, problem (
1.1
) with

\varepsilon>0
can be transformed into either of the following equivalent
forms:

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}u&=\lambda a(x)|u|^{q-2}u-b(x)|u|^{\gamma-2}u,&&x\in\Omega,\\
u&=0,&&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.8)

and

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}u&=a(x)|u|^{q-2}u-\nu b(x)|u|^{\gamma-2}u,&&x\in\Omega,\\
u&=0,&&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.9)

where
\lambda,\nu>0
.

These problems are in one-to-one correspondence with problem (
1.1
)
whenever
\varepsilon\lambda\nu\neq 0
. Namely, if

is a weak solution of
(
1.1
), then the functions

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\varepsilon^{-1/(\gamma-p)}u_{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-(\gamma-q)/(\gamma-p)},

(1.10)

\displaystyle u_{\nu}

\displaystyle:=\varepsilon^{-1/(q-p)}u_{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{(\gamma-q)/(q-p)}

(1.11)

are weak solutions of problems (
1.8
) and (
1.9
),
respectively. The inverse transformations are

u_{\varepsilon}=\lambda^{-1/(\gamma-q)}u_{\lambda},\qquad u_{\varepsilon}=\nu^{1/(\gamma-q)}u_{\nu}.

Hence Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
imply the following assertion.

Corollary 1.2
.

Assume that the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
are satisfied. Then:

(1)

Problems (
1.8
) and (
1.9
) possess ground states

u_{\lambda}
and
u_{\nu}
for

respectively, where

\lambda_{e}^{*}=(\varepsilon_{e}^{*})^{-(\gamma-q)/(\gamma-p)},\qquad\nu_{e}^{*}=(\varepsilon_{e}^{*})^{(\gamma-q)/(q-p)}.

(2)

If, in addition, the hypotheses of Theorem

are
satisfied, then, as

.

.

Problems of the form (

), (

), and (

)
belong to the class of quasilinear parametric problems in which extremal
parameter values and their variational characterization through nonlinear
generalized Rayleigh quotients play a central role; see, for instance,

[

,
17
,
19
,
20
,

]
.

In terms of their general variational structure, these problems are close
to well-known parametric equations with competing nonlinearities, including

[

. However, the case

2
Preliminaries

In what follows,
L^{r}:=L^{r}(\Omega)
,
1<r<+\infty
, denotes the space of
measurable functions with finite norm

\|u\|_{r}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|u|^{r}\,dx\right)^{1/r}.

By the Poincaré–Friedrichs inequality, the norm in
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
can
be equivalently defined by

\|u\|_{1,p}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}\,dx\right)^{1/p}.

We set

T(u):=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}\,dx,\qquad A(u):=\int_{\Omega}a(x)|u|^{q}\,dx,\qquad B(u):=\int_{\Omega}b(x)|u|^{\gamma}\,dx.

Since
b(x)\geq\sigma_{b}>0
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
, we have

B(u)>0\quad\text{for all }u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}.

We also assume throughout that
a\not\equiv 0
. Hence the set

\mathcal{D}:=\left\{u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}:A(u)>0\right\}

is nonempty.

The Nehari manifold associated with problem (
1.1
) is given by

\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}:\varepsilon T(u)-A(u)+B(u)=0\right\}.

Equivalently,

D_{u}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(u)(u)=0.

Observe that, for
\varepsilon>0
, every function

u\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
satisfies

A(u)=\varepsilon T(u)+B(u)>0.

Therefore, when positive values of the parameter are considered, it is natural
to restrict attention to directions
u\in\mathcal{D}
. Only such directions
can generate an intersection of the ray
\{su:s>0\}
with the Nehari
manifold for
\varepsilon>0
.

Following the approach based on nonlinear generalized Rayleigh quotients

[
21
]
, we introduce two Rayleigh quotients associated,
respectively, with the Nehari manifold and with the zero energy level. The
first quotient is defined by

R_{\mathcal{N}}(u):=\frac{A(u)-B(u)}{T(u)},\qquad u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}.

Then

\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}=\left\{u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}:R_{\mathcal{N}}(u)=\varepsilon\right\}.

Fix
u\in\mathcal{D}
and consider the function

s\mapsto R_{\mathcal{N}}(su),\qquad s>0.

We have

R_{\mathcal{N}}(su)=\frac{s^{q}A(u)-s^{\gamma}B(u)}{s^{p}T(u)}=\frac{A(u)s^{q-p}-B(u)s^{\gamma-p}}{T(u)}.

Since
1<p<q<\gamma
, this function has a unique positive critical point,
which is the point of its global maximum. A direct computation gives

s_{\mathcal{N}}(u)=\left(\frac{(q-p)A(u)}{(\gamma-p)B(u)}\right)^{1/(\gamma-q)}.

(2.1)

Substituting
s=s_{\mathcal{N}}(u)
into
R_{\mathcal{N}}(su)
, we obtain
the nonlinear generalized Rayleigh quotient

\varepsilon(u):=R_{\mathcal{N}}(s_{\mathcal{N}}(u)u)=c_{p,q,\gamma}\frac{A(u)^{\frac{\gamma-p}{\gamma-q}}}{T(u)B(u)^{\frac{q-p}{\gamma-q}}},\qquad u\in\mathcal{D},

(2.2)

where

c_{p,q,\gamma}=\frac{\gamma-q}{\gamma-p}\left(\frac{q-p}{\gamma-p}\right)^{\frac{q-p}{\gamma-q}}.

(2.3)

We define the extremal value

\varepsilon^{*}:=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{D}}\varepsilon(u).

(2.4)

Since
a\not\equiv 0
, the set
\mathcal{D}
is nonempty; hence

\varepsilon^{*}>0.

We now show that
\varepsilon^{*}<+\infty
. By the Hölder,
Poincaré–Friedrichs, and Sobolev inequalities, there exists a constant

C>0
, independent of
u
, such that

A(u)=\int_{\Omega}a(x)|u|^{q}\,dx\leq CT(u)^{\frac{\gamma-q}{\gamma-p}}B(u)^{\frac{q-p}{\gamma-p}}.

Indeed, since
q\in(p,\gamma)
, the
L^{q}
-norm is interpolated between
the
L^{p}
- and
L^{\gamma}
-norms, while the
L^{p}
-norm is controlled by

\|\nabla u\|_{p}
. The condition
b(x)\geq\sigma_{b}>0
allows us to replace
the
L^{\gamma}
-norm by the corresponding
B(u)
-term. Substituting this
estimate into (
2.2
), we obtain

0<\varepsilon^{*}<+\infty.

(2.5)

The second Rayleigh quotient corresponds to the zero energy level
(cf.
[
9
,
10
,
18
,
21
]
):

R_{e}(u):=\frac{\frac{1}{q}A(u)-\frac{1}{\gamma}B(u)}{\frac{1}{p}T(u)},\qquad u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}.

Then

R_{e}(u)=\varepsilon\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Phi_{\varepsilon}(u)=0.

For fixed
u\in\mathcal{D}
, the function

s\mapsto R_{e}(su),\qquad s>0,

has a unique positive critical point. It is given by

s_{e}(u)=\left(\frac{\gamma(q-p)A(u)}{q(\gamma-p)B(u)}\right)^{1/(\gamma-q)}.

(2.6)

Substituting
s=s_{e}(u)
into
R_{e}(su)
, we obtain

\varepsilon_{e}(u):=R_{e}(s_{e}(u)u)=c_{e,p,q,\gamma}\frac{A(u)^{\frac{\gamma-p}{\gamma-q}}}{T(u)B(u)^{\frac{q-p}{\gamma-q}}},\qquad u\in\mathcal{D},

(2.7)

where

c_{e,p,q,\gamma}=\frac{p(\gamma-q)}{q(\gamma-p)}\left(\frac{\gamma(q-p)}{q(\gamma-p)}\right)^{\frac{q-p}{\gamma-q}}.

(2.8)

Set

\varepsilon_{e}^{*}:=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{D}}\varepsilon_{e}(u).

The same estimate as above yields

0<\varepsilon_{e}^{*}<+\infty.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
u\in\mathcal{D}
and every
s>0
, the following equivalence
holds:

R_{e}(su)=R_{\mathcal{N}}(su)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s=s_{e}(u).

Proof.

Fix
u\in\mathcal{D}
and put

\psi(s):=R_{e}(su),\qquad s>0.

Then

\psi(s)=\frac{p}{T(u)}\left(\frac{A(u)}{q}s^{q-p}-\frac{B(u)}{\gamma}s^{\gamma-p}\right),

and

\psi^{\prime}(s)=\frac{p}{T(u)}\left(\frac{q-p}{q}A(u)s^{q-p-1}-\frac{\gamma-p}{\gamma}B(u)s^{\gamma-p-1}\right).

On the other hand,

R_{\mathcal{N}}(su)-R_{e}(su)=\frac{1}{T(u)}\left(\frac{q-p}{q}A(u)s^{q-p}-\frac{\gamma-p}{\gamma}B(u)s^{\gamma-p}\right).

Therefore

\psi^{\prime}(s)=\frac{p}{s}\left(R_{\mathcal{N}}(su)-R_{e}(su)\right).

Hence

R_{e}(su)=R_{\mathcal{N}}(su)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\psi^{\prime}(s)=0.

Since the equation
\psi^{\prime}(s)=0
has a unique positive solution, namely

s_{e}(u)
, the lemma follows.

Lemma 2.2
.

For every
u\in\mathcal{D}
, the strict inequality

\varepsilon(u)>\varepsilon_{e}(u)

holds. Moreover,

\varepsilon^{*}>\varepsilon_{e}^{*}.

Proof.

It follows from formulas (
2.2
) and (
2.7
) that

\varepsilon(u)
and
\varepsilon_{e}(u)
differ only by constant
factors. Thus it is enough to prove that

c_{p,q,\gamma}>c_{e,p,q,\gamma}.

We have

The lemma is proved.

\eta>0
,

\operatorname{meas}\{x\in\Omega:\ |u_{\varepsilon}(x)-\bar{u}_{0}(x)|\geq\eta\}\to 0,

that is,

u_{\varepsilon}\to\bar{u}_{0}\quad\text{in measure in }\Omega.

(4.9)

We now prove strong convergence in
L^{r}(\Omega)
for
1\leq r<\gamma
.
Since
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}
is bounded in
L^{\gamma}(\Omega)
and

\bar{u}_{0}\in L^{\gamma}(\Omega)
, the family

\{u_{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}_{0}\}
is bounded in
L^{\gamma}(\Omega)
.
Therefore, for every
1\leq r<\gamma
, the family

\{|u_{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}_{0}|^{r}\}
is bounded in

L^{\gamma/r}(\Omega)
, where
\gamma/r>1
. Hence it is uniformly
integrable. Together with (
4.9
), Vitali’s
convergence theorem gives

\int_{\Omega}|u_{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}_{0}|^{r}\,dx\to 0.

Thus

u_{\varepsilon}\to\bar{u}_{0}\quad\text{strongly in }L^{r}(\Omega),\qquad 1\leq r<\gamma.

Moreover,

u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\bar{u}_{0}\quad\text{weakly in }L^{\gamma}(\Omega).

Indeed, the family
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}
is bounded in the reflexive space

L^{\gamma}(\Omega)
. Hence every sequence
\varepsilon_{m}\to 0^{+}
contains
a subsequence converging weakly in
L^{\gamma}(\Omega)
. By
(
4.9
), any such weak limit must coincide with

\bar{u}_{0}
. Therefore the whole family
u_{\varepsilon}
converges weakly
to
\bar{u}_{0}
in
L^{\gamma}(\Omega)
. Since
\Omega
is bounded, it
follows in particular that

u_{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}_{0}\rightharpoonup 0\quad\text{weakly in }L^{r}(\Omega),\qquad 1<r\leq\gamma.

Finally, by the definition of
\bar{u}_{0}
, we have

a(x)\bar{u}_{0}^{q-1}(x)-b(x)\bar{u}_{0}^{\gamma-1}(x)=0\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega.

Thus
\bar{u}_{0}
satisfies the limiting equation (
1.1
) with

\varepsilon=0
:

a(x)\bar{u}_{0}^{q-1}=b(x)\bar{u}_{0}^{\gamma-1}\quad\text{a.e. in }\Omega.

The theorem is proved.
