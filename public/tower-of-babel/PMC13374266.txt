Carbadox, a veterinary growth promoter exhibits anticlostridial activity through induction of oxidative stress in Clostridioides difficile
Authors: Ahmed A Abouelkhair, Nader S Abutaleb, Clayton C Caswell, Mohamed N Seleem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClostridioides difficile is the leading cause of antibiotic-associated diarrhea in hospitalized patients and is classified as an urgent public health threat. Current therapies, while effective, are limited by antibiotic-induced dysbiosis, treatment failure, and high recurrence rates, underscoring the need for novel therapeutics. Carbadox (CRX), an antibacterial growth promoter, has recently been identified as a potential anti-C. difficile inhibitor. This study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the anti-C. difficile activity, mechanism of action, and in vivo efficacy of CRX.MethodsCRX activity was assessed against a diverse panel of clinical C. difficile isolates using MIC determination, time-kill kinetics, and post-antibiotic effect assays. Its effects on toxin production were evaluated at subinhibitory concentrations, while post-germination vegetative growth was assessed under germination-permissive conditions at bactericidal concentrations. Mechanistic studies included macromolecular synthesis assays, reactive oxygen species (ROS) quantification, and antioxidant rescue experiments. Potential drug interactions were tested in combination assays with standard-of-care agents. Finally, the efficacy of CRX was examined in both acute and recurrent C. difficile infection (CDI) mouse models.ResultsCRX demonstrated potent in vitro activity, inhibiting 50% of clinical isolates at an MIC₅₀ of 1 µg/mL. In time-kill assays, CRX rapidly cleared high bacterial inoculum within 4 h and displayed a prolonged post-antibiotic effect of up to 14 h. At subinhibitory concentrations, CRX significantly reduced toxin production compared with vancomycin, while at bactericidal concentrations it suppressed vegetative growth following spore germination. Mechanistic analysis revealed marked inhibition of DNA synthesis and increased oxidative stress–associated DNA damage signals, which was reversed by N-acetyl-L-cysteine supplementation. In vivo, CRX showed no recurrence among mice surviving to the end of therapy in both CRX treatment regimens, outperforming vancomycin in relapse prevention.ConclusionCRX exhibits strong anti-C. difficile activity through DNA synthesis inhibition and increased oxidative stress–associated DNA damage, coupled with suppression of toxin production and inhibition of vegetative growth post-spore germination. Its efficacy in both acute and recurrent CDI mouse models highlights CRX as a useful proof-of-concept scaffold for the development of safer analogs targeting C. difficile. Introduction

Clostridioides difficile (C. difficile), a spore-forming Gram-positive bacterium, is a leading cause of nosocomial infections in the United States [1, 2]. Each year, approximately 223,900 cases are infected, resulting in 12,800 deaths and $1 billion in healthcare expenses [3]. Toxins and spores are the two main virulence factors of C. difficile. In addition to the crucial role that C. difficile toxins play in causing colonic inflammation and resulting in C. difficile infection (CDI), C. difficile spores can dormantly survive on inanimate surfaces for up to six months and are resistant to most medications and disinfectants [4], making them a sustainable source of C. difficile transmission and spread. Furthermore, the spores may remain in the gastrointestinal tracts of infected patients, resulting in recurrent C. difficile infection (r-CDI) [5, 6].

Opportunistic bacteria such as C. difficile colonize the intestine as a result of the misuse and administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics, which disturb the gut microbiota and decrease the production of metabolites required to keep the intestinal tract healthy [7, 8]. For example, broad-spectrum antibiotics may interfere with the gut microbiota species that transform primary bile acids into secondary bile acids, resulting in increased levels of primary bile acids that are required to promote the germination of C. difficile spores [4, 9–11]. Upon successful colonization, toxigenic C. difficile strains predominantly produce toxin A (TcdA) and toxin B (TcdB), the principal virulence factors responsible for intestinal epithelial damage and the hallmark clinical manifestations of C. difficile infection, including diarrhea, pseudomembranous colitis, and potentially fatal colonic injury [12]. Although a subset of hypervirulent strains also produce the binary toxin (CDT), its presence is strain restricted and its contribution to disease severity remains controversial [13]. However, some studies indicated that CDT increases the severity of CDI symptoms and patients infected with CDT-producing strains had higher case fatality rates than patients infected with other strains [14–17].

Metronidazole (MTZ) was previously recommended for the treatment of adult CDI. Yet, it is no longer recommended because of the high rate of treatment failure, and its usage is currently limited to non-severe CDI infections when patients lack access to vancomycin or fidaxomicin, the second two approved anti-C. difficile drugs [18]. Currently, the only available treatment options for CDI are vancomycin (VAN) and fidaxomicin (FDX) [19]. Nevertheless, neither drug has satisfactory treatment outcomes. Following treatment discontinuation, recurrent CDI occurs in approximately 35% and 20% of patients treated with VAN and FDX, respectively [20, 21]. Furthermore, C. difficile is becoming less susceptible to these drugs [22, 23]. Moreover, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is an effective treatment for recurrent C. difficile infection because it restores the gut microbial balance. FDA-approved products such as Rebyota® and Vowst™ offer standardized, safer alternatives. However, FMT has limitations, including a risk of infection transmission, variability in donor material, and unclear long-term safety [24, 25]. Taken together, there is an unmet need for innovative, potent therapies to treat CDI.

Compared with the conventional de novo drug discovery approach, in which new therapeutic compounds are designed and developed from scratch, drug repurposing, the strategy of identifying new clinical uses for existing drugs, can substantially reduce the time and cost required to develop new medications [26, 27]. Veterinary medicine, in particular, has served as an important source of antimicrobial agents later adopted for human use. Well-established examples include ivermectin [28], praziquantel, and nitroimidazoles such as metronidazole [29], which transitioned from veterinary or antiparasitic applications to become mainstays of human infectious disease therapy. Notably, ridinilazole, a compound initially developed for veterinary use, has emerged as a highly promising therapeutic for CDI in humans [30]. Ridinilazole exhibits potent and selective anti-C. difficile activity, minimal disruption of the gut microbiota, and significantly lower recurrence rates compared with VAN in advanced clinical trials. This precedent highlights the translational potential of veterinary-derived antimicrobial scaffolds for CDI and supports the exploration of additional veterinary antibiotics with favorable selectivity and mechanistic relevance.

By utilizing the drug repurposing approach, we identified carbadox (CRX) as a potent anti-C. difficile inhibitor [31]. CRX is a synthetic quinoxaline-di-N-oxide antibiotic historically used in veterinary medicine, primarily as a growth promoter and to prevent swine dysentery and salmonellosis in pigs [32, 33]. The compound is typically administered via feed at doses ranging from 10 to 55 mg/kg in pigs, depending on the treatment duration and indication [34]. Mechanistically, CRX exerts a bactericidal effect by inhibiting DNA synthesis and inducing DNA strand breaks through the generation of reactive intermediates following reductive activation, a process that is enhanced under anaerobic conditions. This mode of action underlies its pronounced activity against anaerobic and microaerophilic pathogens, particularly Clostridium spp. and Brachyspira spp., with reported minimum inhibitory concentrations ranging from 0.002 to 0.5 µg/mL [35–37]. The preferential efficacy of CRX against Gram-positive bacteria and obligate anaerobes provides a strong mechanistic rationale for its activity against C. difficile, an obligate anaerobic pathogen that relies on tightly regulated DNA replication for vegetative growth and toxin production.

Despite its demonstrated efficacy in controlling enteric infections in animals, concerns have been raised regarding its long-term safety profile, particularly its potential for genotoxic and carcinogenic effects following chronic exposure [38]. While its carcinogenic risk in humans has not been formally evaluated by the U.S. Integrated Risk Information System (IRIS) or the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) and it is still under review by the food and drug administration (FDA), CRX has been prohibited for use in food-producing animals in regions such as the European Union, Australia, and Canada due to concerns about human health risks [32, 39].

We previously reported that CRX has a potent anti-C. difficile activity, standing as a top hit from screening of the microbial metabolite library against C. difficile [31], but it is uncertain how CRX affects the C. difficile growth as well as C. difficile virulence factors. Building upon our previous work, the aim of this study was to comprehensively profile the anti-C. difficile activity of CRX. This work focused on evaluating the CRX’s activity against a broad panel of clinical C. difficile isolates and investigating its bactericidal dynamics, post-antibiotic effect, and potential synergism with standard anti-C. difficile drugs. Additionally, we examined its impact on toxin production at subinhibitory concentrations, evaluated vegetative growth following spore activation under germination-permissive conditions, and elucidated its mechanism of action against C. difficile. Finally, the efficacy of CRX was assessed in both acute and recurrent mouse models of CDI.

Materials and methods

The C. difficile clinical isolates used in the study are detailed in Table S1. Drugs were purchased as follows: colistin and carbadox (Cayman Chemicals), fidaxomicin (Biosynth Carbosynth); metronidazole (Alfa Aesar); vancomycin (Gold Biotechnology); gentamicin; kanamycin; and linezolid (Chem Impex); oxacillin; triclosan; and clindamycin (TCI America); and ciprofloxacin and rifaximin (Sigma-Aldrich). Carbadox was freshly prepared and dissolved in 1 M NaOH. All experiments were done in media with the pH adjusted to ~ 7.2 before adding bacteria.

Chemicals and media were purchased as follows: brain heart infusion broth and Anaerobic GasPak Sachets with Indicator (Becton Dickenson and Company), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Corning), yeast extract (Fisher Scientific), L-cysteine (Alfa Aesar), glycine, vitamin K, hemin, NAC (N-acetyl-l-cysteine), and 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) (Sigma-Aldrich), taurocholic acid (Chem Impex), trichloroacetic acid (G-Biosciences) and Hoechst 33342 and Dihydroethidium (MedChemExpress).

Determination of the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The MICs of CRX were determined against a panel of clinical isolates of C. difficile as described previously [31, 40, 41]. CRX and the control antibiotics; VAN, FDX, and MTZ, were serially diluted in 96-well plates and added to a bacterial solution of C. difficile (~ 5 × 105 CFU/mL) in brain heart infusion-supplemented (BHIS) broth. The 96-well plates were incubated anaerobically for 48 h before the MICs were determined.

Time-kill kinetics assay against logarithmic phase and stationary-phase cultures of C. difficile

To evaluate the killing kinetics activity of CRX against C. difficile, we used the time kill assay as previously mentioned [31, 42, 43]. C. difficile ATCC 43255 (ribotype 087) and AR-1095 (ribotype 027) cultures, at either the logarithmic phase (log-phase) or the stationary-phase, were exposed to CRX (at 3⋅ MIC and 5⋅ MIC) or control antibiotics (VAN, FDX, and MTZ) (at 5⋅ MIC) in triplicates. As a growth control, 1 M NaOH (at a final concentration ≤ 0.1% v/v) was included, and the pH adjusted to ~ 7.2. Aliquots were collected at the corresponding time points, diluted, and plated onto pre-reduced BHIS agar plates. Agar plates were then incubated anaerobically at 37 °C before the bacterial CFUs were determined.

Post-antibiotic effect (PAE)

The PAE of CRX was tested against C. difficile ATCC 43255 (ribotype 087) and AR-1095 (ribotype 027) following the procedure previously described [44, 45]. Briefly, a log-phase culture of C. difficile was diluted to approximately 106 CFU/mL in pre-reduced BHIS broth and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C with either CRX, VAN, or FDX (at 5⋅ MIC, in triplicates) for 1 h, while 1 M NaOH (at a final concentration of 0.1%) was used as a negative control, and the pH adjusted to ~ 7.2. Drugs were then removed by diluting each tube in fresh pre-reduced BHIS broth and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Samples (100 µL) were collected every 2 h, serially diluted, and plated onto pre-reduced BHIS agar plates. Plates were incubated at 37 °C to determine the number of viable CFUs. The PAE was calculated as follows (PAE = T – C), where T and C are the time needed for bacteria treated with drugs and the negative control, respectively, to achieve a 1 log10 CFU/mL increase.

Toxin inhibition assay against log-phase and stationary-phase cultures of C. difficile

To investigate the CRX’s inhibitory effect on C. difficile toxin production, we used the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay kit (tgc BIOMICS, Germany) as outlined in previous studies [40, 46]. CRX and control antibiotics (VAN, FDX, and MTZ), at 0.5× MIC in triplicates and 1 M NaOH (final concentration of 0.01%, as a negative control), were added to log-phase or stationary-phase cultures of C. difficile ATCC 43255. Following a 6-hour incubation period, supernatants from the tubes were collected, and the total concentration of C. difficile toxins (TcdA and TcdB) was determined following the manufacturer’s instructions. The optical density (OD450 and OD620) was measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 Hybrid microplate reader. To minimize confounding effects due to growth inhibition, the bacterial CFU for tested samples were determined.

Vegetative C. difficile growth and viability following spore germination

C. difficile ATCC 43255 was grown on pre-reduced 70:30 agar plates to enhance spore formation [47]. After 6 days of incubation, the spores were suspended in PBS, washed twice with PBS, heated to 65 °C for 45 min. to eliminate vegetative cells. Spore purity was assessed by phase-contrast microscopy prior to use, confirming that ≥ 95% of the preparation consisted of phase-bright spores, with ≤ 5% residual vegetative cells. The purified spore suspension was stored at 4 °C until use.

To evaluate the inhibitory activity of CRX on vegetative growth following spore activation under germination-permissive conditions, purified spores C. difficile ATCC 43255 were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C with CRX and control antibiotics (VAN and FDX) (at 5× MIC in triplicates) in pre-reduced BHIS broth containing germinant (0.2% taurocholic acid (TA) and 50 mM glycine) as reported previously [48, 49]. 1 M NaOH (final concentration of 0.1%), with or without germinant, served as positive and negative controls, respectively. Assays were performed in triplicate. After 48 h of incubation, aliquots (100 µL) were washed, serially diluted and plated onto pre-reduced BHIS agar supplemented with 0.1% taurocholic acid to enumerate total CFU (vegetative cells plus spores). Heat-resistant spore counts were determined by subjecting aliquots to heat treatment (65 °C for 45 min) prior to plating. This assay was designed to assess the ability of CRX to suppress vegetative growth and survival following spore germination, rather than to measure the activity of CRX on the spores germination kinetics.

Frequency of spontaneous mutation

CRX was tested against C. difficile to ascertain the probability of spontaneous mutation formation as previously mentioned [50, 51]. Briefly, BHIS agar was mixed with CRX and VAN to reach final concentrations of 5 × MIC and 10 × MIC. The mixture was then placed into plates and allowed to dry. Before the plates were examined for potential bacterial growth, an inoculum of approximately 109 CFU/mL of C. difficile ATCC 43255 was placed over them and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 h.

Cell cytotoxicity assay

To evaluate the potential toxic effect of CRX, cytotoxicity assay was performed against cell lines as has been described previously [52, 53]. Human epithelial colorectal (Caco-2) were cultured at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ in EMEM supplemented with 20% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were seeded into treated 96-well plates (80–90% confluence) and exposed to CRX (up to 128 µg/mL). After 24 h, cell viability was quantified using an MTS/PMS assay with absorbance read at 490 nm. Percent viability was calculated relative to the control (1 M NaOH at a final concentration of ≤ 1%).

Checkerboard assay

To evaluate the interactions between CRX and the anti-C. difficile drugs VAN, MTZ, FDX, and rifaximin (RFX)), a standard checkerboard assay was utilized [40]. Briefly, two-fold serial dilutions of CRX were combined with corresponding two-fold serial dilutions of each comparator antibiotic in sterile 96-well microtiter plates. A standardized C. difficile inoculum (~ 5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL) was added to each well, and plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 h prior to assessment of bacterial growth. The fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) index was derived using the following formula: FIC index = FICA + FICB, where FICA = MICA+B/MICA and FICB = MICB+A/MICB, where MICA or MICB are the MCs of drugs A and B alone, respectively, and MICA+B and MICB+A are the MICs of the drugs in combination. An FIC index of ≤ 0.5 indicates synergistic interactions (SYN); an FIC value of > 0.5 to 1.25 was considered additive (ADD); an FIC value of > 1.25 to 4 was considered indifference (IND); and an FIC value of > 4 was classified as antagonistic (ANT) [40, 54].

Macromolecular synthesis assays

To assess the effects of CRX on essential cellular processes in C. difficile and identify potential molecular targets, macromolecular synthesis assays were performed as described previously [55]. Radiolabeled precursors were utilized to monitor the synthesis of key macromolecules; [3H] thymidine (0.25 µCi/mL) for DNA, [3H] uridine (0.4 µCi/mL) for RNA, [14C] leucine (0.4 µCi/mL) for protein, [14C] acetic acid sodium (0.3 µCi/mL) for lipids, and [14C] N-acetylglucosamine (0.2 µCi/mL) for cell wall. All radiolabeled compounds were obtained from Revvity. Briefly, C. difficile ATCC 43255 during the early log-phase was washed with PBS and resuspended in pre-reduced BHIS broth (OD600 of ~ 1.2) and then supplemented with the respective radiolabeled precursors before the addition of CRX. Ciprofloxacin, rifaximin, linezolid, triclosan, and oxacillin were used as positive controls for inhibiting DNA, RNA, protein, lipid, and peptidoglycan synthesis, respectively. Following a 4-h of anaerobic incubation at 37 °C, reactions were terminated by adding ice-cold 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). The TCA-precipitable macromolecules were filtered through a glass fiber membrane filter, GF-75 (Sterlitech) using a Millipore glass microanalysis filter holder kit (Sigma Aldrich). The precipitated macromolecules were washed with 5% TCA (10 mL) followed by 75% ethanol (10 mL) to remove unincorporated radiolabels. The glass filters were air-dried at room temperature before using a Tricarb 5110TR liquid scintillation analyzer (Perkin Elmer) to quantify the percent incorporation of radiolabeled materials.

DNA oxidative damage

An OxiSelect Oxidative DNA Damage ELISA Kit (Cell Biolabs) was used to detect the level of 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), an oxidative DNA damage byproducts as reported previously [35]. Briefly, C. difficile cultures (~ 10⁶ CFU/mL) in the early exponential growth phase were handled anaerobically and washed twice with pre-reduced PBS by centrifugation (5,000 × g, 10 min) to remove residual media. C. difficile was treated with either CRX and VAN (negative control) (at 5× MIC), or H2O2 (200 µM, as a positive control) and 1 M NaOH (at a final concentration of 0.1%) was used as a negative control. Following treatment, cells were harvested, and DNA extraction was performed using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions; these downstream steps, including DNA extraction and ELISA measurements, were conducted aerobically as required by the kit protocol. Purified DNA was resuspended in sterile water, and 8-OHdG levels were quantified using the OxiSelect Oxidative DNA Damage ELISA kit following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Fluorescence microscopy

To confirm that CRX treatment causes intracellular DNA damage, treated cells were examined under a fluorescence microscope using the blue fluorescent DNA stain, Hoechst [56]. Briefly, an overnight BHIS culture of C. difficile ATCC 43255 was washed and diluted in pre-reduced PBS. Then, the cells were treated with either CRX (at 2× and 5× MIC) or VAN (at 5× MIC). and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 150 min. 1 M NaOH (final concentration of ≤ 0.1%) was used as a negative control. Then, Hoechst 33,342 stain was added at a final concentration of 12 µg/mL and the mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 25 min. The cells were centrifuged again and then re-suspended in 500 µL of sterile PBS. To image the cells, 4 µL of culture was placed on 1% agar pad mounted on a slide and sealed with a coverslip. Imaging was performed using fluorescence microscopy (Nikon, ECLIPSE Ti2, Japan) with a Digital Sight 50 M outfitted with CMOS image sensors, PLAN APO λD 60xOIL OFN25 DIC (Differential Interference Contrast) N2 objective, and a DAPI filter was utilized to observe the blue fluorescence of the Hoechst stain of the samples. All channels were exposed for 400 ms, and the intensity values for the DIC and fluorescence filters were 157 and 5, respectively.

Reactive oxygen species analysis

The production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by C. difficile ATCC 43255 after treatment with CRX was evaluated using a peroxynitrite indicator, 2’,7’- dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), as reported earlier [57]. Briefly, log-phase C. difficile ATCC 43255 cultures were handled anaerobically and washed twice with pre-reduced PBS by centrifugation (5,000 × g, 10 min) to remove residual growth medium. The bacterial pellets were resuspended and adjusted to an OD₆₀₀ of ~ 1 in pre-reduced PBS under anaerobic conditions. Bacteria were treated with either CRX alone, CRX with 10 mM NAC (a free radical scavenger), or VAN (at 5× MIC). Additionally, H2O2 (500 µM) and 1 M NaOH (final concentration of 0.1%) were included as a positive and a negative control, respectively. Samples were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 120 min, after which DCFH-DA (final concentration 10 µM) was added and the mixtures were further incubated anaerobically for 30 min at 37 °C. Fluorescence was subsequently measured aerobically using a BioTek microtiter plate reader at excitation/emission wavelengths of 485/533 nm. Autofluorescence from bacterial cells incubated without the probe and background fluorescence from PBS were measured and subtracted to calculate net fluorescence. Experiments were performed in three independent runs, each conducted in triplicate.

Measurement of intracellular superoxide production using dihydroethidium

Superoxide production was assessed using dihydroethidium (DHE) as previously described [58]. Log-phase C. difficile cultures were washed twice with pre-reduced PBS and adjusted to an OD₆₀₀ of approximately 1. Cultures were incubated anaerobically with CRX or VAN at 5× MIC for 150 min at 37 °C. Hydrogen peroxide (500 µM) and 1 M NaOH (final concentration of 0.1%) were included as positive and negative controls, respectively. DHE was added to a final concentration of 5 µM during the final 30 min of incubation. Fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader at excitation/emission wavelengths of 518/605 nm. Background fluorescence was subtracted, and fluorescence signals were normalized to cell density. Where indicated, NAC was included as a superoxide scavenger to confirm assay specificity.

C. difficile mouse model

Female 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice (Jackson Laboratories) were housed in individually ventilated autoclaved cages and received sterile food and water ad libitum throughout the duration of the experiment. The CDI mouse model was performed as described previously [27, 59]. This model was designed to assess early therapeutic intervention during acute CDI rather than treatment of advanced disease. The mice were sensitized to CDI with administration of an antibiotic cocktail containing kanamycin (0.4 mg/mL), gentamicin (35 mg/mL), colistin (850 U/mL), metronidazole (0.215 mg/mL), vancomycin (0.045 mg/mL) and sucrose (75 mg/mL), for 5 days. Afterwards, the mice were switched to regular autoclaved water for 2 days and were administered a single I.P. dose of clindamycin (10 mg/kg) one day before infection. Mice were infected with 3.9 × 105 CFU/mL of C. difficile ATCC 43255 spores via oral gavage (7.8 × 10⁴ CFU per mouse). Following infection, mice were randomly allocated to treatment groups (n = 8 per group) using a simple randomization approach based on cage-independent assignment. Two hours post-infection, mice were treated with either CRX (20 mg/kg) once daily or twice daily via oral gavage. As controls, one group received oral gavage treatment with VAN (10 mg/kg) once a day orally, or the vehicle (control). Mice were monitored for symptoms of the disease during the 5 days of treatment.

To assess the ability of the tested compounds to prevent CDI recurrence, surviving mice were maintained and monitored daily from therapy cessation (day 5) through day 21 post-infection. Recurrence was defined as the reappearance of clinical disease following completion of therapy, including renewed body weight loss (≥ 10%), diarrhea, perianal soiling, or clinical signs of morbidity (ruffled fur, hunched posture, lethargy), as well as mortality. To quantify the C. difficile toxin titer, fecal pellets were collected aseptically on day 5 and suspended in sterile PBS, and the supernatants were collected for C. difficile toxin evaluation, using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay kit (tgc BIOMICS) [60, 61].

Results

CRX exhibits a potent anti-C. difficile activity

The anti-C. difficile activity of CRX was evaluated against a panel of 26 pathogenic clinical isolates of C. difficile, including the most common ribotypes. Table 1 illustrates that CRX inhibited the growth of 90% (MIC90) of the tested isolates at the concentration of 2 µg/mL. We discovered that the activity of CRX was comparable to that of the standard-of-care VAN, which showed MIC50 and MIC90 of 0.5 and 2 µg/mL, respectively. The strains tested were inhibited by FDX at doses between ≤ 0.008 and 0.125 µg/mL. In contrast to MTZ, which had MIC50 and MIC90 values of 0.5 and 1 µg/mL, respectively, FDX had MIC50 and MIC90 values of 0.03 and 0.06 µg/mL against the tested isolates.

CRX is bactericidal against log-phase and stationary-phase C. difficile

To evaluate the killing kinetics of CRX against C. difficile, time–kill assays were performed using log-phase cultures of C. difficile ATCC 43255 and AR-1095. As shown in Fig. 1, exposure to CRX at 3× MIC resulted in an initial reduction in viable cell counts followed by partial recovery over the 12 h time course, indicating a transient and incomplete killing effect rather than a strictly bacteriostatic phenotype. In contrast, CRX at 5× MIC exhibited a rapid and sustained bactericidal effect against both C. difficile ATCC 43255 (Fig. 1A) and AR-1095 (Fig. 1B), with bacterial counts reduced below the limit of detection within 4 h. Notably, CRX demonstrated greater killing activity than MTZ and FDX in the time–kill assay. While MTZ and FDX reduced C. difficile counts to below the detection limit within 24 h, VAN produced only a modest reduction of approximately 2 log₁₀ CFU/mL after 24 h (Fig. 1).

CRX suppresses vegetative C. difficile growth following spore germination

Following confirmation of CRX-mediated inhibition of C. difficile toxin production, we next evaluated its impact on vegetative growth and viability following spore activation under germination-permissive conditions. Purified spores were exposed to taurocholate and glycine to initiate germination, and antibiotic effects were assessed after 48 h of incubation. CRX, at 5× MIC, significantly reduced total viable counts and heat-resistant spore counts by approximately 4.5 and 3.8 log₁₀ CFU/mL, respectively (Fig. 3A and B). Mechanism of action of CRX against C. difficile

Macromolecular synthesis assays with vegetative cells

To investigate the mechanism of action of CRX against C. difficile, macromolecular biosynthesis assays were first conducted using the vegetative cells of C. difficile ATCC 43255. Briefly, incorporation of the radiolabeled precursors, [3H] thymidine into DNA, [3H] uridine into RNA, [14C] leucine into protein, [14C] acetic acid sodium into lipids, and [14C] N-acetylglucosamine into the cell wall, was monitored and compared to the positive controls (ciprofloxacin, rifaximin, linezolid, triclosan, and oxacillin for DNA, RNA, protein, lipid, and peptidoglycan synthesis, respectively). Interestingly, CRX exhibited a pronounced inhibitory effect on DNA biosynthesis, reducing the incorporation of [3H] thymidine by 95.5 ± 3.2%, compared with 100.4 ± 0.78% for the positive control, ciprofloxacin (Fig. 5). This effect was followed by an inhibition of protein synthesis, which was significantly affected, with approximately 70.24 ± 7.1%, compared with linezolid (99.83 ± 2%), followed by lipid synthesis inhibition at 60.87 ± 8.5%, relative to triclosan (99.69 ± 0.3%). CRX exhibited only moderate inhibition of RNA or peptidoglycan with a reduction of 37.25 ± 7.9 and 28.38 ± 5.6%, respectively. These values were notably lower than those observed for the positive controls, rifaximin (95.48 ± 3.4%) and oxacillin (59.77 ± 6.9%), suggesting that RNA and cell wall synthesis are not primary targets of CRX.

Since inhibition of DNA biosynthesis was the most profoundly affected pathway following CRX treatment, we sought to further validate this proposed mechanism of action. CRX Induces DNA damage in C. difficile

Furthermore, we used the fluorescent DNA stain, Hoechst, to examine the DNA morphology of C. difficile cells treated with CRX in order to test and confirm that the anti-C. difficile activity of CRX is mediated through DNA damage. Prior to fluorescence microscopy imaging, treated C. difficile cells treated with CRX, VAN (5× MIC), or NaOH for 150 min were stained with Hoechst. Only foci co-localized with Hoechst in the deconvolved three-dimensional image were quantified. As can be seen in Fig. 6E, CRX treatment caused concentration dependent DNA fragmentation as demonstrated by the percentage of cells with foci (42% and 78% with 2× MIC and 5× MIC, respectively) (Fig. 6F). Conversely, cells treated with VAN exhibited minimal DNA damage (~ 12%), comparable to that of the negative control.

Measurement of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production

We next evaluated the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) following CRX treatment. Quinoxaline 1,4-dioxide derivatives (QdNOs), including CRX, have been reported to exert antibacterial activity through ROS generation, leading to oxidative stress and DNA damage [35, 36, 62]. Log-phase C. difficile cultures were exposed to CRX for 120 min, and intracellular ROS levels were quantified using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). Notably, CRX treatment (5× MIC) resulted in an approximately 75% increase in ROS production compared with the H₂O₂-treated positive control (Fig. 6C), whereas VAN, as expected, did not significantly alter ROS levels. Co-treatment with the ROS scavenger N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC; 10 mM) markedly attenuated CRX-induced ROS generation, reducing fluorescence to levels comparable to the untreated control (~ 30%).

To further characterize the nature of CRX-induced oxidative stress, superoxide production was directly assessed using dihydroethidium (DHE). CRX-treated C. difficile exhibited a pronounced increase in superoxide levels (~ 8100 RFU), whereas co-administration of NAC significantly reduced fluorescence to approximately 5500 RFU. In contrast, VAN-treated cells displayed lower superoxide levels (~ 5000 RFU), consistent with its ROS-independent mechanism of action (Fig. 6D).

Importantly, time-kill kinetic analyses demonstrated that NAC alone did not affect C. difficile viability. However, the addition of NAC to CRX treatment substantially diminished the bactericidal activity of CRX, resulting in bacterial growth kinetics comparable to those of the untreated control (Fig. 6B). Together, these findings provide strong evidence that CRX-mediated killing of C. difficile is driven, at least in part, by ROS, particularly superoxide generation, and that scavenging oxidative species compromises its antibacterial efficacy.

CRX protects mice against CDI and prevents CDI recurrence

Given the potent activity of CRX against C. difficile in vitro (i.e., under controlled laboratory conditions outside a living organism), we next evaluated the in vivo efficacy of CRX (i.e., within a living organism) using a mouse model of C. difficile infection (CDI) (Fig. 7). Mice infected with C. difficile were treated with CRX (20 mg/kg) either once or twice a day, or with VAN (10 mg/kg) once a day. As illustrated in Fig. 7A; after 5 days of treatment, CRX (once daily treatment) protected 62.5% of mice against the lethal C. difficile challenge, whereas increasing the frequency of CRX treatment to 20 mg/kg twice daily resulted in protecting 100% of mice against CDI, similar to VAN. On the other hand, the vehicle-treated mice (control) succumbed to infection, and all mice died on day 3 of the treatment (Fig. 7A).

To assess post-treatment disease relapse, surviving mice were monitored daily from therapy cessation on day 5 through day 21 post-infection for the reappearance of clinical disease signs (≥ 10% body weight loss, diarrhea, perianal soiling, or signs of morbidity) and/or mortality. Under both CRX dosing regimens, mice exhibited sustained clinical recovery, with no observable recurrence of disease among mice surviving to the end of therapy (0% recurrence; 5/5 mice for once-daily and 8/8 mice for twice-daily dosing). In contrast, VAN-treated mice exhibited delayed disease recurrence after treatment cessation, resulting in only 37.5% survival (3/8) by day 21 (Fig. 7B).

We also quantified the titer of C. difficile toxin in the fecal pellets of the surviving mice. For the vehicle, group, fecal pellets were collected on day 2 post-infection as they succumbed to infection rapidly, whereas samples from treatment groups were collected on day 5 post-infection, and the levels of toxins found were determined. CRX-treated mice exhibited substantially reduced fecal toxin titers (Fig. 7C). Because samples were obtained at non-contemporaneous time points, direct quantitative comparisons across groups should be interpreted with caution, and the data primarily reflect toxin burden at the respective disease stages. Consistent with these results, longitudinal body weight measurements revealed distinct treatment-associated trajectories. Vehicle-treated mice (control group) exhibited rapid and progressive weight loss leading to death, underscoring the severity of untreated CDI (Fig. 7D). In VAN-treated mice, weight loss resumed after treatment cessation, coinciding with disease recurrence between days 8 and 10, followed by partial recovery in surviving animals, consistent with previous reports [59]. In contrast, CRX-treated mice showed only transient early weight loss, followed by steady and sustained recovery throughout the study period (Fig. 7D). Collectively, these findings indicate that CRX mitigates disease severity and supports durable clinical recovery more effectively than VAN in this model.

Discussion

C. difficile is considered the leading cause of antibiotic-associated diarrhea in the USA and CDI has been reported to be on the rise despite continued efforts aimed at combating C. difficile [63]. Hence, there is an urgent need for new effective therapeutics to tackle CDI. We previously screened the microbial metabolite library against C. difficile [31] and CRX was identified as a potent anti-C. difficile inhibitor. Herein, we set out to comprehensively profile the anti-C. difficile activity of CRX both in vitro and in vivo.

In this study, we assessed the anti-C. difficile activity of CRX against a panel of pathogenic C. difficile strains. CRX exhibited potent activity against C. difficile isolates, inhibiting their growth at concentrations ranging from 1 to 2 µg/mL, which was comparable to the activity of VAN. While CRX’s MICs are slightly higher than those of standard therapeutics, they remain within a therapeutically relevant range and are consistent with previous reports evaluating novel agents against C. difficile [64]. The drug also maintained the same potency against the different ribotypes of C. difficile, including well-characterized hypervirulent and clinically relevant strains such as ribotype 027 (e.g., ATCC BAA-1870) and ribotype 087 (e.g., ATCC 43255). Although ribotype 027 is no longer universally predominant, it has historically been associated with major global outbreaks and increased disease severity, and it remains a widely used reference strain for evaluating anti-C. difficile activity and virulence-related phenotypes [65]. Collectively, the observed activity against these established ribotypes supports the broad anti-C. difficile potential of the compound, while acknowledging the evolving epidemiology of C. difficile ribotypes.

Given that the bacteria, at the stationary-phase, are significantly less susceptible to antibiotics than fast-growing (log-phase) bacteria [66], significantly, CRX exhibited rapid bactericidal activity against C. difficile in both phases. It is interesting to note that rapid bactericidal activity is highly desirable for CDI to reduce the emergence of bacterial resistance to drugs and prevent the progression of the disease [67]. In contrast, VAN showed a gradual decline in the C. difficile count throughout the log-phase, while FDX and MTZ required 24 h to show bactericidal activity against C. difficile, which is consistent with previous findings [68]. However, at the stationary-phase of culture, neither VAN nor MTZ exhibited any activity against C. difficile, whereas CRX significantly reduced C. difficile in comparison to FDX [68].

Determining the PAE of new antimicrobials agents is an important step to determine the frequency of dosing [69, 70]. Therefore, we determined the CRX’s PAE against C. difficile. It had a prolonged PAE of up to 14 h., exceeding that of FDX and VAN, which had shorter PAEs (2–3 h, and 5–7 h, respectively), in line with previous studies [44, 68, 94]. Drugs with extended PAE are highly desirable because they have many benefits, such as reducing the cost of treatment, improving patient compliance, and reducing drug toxicity [71].

Beyond direct bactericidal activity, CRX significantly modulated key virulence determinants of C. difficile. At sub-inhibitory concentrations, CRX reduced TcdA and TcdB production in both log-phase and stationary-phase, with efficacy comparable to FDX, a drug well recognized for suppressing toxin synthesis [72]. Importantly, viable cell counts were not reduced at these concentrations (data not shown), indicating that toxin inhibition was not a secondary consequence of impaired growth but rather reflected a direct anti-virulence effect. In contrast, VAN and MTZ did not reduce toxin levels, consistent with previous reports [74, 73]. Given the central role of toxins in CDI pathogenesis [74], this property represents a meaningful therapeutic advantage.

Initiating CDI requires the germination of C. difficile spores. The sensing of bile acids, such as taurocholate, glycocholate, deoxycholate, and l-glycine, by the host’s small intestine causes the C. difficile spores to germinate into active vegetative cells [75]. Therefore, we evaluated the ability of CRX to interfere with the vegetative growth and survival under spore germination-permissive conditions, rather than its effects on early germination events. CRX significantly reduced both total viable counts and heat-resistant spore-associated CFU after prolonged incubation, indicating effective inhibition of vegetative cells emerging from germinated spores. Importantly, CRX did not inhibit spore viability, germination, or dipicolinic acid release in dedicated germination assays (data not shown), a finding that is mechanistically consistent with its established mode of action targeting DNA synthesis and intracellular metabolism, processes absent in dormant spores. The observed activity of CRX was comparable to FDX, which is known to limit vegetative growth following spore germination, and superior to VAN, in agreement with previous reports [49]. This experimental design with the prolonged incubation period (48 h) was intended to model post-germination vegetative growth and persistence in a bile acid–rich environment, mimicking conditions encountered by spores upon entry into the host intestine, where therapeutic agents act on newly germinated cells rather than on dormant spores. Yet, the long incubation effects (sporulation, nutrient depletion) may have confounding effects on the results. Therefore, in our future investigations, outgrowth assays will be conducted over a limited time window (≤ 8 h), which precedes the onset of secondary sporulation and significant nutrient depletion under these experimental conditions.

In healthcare, combination therapy is frequently utilized to obtain the benefits of combined medications, including distinct modes of action, reduced toxicity, possible synergism, and a decreased likelihood of developing resistance to both agents [76, 77]. This approach is particularly relevant for the management of CDI, where severe or refractory disease may limit the effectiveness of monotherapy. Checkerboard analyses conducted using the reference strain C. difficile ATCC 43255 further substantiated these findings, demonstrating concentration-dependent synergistic interactions marked by reduced effective concentrations of CRX when combined with either VAN or MTZ. Collectively, these results indicate that CRX has the capacity to potentiate standard CDI therapies and support its further evaluation as a component of combination regimens, especially in clinical settings where enhanced antibacterial activity or dose-sparing effects may be beneficial.

Compared with standard antibiotics used for C. difficile infection (CDI), CRX demonstrates a unique mechanism of action. To elucidate its mode of action, we assessed the inhibitory effect of CRX on the incorporation of radiolabeled precursors for macromolecular synthesis, including DNA, RNA, proteins, lipids and cell wall. Notably, CRX exhibited a pronounced inhibitory effect on DNA synthesis, closely matching ciprofloxacin, the control drug, while it exhibited a moderate inhibition in RNA, protein synthesis. In contrast, its inhibitory effect on lipid and cell wall synthesis were lower than those observed for their respective control inhibitors. These findings align with previous reports [78–80], suggesting that DNA synthesis inhibition results in a sharp decline in bacterial protein and lipid synthesis. Collectively, the results of the macromolecular synthesis assays indicate that CRX primarily interferes with C. difficile’s DNA synthesis, with secondary inhibitory effects on protein and lipid biosynthesis. The relatively weaker impact on RNA and peptidoglycan synthesis further supports the notion that CRX selectively targets nucleic acid metabolism while also affecting other essential macromolecular processes.

Previous studies have demonstrated that quinoxaline 1,4-di-N-oxides (such as CRX) induces oxidative stress in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria [35]. Given that the presence of 8-OHdG is a hallmark of oxidative DNA damage [35, 36], it is interesting to note that CRX-treated C. difficile exhibited significantly higher levels of 8-OHdG, whereas the VAN-treated group was similar to the negative control group, which is consistent with other findings [81]. Moreover, in accordance with previous studies [82–85], quinoxaline 1,4-di-N-oxides are enzymatically reduced by bacterial metabolism under anaerobic conditions to produce products that can interact with bacterial DNA, inducing mutations and degradation. Consistent with this, our findings indicate that CRX treatment was associated with DNA damage in C. difficile cells, suggesting that oxidative stress may contribute to its antibacterial activity. However, additional studies will be required to determine whether ROS generation plays a direct causal role in bacterial killing.

Consistent with this mechanism, CRX induced significant oxidative stress in C. difficile, as evidenced by elevated levels of 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), a hallmark of oxidative DNA damage [35, 36]. CRX inhibits C. difficile growth that is associated with increased oxidative stress signals and subsequent oxidative damage which is consistent with emerging paradigms in C. difficile pathophysiology and microbial competition within the gut. A notable precedent is reuterin, a low-molecular-weight antimicrobial metabolite produced by Lactobacillus reuteri, which was shown to kill C. difficile through the induction of intracellular oxidative stress and subsequent macromolecular damage [57].

This mechanism coincides with the observed bactericidal activity of CRX that has been observed, indicating that its efficacy against C. difficile may be attributed to its capacity to produce ROS and subsequently damage bacterial DNA. Notably, ridinilazole exerts its anti-C. difficile activity by binding to the DNA minor groove of C. difficile [86], thereby disrupting cell division, manifested as bacterial filamentation, and impairing energy production, ultimately arresting bacterial growth and reducing toxin release. In contrast, ibezapolstat selectively inhibits DNA polymerase IIIC, a replication enzyme unique to low-G + C Gram-positive bacteria, resulting in targeted suppression of DNA replication without broad microbiota disruption [87]. Collectively, these mechanistic distinctions underscore the therapeutic value of targeting DNA integrity and redox homeostasis in C. difficile and support the continued exploration of CRX and related quinoxaline-based scaffolds as mechanistically innovative leads for CDI drug discovery.

The minimal cytotoxicity of CRX in mammalian cells, with IC₅₀ values > 128 µg/mL in Vero cell viability assays (previously reported [31] and shown in Fig. S2A) and in human colonic epithelial Caco-2 cells following 24 h exposure (Fig. S2B), together with its potent in vitro activity against C. difficile and the absence of detectable mutagenicity in C. difficile (Table S6), provided a strong rationale for evaluating its in vivo efficacy in both acute and recurrent CDI models. In the acute CDI mouse model, VAN (10 mg/kg) protected 100% of the mice for up to 5 days, as previously reported [27, 59]. Similarly, CRX (20 mg/kg, twice daily), was able to protect 100% of the mice against C. difficile during the 5-days treatment period, while once daily CRX treatment protected only 62.5% of the mice. This dosing-dependent difference in efficacy aligns with the post-antibiotic effect (PAE) observed in this study, wherein CRX suppressed C. difficile regrowth for approximately 12–14 h following drug removal. Accordingly, a twice-daily dosing regimen maintains inhibitory drug exposure across the full dosing interval, whereas once-daily administration allows a recovery window that compromises therapeutic efficacy. These findings provide a preliminary pharmacodynamic rationale for the superior performance of the twice-daily CRX regimen.

Symptomatic recurrence of CDI occurs in approximately 20% of patients and is challenging to treat [88, 89], and it has been reported that 1 out of every 5 patients who experienced C. difficile recurrence episodes died within 30 days of diagnosis [2]. Given our in vitro findings, which showed that CRX inhibited C. difficile growth and reduced the toxin production, we investigated the potential benefit of CRX in preventing C. difficile recurrence. According to earlier research, VAN-treated mice were prone to C. difficile recurrence, and as expected, 62.5% of them died after terminating VAN treatment [27, 59]. In contrast, CRX showed 0% recurrence among mice surviving to the end of therapy. An additional consideration for CDI therapeutics is their impact on the intestinal microbiota, as preservation of colonization resistance is strongly associated with reduced recurrence. Although gut microbiota composition was not directly evaluated in the present study, prior reports indicate that CRX exhibits relatively limited activity against key beneficial commensals, including Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, while demonstrating greater activity against Bacteroides spp [31]. This profile suggests a potentially narrower functional spectrum than conventional broad-spectrum agents such as MTZ and VAN and is conceptually aligned with the design strategy of several next-generation CDI therapeutics currently in late-stage clinical development such as CRS3123 that has been developed to selectively target C. difficile while minimizing collateral disruption of the gut microbiota [90], an approach that has been associated with improved sustained clinical response and lower recurrence rates in clinical trials.

Although our findings support CRX as a useful lead scaffold with meaningful activity against C. difficile, advancing it toward clinical application will require careful navigation of its known toxicity liabilities. Quinoxaline-1,4-di-N-oxide (QdNO) compounds, including CRX, have historically raised concerns related to genotoxicity and carcinogenicity, largely derived from high-dose, chronic exposure paradigms in food-animal settings [38, 91]. Importantly, these conditions differ fundamentally from the short-course, localized treatment regimens envisioned for C. difficile infection (CDI), where therapeutic benefit is driven by transient luminal exposure within the colon rather than prolonged systemic exposure. Even so, addressing these risks remains essential for clinical translation. A feasible translational strategy is to prioritize gastrointestinal-restricted exposure, using formulations that limit systemic absorption while maintaining high colonic concentrations, analogous to FDX. Future studies will incorporate host safety readouts, including colonic histopathology and serum chemistry, alongside pharmacokinetic analyses to confirm minimal systemic exposure. In parallel, SAR-guided optimization of the QdNO scaffold will be pursued to reduce genotoxic potential while preserving anaerobe-selective activity. Additionally, the synergistic activity observed between CRX and established anti-C. difficile agents suggests that combination therapy may further mitigate toxicity by enabling dose reduction without compromising efficacy. With these considerations in mind, our evaluation of CRX’s in vivo performance position CRX as a lead scaffold, rather than a final drug candidate. Robust chemical optimization and translational safety assessment will ultimately be indispensable for the safe and effective advancement of CRX-based therapeutics into the clinical setting, to refine the quinoxaline-di-N-oxide core, mitigate genotoxic liabilities, and preserve or enhance antimicrobial potency. Moreover, the substantial anatomical and microbiological similarities between porcine and human gastrointestinal tracts further strengthen the rationale for exploring CRX-derived scaffolds as short-course therapeutic agents for CDI [33, 92, 93], provided that safety-driven structural modifications are successfully implemented.

Although CRX treatment resulted in sustained clinical remission and improved survival, this study did not directly quantify fecal spore shedding, tissue-associated spore reservoirs, or longitudinal toxin levels following treatment cessation, or in vivo germination and sporulation markers, limiting definitive conclusions regarding spore eradication. In addition, fecal toxin measurements were collected at different infection time points (day 2 for vehicle-treated animals (control) due to rapid mortality and day 5 for treated groups), which limits direct comparisons of toxin levels across groups. Future studies incorporating time-matched sampling will be necessary to more precisely define the effects of CRX on toxin production during CDI infection. The impact of CRX on the intestinal microbiota was not evaluated in vivo, despite the importance of microbiota preservation in preventing CDI recurrence. In addition, although CRX is known to induce oxidative stress and DNA damage in bacterial cells, the precise contribution of these processes to its antibacterial activity was not definitively established in this study. Future studies incorporating normalization of fluorescence signals to viable cell counts, as well as direct assessment of CRX stability in the presence of NAC, would further strengthen the mechanistic interpretation. Furthermore, in vivo host toxicity was not directly assessed; comprehensive safety evaluations, including hepatic, renal, and hematological analyses, as well as assessments of potential oxidative or genotoxic effects in host tissues. Also, pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic parameters were not determined in the murine CDI model. Finally, we acknowledge that the study did not assess resistance development under prolonged or repeated sub-inhibitory exposure, nor did it evaluate potential cross-resistance with other quinoxaline derivatives.

Conclusion

Our in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated its potent anti-C. difficile activity, including low MICs, rapid bactericidal effects, and inhibition of both toxin production and inhibition of vegetative growth following spore activation at bactericidal concentrations. CRX treatment showed no recurrence among mice surviving to the end of therapy. Taken together, these findings establish CRX as a useful scaffold for CDI drug discovery, paving the way for the development of novel more exceptional anti-CDI therapeutics by leveraging its robust antibacterial properties and addressing its safety limitations through SAR-guided optimization to mitigate genotoxic liabilities, and preserve or enhance antimicrobial potency.
