 ABSTRACT

Additive manufacturing of single crystals made of Ni-based superalloys offers major cost savings for gas
turbine engines with the inclusion of internal cooling channels. However, the lack of understanding of the
effect of transient thermal conditions on solidification grain structure during additive manufacturing
hinders the potential for process control to maintain the single crystal quality. The use of high-fidelity
simulations through high performance computing to predict the evolution of the solidification
microstructure will enhance the abilities to tailor the microstructures through process optimization. Phase
field simulations are used to determine the effect local thermal conditions and defects on the stability of
the solidification morphology, specifically with respect to the onset of columnar-to-equiaxed transition
that results in the loss of the single crystal. The results are expected to be instrumental for developing
future surrogate models to speed up the integration of design and manufacturing of turbine blades under
the harsh in-service conditions.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Single crystals (SX) of Ni-based superalloys are used in the hottest sections of gas turbine engines.
During directional growth of a single crystal (SX) by casting, the microstructure consists of columnar
dendrites of the same orientation filling the entire grain [1]. The dendritic solidification microstructure
often causes inhomogeneous elemental partitioning with the primary dendrite spacing as large as several
hundred microns. Recently, the ability to produce large single crystals of the nickel-base superalloy
Inconel 718 (IN718) in the [001] direction by additive manufacturing (AM) has been demonstrated [2].
Due to high solidification velocities and high thermal gradients, the dendrite arm spacings are much
smaller compared to the classical Bridgman process. The selection of the substrate temperature and
methodology limited the production of SX to electron beam powder bed fusion (EB-PBF [2, 3]).
However, surface roughness and the drastic effect of part geometry on location specific thermal gradient
limit the expansion of EB-PBF SX to actual blade geometry with internal cooling channels.

Alternatively, localized control of the cooling rates via the use of overlapping dual lasers [4] in
commercially of the shelf (COTS) machines such as SLM 280 could be utilized with access to the
machine control software. Commercialization efforts for dual and multi-laser LPBF AM is motivated by
significant benefits due to increased productivity [5, 6], microstructure control [7, 8], and reduced residual
stress [9]. The PF simulations were performed using the MEUMAPPS-SL (Microstructure Evolution
Using Massively Parallel Phase Field Simulations code developed at the Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL), with its ability to realistically capture the growth aspects of solidification microstructure
development under AM thermal conditions [10].

In the long term, new processing controls and processing maps should be developed to optimize the
localized melting, and solidification microstructure with the aid of an ICME framework including process
simulations that accounts for the effects of local blade geometry. The current research is aimed at accurate
predictions of the solidification microstructure as a function of location-specific thermal profile to aid
future optimization of the AM processing windows that would ultimately result in significant energy
savings via improvements in material performance in harsh service environments (e.g., gas turbine
engines).

1.1 OBJECTIVE

The main goal is to utilize HPC to guide the development of powder bed fusion with or without auxiliary
heating to form single crystal IN718 for gas turbine engine applications. SX coupons of IN718 have been
demonstrated using electron beam powder bed fusion (EB-PBF) with constant process parameters across

 1

the whole build for simple geometries [2, 3]. However, manufacturing turbine blades with complicated
geometries has been a major challenge due to the need for a stable solidification mode in the
cellular/dendritic region without the onset of the columnar to equiaxed transition (CET) [11] under widely
varying thermal conditions dictated by local geometry. In addition, dendritic solidification results in inter-
dendritic liquid (IL) that promotes solidification cracking. While large undercooling reduces the tendency
for forming the IL [12, 13], it also leads to high s-l interface velocities that promote CET. Thermal
conditions must therefore be optimized such that CET does not occur during solidification of the single
crystal in the cellular / dendritic mode, while simultaneously eliminating hot cracking associated with the
formation of a persistent, continuous IL film. The tendency for CET due to heterogeneous nucleation is
particularly enhanced at the vicinity of internal surfaces and voids.

This study utilized high-fidelity, high performance computing (HPC) using the phase field (PF) approach
to simulate the evolution of the solidification microstructure including grain morphology,
microsegregation, and formation of IL over a range of time varying thermal gradients, and solidification
velocities of the solid-liquid (s-l) interface expected during the hybrid AM process for SX IN718.
Specifically, PF simulations integrated with a physically based heterogeneous nucleation model using the
classical nucleation theory [14, 15] were used to investigate the onset of CET under a range of thermal
gradients and solidification rates typically encountered under AM conditions. The MEUMAPPS-SL PF
were performed using the Kestrel leadership class HPC capability at the National Environmental
Research Laboratory (NREL) (currently National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR)) and the Cades cluster
at ORNL.

 2. APPROACH

The PF code MEUMAPPS-SL was developed in-house at ORNL through ExaAM, the additive
manufacturing application area for the US Exascale Computing Project. This section describes the PF
model used in the simulations, and the implementation of the thermodynamically consistent nucleation
model within the phase field framework

2.1 PF MODEL IN MEUMAPPS-SL

In general, the PF method involves simulating the movement of diffuse phase interfaces driven by
relevant chemical driving forces and interfacial energies. The decrease in the total energy of the system
due to interface motion is simulated by solving a set of partial differential equations that govern the
evolution of (1) a non-conserved order parameter representing the relative amounts of the evolving phases
(Allen-Cahn or time-dependent Ginzburg-Landau equation) and (2) conserved variables such as species
concentrations (Cahn-Hilliard equation).

The PF approach used in this research is based on the formulation proposed by Kim [16], which extends
an earlier model developed by Kim et al. for binary alloys [17] to multicomponent alloys. In these
models, the diffuse interface between two phases is assumed to consist of mixtures of the two phases with
different compositions but identical chemical potentials. In the present work, enforcement of equal
chemical potential is obtained using CALPHAD based expressions for the free energies of the solid and
liquid phases rather than ideal or regular or dilute solution approximations as used in the original model
development. PF models described in [16] and [17] were selected based on prior experience applying this
framework to solid-state transformations in Ti–6Al–4V alloy with thermodynamic input from CALPHAD
based free-energy models [18]. An anti-trapping flux term is included in the concentration evolution
equation to eliminate solute-trapping arising from non-equilibrium effects at the s-l interface at high
solidification velocities [16]. An additional feature of the PF simulations is the inclusion of a Langevin
noise superimposed on the order parameter, to facilitate destabilization of the s-l interface and promote

the formation of primary and secondary arms, depending on the local thermal conditions and
thermodynamic considerations.

2.1.1 Nucleation model

 An important feature of the PF model is a physically based nucleation model derived from classical
nucleation theory and coupled with alloy thermodynamics. There have been attempts to replace the
simple nucleation models used for nucleation of CET with models that are more thermodynamically
consistent. A thermodynamic framework for calculating the composition, 𝐶𝑠, of a solid nucleus form
solute enriched liquid of composition 𝐶𝑙 was proposed by Thompson and Spaepen [14], based on the
suggestion that 𝐶𝑠 maximizes the resulting thermodynamic driving force per unit volume, ∆𝐺𝑣. Their
calculations show that, under this assumption, the change in the chemical potentials of the solutes due to
the phase change is the same, which is the same consistent with Eqn. 15. Incorporating this value of ∆𝐺𝑣,
Li et al. [15] used the following equation based on the classical nucleation theory (CNT) to describe the
heterogeneous nucleation rate in the zone where constitutional supercooling (CS) exists:

The nucleation model used in the current PF simulations differs from the PF based nucleation approach
by Gr𝑎n𝑎sy et al [21]. The main argument against the CNT based, sharp interface model of nucleation is
that it fails under conditions of homogeneous nucleation where the nucleus size is typically smaller than
the width of the diffuse interface. In the case of heterogeneous nucleation, the complex ordering processes
at the interface between the nucleant and the liquid cannot be easily captured by the sharp interface
models. Nevertheless, CNT based nucleation model is used in the current simulations because of its
simplicity. The present simulations do not explicitly consider heterogeneous particles explicitly but
instead assume the presence of nuclei with a known potencies based on an assumed wetting angles
between the particles and the liquid.

2.1.2 Thermodynamic Integration

Two separate approaches were used to integrate the alloy thermodynamics with the PF simulations. The
first approach was the same as the previously implemented method in MEUMAPPS-SL in which the
Gibbs energies were approximated as quadratic functions of the solute concentrations. The approach was
previously used to simulate cellular and dendritic columnar solidification microstructures in a Ni-Fe-Nb
alloy that served as a surrogate for IN718 [10]. This assumption of quadratic form for the Gibbs energies
results in linear expressions for the derivatives with respect to solute concentrations (Eqn. 15) that allows
interface concentrations to be calculated by solving a system of linear equations, thereby eliminating the
need for time-consuming Newton-Raphson iterative approach. In the present research, initial simulations
of CET in IN718 were performed with this thermodynamic model, with the incorporation of the
nucleation model described in section 2.1.1. However, to improve the accuracy of the CET predictions, a
more accurate thermodynamic model incorporating several additional substitutional solutes present in
IN718, such as Mo, Ti, Cr, Al, was developed using a NN approach [22]. A brief description of the NN
Gibbs energy model follows.

2.1.2.2 Implementation of the NN model in MEUMAPPS-SL

The NN model receives each training data point in form a column array, x, where the number of columns
depend upon the number of composition variables that are active in the phase field model, as well as the
solidification temperature. As mentioned previously, several different attempts were made to integrate the
NN mode with MEUMAPPS. Initially, all six substitutional elements besides Ni (Fe, Cr, Al, Nb, Mo and
Ti) were considered. While the NN model using eight hidden layers provided an accurate fit to the Gibbs
energies obtained from ThermoCalc, the numerical implementation of the six-species NN model with the
NR iterative scheme for obtaining the diffuse interface compositions used in the KKS PF model proved
impractical. Execution halted at different time steps due to failed convergence in the NR iterations.

Subsequently, the number of independent solutes was reduced to three, while the composition of the other
four were held constant. This approximation is reasonable because Ni, Fe, Cr and Al are weakly
partitioning elements, and therefore their concentrations in the liquid and solid do not vary appreciably
during solidification. For the three-solute model, each input data x is represented as x(4,1), where x(1,1),
x(2,1), and x(3,1) are the Nb, Mo and Ti concentrations (in mole fractions) and x(4,1) is the temperature.

3.3 MEUMAPPS PF SIMULATIONS

The application of MEUMAPPS-SL to simulate CET in IN718 was carried out in two stages. In the first
stage, the focus was on utilizing a Ni-Fe-Nb ternary alloy that served a surrogate for IN718. The
composition of the alloy was tailored such that its solidification temperature range matched that of IN718.
The thermodynamic model for the ternary alloy was based on known CALPHAD representations of the
liquid and solid phases in this system. The integration of the CALPHAD model for the ternary into
MEUMAPPS had been developed in a previous effort [10].

The nucleation model, in combination with the CALPHAD ternary model, was then used to simulate CET
in the ternary alloy. The goal was to calibrate the nucleation parameters based on the CET simulation data
provided by Raghavan et al [23], shown Fig. 9. The thermal conditions, G and R, were obtained by
modeling heat transfer during solidification of spot welds and extracting the G and R along the weld
center line during solidification. In the early stages of solidification, the thermal conditions are
characterized by high G and low R, whereas at the end of the solidification, the conditions shift to low G
and high R. The resulting G–R path was superimposed on the process diagram for IN718. These data
provide the basis for calibrating and validating the MEUMAPPS CET simulations.

3.5 IN718 WITH NN THERMODYNAMIC MODEL

The NN fit for the Gibbs energies of liquid and solid shown in Eqn. 19 was coupled with MEUMAPPS to
calculate the diffuse interface concentrations of Nb, Mo and Ti based on the KKS phase field model.
Even with just three active solutes the convergence of the NR step was extremely slow. To simplify the
simulations even further, the convergence of each solute was obtained sequentially for each solute. The
converged value for the first solute was held constant while performing the convergence steps for the
second solute, and the converged values of the first two solutes were held constant while performing the
convergence for the third solute. Even with the above simplification, the NR iterations sometimes failed
to converge when the converged values from the previous step were used as the initial approximations for
the subsequent step. Therefore, for each step, the initial approximations from the first step were reused
achieve convergence in the subsequent steps.

3.5.2 Calibration of the NN model

The NN model was used to investigate the microstructure development during solidification in the
presence of G and R. The initial task was to calibrate the nucleation model, essentially the 𝑓(𝜃) parameter
in Eqn. 16. Calibration was performed by imposing the G and R required for the onset of CET as shown
in Figure 9. Accordingly, G = 1 × 105 k/m and R = 0.055 m/s were imposed. The simulations were
performed using a domain size of 1000 × 2 × 2000 mesh points, with G assumed to be the same in the
solid and liquid phases.

Initially, the s-l interface was located at 1000 mesh spacings in the growth direction. The solid and liquid
concentrations were set equal to the nominal alloy composition. Since significant s-l interface
undercooling is required to achieve the imposed R, the s-l interface temperature was initially set at 1555
K, corresponding to an undercooling of roughly 50K. The s-l interface initially moves downwards,
causing some melting of the solid below, before solidifying in the upward direction. The imposed R is
obtained after a considerable number of simulation steps, as shown in Figure 13. Once the desired R is
reached, the nucleation in the liquid is activated.

3.5.3 MEUMAPPS simulations of solidification structure during DED

Subsequent simulations were performed by using G and R obtained from thermal modeling of the DED
process as input to the PF simulations. The G and R values shown in Table 1 were used. Due to the small
time steps required in the PF simulations, it becomes computationally infeasible to use the time varying G
and R explicitly. Since the likelihood of CET occurring increases with decreasing G and increasing R, the
simulations focused on the top of the melt pool, which has the lowest G of 1054732 K/m and the highest
R of 0.008039 m/s. However, based on the solidification map shown in figure 9, it is evident that CET
onset is unlikely for this G and R combination. PF simulations were performed to rule out CET.

To obtain a steady-state condition with the above G and R values, an initial run was performed through
the transient solidification regime, with bulk nucleation turned off, as in the previous simulations. To
accelerate the transient solidification range, the initial undercooling of the s-l interface was increased
compared to the run shown in Figure 13. The s-l liquid interface was kept at the solidus temperature of
1531K for the IN718 alloy composition. The simulations used a domain size of 1000 × 2 × 2000 mesh
points and 256 nodes in the ORNL Cades cluster. Convergence of G and R to the steady state values was
extremely slow, particularly at later times as the solidification rate approached the imposed R of 0.008
m/s.

 4. DISCUSSION

PF simulations using MEUMAPPS-SL were performed to investigate the thermal conditions, G and R,
under which bulk nucleation in the liquid and the onset of CET occur in IN718. The simulations were
initially performed using a Ni-Fe-Nb ternary alloy, previously used for investigating cellular and cellular-
to-dendritic transitions under AM thermal conditions, with the addition of a heterogeneous nucleation
model. These simulations indicated that the CET model could be used to calibrate 𝑓(𝜃) for the onset of
CET in Ni-Fe-Nb surrogate ternary, using the solidification process map for IN718. Increasing G/R ratio
above the critical limit led to a cellular grain structure without CET. However, to capture the effect of
multiple solutes present in IN718 more realistically, a more accurate thermodynamic model was
developed based on the state-of-the-art thermodynamic assessments from commercial databases such as
ThermoCalc.

The NN based fit to the Gibbs energies of solid and liquid phases, incorporating all the six substitutional
elements present in IN718, provided a differentiable analytical function that was used for calculating the
diffuse interface concentrations required by the KKS [16, 17] phase field model. Calculation of these
diffuse interface concentrations requires the first and second derivatives of the Gibbs energy fit (Eqn. 19).
As described previously, only three active solutes could be considered in the NR scheme due to
convergence issues. A “pseudo” NR approach was adopted to simplify the calculations further. The
underlying cause for the unexpected reverse segregation of Ti in the simulations could not be identified.
However, a more rigorous NR implementation and expanded NN training data for Ti may improve the
predicted partitioning to better match experimental observations. Alternate numerical schemes to
accelerate convergence in calculating the diffuse interface concentrations should also be considered.

The NN based Gibbs energy model was effectively used to fit 𝑓(𝜃) for the onset of bulk nucleation and
CET in IN718. Similar Ni-Fe-Nb ternary, variation of G and R beyond the CET regime suppressed bulk
nucleation. A key finding from the PF simulations is that when the G and R are slightly beyond the CET
regime, a columnar structure develops, with the columnar grains nucleated in the bulk liquid ahead of the
advancing epitaxial columnar front from below. Such columnar grains would be undistinguishable from
the advancing epitaxial front unless detailed orientation imaging is performed.

In Eqn. 16, the heterogeneity is described by the function 𝑓(𝜃) = (2 ― 3 cos 𝜃 + 𝑐𝑜𝑠3 𝜃)/4. Here, 𝑓(𝜃)
= 0 corresponds to complete wetting (𝜃=0°) with the nucleant surface, while 𝑓(𝜃)=1 corresponds to
homogeneous nucleation (𝜃=180°) indicating total lack of wetting. The two conditions are shown
schematically in figure 19.

surface.

contact angle 𝜃 shown in Fig. 19 by considering the function 𝑓(𝜃), which is plotted as a function of cos(𝜃

From figure 20, that the critical 𝑓(𝜃) of 0.016 for the onset of CET in IN718 based on the PF simulations
corresponds to cos 𝜃 = 0.85, which gives a contact angle, 𝜃 = 31.78 degrees. For CET to occur at the top

the simulation results were compared with the grain microstructures obtained at the top of the melt pool

It appears from figure 21 that there are some equiaxed grains at the top of the melt pool; however, their
presence can only be confirmed through detailed orientation imaging to distinguish the equiaxed grains
from the columnar grains below. Based on the PF simulations, the formation of the equiaxed grains is
quite likely since only a small change in 𝜃 is required for CET. The observed microstructure was
compared with results from the literature. Sreekanth et al. [24] performed a comprehensive study on the

Figure 22. Orientation imaging of IN718 weld track cross section on IN718 substrate using a specific power input of 53.3 J/mm2

All PF simulations of CET were performed by first turning off bulk nucleation until a steady-state G and
R were established. While this steady state assumption is reasonable at the top of the weld pool where the
solidification process is fully developed, steady state may not occur at the bottom, where transient melting
of the substrate precedes solidification in the build direction. Extreme undercooling below the solidus has
been shown to produce non-equilibrium microstructures due to thermally controlled solidification [25], as
well as regimes where banded structures form [26] that are characterized by non-equilibrium partitioning
of solutes across the s-l interface. Although extreme undercooling can lead to the nucleation of new grains
in these regimes, because of the prevailing thermal conditions, these grains grow in a cellular mode.
Another source of bulk nucleation that leads to CET at the fusion line has been reported for IN718 [27].
In this case, nucleation is highly heterogeneous, driven by the formation of icosahedral short range order
(ISRO) in the liquid due to metastable states during rapid remelting of the base layer. The current PF
simulations do not apply to these extreme or transient regimes.
