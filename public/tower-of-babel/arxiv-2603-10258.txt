Two–Path Operators, Triadic Decompositions, and Safe Quotients for Ego–Centered Network Compression
Moses Boudourides School of Professional Studies, Northwestern University Moses.Boudourides@northwestern.edu

Abstract
Two–paths (wedges) are the elementary combinatorial objects behind clustering, triadic closure, redundancy, and brokerage. Motivated by a two–path formalism that links Burt’s structural holes to node–centered ego networks, we develop an operator viewpoint in which wedge incidence induces a canonical “two–walk” matrix and a unique decomposition into an edge–supported (triadic) part and a nonedge–supported (open) part. We then study quotient/contraction constructions designed to compress collections of dominating ego networks together with selected “traversing” nodes, and we prove a safe (inequality) transfer theorem for two–walk mass under contraction, with an explicit nonnegative error and an equality characterization in terms of a wedge–equitable partition. Finally, we illustrate the theory on ten benchmark graphs and their ego–traversing contractions using table–driven diagnostics and two distribution figures.



1
Why wedges as operators matter in network science

Triadic closure and its complement—open two–paths—are central to network science.
Watts–Strogatz clustering and Newman’s transitivity quantify closure at node and graph scales
[
16
,
12
,
13
]
,
while Burt’s theory of structural holes interprets openness as redundancy/brokerage in ego networks
[
5
,
2
,
14
]
.
All of these notions are naturally expressed in terms of
two–paths
(wedges), but typical summaries compress the wedge structure into a scalar.
Our goal is to keep wedge information
matrix–valued
and thus suitable for algebraic and spectral analysis, and to connect the resulting invariants
to compression procedures that aggregate ego–centered subgraphs.

The operator viewpoint has two practical payoffs.
First, it yields a canonical decomposition of the two–walk operator into a triadic part supported on edges and an open part supported on nonedges.
Second, it provides a principled way to diagnose how much two–walk structure is distorted by contracting families of ego networks into “supernodes”,
a common step in network visualization and multiscale modeling.
The main technical point is that naive quotient formulas can fail unless one states explicit regularity hypotheses: contracting vertices into blocks may artificially mix two–walk contributions coming from different vertices in the same block, thereby overcounting the amount of traversable two–step structure in the quotient network.
Consequently, a coarse-grained network can appear to preserve two–walk connectivity even when the original network does not.
We therefore emphasize
safe
statements (inequalities with explicit errors) and characterize precisely when equalities hold.

Preliminaries and notation

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite, simple, undirected graph with
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\left|E\right|=m
.
Write
i\sim j
when
\{i,j\}\in E
.
Let
\mathbf{A}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
be the adjacency matrix and
\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
the degree matrix.

Definition 2.1

(Two–paths and wedges)
.

A
two–path
is an ordered triple
(i,j,k)
of distinct vertices with
i\sim j
and
j\sim k
.
It is
closed
if
i\sim k
and
open
otherwise.

Let
\tau(i)
denote the number of triangles containing vertex
i
; the total number of triangles is

\tau=\frac{1}{6}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A}^{3})
and
\sum_{i\in V}\tau(i)=3\tau

[
13
]
.
Let
\pi_{2}(i)=\binom{d_{i}}{2}
be the number of unordered neighbor pairs of
i
.
The (Watts–Strogatz) local clustering coefficient is
C(i)=\tau(i)/\pi_{2}(i)
for
d_{i}\geq 2

[
16
,
15
]
.

Define the total number of two–paths in
G
(counting wedges by middle vertex) as

m_{2}:=\sum_{i\in V}\binom{d_{i}}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in V}d_{i}(d_{i}-1).

(1)

Newman’s transitivity (global clustering coefficient) can be written as
3\tau/m_{2}
when
m_{2}>0

[
12
]
.

3
Incidence and the two–walk operator

We recall the wedge incidence construction from
[
3
]
and develop an operator normalization.

3.1
Two–incidence and Gram identities

Let
B\in\{0,1\}^{n\times m}
be the unoriented incidence matrix of
G
; then
BB^{\top}=\mathbf{D}+\mathbf{A}
.
Similarly, index the set of (unordered) two–paths by
\{1,\dots,m_{2}\}
, and define the
two–incidence matrix

B_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times m_{2}}
by
(B_{2})_{i,p}=1
if vertex
i
is an endpoint of the two–path
p
, and
0
otherwise.

Proposition 3.1

(Wedge Gram identity)
.

There exists a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}_{2}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{2,1},\dots,d_{2,n})
such that

B_{2}B_{2}^{\top}=\mathbf{D}_{2}+\mathbf{A}^{2}.

(2)

Moreover,
d_{2,i}=\sum_{j}(\mathbf{A}^{2})_{ij}-d_{i}
is the number of two–paths incident to
i
(counting by endpoints), and

\sum_{i}d_{2,i}=2m_{2}
.

Proof.

Fix
i\neq j
. The
(i,j)
entry of
B_{2}B_{2}^{\top}
counts the number of two–paths for which both
i
and
j
are endpoints.
Such a two–path exists precisely when
i
and
j
have a common neighbor
k
, and each common neighbor contributes exactly one such two–path.
Hence
(B_{2}B_{2}^{\top})_{ij}=(\mathbf{A}^{2})_{ij}
for
i\neq j
.
The diagonal entry
(B_{2}B_{2}^{\top})_{ii}
counts two–paths incident to
i
as an endpoint; denote this by
d_{2,i}
and set
\mathbf{D}_{2}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{2,i})
.
Finally, summing
d_{2,i}
over
i
counts each two–path twice (once per endpoint), giving
\sum_{i}d_{2,i}=2m_{2}.

∎

3.2
The canonical two–walk operator

Define the
two–walk operator
(endpoint co–incidence) by

\mathbf{W}:=\mathbf{A}^{2}-\mathbf{D}.

(3)

For
i\neq j
,
\mathbf{W}_{ij}
equals the number of common neighbors of
i
and
j
.
The diagonal convention in (
3
) is chosen so that
\mathbf{W}\mathds{1}=(d_{2,1},\dots,d_{2,n})^{\top}
.
Proposition
3.1
implies
\mathbf{W}=B_{2}B_{2}^{\top}-\mathbf{D}_{2}-\mathbf{D}
.

4
A unique triadic/open decomposition of the wedge operator

We now express triadic closure and openness as a unique support–restricted decomposition of
\mathbf{W}
.

4.1
Edge triangle multiplicity and triadic intensity

For an edge
e=\{i,j\}\in E
, define its
triangle multiplicity

t_{e}
as the number of triangles containing
e
.
Equivalently,
t_{\{i,j\}}=(\mathbf{A}^{2})_{ij}
for
\{i,j\}\in E
.

Definition 4.1

(Triadic and open parts of
\mathbf{W}
)
.

Let
\mathbf{J}=\mathds{1}\mathds{1}^{\top}
.
Define matrices

\mathbf{T}:=\mathbf{A}\circ\mathbf{W},\qquad\mathbf{O}:=(\mathbf{J}-\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})\circ\mathbf{W},

(4)

where
\circ
denotes the Hadamard (entrywise) product.

Theorem 4.2

(Canonical wedge decomposition and uniqueness)
.

Let
G
be any simple undirected graph and define
\mathbf{W}
by (
3
).
Then:

1.

For all
i\neq j
,
\mathbf{W}_{ij}=\mathbf{T}_{ij}+\mathbf{O}_{ij}
and
\mathbf{T}_{ij}\mathbf{O}_{ij}=0
.

2.

\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{T})\subseteq E
(off-diagonal support on edges) and
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{O})\subseteq V\times V\setminus(\mathbf{I}\cup E)
(off-diagonal support on nonedges).

3.

(Uniqueness) If
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
satisfy
\mathbf{W}_{ij}=X_{ij}+Y_{ij}
for all
i\neq j
, with
X_{ij}=0
whenever
\{i,j\}\notin E
and

Y_{ij}=0
whenever
\{i,j\}\in E
, then
X=\mathbf{T}
and
Y=\mathbf{O}
off-diagonal.

Moreover, for
\{i,j\}\in E
we have
\mathbf{T}_{ij}=t_{\{i,j\}}
(triangle multiplicity), and for
\{i,j\}\notin E
,
\mathbf{O}_{ij}=\mathbf{W}_{ij}
counts open two–paths with endpoints
i,j
.

Proof.

For
i\neq j
, either
\{i,j\}\in E
or not.
If
\{i,j\}\in E
, then
(\mathbf{J}-\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})_{ij}=0
and
\mathbf{A}_{ij}=1
, hence
\mathbf{T}_{ij}=\mathbf{W}_{ij}
and
\mathbf{O}_{ij}=0
.
If
\{i,j\}\notin E
, then
\mathbf{A}_{ij}=0
and
(\mathbf{J}-\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})_{ij}=1
, hence
\mathbf{T}_{ij}=0
and
\mathbf{O}_{ij}=\mathbf{W}_{ij}
.
This proves (1)–(2) and the interpretation.
For uniqueness, fix
i\neq j
.
If
\{i,j\}\in E
, the support constraints force
Y_{ij}=0
, hence
X_{ij}=\mathbf{W}_{ij}=\mathbf{T}_{ij}
.
If
\{i,j\}\notin E
, the support constraints force
X_{ij}=0
, hence
Y_{ij}=\mathbf{W}_{ij}=\mathbf{O}_{ij}
.
∎

4.2
A sharp extremal characterization via open–wedge mass

Define the
open–wedge mass
(global openness) by

\omega(G):=m_{2}-3\tau.

(5)

Using
\sum_{e}t_{e}=3\tau
(each triangle has three edges), Theorem
4.2
implies that
\omega(G)
is exactly the total number of open wedges counted by middle vertex.

Definition 4.3

(
P_{3}
-free graph)
.

A graph is called
P_{3}
-free
if it contains no induced path on three vertices.
Equivalently, every connected component of a
P_{3}
-free graph is a clique.

Theorem 4.4

(Sharp openness inequality and equality graphs)
.

For every simple graph
G
,
\omega(G)\geq 0
.
Moreover,
\omega(G)=0
if and only if
G
is a disjoint union of cliques (equivalently,
G
is
P_{3}
–free).

Proof.

Each two–path is either open or closed.
Counting wedges by middle vertex gives
m_{2}
total wedges, and each triangle contributes exactly three closed wedges (one for each choice of middle vertex).
Hence
m_{2}=3\tau+(\text{\# open wedges})
, so
\omega(G)\geq 0
.

If
G
is a disjoint union of cliques, then every wedge is closed, so
\omega(G)=0
.
Conversely, if
\omega(G)=0
then there are no open wedges, i.e., no induced path on three vertices.
Thus
G
is
P_{3}
–free.
A standard characterization of
P_{3}
–free graphs is that each connected component is a clique (sometimes called a
cluster graph
); see, e.g.,
[
7
, Ch. 1]
.
∎

5
Open wedge matrix and redundancy as nonedge common neighbors

The open part
\mathbf{O}
from Definition
4.1
records
nonedge
pairs that share neighbors.
This gives a direct bridge to structural holes: nonadjacent alters in an ego neighborhood create open wedges, and
\mathbf{O}
aggregates these across the whole graph.

Theorem 5.1

(Open wedge mass as a nonedge sum)
.

Let
\mathbf{O}
be defined by (
4
). Then

\omega(G)=m_{2}-3\tau=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j\\
i\not\sim j\end{subarray}}\mathbf{O}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq j\\
i\not\sim j\end{subarray}}\mathbf{W}_{ij}.

(6)

Proof.

For a fixed unordered pair
\{i,j\}
with
i\not\sim j
, the entry
\mathbf{W}_{ij}=(\mathbf{A}^{2})_{ij}
counts the number of common neighbors
k
of
i
and
j
.
Each such
k
yields an open two–path
(i,k,j)
whose endpoints are precisely
i
and
j
.
Thus
\sum_{i<j,\,i\not\sim j}\mathbf{W}_{ij}
counts each open wedge exactly once.
By Theorem
4.2
,
\mathbf{O}_{ij}=\mathbf{W}_{ij}
on nonedges, giving the first equality.
The second equality is just symmetrization over
i\neq j
.
Finally,
\omega(G)
equals the total number of open wedges by Theorem
4.4
, so the identity holds.
∎

Remark 5.2

(Ego redundancy as a row–sum)
.

For a vertex
v
, the number of open wedges
through

v
is
\binom{d_{v}}{2}-\tau(v)
.
Theorem
5.1
is the global version of the same idea:
\mathbf{O}
aggregates open wedges by
endpoints
rather than by middle vertex.

6
Triangle incidence, edge multiplicities, and operator factorizations

The decomposition of Theorem
4.2
isolates the
support
of closure versus openness.
To study closure more finely, we pass from vertex wedges to
edge triangle multiplicities
and an incidence factorization.

6.1
Edge–triangle multiplicity matrix

Define the
edge triangle multiplicity matrix

M_{\triangle}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
by

(M_{\triangle})_{ij}:=\begin{cases}(\mathbf{A}^{2})_{ij},&\text{if }i\sim j,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Thus
M_{\triangle}=\mathbf{T}
off-diagonal, and its nonzero entries are exactly the triangle multiplicities
t_{\{i,j\}}
.

Remark 6.1
.

The matrix
M_{\triangle}
coincides with the triadic matrix
\mathbf{T}
defined in
Definition
4.1
. Indeed, for
\{i,j\}\in E
we have

(M_{\triangle})_{ij}=(\mathbf{A}^{2})_{ij}=t_{\{i,j\}}=T_{ij}
, while for

\{i,j\}\notin E
both matrices vanish, and both have zero diagonal.
Thus
M_{\triangle}=T
. We retain the notation
M_{\triangle}
to emphasize its
interpretation as the edge triangle multiplicity matrix, isolating the
triangle-supported part of the two-walk operator.

A basic identity connects
M_{\triangle}
to vertex triangle counts.

Proposition 6.2

(Edge multiplicities sum to triangles)
.

For every vertex
i\in V
, the following identity holds:

\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}t_{\{i,j\}}=3\tau,\qquad\sum_{j\sim i}t_{\{i,j\}}=2\tau(i).

(7)

Proof.

Each triangle has exactly three edges, so it contributes
1
to the multiplicity of each of its three edges, giving the first identity.
Fixing a vertex
i
, each triangle containing
i
has exactly two edges incident to
i
, hence contributes
1
to
t_{\{i,j\}}
for each of those two edges.
Summing over neighbors
j\sim i
counts each triangle containing
i
twice, giving the second identity.
∎

6.2
Triangle incidence and a Gram factorization

Let
\mathcal{T}
denote the set of triangles in
G
(unordered
3
–cycles),
\left|\mathcal{T}\right|=\tau
.
Define the
triangle incidence matrix

B_{\triangle}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times\tau}
by

(B_{\triangle})_{i,t}=1
if vertex
i
lies in triangle
t
and
0
otherwise.

Theorem 6.3

(Triangle Gram factorization)
.

The matrix
B_{\triangle}B_{\triangle}^{\top}
satisfies

(B_{\triangle}B_{\triangle}^{\top})_{ii}=\tau(i),\qquad(B_{\triangle}B_{\triangle}^{\top})_{ij}=\begin{cases}t_{\{i,j\}},&i\sim j,\\
0,&i\not\sim j.\end{cases}

Equivalently,

B_{\triangle}B_{\triangle}^{\top}=\operatorname{diag}(\tau(1),\dots,\tau(n))+M_{\triangle}.

(8)

Proof.

The diagonal entry counts the number of triangles containing vertex
i
, which is
\tau(i)
by definition.
For
i\neq j
, the
(i,j)
entry counts triangles containing both vertices
i
and
j
.
Such a triangle exists if and only if
i\sim j
and the edge
\{i,j\}
belongs to that triangle; the count is precisely
t_{\{i,j\}}
when
i\sim j
, and
0
otherwise.
∎

Remark 6.4

(Why this is more than clustering)
.

B_{\triangle}
and (
8
) retain the full distribution of triangle overlap patterns: two vertices can be strongly coupled by many shared triangles even if their local clustering coefficients are modest.
This allows one to study closure using linear algebra (spectra, norms, ranks) rather than scalars alone.

7
Spectral bounds and extremal structure

We record two bounds that connect triadicity to classical spectral graph theory.
They are not “new” in isolation, but they are essential for a proof–oriented paper because they provide rigorous control of the operators we introduce.
Throughout, let
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
.

Proposition 7.1

(A universal upper bound on triangles)
.

For every graph
G
,

\tau=\frac{1}{6}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A}^{3})=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{3}\leq\frac{\lambda_{1}}{6}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{3}m.

(9)

Equality holds if and only if all nonprincipal eigenvalues satisfy
\lambda_{i}^{3}=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{i}^{2}
, i.e.,
\lambda_{i}\in\{0,\lambda_{1}\}
for
i\geq 2
.

Proof.

The trace identities are standard
[
4
,
13
]
.
Since
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{i}
for all
i
, we have
\lambda_{i}^{3}\leq\lambda_{1}\lambda_{i}^{2}
for each
i
.
Summing yields
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{3}\leq\lambda_{1}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}
.
Finally,
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A}^{2})=2m
.
The equality characterization follows from entrywise equality in
\lambda_{i}^{3}\leq\lambda_{1}\lambda_{i}^{2}
.
∎

Proposition 7.2

(An operator–norm bound for closure mass)
.

Let
\|\cdot\|_{F}
and
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denote the Frobenius and spectral norms.
Then

\|\mathbf{T}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}t_{\{i,j\}}^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{A}^{2}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{4}\leq n\lambda_{1}^{4}.

Here
\|\mathbf{A}^{2}\|_{F}^{2}=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A}^{4})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{4}
since
\mathbf{A}^{2}
is symmetric.
In particular, closure concentrated on a small set of edges forces large
\|\mathbf{T}\|_{F}
and therefore a large spectral radius.

Proof.

The first identity is the definition of
\mathbf{T}
as edge–supported entries equal to
t_{\{i,j\}}
.
Entrywise,
\mathbf{T}
is obtained from
\mathbf{A}^{2}
by zeroing nonedges, hence
\|\mathbf{T}\|_{F}\leq\|\mathbf{A}^{2}\|_{F}
.
Finally,
\|\mathbf{A}^{2}\|_{F}^{2}=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A}^{4})=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{4}\leq n\lambda_{1}^{4}
.
∎

Remark 7.3

(Extremal intuition)
.

Theorem
4.4
shows that
all
wedges are closed exactly for cluster graphs.
Proposition
7.1
complements this by controlling the total number of triangles by
(m,\lambda_{1})
, which is useful when comparing graphs of different sizes.

8
Clustered and traversing vertices, corrected

The note
[
3
]
introduces
clustered
vertices (those in at least one triangle) and
traversing
vertices (triangle–free),
and discusses bridges.
The bridge statement must be handled carefully: triangle–free does
not
imply all incident edges are bridges.
The correct local characterization is purely neighborhood–based.

Definition 8.1

(Clustered and traversing vertices)
.

A vertex
v
is
clustered
if
\tau(v)>0
, and
traversing
if
\tau(v)=0
.
Write
V_{\mathrm{cl}}
and
V_{\mathrm{tr}}
for the corresponding sets.

Proposition 8.2

(Equivalent traversing characterizations)
.

For a vertex
v
, the following are equivalent:

1.

v
is traversing, i.e.,
\tau(v)=0
.

2.

Let
N(v)=\{u\in V:u\sim v\}
denote the (open) neighborhood of
v
.
The induced subgraph
G[N(v)]
has no edges.

3.

For every pair of distinct neighbors
x,y\in N(v)
, we have
x\not\sim y
.

Proof.

v
lies in a triangle if and only if two of its neighbors are adjacent.
Thus
\tau(v)=0
if and only if no edge exists among vertices in
N(v)
, proving the equivalence.
∎

9
Combinatorial bounds for clustered structure and ego domination

We collect two elementary but useful bounds that turn qualitative statements about “clustered” vertices into quantitative lemmas
that can be used later in proofs.

Proposition 9.1

(Clustered vertices versus triangle count)
.

If
\tau>0
then
3\leq\left|V_{\mathrm{cl}}\right|\leq 3\tau
.

Proof.

If
\tau>0
, at least one triangle exists and contributes three clustered vertices, so
\left|V_{\mathrm{cl}}\right|\geq 3
.
Conversely, every clustered vertex lies in at least one triangle.
Counting vertex–triangle incidences gives
\sum_{v\in V_{\mathrm{cl}}}\tau(v)=3\tau
and each
\tau(v)\geq 1
, hence
\left|V_{\mathrm{cl}}\right|\leq 3\tau
.
∎

Definition 9.2

(Traversing vertex types)
.

Let
G
be decomposed into clustered vertices
V_{\mathrm{cl}}
and traversing vertices
V_{\mathrm{tr}}
.
Among the traversing vertices we distinguish two types:

•

T_{3}
: traversing vertices adjacent to exactly one clustered block but not internal to it;

•

T_{4}
: traversing vertices adjacent to two or more clustered blocks.

Thus
V_{\mathrm{tr}}=T_{3}\cup T_{4}
.

Proposition 9.3

(Dominating set size and quotient size)
.

Let
S
be a dominating set of the clustered core
H=G[V_{\mathrm{cl}}]
and construct the ego–traversing partition
\Pi
by aggregating
dominating ego blocks and retaining
T_{3}\cup T_{4}
traversing vertices as singleton blocks.
Then the number of blocks is

r=\left|S\right|+\left|T_{3}\right|+\left|T_{4}\right|.

Proof.

By construction there is exactly one block per dominating ego center
s\in S
.
All other vertices are assigned to one of these ego blocks except those traversing vertices retained as singletons, which are precisely
T_{3}\cup T_{4}
.
The blocks are disjoint and cover
V
, so the count follows.
∎

Remark 9.4
.

Proposition
9.1
and the identity
\omega=m_{2}-3\tau
imply that
\left|V_{\mathrm{cl}}\right|
is simultaneously controlled by closure (
\tau
)
and openness (
m_{2}
).
In particular, for sparse graphs with fixed
m
and small
\lambda_{1}
(Proposition
7.1
),
\tau
and thus
\left|V_{\mathrm{cl}}\right|
cannot be large.

10
Dominating ego networks and a safe contraction theorem

We now formalize the “dominating ego networks + traversing nodes” construction from
[
3
]

and prove a safe two–walk transfer theorem under contraction.

10.1
Dominating sets on the clustered core

Assume
V_{\mathrm{cl}}\neq\emptyset
and let
H:=G[V_{\mathrm{cl}}]
be the induced subgraph on clustered vertices.
A set
S\subseteq V_{\mathrm{cl}}
is a
dominating set
of
H
if every vertex in
V_{\mathrm{cl}}
is in
S
or adjacent in
H
to some
s\in S

[
10
]
.
Vertices in
S
are called
dominating clustered vertices
and those in
V_{\mathrm{cl}}\setminus S

dominated clustered vertices
.

Following
[
3
]
, we also partition traversing vertices into four types according to their adjacency to dominating/dominated clustered vertices.
We use this only to define which traversing vertices are retained as singletons in the contraction.

10.2
Partitions and the directed edge–sum quotient

Let
\Pi=\{P_{1},\dots,P_{r}\}
be a partition of
V
into disjoint nonempty blocks.
Write
b(v)\in\{1,\dots,r\}
for the block index of
v
.
Define the
directed edge–sum quotient matrix

B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
by

B_{ab}:=\#\{(u,v)\in V\times V:u\sim v,\ b(u)=a,\ b(v)=b\}.

(10)

Thus, for
a\neq b
,
B_{ab}
equals the number of edges between
P_{a}
and
P_{b}
(counted once per direction), and
B_{aa}=2\left|E(P_{a})\right|
counts internal directed edges.
Since the underlying graph is undirected, the matrix
B
is symmetric off–diagonal, i.e.,
B_{ab}=B_{ba}
for
a\neq b
.

Define also the
aggregated two–walk matrix

M\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
by

M_{ab}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in P_{a}\\
w\in P_{b}\end{subarray}}(\mathbf{A}^{2})_{uw}.

(11)

10.3
Wedge–equitable partitions and comparison to equitable partitions

The equality regime in Theorem
10.4
defines a regularity notion that is strictly stronger than the classical equitability
condition for adjacency quotients
[
8
,
4
]
.

Definition 10.1

(Equitable and wedge–equitable partitions)
.

A partition
\Pi=\{P_{1},\dots,P_{r}\}
is
equitable
(for
\mathbf{A}
) if for every pair
(a,c)
, every vertex
x\in P_{c}
has the same number of neighbors in
P_{a}
;
i.e.,
d_{a}(x)
is constant over
x\in P_{c}
.
We call
\Pi

wedge–equitable
if for every triple of blocks
(a,b,c)
there exists a vertex
x^{\star}\in P_{c}
such that

d_{a}(x)d_{b}(y)=0
for all
x,y\in P_{c}
with
(x,y)\neq(x^{\star},x^{\star})
.

Proposition 10.2
.

Every wedge–equitable partition is equitable, but not conversely.

Proof.

If
\Pi
is wedge–equitable, fix blocks
(a,c)
and take
b=a
.
Then for each
P_{c}
there is a vertex
x^{\star}
such that
d_{a}(x)d_{a}(y)=0
for all distinct
x,y\in P_{c}
.
Thus at most one vertex in
P_{c}
can have
d_{a}(\cdot)>0
, and for all other vertices
d_{a}(\cdot)=0
.
In particular,
d_{a}(x)
is constant on
P_{c}
if
P_{c}
has size
1
, and otherwise it is constant
except possibly at one vertex
.
However, wedge–equitable also requires this to hold for all
b
simultaneously, forcing the exceptional vertex to be the same across
a
,
and hence forcing constant neighbor counts across
P_{c}
for each
a
.
This is exactly equitability.

To see nonconverse, consider a regular partition in which each block has two vertices each connected in the same way to another block.
Such a partition can be equitable while having two distinct vertices in a block that both connect to the same external block, violating the “single middle vertex” requirement in (
13
).
An explicit example is given in Appendix
A
.
∎

Remark 10.3

(Interpretation)
.

Equitable partitions preserve adjacency quotients exactly.
Wedge–equitable partitions preserve
two–walk
structure (off diagonal) exactly.
Theorem
10.4
therefore identifies the correct regularity notion for contractions meant to preserve wedge/triadic information.

Theorem 10.4

(Safe two–walk transfer under contraction)
.

For any partition
\Pi
of
V
, the matrices
B
and
M
defined by (
10
)–(
11
) satisfy the entrywise inequality

M\leq B^{2}.

(12)

Moreover, for
a\neq b
the overcount has the explicit form

(B^{2}-M)_{ab}=\sum_{c=1}^{r}\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in P_{c}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}d_{a}(x)\,d_{b}(y)\ \geq 0,

(13)

where
d_{a}(x)
denotes the number of neighbors of
x
that lie in block
P_{a}
.
Equality
M=B^{2}
holds off-diagonal if and only if for every block
P_{c}
and every pair of blocks
(a,b)
,
the sequences
\{d_{a}(x)\}_{x\in P_{c}}
and
\{d_{b}(x)\}_{x\in P_{c}}
are supported on a common single vertex (equivalently, each block is
wedge–equitable
).

Proof.

Fix
a,b
.
By definition of
\mathbf{A}^{2}
,

M_{ab}=\sum_{u\in P_{a}}\sum_{w\in P_{b}}\sum_{v\in V}\mathbf{A}_{uv}\mathbf{A}_{vw}=\sum_{c=1}^{r}\ \sum_{v\in P_{c}}\Big(\sum_{u\in P_{a}}\mathbf{A}_{uv}\Big)\Big(\sum_{w\in P_{b}}\mathbf{A}_{vw}\Big).

For
v\in P_{c}
, define
d_{a}(v):=\sum_{u\in P_{a}}\mathbf{A}_{uv}
and
d_{b}(v):=\sum_{w\in P_{b}}\mathbf{A}_{vw}
.
Then

M_{ab}=\sum_{c=1}^{r}\sum_{v\in P_{c}}d_{a}(v)d_{b}(v).

By definition of the directed edge–sum quotient matrix,

B_{ac}=\sum_{v\in P_{c}}d_{a}(v),

since
d_{a}(v)
counts the number of neighbors of
v
in block
P_{a}
and the quotient entry aggregates these contributions over all vertices of
P_{c}
.

On the other hand,

(B^{2})_{ab}=\sum_{c=1}^{r}B_{ac}B_{cb}=\sum_{c=1}^{r}\Big(\sum_{v\in P_{c}}d_{a}(v)\Big)\Big(\sum_{v\in P_{c}}d_{b}(v)\Big)=\sum_{c=1}^{r}\sum_{x,y\in P_{c}}d_{a}(x)d_{b}(y).

Subtracting yields (
13
), hence
M\leq B^{2}
entrywise.
The equality characterization follows by examining the condition

\sum_{x,y\in P_{c}}d_{a}(x)d_{b}(y)=\sum_{v\in P_{c}}d_{a}(v)d_{b}(v).

(
\Rightarrow
) If
M=B^{2}
off–diagonal, then for every block
P_{c}
and every pair
(a,b)
all cross terms with
x\neq y
must vanish. Hence whenever
d_{a}(x)>0
and
d_{b}(y)>0
with
x,y\in P_{c}
, we must have
x=y
. Thus the sequences
\{d_{a}(x)\}_{x\in P_{c}}
and
\{d_{b}(x)\}_{x\in P_{c}}
are supported on a common single vertex.

(
\Leftarrow
) Conversely, if for every block
P_{c}
and every pair
(a,b)
the sequences
\{d_{a}(x)\}_{x\in P_{c}}
and
\{d_{b}(x)\}_{x\in P_{c}}
are supported on a common single vertex, then all cross terms
d_{a}(x)d_{b}(y)
with
x\neq y
vanish, and therefore

\sum_{x,y\in P_{c}}d_{a}(x)d_{b}(y)=\sum_{v\in P_{c}}d_{a}(v)d_{b}(v).

Summing over
c
gives
M_{ab}=(B^{2})_{ab}
for
a\neq b
, as claimed.
∎

Remark 10.5

(Why this avoids quotient traps)

Theorem
10.4
is deliberately an
inequality
with an explicit error and a sharp equality condition.
This is the safe form of a “two–walk transfer” statement: without wedge–equitable hypotheses, an equality claim is generally false.

Directed and weighted extensions (brief)

Although we work with simple undirected graphs, the wedge–operator approach extends cleanly.

12.1
Directed graphs

For a directed graph with adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
, a natural two–step operator from sources to targets is
\mathbf{A}^{2}
as before.
However, wedge types can be distinguished: “out–out” wedges correspond to
\mathbf{A}^{2}
, while “in–in” wedges correspond to
(\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{2}
,
and mixed wedges correspond to
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
.
Each admits a canonical support decomposition analogous to Theorem
4.2
by Hadamard masking with the directed edge set and its complement.
This yields a family of triadic/open operators tailored to directed transitivity notions in social networks.

12.2
Weighted graphs

For a weighted undirected graph with symmetric weight matrix
W=(w_{ij})
, define
\mathbf{A}_{W}
with
(\mathbf{A}_{W})_{ij}=w_{ij}
for
i\neq j
and
0
on the diagonal.
Then
(\mathbf{A}_{W}^{2})_{ij}=\sum_{k}w_{ik}w_{kj}
is a weighted common–neighbor intensity, and one can define

\mathbf{W}_{W}=\mathbf{A}_{W}^{2}-\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{A}_{W}\mathds{1})
and its support decomposition exactly as in (
4
).
The contraction theorem (Theorem
10.4
) also holds verbatim for nonnegative weights, replacing edge counts by weight sums.

Proposition 12.1

(Weighted transfer theorem)
.

Let
G
be a weighted undirected graph with symmetric weights
w_{uv}\geq 0
and weighted adjacency
\mathbf{A}_{W}
.
For any partition
\Pi
define the weighted directed edge–sum quotient
B^{(w)}
by summing weights in (
10
),
and define
M^{(w)}
by aggregating
(\mathbf{A}_{W}^{2})_{uw}
as in (
11
).
Then
M^{(w)}\leq(B^{(w)})^{2}
entrywise, with an error formula identical to (
13
) where
d_{a}(x)
becomes the total weight from
x
into block
P_{a}
.

Proof sketch.

Repeat the proof of Theorem
10.4
with
\mathbf{A}
replaced by
\mathbf{A}_{W}
.
Nonnegativity of weights ensures that the cross-term expansion remains
\geq 0
.
∎

(Hypergraph direction)

Replacing two–paths by length–2 walks in bipartite or hypergraph incidence graphs suggests higher–order analogues of

Discussion and directions

The operator decomposition
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{T}+\mathbf{O}
(Theorem
4.2
) provides a matrix–valued refinement of clustering that separates triadic closure from openness in a way that is both canonical and algebraically tractable.
The sharp characterization
\omega=0
(Theorem
4.4
) identifies exactly when wedge openness vanishes: cluster graphs (disjoint unions of cliques).
Finally, the safe transfer theorem (Theorem
10.4
) explains how two–walk structure behaves under contraction and makes explicit the wedge–equitable regime in which equality can be expected.

Several mathematically natural extensions are immediate.
First, one may replace
\mathbf{A}^{2}
by motif adjacency operators of higher order
[
11
,
1
]
and seek analogous canonical support decompositions.
Second, one can develop spectral bounds for
\tau
and
\omega
in terms of
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A})
and degree moments, sharpening classical inequalities
[
6
,
4
]
.
Third, the wedge–equitable condition suggests a new hierarchy of partitions between equitable partitions (for
\mathbf{A}
) and regular partitions for higher–order operators
[
8
]
.
We view these as promising directions for a theorem–driven network science agenda.

Appendix A
A counterexample to naive quotient equality

A common trap in quotient constructions is to implicitly assume that block–level two–walk counts equal the aggregation of vertex–level two–walk counts.
Theorem
10.4
shows that, in general,
B^{2}

overcounts

M
unless a wedge–equitable condition holds.

Consider the 4–cycle
C_{4}
on vertices
\{1,2,3,4\}
with edges
(1,2),(2,3),\allowbreak(3,4),(4,1)
.
Partition
V
into two blocks
P_{1}=\{1,3\}
and
P_{2}=\{2,4\}
.
Then
B_{12}=B_{21}=4
(each vertex in
P_{1}
connects to both vertices in
P_{2}
) and
B_{11}=B_{22}=0
.
Hence
(B^{2})_{12}=B_{11}B_{12}+B_{12}B_{22}=0
while
(B^{2})_{11}=16
and
(B^{2})_{22}=16
.

2
3
4

The cycle
C_{4}
with partition
P_{1}=\{1,3\}
(blue) and
P_{2}=\{2,4\}
(red).

On the other hand,
\mathbf{A}^{2}
for
C_{4}
has
(\mathbf{A}^{2})_{13}=2
and
(\mathbf{A}^{2})_{24}=2
(two length–2 walks between opposite vertices).
Thus
M_{11}=(\mathbf{A}^{2})_{11}+(\mathbf{A}^{2})_{13}+(\mathbf{A}^{2})_{31}+(\mathbf{A}^{2})_{33}=8
and similarly
M_{22}=8
.
This strict gap reflects the fact that within each block there are
two
distinct middle vertices contributing to two–walks, violating wedge–equity.

More generally, the same phenomenon occurs for complete bipartite graphs.
Let the complete bipartite graph
G=K_{m,n}
with bipartition
(U,V)
.
If vertices from each side are aggregated into blocks that mix vertices with different neighborhoods,
the quotient matrix again overcounts two–walk contributions.
In particular, distinct middle vertices in the original graph contribute separate two–walks between vertex pairs,
but these contributions become artificially combined in the quotient.
Thus the inequality
M\leq B^{2}
can be strict for the same structural reason as in the
C_{4}
example,
namely the presence of multiple distinct middle vertices within a block.

Appendix B
Algorithmic construction of the ego–traversing partition

For completeness, we specify the deterministic partition rule used in the computational section.

Input.

A graph
G=(V,E)
and a dominating set
S
of
H=G[V_{\mathrm{cl}}]
.

Step 1 (clustered/traversing split).

Compute
\tau(v)
(e.g., via triangle enumeration) and set
V_{\mathrm{cl}}=\{v:\tau(v)>0\}
,
V_{\mathrm{tr}}=V\setminus V_{\mathrm{cl}}
.

Step 2 (dominated clustered assignment).

For each
u\in V_{\mathrm{cl}}\setminus S
, choose a (deterministic) adjacent dominator
s\in S\cap N(u)
and assign
u
to the ego block of
s
.
Existence is guaranteed because
S
dominates
H
.

Step 3 (traversing types).

Classify
v\in V_{\mathrm{tr}}
by its adjacency to
S
and
V_{\mathrm{cl}}\setminus S
into
T_{1},\dots,T_{4}
as in
[
3
]
.

Step 4 (retain
T_{3}\cup T_{4}
singletons).

Create singleton blocks for each
v\in T_{3}\cup T_{4}
.

Step 5 (assign remaining traversing vertices).

For each
v\in T_{1}\cup T_{2}
, choose a dominator
s\in S\cap N(v)
and assign
v
to the ego block of
s
.

The resulting blocks are disjoint and cover
V
.
The quotient matrix
B
is then computed by directed edge counts (
10
).
The aggregated two–walk matrix
M
can be computed as in (
11
) either by summing entries of
\mathbf{A}^{2}
or (more efficiently) by counting middle vertices and their blockwise degrees.
