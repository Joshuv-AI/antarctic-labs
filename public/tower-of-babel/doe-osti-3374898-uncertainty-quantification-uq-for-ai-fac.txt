 UNCERTAINTY QUANTIFICATION (UQ) FOR AI
 FACT SHEET
 BY: THE STEEL THREAD UQ WORKING GROUP

This is a primer for UQ in the age of AI: explaining its challenges, establishing a path forward, and setting expectations.

 ELEMENTS OF UQ FOR AI

UQ is traditionally defined as quantitative characterization and estimation of uncertainties in both computational and
real-world applications. It tries to determine how likely certain outcomes are if some aspects of the system are not
exactly known. Some aspects of uncertainty that may be quantified include1:

 • Global uncertainty: Uncertainty ascribed to the performance metric of a model to do well on a particular task
 • Predictive uncertainty: Confidence in the accuracy of a particular output
 • Consistency: Uncertainty associated with the inherent stochasticity of a model
 • Coverage: The long-term frequency with which a constructed interval contains the true value of the quantity
 of interest

While these aspects of UQ remain relevant, modern generative AI requires more than traditional notions of statistical
guarantees. Here, we describe the added complexity and scale posed by AI systems, particularly LLMs, that require a
new perspective on increasing trust.

UQ and Trustworthy AI

UQ is one part of a larger, emerging framework for increasing the
trustworthiness and usability of AI systems. A trust umbrella for AI involves well-
characterized performance: defining what answer is “correct” in a possibly
subjective task while capturing uncertainty and calibration along the traditional
UQ definition. Further, calibrated trust requires quantifying and communicating
the AI system’s consistency (i.e. bias & robustness) and improving the
transparency of the system (and resulting explainability of the response). Each
component is interconnected and important to establishing trust. Frameworks
that adopt the more traditional view without modification may create an illusion
of confidence that is not attainable in the modern age of AI.

 CHALLENGES

Calibration

In many cases, UQ estimates can be misleading because the AI system can be confidently wrong. For many tasks, this is
because there is no clear definition of “truth” (or “wrong”). UQ is often focused on variance in the output and
deviation from correct, but, for example, a “correct” generated textual answer can be written in near-infinite ways.

Generated Text and Visual media

For outputs of supervised models, such as regression models, the concepts of “correctness” and distance allow one to
quantify accuracy (how often a model is correct, usually through the proportion of correct predictions) and precision
or uncertainty (consistency of the answers or how reliable the model’s predictions are). For generated text and visual
media, it is not so simple. There are scrappy methods to estimate uncertainty for generative models, but they are not
principled because they cannot account for distribution shift.UQ for foundation models remains fundamentally
challenging, particularly under distribution shift, which directly impacts a model’s ability to generalize beyond training
conditions. Even well-calibrated models can become overconfident or underconfident when exposed to out-of-
distribution data, degrading the reliability of their predictions. These difficulties are compounded in generative tasks,
e.g., document summarization, where the notion of correctness is ambiguous and evaluation metrics are often ad hoc.
For example, assessing the factual consistency of generated summaries remains an open problem, as existing metrics
may fail to detect factual inconsistencies, limiting their reliability for UQ estimates

Attributing Uncertainty

Relating the uncertainty in the model output to the root cause is known as
UQ attribution. Examples of root causes that can imbue different levels of
uncertainty in the model include the training data sample, the sensitivity
to the input prompt, or the inherent stochastic nature of the model.
Performing UQ attribution helps model developers more efficiently assign
resources to confine uncertainty to desirable levels. For model users, well
defined UQ attribution provides a guide for how to use the model to
obtain more reliable predictions. While UQ attribution is important, it
requires advanced statistical methods or a well-designed experiment,
which can be computationally or logistically prohibitive for advanced AI
and so merits future research.

Trust Calibration Maturity Model (TCMM)

The primary purpose of having well-quantified uncertainty is understanding how to integrate the output of a system
into a larger calculation or how to act on the output of that system while balancing the risk of error. Fulfilling this
purpose, i.e. having calibrated trust in an AI system, requires more than just UQ. When a colleague provides an
answer to a question, they provide more than “I am 93% +- 2% accurate at answering similar questions”. Often he or
she provides a transparent and truthful explanation of the process by which they came to the answer. A trustable
colleague will also disclose if this question is like one they have often gotten wrong in the past. In short, there are
several dimensions that lead to trust, and these can be quite complex to assess in AI systems.

Steel Thread developed the Trust Calibration Maturity Model2 (TCMM) to facilitate rapid and actionable assessments
of the trustworthiness of an AI system across 5 dimensions on a 1-4 scale. These dimensions were selected as a
simplification of a diverse literature and chosen to enable users, researchers, developers, and customers to all
understand and agree on task demands in relation to AI system capabilities and form the trust umbrella in Figure 1.

UQ for Multi-Modal Models

It is critical to develop ways to perform UQ across the emerging spectrum of multi-modal models. Vision LLMs, such as
the open-source LLaVA model, are capable of reasoning over images and responding to questions and requests. Other
models, including those developed within Steel Thread, operate on even more complex modalities, such as niche
image domains, seismic activity, and time-series data. This wider literature on UQ for generative models has focused
exclusively on text models.

Steel Thread has been evaluating UQ and calibration in multi-modal models, as well as designing new, modality-
general techniques for UQ to handle models trained on seismic or time-series data. Highlighted in this work is an effort
to adapt existing techniques that quantify uncertainty as the consistency of outputs across multiple, random
generation, which has shown popular vision LLMs to be poorly calibrated, particularly on complex, open-ended
response tasks. Steel Thread is also developing new variants of this “consistency” approach that can generalize to non-
textual modalities, such as image generation models.

UQ on Metric Values (Global Uncertainty)

Traditional leaderboards often rank foundation models by aggregating performance metrics on a variety of tasks,
typically with unweighted arithmetic averages and with no quantified uncertainties. This can lead to poor deductions
regarding AI model performance and comparisons that are not relevant to an end user and gives little information on
where the model performs well. Steel Thread has developed an approach to use statistics to rigorously assess
performances across different task weightings and with uncertainties on the performance metrics. While these ideas
have been used for other statistical applications, we are the first to probe specifically for AI model performance
comparisons, especially with foundation models.
