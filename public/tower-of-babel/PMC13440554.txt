Fulminant lupus myopericarditis with Class III lupus nephritis and catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome-like thrombosis: a case report
Authors: Alfredo Perez Tagle Tejeda, Issa Jundi, Dahlia Ann Blake, Charles Bonanno, Walif Aji, Doralisa Morrone, Youssef Mahmooud AbdElMoneim, Deepti Ranganathan

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multisystem autoimmune disease with cardiac involvement reported in up to 50% of patients during the disease course. Lupus myocarditis, however, is uncommon, occurring in only 1%–3% of cases, and may lead to severe ventricular dysfunction, arrhythmias, cardiogenic shock, and death.1–5

The simultaneous presentation of lupus myopericarditis and Class III proliferative nephritis is unusual and reflects severe multisystem immune dysregulation. Myeloperoxidase antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (MPO-ANCA) positivity may accompany lupus nephritis and has been associated with a more severe renal phenotype, while antiphospholipid-antibody-associated thrombosis and a catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS)-like thrombotic syndrome further complicate diagnostic classification and treatment.6–9

We describe a previously healthy 29-year-old man with lupus myopericarditis, Class III proliferative nephritis, MPO-ANCA positivity, and CAPS-like thrombosis, followed by partial recovery and an influenza A–associated relapse complicated by cardiogenic shock, multifocal cerebral infarctions, and severe infectious complications.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 29-year-old man presented with 3 weeks of progressive dyspnoea, orthopnoea, and lower-extremity oedema. He developed acute hypoxemic respiratory failure from hypertensive pulmonary oedema, requiring emergent intubation and intensive care unit admission. N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide was 206 000 pg/mL, troponin I 0.292 ng/mL, and creatinine 371 µmol/L (4.2 mg/dL) with anuria and metabolic acidosis. Echocardiography demonstrated left ventricular dilation, global hypokinesis, and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) 36% (Figure 1). Haemodialysis was initiated for refractory volume overload, allowing extubation by Day 3.

Transthoracic echocardiogram: Biplane Simpson quantification from apical four- and two-chamber views demonstrating left ventricular dilation and global hypokinesis: end-diastolic volume 242 mL, end-systolic volume 155 mL, left ventricular ejection fraction 36%.

Autoimmune testing showed antinuclear antibody positivity, high-titre anti-dsDNA antibodies, hypocomplementemia, and triple-positive antiphospholipid antibodies, including anti-β2-glycoprotein I immunoglobulin (Ig)G, anticardiolipin IgG, and lupus anticoagulant. High-dose intravenous methylprednisolone was initiated, followed by mycophenolate mofetil and hydroxychloroquine for suspected SLE with myocarditis and nephritis.

Kidney biopsy confirmed Class III proliferative lupus nephritis with a full-house immune-complex pattern and no evidence of pauci-immune necrotizing or crescentic glomerulonephritis, vasculitis, or thrombotic microangiopathy (Figure 2). Although serological testing was positive for MPO-ANCA, the biopsy findings did not support ANCA-associated vasculitis. Given the severity of the multi-organ disease, including dialysis-requiring lupus nephritis and lupus myopericarditis, and concern that MPO-ANCA positivity might identify a higher-risk renal phenotype, rituximab was selected by multidisciplinary consensus as individualized adjunctive B-cell-depleting therapy.

(A) Immunofluorescence for C1q showing granular mesangial and capillary-loop deposition, forming part of the full-house immune-complex pattern. (B) Periodic acid–Schiff stain, high-power view, showing segmental endocapillary hypercellularity and early double-contour formation, consistent with ISN/RPS class III lupus nephritis.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated myocardial oedema and subepicardial late gadolinium enhancement involving the apical inferior and lateral walls, with pericardial involvement, supporting lupus myopericarditis (Figure 3).

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging: (A) T2 mapping with diffuse myocardial oedema; (B) short-axis image demonstrating patchy subepicardial late gadolinium enhancement.

On Day 15, the patient developed new right upper-extremity weakness and diffuse back pain. Brain MRI revealed an acute left frontoparietal cortical infarction, and abdominal computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a splenic infarction. Rapid thrombosis involving two organs, severe thrombocytopenia, and triple-positive antiphospholipid antibodies raised concern for a CAPS-like thrombotic syndrome. Anticoagulation, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and therapeutic plasma exchange were initiated while corticosteroids continued.

The course was later complicated by pneumoperitoneum, requiring right hemicolectomy with end ileostomy. Pathology demonstrated cytomegalovirus peritonitis, and intravenous ganciclovir was initiated. LVEF subsequently improved to 38%, and he was discharged on guideline-directed medical therapy, tapering immunosuppression, and long-term anticoagulation.

Four weeks later, he was readmitted with fever, cough, altered mental status, agonal breathing, and haemodynamic collapse requiring intubation. Influenza A infection, methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) bacteraemia, and Citrobacter freundii pneumonia were diagnosed. Mixed septic and cardiogenic shock with LVEF 17% required norepinephrine, vasopressin, dobutamine, and continuous renal replacement therapy.

The platelet count fell below 14 × 109/L, and brain MRI demonstrated multifocal acute infarctions in both frontal lobes, the left occipital lobe, and both cerebellar hemispheres (Figure 4). Evaluation for alternative causes of thrombocytopenia and multi-territorial infarction, including disseminated intravascular coagulation, thrombotic microangiopathy, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, and septic embolization, was unrevealing. CAPS-directed therapy included anticoagulation as clinically feasible, intravenous immunoglobulin, and five plasma-exchange sessions. Plasma microbial cell-free DNA testing identified Aspergillus flavus, prompting isavuconazole.

Brain diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging: (A) multifocal bilateral frontoparietal acute infarctions; (B) bilateral cerebellar acute infarctions, demonstrating multi-territorial thrombotic involvement in the setting of catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome–like disease.

Tracheostomy was performed for prolonged ventilatory dependence. Vasopressors and inotropes were subsequently discontinued, and he was liberated from mechanical ventilation to a tracheostomy collar. Repeat echocardiography showed partial biventricular recovery with LVEF 30%–35%, severe aortic regurgitation, and moderate mitral regurgitation. Transoesophageal echocardiography showed no vegetations.

After a cumulative 123 hospital days across two admissions, the patient was discharged to inpatient rehabilitation, on intermittent haemodialysis, guideline-directed medical therapy, and oral anticoagulation.

Discussion

This case illustrates an unusual longitudinal convergence of autoimmune, thrombotic, and infectious complications in newly diagnosed SLE. Fulminant lupus myopericarditis, class III proliferative nephritis, MPO-ANCA positivity, and CAPS-like thrombosis created substantial diagnostic and therapeutic complexity and required coordinated multidisciplinary care.2–9

Cardiac MRI and kidney biopsy were central to defining organ injury. Cardiac MRI showed myocardial oedema and a subepicardial enhancement pattern supporting lupus myopericarditis, while kidney biopsy demonstrated full-house immune-complex class III proliferative nephritis without pauci-immune necrosis, crescents, vasculitis, or thrombotic microangiopathy. This CMR-based approach aligns with the 2025 ESC Guidelines, which recognize CMR and endomyocardial biopsy as complementary diagnostic pathways selected according to clinical presentation and risk.10 Although MPO-ANCA positivity raised concern for a possible overlapping inflammatory phenotype, the biopsy findings supported lupus nephritis rather than ANCA-associated vasculitis. Therefore, MPO-ANCA positivity was interpreted as a marker of a potentially more severe lupus nephritis phenotype rather than diagnostic evidence of ANCA-associated vasculitis.6,11 Rituximab was selected as individualized adjunctive B-cell-depleting therapy because of the combined severity of dialysis-requiring nephritis and lupus myopericarditis.12

The thrombotic presentation required distinction between clinical treatment and formal classification. The patient developed cerebral and splenic infarctions over a short period in association with severe thrombocytopenia and triple-positive antiphospholipid antibodies at presentation. Repeat testing demonstrated persistent triple-positive antiphospholipid–antibody positivity during the clinical course, including lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin IgG, and anti-β2-glycoprotein I IgG. The rapid temporal evolution was compatible with a CAPS phenotype; however, only two thrombotic organs were documented and histological confirmation of small-vessel thrombosis was unavailable. The presentation therefore did not fulfil criteria for definite or probable CAPS and was classified as a CAPS-like thrombotic syndrome. Given the rapid progression and high-risk antibody profile, empirical treatment with anticoagulation, corticosteroids, plasma exchange, and IVIG was initiated.7–9,13

The newly identified severe aortic and moderate mitral regurgitation were likely multifactorial. Mitral regurgitation may have been partly functional, related to ventricular dilation and systolic dysfunction, whereas severe aortic regurgitation raised concern for primary leaflet involvement, including SLE- or antiphospholipid–antibody-associated valvulopathy. MSSA bacteraemia also required consideration of infective endocarditis; however, transoesophageal echocardiography showed no vegetations. Critical-illness loading conditions may have influenced both lesions. Without valve pathology, a single mechanism could not be established.14

The second hospitalization highlights the vulnerability of survivors after fulminant SLE and intensive immunosuppression. Influenza A, bacterial coinfection, and invasive aspergillosis accompanied recurrent infarctions and cardiogenic shock, illustrating the balance between treating organ-threatening autoimmunity and infectious risk. This supports vaccination review, infection prevention, early evaluation of febrile illness, and close cardiovascular and rheumatologic follow-up.

Limitations include the absence of endomyocardial biopsy, histologic confirmation of small-vessel thrombosis, and valve pathology. Although transoesophageal echocardiography showed no vegetations, sepsis, renal failure, vasoactive therapy, and changing loading conditions limit causal attribution of the ventricular and valvular findings. Despite these limitations, the two-admission course provides a valuable longitudinal view of myocardial recovery followed by infection-associated thrombotic and cardiogenic relapse.
