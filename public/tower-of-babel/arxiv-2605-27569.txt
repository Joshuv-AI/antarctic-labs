RULER: Representation-Level Verification of Machine Unlearning
Georgina Cosma, Axel Finke

Abstract
Machine unlearning aims to remove the influence of specific training records from a deployed model without retraining from scratch. Current protocols verify this at the output level through membership inference, retain accuracy, and forget-set accuracy, but a model can satisfy all three whilst still encoding forgotten records in its intermediate representations. We introduce RULER, a set of representation-level verification metrics. The oracle-comparative metric M 2 M_{2} measures whether forget-set records occupy the same representational position as in a model retrained without them. The oracle-free metric M 4 M_{4} detects residuals from the unlearned model’s internal similarity structure alone, without retraining. Four approximate unlearning methods all pass output-level evaluation, yet under a linear mixed-effects model M 2 M_{2} detects significant residuals in 10 of 12 conditions ( p < 0.05 p<0.05 ), with effect sizes growing as the forget fraction increases. A fifth method, Bad Teacher, shows the same residuals despite a different forgetting mechanism. M 4 M_{4} acts as a pre-unlearning diagnostic across tabular, image, clinical text, and face-identity settings: it detects identity-level memorisation in face recognition models where no tested method fully erases the signal.

Machine unlearning removes the influence of a specified subset of training records (the
forget set
) from a trained model without retraining from scratch, whilst preserving the model’s behaviour on the remaining records (the
retain set
)
[
2
,
1

. Approximate methods such as gradient ascent, fine-tuning, and distillation achieve this at a fraction of the computational cost of full retraining
[

. Determining whether unlearning has succeeded requires an evaluation protocol; most existing protocols rely on three output-level criteria: (a) membership inference attack (MIA) accuracy close to the 0.50 chance level
[

; (b) preserved retain-set accuracy; (c) forget-set accuracy matching a retrain oracle (a model trained from scratch on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}

[

[
4

. We report this discordance across four approximate unlearning methods, three gradient-based (Gradient Ascent
[

[
9
]
), and corroborate it with a fifth method, Bad Teacher
[
3
]
. All pass output-level evaluation but leave statistically significant traces of forgotten records in their internal representations (Fig.
1
). Our contributions are as follows.

-

We identify a discordance between output-level and representation-level evaluation in four approximate unlearning methods.

-

We introduce RULER, a set of representation-level verification metrics: an oracle-comparative metric
M_{2}
that measures deviation from a retrained reference model, and an oracle-free metric
M_{4}
that detects residuals from the unlearned model alone.

-

We show that residuals are small but directionally consistent across datasets, forget fractions, and training seeds;

-

We demonstrate that

and a

that flags incomplete erasure or over-displacement, across tabular, image, clinical text, and face-identity settings.

Related Work

Approximate unlearning and its evaluation.

Approximate methods modify a trained model without full retraining: gradient ascent on the forget set and fine-tuning on the retain set serve as baselines
[
5
]
, NegGrad
+

[
9
]
combines them, and SCRUB
[
9
]
uses teacher-student distillation. Evaluation typically relies on output-level criteria relative to a retrain oracle: MIA accuracy, retain accuracy, and forget accuracy
[
5
,
9
,
10
]
. Hayes et al.
[
7
]
showed that unlearning MIAs can overestimate privacy protection; Goel et al.
[
4
]
demonstrated that indistinguishability protocols are incomplete; Thudi et al.
[
11
]
argued that parameter-space comparisons cannot certify unlearning. Wang et al.
[
12
]
propose evaluating via model differences using influence functions. Chundawat et al.
[
3
]
introduce the Zero Retrain Forgetting (ZRF) metric, which measures Jensen–Shannon divergence between the unlearned model’s forget-set outputs and a randomly initialised teacher, alongside the Bad Teacher framework used in our evaluation.

Representation-level analysis.

Golatkar et al.
[
5
]
introduced weight scrubbing, observing that output metrics may not reflect whether information persists in the network; feature spaces are known to encode structured information not visible from outputs
[
8
]
. Existing metrics operate at the parameter or output level: activation distance
[
6
,
3
]
compares softmax outputs (despite its name, not an intermediate-layer measure); completeness
[
2
]
measures predicted-class agreement; MIA
[
10
]
assesses membership leakage. None examine how a model internally organises information about individual records, or how forget records are treated relative to retain records. RULER addresses this gap with per-record penultimate-layer metrics calibrated against partition-aware nulls (
M_{2}
,
M_{4}
).

Tabular data.

Much of the existing machine unlearning literature focuses on image classification
[
5
,
9
]
, whereas tabular data presents distinct challenges including heterogeneous feature types, frequent class imbalance, and small forget sets at low forget fractions that limit statistical power. We therefore use tabular data as the primary experimental setting and extend the evaluation to image, clinical text, and face-identity domains (Section
5.5
).

3
Representation-Level Evaluation

3.1
Problem statement

We formalise RULER in the primary setting of binary classification on tabular data. The training dataset
\mathcal{D}
consists of records
(x,y)
with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y\in\{0,1\}
. The predicted class probabilities are
\sigma(f_{\theta}(x))
, where
\sigma
is the softmax function and
f_{\theta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a model with parameters
\theta
trained on
\mathcal{D}
. The metric definitions are label-free and do not depend on the number of classes; the diagnostic experiments (Section
5.5
) therefore apply RULER without modification to multi-class image, masked-language-modelling, and face-identity settings, with
f_{\theta}
and the embedding dimension differing per architecture (Appendix Table
2
).

We assume that the full training set is partitioned into a retain set
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
and a forget set
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}
, and we define the
forget fraction

\texttt{ff}\coloneqq|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}|/|\mathcal{D}|
. Three models are defined:

•

the
original
model
\theta^{\mathrm{o}}
trained on
\mathcal{D}
;

•

an
unlearned
model
\theta^{\mathrm{u}}
produced by applying an unlearning algorithm to
\theta^{\mathrm{o}}
;

•

the
retrain oracle

\theta^{\mathrm{r}}
trained from scratch on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}
alone.

The oracle serves as the gold-standard reference: a model with no knowledge of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}
. Starting from
\theta^{\mathrm{o}}
, the goal of machine unlearning is to produce an unlearned model
\theta^{\mathrm{u}}
that behaves as if
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}
were never seen during training, matching the oracle
\theta^{\mathrm{r}}
in outputs and internal representations, without incurring the computational cost of retraining from scratch. Reliable evaluation metrics are therefore needed that verify erasure at the representation level, not only at the output level.

3.2
Penultimate-layer cosine similarity

The proposed metrics use cosine similarity between penultimate-layer embeddings. Let
h_{\theta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
denote the penultimate-layer embedding function of a model
\theta
, i.e. the activation immediately before the task-specific output head
g_{\theta}
, so that
f_{\theta}=g_{\theta}\circ h_{\theta}
. The embedding dimension
p
is fixed by the architecture; in the primary tabular experiments (Section
4.1
)
p=128
, and the diagnostic experiments use the analogous penultimate activation of each architecture (Section
5.5
). All embeddings are L2-normalised prior to any similarity computation.

Thus, for any two records
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and models
\theta,\theta^{\prime}
, the cosine similarity in the embedding space is:

\mathrm{sim}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{h_{\theta}(x)^{\top}h_{\theta^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})}{\lVert h_{\theta}(x)\rVert\,\lVert h_{\theta^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})\rVert}=h_{\theta}(x)^{\top}h_{\theta^{\prime}}(x^{\prime}),

(1)

where the second equality follows from L2-normalisation. The metrics compare either the same record across two models (
cross-model similarity
,
\mathrm{sim}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}(x)\coloneqq\mathrm{sim}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}(x,x)
) or two records within the same model (
within-model similarity
,
\mathrm{sim}_{\theta}(x,x^{\prime})\coloneqq\mathrm{sim}_{\theta,\theta}(x,x^{\prime})
).

The penultimate layer is the final feature abstraction before the task-specific output head; earlier layers share lower-level features across records, and the output layer collapses representations into logits or per-token distributions, discarding geometric structure. Cosine similarity operates per record (necessary for small forget sets), is magnitude-invariant in L2-normalised space (enabling cross-dataset comparison), and produces a scalar for statistical testing against a calibrated null. Centred kernel alignment (CKA)
[
8
]
and mutual information estimators produce matrix-level summaries that do not naturally decompose per record, making them unsuitable for verifying individual erasure requests under GDPR Article 17.

3.3
Lens 1: oracle-comparative metrics (
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
,
M_{3}
)

Lens 1 metrics require access to the retrain oracle
\theta^{\mathrm{r}}
and measure how closely the unlearned model’s representations of records in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}
agree with those of the oracle. All Lens 1 metrics use a paired-seed design: the original model
\theta^{\mathrm{o}}
and retrain oracle
\theta^{\mathrm{r}}
share the same random initialisation seed, so that representational differences reflect the unlearning procedure rather than geometric variation across random initialisations.

M_{1}
: oracle-comparison similarity.

For each forget record,
M_{1}
computes the cosine similarity between its embedding under the unlearned model and its embedding under the oracle, then averages these similarities across the forget set:

M_{1}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}}\mathrm{sim}_{\theta^{\mathrm{u}},\theta^{\mathrm{r}}}(x).

(2)

A value near 1.0 indicates that the unlearned model places forget-set records in the same representational neighbourhood as the oracle.
M_{1}
provides an absolute similarity measure but has no fixed reference value against which to test significance, because its expected value under successful unlearning depends on the dataset and random seed. Here and below, we drop the class labels
y
to simplify the notation, writing ‘
x\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
’ as shorthand for ‘
(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
’ for any dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.

M_{2}
: signed calibration gap.

To address
M_{1}
’s lack of a fixed reference value,
M_{2}
subtracts the median cross-model similarity that retain-set records achieve between the same unlearned and oracle model pair, producing a calibrated gap with a null of zero:

M_{2}=M_{1}-\underset{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}}{\mathrm{median}}\;\mathrm{sim}_{\theta^{\mathrm{u}},\theta^{\mathrm{r}}}(x).

(3)

The median is preferred over the arithmetic mean because the retain-set similarity distribution is right-skewed. A small number of retain records achieve disproportionately high cosine similarity to the oracle, inflating the mean above the median. The size of this inflation is comparable to the gap itself. Replacing the median with the mean baseline shifts
M_{2}
by
+0.0023
for NegGrad
+
and by
+0.0013
for Fine-Tuning at
\texttt{ff}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}$
(Appendix Table
5
), the latter exceeding the median-baseline gap and reversing its sign. Consequently, replacing the median with the mean baseline reduces significant conditions from
12
of
12
(Wilcoxon test) to
3
of
12
and reverses the sign in
5
of
12
. The forget set uses the mean (
M_{1}
) because it is smaller, and every forget record, including outliers that may signal incomplete erasure, should contribute to the summary. The retain-set median is estimated per seed from a random subsample of
\min\{500,|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}|\}
records. The null hypothesis is
M_{2}=0
, meaning forget-set records attain the same similarity to the oracle as retained records (oracle-level forgetting); we confirm this null is empirically achievable using independently retrained oracle pairs in Appendix
0.A.5
.
M_{2}
should be interpreted alongside retain accuracy, since a gap of zero is only meaningful if the model remains predictive on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}
. Negative values indicate residual memorisation; positive values indicate the unlearning procedure has moved forget-set representations closer to the oracle than retained records. This is the
primary Lens 1 metric
.

M_{3}
: representation shift.

M_{3}
asks whether unlearning moved forget-set representations closer to or further from the oracle, by comparing each record’s unlearned-to-oracle similarity against its original-to-oracle similarity before unlearning:

M_{3}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}}\bigl[\mathrm{sim}_{\theta^{\mathrm{u}},\theta^{\mathrm{r}}}(x)-\mathrm{sim}_{\theta^{\mathrm{o}},\theta^{\mathrm{r}}}(x)\bigr].

(4)

Positive values indicate that unlearning moved forget-set representations closer to the oracle (the intended outcome). Values near zero indicate that unlearning had negligible effect on the similarity to the oracle at the representation level. Negative values indicate that unlearning moved forget-set representations further from the oracle rather than closer.

3.4
Lens 2: oracle-free metric
M_{4}

Lens 2 requires no retrained oracle and operates solely on the internal geometry of the unlearned model
\theta^{\mathrm{u}}
and the data partition
\{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}\}
. For each forget record, we find its nearest neighbour in the retain set under the unlearned model and measure their cosine similarity. We then rank this similarity within the leave-one-out nearest-neighbour similarities of the retain records themselves. A forget record that blends in with retained records will sit at the median of this distribution, giving a percentile rank of
0.50
.

Formally, for each forget record
x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}
, define its nearest-retain-neighbour similarity under the unlearned model:

s_{\mathrm{f}}(x)=\max_{x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}}\;\mathrm{sim}_{\theta^{\mathrm{u}}}(x,x^{\prime\prime}).

(5)

For each retain record
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}
, define its leave-one-out nearest-retain-neighbour similarity:

s_{\mathrm{r}}(x^{\prime})=\max_{x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}\setminus\{x^{\prime}\}}\;\mathrm{sim}_{\theta^{\mathrm{u}}}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}).

(6)

Both
s_{\mathrm{f}}
and
s_{\mathrm{r}}
use only the unlearned model, with no oracle required. A retain record would otherwise match itself at similarity
1.0
, which is why
s_{\mathrm{r}}
takes the maximum over
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}\setminus\{x^{\prime}\}
rather than all of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}
; a forget record has no such self-match to exclude. Both quantities therefore measure similarity to the closest distinct retain record, so the percentile rank is not inflated by giving forget records an extra candidate neighbour.

M_{4}(x)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}|}\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}}\mathbf{1}\bigl[s_{\mathrm{r}}(x^{\prime})\leq s_{\mathrm{f}}(x)\bigr],

(7)

where
\mathbf{1}[\,\cdot\,]
is the indicator function. The aggregate
M_{4}
reported in the results is the mean of
M_{4}(x)
over all
x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}
.

The null hypothesis is
M_{4}=0.50
. A forget record that has been properly erased should be geometrically indistinguishable from a retained record, sitting at the median of the retain nearest-neighbour distribution. A value above
0.50
indicates the forget record’s nearest-retain-neighbour similarity exceeds that of the median retained record, a sign of residual memorisation. A value below
0.50
indicates over-displacement, meaning the record has been pushed further from the retain distribution than a correctly retrained model would produce. This is the
primary Lens 2 metric
.

The use of
M_{4}
assumes that memorisation is reflected as a geometric deviation in the representation space under the chosen similarity measure. If such deviations are not present, the metric may not detect memorisation.

Relationship between the two lenses.

M_{2}
measures repositioning relative to the oracle and supports population-level inference across datasets, with negative values indicating residual memorisation, positive values over-correction, and zero values oracle-level erasure.
M_{4}
measures geometric indistinguishability within the unlearned model alone, making it oracle-free but inherently dataset-specific. We therefore report
M_{4}
as a per-dataset diagnostic rather than a population-level test.

4.3
Evaluation protocol and statistical analysis

The oracle-comparative metrics operate on penultimate-layer embeddings; for the retain-set baseline in
M_{2}
, a subsample of
\min(500,|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}|)
records (random state 42) is drawn per seed.
M_{4}
computes nearest-retain-neighbour similarities across all
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}
records, capped at 2,000 for memory. Output-level metrics are computed in parallel: MIA accuracy (threshold-based attack using per-sample cross-entropy loss, with balanced accuracy reported), retain, forget, and test accuracy. Let
\overline{\text{MIA}}
denote the mean MIA accuracy across the 10 training seeds per (dataset, method, forget fraction) condition. The pass threshold
|\overline{\text{MIA}}-0.50|<0.05
accommodates dataset-level variation.

We test
M_{2}
against null
0
and
M_{4}
against null
0.50
. The primary analysis is a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) with random intercept for dataset, fitted via restricted maximum likelihood (REML) to all
N=100
observations; the fixed intercept is tested using the Wald
z
-statistic. With only 10 clusters this test can overstate significance, so we also report the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on
N=10
dataset-level means. The intra-class correlation
\mathrm{ICC}=\sigma_{u}^{2}/(\sigma_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})
, where
\sigma_{u}^{2}
is the between-dataset (random-intercept) variance and
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
the within-dataset (residual) variance estimated by the LMM, quantifies between-dataset variance share. Effect sizes are reported as rank-biserial correlation
r_{\mathrm{rb}}=1-4W/[n(n+1)]
, where
W
is the Wilcoxon statistic and
n
the number of non-tied observations;
|r_{\mathrm{rb}}|\geq 0.10,0.30,0.50
denote small, medium, large effects per conventional thresholds.

Results

Key findings.
All four methods pass output-level evaluation (Appendix Table
3
), but
M_{2}
detects significant residuals in 10 of 12 conditions under the LMM (Table
1
), with effect sizes growing with the forget fraction and directional consistency across datasets (Appendix Fig.
6
). After correction, methods are statistically indistinguishable on
M_{2}
,
M_{4}
, and MIA, indicating the discordance is a property of the task, not any algorithm. Bad Teacher reproduces the same negative
M_{2}
at every fraction despite a fundamentally different mechanism (Section
5.4
).
M_{4}
is dataset-specific (ICC
=0.89
) and serves as a per-dataset diagnostic, sensitive to identity-level memorisation (Appendix Fig.
8
) and over-displacement on large architectures.

\pm 0.05

5.3
Methods are interchangeable on representation metrics but differ on retain accuracy

Post-hoc pairwise Wilcoxon tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all six method pairs and four metrics (24 tests) show that no pair is distinguishable on
M_{2}
,

, or MIA accuracy after correction (

5.5

beyond tabular MLPs

Beyond verifying unlearning,

can indicate whether unlearning is necessary at the representation level. Applied to the original model, it reveals whether forget-set records are memorised or blend into the general distribution; when memorisation is absent, applying unlearning may introduce artefacts. We evaluate this diagnostic across four settings: large-scale tabular data (Covertype, Higgs; 100,000 samples; MLP, Residual MLP, FT-Transformer), image classification (CIFAR-10, SVHN, CIFAR-100; three-layer CNN and ResNet-18), clinical text (MTSamples with Bio_ClinicalBERT, masked language modelling), and face-identity unlearning (LFW; ResNet-18 trained for gender classification). and

6
Discussion and Conclusion

All four approximate methods pass output-level evaluation but leave statistically significant residuals on
M_{2}
. Fine-Tuning and SCRUB preserve retain accuracy better than Gradient Ascent and NegGrad
+
, but this advantage does not extend to representation-level metrics. Bad Teacher reproduces the same outcome despite a different forgetting mechanism, indicating that the discordance reflects a limitation of current approaches rather than any individual method. NegGrad
+
and SCRUB on deeper architectures introduce geometric artefacts where none existed prior to unlearning.
For practitioners,
M_{4}
provides information at two stages. Before unlearning, values near
0.50
on the original model indicate unlearning may be unnecessary. Values far above
0.50
(
M_{4}=0.73
to
0.94
on LFW) confirm identity-level structure that no tested method fully erases. After unlearning, deviations from
0.50
flag incomplete erasure or over-displacement that output-level metrics miss.
M_{4}
requires only the model and the data partition. For method developers, the consistency of residuals suggests future unlearning objectives should incorporate representation-level constraints directly. Methodological limitations are detailed in Appendix
0.A.2
.

0.A.2
Methodological limitations

The following limitations and assumptions apply to the framework presented in the main text. (1) The metrics are technical verification tools rather than legal compliance tests under GDPR Article 17; they characterise representational residuals, not regulatory adequacy. (2) Cosine similarity is not rotation-invariant, so
M_{2}
requires the paired-seed design described in Section
3.3
to remain meaningful. (3) Diagnostic experiments (large-scale tabular, image, clinical text, LFW) use five seeds per condition, leaving open whether the over-displacement observed for NegGrad
+
and SCRUB on deeper architectures can be mitigated by hyperparameter tuning. (4) A single unlearning seed is used per training seed to separate variability from training initialisation from that of the unlearning procedure; sensitivity to the unlearning seed is untested for the four main methods, though sensitivity to forget-set sampling is characterised across five additional seeds (Appendix
0.A.8
, Table
9
) and Bad Teacher is stable across three teacher seeds (Appendix
0.A.11
). (5) The primary tabular experiments use uniformly random forget sets, and the LFW experiment uses structured identity-level forget sets; broader coverage of structured deletion scenarios (e.g. demographic groups, temporal cohorts) remains an open direction.

0.A.11
Comparison with Bad Teacher

Gradient Ascent, NegGrad
+
, Fine-Tuning, and SCRUB all modify the original model’s parameters directly. Bad Teacher
[
3
]
operates differently, training a student to match a randomly initialised teacher on forget-set records. Testing whether the same residuals appear under this mechanism indicates whether the discordance is method-specific or structural.

Implementation.

The implementation follows Chundawat et al.
[
3
]
. A randomly initialised
TabularMLP
is frozen as the teacher. A student copy of the original model is trained for 10 epochs using Adam at
\eta_{u}=5\times 10^{-4}
,
\alpha=0.6
,
T=2.0
, matching the SCRUB hyperparameters. On forget-set records the student is trained to match the random teacher’s output distribution; on retain-set records it minimises both the KL divergence to the random teacher and the cross-entropy loss on true labels, following the loss formulation in
[
3
]
with the random teacher in place of the incompetent teacher. A single unlearning seed (
j=100
) is used, matching the design of the main experiment. To assess sensitivity to teacher initialisation, we repeated the analysis with two additional teacher seeds (101, 102).
M_{2}
remained negative for all ten datasets at all three forget fractions across all three seeds.
M_{4}
values were stable across seeds, with maximum within-dataset variation below
0.007
. These results confirm that the findings are not specific to the choice of teacher initialisation.

Results.

Bad Teacher passes output-level evaluation at all three forget fractions.
M_{2}
detects significant residuals throughout (
p<0.01
; Table
10
), meaning forget-set records remain displaced from where the oracle places them.
M_{4}
does not reach significance at any fraction, with mean ranks remaining above
0.50
(
0.528
,
0.514
,
0.505
at
\texttt{ff}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}$
,
5\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
,
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
), consistent with the attenuation observed in Section
5.2
. Paired comparisons on
M_{4}
(Table
11
) show that Bad Teacher displaces forget-set records further from the retain set than all four approximate methods at
\texttt{ff}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}$
(all
p\leq 0.01
). At larger fractions no comparison reaches significance. This stronger displacement does not achieve complete erasure:
M_{2}
remains negative at every fraction, confirming forget-set records still fall below the retain-set baseline in the oracle’s representation space. The discordance therefore holds across five methods spanning direct parameter modification and distillation-based unlearning, indicating that current approximate methods are optimised for output-level criteria that leave intermediate-layer representations unconstrained.

0.A.16
Reproducibility

All code to reproduce the experiments, metrics, and figures is available at an anonymised repository (URL provided upon acceptance). All experiments use fixed random seeds propagated to dataset splitting (random state 999), forget-set sampling (random state 999), training initialisation (seeds 0 to 9), and unlearning (seed 100). For each training seed
i\in\{0,\ldots,9\}
, the original model
\theta^{\mathrm{o}}
and retrain oracle
\theta^{\mathrm{r}}
are trained with the same random seed. Same-seed oracle–original similarity has a grand mean of
0.993
across ten datasets, compared with
0.431
for differently seeded oracle pairs, confirming that shared initialisation produces similar representational geometry. Original models and retrain oracles are cached as
.pt
files after the initial training run, and all unlearning procedures operate from these identical cached weights.
