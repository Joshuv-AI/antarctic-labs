Hidden oral inflammation and biomarker misclassification in obstructive sleep apnea trials
Authors: Solomon Olusegun Nwhator
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory biomarkers are used as surrogate endpoints in obstructive sleep apnea trials, assuming the measured signal originates from the airway or systemic response to intermittent hypoxia. Periodontitis—a common, extrapulmonary inflammatory reservoir—elevates systemic active matrix metalloproteinase-8 independently of obstructive sleep apnea. This Perspective examines whether unmeasured oral inflammation confounds biomarker attribution in obstructive sleep apnea trials. Epidemiological and interventional evidence indicates that periodontitis is highly prevalent, is more common in individuals with obstructive sleep apnea, and substantially elevates circulating active matrix metalloproteinase-8. Moreover, non-surgical periodontal therapy reduces this signal to a degree comparable to early Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) effects. In published CPAP trials that reported inflammatory or cardiovascular outcomes, periodontal status has not been reported or incorporated into primary analyses. We propose a heuristic conceptual model: measured circulating active matrix metalloproteinase-8 = obstructive sleep apnea-related component + periodontal-derived component + measurement noise. Without adjustment for periodontal status, observed changes cannot be uniquely attributed to obstructive sleep apnea treatment. The ORBIT heuristic framework (Oral Reservoir Bias Inflammatory Trial) is introduced as a conceptual prompt for reporting oral inflammatory burden. Inflammatory biomarker responses in obstructive sleep apnea trials are consistent with a composite signal that includes an unmeasured oral component. A testable prediction is that adjusting for periodontal status would reduce between-trial heterogeneity of matrix metalloproteinase-8effect estimates without altering within-subject respiratory outcomes. Prospective validation is warranted. The framework extends beyond active matrix metalloproteinase-8 to any circulating inflammatory marker influenced by extra-target inflammatory reservoirs.

Introduction

Inflammatory biomarkers are increasingly used as surrogate endpoints in obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) trials. Their interpretation rests on a critical assumption: that the measured signal originates primarily from the airway or from the systemic response to intermittent hypoxia. This Perspective does not assess CPAP clinical efficacy outcomes; it focuses on the identifiability of inflammatory biomarker signals in OSA trials. It argues that an unmeasured oral inflammatory reservoir—periodontitis—systematically violates that assumption, leading to composite signal bias (i.e. non-specificity of circulating biomarker attribution across inflammatory compartments) and contributing to unexplained heterogeneity across CPAP trials.

We use active matrix metalloproteinase-8 (aMMP-8) as a tractable exemplar. aMMP-8 has attracted interest because of its links to neutrophilic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and tissue remodeling [1–3]. However, aMMP-8 is also the most specific biomarker of active periodontal tissue destruction [4, 5]. The broader principle applies to any circulating inflammatory marker influenced by extra-target inflammatory reservoirs, particularly neutrophil-derived proteases and inflammatory enzymes. MMP-8 is used here as an illustrative but not exclusive biomarker class. The central question is not whether periodontitis matters for oral health, but whether its systemic inflammatory signal distorts the interpretation of trial endpoints that rely on circulating inflammatory markers.

This argument pertains to biomarker inference, not to the clinical effectiveness of OSA treatment.

Evidence Base

The hidden oral inflammatory reservoir

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease caused by dysbiotic oral biofilm, affecting the tooth-supporting tissues. It is common: approximately half of adults worldwide have some form of periodontitis [6]. The prevalence is even higher among individuals with OSA. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Latorre et al. reported a significant association between OSA and periodontitis (OR 1.84; 95% CI = 1.45% to 2.34%) [7]. More recently, Arango Jimenez and colleagues confirmed this association and highlighted mouth breathing as a contributing mechanism [8]. Because OSA patients have a higher baseline burden of periodontal disease, the potential for confounding of inflammatory biomarkers is magnified in this population—making the ORBIT framework (Oral Reservoir Bias Inflammatory Trial) particularly relevant. In periodontitis, the inflamed pocket epithelium becomes ulcerated, creating a direct vascular interface that allows locally produced enzymes, including aMMP-8, to enter the systemic circulation [9]. Once in blood or plasma, the tissue origin of aMMP-8 cannot be distinguished.

The magnitude of this systemic effect is substantial. Multiple published studies consistently report large elevations in serum/plasma aMMP-8 in periodontitis patients compared to healthy controls [10, 11]. More importantly, non-surgical periodontal therapy reduces plasma MMP-8 by 35% (p < .02) [12] and serum MMP-8 by significant margins [13, 14]. Thus, periodontitis is not a static confounder; its treatment actively lowers the very biomarker that OSA trials measure.

The absence of periodontal data means that any observed change in circulating inflammatory markers (including MMP-8) cannot be uniquely attributed to OSA or its treatment. The 35% reduction in plasma MMP-8 after periodontal therapy alone is similar in magnitude to early CPAP-induced declines [1, 12], raising the possibility that a proportion of the signal observed after CPAP may partially covary with untreated periodontal inflammation—consistent with the measurement model presented next.

Conceptual model

Consequence for CPAP trials

The observed change after CPAP, ΔMMP-8(measured), is: ΔMMP-8(measured) = ΔMMP-8(OSA-related) + ΔMMP-8(periodontitis) Without measurement of periodontal status, ΔMMP-8(periodontitis) is unobservable. Therefore, ΔMMP-8(measured) cannot be uniquely attributed to OSA treatment. Residual confounding occurs because periodontitis may remain stable, progress, or improve spontaneously, independent of CPAP.

Implications for OSA trial interpretation

The implications extend beyond MMP-8 and beyond sleep medicine. Any trial that uses inflammatory biomarkers as surrogate endpoints—in cardiology, metabolism, aging, or respiratory medicine—may be vulnerable to unmeasured oral inflammatory burden. Failure to measure common inflammatory reservoirs may partly explain inconsistent biomarker responses across CPAP trials despite similar physiologic treatment effects.

The ORBIT heuristic framework is introduced as a conceptual prompt to encourage consideration of oral inflammatory burden in trial design and reporting. It does not replace existing reporting guidelines (CONSORT, STROBE) but highlights a previously unstructured confounding domain. For future OSA trials that measure inflammatory biomarkers, the ORBIT framework may encourage minimum reporting of the following domains:

Periodontal status—e.g. Periodontal Screening and Recording (PSR) index or a simple question on gum bleeding.

Mouth breathing (self-reported or clinically assessed during sleep study)—a potential mechanical link between OSA and oral dysbiosis that may exacerbate periodontal inflammation. Where feasible, point-of-care aMMP-8 testing in oral fluids—a validated, non-invasive, low-cost tool [5].

As an example of operationalization, a trial’s case report form could include: “Does the participant report gum bleeding or loose teeth?” and “Periodontal Screening and Recording (PSR) score (if assessable).”

This proposal is deliberately framed as a heuristic to stimulate discussion and covariate awareness, not as a prescriptive guideline. It is not burdensome and can be implemented in routine clinical trial workflows with minimal additional cost.

Future Directions

A falsifiable prediction

A testable implication of this framework is that adjustment for periodontal status (or oral aMMP-8) would be expected to reduce between-trial heterogeneity of MMP-8 effect estimates (τ2), acknowledging that the magnitude depends on meta-analytic assumptions and outcome harmonization, without altering within-subject respiratory outcomes. Specifically:

Respiratory efficacy measures (e.g. Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI), oxygen desaturation index) should remain unchanged after adjusting for periodontal status.

Testable prediction

If periodontal status is included as a covariate in a meta-analysis of CPAP trials, the between-study heterogeneity (τ2) in MMP-8 change scores would be expected to decrease, while respiratory outcomes remain unchanged.

This separation— where a confounder affects biomarker endpoints but not the primary physiological outcome— is a hallmark of composite signal bias. Prospective meta-analyses of existing CPAP trials could test this prediction by reanalyzing available data from studies that inadvertently recorded periodontal parameters (e.g. through routine dental notes) or by conducting a pooled analysis of trials that collected oral health information. This prediction is conceptual; its empirical confirmation would require careful meta-analytic methods that account for study-level covariates.

Limitations and cautionary note

This Perspective does not claim that periodontitis explains the results of any specific trial. The effect size of periodontal adjustment on clinical outcome estimates remains to be tested prospectively. Moreover, periodontitis is one of several potential confounders; others include adherence, phenotype differences, and baseline inflammatory status. Our argument is that it is a plausible, measurable, and currently overlooked confounder—and therefore should be recorded. The heuristic bias model (Box 1) provides a testable structure rather than a definitive claim. The additive decomposition is a heuristic; biological interactions may produce non-linear effects, which would modify the magnitude but not the existence of the confounding. The analysis is restricted to neutrophil-linked proteases and inflammatory enzymes (exemplified by aMMP-8); generalization to all inflammatory biomarkers requires further empirical justification.

Conclusion

Inflammatory biomarker responses in OSA trials are consistent with a composite signal that includes an unmeasured oral component. Periodontitis is common, it elevates systemic aMMP-8 substantially, and treating it lowers that signal by an amount comparable to early CPAP effects. In the trials surveyed in this Perspective, periodontal status was not reported or incorporated into analyses.

We propose the ORBIT framework as a conceptual prompt to include oral inflammatory burden in reporting frameworks. A testable prediction is that adjusting for periodontal status would reduce between-trial heterogeneity of biomarker effect estimates without altering respiratory outcomes—an empirical pathway to validate or refute the framework. While illustrated with aMMP-8, the ORBIT framework is intended to be biomarker-agnostic.
