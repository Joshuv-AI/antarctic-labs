CD90 mediates gastric cancer immune evasion though regulating IGF2BP2 to stabilize the m6A-CD47/SIRPα axis
Authors: Siyi Liu, Lin Liang, Zihua Zhou, Chenyu Zhang, Shan Liao, Qian He, Yan Lei, Juan Xu, Gengqiu Luo, Yanling Li, Yanhong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a cell surface glycoprotein, CD90 plays a significant role in the initiation and progression of malignancies such as gastric cancer (GC) by influencing tumor cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis. However, its specific role in immune evasion in GC and its potential therapeutic value have not been fully explored. In this study, we report that CD90 is highly expressed in GC tissues and is positively correlated with macrophage immune infiltration. Furthermore, we demonstrate that CD90 can mediate immune evasion in GC by affecting the phagocytic function of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Mechanistically, CD90 functions as a scaffold protein, inhibiting the interaction between IGF2BP2 and TRIM21. This inhibition stabilizes the expression of the m6A methylation reader protein IGF2BP2. Subsequently, IGF2BP2 enhances the mRNA stability of the immune checkpoint molecule CD47 in an m6A-dependent manner, leading to the activation of the CD47/SIRPα axis. Ultimately, by inhibiting the phagocytic function of TAMs, CD90 mediates immune evasion in GC. In summary, our work highlights the critical role of the CD90-IGF2BP2-CD47 axis in immune evasion in GC. This finding is expected to provide significant experimental evidence for elucidating the pathogenesis of GC and discovering potential molecular targets for clinical treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12935-026-04406-z.Keywords: Gastric cancer, CD90, IGF2BP2, CD47, Tumor immune evasion

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a highly heterogeneous disease in terms of its molecular and phenotypic characteristics. It ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related death and the fifth most common cancer worldwide [1]. Due to the lack of clear clinical symptoms, most GC patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, resulting in a poor prognosis [2]. In recent years, immunotherapy has emerged as a promising new approach for treating GC, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors such as PD-1 antibodies. These treatments have significantly improved the prognosis of GC patients. However, the tumor microenvironment often suppresses the activation and cytotoxic effects of the immune system, making the restoration of immune cell function a major challenge in current GC research [3].

CD90 is a cell surface glycoprotein anchored to the cell surface by GPI and characterized by a high degree of N-glycosylation [4, 5]. It has been identified as a key regulatory factor in cell adhesion and communication, cell-matrix interactions, and the immune and nervous systems. In solid tumors such as GC, CD90 plays a significant role in tumor cell proliferation, metastasis, and angiogenesis, and has been recognized as a poor prognostic indicator for many malignant tumors [4–10]. Previous studies have shown that CD90 is closely related to TAMs. For example, the USF1/CD90 signaling pathway maintains the stemness of glioblastoma cells and their adhesion to TAMs [4]. Additionally, CD90 may participate in angiogenesis by recruiting and functionally regulating TAMs through interactions with integrins expressed on circulating monocytes [11]. Research has also demonstrated that CD90 can serve as a tumor stem cell marker for various malignant tumors, and blocking CD90 activity with anti-CD90 monoclonal antibodies can enhance anti-tumor effects [12]. Furthermore, CD90+ NK1.1− innate lymphocytes play a key role in tumor clearance and hold potential value for chemoimmunotherapy [13]. In summary, CD90 may influence tumor cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis through TAMs, thereby contributing to the occurrence and development of malignant tumors such as GC and its immunotherapy. However, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying CD90-mediated immune escape in GC require further investigation.

Differentiation cluster 47 (CD47) is highly expressed on the surface of most normal cells, with the exception of aging red blood cells. CD47 is also highly expressed in a variety of cancer cells, including acute myeloid leukemia (AML), glioblastoma, ovarian cancer, breast cancer, and colon cancer [14–16]. Signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) is an immune checkpoint present on macrophages, dendritic cells, and neutrophils, with CD47 serving as its ligand [17, 18]. In recent years, the potential role of the CD47-SIRPα interaction in mediating macrophage phagocytosis of tumor cells and tumor immune escape has been extensively studied [19–21]. For example, Siddhartha Jaiswal et al. found that overexpression of CD47 on myeloid leukemia cells prevents macrophages from clearing tumor cells, thereby enhancing the in vivo survival of these cells [19]. Additionally, Zhao X et al. demonstrated that interfering with the CD47-SIRPα interaction (e.g., through CD47 knockout or the use of anti-CD47 or anti-SIRPα antibodies) significantly enhanced the killing capacity of macrophages against Her2/Neu-positive breast cancer cells in vitro [20]. In summary, the CD47/SIRPα axis is a promising innate immune checkpoint in tumors and plays a crucial role in tumor immune escape. However, the role of the CD47/SIRPα axis in immune escape in GC remains unclear.

In this study, we report that CD90 and m6A modifications are involved in regulating the phagocytic function of TAMs, thereby mediating immune escape in GC. Specifically, in GC cells, CD90 positively regulates the expression of the m6A methylated reading protein IGF2BP2. This regulation enhances the mRNA stability of the immune checkpoint molecule CD47, thereby activating the CD47/SIRPα axis. Ultimately, this process mediates GC immune escape by inhibiting macrophage phagocytic function. This study aims to elucidate the role and molecular mechanism of CD90 in GC immune escape, and to provide a new perspective for the immunotherapy of GC.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimen

GC tissues and corresponding adjacent non-cancerous tissues were collected from 18 GC patients at Xiangya Medical College Affiliated Cancer Hospital/Hunan Provincial Cancer Hospital of Central South University from 2024 to 2025. All specimens were collected with the patients’ informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Medical College Affiliated Cancer Hospital/Hunan Cancer Hospital of Central South University and complies with the ethical standards set forth in the Helsinki Declaration.

Gastric cancer cell lines and cell culture

The GC cell lines HGC-27, AGS, MFC, and THP-1 were obtained from the Institute of Oncology, Xiangya School of Basic Medicine, Central South University. These cell lines are regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination to ensure their purity. The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Procell, PM150110, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Procell, 164210-50, Wuhan, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S, Macgene, Beijing, China). All cells were cultured at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO₂.

Plasmid and cell transfection

In this study, the eukaryotic expression vectors used were as follows: pcDNA3.1-3xFlag-C, pcDNA3.1-HA, pcDNA3.1-EGFP-C2, pcDNA3.1-3xFlag-C-CD90, pcDNA3.1-3xFlag-C-mCD90, pcDNA3.1-EGFP-C2-mCD90, pcDNA3.1-HA-IGF2BP2, pcDNA3.1-3xFlag-C-IGF2BP2, pcDNA3.1-HA-TRIM21, and pcDNA3.1-HA-Ub. These vectors were purchased from Changsha Youbao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Additionally, PLKO.1-PURO-shCD90, PLKO.1-PURO-shIGF2BP2, and PLKO.1-PURO-shTRIM21 were purchased from Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Transfection was performed using Neofect DNA transfection reagent (Neofect Biotech, China) or polyplus transfection reagent (Sartorius Stedim Biotech, France). When conducting plasmid transfection experiments, we strictly followed the instructions provided by the corresponding manufacturers.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRnaZol Reagent (M5101) (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using All-in-One Script RT Premix (with dsDNase) (Kermey Biotech, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted on the Bio-Rad CFX96 qPCR system using specific primers and 2× SYBR Green qPCR Premix (Universal) (Kermey Biotech, China). The 2^−ΔΔCT method was used to calculate fold changes for relative quantification. The detailed sequences of the primers used in this study are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed according to the previously established procedure [22]. In brief, proteins were separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). After blocking the membrane with 5% skim milk, specific primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. The protein bands were visualized and photographed. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software (V1.52). For detailed information on antibodies, please refer to Supplementary Table 2.

Co-immunoprecipitation mass spectrum (Co-IP-MS)

Total cellular protein was extracted using IP lysis buffer (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors and/or phosphatase inhibitors (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China). Immunoprecipitation of the lysate supernatant was performed overnight at 4 °C using protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher, USA), anti-FLAG beads (Sigma Aldrich, USA), or anti-HA beads pre-bound with specific primary antibodies. The next day, the precipitated protein mixture was washed 5–6 times with IP lysis buffer. After immunoprecipitation was complete, MS analysis was performed on the precipitated protein mixture. For detailed information on antibodies, please refer to Supplementary Table 3.

Immunohistochemistry(IHC)

Tumor samples were fixed, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin for sectioning according to the previously described method [23]. In brief, tissue sections were boiled in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval. Next, the sections were blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h and incubated overnight with the primary antibody at 4 ℃, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody at 37 ℃ for 30 min. The stained sections were then imaged and analyzed using the Saiviewer software. For detailed information on antibodies, please refer to Supplementary Table 3.

Differentiation of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) and co culture with tumor cells

Mouse bone marrow cells were derived from C57BL/J6 mice as previously described [24]. We collected cells from mouse femur and tibia and plated them in complete DMEM medium containing 100 ng/mL recombinant macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) (Novoprotein Scientific Inc., China) for differentiation for 4 days. On the 5th day, replace the culture medium and obtain mature BMDM on the 7th day of cultivation. Meanwhile, tumor cells were transfected accordingly. Subsequently, the mature BMDM and transfected tumor cells were inoculated into a culture plate at the desired ratio, and fresh culture medium was added for further cultivation.

Induced differentiation of THP-1 cells and co culture with tumor cells

THP-1 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected and inoculated into new culture plates. An appropriate amount of phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) solution was added to achieve a final concentration of 100 ng/mL. The cells were then cultured for approximately 48 h, during which THP-1 cells transitioned from a suspended state to an adherent state and gradually differentiated into macrophage-like cells. Meanwhile, tumor cells were transfected accordingly. Subsequently, the induced THP-1 cells and transfected tumor cells were inoculated into a culture plate at the desired ratio, and fresh culture medium was added for further cultivation.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells were inoculated onto glass coverslips at a density of approximately 50%. After cell attachment, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15–20 min. The cells were then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, the specific primary antibody was diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated overnight at 4 ℃. The cells were then incubated with the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody at 37 ℃ in the dark for 1 h. Next, the cell nuclei were stained with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) in the dark for 30 min to label them. Finally, the samples were sealed with an anti-quenching mounting medium. For detailed information on antibodies, please refer to Supplementary Table 3.

Flow cytometry (FCM) analysis of phagocytic function of TAMs

GC HGC-27, AGS and MFC cells were labeled with the green fluorescent dye carboxyfluorescein succinimide ester (CFSE) (C1031, Beyotime Biotech, China). PMA-induced THP-1 cells were labeled with the red fluorescent dye PKH26 (D0030, Solarbio, China). The labeled HGC-27 or AGS cells were then co-cultured with macrophages and treated with or without the CD47 inhibitor RRx001 (Targeted Mol, USA). After 4 h, the co-cultures were analyzed using flow cytometry and FlowJo software. The phagocytic index was calculated as the proportion of cells expressing both PKH26 or F4/80 and CFSE or GFP signals simultaneously (CFSE+ PKH26+ or GFP+ F4/80+ or CFSE+ F4/80+). For detailed information on antibodies, please refer to Supplementary Table 3.

RNA immunoprecipitation

After transfecting HGC-27 and AGS cells, we followed the manufacturer’s instructions and used the BeyoRIP™ RIP Assay Kit with Protein A/G Agarose (P1801S, Beyotime Biotech, China) for RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP). In brief, the cells were thoroughly lysed using Lysis Buffer containing 1 mM DTT, 100 U/ml RNase inhibitor, and 0.2 mM PMSF to obtain the cell lysate. Subsequently, the cell lysate was immunoprecipitated with Protein A/G Agarose pre-bound with specific antibodies at 4 °C for 4 h. Next, the RNA-protein complex was eluted and purified. An appropriate amount of RNA was then taken for qRT-PCR analysis (as described above). Finally, the fold enrichment of the target gene relative to the negative control was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. For detailed information on antibodies, please refer to Supplementary Table 3.

RNA stability analysis

Cells were divided into different time groups (2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 8 h, and 10 h) and a control group. An appropriate amount of Actinomycin D solution (A432787, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) was added to achieve a final concentration of 10 ng/mL. The control group received an equal volume of DMSO without Actinomycin D, was gently mixed, and continued to be cultured in the incubator. Total RNA was extracted from each group of cells and subjected to qRT-PCR analysis. The relative expression levels of the target RNA at different time points were then calculated relative to the control group (0 h) using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. By plotting the curve of the relative expression level of the target RNA over time, its stability was analyzed.

Nude mouse experiment

All mouse experiments were conducted in accordance with the approved protocol of the Department of Experimental Zoology at Xiangya Medical College, Central South University. Mice were maintained under standard light-dark cycle conditions with free access to water and food. Mice were euthanized before the tumor exceeded 200 mm in length and width or reached a volume of 2000 mm³, or if they exhibited any signs of distress (e.g., wrinkled fur, significant weight loss, poor physical condition, or poor grooming related to frailty).

Mouse GC MFC cells (1 × 10^6) transfected with EGFP-Ctrl and M-EGFP-CD90 were subcutaneously injected into female BALB/c nude mice at a volume of 100 µL. After tumor formation, mice were subjected to intraperitoneal injection or no injection of the CD47 inhibitor RRx001. Tumor volume was measured on designated dates and calculated as (longest diameter × shortest diameter²) × 0.5. After the experiment, tumors were excised from the mice, washed with pre-cooled PBS, and placed in RPMI-1640 medium (10% FBS) for flow cytometry analysis or frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Bioinformatics analysis was conducted using a combination of web-based bioinformatics tools. The following public databases were searched: the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (Broad GDAC Firehose), the GSCA database (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/GSCA/#/) and GEPIA database (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index).

The grayscale values of protein bands in Western blot experiments were quantified using ImageJ software. Statistical charts were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Student’s t-test was used to evaluate the degree of difference between the means of any two groups of samples, while one-way ANOVA was used to analyze the degree of difference between the means of three or more groups of samples. Each experiment was repeated at least three times. Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05 and is expressed as follows: *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; ****, P < 0.0001.

Results

CD90 mediates immune escape in gastric cancer by inhibiting the function of tumor associated macrophages

We analyzed the expression of CD90 in GC tissue and gastric epithelial tissue using the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset. The results showed that, compared with gastric epithelial tissue, CD90 was highly expressed in GC tissue (Fig. 1A). Given the close correlation between immune cells in the tumor microenvironment and tumor progression, we further analyzed the correlation between CD90 and immune cell infiltration using the GSCA database. The results indicated that CD90 was most significantly correlated with macrophage immune infiltration (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, we conducted immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) experiments on GC tissue, which further confirmed the positive correlation between CD90 and macrophage immune infiltration in the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 1C-D).

To investigate the correlation between CD90 and macrophage phagocytic function, we constructed a model using BALB/c nude mice. Mouse GC MFC cells overexpressing a CD90 plasmid carrying GFP (M-EGFP-CD90-MFC) and their corresponding control group cells (EGFP-Ctrl-MFC) were subcutaneously injected into nude mice. After 6 days, tumors formed, and we began measuring tumor volume. After 12 days, the tumor tissues were removed and their masses were measured. The results showed that, compared with the control group, the CD90 overexpression group had an accelerated tumor growth rate, significantly increased tumor volume, and greater tumor mass. This indicates that CD90 significantly promotes the proliferation of GC cells (Fig. 1E-G) (Figure S1). Next, we prepared a single-cell suspension of the nude mouse tumor tissue and analyzed the percentage of MFC cells (GFP+ F4/80+) engulfed or interacting with TAMs by flow cytometry to assess the phagocytic ability of TAMs. The results showed that the percentage of GFP+ F4/80+ cells in the CD90 overexpression group decreased (Fig. 1H-I). Simultaneously, in order to clarify the effect of CD90 on the phagocytic function of TAMs in vitro, we overexpressed CD90 plasmid (OE-CD90) in mouse GC MFC cells (labeled with CFSE) and co-cultured them with BMDM cells. And then we analyzed the percentage of MFC cells (CFSE+ F4/80+) engulfed or interacting with BMDB by flow cytometry. The results showed that the proportion of CFSE+ F4/80+ cells in the CD90 overexpression group was reduced compared to the control group. The above results indicate that CD90 inhibits the phagocytic function of TAMs (Fig. 1J-K). To further confirm the effect of CD90 on the phagocytic function of TAMs, we overexpressed CD90 plasmid or knockdown of CD90 (shCD90) in human GC HGC-27 and AGS cells (labeled with CFSE) and co-cultured them with PMA-induced THP-1 cells (labeled with PKH26) (Figure S2). Through flow cytometry, we found that the proportion of CFSE+ PKH26+ cells in the CD90 overexpression group was reduced compared to the control group while the proportion in the CD90 knockdown group increased compared to the control group (Fig. 1L-O).

In summary, CD90 mediates GC immune escape by inhibiting the phagocytic function of TAMs.

CD90 inhibits the phagocytic function of tumor associated macrophages through IGF2BP2 and mediates immune escape in gastric cancer

To further investigate the specific mechanism by which CD90 affects the phagocytic function of TAMs and mediates immune escape in GC, we transfected Flag-CD90 into GC HGC-27 cells and performed co-immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (Co-IP-MS). The results showed that CD90 is closely related to m6A molecules, specifically the IGF2BP family, including IGF2BP1, IGF2BP2, and IGF2BP3. Therefore, we overexpressed Flag-CD90 in GC HGC-27 and AGS cells and explored whether CD90 interacts with IGF2BP1, IGF2BP2, and IGF2BP3 through Co-IP experiments. The results indicate that there is an endogenous interaction between CD90 and IGF2BP2 (Fig. 2A). Additionally, we overexpressed IGF2BP2 in GC HGC-27 and AGS cells, and the Co-IP experiment results confirmed the interaction between IGF2BP2 and CD90 (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, we analyzed the co-localization of CD90 and IGF2BP2 proteins in GC cells through IF. The results showed significant co-localization signals between CD90 and IGF2BP2 proteins (Fig. 2C-D). These results confirm that there is an interaction between CD90 and IGF2BP2 in GC HGC-27 and AGS cells.

Since IGF2BP2 is an m6A methylated reading protein, we first used RT-qPCR and Western blot experiments to detect the effect of IGF2BP2 on CD90 expression. The results indicated that IGF2BP2 does not affect the mRNA and protein levels of CD90 (Fig. 2E-F) (Figure S3A-D). Next, we investigated the effect of CD90 on IGF2BP2 expression. The results showed that CD90 did not affect the mRNA level of IGF2BP2 (Fig. 2G) (Figure S3E-G), but upregulated its protein level (Fig. 2H) (Figure S3H). Subsequently, to further confirm the upregulation of IGF2BP2 protein levels by CD90, we overexpressed CD90 in HGC-27 and AGS cells and revealed through IF that CD90 positively regulates IGF2BP2 expression (Fig. 2I).

Based on the above results, we hypothesized that CD90 inhibits the phagocytic function of TAMs by regulating IGF2BP2. To test this hypothesis, we overexpressed CD90 or knocked down IGF2BP2 in HGC-27 and AGS cells (labeled with CFSE) and co-cultured them with PMA-induced THP-1 cells (labeled with PKH26) to evaluate the phagocytic ability of TAMs by flow cytometry. The results showed that compared with the control group, the percentage of CFSE+ PKH26+ cells in the CD90 overexpressing group decreased, while knocking down IGF2BP2 partially reversed the inhibition of CD90 on TAMs’ phagocytic ability (Fig. 3A-D).

CD90 acts as a scaffold protein to inhibit the interaction between IGF2BP2 and TRIM21, stabilizing the expression of IGF2BP2

Through the above experiments, we found that CD90 upregulates the protein level of IGF2BP2 but has no significant effect on its mRNA level. Therefore, we hypothesized that CD90 may regulate the protein level of IGF2BP2 by affecting its stability. To verify this hypothesis, we first knocked down CD90 in HGC-27 and AGS cells and treated them with 50 µM cycloheximide (CHX) for different durations to block protein synthesis. Subsequently, we analyzed the protein degradation rate of IGF2BP2 by Western blot to investigate whether CD90 upregulated IGF2BP2 protein levels by enhancing its stability. The results showed that knocking down CD90 significantly accelerated the degradation rate of IGF2BP2 compared to the control group, confirming that CD90 indeed upregulates the protein level of IGF2BP2 by enhancing its stability (Fig. 3E-F).

The ubiquitin-proteasome system is one of the main pathways for protein degradation in cells. To investigate whether CD90 affects the ubiquitination of IGF2BP2, we conducted ubiquitination experiments. In HGC-27 and AGS cells, we overexpressed exogenous Flag-IGF2BP2 and performed experiments under conditions of overexpression or non-overexpression of HA-Ub and/or CD90. Cell lysates were immunoprecipitated using an anti-Flag antibody, followed by Western blot analysis using an anti-HA antibody. The results showed that in the presence of overexpressed CD90, the ubiquitination level of IGF2BP2 was lower (Fig. 3G). Additionally, we overexpressed exogenous Flag-IGF2BP2 in HGC-27 and AGS cells and performed experiments under conditions of overexpression or non-overexpression of HA-Ub and/or shCD90. The results also showed that knocking down CD90 resulted in higher levels of ubiquitination of IGF2BP2 (Fig. 3H). These results indicate that CD90 can inhibit the ubiquitination of IGF2BP2, thereby affecting its protein stability. To further evaluate the relationship between CD90-mediated IGF2BP2 protein stability and the ubiquitin-proteasome system, we used the 26 S proteasome inhibitor MG132 in GC HGC-27 and AGS cells. The results showed that MG132 treatment promoted the accumulation of IGF2BP2 protein. Meanwhile, in CD90 knockdown cells, the level of IGF2BP2 protein did not further decrease after MG132 treatment, indicating that CD90 enhances the protein stability of IGF2BP2 through the ubiquitin-proteasome system (Fig. 3I).

To further investigate how CD90 affects the ubiquitination of IGF2BP2, we analyzed the Co-IP-MS results. The results suggest a potential interaction between the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21 and CD90 in GC cells. Based on this discovery, we overexpressed Flag-CD90 in GC HGC-27 and AGS cells and conducted Co-IP experiments. The experimental results confirmed the endogenous interaction between CD90 and TRIM21 (Fig. 4A). Additionally, we overexpressed HA-IGF2BP2 in HGC-27 and AGS cells and found that TRIM21 also interacts with IGF2BP2 (Fig. 4B). Subsequently, we investigated the regulatory relationship between TRIM21, IGF2BP2, and CD90 in HGC-27 and AGS cells. After overexpression or knockdown of TRIM21, Western blot results showed that TRIM21 could downregulate the protein level of IGF2BP2 (Fig. 4C-D). However, RT-qPCR results showed that TRIM21 did not affect the mRNA levels of IGF2BP2 (Figure S4A-C). Similarly, after overexpression or knockdown of IGF2BP2, RT-qPCR results showed that IGF2BP2 did not affect the mRNA level of TRIM21 (Figure S4D), and Western blot results also showed that IGF2BP2 did not affect the protein level of TRIM21 (Figure S4E-F). These findings indicate that TRIM21 has no significant regulatory effect on IGF2BP2 at the mRNA or protein level. Additionally, after overexpression of TRIM21, RT-qPCR experiments showed that TRIM21 did not affect the mRNA level of CD90 (Figure S4G), and Western blot results also showed that TRIM21 did not affect the protein level of CD90 (Figure S4H). This suggests that TRIM21 has no significant regulatory effect on CD90. Meanwhile, overexpression or knockdown of CD90 did not affect the mRNA level of TRIM21 according to RT-qPCR results (Figure S4I), and Western blot results also showed that CD90 did not affect the protein level of TRIM21 (Figure S4J-K). These results further confirm that there is no significant regulatory relationship between CD90 and TRIM21 at the mRNA or protein level. However, as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, it is crucial to investigate whether TRIM21 affects the stability of CD90 in order to elucidate the interaction network among CD90, TRIM21, and IGF2BP2. Therefore, we overexpressed exogenous Flag-CD90 in HGC-27 and AGS cells and conducted ubiquitination experiments under conditions with or without overexpression of HA-Ub and/or shTRIM21. The experimental results showed that regardless of changes in the expression level of TRIM21, the ubiquitination level of CD90 did not significantly change, suggesting that TRIM21 does not affect the protein stability of CD90 through ubiquitination modification (Figure S5).

To further confirm the effect of TRIM21 on the degradation rate of IGF2BP2 protein, we knocked down TRIM21 in GC HGC-27 and AGS cells, treated the cells with cycloheximide (CHX), and then analyzed the protein degradation rate of IGF2BP2 by Western blot. This result indicates that TRIM21 reduces the stability of IGF2BP2 protein by promoting its degradation (Fig. 4E-F). Subsequently, to confirm whether TRIM21 affects the degradation of IGF2BP2 protein through ubiquitination, we overexpressed exogenous Flag-IGF2BP2 in HGC-27 and AGS cells and conducted ubiquitination experiments under conditions of overexpression or non-overexpression of HA-Ub and/or shTRIM21. It is known that CD90 can inhibit the ubiquitination of IGF2BP2, and there is an interaction between CD90, TRIM21, and IGF2BP2. Meanwhile, TRIM21 can promote the ubiquitination of IGF2BP2. To verify this hypothesis, we further investigated whether there is a competitive binding relationship between CD90, TRIM21, and IGF2BP2. We overexpressed HA-IGF2BP2 in HGC-27 and AGS cells and collected whole-cell extracts under conditions of overexpression or non-overexpression of Flag-CD90. Subsequently, immunoprecipitation was performed using an anti-HA antibody, and the amount of TRIM21 protein interacting with HA-IGF2BP2 was detected by Western blot. The experimental results showed that overexpression of Flag-CD90 reduced the amount of TRIM21 protein interacting with HA-IGF2BP2 (Fig. 4I). Meanwhile, we overexpressed Flag-CD90 in HGC-27 and AGS cells and collected whole-cell extracts under conditions of overexpression or non-overexpression of HA-IGF2BP2. Subsequently, immunoprecipitation was performed using an anti-Flag antibody, and the amount of TRIM21 protein interacting with Flag-CD90 was detected by Western blot. The experimental results showed that regardless of overexpression of IGF2BP2, there was no significant change in the amount of TRIM21 protein interacting with CD90 (Figure S6). This result indicates that CD90 can competitively bind to IGF2BP2, inhibit the interaction between IGF2BP2 and TRIM21, and thereby affect the degradation of IGF2BP2 protein by TRIM21.

Additionally, to further validate the mechanism by which CD90 stabilizes IGF2BP2 expression through TRIM21, we knocked down CD90 or simultaneously knocked down CD90 and TRIM21 in GC HGC-27 and AGS cells. IF experiment results showed that when CD90 was knocked down, the red fluorescence intensity of IGF2BP2 decreased compared to the control group, indicating a reduction in the expression level of IGF2BP2. However, when both CD90 and TRIM21 were knocked down simultaneously, the inhibitory effect of knocking down CD90 on IGF2BP2 expression was partially restored. This result further confirms that CD90 stabilizes the expression of IGF2BP2 through TRIM21 (Fig. 4J) (Figure S7). In summary, CD90 acts as a scaffold protein to inhibit the interaction between IGF2BP2 and TRIM21, stabilizing the expression of IGF2BP2.

Blocking the interaction between CD47 and SIRPα can enhance the clearance of cancer cells by phagocytic cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs), thereby activating innate immune responses [25, 26]. Discussion

This study reveals for the first time that CD90, as a scaffold protein, can inhibit the interaction between IGF2BP2 and TRIM21, thereby stabilizing the expression level of IGF2BP2. Further research has shown that CD90 regulates the stability of IGF2BP2, affects the m6A-modified CD47/SIRPα axis, and mediates immune escape in GC. Specifically, in GC cells, CD90 positively regulates the expression level of the m6A methylated reading protein IGF2BP2. IGF2BP2 enhances the stability of CD47 mRNA by binding to the m6A-modified region of CD47 mRNA, thereby increasing the expression level of the CD47 protein. The interaction between CD47 and SIRPα further activates the CD47/SIRPα axis, ultimately mediating immune escape in GC by inhibiting the phagocytic function of TAMs.

Increasing evidence indicates that CD90, as a GPI-anchored glycoprotein, serves as an anchor point for monocyte/macrophage adhesion in various cancers. It interacts with integrins from monocytes/macrophages to mediate physical interactions between CD90high cells and these immune cells [4, 30, 31]. These studies suggest that CD90 can promote the immunosuppressive properties of TAMs, thereby facilitating tumor progression. They also hint at a potential strategy to weaken the immunosuppressive properties of TAMs by interfering with the signal transduction between CD90 and integrins. However, the specific relationship between CD90 and the phagocytic function of TAMs remains unclear. Therefore, this study confirms that CD90 can inhibit the phagocytic function of TAMs and significantly promote the proliferation of gastric cancer cells. This finding not only underscores the critical role of CD90 in GC progression but also provides new targets and a theoretical basis for developing immunotherapy strategies targeting CD90 and its associated signaling pathways.

In elucidating the potential mechanism by which CD90 regulates the phagocytic function of gastric cancer associated TAMs, we have confirmed the endogenous interaction between CD90 and IGF2BP2. IGF2BP2 is a newly discovered RNA-binding protein (RBP) with m6A methylation reading function. Its main function is to recognize and bind to the m6A modification site of downstream target gene mRNA, enhancing its stability [32–34]. In recent years, IGF2BP2 has been shown to play an important role in immune escape in various malignant tumors such as GC and colorectal cancer (CRC) through m6A modification [32, 35–41]. Furthermore, studies have shown that IGF2BP2-m6A is also of great significance for the M1 and M2 polarization of macrophages [42, 43]. Based on the above research background, we raise the following question: Does IGF2BP2 play a key role in the inhibition of TAM phagocytosis by CD90? To answer this question, we conducted a series of experiments in GC HGC-27 and AGS cells. The experimental results indicate that CD90 inhibits the phagocytic function of TAMs by upregulating the expression of IGF2BP2, thereby mediating immune escape in GC.

To further investigate the molecular mechanism by which CD90 regulates IGF2BP2, we conducted experiments such as Co-IP combined mass spectrometry, ubiquitination experiments, and immunofluorescence, revealing the interaction between CD90, TRIM21, and IGF2BP2. Moreover, CD90 inhibited the interaction between IGF2BP2 and TRIM21, stabilizing the expression of IGF2BP2. There are two possible molecular mechanisms for the competitive interference of CD90 on the binding of TRIM21 and IGF2BP2: one is the overlap of the binding domain between CD90 and IGF2BP2, and the binding domain between TRIM21 and IGF2BP2 (direct competition). In this case, CD90 and TRIM21 compete for the same binding site of IGF2BP2, and the interaction between CD90-TRIM21 may reflect more of the instantaneous ternary complex or ineffective collision they form when competing for binding with IGF2BP2. At this point, the interaction between CD90-IGF2BP2 has a clearer function, which is to stabilize the IGF2BP2 protein and prevent it from being degraded by TRIM21 mediated ubiquitination. Another possible mechanism is that CD90-TRIM21 binding blocks TRIM21’s recognition of IGF2BP2 (allosteric inhibition). In this case, CD90 acts as a conformational regulator of TRIM21, inducing conformational changes in TRIM21 and causing it to lose its ability to bind to IGF2BP2. At this point, the interaction between CD90-IGF2BP2 may mainly recruit CD90 to the vicinity of IGF2BP2, creating a local high concentration microenvironment for this conformational inhibition, thereby achieving the effect of stabilizing IGF2BP2 protein. However, in the absence of precise domain localization and point mutation functional validation, our current experimental results are insufficient to determine the exact molecular mechanisms involved. In subsequent experiments, constructing IGF2BP2 mutants, peptide competition binding experiments, or using deep learning based complex structure prediction models (such as AlphaFold 3) to further explore the interaction mechanism between CD90-IGF2BP2-TRIM21 are necessary steps to validate the above models and reveal the complete molecular mechanism. Our current discovery —— CD90 functions by interfering with the TRIM21-IGF2BP2 axis —— provides a solid foundation for this important regulatory pathway.

Subsequently, to analyze the specific mechanism by which IGF2BP2 affects the phagocytic function of GC-associated TAMs, we conducted bioinformatics analysis, RIP-qPCR, and other experiments. The results showed that CD90 stabilizes CD47 expression through IGF2BP2-m6A modification, thereby activating the CD47/SIRPα axis and inhibiting the phagocytic function of TAMs. It should be noted that although this study confirmed that CD90 enhances the overall function of IGF2BP2, it has not yet been able to analyze whether membrane-bound IGF2BP2 still possesses m6A reader activity. This constitutes a limitation of the current research and points to a key direction for future research. Distinguishing the molecular activity of IGF2BP2 in the “membrane-anchored” and “cytosolic free” states is crucial for accurately mapping its functional regulatory network. Subsequent work could attempt to isolate cell membrane components for in vitro RNA pull-down experiments to explore whether IGF2BP2 interacting with CD90 on the cell membrane can still exert m6A reader function, and clarify the specific contributions of IGF2BP2 with different localizations in tumor progression.

Previous studies have confirmed that CD47, upon binding to SIRPα, transmits a “don’t eat me” signal to bone marrow cells, thereby helping tumor cells evade phagocytosis by macrophages and evade innate immune responses [44–46]. In addition, the study by Panpan Dai et al. revealed that inhibiting USP2 can induce CD47 protein degradation, thereby enhancing the phagocytic function of macrophages and reshaping the tumor microenvironment - manifested as an increase in M1 macrophages and CD8⁺ T cells, a decrease in M2 macrophages, and a synergistic enhancement of the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy [47]. Inhibition of the CD47/SIRP α axis may also indirectly enhance T cell response through myeloid immune cells, including dendritic cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and NK cells [48]. In our study, CD90 stabilized CD47 expression through IGF2BP2, ultimately inhibiting the phagocytic function of TAMs. However, a question worth exploring in depth is: after CD90 regulates CD47 expression, is its impact on the tumor immune microenvironment limited to tumor associated macrophages? Further exploration of whether and how the CD90/CD47 axis affects the anti-tumor function of other immune cells in the tumor microenvironment, such as CD8⁺ T cells, neutrophils, etc., is of great significance for comprehensively evaluating the potential of CD90 as a therapeutic target and designing reasonable combination immunotherapy plans for it.

However, there are still some limitations to this study. For example, we conducted relevant in vivo experiments in BALB/c Nude mice. Although the BALB/c Nude mouse model is widely used in tumor research, its T cell defects may interfere with the polarization state and function of TAMs through multiple pathways, disrupting their synergistic balance with other immune cells. Given the complex cellular communication network and immune regulatory mechanisms in the tumor microenvironment, the observed macrophage function in this model may differ from the true situation in an immune healthy environment. In order to more rigorously verify the accuracy of the conclusions in this study regarding the regulation of TAMs and tumor cell survival by the CD90-IGF2BP2-CD47 axis, future studies should use animal models with immune function. Specifically, the subcutaneous transplantation model established by injecting gastric cancer MFC cells into 615 mice, as well as the depletion mouse model targeting macrophage specificity, will provide more accurate evidence for the role of CD47 mediated “don’t eat me” signaling in the tumor microenvironment. Therefore, in future research, the use of more advanced animal models will help to more accurately simulate the interactions of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment.

Meanwhile, this study preliminarily revealed the correlation between CD90 and impaired phagocytic function of tumor associated macrophages. However, further validation is needed to determine whether macrophages are key effector cells for CD90 mediated immune escape in gastric cancer. Ideally, this should be confirmed through in vivo macrophage clearance experiments, such as using chlorophosphonic acid liposomes, anti-CSF-1R antibody mediated macrophage clearance, or conducting relevant experiments using the Cx3cr1-DTR mouse model (macrophage specific clearance). If the pro tumorigenic or immunosuppressive effects of CD90 are significantly weakened in the absence of macrophages, CD90 mediated tumor immune dependent macrophages can be established. Unfortunately, due to current experimental conditions and model accessibility, this study was unable to provide direct evidence in this direction. Therefore, elucidating the exact role of macrophages in the CD90 signaling pathway will be a key direction for subsequent mechanism research. Additionally, TAMs in the body are located in a complex tumor microenvironment and are influenced by various cytokines, chemokines, and intercellular interactions. Therefore, in subsequent studies, isolating monocytes from GC patients for in vitro induction and culture to establish TAM models that are closer to the in vivo human environment may be a more feasible strategy. This will help to more accurately study the impact of CD90 on TAM function and its specific mechanism in tumor immune escape.

In summary, this study reveals the key role and molecular mechanism of CD90 in the immune escape of GC. Specifically, CD90 positively regulates the expression level of the m6A methylated reading protein IGF2BP2, enhances the mRNA stability of the immune checkpoint molecule CD47, activates the CD47/SIRPα axis, and ultimately mediates immune escape in GC. This discovery not only elucidates the important mechanism of CD90 in the progression of GC but also provides important experimental evidence for identifying potential therapeutic molecular targets for GC. Future research will further validate these findings and explore the clinical potential of novel immunotherapy strategies based on CD90 and its related signaling pathways (Fig. 6H).
