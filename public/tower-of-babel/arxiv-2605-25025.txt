Micro-Swarm Locomotion Optimization in Dynamic Flow using Multi-Objective Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning
Josef Berman, Oren Gal

Abstract
Coordinating micro-robotic swarms in physiologically realistic, time-dependent fluid environments remains an unsolved challenge for biomedical and environmental applications. We present a hybrid Computational Fluid Dynamics–Multi-Objective Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (CFD-MO-MARL) framework that directly couples a high-fidelity incompressible Navier-Stokes solver with decentralized proximal policy optimization to learn physically consistent swarm control strategies in oscillatory flow. Sixteen magnetically actuated micro-robots navigate a pulsatile arterial waveform (peak systolic velocity 400 mm/s) within a 2 mm channel, simultaneously optimizing upstream progression, energy conservation, and motion smoothness. Competing policy gradients across these objectives are reconciled using Projected Conflicting Gradient (PCGrad) surgery, which projects conflicting gradient components into orthogonal subspaces to prevent destructive inter-objective interference. Without PCGrad, energy efficiency and smoothness rewards collapse to near zero within 10,000 training steps while progress exhibits persistent large-amplitude oscillations, confirming that gradient conflict resolution is a structural requirement rather than an optional refinement in this domain. The converged policy achieves a progress reward of 6.5-7.0, a sustained energy efficiency of 0.63–0.65, and near-maximum smoothness (0.97–0.99), representing improvements exceeding 8 reward units over brute-force baselines on the primary objective while both baselines yield negative energy efficiency throughout. Training reveals three emergent behavioral phases not encoded in the reward function: a collective two-layer hydrodynamic throttling formation that suppresses peak channel velocities from 700 mm/s to approximately 400 mm/s during forward flow, a cycle-synchronized ratchet mechanism that exploits flow reversals for upstream repositioning, and an individualized final approach as agents near the success boundary. These results establish that time-dependent fluid-agent interactions can be captured directly within multi-objective reinforcement learning loops, offering a scalable and physically grounded paradigm for micro-swarm control in biomedical navigation, environmental monitoring, and industrial microfluidics.



I
Introduction

Coordinating the locomotion of micro-robotic swarms in time‐dependent fluid environments remains a pivotal challenge for both biomedical and environmental applications. At microscopic scales, viscous forces dominate inertial effects, such that reciprocal actuation produces no net displacement – a phenomenon known as Purcell’s “Scallop Theorem”
[
44
]
. To achieve propulsion, micro-swimmers must therefore employ non-reciprocal strategies that break time-reversal symmetry. When the ambient flow oscillates, as in pulsatile arterial blood (characterized by Womersley numbers exceeding unity) or pump-induced pressure cycles in pipelines, micro-swimmers are subjected to cyclic shear gradients, flow reversals, and transient boundary layers that can trap them in recirculation zones or force them against confining walls
[
5
,
10
]
. Moreover, hydrodynamic coupling among micro-swimmers produces time-varying wakes and vortices, which may either stabilize or destabilize swarm formations depending on phase relationships and inter-swimmer spacing
[
12
]
. The core problem therefore lies in devising control paradigms that enable a micro-swarm to maintain smoothness of action, conserve energy, and sustain directed progression against or across oscillatory flows.

I-A

Micro-swimmer Propulsion Mechanisms

At the microscale, where viscous forces dominate inertial effects, propulsion must circumvent the constraints of the “scallop theorem”, which prohibits net motion via reciprocal strokes in a Newtonian fluid. Seminal theoretical investigations by Hancock and Purcell demonstrated that non-reciprocal, traveling‐wave undulations of slender filaments generate a net thrust by continuously breaking time-reversal symmetry
[
25
,
44
]
. These insights established the fundamental role of helical and flexural deformation modes in producing locomotion at vanishing Reynolds numbers and underscored the necessity of cyclic shape changes or external field interactions to achieve propulsion.

Based on these principles, a variety of bio-mimetic micro-swimmers have been engineered to emulate the solutions of nature. Magnetic helical swimmers employ rigid ferromagnetic helices actuated by rotating magnetic fields; by tuning field frequency and amplitude, they achieve precise three‐dimensional steering, though deployment often requires bulky coils or permanent magnets proximate to the swimmer
[
43
,
68
]
. In contrast, acoustic streaming motors exploit ultrasonic transducers to establish steady streaming flows around asymmetric micro-structures. These contactless actuators offer high actuation rates and localized control but can generate undesirable heating and cavitation effects in surrounding fluids
[
11
]
.

A second class of autonomous swimmers leverages chemical energy: Janus particles with asymmetric surface coatings catalyze reactions (e.g., peroxide decomposition) to produce self-generated concentration gradients, driving phoretic motion without external fields. While inherently autonomous, these swimmers contend with limited directional persistence and fuel depletion over time
[
62
]
. Similarly, photophoretic micro-swimmers absorb focused light to create localized thermal gradients, which induce thermophoretic forces; these approaches enable remote, spatially resolved actuation but suffer from shallow penetration depths in optically dense media and potential photothermal damage
[
34
]
.

Despite the rich diversity of propulsion strategies, the vast majority of studies have characterized swimmer performance in quiescent or steadily driven flows. The behavior of these microscale propellers under oscillatory or pulsatile forcings, ubiquitous in biological environments such as blood vessels and respiratory airways, remains largely unexplored. Understanding how unsteady background flows interact with swimmer kinematics is critical for translating micro-swimmer technology into practical biomedical and environmental applications.

I-B

Swarm Coordination in Steady Flows

Swarm robotic systems draw inspiration from collective behaviors in biological populations, such as fish schooling and bacterial clustering, where simple, local interaction rules give rise to resilient, emergent patterns
[
71
]
. In microfluidic Poiseuille flows, magnetic micro-robotic swarms exploit rotating or oscillating external fields to trigger self-assembly into one-dimensional chains or compact clusters. By synchronizing field frequency and orientation with the background flow speed, these swarms can navigate upstream against the pressure-driven profile or drift downstream in a controlled manner
[
13
,
33
]
.

At the heart of these maneuvers lies the delicate balance between magnetic dipole interactions, which draw neighboring particles into aligned formations, and viscous drag forces that tend to disperse them and oppose motion. Researchers have demonstrated that by tuning the field strength and rotation rate, one can modulate the dipole-dipole attraction to overcome Brownian dispersion, preserving coherent formations over distances comparable to channel lengths. Such field-modulated navigation strategies have been validated in straight micro-channels and simple bifurcating networks, enabling swarms to reverse direction or traverse junctions without distributed sensing or inter-agent communication.

Despite these successes, existing coordination methods rest on the assumption of a time-invariant flow field. In many real-world settings, blood vessels, respiratory pathways, and environmental conduits, pressure and velocity profiles oscillate or pulsate. The influence of such unsteady flow conditions on swarm coherence, steering accuracy, and collective stability remains largely uncharacterized, presenting a critical gap in the translation of micro-robotic swarms to dynamic, physiological, and industrial environments.

I-C

Challenges in Oscillatory Fluid Environments

Pulsatile flow in blood vessels features phase-lagged velocity profiles and evolving boundary layers that fluctuate throughout the cardiac cycle, creating time-varying shear stresses on suspended particles
[
32
,
10
]
. Similarly, engineered pipelines subject to cyclic pumping generate oscillatory currents that impose rapidly changing pressure gradients and recirculation zones
[
53
,
5
]
. Under these conditions, micro-swarms may experience trapping in transient vortices during deceleration phases or be driven into vessel walls when flow direction reverses. The unsteady shear environment also modulates lift forces on individual agents, potentially disrupting previously stable formations. Crucially, the spatiotemporal interplay of wakes shed by neighboring swimmers can either reinforce collective motion or lead to intermittent clustering and dispersion, depending on the synchronization of their actuation cycles
[
12
]
.

Reinforcement learning (RL) frames control as a Markov decision process where agents learn policies by maximizing cumulative rewards through interaction

Model-free algorithms like PPO, SAC, and TRPO bypass explicit dynamics modeling, directly optimizing policies through sampled interactions

Multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) balances competing goals by treating rewards as vectors rather than scalars, seeking Pareto-optimal policies

I-G
2
Gaps in CFD-MO-MARL

No prior work has integrated multi-objective multi-agent RL with high-fidelity, time-dependent CFD for swarm locomotion in oscillatory flows. Existing CFD-RL studies address single-objective tasks with single agents or homogeneous swarms sharing scalar rewards
[
66
]
. Multi-objective formulations in CFD use offline optimization (evolutionary algorithms, Bayesian optimization) rather than online RL
[
41
]
. Pulsatile flows in physiological systems and industrial applications remain unexplored for micro-robotic swarms, despite being ubiquitous
[
31
]
. The challenge involves multi-scale temporal dynamics where agents must exploit flow phases, synchronize with neighbors, and maintain formation over millisecond-to-second timescales while balancing competing objectives decentrally.

This work addresses this gap by coupling a Navier-Stokes solver with decentralized multi-objective policies learned via PPO and reconciled through PCGrad. We demonstrate that time-dependent fluid-agent interactions can be captured directly within RL loops, enabling physically grounded swarm control balancing upstream progression, energy conservation, and motion smoothness. Each micro-robot optimizes three concurrent objectives whose competing gradients are reconciled using PCGrad to prevent destructive interference and accelerate convergence toward Pareto-optimal policies. This establishes a new paradigm for learning-based micro-swarm control in dynamic fluid environments with applications in biomedical navigation, environmental monitoring, and industrial microfluidics (Fig.
1
).

II-A

Physical Setup

In this study, all computational fluid‐dynamics simulations were carried out using the open‐source PhiFlow framework
[
28
]

PhiFlow’s built‐in incompressible Navier–Stokes solver combines a semi‐Lagrangian advection scheme with a projection‐based pressure correction to enforce mass conservation on a uniform Cartesian grid. The flow domain was discretized at a fixed resolution, and time integration was performed using an explicit, second‐order scheme with a constant time step chosen to ensure numerical stability. No‐slip boundary conditions were imposed on solid surfaces, while zero-gradient boundaries were applied in the transverse directions. Fluid properties, including dynamic viscosity and density, were specified to match the physical system, and velocity and pressure fields were recorded at regular intervals for downstream analysis.

Numerical Discretization and Solver Pipeline

The domain is discretized on a uniform Cartesian grid, with spatial steps

II-E

Control Problem and Optimization Framework

To optimize micro-robotic locomotion in time‐dependent flow, we cast the task as a multi‐agent multi-objective Markov Decision Process (MA-MOMDP) and employ model‐free deep reinforcement learning (PPO) with a Centralized Training, Decentralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm. The following summarizes the scientific conceptualization and technical specifications of this framework.

II-E
1
MDP Specification and Transition Dynamics

The state space
S

(x,y)

s\in S

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

A policy-gradient algorithm aimed to balance sample efficiency in observation space with policy update stability by limiting the magnitude of policy updates. At each iteration, PPO collects a batch of trajectories by rolling out the current policy and estimates the Generalized Advantage Estimation

6
Gradient Conflict Resolution using PCGrad algorithm

To reconcile the competing objectives of upstream locomotion, energy conservation efficiency, and motion smoothness, we employ Projected Conflicting Gradient (PCGrad) – a gradient-surgery technique originally developed for multi-task learning
[
74
]
. At each PPO update step, we compute the policy-gradient estimate
\mathbf{g}_{i}
for each objective
i
. PCGrad algorithm detects gradient conflicts by evaluating the dot product between each pair of gradients. If the dot product is negative (indicating that objectives would push the policy in opposing directions), the algorithm projects one gradient onto the normal plane of the other before aggregation. Formally, for each pair of objectives
(i,j)
the modified gradients will be orthogonal (Eq. (
15
)).

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{i}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{g}_{i}-\dfrac{\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{g}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{j}\|^{2}}\mathbf{g}_{j},&\text{if }\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{g}_{j}<0\\[10.00002pt]
\mathbf{g}_{i},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(15a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{\text{PCGrad}}=\sum_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{i}

(15b)

This procedure preserves complementary gradient components while removing destructive interference, thereby preventing one objective’s update from degrading another. By integrating PCGrad into our multi-agent PPO framework, we achieve stable learning of decentralized policies that balance locomotion efficiency, energy efficiency, and smoothness.

III
Results

III-A

Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning Convergence and Policy Stabilization

Fig.
6
presents the per-episode learning curves for the three reward objectives – progress, energy efficiency, and smoothness – normalized by episode length, across approximately 110,000 global environment steps. All three objectives are evaluated against two fixed brute-force baselines: a strategy that applies maximal thrust directly upstream, and one that drives the swarm members toward the channel walls while moving upstream.

The progress reward, measuring the swarm’s ability to navigate against the fluid flow, exhibits the clearest learning dynamic. From an initial value near zero – comparable to the brute-force baselines, which remain stagnant at approximately -2.0 and -2.5 throughout training – the learned policy improves rapidly between approximately 5,000 and 25,000 global steps, converging to a sustained mean of roughly 6.5-7.0. This represents an improvement of more than 8 reward units over either baseline, demonstrating that the learned policy acquires non-trivial flow exploitation strategies that fixed control schemes cannot achieve. A notable period of elevated inter-environment variance is observed between approximately 30,000 and 70,000 steps, reflected in the widened confidence band, suggesting that the policy undergoes iterative refinement and temporary destabilization as it explores higher-reward behavioral modes before restabilizing. By approximately 75,000 steps, the mean progress reward recovers and the variance narrows, indicating policy consolidation.

The energy efficiency reward exhibits a smoother convergence profile. Starting at approximately 0.30 in early training, it rises monotonically to a plateau near 0.63-0.65 by 20,000 steps and remains broadly stable for the remainder of training, with only moderate variance. Critically, both brute-force baselines yield negative energy efficiency (approximately -0.15 and -0.27, respectively), indicating that fixed-force strategies expend thrust in directions misaligned with actual displacement – a consequence of fighting the flow rather than exploiting it. The learned policy, by contrast, consistently achieves positive energy efficiency, confirming that it develops force application patterns that are directionally coherent with member motion.

The smoothness reward displays the fastest convergence among all three objectives. After an initial transient near 0.87 in the first few thousand steps, the learned policy rapidly stabilizes in the range of 0.97-0.99 by approximately 5,000 global steps and sustains this level for the remainder of training with very low variance. Notably, both brute-force baselines achieve a smoothness of 1.00, the theoretical maximum, as their fixed action directions produce perfectly consistent, zero-variance action sequences by construction. The learned policy falls marginally short of this ceiling, reflecting the inherent cost of adaptive, state-dependent control: a policy that modulates its actions in response to local flow conditions will necessarily incur some degree of temporal variation that a static rule does not. The gap between the learned policy and the brute-force baselines on this objective is nonetheless small in absolute terms (below 0.03 across the entire stable training regime), and must be interpreted in the context of the far larger improvements the learned policy achieves on the progress and energy efficiency objectives, where both baselines fail severely. This trade-off confirms that the MOMAPPO agent learns to balance the competing demands of navigational effectiveness and action consistency, accepting a modest smoothness cost in exchange for the substantially superior upstream progress and energy performance that define the primary task.

Taken together, the convergence profiles across all three objectives demonstrate that the MOMAPPO framework successfully learns a Pareto-improving policy relative to both brute-force baselines simultaneously – without sacrificing any individual objective – and that the learned policy generalizes stably across parallel environment instances, as evidenced by the narrow confidence bands in the steady-state regime.

III-B
1
Phase 1: Collective Flow Throttling During Peak Forward Flow

The most striking and counterintuitive emergent behavior is the formation of a deliberate two-layer hydrodynamic obstruction during the peak forward-flow phase of the triphasic waveform. Under the untrained policy, all swarm members cluster against the upper wall (
y\approx 2\;mm
). Rather than sheltering the members from the bulk flow, this configuration creates a severe constriction between the swarm and the opposite wall that locally accelerates the fluid to velocities exceeding
700\;mm/s
. The resulting drag force is sufficient to drive the entire swarm past the episode-failure boundary at
x=80\;mm
within a few time steps (Fig.
7
, panels 1–4).

The trained policy resolves this self-defeating behavior through a qualitatively different spatial arrangement. Approximately half of the swarm members anchor against the lower wall (
y\approx 0\;mm
) while the remaining members position themselves directly above, forming a layered obstruction that spans a larger fraction of the channel cross-section. By doing so, the swarm acts as a distributed baffle that throttles the flow and suppresses the formation of high-velocity jets. Peak fluid velocities in the swarm vicinity are reduced to approximately
400\;mm/s
– nearly half the values observed in the untrained case – and the swarm is prevented from being advected past the 80 mm mark.

Crucially, the energy-efficiency component of the reward incentivizes the swarm to drift passively with the residual forward flow rather than expend actuation force resisting it; the members therefore translate downstream during this phase, but do so in a controlled manner that preserves the structural integrity of the two-layer formation and keeps the center of mass well within the safe operating corridor.

III-B
2
Phase 2: Coordinated Dispersion and Upstream Repositioning During Reverse Flow

When the triphasic waveform transitions into its reverse-flow phase, the swarm executes a rapid reorganization. Members detach from the two-layer formation, spread laterally across the channel, and re-anchor preferentially near the lower wall while simultaneously advancing upstream. The timing of this dispersion is significant: by redistributing along the lower wall before the subsequent forward-flow phase resumes, the swarm preemptively adopts a configuration that limits the downstream displacement that the next forward pulse can impose. This anticipatory repositioning functions as a flow-cycle-aware ratchet – the swarm advances upstream during each reverse phase and arrests its downstream drift during each forward phase – progressively displacing the center of mass toward the
x=20\;mm
success boundary across successive cardiac cycles.

The behavior also reflects an implicit trade-off between the progress and smoothness objectives. During the reverse-flow phase, members adopt strong upstream action vectors, accepting a temporary reduction in action smoothness in exchange for substantial upstream displacement. During the forward phase, actions become smoother and more uniform as members settle into the two-layer throttling formation, recovering the smoothness reward at the cost of minimal forward progress expenditure.

III-B
3
Phase 3: Individualized Final Approach

As the center of mass nears the
x=20\;mm
success threshold, the tight inter-member coordination that characterized the earlier phases gives way to a more individualized navigation strategy. Members spread across the channel with little apparent collective structure, each independently advancing upstream without the synchronized two-layer formation or the cycle-timed dispersion bursts observed in phases 1 and 2. This dissolution of collective behavior likely reflects the fact that, by this stage of the episode, members are already well-separated along both x and y from the repositioning maneuvers of Phase 2, so the geometric prerequisites for assembling a coherent flow-throttling formation are no longer met within the timescale of a single flow cycle. With the swarm dispersed and each member already anchored near the lower wall, the dominant remaining task is simply to sustain upstream displacement through successive reverse-flow pulses – a goal that each member can pursue independently. The progress reward is sufficient to drive individual members to the success boundary without requiring the inter-agent spatial coordination that was essential when the swarm was concentrated in mid-channel and vulnerable to being swept downstream as a unit.

III-C

Influence of Gradient Surgery on Learned Policy

To assess the contribution of PCGrad to training stability and multi-objective performance, we compare the full MOMAPPO policy against an ablated variant in which the standard summed-gradient update replaces the PCGrad merging step, leaving all other hyperparameters and architectural choices identical. The results are presented in Figure
9
.

The contrast between the two conditions is most pronounced in the energy efficiency and smoothness objectives, where the effect of conflicting gradients manifests with particular severity. Without PCGrad, the energy efficiency reward rises transiently during the first few thousand steps, reaching values comparable to the PCGrad variant, before collapsing abruptly to near zero by approximately 10,000 steps, where it remains flat for the remainder of the observed training window. The PCGrad variant, by contrast, sustains a stable plateau near 0.60–0.65 throughout training. The smoothness reward exhibits an even more dramatic collapse: after an early peak near 0.85, the without-PCGrad policy rapidly degrades to approximately 0.00 by 10,000 steps, effectively losing all temporal consistency in its action sequences. The PCGrad policy converges to near-maximum smoothness (
\sim
0.97–1.00) within the same window and maintains it stably. These collapses are consistent with the known failure mode of naive gradient summation under conflicting objectives: the gradient of the progress objective dominates and actively interferes with the energy and smoothness objectives, erasing the learning signal for both.

The progress reward tells a complementary story. While the without-PCGrad policy does not flatly fail on this objective, it occasionally achieves positive and even high instantaneous rewards, it exhibits severe and persistent oscillations throughout the observed training period, with swings spanning the range of approximately -15 to +10. The high inter-environment variance reflects policy instability rather than productive exploration. The PCGrad variant, by contrast, exhibits a clean monotonic rise from near zero to a stable mean of approximately 6.5-7.0 with progressively narrowing variance, converging to a well-defined behavioral mode. The absence of stable progress in the without-PCGrad condition is therefore attributable not to an inability to discover rewarding behaviors, but to the inability to consolidate them in the presence of destructive gradient interference.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that PCGrad is a critical component of the MOMAPPO framework as applied to this domain. The energy efficiency and smoothness objectives, which carry smaller gradient magnitudes than the progress objective, are effectively sacrificed when gradients are summed naively, reducing the multi-objective problem to a de facto single-objective one with degraded and unstable optimization dynamics. Gradient surgery preserves the learning signal for all three objectives simultaneously, enabling the stable and Pareto-improving convergence reported in the previous section. The full characterization of the without-PCGrad condition over the complete training horizon will be reported in the final version of this work.

IV
Discussion

This work demonstrates that coupling a high-fidelity Navier-Stokes solver with a multi-objective multi-agent reinforcement learning framework is both computationally tractable and scientifically productive for micro-swarm locomotion in physiologically realistic flow conditions. The results establish three principal contributions: a training paradigm that achieves stable Pareto-improving convergence across competing objectives, a repertoire of non-obvious emergent collective behaviors arising from the interplay of fluid physics and decentralized reward signals, and a quantitative demonstration that gradient conflict resolution is functionally necessary in this domain.

The most consequential finding is not the convergence of the reward curves per se, but what the converged policy actually does. Rather than applying maximum thrust upstream – the strategy both brute-force baselines approximate, and which fails catastrophically – the trained swarm discovers a multi-phase ratchet mechanism in which the forward-flow phase is exploited for passive structural anchoring while the reverse-flow phase drives active upstream repositioning. This strategy was never seeded by the reward function, which encodes only scalar objectives for progress, energy alignment, and temporal smoothness without any knowledge of the triphasic waveform structure.
Its emergence reflects genuine policy discovery driven by the physical consistency of the coupled CFD environment – a result inaccessible to frameworks using idealized or surrogate flow models. The two-layer throttling formation of Phase 1 is particularly notable: by spanning a larger fraction of the channel cross-section, the swarm collectively reduces peak local fluid velocity from approximately
700\;mm/s
to
400\;mm/s
, implicitly exploiting the energy efficiency objective to favor passive flow manipulation over active resistance. The subsequent transition from tight collective coordination in Phases 1 and 2 to individualized action in Phase 3 reflects a rational mode-switch – collective formation yields diminishing returns once members are sufficiently dispersed near the success boundary, and the progress objective alone is sufficient to drive individual completion.

The ablation study provides the most practically significant finding of this work. Without PCGrad, energy efficiency and smoothness collapse to near zero within 10,000 training steps while progress exhibits persistent oscillations spanning roughly -15 to +10. The progress gradient, carrying the largest magnitude, dominates and overwrites the learning signal for both secondary objectives, reducing the MO-MARL problem to an unstable single-objective one. That this collapse occurs before the policy has stabilized on any objective indicates it is a structural feature of the problem geometry rather than a transient initialization artifact. The without-PCGrad condition does not fail entirely on progress, it occasionally discovers high-reward behaviors, but cannot consolidate them in the presence of destructive inter-objective interference. Gradient surgery should therefore be treated as a first-class algorithmic requirement in any CFD-MO-MARL system where objectives carry heterogeneous gradient magnitudes.

Limitations and future directions: The two-dimensional setup precludes out-of-plane dynamics and full three-dimensional wake structure; extension to 3D simulation with GPU-accelerated solvers
[
30
]
is a natural next step. The uniform magnetic gradient assumption will require spatial resolution for translational relevance, and generalization to variable swarm sizes would benefit from graph neural network policy parameterizations
[
29
]
. Validation under pathological flow profiles – stenotic jets, irregular pulsatility, or elevated viscosity remains necessary before clinical application. More broadly, the demonstration that time-dependent fluid-agent interactions can be embedded directly within multi-objective RL loops, without surrogate modeling, establishes a methodological template for any control domain governed by PDE dynamics, with direct applicability to environmental monitoring and industrial microfluidics as differentiable physics solvers continue to mature
[
27
,
30
]
.
