A Pilot and Feasibility Study of a Four‐Stage Neurological Staging Framework in Pediatric Tracheal Extubation During Anesthesia Emergence
Authors: Megumi Okuyama, Katsuhiko Ishibashi, Shiori Nakao, Tomoharu Ukiya, Kohei Takahashi, Taiki Kojima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPostoperative tracheal extubation is a particularly high‐risk period in pediatric anesthesia. Introduction

Postoperative tracheal extubation remains a high‐risk period in pediatric anesthesia [1, 2]. Despite its clinical importance, robust evidence‐based criteria for determining the optimal timing of tracheal extubation remain limited, and clinical practice often relies on the individual experience and judgment of clinicians. Emergence from anesthesia is a complex process that follows a neuroanatomical sequence, progressing from the medulla to the pons, midbrain, and, eventually, the cerebral cortex [3, 4, 5]. Brainstem structures mediate primitive airway reflexes, while higher cortical centers modulate and inhibit exaggerated responses [6, 7]. This layered recovery process has made it particularly challenging to establish reliable criteria based solely on select physiological indicators or monitoring metrics. This hierarchical recovery, mapped to specific clinical signs in our neurological four‐stage classification (Figure 1), suggests that during intermediate stages—when brainstem reflexes have returned, but cortical control remains incomplete—patients may pass through a “vulnerable window” that is prone to respiratory adverse events (RAEs), such as laryngospasm. Therefore, we recognize the need to explore the underlying physiological mechanisms and conceptual frameworks that define a “safe state” for extubation. We propose that this physiological interpretive model is applicable to all pediatric patients, regardless of the clinical setting, providing a foundational basis for extubation strategies and offering significant educational value for clinicians.

Neuroanatomical framework and the four‐stage emergence classification. The schema summarizes the brainstem regions (medulla, pons, and midbrain) and the cerebrum, along with their corresponding clinical signs. (Upper) Emergence is categorized into four stages based on the sequential recovery of cranial nerve activity, progressing from the medulla toward the cerebral cortex: Stage 1 (Medulla): Regular spontaneous breathing, movement excluding cough, or swallowing. Stage 2 (Pons): Tearing or facial grimacing. Stage 3 (Midbrain): Conjugate gaze or eye‐opening. Stage 4 (Cerebrum): Purposeful movement. (Lower) Relationship between airway protective reflexes and the central nervous system (CNS) control. Stages 2 and 3 represent a hypothesized “vulnerable window” in which airway reflexes (e.g., laryngeal closure and coughing) are partially recovered and often exaggerated, while cortical CNS control remains limited. Based on this conceptual model, this period is hypothesized to pose a higher potential risk for tracheal extubation than the cortical stage (Stage 4), a trend that requires further definitive validation in larger registry studies.

A previous single‐center observational study reported that the presence of specific clinical signs—such as facial grimacing, eye opening, purposeful movement, and conjugate gaze—is associated with successful awake tracheal extubation in children emerging from volatile anesthesia [3]. However, these findings are context‐specific and their generalizability to diverse settings, such as deep extubation or total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA), remains uncertain. To develop a more objective and anatomically grounded assessment, we integrated these clinical signs into a hierarchical neurological four‐stage classification based on the neurological framework proposed by Reshef et al. [4] This classification system maps specific cranial nerve signs to the neuroanatomical sequence of emergence, progressing from the medulla (Stage 1) to the cerebral cortex (Stage 4).

This study was conducted as a preliminary pilot investigation for evaluating the procedural feasibility of this staging framework. Our primary objectives were to: (1) assess the feasibility of the protocol based on the predefined success criteria, including data completeness, resource demand, and operational challenges; (2) identify potential areas for optimization to enhance recruitment efficiency and data reliability for the subsequent multicenter Extubation in Pediatric Anesthesia (ExPEDIA) registry; and (3) provide foundational descriptive incidence data of RAEs across the different emergence stages. We hypothesized that the specific combination and neuroanatomical sequence of these signs—rather than a simple numerical count—would more accurately reflect airway stability and identify the transition from “vulnerable” to “stable” emergence. Ultimately, this pilot study serves as a critical step toward the goal of the ExPEDIA study, seeking to establish a potential universal framework that enhances safety for all pediatric patients and supports clinicians dedicated to pediatric anesthesia care.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Methods

This prospective, single‐center, observational cohort study was conducted at Chiba University Hospital from July 2024 to April 2025. After obtaining institutional review board approval and written informed consent, we enrolled children younger than 7 years undergoing general anesthesia with tracheal extubation. We limited our study population to patients under 7 years of age, consistent with the previous study by Templeton et al. [3]. As a pilot study, we focused on this specific age group to limit the sample and maintain cohort homogeneity. Patients with tracheostomies, those requiring postoperative mechanical ventilation, or those whose guardians refused to provide informed consent were excluded.

Clinical Variables and Data Collection

Data on patient characteristics, surgical parameters, anesthesia and airway management, signs of emergence from anesthesia, and RAEs were gathered by attending anesthesiologists through standardized research reporting forms. Anesthesiologists performed tracheal extubation using their preferred strategy and recorded clinical signs indicative of emergence. Clinical recovery signs observed before extubation were also documented. These signs—predetermined based on previous studies [3, 7]—included spontaneous regular breathing, tidal volume of at least 5 mL/kg, movement other than coughing, swallowing, tearing, facial grimacing, gaze, eye opening, and purposeful movement.

Additionally, clinical variables were identified based on the literature [1, 2, 7] and consensus among the research team: (1) airway hypersensitivity, defined as active or recent (within 14 days) upper respiratory infection symptoms, asthma, or exposure to household tobacco smoke; (2) difficult airway features, including predictors of a difficult airway, difficult mask ventilation during anesthesia induction, or more than three attempts at tracheal intubation; and (3) birth abnormalities, specifically preterm birth or low birth weight. Additional recorded variables included age, premedication, operating time, anesthesia maintenance, end‐tidal CO2, and end‐tidal sevoflurane concentration immediately before tracheal extubation.

Feasibility Outcomes and Success Criteria

The primary outcome of this study was to assess the feasibility of the outlined protocol. In accordance with the CONSORT extension for pilot and feasibility trials, we identified the following feasibility outcomes: (1) rates of recruitment, approach, and consent; (2) data capture and completion rate; (3) missingness of key variables; (4) capacity to assign stages; and (5) protocol burden.

To evaluate the feasibility of this approach, we established success criteria: (1) recruitment, approach, and consent rate ≥ 90%; (2) data capture and completion rate ≥ 95%; (3) missingness of key variables ≤ 5%. Reasons for non‐participation, such as ineligibility or missed eligible cases due to clinical workload, were documented. The progression of participants from screening through final analysis was monitored to guide recruitment strategies for the future multicenter ExPEDIA registry.

The Outcomes of Interest

The outcomes of interest included the incidence of any RAEs from the moment of tracheal extubation until the patient's discharge from the operating room. To maintain consistency with established pediatric literature [1, 2], the composite outcome of interest for RAEs was defined as the occurrence of one or more of the following: sustained desaturation (SpO2 < 93% for ≥ 30 s), persistent cough (lasting ≥ 10 s), upper airway obstruction (stridor or paradoxical chest movement requiring airway maneuvers), laryngospasm (complete glottic closure requiring positive pressure ventilation or administration of muscle relaxants), and bronchospasm (expiratory wheezing accompanied by increased airway pressure). “Any desaturation” (SpO2 < 95%) was evaluated as a separate variable.

Statistical Analysis

The study aimed to recruit 300 patients in 12 months but was terminated early in April 2025 due to a smaller‐than‐expected population and sufficient interim data to refine the protocol for the upcoming multicenter registry.

For summary statistics, continuous variables are reported as means and standard deviations (SDs), or medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Given the pilot nature of this study and the limited number of observed RAEs, our analysis focused on descriptive and exploratory measures to avoid over‐interpretation. For each of the four emergence stages, we calculated the number of patients, the incidence of RAEs (risk %), and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The risk and 95% CIs were calculated using the exact (Clopper–Pearson) method.

To investigate our physiological hypothesis regarding the “vulnerable window,” we calculated relative risks (RRs) with 95% CIs to assess the association between individual clinical signs and RAEs. Due to the exploratory nature of these analyses and the potential for type I error inflation due to multiple comparisons, p‐values were not emphasized; instead, all estimates were interpreted as foundational data for future study planning. Relative risks and relative differences (RDs) with 95% CIs were computed using the Wald method. For clinical signs with zero events in one group (e.g., tearing), the Haldane–Anscombe correction was applied by adding 0.5 to each cell count, enabling calculation of RRs and 95% CIs. All statistical analyses were conducted with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Participant Flow and Feasibility Outcomes

A participant flow diagram is summarized in Figure 2. Among 174 patients screened for eligibility, 167 satisfied the inclusion criteria. Of these, 13 eligible individuals were not approached for consent due to clinical workload and administrative issues, including delays in communication with the research team during peak hours, while one patient declined participation. Thus, the guardians of 153 patients consented to the study (approach/consent rate: 91.6% [153/167]). Data worksheets were completed for 89 of the 153 consenting participants, yielding a data capture rate of 58.2% (89/153) within routine clinical operations.

Participant flow diagram. The recruitment and exclusion processes for study participants are illustrated.

Data capture failures (n = 64) arose primarily from worksheet omission during periods of high clinical activity by attending anesthesiologists (n = 42), selection of a supraglottic airway device (SGA) during anesthesia planning (n = 21), and alteration of postoperative airway management requiring sedation and ventilation by the surgical team (n = 1). Excluding these 22 protocol‐ineligible cases (SGA and postoperative ventilation), the data capture rate for eligible cases managed with endotracheal intubation was 67.9% (89/131). Considering pre‐operative exclusions as successful protocol tracking, the worksheet completion rate reached 73.2% (112/153). Ultimately, 89 patients were included in the final analysis.

All 89 patients were successfully classified into emergency stages, with no missing data for the clinical parameters documented in the analyzed worksheets (Table S1).

Discussion

In this pilot and feasibility study, we assessed a four‐stage neuroanatomical framework for pediatric anesthesia emergence to determine its procedural feasibility, while identifying critical clinical workflow barriers and necessary protocol modifications to maximize data capture rates and minimize missing values. The emergence‐stage classification was feasible and effectively implemented across all participants included in the final analysis, yielding 100% data completeness for essential variables on the analyzed worksheets. The overall data capture rate was 58.2% (89/153), reflecting real‐world challenges in data collection. Notably, 21 patients managed with a supraglottic airway rather than an endotracheal tube, along with one patient whose postoperative airway protocol was altered, were considered per‐protocol exclusions rather than data capture failures. Adjusting for these 22 cases, the worksheet processing rate increased to 73.2% (112/153). Despite this adjusted improvement, 42 of 154 consenting patients (27.3%) were excluded solely due to data‐collection failures, a higher‐than‐expected attrition rate concentrated in the initial study phase and during staff turnover. These omissions may not have occurred at random, raising a risk of selection bias, as cases that were more difficult or clinically complex around extubation may have been less likely to be captured. These missing data delineate the operational limits of the current protocol and inform data collection strategies for the upcoming multicenter registry.

This study has certain limitations that warrant consideration. First, as a single‐center pilot study with a modest sample size, our data capture rate did not meet the predefined target, and the associated risk of selection bias represents a major barrier to real‐time manual data capture in this setting. Second, some items in our staging framework required direct, synchronous observation, making it challenging for clinicians to recall and record them accurately after conclusion of the case. Furthermore, as an investigator‐developed tool, the inter‐rater reliability and formal validation of this staging framework have not yet been evaluated, which introduces a potential risk of misclassification and interpretation bias. For instance, while conjugate gaze was theoretically categorized as a Stage 3 sign in our initial framework, it was observed exclusively in Stage 4 and was documented in only 9.0% of total cases. This discrepancy likely reflects a practical reporting bias rather than a true physiological absence. Consequently, it was challenging to determine whether the sign was genuinely missing or simply unverified. Third, the current analysis lacked multivariable adjustment for key confounders—such as difficult airway features, airway sensitivities, and anesthetic techniques—that might have influenced risk across stages. Finally, the composite respiratory adverse event (RAE) outcome encompasses a wide range of clinical severity and distinct pathophysiologies; hence, arousal stage influences may vary significantly by specific event type and severity.

To address these challenges in the upcoming multicenter ExPEDIA registry, protocol adjustments have been implemented based on these feasibility findings. To minimize selection bias and improve generalizability, the data collection tool will prioritize essential signs requiring real‐time observation and will be supplemented by electronic anesthesia records and physiological data logs where possible. An “unconfirmed” (not assessed) category will be added to address the gaze‐reporting issue noted above. The registry will also leverage the data validation and site‐network infrastructure of the JPEDIA registry, which has achieved a data capture rate exceeding 95% [8], including specific research leaders responsible for data integrity, a REDCap‐based system that restricts submission of incomplete records, and a pre‐study run‐in period to build staff proficiency before enrollment. This multifaceted strategy is anticipated to comprehensively eliminate missing data and enhance overall data accuracy. Although no cases of laryngospasm were observed in this pilot cohort, a 2.0% incidence rate was adopted based on previous literature,1 yielding an estimated requirement of approximately 8500 cases for the ExPEDIA registry.

In conclusion, while stage assignment was possible among captured cases, the predefined data capture targets were not met due to clinical workload constraints. The primary value of this pilot study was the identification of key feasibility and workflow barriers, which establish a foundation for refining protocols and data capture strategies in the upcoming multicenter ExPEDIA registry.
