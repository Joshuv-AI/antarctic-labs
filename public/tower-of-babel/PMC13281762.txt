A Comparison of Tegileridine Fumarate versus Sufentanil on the Quality of Postoperative Recovery After Laparoscopic Sleeve Gastrectomy: A Preliminary Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Qiqian Zheng, Yue Chen, Fengjuan Sui, Yuhan Fu, Yong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveLaparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) is associated with substantial postoperative pain and a high rate of postoperative nausea and vomiting. Tegileridine fumarate, a biased µ-opioid receptor agonist, may offer effective analgesia with fewer adverse effects than conventional opioids. This study compared the impact of tegileridine versus sufentanil analgesia on postoperative recovery quality in obese patients.MethodsSeventy-two patients were randomly assigned to either the tegileridine group (group T, n = 36) or the sufentanil group (group S, n = 36). The primary outcome was the Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) score at 24 h postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included: the visual analogue scale (VAS), time to first rescue analgesia, rescue analgesia and antiemetic use, cumulative opioid consumption, incidence of adverse events, and levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6).ResultsThe median (IQR) total QoR-15 score of 105.5 (103–120.8) was higher (p < 0.0001) in the group T compared with the score of 100.5 (93.5–104) in the group S. Group T demonstrated superior early postoperative analgesia, with lower VAS scores at rest (3.61 ± 0.57 vs. 3.91 ± 0.61, P = 0.033) and during movement (4.86 ±0.76 vs. 5.23 ±0.72, P = 0.039). The time to first rescue analgesia was longer in group T compared with group S [38 (32.25–43.75) minutes vs. 21 (17.25–30) minutes, P < 0.001]. Group T had a lower incidence of nausea (36% vs. 23%, P < 0.05) and vomiting (11% vs. 33%, P < 0.05). Rescue analgesic demand, PCIA use and CRP or IL-6 levels at any measured time point showed no significant difference.ConclusionIn patients undergoing LSG, tegileridine demonstrated a modest improvement in recovery quality, comparable analgesia, and significantly fewer opioid-related adverse events versus sufentanil.Keywords: tegileridine fumarate, biased µ-opioid receptor agonist, quality of recovery, laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy

Introduction

The rising prevalence of overweight and obesity represents a major challenge for global public health.1 Currently, laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) is the most commonly performed bariatric metabolic procedure worldwide, accounting for more than 60% of all bariatric surgeries.2,3 This procedure achieves sustained weight loss, reduces obesity-related comorbidities and mortality rates, and improves quality of life.4

Although minimally invasive laparoscopic approaches reduce surgical trauma compared with conventional open surgery, patients still experience substantial surgical stress responses.5 Obese patients are particularly susceptible to acute postoperative pain (APP), which occurs more frequently and severely in this population.6 More than 80% of surgical patients experience APP, and approximately 75% report moderate to severe or extreme pain following surgery.7 APP not only delays postoperative recovery but also increases pulmonary complications and reduces patient satisfaction.8,9 Opioid analgesics possess broad-spectrum analgesic activity and are widely used to manage various types of pain. Effective control of moderate to severe postoperative pain often requires opioid administration.10 Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is among the most frequent complications following bariatric surgery.11 Despite established guidelines and preventive strategies for PONV, the incidence in bariatric patients may still exceed 70%.12,13 Therefore, achieving effective analgesia while maximizing patient safety and comfort remains a considerable clinical challenge for this population.

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols advocate multimodal analgesia to minimize opioid-related risks. Accordingly, choosing appropriate opioid agents is particularly important. Among commonly used perioperative opioids, sufentanil offers rapid onset and short duration of action, contributing to its widespread use in bariatric procedures due to its high potency and ease of titration. However, the clinical utility of sufentanil is constrained by dose-dependent adverse effects, such as nausea/vomiting or respiratory depression.10 Tegileridine is a novel μ-opioid receptor biased agonist that selectively activates the G-protein-coupled pathway, which mediates strong central analgesic effect. Tegileridine only weakly activates the β-arrestin-2 pathway implicated in adverse events like respiratory depression and gastrointestinal dysfunction, has half-life of approximately 6–7 hours,thus producing analgesic effects with fewer side effects.14,15 Previous studies have shown that, when used for postoperative analgesia in adults undergoing abdominal surgery, tegileridine fumarate provides analgesic efficacy comparable to that of morphine, while being associated with a lower incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting and respiratory depression.15 However, to date, no studies have investigated the effect of tegileridine fumarate on postoperative recovery quality in patients undergoing bariatric surgery.

Based on these pharmacological characteristics, tegileridine fumarate may be particularly suitable for patients undergoing bariatric surgery. Therefore, this randomized controlled trial aimed to test the hypothesis that, compared with sufentanil, tegileridine provides better postoperative recovery quality and analgesic efficacy following bariatric surgery.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethical Statements

This single-center, prospective, randomized, double-blind clinical trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Lianyungang Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (Medical Ethics Approval: KY-20241226002-02) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number: ChiCTR2500108386; registration date: August 29, 2025). Patients who underwent elective laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy under general anesthesia August 2025 to March 2026 were screened and enrolled as study subjects. Written informed consent was obtained from all enrolled patients. The ethical procedures of this study were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the reporting of this trial adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Patient Selection

All participants provided informed consent and were considered eligible if they met the following criteria: American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III; BMI> 30kg.m2; aged > 18 years; and treated with Laparoscopic Sleeve Gastrectomy. Exclusion criteria were:1) allergy to the study medications; 2) acute or severe bronchial asthma; 3) long-term use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, opioids, or sedatives; 4) any neurological or psychiatric disorders, including a history of substance abuse, anxiety, or depression, or ongoing treatment for these conditions; 5) significant organ dysfunction, including cardiac dysfunction; hepatic dysfunction (alanine aminotransferase or aspartate aminotransferase ≥ 2 times the upper limit of normal, or total bilirubin > 1.5 times the upper limit of normal); or renal dysfunction (serum creatinine ≥ 2 mg/dL).

Randomization and Blinding

An independent investigator who was not involved in the trial procedures or statistical analysis generated the random allocation sequence using a computer-generated random number table Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the tegileridine group (Group T) or the sufentanil group (Group S). Each patient’s allocation was placed in a sealed, opaque envelope, which was opened upon entry into the operating center by an anesthesiologist who was not involved in patient care or data collection at any other stage of the study. This anesthesiologist was responsible for preparing syringes containing the induction and additional dose of tegileridine or sufentanil, ensuring that the appearance and volumes were indistinguishable, as well as the patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) pumps. The following personnel were blinded to group allocation: study participants; the surgical team; the anesthesiologists responsible for intraoperative management; anesthesiologists collecting postoperative data in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) and the ward; and follow-up staff administering the QoR-15 questionnaire. The surgical and anesthesia teams remained unchanged throughout the study.

Anaesthesia Protocol and Postoperative Analgesia Management

All patients fasted overnight and received no premedication. Upon arrival in the operating room, invasive arterial catheterization was performed. Continuous monitoring included electrocardiography (ECG), pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2), noninvasive blood pressure (NIBP), invasive arterial blood pressure (IBP), bispectral index (BIS), body temperature (T), and neuromuscular function.

Adequate preoxygenation was performed before anesthesia induction (FiO2 100% for 5 min). General anesthesia with combined intravenous and inhalational techniques was used. Intravenous induction consisted of propofol 2.0–2.5 mg/kg (total body weight), sufentanil 0.3–0.5 μg/kg (lean body weight) or tegileridine 15–25 μg/kg (lean body weight), and vecuronium 0.08–0.10 mg/kg (lean body weight). Endotracheal intubation was performed once adequate conditions were achieved.

Anesthesia maintenance involved continuous inhalation of a 60% air-oxygen mixture. Mechanical ventilation utilized the PCV-VG mode with tidal volumes of 6–8 mL/kg ideal body weight, an inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio (I:E) of 1:2, and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 5–10 cmH2O. Ventilatory adjustments aimed to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide (PETCO2) between 35 and 45 mmHg. Intermittent manual lung recruitment maneuvers were employed intraoperatively (peak airway pressure of 30 cmH2O for 7–8 s). If intraoperative SpO2 fell below 92%, strategies included intermittent lung recruitment, increased PEEP (up to 10 cmH2O), higher fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2 ≤80%), increased tidal volume (≤8 mL/kg), elevated respiratory rate (≤20 breaths/min), and adjusting the I:E ratio to 1:1.5. If SpO2 remained below 92%, further adjustments included increasing FiO2 beyond 80% and tidal volumes above 8 mL/kg for patient safety. Anesthesia maintenance medications included sevoflurane at 0.7–1 minimum alveolar concentration (MAC), propofol infusion at 4–10 mg/(kg·h) (lean body weight), and remifentanil infusion at 0.05–0.2 μg/(kg·min) (lean body weight). Additional vecuronium was administered as required. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and heart rate (HR) were kept within ±20% of baseline values, and BIS values were maintained between 40 and 60. All patients routinely received intravenous dexamethasone (10 mg) and palonosetron hydrochloride (0.25 mg) intraoperatively to prevent PONV. Thirty minutes before surgery completion, patients received intravenous sufentanil (5 μg for Group S) or tegileridine fumarate (1 mg for Group T).

At surgery completion, neuromuscular blockade was reversed with sugammadex sodium under neuromuscular monitoring. Awakening time was defined as the interval from anesthetic discontinuation to eye-opening upon verbal command. Patients were extubated in a semi-recumbent position after regaining full consciousness, normal muscle tone, stable hemodynamics, spontaneous breathing with adequate tidal volume, and gentle oral secretion suctioning. After transfer to the PACU, PCIA was initiated. For Group S, sufentanil was diluted to 1 μg/mL with normal saline to a total volume of 100 mL; each bolus dose was 2 μg, with no background infusion and a 10-minute lockout interval. For Group T, tegileridine was diluted to 0.05 mg/mL with normal saline to 100 mL total volume; each bolus dose was 0.05 mg, with no background infusion and a 10-minute lockout interval. Modified Aldrete–Kroulik scores were assessed every 15 minutes until achieving a discharge readiness score of ≥9.

Postoperatively, if the VAS score at rest was ≥4 or during movement ≥7 and pain was uncontrolled by Patient-Controlled Intravenous Analgesia(PCIA), parecoxib (40 mg) was administered. Palonosetron hydrochloride (0.25 mg) was provided when PONV reached Word Health Organization (WHO) grade ≥III.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was quality of recovery, assessed using the Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) scale at 24 h postoperatively. QoR-15 is one of the most widely adopted tools for evaluating recovery quality following anesthesia and surgery. The score assesses five domains of patient-reported health status: pain, physical comfort, physical independence, psychological state, and emotional state. It’s overall score ranges from 0 to 150, with a higher score indicating better postoperative recovery.

Secondary outcomes included Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain scores at rest and during turning at 12, 24, 48, and 72 hours postoperatively; cumulative postoperative analgesic consumption via PCIA at 48 hours; time to first rescue analgesia; total dosage of rescue analgesics required; postoperative antiemetic usage rate; patient satisfaction with analgesia; the incidence of adverse events within 72 hours after surgery (nausea and vomiting, skin pruritus, somnolence, respiratory depression [defined as oxygen saturation <90% for >10 seconds, and/or respiratory rate <8 breaths/min, and/or apnea >15 seconds], dizziness);occurrence of pulmonary complications within 7 days postoperatively, as well as preoperative and postoperative levels of C-reactive protein and interleukin-6.

We also monitored adverse events. Specifically, we recorded the occurrence of hypotension (systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg or a decrease of >30% from baseline), hypertension (systolic blood pressure >180 mmHg or an increase of >30% from baseline), bradycardia (heart rate <45 beats/min or a decrease of >30% from baseline), tachycardia (heart rate >100 beats/min or an increase of >30% from baseline), and oxygen desaturation (peripheral oxygen saturation <90%). The following parameters were also recorded: medication dosage during anesthesia, pneumoperitoneum pressure, volume of fluid infusion, urine output, blood loss and transfusion volume, body temperature, extubation time, and duration of anesthesia and surgery.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size calculation was based on the total QoR-15 score at 24 hours postoperatively. According to Myles et al,16 the minimal clinically important difference for detecting intervention effects using the QoR-15 scale is 8.0, with a standard deviation of approximately 16 following major surgery. Therefore, to detect an 8-point difference in the total QoR-15 score at a significance level of 5% and statistical power of 80%, 30 patients per group were required for a two-tailed analysis. Considering a dropout rate of 10%, 35 patients per group were needed, totaling 70 patients.

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25.0. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviation (x ± s) and compared using independent samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were reported as medians (M) and interquartile ranges (IQR), with comparisons made using the rank-sum test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (n) and percentages (%) and compared using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests. Repeated measures analysis of variance with Bonferroni correction was used for comparisons across multiple time points. A P-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Primary Outcome

The median (IQR) total Quality of Recovery-15 questionnaire score of 105.5 (103–120.8) was higher (p < 0.0001) in the group T compared with the score of 100.5 (93.5–104) in the group S. The 95% confidence interval for the between-group difference was 4.5 to 10.9. Specifically, Group T had significantly better scores in the pain control (p < 0.0001), physical comfort (p =0.004), and emotional state domains (p < 0.0001). Physical independence (p=0.984) and psychological support (p=0.838) showed no significant difference (Table 2).

Two sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary outcome. First, after square transformation of the QoR-15 scores to approximate normality, an independent-samples t-test confirmed a significant between-group difference (t (70) = 4.80, P < 0.001). Second, after adjusting for age, sex, and preoperative BMI in a general linear model, the group effect remained significant (F (1,67) =23.0, P<0.001). Both analyses yielded results consistent with the primary analysis.

Secondary Outcomes

Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed that the group × time interaction for resting VAS scores was significant (P = 0.034), and the interaction for VAS scores during movement was significant (P = 0.043), indicating that the temporal trajectories of pain scores differed between the two groups. Group T demonstrated superior early postoperative analgesia, with lower VAS scores at rest (3.61 ± 0.57 vs. 3.91 ± 0.61, P = 0.033) and during movement (5.06 ± 0.90 vs. 6.18 ± 0.79, P = 0.039). No other temporal intervals exhibited statistically discernible intergroup differences (all P > 0.05) (Figure 2 and Table 3). The time to first rescue analgesia was longer in group T compared with group S [38 (32.25–43.75) minutes vs. 21 (17.25–30) minutes, P < 0.001]. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in cumulative opioid consumption within 48 hours postoperatively (p=0.581) or in the total amount of rescue analgesics administered (p=0.586). Compared with group S, group T had a lower incidence of nausea (23% vs. 13%, P < 0.05) and vomiting (11% vs. 33%, P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in other adverse events (all P > 0.05, Table 4). There was no statistically significant difference among the two groups in terms of basal levels of IL-6, CRP and all levels increased after surgery (all P<0.0001). Additionally, no significant differences were found in postoperative CRP or IL-6 levels at any measured time point between the two groups (all P > 0.05, Figure 3).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that, compared to sufentanil, tegileridine fumarate demonstrated a modest improvement in postoperative recovery. Additionally, patients treated with tegileridine showed reduced early postoperative VAS pain scores both during rest and movement, and a prolonged interval before requiring first rescue analgesia. Furthermore, tegileridine significantly lowered the incidence of PONV compared with sufentanil.

Currently, no standardized dosing guidelines exist for tegileridine fumarate in anesthesia induction. The dosage employed here was based on previous pharmacological equivalence analyses and clinical studies. Existing data indicate that tegileridine fumarate has approximately nine-fold greater analgesic potency than morphine.17 Equianalgesic conversion suggests that 10 μg/kg tegileridine is roughly equivalent to 0.1 mg/kg morphine, corresponding to about 0.1 μg/kg sufentanil. However, an induction dose of 15–25 μg/kg for tegileridine was selected. This conservative dosing approach was adopted due to limited dose-response data in obese adult populations and concerns regarding possible adverse effects. Preliminary studies also suggest that this dosage range is both safe and effective.

Quality of recovery (QoR) following anesthesia represents an essential indicator of patients’ postoperative health.18 The QoR-15 offers a comprehensive, efficient evaluation of recovery quality after surgery and anesthesia19 and is internationally recognized as a valid instrument for assessing postoperative recovery.20 Our results demonstrated that, in obese patients undergoing laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, the QoR-15 score at 24 hours postoperatively was significantly higher in the tegileridine group than in the sufentanil group, and this finding was corroborated by a sensitivity analysis using square-transformed data under a parametric framework.

However, the Hodges-Lehmann median difference (5.0 points) fell below the pre-specified MCID of 8 points. Although the mean difference after data transformation in the sensitivity analysis reached 7.78 points, the lower bound of its 95% CI (4.35 points) remained below the MCID threshold, while the upper bound (11.21 points) exceeded it; the wide interval reflects the imprecision of the effect size estimation. This imprecision is largely attributable to the exploratory design and limited sample size of the present study. Therefore, the current evidence is insufficient to conclude that tegileridine has a definitive clinical advantage over sufentanil. Nevertheless, the observed score exceeded the lower MCID defined in other studies (eg., 6 points21). Taken together, these findings suggest that tegileridine may have potential clinical value in improving postoperative recovery quality in bariatric surgery patients, although confirmation in larger, equianalgesic-dose trials is required.

Specifically, patients receiving tegileridine reported higher subdomain scores related to pain, emotional well-being, and physical comfort. Tegileridine not only decreased early postoperative pain in patients undergoing bariatric surgery but also reduced opioid-related adverse events, such as nausea and vomiting. These effects directly improved patient comfort and facilitated recovery of appetite. However, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in the physical independence domain, possibly because patients remained predominantly bedridden on postoperative day 1 and engaged in only brief ambulation on postoperative day 2 following laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy.

Previous studies have established a direct association between increased pain scores and poorer postoperative recovery quality.22 In the present trial, the mean VAS scores at rest were below 4 in both groups, suggesting clinically acceptable overall analgesia. Pain scores at 12 hours postoperatively were lower in the tegileridine group, possibly due to its linear pharmacokinetic profile, rapid attainment of peak plasma concentrations after infusion, and relatively long elimination half-life of approximately 6–7 hours.23 Tegileridine’s stable plasma concentration provides analgesia lasting at least 6 hours, significantly enhancing pain tolerance. These pharmacological characteristics likely contributed to the significantly delayed first rescue analgesia requirement observed in the tegileridine group. By contrast, sufentanil exhibits a shorter duration of action, quicker redistribution, and faster elimination (distribution volume: approximately 500 L for tegileridine versus 344 L for sufentanil), which may account for higher early postoperative pain scores. During the study period, no statistically significant differences were noted in patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) consumption or the requirement for rescue analgesics between groups, indicating that tegileridine provided analgesic efficacy comparable to sufentanil.

PONV is a common adverse event, affecting approximately 30% of the general surgical population and up to 80% in high-risk patients.24 For patients, PONV not only induces physical discomfort but also negatively impacts emotional health, prolongs hospitalization, and increases healthcare costs. Substantial evidence indicates that laparoscopic surgery significantly elevates PONV risk.25 The current study found a markedly lower incidence of nausea and vomiting in the tegileridine group, consistent with earlier studies. For instance, a previous clinical trial involving orthopedic surgery showed that tegileridine offered effective analgesia and favorable safety, significantly reducing the incidence of vomiting, moderate nausea, and antiemetic medication use.26 Similar outcomes were reported in another study involving 526 adults (mean age, 42 years) undergoing abdominal surgery. Tegileridine significantly alleviated APP and reduced the relative risk of nausea and vomiting by approximately 20% and 32%, respectively.27 Opioid receptor activation initiates intracellular G protein coupling, followed by recruitment of the multifunctional protein β-arrestin.28 The G protein–dependent pathway mediates analgesia and nociceptor modulation, whereas adverse effects such as respiratory depression, constipation, tolerance, and addiction are predominantly mediated via the μ-opioid receptor β-arrestin pathway.28,29 μ-Opioid receptor–biased agonists preferentially activate the G protein signaling pathway while minimizing β-arrestin–mediated adverse effects, optimizing overall recovery. Tegileridine activates the β-arrestin pathway at approximately 10% of morphine’s level, retaining potent analgesic effects while minimizing adverse outcomes. Thus, the findings of the current study hold important clinical implications for this specific patient population.

We found that IL-6 and CRP levels at 24 hours postoperatively increased significantly compared to preoperative values in both groups; however, differences between groups were not statistically significant. This finding aligns with results reported by A. L. Kvarnström et al,30 who demonstrated that surgical trauma triggers substantial changes in host immune responses, including activation of the complement cascade and release of proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines during and following major surgery. Prior studies have indicated that surgical trauma elevates plasma IL-6 concentrations proportionally to procedure severity.31 Among the well-known adverse effects of opioids, their immunosuppressive properties have attracted increasing attention. Short-acting opioids such as morphine significantly impair immune function, while long-acting opioids like buprenorphine may allow a gradual recovery of immune responses and cytokine levels.32 Although both tegileridine and buprenorphine are μ-opioid receptor–biased agonists, no statistically significant differences in inflammatory or immune markers were observed between the tegileridine and sufentanil groups. This result is similar to the findings of Lin, Chunmei et al33 This finding may be attributed to two key limitations: the lack of strict equianalgesic dosing between groups, and the insufficient statistical power arising from the small sample size.

Obese patients are predisposed to obstructive sleep apnea due to their unique anatomical and physiological features. Clinical trials suggest that tegileridine fumarate may offer an advantage by reducing respiratory depression, potentially making it particularly suitable for obese patients. Nevertheless, no significant difference was found between groups regarding postoperative respiratory depression or pulmonary complications in this study. This finding may be attributed to the surgical team’s extensive experience in perioperative management of obese patients and rigorous postoperative monitoring and care, substantially reducing the risk of oxygen desaturation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 72) and the single-center design may limit the generalizability of findings. Second, the dosing regimens of tegileridine and sufentanil in this study were not calibrated on a strict equianalgesic basis, primarily because tegileridine was only recently approved and no validated equianalgesic conversion data are currently available for the obese population. This limitation resulted in unequal opioid exposure between groups across perioperative phases, potentially compromising the reliability of comparative efficacy and safety analyses. Third, the pharmacokinetic profile of intravenous tegileridine in obese patients remains unclear. Tegileridine fumarate undergoes extensive hepatic metabolism primarily via CYP3A4, CYP3A5, and CYP2D6, with mono-oxidation and glucuronidation as the main metabolic pathways.34 Hepatic functional changes associated with fatty infiltration could affect tegileridine metabolism. However, this potential impact could not be evaluated with the collected data, nor was it possible to determine the optimal dosing strategy for this patient population. Future studies with larger sample sizes, extended follow-up durations, and investigation of tegileridine in diverse surgical contexts and patient groups are recommended.

Conclusion

In this preliminary randomized trial of obese patients undergoing laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, tegileridine, compared with sufentanil, demonstrated a statistically significant but modest improvement in postoperative recovery quality, along with significantly lower early pain scores and a significantly lower incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting. These findings suggest that tegileridine may offer a potentially favorable profile for improving early recovery while reducing opioid-related adverse events. Future studies should further investigate the effects of these two agents on postoperative recovery in patients undergoing sleeve gastrectomy under equianalgesic dosing conditions, so as to achieve more effective management of postoperative analgesia.
