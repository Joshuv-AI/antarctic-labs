Genomic insights into Greek sheep structure and admixture with global domestic breeds
Authors: Valentina Tsartsianidou, Konstantinos Gkagkavouzis, Arianna Manunza, Paolo Cozzi, Aristotelis Moulistanos, Alessandra Stella, Dimitrios Chatziplis, Alexandros Triantafyllidis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGreece holds a critical geographic position in the introduction of domestic sheep into Europe during Neolithic and post-Neolithic migrations, acting as a bridge with Asia. The genomic profile of Greek sheep remains underexplored compared to global breeds, despite their importance for sustainable livestock production. In this study, we constructed an extensive 50 K SNP data collection from 1,625 Greek indigenous sheep reared in 23 flocks compared to southwest Asian, Asian, south, central and north European populations.ResultsWe detected a diverse genomic profile for Greek breeds linked to both European and Asian ancestry using PCA, admixture and NeighborNet analyses. The Greek breeds were clearly differentiated at the breed level with close genetic relationships between Greek mainland populations and the Italian Zackel-type sheep. PCA clustering and network topology showed an east-to-west genetic axis extending from Asia to the Iberian Peninsula across the Mediterranean, consistent with the Mediterranean dispersal of domestic sheep, with Greece serving as an interlink between the two continents. The Greek cluster showed the highest reticulation level in the regional phylogenetic analysis, implying recent admixture events compared to other geographic clusters. The average observed heterozygosity in Greek sheep (Ho = 0.358), ranging from 0.326 to 0.376, was comparable to that of Mediterranean breeds (Ho = 0.366). At local scale, the analysis of multiple farms revealed heterogeneous management practices, enabling genetic identification in particular cases as evidenced by the admixture profiles by both between- and within-breed comparisons. Semi-fat-tailed Chios (FIS= 0.17) and Pelagonia (FIS= 0.10) sheep displayed higher inbreeding across farms compared to the other Greek sheep attributed to long-lasting selection pressure, conservation efforts and sampling scheme, respectively.ConclusionsCollectively, our findings can be integrated into national conservation actions, especially for endangered populations, to prevent genetic erosion and support sustainable management of local livestock genetic resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13013-1.

Background

Domestic sheep dispersed across Europe during early Neolithic times via two independent migration routes: (i) the Mediterranean region, following a northern and a southern coastal direction, and (ii) the Danubian valley through the Balkan peninsula [1, 2]. These findings align with paleogenomic evidence indicating that Aegean Neolithic people (early Greeks and southwest Anatolians) were ancestral to Europe’s earliest farmers, supporting the existence of two independent dispersal pathways: one from the Aegean into southwestern Europe, and another leading to Germany via Hungary [3]. During the Roman Empire, the post-domestication dispersal of the ‘Tarentine’ sheep, also known as ‘Greek’ sheep, has also been documented within southwest and afterwards central and northern Europe [4].

Greece occupies a critical geographic location for the initial introduction of domestic sheep into Europe and for post-domestication events, serving as a continuous interlink between Europe and Asia [5]. The country hosts a large and diverse pool of sheep genetic resources [6] shaped by its variable geomorphological and climatic features that facilitated the development of numerous endemic sheep [1]. Since the 20th century, historical events, human migrations and financial changes, such as land distribution for cultivation due to population increase and consequent decline of transhumance practices, radically altered the Greek social network, influencing sheep distribution and breed development [7]. Crossbreeding practices were extensively carried out for improvement purposes, high-yielding foreign sheep breeds have been introduced to Greece, and selection pressure has been applied on specific productive local breeds [8]. Currently, 23 local sheep breeds are recognized in the Domestic Animal Diversity Information System (DAD-IS) (https://www.fao.org/dad-is/browse-by-country-and-species/en/) of Food and Agriculture Organization [9]. However, many indigenous breeds have experienced population declines, and some may have already become extinct due to these practices.

To obtain a comprehensive overview of the local sheep genetic resources, extensive genotyping was conducted using the Illumina Ovine SNP50 Bead Chip on five native breeds: Bouts(i)ko (Orino of Epirus), Pelagonia (Florina), Chios, Mytilene (Lesvos) and Frizarta sheep. These breeds were analyzed in the context of European and Asian populations [10] to explore their genetic relationships, admixture events that shaped their genetic profile and within-breed genetic variability. The genetic origin of modern Greek sheep has been mostly linked to Zackel type but also to Ruda and semi-fat tailed breeds [11]. Chios and Frizarta represent high-producing commercial dairy sheep populations semi-intensively and intensively reared in north and south mainland Greece, respectively. Both are incorporated into genetic selection aimed at improving milk production, with Chios having undergone long-term selection. Chios sheep is a semi-fat tailed breed, potentially developed through crosses between local sheep of Chios island and fat-tailed Anatolian breeds, such as Kivircik and Daglic [1]. Frizarta is a composite breed developed between 1960 and 1980 through crosses of local Zackel-type ewes with East Friesian rams [12]. Mytilene (semi-fat tailed) dairy sheep, originating from the island of Lesvos, are well adapted to hot, dry climates and poor soils [13], forming the largest indigenous population in Greece, reared in semi-intensive farming system. In terms of exploiting underutilized genetic resources, we also studied two dual-purpose (milk and meat) breeds with special adaptive traits and high-quality products, thin-tailed Boutsko and Pelagonia, both classified as ‘endangered maintained’ under the risk status system established by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). Boutsko is a mountainous Zackel-type breed semi-extensively reared in western Greece with seasonal transhumance practices applied based on pasture availability. Pelagonia sheep likely descend from the Ruda wool-type sheep [14], a subvariety of the Zackel type, with only 2,298 animals recorded in 2023 [9]. It is well known for high meat quality, disease resistance and lamb survival while it is adapted in less favored areas [15]. National conservation actions have been previously implemented, however, the genome-wide variation remains poorly exploited [11].

Previous research on the origin of European sheep revealed that the Balkan region and Italy were the main migration hubs within Europe, suggesting that Greek sheep were an intermediate between Asian fat-tailed and European populations, acting as a barrier between the two continents [4]. However, that study relied on a limited dataset of island breeds, mostly related to Asian fat-tailed populations (Chios, Lesvos). Additionally, a recent survey conducted on nine Greek indigenous breeds primarily focused on the genetic relationships among them, while also providing insights into their relatedness to global sheep [11]. Here, we present a comprehensive genomic analysis based on an extensive genotype collection of 1,625 Greek sheep integrated with global data. We aimed to investigate the genomic diversity, population structure, admixture patterns, and relationships of Greek sheep breeds within the broader context of southwest Asian, Asian, south, central and north European populations. Additionally, within-breed genomic variation and inbreeding were further explored to provide insights relevant to conservation and breeding strategies for the sustainable management of local sheep genetic resources under changing environments.

Methods

Dataset construction and animal sampling

Five Greek sheep breeds were genotyped within the framework of the SMARTER project (https://www.smarterproject.eu/), and samples were collected from 23 farms across Greece, as shown in Fig. 1 and also described in Additional File 1: Table S1. No animal was sacrificed or anesthetized in this study. A total of 1,625 blood samples were collected from the jugular veins and transferred to EDTA vacutainers, including Chios (n = 813), Frizarta (n = 353), Mytilene (n = 107), Boutsko (n = 292, 3 farms) and Pelagonia sheep (n = 60). Particularly, Chios (CHI1-CHI4) and Frizarta (FRZ1-FRZ2) sheep were sampled from highly producing purebred farms of northern/central and south-western Greece, respectively. Mytilene samples were gathered from ten farms distributed across Lesvos island. Boutsko blood samples were collected from the transhumant populations BOU1 and BOU2, moving between the mountains of northwestern Greece and the lowlands of Thessaly, and population BOU3 permanently located in the Epirus region. Pelagonia samples were gathered from the nucleus farms PEL1 and PEL2 created for conservation purposes at the Research Institute of Animal Science of the Hellenic Agricultural Organisation DIMITRA (RIAS), and the facilities of the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki (AUTH) and the smallholder farms PEL3 and PEL4.

Sampling locations (n = 23) of the studied Greek sheep breeds

Total genomic DNA was extracted from the collected blood samples according to the standard protocol of the QIAamp DNA Mini and Blood Mini kit (QIAGEN, USA), following the manufacturer’s recommendations with minor modifications. DNA and samples were quantified using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and stored at -20 °C until use. Subsequently, DNA samples were oven-dried and 500–1000 ng per sample were transferred to 96-well plates. All DNA samples were genotyped with the Illumina’s OvineSNP50 Genotyping BeadChip v2 that features 53,516 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) across the 27 ovine chromosomes.

A second dataset was constructed merging the newly generated genomic data with published 50 K genotypes from 31 global sheep breeds of Sheep HapMap project [16], available from the International Sheep Genomics Consortium and the SMARTER-database [10], using PLINK v1.9 [17]. Both the newly generated data and the Sheep HapMap dataset were provided in Illumina TOP allele format and aligned to the Oar_v3.1 sheep genome assembly before merging. European and West-Asian populations were primarily included to investigate their genetic relationships with Greek sheep; however, a limited number of East Asian, American and African populations were also incorporated to contextualize findings within a broader global perspective. The final dataset consisted of 36 breeds and 3,044 sheep with a detailed description provided in Table S2 and sampling location shown in Fig. 2.

Genotype quality control

Quality control was performed for both datasets by setting sample, marker call rate and minor allele frequency (MAF) thresholds at 0.95, 0.98 and 0.02 respectively, using PLINK v1.9 [17]. Only autosomal SNPs with known chromosomal position were included. Then, a representative sample (n ≤ 40) was generated for each population, implementing the R package BITE v1.2.000747 [18], to balance the global dataset per breed for population structure analyses, preserving within-breed genetic diversity and discarding highly related individuals (IBS > 0.95). Finally, SNPs were also subjected to Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) pruning with PLINK v1.9 using indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2 [17] to alleviate ascertainment bias [19]. The filtered global dataset consisted of 922 genotypes and 36,735 SNPs and the Greek dataset comprised the total number of animals genotyped (n = 1,625) and 32,394 SNPs available for the subsequent genetic analyses, with an average genotyping rate of 0.999.

Genetic diversity and differentiation

The observed (Ho) heterozygosity levels and the inbreeding coefficient (FIS), based on the difference between the observed and expected number of homozygous genotypes under Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, were estimated for each population using PLINK v1.9 [17]. The same software was used to estimate FROH for the complete Greek dataset within-breed level using unpruned SNP data with the following parameters: --homozyg --homozyg-window-snp 50 --homozyg-kb 1000 --homozyg-density 50 --homozyg-gap 500 --homozyg-het 1 --homozyg-window-missing 2. Additionally, the R package hierfstat v0.5-11 [20] was used to calculate pairwise FST values between sheep populations, while the R packages ggplot2 v3.5.2 [21] and reshape2 [22] were implemented to visualize a heatmap of the FST matrix.

Population structure and genetic relationships

Principal component analyses (PCA) were conducted to explore the population structure of the studied breeds using GEMMA software [23]. The genomic relatedness matrix (GRM) was decomposed and then visualized with the R package ggplot2 v3.5.2 [21]. Individual ancestry proportions were inferred using unsupervised clustering with ADMIXTURE v1.3.0 software [24], testing K values ranging from 2 to 45 to infer admixture events. Fivefold cross-validation (CV) error values and the number of required iterations to reach model convergence were utilized to evaluate the optimal number of ancestral populations. The R package BITE v1.2.000747 [18] and particularly the membercoef.cv and membercoef.circos functions were implemented to plot the admixture and cross-validation results, respectively. A Greek representative (n = 158) dataset was also analysed using PCA and admixture with K values from 2 to 20 to assess in detail the across and within-breed genetic structure and admixture patterns, whereas the admixture patterns were also separately evaluated for each breed using an extensive dataset (n = 1,315), taking advantage of the large number of farms sampled and animals genotyped, excluding up to first-degree of relatedness (see Additional File 1: Table S1). Additionally, a neighbor-joining (NJ) tree was constructed based on allele-sharing distances (--distance 1-ibs) using a subset of the whole Greek dataset for clear vizualization of the individual level clustering (n = 316, see Additional File 1: Table S1) and visualized using iTOL [25].

To assess reticulate relationships among the studied populations, the Reynold’s genetic distances were calculated using the R package adegenet v2.1.11 [26] and visualized through a NeighbourNet graph using SplitsTree v6.4.14 [27]. Delta score and Q-residual metrics were also estimated from the NeighborNet analysis to evaluate the degree of conflicting phylogenetic signals within the network and fit of the data to a tree- or network-like structure [28]. The Delta score measures the extent of reticulation for the entire network with values ranging from 0 (tree-like) to 1 (network-like), indicating the presence of reticulation events. The Q-residual quantifies deviations from tree-like structure based on local inconsistencies of quartets that represent sets of four taxa, also ranging between 0 and 1 values [29]. In addition, an FST-based NeighborNet graph was constructed to further explore clustering at the beed level for comparison reasons to Reynold’s-based network.

Furthermore, populations were grouped and re-analyzed based on the genetic relatedness and origin observed to clarify the position of Greek breeds that showed varied ancestry (see Additional File 1: Table S3). FRZ was excluded due to partial genetic origin from Friesian sheep and Greek CHI was grouped with the Turkish SKZ sheep (Greece-Turkey cluster) due to its origin from Chios sheep.

Results

Genomic diversity and differentiation

Descriptive statistics of genomic diversity were estimated for both the global (n = 3,044) and representative (n = 922) samples and are summarized in Additional File 1: Table S1 with almost identical results. The average observed heterozygosity in Greek sheep (Ho= 0.358) was comparable to that of Mediterranean breeds (Ho= 0.366) and higher than central and northern European populations. Among the Greek local breeds, within-breed heterozygosity ranged from 0.326 in Chios (CHI) to 0.376 in Frizarta (FRZ). West Asian fat-tailed breeds (KRS, AFS) also displayed similar heterozygosity levels to Greek sheep, whereas the Cyprus Fat Tail (CFT) showed lower diversity. The individual genome-wide inbreeding coefficient (FIS) was assessed based on the difference between the observed and expected number of homozygous genotypes. Among the Greek sheep, CHI (FIS= 0.17) and PEL (FIS= 0.10) showed the highest inbreeding levels. In contrast, FRZ, MYT and BOU presented the lowest genomic inbreeding, along with the Mediterranean populations (FIS= 0.02–0.05), compared to other geographic clusters of the global sheep dataset, as also depicted by heterozygosity levels.

Based on pairwise FST results (Fig. 3), high genetic differentiation of CHI sheep from all other Greek breeds (FST= 0.08–0.11) and non-Greek populations was observed. On the other hand, low to moderate genetic differentiation was observed between the mainland Greek FRZ, MYT and BOU and with the Mediterranean populations, especially the Italian sheep (FST= 0.04–0.05). Interestingly, the west Asian KRS and AFS were less differentiated from the Greek FRZ, MYT, BOU and the Italian ALT, COM, LEC (FST= 0.05–0.09) compared to CHI (FST= 0.12). High genetic differentiation was observed between Greek and the North European BRL, DSH, EFB, EFW, ranging between 0.13 and 0.24, and lower with the old Norwegian NSP and the Scottish Blackface (FST= 0.08–0.14). The exact estimates are summarized in Additional File 1: Table S4.

Population structure and phylogenetic relationships

Population structure for the global dataset was inferred through principal component analysis (Fig. 4), where the percentage of total genetic variance explained by the first ten principal components (PCs) accounted for 11.89% (see Additional File 2: Figure S1). The first PC, accounting for 2.68% of total variation, showed an Asia (east) to Europe (west) genetic cline. Additionally, a gradient was observed across the Mediterranean region, beginning with the Greek sheep, followed by the Italian and the rest southwestern European breeds. Greek BOU and FRZ clustered very closely with the Italian COM, LEC and ALT sheep. The second (1.48%) and third (1.34%) PCs showed a south to north European genetic cline and separated the African breeds from Asia and Europe, accordingly. Greek sheep were clustered in the center of PCA plot among Asian, European and African breeds with near zero PC values (Fig. 4B). The Greek CHI sheep is more distantly positioned from the other Greek populations and the European group, clustering closely with Turkish SKZ.

The visualization of Reynolds’ genetic distances in a NeighborNet phylogenetic graph (fit = 100, Fig. 5A) revealed geographic clustering by continent and within-continent of origin. Greek CHI and Turkish SKZ share the same overall branch with west Asian (AFS, KRS, CFT), Asian (TIB, GUR) and African (ADP, RDA) sheep. Greek MYT, BOU and PEL form a sub-group, indicating shared ancestry among these populations, closely located to CHI and west Asian group. FRZ sheep grouped with the Friesian EFW, EFB in a separate branch in a web-like structure, showing the genetic origin of this Greek synthetic breed. Shorter branches were observed for MYT, BOU and FRZ, Spanish, Italian and American breeds, all with a high observed heterozygosity. Longer branches were found for highly selected or isolated populations, such as Greek CHI and PEL, north European and insular populations consistent with their low heterozygosity. To assess the degree of tree- versus network-likeness structure among the sheep breeds, Delta scores and Q-residual values were calculated (Table 1). The average Delta score across breeds (0.33) indicated a moderate level of reticulation, with the highest values in Greek FRZ, MYT and the American BCS and GCN. Q-residual values were consistently low, and the highest values were observed for MYT and FRZ. These estimates imply a network-like structure to a moderate extent, explained by reticulated relationships between specific studied populations. An additional NeighborNet graph was generated after excluding the Greek FRZ and the American BCS, GCN breeds to assess potential changes in network topology associated with their high reticulation. These populations exhibited high levels of admixture, potentially attributed to their recent demographic history and inbreeding relative to the other populations analyzed. Indeed, the Italian and western Mediterranean populations clustered closely with the Greek breeds, followed by the central and northern European sheep (Additional File 2: Figure S2).

The FST-based NeighborNet graph (Additional File 2: Figure S3) also showed the close genetic relationships between the Greek and the Italian Zackel-type breeds, which clustered on neighboring branches. Furthermore, the east-to-west and south-to-north genetic clines were evident across and within the European continent. Overall, shorter branch lengths were observed in the FST network compared to those inferred using Reynold’s genetic distances.

Based on geographic grouping, Reynold’s-based network topology showed a clear east-to-west genetic axis from Asia to Spain across the Mediterranean countries, with Greece linking Europe with Asia in consistence with PCA results (Fig. 5B). The highest level of reticulation estimated for the Greek cluster (Delta score = 0.39) consisting of the MYT, BOY and PEL breeds and the lowest for the West-Asian sheep (Delta score = 0.27) (Table 1).

Individual ancestry proportions were estimated for the global dataset using model-based admixture analysis. Clustering solutions for informative K values (K2, K3, K4, K6, K12, K20, K32, K36) were illustrated in Fig. 6, while the optimal K value for the global dataset was 32, showing the lowest cross-validation error (see Additional File 2: Figure S4). Asian fat-tailed and European genetic components were clearly observed for all Greek breeds at K = 2–3 [4] with the smallest European contribution observed for CHI sheep, further supporting PCA and NeighborNet results. The genetic cluster that corresponds to European sheep (light green) may reflect the genetic legacy of the first domesticated sheep introduced into Europe. At K = 4, the light pink component, assigned to Friesian EFB breed, is also present at Greek FRZ in agreement with NeighborNet clustering and the evolutionary history of FRZ as a synthetic breed developed through mating of East Friesian and Greek indigenous sheep. For K = 8–14, Mediterranean sheep shared a genetic component that was absent from most northern and central European breeds. Additionally, all Greek sheep share a genetic component with Asian GUR, TIB and south-west Asian AFS, KRS at K = 12 which is not present in CHI sheep in line with previous section findings. Finally, most of the studied populations showed a distinct genomic profile at K = 32 apart from Spanish OJA and RAA that depicted identical genetic structure.

Local-scale population structure and admixture

Local-scale analyses were also conducted to enhance the resolution and further elucidate population structure in the extensive genetic database of the indigenous sheep breeds. A representative dataset (n = 158) was used for between-breed comparisons, whereas a larger dataset, excluding individuals related up to the first-degree, was used for within-breed analyses (n = 1,315). Both datasets were derived from all farms sampled across Greece (Fig. 1). Based on the representative dataset, all individuals were successfully assigned to their phenotypically recognized breeds with principal component analysis (PCA) and model-based admixture clustering. The ewes raised in farm PEL3 were an exception, as they were closely grouped to MYT sheep and were clearly separated from the other PEL farms, potentially suggesting recent admixture events with MYT sheep (Fig. 7A and B). In agreement with the PCA and admixture results, the NJ tree based on allele-sharing distances separated individuals according to their breed of origin, apart from PEL3 individuals, which clustered close to the branch of Mytilene sheep (see Additional File 3: Figure S1). The first principal component (PC1 = 4.50%) separated CHI and PEL sheep from the other Greek breeds, forming three distant genetic clusters with MYT, BOU and FRZ centrally positioned along the PC1 axis (Fig. 7A). This pattern was also evident in the admixture analysis at K = 2, where CHI sheep were almost exclusively defined by the light blue-green component, PEL sheep by the dark green component, and the remaining populations comprised of a mixture of both genetic components (Fig. 7B). Based on PCA results, MYT sheep, collected and originated from the east-Aegean region (Lesvos island), clustered closely with mainland BOU, PEL3 and FRZ individuals, likely reflecting past crossbreeding or shared ancestry. However, clear differentiation between MYT and FRZ was observed along PC3, and between MYT and PEL3 along PC4 (see Additional File 2: Figures S5A & S5B). Distinct genomic profiles were obtained across Greek breeds at K = 5, which represents the optimal K value (see Additional File 2: Figures S6), equal to the number of breeds studied. Within-breed structure was also observed for CHI, BOU and FRZ from K = 13 onwards (Fig. 7B).

MYT sheep exhibited an identical admixed profile between several farms (Fig. 7B), suggesting substantial gene flow among sampling sites across the island of Lesvos (Fig. 1). However, the genetic composition of MYT1, MYT7, and MYT10 farms was differentiated at higher K values in both across (Fig. 7B) and within-breed admixture analyses (see Additional File 2: Figure S7). Estimated inbreeding levels were low across Mytilene farms and were lower than those observed in the other Greek sheep breeds studied (see Additional File 1: Table S1). Northern farm CHI1 demonstrated more homogeneous genetic structure (K = 8–20) compared to CHI2, CHI3 and CHI4 populations, as indicated by both across (Fig. 7B) and within-breed comparisons (see Additional File 2: Figure S7). Additionally, a subset of individuals collected from the CHI4 farm located in Central Greece was clustered within the branch of CHI3 sheep in Northern Greece, as depicted in the neighbor-joining tree based on allele-sharing distances between individuals (see Additional File 3: Figure S1). Quite similar genomic profiles were obtained for the PEL1, PEL2 and PEL4 farms, except PEL3 from both across and within-breed analyses, which also exhibited very similar levels of inbreeding (Additional File 1: Table S1). More homogeneous genetic structure was detected for the mountainous populations reared in BOU2 and BOU3 flocks (Fig. 7B), especially depicted in the within-population analysis using the extensive dataset (see Additional File 2: Figure S7), whereas BOU1 showed a more admixed profile, indicating diversified management practices within the breed-level of transhumant populations.

Discussion

Greece offers a critical window into early and later introductions of domestic sheep into Europe, situated at a key migration corridor. This study offers high-resolution insights into the population structure, admixture history, and genetic diversity of modern Greek sheep by integrating extensive national genotyping with global comparative genomic data. A newly developed dataset of 50 K SNP genotypes for five indigenous Greek breeds was analyzed together with data from the Sheep HapMap project. The dataset comprises 1,625 individuals from 23 locations across Greece, providing the most detailed genomic resource for Greek sheep to date and capturing both inter- and intra-breed variation.

A combination of analytical approaches was applied to investigate genomic variation and population structure in global and local contexts. At the global level, principal component analysis, model-based admixture, and NeighborNet phylogenetic networks revealed the complex evolutionary positioning of Greek sheep breeds, highlighting their intermediate role between Asian fat-tailed and European thin-tailed lineages, as well as their contribution to east-west and north-south genetic clines across the Mediterranean and Eurasia. Although several Zackel local breeds are found across Greece, the two northern thin-tailed populations analysed here (Boutsko, Pelagonia) are reared in the main potential corridor between Asia and Europe within Greece. These analyses also identified regionally structured ancestry of Greek breeds and demonstrated breed-specific differentiation shaped by gene flow, artificial selection and farm-level stratification revealed by detailed PCA and admixture analyses. Finally, global and local diversity metrics, inbreeding coefficients, and pairwise FST estimates were integrated to contextualize the genetic distinctiveness and conservation relevance of Greek sheep breeds within a broader geographic and evolutionary framework.

Demographic history of greek sheep breeds with a global perspective

Global PCA results placed Greek populations within the European cluster but also close to Asian breeds based on the first three principal components (Fig. 4), consistent with a similar intermediate position previously reported for Greek island breeds [16]. This central positioning may reflect Greece’s role as a major entry point for the initial introduction of domestic sheep from Asia into Europe, followed by domestication waves and geographic dispersals. Genetic evidence suggests that European early farmers were descended from populations in Early Neolithic Greece and northwestern Anatolia [3], supporting the hypothesis that the observed PCA clustering reflects parallel human and sheep co-migration events.

Previous research on the origin of European sheep suggested that the Balkan region and Italy served as the main migration hubs within Europe, with Greek island breeds described as an intermediate barrier between Asian fat-tailed and European sheep [4]. Our data also suggest a complex genomic profile for mainland and insular Greek breeds, allowing gene flow from multiple directions. Diverse breed origins and admixture histories were evident among the five indigenous Greek populations analyzed. Among the studied populations, Chios sheep (CHI) diverged most strongly from other Greek breeds based on PCA clustering. In contrast, Frizarta (FRZ), Boutsko (BOU), and Pelagonia (PEL) clustered more closely with southern European breeds, particularly the Italian sheep ALT, COM, LEC [30, 31]. These patterns support earlier reports of close genetic relationships between mountainous Greek and southern Italian breeds such as Altamurana [32] and are also consistent with previous evidence of Balkan Zackel ancestry in most of the mainland Greek populations studied [11, 12, 33]. The semi-fat-tailed Mytilene (MYT) sheep showed an intermediate position between Europe and Asia in concordance with its known mixed ancestry from Asian fat-tailed and local thin-tailed Zackel breeds [9]. These findings were also confirmed by low pairwise FST estimates observed between FRZ, BOU, MYT and the Italian populations. In contrast, CHI sheep displayed the highest genetic differentiation levels (FST= 0.12–0.16) with the Mediterranean breeds.

NeighborNet phylogenetic analysis further corroborated these relationships, organizing breeds into distinct continental clusters, while resolving regional substructures (Fig. 5A and B). Chios formed a shared branch with Turkish Sakiz (SKZ) [34] and west Asian breeds (KRS, AFS), and was also connected to south Asian (TIB, GUR) and south African breeds (ADP, RDA), indicating a strong connection to non-European fat-tailed lineages. This agrees with Y-chromosome analyses linking Chios and eastern Mediterranean fat-tailed with southwest Asian haplogroup y-HB [35]. Mytilene, Boutsko, and Pelagonia formed a distinct Greek subgroup and Frizarta (FRZ) clustered with the Friesian breeds (EFW, EFB), confirming its composite origin from East Friesian and local sheep crossbreeding. Shorter branch lengths for MYT, BOU, and FRZ suggest higher within-breed diversity, while the longer branches for CHI and PEL are indicative of isolation, strong selection, or genetic drift. When breeds were re-grouped by geographic origin and genetic relatedness (see Table 1; Fig. 5B), the graph topology showed a continuous east-west genetic cline, extending from south-west Asia to south-west Europe. Within this pattern, the Greek cluster (MYT, BOU, PEL) served as a genetic link between Asia and Europe, likely reflecting the Neolithic and post-Neolithic migration routes across the Mediterranean region. Specifically, the Greek MYT, BOU and PEL formed a cluster located on neighboring branches following the west-Asian and East Aegean populations (CHI, SKZ) and preceding the south Italian populations (COM, LEC, ALT) with the south-west European breeds positioned further to the right (Fig. 5B). Ciani et al. identified a genetic axis extending from the Balkans to Central and Northern Italy, and further into Central and Northern Europe, illustrating another major entry route of domestic sheep into Europe via the Danubian corridor [4].

Delta scores and Q-residuals calculated from the NeighborNet graph further quantified the network-like structure. Moderate reticulation was observed across the global dataset (average Delta ≈ 0.32), suggesting post-breed development admixture events and a moderate level of relationship complexity. The highest values estimated for Frizarta (0.39), Mytilene (0.36) and the American BCS (0.37) and GCN (0.38) are consistent with their mixed ancestry observed in admixture analysis (Fig. 6). The lowest scores were estimated for the west Asian breeds (0.29), potentially reflecting the absence of recent crossbreeding. In the regional breed grouping (Fig. 5B), the Greek cluster (MYT, PEL, BOU) displayed the highest reticulation (Delta = 0.39, Q-residual = 0.004), consistent with recent admixture events, especially for Frizarta, as previously reported [12].

Admixture analysis provided additional resolution of breed-specific ancestry patterns across a range of K values (Fig. 6). The Asian ancestry of Chios sheep was proportionally higher compared to the European genetic component, in contrast to the rest Greek indigenous sheep studied (K = 2). Additionally, the East Friesian origin of the Frizarta sheep was observed, consistent with its synthetic breeding history. At K = 12, Greek breeds (excluding CHI) shared a genetic component with Mediterranean sheep, which was mostly absent from northern and central European breeds, as well as with Asian breeds, including TIB, GUR, and the southwest Asian AFS and KRS. Although earlier gene flow or common ancestry between Chios and Mediterranean sheep likely occurred (visible at K = 2–4), standardized breeding schemes may have led to its distinctiveness at higher K levels (K ≥ 6). At higher K values, breed-specific genomic profiles were evident. At the optimal K = 32, all Greek populations exhibited distinct genomic profiles, with Chios remaining genetically isolated and PEL showing high breed-specific differentiation, although PEL3 individuals displayed admixed profile. Mytilene and Boutsko showed signs of mixed ancestry, partly due to historical crossbreeding. The genetic differentiation of Pelagonia sheep may also reflect the effects of recent targeted conservation efforts. Frizarta was highly admixed, especially evident at high K values, consistent with the elevated reticulation levels estimated in NeighborNet analysis.

The genetic relationships observed between Greek and global sheep breeds in the present study mirror patterns previously reported for Greek goats (Eghoria, Skopelos), which clustered closely to neighboring populations such as Balkan, Italian and Turkish goats [36–38] and showed comparable levels of heterozygosity and inbreeding [39]. These findings across sheep and goats reflect Greece’s pivotal geographic location at the crossroads of Europe and the Near East during domestication and post-domestication times of small ruminants. Archaeogenetic and genomic studies in both species [40–43] have shown that domestic lineages first entered Europe from multiple domestication centers in the Fertile Crescent and Anatolia, following maritime and terrestrial routes that passed through the Aegean and Balkan Peninsula. Later periods of trade, transhumance, and breed improvement facilitated genetic exchanges between southeastern Europe, the Mediterranean islands, and Anatolia, resulting in close clustering observed among Greek, Balkan, Italian, and Turkish small ruminant breeds today.

Local-scale genetic relationships between and within greek sheep breeds

Local-scale analyses across and within Greek indigenous breeds, based on both a representative (n = 158) and an extensive dataset (n = 1,315), revealed clear breed-level differentiation and also evidence of admixture and regional crossbreeding practices. The latter dataset, which included a large number of genotyped animals, enabled the capture of fine-scale population structure after excluding individuals related up to the first degree. Zackel and fat-tailed Asian ancestry, along with elevated inbreeding levels, emerged as major factors shaping breed clustering, while more recent crossbreeding also influenced genetic relationships among Greek breeds.

Increased FIS-based inbreeding estimates in Chios and Pelagonia sheep, compared to the other Greek breeds, likely reflects long-term directional selection and conservation actions, respectively. Farm-level FROH estimates indicated lower inbreeding levels (see Additional File 1: Table S1), suggesting that the observed heterozygosity deficit in FIS estimates may partially result from subpopulation structure associated with these management practices.

The first principal component (PC1) differentiated Chios and Pelagonia sheep from the other studied populations, indicating their genetic differentiation compared to BOU, FRZ and MYT sheep (Fig. 7A). The third principal component (PC3) separated the populations into two major groups, likely corresponding to northern (BOU, MYT, PEL, CHI) and southern (FRZ) geographic locations and/or also reflecting the East Friesian ancestry of the composite Frizarta sheep (see AdditIonal File 2: Figure S5A). Although previous research showed a mainland-island genetic differentiation between Mytilene (Lesvos) sheep and certain indigenous mainland populations [44], our study showed high genetic proximity between the mainland Boutsko, Frizarta, PEL3 population and the insular Mytilene sheep according to PC1 and PC2 axes (Fig. 7A). This pattern may reflect the widespread use of Mytilene sheep to upgrade mainland and mountainous populations, owing to their adaptability to marginal environments [13]. This finding aligns with an earlier study indicating close genetic relatedness between Mytilene and other mountainous breeds of Greece [45]. Alternatively, it could also be attributed to partially common ancestry as the origin of Mytilene sheep is related to crossbreds between Zackel and eastern fat-tailed sheep [9]. However, Frizarta, Pelagonia (PEL3) and Boutsko were clearly distinguished from Mytilene sheep along PC3 and PC4 axes (see Additional File 2: Figures S5A & 5B), indicating that a specific subset of SNP markers is particularly informative for their differentiation. The observed admixture patterns (K = 2–20) support these PCA findings. More specifically, Frizarta breed and PEL3 population were represented by distinct genetic composition compared to Mytilene sheep from K = 5 and onwards. Especially for PEL3 population, these results could likely reflect that, although these sheep may have historically been crossbred with the Mytilene breed, as they still share a genetic component at K = 5 (Fig. 7B), later conservation efforts potentially led to its genetic differentiation. This finding is consistent with their clustering near the Mytilene branch in the Neighbor-joining tree (see Additional File 3: Figure S1) and may indicate more recent crossbreeding, in line with the use of MYT sheep to improve the adaptability of other breeds. Within breeds, PCA also indicated farm-level clustering with close genetic relationships among farms. Especially, clear clustering was observed among farms of Boutsko, Frizarta and Chios sheep, suggesting limited genetic exchange between farms. Mytilene farms showed partial overlap, indicating a higher degree of within-breed gene flow, as also indicated by their admixture profiles.

Additionally, model-based analyses revealed patterns of differentiation across farms, reflecting local adaptation, genetic drift, or gene flow, shaped by heterogeneous flock management, production intensity, and animal exchange. Among Chios sheep, variable management practices were evident, both uniform (CHI1) and more admixed genetic profiles (CHI2, CHI3, CHI4) were depicted. In contrast, the lack of genetic differentiation among several Mytilene farms showed less intervention in animal crosses. Although multiple flocks were sampled across Lesvos Island, five out of ten populations displayed a homogeneous genetic profile, pointing to gene flow across farms in concordance with levels of reticulation observed in global-scale analysis (Delta score = 0.36). Previous study on the genomic profile of Mytilene sheep also demonstrated a common admixture pattern among the majority of the insular herds sampled [46]. These results may reflect the breeding scheme applied over recent decades by the cooperative across nearly all flocks of the island of Lesvos, which involved the systematic exchange of rams among flocks, as previously described [47]. In the case of the mountainous Boutsko sheep, although both BOU1 and BOU2 are transhumant populations migrating between northwestern mountainous and central lowland regions of Greece, BOU1 showed greater within-population heterogeneity compared to BOU2 and BOU3 populations displaying a more homogeneous genetic background. Additionally BOU1 population showed the lowest inbreeding levels compared to BOU2 and BOU3 populations. These findings possibly reflect differential management associated with vertical transhumance practices. The similar genetic profile observed in Pelagonia sheep populations PEL1, PEL2, PEL4 may reflect both the recent population decline and the subsequent conservation efforts. These factors may have also contributed to higher inbreeding levels compared with the PEL3 population, which exhibited lower inbreeding and a different admixture profile, as well as compared with the other Zackel sheep breeds included in this study. These patterns emphasize the importance of ongoing genetic monitoring at both breed and farm levels to safeguard genetic diversity and ensure the breed integrity of indigenous sheep populations.

Conclusions

Overall, the integrated analyses based on the ovine SNP50 BeadChip provide an overview of the global demographic context of Greek sheep, confirming that these populations reflect a rich mosaic of historical migration, regional gene flow, and breed development. These results underscore the importance of Greece as a key geographic corridor in early sheep domestication and highlight the local Greek sheep as a reservoir of unique and diverse livestock genetic resources. The study reveals within-breed management diversity, highlighting the need to integrate genomic SNP markers into national sheep breeding programs to preserve genetic heritage, monitor breed integrity and support sustainable management of local resources.
