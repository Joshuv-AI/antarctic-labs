Nonautonomous systems of evolution inclusions
Bernhard Aigner, Jacson Simsen, Marcus Waurick

Abstract
. We prove the existence of global solutions for some coupled systems of partially nonautonomous evolution inclusions comprised of a Cauchy problem with a compact resolvent semigroup generator and an evolution equation governed by a subdifferential of a real potential. Our system in particular includes nonautonomous generalized Schrödinger–Debye systems of inclusions with variable exponents, but extends to hyperbolic-parabolic systems of inclusions in particular to Maxwell–parabolic systems of inclusions. Methodologically, we extend an approach of Vrabie et al. to the nonautonomous case and make use of standard semigroup tools to accomodate non-parabolic behaviour of solutions paired with a new existence result for measurable selections. The combination of the latter two requires the set-valued coupling terms to be Hausdorff-continuous, to take bounded, convex and closed values, and to satisfy weak continuity with respect to one variable.

2.1.
The monotone evolution problem

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a real and separable Hilbert space. The maximal monotone operator
A(t)
for
t>0
we assume to be of
subdifferential type
, i.e.,
A(t)=\partial\phi^{t}
for some real potential
\phi^{t}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
. We briefly recap the notion of a subdifferential
\partial\phi^{t}
:

Definition 2.1.1
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a lower semicontinuous convex function. The
effective domain
of
\phi
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{dom}(\phi)

\displaystyle\coloneq\{u\in H;\phi(u)<+\infty\},

which we assume to be non-empty. For each

the set

\displaystyle\partial\phi(u)

\displaystyle\coloneq\{w\in H;\forall v\!\in\!\mathcal{H}\colon\phi(v)-\phi(u)\geq\langle w,v-u\rangle\}

is called the
subdifferential
of
\phi
at
u
and the domain of the subdifferential
\partial\phi
is defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{dom}(\partial\phi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{u\in\operatorname{dom}(\phi);\partial\phi(u)\neq\emptyset\}.

We remark that the definition of the subdifferential
\partial\phi
renders it a monotone relation. It is well-known that
\partial\phi
is maximal monotone in
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.
\bigl(1+\lambda\partial\phi\bigr)^{-1}=\mathcal{H}
for all
\lambda>0
(see
[
38
, thm. 4]
). We note that in general,
\partial\phi
need not be single-valued.

We can now formally provide our assumptions on the operator
A
, which boil down to assumptions on the potential
\phi
.

Assumptions 2.1.2
.

Let
T>0
. We assume that

(i)

There exists
Z\subseteq(0,T]
, a set of measure zero, s.t. for all

,

is lower semicontinuous, convex and has non-empty effective domain. Furthermore we assume

•

\exists\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{H}\text{ dense }\forall t\in(0,T]\!\setminus\!Z\colon\operatorname{dom}\bigl(\phi^{t}\bigr)=\mathcal{D}
.

•

\forall t\in(0,T]\!\setminus\!Z\colon\partial\phi^{t}
is single-valued.

•

\forall t\in(0,T]\!\setminus\!Z\colon\partial\phi^{t}(0)=\{0\}\text{.}

(ii)

The potentials
\phi^{t}
adhere to the following regularity condition:

Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
and set

\beta\coloneq\begin{cases}2&\text{if}\ 0\leq\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\text{,}\\
\tfrac{1}{1-\alpha}&\text{if}\ \tfrac{1}{2}\leq\alpha\leq 1\text{.}\end{cases}

Then for any

we assume there exist

•

K_{n}>0
,

•

absolutely continuous satisfying

and

•

of bounded variation s.t.

\displaystyle\forall

\displaystyle t\in[0,T]\!\setminus\!Z\,\,

\displaystyle\forall w\!\in\!\operatorname{dom}\bigl(\phi^{t}\bigr)\cap B[0,r]\,\,\forall s\!\in\![t,T]\!\setminus\!Z\,\,\exists\widetilde{w}\!\in\!\operatorname{dom}\bigl(\phi^{s}\bigr)\colon

\displaystyle\lVert\widetilde{w}-w\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\big\lvert g_{n}(s)-g_{n}(t)\big\rvert\bigl(\phi^{t}(w)+K_{n}\bigr)^{\alpha}\;\text{ and }

\displaystyle\phi^{s}(\widetilde{w})

\displaystyle\leq\phi^{t}(w)+\big\lvert h_{n}(s)-h_{n}(t)\big\rvert\bigl(\phi^{t}(w)+K_{n}\bigr)\text{.}

(2)

for some

2.2.
The Cauchy problem

For the semigroup part, we additionally recap the notion of
extrapolation

Definition 2.2.1
.

Let
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
be Hilbert spaces and let
C\colon H_{0}\supseteq\operatorname{dom}(C)\to H_{1}
be linear, densely defined and closed. We set

\displaystyle C^{\diamond}

\displaystyle\colon H_{1}\to\operatorname{dom}(C)^{\prime}\text{,}

\displaystyle(C^{\diamond}\varphi)(x)

\displaystyle\!\coloneq\langle\varphi,Cx\rangle_{H_{1}}\qquad\varphi\in H_{1},x\in\operatorname{dom}(C)\text{.}

We call

For an introduction to and properties of extrapolation spaces we refer to
[
40

Lemma 2.2.2

(Solution of the semigroup part;
[
34
, sec. 4.2]
)
.

For
T_{m}>0
,
u_{0}\in H
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T_{m};H)
, the problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}u+Eu&=f&&\text{on}\ (0,T_{m})\text{,}\\

has a unique (mild) solution

for any

This result is well-known; the estimate follows from a simple application of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the contiuity can be obtain by substracting

and using standard estimates as well as the strong continuity of

Remark 2.2.3
.

One way to prove the representation formula via Duhamel’s principle is to use the extrapolation space

and realise that then

(an extension of

) on
\mathrm{H}^{-1}(E)
, and

; see, e.g.,
[

for extrapolation of generators and their semigroups. Hence, the argument in
[

applies to obtain the claimed variation of constants formula, i.e., the validity of Duhamel’s principle.

3.
Selections of multivalued maps

3.1.
Properties of multivalued maps

To formulate the necessary conditions for the right-hand sides
F
and
G
in (
1
), we recall several key notions for multivalued maps. We write
\mathcal{P}(M)
for the power set of a set
M
.

Definition 3.1.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and
U
a topological space.

•

A mapping
G\colon U\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(X)
is called
upper semicontinuous (weakly upper semicontinuous)
at
u\in U
, if for each open (relatively weakly open) subset
D
in
X
satisfying
G(u)\subseteq D
, there exists a neighbourhood
V
of
u
s.t.
G(v)\subseteq D
for each
v\in V
.

If
G
is upper semicontinuous (weakly upper semicontinuous) at each
u\in U
, then it is called
upper semicontinuous (weakly upper semicontinuous)
on
U
.

•

A function
f\colon U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}
is called
lower semicontinuous
at a point
u\in U
if for every real
y<f(u)
there exists a neighbourhood
V
of
u
s.t.
f(v)>y
for each
v\in V
.

Clearly, each upper semicontinuous map
G\colon U\to\mathcal{P}(X)
is weakly upper semicontinuous. Furthermore, if
G
is a univalued map (i.e., the images of singletons are singletons), it is upper semicontinuous (weakly upper semicontinuous) on
U
if and only if it is a continuous (weakly continuous) map on
U
in the usual sense.

Often times, continuity properties alone do not prove to be enough for technical reasons and we will make use of the following terminology.

Definition 3.1.2
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and
U
a topological space. A mapping
G\colon U\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(X)
is called
bcc
if for every
u\in U
, the image
G(u)
is nonempty, bounded, closed and convex.

We remark that this property is sometimes included in the notion of semicontinuity, e.g., in
[
46
, def. 3.1.3]
.

Definition 3.1.3
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2},H
be Hilbert spaces and
F\colon H_{1}\times H_{2}\to\mathcal{P}(H)
.

•

F
is called
bounded
if it maps bounded subsets of
H_{1}\times H_{2}
into bounded subsets of
H
.

•

F
is said to satisfy a
linear growth bound
if

\exists a,b,c\!>\!0\,\forall(u,v)\!\in\!H_{1}\!\times\!H_{2}\,\forall f\!\in\!F(u,v)\colon\lVert f\rVert_{H}\leq a\lVert u\rVert_{H_{1}}+b\lVert v\rVert_{H_{2}}+c.

Remark 3.1.4
.

Definition

3.1.3
provides a traditional linear growth bound that we will use for our existence result in
section

5.3
, i.e.,
theorem

5.3.2
. We further comment that the notion of “positive sublinearity” used in
[
42
]
, that was introduced in
[
46
, def. 3.2.5]
, where the linear growth bound only has to hold outside of a ball around the origin and under certain dual pairing conditions, is too weak for our purposes and seems of pure mathematical convenience anyway, unifying a “usual sublinear growth condition” and a “sign condition”. To us it is unclear when such a weakening is actually necessary or practical in applications.

The overal general set-up applies to the semigroup side of problem (
1
) without maximal regularity. This however was the key element why earlier approaches worked. To overcome this, we ask for stronger continuity conditions on the set-valued side. For that purpose we recall the notion of Hausdorff-distance next.

Definition 3.1.5
.

For a Banach space
X
and subsets
A,B\in\mathcal{P}(X)
we define the
Hausdorff-distance
as

d_{\mathrm{Hd}}(A,B)\coloneq\max\bigl\{\underset{a\in A\,b\in B}{\mathrm{sup}\;\mathrm{inf}}d(a,b),\underset{b\in B\,a\in A}{\mathrm{sup}\;\mathrm{inf}}d(a,b)\bigr\}.

The Haudorff-distance defines a semi-metric on the set of all bounded subsets of
X
. If both sets
A,B
are (bounded and) closed then
d_{\mathrm{Hd}}(A,B)=0
implies
A=B
, see
[
23
, §28]
.
We further introduce

\mathcal{BC}(X)\coloneqq\{A\in\mathcal{P}(X);A\text{ bounded, closed, non-empty}\}.

A bcc map
F\colon X\to\mathcal{P}(X)
is called
Hausdorff-continuous
, if
F
is continuous as a mapping from
(X,\|\cdot\|)
to
(\mathcal{BC}(X),d_{\mathrm{Hd}})
.

Remark 3.1.6
.

It is easy to verify that any bcc map, which is Hausdorff-continuous is indeed upper semicontinuous.

3.2.
Selections

Since we investigate the multivalued problem (
1
), we have to introduce the key notion of
selections
for solvability. For the purposes of this subsection, let
X
be a Banach space and
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

, a measurable subset.

Definition 3.2.1
.

A map
F\colon A\to\mathcal{P}(X)
is called

if for each closed subset

the preimage

F^{-1}(C)=\{y\in A;F(y)\cap C\neq\emptyset\}

is Lebesgue measurable.

A

of

, is a function
f\colon A\to X
with
f(y)\in F(y)
for almost every

. We denote

{\swabfamily Sel}(F)=\{f;f\text{ measurable selection of }F\}.

If
F
is a univalued map, the definition of measurability is equivalent to the usual definition of a measurable function. We note that
{\swabfamily Sel}(F)
may be empty. Thus, we require criteria to find suitable selections, which we present next.

Lemma 3.2.2

(Kuratowski–Ryll–Nardzewski,
[
46
, thm. 3.1.1]
)
.

If
X
is separable and
F\colon A\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(X)
is measurable s.t. for a.e.

,

is nonempty and closed, then
{\swabfamily Sel}(F)\neq\emptyset
.

If
U
,
X
,
M
are sets and

,
F\colon U\to\mathcal{P}(X)
, we abbreviate

F(u)\coloneqq F\circ u\colon M\to\mathcal{P}(X).

Theorem 3.2.3

(
[
46
, thm. 3.1.2]
)
.

Let
U
be a topological space and let
\emptyset\neq M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded and Lebesgue-measurable,

. Let
F\colon U\to\mathcal{P}(X)
be weakly upper semicontinuous. Assume

and
f_{n}\in{\swabfamily Sel}F(u_{n})
for

satisfy

f_{n}\rightharpoonup\colon f\quad\text{in}\ \mathrm{L}^{1}(M;X)\qquad\text{and}\qquad u_{n}(y)\to u(y)\quad\text{a.e.\ in}\ M\text{.}

Then

.

3.3.
Approximation of selections

In this part we provide a result regarding availability of selections close enough in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-norm, which — to the best of our knowledge — is new. The result reads as follows.

Theorem 3.3.1
.

Let
H
be a separable Hilbert space. Let
F\colon H\to\mathcal{P}(H)
be bcc and Hausdorff-continuous. Let
(u_{n})_{n}
in
\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T];H\bigr)
converge to
u\in\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T];H\bigr)
. Then

\forall\varepsilon\!>\!0\,\exists n_{0}\!\in\!\mathbb{N}\,\forall n\!\geq\!n_{0},f\in{\swabfamily Sel}F(u)\,\exists f_{n}\!\in\!{\swabfamily Sel}F(u_{n})\colon\lVert f_{n}-f\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;H)}\leq\varepsilon.

The proof of
theorem

3.3.1
requires a lot of preparation. Partly this is due to the fact that Hausdorff-continuity in inifinite-dimensional contexts appears to be a non-standard setting. The reason for that might be that often times compactness is used as a standard assumption for the sets involved, which we choose to avoid here. Yet, the Hilbert space structure helps a lot in overcoming potential difficulties. For convenience of the reader, we provide a rather detailed presentation of the subtleties in this context.

In the following remark, we recall some standard properties of the metric projection in Hilbert spaces.

Remark 3.3.2
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and
C\subseteq H
be non-empty, closed and convex.

(i)

The
metric projection
given by

p_{C}\colon H\to C,\quad x\mapsto\mathrm{argmin}_{c\in C}\lVert c-x\rVert_{H}

is well-defined and Lipschitz-continuous with Lipschitz-constant
1
, see
[
7
, p. 142]
.

(ii)

For all
x\in H
and
y\in H
we have
y=p_{C}(x)
if and only if (again see
[
7
]
)

\forall r\in C\colon\langle x-y,r-y\rangle\leq 0.

(iii)

If
D\subseteq H
is non-empty, bounded, closed, and convex, then for
x\in H

\lVert p_{C}(x)-p_{D}(x)\rVert\leq\sqrt{(4\|x\|+2R)d_{\mathrm{Hd}}(C,D)},

where
R>0
is a common bound for both
C
and
D
.

Indeed, let
\varepsilon>d_{\mathrm{Hd}}(C,D)
,
x\in H
. Then we find
p\in C
and
q\in D
s.t.

\max\{\|q-p_{C}(x)\|,\|p-p_{D}(x)\|\}<\varepsilon.

\displaystyle\qquad\big\lVert p_{C}(x)\!-\!p_{D}(x)\big\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\big\langle x\!-\!p_{D}(x)-\big(x\!-\!p_{C}(x)\big),p_{C}(x)\!-\!p_{D}(x)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle x\!-\!p_{D}(x),p_{C}(x)\!-\!p_{D}(x)\big\rangle+\big\langle x\!-\!p_{C}(x),p_{D}(x)\!-\!p_{C}(x)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle x\!-\!p_{D}(x),p_{C}(x)\!-\!q\big\rangle+\big\langle x\!-\!p_{D}(x),q\!-\!p_{D}(x)\big\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\big\langle x\!-\!p_{C}(x),p_{D}(x)\!-\!p\big\rangle+\big\langle x\!-\!p_{C}(x),p\!-\!p_{C}(x)\big\rangle

\displaystyle\mathop{\leq}^{\ref{it:ProjCrit}}_{\text{Cauchy--Schwarz}}\big\lVert x\!-\!p_{D}(x)\big\rVert\varepsilon+\big\lVert x\!-\!p_{C}(x)\big\rVert\varepsilon.

Finally, from

\displaystyle\lVert x-p_{D}(x)\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\|x\|+\|p_{D}(0)\|+\|p_{D}(x)-p_{D}(0)\|\leq 2\|x\|+\|p_{D}(0)\|,

and similarly,

\displaystyle\lVert x-p_{C}(x)\rVert

\displaystyle\leq 2\|x\|+R,

it follows that

\lVert p_{C}(x)-p_{D}(x)\rVert\leq\sqrt{(4\|x\|+2R)d_{\mathrm{Hd}}(C,D)}.

Next, we recall a well-known fact on the characterisation of compact subsets of metric spaces.

Remark 3.3.3
.

Let
X
be a metric space. Then
X
is compact, if and only if,
X
is complete and
pre-compact
(i.e.,
totally bounded
: for all
\varepsilon>0
there is a finite set
G\subseteq X
such that
X=\bigcup_{r\in G}B(r,\varepsilon)
). Note that, as
X
is metric,
X
is compact if and only if it is sequentially compact. This observation serves as a sensible step to show the equivalence.

Lemma 3.3.4
.

Let
M
be a set,
X
a complete metric space,
\phi\colon M\to X
and
\phi_{n}\colon M\to X
s.t.
\phi_{n}[M]
is pre-compact for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
\phi_{n}\to\phi
uniformly, then

\overline{\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\phi_{n}[M]}\subseteq X\text{ is compact.}

Proof.

Before we come to the proof, we require the following observation
(i)
.

(i)

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\colon M\to X
and
G\subseteq X
with
\psi_{1}[M]\subseteq\bigcup_{r\in G}B(r,\varepsilon)
. If
\sup_{x\in X}d_{X}\bigl(\psi_{1}(x),\psi_{2}(x)\bigr)\leq\varepsilon
, then

\psi_{2}[M]\subseteq\bigcup_{r\in G}B(r,2\varepsilon).

(ii)

Next, we claim that
\phi[M]
is pre-compact.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then uniform convergence yields
n\in\mathbb{N}
s.t.
d_{X}\bigl(\phi(x),\phi_{n}(x)\bigr)<\varepsilon
for all
x\in M
. Since
\phi_{n}[M]
is pre-compact, we find
G\subseteq X
finite with
\phi_{n}[M]\subseteq\bigcup_{r\in G}B(r,\varepsilon)
. Hence, by our previous observation applied to
\psi_{1}=\phi_{n}
and
\psi_{2}=\phi
,

\phi[M]\subseteq\bigcup_{r\in G}B(r,2\varepsilon)

and
\phi[M]
is pre-compact.

(iii)

Next, we show the actual claim. Note that both the finite union of pre-compact sets and the closure of a pre-compact set is pre-compact again. Hence, by
remark

3.3.3
and since closed subsets of complete metric spaces are complete, it suffices to show that
\bigcup_{n\geq n_{0}}\phi_{n}[M]
is pre-compact for some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
. For this let
\varepsilon>0
. Since
\phi[M]\subseteq X
is pre-compact, we find
G\subseteq X
finite with
\phi[M]\subseteq\bigcup_{r\in G}B(r,\varepsilon)
. By uniform convergence, we find
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
s.t. for all
n\geq n_{0}
and
x\in M
we have

d_{X}\bigl(\phi(x),\phi_{n}(x)\bigr)<\varepsilon.

By observation
(i)
, applied to
\psi_{1}=\phi
and
\psi_{2}=\phi_{n}
for any
n\geq n_{0}
, we deduce

\phi_{n}[M]\subseteq\bigcup_{r\in G}B(r,2\varepsilon);\text{ hence, }\bigcup_{n\geq n_{0}}\phi_{n}[M]\subseteq\bigcup_{r\in G}B(r,2\varepsilon)

and the claim follows.∎

Lemma 3.3.5
.

Let
X
be a compact topological space,
Y,Z
be metric spaces,
Y
complete and let
(u_{n})_{n}\in\mathcal{C}(X;Y)^{\mathbb{N}}
converge to
u\in\mathcal{C}(X;Y)
, where the metric in
\mathcal{C}(X;Y)
is given by

d_{\mathcal{C}(X;Y)}(v,w)\coloneqq\sup_{x\in X}d_{Y}\bigl(v(x),w(x)\bigr).

If
F\colon Y\to Z
is continuous, then
F\circ u_{n}\to F\circ u
in
\mathcal{C}(X;Z)
.

Proof.

We proceed in two steps:

(i)

At first we show the claim under the additional assumption that
F
is uniformly continuous. Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then we find
\delta>0
such that whenever
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
satisfy
d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})\leq\delta
, we infer
d_{Z}\big(F(y_{1}),F(y_{2})\big)\leq\varepsilon
. By assumption, there is
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
s.t. for all
n\geq n_{0}
, we have

d_{\mathcal{C}(X;Y)}(u_{n},u)=\sup_{x\in X}d_{Y}\bigl(u_{n}(x),u(x)\bigr)\leq\delta.

Hence, for
n\geq n_{0}

d_{\mathcal{C}(X;Z)}(F\circ u_{n},F\circ u)=\sup_{x\in X}d_{Z}\bigl(F(u_{n}(x)),F(u(x))\bigr)\leq\varepsilon.

(ii)

For the general case, as
X
is compact, it follows that
u_{n}[X]\subseteq Y
is compact. In particular, it is pre-compact by
remark

3.3.3
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
u_{n}\to u
uniformly and
Y
is complete, it follows that

\widetilde{Y}\coloneqq\overline{\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}u_{n}[X]}\subseteq Y

is compact. Note in passing that
u[X]\subseteq\widetilde{Y}
. Finally,
\widetilde{F}\coloneqq F|_{\widetilde{Y}}
is uniformly continuous as it is a continuous mapping of metric spaces defined on a compact set. Moreover, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
F\circ u_{n}=\widetilde{F}\circ u_{n}
and
F\circ u=\widetilde{F}\circ u
, by construction. Hence,
(i)
applies to
\widetilde{F}\circ u_{n}
and the claim follows.∎

Next, we provide some results anticipating the subtleties of discontinuity of the intersection for Hausdorff-continuity.

Lemma 3.3.6
.

Let
F\colon H\to\mathcal{P}(H)
be bcc and Hausdorff-continuous,
y\in H
,
\varepsilon>0
. Then there is
\delta>0
s.t. for all
z\in B(y,\delta)
and
x\in F(y)
:

B(x,\varepsilon)\cap F(z)\neq\emptyset.

Proof.

Since
F
is Hausdorff-continuous, we find
\delta>0
s.t. for all
z\in B(y,\delta)
, we have
d_{\mathrm{Hd}}(F(y),F(z))<\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{2}}
. Let
x\in F(y)
. Then
\operatorname{dist}\bigl(x,F(z)\bigr)\leq d_{\mathrm{Hd}}\bigl(F(y),F(z)\bigr)
and we find
x^{\prime}\in F(z)
such that

\|x^{\prime}-x\|\leq\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{2}}.

In particular,
x^{\prime}\in B(x,\varepsilon)\cap F(z)
.
∎

The next result is an infinite-dimensional analogue of linear regularity for convex sets, see, e.g.,
[
4
]
.

Lemma 3.3.7
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space,
A,B\subseteq H
closed, convex and bounded. Assume there exists
x_{0}\in A\cap B
and
\rho>0
s.t.
B[x_{0},\rho]\subseteq B
.
Then for all
x\in H

\operatorname{dist}(x,A\cap B)\leq\big(1+\tfrac{d}{\rho}\big)\big(\operatorname{dist}(x,A)+\operatorname{dist}(x,B)\big),

where
d\coloneqq\operatorname{diam}(A\cup B)
.

Proof.

Let
x\in H
and define
a\coloneqq p_{A}(x)
, the metric projection of
x
onto
A
. It follows that
\operatorname{dist}(x,A)=\|x-a\|
. For
\lambda\in[0,1]
define

z_{\lambda}\coloneqq(1-\lambda)a+\lambda x_{0}\in A,

by convexity of
A
and introduce

B_{\lambda}\coloneqq(1-\lambda)B+\lambda B[x_{0},\rho].

Since
B[x_{0},\rho]\subseteq B
and
B
is convex, we infer
B_{\lambda}\subseteq B
. Next,
we compute

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(z_{\lambda},B)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{dist}(z_{\lambda},B_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\inf_{b\in B,u\in B[0,1]}\big\lVert z_{\lambda}-\big((1-\lambda)b+\lambda(x_{0}+\rho u)\big)\big\rVert

\displaystyle=\inf_{b\in B,u\in B[0,1]}\big\lVert(1-\lambda)a+\lambda x_{0}-\big((1-\lambda)b+\lambda(x_{0}+\rho u)\big)\big\rVert

\displaystyle=\inf_{b\in B,u\in B[0,1]}\|(1-\lambda)(a-b)-\lambda\rho u\|.

For fixed
b\in B
, if
\lVert(1-\lambda)(a-b)\rVert\leq\lambda\rho
, then

\inf_{u\in B[0,1]}\big\lVert(1-\lambda)(a-b)-\lambda\rho u\big\rVert=0.

If
\lVert(1-\lambda)(a-b)\rVert>\lambda\rho
, then solving the minimisation problem for fixed
b
in
u
yields

\displaystyle\inf_{u\in B[0,1]}\big\lVert(1-\lambda)(a-b)-\lambda\rho u\big\rVert

\displaystyle=\Big\lVert(1-\lambda)(a-b)-\lambda\rho\tfrac{(a-b)}{\|a-b\|}\Big\rVert

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\|a-b\|-\lambda\rho.

Thus, in any case,

\operatorname{dist}(z_{\lambda},B)\leq\inf_{b\in B}\max\big\{0,(1-\lambda)\|a-b\|-\lambda\rho\big\}=\max\big\{0,(1-\lambda)\operatorname{dist}(a,B)-\lambda\rho\big\}.

Choose
0<\lambda=\frac{\operatorname{dist}(a,B)}{\operatorname{dist}(a,B)+\rho}<1
. Then
z_{\lambda}\in B
. Hence, we compute

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(x,A\cap B)

\displaystyle\leq\|x-z_{\lambda}\|\leq\|x-a\|+\|a-z_{\lambda}\|

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}(x,A)+\lambda\|a-x_{0}\|

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}(x,A)+\frac{\operatorname{dist}(a,B)}{\operatorname{dist}(a,B)+\rho}\|a-x_{0}\|

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{dist}(x,A)+\frac{\operatorname{dist}(a,B)}{\operatorname{dist}(a,B)+\rho}d;

with
\|a-x\|=\operatorname{dist}(x,A)
and, thus,
\operatorname{dist}(a,B)\leq\operatorname{dist}(x,A)+\operatorname{dist}(x,B)
we obtain

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{dist}(x,A)+\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x,A)+\operatorname{dist}(x,B)}{\operatorname{dist}(a,B)+\rho}d

\displaystyle\leq\big(1+\tfrac{d}{\rho}\big)\big(\operatorname{dist}(x,A)+\operatorname{dist}(x,B)\big).\qed

In certain situations, the intersection is continuous w.r.t. Hausdorff-convergence, as the next result confirms.

Lemma 3.3.8
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space,
(c_{n})_{n}
a convergent sequence in
H
,
c\coloneqq\lim c_{n}
, and
(B_{n})_{n}
a sequence of closed, bounded, convex subsets of
H
converging to some bounded, closed, convex, non-empty
B\subseteq H
w.r.t.
d_{\mathrm{Hd}}
,
r>0
. If
B(c,r)\cap B\neq\emptyset
, then
B[c_{n},r]\cap B_{n}\to B[c,r]\cap B
w.r.t.
d_{\mathrm{Hd}}
.

Proof.

We put
A_{n}\coloneqq B[c_{n},r]
and
A\coloneqq B[c,r]
.
Denote
d\coloneqq\sup_{n}(\operatorname{diam}(A_{n}\cup B_{n}))
. Since
B
is bounded and
B_{n}\to B
in the Hausdorff-metric, we find
\varepsilon>0
s.t.
B_{n}\subseteq B+\varepsilon B[0,1]
for eventually all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, by boundedness of each individual
B_{n}
and from

A_{n}\subseteq\bigcup_{x\in H,\|x\|\leq\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|c_{n}\|}B[x,r]\subseteq B[0,r+\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|c_{n}\|]

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we infer
d<\infty
. Note that we also showed that we find
R>0
such that
\bigcup_{n}A_{n}\cup B_{n}\cup A\cup B\subseteq B[0,R]
.

We define
K_{n}\coloneqq A_{n}\cap B_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
K\coloneqq A\cap B
.

(i)

At first, we show that
\sup_{x_{n}\in K_{n}}\operatorname{dist}(x_{n},K)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

For
x_{n}\in K_{n}=A_{n}\cap B_{n}
we estimate

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(x_{n},A)

\displaystyle=\inf_{u\in B[0,1]}\big\lVert x_{n}-(c+ru)\big\rVert=\max\big\{0,\|x_{n}-c\|-r\big\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\big\{0,\|x_{n}-c_{n}\|+\|c_{n}-c\|-r\big\}

\displaystyle\leq\|c_{n}-c\|

and
\operatorname{dist}(x_{n},B)\leq d_{\mathrm{Hd}}(B_{n},B)
.
Thus, by
lemma

3.3.7
, we infer

\operatorname{dist}(x_{n},K)\leq\big(1+\tfrac{d}{r}\big)\big(\|c_{n}-c\|+d_{\mathrm{Hd}}(B_{n},B)\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(ii)

It remains to prove
\sup_{x\in K}\operatorname{dist}(x,K_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Let
x_{0}\in B(c,r)\cap B
and
\rho>0
be s.t.
B(x_{0},\rho)\subseteq B(c,r)
. Next, let
\lambda\in(0,1]
and define for
x\in K=A\cap B

x_{\lambda}=(1-\lambda)x+\lambda x_{0}\in B,

by convexity of
B
.
From
\|x-c\|\leq r
and

\|c-x_{0}\|+\rho=\Big\lVert c-x_{0}+\tfrac{\rho(c-x_{0})}{\|c-x_{0}\|}\Big\rVert\mathop{\leq}_{B(x_{0},\rho)\subseteq B(c,r)}r

we infer
\|x_{0}-c\|\leq r-\rho
. Consequently,

\|x_{\lambda}-c\|\leq(1-\lambda)r+\lambda r-\lambda\rho=r-\lambda\rho.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, by convexity and closedness of
B_{n}
, we find
z_{n}(x)\in B_{n}
s.t.

\|z_{n}(x)-x_{\lambda}\|\leq d_{\mathrm{Hd}}(B_{n},B).

Then

\displaystyle\|z_{n}(x)-c_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|z_{n}(x)-x_{\lambda}\|+\|x_{\lambda}-c\|+\|c-c_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathrm{Hd}}(B_{n},B)+r-\lambda\rho+\|c-c_{n}\|.

We find
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
s.t.
d_{\mathrm{Hd}}(B_{n},B)+\|c-c_{n}\|\leq\lambda\rho
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Thus, for
n\geq n_{0}
,
\|z_{n}(x)-c_{n}\|\leq r
and, hence,
z_{n}(x)\in K_{n}
.

Next, as

\quad\operatorname{dist}(x,K_{n})\leq\|x\!-\!z_{n}(x)\|\leq\|x\!-\!x_{\lambda}\|+\|x_{\lambda}\!-\!z_{n}(x)\|\leq 2\lambda R+d_{\mathrm{Hd}}(B_{n},B),

we infer

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\in K}\operatorname{dist}(x,K_{n})\leq 2\lambda R.

As
\lambda\in(0,1]
was arbitrary, we deduce
\sup_{x\in K}\operatorname{dist}(x,K_{n})\to 0
, which proves the desired Hausdorff-convergence.∎

Before we finally turn to the proof of our main technical result on approximation of selections we recall Lusin’s theorem.

Theorem 3.3.9

(Lusin’s theorem)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, be compact and endowed with the measure
\mu
induced by the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
X
be a separable metric space and
f\colon U\to X
measurable. Then

\forall\varepsilon\!>\!0\,\,\exists K\!\subseteq\!U\,\text{compact}\colon\mu\bigl(U\!\setminus\!K\bigr)\!<\!\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad f|_{K}\,\text{is continuous}.

For a proof of Lusin’s theorem for functions
f\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
we refer to
[
11
, thm. 7.4.3]
. The generalization is proven by standard tools. In our case, we will use
U=[0,1]
, and
X=H
a Hilbert space. In this case, the corresponding result can be obtained from
[
11
, thm. 7.4.3]
using an orthonormal basis. For this, note that, by Pettis’ theorem (see, e.g.,
[
40
, thm. 3.1.10]
), a measurable
f\colon[0,1]\to H
is characterised by being a.e. separably valued (i.e., there is a set of measure zero
Z\subseteq[0,1]
so that
f\big[[0,1]\!\setminus\!Z\big]
is separable) and weakly measurable (i.e., for all
x^{\prime}\in H
,
\langle f(\cdot),x^{\prime}\rangle
is measurable; hence admitting a Borel measurable representative).

Proof of
theorem

3.3.1
.

Note that all mentioned sets of selections are nonempty appealing to
lemma

3.2.2
: First observe that
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;H)
is separable since
H
is and that Hausdorff-continuity of
F
implies upper semicontinuity, hence implies measurability. Together these facts assure applicability of
lemma

3.2.2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
As
u_{n}\to u
uniformly (i.e., in
\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T];H\bigr)
), by
lemma

3.3.5
, we deduce
F\circ u_{n}\to F\circ u
uniformly (where
F
takes values in
\bigl(\mathcal{BC}(H),d_{\mathrm{Hd}}\bigr)
). By
lemma

3.3.6
(applied to
F
and
y=u(t)
) and compactness of the closure of the union of the images of
u_{n}
(see
lemma

3.3.4
), we find
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
s.t. for all
t\in[0,T]
and
f\in{\swabfamily Sel}F(u)
,
B\big(f(t),\varepsilon\big)\cap F\big(u_{n}(t)\big)\neq\emptyset
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Hence, for all
t\in[0,T]
,
f\in{\swabfamily Sel}F(u)
,

\chi_{n}(t)\coloneq\bigl\{x\in F\big(u_{n}(t)\big);\lVert x-f(t)\rVert_{H}\leq\varepsilon\bigr\}

becomes non-empty eventually for
n
large enough (uniformly in
f
and
t\in[0,T]
). Let
f\in{\swabfamily Sel}F(u)
and define
\chi_{n}(t)
as above.

Because
F
is bcc, the sets
\chi_{n}(t)
are convex (as intersection of two convex sets) and (weakly) closed by Mazur’s lemma, because both individual sets are strongly closed.

Since
H
is a Hilbert space, we can define the pointwise bestapproximation

\pi_{n}\colon[0,T]\ni t\mapsto\mathrm{argmin}_{x\in\chi_{n}(t)}\lVert x-f(t)\rVert_{H}.

To verify that
\pi_{n}
is measurable, we will first show that
\pi_{n}
is continuous when restricted to large sets, hence rendering it a measurable function on such sets. Denoting the Lebesgue measure by
\lambda
, we proceed as follows:

1.

For some
0<\delta<1
let
K_{1}
be the compact set from Lusin’s
theorem

3.3.9
that renders
f
continuous on
K_{1}
and satisfies
\lambda\big([0,T]\!\setminus\!K_{1}\big)<\delta
.

2.

We can now iteratively start with
K_{k}
and find a compact set
K_{k+1}
s.t.
f
is continuous on
K_{k+1}
and
\lambda\bigl([0,T]\!\setminus\!\bigcup_{1\leq l\leq k+1}K_{l}\bigr)<\delta^{k+1}
.

3.

We define
K\coloneq\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}K_{k}
, which is a measurable set. By construction,

and
f
is continuous on any restriction to some

. We note, that
K
is dense in
[0,T]
, since otherwise
[0,T]\!\setminus\!K
would need to contain an interval, hence having positive measure.

4.

\chi_{n}|_{K_{k}}
is continuous for
n
large enough:

By construction, for all
t\in K_{k}
,

B\big(f(t),\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{3}}\big)\cap F(u(t))\neq\emptyset.

Since
f|_{K_{k}}
, and
F\circ u
are continuous, by
lemma

3.3.8
, it follows that

B[f(s),\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{3}}]\cap F(u(s))\to B[f(t),\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{3}}]\cap F(u(t))\quad\text{as}\ s\to t.

Thus, there exists
\eta>0
s.t. for

with

we have

B[f(s),\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{3}}]\cap F(u(s))\neq\emptyset.

Similarly, since
F\circ u_{n}\to F\circ u
uniformly, we find
n_{0}(t)\in\mathbb{N}
s.t.

B[f(s),\nicefrac{{2\varepsilon}}{{3}}]\cap F(u_{n}(s))\neq\emptyset

for all

with

for all

. Since

s.t. for all

and

. In particular,
\chi_{n}(t)\neq\emptyset
for all

and, by

3.3.8
,

K_{k}\ni t\mapsto B[f(t),\varepsilon]\cap F(u_{n}(t))=\chi_{n}(t)

is continuous in the Hausdorff-distance.

5.

Next, we argue that
\pi_{n}|_{K_{k}}
is continuous. For this, we recall from
remark

3.3.2
that

\displaystyle p_{C}

\displaystyle\colon H

\displaystyle\to C,

\displaystyle\quad x

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathrm{argmin}_{c\in C}\lVert c-x\rVert_{H}

is Lipschitz-continuous for any fixed, nonempty, closed and convex subset

. We define

\displaystyle p_{k,C}

\displaystyle\colon K_{k}

\displaystyle\to C,

\displaystyle\quad t

\displaystyle\mapsto p_{C}(f(t)).

Note that
f[K_{k}]
, as the continuous image of a compact set, is bounded. Then we find
R>0
such that

\chi_{n}[K_{k}]\subseteq\bigcup_{y\in f[K_{k}]}B(y,2\varepsilon)\subseteq B[0,R].

Finally, let
t,s\in K_{k}
, then appealing to
item

(iii)
of
remark

3.3.2
, we estimate

\displaystyle\qquad\lVert\pi_{n}(t)\!-\!\pi_{n}(s)\rVert

\displaystyle=\|p_{k,\chi_{n}(t)}(t)-p_{k,\chi_{n}(s)}(s)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|p_{k,\chi_{n}(t)}(t)-p_{k,\chi_{n}(t)}(s)\|+\|p_{k,\chi_{n}(t)}(s)-p_{k,\chi_{n}(s)}(s)\|

\displaystyle=\|p_{\chi_{n}(t)}(f(t))-p_{\chi_{n}(t)}(f(s))\|+\|p_{\chi_{n}(t)}(f(s))-p_{\chi_{n}(s)}(f(s))\|

\displaystyle\leq\|f(t)-f(s)\|+\sqrt{(4\|f(s)\|+2R)d_{\mathrm{Hd}}(\chi_{n}(t),\chi_{n}(s))}.

By our previous observation, the latter tends to
0
, as
t\to s
.

6.

Iterating this process, we retain
\pi_{n}\colon[0,T]\to H
as a pointwise limit of measurable functions, rendering it a measurable function itself (see
[
40
, prop. 3.1.3]
).

It remains to show the desired inequality: Indeed, since
\pi_{n}
satisfies the pointwise estimate
\lVert\pi_{n}(t)-f(t)\rVert_{H}\leq\varepsilon
for almost all
t\in[0,T]
, for the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-norm we obtain:
\lVert\pi_{n}-f\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;H)}\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{T}
. In particular,
\pi_{n}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;H)
.
∎

Remark 3.3.10
.

Note that a close inspection of the proof reveals that the theorem statement can be replaced by the following: For all

there is
\delta>0
s.t. for all

and

4.
Uniform convergence of solutions

To establish local well-posedness of the coupled problem (
1
), it will become necessary to establish uniform convergence of solutions
u_{n}
and
v_{n}
of the decoupled problems

\begin{cases}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}u+Eu\hskip-6.45831pt&=f_{n}\\
\hfill u(0)\hskip-6.45831pt&=u_{0}\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad\begin{cases}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}v+A(\cdot)v\hskip-6.45831pt&=g_{n}\\
\hfill v(0)\hskip-6.45831pt&=v_{0}\end{cases}

associated to some suitable right-hand sides
f_{n}
and
g_{n}
respectively. Accordingly, we split this section into two parts.

4.1.
Uniform convergence of semigroup solutions

For technical reasons, we will require uniform convergence of solutions
u_{n}
to the problem

(3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}u_{n}+Eu_{n}&=f_{n}&&\text{on}\ (0,T_{0})\text{,}\\
u_{n}(0)&=u_{0}&&\text{in}\ H\text{.}\end{aligned}\right.

This problem was touched upon in
[
30
, sec. 3]
for the special case where
-E
is the generator of the Schrödinger semigroup. There it appears to have been wrongly claimed that the solutions of problem (
3
) adhere to the concept of maximal regularity. This section elaborates on the subject and showcases an example, where Lipschitz-continuity of solutions fails and hence, a straigtforward application of the Arzelà–Ascoli-theorem to obtain a continuous limit solution fails. We also present a result that salvages the convergence under (necessarily) stricter assumptions.

Example 4.1.1
.

Let
\Delta
be the Dirichlet–Laplacian on
H=\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\pi)
with
\operatorname{dom}(\Delta)=\mathrm{H}^{2}(0,\pi)\cap\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(0,\pi)
. We denote by
S
the semigroup generated by
\mathrm{i}\Delta
. We construct
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\pi)

so that
t\mapsto S(t)f
is not Lipschitz-continuous. It is well-known that the normalized eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of
\Delta
are

\phi_{n}(x)=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\sin(nx),\qquad\lambda_{n}=-n^{2},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence,
S(t)\phi_{n}=e^{-\mathrm{i}tn^{2}}\phi_{n}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
a_{n}\coloneqq(1+n^{2})^{-3/4}
and let
f\coloneq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\,\phi_{n}\in L^{2}(0,\pi)
. Since

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{2}a_{n}^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{4}(1+n^{2})^{-3/2}\sim\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n=+\infty,

f\notin\operatorname{dom}(-\Delta)
.

Let
u\coloneqq S(\cdot)f
.
By orthogonality of the eigenbasis,

\lVert u(t)-u(0)\rVert_{H}^{2}=\lVert u(t)-f\rVert_{H}^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|e^{-itn^{2}}-1|^{2}\,a_{n}^{2}.

We find
c>0
such that, for
\lvert\theta\rvert\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
,

\lvert e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}-1\rvert\geq c\lvert\theta\rvert.

Hence, for
\lvert t\rvert>0
and integers
1\leq n\leq N(t)\coloneqq\bigl\lfloor(2\lvert t\rvert)^{-1/2}\bigr\rfloor
, we have
\lvert t\rvert n^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
and

\lvert\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tn^{2}}-1\rvert\geq c\lvert t\rvert n^{2}.

Thus with
N(t)\simeq(2|t|)^{-1/2}
,

\displaystyle\lVert u(t)-f\rVert_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq c^{2}\lvert t\rvert^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N(t)}n^{4}a_{n}^{2}=c^{2}\lvert t\rvert^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N(t)}n^{4}(1+n^{2})^{-3/2}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\lvert t\rvert^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N(t)}n\sim\lvert t\rvert^{2}N(t)^{2}\simeq\lvert t\rvert.

Consequently,

\frac{\lVert u(t)-u(0)\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}}{\lvert t\rvert}\gtrsim\lvert t\rvert^{-1/2}\xrightarrow[t\to 0]{}\infty\text{.}

In particular,
u
is not Lipschitz-continuous.

In the following, when not expressed differently explicitly, we endow
\operatorname{dom}(E)
for a closed linear operator
E
with its graph scalar product rendering it a Hilbert space.

Lemma 4.1.2
.

Let
-E
be the generator of a
\mathrm{C}_{0}
-semigroup
(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
on a Hilbert space
H
and let
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\operatorname{dom}(E)
be bounded.

Then
\big(u_{n}(\cdot)\big)_{n}\coloneqq\big(T(\cdot)f_{n}\big)_{n}
is equicontinuous on compact time-intervals. More specifically, on compact time-intervals, all
u_{n}
are Lipschitz-continuous sharing a common Lipschitz-constant.

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
T_{0}>0
. For
f_{n}\in\operatorname{dom}(E)
,
t\mapsto u_{n}(t)=T(t)f_{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}
satisfies

u_{n}^{\prime}(t)=\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}T(t)f_{n}=-T(t)(Ef_{n}).

Hence, for any
t,s\geq 0
,

u_{n}(t)-u_{n}(s)=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pts}^{t}u_{n}^{\prime}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}{}\tau=-\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pts}^{t}T(\tau)(Ef_{n})\,\mathrm{d}{}\tau.

As
\big(T(t)\big)_{t\in[0,T_{0}]}
is bounded, we infer

\lVert u_{n}(t)-u_{n}(s)\rVert_{H}\leq\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pts}^{t}\lVert T(\tau)(Ef_{n})\rVert_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}\tau\leq C\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pts}^{t}\lVert Ef_{n}\rVert_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}\tau.

From the uniform graph-norm bound
\lVert Ef_{n}\rVert_{H}\leq M
for some
M\geq 0
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we see that

\lVert u_{n}(t)-u_{n}(s)\rVert_{H}\leq CM\lvert t-s\rvert.\qed

Lemma 4.1.3
.

Let
T_{0}>0
. Let
-E
be the generator of a
\mathrm{C}_{0}
-semigroup
(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
on the Hilbert space
H
. Let
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}\big(0,T_{0};\operatorname{dom}(E)\big)
be bounded.
Let

u_{n}(t)\coloneq\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}T(t-s)F_{n}(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s,\qquad t\in[0,T_{0}].

Then
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
\mathcal{C}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\big([0,T_{0}];H\big)\cap\mathcal{C}\big([0,T_{0}];\operatorname{dom}(E)\big)
. In particular,
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is equicontinuous.

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
0\leq s\leq t\leq T_{0}
. Using Duhamel’s formula we write

\displaystyle u_{n}(t)-u_{n}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}T(t-\tau)F_{n}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}{}\tau-\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{s}T(s-\tau)F_{n}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}{}\tau

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pts}^{t}T(t-\tau)F_{n}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}{}\tau}_{\eqcolon I_{1,n}(s,t)}+\underbrace{\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{s}\big[T(t-\tau)-T(s-\tau)\big]F_{n}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}{}\tau}_{\eqcolon I_{2,n}(s,t)}\text{.}

We estimate the first term using the Cauchy–Schwarz-inequality and the boundedness of the semigroup (on the compact interval
[0,T_{0}]
):

\lVert I_{1,n}(s,t)\rVert_{H}\leq C\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pts}^{t}\lVert F_{n}(\tau)\rVert_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}\tau\leq C\sqrt{t-s}\lVert F_{n}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T_{0};H)}\leq CM\sqrt{t-s}.

For the second term, first observe that for
g\in\operatorname{dom}(E)
, the orbits
T(\cdot)g
are
\mathcal{C}^{1}
with values in
H
and for
h\geq 0
,

T(h)g-g=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{h}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}\theta}T(\theta)g\,\mathrm{d}{}\theta=-\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{h}T(\theta)(Eg)\,\mathrm{d}{}\theta\text{.}

Consequently,

\lVert T(h)g-g\rVert_{H}\leq C\lvert h\rvert\lVert Eg\rVert_{H}.

By applying this observation with
h=t-s
and
g=T(s-\tau)F_{n}(\tau)
and observing that
T(s-\tau)F_{n}(\tau)\in\operatorname{dom}(E)
one infers that for a.e.
\tau\in[0,s]
,

\displaystyle\big\lVert\bigl[T(t-\tau)-T(s-\tau)\bigr]F_{n}(\tau)\big\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle=\lVert T(t-s)g-g\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle\leq C\lvert t-s\rvert\lVert ET(s-\tau)F_{n}(\tau)\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle=C\lvert t-s\rvert\lVert T(s-\tau)EF_{n}(\tau)\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\lvert t-s\rvert\lVert EF_{n}(\tau)\rVert_{H}\text{.}

Integrating over
\tau\in[0,s]
and using the Cauchy–Schwarz-inequality produces an estimate for
I_{2,n}
:

\lVert I_{2,n}(s,t)\rVert_{H}\leq C^{2}\lvert t-s\rvert\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{s}\lVert EF_{n}(\tau)\rVert_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}\tau\leq C^{2}\lvert t-s\rvert\sqrt{s}\lVert EF_{n}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T_{0};H)}.

Since
s\leq T_{0}
and
\lVert EF_{n}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T_{0};H)}\leq M
, we obtain

\lVert I_{2,n}(s,t)\rVert_{H}\leq C^{2}M\sqrt{T_{0}}\lvert t-s\rvert.

Combining the two estimates yields

\lVert u_{n}(t)-u_{n}(s)\rVert_{H}\lesssim\sqrt{t-s}+\sqrt{T_{0}}\lvert t-s\rvert,

uniformly in
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, as
t\downarrow s
, the right-hand side tends to
0
, uniformly in
n\in\mathbb{N}
, proving equicontinuity.
∎

If we additionally assume compactness of the resolvent, we obtain a suitable compactness result.

Theorem 4.1.4
.

Let
T_{0}>0
and
-E
be generator of a
\mathrm{C}_{0}
-semigroup
(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
with compact resolvent. Let
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}\big(0,T_{0};\operatorname{dom}(E)\big)
and

(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\operatorname{dom}(E)
both be bounded.

Then the sequence of (mild) solutions
u_{n}(t)\coloneq T(t)f_{n}+\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}T(t-s)F_{n}(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s
,
t\in[0,T_{0}]
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, of

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}u+Eu&=F_{n},\\
u(0)&=f_{n},\end{aligned}\right.

admits a convergent subsequence in
\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T_{0}];H\bigr)
.

In particular, if both
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}\big(0,T_{0};\operatorname{dom}(E)\big)
and
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\operatorname{dom}(E)
are weakly convergent to some
F
and
f
respectively, then
u_{n}\to u
in
\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T_{0}];H\bigr)
, where
u
satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}u+Eu&=F,\\
u(0)&=f.\end{aligned}\right.

Proof.

From
lemma

4.1.3
and
lemma

4.1.2
we infer that
(u_{n})_{n}
is equicontinuous in
\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T_{0}];H\bigr)
. Moreover, since
(u_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
C\bigl([0,T_{0}];\operatorname{dom}(E)\bigr)
, for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
,
(u_{n}(t))_{n}
is bounded in
\operatorname{dom}(E)\hookrightarrow H
. In particular, because
E
has compact resolvent, that embedding is compact, so for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
, the set
\{u_{n}(t)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is relatively compact in
H
. Thus, the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem (see
[
18
, thm. 7.5.7]
) yields that
(u_{n})_{n}
admits a convergent subsequence.

The last part follows by weakly testing the solution formula for
u_{n}
. Then it follows that
u_{n}\to u
pointwise weakly in
H
. The compactness statement of the theorem establishes the assertion upon using a subsubsequence argument.
∎

Remark 4.1.5
.

Subsequently, we will apply
theorem

4.1.4
(and its special case for weakly convergent sequences) for a sequence
(F_{n})_{n}
bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T_{0};H)
and a constant sequence of initial values
f_{n}=u_{0}\in H
. The considered generator
-E_{-1}
is then the one of the extrapolated semigroup
(T_{-1}(t))_{t\geq 0}
. Note that compactness of
\operatorname{dom}(E)\hookrightarrow H
implies the same for
\operatorname{dom}(E_{-1})=H\hookrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{-1}(E)
. As a consequence, the corresponding mild solutions (in
H
) and, at the same time, classical solutions (in
\mathrm{H}^{-1}(E)
) are relatively compact in
\mathcal{C}\big([0,T_{0}];\mathrm{H}^{-1}(E)\big)
. In particular, if
(F_{n})_{n}
is weakly convergent to some
F\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T_{0};H)
, the corresponding limit solution belongs to
\mathcal{C}\big([0,T_{0}];H\big)
by Duhamel’s formula, yet it is approached by the pre-asymptotic solutions in
\mathcal{C}\big([0,T_{0}];\mathrm{H}^{-1}(E)\big)
.

4.2.
Uniform convergence of solutions to the monotone evolution problem

In the preceeding
section

4.1
we have already established a compactness result for the semigroup part of problem (
1
). For solutions to the monotone evolution problem

(4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}v+A(t)v&=g,\\
v(0)&=v_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

we quote a result establishing the compactness of the solution family. In order to do so, let
T>0
,
\mathcal{H}
be a separable real Hilbert space and let
A(\cdot)
be a maximal monotone operator. Problem (
4
) admits a solution
v_{g}\in\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T];\mathcal{H}\bigr)
for all
g\in\mathrm{L}^{2}\bigl(0,T;\mathcal{H}\bigr)
, and
v_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
.

We want to make use of regularity properties of the solution operator

\Sigma\colon\mathrm{L}^{2}\bigl(0,T;\mathcal{H}\bigr)\to\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T];\mathcal{H}\bigr),\quad g\mapsto v_{g},

where
v_{g}
is the unique solution of problem (
4
) for given
g\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H})
.

The compactness result we are after, states that weakly converging right-hand sides are turned into uniformly convergent solutions of problem (
4
). We simply quote the following statement, which in itself is a corollary of
[
21
, thm. 4.1]
.

Proposition 4.2.1

(
[
27
, lem. 1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable, real Hilbert space and
A(t)=\partial\phi^{t}
,
t>0
, where

(\phi^{t})_{t>0}
satisfies
2.1.2
. Let
v_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
.

Then
\Sigma
is completely continuous, that is,
\Sigma
maps weakly convergent sequences (in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H})
) to strongly convergent sequences (in
\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T];\mathcal{H}\bigr)
).

In the literature, various criteria are available to obtain a similar statement as in
proposition

4.2.1
; for instance if the potentials
\phi^{t}
are of
compact type
, see
[
24
, prop. 3.3]
, the inhomogeneity exhibits Lipschitz-properties, see
[
31
, thm. 6]
or if
(A(s))_{t>0}
generates a compact evolution process, see
[
42
, thm. 21]
.

5.
Existence results

5.1.
Auxiliary results from functional analysis

Before we turn our attention to the actual existence proof, we gather some results of general nature. In order to show the existence of local solutions to problem (
1
), we aim to apply the following fixed point theorem.

Theorem 5.1.1

(
[
16
, thm. 3.5]
)
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and let
\emptyset\neq K\subseteq X
be weakly compact. Let
F\colon K\to\mathcal{P}(X)
be bcc. If the graph of
F
is weakly
\times
weakly sequentially closed in
K\times X
, then
F
admits at least one fixed point, i.e., there exists at least one
u\in K
such that
u\in F(u)
.

Next, we recall an elementary inequality.

Lemma 5.1.2
.

Let
T_{0}>0
,
u\in\mathcal{C}[0,T_{0}]
,
u\geq 0
,
h\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,T_{0})
,
c\geq 0
. If, for all
0\leq t\leq T_{0}
,

u(t)^{2}\leq c^{2}+\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}h(s)u(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s,

then

u(t)\leq c+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}h(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s.

Proof.

For
t\in[0,T_{0}]
, we introduce

X(t)\coloneqq\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}h(s)u(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s.

The corresponding weak derivative is

X^{\prime}(s)=h(s)u(s)\leq h(s)\sqrt{c^{2}+X(s)}.

Hence, integration of
X^{\prime}(s)\big(c^{2}+X(s)\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq h(s)
over
s\in[0,t]
yields

2\sqrt{c^{2}+X(t)}-2c\leq\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}h(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s.

Finally, for
t\in[0,T_{0}]
we obtain

u(t)\leq\sqrt{c^{2}+X(t)}\leq c+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}h(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s.\qed

Lemma 5.1.3
.

Let
H,K
be Hilbert spaces,
(T_{n})_{n},T
in
L(H,K)
. Assume that
T_{n}^{\ast}\to T^{*}
in the strong operator topology. Then the family
(T_{n})_{n}
considered as mappings from
\bigl(B_{H}[0,1],\sigma(H,H^{\prime})\bigr)
to
\bigl(K,\sigma(K,K^{\prime})\bigr)
is equicontinuous.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,
G\subseteq K
finite and define

V\coloneqq\{z\in K;\forall y\in G\colon|\langle z,y\rangle|<\varepsilon\}.

By assumption,
(T_{n}^{*}y)_{n}
is convergent for every
y\in G
, so that

M\coloneqq\overline{\bigcup\{T_{n}^{*}y;n\in\mathbb{N},y\in G\}}\subseteq H

is norm-compact. In particular,
M
is totally bounded and we find
F\subseteq H
finite s.t.

M\subseteq\bigcup_{\phi\in F}B(\phi,\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{2}}).

For
y\in G
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
we find
\phi\in F
such that
\|T_{n}^{*}y-\phi\|<\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{2}}
. Now define the weakly open neighbourhood

U\coloneqq\{x\in H;|\langle x,\phi\rangle|<\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{2}}\text{ for all }\phi\in F\}\cap B_{H}[0,1]

and compute for
x\in U

|\langle T_{n}x,y\rangle|\leq|\langle x,T_{n}^{*}y-\phi\rangle|+|\langle x,\phi\rangle|\leq\|T_{n}^{*}y-\phi\|+|\langle x,\phi\rangle|<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon.

Thus
T_{n}[U]\subseteq V
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that is,
(T_{n})_{n}
is equicontinuous at
0
, which by linearity is enough to establish equicontinuity everywhere.
∎

Remark 5.1.4
.

.

(i)

The result in
lemma

5.1.3
also applies to nets
(T_{\lambda})_{\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}}
for
\lambda\to\infty
as long as
[\lambda_{0},\infty)\ni\lambda\mapsto T_{\lambda}^{*}\in\mathcal{L}(K,H)
is norm-continuous. Indeed, in that case,
M
in the proof can be chosen to be the union of
M_{y}\coloneqq\overline{\{T_{\lambda}^{*}y;\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}\}}
over
y\in G
. By continuity of
\lambda\mapsto T_{\lambda}^{*}
, on bounded, closed intervals, the considered orbits are compact; the limiting behaviour ensures compactness of
M_{y}
and, hence, of
M

(ii)

(iii)

6.1.
Suitable right-hand sides

Let
X
be a separable Hilbert space and let
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
X
be an ONB. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
\varphi_{n}\colon H\times\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous, bounded with
\bigl(\lVert\varphi_{n}\rVert_{\infty}\bigr)_{n}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
and such that
\varphi_{n}(\cdot,y)
is weakly continuous on bounded sets for all
y\in\mathcal{H}
. Then define

F(u,v)\coloneq\overline{\mathrm{co}}\bigl\{\varphi_{n}(u,v)e_{n};n\in\mathbb{N}\bigr\}\text{,}

which are by definition nonempty, closed and convex sets. Bessel’s inequality shows that the sets are bounded as well. First we verify Hausdorff-continuity of
F\colon H\times\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{P}(X)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\lVert\varphi_{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}<\varepsilon
. Let
(u,v)\in H\times\mathcal{H}
. Appealing to continuity of
\varphi_{n}
, there exist open neighbourhoods
U_{n},V_{n}
of
u
and
v
respectively s.t.

\forall 1\!\leq\!n\!\leq\!N\,\,\forall\bigl(\overline{u},\overline{v}\bigr)\in U_{n}\times V_{n}\colon\lvert\varphi_{n}(\overline{u},\overline{v})-\varphi(u,v)\rvert<\varepsilon\text{.}

Since

U\coloneq\bigcap_{1\leq n\leq N}U_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad V\coloneq\bigcap_{1\leq n\leq N}V_{n}

are open neighbourhoods of
u
and
v
, we proceed to estimate
d_{\mathrm{Hd}}\bigl(F(u,v),F(\overline{u},\overline{v})\bigr)
for all
(\overline{u},\overline{v})\in U\times V
. To that end, let
x\in F(u,v)
and write
x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\varphi_{k}(u,v)e_{k}
for some convex combination
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}=1
. We compute

\displaystyle d\big(x,F(\overline{u},\overline{v})\big)=\inf\biggl\{\bigg\lVert x-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}\varphi_{k}(\overline{u},\overline{v})e_{k}\bigg\rVert;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}=1\,\text{convex combination}\biggr\}

\displaystyle\leq\inf\biggl\{\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\underbrace{\lvert\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k}\rvert^{2}}_{\leq\lambda_{k}}\lvert\varphi_{k}(u,v)-\varphi_{k}(\overline{u},\overline{v})\rvert^{2}\underbrace{\lVert e_{k}\rVert^{2}}_{=1}\Bigr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}};\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}=1\,\text{conv. comb.}\biggr\}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}\left.\underbrace{\lvert\varphi_{k}(u,v)-\varphi_{k}(\overline{u},\overline{v})\rvert}_{\leq\varepsilon}\right.^{2}\Bigr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\Bigl(\underbrace{\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\lvert\varphi_{k}(u,v)-\varphi_{k}(\overline{u},\overline{v})\rvert^{2}}_{\leq\varepsilon^{2}}\Bigr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+\varepsilon\text{,}

where
N
is an index chosen suitably large enough. A similar calculation shows for
\overline{x}\in F(\overline{u},\overline{v})
,

d(\overline{x},F(u,v))\leq 2\varepsilon\text{.}

Consequently,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{Hd}}\bigl(F(u,v),F(\overline{u},\overline{v})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\max\biggl\{\sup_{x\in F(u,v)}d\bigl(x,F(\overline{u},\overline{v})\bigr),\sup_{\overline{x}\in F(u,v)}d\bigl(\overline{x},F(u,v)\bigr)\biggr\}\leq 2\varepsilon

Hence,
F
is Hausdorff-continuous. In order to show that
F
is half weakly continuous, choose correspondingly different neighbourhoods
U_{n}
for fixed second arguments in the above and proceed similarly.

We now comment on our assumptions for global solutions:

For global solutions, we demand a linear growth bound (see
definition

3.1.3
) in
theorem

5.3.2
. If we demand that
F\colon H\times\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{P}(X)
be of the form as above and

\forall(u,v)\in H\times\mathcal{H}\colon\varphi_{k}(u,v)=c_{k}\langle u,e_{k}\rangle+\nu_{k}(v)\lVert v\rVert\text{,}

where
\nu_{k}\colon H\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous, bounded and satisfies
\bigl(\lVert\nu_{k}\rVert_{\infty}\bigr)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
and
c_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant with
(c_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
, then
F
satisfies a linear growth bound. We note in passing that this class of examples also satisfies the half weak continuity requirement.

For
x\in F(u,v)
we can estimate

\displaystyle\lVert x\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}\varphi_{k}(u,v)^{2}\underbrace{\lVert e_{k}\rVert^{2}}_{=1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}\bigl(c_{k}\langle u,e_{k}\rangle+\nu_{k}(v)\lVert v\rVert\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}c_{k}^{2}\langle u,e_{k}\rangle^{2}+\nu_{k}(v)^{2}\lVert v\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}^{2}\lVert u\rVert^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu_{k}(v)^{2}\lVert v\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=2\big\lVert(c_{k})_{k}\big\rVert_{2}^{2}\lVert u\rVert^{2}+2\big\lVert(\lVert\nu_{k}\rVert_{\infty})_{k}\rVert_{2}^{2}\lVert v\rVert^{2}\text{,}

which proves the linear growth bound.

6.2.
A time-dependent maximal monotone operator for the Schrödinger–Debye system

We first briefly recapitulate Lebesgue–Sobolev spaces with variable exponents.

Consider
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

, a bounded smooth domain. Let

the corresponding norms are given by

\lVert f\rVert_{p(x)}\coloneq\inf\Bigl\{\lambda>0;\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt\Omega}\big\lvert\tfrac{f(x)}{\lambda}\big\rvert^{p(x)}\,\mathrm{d}{}x\leq 1\Bigr\}\text{.}

For details on and properties of
\mathrm{L}^{p(x)}(\Omega)
, we refer to
[
17
, ch. 3]
. The Sobolev spaces
\mathrm{W}^{k,p(x)}(\Omega)

f\in\mathrm{L}^{p(x)}(\Omega)
s.t. all distributional derivatives of order

\mathrm{L}^{p(x)}(\Omega)

\lVert f\rVert_{k,p(x)}\coloneq\inf\Bigl\{\lambda>0;\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq k\colon\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt\Omega}\big\lvert\tfrac{\partial^{\alpha}f(x)}{\lambda}\big\rvert^{p(x)}\,\mathrm{d}{}x\leq 1\Bigr\}\text{.}

For details and properties of
\mathrm{W}^{k,p(x)}(\Omega)
we refer to
[
17
, ch. 8]
.

To define our example, let
\mathcal{H}=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and let

(14)

p^{+}\coloneq\max_{(x)\in\overline{\Omega}}p(x)\geq\min_{(x)\in\overline{\Omega}}p(x)\eqcolon p^{-}>2.

Then

with continuous and dense embeddings:

•

Appealing to
[
17

is assumed to be bounded,

The continuity and density of the embedding

Lastly, the compact, dense embedding

The second inclusion is proven by similar arguments using duality.

We will make use of coefficients

6.5.
Wave semigroup

On a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
we can pose the wave equation, for some
a^{*}=a\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d\times d}
uniformly strictly positive definite,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t^{2}}u-\operatorname{div}a\operatorname{grad}_{0}u&\in F&&\quad\text{on}\ (0,T)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}),\\
u(0)&=u_{0}&&\quad\text{in}\ \mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}),\end{aligned}\right.

as a system as follows

\biggl[\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&\operatorname{div}\\
\operatorname{grad}_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr]\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in F(u_{1},u_{2},v)\text{;}

see
[
40
, p. 92ff]
for details. Consequently, we arrive at the formulation

(18)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \biggl[\partial_{t}\!\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\!-\!\begin{pmatrix}0&\operatorname{div}\\
\operatorname{grad}_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\!\biggr]\!\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix}&\in F(u_{1},u_{2},v)&&\text{on}\ (0,T)\!\times\!H,\\
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}v+A(t)v&\in G(u_{1},u_{2},v)&&\text{on}\ (0,T)\!\times\!\mathcal{H},\\
\big(u_{1}(0),u_{2}(0),v(0)\big)&=(u_{1,0},u_{2,0},v_{0})&&\text{in}\ H\!\times\!\mathcal{H},\end{aligned}\right.

where again,
A
satisfies the assumptions of
theorem

5.2.2
and

\mathrm{H}^{1}

\operatorname{dom}(E)\coloneq\mathring{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}(\operatorname{div},\Omega)\subseteq H.

\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)=\nabla\mathring{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)\oplus\ker(\operatorname{div}).

\operatorname{curl}\mathrm{H}(\operatorname{curl},\Omega)\subseteq\ker(\operatorname{div})

\widetilde{H}=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\nicefrac{{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}}{{\ker(\operatorname{div})}},

\mathring{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)\overset{\mathrm{c}}{\hookrightarrow}\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\lVert f_{n}-f_{m}\rVert^{2}

by definition of the adjoint

Corollary 6.5.1

and

(u_{1},u_{2},v)\in\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T];\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\bigr)\times\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T];\nicefrac{{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}}{{\ker(\operatorname{div})}}\bigr)\times\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T];\mathcal{H}\bigr).

If additionally,
F,G
satisfy a linear growth bound, the solution is global.

Proof.

Apply
theorem

5.2.2
and
theorem

5.3.2
.
∎

6.6.
Maxwell semigroup

On a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
with dielectricity
\varepsilon\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
, permeability
\mu\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
and (external) current density
j\colon\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
we incorporate Maxwell’s equations into our setting by posing

(19)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}\varepsilon u_{1}\!+\!\sigma u_{1}\!-\!\operatorname{curl}u_{2}&\in j(u_{1})\!+\!F_{1}(u_{1},u_{2},v)&&\text{on}\ (0,T)\!\times\!\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{3},\\
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}\mu u_{2}\!+\!\operatorname{curl}_{0}u_{1}&\in F_{2}(u_{1},u_{2},v)&&\text{on}\ (0,T)\!\times\!\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{3},\\
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}v\!+\!A(\cdot)v&\in G(u_{1},u_{2},v)&&\text{on}\ (0,T)\!\times\!\mathcal{H},\\
(u_{1}(0),u_{2}(0),v(0))&=(u_{1,0},u_{2,0},v_{0})&&\text{in}\ \mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C})^{3}\!\times\!\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C})^{3}\!\times\!\mathcal{H},\end{aligned}\right.

where again,
A
satisfies the assumptions of
theorem

5.2.2
and
\operatorname{curl}_{0}
denotes the weak rotation with Dirichlet boundary (defined similarly as the gradient above, see, again,
[
40
, p. 92ff]
for details). We point out that the left-hand side of the system

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}\varepsilon u_{1}-\operatorname{curl}u_{2}&\in j(u_{1})+F_{1}(u_{1},u_{2},v)&&\text{in}\ (0,T)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{3},\\
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}t}\mu u_{2}+\operatorname{curl}_{0}u_{1}&\in F_{2}(u_{1},u_{2},v)&&\text{in}\ (0,T)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{3},\end{aligned}\right.

can be written as

\biggl[\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon&0\\
0&\mu\end{pmatrix}+\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0&-\operatorname{curl}\\
\operatorname{curl}_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}}_{\eqcolon E}\biggr]\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in F(u_{1},u_{2},v).

E
is a skew-selfadjoint operator on
H\coloneq\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{3}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{3}
. Hence,
E
generates a
\mathrm{C}_{0}
-semigroup. Note that
E
does not have compact resolvent, since gradient fields are contained in
\ker(\operatorname{curl})
, i.e.,
\nabla\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\subseteq\ker(\operatorname{curl})
and
\mathring{\nabla}\mathring{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)\subseteq\ker(\operatorname{curl}_{0})
.

By refining the Helmholtz-decomposition from
section

6.5
, we obtain (see
[
3
, thm. 5.3]
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{3}

\displaystyle=\nabla\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\oplus\operatorname{curl}_{0}\mathrm{H}(\operatorname{curl}_{0};\Omega)

\displaystyle\oplus\ker(\operatorname{curl}_{0})\cap\ker(\operatorname{div})

\displaystyle=\mathring{\nabla}\mathring{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\oplus\operatorname{curl}\mathrm{H}(\operatorname{curl};\Omega)

\displaystyle\oplus\ker(\operatorname{curl})\cap\ker(\operatorname{div}_{0}),

where the last terms on the right vanish for topologically trivial domains
\Omega
, i.e.,
\Omega
simply connected and
\mathbb{R}^{3}\!\setminus\!\Omega
connected (see
[
3
, rem. 5.4]
,
[
33
,
35
]
). We will assume this case. We can then factorize the null spaces

\ker(\operatorname{curl})=\operatorname{grad}_{0}\mathring{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ker(\operatorname{curl}_{0})=\operatorname{grad}\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)

to obtain the quotient Hilbert space

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}

\displaystyle\coloneq\nicefrac{{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{3}}}{{\operatorname{grad}\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}}\times\nicefrac{{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{3}}}{{\operatorname{grad}_{0}\mathring{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)}}

and the corresponding semigroup generator
\widetilde{E}
with domain

\displaystyle\operatorname{dom}\bigl(\widetilde{E}\bigr)

\displaystyle\coloneq\nicefrac{{\mathrm{H}(\operatorname{curl}_{0};\Omega)}}{{\operatorname{grad}\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}}\times\nicefrac{{\mathrm{H}(\operatorname{curl};\Omega)}}{{\operatorname{grad}_{0}\mathring{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)}}\subseteq\widetilde{H}.

To verify compactness of the embedding
\operatorname{dom}(\widetilde{E})\hookrightarrow\widetilde{H}
, one can use that both spaces in the product can be seen as spaces
\mathrm{H}(\operatorname{div};\Omega)
and
\mathrm{H}(\operatorname{div}_{0};\Omega)
with certain tangential trace conditions attached. In fact, this follows from the Picard–Weber–Weck selection theorem, see
[
36
]
. For convenience, we also refer to a similar rationale as in
section

6.5
in
[
32
, thm. 4.1]
. Hence, one arrives at the following result.

Corollary 6.6.1
.

If
F
and
G
are bcc, Hausdorff-continuous, and half weakly continuous and
j\colon\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{2}\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{3}
is continuous, problem (
17
) posed on
\widetilde{H}\times\mathcal{H}
has a unique local solution

(u_{1},u_{2},v)\in\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T];\nicefrac{{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{3}}}{{\operatorname{grad}\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}}\bigr)\times\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T];\nicefrac{{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{3}}}{{\operatorname{grad}_{0}\mathring{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)}}\bigr)\times\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,T];\mathcal{H}\bigr).

If additionally,
F,G
satisfy a linear growth bound, the solution is global.

Proof.

Apply
theorem

5.2.2
and
theorem

5.3.2
.
∎
