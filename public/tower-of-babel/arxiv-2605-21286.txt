Software Between Quantum and Machine Learning – And Down to Pulses
Maja Franz * 4 , Melvin Strobl * 2 , Jonathan Hunz 2 , Lukas Scheller 2 , Lucas van der Horst 2 , Eileen Kuehn 2 , Achim Streit 2 , Wolfgang Mauerer 4 5

Abstract
Contemporary quantum computing platforms remain, in essence, programmable physical systems whose control is typically mediated through unitary gate abstractions. While such abstractions provide a uniform interface, they obscure important aspects of the underlying hardware and may limit the exploitation of its full capabilities. Direct operation at the level of control pulses offers a more expressive and physically faithful paradigm, enabling, for instance, the implementation of tailored error-mitigation and optimisation strategies. However, this increased expressivity comes at the cost of greater complexity from the perspective of quantum software development, necessitating structured and accessible tooling. We present a software framework, integrated within the QML-Essentials package, that extends quantum machine learning (QML) methodologies to encompass pulse-level modelling. By embedding quantum optimal control (QOC) techniques within a QML setting, our approach enables the seamless combination of gate-based and pulse-level representations in a unified modelling paradigm. The framework provides a comprehensive suite of modelling and analytical capabilities. In particular, we introduce composable ansatz constructions based on interchangeable building blocks, alongside support for end-to-end optimisation of pulse parameters. Motivated by the central role of quantum Fourier models (QFMs) , we further incorporate a range of Fourier-analytic diagnostics, including the Fourier fingerprint and the Fourier coefficient correlation (FCC) , complemented by extended measures of entanglement. All performance-critical components are implemented in a high-performance environment using JAX and supported by a dedicated quantum simulator. Taken together, the framework facilitates reproducible and systematic investigations in QML research, while bridging the conceptual and practical divide between abstract circuit-based models and hardware-aware pulse-level optimisation. It provides a robust foundation for future developments at the intersection of quantum machine learning and quantum control.

III
Preliminaries on Quantum Fourier Models

The goal of
QML
aligns with classical machine learning:
learning
a function
f
using a function approximator
\tilde{f}
parametrised by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
The parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
are
trained
to minimise the difference between
f({\boldsymbol{x}})
and
\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
according to some metric, for a
D
-dimensional input
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{D})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, using classical optimisation routines, such as gradient descent
[
71
]
.

In
QML
, the function approximator is an
n
-qubit quantum circuit whose expectation value, using the observable
\mathcal{M}
, is given by

\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\langle 0|^{\otimes{n}}\hat{U}^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\mathcal{M}\hat{U}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|0\rangle^{\otimes{n}},

(1)

parametrised by the trainable parameter vector
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{L+1})\in[\mathbb{R}^{K}]^{L+1}
with
L
layers of
K
parameters per layer
1
1

1

Without loss of generality, we assume identical numbers of parametrised gates per layer.
.

We build the eventual unitary
\hat{U}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
as products of multiple layers (
\ell\in[1,L]
) of consecutive encoding unitaries
\hat{S}^{(\ell)}({\boldsymbol{x}})
and trainable unitaries
\hat{W}^{(\ell)}\coloneqq\hat{W}^{(\ell)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\ell})
as follows:

\hat{U}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\hat{W}^{(L+1)}\hat{S}^{(L)}({\boldsymbol{x}})\hat{W}^{(L)}\cdots\hat{W}^{(2)}\hat{S}^{(1)}({\boldsymbol{x}})\hat{W}^{(1)}

(2)

where an additional last trainable layer
\hat{W}^{(L+1)}
is added.
To allow for an arbitrary circuit structure and placement of unitary and encoding gates, we construct
\hat{S}^{(\ell)}({\boldsymbol{x}})
to comprise combinations of
D
unitaries
\hat{S}^{(\ell)}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})
, each of which encodes one single input feature
x_{i}
:

\hat{S}_{i}^{(\ell)}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\exp{(-\imath\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{x}}\hat{G}_{i}^{(\ell)})}=\exp{(-\imath x_{i}\hat{G}_{i}^{(\ell)})}

(3)

with Hermitian generator
\hat{G}_{i}^{(\ell)}
and standard basis vector
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
.

As established in Refs.
[
62
,
72
]
, this architecture allows rewriting
Eq. 1
as a partial Fourier series

\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}c_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})e^{\imath{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{x}}},

(4)

where
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
contains the unique frequencies resulting from the eigenvalues of each generating encoding Hamiltonian
\hat{G}_{i}^{(\ell)}
.
The corresponding complex Fourier coefficients are given by
\{c_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}
.

From
Eq. 4
it can be inferred that the Fourier frequencies derive from the encoding gates (
i.e.
, the feature map), while the Fourier coefficients, parametrised by the parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
of the circuit, solely depend on the circuit structure, which we refer to as
ansatz
in the further course of this work.
In
Sec. VI-A
, we describe our implemented metrics for circuit analysis that derive from the Fourier spectrum and how we compute coefficients in the framework.

IV-A

Model and Ansatz Overview

The
Model
forms the core of the package, built upon a given ansatz.
An ansatz can be imported from the ansatz-library, which contains, among others, a
strongly-entangling ansatz (SEA)

[
70
]
(shown as an example at the top of
Fig. 1
), a
hardware-efficient ansatz (HEA)

[
39
]
and almost all ansätze introduced in Ref.
[
73
]
.
Alternatively, we refactored custom ansatz definitions to allow composition via multiple
Block
instances, as shown on the right-hand side of
Fig. 1
.
A
Block
takes a set of quantum gates and a topology, which describes the order and layout of two-qubit operations in a circuit.

The resulting ansatz represents one variational layer of the model of size
n_qubits
, which is repeated multiple times, depending on the
n_layer
argument of the
Model
constructor, to build the full circuit.
For
QML
use cases, the unitary ansatz comprises multiple rotational gates (
e.g.
,
\mathrm{\hat{R}_{X}}
,
\mathrm{\hat{R}_{Y}}
,
\mathrm{\hat{R}_{Z}}
), parametrised by at least one angle.

We refer to the set of angles corresponding to all rotational gates in the circuit as the
parameters
of the model, which may be subject to optimisation in a training process.
For the initialisation of the parameters, we provide multiple approaches, such as setting the parameters to specific values (zero or
\pi
), or uniformly at random in a specific domain.

Following the data re-uploading mechanism from
Eq. 3
, we support input encodings interleaved with the variational layers, where encoding positions can be controlled with the
data_reuploading
argument of the
Model
constructor.
While the default input encoding scheme is an angle encoding (using a
\mathrm{\hat{R}_{X}}
gate), we also implemented the encoding schemes of Ref.
[
63
]
:
As introduced in
Sec. III
, the number of frequencies (and therefore the number of coefficients) depends on the chosen feature map.
Hence, we provide an interface to choose between different encoding methods in addition to providing a custom feature map:

(1)

Hamming encoding (default, linear spectrum),

(2)

binary encoding (exponential spectrum), and

(3)

ternary encoding (exponential spectrum).

In addition, trainable parameters can be applied in the feature map as introduced in Ref.
[
37
]
.

Additionally, we allow to view ansätze from a lower abstraction level than unitary gates, by providing the feature to parametrise an ansatz directly with pulses, using
pulse parameters
(
cf.

Sec. V
), instead of rotational angles.

IV-B

Execution with Input- and Parameter-Batching

Depending on the application or metric that is to be evaluated for a model, different modes of execution may be required.
In
QML-Essentials
, this mode can be selected by setting the
execution_type
argument when calling the model.
In particular, this value can be set to

(1)

"expval"
, which gives the expectation value of an observable (
e.g.
, the default is
\hat{Z}
, acting on a specified set of qubits),

(2)

"density"
, which gives the density matrix,

(3)

"state"
, which gives the state-vector representation, and

(4)

"probs"
, which gives the output probability distribution over a specified set of qubits, using a finite number of shots.

To evaluate models with concurrent sets of inputs and (pulse) parameters, we handle batch dimensions internally to cover all possible combinations.

We utilise our own simulator to carry out the computation of the circuit with the specified observables.
This simulator and all implementations of the metrics presented in this work are entirely built upon the JAX
[
8
]
library for high-performance array computations and utilisation of parallelisation where possible.
To solve the differential equation required for pulse-level simulation, our framework supports adaptive Runge-Kutta methods (e.g., Dormand-Prince of order
8

[
16
]
via Diffrax
[
42
]
) as well as commutator-free Magnus integrators tailored for highly oscillatory Hamiltonian dynamics.

Our simulator is continuously tested against the PennyLane
[
6
]
simulator to ensure correctness of the computations.

V
Pulse-Level Simulation

Generally, unitary gates are used to train
QFM
models.
However, there exist lower-level parameters that allow controlling the exact pulse of each gate applied to a quantum system, to which we refer as
pulse parameters
.
These parameters are usually subject to a fine-tuning process carried out by the hardware provider in a process commonly termed
QOC
.
In our framework, we provide an interface to conduct experiments that require access to the pulse parameters, therefore enabling research that is more tailored to the actual quantum hardware.

V-A

Pulses of Basis Gates

Generally, the dynamics of a closed quantum system are described by the unitary time evolution of a state
\psi
.
For many platforms (
e.g.
, superconducting transmons), dynamics from time
t_{0}
to
t_{0}+\delta t
are governed by the time-dependent Schrödinger equation
[
18
,
67
]
(assuming the convention
\hbar=1
):

\left|\psi(t_{0}+\delta t)\right\rangle=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-\imath\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\delta t}\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t,{\boldsymbol{p}})\mathrm{d}t\right)\left|\psi(t_{0})\right\rangle,

(5)

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the time-ordering operator, and
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t,{\boldsymbol{p}})
is the time-dependent Hamiltonian parametrised by pulse parameters
{\boldsymbol{p}}
(distinct from the Hadamard gate
\hat{\mathrm{H}}
).

Typically, the Hamiltonian decomposes into a static operator
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{static}}
(unperturbed energy levels) and
m
control operators
\{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
driven by time-dependent signals
S_{j}(t;{\boldsymbol{p}})
, such that

\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t,{\boldsymbol{p}})=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{static}}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}S_{j}(t;{\boldsymbol{p}})\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{j}.

(6)

Throughout our work, we choose
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{static}}=\frac{\omega_{q}}{2}\hat{\mathrm{Z}}
for general periodically driven two-level systems
[
86
]
, with the qubit frequency
\omega_{q}=10\pi

The control signals are typically composed of an envelope function

\omega_{c}

To simplify the evolution in

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{static}}

Our software framework supports the exact numerical integration of these full interaction-picture dynamics, either directly in the laboratory frame or via a product-to-sum drive frame decomposition that explicitly resolves slow and fast oscillatory modes.
However, for the numerical experiments in this work, we primarily utilise the
rotating-wave approximation (RWA)

The
RWA
significantly reduces the integration stiffness and accelerates simulation while maintaining high accuracy for near-resonant drives.

VI-A

Fourier Spectral Properties

Calculating the Fourier spectrum of
QFMs
is a central tool for analysing the capabilities of different ansätze and data encodings
[
85
,
56
,
78
,
77
,
24
]
(
cf.

Sec. III
).
Therefore, computing Fourier coefficients, frequencies and other metrics that derive from them are a key feature of the
QML-Essentials

VI-A
1
Coefficients

Fourier coefficients can be obtained with two different approaches in the
QML-Essentials
package:
Either an analytical expression for the coefficients can be derived for a model, or the
FFT
is used.

For the former, the analytical method uses an algorithm proposed by
[
59
]

[
59
]
, extended in Ref.
[
85
]
.

Here, the expectation value from
Eq. 1
is expanded in terms of trigonometric polynomials.
However, in its current state, this method is comparatively slow and only implemented for a single input feature, serving as an evaluation of the second,
FFT
-based method.
In this second method, the
FFT
over a range of input samples yields a numerical approximation of the coefficients based on the expectation value in
Eq. 1
, given the number of frequencies for a model, for which an upper bound can be derived from the input encoding
[
63
]
.

VI-A
2
Fourier Coefficient Correlation

The
FCC
is a metric to quantify the correlation between the intrinsic Fourier coefficients of a
QFM

[
77
]
.
This is computed numerically by first executing the
FFT
to obtain a set of Fourier coefficients while sampling
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta
and then calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient
[
27
]

r_{\Theta}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\prime})=\frac{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta}\left(c_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\bar{c}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}\right)\left(c_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\bar{c}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\prime}}\right)}{\sqrt{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta}\left(c_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\bar{c}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}\right)^{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta}\left(c_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\bar{c}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\prime}}\right)^{2}}}

(7)

between each pair of Fourier coefficients.
Here,
\bar{c}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}
and
\bar{c}_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\prime}}
represent the average value of the coefficient at frequency
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\prime}
, respectively.
This pairwise correlation is termed Fourier fingerprint, and averaging over all pairs results in the
FCC

\mathtt{FCC}_{\Theta}\coloneq\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}},{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\prime}\in{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\left|r_{\Theta}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\prime})\right|.

(8)

Our contribution covers the implementation of both, the Fourier fingerprint and the
FCC
with the default parameters set to replicate the results from Ref.
[
77
]
.

VI-B

In
QML-Essentials
, the expressibility is computed by taking the inverse of the
Kullback-Leibler (KL)
-divergence between states sampled from a given ansatz and the Haar measure (
cf.
Apx.
A
for details).
Therefore, the expressibility metric introduced in Ref.
[
73
]
evaluates how well an ansatz can approximate random states in Hilbert space.
However, expressibility does not reflect whether circuits are suitable for learning settings, as functional constraints or input encodings are not considered in this approach.
As shown in Ref.
[
77
]
, the
FCC
addresses this limitation by revealing coefficient dependencies that limit the effective number of learnable frequency components.

VI-C

Entangling Capability

The entangling capability aims to describe the amount of entanglement a circuit can create.
It can also be understood as a measure of the
quantumness
of a particular circuit, as entanglement is one of the central properties that distinguish a quantum system from classical surrogates.
As there are different ways of quantifying entanglement, there also exist various entangling capability metrics
[
82
,
64
]
, of which the
Meyer-Wallach (MW)
measure
[
55
,
9
]
and
Bell measurements (BMs)

[
22
,
32
]
are included in
QML-Essentials
.
The definitions and implementation details for these two metrics are summarised in Apx.
B
.
In addition, we implement the
EF

[
35
]
and
CE

[
4
]
to also support noisy states.

VI-C
1
Entanglement of Formation

Following Ref.
[
64
]
, the
EF

[
35
]
is usually defined as

E_{\text{EF}}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)=\inf\left\{\sum_{i}p_{i}E\left(\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{i}\right|\right):\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{i}\right|\right\},

(9)

and represents the averaged entanglement of a pure state decomposition of a density matrix
\rho
, where the minimum over all possible decompositions then results in the
EF
.
The entanglement for pure states is calculated using the
MW
measure as introduced in
Eq. 13
.
Finding a pure state decomposition, however, is a non-trivial task.
In our implementation, we utilise NumPy’s
[
31
]
eigenvalue and eigenstate decomposition of the density matrix
\rho
.
Therefore, this metric is not applicable when using real devices.
Based on the resulting decomposition, we proceed with calculating the entanglement of each eigenstate while weighting it by its eigenvalue as described in the left part of the right-hand side of
Eq. 9
.
However, the eigenvalue decomposition is not unique, resulting in several potential decompositions, which can lead to different values for the entangling capability.
Hence, the resulting values of the
EF
represent an upper bound.

VI-C
2
Concentratable Entanglement

CE
is a new family of multipartite entanglement measures introduced by
[
4
]
in
[
4
]
.
Let
\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathbb{H}
be a pure state over
n
qubits labelled by the set
S=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For any non-empty subset
s\subseteq S
of qubit labels, the
Concentratable Entanglement
is defined as

\mathcal{C}_{\left|\psi\right\rangle}(s)=1-\frac{1}{2^{\lvert s\rvert}}\sum_{\alpha\in 2^{s}}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\alpha}^{2}],

(10)

where
2^{s}
denotes the power set of
s
and
\rho_{\alpha}
is the result of tracing out all qubits not in
\alpha
.
CEs can be efficiently computed on a quantum computer using an
n
-qubit parallelised SWAP test
[
4
]
.
This method requires two copies of the state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
and
n
additional ancillary qubits, resulting in
3n
qubits in total.

[
5
]
show in
[
5
]
that CEs can be approximated from the results of Bell-basis measurements, hence using a circuit without additional ancillas.
CEs, as described, are defined only for pure states. As in the case of the Entanglement of Formation, an entanglement measure for mixed states can be derived from CEs using a convex roof extension:

\mathcal{C_{\rho}}(s)=\inf\Biggl\{\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathcal{C}_{\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle}(s):\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{i}\right|\Biggr\}.

(11)

In general, computing convex roof extension measures presents a hard optimisation problem.
Efficient lower and upper bounds for this value are proven by
[
5
]

[
5
]
and
[
23
]

[
23
]
.
Experimentally, however, we observe that computation of the CEs using the
n
-qubit parallelised SWAP test still yields suitable results under the influence of lower levels of noise.

VII
Conclusion and Outlook

The software library introduced in this work provides a comprehensive environment for studying
QML
models from both a Fourier-analytic and hardware-aware perspective.
By combining modular ansatz construction, pulse-level modelling, Fourier-spectral diagnostics, entanglement analysis, and a differentiable simulation backend, our software enables a reproducible workflow for a broad range of controlled numerical experiments within a single package.

This paves the way to investigate architectural ansatz choices and physical constraints in the context of
QML
.
At the same time, we acknowledge that significant computational costs are associated with training
QML
models, particularly in pulse-level and noise-aware settings.
While our framework incorporates performance optimisations to mitigate computational overheads, the primary contribution of this work is scientific rather than immediately application-driven.

In particular, we view the framework as a tool for the systematic and reproducible investigation of pulse-level learning with
QFMs
.
While both perspectives have independently received considerable attention in the
QML
literature, with pulse-level approaches for improving hardware control and Fourier analysis for understanding the functional structure of circuits, to our best knowledge, our software is the first to allow for their combined investigation.
With the foundation provided in this work, we leave experiments and analysis to connect the physical implementation of
QML
models with their spectral properties to future work.

Our framework further provides the basis for a variety of systematic studies.
For instance, it can be investigated to a greater extent how specific data encoding strategies and ansätze, with their intrinsic entangling capability and expressibility, influence trainability and set quantum models apart from classical
ML
models.
Additionally, the differentiable pulse-level backend enables experiments for the direct optimisation of pulse parameters in certain learning tasks, as well as the exploration of error mitigation strategies.
Notably, our implementation allows combining pulse-level computation with any of the available noise models, therefore enabling a hardware-aware study of
QML
models.
In future work, this may include adding hardware-aware system dynamics, such as damping due to hardware limitations.
Ultimately, we aim to provide a comprehensive toolbox for reproducible
QML
research.

\int_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\text{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\left|\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right|\right)^{\otimes t}

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P_{\text{Model}}(F;{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|P_{\text{Haar }}(F)\right).

F\coloneq\lvert\left\langle\psi_{1}\middle|\psi_{2}\right\rangle\rvert^{2}

P\left(F\right)
.

This metric yields zero if
P_{\text{Model}}(F;{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=P_{\text{Haar }}(F)
, meaning the states sampled from the
QFM
are Haar distributed.
For the least expressive case, that is, the empty circuit
U=\mathds{1}
, the KL divergence becomes
\ln(n_{\text{bins}})
where
n_{\text{bins}}
describes the number of bins that are used for discretising the probability distribution using a histogram.
Expressibility is thus regarded as the inverse
KL
-divergence.

Appendix B
Entangling Capability Metrics

This section provides the definitions for the implemented entangling capability metrics in
QML-Essentials
.

B-A

Meyer-Wallach Measure

The
MW
entangling capability
[
55
,
9
]
of a state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
is defined as

E(\left|\psi\right\rangle)=2\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho_{k}^{2}\right]\right),

(13)

based on partial density matrices
\rho_{k}
, where
k
denotes the subsystem (
i.e.
, qubit index).

This metric has the property that if
\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho_{j}^{2}\right]=1\quad\forall j
, implying
E=0
,
\left|\psi\right\rangle
is a product state whereas
E=1
iff
\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho_{k}^{2}\right]=1/2\quad\forall k
meaning the state is maximally mixed. Notably, this metric is restricted to pure states and cannot be used for mixed states as they occur in decoherent noisy circuits.

B-B

Bell Measurement

The
BM

[
22
,
32
]
provides an alternative way to compute
\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho_{k}^{2}\right]
in
Eq. 13
.
Here, a circuit is considered, where the state of interest is vertically prepared twice, thereby requiring
n
additional ancillary qubits, and an inverse Bell-state is applied onto each pair of qubits between the two subsystems.

As shown in Ref.
[
32
]
, the squared trace of the density matrix is linearly dependent on the probability of measuring the parity between each of the qubits in the individual subsystems:

\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho_{k}^{2}\right]=1-2\cdot P_{\text{odd},k},

(14)

where
P_{\text{odd},k}
is the probability of odd, non-zero parity in the outcomes of the
k
th qubit on each copy.
Inserting this in
Eq. 13
, yields the same estimate of the entangling capability as when using the
MW
metric, yet uses a physical observable measurement applicable to real quantum systems.
However, when using mixed states, impurities from the interaction with the environment may overestimate the entangling capability
[
23
]
.
Therefore, the resulting values of the
BM
for mixed states rather present an upper bound for the entangling capability.
