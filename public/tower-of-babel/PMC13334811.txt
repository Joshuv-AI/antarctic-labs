Comparative evaluation of oncolytic viruses reveals opposing preferences for glioblastoma subtypes
Authors: Tine Deconinck, Tim Dierckx, Frederik De Smet, Jim Baggen, Dirk Daelemans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive primary brain tumor in adults characterized by poor long-term survival and frequent tumor recurrence, highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies. Oncolytic viruses (OVs) preferentially infect and kill cancer cells while stimulating an antitumor immune response. Oncolytic virotherapies have shown encouraging results in preclinical GBM models and some clinical settings. However, high tumor heterogeneity poses a major obstacle. Only a subset of GBM patients responds well, and improvement in survival is usually limited. It is therefore crucial to better understand determinants of OV effectiveness and to consider multiple OVs. Here, we report a comparative analysis of the oncolytic efficacy of 15 clinically relevant viruses across a diverse panel of 14 heterogeneous patient-derived GBM cell lines. Correlation analysis revealed two clusters of viruses with opposing oncolytic activity profiles and opposing preferences for GBM subtypes. Oncolytic activities correlated with expression levels of interferon-, neurodevelopment-, and extracellular matrix-related gene sets, with inverse correlations observed between the two OV groups. Together, these data reveal that diverse viruses share similar determinants of oncolytic activity. Our findings pave the way toward combinatorial or personalized OV therapies in GBM, where tumor subtype could guide selection of the most effective OV.Keywords: oncolytic virotherapy, glioblastoma, brain tumor, CNS tumor, personalized medicine

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive type of brain tumor, with a median survival of only 14 months and a five-year survival rate below 5%.1,2 The current standard of care for GBM consists of surgery, radiotherapy, and temozolomide-based chemotherapy. Therapeutic escape and recurrence is common and can be attributed to high inter- and intratumoral heterogeneity and resistance development in specific tumor subclones.3 Few treatment options exist for relapsed GBM and none of them is particularly effective.4

One of the currently investigated alternative treatment options for GBM is oncolytic virotherapy. An oncolytic virus (OV) is defined as a virus that preferentially infects and kills cancer cells without harming healthy tissues. This tumor tropism is based on specific characteristics of tumor cells, e.g., expression of virus receptors (e.g., CD46, a known entry receptor for measles virus and certain adenoviruses), abnormal signaling pathways (aberrant interferon signaling, less p53 signaling), or hypoxia.5,6,7 OV infection has two distinct antitumoral effects: first, cancer cells are destroyed through virus-mediated lysis or cell death. Infected cells release new viral particles that infect more tumor cells. Second, OVs also have the potential to activate an antitumor immune response by inducing immunogenic cell death.8 This cell death mechanism involves the release of tumor antigens and danger signals (DAMPs/PAMPs),9 which together trigger antitumor immunity by activating antigen-presenting cells, natural killer cells, and T cells.10 Infiltration of these immune cells can overcome the obstacle of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment present in many solid tumors, including GBM.11,12

Numerous viruses have been investigated as potential oncolytic agents against GBM, both in preclinical models and in clinical trials. OVs are frequently genetically modified or armed (e.g., adenovirus type 5 with a deletion in the E1A region and a modification in the virus fiber [DNX-2401] and vesicular stomatitis virus pseudotyped with the glycoprotein of the lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus [VSV-GP]) to reduce their neurovirulence and improve their therapeutic effect.13 Teserpaturev (G47Δ), a third-generation, triple-mutated herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), is the first and so far the only approved OV for treatment of patients with malignant gliomas. G47Δ was well tolerated and showed an increased 1-year overall survival in a single-arm phase 2 trial. However, only a small number of patients survived longer than 3 years.14 In general, OVs have thus far demonstrated durable responses only in a subset of patients, without achieving complete remission.15 A clearly superior oncolytic agent has not yet emerged. This highlights the importance of considering multiple viruses, studying their mechanisms of action, and subsequently personalizing treatment accordingly.

Current OV research studies primarily focus on a single or a limited number of virus types, assessing their efficacy as monotherapy. This narrow focus hinders the effective translation of laboratory findings into clinical applications.16,17,18 A comprehensive comparative evaluation of the various OVs currently being tested against GBM in clinical trials is still lacking. In 2005, Wollmann et al. compared the oncolytic potential of nine viruses in the commercial GBM cell lines U87 and M059J.19 Although widely used in OV research,20 such commercial GBM cell lines often do not retain the genotype, phenotype, and intratumoral heterogeneity of the original tumor. In contrast, patient-derived cell lines (PDCLs) are more representative in vitro models of the disease.21 Furthermore, most studies investigate OVs in a limited number of cell lines, which does not reflect the high intertumoral heterogeneity of GBM patients.19 This may also contribute to the observed reduction in efficacy when transitioning from preclinical research to clinical trials.22 Evaluating the efficacy of multiple OVs across a diverse panel of GBM-PDCLs could provide a more robust foundation for preclinical testing and help identify patient-specific characteristics influencing OV efficacy.

In this study, we aimed to identify tumor-intrinsic factors associated with the efficacy of oncolytic virotherapies. By mapping oncolytic activities of a broad virus panel across a large number of GBM-PDCLs, we identify groups of viruses exhibiting similar cell preferences. We show that GBM subtypes and expression of multiple gene sets correlate with oncolytic activity, with inverse associations observed for two groups of OVs.

Composing a broad panel of OVs and patient-derived GBM cells for evaluation of oncolytic efficacy

We selected 15 viruses that are clinically or preclinically used as oncolytic agents against GBM or other cancers. Characteristics of selected oncolytic viruses

To assess the oncolytic potential of these viruses, we assembled a diverse panel of GBM-PDCLs. This panel contains 14 cell lines from 10 patients (Table S1) and includes four pairs of cell lines originating from the same patient, collected at different tumor stages (CME014 and CME016, CME035 and CME036, CME037 and CME038) or different regions of origin (LBT007/i and LBT007/B). To check whether our cell panel captures the inter- and intratumoral heterogeneity typically observed in GBM,57 we calculated GBM subtype scores of the GBM-PDCLs according to Neftel et al.58 based on bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data. Individual GBM subtype scores (Figure 1A) represent the distribution of all four subtypes within each GBM-PDCL. In Figure 1B, all GBM-PDCLs are divided in a quadrant based on the predominant subtype (maximal subtype score), which shows that each GBM subtype is represented in our cell panel. Second, for eight GBM-PDCLs within our panel, the presence of different subpopulations was revealed through single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis (Figure S1). Next to these GBM-PDCLs, we used three non-cancerous primary cell lines (microglia, astrocytes, and neurons) to assess the tumor specificity and neurotoxicity of the viruses.

OVs have variable oncolytic effects across patient-derived GBM and non-cancer cell lines

To determine the (onco)lytic potential of our virus panel, we infected GBM-PDCLs as well as non-cancerous primary cells with a dilution series of virus and measured their viability, resulting in a dose-response curve for each virus-cell combination (Figure S2). Based on real-time analysis of cytopathogenic effect (CPE) development (Figure S3), we chose a standardized endpoint at day 7 after infection to allow slow-acting viruses to manifest CPE. Although oncolytic activities are frequently expressed as EC50 values,59 we encountered three important limitations. First, because EC50 values are calculated based on a fitted model rather than the actual data, poor fitting can lead to misleading estimates. Second, EC50 values can only be derived from curves that reach 50% cell viability. Indeed, EC50 values could not be determined for 5% (11/210) of virus-GBM-PDCL combinations in this study (Table S2), because the cell viability did not exceed or fall below 50% across the dilutions. Third, EC50 values do not convey information about the maximum effect of an OV. For these reasons, we defined a new (onco)lytic score that captures the entire course of the curve and considers the maximum cytotoxic effect. This score is calculated using the total area under the (dose-response) curve and the minimal relative viability (Figure 2A), resulting in a value between 0 and 100, with higher scores corresponding to a higher (onco)lytic effect.

To evaluate the neurotoxicity of the selected viruses, lytic scores were calculated for healthy brain-derived primary cell lines: human microglia, astrocytes, and neurons (Figure 2B; Figure S4). Viruses were generally well tolerated by microglia (average lytic score 25.73 ± 25.50), with notable exceptions HSV-1 and Coxsackievirus A21 Kuykendall strain (CVA21). Compared to microglia, significantly more toxic effects were observed in astrocytes (average lytic score 53.93 ± 25.99, t test p = 0.0056) and neurons (average lytic score 64.82 ± 20.63, t test p = 7.92 × 10−5), especially for the different strains vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) and VSV.GP, Adenoid 75 (Ad5) and DNX-2401, vaccinia virus Copenhagen strain (VV.C) and vaccinia virus Western Reserve strain (VV.WR). All OVs except CVA21 were selected based on previous evidence of efficacy against GBM. GBM commonly arises from neural stem cells (NSCs), NSC-derived astrocytes, or oligodendrocyte precursor cells and exhibits similarities in cell morphology and gene expression profiles with these three cell types of origin.60 The increased toxicity of OVs in astrocytes and neurons may be attributed to this cellular origin of GBM.

To test the oncolytic potential of our viruses, we assessed the oncolytic scores across the panel of GBM-PDCLs (Figure 2C). Evaluation of oncolytic scores for all virus-PDCL combinations revealed consistent results over two experimental replicates with a median coefficient of variation (CV) of 3.62% ± 2.29% (Figure S4). Some viruses were highly oncolytic (oncolytic score >50) with little variation (CV < 15) between cell lines, as was observed for VSV, VSV.GP, Newcastle disease virus Ulster strain (NDV.Ulster), Ad5, DNX-2401, Newcastle disease virus La Sota strain (NDV.La.Sota), HSV-1, VV.C, and VV.WR (Figure 2D). In contrast, CVA21, Sindbis virus (SINV), Zika virus (ZIKV), measles virus Edmonton-Zagreb vaccine strain (MV.EZ), H1 parvovirus Toolan strain (H1PV), and Reovirus type 3 (Reo3) showed more variation (CV > 15) in oncolytic activity, being oncolytic (oncolytic score >50) only in a subset of GBM-PDCLs. Paired cell lines from different tumor stages (CME014 and CME016, CME035 and CME036, CME037 and CME038) and strains/modifications of the same virus type (VSV and VSV.GP, VV.WR and VV.C, Ad5 and DNX-2401) clustered together (Figure 2C). To evaluate the extent to which oncolytic responses measured in 2D adherent cultures capture those observed in more physiologically relevant 3D spheroid conditions, we performed parallel assays in spheroid models for two representative GBM-PDCLs (LBT007/i and LBT123). Oncolytic scores showed a strong and significant Pearson’s correlation between adherent and spheroid cultures (LBT007/i: ρ = 0.69, p = 0.026; LBT123: ρ = 0.84, p = 0.0022; Figure S5), supporting the validity of our 2D adherent GBM-PDCLs measurements for evaluation of oncolytic activity. Because we observed variable effects of OVs across GBM-PDCLs, we next compared cell line preferences of the OVs.

Clustering based on oncolytic activity reveals two opposite viral groups

To identify potential similarities in the cell line preferences of different OVs, we calculated the correlation coefficients between oncolytic activity profiles for each virus pair (Figure 2E). Oncolytic scores exhibited a similar profile across GBM-PDCLs within three groups of viruses: MV.EZ, SINV, VSV, VSV.GP, HSV-1, VV.C, VV.WR, and ZIKV (group 1), NDV.La.Sota, NDV.Ulster, H1PV, CVA21, and Reo3 (group 2) and Ad5 and DNX-2401 (group 3). Remarkably, group 1 and group 2 viruses showed distinct and opposite oncolytic effects in our examined GBM-PDCLs. Notably, all viruses within group 1 are enveloped, while group 2 viruses are naked with the exception of both NDV strains, which cluster separately from the rest of their group. These observations imply that the GBM-PDCLs in which these OV groups show strong lytic effects share common underlying characteristics.

Response to OVs correlates with expression of GBM subtype markers

Since GBM is well-known for its high heterogeneity, we explored whether oncolytic activities of the selected viruses are associated with tumor composition. We therefore calculated the correlation between oncolytic scores and all four continuous GBM subtype scores58 (as shown in Figure 1A) derived from the transcriptome of the GBM-PDCLs prior to OV treatment (Figure 3A). The oncolytic activity of most viruses significantly correlated with at least one of the four GBM subtypes, except for Ad5, NDV.Ulster, CVA21 and DNX-2401. Hierarchical clustering revealed two OV groups exhibiting opposite preferences for GBM subtypes, corresponding with the previously defined group 1 and group 2 viruses (Figure 2E). Group 1 viruses preferably induced oncolysis in GBM-PDCLs with high astrocyte-like (AC), neural-progenitor-like (NPC) or oligodendrocyte-progenitor-like (OPC) subtype scores, while group 2 viruses showed a preference for the mesenchymal-like (MES) subtype.

We further validated this finding in an independent set of four GBM-PDCLs; two with high MES and low AC/OPC/NPC scores and two with an opposite pattern (Figure 1A). Oncolytic scores were determined based on the dose-response curves as previously described (Figure 3B; Figure S6). To highlight the observed association between molecular subtype and oncolytic activity, we selected GBM-PDCLs with the most extreme MES versus AC/OPC/NPC subtype scores from our initial set and calculated the average difference in oncolytic scores (Figure 3C). This analysis recapitulated the opposing subtype preferences of group 1 and group 2 viruses (Figure 3A). In the validation cell lines, the same preference for MES versus AC/OPC/NPC subtypes was observed for all tested viruses, except for Reo3 (Figure 3C). These data confirm the observed differential preferences for MES versus AC/OPC/NPC subtypes between group 1 and group 2 viruses. Having observed these virus groups have inverse associations to gene expression profiles associated with GBM subtypes, we next sought to identify which genes and biological processes underlie these subtype-specific preferences.

Virus clusters exhibit opposing tumor-intrinsic determinants of oncolysis

To elucidate which tumor-intrinsic genes influence oncolytic activity, we analyzed the correlation between oncolytic scores and gene expression values from bulk RNA-seq of each GBM-PDCL in our set. Genes previously shown to be involved in viral cell entry, replication or tumor tropism of each virus were explicitly investigated (full lists in Table S3). Surprisingly, expression of these genes was generally not significantly correlated with oncolytic activity (Figure 4A). Notable exceptions were intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1) (ρ = 0.81, p = 8.06 × 10−4) and F11 receptor (F11R) (ρ = 0.81, p = 5,60 × 10−5), which encode entry receptors for CVA2161 and Reo3,62 respectively.

To identify genes with a potential role in the oncolytic activity of OV groups, we ranked genes by their correlation coefficients and calculated the median rank across group 1 or 2 viruses. We highlighted the top 10 and bottom 10 genes for both OV groups based on their median ranks (Figure 4B). Additionally, we identified the 10 most divergent genes between the two groups by calculating the absolute difference between their median ranks. These divergent genes represent important contributors to the molecular distinction between the two OV groups. As expected, group 1 and 2 viruses cluster separately based on the correlation of their oncolytic activity with expression of top 10, bottom 10, and divergent genes. Remarkably, genes positively correlated with oncolytic activity of group 1 viruses are generally negatively correlated with oncolytic activity of group 2 viruses, and vice versa. Among these genes were gene subsets associated with glutamatergic synaptic transmission (GRIA1, NLGN2, and CNIH2), regulation of protein transport (RAB11FIP3, DPH3, and NLGN2), protein polyubiquitination (FBXO4, RNF135, and SMURF1) and cell-cell adhesion via plasma membrane adhesion molecules (IGSF9, CADM4, and CLDN23). To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the biological pathways associated with the oncolytic efficacy of each virus, we performed gene set-level analyses.

To attribute functional meaning to the gene lists resulting from the correlation analysis between oncolysis and gene expression, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). A significant normalized enrichment score (NES) reflects the tendency of genes within a particular gene set to be positioned at the top (positive NES) or bottom (negative NES) of the gene list ranked according to their correlation with oncolytic activity. Similar to the analysis of individual genes, we ranked gene sets representing biological processes by their NES and calculated the median rank across group 1 or group 2 viruses. We selected the top 10 and bottom 10 gene sets for both OV groups based on their median ranks. Similar to the gene-level observations, gene sets positively associated with oncolytic activity of group 1 viruses were generally negatively associated with oncolytic activity of group 2 viruses, and vice versa (Figure S7). Next to this, we identified the 10 gene sets with the highest absolute difference in median ranks between both OV groups, to explain the distinct oncolytic activity profiles of OV groups across GBM-PDCLs (Figures 5A and 5B; Figure S7). Gene sets associated with neurodevelopment, particularly those related to synaptic function and α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) glutamate receptor complex, showed a positive NES for group 1 viruses and a negative NES for group 2 viruses (Figure 5A). Thus, expression of these genes positively correlates with oncolytic activity of group 1 viruses, while a negative correlation is observed for group 2 viruses. While group 1 viruses showed an expected negative correlation with interferon- and immune response-related genes, group 2 viruses showed a remarkable positive association, where higher oncolytic activity coincides with higher expression of these genes (Figure 5B). A similar trend was seen for genes associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) organization. Combined, these results suggest that interferon/inflammatory-related, ECM-related, and neurodevelopment-related pathways could be tumor-intrinsic factors responsible for the observed oncolytic preferences of the two OV groups. Next, we further investigated the involvement of AMPA glutamate receptor complex-related genes, whose expression showed opposing associations with oncolytic activities of group 1 and group 2 viruses.

AMPA glutamate receptor signaling differentially modulates SINV and H1PV oncolytic activity

To validate one of the observations in Figure 5A, we tested the functional relevance of the AMPA glutamate receptor complex for oncolytic activity. Pharmacological inhibition of the AMPA glutamate receptor with perampanel did not influence baseline viability of LBT123 cells (Figure 5C). Consistent with the positive correlation between this gene set and the oncolytic activity of the group 1 virus SINV, AMPA glutamate receptor inhibition significantly (t test p = 0.0033) reduced SINV-mediated oncolysis in LBT123. In contrast, the group 2 virus H1PV, whose oncolytic activity negatively correlated with this gene set, displayed no reduced oncolytic activity upon perampanel treatment (Figure 5C). These findings demonstrate that AMPA receptor signaling is required for the oncolytic activity of SINV, but not of H1PV, explaining the observed correlations between this gene set and activity of these viruses.

Discussion

In this study, we have demonstrated the heterogeneous effects of a wide range of OVs on a diverse panel of GBM-PDCLs. We observed significant correlations between oncolytic activity and GBM subtype scores across most viruses, with distinct and contrasting correlation patterns between two identified OV groups. Finally, we identified gene sets associated with neurodevelopment, inflammatory/interferon responses, and ECM organization as key determinants distinguishing the two OV groups identified in our study.

Assessing the oncolytic activities of viruses across GBM-PDCLs showed that some viruses exhibit consistently high oncolytic potency across cell lines, whereas others show variable oncolytic effects (Figures 2C and 2D). However, this does not necessarily indicate that the latter OVs should be disregarded as therapeutic agents. We observed that this heterogeneity in response may be explained, at least in part, by subtype-specific susceptibility (Figures 3A and 3C), highlighting the necessity and potential of personalized OV therapy by aligning OVs with the molecular subtype of the patient’s tumor.

GBM-PDCLs derived from the same patient at different tumor stages (CME014 and CME016, CME035 and CME036, CME037 and CME038) showed the same subtype classification (Figure 1B) and consistently clustered together based on oncolytic scores (Figure 2C). In contrast, GBM-PDCLs obtained from different regions within the same tumor (LBT007/i and LBT007/B) displayed contrasting subtype classifications and oncolytic activities. These observations suggest that intratumoral heterogeneity, likely underlying subtype differences, has a greater influence on OV response than temporal progression.

Based on the correlation between oncolytic activities of our viruses (Figure 2E), we identified different OV groups, two of which showed opposing correlations between oncolytic activity and GBM subtype (Figure 3A). Further analysis indicated that specific gene sets more accurately predict the responsiveness of GBM-PDCLs to OVs (Figures 4B, 5A, and 5B) than individual cellular host factors reported to be important for virus infection and replication (Figure 4A; Table S3). A key difference between the two virus groups appears to be their enveloped (group 1) or non-enveloped (naked, group 2) nature, with NDV as the sole exception. This distinction may be connected to the observed involvement of ECM-related genes in differentiating these virus groups (Figure 5B). Enveloped viruses can enter host cells via receptor binding, followed by fusion of viral and cellular membranes.63 Increased density and stiffness of the ECM might hinder the fusion process, reducing the infection efficiency of these viruses.64 Additionally, high expression of ECM-related genes was previously linked to the MES subtype,58 which correlates with the observed reduced oncolytic activity of group 1 viruses (Figure 3A). In contrast, naked viruses do not enter cells via membrane fusion and often exploit cell surface proteins that are part of or related to the ECM. For instance, Reo3 entry requires the receptor Junctional Adhesion Molecule-A (JAM-A; encoded by F11R),62 for which interplay with the ECM has been reported.65 Next to this, the naked oncolytic adenovirus was shown to exploit ECM changes to facilitate its spread.66 Bio-/physico-chemical and ECM-targeted OV strategies to enhance OV penetration through the ECM have previously been suggested.64 Although this approach may yield promising results, we show that ECM barriers may not hinder the spread of certain viruses, such as the (naked) group 2 viruses identified in this study. The negative association between the ECM and oncolytic activity of enveloped OVs might also explain the unexpected negative correlation between oncolytic activity of VV.WR and VV.C and expression of their entry receptor integrin beta-1 (ITGB1) (Figure 4A). ITGB1 namely links the cytoskeleton with the ECM and activates bidirectional signaling which might, at high ITGB1 expression levels, hinder fusion of the enveloped VV.67

The oncolytic activity of enveloped group 1 viruses showed a positive association with the AC, OPC, and NPC subtypes, as well as neurodevelopment-related gene sets. For HSV-1,26 ZIKV,36 and VV,32 this aligns with their natural tropism for neurotropic environments. More specifically, elevated expression of genes involved in the AMPA glutamate receptor complex was associated with higher oncolytic activity of group 1 viruses (Figure 5A). Glutamate, and consequently stimulation of the glutamate receptor, was previously described to contribute to SINV-induced neuronal death.39 Also, metabotropic glutamate receptor subtype 2 was identified as a factor supporting internalization of the enveloped virus severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2).68 These findings suggest that group 1 viruses may possibly engage the glutamate receptor for entry and potentially exploit glutamate receptor signaling to induce cell death.

It has been reported that co-administration of ruxolitinib, a JAK1 inhibitor, may overcome resistance to OVs such as VSV,69 MV.EZ,70 and HSV-1,71 aligning with the observed negative correlation between the oncolytic activity of these three group 1 viruses and the expression of interferon-related genes (Figure 5B). In contrast to group 1 viruses, group 2 viruses present a somewhat unexpected pattern, as increased expression of interferon-related pathways appears to enhance their oncolytic efficacy (Figure 5B). This suggests that group 2 viruses may either exploit interferon signaling to induce oncolysis or remain less susceptible to interferon-mediated inhibition, setting them apart from the typically observed role of interferons in mounting an antiviral response. Supporting this notion, a previous study72 showed the potential association between type 1 interferon signaling and the induction of a cytokine storm, which may contribute to cell death following infection with the group 2 virus NDV. These contrasting associations imply that therapeutic strategies involving JAK1 inhibition, which may be beneficial for group 1 viruses, could be less effective or even counterproductive for group 2 viruses. These findings highlight the importance of tailoring therapeutic strategies.

GBM tumors are highly heterogeneous across MES, OPC, NPC, and AC states, with notable variability between patients. Some tumors display a clear predominance of one subtype, whereas others exhibit more mixed subtype compositions. The contrasting GBM subtype preferences of the two virus groups observed in this study advocate a subtype-guided clinical strategy: using a single virus when a predominant subtype is present, while using cross-group combinations when tumors have a more mixed composition. Further testing in animal models or humans is needed to test the feasibility of such personalized and combinatorial approaches. Because subtype scores can be influenced by the tumor microenvironment and exhibit plasticity,58 biopsies should be collected at multiple time points and different regions, as previously described.73 It is also critical to investigate combining OVs with more conventional therapies—including radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors—on a case by case basis for each OV, as therapy combinations with different viruses may yield varying outcomes. Importantly, to enhance therapeutic success, a multi-modal predictive framework for OV response may be warranted, integrating subtype scores, expression of specific gene sets, demographic characteristics, immunological markers, and other relevant parameters.

An important limitation of this study is that we did not test the efficacy of viruses in vivo, which is not feasible with such a large number of virus-cell combinations. Our in vitro models lack an immune system, which is a key determinant of the efficacy and neurotoxic profile of OVs. Therefore, careful consideration is necessary when interpreting findings related to the immune system, interferon response and neurotoxicity. In vivo experiments will be required to confirm these findings and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. A strength of this study is that it contains the largest comparative evaluation of OVs described to date, with a broad panel of 14 GBM-PDCLs, originating from 10 patients, and an extensive set of 15 viruses. In comparison, recent studies by Vazaios et al.59 and Schäfer et al.74 assessed OV efficacy in 14 patient-derived pediatric brain tumor or pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) cultures, respectively, but these studies were limited to only four to five viruses. Consistent with our results (Figure 4A), both studies indicated that expression levels of viral entry-related genes are not generally correlated with oncolytic activity. Possibly, the expression of these factors at low levels is sufficient to enable viral entry. Gene sets that were associated with oncolytic activity of the identified OV groups (Figures 5A and 5B) were not reported by Vazaios et al., except for ECM-cell signaling, which correlated with resistance to the HSV-1 variant rQNestin34.5v1, aligning with our results for WT HSV-1. Although their study also focused on high-grade brain tumors, they used pediatric in vitro models whereas we included adult-derived models, which might explain the different outcomes. Next to this, we applied an alternative approach by focusing on gene sets displaying opposite associations with the two OV groups. Finally, we used a novel approach by linking oncolytic activity with subtype scoring of GBM-PDCLs, which has not been explored before for GBM. Schäfer et al. also used this approach in PDAC and reported the association between the quasi-mesenchymal PDAC subtype and increased sensitivity to H1PV, Reo3, and HSV-1.74 These findings further underscore the relevance of molecular subtyping in the personalization and predictive modeling of OV treatment, in GBM as well as other cancers.

In conclusion, we revealed a link between OV efficacy and GBM subtypes and identified groups of viruses with opposite subtype preferences. Despite their biological diversity, viruses within each group appear to rely on common determinants of oncolysis, warranting further research into the underlying mechanism. Understanding these shared requirements for oncolysis will be crucial to improve GBM treatment efficacy, by developing personalized and combinatorial OV therapies using viruses with distinct GBM subtype preferences.

Material and methods

Cell culture

A total of 21 cell lines were used in experiments reported in this manuscript, three non-cancerous primary cell lines (primary neurons, immortalized brain microglia and induced pluripotent stem cells [iPSC]-derived astrocytes), 14 GBM-PDCLs for initial oncolytic activity assessment and correlation analyses, and 4 GBM-PDCLs for validation of OV-subtype associations. GBM-PDCLs, LBT, and CME, were obtained from the Laboratory for Precision Cancer Medicine at KU Leuven. These PDCLs were established from fresh tumor tissue collected from patients undergoing surgical resection at UZ Leuven (all patients provided informed consent) or from retrospective dissociated tumor tissue samples obtained from the Center for Human Genetics. GBM-PDCLs were cultured in NeuroCult NS-A Basal medium (human, STEMCELL Technologies, #05750) with NeuroCult Proliferation Supplement (human, STEMCELL Technologies, #05753), Human Recombinant basic Fibroblast Growth Factor (20 ng/mL, STEMCELL Technologies, #78003.2), Human Recombinant Epidermal Growth Factor (20 ng/mL, STEMCELL Technologies, #78006.2), 0.0002% Heparin sodium salt (STEMCELL Technologies, #07980), and 1% Antibiotic Antimycotic (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #15240062). Plates or flasks were coated with 5 μg/mL laminin (Sigma-Aldrich, #L2020) at least 1 h prior to seeding at 37°C. Human iPSC-derived Astrocytes (Tempo-iAstro, Tempo Bioscience, SKU101) were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)/F12 supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS, HyClone). Culture vessels and 96-well plates were coated with Geltrex LDEV-Free Reduced Growth Factor Basement Membrane Matrix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A1413201) at least 1 h prior to astrocyte seeding at 37°C. Primary Human Neurons (Neuromics, #HNC001) and Immortalized Human Brain Microglia Cells (Neuromics, #HBMCs001) were cultured in Neuron Growth Medium (Neuromics, #HNM001) and Alpha-glia Expansion medium (Neuromics, #AGEM-001), respectively. Culture flasks and multi-well plates were coated with AlphaBioCoat solution (Neuromics, #AC001) 1 h prior to seeding at 37°C. All cells were maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2 and were routinely tested for contamination by mycoplasma. Prior to passaging, cells were stained with Trypan Blue Stain (0.4%, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #15250) and counted with the LUNA-II Automated Cell Counter System (Logos Biosystems, #L40002).

Ethics approval

The Ethics Committee Research UZ/KU Leuven approved the use of GBM-PDCLs for this study under protocol S67312.

Viruses

In total, 15 different virus strains were investigated. Sindbis virus AR-339 strain (SINV; ATCC VR-1585), vaccinia virus Copenhagen strain with insertion of the GFP gene in the thymidine kinase J2R locus (VV.C; kindly gifted by Robert Drillien, E0345 INSERM, EFS-Alsace, France),75 vaccinia virus Western Reserve strain (VV.WR; ATCC VR-1354), measles virus Edmonton-Zagreb vaccine strain (MV.EZ; Serum Institute of India),76 vesicular stomatitis virus Indiana strain (VSV; ATCC VR-1238) and zika virus MR-766 strain (ZIKV; ATCC VR-1838) were propagated in VeroE6 cells (ATCC CRL-1586; RRID: CVCL_0574). Coxsackievirus A21 Kuykendall strain (CVA21; ATCC VR-850) and adenovirus type 5 adenoid 75 strain (Ad5; ATCC VR-5D) were propagated in HeLa cells (ATCC CCL-2; RRID: CVCL_0030). HSV-1 KOS strain (HSV-1; ATCC VR-1493) was propagated in HEL299 cells (ATCC CCL-137; RRID: CVCL_2480) and H1 parvovirus Toolan strain (H1PV; ATCC VR-356) in HeLa Chang liver cells (Cytion #300139; RRID: CVCL_0238). Propagation of SINV, VV.C, VV.WR, MV.EZ, VSV, ZIKV, CVA21, Ad5, HSV-1, and H1PV was performed in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #41965039). VSV.GP77 was propagated in BHK21 clone 13 cells (RRID: CVCL_1915; VSV.GP and cells were kindly gifted by Dr. Guido Wollmann from Medical University of Innsbruck). Adenovirus type 5 with a deletion of 24 base pairs in the E1A region and a modification in the virus fiber with an Arginyl-Glycyl-Aspartic acid (RGD)-4C motif (DNX-2401; gifted by Prof. Dr. Marta Alonso from Cima Universidad de Navarra)78 was propagated in A549 cells (ATCC CCL-185; RRID: CVCL_0023) and Reovirus type 3 Dearing strain (Reo3; ATCC VR-824) was propagated in LLC-MK2 cells (ATCC CCL-7; RRID: CVCL_3009). Propagation of VSV.GP was performed in Glasgow’s MEM (GMEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11710035) with 5% tryptose phosphate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #18050039) and 1% Pen-Strep (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #15140122), propagation of DNX-2401 was performed in Ham’s F12K medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #21127030) and Reo3 propagation was performed in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11320033). Virus stocks were produced by seeding their appropriate propagation cells in medium with 5%–10% heat-inactivated FBS (HyClone) to reach a confluency of ∼80% the next day. Then, medium was replaced by medium with 2.5%–5% heat-inactivated FBS (HyClone) and cells were infected with virus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) ranging from 0.01 to 0.1. When a CPE was observed throughout the whole culture flask, supernatant was collected, centrifuged to remove cell debris, and stored at −80°C. After production, virus stocks were titrated by endpoint dilution assays in the corresponding propagation cell line. Cells were infected with 10-fold serial dilutions of the virus for 7 days at 37°C, followed by visual scoring of CPE. The tissue culture infectious dose 50% (TCID50) was calculated using the method of Reed and Muench.79 Stocks of (NDV.La.Sota; La Sota/Gallus_gallus/Belgium/634/2020) and Newcastle disease virus Ulster strain (NDV.Ulster; Ulster/Gallus_gallus/Belgium/12922/2020) were purchased from Sciensano (Ixelles, Belgium).

Cell viability assay

To evaluate cell viability after viral infection, GBM-PDCLs (10,000 cells/well), primary neurons (7,500 cells/well), immortalized brain microglia (10,000 cells/well) or iPSC-derived astrocytes (50,000 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates. For validation experiments, GBM-PDCLs (5,000 cells/well) were seeded in 384-well plates. For spheroid testing, LBT007/i and LBT123 cells (5,000 cells/well) were seeded, in parallel with standard 384-well plates, in PrimeSurface 384U plates (#MS-9384UZ, FUJIFILM). The following day, cells were infected with virus dilutions ranging from 1/4 to 1/400,000,000. Seven days post-infection, medium was removed from the cells and replaced by 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium (MTS) reagent (CellTiter 96 AQueous Non-Radioactive Cell Proliferation Assay (MTS; Promega, #G5430)), 6-fold diluted in PBS. For spheroid testing, MTS reagent was added directly to the medium in a 1:10 dilution. Absorbance at 490 nm was measured with the SpectraMax Plus 384 Microplate reader (VWR, #735-0346) or Tecan Spark Multimode Microplate reader (VWR, #735-0399). Cell viability experiments were performed twice. For each experiment, we included 2 wells per cell-virus dilution condition.

For experiments with perampanel (#TRC-P285520, LGC standards), LBT123 cells were seeded (10,000 cells/well) in 96-well plates. The following day, cells were treated with 10 μM perampanel 5 h prior to infection with SINV (MOI 1) or H1PV (MOI 25). Seven days post-infection, a cell viability assay using MTS (as described previously) was performed. Cell viability experiments were performed thrice. For each experiment, we included 4 wells per condition.

RNA-seq and transcriptomic analyses

Total RNA of non-infected GBM-PDCLs was extracted using the RNeasy kit (QIAGEN, #74104) and was sequenced in 3 batches. Batch 1 contained GBM-PDCLs CME035, −036, −038, LBT024/B and LBT009, batch 2 contained CME014, −016, −027, −032, −037, −013, and LBT081, −096, −037, and −123, and batch 3 contained LBT024/i, −007/i, and −007/B. RNA-seq was performed by VIB Nucleomics Core (Leuven, Belgium). RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Library Prep Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was performed on Illumina platforms NovaSeq6000 (batch 1) or NextSeq500 (batch 2 and 3) using the High Output kits. Single-end 100-bp (batch 1) or 75-bp (batch 2 and 3) reads were generated. The sequencing quality was monitored and samples were checked to have a Phred quality score above Q30. Low quality ends (<Q20) were trimmed with FastX 0.0.14. Adapters were trimmed with cutadapt 1.15 (RRID: SCR_011841) and reads shorter than 35 bp after (adapter) trimming were removed. Poly-A (>90%), ambiguous (containing N) and low quality (>50% of bases <Q25) reads were removed using FastX 0.0.14 and ShortRead 1.40.0 (RRID: SCR_006813). Using bowtie 2.3.3.1 (RRID: SCR_016368), reads aligning to the PhiX control were removed to ensure removal of contaminants. Filtered reads were mapped to the human genome (GRCh38) using STAR aligner (v.2.5.2b; RRID: SCR_004463). Samtools 1.5 (RRID: SCR_002105) was used to remove non-primary mappings or reads with mapping quality ≤20 and to sort and index the resulting bam-files. FeatureCounts 1.5.3 (RRID: SCR_012919) was used to quantify gene expression at the transcript level. Batch correction was applied using Combat_seq80 (RRID: SCR_010974). For transcriptomic analyses, raw read counts were normalized using the counts per million (CPM) method. Genes with less than 1 CPM in all samples were removed.

GBM subtype classification

GBM subtype scores were calculated as previously described by Neftel et al.58 Seurat 5.1.0 (RRID: SCR_016341) was used to create a Seurat object of the batch-corrected raw read counts of each GBM-PDCL. The Seurat object was log-normalized and GBM subtype scores were calculated with the AddModuleScore function using the GBM meta-module gene lists from Neftel et al.58 The maximum subtype score of each GBM-PDCL determines in which quadrant the cell line is localized in Figure 1B. Coordinates are determined based on log2(|SC1−SC2|+1) with SC1 and SC2 being the maximum subtype score and the subtype score of the neighboring quadrant, respectively. The GBM subtype plot was created using ggplot2 3.5.1 (RRID: SCR_014601).

Investigation of oncolytic score correlates

We calculated the Spearman’s correlation (1) between viruses (based on oncolytic scores) (Figure 2E), (2) between oncolytic scores and GBM subtype scores (Figure 3A), and (3) between oncolytic scores and gene expression levels of GBM-PDCLs prior to OV treatment (Figures 4A and 4B). Spearman’s correlation was calculated using the cor.test function from the stats 4.4.2 package in R and yielded Spearman’s correlation coefficients (ρ) and p values for each OV-OV, OV-subtype score, or OV-gene combination. All host genes were ranked according to their Spearman’s correlation coefficients and functional annotation was then performed using GSEA with the biological process gene sets of the Molecular Signatures database81 (RRID: SCR_016863) using fgsea82 1.20.0 (RRID: SCR_020938) with p values calculated from 10,000 permutations.

scRNA-seq

MULTI-seq was used for labeling of cells prior to pooling and scRNA-seq,83 as previously described.84 Briefly, cells were treated with accutase (STEMCELL Technologies, #07920) for 5 min at 37°C. Single cell suspensions were washed with PBS and resuspended in Anchor:Barcode solution, followed by resuspension in Co-Anchor solution. Cells were washed, counted, and resuspended in 1% BSA in PBS at a concentration of 106 cells/mL. The pooled cell suspension containing several, individually barcoded samples was processed with 10× Genomics Technology. The deMULTIplex R package (https://github.com/chris-mcginnis-ucsf/MULTI-seq) was used to link the cell and MULTI-seq barcode. The Illumina NovaSeq6000 system was used for sequencing. CellRanger (10× genomics; RRID: SCR_017344) was used for mapping to the GRCh38 human reference genome and generation of raw gene expression matrices. Downstream analysis was performed with the Seurat R package 5.1.0.

Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was employed for two-dimensional visualization of intratumoral heterogeneity of the GBM-PDCLs using the first 10 principal components with Seurat’s RunUMAP function. Clusters were defined with Seurat’s FindClusters with a defined resolution of 0.5. Seurat’s DimPlot function was used for visualization of cluster groupings with UMAP reduction.

Statistical analysis

Heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap package 1.0.12 in R with hierarchical clustering using the Euclidean distance metric and complete linkage method. Unless otherwise noted, statistical significance was assumed when p values were smaller than 0.05. Multiple testing correction used the Bonferroni procedure. Median or average values are reported ± median absolute deviation or standard deviation, respectively.
