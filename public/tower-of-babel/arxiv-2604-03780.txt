Ordinary differential equations for regularized variational problems involving semi-discrete optimal transport
Adrien Cances, Luca Nenna, Daniyar Omarov and Brendan Pass

Abstract
We consider entropically regularized, semi-discrete versions of variational problems on the set of probability measures involving optimal transport as well as other terms. We prove that the solutions can be characterized by well-posed ordinary differential equations in the regularization parameter. The initial conditions for these equations, corresponding to solutions to completely regularized problems, are typically known explicitly. The ODE can then be solved to recover the solution for an arbitrary degree of regularization; we verify that the solution is continuous in the regularization parameter, implying that taking the limit of the trajectory yields the solution to the fully unregularized problem. We establish analogous results for a version of the problem when the non-optimal transport term is not scaled with the regularization parameter. We exploit our characterization to numerically solve several example problems using standard ODE methods; this strategy exhibits superior robustness to alternatives such as Newton’s method, as arbitrary initializations are not required.

Introduction

Optimal transport (OT) provides a mathematically rigorous framework for comparing and interpolating probability distributions by computing the minimum cost of moving mass from one measure to another. This simply stated problem enjoys deep connections to a wide variety of mathematical fields, including analysis, geometry, partial differential equations and probability, and a far-reaching suite of applications, in areas such as statistics and machine learning, economics, meteorology and operations research, among others; see, for instance, the monographs
[
21
,
1
,
20
]
for a detailed review.

In this paper, we are interested in class of variational problems involving optimal transport but also other terms. These problems can be generally formulated as follows:

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)\\
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\end{subarray}}\int_{X\times Y}c\;\mathrm{d}\gamma+\,F(\nu)\ ,

(1)

where
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
is a prescribed probability measure on a set
X
,
\nu
a free probability measure on a set
Y
,
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\subset\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)
the set of couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
,
c:X\times Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a given cost function and
F
a given potential function. For different forms of
F
and
c
, this problem models a wide variety of phenomena, including:

•

When
c(x,y)=\lambda|x-y|^{2}
is quadratic on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\lambda\in[0,1]
and
F(\nu)=(1-\lambda)W_{2}^{2}(\nu,\rho)
is
1-\lambda
times the squared Wasserstein distance between
\nu
and a second fixed probability measure
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, minimizers are McCann’s displacement interpolants, parametrized by
\lambda

[
16
]
.

•

Again for
c(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\tau}|x-y|^{2}
, the minimization represents one step in the discrete time Wasserstein gradient flow of
F
, which, for suitable choices of
F
, are equivalent to various PDE, as established by Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto
[
13
]
and systematized in the influential monograph of Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré
[
1
]
.

•

Cournot-Nash equilibria in game theory, where
c(x,y)
models preferences of agents
x\in X
for available strategies
y\in Y
and the first differential of
F
represents a cost agents pay for taking the given strategy (which depends on the distribution
\nu
of strategies the other agents take as well)
[
3
,
2
,
4
]
.

•

In urban planning, where
\mu
represents a population distribution,
\nu
a distribution of locations of a given service,
c(x,y)
the transport cost to move from
x
to
y
and
F
a functional to be minimized (taking into account minimizing congestion, for example)
[
5
,
6
]
.

•

In hedonic price theory, when
c(x,y)
represents the cost to a seller

to produce a good

,
F(\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\nu,\rho)}\int-b(y,z)d\pi(z,y)
is the transport cost between
\nu
and a fixed distribution
\rho
of buyers with respect to transport cost
-b
, where
b(z,y)
represents the preference of buyer
z
for good
y

[
10
]
.

In practice, when computing solutions, it is often desirable to discretize the state space
Y
, making the measures
\nu
discrete and the optimal transport problem represented by the first term in (
1
) semi-discrete. We note, in addition, that assuming the set
Y
is discrete is actually natural in some of the applications described above; for instance, in Cournot-Nash problems, there may be only a finite set of possible strategies, while in hedonic pricing problems, there may be only a finite number of different goods which can be traded. An advantage of semi-discrete optimal transport problems (and, as we will show, of the semi-discrete version of the variational variant (
1
) as well), is that the problem amounts to a finite dimensional convex optimization problem; see, for instance, the detailed treatment in
[
17
]
. Efficient Newton-type solvers were developed in
[
14
,
15
]
, and the regularity of Laguerre cells with respect to the dual potential was analyzed in
[
8
]

Another, extremely pervasive, tool when computing solutions to optimal transport problems is entropic regularization, in which adding an entropic penalty to the discrete transport problem yields a strongly convex objective solvable by Sinkhorn iterations, at a cost several orders of magnitude lower than solving the original linear program
[
7
]
. In the semi-discrete setting, for the quadratic cost, convergence rates as the regularization parameter vanishes were obtained by Delalande
[
9
]
, who also derived an ODE satisfied by the family of solutions to the semi-discrete entropic problem. Hiew, Nenna, and Pass
[
12
,
19
]
established that, for fully discrete marginals and general cost functions, the unique solution to the entropically regularized OT problem is in fact
fully characterized
by a well-posed initial-value problem (IVP). Solving this IVP numerically yields a new algorithm whose output interpolates between the fully regularized and unregularized solutions. Nenna, Omarov, and Pass
[
18
]
proved an analogous result for semi-discrete problems and established a uniform lower bound on the smallest eigenvalue of the Hessian of the dual functional, which is crucial for both theoretical well-posedness and numerical stability.

In this paper, we consider an entropically regularized, semi-discrete version of (
1
). Our main goal, analogous to the work in
[
12
]
and
[
18
]
in the straight optimal transport problem, is to characterize the solution (which now depend on the regularization parameter
1-t
) and exploit it to compute solutions. As in those works, the initial condition, at
t=0
, can often be readily found in closed form, and, unlike the Sinkhorn algorithm, solving the ODE yields simultaneously the entire curve of solutions. As we will show, taking the limit as
t\rightarrow 1
gives the solution to the unregularized problem.

Organization

2
Problem Statement and Preliminary Results

Let
Y=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a set of
N
distinct points and let
X\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a compact set.
Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{ac}(X)\cap L^{\infty}(X)
be an absolutely continuous probability measure on
X
with an
L^{\infty}
density; with a slight abuse of notation we also denote its density by
\mu
. The cost function
c:X\times Y\to{\mathbb{R}}
is assumed to be
\mathcal{C}^{2}
in
x
. We consider the problem

\boxed{\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)\\
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\end{subarray}}t\int_{X\times Y}c\;d\gamma+(1-t)\mathrm{Ent}(\gamma|\mu\otimes\sigma)+tF(\nu)\ ,}

(2)

where
t\in[0,1]
,
\sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{y_{i}}
denotes the counting measure on
Y
, and
F:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
is a lower semi-continuous function whose Legendre transform is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
and
m
-strongly convex. We identify the measure
\nu
with the vector in
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}
whose
i
-th component is
\nu_{i}:=\nu(\{y_{i}\})
, and
\mathrm{Ent}(\cdot|\mu\otimes\sigma)
is the Boltzmann-Shannon relative entropy (or Kullback-Leibler divergence) w.r.t. the product measure
\mu\otimes\sigma
, defined for general probability measures
p,q
as

\mathrm{Ent}(p\,|\,q)=\begin{cases}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\eta\log(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}q&\text{if $p=\eta q$}\ ,\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}\ .\end{cases}

The fact that
q
is a probability measure ensures that
\mathrm{Ent}(p\,|\,q)\geq 0
.
We will also assume that the cost function
c
is
twisted
, meaning that for each fixed
x\in X
, the mapping

y_{i}\mapsto\nabla_{x}c(x,y_{i})

is injective on
Y
.

Introducing Lagrange multipliers
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(X)
and
\psi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, problem (
2
) can be written as

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}_{+}\\
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(X\times Y)\end{subarray}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(X)\\
\psi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\end{subarray}}\quad\langle tc,\gamma\rangle+(1-t)\mathrm{Ent}(\gamma|\mu\otimes\sigma)+tF(\nu)+\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle+\langle\psi,\nu\rangle-\langle\varphi\oplus\psi,\gamma\rangle\ .

The supremum over
\varphi
enforces that
\gamma
has first marginal
\mu
, hence
\gamma
is a probability measure; consequently, the supremum over
\psi
forces the components
\nu_{i}
to be nonnegative and to sum to one. By the Fenchel–Rockafellar theorem we may interchange the supremum and the infimum, yielding

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(X)\\
\psi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\end{subarray}}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}_{+}\\
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(X\times Y)\end{subarray}}\quad\langle tc,\gamma\rangle+(1-t)\mathrm{Ent}(\gamma|\mu\otimes\sigma)+tF(\nu)+\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle+\langle\psi,\nu\rangle-\langle\varphi\oplus\psi,\gamma\rangle\ .

Identifying the Legendre transforms that arise from the infima over
\nu
and
\gamma
, and noting that for any fixed
\psi
the optimal choice of
\varphi
is

\varphi(x)=-(1-t)\log\!\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{\frac{\psi_{i}-tc(x,y_{i})}{1-t}}\Big)\ ,

we obtain the dual problem

\sup_{\psi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\Phi(\psi,t):=\underbrace{-(1-t)\int_{X}\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{\frac{\psi_{i}-tc(x,y_{i})}{1-t}}\right)d\mu(x)}_{=:\mathcal{K}(\psi,t)}-(1-t)-tF^{*}\left(-\frac{\psi}{t}\right)\ .

(3)

Remark 2.1
.

For
t=0
, problem (
2
) reduces to minimizing the entropy with respect to
\mu\otimes\sigma
. The unique solution is therefore
\nu=\tfrac{1}{N}\sigma
(the uniform probability measure on
Y
) and
\gamma=\mu\otimes\nu
.

To simplify notation, write
c_{i}
for the function
c_{i}(x)=c(x,y_{i})
. Each map
\mathcal{K}(\cdot,t)
is readily seen to be concave, and elementary computations give the following expressions for its derivatives, which we will use later on:

\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(\psi,t)=-\int_{X}\pi^{t}(\psi)\,d\mu\ ,

(4)

D^{2}_{\psi,\psi}\mathcal{K}(\psi,t)=\frac{1}{1-t}\int_{X}[\pi^{t}(\psi)\pi^{t}(\psi)^{T}-\operatorname{diag}(\pi^{t}(\psi))]\,d\mu\ ,

(5)

[\partial_{t}\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(\psi,t)]_{j}=\int_{X}\sum_{k=1,k\neq j}^{N}\left(\frac{\Delta_{kj}^{1}(\psi)}{(1-t)^{2}}\right)\pi_{j}^{t}(\psi)\pi_{k}^{t}(\psi)\,d\mu\ ,

(6)

where
\Delta^{t}_{kj}(\psi)=(\psi_{k}-tc_{k})-(\psi_{j}-tc_{j})
and

[\pi^{t}(\psi)]_{j}=\frac{e^{\frac{\psi_{j}-tc_{j}}{1-t}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\frac{\psi_{k}-tc_{k}}{1-t}}}=\frac{1}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\frac{\Delta_{kj}^{t}}{1-t}}}\ .

(7)

In particular,
\mathcal{K}(\cdot,t)
is Lipschitz uniformly in
t
, since each component of its gradient is bounded by one.

Remark 2.2
.

The calculations above hold for all
t<1
. The dual of the unregularized problem arising in (
2
) when
t=1
is

\sup_{\psi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\Phi(\psi,1):=\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{\mathrm{Lag}_{j}(\psi)}[c(x,y_{j})-\psi_{j}]d\mu(x)}_{=:\mathcal{K}(\psi,1)}-F^{*}\left(-\psi\right)\ ,

(8)

where
\mathrm{Lag}_{j}(\psi)=\{x\in X:c(x,y_{j})-\psi_{j}\leq c(x,y_{k})-\psi_{k}\ \forall k\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket\}
denotes the Laguerre cell corresponding to
y_{j}

and weights
\psi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.
The components of the gradient of
\mathcal{K}(\psi,1)
are given by

\partial_{\psi_{j}}\mathcal{K}(\psi,1)=-\int_{X}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{Lag}_{j}(\psi)}\,d\mu=\mu(\mathrm{Lag}_{j}(\psi))\ .

As
t\rightarrow 1^{-}
, we have that
\mathcal{K}(\psi,t)\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(\psi,1)
and
\partial_{\psi_{j}}\mathcal{K}(\psi,1)\rightarrow\partial_{\psi_{j}}\mathcal{K}(\psi,1)
for each
j
, locally uniformly, by Proposition 53 in
[
17
]
.

The derivatives of
\Phi
which we will need are

\nabla_{\psi}\Phi(\psi,t)=\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(\psi,t)+\nabla F^{*}\left(-\frac{\psi}{t}\right)\ ,

(9)

D^{2}_{\psi,\psi}\Phi(\psi,t)=D^{2}_{\psi,\psi}\mathcal{K}(\psi,t)-\frac{1}{t}D^{2}F^{*}\left(-\frac{\psi}{t}\right)\ ,

(10)

\partial_{t}\nabla_{\psi}\Phi(\psi,t)=\partial_{t}\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(\psi,t)+\frac{1}{t^{2}}D^{2}F^{*}\left(-\frac{\psi}{t}\right)\psi\ .

(11)

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be differentiable and
m
-strongly convex, and let
g_{t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
t\in(0,1)
, be convex,
\mathcal{C}^{2}
differentiable Lipschitz maps with Lipschitz constants uniformly bounded by
L
. For
t\in(0,1)
, let
\psi(t)
be the unique solution of
\inf_{\psi}g_{t}(\psi)+tf(\frac{\psi}{t})
. Then
\frac{\psi(t)}{t}
,
t\in(0,1)
, is uniformly bounded.

Proof.

Since
f
is strongly convex, the map
x\mapsto|\nabla f(x)|
is coercive. Indeed, for any
x\neq 0
we have

\displaystyle\langle x,\nabla f(x)\rangle=\langle x,\nabla f(0)\rangle+\int_{0}^{1}\underbrace{\langle x,D^{2}f(sx)x\rangle}_{\geq m|x|^{2}}\,ds\ ,

so, dividing by
|x|
and using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle|\nabla f(x)|\geq m|x|-|\nabla f(0)|\ .

The first-order condition reads
\nabla g_{t}(\psi(t))+\nabla f\big(\frac{\psi(t)}{t}\big)=0
, hence
|\nabla f(\frac{\psi(t)}{t})|\leq L
. Coercivity of
|\nabla f|
therefore implies that
\frac{\psi(t)}{t}
is uniformly bounded for
t\in(0,1)
.
∎

Thanks to the strong convexity assumption on
F^{*}
, Lemma
2.3
directly yields the following result.

Proposition 2.4
.

For
t\in(0,1)
, problem (
3
) has a unique solution
\psi(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. Moreover, the family
\{\frac{\psi(t)}{t}:t\in(0,1)\}
is uniformly bounded; consequently
\psi(t)\xrightarrow[t\to 0+]{}0
.

3
The Governing ODE

Since the dual functional
\Phi(\cdot,t)
is concave it easily follows that the minimizers
\psi(t)
for any
t
are characterized by the first order condition
\nabla_{\psi}\Phi(\psi(t),t)=0
which can be differentiated with respect to
t
via the implicit function theorem, yielding an ODE for the trajectory of solutions. Indeed, the map
(\psi,t)\mapsto\nabla_{\psi}\Phi(\psi,t)
is smooth on
(0,1)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, and its Jacobian in
\psi
is uniformly negative definite because
F^{*}
is strongly convex; hence the hypotheses of the implicit function theorem are satisfied. It follows that the trajectory
t\mapsto\psi(t)
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
on
(0,1)
and satisfies the following ODE on this interval:

D^{2}_{\psi,\psi}\Phi(\psi(t),t)\psi^{\prime}(t)+\partial_{t}\nabla_{\psi}\Phi(\psi(t),t)=0\ .

(12)

Substituting the expressions for the derivatives of
\Phi
, the ODE can be written as

\left[D^{2}_{\psi,\psi}\mathcal{K}(\psi(t),t)-\frac{1}{t}D^{2}F^{*}\left(-\frac{\psi(t)}{t}\right)\right]\psi^{\prime}(t)+\partial_{t}\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(\psi(t),t)+\frac{1}{t^{2}}D^{2}F^{*}\left(-\frac{\psi(t)}{t}\right)\psi(t)=0\ .

Lemma 3.1
.

The trajectory
t\mapsto\psi(t)
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
on
[0,1)
.

Proof.

Multiplying the ODE by
t
and rearranging terms yields

\displaystyle\left[tD^{2}_{\psi,\psi}\mathcal{K}(\psi(t),t)-D^{2}F^{*}\left(-\frac{\psi(t)}{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\left(\psi^{\prime}(t)-\frac{\psi(t)}{t}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\;t\left[D^{2}_{\psi,\psi}\mathcal{K}(\psi(t),t)\frac{\psi(t)}{t}+\partial_{t}\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(\psi(t),t)\right]=0\ .

(13)

By Lemma
2.3
and the smoothness of
\mathcal{K}
, the expression in the second brackets is bounded, so the second term tends to zero as
t\to 0^{+}
. Moreover, the concavity of
\mathcal{K}(\cdot,t)
together with the strong convexity of
F^{*}
implies that the matrix in the first brackets is uniformly negative definite for
t
near
0
. Combining these observations shows that
\psi^{\prime}(t)-\frac{\psi(t)}{t}
converges to
0
as
t\to 0
.

On the other hand, the first-order condition for
t\in(0,1)
can be written

\frac{\psi(t)}{t}=-\nabla F(\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(\psi(t),t))\ .

Since
\lim_{t\to 0}\psi(t)=0
, passing to the limit and using (
4
) and (
7
) yields

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\psi(t)}{t}=-\nabla F\!\left(\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}\right)\ .

(14)

The previous convergence result therefore implies that
t\mapsto\psi(t)
extends to a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
function on
[0,1)
by setting
\psi(0)=0
, and moreover

\psi^{\prime}(0)=-\nabla F\!\left(\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}\right)\ .\qed

(15)

Lemma 3.2
.

The map
t\mapsto\psi(t)
is continuous at
t=1
.

Proof.

Let
(t_{n})
be a strictly increasing sequence with
t_{n}\to 1^{-}
. By Lemma
2.3
, the sequence
(\psi(t_{n}))
has a convergent subsequence; denote its limit by
\widetilde{\psi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. The function
\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(\cdot,t)
converges to
\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(\cdot,1)
locally uniformly as
t\to 1
(see Proposition 53 in
[
17
]
)so taking the limit
n\to\infty
in the first-order condition gives

\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(\widetilde{\psi},1)+\nabla F^{*}(\widetilde{\psi})=0\ ,

which is precisely the first-order condition at
t=1
. Hence
\widetilde{\psi}=\psi(1)
, and continuity at
t=1
follows.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\psi(t)
be a solution to (
3
) for
t\in[0,1)
and set
z(t)=\frac{\psi(t)}{t}
. Then the trajectory
t\mapsto z(t)
is smooth on
(0,1)
and, for each
t\in(0,1)
,
z(t)
is the unique solution of the Cauchy problem

\boxed{\begin{cases}&\left[tD^{2}_{\psi,\psi}\mathcal{K}(tz(t),t)-D^{2}F^{*}\left(-z(t)\right)\right]z^{\prime}(t)+\left[D^{2}_{\psi,\psi}\mathcal{K}(tz(t),t)z(t)+\partial_{t}\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(tz(t),t)\right]=0\ ,\\
&z(0)=-\nabla F(\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1})\ .\end{cases}}

(16)

Proof.

The limit of
z(t)
as
t
tends to
0
follows from (
14
). Dividing (
13
) by
t
and changing variables from
\psi
to

then implies that

solves (
16
).

It remains to show uniqueness; for this, we will apply the Cauchy-Lipschitz theorem to (
16
). Note that since
z(t)
has a limit as
t\rightarrow 0
and is uniformly bounded in
t
by Proposition
2.4
, we can work on the compact domain
[0,\bar{t}]\times\bar{B}(0,R)
, for some
\bar{t}<1
and
R>0
. Since
\mathcal{K}
and
F^{*}
are smooth on this domain, all of their derivatives are bounded there, and by the concavity of
\mathcal{K}(\cdot,t)
and the strong convexity of

, we can rewrite the ODE in (
16
) as

\displaystyle z^{\prime}(t)=G(z(t),t):=-\left[tD^{2}_{\psi,\psi}\mathcal{K}(tz(t),t)-D^{2}F^{*}\left(-z(t)\right)\right]^{-1}\left(D^{2}_{\psi,\psi}\mathcal{K}(tz(t),t)z(t)+\partial_{t}\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(tz(t),t)\right)\ .

The right hand side is Lipschitz on
[0,\bar{t}]\times\bar{B}(0,R)
, and so the Cauchy-Lipschitz theorem then guarantees existence and uniqueness on
[0,\bar{t}]
. Since
\bar{t}<1
was arbitrary, the result holds on
[0,1)
.
∎

The preceding result implies that we can recover the solution
\psi(t)=tz(t)
to the dual problem (
3
) for any

by solving the Cauchy problem (
16
). Lemma
3.2
then implies that we can obtain an arbitrarily good approximation of the solution to the unregularized problem by taking

.

Note that we prove well-posedness of the Cauchy problem (

) because the original ODE (

) is not well defined at

(as the derivatives in (

) and (

) are not well defined there). When solving numerically, as an alternative to solving (

12
) together with the initial condition
\psi(0)=0
, but using directly the derivative
\psi^{\prime}(0)=-\nabla F(\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1})
from (
15
) in the first time step of the numerical scheme (rather than using the ODE to compute the first value of
\psi^{\prime}(0)
). We do this in our computations in Section
5
below.

4
Parameter-Independent Functional

To motivate this section, let us briefly recap what we have done so far. The ultimate goal of the ODE method presented above is to characterize and compute solutions to the unregularized problem (
1
).

The method to do this developed above is to solve the regularized version (
2
), or equivalently its dual (
3
) via the ODE (
16
) and take the limit as
t\rightarrow 1
. Since when
t=1
(
2
) coincides with (
1
), this yields the solution to the unregularized problem.

In this section, we present an alternate, but closely related approach; namely, we regularize the problem but do not multiply the functional
F
by the regularization parameter
t
. Consider the problem

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)\\
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\end{subarray}}t\int_{X\times Y}c\;d\gamma+(1-t)\mathrm{Ent}(\gamma|\mu\otimes\sigma)+F(\nu)\ ,

(17)

and as above, note that at
t=1
(
17
) coincides with (
1
). We present here an alternate ODE method to characterize and compute solutions to (
1
) using this variant; the calculations are very similar, and in several cases simpler than, those for (
2
).
For this problem one can derive the dual formulation and its gradient:

\displaystyle\sup_{\psi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\Psi(\psi,t):=\mathcal{K}(\psi,t)-(1-t)-F^{*}\left(-\psi\right)\ ,\ \nabla\Psi(\psi,t)=\nabla\mathcal{K}(\psi,t)-\nabla F^{*}\left(-\psi\right)\ .

(18)

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be differentiable and
m
-strongly convex, and let
g_{t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
t\in(0,1]
, be convex,
\mathcal{C}^{2}
differentiable Lipschitz maps with Lipschitz constants uniformly bounded by
L
. For
t\in[0,1]
, let
\psi(t)
denote the unique solution of
\inf_{\psi}\,g_{t}(\psi)+f(\psi)
. Then
\psi(t)
,
t\in[0,1]
, is uniformly bounded.

Proof.

The proof follows directly by applying the same arguments as in Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

The mapping
t\mapsto\psi(t)
is continuous at
t=1
, where
\psi(t)
is the unique maximizer of the concave function
\psi\mapsto\Psi(\psi,t)
in (
18
) at time
t
.

Proof.

Let
(t_{n})
be a strictly increasing sequence with
t_{n}\to 1
. By Lemma
4.1
, the family
\{\psi(t_{n})\}_{n}
is bounded, hence, up to a subsequence,
\psi(t_{n})\to\widetilde{\psi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. The gradient
\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(\cdot,t)
converges to
\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(\cdot,1)
locally uniformly as
t\to 1
(see Mérigot). Passing to the limit in the first-order optimality condition for
\psi(t_{n})
yields

\nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{K}(\widetilde{\psi},1)+\nabla F^{*}(\widetilde{\psi})=0\ ,

which is precisely the first-order condition for the unreqularized dual (
8
) at
t=1
. Hence
\widetilde{\psi}=\psi(1)
and the claim follows.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\psi(t)
be a solution to (
18
) for
t\in[0,1)
. Then the trajectory
t\mapsto\psi(t)
is smooth and, for each
t\in[0,1)
,
\psi(t)
is the unique solution of the Cauchy problem

\boxed{\begin{cases}&D_{\psi,\psi}^{2}\Psi(\mathbf{\psi}(t),t)\mathbf{\psi}^{\prime}(t)+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla_{\psi}\Psi(\mathbf{\psi}(t),t)=0\ ,\quad t\in[0,1)\ ,\\
&\mathbf{\psi}(0)=\log N\cdot\mathbf{1}\ .\end{cases}}

(19)

Proof.

The proof is the same as that of Theorem
3.3
, with Lemma
4.1
replacing Lemma
2.3
.
∎

5
Computational Examples

In this section we report numerical simulation results for four variational problems associated with the quadratic cost function
c(x,y)=\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}
, unless otherwise specified. Set

\displaystyle\{G_{1}(\psi,t)\}_{j}

\displaystyle=e^{-\psi_{j}}-\int_{X}\frac{e^{\frac{\psi_{j}-tc(x,y_{j})}{1-t}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\frac{\psi_{k}-tc(x,y_{k})}{1-t}}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x),\quad j=1,2,\dots,N\ ,

(20)

\displaystyle\{G_{2}(\psi,t)\}_{j}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{\psi_{j}}{t}}-\int_{X}\frac{e^{\frac{\psi_{j}-tc(x,y_{j})}{1-t}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\frac{\psi_{k}-tc(x,y_{k})}{1-t}}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\ \ ,\ \ j=1,2,\dots,N\ ,

(21)

\displaystyle\{G_{3}(\psi,t)\}_{j}

\displaystyle=e^{-\psi_{j}}-\int_{X}\frac{e^{\frac{\psi_{j}-tc(x,y_{j})-v_{j}}{1-t}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\frac{\psi_{k}-tc(x,y_{k})-v_{k}}{1-t}}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x),\quad j=1,2,\dots,N\ ,

(22)

\displaystyle\{G_{4}(\psi,t)\}_{j}

\displaystyle=\rho[\mathrm{Lag}_{j}(-\tfrac{\psi_{j}}{t})]-\int_{X}\frac{e^{\frac{\psi_{j}-tc(x,y_{j})-v_{j}}{1-t}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\frac{\psi_{k}-tc(x,y_{k})-v_{k}}{1-t}}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x),\quad j=1,2,\dots,N\ ,

(23)

where
v_{j}=\|y_{j}-P\|^{2}
for some point
P\in X
and
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{ac}(X)
is an absolutely continuous probability measure on
X
. Note that (
20
) and (
21
) represent the gradients of the objective functions in the dual problems corresponding to (
17
) and (
2
), respectively, both associated with

F(\nu)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nu_{i}\log\nu_{i}\ .

The primal problem corresponding to (
22
) is (
17
) with

F(\nu)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\nu_{i}\log\nu_{i}+\|y_{i}-P\|^{2}\bigr)\ .

The primal problem associated with the gradient of the dual objective function
(
23
) is

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)\\
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\end{subarray}}t\int_{X\times Y}c\,\mathrm{d}\gamma+(1-t)\,\mathrm{Ent}(\gamma\mid\mu\otimes\sigma)+tW^{2}_{2}(\rho,\nu)\ ,

(24)

where

\displaystyle W^{2}_{2}(\rho,\nu)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in\Gamma(\rho,\nu)\end{subarray}}\int_{X\times Y}\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\ .

In the non-regularized case
t=1
, the objective of (
24
) is to determine a probability measure
\nu
supported on the finite set
Y=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}
that balances the transport costs between the two populations. When
c
is quadratic, this corresponds to a discrete version of the displacement interpolation of McCann
[
16
]
; in another context, it represents the equilibrium distribution of contracts in a hedonic pricing model, where distributions of buyers and sellers are continuous (modeled by
\mu
and
\rho
respectively) while the set of possible contracts is discrete, modeled by
Y=\{y_{1},...,y_{N}\}

[
10
]
.

For each case we obtain the following initial-value problem (IVP):

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-[\nabla G_{1}(\psi(t),t)]^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G_{1}(\psi(t),t),\quad\psi(0)=\log N\cdot\mathbf{1}\ ,

(P1)

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-[\nabla G_{2}(\psi(t),t)]^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G_{2}(\psi(t),t),\ \psi(0)=\mathbf{0}\ ,\psi^{\prime}(0)=-\log{\frac{1}{N}}\times\mathbf{1}\ ,

(P2)

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-[\nabla G_{3}(\psi(t),t)]^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G_{3}(\psi(t),t),\quad\psi(0)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}+\log\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{-\frac{1}{2}v_{k}}\bigg]\ ,

(P3)

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-[\nabla G_{4}(\psi(t),t)]^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G_{4}(\psi(t),t),\quad\psi(0)=\mathbf{0}\ ,\psi^{\prime}(0)=-\xi^{*}\ ,

(P4)

where
\xi^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a solution of the problem
\rho[\mathrm{Lag}(\xi)]=\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}
. The initial conditions for each problem are obtained either explicitly or from results derived in the previous sections.

In addition, we compute the derivative and Jacobian terms analytically and evaluate the arising integrals numerically using
MATLAB
’s integration routines:
integral
for one-dimensional integrals and
integral2
for two-dimensional integrals.

To solve the IVP-s above we use the Runge–Kutta method from Remark
5.1
with parameters
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{8}
and
\beta=\tfrac{1}{4}
. For each problem, we report the sup-norm of the residual at
t=1
, namely

\texttt{Error}=\|G_{k}(\psi(1),1)\|_{\infty}\ ,\qquad k=1,2,3,4\ .

Remark 5.1

(Third-Order Runge-Kutta Method)
.

We consider the following two-parameter family of third-order Runge-Kutta methods, parameterized by
\alpha
and
\beta
:

\displaystyle k_{1}=f(t_{n},y_{n})\ ,\quad k_{2}=f(t_{n}+c_{2}h,y_{n}+a_{21}hk_{1})\ ,\quad k_{3}=f\!\left(t_{n}+c_{3}h,y_{n}+h(a_{31}k_{1}+a_{32}k_{2})\right)\ ,

\displaystyle y_{n+1}=y_{n}+h\bigl[b_{1}k_{1}+b_{2}k_{2}+b_{3}k_{3}\bigr]\ ,

where
\alpha,\beta\neq 0
,
\alpha\neq\beta
, and
\alpha\neq\tfrac{2}{3}
. The corresponding coefficients are given by

\displaystyle a_{21}=\alpha\ ,\quad a_{31}=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\frac{\beta-3\alpha(1-\alpha)}{3\alpha-2}\ ,\quad a_{32}=-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\frac{\beta-\alpha}{3\alpha-2}\ ,

\displaystyle b_{1}=1-\frac{3\alpha+3\beta-2}{6\alpha\beta}\ ,\quad b_{2}=\frac{3\beta-2}{6\alpha(\beta-\alpha)}\ ,\quad b_{3}=\frac{2-3\alpha}{6\beta(\beta-\alpha)}\ ,\quad c_{1}=\alpha\ ,\quad c_{2}=\beta\ .

Remark 5.2

\displaystyle\psi^{(k+1)}=\psi^{(k)}-[\nabla G(\psi^{(k)})]^{-1}G(\psi^{(k)})\ .

\displaystyle\{\nabla G(\psi)\}_{ii}=-e^{-\psi_{i}}-\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}\{\nabla G(\psi^{(k)})\}_{ij}=-e^{-\psi_{i}}-\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}\frac{\mu(x_{ij})}{2|y_{i}-y_{j}|}\ ,

P1
) and (

\displaystyle\text{Scaled parabola:}\quad y(t)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}t\\

N
, the target points are distributed uniformly with respect to the parameter
t
over the interval
[0,1]
. In
[
11
]
it was shown that, in the unregularized case, this problem admits a nested structure; in terms of Laguerre cells, the triple intersections of successive cells are empty:

\mathrm{Lag}_{i-1}(\mathbf{v})\cap\mathrm{Lag}_{i}(\mathbf{v})\cap\mathrm{Lag}_{i+1}(\mathbf{v})=\emptyset\ ,\ i=2,3,\dots,N-1\ .

11
reports the numerical results obtained with the ODE solvers and illustrates the second-order convergence in
t
. A comparison with Newton’s method and with the sequential method (which directly exploits the nested structure), as reported in Table 1 of
[
11
]
, indicates an approximately order-of-magnitude disadvantage in computational time for the ODE-based approach. This behavior is to be expected: the initial-value formulation provides, alongside the terminal solution, a continuous evolution of the Laguerre cells, tracing the transition from the entropy-regularized state to the solution of the original problem (see Figure
4
); such transient information is not produced by Newton’s method or sequential algorithm.
