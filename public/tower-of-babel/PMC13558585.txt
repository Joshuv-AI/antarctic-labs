A machine learning-based framework for predicting hypertension using serum hematological factors
Authors: Mina Moradi, Vahid Mahdavizadeh, Aida Yavari Kondori, Niloufar Kamkar, Amin Mansoori, Jalal A. Nasiri, Gordon Ferns, Habibollah Esmaily, Majid Ghayour-Mobarhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypertension (HTN) is a leading global cause of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and all-cause mortality, underscoring the need for early detection and intervention. This study aimed to develop a machine learning (ML)-based predictive framework for HTN using routinely available hematologic and clinical parameters. We analyzed data from the Mashhad Stroke and Heart Atherosclerotic Disorder (MASHAD) study (2010–2020). From an initial 9,704 participants, 4,923 individuals (3,033 without and 1,890 with incident HTN) were included after applying predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria and preprocessing. Predictors included hematologic biomarkers (WBC, RBC, MCV, PLT, RDW, PDW, NLR) and clinical/demographic variables (age, sex, smoking, BMI, GFR, physical activity). Missing values (< 10% for most variables) were addressed via median imputation for continuous features. We employed multiple ML algorithms (XGBoost, LightGBM, logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting, k-nearest neighbors, naive Bayes, ExtraTrees, AdaBoost) to develop and compare models. Following model training and performance-based ranking, XGBoost emerged as the top-performing classifier, achieving a ROC-AUC of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.63–0.69). Feature importance analysis identified age, BMI, RBC count, and MCV as the most influential predictors, with consistent rankings across other models. All models exhibited stable performance across training and test sets, indicating minimal overfitting. Our findings demonstrate an ML-driven framework exploring potential signals in HTN risk using routinely available clinical and hematologic data. While the predictive performance reflects the inherent challenge of forecasting a multifactorial disease from baseline variables, this work establishes a transparent, interpretable exploratory benchmark and identifies key modifiable predictors for future validation and enhancement in more comprehensive datasets. Introduction

Hypertension (HTN) is the primary etiological factor for cardiovascular disorders (CVD) and significantly contributes to overall mortality on a global scale1–3. Based on the guidelines set forth by the American Heart Association (AHA), normal blood pressure can be characterized as having a systolic blood pressure measurement below 120 millimeters of mercury (mmHg) and a diastolic blood pressure measurement under 80 mmHg4. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that approximately 1.13 billion individuals worldwide are affected by HTN5. In Iran, the prevalence of HTN among patients with CVD is reported to be 44% overall, with 42% in males and 67% in females6. Despite advances in prevention and treatment of HTN, a large proportion of patients remain undiagnosed until complications develop. This highlights the importance of early risk identification strategies.

Recent research indicates that HTN is linked to notable fluctuations in various hematological indices7. Evidence suggests that changes in hemoglobin (Hgb) levels, mean platelet volume (MPV), white blood cell (WBC) count, hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), red cell distribution width, and mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) may be instrumental in the pathogenesis and underlying mechanisms of HTN8. Notably, alterations in these hematological dimensions are frequently observed in hypertensive cohorts when compared to normotensive controls9. Furthermore, disorders such as polycythemia vera have been shown to exacerbate HTN, illustrating that specific hematological conditions can considerably complicate the management of HTN and its outcomes9,10.

On the other hand, Research suggests that inflammatory processes, as evidenced by alterations in hematological parameters, contribute substantially to the onset and progression of high blood pressure11,12. Elevated levels of inflammatory markers, including WBC count and platelet-distribution-width (PDW), have been associated with hypertensive conditions, highlighting the intricate relationship between immune response and cardiovascular health13,9,14.

In parallel, recent advancements in machine learning (ML) algorithms have gained increasing attention in cardiovascular risk prediction; however, their performance is often comparable to traditional statistical models when applied to clinically constrained datasets. ML techniques are adept at analyzing extensive datasets, encompassing various elements, including body mass index (BMI), baseline blood pressure, age, and smoking status, to identify patterns that can lead to more accurate predictions of HTN risk, thereby underscoring the potential to facilitate targeted and effective treatment strategies15–17. Studies have assessed various algorithms of ML for this purpose17,18. A large-scale study involving 818,603 participants from South Asia demonstrated that advanced models like XGBoost can achieve remarkable accuracy in predicting HTN risk19.

Most existing HTN prediction models rely on vital signs, demographic characteristics, anthropometric measures, lifestyle factors, and in some cases genetic data. In contrast, few studies have examined complete blood count (CBC) parameters as predictive features.

These ML approaches not only contribute to a deeper understanding of the pathophysiology of HTN but also facilitate personalized treatment strategies, which may ultimately result in better patient outcomes20. The capability of these technologies to enhance risk prediction, improve diagnostic precision, and tailor treatment protocols stems from their ability to discern patterns within extensive datasets21. Nonetheless, several challenges like data bias and the interpretability of the resulting AI models remain. Addressing these issues is crucial for fully leveraging their benefits in clinical applications22,23. Furthermore, integrating AI with hematological research could potentially transform HTN management, fostering innovative, data-driven healthcare strategies24,25.

Therefore, this study was aimed to develop and compare multiple ML models for predicting incident HTN using routinely available hematologic and clinical variables in a large Iranian cohort to explore whether inflammatory and hematologic markers provide additional predictive signal for HTN risk assessment. In this context, the primary aim of the present study is to benchmark multiple ML models within a unified analytical framework and to assess the predictive signal of routinely available hematological parameters, rather than to establish the superiority of ML approaches over conventional statistical methods.

Methods

Study design

This study was part of a larger cohort study, the Mashhad Stroke and Heart Atherosclerotic Disorder (MASHAD) study, conducted in Mashhad, Iran26. It encompassed data on demographic, anthropometric, lifestyle, and laboratory measurements. The subjects included in the MASHAD study were selected through stratified cluster random sampling. Out of the initial 9,704 participants in baseline (in phase I), the ultimate number of subjects included was 4,923 (in phase II), of whom 3,033 did not develop HTN, while 1,890 did. The reduction in sample size resulted from the application of predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants with prevalent HTN at baseline, those lacking sufficient follow-up information to ascertain incident HTN, and individuals with missing data in key variables required for the analyses were excluded prior to model development. These exclusions were necessary to ensure a well-defined outcome and consistent data quality across all analyses. Figure 1 shows the flowchart of the data. Participants with missing follow-up measurements were excluded at the cohort-definition stage, as incident HTN could not be determined without longitudinal blood pressure data. No imputation was applied for missing follow-up information. Missing values in baseline predictor variables were handled subsequently during the data preprocessing stage. The detection of patients with HTN at baseline and in follow-up was based on history and physical examination. A history of taking anti-hypertensive medication together with a blood pressure measurement of SBP > 130 and/or DBP > 80 was considered hypertensive. This definition aligns with the 2017 ACC/AHA guidelines, which lowered the threshold to improve early detection and preventive interventions27. Using this definition allows identification of individuals at risk who may be classified as normotensive under the traditional 140/90 mmHg criteria. Medication use was included to correctly classify participants whose blood pressure is controlled pharmacologically. This approach ensures that individuals at risk are appropriately identified without affecting the integrity of the dataset or the analytical process. The systolic and diastolic blood pressure readings were calculated by averaging the results of the second and third consecutive tests, while adhering to standard measurement criteria. Two trained healthcare professionals and a nurse conducted interview sessions to obtain the medical, pharmaceutical, and social histories of the participants. Furthermore, blood and midstream urine samples were employed to conduct biochemical analyses of the patients. Current smoking status was defined as smoking at least once daily, while non-smoker status was defined as having no history of smoking or a history of smoking but no longer engaging. The study focused on blood biomarkers and several baseline demographic and clinical variables (age, sex, BMI, GFR, smoking status, physical activity). While other clinically important variables, such as baseline SBP/DBP progression, lipid profile, glucose, and medication history, are relevant, they were not included in this study due to the research question. The outcome of interest in this study was the occurrence of primary HTN during follow-up. Figure 2 illustrates the schematic overview of the study workflow.

Data preprocessing

Preprocessing raw data before applying ML algorithms ensures that models receive clean and consistent inputs. Thus, the predictions of the model become more reliable. In this study, the data preprocessing was carried out in a structured pipeline consisting of four sequential steps: binary encoding of categorical variables, imputation of missing values, feature scaling, and splitting the dataset into training and test sets. Each step was carefully implemented to prepare the data for subsequent model training and evaluation. To improve clarity, additional details about the preprocessing pipeline were incorporated. Records containing missing or clinically implausible values were excluded based on predetermined criteria28.

Binary encoding

Categorical variables are defined as variables that belong to distinct categories such as labels, names or types. Given that most ML algorithms require numerical inputs, encoding categorical data to numerical data in an appropriate manner gives rise to accurate and meaningful interpretation, bias reduction and enhance predictive accuracy. In order to enhance interpretability, we converted categorical features into binary classifications. Consequently, smoking was categorized as non-smoking (with no history or a past-smoking history) and current smoking, and physical activity as high or other. This binary encoding was chosen to simplify the modeling process and enhance interpretability, while retaining the main distinctions associated with HTN risk.

Missing value imputation

Missing values were imputed during the processing procedure using SimpleImputer. The non-normal distribution of the data led to the selection of the “median” strategy for the continues features. Also, we added a boxplot for each variable as a supplementary file. The use of the median provides a more representative central value under skewed distributions, ensuring that the imputation process did not bias the data and that the statistical characteristics of the features were preserved as much as possible. We assessed the missing data for all variables. The proportion of missing values was low to moderate (generally < 10%). Median imputation was used for continuous variables as it provides a robust measure of central tendency, minimally influenced by skewed distributions and extreme values, thus preserving the statistical characteristics of the data. For categorical variables, missing values were minimal and were handled after binary encoding by assigning the most frequent category or by excluding records with incomplete essential categorical information, according to predefined criteria. Given the low to moderate proportion of missing values (< 10%) and the non-normal distribution of continuous variables, median imputation was chosen as a robust strategy that preserves statistical characteristics without introducing bias.

Feature scaling

Although most tree-based models do not require feature scaling, we applied StandardScaler uniformly to all numerical features of training set to maintain consistency across models and to facilitate fair performance comparison. Distance-based or gradient-based models benefit from scaling, while tree-based models remain unaffected. StandardScaler is a feature scaling method that is employed to normalize the values of numerical features. This technique subtracts the mean of the data and divides it by the standard deviation. As a result, the data is centered around zero and its variability is standardized. Two of the strengths of this method are its ability to handle features measured in different units and the reduction of the impact of outliers without completely eliminating them29.

Train/test split

Furthermore, in order to ensure that the models were evaluated over the same individuals in the test set, the dataset was divided into 70% training and 30% testing sets using a constant method. The train set is the portion of the data that is employed to fit the model and on which the model is trained. The test dataset is a separate subset of the overall data, utilized to evaluate the performance of the final model on unseen data.

Class imbalance assessment

The distribution of the outcome variable was imbalanced, with 3,033 participants remaining non-hypertensive and 1,890 developing HTN during follow-up. Given that the degree of imbalance was moderate, no oversampling, undersampling, or class-weighting techniques were applied. All models were trained on the original distribution to preserve the natural prevalence of HTN in the cohort.

Exploratory data analysis (EDA)

It is essential to understand the underlying structure and characteristics of the data set before applying any statistical modeling or ML techniques. In this study, EDA was conducted to examine the distribution of variables, identify potential patterns, and highlight differences between study groups. The typical summary of continuous data depends on the shape of their distribution. If the data are normally distributed, usually mean is used to summarize the data, as it accurately represents the center of normally distributed data and adequately reflects the entire dataset. For non-normal distributed data using median is preferable, as it is not affected by extremely large or small values and represents a central value of the dataset. Additionally, the interquartile range (IQR) is used to understand how the data are distributed. It shows the range within which most of the data lie and is not highly sensitive to outliers. Consequently, in this study, the continuous variables were reported as median and IQR due to the non-normal distribution of the data. The categorical variables were reported as frequency (%). On the basis of the presence or absence of HTN during the follow-up, the study sample was divided. Then, non-parametric tests were employed to compare the continuous and categorical features among the groups within each classification. Given that the majority of continuous variables in our dataset were found to deviate significantly from a normal distribution upon Shapiro–Wilk testing parametric assumptions were not met. Therefore, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed for comparisons of continuous outcomes between two independent groups. For categorical variables associations and comparisons of proportions were assessed using the Chi‑square and Fisher’s exact tests.

Model training and evaluation

All models were trained following a unified workflow to ensure consistency. The dataset was divided into training and test sets based on the predefined split described earlier. Each algorithm was trained using its standard computational procedure, and multiple runs were performed to ensure stability of performance metrics.

Logistic regression analysis was involved to evaluate the association between selected features and hypertension outcomes for interpretability purposes, and was not used to pre-select features for machine learning models. Initially, the selected features were employed to construct a logistic regression model, which was then adjusted to account for confounding variables28. The objective was to determine the Odds Ratio (OR) of the selected features in order to identify risk and protective factors for HTN. Variables with a p-value < 0.20 in the univariate logistic regression analysis were identified as candidates for inclusion in the initial multivariate model. To address concerns regarding pre-screening, the p < 0.20 threshold was used exclusively within the logistic regression framework to avoid excluding potentially relevant variables for inferential analysis, rather than for machine learning feature selection. This selection process was restricted to the logistic regression analysis itself for the purpose of estimating adjusted associations and was not used to filter or restrict the feature set for machine learning models. The selected features were evaluated in terms of the variance inflation factor (VIF) to reduce the risk of multicollinearity.

A wide range of models was evaluated, including XGBoost, LightGBM, Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting Classifier, k-Nearest Neighbors, Naive Bayes, ExtraTrees, and AdaBoost (Table 1). Logistic Regression was included as one of the candidate models within this unified framework and was evaluated alongside other algorithms using the same set of input variables to ensure consistency across comparisons. A VIF threshold of 5 was applied. Predictors with a VIF > 5 were considered to indicate high multicollinearity, warranting further investigation (e.g., removal of one of the correlated variables) to ensure model stability and the reliability of coefficient estimates (Table 2). Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was performed by repeating the machine learning models without any logistic regression–based pre-screening, and consistent performance trends were observed, supporting the robustness of the results.

For its ML methodology, this study implemented Python 3.11, Jupyter Notebooks2, SciPy 1.11.4, the Scikit Learn module 1.2.2, Pandas 2.1.4, NumPy 1.26.4, XGBoost 2.1.3, and LightGBM 4.5.0. The results were visualized using Seaborn 0.13.2 and Matplotlib 3.7.5. Missing values were imputed during the preprocessing procedure using Simple Imputer (Sect. 2.1.2).

GridSearchCV was implemented to optimize the model parameters using stratified 5-fold cross-validation, selected to balance computational efficiency and robustness. Hyperparameter optimization was performed using a grid-search strategy combined with stratified k-fold cross-validation on the training dataset. Multiple candidate hyperparameter combinations were systematically evaluated for each algorithm, and the optimal configuration was selected based on the highest mean ROC-AUC across cross-validation folds. All hyperparameter tuning procedures were conducted exclusively on the training set to avoid data leakage, while the held-out test set was used only for final model evaluation.

After identifying optimal hyperparameters, stratified 10-fold cross-validation was applied on the training set to obtain more stable estimates of model performance. Hyperparameter ranges were selected according to commonly reported values in prior studies. A structured grid-search approach was applied to identify configurations that yielded the most stable and reliable performance for each algorithm. The models were optimized for the ROC-AUC metric to ensure that we focused on maximizing the model’s ability to distinguish between the positive and negative classes.

Given the moderate imbalance in the outcome distribution (incident HTN vs. non-HTN), PR-AUC was also reported alongside ROC-AUC as a complementary evaluation metric. PR-AUC provides a more informative assessment of model performance in identifying the positive class, particularly in imbalanced datasets where ROC-AUC alone may present an overly optimistic view of performance. Model calibration was additionally evaluated using the Brier score, calibration slope, calibration intercept, and calibration curves to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes.

The model was evaluated on the training data using 10-fold cross-validation after the optimal hyperparameters were identified. Following cross-validation, each model was retrained on the full training dataset using the optimal hyperparameters. The final trained model was then applied to the independent test set to obtain a single performance estimate, ensuring an unbiased evaluation on unseen data. In order to mitigate the probability of overfitting or underfitting, the test set was ultimately reserved for the final stage.

We evaluated a wide range of ML models during the initial phase of model training. The models were explained in Table 1. After conducting an evaluation of all models, we determined the four most effective classifiers. These four most effective classifiers were selected based on their overall performance across multiple evaluation metrics, with primary emphasis on ROC-AUC. To balance model comprehensiveness with interpretability, a limited number of classifiers were retained for downstream analysis. The selection of four models was based on their consistent and stable performance across multiple evaluation metrics rather than on a strict numerical cutoff. This strategy was adopted to avoid redundancy among models with comparable performance while maintaining clarity in result presentation.

Additionally, the Decision Tree estimator was chosen to plot a decision tree. These models were selected based on the most advantageous overall performance across a variety of evaluation metrics and were subsequently employed for a more comprehensive analysis. Given the low proportion of missing data and the study’s emphasis on predictive performance and model comparability, simple imputation strategies were preferred over more complex approaches such as multiple imputation or k-nearest neighbors, in order to maintain reproducibility and avoid introducing additional model-dependent uncertainty into the learning pipeline.

Model interpretation

We visualized feature importance scores for tree-based models to obtain insight into the most influential predictors. Feature importance for tree-based models was derived using the impurity-based method (mean decrease in impurity, also known as Gini importance), which is the default approach implemented in these algorithms. This method reflects the relative contribution of each feature to reducing node impurity across the ensemble. The resulting importance values were used for descriptive interpretation of model behavior rather than for causal inference, acknowledging that impurity-based measures may be influenced by feature scale and correlation. Feature importance diagrams were generated to improve understanding of the model’s decision-making process. Feature importance scores were extracted from the final fitted tree-based models trained on the full training dataset after hyperparameter optimization, rather than being averaged across cross-validation folds. These importance values were intended for descriptive interpretation of model behavior only It should be noted that impurity-based feature importance measures are known to be biased toward variables with higher cardinality or a greater number of potential split points. Accordingly, these importance scores were used solely for exploratory and descriptive interpretation of model behavior and were not intended for definitive variable ranking or causal inference. In the present study, interpretability was addressed through complementary approaches, including multivariate logistic regression for estimating effect sizes, impurity-based feature importance for identifying influential predictors, and decision tree visualization for clinically interpretable decision pathways. While more advanced techniques such as could provide additional insights into feature interactions and local explanations, these methods were beyond the scope of the current study, which focused primarily on model benchmarking and evaluation of predictive signals. Future studies may incorporate SHAP-based analyses to further enhance interpretability.

Ethics

This analysis of the MASHAD cohort data was conducted under the ongoing ethical approval (IR.MUMS.IRH.REC.1395.458), which covers all phases (phase I in 2007 and phase II in 2017) of the study.

Results

Comparison of demographic and clinical factors between subjects with and without HTN during the follow-up period is shown in Table 4. The findings indicated that the number of females was higher in the hypertensive and non-hypertensive, while the difference was statistically non-significant (p-value = 0.47(. The median age of patients with HTN was considerably higher than that of those without (48 years, IQR 12 versus 44 years, IQR 10 respectively) (p < 0.001). The median BMI of patients with HTN was 27.92 (IQR 5.62), which was significantly higher than that of non-hypertensive subjects (26.44, IQR 5.80) (p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Furthermore, these features remained significant after a multivariate Logistic Regression was conducted and the confounding variables (age, sex, smoking status, BMI, GFR, and physical activity) were adjusted. While statistically significant, the clinical impact of platelets alone is limited, contributing mainly in combination with other features in the multivariate model. Although platelet count showed a statistically significant association with incident HTN (p < 0.05), the effect size was very small (e.g., odds ratio = 1.001, 95% CI: 1.000–1.002). This suggests that platelet count, despite being statistically significant in our large cohort, has negligible practical impact on HTN risk prediction.

Training the models and subsequently ranking them according to their performance led to the selection of four models. The XGBoost Classifier was the highest-ranked classifier between four models, with a ROC-AUC of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.63–0.69), PR-AUC of 0.52 (Fig. 4), an accuracy of 0.63, and an F1 score of 0.57 (Table 5). In addition to discrimination metrics, model calibration was assessed to evaluate the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. The XGBoost model yielded a Brier score of 0.212, a calibration slope of 1.14, and a calibration intercept of 0.048, indicating reasonably good overall calibration with slight overestimation of predicted risks. The calibration curve further demonstrated the alignment between predicted and observed probabilities across risk strata (Fig. 5). Additionally, the XGBoost model effectively identified 353 positives out of a total of 566 (Fig. 6). A comprehensive report of the performance metrics for each model is presented in Table 5. Consequently, it is noteworthy that all of the models demonstrated consistent performance in both the training and test sets, indicating that there was negligible overfitting. The similarity in ROC-AUC values across different ML algorithms indicates that the predictive information contained in the available baseline variables was captured consistently by all models. Given that all algorithms were trained on the same set of predictors and identified similar key features, convergence in performance is expected. This finding suggests stable, albeit moderate, discriminative ability when predicting incident HTN based on routinely collected baseline data. From a clinical standpoint, a ROC-AUC of 0.66 reflects moderate discriminative ability. While this performance may not be sufficient for standalone clinical decision-making, it demonstrates the presence of meaningful predictive patterns in routinely collected baseline data and may support population-level risk stratification or future model refinement. Notably, variables such as age, BMI, and RBC ranked highly in both approaches (ML models and multivariable logistic regression), suggesting consistent associations with incident HTN. This concordance reinforces the robustness of these predictors from a descriptive perspective, though it does not imply causal inference from either method alone.

Discussion

This study presents compelling evidence for the association between hematological parameters and HTN, emphasizing the role of ML as a complementary analytical tool for evaluating predictive patterns rather than as a superior alternative to conventional methods. The findings reveal that RBC count, MCV, and platelet count significantly correlate with HTN, as demonstrated in both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. These results align with previous research reporting hematological changes in hypertensive populations, suggesting their involvement in the pathophysiology of elevated blood pressure7,8,19.

The link between hematological indices and systemic HTN is grounded in the basic principles of fluid dynamics within the vascular system. RBCs represent the major cellular component of blood and are the primary determinants of whole blood viscosity. In hypertensive populations, both quantitative and qualitative changes in the RBC compartment are commonly observed and reflect chronic systemic adaptation and target organ stress7. Elevated RBC levels directly affect blood viscosity, leading to an increase in systemic vascular resistance and, as a result, a rise in blood pressure. This observation is supported by large cross sectional and longitudinal studies that documented elevated RBC counts in individuals with HTN. The MASHAD study including 9,808 participants reported significantly higher mean RBC counts in hypertensive subjects compared with normotensive controls in this study. In contrast, a cohort from Northeast Ethiopia showed RBC counts of 5.09 ± 0.58 × 10^6/µL in hypertensive patients versus 5.33 ± 0.35 × 10^6/µL in apparently healthy blood donors8,10. These variants may exist depending on the specific age and gender match.

The clinical impact of RBC count is closely linked to HCT which represents the proportion of blood volume occupied by RBCs. HCT is considered an independent risk factor for HTN45. In a univariate logistic regression analysis, Emamian et al. identified HCT as a highly significant predictor of HTN. Similar findings were reported in large Chinese cohorts where increased RBC count and hemoglobin mass were positively associated with incident HTN. The underlying biophysical mechanism relates to the direct relationship between HCT and whole blood viscosity. Higher HCT increases resistance to blood flow which elevates peripheral resistance and contributes directly to increased diastolic blood pressure (DBP)10,46,47.

At the same time, RBCs play a dual role in cardiovascular physiology. While elevated RBC counts increase viscosity and favor vascular occlusion thereby increasing the risk of myocardial infarction and stroke, RBCs also exert cardioprotective effects48. RBCs carry nitric oxide synthase (NOS) which is responsible for nitric oxide (NO) production. NO is a potent vasodilator that preserves endothelial function and vascular homeostasis. Under hypoxic conditions or mechanical shear stress, RBCs release adenosine triphosphate (ATP) which stimulates endothelial NO production and promotes vasodilation with a consequent reduction in blood pressure. In hypertensive individuals this protective mechanism is often impaired due to oxidative stress. Increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage the RBC membrane which reduces deformability and increases intravascular hemolysis. The release of free hemoglobin into plasma then scavenges NO and eliminates its vasodilatory effect thereby creating a pro-hypertensive state49,50.

Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) reflects the average size of RBCs and serves as an indicator of erythropoietic quality and volume regulation. The association between MCV and HTN is complex and is partly masked by the inverse relationship between MCV and total RBC count. In the present study, lower MCV values were significantly associated with HTN, aligning with Alemayehu et al., who similarly identified lower MCV levels in hypertensive patients, aligning with our findings8.

Data from large clinical cohorts such as the Honolulu Heart Program suggest that increasing RBC counts are often accompanied by reduced individual cell size. This adaptation may help preserve capillary flow despite an increased total cellular volume. Nevertheless, microcytic states are commonly linked to poorer outcomes in chronic diseases. In HTN, disease duration shows a negative correlation with MCV which suggests progressive impairment of mechanisms that regulate RBC size. The coexistence of microcytic anemia and HTN substantially increases cardiovascular burden because reduced hemoglobin levels impair oxygen delivery and trigger compensatory hemodynamic responses that further elevate heart rate and blood pressure51,52. In contrast, some smaller studies have reported higher MCV values in hypertensive groups. These discrepancies may be related to differences in patient characteristics or treatment regimens like diuretic use which can modify the relationship between MCV and blood pressure indices. This variability underscores the need for careful patient stratification in studies of MCV and HTN8.

The role of platelet count is more nuanced than often recognized. Even slight increases can indicate heightened platelet aggregation and activation, which are processes associated with vascular injury, an increased thrombotic risk, and damage to end organs from HTN. Karabulut and Karadag highlighted that variations in platelet indices, such as MPV and PDW, frequently occur alongside HTN, suggesting a link to altered thrombopoiesis and systemic inflammation53. Recent genetic and observational studies suggest that platelet count may exert a modest causal effect on blood pressure. Observational data from the Lifelines Cohort Study involving 167,785 participants demonstrated positive associations between platelet count and both systolic blood pressure (SBP) and DBP. Bi directional Mendelian randomization analyses using UK Biobank data further supported this relationship by showing that genetically predicted higher platelet counts are associated with increased blood pressure whereas elevated blood pressure does not appear to increase platelet count. These findings imply that platelets may contribute directly to HTN development rather than serving solely as inflammatory markers54.

Interestingly, while parameters such as RDW, PDW, and NLR did not reach statistical significance in this study, they have been extensively documented in other studies as indicators of subclinical inflammation and immune activation in HTN. Our findings hint at the potential for population-specific variability or underpowered subgroup effects, which merit further investigation11,12.

The relationship between inflammation and hematological changes is well established. HTN can instigate inflammatory responses, which are marked by the release of cytokines, oxidative stress, and the activation of leukocytes. In turn, inflammation affects hematopoiesis, resulting in alterations in WBC count, RDW, and other related indices. As highlighted in the literature, this feedback loop may contribute to vascular remodeling, increased arterial stiffness, and endothelial injury, all of which are key characteristics of chronic HTN9,19,22.

This study is among the first to integrate ML approaches with hematological and clinical variables for HTN prediction. Most existing HTN models rely mainly on demographic data, vital signs, lifestyle factors, and occasionally genetic information. Few studies have systematically evaluated CBC parameters as predictors. Conventional statistical methods, such as logistic regression, confirmed the independent associations of RBC count, MCV, and platelets. However, the ML models offered deeper pattern recognition across high-dimensional data. A significant innovation in this research was the application of advanced ML techniques to identify and prioritize predictors of HTN55,56. Among the various models tested, the XGBoost classifier demonstrated the highest performance, achieving a ROC-AUC of 0.66 and a PR-AUC of 0.52, successfully identifying a substantial number of hypertensive cases57,58.

While the XGBoost model’s performance (indicated by a ROC-AUC of 0.66) may be modest, it is still meaningful given the complexity of HTN pathogenesis and the limited input variables. Similar findings were reported in studies by Zhao et al. and Mroz et al., where ML models also predicted HTN using common clinical features, yielding comparable AUC values. Even models with moderate accuracy can offer clinical value when applied at the population level by identifying individuals who may benefit from closer monitoring or early lifestyle intervention9,16. Although the inclusion of additional biochemical or lifestyle variables could further enhance predictive performance, the primary objective of this study was to assess the independent predictive value of routinely collected hematologic markers.

Importantly, the comparable performance observed across different ML algorithms and logistic regression in this study suggests that predictive performance is primarily driven by the available feature set rather than the choice of modeling technique. This finding aligns with recent evidence indicating that, in clinically constrained datasets, ML models often provide limited incremental benefit over traditional approaches. Therefore, the value of this study lies in systematically benchmarking multiple models and evaluating the contribution of hematological parameters within a consistent framework.

In our analysis, decision tree models illuminated high-risk subgroups, specifically older adults (aged over 48.5 years) presenting with low BMI, elevated RBC, and increased red cell distribution width. This subgroup demonstrated a HTN prevalence rate of 100% in the corresponding decision branch. Such findings offer significant opportunities for actionable insights in clinical triage and risk stratification.

Across all tree-based models, including XGBoost, Extra Trees, GBC, and AdaBoost, RBC and MCV consistently ranked among the top five features, alongside age and BMI. This alignment of statistical and computational findings enhances their credibility as reliable biomarkers. The feature importance diagrams not only offer internal validation but also provide a transparent decision-making framework that clinicians can trust and interpret (an essential aspect of integrating AI into healthcare)20,21.

The value of ML models lies in their capacity to identify nonlinear interactions and variable combinations that may not be detected by conventional methods59,60. The primary aim of HTN risk prediction is early identification of individuals at risk. Even a model with moderate accuracy can be useful as a screening tool by separating populations into higher and lower risk groups and guiding further evaluation or preventive strategies.

While the model demonstrates moderate discriminative performance, this level of accuracy is not sufficient for direct clinical deployment. Instead, it should be interpreted as an exploratory tool for identifying potential predictive patterns in routinely collected data.

From a public health standpoint, utilizing routine hematological parameters for predicting HTN risk holds significant potential61. For clinicians in cardiology and hematology, incorporating CBC parameters alongside standard hemodynamic assessments is becoming an essential component of modern risk stratification. These tests are not only widely accessible and cost-effective but also non-invasive, making them particularly suitable for incorporation into screening programs, especially in resource-limited environments62,63.

Moreover, the findings support the concept of personalized medicine in the management of HTN. As highlighted by Savoia et al., tailored strategies (based on individualized risk profiles drawn from clinical and laboratory data) can lead to markedly improved health outcomes. AI models, developed using this multidimensional data, including hematological metrics, facilitate the early identification of at-risk individuals and enable the creation of customized intervention plans, ranging from lifestyle modifications to pharmacological treatments14.

Many published ML models lack external validation because they are trained on limited cohorts. Our study is based on a large community cohort of approximately 4,900 participants with ten years of follow up which provides a robust development dataset. Although external validation was not performed, establishing a transparent benchmark within the MASHAD cohort is a necessary preliminary step before broader application.

Unlike prior studies that reported only final accuracy metrics, this work includes a detailed feature importance analysis across multiple models and consistently identifies age, BMI, RBC count, and MCV as the most influential predictors. The study also presents a clearly defined analytical pipeline including preprocessing, median imputation, and systematic model comparison. By sharing this structured framework, the study provides a reproducible baseline for future research.

Furthermore, the dual role of RBCs as both contributors to vascular risk and carriers of protective signaling pathways represents a promising area for future investigation. Targeting mechanisms that preserve RBC integrity and optimize microvascular flow may lead to novel therapeutic strategies for HTN.

Challenges and future directions

This study has important limitations that contextualize its findings and highlight directions for future research. The models are derived from a single-center cohort (the MASHAD study) and lack external validation, which limits generalizability to populations with differing genetic, environmental, or healthcare backgrounds as highlighted in research conducted by Cho and Park, as well as by Chaikijurajai et al.17,20. The modest predictive performance—reflected in ROC-AUC (≈ 0.66) and PR-AUC (≈ 0.52) values—can be primarily attributed to the restricted set of baseline demographic and hematological predictors. More informative predictors such as longitudinal physiological trends, detailed lifestyle and dietary records, genetic markers, and psychosocial variables were unavailable, inherently capping the attainable accuracy. This interpretation is supported by the consistent performance plateau observed across multiple ML algorithms, indicating a fundamental constraint of the available feature space rather than a failure in model selection or hyperparameter tuning. Furthermore, while interpretability was prioritized through methods such as decision trees, certain branches exhibited 100% classification certainty within small subgroups—a pattern suggestive of overfitting rather than definitive clinical insight. Residual confounding and potential selection bias, inherent to observational designs, also warrant consideration. We recognize that the adoption of the 2017 ACC/AHA definition of hypertension (≥ 130/80 mmHg) differs from the traditional 140/90 mmHg threshold used in many previous hematology–hypertension studies. As a result, the prevalence of hypertension in our cohort is likely higher than in studies using the older definition, and our findings may reflect associations with earlier-stage blood pressure elevation. This should be considered when comparing our results to prior literature. Future studies should report both definitions where possible to facilitate cross-study comparability. A methodological limitation of this study is that the impurity-based feature importance measures derived from tree-based models are non-causal and may be biased toward variables with higher variance or a greater number of split points. Therefore, these rankings should not be interpreted as evidence of causal effects. Instead, we present them as hypothesis-generating findings that require validation through complementary analytical approaches or external mechanistic evidence. We acknowledge limitations regarding generalizability. Participants with baseline hypertension were excluded by design to estimate incident hypertension; thus, our findings do not apply to individuals with pre-existing hypertension. Additionally, 1,715 participants were lost to follow-up. In light of these limitations, the present work should be regarded as a proof-of-concept and an exploratory benchmark. Its primary value lies not in immediate clinical deployment, but in establishing a reproducible, data-driven baseline using routinely available variables, identifying candidate predictors for future hypothesis-driven research, and providing a methodological framework that can be iteratively refined. Future efforts should focus on integrating richer multimodal data, applying advanced feature engineering or selection techniques, and conducting rigorous external validation to determine whether more robust predictive utility can be achieved. Our models were trained on the original, moderately imbalanced class distribution to preserve the natural clinical prevalence. While this supports real-world interpretability, it may have limited the sensitivity for the minority class; future work should evaluate advanced imbalance-handling techniques, such as weighted loss functions or synthetic sampling, to potentially improve predictive performance for incident HTN. Also, future work should compare alternative imputation strategies (e.g., multiple imputation) to confirm the robustness of findings, especially in datasets with greater proportions of missing values.

Ethical and systemic considerations

Finally, the responsible use of AI in healthcare requires a strong focus on addressing bias, ensuring privacy, and implementing regulatory oversight. As Gudigar et al. point out, models that are not properly validated can exacerbate disparities, particularly affecting underrepresented communities21.

Conclusion

This study establishes a significant association between routine hematological factors (RBC, MCV, platelet count) and incident HTN, highlighting their potential role in risk stratification. While the current predictive performance of our models is modest (ROC-AUC ≈ 0.66), reflecting the inherent limitations of using baseline clinical variables, the application of ML provides distinct value beyond traditional regression. It delivers a transparent, interpretable framework—through decision rules and feature importance—that is inherently scalable for integrating richer future data. Rather than serving as a standalone diagnostic tool, this framework offers a practical foundation for a clinical decision-support aid. Rather than serving as a standalone clinical decision-making tool, this framework should be considered as an exploratory and hypothesis-generating approach. While it highlights potentially informative predictors, further work including external validation, calibration assessment, and decision curve analysis is required before any clinical implementation can be considered64. It is important to emphasize that the proposed machine learning framework is exploratory in nature and is not intended for direct clinical decision-making or as a standalone diagnostic or triage tool. Consistent with contemporary standards in health education and clinical computing, our model should be viewed as a population-level risk stratification tool aimed at generating epidemiological insights and supporting future model development. Any translation of this approach into clinical practice would require prospective validation, regulatory oversight, and integration into established clinical workflows. Future research must focus on validating this approach in diverse cohorts, enriching predictive models with longitudinal and multi-modal data, and rigorously evaluating its utility in real-world clinical settings to responsibly advance the integration of AI in combating the global burden of HTN.
