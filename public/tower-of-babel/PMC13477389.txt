External Validation of Published Scoring Systems to Predict Incident Atrial Fibrillation After Cryptogenic Stroke/Embolic Stroke of Undetermined Source
Authors: Gaetano Pinnacchio, Roberto Scacciavillani, Maria Lucia Narducci, Francesco Perna, Gianluigi Bencardino, Giovanni Frisullo, Aldobrando Broccolini, Giacomo Della Marca, Pietro Caliandro, Nicoletta Di Giorgi, Jacopo Lenkowicz, Antonella Carcagnì, Paolo Calabresi, Tommaso Sanna, Gemma Pelargonio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is a common but often undetected cause of cryptogenic stroke (CS) and embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS). Implantable cardiac monitors aid in detecting subclinical AF, but their widespread use is limited by cost and logistical challenges. Several scoring systems exist to predict AF in patients with CS/ESUS, though few have been externally validated. This study was designed to externally validate 7 scoring systems for predicting incident AF in patients with CS/ESUS.MethodsIn this observational study, we analyzed 132 patients with CS or transient ischemic attack who received implantable cardiac monitors. After a comprehensive literature search, 7 prediction models (Coronary Artery Disease or Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease, Hypertension, Elderly, Systolic Heart Failure, Thyroid Disease [C2HEST], Brown ESUS‐AF, ESUS‐AF, Hypertension, Age, Valvular Heart Disease, Peripheral Vascular Disease, Obesity, Congestive Heart Failure, Coronary Artery Disease [HAVOC], Age, Hypercholesterolemia, Tricuspid Regurgitation, Left Ventricular End‐Diastolic Volume, Left Atrium [ACTEL], Age, Stroke Severity, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale >5 to Find Atrial Fibrillation [AS5F], and Coronary, Heart Failure, Age, Stroke Severity, Lipidemia, Sugar, Prior Stroke [CHASE‐LESS]) were applied. The primary outcome was the discriminatory ability to detect AF. Secondary outcomes included evaluating the threshold scores and the performance of such threshold‐based criteria for predicting nonoccurrence of AF with high specificity.ResultsAF was detected in 40 patients (30.3%) over a mean follow‐up of 13±12 months. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curves were C2HEST (0.704), Brown ESUS‐AF (0.755), AS5F (0.726), ESUS‐AF (0.607), HAVOC (0.661), ACTEL (0.650), and CHASE‐LESS (0.671). C2HEST, Brown ESUS‐AF, and AS5F showed acceptable predictive performance (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve >0.7). Threshold‐based criteria using these scores achieved high specificity and positive predictive value. Combining thresholds across scores improved identification of patients unlikely to develop AF.ConclusionsC2HEST, Brown ESUS‐AF, and AS5F scores reliably predicted AF in patients with CS/ESUS and may help identify low‐risk individuals, supporting more targeted use of diagnostic tools and guiding stroke prevention strategies.Keywords: cryptogenic stroke, embolic stroke of undetermined source, insertable cardiac monitor, scoring systems, subclinical atrial fibrillationSubject Categories: Atrial Fibrillation, Clinical Studies, Quality and Outcomes, Ischemic Stroke

Ischemic stroke is a major global cause of disability and death, with a high recurrence risk. 1 In a large population‐based study of patients with a first transient ischemic attack (TIA) or ischemic stroke and an extensive follow‐up of 10 698 patient‐years, the 10‐year risk of recurrent stroke was 31.9% in patients with cryptogenic stroke (CS), 26.9% in those with small vessel disease or large artery disease, and 40% in those with cardioembolic stroke, with no significant differences among the various subtypes. 1

Although most cases result from identifiable mechanisms, ∼30% to 35% are classified as CS due to unclear cause despite an extensive evaluation. 1 , 2 A subgroup of CS that exhibits radiological features consistent with an embolic origin, yet whose source remains unidentified despite an extensive diagnostic evaluation, is defined as embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS). 2 Atrial fibrillation (AF), a common cause of ischemic stroke, often goes undiagnosed because of its asymptomatic and paroxysmal nature. 3 Prolonged cardiac monitoring, particularly with implantable cardiac monitors (ICMs), can detect subclinical AF in ∼15% of CS cases. 4 , 5 , 6

The CRYSTAL AF (Study of Continuous Cardiac Monitoring to Assess Atrial Fibrillation After Cryptogenic Stroke) trial demonstrated increasing AF detection rates with prolonged monitoring—up to 30% at 3 years. 5 This has led to growing interest in using ICMs for secondary stroke prevention. 7 However, the temporal link between subclinical AF and stroke remains uncertain, 8 , 9 and some evidence suggests that atrial cardiomyopathy might play a more direct role in stroke risk than AF itself. 10 The ARCADIA (Atrial Cardiopathy and Antithrombotic Drugs in Prevention After Cryptogenic Stroke) trial, which assessed anticoagulation in patients with atrial cardiopathy but no diagnosed AF, found no significant benefit of apixaban over aspirin. 11

Conversely, trials focusing on patients with device‐detected AF episodes, such as ARTESIA (Apixaban for the Reduction of Thrombo–Embolism in Patients With Device–Detected Sub–Clinical Atrial Fibrillation) 12 and NOAH‐AFNET 6 (Non–Vitamin K Antagonist Oral Anticoagulants in Patients With Atrial High Rate Episodes), 13 showed that anticoagulation can reduce stroke risk—though with an increased risk of bleeding. In particular, in a prespecified subgroup analysis of the ARTESIA trial, which tested the hypothesis that individuals with subclinical AF and a history of stroke or TIA—known to be at higher risk of recurrent stroke—would derive greater benefit from oral anticoagulation for secondary stroke prevention compared with those without a prior history of stroke or TIA, treatment with apixaban resulted in a 7% absolute risk reduction in stroke or systemic embolism over 3.5 years (nearly 2% per year), more than double the corresponding 3% absolute increase in the risk of major bleeding. 14 A meta‐analysis of these 2 larger randomized trials confirmed the benefit of oral anticoagulation in reducing stroke in this population, supporting the clinical relevance of subclinical AF detection. 15

Given these findings, timely AF identification—potentially via ICMs—has emerged as a practical approach to reduce stroke recurrence. However, due to the cost and complexity of widespread ICM use, selecting appropriate candidates is crucial. Several scoring systems have been proposed to predict AF risk post CS, but few have been externally validated.

This study aims to evaluate the predictive performance of several established scoring models for incident AF in a cohort of patients with CS/ESUS monitored with ICMs.

METHODS

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Objectives

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the ability of existing scoring systems to predict incident AF in patients with CS who received an ICM. Secondary aims included identifying score thresholds to predict the nonoccurrence of AF with high specificity and evaluating the performance of both individual and combined threshold‐based criteria.

Study Population

This is an observational study involving 132 consecutive patients who presented to the emergency department of our institution with a diagnosis of CS/ESUS or TIA, all of whom underwent long‐term ECG monitoring via ICMs in accordance with the current guidelines. 16 Exclusion criteria included known stroke cause, prior AF or atrial flutter, recent myocardial infarction or cardiac surgery, permanent indication or contraindication to anticoagulation, and incomplete diagnostic workup. CS/TIA diagnosis was confirmed through clinical symptoms and imaging, with cryptogenic classification following comprehensive evaluation, including 12‐lead ECG, at least 24‐hour ECG monitoring, echocardiography, neck vessel ultrasonography, magnetic resonance angiography or computed tomography angiography of the head and neck for all patients, and thrombophilia screening for patients aged <55 years.

Baseline data collected included demographics, cardiovascular risk factors, CHA2DS2‐VASc and HAS‐BLED scores, renal function, and echocardiographic parameters.

AF was defined as an irregular rhythm lasting >30 seconds without P waves.

All patients underwent ICMs implantation (Reveal XT or LinQ devices, Medtronic) within 30 days of the index neurological event.

Patients were enrolled in a remote monitoring service (Home Monitoring) to allow timely review of data recorded by the ICM and to facilitate early detection of AF, particularly when asymptomatic. Two cardiologists independently reviewed ICM data for AF confirmation, with a third consulted in case of disagreement. Clinical follow‐up visits and in‐person ICM checks were performed at 6 months and annually thereafter, or sooner if deemed necessary based on clinical judgment (eg, AF diagnosis on ICM through home monitoring). AF onset, anticoagulation initiation, and recurrent stroke events were recorded.

Scoring Systems for Validation

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify validated scoring systems for predicting incident AF in patients with CS/ESUS (Figure S1). In summary, we searched PubMed for English‐language studies that developed or validated risk scores for the detection of AF through long‐term ECG monitoring following CS/ESUS. The search strategy required the presence of the following keywords in the title: “stroke” or “poststroke,” “score,” and “atrial fibrillation.” Starting from an initial yield of 209 studies, 7 score were included for external validation after confirming that each scoring system could be retrospectively applied to the data available in our cohort: Coronary Artery Disease or Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease, Hypertension, Elderly, Systolic Heart Failure, Thyroid Disease (C2HEST), 17 Brown ESUS‐AF, 18 ESUS‐AF, 19 Hypertension, Age, Valvular Heart Disease, Peripheral Vascular Disease, Obesity, Congestive Heart Failure, Coronary Artery Disease (HAVOC), 20 Age, Hypercholesterolemia, Tricuspid Regurgitation, Left Ventricular End‐Diastolic Volume, Left Atrium (ACTEL), 21 Age, Stroke Severity, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale >5 to Find Atrial Fibrillation (AS5F), 22 and Coronary, Heart Failure, Age, Stroke Severity, Lipidemia, Sugar, Prior Stroke (CHASE‐LESS)23 (see Figure S1 for the search string used and the detailed flow chart of study selection). Each score was retrospectively calculated for all patients using original criteria (Table S1).

Statistical Analysis

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the populations were summarized using descriptive statistical methods. In particular, continuous variables were reported as mean±SD or median and interquartile range, and categorical variables as percentages.

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Bartlett's test.

Comparisons between groups were conducted using Student's t‐test (or Welch's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test when assumptions were not met) for quantitative variables, and the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression model was implemented to identify risk factors of incident AF.

Model assumptions were evaluated using residual‐based methods, including Schoenfeld residuals to assess the proportional hazards assumption.

When evidence of nonlinearity was detected, continuous variables were modeled using restricted cubic splines within the Cox proportional hazards regression framework.

Linearity was assessed by comparing the linear model with the restricted cubic spline model using a likelihood ratio test. If the likelihood ratio test did not reach statistical significance, the linear Cox proportional hazards model was retained for parsimony.

To construct the multivariable Cox model, candidate variables were selected based on clinical relevance and prior evidence in order to reduce the risk of overfitting. Univariable P values (<0.10) were used as an additional screening criterion to further limit the number of variables included in the final model. When multiple ECG‐derived parameters reflecting the same underlying physiological construct were associated with the outcome, only 1 representative variable was included in the multivariable model to minimize collinearity and improve model stability, prioritizing clinical interpretability rather than statistical significance alone.

Receiver operating characteristic curves were generated to evaluate the discriminative ability of each scoring system. Sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were calculated with corresponding 95% CIs. AUC values were interpreted as acceptable (0.7–0.8), good (0.8–0.9), or excellent (>0.9).

For scores with at least acceptable discrimination (AUCs ≥0.7), thresholds ensuring ≥85% specificity for predicting absence of AF were derived from receiver operating characteristic coordinates. Patients were then categorized according to whether they met these thresholds. Differences between AUC values were compared using the DeLong test. The diagnostic performance of individual score (ie, CHEST, BROWN, AS5F) and of combined threshold strategies (meeting 2 or 3 thresholds) was evaluated. For each predefined cutoff, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, positive and negative likelihood ratios and odds ratio (ORs) were calculated with corresponding 95% CIs. ORs with 95% CIs were derived from contingency tables. When zero cell counts resulted in infinite OR estimates (eg, absence of false positives when specificity=100%), ORs were reported as infinite. When appropriate, a Haldane–Anscombe continuity correction (adding 0.5 to each cell) was applied to allow estimation of CIs. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test.

A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM, USA).

We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology cohort checklist and we have adopted Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis guidelines when writing our report. 24

DISCUSSION

In this study, we externally validated seven scoring systems for predicting incident atrial fibrillation in patients with CS or ESUS, identifying C2HEST, Brown ESUS‐AF, and AS5F as reliable predictors capable of distinguishing patients at low versus higher risk, supporting their potential use in guiding targeted diagnostic and preventive strategies.

Baseline characteristics, rate of incident AF, time to first AF, AF duration and predictors of incident AF were analyzed to verify the suitability of our study population for the external validation of scoring systems designed to predict incident AF in patients with CS/ESUS. The baseline characteristics of our study population closely resembled those reported in the existing literature on CS and ESUS. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 The incidence of AF in patients with CS and ESUS monitored with ICMs demonstrates significant variability across published studies, largely influenced by the duration of monitoring. Detection rates range from 12% at 12 months to 30% at 36 months. 5 , 6 , 25 Furthermore, real‐world evidence has reported even higher detection rates, with some studies identifying AF in up to 40% of patients over extended monitoring periods. 26 Consequently, the AF incidence observed in our cohort appears consistent with these previously reported findings. Older age, increased prevalence of diabetes, and higher CHA2DS2‐VASc scores observed in patients who developed incident AF within our population align with the well‐recognized predictors of AF documented in existing studies. 5 , 6 , 27 Among the ECG parameters, longer P‐wave duration, greater P‐wave dispersion, and a higher prevalence of interatrial block observed in our cohort corroborate previous findings. 28 Similarly, echocardiographic features such as larger left atrial volumes, greater diastolic dysfunction, and elevated estimated left ventricular filling pressure in patients who developed incident AF align with existing research on patients with CS/ESUS, further reinforcing these associations. 27 Multivariate analysis identified several predictors independently associated with incident AF in our cohort at follow‐up, including the CHA2DS2‐VASc score, P wave dispersion, and E/e' ratio. These findings support established risk stratification models for incident AF after CS/ESUS. All patients diagnosed with incident AF initiated oral anticoagulant therapy, reflecting adherence to current clinical guidelines aimed at reducing the risk of stroke. Furthermore, the occurrence of stroke or TIA in a portion of patients aligns with outcomes reported in previous studies. 5 In summary, the alignment of our findings with existing literature supports the suitability of our study population for the external validation of published scoring systems predicting incident AF in patients with CS and ESUS. Based on this premise, the analysis of these scoring systems, which was the primary objective of our study, provided significant insights into their discriminatory power for predicting incident AF. All scoring systems evaluated—C2HEST, BROWN ESUS‐AF, ESUS‐AF, HAVOC, ACTEL, AS5F, and CHASE‐LESS—showed higher scores in patients who developed AF compared with those who did not, confirming their potential clinical utility. The analysis of the discriminatory performance of the individual scoring systems demonstrated that the C2HEST, BROWN ESUS‐AF, and AS5F scores exhibited formally acceptable discriminatory abilities in our study population. Although not all scoring systems reached the predefined thresholds for acceptable discriminatory power, it is important to highlight that the CIs for the AUC of all evaluated systems encompassed values consistent with acceptable or superior discriminative ability. This suggests that, despite some scores not achieving formal statistical significance, the potential for adequate discriminatory performance remains possible for the other scoring systems. The absence of formal significance may be attributable to the limited sample size in our validation study. In addressing the secondary objectives of our study, a pragmatic strategy was employed to develop criteria aimed at identifying patients with a low likelihood of developing incident AF by defining score thresholds that achieved a specificity of at least 85%. This threshold‐based analysis was confined to scoring systems that demonstrated at least acceptable discriminatory ability within our population—namely, the C2HEST, BROWN ESUS‐AF, and AS5F scores. Overall, the performance metrics associated with the CHEST, BROWN, and AS5F threshold criteria underscore their capacity to effectively classify patients both in the derivation and validation cohort. Of interest, the application of these cutoffs revealed that a significant proportion of patients met score‐specific threshold‐based criteria, supporting a potentially relevant role for a tailored resource allocation approach. In addition to evaluating the predictive accuracy of individual thresholds, we also assessed the impact of combining multiple score‐specific threshold criteria to identify patients with a low likelihood of developing AF during follow‐up. The simultaneous presence of at least 2 or 3 score‐specific threshold criteria further enhanced the discriminative power compared with using a single criterion in the derivation cohort, and these findings were confirmed in the validation cohort.

In the context of increasingly limited health care resources, there is a pressing need for more efficient use of available assets. This can be achieved through improved patient risk stratification and the adoption of personalized medicine approaches. Traditionally, scoring systems have been used to predict which patients are at higher risk of developing incident AF. However, our study explored an alternative approach: validating scoring systems to accurately identify, with high specificity, those patients unlikely to develop AF. This reverse‐stratification model, if validated, could potentially enable health care systems to allocate resources more effectively by reducing the use of unnecessary, resource‐intensive diagnostic procedures in low‐risk individuals. This approach would not only enhance cost‐efficiency but also improve the targeted use of diagnostic tools for higher‐risk patients, ultimately supporting better patient care. This shift toward risk‐based resource allocation aligns with the broader goals of precision medicine, which aims to tailor interventions based on individual patient profiles, optimizing clinical outcomes while minimizing unnecessary health care expenditures.

Limitations

One of the limitations of our study is that a true statistical comparison between the baseline clinical characteristics of our population and those of the original cohorts in which the individual scoring systems were validated is not feasible, as we did not have access to patient‐level data from the original studies. This lack of access limits our ability to robustly assess the generalizability of our findings across different patient populations. Additionally, although the criteria developed to classify patients as low risk for incident AF were based on established scores from the literature, the score‐specific threshold‐based criteria used in our study require further validation. It is essential that these criteria are confirmed in external validation cohorts to ensure their applicability and reliability in clinical practice. Without this validation, the use of these scoring systems in diverse populations remains uncertain. Furthermore, this study has a relatively small sample size. Given the limited number of events, the risk of overfitting in the multivariable model cannot be completely excluded, despite efforts to restrict the number of covariates included in the analysis.

CONCLUSIONS

Among the scoring systems analyzed in this study (C2HEST, BROWN ESUS‐AF, ESUS‐AF, HAVOC, ACTEL, AS5F, and CHASE‐LESS), the C2HEST, BROWN, and AS5F scores confirmed their ability to effectively predict incident AF following CS/ESUS in our population. The identification of score‐specific thresholds to classify patients as unlikely to develop AF with high specificity provides a foundation for future studies aimed at validating a reverse‐stratification model. This model could support more personalized and efficient allocation of health care resources by reducing the use of unnecessary, resource‐intensive diagnostic procedures in low‐risk individuals.
