Some Remarks about Integral Categories
Yaroslav Kopylov, Max Zinchenko

Abstract
. We consider integral categories, a class of categories that gained importance in the last three decades in the categorical foundations of algebra and functional analysis. We discuss some familiar criteria for integrality and prove new criteria for one- and two-sided integrality. For the first time in the literature, an example is given of a right but not left integral category.

1.
Introduction

Integral categories were first considered by Bănică and Popescu in 1965 in
[
1
]
under the name
préabéliennes spéciales
. These are preabelian categories (i.e., additive categories with kernels and cokernels) in which epimorphisms are stable under pullbacks and monomorphisms are stable under pushouts. In 2001, Rump called such categories integral and introduced the notions of a left integral category (a preabelian one in which epimorphisms are stable under pullbacks) and a right integral category (a preabelian one in which monomorphisms are stable under pushouts).

Integral categories have recently been considered in representation theory (see, for example,
[
2
,
3
,
12
]
and in functional analysis (see

[
5
,
10
]
).

Numerous examples and non-examples of integral categories can be found in
[
5
]
. However, no example seems to have been known of a right integral category that is not left integral (or vice versa).

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
,
we give the necessary definitions and discuss the interrelationship between the types of preabelian categories we consider. In Section
3
, we discuss some familiar criteria for integrality and prove some extra criteria for one- and two-sided integrality of a preabelian category. Then, in Section
4
, we adapt the results of Kopylov and Wegner
[
9
]
on the derivation of exact couples in a semi-abelian category in the sense of Raikov (using the approach of Eckmann, Hilton
[
4
]
for constructing exact couples, see
[
11
]
), to integral categories. In the last section, Section
5
, we give an explicit example of a right integral category that is not left integral and discuss its consequences.

2.
Notations and Preliminaries

In the sequel, let
\mathcal{A}
be a preabelian category, i.e. an additive category with kernels and cokernels. For a morphism
f\colon E\rightarrow F
in
\mathcal{A}
we denote by
\ker f\colon\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits f\rightarrow E
its kernel and by
\operatorname{cok}\nolimits f\colon F\rightarrow\operatorname{Cok}\nolimits f
its cokernel. Note that kernels and cokernels are unique only up to isomorphisms; we will however speak of
the
kernel and
the
cokernel of a given morphism
f
. We say that
f
is a
kernel
if there is a morphism
g
such that
f=\ker g
.
Cokernels
are defined dually. We denote by
\operatorname{coim}\nolimits f\colon E\rightarrow\operatorname{Coim}\nolimits f
the cokernel of
\ker f
and by
\operatorname{im}\nolimits f\colon\operatorname{Im}\nolimits f\rightarrow F
the kernel of
\operatorname{cok}\nolimits f
. As above,
image
and
coimage
are unique only up to isomorphims but we will also use definite articles in the sequel; the same refers to pullbacks and pushouts. Any morphism
f
in a preabelian category admits a canonical decomposition
f=(\operatorname{im}\nolimits f)\overline{f}\operatorname{coim}\nolimits f
. Following Bănică, Popescu
[
1
]
or Schneiders
[
16
, Definition 1.1.1]
, we say that
f
is
strict
if
\overline{f}
is an isomorphism. By definition,
\mathcal{A}
is abelian if and only if every morphism is strict.
We say that a kernel
\alpha
is semi-stable if all its pushouts along arbitrary morphisms are again kernels. Semi-stable cokernels are defined dually.

According to Rump
[
14
, p. 167]
, we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
left semi-abelian
if
\overline{f}
is a monomorphism for each morphism
f
. Dually, we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
right semi-abelian
if
\overline{f}
is an epimorphism for each morphism
f
. If
\mathcal{A}
is left semi-abelian then each morphism
f
admits a decomposition
f=ip
with a cokernel
p
and a monomorphism
i
. If dually
\mathcal{A}
is right semi-abelian then each morphism
f
admits a decomposition
f=ip
with an epimorphism
p
and a kernel
i
. In fact, the last assertions are even equivalent to the definitions of left and right semi-abelianity (see
[
14
, p. 167]
). A left and right semi-abelian category
is called a semi-abelian category (in the sense of Palamodov):
\mathcal{A}
is semi-abelian iff
\overline{f}
is a
bimorphism
(=
regular morphism
), i.e.
\overline{f}
is a monomorphism and an epimorphism simultaneously, for each morphism
f
.

We need some results on preabelian categories. The first lemma summarizes well-known facts, see, e.g.,
[

. In every such representation we have

(iii)

In every pullback

We stress that the last statement of Lemma

implies that in any preabelian category, kernels pullback to kernels and cokernels pushout to cokernels.

Following Rump
[
14
]
, we say that a preabelian category

\mathcal{A}
is called
left integral
if pullbacks of epimorphisms in
\mathcal{A}
are epimorphisms. Dually,

\mathcal{A}
is
right integral
if pushouts of monomorphisms are monomorphisms.

It is clear that a left (resp., right) integral category is left (resp., right) semi-abelian.

A left and right integral category is called
integral
.

A
left quasi-abelian category
is a preabelian category in which pullbacks of cokernels are cokernels; a
right quasi-abelian category
is a preabelian category in which pushouts of kernels are kernels. A left and right quasi-abelian category is called
quasi-abelian
.

\beta^{\prime}
with
\gamma^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}=0
and
\rho^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}=\beta
. Next, we form the pushout to obtain
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\beta_{1}^{-}
. By the universal property of the pushout we finally get
\gamma_{1}^{-}
with
\gamma_{1}^{-}\beta_{1}^{-}=0
and
\gamma_{1}^{-}\sigma^{\prime}=\gamma^{\prime}
. By
[
8
, Lemma 2.1(iii)]
,
\rho^{\prime}
is a kernel and
\sigma^{\prime}
is a cokernel. The right diagram is constructed dually. The diagrams in (
3
) are obtained by setting
\alpha_{1}=\sigma\rho
.

Lemma 4.5
.

[
9
, Lemmas 3 and 4]
Let
\mathcal{A}
be left semiabelian and let
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\rho
be morphisms. If
\alpha=\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\beta
and
\operatorname{im}\nolimits\beta=\ker(\rho\alpha)
then
\rho
is a monomorphism.

Dually, let
\mathcal{A}
be right semiabelian and let
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\rho
be morphisms. If
\operatorname{coim}\nolimits\alpha=\operatorname{cok}\nolimits(\rho\beta)
and
\rho
is a kernel then
\operatorname{im}\nolimits\beta=\ker(\alpha\rho)
.

Now, we prove Theorems
4.3
and
4.4
.

Proof.

(of Theorem
4.3
)
(i) We show
\operatorname{im}\nolimits\beta_{1}^{-}=\ker\gamma_{1}^{-}
,
\operatorname{im}\nolimits\alpha_{1}=\ker\beta_{1}^{-}
and
\operatorname{im}\nolimits\gamma_{1}^{-}=\ker\alpha_{1}
. By Lemma
4.5
,
\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\beta=\operatorname{coim}\nolimits\gamma
and
\beta=\rho^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}
imply that
\operatorname{im}\nolimits\beta^{\prime}=\ker(\gamma\rho^{\prime})
. Hence,
\operatorname{im}\nolimits\beta^{\prime}=\ker(\rho\gamma^{\prime})=\ker\gamma^{\prime}
and therefore
\operatorname{coim}\nolimits\gamma^{\prime}=\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\operatorname{im}\nolimits\beta^{\prime}=\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\beta^{\prime}
. By
[
8
, Lemma 2.1(iii)]
, we have
\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\beta^{\prime}=(\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\beta_{1}^{-})\sigma^{\prime}
. In addition
\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma_{1}^{-}\sigma^{\prime}
and
\sigma^{\prime}
is a cokernel. Therefore,
\operatorname{coim}\nolimits\gamma^{\prime}=(\operatorname{coim}\nolimits\gamma_{1}^{-})\sigma^{\prime}
by
[
8
, Cor. 2.3(ii) and Proposition 3.2]
. We compute
(\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\beta_{1}^{-})\sigma^{\prime}=\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\beta^{\prime}=\operatorname{coim}\nolimits\gamma^{\prime}=(\operatorname{coim}\nolimits\gamma_{1}^{-})\sigma^{\prime}
which gives
\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\beta_{1}^{-}=\operatorname{coim}\nolimits\gamma_{1}^{-}
since
\sigma^{\prime}
is epic. This is equivalent to
\operatorname{im}\nolimits\beta_{1}^{-}=\ker\gamma_{1}^{-}
.

We have
\beta=\rho^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}
,
\beta
is strict and
\rho^{\prime}
is a kernel. Thus,
\rho^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}=\operatorname{im}\nolimits(\rho^{\prime}\beta^{\prime})\overline{\beta}\operatorname{coim}\nolimits(\rho^{\prime}\beta^{\prime})=\rho^{\prime}(\operatorname{im}\nolimits\beta^{\prime})\overline{\beta}\operatorname{coim}\nolimits(\rho^{\prime}\beta^{\prime})
where the last equality follows from
[
8
, Corollary 2.3(i) and Proposition 3.1]
and
\overline{\beta}
is an isomorphism. We get
\beta^{\prime}=(\operatorname{im}\nolimits\beta^{\prime})\overline{\beta}\operatorname{coim}\nolimits(\rho^{\prime}\beta^{\prime})
and thus
\beta^{\prime}
is strict by
[
16
, Remark 1.1.2(c)]
. Now we can use Proposition
3.1
to conclude that the morphism
\widehat{\sigma}
defined by the equality
\sigma(\ker\beta^{\prime})=(\ker\beta_{1}^{-})\widehat{\sigma}
is an epimorphism. We have
\ker\beta^{\prime}=\ker(\rho^{\prime}\beta^{\prime})=\ker\beta=\rho
and hence
\alpha_{1}=\sigma\rho=(\ker\beta_{1}^{-})\widehat{\sigma}
. Therefore,
\operatorname{im}\nolimits\alpha_{1}=\ker\beta_{1}^{-}
by
[
8
, Corollary 2.3(i) and Proposition 3.1]
.

By dualizing the last paragraph, we obtain
\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\gamma_{1}^{+}=\operatorname{coim}\nolimits\alpha_{1}
and thus
\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\gamma_{1}^{-}=\operatorname{cok}\nolimits(\gamma_{1}^{+}\omega)=\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\gamma_{1}^{+}
with
\omega
as in Theorem
4.1
. Therefore,
\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\gamma_{1}^{-}=\operatorname{coim}\nolimits\alpha_{1}
which is equivalent to
\operatorname{im}\nolimits\gamma_{1}^{-}=\ker\alpha_{1}
.

The exactness of the second couple can be proved dually. The rest of the proof is exactly the same as that of
[
9
, Theorem 1.]
.
∎

Proof.

(of Theorem
4.4
)

Since in a quasi-abelian category kernels are stable under pushouts and cokernels are stable under pullbacks, both
\ker\partial
and
\operatorname{cok}\nolimits\partial
are, in particular, semistable. Hence, by
[
9
, Theorem 1(iii)]
, the associated derived couples coincide.
∎

5.
An Example of one-sided integrality

In this section, we present an elegant example of a non-semi-abelian preabelian category
which was considered by Jeremy Rickard on Math StackExchange.
1
†
†

1

https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/3631067/example-of-a-pre-abelian-category-but-not-a-semi-abelian-category

Below we prove that it is right integral but not left integral.
Since a semi-abelian category is left integral if and only if it is right integral
[
14
, p. 12, Corollary]
, such a category is necessarily not left semi-abelian.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the category of diagrams

U\xrightarrow{\alpha}V\xrightarrow{\beta}W

of vector spaces over a fixed field
k
. Since the category of vector spaces over a fixed field is abelian, it follows that
\mathcal{C}
is preabelian because kernels and cokernels are computed componentwise:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Cok}\nolimits f}

A morphism
(f,g,h)
in
\mathcal{C}
is strict if and only if each of the morphisms
f
,
g
, and
h
is strict in the category of vector spaces. Hence
\mathcal{C}
is an abelian category.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the full subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
consisting of those diagrams for which

V=\operatorname{im}\nolimits\alpha+\ker\beta.

The subcategory
\mathcal{D}
is closed under quotients in
\mathcal{C}
, and therefore cokernels in
\mathcal{D}
exist and coincide with those in
\mathcal{C}
. It follows that
\mathcal{D}
also has kernels. Indeed, let

\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits f\xrightarrow{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits g\xrightarrow{\beta^{\prime\prime}}\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits h

be the kernel of a morphism
(f,g,h)
in
\mathcal{C}
. Then the kernel in
\mathcal{D}
is given by

\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits f\xrightarrow{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}\operatorname{im}\nolimits\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\ker\beta^{\prime\prime}\xrightarrow{\beta^{\prime\prime}}\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits h.

Thus,
\mathcal{D}
is a preabelian category.

Now consider the following nonzero morphism:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces k}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces k}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces k}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces k}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

Its coimage in
\mathcal{D}
is

k\to k\to 0,

whereas its image is

k\to 0\to 0.

Therefore, the canonical morphism from the coimage to the image is not a monomorphism. Hence
\mathcal{D}
is not left semi-abelian, and therefore it is left integral.

Observe that a morphism
(f,g,h)
is a monomorphism in
\mathcal{D}
if and only if it is a monomorphism in
\mathcal{C}
.

Indeed, suppose that
(f,g,h)
is a monomorphism in
\mathcal{D}
, and consider the diagram
(1)
where

\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits f\xrightarrow{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits g\xrightarrow{\beta^{\prime\prime}}\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits h

is the kernel of
(f,g,h)
in
\mathcal{C}
. Then

\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits f\xrightarrow{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}\bigl(\operatorname{im}\nolimits\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\ker\beta^{\prime\prime}\bigr)\xrightarrow{\beta^{\prime\prime}}\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits h

is the kernel of
(f,g,h)
in
\mathcal{D}
. Since
(f,g,h)
is a monomorphism in
\mathcal{D}
, it follows that

\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits f=\operatorname{im}\nolimits\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\ker\beta^{\prime\prime}=\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits h=0.

Since
\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits f=0
, we have
\operatorname{im}\nolimits\alpha^{\prime\prime}=0
, and therefore
\ker\beta^{\prime\prime}=0
. If
\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits g\neq 0
, then

\ker\bigl(\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits g\xrightarrow{\beta^{\prime\prime}}0\bigr)=\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits g\neq 0,

which is impossible. Hence
\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits g=0
. Therefore
(f,g,h)
is a monomorphism in
\mathcal{C}
.

Let

\operatorname{im}\nolimits(\alpha_{1}\oplus\alpha_{2})+\ker(\beta_{1}\oplus\beta_{2})=(\operatorname{im}\nolimits\alpha_{1}\oplus\operatorname{im}\nolimits\alpha_{2})+(\ker\beta_{1}\oplus\ker\beta_{2}),

\operatorname{im}\nolimits(\alpha_{1}\oplus\alpha_{2})+\ker(\beta_{1}\oplus\beta_{2})=(\operatorname{im}\nolimits\alpha_{1}+\ker\beta_{1})\oplus(\operatorname{im}\nolimits\alpha_{2}+\ker\beta_{2}).

. Thus
\mathcal{D}
is closed under direct sums.

Consequently, pushouts of morphisms in

is not left integral but right integral.
