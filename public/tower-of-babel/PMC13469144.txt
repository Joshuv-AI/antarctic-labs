Characterization and application potential of a robust recombinant laccase rTpLcc3 from Trametes polyzona for textile dye decolorization
Authors: Öznur Pehlivan-Günaydın, Ayfer Dilara Bayezit, Dicle Malaymar Pinar, Orkun Pinar, İdil Arslan-Alaton, Ayten Yazgan-Karataş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLaccases (EC 1.10.3.2) are promising biocatalysts for textile wastewater treatment. Here, we identified a novel laccase, TpLcc3, from Trametes polyzona MUCL 38443, heterologously expressed it in Pichia pastoris, and evaluated its biochemical properties and dye-removal performance in citrate buffer (CB) and a simplified simulated industrial dyebath effluent (DB). The glycosylated recombinant enzyme (rTpLcc3) exhibited maximal activity at 70 °C and pH 3.0, broad pH tolerance (pH 4.0–8.0 at 25–30 °C), and substantial thermal stability with half-lives of 187 min at 50 °C and 19 min at 70 °C. rTpLcc3 showed high catalytic efficiency toward ABTS (kcat/Km = 576.76 µM⁻¹ s⁻¹), placing it among high-performing fungal laccases. rTpLcc3 alone effectively decolorized azo dyes-Acid Black 194 (AB194) and Bismarck Brown, with AB194 showing the highest removal in CB (71.39 ± 6.16%) and DB (42.17 ± 5.69%). Mediator supplementation markedly enhanced decolorization, with ABTS enabling rapid, complete removal of AB194, and > 90% removal of Malachite Green in both matrices. Across dye classes, ABTS and syringaldehyde were the most effective mediators in CB (72.4 ± 21.8% and 71.4 ± 28.3% mean decolorization, respectively), while ABTS remained superior in DB (80.1 ± 27.8%). Notably, the TpLcc3/mediator systems significantly reduced cytotoxicity of Malachite Green and Crystal Violet while no increase in cytotoxicity was observed for the remaining dyes under the tested conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-58853-9.Keywords: Laccase, Redox mediators, Simulated dyebath effluent, Textile dye decolorization, Trametes polyzona, Wastewater treatmentSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Environmental sciences, Microbiology

Introduction

The textile industry generates wastewater with highly variable compositions across processing steps, containing dyes, alkaline reagents, buffers, surfactants, binders, and electrolytes. Diverse dye classes, including azoic, anthraquinone, and triarylmethane dyes, are discharged particularly during dyeing and rinsing operations1. Textile dyes represent major persistent pollutants because of their resistance to heat, light, oxidizing agents, and biodegradation1. Their release into aquatic systems causes color and turbidity, limiting light penetration, impairing photosynthetic activity, and ultimately reducing dissolved oxygen levels2. Moreover, dye contamination raises significant toxicological concerns, as anaerobic degradation products can exhibit cytotoxic and carcinogenic properties3. Despite these hazards, many dyes are insufficiently treated prior to discharge because of their refractory nature4.

A range of physical, chemical, and biological approaches has been applied for dye removal. However, physicochemical methods often require high energy inputs, incur substantial operating costs, and may generate secondary pollutants, prompting the need for more sustainable alternatives. Biological treatments offer advantages due to their operation under milder conditions and generally eco-friendly profiles4–6. Dye removal can be achieved using microorganisms or biologically active molecules, yet microbial processes are frequently constrained by dye toxicity and the complexity of maintaining stable process control7,8. In contrast, enzyme-based treatments provide a more controllable and less complex strategy and have therefore attracted increasing attention8.

Laccases (EC 1.10.3.2) constitute a distinctive class of enzymes capable of oxidizing textile dyes8. These copper-containing oxidoreductases catalyze the oxidation of phenolic and non-phenolic substrates coupled to oxygen reduction9, enabling broad industrial applicability in food, beverages, pulp and paper, and textiles9–11. In laccase–mediator systems (LMS), synthetic mediators such as 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) and 1-hydroxybenzotriazole (HBT), as well as natural mediators including syringaldehyde (SA), p-coumaric acid (pCA), and acetosyringone, act as electron shuttles that expand the substrate scope and enhance oxidation efficiency11,12. Importantly, laccase performance is strongly influenced by the producing organism. White-rot fungi typically express multiple laccase isoenzymes involved in lignin degradation, and their laccases often exhibit higher redox potentials than those from other sources13.

Trametes polyzona is a white-rot fungus distinguished by high laccase production. Several strains have been applied to decolorize synthetic dyes: T. polyzona MUCL 38443 efficiently decolorized Acid Blue 6214, and a biofilm of strain LBM-TM5 showed strong activity against azo, anthraquinone, and phthalocyanine dyes15. Multiple isoenzymes from T. polyzona have also been characterized, including laccases from WR710-1, KU-RNW027, and WRF03 with activity toward azo, triarylmethane, and anthraquinone dyes16–18. Nevertheless, each newly identified laccase may reveal isoform-specific catalytic traits that improve performance for targeted remediation, underscoring the need to expand enzymatic options for dye treatment.

Most laccase-based decolorization studies have been conducted in enzyme activity-optimized buffer systems, whereas comparatively few have examined laccase performance under real or simulated wastewater conditions that more faithfully capture effluent complexity. Here, a novel laccase gene, tplcc3, was identified from T. polyzona MUCL 38443 and characterized at the sequence and structural levels, including comparative alignment, phylogenetic analysis, and three-dimensional modelling. The gene was heterologously expressed in Pichia pastoris, and the recombinant enzyme (rTpLcc3) was biochemically characterized. Its decolorization efficiency was evaluated in both optimized citrate buffer and simulated textile dye bath effluent conditions to provide a more realistic assessment of laccase applicability for industrial wastewater treatment. Cytotoxicity was determined before and after enzymatic treatment, enabling an integrated evaluation of its decolorization performance and detoxification capacity.

Materials and methods

Strains, plasmids and media used in the study

The laccase producing white-rot fungus Trametes polyzona MUCL 38443 was supplied through EU-FP6 SMI-IP (IP: 505899). Escherichia coli DH5α and E. coli Top 10F’ cells were used for cloning studies. Wild-type Pichia pastoris (Komagataella phaffii) X-33 strain (Invitrogen™), is a methylotrophic yeast used as the heterologous expression host. All media and protocols related to P. pastoris were performed according to the EasySelect™ Pichia Expression Kit Manual (Invitrogen). Genotypes of all organisms, detailed composition of all media used, and incubation conditions are described in the Supplementary Files.

Cloning of the full-length tplcc3 laccase gene and cDNA

Total RNA was extracted from T. polyzona MUCL 38443 mycelia using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN). To isolate the tplcc3-encoding gene, 5’- and 3’- ends of tplcc3 were determined by employing 5’ and 3’ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) using BD SMARTer™ RACE cDNA Amplification Kit (Clontech). Genomic DNA was isolated from T. polyzona MUCL 38443 mycelia using Fast DNA Spin Kit (MPBio) to serve as a template for amplifying the full-length tplcc3 gene. The full-length tplcc3 gene and cDNA were amplified using SeqAmp DNA Polymerase (Clontech), with genomic DNA and total cDNA serving as template, respectively. All RACE and PCR products were purified from agarose gels using NucleoSpin® Gel and PCR Clean-up kit (Macherey-Nagel) and cloned into a pGEM®-T Easy Cloning Vector (Promega) for sequencing. Details of all cloning studies are described in the Supplementary Materials.

Bioinformatic and structural analysis

The full-length tpcc3 gene and its coding region (cDNA) have been sequenced and deposited under the GenBank accession number PX441362 and PX441363, respectively. Sequence similarities were assessed by aligning laccase gene sequences from the GenBank, EMBL, DDBJ, and PDB databases through the BLAST homology search tool. The predicted amino acid sequence encoded by tplcc3 coding sequence (CDS) was identified using the ExPASy-Translate tool. The molecular weight of the TpLcc3 laccase protein was calculated using the full-length amino acid sequence with the theoretical MW calculator (Compute pI/MW, ExPASy). Multiple sequence alignment was performed with ClustalW2 and visualized with Jalview v.2.11.4.1. Phylogenetic analysis was performed with MEGA 12 software, and the phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Multiple Sequence Alignment (MSA) with the Maximum Likelihood method and the Nearest-Neighbor Interchange model. The 3D structure of TpLcc3 laccase was constructed with ColabFold v1.5.5: AlphaFold2 on the MMseqs2 platform and three-dimensional (3D) model was visualized with ChimeraX v1.9. The secondary structural elements of the predicted 3D protein model, along with laccases from Trametes polyzona, were analyzed using the Easy Sequencing in PostScript (ESPript 3) tool.

Heterologous expression of rTpLcc3 in P. pastoris

For heterologous expression of rTpLcc3 laccase in P. pastoris, the full length tplcc3 cDNA was subcloned into the Pichia expression vector pPICZ B (Invitrogen). Competent P. pastoris cells were transformed using the EasyComp™ Transformation Kit and transformants were screened on Zeocin-supplemented YPD agar plates. Further selection was performed on Minimal Methanol Agar plates with ABTS to confirm laccase expression and secretion. The best laccase producing transformant was selected in BMMY broth after 3 days of incubation at 28 °C with shaking at 250 rpm. To increase the laccase production, the top laccase-producing transformant was grown in 25 mL BMGY medium at 28 °C and 250 rpm until OD600 reached 2.0–6.0. After centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in 25 mL of BMMY medium with an OD600 of 1.0. The effects of methanol (0–1.5% v/v), copper sulfate (0–1.2 mM), and L-alanine (0–1% w/v) concentrations were subsequently assessed.

Purification of recombinant laccase rTpLcc3

Recombinant rTpLcc3 production was performed under optimized conditions, and culture supernatants were harvested by centrifugation (5000 × g, 30 min, 4 °C). The crude enzyme was concentrated by 90% ammonium sulfate precipitation at 4 °C, and collected by centrifugation (5000 × g, 1 h). The pellet was resuspended in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.3) and dialyzed against the same buffer. To remove brown pigments produced by P. pastoris, the enzyme sample was, first, passed through Streamline™ Q XL anion exchange column (Cytiva). The flow-through was buffer exchanged into 20 mM citrate (pH 4.0) and applied to a HiTrap™ SP HP cation-exchange column (Cytiva), eluting stepwise with NaCl (0.1–0.5 M). The active fraction, eluted at 0.3 M NaCl, was further purified on a hydrophobic interaction chromatography column, EconoPac® methyl-HIC (Bio-Rad), equilibrated with 50 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) containing 2 M (NH4)2SO4. Elution was achieved by decreasing the (NH4)2SO4 concentration in 0.25 M steps. The rTpLcc3 fraction eluting at 1 M (NH4)2SO4 was desalted using Amicon® Ultra-15 centrifugal filters (10 kDa MWCO).

Protein concentration was measured by the Bradford assay19, purity of protein samples assessed by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), and laccase identity verified by zymography. For PAGE, 5% stacking and 12% resolving gels were prepared following Laemmli’s protocol20, with PageRuler™ prestained ladder as marker. SDS-PAGE samples were run without boiling or reducing agents to preserve activity. Zymograms were developed by washing gels in 100 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 3.0) and incubating them with ABTS until green bands appeared.

Laccase activity was measured by monitoring the oxidation of 2.5 mM ABTS in 100 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 3.0) after incubation for 3 min at 25 °C (ε420 = 3.6 × 104 M-1 cm-1) One unit (U) of laccase activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to oxidize 1 μmol of ABTS per minute under these conditions (Eq. 1).

A: change in absorbance at 420 nm wavelength, ε: substrate extinction coefficient, d: the distance light passes through, V: total reaction volume, v: enzyme volume in the reaction. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Biochemical characterization of recombinant laccase rTpLcc3

rTpLcc3 activity and stability were assessed across pH 3.0–10.0 and 30–80 °C using 2.5 mM ABTS. Substrate specificity was tested with 5 mM ABTS (ε420 = 36,000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹), 5 mM DMP (ε470 = 35,645 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹), and 5 mM guaiacol (ε436 = 6,400 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹). Effects of metal salts, inhibitors, reducing agents, and detergents were examined by measuring residual activity. Kinetic parameters (Km, Vmax, kcat, and kcat/Km) were determined with ABTS, syringol, and guaiacol using Michaelis–Menten analysis in GraphPad Prism 8.

Decolorization of textile dyes with recombinant rTpLcc3

Decolorization of textile dyes by rTpLcc3 was analyzed following Malcı et al. (2023)21. Reactions (1 mL) contained 50 µM dye, 100 µM mediator (ABTS, p-coumaric acid, or syringaldehyde), and 0.1 U mL− 1 rTpLcc3 in 100 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.0). Controls included blanks (no dye) and negatives (no enzyme). Based on preliminary enzyme titration experiments presented in the Supplementary Materials (Fig. S4), 0.1 U mL⁻¹ was selected as the optimum enzyme concentration for the subsequent decolorization assays. For all tested dyes, increasing the enzyme concentration from 0.01 to 0.1 U mL⁻¹ enhanced the decolorization efficiency, with the optimum values being obtained at 0.1 U mL⁻¹. Further increases in enzyme concentration did not improve dye removal. Assays were performed at 30 °C, 150 rpm for 24 h, and dye removal was monitored spectrophotometrically at 1–6 h, 12 h, and 24 h using a Synergy II Microplate Reader (BioTek). Rates were calculated using Eq. 2, where A0 represents the initial peak absorbance of the dye chromophore, and A1 represents the final absorbance after the enzymatic reaction.

The composition of the simplified simulated dyebath effluent (DB) conditions for the textile dyes was obtained from a local dyehouse located in Türkiye. The simplified simulated DB was prepared with 7 g L− 1 NaCl, 0.5 g L− 1 Na2CO3, and 0.3 g L− 1 NaOH which were common reactive dye assisting chemicals. Initially, the relative activity and stability of the rTpLcc3 laccase were evaluated using reactive DB, both unadjusted and adjusted to pH 4.0 using 1 M citric acid, in comparison to 100 mM citrate buffer (pH 4.0). Subsequently, the decolorization efficiency of the dyes in DB (pH 4.0) was evaluated in a similar manner to that in citrate buffer.

Toxicity assessment

Cytotoxicity of laccase-treated and untreated dyes was evaluated on HEK293T cells using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (GLPBIO) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, and detached with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA. HEK293T cells (5 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates, exposed to samples for 24 h, and then incubated with 10 µL reagent for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and cell viability (%) was calculated relative to untreated controls (set as 100%).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8. One-way and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were applied to evaluate differences in decolorization efficiencies among dyes, mediator systems, and buffer conditions. One-way ANOVA was used for single-factor comparisons, while two-way ANOVA was employed to assess the main and interaction effects between variables, including mediator type, dye class, and reaction environment (citrate buffer vs. simulated dyebath). Where appropriate, Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used for post hoc analysis, and the Geisser–Greenhouse correction was applied when assumptions of sphericity were not met. A significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were performed in triplicate biological replicates (n = 3).

Results

Isolation, characterization and bioinformatics analysis of genomic and cDNA sequences encoding the laccase TpLcc3

The full-length tplcc3 cDNA was obtained by 5′/3′ RACE, and both the genomic tplcc3 sequence and its corresponding cDNA were successfully amplified and sequenced. The tplcc3 gene spans 2,145 bp and contains a 1,563 bp coding sequence (CDS) with a GC content of 59%, encoding a 520-amino acid protein with a 21-residue signal peptide. The CDS is interrupted by 10 introns ranging from 52 to 77 bp, and the splicing junctions conform to the canonical 5′-GT…AG-3′ rule.

Multiple sequence alignment performed using ClustalW2 and visualized with Jalview revealed that TpLcc3 is closely related to Trametes laccases with ≥ 70% identity. Phylogenetic analysis conducted using MEGA 12 with the Maximum Likelihood method supported this clustering (Fig. 1).

(B) Multiple sequence alignment of TpLcc3 with laccases from T. polyzona MUCL38443 and other well-characterized laccases visualized using Jalview. Conserved regions (≥ 70% identity) are highlighted in blue, conserved histidines in red, the LEA motif in turquoise, and residues Cys474 and Phe484 in orange.

Within T. polyzona MUCL 38443, it shares 76.92% with TpLcc1 (AOZ19964.1) and 71.56% with TpLcc2 (WPH61293.1). Across other T. polyzona strains, sequence conservation is also high, including 99.23% with LMB-TM5 Lac2 (UYM28066.1), 79.48% with LMB-TM5 Lac1 (UYM28065.1), and 77.12% with KU-RNW027 Laccase 1 (BBJ00755.1). pLcc3 also showed high similarity to structurally characterized fungal laccases, including 87.17% with T. versicolor Laccase-2 (PDB:1GYC), 76.71% with T. cinnabarina laccase (PDB:2XYB), 76.15% with T. trogii laccase (PDB:2HRG), 76.15% with Coriolopsis gallica (PDB:4ZAF), 75.35% with Aspergillus oryzae (PDB:6H5Y), and 75.55% with Cerrena caperata (PDB:4JHU).

Theoretical calculations predicted a molecular weight of 54.3 kDa and an isoelectric point (pI) of 6.24 for TpLcc3 (excluding the signal peptide). The deduced amino acid sequence contains five potential N-glycosylation sites (Asn-X-Ser/Thr). The three-dimensional structure of TpLcc3 was predicted using ColabFold (AlphaFold2 implementation on the MMseqs2 platform) and visualized with ChimeraX. The model revealed a typical three-domain laccase architecture with Greek-key β-motifs (Fig. 2A–B). Four conserved copper-binding motifs (L1–L4) characteristic of multicopper oxidases were identified (Fig. 2C): L1 (HWHG; Ile84–Phe89), L2 (HSH; Tyr129–Leu133), L3 (HPXHLH; Pro415–Gly422), and L4 (HCHIDFH; Leu474–Leu480). The conserved Phe484 residue was identified as the axial ligand at the Type I copper site, and an adjacent LEA motif (positions 480–482) was also present (Fig. 1B).

Heterologous expression and purification of recombinant TpLcc3 (rTpLcc3)

The full-length tplcc3 cDNA was cloned into pPICZ B and heterologously expressed in P. pastoris X-33 under its native secretion signal. Thirteen transformants producing green halos on indicator plates were screened for extracellular laccase activity, and the highest producer (76.5 U·L⁻¹) was selected for further optimization. The optimal expression conditions were determined as 1.5% methanol, 0.8 mM CuSO4, and 0.4% L-alanine at 28 °C with shaking at 250 rpm for 7 days, as detailed in the Supplementary Materials (Fig. S11-S14). Under these optimized conditions, rTpLcc3 activity reached 1,159 U L⁻¹, representing an overall 15.15-fold improvement relative to the unoptimized culture condition.

Extracellular rTpLcc3 was concentrated by ammonium sulfate precipitation and purified using a three-step chromatography workflow. Pigments in the P. pastoris supernatant were first removed using Streamline™ Q XL resin (Cytiva), followed by cation-exchange and hydrophobic interaction chromatography. The final preparation showed a specific activity of 274.24 U mg-1 (Table 1). SDS-PAGE and zymogram analysis revealed a single diffuse band (Fig. 3A).

Biochemical characterization of rTpLcc3

Optimum pH and the pH stability of rTpLcc3

The activity of rTpLcc3 was evaluated toward ABTS, syringol, and guaiacol across pH 3.0–8.0 at 25 °C. Maximum activity was observed at pH 3.0 for ABTS and syringol, and at pH 4.0 for guaiacol. Enzymatic activity decreased progressively with increasing pH and was completely abolished at pH 8.0 (Fig. 3B). Among the tested substrates, ABTS exhibited the highest catalytic efficiency (576.76 µM-1 s-1). rTpLcc3 also oxidized syringol (3.47 µM-1 s-1) and guaiacol (3.00 µM-1 s-1) at the respective optimum pH values for each substrate.

The pH stability of rTpLcc3 was examined across pH 3.0–10.0 after 24 h of incubation at 4 °C, 25 °C, and 30 °C (Fig. 3C). At 4 °C, rTpLcc3 remained stable across all tested pH values except pH 10.0, where relative activity declined to 48.67 ± 6.33% after 24 h at 25 °C and 30 °C, the enzyme retained > 80% of its initial activity after 24 h between pH 4.0 and 8.0.

The optimum temperature and thermal stability of rTpLcc3

The optimum temperature and thermal stability of rTpLcc3 were evaluated in the 30–80 °C range (Fig. 3D–E). Maximum activity was observed at 70 °C, and the enzyme retained approximately 80% of maximal activity at 30 °C. Thermal unfolding analysis yielded a melting temperature (Tm) of 51.95 °C. The half-life (t1/2) values were 187 min at 50 °C, 79 min at 60 °C, 19 min at 70 °C, and 17 min at 80 °C.

The effect of various metal salts on rTpLcc3

The effect of metal salts on rTpLcc3 activity was evaluated at 5 mM (Fig. 3F). Alkali metal chlorides had minimal effects, with activities of 102.57 ± 7.82% (NaCl) and 102.78 ± 7.08% (KCl). In contrast, nitrate salts increased activity to 114.94 ± 14.84% (NaNO3) and 113.05 ± 6.96% (KNO3). Among alkaline earth metal salts, moderate inhibition was observed: 79.98 ± 2.89% activity with CaCl2, 86.51 ± 4.46% with MgCl2, and 86.17 ± 4.63% with BaCl2. For transition metals, rTpLcc3 retained substantial activity in the presence of MnCl2 (89.50 ± 0.42%), ZnCl2 (86.84 ± 3.32%), and CoCl2 (83.09 ± 2.74%), while FeCl3 caused stronger inhibition (74.75 ± 5.36%). Most notably, FeSO4 completely abolished rTpLcc3 activity.

The effect of reducing agents, potential inhibitors and detergents on rTpLcc3

rTpLcc3 activity was markedly influenced by reducing agents, inhibitors, and detergents (Fig. 3G). Thiol-containing reducing agents exerted the strongest inhibition. L-cysteine reduced activity to 81.97 ± 4.96% (1 mM) and 23.32 ± 5.67% (5 mM). 2-Mercaptoethanol decreased activity to 48.42 ± 0.76% and 32.24 ± 2.48% at 1 and 5 mM, respectively. DTT reduced activity to 87.23 ± 4.24% (1 mM) and 14.15 ± 1.15% (5 mM). Sodium azide was the strongest inhibitor, reducing activity to 16.91 ± 1.62% (1 mM) and 6.57 ± 0.77% (5 mM).

The anionic detergent SDS showed a concentration-dependent effect: activity increased slightly to 111.06 ± 3.03% at 1 mM but was completely abolished at 5 mM. Nonionic detergents stimulated rTpLcc3 activity in a concentration-dependent manner. Polysorbate-20 increased activity to 116.72 ± 5.33% (1 mM) and 113.77 ± 5.63% (5 mM). Polysorbate-80 produced the strongest enhancement among nonionic detergents, with 135.26 ± 2.92% (1 mM) and 123.57 ± 9.23% (5 mM). Triton X-100 also enhanced activity to 122.12 ± 3.00% (1 mM) and 115.45 ± 7.78% (5 mM). The cationic surfactant CTAB strongly stimulated enzyme activity, increasing it to 136.82 ± 2.72% (1 mM) and 143.40 ± 4.05% (5 mM).

Chelating agents EDTA and EGTA had minimal impact on rTpLcc3 activity. rTpLcc3 retained 111.26 ± 5.13% and 99.16 ± 13.13% activity in the presence of EDTA at 1 and 5 mM, respectively. With EGTA, activity was 115.96 ± 1.19% (1 mM) and 113.04 ± 1.70% (5 mM).

Decolorization efficiencies of combinatorial rTpLcc3/mediator systems in controlled buffer environment

The decolorization kinetics and overall efficiencies of rTpLcc3 in citrate buffer (CB) were monitored over a 24 h incubation period (Fig. 4). The reactions were performed in the presence or absence of the mediators p-coumaric acid (pCA), syringaldehyde (SA), and ABTS using six representative textile dyes. The tested dyes comprised three anionic azo dyes, Acid Black 194 (AB194), Reactive Black 5 (RB5), and Reactive Red 195 (RR195), as well as the cationic azo dye Bismarck Brown (BB) and two cationic triarylmethane dyes, Malachite Green (MG) and Crystal Violet (CV).

Decolorization performance and kinetics of six textile dyes by rTpLcc3 in citrate buffer (CB). (A) Decolorization efficiencies after 24 h. (B–G) Decolorization kinetics monitored at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 12, and 24 h for (B) Acid Black 194 (AB194), (C) Reactive Black 5 (RB5), (D) Reactive Red 195 (RR195), (E) Bismarck Brown (BB), (F) Malachite Green (MG) and (G) Crystal Violet (CV) in the presence or absence of mediators. Data represent mean ± SD of three independent experiments. “ns” indicates statistically insignificant differences between samples (p > 0.05).

Among the azo dyes, AB194 was most effectively decolorized by rTpLcc3 alone, reaching 71.39 ± 6.16% within 24 h (Fig. 4A), whereas ABTS supplementation enabled complete decolorization (100.00 ± 0.01%) within 1 h (Fig. 4B). By contrast, RB5 was recalcitrant to treatment with rTpLcc3 alone, showing only 12.89 ± 1.78% decolorization after 24 h. This limitation was substantially overcome by mediator supplementation. ABTS increased RB5 decolorization to 73.19 ± 0.49% within 3 h and 81.01 ± 3.23% at 24 h, while SA produced a comparable effect, achieving 72.33 ± 1.36% within 1 h and 79.80 ± 1.77% after 24 h. For both mediators, decolorization approached maximal levels within the first 3 h, with little additional increase thereafter (Fig. 4C). Accordingly, no significant difference was detected between ABTS- and SA-mediated treatments at 24 h (p > 0.05) (Fig. 4A).

RR195 was highly recalcitrant to oxidation by rTpLcc3 alone, with decolorization reaching only 1.84 ± 0.24% after 24 h. In contrast, ABTS significantly improved decolorization to 61.35 ± 11.96% at 24 h (p < 0.05). Although the ABTS-mediated reaction was limited during the first 2 h, decolorization increased substantially between 12 and 24 h, with the 12 h values already approaching the final range (Fig. 4D). By comparison, SA showed little promoting effect, resulting in only 11.2 ± 1.87% decolorization after 24 h, which was not significantly different from the mediator-free control (p > 0.05) (Fig. 4A).

For the triarylmethane dyes MG and CV, rTpLcc3 alone exhibited limited decolorization (18.48 ± 2.47% to 31.17 ± 2.28%). This limitation was markedly overcome by mediator supplementation. For MG, all three mediators substantially improved decolorization, reaching 93.59 ± 0.23% with ABTS, 92.10 ± 2.06% with SA, and 84.27 ± 4.56% with pCA, with no significant differences among treatments (p > 0.05) (Fig. 4A). ABTS-mediated MG decolorization was rapid, reaching near-maximal levels within 2 h and remaining stable thereafter (Fig. 4F). For CV, SA and pCA were more effective than ABTS, yielding 80.67 ± 6.55%, 75.80 ± 6.40%, and 54.69 ± 1.46% decolorization, respectively; no significant difference was observed between SA and pCA (p > 0.05) (Fig. 4A). In citrate buffer, SA- and ABTS-mediated CV decolorization approached final levels by approximately 12 h (Fig. 4G).

Collectively, ABTS was the most effective mediator for azo dye decolorization, yielding the highest mean removal (71.5 ± 22.8%), followed by SA (63.9 ± 28.6%), whereas both rTpLcc3 alone (37.4 ± 31.8%) and pCA (35.4 ± 33.6%) were markedly less effective. In contrast, SA performed best for triarylmethane dyes, with a mean decolorization of 86.4 ± 4.5%, followed by pCA (80.0 ± 5.7%) and ABTS (74.1 ± 15.2%), while rTpLcc3 alone again showed substantially lower activity (24.8 ± 7.5%). Kinetic analysis indicated that most mediator-assisted reactions reached near-plateau levels within 6–12 h, with only minor changes observed thereafter up to 24 h.

Decolorization efficiencies of combinatorial rTpLcc3/mediator systems in simplified simulated DB

To evaluate the practical applicability of rTpLcc3 laccase for industrial wastewater treatment, its decolorization kinetics and overall performance was monitored in a simplified simulated dyebath effluent (DB) adjusted to pH 4.0 over a 24 h incubation period (Fig. 5). Under these conditions, AB194 retained moderate decolorization by rTpLcc3 alone in DB (42.17 ± 5.69%) compared with CB (71.39 ± 6.16%). ABTS supplementation restored complete decolorization (100.00 ± 0.01%) in DB, whereas pCA and SA resulted in only 41.75 ± 2.91% and 55.03 ± 3.58% decolorization, respectively (Fig. 5A). Kinetic analysis further showed that, in the presence of ABTS, complete decolorization was achieved by 12 h in DB, whereas the same level was reached within 1 h in CB, indicating slower reaction kinetics under dyebath conditions (Fig. 5B).

RB5 likewise showed low decolorization in the absence of mediators, with similar values in DB (14.64 ± 7.06%) and CB (12.89 ± 1.78%). Mediator addition remained essential, with SA and ABTS increasing RB5 decolorization to 80.68 ± 20.07% and 91.61 ± 7.89%, respectively (Fig. 5A). Kinetic analysis revealed a clear plateau after approximately 12 h, particularly for SA- and ABTS-mediated reactions (Fig. 5C), highlighting the strong dependence of RB5 removal on mediator-assisted oxidation under dyebath conditions.

RR195 remained highly recalcitrant in DB, showing limited decolorization with rTpLcc3 alone (6.88 ± 7.56%) or in the presence of pCA (5.04 ± 2.59%). SA produced only a minor increase (10.06 ± 0.67%), whereas ABTS enhanced decolorization to 34.25 ± 2.04%. However, this efficiency was still markedly lower than the ABTS-mediated decolorization observed in CB (61.35 ± 11.96%) (Fig. 5A). Kinetic analysis showed a similar pattern to that observed in CB (Figs. 4D and 5D). For BB, although decolorization by rTpLcc3 alone declined markedly in DB (19.94 ± 2.57%) relative to CB (63.51 ± 16.58%), mediator addition improved removal efficiency, with pCA providing moderate enhancement (37.54 ± 8.79%) and SA and ABTS supporting high decolorization levels of 75.75 ± 3.06% and 83.42 ± 3.95%, respectively (Fig. 5A). Both SA- and ABTS-mediated reactions approached near-final decolorization levels by 3 h (Fig. 5E).

For BB, decolorization by rTpLcc3 alone decreased substantially in DB to 19.94 ± 2.57%, compared with 63.51 ± 16.58% in CB. Mediator supplementation improved BB removal, with pCA providing only moderate enhancement (37.54 ± 8.79%), whereas SA and ABTS retained high activity in DB, achieving 75.75 ± 3.06% and 83.42 ± 3.95% decolorization, respectively (Fig. 5A). Kinetic analysis showed that SA- and ABTS-mediated reactions approached near-final decolorization levels by 3 h (Fig. 5E).

For the triarylmethane dyes, MG and CV were strongly affected by the dyebath matrix, with decolorization by rTpLcc3 alone decreasing from 18.48 ± 2.47% and 31.17 ± 2.28% in CB to 0.65 ± 0.67% and 3.80 ± 0.27% in DB, respectively. This limitation was largely overcome by mediator supplementation. For MG, SA and ABTS restored decolorization to 95.87 ± 6.92% and 97.85 ± 2.93%, respectively, whereas pCA remained ineffective (< 5%). For CV, SA and ABTS increased decolorization to 54.99 ± 0.77% and 84.41 ± 0.79%, respectively (Fig. 5A). Kinetic analysis showed that SA- and ABTS-mediated MG decolorization reached near-maximal levels within 6 h, while ABTS-mediated CV decolorization increased more gradually, with a marked rise after 12 h and plateau formation between 12 and 24 h (Fig. 5F-G).

Cytotoxicity of laccase-/LMS-products

Cytotoxicity of dye solutions before and after enzymatic decolorization was evaluated using HEK293T cells to determine whether rTpLcc3-mediated transformation altered mammalian cell viability. As shown in Fig. 6, for AB194, RB5, RR195, and BB, no significant cytotoxicity was detected before treatment, and this non-cytotoxic profile was maintained after enzymatic decolorization across all treatment groups, including rTpLcc3 alone and mediator-assisted systems (p > 0.05), indicating that neither treatment substantially altered the cytotoxicity of these dyes. In contrast, MG and CV were clearly cytotoxic before treatment, but this toxicity was clearly reduced especially after mediator-assisted enzymatic decolorization. For MG, mediator-assisted treatment resulted in a significant reduction in cytotoxicity; notably, ABTS, SA, and pCA increased HEK293T viability by 1.36, 1.61, and 2.01-fold, respectively, whereas rTpLcc3 alone did not significantly affect viability. A similar trend was observed for CV, where ABTS and pCA-assisted treatments increased HEK293T viability by 1.78 and 1.85-fold, respectively, whereas rTpLcc3 alone and SA did not produce significant changes. Together, these findings demonstrate that mediator-assisted laccase treatment not only removed color, but also substantially reduced the cytotoxic effects of the initially cytotoxic dyes MG and CV, thereby improving mammalian cell viability under the tested conditions.

Discussion

TpLcc3 was identified as a new fungal laccase isoenzyme with sequence and structural features consistent with a typical high-redox-potential multicopper oxidase. The gene architecture (10 introns with canonical GT–AG splice junctions), high sequence identity to Trametes laccases, and conserved copper-binding motifs confirm that TpLcc3 belongs to the fungal laccase family and shares the expected catalytic framework of three-domain laccases22–28. The presence of Phe484 as the axial ligand at the Type I copper site is notable because this residue is commonly associated with high-redox-potential fungal laccases, in contrast to bacterial enzymes that often contain methionine at this position27. Together with the conserved LEA motif, these features support the view that TpLcc3 preserves the canonical redox architecture while exhibiting isoenzyme-level sequence variation.

Heterologous expression in P. pastoris produced an active secreted enzyme, and optimization of methanol, CuSO4, and L-alanine substantially improved production yield. The pronounced effects of methanol and copper supplementation, with 1.5% methanol yielding a 23.5-fold increase and 0.8 mM CuSO₄ enhancing activity 1.84-fold, are consistent with previous reports describing host- and enzyme-dependent expression behavior in methylotrophic systems29–31.

The diffuse SDS-PAGE band observed for purified rTpLcc3 is also consistent with heterogeneous glycosylation, which is commonly seen in secreted fungal enzymes produced in P. pastoris32,33. This finding is particularly noteworthy because variation in glycosylation may contribute to the functional divergence among T. polyzona laccase isoforms. Notably, rTpLcc1 was reported to be non-glycosylated despite expression in the same host (P. pastoris)30,34, whereas rTpLcc2, produced in S. cerevisiae, displayed only limited glycosylation30,34. Together, these differences support the possibility that host- and isoform-dependent glycosylation contributes to the distinct biochemical properties of rTpLcc3.

Biochemically, rTpLcc3 showed a highly favorable property set for biotechnological use: acidic activity optimum (as expected for fungal laccases), broad short-term pH stability, high catalytic activity at elevated temperature, and notable thermal persistence. The optimum pH values for ABTS and syringol (pH 3.0) and guaiacol (pH 4.0) are consistent with the acidic profiles typically reported for fungal laccases30,34–36. However, rTpLcc3 displayed a comparatively broad pH stability range, retaining substantial activity between pH 4.0 and 8.0 at 25–30 °C. This feature distinguishes rTpLcc3 from many fungal laccases that rapidly lose activity under near-neutral conditions37, and may enhance its applicability in processes where precise pH control is not feasible.

The thermal profile of rTpLcc3 is also notable. Although the Tm (51.95 °C) is within the range often seen for fungal laccases, the enzyme retained high activity at 70 °C and showed longer half-life values than several previously reported Trametes laccases, including rTpLcc1 and rTpLcc2 from the same strain background30,34. The combination of a high temperature optimum and useful operational half-life suggests that rTpLcc3 can remain functionally active under process conditions that are challenging for many fungal enzymes38,39. This may reduce enzyme loading requirements in dye treatment systems operated at elevated temperature.

The inhibitor and additive profile further support the robustness of rTpLcc3. As expected for multicopper oxidases, thiol compounds and sodium azide strongly inhibited activity due to interference with the copper centers and redox-active catalytic machinery40–43. In contrast, EDTA and EGTA caused minimal inhibition, implying that the catalytic copper ions are tightly coordinated and not easily removed under the assay conditions44,45. The strong tolerance to several salts, especially alkali metal chlorides and nitrates, and only moderate sensitivity to many divalent ions, is advantageous for effluent treatment applications where ionic strength is high. The severe inhibition by ferrous and ferric salts is consistent with prior reports across fungal laccases40,41,46, mechanistically, Fe2+ acts as a competitive reductant that quenches substrate-derived radicals, whereas Fe3+ directly interferes with the T1 copper site and electron transfer46,47, indicating that iron-rich matrices may require pretreatment or process adjustment.

One particularly interesting feature is the pronounced surfactant tolerance and, in some cases, surfactant-mediated activation. Nonionic detergents (Polysorbate-20, Polysorbate-80, Triton X-100) and CTAB enhanced rTpLcc3 activity, whereas SDS was stimulatory only at low concentration and strongly inhibitory at higher concentration. Similar enzyme-specific surfactant effects have been reported in other fungal laccases47–52, but the magnitude of activation observed here, especially with CTAB and Polysorbate-80, suggests that rTpLcc3 may operate efficiently in surfactant-containing textile matrices or formulated systems. This is a practical advantage because many real dye baths and finishing streams contain residual surfactants.

The catalytic constant set for ABTS is on the of major highlights of this study. rTpLcc3 exhibited a very low Km and high kcat, giving a kcat/Km of 576.76 µM⁻¹ s⁻¹, indicating efficient substrate turnover under the tested conditions. Although previously reported fungal laccases, including rTpLcc1 and rTpLcc230,34,53,54, have shown varying catalytic efficiencies, direct cross-study comparisons should always be interpreted cautiously due to assay-condition differences. the observed value suggests that rTpLcc3 is an efficient catalyst for ABTS oxidation. The observed differences among rTpLcc1, rTpLcc2, and rTpLcc3 may also be linked to isoenzyme-specific sequence features and differential glycosylation states. In this context, the glycosylation of rTpLcc3 in P. pastoris, in contrast to the non-glycosylated or mildly glycosylated behavior reported for rTpLcc1 and rTpLcc230,34, respectively, may contribute to improved stability and catalytic performance by reinforcing folding and preserving active-site geometry55,56. Although this remains a mechanistic hypothesis, it is consistent with the broader pH stability and longer thermal half-life observed for rTpLcc3.

The catalytic constants determined for ABTS represent one of the notable findings of this study. Under the tested assay conditions, rTpLcc3 exhibited a low Km and high kcat, resulting in a kcat/Km value of 576.76 µM⁻¹ s⁻¹, which indicates efficient ABTS oxidation. Previously reported native and recombinant fungal laccases, including rTpLcc1 and rTpLcc230,34,54,55, have shown variable catalytic efficiencies toward ABTS; however, direct cross-study comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because kinetic parameters are highly dependent on assay conditions, substrate concentrations, pH, temperature, enzyme preparation, and experimental protocols. Thus, the present data support the notable catalytic performance of rTpLcc3 under the conditions used in this study, without implying universal superiority over other fungal laccases. The observed differences among rTpLcc1, rTpLcc2, and rTpLcc3 may be linked to isoenzyme-specific sequence features and differential glycosylation states. In this context, the glycosylation of rTpLcc3 in P. pastoris, in contrast to the non-glycosylated or mildly glycosylated behavior reported for rTpLcc1 and rTpLcc230,34, respectively, may contribute to improved stability and catalytic behavior by supporting protein folding and helping preserve active-site geometry56,57. Although this remains a mechanistic hypothesis, it is consistent with the broader pH stability and longer thermal half-life observed for rTpLcc3.

The decolorization experiments revealed that rTpLcc3 performance depends strongly on dye structure and mediator chemistry. In citrate buffer, AB194 was readily decolorized even without mediator, whereas RB5 and RR195 were much more resistant to direct oxidation. This pattern is consistent with the greater structural complexity and steric and electronic effects associated with reactive azo dyes, especially diazo structures and bulky substituents that can limit enzyme access and reduce redox susceptibility 21,57–59 .The strong improvements observed with ABTS and SA for RB5 and, to a lesser degree, RR195 highlight the central role of mediator-generated radicals in extending the oxidation range of laccases beyond directly accessible substrates. This trend was also reflected in the overall performance data, where ABTS was the most effective mediator for azo dye decolorization, yielding the highest mean removal (71.5 ± 22.8%), followed by SA (63.9 ± 28.6%), whereas both rTpLcc3 alone (37.4 ± 31.8%) and pCA (35.4 ± 33.6%) were markedly less effective. For the triarylmethane dyes MG and CV direct oxidation by rTpLcc3 alone was limited (24.8 ± 7.5%), whereas mediator supplementation substantially enhanced decolorization. In this group, SA performed best, with a mean decolorization of 86.4 ± 4.5%, followed by pCA (80.0 ± 5.7%) and ABTS (74.1 ± 15.2%). Collectively, these findings indicate that decolorization efficiency is governed not simply by dye class, but by the interplay between substrate structure, mediator properties, and reaction kinetics.

The DB experiments provided one of the most application-relevant findings of this study. Overall, ABTS was the most robust mediator under dyebath conditions, followed by SA, with mean decolorization values of 80.1 ± 27.8% and 62.1 ± 27.1%, respectively, across all dyes in DB. Both clearly outperformed pCA (17.4 ± 12.8%) and rTpLcc3 alone (14.7 ± 13.1%). For azo dyes, ABTS and SA achieved mean decolorization values of 74.6 ± 20.7% and 55.4 ± 24.5%, respectively, whereas for triarylmethane dyes the corresponding values were 91.1 ± 9.6% and 75.4 ± 28.5%. Cross-matrix comparison further showed that ABTS retained high activity in both CB and DB (72.4 ± 21.8% vs. 80.1 ± 27.8%), whereas SA exhibited only a moderate, non-significant decline (from 71.4 ± 28.3% to 62.1 ± 28.6%, p = 0.335). By contrast, both pCA and rTpLcc3 alone were significantly impaired in DB, with pCA decreasing from 50.3 ± 29.4% to 17.4 ± 15.3% (p = 0.0011) and rTpLcc3 alone from 33.2 ± 26.6% to 14.7 ± 14.1% (p = 0.0176). At the dye-class level, the reduction from CB to DB was not significant for azo dyes (52.1 ± 34.4% vs. 43.5 ± 31.8%, p = 0.212), but was significant for triarylmethane dyes (66.3 ± 29.1% vs. 43.7 ± 41.0%, p < 0.05). Together, these results show that matrix effects strongly suppress direct laccase oxidation and pCA-mediated systems, whereas ABTS remains highly effective and SA retains useful performance across most dyes. Together, these results indicates that mediator selection should be considered not only substrate-specific but also matrix-dependent. The high ionic strength and the presence of carbonate and alkaline components in the dyebath matrix likely affect enzyme conformation, electron-transfer efficiency, and mediator reactivity, thereby amplifying performance differences among mediator systems. In this context, the consistent performance of ABTS in both citrate buffer and dyebath effluent suggests that it is the most robust mediator for rTpLcc3 under variable conditions, while SA represents a promising alternative for several dyes under dyebath conditions. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that laccase performance in real or simulated textile effluents is strongly influenced by effluent composition, mediator choice, and process design (Table 2)21,34,40,58–65. Although effective decolorization has been reported in complex matrices, performance is often constrained under highly alkaline conditions or in the absence of suitable mediators. Our results reinforce this view, demonstrating that mediator-assisted systems are required to achieve stable, high-level decolorization under realistic effluent conditions, and that ABTS- and SA-like mediators are more effective than pCA for the rTpLcc3 system evaluated here. However, it should be noted that the simulated dyebath effluent employed here represents a simplified model system and does not fully capture the complexity of industrial textile wastewater, which may contain additional components such as surfactants, mixed dye formulations, and heavy metals that can further influence enzyme activity and mediator performance.

A key strength of this study is the integration of cytotoxicity assessment with enzymatic decolorization analysis, allowing evaluation of the biological response of treated dye solutions in addition to color removal efficiency. Importantly, none of the post-decolorization samples exhibited increased cytotoxicity toward HEK293T cells relative to their corresponding untreated dye solutions, indicating that rTpLcc3-mediated dye transformation did not increase cytotoxicity under the tested conditions. In several cases, particularly for the triarylmethane dyes, mediator-assisted treatment was associated with improved mammalian cell compatibility, suggesting a reduction in dye-associated cytotoxicity following enzymatic treatment. These findings are particularly relevant given the frequent concern that enzymatic dye degradation may generate reactive or toxic intermediates. Indeed, previous studies have shown that laccase-mediated oxidation can either reduce toxicity or yield products with equal or greater biological activity, depending on substrate structure and reaction pathway66–68. Consistently, the present results demonstrate that rTpLcc3-based treatment did not increase cytotoxicity for any of the tested dyes under the experimental conditions used, supporting the environmental safety of this enzymatic system and reinforcing its potential applicability for textile wastewater remediation. simplified However, these findings should be interpreted within the limitations of the experimental design. The toxicity assessment was limited to short-term cell viability analysis using a single mammalian cell line, HEK293T, and therefore should not be considered evidence of complete detoxification or definitive absence of harmful end-products. Broader toxicological evaluations, including multiple cell types, longer exposure periods, and additional toxicity endpoints such as oxidative stress, genotoxicity, apoptosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction, are required to more comprehensively assess the safety profile of the treated products. Moreover, the chemical structures of the dye degradation products were not characterized in this study. Since enzymatic transformation may generate intermediate compounds with unknown toxicological properties, future studies should incorporate analytical approaches such as liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry or gas chromatography–mass spectrometry to identify degradation products and evaluate their potential biological and environmental effects.

Conclusion

This study presents the identification and functional characterization of a novel, typically high-redox-potential recombinant laccase, rTpLcc3, from T. polyzona MUCL 38443 and demonstrates its potential as a biocatalyst for textile dye degradation under both controlled and simulated effluent conditions. Overall, the data show that rTpLcc3 combines high catalytic efficiency with favorable pH and temperature robustness, surfactant tolerance, and strong mediator-compatible decolorization performance. Importantly, enzymatic treatment did not increase the cytotoxicity of any tested dye under tested condition, while mediator-assisted decolorization improved mammalian cell viability for triarylmethane dyes, supporting the environmental safety of the rTpLcc3/mediator system. Collectively, these findings highlight the promise of rTpLcc3 for sustainable enzymatic detoxification of textile dye effluents and identify the rTpLcc3/mediator platform, particularly rTpLcc3/ABTS, as a promising and scalable strategy for textile wastewater treatment.
