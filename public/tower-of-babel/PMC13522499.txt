The effect of voluntary environmental regulation on corporate financialization in China
Authors: Zhiguang Zhou, Shiyan Zong, Yifeng Qiu, Fengjuan Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Government regulation is an important factor influencing corporate investment and financing decisions. However, little research has examined the role of voluntary regulation. This paper focuses on how voluntary environmental regulation shapes this process by investigating the impact of Green Factory Certification on corporate financialization. Using a multi-period difference-in-differences model and data on China’s A-share listed manufacturing firms from 2012 to 2023, we find that Green Factory Certification significantly curbs corporate financialization. This inhibitory effect is more pronounced in companies with stronger financing constraints, better corporate governance quality, and cities with stricter financial and environmental regulations. Furthermore, the mechanism analysis reveals that voluntary environmental regulation alleviates the “reservoir” motive via “green signal transmission” (improving financing conditions and securing government subsidies) and weakens the “investment substitution” motive through “voluntary commitment and reputation maintenance” (proactively boosting fixed investments and green innovation). This study enriches research on voluntary environmental regulation’s economic effects and provides empirical support for optimizing green industrial policies.

Introduction

Since the outbreak of the global financial crisis in 2008, the world economy has undergone profound structural changes. The financial industry, with its high returns, has attracted a large influx of capital, further intensifying the trend of economic virtualization. Sweezy1 first identified this phenomenon, which was later defined by Krippner2 as “financialization.” On the micro level, financialization is manifested in non-financial firms reducing their investments in the real economy and increasing their allocation to financial assets, known as “corporate financialization”3,4. While financial markets provide companies with diversified investment channels and high liquidity, they have also led firms to allocate capital to financial assets in pursuit of short-term returns, further exacerbating this trend5. However, capital is limited, and its shift towards the financial sector crowds out investment in the real economy, causing a “crowding-out effect” that undermines the long-term development foundation of firms6,7. The negative impacts of corporate financialization are widely acknowledged: it not only increases financial risks but may also lead to economic stagnation or even recession8,9. For China, revitalizing the real economy is a core strategy. However, structural contradictions brought about by market-oriented reforms-such as the fading of institutional dividends and rising enterprise costs-have compressed profit margins in the real economy10, intensifying the imbalance between the expansion of the virtual economy and the slowdown of the real economy, with corporate financialization becoming an increasingly prominent issue11. According to the Wind database, by 2022, the total amount of trading financial assets held by A-share listed companies exceeded \$219 billion, accounting for more than 30% of total assets, a rise of over 30% compared to 2010. This trend highlights the necessity of reexamining corporate financialization behaviors.

Fundamentally, corporate financialization is a resource allocation decision driven by the risk-return trade-off between real-sector investments and financial speculations. Consequently, environmental regulation systems, which significantly alter the costs and returns of industrial production, are inherently linked to corporate financialization. However, the existing literature primarily explores this relationship through the lens of mandatory environmental regulations (such as pollution taxes or emission caps). While mandatory policies compel environmental compliance, they often impose severe compliance costs that squeeze real-sector profit margins. This “cost-compliance” pressure can inadvertently drive firms to allocate capital toward high-return financial assets as a hedging strategy to offset declining operational profitability, thereby exacerbating the financialization trend6,7,12.

Given this dilemma, a critical research gap emerges regarding the role of voluntary environmental regulations. Although previous studies have examined voluntary tools like ISO 14,00113, they have largely focused on corporate innovation and production efficiency, neglecting their profound impact on corporate asset allocation structures. Unlike the “command-and-control” nature of mandatory policies, voluntary environmental regulations-which combine corporate autonomy with policy guidance-operate through market incentives, credible signaling, and reputation building14. This fundamental difference suggests that voluntary regulations possess the unique potential to alleviate financing constraints and stimulate endogenous green innovation, thereby altering the relative attractiveness of real versus financial investments. Therefore, systematically investigating how voluntary environmental regulation impacts corporate financialization is not merely an empirical exercise to fill a cross-disciplinary gap; it is theoretically essential to determine whether incentive-based environmental policies can effectively reverse the “deindustrialization” trend and guide capital “from virtual to real” without suppressing corporate operational vitality.

The selection of China’s Green Factory Program as the research context is strategically grounded in its role as the world’s largest manufacturing hub. China currently faces the dual challenge of structural ‘deindustrialization’ and an urgent need for green transformation, making it an ideal ‘natural laboratory’ to study capital reallocation under environmental constraints15. Unlike market-driven voluntary programs in Western economies, China’s initiative represents a ‘hybrid’ model-combining authoritative government endorsement with market incentives. This institutional design provides a replicable policy template for other emerging markets and transitional economies grappling with similar dilemmas of economic virtualization and environmental degradation. Green Factory Certification, as a government-led voluntary environmental regulation in China, is unique in that it combines government endorsement with a dynamic regulatory mechanism. This ensures policy flexibility while maintaining substantive constraints. Unlike purely voluntary certifications such as ISO 14,001, Green Factory Certification requires firms to make substantial green investments, such as upgrading environmental protection facilities and developing green technologies, which directly affect corporate capital allocation decisions and align with the core characteristics of financialization. Additionally, the Green Factory Certification was launched in phases starting in 2017, creating a natural multi-period policy shock that provides an ideal condition for using the DID method to separate policy effects. By 2022, the certification covered approximately 70% of China’s manufacturing enterprises16, which are the main group affected by the crowding-out effect of financialization10,17, making the sample highly representative.

The Green Factory Certification alleviates financing constraints through a “green signaling” mechanism, curbing the “reserve motive” of financialization. It also stimulates an endogenous drive for reputation maintenance among firms, prompting them to increase green investment and weakening the “speculative motive” of financialization. Simultaneously, the certification complements macro- and regional policy financial and industrial policies. Historical evidence shows that environmental and climate-related regulations systematically influence firms’ balance sheet structures and capital flows6,7,18. As a verifiable “green label,” Green Factory Certification effectively solves the information asymmetry between macro policies and micro firms, helping financial institutions precisely identify high-quality green enterprises and ensuring that policy resources are effectively implemented. This creates a closed-loop collaborative mechanism involving policy signals, market recognition, and capital allocation.

Based on the above background, this study uses a sample of A-share listed manufacturing enterprises in China from 2012 to 2023 and applies a multi-period DID model to systematically examine the impact of Green Factory Certification on corporate financialization, its mechanisms, and heterogeneity characteristics. The empirical results show that Green Factory Certification significantly suppresses corporate financialization. Further heterogeneity analysis reveals that this suppressive effect is more pronounced in firms with stronger financing constraints, better corporate governance quality, and in cities with stricter financial and environmental regulations.

This study makes three main contributions: First, in terms of theoretical contribution, it reveals the dual mechanism of voluntary environmental regulations on corporate financialization, filling the theoretical gap regarding the impact of voluntary environmental regulations on corporate asset allocation structures. Second, in terms of research perspective, it systematically analyzes the uniqueness of Green Factory Certification and its policy synergy mechanisms, enriching the research dimensions on the economic consequences of voluntary environmental regulations. Third, regarding practical contributions, this study embeds its findings deeply within China’s unique institutional background-specifically the national strategic mandate of preventing the economy from shifting “from virtual to real”. By demonstrating how China’s government-led “hybrid” voluntary regulation effectively redirects corporate capital back into green, real-sector investments, this research provides actionable insights for Chinese policymakers to optimize green industrial policies. Furthermore, it offers a replicable practical blueprint for other emerging economies facing the dual challenges of premature deindustrialization and environmental degradation.

Institution background and hypothesis development

Institution background

The institutional origins and policy evolution of the green factory program

The Green Factory Program, as the core policy of China’s green manufacturing system, originates from the national green development strategy. In 2016, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, along with the National Development and Reform Commission and other relevant departments, issued the “Green Manufacturing Engineering Implementation Guidelines (2016–2020),” which first identified the Green Factory as the key path for the green transformation of the manufacturing industry. It outlined fundamental principles such as land use efficiency, non-harmful raw materials, clean production, waste recycling, and low-carbon energy. The policy established a standard evaluation system and a four-tiered nurturing mechanism, laying the foundation for the institutional framework. Through government assurance, credibility, and voluntary enterprise participation, the Green Factory Program has implemented a collaborative mechanism of application, evaluation, and incentives, motivating enterprise engagement. The integration of market-based tools such as green credit and green bonds has enhanced the policy’s effectiveness, promoting a positive interaction between policy and the market. The policy has gone through different stages: from 2016 to 2020, it focused on standard system construction, with an emphasis on establishing standards and fostering demonstrations, culminating in the release of the “Green Factory Evaluation General Principles” (GB/T 36,132–2018); starting in 2021, it entered the dynamic management phase, shifting focus to performance improvement and graded supervision. The implementation of the “Enterprise Green Code” grading system is expected in 2024, with annual data updates enabling visual tracking.

Following the release of the “Guidelines for the Implementation of the Green Manufacturing Project (2016–2020)” in 2016, which initiated the concept of green factories, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology officially launched the national-level green factory selection and recognition work in 2017, adopting a phased promotion model to steadily advance the construction of the green manufacturing system. Adhering to the principle of “cultivating a batch, declaring a batch, and recognizing a batch,” this initiative has formed a standardized and long-term batch-based selection mechanism, with 9 batches of national-level green factories announced successively by 2024 to continuously expand the coverage of green manufacturing benchmarks. The number of green factories in each batch has shown an overall upward trend, evolving from 201 enterprises in the first batch to a peak of 1488 in the 8th batch, reflecting the deepening of policy implementation, the increasing enthusiasm of enterprises for green transformation, and the continuous improvement of the gradient cultivation system. The policy structure and operational mechanism of the green factory program

The core framework of the Green Factory Program is based on a standardized evaluation system. According to the “Green Factory Evaluation General Principles” (GB/T 36,132–2018), it sets up six primary indicators and 25 secondary indicators, employing a 100-point scoring system with the requirement that “all core indicators must be met and the total score must be ≥ 60” to qualify for certification. At the local level, a graded nurturing mechanism is implemented, categorizing enterprises into A + , A, and B levels, with each assigned an “Enterprise Green Code” label. This differentiated grading system guides enterprises in optimizing their behavior. The program’s operational mechanism follows a full process certification procedure: voluntary enterprise application-third-party evaluation-provincial-level review-national certification. It is supported by financial subsidies (with a maximum reward of 1 million yuan for national-level factories), green credit (interest rate reduction of 15–20%), and technical consulting, forming a closed-loop system of entry, incentives, and supervision. This mechanism significantly reduces the cost of enterprises’ green transformation and strengthens the demonstration effect through collaboration with local energy-saving and emission-reduction plans and green supply chain policies. The Green Factory Program complements energy-saving standards for high-energy-consumption industries and the circular economy strategy, promoting the institutionalization and normalization of enterprises’ green behavior.

Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses

This study constructs its theoretical analysis based on three core theories: signal transmission theory19, principal-agent theory20, and the dual-motive theory of corporate financialization (“reservoir motive” and “investment substitution motive”). Signal transmission theory holds that credible signals can reduce information asymmetry between enterprises and external stakeholders; principal-agent theory points out that managerial short-termism and opportunistic behavior lead to inefficient resource allocation; the dual-motive theory identifies two core drivers of corporate financialization, namely the “reservoir motive” (holding financial assets for liquidity reserves21,) and the “investment substitution motive” (pursuing short-term financial returns instead of core business investment22,). Combined with the policy complementarity characteristic of environmental regulations6,7, these theories jointly form the logical basis for deriving research hypotheses.

From a theoretical perspective, the tilt of corporate funds towards financial assets is influenced not only by risk-return comparisons and capital cost constraints but also by managerial short-termism and internal governance limitations. The direct result of this is the “crowding-out effect” on productive investments and the imbalance in capital allocation3,9,10,23. It is important to clarify that the impact of Green Factory Certification on corporate financialization stems from enterprises’ successful acquisition of the certification rather than the mere issuance of the policy itself, which distinguishes it from other green policies. Unlike mandatory environmental policies that exert universal constraints upon issuance, Green Factory Certification requires enterprises to voluntarily participate in evaluation, meet strict quantitative standards (e.g., energy consumption, emissions), and undergo third-party verification and dynamic supervision-only enterprises that successfully obtain the certification can gain the official-endorsed “green label” and trigger subsequent economic effects. The Green Factory Certification, as a verifiable and sustainable management system and performance evaluation mechanism, reshapes the boundaries of corporate capital allocation in three synergistic dimensions: First, the certification requires the integration of processes and performance goals into daily operations, enhancing the observability of resource usage by the board and external governance, thereby weakening managerial short-term behavior and the opportunistic allocation of free cash flow-this aligns with the logic of principal-agent theory and reduces the incentive for funds to be diverted towards financial assets24–27. Second, the certification, as a credible signal based on signal transmission theory, changes the cost and contractual conditions of external financing: For capital providers sensitive to carbon risk, they will reassess the risk premium and funding restrictions of certified companies based on the certification results, alleviating the funding bottleneck for real investments and increasing the opportunity cost of holding financial assets, thus driving funds back into the real economy28. Third, the certification complements macro and regional policy finance and industrial policies-historical evidence shows that environmental and climate-related regulations systematically affect enterprises’ balance sheet structures and capital flows6,7,18, and this certification acts as a bridge between micro enterprises and macro policies, solving information asymmetry and enhancing policy effectiveness. Based on this, the Green Factory Certification, through enhanced governance, capital cost repricing, and policy complementarity, reduces the relative attractiveness of financial assets and increases the marginal return and accessibility of real investments, thereby significantly alleviating corporate financialization. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis H1.

H1:

Obtaining Green Factory Certification can significantly alleviate corporate financialization.

From the perspective of corporate behavior logic, there is a profound interaction between the decision to participate in Green Factory Certification and the motivations behind corporate financialization, which is the core issue this paper aims to clarify. Hypothesis H1 reflects the total inhibitory effect of Green Factory Certification on corporate financialization, while Hypothesis H2 further clarifies the underlying mechanism paths, and the three core arguments (green signal transmission, environmental compliance pressure, policy complementarity) reflect the combined effects of dual mechanisms and synergistic enhancement. In academia, research on the purposes of corporate financialization can be divided into two main theoretical streams: the “reservoir” theory and the “investment substitution” theory. Specifically, some literature suggests that financial assets have “reservoir” characteristics because, compared to fixed assets, financial assets are more liquid29, and the motivation for holding financial assets is to serve as a liquidity reserve to cope with future liquidity risks21. On the other hand, some literature argues that financial assets have “investment substitution” characteristics, where the purpose of corporate financialization is to achieve investment returns higher than those from core business activities22. Furthermore, in the current ownership structure in China, controlling shareholders often lack effective supervision by minority shareholders, and controlling shareholders may use the funds of minority shareholders to engage in high-return, high-risk financial investments to maximize personal interests23. Therefore, analyzing how voluntary environmental regulation reduces corporate financialization requires examining both how Green Factory Certification enhances a company’s ability to respond to liquidity risks and how companies are compelled to invest in non-financial sectors, thereby reducing corporate financialization.

On the one hand, the “green signal transmission” mechanism (rooted in signal transmission theory) from enterprises’ successful acquisition of Green Factory Certification directly impacts the “reservoir” logic of corporate financialization. By obtaining the certification, companies send a credible signal of good environmental performance to the market, which can enhance their market reputation, attract long-term real investment capital, and alleviate financing constraints30,31. They may also benefit from direct policy incentives such as government subsidies, tax reductions, and green credit preferences, all of which help mitigate liquidity risks, thereby weakening the motivation for companies to allocate funds to financial assets in pursuit of a “reservoir”32-this is the first core mechanism corresponding to the “reservoir motive” in the dual-motive theory. On the other hand, the “voluntary commitment and reputation maintenance” mechanism brought about by certification reshapes the “investment substitution” logic: to sustain the market premium of the “green label,” certified companies are endogenously motivated (rather than passively forced) to proactively invest in upgrading environmental facilities and green technology research and development32. This mandatory investment in real assets not only crowds out the allocation space for financial assets but also enhances corporate long-term profitability through increased operational efficiency, thus reducing the need for “investment substitution”-this is the second core mechanism corresponding to the “investment substitution motive” in the dual-motive theory. Additionally, the “policy complementarity” characteristic of the certification6,7,18 serves as a synergistic enhancement path: as a micro-level voluntary environmental regulation, the certification bridges macro policies and micro enterprises, improving the efficiency of green credit and industrial subsidies, which in turn strengthens the effectiveness of the “green signal transmission” and “voluntary commitment and reputation maintenance” mechanisms. These three core arguments are not isolated: the first two are direct mechanism paths targeting the dual motives of financialization, and the third is a synergistic path that amplifies the first two, jointly forming the logical chain for the certification to alleviate corporate financialization. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis H2.

H2:

Green Factory Certification alleviates corporate financialization by weakening both the “reservoir” and “investment substitution” motives.

Data and models

Sample selection and data sources

This study selects A-share listed manufacturing enterprises in China as the research sample, with the observation period spanning 2012–2023. The time is determined to cover five years before and after the official launch of Green Factory Certification in 2017, ensuring sufficient pre-policy and post-policy observations for multi-period DID identification. The sample is restricted to manufacturing firms because the certification specifically targets this sector16, minimizing industry-specific confounding factors. To ensure data quality, we implement the following screening procedures: exclude ST/*ST and delisted enterprises with abnormal operations,remove observations with missing key variables (e.g., financialization indicators, control variables); eliminate firms with non-positive net profits; and winsorize continuous variables at the 1st and 99th percentiles to mitigate extreme value interference. The final sample comprises 17,110 firm-year observations.

Core data is sourced from authoritative databases and official documents: Green Factory Certification status is confirmed by manually collating the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology’s demonstration lists (2017–2023) and cross-referencing with “Tianyancha”. Enterprise financial and governance data (e.g., financial assets, board size, ownership concentration) comes from the CSMAR and Wind databases. Green innovation data is extracted from the CSMAR Patent Database, while ESG scores adopt the Huazheng ESG Rating. City-level variables (e.g., per capita GDP, industrial structure, digital infrastructure) are sourced from the China City Statistical Yearbook and local government work reports. Government science and technology support intensity is compiled from local fiscal expenditure reports, ensuring comprehensive and reliable data for empirical analysis.

Variable definitions

Independent variable

This study employs the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology’s published list of “Green Factory” certifications as an exogenous policy shock, selecting A-share listed manufacturing firms as the research sample, with the aim of examining the impact of this policy on firm behavior. The research period spans from 2012 to 2023, thereby encompassing a ten-year window around the first certification in 2017 (five years before and five years after). This timeframe not only allows for the evaluation of the long-term effects of the policy but also ensures comparability across different sample sub-periods.

Given that the “Green Factory” certification primarily targets the manufacturing sector, the sample is restricted to listed manufacturing firms. This choice is based on three main considerations. First, as a policy tailored specifically to manufacturing, focusing on listed manufacturing firms minimizes systematic differences between the treatment and control groups in terms of industry characteristics, production patterns, and regulatory environments. Second, manufacturing is the cornerstone of China’s industrial economy, accounting for approximately 70% of national industrial energy consumption16. Thus, listed manufacturing firms serve as a representative proxy for China’s industrial enterprises as a whole. Finally, listed firms are subject to stricter disclosure and compliance requirements, which leads to higher-quality financial and environmental data and thereby enhances the representativeness and reliability of the study’s findings.

The certification status of each firm was determined by manually compiling the seven batches of the Demonstration List of Green Manufacturing System Construction published by the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology between 2017 and 2023, and cross-referencing them with firm names and stock codes using information from the “Tianyancha” database. The raw data underwent systematic cleaning and processing steps, including the exclusion of firms under special treatment (ST) status to mitigate the influence of outliers; removal of observations with missing values for key variables to ensure data completeness; elimination of firms with non-positive net profits; and winsorizing all continuous variables at the 1st and 99th percentiles to reduce the influence of extreme values. Following these procedures, the final dataset comprises 17,110 observations.

Dependent variable

In this study, the level of corporate financialization is evaluated primarily using two approaches: the income statement method and the balance sheet method. The income statement method measures the proportion of profits derived from financial assets as the core indicator. This approach provides an intuitive reflection of firms’ financial investment performance. However, it is highly sensitive to short-term fluctuations in financial markets and therefore fails to fully capture the underlying logic of resource allocation toward financial assets, making it difficult to reveal the essential characteristics of financialization.

By contrast, the balance sheet method, which assesses financialization from the perspective of asset structure, offers a more comprehensive depiction of the overall extent of corporate financialization. This method is particularly well suited for analyzing structural changes in the financialization process. Given that the present study aims to investigate the mechanisms through which voluntary environmental regulation (Green Factory Certification) affects firms’ asset allocation strategies, with a particular emphasis on resource reallocation at the asset level, the balance sheet method is more consistent with the objectives of this research.

Accordingly, this study measures the degree of corporate financialization using the balance sheet method, defined as the ratio of financial assets to total assets10. Financial assets are operationalized as six categories: trading financial assets, derivative financial assets, net loans and advances, net available-for-sale financial assets, net held-to-maturity investments, and net investment properties.

Furthermore, to determine whether firms exhibit excessive financialization, the actual level of financialization is compared against an estimated optimal level. Specifically, this study adopts the investment efficiency residual model proposed by Richardson25 to estimate the degree of excess financialization. Control variables

To mitigate omitted variable bias, this study incorporates eight control variables in the empirical analysis. Specifically: the Independent Director Ratio (Indep) is measured by the proportion of independent directors on the board, reflecting the board’s independence and supervisory role; Firm Size (Size) is proxied by the natural logarithm of total assets, indicating a firm’s resource allocation capacity and ability to withstand market shocks; Operating Cash Flow (Cflow) is calculated as the ratio of net operating cash flow to total assets, capturing short-term liquidity and investment capacity in daily operations or strategic projects; Leverage (Lev), defined as the ratio of total liabilities to total assets, reveals financing constraints (with highly leveraged firms tending to cut non-essential expenditures); Ownership Concentration (Top1) is assessed via the largest shareholder’s shareholding ratio, reflecting ownership concentration and its impact on corporate decision-making; Profitability (Roa), measured as net profit-to-total assets, suggests stronger strategic advantage incentives for more profitable firms; Board Size (Board) is the total number of board members, reflecting the decision-making body’s scale and efficiency impact; and Cash Holdings (Cash) is the ratio of monetary funds and equivalents to total assets, indicating short-term payment capacity and financial flexibility. Variable definitions.

Factors such as regional economic development level may simultaneously affect corporate financialization and the number of green factory-certified enterprises; therefore, this study adds city-level control variables. Specifically, we use the logarithm of per capita regional gross domestic product to measure urban economic development level (pgdp), the ratio of the added value of the tertiary industry to regional gross domestic product to measure industrial structure upgrading (structure), the number of college students per 10,000 people to measure human capital level (humancapital), the ratio of internet broadband access users to the year-end total population to measure digital infrastructure construction level (infra), the ratio of local fiscal expenditure on science and technology to local general public budget expenditure to measure government science and technology support intensity (govtec), and the ratio of actually utilized foreign capital in the current year to regional gross domestic product to measure the degree of opening-up (openness). These city-level controls are commonly applied in regional economic research to capture multidimensional development influences on firm outcomes34. These variables cover multiple dimensions, including urban economy, industry, factors, and policies, providing a solid foundation for controlling potential interfering factors and accurately identifying policy effects in subsequent empirical analyses.

Goodman-Bacon decomposition

This study employs a DID framework to identify the staggered implementation effects of VER. Within this framework, the treatment group consists of both early and late adopters of the certification, which may introduce heterogeneity bias into the estimation results. Recent literature has proposed strategies to address such concerns38,39. As highlighted by Goodman-Bacon40, multi-period DID estimates can be decomposed into three core comparisons: (1) “Treated vs. Never Treated” (certified firms vs. firms never certified), (2) “Later Treated vs. Earlier Treated” (firms certified in later batches vs. firms certified in earlier batches), and (3) “Earlier Treated vs. Later Treated” (firms certified in earlier batches vs. firms certified in later batches). The “Weight” column in the decomposition results reflects the relative contribution of each comparison to the overall estimate. Figure 3 shows that the second comparison (“Later Treated vs. Earlier Treated”) accounts for only 4.2% of the total weight, while the first comparison (“Treated vs. Never Treated”) contributes 82.4%-a distribution consistent with theoretical expectations. The Goodman-Bacon decomposition thus indicates that bias arising from staggered treatment timing is minimal, confirming the robustness of the multi-period DID estimates. Taken together, these results further affirm the reliability of the study’s multi-period DID analytical framework.Fig. 3Results of Goodman-Bacon decomposition.

Addressing endogeneity concerns

A potential concern in our baseline analysis is endogeneity arising from self-selection bias and reverse causality. It could be argued that companies with lower levels of financialization and a stronger focus on the real economy are inherently more capable of meeting environmental standards and, therefore, are more likely to self-select into the Green Factory Certification program. Although the parallel trend test and PSM-DID have partially mitigated these concerns, we further employ the Instrumental Variable (IV) approach and the Heckman two-step method to rigorously address this endogeneity issue.

Instrumental variable (IV) approach.

Instrumental variable (IV) approach.

Instrumental variable (IV) approach.

To address potential reverse causality, we adopt a two-stage least squares (2SLS) approach. We construct the instrumental variable (IV_VEP) as the average Green Factory Certification rate of other enterprises in the same industry within the same province. Although the certification is implemented as a city-level policy shock, constructing the IV at the province-industry level is based on rigorous methodological considerations. First, according to the official procedure in China, enterprises must undergo a rigorous review and recommendation process by provincial-level industrial departments. Thus, the provincial average certification rate directly reflects regional policy intensity.

This construction naturally satisfies the two fundamental requirements of an instrumental variable. Regarding the relevance condition, peer effects within the same region and industry significantly drive corporate strategic behaviors, meaning a higher peer certification rate strongly incentivizes the focal firm to pursue certification to maintain competitiveness41. Regarding the exclusion restriction, compared to city-level peers, province-industry aggregated data represents a macro-level administrative pressure that does not directly dictate the focal firm’s internal financial asset allocation decisions. Furthermore, to explicitly address potential concerns that the instrumental variable might partially capture the influence of regional financial development or marketization (which could simultaneously affect a firm’s precautionary savings and real investment opportunities), our 2SLS estimations strictly control for time-varying city-level macroeconomic indicators (such as urban economic development and openness). Additionally, the inclusion of firm fixed effects effectively absorbs unobservable, time-invariant regional financial characteristics. This rigorous design ensures that the instrumental variable solely captures exogenous policy peer pressure rather than regional financial development levels.

Mechanism analysis

In the baseline regression analysis presented earlier, this study demonstrates that Green Factory Certification (VER) significantly inhibits corporate financialization (FIN). Building on the theoretical framework outlined in H2, this effect is primarily driven by two core mechanisms with distinct attributes: the first is the “Green Signal Transmission” mechanism (a market-incentive tool), which targets the “precautionary savings (cash reservoir)” effect, and the second is the “Voluntary Commitment and Reputation Maintenance” mechanism (a self-enforcing reputational tool), which addresses the “investment substitution” effect. These two mechanisms directly align with the dual motivations of corporate financialization highlighted in H2. Their effectiveness is validated through four proxy variables-Government Subsidies (GS), ESG Score (ESG), Fixed Asset Ratio (FIX), and Green Innovation (GI)-each of which is explicitly linked to the theoretical arguments of H2.

Green signal transmission mechanism: capturing the “precautionary savings” effect

The “Green Signal Transmission” mechanism is inherently a market-incentive tool, as it relies on credible policy signals to trigger positive market responses and policy preferences, thereby alleviating enterprises’ “precautionary savings motive” for holding financial assets-this directly aligns with the theoretical argument of H2 that “green signal transmission weakens the reservoir motive”. The core logic is that Green Factory Certification, as an official-endorsed and third-party verified “green label”, reduces information asymmetry between enterprises and external stakeholders, enabling enterprises to obtain substantive support that mitigates liquidity risks, thus reducing the need to hold financial assets as “cash reservoirs”.

GS and ESG Score are selected as proxy variables precisely because they directly reflect the realization of this market-incentive effect and are closely tied to H2’s theoretical arguments. GS is defined as “the ratio of government subsidies obtained by an enterprise to its total assets”, which directly reflects the official financial support brought by the green signal. Green Factory Certification serves as credible evidence of enterprises’ compliance with environmental standards, making them eligible for special government subsidies30. These subsidies directly supplement enterprises’ operational liquidity, alleviate potential cash flow shortages, and reduce the urgency of holding financial assets as precautionary reserves-this directly validates H2’s claim that “green signal transmission alleviates financing constraints to weaken the reservoir motive”. ESG Score adopts the Huazheng ESG Rating, which comprehensively reflects enterprises’ environmental performance and sustainable development capabilities. Certification sends a positive signal of long-term operational stability to the market, helping enterprises attract long-term capital that favors sustainable investments31. This not only reduces enterprises’ reliance on short-term financial returns but also improves information transparency, restraining managers’ speculative tendencies to hoard financial assets for temporary liquidity needs-further reinforcing the weakening of the “precautionary savings motive” as proposed in H2. Voluntary commitment and reputation maintenance mechanism: capturing the “investment substitution” effect

Unlike mandatory environmental policies that heavily rely on external penalties to enforce compliance45, the Green Factory Certification operates as a self-enforcing mechanism driven by voluntary commitment and reputation maintenance. Once enterprises successfully obtain this official “green label,” they establish a credible reputational contract with stakeholders. To sustain the market premiums associated with this certification and maintain their competitive advantage, enterprises are endogenously motivated to continuously optimize their resource allocation. This proactive pursuit of long-term green development directly weakens the “investment substitution” motive of financialization. We select Fixed Asset Ratio (FIX) and Green Innovation (GI) as proxy variables to capture this endogenous resource reallocation process.

FIX reflects the proportion of fixed assets to total assets, indicating an enterprise’s substantive investments in upgrading production equipment and environmental protection facilities. Rather than being passively forced by command-and-control regulations, certified enterprises view these capital expenditures as proactive strategic moves to solidify their green reputation and fulfill their voluntary environmental commitments. This endogenous drive translates into allocating funds to energy-efficient production equipment, sewage treatment facilities, and other fixed assets32. Given the scarcity of corporate resources, such real investments directly crowd out the space for financial asset allocation. Consequently, enterprises proactively abandon the speculative pursuit of financial assets, directly weakening the “investment substitution” motive.

Furthermore, GI, measured by the number of green patent applications, represents an enterprise’s proactive efforts in long-term technological improvement. To maintain their certification status and continuously signal environmental leadership to the market, enterprises must proactively increase investments in green R&D, such as energy-saving technologies and emission reduction processes46. Recent studies confirm that such policy-supported “green signals” fundamentally stimulate enterprises’ intrinsic motivation for continuous green innovation47. This proactive and long-term strategic focus on core business operations not only occupies resources that might otherwise be diverted to financial investments but also enhances the long-term profitability of the enterprise through technological upgrading. As the marginal return and strategic value of real green investments increase, the relative attractiveness of short-term financial assets declines. While initial green investments may merely crowd out financial assets in the short term due to budget constraints, the long-term operational efficiency improvements and sustained policy premiums (e.g., lower financing costs) fundamentally bridge the yield gap between real and financial investments. Consequently, on a risk-adjusted basis, management’s risk appetite fundamentally shifts away from speculative “investment substitution,” ensuring that the reversal of financialization is persistent rather than temporary.

The regression results in Columns (3)-(4) of Table 8 show that VEP has a significant positive impact on both FIX and GI. This indicates that Green Factory Certification effectively guides enterprises to proactively shift resources from the financial sector to green real operations through the endogenous “Voluntary Commitment and Reputation Maintenance” mechanism. This accurately captures the “investment substitution” effect and demonstrates how voluntary environmental regulation inherently curtails corporate financialization.

Heterogeneity analysis

Financial constraints

Enterprises facing tighter financial constraints prioritize the safety and liquidity of capital over the pursuit of high returns, thereby weakening their investment substitution motive. When confronted with higher climate risks, these companies may adopt a conservative stance-reallocating resources to precautionary reserves to cope with potential capital shortages. In contrast, companies with relatively loose financial constraints have greater flexibility in portfolio optimization48. Therefore, as enterprises participate in VER, they are more capable of reallocating capital to long-term investment projects that can meet more stringent environmental reviews. For these companies, the impact of VEP on FIN may be weakened or even insignificant.

The above discussion indicates that there is potential heterogeneity in the relationship between VEP and corporate financialization among enterprises with different financial constraints. To provide empirical evidence, we adopt the SA index proposed by Hadlock and Pierce (2010) to measure corporate financing constraints, as it effectively avoids endogeneity issues associated with subjective indicators and is widely used in academic research on financing constraints. The specific calculation formula of the SA index is:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{SA}} = - 0.737{\mathrm{*Size}} + 0.043{\mathrm{*Size}}^{2} - 0.04{\mathrm{*Age}}$$\end{document}where Size denotes the natural logarithm of the enterprise’s total assets (to reflect firm scale), and Age represents the number of years since the enterprise’s establishment (to reflect operational maturity). Second, median grouping is a conventional practice in heterogeneous analysis of corporate financing constraints18,49, which enhances the comparability and credibility of our research findings with existing literature.

Quality of corporate governance

Corporate governance is a key mechanism for regulating managerial behavior, aligning the interests of shareholders and managers, and reducing agency costs51,52. Enterprises with high governance quality, characterized by independent board oversight and transparent decision-making, can effectively restrain managers’ short-term opportunistic behaviors (e.g., speculative financial investments). In contrast, enterprises with low governance quality suffer from inefficient supervision and incentive mechanisms, leading managers to prioritize their own interests and weaken the policy’s role in guiding resource allocation. Based on this, we infer that the inhibitory effect of VEP on FIN exhibits heterogeneity across enterprises with different governance quality: enterprises with high governance quality are more likely to respond to the orientation of certification, strengthening the inhibition of financialization,enterprises with low governance quality may weaken the policy effect due to insufficient internal checks and balances.

To verify the above inference, with reference to Larcker et al.53, this study selected seven indicators from four dimensions-”governance incentives,” “board oversight,” “ownership structure supervision,” and “CEO decision-making power” (with data sourced from the CSMAR database)-to construct a corporate governance quality system. A composite score of corporate governance quality for each enterprise (denoted as Corgov) was obtained using principal component analysis, where a higher score indicates better corporate governance quality. We adopted an interaction term approach by introducing both Corgov and its interaction term with VEP into the baseline model to examine heterogeneity, which can directly capture the marginal change of the policy effect without sample loss. The regression results are shown in Column (2) of Table 9. The findings reveal that the interaction term Corgov × VEP is significantly positive at the 1% level, indicating that as corporate governance quality improves, the inhibitory effect of Green Factory Certification on corporate financialization becomes more pronounced. This confirms the theoretical inference: effective corporate governance can constrain managers’ short-term motives, enabling enterprises to fully respond to the orientation of VEP and optimize resource allocation; in contrast, due to insufficient internal checks and balances, enterprises with weak governance struggle to translate policy requirements into action, thereby weakening the effect of VEP in inhibiting financialization.

Urban regulatory intensity

At the urban level, both financial regulation and environmental regulation are key macro factors influencing the effectiveness of the green factory certification policy. These two factors are deeply linked to the core mechanisms of the paper-financial regulation intensity determines the efficiency of the “green signal transmission” mechanism, impacting the accessibility of green credit and government subsidies; environmental regulation intensity directly strengthens the constraints of the “environmental compliance pressure” mechanism, determining the effectiveness of subsequent supervision and penalties. Exploring the heterogeneity between these two factors helps to delineate the macro boundaries of policy effectiveness, thus this paper analyzes both dimensions.

Urban financial regulation intensity (Finreg) is measured as the ratio of “financial regulatory expenditure to the value added by the financial industry,” reflecting the city’s strength in regulating and guiding the financial market. The data source is from the China City Statistical Yearbook, with specific reference to the research design of Zhang and Dai54. Urban environmental regulation intensity (Envreg) is measured by the proportion of the total number of words in sentences containing environmental protection-related terms in the city’s government work report to the total number of words in the report. This indicator directly reflects the local government’s emphasis on environmental protection and policy implementation orientation, with the construction method referencing the research by Xie and Huang55.

Regarding the grouping criteria, this paper uses the annual median of the two indicators as the boundary, assigning a value of 1 to cities above the median (high regulation intensity group) and a value of 0 to cities below the median (low regulation intensity group). The rationale for this grouping method is twofold: first, the median is robust to extreme values, preventing the distortion of the grouping boundary by atypical statistical values from individual cities; second, this method is a conventional practice in urban-level heterogeneity analysis, as shown in Liu and Lv18, which enhances the comparability of the results with existing literature.

This paper employs an interaction term approach to test the heterogeneity, incorporating both urban regulatory intensity variables (Finreg/Envreg) and their interaction terms with VEP into the baseline model, while also controlling for the independent terms of the variables. The regression results are shown in columns (3) and (4) of Table 9. The results indicate that the coefficients of the interaction terms for both variables are significantly positive at the 1% level, suggesting that the higher the financial regulation and environmental regulation intensity in a city, the more pronounced the inhibiting effect of green factory certification on corporate financialization.

From a mechanism perspective, in cities with well-established financial regulations, the financial market is more efficient in recognizing and pricing environmental information from enterprises. As a result, the “green signal” from certification is more likely to be converted into actual financing advantages, alleviating corporate liquidity constraints and significantly weakening the “reserve motivation.” In contrast, in cities with stricter environmental regulations, enterprises that obtain certification face greater ongoing compliance pressure. They are required to continuously invest in upgrading environmental protection facilities and green technology development, which compresses the financial asset allocation space while weakening the “investment substitution motivation,” thereby amplifying the policy’s inhibiting effect.

Economic consequences analysis

Reduction of bankruptcy risk

Verifying the impact of VEP on corporate bankruptcy risk is of great significance. Previous analysis has confirmed that VEP can inhibit corporate FIN, and bankruptcy risk, measured by the Z-score, is a key indicator of corporate financial health and sustainable operation. Excessive FIN increases corporate financial vulnerability, such as cash flow volatility and declining long-term profitability. To address the question of whether the economic consequence is driven by VEP’s inhibition of FIN, this study adopts the mediating effect test method proposed by Baron and Kenny56, using corporate FIN as the mediating variable to clarify the transmission mechanism of “VEP influencing FIN, which in turn affects bankruptcy risk.”

Theoretically, VEP reduces corporate bankruptcy risk by first inhibiting FIN. On one hand, curbing financial speculation reduces the volatility of corporate cash flows; on the other hand, guiding capital back to the real economy enhances long-term profitability, thus lowering bankruptcy risk. Meanwhile, the coefficient of VEP remains negative, but its absolute value decreases compared to Column (1). Increase in corporate green investment

Building on previous conclusions, VEP can significantly inhibit corporate financialization. However, a core question remains: where does the capital released by reducing financial asset allocation flow? To confirm that the capital is directed to the green real sectors, this study also adopts the mediating effect test framework of Baron and Kenny56, with corporate FIN as the mediating variable, to verify the transmission mechanism of “VEP influencing financialization, which in turn drives green investment.” This design directly addresses whether the increase in green investment results from VEP’s inhibition of financialization.

Green investment (Greeninv) is defined as the ratio of annual expenditures on pollution prevention, ecological governance, and green production (e.g., desulfurization and denitrification, sewage treatment, energy conservation, and green technology R&D) to total assets at the end of the period, with data sourced from corporate annual reports, social responsibility reports, and official environmental protection disclosure documents26,27. Theoretically, VEP promotes green investment by inhibiting financialization: reducing the allocation of financial assets releases capital, which is then invested in green real operations to meet the environmental compliance requirements of certification. The regression results are reported in Columns (3)-(4) of Table 10. Column (3) shows that the coefficient of VEP is significantly positive at the 5% level, indicating that VEP has a positive impact on green investment. Column (4) includes both VEP and the mediating variable FIN, and the coefficient of FIN is significantly negative at the 1% level, meaning higher financialization significantly crowds out green investment. Meanwhile, the coefficient of VEP remains positive, and its statistical significance is maintained. These results confirm the existence of the mediating effect: Green Factory Certification releases capital by inhibiting financialization, which then significantly increases corporate green investment. This demonstrates that the policy’s goal of promoting corporate low-carbon transformation is realized through the pathway of inhibiting financialization and reallocating resources to green sectors.

Consistent with the above test, we conduct a bias-corrected Bootstrap test with 5000 resamples57, and the indirect effect of VEP on green investment through FIN is significant at the 5% level, as its 95% confidence interval does not contain zero.

Spillover effect on other enterprises in the same city

Verifying the spillover effect of VER on the financialization of other enterprises in the same city is key to measuring the regional influence of the policy. Previous analysis focused on the effect of VEP on certified enterprises themselves; if the policy can drive uncertified enterprises in the same city to adjust their financialization behaviors, it can reflect the policy’s regional diffusion value and address whether green policies operate solely at the firm level or contribute to a broader regional green development environment. The underlying rationale for such spillover effects is grounded in the literature on peer and spatial spillovers, which documents that policy-induced changes in one enterprise can significantly influence the behavior of other proximate firms through competitive interactions and normative pressures58. Thus, VEP is expected to reduce the financialization of other enterprises in the same city, and the core explanatory variable VEP is expected to have a significantly negative coefficient.

The indicator Mcity is measured as the average financialization level of other enterprises in the same city excluding the enterprise itself, and is included in Eq. (2) as the dependent variable. This research design, which uses the average behavioral indicator of peer enterprises in the same region to test policy spillover effects, is widely adopted in studies on corporate peer effects and regional policy diffusion and has sufficient literature support. Chen and Ma41 employed a similar indicator of regional peers’ average investment behavior to examine corporate investment peer effects, demonstrating the effectiveness of this method in identifying inter-enterprise behavioral diffusion,additionally, recent work on peer effects of corporate environmental protection expenditures shows that the environmental behavior of peer firms significantly influences focal firms’ strategic decisions, further confirming that using the average indicator of other enterprises in the same region can effectively isolate the indirect spillover effect from the direct policy impact59. The regression results in Columns (5)-(6) of Table 10 show that the VEP coefficient maintains statistical significance regardless of whether control variables are included. This confirms that Green Factory Certification has a significant urban spillover effect, which effectively inhibits the financialization of other enterprises in the same city, promotes resource allocation to real sectors at the regional level, and expands the practical impact boundary of the policy.

Conclusions

Conclusion and discussion

This study selects A-share listed manufacturing enterprises in China from 2012 to 2023 as the research objects, employing a multi-period DID model and rigorous empirical analysis methods to comprehensively explore the impact of Green Factory Certification (a voluntary environmental regulation tool) on enterprises’ resource allocation behavior. The core findings are as follows: First, Green Factory Certification significantly inhibits corporate financialization, a conclusion that remains robust after a series of tests, including parallel trend tests, placebo tests, the exclusion of concurrent policy interference, and Goodman-Bacon decomposition. Second, the inhibitory effect exhibits significant heterogeneity, being more pronounced in enterprises with stronger financing constraints and better corporate governance quality, as well as in cities with stricter financial and environmental regulations. Third, mechanism analysis reveals that the policy achieves this inhibitory effect through two complementary paths with distinct attributes: “green signal transmission” (a market-incentive tool) and “voluntary commitment and reputation maintenance” (a self-enforcing reputational tool). Fourth, economic consequences analysis demonstrates that the certification reduces corporate bankruptcy risk, increases green investment by releasing capital from financial assets, and produces urban spillover effects that inhibit the financialization of other enterprises in the same city, forming a transmission chain from “policy guidance” to “resource optimization” and ultimately to “sustainable development”.

These findings carry profound theoretical and practical significance when evaluated within the context of China’s unique institutional background and current economic transition. Specifically, China is actively pursuing the strategic mandate of revitalizing the real economy and preventing the economic shift “from virtual to real” amid structural contradictions that have compressed real-sector profit margins. Unlike purely voluntary programs in Western contexts that rely heavily on non-governmental market pressures, China’s Green Factory Certification represents a unique ‘hybrid’ institutional innovation that merges government endorsement with market-based incentives. Consequently, our findings offer critical implications beyond the Chinese context. For other emerging economies facing the twin threats of premature deindustrialization and environmental decay, this hybrid model serves as a practical blueprint. It demonstrates how state-guided voluntary tools can effectively bridge macro-sustainability goals with micro-asset optimization, guiding capital away from speculative financialization and toward global green real-sector revitalization The effectiveness of the two core mechanisms lies in their precise targeting of the dual motives of corporate financialization. On one hand, the “green signal transmission” mechanism bridges the information asymmetry between micro-enterprises and macro-policies in China’s credit-driven market, mitigating the “reservoir motive”. On the other hand, the strict dynamic supervision forces enterprises to translate their “voluntary commitment” into substantive fixed asset investments and green innovation, which directly crowds out the allocation space for financial assets and fundamentally weakens the “investment substitution motive”. By integrating these perspectives, this study not only enriches the literature on the economic effects of voluntary environmental regulations but also provides a micro-foundation for understanding how Chinese environmental policies shape corporate asset allocation structures.

Policy implications and future research

Practically, the findings verify that Green Factory Certification can effectively guide capital from virtual to real, offering crucial insights for optimizing green industrial policies and corporate decision-making. Based on the empirical results, this study proposes several actionable policy implications:

First, for government policymakers and market regulators, it is essential to amplify the effectiveness of “green signals” by establishing deeper linkages between the certification, government subsidies, and official ESG rating systems. Furthermore, given the heterogeneity observed, policymakers should strengthen targeted incentive mechanisms to encourage the participation of non-state-owned enterprises (non-SOEs), while local governments must continuously improve the intensity of regional financial and environmental regulations to ensure the policy’s macro-environment is conducive to real-sector investments. Local governments should also leverage the proven urban spillover effect, utilizing certified enterprises as regional benchmarks to foster green collaborative governance, thereby promoting the integrated development of industrial green transformation and real economy revitalization.

Second, for corporate managers and boards of directors, the strategic mindset must shift toward proactive capital reallocation. Recognizing that Green Factory Certification serves as a self-enforcing reputational contract, corporate decision-makers should consciously embed environmental commitments into their core financial strategies. Instead of pursuing short-term financial speculation, managers should channel the capital released from financial assets into long-term technological R&D and equipment upgrading. This endogenous drive will not only sustain their “green premium” but also fundamentally reduce corporate bankruptcy risk and enhance the long-term profitability of their core businesses.

This study also has certain limitations that provide directions for future research. First, the sample is limited to A-share listed manufacturing enterprises. Because non-listed enterprises face more severe financing constraints and non-manufacturing sectors possess different financialization motives, future research should expand the sample scope to verify the external validity of these findings. Second, the measurement of green investment currently relies on the manual retrieval of keywords, which may miss some implicit green expenditures, and corporate financialization is measured solely via the balance sheet method. Future research can utilize machine learning to optimize variable measurement accuracy and adopt multi-dimensional indicators (such as the income statement method) for cross-validation. Third, the mechanism analysis primarily focuses on “green signal transmission” and “environmental compliance pressure.” Future research should explore other intermediate mechanisms, such as supply chain spillover effects or the moderating role of executive characteristics, to enrich the theoretical chain. Finally, because Green Factory Certification features unique Chinese institutional characteristics-such as government endorsement and dynamic supervision-future research could conduct cross-country comparisons to explore how institutional differences shape the effectiveness of voluntary environmental regulations globally.
