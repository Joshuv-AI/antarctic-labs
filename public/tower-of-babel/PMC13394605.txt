Circadian Clock Gates Verticillium dahliae Defence in Cotton by Modulating JAZs Expression
Authors: Ping Wang, Mingming Liu, Chenyu Zhao, Qiaojia Lu, Shiqi Gao, Lijun Guo, Yuzhe Li, Jinping Wan, Li Yuan, Feifei Yi, Chaowei Cai, Jinggao Liu, C. Robertson McClung, Xiao Zhang, Xiao Liu, Xiaodong Xu, Yingfan Cai, Qiguang Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe circadian clock plays critical roles in orchestrating temporal regulation of diverse physiological processes. However, its role in mediating root‐associated immunity against soil‐borne pathogens in crops remains poorly understood. Here, we show that cotton circadian clock gates nighttime resistance to vascular fungal pathogen Verticillium dahliae by shaping rhizosphere microbiome composition and activating jasmonic acid (JA)‐dependent immune signalling. Pathogen infection, in turn, accelerates clock pace and perturbs nighttime microbial community structure. We identify GhLUX, an evening complex component, as a key regulator of this defence program. GhLUX enhances immunity by repressing GhJAZ expression, thereby amplifying JA signalling at night. Overexpression of GhLUX in field trials confers enhanced resistance to V. dahliae while simultaneously improving cotton fibre yield and quality. These findings uncover a mechanistic link between the circadian clock, rhizosphere microbiota and root immunity in cotton and suggest that circadian regulators can be harnessed to optimize both disease resistance and agronomic performance.

Circadian clock regulatory networks enable plants to integrate environmental temporal cues and respond rapidly to stress signals, greatly enhancing their environmental adaptation (Wang et al. 2011; Xu, Yuan, Yang, et al. 2022). Circadian gating mechanisms help to synergize interrelated or isolate conflicting physiological and biochemical activities throughout the day. In short, plant and crop circadian clocks can allocate energy and resources more efficiently during the trade‐off between growth and stress resistance (Greenham and McClung 2015; Xu, Yuan, and Xie 2022). The circadian clock regulates many important agronomic traits, which have good prospects for agricultural production applications (Steed et al. 2021). Most of the core components in the plant circadian working model established so far are transcription factors or transcriptional cofactors, and thus interlocked TTFLs (Transcriptional‐Translational Feedback Loops) constitute the core mechanism of the circadian regulation network (Nohales and Kay 2016). LUX ARRHYTHMO/PHYTOCLOCK1 (LUX) as a transcription factor recruits EARLY FLOWERING4 (ELF4) and EARLY FLOWERING3 (ELF3) to form the circadian evening complex (EC) that represses the expression of core circadian genes such as CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED1 (CCA1), NIGHT LIGHT–INDUCIBLE AND CLOCK‐REGULATED1/2 (LNK1/2), PSEUDO‐RESPONSE REGULATOR9/7 (PRR9/7), GIGANTEA (GI) and LUX itself at the end of the day and early night (Ezer et al. 2017; Helfer et al. 2011; Mizuno et al. 2014; Nusinow et al. 2011; Zhang et al. 2019). In turn, the circadian morning components, CCA1/LHY, repress LUX expression (Hazen et al. 2005), whereas RVE8, a related single Myb domain subfamily member, antagonistically activates LUX expression (Hsu et al. 2013). In addition, expression of LUX is also affected by PRR5 and TIMING OF CAB EXPRESSION 1 (TOC1, also known as PRR1; Huang et al. 2012; Nakamichi et al. 2012).

The study of circadian clock‐gated rhizosphere microbial community diversity and function is an emerging interdisciplinary field. The rhizosphere microbiome exhibits dynamic rhythmic changes over a 24‐h period, and the host plant's internal clock or chronotype is the decisive factor in regulating these dynamics (Bending et al. 2025; Xu and Dodd 2022). The endogenous plant circadian clock may affect the composition and abundance of root exudates by regulating the rhythm of plant metabolism, further driving the rhythmicity of plant‐microbe carbon metabolism and exchange (Staley et al. 2017). Genetic perturbation of the plant circadian clock in lines including lhy‐11, LHY‐ox, CCA1‐ox, toc1‐21 or ztl‐30 (inhibiting TOC1 degradation) alters the rhythmicity of specific rhizosphere microbial communities under diurnal conditions (Hubbard et al. 2018; Newman et al. 2022; Staley et al. 2017). In rice, there were huge differences in the composition and activity of the rhizosphere microbial communities between a light–dark cycling regimen and continuous darkness (Zhao et al. 2021). This likely reflects the dramatic environmental changes in the rhizosphere induced by the light–dark cycle. Photosynthetic activity in the light induced an increase in oxygen and dissolved organic carbon leading to unique microbial assemblies in light versus dark. Similarly, oxygen released into the otherwise anaerobic rhizosphere from rice roots during photosynthesis alters the redox and electron supply status in the rhizosphere. However, whether this situation is unique to rice needs further study in other plant species (Zhao et al. 2021).

Cotton (Gossypium spp.) is the most important natural fibre crop worldwide, yet its fibre yield and quality have been seriously restricted by Verticillium wilt, caused by Verticillium dahliae (V. dahliae) (Umer et al. 2023). The plant hormone jasmonates (JAs) balance immune and growth responses and improve plant survival in harsh environments (Howe et al. 2018; Li et al. 2023). Previous studies have indicated that the JA biosynthesis and signal transduction pathways in cotton play major roles in V. dahliae resistance (Shaban et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2017; Xiong et al. 2021). JAZs serve as a key regulatory node, and different family members are regulated at both transcriptional and post‐transcriptional levels and may serve as a switch that balances resistance and growth pathways. For example, GhWRKY1 and GhWRKY70 regulate the abundance of JAZs at the transcriptional level (Li et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2023), whereas GhRVE2 acts at the post‐transcriptional level (Liu et al. 2023), ultimately regulating resistance to V. dahliae through the JAZ‐MYC module. Increasing evidence shows that the circadian clock governs numerous elements of JA signalling and response including the timing of biosynthetic gene expression and sensitivity to JA (Covington et al. 2008; Shin et al. 2012; Zhang et al. 2019). However, how the circadian system interacts with the JA pathway during V. dahliae infection in cotton remains poorly understood. Although studies in several crop species have elucidated circadian control of agronomic traits, equivalent research in cotton has lagged, largely due to its complex polyploid genome. In Arabidopsis, the core evening complex component LUX not only maintains circadian rhythmicity but also regulates salicylic acid (SA) and JA signalling, contributing to resistance against foliar pathogens such as Pseudomonas syringae (Zhang et al. 2019). Despite this, it is unknown whether LUX also mediates clock‐dependent regulation of root‐associated microbial communities. Here we investigated how the endogenous circadian clock of cotton gates the composition of the rhizosphere microbial community, with a specific focus on the role of the LUX ortholog GhLUX. Using genetic manipulation of GhLUX expression, we assess its impact on microbial community structure and resistance to V. dahliae. Additionally, we explore the dynamic interactions between V. dahliae and other rhizosphere microbes, uncovering a previously unrecognized role for V. dahliae as a Zeitgeber—a temporal cue capable of resetting the host circadian system. Our data show that V. dahliae can modulate clock function via GhLUX, leading to the activation of JA‐dependent defence pathways. This study provides the first direct evidence that GhLUX enhances cotton defence against a soil‐borne vascular pathogen through circadian regulation. Importantly, our findings bridge a critical gap between model systems and crop species, offering translational insights into circadian‐mediated immunity in economically important plants. As highlighted in recent literature, the validation of model‐derived regulatory mechanisms in crop systems is essential for advancing their practical application in agriculture (Roeder et al. 2025).

Results

The Cotton Circadian Clock Maintains the Stability of the Rhizosphere Microbiome at Nighttime

The optimization of root‐associated bacterial and fungal communities, the rhizosphere microbiome, can improve soil properties, increase plant nutrient utilization efficiency, and inhibit pathogen infestation of plants through roots, promoting plant growth, health and resistance (Lee et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2024). The rhizosphere microbiome shows temporal dynamics throughout the 24‐h day, and plant‐microbial interactions are influenced both by external periodic environmental cues and the plant's endogenous circadian clock (Newman et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2021). However, how the plant circadian clock affects the rhizosphere microbial communities is incompletely understood. To investigate the crosstalk between the cotton circadian clock and the rhizosphere microbiome, we disrupted the cotton circadian clock by artificially disrupting the environment's normal photoperiod, and then compared the diel changes of the rhizosphere microbiome in disrupted clock (DC) plants with that in normal circadian clock (NC) plants (Figure 1A). We disrupted the cotton's circadian clock by reversing the light–dark (LD) cycles every 2 days. This protocol has significant advantages because the cotton's clock needs to be reset every 2 days, which will have an extremely adverse effect on growth, development and environmental adaptability. qRT‐PCR results showed that, the transcriptional peak phases of GhLUX and GhCCA1 were significantly shifted, and their amplitude fluctuations became more irregular under DC conditions compared with NC (Figure 1B). After DC treatment, significant phase shifts were observed in the other core circadian genes. The robustness of the morning gene GhPRR9 was significantly reduced, and the evening genes GhLUX, GhTOC1, GhELF3 and GhELF4 also exhibited double expression peaks in the second cycle (Figure S1). Leaf movement rhythm can directly reflect changes in the endogenous circadian clock, and we also examined the effects of DC treatment on leaf movement rhythm. The result show that the phase of leaf movement in DC‐treated plants was significantly shifted, indicating that the DC experiment had achieved the goal of disrupting the circadian clock (Figure S2). After the cotton seedlings were cultured under a light–dark cycle (12L/12D) for 27 days, they were cultured for another 6 days or transferred to jet lag conditions (altering the photoperiod, which caused DC) for 6 days. Then at time points ZT1 or ZT13 (Zeitgeber Time 1 or ZT 13, where ZT 0 corresponds to lights on), root zone soil samples were collected at the indicated time points (Figure 1A). Both the experimental group and the control group were subjected to the same light–dark cycle at the same sampling time points. Therefore, through experimental design, when analysing and comparing the data later, the possibility that light might influence the rhizosphere microbial community through the vascular bundles can be ruled out. Sequencing raw reads were processed through quality control, yielding high‐quality clean reads, followed by denoising to obtain denoised reads. These were merged and non‐chimeric reads were retained for final analysis (Table S1).

The results showed that, DC exhibited significant effects on fungal α‐diversity (p < 0.05) at ZT13. Although there was no significant difference in the Chao1 index (species richness), both the Simpson index (dominance and stability, higher values indicating greater dominance) and the Shannon index (integrating richness and evenness, higher values representing higher diversity and even distribution) increased significantly at ZT13 between DC and NC (Figure 1C). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distances revealed high similarity in β‐diversity of the cotton rhizosphere fungi diversity at ZT1 and ZT13 in NC. Specifically, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) analysis indicated no significant difference in fungal β‐diversity between ZT1 and ZT13 (R 2 = 0.073, p‐value = 0.202) (Figure 1D). We then compared the differences in the rhizosphere microbiome at ZT1 and ZT13 under conditions of DC and NC. PCoA results at ZT1 showed no significant differences between two conditions (R 2 = 0.083, p‐value = 0.088) (Figure 1E). However, at ZT13, compared with the NC, the eight biological replicates of the DC experiment were clustered in relatively independent areas, and the degree of dispersion between samples was large (R 2 = 0.132, p‐value = 0.001) (Figure 1F). We also sampled non‐planted soil as a control group to rule out the possibility that light and dark cues directly affect rhizosphere microbiome. Analysis using the Chao1 index, Simpson index, and Shannon index revealed no significant differences, particularly at ZT13, where there was no significant difference between NC and DC (Figure S3). These results indicate that even if light and dark cues can be directly transmitted to soil microorganisms (e.g., through the plant's vascular bundle), they do not cause differences in rhizosphere microbiome. Therefore, the differences in rhizosphere microbiota at ZT13 are due to the plant's endogenous circadian clock. Using Metastats, we compared microbial communities between various conditions: ZT1 of NC versus ZT13 of NC, ZT1 of NC versus ZT1 of DC and ZT13 of DC versus ZT13 of NC (Table S2). Unexpectedly, there were no significant changes in the relative abundance of the top 10 fungal genera between in‐phase ZT1 and in‐phase ZT13 samples (Figure S4). However, the decrease in Penicillium abundance (DC vs. NC) was more significant in ZT13 compared with ZT1, whereas unclassified Ascomycota increased significantly in ZT1 (DC vs. NC), which was even not ranked in the top 10 in ZT13 (Figure 1G,H). Furthermore, at ZT13, the disrupted circadian rhythm affected multiple fungal taxa, including Fusarium, Candida, Penicillium, Trichoderma and Mortierella, showing significant changes (Figure 1H). In summary, disruption of the cotton circadian clock had less significant effect on the rhizosphere microbiome at ZT1, but it caused significant changes at ZT13, suggesting that the cotton circadian clock plays a key role in maintaining the stability of the rhizosphere microbiome at nighttime.

Verticillium dahliae Inoculation Accelerates the Circadian Clock and Alters the Rhizosphere Microbiome of Cotton

Verticillium dahliae, a widespread soil‐borne vascular pathogen, is the main pathogen causing the devastating Verticillium wilt of cotton. In this study, we found that although the overall abundance of Verticillium was low (~0.1%) and showed no significant difference between ZT1 and ZT13 in NC, its abundance significantly decreased at ZT1 in DC versus NC (Figure S5). Further analysis of cotton roots inoculated with V. dahliae revealed a significant increase in Verticillium abundance at both ZT1 and ZT13, confirming successful inoculation (Figure 2A). PCoA analysis showed that microbial communities in eight inoculated biological replicates clustered separately from mock at both time points (R 2 = 0.096, p‐value = 0.008 at ZT1; R 2 = 0.103, p‐value = 0.002 at ZT13), indicating that V. dahliae inoculation significantly altered the composition of fungal communities in the cotton rhizosphere (Figure 2B,C). Further analysis showed that V. dahliae inoculation at ZT1 suppressed Penicillium abundance, whereas at ZT13, significant differences were observed in Fusarium, Penicillium and Mortierella abundances (Figure 2D,E and Table S3), indicating that the endogenous circadian clock of cotton gates the response of microbial communities to V. dahliae treatment.

To further investigate the feedback regulation of V. dahliae on the cotton circadian clock, we first detected the diurnal (light–dark cycles) and circadian (constant conditions) phenomenon of V. dahliae growth patterns. We cultured V. dahliae under light–dark cycles (12L/12D) and then transferred to continuous darkness (DD). In DD, the cultures showed a uniform growth pattern, whereas under 12L/12D cycles, concentric rings of growth appeared, suggesting that the growth of V. dahliae has a diurnal rhythm but fails to display an overt circadian rhythm under DD (Figure 2F), which is consistent with earlier reports (Cascant‐Lopez et al. 2020). We then extracted root exudates from cotton at ZT1 and ZT13 to explore their effects on V. dahliae growth. Both ZT1 and ZT13 root exudates inhibited V. dahliae growth, with ZT1 showing a stronger inhibitory effect (Figure 2G). Pathogenesis‐related (PR) genes, PR1 and PR4, were significantly induced after V. dahliae inoculation, with a stronger induction at ZT1 (Figure S6), suggesting that the cotton circadian clock gates resistance to V. dahliae at the genetic and metabolic levels. Furthermore, we used rhythmic cotyledon movement to analyse the effect of V. dahliae infection on the cotton circadian clock. V. dahliae isolate V991 inoculation at ZT1 and ZT13 resulted in a short‐period length of leaf circadian movement phenotype (Figure 2H and Table S4), indicating that V. dahliae infection indeed affects the cotton circadian clock, consistent with previously reported observations that bacterial infection causes acceleration of the circadian pace in Arabidopsis (Zhang et al. 2013).

The Circadian Clock Gates Cotton Resistance to Verticillium dahliae Infection

After V. dahliae infects susceptible cotton plants, symptoms of Verticillium wilt gradually worsen, progressively causing the yellowing of leaves, stem dryness and root rot, ultimately severely affecting the yield and quality of cotton. Although damage to cotton is a long‐term process, our experimental results show that cotton's resistance to V. dahliae is gated by the circadian clock such that resistance is stronger in the early morning than in the evening. To determine whether the cotton circadian clock gates resistance to V. dahliae infection under more realistic conditions, we transferred cotton (Cultivar, ‘BM‐1’) seedlings cultured in 12L/12D for 27 days under strict greenhouse conditions to NC and DC photoperiods for continuous growth. Three days later, V. dahliae infection was performed at ZT1 and ZT13 (Figure 3A). Compared to wild‐type plants, cotton seedlings with disrupted circadian rhythms did not show a significant reduction in vegetative growth phenotype before inoculation (Figure S7A). If the circadian clock gates cotton's resistance to V. dahliae, then there will be temporal differences in infection efficiency. After 16 days post‐inoculation (dpi), cotton grown under DC showed more disease symptoms compared with NC (Figure 3B), indicating that the circadian clock is involved in regulating resistance to V. dahliae. We harvested tissues at ZT1 and ZT13 and further compared the fungal biomass of V991, disease index and fungal recovery rate in DC and NC (Figure 3C–E). Notably, all three assays consistently showed that disrupting the circadian clock significantly reduced V. dahliae resistance, and the resistance was stronger at ZT1 than at ZT13.

Although V. dahliae V991 exhibited growth rhythmicity only under LD in culture medium (driven by the external photoperiod), related studies have not determined whether V. dahliae exhibits circadian rhythmicity (regulated by the plant's endogenous metabolic circadian rhythms) in host plants or under specific environmental conditions (Cascant‐Lopez et al. 2020). To further test whether cotton's endogenous clock enhances defence against V. dahliae, we compared the relative biomass of V. dahliae V991 in cotton roots at 3 h, 6 h and 9 h post inoculated (hpi) entrained either in‐phase or out‐of‐phase (Figure 3F). The results showed that after inoculation with V. dahliae at ZT1, there was no significant difference in the accumulation of V991 biomass in cotton roots, regardless of whether the photoperiods of cotton and V. dahliae were synchronized or not. However, at ZT13, the accumulation of V991 biomass in cotton roots significantly increased under the opposite photoperiod condition compared to the synchronized condition (Figure 3G). These data suggest that when cotton and V. dahliae share the same diurnal cycle, cotton is able to mount relatively effective defences against V. dahliae in both the morning and evening. However, when their photoperiods are not synchronized, cotton's nighttime defence capability is more strongly diminished. Since cotton's time‐specific defence responses are gated by the circadian clock, we hypothesized that cotton core circadian evening genes may play an important role. Therefore, we inoculated cotton with V. dahliae spore suspensions or water (mock) at ZT13 and investigated the transcript abundance of GhLUX, GhELF3 and GhTOC1 evening gene copies in different cotton tissues (Figure 3H and Figure S7A–E). The results showed that these genes responded differently to V. dahliae, with GhLUX showing the strongest response, particularly GhLUX07. Furthermore, the expression patterns of these genes varied across tissues, being primarily induced in roots and stems (Figure 3H and Figure S8). These findings suggest that the core circadian clock genes GhLUX, GhELF3 and GhTOC1 respond to V. dahliae infection at night and may be involved in cotton's resistance to the pathogen.

GhLUX, Component of the Evening Complex (EC), Positively Regulates Cotton Resistance to Verticillium dahliae and Increases Cotton Yield and Fibre Quality

Next, we focused on the role of the core circadian evening genes GhLUX, GhELF3 and GhTOC1 in cotton resistance to V. dahliae. Through sequence blast and alignment, we identified a total of six LUX homologous genes in cotton. Based on the chromosomes where these LUX genes are located, we named them GhLUXA03, GhLUXD03, GhLUXA05, GhLUXD05, GhLUXA07 and GhLUXD07 (Figures S9F and S10). We selected fragments that specifically target GhLUX05 (GhLUXA05 and GhLUXD05) and GhLUX07 (GhLUXA07 and GhLUXD07) (Figure S11). GhELF3 and GhTOC1 each have two copies in the cotton genome, and their expression is spatiotemporally specific, both being nighttime genes (Figure S1 and Figures S9D and S12). The CDS sequences of the two GhELF3s (Gh_A09G097800 and Gh_D09G093100) are 96.79% similar and highly conserved (Figure S9A). The CDS sequences of the two GhTOC1s (Gh_A03G084800 and Gh_D03G106900) are 97.95% similar and also highly conserved (Figure S13A). We selected fragments that specifically target GhELF3s and GhTOC1s to silence these clock genes through Virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) (Figure S14A). Since previous results have demonstrated that LUX, a core component of the circadian clock, is an important node that mediates circadian clock function and defence responses (Zhang et al. 2019), we first investigated the role of GhLUXs in resistance to V. dahliae. Preliminary experiments showed that silencing the GhLUXs gene decreased cotton (TM‐1) resistance to V. dahliae. To test the importance of GhLUX more rigorously in cotton resistance to V. dahliae, we used VIGS to knockdown GhLUX expression in the resistant cultivar Zhongzhimian 2 (ZZM2). Compared to mock treatment, VIGS knockdown cotton seedlings did not show a significant reduction in vegetative growth phenotype before inoculation (Figure S7B). After inoculating V. dahliae, the silenced TRV:GhLUX05 or TRV:GhLUX07 plants showed more severe symptoms and higher disease index than the control (TRV:00) plants (Figure 4A,D). We found that TRV:GhLUX05 and TRV:GhLUX07 could specifically and effectively inhibit the corresponding endogenous genes without affecting other GhLUX copies (Figure 4B,C and Figure S15), indicating that both of them are involved in regulating resistance to V. dahliae (Figure 4D,E). Consistent with this, observed browning of vascular tissue, and fungal recovery assay, all supported that reduced expression of GhLUXs reduced cotton resistance to V. dahliae (Figure S16A,B). However, silencing of GhELF3s and GhTOC1s had little effect on cotton resistance to V. dahliae (Figures S13B and S14B). Although the qRT‐PCR results demonstrated that we had effectively inhibited the expression of GhELF3s and GhTOC1s (Figures S13C and S14C), further disease index, observed browning of vascular tissue, and fungal recovery assay all indicated that both GhELF3s and GhTOC1s made little contribution to V. dahliae resistance (Figures S13D–F and S14D–F). This suggests that not all core evening genes of the circadian clock are involved in V. dahliae resistance, and perhaps only GhLUXs play a dominant role in defence against V. dahliae.

To further validate the role of GhLUX in Verticillium wilt resistance and its potential application in cotton production, we constructed a cotton GhLUXA07 overexpression vector driven by the CaMV35S promoter. Stable transgenic plants were obtained through genetic transformation (Figure S17). We first assessed the V. dahliae resistance of these GhLUXA07 overexpression plants. After inoculation with V. dahliae V991, the vascular browning and mycelium formation in excised cotton stems were reduced in GhLUX‐OE3 and GhLUX‐OE4 plants, consistent with their fungal renewal cultivation levels (Figure S16C,D). G. hirsutum cultivar BM‐1 exhibited severe wilting and chlorosis, whereas the GhLUX‐OE3 and GhLUX‐OE4 plants showed milder symptoms and lower disease index (Figure 4F–H and Figure S18A,B). To investigate whether GhLUX participates in the early defence response against V. dahliae, we inoculated the plants with V. dahliae V991 at ZT13. The results showed that at 3, 6 and 9 hpi, the biomass of V. dahliae in GhLUX‐OE plants was reduced, and fewer V991‐GFP spores were attached to the root surface (Figure S18C,D), indicating that GhLUX also regulates the early immune response to V. dahliae. These results indicate that overexpression of GhLUXA07 significantly enhances cotton's resistance to V. dahliae. We further investigated the conservation of LUX function in different plant species, and the results showed that overexpression of GhLUXA07 in Arabidopsis lux‐4 mutant and wild‐type could rescue and enhance the resistance phenotype to Verticillium wilt (Figure S19). Analysis of cotyledon movement showed that overexpression of GhLUX led to a gradual decrease in the robustness of the circadian rhythm (Figure S20), which is similar to previously published results in Arabidopsis.

Cotton is an important fibre crop, but there is often a trade‐off between disease resistance and growth/development traits. Typically, enhanced disease resistance negatively affects growth traits including fibre yield and quality. Therefore, we evaluated the fibre yield and quality of the GhLUX‐OE plants. The results showed that overexpression of GhLUX increased fibre length, strength and spinning consistency index (Figure 4I,J). Additionally, GhLUX‐OE plants exhibited increased unginned cotton, ginned cotton and boll weight, but no significant changes were observed in lint percentage or the number of bolls per plant. The overall improvement in these agronomic traits led to an increase in fibre yield (Tables S5 and S6). These results suggest that overexpression of GhLUX not only enhances resistance to V. dahliae but also improves fibre yield and quality without compromising cotton growth and development.

GhLUX Inhibits GhJAZs of JA Pathway to Enhance Resistance Against Verticillium Wilt

Given that the overexpression of GhLUX enhances resistance to V. dahliae and improves cotton fibre yield and quality, we aimed to uncover the regulatory mechanisms underlying this phenomenon, providing a foundation for molecular genetic breeding. We hypothesized that GhLUXs, as part of the circadian EC, may directly regulate downstream target genes to confer resistance to Verticillium wilt or indirectly modulate resistance through the circadian clock output pathways. First, we confirmed that under LD conditions, the transcript abundance of GhLUXA07 in the overexpressing lines (GhLUX‐OE3 and GhLUX‐OE4) was significantly higher than those of the endogenous GhLUXA07 in the BM‐1, and lost rhythmicity (Figure S21A). We also analysed the expression patterns of other core circadian genes in the overexpression lines and BM‐1. In contrast to GhLUXA07, the expression of GhCCA1 was reduced in GhLUX‐OE3 and GhLUX‐OE4, but its expression phase was not significantly altered, with GhCCA1 still peaking in the morning (Figure S21B). Similarly, the expression of GhPRRs (GhPRR9, GhPRR7, GhPRR5 and GhTOC1) and other EC complex genes (GhELF4, GhLUX03 and GhLUX05) was also suppressed in the GhLUX‐OE lines. Although the transcriptional robustness was reduced, the expression phase remained unaffected (Figure S21C–I). These results suggest that sustained overexpression of GhLUXA07 inhibits the transcription of core circadian genes.

To explore the potential mechanism by which GhLUX regulates cotton resistance to V. dahliae, we performed RNA‐Seq experiments using the GhLUX overexpression lines inoculated with V. dahliae V991. By comparing the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between GhLUX‐OE4 and BM‐1 plants inoculated with V. dahliae V991 (Table S7), we found that the upregulated DEGs were primarily enriched in biological processes related to cell wall modification, pectin catabolic processes and xyloglucan metabolic processes. These processes are associated with cell wall biogenesis, as well as immune responses such as chitin catabolic processes and systemic acquired resistance (Table S8). Notably, eight downregulated DEGs in the jasmonic acid (JA)‐mediated immune response pathway were all GhJAZs (Figure 5A). We then analysed the expression of all members of the GhJAZ family (30 genes in total) in upland cotton ( Gossypium hirsutum ), and as expected, most GhJAZ genes were downregulated in the GhLUX‐OE4 plants (Figure 5B). Further analysis of the promoter regions (3000 bp upstream of the ATG codon) of these GhJAZ genes revealed that 27 out of 30 GhJAZ promoters contained LUX Binding Site (LBS) elements. We further examined the diurnal expression patterns of representative GhJAZ2, GhJAZ11 and GhJAZ26 genes in BM‐1 and GhLUX‐OE plants. Each of these three genes exhibited a 24‐h diurnal rhythm in BM‐1, but their expression was significantly suppressed in the GhLUX‐OE plants (Figure 5C). We inoculated plants with V. dahliae V991 at ZT13 and then compared the transcriptional response between GhLUX overexpressing lines and BM‐1. The basal expression levels of GhJAZ2 and GhJAZ11 were already significantly reduced in the GhLUX overexpression plants. We hypothesized that GhJAZ2 and GhJAZ11 may be circadian target genes, which transcription peak is approximately ZT16 (= ZT13 + 3), so there showed a turning point at 3 hpi. Presumably GhLUX is a negative regulator of GhJAZ2 and GhJAZ11 expression, and thus the most significant differences in GhJAZ2 and GhJAZ11 transcript abundance were found between the GhLUX‐OE and BM‐1 strains at 3 hpi. In addition, we found that GhJAZ26 was slightly upregulated at 3 hpi in GhLUX‐OE lines compared to BM‐1, although the abundance of JAZ26 transcripts was consistently low in the GhLUX‐OE lines. In contrast, as a control, the expression of the JA signalling pathway marker gene GhPR4 was significantly upregulated 6 and 9 h after V. dahliae infection in both BM‐1 and in GhLUX‐OE lines (Figure 5D).

We used a transient expression system in the model plant Nicotiana benthamiana to further verify that overexpression of GhLUXA07 could significantly inhibit the bioluminescence intensity of luciferase driven by the GhJAZ2, GhJAZ11 and GhJAZ26 promoters (Figure 5E,F). Yeast one‐hybrid analysis also demonstrated that GhLUXA07 could directly bind to the promoters of GhJAZ2, GhJAZ11 and GhJAZ26 (Figure 5G). Finally, we performed the ChIP experiments to confirm that GhLUXA07 can indeed bind to the specific LBS cis‐elements of the GhJAZ2 and GhJAZ11 promoters in vivo, but no immediate enrichment signal was detected in the LBS region of the GhJAZ26 promoter. However, GhLUXA07 was still detected to be enriched to a certain extent in the non‐LBS region of the GhJAZ26 promoter, suggesting that there may be other potential binding sites or other reasons (Figure 5H). Since GhLUX regulates the transcription of almost all GhJAZ family members, we further investigated genes, such as GhPDF1.2, GhVSP, GhVSP2‐2, GhTHI2‐1, GhTHI2‐2 and GhTHI2‐4, which are downstream of the JA signalling pathway that may be involved in V. dahliae resistance. The results showed these JA downstream genes were generally downregulated in GhLUX knockdown VIGS lines and significantly upregulated in GhLUX overexpression lines. These results indicate that GhLUX enhances V. dahliae resistance by inhibiting the GhJAZ family and thereby activating the JA signalling pathway (Figure S22). Taken together, these results support a model in which the cotton circadian clock can shape rhizosphere community structure and microbe–microbe interactions, thereby affecting the temporal‐specific infection and resistance to V. dahliae. The core component of the circadian clock, GhLUX, can quickly respond to the infection of V. dahliae. As a transcriptional repressor of the circadian clock in the evening, it can inhibit the expression of core genes GhJAZ2, GhJAZ11 and GhJAZ26 in the JA signalling pathway, ultimately gating resistance to Verticillium wilt (Figure 5I).

Discussion

Endogenous circadian clocks are timekeeping mechanisms found broadly among eukaryotes and some prokaryotes. Although their core components are different, most eukaryotic clocks share the mechanism of TTFLs, which gate multiple physiological and biochemical activities in output pathways at different levels, ranging from transcription to metabolism. Cotton may have more complex circadian clock TTFLs due to the genome‐wide gene multicopy phenomenon (Hao et al. 2021; Lou et al. 2012; Wang, Du, et al. 2020). Conversely, V. dahliae, whereas it has the key components of the fungal clock, only exhibits diurnal rhythms under the conditions studied so far (Figure 2F; Cascant‐Lopez et al. 2020), but the presence or absence of endogenous circadian rhythms under the post‐infestation parasitic stage is not clear. Here we show that disrupting the cotton circadian clock by repeatedly reversing the photoperiod had a significant effect on the composition and structure of rhizosphere community at ZT13 (Figure 1C–H). Further experimental results of inoculating V. dahliae at different time points also support that ZT13 has a significantly higher impact on rhizosphere microorganisms than ZT1 (Figure 2B–E). The results suggest that the cross‐talk between the cotton circadian clock and rhizosphere microorganisms may mainly occur at night. Previous studies on exudate profiles have shown that specific soluble sugars, lipids and flavonoids, secondary metabolites closely related to plant‐microbe interactions, exhibit diurnal fluctuations in their secretion patterns (Badri et al. 2010; Brauner et al. 2018; McLaughlin et al. 2023; Robert et al. 2025). The cotton LUX involved in this study belongs to the MYB family transcription factor (type SHAQKYF) expressed in the evening, and temporal recruits ELF3 and ELF4 to form the circadian evening complex, exerting transcriptional repression at night. Our study strongly suggests that circadian core components, including LUX, may participate in regulating plant‐microbe interactions by directly or indirectly modulating the synthesis, transport, and root exudation of specific metabolites. LUX may affect resistance to V. dahliae by mediating the nighttime rhizosphere microbiome of cotton, which requires further confirmation in future studies. More interestingly, we found that V. dahliae infection can accelerate the pace of cotton's circadian clock (Figure 2H). A similar phenotype, in which bacterial infection leads to a shortened circadian period, has also been reported in Arabidopsis (Zhang et al. 2013), and our results suggest that certain specific rhizosphere microorganisms might function as zeitgebers.

To date, there has been little research evidence as well as speculation about how plants actively regulate rhizosphere microbial communities on time scales aligned with diurnal or circadian cycles (Hubbard et al. 2018; Newman et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2021). Based on previous studies, we make two reasonable speculations. First, since the circadian clock gates many physiological and biochemical processes, the mechanisms of root secretion and water uptake are both likely to be gated by the circadian clock (McLaughlin et al. 2023; Staley et al. 2017; Takase et al. 2011). Second, both diurnal rhythms of plant rhizosphere exudates and root water use efficiency cause daily fluctuations in carbon sources or other nutrients, water, and even signalling molecules in the root zone, all of which will ultimately affect the structural and functional diversity of microbial communities in the rhizosphere. In this study, we found that compared with mock, inoculation of V. dahliae V991 during the day or at night could significantly increase the abundance of Verticillium, which is also very reasonable. Interestingly, a significant decrease in the relative abundance of Fusarium and Penicillium, along with a significant increase in Mortierella, was observed in the rhizosphere microbial communities of V. dahliae V991 inoculated samples at ZT13 compared to the mock (Figure 2E). Mortierella species are generally non‐pathogenic to plants and appear to help promote plant development and suppress soil diseases (Wang et al. 2022). Fusarium is a widespread fungal plant pathogen and can infect numerous crops (Beccari et al. 2022). For example, our recent study shows that the circadian clock in Fusarium oxysporum regulates its pathogenicity toward tomato by controlling the zinc starvation response and secondary metabolism (Lu et al. 2025). Penicillium is somewhere in between the above two, including beneficial, neutral and harmful, and its impact on plants may be related to the specific species and the corresponding host plants (Srinivasan et al. 2020). Our results suggest that the cotton circadian clock gates the interaction between V. dahliae and other rhizosphere microbial communities. Since the relative abundance of Fusarium, Penicillium and Mortierella also changed in disrupted cotton clock samples at ZT13 compared to the normal clock samples (Figure 1H), we are more inclined to believe that alterations in the microbiome are due to active recruitment of plants mediated by the root circadian clock rather than passive results of V. dahliae inoculation. However, this speculation still needs further investigation.

Recent studies have shown that biotic stressors such as nematode and pathogen can directly regulate the host's circadian clock through effectors (Fu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2026). The results showed that inoculation with V. dahliae also accelerates the circadian rhythm of cotton (Figure 2H). This suggests that circadian clocks not only regulate stress pathways, but that stress pathways can also regulate circadian rhythms via feedback. However, subsequent work is required to determine whether this period shortening affects resistance or is merely a side effect. Using wild‐type with disrupted circadian clock, GhLUX knockdown and overexpressed cotton lines, we demonstrated that the maintenance of normal circadian clock function is essential for cotton Verticillium wilt resistance. The evening complex component GhLUX gates defence response to V. dahliae (Figures 3A–E and 4A–H), which belongs to an output pathway of the circadian clock. At the same time, we identified cotton GhLUX05 and GhLUX07 copies that could directly respond to V. dahliae invasion (Figure 3F–H), suggesting that the input pathway of V. dahliae infection to the circadian clock is also mediated by GhLUX. The simultaneous involvement of GhLUX in both input and output pathways suggests that GhLUX acts as a key node between the Verticillium wilt signalling pathway and the circadian clock regulatory network. When we analysed the transcriptomic data of GhLUX regulating cotton resistance, we found that almost all JAZ family members in the overexpressing GhLUX lines were significantly down‐regulated after V. dahliae infection (Figure 5B,C). Subsequently, we combined tobacco leaf transient transfection, yeast one‐hybrid and ChIP methods to demonstrate that GhLUX directly inhibits the expression of cotton JAZs at the transcriptional level (Figure 5E–H). This is an unexpected but very reasonable result, because studies have shown that the JA pathway is involved in cotton's resistance to V. dahliae (He et al. 2018; Ji et al. 2023; Li et al. 2014; Liu et al. 2023). A major challenge in the breeding of cotton and other crops is the integration of desirable traits. Due to the widespread trade‐off between growth and defence, improved disease resistance is often accompanied by a decline in yield or fibre quality. However, we investigated that cotton plants overexpressing LUX do not have any negative effect on cotton fibre yield and quality, and even enhance them with increasing the boll weight. Knockout or overexpression of core clock genes is often accompanied by photoperiod‐dependent flowering phenotypes. Therefore, in breeding, the strategy of overexpressing circadian genes throughout the entire plant may affect cultivation regionality. Previous studies suggest that overexpressing circadian genes using tissue‐specific promoters in roots, mesophyll cells, or leaf epidermal cells, which do not significantly affect flowering time (Endo et al. 2014), which may be a good option for applying LUX in cotton breeding in the future. This implies that we have good prospects for application in cotton agricultural production. Perhaps we can achieve the breeding goals of increasing V. dahliae resistance as well as improving cotton yield and quality at the same time by overexpressing GhLUX specifically in whole plants or specific tissues and organs.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Cotton ( Gossypium hirsutum L.) materials used in this study were cultivars ‘Texas Marker‐1’ (TM‐1), a genetic standard for G. hirsutum and cultivars ‘zhongzhimian 2’ (ZZM2), also the resistant cultivars in G. hirsutum . Cultivars ‘Baimian‐1’ (BM‐1) were used as background to create GhLUXA07‐overexpression transgenic lines using the vector of MWV066 modified with aminoglycoside 3′‐adenylyltransferase (AADA) selection marker and 3×Flag target, and T3 generation seedlings were used for Verticillium resistance verification. Cotton plants were cultivated with sterilized soil (a mix of peat and sawdust) in growth chamber at 75% relative humidity, 23°C, and a 12‐h light/12‐h dark (12L/12D) photoperiod, light intensity is approximately 100 μmol m−2 s−1.

DNA Extraction, PCR Amplification and Bioinformatic Analysis

The soil samples were collected from the field of Jinming Campus of Henan University, Kaifeng City, Henan Province, China (114°19′ E, 34°49′ N). The average annual temperature was 15.9°C, and the average precipitation was 983.0 mm. The soil was screened with a 2 mm sieve, and the nutrient soil (PINDSTRUP) was mixed in a 2:1 ratio, then 150 g mixed soil was packed in a 7 × 7 × 8 cm box for cotton cultivation. The cotton seeds were soaked in water for 12 h, 30°C, and then transferred to a humid environment for further germination for 12 h. When the radicle length of the seeds reached about 1 cm, the seeds were planted in a box with mixed soil, and cultivate for 30 days. The DNA was extracted from rhizosphere and bulk soils (0.5 g soil samples) using the TGuide S96 Magnetic Soil/Stool DNA Kit (TIANGEN, DP812) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The quantity and quality of DNA were determined using a BioTek Synergy HTX before PCR. The ITS1 and ITS2 region of fungal were amplified through primer pairs ITS1F (5′‐CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA‐3′) and ITS2 (5′‐GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC‐3′) and ITS2F (5′‐GCATCGATGAAGAACGCAGC‐3′) and ITS2R (5′‐TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC‐3′), respectively. Subsequently, the library was inspected by Qsep‐400 method, and sequencing was performed by Biomarker Technologies Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China, Illumina NovaSeq 6000).

According to the quality of single nucleotide, raw data were primarily filtered by Trimmomatic (version 0.33). Identification and removal of the primer sequence were processed by Cutadapt (version 1.9.1). PE reads obtained from previous steps were assembled by USEARCH (version 10) and followed by chimera removal using UCHIME (version 8.1). The high‐quality reads generated from the above steps were used in the following analysis. OTU Clustering: USEARCH (version 10.0) was applied to cluster sequences with similarity over 97% (default). Conservative threshold for OTU filtration is 0.005%. ASV Analysis: DADA2 method in QIIME2 (version 2020.06) was applied to de‐noise sequences, generating ASVs. Conservative threshold for ASV filtration is 0.005%. Using SILVA (Release138, http://www.arb‐silva.de) as reference database, the Naive Bayes classifier was used to annotate the feature sequences, and the species classification information corresponding to each feature, and then the sample community composition could be counted at various levels (phylum, class, order, family, genus, species). Alpha (Chao1, Shannon and Simpson index) and Beta Diversity (PCoA) analyses were conducted using QIIME2 (https://qiime2.org/). Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was employed to evaluate significant differences between different groups.

Cultivation of Verticillium dahliae and Cotton Root Inoculation

The highly aggressive V. dahliae strain ‘V991’ (and ‘V991‐GFP’, kindly provided by Cotton Institute of the Chinese Academy of Agriculture Sciences) was stored at −80°C as conidial suspensions preserved in 50% glycerol. To obtain V. dahliae plates, 1 μL conidial suspensions were incubated on every potato‐dextrose agar (Solarbio, Cat#P8931) plate. For plate assays, the plates were cultured in 25°C incubators with different light cycles: constant darkness, constant light, or 12L/12D cycles. To get a spore suspension, cultures on the plate were washed with 1 mL sterile water; the liquid was collected and inoculated into 50 mL potato dextrose broth (Solarbio, Cat#P9240). Then the broth was placed in a shaker at 120 rpm and 25°C for about 5 days to obtain a liquid culture. To infect cotton roots, the Czapek broth in which V. dahliae was cultured was filtered through gauze to remove hyphae and solid impurities, leaving the spore suspension liquid. The spore suspension liquid needed to be diluted to 1 × 107 conidia/mL with sterile distilled water for further infection experiment. When cotton plants were at the state of two true leaves, the seedlings were carefully removed from the soil; then the roots were dipped in the spore suspension liquid for 2 min to inoculate V. dahliae, and finally the seedlings were replanted into new soil (Chang et al. 2020). Root inoculation treatment of cotton plants in ZT13 was operated under a green light source (applies to all experiments conducted in dark conditions in this study).

Extraction of Metabolites From Cotton Root and Fungi Inhibition Assay

Cotton seedlings were grown to two true leaves states under conditions of 23°C, 75% relative humidity, and photoperiodic of 12‐h light/12‐h dark, and samples of cotton roots were collected at ZT1 and ZT13 (operated under a green light source), respectively, then each sample was fully ground with liquid nitrogen and 200 mg powder was taken in a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube for total metabolite extraction. Add 750 μL extract A (ddH2O) and ultrasonic crushing at 0°C for 30 min; all supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 15 000 rpm, 4°C, for 10 min. Then add 750 μL extract B (methanol: acetonitrile = 1:1) and ultrasonic crushing at 0°C for another 30 min, centrifugation at 15 000 rpm, 4°C, for 10 min. Combined the two supernatants, dried with nitrogen, and dissolved with 100 μL 50% methanol solution.

Dropped the conidia suspension of V. dahliae V991 at the center of the PDA plate and then cultured it under 25°C for about 1 week. 50 μL metabolites solution dropped on a filter paper which was placed on the edge of the V991 cultures, equal volume of 50% methanol solution as mock. After that, the PDA plate was continued to be cultured with the previous conditions, and observed the appearance of the fungus inhibition zone around the metabolites and mock.

Circadian Rhythm of Cotton Cotyledon Movement

Leaf movement was measured as described in soybean previously with minor modifications (Wang, Yuan, et al. 2020). Briefly, cotton (cultivar ‘BM‐1’) seedlings were grown under 75% relative humidity, 23°C, and a 12L/12D cycle for about 14 days. Poured 30 mL 1 × 107 conidia/mL V. dahliae V991 conidia suspension or mock (water) into each pot (2 plants/pot) at ZT1 or ZT13. Cotton plants then were released into free‐running conditions (constant light). Images of the tip of the unifoliolate leaf and the first true leaf position were recorded 30 min apart for 4 days using a Hikvision digital camera (DS‐2CD3T45F (D)‐I5S). The leaf position was assessed from the time series images using Image J and quantified with BRASS software.

Circadian in Phase or Out of Phase Inoculation

Cotton (cultivar ‘BM‐1’) seedlings were grown under 75% relative humidity, 23°C, and a 12‐h light/12‐h dark photoperiod for about 21 days. The 200 mL potato dextrose broth (PDB) was divided into four 100 mL conical bottles, autoclaved at 121°C for 20 min, each of which was inoculated with 1 mL 1 × 107 conidia/mL V. dahliae V991 conidia suspension. All the conical bottles were placed in a shaker with 12L/12D light cycles, two of which are covered (or removed) with tin foil to create 12D/12L photoperiodic culture conditions, 120 rpm, 25°C, for 5 days. The cultures were prepared into 1 × 107 conidia/mL conidia suspension and infected cotton roots at ZT1/ZT13.

Biomass Detection of Verticillium dahliae V991 in Cotton Roots

Specific amplified primers were designed (primer 5.0) according to the ITS (Internal Transcribed Space) region of the V. dahliae V991 ribosome gene (Table S9). Fresh cotton tissue inoculated with V. dahliae V991 was sampled, all tissues in each sample were fully ground with liquid nitrogen, then 100 mg powder was taken for total genomic DNA extraction, using a FastPure Plant DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Using GhUBQ7 gene as an internal reference gene, we measured the relative expression of V991 DNA by qPCR, thus reflecting the relative biomass of V991 in Cotton (Luo et al. 2021).

Virus‐Mediated Gene Silencing (VIGS) Assay

The Virus‐mediated Gene Silencing (VIGS) assay was performed as previous described. Briefly, pTRV2 vector inserted with target gene's specific fragment or GhCLA (positive control) gene, pTRV2 (empty) and pTRV1 vectors were transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101 strain, respectively. When the OD600 value of GV3101 culture reached 0.7–0.8, collect the bacteria and resuspended with VIGS infection solution (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl2, 200 μM acetosyringone), then co‐cultured at 25°C, 150 rpm, in the dark for 2–3 h. The cotton seedlings were cultured at 23°C, 75% humidity, 12 h light and 12 h dark light cycle until the cotyledon was flat. The infection solution of TRV:target, TRV:GhCLA and TRV:00 vectors were mixed with pTRV1 solution in equal volume, respectively, and then injected the mixed solution into the young cotyledon of cotton through a syringe (Gao et al. 2013). Cotton seedlings were continue cultivated under the former conditions until TRV:GhCLA plants showed albino phenotype, the expression level of target gene was detected by RT‐PCR for interference efficiency monitoring. All primer sequences are listed in Table S12.

RNA‐Seq Analysis

Seedlings of ‘BM‐1’ and GhLUX‐OE4 were grown under 12L/12D cycles at 23°C for 21 days, and then were inoculated with 1 × 107 conidia/mL V. dahliae V991 conidia suspension or mock (water) at ZT1/ZT13. Then roots were collected at 3 hpi (hours post inoculated) and were frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen for total RNA extraction. RNA‐seq libraries were prepared using the Illumina Directional mRNA‐Seq Library Preparation Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Biomarker, Beijing, China), resulting in double‐end 150‐bp reads in each sample. Sequenced reads were aligned to the Gossypium hirsutum CRI_v1 genome (https://cottonfgd.net/) and analysed using BMKCloud (www.biocloud.net). DEGs were identified by a significance analysis when Fold Change ≥ 2 and FDR (False Discovery Rate) < 0.01. Gene ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis for the DEGs and GhJAZs gene expression heatmap were performed using BMKCloud (www.biocloud.net).

ChIP Assays

ChIP assays were performed as previously described (Saleh et al. 2008). Seedlings of 35S:GhLUX‐OE‐Flag were grown under 12L/12D cycles at 23°C for 21 days at ZT13. The materials were harvested and crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde (Sigma‐Aldrich, F8775). Protein G‐Agarose beads (Roche, Cat# 11243233001) and an anti‐Flag antibody (Sigma‐Aldrich, Cat# F1804) were used for ChIP analysis. Primers amplifying a fragment in GhCCA1 LBS (LUX binding site) or GhUBQ7 were used for the positive control or negative control, respectively. All primer sequences are listed in Table S11.

Luciferase Reporter Assay

The promoter sequences of GhJAZ2 (−841 to −2902 bp), GhJAZ11 (−58 to −3000 bp) and GhJAZ26 (−197 to −2856 bp) were cloned into pGreenII 0800‐LUC vector, respectively, serving as reporters. Full‐length GhLUXA07 CDS (coding sequences) were cloned into pGreenII 62‐SK vector, serving as the effector. The constructs were then transformed into Agrobacterium strain GV3101 to transform tobacco. Agrobacterium strain GV3101 cells with reporter and effector were diluted to OD600 = 0.8 and co‐infiltrated into leaves of N. benthamiana. The infection solution with reporter and effector was mixed in equal volume. After 48 h of culture in the dark, fluorescein substrate (0.2 mg mL−1) was sprayed on the surface of the leaves, and the fluorescein signal in the leaves was detected by Fusion‐FX chemiluminescence instrument (Vilber, Paris, France). All primer sequences for vector construction are listed in Table S13.

Y1H Assay

The Matchmaker Gold Yeast One‐Hybrid Library Screening System was used for detecting interaction between GhLUXA07 and GhJAZ2, GhJAZ11 or GhJAZ26 promoters. The promoter sequences of GhJAZ2 (−841 to −2902 bp), GhJAZ11 (−58 to −3000 bp) and GhJAZ26 (−197 to −2856 bp) were cloned into pAbAi vector to obtain pBait‐AbAi constructs, respectively. The pBait‐AbAi recombinants were linearized with BbsI endonuclease, and integrated into the Y1HGold yeast genome by homologous recombination method. Specific Y1HGold reporters containing GhJAZs promoters serve as reporters. Full‐length GhLUXA07 CDS (coding sequences) were cloned into pGADT7 vector, and then transformed into Y1HGold strain as the effector. The recombinant yeast was inoculated on SD/‐Ura or SD/‐Leu plates containing aureobasidin A (AbA) at different concentrations and cultured in incubator at 30°C. All primer sequences are listed in Table S13.
