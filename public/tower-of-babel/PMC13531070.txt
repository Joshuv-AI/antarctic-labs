Mechanism and Treatment of Calcified Nodule-Driven Early Stent Failure

ELCA = excimer laser coronary atherectomy; ISR = in-stent restenosis.

History of Presentation

An 81-year-old man on chronic hemodialysis presented with acute chest pain and hypotension during dialysis. Electrocardiography demonstrated ST-segment elevation in the inferior leads, leading to a diagnosis of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.

Past Medical History

The patient had nephrosclerosis requiring hemodialysis for 2 years. Additional risk factors included hypertension and dyslipidemia.

Differential Diagnosis

Considerations included neointimal hyperplasia, neoatherosclerosis, stent underexpansion, and calcified nodule reprotrusion.

Investigations

Emergent coronary angiography demonstrated total occlusion of the right coronary artery (RCA) ostium (Figure 1). Initial intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) revealed circumferential severe calcification extending from the RCA ostium to segment 2, featuring a prominent calcified nodule. Primary percutaneous coronary intervention was performed using intravascular lithotripsy followed by implantation of a 3.5 × 16 mm Synergy Megatron drug-eluting stent (Boston Scientific) (Figure 2).

(B and C) Coronary angiography of the left coronary artery showing severe stenosis in the left anterior descending artery (segment 6) and diffuse severe stenosis in the left circumflex artery (segments 11-13).

(A to C) Preprocedural IVUS images demonstrating concentric severe calcified plaque with a calcified nodule at the ostial RCA. (A′ to C′) Poststent implantation IVUS images showing adequate stent expansion and apposition. IVUS = intravascular ultrasound; RCA = right coronary artery.

Three months later, the patient was readmitted with recurrent chest pain. Coronary angiography revealed 99% in-stent subocclusion at the RCA ostium. IVUS identified an in-stent calcified plaque flap, suggesting mechanical reprotrusion of the underlying peristent calcified nodule through the stent struts, consistent with a structural mechanism of restenosis rather than neointimal proliferation (Figure 3).

Intravascular Ultrasound Findings of Early In-Stent Restenosis in the RCA

(A to C) IVUS images demonstrating an in-stent calcified plaque flap (dotted circle) at the RCA ostium. IVUS = intravascular ultrasound; RCA = right coronary artery.

Management

Given the early recurrence and rigid calcification, we opted for excimer laser coronary atherectomy (ELCA). Initial attempts using a 0.9-mm X-80 excimer laser catheter (Philips Spectranetics) resulted in insufficient fragmentation of the protruding calcified tissue; therefore, the catheter was upsized to a 1.4-mm X-80 excimer laser catheter with a fluence of 60 mJ/mm2 and a repetition rate of 40 Hz.

Repeated slow passes were performed using the 1.4-mm X-80 excimer laser catheter, resulting in fragmentation of the protruding calcified nodule and photomechanical disruption of the peristent calcium behind the stent struts. ELCA was performed under continuous saline flushing without intentional contrast injection.

IVUS demonstrated fragmentation and marked reduction of the protruding calcified nodule, along with photomechanical disruption of the adjacent peristent calcium. A newly created space adjacent to the stent struts was observed after ELCA, suggestive of vaporization cavity formation (Figure 4).

(C) IVUS after ELCA demonstrated marked reduction and fragmentation of the protruding calcified material, with formation of a peristent cavity suggestive of plaque vaporization. Following ELCA, drug-coated balloon angioplasty was performed using a 4.0 × 20 mm Agent drug-coated balloon (Boston Scientific). Although transient localized contrast staining was observed at the ostium (Figure 5), no evidence of coronary perforation or hemodynamic compromise was noted.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At the 6-month follow-up, angiography confirmed preserved patency of the RCA ostium, and optical coherence tomography (OCT) demonstrated adequate stent expansion, endothelialization, and stable healing of the peristent tissue (Figure 6). These findings were consistent with durable fibrocalcific healing following ELCA-mediated plaque disruption.

(Left) Angiography demonstrates improvement in contrast staining outside the stent and no evidence of restenosis in the RCA ostium. (Right) Optical coherence tomography of the respective segments from the angiogram demonstrate healed peristent tissue with formation of a fibrocalcific bridge, preserved stent patency, and no evidence of residual calcified plaque flap. RCA = right coronary artery.

Discussion

In-stent restenosis (ISR) pathologically involves neointimal hyperplasia and neoatherosclerosis.1 Calcified nodules are characterized by fibrous cap disruption and protrusion of nodular calcium into the lumen, particularly in heavily calcified lesions and patients undergoing hemodialysis.2, 3, 4 Chronic hemodialysis is also an established predictor of ISR regardless of stent generation.5

In the present case, IVUS demonstrated a protruding nodular calcified mass extending through the stent struts into the lumen, with marked acoustic shadowing and an irregular luminal surface. These findings were consistent with progressive extrusion of nodular calcium rather than conventional neointimal hyperplasia or neoatherosclerosis.

The lesion morphology suggested an eruptive calcified nodule pattern, in which recurrent protrusion of calcified material through the stent struts contributed to early ISR. This case demonstrates that certain forms of ISR are structurally driven by recurrent calcified nodule extrusion rather than biologically driven neointimal proliferation. A similar mechanism involving recurrent protrusion of calcified nodules and stent deformation has recently been reported in hinged coronary lesions.6

Although drug-coated balloons are commonly used for ISR, ELCA may serve as a potent adjunctive strategy in lesions with protruding nodular calcium.7

Alternative treatment modalities for calcified coronary lesions, including intravascular lithotripsy, have recently gained clinical traction.8 Although intravascular lithotripsy has emerged as an important option for heavily calcified coronary lesions, evidence regarding its use for protruding in-stent calcified nodules remains limited. Therefore, in the present case, ELCA was selected to achieve direct photomechanical disruption and fragmentation of the protruding calcified tissue.

ELCA modifies coronary lesions through photochemical, photothermal, and photomechanical mechanisms.9 The photomechanical effect generated by vapor bubble expansion and acoustic shockwaves may have contributed to fragmentation of the protruding calcified nodule and disruption of peristent calcium beyond the stent struts. In the present case, IVUS findings suggested superficial debulking and fragmentation of the protruding calcified nodule, with creation of a vaporization cavity extending beyond the stent struts. The localized contrast staining observed postprocedure likely represented therapeutic vaporization of peristent plaque, consistent with prior reports describing photomechanical plaque vaporization during ELCA.9 Previous OCT-based studies have also demonstrated that ELCA may induce fracture and disruption of peristent calcium, a phenomenon referred to as “peristent modification.”7

At the 6-month follow-up, OCT demonstrated stable fibrocalcific healing with preserved luminal patency, suggesting that ELCA-mediated plaque disruption may subsequently lead to durable vessel healing.

This case underscores the importance of mechanism-based lesion assessment and tailored device selection for structurally driven ISR caused by recurrent calcified nodule extrusion.

Conclusions

ELCA may be an effective modality for managing early ISR driven by calcified plaque reprotrusion, ensuring durable vessel patency in high-risk hemodialysis patients.
