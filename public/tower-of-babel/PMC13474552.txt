Human single-cell atlas of proliferative diabetic retinopathy reveals a SOX15-overexpressing stromal population driving retinal fibrosis through the endothelin-1 -EDNRB axis
Authors: Fen Tang, Wenjing He, Zhijie Niu, Huangli Lu, Jiali Lin, Yanhua Lai, Jiazhi Li, Wen Deng, Biyan Peng, Hao Wang, Zhou Zhou, Qi Chen, Shanshan Huang, HaiBin Zhong, Ling Cui, Fan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) is a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, characterized by pathological neovascularization and progressive retinal fibrosis. Although anti-VEGF therapy effectively suppresses neovascularization, it does not address fibrovascular membranes (FVMs) formation and may paradoxically accelerate membrane contraction, increasing the risk of tractional retinal detachment. The lack of effective anti-fibrotic strategies highlights the importance to elucidate the key cellular mechanisms underlying retinal fibrosis in PDR.MethodsWe performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on primary retinal tissues from PDR organ donors and non-diabetic controls, generating a single-cell transcriptomic atlas of the human PDR retina. Critical findings were validated by immunofluorescence, in vivo and in vitro functional assays. Stromal-specific Sox15 conditional knockout mice in a two-stage laser-induced fibrosis model and siRNA-mediated knockdown in fibroblasts, and intravitreal administration of the EDNRB antagonist were performed.ResultsscRNA-seq of primary human PDR retinas generated the first comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic atlas of human PDR, revealing profound alterations in cellular composition including microglial expansion and Müller glia reprogramming as central neuroinflammatory features of PDR, and selective loss of metallothionein enriched rods subpopulation and S-cones. Notably, We identified a stromal cell population that was enriched in PDR retinas, localized in FVMs, and characterized by high SOX15 expression. Cell-cell communication analysis revealed that EDN1-EDNRB signaling between these stromal cells and Müller glia represented the most prominent intercellular interaction in PDR. Mechanistically, SOX15 regulates EDN1 transcription through binding to its promoter, driving EDN1 expression that activates EDNRB-expressing retinal macroglia to promote reactive gliosis and retinal fibrosis. Stromal-specific Sox15 conditional knockout significantly attenuated fibrotic lesion formation in vivo, and PDR-derived fibrocytes with elevated SOX15 exhibited enhanced fibroblast differentiation and EDN1 secretion, providing translational validation in human disease. Intravitreal EDNRB blockade with BQ-788 significantly reduced fibrotic area in a murine fibrosis model.ConclusionsThis study identifies key pathological features of the human PDR retina, including pervasive neuroinflammation and selective loss of photoreceptor subpopulations, and delineates the SOX15–EDN1-EDNRB axis as a novel mechanism driving retinal fibrosis. Targeting this axis may represent a promising anti-fibrotic strategy for advanced PDR.Graphical Abstract Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08677-y.

Proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) is a highly prevalent and devastating complication of diabetes mellitus (DM), affecting a large number of individuals worldwide and leading to irreversible vision loss and blindness [1, 2]. Chronic hyperglycemia drives progressive capillary obstruction, retinal ischemia, and the subsequent upregulation of angiogenic factors including VEGF, ultimately culminating in pathological neovascularization at the vitreoretinal interface. [3, 4]. As these nascent vessels regress, they are replaced by fibrous tissue that forming fibrovascular membranes (FVMs that generate traction on the retina, leading to tractional retinal detachment and blindness [4–7]. Despite major advances in anti-VEGF therapy which effectively suppresses neovascularization, the fibrotic component of FVMs remains refractory to current pharmacological interventions, and anti-VEGF agents may paradoxically accelerate tractional retinal detachment in eyes with established fibrosis [8–10]. Thus it’s a critical and urgent clinical need to elucidate the cellular and molecular mechanisms driving retinal fibrogenesis in PDR.

FVMs are contractile structures composed of vessels, stromal cells, and extracellular matrix (ECM) components [11, 12]. Their formation involves the coordinated migration, proliferation, and transdifferentiation of multiple cell populations [13]. Crucial cells involved include circulating bone marrow-derived fibrocytes, which are recruited to ischemic sites and, under the influence of TGF-β, differentiate into myofibroblasts that promote collagen deposition and membrane contraction [5, 13]. Retinal glial cells, macrophages, and endothelial cells are also implicated as contributors to ECM deposition and progressive fibrosis [13–15]. Despite these insights, the cellular mechanism of retinal fibrosis and fibrovascular membranes formation within PDR retinas are largely lacking.

The cellular complexity of PDR and the mechanisms governing FVM formation remain incompletely understood. The emergence of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has opened new avenues for dissecting the cellular heterogeneity of complex retinal diseases at single-cell resolution [16]. Prior scRNA-seq studies have profiled surgically excised FVMs from PDR patients and provided important insights into fibrogenic cell populations within these membranes. Hu et al. performed scRNA-seq on surgically harvested PDR-FVMs and generated a comprehensive cell atlas, identifying eight cell clusters with microglia as the major population [14]. Corano Scheri et al. characterized preretinal FVM cell populations by scRNA-seq and identified AEBP1 as a previously unrecognized profibrotic transcription factor upregulated in stromal clusters [12]. However, these studies were conducted on surgically excised FVM specimens, a sampling approach that, while informative, captures only the end-stage fibrotic product and necessarily misses the broader retinal microenvironment. A comprehensive single-cell atlas of the intact human PDR retina would provide an unbiased view of the full transcriptomic landscape of human PDR.

In the present study, we performed scRNA-seq on primary human PDR retinal tissues from organ donors, with non-diabetic retinas as controls, generating the first single-cell transcriptomic atlas of the intact human PDR retina. We identified a distinct DSP+ SDC4+ stromal cell population enriched in PDR retinas. This population is characterized by high SOX15 expression, and comprehensive in vitro and in vivo investigations showed that SOX15 drives EDN1 transcription in stromal cells, which in turn activates EDNRB signaling in retinal macroglia to promote reactive gliosis and fibrosis formation. Pharmacological EDNRB blockade significantly attenuated retinal fibrosis in a murine model. By integrating human scRNA-seq discovery with experimental mechanistic validation, this study establishes the SOX15-Overexpressing stromal cell population as a key driver of retinal fibrosis in PDR, and defines the SOX15–EDN1-EDNRB axis as a potential novel anti-fibrotic therapeutic target.

Methods

Collection of human specimens

The retinal tissues were collected from post-mortem donors. Organ donors diagnosed with PDR(two subjects with 4 retinas) or Control individual(three subjects with 6 retinas), were recruited from The People’s Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region. Control individuals without any ocular diseases or Diabetes were selected based on similar age and gender distribution as the PDR group. Detailed clinical characteristics of the subjects can be found in Supplementary Table 1. Retinal samples were cut into pieces and immediately transferred to a sterile container filled with a suitable storage medium to maintain cellular viability. This study was conducted following the guidelines of Declaration of Helsinki, ethics approval was obtained from the People’s Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region (KY-GZR-2023–106). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

FVMs were collected from patients with PDR undergoing pars plana vitrectomy for tractional retinal detachment. Three Proliferative vitreoretinopathy membranes (PVRMs) were collected from patients with rhegmatogenous retinal detachment complicated by PVR grade C or D, who required pars plana vitrectomy with membrane peeling. The FVMs and PVRMs were immediately fixed in 4% PFA for immunofluorescence, or ice-cold Dulbecco’s PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for live-cell flow cytometry analysis.

scRNA sequencing and analysis

Retinal tissues were dissociated into single cells using enzymatic digestion and mechanical dissociation. Dissociated cells were then subjected to single-cell capture using droplet-based systems. The captured cells were processed to obtain single-cell cDNA libraries for subsequent sequencing. The obtained single-cell cDNA libraries were prepared using a commercially available library preparation kit. The libraries were barcoded to enable multiplexing and sequenced using high-throughput sequencing platforms 10x genomics, generating paired-end reads with sufficient depth for transcriptomic analysis. Low-quality cells with <500 or >6,000 unique features as well as cells with >30% mitochondrial RNA were filtered out. Seurat packages in R studio were used to perform data analysis on the UMI count matrix. The QC metrics was summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Doublets were identified using scDblFinder.SCTransform was used for integration and batch effect correction. Principal component analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), were employed for Dimension Reduction. Cell types were annotated based on the expression of known marker genes and reference databases. The Seurat function ‘‘FindMarkers()’’ was used to conduct differential gene expression analysis and identify Differential expression genes (DEGs) with significantly altered expression between two groups. Gene ontology (GO)-based gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)-based GSEA were performed to identify enriched biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular pathways. Cell-cell communication analysis was performed using CellChat with the human ligand-receptor interaction database (CellChatDB.human). Overexpressed signaling genes and ligand-receptor interactions were identified using the default CellChat workflow, in which over-expressed signaling genes were detected for each cell group using a one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test with a significance threshold of thresh.p = 0.05.

Immunofluorescence on retina and FVMs

Human eyeballs from PDR and non-diabetic control donors were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and processed for cryosectioning. When performing immunostaining, retinal sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: anti-VEGFA (ab52917, Abcam, 1:200), anti-VEGFB (ab133606, Abcam, 1:100), anti-GFAP (ab7260,Abcam,1:500),anti-CRALBP(ab154898,Abcam,1:100),anti-S100β(ab52642, Abcam,1:100), and anti-EDNRB (ab117529, Abcam, 1:100).The FMVs and PVRMs membranes were flat mounted for immunostaining. After permeabilization and blocking, primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C: anti-Desmoplakin (DSP; ab109445, Abcam, 1:200), anti-Syndecan-4 (SDC4; ab74139, Abcam, 1:100), anti-PAX6 (ab5790, Abcam, 1:200), anti-SOX15 (ab229260, Abcam, 1:150), anti-CD31 (ab9498, Abcam, 1:100), anti-von Willebrand Factor (vWF; ab154193, Abcam, 1:200), anti-GFAP (ab7260, Abcam, 1:500), anti-Endothelin-1 (ab2786,Abcam,1:100) and anti-Vimentin (ab92547, Abcam, 1:500). Sections were then incubated for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark with appropriate species-matched Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cell culture, immunostaining and siRNA treatment

NIH-3T3 were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco),and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. The passages between 4 and 6 were used in experiments. After serum starvation in basal DMEM (0.5% FBS) for 24 hours, cells were cultured under normal glucose (NG) conditions (5.5 mM D-glucose) or high glucose (HG) conditions (25 mM D-glucose) for the indicated durations. Cells were fixed for immunofluorescence staining to assess expression of SOX15 (#720155, Invitrogen). SOX15 gene silencing was achieved using a Silencer Select siRNA targeting mouse SOX15 (#4390771; ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). A non-targeting Silencer Select Negative Control siRNA (#4390843; ThermoFisher Scientific) was used as a control. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Knockdown efficiency was validated at both the mRNA and protein levels (Supplementary Figure 8). Cells were then fixed for immunofluorescence staining of fibronectin (ab268020, Abcam; 1:100), and Collagen I (ab316222, Abcam; 1:100).

Sox15 conditional knockout mice and two-stage laser-induced retinal fibrosis Model

Sox15 conditional knockout mice with fibroblast-specific deletion were generated using a Col1a2-2A-CreERT2–loxP system. Sox15fl/fl mice and Col1a2-2A-CreERT2 mice were obtained from Shanghai Model Organisms Center. Sox15fl/fl mice were crossed with Col1a2-2A-CreERT2+/− mice to generate Sox15fl/fl;Col1a2-2A-CreERT2+/− offspring (Sox15ΔFIB). Genotyping was confirmed by PCR using tail biopsies. To induce fibroblast-specific Sox15 deletion, 6–8-week-old mice received five consecutive daily intraperitoneal injections of tamoxifen (75 mg/kg/day; Sigma-Aldrich, T5648) dissolved in corn oil. After a 7-day washout period to allow clearance of tamoxifen and ensure complete Cre-mediated recombination, retinal fibrosis was induced using a two-stage laser photocoagulation protocol adapted from Lambert et al. [17]. Knock out efficiency was verified by RT-qPCR in bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) isolated from Sox15^WT and Sox15^ΔFIB mice (Supplementary Figure 8 A-B). Laser burns (spot size 50 μm; duration 100 ms; power 200–250 mW) were applied around the optic nerve(3–4 burns per eye). Seven days later, laser photocoagulation was repeated at the same locations to promote persistent fibrovascular lesion formation. For all quantifications, values from multiple lesions were averaged to generate a single value, and treated as one biological replicate for statistical analysis. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and adhered to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Ten days or twenty days after the second laser treatment, mice were euthanized and choroidal flat mounts were prepared for immunofluorescence staining. Tissues were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against collagen I (#MA569756,ThermoFisher), CD45 (#ab25386,Abcam), CD31 (#AF3628,R&D Systems), fibronectin (ab268020, Abcam), and Isolectin B4 (#ab176753,Abcam), and Iba1(#ab153696,Abcam). Images were acquired using a Leica confocal microscope. Fibrotic area was quantified using ImageJ, and fibroblast cell infiltration was determined by manual counting. Mouse vitreous was collected at the time of sacrifice. EDN1 concentrations were quantified using ELISA kit (Endothelin-1 Quantikine ELISA Kit; R&D Systems, #DET100). The assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

ChIP-seq, ChIP-qPCR, Motif Analysis, and luciferase reporter assay

ChIP-seq was performed to identify genome-wide SOX15-binding sites. After formaldehyde crosslinking, chromatin was sheared and immunoprecipitated with a SOX15 antibody (Santa Cruz, #sc-166964X) or normal IgG as a control, with an input sample retained. Enriched DNA was purified and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using Bowtie2, Peak calling was performed with MACS2, and peak annotation and motif enrichment analyses were conducted using HOMER. ChIP-seq signals were visualized using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV). Known motif enrichment analysis was performed using the HOMER findMotifsGenome.pl pipeline. Candidate SOX15-binding motifs within the EDN1 promoter region were predicted using the JASPAR database. High-confidence JASPAR-predicted motifs were further compared with the HOMER-derived SOX15 motif using the TOMTOM motif comparison tool.

Based on the ChIP-seq peak and integrated motif analyses, ChIP-qPCR primers were designed and validated using NCBI BLAST. The primer sequences were:Forward:5′-TAGGCAACATTCCTTGACCCTCC-3′;Reverse:5′-AGCTACAGGGGCTGATGTTTT-3′.Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed using the anti-SOX15 antibody or normal IgG control, and enrichment at the EDN1 promoter was quantified by quantitative PCR using the percent-input method.

For the luciferase reporter assay, the EDN1 promoter region containing the SOX15 binding sequences (chr6:12,289,474– 12,289,483) was PCR-amplified and cloned into the pGL3-Promoter vector to generate the wild-type (WT) reporter. In the mutant (MUT) reporter, the core SOX15-binding motif within this region was disrupted: 5′-TTTTTGTCCT-3′ was replaced by 5′-TTCCCACCCT-3′(Supplementary figure 10B). The remaining procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Fibrocyte isolation, culture and differentiation

Peripheral blood was collected from 5 PDR patients and 5 healthy adult volunteers with no history of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or chronic inflammatory conditions into EDTA-coated vacutainer tubes (BD Biosciences) and processed within 4 hours. Demographic and clinical information, including age, sex, diabetes status, diabetes duration, HbA1c level, medication history, and inflammatory comorbidities, were collected and summarized in Supplementary Table S4. PBMCs were isolated by Lymphoprep (STEMCELL Technologies, #07861) density gradient centrifugation at 800×g for 20 minutes at room temperature. Fibrocytes were cultured using a protocol from established methods [18, 19]. For fibrocyte differentiation assays, isolated PBMCs were seeded at 5 × 106 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured in complete DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. After 2 days, non-adherent cells (primarily lymphocytes) were removed by gentle aspiration, and adherent cells were maintained in fresh complete medium. At day 6, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for immunofluorescence and morphological analysis to assess fibrocyte differentiation into spindle-shaped fibroblast-like cells. Cell culture supernatants were collected at day 6, centrifuged at 2,000×g for 10 minutes, and stored at −80 °C. EDN1 levels were measured by ELISA using Human Endothelin-1 Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, #DET100) according to manufacturers’ instructions.

For SOX15 expression analysis in freshly isolated fibrocytes, PBMCs were stained with anti-CD45 (BioLegend, #304006), and anti-Collagen I (Abcam, #ab316222). CD45+ Collagen I+ fibrocytes were sorted using BD flow cytometer. Sorted cells were immediately seeded onto glass chamber slides, allowed to adhere, then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes and stained with anti-SOX15 antibody (Abcam, #ab229260) overnight at 4 °C following standard immunofluorescence protocols.

Intravitreal injection of EDNRB antagonist BQ-788

The wild type C57/B6 mice undergoing two-stage laser photocoagulation received intravitreal injections of the selective EDNRB antagonist BQ-788 (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog #B157) or vehicle control (DMSO). BQ-788 was dissolved in sterile DMSO to a stock concentration of 1.2 mg/ml, and diluted to a working concentration of 0.2 mg/ml immediately before injection. Injections were administered immediately after each laser treatment. Under a surgical microscope, a 33-gauge Hamilton syringe was inserted through the sclera approximately 0.5 mm posterior to the limbus to avoid lens damage. 1 µL of BQ-788 working solution (0.2 µg) or sterile vehicle was injected into the vitreous cavity. Mice were sacrificed 20 days after the second injection, and eyeballs were processed for choroidal flat-mount analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Comparisons among two groups were performed using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Comparisons among the three groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

PDR retinas exhibited profound alterations in cellular composition. Rod photoreceptors were markedly reduced (69.72% to 47.04%), accompanied by a decline in cones, while microglia expanded nearly ninefold (2.04% to 18.99%) alongside increases in astrocytes and inflammatory cells, collectively indicating photoreceptor apoptosis and neuroinflammatory activation (Fig. 1D). Additionally, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the Ctrl and PDR were identified in each clusters, and the top upregulated and downregulated DEGs across all clusters were displayed in Fig. 1E, revealing widespread transcriptional reprogramming in PDR, with Müller glia carrying the highest DEG burden and downregulated genes predominating over upregulated genes across all cell types (Fig. 1F). Histological analysis corroborated these findings, showing retinal layer disorganization, nerve fiber layer edema, and disruption of the ganglion cell and nuclear layers (Fig. 1G).

To further dissect photoreceptor heterogeneity, we performed subclustering analysis on rods, identifying 9 distinct subpopulations (Supplementary Figure 4A–B). MALAT1-negative degenerating rods were increased in PDR, while healthy rod clusters were significantly reduced. Among these, the C4 subpopulation showed the most pronounced depletion (Supplementary Figure 4C–D), and was uniquely enriched in metallothionein genes (MT1E, MT2A, MT1X), with GSEA confirming significant enrichment of “cellular zinc ion homeostasis” pathways (Supplementary Figure 4E–F), suggesting a specialized role in retinal zinc regulation. Pseudotime trajectory analysis (Monocle3) reconstructed the progression from healthy to degenerating rods, identifying suppression of heat shock protein genes (HSPA1A, HSPA1B) and MALAT1, alongside upregulation of mitochondrial-encoded genes (MT-ATP6, MT-CO1, MT-ND5), as key transcriptional hallmarks of rod degeneration (Supplementary Figure 4 G–H). Cone subclustering revealed disproportionate loss of S-cones in PDR (6.69% to 1.53%), indicating selective vulnerability of short-wavelength–sensitive photoreceptors (Supplementary Figure 5). Collectively, PDR retinas undergo extensive cellular remodeling characterized by microglial expansion, broad photoreceptor depletion, preferential loss of zinc homeostasis–associated rod subpopulations, and selective S-cone depletion.

A PDR-enriched stromal cell population was identified and involved in PDR progression

The stromal cell cluster was initially identified by overexpression of canonical mesenchymal stromal cell markers AKR1C2, S100A4, and THY1 (Supplementary Figure 6A). Lineage-marker analyses further excluded potential contributions from other retinal cell populations (Supplementary Figure 7F). Subclustering analysis revealed four distinct subtypes (C0–C3) (Fig. 2A). The stromal population was dominated by C0 and C1, which together accounted for approximately 90% of all stromal cells, whereas C2 and C3 represented only a minor fraction (Supplementary Figure 7A). Among these, C0, C1, and C3 were preferentially enriched in PDR retinas, while C2 was equally distributed between Ctrl and PDR (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Figure 7B). C2 uniquely overexpressed ANXA5 and lacked expression of the other stromal markers (Fig. 2C–D). In further, the donor contribution and composition were examined. The stromal cells were detected in both PDR donors, with 80.6% derived from PDR2, 16.2% from PDR1, and only 3.2% from a single control donor (Ctrl3). DEG analysis using FindAllMarkers identified PAX6, DSP, SDC4, and SOX15 as the most prominently overexpressed genes within the stromal cluster (Fig. 2B, red box). Critically, PAX6 and SOX15 are transcription factors governing stem cell maintenance and differentiation potential.

To functionally characterize this population, we analyzed 952 DEGs from the stromal cluster. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in “regulating the pluripotency of stem cells”, consistent with the overexpression of PAX6 and SOX15 (Fig. 2E). Complementarily, GSEA identified strong enrichment of liver fibrosis in this population (Fig. 2F). These enrichment results suggest that this PDR-enriched stromal population may engage transcriptional programs related to tissue fibrosis. Taken together, these data identify a stromal cell population enriched in PDR retina and provide a basis for further investigations into its potential role in retinal fibrosis.

The DSP+ SDC4+ stromal cells are enriched in FVMs and exhibit predominant SOX15 expression

To confirm the existence and spatial localization of the stromal cell population identified by scRNA-seq, immunofluorescence staining was performed on both retinal tissue sections and FVM tissues from PDR patients. In contrast, DSP+ SDC4+ cells were identified within FVM tissues from PDR patients, displaying strong DSP and moderate SDC4 immunoreactivity (Fig. 3C). In comparison, a subset of DSP+ SDC4+ cells, shown in the top panel (magnified region within the yellow rectangle), exhibited nuclear PAX6 immunoreactivity. Comparative analysis of VEGF signaling networks revealed distinct pattern in PDR compared to controls. In control retinas, VEGF signaling occurred primarily between ECs, whereas in PDR, complicated cellular interactions between astrocytes, Müller glia, EC and stromal cells are observed within the VEGF network (Fig. 6C–D). Differential expression analysis of VEGF family ligands and receptors revealed isoform-specific patterns (Fig. 6E). VEGFA was massively upregulated in PDR retinas, primarily expressed by Müller glia, stromal cells and astrocytes, but minimally detectable in controls. In contrast, VEGFB expression was comparable between the PDR and control. VEGFC expression was negligible in both groups. Ligand-receptor pairing analysis showed that VEGFA-VEGFR1 interactions contributed most significantly to the VEGF signaling network in PDR, whereas VEGFB-VEGFR1 predominated in controls (Fig. 6F). These findings consistent with the fact that VEGFA as the primary driver of pathological neovascularization in PDR, while VEGFB appears to maintain physiological retinal vasculature.

To validate these findings, we performed immunofluorescence on retinal cryosections from control and PDR donors. VEGFA immunoreactivity was markedly elevated in the NFL layer of PDR retinas compared to controls (Fig. 6G). VEGFB expression and distribution were comparable between groups, corroborating the scRNA-seq data. Collectively, these results establish the remodeling of the VEGF Signaling Network, and stromal cells may contribute to the pathological VEGFA pool in PDR.

To validate these findings, we performed immunofluorescence on human PDR retinal cryosections and FVMs. In healthy retinas, GFAP+ cells were largely confined to the NFL (Fig. 8C,E). In PDR retinas, GFAP+ CRALBP+ activated Müller glia processes extended radially through the INL and ONL, characteristic of reactive gliosis (Fig. 8C, top panel). However, activated Müller glia were substantially more abundant than astrocytes in PDR retinal tissues, suggesting that activated Müller glia may represent a major, although not exclusive, contributor to increased retinal EDNRB expression, suggesting enhanced responsiveness to EDN1 in the PDR microenvironment. These results corroborate our scRNA-seq data and support the hypothesis that upregulated EDN1-EDNRB signaling in retinal macroglia, especially in Müller glia promotes reactive gliosis in PDR, which is the early pathological response preceding fibrosis.

To investigate the anti-fibrotic potential of targeting the EDN1-EDNRB axis, we administered the selective EDNRB antagonist BQ-788 via intravitreal injection in the two-stage laser-induced fibrosis model. Mice received BQ-788 or vehicle control immediately after each laser treatment and were sacrificed 20 days after the second laser. Choroidal flat-mount immunofluorescence revealed that BQ-788 treatment significantly reduced Fibronectin+Collagen I+ fibrotic lesions compared to vehicle controls (Fig. 8I). Quantitative analysis demonstrated a 43% reduction in fibrotic area in BQ-788-treated eyes (7,770 µm2 vs 13,762 µm2 in controls; p = 0.0089; Fig. 8J). Additionally, we also observed spindle-shaped fibroblast cells surrounding fibrotic lesions (Fig. 8I, red arrows). BQ-788 treatment markedly reduced the number of these perifibrous fibroblasts (3.88 cells/lesion vs 10.75 cells/lesion in controls; p < 0.01; Fig. 8 K). Additional analyses demonstrated that BQ-788 treatment did not significantly alter vascular lesion or IBA1+ infiltration area (Supplementary Figure 11). These findings together support the critical contribution of enhanced EDN1-EDNRB signaling to retinal fibrotic remodeling in PDR.

Discussion

In this study, we present the first comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic atlas of human PDR retinas derived from primary patient tissues, revealing profound alterations in cellular composition and transcriptomic landscapes that recapitulate the pathological hallmarks of PDR. Building on this atlas, we identify a DSP+SDC4+stromal cell population, which enriched in FVMs of PDR and overexpressing SOX15, plays a crucial role in retinal fibrosis. We further found that SOX15 functions as a critical transcriptional factor to promote EDN1expression. Enhanced EDN1-EDNRB signaling in retinal microglial was observed in PDR and contribute to retinal gliosis and retinal fibrosis, a pathway validated through stromal cell Sox15 conditional knockout and pharmacological EDNRB blockade. Collectively, these findings establish the SOX15–EDN1-EDNRB axis as a novel mechanism and promising therapeutic target in retinal fibrosis, offering a potential anti-fibrotic strategy that addresses critical limitations of current anti-VEGF paradigms in PDR.

At the cellular composition level, our atlas reveals two major pathological signatures in PDR retinas. The first is pervasive neuroinflammation. The near-ninefold microglial expansion corroborates previous studies identifying microglia as the dominant population in PDR fibrovascular membranes [14]. Notably, Müller glia exhibited the highest DEG burden among all retinal cell types, suggests that Müller glia undergo profound transcriptional reprogramming in PDR [26]. Together, these findings establish microglial expansion and Müller glial reprogramming as central neuroinflammatory features of PDR.

The second major pathological signature is widespread photoreceptor neurodegeneration, evidenced by the dramatic reduction in rods (69.72% to 47.04%) and concurrent cone decline. Among rod subpopulations, a metallothionein-enriched subcluster (C4; MT1E, MT2A, MT1X) critical for retinal zinc ion homeostasis was completely depleted in PDR. Previous studies have emphasized the importance of calcium homeostasis in maintaining photoreceptor health during DR [27], our findings highlight zinc dyshomeostasis as a previously unrecognized vulnerability; The selective loss of C4 rods may suggest that zinc supplementation warrants investigation as a neuroprotective strategy. At the cone level, we detected that cones apoptosis in PDR was mainly enriched on S-cones (short-wavelength-sensitive cones), which are primarily responsible for blue color vision. This observation correlates with clinical findings demonstrating tritan-like color vision deficiency in DR patients [28], providing a cellular basis for this clinical phenomenon, and suggest S-cone loss could serve as a non-invasive biomarker for PDR neurodegeneration.

The key finding of our study is the identification of a stromal cell population that is enriched in PDR, representing a critical pathological hallmark distinguishing PDR from non-diabetic retinas. Spatial validation by immunofluorescence confirmed that DSP+ SDC4+ stromal cells are enriched within FVMs, a localization that positions them as crucial contributors to fibrotic membrane formation. Their absence in PVRM is particularly informative. Although PVR similarly involves epiretinal fibrosis, its primary pathogenic mechanism is epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of RPE cells triggered by rhegmatogenous retinal detachment, a process fundamentally distinct from the chronic hyperglycemic of PDR [29, 30]. This PDR-enriched, together with our in vitro finding that SOX15 upregulation in fibroblasts is inducible by high-glucose conditions, suggests that the hyperglycemic microenvironment might necessary for the occurrence and maintenance of this stromal cell population.

It is also worth noting that prior scRNA-seq studies of PDR have focused on FVM tissues surgically retrieved during vitrectomy. One landmark study identified eight cellular populations in FVMs with microglia as the dominant cell type, and found mesenchymal lineage accounting for 8.2% [14]. A subsequent scRNA-seq study of PDR FVMs proposed AEBP1 as a critical modulator of fibrogenesis, supported by bioinformatic analysis and tissue-level immunostaining [12]. However, both of these studies analyzed only the portion of FVM tissue that could be surgically peeled during vitrectomy (represent the superficial, vitreous-facing layers), and therefore systematically excluded the deepest FVM layers that remain tightly adherent to the retinal surface. In contrast, our study utilized whole retinal specimens that inherently capture the full FVM including the retina-adherent component, providing a more complete and less selection-biased representation of FVM cellular biology.

Another major contribution of this study is the identification of SOX15 as a novel transcriptional regulator of retinal fibrosis, a role that has not previously been investigated in ocular disease. The SOX (SRY-related HMG-box) transcription factor family comprises approximately 20 members that govern cell fate determination, stem cell pluripotency, and differentiation across diverse tissues [31]. Several SOX family members have been implicated in fibrogenesis across organ systems. Notably, SOX9 functions as a transcription factor that promotes cardiac fibrosis and inflammation following myocardial infarction [32], and endothelial-specific SOX9 overexpression induces fibrosis in the heart, lung, and liver [33]. In addition, SOX2 has been identified as a key mediator of fibrogenesis in vascular calcification and systemic sclerosis [34]. Against this background, our findings that SOX15, which previously known for its roles in embryonic stem cell maintenance [35] and neural fate determination [36], is overexpressed in the stromal cell within FVMs and circulating fibrocytes from PDR, suggest it might play a critical role in retinal fibrosis. Functional validation further supports this hypothesis: stromal-specific Sox15 deletion in vivo significantly reduced fibrotic lesions in a two-stage laser-induced fibrosis model, while siRNA-mediated Sox15 knockdown in fibroblasts in vitro led to decreased extracellular matrix production. Together, these in vivo and in vitro findings establish SOX15 as a key transcriptional regulator of retinal fibrosis.

Our cell-cell communication analysis identified the EDN1-EDNRB signaling axis between stromal cells and Müller glia as the most prominent intercellular interaction within the PDR retinal microenvironment. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that EDN1 upregulation is transcriptionally driven by SOX15. CHIP-Seq, silico motif analysis and CHIP qPCR revealed SOX15-binding within the EDN1 promoter, and luciferase reporter assays confirmed that SOX15 enhances EDN1 promoter activity. This regulatory relationship was further validated in vivo, where Sox15ΔFIB mice exhibited a 67% attenuation of EDN1 induction in the retinal fibrosis model. Importantly, circulating fibrocytes derived from patients with PDR, characterized by elevated SOX15 expression, secreted significantly higher levels of EDN1 and displayed enhanced fibroblast differentiation compared with controls, thereby providing translational evidence supporting activation of the SOX15–EDN1 axis in human disease.

EDN1 is a well-established vasoactive peptide with potent pro-fibrotic effects across multiple organ systems, including the lung, heart, and liver [37–39]. While its role in retinal fibrosis remains unexplored, increased EDN1 levels have been consistently observed in the vitreous and fibrovascular membranes of PDR eyes, implicating EDN1 in disease progression [40, 41]. However, the upstream regulatory mechanisms driving EDN1 expression and its downstream cellular targets in PDR have remained largely undefined. Our findings address this critical gap by identifying SOX15 as a previously unrecognized transcriptional regulator of EDN1 and by establishing a mechanistic framework linking stromal cell activation to retinal fibrosis via paracrine signaling. We note that EDN1 within the retina is not produced by a single cell type: it can be derived from multiple retinal cell populations, including vascular endothelial cells, macroglia, and RPE cells [42, 43]. Against this background, our results and findings extends this landscape by establishing these stromal cells as an additional important source of intraocular EDN1 in PDR.

Reactive gliosis of Müller glia represents an early and pivotal event in retinal fibrogenesis, characterized by upregulation of intermediate filament proteins such as GFAP and vimentin [44, 45]. Consistent with this paradigm, our analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing datasets revealed a marked expansion of GFAP+ Müller glia in PDR. This observation was corroborated by immunofluorescence analyses of PDR retinal sections and fibrovascular membranes, which demonstrated robust Müller glia activation and reactive gliosis, compared with non-diabetic controls. Notably, our data indicate that increased EDN1 may act on retinal macroglia via overexpressed EDNRB, a G protein–coupled receptor that we found upregulated on both activated Müller glia and astrocytes.

EDNRB is a seven-transmembrane GPCR capable of coupling to multiple Gα subunits and activating diverse intracellular signaling cascades, including PKC/ERK, PI3K/AKT, and RhoA/ROCK pathways [46, 47]. Activation of these pathways within Müller glia is known to promote cytoskeletal remodeling, proliferation, and extracellular matrix production, all of which are hallmarks of reactive gliosis and fibrogenesis [48, 49]. A previous experimental study in STZ induced diabetes rats have shown dynamic changes in EDN receptor expression, with EDNRB levels increasing in the late stage of disease alongside elevated EDN1 signaling [50]. This is consistent with our findings in human PDR tissues, where EDNRB is significantly upregulated in retinal macroglia, and coincides with increased EDN1-EDNRB ligand-receptor interactions between stromal cells and glial populations. It is worthy to note that EDNRB was upregulated greatly on activated Müller glia, it was also increased in astrocytes in PDR retinas. Given the substantially greater abundance of activated Müller glia compared with astrocytes in PDR tissues, Müller glia are likely to represent a major, although not exclusive responding population. Collectively, these data support a model in which SOX15-driven EDN1 overproduction establishes a paracrine signaling axis that engages retinal macroglia, particularly activated Müller glia via EDNRB, thereby promoting reactive gliosis and contributing to retinal fibrotic remodeling. However, future studies using Müller glia-specific Ednrb deletion and EDN1 stimulation assays in purified Müller glia will be important to define the Müller glia’s contribution to EDN1-driven gliosis and fibrosis.

From a therapeutic perspective, the EDN1-EDNRB axis represents an attractive and clinically actionable target. Endothelin receptor antagonists (ERAs), such as bosentan and ambrisentan, are already approved for the treatment of pulmonary arterial hypertension and have demonstrated acceptable safety [51, 52]. For instance, clinical trials have suggested benefit of ERAs in diabetic nephropathy [53], raising the possibility that similar approaches might be effective in DR. Moreover, a study suggest potential benefits of ERAs in diabetic microvascular complications in mice. Experimental evidence in rodent models of hypertensive and diabetic retinopathy has further shown that pharmacological blockade of endothelin receptors EDNRB could reduces retinal vascular leakage and VEGF expression [54]. Consistent with these findings, our study further demonstrates that inhibition of the EDN1-EDNRB pathway attenuates retinal fibrogenesis in mouse models.

Study limitations

While this study provides important mechanistic insights into retinal fibrosis in PDR, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the absence of non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) samples in our scRNA-seq cohort represents a missed opportunity to characterize the transcriptomic transition from non-proliferative to proliferative disease and to identify early cellular markers preceding FVM formation. Second, due to the limited availability of suitable human specimens, our donor cohort was relatively small. A larger cohort would further strengthen the robustness and generalizability of the disease-associated cellular and transcriptional changes identified in this study. Third, our in vitro mechanistic studies relied, in part, on NIH-3T3 fibroblasts. Future studies using primary human derived stromal cells will be valuable to further strengthen their translational relevance. Fourth, although we demonstrate that SOX15 is necessary for retinal fibrosis and promote EDN1 transcription, the upstream signals that induce SOX15 upregulation in the diabetic retinal microenvironment remain incompletely defined. Identifying these upstream regulators could reveal additional nodes for therapeutic intervention within this pathway. Fifth, the Col1a2-2A-CreERT2 driver targets the broad fibroblast/stromal population and does not precisely correspond to the DSP+ SDC4+ SOX15+ stromal population identified in human PDR tissue, although the present findings support a functional contribution of stromal Sox15 to retinal fibrosis, more refined lineage-specific genetic models will be valuable for future studies. Lastly, our in vivo validation employed a laser-induced subretinal fibrosis model, which recapitulates acute injury-driven fibrosis but does not fully replicate the chronic, metabolically driven fibrotic process characteristic of human PDR. Future studies using longitudinal diabetic animal models or humanized systems will be important to validate the translational relevance of these findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study uncovers an enhanced EDN1-EDNRB signaling axis that promote retinal fibrosis in PDR. By integrating single-cell transcriptomics with functional validation, we highlight a mechanism of fibrotic remodeling mediated by a SOX15-Overexpressing Stromal Population. These findings not only advance our understanding of retinal fibrosis in PDR pathogenesis but also suggest that SOX15 and EDN1-EDNRB may represent promising therapeutic targets. Future translational efforts will be essential to determine whether targeting this pathway can improve visual outcomes beyond current anti-VEGF therapies.
