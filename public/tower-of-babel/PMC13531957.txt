Judicial substitute consent for medical procedures in adults lacking decision-making capacity: a five-year experience from a Polish tertiary hospital
Authors: Michał Sekuła, Maciej Dubaj, Rafał Patryn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInformed consent is a fundamental ethical requirement in medical practice and an expression of respect for patient autonomy. However, when adult patients lack decision-making capacity and no legal representative is available, Polish law requires substitute consent from a guardianship court. Empirical data on this mechanism in routine clinical practice remain limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective review of court petitions submitted by a tertiary academic medical center in eastern Poland seeking judicial authorization for medical procedures involving adult patients between June 2019 and June 2024. In total, 240 case files were analysed, including data on patient characteristics, causes of incapacity, requested procedures, court decisions, and time to judicial decision.ResultsThe median patient age was 73 years (interquartile range 62–83), and 76.7% of patients were aged 61 years or older. The most common cause of incapacity was impaired verbal–logical communication (65.0%), followed by unconsciousness (20.8%) and dementia (7.5%). The most frequently requested procedures were blood transfusions or administration of blood products (n = 201), placement of a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube (n = 149), and tracheostomy (n = 27). Court authorization was granted in 97.1% of cases, with a median decision time of 5 days.ConclusionsJudicial substitute consent in Poland primarily concerns older patients with acute neurological impairment or advanced multimorbidity. Although approval rates are high and median processing times are relatively short, even brief delays may have clinically and ethically significant consequences in time-sensitive situations. These findings highlight the need to balance legal safeguards, patient autonomy, and timely access to care.Keywords: Substitute consent, Decision-making capacity, Patient autonomy, Judicial authorization, Medical decision making

Introduction

Contemporary medicine has evolved from a paternalistic model of care, in which physicians held primary decision-making authority, to a partnership-based approach that emphasises patient autonomy and shared decision-making [1]. Informed consent is a central element of this framework, requiring that patients receive adequate information and voluntarily agree to or refuse proposed medical interventions. The ethical and legal foundations of informed consent were shaped throughout the twentieth century and are now firmly embedded in national and international legal systems [2–6].

Under Polish law, patients have the right to grant or refuse informed consent after receiving appropriate information. In the case of minors and legally incapacitated persons, consent for medical treatment is generally provided by a statutory representative; however, in situations where such a representative is absent, unavailable, or disagrees with the proposed intervention, the matter may be referred to a guardianship court. A different mechanism applies to adult patients who retain full legal capacity but are temporarily unable to express a valid decision: in such cases, substitute consent for a medical procedure is granted by a guardianship court. Many of the clinical conditions leading to temporary or permanent loss of decision-making capacity—such as acute neurological disorders, dementia, or severe systemic illness—can also be understood as forms of disability that significantly impair an individual’s ability to exercise autonomy in medical decision-making. An exception applies in emergency situations involving an immediate threat to life or health, where treatment may proceed without prior judicial approval in accordance with statutory provisions. In the Polish legal system, the only formal alternative to obtaining substitute consent from a guardianship court in the case of adult patients who have not previously been legally incapacitated is the institution of legal incapacitation (full or partial), which enables the appointment of a statutory representative. However, this procedure is time-consuming and is intended for cases of permanent or long-term incapacity to make decisions. Moreover, in the case of socially isolated patients, the initiation of such proceedings may be significantly hindered. Consequently, it does not constitute a practical solution in emergency situations where rapid medical decision-making is required. In clinical practice, this means that the guardianship court becomes the primary, and often the only practical, mechanism for obtaining substitute consent [7–14].

Empirical data indicate that the scale of such judicial proceedings is substantial. In one of the largest analyses conducted to date, Słyk et al. estimated that the number of guardianship court proceedings concerning medical consent nationwide exceeds 5,500 per year [15]. Additional insight is provided by the 2024 Report of the Patient Ombudsman, which documented 603 complaints related to violations of the right to informed consent (0.5% of all complaints received that year); in 61% of these cases, a legal violation was confirmed [16].

Although this legal mechanism is intended to safeguard patient autonomy and ensure procedural oversight, it may also generate practical challenges in clinical settings. Judicial involvement can influence the timeliness of therapeutic decisions, particularly in time-sensitive cases, and may create additional administrative burdens for healthcare institutions. Despite its importance for both patient rights and healthcare delivery, empirical evidence on how substitute consent functions in routine hospital practice remains limited. In particular, little is known about the characteristics of patients requiring judicial authorization, the types of medical procedures involved, approval rates, and the time required to obtain court decisions.

The aim of this study was to systematically analyse cases involving judicial substitute consent for adult patients treated at a tertiary academic medical center in Poland between 2019 and 2024. By examining patient characteristics, clinical context, requested procedures, court decisions, and time to authorization, this study provides empirical insight into the functioning of this legal mechanism in everyday clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and data collection

We conducted a retrospective review of all petitions submitted by a tertiary academic medical center in eastern Poland to the guardianship court requesting judicial authorization for medical procedures involving adult patients between June 2019 and June 2024. All eligible cases within the study period were considered.

A total of 253 case files were reviewed, of which 240 were included in the final analysis. Eleven cases were excluded due to incomplete documentation or ongoing judicial proceedings at the time of analysis, and two were excluded because they involved paediatric patients from the neonatology ward.

The analysis included patient age and sex; clinical conditions leading to incapacity to provide informed consent; the petitioning hospital department; the scope of medical procedures for which court authorization was sought; the duration of judicial proceedings; and the content of the final court decision. Invasive diagnostic procedures in the fields of neurosurgery, otolaryngology, or cardiology such as brain biopsy or coronary angiography were classified as diagnostic procedures that pose an increased risk to the patient. The primary reason for hospital admission was not analysed as an independent variable, as incapacity to provide informed consent was assessed based on the patient’s clinical condition at the time consent was required.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the data. Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Statistical calculations were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee at the Medical University of Lublin (approval no. KB-0024/88/05/2025). Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Duration of judicial proceedings

The analysis of time to court decision was performed for cases in which a court decision was issued (n = 233). Time to decision was measured from the date on which the petition was formally received by the court or, if earlier, from the date on which judicial proceedings were initiated, to the date on which the court decision was issued. Cases in which proceedings were discontinued due to the death of the patient before a decision was issued were excluded from this analysis, as no measurable time to decision was available. The median time to obtain a court decision was 5 days (IQR 4–6 days), with a range from 1 to 112 days, and a mean of 7.0 ± 9.7 days. Overall, 200 court decisions (85.8%) were issued within 2–7 days. The shortest proceedings were concluded within one day (2 cases, 0.9%), whereas the longest proceeding lasted 112 days and represented the only case exceeding three months (1 case, 0.4%). The distribution of time to decision was right-skewed, reflecting a small number of markedly prolonged proceedings. All court proceedings lasting longer than 30 days (n = 6) concerned petitions related to placement in a long-term care facility.

Discussion

This study is one of the most comprehensive analyses of the practice of obtaining substitute consent from a guardianship court for the provision of healthcare services to adult patients in the Polish healthcare system. There is a lack of comparable empirical studies in the medical literature; existing work on this topic has predominantly adopted a theoretical approach.

The analysis showed a clear predominance of elderly patients, with a median age of 73 and more than 75% of patients aged 61 years or older. This result is consistent with demographic and clinical trends observed in recent years, indicating that it is the geriatric population that most often experiences temporary or permanent loss of decision-making capacity during hospitalization [13, 17]. The literature emphasizes that in older people, the ability to give informed consent is often limited not only by severe general condition associated with multimorbidity, but also by dementia, delirium, or acute neurological syndromes [18, 19]. The largest number of petitions to court were initiated by neurology and intensive care units, which is directly related to the clinical profile of patients. Similar trends are described in other studies. Dyer pointed out that in these areas of medicine, there is most often a conflict between the need for immediate action and formal requirements [12]. The significant contribution of internal medicine departments confirms that the problem of substitute consent also affects patients with decompensated chronic diseases and severe general condition caused by multimorbidity [20].

The most common court petitions concerned consent for blood transfusions and blood products, placement of a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG), and tracheostomy. For many years, the medical law literature has highlighted the ambiguity surrounding the classification of blood transfusions as procedures requiring separate consent from the patient or the court, particularly in emergency situations [21]. This issue is especially relevant for individuals who refuse transfusions on religious grounds and who often express their wishes in the form of pro futuro declarations. In such cases, the dilemma between saving a life at risk and respecting freedom of religion poses a challenge for both medical professionals and the courts [22]. In the analyzed material, blood transfusion was included in court petitions as an adjunct to other medical interventions in nearly all cases, rather than as their sole subject, which may reflect both the clinical nature of transfusion as a supportive component of treatment and the legal caution exercised by medical staff in normatively ambiguous situations.

The very high frequency of petitions concerning blood transfusion and its components may result from the fact that, in clinical practice, consent for such interventions is often included as a precautionary element of planned medical procedures, particularly surgical interventions. Many procedures carry a risk of complications in the form of uncontrolled bleeding requiring urgent transfusion; therefore, petitions submitted to the court frequently include consent for blood transfusion not as a separate procedure, but as a potential component of complication management.

Procedures such as PEG placement or tracheostomy are life- and health-saving interventions; however, they entail significant and long-term consequences for patient functioning and quality of life. Bioethical and clinical literature indicates that decisions to perform these procedures in patients unable to provide consent often lie at the boundary between curative treatment and life-sustaining therapy, giving rise to particular ethical dilemmas [23, 24].

In the analyzed material, court approval was granted in 97.1% of cases. This result is consistent with the analysis conducted by Słyk, who showed that the vast majority (79.2%) of proceedings end with the issuance of a permit to provide health care to an adult patient [15]. The difference described results from the different groups of subjects – this study analyzed only cases of adult patients, while Słyk analyzed pediatric cases, where in 5.3% of cases consent for the medical procedure was given by a legal guardian [15]. Słyk notes that the formalization of the court procedure and the fact that the decision is based almost exclusively on medical documentation mean that the risk of refusal of consent is marginal, and the court most often acts as an entity legitimizing the actions of doctors [15].

Such a high rate of approval may be explained by several factors. First, the guardianship court does not possess specialized medical expertise and therefore relies primarily on the medical documentation and the opinion of treating physicians. In the absence of clear contraindications, court decisions are typically guided by the assumption that physicians act in the patient’s best interest. Second, the court operates under significant time constraints, as many of the medical procedures in question require prompt decision-making. This limits the extent to which the court can conduct a more detailed examination of the case, including the appointment of expert witnesses or extensive evidentiary proceedings. Third, the patient’s autonomy is, in principle, safeguarded by a court-appointed legal representative, whose role is to ensure the protection of the patient’s rights and procedural interests during the proceedings. In situations of uncertainty, the court may also seek information from the patient’s relatives or close persons in order to better reconstruct the patient’s presumed will.

The median time for obtaining a court decision, which is 5 days, can be considered relatively short from the perspective of court proceedings. Nevertheless, in clinical practice, especially in emergency situations, even a delay of several days can have a significant impact on the course of treatment and the patient’s prognosis [12]. The literature also points out that excessive legal caution on the part of staff can lead to unjustified delays in healthcare services [12, 20]. However, the median in this study is longer than that indicated by Słyk. In his analysis, 42.5% of cases were considered on the same or the following day [15]. Particular attention should be paid to cases of proceedings lasting more than 30 days, which concern the placement of a patient in a long-term care facility. This result confirms that the greatest procedural difficulties arise in cases of a care and organizational nature, rather than strictly medical ones, which is reflected in analyses of end-of-life decisions and futile treatment [24, 25]. The economic dimension and financial burden on healthcare are also important aspects. One day of a patient’s stay in the hospital ward where the examination was conducted costs PLN 1,500 (approximately 356 euros) [26]. Therefore, any delay in deciding on the patient’s further treatment also represents a significant financial burden for the hospital and, consequently, for the entire system. From an ethical perspective, this raises concerns related to justice, as delays in decision-making may adversely affect particularly vulnerable patients whose autonomy is already compromised due to impaired decision-making capacity.

Taken together, these findings suggest that while judicial oversight is intended to safeguard patient autonomy, in practice it may offer limited additional protection while simultaneously potentially leading to delays in necessary medical interventions.

Compared to solutions in foreign countries, the Polish model of substitute consent by a guardianship court appears to be highly formalized [18, 27]. In some European and Anglo-Saxon countries, declarations of intent pro futuro and the institution of medical proxy are playing an increasingly important role, allowing patients to retain their autonomy despite their loss of decision-making capacity [6, 18, 27]. The lack of comprehensive regulation of these instruments in the Polish legal system encourages frequent involvement of the guardianship court, even in situations where it would be possible to reconstruct the patient’s will on the basis of previous declarations or professed values [6, 27]. Particularly relevant in this context is the provision of the Oviedo Convention (1997), whose Article 9 requires that the patient’s previously expressed decisions regarding the use of a given medical procedure be taken into account [28].

In some legal systems, alternative mechanisms are available, such as the possibility of designating a representative in advance or relying on consent provided by family members, which may reduce the need for judicial involvement and help to mitigate delays in time-sensitive clinical situations.

In clinical practice, previously expressed patient wishes are very rarely present and documented in a way that allows their direct consideration in judicial proceedings. When such statements are available, they most commonly concern refusal of blood transfusion or its components on religious grounds, particularly among Jehovah’s Witnesses. In such cases, if the declaration does not raise doubts as to its authenticity, it may influence clinical decision-making and, in some instances, reduce the need to seek judicial authorization for blood transfusion or its components. Nevertheless, the absence of clear legal regulations and standardized procedures significantly limits the systematic incorporation of such prior wishes into the decision-making process [6, 27]. In practice, this means that court decisions are based primarily on medical documentation and the assessment of the necessity of the intervention.

In the cases analyzed in this study, patients were represented in the proceedings by a court-appointed legal representative, which constitutes a standard element of such proceedings. The role of the representative is to protect the patient’s interests. Where previously expressed wishes exist, it is within the representative’s role to seek their disclosure and consideration in the course of the proceedings. In practice, however, such statements are not commonly used in Poland, which further limits the possibility of their meaningful incorporation into decision-making.

This points to the need to design solutions that minimize the substitution of the patient’s decisions by public institutions [27].

Limitations

The limitations of the study include its retrospective nature and the analysis of data from a single clinical centre, which may limit the generalizability of the results. The clinical effects of delays resulting from court proceedings were also not analyzed. However, the size of the sample and the detail of the documentation allow the results to be considered a significant contribution to the debate on the functioning of substitute consent in Poland.

Conclusions

Judicial substitute consent remains an important mechanism enabling the provision of medical treatment to adult patients who temporarily lack decision-making capacity. The findings of this study indicate that such proceedings most frequently concern elderly patients treated in neurology, intensive care, and internal medicine departments. Although court authorisation is granted in the vast majority of cases, the time required to obtain judicial decisions may affect the timeliness of medical interventions and generate organisational and financial burdens for healthcare institutions. These findings highlight the need for further reflection on the functioning of the substitute consent system in Poland. Future research and administrative initiatives should focus on developing and evaluating practical legal instruments, such as advance directives and healthcare proxies, as well as on assessing their real-world applicability in clinical settings. In particular, further studies should investigate whether the patterns observed in this study are consistent at the national level and whether they vary across different regions or judicial jurisdictions, especially in light of partially divergent findings reported in studies based on court records. If such patterns are confirmed, consideration should be given to developing alternative legal mechanisms that could reduce the burden on guardianship courts, including instruments enabling patients to express their will in advance or to designate a representative pro futuro. Additionally, survey-based studies involving both physicians and judges could provide valuable insight into the practical challenges of the current system and inform potential reforms. Ongoing research by the authors includes physician surveys and expanded analysis of hospital documentation across different judicial jurisdictions.
