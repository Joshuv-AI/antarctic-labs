Umbilical vein recanalization identified on contrast-enhanced ultrasound in a patient with hepatocellular carcinoma during TACE assessment
Authors: Christian KuonYeng-Escalante, Ji-Bin Liu, Junmei Xu, Stephan Leung, Andrej Lyshchik, John R. Eisenbrey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This case report demonstrates the potential use of contrast-enhanced ultrasound as a support tool for vascular abnormality findings previously seen on cross-sectional imaging. We describe a 62-year-old woman with history of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease who presented abdominal pain. Ultrasound demonstrated cirrhosis and indeterminate liver lesions. With these findings, contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging was performed and 2 LI-RADS 5 lesions < 3 cm and vascular changes consistent with portal hypertension were observed. Before transplant, the patient underwent transarterial-chemoembolization for bridging. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound was used to assess tumor vascularity pre- and post-TACE. During the post-TACE exam, the sonographer showed an immediate lack of intra-tumoral enhancement, but also observed a hyper-enhanced vessel, characteristic of umbilical vein recanalization previously seen on cross-sectional imaging. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound, used for characterization of focal liver lesions, can aid in the visualization of vascular changes during portal-hypertension due to its ability to visualize perfusion in real-time.

Introduction

Umbilical vein recanalization (UVR) is a finding that can be seen in patients with liver cirrhosis or with long-standing portal hypertension [1]. Patients with cirrhosis have structural abnormalities leading to distortion of the angioarchitecture of the liver [2]. When there is a change in the angioarchitecture, portal pressure increases, leading to portal hypertension [2]. In patients with portal hypertension, collateral blood flow helps reduce the pressure in portal circulation. One of the mechanisms seen is the recanalization of the umbilical vein [3]. Usually, the umbilical vein closes within the first week after birth, and the remnant is known as the ligamentum teres, which can reopen under extreme pressure [3].

Ultrasound is widely used for screening and surveillance of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients [4]. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) can be used for characterizing focal liver lesions and well as for monitoring response to locoregional therapy [[5], [6], [7]]. Ultrasound contrast agents consist of gas bubbles stabilized by phospholipids, and are injected intravenously [8]. We present a case of a UVR found on cross-sectional image and confirmed with CEUS during the evaluation of HCC in a patient being treated with transarterial chemoembolization (TACE).

Case presentation

A 62-year-old woman with history of biopsy-proven metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) initially presented with burning abdominal pain localized in the right upper quadrant that subsided within 30 minutes. Physical examination was performed; bowel sounds were normal with no abdominal distension. and soft on palpation with no tenderness or rebound without organomegaly.

With no relevant findings on physical examination abdominal ultrasound was performed, and liver cirrhosis was observed with indeterminate liver lesions. With these findings, contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (CE-MRI) was performed, and 2 adjacent lesions were observed that demonstrate arterial phase enhancement and washout in segment 4B/1, classified as LI-RADS 5. Besides these, 2 more lesions with arterial enhancement but not clear washout in segment 8 and 4B/5 were observed, classified as LI-RADS 4. During multidisciplinary tumor board review, the team believed the LI-RADS 4 lesions were perfusions anomalies rather than true masses. Collateralization of multiple vessels and a recanalized umbilical vein were also observed. Chest x-ray showed no focal lesions, ruling out metastasis to the lungs.

Laboratory tests were performed to rule out other causes of liver damage (Table 1). Autoimmune and infectious cases of cirrhosis were ruled out. Liver function test, renal function tests and electrolyte values were performed with no alterations observed. Based on these findings, the patient fulfilled the MILAN criteria for liver transplant (up to 3 tumors measuring <3 cm, no metastasis and no major vessel involvement). Based on clinical and laboratory parameters, the patient was classified as Child-Pugh A. The patient underwent locoregional therapy as a bridge to transplant. Transarterial radioembolization was suggested but aborted due to radiotracer accumulation in the duodenum during treatment planning. Due to this finding, conventional TACE (cTACE) therapy was indicated, using a lipidiol-based protocol. Before performing cTACE, the patient underwent CEUS imaging for the characterization of the tumor and its vascularity. For imaging acquisition, a Logiq E10 scanner (GE-Healthcare) was used. Baseline B-mode measurements and sweeps were acquired. CEUS was performed on the largest LI-RADS 5 lesion undergoing TACE, which measured 2.8 cm x 1.9 cm on CE-MRI, and was located between segment 4B and 1. The patient was scanned with a C1-6 transducer. A mechanical index (MI) of 0.8 was used to minimize microbubble destruction during imaging. The imaging was performed using dual B-mode and contrast imaging mode. During the scanning, the patient received a bolus injection of 2.0 mL of sulfur hexafluoride lipid-type A microspheres (Lumason, Bracco) as contrast agent, followed by 10 mL of saline flush. 2D CEUS images of the tumor were obtained during breath holding until complete tumor enhancement was achieved followed by imaging sweeps through the tumor. The time for enhancement phases during the scanning were ∼10 seconds after injection until ∼45 seconds for arterial phase, ∼45 seconds after injection to ∼2 minutes for portal phase and ∼2 minutes after injection to ∼5 minutes for late phase.

During the procedure, the sonographer observed a hyper-enhanced vessel that goes from the surface of the liver towards the abdominal wall (Fig. 1). The vessel size was approximately 9 mm in caliber and followed a characteristic course from the left portal vein along the ligament teres to the anterior abdominal wall. Although Doppler was not performed, real-time CEUS demonstrated directional microbubble movement away from the liver, consistent with hepatofugal flow, supporting portal-system collateralization. This enhancement was distinguished from residual tumor based on morphology, distribution, and dynamics. It appeared as a continuous, tubular structure along the ligamentum teres extending toward the anterior abdominal wall, consistent with vascular filling of the recanalized umbilical vein, rather than focal or nodular intratumoral enhancement expected for viable tumor. This observation was characteristic of the UVR previously observed on the CE-MRI (Fig. 2). On review of the MRI images it was noticed that besides the UVR, various collateral vessels were present and there was also a dilation of the splenic vein (Fig. 3) consisting with portal hypertension. CEUS was repeated 30 mins post TACE following the same parameters and increasing the contrast agent dose at 2.2 ml. After bridging therapy with cTACE, the patient went under liver transplant evaluation (LTE). After CE-MRI follow-up evaluation, the patient remained stable with no complications from portal hypertension, and the tumor board paused the LTE until further interdisciplinary evaluation.

Discussion

Ultrasound imaging is well known as a diagnostic tool for liver pathologies. CEUS is well validated for the characterization of liver lesions as well as evaluation of TACE treatment response [9]. In comparison with other studies (such as CE-CT/MRI), CEUS can be significantly less expensive, it does not have risk of radiation and can be performed at the bedside. CEUS usage continues to expand, due to its safety and capability to provide a reliable diagnosis [6,10].

HCC is a complication of liver cirrhosis. Besides HCC, patients with liver cirrhosis that are not treated in time are susceptible to other complications. The most common complication is portal hypertension, which can lead to esophageal varices (and posterior rupture), splenic dilation, and UVR, among others pressure-compensation mechanisms.

UVR has been described previously as a finding in patients with decompensated cirrhosis [11,12]. To our knowledge UVR is rarely described on CEUS, and its incidental finding during TACE monitoring is highly novel. This case report identifies the importance of CEUS alongside cross-sectional imaging in the diagnosis of complications and clinical findings in patients with liver disease.

Conclusion

During evaluation, the patient underwent a variety of imaging diagnostic tests (Ultrasound → CE-MRI → pre-TACE CEUS → post-TACE CEUS → 1 month CE-MRI). UVR, a shunting mechanism to decrease portal vein pressure in patients with portal hypertension, can be well demonstrated not only in cross-sectional images but also with CEUS, as demonstrated with this patient. It is essential that the sonographer or physician possess a comprehensive understanding and expertise in liver imaging to minimize errors and detect findings that are not readily visible.
