Sexual violence in dating relationships among high school adolescents: a
school-based study

Isaura Barros Alves Pinto, Rita Mafalda Costa Francisco, Franciele Marabotti Costa Leite

Abstract

Objective: to analyze the prevalence of sexual violence in dating relationships among high
school adolescents and its association with socioeconomic and behavioral
characteristics and experiences of violence. Method: a cross-sectional, analytical study was conducted among 1,238 adolescents aged 14
to 19 years enrolled in public and private schools who were in romantic
relationships at the time of the study. Sampling was by clusters and
stratification. Results: of the 1,238 adolescents in romantic relationships, 7.8% (95% CI: 6.4-9.4)
reported having experienced sexual violence in dating. The prevalence was 9.3%
among girls and 5.1% among boys. There was a higher likelihood of sexual violence
in dating relationships among non-heterosexual adolescents, those who had ever had
sexual intercourse, those who used drugs, and those who had experienced bullying
and cyberbullying (p<0.05). Conclusion: it is essential that these findings contribute to the strengthening of educational
campaigns in schools, addressing not only abusive behaviors but also the
importance of consent, mutual respect, and the construction of secure emotional
bonds among adolescents.
(1) Prevalence of 7.8% of sexual violence in adolescent dating
relationships. 
(2) Findings reinforce the urgency of addressing violence in youth
relationships. 
(3) Sexual victimization affects adolescents in their first romantic
relationships. 
(4) Sexual violence in dating is associated with drugs, bullying,
cyberbullying, ever having sexual intercourse, and non-heterosexuals. 
(5) Partnerships with health, school, and family promote listening and
abuse prevention.

Introduction

Adolescence marks a period of transition in which interpersonal relationships,
especially friendships and romantic relationships, take center stage, while family ties
are reconfigured. At this stage, the interaction of individual and social factors can favor the
internalization of norms that normalize sexual violence
-
.

Dating violence (DV), understood as any form of physical, emotional, or sexual
aggression by an intimate partner, often manifests itself as a mechanism of control and domination. Among its
expressions, sexual violence stands out for involving coercion, harassment, or the
imposition of non-consensual sexual acts, constituting a serious violation of human
rights. Studies indicate that this violence can occur in non-marital relationships,
regardless of sexual orientation.

Epidemiological data illustrate the magnitude of the problem: a survey of Brazilian
adolescents found that 38% reported experiences of sexual violence in dating relationships,
including pressure to engage in intimate activities and non-consensual sharing of
images. These experiences are correlated with consequences such as depression,
post-traumatic stress, and substance use. Furthermore, the normalization of these aggressions, often rooted in gender
stereotypes, makes identification and reporting difficult.

Given this scenario, this study aims to analyze the prevalence of sexual violence in
dating among high school adolescents and its association with socioeconomic and
behavioral characteristics and experiences of violence.

Method

Study design

This is a cross-sectional, analytical study conducted in public and private schools
in municipalities within the Greater Vitória Metropolitan Area (RMGV) in the state of
Espírito Santo.

Location

The Greater Vitória Metropolitan Area (RMGV, in Portuguese) comprises the
municipalities of Cariacica, Fundão, Guarapari, Serra, Viana, Vila Velha, and
Vitória. This region is home to approximately 1,880,828 inhabitants, spread over an
area of 2,331 km2.

Period

Data collection took place between March and December 2023.

Population

The target population of the study was high school adolescents aged 14 to 19 years,
regularly enrolled in public or private schools in the RMGV who participated after
their guardians accepted the Free and Informed Consent Form (FICF) and the
adolescents signed the Informed Assent Form (IAF), after being informed about the
objectives of the research and its possible risks and benefits.

Sample definition

The sampling used a 95% confidence level and a maximum error of 5%. Across strata of
municipalities, the maximum error was 10%, with a minimum sample size of 4,416
students. For this study, only adolescents who were in some type of relationship at
the time of the interview were selected, totaling a sample of 1,238 adolescents.

Before the data collection stage, the selected schools were invited by email to
participate in a seminar presenting the research and subsequently contacted by
email/telephone to schedule data collection. It is important to note that, preceding
the fieldwork, the team was recruited and trained, and a pilot test was conducted in
February 2023. Data collection took place from March to December 2023, using tablets,
with adolescents self-responding to a questionnaire in the REDCap (Research
Electronic Data Capture) system.

Study variables

The outcome in the present study was sexual violence in dating relationships, which
was dichotomized (yes/no). The variable-sexual violence in dating relationships-was
considered affirmative if the adolescent had experienced at least one of the
following situations: “your partner caressed you without your consent and against
your will, forced you to have sex when you did not want to, prevented you from using
condoms, induced you to have an abortion, or any other issue that infringed on your
sexual and reproductive rights”.

As for the independent variables, the following sociodemographic variables were
analyzed: gender (female/male); age group (≤15 years/≥16 years); cisgender (yes/no);
heterosexual (yes/no); race/color (white/black/brown); economic classification
measured by the Economic Classification Criteria of the Brazilian Association of
Research Companies (ABEP), which classifies into class A/B/C/D/E; has work/employment
(no/yes); and religion (none/Catholic/Evangelical/others). The following behavioral
variables were also analyzed: sexual intercourse at some point in life (no/yes);
current cigarette use (no/yes); current drug use (no/yes) and current alcohol use
(no/yes), as well as variables related to experiences of violence: ever experienced
cyberbullying (no/yes); ever engaged in cyberbullying (no/yes); ever experienced
bullying (no/yes); ever engaged in bullying (no/yes).

Data processing and analysis

For descriptive statistical analyses, absolute and relative frequencies were
calculated. In the bivariate analysis, Pearson’s chi-square test (χ2) was used as
assumed, with respective 95% confidence intervals. For crude multivariate analysis
and odds ratio adjustment using logistic regression to assess associations between
independent variables and dating violence, descriptive and bivariate analysis were
initially performed with calculation of the crude odds ratio (OR). Variables with p
≤0.20 were included in the multivariate model adjusted for confounding factors and
retained at p ≤ 0.05 ().

Ethical considerations

In accordance with ethical considerations, the study was submitted to the Ethics and
Research Committee of the Federal University of Espírito Santo and was carried out
after approval and registration under opinion 5,900,370.

Discussion

The overall prevalence of sexual violence in dating relationships among high school
adolescents found in this study was 7.8% (95% CI: 6.4-9.4), indicating that a
considerable proportion of adolescents experience sexual violence in their romantic
relationships, which is a worrying reality considering the impacts of sexual violence at
this stage of development.

In the United States, a survey conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and
Prevention (CDC) found that approximately 7.5% of adolescents (95% CI: 6.4-8.7) had
experienced sexual violence in dating relationships. In Brazil, data from the National School Health Survey (PeNSE, in Portuguese)
indicate that approximately 4% of adolescents reported having been forced to have sex,
with their boyfriend or girlfriend or ex-boyfriend or ex-girlfriend being the main
perpetrator.

For girls, the prevalence of sexual violence in dating relationships was 9.3% (95% CI:
7.4-11.5) and 5.1% (95% CI: 3.4-7.6) for boys. A study on sexual violence among
schoolchildren in Brazil, using data from the 2019 national school health survey, showed
a prevalence of sexual violence in dating relationships of 8.8% (95% CI: 8.4-9.3) among
females and 3.7% (95% CI: 3.3-4.0) among males. In the United States, the CDC identified a significant gender disparity in
dating violence, with 11.3% of girls compared to 3.5% of boys, indicating that female
adolescents are three times more at risk. It is a fact that girls are more vulnerable to this type of violence due to
factors such as gender inequality, socialization based on female submission, and
cultural norms that tolerate abusive behavior by romantic partners.

This study indicated that non-heterosexual adolescents were approximately twice as
likely to experience sexual violence in dating relationships (ORa: 2.05; 95% CI:
1.32-3.16). Similarly, data from the 2021 Youth Risk Behavior Survey, conducted in the
United States, show that adolescents who identify as LGB reported experiences of sexual
violence in dating relationships at a higher rate than their heterosexual peers. Specifically, 17% of LGBTQ+ adolescents reported having been forced to have
sex, compared to 6% of heterosexual adolescents. This evidence reveals that belonging to sexual minorities represents an
additional risk factor for dating violence, especially sexual violence. LGBTQIA+
adolescents are more exposed to various forms of violence and vulnerability due to
structural discrimination, social stigma, and family rejection, which contributes to the
invisibility and underreporting of these cases.

This violence, which is often normalized or overlooked, occurs not only in
relationships, but also in institutions such as schools and health services, which are
not always prepared to welcome and protect these populations. In addition, the lack of recognition of gender identity and the invalidation of
sexual orientation by families intensify psychological distress, increasing the risk of
depression, anxiety, substance use, and suicidal ideation.

Furthermore, the results of this study indicate that adolescents who have already begun
their sexual lives are more likely to suffer sexual violence in the context of dating
(ORa: 1.91; 95% CI: 1.15-3.16). This finding is consistent with recent literature, which
associates early or unplanned sexual initiation with a series of social and behavioral
vulnerabilities that may favor situations of abuse. According to the study, adolescents
who began their sexual lives at age 13 or younger had a higher prevalence of risky
sexual behaviors, including less condom use and greater exposure to sexual violence.

Current drug use was also associated with a 2.19-fold increase in the likelihood of
suffering sexual abuse by a partner (95% CI: 1.31-3.66). A literature review highlights
that dating violence among adolescents is a relevant public health issue, influenced by
factors such as psychoactive substance use, which can contribute to increased aggressive
behavior in relationships among this age group. Additionally, a study conducted in Nicaragua with 1,799 adolescents revealed
that the prevalence of dating violence was associated with the use of psychoactive
substances such as alcohol, traditional cigarettes, marijuana, electronic cigarettes,
and external behaviors such as aggression, disobedience, antisocial conduct, and other
disruptive behaviors. The results indicate that adolescents who consume psychoactive
substances are more likely to engage in abusive relationships.

It has been observed that experiencing bullying is associated with greater vulnerability
among adolescents to experiences of violence and interpersonal relationships marked by
coercion and abuse, indicating the possibility of victimization being transferred
between different social contexts. Population-based evidence shows that adolescents
exposed to bullying are more prone to risky behaviors and unhealthy interpersonal
relationships, increasing their susceptibility to abusive experiences throughout their
development
-
. Similarly, a study identified the relationship between bullying and violence
among adolescent couples, highlighting that those who have experienced aggression or
humiliation in the school environment are more likely to tolerate abusive behavior in
their relationships.

The limitations of this study include its cross-sectional design, which captures a
single point in time, precluding robust causal analyses and leaving it susceptible to
recall bias in the data collection instruments. However, the study makes a substantial
contribution by pioneering the mapping of patterns of dating violence among young people
in a major Brazilian city, providing representative data for this specific
population.

Conclusion

The prevalence of 7.8% of sexual violence in dating relationships identified among the
adolescents participating in this study highlights the relevance and urgency of
addressing this specific type of violence in youth romantic relationships. This data
reinforces that sexual victimization is already present in early romantic
experiences.

The present study demonstrated that sexual violence in adolescent dating relationships
is significantly associated with factors such as current use of psychoactive substances,
non-heterosexual adolescents, those who have had sexual intercourse at some point in
their lives, and those who have suffered bullying and cyberbullying. These findings
indicate that sexual victimization in the context of romantic relationships among
adolescents occurs concurrently with other experiences of exposure to violence and risk
behaviors, highlighting the importance of prevention strategies that consider the
complexity of these associations and the coexistence of different forms of vulnerability
during adolescence.

Such evidence is particularly relevant for health, education, and social work
professionals, as well as policymakers, as it identifies groups and contexts that
warrant greater attention in prevention and protection efforts for adolescent romantic
relationships. Recognition of these associated factors can inform the development of
intersectoral strategies that promote safer, more equitable, and respectful
relationships among adolescents.

Therefore, the results of this study reiterate the importance of developing and
implementing policies and programs to prevent sexual violence in dating relationships,
with an emphasis on reducing identified associated factors and promoting healthy
emotional relationships among adolescents.
