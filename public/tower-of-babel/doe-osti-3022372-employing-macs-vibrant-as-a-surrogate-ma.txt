 ABSTRACT
 Advanced nuclear reactors are a key part of the future of nuclear energy both
in the United States and globally. They offer unique benefits for various energy-
demanding applications, including use in remote locations, compact size,
modular manufacturing, remote monitoring, low and/or variable power rating
operation, and reliance on novel technologies to enhance operational safety.
 To achieve economic feasibility, advanced reactors must significantly reduce
their workforces in comparison with the current fleet. Achieving this reduction
will occur through reducing staff workloads using technology to achieve
autonomous or semi-autonomous operations, demonstrated by comprehensive
testing and validation activities. These operations will require both software and
hardware platforms during the design and testing phases. While simulations are
useful during the design phase, their performance can significantly deviate during
actual deployment on hardware.
 This report presents the outcomes of a collaborative technical initiative
between the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Microreactor Program (MRP)
and Advanced Sensors and Instrumentation (ASI) Program. The collaboration
utilized the Microreactor Automated Control System (MACS) hardware platform
to bridge the gap between theoretical reactor design and actual startup and
control operations. Two key use cases were investigated: facilitating the startup
testing period and demonstrating supervisory control.
 The first use case details the key Microreactor Applications Research
Validation and Evaluation (MARVEL) reactor startup physics testing activities
conducted using the MACS platform. These activities included drum worth
measurements, shutdown margin assessment, temperature feedback analysis, and
scram time evaluation, as well as unique testing that would apply to the
MARVEL reactor to demonstrate the testing methodologies in a low-risk
environment. The MACS platform, serving as a surrogate representation of the
MARVEL reactor, proved instrumental in performing these tests. The exercise
revealed aspects that led to optimized processes, refined hardware design, and
enhanced base software capabilities. By maturing methods and technologies in
this manner, the initiative promises to reduce wasted time in the actual on-site
reactor deployment effort, thereby saving significant time and resources.
 The second use case focuses on the development and implementation of
supervisory control methods aimed at managing core tilt, which can result from
asymmetrical operations or manufacturing imperfections in fuel rods or reactivity
control devices. A key objective was to assess and compare the use of artificial
intelligence (AI) for supervisory control. The effort aimed to define the role of
supervisory control to enhance performance without risking control instability.
This effort explored three distinct approaches: rules-based (RB) methods,
optimization techniques, and reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms. Each
approach was evaluated for its ease of implementation, its usability, and its
effectiveness in responding to asymmetries in neutron flux. Comparative analysis
of these approaches provided valuable insights into their applicability and
effectiveness, offering a robust framework for advanced reactor operations.
 Together, these two use cases highlight the potential of hardware test beds to
help streamline the design, operation, and control of advanced nuclear reactors.

This collaborative effort underscores the importance of continued innovation and
experimentation in achieving the next generation of safe, reliable, and
economically viable nuclear energy solutions.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Advanced nuclear reactors are a key part of the future of nuclear energy both in the United States and
globally. They offer unique benefits for various energy-demanding applications, including use in remote
locations, compact size, modular manufacturing, remote monitoring, low and/or variable power rating
operation, and reliance on novel technologies to enhance operational safety.
 To achieve economic feasibility, advanced reactors must deviate from the workforce scale of the
current fleet. A typical light-water reactor requires 500–1,000 staff for operation. If an advanced reactor
produces 1/20th the energy of a light-water reactor, it should ideally employ only 25–50 staff to sustain
the energy-to-staff economic ratio. Achieving this reduction is impractical with manual operations, and
necessitates that advanced reactors operate either autonomously or semi-autonomously. This includes
comprehensive online monitoring of the reactor and the implementation of robust control methods to
enable responsive adjustments to both internal conditions and external demands.
 In a prior effort [1], a comprehensive framework was developed to achieve robust control of reactors.
This framework provides a detailed view of the elements within a typical control loop. A plant is
represented by physical equipment and passive control means, utilizing high- and low-fidelity digital twin
(DT) models for condition monitoring and rapid decision-making. The operational state, along with
insights from the DT and external requirements, is fed into a supervisory control layer that optimizes
decisions and relays them to logical and high-performance control layers. The supervisory control does
not directly interact with the plant but influences lower-level deterministic controllers. This separation
allows for flexibility in enhancing the capabilities of supervisory controls, without the regulatory
implications of having intelligent methods—possibly employing artificial intelligence (AI)—directly
controlling the plant.
 To develop and evaluate advanced control methods, both software and hardware platforms are
essential. While simulations are useful for designing control systems, their performance can significantly
deviate during actual deployment, as observed in a prior effort [2]. This discrepancy led the U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) Microreactor Program (MRP) and Advanced Sensors and Instrumentation
(ASI) Program to develop the Microreactor Automated Control System (MACS) [3] hardware platform
(detailed in Section 2) and the Control and Optimization Modular Modeling Application for Nuclear
Deployment (COMMAND) software [4]. These platforms were integrated in a previous initiative,
enabling MACS to function as a physical twin for the Microreactor Applications Research Validation and
Evaluation (MARVEL) microreactor and facilitating the development and evaluation of various control
methods [5]. Evaluations using this hardware setup began in 2023 and have been ongoing ever since.
 In this effort, the objectives are to (1) enhance the MACS platform to more accurately replicate
control functions performed by advanced nuclear reactors, (2) improve the COMMAND platform to
incorporate supervisory control functions, and (3) utilize the enhanced MACS to evaluate various levels
of supervisory control performance.
 The primary focus of the enhanced MACS capability is to demonstrate the use of MACS in
performing realistic measurement methods for a representative reactor control system. Section 3 discusses
key startup measurements such as drum-worth, shutdown margin, temperature feedback, and scram times,
as well as a set of reactor-specific processes that includes position calibration and reactivity adjustments
using hard-stops. In addition, this section also provides a detailed description of the supporting
mechanical, electrical, and software characteristics. Once these reactivity curves and other reactor
characteristics are generated, they can be incorporated into the COMMAND platform to emulate the
reactor’s response to the control drums, thus enabling MACS to perform as a physical twin of an

 1

advanced reactor. Specifically, this ensures that MACS reacts in a semi-realistic manner to drum
movements, which is crucial for evaluating control methods, including supervisory control.
 Three levels of supervisory control are explored in this effort: rules-based (RB) control, which is
based on pre-set rules derived from system and plant knowledge, along with some trial and error;
optimization-based supervisory control, which relies on a typical optimizer with desired objective
functions; and AI-based supervisory control, which utilizes reinforcement learning (RL). These control
methods are explained in detail in Section 4, including via a use case demonstrating and comparing the
various degrees of supervisory control. Section 5 presents the common conclusions drawn from this
effort.

 2. MACS
 This joint effort uses the MACS/Visual Benign Reactor as Analog for Nuclear Testing (ViBRANT)
platform, which was developed by the DOE MRP [3]. While MACS/ViBRANT was not designed to
mirror a specific microreactor, its initial design was developed in concert with Idaho National Laboratory
(INL)’s Microreactor Applications Research Validation and Evaluation (MARVEL) [6] microreactor,
which is a nominally 85 kW thermal research reactor that utilizes 36 fuel rods containing
uranium-zirconium-hydride (UZrH) fuel [7,8]. The core features metallic beryllium, beryllium oxide
(BeO), and graphite neutron reflectors and is controlled via four control drums containing boron carbide
neutron absorbers. The reactor uses a sodium-potassium alloy (NaK) as its coolant.
 MACS/ViBRANT was designed with a mechanical-electrical configuration that matches the primary
components of a nuclear reactor, along with flexible software to leverage this configuration. It is
composed of an actuation cell, surrogate-reactor cell, and supporting A-frame structure, as shown in
Figure 1. The cells have a MARVEL-like composition; the reactor cell includes four drums surrounding a
core, and the connected actuation cell provides the corresponding physical actuators and associated
control cabinet. (Note: a central insurance absorber, which is a feature of MARVEL, is not currently
included in MACS).
 Each actuator is enhanced with several key components, including a motor, resolver, gearhead,
electromagnetic clutch, limit switches for the full in/out position enforcement, hard stop, second position
indicator for absolute position indication, and torsional spring-damper combination that drives the scram
response. However, the MACS actuators have fewer design constraints than in the MARVEL actuator
design, in that they are faster thanks to a 100:1 (as opposed to 10,000:1) gearhead combination and a
slightly different potentiometer design. A detailed account of these actuators is included in the Gateway
for Accelerated Innovation in Nuclear (GAIN) webinar [9], and their development can be observed in
both MACS/ViBRANT milestone reports [10,11] and in the MARVEL reactivity control system
milestone report [12].
 The reactor cell employs ViBRANT technology, which applies light physics—with some digital
augmentation—as a scaled version of the reactor physics. The ViBRANT-barrel version, which was
applied in this study, uses 18 strips to represent the outer layer of a 3-layer hexagonal core. With 35 light-
emitting diodes (LEDs) per strip (630 total LEDs), the system is well enabled to represent axial profiles as
well as lateral core tilt. Fifteen photodiodes effectively measure the responses. Arduino microcontrollers
with on-board programs read from and write to the LEDs and photodiodes, respectively. A more in-depth
discussion of the ViBRANT barrel was presented at the American Nuclear Society (ANS) Pacific Basin
Nuclear Conference [13].
 DTs and hardware-in-the-loop methods can be used to accelerate development of the procedures, as
well as the algorithms necessary to perform them, but there are limitations. DTs are limited by the
accuracy and approximations of their underlying models, and hardware-in-the-loop systems investigate
only the limited aspects of the full system for which the hardware-in-the-loop system was designed. For
example, the MARVEL program’s primary-coolant apparatus hardware-in-loop test specifically focuses
on MARVEL’s primary coolant system [14].
 This study employed the MACS/ViBRANT platform as a compromise between these two methods.
MACS serves as a hardware-in-the-loop platform for testing the hardware components of a reactor control
system, while ViBRANT converts a reactor core physics DT from the simulation to the hardware realm
via light and photodiodes. The result is a platform that enables an entire control system and associated
operational and tuning processes to develop and mature in parallel with the physical reactor build. Using
this surrogate-reactor tool allows researchers to gain appreciation for the physical hardware and the
complications components bring. Throughout this report, the nuances of working with integrated systems
will become apparent, and these issues will be highlighted as they are encountered.

 3

 Although well enabled, the system configuration employed at the time of this study manifested a few
bottlenecks. One is that the LED intensity was limited to a 0%–50% range, as dictated by the employed
power supply, and had resolution increments of 1%. It should be noted that various means to modify the
configuration and/or on-board software program are available to improve this range and resolution.
 Another bottleneck was the serial communication between the Arduino boards, which created a 0.5-
second limit in the timing resolution. This did not prevent the tests from being effectively conducted, but
actions such as optimizing the program delays for serial-communication commands may ease this
bottleneck.
 As documented in the milestone report “Demonstrate actuation of control system using Microreactor
Automated Control System (MACS) [10],” MACS software architecture is sufficiently dynamic to
communicate with a diverse set of users. The system uses Google’s open-source remote procedure call
(gRPC) architecture, written in LabVIEW, for communication control. The protocol allows more
communication uniformity from clients making calls to the system, with clients ranging from local
computer programs all the way to the high-performance computing center. For MACS, creating
subroutines for each of the different hardware points was required to provide translation capabilities.
However, all modules are now accessible through a single unified interface.

 3. USE CASE 1: REACTOR STARTUP PHYSICS TESTING
 For MARVEL to achieve functional operation, it is imperative to characterize several aspects of the
physical system that then allow one to predict the system’s actual behavior and to develop corresponding
control methods. These characterizations are initially done during zero-power physics testing and are
repeated, as necessary, throughout the reactor’s lifetime.
 This testing is well known and outlined in such standards as ANSI/ANS-19.13, “Initial Fuel Loading
and Startup Physics Tests for First-of-a-Kind Advanced Reactors” [16]. However, as with most standards,
the concepts cannot cover the nuance embedded in all nuclear reactors, especially those that employ
cutting-edge technologies. MARVEL is no exception, and the practices executed in this report provide an
example of general methods being refined and applied to a unique system.
 3.1. Software Overview
 A new software project was created to perform the reactor-startup tests. The project’s main function
was to provide physics and information mapping. The software program is called the Baseline code so as
to distinguish it from other codes that can link to MACS by means of gRPC. Other codes may fully
replace the Baseline code and all its functionality, or employ parts of it as appropriate. Future applications
may benefit from a blackbox version of the Baseline code. The LabVIEW Baseline code performed all
physics calculations directly, rather than offloading them to other applications such as Reactor Excursion
and Leak Analysis Program (RELAP5).
 The relationships between the software blocks (blue) and hardware blocks (yellow) of MACS are
presented in Figure 2. The dashed-block outlines represent optional components, and the green power-
profile block represents the desired power-level over time. The software’s human-machine interface
(HMI) supporting this architecture was based on a prototype MARVEL simulator (Figure 3).

3.1.1.1. Drum Position Calibration
 With user inputs defined, the drums are commanded to move to the desired starting positions. The
defined starting temperature and power initialize the code’s simulator portions. The starting core power
activates the lights to a specified intensity, corresponding to the reactor power value. The photodiode
outputs are averaged to create a starting baseline reactor power measurement, then calibrated according to
the starting core power.
 In this study, the potentiometers initially malfunctioned; thus, motor resolvers were required for
position information. However, the resolver positions are absolute to the motor but not necessarily to the
drum, as a deactivated electromagnetic clutch isolates the two systems. This allows the motor to move
independently of the drum, or the drum to be driven (most often by the shutdown torsion spring)
independently of the motor and its associated resolver. This could result in a resolver position
measurement that does not accurately represent the position of the drum itself.
 Nevertheless, for typical operation, it is important that both the resolver and potentiometer initially be
set to have the same value at the same reference. This is known as the zeroth position, and is the drum’s
shutdown position—where the poison is directed exactly at the core, and the shutdown cam is against its
hard stop. This is the most common position reference when discussing nuclear concepts.
 For the resolver, the zeroth position is achieved by executing the homing process. This position is
defined as the activation of the full IN limit switch and is indicated by the HMI’s green full IN LED. The
method employed for this study initially moves the motor away from the full IN limit switch, and then
approaches it until the HMI’s green full IN LED is active. Next, that position is set as the zeroth position.
However, a degree of inconsistency is potentially seen in this method, as the limit switch may not activate
the same way every time, due to such influences as wear or thermal effects. Thus, in future tests or on the
MARVEL reactor itself, a more reliable method would be to release the clutch, allowing the spring to
drive the shutdown cam against the hard stop; reactivate the clutch; and define that position as the zeroth
position.

 When the resolver is homed, the read potentiometer-voltage value is defined as the zeroth position.
Upon first deployment or as confirmation, a calibration process is employed to relate the angle to position
throughout the drum’s range of motion (see Section 3.2.5).
3.1.1.2. Drum Movement Software Element
 The Baseline code provided the logic that dictates only one drum be activated at a time, mimicking
the constraint of MARVEL that is enforced through relay interlocks. Furthermore, the HMI provides user
feedback like a switch indicator that changes based on the active drum to show a green line around the
activated drum in the picture, as shown in Figure 3. The action to change the active drum is triggered by
clicking on the target control on the ends of the switch indicator, or by clicking on the picture of the drum
one wishes to select. In the code, when a drum is selected all the other drives are disabled. In the
MARVEL reactor is a relay circuit that can only physically route power to one drum at a time, making it
the only one that can physically move. The Baseline code imposed these limitations.
 One nuance of drum control when comparing the native operation of the motors against what is
desired for human performance is position command. Operators like to use toggle switches to move the
reactor power up and down. It is sometime misunderstood that the drum position dictates the power level;
however, this is untrue because power level is relative and is dictated by initial conditions, prior history,
and the net reactivity of the core. Thus, it is beneficial for operators to “adjust” the rods instead of moving
them to “fixed” positions. However, the motor controllers operate based on the position being
commanded. The software’s toggle switch provides the toggling feel while enforcing a maximum speed
requirement to limit reactivity insertion.
 However, there is complexity in translating the motor controllers’ need to receive position commands
into a toggling ability. Limitations were encountered when the time step resolution decreased below 0.5
seconds. The reasons for the limitations were not investigated further, but could be due to the gRPC
framework or to the smallness of the requested movement. This issue of translation between position to
relative motion should be paid attention to by reactor developers.
 The toggling method applied during this study used an iterative scheme that employed pooling to
watch for the user to press the button, then added a change in position to the measured position, based on
the cycle time and the desired speed:
 The ability to command a position directly was also permitted, but that optional motion allowed the
drums to move much faster than the rate limit imposed to limit reactivity insertion. The Insert and
Withdrawal buttons represent the toggle switching of the drums, and the Apply Position button allows for
a direct position command at full speed.
3.1.1.3. Light System Software Element
 Light intensity is fairly limited in comparison to full reactor operation. The operation of an 85 kW
thermal reactor such as MARVEL spans about 11 orders of magnitude. At the time of this study, the light
intensity of the ViBRANT barrel with the employed software was limited to 50 discrete values (i.e., 0%
to 50%, at 1% increments). A novel method developed for this study embodied the function of a selector
switch present on detectors. However, instead of changing the sensor range, the surrogate-power range
was adjusted so that the flux representation was always within the system’s capabilities. While detectors
use a selector knob with a base-10 system, it was found that a base-4 system operated more appropriately
and allowed the system to span 50 levels. In this way, the light increments represented a smaller power
span, and a range index was kept to track the shifting up or down from starting power. This range index
was also required for the photodiodes to translate the photodiode response back into measured power. The

  3.1.2. Software for Physics (Point Kinetics, Thermal Model, Drum Model,
 Inverse Kinetics)
 Physics computations can be divided into two major categories. The first category is the physics
simulations required to represent reactor behavior on MACS/ViBRANT. The second is the physics
computations completed using physical measurements that are used to infer phenomena that cannot be
directly measured or to provide feedback for operator control. Figure 2 shows the physics computations
for simulation in the blue boxes. These calculations include point kinetics, a drum model, and a thermal
model.
3.1.2.1. Simulator Software Element
 Point kinetics is a robust, time-proven method that represents a reactor’s bulk behavior. The fact that
it is applicable to more than 90% of the operating envelope, including basic startup physics, and has the
ability to operate in real-time make it an ideal candidate for characterizing the physical reactor’s
fundamental behavior. It leveraged key data from the MARVEL Monte Carlo N-Particle (MCNP) and
RELAP5 models to optimize its relevancy for this MARVEL startup study. The model relies on reactivity
as an input, and calculates the power as an output. The reactivity inputs come from the drum model and
thermal model.
 The drum model translates the measured drum positions to reactivity. With the position measurement
being limited to the resolver at the time of this study, the measured value and commanded values both
potentially differ from the actual position. This measured position is the best that can be done with MACS
since the drums do not physically interfere much with the light, although progress is being made to
further enable ViBRANT with such a capability.
 The conversion between position and reactivity came from MARVEL’s MCNP calculations,
documented in Engineering Calculations and Analysis Report (ECAR)-6099 [18]. However, these data
points were fit to an analytical function to provide finer data resolution. The need for this function was
first encountered during the drum-worth calibration test. The initial results showed flat regions on the
differential curve and were attributed to numerical artifacts stemming from linear interpolation between
data points, as well as to a sparse number of data points.
 The thermal model translates the reactor temperature into reactivity feedback. The conversion from
temperature to reactivity also came from MCNP data [18]. The reported reactivity feedback as a function
of temperature is fairly linear. The thermal model also keeps track of energy buildup and heat loss. The
model applied some rather gross approximations, including (1) application of a bulk temperature instead
of a distribution and (2) saying that the temperature within the BeO reflector was the same as that of the
NaK coolant and fuel.
 Bulk temperatures were calculated by numerically integrating the power from point kinetics to
determine net energy and then applying the mass and specific heat of the material in the core to arrive at a
temperature. The heat loss was approximated via an exponentially decaying factor applied to the energy
between timesteps. The factor was determined through analysis of the RELAP5 MARVEL data presented
in ECAR-6332 [19] and the power lost through the walls at a fixed reactor power. Equation 3 shows the
recursive relationship for the energy calculation; the power multiplied by time represents the change in

3.1.2.2. User or Inferred Physics Software Element
 The second category of the physics software involves interpretations from the sensors to inform
operators. Drum-position indication is rather straightforward because the measured quantity is the value
of interest and is directly interpretable by operators. The photodiode light sensors, however, have several
issues that require data manipulation in order to be meaningful.
 The first issue with the photodiodes is that they must be calibrated to the referenced power, and the
translation to a power output requires applying the inverse of the mathematics used to translate reactor
power to light intensity for projection onto ViBRANT. Furthermore, the output of the photodiodes is

 10

rather noisy. To obtain a clean power-signal representation, five photodiodes were averaged, and the
signal was heavily filtered by using a low-pass filter set to 0.02 Hz. This filtered value is shown to the
operator as the power value and represents the neutron-detector signal after it has been scaled to power.
 The inverse-kinetics algorithm takes power or neutron flux as an input and then outputs reactivity.
The input to inverse kinetics does not need to be scaled to some known quantity; instead, it must scale
linearly with the power or neutron flux. The relative shape is what matters. The inverse-kinetics algorithm
suffered from the photodiode noise. Despite averaging and filtering the signals, the inferred reactivity had
too much noise from the derivative term to produce useful results. The inferred reactivity from inverse
kinetics tended to follow the true reactivity, but the variation was too great to be useful. Because of this,
the true reactivity used in the point-kinetics equation was directly employed for analysis in the tests.
Inverse kinetics is sufficient with neutron detectors and properly set-up systems. However, as things
currently stand, inverse kinetics is not usable in MACS without further testing and development work
first being conducted to optimize it.

  3.2.1. Drum-Worth Measurement
 Aside from establishing criticality for the first time, reactivity control is one of the first experimental
subjects that must take place in a startup campaign, and must encompass excess-reactivity and shutdown-
worth measurements. After those margins have been established, the drums can be characterized. The
drum-worth curves are integral in nearly all follow-on experiments in a given startup campaign.
 Methods may vary from reactor to reactor. The main idea of a rod- or drum-worth calibration
measurement is to generate the relevant integral worth curve, such as the one shown below in Figure 6
(taken from ECAR-6099) [18].

Figure 6. MARVEL’s drum-worth curve.

 The integral worth curve can be measured in different ways and must consider the limitations of the
reactor operation and measurement abilities. Reactivity is most commonly measured either via inverse
kinetics, which return a time-dependent value, or through period measurements, which must be subject to
specific conditions in order to be meaningful.
 Most reactivity measurements are best taken at near-criticality. Deep sub-critical measurements tend
to be inaccurate, and large super-critical measurements may not be allowed due to operational and
physical constraints.
 The drum-worth measurement method invoked in this test was to perform a series of step changes in
drum position so as to develop a differential worth curve. By using an equation to describe the differential
worth curve, the differential worth curve can be integrated to develop an analytical expression for the
integral worth curve. Fitting the differential worth data to a model allows parameters to be determined
and the integral worth curve to be predicted.
 Because the process of measuring differential worth requires that a near-critical reactor state be
maintained, different drums are used to make adjustments to offset the change caused by the drum being
measured. This process is called rod swapping. Furthermore, this method of swapping drums or rods also
allows two drums to be measured at once. For the sake of demonstration, this process was applied to only
one drum, whereas in MARVEL, all drums will require characterization.

value. However, the method demonstrates that one can reconstruct simulator physics without knowing the
simulator information, which is also the case in the real world, where physics are not completely known
and the experimental method is meant to establish a model for the physics.
 For the tests performed for the drum-worth measurements, it was prudent to utilize the simulator’s
known reactivity. This is because the derivative term in the inverse-kinetics equations was producing too
much noise to be usable. The source of this noise originated in the noise of the photodiodes; this was even
after significant filtering and averaging had taken place. Period measurements could have been done
instead of using the known reactivity; however, for time’s sake, only one period measurement was
performed to demonstrate the application and verify that it closely matched the known reactivity from the
simulator. The time needed to perform period measurements would have extended the drum-worth testing
to a period of several days as a result of the added difficulty in performing the measurement and analysis.
 To perform a period measurement, one must fit the power data to an exponential. However, one must
provide enough time for the transient terms to die away and leave the asymptotic period. This is why the
data need to be extended by about 2 decades in terms of power. One can test whether the asymptotic
period has been reached by moving the exponential fit through the data and observing when the resultant
exponent does not change substantially. The exponent in the exponential is the inverse period, with units
of inverse seconds. This inverse period, or period if inverted, is applied to an equation called the “in-hour
equation,” which will translate between the inverse period and the reactivity. This equation is only

 15

intended for use on constant-step reactivity insertions where the period is the asymptotic period and all
the transient terms are negligible.

  3.2.4. Scram Times
 A typical test in the technical specifications for a reactor is the time it takes for the reactor to scram.

MARVEL actuators, their scram function is based on the interplay and sizing of the clutch, spring, and
damper. Many analyses and milestone reports have been produced to document this actuator design, as
included in references ECAR-7228 [21] and M3AT-23IN0805074 [12].

clutch deactivation via the scram-button interrupt action. The full-in limit switch marked the shutdown

  3.2.5. Potentiometer Calibration and Usage
 Potentiometers attached to the shaft report the drum’s absolute position so long as it does not change
relative to the shaft. The potentiometers that malfunctioned at the beginning of the study were added after
several tests had been performed. The potentiometers available to support the resolver-position
information provided some useful insights into MARVEL control-software needs.
 The motor-resolver position reading, as communicated from the motor drive, is aways relative to
wherever the controller is “homed.” In fact, the drive can keep track of this position regardless of the
100:1 gearhead’s influence, which allows 50 rotations over the 180-degree range and reports a digital
signal relative to the homed position. However, the potentiometer is seen by LabVIEW software as an
analog voltage signal that changes as the potentiometer wiper traverses its range. Relating the observed
voltage to position must be done with the software itself, via a calibration process.

 20

 For MACS, the potentiometer calibration process proved to involve nuance, necessitating that a
standard be created. For all measurements, the starting reference point was designated as the drums being
scrammed such that they would return to the shutdown position. This was verified by manually rotating
all the drums not in the shutdown position back to that position. Some of the drums did not go completely
back to the hard stop after being released from the clutch; this will be resolved in the future by adjusting
the spring preset position, which is a friction-compensation measure (some drums have greater resistance
than others, due to alignment and other factors). Additionally, using the motor to drive the system to shut
down had its own nuances, with the speed of rotation toward the starting position potentially influencing
where the drum stops. Furthermore, the analog voltage coming from the potentiometers at shutdown does
not report 0, is different for each potentiometer, and requires individual offsets.
 Because there was no other means to verify the potentiometers, the resolvers were used as the primary
source of truth. Factors such as clutch slip proved this to be a bad assumption, and a different means of
calibration should be pursued in the future.
 The potentiometer calibration process employed in this study involved commanding the motor to
move to a fixed position and then hold that position for a few seconds. Each step provided a lookup table
value between the position and the voltage coming from the potentiometer. The best data came from
Control Drum 1 (CD-1) and is shown below in Figure 13.

 3.2.6. Hard-Stop Adjustment
 The hard stops are one of the most-important features with respect to the safety basis in the MARVEL
design. The process of performing a startup campaign for MARVEL will include many adjustments of the
hard stops throughout the process in order to maintain conservative limits on each step in the process.
 The MACS hard stops use a clamping method that secures to the shaft of the corresponding cam that
interfaces with the hard stop. The clamping method allows infinite adjustment. However, the MARVEL
hard stops will be keyed to specific angles so that mistakes are minimized.
 The process of adjusting a hard stop in MACS requires use of an Allen wrench to tighten and loosen
the hard stop at the desired angle. In MACS, the desired angle can be set by moving and watching the
potentiometer, driving the drum out to this position, and then tightening the set screw. However, that

 22

process is not likely to be done on the reactor, as it involves moving drums during the adjustment.
Instead, hard stops need to be set based on a visual reference, such as a protractor overlayed on the drum,
and the keying of the hard-stop.

encountered.

3.2.6.3.      Drum Worth
 It is advantageous to set the hard stops such that the drums can maximize their range of motion. If the
rod worth measurements are performed after sufficient data have been obtained from thermal feedback,
the case can be made to allow much more than 40 cents in excess, though the amount must still be less
than what is needed for hot full-power conditions (i.e., around $2.30 in excess). The total worth of a drum
is about $4.31. Thus, with such allowance, the drums can move just past the halfway point. The absolute
maximum placement for the hard stops would be about $2.30 plus the 40 cents extra, or around $2.70.
 This might be achieved with a scheme such as:
• The reactor is set to critical, with one drum fully in and another fully out, and the remaining two
 drums set to 110 degrees, with their hard stops set to those positions as well.
• The hard stop is set on the drum that starts at the shutdown position, such that it can only go to around
 $2.30 worth, which is about 76 degrees. Thus, this drum would only be able to rotate from 0 to
 75 degrees.
• The drum at the shutdown position is swapped with the drum in the full-out position, following the
 procedure outlined in Section 3.2.1.
• The two drums may not be perfectly balanced, and the other two drums may be adjusted to provide a
 little extra motion. However, the limitation is the amount of excess reactivity allowed in the core by
 the placement of the hard stops.
• The measurements should calibrate the fully inserted drum from 0 to ~75 degrees—and 180 to
 ~70 degrees for the drum that started fully withdrawn.
 - The hard stops are then switched on the fully inserted and fully withdrawn drums so that the
 remaining positions may be measured.
 - The process is then repeated for the remaining two drums that were used to establish criticality.
 - This example requires one set of hard stops to be fabricated. However, the hard stops will be
 shifted to a different drum for each calibration and require a total of four adjustments.

 4. USE CASE 2: TILT-COMPENSATING SUPERVISORY CONTROL
 To achieve an autonomous operations model, advanced reactor control systems must increase in
complexity. This demands the integration of substantial new technologies and monitoring systems,
including numerous additional sensors and the deployment of advanced technologies such as DTs. These
tools are essential for supporting the monitoring and decision-making processes that would ordinarily be
handled by a team of plant staff. Consequently, there is a critical need for development of reliable and
robust supervisory control methods to coordinate and interpret this vast array of new capabilities and
information. Supervisory control, discussed in detail in Section 4.2, generally refers to control methods
that make the types of higher-level decisions (as compared with high-performance or logical control
methods) typically made by humans in order to adapt to diverse plant conditions.
 The nuclear research community is exploring the use of AI tools such as agentic operators to handle
some of these diverse plant conditions. These powerful tools can provide higher-level reasoning
capabilities, without the need to manually program or design every possible decision into the supervisory
control system—a task that may be impractical. However, the increased power and flexibility of these
tools entail challenges, as they are not perfectly understood. Therefore, there must be resilience to
suboptimal decisions made by these supervisory control strategies. This is a key idea motivating the
discussion and definition of supervisory control in Section 4.2.
 As a use case, this effort investigated the detection and response to asymmetries in neutron flux that
might result from asymmetrical operations and/or imperfections in the manufacturing of fuel rods or
reactivity control devices. While total reactor power is a critical process parameter that must be controlled
to meet demand and maximize generation, the neutron flux distribution is also crucial for performance
and safety. An asymmetric flux distribution, referred to as core tilt, can lead to several adverse effects.
Uneven power distribution can cause asymmetric temperatures and localized hotspots, thus accelerating
component degradation and increasing overall risk factors. Additionally, uneven temperatures can result
in increased thermal stresses and can reduce thermodynamic efficiency, leading to inefficiencies in
reactor operation.
 This use case was addressed via a supervisory control architecture that coupled the primary reactor
power control loop with the secondary core tilt control loop. Using the definition and details described in
Section 4.2, this architecture was designed to enhance the robustness and resilience of operations, even in
the presence of potentially suboptimal decision-making by the supervisory controller. Further details on
this use case and the supervisory control architecture are provided in Section 4.3.
 The power control loop functioned as a direct controller influencing the plant's reactivity. It managed
the reactor's reactivity through control mechanisms such as control rods or control drums, which are
crucial for maintaining stability and ensuring safe operation by adjusting the reactor's power output.
Additionally, to interface with the supervisory controller, the power controller required a mechanism (that
was adjusted by the supervisory controller) to compensate for core asymmetry. The specifics of this
power controller are detailed in Section 4.4.
 The primary objective of this effort was to implement, compare, and contrast a range of supervisory
control methods to determine their efficacy at managing core tilt and enhancing reactor performance. This
investigation focused on three approaches: RB methods, optimization techniques, and RL algorithms.
Each method corresponds to unique advantages and challenges, and comparative analysis affords valuable
insights into their applicability and effectiveness within a nuclear reactor context. Detailed descriptions,
methodologies, and evaluations of these supervisory control methods are provided in Section 4.5,
highlighting how each approach addresses the complexities and demands of advanced reactor operations.
 To achieve these objectives, software integration was necessary to integrate MACS with advanced
controls algorithms. The COMMAND software was employed. COMMAND, a Python-based modular
software tool developed at INL, facilitates rapid simulation and evaluation of dynamic systems and

controllers, the aim being to develop and validate control methods for advanced reactors. More details on
COMMAND and its role in this effort are provided in Section 4.1.

 4.2. Overview of Supervisory Control
 The first objective of this use case was to investigate and develop a definition of supervisory control
that could be used for designing a hierarchical control system. Supervisory control is a method of
managing and directing the operations of automated systems, typically within industrial settings such as

 28

the manufacturing, energy, and transportation sectors. It involves high-level decision-making processes
that oversee and coordinate lower-level control systems, ensuring optimal performance, safety, and
efficiency in the face of internal or external events that force the system to change its behavior. In this
hierarchical structure, the highest layer of the hierarchy often corresponds to events with slow dynamics
(i.e., behavior evolves over a long time scale), while lower layers are often characterized by faster
dynamics.
  4.2.1. Literature Review
 Supervisory control is widely utilized across various industries. In manufacturing, it optimizes
production lines, reduces waste, and improves product quality [23,24]. The transportation sector benefits
from supervisory control through improved traffic management and the facilitation of autonomous vehicle
operations [25,26,27,28]. In the energy sector, it plays a critical role in managing power grids so as to
ensure stable, efficient electricity distribution [29,30]. For nuclear power plants in particular, supervisory
control assists in the operation and management of plants, ensuring their safety, efficiency, and reliability
[31,32]. Given the complex and hazardous nature of nuclear power generation, supervisory control
systems are designed to oversee and coordinate the myriad processes involved, and to integrate various
subsystems, including reactor control, turbine and generator control, cooling systems management, safety
systems, and the waste management system.
 Application of supervisory control in the nuclear industry has been documented in several papers and
reports. The 2019 International Atomic Energy Agency report [33] discussed a supervisory control
architecture for multi-module small modular reactors (SMRs), where “the final authority for decisions and
goal setting remains with the human but the control system assumes expanded responsibilities for normal
control action.” It also points out that some existing nuclear power plants, such as Pickering A in Canada,
share certain elements of this architecture, but not to the level anticipated for SMRs. In [34], a supervisory
control system proposed for a generic SMR incorporated decision making into the system layers via the
introduction of a tiered taxonomy of plant subsystems. A two-layer supervisory controller for autonomous
operation of advanced reactors was introduced in [35], with the high-level layer processing load-
following requests based on the plant’s operational constraints while the low-level layer, in the form of
PID controllers, interfaces with the actuators to meet these requests. In [36], a hierarchical control system
proposed for coordination control of multiple SMRs consisted of three layers: (1) supervisory control for
core reactivity and balance-of-plant control, (2) tactical control for prognostics and health management,
and (3) strategic control for optimizing the daily dispatch of the multiple SMRs at a single plant.
Recently in [37], a fuzzy-logic-based supervisory controller was proposed to regulate the average coolant
temperature in lead-bismuth-cooled reactors.
  4.2.2. Function of Supervisory Control
 The emergence of supervisory control dates back to the late 1960s [38], when the advent of
minicomputer-based systems revealed the potential for significantly enhancing data acquisition. This
originated the expression “supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA),” which is still in use today.
The key functions of supervisory control can be summarized as:
 • Monitoring: Continuously tracking system performance and external conditions to ensure
 operations are within desired parameters.
 • Decision-Making: Utilizing algorithms and models to make informed decisions on system
 adjustments and responses to changing conditions.
 • Coordination: Synchronizing the activities of various subsystems to work harmoniously toward
 overall operational goals.
 • Optimization: Enhancing system performance by making real-time adjustments to improve
 efficiency, reduce costs, and maintain safety standards.

 29

 • Fault Detection and Management: Identifying, diagnosing, and responding to system faults or
 anomalies to prevent or mitigate disruptions.
 The benefits of supervisory control systems include enhanced operational efficiency, reduced
downtimes, increased safety, and the ability to handle complex and dynamic environments. By integrating
advanced technologies such as machine learning (ML) and AI, supervisory control systems are becoming
more sophisticated, providing predictive analytics and autonomous decision-making capabilities.

  4.2.4. Definition of Supervisory Control
 Detailed definitions for supervisory control are not universal, but rather application dependent. Thus,
for the sake of clarity, this effort developed the following definition used throughout this work.
 Supervisory control refers to a system architecture in which a controller (termed the
 supervisory controller) oversees and influences the operation of other controllers (termed
 the supervised controllers). This can result in a hierarchical control system where
 supervisory controllers supervise other supervisory controllers. A supervisory controller has
 the following properties:
 1. Provides higher-level guidance, adjustments, and optimizations based on the
 performance and objectives of the processes and/or other supervised controllers.
 2. If it is removed, the supervised control loop remains closed and operable but may
 not be optimal (e.g., remains stable but the performance deteriorates).
 3. May operate with slower time constants than the supervised controllers.
 As this effort examines the role of AI in supervisory control, this second property becomes crucial.
The motivation for it is that if the AI is removed or makes a suboptimal decision, the system should still
be able to function. In contrast, erratic movements by a drum controller that maintains reactor power (i.e.,
a supervised controller) could prevent the reactor from operating properly, potentially resulting in unmet
demand or an unplanned shutdown. Requiring that the supervised control loop remains closed and
operable, even without the supervisory controller, adds robustness and reliability to the system. This
property ensures that while the supervisory controller can enhance performance and optimize operations,
its absence, malfunction, or sub-optimal performance does not compromise the fundamental safety and
functionality of the controlled process, thereby meeting a key regulatory requirement.
 4.3. Problem Statement
 The following problem statement guided the development of a control architecture demonstrating AI's
impact on supervisory control. This statement and the definition of supervisory control as established in
the previous section informed the creation of a reactor power and tilt control architecture that separated
control functionality into supervisory and supervised loops.
 Problem Statement: Control the reactor power of MACS to a specified set point
 while minimizing core tilt, with the constraint of moving only one control drum at
 a time.
 Figure 19 shows the block diagram of the control structure for the reactor power and core tilt control
use case. In dark blue is the reactor power, or supervised, control loop. In the context of this use case, this
is considered the primary loop, as maintaining reactor power is considered the most important objective.

 31

This loop receives a reference reactor power setpoint and manipulates control drum positioning to achieve
the desired reactor power. This is done in two stages. First, the Power Controller takes a measurement of
the current reactor power, compares it with the reference reactor power, and outputs the total amount of
drum change required to meet the reference reactor power. A second stage, called the Balancing Drum
Controller, decides which drum should be moved at each execution of the controller, given the constraint
in the problem statement that only one drum can be moved at any given time.
 The supervisory loop, shown in yellow, is responsible for compensating for any tilt observed in the
core. It receives a measurement of core tilt and then outputs drum bias adjustments that correct for the
reactor tilt. Comparing this block diagram with the three types of supervisory control detailed in Section
4.2, this supervisory loop would be considered an example of parameter adaptation. The definition of
supervisory control, as adopted by this report, necessitates that the primary (supervised) loop must remain
operable and functional in the absence or malfunction of the supervisory loop. The architecture meets this
requirement by modifying parameters (that would otherwise remain stationary or have default values)
within the Balancing Drum Controller in such a way that the primary loop commands are always met.
Details of this prioritization are given in the subsequent section.

 Of the three controllers present in this architecture—the Power Controller, the Balancing Drum
Controller, and the Tilt Controller—two were developed in this effort. The Power Controller was
developed in a previous effort, but could only control one drum and had no ability to incorporate
supervisory commands [2,5]. This effort focused on developing the Balancing Drum Controller to expand
power control from one drum to all four, and the Supervisory Tilt Controller to provide tilt compensation.
In addition, this effort integrated these three individual controllers into one complete supervisory control
system and tested its performance across a variety of scenarios.
 4.4. Four-Drum Balancing Controller
  4.4.1.      Algorithm
 In previous efforts, reactor power was controlled by holding three drums stationary while all control
actions were executed using the fourth drum [2,5]. This fourth drum was regulated by a PID controller—
the Power Controller—that adjusted the drum angle, based on the error between the measured and
setpoint reactor power. This strategy was implemented because the system was constrained so as to allow
only one drum to move at a time, making it impossible to control all four drums simultaneously via a
single PID controller. In terms of power control, the problem was redundant, meaning that only one drum
had to be moved for most power control actions. However, using a single drum for all control actions
could introduce tilt into the core, which is not optimal. To address this issue, the new four-drum

 32

Balancing Drum Controller was developed. The aim was to track the setpoint reactor power while
balancing tilt—both under nominal conditions and in response to core asymmetries—via coordination
with a supervisory controller.
 The first step was to modify the PID controller to calculate the required change in drum angle, rather
than the actual drum angle. In other words, instead of instructing the fourth drum to move from 120
degrees to 121 degrees, the new PID controller would inform the balancing controller that a 1-degree
drum rotation was needed. This change in angle, as a function of discrete timestep 𝑡, is denoted by 𝛿(𝑡).
 The Balancing Drum Controller would then determine which drum should move the additional 1
degree of drum rotation. This approach also resulted in a highly modular design; were it desired to replace
the PID controller with a different control strategy, the Balancing Drum Controller could still perform its
function effectively.
 To determine which drum should move, the decision was transformed into an optimization problem.
First, the vector of drum angles and the general update rule were defined as follows:

element of these vectors, e.g., 𝜃𝑖 (𝑡). Using this update rule, the constraint of moving one drum at a time
can be defined mathematically as ∑𝑖 𝑏𝑖 (𝑡) = 1.

supervisory controller could adjust to compensate for tilt. This was achieved through the following virtual

 4.5. Supervisory Control Algorithms
 This section provides an overview of the supervisory control algorithms investigated in this work:
RB, optimization, and RL. Despite their differences, these algorithms function in a similar fashion. They
receive measured or calculated information about the spatial distribution of reactor power and other
relevant measurements such as drum positions 𝜃(𝑡), and use this information to calculate the values of the
bias terms 𝛼(𝑡).
 The simulation and hardware experiments required different approaches for measuring the spatial
distribution of power. The MACS hardware included four equally spaced photodiode sensors to measure

the spatial distribution of the light core, referred to as the quadrant powers. For the simulation, virtual
sensors were created based on the distribution calculated from the MCNP surrogate model. This involved
averaging the powers of the pins closest to the physical sensor locations. Figure 23 illustrates this setup
via a cross-sectional view of the system, with the corresponding pins associated with each virtual sensor
labeled from 1 to 4. While these virtual sensors reasonably approximate the sensor measurements, it was
expected that this approach would result in discrepancies between the simulation and hardware results.

 Each tilt controller—RB, optimization, and RL—was tested both in simulation and on hardware,

core quadrants, as identified in Figure 23. Subplot (c) shows the position of each of the four control

within a single test or between tests, and thus does not have meaningful units. This means it is more

 For this testing, the primary control loop, consisting of the Power Controller and Balancing Drum
Controller, was run with a timestep of 0.2 seconds. This value was selected because it achieved balance
between computational complexity and performance in response to changes in power demand. The
supervisory loop, which contains the Tilt Controller, was run with a timestep of 10 seconds, as the

simulations and tests. The duration of the testing depended on the amount of time it took for the
supervisory controller to reach steady state, and this varied across the three controllers. For RB control
testing, the total test length was set to 800 seconds, the optimization controller used a test length of 1,000
seconds, and the RL tilt control tests were run for 200 seconds.
 One challenge in implementing this testing was the computational complexity combined with the 0.2
second sampling time used for control performance. Although new methods of addressing this complexity
are being investigated, this effort ran all tests in slower than real time in order to ensure that the
computations and MACS hardware were properly coordinated. Care was taken to ensure that all
components operated consistently at a slower than real time pace, and that all constraints adhered to the
simulation time (i.e., the elapsed time of the models and control module) rather than the clock time (i.e.,
the actual elapsed time as measured by a clock).
  4.5.2. Rules-Based Approach
4.5.2.1.       Algorithm
 The RB tilt control algorithm iteratively adjusts 𝛼(𝑡) during each control execution to balance the
power output among the four quadrants within a specified tolerance. The algorithm is built on a
predefined logic-based control law derived from expert knowledge of the system. A key distinction
between the RB approach and the subsequent supervisory methods described in this report is that any
input to the RB tilt control algorithm has a deterministic output.
 At execution, the RB tilt control algorithm takes three inputs: a vector containing a measurement of
the average outer pin power in each quadrant, 𝑄(𝑡), a vector containing the current measurement of each
drum position, 𝜃(𝑡), and a vector containing the previously issued drum offset parameters, 𝛼(𝑡 − 1).
 For each Quadrant 𝑖, the first step of the RB algorithm is to evaluate if that quadrant’s corresponding
drum offset 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) is eligible for an update. This is based on whether the previous drum offset, 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡 − 1),
has been achieved. If it has, the algorithm considers an update for 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡). If not, 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡 − 1) is reissued as
 This step prevents several undesirable outcomes. First, if the physical process has not caught up to the
issued command (i.e., drum 𝑖 has not rotated enough to meet 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡 − 1)), a scenario is prevented in which
a new 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) is continuously issued to the system despite the old 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡 − 1) never being met. A second
prevented scenario is that the Balancing Drum Controller does not enact the commanded 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡 − 1) due to
power demands incompatible with the commanded 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡 − 1). In this scenario, 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) is not changed, as
the system currently lacks the bandwidth to meet the tilt compensation command, and any further changes
to 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) would also not be met.
 If the algorithm passes the first step, the second step of the RB tilt controller evaluates the primary tilt
control criterion: whether the power generated by a quadrant differs from another quadrant beyond a set
tolerance, Δ 𝑇ℎ𝑟𝑒𝑠ℎ𝑜𝑙𝑑 . The controller calculates the difference between each quadrant’s average power,
𝑄𝑖 (𝑡), and the quadrants with the minimum and maximum average power. This results in Δ𝑄𝑖,𝑚𝑖𝑛 (𝑡), the
difference in power from Quadrant 𝑖 to the quadrant with the minimum power, and Δ𝑄𝑖,𝑚𝑎𝑥 (𝑡), the
difference in power from Quadrant 𝑖 to the quadrant with the maximum power:

 37

                                    Δ𝑄𝑖,𝑚𝑖𝑛 (𝑡) = 𝑄𝑖 (𝑡) − min(𝑄(𝑡))                                     (15)
                                    Δ𝑄𝑖,𝑚𝑎𝑥 (𝑡) = 𝑄𝑖 (𝑡) − max(𝑄(𝑡))                                     (16)
The absolute value of these two quantities is then compared against Δ 𝑇ℎ𝑟𝑒𝑠ℎ𝑜𝑙𝑑 . If either 𝑎𝑏𝑠(Δ𝑄𝑖,𝑚𝑖𝑛 (𝑡))
or 𝑎𝑏𝑠(Δ𝑄𝑖,𝑚𝑎𝑥 (𝑡)) exceeds Δ 𝑇ℎ𝑟𝑒𝑠ℎ𝑜𝑙𝑑 , a tilt adjustment is required for Quadrant 𝑖. If neither exceeds
Δ 𝑇ℎ𝑟𝑒𝑠ℎ𝑜𝑙𝑑 , the tilt for Quadrant 𝑖 is within tolerance and no change to 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) is required.
 If an adjustment to 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) is required, the next step in the algorithm determines the value of the
adjustment needed, both in terms of increasing or decreasing 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) as well as the magnitude of said
change, expressed as Δ𝛼𝑖 (𝑡). First, Δ𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) is computed as a function of 𝑄𝑖 (𝑡), 𝑄𝑎𝑣𝑔 (𝑡)—the average
power of all quadrants—and a user-defined gain, 𝑘𝛼 :

                                  Δ𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) = 𝑘𝛼 ∗ 𝑎𝑏𝑠 (𝑄𝑖 (𝑡) − 𝑄𝑎𝑣𝑔 (𝑡))                                 (17)

If the absolute difference of the current quadrant power from the minimum quadrant power is greater than
the difference from the maximum quadrant, the value of 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡 − 1) is reduced by Δ𝛼𝑖 (𝑡). This requests
that the Balancing Drum Controller lower the angle of drum 𝑖, thus reducing the power in Quadrant 𝑖.
This is expressed mathematically as:

              𝑖𝑓 𝑎𝑏𝑠 (Δ𝑄𝑖,𝑚𝑖𝑛 (𝑡)) > 𝑎𝑏𝑠 (Δ𝑄𝑖,𝑚𝑎𝑥 (𝑡)) , 𝑡ℎ𝑒𝑛 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) = 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡 − 1) − Δ𝛼𝑖 (𝑡)              (18)

If the absolute difference of the current quadrant power from the minimum quadrant power is less than
the difference from the maximum quadrant, the value of 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡 − 1) is increased by Δ𝛼𝑖 (𝑡). This requests
that the Balancing Drum Controller increase the angle of drum 𝑖, in turn increasing the power in Quadrant
𝑖. This is expressed mathematically as:

              𝑖𝑓 𝑎𝑏𝑠 (Δ𝑄𝑖,𝑚𝑖𝑛 (𝑡)) < 𝑎𝑏𝑠 (Δ𝑄𝑖,𝑚𝑎𝑥 (𝑡)) , 𝑡ℎ𝑒𝑛 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) = 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡 − 1) + Δ𝛼𝑖 (𝑡)              (19)

However, 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) is only increased if the current position of drum 𝑖, 𝜃𝑖 (𝑡), is below the maximum allowed
drum position 𝜃𝑚𝑎𝑥 . Otherwise, 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) = 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡 − 1). This is expressed mathematically as:
                                 𝑖𝑓 𝜃𝑖 (𝑡) ≥ 𝜃𝑚𝑎𝑥 , 𝑡ℎ𝑒𝑛 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡) = 𝛼𝑖 (𝑡 − 1)                             (20)
 This process is completed for each Quadrant 𝑖, effectively comparing all quadrants with each other.
Unlike the other supervisory control approaches discussed later, which directly use the tilt metric to
compare the quadrant powers, this algorithm must instead approximate the comparisons between
quadrants inherent in the tilt metric through a set of rules.

4.5.2.3.      Observations
 Presented in Section 4.5.2 is the final implemented version of the RB tilt control algorithm, and the
version used to produce the results presented in this report. However, multiple iterations of the controller
were required to arrive at the version presented herein. Therein lies one of the downsides of RB control
methods. Implementing RB control is an inherently iterative approach that relies on trial and error to
achieve satisfactory results, even with expert knowledge. A set of rules needs to be defined, tested, and
evaluated in terms of performance in order to judge whether the rules are sufficient, require modification,
or need replaced. In cases when new rules are required, their implementation must not only be evaluated
as being effective, but must also be verified as not conflicting with any previously implemented rules.
While this can be a straightforward for simple RB control implementations involving few rules, it can
become difficult and time consuming for complex RB control implementations involving many rules.
 An example of this trial-and-error approach was encountered in developing the RB controller. In an
early iteration of the of the RB controller, there was no consideration as to whether the previously issued
𝛼(𝑡 − 1) had been achieved by the system. The only condition the RB controller checked was whether or
not Δ 𝑇ℎ𝑟𝑒𝑠ℎ𝑜𝑙𝑑 had been met. However, scenarios were encountered in which Δ 𝑇ℎ𝑟𝑒𝑠ℎ𝑜𝑙𝑑 would be
exceeded despite 𝛼(𝑡 − 1) not having been achieved. For example, take 𝛼(𝑡 − 1) = [5 0 0 0], 𝜃(𝑡) =
[127 125 125 125], and Δ𝑄𝑖,𝑚𝑎𝑥 (𝑡) > Δ 𝑇ℎ𝑟𝑒𝑠ℎ𝑜𝑙𝑑 . In this scenario, the intended 𝛼(𝑡 − 1) had not been
met by time 𝑡, and Δ 𝑇ℎ𝑟𝑒𝑠ℎ𝑜𝑙𝑑 was exceeded. This means that a new 𝛼(𝑡) would be issued and could
result in 𝛼(𝑡) = [6 0 0 0]. However, the balancing controller may not actually move the drums, possibly
to maintain the power level, resulting in 𝜃(𝑡) = 𝜃(𝑡 + 1) = [127 125 125 125]. This cycle then repeats
itself, with 𝛼1 continuing to increase after no movement in the drums. Eventually, when an updated
power command is received that allows movement of the drums, 𝛼 would be so out of sync with the true
state of the drums that the commands issued from the RB controller would be irrelevant to the current
state of the system. This is analogous to integrator windup in a PID controller, described in detail in a
prior report [2]. The fix for this was simple in this case: add a rule that 𝛼(𝑡) does not change until 𝛼(𝑡 −
1) is met. But this illustrates a key limitation of RB control—another rule can always be added to
improve performance, but eventually “one more rule” will result in a very complex—and possibly
delicate—control logic

 40

 The test results demonstrated that an additional rule should have been added. It was found that
eventually a steady state is reached in the tilt metric, where tilt can no longer be lowered even with
continued drum movements. This occurs at approximately 𝑡 = 400 𝑠𝑒𝑐 in Figure 24. Because no rule
exists to check for improvement to the tilt metric, the controller continues to adjust 𝛼, thus causing
additional drum position movement until 𝜃1 reaches 𝜃𝑀𝑎𝑥 . However, this additional drum movement does
nothing to improve the reactor tilt. Any future use of the RB tilt controller should take into consideration
the change in the tilt metric over time.
 After implementing and testing the RB supervisory controller, the following observations and
conclusions can be made about its performance and development:
 1. The performance of the RB control is predictable and intuitive.
 2. Expert knowledge of the system and process is required to develop suitable rules.
 3. Each implementation must be uniquely designed for its specific application, preventing any
 components of the controller from being transferred to other applications.
 4. Minimal computation demands arise during development.
 5. Performance of RB control is not optimal. Control stops taking action once specified
 conditions are met, not when an optimal state is reached.
 a. For example, if Δ 𝑇ℎ𝑟𝑒𝑠ℎ𝑜𝑙𝑑 is set to 100 W, the controller stops acting when that
 threshold is met, even if the system has the bandwidth to bring the tilt difference
 down to 50 W.
 b. Whether this matters or not is context dependent. Sometimes “good enough” is
 sufficient and optimality is not of concern; at other times, optimality is a high
 priority.
 6. Rules or parameter calibration must be adjusted to meet performance requirements.
 7. The performance of the controller can be altered by adding “one more rule.” The positive or
 negative impact of this depends on the context. For simple control, adding one more rule can
 be quick to implement. However, for complex control, it may be harder to add another rule
 without disrupting the existing control logic, and too many instances of “one more rule” may
 be detrimental.
 8. Computation time during operation is minimal.
 9. Controller response is immediate. System response time is dictated by rules and parameters.
  4.5.3. Optimization Approach
4.5.3.1.      Algorithm
 The optimizing supervisory controller leverages tools from the field of optimization to determine the
optimal values of 𝛼(𝑡). Unlike other methods, this approach does not rely on any system model or prior
knowledge, making it the least dependent on predefined information. Instead, it learns and improves
through experiential learning to achieve better solutions.
 In optimization problems, there are often two competing mechanisms when searching for better
solutions: exploration and exploitation. Exploration involves investigating new or unexplored regions of
the solution space in the hopes of discovering better solutions or gaining a broader understanding of the
objective function. On the other hand, exploitation refers to utilizing previously acquired knowledge to
refine and improve the existing solution. The developed approach effectively integrates both mechanisms.

 41

 The proposed approach is iterative, making small adjustments to the parameters in an attempt to move
the drums toward more optimal solutions:
                                             𝛼(𝑡) = 𝛼 ∗ + 𝑠𝑣(𝑡)                                           (21)
where 𝑠 represents a user-defined step size, 𝑣(𝑡) is the direction in which to move the existing solution,
and 𝛼 ∗ corresponds to the parameters of the current best solution. The current best solution is defined by
the solution that achieves the lowest tilt metric 𝑇(𝑡). To achieve both exploration and exploitation, two
mechanisms were employed for selecting the direction. Initially, the exploration mechanism moves the
solution in a random direction, as the algorithm lacks prior knowledge to perform exploitation. This
random movement is the only valid option for the first few iterations. Once sufficient data are collected,
the exploitation mechanism calculates an empirical estimate of the gradient of the objective function with
respect to the parameters. The algorithm then takes a gradient descent approach by moving in the opposite
direction of the gradient, effectively utilizing past data to determine the most efficient direction via which
to reduce reactor asymmetry. These two mechanisms are alternated randomly with equal probability,
ensuring that new candidate solutions equally explore and exploit opportunities.
 The empirical estimate of the gradient was derived by fitting a linear regression model by using the
historical values to estimate the measured objective functions:
                                               𝑇̂(𝛼) = 𝐴𝛼 + 𝑏                                             (22)
where 𝑇̂ ∈ ℝ is an estimate of the tilt measure as a function of the historical values of 𝛼, and 𝐴 ∈ ℝ1×4
and 𝑏 ∈ ℝ are the model coefficients. In this model, once the calculation is complete, the gradient of the
objective function is represented by the fitted coefficients 𝐴. The model coefficients were solved for via
weighted least-squares regression. The weight values were determined by a Gaussian kernel with a user-
defined bandwidth that determined how quickly past values would be assigned lower weights. This
weighting process was crucial because the gradient can vary significantly across the solution space, and
because the assumption that the objective function is linear holds true only in a region close to the point
of interest.
 Instead of maintaining a complete history of past values, this algorithm utilized a rolling history of the
previous 20 iterations. This rolling history was employed to calculate both 𝛼 ∗ and the gradient. This
method helped managed noise, and would be essential for adapting to tilt conditions that change over
time.

 Results from the simulation and hardware tests are shown in Figure 26 and Figure 27, respectively.
Although they illustrate similar concepts, they do so in slightly different ways. First, in looking at the
simulation results in Figure 26(b)-(d), there is a noticeable long plateau at the beginning. During this

This behavior is likely due to one of the following factors: it could be trapped in a local minimum and

4.5.3.3.      Observations
 The optimization approach was originally developed and tested in a simulation environment with zero
noise. After creating the method algorithm, the focus was on selecting and tuning the hyperparameters to
ensure that the concept worked. The primary hyperparameters examined were step size and Gaussian
kernel bandwidth. As regards the step size, smaller sizes resulted in slower convergence but allowed for
greater precision. This was because the controller could not move in an amount below the step size, so if
more refined solutions existed close to the current best, it would be unable to reach them. For the noise-
free environment, smaller steps on the order of 0.5 degrees performed well, as illustrated in Figure 28. For

 43

this simulation, the supervisory controller updated 𝛼 every 2 seconds instead of every 10 seconds, which
expedited the simulation process given the smaller update amount. These results indicate that with the
smaller step size, the drum positions and tilt metrics converged to values similar to those in the simulation
results shown in Figure 26. This suggests that the finer adjustments did not have a major impact in this
scenario.

Figure 28. Simulation performance without noise of the optimization tilt controller, with -5% tilt applied
to Quadrant 1.

 Once the controller was moved to a noisy simulation environment, its performance deteriorated.
There were several reasons for this. First, the value of the measured tilt corresponding to 𝛼 ∗ was highly
susceptible to noise. If the measured tilt was artificially low due to noise, it would be challenging to find a
better solution, effectively creating a noise-induced local minimum. This occurred because the change in
the objective function could be smaller than or comparable to the magnitude of the noise, depending on
the selected step size. Second, the gradient estimate was also vulnerable to noise, as outliers could
significantly distort it. Both issues were mitigated using the rolling history of past values. This approach
was beneficial because if an artificially low measured tilt was recorded, the rolling history would
eventually discard the noisy value, allowing the algorithm to escape from the local minimum.
 Even with the rolling history, there were still issues due to small step size selected from the noise-free
environment. A primary reason for this is that small steps result in correspondingly small improvements
in the tilt metric, and these were difficult to detect in the presence of noise. To address this issue, the step
size had to be significantly increased until the resulting effect was large enough to be detected in the
noise. This tuning process involved incrementally increasing the step size until the controller was able to
consistently reduce the tilt metric, which required a step size of 5 degrees. Although this does not seem to
have significantly impacted the resulting tilt metric, the larger step size may lack the capability in some
scenarios to achieve the fine-tuned accuracy that a smaller step size can provide.
 One possible solution to this tradeoff would have been to use an adaptive step-size algorithm to
balance convergence speed, signal-to-noise ratio, and precision. In applications with less noise, this
approach could prove highly beneficial. However, the large noise magnitude seen in these experiments
would have rendered this adaptive algorithm useless, and so it was not pursued further.

 44

 The other key hyperparameter to tune was kernel bandwidth. Although it impacted performance, this
impact was less significant than that of the step size. An effective value for the kernel bandwidth was to
be similar to the step size, thus ensuring that weights quickly dropped off for points more than one step
size away.
 One conclusion from analyzing the results of the optimization approach is that, among the three
algorithms, this one is the most sensitive to noise. Therefore, the same tilt condition, when subjected to a
different sequence of noise values, could produce a very different simulation outcome. Despite this, the
overall trends are expected to remain similar.
 After implementing and testing the optimization supervisory controller, the following observations
and conclusions can be made about its performance and development:
 1. The algorithm is entirely data-driven, requiring no prior knowledge or understanding of the
 system dynamics for development.
 2. It can be easily adapted to other applications.
 3. The implementation is straightforward, with just tuning needed to achieve functionality.
 4. The algorithm does not depend on the quality or accuracy of models or underlying
 assumptions.
 5. It may be highly sensitive to noise when the signal-to-noise ratio is low.
 6. The behavior is non-linear and unpredictable, influenced by the stochastic nature of the
 measurements and the algorithm itself.
 7. The algorithm operates slowly, as it must learn the system dynamics in real-time.
 8. The performance cannot be accelerated or enhanced by increased computational resources, as
 it runs at the pace dictated by the physical processes involved.
  4.5.4. Reinforcement Learning Approach
4.5.4.1.      Algorithm
 The RL supervisory controller leverages the RL framework to develop an effective supervisory
control strategy. RL is an ML method in which an agent learns to make optimal decisions based on
observed states. It does so through trial and error, using feedback from its own actions and experiences to
improve its performance over time. An RL algorithm consists of two main components: the environment
and the agent. The environment represents the system (or simulation of the system) in which optimal
decisions are to be learned; the agent is the decision-maker interacting with this environment. The agent is
also the supervisory controller in this application. The learning process involves a cycle of observations,
actions, and rewards. The environment provides observations that describe its current state to the agent. In
response, the agent takes actions within the environment to achieve desired outcomes. Finally, the
environment gives rewards based on the quality of the agent’s decisions.
 The environment was defined by running a simulation—offline from any final testing—with
simulated tilt. To ensure that the trained agent would be robust against any size and type of tilt, the
amount and quadrant of tilt were randomly selected and changed every 10 minutes. This effort tested tilt
compensation exclusively at full power, so the agent was trained solely at full power. If additional power
levels were required, the environment would need to include a range of power levels in addition to the
simulated tilt, or the RL agent would need to be trained in an online manner as the reactor reached new
power levels. If it is desired to operate at other power levels, additional training time would be expected
for the algorithm to converge.
 In designing the agent, one of the initial decisions was whether to implement it as functional or
dynamic. In this context, functional means that the output is purely a function of the measured states,

 45

making it independent of prior output values, 𝛼(𝑡) = 𝑓(𝑄(𝑡), 𝜃(𝑡)), where 𝑓(⋅) is the agent’s action. This
approach allows for rapid decisions based on the overall situation. Conversely, dynamic means that the
output is constrained by the previous output, 𝛼(𝑡) = 𝛼(𝑡 − 1) + 𝑓(𝑄(𝑡), 𝜃(𝑡)). One way in which these
two approaches differed was in how they interpreted the bounds of the RL actions (a required step in
setting up the environment). In the functional mode, the controller could select any values of 𝛼 within the
predetermined global range. In the dynamic mode, the bounds restrict how much α can change at each
decision point. This effort opted for the functional mode so as to avoid limiting the capability to make
global decisions.
 The bounds themselves presented another challenge to implementation. Since the Balancing Drum
Controller focuses solely on relative differences between virtual drum angles, adding or subtracting a
constant to α has no effect. Therefore, it was crucial to ensure that the agent could not select redundant
actions, such as [0, 10, 0, 10] and [10, 20, 10, 20]. Without this careful consideration, the agent would
have faced significant difficulty in learning, given that many actions could yield the same result. The
solution implemented involved parameterizing the agent’s actions to prescribe three non-negative values
and one zero value for the four elements in the vector. This worked because ensuring that the minimum
value equals zero resulted in every action being unique.
 The agent utilized a neural network for its policy, trained using the proximal policy optimization RL
algorithm [45]. This algorithm is effective at preventing the agent from making drastic changes to its
strategy all at once, resulting in a more stable learning process. Additionally, proximal policy
optimization is known for its efficiency, as it can learn and achieve good performance with less required
training time.
 The interactions between the environment and the agent included observations, actions, and rewards.
The observations consisted of the quadrant powers, 𝑄(𝑡), and drum angles, 𝜃(𝑡). The actions provided to
the environment were the offset values, 𝛼(𝑡). The actions and observations were normalized to fall within
the range of -1 to 1. The rewards were designed to be similar to the tilt metric, but with some notable
differences for scaling purposes:

 One challenge in RL is that during training, the agent and environment pair typically only experience
a single condition at a time, making the training heavily dependent on the randomness of the simulation.

 46

To stabilize this process, a common approach is to vectorize the environments. This means running
multiple environments in parallel with the same agent to collect data from a range of conditions. By doing
so, the agent can gather more diverse experiences faster under a fixed policy before making an update.
This results in significantly more stable learning and allows the agent to leverage modern multi-core
processors, thereby making better use of computational resources.
 To achieve this, the effort vectorized the COMMAND software platform, allowing many simulations
to run in parallel. Specifically for this task, 48 simulations were run simultaneously, enabling the agent to
experience 48 different tilt conditions and collect data before making any updates. This approach allowed
the agent to encounter more diverse situations. The implementation was highly modular, ensuring that
other efforts using different simulations or models could utilize the COMMAND platform and similar
tools without requiring major modifications.

 One of the challenges in implementing this algorithm was the computational cost. The RL agent was

 Based on the implementation and results, some observations about the RL supervisory controller were
made:
 1. The algorithm requires system models in order to create the RL environment. However, since
 models are often needed during the design phase, this may not be a significant burden.
 2. Setting up the environment and training the algorithm necessitates knowledge of ML and RL.
 3. While some components of the RL algorithm and general framework can be adapted from
 other systems, customization and fine-tuning are essential to achieve good performance.
 4. Training the algorithm incurs high computational costs, although this process can often be
 conducted offline. Once trained, the computational costs associated with inference are
 minimal.
 5. The RL controller can learn to make optimal (or at least high-performing) decisions in
 challenging use cases that might otherwise be difficult to manage.
 6. Performance should remain predictable unless the controller encounters scenarios not covered
 during training.
 7. The learned controller can make rapid decisions because it has previously encountered
 similar conditions and knows the appropriate actions to take.

 4.6. Discussion
 All three supervisory control methods were able to successfully reduce reactor core power tilt, and in
most cases reduce it to the same degree. However, the methods differ in their development effort and in
their performance, even though they eventually achieve a similar end state.
 RB control required the largest time investment—specifically from an expert familiar with the
functionality and system dynamics of MACS and MARVEL. Most of this time was spent developing and
iterating through control rules tailored specifically to MACS and MARVEL. The computational and data
requirements were relatively minor, with simulations being required for each rule addition or iteration.
The optimization controller required the least amount of development time and effort. Most notably,
expert knowledge of the controlled system was not required for development of the algorithm itself. Only
a small amount of system expert knowledge was required for final implementation and tuning of the
algorithm. Similar to RB control, the computational resources and data requirements for the optimization
control were relatively minor, limited only to the simulation time required for algorithm tuning. RL
required a moderate amount of development effort in terms of personnel time spent on the controller.
Notably, expert knowledge of RL is required for implementation. In addition, developing an RL
controller requires large computational resources. This specific effort utilized a large number of high-
performance computing hours to train the controller. While not all these hours involved human
supervision, it still represents a significant development cost.
 While all three controllers did effectively control reactor core power tilt and eventually resulted in
similar tilt metrics and drum positions, the path taken by each controller to achieve each state differed.
The RB controller was the slowest of the three to reach its minimal reactor tilt, mainly because it was
limited to making small changes in drum offset at each timestep of the controller, with no knowledge of
whether the system was improving in tilt, just whether or not its rules had been satisfied. Also, as
previously noted, a flaw in the control logic was discovered that allowed a drum to reach its maximum
position limit, meaning that an additional control rule would be required for any future use of this
controller. Meanwhile, the optimization controller was the slowest of the three to respond to any reactor
tilt, but not necessarily the slowest to reach its end state. Because the controller had to first learn the
dynamics of the system, it took time for the controller to detect and appropriately respond to the tilt.
However, once the optimization controller did learn the system dynamics, it quickly drove the system to
the minimum tilt state, aided in part by the gradient information the controller collects as it operates.
Finally, the RL controller was by far the fastest of the three controllers to respond to and resolve the
reactor tilt. Because the RL controller is trained on all possible tilt scenarios and has an embedded control
policy for each of these scenarios, it almost immediately responds to the detection of tilt and quickly
directs the reactor to a state that will minimize it.
 A note needs to be made on the tilt metric when comparing simulation and hardware results. Namely,
the tilt metric is higher for simulation than for hardware. This is presumed to be the case because
simulation uses virtual sensors, with no interference between virtual sensor locations. In hardware,
physical photodiodes are used for measuring the total magnitude of light present. Because there is no
physical isolation of the light emitted by a core quadrant from the photodiodes of another quadrant, light
bleed between quadrants makes the measured tilt appear to be less than what was actually present. This
was not compensated for in this testing, but is an important consideration for future hardware testing.
 While this work is not explicitly focused on comparing the performance of the developed controllers
in regard to simulation and hardware, it is worthwhile to mention some of the lessons learned when
deploying the controllers to the MACS hardware. In general, deployment of all the controllers presented
in this report—the Balancing Drum Controller and RB, optimization, and RL supervisory controllers—
was very straightforward and went smoothly. No major challenges were encountered in getting each
controller to run successfully. However, it should be noted that this smoothness and success was built in
part on the previous efforts of both ASI and MRP in developing MACS into a useful testing platform

 49

[2,4,5,10,11,12]. While deployment of the controllers to hardware did go smoothly, one challenge was
discovered with hardware testing—specifically as it relates to photodiode calibration. It was discovered
during testing that controller performance was quite sensitive to photodiodes calibration. This made it
necessary to implement a regimented calibration procedure for the photodiodes for each round of
hardware testing conducted.
 As mentioned earlier, in addition to the testing already shown with a -5% tilt applied to Quadrant 1,
similar experiments were conducted to demonstrate that the performance of the tilt controllers was not a
one-off case limited to a single quadrant or magnitude of tilt. Experiments were also run for a -5% tilt
applied to Quadrant 2, and a -2% tilt applied to Quadrant 1. The -5% tilt in the Quadrant 2 testing showed
the performance of all three tilt controllers to be largely identical to that of the tilt controller for -5% tilt
applied to Quadrant 1. The same conclusions previously presented for the three controllers applied to the
tilt in Quadrant 1 remain valid for the tilt applied to Quadrant 2.
 Applying a -2% tilt to Quadrant 1 resulted in mixed performance from the three tilt controllers.
Simulation performance was largely similar for all three controllers when compared to the -5% tilt results.
The same trends observed for each control method when applied to the larger tilt were also observed
under the smaller tilt in simulation. However, hardware performance was slightly more mixed. RB control
on hardware responded much the same as it did for the -5% tilt testing, with the hardware controller being
slower to respond than in simulation yet remaining effective at reducing tilt. The optimization controller
is where the main differences were observed. The hardware implementation of the optimization controller
was largely ineffective. No measurable tilt control was achieved. The assumed reason is because, with the
small amount of tilt applied to the core, the photodiodes lacked the sensitivity to detect the tilt at a
magnitude to which the optimization controller could respond. Lastly, the hardware implementation of the
RL controller also showed degraded performance under -2% tilt when compared to its simulation
counterpart and the -5% tilt testing. In this case, “degraded performance” means that the RL controller
actions result in a tilt metric that is larger than that seen in the simulation results. As this phenomenon
only appears on hardware testing at -2% tilt, it is assumed that the issue also stems from sensor resolution,
just as was assumed in the optimization controller case.

 5. CONCLUSIONS
 This collaborative effort between the MRP and ASI Program successfully demonstrated the utility of
the MACS/ViBRANT platform as a surrogate reactor for MARVEL and for use in the advanced reactor
space. Through the exploration of two key use cases, this report highlights significant strides made in
both reactor startup characterization and the development of supervisory control methods.
 The first use case focused on reactor startup physics testing using the MACS/ViBRANT platform as a
surrogate reactor for the MARVEL microreactor. The MACS platform's role as a surrogate reactor
facilitated the execution of startup physics tests, underscoring the importance of utilizing such a system to
refine hardware designs and improve software capabilities. This approach will ultimately reduce resource
waste during actual reactor deployments.
 The second use case focused on implementation of supervisory control methods to address core tilt, a
critical concern for reactor performance and safety. By evaluating three distinct approaches—RB
methods, optimization techniques, and RL algorithms—this effort provided valuable insights into their
respective strengths and the challenges regarding their use with advanced reactors. Comparative analysis
of these methods not only highlighted their applicability in advanced reactor operations, but also
established a robust framework for future supervisory control applications.
 Overall, the accomplishments outlined in this report bridge the gap between theoretical reactor design
and actual startup and control operations, thus paving the way for safer, more efficient reactor operations
capable of meeting the growing demand for reliable energy solutions.
