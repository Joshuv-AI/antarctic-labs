The phantastic organ: predictive processing in the brain

Karl Friston, Thomas Parr

Abstract

Neuroscience has witnessed a curious inversion over the past decades, with a switch from views of the brain as abstracting information from sensory input to a view of the brain as a constructive organ, actively generating explanations for – and sampling – the sensorium. Perhaps this inversion is not strange, but a reversion to the ‘facts of perception’ as laid out by Helmholtz. His enduring view of perception as inference – to the best explanation – now dominates the neurosciences in the form of the Bayesian brain paradigm and the Free Energy Principle. This review follows the story from Helmholtz’s unconscious inference to modern day active inference. In particular, it uses the history of predictive coding – and how it has been generalized over the years – to furnish a framework that brings theory and empirical study in close contact, through generating mechanistic (anatomical and physiological) hypotheses about sense-making in the brain.

Introduction

“...by the power of phantasy we see Colors in a Dream, or a mad Man sees things before him which are not there; or when we see Fire by striking the Eye, or see Colors like the Eye of a Peacock’s Feather, by pressing our Eyes in either corner whilst we look the other way.” – Isaac Newton, 1704, The First Book of Opticks Part II, p118

Predictive coding can mean different things in different disciplines. It is therefore interesting to track the history of predictive coding from its inception to see the different ways it underwrites current thinking about functional brain architectures; ranging from synaptic self-organization to structure learning. The increasing compass of predictive coding presents great opportunities but also challenges. The opportunities at hand are the ability to unite disciplines and perspectives on common ground. The challenges rest on understanding one discipline’s view of predictive coding from the perspective of another. The aim of this review is to foreground the opportunities (and challenges) by unpacking the many aspects of predictive coding as they have emerged over time.

The core theme that ties all these aspects together is that the brain is a ‘phantastic’ organ, in the sense that its function depends upon an internal model of an external reality. In effect, this model generates an internal reality. The ‘ph’ spelling of phantasy emphasizes both an archaic English spelling used by Newton (see above) to describe forms of visual imagery not directly attributable to the properties of light, and links to (psychoanalytic) ideas from developmental psychology (), in which an unconscious internal ‘psychic’ reality is adapted to an external reality. The imperative to align one’s internal model with their external world is central to each of the themes we examine in this paper.

In what follows, we begin with advances in theoretical physics and information theory in the mid-20th century, which portended the development of predictive coding – first as a statistical device for data compression and then as a theory of neural circuitry. Implicit in this theory is the notion that our brains engage in a kind of inference about the causes of our sensations. The mechanistic implications of this idea make sense of the laminar connectivity patterns observed within and between regions of cerebral cortex. This allows one to interpret ‘descending’ axonal projections from multimodal cortical to primary sensory regions of the brain as conveying predictions that are corrected by prediction errors communicated by the reciprocal ‘ascending’ projections.

We next turn to precision – an important quantity that helps to determine the confidence with which a prediction is made and therefore the relevance of different kinds of prediction error. Neurobiologically, precision manifests as a gain-like synaptic modulation of neurons representing prediction errors (i.e., superficial pyramidal cells), effectively turning up or down the volume of the prediction error signal conveyed to higher cortical regions. As our brains optimize precision, we see the emergence of phenomena like representational sharpening, sensory attenuation and selective attention. Interestingly, aberrant precision estimation has been implicated in a wide range of neurological and psychiatric conditions, underlining the translational potential of predictive coding.

At this point in the story, one is well-placed to account for a range of neuroanatomical and physiological findings, but there is a key ingredient missing: action. Here, one must engage with the ways in which our brains account for an itinerant world, including changes that we ourselves bring about. In doing so, we arrive at active inference in which one can resolve precision-weighted prediction errors not only through altering our predictions but also through action, so that sensory samples conform to descending predictions. Physiologically, this calls for circuitry resembling that of a spinal or brainstem reflex arc that ‘corrects’ proprioceptive input from stretch receptors by altering muscle tone. With these ingredients in place – prediction error minimization, precision modulation, and reflexive fulfillment of predictions through action – one can start to understand the links between predictive processing, reward learning, and even the recent re-emergence of predictive coding in machine learning.

Ultimately, the above turn out to follow from the same variational principles developed in the 1950s. With this understanding, one can revisit this history in reverse to understand how each of these developments is in fact a special case of the Free Energy Principle. One can then go further and use this principle to point the way forward in understanding how we select among alternative futures (i.e., how we plan based upon the anticipated consequences of our actions) and even how we optimize the structure of the internal world model under which we predict, perceive, and act upon our environment.

Predictive coding: a brief history

Two papers were published in 1955 that were to set the scene for a formal understanding of predictive processing in the brain some half-century later. The first introduced a simple and powerful path-integral approach to inference () that would be later known as the Feynman variational principle, variously referred to as the Jensen-Feynman or Gibbs-Bogoliubov-Feynman inequality (). The second heralded the inception of predictive coding (), following key developments in information theory (). Decades later, it transpired that both papers offered complementary answers to the same question: how do we make sense of data?

Predictive coding was originally developed as a powerful compression technique, that is still used in speech analysis today (). The notion of compression speaks to an intimate relationship between (Bayes) optimal communication, information theory and algorithmic complexity. Examples include the compression implicit in minimum description and message length formulations of efficient communication and representation (; ). Indeed, this line of thinking led to Solomonoff induction and notions of universal – albeit incomputable – computation (; ). A more pragmatic line of thinking can be traced through the principle of maximum efficiency that rests upon maximizing mutual information, under constraints (; ; ; ; ). For some, the implicit minimisation of complexity has played a foundational role in approaches to artificial intelligence and machine learning (; ). From a neuroscience perspective, these notions emerged as the principle of minimum redundancy, when considering the functional architecture of the visual cortex (; ), with attendant notions of inference to the best explanation, in terms of perception as inference or hypothesis testing (, ). See .

But what about predictive coding per se? The principles of predictive coding were first applied in neurobiology to neuronal circuits in the retina in the 1980s () and then generalized to hierarchical visual processing in the 1990s. The most explicit early formulation was provided by David Mumford (), who pre-empted subsequent formulations with remarkable precision:

“I put forward a hypothesis on the role of the reciprocal, topographic pathways between two cortical areas, one often a ‘higher’ area dealing with more abstract information about the world, the other ‘lower’, dealing with more concrete data. The higher area attempts to fit its abstractions to the data it receives from lower areas by sending back to them from its deep pyramidal cells a template reconstruction best fitting the lower level view [prediction]. The lower area attempts to reconcile the reconstruction of its view that it receives from higher areas with what it knows, sending back from its superficial pyramidal cells the features in its data which are not predicted by the higher area [prediction errors]. The whole calculation is done with all areas working simultaneously, but with order imposed by synchronous activity in the various top-down, bottom-up loops.” [Our insertions] ()

All the key architectural themes would re-emerge in subsequent formulations (; ; ; ; ); ranging from recurrent counter-streams of descending predictions and ascending prediction errors in cortical hierarchies to an emphasis on deep (i.e., the hierarchical) architectures with local computations at each level. See and .

Mumford and colleagues () pursued these ideas using particle filters and Bayesian belief propagation, which we will later see can be read as a form of predictive coding. In the interim, Rao and Ballard wrote their seminal paper on predictive coding in the visual cortex (). This, and a companion treatment (), were important for several reasons. One, possibly underappreciated, reason was the distinction between optimizing representations of external (a.k.a., hidden) states causing observations – i.e., inference – from the parameters encoding the underlying cause-effect structure – i.e., learning. In short, inference and learning were both explainable in terms of minimizing prediction error. The second contribution was to establish the equivalence between predictive coding and Kalman filtering. This meant predictive coding could be read as a special case of Bayesian filtering (; ; ; ); namely, sequential data assimilation under a state-space generative model. This extended the compass of predictive coding to include the Bayesian brain hypothesis () and, indeed, motor control (; ). The latter link was gracefully provided by a formal equivalence between Kalman filtering and linear quadratic control, as noted by Kalman himself (; ).

Retrospectively, the Bayesian filtering perspective is compelling, when one appreciates that an idealized particle filter is an extended Kalman or variational filter (; ). Intuitively, particle filtering (and related procedures like unscented filtering ()) use realizations of lots of little predictive coding schemes to obtain an ensemble estimate. However, if one assumes that one has an infinite number of particles, the ensuing probability density dynamics can be written as a variational Bayesian filter (), which just is predictive coding.

Around the turn of the century, the Bayesian brain hypothesis started to attract more attention (; ); echoing – in one sense – earlier ideas of perception as hypothesis testing and analysis by synthesis (, ; ). These ideas themselves spoke to Helmholtz’s notion of unconscious inference (, ) nicely summarized as:

“Objects are always imagined as being present in the field of vision as would have to be there in order to produce the same impression on the nervous mechanism”.

Indeed, Peter Dayan and Geoffrey Hinton created a Helmholtz machine () predicated on variational free energy – introduced by and used by Feynman (, ) – to convert an intractable (path integral) inference problem into a tractable (computable) optimisation problem. In short, variational free energy bounds the likelihood of some data under a model of how those data were caused, such that minimizing free energy implicitly identifies the best explanation for those data (c.f., Inference to the best explanation). So, was the Helmholtz machine a predictive coding machine in disguise? Probably not. The Helmholtz machine and variants – such as modern day variational autoencoders () – came from machine learning. The emphasis here is on learning, as opposed to inference or state-estimation afforded by Bayesian (e.g., particle) filters (; ). If we roll forward a few decades, these early Bayesian learning machines are now seen as learning to infer, sometimes referred to as amortizing inference (; ). In other words, inference optimizes beliefs about the causes of some data on a case-by-case basis. Amortization learns a direct mapping from data to beliefs from multiple cases.

Inference and learning are best described in relation to a generative model. Any scheme that explains or predicts (sensory) data must entail a generative model: i.e., a probabilistic model that can generate the observable (sensed) consequences of unobservable (hidden) causes. The generative model covers different kinds of hidden (a.k.a., random) variables; such as states of the world that are hidden behind the sensory apparatus (e.g., sensory epithelia for the skull-bound brain). These include the states, parameters and structure of the generative model, which have to be inferred, learned and selected, respectively. In order to optimize or estimate these unknowns, it is necessary to encode the uncertainty inherent in those estimates, so that uncertainty about one set of unknowns informs the estimates of others.

The importance of being precise: neuromodulation and representational sharpening

Within inference, one can distinguish between the expected state of the world and the uncertainty associated with that estimate. This uncertainty – or its complement, precision – equips predictive coding with an inferential aspect; much in the same way that a statistician measures some effect size in relation to its standard error. The requisite estimate of confidence or credibility is often referred to as precision. Mathematically, it is easier to deal with precision than its complement such as uncertainty (; ; ), variance (; ), entropy (; ), volatility (; ), et cetera. One reason to think in terms of precision inherits from its role in predictive coding as a rate constant or learning rate. We will return to this later when considering reward prediction errors. At present, the separation of various unknowns – into expectations, precisions and parameters – lends itself to a graceful interpretation in terms of perceptual inference, attention and learning as illustrated in .

The separation in was one of the key commitments of an early formulation () of cortical responses – predicated on predictive coding – that would later form the basis of the free energy principle (). In brief, not only did one have to estimate the latent states and parameters – under a given model structure – one also had to estimate the confidence, credibility or precision afforded those estimates. This was important because it gave rise to different strands of predictive coding research and, arguably, a divergence of research agendas.

From the perspective of engineering, precision underwrites the Kalman gain applied to prediction errors during the update step of a Kalman filter. The Kalman gain is effectively the ratio of precisions afforded sensory prediction errors relative to state prediction errors, under a generative model. [A generative model is just a joint probability distribution over unobservable causes and observable consequences. It is generally decomposed into the likelihood of consequences, given their causes, and a prior over causes]. In predictive coding, precisions are associated with expected squared prediction errors because the prediction errors score the amplitude of random fluctuations, which have the attribute of precision. If the sensory precision or Kalman gain is high, then sensory prediction errors are afforded more weight and are thereby preferentially selected for updating estimates of latent states: see . This means the implicit Bayesian belief updating rests upon the precision of prior beliefs (i.e., state noise), relative to the precision of sensory observations (i.e., observation noise).

This important aspect of predictive coding – and its context sensitivity – is largely ignored in some treatments; e.g., (; ). Other disciplines have focused almost exclusively on precision (); particularly, in cognitive neuroscience and computational psychiatry; e.g., (; ; ; ; ; ). Key applications in cognitive neuroscience associate the optimization of precision with attentional selection (and sensory attenuation) (; ; ), offering a formal account of things like representational sharpening and biased competition (; ). See . In computational psychiatry, aberrant precision weighting or (postsynaptic) gain control is a dominant theme in understanding psychopathology in terms of false inference (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ): see references in (). See . Interestingly, hierarchical (c.f., metacognitive) precision weighting has also been a focus in the emerging field of neurophenomenology (; ; ).

To contextualize the work in these fields, we have to consider two generalizations of predictive coding. Thus far, we have a formulation of predictive coding as a particular instance of Bayesian filtering or, technically, approximate Bayesian inference. Approximate Bayesian inference refers to the optimization of a variational bound on the evidence for the requisite generative model used to compress or represent sensory data. This is the variational free energy due to Feynman (; ; ; ) that rests upon specifying a functional form for posterior beliefs (usually a Gaussian form, leading to a generalization of the Laplace assumption). See for a discussion of free energy in this setting.

Technically, variational free energy converts an impossible marginalization problem – for any inference – into a tractable optimisation problem, where one minimizes variational free energy and thereby maximizes model evidence (a.k.a., marginal likelihood) to within a bound approximation. In machine learning, negative variational free energy is known as an evidence lower bound or ELBO (). The details are not important here, other than to appreciate that (precision-weighted) prediction errors – that define predictive coding – are simply the gradients of variational free energy with respect to the unknown quantities (). However, this is only true under the Laplace assumption, which assumes that all random fluctuations are well-behaved; i.e., are drawn from a Gaussian distribution. While this seems a severe criterion, note that almost all random fluctuations in continuous space are Gaussian up to a quadratic approximation of their (log) probability densities around a mode. With this simple assumption, one can then write down a predictive coding or Bayesian filtering scheme for any generative model and minimize variational free energy with respect to the unknown states, parameters and structure. This variational formulation provides the calculus that grandfathers any implementation of the Bayesian brain under Gaussian assumptions. On this view, one could define predictive coding as the variational inversion of a continuous state-space model under Gaussian assumptions about random fluctuations. Is this a sufficient definition?

Not quite. One has to ask two things of the generative model. First, what is the order of the dynamics? In other words, much like the brain encodes position and velocity (and higher of orders of motion), one can ask how many orders of motion are implicit in the state-space models used for predictive coding. The answer – for a vanilla Kalman filtering scheme – is two orders of motion; namely, position and velocity. But why stop there? This leads to the first generalization of hierarchical predictive coding to accommodate high order dynamics, such as acceleration and jerk. This in turn leads to generalized predictive coding or filtering (; , ) that becomes especially important for the second generalization; namely, active filtering or inference; e.g., (; ; ).

From unconscious inference to active inference

This generalization reflects the enactive turn at the beginning of the century and asks, could one deploy predictive coding in the setting of motor control? In one sense – following Todorov and colleagues (; ) – we already know the answer: one simply uses a predictive coding scheme to control the motor plant (). In engineering, this is commonplace, and is variously known as path-integral control (; ), model predictive control (; ; ; ) or control as inference (; ; ). Indeed, in neurobiology this perspective emerges in perceptual control theory () and the equilibrium point hypothesis () – see () for a recent discussion of these schemes in relation to active inference. Interestingly, one of the key architects of predictive coding in the brain has now turned to active inference and sensing, in terms of active predictive coding ().

We now arrive at a generalization of predictive coding – under continuous state-space models – that can be summarized as the minimization of variational free energy under the Laplace assumption through both perception and action. The active element is facilitated by the use of higher orders of motion, as we now have an opportunity to resolve sensory prediction errors via the gradients of sensory input with respect to action – which depend upon higher orders of motion affecting hidden states, e.g., in a Newtonian world one acts through inducing accelerations by applying forces.

Predictive coding, so defined, can handle difficult problems that entail nonlinear, hierarchical models with separation of temporal scales. See ref () for an example of recognizing structured speech or syntax, under the right kind of generative model. provides an example of the same generalized predictive coding scheme that was used to simulate handwriting. It is at this point we can now turn to predictive processing.

From predictive coding to predictive processing

Predictive processing was introduced in Andy Clark’s seminal 2013 treatment of the predictive brain (). One might ask why Andy chose predictive processing, which has enjoyed enormous uptake in the philosophy and cognitive neuroscience literature. The reason was subtle but compelling. He did not want to limit the scope of prediction to mere predictive coding formulations that ignored active sensing of the kind emphasized by . See also (). For example:

“Each movement we make by which we alter the appearance of objects should be thought of as an experiment designed to test whether we have understood correctly the invariant relations of the phenomena before us, that is, their existence in definite spatial relations.” ‘The Facts of Perception’ (1878) in The Selected Writings of Hermann von Helmholtz, Ed. R. Karl, Middletown: Wesleyan University Press, 1971 p. 384

On the other hand, he did not want to burden predictive processing with the broader commitments of the free energy principle based upon Feynman’s variational principle (), which was emerging at that time. He therefore focused on predictive coding in an enactive and embodied setting (, ). Furthermore, subsequent work quickly highlighted the importance of precision (, , , ; ), such that predictive processing became synonymous with the minimisation of precision-weighted prediction errors. This was important because it invites cognitive neuroscientists – interested in attention and salience – to engage with predictive processing accounts: e.g., (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ).

On a philosophical note, the equivalence between minimizing precision-weighted prediction errors, minimizing variational free energy and maximizing model evidence leads to the nice notion of self-evidencing (). In other words, predictive coding may be the mechanism by which we gather or accumulate evidence (; ) for our models of the lived world.

In a translational context, it also speaks to the importance of predictive coding for explaining psychopathology. The story here is fairly straightforward: If psychology can be cast as inference to the best explanation (), then psychopathology can be cast as false inference. Here, false inference can be read in its simplest sense of type I and II errors. Type I errors are when one infers something is there when it is not (e.g., hallucinations and delusions). Conversely, type II errors involve inferring something is not there when it is (e.g., neglect syndromes and dissociative disorders). As noted above, this led to a whole literature in computational psychiatry; in which many neurological and psychiatric conditions (see ) were cast in terms of aberrant precision weighting: e.g., (; ; ; ; ; ; ). One interesting (aberrant salience) story () rests on the notion of reward prediction errors.

Predictive coding in reward learning

With the emergence of predictive coding as a scheme for belief-updating in the brain, progress was being made in the field of reinforcement learning (; ; ; ; ; ). Much of this progress turned upon the notion that dopamine encoded a reward prediction error. This was perhaps a little unfortunate, because it gave the specious impression that dopamine was encoding a prediction error in the spirit of predictive coding. However, when applying the principles of predictive coding to decision-making – in the sense of planning as inference () – it became evident that dopamine was not encoding prediction errors per se but the precision of inferences about choices or plans (,). Intuitively, this would look much like a reward prediction error in some circumstances, as achieving an unexpected reward might increase one’s confidence in the current course of action. In contrast, an outcome that is more aversive (or less rewarding) than anticipated, might decrease one’s confidence that they are pursuing a likely course of action, thereby signaling what looks like a negative prediction error. To this extent, the precision account of dopamine makes the same empirical predictions as the reward prediction error account. However, one can go beyond this. If phasic dopamine signals changes in policy precision, then one would expect similar dopaminergic responses to (non-rewarding) information that informs choice behavior. This is compatible with the literature reviewed in () in which (non-rewarding) cues – that impose constraints upon the likely course of action – can themselves trigger dopamine release.

An important distinction between dopamine, as encoding reward prediction error versus policy precision, rests on the fact that reward prediction error formulations inherit from reinforcement learning, while active inference encompass both inference and learning. For example – in the setting of hypodopaminergic clinical states – the difference between a bradykinetic presentation of Parkinson’s disease, before and after dopaminergic medication, is evident before any learning can occur (). This clinical observation lies outside the explanatory scope of reward-learning but is neatly accommodated in a precision-based account under which imprecise motor planning results in impoverished movement.

Subsequent interpretations of dopaminergic responses focused on the implicit encoding of precision, novelty and related quantities (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). This speaks to an important link between precision, Kalman gain and learning rate.

Recall from above that the relative precision of priors and sensory evidence determines the Kalman gain and thereby, the rate of belief updating. A simple dimensional analysis of precision – under any state-space model – shows that precision has the units of per time and therefore scores the plasticity or learning rate in any scheme that can read as predictive coding. On this view, dopamine acts as a learning rate (; ), consistent with its role in working memory and gain control (; ; ; ; ).

In predictive coding terms, this suggests certain dopaminergic functions – e.g., of D1 receptors in prefrontal cortex () – are to mediate the precision-weighting of precision-weighted prediction errors. This view of dopaminergic function admits a plurality of functional roles (), depending upon where precision-weighting is mediated (e.g., mesocortical, mesolimbic, nigrostriatal, et cetera); thereby linking its role in working memory (), planning (; ), addiction (), Parkinson’s disease (), schizophrenia () and so on. These roles can be formulated – under predictive coding – in terms of aberrant salience (), cortical gain control (), and excitation-inhibition balance (; ), and concomitant failures of selective attention and sensory attenuation (; ; ).

Predictive coding in machine learning

In a further turn of affairs, the machine learning community [re]turned to predictive coding in the past decade, with an interesting twist. In brief, the kind of predictive coding one finds in this literature focuses on the problems encountered with back propagation of errors: e.g., vanishing gradients (), overfitting (Sengupta and ), and so on. The key thing this field takes from predictive coding is the local (variational free) energy minimisation at each level in a deep (hierarchical) generative model: c.f., (). This means, in principle, predictive coding offers an alternative to back-propagation of errors that, in principle, should be more efficient than current machine learning procedures (; ,; , ).

The twist here is that instead of optimizing the structure and functional form of the generative model, machine learning variants apply the principles of predictive coding to a standard neural network architecture. Although there is current excitement about the potential of predictive coding in this setting, it has yet to be established at scale. From the perspective of predictive coding, the focus on learning the parameters (i.e., weights) of a neural network means these applications are largely limited to static models (that preclude dynamics). Another issue – with this application of predictive coding – is a failure to encode uncertainty about the model parameters, which means computation of the requisite variational free energy gradients (or fixed-point solutions) is compromised. This follows because predictive coding – in its generalized form – requires probability densities over all random variables, including model parameters: c.f., .

Having said this, there are interesting developments in connecting transformer architectures to Bayesian filtering, where attention heads can be cast as estimates of precision or Kalman gain (). This speaks to a nice convergence of machine learning and computational neuroscience on the mechanisms of attentional selection (; ; ). More generally, it completes the convergence of predictive coding () and Bayesian filtering under Feynman’s variational principle of least action (); in other words, there is a general account of predictive processing that simply invokes the minimisation of a variational bound on the evidence for generative models; namely, the free energy principle.

A reverse history of predictive coding: neurobiology from first principles

In retrospect, we can now review the history of predictive coding through its generalizations. Retrospectively, the starting point is the free energy principle, which points to predictive coding as a universal description of any open (brain-like) system that self-organizes to some characteristic states. The mathematical details are not important here. The key thing to take away is that any (multidimensional random dynamical) system – from a postsynaptic process () to a person () – can be described as minimizing its variational free energy (). This description rests upon associating the internal states (e.g., neuronal activity and synaptic efficacy) with the parameters of some Bayesian beliefs about the external causes of sensory inputs (including the system’s own actions). If we make the further assumption that the random fluctuations – inherent in any random dynamical system – are (by central limit theorem) Gaussian, then we have predictive coding. In short, anything that exists in the sense of possessing characteristic states can be described as if it is engaged in predictive coding (; ): see .

This move elevates predictive coding from some ad hoc process theory to a first principles account; or at least an application of first principles [see for a detailed analysis of this distinction]. This is important because questions like “what is the evidence for predictive coding?” can be reframed as “how is predictive coding instantiated in this brain?”. In other words, key questions are less about whether predictive coding is an apt description; they are about how predictive coding is implemented mechanistically-and under which generative model-in a substrate-dependent fashion (, , ). This is sometimes discussed in terms of mortal computation (, ). Furthermore, the same questions can be asked at multiple scales; ranging from cortical and subcortical hierarchies, down to the intracellular mechanisms that underwrite synaptic processes-and possibly subcellular predictive coding mechanisms: e.g., (, , ).

The above implies the following taxonomy of principles and processes we have encountered so far. The free energy principle is a means of specifying the behavior of systems in terms of the ‘beliefs’ one part of that system holds about another. This formulation turns upon an internal model, against which free energy is evaluated. The notion of internal generative models-to understand perception and behavior-in a broad, philosophical, setting underwrites the field of predictive processing in which one seeks to align an internal model to an external world. Active inference emerges from the free energy principle, when bringing about this alignment through action. When one commits to specific forms for the generative model, one arrives at theories of brain function. For instance, a commitment to Gaussian fluctuations implies predictive coding. At this point, it is possible to express belief-updating through a series of differential equations (e.g., Bayesian filters), whose solutions furnish the predictions and errors one would expect to see under predictive coding. One can then go further and propose process theories, about the instantiation of predictive coding in neuronal networks.

The equations in and are special cases of the paths of least action in . Their functional form equips predictive coding with the ability to make some clear and principled predictions about what one would expect to see empirically, at various scales in the brain. For example, one would expect to see some form of hierarchical anatomy, within which neuronal dynamics couple hierarchical levels in a recurrent fashion (; ; ). The requisite forward and backward neuronal message-passing should show some fundamental asymmetries in terms of their sources and targets (e.g., laminar specificity in the cortex) (; ; ; ; ). Dynamically, one would predict that forward connections are linear or driving in nature because the dynamics (black box equations in ) are linear in the prediction errors. Conversely, we would expect the backward predictions to be nonlinear and modulatory (; ; ), because the dynamics of prediction errors (red box equations in ) are nonlinear in the predictions.

However, predictive coding goes further than heuristic notions of providing top-down, modulatory or contextual guidance. This is because the nonlinearities in predictive coding come in two separable flavors. The first is due to nonlinearities in the generative model (see ). In other words, latent states interact and conspire in a nonlinear way to generate sensations. One simple example is visual occlusion (; ). This kind of nonlinearity is distinct from the precision-weighting of the prediction errors that can be associated with attentional selection. This distinction is highlighted in that considers predictions of precision, which themselves predict things like representational sharpening, and related phenomena such as biased competition (; ; ; ). This architecture suggests that thalamocortical projections instantiate precision or gain control, where: “This selective amplification is implemented by a cooperative mechanism requiring thalamic input from the pulvinar” (). Interestingly, a similar parallel or dual architecture – mediating predictions of content and precision, respectively – is found in thalamocortical projections to the prefrontal cortex: “The largely parallel thalamo-cortical pathways terminated among distinct and laminar-specific neurochemical classes of inhibitory neurons that differ markedly in inhibitory control” (). In turn, this implicates inhibitory interneurons in the deployment of precision. See for a related discussion of descending pathways from hippocampus.

One can now go further and make predictions about the physiological concomitants of these functional asymmetries. For example, if forward connections are largely linear (i.e., driving) and backward connections are nonlinear (i.e., modulatory), one would expect a period halving or increase in the characteristic frequencies of forward, relative to backward afferents (). This prediction was subsequently verified empirically and is now almost a predictive coding meme: e.g., (; ). Predictions that have subsequently been confirmed are not restricted to neurophysiology. For example, in neuroanatomy: “a neural inference arising from the earliest formulations of predictive coding is that the source populations of forward and backward pathways should be completely separate, given their functional distinction; this aspect of circuitry – that neurons with extrinsically bifurcating axons do not project in both directions – has only recently been confirmed.” () p. 1792. As time has passed, the predictions furnished by predictive coding have progressively verified with careful psychophysical and electrophysiological studies of increasing resolution and mechanistic detail: e.g., (). For example, predictions that neurons encode prediction errors are now being verified empirically at the single unit level: e.g., ().

Predictive processing everywhere: planning and the consequences of action

Predictive processing – read as active inference – comes in two flavors. First, there is mere active inference or active predictive coding, in which the generative model simply supplies prior predictions that are fulfilled by reflexes (; ; ; ): See for an example. The other kind – used by you and me – turns on generative models that include the consequences of action. This equips certain natural kinds with the ability to plan and select among courses of action (that minimize expected free energy) (; ). In the technical literature, this distinction is read as a difference between control (; ) and planning as inference (; ; ).

A key distinction is that planning toward intended goals entails the selection among a number of discrete policies or paths into the future (where the expected free energy scores the probability of selecting a particular policy – see below). In this setting, prediction takes on its vernacular meaning as predicting the future (as opposed to predicting what I would see, if I knew the causes of my sensations). A subtle aspect of the generative models required for planning as inference – and ensuing action selection – is that one has to select from a number of discrete policies. This brings us to the final generalization of predictive processing; namely, from continuous to discrete representations.

The foundational role generative models entailed by predictive processing and inference cannot be overstated. But what kind of generative model? Previously, we have limited the discussion to continuous state-space models. But are these the most apt for describing the brain? One could argue that – at least in some parts of the brain – discrete state-space models are more appropriate. Discrete models basically carve nature at its joints by assuming that the state of the world can be in one of a finite number of states. In factorial generalizations, there can be multiple factors with that can interact to generate outcomes (or states at a lower hierarchical level). In computational neuroscience, these are generally referred to as partially observed Markov decision processes (POMDP) and have been the workhorse of many advances in active inference (,; ; ), when applied to choice behavior and belief updating. So, what is the evidence for discrete representations in the brain?

Perhaps the clearest example is the existence of receptive fields. For example, place cells () tell one immediately that the encoding of location in the brain is not in terms of continuous (e.g., GPS) coordinates but in terms of discrete representations; namely, “I am here”. One can generalize this notion to any receptive field. For example, motion-sensitive neurons in the visual hierarchy do not respond more vigorously to faster optical flow. They have a preferred velocity, effectively representing hidden states over a number of mutually exclusive generalized motions. If one generalizes the notion of predictive coding to cover discrete state-space models, one can now interpret neuronal dynamics (and plasticity) in terms of schemes called belief propagation and variational message passing (; ; ; ; ,).

The functional form of the ensuing inference and learning schemes is isomorphic to predictive coding but with some subtle differences () that could have implications for how we test mechanistic hypotheses about neuronal implementation. One interesting example is that canonical microcircuit formulations of predictive coding (; ; ) assume distinct neurons encode precision-weighted prediction errors and that these drive representational units encoding expected latent states: see . However, in discrete schemes, a more biologically plausible hypothesis is that prediction errors are encoded by postsynaptic currents, while expectations are encoded in terms of depolarization and subsequent activity or firing (). This provides a nice example of hypotheses that could be disambiguated empirically; namely, are prediction errors encoded by the activity of distinct neurons or by the distinct activities of any neuron? In summary, “one can think about process theories for variational treatments of Markov decision processes as formally similar to equivalent process theories for state-space models, such as predictive coding (; ). There are some finer, neurobiologically plausible details of the dynamics of expectations about hidden states that we will consider elsewhere. For example, activity-dependent (e.g., NMDA-R dependent) depolarization that underwrites excitation-inhibition balance” ().

Another interesting question is the encoding of precision in discrete state-space models. At present, there are two ways of encoding probabilistic mappings in terms of synaptic connection strengths. The first rests upon something called a Dirichlet encoding in which there are, effectively, tags that are accumulated in an experience-dependent way and then re-normalized to produce likelihood mappings and transition probabilities (). Again, this is a biologically plausible scheme in relation to associative plasticity and accompanying decay terms. However, it does not accommodate context sensitive or fast fluctuations in precision of the sort associated with attention (, ). This kind of precision control may involve a different (Gibbs) kind of parameterisation that has been associated with classical neuromodulators ().

Functionally, the benefit of discrete state generative models is that they greatly simplify planning and decision making – which both depend upon selection between alternative courses of action one could pursue. This raises an interesting question. If one’s generative model includes several plausible future trajectories and their consequences, how does one draw inferences about these future trajectories for which no data are yet available? In the absence of informative data, beliefs about what to do next must be determined by prior beliefs. This is where expected free energy comes in (). Expected free energy is a functional (function of a function) of our current beliefs that acts as a potential that scores the implausibility of alternative futures. In effect, it determines the sort of creature we believe ourselves to be and the kinds of behaviors we expect ourselves to engage in.

While superficially similar in form to variational free energy (see ), expected free energy scores how risky and ambiguous a policy is. Risk, here, is how far one expects a trajectory to deviate from (prior) preferred outcomes, while ambiguity scores the uncertainty with which sensory outcomes are predictable under the hidden states predicted along that path into the future. Another way of putting this is that we expect to behave to achieve preferred outcomes whilst also selecting those data that help resolve our uncertainty about hidden states (; ), parameters (; Kaplan and ), and structures () in our generative model. It is to the last of these that we turn in our concluding section.

Structured predictions: causal discovery in the brain

There are clearly many avenues one could pursue under the predictive processing framework. We close with one important issue – structure learning – that is engaging many people in neuroscience (; ; ; ; ) and beyond (; ; ; ; ). Intuitively, structure learning can be understood as the process by which one discovers the best internal model to use. Unlike the forms of predictive processing discussed above, this means one cannot assume a set number of variables that generate predictions, or even that variables are related, such that one can be used to make predictions about the other. So how does structure learning relate to predictive processing? Under the above arguments, predictive processing applies at multiple scales and to kinds of variables. When viewed through the lens of minimizing variational free energy, a key issue comes into focus: variational (a.k.a., approximate) Bayesian inference is defined in terms of a mean field approximation, in which different kinds of unknowns are represented as marginal posteriors. This may sound complicated but is the raison d’etre for using variational calculus in active inference; i.e., one unknown contextualizes belief updating about another. For example, one has to learn the right parameters or synaptic connections in order to optimize state-estimation or inference. Conversely, one has to have the right inference in order to supply prediction errors that are accumulated during learning. See .

The mean field approximation rests on a partition of the unknown attributes of a generative model into states that change quickly, precision that contextualizes inference, and slowly changing parameters. However, there is a further attribute that we have not addressed; namely, the structure of the model; i.e., architecture and functional form of its parameterization. The distinction between model states, parameters and structure leads to a distinction between inference, learning and selection. But why selection? In variational inference, each structure corresponds to a particular model or hypothesis that could best explain (sensory) data. To disambiguate among different models or hypothesis, one simply selects the model with the greatest evidence (or smallest variational free energy). This is known as Bayesian model selection. Indeed, one could argue that this is the mathematical image of natural selection at an evolutionary timescale (; ; ) or attentional selection during perceptual inference (; ) or, indeed action selection during active inference (; ; ).

Another view of Bayesian model selection is afforded by causal discovery and structure learning (; ; ; ; ). The structure learning or model selection problem is a challenging problem because it calls upon the exploration of a model or hypothesis space that is not straightforward to specify or explore. There are generally two approaches to the model selection problem; namely, a top-down approach in which one starts with an overly expressive model and prunes away redundant parts, through things like Bayesian model reduction and synaptic pruning (; ; ). Alternatively, one can grow a model de novo, under the principles of least action and redundancy. This brings us back to where we started in terms of compression and maximizing mutual information under constraints. In some senses, many current endeavors in systems neuroscience – e.g., (; ) – could be viewed as discovering the right structures that constitute our generative models; namely, carving nature at its joints in the right way. Although this takes us a long way from the original conception of predictive coding, it foregrounds the foundational role of hypotheses or generative models; both in terms of neuroscientific enquiry and the subject of that enquiry; namely, ourselves.

Conclusion

Bring this right back to the beginning, each of the themes we have encountered here draw from the phantastic properties of our brains. Predictive coding and its generalizations formalize the notion that perception (and action) are constructive endeavors that make sense of reality through finding the explanations from which that reality can best be generated. Internal generative models thereby offer a unifying synthesis of the developments from Helmholtz’s unconscious inference, through 20th century developments in variational calculus, to the mechanics of predictive coding. They are just as central to modern extensions into predictive processing and active inference, in addition to underwriting generative artificial intelligence and machine learning. From the perspective of theoretical neurobiology, predictive coding has taught us that to understand the brain, the fundamental problem one must address is how the brain understands the world.
