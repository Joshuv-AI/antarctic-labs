Aberrant large- and mesoscale network segregation and integration in bulimia nervosa
Authors: Peng Zhang, Lirong Tang, Weihua Li, Miao Wang, Yulong Jia, Fengxia Yu, Zhanjiang Li, Zhenchang Wang, Jiani Wang, Guowei Wu, Yiling Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGrowing evidence indicates that bulimia nervosa (BN) is more likely driven by network-level dysfunctions rather than abnormalities in isolated brain regions. However, previous studies focusing on specific regions or connectivity features have led to a limited understanding of the complexity and integrality of network in BN. This study aimed to investigate large- and mesoscale network alterations in BN from the perspective of network segregation and integration.MethodsUsing resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging data from 85 BN patients and 71 healthy controls (HCs) matched for age, sex, and education, we applied a graph-theoretic framework to analyze functional network architecture in BN. Specifically, we examined group differences in within- and between-system functional connectivity (FC) as well as system segregation at both the whole-brain and system levels. Associations between network measures and clinical features were further explored.ResultsCompared with HCs, BN patients exhibited whole-brain reduced within- and between-system FC accompanied by increased system segregation. At the system level, patients showed increased between-system FC of the default mode network (DMN), control network and ventral attention network, along with decreased DMN system segregation. The increased integration of DMN with other systems was associated with higher Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-21) scores in BN patients.ConclusionsThe neural mechanisms of BN involve an imbalance between network segregation and integration at both global and system levels, most pronounced within the DMN, whose alterations are associated with depression and disordered eating behavior. These findings may provide potential neural substrates for some core behavioral deficits in BN.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-026-01671-1.

Introduction

Bulimia nervosa (BN) is a complex eating disorder (ED) that predominantly affects young women [1, 2]. It is closely linked to significant psychiatric comorbidities, serious medical complications, and an elevated risk of premature mortality [2–4]. Defined by recurrent episodes of binge eating followed by inappropriate compensatory behaviors, BN involves profound disturbances in emotion regulation [5], impulse control [6], and body image perception [7], reflecting the intricate interplay of multiple psychological dysfunctions. Among these psychological dysfunctions, the relationship between emotional dysregulation and ED has gained interest, especially after the emergence of the emotional eating theory that highlights the fact that eating is used by individuals with ED to regulate their emotions, and that difficulties in emotion regulation represent a transdiagnostic factor across all EDs. Depression is a co-morbidity that has been frequently linked to ED (especially BN), with evidence suggesting a bidirectional association between the two conditions. In BN, comorbid depression is associated with increased risk of suicide attempts and poorer clinical outcomes [8]. Currently, the treatment of BN still faces significant clinical challenges. Recent reports indicate that only 26%–50% of patients achieve long-term remission [9], underscoring the urgent need for more in-depth investigations into its underlying neuropsychological mechanisms and the specific therapeutic targets.

Advances in neuroimaging, particularly resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI), have provided new insights into the mechanisms of neural dysregulation in BN, consistently identified abnormal local neural activities in multiple brain regions, including subcortical structures (e.g., striatum, thalamus) [10–13] and the prefrontal cortex [14, 15]. Whereas, accumulating evidence suggests that BN is more likely driven by network-level dysfunctions rather than abnormalities in isolated brain regions. Specifically, seed-based functional connectivity (FC) studies in BN have revealed aberrant connectivity primarily involving the sensorimotor network [10, 11], control network (CON) [11], subcortical and visual regions [10, 12], as well as the default mode network (DMN) [16]. In addition, other studies have applied region-of-interest analysis [17] and graph theory analysis [18, 19] to delineate the aberrant functional network architecture of BN. However, these studies have largely focused on the reorganization of specific regions or networks, under-characterizing the complexity and integrative properties of large-scale brain networks in BN patients. Resting-state brain networks are inherently organized in a high-dimensional manner: brain areas interact to form distinct but interrelated systems, or modules, which are characterized by dense within-system connectivity supporting specialized processing and relatively sparser between-system connectivity enabling cross-system communication [20]. On the one hand, these systems achieve relative independence through segregation, which promotes efficient specialization of cognitive operations; on the other, they rely on integration to support global coordination, thereby enabling the brain’s dynamic adaptability to varying cognitive demands. Maintaining a balance between segregation and integration is considered crucial for supporting diverse cognitive processes and flexible task switching [21]. Evidence from other neuropsychiatric disorders has demonstrated that disruptions in this balance can account for disorder-specific clinical features [22–25], and emerging evidence suggests that this may also apply to anorexia nervosa (AN), where altered network system integration or segregation have been linked to symptom severity, body dissatisfaction, and drive for thinness [26, 27]. Graph-theoretical and connectomic studies in BN are steadily increasing. Although prior studies have provided initial evidence for abnormal modular segregation in BN [28], the joint pattern of within-system connectivity, between-system connectivity, and system segregation at the whole-brain and system levels, as well as its clinical relevance, has not yet been fully characterized.

To date, studies on the system segregation and integration largely relied on graph-theoretical approaches. A common approach is to threshold the FC matrix and construct a binary graph, which is computationally simple but may result in the loss of information on connection strength during thresholding [29]. Meanwhile, some studies have proposed measuring system segregation and integration based on whole-brain weighted graphs [30, 31]. For example, Chan et al. [31] introduced a system segregation metric that retains connection weights when constructing the connectivity matrix, thereby providing a more accurate and comprehensive representation of brain network properties.

Here, we applied graph-theoretic analyses to characterize the functional network architecture of patients with BN compared to healthy controls (HCs). We constructed a whole-brain weighted network to examine within-system and between-system connectivity as well as system segregation. Within-system and between-system connectivity respectively reflected the functional consistency within specific systems and the degree of information exchange between systems. System segregation is an index used to summarize values of within-system correlations in relation to between-system correlations, reflecting the ability to process information within a specific functional system. These complementary measures allowed us to characterize both system-specific segregation and cross-system interaction in BN. Furthermore, we explored associations between these network characteristics and abnormal eating behaviors and emotional disturbances in BN. We hypothesized that, compared to HCs, BN patients exhibit disrupted segregation and integration across whole-brain systems, particularly in systems closely related to core disease symptoms, and that these network abnormalities are associated with behavioral abnormalities.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital (approval No. 2021-P2-052-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Participants

Initially, a total of 92 patients with BN were recruited from outpatient clinics, along with 80 HCs matched for age, sex, and education level through online advertisements. The diagnosis and recruitment of BN participants were independently conducted by two psychiatrists with expertise in eating disorders, using the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) 7.0.2 [32], a brief structured interview developed in accordance with the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) [33].

BN patients were excluded if they had any current comorbid major psychiatric disorders (other than major depressive disorder or anxiety disorder), such as bipolar disorder, substance abuse and dissociative disorder. And patients who had used antipsychotic medications within the two months prior to the study were excluded. Among the 92 BN patients, 4 patients had a prior history of anxiolytic use and three had previously taken antidepressants; however, none had used psychotropic medication for at least two months before participation.

HCs were required to have a normal body weight and no current or past psychiatric disorders. The MINI was also administered to HCs to exclude those with potential psychiatric conditions.

Additional exclusion criteria for both two groups included a history of substance use disorders (alcohol, nicotine, or other drugs), traumatic brain injury, neurosurgery, neurological disorders, pervasive developmental disorders, or intellectual disability. Participants were also excluded if they had major medical conditions (e.g., diabetes, hyperthyroidism, hyperlipidemia, or chronic illnesses such as hypertension), were pregnant or lactating, had claustrophobia, or possessed metal implants contraindicated for MRI. During screening, 7 BN patients were excluded (two for alcohol abuse, two for nicotine abuse, two for metal implants, and one for claustrophobia), and 9 HC was excluded due to MRI contraindications. The final sample therefore comprised 85 BN patients and 71 HCs who proceeded to subsequent analyses.

Clinical assessment

On the day of the experiment, all participants were instructed to fast for at least four hours prior to the scan. Demographic information was collected before the scans, and for BN patients, medical data including illness duration and the frequency of binge-eating and purging behaviors were also recorded. Each participant was then required to completed six standardized self-report questionnaires under the guidance of a trained psychiatrist. These included the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) to assess current hunger on a scale from 0 (not at all hungry) to 10 (extremely hungry); the Chinese version of the Dutch Eating Behavior Questionnaire (DEBQ) [34], a 33-item instrument evaluating restrained, emotional, and external eating; the bulimia subscale of the Eating Disorder Inventory (EDI-BN) [35] to measure bulimic tendencies; the Eating Attitudes Test-26 (EAT-26) [36] for assessing disordered eating attitudes and behaviors; the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [37] to evaluate depressive symptoms; and the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) [38] to assess anxiety. The Chinese versions of these instruments have been validated with good reliability and construct validity in evaluating eating disorder-related psychopathology among Chinese populations [34, 36–39].

MRI data acquisition

To reduce immediate postprandial variability and to standardize metabolic/satiety state prior to resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging acquisition, participants were asked to fast for at least 4 h before scanning. Prior studies have suggested that hunger/satiety state and glucose-related metabolic state may influence resting-state brain activity and functional connectivity [40–42]. The fasting requirement was intended only to reduce immediate postprandial variability before rs-fMRI acquisition, rather than to induce hunger or eating-disorder-related symptoms. This interval was not based on a specific validation study establishing 4 h as an optimal threshold; rather, it was adopted as a relatively brief and pragmatic standardization procedure, with consideration of both methodological consistency and participant safety, particularly for individuals with BN. To further minimize participant burden and potential risk, several protective measures were implemented. First, hunger ratings were recorded before scanning using VAS, and only participants with ratings between 3 and 7 proceeded to MRI, in order to avoid scanning under conditions of extreme hunger or satiety. Second, participants were monitored throughout the scanning procedure and could request termination of the scan at any time if they felt unwell. Third, post-scan food support was available when needed.

MRI data for all participants were acquired using a 3.0-T scanner (Prisma, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 64-channel phased-array head coil. During scanning, participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed, remain calm and awake, and minimize head movement. Earplugs and foam pads were provided to reduce scanner noise and further restrict motion. A conventional axial T2 sequence was first acquired to screen for visible brain abnormalities. Then we acquired high-resolution sagittal T1-weighted images utilizing a three-dimensional magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (3D-MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) = 2,530 ms/2.98 ms; inversion time (TI) = 1,100 ms; flip angle = 7°; data matrix = 256 × 256; voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3; field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm2; slice thickness/gap = 1.0 mm/1.0 mm; and slice number = 192. And functional images were acquired using an echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with the following parameters: TR/TE = 2000/30 ms, flip angle = 90°, matrix = 64 × 64, FOV = 224 × 224 mm², slice thickness/gap = 3.5 mm/1 mm, 33 slices, and 240 time points. And After completing the MRI scans, all participants confirmed that they had remained awake throughout the scanning procedure.

MRI data processing

Standard functional MRI preprocessing

Functional images were initially preprocessed using fMRIPrep (version 25.2.5) and XCP-D (version 0.14.1) to minimize artifacts [43]. The preprocessing pipeline involved the following steps: (i) correction of intensity discrepancies between odd and even slices caused by interleaved acquisition, (ii) adjustment for head motion both within and across runs, and (iii) across-run intensity normalization to a whole-brain mode value of 1000. Atlas transformation of the functional data was computed for each individual using their T1-weighted structural image registered to the MNI152 standard template. Each run was then resampled to an isotropic 3-mm atlas space, combining motion correction and atlas transformation in a single cubic spline interpolation [44, 45]. This single interpolation procedure minimizes blurring that could be introduced by multiple resampling steps. Additional motion quality control metrics, including the proportion of scrubbed volumes, retained data after censoring, and group comparisons of motion indices (framewise displacement), are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

FC preprocessing

Functional MRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep and XCP-D, which together implemented a series of procedures to ensure data quality and reliability. Before the FC calculation, several additional preprocessing procedures were applied to the fMRI data to minimize spurious variance unlikely to reflect neural activity, including the following steps: (i) multiple regression of the blood oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) time series with the global signal [46–48], ventricular and white matter signals, six head motion parameters from rigid-body correction, and their first-order derivatives [49, 50]; (ii) temporal band-pass filtering (0.009–0.08 Hz) [51]; (iii) spatial smoothing with a 3D Gaussian kernel (6 mm FWHM) [52]; and (iv) motion scrubbing, which involved identifying volumes with excessive head motion and marking them for exclusion in subsequent analyses [51, 53]. Specifically, framewise displacement (FD) was calculated for each volume as the sum of absolute frame-to-frame differences across the six motion parameters (three translational and three rotational) [51]. Volumes with FD > 0.3 mm, as well as the frames immediately preceding and following them, were flagged and excluded from subsequent regression and correlation calculations.

Surface preprocessing

To improve cortical alignment accuracy, surface-based preprocessing was performed using FreeSurfer 5.0, as landmark-based registration has been shown to provide more precise cortical correspondence than volume-based methods. After volumetric registration and motion scrubbing, each participant’s MP-RAGE image was processed with FreeSurfer’s recon-all pipeline, including brain extraction, segmentation, generation of white matter and pial surfaces, inflation, and spherical registration to the fsaverage template [54–58]. The left and right fsaverage surfaces were then aligned using landmark-based deformation maps to a hybrid left-right surface atlas (fs_LR) [59]. Each fs_LR surface was resampled to 164,000 vertices using Caret tools [60] and subsequently downsampled to a 32,492-vertex mesh (fs_LR 32k) for computational efficiency while maintaining adequate spatial resolution. All deformation fields from native space to fs_LR 32k were combined into a single transformation to minimize interpolation errors. This procedure resulted in a standardized surface space that enabled consistent analysis across participants and hemispheres, and allowed for time series extraction from nodes defined on the same surface.

Network node definition and FC calculation

Network nodes were defined using the Schaefer resting-state functional connectivity parcellation (Schaefer-1000, 17-network version), which subdivides the cortical surface into 1000 parcels using gradient-weighted Markov Random Field modeling [61]. Each parcel was treated as a node, and for each participant, the mean BOLD signal across all vertices within each node was extracted as its representative time course. Pairwise Pearson correlations between the time courses of all nodes were then calculated to generate a 1000 × 1000 FC matrix, and correlation coefficients were transformed into Fisher z-values. Although negative relationships could be included in the network analysis [62], negative z-values were excluded from the data matrix in this study due to the current ambiguity surrounding the interpretation of negative correlations [63–65]. Then negative correlations and diagonal values were set to zero, resulting in a fully connected, weighted 1000 × 1000 z-matrix for each participant. Each node was further assigned to one of seven canonical functional systems based on the parcellation’s system labels defined by Yeo et al. [66]. Cortical nodes were assigned to seven canonical large-scale systems: visual network (VIS), auditory network, somatomotor network, language network, ventral attention network (VAN), CON, DMN, and dorsal attention network. To capture mesoscale organization, several systems were further partitioned into subnetworks: DMN, CON, and visual network were divided into three subnetworks each; VAN, dorsal attention network, and somatomotor network were divided into two subnetworks each; the language network comprised a single subnetwork [67, 68](see Fig. 1A for color legend).

System segregation analysis

The whole brain system segregation index was calculated to quantify the balance between functional integration and segregation across large-scale brain systems. Specifically, it is calculated by taking the difference between the mean within-system correlation values (here we applied the Fisher z-transformed correlation value, denoted as Wsame) and the mean correlation values between a given system and other systems (denoted as Bother), then dividing this difference by the mean within-system correlation (Wsame). The detailed formula is shown in Fig. 1C, which was also derived from the study of Chan et al. [31]. This index preserves the weights of all positively correlated connections, enabling even weaker connections to reflect the overall organizational characteristics of the system to some extent.

Demographic, clinical and network variables statistical analysis

Group differences in demographic and clinical characteristics between the BN and HC groups were examined using SPSS version 26.0. The normality of continuous variables was first assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Variables following a normal distribution were compared using independent-sample t-tests, while those not meeting normality assumptions were analyzed with the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test. The categorical variable (the sex variable) was compared using the chi-square test. Statistical significance for all group comparisons was defined as p < 0.05.

Group differences in within- and between-system connectivity and system segregation between the BN and HC groups were examined using independent-samples t-tests, with age, education level, and body mass index (BMI) as covariates. Multiple comparisons were corrected applying the false discovery rate (FDR) correction method, and group differences with adjusted p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Given that our previous published studies have all revealed significant differences in depressive symptoms (BDI scores), disordered eating behaviors (EDI-BN scores), and attitudes (EAT-26) between BN and HC, additional analyses were conducted to examine whether the observed group differences in network measures were independent of these BN emotional and dietary-related symptoms. Specifically, group comparisons were repeated using general linear models, with BDI scores, EDI-BN scores, and EAT-26 scores entered as covariates in separate models respectively. Age, education level, and BMI were included as covariates, consistent with the primary analyses. These analyses were designed to disentangle the relative contributions of depressive and disordered eating-related symptoms to the observed network alterations.

Comprehensive network-clinical association analysis

To determine whether the network alterations that distinguished BN patients from HCs were also clinically meaningful, we performed a comprehensive multivariable network-clinical association analysis using only the whole-brain segregation, system-level segregation, and between-system connectivity measures that showed significant group differences in the case-control comparison. These network measures were treated as candidate neural predictors because they represented the specific topological abnormalities of interest and allowed us to test whether the same altered network architecture was related to variation in eating pathology, eating-related behavioral tendencies, and affective symptoms. Rather than examining each network measure in isolation, all retained neural predictors were entered jointly into the same model so that each effect could be interpreted as the unique contribution of a given network feature after accounting for shared variance with the other altered network measures.

For each clinical phenotype, three complementary regression frameworks were implemented. First, a patient-only model was used to test whether interindividual variation in altered network organization predicted symptom severity within the BN group. Second, a pooled full-sample model was used to test whether network measures were associated with clinical variation across the entire sample while controlling for group differences. Third, a moderation model was used to determine whether the association between a given network measure and a clinical variable differed by group by including both the main effects of network measures and their interaction terms with group. In all models, years and BMI were included as covariates. This three-part strategy allowed us to distinguish patient-specific effects, continuous dimensional effects across all participants, and formal group-dependent differences in slope within a unified framework.

Because the altered network measures were partly correlated with one another, multicollinearity was addressed before final inference using an iterative variance inflation factor procedure. Variance inflation factors (VIF) were computed at the model-matrix level, and in the moderation models each neural predictor was evaluated together with its corresponding interaction term as one predictor block. When the largest block-level variance inflation factor exceeded 5, the corresponding network measure was removed and the model was refitted. This procedure was repeated until all retained predictors satisfied the prespecified collinearity threshold. In this way, the final models preserved the multivariable structure of the altered network profile while reducing redundancy-driven instability in coefficient estimation.

To ensure robust model evaluation, assumption diagnostics were performed for every final regression model using a standardized model-checking procedure that examined residual behavior and overall model adequacy through visual diagnostic panels. Multiple comparison control was implemented at the model level: omnibus model p values derived from the overall F test were false-discovery-rate corrected separately within each model family, and only models surviving this correction were taken forward for focused interpretation and visualization. For moderation models, post hoc simple-slope analyses were conducted to estimate and test the within-group association between that network measure and the clinical outcome separately in patients and healthy controls.

Code availability

We have uploaded our codes of network integration and segregation analysis, network-clinical association analysis, as well as the subsequent statistical analyses to github for more transparent and reproducible science (https://github.com/guoweiwuorgin/BN_Large_scale_network_Seg_Integ).

Results

Demographic and clinical data

Following a rigorous inclusion and exclusion process, this study ultimately enrolled 85 patients with BN and 71 HCs. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age, sex, education level, BMI, or FD. Scores on all scales were significantly higher in the BN group than in the HC group. Detailed demographic and clinical data for both groups are presented in Table 1.

Whole-brain system segregation and integration

Compared with HCs, patients with BN exhibited a distinct alteration in whole-brain network architecture. Specifically, patients showed significantly reduced whole-brain between-system connectivity and decreased within-system connectivity, accompanied by an increase in whole-brain system segregation (Table 2; Figs. 1B and 2).

System-level segregation and integration

At the system level, compared to HCs, BN patients displayed increased FC between nodes within DMN and nodes across all brain network systems (including both the DMN itself and non-DMN systems, abbreviated as DMN_B_all), and between nodes within DMN and nodes from other systems (abbreviated as DMN_B_other). And patients also showed decreased system segregation of the DMN from all other network systems (abbreviated as DMN_S_all). In addition, the higher FC between nodes within CON/VAN and nodes of other network systems was observed in the BN patients relative to HCs (abbreviated as CON_B_other and VAN_B_other) (Table 2; Figs. 1B and 3). In addition, to ensure completeness and transparency, all system-level analysis results, including both significant and non-significant findings, are presented in the Supplementary Table S1.

Association of network system features with clinical variables

Network measures showing significant group differences were used as predictors, including whole-brain integration and segregation indices and system-level connectivity and segregation measures of the DMN, CON, and VAN. Clinical variables were treated as outcomes, encompassing binge eating–purging frequency and measures of eating-related psychopathology and affective symptoms (DEBQ-restrained eating, DEBQ-emotional eating, DEBQ-external eating, EDI-BN, EAT-26, BDI, and SAS scores). Associations were examined using patient-only, full-sample, and group moderation models. Among the final models retained for interpretation, significant associations were observed primarily for BDI. Overall model-fit statistics and omnibus test results for all tested models are summarized in the Supplementary Table S2. In the full-sample main effect models, both DMN_B_other and DMN_S_all remained positively associated with BDI after adjustment for group and covariates (DMN_B_other: β = 2.235, SE = 0.887, t = 2.520, p = 0.013, 95% CI [0.482, 3.988] ; DMN_S_all: β = 1.837, SE = 0.848, t = 2.166, p = 0.032, 95% CI [0.161, 3.513]). In the moderation models, the interaction between DMN_B_other and group was significant for BDI (β = 4.103, SE = 1.363, t = 3.010, p = 0.003, 95% CI [1.409, 6.798]) and for EDI_BN (β = 2.251, SE = 1.027, t = 2.192, p = 0.030, 95% CI [0.221, 4.280]). Similarly, DMN_B_other was positively associated with EDI_BN in the BN group (slope = 1.766, SE = 0.690, t(144) = 2.558, p = 0.012), whereas no significant association was observed in the HC group (slope = -0.485, SE = 0.756, t(144) = − 0.641, p = 0.522). Discussion

Accumulating evidence indicates that disrupted functional network properties are implicated in the neuropathological mechanisms of BN. Hence, our study characterized global and system-specific profiles of functional network segregation and integration of patients with BN compared to HCs using the graph-theoretic framework. Our analyses primarily revealed that BN patients showed reduced whole-brain integration and increased segregation, alongside stronger DMN, CON, and VAN integration and reduced DMN segregation at the system level. Importantly, these network alterations demonstrated significant clinical relevance: for BN patients, increased DMN integration (DMN_B_other) and reduced DMN segregation (DMN_S_all) were associated with more severe depressive symptom (BDI scores), and increased DMN integration (DMN_B_other) was associated with more severe eating-related pathology behavior (EDI_BN scores). Reduced whole-brain within-system connectivity was also linked to more severe depressive symptoms. And the aforementioned associations do not exist in HCs. Together, these findings support a network-based dimensional psychopathology model of BN, in which large-scale network alterations, particularly involving the DMN, are related to both depressive symptoms and disordered eating behaviors. Additional covariate analyses further suggested that some aberrant network integration and segregation in BN, especially within the DMN, may be modulated by depressive symptoms and disordered eating behaviors.

Across the whole-brain analyses, we observed that BN patients showed reduced within- and between-system connectivity but increased overall system segregation, suggesting a global imbalance between integration and segregation for patients. Previous studies have reported that BN patients exhibit abnormal connectivity both within several network systems (e.g., visual and sensorimotor networks) [18] and between different systems (e.g., aberrant functional connectivity between the DMN and VAN) [17], which may underlie this observed imbalance. Healthy young brains at rest tend to maintain a balance between segregation and integration [20]. Disruption of this balance may compromise the functional specialization of individual systems and weaken the efficiency of global information exchange, thereby contributing to aberrant cognitive processing and behavioral manifestations [30, 71]. Such imbalance has been consistently implicated in the neural mechanisms of various psychiatric disorders, including major depressive disorder (MDD) [72, 73], autism spectrum disorder [74], insomnia with comorbid depression [75], etc. Viewed in this context, the abnormal global segregation and integration observed in BN may be involved in the concurrent presence of multiple psychopathological dimensions in these patients. Our findings provide further evidence, from the perspective of large-scale brain network, that BN could be conceptualized as a network dysfunction-related disorder.

At the system level, abnormalities were most pronounced in the DMN, which were related to the more severe depressive symptoms and bulimic tendencies of BN patients. And the additional covariate analyses further demonstrated that the DMN-related network alterations were modulated by both the depressive and disordered eating symptoms, suggesting that BN’s DMN integration and segregation may result from the combined influence of them. Relative to HCs, BN patients displayed stronger DMN connectivity across systems but weaker segregation from other systems. The DMN, comprising core regions such as the posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, and medial prefrontal cortex [66], is primarily involved in self-referential thinking and internally oriented cognition [76, 77]. Previous studies have demonstrated that BN patients exhibit general dysconnectivity both between DMN core nodes and other systems [16, 28, 78], and within DMN nodes themselves [18]. Prior resting-state studies in AN individual have reported DMN-related connectivity abnormalities from different analytical perspectives. Although existing studies differ in methodology and in the disease stage of the patients examined, they generally support the involvement of DMN abnormalities in the pathophysiology of AN [26, 79–82]. Therefore, abnormalities in DMN segregation and integration may represent one of the network features shared by BN and AN. The hyperactivity of DMN has been extensively reported linked to excessive negative, self-focused [83], and body-related thoughts [84, 85]. For instance, women with BN show reduced activation in anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and precuneus regions when evaluating distorted self-images, but heightened activation when imagining others comparing their body with an “overweight” computer-generated model [86]. Behaviorally, BN patients excessively focus on negative self-referential thoughts, especially concerning body shape, which reinforces body image disturbance and sustains binge-purge behaviors [1]. Converging evidence from the large-scale meta-analysis in MDD has consistently demonstrated that DMN alterations are among the most robust neuroimaging findings in depression, typically characterized by increased FC and abnormal network integration [87]. These DMN abnormalities have been closely linked to maladaptive self-referential processing and rumination, which are the core features of depressive cognition. Taken together, the increased DMN integration and reduced segregation observed in BN may reflect heightened vulnerability of self-referential processes to external cues (e.g., food- and body-related stimuli) and impaired regulation of negative self-focused cognition. And such DMN abnormalities may reflect a shared network mechanism underlying maladaptive self-referential processing across affective and eating-related psychopathology, and may represent a mesoscale network-level neural mechanism underlying the pathological preoccupation with negative self-referential thoughts in BN. On the other hand, DMN alterations were also associated with specific eating psychopathology of BN. DMN integration (DMN_B_other) showed a significant interaction with group in predicting EDI-BN scores, and was positively associated with EDI-BN scores only in BN patients. And the segregation (DMN_S_all) and integration (DMN_B_other) of the DMN are modulated by disordered eating behavior (EDI-BN) and attitudes (EAT). indicating a BN-specific relationship between DMN network integration and core eating-disorder behavioral and cognitive pathology. Overall, the above findings extend the role of DMN dysconnectivity beyond affective symptomatology, highlighting its contribution to BN-specific eating cognitive disturbances.

We found that BN patients exhibited significantly enhanced FC between both the VAN and CON with other network systems, suggesting a characteristic of excessive integration for the two systems. The primary role of the VAN is stimulus-driven attentional reorientation toward salient external cues [88, 89], and its core nodes typically include the anterior insula, the temporoparietal junction and the inferior frontal gyrus [66]. The CON—comprising the ACC, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and inferior parietal lobule—plays a critical role in executive control, impulse inhibition, and conflict monitoring [66, 90, 91]. Consistent with our findings, previous neuroimaging studies have reported increased connectivity between VAN/CON regions and nodes of other networks, particularly the DMN in BN patients [17]. For instance, our previous study reported that in BN patients, the orbitofrontal cortex (a region within the CON) exhibited abnormally increased functional connectivity with regions of the DMN (precuneus) and the VIS (middle occipital gyrus), which was associated with a higher frequency of binge eating-purging episodes [78]. Two published studies have demonstrated abnormal FC between the insula (a core node of the VAN) and regions within the VIS and DMN in BN patients [16, 92]. Moreover, enhanced FC between the insula and the precuneus was significantly correlated with symptom severity [16]. The CON- and VAN-related connection abnormalities have also been reported in patients with AN. In AN, the available evidence for VAN-related alterations more often points to reduced connectivity, particularly in the inferior frontal gyrus [93, 94], whereas findings for executive/control-related network systems are more heterogeneous and have not converged on a consistent direction of alteration [26, 82]. Clinically, BN patients frequently exhibit attentional bias toward food/body-related cues and difficulty shifting attention away from them [95], and they show impaired inhibitory control and difficulties in regulating impulsive eating and compensatory behaviors [96–98]. Spalatro et al. has discovered that individuals with eating disorders exhibit abnormal neural plasticity in the VAN, which correlates with their excessive preoccupation with body image and food intake [99]. Moreover, findings from anxiety disorders indicate that altered VAN activation and connectivity underlie attentional bias toward emotional or threatening stimuli [100]. Taken together, we speculate that excessive system-level integration of VAN and CON may explain the difficulty in attention shifting and impulse behavior regulation observed in BN patients. Existing research indicates that potential neuromodulatory targets for eating disorders predominantly reside within the CON, DAN, and DMN [101, 102]. This finding underscores that the aberrant properties of these core networks may represent a key neural substrate underlying such disorders, in line with our results. Notably, covariate analyses revealed that the CON_B_other was no longer significant after controlling for BDI scores, suggesting that CON integration may be moderated by depressive symptom severity. Moreover, compared to the DMN, its abnormal integration seems to be less susceptible to regulation by abnormal eating behaviors. In contrast, VAN_B_other remained stable after covariate adjustment, indicating that the VAN integration may represent a relatively robust network feature of BN.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design limits our ability to infer causal relationships or to delineate the developmental trajectory of altered network segregation and integration characteristics in BN. Second, given the high comorbidity of BN with anxiety and depression, we did not exclude patients with these comorbid conditions, which may have introduced potential confounding effects. However, such a sample may better represent the typical BN population. Third, we used only a single high-resolution atlas to construct the functional networks. While this atlas provides fine-grained parcellation, the findings may vary with different parcellation schemes, and future studies should validate the robustness of the results across multiple atlases. Fourth, although we focused on global- and system-level segregation and integration, we did not further localize abnormal connectivity patterns at the node level within specific networks, which limits our ability to pinpoint voxel- and region-level alterations in BN.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that BN patients exhibited a global pattern of reduced system integration and increased segregation, together with altered integration and segregation within the DMN, CON, and VAN-widely recognized core networks implicated in BN behavioral characteristics, with alterations most prominent in the DMN. Notably, increased DMN integration in BN was associated with more severe depressive symptoms and disordered eating behaviors and was modulated by these clinical dimensions, highlighting the clinical relevance of DMN-related network abnormalities. These findings provide network-level evidence for the neural basis of excessive self-focused thinking, impaired attentional shifting, and difficulties in impulse regulation in BN.
