Short- and long-term effects of semaglutide 2.4 mg on energy intake, appetite, and food reward: a 60-week, double-blind randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jena S Tronieri, Kelly C Allison, Katie DeRouen, Anastasia Amaro, Kayla G Collins, Anokha Roy, Sanaiya Watts, Tasnia Ananna, Thomas A Wadden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudies of ≤20 wk have demonstrated reductions in energy intake, appetite, and food reward with semaglutide that may drive initial weight loss. These brief studies could not assess whether semaglutide has long-term effects on eating that support the maintenance of lost weight.ObjectivesThe goal of the present study was to compare short- and long-term energy intake, appetite, and food reward with semaglutide 2.4 mg to placebo.MethodsThis 60-wk, double-blind trial randomly assigned 120 adults with overweight/obesity in a 3:2 ratio to semaglutide 2.4 mg or placebo. In laboratory assessments at weeks 0, 20, 40, and 60, participants rated their appetite when fasting and for 4 h after a standardized breakfast. Energy intake was measured during an ad libitum lunch. Measures of food reward included the Power of Food Scale. Missing data were estimated using jump-to-reference multiple imputation, and analyses of covariance compared the groups in change at each assessment, controlling for baseline.ResultsThe semaglutide group had significantly larger reductions from baseline in ad libitum energy intake relative to placebo at weeks 20, 40, and 60, consuming a mean ± standard error of 291.9 ± 64.4, 240.2 ± 87.8, and 269.5 ± 83.6 kcal less, respectively. At week 20, semaglutide-treated participants had significantly greater increases from baseline in appetite suppression after the standardized breakfast [mean difference (MD) = 2197 ± 823] and greater reductions in past-week hunger (MD = −17.4 ± 4.8) and food preoccupation (MD = −14.9 ± 4.5). However, the groups did not differ significantly in appetite outcomes at weeks 40 or 60. The semaglutide group had greater reductions in responsiveness to the rewarding value of food at weeks 20 and 40 (MD = −0.4 ± 0.2 and −0.6 ± 0.2, respectively).ConclusionsSemaglutide 2.4 mg continues to help participants consume fewer calories at 20, 40, and 60 wk of treatment, even though some subjective benefits decline. These findings suggest that reduced energy intake is a central mechanism by which semaglutide both induces and maintains weight loss.This study is registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT05548647.Keywords: dietary intake, appetite, eating behaviors, antiobesity medications, glucagon-like peptide receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), obesity management, obesity

Introduction

Semaglutide 2.4 mg is a once-weekly subcutaneous glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA) approved for chronic weight management in people with overweight/obesity. Semaglutide produces a mean loss of ∼15% of baseline body weight in 68 wk [1]. Short-term studies of ≤20 wk have demonstrated reductions in appetite, food reward, and energy intake with semaglutide that appear to drive its strong initial effects on body weight [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. For example, participants assigned to take semaglutide 2.4 mg for 20 wk consumed 35% fewer calories during an ad libitum lunch than those taking a placebo (estimated treatment difference [ETD]: −225 kcal) [3]. Semaglutide-treated participants also reported greater mean increases in fullness and satiety, as well as greater reductions in hunger and prospective food consumption.

These brief treatment studies could not assess whether semaglutide has long-term effects on energy intake, appetite, or food reward that support the maintenance of its large initial weight losses. Large short-term weight losses have been shown to produce long-term compensatory changes in appetite-regulating hormones and increases in subjective appetite that likely promote weight regain [6]. In a 52-wk study of the appetitive effects of another GLP-1RA, liraglutide 3.0 mg, Tronieri et al. [7] demonstrated that the large early reductions in hunger and food preoccupation, as well as increases in fullness, experienced by liraglutide-treated participants were not maintained at weeks 40 or 52, despite the maintenance of relatively greater weight losses at these times in the liraglutide-treated participants, compared with lifestyle modification alone. In that study, mean weight loss in the liraglutide group reached its nadir at week 40 and remained relatively stable through week 52.

Weight loss with semaglutide typically continues until approximately week 60 [1,8], and longer-term studies suggest that a majority of lost weight is maintained 1 to 3 y later with continued use of the medication [9,10]. In the 2-y Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with obesity (STEP) 5 trial, semaglutide-treated participants continued to report greater improvements relative to placebo in craving control at 104 wk of treatment [11]. However, initial differences between the groups in hunger and fullness did not persist beyond week 20. The long-term maintenance of lost weight in both STEP 5 and the Tronieri et al. [7] study suggests that objective differences in eating behavior may persist beyond the decline of some of the medications’ perceived appetitive benefits. However, neither long-term study included a direct measure of energy intake.

The goal of the present 60-wk, randomized controlled trial was to assess both objective and subjective eating behavior associated with semaglutide 2.4 mg after the point at which mean weight loss begins to plateau. The primary hypothesis was that semaglutide-treated participants would have greater short- and long-term reductions from baseline than placebo-treated participants in energy intake during an ad libitum lunch at weeks 20, 40, and 60. Secondary hypotheses were that the semaglutide group would have greater improvements in subjective appetite and food reward than the placebo group at each assessment.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a 60-wk, single-center, double-blinded, parallel-group design randomized trial conducted at the University of Pennsylvania (PA, USA), whose institutional review board approved the study protocol (clinicaltrials.gov number, NCT05548647). The trial was supervised by an independent data and safety monitoring committee, and all participants provided written informed consent. The Supplementary Methods provide additional information about the randomization procedures, dose titration schedule, lifestyle treatment, and statistical analyses.

Participants

Eligible participants were aged 18 to 70 y and had a BMI of ≥27 kg/m2 with an obesity-related comorbidity or a BMI of ≥30 kg/m2. Exclusion criteria included personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer or multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2; pancreatitis in the last 6 mo or chronic pancreatitis; types 1 or 2 diabetes or hemoglobin A1c >6.5%; renal, hepatic, or recent cardiovascular disease; blood pressure ≥160/100 mmHg; use of medications that substantially affect body weight (e.g., corticosteroids, obesity management medications) or any GLP-1RA in the past 6 mo; weight change ≥5 kg in the past 90 d; bariatric surgery; current psychotic or bipolar disorder, severe depressive or anxiety disorder, substance abuse, or suicidal ideation; past-year suicide attempt; bulimia nervosa in the past 5 y; and pregnancy/lactation. Other chronic medications were permitted, provided they had been dose-stable for ≥3 mo. Individuals who were unable or unwilling to consume the foods used in the study assessments were also excluded (gluten-free and vegetarian options were available).

Procedures

Participants were recruited between July 2022 and February 2024 via website advertisements. Applicants completed an initial phone screen. Those who appeared eligible attended a screening visit with a psychologist, who obtained written informed consent and assessed behavioral and psychological eligibility. Individuals who passed this screening completed a medical history, physical examination, fasting blood draw, and urine pregnancy test (for females of child-bearing age) to determine final eligibility.

Treatments

Eligible participants completed a baseline assessment at which they were randomly assigned, in a 3:2 ratio, to once-weekly subcutaneous semaglutide 2.4 mg/wk or a matching placebo. Trial product was initiated at 0.24 mg/wk and increased every 4 wk until a dosage of 2.4 mg/wk was reached at week 16. Participants who did not tolerate a dose increase could have a delayed escalation or could continue to receive a lower dose at the investigators’ discretion (Supplementary Table 1).

All participants received 15-min, individual lifestyle counseling sessions as used in STEP 1 [1]. Sessions were provided every 2 wk from week 0 (i.e., baseline/randomization) to week 4 and every 4 wk thereafter (17 total sessions). Session content followed a standardized intensive behavioral therapy (IBT) protocol [12], which provided calorie and physical activity goals and a curriculum of behavioral strategies.

Assessments

Participants attended 5-h, in-person assessments at weeks 0 (baseline/randomization), 20, 40, and 60. Visits were scheduled to begin between 08:00 and 09:30 after a 12-h overnight fast and occurred individually in a private setting.

Participants’ body weight (Detecto, model 6800A) and height (wall-mounted stadiometer; Veeder-Root) were measured in duplicate. A fasting blood sample was collected (safety assessment), and participants rated their fasting appetite using 100-mm visual analog scales (VAS), as described below [13,14]. They were then asked to consume, in a 15-min period, a standardized breakfast consisting of a liquid shake and conventional foods that provided a total of 550 kcal (13% protein, 25% fat, and 62% carbohydrate) [3].

VAS ratings were repeated after the completion of the breakfast and at 30-min intervals thereafter for 4 h. Between ratings, participants completed the Leeds Food Preference Task (LFPT [15,16]) and survey questionnaires. After their final VAS rating, participants were provided lunch. Participants selected their preference from among 4 entrée options (e.g., macaroni-and-cheese, lasagna) that had similar calorie and macronutrient content (∼16% protein, 40% fat, and 44% carbohydrate). Participants were provided with the same entrée at each subsequent assessment, along with standardized side offerings consisting of rolls (with butter) and fresh-baked cookies (4% protein, 50% fat, and 46% carbohydrate). Entrée and side portions were served in excess [measured mean of 1346 kcal (SD = 156) for the entrée and 2109 kcal (SD = 163) in total], and participants were instructed to eat until they were “comfortably full and satisfied.” Each food was measured to the ∼0.1 g before and after consumption. After lunch, participants completed post meal VAS ratings.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the change from baseline to weeks 20, 40, and 60 in ad libitum energy intake (kcal) as calculated from the amounts consumed during the laboratory lunch meals. Secondary endpoints were changes from baseline to weeks 20, 40, and 60 in: subjective appetite, measured both during the standardized breakfast in the lab and as experienced over the past week; explicit food reward; and implicit food reward for high-fat, savory (i.e., nonsweet) foods. Laboratory-based appetite was assessed via 100-mm VAS ratings as described above. An appetite suppression composite score was calculated for each administration by averaging ratings of hunger (reverse scored), fullness, satiety, and prospective food consumption (i.e., “how much do you think you could eat right now,” reverse scored). Fasting appetite suppression and the 4-h postprandial AUC were the prespecified endpoints. Past-week appetite was measured using separate 100-mm VAS ratings for hunger, fullness, and food preoccupation using the 2017 Control of Eating Questionnaire [2]. Past-week ratings of these appetite sensations have been shown to have strong correlations and good agreement with mean daily appetite ratings [17]. Explicit food reward was measured with the Power of Food Scale [18]. Implicit food reward (wanting) of high-fat, savory foods was measured with the LFPT [15,16], a computer task in which participants respond quickly to forced-choice pairings of foods from 4 categories: high-fat savory, high-fat sweet, low-fat savory, and low-fat sweet. A frequency-weighted algorithm was used to account for both reaction time and preference when calculating implicit wanting of each category [16].

Exploratory endpoints included: laboratory VAS ratings of nausea, thirst, and well-being [2,3], and of palatability of the lunch (mean of “how pleasant//how tasty was this meal?”) and body weight in kg. We also explored differences in fasting and postprandial AUC for each individual VAS appetite item rated in the laboratory.

A priori power analysis

In a prior study of ad libitum energy intake, Friedrichsen et al. [3] reported that reductions from baseline to week 20 were 225.0 kcal (SD = 212.8; 940 ± 889.1 kJ) larger with semaglutide 2.4 mg than with placebo, Cohen’s d = 1.06. In selecting an estimated effect size of d = 0.6 for the between-group difference in energy intake at week 60 (assuming effects of d = 1.0 and 0.8 at weeks 20 and 40, respectively), we considered the longer time frame of the present study and that effects might decline over time [7,11]. On the basis of similar studies [19], we assumed a 10% attrition rate at week 60. Given these estimates, a baseline sample size of 120 participants (72 assigned to semaglutide and 48 assigned to placebo) was expected to provide 84% effective power (the product of marginal power for the preceding outcomes) to detect between-group differences in the primary outcome at week 60. The power analysis was conducted using G∗Power 3.1.

Statistical analyses

For continuous outcomes, we conducted analyses of covariance with change in the variable as the outcome, controlling for baseline. Analyses of postprandial appetite suppression also controlled for measured energy intake during the standardized breakfast. All analyses were 2-tailed and were conducted in SPSS v.28.0.1.1 or R v.4.4.3. To control for type 1 error, the Benjamini–Hochberg step-up procedure [20] was applied to maintain a false discovery rate of 0.05 across the 8 primary and secondary outcomes (24 total comparisons). Adjusted P values are reported, and statistical significance was evaluated at α = 0.05. Exploratory endpoints were evaluated without adjustment for multiple comparisons and, therefore, cannot be used to definitively determine treatment effects.

Data were analyzed under the intention-to-treat principle, including all randomly assigned participants. Missing data were imputed using jump-to-reference multiple imputation, which assumes that individuals in the semaglutide group who discontinue treatment had an effect similar to the placebo group [21]. One hundred imputations [22] were conducted using the R package RefBasedMI (v.0.2.0) (Kevin McGrath [aut], Matteo Quartagno [cre]) and pooled using Rubin’s approach [23] using mice (v.3.18.0). Sensitivity analyses were conducted for primary and secondary outcomes using missing at random multiple imputation, which was expected to be less conservative. Primary and secondary endpoints for participants who completed the study on the drug (defined as having taken ≥1 dose of trial product within 2 wk) were also examined to characterize appetitive effects for individuals who adhered to treatment.

Figure 1 shows the progression of participants through the study. Overall, 89.2%, 74.2%, and 73.3% of participants completed assessments at weeks 20, 40, and 60, respectively. A significantly higher proportion of semaglutide-treated participants completed assessment measures at all 3 timepoints due to greater withdrawal from the trial and loss to follow-up in the placebo group.

Primary endpoint: ad libitum energy intake

The semaglutide group had significantly larger reductions from baseline in ad libitum energy intake during lunch relative to the placebo group at all 3 post baseline assessments (Table 2). Semaglutide-treated participants reduced their energy intake by 367.0 kcal (1535.5 kJ), 317.0 kcal (1326.3 kJ), and 200.0 kcal (836.8 kJ) relative to baseline at weeks 20, 40, and 60, respectively (Figure 2A). The placebo group had mean changes of −75.1 kcal (−314.2 kJ), −76.8 kcal (−321.3 kJ), and +69.6 kcal (+291.2 kJ), respectively. Post hoc analyses showed that total calorie intake was 29.9%, 24.6%, and 24.0% lower in the semaglutide group than in the placebo group at weeks 20, 40, and 60, respectively (Figure 2B). Percentage reductions from baseline in energy intake were 27.7%, 20.5%, and 26.1% more, respectively, with semaglutide than with placebo. We also explored temporal patterns in ad libitum energy intake. Post hoc analyses using linear mixed models suggested that average calorie intake during lunch did not increase significantly between weeks 20 and 40 [total mean change of 32.1 kcal, 95% confidence interval (CI): −30.9, 95.2]. Secondary outcomes: appetite and food reward

Estimated group means and mean differences for secondary outcomes are reported in Table 2. At week 20, increases from baseline in both fasting appetite suppression and postprandial appetite suppression after the standardized breakfast were significantly greater with semaglutide than with placebo (Figure 3). Participants treated with semaglutide also had significantly greater reductions from baseline to week 20 in past-week hunger and food preoccupation. Although a similar pattern was observed at weeks 40 and 60, between-group differences were smaller and no longer reached statistical significance for these outcomes. Changes from baseline in past-week fullness after meals did not differ significantly between the treatment groups at any assessment. Ratings of individual appetite sensations in the laboratory followed a similar pattern to the appetite suppression composite score (Supplementary Table 5, Supplementary Figure 1).

Body weight

Discussion

This study’s principal finding was that the larger reductions in ad libitum energy intake observed at week 20 in individuals treated with semaglutide 2.4 mg as compared with placebo were maintained at weeks 40 and 60 with continued treatment. At week 20, semaglutide-treated participants ate 292 fewer kcal during a laboratory lunch than those assigned to placebo. Semaglutide-treated participants, relative to placebo, similarly consumed 240 and 270 fewer kcal at weeks 40 and 60, respectively. The semaglutide group lost an average of 9.9 kg at week 20, with an additional 5.1 kg loss from weeks 20 to 40, and then 0.4 kg more from weeks 40 to 60, yielding a total loss of 15.4 kg (15.1%) at week 60 [corresponding changes for the placebo group were −4.7 kg, −0.8 kg, and +2.1 kg, respectively, yielding a total loss of 3.6 kg (3.4%) at week 60]. Together, these results indicate that participants treated with semaglutide continued to eat significantly less than those treated with placebo even after their weight loss had slowed (weeks 20–40) or reached a plateau (weeks 40–60).

Post hoc analyses suggested that average energy intake increased gradually in both groups after week 20 but was not significantly higher than this nadir until week 60. Despite this gradual rebound, semaglutide appeared to help participants maintain a calorie deficit relative to baseline through week 60, consistent with the sustained smaller energy reductions required to maintain weight loss [24,25]. The intake of placebo-treated participants did not differ significantly from baseline at any assessment, and they regained weight, on average, after week 40. These findings suggest that reduced energy intake is a central mechanism by which semaglutide both induces and then maintains weight loss.

Semaglutide-treated participants, relative to placebo, reported greater improvements in subjective appetite control (including fasting and postprandial appetite suppression, past-week hunger, and past-week food preoccupation) from baseline to week 20, the period of their largest, most rapid weight loss. However, at weeks 40 and 60, between-group differences in appetite as assessed both in the laboratory and over the past week were small and not statistically significant for any secondary endpoint. Within the semaglutide group, mean changes from baseline in appetite ratings appeared to decrease over time, though most CIs indicated improvement relative to baseline at all 3 assessments. Changes from baseline in the placebo group also were numerically smaller at weeks 40 and 60, as compared with week 20, for most outcomes. This pattern suggests that the smaller between-group differences at weeks 40 and 60 were driven by the semaglutide group experiencing a deterioration in initial improvements in appetite, rather than by the placebo group experiencing greater improvements later in treatment.

The gradual increases in caloric intake observed after 20 to 40 wk also could explain, in part, why appetite improvement appeared to be smaller in both groups after that time. Participants’ subjective ratings of their appetite may have been recalibrated relative to the week 20 nadir in food intake, resulting in appetite being perceived as relatively stronger, even for the semaglutide-treated participants who continued to eat less than they had at baseline.

Postbaseline decreases in responsiveness to the food environment (a measure of food reward) were larger in the semaglutide group relative to placebo at both weeks 20 and 40. This pattern was similar at week 60, but the between-group difference was small and not statistically significant (whereas there was a medium-sized effect at weeks 20 and 40). These findings largely replicate those of Wharton et al. [11], who reported differences between semaglutide and placebo in craving control, another indicator of the rewarding value of food, at 20, 52, and 104 wk. Similar to the present study, the groups studied by Wharton et al. only differed in ratings of hunger and fullness at week 20. Together, these findings suggest that semaglutide-treated individuals continue, with long-term therapy, to note greater reductions in aspects of food reward, but not in appetite.

We did not find between-group differences in implicit food reward (i.e., wanting) of high-fat, savory foods at any assessment. A majority of studies that have used the LFPT to measure implicit wanting were conducted in the United Kingdom, where the food image set was validated [16]. Differences between the typical presentation of the selected foods in the United States could have influenced their implicit appeal to our participants and impacted our ability to detect between-group differences.

Overall, these findings have important clinical implications. Patients’ willingness to continue to take an obesity management medication for weight loss maintenance may wane if they believe that it is no longer working based on perceptions of diminished appetite control. Indeed, patients’ beliefs about medication effectiveness have been identified as a predictor of discontinuation [26]. In light of these findings, patients could be counseled to expect a partial return of baseline appetite sensations after the first months of treatment, but, despite these changes, they will continue to eat less food. The continued reduction in food intake is required to maintain the new, reduced body weight, with its decreased energy requirements.

Strengths of this study included the objective measurement of energy intake in conjunction with subjective measures of appetite and food reward, which are thought to contribute to food consumption. Furthermore, the study’s longer-term design assessed these constructs until the point at which weight loss typically plateaus, so that we could examine the mechanisms that promote both the induction of weight loss and weight loss maintenance.

The study’s primary limitation was the greater-than-expected attrition in the placebo group, which was significantly higher than with semaglutide. We attribute this attrition, in part, to semaglutide’s having already been approved for weight loss and available to participants outside of the trial. Thus, some individuals who lost less weight may have opted to pursue treatment elsewhere. We used a robust method of handling missing data that produced conservative estimates of between-group differences by assuming that semaglutide-treated participants approximated the placebo group. However, we cannot know how the inclusion of the unmeasured placebo-treated participants would have influenced our results. Furthermore, the uncertainty of imputing missing values may have reduced the power to detect group differences. In addition, a majority of study participants were female, non-Hispanic, and White, and results may not generalize to other populations.

The present study only assessed ad libitum intake at a single laboratory test meal. Results from previous trials suggest that intake during subsequent meals in the laboratory would differ to a similar degree between the treatment groups [2,3]. However, laboratory studies may not capture differences in other food intake patterns that arise in a free-living environment where food choice plays a larger role in ingestive behavior and where individuals choose whether and when to eat. Future investigations of the long-term mechanisms of semaglutide should measure these effects.

In conclusion, semaglutide 2.4 mg continued to help participants consume fewer calories at 20, 40, and 60 wk of treatment. Semaglutide also continued to improve responsiveness to the food environment for ≥40 wk. In contrast, perceptions of appetite (e.g., hunger and satiety) and food preoccupation were improved relative to placebo at 20 wk but not at weeks 40 or 60. Together, these findings suggest that objective improvements in eating behavior are maintained with semaglutide even after some of its subjective benefits decline. Reduced energy intake is a key mechanism through which semaglutide both induces and maintains weight loss.
