Multifocal osteomyelitis and abscess due to Bacteroides caccae in a child with sickle cell disease
Authors: Yara Neaimeh, Mit Shah, Faryal Munir, Maria Frost, Lakshmi Srivaths, Susan H. Wootton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiagnosing osteomyelitis in pediatric patients with sickle cell disease (SCD) is challenging as bone pain is often attributed to vaso-occlusive episodes (“pain crisis”). Such episodes can result in bone infarcts which can subsequently become sites of infection. Among non-SCD patients, Staphylococcus species are the most common cause of osteomyelitis whereas Salmonella spp. are more frequently the causative organisms in SCD patients [1]. We report the first case of Bacteroides caccae osteomyelitis in a SCD pediatric patient.Case presentationOur patient is a 6-year-old non-verbal male with autism and SCD who presented with fever, headache and irritability and developed new onset back and neck pain during admission. He was subsequently diagnosed with Bacteroides caccae bacteremia complicated by multifocal osteomyelitis and L5/S1 spinal epidural abscess. He underwent left femur subperiosteal abscess irrigation and drainage and L5 bone biopsy. Pathology was consistent with acute osteomyelitis. He received long term Metronidazole with improvement in his condition.ConclusionsWe report the first case of a child with autism and SCD who developed multifocal osteomyelitis and L5-S1 spinal epidural abscess due to Bacteroides caccae with an improvement after prolonged Metronidazole therapy.

Background

SCD leads to vascular and hematologic complications and increases infection risk. The condition’s vaso-occlusive characteristics contribute to splenic infarction and functional asplenia, thereby increasing vulnerability to infections by encapsulated bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria species and Haemophilus influenzae. Moreover, this patient population has a higher risk of osteomyelitis caused by Salmonella species, potentially due to increased vascular damage in the gastrointestinal tract and bones, allowing enteric bacteria to translocate and infect necrotic bone tissue [2]. In this study, we describe a rare case of multifocal osteomyelitis with epidural spinal abscess formation caused by Bacteroides caccae in a 6-year-old boy with SCD, demonstrating the challenges in diagnosis and management.

Case presentation

A 6-year-old non-verbal male with autism and SCD SS disease was admitted with 3 days of right sided headache radiating to his neck and right upper back. Additional symptoms included generalized irritability and multifocal abdominal, head and neck pain. The patient had two recent admissions including one 3 weeks prior for pain crises and another 1 week prior for cough and fever. At admission, the patient was febrile (101.8 °F), had mild leukocytosis (13.7 × 10^3/uL) and elevated procalcitonin (32.26 ng/mL) and CRP (278 mg/L). His physical exam was unremarkable except for limited neck range of motion. Chest radiograph was significant for left lower lung opacity concerning for atelectasis versus pneumonia and lumbar puncture results included neutrophilic-predominant pleocytosis (WBC 9/mm3 with 51% neutrophils, glucose 59 mg/dl, protein 16 mg/dl, RBC 1/mm3). Initial blood, urine, and CSF cultures were negative. MRI brain with and without contrast was unremarkable. Broad antibiotic therapy (Vancomycin, Ceftriaxone and Azithromycin) was initiated.

On hospital day (HD) 3, he continued having febrile episodes with new onset back and lower extremity pain. On HD 4, spine and bilateral hips, femurs, and knees MRI with and without contrast showed extensive spinal (T1, T3, T5, T10, L1-L3 and L5), pelvic, and bilateral femoral infarcts as well as L5/S1 epidural/paraspinal collection concerning for phlegmon and left femoral subperiosteal fluid collection (Fig. 1). On HD 4, patient was admitted to Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) for intensive monitoring given the concern for cord compression. On HD 5, antimicrobial therapy was escalated from Ceftriaxone to Meropenem. On HD 8, Bacteroides caccae was isolated from HD 5 anaerobic blood culture, and the patient underwent left femur subperiosteal abscess irrigation and drainage during which time 30–40 mL of purulent fluid was drained. On HD 9, Interventional Radiology (IR) performed a L5 bone biopsy which yielded around 15 mL of purulent drainage. B. caccae was detected by 16 S bacterial DNA PCR testing of femoral and L5 drainage. In addition, Microbial Cell-Free DNA testing detected B. caccae [92,903 DNA molecules per microliter]. Femur and L5 bone biopsies were consistent with acute osteomyelitis. On HD 18, Meropenem was de-escalated to Metronidazole.

The patient was clinically stable with improving inflammatory markers. However, he continued to have daily febrile episodes. On HD 31, due to persistent febrile episodes, empiric Ampicillin-Sulbactam was started and an echo was negative for vegetations. On HD 32, a whole-body positron emission tomography – computed tomography (PET-CT) showed multifocal bone hypermetabolic activity, compatible with multiple sites of known osteomyelitis, most intense at the L5 vertebral body level and left distal femur (Fig. 2). On HD 34, he developed PICC-line associated Streptococcus mitis/oralis bacteremia and received Vancomycin for 15 days. On HD 38, left femur, bilateral humeri and lumbar spine MRI showed recurrence of the left femur subperiosteal abscess. As such, he underwent a second left femur subperiosteal abscess irrigation and drainage during which time a small amount of murky-colored fluid was expressed, indicative of abscess reformation. Empiric Ampicillin-Sulbactam was discontinued on HD 38 and he was transitioned to oral Metronidazole on HD 47 after completing 6 weeks of IV therapy. On HD 51, he was discharged to continue long-term Metronidazole. At discharge, plain films showed osteo-necrotic lesions on L5, bilateral humeri, and distal left femur. After discharge, patient was seen by dentistry for known right upper molar cavity (tooth #14, diagnosed 1 year prior and was being followed-up prior to admission). Plain films noted significant decay so patient underwent tooth extraction during which time no abscess, purulence or gingival inflammation was noted. As expected, multiple organisms were isolated from the extracted tooth culture (Streptococcus mitis/oralis, Candida albicans, and Lactobacillus Plantarum) and swab of gum site (Prevotella Nigrescens, Prevotella melaninogenica, Candida albicans, Streptococcus mitis/oralis, Haemophilus parainfluenzae, Neisseria sicca, and Actinomyces naeslundii).

After discharge, his clinical status continued to improve and inflammatory markers trended down, normalizing at 5 months. Serial extremity plain films and repeat spine CT/MRI imaging showed improvement in osteomyelitis sites except for the L5 vertebral body (Fig. 3). Two weeks post discharge, lumbar spine plain films showed increased bony destruction of L5 vertebral with loss of height; however, no neuroforaminal compression was noted. CT lumbar spine done 4 months after discharge showed no focal erosion to indicate the presence of acute osteomyelitis. As of 6 months post discharge, patient was able to ambulate independently for short distances but with a wide based gait. Strength and gait were expected to improve with physical therapy and improved core strength. He received Metronidazole for a total duration of 5 months. After therapy completion, given his underlying SCD, he underwent stem-cell transplant.

Discussion

In this report, we summarize the first case of Bacteroides caccae bacteremia leading to multifocal osteomyelitis and spinal epidural abscess in a 6-year-old boy with SCD. Osteomyelitis is a significant complication of SCD, affecting approximately 18% of patients, with Salmonella and Staphylococcus species being the most common organisms. Although rare, there have been few case reports of Bacteroides species osteomyelitis in SCD patients, notably due to Bacteroides fragilis [3–5]. In these reports, infection primarily affected children and young adults, with the vertebra being the most frequently involved site followed by tibia and humerus [4]. Similar to our patient, presentations typically included fever and focal pain prior to admission. Management has been variable. Although some cases, including ours, require surgical debridement in addition to antimicrobial therapy, other cases have been successfully managed with antibiotics alone without surgical intervention [4, 5]. To our knowledge, there has been no previously published cases of Bacteroides caccae osteomyelitis, though it has been reported as a causative organism of bloodstream infections in adult patients [6, 7].

Most Bacteroides species are part of the normal flora of the oral, gastrointestinal and genitourinary tracts. Proposed pathway of infection in individuals with SCD involve intestinal mucosal ischemia, resulting in bacterial translocation and colonization within the metaphysis of infarcted long bones, causing osteomyelitis. This can happen with the absence of abdominal symptoms [8]. In addition to mucosal bacterial translocation, anaerobic bacterial osteomyelitis, such as Bacteroides and Fusobacterium, have infected individuals after dental procedures and as complications of tooth decay. Complications included jaw, ankle and hip osteomyelitis [9,10]. In our patient, dental caries were present around 1 year prior to admission and were being followed-up. Tooth extraction and subsequent cultures were obtained after several months of metronidazole therapy and did not yield Bacteroides species, limiting our ability to determine whether his history of dental caries contributed to the infection. Nevertheless, the most likely source remains gastrointestinal translocation with subsequent hematogenous seeding of bone in the setting of underlying sickle cell disease. Of note, Streptococcus mitis/oralis was isolated from the tooth culture, suggesting a potential oral source for the patient’s prior S. mitis/oralis bacteremia.

Distinguishing between vaso-occlusive pain crises and osteomyelitis-related pain is challenging. Berger et al. conducted a retrospective study of children age < = 18 years with SCD presenting with bone pain to identify clinical and laboratory features predictive of osteomyelitis. Significant predictors of osteomyelitis were duration of fever (OR, 1.8; 95% CI, 1.2–2.6) and pain (1.2; 1.0-1.4) before presentation and swelling of the affected limb (8.4; 3.5–20.0). The risk of osteomyelitis was decreased if more than 1 painful site was present (OR, 0.7; 95% CI, 0.5-1.0) [11]. Our patient had several days of fever at home and during prior admission, but no limb swelling upon presentation. Determination of pain location was challenging due to patient’s non-verbal status. It is worth noting that nutritional deficiencies, common among children with autism can lead to both symptomatic and radiographic findings similar to osteomyelitis [12, 13]. In a report by Gongidi et al., an autistic child with scurvy presented with osseous findings including subperiosteal fluid collection, cortical demineralization, and subperiosteal hemorrhaging [14]. Our patient had weight loss of 5.4 kg during admission. Nutrition assessment was performed, and he was noted to have decreased oral intake. No vitamin level was obtained.

The patient’s course was particularly challenging due to prolonged, intermittent fever occurring over 7 weeks. Among patients with osteomyelitis, multiple complications can occur including pathologic fractures or subperiosteal abscess that may require additional intervention [15]. In a review of 319 pediatric cases of bacterial osteomyelitis without SCD by Krzysztofiak et al., severe presentation with sepsis and hypergammaglobulinemia were associated with a higher frequency of late sequelae [16]. A variety of scoring systems have been published to assist in the management of osteomyelitis including the acute complication score (A-score) and chronic complication score (C-score) [17, 18]. Our patient exhibited prolonged fever, bone abscess development, and evidence of disseminated disease, resulting in an A-score exceeding 4, indicating a high risk of acute complications. Additionally, he had a consistently elevated CRP level for more than 4 days, yielding the maximum C-score of 4, thus signifying an increased risk of outpatient complications. However, these scores were not validated in SCD patients. Our patient’s prolonged fever was due to need for repeat surgical drainage.

Our case highlighted the utility of different imaging modalities among SCD patients with osteomyelitis. In general, and per the 2021 Clinical Practice Guideline by the Pediatric Infectious Diseases Society and the Infectious Diseases Society of America, both plain films and MRI are used to evaluate patients with suspected osteomyelitis. While plain radiographs have limited sensitivity for the diagnosis of acute osteomyelitis, they remain valuable in the initial evaluation to identify or exclude other pathologic conditions, including fractures and bone neoplasms. MRI of the affected bone is the preferred imaging modality for confirming the diagnosis and defining the extent of infection [19]. However, interpretation of MRI among patients with SCD and osteomyelitis can be challenging due to the difficulty in differentiation between infection and infarction [20]. Other imaging modalities include bone scintigraphy, which involves relatively low radiation exposure but has limited ability to delineate disease extent and often requires follow-up with MRI. Technetium-labeled WBC scans represent another option though experience with the diagnosis of osteomyelitis in children is more limited compared to bone scans [19, 21]. In our case, an FDG-PET/CT scan was obtained for further evaluation in the setting of persistent fever and difficulty localizing pain due to patient’s non-verbal status. Bone scintigraphy and technetium-labeled WBC imaging were not pursued, as a whole-body imaging modality was preferred to comprehensively evaluate for occult infection across multiple potential sites, differentiating between infected and healed bone. With a sensitivity of 81%-100% and a specificity of 87%-100%, FDG-PET/CT provides high resolution imaging for diagnosis of osteomyelitis, assessing multifocal infection sites when hematogenous spread is suspected [22]. In this patient, the PET/CT scan was valuable in excluding additional sites of infection and identifying recurrence of the femur sub-periosteal abscess.

Regarding antimicrobial management, most cases of acute osteomyelitis without a drainable abscess can be effectively managed with a brief period of intravenous antibiotics, followed by at least three weeks of oral antibiotics. Longer durations are typically required for chronic osteomyelitis. The total duration of antibiotic therapy is guided by the patient’s clinical condition and progress, inflammatory markers improvement and whether additional debridement surgeries are needed [19]. Extended antibiotic durations are necessary in cases of complex infections with advanced disease or spinal involvement [15, 23]. In light of reported failure rates in chronic osteomyelitis management in adult patients, some authors advocate for a total antimicrobial course of approximately 4–5 months, consisting of 6–8 week intravenous therapy followed by an extended oral regimen [24]. In our case, the patient was initially started on Meropenem and subsequently de-escalated to Metronidazole on HD 18 in addition to surgical interventions given the extent of his disease. He was later transitioned to oral Metronidazole after completing 6 weeks of IV therapy. Bacteroides caccae was identified from anaerobic blood cultures. No susceptibility testing was performed in our institution as this organism is known to be susceptible to Meropenem and Metronidazole [25, 26]. Metronidazole provided more targeted therapy while facilitating transition to an oral regimen. Patient received therapy for Bacteroides osteomyelitis for a total duration of 5 months. Duration was guided by normalization of inflammatory markers at 5 months, radiographic evidence of new bone formation and reassuring CT spine findings at 4 months, as well as clinical improvement, with the patient achieving independent ambulation by approximately 6 months. The epidural collection was drained by interventional radiology. However, no surgical debridement of bone was performed. This was also considered in favoring a more prolonged and conservative treatment duration.

Finally, it is important to emphasize that our patient had several admissions before arriving at the correct diagnosis. A retrospective study performed by Congdon et al. from October 2018 to September 2020 found that diagnostic errors leading to pediatric readmissions were attributed to delay in considering correct diagnosis and failure in ordering the appropriate diagnostic tests. The review did not identify any specific conditions associated with a higher rate of diagnostic errors [27]. In a case study by Kinlin et al., a 10-year-old boy with autism had scurvy initially misdiagnosed as osteomyelitis due to his non-verbal status [28]. In our case, the patient was readmitted twice before the correct diagnosis was determined and appropriate management began. This was attributed to the patient’s non-verbal state and the challenges in assessing his symptoms.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we report the first case of a child with autism and SCD who developed multifocal osteomyelitis and L5-S1 spinal epidural abscess due to Bacteroides caccae with an improvement in patient’s condition after prolonged Metronidazole therapy. Children with developmental delay are at increased risk for missed diagnoses underlying the importance heightened clinical diligence, diagnostic quality and coordinated subspecialty care. Imaging modalities such as PET/CT offer additional strategy for managing complications of osteomyelitis.
