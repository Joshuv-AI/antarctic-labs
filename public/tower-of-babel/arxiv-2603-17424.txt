Lattice Structure and Efficient Basis Construction for Strongly Connected Orientations
Siyue Liu, Olha Silina

Abstract
Let G → = ( V , E + ∪ E − ) \vec{G}=(V,E^{+}\cup E^{-}) be a bidirected graph whose underlying undirected graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) is 2 2 -edge-connected. A strongly connected orientation (SCO) is defined as a subset of arcs that contains exactly one of e + , e − e^{+},e^{-} for every e ∈ E e\in E and induces a strongly connected subgraph of G → \vec{G} . Given a family ℱ \mathcal{F} of proper subsets of V V , we call an SCO tight if there is exactly one arc entering U U for every U ∈ ℱ U\in\mathcal{F} . We give a polynomial-time algorithm to construct a set ℬ \mathcal{B} consisting of tight SCO’s which forms an integral basis for the linear hull of tight SCO’s. This means that ℬ \mathcal{B} is a linearly independent subset of tight SCO’s, and every integer vector in the linear hull of tight SCO’s can be written as an integral combination of ℬ \mathcal{B} . This extends the main result of Abdi, Conuéjols, Liu and Silina (IPCO 2025), who gave a non-constructive proof of the existence of such a basis in an equivalent setting. While their proof uses polyhedral theory, our proof is purely combinatorial and yields a polynomial-time algorithm. As an application of our algorithm, we show that parity-constrained tight strongly connected orientation can be solved in deterministic polynomial time. Along the way, we discover appealing connections to the theory of perfect matching lattices.

Introduction

Given a
2
-edge-connected undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, its

graph
\vec{G}=(V,E^{+}\cup E^{-})
is obtained from copying every edge
e\in E

and

In this work, we consider a class of SCO’s restricted by a family

Given

(G,\mathcal{F})
. We study the
lattice of tight strongly connected orientations

\lat(\sco(G,\mathcal{F}))

\lat(\sco(G,\mathcal{F}))\subseteq\lin(\sco(G,\mathcal{F}))\cap\mathbb{Z}^{A}

are linearly independent. Thereby we may assume w.l.o.g. that

(G,\mathcal{F})
is bounded by the size of the graph. Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1

Let

2
-edge-connected undirected graph. For an arbitrary

This result implies two things: first,

This result is essentially the algorithmic counterpart of Abdi, Conuéjols, Liu and Silina

3

Theorem 1.2

(
[
3

Let

One can easily go between tight strengthenings in a digraph

Our main motivation for studying tight strengthenings is the following conjecture by Woodall
[
48
]
.
In a digraph, a
dicut
is the set of arcs leaving a nonempty proper vertex subset with no incoming arcs, i.e., an arc set of the form
\delta^{+}(U)
for some
\emptyset\neq U\subsetneqq V
such that
\delta^{-}(U)=\emptyset

is an arc subset that intersects every dicut at least once. In order to make the digraph strongly connected, at least one arc from each dicut has to be flipped, and thus, every strengthening is a dijoin. Woodall conjectured that the maximum number of disjoint dijoins is equal to the minimum size of a dicut. Schrijver
[
44

reformulated Woodall’s conjecture in the following:

Conjecture 1.3

(
[
44
]
)
.

Let
\tau\geq 2
be an integer, and let
D=(V,A)
be a digraph where the minimum size of a dicut is
\tau
. Then
A
can be partitioned into
\tau
strengthenings.

Observe that if such a statement were true, the only strengthenings allowed in a partition would be the ones that intersect every dicut of size
\tau

exactly
once. Therefore, it suffices to restrict ourselves to those strengthenings, which are precisely
\scr(D,\mathcal{F})
for
\mathcal{F}
being the family of sets
U\subseteq V
that induce dicuts
\delta^{+}(U)
of size
\tau
.
Theorem

1.2
implies a relaxation of

[
3

\lambda_{J}\in\mathbb{Z}

and
\sum_{J}\lambda_{J}=\tau

Proof overview

We outline the proof of
Theorem

Reduction to digrafts

First, we reduce from SCO’s in undirected graphs to dijoins in bipartite digraphs. A digraph is
bipartite
if every node is a source or a sink. The following notion of
digrafts
is defined similarly in
[
3
]
:

Definition 1.5

(digraft)
.

A
digraft
is a pair
(D,\mathcal{F})
where
D=(V=S\dot{\cup}T,A)
is a bipartite digraph with sources
S
and sinks
T
such that
|S|\leq|T|
, whose underlying undirected graph is
2
-edge-connected, and
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{V}\setminus\{\emptyset,V\}
such that (a) if
U\in\mathcal{F}
then it induces a dicut
\delta^{+}(U)
, (b)
V\setminus v\in\mathcal{F}
for every sink
v\in T
.

We will be interested in a specific class of dijoins. We say a dijoin is
tight
if it intersects
\delta^{+}(U)
exactly once for every
U\in\mathcal{F}
. Denote by
\mathcal{J}(D,\mathcal{F})
the set of tight dijoins of
(D,\mathcal{F})
. A digraft is
tight dijoin-covered
if every arc
e\in A
is contained in some tight dijoin. A dicut is
tight
if every tight dijoin intersects it exactly once. A node
v\in V
is
tight
if every tight dijoin has degree
1
on
v
. The order in which we define tight dijoins and tight dicuts (nodes) is important for these definitions. Tight dijoins are explicitly defined as degree-constrained dijoins, while tight dicuts are implicit, dependent on tight dijoins.

For an arbitrary
2
-edge-connected undirected graph
G
and a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{V}\setminus\{\emptyset,V\}
, there is an identity map between
\sco(G,\mathcal{F})
and
\mathcal{J}(D^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
for some digraft
(D^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})

[
10
,
3
]
. Thereby we may restrict ourselves to the setting of digrafts. We give the proof of the reduction to digrafts in
Appendix

A
.

Non-basic digrafts

We further reduce general digrafts to the following class of digrafts, where the family of dicuts
\mathcal{F}
consists only of shores of trivial dicuts.

Formally, let
D=(V=S\dot{\cup}T,A)
be a bipartite digraph and let
S^{t}\subseteq S
be a subset of sources. We consider digrafts
(D,\mathcal{F})
where
\mathcal{F}:=\big\{\{v\}:v\in S^{t}\big\}\bigcup\big\{V\setminus v:v\in T\big\}
. For simplicity, we denote these types of digrafts as
(D,S^{t})
. Define
V^{t}:=S^{t}\cup T
.

In this setting, a tight dijoin is simply a dijoin that has exactly one arc incident to every
v\in V^{t}
.
Denote the set of tight nodes as
\widebar{V^{t}}
and the set of tight sources as
\widebar{S^{t}}
. Note that every
v\in V^{t}
is a tight node, and thus
V^{t}\subseteq\widebar{V^{t}}
and
S\subseteq\widebar{S^{t}}
, but there could be more vertices in
\widebar{V^{t}}\setminus V^{t}
that are forced to have degree
1
for every tight dijoin. Also notice that every sink is tight by definition, so
\widebar{V^{t}}\setminus V^{t}
consists solely of sources. By definition of tight nodes,
\mathcal{J}(D,S^{t})=\mathcal{J}(D,\widebar{S^{t}})
.

General digrafts
(D,\mathcal{F})
can be reduced to digrafts
(D,S^{t})
through
tight dicut decomposition
, which is described as follows. A dicut
\delta^{+}(U)
is
nontrivial
if
1<|U|<|V|-1
; otherwise, it is
trivial
. If there is some
U\in\mathcal{F}
with
1<|U|<|V|-1
, it induces a nontrivial tight dicut
C=\delta^{+}(U)
. By contracting the shores
U
,
V\setminus U
while preserving the family
\mathcal{F}
of tight dicut out-shores induced on the smaller digraphs, one obtains two digrafts, called
C
-contractions
. When
C
is a nontrivial tight dicut, there is a natural way to obtain an integral basis for
\lin(\mathcal{J}(D,\mathcal{F}))
by combining the bases of the two
C
-contractions (
Lemma

3.8
from
[
2
]
), which can be done in polynomial time. After repeatedly applying this process, we may assume
\mathcal{F}
contains only trivial dicut out-shores. This way, we reduce
(D,\mathcal{F})
to a list of digrafts of the form
(D,S^{t})
.

A digraft
(D,S^{t})
may still have (implicit) nontrivial tight dicuts. The next step is to further apply tight dicut decomposition on
(D,S^{t})
. To do this algorithmically, we identify two types of tight dicuts:
barrier dicuts
and
2
-separation dicuts
. The terminology is inspired by matching theory
[
28
,
27
]
. The following theorem allows us to find a nontrivial tight dicut for a digraft
(D,S^{t})
in polynomial time, if one exists.

Theorem 1.6
.

A tight dijoin-covered digraft
(D,S^{t})
has no nontrivial tight dicuts if and only if it does not contain any nontrivial barrier dicuts or
2
-separation dicuts.

After applying tight dicut decomposition, we obtain a list of
basic digrafts
, defined as follows.

Definition 1.7

(basic digraft)
.

A digraft
(D,S^{t})
is
basic
if it is tight dijoin-covered, and does not contain any nontrivial tight dicuts.

We further differentiate between two types of basic digrafts.

Definition 1.8

(brick, brace)
.

A basic digraft
(D,S^{t})
is a
brick
if
|S|<|T|
; it is a
brace
if
|S|=|T|
.

The terminology of bricks and braces is inspired by the theory of matching lattices by Lovász
[
28
]
, and Edmonds, Pulleyblank and Lovász
[
17
]
. Interestingly, in a tight dijoin-covered digraft
(D,S^{t})
with
|S|=|T|
, a subset
J\subseteq A
is a tight dijoin if and only if it is a perfect matching. So, our definition of braces is the same as the one for perfect matchings
[
28
]
. This is not the case for bricks. Nevertheless, we reuse the terminology due to its crucial role in determining the dimension of the lattice (
Theorem

1.9
). Similar to the perfect matching setting
[
17
]
, we show that the results of any two tight dicut decomposition procedures on a digraft
(D,\mathcal{F})
are the same list of bricks and braces up to the multiplicities of the arcs (
3.9
). In particular, the number of bricks after applying tight dicut decomposition to
(D,\mathcal{F})
is invariant, which we denote as
b(D,\mathcal{F})
.
The following is our main theorem for digrafts:

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
(D=(V,A),\mathcal{F})
be a tight dijoin-covered digraft. Then, we can construct an integral basis
\mathcal{B}
for
\lin(\mathcal{J}(D,\mathcal{F}))
that consists of tight dijoins in polynomial time. Moreover,
|\mathcal{B}|=|A|-|\widebar{V^{t}}|-b(D,\mathcal{F})+2
.

From now, we may assume the digraft is either a brick or a brace. The case of braces is identical to the setting of bipartite matchings so we omit it. We sketch the main steps in our proof to deal with bricks.
The first step is to reduce the digraft to
robust
ones, and then to
elementary
ones. We present how to construct integral basis for
\lin(\mathcal{J}(D,\mathcal{F}))
in the bottom-up order: elementary, robust, and non-robust digrafts.

Elementary digrafts

For the base case of the proof we define a new class of digrafts.

Definition 1.10

(elementary digraft)
.

A digraft
(D,S^{t})
is
elementary
if it is tight dijoin-covered and
|S^{t}|=|S|-1
.

In an elementary digraft, every tight dijoin has a fixed degree on every
v\in V
. In particular, when
|S|=|T|
, a tight dijoin is a perfect matching. Thus, it includes the case where
(D,S^{t})
is a brace. We generalize the
ear decomposition
for bipartite matchings to elementary digrafts (see e.g.
[
38
]
Ch 4.1). We build a sequence of elementary digrafts by removing one ear at a time. The addition of each ear will increase the dimension of the lattice by exactly one, so we will add an appropriate tight dijoin to the basis. This gives us an algorithm to construct an integral basis for
\lin(\mathcal{J}(D,S^{t}))
.

Non-elementary robust digrafts

An
edge cover
is a subset of edges that has degree at least
1
on every
v\in V
. We say an edge cover is
tight
if it has degree
1
on every
v\in V^{t}
. Since in a digraft every node
v\in V
induces a dicut
\delta(v)
, every tight dijoin is a tight edge cover, but the converse is not necessarily true. We say a tight dijoin-covered digraft is
robust
if the opposite also holds:

Definition 1.11

(robust digraft)
.

A digraft
(D,S^{t})
is
robust
if it is tight dijoin-covered and every tight edge cover is a tight dijoin.

Definition 1.12

(separating dicut)
.

In a tight dijoin-covered digraft
(D,S^{t})
, a dicut
C
is
separating
if it is nontrivial and there is a tight edge cover
J
such that
J\cap C=\emptyset
.

Clearly, a tight dijoin-covered digraft is robust if and only if it does not have a separating dicut. We give a polynomial time to test whether a digraft is robust:

Theorem 1.13
.

There exists a polynomial-time algorithm that, given a digraft
(D,S^{t})
, checks whether
(D,S^{t})
is robust. Moreover, if the answer is no, it returns a separating dicut.

In robust digrafts, a tight dijoin only needs to cover trivial dicuts as opposed to the entire family of dicuts. This makes robust digrafts exhibit many nice properties. We show that in a robust digraft
(D,S^{t})
with
|S^{t}|<|S|-1
, the digraft
(D,S^{t}\cup\{v\})
obtained from setting an arbitrary
v\in S\setminus S^{t}
to be tight remains robust (
4.4
). Furthermore, there exists a tight dijoin
J
of
(D,S^{t})
that has exactly
2
arcs incident with
v
(
4.5
). We show that adding
\{J\}
to an integral basis for
\lin(\mathcal{J}(D,S^{t}\cup\{v\}))
gives us an integral basis for
\lin(\mathcal{J}(D,S^{t}))
. Repeatedly setting the vertices in
S\setminus S^{t}
to be tight, we arrive to the case of elementary digrafts with
|S^{t}|=|S|-1
.

Non-robust bricks

This is the most challenging case of our proof. We need to further reduce the brick
(D,S^{t})
if it is not robust. To this end, we either set a non-tight vertex
v\in S\setminus S^{t}
to be tight or contract a nontrivial dicut. Such dicuts have to be one of the following:

We say a digraft
(D,S^{t})
is a
near-brick
if it is tight dijoin-covered and has exactly one brick, i.e.,
b(D,S^{t})=1
. Ideally, we would like to find a stable dicut or contractible dicut
C
such that after setting
C
to be tight, the dimension of the lattice only drops by one. For this, we define the following notion of
good dicuts
, which turns out to have the aforementioned property:

Definition 1.14

(good dicut)
.

In a brick
(D,S^{t})
, a trivial dicut induced by node
u\in S\setminus S^{t}
is
good
if
(D,(S^{t})^{\prime}:=S^{t}\cup\{u\})
is a near-brick and
\widebar{(S^{t})^{\prime}}=(S^{t})^{\prime}
. A nontrivial dicut

is
good
if both
C
-contractions
(D_{i},S_{i}^{t})
are near-bricks and satisfy
\widebar{S_{i}^{t}}=S_{i}^{t}
,
i=1,2
.

Recall that we can find a separating dicut in polynomial time if a digraft is not robust. This dicut will be a starting point for a search of good dicuts. We give a polynomial time algorithm to find a good dicut, starting from an arbitrary separating dicut (Theorems
5.2
and
5.3
).
The algorithm is a dual procedure where we keep finding improving dicuts
C
in the sense that for an arbitrary dijoin
J
, the size of their intersection
|J\cap C|
is non-increasing. We show that the dicuts we find are linearly independent and thus the length of the procedure is bounded by the dimension of the linear hull. The structural
Theorem

1.6
plays a crucial role in finding improving dicuts.

Finally, after finding a good dicut
C
, we set
C
to be tight if it is trivial or contract
C
if it is nontrivial. We construct a basis for the tight dijoins that intersect
C
exactly once. It turns out we only need to add one additional tight dijoin
J
to the basis, with the property that
|J\cap C|=2
(
4.6
). The existence of such a tight dijoin follows from the

(
2.4
from
[
2
]
) and can be found in polynomial time. This concludes the basis construction for non-robust bricks.

Comparison to existing proofs

Abdi, Cornuéjols, Liu and Silina
[
3
]
initiated the study of the lattice of tight strengthenings. They gave a non-constructive proof of
Theorem

1.2
. Their argument can be summarized as follows. First, they use the same reduction to the digraft setting
(D,\mathcal{F})
. They closely work with the polyhedron of tight dijoins,
\dij(D,\mathcal{F}):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{A}:x(\delta^{+}(U))\geq 1\ \forall\text{ dicut }U;\ x(\delta^{+}(U))=1\ \forall U\in\mathcal{F}\}
, which is given by
[
30
]
. They reduce to basic digrafts by contracting tight dicuts. This step is non-constructive. Observe that
\dij(D,\mathcal{F})
of the basic digraft does not have implicit equalities except those defined by trivial tight dicuts induced by tight nodes. If all facet-defining inequalities (FDI) are defined by trivial dicuts or non-negativity constraints, the digraft is robust (this is indeed equivalent to
1.11
because the bipartite edge cover polytope is integral, see e.g.
[
42
]
Ch 19.5). Using the integer decomposition property of tight edge covers and a result of Gerards and Sebő
[
22
]
, they show that an integral basis consisting of tight dijoins exists. If there is a nontrivial dicut
C=\delta^{+}(U)
that induces an FDI, they set
C
to be tight which reduces the dimension of
\lin(\mathcal{J}(D,\mathcal{F}))
by one. Using the Jump-free lemma, they are able to find one additional tight dijoin
J
with
|J\cap C|=2
to add to the basis.
To make their proof constructive, the highly nontrivial step of finding an FDI defined by a nontrivial dicut must be made algorithmic. For a general polyhedra, the problem of FDI recognition is
D^{p}
-hard
[
37
]
.
Even for well-structured polyhedra such as perfect matching polytope, the similar question of finding an FDI defined by a nontrivial odd set is open. We discuss this in detail in the next paragraph.
We overcome these difficulties by first, providing a recognition algorithm for robust digrafts and second, finding a good dicut that is, in fact, an FDI in polynomial time.

Perfect matching lattices

Some results of this work are reminiscent of the setting of perfect matchings. In an (undirected) graph
G=(V,E)
a
perfect matching
is a set of edges that is incident with each
v\in V
exactly once. A graph is
matching-covered
if every edge

belongs to some perfect matching. Denote the set of perfect matchings in

A well-celebrated result due to Lovász

in the theory of matching covered graphs is that for every

In a series of follow-up works of Carvalho, Lucchesi, and Murty, it was shown that the lattice of perfect matchings

In proving these results, the authors leverage many structural results on matching-covered graphs, such as tight cut decomposition established by Edmonds, Pulleyblank and Lovász
[

]
. The notions of

We give a characterization of when a digraft has a tight dijoin that has an interesting resemblance to Tutte’s theorem for perfect matchings
[

]
. This allows us to identify similar notions of barrier dicuts and
2
-separation dicuts in digrafts.
However, there are important distinctions between perfect matchings and tight dijoins in digrafts. For example,
a well-studied problem in matching theory is testing whether a graph is

[

,
12

11

13

.
In contrast, we are able to recognize robust digrafts in polynomial time, which are precisely the digrafts whose polytope of tight dijoins are solely defined by non-negativity and degree constraints. This distinction allows us to greatly simplify the proof of Carvalho, Lucchesi, and Murty, and bypass the hardness of finding the analogous notion of contractible cuts in perfect matchings.

Congruency-constrained optimization

The literature of congruency-constrained combinatorial optimization is quite rich, e.g.,

[

19

,

In particular, it is a major open problem whether there is a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm for the

, and a number

Tight strongly connected orientations can be captured by matroid intersection
[
21

Our result on parity strongly connected orientations provides a new class of matroid intersection whose parity-constrained problem can be solved in polynomial time, generalizing the result on perfect matchings for bipartite graphs.

3.2
Two types of tight dicuts and basic digraft recognition

Consider an arbitrary tight dijoin-covered digraft
(D,S^{t})

Barrier dicuts.

First, if there is some
\emptyset\neq X\subseteq S^{t}\text{ or }\emptyset\neq X\subseteq T
, such that
\sigma(V\setminus X)=|X|
, then each connected component of

induces a tight dicut. Indeed, suppose

U_{1},...,U_{k}
be the connected components of

Then, for each

\delta^{+}(U_{i})=\emptyset
, which means
\delta^{-}(U_{i})
is a dicut.
Thus, every dijoin

should satisfy
|J\cap\delta^{-}(U_{i})|\geq 1
. In particular,

should have at least one arc going from

to
U_{i}
for each
U_{i}
since
U_{1},...,U_{k}
are disconnected with each other. Therefore,

. Moreover, if

is a tight dijoin,
d_{J}(v)=1\ \forall v\in X

. Thus,

. Therefore, there is exactly one arc going from

to each

. We refer to each dicut induced by the

or

is a sink because

is connected. Therefore,

and

Second, if there exist

-matching in

.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

6.1
.

Let
(D=(S\dot{\cup}T,A),S^{t})
be an elementary digraft.
Let
J_{0}
be an arbitrary tight dijoin and let

. Let
S_{0}:=\{s_{0}\}
and

. Let
(D_{0}=(S_{0}\dot{\cup}T_{0},F),S^{t}_{0}=\emptyset)

is a tight dijoin of

. If

, say

. Pick an arbitrary tight dijoin

is tight dijoin-covered. The symmetric difference

and

be the alternating path in

is tight dijoin-covered because

We next show how to construct a basis

for
\lat(\mathcal{J}(D,S^{t}))

is a source and the other is a sink. We may assume w.l.o.g. that

and

be defined as

and

to elementary digraft

-matching

in the digraft obtained from deleting

and

if

to

. Further let

be obtained from adding even arcs of

to

for

Let

be a brace. Let

be an arbitrary subset of sources. Since

, every tight dijoin of

has degree exactly

on each

∎

Parity-constrained strongly connected orientations

Recall the parity strongly connected orientation (parity SCO) problem. Given a
2
-edge-connected undirected graph
G=(V,E)
whose bidirected graph is
\vec{G}=(V,E^{+}\cup E^{-})
, a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{V}\setminus\{\emptyset,V\}
, and a subset of red arcs
R\subseteq E^{+}\cup E^{-}
, we say a tight SCO of
(G,\mathcal{F})
is
even (odd)
if it has an odd (even) number of red arcs. The goal is to find a tight even (odd) SCO of
(G,\mathcal{F})
if it exists.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

First, we reduce parity SCO to
odd SCO
, i.e., find a SCO with an odd number of red arcs. Suppose we are looking for a tight even SCO of
(G,\mathcal{F})
. We may assume without loss of generality that at least one
u\in V
has
\{u\},V\setminus\{u\}\notin\mathcal{F}
, since the out (in) degree of
u
in any strongly connected orientation is implied by the out (in) degrees of vertices in
V\setminus\{u\}
. Let
G^{\prime}
be obtained from adding a new node
w
and two parallel edges
e,f=\{u,w\}
, so that
e^{+},f^{+}=(u,w)
and
e^{-},f^{-}=(w,u)
. Color
e^{+},f^{+}
red. Let

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}:=\Big\{U:U\in\mathcal{F},u\notin U\Big\}\bigcup\Big\{U\cup\{w\}:U\in\mathcal{F},u\in U\Big\}.

Every tight SCO
O^{\prime}
in
(G^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
is the union of a tight SCO
O
in
(G,\mathcal{F})
and either
\{e^{+},f^{-}\}
or
\{f^{+},e^{-}\}
. Moreover,
O
is a tight even SCO of
(G,\mathcal{F})
if and only if
O^{\prime}
is a tight odd SCO of
(G^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
.

Now, we describe how to solve the odd SCO problem.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a
2
-edge-connected graph, whose bidirected graph is
\vec{G}=(V,E^{+}\cup E^{-})
. We use
Theorem

1.1
to construct a basis
\mathcal{B}
for
\lat(\sco(G,\mathcal{F}))
that consists of tight SCO’s. Since every tight SCO
O^{*}
can be written as
\mathbf{1}_{O^{*}}=\sum_{O\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda_{O}\mathbf{1}_{O}
for some
\lambda_{O}\in\mathbb{Z}\ \forall O\in\mathcal{B}
, if
(G,\mathcal{F})
has a tight odd SCO
O^{*}
,

\sum_{O\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda_{O}\mathbf{1}_{O}(R)=\mathbf{1}_{O^{*}}(R)\not\equiv 0\pmod{2}.

Therefore, there is some
O\in\mathcal{B}
such that
|O\cap R|
is odd, which is a desired tight odd SCO.
∎

\overrightarrow{G}=(V,E^{+}\cup E^{-})

1.2
.

Let

be its bidirected graph. By

, there exist

It follows from the condition

Appendix B
Reduction to basic digrafts

The goal of this section is to prove the reduction to basic digrafts, namely

Finally, we prove

. Our proof is inspired by the proof of uniqueness of tight cut decomposition for matching-covered graphs in

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq 2^{V}\setminus\{\emptyset,V\}
be a family of tight dicuts out-shores that we use in some tight dicut decomposition procedure. Then
\mathcal{S}
is cross-free by construction. Furthermore, notice that any tight dicut in the contracted digraft corresponds to a tight dicut in the original digraft. This means that any tight dicut decomposition corresponds to a
maximal
cross-free family of tight dicut out-shores.

Proof of
3.9
.

Consider a digraft
(D=(V,A),\mathcal{F})
where
\mathcal{F}
is cross-free. Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
be two families of tight dicut out-shores used in two tight dicut decomposition procedures. We denote by
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
if decomposing along
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
ends up with the same list of basic digrafts. We prove that
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
by induction on the size
|V|
.
Consider the following cases:

1.

\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
have a common member
U
.

In this case, consider starting the decomposition process with
\delta^{+}(U)
in both cases. After this step, we end up with two digrafts whose sizes are both less than
|V|
, so the inductive hypothesis applies.

2.

There exist
U\in\mathcal{S}
and
U^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
that do not cross.

Consider a maximal cross-free family
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
of tight dicuts out-shores that contains both
U
and
U^{\prime}
. By the previous case,
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}\sim\mathcal{S}
as well as
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}\sim\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, so
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

3.

There exist
U\in\mathcal{S}
and
U^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
such that
|U\cap U^{\prime}|\neq 1
or
|U\cup U^{\prime}|\neq|V(D)|-1

Let
U^{\prime\prime}
be either
U\cap U^{\prime}
or

whichever induces a nontrivial dicut. By Lemma
2.5
,
\delta^{+}(U^{\prime\prime})
is a tight dicut. Let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
be any maximal cross-free family of nontrivial tight dicuts out-shores containing
U^{\prime}
. Since
U^{\prime\prime}
do not cross
U
or
U^{\prime}
, by the second case,
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

4.

In the remaining case, for any
U\in\mathcal{S}
and
U^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, one has
U\cap U^{\prime}=\{u\}\subseteq S

\overline{U\cup U^{\prime}}=\{v\}\subseteq T
for some

.

By
2.5
, both
u
and

are tight nodes. Further, since
\delta^{+}(U),\delta^{+}(U^{\prime})
are both dicuts, there is no arc in between

and

forms a
2

\delta^{+}(U^{\prime})

\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
with

and

, respectively, we get two pairs of isomorphic (up to arc multiplicities) digrafts. Applying the inductive hypothesis to the two contracted digrafts implies
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

Proof of
4.6
.

Recall, this lemma states that one can combine an integral basis for
\lin(\{J\in\mathcal{J}(D,S^{t}):|J\cap C|=1\})
and a tight dijoin
J_{0}
with
|J_{0}\cap C|=2
into an integral basis for
\lin(\{J\in\mathcal{J}(D,S^{t}):|J\cap C|=1\}\cup\{J_{0}\})
.

Set
\mathcal{J}_{C}:=\{J\in\mathcal{J}(D,S^{t}):|J\cap C|=1\}
. Suppose
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\{J_{1},\ldots J_{k}\}\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{C}
. First, we show that
\mathcal{B}
is linearly independent. Suppose for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
,
\mathbf{0}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda_{j}\mathbf{1}_{J_{j}}
. Then,

0=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda_{j}\mathbf{1}_{J_{j}}(C)=2\lambda_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{0}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda_{j}.

However, pick any
v\in T
, we have

0=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda_{j}\mathbf{1}_{J_{j}}(\delta(v))=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda_{j}.

This implies
\lambda_{0}=0
. Then, we conclude that
\mathbf{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\mathbf{1}_{J_{j}}
, which implies that
\lambda_{1}=\ldots=\lambda_{k}=0
since
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is linearly independent. This proves that
\mathcal{B}
is linearly independent. Since
\dim(\lin(\mathcal{J}_{C}\cup\{J_{0}\}))\leq\dim(\lin(\mathcal{J}_{C}))+1=|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|+1=|\mathcal{B}|
,
\mathcal{B}
forms a linear basis for
\lin(\mathcal{J}_{C}\cup\{J_{0}\})
.

It remains to show that
\mathcal{B}
is also an integral basis. For an arbitrary
x\in\lin(\mathcal{J}_{C}\cup\{J_{0}\})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{A}
, there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

. Then,

\lambda_{0}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda_{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda_{j}\mathbf{1}_{J_{j}}(C)=x(C)\in\mathbb{Z}.

On the other hand, pick any

\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda_{j}=x(\delta(v))\in\mathbb{Z}.

This implies
