1.
Introduction

The decomposition of individual elements into products of involutions (elements of order two) is a classical problem in geometry and linear algebra.
More generally, the study of products of two involutions
[
Dj
,
GHR
]
is closely connected to the concept of

reversibility
in groups: an element
\displaystyle A
is said to be
reversible
in a group
\displaystyle G
if it is conjugate to its inverse, and
strongly reversible
if the reversing element can itself be chosen to be an involution in
\displaystyle G
.
Equivalently, an element is strongly reversible if and only if it can be expressed as a product of two involutions in
\displaystyle G
; see
[
OS
,
Wo
]
for a detailed exposition of reversibility in diverse mathematical contexts.

For the group of invertible matrices over the complex numbers, the decomposition of elements into products of involutions has attracted considerable attention from diverse perspectives.
Liu
[
Li
]
established necessary conditions for a matrix to be representable as a product of three involutions,
while Knüppel and Nielsen
[
KN
]
showed that any element in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}(V)
can be expressed as a product of
at most four involutions.

Note that the field of complex numbers carries a natural involution given by complex
conjugation. A matrix
\displaystyle T\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{C})
is called
coninvolutory
if

\displaystyle T\overline{T}=I
, see
[
Gr
]
. This naturally raises the question of whether matrices over

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
can be decomposed into products of coninvolutions.
Although the decomposition of matrices into products of classical involutions is well-studied,
the analogous problem for coninvolutions has received comparatively less attention. In
[
HM
,
BHH
]
the authors have discussed this problem regarding coninvolutions.

Ballantine
[
Ba
]
proved that a matrix
\displaystyle T
is a product of four
coninvolutions if and only if
\displaystyle|\det(T)|=1
. Abara, Merino, and Paras

[
AMP
]
showed that
\displaystyle T
is a product of two coninvolutions if and only if

\displaystyle T
is similar to
\displaystyle{\overline{T}}^{-1}
.
Coninvolutions are also closely related to the notions of consimilarity and
condiagonalizability; see
[
HH88
,
HH86
,
Ik
]
.

More recently, in the context of the classification of projective transformations,
[
GM
]
introduced the notion of conjugate-reversible (or
\displaystyle c
-reversible) elements in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{C})
.
An element
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{C})
is called
\displaystyle c
-reversible
if

\displaystyle A
is conjugate to
\displaystyle\overline{A}^{-1}
, and
strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible
if the
reversing element can be chosen to be coninvolutory in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{C})
. They proved that every

\displaystyle c
-reversible element in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{C})
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible
and characterized such elements using trace invariants. Consequently, the
classification of products of two coninvolutions reduces to the study of
strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible elements.

A similar notion of
\displaystyle c
-reversibility can be introduced for affine
transformations, providing a natural framework for studying the decomposition
of affine maps into products of coninvolutions.

Let
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
denote the group of affine transformations
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{n}
, given by

g:x\mapsto Ax+v,\quad A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C}),\;v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

This group can be identified with the semidirect product

\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})\ltimes\mathbb{C}^{n}.

We denote such an affine transformation
\displaystyle g
by
\displaystyle(A,v)
, and linear part
\displaystyle A
of
\displaystyle g
is denoted by
\displaystyle L(g)
.

Definition 1.1
.

An element
\displaystyle g=(A,v)\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
is called
\displaystyle c
-reversible if there exists
\displaystyle h\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
such that
\displaystyle hgh^{-1}=\overline{g}^{-1}
, where
\displaystyle\overline{g}=(\overline{A},\overline{v})
; it is called strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible if, in addition,
\displaystyle h\overline{h}=e=(I_{n},0)
, in which case
\displaystyle h
is called a coninvolution.

In this paper, we investigate
\displaystyle c
-reversible and strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible
elements in the affine group
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
. This work is partly motivated by a problem posed by O’Farrell and Short

[
OS
, p. 78–79]
. Reversibility in the Euclidean isometry group

\displaystyle\mathrm{O}(n,\mathbb{R})\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
was studied in
[
Sh
]
,
and was later extended in
[
GL
]
to the complex and quaternionic settings.
As a result, reversibility is now well-understood in the groups

\displaystyle\mathrm{U}(n,\mathbb{F})\ltimes\mathbb{F}^{n}
, where

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{H}
.

The reversible and strongly reversible elements of the affine group were
subsequently classified in
[
GLM
]
for the affine group

\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{F})
.
In particular, it was shown in
[
GLM
]
that every element of

\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{F})
can be expressed as a product of at most four
involutions.

Building on the study of
\displaystyle c
-reversibility in the linear group
\displaystyle\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
,
in the present paper we investigate
\displaystyle c
-reversible and strongly

\displaystyle c
-reversible elements in the affine group
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
.
Our goal is to understand the decomposition of affine transformations
into products of coninvolutions and to determine the minimal number of
coninvolutions required to express an arbitrary element of the group.

The main results of this paper are summarized in the following three theorems. First, the following theorem provides a complete classification of strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible
elements.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\displaystyle g\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\displaystyle g
is
\displaystyle c
-reversible.

(ii)

\displaystyle g
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible, i.e.
\displaystyle g
is a product of two coninvolutions.

(iii)

\displaystyle L(g)
is
\displaystyle c
-reversible in
\displaystyle\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
.

Consequently, the problem of decomposing an affine transformation into a
product of two coninvolutions reduces to the study of the
\displaystyle c
-reversibility
of its linear part. Next we determine when an affine transformation is a product of three coninvolutions and use consimilarity of two matrices for this purpose.

Definition 1.3
.

An element
\displaystyle g\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
is said to be

consimilar
to
\displaystyle h\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
if there exists

\displaystyle k\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
such that

h=kg\overline{k}^{-1}.

Note that consimilarity defines an equivalence relation on

\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\displaystyle g=(A,v)\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
satisfy
\displaystyle|\det(A)|=1
.
Then
\displaystyle g
can be expressed as a product of three coninvolutions if and only
if
\displaystyle g
is consimilar to a product of two coninvolutory affine transformations.

Finally, the following theorem gives that at most four coninvolutions are needed to decompose an arbitrary affine transformation.

Theorem 1.5
.

Every element
\displaystyle g=(A,v)\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
with

\displaystyle|\det(A)|=1
can be expressed as a product of at most four coninvolutions.

Note that, every coninvolutory matrix has determinant modulus
\displaystyle 1
.
Hence, Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
do not hold when
\displaystyle|\det(A)|\neq 1
.

We remark that, while analogous results could in principle be formulated for affine
transformations over the quaternions, we do not pursue this direction here.
The main obstruction is that the property of being strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible
is not invariant under conjugation for quaternionic affine transformations.
Consequently, a quaternionic analogue would require a suitably conjugation-invariant
notion of coninvolution, which must first be carefully formulated.

2.
Preliminaries

Definition 2.1

(cf.
[
Ro
, p. 94]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. The
Jordan block

\displaystyle\mathrm{J}(\lambda,m)
is the
\displaystyle m\times m
matrix

\mathrm{J}(\lambda,m)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\lambda&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&\lambda&1\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}.

That is,
\displaystyle\lambda
appears on the diagonal entries,
\displaystyle 1
on the super-diagonal entries,
and zero elsewhere. A block diagonal matrix whose diagonal blocks are Jordan blocks
is called a
Jordan form
.

Jordan canonical forms in
\displaystyle\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
are well studied in the literature;
see
[
Ro
, Chapter 5, Chapter 15]
.

Recall that an element
\displaystyle U\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
is called
unipotent

if all of its eigenvalues are equal to
\displaystyle 1
.

Lemma 2.2

(cf.
[
Ro
, Theorem 15.1.1, Theorem 5.5.3]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
be unipotent. Then there exists

\displaystyle S\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
such that

SAS^{-1}=I_{m_{0}}\oplus J(1,m_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus J(1,m_{k}),

where
\displaystyle m_{0}\geq 0
,
\displaystyle m_{i}\geq 2
for
\displaystyle i\geq 1
, and

m_{0}+m_{1}+\cdots+m_{k}=n.

We now prove some basic facts concerning conjugate reversibility
and strong conjugate reversibility.

Lemma 2.3
.

The properties of being
\displaystyle c
-reversible and strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible
are invariant under conjugation in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

Suppose
\displaystyle g
is
\displaystyle c
-reversible. Then there exists
\displaystyle h\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})

such that

hgh^{-1}=\overline{g}^{\,-1}.

If
\displaystyle k\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
then

\displaystyle(\overline{k}hk^{-1})(kgk^{-1})(\overline{k}hk^{-1})^{-1}=\overline{k}hgh^{-1}\overline{k}^{\,-1}={k}\,\overline{g}^{\,-1}\,\overline{k}^{\,-1}=\overline{(kgk^{-1})}^{\,-1}.

Hence
\displaystyle kgk^{-1}
is
\displaystyle c
-reversible.

If, in addition,
\displaystyle h\overline{h}=e
, then

\displaystyle(\overline{k}hk^{-1})\,\overline{(\overline{k}hk^{-1})}=\overline{k}(h\overline{h})\overline{k}^{-1}=e.

Thus strong
\displaystyle c
-reversibility is also preserved under conjugation.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

An element
\displaystyle g\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible
if and only if
\displaystyle g
is a product of two coninvolution.

Proof.

Suppose
\displaystyle g
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible. Then there exists an element
\displaystyle h\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
such that

hgh^{-1}=\overline{g}^{-1}\quad\text{with}\quad h\overline{h}=e.

Therefore
\displaystyle g=(h^{-1})(\overline{g}^{-1}h)
. Since
\displaystyle h
is a coninvolution,
\displaystyle h^{-1}
is also a coninvolution. Also,
\displaystyle\overline{g}^{-1}h
is a coninvolution , because

(\overline{g}^{-1}h)\overline{(\overline{g}^{-1}h)}=\overline{g}^{-1}hg^{-1}\overline{h}=h\overline{h}=e.

Conversely, suppose
\displaystyle g
is a product of two coninvolution. That is, suppose
\displaystyle g=ab
, where
\displaystyle a\overline{a}=e
and
\displaystyle b\overline{b}=e
. Then

a^{-1}ga=ba=\overline{g}^{-1}.

Thus,
\displaystyle g
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible.
∎

3.
Product of two coninvolutions

We embed
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{n}
into
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
as the affine hyperplane

P=\{(z,1)\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\mid z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\}.

Consider the homomorphism

\Theta:\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})\longrightarrow\mathrm{GL}(n+1,\mathbb{C})

defined by

\Theta((A,v))=\begin{pmatrix}A&v\\
\mathbf{0}&1\end{pmatrix},

where
\displaystyle 0
denotes the zero vector in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
The action of
\displaystyle\Theta(\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C}))
on
\displaystyle P
coincides with the natural action of

\displaystyle\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

In the next two lemmas (
3.1
and
3.2
), we classify reversible and strongly reversible elements in the affine group
\displaystyle\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle g=(A,v)\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
. Then
\displaystyle g
is conjugate reversible in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
if and only if there exists
\displaystyle h=(B,w)\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
such that:

(a)

\displaystyle BAB^{-1}=\overline{A}^{-1}
,

(b)

\displaystyle(\overline{A}^{-1}-I_{n})w=Bv+\overline{A}^{-1}\overline{v}
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle g=(A,v)\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
. Then

\overline{g}^{-1}(z)=\overline{A}^{-1}z-\overline{A}^{-1}\overline{v}.

For
\displaystyle h=(B,w)\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
, we have

hgh^{-1}=\overline{g}^{-1}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;hg=\overline{g}^{-1}h.

Now,

hg(z)=BAz+(Bv+w),\quad\overline{g}^{-1}h(z)=\overline{A}^{-1}Bz+\overline{A}^{-1}w-\overline{A}^{-1}\overline{v}.

Comparing terms gives

BAB^{-1}=\overline{A}^{-1},\quad(\overline{{A}^{-1}}-I_{n})w=Bv+\overline{A}^{-1}\overline{v}.

This completes the proof.
∎

In the next lemma we shall discuss strongly conjugate reversibile of the elements of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle g=(A,v)\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
. Then
\displaystyle g
is strongly conjugate reversible in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
if and only if there exists
\displaystyle h=(B,w)\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
such that:

(a)

\displaystyle BAB^{-1}=\overline{A}^{-1}
and
\displaystyle B\overline{B}=I_{n}
,

(b)

\displaystyle(\overline{A}^{-1}-I_{n})w=Bv+\overline{A}^{-1}\overline{v}
and
\displaystyle B\overline{w}+w=0
.

Proof.

From Lemma
3.1
,
\displaystyle g
is conjugate reversible if and only if

BAB^{-1}=\overline{A}^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad(\overline{A}^{-1}-I_{n})w=Bv+\overline{A}^{-1}\overline{v}.

As
\displaystyle g
is strongly
c
-reversible, therefore

(B,w)(\overline{B},\overline{w})=(I_{n},0).

It implies

B\overline{B}=I_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad B\overline{w}+w=0.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.3

(
[
GLM
]
)
.

Every element
\displaystyle g\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
is conjugate to an element of the form

\displaystyle g=(A,v)
, where

A=T\oplus U,

with
\displaystyle T\in\mathrm{GL}(n-m,\mathbb{C})
and
\displaystyle U\in\mathrm{GL}(m,\mathbb{C})
such that
\displaystyle T
has no eigenvalue equal to
\displaystyle 1
, and
\displaystyle U
has only eigenvalue
\displaystyle 1
. Moreover, the translation part
\displaystyle v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
can be written as

v=(0,\ldots,0,v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}),

where
\displaystyle 0\leq m\leq n
is the multiplicity of the eigenvalue
\displaystyle 1
of the linear part
\displaystyle A
.

Furthermore, if
\displaystyle 1
is not an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle A
(i.e.,
\displaystyle m=0
), then
\displaystyle g
is conjugate to
\displaystyle(A,0)
.

It is difficult to find a strongly
\displaystyle c
-reverser directly when an affine transformation has a non-trivial unipotent part. To overcome this difficulty, we pass to the Lie algebra of the affine group and use the adjoint action.
We introduce a notion which is similar to adjoint reality of the Lie algebra of classical lie groups (see
[
GM23
]
) called adjoint conjugate reality. Let
\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
denote the Lie algebra of the affine group
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
, that is,

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb{C})\ltimes\mathbb{C}^{n}.

The Lie algebra
\displaystyle\mathfrak{aff}(n,\mathbb{C})=\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb{C})\ltimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
admits a faithful embedding into
\displaystyle\mathfrak{gl}(n+1,\mathbb{C})
given by

(X,u)\;\longmapsto\;\begin{pmatrix}X&u\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Note that the adjoint action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
on its Lie algebra
\displaystyle\mathfrak{aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
is given by

\mathrm{Ad}:G\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g},

\mathrm{Ad}_{(A,v)}(X,u)=\left(AXA^{-1},\;A(u-Xv)\right),

where
\displaystyle G=\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
and
\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
.

An element
\displaystyle X\in\mathfrak{g}
is called
adjoint
\displaystyle c
-real
(or
adjoint conjugate real
) if there exists
\displaystyle g\in G
such that

\mathrm{Ad}_{g}(X)=-\overline{X}.

It is called
strongly adjoint
\displaystyle c
-real
if, in addition,
\displaystyle g\overline{g}=I
. Note that if
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ad}_{g}(X)=-\overline{X}
, then

g(\exp X)g^{-1}=\exp(-\overline{X})=(\overline{\exp X})^{-1}.

In particular, if
\displaystyle X\in\mathfrak{g}
is adjoint
\displaystyle c
-real (respectively, strongly adjoint
\displaystyle c
-real), then
\displaystyle\exp X
is
\displaystyle c
-reversible (respectively, strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible).

Remark 3.4
.

The converse is not true in general. For example,

A=\operatorname{diag}(r+2\pi i,\,-r),\quad r\in\mathbb{R},

is not
adjoint
\displaystyle c
-real
since

A\not\sim-\overline{A}.

However,

\exp(A)=\operatorname{diag}(e^{r},\,e^{-r})

has eigenvalues in reciprocal pairs, and hence

\exp(A)\sim\exp(A)^{-1}=\overline{\exp(A)}^{-1}.

Therefore
\displaystyle\exp(A)
is
\displaystyle c
-reversible.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\displaystyle(N,x)\in\mathfrak{aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
then
\displaystyle(N,x)
is strongly
adjoint
\displaystyle c
-real
if and only if there exists
\displaystyle(B,w)\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
which obeys the following conditions.

(a)

\displaystyle BNB^{-1}=-\overline{N}
and
\displaystyle B\overline{B}=I_{n}
,

(b)

\displaystyle Bx+\overline{x}=-\overline{N}w
and
\displaystyle B\overline{w}+w=0
.

Lemma
3.5
can proved in the same line as Lemma
3.2
. Therefore we omit it here.

Using Lemma
3.5
we can prove our next Lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

The element
\displaystyle(\mathrm{J}(0,n),x)\in\mathfrak{aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
is strongly
adjoint
\displaystyle c
-real
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and write
\displaystyle x_{n}=r_{n}e^{i\theta}
with
\displaystyle r_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Set
\displaystyle a=e^{-2i\theta}
and define

B=\mathrm{diag}(a,-a,a,-a,\ldots).

Define
\displaystyle w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})^{t}
by

w_{k}=\begin{cases}-(ax_{k}+\overline{x}_{k}),&k\text{ odd},\\
\;\;ax_{k}-\overline{x}_{k},&k\text{ even}.\end{cases}

Then a direct computation shows that
\displaystyle BNB^{-1}=-\overline{N}
and

\displaystyle B\overline{w}+w=0
. Moreover,

Bx+\overline{x}=-\overline{N}w.

Hence the conditions of Lemma
3.5
are satisfied for
\displaystyle N=\mathrm{J}(0,n)
.

∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\displaystyle g=(A,v)\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
with
\displaystyle A=\mathrm{J}(1,n)
. Then
\displaystyle g
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle N=J(0,n)
. Then
\displaystyle\exp(N)
is a unipotent matrix whose Jordan form is

\displaystyle J(1,n)
. Hence there exists
\displaystyle B\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
such that

B\,\exp(N)\,B^{-1}=A.

Consider the exponential map in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
:

\exp(N,x)=(\exp(N),Cx),

where

C=I+\frac{N}{2!}+\frac{N^{2}}{3!}+\cdots+\frac{N^{n-1}}{n!}.

Since
\displaystyle C
is invertible. Hence there exists
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

such that
\displaystyle Cx=v
.

Now conjugating
\displaystyle\exp(N,x)
by
\displaystyle(B,0)
we obtain

(B,0)\exp(N,x)(B,0)^{-1}=(A,v)=g.

Since
\displaystyle(N,x)
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-real by lemma
3.6
, it follows that
\displaystyle\exp(N,x)
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible. and the property is preserved
under conjugation, it follows that
\displaystyle g
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\displaystyle g=(U,v)\in\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
, where
\displaystyle U
is unipotent.
Then
\displaystyle g
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible. In particular,
\displaystyle g
can be written as a

\displaystyle\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle U
is unipotent, it is conjugate to a direct sum of Jordan blocks

\displaystyle\mathrm{J}(1,n_{i})
. By the previous lemma, each affine transformation of the form

\displaystyle(\mathrm{J}(1,n_{i}),v_{i})
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible. Hence
\displaystyle(U,v)
is strongly

\displaystyle c
-reversible. Consequently,
\displaystyle g
can be written as a product of two

\displaystyle\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
.
∎

is immediate from the definition of strong
\displaystyle c
-reversibility.

Finally, we prove that
\displaystyle(iii)\Rightarrow(ii)
.
Suppose that the linear part
\displaystyle L(g)=A
is
c
-reversible.
Up to conjugacy in
\displaystyle\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
, we may assume that

g=(A,v)

is of the form

A=\begin{pmatrix}T&0\\
0&U\end{pmatrix},\qquad v=\begin{pmatrix}0_{\,n-m}\\
\widetilde{v}_{\,m}\end{pmatrix},

where
\displaystyle T\in\mathrm{GL}(n-m,\mathbb{C})
has no eigenvalue equal to
\displaystyle 1
,
and
\displaystyle U\in\mathrm{GL}(m,\mathbb{C})
has
\displaystyle 1
as its only eigenvalue.
Here
\displaystyle 0_{\,n-m}
denotes the zero vector in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{\,n-m}
and

\displaystyle\widetilde{v}_{\,m}\in\mathbb{C}^{\,m}
.

Since
\displaystyle A
is
c
-reversible, there exists
\displaystyle B\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
such that;

BAB^{-1}=\overline{A}^{-1}.

Let

B=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}&B_{2}\\
B_{3}&B_{4}\end{pmatrix}.

Then

BA=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}T&B_{2}U\\
B_{3}T&B_{4}U\end{pmatrix},\quad\overline{A}^{-1}B=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{T}^{-1}B_{1}&\overline{T}^{-1}B_{2}\\
\overline{U}^{-1}B_{3}&\overline{U}^{-1}B_{4}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus,

B_{1}T=\overline{T}^{-1}B_{1},\;B_{2}U=\overline{T}^{-1}B_{2},\;B_{3}T=\overline{U}^{-1}B_{3},\;B_{4}U=\overline{U}^{-1}B_{4}.

Since
\displaystyle\sigma(U)=\{1\}
and
\displaystyle 1\notin\sigma(T)
, the Sylvester equations

B_{2}U=\overline{T}^{-1}B_{2},\quad B_{3}T=\overline{U}^{-1}B_{3}

imply
\displaystyle B_{2}=0
and
\displaystyle B_{3}=0
. Hence

B=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}&0\\
0&B_{4}\end{pmatrix}.

As
\displaystyle B
is invertible, it follows that
\displaystyle B_{1}
and
\displaystyle B_{4}
are invertible. Therefore

B_{1}TB_{1}^{-1}=\overline{T}^{-1},

so
\displaystyle T
is
\displaystyle c
-reversible.
Thus
\displaystyle T
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible in
\displaystyle\mathrm{GL}(n-m,\mathbb{C})
. Hence

T=T_{1}T_{2},

where
\displaystyle T_{1}
and
\displaystyle T_{2}
are coninvolutions in
\displaystyle\mathrm{GL}(n-m,\mathbb{C})
.

Now
\displaystyle(U,\tilde{v})
is also strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible by Proposition
3.8
.
Hence

(U,\tilde{v})=(U_{1},v_{1})(U_{2},v_{2}),

where
\displaystyle(U_{1},v_{1})
and
\displaystyle(U_{2},v_{2})
are coninvolutions.

Thus

g=(A,v)=(T_{1}\oplus U_{1},\,0\oplus v_{1})\,(T_{2}\oplus U_{2},\,0\oplus v_{2})

so
\displaystyle g
is a product of two coninvolutions. This completes the proof.∎

4.
Product of three coninvolutions

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
\displaystyle g
is a product of
\displaystyle(2m+1)
coninvolutions in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
. Then any consimilar element of
\displaystyle g
can also be written as a product of
\displaystyle(2m+1)
coninvolutions.

Proof.

Let

g=g_{1}g_{2}\cdots g_{2m+1},

where each
\displaystyle g_{i}
is a coninvolution.

Let
\displaystyle h\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
. Then

hg\overline{h}^{-1}=(hg_{1}\overline{h}^{-1})(\bar{h}g_{2}{h}^{-1})\cdots(hg_{2m+1}\overline{h}^{-1}).

Set

g_{i}^{\prime}=hg_{i}\overline{h}^{-1},\quad i=1,\dots,2m+1.

Since consimilarity preserves coninvolutions, each
\displaystyle g_{i}^{\prime}
is a coninvolution. Hence

hg\overline{h}^{-1}=g_{1}^{\prime}g_{2}^{\prime}\cdots g_{2m+1}^{\prime},

which is a product of
\displaystyle(2m+1)
coninvolutions.

This completes the proof.
∎

We shall use the following result of Horn and Johnson in the Lemma
4.3
.

Lemma 4.2

(cf.
[
HJ
, Lem. 4.6.9]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C)}
be given. Then
\displaystyle A\bar{A}=I
if and only if there is a nonsingular

\displaystyle S\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C)}
such that
\displaystyle A=S\bar{S}^{-1}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\displaystyle g\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
be coninvolution. Then there exists
\displaystyle h\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
such that

g=h\overline{h}^{-1}.

Proof.

If
\displaystyle g=h\overline{h}^{-1}
, then

g\overline{g}=h\overline{h}^{-1}\,\overline{h}h^{-1}=e.

Conversely, let
\displaystyle g=(A,v)\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
satisfy
\displaystyle g\overline{g}=e
. Then

A\overline{A}=I\quad\text{and}\quad A\overline{v}+v=0.

By lemma
4.2
, there exists
\displaystyle B\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
such that

A=B\overline{B}^{-1}.

Define

h=\left(B,\frac{v}{2}\right).

Then

g\overline{h}=(A,v)\left(\overline{B},\frac{\overline{v}}{2}\right)=\left(A\overline{B},\,A\frac{\overline{v}}{2}+v\right)=\left(B,\,-\frac{v}{2}+v\right)=\left(B,\frac{v}{2}\right)=h,

where we used
\displaystyle A\overline{B}=B
and
\displaystyle A\overline{v}=-v
. Hence
\displaystyle g=h\overline{h}^{-1}
.
∎

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Suppose
\displaystyle g=g_{1}g_{2}g_{3}
, where each
\displaystyle g_{i}
is a coninvolution.

By lemma
4.3
, there exists
\displaystyle h\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
such that

g_{1}=h\overline{h}^{-1}.

Hence,

h^{-1}g\overline{h}=(\overline{h}^{-1}g_{2}h)(h^{-1}g_{3}\overline{h})=g_{2}^{\prime}g_{3}^{\prime}.

Since consimilarity preserves coninvolutions, So
\displaystyle g_{2}^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle g_{3}^{\prime}
are coninvolutions.
Thus
\displaystyle g
is consimilar to a product of two coninvolutory affine transformations.

Conversely, suppose there exists
\displaystyle h\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
such that

hg\overline{h}^{-1}=g_{1}g_{2},

where
\displaystyle g_{1},g_{2}
are coninvolutions. Then

g=h^{-1}g_{1}g_{2}\overline{h}=(h^{-1}g_{1}h)\,(h^{-1}g_{2}h)\,(h^{-1}\overline{h}).

Set

g_{1}^{\prime}=h^{-1}g_{1}h,\quad g_{2}^{\prime}=h^{-1}g_{2}h,\quad g_{3}^{\prime}=h^{-1}\overline{h}.

Each
\displaystyle g_{i}^{\prime}
is a coninvolution. Hence

g=g_{1}^{\prime}g_{2}^{\prime}g_{3}^{\prime},

so
\displaystyle g
is a product of three coninvolutions.

This completes the proof. ∎

5.
Product of four coninvolutions

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\displaystyle g\in\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
be a product of
\displaystyle 2m
coninvolutions. Then any conjugate of
\displaystyle g
in
\displaystyle\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
is also a product of
\displaystyle 2m
coninvolutions.

Proof.

Suppose

g=(a_{1}a_{2})(a_{3}a_{4})\cdots(a_{2m-1}a_{2m}),

where each
\displaystyle a_{i}
is a coninvolution. Then each product
\displaystyle a_{2i-1}a_{2i}
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible.

Let
\displaystyle h\in\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
. Then

hgh^{-1}=(ha_{1}a_{2}h^{-1})\cdots(ha_{2m-1}a_{2m}h^{-1}).

Since strong
\displaystyle c
-reversibility is conjugacy invariant, each

ha_{2i-1}a_{2i}h^{-1}

is again a product of two coninvolutions. Hence
\displaystyle hgh^{-1}
is a product of
\displaystyle 2m
coninvolutions.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

For an odd number of factors, being a product of coninvolutions is not, in general, a conjugacy invariant, whereas for involutions this property is always preserved under conjugation.

Example 5.3
.

This example shows that coninvolutions are not conjugacy invariant in

\displaystyle\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
.

Consider
\displaystyle g=(I,v)\in\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
. Then

g\overline{g}=(I,v)(I,\overline{v})=(I,v+\overline{v}),

so
\displaystyle g
is a coninvolution if and only if
\displaystyle v+\overline{v}=0
.

Now take
\displaystyle h=(A,0)
with
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
. Then

hgh^{-1}=(I,Av).

Moreover,

(Av)+\overline{(Av)}=Av+\overline{A}\,\overline{v}.

Since
\displaystyle v+\overline{v}=0
, we have
\displaystyle\overline{v}=-v
, and hence

(Av)+\overline{(Av)}=(A-\overline{A})v,

which is not zero in general unless
\displaystyle v\in\ker(A-\overline{A})
.
Thus
\displaystyle hgh^{-1}
need not be a coninvolution, showing that coninvolutions are not preserved under conjugation.

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

In this theorem, we aim to show that every affine transformation

\displaystyle g=(A,v)\in\operatorname{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
with
\displaystyle|\det A|=1
can be written as a product of at most four coninvolutions.

By the preceding lemma, it suffices to consider the case where, up to conjugacy,

A=\begin{pmatrix}T&0\\
0&U\end{pmatrix},\qquad v=\begin{pmatrix}0_{\,n-m}\\
\widetilde{v}_{\,m}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\displaystyle U
is unipotent,
\displaystyle\det U=1
, and hence
\displaystyle|\det T|=1
. By
[
Ba
, Theorem 5]
,
\displaystyle T
can be written as a product of four coninvolutions:

T=T_{1}T_{2}T_{3}T_{4}.

By Proposition
3.8
,
\displaystyle(U,\widetilde{v})
is strongly
\displaystyle c
-reversible. Hence there exist
\displaystyle(U_{1},v_{1}),(U_{2},v_{2})\in\mathrm{Aff}(n,\mathbb{C})
such that

(U,\widetilde{v})=(U_{1},v_{1})(U_{2},v_{2}),

where
\displaystyle(U_{1},v_{1})
and
\displaystyle(U_{2},v_{2})
are coninvolutions.

g_{1}=(T_{1}\oplus U_{1},\;0_{\,n-m}\oplus v_{1}),\quad g_{2}=(T_{2}\oplus U_{2},\;0_{\,n-m}\oplus v_{2}),

g_{3}=(T_{3}\oplus I_{m},\;0),\quad g_{4}=(T_{4}\oplus I_{m},\;0).

Then

g=g_{1}\,g_{2}\,g_{3}\,g_{4},

where each
\displaystyle g_{i}
is a coninvolution. This completes the proof.∎
