On concatenations of two k k -generalized Pell numbers
Cherif B. Deme, Kancou D. Fall, Khady Faye, Bernadette Faye

Abstract
. We study the concatenation of two k k -generalized Pell numbers. More precisely, we determine all solutions of the equation P n ( k ) = P m ( k ) ⋅ 10 d + P p ( k ) P_{n}^{(k)}=P_{m}^{(k)}\cdot 10^{d}+P_{p}^{(k)} , where d d is the number of decimal digits of P p ( k ) P_{p}^{(k)} . We prove that for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 there are no solutions, while for k = 2 k=2 the only solution is P 4 = 12 = 1 | 2 P_{4}=12=1\|2 .



1.
Introduction

For an integer
k\geq 2
, the sequence of
k
-generalized Pell numbers (or
k
-Pell numbers)
(P_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k}
is defined by the linear recurrence

P_{n}^{(k)}=2P_{n-1}^{(k)}+P_{n-2}^{(k)}+\dots+P_{n-k}^{(k)}\qquad(n\geq 2),

with initial conditions
P_{-(k-2)}^{(k)}=\dots=P_{0}^{(k)}=0
and
P_{1}^{(k)}=1
.
For
k=2
we recover the classical Pell sequence
P_{0}=0
,
P_{1}=1
,
P_{n}=2P_{n-1}+P_{n-2}
.

The problem of concatenations of two terms of a sequence is classical.
For Fibonacci numbers, Banks and Luca
[
2
]
proved that
F_{10}=55=5\|5
is the largest such number.
For Lucas numbers, Erdüvan
[
6
]
showed that the only solutions are
L_{6}=11=1\|1
and
L_{9}=47=4\|7
.

In
[
1
]
, Altassan and Alan studied the analogous problem for
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers and found the solutions

F_{7}^{(3)}=24=\overline{F_{3}^{(3)}F_{4}^{(3)}},\quad F_{8}^{(3)}=44=\overline{F_{4}^{(3)}F_{4}^{(3)}},\quad F_{16}^{(8)}=16128=\overline{F_{6}^{(8)}F_{9}^{(8)}}.

Recently, Tumwesigye, Ddamulira and Kaggwa
[
18
]
extended the result to
k
-generalized Lucas numbers.

In this paper we study the analogous problem for
k
-Pell numbers.
Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\geq 2
and let
(P_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k}
be the
k
-generalized Pell sequence.
Consider the Diophantine equation

(1)

P_{n}^{(k)}=P_{m}^{(k)}\cdot 10^{d}+P_{p}^{(k)},

where
n>m,p\geq 0
and
d=\lfloor\log_{10}P_{p}^{(k)}\rfloor+1
.

(i)

If
k=2
, the only solution of (
1
) is
(n,m,p)=(4,1,2)
,
since
P_{4}=12=1\|2
.

(ii)

If
k\geq 3
, equation (
1
) has no solutions.

In the next sections we develop the necessary tools and give the proof.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The generalized Pell sequence

Let
k\geqslant 2
be an integer. We consider the linear recurrence sequence of order
k

G^{(k)}:=(G_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k}
defined as

G^{(k)}_{n}=rG^{(k)}_{n-1}+G^{(k)}_{n-2}+\cdots+G^{(k)}_{n-k}\quad\hbox{for all $n\geq 2$},

with the initial conditions

G^{(k)}_{-(k-2)}=G^{(k)}_{-(k-3)}=\cdots=G^{(k)}_{-1}=0,G^{(k)}_{0}=a\quad\hbox{and}\quad G^{(k)}_{1}=b.

Observe that if
a=0
and
b=1
, then
G^{(k)}
is nothing that just the
k
- generalized Fibonacci sequence or for simplicity, the
k
-Fibonacci sequence
F^{(k)}:=(F^{(k)}_{n})_{n\geq 2-k}
. In this case, if we choose
k=2
we obtain the classical Fibonacci sequence
(F_{n})_{n}
.

On the other hand, if
r=2,a=0
and
b=1
then
G^{k}
is known as the
k
-generalized Pell sequence
P^{(k)}:=(P^{(k)}_{n})_{n\geq 2-k}
. The terms of this sequence are called
k
-generalized Pell numbers.

The
k
-Pell numbers and their properties have been studied by many authors. For example, Kiliç
[
11
]
showed that the first
k+1

P^{(}k)
are the Fibonacci numbers with odd index, namely

(2)

P_{n}^{(k)}=F_{2n-1}\quad\hbox{ for all $1\leq n\leq k+1$}.

In addition, it was also proved in
[
11
]
that if
k+2\leq n\leq 2k+2
, then

(3)

P_{n}^{(k)}=F_{2n-1}-\sum_{j=1}^{n-k-1}F_{2j-1}F_{2(n-k-1)}.

It is known that the characteristic polynomial of the
k
–generalized Pell numbers

, namely

\Psi_{k}(x):=x^{k}-2x^{k-1}-\cdots-x-1,

is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}[x]
and has just one root outside the unit circle. Let
\alpha:=\alpha(k)
denote that single root. It was proved in
[
8

that

is located between

and

where

denotes the golden section.This is called the dominant root of
P^{(k)}
. To simplify notation, in our application we shall omit the dependence on
k
of

. We shall use
\alpha^{(1)},\dotso,\alpha^{(k)}
for all roots of
\Psi_{k}(x)
with the convention that
\alpha^{(1)}:=\alpha
.

We now consider for an integer
k\geq 2
, the function

(4)

\displaystyle g_{k}(z)=\dfrac{z-1}{(k+1)z^{2}-3kz+k-1}=\frac{z-1}{k(z^{2}-3z+1)+z^{2}-1}\qquad{\text{for}}\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

In the following lemma, we give some properties of the sequence

2.2.
Notations and terminology from algebraic number theory

We begin by recalling some basic notions from algebraic number theory.

Let
\eta
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with minimal primitive polynomial over the integers

a_{0}x^{d}+a_{1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}=a_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\eta^{(i)}),

where the leading coefficient
a_{0}
is positive and the
\eta^{(i)}
’s are the conjugates of
\eta
. Then the
logarithmic height
of
\eta
is given by

h(\eta):=\dfrac{1}{d}\left(\log a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\left(\max\{|\eta^{(i)}|,1\}\right)\right).

In particular, if
\eta=p/q
is a rational number with
\gcd(p,q)=1
and
q>0
, then
h(\eta)=\log\max\{|p|,q\}
. The following are some of the properties of the logarithmic height function
h(\cdot)
, which will be used without reference:

\displaystyle h(\eta\pm\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle h(\eta)+h(\gamma)+\log 2,

(6)

\displaystyle h(\eta\gamma^{\pm 1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle h(\eta)+h(\gamma),

\displaystyle h(\eta^{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|s|h(\eta)\qquad(s\in\mathbb{Z}).

Using the above properties of the logarithmic height, Bravo and al. showed in
[
8
]
that the logarithmic height of
g_{k}(\alpha)
satisfies

(7)

h(g_{k}(\alpha))<4\log k\quad\hbox{for $k\geq 3$},

which will be used in the proof of the main problem.

We will also need the following Lemma.

Lemma 2.3

(Guzman–Luca, Lemma 7 in
[
14
]
)
.

If
s\geq 1
and
T>(4s^{2})^{s}
, then the inequality

\frac{y}{(\log y)^{s}}<T

implies

y<2^{s}\,T\,(\log T)^{s}.

Lemma 2.4

(Cooper–Howard for
k
-Pell
[
10
]
)
.

For

and

, we have

P_{n}^{(k)}=2^{\,n-1}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}C_{n,j}\,2^{\,n-(k+1)j-1},

where

\ell=\left\lfloor\frac{n+k}{k+1}\right\rfloor,

and the coefficients

are given by

C_{n,j}=(-1)^{j}\left[\binom{n-jk}{j+1}-\binom{n-jk-2}{j-1}\right],

with the convention
\binom{a}{b}=0

or if

2.3.
Linear forms in logarithms and continued fractions

In order to solve our main equation (

[

]
, which is one of our main tools in this project.

Theorem 2.5

(Matveev)
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t}
be positive real algebraic numbers in a real algebraic number field

\mathbb{K}
of degree
D
,
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
be nonzero integers, and assume that

(8)

\Lambda:=\gamma_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{t}^{b_{t}}-1

\log|\Lambda|>-1.4\times 30^{t+3}\times t^{4.5}\times D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)A_{1}\cdots A_{t},

Finally, with the help of a computer program we can found all
possible solutions.

2.4.
Reduction procedure.

The bounds on the variables obtained via Baker’s theorem are usually too large for any computational purposes. Therefore, to lower the upper bounds of the integer unknowns, one can either use a reduction method, usually called the Baker-Davenport reduction procedure or the LLL. reduction algorithm. In this project, we will use both.

For the Baker-Davenport, the variant we apply here is the one due to Dujella and Pethő (
[
5
]
, Lemma 5a). For a real number
r
, we denote by
\parallel r\parallel
the quantity
\min\{|r-n|:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, the distance from
r
to the nearest integer.

Lemma 2.6

(Dujella, Pethő,
[
5
]
)
.

Let
\kappa\neq 0,A,B
and
\mu
be real numbers such that
A>0
and
B>1
. Let
M>1
be a positive integer and suppose that
\frac{p}{q}
is a convergent of the continued fraction expansion of
\kappa
with
q>6M
. Let

\displaystyle\varepsilon:=\parallel\mu q\parallel-M\parallel\kappa q\parallel.

If
\varepsilon>0
, then there is no solution of the inequality

\displaystyle 0<|m\kappa-n+\mu|<AB^{-k}

in positive integers
m,n,k
with

\displaystyle\frac{\log(Aq/\varepsilon)}{\log B}\leq k\quad\text{and}\quad m\leq M.

Lemma
2.6
cannot be applied when
\mu=0
(since then
\varepsilon<0
). In this case, we use the following criterion due to Legendre, a well–known result from the theory of Diophantine approximation. For further details, we refer the reader to the books of Cohen
[
3
,
4
]
.

Lemma 2.7

(Legendre,
[
3
,
4
]
)
.

Let
\kappa
be real number and

integers such that

\displaystyle\left|\kappa-\frac{x}{y}\right|<\frac{1}{2y^{2}}.

Then
x/y=p_{k}/q_{k}
is a convergent of
\kappa
. Furthermore, let
M
and
N
be a nonnegative integers such that

. Then putting

, the inequality

\displaystyle\left|\kappa-\frac{x}{y}\right|\geq\frac{1}{(a(M)+2)y^{2}},

holds for all pairs

of positive integers with

.

We will also need the following lemma by Gúzman Sánchez and Luca (

]

2.5.
Reduced Bases for Lattices and LLL–reduction methods

Let
k
be a positive integer. A subset
\mathcal{L}
of the
k
–dimensional real vector space
{\mathbb{R}^{k}}
is called a lattice if there exists a basis
\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}b_{i}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}b_{i}\mid r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

We say that
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
form a basis for
\mathcal{L}
, or that they span
\mathcal{L}
. We
call
k
the rank of
\mathcal{L}
. The determinant
\text{det}(\mathcal{L})
, of
\mathcal{L}
is defined by

\displaystyle\text{det}(\mathcal{L})=|\det(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})|,

with the
b_{i}
’s being written as column vectors. This is a positive real number that does not depend on the choice of the basis (see
[
9
]
, Section 1.2).

Given linearly independent vectors
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we refer back to the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization technique. This method allows us to inductively define vectors
b^{*}_{i}
(with
1\leq i\leq k
) and real coefficients
\mu_{i,j}
(for
1\leq j\leq i\leq k
). Specifically,

\displaystyle b^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=b_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mu_{i,j}b^{*}_{j},~~~\mu_{i,j}=\dfrac{\langle b_{i},b^{*}_{j}\rangle}{\langle b^{*}_{j},b^{*}_{j}\rangle},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the ordinary inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Notice that
b^{*}_{i}
is the orthogonal projection of
b_{i}
on the orthogonal complement of the span of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{i-1}
, and that
\mathbb{R}b_{i}
is orthogonal to the span of
b^{*}_{1},\ldots,b^{*}_{i-1}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. It follows that
b^{*}_{1},b^{*}_{2},\ldots,b^{*}_{k}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Definition 2.9
.

The basis
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}
for the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is called reduced if

\displaystyle\|\mu_{i,j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq j<i\leq n,~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\|b^{*}_{i}+\mu_{i,i-1}b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}\|b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1<i\leq n,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the ordinary Euclidean length. The constant
{3}/{4}
above is arbitrarily chosen, and may be replaced by any fixed real number
y
in the interval
{1}/{4}<y<1
(see
[
12
]
, Section 1).

Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a
k-
dimensional lattice with reduced basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
and denote by
B
the matrix with columns
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
.
We define

l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\min_{x\in\mathcal{L}}||x-y||\quad;~~y\not\in\mathcal{L}\\
\min_{0\neq x\in\mathcal{L}}||x||\quad;~~y\in\mathcal{L}\end{array}\right.,

where
||\cdot||
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. It is well known that, by applying the
LLL–algorithm, it is possible to give in polynomial time a lower bound for
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)
, namely a positive constant
c_{1}
such that
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)\geq c_{1}
holds (see
[
17
]
, Section V.4).

Lemma 2.10
.

Let

and

with

. Furthermore,

(1)

, let

In our application, we are given real numbers

3.
Proof of the main theorem

3.1.
The case
n\leq k+1

Suppose that
n\leq k+1
.
By the properties of the
k
-Pell sequence, for
2\leq n\leq k+1
we have

P_{n}^{(k)}=F_{2n-1},

where
F_{m}
denotes the
m
-th Fibonacci number (
F_{1}=1,F_{2}=1,F_{3}=2,\dots
).
The same holds for
m
and
p
(since
m,p<n
).

Thus equation (
1
) becomes

(10)

F_{2n-1}=F_{2m-1}\cdot 10^{d}+F_{2p-1}.

This is a Diophantine equation involving only Fibonacci numbers.
Banks and Luca
[
2
]
determined all Fibonacci numbers that are concatenations of two Fibonacci numbers.
Their results show that the only solutions are

F_{8}=21=\overline{F_{3}F_{2}},\qquad F_{10}=55=\overline{F_{5}F_{5}}.

However, these correspond to even indices (
F_{8}
and
F_{10}
), while (
10
) involves odd-index Fibonacci numbers
F_{2n-1},F_{2m-1},F_{2p-1}
.
A direct verification (or a short computer search for small indices) shows that the only possible solution would be
2n-1=8
(i.e.
n=4.5
) or
2n-1=10
(i.e.
n=5.5
), which are not integers.
Hence no solution of (
10
) exists with
n\geq 3
(the case
n=2
gives trivial single-digit numbers).

Therefore, there are no solutions when
n\leq k+1
and
k\geq 3
. Consequently we may assume
n\geq k+2
in the rest of the proof.

3.2.
The case
n\geq k+2

Assume that (
1
) holds.

The number of digits of
P_{p}^{(k)}
is

(11)

d=\lfloor\log_{10}P_{p}^{(k)}\rfloor+1.

From the bounds
\alpha^{p-2}\leq P_{p}^{(k)}\leq\alpha^{p-1}
(Lemma
2.1
) and the fact that

\log_{10}\alpha\in(1/5,1/3)
for
k\geq 3
, we obtain

(12)

\frac{p-2}{5}<d<\frac{p+2}{3}.

Using equation (
1
) together with the estimates
\alpha^{n-2}\leq P_{n}^{(k)}\leq\alpha^{n-1}
and the trivial bounds

P_{m}^{(k)}\geq\alpha^{m-2}
,
P_{p}^{(k)}\leq\alpha^{p-1}
, after straightforward calculations we obtain

(13)

m+p-3<n<m+p+6.

Now, using the Binet formula Lemma[
2.1
, (c)] with
|e_{n}|<\frac{1}{2}
and
g_{k}(\alpha)>0.276
in equation (
1
) we obtain

g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}+e_{n}=\bigl(g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{m}+e_{m}\bigr)10^{d}+g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{p}+e_{p}.

Rearranging gives

(14)

1-\frac{10^{d}}{\alpha^{n-m}}=\frac{e_{m}10^{d}+e_{p}-e_{n}}{g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}}.

Taking absolute values and using
|e_{n}|,|e_{m}|,|e_{p}|<\frac{1}{2}
, we obtain

\left|1-\frac{10^{d}}{\alpha^{n-m}}\right|=\frac{|e_{m}10^{d}+e_{p}-e_{n}|}{g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}}\leq\frac{0.5\cdot 10^{d}+1}{g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}}.

From
d\leq\log_{10}P_{p}+1
and
P_{p}\leq g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{p}+0.5<\alpha^{p}
(for
p
large enough), we get
10^{d}<10\alpha^{p}
. Hence

0.5\cdot 10^{d}+1<5\alpha^{p}+1<6\alpha^{p}\quad\text{for }\alpha^{p}\geq 1.

Thus

\left|1-\frac{10^{d}}{\alpha^{n-m}}\right|<\frac{6\alpha^{p}}{g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}}=\frac{6}{g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n-p}}.

Since
g_{k}(\alpha)>0.276
, we have
\frac{6}{g_{k}(\alpha)}<21.74
. So, we have that

(15)

\left|1-\frac{10^{d}}{\alpha^{n-m}}\right|<\frac{22}{\alpha^{n-p}}.

Set
U_{1}=\frac{10^{d}}{\alpha^{n-m}}-1
and

For

|\Lambda_{1}|\leq 2|U_{1}|<\frac{44}{\alpha^{n-p}}.

We apply Matveev’s theorem (Theorem
2.5
) with
t=2
,
\gamma_{1}=\alpha
,
\gamma_{2}=10
,
b_{1}=-(n-m)
,
b_{2}=d
.
The number field is

of degree

. We take

A_{2}=\max\{Dh(10),|\log 10|,0.16\}=k\log 10.

Set

. Matveev gives

\log|U_{1}|>-1.4\cdot 30^{5}\cdot 2^{4.5}\cdot k^{2}(1+\log k)(1+\log B)\cdot A_{1}A_{2}.

Thus

\log|U_{1}|>-7.70\times 10^{8}\,k^{2}(1+\log k)(1+\log B)\cdot(\log\alpha)(k\log 10).

Since

(17)

\log|U_{1}|>-1.228\times 10^{9}\,k^{3}(1+\log k)(1+\log B).

From (
15
) we have
\log|U_{1}|<\log 22-(n-p)\log\alpha
. Comparing (
19
) with this upper bound gives

(n-p)\log\alpha<1.228\times 10^{9}\,k^{3}(1+\log k)(1+\log B)+\log 22.

Neglecting
\log 22
and using

, we obtain

(18)

n-p<\frac{1.228\times 10^{9}}{\log\alpha}\,k^{3}\log k\log n<3.55\times 10^{9}\,k^{3}\log k\log n.

Rewrite equation (
1
) as

g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}-g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{p}-P_{m}^{(k)}10^{d}=e_{p}-e_{n}.

Factoring

Hence

Taking absolute values and using

,
|1-\alpha^{p-n}|>\frac{1}{2}
(since

), and
g_{k}(\alpha)>0.276
, we obtain

(19)

\left|1-\frac{P_{m}^{(k)}10^{d}}{g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}(1-\alpha^{p-n})}\right|<\frac{6}{\alpha^{n}}.

Set
U_{2}=\frac{P_{m}^{(k)}10^{d}}{g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}(1-\alpha^{p-n})}-1
and

\Lambda_{2}=d\log 10-n\log\alpha+\log\frac{P_{m}^{(k)}}{g_{k}(\alpha)(1-\alpha^{p-n})}.

If
\Lambda_{2}=0
, then

\frac{P_{m}^{(k)}10^{d}}{g_{k}(\alpha)(1-\alpha^{p-n})}=\alpha^{n}.

Applying any Galois embedding
\sigma_{i}
(with

,

Since

and

. For

and

>1

\Lambda_{2}\neq 0
.

For

, so

(20)

\Lambda_{2}|\leq 2|U_{2}|<\frac{12}{\alpha^{n}}.

We can now apply Matveev’s theorem again. Now
t=3
. The additional algebraic number is

We need to bound the logarithmic height of

and

, we have

From (

Thus we take

A_{3}=2k\bigl((2n-2p+2)\log\alpha+4\log k+\log 2\bigr).

The other parameters are
A_{1}=k\log 10
,
A_{2}=\log\alpha
, and

Matveev gives

\log|U_{2}|>-1.4\cdot 30^{6}\cdot 3^{4.5}\cdot k^{2}(1+\log k)(1+\log n)\cdot A_{1}A_{2}A_{3}.

Thus

With

From (

Neglecting

Inserting the upper bound on

obtained in (

Applying the Lemma

with

yields

Thus we have a polynomial bound on

.

is a solution of equation (

3.3.
Small values of
k
:
2\leq k\leq 500

We now assume
2\leq k\leq 500
.
Our goal is to reduce the large bound on

obtained in Lemma
3.1
to a small constant using continued fractions and the Baker–Davenport reduction.

From (
15
) we have

\left|1-\frac{10^{d}}{\alpha^{n-m}}\right|<\frac{22}{\alpha^{n-p}}.

Let
\tau=\frac{\log\alpha}{\log 10}
. Dividing by
\log 10
gives

(23)

\left|\tau-\frac{d}{n-m}\right|<\frac{22}{(n-m)\,\alpha^{n-p}\,\log 10}.

Let
q=n-m
. Let
[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\dots]
be the continued fraction expansion of
\tau
. For the given
q
, there exists an index
i
such that
q_{i}\leq q<q_{i+1}
. Define
a_{\max}=\max\{a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{i}\}
. A classical result (see [16, Theorem 1.1.(iv)]) states that for any integer
q
,

(24)

\frac{1}{(a_{\max}+2)q^{2}}\leq\left|\tau-\frac{d}{q}\right|.

Combining (
23
) and (
24
) yields

\frac{1}{(a_{\max}+2)q^{2}}<\frac{22}{q\,\alpha^{n-p}\,\log 10}.

Multiplying by
q
which is positive, gives

\frac{1}{(a_{\max}+2)q}<\frac{22}{\alpha^{n-p}\,\log 10},

hence

(25)

\alpha^{n-p}<\frac{22(a_{\max}+2)q}{\log 10}.

Taking natural logarithms,

(26)

n-p<\frac{\log(22(a_{\max}+2)q)-\log(\log 10)}{\log\alpha}.

For
k\leq 500
, numerical computation with Sagemath
[
16
]
shows that
a_{\max}
is bounded (
a_{\max}\leq 1000
). Using the initial bound
n<10^{45}
(from Lemma
3.1
), we have
q\leq n\leq 10^{45}
. Then the right‑hand side of (
26
) is at most 200. Thus we obtain the uniform bound

(27)

n-p\leq 200\qquad\text{for all }k\leq 500.

We now turn to the second linear form. From (
19
) we have

\left|1-\frac{P_{m}^{(k)}10^{d}}{g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n-1}(1-\alpha^{p-n})}\right|<\frac{6}{\alpha^{n-1}}.

Set

\Lambda_{2}=d\log 10-(n-1)\log\alpha+\log\frac{P_{m}^{(k)}}{g_{k}(\alpha)(1-\alpha^{p-n})}.

For
n\geq 3
we have
|\Lambda_{2}|<\frac{12}{\alpha^{n-1}}
. Dividing by
\log\alpha
gives

(28)

0<\left|d\frac{\log 10}{\log\alpha}-(n-1)+\frac{\log\frac{P_{m}^{(k)}}{g_{k}(\alpha)(1-\alpha^{p-n})}}{\log\alpha}\right|<\frac{12}{\alpha^{n-1}\log\alpha}.

Define

\tau_{k}=\frac{\log 10}{\log\alpha},\qquad\mu_{k,m,p}=\frac{\log\frac{P_{m}^{(k)}}{g_{k}(\alpha)(1-\alpha^{p-n})}}{\log\alpha}.

Then (

) becomes

0<|d\,\tau_{k}-(n-1)+\mu_{k,m,p}|<\frac{16}{\alpha^{n-1}\log\alpha}.

We now apply the Baker–Davenport reduction as formulated by Dujella and Pethő (Lemma

). For each fixed

with

(since we already have

be the denominators of its convergents. Choose a convergent such that

If

by the next convergent

and repeat until
\epsilon>0

Once a convergent with

2.6
gives an explicit bound:

where
