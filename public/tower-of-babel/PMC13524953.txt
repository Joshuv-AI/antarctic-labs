Spatial differentiation and influencing factors of urban economic resilience: an empirical study from China
Authors: Wang Hongyue, Wang Bida, Li Xuerong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Given the intensifying geopolitical tensions, public health emergencies and frequent natural disasters, urban economic stability has become increasingly challenging, and determining how to achieve stable and high-quality urban economic development is a critical issue. In this study, the economic resilience of 286 Chinese prefecture-level cities from 2008 to 2022 is evaluated via the CRITIC-TOPSIS method. Natural breakpoint classification, kernel density estimation, and geographical detector and MGWR models are adopted for hierarchical division, spatial evolution and heterogeneous driving factors. The key findings are as follows: (1) Urban economic resilience grows steadily with obvious hierarchical differentiation; the high-resilience areas are mainly large cities, whereas the level of resilience of small cities remains low. (2) Among the resilience subdimensions, resistance capacity is dominant, followed by recovery and innovation capacity, while innovation capacity shows the strongest growth momentum. (3) Spatially, eastern and central cities exhibit gradient agglomeration characteristics, whereas western and northeastern cities show prominent polarization. (4) The factors that influence display evident spatial heterogeneity. Digitalization, medical care and transportation positively affect resilience, and the effects increase from east to west. Urbanization has stronger positive effects in the eastern and central regions, whereas industrialization has inhibitory effects, particularly in resource-dependent cities. Introduction

In 2020, the sudden onset of the COVID-19 pandemic generated pervasive shocks to global economic systems, public healthcare infrastructure, and corporate operational activities1,2 There were heterogeneous economic responses to this exogenous shock across countries, with significant disparities in the areas of shock resistance and resilience. According to the International Monetary Fund (2024), the economic growth rates of the United States were − 2.28%, 5.9%, and 2.1% in 2020, 2021, and 2022, respectively. Germany’s economic growth rates were − 3.8%, 3.2%, and 1.8%, whereas Japan experienced more significant fluctuations, with rates of − 4.3%, 2.0%, and 1.0%, respectively. In contrast, China’s economic growth rates were 2.2%, 8.4%, and 3%, respectively, over the same period, which fully demonstrates the strong resilience and development vitality of a major economy. As the core carriers of national economic development, cities concentrate key factors such as labour, capital, and technology. As they rely on diversified industrial systems, efficient resource allocation mechanisms, and innovation ecosystems, cities not only possess risk resistance capabilities but also have remarkable potential for self-repair, transformation, and upgrading after crises, becoming the core engine driving the sustainable development of regional economies. On December 5, 2024, the General Office of the State Council issued the Opinions on Promoting the Construction of New-Type Urban Infrastructure and Building Resilient Cities, which explicitly presented the overall requirement of “promoting the digital transformation of urban infrastructure, continuously enhancing urban facility resilience, management resilience, and spatial resilience, and advancing the safe development of cities.” This strategic deployment underscores the significant importance of urban economic resilience for the safeguarding of national economic security and the promotion of high-quality development.

Literature review

Research on economic resilience

The concept of resilience, which is originate from engineering, was first used to describe the recovery time after a system is affected by shocks3. Holling further extended this concept to ecology, emphasizing multiple equilibria rather than original state restoration4. However, this concept still cannot be adapted to systems that change over time. To address this issue, Walker proposed evolutionary resilience, which emphasizes the dynamic adaptability of the environment5. Since then, spatial economics has been brought into evolutionary resilience to account for agglomeration phenomena6. Since the 1990s, the rise of evolutionary economic geography has provided solid theoretical support for resilience theory. Many researchers are interested in economic resilience. From the short-term perspective, resilience is focused on resistance and recovery. However, from the long-term perspective, the concept of resilience highlights innovative development7. Overall, resilience has been summarized as the capacity for resistance, recovery, and innovation from an adaptive perspective8,9.

Variable selection for economic resilience

To select the variables for economic resilience, single-index and multi-index evaluation metrics were used. The former highlights the dynamic evolution and recovery process of the system. By depicting the time changes before and after the disturbance through a single variable, the system’s adaptability to the shock can be intuitively reflected. The variables selected include sensitivity indices such as the regional employment level, real regional GDP, the change rate of employees, and the volume of foreign trade10–13. However, because of the complex and multidimensional characteristics of the urban economic system, comprehensively covering the complex changes in the system with a single indicator is difficult. Hence, the multi-indicator assessment method can be used to more comprehensively reflect the level of resilience. From a macro perspective, the economic resilience index includes industrial structure, the labour market, financial development, government institutions, and infrastructure14,15. In accordance with Martin’s definition of resilience, the measurement framework includes resistance and recovery capability, adaptation capability, and innovation transformation capability16–19, which are variables that can be used to more systematically and comprehensively assess economic resilience.

Measurement methods and objects of economic resilience

For measurement methods, most existing studies construct indicator systems are first constructed in most existing studies, followed by the adoption of the entropy method20–22, coefficient of variation method23, and subjective evaluation methods, which are widely applied because of their objectivity in weight calculation, to assess economic resilience24. However, these methods may suffer from issues such as expert bias, cognitive bias, ignoring the interrelationships between indicators and consistency testing problems, which can introduce bias into the evaluation results. Hence, selecting appropriate evaluation methods is important. The effectiveness of measurement methods depends on the definition of the research object. The initial focus of the literature was on the quantitative evaluation of economic resilience at the national and regional macro scales25–28. Considering the inadequate interpretation of the microscopic mechanisms embedded in macrolevel analyses, scholarly attention has gradually shifted towards industrial and firm-level micro investigations29–32. Additionally, macro and micro analytical perspectives have been integrated in contemporary scholarship. Such integrated frameworks combine the behavioural autonomy of microeconomic agents with the regulatory effects of macro policies, thereby facilitating the improvement of economic resilience via multidimensional synergistic governance33,34.

Factors that influence urban economic resilience

Scholarly works have extensively investigated the determinants of urban economic resilience, including the digital economy35,36, industrial diversification37, financial development38, technological innovation39, and governmental regulation40. Among the multifaceted influencing factors, the core enablers are factor agglomeration and the digital economy. As proposed by Krugman, the fundamental determinants of economic agglomeration are increasing returns to scale, transportation costs, and factor mobility. Driven by self-reinforcing mechanisms, agglomeration continues to persist and evolve, ultimately forming a typical core-periphery spatial structure41,42. Empirical evidence further confirms that industrial agglomeration can increase urban productivity, strengthen the ability of urban areas to withstand external shocks and achieve transformative recovery, thereby underpinning an improvement in economic resilience43. In the context of the digital economy, industrial agglomeration leads to the optimisation of factor allocation and the consolidation of economic foundations to reinforce the level of urban risk resistance risk. Moreover, digital platforms decrease transaction costs and facilitate precise supply–demand coordination, which contributes to post-shock economic recovery44–46. Additionally, the knowledge spillover effect of internet technology promotes corporate technological advancement and stimulates urban innovative momentum47,48. In addition to economic and industrial factors, the literature highlights the notion that infrastructure acts as a crucial safeguard for the mitigation of economic systemic risk to cities.

On the basis of the abundant research achievements in economic resilience, a solid theoretical foundation has been laid by the literature, which provided methodological references for this paper. However, there are still areas in the existing research that need further exploration and improvement. In terms of evaluation methods, traditional approaches ignore the correlations among indicators. In terms of research scope, most of the existing studies are focused on countries, provinces and urban agglomerations49–53, with insufficient analyses of the 286 prefecture-level cities54–57. The existing research on economic resilience is focused mostly on the overall level, while systematic investigations into its subdimensions remain relatively scarce. In terms of driving factor identification, when a single factor is taken as the main research focus, traditional OLS and WGR models are constrained by spatial limitations and become unable to precisely quantify the magnitude of influencing factors58.

On this basis, the marginal contributions of this paper are as follows: First, the CRITIC-TOPSIS framework is constructed, which comprehensively considers the information content and inherent correlations among indicators, thus yielding more objective and reliable weighting results. Second, with 286 prefecture-level cities in China as the research objects, urban economic resilience is decomposed into three dimensions—resistance, recovery, and innovation capacity—to enable refined regional comparative analysis. Third, the inclusion of factors such as agglomeration, digitalization, and infrastructure in the empirical research enriches the study, and an analytical framework that integrates the geographical detector and MGWR is developed, breaking the spatial homogeneity assumption embedded in conventional models and systematically revealing the spatiotemporal evolution, driving determinants, and heterogeneous characteristics of urban economic resilience.

Second, TOPSIS was employed to approach the ideal solution. Logical connections between methods

First, the CRITIC-TOPSIS method was used to construct the urban economic resilience index. Kernel density estimation was conducted to visualise the spatial dynamics of the resilience index, revealing distribution patterns, agglomeration cores, and evolutionary trends. Geographical detectors quantitatively screened the key driving factors behind the spatial differentiation of resilience. The resilience index was used as the dependent variable to measure the explanatory power of various factors and identify the core set of drivers. This not only helped explain the spatial patterns but also served as a preliminary screening step for the MGWR to avoid the inclusion of nonsignificant variables and ensure model simplicity. Multiscale geographically weighted regression (MGWR) was then used to refine the analysis by characterizing the local effects and spatial scales of each key driver. MGWR enables each variable’s relationship to vary by geographic location and estimates its own optimal bandwidth. Thus, how strongly each driving factor influences resilience in each specific city can be precisely quantified, thereby providing empirical support for differentiated regional policies.

These four methods constitute a complete and rigorous logical analytical chain from “assessment (what)” to “description (where)”, then to “diagnosis (shy)”, and finally to “analysis (how).” The methods complement each other and progressively deepen the analysis, collectively revealing the comprehensive level, spatial pattern, underlying causes, and local operating mechanisms of urban economic resilience (see Fig. 1).

Study area, data sources and descriptive statistics

On the basis of the accessibility and feasibility of the data, 286 Chinese cities were selected as the basic spatial units for sampling (see Fig. 2). The main focus of the research is the resilience intensity demonstrated by cities in the face of internal and external shocks. Therefore, the research period ranges from 2008 to 2022. The data are mainly obtained from the China City Statistical Yearbook (2009–2023), ESP data, night light data, and statistical reports on the national economic and social development of each city. The linear interpolation method is used to complete the missing data of individual cities. The study presents descriptive statistics for the original indicators, including sample size, average value, standard deviation, and minimum and maximum values. (see Table 3).

Overall level, multidimensional development, and dynamic evolution of urban economic resilience

Overall level of urban economic resilience

With the help of the ArcGIS natural breakpoint approach, the index was classified into five grades: low level, relatively low level, medium level, relatively high level, and high level. Through spatial visualization technology, the geographical spatial distribution of urban economic resilience was plotted to systematically reveal the spatial–temporal evolution characteristics in 2008, 2012, 2016 and 2020.

As shown in Fig. 3, the average value of urban economic resilience increased steadily from 0.344 to 0.400 between 2008 and 2020, indicating the continuous increase in systemic risk resistance and adaptive capacity of China’s urban economies. Spatially, urban economic resilience followed a typical core-periphery agglomeration structure. Megacities, including Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou and Wuhan, functioned as high-resilience cores, with their resilience score rising from 0.417 to 0.531 at an annual growth rate of 1.73%. In comparison, peripheral large and medium-sized cities experienced mild growth, rising from 0.342 to 0.398, with an annual growth rate of 1.1%. Hierarchical differentiation was prominently observed across the urban samples. High-tier megacities maintained superior resilience endowments that were driven by solid economic bases and locational advantages. Upper-middle-level cities such as Tianjin and Hangzhou strengthened their crisis responsiveness capacity through technological innovation and industrial restructuring, with their share expanding from 3.85% to 10.14%. The medium-resilience cities, which are predominantly concentrated in eastern and southern China, were strongly constrained by regional economic gradients. Notably, the number of lower-tier cities increased from 20 to 83, highlighting the daunting pressure on such cities to improve their levels of resilience. In contrast, lagging cities, including Hegang and Heihe, suffer from infrastructural shortages, industrial rigidities and talent scarcity, resulting in their inferior shock resistance and post-disruption recovery capacity. Overall, China’s urban economic resilience presents pronounced hierarchical stratification and aggravated polarization. Persistent intercity disparities present considerable challenges to balanced regional development and refined urban governance.

Multidimensional development of urban economic resilience

In this paper, urban economic resilience is analysed from three dimensions: resistance, recovery, and innovation capacity (Fig. 4). The time series results indicate that all three dimensions steadily increased from 2009 to 2022. The average values of resistance, recovery, and innovation capacity rose from 0.442, 0.283, and 0.051 to 0.508, 0.381, and 0.093, with annual growth rates of 1.00%, 2.15%, and 4.39%, respectively. Such evolutionary trends reflect China’s sustained achievements in consolidating economic foundations, expanding market scale, and facilitating innovative transformation. Urban economic resilience presents a hierarchical gradient of “resistance > recovery > innovation”, while its growth momentum follows the reverse order of “innovation > recovery > resistance”. Metropolises, including Shanghai, Beijing and Shenzhen, exhibit prominent resistance capacities because of their solid economic foundations and superior average employment and income, which together construct a robust risk defence system. The recovery capacity displays a multicentred and hierarchical spatial pattern with evident north–south differentiation; most northern cities remain at low recovery levels, whereas the southern cities generally have medium-to-high recovery capacity. External shocks such as the financial crisis and COVID-19 accelerated resource reallocation and industrial restructuring during the sample period, which improved the urban recovery capacity through consumption expansion, investment optimization and industrial adjustment. Additionally, the capacity for urban innovation remains generally underdeveloped, following a typical core-periphery spatial pattern. Megacities such as Beijing, Shanghai and Chengdu constitute innovation agglomeration cores, whereas most other cities lag, reflecting distinct scale heterogeneity in innovation development. Therefore, constructing a complete innovation system driven by technological progress and high-end human resources is essential for urban economic stabilization, transformation and upgrading amid external disturbances.

Dynamic evolution of urban economic resilience

As presented in Fig. 5, the national kernel density curve of economic resilience displays obvious right-tailed characteristics and generally shifted rightward from 2008 to 2022, indicating a steady improvement in urban economic resilience. A declining peak height indicates a divergent developmental trend without stable convergence. After 2019, the curve changes from unimodal to bimodal, suggesting apparent stratification between high-resilience and low-resilience cities across China.

Regionally, the eastern, western and northeastern regions show a fluctuating peak trajectory of “decline followed by growth”, which reflects an initial expansion and subsequent contraction of intraregional disparities. Conversely, the central region exhibits continuously increasing peaks with narrowing internal gaps. In terms of distribution extensibility, the right-tailed curves in the eastern and central regions denote prominent hierarchical differentiation, whereas the annually extended right tails in the western and northeastern regions reveal widening urban disparities. In terms of polarization features, the eastern and central regions consistently maintain unimodal distributions with mild internal gaps and high-level agglomeration. By comparison, the western and northeastern regions shift from unimodal to bimodal patterns, which indicates intensifying polarization and increasingly distinct stratification between the high-resilience and low-resilience city groups.

Influence of medical level on urban economic resilience

The results are shown in Fig. 6 (6 − 1). In 2021, the average influence coefficient of the medical level reached 0.218 across the country, which underscores the notion that the medical level plays a relatively large role in enhancing Chinese economic resilience. In terms of geographical space, the influence coefficient shows a regional distribution characteristic as it moves from east to west: “eastern region (0.213) < central region (0.217) < western region (0.22) < northeastern region (0.227).” Since the outbreak of the pandemic in 2020, the degree of impact of the urban medical level on Chinese economic resilience has significantly increased. The coefficient in the northeast was 6.6% greater than that in the east, which underscores the critical role of robust healthcare systems in stabilizing the economy.

Influence of the digitalization level on urban economic resilience

In 2021, the national average coefficient of the effect of digitalization on urban economic resilience was 0.072. The data presented in Fig. 6 (6 − 2) reveal a significant east‒west gradient. In the economically mature eastern region (e.g., Shanghai and Nanjing), the average coefficient was 0.064. In contrast, the coefficient increased to 0.073 in the central region, 0.078 in the southwest region and 0.084 in the northwest region. This means that a one-unit increase in the digitalization index had a 31% stronger effect on economic resilience in the northwest (0.084) than it did in the east (0.064), which highlights the higher marginal returns of digital investment in less developed western regions.

Influence of the urbanization level on urban economic resilience

In 2021, the average value coefficient of the urbanization level across countries reached 0.541, as shown in Fig. 6 (6 − 3), indicating that the urbanization process has an important positive effect on urban economic resilience. In terms of regional patterns, the influence of the urbanization level clearly reflects the spatial differentiation characteristics of the “centre-periphery” type. Among them, the “centre” region covers those cities in the eastern and central regions, while the “periphery” region includes the cities in the northeastern, northwestern, southeastern, and southwestern regions. The average influence coefficient of urbanization in the “center” region is 0.648, while that in the “periphery” areas is only 0.401. The reason for this is that the “center” region, with its superior geographical location, complete infrastructure system, and excellent business environment, has become the core area for the agglomeration of various economic elements.

Influence of the transportation level on urban economic resilience

In 2021, the national average coefficient for the transportation level was 0.150, as shown in Fig. 6 (6 − 4). The impact intensified from the east to the west, with coefficients ranging from as low as approximately 0.14 in the eastern coastal cities to a high of 0.159 in western cities such as Chengdu and Lanzhou. The 13.6% greater impact in the west confirms that efficient logistics systems are more binding constraints and more potent drivers of economic resilience in China’s interior regions, where traditional manufacturing and resource industries still dominate.

Influence of traffic accessibility on urban economic resilience

Traffic accessibility clearly demonstrated a dual-effect mechanism. Its coefficient was negative (− 0.06 on average) in highly developed provinces such as Shanghai, Jiangsu, and Guangdong, which shows a saturation effect where further investment can yield diminishing returns or even resource misallocation. Conversely, in less developed areas, the coefficient was positive (0.076 on average). The absolute difference of 0.136 between these two contexts quantitatively captures this pivotal shift from a positive enabler to a potential liability, guiding radically different infrastructure investment strategies.

Influence of the industrialization level on urban economic resilience

The national average coefficient for industrialization was − 0.600, indicating a substantial negative correlation. The impact was more pronounced in the south, with coefficients reaching as low as − 1.288 in some highly industrialized cities, compared with a milder negative impact in the north. This more than twofold difference in the magnitude of the negative effect for southern cities underscores the severe challenges that they face in terms of path dependence and global supply chain vulnerabilities.

Conclusions and discussions

Discussion

On the basis of resilience theory and the literature, economic resilience is conceptualised in this study into three dimensions—resistance, recovery, and innovation capacity—with a focus on the meso-level perspective of cities. Constructing a three-dimensional evaluation index system of “resistance–recovery–innovation” enables the classification of cities into different tiers. In this study, the overall development level and evolutionary trends of each dimension of urban economic resilience is systematically analysed, the strengths and weaknesses of different cities in terms of resilience development are revealed, and the spatiotemporal evolution process is fully presented. Furthermore, this study incorporates agglomeration factors, digital economy development, infrastructure, and other elements into the driving factor system of urban economic resilience. The varying impacts of these factors on the resilience of cities of different sizes are empirically examined to strengthen the explanation of the underlying causes of spatial heterogeneity in urban economic resilience levels.

This study has several limitations. On the one hand, owing to constraints such as data availability, the influencing factors included may not comprehensively cover all of the relevant variables, which could affect the completeness of the model to some extent. On the other hand, the model constructed on the basis of the current data has limited applicability to international cities, and its generalizability requires further validation. Future research should be focused on expanding the data sources, improving the variable system, and exploring the method’s applicability across different contexts and international cities to enhance the comprehensiveness and broader relevance of the study.

Conclusions and policy recommendations

In this study, the level, dimensional structure, spatial distribution, and drivers of urban economic resilience in China (2008–2022) are analysed. The main conclusions are as follows. First, urban economic resilience steadily increased from 0.344 to 0.400, with temporary slowdowns caused by the 2009 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic. Second, compared with small cities, large cities have greater resilience. The dimensional hierarchy is “resistance > recovery > innovation”, while that of growth momentum follows a “innovation > recovery > resistance” pathway, indicating that innovation is becoming the key driver of long-term resilience. Third, the spatial distribution shows a dual polarization between high- and low-resilience cities. Eastern and central China exhibit high-level agglomeration with shrinking disparities, whereas the western and northeastern regions face widening gaps. Fourth, six spatially heterogeneous drivers are identified. Digitalization, medical resources, and transportation have positive effects, with increasing influence from east to west; urbanization has positive north–south differentiation; industrialization has negative effects; and traffic accessibility has bidirectional effects.

On the basis of the above MGWR empirical results, targeted policy recommendations for the improvement of urban economic resilience are proposed in this section from four perspectives. First, given the higher digitalization coefficients in central and western China, local governments should strengthen digital infrastructure construction and promote industrial digital transformation to leverage digital dividends. Second, prioritized medical investment is needed in the northeastern and western regions because of the dominant positive effect of medical resources, particularly for ageing northeastern cities. Third, differentiated transportation strategies should be implemented: Eastern megacities need intelligent traffic optimization, whereas Central and Western cities require continuous transportation investment for the promotion of regional integration. Fourth, given the negative industrialization effect, northern resource-based cities should pursue industrial diversification, whereas southern industrialized cities should increase innovative investment to reduce industrial lock-in risk. These differentiated optimization paths can provide practical references for the coordinated development of resilience in heterogeneous cities.
