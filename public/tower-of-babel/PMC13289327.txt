Cross-country comparison of perinatal outcomes between japanese mothers in the united states and japan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Asako Mito, Hasumi Tomita, Noriyuki Iwama, Shiori Kawai, Daniel Helkey, Taku Obara, Robert J Huang, Hirotaka Hamada, Aoi Noda, Mami Ishikuro, Shinichi Kuriyama, Fumiaki Ikeno, Naoko Arata, Masatoshi Saito, Latha Palaniappan, Adrian M Bacong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn the USA, studies often show that immigrants exhibit relatively favourable perinatal outcomes compared with their US-born counterparts of the same race/ethnicity, despite socioeconomic and language barriers in healthcare. However, studies with individuals in their home country are limited.Methods and resultsWe analysed 32 028 Japanese who gave singleton births from two data sources: the US vital statistics records (N=19 462) and the Birth and Three-Generation Cohort Study conducted by the Tohoku Medical Megabank Organization (N=12 566) between 2015 and 2017. The US data were divided into two groups based on nativity: the first generation and the second generation and later. Using Japanese women who delivered in Japan as the reference, associations between nativity and perinatal outcomes were evaluated using regression models, with analyses stratified by pre-pregnancy body mass index category. The ORs (95% CI) of preterm birth were higher among Japanese women in the USA (the first generation: 1.20 (1.15 to 1.33); the second generation and later: 1.40 (1.28 to 1.54)), as were the ORs of macrosomia (1.71 (1.48 to 1.98) and 1.98 (1.68 to 2.32), respectively). In contrast, the ORs of term or post-term low birth weight were lower among Japanese women in the USA (0.77 (0.71 to 0.84) and 0.81 (0.71 to 0.91), respectively). The association of nativity on the ORs of low birth weight differed depending on the term of birth.ConclusionsJapanese immigrants to the USA have heterogenous risk of perinatal outcomes compared with Japanese who remain in Japan. Including home country data in immigration studies may provide a more detailed understanding of the impact of immigration on their perinatal outcomes.

Introduction

Asian Americans are the fastest-growing racial/ethnic demographic in the USA, with their population nearly doubling between 2000 and 2019.1 Immigration status is a social determinant that influences individual health.2 The NIHONSAN study36 suggested that immigration to the USA may play a significant role in the occurrence of coronary heart disease for Japanese. Health disparities among immigrants also apply to perinatal outcomes,7 8 but there are limited reports that include analyses of those in their countries of origin.9 10 Asian Americans constitute a diverse subgroup, and the risk of poor perinatal outcomes such as neonatal mortality,11 preterm birth (PTB)12 13 and birth weight14 varies by subgroups. Furthermore, within the same Asian subgroup, research has investigated differences in perinatal outcomes by nativity.1316 Among these studies, only one previous report has explicitly focused on Japanese Americans,16 comparing pregnancy outcomes between US-born and foreign-born Japanese women. Other studies have primarily examined perinatal outcomes across broader Asian subgroups or multiple Asian ethnicities,1315 without contemporaneous comparison to women residing in Japan. Some studies have reported modestly favourable perinatal outcomes among foreign-born women compared with US-born women, a pattern often referred to as the ‘healthy immigrant paradox’. However, these advantages often disappear with longer residence in the USA and in later generations. Moreover, comparisons between foreign-born and US-born individuals often occur many years after arrival in the USA. Thus, social stressors and the process of acculturation/integration may adversely affect health behaviours and healthcare access, leading to an attenuation of the ‘healthy immigrant paradox’. Pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) is a well-established determinant of perinatal outcomes1719 and is also known to differ by nativity and generation among Asian populations in the USA, reflecting lifestyle changes associated with acculturation.2022 Therefore, examining perinatal outcomes stratified by pre-pregnancy BMI may help clarify potential heterogeneity in the association between generation and perinatal outcomes. However, the extent to which perinatal outcomes among immigrant women are similar to women of their home country is unknown. In this study, we investigate the extent to which perinatal outcomes differ among immigrant Japanese in the USA compared with Japanese in Japan.

Methods

Study subject and data resources

The Japanese analysed in this study included data from Japanese residing in the USA obtained from the publicly available US National Vital Statistics System (NVSS),23 which represents the entire USA, and data from Japanese living in Japan from the Birth and Three-Generation Cohort Study conducted by the Tohoku Medical Megabank Organization (ToMMo) (TMM BirThree Cohort Study)24 represent the northern part of Japan. Japanese with singleton live births at 22 weeks of gestation or later were recruited for this study between 2015 and 2017. Within the NVSS birth data, instances where the mother’s race was recorded as Japanese (only) were considered perinatal outcomes of Japanese. In the NVSS, ‘Japanese (only)’ indicates that the mother was recorded as single-race Japanese, meaning that Japanese was selected without any additional race categories. Mothers with nativity recorded as ‘born outside the USA (including possessions)’ were defined as ‘the first generation’, while those recorded as ‘born in the USA (50 US states)’ were defined as ‘the second generation and later’. Regarding the data in Japan, women born outside Japan were excluded. As there were no women in the Japanese cohort study who lacked perinatal care during pregnancy, women with no perinatal care in the NVSS birth data were excluded. Women with pre-existing hypertension and diabetes were excluded from the study because they may reflect lifestyle exposure. After applying the exclusion criteria, 19 462 were included in the analysis: 13 980 first generation and 5482 second generation and later. 12 566 met the inclusion criteria and were included as the Japanese in Japan (figure 1).

Data analysis framework

Perinatal outcomes

The perinatal outcomes in this study were PTB (delivery before 37 completed weeks of gestation) and low birth weight (LBW; birth weight<2500 g). LBW was further classified into preterm LBW (pt-LBW; delivery before 37 completed weeks of gestation) and term or post-term LBW (t-LBW; delivery at or after 37 completed weeks of gestation). Macrosomia (birth weight≥4000 g) was also evaluated.

Body mass index

The prevalence of obesity increases among Asian people in the USA in subsequent generations.25 Pre-pregnancy lifestyle, especially pre-pregnancy BMI, impacts perinatal outcomes among the Japanese.26 BMI was categorised according to the WHO classification27: underweight (BMI<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m2) and obesity (BMI≥30 kg/m2).

Social determinants of health

In this study, we extracted maternal marital status, maternal educational status and conception method as social determinants of health (SDOH) that could confound the association between nativity and perinatal outcomes. Income was not used as it was not available in the NVSS birth data.

Smoking status

We categorised smoking status as never, quit smoking before and after conception, continued smoking during pregnancy.

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy

The diagnoses listed in the NVSS birth data are based on the US Standard Certificate of Live Birth which includes for hypertension: pre-pregnancy (chronic), gestational (pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH), pre-eclampsia) and eclampsia. In this study, gestational was designated as the new-onset hypertensive disorders of pregnanc (HDP). Among the Japanese in Japan, new-onset hypertension with or without proteinuria was defined as new onset HDP.28

Gestational diabetes mellitus

The Certificate of Live Birth includes for diabetes: pre-pregnancy (diagnosis prior to this pregnancy) and gestational (diagnosis in this pregnancy). Among the Japanese in Japan, the diagnosis of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is made in two steps.29

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were expressed as median (IQR) and number (percentage), respectively, because continuous variables showed non-normal distributions. Differences among generations were tested using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables (table 1).

The associations between nativity and perinatal outcomes after stratifying by pre-pregnancy BMI were evaluated using logistic regression models. For PTB, binary logistic regression models were used, whereas for t-LBW, pt-LBW and macrosomia, multinomial logistic regression models were applied (figure 2). The Japanese in Japan were set as a reference category. Model 1 was the unadjusted model. Model 2 was adjusted for pre-pregnancy BMI,17 30 maternal age at delivery,31 conception method,32 parity,33 maternal smoking status,34 marital status,35 maternal highest level of educational level,36 37 month of pregnancy when perinatal care began38 39 and infant sex.40 41 Covariates included in model 2 were selected a priori based on previous epidemiological and clinical studies demonstrating their associations with perinatal outcomes, as well as their biological or clinical relevance as potential confounders. The gestational weight gain (GWG) was not included in model 2 because GWG would be an intervening variable. In the analysis of the association between nativity and LBW in obesity subjects, outcome of LBW was defined as preterm or t-LBW infants due to small numbers of t-LBW infants. In underweight subjects, the OR could not be estimated due to small numbers of macrosomia.

The association between pre-pregnancy BMI and PTB by nativity was evaluated using binary logistic regression. The associations between pre-pregnancy BMI with t-LBW, pt-LBW and macrosomia in each generation were investigated using multinomial logistic regression (figure 3). Normal birth weight was used as the reference category in multinomial logistic regression models. Since the association of pre-pregnancy BMI with each perinatal outcome was potentially non-linear, restricted cubic spline function with four knots was applied. Pre-pregnancy BMI of 20.6 was set as reference value which corresponds to the median of Japanese in Japan. The location of four knots was placed at 5, 35, 65 and 95 percentiles.42 The same adjustment strategy as described above was applied. There was no strong multicollinearity between covariates in model 2. Since the covariates had a non-monotone missing data pattern, missing data on covariates were multiply imputed using multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE). Imputation model was created using each outcome and covariates in model 2.43 After a total of 40 datasets were created using MICE, same analysis was performed in each dataset. Then, each result of 40 datasets were combined using Rubin’s rule.44 A two-sided p value of <0.05 were considered as statistically significant. The SAS software (V.9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA), the Stata software (18 StataCorp LP, College Station, Texs, USA) and R (V.4.3.1; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria)45 were used for statistical analyses.

Results

Participants

Participant characteristics are presented in table 1. The first generation had the highest median maternal age (34 years) at childbirth and the Japanese in Japan had the lowest (32 years). The second generation and later had the highest prevalence of pre-pregnancy overweight (21.2%) and obesity (12.0%), and the prevalence of pre-pregnancy underweight was highest among the Japanese in Japan (15.3%). The proportions of never-smokers and those who attended college or graduate school were significantly lower among the Japanese in Japan. The prevalence of new onset HDP was highest among the Japanese in Japan (7.7%), followed by the second and later (5.0%) and the first (2.2%) generations. The prevalence of GDM was highest in the second generation and later (8.8%), followed by the first generation (5.7%) and Japanese in Japan (2.4%).

Preterm birth

The prevalence of PTB was highest among the second generation and later (8.4%), followed by the first generation (6.4%) and the Japanese in Japan (4.5%). Using the Japanese in Japan as the reference group, the OR of PTB was higher in the first generation in the pre-pregnancy normal weight (1.24; 1.15 to 1.33) and overweight categories (1.40; 1.13 to 1.73); in the second generation and later, the OR of PTB was higher in the pre-pregnancy underweight (1.72; 1.34 to 2.21), normal weight (1.40; 1.28 to 1.54) and overweight categories (1.45; 1.16 to 1.82) (figure 2A). Supplementary tables show maternal and neonatal characteristics by pre-pregnancy BMI for each generation. In all BMI categories, the second generation and later had a higher prevalence of HDP and GDM than the first generation (online supplemental table 4)

In the Japanese in Japan, a low OR of PTB was observed in the pre-pregnancy normal weight category, and an increased OR was observed in the pre-pregnancy underweight, overweight and obese categories (figure 3A, B). In the first generation, the OR of PTB increased as the pre-pregnancy BMI category increased (figure 3C, D) while in the second generation and later, an increased OR was observed in the pre-pregnancy underweight category (figure 3E, F). Trends in the relationship between pre-pregnancy BMI category and OR of PTB were different in each generation.

Low birth weight

The prevalence of LBW was highest in the Japanese in Japan (8.1%), followed by the second generation and later (6.7%) and the first generation (6.0%). Based on Japanese in Japan, the OR of t-LBW is lower in the first generation in the pre-pregnancy underweight (0.76; 0.65 to 0.89) and normal weight categories (0.77; 0.71 to 0.84) and the second generation and later in the pre-pregnancy normal weight category (0.81; 0.71 to 0.91), while the OR of pt-LBW was higher in the second generation and later in the pre-pregnancy normal weight (1.15; 1.01 to 1.30) and overweight categories (1.49; 1.07 to 2.07) (figure 2B, C). Based on the first generation, the OR of t-LBW is higher in Japanese in Japan, while the OR of pt-LBW is higher in the second generation and later (in the pre-pregnancy underweight and normal weight categories) (online supplemental figures B and C).

The ORs of t-LBW and pt-LBW in the pre-pregnancy underweight category were increased in all groups (figure 3G–R). In Japanese in Japan, there was a trend towards a decreased OR of t-LBW as the pre-pregnancy BMI category increased (figure 3G, H), but this was not necessarily true for the first generation and the second generation and later (figure 3I–L).

Macrosomia

The prevalence of macrosomia was highest among the second generation and later (4.8%), followed by the first generation (3.1%) and Japanese in Japan (0.9%). The OR of macrosomia was higher for the first generation (pre-pregnancy normal weight: 1.71; 1.48 to 1.98, overweight: 1.92; 1.44 to 2.58, obesity: 2.73; 1.49 to 4.98) and the second generation and later (pre-pregnancy normal weight: 1.98; 1.68 to 2.32, overweight: 1.68; 1.24 to 2.28, obesity: 2.45; 1.35 to 4.49) in all pre-pregnancy BMI categories compared with Japanese in Japan (figure 2D).

In the analysis of pre-pregnancy BMI and OR of macrosomia, the OR of macrosomia increased with increasing BMI category for the first generation and the second generation and later. A similar trend was observed in the Japanese in Japan (figure 3S–X).

Discussion

In this study, perinatal outcomes of Japanese in the USA were analysed by nativity, including of Japanese in Japan during the same period. Adding the data from home countries to comparisons by nativity, it was understood that the first generation have similar situations in their home countries and influenced perinatal outcomes.

Preterm birth

In the pre-pregnancy normal weight category, later immigrant generations have a higher OR of PTB (online supplemental figure A) even though HDP prevalence was highest in the Japanese in Japan. Since home blood pressure measurement is widespread in Japan, there might be more opportunities for blood pressure monitoring and diagnosis of HDP in Japan than in the USA. In addition, differences in healthcare systems between the two countries might be related to the development of PTB. Previous reports46 have shown that maternity care systems differ substantially between Japan and the USA, including the organisation and continuity of prenatal care, provider mix and the availability of structured postnatal support such as home visits. In particular, the US system is characterised by more fragmented insurance coverage and limited universal postnatal care, whereas Japan provides more continuous prenatal care and extended postpartum support. These system-level differences may contribute to variations in maternal and perinatal outcomes, although they could not be directly evaluated in the present study. In the present study, the analysis by nativity, similar to previous reports on perinatal outcomes among Japanese in the USA,12 13 the OR of PTB was higher in the second generation and later compared with the first generation in the pre-pregnancy underweight and normal weight categories. The fact that HDP and GDM are more common in the second generation and later may be related to this result. The increased GWG despite a higher proportion of PTB among the second generation and later suggests that this is associated with the development of HDP and GDM.

According to Enomoto et al,17 the OR of PTB among Japanese was lowest for those with normal pre-pregnancy BMI, forming a U-shaped curve. In the present study, it represents the northern part of Japan, and clear differences in the OR of PTB were not observed among the Japanese in Japan based on pre-pregnancy BMI, but similarities were observed. Interestingly, for the first generation, pre-pregnancy underweight category tended to have a lower OR of PTB.

Low birth weight

Although the proportion of LBW is highest in the Japanese in Japan, stratified analysis by pre-pregnancy BMI category in this study revealed that the OR of t-LBW was lowest in the first generation, and the OR of pt-LBW was highest in the second generation and later. The low prevalence of HDP in the first generation and the high prevalence of GDM and PTB in the second generation and later may be related to this difference. Even among Asian women, Japanese were with a relatively high proportion of LBW and small for gestational age infants.14 29 47 While some studies have indicated a higher prevalence of LBW among the second generation and later than among the first generation,14 others have shown no difference between the first and second generations and later.15 16 48 Further analysis with more detailed information on gestational week, pregnancy complications and GWG will be necessary. Similar to previous reports,17 as the pre-pregnancy BMI increased the OR of t-LBW decreased among Japanese in Japan.

Macrosomia

In our study, the OR of macrosomia increased in later generations. This may be related to an increase in the rate of GDM and the higher rate of excessive GWG.

Although no clear trend was observed in this study, the OR of macrosomia increased with each increase in the pre-pregnancy BMI category, as previously reported in Japan.17

Conceptual considerations of environmental exposure across generations

When discussing immigration, it is conceptually useful to consider the environments to which individuals may have been exposed in utero and after birth. However, our analysis cannot disentangle environmental exposure from other external factors such as social determinants of health (SOH) or differences in healthcare access, and the following considerations should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. From this perspective, the Japanese in Japan are exposed to the Japanese lifestyle both in utero and after birth, while the second generation and later are exposed to the American lifestyle. The first generation is exposed to the Japanese lifestyle in utero and to a mixture of Japanese and American lifestyle after birth. Our results suggest that environmental change of the immigration to the USA at any point in life may be associated for Japanese in the risk of PTB and macrosomia49 The higher ORs of PTB and pt-LBW observed among the second generation and later compared with the first generation (online supplemental figures A, C) may be consistent with an association between early-life exposure to the US environment and these outcomes, although this interpretation remains speculative.50 In contrast, the similar ORs of t-LBW and macrosomia between the first generation and the second generation and later in analyses stratified by pre-pregnancy BMI (online supplemental figures B, D) suggest that postnatal environmental factors may play a role; however, these effects cannot be distinguished from other correlated factors in the present study. Together, these observations highlight the potential importance of considering individuals’ background and life-course context when interpreting perinatal risks.

Strengths and limitations

This study is a cross-country comparison study of perinatal outcomes, examining differences in nativity among three groups. It is considered a strength that new insights have been added to the perinatal outcomes of Japanese immigrants in the USA, as there have been few cross-national comparative studies in the field of race/ethnicity and health.51 Stratified analysis by pre-pregnancy BMI category has also not been previously reported. However, some stratified analyses, particularly those involving macrosomia and preterm LBW within certain pre-pregnancy BMI categories, were based on relatively small sample sizes. As a result, statistical power may have been limited, and non-significant findings should be interpreted with caution (online supplemental tables 1-3). This study has some limitations. First, the data of ‘Japanese in Japan’ used in this study are limited to the Tohoku region, thus may not reflect the entire population of Japan.52 Second, the duration of residence in the USA for the first generation was unknown. It has been reported that the longer immigrants reside in the USA, the higher the prevalence of pre-pregnancy obesity.53 Thirdly, the generation of Japanese born in the USA is not clear. While there is a trend for the children of immigrants to resemble US-born children in having higher BMI as immigrant families spend more generations in the USA,54 it remains unclear to what extent this phenomenon affects the second generation and later in this study. The fourth point is that, due to the unavailability of household income data in the NVSS, this study has not been able to analyse economic status55 among the SDOH as a confounding factor. The fifth point is that this study has not conducted an analysis on the race/ethnicity of women’s partners. Perinatal outcomes are influenced not only by maternal factors but also by paternal factors,5658 but this study has not accounted for their influence due to known high missingness rates on birth certificates.

Conclusion

While acknowledging the challenges of making direct comparisons owing to differences in healthcare systems, medical procedures and treatments across countries, we believe that, as this study involved a cross-country comparison, it may provide additional insights beyond previous research on immigrant perinatal outcomes.
