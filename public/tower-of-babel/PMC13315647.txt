Vitamin D deficiency and associated factors among cardiology patients in Nouakchott, Mauritania: A cross-sectional study using modified Poisson regression

Introduction

Vitamin D, a fat-soluble vitamin, is essential for bone health, immune function, and metabolic regulation, with 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] serving as the primary biomarker of vitamin D status [1], [2]. Deficiency, defined as 25(OH)D <20 ng/mL, is a global public health concern associated with increased risks of cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and dyslipidemia [3], [4], [5], [6]. Cardiology patients are particularly vulnerable due to the interplay between vitamin D deficiency and cardiometabolic comorbidities, such as hypertension and lipid disorders [7].

Despite abundant sunlight, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Mauritania report high deficiency rates due to cultural practices (e.g., concealing clothing) and limited dietary sources [8], [9].

Epidemiological studies suggest that several demographic and lifestyle factors contribute to vitamin D status, including age, gender, education level, employment status, smoking, and physical activity [10], [11], [12]. Lower vitamin D levels have been observed in older individuals, women, and those with lower education levels [13], [14]. Additionally, smoking and sedentary lifestyles have been linked to an increased prevalence of vitamin D deficiency, whereas regular physical activity and higher socioeconomic status appear to be protective factors [12], [15]. Given these associations, understanding the interplay between vitamin D status and various health determinants is critical for developing effective prevention and intervention strategies.

Beyond demographic and lifestyle influences, vitamin D deficiency has been implicated in multiple clinical conditions, including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular diseases [8], [16]. The role of vitamin D in lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity suggests that its deficiency may exacerbate metabolic dysfunction and increase the risk of chronic disease progression [17]. However, despite extensive research, the specific mechanisms underlying these associations remain an area of ongoing investigation.

Despite the growing body of research on vitamin D deficiency, there is a notable gap in knowledge regarding its prevalence and risk factors in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, including Mauritania, despite abundant sunlight. Many existing studies focus on high-income countries, where lifestyle, diet, and healthcare access differ significantly from those in LMICs [18]. Reliable data on vitamin D deficiency among specific subgroups in these regions are scarce, making it challenging to develop targeted public health interventions.

However, data on vitamin D status among cardiology patients in sub-Saharan Africa, including Mauritania, remain scarce. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate vitamin D deficiency in Mauritanian cardiology patients. We aimed to determine the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency and identify associated demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors.

Objectives

This study aimed to: (1) determine the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency among cardiology patients at the National Cardiology Center in Nouakchott, and (2) identify associated demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors using prevalence ratio (PR) estimation to avoid overestimation of associations given the anticipated high outcome prevalence.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the National Cardiology Center (CNC) in Nouakchott, Mauritania, from January to December 2021. A total of 170 patients with recorded vitamin D testing were identified from CNC's electronic database. The predominance of female participants in this cardiology cohort, while differing from the typical male predominance of CVD incidence, is consistent with hospital-based studies from the region. This likely reflects sex-specific healthcare-seeking behaviors, where women in Mauritania are more likely to attend outpatient cardiology services for routine care and symptom management, whereas men may present later with acute events. Sex was categorized as male or female based on medical records.

Data collection and measurements

Outcome measures

Vitamin D status was defined using serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] concentrations, measured via chemiluminescence immunoassay. Participants were categorized into two groups based on the Institute of Medicine (IOM) guidelines: deficiency (<20 ng/mL or <50 nmol/L) and normal levels (≥20 ng/mL or ≥50 nmol/L) [19], [20]. This cut point of ≥20 ng/mL is widely accepted for maintaining bone health and is consistent with recommendations for assessing vitamin D status in populations at risk for cardiovascular disease [21].

Data collection

Sociodemographic data, including age, sex, locality, education level, and socioeconomic status, were extracted from the CNC database and validated through structured interviews. Anthropometric measurements were collected at enrollment by trained staff using standardized protocols. Height (cm) and weight (kg) were recorded to calculate body mass index (BMI; kg/m2), and nutritional status was categorized according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria (underweight, normal, overweight, obese). Sex and clinical data, including comorbidities, medication use, and cardiovascular health indicators, were retrieved from electronic medical records.

Additional covariates included lifestyle and dietary habits, assessed through semi-structured questionnaire evaluating the frequency of food consumption. Chronic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and obesity were confirmed through medical records.

Statistical analysis

Data cleaning and preparation were prioritized to ensure robustness. Duplicate entries and inconsistencies, such as implausible anthropometric values or missing key variables, were systematically addressed through iterative checks, cross-referencing with hospital records, and consultations with clinical staff. Continuous variables (e.g., age, BMI) and categorical data (e.g., sociodemographic factors, vitamin D status) were coded, harmonized, and merged into a unified dataset for analysis.

Descriptive statistics summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables (e.g., age, 25(OH)D) were reported as means (standard deviation) or medians (interquartile range), and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Given the high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency (74.7%), logistic regression would substantially overestimate associations. Therefore, we used modified Poisson regression with robust (sandwich) variance estimators to directly estimate prevalence ratios (PRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [22], [23]. This approach is widely recommended when the outcome prevalence exceeds 20%.

Variable selection for multivariable models was guided by a priori causal considerations using a directed acyclic graph (DAG) and review of the literature. Age, sex, and education level were included as core confounders in all models. Additional variables (household wealth, dietary habits, physical activity) were retained based on their theoretical importance, irrespective of their significance in univariable analyses.

Univariable modified Poisson regression estimated PRs and 95% CIs for factors associated with vitamin D deficiency. Multivariable modified Poisson regression, stratified by sex, adjusted for the a priori-identified confounders. Sex-stratified analyses were considered exploratory given the limited sample size (males: n = 46; females: n = 124). These results are presented with appropriate caution, emphasizing the wide confidence intervals and the need for confirmation in larger studies. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata v17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA), with significance set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of the University of Nouakchott. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their accompanying person. For illiterate participants, oral consent was documented after a detailed explanation of the study's objectives and procedures. Data anonymization and confidentiality were rigorously maintained throughout the study.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

Of the 170 participants, 74.71% (n = 127) had vitamin D deficiency (25(OH)D < 20 ng/mL), and 25.29% (n = 43) had normal levels (≥20 ng/mL). The mean age was 52.58 years (SD 13.81), with 72.94% females. The median 25(OH)D level was 9.55 ng/mL (IQR 11.9), with significantly lower levels in the deficiency group (8.10 ng/mL, IQR 3.7) compared to the normal group (24.00 ng/mL, IQR 8.4; p < 0.001). High cholesterol (PR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.07–2.51) and high LDL (PR = 1.83, 95% CI: 1.10–3.05) were also significant.

Discussion

High prevalence of vitamin D deficiency

This study found a high prevalence (74.71%) of vitamin D deficiency among cardiology patients in Nouakchott, Mauritania.

This finding aligns with regional studies in North Africa, where cultural practices (e.g., concealing clothing) and dietary insufficiencies limit vitamin D intake and synthesis [24]. Higher education and middle household wealth were unexpectedly associated with deficiency, possibly due to indoor lifestyles reducing sun exposure. These associations were stronger in females, likely reflecting gender-specific clothing practices in Mauritania. To address this, public health interventions should include routine vitamin D screening for cardiology patients, particularly females, and fortification of staple foods like milk and bread.

This results also aligning with regional studies in North Africa where cultural practices (e.g., clothing limiting sun exposure) and dietary insufficiencies are prevalent [9], [25], [26]. The near-universal deficiency highlights a critical public health gap, particularly in populations with limited access to fortified foods or supplements [27], [28], [29].

Our findings align with previous epidemiological studies indicating that vitamin D deficiency is associated with adverse metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes [30], [31]. The interplay between smoking and vitamin D status supports prior research suggesting that smoking-induced oxidative stress impairs vitamin D metabolism. Similarly, the protective role of physical activity in maintaining adequate vitamin D levels is consistent with literature emphasizing outdoor exposure and improved metabolic function [32], [33].

Sociodemographic and lifestyle determinants

The unexpected associations of higher education and middle household wealth with deficiency may reflect indoor lifestyles that reduce sun exposure among more affluent or educated individuals who work indoors. This paradoxical finding has been reported in other LMIC settings where urbanization and indoor employment are increasing [34]. Future research should consider longitudinal studies to explore the causal mechanisms underlying these associations and develop targeted interventions for at-risk populations.

Poor dietary habits and physical inactivity emerged as strong, modifiable risk factors. Patients with poor dietary habits had a 39% higher prevalence of vitamin D deficiency after adjustment for confounders, emphasizing the importance of dietary interventions. Similarly, physically inactive patients had a 79% higher prevalence of deficiency compared to those with regular physical activity.

Sex differences

Sex-stratified analyses were exploratory and underpowered, as evidenced by the wide confidence intervals for estimates in males and females. While the point estimates for education and physical inactivity were higher in females, these differences were not statistically significant and should be interpreted with caution. Larger studies are needed to confirm whether sex modifies these associations [12], [35].

Cardiometabolic comorbidities

The association with high cholesterol and LDL suggests a link between vitamin D deficiency and dyslipidemia, consistent with observational studies [7]. The inverse association with hypertension (OR = 0.27) is intriguing and may reflect confounding or reverse causation, warranting further investigation. Limitations include the cross-sectional design, which precludes causality, and potential unmeasured confounders (e.g., sun exposure duration). Future studies should explore longitudinal effects and the impact of supplementation in this population.

The robust associations between vitamin D deficiency diabetes, and dyslipidemia corroborate global evidence implicating vitamin D in cardiovascular and metabolic regulation [36]. Mechanistically, vitamin D modulates renin-angiotensin system activity, insulin sensitivity, and lipid metabolism, suggesting that deficiency may both contribute to and result from cardiometabolic dysfunction.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including being the first to report vitamin D status among cardiology patients in Mauritania and the use of modified Poisson regression with robust variance to avoid overestimation of associations in the setting of a high-prevalence outcome.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; all reported associations are correlational. Second, selection bias is likely, as vitamin D testing was ordered at the clinician's discretion, potentially inflating the observed prevalence of deficiency. Third, despite our efforts to control for key confounders using a DAG, residual confounding by unmeasured factors (e.g., actual sun exposure, skin type, dietary supplementation) cannot be ruled out. Fourth, no formal sample size calculation was performed a priori, as this was an exploratory study in a setting with no prior published data. Consequently, the study was underpowered for subgroup analyses (e.g., sex-stratified models), as reflected by the wide confidence intervals around sex-specific estimates. Fifth, our findings are derived from a single cardiology center in Nouakchott and may not be generalizable to other populations or primary care settings in Mauritania.

Public health implications and future directions

The results carry significant public health implications, necessitating urgent policy action. This includes the implementation of screening programs that prioritize high-risk groups such as women, obese individuals, and those with diabetes for routine vitamin D testing. To effectively address this issue on a broader scale, comprehensive nutritional interventions should be implemented, advocating for widespread food fortification and robust public education initiatives emphasizing the importance of both sun exposure and dietary sources of vitamin D. Looking towards the future, longitudinal research is crucial to further our understanding, aiming to investigate causality and rigorously evaluate the long-term impact of supplementation on cardiovascular outcomes, thereby informing evidence-based recommendations. Furthermore, advanced analyses, such as employing multinomial regression to identify predictors of severe deficiency versus insufficiency and utilizing mediation models to explore the role of lipid profiles as potential intermediates, could significantly deepen our mechanistic insights into this critical area.

Conclusion

This study found a high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency associated with socioeconomic and lifestyle factors among cardiology patients in Nouakchott, Mauritania. These associations highlight potential targets for future interventional studies and public health strategies, particularly addressing physical inactivity, poor dietary habits, and socioeconomic barriers. Targeted screening and supplementation programs may be considered, especially for females and those with identified risk factors, but causal benefits require confirmation in randomized controlled trials.
