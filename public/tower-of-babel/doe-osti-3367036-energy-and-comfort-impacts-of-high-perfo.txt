 ABSTRACT

Building facades have a major effect on energy use, occupant comfort, and well-being. Yet adoption of
high-performance facades is often slowed by limited data and unclear cost benefits. To address this gap,
Oak Ridge National Laboratory, in collaboration with the Facade Tectonics Institute, conducted whole-
building energy simulations to evaluate fenestration technologies for small and medium office buildings
across three weather locations: hot (Tampa, Florida, weather zone 2A), mixed (New York, New York,
weather zone 4A), and cold (Rochester, Minnesota, weather zone 6A). The simulations included
parametric variations in window-to-wall ratio (30%–70%), U-values (0.1–1 Btu/h∙ft2∙°F), solar heat gain
coefficients (0.2–0.8), and solar control strategies and devices (e.g., interior shades and switchable
glazing). Performance metrics included annual cooling, heating, and total heating, ventilation and air-
conditioning (HVAC) energy use intensity, as well as nonenergy factors such as useful daylight
illuminance, glare frequency, and thermal comfort during typical office hours (8 a.m.–6 p.m.). Results
indicate that cooling energy consumption is most sensitive to solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC) in hot
weather, whereas heating energy consumption is strongly influenced by U-value in cold weather. Total
HVAC reflects these trade-offs, showing up to 60% difference for a window in total HVAC energy use
intensity between different combinations of U-value and SHGC. Daylight dimming generally reduces
cooling loads but can increase heating demands in colder locations. Interior solar shades improve useful
daylight and reduce discomfort glare, whereas switchable glazing delivers the largest cooling reductions
in hot weather but may increase heating loads in winter by limiting passive solar gains. Thermal comfort
improves with lower window-to-wall ratios and lower SHGC in hot locations and with lower U-values in
cold locations.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Building facades are major contributors to building aspects such as energy performance, peak demand,
and passive survivability during power interruptions and extreme weather events. A study estimated that
windows account for 2.15 quads of heating energy consumption and 1.48 quads of cooling energy
consumption annually (Apte & Arasteh, 2008). In addition, they directly affect complex interactions
related to building occupants such as view, daylight, air quality, and thermal and visual comfort, which
are essential for occupant health, well-being, and productivity (Kunwar & Bhandari, 2020; R. Johnson,
1984). The facade quality and appearance are also important factors in the market and for the business
value of a building. The current challenge is that although building facade systems are becoming more
complex, they are being built to relatively low energy and durability standards (Facade Tectonics
Institute, 2024). These systems are not using the commonly available commercial high-performance
systems and their performance are far below the best available products and designs on the market today.
Facade Tectonics Institute (FTI) has studied the barriers to adoption of high-performance fenestration and
facades, and has identified several of the key root causes of this market gap, including lack of
definition/specification for high-performance facade systems and broad economic analyses for cost-
effectiveness (Facade Tectonics Institute, 2024).

Several studies related to window performance have been conducted in the past. Estimation of the effect
of windows on energy consumed by building stock was performed, considering seven residential and five
commercial window types (Apte & Arasteh, 2008); the effect of solar shades on energy and daylighting
performance was also studied (Atzeri et al., 2018; Kunwar et al., 2019). Energy performance of different
window attachments was evaluated for four types of houses at 12 locations(Curcija et al., 2013).
Performance of thermotropic windows on buildings was evaluated for different tilt angle of window
across few combinations of optical properties(Allen et al., 2017). However, these studies only focused on
one to two window types, so they do not provide comprehensive analysis on the effect of solar shades
across different window types with several combinations of U-value and solar heat gain coefficient

 1

(SHGC). Arasteh et. al. evaluated the technical potential of highly insulating windows, dynamic
windows, and integrated facades (Arasteh et al., 2006). The study focused on finding total potential
energy savings over building stock rather than on comparing the performance of different windows over
varying locations and building types.

This report presents work done by Oak Ridge National Laboratory in collaboration with FTI to expand
FTI’s efforts assist industry in identifying and specifying existing and emerging technologies and design
solutions for high performance non-residential facades. Market transformation efforts require improved
high-performance facade/fenestration specifications to facilitate incentive programs, updated business
models, new design tools, and exemplars for high-performance building facades. These efforts address
several critical barriers that building industry professionals have identified as key obstacles to change.
This study provides a detailed analysis of window energy performance and nonenergy benefits using
whole-building energy simulation to determine criteria for high-performance windows.

 2. BUILDING ENERGY SIMULATION

Whole building energy simulation was performed using EnergyPlus (DOE, 2025) for parameters listed in
Table 1. The simulations were performed for combinations of each of the listed parameters, resulting in
more than 3,500 simulations. As indicated in the table, the simulation was performed for two building
types of two vintages at three locations representing hot, mixed, and cold weather locations. For U-value
and SHGC of the facade system, the values were chosen to cover the full range of products from the
lower end to the higher end. For example, U-value of 1 Btu/h∙ft2∙°F represents something like a single-
glazed window, whereas U-value of 0.1 Btu/h∙ft2∙°F could potentially be achieved using recent
technologies or products such as triple-pane windows or vacuum-insulated-glazing windows. The U-value
and SHGC values modeled in the study represents the properties of whole window system, i.e. effective
properties for combination of center of glass values, edge of glass, and frame properties, for both U and
SHGC, that collectively define the total WWR. The US Department of Energy prototype building models
developed by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL, 2019) are used as baseline models in which
changes in the simulation parameters are made. The simulation parameter also includes cases with
shading device and switchable glazing which are further discussed in Section 2.2.

2.2 SHADING DEVICES AND CONTROLS

This research study used two solar control technologies—interior solar shade and switchable glazing—to
evaluate the effect of solar shading compared with the baseline case which is a window without any
shading. Note that each of these design solutions has an impact on daylight transmission, glare and solar
heat gain when deployed. The solar shade had solar and visible transmittance (VT) of 0.1, solar/visible
reflectance of 0.5, a thickness of 5 mm, and thermal conductivity of 0.1 W/m∙K. For switchable glazing,
the clear state was used as original glazing properties; for the dark state, both the SHGC and VT values
were switched to 0.1. Switchable glazing is available that can achieve VT much lower than 0.1 which is
useful for glare control, but analysis of such variations in shading device properties is not in the scope of
this study. The solar shade used two control strategies, and switchable glazing used a single control
strategy, as discussed in the following paragraphs.

Shade_solar: The interior solar shade was closed to cover the whole window when the incident solar
irradiation on the facade was higher than 150 W/m2, and the shade was kept open otherwise. The
threshold of 150 W/m2 was based on past studies in which the value correlated with the presence of direct
sunlight (Karlsen et al., 2016; Kunwar et al., 2019).

Shade_glare: The interior solar shade was closed to cover the whole window when the daylight glare
index (DGI) was higher than 22, and the shade was kept open otherwise. A brief description of DGI is
provided in section 2.3.

Switchable: Switchable glazing was used with solar-based control, which was switched to a dark state
whenever the incident solar irradiation on the facade was higher than 150 W/m2.

2.3 EVALUATION METRICS FOR NONENERGY BENEFITS

Besides reporting cooling, heating, and HVAC energy consumption, several metrics for nonenergy
benefits were used from the simulation results to evaluate daylighting, visual comfort, and thermal
comfort impacts of the window/shade properties. The metrics used are described in the following
paragraphs. The metrics were calculated assuming an occupancy period of 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. in the office
building.

Predicted mean vote (PMV): This scale is calculated based on the Fanger thermal comfort model
(Fanger 1970), in which the thermal sensation scale is based on energy loss and metabolic rate of an
occupant in given environmental conditions. The PMV value is calculated for each zone of the building at
center of the respective zone, but the results are presented only for a south-facing zone as a representative
case in this study. The occupant’s spatial location inside a zone is not considered. For example, distance
of an occupant from the window is not considered , hence, the results are more generic and might not
exactly reflect occupant comfort for a specific real-world scenario.

Useful daylight illuminance (UDI): This daylighting metric measures the percentage of occupied time a
specific indoor point receives, or falls between, useful natural light levels, generally defined as 100–2,000
lux. It identifies when daylight is sufficient, insufficient (too dim), or excessive (potential glare). The
daylight illuminance was divided into three bins: <300 lux, 300–2,000 lux, and >2,000 lux. The lower
limit of UDI was changed from 100 to 300 lux considering only autonomous UDI, without using the
supplemental UDI in a range of 100–300 lux. Results for UDI are provided for four perimeter zones
located with exterior windows in four cardinal directions. UDI is calculated for each zone based on
illuminance at two daylighting reference points approximately at 1.63 m and 3.26 m distance from the
windows, respectively, at a height of 0.76 m from the floor.

Daylight glare index (DGI): This metric calculates glare that can be caused by direct view of the bright
sky from the interior of a building (Hopkinson, 1972). The resulting DGI is presented here as a
percentage of time, DGI > 22, which will cause visual discomfort to the occupant (i.e., a lower value for
this metric indicates a lower probability of causing visual discomfort to the occupant). Results for DGI
are also provided for four perimeter zones based on DGI at two daylighting reference points.

 3. RESULTS

3.1 MEDIUM OFFICE BUILDING

3.1.1 Energy Consumption

The energy consumption results are provided for cooling, heating, and total HVAC applications for Pre-
1980 and IECC-2021 medium office buildings. The effects of window-to-wall ratio (WWR), SHGC, U-
value, and shading devices are discussed for three locations.

3.1.1.1    Pre-1980:

The annual cooling energy consumption expressed per unit floor area of the building (i.e., cooling energy
use intensity [EUI] for Pre-1980 medium office buildings) is presented in Figure 2. The figure shows that
cooling energy consumption is higher in the hotter weather, as expected. The cooling EUI was in the
range of 3.46‒7.18 kWh/ft2 at Tampa, 1.35‒4.17 kWh/ft2 at New York, and 0.61‒2.8 kWh/ft2 at
Rochester. The figure also shows that higher WWR results in higher cooling energy consumption. This
increase in energy consumption is more prominent with higher SHGC. The variation in cooling energy
seems to be higher with SHGC in comparison with that with U-value. Regarding daylight control effects,

the cooling energy consumption is reduced if lights use daylight-based control because of a reduction of
load from lights.

 Figure 2. Pre-1980 medium office building—annual cooling EUI.

The results for heating EUI are presented in Figure 3, which shows that heating energy is higher in colder
weather, as expected. The heating EUI was in the range of 0.03‒1.32 kBtu/ft2 at Tampa, 1.23‒19.27
kBtu/ft2 at New York, and 7.5‒43.87 kBtu/ft2 at Rochester. The heating EUI is affected by both SHGC
and U-value. The impact of U-value is represented by a gap between the lines of different colors, and the
impact of SHGC is represented by variation in heating energy in a single line at different points in the
x-axes. If lighting is dimmed using daylight control, the heating EUI increased by up to 2.63 kBtu/ft2.
This highest increase in heating EUI from lighting dimming controls was for case with highest U-value
and lowest SHGC.

Figure 2 and Figure 3 show opposite influences of daylight control and SHGC in cases of cooling and
heating. Thus, total HVAC EUI is calculated to evaluate the overall energy effect of different glazing
properties (Figure 4). The total HVAC also includes energy consumption from fans and pumps. The fan

progressing toward the colder weather, the effect of U-value in the overall HVAC EUI increases, and the

increases with an increase in SHGC; however, for windows with higher U-value, the increase in SHGC
reduces the overall HVAC energy consumption. As effect of lighting control, the total HVAC EUI
reduced in hot weather of Tampa with higher difference with increasing SHGC. This daylighting control
also increased the total HVAC EUI in cold weather of Rochester with higher difference at lower SHGC
and higher U-value.

Impact of shades: Figure 5 presents an example of the operation of shades for a couple of days with
different shading and controls, as described in Section 2.2. The figure shows that shade and switchable

The annual effect on total HVAC EUI from the use of solar shades and switchable glazing is shown in
Figure 8. The result is provided for three U-values for a building with a WWR of 45%. The results at
Tampa show that use of both shade and switchable glazing reduces total HVAC EUI for most

help offset the cost required to install high-performing windows and/or shading devices .

 4. DISCUSSION

As a reflection of the impact of window properties, the total HVAC EUI was in the range of 21.43–59.7
kBtu/ft2 in Pre-1980 medium office buildings, 8.84–38.35 kBtu/ft2 in IECC-2021 medium office
buildings, 26.06–107.13 kBtu/ft2 in Pre-1980 small office buildings, and 7.91–42.96 kBtu/ft2 in IECC-
2021 small office buildings. These ranges indicate that using higher-efficiency windows offers greater
energy savings in Pre-1980 buildings than in IECC-2021 buildings. This could be a result of higher
energy requirements for meeting the same load in older buildings with lower-efficiency HVAC systems.
Another observation was that small office buildings have a higher EUI consumption than that of medium
office buildings because of the effects of window properties; this difference is because of a higher
percentage of floor area in perimeter zones in small office buildings (~70%) versus that of medium office
buildings (~40%).

The impact of SHGC is highly dependent on building location, window size and orientation, with a
general trend showing SHGC as beneficial in colder weather and detrimental in hotter weather, which is
similar to the trends seen in Lai et al.’s research (Lai et al., 2017). However, if the windows are thermally
superior (i.e., for U-value of 0.1), Figure 11 and Figure 24 show that even in the cold weather of
Rochester, lower SHGC is better to reduce overall HVAC EUI. This means that if windows have high
thermal performance, energy savings can be achieved while controlling the solar gain (i.e., tuning the
solar gain for achieving better thermal/visual comfort by integrating dynamic components like automated
shading or smart glass to the windows). The total HVAC EUI for Rochester in Figure 24 was also lowest
for WWR of 70% when the U-value was 0.1 and SHGC was 0.2, thereby providing an opportunity to
increase WWR for daylight and a view to the outdoors without sacrificing energy performance. Once
windows have high thermal performance, then a higher WWR can be beneficial for energy performance,
as opposed to the typical occurrence of a lower WWR providing better energy performance in windows,
as described in Gigasari et al.’s research (Gigasari et al., 2025).

Based on this limited simulation study, the research team proposes the following preliminary
recommendations:

  Hot-humid weather (2A): Prioritize lower SHGC (and manage WWR) to lower cooling energy
 consumption, use daylight dimming to reduce internal lighting loads, and use shading to mitigate
 glare and excessive illuminance and improve thermal comfort.

  Mixed weather(4A): Lower both U-value and SHGC for overall HVAC energy consumption
 reduction, and select shading controls that mitigate glare and reduce solar gain for visual comfort.

  Cold weather(6A): Prioritize low U-values for heating reduction and comfort, consider moderate
 SHGC where passive gains are beneficial, avoid overly aggressive solar-based switchable control
 that blocks winter gains, and use glare-based shading as needed for visual comfort.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

This study used whole-building energy simulations to assess how facade and fenestration parameters—
WWR, U-value, SHGC, and shading devices affect energy use and occupant comfort in small and
medium office buildings across hot, mixed, and cold weather locations. Nonenergy metrics for
daylighting, glare, and thermal comfort were evaluated during typical office occupancy (8 a.m.–6 p.m.),
providing an integrated view of performance and trade-offs.

 34

Energy performance was strongly location-dependent. Cooling energy was most sensitive to SHGC,
especially in hotter locations, whereas heating energy depended more on U-value in colder locations.
Total HVAC energy reflected these opposing influences: in hot weather , higher SHGC typically
increased total HVAC EUI, whereas in cold weather, lower U-values consistently reduced total HVAC
EUI, and the effect of SHGC varied with U-value and WWR. Daylight dimming reduced cooling energy
by lowering internal gains but tended to increase heating energy in colder locations, underscoring
interaction effects between lighting controls and envelope properties.

Dynamic shading devices and controls delivered meaningful benefits with location and orientation
nuances. Switchable glazing reduced sensible cooling loads most in hot weather but increased heating in
winter by cutting passive solar gains; interior solar shades with the same solar threshold delivered smaller
cooling reductions because heat had already passed through the glazing although the specific performance
depends on the solar optical properties of the shade system. In mixed and cold weather, glare-based solar
shades provided energy savings in combination with higher SHGC, whereas switchable glazing
sometimes increased total energy by limiting beneficial winter solar heat gains. For visual comfort, solar
shades substantially reduced discomfort glare and improved UDI by reducing excessive illuminance,
particularly in the east, west, and south orientations.

Thermal comfort results (i.e. PMV) varied by building type, location, and envelope parameters. In
medium office buildings, lower WWR improved comfort. In hot weather, lower SHGC increased time in
the neutral PMV range, whereas in colder weather, lower U-values were most beneficial. Shading
generally reduced warm sensations and increased cool sensations, with switchable glazing producing the
largest shifts and glare-based shades performing similar to that of the baseline. In small office buildings,
decreasing U-value could enhance comfort in colder location by increasing neutral PMV time, whereas
higher SHGC reduced comfort in hot location and could enhance comfort in cold location, highlighting
the need to balance envelope heat transfer and solar heat gains by weather conditions.

Overall, high-performance facades should be tailored to location, orientation, and building type. In hot
locations, priorities should be lower SHGC, effective shading (including glare-based controls), and
daylight dimming to curb cooling loads and mitigate glare, recognizing potential heating trade-offs. In
cold locations, priorities should be lower U-values to reduce heating, consideration of higher SHGC
where passive solar gains are advantageous, and management of WWR to balance daylight and comfort.
For glare mitigation, recommendations include favoring interior solar shades and using switchable
glazing for energy and thermal impacts. Future work should employ advanced window models (e.g.,
layered systems) to better capture optical and comfort effects of switchable technologies and expand
technoeconomic analyses to support market transformation efforts in high-performance facades. Detailed
analysis of the effect of high-performance facades with appropriate control strategies on peak load and
HVAC sizing reduction could be performed for economic assessment of these technologies.
