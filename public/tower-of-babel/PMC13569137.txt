IrregulAB: An open‐source rule‐based engine for automated red cell antibody panel interpretation
Authors: Davide Crucitti, Violeta Vidal Ballester, Jesús Gómez Fernández, Eugenia Fernandez Mellid, Angie Lizeth Ruge Perdomo, Carlos Pérez Míguez, Adrián Mosquera Orgueira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterpretation of antibody identification panels remains a manual, expertise‐intensive step in transfusion testing; interpretive errors and long turnaround times persist even in well‐equipped hospitals. Existing commercial software is closed source and often omits enzyme‐phase, cold‐phase, and dosage‐sensitive exclusion rules, limiting third‐party verification and local adaptation.Study Design and MethodsWe developed IrregulAB, an open‐source (LGPL) web application that encodes serologic decision rules in two stages. First, a rule‐based screening stage applies LISS, enzyme, and cold rule‐out logic with safeguards for antigen dosage in heterozygotes to discard antibodies incompatible with the observed reaction pattern. Second, a minimal‐set solver exhaustively searches for the smallest antibody combination(s) that reproduce all assessable reactions and reports all equally plausible solutions. The system was evaluated on 108 development antibody‐identification panels and 35 held‐out temporal validation panels.ResultsThe screening stage retained the complete reference antibody set in 93% of development panels and 91% of held‐out temporal validation panels. The minimal‐set stage produced at least one valid explanatory antibody combination in 87% of development panels and 89% of held‐out temporal validation panels.ConclusionIrregulAB is a transparent, rule‐based decision‐support tool for trained immunohematology personnel; it is not an autonomous antibody‐identification or reporting system. By exposing every encoded decision rule, it enables independent verification, local tuning, and more standardized review of routine antibody workups under expert supervision. Its current performance was evaluated retrospectively in a single‐center Bio‐Rad gel‐card dataset; multicenter and multi‐platform validation is required before wide clinical implementation.Keywords: antibody identification, antibody panel interpretation, decision support, immunohematology, rule‐based algorithm, transfusion medicine

INTRODUCTION

Identifying irregular red‐cell antibodies is essential for safe transfusion. These antibodies, typically formed after transfusion or pregnancy (and occasionally spontaneously), can cause hemolytic transfusion reactions and hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn, especially when clinically significant specificities such as anti‐D, anti‐K, or anti‐Fya are involved. 1

Routine identification tests patient plasma against reagent red cells with known antigen profiles and interprets reactivity across phases (e.g., immediate spin, 37°C incubation, and the indirect antiglobulin test). Using exclusion logic, antibodies are ruled out when plasma fails to react with cells expressing the corresponding antigen, preferably in homozygous form to guard against dosage effects. Enzyme‐treated cells (papain/ficin) further refine interpretation by enhancing antigens such as Rh, Kidd, and Lewis while reducing or destroying M, N, S and Duffy; additional enhancers or cold testing can increase sensitivity. The task is to weigh these patterns—phase behavior, dosage, and phenotype—while distinguishing alloantibodies from autoantibodies or mixtures. While this method remains the gold standard, it is time‐consuming, cognitively demanding, and subject to interobserver variability. The need to analyze many complex panels makes computer‐assisted interpretation attractive for standardization and workload reduction. 2

Early efforts to apply computer technology to antibody identification date back to the 1980s. Smith et al. (1985) built RED, an expert system encoding rules to interpret panels. 3 AuBuchon et al. used computational techniques to optimize panel design from donor databases. 4

Later, expert‐system‐driven educational tools such as the Transfusion Medicine Tutor (TMT) were employed to teach laboratory students the logic of antibody identification, yielding measurable improvements in diagnostic reasoning. 5

From the 1990s into the 2000s, tools moved toward practice. Antigen Plus AbID (Rowny Systems) delivered computerized rule‐out with an antigen database, customizable panels, and reported reductions in analysis time with closer adherence to AABB policies. 6 More recent offerings include Antibody Check 7 (lightweight rule‐out for simple, single‐antibody cases; no enzyme or multi‐antibody logic), Resolvigen 6 (Tecnosoft/Ortho; rule‐in/out plus probability ranking with enzyme support for complex patterns), and Bio‐Rad IH‐AbID 8 (integrated with IH‐Com, gel‐card platforms, barcode/LIS integration). Across products, the aim is decision support, rather than replacing expert judgment. However, most remain closed source, limiting external audit and local adaptation of decision rules.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We conducted a retrospective methodological study using routine antibody‐identification panels performed at the Blood Bank of Hospital Clínico Universitario de Santiago. The goal was to develop and validate a fully transparent, rule‐based engine capable of interpreting standard three‐phase red‐cell panels (LISS, enzyme, cold) and proposing the minimal antibody set(s) explaining the observed reactions.

The study protocol involved (i) assembling and cleaning a panel dataset, (ii) formalizing serologic decision rules into a two‐stage algorithm, and (iii) evaluating performance on development and held‐out temporal validation cohorts.

Data collection and preparation

We assembled 154 antibody‐identification panels from the Blood Bank of Hospital Clínico Universitario de Santiago: 116 collected in 2024 for development and 38 collected March–June 2025 pre‐designated as a held‐out temporal validation set (same institution and reagent platform; held out during development).

Each Bio‐Rad panel comprises 11 reagent cells. For every case we transcribed agglutination grades under three conditions: LISS (37°C, low‐ionic‐strength saline), Enzyme (papain‐treated, 37°C), and Cold (4°C).

Exclusions

Eleven panels (7.1%) were removed because a prior investigation led staff to omit one or more required phases, leaving an incomplete work‐up. The final analytic dataset contained 143 panels: 108 for development and 35 for held‐out temporal validation.

Algorithm description

The analysis proceeds in two stages. Stage 1 (Antibody rule‐out) integrates LISS, enzyme, and cold results to eliminate antibodies whose expected reactivity contradicts the panel. Antigens never tested or present in every donor are removed up front. Zygosity is inferred from paired antigens: if one antigen is present and its partner absent, the expressed antigen is treated as homozygous; otherwise, it is considered potentially heterozygous and any negative in that row is “undecidable” and cannot be used to rule an antibody out. Results are processed in order—LISS, then enzyme, then cold, skipping any missing phase. In LISS, an antibody is discarded if its antigen appears in a clearly negative row that is not dosage uncertain. In the enzyme phase, the same logic is applied to enzyme‐resistant antigens; if a previously discarded antibody targets an enzyme‐potentiable antigen and the reaction reappears or exceeds LISS, the antibody is restored and flagged. Antibodies to enzyme‐destroyable antigens are flagged when reactivity disappears relative to LISS. The cold phase follows the same pattern for cold‐potentiable antibodies. Antibodies that survive stage 1 form the Retained Antibody List (RAL); flagged members indicate possible phase‐specific potentiation. For antigens that lack a paired marker in the panel (notably P1 and M on Bio‐Rad cards), zygosity cannot be inferred; negatives in those rows are treated as definitive and the corresponding antibody is discarded—a known source of false exclusions for variably expressed antigens. The algorithm also has no phase credibility assessment: if an entire phase is nonspecifically reactive, all reactions in that phase are weighted equally to those in other phases, which can lead to false candidate retention.

Stage 2 (Search for a minimal explanatory set) tests the retained antibodies in combinations of increasing size (k = 1, 2, …). For each candidate set, the algorithm simulates the panel with an OR rule: a reagent cell row is predicted positive if any antibody in the set targets an antigen present in that row; rows where all matching antigens are potentially heterozygous are treated as undecidable and ignored. For potentiable antibodies, enzyme or cold reactions must exceed the LISS grade unless LISS is already maximal. A set is accepted if its simulated pattern matches every assessable row. As soon as at least one solution is found, the search stops and all solutions of that minimal size are reported as sufficient antibody set(s) for the panel.

Evaluation metrics

Each panel yields two outputs, so we assess performance on two axes. Safety: the retained list must be a superset of the antibodies confirmed by manual serology (no true antibody is lost). Parsimony: at least one minimal explanatory set must be a subset of the reference and non‐empty when irregular antibodies are present. The reference antibody set was defined by routine expert immunohematology interpretation, incorporating the original serologic workup and available historical or follow‐up information when present.

Beyond these pass–fail checks, we cast the task as a 22‐label classification problem. For each panel, we compare the algorithm‐predicted antibody set with the reference across all 22 candidate specificities, form a 2 × 2 confusion matrix, and compute sensitivity (true‐antibody recall), specificity (true‐negative rate), and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), a balanced single‐number summary robust to class imbalance. Metrics are reported per panel, regardless of correctness status; proportions include 95% confidence intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples.

RESULTS

Eighteen panels differed from the expert reference. Most issues involved variable antigen expression—notably panels 22, 38, 75, 109, 114, 120 (anti‐P1) and 48, 51 (anti‐M). These dosage‐prone antigens can be weak or absent in heterozygous donors; the algorithm treated such negatives as incompatible and discarded the antibody, whereas the manual review anticipated variability and retained it.

A second group showed phase‐wide, non‐specific reactivity: panels 60 and 97 in the enzyme phase and panel 70 in the cold phase exhibited broad panagglutination. Human reviewers down‐weighted these noisy phases and confirmed a simpler LISS‐based explanation (e.g., anti‐C), while the algorithm over‐weighed the potentiated phase and retained multiple false candidates.

Two panels displayed atypical potentiation. In panel 72 an anti‐K appeared enzyme‐enhanced, and in panel 69 an anti‐e was cold‐enhanced—patterns that conflict with canonical rules, leading the algorithm to reject the true specificities that experts assigned.

In early‐emergence cases (panels 66 and 100, same patient), the manual record listed anti‐K only, but the algorithm identified K and C; subsequent testing confirmed new anti‐C, suggesting earlier detection than routine serology.

A single panel (138) showed an isolated weak inconsistency: a +/− reaction in a reagent cell negative for Fya. The algorithm therefore retained anti‐Fya, whereas the expert judged the trace as noise and explained the pattern with another specificity.

Finally, two panels (52 and 105) reflected failure to discard a low‐prevalence antigen (Cw). Partial pattern overlap with stronger reactivities (e.g., C, K) prevented algorithmic exclusion, but manual interpretation deemed Cw clinically irrelevant.

Clinically relevant error analysis

Of the 18 discrepant panels, 10 involved false exclusion of a reference antibody (the RAL missed a true specificity) and 6 involved false inclusion of surplus candidate specificities. Two panels (66 and 100, same patient) represented early detection rather than error: the algorithm proposed anti‐C before it appeared in the routine record, and subsequent testing confirmed its presence.

False exclusions carry the highest clinical risk because they could in principle lead to failure to select antigen‐negative units if IrregulAB were used without expert review. In this dataset, all 10 false exclusions were associated with either dosage‐prone or variably expressed antigens (anti‐P1, anti‐M) or atypical phase potentiation (anti‐K enzyme‐enhanced, anti‐e cold‐enhanced)—patterns that fall outside the current rule set. False inclusions increase interpretive workload and require expert adjudication but are less likely to compromise transfusion safety when the system is used as supervised decision support rather than autonomous reporting.

Sensitivity

The RAL distribution is heavily ceiling‐biased (mean 0.93; 95% CI 0.89–0.97). The SAS keeps that ceiling for most panels (mean 0.90; 95% CI 0.85–0.95). When sensitivity drops, it is usually a 1 → 0 transition: because most panels contain only one or two irregular antibodies, missing one collapses panel‐level sensitivity to 0.

Specificity

RAL specificity is high but scattered (mean 0.88; 95% CI 0.85–0.90) owing to tolerated surplus antibodies. After the SAS filter, the violin hugs the top line (mean 0.998; 95% CI 0.996–1.000): nearly every panel is free of false positives, and the average extra‐antibody rate falls from 74% to 3.5%.

Matthews correlation coefficient

MCC mirrors the specificity gain, rising from 0.53 (95% CI 0.48–0.59) with the RAL to 0.83 (95% CI 0.77–0.89) with the SAS—near‐perfect agreement once the minimal set is selected.

In short, stage 1 delivers safety (very high sensitivity with manageable specificity), while stage 2 turns that safety into parsimony by stripping away almost every remaining false lead. Panels with SAS sensitivity = 0 generally coincide with cases where the RAL missed a true antibody.

LIMITATIONS

This study has several important limitations. First, all panels were drawn retrospectively from a single institution and used a single manufacturer's reagent system. Enzyme behavior, antigen expression variability, and cold‐phase sensitivity differ between vendors and platforms, so performance may not generalize to other gel systems, tube LISS/PEG methods, or reference‐laboratory panels. Validation on multi‐center datasets with diverse reagents is needed before broader adoption.

The current rule set models only three phases—LISS, enzyme, and cold—and treats reaction grades in a simplified, largely binary way. It does not incorporate AHG‐IgG reactivity, DTT‐treated results, complement‐dependent patterns, or adsorption studies, nor does it model antigen dosage beyond heterozygous uncertainty. The algorithm cannot interpret warm autoantibodies, panagglutination, or mixed‐field agglutination, and it treats phase‐wide non‐specific reactivity at face value. These gaps contributed to several observed errors and restrict applicability in complex or autoimmune cases.

Zygosity inference from phenotype pairs works well for symmetrical antigen systems (e.g., C/c, E/e) but is brittle for single‐locus or variable antigens such as P1, M, and certain Lewis or Kell‐family markers. Because the Bio‐Rad panel does not provide a paired antigen for several dosage‐prone loci, weak or absent reactions were interpreted as true negatives, producing false exclusions. Broader rule sets or panel‐specific dosage models may be required for reliable handling of variable‐expression antigens.

Several included specificities were absent from the dataset (e.g., Fyb, N, Xga, s), preventing meaningful evaluation of those rules. Sensitivity and specificity estimates for these antibodies therefore remain unknown, and larger, more diverse datasets containing rare antibodies are necessary to assess the full scope of the algorithm.

Finally, this version of IrregulAB is intended strictly as a decision‐support tool. It neither replaces expert interpretation nor automates result reporting. Reaction grades must be transcribed manually, and the system relies on accurate input and appropriate clinical context. Prospective trials are needed to determine effects on workflow time, error rates, and consistency in real‐world practice, as well as to evaluate integration with laboratory information systems.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that a compact, transparent rule‐based engine can interpret routine red‐cell antibody‐identification panels with high safety and strong parsimony. Across 143 Bio‐Rad panels, the stage 1 retained‐antibody list (RAL) included all reference antibodies in over 90% of cases, and stage 2 distilled the explanation to a minimal SAS in roughly 9 out of 10 panels. These findings suggest that a deterministic, explicitly encoded algorithm can reproduce most manual interpretations when rules are carefully structured. The two‐stage architecture provides a practical compromise: stage 1 favors sensitivity and preserves all plausible candidates, while stage 2 restores specificity by selecting the smallest set compatible with the observed reactions.

A notable outcome is the algorithm's ability to surface inconsistencies in historical panel records. Several discordant cases were traced to transcription or documentation errors on retrospective review. Although IrregulAB cannot replace expert analysis, this consistency‐checking behavior is clinically useful: laboratories frequently revisit complex panels for QA, audits, or training, and automated rule‐checking can highlight panels that require closer scrutiny.

The observed limitations reveal important characteristics of both the algorithm and routine immunohematology practice. The dominant failure mode involved dosage‐prone or variably expressed antigens—particularly P1 and M—where the panel design does not provide a paired antigen to infer zygosity. In such cases, human reviewers often allow for weak reactions in heterozygous donors, while the algorithm interprets non‐reactive rows as contradictory and discards genuine specificities. Future versions will allow laboratories to designate specific antigens—such as P1, M, Lewis, and selected Kell‐family markers—as variable‐expression targets. For these loci, a single nonreactive cell in an antigen‐positive donor will trigger an expert adjudication flag rather than serve as grounds for antibody exclusion; exclusion will require either multiple independent negative cells or confirmed homozygous‐equivalent antigen expression.

A second class of discrepancies arose from phase‐wide non‐specific reactivity. Human reviewers routinely down‐weight noisy phases, whereas IrregulAB currently treats each phase as equally informative. A planned phase credibility flag will be triggered when reactivity is present across a high proportion of reagent cells without a discriminating antigen pattern. In such cases, the algorithm will suppress definitive exclusion decisions derived from that phase alone and alert the user that the phase may be nonspecific and requires manual interpretation before further conclusions are drawn.

Two cases showed atypical potentiation (enzyme‐enhanced anti‐K; cold‐enhanced anti‐e), leading the algorithm to reject the correct antibodies. Although these patterns are unusual, they are not unprecedented, and automated systems may need to accommodate such exceptions through configurable rule overrides or heuristics rather than strict rule enforcement.

Beyond failure modes, the dataset itself shaped performance. Several specificities were absent entirely, preventing fair evaluation. More importantly, all panels originated from a single institution, used a single manufacturer's reagent system, and reflected local testing practices. These constraints likely underestimate real‐world variability. Multicenter studies across reagent vendors, methodologies (gel, tube LISS, PEG, AHG cards), and antibody profiles will be essential to assess generalizability.

Despite these caveats, IrregulAB shows that transparent, rule‐driven interpretation can meaningfully support antibody identification. Its open‐source nature is important: current commercial tools provide decision support but do not permit inspection or local adaptation of embedded rules. By exposing every decision, IrregulAB allows laboratories to audit, tune, or extend the logic to reflect local reagent behavior, serologic conventions, or regulatory requirements. This transparency aligns with modern expectations for explainable clinical software and facilitates educational use for trainees learning exclusion logic.

Clinically, IrregulAB is best understood as a decision‐support and quality‐assurance tool. It may support more efficient review, standardize application of exclusion rules, and flag inconsistent findings, but it does not replace expert judgment or interpret complex autoimmune patterns or atypical reaction mechanisms.

Although IrregulAB is open source, open availability does not imply readiness for direct clinical reporting. In FDA‐licensed or otherwise regulated transfusion‐service environments, adoption of this software for result reporting would require prospective local validation before implementation. Such validation should verify performance against the laboratory's own antibody panels, reagent lots, testing platform, grading conventions, user workflows, and laboratory information system interfaces, with predefined acceptance criteria covering common antibodies, clinically significant combinations, dosage‐sensitive specificities, nonspecific reactivity, negative panels, and discrepant or incomplete workups. Any subsequent local modification—of decision rules, source code, antibody catalog, reagent‐cell profiles, user interface, or reporting logic—should trigger documented change control, regression testing, and user acceptance testing before release into clinical use. IrregulAB should therefore be considered a transparent framework for decision support and quality audit, not a cleared or validated medical device for autonomous antibody identification.

In summary, IrregulAB provides an interpretable, openly accessible framework for computer‐assisted analysis of routine antibody‐identification panels. Its performance in retrospective testing suggests that rule‐based systems can offer significant practical value in transfusion services, especially when complemented by expert review. Expanding rule coverage, improving robustness to noisy phases, and validating across broader serologic contexts will be important steps toward enabling reproducible, computer‐assisted serologic practice on a larger scale.

Web application

IrregulAB includes a browser‐based front end that allows trained users to test the algorithm without local installation in research, validation, quality assurance, or educational settings.

On load, the user selects the red‐cell panel lot to analyze from a built‐in catalog. If a lot is missing, the user can upload the manufacturer's PDF datasheet or build the panel in an on‐screen spreadsheet. PDF import currently supports Bio‐Rad panels.

For each reagent cell row, the form provides three fields—LISS, Enzyme, and Cold—for entering reaction grades on a 0–4 scale. After running the analysis, the app returns: (i) the Retained Antibody List (RAL), (ii) antibodies flagged for potentiation or enzyme destruction, and (iii) the minimal Sufficient Antibody Set(s) (SAS) that reproduce the observed pattern. None of the data entered in the webpage is retained or logged by the system.

CONCLUSIONS

IrregulAB demonstrates the feasibility of transparent, rule‐based computer‐assisted review of routine antibody‐identification panels. In a retrospective single‐center dataset comprising a development cohort (n = 108) and a held‐out temporal validation cohort (n = 35), the system preserved all reference antibodies in over 90% of panels and, in roughly 9 out of 10 panels, narrowed the explanation to a single minimal antibody set. The system also flagged several documentation inconsistencies in historical records, supporting its role as a quality‐assurance aid.

IrregulAB is released under LGPL‐3.0‐or‐later and is publicly hosted at https://irregulab.codigorojo.tech; the source code is publicly available at https://github.com/davide-grheco/IrregulAB, enabling integration with proprietary LIS while requiring that distributed modifications to IrregulAB remain open.

Clinically important limitations remain, particularly for dosage‐sensitive or variably expressed antigens and phase‐wide nonspecific reactivity. IrregulAB should therefore be used only as supervised decision support by trained immunohematology personnel, following local validation with predefined acceptance criteria and documented change control after any software or rule modification. Multicenter, multi‐vendor, and prospective workflow studies are required before broader clinical adoption.
