Asymptotic Formula for ( t + 1 ) (t+1) -Regular Partitions
JAYANTA BARMAN, Kamalakshya Mahatab

Abstract
. A partition is t t -regular if none of its parts is divisible by t t . Let p ⁡ ( N , t ) p(N,t) be the number of ( t + 1 ) (t+1) -regular partitions of a positive integer N N . In 1971, Hagis proved an asymptotic formula for p ⁡ ( N , t ) p(N,t) using the circle method, when t t fixed. In this article, we use the saddle point method and extend the result of Hagis in different ranges of t t , obtaining explicit bounds. We also discuss an application of our result to estimate zeros in the character table of the symmetric group.

1.
Introduction

A partition of a positive integer
N
is a tuple
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
of positive integers satisfying
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{\ell}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{i}=N
. Let
p(N)
be the number of such partitions. Although the definition of a partition is straightforward, estimating its growth requires complex techniques. A breakthrough in this study was achieved in 1918 by Hardy and Ramanujan
[
9
]
, who established an asymptotic formula for
p(N)
using the circle method. This result was later refined in 1938 by Rademacher
[
13
]
, leading to the following asymptotic expression:

(1.1)

p(N)=\frac{1}{4N\sqrt{3}}\exp\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{N}\right)\left(1+O\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right).

Similar to unrestricted partitions, many important arithmetic and combinatorial questions also arise from restricted partition functions. One classical example is that of

t
-regular partitions:
a partition
\lambda
of
N
is called
t
-regular if none of its parts is divisible by
t
. Let
p(N,t-1)
denote the number of
t
-regular partitions of
N
. A classical result of Glaisher
[
6
]
states that the number of
(t+1)
-regular partitions of
N
is equal to the number of partitions of
N
in which no part appears more than
t
times. These partitions have been studied extensively and are related to many problems in combinatorics and number theory
[
4
,
11
,
12
]
. Several properties of
p(N,t)
, including arithmetic properties, multiplicative properties, and monotonicity, have been studied in
[
2
,
3
,
10
,
14
,
15
]
.

Although restricted partition functions such as
p(N,t)
were traditionally studied using combinatorial methods, Hagis
[
7
,
8
]
was the first to derive a Rademacher-type
[
13
]
explicit formula for
p(N,t)
with fixed
t\leq N
, using the circle method.
In this article, we use the saddle point method to estimate
p(N,t)
, which refines Hagis’ bound. Below, we recall the generating series and the integral formula for
p(N,t)
, which will be needed to apply the saddle point method.

Let
q=\exp(2\pi iz)
, where
z=x+iy
,
y>0
. Recall that the Dedekind eta function
\eta(z)
is defined by

(1.2)

\eta(z)=\exp\left(\frac{\pi iz}{12}\right)\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-\exp(2\pi inz)).

Following
[
16
]
, we introduce the functions

(1.3)

\mu_{k}(z)=-\frac{z^{k+1}}{2\pi i}\left(\frac{d}{dz}\right)^{k}\log\eta(z),\quad\text{for $k\geq 1$}.

The generating function for
p(N)

[
5
]
is given by

F(q)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})^{-1}=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}p(N)q^{N}=q^{\frac{1}{24}}\frac{1}{\eta(z)}.

Similarly, the generating function for
p(N,t)
(see
[
8
, (2.2)]
) is

G(q,t)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1+q^{n}+q^{2n}+\cdots+q^{tn})=\frac{F(q)}{F(q^{t+1})}=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}p(N,t)q^{N}.

By the Cauchy integral formula, the coefficient of
q^{N}
in the above power series can be expressed as

p(N,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{G(q,t)}{q^{N+1}}dq,

where
C
is a simple, positively oriented closed contour around the origin and lies entirely inside the unit circle. Let
C=\{z:q=\exp(2\pi iz)\}
. The requirement
|q|<1
is equivalent to the condition
y>0
. Specifically, for a fixed
y
, as
x
ranges over any interval of length
1
, the variable
q
traces a complete circular contour of radius
e^{-2\pi y}
. Hence, it follows that

(1.4)

p(N,t)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\exp\left(-2\pi izL\right)g_{t}(z)dx,

where

(1.5)

\displaystyle g_{t}(z)=\frac{\eta((t+1)z)}{\eta(z)}\quad\text{and }L=N+\frac{t}{24}.

The above integral in (
1.4
) will be used in our saddle point analysis. We may note that the right-hand side of (
1.4
) depends on
y
, while the left-hand side is independent of
y
. This suggests that we optimize
y
, which may lead to an asymptotic formula for
p(N,t)
.

1.1.
Saddle point method

Below is an outline of the saddle point method applied to a general sequence.

Let
b(N)
be a sequence of positive numbers for all
N\geq 0
, with
b(0)=1
. Let the generating function associated with
b(N)
be defined by

\displaystyle g(z)=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}b(N)\exp(2\pi iNz).

So, we can write
b(N)
as

(1.6)

\displaystyle b(N)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\exp(-2\pi iNz)g(z)dx,

where
g(z)
is assumed to have no zeros in the upper half plane. Let

\displaystyle a(z)=-2\pi iNz+\log g(z).

The saddle point
y
is obtained by solving
a^{\prime}(iy)=0
. Note that the Taylor expansion of
a(z)
about
iy
is given by

\displaystyle a(z)

\displaystyle=a(iy)+\frac{x^{2}}{2!}a^{\prime\prime}(iy)+\frac{x^{3}}{3!}a^{\prime\prime\prime}(iy)+\frac{x^{4}}{4!}a^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(x^{\prime}+iy)

(1.7)

\displaystyle=a(iy)+\frac{x^{2}}{2}a^{\prime\prime}(iy)\left(1+\frac{x}{3}\frac{a^{\prime\prime\prime}(iy)}{a^{\prime\prime}(iy)}+\frac{x^{2}}{12}\frac{a^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(x^{\prime}+iy)}{a^{\prime\prime}(iy)}\right),

where
x^{\prime}
lies between
0
and
x.

Consider a function of
y
,
e(y)<R
, where
R
is the radius of convergence of the Taylor series in (
1.1
). With
e(y)
, we divide the range of integration in (
1.6
) into two parts:

\displaystyle|x|<e(y)\quad\text{and}\quad e(y)\leq|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}.

In the first region, we use the Taylor expansion of
a(z)
to obtain the leading term. The contribution from the second region is absorbed into the error term. We may note

\displaystyle\int_{|x|<e(y)}\exp(a(z))dx=\exp(a(iy))\int_{|x|<e(y)}\exp\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2!}a^{\prime\prime}(iy)\left(1+\frac{x}{3}\frac{a^{\prime\prime\prime}(iy)}{a^{\prime\prime}(iy)}+\frac{x^{2}}{12}\frac{a^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(x^{\prime}+iy)}{a^{\prime\prime}(iy)}\right)\right)dx.

Here, we bound
a^{\prime\prime\prime}(iy)
and
a^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(iy)
in terms of
a^{\prime\prime}(iy)
, which further contribute to the error term. Now, if we can show that
a^{\prime\prime}(iy)<0
, then the remaining integral reduces to a Gaussian integral that can be evaluated easily.

We now state our main theorem, that gives an asymptotic formula for
p(N,t)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
N
be a positive integer and
t\geq 4
. Then

(
i
)

there exists a unique solution
y>0
satisfying

(1.8)

\frac{\mu_{1}((t+1)iy)-(t+1)\mu_{1}(iy)}{(t+1)y^{2}}=N+\frac{t}{24}.

Furthermore,
y
lies in the range

(1.9)

\sqrt{\frac{t}{24(t+1)\left(N+\frac{t}{24}\right)}}<y<\frac{1}{\sqrt{24N-1}}.

(ii)

Corresponding to the above value of
y
,

\displaystyle p(N,t)=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{t+1}y^{\frac{3}{2}}\exp\left(2\pi y\left(N+\frac{t}{24}\right)\right)\;\eta((t+1)iy)}{\sqrt{(t+1)\mu_{2}(iy)-\mu_{2}((t+1)iy)}\,\,\eta(iy)}\left(1+O(y)\right).

However, the above formula is difficult to use in various applications directly, as
y
is not explicit. In the next theorem, we solve this problem and obtain explicit expressions of
p(N,t)
in different ranges of
t
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let N be a positive integer with
t+1\leq N
. Then the following asymptotic formulas hold for
p(N,t)
:

(i)

If
(t+1)\leq\frac{2\pi}{(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon)\log\left(N+\frac{t}{24}\right)}\sqrt{\frac{24(t+1)\left(N+\frac{t}{24}\right)}{t}}
and
\epsilon>0
, then

p(N,t)=\frac{\sqrt{12}A_{t}(N)\exp\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{\frac{t}{t+1}\left(N+\frac{t}{24}\right)}\right)}{\exp\left(\rho_{1}\exp\left(-2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(24N+t)}{t(t+1)}}+O\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)\right)}\left(1+O\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right),

where
1<\rho_{1}<1.00873
and

\displaystyle A_{t}(N)=t^{\frac{1}{4}}\left((t+1)(24N+t)\right)^{-\frac{3}{4}}.

(
ii
)

If
\frac{2\pi}{(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon)\log\left(N+\frac{t}{24}\right)}\sqrt{\frac{24(t+1)\left(N+\frac{t}{24}\right)}{t}}<t+1\leq\sqrt{24N}
, where
A_{t}(N)
is defined in
(i)
, then

\displaystyle p(N,t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{12}(2t+1)^{\frac{3}{4}}\exp\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{\frac{2t+1}{2t+2}\left(N+\frac{t}{24}\right)}\right)}{\sqrt{t-1}\left((24N+t)(2t+2)\right)^{\frac{3}{4}}\exp\left(\exp\left(-2\pi\sqrt{\frac{24N+t}{t(t+1)}}\right)\right)}\left(1+O\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right).

(
iii
)

If
(t+1)>\sqrt{24N}
, then

p(N,t)=p(N)\exp\left(-\nu_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{\pi(t+1)}{\sqrt{6N}}\left(1+O\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)\right)\right)\left(1+O\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right),

where
1<\nu_{1}<1.00873
.

For
t>\sqrt{24N}
, the asymptotic behavior of
p(N,t)
is relatively straightforward and closely approximates
p(N)
. However, the case where
t\leq\sqrt{24N}
exhibits more intricate behavior due to deeper links with the modular and analytic properties of
g_{t}(z)
.

The following theorem is a special case of part
(i)
of Theorem
1.2
, and provides an asymptotic formula for
p(N,t)
when
t
is fixed, and coincides with Hagis’ asymptotic formula
[
8
]
.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
8
, Corollary 4.2]
)
.

For fixed
t
,
4\leq t\leq N
, we have

p(N,t)=\sqrt{12}t^{\frac{1}{4}}((t+1)(24N+t))^{-\frac{3}{4}}\exp\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{\frac{t}{t+1}\left(N+\frac{t}{24}\right)}\right)\left(1+O\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right),

as
N\to\infty.

Using the above asymptotic result, McSpirit and Ono
[
12
]
established several results concerning zeros in the character tables of the symmetric groups
S_{N}
for fixed
t
. Below, we discuss an application of Theorem
1.2
to estimate the number of zeros in the character table of the symmetric group. This improves our result in Theorem 1.3 of
[
1
]
.

1.2.
Application to Zeros in the character table of the symmetric group

Given two partitions
\lambda
and
\mu
of a positive integer
N
. Let
\chi_{\lambda}(\mu)
be the value of the irreducible character of the symmetric group
S_{N}
labeled by
\lambda
, evaluated at the conjugacy class corresponding to
\mu
. As in
[
12
]
, we define

Z_{t}(N):=\#\{(\lambda,\mu):\chi_{\lambda}(\mu)=0\;\text{with $\lambda$ being a $t$-core}\}.

In the following theorem, we generalize the bounds of McSpirit and Ono
[
12
]
for
Z_{t}(N)
for arbitrary
t
.
The proof relies on Theorem
1.2
, together with existing bounds for the number of
t
-core partitions
c_{t}(N)
, established in
[
16
,
17
]
and subsequently simplified in
[
1
]
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
N\geq 100
be a large positive integer, and let
t\leq N
. Let
A_{t}(N)
be defined as in Theorem
1.2
and
1<\rho_{1},\nu_{1}<1.00873
. Then the following statements hold:

(
i
)

For all
0<\epsilon<1
, if
6\leq t\leq\frac{2\pi\sqrt{2N}}{\sqrt{(1+\epsilon)\log N}}
, then

Z_{t}(N)\geq c_{t}(N)(p(N)-p(N,t-1)),

where

c_{t}(N)=\frac{(4\pi e)^{\frac{t-1}{2}}(t-1)}{\sqrt{4\pi}(t^{2}-t)^{\frac{t}{2}}}\left(N+\frac{t^{2}-1}{24}\right)^{\frac{t-3}{2}}(1+O(t^{-\epsilon}))

and

p(N,t-1)=\frac{\sqrt{12}A_{t-1}(N)\exp\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{\frac{t-1}{t}\left(N+\frac{t-1}{24}\right)}\right)}{\exp\left(\rho_{1}\exp\left(-2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\left(24N+t-1\right)}{t(t-1)}}+O\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)\right)}\left(1+O\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right).

(
ii
)

For

\frac{2\pi\sqrt{2N}}{\sqrt{\log N}}<t<f(N)
, where
f(N)\sim\frac{\sqrt{24N}}{\sqrt{\frac{6}{\pi}-1}}
, we have

Z_{t}(N)\geq c_{t}(N)(p(N)-p(N,t-1)),

where

c_{t}(N)\geq\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}\exp\left(\frac{t}{2}-1.00873te^{-2\pi}\right)\left(\frac{\pi}{6}(24N+t^{2}-1)\right)^{\frac{t-3}{2}}}{t^{t-1}}\left(1+O\left(t^{-1}\right)\right)

and

p(N,t-1)\leq\frac{\sqrt{12}(2t-1)^{\frac{3}{4}}\exp\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{\frac{2t-1}{2t}\left(N+\frac{t-1}{24}\right)}\right)}{\sqrt{t-2}\left((24N+t-1)2t\right)^{\frac{3}{4}}\exp\left(\exp\left(-2\pi\sqrt{\frac{24N+t-1}{t(t-1)}}\right)\right)}\left(1+O\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right).

(
iii
)

For
t\geq f(N)
, where
f(N)
is defined in part (ii), we have

\displaystyle Z_{t}(N)\geq p(N)^{2}\exp\left(-1.00873t\exp\left(-\frac{t(t-1)}{2\left(N+\frac{t^{2}-1}{24}\right)}\right)\right)\left(1-\exp\left(-\nu_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{\pi t}{\sqrt{6N}}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(1+O\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right).

The following corollary is a result of McSpirit and Ono
[
12
]
and follows immediately from
{(i)}
of Theorem
1.4
.

Corollary 1.5

(
[
12
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
t\geq 5
be a fixed positive integer. Then, as
N\to\infty
, we have

Z_{t}(N)\sim c_{t}(N)p(N)\gg_{t}N^{\frac{t-5}{2}}\exp\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{N}\right).

We can extend the range of
t
in the above formula to

t\ll N^{\frac{1}{2}-\delta}
for any
\delta>0
.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
t\ll N^{\frac{1}{2}-\delta}
be any positive integer, and
\delta>0
. Then

Z_{t}(N)\sim c_{t}(N)p(N).

Proof.

From
(i)
of Theorem
1.2
, one can verify that

A_{t-1}(N)=O\left(\,t^{-\frac{1}{2}}N^{-\tfrac{3}{4}}\right).

From the bionomial expansion,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{t-1}{t}\left(N+\frac{t-1}{24}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N}-\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2t}-\frac{(t-1)^{2}}{48t\sqrt{N}}\right)-\frac{\sqrt{N}}{8}\left(\frac{1}{t}-\frac{(t-1)^{2}}{24tN}\right)^{2}-\cdots.

Taking
t\ll N^{\frac{1}{2}-\delta}
, we get

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{\frac{t-1}{t}\left(N+\frac{t-1}{24}\right)}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{N}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\pi\sqrt{N}}{t\sqrt{6}}\left(1+O\left(N^{-2\delta}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\exp\left(\rho_{1}\exp\left(-2\pi\frac{\sqrt{24N+t-1}}{\sqrt{t(t-1)}}+O\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)\right)=1+O\left(\exp\left(-N^{\delta}\right)\right).

Substituting the above estimates in
(i)
of Theorem
1.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle p(N)-p(N,t-1)

\displaystyle=p(N)\left(1-\frac{p(N,t-1)}{p(N)}\right)

\displaystyle=p(N)\left(1+O\left(N^{\frac{1}{4}}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\pi\sqrt{N}}{t\sqrt{6}}\left(1+O\left(N^{-2\delta}\right)\right)\right)\right)\right).

Therefore,

c_{t}(N)\left(p(N)-p(N,t-1)\right)\leq Z_{t}(N)\leq c_{t}(N)p(N),

which implies

Z_{t}(N)\sim c_{t}(N)p(N).

∎

1.3.
Sketch of the proof of Theorem
1.1

The proof starts by partitioning the range of the integration in (
1.4
) into two distinct regions:

\displaystyle|x|<\frac{y}{3}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{y}{3}\leq|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}.

So, we can write equation (
1.4
) as

(1.10)

\displaystyle p(N,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{-y/3}^{y/3}\exp\left(-2\pi izL\right)g_{t}(z)dx+\int_{\frac{y}{3}\leq|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-2\pi izL\right)g_{t}(z)dx

\displaystyle=\exp(2\pi Ly)g_{t}(iy)\int_{-y/3}^{y/3}\exp\left(-2\pi iLx+2\pi i\frac{1}{2\pi i}\log\frac{g_{t}(z)}{g_{t}(iy)}\right)dx

\displaystyle+\exp(2\pi Ly)g_{t}(iy)\int_{\frac{y}{3}\leq|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-2\pi iLx\right)\frac{g_{t}(z)}{g_{t}(iy)}dx.

The Taylor expansion of
\log g_{t}(z)
around
x=0
is given by

(1.11)

\displaystyle\log\frac{g_{t}(z)}{g_{t}(iy)}

\displaystyle=\left(x\frac{d}{dz}\log g_{t}(z)+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}\log g_{t}(z)+\cdots\right)_{z=iy}.

Using
g_{t}(z)
from (
1.5
) and
\mu_{k}(z)
from (
1.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d}{dz}\right)^{k}\log g_{t}(z)=\frac{2\pi i}{(t+1)z^{k+1}}((t+1)\mu_{k}(z)-\mu_{k}((t+1)z)).

From (
1.11
) and the above, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\log\frac{g_{t}(z)}{g_{t}(iy)}

\displaystyle=x\frac{(t+1)\mu_{1}(iy)-\mu_{1}((t+1)iy)}{(t+1)(iy)^{2}}+\frac{x^{2}}{2!}\frac{(t+1)\mu_{2}(iy)-\mu_{2}((t+1)iy)}{(t+1)(iy)^{3}}+\cdots

(1.12)

\displaystyle=x\frac{(t+1)\mu_{1}(iy)-\mu_{1}((t+1)iy)}{(t+1)(iy)^{2}}+\frac{x^{2}}{2!}\frac{(t+1)\mu_{2}(iy)-\mu_{2}((t+1)iy)}{(t+1)(iy)^{3}}

\displaystyle+\frac{x^{3}}{3!}\frac{(t+1)\mu_{3}(iy)-\mu_{3}((t+1)iy)}{(t+1)(iy)^{4}}+\frac{x^{4}}{4!}\frac{(t+1)\mu_{4}(z^{\prime})-\mu_{4}((t+1)z^{\prime})}{(t+1)(z^{\prime})^{5}},

for some
z^{\prime}=x^{\prime}+iy
and
x^{\prime}
is between
0
and
x
.
In Theorem
3.10
, we demonstrate that the first integral of (
1.10
) provides the dominant contribution to the main term. We solve the saddle point
y
from the first order term of the Taylor series (
1.3
) by assuming
\frac{d}{dz}\log g_{t}(z)=2\pi iL
. The second order term gives the Gaussian integral contributing to the main term. The third and fourth-order terms are bounded by the second-order term and absorbed in the error.

In contrast, the second integral of (
1.10
) contributes only to the error term. To control this error, we establish Proposition
3.1
. This is the lengthiest part of our proof as the Taylor expansion of

is not valid in the region

. In this region, we require ‘good’ lower bound for
|\eta(z)|
when

is small. This problem is solved by translating

by
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and then using the functional equation of

.

3.
Preliminaries

In this section, we establish several auxiliary results that will be used in the proofs of our main results. The following proposition is used to tackle the integration of (
1.10
) in the region
\frac{y}{3}\leq|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Since the Taylor expansion is not valid in this region, we instead derive suitable bounds for this contribution.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
\frac{1}{y}\geq 1000
, then

\int_{\frac{y}{3}\leq|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}}\left|\frac{g_{t}(z)}{g_{t}(iy)}\right|dx<2\exp\left(-\frac{\pi}{150y}\right).

Note that the group
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
acts on the upper half–plane

\mathbb{H}=\{\,z\mid\operatorname{Im}z>0\,\}
through the Möbius
transformations, given by

\gamma z=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\quad\text{for}\quad\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}).

As shown in
[
16
]
, the Dedekind eta function (
1.2
) obeys the following transformation identities

(3.1)

\displaystyle\eta(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-iz}}\eta\left(\frac{-1}{z}\right)\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle|\eta(z)|

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{y}{\text{Im}\gamma z}\right)^{-\frac{1}{4}}|\eta(\gamma z)|\quad\text{for all }\quad\gamma\in\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}).

Next, we state three lemmas that are needed to prove Proposition
3.1
.
The following lemma is used to bound
\eta(z)
.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
16
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Suppose that
x\in\mathbb{R}
. For every
y\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
, there exists a
\theta
with

such that

\displaystyle|\eta(z)|=\exp\left(-\frac{\pi y}{12}+\theta 1.01e^{-2\pi y}\right).

To estimate
\eta(iy)
when
y
is large, we apply the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
1
, Lemma 2.3]
)
.

For every
y\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
, one can find a number
\nu
satisfying
1<\nu<1.00873
such that

\eta(iy)=\exp\left(-\frac{\pi y}{12}-\nu e^{-2\pi y}\right).

When

\eta(z)
, as described below.

Lemma 3.4

(
[
1
, Lemma 2.4]
)
