Abstract
In this project, we developed a set of high-fidelity modeling and optimization algorithms to enable
conversion of individual homes into grid-integrated buildings, and a community of homes into a virtual
power plant (VPP).

The developed computational algorithms include a thermal model for homes with a central forced-air
HVAC system that uses a gas furnace for heating and vapor-compression cycle for cooling (typical for a
significant fraction of US homes), a PV generation predictive model, and probabilistic predictive models
for electric water heating and electric vehicle (EV) charging. These models automatically train (calibrate)
themselves using historical data (disaggregated AC power, communicating thermostats, and weather) and
provide superior predictive accuracy and over longer time horizons than the state-of-the-art models.

The computational complexity of the developed set of optimization algorithms scales linearly with regard
the number of households and controllable devices whereas the state-of-the-art MPC algorithms typically
have quadratic computational complexity. This overcomes a severe drawback of the state-of-the art, which
limits the number of controllable devices and/or increases the computational time step, thus negatively
affecting prediction accuracy and solution optimality. In addition, the developed algorithms can optimize
to multiple objects, e.g., optimizing the load shape of the community of homes while simultaneously
minimizing electricity costs for individual homes subject to a time-of-use (TOU) tariff.

We also developed and preliminary tested a technology to perform FDD of heat pumps for Sense customers.
The developed technique is unique to Sense and leverages on Sense’s core competence in disaggregation.
When scaled up, the developed FDD could save up to thousands of dollars for heat pump owners.

Our modeling and optimization technology was developed using data from existing Sense customers. We
validated the technology over the course of a Pilot with newly recruited Sense customers. Special data
platforms (ReTool and Enode) enabled data collection and control communications during the Pilot events,
although this process is not yet fully automated. Lessons learned over the Pilot imply that EV charging is
not a significant source of DR currently, due to a high dropout rate. On the other hand, we showed that
availability of EV charging for DR dramatically enhances the load shaping potential, calling for better
incentives to EV owners to participate.

The detailed power consumption information provided by Sense’s disaggregation technology is a
cornerstone of the developed computational tools, putting the combined disaggregation/grid
services/energy cost minimization offering as a unique value proposition in DR market.

1. Introduction
Until recently, most buildings had a passive role within the electrical grid in the US, consuming around
75% of electricity and driving daily peak power demand [1] . However, many electrical loads in buildings
can be flexible and, if controlled in a coordinated way to draw electricity at specific times and at different
levels, could potentially be used to increase the flexibility of demand. In this scenario, the buildings become
interactive elements of the grid [1].

Currently, building grid services (e.g., peak demand reduction) are still limited as they are usually called
infrequently and over short periods of time (typically during extreme weather), by a relatively small fraction
of grid customers. Expanding the use and flexibility of these services in the near future could help improve
grid stability and reduce costs due to factors including the growth of electric demand and intermittent
renewable electricity generation, and more frequent extreme weather [2].

Several theoretical studies and field pilots researched the potential for continuous building grid services.
For example, in the SunDial project, an integrated system was designed, developed and demonstrated. The
system included three commercial and industrial buildings (~3.8MW, peak), a 1.5 MW solar PV farm, and
a 0.5MW/1.0 MWh battery energy storage system (ESS), with a supervisory control system that optimally
managed facility loads and ESS charging and discharging to mitigate potential problems from PV

intermittency and power backfeed, as well as large ramps in grid power demand [3]. A community of
residential homes was considered by Jin et al. in NREL’s Foresee project [4]. That study included several
NREL model “smart homes” equipped with sensors to submeter power consumption on selected appliances
(e.g., HVAC and DHW) and performed model-predictive control (MPC) using simulations. Again, an ESS
was a vital component of the overall concept.

Battery energy storage is still expensive and could be inconvenient to homeowners/ occupants nowadays
[5]. Many existing homes without an ESS could be a significant grid resource, but currently there are several
barriers to convert existing homes into grid-integrated buildings and perform MPC-based of optimal load
scheduling.

First, unlike in NREL’s “smart homes”, acquiring data for individual loads (i.e., appliances) in existing
homes typically implies installation of sensors to submeter these loads, which is inconvenient, costly, and
sometimes disruptive to the homeowners. Installing new connected devices is costly unless appliances are
already at end-of-life. Electricity consumption data are required to model the appliance power consumption
and its flexibility.

Second, for HVAC loads in MPC applications, the underlying building thermal model (e.g., first order
greybox model as in [4]) and its calibration methods are usually too coarse to adequately represent thermal
behavior of homes under extreme weather conditions, even as those are the conditions most relevant to load
shedding or shifting grid services. Lin et al [6] report a breakdown of a greybox model calibrated on August
data and applied to October data for an office building. The discrepancy between the predicted and actual
indoor temperatures was up to 13°F. Using more accurate models such as EnergyPlus (~200 parameters)
or higher-order greybox models coupled with semi-automatic calibration? tools [7] is not possible in our
setting either because no detailed home information is available. Moreover, nonlinear effects (such as
moisture transport and AC performance curves) are usually neglected in MPC modeling as well as air
leakage [8].

Lastly, the conventional MPC approaches are not directly applicable to a community of homes. Many
researchers have acknowledged that naïve changes in electricity prices by using naïve strategies can result
in unintended consequences if the participants in grid services use the same optimization technique for
scheduling. For example, simultaneous scheduling of flexible appliances during time slots with lower
electricity prices can lead to the rebound peaks [9]. On the other hand, using different prices for different
participants for control purposes to achieve the overall desired grid effect implies that the dimensionality
of the price-setting problem grows with the number of participants, rendering the scheme computationally
expensive. Moreover, such grid services as load shaping may require a computational time step of one
minute or less, which is orders of magnitude smaller than those currently implemented (e.g., 30 minutes
time step and 8-hour MPC horizon in [4]).

The presented project attempts to overcome these barriers. We are using homes equipped with the Sense
[10] Home Energy Monitor (HEM) that is installed on the home’s main breaker and nonintrusively
disaggregates the overall power signal into the power time series of individual major appliances. Thanks to
Sense’s agreement with a CT vendor, Sense obtains CT data in many of their customers’ homes, thus
providing two independent data streams (power draw of major appliances and thermostat setpoints, room
temperature and relative humidity [RH], HVAC state and runtime). In addition to HVAC and electric water
heaters (EWH) that are frequently used in Demand Response (DR) applications, we also incorporate
Electric Vehicle (EV) charging as a third flexible load. In Section 2, we provide information on the homes
and data we are using in this work. We recruited two cohorts of homes in this project, one – for model
development and another one – for a Pilot. Section 2 provides details on the recruited homes and data
collected.

In Section 3, we explain in detail our set of predictive models. This includes: 1) a lumped-parameter (or
greybox) thermal model for home HVAC system; 2) a hybrid physics-based/ machine-learning model for
home PV generation; 3) a hybrid physics-based/statistical model for home DHW; and 4) a model for EV
charging. In Section 4, we explain our approach to optimally schedule the flexible loads in a community of
homes. A computationally efficient Coordination Agent (CA) concurrently optimizes the performance of a
portfolio of homes and individual homes to provide grid services and revenues, subject to household
preferences and grid constraints. Using a novel inverse optimization approach, the CA simultaneously and
automatically converts several services (energy efficiency, load shifting, load shading and load shaping)
into corresponding objective functions and has linear complexity over the number of homes and over the
number of time steps (thus, allowing arbitrary time resolution).

Furthermore, the availability of disaggregated power consumption of a home presents an opportunity not
only for MPC but also for fault detection and diagnostics (FDD). Section 5 presents our advancements in
FDD of a modern HVAC system, the heat pump.

Finally, we tested the developed models and techniques in a field pilot. Section 6 details the pilot design,
recruitment process, communications and data transfer means used, and results. Given the relatively small
number of homes recruited, we also designed and performed a virtual pilot with semi-synthetic data, and
its results are reported in Section 6, too. The last section concludes this Report with a brief discussion and
summary.

2. Experimental Data
2.1 Sense Overview
The Sense home energy monitor (HEM) provides whole-home and device-level electricity consumption
data, and solar PV production data, where available, to consumers in a user-friendly mobile app interface.
Sense gathers amperage and voltage data from the two 120V feeds that power typical split-phase residential
electrical panels and combines them to provide real-time (second-scale) wattage and PV production data to
the homeowner. Sense also applies machine-learning technology to these data to discover electrical
appliance loads in the home and their actual usage and provides this to the homeowner both in historical
summaries and real time within the Sense mobile app. For this project, a small set of the total user
population was identified to provide anonymized data. An additional cohort of users was recruited to
participate in the pilot program.

Sense also offers users an option to connect their Ecobee thermostat in the Sense monitor mobile app to
help Sense improve HVAC detection.

3. Development of Models for MPC
As the name suggests, model-predictive control (MPC) implies models of the controllable (in this context
flexible) loads. In this project, we considered such relatively large residential loads as HVAC, EV charging,
and DHW. In addition, PV generation needs to be modeled, too. In this section, we present development
and validation of these models.

3.1 Thermal Model

3.1.1 Introduction
Coarse-grained lumped-parameter or greybox models predicting thermal response of a building and its
HVAC loads are commonly used due to their acceptable accuracy at a low computational cost [11], [12],
[13] and clear physical interpretability of their parameters [14], [15]. Although the technical field of
greybox models has received significant attention in recent years [8], several modeling aspects still pose a
challenge for practical implementation at scale, especially given very limited home information potentially
available for model calibration. Below we briefly review these aspects.

The key idea behind the lumped system approximation to the heat transfer processes in a building and
between a building and its environment is the assumption that building elements such as external walls,
windows, or internal household contents are thermally thin, i.e., that they can be adequately represented by
a single uniform temperature at any time. This reduces the comprehensive mathematical system of heat
transfer partial differential equations in space and time to a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
                                                Page 19 of 122

 “High-Fidelity Self-Learning Tool for Residential Load and Load Flexibility Forecasting”

in time [16]. Not only does this approximation yield a computationally tractable problem but it also reduces
the home-specific data that need to be parameterized to solve the ODEs.

It is generally acknowledged that the thermally-thin assumption is valid as long as the Biot number of the
lumped element, effectively the ratio of the convective heat transfer coefficient from the surface of a body
to conduction in the body, is less than 0.1 [17]. Kircher and Zhang [16] studied the lumped approximation
for several elements in a building and concluded that Biot numbers as large as 1 for the considered elements
(e.g., a wall subjected to wind/ outdoor temperature changes, and a window) can still maintain the lumped-
solution error under 5%, which can explain the “surprising accuracy” of the greybox models [16]. Yet,
Kircher and Zhang indicated that effects they did not study, e.g., thick/well insulated walls and, apparently,
step changes in the indoor temperature characteristic of MPC, could yield large errors.

Selection of the model order and composition is another sticky point interlaced with the model calibration
problem. In principle, the model order, i.e., the number of heat balance ODEs, naturally equals the number
of lumped elements [15]. In the expected MPC implementation for a community of homes, however, only
the total floorspace of a home (not even zone area) is typically readily available (in the U.S., from public
property databases). The lack of home architectural layout and detail makes physics-based model
construction less reliable. Mathematical methods, primarily based on goodness-of-fit, were alternatively
proposed for (semi) automatic greybox model construction. Athientis et al. detailed a method that starts
with a low-order greybox model and iteratively adds one parameter at a time; once reaching the best
goodness-of-fit to CT data (as measured by a Bayesian information criterion), the procedure is reversed to
delete redundant parameters [12]. This method was further extended to optimal zone selection in [18]. A
somewhat different method was proposed by Madsen et al [19], [20] who used the likelihood ratio test to
select the optimal model order. Without going into a detailed discussion, we note that both methods are
based on the assumed statistical distributions of the temperature prediction residuals: a normal distribution
by Athientis et al. and random walks (i.e., a Winer process) by Madson et al. Both assumptions seem to be
strong as the resultant optimal model orders seldom exceed four-five whereas the actual number of different
lumped elements in a residential building is much larger [15], which implies that the difference between
the predicted and recorded temperatures inevitably includes the neglected higher-order terms. Note also
that a CT (or a dedicated temperature sensor) does not necessarily report accurate zone air temperature in
time [21], [22], often prompting a separate greybox model for the thermostat [19].

As noted by Li et al [8], estimation of greybox model parameters can be performed either by a forward
estimation approach (i.e., based on physical properties and dimensions of building elements) or by an
inverse approach based on fitting the model to the data. Serasinghe, Long and Clark provided a
comprehensive review of the latter approach [11], indicating that the calibration problem is essentially an
optimization problem as a combination of parameter values minimizing an error metric is sought. The most
acute practical challenge in solving this optimization problem is the observed shallowness of the objective
function, which implies that many parameter combinations result in approximately the same value of the
objective function, i.e., the same goodness-of-fit [11], [12]. That said, Serasinghe, Long and Clark suggest
that “in most cases … the task is not necessarily to find the global optimum, but to deliver acceptable results
using a near-optimal solution.”

What accuracy is considered to be “acceptable?” The Ambience Consortium, a European organization,
issued a reference document on greybox models in 2021 [23]. This document suggests [Section 2.3.4, p.
28]: “very good” model fit corresponds to RMSE of less than 0.2°C in training and less than 0.5°C in
validation, whereas training RMSE between 0.2°C and 0.4°C and validation RMSE between 1°C and 2°C
correspond to a “good” grade. The metrics are related to model fitting on two months of data and validating
on one month of data (i.e., the prediction horizon is up to 1 month in validation). We note, however, that
the accuracy question is more complex in the MPC setting, because the prime concern in MPC is HVAC
                                                Page 20 of 122

 “High-Fidelity Self-Learning Tool for Residential Load and Load Flexibility Forecasting”

power consumption prediction (not temperature prediction) and the goodness-of-fit in temperature
prediction strongly depends on the degree of thermostat temperature setpoint variability [22].

Directly related to model calibration is an approach to solving the greybox system of ODEs. The common
approach involving discretization to solve the differential equations that appear in the cited greybox
literature is essentially a first-order Euler method with a constant time step. Given that the heat or cool
supply by the home HVAC system is typically cycling between “on” and “off” states, the resultant
differential equations are stiff [24], prompting a call for more advanced numerical methods such as those
with a variable time step.

The last three greybox challenges on our list are probably secondary, though still important for MPC
implementation. Infiltration contributes to, on average, between 34.2% to 43.1% of space heating loads in
US homes in different climate zones [25], yet air leakage is either totally neglected or, as Li et al. [8] pointed
out, “assumed to be a disturbance or a lumped parameter” in greybox modeling. With few exceptions, both
the heat and cool supply of an HVAC system are typically modeled as constant values at the “on” state and
zero at the “off” state. Whereas this assumption can be a good approximation for a single-stage furnace
[26], it could be too rough for air conditioners (AC) whose cooling output depends on both the outdoor dry-
bulb temperature and the indoor wet-bulb temperature [27]. Finally, the capacity and power draw of HVAC
systems is typically estimated from home floorspace using methods such as ACCA’s manual J [28] and
assumptions on vapor-compression cycle performance instead of using sub-metered or estimates derived
from home’s electric data, introducing further errors in model parameters and, thus, MPC performance.

In this project, we consider greybox modeling of homes equipped with both a CT and an advanced electric
metering device providing disaggregated power consumption data. The latter devices are becoming more
common and can offer opportunities to improve the models used for MPC. That said, their disaggregation
accuracy is not perfect. Below, we will discuss how to convert the disaggregation classification accuracy
into a metric suitable for greybox modeling accuracy. The modeling approach we developed is applicable
to homes with a central forced-air HVAC system [29] that uses a gas furnace for heating and vapor-
compression cycle for cooling. About 53.3 million U.S. households (out of 126.8 million total [30]) had
central gas furnaces in 2020 [31] while about 65 million households had central AC [32], [33]. This
selection defined the selection of homes for model training (see section 2.2).

3.1.3.2 Upgraded greybox model

One way to resolve the potential for unphysical steady-state envelope temperatures yielded by the basic
greybox model is to consider a more refined model for the heat transfer processes acting on the lumped
envelope. Following the thermally thin assumption underlying the lumped-element model, the basic model
assigns the envelope a single temperature, Tw. Whereas this assumption yielded simple conduction-only
terms in our base differential equations (3.7)-(3.9), we had to assume two identical resistances on the two
surfaces of the wall (external and internal), which may not be physically sound. We also simplified the heat
transfer from the envelope to the ambient (convection between the external envelope surface and the outside
air, longwave radiation) by incorporating these processes into the overall envelope thermal resistance (R-
value). Similarly, we did not explicitly model convective heat transfer between the room air and the internal
envelope surface.

In the updated greybox model, we increase model sophistication, albeit in a simplified way. First, to account
for the processes at the external envelope surface, we introduce an external envelope surface temperature,
Tw,e, and a model that relates it to the average wall temperature (effectively Tw) that can be used in the
differential equations. Second, as we do not add new lumped elements and thus the number of differential
equations remains the same (three), we avoid adding a new variable (i.e., the internal envelope surface
temperature Tw,i) by keeping the lumped element modeling of the heat exchange processes between the wall
and indoor air. Accordingly, we assume that the internal envelope surface temperature Tw,i approximately
equals Ta and consequentially Tf. In principle, a similar problem was addressed in [36], by using a series of
connected lumped wall elements. However, using 10+ of such connected elements [36], apparently required
for model accuracy, correspondingly enlarges the number of differential equations, complicating their
numerical solution.

3.1.3.3 Parameter estimation over heating season

Following common model calibration practices [11], we estimated the model parameters by minimizing the
error between the observed and modeled time-dependent indoor temperatures. Given that thermostats are
typically attached to an interior wall (rather than ~ suspended in the air as in some experiments [19]), the
measured temperature is likely to correspond to Tf and not to Tair in Eqs. (3.22)-(3.24) (see also the
thermostat notes in section 3.1.1); therefore, we minimized the difference between the predicted and actual
Tf(t). As some CT or weather data were missing or incomplete, we used several periods of 72 hours over a
month for parameter estimation (i.e., training) and similar periods over another month for testing (i.e.,
validation), a period appreciably longer than is relevant to most building MPC applications. To solve the
system of differential equations numerically, we use MATLAB’s solver for stiff problems (ode23) rather
than the conventional discretization (see section 3.1.1).

In earlier work [26], [38], we developed a computationally efficient procedure to estimate the envelope R-
value (parameter Rw) and the air leakage parameters (C1 and C2, Eq. (3.10)) with a similar stream of CT
and weather data, provided that the heat supply rate (parameter QHVAC) could be derived from utility gas
bills. This leaves us with twelve parameters of Eqs.(3.22)-(3.24) (or (3.7)-(3.15)) that need to be estimated:
(1) QHVAC; (2) q0; (3) Aw; (4) Aroof; (5) Afhf when HVAC is off; (6) Afhf when HVAC is on; (7) Cair; (8) Cw;
(9) Cf; (10) kglaz; (11) kroof; and (12) kwall.

Initially, we attempted to use commercial solvers for the underlying optimization problem, primarily the
MATLAB’s optimization toolbox. Unfortunately, they did not work well as the results were heavily
dependent on the initial (guess) values. Given that the 12 parameters are physical building parameters and
that the greybox model is expected to perform best with the actual physical building parameter values (this
is the essence of the forward estimation method [11]), we established physics-based ranges for each
parameter value and assigned triplets of physically meaningful values representing a low, a high and a mid-
range value. Next, we sample at random the triplets of each of the 12 parameters and run our previously
developed model to estimate the building envelope R-value and air leakage coefficients for the selected
value of the furnace heat supply (parameter 1), solve the system of the differential equations using the data
and record the average temperature error (i.e., RMSE) value. By performing numerous samplings from the
potential parameter combinations from the triplets and conducting the underlying calculations (typically,
we repeat this process 1,000 times), we select the 12 parameter values that yield the best goodness-of-fit.

This procedure, though more robust than the typical gradient-based optimization in curve fitting, suffers
from two drawbacks:
 - The total number of parameter combinations is large (312 = 531,441), so computationally-feasible
 number of combinations (~1,000) represents a tiny fraction of this total.
 - The procedure is supposed to be extended to include a local search in the vicinity of the found
 winning parameter combination to further refine those values [11].

Our experimentation with various methods to address the latter drawback was unsuccessful in the sense
that an iterative optimization procedure of a commercial optimization solver (MATLAB optimization
toolbox) would typically yield a parameter vector close to the initial parameter vector obtained from the
coarse-grained procedure. By being “close” we mean that the difference in the error is of the order of or
even less than the statistical noise in the goodness-of-fit (we can assess that by using different periods of
time in calibration). This problem could be attributed to the shallowness of the error surface, which in turn
can be explained by the large number of parameters (i.e., 12) sought by fitting over a single variable (indoor
temperature). Notably, some papers in the comprehensive review [11] also limited the estimation to the
coarse search, most likely because the obtained goodness-of-fit delivered “acceptable results using a near-
optimal solution” [11].

To address the former drawback, we analyzed the results in terms of the best-fit parameter combinations
obtained and also reviewed the physical basis underlying the parameter values in the triplets. Consequently,
we assigned for some parameters only one or two values while extending the range for some other
parameters. The old and new parameter tuplets are given in Table 3 below. For homes that differ in size
from the one given in the Table, the corresponding parameters are scaled accordingly.

3.1.3.5 Cooling season: modeling approach and results

A single-stage central AC controlled by a single thermostat is the simplest – and most common – central
cooling system installed in US homes. In contrast, two-stage and multi-stage systems are more difficult to
model, since they may have more complex embedded control logic that could be difficult to decipher using
only disaggregated power consumption (which is likely of lower accuracy than for single-stage systems)
and CT runtime data. The single-stage AC systems are also generally easier to control in DR. Therefore,
we consider homes with a single-stage HVAC system first.

Unlike in the heating season, the quantity QHVAC entering the first differential equation, Eq.(3.7) and Eq.
(3.22), cannot be approximated as a (negative) constant in the cooling season. Rather, it (sensible cooling
capacity) is a function of at least two variables, Ta and the wet-bulb indoor temperature (Twb). AC
manufactures provide performance tables listing both sensible and latent capacities for systems as a function
of those temperatures [46] and these tables show significant differences in sensible capacity.

Having calibrated our greybox model using the heating season data, we could, in principle, estimate QHVAC
for a variety of Ta/Twb conditions. However, as AC cycling generally decreases system performance and
can also severely degrade AC latent performance (because it takes time for sufficient moisture to
accumulate on the cooling coil until liquid drips from the coil and into the condensate drain [47]), we shall
down-select periods of time with ~constant Tout and Tin,wb and significant AC runtime fraction.

                                               Page 34 of 122

 “High-Fidelity Self-Learning Tool for Residential Load and Load Flexibility Forecasting”

We see two options for calculating the AC performance tables (or 2D curves) for sensible capacity. A
relatively simple “algebraic” approach assumes steady-state conditions over such periods of time so that
the derivatives on the left-hand side of Eqs. (3.22)-(3.24) are approximately zero and QHVAC values can then
be derived from the resultant algebraic equations. We attempted this approach, selecting 2-hour periods of
time with minimal variations of Ta, Tr (room or zone temperature as recorded by CT), GHI, wind speed,
and indoor RH (needed to calculate the wet-bulb temperature [48]) and a runtime fraction over 30%.
Unfortunately, the obtained values of QHVAC were too few and noisy to meaningfully model its dependence
on the temperatures.

The other approach is to implement a technique similar to the model calibration with heating season data
(see previous section). That is, solve the differential equations, Eqs. (3.22) – (3.24), assuming a given value
of QHVAC and applying the remaining parameter values from the heating-season calibration, and integrating
the equations over a meaningful period of time. That is, the time period should not be so long that the
weather and/or Tair change appreciably and not so short that there is insufficient runtime or the effect of
assuming the initial Tw,ext equals Tout becomes too big. By varying the values of QHVAC over a range of
potential values and selecting the value yielding the minimal temperature prediction error, we can estimate
the AC sensible capacity corresponding to that period of time. Similar to selecting the time interval for the
runtime prediction (see previous section), we selected two hours for the “meaningful period of time”.

changes over the course of the year that could not be readily predicted.

3.3 DHW Model
We performed a literature review for tank-based electric resistance water heaters (ERWHs). Briefly, the
most common ERWH tank sizes are 40 and 50 gallons, although residential tanks up to 80 gallons are sold.
ERWHs typically have two electric-resistance elements, each with its own thermostat. Only one element
can operate at a time, and the top element has priority, first engaging to heat up the water in the top ~1/3rd
of the tank (i.e., where the hot water draws from). Once the upper temperature is satisfied, the lower
resistance element engages to heat the rest of the tank. That said, Mayhorn et al [55] note that “the lower
element provides most of the heating load, with the upper element engaging only when the cold water layer
has moved up the tank near or above the upper electric resistance element.” The power consumption H of
the resistance element when in use is usually between 4 and 5 kW, and the time-dependent power
consumption of the element is well-approximated by a square wave (i.e., a constant value about 4.5 kW
when on and 0 when off).

An ERWH is a thermostatically controlled appliance. As such, the electric consumption events correspond
to either thermostat standby events (to compensate for tank heat losses, no DHW consumption) and water
consumption events. Given that the thermostat deadband for a DHW is typically a few degrees C and that
the temperature of hot water in the tank is about 40-60°C higher than the tap water temperature (and that of
the surrounding air), the generally nonlinear process of tank cooling between the events can be well
approximated by a linear temperature decay. Our analysis of DHW data suggests that, under typical
household conditions, the duration of time off between the standby events is approximately 6 hours [56].
Thanks to the approximate linearity of the water cooling process, we can estimate energy lost from standby
if water consumption event occurs prior to that average time off (i.e., 6 hours or whatever we estimate based
on experimental data). This, in turn, permits approximate calculation of water usage in the water
consumption event.

3.5 Thermal Comfort Model

Human thermal comfort is a critical component of heating, ventilation, and air conditioning control
(HVAC), yet it is highly subjective and therefore a challenging research topic. Two classical approaches to
modeling thermal comfort are the predicted mean vote [58] and adaptive comfort [59], [60] models. Both
have been widely adopted by building standards [61], [62] and are supported by a substantial body of
research [63], [64]. In these models, thermal comfort is determined by several factors, both environmental
(air temperature, relative humidity, etc.) and personal (metabolic rate, clothing, adaptation, etc.). Some
factors are difficult to measure, and must be estimated or assumed to employ these models, reducing model
accuracy.

When collecting or accessing a rich dataset of variables of interest is not feasible, another option is to apply
a different modeling approach based on the available data. For instance, setpoint temperature, which is
readily accessible through many CTs, may be predicted using external factors and used as a proxy for
thermal comfort [65]. However, this is not always a suitable indicator, since occupants may desire an indoor
temperature several degrees warmer or cooler than their actual selected temperature, e.g., to save energy or
money; or, they may change the setpoint when they are out of the home. Additionally, DR studies have
shown that occupants are willing to tolerate a greater degree of thermal discomfort for shorter periods and,
conversely, may tolerate a lesser degree of discomfort for a longer period [66].

Indeed, during DR events, in which occupants allow the utility to temporarily control their thermostat to
reduce or shape electric loads, it may not be necessary to predict specific comfort levels; rather, it may be
more useful to predict whether an occupant will become uncomfortable enough to change the setpoint
within the DR period or opt out of the program entirely. Furthermore, crucial data for assessing thermal
comfort (radiant temperature, clothing, metabolic rate, etc.) are usually not available, compromising the
ability to apply those models. To address this gap, we developed a minimal-data method for estimating the
probability that residential customers will opt-out (i.e., change the thermostat setpoint from the utility-
specified value) during a DR event.
                                                   Page 53 of 122

Out of the 18 homes for model development, we selected three homes that regularly implemented nightly
thermostat setbacks for data analysis. Our approach comprises four steps: schedule extraction; setpoint
change identification; discomfort characterization; and probabilistic modeling. It includes two key
assumptions:

1. Prior to changing the thermostat setpoint, occupants experience discomfort; and
2. Thermal discomfort has two components, namely, the degree of discomfort (the difference between
 preferred and actual temperatures) and the duration of discomfort.

First, we extract the day-night setpoint schedule from the CT data by identifying the overnight setback
value (most frequent setpoint value between 12am and 5am) and determining a 2-hour transition window
during morning and evening when the setback is most likely to end and begin. We then exclude overnight
setback periods from the remaining analysis.

Second, we identify all daytime setpoint change events, filtering them to exclude events that occur >3 hours
after the previous event. That is, we include only those events where the “discomfort period” lasts ≤3 hours.
We do this to satisfy our first assumption, as “discomfort periods” >3 hours may actually reflect vacancy
instead of discomfort. We also filter out events with setpoint changes >3°F, since most utility DR programs
change setpoints ≤3°F.

Third, to characterize the cumulative thermal discomfort experienced by occupants, we calculate a new
quantity for each setpoint change event, as in [67]:

 Cumulative discomfort (CD) = τ×(Tcur-Tprev) (3.30)

where τ is the time since the previous change (discomfort period), and Tprev and Tcur are the temperatures
before and after the change. CD, expressed in degree-hours, captures both parts of thermal discomfort in
assumption (2), i.e., degree and duration.

Finally, we create a probabilistic model for each home by calculating the cumulative distribution function
(CDF) of the CD for the home’s setpoint changes. We envision two ways that a utility could use this model:
(1) Calculate the expected CD based on intended setpoint change and duration, then use the CDF to estimate
the probability that a home will opt out of the DR event. (2) If the utility requires a maximum opt-out rate
of, e.g., 10%, they may use the CDF to calculate the required CD level to ensure a 10% probability of opt-
out for each home, and plan their DR event parameters for each home accordingly.

We support our approach by showing that we can predict CD using external weather variables (ambient
temperature, relative humidity, and solar irradiance) with support vector regression (which is suitable for
the expected nonlinear dependencies), even in the presence of occupancy-related noise. Selected results are
shown in Figure 23 (top); full results (correlation for CD predictions vs actual) are reported in Table 9. For
homes marked (*), prediction results were improved by selecting only the first setpoint change of the day,
rather than all changes throughout the day. These results suggest CD is a useful metric. Figure 23 (bottom)
shows the results of the probabilistic approach for one home (h57197).

4. Optimization/MPC
Throughout this project, we designed load management strategies to keep the aggregated load consumption
of the community of homes at a target level (possibly time-varying) while maintaining the levels of comfort
(temperature) and service (for DHW and EVs) by households. Maintaining the comfort of participants is
vital for the success of residential DR programs, and it is common to represent user comfort by utility
functions. However, there is no simple way to translate user preferences into utility functions. Model
Predictive Control (MPC)-based approaches can ensure user comfort by incorporating inputs such as user
preferences, weather forecasts, appliance properties and appliance dynamics into the decision-making
stage.

We applied MPC-based optimization in a range of load flexibility contexts with different tariff structures,
such as Time-of-Use (TOU), Real-Time Pricing (RTP) and fixed electricity rates (in $ per kWh)used in
different jurisdictions. The objective function and the nature of the optimization problem change with the
tariff structure, and we briefly review those below.

4.7. Conclusions
Developing privacy-preserving, practical and efficient solution mechanisms is as important as designing
effective DR strategies to use in real-world problems. Therefore, we devised distributed optimization
frameworks by decomposing the actions and the computations at the home level for all the DR strategies
we implemented in this project. The code that is utilized in pilot studies is publicly available with useful
explanation [73].

Even though we focused on MPC-based DR strategies throughout this project, the success of these
approaches depends on the quality of the underlying model. Increasing data availability and compute power
lead to a growing interest in adopting AI-based strategies in DR programs. Among these, Reinforcement
Learning (RL) offers an appealing framework for DR applications due to its adaptive nature and its capacity
to learn user preferences through interactions without requiring appliance models. However, the use of RL

5. Fault Detection and Diagnostics (FDD)
5.1 Introduction
Over the course of this project, Sense developed and improved device detection models for residential
HVAC units, including single-speed central AC/heat pump units, variable speed AC/heat pump units, and
auxiliary heat used by heat pump systems. Accurate model detection allows for faults to be detected in
systems when a variance is seen during operation?, potentially enabling faults to be caught early and
corrected to reduce energy use and repairs or replacement if needed before the HVAC system fails.

Sense’s innovative FDD capability directly relates to its core capability of disaggregating loads and
isolating device level power curves or “signatures”. Sense isolates device-level power curves from only
measuring the entire home’s electricity. Therefore, a key differentiator is Sense’s FDD method does not
require device-level submetering and additional sensors or relying on the device’s own runtime data that
most residential customers cannot access. Notably, new deployments of Sense integrated into next-
generation smart meters create an opportunity to scale an effective FDD functionality cost-effectively to
numerous homes.

The two original FDD goals for this project were:

 ● FDD for HVAC: develop methods for ongoing commissioning and FDD using the developed
 HVAC model and its results.
 ● Implement FDD: implement the developed ongoing commissioning and FDD, collect observations
 and feedback.

After focusing on the initial tasks of improving its HVAC device detection models, its final goal for FDD
is to track the efficiency of heat pumps by studying how the compressor and auxiliary heat behave as time-
series data and at different temperatures. Sense characterizes heat pump efficiency based on the joint
behavior of its compressor and auxiliary heat.

This requires solving three technical subproblems:

 1. Disaggregate the compressor wattage.
 2. Model the auxiliary heat.
 3. Analyze their joint behavior.

This requires the ability to disaggregate heat pumps and auxiliary heat signals at a reasonably high time
resolution (1 Hz) and model their power consumption.

A wide variety of heat pumps are present in the Sense user population, with the two high-level categories
of single-speed and variable-speed heat pumps. While Sense has had some success modeling simpler,
single-speed heat pumps with its legacy systems, a significant and rapidly growing fraction of all heat
pumps use variable-speed compressors.
                                                Page 63 of 122

 “High-Fidelity Self-Learning Tool for Residential Load and Load Flexibility Forecasting”

Sense’s core device disaggregation technology leverages a large foundation model for wattage signals.
Foundation models are trained on large amounts of data in a self-supervised manner and can be flexibly
adapted for a number of inference tasks. Much like Transformers have succeeded in modeling language,
the Sense wattage foundation model shows similar promise in modeling home energy time-series data.

The success of this model depends on the availability of ground truth data for specific devices. For this
project, Sense acquired ground truth for single-speed heat pumps, variable-speed heat pumps, and auxiliary
heat systems. Based on the foundation model and this ground truth, Sense built offline machine learning
models to disaggregate these devices from the residential one, two, or three-phase whole home electrical
system (mains) wattage time-series.

With these models, we Sense can performs statistical analysis to understand the behavior of auxiliary heat
as a function of temperature. We Sense ran our its detectors on about 600 houses with a heat pump and
auxiliary heat for 3 three months (12/31/2023 to 03/30/2024). In these data, we they observed the expected
pattern: heat pump heating cycles occurred at both high and low temperatures, while auxiliary heat only
activated at around 40 °F and below. We Sense successfully disaggregated and analyzed heat pump type
and energy consumption, identified high-confidence heat pump installations, and investigated their energy
consumption patterns to find specific outliers.

5.6 Conclusion
Over this study, Sense developed a Fault Detection and Diagnostics (FDD) approach in residential HVAC
systems that leverages its disaggregated data. By training a large foundation model on a large amount of
high-resolution home energy data, Sense created device detectors capable of accurately disaggregating the
power signatures of complex HVAC components, including single-speed compressors, variable-speed
compressors, and auxiliary heat systems. This detailed disaggregation allowed Sense to characterize typical
operational behaviors, such as the temperature thresholds for auxiliary heat activation, and to develop
methods for identifying inefficient outliers and potential system faults. The success of this foundation
model-based approach represents a significant leap forward, offering a scalable and cost-effective FDD
solution that can be deployed widely, particularly through next-generation smart meters, to enhance
residential energy efficiency and prevent critical system failures.

6.6 Thermal model calibration
The originally recruited homes were successfully connected to the Enode platform that allowed data
collection as related to the CTs (i.e., 5-min resolution data on the zone- and setpoint temperatures, only
integer values for both, and thermostat status, e.g., “heating” or “cooling”). The actual data collection
started in late February. Fortunately, in both Northeast and Northwest locations the spring was late in 2024,
which resulted in 4-6 weeks of useful heating data.

                                                        Page 92 of 122

 “High-Fidelity Self-Learning Tool for Residential Load and Load Flexibility Forecasting”

Given that Enode does not record HVAC runtime (at least currently), we developed a computational
procedure to perform the unity operation on the runtime estimated from the current and set temperatures,
and the furnace runtime as reported by the disaggregated data. The latter has 1-min resolution. For some
homes, the Furnace data were populated in the disaggregation weekly reports while for most other homes
we found reasonable data under the Potential Heat Pump category.

We obtained the external weather data from the same weather data vendor (Solcast) that we used for the
previous model-development homes, using the ZIP codes of the recruited homes.

Using the calibration procedure that we developed over the previous work (see section 3.1) and the new
sets of data, we estimated the parameters of the thermal model for the recruited homes. The goodness-of-
fit was somewhat lower than with the previous homes, with a typical RMSE ~ 0.85-1.2 oC over the 72-hour
horizon for temperature prediction (0.4-1 oC previously). We attribute the larger error to the absence of
directly reported runtime and to the coarseness of temperature data (only integers with Enode vs. 0.1 oF
with Ecobee).

Altogether, we were able to calibrate the thermal model for 15 homes out of 18 homes with a single CT.
For the remaining three homes, we could not estimate the furnace runtime as the reported zone temperature
did not fluctuate and no meaningful disaggregation results were available.

Unfortunately, we could not conduct the AC performance curve estimation procedure, because only limited
AC data were collected by mid=July 2024, leaving very limited time frame for the pilot. Moreover, newly
recruited homes (i.e., the second wave recruitment homes) did not have heating data. Given that the pilot-
recruited homes are likely to be similar to some of the originally data-recruited homes we worked with in
model development (see section 3.1), we decided to use the results of both the homes recruited for the pilot
with reasonable heating season data and also some originally recruited homes’ calibration (i.e., parameter
sets) as candidate parameter sets (with scaling of the appropriate parameters with the home floorspace).
With the limited AC data collected by mid-July 2024, we could apply these parameter sets to the new home
data, along with a range of candidate AC sizes, to select the best-fit combination, and assess the goodness-
of-fit.

We realize that this strategy yields thermal parameter estimates that are not as robust as those based on the
full-blown calibration developed earlier (i.e., estimation of most thermal parameters using entire heating
season data followed by estimation of the AC performance curve using entire cooling season data – see
section 3.1). Yet as we will see below, this strategy results in a reasonable goodness-of-fit (for indoor
temperature prediction) for most Pilot homes and thus can be used given the data challenges we experienced
(only four or less weeks of data over heating season for most Pilot homes, only integer values of indoor
temperature, no CT data for HVAC runtime and RH, and very limited AC data over the first half of the
Summer in 2024 in both Northeast and Northwest communities).

6.7.4 Historical data analysis module
This module analyzes historical weather and selects the day within the past 60 days that most closely
matches the characteristics of the forecasted weather in terms of cooling degree days and average GHI. For
that day, the module identifies the temperature setpoint at the time the event is scheduled to begin (e.g., 9
am) and selects that setpoint as the home’s “base setpoint”, which is then used in the setpoint trajectory
generation module. The module also calculates the home’s non-HVAC power consumption for that day,
which is later used as the estimate of the home’s consumption for the event.

6.7.5 Setpoint trajectory generator module
During a pilot event for HVAC systems, each home’s CT is controlled according to a pre-determined
setpoint trajectory, with the objective being to shape the community’s aggregate load profile. We designed
several types of trajectories: offset; simple moving window; simple breakpoint; symmetric; and pre-cooling.
With the exception of the offset type, these trajectories utilize the base setpoint (called T0) outputted by the
historical data analysis module.
 - Offset: Rather than prescribing a specific setpoint, the CT is instead instructed to change the
 setpoint by some offset relative to the setpoint already scheduled by the user.
 - Simple moving window: Setpoint is initially set to T0 at the beginning of the event, then
 increased/decreased by 2-3 degrees Fahrenheit for 2-3 hours, then returned to T0 for the remainder
 of the event.

                                                     Page 97 of 122

 “High-Fidelity Self-Learning Tool for Residential Load and Load Flexibility Forecasting”

 - Simple breakpoint: Setpoint is initially set to T0 at the beginning of the event, then
 increased/decreased by 2-3 degrees Fahrenheit after 2-3 hours. Setpoint remains at this value until
 the end of the event.
 - Symmetric: Setpoint is initially set to T0 at the beginning of the event, then increased/decreased
 by 2-3 degrees Fahrenheit for 2-3 hours. Setpoint is reset to T0 for a period, then
 increased/decreased (opposite direction as before) for 2-3 hours, then reset to T0 until the end of
 the event. The setpoint profile for the duration of the event is designed to be symmetric about T0.
 - Precooling: At the beginning of the event, setpoint is decreased from T0 by 2-3 degrees Fahrenheit
 for 3-4 hours (precooling), then increased to 2-3 degrees above T0 until the end of the event (“ride-
 out” period).

The setpoint trajectory generator module generates several (between 10 and 20) candidate setpoint
trajectories for each home, which it then passes to the HVAC simulator module.

6.7.6 HVAC simulator module
The HVAC simulator module takes as inputs a home’s thermal model parameters, weather forecast, and
candidate setpoint trajectories. For each setpoint trajectory, the module simulates the home’s thermal
response and outputs the estimated HVAC profile required to satisfy the given setpoints under the
forecasted weather conditions.

6.8 EV events
Given that the computations underlying the AC systems characterization for the Pilot homes were
completed only in early August, we decided to start the Pilot with EV charging events. Unfortunately, for
any command to work in the current environment (i.e., ReTool), the EV must be connected to the charger.
This means that if we want an EV to be charged in the morning, we need to issue several “Stop charging”
commands prior to the expected charging time, because as soon as an EV is connected to a charger, it
typically starts charging instantly, and the connection time is random. Accordingly, the Sense team
suggested to use “Cascading Events” – see Figure 56.

Figure 56 Cascading event design to ensure EVs do not charge overnight

Apparently, this particular event setting still has generated errors, as practically no EV has followed our
scheduled charging in both the Northeastern (event on July 24th) and Northwestern (event on July 25th)
communities, and numerous errors were displayed on the ReTool. Moreover, the ReTool crashed and
needed to be rebuilt after these first two events. Also, through direct engagement with users, the Sense team
identified connectivity issues, particularly for EVs parked in garages with poor internet access. This
                                                      Page 98 of 122

 “High-Fidelity Self-Learning Tool for Residential Load and Load Flexibility Forecasting”

limitation hindered the effective execution of events, as instructions could not reach the EVs in a timely
manner.

After the ReTool was rebuilt, we attempted two additional EV events. These two, although they did not
cause the ReTool crash, they were still unsuccessful in the sense of following the designed charging
commands. First, we modified the notification text and requested the users to connect the vehicle either the
evening before (9 pm) or the morning of the event (9 am), and designed the cascading commands
accordingly. Whereas this prevented error messages and indeed most users did connect their EVs as
requested, another problem appeared: practically all EVs that connected following our request, had about
70-80% charge at the time of connection, leaving very little opportunity for charging during the event.
Finally, we observed that many users disconnected their EVs and apparently left in the late morning hours.
Overall, only one or two EVs completely followed the event’s charging command (out of 8-10 homes
participating).

These three challenges, namely
 - Timely connection of EVs to charger
 - Prior high charge of EVs at the connection time
 - High opt-out rate during the event

make EV charging unreliable for the Pilot, at least in this particular setting (with ReTool). Had there been
a more advanced communication with the charger available, we would have addressed the first two
challenges (by issuing the Stop Charging command as soon as the user connects their EV to charger at the
day prior, as opposed to the after 9 pm + 1-hour repeats in the cascading design). However, it is not clear
how to address the unreliability of the users that apparently feel free to opt out at any time (other than using
a significant incentive).

Accordingly, we resorted to HVAC events only, with no coupling to EV events.

6.9 HVAC events

6.9.1 Integrated workflow for event planning
To make planning and implementation of HVAC events more effective, we developed a MATLAB-based
workflow that integrates the prediction models explained above. The event planner workflow is outlined in
Figure 57 below.

The overall workflow is separated into two stages: in the first stage, various forecasts are made for
individual homes by the modules described above (PV generation, historical data analysis, setpoint
trajectory generator, and HVAC simulator). These forecasts are sent to the second stage, where they are
aggregated and passed to the optimization module.

For each home that is selected to participate in a particular event, the event planner first collects the
necessary input data, including the home’s pre-calibrated thermal model parameters, historical weather,
setpoint, and power consumption data, and a 24-hour weather forecast (provided by our vendor Solcast).
These input data are sent to various modules as shown in the figure. The PV prediction module uses the
weather forecast to generate a PV power forecast for the event period. The historical data analysis module
provides a “base setpoint” to the setpoint trajectory generator module, and also estimates the home’s non-
HVAC power consumption during the event. The temperature setpoint generator module generates a variety
                                                Page 99 of 122

 “High-Fidelity Self-Learning Tool for Residential Load and Load Flexibility Forecasting”

of candidate setpoint trajectories according to the strategies outlined above. These trajectories, together
with the weather forecast and thermal model parameters, are used to generate HVAC power consumption
trajectories.

The PV generation and non-HVAC power consumption forecasts for each home are aggregated and,
together with the candidate HVAC trajectories for each home, are inputted to the optimization module,
which outputs optimal HVAC trajectories as well as their respective temperature setpoint trajectories.
Finally, we implement these setpoint trajectories by scheduling events in ReTool.

The optimization module aggregates the PV generation, HVAC consumption, and non-HVAC power
consumption forecasts for the individual homes and calculates the aggregate consumption profile, or Q-
vector, according to the following:

power consumption, and PV power generation, respectively, of the i-th home, and t is the discrete time

The Q-vector is optimized over the HVAC profiles, and optimal HVAC trajectories for each home are
outputted. In this pilot, the objective for the Q-vector is to be as flat as possible (the significant PV-home

 1. For each home, select a candidate HVAC trajectory at random
 2. Calculate the community Q-vector according to Eqn. (1) using the selected trajectories

6.11 Virtual Pilot with Semi Synthetic Data
We can enhance the scope of the Pilot by performing Monte Carlo (MC) simulations with semi-synthetic
data for a larger community size (100-1,000 homes) to test the advantages of the developed models and
optimization algorithms. In this way, we would be able to show how realistic communities of homes with
variable degrees of PV/EV penetration rates react to typical DR programs with and without optimization in
terms of community-level power profile and individual home savings. Moreover, we could also study the
effect of proper modeling (HVAC and PV models), i.e., our modeling vs apparently coarser models that are
being used (e.g., linear models for HVAC, regression models for PV).

Thus, we developed a MC procedure to “create” a virtual but realistic community of homes, based on the
data collected over the field Pilot period and applying our models and optimization algorithms. The
                                                      Page 107 of 122

 “High-Fidelity Self-Learning Tool for Residential Load and Load Flexibility Forecasting”

procedure builds upon the “core” homes (i.e., the homes we used for AC modeling and control) and adds
to these homes PV systems and/or EVs at random, according to pre-defined rates of PV- and EV-
penetrations.

While preparing for this virtual pilot, we realized there might be correlations between HVAC loads,
everything-else loads, thermostat setpoints and temperature prediction errors. Accordingly, initially we just
resample the core homes, adding PV and/or EVs to them based on PV system characteristics and charging
patterns sampled from other field pilot homes. In the future, we can cover more advanced scenarios, e.g.,
using an empirical distribution of home square footage for an actual community (e.g., for a ZIP code 02081),
or a joint distribution of home square footage, PV size, and EV charging rate.

In more detail, the procedure to obtain a 24-h community net power profile with five minutes time resolution
currently works as follows:

6.11.1 Preliminaries
1. Select either a community in the Northeast (NE) or Northwest (NW) to simulate.

2. Select the most frequent ZIP code for a selected community and obtain weather for summer 2024.

3. Obtain 24-h thermostat setpoint profiles for each “core” home and for each day during the summer of
2024. For each obtained setpoint profile record the date and indicate weekday or weekend.

4. Obtain thermal model parameters (available from Pilot), calculate room temperature prediction over the
24-h period and calculate the corresponding indoor temperature prediction error profile, for each day during
the summer of 2024. For each obtained setpoint profile also record the date.

5. Obtain PV prediction parameter sets (one set per a PV home, available from Pilot) for each community.
Record square footage for each set. Calculate 24-h PV prediction error profiles for each day during the
summer of 2024, also indicating dates (similar to #4).

6. Calculate EV characteristic set (charging rate in %/h and kW, initial state of charge (SOC) before
charging, final SOC after charging) for each community (one set per EV, available from Pilot).

7. Obtain 24-h power profiles for “everything else” for each core home and for each day during the summer
of 2024. For each error profile also record date.

6.11.2 Monte Carlo (MC) generation of a community
1. Select either NE or NW community.

2. Select number of homes, N. Typically N will range from 100 to 1,000 in MC simulations.

3. Select PV penetration rate (e.g., 30%) and EV penetration rates (e.g., the probability for a PV home to
have an EV is 0.4 whereas probability for a non-PV home to have an EV is 0.2, for the community
considered).

4. Select a date from summer 2024 to simulate, e.g., July 5th.

5. Obtain the weather profile for this date and 12 h prior.

6. For MC home #1, draw at random a core home number from the previously compiled list of core homes.

7. Perform a random operation to define whether this home has a PV system and/or an EV, according to
the probabilities (step 3). If “yes” then draw at random a set of PV parameters from the PV pool and a set
of EV parameters from the EV pool.

8. Randomly select an “everything else” power profile from the pool of these profiles for the home, keeping
similarity of the weather profile (i.e., pre-selecting 3-4 days with similar weather and same
weekday/weekend as the selected date and then randomly selecting one).

9. Draw at random the thermostat setpoint profile for this home, keeping similarity of the weather profile
and weekday/weekend (similar to #8).

10. Calculate the room temperature prediction using the thermal (i.e., AC) model with the home’s parameter
set, the obtained weather profile and the obtained setpoint profile.

11. Draw at random an indoor temperature prediction error profile keeping similarity of the weather profile
and weekday/weekend, and add to the room temperature prediction.

12. Using the profiles from steps 9-11 above and using the thermal model parameters for this home (in this
case, AC power draw), calculate AC power draw profile.

13. If the home has PV (see 7 above), calculate the PV generation prediction, then draw at random the PV
generation error, keeping similarity of the weather profile, and sum them together.
                                               Page 109 of 122

 “High-Fidelity Self-Learning Tool for Residential Load and Load Flexibility Forecasting”

14. If the home has EV, sample the initial and final SOC using the obtained set (from #7), sample the
starting time and use other parameters from the set to generate EV charging profile.

15. Sum together the power profiles obtained at steps 8, 12, 13 and 14 (if applicable) to generate a simulated
net power profile for home #1.

16. Repeat steps 6-15 for the remaining N-1 homes.

17. Repeat steps 6-16 a number of times (e.g., 10+) to represent variability.

Finally, we would like to see the effect of model accuracy on optimization. To this end, we need to model
the state-of-the-art accuracy. Work of Michael Siemann [Siemann] is very close to our approach and we
consider it as being the state-of-the-art in terms of applying a higher-order greybox model to actual homes.
This work shows a typical prediction error of 0.5°C over 6-hour horizon for a constant setpoint and error
of 0.9°C over a setpoint change. To simulate the error, with used the random walk concept. That is, at each
time increment, we add a normally-distributed random number with 0 mean and standard deviation
calculated to match the total error of 0.5°C over 6 hours, for periods of time with a constant setpoint. For
the setpoint changes, we add an error of ±0.9°C. Note that the average error of our model is at least 2 times
lower.

In this way, we added the temperature error to our AC trajectories, re-calculated the trajectories, obtained
the “optimal” community net profile and repeated this process 10 times. Given that the trajectories now
include significant error, we can expect that the actual community net load would significantly differ from
that “optimal.” We can calculate a good approximation of “actual” by using the original trajectories in lieu
of the re-calculated ones. Figure 73 shows the results. It can be seen in the Figure that using a relatively
coarse state-of-the-art model leads to nonoptimal results. These nonoptimal results are apparently within
the main body of possible trajectories (see Figure 8) and far away from the expected optimum.

Conclusions
The main objective of this project was expanding the Sense’s disaggregation offering to include providing
advanced grid services with homes. The developed technologies include high-fidelity models that advance
state-of-the-art. In particular, the developed thermal model for homes with a central forced-air HVAC
system that uses a gas furnace for heating and vapor-compression cycle for cooling (typical for a significant
portion of US homes), is calibrated using historical CT and weather data and provides superior predictive
accuracy and over longer time horizons than the state-of-the-art models. The preliminary developed thermal
comfort model predicts the expected discomfort tolerance based on the magnitude and duration of indoor
temperature changes during DR events based on the analysis of each household’s historic CT data, thus
having the potential for a lower dropout rate than the state-of-the-art. The developed set of optimization
algorithms offers linear computational complexity with regard to the number of participating households
and controllable devices whereas the state-of-the-art MPC algorithms typically have quadratic
computational complexity, which severely limits the number of controllable devices and/or increases the
computational time step, thus negatively affecting prediction accuracy and optimality of the solution. Not
only do the developed algorithms perform load shaping (i.e., utility service), but they simultaneously
minimize household electricity costs under a TOU tariff, and can accommodate other optimization
objectives.

We also developed and improved device detection models for residential HVAC units, including single-
speed central AC/heat pump units, variable speed AC/heat pump units, and associated auxiliary heat used
by heat pump systems. The developed technique is unique to Sense because it is based on Sense’s ability
to obtain disaggregated data. Using the disaggregated data, Sense can identify homes with excessive use of

                                                         Page 118 of 122

 “High-Fidelity Self-Learning Tool for Residential Load and Load Flexibility Forecasting”

inefficient auxiliary heat by evaluating auxiliary heat use as a function of outdoor temperature and relative
to compressor-based heat pump operation (also as a function of outdoor temperature), as well as the
potential to identify homes with high space heating energy consumption (particularly if combined with
building floorspace and indoor temperature data). When scaled up, these techniques have the potential to
significantly reduce space heating energy consumption, foremost in homes with poor auxiliary heat controls
and/or poor heat-pump performance (e.g., due to refrigerant charge issues)..

Our modeling and optimization technology was developed using data gathered from existing Sense
customers. We validated the technology over the course of a Pilot with newly recruited Sense customers.
Two platforms (ReTool and Enode) enabled data collection and control communications during the Pilot
events, although this process was not yet fully automated. The Pilot has found that EV charging has a much
lower realizable demand management potential than typical EV charging levels because a large portion of
EVs are not connected before or during events and also due to a high dropout rate . On the other hand, we
showed that managing EV charging dramatically enhances the load shaping potential because EVs that are
connected and would charge during an event have a high degree of flexibility, calling for larger incentives
to EV owners to participate.

How much value does Sense’s disaggregation technology bring to grid services of a community of homes
and to customer saving in modern pricing schemes such as TOU pricing? State-of-the-art services typically
offer relatively basic grid services with residential customers, primarily peak load shaving, by precooling
(or preheating) homes prior to DR events and reducing HVAC loads during the events by changing CT
setpoints. A similar technique is used to reduce HVAC loads when TOU pricing is high. These techniques
work on the level of an individual home, but do not achieve an optimal solution for a communities of homes.
The availability of appliance-specific power draw information (i.e., disaggregated AC power draw, electric
DHW power draw, EV charging rate, time-dependent PV generation), coupled with the developed
predictive and optimization algorithms enables provision of more complex grid services, such as
distribution grid net load shaping, while simultaneously minimizing electricity costs for individual homes
and taking into account the specific characteristics and constraints of each individual home..
