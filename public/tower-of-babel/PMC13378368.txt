Identification of FXYD5 as an oncogene in pediatric papillary thyroid cancer
Authors: Ju Yu, Bo Lin, Wanna Chen, Qin Tang, Liang Zheng, Xiaoli Liang, Fang Wang, Sui Peng, Yihao Liu, Jie Li, Rengyun Liu, Haipeng Xiao, Weiming Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPapillary thyroid cancer is one of the most common malignancies in pediatrics, with increasing prevalence. We analyzed the single-cell transcriptomic landscape from 11 pediatric patients. The compositions and functions in the tumor microenvironment showed remarkable differences. Endo_tip was primarily from tumor, receiving angiogenesis-associated signals from epithelia. Abundant immune cells infiltrated into tumor. SPP1+ M2-macrophage and tumor cells interacted with T cells via immunosuppressive ligand-receptor pairs. Trajectory inference identified genes in evolutionary branchpoints that may participate in tumor progression. Analyzing the correlation of genes with thyroid differentiation score, clinicopathological features, and prognosis, we identified that FXYD5 might play a key role. RNA-seq data showed FXYD5 mediated proliferation and migration via apoptosis and adhesion processes. Compared with adults, epithelia in pediatrics exhibit higher enrichment in tumor-associated pathways; however, the differences in tumor microenvironment are less pronounced. Our findings reveal a heterogeneous microenvironment of pediatric PTC and identify FXYD5 as a potential prognostic and therapeutic marker.Keywords: single-cell sequencing, papillary thyroid cancer, intercellular communications, trajectory inference, intratumor heterogeneity

Introduction

Thyroid cancer (TC) is a highly prevalent malignancy, notably characterized by a high rate of lymph node metastasis (LNM).1,2 Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), the most common well-differentiated histological subtype, accounts for over 80% of all TC cases and generally carries a favorable prognosis.1 However, emerging data reveal a concerning trend: The incidence of pediatric PTC has steadily increased since 1975. It now represents 12% of all pediatric malignant tumors and up to 4% of the total TC cases,3,4 underscoring its growing clinical significance in the pediatric oncology landscape. Accumulating evidence highlights both similarities and key distinctions between pediatric and adult TC. Compared to adults, pediatric patients with PTC often present with more advanced disease, including larger primary tumors, higher rates of multifocality, extra-thyroid extension (ETE), lymph node involvement, and even distant metastases.5,6 Clinically, these features translate into a higher burden of perioperative complications and an elevated risk of disease recurrence. Despite these unique challenges, evidence-based diagnostic and therapeutic guidelines tailored to pediatrics remain limited, and there is a critical lack of individualized treatment strategies for pediatric patients.6,7,8 This gap in precision medicine underscores the urgent need to better understand the biological underpinnings of pediatric PTC to improve clinical management.

At the molecular level, pediatric PTC exhibits a distinct genetic profile compared to its adult counterpart. Gene fusion events—predominantly involving RET—are significantly more prevalent than point mutations such as BRAF V600E, a pattern that is reversed in adult PTC.9,10,11 Notably, gene fusion has been associated with aggressive clinicopathological features, including LNM and distant metastasis, and may also influence response to radioiodine therapy.12,13 Intriguingly, studies suggest that prior radiation exposure in childhood increases the likelihood of gene fusions, with a higher fusion frequency observed in younger individuals,11 implicating environmental factors in shaping the molecular architecture of pediatric tumors. In contrast, pediatric patients without radiation exposure history show a more balanced distribution of gene mutations and fusions,13 further indicating the existence of heterogeneous oncogenic drivers in this population. These collective observations—distinct genetic alterations, aggressive phenotypes, and divergent treatment responses—strongly suggest that pediatric PTC may be driven by unique molecular mechanisms that differ fundamentally from those in adults. However, the precise drivers of tumor initiation and progression in pediatrics remain poorly defined, representing a major knowledge gap that hinders the development of targeted interventions and biologically informed therapeutic strategies.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a transformative technology for dissecting tumor heterogeneity and reconstructing cellular developmental trajectories with unprecedented resolution. By enabling transcriptome-wide profiling at the individual cell level, scRNA-seq facilitates the identification of rare cell populations, characterization of intratumoral diversity, and inference of dynamic cellular states during tumor evolution—offering powerful insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance.14,15,16 This approach has already yielded valuable discoveries in adult PTC, revealing complex tumor microenvironments (TMEs), epithelial plasticity, and key regulatory programs driving carcinogenesis.17,18,19,20,21 Yet, despite its proven utility, limited studies have applied scRNA-seq to comprehensively interrogate the pediatric PTC ecosystem.22,23 The absence of single-cell resolution data in this clinically distinct and molecularly unique population represents a significant barrier to understanding its biology. To bridge this gap, we conducted scRNA-seq on pediatric PTC samples to systematically map the cellular composition, delineate the developmental trajectory of malignant epithelial cells, and uncover critical genomic events governing disease progression. Our study aims to decode the tumor ecosystem of pediatric PTC, identify novel driver mechanisms, and ultimately reveal potential therapeutic targets tailored to this vulnerable patient population.

Results

Single-cell landscape of pediatric PTC

To fully understand the TME of pediatric PTC, we applied scRNA-seq to analyze cell compositions in surgical resected specimens from 11 pediatric patients, including 11 primary tumors (T) and 8 adjacent non-cancerous tissues (P) (Figure 1A; Table S1). After rigorous quality filtering and doublets removal (see the METHODS DETAILS section), a total of 145,530 cells were obtained, including 95,579 and 49,951 cells from tumor and adjacent non-cancerous tissues, respectively (Figures 1B; Table S2).

To reduce the dimensionality and group of cells with similar expression profiles, we used principle component analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) algorithm implemented in the Seurat software to identify cell clusters in TME. Each cluster was annotated as a specific cell type with the expression of well-known marker genes to form seven major cell types, including 9,230 B cells (CD79A, CD20, CD79B, and CD19), 2,513 plasma cells (MZB1, SDC1, CD79A, IGHG1), 31,293 T cells (CD3D, CD2, CD3E, CD4 and CD8A), 63,150 epithelia (TG, KRT18, KRT19, and EPCAM), 13,132 fibroblast (DCN, PDGFRB, COL1A1, and COL3A1), 13,768 myeloid (LYZ, S100A8, MRC1 and CD68) and 12,444 endothelia (PECAM1 and VWF) (Figures 1C; Figure S1A). These cell populations were distributed unevenly among patients (Figure S2). The most abundant immune cells in pediatric PTC were T/NK cells, and myeloid cells were predominantly enriched in tumor but rarely in adjacent non-cancerous tissues (Figures 1D and 1E). Comparison analysis of the fraction of seven major cell types between tumor and adjacent non-cancerous tissues and among distinct individuals showed that the abundance changes in cell compositions were dynamic, suggesting heterogeneity and complexity in pediatric PTC (Figures 1D and 1E; Figures S1B and S1C). To reveal the diversity of major cell types, we performed further subcluster analysis to identify heterogeneous subtypes in the following parts.

To systematically characterize the specific features of the TME in pediatric PTC, we integrated public single-cell transcriptomic data from adult PTC (GEO: GSE193581).24 This dataset comprises 7 tumor tissues and 6 adjacent non-tumor tissues, including 2 paired samples. Following batch effect correction (Figure S3), we merged and analyzed the tumor-derived cells from both pediatric and adult cohorts. Unsupervised clustering and annotation based on canonical marker gene expression identified seven major cell types: B cells, T cells, plasma cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, and myeloid cells (Figure S4A). Comparison of the TME composition between pediatric and adult PTC revealed a significantly higher proportion of stromal cells (including fibroblasts and endothelial cells) and a markedly lower proportion of myeloid cells in the pediatric group (p < 0.05) (Figures S4B and S4C; Table S3). To further dissect the heterogeneity of the stromal and immune microenvironments, we performed deep sub-clustering of endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and myeloid cells (Figure S4D). Subpopulation annotation indicated that, apart from mast cells and neutrophils—which were significantly enriched in pediatric tumors—the relative proportions of other cell subtypes showed no statistically significant differences between pediatric and adult groups (p > 0.05) (Figure S4E; Table S3).

Deciphering the stromal changes of compositions and functions in the `tumor ecosystem

We next investigated stromal cells, as shown in Figure 2A, two major cell types were identified in the stromal compartment: endothelia and fibroblasts. Endothelia were re-clustered into five subpopulations: arterial endothelia (endo_art, HEY1, GJA5, GJA4), venous endothelia (endo_vein, SELE, ACKR1, VCAM1, RGS5), capillary endothelia (endo_cap, CA4, RGCC, HLA-DPA1, HLA-DPB1), lymphatic endothelia (endo_lym, CCL21, LYVE1, CD36, PROX1), and tip endothelia (endo_tip, RGCC, CXCR4, PXDN, ESM1, LOX, INSR) (Figures 2A; Figures S5A and S5B).25,26 According to the most variable genes, fibroblasts were further clustered into two subpopulations: fibroblast subtype and pericyte subtype. Fibroblast subtypes included fibro_myo1, fibro_myo2 (COL11A1, POSTN, MMP14, DCN), fibro_infla (C3, C7, CXCL12, CXCL14), and pericytes expressing PDGFRB but not PDGFRA, included immature (peri_imma: RGS5, CD36) and differentiated pericytes (peri_diff: MYH11, MYLK, TAGLN) (Figures 2A; Figures S5A and S5B).27

To explore the difference in stromal compositions between distinct ecosystems, we analyzed the distribution of stromal subpopulations, and we found that the proportions of endo_tip, endo_art, peri_imma, and fibro_myo1 were significantly higher in tumors than in non-cancerous tissues, while endo_lym, endo_cap, endo_vein, peri_diff, and fibro_infla were more common in non-cancerous tissues (Figure 2B). Consistent with previous reports, we found higher expression of extracellular matrix genes, including POSTN, MMP14, COL1A1, and COL1A2, in the fibroblast subtype than in the pericyte subtype (Figure S5A). Importantly, after performing gene enrichment analysis, we found that cell adhesion and migration related processes were enriched in the pericyte subtype in tumor, while extracellular matrix related processes were enriched in fibroblast subtype (Figures 2C; Tables S4; S5), suggesting distinct function of these two subtypes in the pediatric PTC progression.

To verify the characteristics of endothelial subclusters and decipher the molecular relationship between them and epithelia, a crosstalk network was constructed using ligand-receptor (LR) pair inference. Strong interactions about VEGFR-related pairs were observed, such as VEGFA-VEGFR1, VEGFA-VEGFR2, VEGFA-VEGFR1R2, and VEGFB-VEGFR1 (Figure 2D). Besides, a stronger interaction of NOTCH-associated LR pairs (JAG1-NOTCH1 and JAG1-NOTCH4) was observed between fibroblast and endo_tip cells in tumor than non-cancerous tissues (Figure 2E). Since VEGFR2, VEGFR1, NOTCH1 and NOTCH4 serve as key receptors for angiogenesis factors, we analyzed their expression between lesion sites, and found that all of them had a higher expression in primary tumor tissues (Figure S5C). The above results indicated that endo_tip cells might be a key promoter of angiogenesis in pediatric PTC.

Diversity of T/NK cells and myeloid in pediatric PTC

As the cellular composition landscape showed that T/NK cells were the dominant component in TME of pediatric PTC (Figure 1D), we next explored the function of T/NK cell subtypes, and found that these cells can be further sub-clustered into 13 subpopulations, indicating the diversity of T/NK cells (Figure 3A). Specifically, CD4+ T cells consisted of regulatory T cells (CD4_Treg, FOXP3), naive T cells (CD4_Tn, CCR7, TCF7, LEF1, SELL), follicular helper T cells (CD4_Tfh, CXCL13, CD200, TOX) and memory T cells (CD4_Tm, IL7R, GPR183). Some CD4 T cells (CD4_Treg, CD4_Tfh) expressed strong exhausted signatures (TIGIT, LAG3, PDCD1, CTLA4), suggesting their exhaustion state and immunosuppressive functions in pediatric PTC (Figure S6A). CD8+ T cell clusters consisted of exhausted T cells (CD8_Tex, LAG3, HAVCR2, PDCD1), naive T cells (CD8_Tn, CCR7, TCF7, LEF1, SELL), cytotoxic T cells (CD8_Teff, GZMA, GZMK, IFNG), effector memory T cells (CD8_Tem, IL7R, ZNF683, GZMK), resident memory T cells (CD8_Trm, TCF7, ZNF683, XCL1, IL7R), natural killer T cells (GZMA, GZMB, GZMH) and proliferative T cells (prolifer_T, PCNA, CCNB2, MKI67, TYMS) (Figure S6A). Natural killer cells included two subtypes (NK1, NK2) expressing GZMA, GZMB, GZMH, TYROBP, KLRB1, KLRD1. We noticed that CD8 T cells (CD8_Tex, CD8_Tem, CD8_Teff) showed the strongest cytotoxic-related genes (GZMA, GZMK) with relative levels of exhausted signatures (TIGIT, LAG3, PDCD1), indicating the cellular heterogeneity and dynamic evolutionary process of CD8 T cells (Figure S6A).

To explore the difference in T/NK cell compositions between lesion sites, we checked the distribution of the subpopulations. Compared with adjacent non-cancerous tissues, the proportions of NK2 and CD4_Treg cells in tumor were increased (Figure 3B). To better understand the function of NK cells, the enrichment analysis (NK1 vs. NK2) based on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Table S6; Figure S6B) was performed and showed differences in pathways, including cell killing, cytotoxicity and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Figure 3C). To further explore the functional difference of CD8/NK cells between lesion sites, we used exhaustion and cytotoxicity signatures to evaluate CD8/NK cells (Table S7). The results showed no significant difference at the sample level except the cytotoxicity score of CD8_Tem (Figure S6C).

Similarly, we analyzed the components and functions of myeloid in pediatric PTC. A total of 13,768 myeloid are clustered into 13 subclusters, including four macrophages (macro1, macro2, macro3, macro4), one neutrophil (neutro), two monocytes (mono14, mono16), mast, one plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC), and three conventional dendritic cells (cDC1, cDC2, cDC3) according to the canonical expression markers (Figures 3A; Figure S6A). Most of the myeloid cells were from the tumor (81.3%), implying that the myeloid cells were remodeled in TME (Figure 3A). 68.6% of myeloid cells were macrophages (Table S8), including four subpopulations expressing different functional scores (Figure 3D) and signaling pathways (Figure 3E). Specifically, macro1 expressed high levels of proinflammatory chemokines (CCL3L1, CCL4L2, CX3CR1), and had higher M1 scores and lower level of M2 markers, which was enriched in interferon-associated functions. Macro2 can be defined by the expression of SPP1, APOC1, and APOE, and showed high activity in cholesterol homeostasis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), KRAS, PI3K-AKT-MTOR pathways, which promoting tumor cell migration and invasion. Macro3 highly expressed anti-inflammatory markers, including C1QA, C1QB, C1QC, and showed highest phagocytosis scores. Interestingly, macro4 showed the highest angiogenesis scores, which were enriched in relevant pathways (angiogenesis and TGF beta signaling). These results demonstrated that the tumor microenvironment (TME) of pediatric PTC had a high density of tumor-associated macrophages, which may promote tumor invasion and metastasis.

Different regulatory patterns may form a more immunosuppressive phenotype in pediatric PTC

To elucidate the cellular and molecular regulatory mechanisms in CD8+ T/NK cells, we constructed communication networks and detected potential crosstalk between epithelial, immune, and stromal cells.28 Compared with normal tissues, the interactive number and strength between epithelia and CD8+ T/NK cells slightly increased. The total number of interactions between stromal subtypes and CD8+ T/NK cells increased, while the interactive strength decreased, especially fibro_infla, endo_cap, endo_lym and endo_vein (Figure 4A). Expressions of NECTIN2 and NECTIN3 in tumor cells could promote T cells exhaustion by binding to inhibitory receptors of T cells (TIGIT) (Figure 4B).29 Upregulation of chemokines such as CXCL12 in stromal cells can bind to CXCR4 of CD8+ T/NK cells regulating immunity, especially CD8_ Teff and CD8_ Tem cells (Figures 4C–4F).30 The comparison of TIGIT in CD8+ T/NK cells between lesion sites showed no significant difference, however higher CXCR4 expression was observed in non-cancerous tissues (Figure 4G). Considering the complex regulatory relationships between macrophages and T cells in previous studies, we analyzed the cellular interactive LR pairs, and found the CXCL16-CXCR6 (Figures S7A–S7C) and CD86-CTLA4 (Figure S7D) pairs, which may play an important role in recruiting CD8_Tex cells to TME. SPP1 uniquely secreted by macro2 binds to CD44 or ITGA4/ITGB1 of CD8+ T/NK cells, thereby regulating multiple T cell functions, including T cell activation, proliferation, differentiation, trafficking, and migration (Figure S7B).31,32 These results indicated that the intercellular communications of tumor and adjacent non-cancerous immune microenvironments are dynamic and mainly rely on the crosstalk of several specific cell types with important functions.

The heterogeneity of epithelia in pediatric PTC

To explore the heterogeneity of epithelia, we analyzed their transcriptomic profile and performed re-clustering analysis. The results showed that epithelia can be divided into eleven groups (Figure 5A; Table S9), and the UMAP plot clearly differentiated the origin of cancerous or normal cells (Figure 5A). Compared with cells from adjacent non-cancerous tissues (epi1, epi5, epi9), the epithelia in primary tumor tissues showed markedly lower TDS (Figure 5B). Furthermore, malignant epithelial subpopulations displayed various degrees of TDS scores, indicating heterogeneity of cancerous cells. We next detected the function difference of epithelial subpopulations by GSVA, and the results showed that epi4 and epi11, with the lowest TDS score, had higher activities in malignancy-related pathways, such as Wnt/beta-catenin, P53, PI3K-AKT-mTOR, NOTCH, EMT, and angiogenesis pathways (Figure 5C). The GSVA and TDS scoring analysis revealed the heterogeneity of these epithelia.

To explore the interaction between interstitial and epithelia, we analyzed the LR pairs interactions. The communication probabilities of pairs associated with NOTCH, EGFR, EPHA, and EPHB pathways targeted epithelia were stronger in primary tumor tissues than non-cancerous tissues, which were associated with tumor growth and invasion (Figure 5D).33,34,35 Compared with non-cancerous tissues, fibroblasts from tumor tissues, especially pericytes, showed a stronger connection with epithelia by secreting TNFSF12. Previous study indicated that TNF bond to TNF receptor superfamily member and activated the NF-kB signaling pathway, which played an important role in immune, inflammatory and oncogenesis processes.36 Next, we compared the expression level of above receptor genes between tumor and adjacent non-cancerous tissues, and found that the expression of EPHA receptors (EPHA2, EPHA4) in epithelial was higher in primary tumor tissues (Figure 5E).

To further elucidate the potential mechanism in the initiation and development of pediatric PTC, we applied transcriptional trajectory analysis, and the changes in gene patterns involved in cancer cell state transitions were dissected in 7 patients with paired tumor and para-tumor tissues, respectively (Figures S8A and S8B). We defined the highest TDS score point as the pseudotime start point (Figure 5F), and the well-known differentiated markers TG and TPO of TC were highly expressed at the pseudotime start point (Figure 5G). Then, we focused on the 52 differentially expressed genes associated with the states branchpoint to identify the key genes that may initiate tumorigenesis (Figure 5H). After removing genes with an absolute correlation coefficient (correlation between gene expression level and TDS score) less than 0.5, we got 13 genes (Table S10). Among these genes, the expression of FXYD5 was increased along with the pseudotime trajectory (Figure 5I). Moreover and importantly, FXYD5 was highly expressed in thyroid tumors (Figure 5J) and associated with worse clinicopathological features (such as more LNM) (Table S11) and poor prognosis in the TCGA-PTC cohort (Figure 5K). While the other 12 genes were not significantly correlated with prognosis (Figure S9). After further correlation analysis with TCGA-PTC RNA-seq data, we found several significant negative associations between FXYD5 and thyroid differentiation markers (such as DIO1, DIO2, FOXE1, GLIS3, PAX8, SLC264A, TG and TPO) and many markers (such as DIO1, DIO2, FOXE1, GLIS3, PAX8, SLC264A, SLC5A8, TG, TPO and TSH) were higher in FXYD5 low group (Figure S10A; Table S12). And RT-qPCR results after gene knockdown using two siRNAs targeting FXYD5 confirmed the mRNA level changes of differentiation-associated markers, especially DIO1, TSHR, NIS, and TG (Figure S10B).

To further compare the transcriptomic differences in PTC between different ages, we integrated epithelial cells from 7 adult and 11 pediatric patients. To preserve the true biological characteristics of tumor heterogeneity, batch effect correction was not performed during the analysis (Figure S11A). First, we conducted pseudotime analysis on the epithelial cells from the 7 adult patients to identify key genes dynamically associated with the trajectory, ultimately obtaining 44 intersecting genes (Figures S11B and S11C). Notably, FXYD5 was not included in this set of common genes (Table S13), suggesting potential differences in expression patterns between pediatrics and adults. To further evaluate the potential association of these common genes with TDS, we performed correlation analysis. The results showed that the absolute correlation coefficients with TDS scores were generally below 0.5 (Table S13), indicating that these genes may not be significantly associated with the degree of dedifferentiation.

To more intuitively reveal the transcriptomic differences between pediatric and adult malignant epithelial cells, we performed differential enrichment analysis, identifying a total of 546 significantly DEGs, of which 440 were upregulated, and 106 were downregulated in the pediatric group (Table S14). Notably, FXYD5 exhibited a significant upregulation trend in pediatrics, suggesting it may play a potential regulatory role in specific biological processes of pediatric tumors. Functional enrichment analysis further revealed that upregulated genes in the pediatric group were significantly enriched in pathways related to cell adhesion and EMT (Figure S11D), implying that pediatric PTC may possess stronger invasive potential, which might be closely related to their unique tumor biological behavior.

FXYD5 promotes the progression of thyroid cancer

We next tested the effect of FXYD5 on the development of TC. We found that the cell proliferation sharply decreased after gene knockdown using two siRNAs targeting FXYD5 (Figure 6A), and FXYD5 had strong effects on cell proliferation, migration, and invasion of TC cell line TTA-1 and KHM-5M (Figures 6B–6D). Similarly, FXYD5 stably knockdown remarkably suppressed the cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion of TC cells (Figure 6E; Figures S12A–S12E). Compared with the KHM-5M xenografts in control groups, the cells infected by FXYD5 shRNA almost lost the ability of tumor formation in nude mice (Figure 6F).

We next performed global transcriptome analysis (RNA-seq) in cells with or without siRNAs targeting FXYD5, and identified 593 DEGs (275 upregulated and 318 downregulated) between siFXYD5-1 and siFXYD5-2 (Figures 7A and 7B; Table S15). To decipher the biological functions of the 593 DEGs, we performed GSEA and found that the top-ranked category was cell adhesion and apoptosis (Figures 7C; Tables S16 and S17). By performing western blot, we further verified the expression alteration of some key genes, including significantly reduced the expression of N-cadherin, SLUG, p-Akt (Ser473), and p-Akt (Thr308), and increased the expression of cleaved PARP, E-cadherin, and TWIST1 upon FXYD5 knockdown (Figure 6D). Taken together, the data demonstrated that the expression level of genes encoding molecules involved in apoptosis and adhesion was changed after FXYD5 knockdown. We next tested whether the cell growth inhibition induced by FXYD5 knockdown was attributed to cell apoptosis. After siRNA transfection in TC cells for 48 h, the proportion of cells in early and late apoptosis periods was significantly increased (Figure 6E). Taken together, these data showed that FXYD5 mediated the proliferation and migration of malignant thyroid epithelia via regulating the apoptosis and adhesion processes.

Discussion

Pediatric PTC is one of the most common endocrine malignant tumors in underage patients, which exhibits obviously different clinicopathological characteristics from adults. We collected 11 primary tumors and 8 adjacent non-cancerous tissues from 11 patients covering 145,530 cells, and presented a comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic characterization of pediatric PTC. Our in-depth analysis not only found the composition and function differences underlying tumor progression but also reconstructed complex cellular networks. We found that stromal components were totally different between tumor and adjacent non-cancerous tissues, especially that endo_tip cells were enriched in the tumor. Two major immune cell types, T/NK and myeloid, exhibited functional heterogeneity, and complex immunoregulatory molecules were detected, which may promote an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Moreover, we identified crucial gene FXYD5, along with the epithelia progression trajectory, and validated in vitro and in vivo. RNA-seq data from public databases confirmed that FXYD5 expression levels were associated with LNM and poor prognosis, and are negatively correlated with thyroid differentiation markers. RNA sequencing revealed that upon knockdown of FXYD5, adhesion- and apoptosis-related pathways were significantly upregulated. WB and qPCR results also indicated significant changes in the expression of related genes. Finally, by integrating adult scRNA-seq data, we found pediatric tumor cells highly express FXYD5 and show enrichment in tumor-associated pathways, suggesting a potentially higher malignant potential.

In integrating scRNA-seq data across multiple experimental batches, this study inevitably faces two major challenges: batch effects and imbalanced sample sizes. These factors not only compromise data comparability but may also interfere with the identification of genuine biological signals, necessitating careful consideration during result interpretation. Previous studies have indicated that batch effects and limited sample size (i.e., the number of cells and independent biological replicates) can lead to spurious cell annotation clusters, prevent the detection of true DEGs and influence statistical power, thereby impacting subsequent investigations into tumor heterogeneity and TME.37,38,39,40 In this study, we observed batch effects among different samples during the initial cell clustering and annotation. To mitigate this impact, we employed the RunFastMNN correction algorithm to enhance the validity of subsequent analyses. To prevent batch correction from obscuring tumor cell heterogeneity, we did not perform batch effect removal on the epithelial cells. Regarding sample size, after data quality control, we ensured that the number of cells per patient ranged between 5,379 and 20,778. Additionally, the number of replicates for both the tumor and adjacent non-tumor groups exceeded three (11 and 8, respectively). We will continue to expand our cohort to better uncover the characteristic atlas of pediatric TC.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are considered to be a heterogeneous entity that plays a very complex role in cancer.27,41,42,43,44 We identified five subtypes of fibroblasts, and the compositions were significantly different between tumor and non-cancerous ecosystems. CAFs participated in adhesion and growth-associated pathways, which seemed to contribute to tumor progression. CAFs have emerged as key players in enhancing pro-survival signals in BRAF-mutant melanomas, resulting in resistance to BRAF inhibition.45,46 And Yang Z et al. found that CAFs were primarily enriched in metastatic PTC.43 The tip endothelial subtype was a worthwhile focus, due to its essential role in angiogenesis.44,47 We identified five subtypes of endothelia that showed great heterogeneity,48 and among them, tip cells were mainly derived from tumor, which indicated increased angiogenesis activity in TME. We found that VEGFR-associated signals in tumors were stronger, mainly between epithelia and tip cells. And endothelia in tumor expressed higher levels of VEGF receptors (VEGFR1, VEGFR2, and VEGFR1R2). It may help to make individualized and precision therapy,49 and predict the effectiveness of multi-kinase inhibitor (MKI) drugs, such as sorafenib.50,51

TC is a traditional “cold” tumor, and immunotherapy is limited;52 however, it does not mean there are no immunological modulations.53 In previous studies, NK cells can be subdivided into CD56dimCD16hi and CD56brightCD16low, based on the expression levels of CD56 (NCAM1) and CD16 (FCGR3A).54,55,56,57,58,59 Our data showed that less cytotoxic NK2 expressed a lower level of FCGR3A compared with NK1, which may be relevant to the CD56dimCD16hi NK cells, showing a higher ratio in the tumor. Another important component of TME, macrophage, showed heterogeneity, and about four-fifths of them were from tumor. Among them, macro2 scored highest in M2 signature and enriched in EMT, KRAS, PI3K-AKT-MTOR pathways, and macro4 had the highest angiogenesis score and enriched in associated pathways, which were favored in tumor immune escape and progression.60 Among LR pairs between macrophage and CD8+ T/NK cells, SPP1-CD44/ITGA4+ITGB1 have been reported to be associated with immunosuppression and tumor metastasis.31,32 The prominent immunoregulatory LRs from epithelia were NECTIN2/3-TIGIT, which was a potential immunotherapy target.29 Combined blockade of TIGIT and PD-L1 therapy showed complete responses in neuroblastoma.57 Since the current efficacy of PD-1 blockade therapy is very limited in advanced TC,58 TIGIT might serve as a new immunotherapeutic target for TC. The interactions among components of TME may hold significant diagnostic and therapeutic value in pediatric TC. Further in-depth research is warranted in the future, such as the use of multiplex immunohistochemistry or co-culture systems.

Tumor may generate from a single tumor cell that can recapitulate the cellular diversity of the parental tumors.59 In this study, we found that epithelia from tumor can be clearly separated from “normal” epithelia of non-cancerous tissues, which is in line with previous studies.19,61 Unlike using copy-number variation analysis to differentiate malignant cells and normal epithelia,62,63 we established the trajectory start point according to tissue origins and TDS scores because the low frequency of somatic CNVs (27.2%) of PTC.64 Epi1, epi5 and epi9 were mainly derived from non-cancerous tissues, and well-differentiated gene such as TPO or TFF3 was one of the top10 genes. Thus, we assumed the highly TDS-scored state as the pseudotime start point. Epi4 was characterized by the upregulation of immune-related genes in the MHC family and enriched in inflammation-associated pathways. Study in nasopharyngeal carcinoma found an epithelial-immune dual feature in malignant cells, which was associated with worse clinical prognosis.63 And study in non-small cell lung cancer indicated such malignant cells may be associated with immunotherapeutic response.65 Further study was needed to elucidate their role in PTC.

Currently, single-cell studies on TC are increasingly focusing on adults, while research involving pediatric TC remains relatively scarce. One study included 5 adolescent and 6 adult PTC for single-cell sequencing, revealing significant differences in the TME between children and young adults (CAYA) and adults. Specifically, the immune microenvironment in CAYA tended toward an exhausted state, and endothelial cells might play a more prominent role in angiogenesis.22 Another single-cell sequencing study, which incorporated 4 pediatric and 8 adult PTC along with organoid technology, identified the critical role of ITGA2hi tumor cells in metabolic reprogramming and immune microenvironment regulation.23 Our findings show distinct similarities and differences compared to the aforementioned studies. When integrating public adult single-cell sequencing datasets, we observed compositional differences primarily in endothelia and fibroblasts at the sample level. However, no significant differences were found upon further sub-clustering and annotation. Similarly, we did not identify pediatric-specific tumor cell subpopulations. Nevertheless, direct differential enrichment analysis revealed an upregulation trend of tumor-associated pathways in pediatric tumor cells. Currently, there is no consensus regarding the pediatric TME and tumor heterogeneity at the single-cell level. Future studies require larger sample sizes and rigorous analytical pipelines to enhance statistical power and analytical efficacy.

FXYD5 has been linked to adverse clinicopathological characteristics and prognosis in multiple tumors.66 However, studies on TC are rare. Sato et al. performed immunostaining in 51 PTC, 10 FTC, and 31 ATC, and found that FXYD5 expression was higher in ATC and it was negatively correlated with E-cadherin expression.67 Recently, FXYD5 knockdown has been shown to reverse the sorafenib resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma through the Akt-mTOR signaling pathway.68 Previous studies have demonstrated that the Akt-mTOR pathway plays a crucial role in TC differentiation and tumor progression.69,70 Our study found that differentiation, EMT, and Akt/mTOR-related markers were changed after FXYD5 knockdown, but deeper regulation mechanisms are still unclear ,and more studies are needed. Radioiodine uptake assays can verify the impact of FXYD5 on TC differentiation, while future studies are warranted for validation. The lack of specialized pediatric models presents a significant barrier to the accurate assessment of risk in children and adolescents. In this study, we selected two widely used TC cell lines characterized by high proliferation rates to study the biological function of FXYD5 in pediatric PTC progression, which is more of an alternative strategy based on existing resources. Organoid technology and primary culture will provide powerful tools for further validation.

In the prognostic analysis, we found that FXYD5 was associated with poor overall survival (OS) in TC. However, pediatric representation within the TCGA TC cohort is scant.71 Despite several single-center retrospective studies having identified new oncogenic alterations and elucidated the correlations between molecular profiles and clinicopathological features, a comprehensive and universally recognized pediatric cohort remains unestablished.71,72

In summary, our study constructed a comprehensive landscape of pediatric PTC describing their characteristics and interconnected molecular networks, which suggests potential diagnostic and therapeutic implications. We depicted the evolution trajectory of epithelia and illuminated the potential effects of crucial gene FXYD5, regulating PTC progression in vitro and in vivo. This study will provide a solid foundation for further studies that aim to reveal the cellular and molecular basis of tumorigenesis and progression of pediatric PTC, and promote precise diagnosis and individualized therapy.

Limitations of the study

We acknowledge that our study has several limitations. First, the sample size in our study is limited, and further expansion of the study cohort, including multiple lesion sites and complete follow-up, is required. Second, samples for scRNA-seq must be dealt with as soon as possible, and we need to balance the batch effects and the heterogeneity. Third, there is still a lack of standard and reliable approaches to ensure accurate bioinformatic analysis, such as cell populations annotation, doublets removal, and trajectory starting point designation. At last, our study performed a series of bioinformatic tools to dissect the TME of PTC, while the functional validation of these findings is lacking. Further in vivo and in vitro experiments are awaited to elucidate these mechanisms in the future, including the establishment of a mature pediatric TC cell line and investigation into the underlying mechanisms between FXYD5, differentiation or dedifferentiation, and iodine uptake.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) for Clinical Research and Animal Trials of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University ([2021]100). Written informed consent was obtained from each patient and their parents prior to sample collection. All animal experiments were approved and performed according to the guidelines of the IEC for Clinical Research and Animal Trials of The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University ([2024]011).

Patients and collection of samples

Seven patients (age ≤18) with PTC who underwent surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, three at the Second Affiliated Hospital Zhejiang University, one at Guangzhou Women and Children Medical Center, from November 2021 to August 2022, were included in our study. In total, 19 fresh surgical specimens (11 primary tumors and 8 adjacent non-cancerous tissues) were collected. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE)-stained sections of each sample were carefully reviewed by two experienced pathologists to confirm the pathology. De-identified patient clinical information including demographics, tumor clinicopathologic characteristics, and treatment history were summarized in Table S1.

Method details

Tissue dissociation and preparation of single-cell suspension

The fresh pediatric papillary thyroid cancer samples were obtained during the surgeries in size of 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm, and were washed with 1×PBS (Gibco) three times. Each sample was cut into 1–2 mm pieces and enzymatically digested with 5 mL digestion medium (4.7 mL RPMI 1640, 200 μL Enzyme H, 100 μL Enzyme R, 25 μL Enzyme A) according to Miltenyi Biotec Tumor Dissociation Kit (# 130-095-929) for 30 min at 37°C with agitation. After digestion, samples were sieved through a 70-mm and a 30-mm cell strainer in turn (Miltenyi Biotec, # 130-098-462, # 130-098-458), and centrifuged for 7 min at 500 g in 4°C. Single cell suspensions were run through to remove RBCs and debris according to the manufacturer specifications (Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution (# 00430054, eBioscienceTM), the Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, # 130-090-101)). Pelleted cells were then re-suspended in RPMI 1640 with 1% FBS (NEWZERUM, FBS-CE500) and assessed for viability and size using a Countess instrument (Thermo).

Single-cell RNA sequencing library preparation and sequencing

The cell suspension of each sample was subjected to the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Reagent Kits (10× Genomics, v2 chemistry) for library preparation according to the standard protocols (https://www.10xgenomics.com/support/single-cell-immune-profiling/documentation/steps/library-prep/chromium-single-cell-5-reagent-kits-user-guide-v-2-chemistry-dual-index). The single cell libraries were sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 Systems using paired-end sequencing. The gene-barcode matrices were generated by Cell Ranger toolkit (v7.0.1),75 which aligned the droplet-based sequencing data against GRCh38-2020-A human reference genome (https://www.10xgenomics.com/support/software/cell-ranger/latest/release-notes/cr-reference-release-notes) and counted the unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) for each cell.

Single-cell RNA sequencing quality control and preparation

All the scRNA-seq data were loaded and merged into one object with Seurat (v4.3.0).76 The following standards ruled out low-quality cells: low feature counts (<200–400), high feature counts (>4,000–9,000), or high mitochondrial content (>8–35%) (according to different tissue samples). The ribosomal and mitochondrial genes were removed, and the top 2,000 variably expressed genes were identified by the FindVariableFeatures function with “vst” option. We identified the potential doublets as simultaneously expression of marker genes from two or more major cell groups and removed for further analysis. Batch effects among the samples were conducted by using RunFastMNN function in SeuratWrappers package (v0.3.0).77 The built-in SCTransform algorithm processed the Seurat object to perform sample integration, gene normalization, and data scaling. The “RunPCA” function was used to reduce dimension, and the cells were visualized by using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embedding.

Cell clusters annotation

Cell type recognition was verified by cluster-specific differentially expressed genes. The clusters were annotated as seven major cell types based on their average gene expression of canonical markers, including B cells (CD79A, CD79B, and CD19), plasma cells (MZB1, SDC1, CD79A, IGHG1), T cells (CD3D, CD2, CD3E, CD3G and CD8A), epithelia (KRT5, KRT15, KRT18, KRT19, and EPCAM), fibroblast (DCN, COL1A1, and COL3A1), myeloid (LYZ, MS4A6A, and CD68), and endothelia (PECAM1, EGFL7, and VWF). To further identify sub-clusters and annotate them as cell subtypes in each major cell type, the abovementioned steps (normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering) were repeated for each major cell type. Due to the transcriptional similarities between NK and T cells, we conducted the second round of clustering to distinguish T and NK cells. After that, we conducted the “high-resolution” clustering to identify the finer subclusters within each major cell types. Procedures of the second round of clustering were identical to the first one.

Identification of signature genes for cell clusters and differential and enrichment analyses

To define marker genes for each subcluster within the seven major cell types, the FindAllMarkers function implemented in Seurat with default two-sided nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare all genes expression files of one sub-cluster with those of the other subclusters, which will generate significant differentially expressed genes with Bonferroin-adjusted p value lower than 0.05 and an average natural logarithm fold-changes of expression at least 0.25 and expression in above 10% of corresponding subcluster.

DEGs (differential expressed genes) were identified by adjusted p value <0.05 and |average log2FoldChange| > log2(1.5). The enrichment analysis was performed under Gene Oncology gene sets released by MSigDB78 according to clusterProfiler (v4.6.2)79 and ReactomePA (v1.42.0).80 Pathways with p value <0.05 and |NES| > 1 (GSEA, gene set enrichment analysis) were considered as significantly enriched. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed when estimating functional difference among cell subpopulations.81

Signatures scoring and deconvolution analysis in scRNA-seq dataset and bulk RNA-seq profiles

Seurat AddModuleScore function was applied to quantify thyroid differentiation score (TDS), cytotoxicity, exhaustion, M1, M2, angiogenesis, and phagocytosis scores in our scRNA-seq dataset.60,64 We calculated the mean abundance levels of the abovementioned gene lists from individualized level. Deconvolution analysis of the TCGA bulk RNA-seq data against our scRNA-seq dataset was conducted using the CIBERSORT (v0.1.0) package with default settings.82 The deconvoluted cell-type composition of each bulk sample was then utilized for group comparisons (into 2 groups, according to the lower 33% percentile, n = 163 or 326). All the above gene sets were shown in Table S7, and the clinical data of TCGA thyroid cancer cohort was shown in Table S18.

Cell-cell interaction analysis

To investigate the potential interactions between different cell types in the TME of pediatric PTCs, we performed cell-cell interaction analysis using CellChat (v1.6.1),83 which integrates an available repository of ligand-receptor (LR) pairs and a statistical framework. Interaction networks between all cell clusters were investigated. The p value and interaction possibilities were obtained through the statistical framework of CellChat.

Pseudo-time trajectory inference

We use Monocle2 (v2.28.0) to perform pseudotime trajectory inference.84 Briefly, we transformed the Seurat object into the Monocle2 required “cell_data_set”. The pseudotime trajectory was inferred by the “learn_graph” and “order_cells” functions using DDRTree. As the adjacent non-cancerous thyrocytes have been separated and clearly defined in our study, we set these cells with the highest TDS level as the root state and performed the “order” function in Monocle2. The DEGs (differentially expressed genes) changed along with the pseudotime were identified using the differentialGeneTest function in Monocle2.

RNA-sequencing and data analysis

Cell-isolated total RNA was reversely transcribed to cDNA library and purified by the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA), and then sequenced by Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with paired end reads of 150 bp. The clean reads were aligned to the human genome (GRCh38, NCBI) using STAR (v2.7.8a).85 The reads numbers mapped to each gene were counted using RSEM (v1.3.3).86 The FPKM of each gene was then calculated based on the length of the gene and the reads count mapped to it. Differential expression analysis of two groups, each with three biological replicates per condition, was performed using the limma package (v3.54.1).87 To minimize the false discovery rate, the resulting p values were adjusted using Benjamini and Hochberg approach. An adjusted p value less than 0.05 with |log2(FoldChange)| ≥log2(1.5) was set as criteria for significantly differential expression. We used clusterProfiler (v4.6.2) and ReactomePA (v1.42.0) to test the statistical enrichment of differential expression genes in GO/KEGG/HALLMARK pathways.79,80 We calculated the average values of log2(FoldChange) of 2 siRNAs against FXYD5 to conduct enrichment analysis.

The bulk RNA-seq profiles were integrated from the TCGA thyroid cancer dataset. The DEGs between tumors and adjacent non-tumors were calculated by limma (v3.54.1). Survival analysis was performed using the “survival” package (v3.4.0) in R, and we excluded patients whose overall survival less than 3 months and divided the remaining 489 samples into 2 groups according lower 33% percentile of gene expressing levels (n = 163 or 326). The thyroid differentiation score (TDS) based on the expression profiles of 16 thyroid function-related genes (DIO1, DIO2, DUOX1, DUOX2, FOXE1, GLIS3, NKX2-1, PAX8, SLC26A4, SLC5A5, SLC5A8, TG, THRA, THRB, TPO, TSHR) was used to quantify relationships between thyroid differentiation and diverse genetic or epigenetic events.64

Cell culture

Thyroid cancer cell lines TTA-1 and KHM-5M were purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China). These cell lines were used for detecting the gene knockdown, and subjected to a series of functional assays, including cell proliferation, colony formation, cell migration and invasion. The TTA-1 cell was cultured in DMEM medium (Corning, 10-040-CVRC) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, NEWZERUM, FBS-CE500). The KHM-5M cell line was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Corning, 10-040-CVRC) with 10% FBS. All cells were incubated under the condition of 37°C and 5% CO2 (Thermo, Mass., USA). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat analyses and routinely tested for mycoplasma.

Cell transfection

Two siRNAs against FXYD5 (siFXYD5-1 and siFXYD5-2) and the scramble siRNA (named as siNC hereafter) were synthesized by GenePharma. Two specific short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) against FXYD5 (shFXYD5-1 and shFXYD5-2) and the scramble shRNA (named as shNC hereafter) were cloned into pLVX-Puro vector by TSINGKE. The sequences of all RNAs are listed in Table S14. The TTA-1 and KHM-5M cells were plated in 6-well plates and cultured in an incubator for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2 until the cell confluence reached 30–50%. Transfections were executed by Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher, L3000015) or Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, 13778150) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded in triplicate in 96-well plates at a density ranging from 1 500 to 2,500 cells per well. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (DOJINDO, CK04) was performed daily to evaluate cell viability. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Transwell assay

The transwell was pretreated using Matrigel (Corning, 356234). In brief, Matrigel was diluted with DMED or RPMI-1640 at 1:15 and added to the upper chamber (Falcon, 353097), set for 0.5h. A sufficient number of cells in good growth conditions were collected and diluted to the same concentration in an FBS-free medium. The sterile transwell was placed into the small wells of a 24-well plate, 150 μL cell suspension of equal concentration was introduced to the top chamber, and 600 μL complete media supplemented with 10% FBS was introduced to the bottom chamber. The transwell was placed into the cell culture incubator. Later, the transwell was gently rinsed thrice in PBS, placed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and fixed for 30 min. The transwell was removed using a cotton swab, and the non-invaded cells were carefully scraped from the top chamber. The transwell was subsequently washed thrice in PBS. The lower chamber surface of the transwell was soaked in a crystal violet staining solution for about 20 min, and the transwell was subsequently rinsed thrice using water and dried at room temperature. 12 fields were randomly chosen and photographed, and cell number within the photographs was statistically analyzed using ImageJ (v1.48). The cell number within each field of view was counted.

Monolayer colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in triplicate on 6-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well. After 8 to 15 days of culture, cell colonies were washed with PBS, fixed in absolute methanol for 15 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet and photographed.

Cell apoptosis analysis

Cells were seeded on 6-well plates and transfected with siRNAs for 48h as indicated. The Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (KeyGEN, KGA108) was used. The percentage of apoptotic cells were determined by flow cytometry, analyzed and visualized by FlowJo (version 10 for Windows, BD, Ashland, OR, USA).

Western blotting

Cell lysates were prepared by supplementing RIPA buffer (Solarbio, R0010-100) with protease inhibitor (Bimake, B14002) and phosphatase inhibitor (Bimake, B15002). The protein concentration of the lysates was determined using the BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, PC0020-50T). For SDS-PAGE, 20–80 μg of protein extract was loaded. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, IPVH00010) and then blocked with 5% skim milk (Solarbio, D8340-500g) in Tris-buffered saline (Sangon Biotech, B640010-0001) containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T, Sangon Biotech, A600560-0500) for 2 h at room temperature. The membranes were sliced according to their molecular weight and incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were then washed with TBS-T and further incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (CST, 7076S, 7074S) for 1.5 h at room temperature. Finally, the signals were detected using Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Beyotime, P0018AS) and visualized on a GE AI600 imaging system.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018CN) and reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (ThermoFisher, K16225). Gene expression was analyzed in triplicate using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (ThermoFisher, A25742-10) on the Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System. Relative expression of each gene was calculated according to the 2−ΔΔCt method. ACTIN was used as an internal control. Primers used for qRT-PCR were listed in Table S19.

Xenograft tumorigenesis assay

All animal experiments were approved and performed according to the guidelines of the IEC for Clinical Research and Animal Trials of The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University ([2024]011). The 4–5 weeks old female BALB/c nude mice (n = 15) were purchased from the Animal Laboratory Center of The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, China) and housed in its specific pathogen free (SPF) barrier facilities. Mice were randomly assigned to three groups (five mice per group), and KHM-5M (2.5×106) cells with shNC or shFXYD5 were injected subcutaneously into the flank of nude mice. Tumor size was measured daily on the skin surface of the animal using a caliper and tumor volume was calculated using the formula (width2×length×0.5). Mice were sacrificed when xenograft tumors in the control group reached around 1 cm3, and tumors were surgically removed, photographed, and weighted.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed on R (v4.3.2). Two-tailed Student’s t test or Wilcoxon test was applied to determine the significance of the difference between two groups. And other analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0 for Windows; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Two-sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
