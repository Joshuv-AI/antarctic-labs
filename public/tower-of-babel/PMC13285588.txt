Real‐world, multi‐omics validation of the clinical relevance of molecular taxonomy for myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS)

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) are clinically and biologically diverse disorders, emphasizing the need for personalized treatment approaches. The International Working Group for Prognostication of MDS (IWG_PM) recently introduced a molecular classification, referred to as the MDS taxonomy, that categorizes patients into 16 subgroups based on 21 gene mutations, 6 cytogenetic abnormalities, and loss of heterozygosity (LOH) at TP53 and TET2 loci. This study sought to validate and enhance the clinical relevance of the MDS taxonomy by analyzing a large retrospective cohort (n = 5136) and transcriptomic data from a prospective cohort (n = 477). The taxonomy successfully identified subgroups with distinct clinical characteristics and disease progression patterns. However, incorporating gene interactions from taxonomy subgroups did not improve the prognostic performance of the Molecular International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS‐M). We further assessed whether the taxonomy could guide management in patients receiving disease‐modifying therapies. Except for the “TP53‐complex” subgroup, taxonomy classifications were not predictive of hypomethylating agent response or transplant outcomes. Nonetheless, they correlated with overall survival, suggesting that while both IPSS‐M and the taxonomy capture disease biology, other non‐genetic factors may influence treatment response. RNA sequencing confirmed the biological distinctiveness of the taxonomy groups. Transcriptomic profiling of CD34+ bone marrow cells revealed unique, homogeneous gene expression patterns, particularly within the AML‐like, biTET2, SF3B1, and TP53‐complex subgroups. Further integration of multi‐omics data may refine MDS classification, improving clinical decision‐making and guiding the development of targeted therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Myelodysplastic syndromes/neoplasms (MDS) are characterized by ineffective hematopoiesis and increased risk of evolution into acute myeloid leukemia (AML). 1 , 2 Over the years, the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of myeloid neoplasms, primarily based on morphology has served as a status for diagnosis and classification, guiding treatment decisions, clinical trial eligibility, and drug approvals in patients with MDS. 3 , 4 , 5

Recent advancements in genome characterization have revolutionized the study of MDS. 2 High proportions of patients exhibit recurrent somatic mutations that drive clinical phenotype and disease evolution on an individual level. 6 , 7 Accordingly, there is a shift from traditional classification schemes, primarily based on morphological and clinical features, to next‐generation systems that integrate genomic data, providing a more accurate representation of the disease biology and better capturing clinical‐pathological entities. 6 , 7 , 8

In 2022, two distinct classification systems for MDS were released by the WHO and the International Consensus Classification (ICC) committees, incorporating new molecular information to identify specific entities. 9 , 10 In addition to the entity of MDS with del 5q, WHO and ICC systems introduced new genetically‐defined categories based on SF3B1 and TP53 mutations. 9 , 10 , 11 Both classifications still included morphologically‐defined categories which, however, fail to identify groups of patients with distinct genomic profiles. 8 , 12

To expand the integration of molecular features into the classification of MDS, the International Working Group for Prognostication of MDS (IWG‐PM) recently proposed a new molecular taxonomy based on 16 groups, obtained using information from 21 genes, 6 cytogenetic events, and loss of heterozygosity (LOH) at the TP53 and TET2 loci. 13 The groups varied in size from 0.5% to 14% of patients and were associated with distinct clinical phenotypes and outcomes. The authors proposed that this MDS taxonomy could serve as a basis for future classification schemas and translational therapeutic research. 13

In this project, leveraging a large retrospective real‐world population of MDS patients with genomic annotation along with treatment and outcome data, as well as transcriptomic information from a prospective patient cohort, we aimed to validate and expand the clinical relevance of MDS taxonomy by: (i) evaluating its predictive and prognostic value in patients receiving disease‐modifying treatments, including hypomethylating agents (HMA) and allogeneic stem cell transplantation (HSCT); (ii) assessing the biological homogeneity within genetically‐defined entities by analyzing the RNA‐sequencing profiles of isolated CD34+ bone marrow progenitor cells.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study populations

The study was conducted by GenoMed4All, Synthema, and the International Consortium for MDS (icMDS), with the support of EuroBloodNET. The Humanitas Ethics Committee approved the study (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04889729). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The study population consisted of two patient cohorts with available genomic data, allowing for stratification according to the MDS taxonomy.

The first cohort comprised an international retrospective dataset of 5136 patients. Inclusion criteria were age >18 years, a diagnosis of primary or therapy‐related MDS according to the 2016 WHO classification, 3 and availability of IPSS‐M–related variables 14 collected at diagnosis and prior to the initiation of any disease‐modifying treatment.

The second cohort consisted of 447 patients prospectively enrolled at Humanitas Research Hospital (Milan, Italy). Eligible patients had a diagnosis of either primary MDS or AML evolving from MDS, according to the 2016 WHO classification criteria. 3 In addition to clinical and genomic data, transcriptomic data from CD34+ bone marrow progenitors collected at diagnosis were available for this cohort.

All cases were subsequently reclassified according to the 2022 WHO and ICC criteria and then stratified using both IPSS‐R and IPSS‐M risk categories. 14 , 15

DNA/RNA sequencing

Mutation screening of genes related to myeloid neoplasms was performed on DNA from peripheral blood leucocytes or bone marrow mononuclear cells. 6 , 16 Oncogenic mutations with a minimum variant allele frequency (VAF) of 2% were considered. All patients included in the study had the necessary mutational data to allow proper classification according to the MDS taxonomy framework. Information on copy‐neutral loss of heterozygosity (cnLOH) events was not available; therefore, biallelic inactivation of TET2 and TP53 genes was inferred based on mutation number, VAF, and conventional karyotyping data.

RNA was extracted from CD34+ bone marrow progenitors purified by MACS microbead kit (Miltenyi Biotech). RNA was isolated through RNeasy Micro kit (Qiagen); quality control was performed with the Agilent 2200 TapeStation system (Agilent), and RNA with RIN > 7 were used for library preparation. Libraries were prepared by using the SMART‐Seq v4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit (Takara). Libraries were sequenced using the Novaseq. 6000 (Illumina) with paired‐end 150‐bp configuration and aligned against hg38. 17 Uniquely mapped read pairs were counted by featureCounts and used for gene expression analysis. 18 , 19 , 20 Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq. 2. 21 Genes with raw read counts <50 in less than 10% of samples were filtered out. The significance of differential gene expression among groups was calculated using likelihood ratio test including sequencing batch and blast count in the reduced model. Genes with false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.0001 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Raw read counts were normalized by DESeq. 2 by applying variance stabilizing transformation and used for the graphical representation after logarithmic transformation. Pair‐wise Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for all samples using differentially expressed normalized gene expression data. Hierarchical clustering was performed using the calculated Pearson correlation coefficients as the similarity matrix, using the average linkage method. To further explore the relationship between transcriptome and genotype, a random forest‐based classification was carried out using caret R package 22 to predict MDS taxonomy from differentially expressed genes after splitting the cohort in training (0.7) and validation (0.3) set (mtry = 34, cross fold number = 10, repeats = 3). ClusterProfiler package was used for gene pathway enrichment analyses. 23

Statistical analysis

Numerical variables were summarized by median and range; categorical variables were described with count and relative frequency (%) of subjects in each category.

Survival curves were estimated with the Kaplan–Meier method and differences among groups were evaluated by log‐rank test. Overall survival (OS) and leukemia‐free survival (LFS) were defined as the time between diagnosis and death (from any cause) or last follow‐up (for censored observations) and the time between diagnosis and AML evolution (if any) or last follow‐up (for censored observations), respectively. Cumulative risk of AML evolution was modeled using Fine‐Gray regression. When focusing on patient populations receiving a specific treatment, OS was calculated as the time between the start of treatment and death/last follow‐up. The probability of relapse after treatment was estimated considering treatment as a failure at the time of hematologic relapse according to standardized criteria. 24

Multivariable survival analyses were performed by Cox's proportional hazards regression models. We investigated the prognostic effect of MDS taxonomy co‐mutation patterns using multivariate proportional hazards Cox's models with interaction terms across the considered clinical endpoints. The discriminatory power of the predictive models and the relative goodness of fit were evaluated using Harrell's concordance index (C‐index). 25

All the analyses were performed using R software (4.4.1 version).

We applied hierarchical MDS taxonomy rules to reclassify the study population, 13 resulting in 18 subgroups with a distribution percentage akin to the original IWG‐PM cohort. The 16 molecular groups encompassed 83.5% (4286) of patients, relying on the presence or absence of mutations in 21 genes (ASXL1, BCOR, BCORL1, DDX41, DNMT3A, EZH2, FLT3, IDH1, IDH2, MLL, MYC, NPM1, SETBP1, SF3B1, SRSF2, STAG2, TET2, TP53, U2AF1, WT1, ZRSR2), 6 cytogenetic events (inv(3), complex karyotype, der(1;7), −7, del(5q), ‐Y), and LOH at the TP53 and TET2 loci. The molecularly not‐otherwise specified (mNOS) group (7.9%, n = 408) was characterized by the presence of other cytogenetic abnormalities and/or mutations in 29 additional recurrently mutated genes (>0.5%). The “No‐event” category (8.6%, n = 442) was defined by the absence of any recurrent drivers assessed with our technique and aligns with the percentage of patients reported in the original article (Table 1 and Supporting Information S1: Appendix Table S1). The redistribution of patients across different classification systems is shown in the Supporting Information S1: Appendix Figure S6.

The groups exhibited variability in both size (ranging from 0.5% to 20.1% of the cohort) and molecular complexity (with a median of 0 to 5 mutated genes per patient). They demonstrated associations with distinct demographic and clinical presentations (Age, sex, peripheral blood counts and transfusion dependence, Figure 1A), morphological, genetic characteristics and prognostication (percentage of bone marrow blast, cytogenetic risk according to IPSS‐R criteria 15 and IPSS‐M risk categories 14 Figure 1B), and patient outcomes (Overall survival and risk of leukemic evolution, Figure 1C,D).

Prognostic effect of MDS taxonomy

We aimed to explore the potential added prognostic value of MDS taxonomy with respect to conventional stratification according to IPSS‐R and IPSS‐M criteria.

We therefore calculated IPSS‐R and IPSS‐M in the study cohort at diagnosis. 14 , 15 Cytogenetic abnormalities were classified according to the IPSS‐R criteria. 15 For IPSS‐M calculation, gene mutations were considered as binary variables with the exception of TP53 (not mutated, mono‐allelic mutation, multi‐hit mutations) and SF3B1 (SF3B15q [SF3B1 mutation in the presence of isolated del(5q) only or with one additional aberration excluding −7/del(7q)], and SF3B1α [SF3B1 mutation without co‐mutations in BCOR, BCORL1, RUNX1, NRAS, STAG2, SRSF2, and del(5q)] 14 (Table 1).

We analyzed the probability of OS and risk of AML evolution for all MDS taxonomy subgroups (Figure 1C,D). In this analysis, patients who received HSCT were censored at the time of the procedure. MDS taxonomy categories showed significantly different probabilities of both OS and risk of AML evolution (ranging from 1.3 to 10.9 years P < 0.001, and from 0 to 19.1%, P < 0.001). The independent effect of MDS taxonomy on clinical outcome was maintained in a multivariable model including age, sex, neutrophil, hemoglobin, and platelet count as covariates (HR 1.37 [1.29–1.44], P < 0.001 for OS and HR 1.31 [1.22–1.39], P < 0.001 for risk of AML evolution). Analysis of overall survival without censoring at transplantation confirmed that the taxonomy retained its prognostic significance and that its performance is robust and not driven by transplant‐related censoring (Supporting Information S1: Appendix Figure S5).

The MDS taxonomy did not demonstrate superior performance compared to the conventional IPSS‐R and IPSS‐M scoring systems. Regarding OS, the concordance index (CI) for MDS taxonomy was 0.70 (0.69–0.72) versus 0.72 (0.71–0.73) and 0.78 (0.77–0.80) for IPSS‐R and IPSS‐M, respectively Considering leukemia‐free survival (LFS), the CI for MDS taxonomy was 0.69 (0.67–0.71) compared to 0.74 (0.72–0.75) and 0.83 (0.82–0.85) for IPSS‐R and IPSS‐M, respectively (Supporting Information S1: Appendix Figure S4). Additionally, IPSS‐M retained a significant effect on the prognostic stratification of MDS taxonomy groups (HR 1.47 [1.41–1.54] for OS and 1.67 [1.49–1.53] for LFS).

Next, we assessed whether incorporating information on gene interactions (as defined by the MDS taxonomy) could improve the prognostic performance of the IPSS‐M score. To this end, we developed two multivariable Cox models: one including IPSS‐M features alone and another incorporating gene interactions as model's covariates. These interactions terms were selected based on the interaction patterns described in the MDS taxonomy and observed in our patient's cohort. The inclusion of gene interactions did not result in a meaningful improvement in the CI of IPSS‐M, with only marginal increases of 0.01 for OS and 0.002 for LFS, compared to the reference model.

Predictive and prognostic effect of MDS taxonomy in patients receiving specific treatments

In the context of MDS, a growing number of patients undergo disease‐modifying therapies, such as Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation (HSCT) and Hypomethylating Agents (HMA, particularly for high‐risk individuals ineligible for HSCT). 4 , 5 Consequently, we aimed to evaluate whether MDS taxonomy can discern differences in the likelihood of response to specific treatments (predictive value) and effectively stratify the probability of post‐treatment survival (prognostic value) (Figure 2).

Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs reported for each group were derived from Cox proportional hazards models, using the “No‐event” group as reference. MDS, myelodysplastic syndromes/neoplasms; OS, overall survival.

To explore the predictive and prognostic value of these subgroups, we conducted analyses in two distinct populations from the cohort, one receiving HSCT and the other undergoing HMA treatment. In evaluating the predictive value of the classification, primary endpoints included the overall response rate (comprising complete response (CR), partial response (PR), marrow CR (mCR), and Stable Disease with Hematological Improvement (SD‐HI) based on the 2006 International Working Group (IWG) criteria) for patients treated with HMA, and the risk of disease relapse for those treated with HSCT. 24 Simultaneously, the prognostic value of these subgroups was examined based on the probability of OS calculated from the starting date of treatment in both cases.

We first assessed the predictive and prognostic impact of MDS taxonomy in a cohort of high‐risk MDS patients who received HMA according to the current therapy guidelines. 4 , 5 A total of 1915 subjects were included in the analysis.

The median duration of MDS prior to HMA initiation was 6.5 months (range: 1–119). Patients received a median of 4 HMA cycles (range: 1–21), with no significant differences observed across molecular subgroups (P = 0.96). The overall response rate, assessed after 4–6 treatment cycles, was 30.7%, with no significant variation among the different MDS taxonomy categories (P = 0.85). In the HMA‐treated population, the median OS following HMA initiation was 13.8 months (range: 1–119). MDS taxonomy had a significant impact on post‐HMA survival outcomes (P < 0.001). Patients in the SF3B1, DDX41, del(5q), and bi‐TET2 subgroups experienced more favorable outcomes, although without reaching statistical significance (median OS: 47.9, 44.2, 33.0, and 27.9 months, respectively; P values ranging from 0.17 to 0.96). In contrast, patients in the TP53‐complex and −7/SETBP1 subgroups showed significantly inferior survival, with median OS of 5.7 months (P < 0.001) and 10.1 months (P = 0.001), respectively (Figure 2A). In a multivariable model including age, sex, hemoglobin, neutrophil, and platelet counts, and bone marrow blast percentage as covariates, MDS taxonomy retained an independent and significant association with post‐HMA OS only in the TP53‐complex (HR 1.78; P < 0.001) and −7/SETBP1 (HR 1.70; P < 0.001) groups. In HMA‐treated patients, baseline clinical risk was heterogeneously distributed across taxonomy groups, with substantial differences in IPSS‐M risk categories and bone marrow blast burden (Supporting Information S1: Appendix Table S2), indicating that treatment allocation and outcomes are influenced by clinical factors beyond molecular taxonomy.

We then analyzed outcomes in patients who underwent HSCT (1317 out of 5094; 25.9%). The mNOS subgroup had the most favorable posttransplant outcome, with median OS not reached (P = 0.98). Conversely, the ZRSR2 and TP53‐complex groups showed the poorest survival after HSCT, with median OS of 10.2 months (P = 0.039) and 13.5 months (P < 0.001), respectively (Figure 2B). The prognostic relevance of MDS taxonomy post‐HSCT was further evaluated in a multivariable model including recipient age and sex, time from diagnosis to transplant, donor type, disease status at transplant (active/progressive disease vs. complete remission), and conditioning regimen (reduced‐intensity vs. standard) as covariates. In this analysis, MDS taxonomy was not independently associated with post‐HSCT OS (P = 0.64). However, relapse rates were significantly higher in the TP53‐complex group (36.1%; P < 0.001) (Figure 2C), and this association remained significant in multivariable analysis (HR 2.39; P = 0.001).

Transcriptomic profile of genetically‐defined taxonomy subgroups

The transcriptomic profile of CD34+ bone marrow progenitors was analyzed in a prospective cohort of 447 patients (demographic and clinical characteristics are provided in the Table 1).

Consistent with previous studies, 18 , 19 , 20 the CD34+ transcriptome reflected the distribution of hematopoietic progenitor cells within the hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell compartment, with genes highly expressed in erythroid and granulocytic myeloid precursors accounting for 33% of total variability. Supervised differential gene expression analysis identified 1187 genes (P < 0.001) that were differentially expressed across MDS taxonomy subgroups. Unsupervised clustering analysis demonstrated distinct gene expression trajectories among AML‐like, TP53‐complex, and SF3B1 subgroups, which showed a tendency to cluster separately (Figure 3A).

To further investigate the relationship between transcriptomic profiles and genotype, a random forest‐based classification algorithm was applied to predict MDS taxonomy from differentially expressed genes. The algorithm′s accuracy varied across subgroups, with recall rates of 94%, 88%, 75%, and 54% for AML‐like, TP53‐complex, SF3B1, and biTET2 groups, respectively (Figure 3B). Classification accuracy was notably lower for other subcategories, likely due to high transcriptomic heterogeneity or, in some cases, small sample sizes (e.g., the EZH2 plus ASXL1 and ZRSR2 groups, each with fewer than 10 patients, and DDX41 that is not represented in this cohort). Overall, these findings suggest that a clear distinct gene signature characterizes AML‐like, TP53‐complex, SF3B1, and biTET2 subgroup, while higher transcriptome heterogeneity was observed across other genetic entities (Figure 3C).

Noteworthy, pathway analysis found restricted gene‐set enrichment in these groups. Heme and porphyrins metabolism pathways were enriched for SF3B1 subgroup (Figure 3D). The TP53‐complex subgroup exhibited dysregulation of genes associated with interferon signaling, alongside pathways involved in platelet aggregation and activation. In contrast, innate immunity pathways—such as Toll‐like receptor signaling—were more specifically enriched in the bi‐TET2 subgroup. A markedly more heterogeneous set of enriched pathways was observed in the AML‐like subgroup, which included reactive oxygen species and inflammasome‐related pathways, uniquely associated with this group. Additionally, innate immunity and erythroid precursor pathways were identified in AML‐like subgroups overlapping with the bi‐TET2 and SF3B1 categories, respectively (Figure 3D). Overall, the AML‐like subgroup displayed a more intricate transcriptomic landscape, consistent with its classification as the final evolutionary stage of several lower‐risk, molecularly defined myeloid neoplasms.

DISCUSSION

The study of hematological neoplasms has been transformed by genomic annotation. While morphology remains a fundamental tool for diagnosing hematologic neoplasms and serves as a robust basis for future classification, it is now well recognized that dysplastic morphology results from dysregulation of blood cell production, differentiation, or survival that is itself driven by gene mutations or altered gene expression. 1 , 2 , 6 , 7 , 26 Somatic mutations and cytogenetic aberrations have already been incorporated into a robust risk‐stratification system for MDS, the IPSS‐M. 14 However, risk‐stratification predicts patient outcome in an incremental manner, without defining unique biologic disease entities. 14 , 26 The integration of molecular information into classification systems for hematological neoplasms has the potential to define distinct genomic subgroups with different therapeutic implications, responses, and outcomes; to identify biomarkers for disease monitoring; and to ultimately lay the foundation for personalized treatments. 7 Since genomic profiling has become progressively implemented into the diagnostic work‐up of hematologic neoplasms, disease classification is now evolving into an essential tool to drive personalized medicine. 7 , 12 , 27

Recently, the IWG‐PM established a refined molecular classification system, termed MDS taxonomy, to better reflect the underlying biology of these neoplasms. 13 MDS taxonomy validated the clinical relevance of 5 well‐established entities (MDS with SF3B1, TP53, del(5q), and AML‐like subtypes), confirmed the clinical significance of 3 previously reported groups (biTET2, CCUS‐like, and MDS with der(1;7)) and introduced 8 novel subgroups with distinct molecular features (−7/SETBP1, EZH2‐ASXL1, IDH‐STAG2, BCOR/L1, U2AF1, SRSF2, and ZRSR2). 13 In this study, we provided a large, independent confirmation that MDS taxonomy defines subgroups with distinct clinical features and outcomes, both in terms of natural history and within specific therapeutic subgroups.

Although the purpose of MDS taxonomy differs from that of prognostic models such as IPSS‐M, 14 both aim to inform clinical decision‐making. 13 , 14 , 26 We confirmed that IPSS‐M outperforms MDS taxonomy in predicting OS and AML transformation risk. Additionally, we assessed whether incorporating gene interactions as defined by MDS taxonomy could refine the accuracy of IPSS‐M. Our findings indicate that gene interactions featured in the MDS taxonomy do not significantly enhance the predictive accuracy of the conventional models. Instead, IPSS‐M effectively stratified patients within MDS taxonomy subgroups to different survival probabilities.

We next explored whether the MDS taxonomy could provide additional insights into the management of patients receiving disease‐modifying therapies, with the caveat that the retrospective nature of the available data may introduce potential bias due to variability in therapeutic decision‐making across centers and over time.

HMA is the only class of drugs approved for the treatment of higher‐risk MDS in patients ineligible for transplantation. 4 , 5 However, only 40%–50% of patients experience hematologic improvement, and complete response rate is 10‐15%. 28 Predictive models based on clinical features alone are not sufficiently conclusive to withhold HMA treatment from eligible patients, 29 , 30 and IPSS‐M has recently failed to stratify individual response probability—although OS was inversely related to IPSS‐M risk. 26 We observed that MDS taxonomy, despite incorporating genomic information in a different way with respect to IPSS‐M, did not predict individual response probability to HMA. These findings suggest that while genomic information captured by IPSS‐M and MDS taxonomy reflects the disease biology and the aggressiveness of MDS subtypes, additional factors beyond gene mutations can influence HMA sensitivity. 31 , 32

Allogeneic HSCT is the only potentially curative option for MDS patients, yet disease relapse remains the leading cause of failure. 4 , 5 Genomic data incorporated into IPSS‐M has been shown to predict relapse risk, thereby refining patient management. 26 , 33 MDS taxonomy did not significantly improve the identification of patients at high risk of transplant failure, except for the TP53‐complex group, which has been known to be associated with high relapse risk following HSCT. 34 , 35

The MDS taxonomy framework aims to provide a foundation for a more biologically refined classification of these diseases. In addition, it could serve as a stepping stone for future research to generate new hypotheses for the development of subtype‐specific treatments and may help refine inclusion criteria in next‐generation clinical trials, thereby identifying more homogeneous patient populations compared with conventional clinical stratification. 13

In this context, our study incorporated RNA sequencing as an orthogonal technology to further validate the biological distinctiveness of MDS taxonomy groups and the transcriptional consistency within each group. Although some molecular subgroups (e.g., DDX41, EZH2‐ASXL1, and ZRSR2) were underrepresented, transcriptomic profiling of CD34+ cells provided preliminary evidence supporting the biological relevance of the proposed taxonomy.

To investigate the relationship between transcriptome and genotype, we applied a random forest‐based classification algorithm using differentially expressed genes to predict MDS taxonomy categories. The AML‐like, bi‐TET2, SF3B1, and TP53‐complex groups showed recall rates >70%, while classification performance was lower in other subgroups, likely due to greater transcriptomic heterogeneity. Gene ontology analyses revealed significant pathway enrichment in specific MDS taxonomy categories: the SF3B1 subgroup demonstrated robust activation of genes involved in erythroid differentiation, consistent with previous findings. 18 , 19 , 20 The TP53‐complex group showed dysregulation of genes associated with interferon signaling, highlighting the central role of TP53 gene in modulating immune responses. 36 Recent multi‐omics single‐cell analyses of bone marrow cells in TP53‐mutated MDS further confirmed a specific immune dysregulation in the tumor microenvironment, characterized by myeloid‐driven inflammation and impaired antigen presentation, which may drive the dismal prognosis found in these patients. 17 Pathways related to innate immunity—such as Toll‐like receptor signaling—were specifically enriched in the bi‐TET2 group, supporting the emerging role of this gene in regulating immune homeostasis and contributing to clonal evolution in MDS. 37 Finally, the AML‐like subgroup displayed a markedly heterogeneous pattern of enriched pathways, including those related to reactive oxygen species and inflammasome activation—features uniquely associated with this group. 38 Notably, innate immunity and erythroid precursor signatures in AML‐like groups overlapped with the bi‐TET2 and SF3B1 categories, respectively, indicating a more complex and composite transcriptomic landscape that reflects the transitional evolutionary status of the AML‐like group from molecularly defined subtypes of low‐risk MDS. 39 , 40

Overall, these findings suggest that transcriptome heterogeneity varied across the genetic groups, with distinct gene signature evident for AML‐like, biTET2, SF3B1, and TP53‐complex subgroups. Further studies to validate these findings and refine multi‐omics integration approaches are warranted to establish a mechanistic classification of MDS that could be an innovative reference model to improve clinical decision making, optimize the design of clinical trials and potentially guide the development of innovative treatments. 7
