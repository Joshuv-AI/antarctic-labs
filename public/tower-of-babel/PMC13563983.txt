Artemisia argyi-enriched fermented soybean meal alleviates ETEC K88-induced intestinal injury in mice in association with gut microbiota remodeling and purine metabolic alterations
Authors: Weiwei Wang, Huakai Wang, Chenxiang Sun, Qianqian Chen, Yuqiang Zhang, Chengyi Miao, Shiqiang Zhu, Wei Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) K88 is an important enteric pathogen associated with diarrhea, intestinal barrier dysfunction, and gut microbial dysbiosis. Fermented soybean meal (FSBM) has shown potential for improving intestinal health, but its effects on ETEC-induced injury and the related microbiota–metabolite changes remain unclear.MethodsMale BALB/c mice were assigned to control, model, chlortetracycline, and FSBM groups. After ETEC K88 challenge, growth performance, diarrhea score, serum cytokines and immunoglobulins, colonic tight-junction gene expression, fecal physicochemical characteristics, cecal microbiota composition by 16 S rRNA sequencing, and cecal metabolomic profiles by untargeted LC–MS were analyzed.ResultsFSBM improved body-weight recovery and alleviated diarrhea in ETEC K88-challenged mice. It reduced serum TNF-α and IFN-γ levels, increased IL-10, IgA, and IgG levels, and upregulated colonic ZO-1 and ZO-2 expression. FSBM also decreased fecal pH, increased lactic acid and acetic acid levels, and promoted lactic acid bacteria proliferation. In addition, FSBM reshaped the cecal microbial community, notably enriching Lactobacillus and other beneficial taxa. Metabolomic analysis revealed a distinct metabolic profile in the FSBM group, with purine metabolism identified as the most prominent altered pathway.ConclusionsFSBM alleviated ETEC K88-induced intestinal injury in mice and was associated with alterations in gut microbiota composition and purine metabolism. These findings support the potential of FSBM as a functional nutritional strategy for improving intestinal health under enteric stress.

Background

Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC), particularly F4 (K88)-positive strains, is one of the major causes of post-weaning diarrhea, intestinal dysfunction, and growth retardation in young pigs [1]. After colonizing the small intestine through fimbrial adhesion, ETEC secretes enterotoxins that disrupt ion and water transport, impair epithelial barrier integrity, and induce mucosal inflammation and microbial dysbiosis [2]. In addition, the weaning transition itself is accompanied by abrupt nutritional, environmental, and social stressors that further compromise intestinal morphology, immune homeostasis, and microbiota stability, thereby increasing host susceptibility to enteric infection [3]. Against the background of reduced antibiotic use in livestock production, nutritional strategies capable of strengthening gut resilience and limiting pathogen-associated intestinal injury have attracted increasing attention [4].

Soybean meal (SBM) remains the dominant plant-protein ingredient in pig diets, but its application in newly weaned animals is constrained by soybean antigen proteins and other anti-nutritional factors [5]. In particular, glycinin and β-conglycinin can impair growth, damage intestinal barrier integrity, reduce goblet cell function, and disturb gut microbial balance in piglets [6]. Fermentation is therefore considered an attractive processing strategy because it can degrade anti-nutritional compounds, improve protein availability, generate organic acids and bioactive metabolites, and introduce beneficial fermentation-derived functional factors [7, 8]. Recent studies have shown that fermented soybean meal (FSBM) can improve growth performance, nutrient digestibility, intestinal barrier function, inflammatory status, and microbial composition in weaned piglets, including under ETEC K88 challenge conditions [9, 10]. However, although the beneficial effects of FSBM on intestinal health are increasingly recognized, the mechanistic links between microbial remodeling and downstream metabolite alterations remain insufficiently defined [11].

Artemisia argyi (A. argyi) is a traditional medicinal and edible plant rich in polysaccharides, polyphenols, flavonoids, and volatile constituents, and it has attracted renewed attention because of its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and gut-protective activities [12]. Emerging evidence indicates that A. argyi-derived polysaccharides can alleviate diarrhea by strengthening the mucus barrier and suppressing intestinal inflammation [13], whereas A. argyi polyphenols can attenuate colonic injury by remodeling gut microbiota and promoting intestinal fatty acid metabolism [14]. In feed animals, dietary supplementation with A. argyi has also been reported to reduce diarrhea incidence and modulate gut immune function in weaned rabbits [15]. Moreover, fermentation of A. argyi with Lactobacillus plantarum enhanced its anti-inflammatory effects in DSS-induced colitis models, suggesting that combining lactic fermentation with A. argyi may further potentiate its intestinal health benefits [16].

In previous work, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum subsp. plantarum ZA3 was identified as a promising strain with inhibitory activity against ETEC K88 [17]. It was further shown that co-fermentation of SBM with ZA3 and A. argyi improved fermentation quality and reshaped the microbial community during the fermentation process [18]. Based on these findings, it was hypothesized that ZA3- and A. argyi-associated FSBM could alleviate ETEC-induced intestinal injury in association with changes in host immune responses, barrier-related gene expression, gut microbiota, and microbial metabolites. Because the present work was designed as a preliminary mechanistic proof-of-concept study rather than direct validation in the natural host, an ETEC K88-challenged mouse model was used to evaluate host immune, barrier-related, microbial, and metabolic responses under experimental enteric stress. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of FSBM on diarrhea-like symptoms, immune and barrier-related responses, cecal microbial composition, and cecal metabolite profiles in vivo, and to provide preliminary evidence for its potential role as a functional nutritional strategy for improving intestinal resilience under enteric stress.

Materials and methods

Preparation of bacterial suspension and fermented soybean meal

Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) K88 was obtained from the China Veterinary Culture Collection Center (CVCC) and prepared as previously described [17]. Briefly, the strain was cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 12 h. The bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation (8,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), washed twice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in PBS to a final concentration of 1.0 × 109 CFU/mL.

Fermented soybean meal (FSBM) was prepared according to our previous protocol [18]. Briefly, soybean meal (SBM) was thoroughly mixed with 12% (v/w) Lactiplantibacillus plantarum subsp. plantarum ZA3 suspension (1.0 × 108 CFU/mL) and 8% (w/w) powdered Artemisia argyi. The mixture was packed into polyethylene bags (Dragon N-6, Asahi Kasei Co., Tokyo, Japan), vacuum-sealed, and incubated under anaerobic conditions at room temperature for 72 h. ZA3- and A. argyi-treated FSBM showed rapid acidification during fermentation, with the pH decreasing to approximately 4.75 at 24 h and viable lactic acid bacteria reaching approximately 10.6 log CFU/g fresh matter at 36 h. The fermented product also showed increased lactic acid accumulation, enrichment of Lactiplantibacillus, and reduced abundance of undesirable microorganisms such as Acetobacter and Enterobacter, indicating improved fermentation quality and microbial safety of the final fermented ingredient. Because the addition of A. argyi powder and solid-state fermentation may alter the nutritional composition of SBM, including crude protein, gross energy, crude fiber, and fermentation-derived metabolites, FSBM should be regarded as a fermented functional ingredient with a potentially modified nutritional matrix compared with unfermented SBM.

Animals and experimental design

All experimental procedures were authorized by the Committee for the Welfare of Laboratory Animals and the Ethical Review of Experiments at China Agricultural University (AW42405202-1-03) and were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Institute of Nutrition and Health, China Agricultural University.

Male BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, 20 ± 2 g) were obtained from the Henan Provincial Laboratory Animal Center and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle, a temperature of 23 ± 2 °C, and a relative humidity of 50% ± 10%. Food and water were provided ad libitum. After one week of acclimation, the mice were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 10 per group): (1) Control group, (2) ETEC model group, (3) chlortetracycline group, also known as aureomycin, and (4) FSBM group. In the present study, mice were used as a preliminary in vivo proof-of-concept model for mechanistic evaluation rather than as a natural-host validation model.

Mice in the model, chlortetracycline, and FSBM groups were orally gavaged with 200 µL of ETEC K88 suspension (1.0 × 109 CFU/mL) once daily at 9:00 a.m. for 7 consecutive days, whereas mice in the control group received an equal volume of sterile PBS. During the challenge period, all diets were prepared by grinding the standard commercial chow into powder and then mixing it with either unfermented soybean meal or fermented soybean meal. The basal diet consisted of 80% powdered standard chow and 20% SBM. For the FSBM group, the 20% SBM fraction was completely replaced by 20% FSBM, corresponding to an inclusion level of 200 g FSBM/kg complete diet. The chlortetracycline group received the basal diet supplemented with 0.2% chlortetracycline. Because mice were fed ad libitum and individual feed intake was not systematically recorded, the exact FSBM intake expressed as g/kg body weight/day could not be retrospectively calculated. Chlortetracycline was included as an antibiotic positive control rather than as the primary intervention. The inclusion level of 0.2% was selected based on previous Escherichia coli-challenged weaned-pig studies in which 0.2% chlortetracycline-containing diets were used as antibiotic reference treatments for evaluating growth performance, fecal score, and gut-health-related outcomes [19]. In addition, dietary chlortetracycline has been used in mouse studies to evaluate antibiotic-associated alterations in intestinal barrier function, inflammatory responses, and gut microbiota [20]. Therefore, this group was designed to provide an antibiotic benchmark for comparison with FSBM under ETEC K88-induced enteric stress. After the 7-day challenge period, mice were maintained on their respective diets for an additional 14 days. On day 22, mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg body weight). Blood samples, cecal contents, fecal samples, and colon tissues were collected. Cecal contents and serum samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. A segment of colon tissue was also snap-frozen for RNA extraction. For downstream 16 S rRNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomic analysis, five mice per group were selected from the ten mice in each group after sample collection. The selection was performed randomly among mice with complete physiological records and sufficient, uncontaminated cecal-content samples. Sample selection was not based on body weight, diarrhea score, or other physiological treatment responses. The same selected cecal-content samples were used for both 16 S rRNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomic analysis.

Assessment of physiological condition

Body weight was recorded every other day throughout the experiment using an electronic balance. From day 1 to day 21, mice were housed individually in cages lined with clean white paper to facilitate the observation of fecal consistency and perianal contamination.

Diarrhea severity was evaluated daily using a previously described scoring system [21]: 0, normal stool; 1, soft but normally shaped feces accompanied by reduced spontaneous activity and slowed weight gain; and 2, watery, loose, or mucus-containing feces accompanied by perianal soiling, decreased activity, and weight loss. Diarrhea scoring was performed daily by the same trained observer throughout the experiment to minimize inter-observer variation. The observer was blinded to the treatment allocation during scoring to reduce potential assessment bias. Fresh fecal samples were collected aseptically every 3 days and immediately processed or stored appropriately for subsequent analyses.

Determination of serum inflammatory cytokines and immunoglobulins

Blood samples were collected from the retro-orbital venous plexus under isoflurane anesthesia, and serum was obtained by centrifugation at 5,000 × g for 15 min. Serum levels of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) were determined using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Serum concentrations of immunoglobulin G (IgG), IgM, and IgA were measured using commercial turbidimetric assay kits. All kits were purchased from Beijing Dongge Boye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). For each ELISA assay, a standard curve was generated using the standards provided with the kit, and sample concentrations were calculated only when the absorbance values fell within the linear range of the standard curve. All serum samples were measured in duplicate, and assays with a standard-curve correlation coefficient of R² > 0.99 and an intra-assay coefficient of variation below 10% were considered acceptable. For immunoglobulin determination, calibration and quality-control procedures were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from colon tissues using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Residual genomic DNA was removed using a commercial DNase treatment kit, and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using a commercial reverse-transcription kit according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

The mRNA expression levels of tight junction-related genes, including ZO-1 and ZO-2, were quantified by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [22], with GAPDH used as the reference gene after confirming stable amplification across groups. Analysis of fecal pH, organic acids, and culturable bacterial populations

Three grams of fresh feces were homogenized with 27 mL of distilled water and filtered. The filtrate was used for pH measurement and organic acid analysis. Fecal pH was determined using a calibrated pH meter (Mettler Toledo, Greifensee, Switzerland). Organic acids were quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a Waters Alliance e2695 system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA).

For microbial enumeration, the fecal suspension was serially diluted 10-fold with sterile saline and plated in triplicate onto selective media. de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar was used for lactic acid bacteria, eosin methylene blue (EMB) agar for coliforms, and nutrient agar (NA) for total aerobic bacteria. Heat-resistant aerobic spore-forming bacteria were enumerated on NA after heat treatment of the diluted samples at 75 °C for 15 min. MRS plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 h, whereas all other plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 48 h. Results are expressed as CFU/g fresh feces.

16S rRNA gene sequencing and microbial bioinformatics analysis

To characterize the cecal microbial community, cecal-content samples from the five randomly selected mice per group described above were subjected to 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Total microbial DNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Fecal DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quality and concentration were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts and sequenced on an Illumina platform by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Raw reads were quality-filtered, merged, and clustered into OTUs at 97% similarity using UPARSE. Taxonomic assignment was performed against the SILVA 138 rRNA database. Alpha-diversity indices, including Shannon and Chao1, were calculated to evaluate microbial diversity and richness. Differences in overall microbial composition among groups were visualized by principal component analysis (PCA). Relative abundances of bacterial taxa at the phylum and genus levels were compared among groups, and differentially abundant genera were identified using the Kruskal–Wallis H test followed by Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction. Spearman correlation analysis was further performed to explore the relationships between the top 50 most abundant bacterial taxa and serum immune indices [23–26].

Untargeted metabolomic analysis of cecal contents

LC–MS analysis

Metabolite profiling was performed on a Thermo UHPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive high-resolution mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile/isopropanol (1:1, v/v) (solvent B). The gradient elution program is shown in Table 2. The injection volume was 2 µL, the flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. Samples were kept at 4 °C during the analytical sequence.

Mass spectrometric data were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes. The source conditions were set as follows: auxiliary gas heater temperature, 400 °C; sheath gas flow rate, 40 arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 30 arb; spray voltage, + 3.5 kV in positive mode and − 2.8 kV in negative mode. Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) was used for MS/MS collection, with a normalized collision energy of 20, 40, and 60 eV. The scan range was m/z 70–1050.

Data processing and statistical analysis of metabolomics data

Raw LC–MS data were processed using Progenesis QI software (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) for peak detection, retention-time alignment, deconvolution, and metabolite annotation. A pooled quality-control sample was used to monitor analytical stability during the LC–MS run.

Multivariate analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), were performed to evaluate metabolic differences among groups. The reliability of the OPLS-DA model was assessed using 100 permutation tests. Differential metabolites between groups were screened based on the criteria of variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1.0 and P < 0.05. Identified differential metabolites were further annotated using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), and pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database [28].

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), R software, and GraphPad Prism 10. Differences among groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. Changes in body weight over time were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Metabolite extraction

Untargeted metabolomic analysis of cecal contents was performed using the same five randomly selected cecal-content samples per group as those used for 16 S rRNA gene sequencing by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Briefly, 50 mg of each sample was extracted with 400 µL of methanol/water (4:1, v/v), homogenized, vortexed, and centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to LC–MS sample vials for analysis.

A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing equal aliquots from all biological samples. QC samples were analyzed together with the study samples and injected at regular intervals throughout the analytical run to monitor instrument stability and data reproducibility [27].

Results

FSBM improved growth recovery and alleviated diarrhea in ETEC K88-challenged mice

The effects of FSBM on body weight and diarrhea score are shown in Fig. 1. At the end of the ETEC K88 challenge period (day 7), mice in the model, chlortetracycline, and FSBM groups showed lower body weights than those in the control group. During the subsequent recovery period, mice receiving FSBM exhibited a progressive improvement in body weight relative to the model group. By day 21, the body weight of the FSBM group had recovered to 21.84 g, which was comparable to that of the chlortetracycline group (21.92 g). In contrast, body weight remained relatively stable in the control group throughout the experiment.

Consistent with the body-weight changes, diarrhea scores remained low and stable in the control group, whereas ETEC challenge increased diarrhea severity in the model group. FSBM supplementation alleviated diarrhea during the post-challenge period, as reflected by consistently lower diarrhea scores than those in the model group. Although the anti-diarrheal effect of FSBM was less pronounced than that of chlortetracycline, it clearly improved fecal condition compared with spontaneous recovery in the untreated model group.

FSBM modulated serum inflammatory cytokines and immunoglobulin levels

To evaluate the effect of FSBM on systemic immune status, serum cytokines and immunoglobulins were measured (Fig. 2). TNF-α levels differed significantly among groups, with the FSBM group showing the lowest concentration (343.52 pg/mL), significantly lower than those in the model and chlortetracycline groups (P < 0.05), while remaining comparable to the control group (Fig. 2a). In contrast, IL-10 levels were significantly increased in both the chlortetracycline and FSBM groups relative to the model group (P < 0.05), although they remained lower than those in the control group (Fig. 2b). Serum IFN-γ in the FSBM group was significantly reduced compared with the model group but remained significantly higher than that in the control group (Fig. 2c). Among the immunoglobulins examined, IgG levels were significantly higher in the FSBM group than in the model group and were comparable to those in the chlortetracycline group (Fig. 2d). No significant differences in IgM were observed among the four groups (Fig. 2e). In addition, IgA concentrations were significantly increased in the chlortetracycline and FSBM groups relative to the model group, although both remained lower than the control level (Fig. 2f). Collectively, these results indicate that FSBM partially reversed the immune disturbance induced by ETEC K88 challenge.

FSBM enhanced the expression of tight junction-related genes in colon tissue

The effects of FSBM on the mRNA expression of tight junction-related genes in colon tissue are shown in Fig. 3. FSBM supplementation significantly increased the expression levels of both ZO-1 and ZO-2 compared with those in the control, model, and chlortetracycline groups (P < 0.05). For ZO-1, the model and chlortetracycline groups both showed significantly lower expression than the control group, whereas the FSBM group exhibited the highest expression level among all groups (Fig. 3a). For ZO-2, no significant differences were observed among the control, model, and chlortetracycline groups, but expression was markedly elevated in the FSBM group (Fig. 3b). These findings suggest that FSBM enhanced barrier-related transcriptional responses in the colon following ETEC K88 challenge, indicating a potential effect on epithelial barrier regulation at the gene-expression level.

FSBM altered fecal pH, organic acid concentrations, and culturable microbial populations

As shown in Fig. 4a, fecal pH differed significantly among groups after 21 days of feeding. The FSBM group showed the lowest fecal pH (7.06), which was significantly lower than that of the control group (7.36) and lower than those of the other treatment groups, indicating a more acidic intestinal environment after FSBM supplementation.

Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences among groups (P < 0.05). Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 10 per group)

Effects of FSBM on fecal physicochemical characteristics and culturable bacterial populations in ETEC K88-challenged mice. a Fecal pH, b organic acid concentrations, and (c) culturable microbial counts. Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences among groups (P < 0.05). Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 10 per group)

Changes in fecal organic acid profiles are shown in Fig. 4b. Lactic acid was below the detection limit in the model and chlortetracycline groups, and its concentration was highest in the FSBM group. Acetic acid levels were also highest in the FSBM group, reaching 2.09 mg/g after 21 days of feeding. These results suggest that FSBM promoted the production or accumulation of specific fermentation-related organic acids in the intestine.

Fecal microbial enumeration further revealed distinct changes in culturable bacterial populations (Fig. 4c). The FSBM group exhibited the highest lactic acid bacteria count, reaching 10.05 log10 CFU/g, which was significantly higher than those of the other three groups. Heat-resistant aerobic spore-forming bacteria were detected only in the model and chlortetracycline groups. In contrast, the chlortetracycline group showed the lowest counts of total aerobic bacteria and coliforms, indicating a stronger suppressive effect on broad bacterial populations. Overall, FSBM mainly promoted acidification and lactic acid bacterial proliferation rather than broadly suppressing fecal bacteria.

FSBM reshaped the cecal microbial community composition

The cecal microbial communities of the four groups were analyzed by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, and the results are shown in Fig. 5. A total of 599 OTUs were identified across all samples, among which 287 OTUs were shared by the four groups (Fig. 5a), indicating the presence of a common core microbiota together with treatment-related community divergence.

Regarding alpha diversity, the Shannon index was significantly lower in the chlortetracycline and FSBM groups than in the model group (P < 0.05), with values of 3.82 and 4.20, respectively (Fig. 5b). For richness, the Chao1 index was higher in the model and FSBM groups than in the chlortetracycline group (P < 0.05), whereas the chlortetracycline group showed the lowest richness overall (258.27) (Fig. 5c). Principal component analysis revealed clear separation among the four groups, with the chlortetracycline group showing the greatest distance from the other groups, consistent with its marked effect on microbial community structure (Fig. 5d).

At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were dominant in all groups (Fig. 5e). Firmicutes accounted for 62.94%, 67.70%, 60.43%, and 62.36% in the control, model, chlortetracycline, and FSBM groups, respectively, whereas the corresponding abundances of Bacteroidetes were 33.92%, 27.61%, 36.41%, and 32.46%. At the genus level, norank_f__Muribaculaceae and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group were the predominant taxa in all groups (Fig. 5f). Notably, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus was highest in the FSBM group (12.26%), compared with 7.03% in the control group and 4.81% in the model group, whereas Lactobacillus was not detected in the chlortetracycline group. In addition, the relative abundance of Desulfovibrio was higher in the model, chlortetracycline, and FSBM groups than in the control group.

Further genus-level analysis using the Kruskal–Wallis H test showed that FSBM induced marked microbial shifts (Fig. 5g). Specifically, Lactobacillus, norank_f__Eubacterium_coprostanoligenes_group, and ASF356 were significantly enriched, whereas Candidatus_Saccharimonas was significantly reduced in the FSBM group. Correlation analysis between the top 50 most abundant bacterial taxa and serum immune indices further revealed potential microbiota–host immune associations (Fig. 5h). IgG was positively correlated with ASF356 (r = 0.75) and negatively correlated with norank_f__Oscillospiraceae (r = − 0.72) and Turicibacter (r = − 0.65). IL-10 was positively correlated with Ruminococcus (r = 0.64), whereas TNF-α was positively correlated with norank_f__Oscillospiraceae (r = 0.71), Turicibacter (r = 0.65), and norank_f__Ruminococcaceae (r = 0.59), but negatively correlated with ASF356 (r = − 0.78) and Eubacterium_nodatum_group (r = − 0.69). These results suggest potential associations between FSBM-responsive bacterial taxa and serum immune indices.

FSBM induced marked shifts in the cecal metabolomic profile

To further characterize the metabolic changes associated with FSBM supplementation, untargeted metabolomic analysis of cecal contents was performed. PCA showed relatively tight clustering of biological samples within each group, suggesting group-level metabolic consistency (Fig. 6). Compared with the control, model, and chlortetracycline groups, the FSBM group was clearly separated in the score plot, suggesting that FSBM induced a distinct cecal metabolic profile.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified purine metabolism as a common altered pathway

To further screen for treatment-related metabolic alterations, OPLS-DA models were established for the control vs. FSBM, model vs. FSBM, and chlortetracycline vs. FSBM comparisons (Fig. 7a–c). The three pairwise comparisons were clearly separated in positive ion mode, indicating substantial metabolic differences between the FSBM group and the other groups. Discussion

Overall, the present study shows that ZA3- and Artemisia argyi-associated FSBM exerted a broad protective effect against ETEC K88-induced intestinal injury in mice, as reflected by improved body-weight recovery, reduced diarrhea severity, attenuation of inflammatory disturbance, enhanced expression of tight junction-related genes, and coordinated remodeling of the cecal microbiota and metabolome. Recent studies suggest that effective nutritional intervention for post-weaning enteric dysfunction should be evaluated not only by diarrhea reduction, but also by restoration of epithelial barrier integrity [29], recovery of microbiota–metabolite homeostasis [30], and correction of the coupled microbial and metabolic disturbances that characterize diarrheic post-weaning piglets [31]. In ETEC K88-challenged piglets, dietary FSBM has been shown to alleviate diarrhea while modulating inflammatory cytokines and cecal microbiota composition [9]. Consistently, FSBM supplementation in weaned piglets also improved growth performance, nutrient digestibility, and gut microbial profiles [10]. More recently, fermented corn–soybean meal was reported to improve growth, reduce diarrhea incidence, enhance tight-junction expression, and increase Lactobacillus and butyrate levels in weaned piglets [8]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the beneficial effect of FSBM was not limited to symptomatic relief, but reflected multi-level regulation of the disturbed intestinal ecosystem.

Building on the overall multi-level protective pattern described above, the cytokine and immunoglobulin data further suggest that FSBM improved immune homeostasis rather than simply attenuating the clinical manifestation of diarrhea. In our model, FSBM lowered TNF-α and IFN-γ while increasing IL-10, IgA, and IgG, a direction of change consistent with recent piglet studies showing that successful anti-ETEC interventions suppress pro-inflammatory signaling, reduce circulating TNF-α and IFN-γ, and strengthen systemic humoral immune responses [30]. TNF-α is a central amplifier of ETEC-associated intestinal inflammation, and dietary berberine was shown to reverse the ETEC-induced increase in TNF-α in the small intestine of weaned piglets [32], whereas in-feed binding proteins mitigated ETEC-associated post-weaning diarrhea together with attenuation of inflammation [33]. The increase in IL-10 observed here is also biologically meaningful because IL-10 acts as a key counter-regulatory cytokine limiting excessive mucosal immune activation; consistent with this, dietary berberine upregulated colonic IL-10 expression in ETEC-challenged piglets [29]. Meanwhile, the increase in IgA and IgG after FSBM treatment suggests improved immune competence, which agrees with recent evidence that complex probiotics elevate serum IgA and IgG in weaned piglets and are associated with lower inflammatory burden [34]. Therefore, together with previous evidence that dietary FSBM improved intestinal integrity, anti-oxidative capacity, and immune function in weaned pigs [35], our findings support the interpretation that FSBM contributed to re-establishing immune balance under enteric challenge.

Beyond the immune shifts described above, the barrier-related findings are also noteworthy because FSBM markedly increased the colonic mRNA expression of ZO-1 and ZO-2 in the present study, and the magnitude of this transcriptional response exceeded that observed in the chlortetracycline group. This suggests that the beneficial effect of FSBM was not restricted to suppression of enteric symptoms, but may also involve the regulation of epithelial barrier-related transcriptional responses. Recent piglet studies support the importance of tight-junction regulation in nutritional interventions against ETEC-associated enteric injury. Dietary berberine attenuated ETEC-induced mucosal barrier injury and improved physical, chemical, and immune barrier indices [36]. In two independent piglet studies, linarin alleviated small-intestinal and colonic barrier dysfunction, with increased ZO-1, ZO-2, and other junction-related markers, reduced permeability, and improved epithelial architecture [37, 38]. Likewise, Bacillus halotolerans SW207 alleviated ETEC-induced epithelial damage by modulating the intestinal epithelial barrier [39], and fermented corn–soybean meal enhanced tight-junction expression in weaned piglets [8]. However, because tight-junction protein abundance and intestinal permeability were not directly measured in the present study, our results should be interpreted as evidence of enhanced barrier-related transcriptional responses rather than direct proof of restored epithelial integrity. Future studies should include protein-level analyses and functional permeability assays to verify whether these gene-expression changes translate into improved epithelial barrier function.

Consistent with its effects on immune and barrier markers, FSBM also reshaped the luminal environment, as evidenced by lower fecal pH, higher lactic and acetic acid concentrations, and a marked increase in culturable lactic acid bacteria. This pattern is biologically meaningful because acidification of the intestinal milieu can favor acid-tolerant beneficial taxa and create ecological conditions that are less permissive to enteric pathogens [40]. In weaned piglets, fermented corn–soybean meal increased Lactobacillus and butyrate while reducing diarrhea incidence [8], whereas complex probiotic supplementation increased fecal lactobacilli and reduced E. coli abundance [34]. Similarly, MCFA/SCFA supplementation relieved diarrhea and supported gut barrier function while increasing Lactobacillus and Roseburia in weaned piglets [40]. More recently, Bacillus subtilis supplementation in ETEC-challenged weanling pigs was shown to mitigate infection-associated metabolic disruption and gut dysbiosis [41], and Lactobacillus fermentum ZNL16 alleviated ETEC-induced intestinal injury through gut microbiota and short-chain fatty acid metabolism [42]. Taken together, these studies support the interpretation that the lower pH and higher organic acid/LAB levels observed here were not incidental findings, but part of a functionally meaningful shift toward a more protective luminal ecosystem. The increased lactic acid and acetic acid levels in the FSBM group may have originated from both the fermented feed itself and the enrichment of intestinal lactic acid bacteria. These organic acids may lower luminal pH, suppress unfavorable bacteria, support beneficial microbial growth, and participate in epithelial barrier and immune regulation. In addition, lactate and acetate may act as substrates for microbial cross-feeding and indirectly contribute to broader SCFA production. Consistent with this interpretation, a recent piglet study showed that the SCFA-producing bacterium Bacillus velezensis MZ09 alleviated intestinal inflammation by remodeling gut microbiota, activating the SCFAs–GPR43–STAT3 pathway, and suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis [43]. Therefore, the organic acid changes observed here may represent a potential metabolic link between FSBM-induced microbiota remodeling and intestinal protection, although these signaling pathways require further validation in future studies.

At the community level, the sequencing results suggest that FSBM did not merely alter diversity metrics, but selectively redirected the cecal microbiota toward a less inflammation-associated configuration. The most convincing signal was the enrichment of Lactobacillus, which is consistent with the culture-based increase in lactic acid bacteria observed here and with growing evidence that Lactobacillus-centered shifts are closely associated with improved intestinal resilience in weaned pigs [34, 42, 44]. The increase in norank_f__Eubacterium_coprostanoligenes_group may also be biologically relevant, because mechanistic work has shown that Eubacterium coprostanoligenes can enhance goblet-cell mucin secretion and strengthen the intestinal mucus barrier [45]. By contrast, the reduction in Candidatus_Saccharimonas was also notable, although we consider the functional interpretation of this taxon to be hypothesis-generating because its role appears to be context dependent across models. Importantly, our correlation analysis linked Turicibacter and norank_f__Oscillospiraceae with higher TNF-α and/or IFN-γ, whereas ASF356 showed an inverse correlation with TNF-α. These genus-level relationships should be interpreted cautiously. Recent work indicates that Turicibacter can display divergent host associations depending on the disease setting, including metabolically favorable effects in one study [46] but positive correlations with pro-inflammatory cytokines in chronic intestinal inflammation in another [47]. Likewise, ASF356 cannot be simply classified as beneficial, because a recent study identified Clostridium sp. ASF356 as a driver of a phenylacetic acid axis linked to endothelial senescence in mice [48]. Therefore, rather than assigning a definitive role to every individual genus, the more robust interpretation is that FSBM shifted the overall microbial community away from an inflammation-linked structure and toward a more resilient ecological profile.

Beyond the taxonomic shifts, the metabolomic data indicate that FSBM induced a structured reprogramming of the cecal metabolic landscape rather than a nonspecific change in scattered metabolites. This interpretation is supported by recent piglet studies showing that effective intervention against ETEC challenge is accompanied by coordinated hindgut metabolomic remodeling, rather than by isolated changes in a few compounds [41]. Among the pathways identified here, the repeated enrichment of purine metabolism across all three pairwise comparisons were particularly noteworthy, because gut bacteria are now recognized as active regulators of host purine pools and systemic uric acid homeostasis [49]. In our dataset, the simultaneous increase in inosine, xanthosine, dAMP, AMP-related intermediates, deoxyinosine, deoxyadenosine, and deoxyguanosine, together with the reduction in uric acid, suggests that FSBM may have shifted purine turnover away from a more terminal catabolic pattern and toward a less inflammation-associated metabolic state. This interpretation is supported by a recent Advanced Science study showing that microbiota remodeling markedly enriched purine metabolism, increased inosine and other purine metabolites, and coincided with improved barrier integrity and lower immune activation [50]. It is also consistent with recent probiotic work demonstrating that degradation of purine nucleosides can lower uric acid, improve barrier-related proteins, and attenuate inflammatory damage [51]. Although purine signaling in the intestine is complex and context dependent, adenosine- and inosine-related pathways are increasingly regarded as functional components of epithelial and immunometabolic regulation rather than inert by-products of nucleotide turnover [52]. Therefore, the repeated emergence of purine metabolism in the present study suggests that purine metabolism may represent an important metabolic node responsive to FSBM, and one plausible bridge linking microbial remodeling to barrier and immune recovery. A possible explanation for this microbiota–purine metabolism association is that FSBM reshaped the intestinal microbial community and thereby influenced microbial nucleotide turnover and nucleoside metabolism in the cecal environment. The enrichment of lactic acid bacteria, especially Lactobacillus, may have contributed to the accumulation of purine-related nucleosides and nucleotides such as inosine, xanthosine, AMP, and dAMP, either through microbial growth-associated nucleotide metabolism or through altered degradation and salvage of purine intermediates. These purine metabolites are not merely terminal products of nucleotide turnover; inosine- and adenosine-related metabolites may also participate in epithelial barrier regulation and immune homeostasis. Therefore, the FSBM-induced increase in beneficial bacteria and the coordinated alteration of purine metabolites may represent a potential microbiota–purine metabolism axis involved in intestinal recovery. However, because the present evidence is based mainly on association analysis, further studies using targeted metabolomics, strain-level microbiome analysis, and bacterial metabolite-production assays are needed to verify the causal contribution of specific bacteria to purine metabolic remodeling.

Finally, the correlation analysis provides an important bridge between the microbial and metabolomic findings, but it also defines the interpretive boundary of the present work. In our dataset, Lactobacillus was positively associated with several purine-related metabolites, including dAMP, inosine, and xanthosine, whereas other genera such as Enterococcus and Weissella showed distinct associations with selected purine intermediates. This pattern is not implausible, because previous work has shown that gut bacteria can regulate host purine homeostasis [49], and a recent study further demonstrated that Lactobacillus johnsonii enrichment was significantly associated with purine metabolites, particularly inosine [50]. However, these relationships remain correlative, and the present data do not yet establish which strain(s) directly generated the altered metabolites or whether the purine changes were primary drivers of barrier recovery or secondary consequences of a broader improvement in intestinal ecology. For that reason, the current results are best interpreted as evidence for a microbiota–purine metabolism axis that is responsive to FSBM, rather than as definitive proof of causality.

A second point that should be acknowledged explicitly is model limitation. Although the mouse model used here was appropriate for preliminary mechanistic evaluation, ETEC-associated post-weaning diarrhea is fundamentally a piglet disease in its natural host, and piglet challenge studies remain the most translationally relevant platform for validating anti-ETEC nutritional interventions [53]. Experimental piglet infection models can reproduce the expected clinical signs, intestinal lesions, and inflammatory responses of ETEC infection [54]. Therefore, the present study should be regarded as a proof-of-concept mechanistic investigation rather than direct natural-host validation. Another limitation is that individual daily feed intake was not systematically recorded because the diets were provided ad libitum. Therefore, although the dietary inclusion level of FSBM was clearly defined as 200 g/kg complete diet, the exact individual FSBM intake expressed as g/kg body weight/day could not be calculated. Future studies should record individual feed intake to allow more precise dose–response comparisons across studies. Future animal trials should be conducted in ETEC K88-challenged weaned piglets to validate the translational relevance of these findings in the natural host. Such studies should evaluate diarrhea incidence, growth performance, intestinal morphology, barrier-function markers, inflammatory responses, gut microbiota composition, and targeted purine metabolite profiles to determine whether the microbiota–purine metabolism axis identified in mice is reproducible in piglets.

Conclusions

In conclusion, fermented soybean meal alleviated ETEC K88-induced diarrhea and intestinal barrier-related disturbances in mice, as evidenced by improved body-weight recovery, reduced diarrhea severity, partial restoration of immune homeostasis, enhanced expression of colonic tight junction-related genes, and marked alterations in fecal physicochemical characteristics, cecal microbiota, and metabolite profiles. In particular, FSBM promoted the enrichment of beneficial bacterial taxa, increased lactic acid bacteria and organic acid production, and was associated with prominent remodeling of purine metabolism, suggesting a close interaction between microbial regulation and metabolic reprogramming during intestinal recovery. Taken together, these findings indicate that FSBM exerts intestinal protective effects that are associated with changes in barrier-related responses, gut microbiota, and microbial metabolites. Although the present work was conducted in a mouse proof-of-concept model, it provides preliminary mechanistic evidence supporting the potential of FSBM as a functional nutritional strategy for alleviating enteric stress and improving intestinal health.
