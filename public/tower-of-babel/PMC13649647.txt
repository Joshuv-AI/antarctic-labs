A Million-Scale, Difficulty-Stratified Optical-Microscopy Image Annotation for Neuron Reconstruction
Authors: Mingwei Liao, Wu Chen, Shengda Bao, Xueyan Jia, Xiaowei Chen, Chi Xiao, Qingming Luo, Hui Gong, Anan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate reconstruction of neuronal morphology is a cornerstone for understanding the structure and function of neural circuits. Despite significant advances in imaging and reconstruction techniques that have greatly expanded the scale and quality of neuronal data, existing public datasets still lack large-scale, high-quality, and well-annotated resources, limiting their utility for training and evaluating modern AI models. In this study, we present an open, graded neuronal dataset covering the entire mouse brain. Leveraging high-resolution optical microscopy, we combined automated reconstruction with multi-user collaborative proofreading to generate high-precision neuronal annotations. Our dataset was derived from 10,547 neurons across 258 whole-brain samples, and approximately 8,092,547 standardized data units with graded difficulty levels were generated through an interactive collaborative reconstruction process. Moreover, the dataset supports both API access and bulk downloads, offering researchers convenient data acquisition methods. This work fills a critical gap in standardized, annotated whole-brain neuronal resources, provides a high-quality benchmark for AI-driven automated neuronal reconstruction, and lays a solid foundation for building large-scale neural circuit maps.

Background & Summary

Neurons are the fundamental functional units of the nervous system, and the accurate reconstruction of their complete morphology is essential for understanding neural circuits1–3. With the continuous development of labeling and imaging technologies4–6, researchers are now able to obtain high-resolution, three-dimensional whole-brain images of the mouse brain. These images not only preserve the spatial topology of neurons but also provide a solid foundation for neural network modeling and morphological analysis7.

In 2013, scientists first employed fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography (fMOST) to achieve long-range neuronal tracing across the entire mammalian brain8, opening a new era of whole-brain neural circuit research. Subsequently, Winnubst et al. reconstructed 1,000 projection neurons using a high-throughput platform and preliminarily investigated the impact of the number of annotators on reconstruction quality9. Qiu et al. focused on hippocampal projections, reconstructing 10,100 neurons in the mouse hippocampus, and establishing an open online database10. These studies have not only advanced large-scale neuronal reconstruction but also provided key datasets for uncovering the structure and function of the nervous system. However, as imaging data volumes grow rapidly, neuronal reconstruction still relies heavily on manual annotation, which is time-consuming, inefficient, and prone to inconsistencies. Thus, the development of automated and standardized reconstruction methods has become an urgent need.

In recent years, artificial intelligence has made remarkable progress in neuronal reconstruction, significantly improving the efficiency of image processing and recognition11–14. Nevertheless, the performance of reconstruction algorithms largely depends on large-scale, high-quality annotated datasets, which serve as essential benchmarks for model training and validation15. The DIADEM dataset, established in 2010, includes six subsets covering different species, brain regions, neuron types, and visualization modalities16. It played a critical role in driving algorithm development17,18, but its relatively small size and limited diversity restricted its broader applicability19. Later, the BigNeuron project released a larger dataset, with its initial phase mainly focused on the reconstruction of sparsely labeled neurons. The “gold166” subset contains 166 three-dimensional neuronal images with corresponding manual reconstructions, substantially expanding the available annotated resources15. However, this dataset remained limited to isolated neurons, lacking scenarios with complex fiber crossings and densely packed structures20, and therefore did not fully capture the complexity and diversity of neuronal morphology.

Although additional morphological reconstructions have since been accumulated21, several challenges remain in supporting high-precision reconstruction algorithm training. First, reconstruction quality standards are inconsistent, making it difficult to reach the “gold standard” of BigNeuron. Second, the lack of paired raw imaging data restricts precise correspondence between images and reconstructed structures. Finally, significant differences in reconstruction difficulty across brain regions often lead to feature confusion when training on mixed datasets, thereby limiting a model’s ability to capture the essential characteristics of neuronal morphology. The HiNeuron dataset22 improves data consistency by introducing a difficulty grading mechanism. However, its scale remains limited due to the low efficiency and high cost of manual data collection. In addition, the lack of an online query system further restricts its applicability in large-scale scenarios.

Overall, current neuronal datasets still fall short in terms of scale and quality, making them insufficient to effectively support the training and evaluation of modern AI models. Therefore, constructing a large-scale, high-quality dataset with systematic classification and precise annotation has become a critical step for advancing both neuroscience research and intelligent algorithm development.

In this study, we constructed an open, precisely annotated, and difficulty-graded whole-brain neuronal dataset of the mouse, designed to meet the pressing demand for high-quality data in neuroscience. Using high-resolution optical microscopy, we obtained complete three-dimensional morphologies of mouse brain neurons. By integrating automated reconstruction with manual proofreading, we generated high-precision annotations. Through interactive collaborative reconstruction23, we generated 8,092,547 standardized data units with graded difficulty levels from 10,547 neurons across 258 whole-brain samples. In addition, we developed a rational grading strategy that categorizes neuronal images into four difficulty levels, ensuring both high throughput and high accuracy in annotation through the graded reconstruction and collaboration framework. Furthermore, the platform provides API services, enabling programmatic access and bulk downloads. By publicly releasing this dataset, we offer a more accurate and comprehensive resource for whole-brain neuroscience research, filling a critical gap in standardized neuronal annotations and supporting both AI-driven automated reconstruction and large-scale neural circuit mapping.

Methods

Data generation

The neuron grading data were mainly derived from a complete neuronal data processing pipeline (as shown in Fig. 1). Specifically, this pipeline first employs adeno-associated virus (AAV) to sparsely label neurons in specific regions of the mouse brain, effectively reducing neuronal density in the imaging data24,25. Subsequently, the brain samples are embedded in resin to prepare stable specimens suitable for high-resolution imaging. The fMOST imaging system is then used to acquire high-resolution three-dimensional images of the entire mouse brain. Due to the limited field of view in a single acquisition, the system performs sequential scanning, and multiple independent scans are stitched and reconstructed to generate complete coronal slice images. During the imaging process, uneven illumination can introduce artifacts, thereby increasing the difficulty of subsequent neuronal reconstruction. Therefore, data preprocessing is necessary. First, flat-field correction is applied to remove artifacts caused by uneven illumination. Subsequently, linear blending is employed to eliminate seam artifacts generated during image stitching. This preprocessing workflow significantly improves overall image quality and minimizes errors during neuronal tracing to the greatest extent possible.

Workflow of neuronal data acquisition. The process involved sample preparation, optical imaging, data preprocessing, TDat format conversion, neuronal data block partitioning, data grading and neuronal morphology reconstruction to obtain complete neuronal reconstruction data.

After preprocessing, the images were divided into blocks of size 256 × 256 × 256 voxels26 to facilitate efficient data access during reconstruction. Starting from the soma, the system progressively followed the neuronal fibers, extracting image blocks containing neuronal signals along the trajectories of the neurons. The neuron is first divided into independent image-block units. The reconstruction difficulty of each image block is then evaluated using the difficulty assessment method. Subsequently, automatic tracing is performed for each unit, followed by sequential verification and correction by multiple annotators to ensure reconstruction accuracy.

Through this workflow, we ultimately collected neuronal image blocks together with their corresponding reconstruction results, initial tracing points, and tracing directions. Specifically, the “tracing starting point” of an image block is defined as the initial coordinate of the reconstruction task within that block, while the “initial tracing direction” is defined as the local extension direction of the neuronal fiber at the tracing starting point. Based on these resources, we established a large-scale, high-quality graded neuronal database.

Data grading

The segmented neuronal images exhibit varying degrees of complexity. In these images, the structural intricacy and density of neuronal fibers differ significantly, resulting in considerable variation in reconstruction difficulty. If datasets of different complexity levels are mixed during analysis, it becomes difficult to identify the key challenges, thereby reducing the overall accuracy of reconstruction. By implementing fine-grained grading, images can be effectively categorized according to different levels of difficulty.

The grading method used in this study has been described in detail in our previous work23. To accurately assess the difficulty of neuronal reconstruction, the calculation of neuronal difficulty is divided into three parts: neuronal data partitioning, automatic computation of neuronal data difficulty, and adjustment of neuronal data difficulty, as shown in Fig. 2a.

Graded neuronal image data. (a) The workflow for neuronal data grading. (b) Results of neuronal data grading from Level1 to Level4. Scale bar, 10 μm.

Given the massive scale of whole-brain neuronal images, direct processing of the entire dataset is computationally infeasible. Therefore, we adopted an image-blocking approach, dividing the original images into smaller sub-blocks. Each image block is treated as an independent data unit, and the corresponding reconstruction difficulty is assessed accordingly. To accurately evaluate the reconstruction difficulty of each image block, we selected three categories of image-derived indicators: fiber-related, key point–related, and signal-related indicators. For fiber-related indicators, four metrics were chosen: Fiber density, target fiber density, fiber length, and target fiber length. For key point–related indicators, four metrics were selected: key point density, target key point density, number of key points, and number of target key points. For signal-related indicators, three metrics were used: signal density, projection signal density, and weak signal ratio. All selected difficulty quantification indicators are detailed in Table 1.

Quantitative indicators for neuronal reconstruction difficulty and the selected principal components.

Next, we computed quantitative indicators for the images to evaluate the reconstruction difficulty of neuronal data blocks. For fiber-related indicators, fiber skeletons were first extracted using a principal curve method, and fiber length was calculated based on the distances between skeleton points. Fiber density was then quantified by dividing the total skeleton length by the maximum area among the three orthogonal views of the data. For key point–related indicators, key point coordinates were extracted using the feature sphere method. The number of key points was obtained by direct counting, while key point density was calculated by dividing the number of key points by the maximum area among the three views. For signal-related indicators, fiber signals were first extracted using an adaptive threshold method. Signal density was computed as the number of three-dimensional signal points divided by the maximum area among the three views. Projection signal density was obtained by projecting the three-dimensional signals onto the three orthogonal view planes, and the minimum signal density among the three views was taken as the final metric.

After obtaining the quantitative results of the 11 indicators, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied for dimensionality reduction to remove redundant information. Following the criterion of 90% cumulative explained variance, four principal components were retained, and their detailed compositions are provided in Table 1. Based on these four principal components, a difficulty evaluation coefficient was calculated for each data block. Subsequently, hierarchical clustering was applied based on the difficulty evaluation coefficient to construct a dendrogram, and the data were finally divided into four difficulty levels according to the clustering tree, defined as Level 1, Level 2, Level 3, and Level 4 (Fig. 2b). With increasing level, the data complexity gradually rises: Level 1 represents relatively simple images, Level 2 indicates moderate difficulty, Level 3 corresponds to more challenging cases, and Level 4 denotes the most difficult images.

This automatic grading method serves as the foundation for large-scale acquisition of annotated datasets at the million-sample scale. Furthermore, in practical neuronal reconstruction, the reconstruction difficulty level is dynamically adjusted based on the reconstruction results returned by annotators, and the adjustment workflow is illustrated in Fig. 3. After the initial data level is determined by the automatic method, the judgments of each annotator are recorded. Based on these judgments and the reconstruction accuracy of the annotators, the current data entropy is computed. If the resulting entropy exceeds a predefined threshold, it is determined whether the data have already reached the highest difficulty level. If so, the data are assigned to experts for reconstruction. If the highest level has not yet been reached, the difficulty level of the data is increased by one grade, and the data re-enter the next round of manual reconstruction and evaluation, thereby forming a closed-loop dynamic optimization mechanism. If the resulting entropy is below the threshold, the current data are marked as completed. Through this procedure, the final difficulty level of the data is determined.

Dataset construction

In this study, we employed a self-developed reconstruction platform to trace neuronal images. First, large-volume images were progressively divided into smaller sub-blocks along neuronal trajectories. Each image block was automatically traced to generate an initial fiber reconstruction27, which was then loaded into the platform together with the raw image (Fig. 4). Subsequently, reconstruction tasks are assigned to annotators whose capabilities are matched to the difficulty of the data. Multiple annotators independently reconstruct each data block, and the results are iteratively evaluated until the predefined accuracy threshold is achieved. When reconstruction errors are detected, manual proofreading and correction are performed. After completing the current data block, the system automatically allocates the next task, and the process continues until all data blocks have been reconstructed.

Construction results of a large-scale, high-quality graded neuronal dataset(HiNeuron Ver2).

After block-level reconstruction, the blocks were stitched together to form complete neuronal reconstructions. Adjacent blocks required spatial alignment during stitching, ensuring not only consistency of three-dimensional coordinates but also continuity of neuronal fiber morphology for seamless integration. The system’s grading strategy automatically assigned reconstruction tasks to different operators based on complexity: experienced operators were allocated high-complexity, branch-dense regions requiring strong spatial cognition and microscopy interpretation skills, while novice operators were assigned simpler tasks with clean backgrounds and well-defined structures. Through this precise allocation of human–computer collaboration, overall reconstruction efficiency was greatly improved.

The entire workflow balanced efficiency and accuracy. Automated reconstruction enabled the processing of massive datasets, allowing for fast and relatively accurate reconstruction of simpler data while significantly enhancing throughput. Meanwhile, the difficulty-based decision-making strategy ensured reconstruction accuracy, providing a reliable foundation for subsequent morphological analyses.

Through these precise collaborative strategies, we generated 8,092,547 standardized data units with graded difficulty levels from 10,547 neurons across 258 whole-brain samples. Standardized data units are defined as uniformly formatted, easily readable and processable data packages that include both the raw image blocks and their corresponding reconstruction ground truth. Based on these data, we established HiNeuron Ver2, a large-scale, high-quality, and graded neuronal dataset, providing abundant high-quality samples for the training and performance improvement of neuronal reconstruction algorithms. Our platform demonstrated its capability to process whole-brain neuronal datasets at scale, fully showcasing its high-throughput performance.

Data Records

The dataset is available for downloading on Zenodo28 (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20501092). Detailed information about the dataset is provided in the HiNeuron Ver2 dataset section. Instructions for accessing and using the dataset are available on the Zenodo repository. The data are organized in a three-level hierarchical structure. At the first level, each folder is named according to a unique BrainID. Clicking on a BrainID opens the second-level directory, which is organized by NeuronID. Each NeuronID folder contains two subfolders (image and swc) along with a TXT metadata file. The Image folder stores three-dimensional image data in TIFF format, which facilitates convenient downloading, visualization, and analysis. The Swc folder contains the corresponding neuron reconstruction results in SWC format. The accompanying TXT file includes a data identifier consistent with the names of the image and reconstruction files. It also records the initial point and initial tracing direction of the reconstruction, describing where neuron tracing begins and in which direction it proceeds—information that assists researchers in further analyses.

These data are all comprehensively presented in our self-hosted repository. The repository provides convenient data browsing functionality, allowing users not only to view the raw images and corresponding SWC morphologies, but also to freely select and examine data of interest. In addition, all datasets are systematically organized within the repository, enabling users to filter data based on specific criteria for efficient browsing and downloading.

In addition, this dataset provides comprehensive metadata describing the mouse specimens used for image acquisition. Each sample is assigned a unique Brain ID, and detailed attributes such as strain, gender, and age are recorded. The imaging information includes the imaging system, number of imaging channels, and voxel resolution. These metadata are presented in tabular form in the Specimen Information section, comprising the following fields:

Brain ID: A unique identifier for each mouse brain, facilitating data management and retrieval.

Line: The genetic background of the mouse sample (e.g., C57BL/6 J, PV-Cre).

Gender: The biological sex of the mouse (male or female).

Age: The age of the mouse, measured in weeks.

Imaging system: The imaging platform used for data acquisition (e.g., HD-fMOST, TDI-fMOST).

Imaging channel: The fluorescence channels used during imaging (e.g., GFP, YFP).

Voxel resolution: The spatial resolution of the acquired volumetric data.

For example, in the HD-fMOST imaging system, a total of 201 mouse brain samples were acquired, with voxel resolutions of either 0.32 μm × 0.32 μm × 1 μm or 0.32 μm × 0.32 μm × 2 μm. The TDI-fMOST imaging system contains 57 mouse brain samples, each with a voxel resolution of 0.35 μm × 0.35 μm × 1 μm. Each neuron image volume typically spans several hundred pixels per dimension and is paired with a corresponding SWC reconstruction file.

To facilitate efficient data access and downloading, a Download API is provided under the Tools section in the HiNeuron Ver2 Dataset Download API module. This section includes detailed instructions for downloading data using four programming languages: JavaScript, Python and Java. The API accepts three parameters:

DataCount: The number of data samples to download.

TaskLevel: The difficulty level of the data.

SavePath: The local directory where the data will be saved.

By specifying these parameters, users can easily download datasets of various difficulty levels and quantities, enabling flexible and scalable use of the HiNeuron Ver2 data for different research purposes.

Technical Validation

Data quality

During neuronal image preprocessing, we implemented a rigorous preprocessing pipeline to ensure the integrity of the acquired mouse brain neuronal image data. First, the imaging system captured raw strip-shaped images of mouse brain sections with intentional spatial overlap during acquisition. This overlapping configuration effectively prevented structural discontinuities or misalignments at stitching junctions. Subsequently, we employed a feature-based automatic image stitching algorithm to precisely align these overlapping regions, generating seamless and continuous whole-brain images. This stitching process adhered to spatial continuity principles, ensuring structural coherence of the stitched brain images in both two-dimensional and three-dimensional spaces. Through systematic quality checks, all stitched regions achieved high spatial consistency and accuracy. These preprocessed images established a high-quality foundation for subsequent neuronal structure analysis.

During neuron reconstruction, we implemented a multi-operator cross-verification method to ensure reconstruction accuracy. First, two independent reconstruction operators performed separate validity assessments on the same data block. When their judgments matched, we calculated the reconstruction accuracy rate for that block. If the rate reached the predefined threshold, the reconstruction result was validated as correct and adopted as the reference standard. If the initial two assessments disagreed, a third operator was introduced for further evaluation, and a comprehensive accuracy rate was recalculated based on all three judgments. When the accuracy rate met the predefined standard, the reconstruction result was confirmed. If the threshold remained unsatisfied, a fourth operator was engaged, with this iterative process continuing until the target accuracy rate was achieved. This tiered cross-verification strategy ensured the reliability and precision of neuronal image reconstruction results.

Data validity analysis

The neuronal reconstruction platform in this study handled massive whole-brain neuronal datasets, fully demonstrating its high-throughput processing capability. Furthermore, experiments were conducted to verify the impact of the amount of neuronal image data on network training performance and to analyze its role in improving neuron tracing accuracy. In the experiments, a 3D U-Net network29 was employed, trained with 100, 400, and 1,000 three-dimensional neuronal image patches, respectively. The trained network was then used to extract neuronal signals, followed by neuronal tracing using the SparseTracer method30. To quantitatively evaluate the tracing performance, F1-scores were calculated across three data complexity levels (Level1, Level2, and Level3) to measure reconstruction accuracy.

As shown in Fig. 5, compared to direct tracing of raw images, signal extraction through the trained network significantly improved tracing accuracy. Furthermore, network performance demonstrated progressive improvement with increasing training data. Specifically, with 100 training samples, the network showed improved capability in neuronal structure extraction, yet improved the F1-score from 0.5845 to 0.7553. When the sample number increased to 400, the F1-score rose to 0.7824. With 1,000 training samples, the F1-score further increased to 0.8039, enabling more accurate reconstruction of complex neuronal structures, indicating that larger data facilitated learning of more robust feature representations. Performance improvements were also observed for Level2 and Level3 data. These experiments confirmed the positive correlation between data scale and model performance, while revealing performance variation across different complexity levels. The experimental results provided important references for optimizing neuronal reconstruction algorithms.

Data feature analysis

To analyze the impact of neuronal count and data volume on reconstruction difficulty, we plotted a scatter diagram based on 258 mouse brain datasets, comparing the number of neurons with the number of data blocks (Fig. 6a). The results showed that a single dataset contained up to 256 neurons, though most datasets had fewer than 100 neurons. For data blocks, the maximum reached 380,000, while the minimum was only 444, with most datasets containing fewer than 100,000 blocks. Furthermore, the scatter distribution revealed significant differences in data density across regions. Using two dividing lines, we partitioned the overall distribution into three regions: Region I with slopes < 220.37; Region II with slopes in [220.37, 1,506.5); and Region III with slopes ≥ 1,506.5. Most datasets were concentrated in Region II, while Regions I and III were relatively sparse.

Analysis of neuronal reconstruction difficulty. (a) Scatter plot of neuron count and graded data count in mouse brain datasets. (b) Proportion of different data levels across quadrants.

We further calculated the proportions of difficulty levels (Levels 1–4) within the three regions (Fig. 6b). The results indicated that in Region I, Level 1 data accounted for a relatively small proportion and showed a wide distribution, while Level 4 data had a higher proportion, suggesting greater reconstruction difficulty. In contrast, Regions II and III exhibited much higher proportions of Level 1 data and lower proportions of high-level data compared with Region I. Notably, Region II contained the lowest proportions of Level 3 and Level 4 data, indicating the lowest reconstruction difficulty. In Region III, the proportions of Level 2 and Level 3 data were higher than in Region II but lower than in Region I, suggesting an intermediate level of difficulty.

In summary, the analysis showed that when the ratio of neuronal count to data block number in mouse brain datasets was moderate, reconstruction difficulty was minimized. Conversely, datasets with relatively fewer blocks tended to include more high-difficulty data, thereby increasing reconstruction challenges.

In the process of complete neuronal reconstruction, axons and dendrites exhibit substantial structural differences. Axons typically have long branches that can extend across multiple brain regions, whereas dendrites usually form relatively short branches that remain close to the soma31. Therefore, to investigate whether axons and dendrites differ in reconstruction difficulty, we analyzed the number of data samples at different difficulty levels for both axons and dendrites. This analysis provides the basis for selecting distinct reconstruction strategies for axons and dendrites.

In Fig. 7a, the top three panels present the statistics of axonal data across different difficulty levels. The first panel shows the proportion of axonal judgment data at different levels: Level 1 accounts for an average of 66.3%, Level 2 for 26.5%, Level 3 for 6.2%, and Level 4 for 1.0%. The second panel shows the proportion of axonal correction data: 60.6% for Level 1, 30.0% for Level 2, 8.1% for Level 3, and 1.3% for Level 4. The third panel depicts the conversion rates from judgment data to correction data: 59.4% for Level 1, 75.4% for Level 2, 93.5% for Level 3, and 98.7% for Level 4. In contrast, the bottom three panels of Fig. 7a show the corresponding statistics for dendrites. Overall, the proportions of judgment data, corrected data, and conversion rates across difficulty levels for dendrites are very similar to those for axons. This indicates that, despite the pronounced structural differences between axons and dendrites, the distribution of reconstruction difficulty levels remains consistent.

Difficulty differences in axonal and dendritic reconstruction. (a) Quantitative statistics of data at different difficulty levels for axons and dendrites. (b) Probability distribution of data quantities across difficulty levels relative to soma distance. (c) Proportions of different difficulty levels versus neuronal reconstruction length. (d) Variation in higher-difficulty data proportion with increasing neuronal length.

These statistical results demonstrate that data distributions across difficulty levels vary considerably. Low-level data account for the majority, making them suitable for allocation to less experienced personnel. This approach lowers the entry barrier for reconstruction tasks and expands the available workforce, thereby accelerating large-scale neuronal reconstruction. Conversely, more difficult data can be effectively identified through grading and subsequently assigned to experienced personnel, ensuring reconstruction accuracy.

To further examine whether the spatial distribution of reconstruction difficulty exhibits any patterns, we analyzed the probability distribution of data difficulty relative to the distance from the soma (Fig. 7b). Results show that higher-difficulty data (Levels 3 and 4) tend to cluster closer to the soma, suggesting that more challenging tasks are concentrated in this region. However, some high-difficulty data are also distributed farther from the soma, indicating that difficult cases are unevenly distributed spatially but exist throughout different regions. By contrast, lower-difficulty data (Level 1) are evenly distributed within a 10 mm radius from the soma, reflecting a uniform spatial distribution of simpler tasks. Level 2 data show higher density closer to the soma, though a substantial proportion also falls within the 10 mm range.

Finally, to investigate whether the proportion of data difficulty levels is influenced by neuronal length, we analyzed reconstructed neuron lengths alongside the proportion of data across different difficulty levels (Fig. 7c). The results show that within 30 mm, the proportions of difficulty levels exhibit some variation. A closer examination in Fig. 7d reveals that as neuron length increases, the proportion of higher-difficulty data gradually decreases. This suggests that longer neurons tend to have a lower proportion of difficult reconstruction tasks.
