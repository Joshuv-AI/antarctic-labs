Surface energy-driven perpendicular gradient structure in flexible composite dielectrics for high-temperature capacitive energy storage
Authors: Minhao Yang, Huarui Yan, Shiang Zhao, Yanlong Zhao, Haoran Sun, Zhongjun Zhou, Jiajun Qu, Zhenyu Jia, Zhenzhong Yang, Jun Liu, Bobo Tian, Zhi-Min Dang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flexible dielectrics are urgently needed in advanced electrical systems and modern power electronics. Nevertheless, conventional polymer dielectrics suffer from low discharged energy density and charge-discharge efficiency at elevated temperatures due to severe conduction loss. Here, we report a perpendicular gradient structured polymer composite dielectric with an inorganic hybrid crosslinking network of Si-O-Ti. Driven by surface energy difference, SiO2 accumulates on the film surface to block the charge injection, while bulk TiO2 boosts the dielectric constant. The hybrid crosslinking network of Si-O-Ti also enhances the thermal stability, mechanical modulus and insulation strength by reducing free volume. Ultimately, the obtained composite delivers a discharged energy density of 6.04 J/cm3 above 90% efficiency at 200 °C, exhibiting a 364.62% enhancement over the pristine polymer. This strategy effectively modulates surface and bulk properties, accordingly contributing to improving high-temperature capacitive energy storage performance.

Introduction

Flexible dielectrics based electrostatic capacitors are indispensable components in high-voltage power electronics and pulsed power systems owing to their intrinsic advantages of fast charge-discharge rates, high-voltage endurance and graceful failure mechanism1–3. Nevertheless, the commercially available capacitor films represented by biaxially oriented-polypropylene (BOPP) can only operate below 105 °C and its capacitive energy storage performance declines rapidly as temperature rises, failing to meet the surging demand for harsh-temperature environment exceeding 200 °C4,5. From this, heat-resistant polymers such as polyimide (PI) and polyetherimide (PEI) containing a rich number of rigid benzene and imide rings stand out due to their higher glass transition temperature (Tg) over 200 °C6,7. Unfortunately, the above high-resistant polymers usually exhibits an abrupt increase in leakage current at elevated temperatures and electric fields even when the temperature is well below the Tg, consequently giving rise to the exponential growth in conduction loss and notable reductions in the breakdown strength (Eb), discharged energy density (Ue) and charge-discharge efficiencies (η)8,9. For example, at η>90%, the Ue of Ultem PEI film decreases significantly from 1.0 J/cm3 to 0.3 J/cm3 as temperatures rises from 150 °C to 200 °C1,2. According to this equation, exploring polymer dielectrics with a higher dielectric constant is beneficial to boosting the discharged energy density. However, the dielectric constant of commercial Kapton PI and Ultem PEI films is usually below 3.5 at 103 Hz2. Therefore, the simultaneous inhibition of high-temperature conduction loss and enhancement of dielectric constant of polymer dielectrics is of critical importance for high-temperature capacitive energy storage performance.

The high-temperature conduction loss of polymer dielectrics mainly arise from Schottky emission at electrode/dielectric interface and hopping conduction in the bulk of dielectric10–14. At elevated temperatures, the energetic electrons in the metal electrode would overcome the energy barrier and travel to the surface of polymer dielectrics. The majority of free electrons comes from the electrode since the polymer dielectric is nearly insulators. The construction of a wide-bandgap inorganic barrier layer such as silicon oxide (SiO2)11,15,16, aluminum oxide (Al2O3)13,14,17 and boron nitride (BNs)18,19 on the surface of polymer dielectrics have been extensively employed to augment the barrier height and thereby mitigate the charge injection from electrode. Nevertheless, the weak interfacial adhesion and huge mismatch of thermal conductivity and Young’s modulus between organic coating layer and polymer substrate may induce the formation of cracks at the interface, ultimately leading to the intensification of electric fields and aggravating the Schottky injection. On the contrary, hopping conduction describes the bulk-limited conduction process, where the thermally excited charge carriers in the dielectric bulk can jump from one trap site to another owing to the tunneling effect. Incorporating nanostructured inorganic particles have been proven effective in the introduction of deep traps in the polymer dielectrics2,9,17. These deep traps can firmly immobilize the charge carriers and reduce their mobility, eventually contributing to the suppression of conduction loss at elevated temperatures. However, the inorganic nanoparticles tend to aggregate and is intrinsically incompatible with host polymers owing to their high surface energy. As a result, the agglomeration of nanopartilces would induce the formation of voids defects in polymer dielectrics, ultimately causing the premature failure under high electric fields. As for the enhancement of dielectric constant, significant efforts have been devoted to incorporating the inorganic nanoparticles with a high dielectric constant such as TiO29, BaTiO32 and ZrO220 into polymers dielectrics, whereas a higher loading of nanoparticles is usually required to achieve a considerable dielectric constant. Moreover, a remarkable deterioration of breakdown strength and increase of conduction loss is inevitable when the volume fraction of nanoparticles loading exceeds 1 vol%, which are particularly severe at elevated temperatures. Therefore, it is extremely difficult to decouple the insulation property and polarization intensity of polymer dielectrics via such a traditionally simple inorganic/organic composite strategy.

It is well known that the construction of a crosslinked network in polymer dielectrics via covalent linkage between polymer chains can greatly improve the thermal stability and suppress the conduction loss at elevated temperatures9,10,21–23. Nevertheless, the formation of crosslinked network usually compromises the flexibility and mobility of polymer segments. Additionally, the conventional crosslinked sites are based on a type of organic covalent linkage with inferior polarization ability24. Consequently, the enhancement of dielectric constant is seriously limited. More seriously, the high-temperature conduction loss of conventionally crosslinked polymer dielectrics is still prominent since the three dimensional network is usually regarded as a uniform structure rather than a gradient configuration along the out-of-plane directions of dielectric film. Compared with the common uniform network, the gradient structure is supposed to be effective in separately inhibiting the charge injection from electrode and charge transport in the bulk of polymer dielectrics25–28. Herein, we constructed a crosslinked hybrid networks in polymer dielectrics using a mixed inorganic oxides composed of TiO2 and SiO2 as the robust crosslinking sites. The crosslinked PEI/SiO2-TiO2 composite film is prepared via an in situ sol-gel process. This type of crosslinked network not only enhances the thermal stability owing to the restriction of the mobility of polymer chains but also endows the polymer dielectrics an adjustable dielectric property by modulating the crosslinking agent ratio of TiO2 and SiO2. Moreover, due to the large difference of surface energy between TiO2 and SiO2 components, the crosslinked polymer composite dielectric exhibits a gradient structure across the out-of-plane direction of film, where SiO2 is easily to be dispersed on the surface of film while TiO2 tends to be in the bulk. Owing to such a unique gradient structure, the TiO2 components in the bulk can boost the dielectric constant and act as deep traps to restrain the charge transport in the dielectric bulk while the SiO2 phase on the surface can block the charge injection from electrode and charge propagation across the dielectric film. Accordingly, a synergistic suppression of high-temperature conduction loss and enhancement of dielectric constant is achieved in the crosslinked PEI/SiO2-TiO2 composite film. Ultimately, the crosslinked PEI composite film with an optimal ratio of SiO2 and TiO2 exhibits a discharged energy density of 6.04 J/cm3 at 200 °C with a charge-discharge efficiency of 90%, representing a substantial improvement in comparison with the pristine PEI and all the previously reported crosslinked polymer composite dielectrics.

Results

Figure S1 describes the synthesis procedures of PEI/SiO2-TiO2 (PEI/ST) composite with a Si-O-Ti crosslinking network. The polyetheramide acid (PEAA) precursor was end-capped with APrTMOS, featuring a monoamine group, to introduce crosslinking sites29,30. Various amounts of tetraethyl orthotitanate (TEOT) and tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) were mixed with the above PEAA solution and reacted with the aforementioned crosslinking sites to induce the formation of a hybrid Si-O-Ti network. After a thermal imidization process, PEI/ST composites with an adjustable ratio of Ti and Si were obtained. Figure 1a–c depicts the schematic structures of PEI/TiO2 composite (PEI/T) with a Ti-O-Ti crosslinking network and PEI/ST with a Si-O-Ti crosslinking network. Compared with the PEI/T composite, the introduction of Si element in the PEI/ST composite could contribute to the formation of a homogeneous crosslinking network due to the smaller atom size of Si (Fig. 1b). Meanwhile, SiO2 component tends to disperse on the surface PEI/ST composite film while TiO2 prefer to stay in the bulk of film owing to the lower surface energy of SiO2 (Fig. 1c). This type of perpendicular gradient structure is anticipated to improve the high-temperature capacitive energy storage performance of PEI composite film since the SiO2 surface barrier could block the charge injection from electrode and the TiO2 bulk phase could increase the dielectric constant of composite. Figure S2 shows the Fourier transform infrared spectra (FT-IR) of PEI, PEI/T and PEI/ST composites with various concentrations of TiO2 and SiO2. The characteristic absorption peaks of PEI/TiO2, corresponding to Ti-O-Ti bond, were in the range of 450–850 cm−1. The gradually upward-shifted baseline of this absorption peak with increasing the content of TiO2 demonstrates that the Ti-O-Ti network was successfully introduced in the PEI/T composites. The presence of characteristic peaks of Si-O-Si (1000–1100 cm−1) indicates that SiO2 was successfully incorporated in the PEI/ST composites29,30. With increasing SiO2 content in PEI/ST samples, the variation of Si-O-Si and Ti-O-Ti bond peaks indicates that Si-O-Ti bond has been formed, verified by the change of peak at 940 cm−1 29,30. Figure S3 indicates that the final mass fraction of inorganic component (ca. 11 wt%) in the resultant composite samples is similar to the weight percentage (10 wt%) of TEOT or TEOT/TEOS additives in the sample preparation procedures. As shown in Fig S4, PEI/T composite films are insoluble in NMP solvent after vigorous stirring at 60 °C for 24 h owing to the formation of an inorganic Ti-O-Ti crosslinking network. On the contrary, the pristine PEI film dissolves rapidly in the solvent. Additionally, the gel content increases with the concentration of TiO2 and gradually reaches a stable value (Fig S5). Fig S6 and Figure S7 demonstrate that the inorganic Si-O-Ti crosslinking network was also constructed in the PEI/ST composite. The influence of inorganic component content on the structure of PEI composite is systematically investigated by the analysis of X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curves and contact angles. As displayed in Fig. 2a-c, the molecular chain spacing (d-spacing) of PEI/T and PEI/ST composites, deduced from XRD patterns, gradually decreases with the concentration of TiO2 and SiO2, which indicates that the crosslinking network with tightly packed polymer chains can be effectively adjusted by varying the content of inorganic component. Figures 2d, e display the DSC curves of pristine PEI, PEI/T and PEI/ST composites. As summarized in Fig. 2f, the glass transition temperature (Tg) of PEI/T composites exhibits a monotonous increase with the content of TiO2. The Tg increases from 204.8 °C for pristine PEI to 242.2 °C for PEI/12.5 T composite. The significant increase in Tg can be attributed to the robust inorganic crosslinking network, where macromolecular chains are severely constrained. Compared with the common crosslinking strategy using an organic component as a crosslinking agent, polymer dielectrics with an inorganic crosslinking network exhibit robust mechanical strength and excellent heat resistance. Such a characteristic offers the resultant polymer film superior insulation properties and dimensional stability at high temperatures, which is of great importance for high-temperature capacitive energy storage. Interestingly, the Tg decreases from 240.1 °C for PEI/10 T composite to 229.4 °C for PEI/S3T7 composite. Such a decrease is probably caused by the superior flexibility of Si-O-Si bond compared with that of Ti-O-Ti bond. Figure 2g shows the contact angles of pristine PEI, PEI/T and PEI/ST composites. As summarized in Fig. 2h, contact angle firstly increases from 78.2° for pristine PEI to 81.6° for PEI/5 T, followed by a gradual decrease to 77.8° for PEI/12.5 T composite. The increase in contact angle is mainly because of the hydrophobicity of end-capping agent while the subsequent decrease is owing to the hydrophilicity of TiO2. Figure 3 shows the dielectric property, breakdown strength and capacitive energy storage performance of PEI/T and PEI/ST composites with various concentrations of TiO2 and SiO2. As depicted in Fig. 3a, Fig. 3b and Fig. S8, the dielectric constant and loss of PEI/T and PEI/ST composites exhibit a stable frequency-dependent behavior at both room and elevated temperatures in the frequency range of 102 Hz-106 Hz. The dielectric constant increases from 3.05 for pristine PEI to 3.38 for PEI/12.5 T composite due to the high permittivity of TiO2. Conversely, the dielectric constant decreases from 3.36 for PEI/10 T to 3.21 for PEI/S7T3 composite due to the relatively low permittivity of SiO2. This result demonstrates that the bulk TiO2 component could effectively increase the dielectric constant, which is beneficial to improving the capacitive energy storage performance. A two-parameter Weibull statistical model was used to calculate the breakdown strength (Eb) of PEI composite films6. As shown in Fig. 3c, the breakdown strength of PEI/T composites at elevated temperature firstly increases from 445 MV/m for the pristine PEI to 549 MV/m for PEI/10 T composite with increasing the concentration of TiO2 to 10 wt%, followed by an obvious decrease to 521 MV/m for PEI/12.5 T sample. The increase in breakdown strength is mainly attributed to the formation of a Ti-O-Ti crosslinking network, which is helpful to reduce free volume. The deterioration of breakdown strength at higher loading of TiO2 could be ascribed to the aggregation of TiO2 cluster, which may act as a structural defect to induce an early insulation failure. For the PEI/ST composites, the breakdown strength increases from 549 MV/m to 577 MV/m as the content of SiO2 increases up to 3 wt%, followed by a significant decrease to 498 MV/m with further increasing the content of SiO2 (Fig. 3d). The increase in breakdown strength upon introducing Si element into the Ti-O-Ti crosslinking network is mainly attributed to the homogenization of crosslinked network. The Si element with a smaller atomic size could reduce the structural defects of Ti-O-Ti network and improve the insulation strength at elevated temperatures. Owing to the synergetic improvement of dielectric constant and breakdown strength, the high-temperature capacitive energy storage performance of PEI/ST composite is greatly enhanced (Fig. 3e, Fig. 3f, Fig S9 and Fig S10). Figure 4 displays the structural analysis and theoretical simulation of pristine PEI, PEI/10 T and PEI/S3T7 composites. As shown in Fig. 4a, there is no characteristic Ti peak in the XPS survey spectra of pristine PEI. However, a slight signal of Si peak is observed for the PEI/10 T composite owing to the addition of end-capping agent APrTMOS, which contains the Si element. The Ti 2p characteristic peak is visible for PEI/10 T and PEI/S3T7 composites owing to the formation of Ti-O-Ti and Si-O-Ti network. Additionally, PEI/S3T7 composite exhibits a relatively stronger intensity of Si peak compared with that of PEI/10 T, which results from the formation of Si-O-Si network. Other typical peaks of C 1 s, O 1 s and N 1 s are clearly presented in all samples, with positions consistent with those in the previous literature13,29. In order to demonstrate the perpendicular gradient structure, the XPS depth profile analysis of PEI/S3T7 composite film was carried out to reveal the evolution of Si and Ti concentration with increasing surface depth. Figure 4b and Fig. 4c display the binding energies of Si 2p and Ti 2p peaks under various sputtering times during XPS measurement. As displayed in Fig. 4b and Fig. S13, the concentration of Si decreases apparently with increasing sputtering time, indicating that Si concentration in the bulk is less than that on the surface. Nevertheless, the concentration of Ti remains stable with sputtering time (Fig. 4c). The aggregation of Si on film surface indicates that Si can easily migrate to the surface due to its lower surface energy. Figure 4d shows the cross-sectional FIB-SEM image of PEI/S3T7 composite with EDS elemental mapping of Si and Ti. It is obviously observed that Si element exhibits a gradient distribution from film surface to bulk center while the concentration of Ti element remains almost unchanged, which is consistent with the result of XPS depth profile analysis. Figure S14 shows no obvious crystalline structure in the cross-sectional FIB-TEM image. Along with the XRD patterns in Fig. 2b, this result suggests that the obtained SiO2 and TiO2 are in an amorphous state, since the imidization temperature (250 °C) used in this contribution can not trigger the inorganic crystallization. Figure S14 also indicates the uniform dispersion of inorganic components in the composites, as no obvious aggregation is observed. Apart from the gradient structure, the crosslinking network is also systematically analyzed. As displayed in Fig. 4e, PEI/10 T and PEI/S3T7 composites show a lower in-plane coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) value compared to that of pristine PEI because of the formation of Ti-O-Ti or Si-O-Ti crosslinking network. However, compared with the PEI/10 T composite, PEI/S3T7 sample exhibits a higher CTE value, which may be attributed to the superior flexibility of Si-O-Si bond. The effect of the crosslinked network on energy band is displayed in Fig. 4f. The energy band gap decreases from 3.49 eV for pristine PEI to 3.03 eV for PEI/S3T7 composite, which is consistent with the UV-vis spectra analysis (Fig. S15). The ultraviolet photoelectron spectra (UPS) measurement was carried out to reveal the changes of band diagrams at the interface of electrode/dielectric (Fig. S16 and Fig. S17). As summarized in Fig. S18, the calculated barrier height for electrons (ϕe) increases from 1.14 eV for pristine PEI to 1.35 eV for PEI/S3T7 composite. The increase in barrier height indicates that the aggregation of SiO2 on the surface of PEI/S3T7 composite can effectively block the charge injection from electrode, which is helpful to inhibiting the conduction loss at elevated temperatures and electric fields. More importantly, PEI/S3T7 composite exhibits a lower electron affinity (EAp) compared with the pristine PEI, which confirms that the injected charges are difficult to migrate from film surface to bulk phase. In addition, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was also performed to theoretically analyze the crosslinked structure (Fig. 4g). In order to illustrate the effect of perpendicular gradient structure and crosslinked structure on energy storage performance, the comparison of dielectric, insulation and capacitive energy storage performances of pristine PEI, PEI/10 T and PEI/S3T7 composites was systematically analyzed. As shown in Fig. 5a, PEI/10 T and PEI/S3T7 composites exhibit a higher dielectric constant compared with that of the pristine PEI film. Compared with PEI/10 T composite, the dielectric constant of PEI/S3T7 composite is slightly decreased after the incorporation of the Si element owing to the relatively lower dielectric constant of SiO2. Moreover, the breakdown strength increases significantly from 473 MV/m for pristine PEI to 587 MV/m for PEI/S3T7 composite at 200 °C (Fig. 5b). As shown in Fig. S19 and Fig. S20, the formation of a hybrid crosslinking network in the PEI/S3T7 composite contributes to the increase in Young’s modulus, which is responsible for the improved breakdown strength. Meanwhile, PEI/S3T7 composite shows the lowest leakage current density compared with the pristine PEI and PEI/10 T samples (Fig. 5c). The fitting of current density with hopping conduction model indicates that hopping distance reduces significantly from 1.131 nm for pristine PEI to 0.837 nm for PEI/S3T7 composite. These results demonstrate that the trap depth is significantly increased after the formation of an inorganic hybrid crosslinking network of Si-O-Ti in the PEI/S3T7 composite. Additionally, the analysis of thermally stimulated depolarization current (TSDC) was performed. As shown in Fig. 5d, compared with pristine PEI, PEI/10 T and PEI/S3T7 composites exhibit a significant increase in the TSDC peak intensity, indicating the formation of deep carrier traps. The inorganic SiO2 and TiO2 in the PEI/S3T7 composite act as charge traps to reduce the mobility of charge carriers, accordingly leading to a reduction in conduction loss at elevated temperatures. As shown in the Table. S2, compared with the pristine PEI and PEI/10 T samples, PEI/S3T7 composite exhibits the highest trap level (1.925 eV) and trap density (0.97 1020/m3), which are deduced from TSDC curves. Moreover, compared with the PEI/10 T composite, a lower peak temperature in the PEI/S3T7 composite is observed, which is consistent with the DSC result. Owing to the inhibition of conduction loss, the remanent polarization and energy loss are greatly suppressed for PEI/S3T7 composite (Fig. 5e, Fig. S21 and Fig. S22). Consequently, PEI/S3T7 composite exhibits a discharged energy density (Ue) of 6.04 J/cm3 with a charge-discharge efficiency (η) above 90 % at 200 °C, which is greatly higher than that of pristine PEI (1.30 J/cm3) (Fig. 5f). As shown in Fig. S23 and Fig. S24, a gradual decrease in the discharged energy density with increasing temperature is observed owing to the deterioration of insulation properties and an increase in energy loss. As shown in Fig. 5g, an apparent temperature rise is observed for the capacitor consisting of pristine PEI and the maximum temperature reaches almost 490 K exceeding its Tg. As a result, this temperature rise would cause the thermal runaway and breakdown. On the contrary, the temperature for PEI/S3T7 composite capacitor stays almost unchanged and reaches a steady temperature of 478 K, which is much lower than its Tg. In addition, PEI/S3T7 composite shows a stable charge-discharge performance even after 5×104 charge-discharge cycles (Fig. 5h). The discharged energy density with a charge-discharge efficiency above 90 % of PEI/S3T7 composite is higher than those of most reported works (Fig. 5i)1,3,9,11,13–15,21,32–43. We also analyze the capacitive performance and gradient structure retention of PEI/S3T7 composite film after a long bending cycle (Fig. S25). As displayed in Fig. S26, even after 103 bending cycles, PEI/S3T7 composite film exhibits an excellent charge and discharge behavior at elevated temperatures. As summarized in Fig. S27, the discharged energy density of PEI/S3T7 composite film at 90% efficiency drops slightly from 6.04 J/cm3 to 5.74 J/cm3 after 103 bending cycles, demonstrating good capacitive performance retention in this gradient-structured sample. Moreover, the Si 2p XPS depth profile spectra indicate that the gradient structure remains unchanged (Fig. S28), verified by a continuous decrease in the intensity of Si 2p peak. Discussion

A perpendicular gradient structure with a hybrid crosslinking network of Si-O-Ti is constructed in the polymer composite dielectrics via employing the difference in surface energy of SiO2 and TiO2. The SiO2 with a lower surface energy tends to migrate to film surface, while TiO2 with a higher surface energy prefers to be in the bulk region. The aggregation of SiO2 near film surface could mitigate the charge injection from electrode and TiO2 in the bulk phase could enhance dielectric constant. Moreover, the formation of an inorganic crosslinking network is helpful in reducing free volume and improving thermal stability. Consequently, the conduction loss at elevated temperatures and electric fields is significantly inhibited. The resultant perpendicular gradient structured polymer composite dielectric could deliver a discharged energy density of 6.04 J/cm3 with a charge-discharge efficiency of 90% at 630 MV/m, which exhibits an increase of 364.62% in comparison to that of pristine PEI (1.30 J/cm3). The approach reported in this contribution may offer a feasible paradigm to inhibit the conduction loss and enhance the high-temperature capacitive performance of polymer composite dielectrics.

Methods

Materials

Bisphenol A diether dianhydride (BPADA), 4,4’-oxydianiline (ODA), 3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane (APrTMOS), tetraethyl orthotitanate (TEOT), tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), acetylacetone (ACAC), n-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) and other reactants or solvents of analytical reagent grade were purchased from beijing chemical reagents company. All materials were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of polymers

The preparation of pristine PEI and PEI composites is mainly composed of pre-polymerization and thermal imidization. A certain molarity of diamine monomer (ODA) was o thoroughly dissolved in the NMP solution. Then, the same molar amount of dianhydride monomer (BPADA) was added in five sequences and completely dissolved in the above diamine solution. The resultant solution was continuously stirred at room temperature for 6 h to synthesize a viscous solution of polyetheramide acid (PEAA). APrTMOS with a monoamine group was added to the above solution and stirred for 1 h to introduce crosslinking sites. Various amounts of TEOT, TEOS and ACAC were completely mixed with the above PEAA solution for 12 h to uniformly crosslink PEAA. The above crosslinked precursor solution was drop-cast on a clean glass substrate. After solvent evaporation at 80 °C for 2 h, a stepwise thermal treatment with 1 h isothermal holds at 120 °C, 150 °C, 200 °C and 250 °C was carried out to accomplish imidization. Afterwards, the freestanding film was peeled off the substrate by soaking in deionized water, followed by vacuum drying at 120 °C for 4 h to remove residual water. The resultant film is denoted as PEI/10%-SiO2y%-TiO2z% (PEI/SyTz), where 10% represents the total inorganic content (wt %) in the composite based on the assumptions of complete conversion of TEOT and TEOS into TiO2 and SiO2, respectively. As for the PEI/x%-TiO2 (PEI/xT) composite films, the x indicates the TiO2 content (wt %) in the composite on the prerequisite of the complete conversion of TEOT into TiO2. As a reference sample, the pristine PEI film containing only BPADA and ODA was made by a similar procedure. In this contribution, the total inorganic content in the PEI/SyTz sample is 10 wt% since the PEI-x-T film become brittle, accompanied by a rapid degradation of insulation performance when the loading of TiO2 exceeds 10 wt%. For instance, the abbreviation of PEI/S3T7 indicates the composite contains 10 wt % of mixed inorganic oxides with 30 wt % SiO2 and 70 wt % TiO2. The thickness of pristine PEI and PEI composite films is in a range of 10-15 μm.

Characterization

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of pristine PEI and PEI composite films were recorded by a PerkinElmer Spectrum 3TM spectrometer. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of pristine PEI and PEI composite films was carried out on a TA TGA 550 instrument. The furnace was heated from 30 to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min under N2 atmosphere. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were measured on a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer. The contact angle was collected on Krüss DSA25S Drop Shape Analyzer and calculated via an average value of the contact angles from five droplets at different locations of each film. Ultra-violet visible (UV-vis) spectra were measured on a LAMBDA 750 spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer). X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) were obtained by using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nexsa G2 analyzer. The XPS depth profile analysis was carried out on a Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB Xi+ spectrometer using a cluster Ar+ beam with an energy of 6 keV to sputter etch the surface of polymer film. The sputter etching area is 2.5 mm×2.5 mm and the time for each etching cycle is 240 s. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectra (UPS) were collected on a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250XI instrument using He-I (21.2 eV) as the light source. The Fermi level was calibrated using clean Au. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) characterization was performed on a Netzsch DSC 214 Polyma thermal analyzer under a nitrogen atmosphere, with a scanning rate of 5 °C/min, ranging from 50 °C to 300 °C. To eliminate the thermal history, the specimen was subjected to a heating-cooling cycle before formal analysis, and the glass transition temperature (Tg) was extracted from the inflection point on the subsequent second heating curve. Thermomechanical analysis (TMA) was carried out on a Netzsch TMA 402 F3 Hyperion thermal analyzer with a static load of 0.02 N under a nitrogen atmosphere, and the temperature was ramped from 50 °C to 250 °C with a heating rate of 5 °C/min. The in-plane coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) was derived from the dimensional variation recorded in the second heating profile. The Young’s modulus was obtained with an INSTRON 3343 tester at a rate of 200 mm/min. The bending cycling test was carried out on an INSTRON 3343 instrument. The cross-sectional analysis of polymer film was performed on a Focused Ion Beam-Scanning Electron Microscope (FIB-SEM) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Helios G4 UX, ThermoFisher). The high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was conducted on a JEM-2100F instrument (JEOL). The cross-sectional specimen for SEM and HRTEM observation was prepared on the FIB system using Ga-ions to remove the surrounding material with high spatial precision. Before electrical characterization, symmetric gold electrodes with a diameter of 20 mm were deposited on both sides of the specimens via magnetron sputtering. The dielectric spectra and thermally stimulated depolarization currents (TSDC) measurements were then carried out on the obtained samples. Dielectric spectra were collected using a Novocontrol Concept 80 spectrometer at room temperature and 200 °C with a scanning frequency of 102−106 Hz. TSDC measurements were conducted using the Novocontrol platform integrated with a Keysight 2985B electrostatic high-resistance meter. The specimens were polarized for 30 min at 200 °C under a DC electric field of 30 MV/m. Subsequently, the polarized samples were quenched to −10 °C while the DC electric field remained applied. After a 3 min isothermal hold at −10 °C, the external electric field was removed. The samples were short-circuited before heating to 250 °C at 3 °C/min, and the depolarization current was recorded continuously throughout the heating process. To evaluate the dielectric breakdown strength, D-E hysteresis loops, leakage current and cyclic charge-discharge performance, gold electrodes were deposited on both sides of specimens via sputtering. The electrode diameter was fixed at 4 mm unless otherwise specified. Breakdown strength measurements were carried out using a BOHER high-voltage power source. The voltage was increased continuously at a rate of 500 V/s until dielectric breakdown occurred. After conducting at least 10 parallel tests on each sample, the experimental data were then analyzed through a two-parameter Weibull statistic. D-E hysteresis loops and cyclic charge-discharge stability were characterized via a high-voltage polarization test system with a unipolar wave. The frequency of the unipolar wave was fixed at 50 Hz unless otherwise noted. For leakage current testing, a platform integrated a Keithley 2290-10 power supply and a Keithley 2635B current source meter was utilized.

Simulation

Density functional theory (DFT) simulations were performed within Gaussian 16, where the B3LYP functional coupled with D3BJ dispersion correction was consistently applied. Specifically, a 6-31 G(d) basis set was used for geometry optimization and frequency calculations, and single-point energy calculations were implemented at the B3LYP-D3BJ/6-311 G(d, p) level. Frontier orbital and electrostatic potential analysis were accomplished using Multiwfn and VMD packages. The density and FFV values of the samples were theoretically investigated via molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. BIOVIA Materials Studio 8.0 was utilized to construct unit cells for pristine PEI, PEI/10 T and PEI/S3T7 composites, which contain 20 chains with 4 repeat units for each chain. Structural geometry and dynamic optimizations were run in the Forcite module with the universal force field. All systems were dynamically optimized at 300 K for 500 ps with a time step of 1 fs. Ewald summation was utilized for the evaluation of electrostatic interactions, whereas the atom-based cutoff approach was applied to quantify van der Waals interactions. Thermal control was completed by the Nose-Hoover thermostat, and pressure regulation was accomplished through a Berendsen barostat. The overall volume of the simulated systems is composed of the molecular occupied volume (Vo) and free volume (Vf).
