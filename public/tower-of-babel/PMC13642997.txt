Serum ADAMTS18 Levels at Admission Are Associated With 3-Month Functional Outcome After Acute Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Ting Cui, Huan Wang, Yuyi Zhu, Mingxi Chen, Yaqi Chen, Keying Wu, Junfeng Liu, Zilong Hao, Bo Wu, Deren Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aims: ADAMTS18 is a secreted metalloproteinase implicated in vascular homeostasis and protection against ischemic brain injury. However, its clinical relevance in acute ischemic stroke (AIS) remains unclear. Methods: We retrospectively analyzed 194 AIS patients from the Chengdu Stroke Registry. Serum ADAMTS18 levels were measured within 48 hours of admission using ELISA. Associations between ADAMTS18 levels and 3-month poor outcomes (modified Rankin Scale score 3-6) were evaluated using logistic regression. Predictive performance was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). Results: Among 194 patients, the median ADAMTS18 was 21.45 ng/mL (IQR 19.68-23.58 ng/mL), 79 (40.7%) had poor outcomes. Patients with poor outcomes had lower serum ADAMTS18 levels [19.84 (18.46-21.24) vs. 22.88 (21.14-24.81) ng/mL,P＜0.001]. After adjustment, lower ADAMTS18 levels were independently associated with poor outcome (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.58-0.83,P＜0.001). Quartile analysis showed that compared with the lowest quartile, patients in Q3 (OR = 0.26; 95% CI: 0.08-0.83;P = 0.023) and Q4 (OR = 0.02; 95% CI: 0.00-0.11;P＜0.001) had progressively lower odds of poor functional outcome (P for trend ＜0.001). Restricted cubic spline analysis confirmed a negative linear association (adjusted Poverall＜0.001). Incorporating ADAMTS18 into predictive models improved discrimination (AUC = 0.91 vs. 0.86,P = 0.011), IDI (11.14%,P＜0.001), and NRI (77.1%,P＜0.001). Conclusion: Lower admission ADAMTS18 levels are independently associated with poor 3-month functional outcomes after AIS and may provide additional prognostic information.

Ting Cui and Huan Wang contributed equally to this work.

Background

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is a leading cause of mortality and disability in China 1) , with nearly one third of patients dying or remaining disabled within three months 2) . Timely recognition of patients with a high probability of poor outcomes is critical to guide treatment, counseling, and post-discharge planning 3 , 4) . However, existing prognostic models based on clinical and demographic factors show suboptimal predictive accuracy and poor generalizability 5) , prompting growing interest in blood biomarkers that are objective and easily obtainable 6 , 7) .

Here, we investigated circulating levels of ADAMTS18 (a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin type 1 motif, member 18) as a potential serum biomarker. ADAMTS18 is a secreted metalloproteinase with emerging roles in cerebrovascular biology. Experimental studies in mice suggest a protective function in stroke-induced brain injury. Knocking out ADAMTS18 led to larger cerebral infarcts and exacerbated neurological deficits following transient middle cerebral artery occlusion 8) . Conversely, administering exogenously an active ADAMTS18 fragment to mice after such occlusion reduced infarct area and mitigated neurological deficits 9) . ADAMTS18 is primarily expressed in endothelial cells 10) , which are critical for vascular protection in ischemia-related brain injury 11 , 12) . It is involved in extracellular matrix remodeling 13) , cerebrovascular morphogenesis 14) , endothelial sprouting 15) , and blood-brain barrier integrity 16) , suggesting ADAMTS18 may regulate cerebrovascular integrity. In addition, the C-terminal fragment of ADAMTS18 has been reported to induce oxidative platelet fragmentation and promote the dissociation of platelet aggregates 9) , suggesting a role in modulating cerebrovascular hemostasis and thrombosis.

Despite these mechanistic insights, the clinical relevance of circulating ADAMTS18 remains unclear. While lower levels of another ADAMTS family member, ADAMTS13, have been associated with poor outcome in ischemic stroke 17 - 19) , it is unknown whether serum ADAMTS18 levels at admission predict functional outcomes after AIS.

Aim

In this study, we investigated the association between admission serum ADAMTS18 levels and 3-month functional outcomes in AIS patients, aiming to evaluate its potential as a novel prognostic biomarker.

Methods

Patients

We retrospectively analyzed AIS patients admitted to the Department of Neurology, West China Hospital from December 2018 to December 2021, enrolled in the Chengdu Stroke Registry 2) . Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, admission within 24 hours of symptom onset, and a diagnosis of AIS, defined as acute focal neurological deficits attributable to infarction in one or more brain regions, supported by either persistent symptoms ＞24 hours or confirmatory neuroimaging 20 , 21) . Exclusion criteria included pre-stroke disability (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score ＞2), serum samples unavailable for ADAMTS18 measurement due to prior use in other analyses, loss to follow-up, concurrent autoimmune disorders, and severe or advanced comorbid conditions, such as significant liver disease, heart failure, end-stage renal disease, serious infections, and malignancies.

Definitions of Variables and Outcomes

Baseline data included demographic characteristics, stroke severity, stroke subtypes, vascular risk factors, and treatment information. Stroke severity was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) 22) . Stroke subtypes were classified according to the Trial of Org 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment classifcation (TOAST) criteria as large artery atherosclerosis, cardioembolism, small artery occlusion, stroke of other determined etiology, or stroke of undetermined etiology 23) .

Vascular risk factors included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease, atrial fibrillation, current smoking, and current alcohol consumption, as previously defined 24) . Reperfusion therapy was categorized as intravenous thrombolysis alone, endovascular therapy alone, or both.

Functional outcome was assessed at 3 months using the mRS by trained personnel via telephone interview 25) . A poor outcome was defined as an mRS score of 3-6.

Measurement of Serum ADAMTS18

Blood samples collected within 48 hours of admission were stored at −80℃ until analysis. Serum ADAMTS18 concentrations were measured by KJ Biotechnology (Chengdu, China) using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (catalog no. MB-00175A, Jiangsu Meibiao Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China). Laboratory personnel were blinded to clinical data. All samples were assayed in duplicate wells on the same plate under standardized conditions, and the mean value of the two measurements was used for subsequent analyses.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), R version 4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and MedCalc version 18.2.1 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium). A two-sided P＜0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Categorical variables were expressed as n (%), and continuous variables as mean±standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), depending on normality tested by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Between-group differences were evaluated using the χ² or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and the Student’s t test, Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, as appropriate.

Multivariable binary logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between serum ADAMTS18 levels and poor outcome. ADAMTS18 was analyzed both as a continuous variable (per ng/mL increase) and as quartiles. Covariates with P＜0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariable model. This P-value-based approach was used to reduce the risk of overfitting given the number of outcome events. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). For quartile-based analysis, the odds of poor outcome were compared across quartiles, using the lowest quartile as the reference. A linear trend across quartiles was tested by entering the median value of each quartile as a continuous variable 26) . A potential dose–response relationship was examined using restricted cubic splines with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of ADAMTS18 levels 27) .

Two logistic regression models were developed to predict poor outcomes: (1) a base model including all clinical and demographic variables associated with P＜0.10 in univariate analysis, and (2) an extended model that additionally incorporated serum ADAMTS18 levels. Model fit was evaluated using the Cox–Snell R² statistic 28) . Predictive performance was compared using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). All AUC analyses were performed in R using the pROC package. Apparent AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test, whereas internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling to estimate optimism-corrected AUCs and the bootstrap distribution of the AUC difference 29) . In addition, incremental predictive value was further assessed using the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and net reclassification improvement (NRI) indices 30) .

Results

Baseline Characteristics by Functional Outcome

A total of 1,654 patients were screened, and 194 patients were included in the final analysis after applying the predefined criteria ( Fig.1 ) . Among the included patients, the median age was 66.0 years (IQR 55.0-72.0 years), and 70 (36.1%) patients were women. The median ADAMTS18 was 21.45 ng/mL (IQR 19.68-23.58 ng/mL), the median time from symptom onset to blood sampling was 5.0 hours (IQR 3.0-9.5 hours), and 79 (40.7%) experienced poor functional outcomes at 3 months. Compared with patients with good outcomes, those with poor outcomes had significantly lower serum ADAMTS18 levels on admission, were older, and had higher baseline NIHSS scores. They also exhibited a higher prevalence of atrial fibrillation and were more likely to have received endovascular therapy, whereas rates of current smoking, alcohol consumption, and small-artery occlusion stroke subtype were lower (all P＜0.05; Table 1 ).

Baseline Characteristics by ADAMTS18 Levels

To further explore the clinical correlates of ADAMTS18, baseline characteristics were analyzed according to ADAMTS18 quartiles ( Supplementary Table 1 ) . Patients with lower ADAMTS18 levels tended to be older, have higher baseline NIHSS scores and a higher proportion of atrial fibrillation, and have a lower proportion of current smoking (all P＜0.05; Supplementary Table 1 ). Although lower ADAMTS18 levels were associated with a higher proportion of cardioembolism and a lower proportion of small-artery occlusion (P = 0.037; Supplementary Table 1 ), ADAMTS18 levels did not differ significantly across TOAST subtypes overall (P = 0.075; Supplementary Fig.1 ) .

Independent Association between ADAMTS18 and 3-month Functional Outcome

When serum ADAMTS18 was analyzed as a continuous variable, higher levels were independently associated with a lower odds of poor outcome (OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.54-0.74, P＜0.001; Table 2 ). After adjusting for age, alcohol consumption, atrial fibrillation, baseline NIHSS score, current smoking, reperfusion therapy, and stroke subtypes, each 1 ng/mL increase in ADAMTS18 corresponded to a 30% reduction in the odds of poor outcome (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.58-0.83, P＜0.001; Table 2 ).

Stratification by quartiles revealed a stepwise inverse association. After multivariable adjustments, compared with patients in the lowest quartile, those in the third quartile had a significantly lower odds of poor outcome (OR 0.26, 95% CI 0.08-0.83, P = 0.023; Table 2 ), and those in the fourth quartile had an even lower odds (OR 0.02, 95% CI 0.00-0.11, P＜0.001; Table 2 ). The trend across quartiles was statistically significant (Ptrend ＜0.001; Table 2 ), indicating a dose-response relationship. Incremental Prognostic Value of Serum ADAMTS18

Both logistic regression models demonstrated good calibration based on the Hosmer-Lemeshow test ( Table 3 ) . the model incorporating serum ADAMTS18 showed improved model fit and discrimination compared with the clinical model, with a higher explained variance (R² = 0.47 vs. 0.39) and a higher apparent AUC (0.91 vs. 0.86; P = 0.011; Table 3 , Fig.3 ) . After internal validation using bootstrap resampling, the optimism-corrected AUC remained higher for the model including ADAMTS18 compared with the clinical model alone (0.89 vs. 0.85, P＜0.001; Table 3 ). Addition of ADAMTS18 significantly improved outcome prediction, with an IDI of 11.14% (95% CI 6.82-15.46%, P＜0.001; Table 3 ) and a NRI of 77.12% (95% CI 51.3-103.0%, P＜0.001; Table 3 ). These results indicate that serum ADAMTS18 provides meaningful incremental prognostic value beyond conventional clinicodemographic predictors.

Discussion

In this study, we found that higher serum ADAMTS18 levels at admission were independently associated with good functional outcomes after AIS. A significant dose-response relationship was observed, with progressively higher ADAMTS18 levels corresponding to higher likelihood of good outcome. Beyond its standalone association, incorporating ADAMTS18 into prognostic models containing conventional clinicodemographic factors resulted in improved discriminative performance, supporting its incremental prognostic value. Notably, the enhancement in model discrimination persisted after bootstrap-based internal validation, indicating that the observed improvement was robust to correction for optimism and not solely attributable to overfitting.

Our findings are broadly consistent with accumulating genetic and experimental evidence suggesting that ADAMTS18 is relevant to cerebrovascular homeostasis. The ADAMTS18 rs7192208-G variant has been associated with reduced white matter integrity 31) , and preclinical studies have linked ADAMTS18 deficiency to abnormal middle cerebral artery development 14) , metabolic vulnerability 32) , and aggravated ischemic brain injury after transient middle cerebral artery occlusion 8) . Recent data further suggest that ADAMTS18 deficiency may be associated with impaired neurovascular homeostasis under inflammatory conditions, including dysregulation of BBB-related markers and poorer BBB integrity after lipopolysaccharide stimulation 16) . Collectively, these findings provide supportive context for the observed association between lower circulating ADAMTS18 levels and poorer functional recovery after acute ischemic stroke.

Our clinical findings may be interpreted in light of several potential mechanisms. One possible explanation is that lower ADAMTS18 levels may reflect less favorable endothelial homeostasis, which could be relevant to BBB integrity in the downstream ischemic microvasculature rather than at the site of the occluded large artery, although BBB permeability was not directly assessed in the present study. Experimental studies have implicated ADAMTS18 in endothelial biology, including fibronectin-associated extracellular matrix remodeling 13) and endothelial sprouting 15) , supporting a potential link with vascular integrity. In addition, ADAMTS18 may influence thrombotic processes, as a thrombin-cleaved C-terminal fragment of ADAMTS18 has been reported to promote platelet fragmentation, dissolve platelet aggregates, and attenuate arterial occlusion in experimental models 9) . Taken together, higher circulating ADAMTS18 levels may indirectly reflect more preserved vascular homeostasis and a more balanced thrombotic milieu, both of which may be associated with better neurological recovery. Nevertheless, these mechanistic interpretations remain hypothesis-generating, and the ADAMTS18–BBB link should be considered a possible downstream pathway rather than an established explanation for the observed clinical association.

Clinically, our findings suggest that higher serum ADAMTS18 at admission could help identify patients at high likelihood of good outcomes and enhance individualized prognostication. Moreover, ADAMTS18 may represent a potential therapeutic target for neuroprotection, although further studies are needed to explore this possibility. However, several limitations should be noted. First, a substantial proportion of screened patients were excluded, mainly because serum samples were unavailable for ADAMTS18 measurement and some patients were lost to follow-up. Therefore, selection bias could not be avoided, and the included cohort may represent a selected subgroup of patients with acute ischemic stroke, which may limit the generalizability of the present findings. Second, ADAMTS18 activity was not assessed, which may differ from circulating concentrations. Third, serum ADAMTS18 was measured at a single time point during the acute phase of stroke. Despite the relatively short onset-to-sampling interval, potential dynamic changes in ADAMTS18 after stroke onset could not be evaluated, as within-patient temporal trajectories were not available. Fourth, despite the use of resampling-based internal validation, model performance was evaluated exclusively in the derivation cohort without external validation; accordingly, the performance estimates may be optimistic and should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the observational design cannot exclude residual confounding from unmeasured or incompletely measured factors. In particular, occlusion site and reperfusion status were not systematically analyzed, and residual confounding related to large-vessel occlusion may remain despite adjustment for admission NIHSS. Other factors, such as pre-existing small-vessel disease, non-significant infection or inflammatory status, may also affect both ADAMTS18 levels and clinical outcomes. Prospective, multiethnic studies with serial ADAMTS18 measurements are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusion

Higher serum ADAMTS18 levels were associated with a lower likelihood of poor 3-month functional outcome and may provide modest incremental prognostic information beyond conventional clinical models. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the single-time-point measurement, limited sample size, and lack of external validation. Further validation in independent cohorts is needed.
