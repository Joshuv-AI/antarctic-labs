Bidirectional electron donation in Cu1−Ti pairs on TiOx for efficient nitric oxide electroreduction
Authors: Yan Zhang, Ning Yan, Bing Zhou, Guangming Zhan, Jiaxian Wang, Long Zhao, Lufa Hu, Kaiyuan Wang, Junzhou Xu, Ke Cheng, Lingzhi Zhou, Xiang-Kui Gu, Yancai Yao, Lizhi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrocatalytic nitric oxide reduction (NORR) enables the efficient abatement of NO while simultaneously synthesizing valuable ammonia (NH3). However, the strong polarity of NO hinders its effective activation on conventional NORR catalysts because of their single-site interaction mode, wherein either the nitrogen (N) or oxygen (O) atom of NO binds to the catalyst through a unidirectional electron transfer. In this study, we construct dual-atom Cu1−Ti pairs on the titanium oxide (TiOx) substrate by using the complementary affinities of Ti for nitrogen and Cu for oxygen, which simultaneously activates both ends of the N=O bond. The designed Cu1−Ti pairs achieve competitive NORR performance with an NH3 yield of 189.9 μmol h−1 cm−2 and a Faradaic efficiency of 93.7%. In situ techniques and theoretical calculations demonstrate that this dual-atom configuration shifts the mechanism from an end-on adsorption pathway observed on homonuclear pairs to a fully activating side-on mode. By highlighting the importance of adapted active site design in the electrocatalytic activation of polar molecules, this study facilitates the development of next-generation catalysts for complex transformations.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Energy

Introduction

Nitric oxide (NO) is a prevalent air contaminant with annually global emissions of more than 43 million tons from industrial and automobile exhaust1,2, and has caused serious environmental and ecological issues, such as haze, acid rain, and photochemical smog3. Although selective catalytic and selective non-catalytic reduction can effectively remove NO, these methods typically operate under harsh reaction conditions involving high temperatures (300–500 °C) and pressure, utilizing valuable ammonia (NH3) to reduce NO into harmless but worthless nitrogen (N2), resulting in high energy demands and carbon emissions4,5. In terms of waste recycling, NO in exhaust is a promising nitrogen source that can be upgraded to value-added NH36, which is extensively used in modern fertilizer manufacturing and is a carbon-free hydrogen energy carrier7,8. Therefore, simultaneously achieving NO abatement and NH3 production simultaneously can contribute to the sustainable development goals3,9,10.

Electrochemical NO reduction to NH3 (NORR), which is powered by renewable energy, is a promising and sustainable approach for both NO abatement and nitrogen valorization11,12. However, the efficiency of this process is essentially limited by the difficulty of activating the strongly polarized N=O bond in NO13,14. The high bond energy (~607 kJ/mol) and asymmetric electron distribution of this heteronuclear molecule create significant catalytic challenges15. While prior work has shown that the degree of activation is correlated with the degree of charge accumulation on the NO molecule13,14, existing methods—such as heteroatom doping16,17 or defect engineering18–20—typically rely on single-site catalysts that engage only one end of the molecule. This unidirectional electron transfer results in incomplete activation, as indicated by low net charge transfer (typically ≤0.5 e−)20,21 and consequently low NH3 production rates, especially at dilute NO concentrations (<20%), where yields seldom exceed 100 μmol h−1 cm−2 22–24.

Dual-atom sites (M1−M2) present a compelling approach for activating polar molecules like NO by enabling bidentate adsorption25–27. The goal is to pair metals with complementary affinities by using N-philic Ti and O-philic Cu to create a stable M1−N=O−M2 configuration. This geometry facilitates bidirectional electron donation: the unoccupied orbitals of M1 and M2 can accept lone-pair electrons from the N and O atoms of NO, respectively, whereas the electrons in the occupied d-orbitals of the dual metal sites are donated back to the 2π* orbitals of NO through the “donation–backdonation” mechanism13,28. This strong d-2π* orbital coupling can activate the adsorbed NO into a radical-like state, facilitating the elongation of the N=O bond and priming it for subsequent hydrogenation29,30. Moreover, the difference in electron distributions and valence states between M1 and M2 can significantly induce polarization of the adsorbed NO molecule and its activation.

By leveraging these merits of dual-atom site design, we engineer atomically dispersed Cu into the Ti−Ti pair motifs of commercial Ti felt to construct Cu1−Ti dual-atom pairs. At −0.6 V vs. the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE), Cu1−Ti pairs achieve a high Faradaic efficiency for NH3 of 93.7% and a yield rate of 189.9 μmol h−1 cm−2, both of which are higher than those of Ti−Ti pairs (63.5% and 58.5 μmol h−1 cm−2). To elucidate the origin of this improved catalytic performance, we combine in situ spectroscopic measurements and density functional theory (DFT) calculations to probe the NO activation and hydrogenation mechanism.

Results

Theoretical calculation

We first performed DFT calculations on Cu1−Ti pairs modeled on a TiO2 surface (Fig. 1a). On homonuclear Ti−Ti pairs, NO adsorbed in a monodentate configuration via the N atom, with an adsorption energy of −1.67 eV (Supplementary Fig. 1). In stark contrast, the Cu1−Ti pairs promoted a stable, side-on bidentate adsorption geometry (Ti−N=O−Cu; Fig. 1b), with a stronger adsorption energy of −1.90 eV. Among possible bidentate configurations, the Ti−N=O−Cu mode was energetically favorable (Supplementary Fig. 2), which confirmed the predicted affinity of Ti for N and Cu for O. This bidentate binding significantly activated the N=O bond, elongating it from 1.15 Å on Ti−Ti pairs to 1.21 Å. Notably, this improved activation substantially reduced the thermodynamic barrier for the pivotal first hydrogenation step (*NO → *NHO), which shifted the reaction free energy from an endergonic +0.66 eV on Ti−Ti pairs to a strongly exergonic −0.69 eV on Cu1−Ti pairs (Fig. 1c). These results collectively indicated more effective activation and more thermodynamically favorable *NO hydrogenation on Cu1−Ti pairs than on homonuclear Ti−Ti pairs. Notably, these DFT simulations adopted simplified ideal models that ignored complex interfacial and solvent effects, thus providing only qualitative theoretical guidance.

Synthesis and characterization

On the basis of theoretical predictions, we synthesized Cu1−Ti pairs using a straightforward drop-coating–annealing process by combining a defect-engineering strategy and metal–support interactions. The naturally grown titanium oxide (TiOx) layer on the surface of the Ti felt was enriched with oxygen vacancies (OVs), which induced the lattice relaxation and distortion of the initially ordered Ti−O−Ti framework. This structural rearrangement enabled the formation of Ti−OV−Ti sites and coordinatively unsaturated Ti atoms, both of which tend to intrinsically stabilize external atoms through electronic and geometrical interactions31–33. After the Cu precursor on Ti felt was coated, subsequent heat treatment in air resulted in the incorporation of Cu species into these relaxed Ti−OV−Ti motifs, inducing the formation of oxygen-bridged bonds between Cu and Ti species and thereby constructing Cu1−Ti pairs (Supplementary Fig. 3)34. In comparison, Ti felt subjected to the same thermal treatment exhibited a sharp decrease in surface OVs owing to the oxygen refilling, which facilitated the generation of a dense and highly symmetric Ti−O−Ti lattice (Ti−Ti pairs) (Supplementary Fig. 4)35. This controlled synthesis thus provided a direct approach for creating the desired active sites.

Owing to a low Cu loading of 0.38 wt%, as determined by inductively coupled plasma‒atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP‒OES), crystalline Cu or CuOx nanoparticles were not observed in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern or Raman spectra (Supplementary Figs. 5‒6). Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) confirmed that Cu was individually dispersed on the surface of the TiOx support (Fig. 1d). The atomic-scale dispersion was further validated by the corresponding 3D and line-scan intensity profiles (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 7) and by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping, which revealed a homogeneous distribution of Cu throughout the structure with no detectable clusters or nanoparticles (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9).

X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectroscopy was used to elucidate the electronic structure and local coordination of the Cu centers in the Cu1−Ti pairs. The X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) profiles revealed that the Cu K-edge of the Cu1−Ti pairs lay between those of the Cu2O and CuO references (Fig. 1g), indicating an oxidation state between Cu(I) and Cu(II), consistent with X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). Crucially, the Fourier-transform extended XAFS (EXAFS) spectrum (Fig. 1h) and its wavelet transform (Supplementary Figs. 12‒14) showed a dominant peak but lacked a scattering peak near 2.24 Å, which is characteristic of Cu−Cu metallic bonds. This finding confirmed the exclusive presence of atomically dispersed Cu species. Quantitative EXAFS fitting analysis revealed that the primary peak at ~1.50 Å corresponds to the first coordination shell of Cu−O bonds, whereas a distinct secondary peak at ~2.58 Å is attributed to Cu−Ti scattering (Supplementary Table 1)36–38. This finding directly confirmed for the formation of the intended Cu1−Ti pairs. Moreover, the fitting results indicated a coordination number of approximately four oxygen atoms around each Cu center (Fig. 1i), which suggested a highly unsaturated coordination environment that is favorable for adsorbing and activating NO molecules.

Electrocatalytic NORR performance

The electrocatalytic NORR performance of the Cu1−Ti pairs was evaluated in an air-tight H-cell using 0.25 M K2SO4 electrolyte. Upon introducing a 20 vol% NO/Ar feed, the Cu1−Ti electrode exhibited a distinct increase in cathodic current density in linear sweep voltammetry (LSV). In contrast, the Ti−Ti control electrode exhibited negligible response (Fig. 2a), indicating the enhanced NORR activity of Cu1−Ti. We subsequently used ion chromatography and colorimetry to quantitatively determine the NH3 generated during the NORR (Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16). The consistency of the NH3 concentrations in parallel measurements validated the reliability and accuracy of the quantification methods (Supplementary Fig. 17). Compared with the Ti−Ti pairs, the Cu1−Ti pairs achieved significantly higher NH3 yields across a wide potential range (Fig. 2b), revealing a synergistic effect between the Cu and Ti sites for improved NORR.

A detailed potential-dependent study revealed a volcano-shaped relationship between the FENH3 and the applied potential for the Cu1 − Ti pairs, with optimal performance achieved at −0.6 VRHE (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 18). At this potential, the Cu1−Ti pairs delivered a competitive NH3 yield of 189.9 μmol h−1 cm−2 with a FENH3 of 93.7% (Supplementary Fig. 19 and Table 2), and the FEH2 was restricted to 4.7%, outperforming the Ti−Ti pairs control (58.5 μmol h−1 cm−2 and 63.5% FENH3). In addition, NH2OH was detected as the only nitrogen-containing byproduct with a corresponding Faradaic efficiency of 1.5% (Supplementary Figs. 20−22). To evaluate practical applicability, we also conducted NORR tests under a low NO concentration (1 vol% NO/Ar). Notably, the Cu1−Ti pairs achieved an optimized NH3 production rate of 37.59 μmol h−1 cm−2 and an FE of 89.1%, which was also competitive under identical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 23). To assess intrinsic activity, the NH3 yield was normalized to the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) by recording the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves (Supplementary Fig. 24)39. The resulting ECSA-normalized activity of the Cu1−Ti pairs was 1.6 times greater than that of the Ti−Ti pairs (Fig. 2d), a difference likely attributable to the lower charge-transfer resistance and faster electron-transfer kinetics, as indicated by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS, Supplementary Fig. 25).

To unequivocally assign the nitrogen source of the NH3 product, we conducted control experiments. Alternating the gas feed between Ar and NO demonstrated that NH3 production occurred exclusively during NO bubbling (Fig. 2e). In the isotope-labeling experiments, when 14NO was replaced with 15NO, the corresponding 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) signal shifted from a triplet (14NH4+) to a doublet (15NH4+), which confirmed that the NH3 originated solely from the electroreduction of NO (Fig. 2f). Furthermore, the Cu1−Ti pairs were very stable and there was no significant decrease in NH3 yield or FENH3 during 20 consecutive electrolysis cycles (Fig. 2g) and 120 h continuous NORR tests (Supplementary Fig. 26). This robust stability was also confirmed by post-reaction characterization with no notable changes in structure or composition (Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28). To evaluate the appliable potential of heterogeneous catalytic pairs for the NORR, we synthesized other M1−Ti pairs (where M was Fe, Co or Ni). Compared with the Ti−Ti pairs, all these M1−Ti pairs exhibited improved NORR performance, among which the Cu1−Ti pairs achieved the best performance (Supplementary Fig. 29). These results confirmed that the effectiveness of the proposed dual-atom method for NO activation, with Cu1−Ti pairs achieving markedly improved performance. Specifically, the characteristic 3d orbitals of Cu could effectively weaken the N=O bond via a favorable bidirectional “donation–backdonation” mechanism.

Mechanism investigation

We used a selective poisoning approach to elucidate the distinct roles of the Cu and Ti sites in the NORR mechanism. Sulfide ions (S2−) have a strong and specific affinity for Cu, and thus were used to selectively block the Cu sites40. The specific experimental procedure involved immersing the electrode samples in a 10 mM Na2S solution for 20 min, after which the samples were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water to remove residual S2− before testing. After this treatment, the NORR performance of the Ti−Ti pairs remained largely unaffected, whereas the NH3 yield of the Cu1−Ti pairs was substantially decreased (Fig. 3a). These results confirmed that the Cu sites were indispensable for high catalytic activity. We also utilized thiocyanate ions (SCN−) to shelter both Cu and Ti sites because of their strong ability to coordinate both Cu and Ti39,41. After 10 mM KSCN was added to the electrolyte, the NH3 yield of both catalysts decreased significantly (Fig. 3a), which indicated that Ti sites also actively participated in the reaction. These findings collectively indicated that the improved NORR performance stemmed from the synergy of the Cu and Ti sites, rather than the sole action of Cu. Moreover, the NO temperature programmed desorption (NO-TPD) and in situ pyrrole infrared spectroscopy results suggested that Cu1–Ti pairs exhibited stronger NO adsorption than Ti–Ti pairs did (Supplementary Figs. 30−31). In situ Raman spectroscopy was then constructed to elucidate the surface response of the active sites toward NO (Supplementary Figs. 32−33). A new peak at 218 cm−1, corresponding to the A1g symmetry out-of-plane vibrations of Ti and N atoms42,43, was observed for both Ti–Ti and Cu1–Ti pairs, which confirmed the direct adsorption and coordination of N species on Ti sites. The Cu–O Raman signal was not clearly detected in the Cu1–Ti pairs, probably because of the low Cu loading and intrinsically weak scattering intensity of Cu–O species.

To better elucidate the NORR mechanism, we investigated the hydrogen source and the hydrogenation pathway. Considering that NH3 production involves multiple proton-coupled electron transfers, first, we assessed the water dissociation capability of the catalysts via kinetic isotope effect (KIE) measurements31,44. The low KIE values (<1.5) for both Cu1−Ti and Ti−Ti pairs (Supplementary Fig. 34) indicated efficient water dissociation, which confirmed that this step is not the potential-determining step (PDS). Second, we conducted in situ differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) to validate the NORR pathway over Ti−Ti and Cu1−Ti pairs. As shown in Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 35−36, gaseous species consistent with HNO/NOH (m/z = 31), NH2OH (m/z = 33), NH2 (m/z = 16), and NH3 (m/z = 17) were detected for both samples, whereas no signal for the atomic *N (m/z = 14) was observed, suggesting that Ti−Ti and Cu1−Ti pairs followed a similar NORR reaction pathway.

In situ electrochemical attenuated total reflection infrared spectroscopy (ATR-IRAS) was performed to identify the adsorbed intermediates and elucidate the reaction mechanism (Supplementary Fig. 37). Under applied potentials in a NO-saturated electrolyte, Ti−Ti pairs exhibited a weak adsorption peak at 1737 cm−1, assigned to the end-on adsorbed NO45,46. Moreover, the potential-dependent evolution of intermediates (Fig. 3c, d) revealed characteristic bands for *NHO (1540 and 1560 cm−1)2,45, *NH2OH (1181 cm−1)21,46–49, *NH2 (1507 cm−1)21,46,50, and NH3 (1457 cm−1)20,21,49. We proposed that NH3 formation on both Ti−Ti and Cu1−Ti pairs proceeds via the following pathway: NO → *NHO → *NHOH → *NH2OH → *NH2 → NH3.

Isotope labeling experiments using 15NO were conducted to further confirm the NO adsorption configurations and reaction intermediates. After 14NO was substituted with 15NO, the peak at 1737 cm−1 markedly redshifted to 1712 cm−1 (Fig. 3c), consistent with the theoretically predicted N−O stretching frequency at 1706 cm−1, which is based on the reduced mass in a simple harmonic oscillator model. Moreover, the *NHO peaks also exhibited two distinct isotopic redshifts of 16 cm−1 and 28 cm−1, whereas shifts for other N-containing intermediates were indistinct, owing to the small frequency ratio (ν15N/ν14N≈99.78%). Notably, Cu1−Ti pairs exhibited a side-on NO adsorption peak at 1577 cm−1 51, which redshifted to 1548 cm−1 under the feedstock of 15NO (Fig. 3d). The switching of NO adsorption from end-on to side-on configuration on Cu1−Ti pairs, in agreement with our theoretical predictions, provided a dual-electron transfer channel via the proposed Ti–N=O–Cu adsorption motif for more effective *NO activation (Supplementary Fig. S38). Collectively, the complementary results from in situ Raman and isotope-labeled ATR-IRAS provide direct and consistent data for the proposed side-on Ti–N=O–Cu adsorption geometry under NORR reaction conditions (Fig. 3e).

We subsequently performed theoretical calculations to elucidate the catalytic mechanism underlying the improved NORR activity. The activation of the NO molecule hinges on the electron population of its π* antibonding orbital. We reported that electron transfer between the catalyst and adsorbed NO (*NO) differed essentially across sites. On Cu1−Ti pairs, the substrate gave 0.63 |e| to *NO, whereas on Ti−Ti pairs, *NO donated 0.20 |e| to the substrate (Fig. 4a). This opposite direction of electron transfer on Cu1−Ti pairs and Ti−Ti pairs resulted in a stronger adsorption for *NO on Cu1−Ti pairs. In addition, the acceptance of electrons by the *NO molecule from the Cu1−Ti pairs resulted in further occupation of its π* antibonding orbital, which weakened the N=O bond and activated the *NO. In contrast, the transfer of electrons by the NO molecule to the Ti−Ti pairs resulted in the loss of electrons of its π* antibonding orbital, which strengthened the N=O bond.

Furthermore, the partial density of states (PDOS) analysis also indicated that *NO interacted with the unoccupied and occupied d orbitals of Ti−Ti pairs and Cu1−Ti pairs through distinct modes. With respect to the Ti−Ti pairs, only the occupied d-σ orbitals were observed (Fig. 4b), owing to the hybridization between the occupied Ti-d orbitals and *NO-σ orbitals under the Fermi level (EF). First, electrons from the occupied *NO-σ orbitals were first donated into the unoccupied Ti-3d orbitals. Second, *NO itself underwent an electronic rearrangement, where electrons from its π* orbital transferred to σ orbitals. Consequently, Ti accepted electrons from *NO, and the π* orbitals of *NO lost electrons. However, with respect to the Cu1−Ti pairs, in addition to occupied d-σ orbitals, partially occupied d-π* orbitals were formed because the π* orbitals of the *NO molecule interacted with the 3d orbitals of the Cu1−Ti pairs (Fig. 4b). On the one hand, the empty 3d orbitals could accept the electrons from the *NO-σ orbitals. On the other hand, the occupied orbitals are capable of back-donating electrons to the empty π* orbitals of *NO. Therefore, Cu1−Ti pairs and *NO interacted according to an electron “donation−backdonation” mechanism (Fig. 4c). The σ-donation dominated interaction on Ti−Ti pairs stabilized the N=O bond, whereas the π*-backdonation dominated interaction on Cu1−Ti pairs activated the *NO molecule. Projected crystal orbital Hamiltonian populations (pCOHP) analysis was used to elucidate *NO activation (Fig. 4d). The negatively integrated COHP (-ICOHP) value for the NO molecule under vacuum was 18.52, whereas the values were 21.93 and 16.00 after adsorption on the Ti−Ti pairs and Cu1−Ti pairs, respectively, which clearly indicated that the *NO molecule was activated on the Cu1−Ti pairs.

After the *NO activation on Cu1−Ti pairs was well understood, the activity of NORR to NH3 was further compared on Ti−Ti pairs and Cu1−Ti pairs on the basis of the above experiment mechanism (Supplementary Figs. 39−40 and Table 3). On the Ti−Ti pairs, the *NO hydrogenated to *NH2 with a total downhill Gibbs free energy change of −1.38 eV. With respect to the Cu1−Ti pairs, the total downhill free energy change for the above steps was −2.82 eV. Therefore, *NO hydrogenation to *NH2 was more thermodynamically favorable for Cu1−Ti pairs. Notably, the PDS shifted between the two catalysts: on Ti−Ti pairs, the PDS was the initial hydrogenation of *NO to *NHO with a barrier of 0.66 eV, whereas on Cu1−Ti pairs, the PDS shifted to the final hydrogenation of NH2 to NH3 with a lower barrier of 0.53 eV. This shift in the PDS, which highlighted the higher NH3 yield on Cu1−Ti pairs, originated from the improved activation of the polar NO molecule. The transition from a σ-donation-dominated interaction on Ti−Ti pairs to a π*-backdonation-dominated interaction on Cu1−Ti pairs caused a significant decrease in the free energy of the *NO to *NHO process from 0.66 eV to −0.69 eV, thereby altering the overall reaction landscape.

Discussion

In summary, we developed an electrocatalytic strategy on the basis of Cu1−Ti dual-atom pairs for highly efficient conversion of polar NO to NH3. By leveraging the N-philicity of Ti and the O-philicity of Cu, the Cu1−Ti pairs achieved a side-on Ti−N=O−Cu adsorption configuration, which synchronously activated both ends of the NO molecule. The Cu1−Ti pairs reached a FENH3 of 93.7% and an NH3 yield rate of 189.9 μmol h−1 cm−2 at –0.6 VRHE, significantly outperforming homonuclear Ti−Ti pairs (63.5% and 58.5 μmol h−1 cm−2, respectively). The experimental characterization and theoretical simulations collectively confirmed that the Cu1−Ti pairs facilitated the side-on adsorption of NO via optimal structural and electronic matching. Unlike the single d-σ coupling interaction between the Ti−Ti pairs and NO, the Cu1−Ti pairs incorporated additional d-π* coupling to increase charge transfer from the Cu1−Ti pairs to NO. This process resulted in the formation of a “donation−backdonation” mechanism that not only strengthened the activation of the N=O bond but also created favorable conditions for the subsequent hydrogenation process. Our work emphasized the profound effect of atomic-level design in terms of creating asymmetric active sites that are capable of efficiently activating and converting polar molecules.

Methods

Chemicals and materials

The commercial Ti felt was purchased from Qinghe Jianxuan Metal product Co., Ltd., China. Copper chloride (CuCl2·2H2O, AR, 98%) and potassium sulfate (K2SO4, AR, ≥ 99%) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Deuteroxide (D2O, 99%), oxalic acid (H2C2O4, AR, 99.8%), sodium potassium tartrate (NaKC4H4O6, AR, 99%), sodium sulfide (Na2S, AR, 99%), potassium thiocyanate (KSCN, AR, ≥98.5%) para-(dimethylamino) benzaldehyde (C9H11NO, 99%) and Nessler’s reagent were obtained from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36.0–38.0%), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, AR, ≥96.0%), acetone (CH3COCH3, AR, ≥ 99.5%), hydrazine hydrate (N2H4·H2O, 85%) and ethanol (EtOH, AR, ≥ 99.5%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Beijing Co., Ltd. (China). Nitric oxide (NO) and argon (Ar) were obtained from Shanghai Haoqi Co., Ltd. Ultrapure water was used in all experiments. All chemicals were used without further purification.

Characterizations

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on a Shimadzu XRD-6100 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) and a scan rate of ~10° min–1 (2θ). The Raman spectrum was recorded by a Renishaw inVia Qontor Confocal Raman Spectrometer with a laser of 532 nm. Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC HAADF-STEM) images were collected by a JEOL JEM-ARM200F TEM/STEM working at 200 kV. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) elemental mapping images were observed on a Titan G2 60-300 electron microscope equipped with an EDX spectrometer. The operation voltage was set at 300 kV. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was carried out on a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha XPS spectrometer equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα source (hν = 1486.6 eV). The binding energy calibration was standardized to the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. The load of Cu in Cu1-Ti pairs was analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) on a Thermo Scientific iCAP Q ICP-MS. The H2 was analyzed using an online GC (Agilent 7890B) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). The XAS data, which consisted of XANES and EXAFS, of the samples was gathered at the center of the Singapore Synchrotron Light Source (SSLS). The acquired EXAFS data were processed according to standard procedures using the ATHENA module of the IFEFFIT software package. In order to obtain quantitative structural parameters around the central atom, least squares curve parameter fitting was performed using the ARTEMIS module of the IFEFFIT software package.

Synthesis of Ti−Ti pairs and Cu1−Ti pairs

The commercial Ti felt was cut into rectangles with an area of 2 × 2 cm and then sonicated in acetone and water. Subsequently, the Ti felts were treated with 5 wt% oxalic acid solution at 80 °C for 30 min, and the treated Ti felts were rinsed repeatedly with deionized water and dried in an oven at 80 °C for 4 h for subsequent use. The pre-treated Ti felts were further heat-treated at 300 °C for 180 min at an elevated temperature rate of 10 °C/min to obtain Ti−Ti pairs. The Cu precursor solution was prepared by mixing 134.1 mg CuCl2·2H2O with 50 mL of 0.01 M HCl solution. Each mL of Cu precursor solution contained about 1 mg Cu. 1 mL of Cu precursor solution was dropped by drop-drying several times onto the pre-treated Ti felt, and the solvent was removed under infrared light irradiation. Then, the electrode was heated up to 300 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under an air atmosphere and heat-treated at 300 °C for 180 min to obtain Cu1−Ti pairs.

Electrochemical measurement

All the electrochemical tests were performed in a conventional three-electrode system on an electrochemical station (CHI 760E, Shanghai Chenhua, China) in an H-type electrolytic cell separated by a Nafion 117 membrane (3.0 × 3.0 cm2) with a thickness of 183 μm. The Nafion membrane was first soaked in deionized water for 30 min, followed by heating in 5 wt% H2O2 at 80 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the membrane was treated in 5 wt% H2SO4 under identical temperature conditions for another 1 h. Finally, it was rinsed by soaking in deionized water for 30 min. A saturated Hg/Hg2Cl2 electrode and a Pt foil (1.0 × 2.0 cm) were used as the reference electrode and counter electrode. Calibration of the Hg/Hg2Cl2 electrode was performed by measuring the RHE potential under an H2 atmosphere using a Pt wire as the working electrode. The calibration involved a slow cyclic voltammetry (CV) scan at 1 mV s−1 near the open-circuit potential to ensure quasi-equilibrium conditions. The as-synthesized catalyst (with a dimension of 2.0 × 2.0 cm) was directly employed as the working electrode. The electrolyte was freshly prepared by dissolving K2SO4 in deionized water and diluted to 1 L to obtain 0.25 M solution with a volumetric flask, which was sealed and stored at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) before use. Both the anodic and cathodic chambers of the air-tight H-type cell were injected with 40 mL of 0.25 M K2SO4 electrolyte. Prior to electrochemical testing, the electrolyte was purged for 30 min with high-purity argon gas (99.999%) at a flow rate of 100 sccm to remove air. Subsequently, a NO/Ar mixture (20 vol% NO) was introduced at a flow rate of 50 sccm for 30 min to saturate the electrolyte, with this flow rate maintained throughout the electrochemical testing process. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves were obtained at a scan rate of 50 mV s−1 over the potential range from −0.40 to −1.60 V. Potentiostatic tests were conducted for 0.5 h within the potential range of 0 V to −1.2 V at a stirring rate of 750 rpm. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves for determining the electrochemical double-layer capacitance (Cdl) were measured at different scan rates of 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mV s−1. The plot of current density versus scan rate at a fixed potential exhibits a linear relationship, with the slope representing Cdl. All potentials mentioned in this paper were calibrated to a reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) using Eq. 1: ERHE = EHg/Hg2Cl2+0.2415+0.0591 × pH. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were carried out in the frequency range from 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz. The charge transfer resistance (Rct) obtained by fitting the EIS data.

Quantification of NH3 using Nessler’s reagent

The determination of NH3 concentration employs Nessler’s reagent as the color developer. First, a specified volume of electrolyte is withdrawn from the electrolytic cell and diluted to 5 mL to achieve the detection range. Next, 0.1 mL of potassium sodium tartrate solution (c = 500 g/L) is added and thoroughly mixed. Then, 0.1 mL of Nessler’s reagent is added to the solution. After allowing the sample to stand for 20 min, the absorbance is recorded at a wavelength of 420 nm. A concentration-absorbance calibration curve is established using a series of standard ammonium chloride solutions, with ammonium chloride crystals dried for 2 h at 110 °C beforehand.

Quantification of NH3 using ion chromatography (IC)

In this study, NH3 concentration was also detected and quantified using an ion chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Aquion FRIC) equipped with a conductivity detector. A specific volume of the sample was diluted to a detectable range. The mobile phase contained a diluted methanesulfonic acid solution (0.2% w/v), with a constant flow rate maintained at 1.0 mL/min. A series of standard solutions was analyzed using the same procedure to obtain peak area versus ion concentration curves.

Quantification of NH2OH according to Frear’s method

The concentration of NH2OH was determined according to Frear’s method. The standard solution was prepared by diluting the mother liquor (0.5 mM NH2OH·HCl aqueous solution containing 0.25 M K2SO4). First, 1 mL of standard solution was placed in a clean centrifuge tube. Then, 1 mL of 0.1 M PBS solution, 1 mL deionized water, and 0.2 mL of 12 wt% trifluoroacetic acid were added in order, followed by 1 mL of 10 g L−1 8-quinolinol ethanol solution. Finally, 1 mL of 1 M Na2CO3 solution was added. The vials were then placed in a 60 °C oven for 15 min and cooled for another 10 min. The absorption spectrum was recorded on an UV–vis spectrophotometer in the wavelength range 600–800 nm. The solution without NH2OH·HCl was used for background correction. The absorbances were recorded at a wavelength of 705 nm and plotted against the hydroxylamine concentrations. The test of the target sample follows the same process as the standard solution.

Quantification of N2H4 according to Watt–Chrisp method

Ten milliliters of HCL, 2 g of para-(dimethylamino) benzaldehyde, and 100 mL of C2H5OH were dissolved and used as a sensitive chromogenic reagent. Then, 2 mL of solution product was collected from the cathode compartment and uniform mixed with 5 mL of the chromogenic reagent at room temperature. After standing for 20 min in the dark for at room temperature, the light absorbance of the resulting solution was detected by UV-Vis spectrophotometer at the wavelength of 457 nm.

In situ Raman spectroscopy measurements

A confocal Raman microscope (Renishaw, InVia), coupled with a CHI760E electrochemical workstation, was employed for in situ electrochemical Raman spectroscopy with a 532 nm laser as the excitation source. The apparatus was constructed from polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), featuring a quartz window positioned between the sample and the objective lens. The working electrode was immersed in the electrolyte solution through the apparatus wall, with its plane oriented perpendicular to the laser beam. A platinum sheet electrode and an Ag/AgCl electrode served as the counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively.

In situ ATR-FTIR spectroscopy measurements

Electrochemical in situ attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectra were recorded on a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A custom-designed three-electrode electrochemical cell was employed, and in situ ATR-FTIR measurements were performed using a single hemicylindrical silicon crystal. Prior to experiments, a thin gold (Au) film was chemically deposited onto the smooth surface of the silicon crystal. Ti−Ti pairs or Cu1−Ti pairs were carefully exfoliated from the electrode and mixed with Nafion solution to form a uniform slurry. Fifty microliters of the sample slurry were uniformly dispensed onto the gold film surface, serving as the working electrode. Simultaneously, a saturated calomel electrode and a platinum wire served as the reference electrode and counter electrode, respectively. LSV testing was conducted at a scan rate of 2 mV s–1 over a potential range of 0 to –0.6 VRHE.

Online DEMS measurements

A 0.25 M K2SO4 electrolyte is continuously injected into a custom electrochemical cell via a peristaltic pump. Ti−Ti pairs or Cu1−Ti pairs, platinum wire, and Ag/AgCl electrodes serve as the working electrode, counter electrode, and reference electrode, respectively. A porous polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane (pore size: 0.22 μm; thickness: 100–150 μm) was employed. The electrolyte is first purged with high-purity argon gas (99.999%) for 30 min to remove air. Subsequently, 20% NO was introduced into the electrolyte until saturation was reached, and the concentration was maintained constant during DEMS measurements. Constant potential testing was conducted at –0.6 VRHE. After the electrochemical test concluded, the mass spectrometry signal recovered to baseline levels. The next cycle was then initiated under identical test conditions. Measurements concluded after completing five cycles.

NO-TPD measurements

Scrape powder from the electrode surface, weigh 100 mg of sample into a reaction tube, and dry-precondition by programmed heating from room temperature to 200 °C at 10 °C/min. Purge with He gas flow (50 mL/min) for 1 h, cool to 50 °C, then introduce NO mixed gas for 1 h until saturated. Switch to He gas flow (50 mL/min) for 1 h to remove weakly physically adsorbed NO from the surface. Finally, desorb under He atmosphere at a heating rate of 10 °C/min to 600 °C, detecting the eluted gas with a TCD.

Computational method

Spin–polarized DFT calculations were performed by the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)52,53. The exchange-correlation interaction was described by the PBE functional54. The Kohn–Sham equations were solved in a plane wave basis set with a kinetic energy cutoff of 400 eV. The effect of van der Waals interaction was described using the dispersion–corrected DFT–D3 functional55. The model of Ti–Ti pairs was established by 24 Ti atoms and 54 O atoms in a periodic boundary condition, while the Cu1–Ti pairs were simulated by a Cu atom, 23 Ti atoms and 42 O atoms. A vacuum space along the z direction was set to 15 Å to prevent the interaction between the repeating slabs. A k–point mesh of (3 × 3 × 1) was used to sample the slab Brillouin zone. Structure relaxation was performed until the residual forces were less than 0.02 eV/Å. To correct the strong electron–correlation properties of transition metal oxides, DFT + U calculations11 were performed with Ueff Values of 3.5 and 7.0 eV for Ti–3d and Cu–3d, based on the literature38,56,57. The crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) was analyzed by the LOBSTER program58,59.
