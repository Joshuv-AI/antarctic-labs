A complexity phase transition at the EPR Hamiltonian
Kunal Marwaha, James Sud

Abstract
We study the computational complexity of 2-local Hamiltonian problems generated by a positive-weight symmetric interaction term, encompassing many canonical problems in statistical mechanics and optimization. We show these problems belong to one of three complexity phases: 𝖰𝖬𝖠 {\mathsf{QMA}} -complete, 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 {\mathsf{StoqMA}} -complete, and reducible to a new problem we call EPR* . The phases are physically interpretable, corresponding to the energy level ordering of the local term. The EPR* problem is a simple generalization of the EPR problem of [ Kin23 ] . Inspired by empirically efficient algorithms for EPR , we conjecture that EPR* is in 𝖡𝖯𝖯 {\mathsf{BPP}} . If true, this would complete the complexity classification of these problems, and imply EPR* is the transition point between easy and hard local Hamiltonians. Our proofs rely on perturbative gadgets. One simple gadget, when recursed, induces a renormalization-group–like flow on the space of local interaction terms. This gives the correct complexity picture, but does not run in polynomial-time. To overcome this, we design a gadget based on a large spin chain, which we analyze via the Jordan-Wigner transformation.

Introduction

We study the computational complexity of the local Hamiltonian problem, which asks us to estimate the ground state energy of a physical system. While this problem is
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete in general
[
KSV02
]
, it becomes efficiently solvable for certain restricted families, for example via Markov chain Monte Carlo methods or the quantum adiabatic algorithm. This raises a natural question:

Which local Hamiltonians admit efficient algorithms for ground state energy estimation?

Identifying tractable families, however, does not by itself explain
why
certain Hamiltonians are easy or hard. Ideally, we would like go further, by characterizing complexity directly from simple structural features:

What physical properties of a local Hamiltonian problem determine its complexity?

We approach these questions through the lens of computational
phase transitions
. For a family of problems controlled by a real parameter
\theta
, we say that a phase transition occurs if there is a threshold
t
where the complexity of the problem
P(\theta)
abruptly changes at
\theta=t
. Commonly, the problems in
\{P(\theta)\ |\ \theta<t\}
all have the same complexity, which is different from the problems in
\{P(\theta)\ |\ \theta>t\}
. We may think of these two subsets as
phases
, just as a physical system can exhibit different phases of matter.

We show how to answer these questions for Hamiltonians generated by a symmetric
2
-local interaction term with positive interaction strength.
This family was first introduced by Piddock and Montanaro
[
PM15
]
, and covers a range of important problems in statistical physics and computer science.
We show a trichotomy theorem, e.g. the existence of three computational phases, with phase transitions driven by energy level crossings in the local term.
The complexity of each phase is encoded in a simple physical property of the local term’s energy level diagram (see
Figs.

1
and
2
).

To build intuition, we first restrict to a toy model
in which each local term is a weighted sum of two projectors.
In this toy model, one phase transition occurs at the EPR problem of
[
Kin23
]
. Prior work suggests that this problem may lie in
{\mathsf{BPP}}

[
RT25
]
, which would place it at the boundary between easy and hard instances. In the general family of local Hamiltonian problems, the analogous phase transition corresponds to an augmented EPR problem, which we call the
EPR*
problem. Showing
EPR*
is in
{\mathsf{BPP}}
would thus complete the complexity classification of this family.

Results

Theorem 1

Assuming

Assuming

The closer the singlet is to the ground state of the local term

This picture might seem to be a artifact of the definition of

1.1.2
Symmetric 2-local interactions

The larger family of Hamiltonians we consider was first introduced in
[
PM15
]
. We begin by borrowing some of their notation:

Definition 1
.

Given a set of
k
-local Hamiltonian terms
\mathcal{S}
, the
\mathcal{S}^{+}\!
-Hamiltonian
problem denotes the restriction of the
Local Hamiltonian
problem to Hamiltonians of the form
H=\sum_{\ell}w_{\ell}H_{\ell}
, where each
H_{\ell}\in\mathcal{S}
and
0<w_{\ell}\leq{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
, where
n
is the number of qubits. We denote the corresponding Hamiltonians as
\mathcal{S}^{+}\!
-Hamiltonians.

Our work considers singleton sets
S=\left\{K\right\}
where the interaction term
K
is
2
-local and symmetric under the interchange of its qubits (i.e.
K_{ij}=K_{ji}
). These restrictions are physically motivated, containing fundamental problems in both optimization and statistical mechanics:

•

k=2
is the smallest non-trivial value for the
k
-
Local Hamiltonian
problem. Indeed, the general
2
-
Local Hamiltonian
problem is
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete
[
KKR05
]
.

•

A single, symmetric term
K
governs many canonical models in statistical mechanics, such as the Ising, Heisenberg, and XXZ models. Furthermore, antisymmetric terms
K
are already shown to yield
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete problems in
[
PM15
]
.

•

Positive weights allows us to distinguish the complexity of ferromagnetic (i.e.
MinCut
) and antiferromagnetic (i.e.
MaxCut
) interactions.

Building on
[
CM16
]
, we observe that any symmetric
2
-local term

can without loss of generality be written in the form

\displaystyle K=\alpha\ket{\psi^{+}}\bra{\psi^{+}}+\beta\ket{\phi^{+}}\bra{\phi^{+}}+\gamma\ket{\phi^{-}}\bra{\phi^{-}},\qquad\alpha\geq\beta\geq\gamma.

(1)

This form makes it clear that our family of local Hamiltonians is parameterized by the real numbers

, which correspond to the energy of the three triplet states in

. The energy of the singlet is fixed to zero. In this convention,

]

(
[
PM15

)
.

-Hamiltonian

1.

(a)

-complete.

(b)

Otherwise it is in
{\mathsf{StoqMA}}
.

2.

If
\alpha>\beta>0
and
\gamma=0
, then it is
{\mathsf{StoqMA}}
-complete.

We call this a partial result because problems in case

.
We first extend

Theorem 3
.

The
\{K\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian

We complement this hardness result with an “easiness” result. Consider the local term

\displaystyle K_{\text{EPR}}^{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}(b+1)\ket{\psi^{+}}\!\bra{\psi^{+}}+(b-1)\ket{\phi^{-}}\!\bra{\phi^{-}},\qquad-1\leq b\leq 1.

We refer to the

In our larger family, we prove that all symmetric

.

The
\{K\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problem, where

is defined in

Our intuition for the toy model transfers to this more general family of local Hamiltonians.
Again, the complexity of the

The closer the singlet is to the ground state of

We visualize this in

When the singlet is the unique ground state, the problem is

1.2
Techniques

We prove our results via reductions between different
\mathcal{S}^{+}\!
-Hamiltonian
problems. These reductions are enabled by
perturbative gadgets
, which use local terms
\mathcal{S}
to simulate new
effective terms

\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
.

Gadgets.

We use two standard kinds of perturbative gadgets
[
KKR05
,
BH14
,
PM15
,
CM16
]
. We refer to the first kind as
vertex-replacing
gadgets and briefly describe how they work. Suppose we start with an instance of the
\{K\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problem acting on a graph
G
. We first select a small
gadget graph

\widetilde{G}=([\widetilde{n}],\widetilde{E},\{\widetilde{w}_{ij}\})
, and replace each vertex of
G
with a copy of
\widetilde{G}
. We then apply the local interaction
K
to the edges of each copy of
\widetilde{G}
, weighted by a large factor. We choose
\widetilde{G}
so that the resulting Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}=\sum_{(i,j)\in\widetilde{E}}\widetilde{w}_{ij}K_{ij}

satisfies two key properties:

1.

\widetilde{H}
has exactly two degenerate ground states.

2.

There is a spectral gap separating these ground states from the excited states.

These properties let us define a
logical
qubit out of the
physical
qubits in
\widetilde{G}
. The two ground states, denoted
\ket{0^{(L)}}
and
\ket{1^{(L)}}
, are isolated from the rest of the spectrum by the gap. The large weights ensure that all other states have much higher energy, so we can safely treat
\ket{0^{(L)}}
and
\ket{1^{(L)}}
as a two-level system.

Figure 3
:
P_{3}
: A simple vertex-replacing perturbative gadget. Bold lines denote strong interactions, and thin lines denote weak interactions. Each chain of three red qubits defines a single logical blue qubit. The logical qubits carry a new local interaction term, but the effective interaction graph is unchanged.

Once we have logical qubits, we can make them interact. Since we only have access to the original interaction
K
, we connect the physical qubits of different logical qubits. As an example, we let the
P_{3}

gadget
denote the vertex-replacing gadget where each logical qubit is given by a chain of three physical qubits. In
Fig.

3
, we connect the last qubit of neighboring chains with the local interaction
K
. For any such connection, we use first-order perturbation theory to compute the
effective interaction

K^{\prime}
produced by this physical interaction
K
, projected into the ground space of the two logical qubits.

Our other kind of gadget is an
edge-replacing gadget
. This gadget instead replaces each edge of the original graph with a heavily weighted interaction term on a new pair of ancilla qubits. We then use perturbation theory to analyze the effective interaction between physical qubits in the ground space of the ancillae.

Both gadgets create an effective Hamiltonian
H_{\text{target}}
described by the interaction
K^{\prime}
on the edges of
G
. This is done by projecting a physical Hamiltonian
H_{\text{sim}}
described by the interaction
K
on a graph larger than
G
, into some logical ground space. We then say that
H_{\text{sim}}

simulates

H_{\text{target}}
. If this works for any
G
, we say the interaction
K

simulates
the interaction
K^{\prime}
. Our gadgets introduce at most a polynomial increase in the number of local terms, so they constitute a polynomial-time many-one reduction from the
\{K^{\prime}\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problem to the
\{K\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problem.

Flows.

We prove
Theorem

3
by repeatedly applying our gadgets. First,
[

,

]
show that our symmetric

-local terms

can be written without loss of generality in the

Polynomial gaps.

To overcome this issue, we replace the recursive applications of the
P_{3}
gadget with a single application of a gadget whose size grows with
n
. For example, consider the line defined by
a>1
,
b=0
. This corresponds to the antiferromagnetic XY model in statistical mechanics. From
Fig.

4(a)
, we see that this model flows out to

along the
b=0
line. We replace the
P_{3}
gadget with a long spin chain of length
L=\Omega\left(\log{n}\right)
. Then, we are able to flow all the way to the local term
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)XX-ZZ
in a single step. This already simulates
+XX
(
MaxCut
) to inverse polynomial error. We show that it can also simulate the
{\mathsf{StoqMA}}
-complete transverse-field Ising model (TIM). The key challenge is analyzing the length
\Omega\left(\log{n}\right)
XY spin chain. For this we must diagonalize the system using the Bogoliubov transformation
[
Bog58
]
and a mapping to a free-fermion system.

We believe this technique works for the rest of the
{\mathsf{StoqMA}}
-region. This would require analyzing long spin chains for the fully anisotropic XYZ model. Under
periodic

[
Bet31
]
and a reduction to the classical
8
-vertex model. However, a rigorous solution under open boundary conditions is not known to us.
Instead, we complete the proof with a series of gadgets, showing that any point in this
{\mathsf{StoqMA}}
-complete region simulates either the antiferromagnetic XY model above, or the antiferromagnetic XXZ model.
It remains to show this latter model is
{\mathsf{StoqMA}}
-complete.

Like the XYZ model, we do not know of a rigorous solution for the XXZ spin chain under open boundary conditions. We instead analyze a gadget based on a complete bipartite graph
K_{L,L-1}
with
L=\Theta(\log{n})
. The symmetry of the complete bipartite graph greatly simplifies the analysis. We show a single application of this gadget simulates the local term
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)XX-YY-ZZ
, which again we show can simulate the TIM. This XXZ case also corresponds to the toy model Hamiltonians considered in
1
.

Finally, for our easiness results in
Theorem

4
, consider the the green square region of

defined by
-1<a,b<1
. We would like to show that any point in this square can be simulated by an edge of the square, corresponding to

. We can see from
Fig.

that the edges do not flow into the square, so we cannot use the simple

gadget to prove our result. Thus, we handle this region using an edge-replacing second-order perturbative gadget.

1.3
Related work

Complexity classifications and phase transitions.

Our work continues a line of complexity classifications for local optimization problems based on structural properties of the allowed clauses (or Hamiltonian terms). In classical complexity theory,
Schaefer’s dichotomy theorem

[
Sch78
]
, showed that every Boolean constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) defined by a fixed set of relations is either solvable in polynomial time, or
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete. That is, the computational complexity (a global property) can be determined simply from local properties of the allowed relations.

The
Local Hamiltonian
problem can be interpreted as a quantum generalization of CSPs. A complete classification for the
Local Hamiltonian
problem with
arbitrarily
weighted
2
-local terms was completed by
[
CM16
,
BH14
]
. Depending on the set of local terms
S
, the problem is either
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete,
{\mathsf{StoqMA}}
-complete,
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete, or in
{\mathsf{P}}
.
However, the use of negative weights makes it impossible to distinguish between the complexity of ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic interactions. For example, negative weights allow us to encode
MaxCut
problems using only the local terms from
MinCut
.

The complexity classes in these classifications are often separated by computational phase transitions.
Perhaps the simplest example is the
Local Hamiltonian
problem on the Ising model, given by Hamiltonians
H=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}aZ_{i}Z_{j}
for some real parameter
a
. When
a<0
, the problem is ferromagnetic and trivial, with ground state
\ket{0}^{n}
. When
a>0
, the problem is antiferromagnetic and
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete (equivalent to
MaxCut
). Thus,
a=0
marks a transition between
{\mathsf{P}}
and
{\mathsf{NP}}
. A canonical phase transition from classical optimization is random
3
-
SAT
, where instances are typically satisfiable up to a critical clause density and unsatisfiable above that density (e.g.
[
CA96
,
MPZ02
]
). In quantum settings, computational phase transitions occur in the sample complexity of bosonic systems
[
Des+18
]
, the hardness of simulating certain families of graph states
[
Gho+23
]
, and threshold theorems for error correction
[
AB99
]
.

Stoquasticity and the EPR problem.

Stoquastic (also known as sign-problem free) Hamiltonians admit a basis in which all off-diagonal elements are real and non-positive. This allows for Quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) simulation methods based on stochastic sampling of Gibbs or path-integral distributions. The efficiency of these methods depends on the mixing time of the underlying Markov chains. Rapid mixing has been proven in some cases, such as the ferromagnetic transverse-field Ising model
[
JS93
]
and the ferromagnetic XY model
[
BG17
,
RT25
]
. In many other cases, however, efficiency is supported mainly by numerical evidence.

It is possible that stoquasticity fundamentally simplifies ground state energy estimation.
It is known that
{\mathsf{MA}}\subseteq{\mathsf{StoqMA}}\subseteq{\mathsf{AM}}

[
BBT06
]
, and under a plausible conjecture
{\mathsf{MA}}={\mathsf{StoqMA}}

[
AGL25
]
. By contrast, it is unlikely that
{\mathsf{QMA}}\subseteq{\mathsf{AM}}
; in fact, there is oracular evidence that
{\mathsf{BQP}}\nsubseteq{\mathsf{PH}}

[
RT22
]
.
Regardless, quantum adiabatic computation seems to be more powerful than classical methods in finding ground states of stoquastic Hamiltonians (e.g.
[
Has21
,
GV20
,
HBS26
]
).

The
EPR
(and
EPR*
) problems are stoquastic. QMC methods such as the operator-loop update of
[
TSC24
,
RT25
]
are leading candidates for solving the
EPR*
problem in polynomial time. An alternative approach for
EPR
uses the quantum adiabatic algorithm
[
Far+00
]
, analyzed via Lee-Yang theory. Recent work
[
Won+26
]
relates zero-freeness of associated partition functions to efficient adiabatic algorithms for problems similar to
EPR
, suggesting a path towards showing the
EPR
problem is in
{\mathsf{BQP}}
.

The quantum Heisenberg model and Quantum MaxCut.

A key component in our proofs is a perturbative gadget built from a one-dimensional chain of qubits. To analyze this spin chain, we borrow from a long line of research in statistical physics, where our systems are studied under the name
quantum Heisenberg model
. A central goal in this area is to identify integrable models, i.e., those whose eigenspectra admit efficient descriptions. A seminal example is the solution of the XY spin chain by
[
LSM61
]
, which uses a Bogoliubov transformation to map the system to free fermions; see the expositions of
[
Met05
,
Sto14
]
. Building on the Bethe ansatz
[
Bet31
]
,
[
Bax71
,
Bax85
]
solved the XXZ and XYZ spin chains using a connection to the classical six- and eight-vertex models. See
[
Sla20
]
for a modern treatment of this technique.

There has been significant progress on
approximation algorithms
for the antiferromagnetic quantum Heisenberg XXX model, also known as
Quantum MaxCut
. Here, the aim is to determine the ground state energy to some constant factor. This work was initiated by
[
GP19
]
and later improved by
[
PT21
,
PT22
,
Lee22
,
Kin23
,
LP24
,
Jor+24
,
MSS24
,
KKZ24
,
GSS25
,
APS25
,
Apt+25a
]
. The current state-of-the-art approximation ratio is
>0.611

[
Apt+25a
]
; see
[
SM25
]
for a summary of techniques and updated results. It was recently proven by Piddock
[
Pid25
]
that

Quantum MaxCut

is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard to approximate to some constant factor (see also
[
Hwa+22
]
).

2.1

Our results rely on reductions between

-Hamiltonian

Definition 2

•

The image of

•

\|H_{\text{target}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}H_{\text{sim}}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\|\leqslant\epsilon

•

\|\mathcal{E}-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\,\|\leqslant\eta

See
[

-Hamiltonian

-Hamiltonian

-Hamiltonian, or that the local terms

Lemma 1

(Bravyi and Hastings

]
)

Suppose

simulates

(\eta_{1},\epsilon_{1})

with error

\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\leqslant\|H\|

Our proof of

combines a constant number of simulation steps in series.

2.2
Perturbative gadgets

We simulate different
\mathcal{S}^{+}\!
-Hamiltonian
problems using perturbative gadgets. For a more thorough introduction, see
[

]
. These gadgets build a

Denote

and
P_{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}I-P_{-}
. Then for any operator

we use the shorthand notation

\displaystyle O_{--}=P_{-}OP_{-},\quad O_{-+}=P_{-}OP_{+},\quad O_{+-}=P_{+}OP_{-},\quad O_{++}=P_{+}OP_{+}.

We write
\overline{H}_{\text{target}}:=\mathcal{E}H_{\text{target}}\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}
,
for the encoding of

into the (bigger) simulator space.
Then we can borrow the following lemmas:

Lemma 2

(First Order,
[
BH14
, Lemma 4]
)
.

Suppose one can choose
H_{0},V
such that
H_{0}
has ground state energy
0
and all nonzero eigenvalues greater than or equal to
1
, and

\|\overline{H}_{\text{target}}-V_{--}\|\leqslant\varepsilon/2.

Suppose
\|V\|\leqslant\Lambda
. Then
H_{\text{sim}}=\Delta H_{0}+V
simulates

H_{\text{target}}
with error
(\eta,\epsilon)
, provided that
\Delta\geqslant\Omega(\epsilon^{-1}\Lambda^{2}+\eta^{-1}\Lambda).

Lemma 3

(Second Order,
[
BH14
, Lemma 5]
)
.

Suppose one can choose
H_{0},V_{\text{main}},V_{\text{extra}}
such that
H_{0}
has ground state energy
0
and all nonzero eigenvalues greater than or equal to 1,
(V_{\text{extra}})_{+-}=(V_{\text{extra}})_{-+}=0
,
(V_{\text{main}})_{--}=0
, and

\|\overline{H}_{\text{target}}-(V_{\text{extra}})_{--}+(V_{\text{main}})_{-+}H_{0}^{-1}(V_{\text{main}})_{+-}\|\leqslant\varepsilon/2.

Suppose
\|V_{\text{main}}\|,\|V_{\text{extra}}\|\leqslant\Lambda
. Then
H_{\text{sim}}=\Delta H_{0}+\Delta^{1/2}V_{\text{main}}+V_{\text{extra}}
simulates

H_{\text{target}}
with error
(\eta,\epsilon)
, provided that
\Delta\geqslant\Omega(\epsilon^{-2}\Lambda^{6}+\eta^{-2}\Lambda^{2}).

Lemmas

2
and

give us a recipe to simulate new Hamiltonians:
we must first design a heavily-weighted operator
H_{0}
, and perturbative operators

(for first order) or
(V_{\text{main}},V_{\text{extra}})
(for second order). The simulated Hamiltonian is determined by by effect of the perturbative operators in the ground space of

In this work we choose

,

,

to have at most
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)

can be scaled and shifted by identity to meet the requirements of

2
and
3

We always take

to ensure that ground state energies can be estimated to inverse polynomial precision, consistent with the promise gap of the
Local Hamiltonian
problem. This implies
\Delta=\mathcal{O}\left({\mathsf{poly}}(n)\right)
. By

, we can combine a constant number of simulation steps in series while keeping the norm of each Hamiltonian at most

.

2.3
Normal form of local terms

The complexity of the
\{K\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problem is invariant under rescaling by a polynomial factor and under shifting by a polynomial multiple of the identity. Since
K
does not depend on
n
, we can, without loss of generality, shift

so that it is traceless. It is shown in
[
CM16
, Lemma 9]
that any traceless, symmetric, strictly
2
-local term

can be written in the form
aXX+bYY+cZZ
.

Moreover,
[
CM16
, Lemma 8]
shows that conjugating a

-Hamiltonian by a single-qubit unitary applied transversely, i.e.,

, permutes the coefficients of
XX
,
YY
, and
ZZ
. Since this transformation preserves the spectrum of

, it does not affect the complexity of the problem. Thus, we may assume without loss of generality that
a\geq b\geq c
.

A simple linear transformation (see
Section

A.1
) relates the Pauli form of

to the Bell form
\alpha\ket{\psi^{+}}\bra{\psi^{+}}+\beta\ket{\phi^{+}}\bra{\phi^{+}}+\gamma\ket{\phi^{-}}\bra{\phi^{-}}

given in

1
. This mapping from

to

is a bijection. Consequently, permutations of Paulis correspond to permutations of triplet states, so we may assume without loss of generality that
\alpha\geq\beta\geq\gamma

\displaystyle p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3}=1,

Discussion

Our work identifies the
EPR*
problem as a computational phase transition, marking the hardest
\{K\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problem not known to be at least
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard by
Theorems

2
,
3
and
5
. Establishing
2
(that
EPR*
lies in
{\mathsf{BPP}}
) would complete the classification of the
\{K\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problem initiated in
[
PM15
]
.
It would also imply that
EPR
is in
{\mathsf{BPP}}
; so far, we only know a 0.8395-approximation algorithm for this problem
[
Apt+25
]
(see also
[
SM25
]
for a reference for state-of-the-art algorithms).

Extensions

Our techniques suggest extensions to broader classes of
\mathcal{S}^{+}\!
-Hamiltonian
problems.

Transverse fields.

Adding single-qubit transverse-field terms can change the complexity of a Hamiltonian; for example, the
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete antiferromagnetic Ising model turns into a
{\mathsf{StoqMA}}
-complete problem. In other settings, however, transverse fields do not affect complexity (see, e.g.,
[
CM16
,
TSC24
]
). An interesting open question is whether transverse fields would affect the complexity phases shown in
Fig.

2
.

Non-symmetric terms.

We analyze
\{K\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problems where the interaction term
K
is symmetric under interchange of qubits. At the opposite end, fully antisymmetric interactions yield
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete problems
[
CM16
]
. The intermediate regime, where
K
has both symmetric and antisymmetric components, is not well understood.

Multiple interaction terms.

Our results focus on the case
\mathcal{S}=\left\{K\right\}
. When
\mathcal{S}
contains multiple interaction terms, the complexity is less clear. Such Hamiltonians are at least as hard as any convex combination of terms in
\mathcal{S}
. For example, let
\mathcal{S}=\left\{K_{1},K_{2}\right\}
, where

\displaystyle K_{1}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2}{3}\ket{\psi^{+}}\bra{\psi^{+}}-\tfrac{1}{3}\ket{\phi^{+}}\bra{\phi^{+}}-\ket{\phi^{-}}\bra{\phi^{-}},

\displaystyle K_{2}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{3}\ket{\psi^{+}}\bra{\psi^{+}}+\tfrac{2}{3}\ket{\phi^{+}}\bra{\phi^{+}}-\ket{\phi^{-}}\bra{\phi^{-}}.

Both
\{K_{1}\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
and
\{K_{2}\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
reduce to
EPR*
, whereas their average

\displaystyle K^{\prime}=\tfrac{1}{2}(K_{1}+K_{2})=\tfrac{1}{3}\ket{\psi^{+}}\bra{\psi^{+}}+\tfrac{1}{3}\ket{\phi^{+}}\bra{\phi^{+}}-\ket{\phi^{-}}\bra{\phi^{-}}

is
{\mathsf{StoqMA}}
-complete by
Theorem

3
. This demonstrates that mixtures of interaction terms can increase complexity.

Restricted geometries.

Many physically relevant models impose geometric constraints on the interaction graph, such as regular lattices. Prior work
[
BH14
,
PM15
]
studies triangular and square lattices, where
Local Hamiltonian
problems often remain hard. It remains open whether
Theorem

3
can be strengthened to establish
{\mathsf{StoqMA}}
-hardness under such geometric restrictions.

Higher locality.

Extending our framework to
k
-local Hamiltonians presents additional challenges. Unlike the
2
-local case, there is no canonical notion of a maximally entangled state. It is unclear whether states such as
\ket{W}
or
\ket{GHZ}
play roles analogous to the singlet and triplet states. A key question is whether similarly simple structural rules can govern computational complexity and phase transitions in this setting.

A useful repository of complexity results for these generalizations of the
\{K\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problem is given in
[
WME23
]
.

A stronger conjecture

We conclude with a stronger conjecture suggested by our results. In the toy model,
Fig.

1
shows that as the singlet moves down in the energy-level ordering of
K
, the corresponding
\{K\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problem becomes monotonically harder. The same phenomenon appears in the general setting (
Fig.

2
). This suggests the following general principle:

Conjecture 3

(The singlet conjecture)
.

Let
K
be any symmetric
2
-local interaction term, and let
\ket{\tau}
be any triplet state. Define
K^{\prime}=K-\ket{\tau}\bra{\tau}
. Then the
\{K^{\prime}\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problem is at most as hard as the
\{K\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problem.

In other words, the singlet conjecture suggests that lowering the energy of a triplet state (equivalently, raising the singlet in the ordering) should not increase the complexity of the associated
\{K\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problem.

This conjecture has strong consequences. The local term
K
is described by one singlet energy level and three indistinguishable triplet energy levels. Let an
arrangement
of the energy levels denote a weak-ordering (i.e. allowing degeneracies) of these energy levels. We then show

Lemma 11
.

There are exactly
20
unique arrangements of the energy levels of
K
.

The proof is deferred to
Appendix

D
. We treat the triplets as indistinguishable because permuting the triplet states does not change the complexity of the corresponding
\{K\}^{+}
-Hamiltonian
problem (as explained in
Section

2.3
).

In
Fig.

7
, we visualize the implications of
3
across these
20
arrangements.

:

20
distinct arrangements of energy levels of

. In each diagram, the oval denotes the singlet energy and dashes denote triplet energies. Degenerate triplets are shown side by side, while triplets degenerate with the singlet are drawn inside the oval. Red arrangements are

[

). An arrow indicates that one arrangement can be obtained from another by lowering a triplet energy. Under

This picture shows two consequences of

1.

There are green arrangements (which lie in

{\mathsf{NP}}

{\mathsf{P}}

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{j-1}}{\overline{\alpha}_{j}}=\frac{\left(\lambda_{1}-d_{j}\right)-\beta_{j}\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{j+1}}{\overline{\alpha}_{j}}}{\beta_{j-1}}\leq\frac{\left(\lambda_{1}-d_{j}\right)}{\beta_{j-1}}\leq\frac{L+2aj(L-j)}{j(L-j)}\leq 2a+\frac{L}{j(L-j)}\leq 2a+2,

where in the second inequality we used

\displaystyle\leq aL(L-1)+\sqrt{L(L-1)}\leq aL(L-1)+L,

\displaystyle\beta_{j-1}

\displaystyle=j\sqrt{(L-j+1)(L-j)}\geq j(L-j)

and in the last inequality we used that

is minimized in the range

(26)

This, in particular implies that for all

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2a+2}\right)^{j}\leq\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{j}}{\overline{\alpha}_{0}}\leq\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{a}\right)^{j}

The upper bound on

bounds the sum from above

\displaystyle t_{i}^{X}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\frac{\sqrt{j}\sqrt{L-j}}{L-1}\overline{\alpha}_{j}\overline{\alpha}_{L-j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\overline{\alpha}_{j}\overline{\alpha}_{L-j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\overline{\alpha}_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{a}\right)^{L}\leq L\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{a}\right)^{L},

where in the first inequality we used that
\frac{\sqrt{j(L-j)}}{L-1}\leq 1
.

The lower bound on
\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{j}}{\overline{\alpha}_{0}}
bounds the sum from below

\displaystyle t_{i}^{X}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\frac{\sqrt{j}\sqrt{L-j}}{L-1}\overline{\alpha}_{j}\overline{\alpha}_{L-j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\frac{\sqrt{j}\sqrt{L-j}}{L-1}\overline{\alpha}_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2a+2}\right)^{j}\left(\frac{1}{2a+2}\right)^{L-j}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2a+2}\right)^{L}\overline{\alpha}_{0}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\frac{\sqrt{j}\sqrt{L-j}}{L-1}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2a+2}\right)^{L}\left(\frac{a-2-\tfrac{1}{L}}{a-1}\right)\frac{(L+1)}{3}.

and that

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{L(L-1)}\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}j(L-j)=\frac{2}{L(L-1)}\left(\frac{L^{2}(L-1)}{2}-\frac{(L-1)L(2L-1)}{6}\right)=\frac{(L+1)}{3}.

Thus, for any

Appendix D
Proof of
Lemma

11

Let
t\in\{0,1,2,3\}
represent the number of triplet states that are degenerate with the singlet. Let
s=3-t
be the number of remaining triplet states. These
s
triplets must lie either above or below the singlet.

For a fixed
s\geq 1
, we count all possible arrangements by splitting into cases. Suppose exactly
m
of the
s
triplets lie above the singlet, and the remaining
s-m
lie below, where
m=0,1,\dots,s
. These cases are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, so we sum over all
m
.

For a fixed
m
, the
m
triplets above the singlet can be grouped into degenerate energy levels. The number of such groupings is given by the number of
compositions
of
m
. A composition is a way of writing a nonnegative number
n
as a sum over smaller positive numbers. The number of compositions is given by

\displaystyle f(n)=\begin{cases}2^{n-1},&n\geq 1,\\
1,&n=0.\end{cases}

Similarly, the
s-m
triplets below the singlet can be arranged in
f(s-m)
ways. Since these choices are independent, the total number of arrangements for fixed
m
is
f(m)f(s-m)
. Thus, for fixed
s
there are

\displaystyle F(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{s}f(m)f(s-m),

arrangements. We evaluate
F(s)
by separating the edge cases:

•

m=0
:
f(0)f(s)=1\cdot 2^{s-1}=2^{s-1}
,

•

m=s
:
f(s)f(0)=2^{s-1}\cdot 1=2^{s-1}
,

•

1\leq m\leq s-1
:
f(m)f(s-m)=2^{m-1}\cdot 2^{s-m-1}=2^{s-2}
.

There are
s-1
terms
1\leq m\leq s-1
, so

\displaystyle F(s)=2^{s-1}+2^{s-1}+\mathbbm{1}_{s>1}(s-1)2^{s-2}=2\cdot 2^{s-1}+\mathbbm{1}_{s>1}(s-1)2^{s-2}.

Finally, we evaluate for each
s
and find

F(0)+F(1)+F(2)+F(3)=1+2+5+12=20
.
