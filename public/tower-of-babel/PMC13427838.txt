Select plasma oxylipins as potential biomarkers of effective indomethacin treatment response in preterm infants with patent ductus arteriosus
Authors: Khanh C. K. Nguyen, Cindy H. T. Yeung, Allison Scott, Thomas J. Velenosi, Tamorah Lewis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesPatent ductus arteriosus (PDA) is one of the most common heart conditions in preterm infants, and indomethacin treatment is used for PDA closure. However, its efficacy varies among patients. This pilot study aimed to identify novel metabolic biomarkers to predict effective indomethacin response in infants with PDA.MethodsPreterm infants with PDA < 34 weeks gestational age were recruited and treated with indomethacin. Plasma and urine samples were collected before and 2–7 days after initial treatment. Longitudinal targeted oxylipin and untargeted metabolomics analysis were performed on plasma and urine samples.ResultsAmong 17 infants, 12 were responders and 5 non-responders to indomethacin treatment. Before the treatment, four plasma oxylipins: 12,13-DiHOME, 9,10-DiHOME, 15,16-DiHODE, and 9,10-DiHODE were significantly higher in responders compared to non-responders (P < 0.05). Following treatment, these oxylipins decreased in responders but remained stable in non-responders. Urinary 12,13-DiHOME showed an opposite trend, with lower baseline concentrations in the responders.ConclusionsPre-treatment plasma levels of four oxylipins were associated with effective PDA response to indomethacin. The balance of oxylipins and prostaglandins is important in duct patency and may predict which infants responding to indomethacin. These pilot findings contribute to the identification of potential neonatal pharmacometabolomic biomarkers and create new hypotheses that can guide future research towards personalized PDA management in preterm infants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11306-026-02510-9.

Introduction

Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA) is a commonly encountered morbidity of preterm birth, treated with Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs) and acetaminophen. In a study of over 9,000 preterm infants between 2013 and 2018, 23% of them received medication for PDA closure (Bell et al. 2022). Yet, duct closure fails in up to 50% of preterm infants who receive drug therapy, requiring either further rounds of drug therapy or a surgical intervention (Boghossian et al. 2017). Furthermore, the infants who are exposed to these drugs without therapeutic benefit are placed at a risk of toxicity such as renal dysfunction or gastrointestinal perforation (Gill and Newby 2025). Thus, in order to improve the risk/benefit ratio for NSAIDS in preterm infants, we must improve our understanding of the mechanisms of drug success and drug failure in this unique preterm infant patient population.

Indomethacin is used for PDA closure in preterm infants. It has been studied in over 14 randomized controlled trials including over 800 preterm infants and is shown to be effective at ductal closure (Evans et al. 2021). Indomethacin is a potent cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibitor that lowers circulating prostaglandin levels. Since prostaglandins play an important role in maintaining the ductal patency, their reduction promotes ductal closure. Despite being dosed in a standard mg/kg fashion in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), there is known variability in indomethacin pharmacokinetics and plasma levels (Krzyzanski et al. 2023). With current indomethacin dosing paradigms, recent evidence suggests there is a lack of exposure-response relationship for PDA closure (Yeung et al. 2025). Given the lack of generalized efficacy and exposure-response relationship, it is critical to improve our mechanistic understanding that contributes to the variability in indomethacin drug response.

Pharmacometabolomics aims to understand how the metabolome relates to drug response (Prakash et al. 2025). In a patient population with unexplained variability in drug efficacy, it can be a powerful tool to assess changes in metabolite profiles with drug treatment, comparing drug responders to non-responders. If there is a priori knowledge about which pathways may be involved in drug response, targeted metabolomic panels can provide quantitative data on small molecules that may serve as biomarkers for predicting treatment effectiveness.

In this project, we conducted a pilot study to assess the pharmacometabolomic signatures in a group of preterm infants who were treated with indomethacin for PDA closure, with the goal of identifying predictive biomarkers of treatment response. Indomethacin inhibits the synthesis of prostaglandins, a group of oxylipins that keep the ductus arteriosus open by dilating smooth muscle (Pacifici 2013). Therefore, a complete analysis of oxylipins in responders and non-responders may provide insights into whether metabolites in the indomethacin target pathway can predict treatment response. In addition, analysis of all metabolites (untargeted metabolomics) was also included to capture systemic physiological differences between infants, which may also influence drug effectiveness.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cohort of preterm infants was recruited at Children’s Mercy Hospital in Kansas City, Missouri between 2017 and 2021. The Children’s Mercy ethics review board approved the study prior to patient enrollment, and the University of British Columbia Clinical Research Ethics Board (CREB) approved data access for analysis. Preterm infants < 34 weeks gestational age (GA) at birth were eligible if they were treated with indomethacin per routine clinical care for PDA closure and indomethacin was started at greater than 72 h of age. This was a prospective cohort study in which all infants were diagnosed with a clinically symptomatic PDA per local physician judgement and intravenous indomethacin was initiated per a standard local dosing protocol. Although there was no strict protocol of when a PDA was deemed clinically significant and warranted treatment at the time this study was conducted, the common parameters used by physicians to decide to treat a PDA were presence of hypotension, decreased renal function, and left ventricular/left atrial enlargement on echocardiogram. In addition, the dosing regimen and outcome ascertainment for this population were also described in previous studies (Krzyzanski et al. 2023; Yeung et al. 2025).

Patent ductus arteriosus treatment strategy and outcome groups

Indomethacin 0.1 mg/kg was given intravenously for a 3-dose course. After 3 doses, an echocardiogram was performed to assess ductal patency and size. If there was an inadequate drug response, a second course of indomethacin (0.2 mg/kg) could be considered. In infants who were deemed to fail drug therapy, a referral for surgical PDA ligation was made. For study purposes, patients were divided into one of two drug treatment response categories: Those who closed the PDA or had a small residual PDA but did not require further treatment were categorized as “responders”, and those who went on to require surgical PDA ligation were categorized as “non-responders”.

Plasma and urine sample collection

For purposes of metabolic quantification, a urine and plasma sample were collected before indomethacin treatment (baseline) and 2–7 days after the first course of indomethacin was started. Post-treatment samples collection coincided with assessment of clinical response. Supplementary Table S2 and S3 indicate individual patient post-treatment sample timing alongside the number of received courses of indomethacin treatment. These samples were frozen at − 80 °C until they were shipped to the UC Davis Metabolomics Center for metabolite analysis.

Targeted oxylipin and untargeted metabolomics analysis

Targeted oxylipin analysis was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), and untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) following the previously published protocols at the UC Davis West Coast Metabolomics Center (Fiehn 2016; Pedersen and Newman 2018). System suitability, internal standards, blank samples, QC samples, sample preparation and randomization were performed by UC Davis West Coast Metabolomics Center according to their protocols. A targeted oxylipin panel was chosen as the metabolomic test platform in this cohort because NSAIDs are known to interact with this physiologic system. For oxylipin quantitation, the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) values were experimentally determined by analyzing serial dilutions of standard compounds, and selecting the lowest concentration that could be quantified with acceptable precision (coefficient of variation ≤ 20%) and accuracy (measured concentration within ± 20% of the nominal value), in accordance with established bioanalytical method validation guidelines.(“Bioanalytical Method Validation Guidance for Industry” 2018) Oxylipin concentrations below the LLOQ were replaced with 1/2 value of LLOQ. Individual oxylipins with concentrations < LLOQ in all samples were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Clinical data were presented as mean with standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as numbers with percentages for categorical variables. Comparison between responder and non-responder groups was assessed using Student’s t-test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Oxylipin and untargeted metabolomics multivariate statistical analysis was conducted in R (version 4.4.1). Data was centered and pareto scaled. To minimize overfitting in our dataset, we chose to perform a multilevel partial least squares discriminant analysis (mPLS-DA) on plasma and urine samples using mixOmics (6.28.0). Unlike orthogonal PLS-DA, mPLS-DA supports paired multivariate statistical analysis, which separates variation between subjects from variation within subjects (Westerhuis et al. 2010). mPLS-DA provides the net differences between paired observations and was applied to compare metabolic variation between responder and non-responder groups. Metabolites showing trends or differences between outcome groups were further analyzed by univariate statistics using a two-way repeated measures ANOVA. The Spearman correlation test was applied to assess the correlation between baseline plasma concentrations of oxylipins and patient clinical characteristics. Two-way repeated measures ANOVA and Spearman correlation were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.3 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA).

Results

Clinical characteristics

Between 2017 and 2021, parents of eligible preterm infants with clinically symptomatic PDA admitted to Children’s Mercy Hospital were approached for informed consent and a total of 17 preterm infants were enrolled during this period. Ten of the infants received a single 3-dose course of indomethacin and 7 of the infants received two 3-dose courses of indomethacin. Following treatment, 12 infants were identified as “responders” and 5 as “non-responders”. The non-responders all required surgical PDA closure due to drug therapy failure.

Only infants with both pre- and post-treatment samples available were included in the final data analyses. For oxylipin quantitation, 10 responders and 4 non-responders were included for plasma data analysis, and 5 responders and 2 non-responders were included for urine data analysis. The untargeted metabolomics analysis included 5 responders and 2 non-responders for plasma samples, and 6 responders and 3 non-responders for urine samples. Clinical characteristics of infants in the oxylipin and metabolomics analyses are described in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1, respectively. There were no significant differences in weight, sex, gestational age, or number of infants receiving antibiotics during the treatment between responders and non-responders. Responders had a significantly lower postnatal age at the time of indomethacin therapy than non-responders (P < 0.05).

Association between oxylipin levels and indomethacin treatment response

To understand the association between oxylipin levels and treatment response, we compared oxylipin profiles before and after treatment within and between responder and non-responder groups. The LCMS method included a total of 67 oxylipins in the quantification panel.

After excluding oxylipins with concentrations below the LLOQ in all samples, 45 oxylipins were retained for plasma analysis and 43 for urine analysis.

In plasma samples, mPLS-DA showed clear separation between pre- and post-treatment samples in the responder group, as well as between pre-treatment samples from responders and non-responders (Fig. 1a). This separation was primarily driven by elevated levels of diol containing oxylipins in pre-treatment samples from infants in the responder group (Fig. 1b).

At baseline, the levels of four oxylipins, 12,13-DiHOME, 9,10-DiHOME, 15,16-DiHODE, and 9,10-DiHODE, were significantly higher in the responder group than the non-responder group (P < 0.05). After the treatment, 9,10-DiHOME and 15,16-DiHODE were significantly reduced (P < 0.05) while 9,10-DiHODE and 12,13-DiHOME exhibited a decreasing trend in the responder group (P = 0.06 and P = 0.07, respectively). No apparent change was observed between pre- and post-treatment samples in non-responders (Fig. 2). Interestingly, these four oxylipins were derived from the cytochrome P450 – soluble epoxide hydrolase (CYP/sEH) metabolic pathways, and all showed a decreasing trend in levels following treatment, suggesting a consistent pattern and possible converging mechanism related to the treatment response (Fig. 3) (Cambiaggi et al. 2023; Lynes et al. 2017). In addition, mPLS-DA demonstrated that the metabolic variation between the two groups was also associated with increased levels of 13 S-HODE in post-treatment samples from the responder group and TXB2 in pre-treatment samples from the non-responder group. However, these variations were influenced by a single infant in each case.

Association between untargeted metabolite profiles and indomethacin treatment response

A total of 986 metabolites were measured, and 267 metabolites were identified in plasma and urine samples. In both sample types, mPLS-DA demonstrated clear separation between pre- and post-treatment samples within and between responder and non-responder groups (Supplementary Fig. 1). In mPLS-DA, metabolites were ranked based on variable importance in projection (VIP) values. A subset of the top 20 metabolites with the highest VIP scores was selected for further analysis. Among these, lactic acid showed a trend of higher baseline levels in the responder group in both plasma and urine samples. Following treatment, lactic acid levels decreased in 3 out of 5 infants in plasma samples and in 3 out of 6 infants in urine samples in the responder group (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this pilot study, we investigated the pharmacometabolic signatures associated with indomethacin treatment for PDA closure in preterm infants by comparing metabolite profiles before and after treatment within each group (responders and non-responders) as well as between groups. Oxylipin quantification analysis revealed that four oxylipins (12,13-DiHOME, 9,10-DiHOME, 15,16-DiHODE, and 9,10-DiHODE) showed significantly higher baseline plasma levels in responders, and their levels appeared to decrease after treatment. A trend of elevated urinary baseline levels of 12,13-DiHOME and 19,20-DiHDPE were observed in the non-responder group. Untargeted metabolomics also suggested higher baseline levels of lactic acid in responders in both plasma and urine. In addition, responders had a significantly lower postnatal age compared to non-responders.

There is ongoing controversy within the field of neonatology regarding the need to treat PDA and the potential benefit, the optimal timing of treatment (prophylactic, early versus late/symptomatic) and which drug provides the optimal risk to benefit profile (Mitra et al. 2025; Parikh and Velu 2026; Smith et al. 2026). Despite this, when clinicians do indeed decide to treat PDA with drug therapy, an improved understanding of the physiologic risk factors for drug failure is important to understand. Drug therapy for PDA has evolved over time, with indomethacin being one of three most commonly used drugs along with ibuprofen and acetaminophen. The decision of which PDA drug therapy to use is typically driven by individual unit practice, drug formulation availability, infant characteristics, and concern for risk of drug side effects. While it is true that non-indomethacin drugs have increased in use over time, there are no PDA treatment guidelines that suggest use of one drug over the other. All three drugs have a similar ~ 40% failure rate, as demonstrated in multiple publications. In a recent Canadian study by Mitra et al., among 1356 preterm infants who received treatment with any of the three drugs, 464 (42.3%) experienced failure of the primary pharmacotherapy, and 456 (41.6%) received repeat pharmacotherapy course (Mitra et al. 2026). A recent pharmacoepidemiology paper of PDA drug therapy across the United States among 54,813 infants, indomethacin was still the most commonly prescribed monotherapy for PDA up until 2019, with many infants still requiring treatment with multiple different NSAIDs for drug therapeutic failure (Weems et al. 2025). Given that all three drugs have a similar mechanism of COX inhibition, our findings in the indomethacin cohort may be generalizable to other drugs used to treat PDA.

Oxylipins are generated from PUFA oxidation and most PUFAs are selectively transferred in the last trimester of pregnancy (Gil-Sánchez et al. 2011). Studies in pre-term infants evaluate circulating oxylipins following dietary supplementation using arachidonic acid and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) have demonstrated intake dependence (Álvarez et al. 2022). In addition, maternal and cord blood levels of precursor omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids are associated with downstream oxylipin production within the same pathway (Thompson et al. 2022). Prostaglandins are produced downstream of ARA, and while indomethacin and other cyclooxygenase inhibitors reduce prostaglandin E2 levels in infants during PDA treatment, both responders and non-responders experience a similar reduction, suggesting that a reduction in circulating PGE levels is not associated with PDA closure (Nagafuji et al. 2025).

In our study, baseline plasma levels of 12,13-DiHOME, 9,10-DiHOME, 15,16-DiHODE, and 9,10-DiHODE were found to be significantly higher in preterm infants who responded to indomethacin treatment. 12,13-DiHOME and 9,10-DiHOME are derived from linoleic acid (LA) via the cytochrome P450 – soluble epoxide hydrolase (CYP/sEH) pathways (Fig. 3) (Lynes et al. 2017). Additionally, LA also serves as a precursor for arachidonic acid, which is the substrate for prostaglandin synthesis via COX enzymes (Dos Santos and Fleming 2020). Elevated DiHOME levels may suggest a relative shift in LA metabolism away from prostaglandin precursors and towards the DiHOME pathway, decreasing baseline circulating levels of prostaglandins. Given the critical role of prostaglandins in ductal patency and the improved treatment response in infants with a shift toward LA metabolism, the use of prostaglandin-lowering indomethacin in these patients may be associated with better outcomes.

Previous evidence of relative alterations in plasma 12,13-DiHOME is limited. In neonates, 12,13-DiHOME fecal concentrations were associated with risk of asthma and atopic diseases (Fujimura et al. 2016). In a cold exposure experiment, adults demonstrated elevated plasma 12,13-DiHOME resulting in increased brown adipose tissue fatty acid uptake (Lynes et al. 2017). Similar to the generation DiHOME oxylipins following LA oxidation, 15,16-DiHODE and 9,10-DiHODE are also CYP-derived metabolites, but from α-linolenic acid (ALA) (Cambiaggi et al. 2023). While the association between the metabolism of ALA and these oxylipins with PDA pathophysiology remains unclear, their association with treatment response suggests that other biological processes may be involved in ductal closure. In total, four oxylipins (12,13-DiHOME, 9,10-DiHOME, 15,16-DiHODE, and 9,10-DiHODE) may serve as potential biomarkers, and with further validation studies, may eventually serve to help clinicians predict drug response outcomes prior the treatment. Infants who are less likely to respond could receive alternative therapies, minimizing unnecessary exposure to indomethacin. In addition, a recent study highlighted the need for further dose-escalation studies of indomethacin to study the exposure–response relationship for PDA closure (Yeung et al. 2025). The use of accessible and validated biomarkers could reduce reliance on PDA outcomes alone for each assessment and provide mechanistic insights into treatment response across different dosing regimens.

Physiological levels of DiHOMEs and DiHODEs may be influenced by the biosynthetic activity and intake of their precursor fatty acids from diet, LA and ALA (Hildreth et al. 2020). In addition, 12,13-DiHOME has also been detected in human breast milk (Wolfs et al. 2021). In our cohort, infants received Intralipid, an intravenous fat emulsion composed of 20% soybean oil, which is rich in LA and ALA (Mezoff et al. 2020). Since DiHOMEs and DiHODEs are not present in Intralipid, the elevated levels of these oxylipins are likely due to the endogenous metabolism of LA and ALA, respectively. Indeed, a previous study demonstrated elevated plasma levels of 12,13-DiHOME following intralipid supplementation (Hildreth et al. 2020). However, all infants in this study received intralipid, which suggests that differences in endogenous metabolism between responders and non-responders may be linked to PDA treatment outcome. Additionally, no studies have reported an increased incidence of PDA associated with early intravenous lipid infusion in preterm infants and no significant differences in PDA incidence have been observed between infants receiving lipid emulsions with high (100% soybean oil) versus lower (20% soybean oil) levels of LA and ALA (Salama et al. 2015; Savini et al. 2013). This evidence further supports the potential link between endogenous metabolism of LA and ALA with PDA treatment outcomes.

Oxylipins are mediators of inflammatory processes (Misheva et al. 2022). Therefore, baseline differences in oxylipin levels may reflect the difference in inflammatory status between responders and non-responders and could act as a confounding factor. However, there were no significant differences in number of infants receiving antibiotics during indomethacin treatment between two groups (Table 1). In addition, previous studies have shown that infection negatively affects the pharmacologic PDA closure and increases the need of additional treatment or surgical intervention (Pistulli et al. 2014). In contrast, we observed higher baseline oxylipin levels in infants who responded to indomethacin, suggesting that potential confounding variable of inflammatory status was limited.

Among clinical characteristics, infants in the responder group had a significantly lower postnatal age compared to non-responders. Postnatal age is an important factor influencing the maturation of indomethacin metabolism and clearance (Krzyzanski et al. 2023). Compared to more mature infants, those with lower postnatal age have less hepatic metabolism and renal clearance, leading to higher exposure to indomethacin at the same weight-based dose, which may increase the likelihood of PDA closure. The baseline plasma concentrations of 12,13-DiHOME, 9,10-DiHOME, 15,16-DiHODE, and 9,10-DiHODE collected in this study varied among infants. Correlation analysis between plasma levels and clinical characteristics was conducted to explore potential sources of variability. However, no significant associations were found, suggesting that other factors may contribute to the observed variability in baseline oxylipin levels.

In addition to oxylipins, plasma lactate between pre- and post- samples differed in responders compared to non-responders. Infants who responded to indomethacin had an average decrease in plasma lactate, while those who were non-responders had an average increase. This finding also has biologic plausibility but is more a pharmacodynamic biomarker of drug response. Lactate is a marker of tissue hypoperfusion (Andersen et al. 2013) and preterm infants with large open PDA are known to have systemic steal from their circulation and are at risk for distal organ hypoperfusion and lactatemia (Okur et al. 2019).

Our study has strengths and weaknesses. We were able to analyze a group of preterm infants with clinically symptomatic PDA and obtained both pre- and post-treatment samples from most participants despite the challenges of collecting multiple samples in this critically ill preterm infant patient population. Untargeted metabolomics and targeted oxylipin analysis from both plasma and urine samples provided a comprehensive investigation of metabolites and their pathways associated with indomethacin response. Another strength is the standard indomethacin dosing in the NICU, so dose variability does not confound our findings. Although PDA is common in preterm infants, it is rare in full-term infants, which limits the available study population (Dice and Bhatia 2007). As a result, our single-center study had a relatively small sample size and we acknowledge the sample size as a limitation of our study. In addition, non-responders were conservatively grouped as patients that required surgical duct closure. Due to the limited sample size, we were unable divide patients into further treatment subgroups. Therefore, oxylipin biomarkers associated with responders collectively include patients that did not require surgery and further studies are needed to determine whether biomarkers can divide patients by individual treatment subgroups. In addition, the timing of post-treatment samples varied between infants, which may have contributed to oxylipin variability.

Future research with larger, multicenter cohorts is needed to validate the selected oxylipins identified as potential pre-treatment biomarkers.

Conclusion

We conducted a prospective pilot study to explore the pharmacometabolomic signatures associated with indomethacin treatment for PDA closure in preterm infants. We identified that pre-treatment plasma levels of 12,13-DiHOME, 9,10-DiHOME, 15,16-DiHODE, and 9,10-DiHODE were significantly elevated in infants who responded to indomethacin. Following this hypothesis-generating pilot study, future prospective validation of these findings is necessary and may contribute towards the potential use of oxylipins as prognostic biomarkers to guide PDA drug therapy in preterm infants.
