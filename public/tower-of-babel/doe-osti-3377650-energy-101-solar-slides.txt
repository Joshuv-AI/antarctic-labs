 Resource: Definitions

Irradiance (power/area): Irradiance is the amount of solar power hitting a surface at a
specific moment in time. Typical peak value of irradiance [midday in the summer in a
sunny area] is ~ 1 kW/m2).

Insolation/Irradiation (energy/area): The integral of the irradiance over a given time
period. Typical average daily insolation on a horizontal surface in Colorado is ~ 4.9
kWh/m2. The unit kWh/m2 is sometime called a “sun-hour”.

Irradiance is the instantaneous solar power per unit area, whereas insolation (or
irradiation) is the cumulative solar energy per unit area over a period of time.

Source: Sandia National Laboratory, n.d.

                                                                          National Laboratory of the Rockies   |   3

 Resource: Components
Direct Normal Irradiance (DNI): the amount of solar radiation received per unit area by a surface that is
always held perpendicular (or normal) to the rays that come in a straight line from the direction of the sun
at its current position in the sky. Typically, you can maximize the amount of irradiance annually received
by a surface by keeping it normal to incoming radiation. This quantity is of particular interest to
concentrating solar thermal installations and installations that track the position of the sun.

Diffuse Horizontal Irradiance (DHI): the amount of radiation received per unit area by a surface (not
subject to any shade or shadow) that does not arrive on a direct path from the sun but has been scattered
by molecules and particles in the atmosphere and comes equally from all directions.

Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI): the total amount of radiation received from above by a horizontal
surface. This value is of particular interest to photovoltaic installations and includes both Direct Normal
Irradiance (DNI) and Diffuse Horizontal Irradiance (DHI).

Reflected Irradiance: the amount of radiation received per unit area by a surface that has been reflected
(typically) from the ground.
Source: Sandia National Laboratory, n.d.

 Steps for Planning for Residential Solar

There are a number of steps when exploring whether to add solar energy to
your home.
1. Investigate your home's energy use and consider energy efficiency
 upgrades
2. See how much potential solar energy can be produced at your home
3. Assess what options you have for using solar energy
4. Estimate how much solar energy your home needs
5. Get bids and assessments from contractors
6. Learn about financing and incentives
7. Ready to install? Work with the installer and utility company.

 Cost

A residential solar PV system typically costs around $2.90 per watt (Wdc). However,
 actual costs vary depending on several favors including:
 – Household electricity demand
 – System size
 – Geographic location
 – Permitting and interconnection requirements
 – Installation complexity
 – Roof type and structural characteristics
 – Panel type and equipment selection
 – Available incentives, tax credits, or rebates.
Projects located in remote or island areas may experience higher costs due to
  transportation, labor, availability, and other location-specific challenges.
Source: Solar Installed System Cost Analysis, NLR, 2026

• How much power is needed?

• How much maintenance will be required? What is the lifetime of the system?
• What is the estimated project cost? What long-term savings or other benefits
  are there?
• Is net metering available? Are there other incentives available?

 Community Solar – How it Works
• Community solar allows multiple customers (subscribers) to purchase the output
  from solar PV.
• The solar project is not located at the house or business where electricity is used.
• Subscribers receive a credit on their utility bill based upon the amount of electricity
  produced by their share of the solar project.
• This crediting system is called virtual net metering.
 Imagine your share of a community solar project produces 600 kWh of energy
 in one month.

 On the next utility bill, you would receive credit for the 600 kWh of electricity.

 If your total consumption was 800 kWh that month, you would only need to pay the
 utility company for the remaining 200 kWh of electricity.

 Concentrated Solar Power

  • Concentrated solar power (CSP) is an emerging technology that uses reflectors to
 focus sunlight and create heat.
  • For electricity generation, it can then feed solar heat into steam turbines with synchronous
 generators, thereby providing inertia, stability, and resilience for the grid.
  • CSP can provide reliable heat or electricity by integrating long-duration thermal energy
 storage (TES) for 10 hours or more.

SolarReserves Crescent Dunes CSP Project, near Tonopah, Nevada, has an electricity generating capacity of 110 MW.

 Passive Solar Design

• A passive solar building collects heat from the sun and stores that heat in
  materials throughout the building.
• A successful design must include the following elements:
  • Aperture—a large glass area through which sunlight enters the building.
  • Thermal mass—commonly concrete, brick, stone, and tile.
  • Distribution—a method by which solar heat is transferred from where it
 is collected and stored to different areas of the house.
  • Control—devices such as roof overhangs used to shade the aperture
 area during summer months.

 Financing

Financing falls into two broad categories based on ownership of the solar PV
system:
1. Third-party ownership
 a) Third-party ownership of residential solar systems allows homeowners to avoid high, up-front
 system costs and instead spread out their payments over time. It also often puts some or all of
 the responsibility for system operation and maintenance on the third-party owner.
2. Homeowner ownership via a loan
 a) Solar loans allow customers to borrow money from a lender or solar developer for the
 installation of a solar PV system. With this approach, the homeowner owns the installed
 system.
