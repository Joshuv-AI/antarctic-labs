FLAME: Condensing Ensemble Diversity into a Single Network for Efficient Sequential Recommendation Conference: the 49th International ACM Conference on Research and Development in Information Retrieval; July 20–24, 2026; Melbourne, Australia CCS: Information systems Recommender systems
WooJoo Kim, JunYoung Kim, JaeHyung Lim, SeongJin Choi, SeongKu Kang, HwanJo Yu

Abstract
. Sequential recommendation requires capturing diverse user behaviors, which a single network often fails to capture. While ensemble methods mitigate this by leveraging multiple networks, training them all from scratch leads to high computational cost and instability from noisy mutual supervision. We propose F rozen and L earnable networks with A ligned M odular E nsemble ( FLAME ), a novel framework that condenses ensemble-level diversity into a single network for efficient sequential recommendation. During training, FLAME simulates exponential diversity using only two networks via modular ensemble . By decomposing each network into sub-modules (e.g., layers or blocks) and dynamically combining them, FLAME generates a rich space of diverse representation patterns. To stabilize this process, we pretrain and freeze one network to serve as a semantic anchor and employ guided mutual learning . This aligns the diverse representations into the space of the remaining learnable network, ensuring robust optimization. Consequently, at inference, FLAME utilizes only the learnable network, achieving ensemble-level performance with zero overhead compared to a single network. Experiments on six datasets show that FLAME outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, achieving up to 7.69 × \times faster convergence and 9.70% improvement in NDCG@20. We provide the source code of FLAME at https://github.com/woo-joo/FLAME_SIGIR26 .

These challenges highlight the need for models that can interpret sequences from multiple perspectives.
Instead of relying on a single model to capture the full range of behavioral patterns, a promising direction is to combine multiple models, each focusing on complementary aspects of the data.
This is the core idea behind ensemble methods
(

In this light, ensemble learning is particularly well-suited for sequential recommendation, where user intent and item semantics vary widely.

Recently, several ensemble-based methods have been proposed to capture the diverse dynamics embedded within user sequences
(

54

These approaches commonly train multiple networks from scratch in parallel, with each network encouraged to specialize in complementary behavioral patterns.
Inspired by recent advancements in computer vision field

(

further incorporate mutual knowledge transfer among networks to enhance collective learning.
For example, EMKD
(

performs both representation-level contrastive learning and logit-level knowledge distillation, allowing individual networks to align their outputs while maintaining diversity in internal representations.
Such strategies aim to promote inter-network agreement and help each network effectively navigate the extremely large parameter search space inherent to deep neural networks, enabling more efficient optimization than training networks independently.

Despite their effectiveness, recent ensemble-based methods suffer from two major limitations:
computational inefficiency
and
training instability
.
First, employing multiple networks significantly increases computational costs, including parameters, memory footprint, and training time.
In particular, typical ensemble prediction requires forwarding each network independently and aggregating their output, resulting in training and inference costs that are several times higher than those of a single network.
This makes such methods impractical for real-time services.
Second, training all networks from scratch with mutual knowledge transfer often leads to unstable optimization, especially in the early stages when none of the networks has reliable prior knowledge
(
25
;
51
)
, ultimately limiting performance.
This problem can be substantially aggravated in sequential recommendation, where data tends to be sparse and noisy
(
16
)
.
In our experiments, existing ensemble methods show large training fluctuations and require 2.06 to 2.94 times longer to converge than standard model (see Section
5.6

To address the inefficiency and instability of ensemble-based sequential recommendation methods, we focus on two key insights informed by previous literature and our empirical study.
First,
different sub-modules within a single network can capture distinct patterns
.
Prior works
(
10
;
22
;
45
)
commonly observe that as layers deepen, neural representations become increasingly abstract and discriminative.
A similar phenomenon can be expected in sequential recommendation, where, for instance, the embedding module captures static item semantics, while early layers of the sequence encoder reflect localized and item-specific signals, and deeper layers encode temporally smoothed representations of user preferences.
This structural separation suggests that even a single network can internally generate diverse representations by interpreting sequences from multiple perspectives.
Building on this, our key idea is to simulate ensemble-like behavior by leveraging an exponential number of sub-module combinations constructed from just two networks–without explicitly constructing multiple models.

•

•

We conduct extensive experiments on six real-world datasets, where FLAME consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines. Our results show that FLAME achieves superior accuracy with significantly lower training cost and inference latency.

Preliminary and Related Work

2.1.
Problem Formulation

In the sequential recommendation setting, the goal is to predict which item a user will interact with next, given her chronological sequence of past interactions. Let
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{I}
denote the sets of users and items, respectively. Each user
u\in\mathcal{U}
is associated with an ordered sequence of items
s_{u}=[i_{u,1},i_{u,2},\ldots,i_{u,|s_{u}|}]
, where
i_{u,t}\in\mathcal{I}
indicates the
t
-th item that user
u
has interacted with. Based on the observed sequence
s_{u}
, the sequential recommendation task aims to predict the next item
i_{u,|s_{u}|+1}
that user
u
is likely to interact with, which can be formalized as:

(1)

\underset{i\in\mathcal{I}}{\operatorname{argmax}}\ p(i_{u,|s_{u}|+1}=i\mid s_{u}).

2.2.
Ensemble-based Sequential Recommender

Ensemble learning is a well-established machine learning technique that combines the outputs of multiple networks to enhance prediction accuracy and generalization performance
(
3
;
14
;
9
)
.
In the context of sequential recommendation, ensemble methods can be applied to capture diverse user behavior patterns, thereby enhancing top-k recommendation accuracy.
Concretely, a typical ensemble-based sequential recommender constructs
N
separate networks and forwards each user sequence
s_{u}
through all of them in parallel, as illustrated in
Figure 2
a. This results in a set of representations
\{\tilde{h}_{u}^{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, where
n
indexes each network. The model is then trained using a composite objective function:

(2)

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\left(\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\mathcal{L}_{rec}\left(u;\tilde{h}_{u}^{n}\right)+\mathcal{L}_{mkt}\left(u;\tilde{h}_{u}^{1},\tilde{h}_{u}^{2},\ldots,\tilde{h}_{u}^{N}\right)\right).

Here,
\mathcal{L}_{rec}
ensures that each network individually learns the sequential recommendation task, while
\mathcal{L}_{mkt}
facilitates mutual knowledge transfer among networks through pairwise objectives:

(3)

\mathcal{L}_{mkt}=\sum_{1\leq n_{1},n_{2}\leq N,n_{1}\neq n_{2}}\mathcal{L}_{pair}(\tilde{h}_{u}^{n_{1}},\tilde{h}_{u}^{n_{2}}).

Recent ensemble-based approaches adopt this training paradigm.
For instance, EMKD
(
11
)
constructs an ensemble of BERT4Recs
(
42
)
and optimizes them using knowledge distillation.
SEM
(
12
)
utilizes multiple networks for diversity-aware ensemble learning.
FamouSRec
(
54
)
leverages heterogeneous architectural networks to specialize in different frequency patterns by aligning them through contrastive learning.
It is worth noting that while ensembling networks is also explored in click-through rate prediction task
(
59
;
53
;
28
)
, the objective there is to handle diverse external features, which is distinct from our goal of capturing varied patterns within ID-based user sequences.
Despite of their effectiveness, conventional ensemble-based sequential recommenders require explicitly constructing multiple networks to ensure diversity, incurring several times the computational cost.
Furthermore, mutual knowledge transfer among randomly initialized networks–without any prior knowledge–can lead to unstable training and ultimately result in sub-optimal performance.

Backbone Network

Motivated by the capability of Transformer to model intricate sequential patterns

(
20
)
as the backbone for each network.
A wide range of recent studies
(

(

The backbone SASRec is composed of two primary components: an embedding module and a sequence encoding module.

2.3.1.
Embedding Module

The network maintains two learnable embedding tables: an item embedding matrix
I\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}|\times d}
and a position embedding matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
, where
T
is the maximum sequence length. For a given sequence
s_{u}
, its length is first adjusted to
T
by either truncating to the most recent
T
items or applying zero padding at the beginning. The sequence embedding
e_{u}
is then constructed as follows:

(4)

e_{u}=[I_{i_{u,1}}+P_{1},I_{i_{u,2}}+P_{2},\ldots,I_{i_{u,T}}+P_{T}]\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d},

where
I_{i_{u,t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
P_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
correspond to the embeddings of item
i_{u,t}
and position
t
, respectively.

2.3.2.
Sequence Encoding Module

The resulting embedding
e_{u}
is then processed by a
L
-layer multi-head Transformer encoder whose
l
-th layer is denoted as
\text{Trm}^{l}(\cdot)
. With
H_{u}^{0}=e_{u}
, each layer yields:

(5)

H_{u}^{l}=[h_{u,1}^{l},h_{u,2}^{l},\ldots,h_{u,T}^{l}]=\text{Trm}^{l}(H_{u}^{l-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}.

Here, the hidden representation corresponding to the final position in the output of the last layer, denoted as
h_{u,T}^{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is utilized as the final representation of the user sequence
s_{u}
.
For brevity, we denote the final representation of user
u
as
h_{u}
hereafter.

Before detailing the proposed FLAME framework, we first elaborate on its two foundational designs–
modular ensemble
and
guided mutual learning
–which are designed to mitigate the computational inefficiency and training instability inherent in conventional ensemble approaches.

3.1.
Modular Ensemble

Modular ensemble, depicted in
Figure 2
b, addresses the computational inefficiency of conventional ensemble methods while preserving, or even enhancing, representational diversity.
Unlike conventional ensemble, which generates representations by forwarding the input through an entire network as a whole, modular ensemble flexibly chooses the sub-module to use at each stage from any of the networks.
This design allows for a combinatorial expansion of representation paths without increasing the number of complete networks.
For example, with three networks each split into three sub-modules, the modular ensemble produces
3^{3}=27
unique representations, compared to only 3 in a conventional setup.
Conversely, even a small number of networks (e.g., two networks with three sub-modules each) can simulate the effect of an 8-network conventional ensemble through
2^{3}=8
modular combinations, significantly improving efficiency.

3.2.
Guided Mutual Learning

Another critical challenge of conventional ensemble training, as observed in
Figure 1
, is optimization instability, presumably stemming from noisy supervision exchanged among randomly initialized networks.
This issue can be alleviated by pretraining and freezing some of the networks in ensemble.
The frozen networks, imbued with prior knowledge, serves as a stable reference during training, guiding the rest learnable networks toward faster and more reliable convergence.
In addition to stabilizing, this setup also improves the standalone quality of the learnable networks through mutual enhancement between networks
(
55
;
23
;
51
)
.
This strategy integrates particularly well with modular ensemble, where fine-grained knowledge exchange occurs structurally at the sub-module level.

3.3.
Empirical Evidence

To motivate the integration of the two core designs into FLAME framework, we empirically examine their effectiveness in the context of ensemble learning.
For an ensemble to be effective, it must satisfy two critical conditions:
diversity
among representations and
reliability
of each individual network
(
9
;
58
)
.
From this perspective, we evaluate how the introduced designs–modular ensemble and guided mutual learning–are specifically tailored to achieve diversity and reliability, respectively, and thus serve as a solid foundation for an ensemble recommender.

Diversity.

Figure 3
a visualizes the distribution of sequence representations generated by two distinct SASRec-based ensemble strategies: 1) a 2-network conventional ensemble and 2) a 2-network modular ensemble, where each network is decomposed into two sub-modules.
While both methods produce separated representations, modular ensemble doubles the output diversity (four cluster types versus two) for the same number of networks.
Specifically, because different sub-modules within a single network capture distinct patterns, their combinations enable the creation of an exponentially diverse set of representations.
Consequently, modular ensemble expands its representational coverage to include the diverse user behaviors (i.e., the yellow and green clusters) entirely missed by conventional ensemble.
This capacity for capturing the multifaceted nature of user interests is critical for advancing sequential recommendation performance.

Reliability.

Figure 3

The learnable network in Ensemble-Guide achieves the highest performance among all four networks, indicating the guidance from the frozen network during training enhances the reliability of individual network.
It is also noteworthy that the two networks in Ensemble-Scratch both underperform compared to the frozen network in Ensemble-Guide (or equivalently, a single SASRec), suggesting that noisy mutual supervision among networks in the early stages of training can lead to sub-optimal convergence.

Put it together.

Both methods produce four representations, and we evaluate their diversity and reliability via the pairwise exclusive-hit ratio (PER)

4.1.
Ensemble Construction

4.1.1.
Network and Sub-Module Configuration

FLAME constructs a modular ensemble of
N=2
networks: a pretrained
Frozen
network, denoted as
f^{frz}(\cdot)
, and a
Learnable
network,
f^{lrn}(\cdot)
, which is trained from scratch.
To encourage diversity, each network is further decomposed into
M
sub-modules, with
f^{frz}_{(i)}(\cdot)
and
f^{lrn}_{(i)}(\cdot)
denoting the
i
-th sub-modules of the
Frozen
and
Learnable
networks.
For given user sequence
s_{u}
, it is processed sequentially through the sub-modules from the first to the
M
-th, with the final sub-module output serving as the resulting representation:

(6)

\displaystyle f^{frz}(s_{u})

\displaystyle=\left(f^{frz}_{(M)}\circ f^{frz}_{(M-1)}\circ\ldots\circ f^{frz}_{(1)}\right)(s_{u})=h_{u}^{frz},

\displaystyle f^{lrn}(s_{u})

\displaystyle=\left(f^{lrn}_{(M)}\circ f^{lrn}_{(M-1)}\circ\ldots\circ f^{lrn}_{(1)}\right)(s_{u})=h_{u}^{lrn}.

Instantiation.

In our implementation, we partition each network into
M=2
sub-modules: an embedding module and a sequence encoding module.
While increasing
M
could bring higher diversity, we empirically obtain satisfactory results with
M=2
.
Further investigation of finer-grained sub-module decompositions (i.e., larger
M
) is discussed in Section
5.4
.

4.1.2.
Sub-Module Combination

FLAME constructs a rich representation space from only two base networks by creating diverse sub-module combinations, each capturing a unique user behavioral pattern.
This is achieved by deciding at each of the
M
sub-module stages whether to use the component from the
Frozen
or
Learnable
network, resulting in
2^{M}
distinct computational decision paths.
Formally, each representation is defined as:

(7)

\left(f^{p_{M}}_{(M)}\circ f^{p_{M-1}}_{(M-1)}\circ\ldots\circ f^{p_{1}}_{(1)}\right)(s_{u})=\tilde{h}_{u}^{p},

where
p=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{M})\in\mathcal{P}=\{frz,lrn\}^{M}
specifies the decision path across sub-module stages.
Each
p_{i}\in\{frz,lrn\}
indicates whether the
i
-th sub-module is taken from the
Frozen
(
frz
) or
Learnable
(
lrn
) network.
Thus, each sequence representation
\tilde{h}_{u}^{p}
is the result of a unique path through modular ensemble.

4.2.
Mutual Knowledge Transfer

FLAME aligns the numerous representations from its sub-module combinations into a unified semantic space for guided mutual learning.
This alignment serves two purposes: improving the reliability of learned representations
(
26
;
13
)
and leveraging the
Frozen
network to provide stable guidance during training
(
1
)
.

To achieve this, we adopt a pairwise contrastive learning objective where representations derived from the same user sequence are considered as positives, while those generated from different users in the same mini-batch act as negatives
(
52
;
38
)
.
Concretely, the alignment procedure is formulated as:

(8)

\mathcal{L}_{cl}(u)=\sum_{\{p,q\}\subset\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{L}_{nce}(\tilde{h}^{p}_{u},\tilde{h}^{q}_{u})

and a noise contrastive estimation objective
(
32
;
29
)
for each pair can be computed as follows:

(9)

\mathcal{L}_{nce}(\tilde{h}^{p}_{u},\tilde{h}^{q}_{u})=-\log\frac{\exp(\tilde{h}^{p}_{u}\cdot\tilde{h}^{q}_{u}/\tau)}{\sum_{s_{u^{\prime}}\in B}\exp(\tilde{h}^{p}_{u}\cdot\tilde{h}^{q}_{u^{\prime}}/\tau)},

where
\tau
is the temperature parameter, and
B
is the mini-batch of sequences.

Similarity-based Weighting.

The
2^{M}
modular representations are not independent, as pairs sharing more sub-modules are inherently more similar due to their overlapping computational paths.
Consequently, a standard contrastive objective that treats all pairs as equally informative becomes inefficient, with the training loss being disproportionately dominated by these structurally similar pairs.

To mitigate this imbalance, we modify
\mathcal{L}_{cl}
to dynamically adjust the weight assigned to each pair based on their similarity:

(10)

\mathcal{L}_{mkt}(u)=\sum_{\{p,q\}\subset\mathcal{P}}w_{p,q}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{nce}(\tilde{h}^{p}_{u},\tilde{h}^{q}_{u})

and the similarity-based weight between each pair can then be formulated as follows:

(11)

\displaystyle sim_{p,q}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{s_{u}\in B}\tilde{h}^{p}_{u}\cdot\tilde{h}^{q}_{u},

\displaystyle w_{p,q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(-sim_{p,q})}{\sum_{\{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\}\subset\mathcal{P}}\exp(-sim_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}})}.

Specifically,
\mathcal{L}_{mkt}
down-weights pairs that are more similar, preventing them from dominating the overall objective and thereby promoting more balanced optimization.

4.3.
Optimization

To endow the
Learnable
network with the ability to directly perform sequential recommendation, we incorporate a next-item prediction objective into the training process of FLAME:

(12)

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{u}

\displaystyle=\text{Softmax}(h_{u}^{lrn}(I^{lrn})^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}|},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{rec}(u)

\displaystyle=-\log\hat{y}_{u}[g_{u}],

where
g_{u}
denotes the ground-truth item index for a user
u
.
3
3

3

The
Frozen
network is pretrained using the same objective as in
Equation 12
with
h_{u}^{lrn}
and

h_{u}^{frz}

In parallel, the contrastive alignment objective enhances the reliability and diversity of this representation.
To integrate these two complementary learning signals, we jointly optimize
\mathcal{L}_{rec}
and
\mathcal{L}_{mkt}
under a multi-task training strategy:

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{rec}(u)+\lambda\cdot\mathcal{L}_{mkt}(u)\right),

where
\lambda
controls the intensity of
\mathcal{L}_{mkt}
.

Coefficient Annealing.

To prevent the
Learnable
network from overfitting to the representation space shaped by the
Frozen
network, we gradually anneal the weighting coefficient
\lambda
as training progresses:

(14)

\lambda(r)=\lambda_{0}\left(\frac{\lambda_{R}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)^{\frac{r}{R}},

where
\lambda(r)
denotes the coefficient of contrastive objective at epoch
r
, annealed from
\lambda_{0}
and to
\lambda_{R}
over
R
total training epochs.
This scheduling allows the
Learnable
network to benefit from the guidance of contrastive alignment in early phase, while gradually shifting focus toward improving its own task-specific representation quality.

5.1.4.
Implementation

FLAME and all baselines are implemented using PyTorch.
For fair comparison, we perform thorough hyperparameter tuning for each baseline based on its original paper.
For EMKD, we construct an ensemble of four BERT4Rec networks.
For DHEN, which ensembles two heterogeneous networks from three architectural options (i.e., self-attention, MLP and CNN), we empirically find that the self-attention and MLP pairing is the most effective combination.
For FamouSRec, the model integrates four heterogeneous architectures, including self-attention, GRU, MLP and Mamba-based networks.
In FLAME, the Transformer encoder consists of
L=2

The embedding dimension

As described in Section

For the contrastive alignment loss, the temperature

5.2.
Performance Comparison (RQ1)

To assess the effectiveness of FLAME in sequential recommendation, we conduct extensive experiments on six datasets, whose results are summarized in
Table 2
.
Across all datasets and evaluation metrics, FLAME consistently outperforms all baselines, achieving relative improvements ranging from 4.07% to 10.57%.
Beyond this strong performance, we highlight three key observations that provide deeper insight into the comparative performance:

•

General sequential recommenders exhibit overall inferior performance, suggesting that a single network struggles to capture the diverse user behavior patterns and item characteristics in sequential recommendation data.

•

Contrastive learning models outperform general baselines, indicating the effectiveness of contrastive objectives and supporting the validity of FLAME’s representation alignment strategy.
Despite their improvements, however, these methods still operate within a single-network paradigm, limiting their ability to model representation diversity.

•

Ensembles like FamouSRec use multiple networks to achieve strong performance through diversity, but often suffer from training instability caused by noisy mutual supervision (see Section
5.6
).
In contrast, FLAME addresses this issue by incorporating a pretrained and frozen network into modular ensemble design, stabilizing training while preserving diversity.

6.
Conclusion

We present FLAME, a novel framework achieving the benefits of ensemble learning–diversity and reliability–without the associated high costs.
FLAME utilizes just two networks, one frozen and one learnable, to efficiently generate diverse representations via sub-module combinations, while the frozen network provides stable guidance.
Crucially, this design condenses ensemble power into a single learnable network, ensuring zero-overhead inference suitable for real-time applications.
Consequently, FLAME trains faster and more stably while achieving stronger performance, suggesting that scalable and effective ensemble learning is attainable without typical trade-offs.
Future work could investigate elastic inference mechanisms that dynamically activate ensemble paths for high-uncertainty users to further optimize the accuracy-efficiency trade-off.
