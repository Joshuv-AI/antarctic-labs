GenOT: generative optimal transport enables spatiotemporal interpolation and generation in cross-platform spatial transcriptomics
Authors: Rui Wang, Xinxin Liu, Linlin Zhuo, Zhecheng Zhou, Junlin Xu, Quan Zou, Xiangzheng Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spatial transcriptomics technologies have revolutionized the analysis of spatial gene expression, yet integrating spatial information and generating data across heterogeneous samples remain challenging. We present GenOT, a generative framework combining multi-scale graph self-supervised contrastive learning with optimal transport barycenter theory for efficient cross-slice and cross-platform spatiotemporal interpolation. The core innovation of GenOT lies in introducing an optimal transport barycenter–based interpolation algorithm, which mathematically models spatial distribution differences across heterogeneous samples to reconstruct spatiotemporal gene expression dynamics. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that GenOT consistently outperforms existing approaches in spatial domain identification, cross-platform interpolation, and developmental trajectory reconstruction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-026-04166-z.

Background

In multicellular organisms, cells with similar characteristics physically aggregate to form functional units with well-defined spatial architectures. Integrating gene expression profiles with spatial localization information provides new insights into the intrinsic regulatory mechanisms within tissues and opens new avenues for elucidating microenvironmental heterogeneity under pathological conditions [1–3]. In recent years, advances in spatial transcriptomics (ST) technologies have enabled researchers to simultaneously obtain both gene expression profiles and precise spatial coordinates of individual cells within tissue sections, thereby offering powerful tools to decipher the spatial organization of cellular functions and their dynamic changes during development and disease [4]. The typical evolution of spatial transcriptomics technologies is characterized by continuous improvement in spatial resolution, ranging from 10 × Genomics Visium (55 µm spot resolution, capturing approximately 1–10 cells per spot) [5], to Slide-seq (~ 10 µm, near-cellular resolution) [6], and finally to Stereo-seq (0.22 µm, subcellular resolution) [7]. ST analysis typically involves two fundamental tasks: (1) spatial domain identification [8], which integrates spatial locations and gene expression profiles to delineate biologically meaningful tissue regions; and (2) data imputation and multimodal/cross-technology integration [9], which aims to enhance expression resolution and integrate multi-omic information.

The early mainstream domain identification methods were primarily rooted in unsupervised algorithms like K-means and Louvain [10]. These methods rely on gene expression similarity while neglecting spatial proximity constraints. This strategy often leads to fragmented spatial domain boundaries that fail to accurately reflect coherent functional unit structures in tissues. To overcome this limitation, recent studies have proposed a new multimodal integration paradigm that jointly leverages gene expression and spatial neighborhood information. For example, Bayesian statistical approaches, such as BayesSpace [11], optimize clustering by incorporating spatial neighborhood information, whereas BASS [12] employs a hierarchical Bayesian model to simultaneously identify spatial domains and cell type clusters across tissue sections. Further, deep learning methods based on graph neural networks (GNNs) have rapidly emerged in ST analysis [13, 14]. SCAN-IT [15] performs spatial clustering via image segmentation technology; SpaGCN [16] integrates gene expression, spatial coordinates, and histology images through graph convolutional networks for precise domain identification; STAGATE [17] converts spatial information into neighborhood networks and trains graph attention autoencoders for clustering; SEDR [18] employs multiple variant autoencoders for multimodal data processing. Additionally, emerging approaches have explored self-supervised learning with unlabeled data to address the limitations of existing methods in multi-omics feature integration. GraphST [19] performs contrastive training between original and perturbed graph representations; ConST [20] introduces a two-stage contrastive learning framework with multi-level comparisons; and ConGI [21] integrates histological images and gene expression using three types of contrastive losses.

Although existing approaches have achieved remarkable progress in ST data imputation and generation tasks [22–24], most remain confined to single-slice analyses and lack effective mechanisms to handle cross-slice heterogeneity [25], including nonlinear deformation, resolution discrepancies, and platform-specific variations. The absence of alignment in the latent embedding space across samples further hinders the analysis of spatio-temporal developmental dynamics. More importantly, the field still lacks a computational framework capable of generating or interpolating biologically meaningful spatiotemporal transcriptomic data that represent intermediate states within dynamic processes. This gap prevents fine-grained computational inference of developmental and disease progression trajectories from a spatiotemporal perspective. Fortunately, optimal transport (OT) theory has demonstrated unique advantages in bioinformatics by quantifying transformation costs between probability distributions [26–28], providing feasible and efficient mathematical frameworks for multi-omics integration [28]. Current applications of OT in transcriptomics include: (1) cross-platform batch effect correction [29]; (2) cellular trajectory reconstruction [30]; and (3) spatial annotation of scRNA-seq data [31]. Notably, recent studies have extended OT to spatiotemporal modeling (MOSCOT [9]) and tissue reconstruction in spatial transcriptomics, including platform alignment (PASTE2 [32]) and cell–cell communication analysis (COMMOT [33]). These advances motivate us to further apply the OT technology to cross-slice imputation analysis.

Therefore we developed GenOT, a cross-platform and cross-slice spatiotemporal data interpolation and generation framework to alleviate data scarcity in studies of developmental processes and disease progression. The power of GenOT stems from the synergistic design of its two core modules. Firstly, GenOT designed a self-supervised spatial transcriptomics learning framework that fuses gene expression and spatial information through a multi-scale graph convolutional network with an encoder-shared decoder structure, combined with contrastive learning to enhance the quality of gene expression embeddings. The results of domain identification validated the high quality of the embeddings generated by this module, laying a solid foundation for subsequent cross-slice spatiotemporal interpolation. Secondly, GenOT designed a generative strategy based on OT barycenter technology, which enables spatiotemporal imputation of spatial transcriptomics data across slices/platforms. GenOT follows an “encode-generate” paradigm, excelling not only in traditional spatial domain identification tasks but also demonstrating cross-platform and cross-slice generative capabilities. Experimental results show that GenOT effectively handles complex tissue heterogeneity, resolution discrepancies, and inter-platform variations. More importantly, by constructing a computable densely-sampled spatiotemporal trajectory, GenOT provides a powerful new paradigm for uncovering transient biological events hidden within static data snapshots, offering insights into dynamic biological processes.

Results

GenOT framework

GenOT consists of five core components (Fig. 1): (a) multi-scale graph convolutional network (MGCN), (b) shared latent-space alignment, (c) barycenter construction with cost-matrix computation, (d) OT-based generation and interpolation, and (e) downstream analysis. In module (a), MGCN constructs a k-nearest-neighbor (KNN) [34] graph from spatial coordinates after gene selection and extracts multi-scale embeddings using a multi-scale feature aggregation (MFA) module and GCN encoder. It is then optimized using self-supervised contrastive learning combined with a reconstruction loss. In module (b), embeddings from heterogeneous datasets are projected into a unified latent space through alternately iterative training of a shared decoder, enabling preliminary cross-sample alignment. In module (c), embedding and spatial barycenters are computed with the FGW framework, followed by construction of a transport plan and cost matrix; minimizing this cost yields a consistent OT plan. In module (d), gene expression is decoded from the embedding barycenter using the shared decoder. When target coordinates are available, an OT mapping step projects the generated expression onto the reference spatial layout, while the barycenters and cost matrix guide the computation of the OT plan; integrating decoded expression with this plan yields the final interpolation output, which can be compared with raw data. GenOT supports two distinct generative modes: coordinate-guided interpolation and coordinate-free generation. When target coordinates are available, GenOT maps the generated expression profiles onto the reference spatial layout (Eqs. 23– 24). Spatial domain identification analysis on human DLPFC (10 × Visium) and mouse embryo (Stereo-seq) (RQ1)

This study evaluated the performance of the spatial transcriptomics self-supervised learning framework MGCN (denoted as GenOT in Fig. 2) on the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) [35] and mouse embryo datasets [7]. The DLPFC dataset contains 12 spatial transcriptomics slices covering 4 to 6 cortical layers and adjacent white matter, and the mouse embryo dataset contains Stereo-seq data spanning developmental stages E9.5 to E12.5. The analysis comprised three components, including a comparison with state-of-the-art methods on both datasets as shown in Table 1 and Fig. 2a, c, and d; an ablation experiment assessing the contribution of each key component in the MGCN framework, illustrated in the four rightmost columns of Fig. 2a; and an evaluation of the effectiveness of co-training across the two datasets as depicted in Fig. 2b. Component-wise contribution analysis of MGCN

Figure 2a shows the results of MGCN and its ablation variants on DLPFC slices 151673–151675, where GenOT denotes the complete framework and the w/o MFA, w/o SCL, w/o PCA, and w/o REG variants remove the multiscale aggregation, self-supervised contrastive learning, PCA-based gene selection, and reconstruction loss modules, respectively. Removing MFA weakens the integration of multiscale spatial information and markedly reduces clustering accuracy, underscoring its role in generating discriminative spatial features. Excluding SCL diminishes the robustness of latent representations and impairs the encoding of fine-grained spatial patterns, leading to degraded clustering quality and visible layer mixing; notably, even without the reconstruction loss, the model still learns informative representations when SCL is retained, highlighting SCL as an essential component for maintaining spatial coherence and clear cell boundaries. Omitting PCA forces the model to learn from noisy high-dimensional gene expression data, which interferes with feature extraction and further deteriorates clustering outcomes, demonstrating the necessity of appropriate dimensionality reduction. Together, these results show that MFA, SCL, PCA, and REG contribute complementary functions that enable MGCN to extract biologically meaningful tissue structures from complex spatial transcriptomics data.

Coordinate-free slice sequence data generation

Constructing a complete spatiotemporal developmental trajectory remains one of the major challenges in modern biology, as the high cost of data acquisition leads to limited availability of developmental-stage data. The GenOT generation framework introduces a interpolation parameter λλ (see Eqs. 15 and 16), enabling precise and controllable reconstruction of key intermediate developmental states, thereby helping to alleviate this limitation. Taking two different slices of an E11.5 mouse embryo (E11.5S1, E11.5S2) as an example, we successfully constructed seamless transcriptomic intermediates between them (see Fig. 5c). Quantitative analysis shows that the value of λλ is directly related to the conservation of transcriptomic features: when λ>0.5λ>0.5, the interpolation result maintains high transcriptomic fidelity with the donor slice; whereas as λ approaches 0, the influence of the recipient slice on the spatial layout increases substantially. It is important to note that barycenter-based interpolation can produce expression patterns that do not correspond to any real biological state. For example, when interpolating between two E11.5 embryo slices, we observed apparent Otx2 expression in the posterior region—a pattern absent from both source slices. Since Otx2 is a well-characterized transcription factor with expression predominantly restricted to the anterior neural region at this developmental stage, this observation most likely reflects an artifact of the balanced OT framework, which enforces complete mass redistribution between source distributions. Such artifacts arise when individual anatomical variation between source slices creates imperfect spatial correspondences, causing the barycenter to redistribute expression into biologically implausible regions. This example underscores that novel expression patterns identified through computational interpolation should be treated as hypotheses requiring independent experimental validation.

Furthermore, Leiden clustering was performed on the PCA-processed gene expression matrices of the intermediate slices generated by GenOT between E11.5S1 and E11.5S2 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda \in \left[0.1, 0.9\right]$$\end{document}λ∈0.1,0.9) as well as on the original slices E11.5S1 and E11.5S2 to evaluate the validity and biological plausibility of the GenOT-generated data (see Fig. 5d). The results also showed that as λλ decreased, the data structure of the interpolated slices gradually approached that of the donor slice E11.5S2, whereas increasing λλ made it closer to E11.5S1. At λ =0.5λ=0.5, the interpolated slice effectively integrated information from both donors. As a novel generative framework, GenOT enables the construction of intermediate data states, offering a new perspective for exploring dynamic cellular and genetic changes during development.

Discussion

The analysis of dynamic biological processes from spatial transcriptomics data is fundamentally limited by the discrete nature of data acquisition. Existing computational tools excel at analyzing individual time points or tissue sections, but lack a robust framework for modeling the transitions between them. To fill this methodological gap, we have developed GenOT, a computational framework designed for reconstructing and generating spatiotemporal trajectories. GenOT’s architecture uniquely integrates two synergistic modules: a graph self-supervised contrastive learning framework for generating high-fidelity latent representations of individual samples; and a generative module that utilizes OT barycenter theory to compute biologically meaningful intermediate states between these samples.

This study comprehensively evaluated the feature extraction capability of GenOT across multiple benchmark datasets, including the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), developing mouse embryos, and mouse brain tissues, integrating data from several mainstream platforms (10 × Visium, Slide-seqV2, and Stereo-seq). In standard spatial domain identification tasks, GenOT consistently outperformed state-of-the-art methods. Its superior clustering performance confirms the model’s effectiveness in capturing both transcriptional signatures and spatial neighborhood information, enabling the generation of robust embeddings crucial for reliable downstream spatiotemporal interpolation. Furthermore, we assessed GenOT’s core cross-slice and cross-platform interpolation capability. By applying OT barycenter theory to the learned embeddings, GenOT precisely interpolates intermediate slices, even across distant temporal stages or heterogeneous platforms. Quantitative evaluations revealed strong correlations and structural similarity between interpolated and reference samples. Comparison (Additional file 1: Note 1; Additional file 1: Tables S3 and S4; and Additional file 1: Figs. S10 and S11) against four baseline methods—including spatial interpolation approaches (Linear Average, Inverse Distance Weighting) and standard OT methods (Wasserstein Barycenter, OT Displacement Interpolation)—confirmed that GenOT’s FGW-based formulation provides measurable advantages, with improvements scaling with task difficulty: modest gains in same-stage interpolation and more pronounced advantages in cross-stage settings where spatial expression patterns differ substantially between references. Importantly, this interpolation capability enables the computational reconstruction of intermediate developmental states that are not directly observed experimentally. However, we caution that interpolated expression patterns should be interpreted as computational hypotheses rather than definitive biological findings, as the balanced OT framework may produce artifacts when source slices exhibit substantial anatomical variation (see the Otx2 case study in the “ De novo spatiotemporal generation analysis (RQ4)” section).

Several limitations of the current framework should be noted. First, the balanced FGW formulation enforces mass conservation, which may produce biologically implausible intermediate states when interpolating between samples with substantially different cell compositions or anatomical structures. Future work will explore unbalanced FGW formulations to address this constraint (The analysis related to unbalanced FGW barycenters is provided in Additional file 1: Note 2 and Additional file 1: Fig. S12). Second, the FGW barycenter is solved independently for each value of the interpolation parameter λλ, without formal guarantees of smooth variation across λλ values; our empirical analysis (Additional file 1: Fig. S13) indicates that outputs are approximately smooth in practice, but formal continuity cannot be guaranteed. Third, the use of PCA-based embedding constrains the barycenter to approximate linear combinations of source expression profiles, resulting in some dynamic range compression in generated data; future versions could incorporate nonlinear embedding methods such as variational autoencoders to address this limitation. Fourth, our failure mode analysis (Additional file 1: Note 3 and Additional file 1: Fig. S14) suggests that interpolation is less reliable for genes with weak spatial structure, low coefficient of variation, low spatial autocorrelation, and broadly uniform expression patterns. We recommend that users compute inter-reference spatial concordance as a pre-screening metric to identify genes at risk of interpolation failure.

Conclusions

In summary, GenOT provides a generalizable framework for the integrated analysis of spatiotemporal transcriptomic data. It not only establishes new performance benchmarks in spatial clustering but also introduces a necessary generative modeling paradigm for characterizing tissue dynamics. This framework enables the connection of sparsely sampled temporal slices, the harmonization of heterogeneous datasets across platforms within a shared embedding space, and the computational simulation of developmental and pathological processes. The fundamental principles of GenOT can be extended to three-dimensional reconstructions and integrated with multimodal data such as histopathology, proteomics, and epigenomics to support causal spatiotemporal inference. Future work will focus on enhancing its constrained and counterfactual generative capabilities for modeling disease progression and therapeutic intervention, as well as developing standardized benchmarks and open-source toolchains to promote reproducible applications and translational research in developmental and clinical biology.

Methods

This research is based on the ST generation framework GenOT, which utilizes multi-scale graph self-supervised contrastive learning and OT barycenter technology for efficient cross-slice/cross-platform spatio-temporal imputation. Compared to other methods, GenOT mainly innovates in two aspects for the imputation of spatial transcriptomics data. GenOT proposes a spatial transcriptomics self-supervised learning framework for integrating gene expression and spatial information, which includes a multi-scale graph convolutional network based on an encoder-shared decoder architecture, and a self-supervised contrastive learning module. The former fuses gene expression with multi-scale spatial information to mitigate the issue of inconsistent spatial density and guarantees that subsequent generation occurs in a unified space. The latter captures local spatial features to enhance the quality of gene expression representation. GenOT designs a generation strategy based on OT barycenter technology, which can achieve cross-slice/cross-platform spatio-temporal imputation. Therefore, GenOT is expected to play a key role as a significant technology in the analysis of tissue structures and the modeling of developmental processes at single-cell resolution. The following will detail these techniques.

Self-supervised graph learning for spatial transcriptomics

This study introduces a self-supervised graph learning framework for spatial transcriptomics that integrates spatial coordinates with gene expression data. The framework comprises three components: a multiscale graph convolutional encoder, a self-supervised contrastive learning module, and a shared decoder. The multiscale encoder fuses gene expression with spatial information at different scales to mitigate spatial density variation, the contrastive module enhances local spatial feature learning to improve expression representations, and the shared decoder ensures that all generated outputs are mapped into a consistent space structure.

Cross-sample generation based on OT barycenter technique

This study introduces a cross-sample generation strategy grounded in OT barycenter theory to achieve spatiotemporal interpolation of spatial transcriptomics data. Given that spatial transcriptomic profiles can vary markedly across samples due to differences in experimental conditions or sampling time points, integration and interpolation remain challenging. To address this issue, we construct a cross-sample generative model by solving the FGW barycenter problem, jointly estimating the barycenter of gene expression embeddings and spatial coordinates to maintain continuity and consistency across datasets. We further refine the alignment by incorporating an OT plan and adjusting the barycenter using a cost matrix derived from optimal matching principles. Together, these components enable high-fidelity data generation at arbitrary spatiotemporal locations and provide a powerful computational tool for developmental biology research.

Alignment of spatial and embedding barycenters

The distance matrix computed directly from the barycenter coordinates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{S}}_{b}$$\end{document}Sb shows high similarity to the generated structural matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{{\boldsymbol{S}}}$$\end{document}CbS, confirming the validity of the resulting barycenter (see Additional file 1: Fig. S18). However, because the spatial and embedding barycenters lie in different representational spaces, they cannot be directly integrated.
