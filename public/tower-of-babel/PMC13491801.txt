Factors influencing surgical residents attrition: a mixed-methods study from a low-middle-income country
Authors: Rabeet Tariq, Nadeem Ahmed Siddiqui, Saad Akhtar Khan, Marium Khan, Dua Siddiqui, Saleem Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePremature loss of residents prior to completion of training, is common among surgical specialties, negatively impacts other trainees and program leadership, and disrupts the continuity of care. Developing countries face various challanges which may impact this attrition, however, there is a lack of literature on the rate of resident attrition in low-middle-income countries (LMICs). We aimed to identify the rates and predictors of surgical residents’ attrition at a single institution.MethodologyWe conducted a mixed-methods observational study; single center retrospective review and prospective survey including quantitative and qualitative analyses of trainees who left prior to completion of training. Data regarding gender, marital status, locality, year of training, surgical specialty, and reasons for attrition were collected. The trainees were then contacted for a follow-up survey to assess their reasons as well as pathways after leaving, opinions about the program, program director, relationship with peers, and their satisfaction with the decision.ResultsBetween 2005 and 2024, 617 surgical residents were enrolled, of whom 51.0% (n=315) completed training, 30.5% (n=188) left before completion (attrition), and 18.5% were still in training. Gender and geographic background (local vs. non-local) were not associated; however, marital status significantly influenced attrition, with singles more likely to drop out (60.5% vs. 34.3% among graduates (p<0.001). Most departures occurred in the first year (54.3%; n=102) particularly in pediatric surgery (62.2%; n=23/37) and general surgery (44.1%; n=57/129). At exit interviews, the most common reasons included family commitments (34.2%), personal reasons (9.6%), and better job opportunities (9.5%). A follow-up survey of 59 former residents revealed that 61% (n=36) switched to another program in the same specialty, while 30.5% (n=18) changed specialties. Despite moderate satisfaction with training (3.07/5) and program leadership (3.14/5), respondents reported high satisfaction with their decision to leave (4.31/5). Main concerns included unprofessional behavior (30.5%), toxic work environments (20.3%), and excessive workload (16.9%).ConclusionSurgical resident attrition at our center was high, particularly in the early years of training and in certain specialties, with marital status emerging as a significant predictor. Efforts at reducing reasons for attrition are critical for the future. Keywords: Attrition, Medical training, Surgical training, Surgical residency

Introduction

Resident attrition or the premature loss of residents prior to completion of training, negatively impacts other trainees in terms of workload and morale as well as making it challenging for program leadership [1]. In 2021–2022, the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education in the United States reported that 2,366 residents within accredited residency programs failed to complete training [2]. This attrition can also affect patient care by reducing services and disrupting the continuity of care. Recent meta-analysis shows that the surgical programs are known to have higher rates of attrition with a pooled estimate of around 17–18% [3, 4]. This has been attributed to the intensity of the work involved, length of training, longer work hours, and also a perception of a more ‘toxic’ culture in the programs [3, 4].

Several factors have been studied as predictors of attrition among surgical residents. Gender has been widely reported as a factor, with women more likely to leave programs without completing training [5–9]. Residents in their initial two years of training have higher chances of leaving, suggesting a degree of regret with career choice or inadequate understanding of what the training constituted [6, 10–12]. Racial disparities are also noted among residents in the United States with Black/African American residents having a higher rate (6.2%), compared to white or Caucasian residents [11]. Academic predictors such as lower USMLE scores [13], courses repeated, “C” grades on assessments and lack of superlative comments in the dean’s letter [7] have also been reported to be predictors of attrition, as well as making it difficult to even enter surgical training in particular.

Most studies regarding resident attrition are from high income countries (HIC) such as those in North America where the induction and training processes, as well as the outcomes, are very different from the rest of the world. The Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) keeps records of rates of induction and attrition of residents in the United States along with their characteristics; however, most other countries lack central records. Developing countries face challenges that are not seen in the developed world such as lack of capital, no unified training guidelines, cultural differences, and resource-limited hospitals and settings; all of which may impact attrition. In addition to these, there is also the ever-present issue of ‘brain drain’ where students, residents, and even faculty migrate to HIC’s. There is also a lack of literature on the rates of overall attrition and across different specialties in low-middle income countries (LMICs).

This study aimed to identify the prevalence of attrition among surgical residents across all specialties from a single tertiary care hospital in Pakistan. We further wish to identify the factors associated with surgical residents’ attrition including gender, years of training, academic performance, and surgical specialty, so as to understand and then potentially institute mechanisms to reduce them leaving.

Methods

Review of the departmental records

A retrospective review was conducted with data from department of surgery records of the Aga Khan University Hospital, Karachi, Pakistan. The data from 2005 to 2024 was obtained and included variables such as gender, year of training, surgical specialty, and reason for attrition as indicated in the resignation form.

Survey of departed trainees

The individuals from the attrition group were then contacted via email followed by phone calls and were requested to fill out an online survey circulated via Google forms.

This survey was created after a focused discussion between surgeons, departmental manager, surgical educationists and other researchers. It included basic demographic details, details of the individual when they left residency (age, marital status, year of residency, etc.), reason(s) for leaving, and career after that; followed by Likert-scale questions to rate their satisfaction with the training program, program director, relationship with peers, and satisfaction with their decision to leave; and finally an open-ended question regarding their suggestions. The responses to the open-ended question were then assessed via thematic analysis.

Statistical analysis

The data was transferred to SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk NY, USA). For the descriptive analysis, frequencies and percentages were reported for gender, year of residency, surgical specialty, and the reasons described for attrition. The chi-squared test was used to assess the relationship between gender and attrition, as well as between surgical specialties and the outcome variable (attrition), whereas the Mann-Whitney U test was used to assess the difference between postgraduate medical education (PGME) test scores of residents who completed residency to those who left.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of the Aga Khan University Hospital (2024-10242-32518). The participants signed a consent form before filling out the survey and all identities were anonymized throughout to ensure the privacy and confidentiality of individuals involved.

Results

A total of 617 surgical residents were identified from 2005 to 2024. Out of these, 315 (51.0%) completed training, whereas 188 (30.5%) did not, with 114 individuals still under training (18.5%).

The number of residents graduating as well as leaving from 2005 to 2024 is shown in Fig. 1. Among the graduating group, 213 (67.6%) were male and 102 (32.4%) were female, whereas in the attrition group 113 were male (60.4%) and 74 (39.6%) were female, with no difference for gender between the groups (p = 0.103). Similarly, there was no significant difference in these groups when comparing locals to those who came from different cities/countries (p = 0.93). Marital status, however, was significantly associated with attrition (p < 0.001). It is worth noting that all individuals who left did not specify their marital status, hence this was known about 157 out of total 188 (83.5%) of the individuals from the attrition group. When comparing the marital status of the genders, almost half the females were single (n = 82, 49.1%) and almost half were married (n = 85,50.9%). However, more men were married (n = 184, 60.3%) than single (n = 121, 39.7%). The difference between the marital status of males and females was statistically significant (p = 0.048).

During the routine exit interviews, the most frequently described reasons for attrition were family commitments (n = 50; 34.2%), personal reasons (n = 14; 9.6%), better job prospects overseas or locally (n = 14; 9.5%), going for other further studies (n = 8; 5.5%), dissatisfaction with the work environment, and relocation (n = 6; 4.1%). A specialty‑specific analysis of exit interview reasons (n = 174 residents with documented reasons) showed significant variation across surgical disciplines (p = 0.020), however, results should be interpreted cautiously due to relatively small sample sizes in many subspecialties. Family commitments were the single most common reason in General Surgery (30.0%), Orthopaedic Surgery (35.0%), Otolaryngology (33.3%), and Cardiothoracic Surgery (42.9%). Personal reasons were high in Pediatric Surgery (22.7%) and Neurosurgery (18.2%). Dissatisfaction with work environment appeared exclusively in Orthopaedic Surgery (5.0%), Urology (16.7%), and Neurosurgery (9.1%). Better prospects (locally or overseas) were most frequently cited in General Surgery (18.0% combined) and were absent in Pediatric Surgery, Otolaryngology, and Urology. Otolaryngology had a notable rate of department‑recommended contract endings (22.2%), suggesting that some attrition in these specialties was non‑voluntary or related to pre‑matriculation factors.

The postgraduate exam scores were available from 2010 onwards. The mean score of graduating residents (54.62) was higher than that of the attrition group (51.02), however, this was not significant (p = 0.186).

Survey results

Among the trainees from the attrition group, 162 were reachable and were contacted, among whom 59 responded to our survey (Response rate: 36.4%). Among the respondents, 40 (67.8%) were males and 19 (32.2%) were females. The mean age was 28.47 ± 2.104. Most respondents were in the initial years when they left (first: n = 32, 54.2%, second, n = 10, 16.9%; third: n = 11, 18.6%; fourth: n = 4, 6.8%; and fifth: n = 2, 3.4%). Regarding marital status, 54.2% (n = 32) were married and 42.4% were single (one was engaged, one was divorced) when they left.

Regarding their pathway after leaving residency, 61.0% (n = 36) responded that they switched to another program for the same specialty, 30.5% (n = 18) switched specialty, 5.1% (n = 3) moved abroad, and 3.4% (n = 2) left medical practice (both switched to entrepreneurship). The participants were then given a Likert scale (1 being the lowest and 5 being the highest) to rate their satisfaction with the program, program director, peers, and their decision to leave. The mean score of satisfaction with the overall training program was 3.07 (± 1.35), and satisfaction with the program director was 3.14 (± 1.36). They rated their relationship with their peers at 3.78 (± 1.10). The respondents were overall satisfied with their decision, with the mean score of satisfaction being 4.31 (± 1.05). The findings from a thematic analysis of an open-ended question regarding comments and suggestions identified recurring themes. Unprofessionalism, bullying, and excessive workload imposed by seniors, consultants, or program directors emerged as the most prevalent issue, reported by 30.5% of respondents. A harsh or toxic work environment with unhealthy competition were each cited by 20.3% of respondents, while long working hours and high workload leading to burnout and disrupted work/life balance affected 16.9%. Some trainees believed that the training was inadequate, particularly the need for more hands-on experience (18.6%). Other concerns included a lack of resident/trainee-friendly practices, such as not considering their needs (15.3%), financial issues (13.6%), and technical challenges (8.5%).

Discussion

We conducted a comprehensive review of surgical resident attrition at a large tertiary-care hospital in Pakistan over a 19-year period, supplemented by a follow-up survey of former trainees. We found that nearly one-third (30.5%) of residents left training before completion, a rate higher than many reports from HIC’s. Attrition was most common in the early years of residency, particularly in certain specialties, and was significantly associated with single marital status. Gender or geographic background, however, were not significant issues. The primary reasons cited for leaving included family commitments, personal issues, and better job opportunities, while follow-up surveys highlighted unprofessional behavior, toxic work environments, and excessive workload as major concerns. Despite moderate satisfaction with training and program leadership, most respondents expressed high satisfaction with their decision to leave.

Attrition rate

The attrition rate of surgical specialties in our institution was very high (30.5%) compared to other reports from around the globe. Yeo et al. (from the US) reported the cumulative risk of resignation across all years, including research, to be 19.5% [6]. An attrition rate of 29% was reported in neurosurgery residents from the Middle East [14]. Yaghoubian et al. conducted a retrospective analysis of 348 general surgery residents in 6 programs in the USA, and reported that 15.8% (n = 55) of the residents left their programs, whereas 31% (n = 107) required remediation, of which 25.2% were remediated more than once [13]. An older study from our center reporting the attrition rate from 2005 to 2011 reported an attrition rate of 39% [15], however, when we assessed the data of 2005–2024, this seems to have reduced.

The attrition rate in this study varied significantly by subspecialty (p < 0.001), with pediatric surgery having the highest percentage (62.2%), followed by general surgery (44.1%), Otolaryngology / ENT (37.5%), and orthopedic surgery (37.1%). Given that the survey strongly suggested that there were issues with the work culture, these sections need to look at their systems and work to make them better. Work culture is a complex issue in the context of Pakistan as other programs in Karachi and elsewhere likely having shorter daily hours and total duration of training as well as allowing moonlighting type opportunities. Despite the high overall rate of attrition for the duration of this study, there was no trend of any increase or decline in the rate throughout the years, which was surprising with the changes in the economic condition of the country.

Factors associated with attrition

Contrary to our findings, several other studies have reported gender to be associated with attrition, with female surgical residents more likely to leave than males [5–8, 16]. Haruno et al. analyzed the trend in racial and gender differences in resident attrition among surgical specialties and reported an overall attrition rate of 6.9% and an unintended attrition rate of 2.3%, that included withdrawals, dismissals, and transfers except for changing career. Female residents had a significantly higher relative risk of attrition (RR = 1.16; p < 0.001) and unintended attrition (RR = 1.17; p< 0.001) [8]. Yeo H et al. also reported that female residents were no more likely to leave programs compared with their male counterparts (2.1% vs. 1.9%) [6]. Lynch G reported that in neurosurgical residents, females are significantly more at risk (p < 0.005) of not completing training [5]. Yeo HL et al. reported that following the fourth year of residency, higher attrition was encountered among women; however, this was not statistically significant (p = 0.5) [9]. Similarly, females were more likely to leave orthopedic training (p = 0.0018) [16]. A meta-analysis of 22 studies showed higher attrition rates among females in general surgery residency, however, similar to our finding, another random-effect meta-analysis on 26 studies found no statistically significant association of gender with attrition [17]. Additionally, an older study (2005–2011) from our center also reported females to have higher attrition rates [15], but in our assessment of the wider timespan, this seems to have changed. This may be because our data, spanning 19 years, is less homogenous and incorporates various surgical subspecialties; however, the demographics of our population are widely different from those previously published in the literature. It is also worth mentioning that this finding shed light on an important social bias towards female residents and trainees. While women make up the majority in Pakistani medical schools, they are not as widely represented in the medical workforce. This has various reasons, including social, organizational, and individual factors, tied together by social norms [18]. However, our data shows that once women start surgical residencies, they are not more likely to leave than men. This warrants further multi-center and larger studies exploring the recent trends in female trainees in competitive specialties.

Even though trainees who came from different cities/countries may have a harder time adjusting to a new setting, we found no significant association of attrition with locality (p = 0.93). Similar to several previous studies, we also found that most of the trainees in the attrition group left in the initial years of residency. Leibrandt TJ et al. circulated a questionnaire among program directors inquiring about the number and details of general surgery residents who left voluntarily in 2003. Most attrition was reported at first (47%) and second (28%) years of residency. Personal issues and work hours/lifestyle were cited as the most common reasons for leaving [10]. Yeo H et al. also found that the postgraduate year level was the only independent predictor of attrition in multivariate analysis [6]. Sullivan MC et al. showed that the first year of residency (9.4% risk vs. 4.5% risk for PGY-2, p < 0.001) was associated with higher attrition [12]. Salles A et al. also showed that most attrition in their program occurred during the first or second years of residency and the attrition group was also observed to be significantly older at the beginning of residency training(p < 0.001) [11]. This was also confirmed in a meta-analysis mentioning that most attrition occurs in the initial two years of residency (p < 0.001) [17].

Another important finding in our study was that marital status was significantly associated with attrition (p < 0.001). In the attrition group, 60.5% were single and 39.5% were married; whereas this trend was opposite in the graduating group, 34.3% were single and 65.7% were married. An important confounding factor to consider here is that most attrition occurs in the initial years, when the trainees are younger, and the graduating group has completed their 5–7 years of surgical training, and many get married during their residency. Nonetheless, unmarried individuals are more likely to take more career risks as marriage itself, and especially the birth of a first child temporarily reduces adults’ willingness to take risks, while divorce or separation (removing family obligations) increases risk-taking [19], as after marriage and children, people often become “more cautious and careful” in financial and career decisions [19].

Another important factor that could not be assessed in our study is grit and belonging. Burkhart RA et al. defined grit as ‘perseverance and passion for long-term goals’ and evaluated it as a marker and potential risk factor for resident attrition. They reported one-third of all general surgery residents had considered leaving surgical residency; however, those with below-median grit were more than twice as likely to consider leaving (p= .032). Further, the primary support strategies for residents included family, friends outside of residency, co-residents, and formal mentorship through their particular residency, exercise, social activities, and faith [20]. Salles A et al. reported that belonging was negatively correlated with risk of attrition as measured by frequency of thoughts of leaving residency (p < 0.0001) [11].

Causes of attrition

In our center, the most frequently described reasons for attrition during resignation were family commitments (n = 50; 34.2%), personal reasons (n = 14; 9.6%), and better job prospects overseas or locally (n = 14; 9.5%). On follow-up, thematic analysis of open-ended responses revealed several recurring issues, with 30.5% citing unprofessional behavior, bullying, and excessive workload as primary concerns. The work environment and unhealthy competition were each mentioned by 20.3% of respondents. Burnout due to long working hours and heavy workload was reported by 16.9%, while 18.6% expressed dissatisfaction with the adequacy of training, particularly the lack of hands-on experience.

In Pakistan, surgical trainees have reported high-stress scores, which were more prevalent in females (p = 0.008) as compared to their male counterparts. Work-life imbalance, workload, contradicting programs, and hospital policies were reported as major stressors during residency. As a result, 65% of surgical residents utilize self-distraction as a maladaptive strategy, respectively [21]. Furthermore, a study on workplace harassment reported that it was experienced by 54.4%(n = 80) trainees; however, only 11.3% (n = 9) reported it to the administration. Among the males, it was reported by 49%(n = 24) and among the females by 57%(n = 56), female gender to be significantly associated with sexual harassment (OR: 4.261 [95% confidence interval: 1.067–17.019]). Severe self-perceived burnout was reported by 69.4%(n = 102), and severe self-perceived depression by 46.9%(n = 69). Respondents experiencing bullying were more likely to report severe self-perceived burnout than those not experiencing bullying (p = 0.02) [22].

The mean satisfaction score for the overall training program and program directors was moderate, while peer relationships received a relatively positive rating. Respondents expressed high satisfaction with their decision to leave the program. Sullivan MC et al. conducted a prospective study determining individual and programmatic factors predictive of resident attrition. They reported that when compared with their colleagues, those in the attrition group were significantly less likely to report overall program satisfaction, to look forward to work, less likely to express satisfaction with their program’s operative experience, and were less likely to feel that their operative skill was level appropriate. They also reported a lower likelihood of good program fit, and were more than 3 times more likely to have considered leaving their training programs in the past year (49.6% vs. 15.0%, p < 0.001) [12].

Leibrandt TJ et al. circulated a questionnaire among program directors inquiring about the number and details of general surgery residents who left voluntarily in 2003. A voluntary attrition of 148 residents was reported by 102 programs (average of 1.5 residents’ attrition per program), which was similar to residents’ attrition before implementation of the 80-hour mandate, which was 167 residents from 110 programs (0.7 residents per program). Personal issues and work hours/lifestyle were cited as the most common reasons for leaving [10]. Everett CB et al. also surveyed program directors of general surgery residency programs in the United States and reported that the total attrition rate increased from 0.6 residents lost/program/year to 8 (p = 0.0013), with lifestyle concerns the most commonly reported reason, similar to earlier reports [23]. Yeo H et al. reported that residents who left surgical residency most often pursued nonsurgical residencies (62%) [6].

Strengths and limitations

This study covers a large cohort of surgical residents over nearly two decades, providing a long-term and comprehensive view. This study is among the few studies from an LMIC. The use of both institutional records and follow-up surveys allowed triangulation of quantitative data with qualitative insights, and identified the major factors that require improvement in order to decrease resident attrition. However, the reasons for leaving provided may not be accurate as the exiting residents may not always be honest. In addition, the HR or departmental data collectors may have attempted to fit the reasons in categories.

Nonetheless, it is important to acknowledge the limitations, the main one being the single-center design that may limit external validity, as residency structures and experiences could differ across institutions in Pakistan or other LMICs. The survey response rate was modest, introducing non-response bias, as individuals with stronger views may have been more likely to participate. Important unmeasured factors, such as personality traits, resilience, mentorship quality, or socio-economic constraints, could also not be fully assessed.

Conclusions

As this study identifies various predictors and causes of resident attrition, such as bullying, excessive workload leading to burnout, it is important to explore interventions such as structured mentorship programs, resident wellness initiatives, and institutional policies to address workplace toxicity, harassment, and burnout. We must make honest efforts to address these issues and make the workplace more trainee friendly and respond adequately to feedback such as this. We believe that studies such as this one will help to start the process of making improvements.

Future research should focus on multi-center studies across different regions of Pakistan and other LMICs to validate these findings and capture variations in training environments. Additionally, qualitative studies using in-depth interviews or focus groups could provide richer insights into resident experiences and guide the development of targeted strategies to improve retention and well-being.
