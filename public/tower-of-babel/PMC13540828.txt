A novel classification system based on the lateral view of 3D-CT for predicting the reducibility of intertrochanteric fractures: a retrospective study
Authors: Shiyun Qian, Yong Zhu, Kaihu Li, Zhangyuan Lin, Haitao Long, Ruibo Zhao, Buhua Sun, Shushan Zhao, Liang Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCurrent classifications for intertrochanteric fractures poorly predict the difficulty of achieving closed reduction. This study aimed to develop and validate a novel classification system based on the lateral view of three-dimensional computed tomography (3D-CT) to preoperatively assess reducibility.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 609 patients with intertrochanteric fractures. After screening, 318 cases (117 AO/OTA 31-A1 and 201 AO/OTA 31-A2) were included. Based on preoperative 3D-CT, fracture morphology was categorized as flexed, neutral, or extended types. The intraoperative outcome of closed reduction was used to define fractures as reducible or irreducible.ResultsAmong the 318 fractures, 174 (54.7%) were reducible and 144 (45.3%) were irreducible. The reducible group included 105 flexed, 21 neutral, and 48 extended types. The irreducible group comprised 90 flexed-type and 54 extended-type fractures. Irreducible flexed-type and extended-type fractures were further subdivided into 3 distinct subtypes each, based on characteristic radiological features.ConclusionThe proposed classification system, which integrates the AO/OTA framework with 3D-CT morphology, effectively identifies fractures at high risk for irreducible closed reduction. This system can help surgeons avoid futile reduction attempts and guide the choice of an appropriate reduction strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-026-07044-3.

Introduction

The global incidence of hip fractures continues to rise, posing a significant public health challenge. It is projected that the annual number of new hip fracture cases will reach approximately 4.5 million by 2050 [1]. Intertrochanteric fractures account for 40%–60% of all hip fractures [2]. Intertrochanteric fractures represent a terminal event for many elderly patients, associated with significant morbidity and potential complications such as pressure sores, pneumonia, and deep vein thrombosis, particularly when managed conservatively. These sequelae often lead to substantial disability and high mortality rates [3]. Consequently, early surgical intervention has been widely established as the standard of care [4–6].

The outcomes of surgical treatment are influenced by multiple factors, including bone quality, fracture morphology, reduction quality, and the selection and positioning of the implant [7]. Among these, the quality of reduction represents a critical factor under the surgeon’s direct control and is considered paramount for functional recovery [8]. Inadequate reduction significantly increases the risk of complications, including varus collapse, implant failure, and re-fracture [9]. Furthermore, achieving an anatomical reduction is a prerequisite for the optimal placement of internal fixation [10].

Currently, the most commonly used classification systems for intertrochanteric fractures are the AO/OTA classification [11] and the Evans-Jensen classification [12]. While valuable for assessing fracture stability, these systems have limited utility in predicting the difficulty of achieving closed reduction intraoperatively. This limitation stems primarily from their reliance on anteroposterior radiographs, which fail to adequately incorporate the critical influence of sagittal plane displacement. Ikuta et al. [13] proposed a classification based on lateral radiographs, categorizing fractures by the anteroposterior displacement of the proximal fragment relative to the distal one, thus offering a new perspective. Building upon this work, and inspired by the classification of pediatric supracondylar humeral fractures, this study introduces a novel approach. We propose, for the first time, a classification of intertrochanteric fractures into flexed, neutral, and extended types, based on the sagittal alignment of the fracture fragments observed on preoperative 3D-CT lateral views. Furthermore, based on the intraoperative manoeuvres required, fractures within each type are categorized as “reducible” or “irreducible.” A “reducible” fracture is one that can be satisfactorily reduced using standard closed techniques, whereas an “irreducible” fracture necessitates additional assisted closed reduction manoeuvres or even open reduction [14–18].

Materials and methods

Patient recruitment

A retrospective analysis was conducted on 609 patients who underwent surgical treatment for intertrochanteric fractures at our institution between December 2021 and December 2024. Inclusion criteria were as follows: [1] closed fracture with an injury-to-surgery interval within 2 weeks; [2] availability of complete clinical data, including preoperative 3D-CT and intraoperative fluoroscopic images; and [3] age over 18 years. Exclusion criteria included: [1] pathological, open, or periprosthetic fractures; [2] multiple fractures involving other anatomical sites; [3] pre-existing femoral deformities; [4] hip fusion due to ankylosing spondylitis; [5] femoral head necrosis with significant collapse; and [6] AO/OTA 31A3 fractures; [7] isolated greater trochanter fractures. After screening, 318 patients were ultimately included in the study. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Approval No. 2026020331).

Preoperative imaging evaluation

All CT data were reconstructed by a senior radiologist using multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) on a PACS workstation. In this study, all CT images were independently assessed and classified by two senior attending physicians. Any discrepancies in classification were resolved through consensus review, with a final review conducted separately by a chief physician. Fractures were initially classified according to the AO/OTA classification system [11]: A1-type refers to simple fractures with an intact lesser trochanter (LT), while A2-type refers to comminuted fractures with a detached LT. In this study, cases where the LT was split into two parts were categorized as A1-type.

Subsequently, based on the relative sagittal alignment of the proximal and distal fragments on the 3D-CT lateral view, fractures were further categorized into three subtypes (Fig. 1):

The association between this 3D-CT-based classification and closed reduction outcomes was systematically evaluated. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the imaging characteristics of irreducible fractures.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by two senior orthopedic surgeons. Under combined nerve block and general anesthesia, patients were placed supine on a fracture traction table. Following anesthesia induction, closed manual reduction was attempted using specific techniques based on the preoperative fracture type. To ensure the objectivity of the ‘irreducibility’ definition, a standardized three-step escalation protocol was implemented. Each attempt was informed by the preoperative 3D-CT classification to target specific mechanical interlocks:

First attempt : ①For Flexed-type, the limb was externally rotated 90° to disengage impaction, followed by longitudinal traction and internal rotation to 15°; ②For Neutral-type, reduction relied on longitudinal traction with mild internal rotation; ③For Extended-type, initial internal rotation was used to disengage fragments, followed by traction and adduction, ending in 0°–15° external rotation.Second attempt (Re-evaluation and adjustment): If the first attempt failed, the surgeon re-evaluated intraoperative fluoroscopy to identify residual bony obstacles. Traction strength was optimized, and the rotation or abduction/adduction angles were further adjusted (by approximately 5°–10°) to ‘unlock’ the specific impingement identified on the 3D-CT.Third attempt (Maximized manual maneuvers): A final effort was made using maximized manual manipulation or the ‘lead-pipe’ technique (slight over-reduction to bypass cortical spikes).

Reduction quality was assessed intraoperatively using C-arm fluoroscopy. Fracture alignment was evaluated according to the Kim criteria [19], defined as acceptable if cortical displacement was less than one cortical thickness on both anteroposterior and lateral views. The mechanical axis was assessed using the Baumgaertner–Solberg criteria [20], requiring a neck–shaft angle of 123°–140° on the anteroposterior view and angulation < 20° on the lateral view. Reduction quality was jointly evaluated by a senior attending surgeon and an associate chief physician. Reduction was deemed acceptable only if both alignment and mechanical criteria were satisfied. Fractures were defined as irreducible only if satisfactory alignment could not be achieved after these three distinct, optimized steps. After satisfactory reduction, internal fixation was performed using either a PFNA or InterTan implant based on fracture characteristics and patient factors. If closed reduction failed, a transition was made to either minimally invasive open reduction or instrument-assisted reduction. All patients ultimately received intramedullary fixation.

Reliability of the classification

To assess the reproducibility of the proposed 3D-CT classification, a reliability study was conducted using a random subset of 50 cases. Three independent orthopedic surgeons with varying experience levels—a senior consultant (> 15 years), an attending surgeon (8 years), and a senior resident (3 years)—evaluated the images blinded to the clinical data. The assessment was repeated after a 4-week washout period. Inter-observer reliability among the three reviewers was calculated using Fleiss’ kappa, while intra-observer reliability was determined using Cohen’s kappa. Kappa values were interpreted as: 0.00–0.20 (slight), 0.21–0.40 (fair), 0.41–0.60 (moderate), 0.61–0.80 (substantial), and 0.81–1.00 (almost perfect).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed for all variables. Continuous data for normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical data were presented as number (percentage). To compare parameters between reducible and irreducible groups, appropriate statistical tests—such as the independent samples t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, Chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test—were applied based on data distribution and variable type. To investigate the association between fracture reduction failure and various CT imaging parameters, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. In this analysis, a variable was considered a risk factor for reduction failure if its OR was greater than 1 and the associated p-value was less than 0.05. Inter-observer and intra-observer agreements were determined using Fleiss’ kappa and Cohen’s kappa, respectively. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA), and a two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

A total of 318 patients with intertrochanteric fractures were included in this study. The baseline characteristics of the overall cohort were as follows: the mean age was 77.02 ± 9.15 years. Among them, 45 patients (14.2%) were aged < 65 years, and 273 patients (85.8%) were aged ≥ 65 years; 114 patients (35.8%) were male, and 204 (64.2%) were female. Based on reduction difficulty, the patients were divided into two groups: the reducible group (n = 174, 54.7%) and the irreducible group (n = 144, 45.3%). Intergroup comparisons revealed no significant differences in age, sex, time from injury to surgery, or BMI between the two groups (P > 0.05). However, the operative time was significantly longer in the irreducible group compared to the reducible group (94.3 ± 48.5 min vs. 64.3 ± 19.5 min, P < 0.001). Regarding fracture classification, the distribution of the AO/OTA classification differed significantly between the two groups (P < 0.01), with A2-type fractures being more prevalent in the irreducible group. A significant difference was also found in the distribution of the 3D-CT lateral view classification (P = 0.002), in which the Extended-type fracture was more common in the irreducible group (Table 1).

Predictive factors for irreducible fractures

Multivariable analysis identified the following five radiographic features as independent factors associated with preoperative fracture irreducibility (Table 2): attachment of both greater trochanter (GT) and LT fragments to the proximal main fragment (p < 0.001, OR = 23.984); in flexed-type fractures, impaction of the proximal fragment into the medullary canal of the distal fragment in A2-type fractures (p = 0.01, OR = 2.16) and lateral wall involvement (p < 0.001, OR = 35.937); and in extended-type fractures, interlocking of the fracture fragments (p < 0.001, OR = 13.765) and significant anterior displacement of the proximal fragment (p < 0.001, OR = 8.364).

Reliability of the classification

The proposed 3D-CT classification system demonstrated excellent reproducibility across all levels of surgical experience. The inter-observer Fleiss’ kappa was 0.812 (95% CI, 0.756–0.868), indicating almost perfect agreement. The mean intra-observer Cohen’s kappa was 0.850, with individual values of 0.882, 0.846, and 0.821 for the senior, mid-career, and junior reviewers, respectively (Table 4).

Discussion

Clinical significance of preoperative reducibility assessment

Achieving anatomical reduction in intertrochanteric fractures is critical for minimizing the risk of implant failure and enabling early postoperative mobilization [23, 24]. The ability to preoperatively predict reducibility and accordingly plan a tailored surgical approach can significantly reduce operative time and improve reduction quality. Therefore, identifying preoperative imaging features associated with irreducibility is of considerable clinical value.

Moehring et al. [25] were among the first to describe irreducible fracture patterns, characterized by a bisected LT with the iliopsoas attached to the proximal fragment, causing posterior tilting—features consistent with our irreducible flexed-type I-3 and I-4. Kim et al. [22] reported anteriorly displaced proximal fragments with a bifid LT, corresponding to our irreducible extended-type II-1. Currently, a bifid LT is widely recognized as a hallmark of irreducibility [14, 26, 27]. Said et al. [28] described another irreducible variant in which the LT remains attached to the shaft, and a long medial spike from the proximal fragment becomes entrapped between the iliopsoas and the LT, resembling our irreducible flexed-type I-4. Carr [29] introduced the “posterior sagging” type, marked by posterior displacement and overlap of the proximal fragment, matching our irreducible flexed-type II. Chun et al. [16] proposed the concept of “sagittally unstable fractures,” where the distal fragment displaces posteriorly and the proximal fragment anteriorly. Although anteroposterior radiographs may show reduced displacement, lateral views reveal persistent anterior-superior displacement of the proximal fragment, particularly when accompanied by a long medial spike or an intact LT, consistent with our irreducible extended-types II-2 and III fractures. Despite increasing reports on irreducible fractures [14, 26, 27], a systematic, intuitive classification to guide intraoperative reduction is still lacking. To address this gap, we developed a novel classification system based on the lateral view of 3D-CT and described specific closed reduction techniques for each subtype.

Mechanisms of irreducibility

In flexed-type fractures, the proximal fragment is often impacted into the distal medullary canal or displaced posteriorly, leading to fragments overlap and mechanical interlock. If traction combined with external rotation fails to disengage the fragments, the fracture is deemed irreducible. In extended-type fractures, the proximal fragment is generally not impacted and remains mobile in the sagittal plane, rendering it inherently unstable. If traction with internal rotation cannot correct the anterior displacement, the fracture is similarly classified as irreducible.

In A1-type fractures, irreducibility frequently results from muscular attachments and traction forces. The gluteus medius and minimus, attached to the GT, contribute to abduction and internal rotation; the iliopsoas, inserted on the LT, promotes flexion and external rotation; and the femoral shaft is influenced by the adductors. When the proximal fragment retains attachment to the GT or LT, muscular traction can displace and lock the fracture, making conventional closed reduction ineffective. Notably, anatomical variations in the iliopsoas tendon play a key role. Studies show that in approximately 64% of individuals, the iliopsoas splits into two insertions on the LT: the psoas tendon attaches to the superomedial tip, while the iliacus tendon wraps posteriorly and extends to the inferolateral base [30, 31]. A “bare area” devoid of soft tissue exists between these two insertions [32]. When a fracture traverses this region, the LT splits, leaving the proximal fragment connected to the psoas tendon and the distal fragment to the iliacus and femoral shaft. The primary mechanism of irreducibility in such cases is the impaction of the proximal fragment’s medial spike between the iliopsoas and the residual distal LT. In our irreducible extended-type I-1, the medial spike locks anterior to the LT, further implicating soft tissue interposition as a key obstacle to reduction.

In A2-type fractures, reducibility is closely associated with the extent of soft tissue injury. Zhu et al. [27] suggested that impaction of the proximal fragment into the shaft is a predictor of reducibility. However, our findings indicate that it is often difficult to radiographically distinguish between reducible flexed-type II and irreducible flexed-type II. We propose that the “splinting effect” of preserved soft tissues is crucial for maintaining alignment and facilitating reduction. When the fracture involves the lateral cortex of the femoral shaft—as in irreducible flexed-type III—it often indicates high-energy trauma and severe soft tissue disruption. The loss of the splinting effect, combined with the lack of medullary impaction, contributes to irreducibility. Sharma et al. [14] observed that in extended-type fractures where the proximal fragment is not impacted (irreducible extended-type III), sagittal plane mobility is increased, and the free LT fragment can push the proximal fragment anteriorly. Our results support this: even when detached, the iliopsoas-attached LT can exert anterior force on the proximal fragment, particularly if the LT fragment is large. The soft tissue splinting effect remains critical: if minimally damaged, it may limit displacement; if severely compromised, anterior shift is exacerbated. When the anteriorly displaced proximal fragment becomes keyed into the anterior cortex of the shaft, as in irreducible extended-type I-2, closed reduction is typically unsuccessful due to mechanical interlock.

Reduction strategies based on fracture type

While most intertrochanteric fractures can be managed by closed reduction, systematic descriptions of reduction techniques remain limited. Ottolenghi [33] noted that fractures located lateral to the piriform fossa, with attached external rotators, may reduce better in external rotation; this also applies to comminuted fractures. Bennister et al. [34] recommended neutral rotation with traction as generally effective, internal rotation for 2- or 3-part fractures, and external rotation for 4-part fractures. Ikuta et al. [18] advised neutral or mild internal rotation when the proximal fragment includes the head and neck, and mild external rotation when it also includes the GT. Based on our classification, we propose targeted reduction strategies: For flexed-type fractures, where the proximal fragment is often impacted into the distal canal with cortical interlock, full external rotation is first applied to disengage the interlock, followed by manual traction, and finally fixation on the traction table. Extended-type fractures are generally reduced successfully with internal rotation followed by traction. Notably, one extended-type fracture showed malreduction after initial internal rotation but was successfully reduced after switching to external rotation (Fig. 4). We analyzed that the initial internal rotation caused interlocking between the cortex of the proximal medial fragment and the distal LT, requiring external rotation to first disengage the interlock before reduction.

When closed reduction fails, instrument-assisted techniques are valuable. For flexed-type fractures, percutaneous leverage can disimpact the proximal fragment from the canal [19, 29] (Fig. 5). For extended-type fractures, pushing the anteriorly displaced proximal fragment posteriorly via a bone tamp is effective [35, 36] (Fig. 6). Given the variability in fracture patterns, the choice of reduction aid and technique should be individualized based on the specific displacement characteristics.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although we performed a formal reliability analysis with substantial agreement among reviewers of different experience levels, the retrospective and single-center design may still be subject to inherent selection bias. Second, while our cohort of 318 cases is substantial, a larger sample size would further enhance the comprehensiveness of the classification and its rare subtypes. Third, the study focused on the morphological prediction of intraoperative reduction difficulty; as such, the direct correlation between this classification and long-term functional outcomes or specific implant-related complications was not evaluated. Finally, while our three-step escalation protocol provides a standardized framework, the definition of irreducibility could be further refined with quantitative intraoperative force measurements in future prospective trials.

Conclusion

The novel classification system introduced in this study, which integrates the lateral view of 3D-CT with the AO/OTA classification, effectively distinguishes and predicts the reducibility of most intertrochanteric fracture patterns. This framework provides surgeons with a practical tool for preoperative planning and supports the selection of a tailored reduction strategy. Its clinical application may contribute to optimized surgical workflow, improved reduction success rates, and reduced operative risks.
