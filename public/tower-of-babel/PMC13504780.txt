Moderate-high heritability and QTL associated with Manila clam Ruditapes philippinarum resistance to the parasite Perkinsus olseni
Authors: I. M. Sambade, A. Cao, D. Costas, J. A. de Santiago, M. Ortega-Iñarrea, M. Hermida, B. G. Pardo, A. Caballero, M. Gallo, A. Villalba, P. Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Manila clam (Ruditapes philippinarum) is one of the most valuable bivalves in global aquaculture, but its production is increasingly threatened by Perkinsus olseni, a protozoan parasite responsible for severe mortality events on shellfish beds. Understanding the genetic basis of resistance to this pathogen is essential for developing sustainable breeding strategies and reducing economic losses.ResultsWe conducted a genome-wide association study (GWAS) on 606 Manila clam offspring from 32 full-sib families to investigate the heritability and genomic architecture of resistance to P. olseni. Following quality control and exclusion of the 42 smallest individuals potentially affected by size-related exposure bias, 564 offspring were retained for the final GWAS analyses. Genomic screening was performed with a newly developed multispecies SNP array comprising 49,392 SNPs for R. philippinarum. Experimental challenges were carried out in two tanks (challenged and control), each containing 1,500 offspring, and parasite load was quantified by qPCR using control Ct values as a non-infected reference. A substantial proportion of phenotypic variation in parasite load was explained by additive genetic effects, with heritability estimated at h² = 0.45 ± 0.09 using the full dataset and increasing to h² = 0.53 ± 0.08 after filtering. Several genome-wide significant quantitative trait loci (QTL) were identified across seven chromosomes, supporting a polygenic basis for resistance. Notably, one QTL on chromosome 18 also showed a highly significant association within one of the largest families. Patterns of linkage disequilibrium (LD) revealed rapid decay across most chromosomes, typical of marine bivalves, except for three chromosomes showing extended LD patterns compatible with putative structural polymorphisms, including possible inversions.ConclusionsThese results demonstrate a substantial additive genetic component underlying resistance to P. olseni in Manila clam and highlight chromosome 18 as an important genomic region associated with parasite burden. Overall, our findings support the future integration of genomic tools into selective breeding programmes aimed at improving disease resilience and promoting the long-term sustainability of Manila clam aquaculture under increasing environmental and pathogen pressures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13057-3.

Background

The Manila clam (Ruditapes philippinarum) is a bivalve mollusc native to the western Pacific Ocean, widely distributed across intertidal zones on sandy or muddy bottoms, where it can burrow to depths of 15–20 cm [1]. This species plays a key ecological role in coastal ecosystems by filtering large volumes of seawater, removing phytoplankton and suspended particles, and providing food for a wide range of marine organisms. R. philippinarum tolerates a wide range of salinity and temperature conditions and exhibits robust innate immune responses typical of marine bivalves [2] .

Originating in East Asia, it supports major aquaculture industries in China (over three million tonnes annually; [3]) and has been successfully introduced to other regions, due to its rapid growth and high fecundity. It is one of the most valuable bivalve species worldwide, including the west coast of North America, the French Atlantic coast, the Adriatic Sea, and the Iberian Peninsula. In Spain, R. philippinarum has become a key species in the shellfishery sector, significantly contributing to coastal economies [4].

Protozoan parasites are among the primary causes of diseases with severe socio-economic consequences in marine bivalves [5]. Species of the genus Perkinsus cause perkinsosis, a parasitic infection affecting multiple mollusc species worldwide, often leading to mass mortalities and substantial economic losses in fisheries and aquaculture [6]. Like other venerid clams, R. philippinarum is susceptible to infection by the protozoan parasite Perkinsus olseni. The interaction between R. philippinarum and P. olseni involves a coevolutionary arms race, where the parasite evolves strategies to persist in host tissues while clams deploy immune mechanisms to limit infection. Unlike Perkinsus marinus infections in oysters, where haemocytes phagocytose internalised parasites, P. olseni remains predominantly extracellular in R. philippinarum, often becoming encapsulated by haemocytes that release cytotoxic molecules to impair parasite viability. These contrasting host responses highlight the diversity of defence strategies among bivalves and the importance of extracellular immune processes in Perkinsus–clam interactions [7]. Among the currently recognised species of the genus Perkinsus, P. marinus and P. olseni are the most significant in bivalve pathology due to their high virulence and global socio-economic impact and are listed by the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) as notifiable pathogens due to their high socio-economic impact and transboundary relevance [8].

P. olseni is a protozoan parasite infecting multiple mollusc species, including the commercially relevant clams R. philippinarum and R. decussatus [9, 10]. It was first detected in Europe in the late 1970s infecting R. decussatus from Italy [11] and is now recognised as a major threat to clam fishery and aquaculture in both Europe and Asia [6, 12]. The parasite proliferates throughout host connective tissues — primarily gills, digestive gland, gonad and mantle — inducing strong inflammatory responses where haemocytes aggregate and encapsulate extracellular trophozoites [9, 13]. High parasite burdens can impair growth and reproduction, disrupt recruitment, and lead to heavy mortalities under warm conditions [14–16]. These recurrent mass mortalities continue to threaten productivity and sustainability of R. philippinarum aquaculture worldwide.

All life stages of P. olseni are infective [17]. Infection begins when trophozoites are filtered from the water column. These spherical cells, with a large vacuole and peripheral nucleus, proliferate within host tissues via multiple karyokinesis and cytokinesis events, forming multinucleated schizonts or tomonts [18]. Upon cell wall rupture, daughter trophozoites are released and can either reinfect host tissues or be expelled via pseudo-faeces, faeces, or post-mortem tissue decomposition [19]. Under adverse environmental conditions—such as hypoxia, low pH, or nutrient limitation—trophozoites differentiate into hypnospores, a resistant stage with a thick cell wall enabling long-term survival in sediments [20, 21]. When conditions improve, hypnospores germinate into zoosporangia, producing hundreds of biflagellated zoospores capable of active swimming and host infection [22, 23].

The severity and outcome of P. olseni infection are strongly modulated by environmental factors, particularly temperature, with higher prevalence and mortality typically observed during warm, dry periods [12, 16, 17, 24]. Although elevated temperatures can promote faster growth in clams, they are also associated with a higher prevalence of infections by thermophilic pathogens. Controlling P. olseni in natural beds is particularly challenging, as transmission occurs directly without intermediate hosts and environmental reservoirs facilitate reinfection [6].

Given the high economic value of R. philippinarum and the recurrent issues faced by farmers, the development of well-structured breeding programs targeting resistance to P. olseni is essential. Selective breeding exploiting natural genetic variation has already proven effective in other bivalves, improving resistance to pathogens such as Ostreid herpesvirus-1 in Pacific oysters (Crassostrea gigas) and Marteilia cochillia in cockles (Cerastoderma edule) [25–27]. Reported narrow-sense heritability values for disease resistance in bivalves are often moderate-high (0.4–0.99), supporting the feasibility of genetic improvement as a long-term mitigation strategy [25, 28]. Field challenges in R. philippinarum have demonstrated that resistance to P. olseni is heritable (h² = 0.51 ± 0.20), although estimates should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of tested families [29]. Furthermore, in the closely related grooved carpet shell (R. decussatus), population genomics revealed significant heritability and enabled the identification of genomic regions consistently associated with infection levels, suggesting a strong genetic component underlying resistance/tolerance [30]. Candidate genes identified in these studies include those involved in energy metabolism, apoptosis, proteasome activity, and immune regulation. Transcriptomic analyses have provided valuable insights into the molecular dynamics between R. philippinarum and P. olseni. Haemocytes of infected clams exhibit a strong transcriptional activation of immune-related pathways including NF-κB signalling, apoptosis, ubiquitination, antioxidant defence, and lectin-mediated recognition [31, 32]. Comparative analyses between experimental and natural infections confirmed consistent upregulation of stress and inflammatory genes, highlighting a robust and coordinated immune response [32]. Moreover, dual RNA-seq in vitro studies have shown that the parasite itself modulates the expression of virulence factors and detoxification enzymes when exposed to clam plasma, suggesting an active molecular interplay host-parasite during infection [33].

A comprehensive understanding of the genetic basis of resistance to P. olseni is a critical prerequisite for the design of effective breeding programs. In this study, we evaluated variation in resistance in an indoor common-garden experiment, genotyped individuals with a medium-density SNP array, and performed a genome-wide association study (GWAS) to understand the genetic basis of resistance. Candidate genes located near significant SNPs were annotated and functionally characterised, providing insights into the molecular mechanisms of host–parasite interactions and establishing the foundations for the genetic improvement of resistance to perkinsosis in the Manila clam.

Materials and methods

Production of full-sib families of Manila clam seed and challenge with Perkinsus olseni zoospores

Obtaining full-sib families

Between June and July 2022, 2,041 adult Manila clams R. philippinarum were collected from the natural shellfish bed of Placeres (Ría de Pontevedra, NW Spain) and transferred for conditioning to the ECIMAT hatchery facilities (Toralla Marine Science Station - CIM, Vigo, Spain). Clams were kept individually in glasses of 0.3 L with 1 μm filtered, aerated seawater. Spawning was induced by thermal shocks between 10 and 22 °C for 10 h and the quality of the oocytes and sperm was evaluated under a light microscope. The number of oocytes released by each female was estimated by counting aliquots of the spawning suspension using a Neubauer chamber. Controlled fertilization was carried out by crossing a single male with a single female (1 male × 1 female), adding sperm to oocytes at a ratio of 10:1 (sperm-to-oocyte ratio). D-shaped larvae were obtained 24 h post fertilization with a transformation rate from trochophore larvae of 42 ± 19%. Following this protocol, a total of 69 full-sib (FS) families were obtained over a period of two months. Among the 69 crosses performed, the best 44 performing families were finally selected to be used in the challenge with P. olseni.

Larvae from each family were cultured in individual 150 L cylindrical-conical tanks at a density of 8 ± 3 larvae mL-1 with sea water filtered at 1 μm and treated with UV, slight aeration, and temperature 19.0 ± 1.4 °C in an open circuit with a renewal of 5% volume / hour. The diet consisted of Tisochrysis lutea (ECC038), Chaetoceros neogracile (ECC007), Phaeodactylum tricornutum (ECC028) and Rhodomonas lens (ECC030) in a ratio of 1:1:1:1 (according to the cell count), and Tetraselmis suecica (ECC036) was included from the seventh day of culture. The daily algal ration was supplied automatically in six meals administered every 4 h, aiming to maintain the algal concentration in the tanks within a target range of 20–40 cells µL⁻¹. At 14 days post-fertilization (dpf), pediveliger larvae from each family were transferred to separate 50 L tanks in suspended baskets with constant aeration, temperature 18.4 ± 0.5 °C and a renewal rate of 50 L day-1. The animals were fed with the same diet as described above but maintaining a constant density of 168 ± 48 larvae cm3. Metamorphosis of larvae took place in those tanks.

Experimental challenge with P. olseni

When clam seed reached the minimum size established for infection trials (2 mm in length), they were transferred to the Centro de Investigaciones Marinas (CIMA, Vilanova de Arousa, Spain) on 22nd September 2022, acclimated for one week in open-flow seawater with supplementary food from mesocosm-generated phytoplankton blooms, and subsequently maintained in closed-circuit systems for parasite exposure, as described below.

The challenge design involved exposing Manila clam seeds to P. olseni zoospores, thus mimicking the natural infection path [6, 34]. The plan was to add P. olseni zoosporangia to the tank holding the clam seeds and those zoosporangia would release zoospores. The process to obtain the P. olseni zoosporangia needed for challenge started enough in advance to have them ready for the challenge time, namely after Manila clam seed acclimation in CIMA facilities. A total of 115 grooved carpet-shell clams R. decussatus were collected from Ría de Pontevedra on 7th November 2022 for parasite isolation. The clams were kept in close circuit with UV-sterilised, aerated seawater for depuration for 7 days, to minimise bacterial contamination of eventual isolated parasites. The soft tissues of each clam were incubated in Ray’s fluid thioglycollate medium (RFTM) for 1 week, at 28 °C, to induce transformation of P. olseni trophozoites occurring in the soft tissues into prezoosporangia [20]. Then, a branchial fragment was checked to assess infection intensity; each branchial fragment was stained with Lugol’s iodine and perkinsosis intensity was ranged using Mackin’s scale (0–5) under light microscopy [17]. The 12 most heavily infected clams (score 5) were used for isolation of prezoosporangia, as described by Casas et al. [20], on 24th November 2022. Briefly, gills, siphons, and visceral tissues were enzymatically digested with trypsin (0.25%) for 60 to 90 min and prezoosporangia were separated from tissue debris by sequential filtration (300, 150, 100, and 20 μm). Prezoosporangia in the 20 μm sieve were washed with sterile filtered (0.22 μm) seawater (SFSW) plus antibiotics (200 U ml–1 Penicillin G and 400 µg ml–1 Streptomycin). Aliquots were stained with neutral red and quantified using a Neubauer chamber to estimate prezoosporangia concentration and viability [20]. A total of ca. 60 × 106 prezoosporangia were distributed into culture flasks filled with SSPF plus antibiotics, which were kept in an incubator at 20 °C for four days. Those were favourable conditions for prezoosporangia starting zoosporulation process, by which prezoosporangia (uninucleate cells) became zoosporangia. The zoosporulation process of P. olseni involves successive mitoses giving rise to hundreds of daughter cells enclosed within the original prezoosporangia wall; those daughter cells transform into biflagellate zoospores, which eventually are released through a discharge tube [23]. Zoospores can survive in the seawater various days, even weeks [20]. Temperature and salinity strongly influence duration of zoosporulation process, with maximum speed and synchrony among zoosporangia around 28 °C and above 25 psu. As temperature decreases, the zoosporulation duration increases as well as its variability, thus asynchrony among zoosporangia increases. Similar effects are expected with salinity below 25 psu [20]. According to it, incubation of the isolated prezoosporangia in SFSW at 20 °C would allow the progression of zoosporulation but mass zoospore release would not be expected before 5 days. Thus, prezoosporangia / zoosporangia were incubated four days and then used for challenge, which lasted seven days. Mass release of zoospores was expected to occur during the challenge period. Considering that the challenge was performed in late November 2022, when temperature of seawater pumped from Ría de Arousa to CIMA facilities was around 16 °C and salinity around 33 psu, seawater temperature in the challenge had to be raised to 19 °C, to increase zoosporulation success and zoospore abundance during challenge.

Two cylindrical 750-L tanks (Fig. S1A) were used for the challenge: one to which zoosporangia were added (infection tank) and one control tank. Approximately 1,500 Manila clam seeds randomly chosen among offspring of the 44 families were stocked per tank. The clam seeds were set into partially submerged floating PVC cylinders (30 cm in diameter, 14 cm in height) with a mesh (500 μm) screen at the bottom, on which the clams were laid; an air-lift system provided continuous circulation of the seawater from the tank top-down through the cylinder (Fig. S1B). Infection tank received ~ 35 million P. olseni zoosporangia, while zoosporangia were not added into the control tank. Tanks were maintained in closed circuit for seven days to maximise parasite exposure of clam seeds, with aeration and constant food supply, from mesocosm-generated phytoplankton blooms (Fig. S1C), to stimulate clam filtration. A heater submerged in each tank allowed to keep temperature at 19 °C. Subsequently, the PVC cylinders holding the clams were transferred to a raceway with open-flow seawater and food supplement (Fig. S1D) and maintained for three weeks post-exposure to allow putative P. olseni infections to progress.

At the end of the trial, clams from both infected and control tanks were measured (shell length and height), dissected, and soft tissues preserved in absolute ethanol for DNA extraction.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from gill tissue both for offspring of the infected tank as well as for their parents for subsequent parentage analysis to identify families. Depending on sample size, DNA was extracted using either the E-Z 96™ Plant DNA DS Kit, the E.Z.N.A.® Plant DNA DS Kit, or the E.Z.N.A.® MicroElute® Genomic DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocols. For very small specimens, a decalcification step was performed prior to DNA extraction to avoid shell-derived inhibitors. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and adjusted to 60 ng/µL. Extracted DNA was used for parasite load quantification by qPCR and SNP genotyping. This information was used for downstream analyses.

Quantification of P. olseni load by qPCR

To precisely quantify P. olseni parasite load on infected R. decussatus individuals by qPCR, a standard curve based on amplification cycle (cycle threshold, Ct) versus P. olseni cell number was designed, comparing the group that was put in contact with the parasite and the control group. Genomic DNA was extracted using the NucleoSpin Tissue, Mini kit for DNA from cells and tissue (Macherey-Nagel, Germany), according to manufacturer’s instructions and quantified on a NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). To design the curve, tenfold serial dilutions of the extracted DNA were performed in duplicate to a final theoretical quantity of 5 cells (5⋅106, 5⋅105, 5⋅104, 5⋅103, 5⋅102, 5⋅10, 5 cells).

Parasite load was quantified by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using the species-specific primers Perk-ITS-q F1 and Perk-ITS-q R2 targeting the internal transcribed spacer regions ITS-1 and ITS-2 of ribosomal DNA, following Ríos et al. [35]. Amplifications were performed in a total volume of 20 µL using the CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection Systems (BioRad). Each reaction contained 1 µL of DNA from gill tissue, 10 µL of AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (NeoBiotech), 0.4 µL of each primer (10 µM) and 8.2 µL of mQH2O. Amplification was performed under the following conditions: denaturation for 10 min at 95 °C, amplification by 40 cycles of 30 s at 95 °C and 60 s at 60 °C, melting curve evaluation 1 min at 95 °C, and increase of 0.5 °C each 30 s starting in 60 °C, end at 95 °C for 15 s. All reactions were performed using two technical replicates. Parasite infection level in each individual was estimated from the cycle threshold (Ct) value, corresponding to the number of amplification cycles required to reach the fluorescence detection threshold. Lower Ct values indicated higher parasite loads, whereas higher Ct values reflected low or undetectable infections (similar to controls).

SNP genotyping and parentage assignment

Genotyping was performed in a random sample of ~ 700 out of 1,500 individuals from the infected tank, as well as in the parental individuals, using a clam multispecies Axiom SNP array developed by the University of Padova, the Interdisciplinary Centre of Marine and Environmental Research of University of Porto and the Universidade de Santiago de Compostela in collaboration with Benchmark Genetics and Thermo-Fisher Scientific. This array comprises 63,585 SNPs, including 49,392 from R. philippinarum (Manila clam) that were applied in this study, and 14,193 in R. decussatus (grooved carpet shell clam). This means a SNP density of 36.8 SNPs / Mb assuming a genome size of 1.341 Gb for Manila clam [36].

Parentage assignment was conducted with the software FAP (Family Analysis Program; [37]), which determines the most probable family allocation based on parental and offspring genotypes, applying Mendelian principles to calculate the likelihood of all candidate relationships within a subset of individuals. FAP uses an exclusion-based approach and provides: (i) a panel of loci for optimal assignment; (ii) the most likely parental pair for each offspring; and (iii) the identification of loci potentially affected by genotyping errors. This software is particularly suited for cases in which all candidate parents are known. The value reported by FAP corresponds to the assignment error rate, defined as the proportion of genotype inconsistencies between offspring and candidate parental pairs based on Mendelian expectations. Assignments with error rates above 0.05 were discarded following the software’s recommendations.

GWAS analysis

GWAS was performed using a random sample of individuals from the challenge tank. A sample of 50 individuals from the control tank were evaluated for parasite load as a Ct reference. Variance components and heritability for parasite load were estimated using ASReml v4.1 [38] under a linear mixed model approach. The trait considered was parasite load, estimated through the quantitative qPCR Ct value for P. olseni detection. For interpretability, results were also confirmed using the transformed variable (− Ct), which yielded equivalent estimates.

Gene mining: linkage disequilibrium and gene annotation

Following GWAS, SNPs associated at genome-wide level with parasite load (lowest p-values) were selected for genomic inspection to identify putative candidate genes involved in resistance to P. olseni.

To explore the size of the window for identification of candidate genes within QTL regions, linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay was estimated using SNP-array data separately for each chromosome using PopLDdecay [42] and SNP array genotypes.

Using that information, candidate gene searches were conducted within ± 200 kb windows upstream and downstream of each significant SNP. Annotated genes within these regions were identified, and the corresponding encoded proteins and Gene Ontology (GO) terms were retrieved. Functional enrichment analysis of candidate genes was conducted with the gofuncR R package [43], which implements randomisation-based gene set enrichment testing while accounting for gene length and other biases. Gene Ontology (GO) categories (Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component) were tested for overrepresentation relative to the genome-wide gene set, and p-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR).

Results

Parasite load and phenotypic variation

A total of 696 individuals were analysed, including 44 females, 43 males, and 609 offspring (of which 606 individuals passed the QC filtering for genotyping) Parasite load in controls (Ct) was consistently > 34, while in challenged clams qPCR cycle threshold (Ct) ranged between 12 and 36, indicating absence of infection in controls and a broad infection range in challenged clams. Shell length and height were highly correlated (r = 0.996, p < 0.001), therefore, only shell height was retained for further analyses. Shell height ranged from 2.09 to 11.93 mm (6.40 ± 0.12) and showed a significant correlation with Ct values (r = -0.325, p < 0.001), indicating more amplification cycles in smaller individuals, consistent with low or undetectable infection (similar to controls) (Table S1). This pattern suggested that the smallest individuals may have experienced reduced exposure to the parasite due to lower filtration capacity, potentially due to reduced filtration capacity associated with smaller branchial surface. This pattern suggested that the smallest individuals may have experienced reduced exposure to the parasite due to lower filtration capacity associated with smaller branchial surface, potentially generating an artefactual pattern of low or undetectable parasite load. To minimise this size-related bias, the 42 smallest offspring (shell height < 3.45 mm) were excluded from the analysis, resulting in a final dataset of 564 individuals used for subsequent association analyses between parasite load and SNP genotyping (Fig. 1). After this filtering step, the correlation between size and parasite load was very low and non-significant (r = 0.051, p > 0.05). Excluding the 42 smallest individuals did not substantially alter family representation in the filtered dataset, as excluded individuals were distributed across multiple full-sib families and all major families remained represented in the final analyses.

SNP genotyping and quality control

Both offspring and parents were genotyped using the clam multispecies Axiom™ SNP array. According to the manufacturer’s quality metrics [44], a total of 39,903 SNPs were classified as PolyHighResolution or NoMinorHom, representing the recommended categories for downstream analyses. Among these, 518 markers located on unplaced scaffolds of the R. philippinarum genome (GCA_964294305.1, ENA) were excluded, leaving 39,385 SNPs mapped to the 19 assembled chromosomes. SNPs classified as CallRateBelowThreshold or MonoHighResolution were not retained. This final dataset provided a uniform genomic coverage of approximately 31.8 SNPs/Mb, suitable for genome-wide association analyses.

Parentage assignment

Parentage analysis using FAP [37] assigned offspring to the most likely parental pair based on Mendelian exclusion. Assignments with estimated Mendelian error rates above 0.05 were discarded following the software recommendations. This resulted in the inclusion of 32 full-sib families (Fig. 2). The final dataset comprised 32 full-sib families ranging from 1 to 138 offspring (606 in total), all compatible with the crosses performed at hatchery.

Genome-wide association analyses

Estimates of narrow-sense heritability for parasite load were high and significant, supporting a substantial genetic component underlying resistance. GWAS was conducted with the 564 offspring with full information and significant associations at genome-wide level were detected on chromosomes 6, 8, 10, 13, 15, 16, and 18 (Fig. 3). We also explored the association in the two large families (F6/M8: 130 offspring and F1/M3: 76 offspring; Fig. S2) and a highly significant QTL was identified in the second family in chromosome 18, spanning approximately 1–5.5 Mb (Fig. S2B).

To evaluate the impact of the filtering step, we compared the main genetic results before and after excluding the smallest individuals. In the full dataset, heritability was estimated at h² = 0.45 ± 0.09 and genome-wide significant associations were detected on five chromosomes. After filtering, heritability increased to h² = 0.53 ± 0.08 and significant associations were detected on seven chromosomes, while no correlation between shell size and parasite load was then detected. These results indicate that excluding the smallest individuals reduced size-related confounding and improved the power to detect genetic signals. Results were further validated using the SNPassoc package, and consistent with the Manhattan plots, the strongest associations were detected in the same chromosomal regions. Notably, additive and dominant models produced highly similar association patterns, with coincident peaks across models. This consistency indicates that the detected signals are robust and not strongly dependent on the assumed genetic model. In several cases, the statistical support across models was very similar, preventing a clear distinction of a single best-fitting model (Fig. S3).

To assess the robustness of the GWAS results, a Q–Q plot (Fig. S4) was generated, and the genomic inflation factor was calculated. The observed p-values closely followed the expected null distribution, with deviation only at the extreme tail, and the genomic inflation factor was low (λ = 1.027), indicating appropriate control of inflation and relatedness effects.

Linkage disequilibrium, gene mining and enrichment analysis

To define genomic windows around significant SNPs, linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay was estimated across the R. philippinarum genome. Mean pairwise r² values declined below 0.2 at ~ 200 kb for most chromosomes (Fig. 4), except for chromosomes 1, 8, and 16, where extended LD patterns compatible with putative structural polymorphisms, including possible inversions, were observed (Fig. S5). Based on these results, a ± 200 kb window around each significant SNP was selected to search for candidate genes potentially involved in resistance to P. olseni.

Genes located within ± 200 kb around genome-wide significant SNPs were retrieved from the R. philippinarum reference genome. Multiple candidate genes with functions related to immune recognition, oxidative stress, transcriptional regulation, and cell adhesion were identified (Table S2), providing insights into molecular mechanisms potentially underlying resistance to P. olseni.

On chromosome 6, several genes involved in immune signalling and cellular metabolism were detected, including Toll-like receptor Tollo, DNA ligase 4, ceramide kinase, and neurogenic locus Notch protein. These genes suggest the involvement of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) and Notch-mediated pathways in the early detection and signalling response to protozoan parasites, consistent with previous studies in other bivalves [45, 46]. The region also contained F-box only protein 32, COP9 signalosome subunit 5, and Forkhead box protein D3-B, regulators of protein degradation and transcription, as well as mitochondrial inner membrane protease ATP23, 7-dehydrocholesterol reductase, and regucalcin, pointing to metabolic reprogramming associated with immune activation [47].

On chromosome 8, the associated interval contained genes such as metal-response element-binding transcription factor 2, RING finger protein 44, transmembrane protease serine 12, coagulation factor XI, and several encoding sulfotransferases (SULT1C2, SULT6B1). These genes are implicated in transcriptional regulation, ubiquitination, and detoxification pathways that mitigate oxidative damage during infection [48–51].

The chromosome 10 region included several innate immune components, such as Complement C1q-like protein 4 and Protein Toll, suggesting complement-like opsonisation and Toll-dependent recognition mechanisms, as described in Crassostrea and Ostrea species [52–54]. The presence of Mediator of RNA polymerase II transcription subunit 4 and the enrichment of GO terms related to “RNA polymerase II transcription regulatory region sequence-specific DNA binding” suggest that transcriptional control contributes to variability in infection outcome [55, 56].

The region on chromosome 13 included several genes associated with immune and cell-surface functions, such as IgGFc-binding protein, sushi/von Willebrand factor type A/EGF/pentraxin domain-containing protein 1, and Suppressor of hairless (Notch pathway), together with ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase MINDY-1 and ATP-dependent RNA helicase DDX55. The presence of multiple Notch-related genes in independent regions suggests that this signalling cascade might play a relevant role in haemocyte differentiation and phagocytosis [57, 58].

On chromosome 15, genes associated with cell adhesion and tissue repair were predominant, including Integrin alpha-4, Fibroblast growth factor receptor 4, Thrombospondin-2, and PR domain zinc finger protein 15. Together with Glutathione S-transferase Mu 3 and Glutathione hydrolase 1, these candidates suggest coordinated regulation between oxidative stress responses and cellular adhesion during infection and repair [59–61].

The chromosome 16 region was particularly rich in immune and regulatory elements, including Toll-like receptor 4, E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase TRIM33, Histone deacetylase 6, Transcription intermediary factor 1-alpha, and several Plexins (A1, A2, B2, B3). These genes are involved in innate immunity, chromatin remodelling, and cell migration, supporting the hypothesis that resistance involves both rapid pathogen recognition and fine transcriptional regulation of haemocyte activity [62, 63].

Finally, on chromosome 18 we identified a gene-rich interval containing N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphotransferase α/β subunits, hyaluronan-binding protein 2, DnaJ homolog subfamily A member 1, and U6 snRNA-associated Sm-like protein LSm7. These genes are mainly related to protein and RNA processing, consistent with previous evidence that intracellular pathogens can manipulate host vesicular and transcriptional machinery to favour persistence [64–66].

Functional enrichment analysis across all associated regions detected a single significantly overrepresented GO term (raw_p_overrep < 0.05): RNA polymerase II transcription regulatory region sequence-specific DNA binding, suggesting a role for transcriptional regulation in the genetic response to infection.

In addition to the signal detected in the full dataset, GWAS in family F1M3 disclosed 29 significant SNPs in a region < 1 Mb in chromosome 18 (Fig. S2B; Table S3), where genes appeared involved in diverse biological processes including immune recognition and effector mechanisms (e.g. Ficolin-1, Cathepsin L, Peroxidasin homolog; [67–69], signal transduction (Guanine nucleotide-binding protein subunit alpha-11, Phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate 5-kinase type-1 alpha; [70]), vesicular and cytoskeletal dynamics (Dynein axonemal heavy chain 6, Tropomodulin; [71]), protein turnover (E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase RZF1; [68]), and carbohydrate metabolism (Alpha-mannosidase 2x, UDP-D-xylose: L-fucose alpha-1,3-D-xylosyltransferase 3; [72]) (Table S3).

Discussion

This study provides genome-wide insights into the genetic basis for resistance to Perkinsus olseni in the Manila clam (Ruditapes philippinarum), a pathogen responsible for recurrent mass mortalities in natural and farmed populations across Europe and Asia [17, 29]. Using a new high-density SNP array and an infection challenge by immersion, we estimated moderate-high heritability for resistance to this parasite using 32 full-sib families after excluding the smallest individuals (h2 = 0.53). Our results align with previous findings in R. decussatus and other bivalves where disease resistance traits often exhibit moderate to high heritability [25, 28, 30], supporting the feasibility of selective breeding as a long-term mitigation strategy.

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis indicated that LD decayed below r² = 0.2 within ~ 200 kb for most chromosomes, providing a framework for candidate gene mining. Comparable LD decay patterns have been reported in other molluscs, although the extent and rate vary markedly across taxa and breeding histories. In the Pacific oyster (Crassostrea gigas), r² values dropped to 0.15 within only 500 bp, with faster decay in wild than in farmed populations [73]. Similarly, in the blue mussel (Mytilus edulis), LD decayed rapidly, reaching mean r² values of 0.12–0.06 at < 15 kb and below 0.05 at 100 kb [74]. These short-range LD patterns reflect the high effective population sizes and recombination rates typical of marine invertebrates. In contrast, domesticated or bottlenecked populations—such as those under artificial selection in aquaculture—often exhibit slower LD decay due to reduced genetic diversity [75]. The slower LD decay observed in R. philippinarum (~ 200 kb) suggests more extended haplotype blocks, possibly reflecting founder effects associated with the introduction of an exotic species from Asia. Regions of extended LD were observed on chromosomes 1, 8, and 16. These patterns are compatible with candidate structural polymorphisms, including possible inversions, but LD patterns alone do not demonstrate structural variation or define its breakpoints. Further validation using WGS and/or complementary structural-variant detection approaches would be required to confirm their structural basis.

Several genomic regions showed significant association with parasite load as supported by the GWAS analysis. Consistent signals across different analytical approaches—GCTA with LOCO correction and SNPassoc under dominant and codominant models—revealed confident associations on chromosomes 6, 8, 10, 13, 15, 16, and 18 after filtering out the smallest offspring. We mined around these regions and could identify candidate genes and biological processes potentially involved in the host–parasite interaction. The set of genes identified across significant regions supports a multifactorial architecture of resistance, involving immune recognition, stress response, and cellular regulation. The presence of Toll-like receptors, complement-related proteins, and glutathione metabolism enzymes suggests that R. philippinarum relies on coordinated innate and oxidative stress responses against P. olseni, consistent with immune activation patterns described in this species [31] and other bivalves [76]. In particular, previous transcriptomic studies in R. philippinarum have shown that immune-related pathways, including Toll-like signalling, oxidative stress response, and lectin-mediated recognition, are significantly modulated during Perkinsus infection [31–33], supporting the biological relevance of the candidate regions identified here. Moreover, genes involved in transcriptional regulation (myc, trim33, foxd3) and cell adhesion (integrins, plexins, titin) point to downstream mechanisms controlling haemocyte behaviour, apoptosis, and tissue repair [7, 31–33]. Among these candidates, glutathione S-transferases are particularly relevant because they participate in detoxification and antioxidant defense by conjugating glutathione to reactive compounds and reducing oxidative damage generated during immune activation. In bivalves, Perkinsus infection is associated with haemocyte-mediated inflammatory responses, production of cytotoxic molecules, and modulation of oxidative stress pathways. Therefore, the presence of glutathione S-transferase-related genes among the candidate regions may reflect variation in the capacity of Manila clams to regulate oxidative stress and inflammation during P. olseni infection. This interpretation is consistent with previous transcriptomic studies showing modulation of antioxidant defense, detoxification, and inflammatory pathways during Perkinsus–clam interactions [31–33], although functional validation will be required to confirm the specific role of these genes in resistance.

Altogether, these findings indicate that resistance in R. philippinarum may not depend on a single effector but on the integration of immune sensing, stress mitigation, and cellular homeostasis pathways, a profile typical of long-lived bivalves facing chronic protozoan infections. However, it is important to note that these functional interpretations are based on genomic location and annotation and therefore represent hypotheses that will require further validation through gene expression or functional assays.

Although the associated SNPs in the global and family-based analyses map to regions separated by 6 Mb in chromosome 18, both intervals show notable functional convergence. Genes identified in the population-wide GWAS are mainly involved in transcriptional regulation, RNA processing, and intracellular protein trafficking, whereas the F1M3-specific signals include genes related to lysosomal activity, vesicular transport, and innate immune recognition. Despite these differences, both sets of genes point towards cellular processes commonly targeted during intracellular infection, including regulation of gene expression, protein turnover, and endo-lysosomal pathways [7, 52, 55, 62, 64–66, 70]. This functional overlap suggests that chromosome 18 harbours multiple loci affecting different layers of the host response, ranging from core regulatory mechanisms detectable at the population level to more specialised immune effector pathways segregating within specific families.

Overall, these findings suggest that genetic variation in R. philippinarum response to P. olseni infection is influenced by a combination of innate immune recognition, oxidative stress response, transcriptional regulation, and cellular maintenance. This molecular architecture mirrors mechanisms reported in other venerid clams, including R. decussatus, where genes related to immunity, metabolism, and apoptosis were also implicated. Combined with the moderate-high heritability estimates obtained in this study, these results support the existence of a genetic component underlying parasite load variation under controlled experimental challenge conditions and highlight the potential utility of these phenotypes for future selective breeding approaches in Manila clam aquaculture. However, the phenotype analysed here corresponds to parasite load estimated from qPCR Ct values and should therefore not be interpreted as a direct measure of field resistance, survival, tolerance, or epidemiological transmission. Likewise, the QTL and candidate genomic regions identified in this study represent promising targets for future marker-assisted or genomic selection approaches, but additional validation across independent cohorts, natural infection conditions, and different Manila clam populations will be necessary before operational implementation. Finally, it is important to note that functional interpretations are based on genomic mapping and annotation and therefore represent hypotheses that will require further validation. Future studies integrating gene expression analyses and functional approaches, such as RNA-seq or CRISPR-based gene editing, will be essential to confirm the role of these candidate genes in resistance to Perkinsus olseni.
