Title: Optical trapping and manipulation of single particles for MDP

Executive Summary

This two-year project, conducted under Grant Number DE-SC0021030, focused on the
development and application of advanced optical trapping and manipulation technologies to
study magnetized dusty plasma (MDP) systems and enhance diagnostic capabilities at the
Magnetized Dusty Plasma Experiment (MDPX) facility at Auburn University. The central
objective was to enable precise, non-invasive control and measurement of single particles within
plasma environments, thereby addressing key limitations in existing plasma diagnostic
techniques.

Over the course of the project (September 2020 through December 2023, including a no-cost
extension), the research team successfully designed, implemented, and validated a novel optical
trapping framework tailored for large-scale plasma systems. A major innovation was the
transition from conventional microscope-objective-based trapping systems to a scalable lens-
based optical trapping configuration. This advancement overcame critical spatial and operational
constraints associated with large plasma chambers, such as the MDPX device, and established a
practical pathway for deploying optical trapping in complex plasma environments.

The project achieved several significant technical milestones. First, a fully integrated optical
trapping–plasma system was developed and tested both in laboratory radio-frequency (rf) plasma
setups and at the MDPX facility. This system enabled the trapping and manipulation of single
particles under a wide range of experimental conditions, including inside and outside the plasma
sheath. Second, the team conducted extensive measurements of particle dynamics—such as
oscillation, rotation, and transport—providing new insights into particle charge behavior, electric
field interactions, and plasma-particle coupling mechanisms. These findings contribute directly
to improving the fundamental understanding of MDP systems.

 1

A key breakthrough of the project was the first-ever demonstration of optical trapping of
particles in atmospheric plasma environments, including helium plasma jets. This achievement is
particularly notable because of the challenging conditions posed by gas flow turbulence and
competing plasma-induced forces. The results showed that optical trapping forces can overcome
Coulomb forces in plasma, validating the feasibility of optical manipulation in both atmospheric
and low-pressure plasma systems. Additionally, the successful implementation of a Universal
Optical Trapping (UOT) system at the MDPX device demonstrated that this technology can be
scaled and adapted for large experimental facilities, significantly enhancing in-situ plasma
diagnostics.

The project also produced meaningful scholarly and workforce development outcomes. It
resulted in two peer-reviewed journal publications and seven conference presentations,
contributing to the dissemination of knowledge within the plasma physics community.
Importantly, the project supported the training of one PhD student—whose research was largely
based on this work—and one undergraduate student, both of whom gained specialized expertise
in optical trapping and plasma diagnostics.

In summary, this two-year project delivered transformative advances in optical trapping
technology for plasma research. By enabling precise control and measurement of single particles
in complex plasma environments, it establishes a powerful new diagnostic tool for MDP and
related systems. These innovations not only enhance experimental capabilities at facilities like
MDPX but also open new avenues for studying plasma physics phenomena and validating
theoretical models.

manipulation technology to study MDP fundamentals and to enhance the diagnostic capability of

C. Significant results

1. A new lab-based optical-trapping plasma system with three different optical
configurations

• A new optical trapping-plasma system is developed for lab testing
• A short working distance (~ mm) of micro objectives (MOs) hinders the implementation of the
  MO-based trapping in the MDP facility which has dimensions of several tens of centers
• The current MO-based trapping is replaced with the lens-based trapping that has been
  demonstrated to be a solution to the proposed MDP experiment

Current optical trapping systems are mainly based on using micro objectives to configure a trap,
which is not only expensive but also limited to space arrangements though it has a higher trapping
power. MOs have a short working distance, e.g., a few mm to 10 mm, which can be used only in
a small-scale experimental system. In the proposed MDP experiment at Auburn University, the
plasma chamber has a dimension of 35.5 cm in inner diameter. Therefore, it is impossible to build
a trapping system using MOs. In the current project period, we have developed and tested three
different optical trapping configurations: MO-trapping, aspherical lens-trapping, and lens-
trapping. Results support that the lens-trapping configuration will be adopted in the design of an
trapping system for the MDP experiment. Figure 1 shows a picture of the experimental system
developed, in which MOs are initially used for trapping. The new experimental setup consists of
an optical trapping system, an plasma device, an real-time imaging system, and a plasma emission
spectroscopy system, and a gas flow control portion. The three different optical trapping
configurations can all be used in the setup.

2. Updated the atmospheric plasma system for particle trapping

• A 3D-printed atmospheric plasma system is constructed
• The plasma device is configured for the compatibility with the optical trapping
• A low gas flow helium plasma with minimum gas turbulence is used for particle trapping

Prior to the optical trapping at the Auburn MDP facility, a lab-based plasma system is developed
for practice particle trapping in plasma. We have updated our atmospheric plasma jet system using
1) a newly designed plasma device that was 3D printed to meet requirements of the optical trapping
configurations and space arrangements, 2) a lower gas flow rate so that trapped particles will not
be destroyed by the gas-flow turbulence, and 3) an integration of an imaging system to view the
plasma, particles, and the trapping region in real time. Figure 3(a) shows the newly developed
plasma device for the integration of an optical trapping system into it. The plasma is made up of a
dielectric cylinder of inner and outer diameters of 19.0 mm and 28.0 mm, respectively. Two
circular copper electrodes are attached on opposite sides of a centrally punctured dielectric disc of
thickness of 4 mm fixed at one end of the cylinder, and the other end of the cylinder is sealed off
with a circular aperture of 2 mm in diameter open for the connection to the helium gas flow. The
electrodes are connected via a 0.6 m low loss coaxial cable to a high voltage pulse generator (DEI
PVX-4110). The high voltage pulse generator is supplied by a high voltage power supply
(Spellman SL 1200) and a function generator with built-in arbitrary function capability (KUMAN).
In the picture shown in Figure 3(b), the plasma is generated by a square wave of 1 µs duration at
a repetition rate of 6 kHz. The voltage supplied by the high voltage power supply is fixed
at 4.4 kV (6.1 mA), with helium gas flow of 961std. ml/min.

3. Observation of particles trapped in the atmospheric helium plasma jet

  • We observed particles (carbon nanotubes) trapped in air by the plasma
  • In order to trap a particle and manipulate the trapped particle, optical trapping force must be
 stronger than the plasma-induced Coulomb’s force

Figure 4 shows detailed views of the key parts of the optical trapping-plasma system, including
the counter-propagating beams, lens-based trapping, plasma device, trapping cell, and particle
introduction. Although atmospheric plasma jets have been investigated for decades, no one has
reported on particle trapping in the plasma, either by the optical trapping or by the plasma itself.
For the first time, we observed carbon nanotube particles are trapped in the region close to the
negative electrode. We tentatively explain the phenomenon was due to the Coulomb’s force
between the particle charge and the electrode. In the experiment, by turning on/off the plasma,
keeping the illuminating beam on, we observed particles trapped and run-away, respectively.
Details can be seen in Video A (available upon request). This observation suggests that we would
have a stronger optical force than the plasma-induced Coulomb’s force in order to trap a particle
in the plasma. This finding further guides our system designs.

is on; the trapped particles are gone when the plasma is off; and then particles are trapped
again when the plasma is on again.

4. Optical trapping of particles in the atmospheric plasma

 • We report optical trapping particles in the atmospheric plasma
 • The trapping system uses simple lenses that allow for scaling-up

In the proposed MDP experiment, particles will be trapped and manipulated in the MDP plasma.
The MDP plasma is operated at reduced pressure and the plasma plume is uniform and has no gas
flow turbulence. Therefore, theoretically, it is easier to trap particles using the light force in the
MDP than in the atmospheric plasma. In this research period, we started with a tougher experiment:
trapping particles in the atmospheric plasma with constant plasma gas flow. Because the gas flow
can readily blow away a particle, trapping particles under this condition is very challenging and
no one has reported optical trapping particles in atmospheric plasma to date. We, however, have
trapped particles in two scenarios: particles trapped in the edge of the arc discharge plasma and
the downstream of the plasma jet. Figure 5 shows a single-wall carbon nanotube (SWCNT)

1) Design of an optical trapping system for the MDP experiment

 • Design of optical trapping for a large-scale application is done
 • Specifically-designed lenses will be used for the optical trapping in MDP

Due to the large scale of the MDP facility (~25 cm in diameter), current optical trapping optics
and their specifications are not applicable for particle trapping in the MDP plasma. We have tested
three different optical configurations using MOs, aspherical lenses, and lenses. Based on the results
obtained in this reporting period, we will use specially-designed lenses to form optical traps (we
called super optical trapping below) which will be deployable and configurable with the MDP
chamber. Figure 7 is the validated optical design for the super optical trapping. We are seeking
two vendors for manufacturing the special optics, such as lenses in a diameter of 5 cm and a focal
length of 15 cm, with transmission coatings at the trapping laser wavelength. In addition, we also
need large-dimension axicons (an angle of 175o) to form a hollow beam, associated large reflective
mirrors, and beam splitters (45/55), which are shown in Figure 7. Currently there is no such
trapping optics commercially available.

Abstract This experimental report details the establishment of a universal optical trapping system
for plasma diagnostics on the Magnetized Dusty Plasma Experiment (MDPX) device. Optical
trapping provides a versatile and non-invasive method for manipulating and studying charged
particles in plasmas. The experiment focused on designing and implementing an optical trapping
system to analyze and control particles within the complex environment of the MDPX device. The
results showcase the successful integration of optical trapping techniques for enhanced plasma
diagnostics.

Introduction

Plasma diagnostics are crucial for understanding the behavior of complex plasma systems, such as
those encountered in the Magnetized Dusty Plasma Experiment (MDPX). This experiment aimed
to utilize universal optical trapping techniques to facilitate non-destructive manipulation and
analysis of charged particles within the MDPX device, contributing to improved diagnostics
capabilities.

Experimental Setup

a). MDPX system b). RF plasma is installed. C). A UOT trapping system is
installed.

The experimental setup involved integrating an optical trapping system into the MDPX device. A
laser system was employed to generate counter-propagating focused laser beams for trapping
charged particles. The optical trapping system was designed to be versatile, enabling the trapping
of various particle types (1) within the dusty plasma environment of the MDPX. The primary
objective was to establish a universal optical trapping system capable of trapping charged particles
in the MDPX device. Special attention was given to adapting the optical trapping system to the
unique challenges posed by the large MDPX device (2). The UOT–RF plasma system was installed
on the MDPX device, ready for use upon our arrival, as seen in image 1. Subsequently, the 6-way
cross RF plasma was installed (image b), and finally, the UOT system was installed and aligned,

Improving the imaging system

The imaging system was enhanced in two ways. First, by employing two cameras simultaneously,
enabling the study of particle motion in both the horizontal and vertical axes. This capability will
prove highly beneficial for future particle manipulation and motion studies. Additionally, with the
use of two cameras, a second laser was introduced to illuminate the horizontal plane. This
illumination allows us to pinpoint dust particles trapped by the plasma sheath, a crucial aspect for
differentiating between sheath trapping and optical trapping and for seeing where each occurs.

The results demonstrate the successful implementation of the universal optical trapping system
within the MDPX device. Trapping efficiency was evaluated for different particle sizes and types

Discussion

The optical trapping system provides a valuable tool for plasma diagnostics on the MDPX device.
The ability to manipulate and study charged particles in situ enhances our understanding of
complex plasma interactions. The experiment lays the groundwork for further investigations into
particle dynamics and behavior within dusty plasmas.

Conclusion

 15

This experiment successfully established a universal optical trapping system for plasma
diagnostics on the Magnetized Dusty Plasma Experiment (MDPX) device. The results highlight
the versatility and effectiveness of optical trapping techniques in the challenging environment of
dusty plasmas, providing new opportunities for enhanced optical diagnostics. The study concludes
that UOT can be adapted as an optical diagnostic tool for any large plasma device.

Future Work

Future experiments could concentrate on extending the capabilities of the optical trapping system,
such as integrating real-time imaging for enhanced particle tracking. Furthermore, exploring the
application of the optical trapping system for specific plasma phenomena within the MDPX device
could further enhance its diagnostic utility, especially when the magnetic field is applied.

D. Key outcomes
1. New findings

 1.1 Observations of particles trapped in the atmospheric plasma without using optical
force. When particles are introduced into a plasma, they may charge via charge transfer from the
plasma electrons to the surface of the particles. Based on different weight, size, and formation of
the particle, it will be trapped in some place near the discharge electrode (a metal ring) based on
Coulomb’s force. Particles trapped in the sheath region of a dust plasma is a typical phenomenon.
However, the observation of particles trapped in an atmospheric plasma has not been reported to
date. It suggests that we will have to have a stronger optical force to trap particles in plasma as the
optical force will have to overcome the Coulomb’s force from the plasma. This finding helps our
optical and experimental design.

 1.2 Optical trapping of particles in the atmospheric plasma. Trapping particles in air
is challenging; but trapping a particle in an atmospheric plasma with constant plasma gas flow is
much more challenging. However, in this research, we have demonstrated trapping particles in the
downstream of the atmospheric helium plasma jet, where the optical emission spectrum shows the
plasma properties in the trapping region and the trapped particle is viewed by the embedded
imaging in real time. This finding shows a great promising of the optical trapping technology for
the study of MDP and other dust plasma. As a particle can be trapped in the atmospheric plasma,
there is no reason that a particle cannot be trapped in an MDP which is operating at reduced
pressure and of no gas-flow turbulence.

 Impact

A. Impact within Discipline

 20

Our knowledge of MDP is far from completion and the exploration of magnetization phenomena
has just started due to its complicated nature. As MDP involves E, B, electrons, ions, and charged
particles, a light-based optical tool or diagnostic technique such as video imaging or light-force is
the best option to study MPD since it has negligible influence on plasma conditions. However,
none of these optical tools currently used can provide control of a single particle in MDP, e.g.,
trapping a particle at a specific spot as desired or controlling its motion in 3D. The proposed
research of optical trapping in plasma will generate technological breakthrough and enable
scientists to better understand MDP using new experimental data that is no available based on the
current plasma diagnostic techniques.

The research activities during this reporting period have generated two technological
breakthroughs:
 • Integration of an optical trapping system into an atmospheric plasma system, which
 has not been reported to date. This breakthrough will bring a lot of opportunities to
 study plasma using a particle as a trace or a probe.
 • Trapping single particles in the low temperature atmospheric plasma, which has not
 been reported before. This breakthrough enables scientists to use optical trapping
 as a new plasma diagnostic tool.
 • Using asymmetric trapping configuration, we demonstrated the plasma particle
 transport in a large plasma voume, for the first time.

Optical Trapping of Single Dust Particles for In-Situ Plasma Diagnostics
Pubuduni Ekanayaka,1 Chuji Wang,1* Saikat Chakraborty Thakur,2 And Edward Thomas 2
1 Mississippi State University, Starkville, MS, 39759, USA

2 Auburn University, Auburn, AL, 36849, USA

*cw175@msstate.edu.

Abstract
Plasma diagnostics remains one of the grand challenges in the field. Optical trapping (OT) uses photon radiation pressure and/or
photophoretic force to levitate, trap, or transport micron-sized particles in different media including air, solutions, and reactive
gases. Recently, this technology has been demonstrated in plasmas, showing great promise as a new tool using a single dust particle
for in situ noninvasive plasma diagnostics. Here, we present our recent developments in OT of single dust particles in various
plasmas. We demonstrate that a trapped particle can be smoothly transported in RF dusty plasmas from one location to another.
Additionally, we showcase high-precision measurements of the electric field in an argon RF dusty plasma using a single trapped
particle. We also discuss the further potential for in situ, noninvasive plasma diagnostics at the microscopic level using micron-
sized particles.

Introduction
 Dust-containing (dusty or complex) plasmas find diverse applications in industry and present fascinating phenomena in nature.
These include semiconductor fabrication, material processing, investigations into plasma-material interactions, and studies of
impurity transport in plasma experiments. They also extend to the study of comets, planetary rings, and other celestial phenomena.1-
7 One intriguing aspect of dusty plasmas is the varied impact of charged dust particles, which can be detrimental, beneficial, or

neutral depending on the specific case and the methods employed to control and stabilize them within the plasma. Dust particles,
whether stationary or in motion, individually or collectively, can serve as effective probes for plasma diagnostics, influencing nearly
all plasma parameters such as electron, ion, and neutral atom or molecule densities, electron temperature, electric field (E), electric
potential (V), sheath structure, and more.
 When a particle is levitated in its sheath equilibrium position in a dusty plasma by balancing the electrostatic force Fe,
gravitational force Fg, ion drag force Fid, neutral drag force Fnd, and thermophoretic force, it can be set in motion by various methods,
observations of subsequent motions can lead to measurement of the basic plasma parameters, such as the particle charge Q, charge
variations, E, V, inter-particle coupling, sheath structure, etc. For example, using the driven damped oscillation model of a particle
levitated in the plasma sheath, the particle charge Q was estimated;9-10 the plasma crystals melt-up was studied by a laser sheet
interacting with an array of particles levitated in the plasma sheath.11 However, due to the constraint of gravity which restricts dust
particle experiments to the plasma sheath where charged particles are levitated.9-12 Unless the micro-gravity experiments, a ground-
based laboratory experiment would require additional force to eliminate the constraint of gravity, enabling dust particles to be
levitated beyond the plasma sheath region. Thanks to the optical trapping (OT) technology that has been demonstrated in recent
experiments to be able to trap and transport single dust particles in any location of the whole plasma volume.13-15
 The concept of OT and detailed calculations of optical trapping forces, e.g., radiation pressure and photophoretic forces, can
be seen elsewhere.16-17 But their orders of magnitudes can be illustrated, for example, using a carbon nanofoam particle of 2 µm in
diameter and under the illumination of a laser beam at 10 W/mm2. The thermal conductivity is 0.7 Wm/K at room temperature. The
density of bulk carbon nanotubes is 140 kg/m3. The estimated radiation pressure force and photophoretic force are ~3×10-15 N and
~4×10-11 N, respectively. The gravitational force is ~1×10-16 N. If the particle’s initial and settling speeds are 1 m/s and ~10 mm/s
in air, respectively, the drag force is ~1×10-13 N. Depending on particle materials, e.g., light absorbing, nonabsorbing, or between,
one of the two types of optical forces can be more dominant or even serval orders of magnitude larger than the other. For a charged
particle in a dusty plasma, OT forces can be an effective component of the forces that levitate the particle or drive the particle’s
motion in the plasma.
 Different from the optical configuration used in the initial demonstration of particle levitation, in which a single tightly focused
laser beam (termed as optical tweezers) was used,18 in our studies, two counter-propagating hollow beams are used to form a
universal optical trap (UOT).19-20 The UOT is capable of trapping single particles with diverse properties, ranging from transparent
to absorbing, from inorganic to biological, from solids to droplets, and from spherical to irregularly shaped particles, spanning size
from sub-micron to approximately 50 µm in various media including air, solution, reactive gases, and plasmas. Moreover, the UOT
offers high trapping rigidity and trapping efficiency, attributes particularly crucial for plasma trapping. Here, we highlight our latest
results involving the trapping and transporting of single particles in plasmas using a UOT and high-precision measurements of
electric field using a single trapped particle in an RF plasma.

Results and discussion
 Figure 1 shows images of a single particle trapped in three different plasmas. Fig. 1(a) shows a single carbon nanotube particle
of 10 -20 µm in size trapped downstream of an atmospheric pressure argon plasma jet. Fig. 1(b) illustrates a single carbon nanotube
particle trapped near an atmospheric DC discharge. Fig. 1(c) depicts single carbon nanotube particles trapped within a dielectric
barrier discharge. Each trapped particle is circled, and an image of the plasma is shown in the inset. Trapping particles in
atmospheric plasmas such as a plasma jet, DC discharge, or DBD is extremely challenging due to open-air turbulence and
interactions with plasma gas flow (Figs.1(a) and 1(c)), which pose significant hurdles in stabilizing trapped particles. Even with
the compact size of these plasmas, microscope objectives are impractical for forming a UOT due to their short working distance.
In these plasma trapping experiments (and others detailed below), all UOTs are created using optical lenses with a low numerical
aperture. These experiments demonstrate the feasibility of trapping particles in atmospheric plasmas. Further development efforts
are encouraged to improve particle trapping in atmospheric plasmas.

Figure 1. Optical trapping of single particles in atmospheric plasmas, (a) atmospheric argon plasma jet, (b) DC discharge in air,
(c) helium DBD in air. The insets show corresponding plasma images.

 Figure 2 shows images of a single particle trapped in an RF dusty plasma generated by a 13.56 MHz RF plasma generator (RF
VII, RF-3-XIII) and confined in a metal chamber (30 cm x 30 cm x 20 cm).15 The input RF power ranged from 1.0 to 10.0 W,
operating at gas pressure from 2.7 Pa to 27 Pa. Fig.2 (a, b, and c) demonstrates that the particle can be trapped in various locations
inside the plasma: at the left edge of the electrode (a), the center of the electrode (b), and the right edge of the electrode (c), viewed
from a side perspective perpendicular to both the gravitational field and the optical axis of the UOT. Similarly, particles can be
trapped at different heights above the electrode surface. The trapped particle in Fig. 2 is a 6.2 µm glass sphere. The RF plasma
electrode is illuminated by scattered light from the 532-nm trapping laser beam, while a blue illuminating beam is used for imaging
the trapped particle in the plasma. In experiments, glass spheres of different diameters (200 nm, 2.0 µm, 6.2 µm) were also
successfully trapped in the plasma. Furthermore, strongly absorbing carbon nanotube particles, with extremely different material
properties in terms of light absorbance and particle morphology, were trapped in the plasma under various operating conditions.
These experiments demonstrate that micro dust particles of diverse properties (light absorbance, size, and morphology) can be
optically trapped in rf plasmas.

 Figure 2. Side views of optical trapping of single particles in an RF argon dusty plasma in various locations, (a) the left edge of
the electrode, (b) the center of the electrode, and (c) the right edge of the electrode. The trapped particle is a 6.2 µm glass sphere.

 One of our research objectives is to develop a single-particle probe for deployment in a dusty plasma facility. The initial
challenge is to integrate an optical trapping system into an existing plasma system. All optical designs and configurations must be
tailored to fit within the predefined space and geometry of the plasma system while ensuring sufficient optical trapping robustness
and efficiency. Additionally, we must consider unavoidable mechanical vibrations and potential electromagnetic interference, which
affect the optical trapping system. In our first optical trapping experiment in the MDPX system,21 as shown in Fig. 3(a), single
particles were successfully trapped. Specifically, 6.2 µm glass spheres were introduced into the plasma and trapped near the
geometric center of the plasma volume above the electrode surface, as shown in Fig. 3(b, c), in which (b) and (c) are the side and
top views respectively. In addition to the optically trapped particle (circled), there are some particles trapped in the sheath, as
viewed from the top. During this ten-day experiment, no attempts were made to manipulate trapped particles in the plasma, nor
were measurements of plasma parameters carried out using the trapped particle. Future developments are focused on upgrading the

 Smoothly transporting a particle in plasma is crucial for using the single particle as a probe in plasma diagnostics. In the
current studies of dusty plasmas using particles levitated in the plasma, the particles cannot be levitated beyond the plasma sheath.
However, we use optical trapping to demonstrate that a single particle can be trapped in any location and smoothly transported
from one location to another in the whole plasma volume. Figure 4 shows a single glass sphere being trapped and transported along
the x-axis, originating from the center of the trap and perpendicular to the gravitational field. This particle transport was achieved
by adjusting the trapping laser power to change the trapping forces. In Fig. 4 (left-a), as the laser power was decreased incrementally,
the trapped glass sphere moved step by step from right to left along the optical axis of the trapping beams (also along the x-axis);
similarly, increasing the laser power caused the same trapped particle to move stepwise from left to right, as shown in Fig. 4(left-
b). The right side of the figure shows the results of transporting a nonspherical, carbon nanotube particle back and forth along the
x-axis in the plasma.

and a carbon nanotube particle (right-a,b) back and forth in the plasma along the x-axis that is perpendicular to the gravitational

 Electric field E and particle charge Q are two crucial parameters in dusty plasma, which influence key plasma properties such
as electron, ion, and neutral atom or molecule densities, electron temperature, electric potential, and sheath structure. In many cases,
E at a location where a charged particle resides can be determined given a known Q, and vice versa. When a charged particle is
optically trapped within a dusty plasma, its subsequent motions are primarily governed by electrostatic force Fe, gravitational force
Fg, ion drag force Fid, and neutral drag force Fnd when optical trapping forces are off. Assuming negligible speeds of the particle
and the absence of other nearby particles, Fid and Fnd can be neglected. As Fg remains unchanged since the particle’s mass is constant
and Fe varies proportionally with E as Q is a constant within a small region in the plasma, the particle’s motions are driven by
changes in E. Following this principle, we trapped a single particle in a specific location and recorded its subsequent motions when
the trapping force was off. Using a known particle charge Q derived from a similar particle (same size and material) charged in a
RF plasma under identical operational conditions (input RF power, plasma gas, pressure, and configuration), we measured E along
the particle's trajectory. Fig.5(a) presents the recoded particle’s motion trajectories in the x-z plane, and Fig.5 (b) shows results
resulting from the analysis of the x- and z- components of each trajectory, leading to corresponding Ex and Ez components. Two
sets of data in Figs. 5(a) and (b) are from the repeated measurements involved in trapping a single particle in the approximately
same (x, z) location where the particle started its motions after the trapping force was off. The data in (a) and (b) show good
reproducibility. The measured E distributions have a spatial resolution of up to tens of microns along the z-axis. Correspondingly,
the precision of E measurements exceeds 2 V/m. Note that the trapped-and-released particle travels in a longer distance along the
x-axis than along the z-axis. This disparity is because Ex remains approximately constant in the traveled range along the x-axis,
while Ez changes drastically in the z-direction, as illustrated in Fig. 5(b).22,23

Figure 5. High precision mapping electric field E around a localized position using a trapped particle, with a space resolution of
micrometers. (a) Recorded motion trajectories of the single trapped particle when the trapping force was off, (b) measured Ez
distribution along the z-axis. The inset in (b) shows that Ex is nearly the same in the range of 5000 µm. The color and symbol
denote repeated measurements.

Concluding remarks
 Our recent experiments demonstrate the capability to trap single particles in several types of low-temperature plasmas
operating at either atmospheric or reduced pressure. Current measurements of plasma parameters using a single trapped particle
involve using a known parameter to determine another, such as using a known Q to measure E, and vice versa. In future studies,
absolute measurements of plasma parameters, e.g., both E and Q, are expected, and the study of dusty plasma dynamics using a
single trapped particle as a probe is also highly conceivable. Unlike conventional plasma probes such as Langmuir probes, a single-
particle probe is nonintrusive and offers high spatial resolution. By overcoming gravitational constraints, a single particle can be
trapped anywhere within the entire plasma volume, extending beyond the sheath region where conventional studies using levitated
particles as a probe have been limited.
 Optical trapping and manipulation of single dust particles in plasma represent an emerging research frontier, with only a few
experiments reported. Nevertheless, results have shown promising potential for a new plasma diagnostic tool employing a single
micron-sized particle as an in-situ probe to measure plasma parameters with high precision. The optical trapping technology also
offers a unique approach to studying plasma-particle interactions at a microscopic scale.

 ABSTRACT
 We report the experimental realization of optical trapping and controlled manipulations of single particles of arbitrary properties, e.g.,
 nano- to micrometer in size, transparent spheres to strongly light absorbing nonspherical particles, in low-pressure rf plasmas. First, we show
 optical trapping and transport of single particles in an unmagnetized rf plasma. Then, we show similar observations in a weakly magnetized

 rf plasma. This is the first demonstration of actively transporting (pushing and pulling) light-absorbing, nonspherical single particles in plas-
 mas. The result suggests that optically trapped, actively controlled, single plasma dust particles (not limited to those externally sampled
 spheres) could be an in situ micro-probe for dusty plasma and magnetized dusty plasma diagnostics.
