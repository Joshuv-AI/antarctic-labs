1.
Introduction and statement of results

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a smooth projective curve of genus
g
over a field of
characteristic
p>0
.
The curve

\mathcal{C}
is
ordinary
if, in terms of the
zeta function of
\mathcal{C}
, the middle coefficient of the numerator is
prime to
p
. For an elliptic curve, this middle coefficient
is the classical
Hasse invariant
.
In higher genus, the analogous object is the Hasse–Witt matrix,
or its dual the
Cartier–Manin matrix,
and ordinarity is detected by the nonvanishing of
its determinant.
Equivalently,
\mathcal{C}
is ordinary if its Jacobian is an ordinary
abelian variety, that is, its Newton polygon has
only slopes
0
and
1
.

The ordinary locus in the moduli space of curves
\mathcal{M}_{g}
is
open, dense, geometrically irreducible, and the corresponding homomorphism

\pi_{1}\rightarrow\mathrm{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is surjective, see
[
FG04
,
FC90
]
.
Analogous statements hold for the ordinary locus in the hyperelliptic locus
\mathcal{H}_{g}

(see
[
GP05
,
AP08
]
, and also
[
PZ12
]
for the characteristic-
2
case).

Given a one-parameter family of hyperelliptic curves over a number field,
with “large monodromy”— that is, whose topological monodromy is of finite index in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
— does it always meet the ordinary locus in
\mathcal{H}_{g}
for every prime
p
?
Katz’s family

\mathcal{K}:y^{2}=x^{d}-dx+t,

over (the smooth locus of) the
t
-line has large monodromy
(as shown in
[
Kat14
]
). However,

\mathcal{K}
may fail to be generically ordinary for small
p

(see
[
Kat18
]
).

Conjecture 1.1

(Katz 2018)
.

Let
d\in\{2g+1,2g+2\},
and
g\geq 2
.
There exists a quadratic polynomial
P^{+}(d)

such that the family
\mathcal{K}
is generically ordinary
at
p
for all
p>P^{+}(d)
.

Remark

(The elliptic curve case)
.

In the case
g=1
(that is
d=3,4
) the short Weierstrass family

y^{2}=x^{d}+\alpha x+t
over the
t
-line
is generically ordinary at every prime
p>3
and
p\nmid\alpha
.
The case
g=2
can be proved by Will Sawin’s argument

[
Saw16
]
.
This paper settles this conjecture for all
g\geq 2
.

We use the following standing notation throughout the paper:

Notation 1.2
.

Let
d\in\{2g+1,2g+2\}
and
g\geq 2
.
Let
\alpha\neq 0
be an algebraic integer and let

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(t):\quad y^{2}=x^{d}+\alpha x+t.

Let
D_{\alpha}(t)
denote
the discriminant
\mathrm{disc}_{x}(x^{d}+\alpha x+t)
.
Denote

\mathcal{S}^{\circ}\coloneqq\mathrm{Spec}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)}[1/2,t,D_{\alpha}(t)^{-1}].

Let

P^{+}(d)=\begin{cases}d^{2}-4d+2&\mbox{ if }d=2g+1\\
(d^{2}-3d+2)/2&\mbox{ if }d=2g+2.\end{cases}

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\alpha
be a nonzero algebraic integer and let
F=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
.
Then for every prime
p>P^{+}(d)
and
p\nmid N_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)
,
the family
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
of genus-
g
curves

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\circ}

is generically ordinary at each prime
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
of
F
.

Remark 1.4
.

Notice that the Katz family
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{C}_{-d}
is a special case;
hence Conjecture
1.1
follows from Theorem
1.3
.

Remark 1.5
.

The bound
P^{+}(d)
cannot be weakened to
p\geq P^{+}(d)
in the theorem.
The data provided by Katz in
[
Kat18
]

shows that the generic ordinarity fails for
p=P^{+}(d)

in the following cases:

(d,p)=(21,359),(31,839),(51,2399),

and further computations suggest the same for

(65,3967),(105,10607).

In all of these cases,
d
is odd and
p\equiv 2\bmod d
.
See Corollary
4.5
for a refinement of the
bound in some cases.

Remark 1.6
.

Katz showed in
[
Kat14
]

that for any Morse polynomial
h(x)
of degree
d

the family of curves
y^{2}=h(x)+t
has large monodromy.
It is straightforward to verify by definition
that
h(x)=x^{d}+\alpha x

in characteristic
0
is Morse if and only if
\alpha
is nonzero.
In characteristic
p
it is Morse if and only if
p\nmid d(d-1)
and

\alpha
is nonzero.
This shows that
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
has topological monodromy of finite index in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
. Hence Theorem
1.3
shows that
part of Conjecture 1 of
[
Kat18
]
holds for this family

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
.

For
g\geq 2
we have
P^{+}(d)>d
. Every
prime
p>P^{+}(d)>d
implies
p\nmid d(d-1)
.
At every prime
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
for which
\alpha\not\in\mathfrak{p}
,
the polynomial

x^{d}+\alpha x\bmod\mathfrak{p}
is Morse.
This implies, by Katz’s argument
[
Kat14
]
, that

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
at
\mathfrak{p}
has large monodromy.

Our result lies in the tradition of Miller’s paper
[
Mil72
]
.
Miller exhibited two families over the
t
-line

\begin{cases}y^{2}&=x^{2g+1}+tx^{g+1}+x\quad\mbox{ if $p\nmid g$}\\
y^{2}&=x^{2g+2}+tx^{g+1}+1\quad\mbox{ if $p\mid g$}\end{cases}

are generically ordinary.
The first one was in fact proposed by
J. Lubin and N. Katz (unpublished).
For Miller’s families, the coefficient matrix has the support of a permutation
matrix. By contrast, the matrix for
\mathcal{K}

is generally not monomial and
can contain many nonzero entries, so its determinant need not reduce to a single product.
A different and new approach is needed.

This paper is organized as follows.
We present in Section 2 how to evaluate a determinant

\Delta
and prove that prime factors of
\Delta
are bounded above by
P^{+}(d)
.
In Section 3, we define the Hasse–Witt polynomial
H_{p,\alpha}
,
of
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
over
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)}
,
and show that it is nonzero at each prime
\mathfrak{p}\mid p

for
p>P^{+}(d)
and
p\nmid N_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)
.
This is accomplished by showing
its leading coefficient is, up to a
p
-adic unit factor,
congruent to
\Delta
mod
p
.
In Section 4 we prove the generic ordinarity
of the family
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(t)
. Theorem
4.1

proves Theorem
1.3
.
We then deduce Katz’s Conjecture
1.1
in
Corollary
4.2
.
As a natural application we also derive a similar result for a two-parameter family over the affine plane
in Corollary
4.3
.

2.
Prime factors of a determinant

2.1.
Product formula for a binomial determinant

The goal of this subsection is to produce a product formula for a determinant
\Delta
in Proposition
2.4
.
The core technique in the proof is to reduce it to a
Vandermonde-type determinant, which has a natural product formula.

For any integer
1\leq r\leq g-1
we define a positive
rational number

(1)

\lambda_{r}=\frac{d(2r+1)}{2(d-1)}.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
1\leq r\leq g-1
,
\lambda_{r}
is not an integer.

Proof.

First notice
3\leq 2r+1<d-1
.
If
d
is odd, then
(2(d-1),d)=1
. Since
2(d-1)\nmid(2r+1)
,
\lambda_{r}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
.
If
d
is even, then
\lambda_{r}=\frac{\frac{d}{2}(2r+1)}{d-1}
.
Since
(d-1,\frac{d}{2})=1
and
d-1\nmid 2r+1
,
\lambda_{r}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
P_{j}(T)=\prod_{r=1}^{j-1}(T-r)\prod_{r=j}^{g-1}(T-\lambda_{r}).

Let

P(x_{1},\dots,x_{g})=\det_{1\leq i,j\leq g}P_{j}(x_{i}).

Then

P(x_{1},\dots,x_{g})=\displaystyle\prod_{i<j}(x_{j}-x_{i})\displaystyle\prod_{r=1}^{g-1}\prod_{k=1}^{r}(k-\lambda_{r}).

Proof.

The determinant
P
is alternating in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{g}
,
hence
\prod_{i<j}(x_{j}-x_{i})
divides
P
.
Since
x_{i}
occurs only in the
i
-th row and each
P_{j}
has degree
g-1
, we have

\deg_{x_{i}}P\leq g-1
for each
i
.
Meanwhile,
\prod_{i<j}(x_{j}-x_{i})
has degree exactly
g-1
in each
x_{i}
,
hence

(2)

P(x_{1},\dots,x_{g})=C\prod_{i<j}(x_{j}-x_{i})

for some constant
C
.
To compute
C
, set
x_{i}=i
for
1\leq i\leq g
.
The matrix
(P_{j}(i))_{i,j}
is lower triangular with diagonal entries

P_{i}(i)=\prod_{r=1}^{i-1}(i-r)\prod_{r=i}^{g-1}(i-\lambda_{r})=(i-1)!\prod_{r=i}^{g-1}(i-\lambda_{r}).

Therefore, (
2
) becomes

\displaystyle P(1,2,\dots,g)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{g}P_{i}(i)=\prod_{i=1}^{g}\left((i-1)!\prod_{r=i}^{g-1}(i-\lambda_{r})\right)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{g}(i-1)!\right)\prod_{i=1}^{g}\prod_{r=i}^{g-1}(i-\lambda_{r})

\displaystyle=C\prod_{i<j}(j-i)=C\prod_{i=1}^{g}(i-1)!.

Thus we get

C=\prod_{i=1}^{g}\prod_{r=i}^{g-1}(i-\lambda_{r})=\prod_{r=1}^{g-1}\prod_{k=1}^{r}(k-\lambda_{r}).

Substituting
C
back to (
2
), this finishes the proof.
∎

We define for any nonnegative integer
k
,

\binom{z}{k}=\frac{z(z-1)\cdots(z-k+1)}{k!}

and set
\binom{z}{k}=0
for
k<0
.
Moreover, if
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
z<k
then
\binom{z}{k}=0
.
For the rest of the paper
fix a set

\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\{1,2,\ldots,d-1\}\backslash\{\frac{d}{2}\}.

When
d
is odd, the exclusion is vacuous.
Let
s_{1},\ldots,s_{g}\in\mathcal{A}
be distinct integers.
Let

(3)

\beta_{i}\coloneqq\frac{s_{i}}{d}-\frac{1}{2}.

Remark 2.3
.

Note that
\frac{-1}{2}<\beta_{i}<\frac{1}{2}
and
\beta_{i}\neq 0
.
This implies that
\binom{\beta_{i}}{s_{i}-1}\neq 0
.

The determinant
\Delta
below
will later occur as the reduction mod
p

of a deciding factor
\Delta_{p}

in the leading coefficient of the Hasse–Witt polynomial.
(See Proposition
3.7
.)

Proposition 2.4

(Product formula)
.

Define

\Delta(s_{1},\ldots,s_{g})=\det_{1\leq i,j\leq g}\binom{\beta_{i}}{s_{i}-j}.

Then we have

\boxed{\Delta(s_{1},\ldots,s_{g})=\left(\frac{-d}{d-1}\right)^{G}\displaystyle\prod_{i<j}(s_{j}-s_{i})\cdot\displaystyle\left(\prod_{r=1}^{g-1}\prod_{k=1}^{r}(k-\lambda_{r})\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{i=1}^{g}\frac{\binom{\beta_{i}}{s_{i}-1}}{\prod_{r=1}^{g-1}(s_{i}-\lambda_{r})}\right)}

where
G=g(g-1)/2
.
Moreover,
\Delta
is a nonzero rational number.

Proof.

We first reduce this determinant to
P(s_{1},\ldots,s_{g})
from above.
Notice from Lemma
2.1
that
s_{i}-\lambda_{r}\neq 0
since
s_{i}
is integer and

\lambda_{r}
is not, so the denominators on the right-hand side are nonzero.
Thus the right-hand side of the formula is well-defined.

Since
\binom{\beta_{i}}{s_{i}-1}\neq 0
by Remark
2.3
,
we rewrite

(4)

\Delta(s_{1},\ldots,s_{g})=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{g}\binom{\beta_{i}}{s_{i}-1}\right)\det_{1\leq i,j\leq g}K_{ij},

where

K_{ij}\coloneqq\frac{\binom{\beta_{i}}{s_{i}-j}}{\binom{\beta_{i}}{s_{i}-1}}.

For
j=1
,
K_{i1}=1
; for
j\geq 2

we have

K_{ij}=\prod_{r=1}^{j-1}\frac{s_{i}-r}{\beta_{i}-s_{i}+r+1}=\left(\frac{-d}{d-1}\right)^{j-1}\frac{1}{\prod_{r=1}^{g-1}(s_{i}-\lambda_{r})}\cdot P_{j}(s_{i}).

Thus by Lemma
2.2
,

\displaystyle\det_{1\leq i,j\leq g}(K_{ij})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{-d}{d-1}\right)^{G}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{g}\prod_{r=1}^{g-1}(s_{i}-\lambda_{r})}P(s_{1},\dots,s_{g})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{-d}{d-1}\right)^{G}\cdot\frac{\prod_{i<j}(s_{j}-s_{i})\prod_{r=1}^{g-1}\prod_{k=1}^{r}(k-\lambda_{r})}{\prod_{i=1}^{g}\prod_{r=1}^{g-1}(s_{i}-\lambda_{r})}.

Substituting this into (
4
) proves our formula.

We see that
\Delta
is a nonzero rational number
since the
s_{i}
are distinct, and
\lambda_{r}

is not integer by Lemma
2.1
, so
s_{i}-s_{j}\neq 0
and
k-\lambda_{r}\neq 0
.
As we have seen above,
\binom{\beta_{i}}{s_{i}-1}\neq 0
,
hence all factors in the numerator are nonzero.
∎

2.2.
A quadratic bound for the prime factors

To obtain the desired bound
P^{+}(d)
it is not sufficient to
simply estimate each factor in the product formula of
\Delta
in Proposition

2.4
.
It is necessary to take certain cancellations into
account (see Lemma
2.6
).

Consider two regions in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
:

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\mid g+1\leq x\leq d-1,g\leq y\leq x-1,x\in\mathcal{A}\}

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\mid 1\leq x<g,x\leq y\leq g-1,x\in\mathcal{A}\}.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
h(x,y)=(2d-2)x-2dy-d
.
Then
h
is an integral-valued function on
U
and
V

such that

\max_{U}|h(x,y)|=\begin{cases}d^{2}-4d+2&\mbox{ if $d=2g+1$}\\
d^{2}-3d+2&\mbox{ if $d=2g+2$}.\end{cases}

\max_{V}|h(x,y)|=\begin{cases}d^{2}-4d+2&\mbox{ if $d=2g+1$}\\
d^{2}-5d+2&\mbox{ if $d=2g+2$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

(1) We opt to give a detailed and direct proof for
the first case about
U
.
Since
h
is a linear function in both variables, its extreme values are achieved
at vertices of the convex hull of its (lattice) domain
U
.
We separate into two cases:

\bullet

If
d=2g+1
then the vertex set of the convex hull of

U
is
\{(g+1,g),(2g,g),(2g,2g-1)\}
.
Let

h_{\min}
and
h_{\max}
denote the minimal and maximal values at
these vertices, respectively. Then

\displaystyle h_{\min}

\displaystyle=\min(h(g+1,g),h(2g,g),h(2g,2g-1))=2-d,

\displaystyle h_{\max}

\displaystyle=\max(h(g+1,g),h(2g,g),h(2g,2g-1))=d^{2}-4d+2.

It follows that
\max_{U}|h(x,y)|=\max(h_{\max},-h_{\min})=d^{2}-4d+2.

\bullet
If
d=2g+2
then
x\neq g+1
, so the vertex set of
U

is
\{(g+2,g),(2g+1,g),(2g+1,2g),(g+2,g+1)\}
. Then

h_{\min}=2-d
and
h_{\max}=d^{2}-3d+2
. It follows that
\max_{U}|h(x,y)|=d^{2}-3d+2
.

(2) The vertex set of the convex hull of
V
is

\{(1,1),(1,g-1),(g-1,g-1)\}
for all
d
.
For
d=2g+1
,
h_{\min}=h(1,g-1)=-(d^{2}-4d+2)

and
h_{\max}=h(1,1)=-d-2
, hence
\max_{V}|h(x,y)|=d^{2}-4d+2
.
For
d=2g+2
, we have

h_{\min}=h(1,g-1)=-(d^{2}-5d+2)
and

h_{\max}=h(1,1)=-d-2
, thus in this case

\max_{V}|h(x,y)|=d^{2}-5d+2
.
∎

Let

\displaystyle J_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{r}(k-\lambda_{r})\qquad\mbox{for $1\leq r\leq g-1$,}

\displaystyle R_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{\frac{s}{d}-\frac{1}{2}}{s-1}}{\prod_{r=1}^{g-1}(s-\lambda_{r})}\qquad\mbox{ for $s\in\mathcal{A}$}.

For any
\zeta\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
, by a
prime factor
of
\zeta
,
we mean a prime
p
such that
v_{p}(\zeta)\neq 0
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Every prime factor of

J_{r}
or
R_{s}

is at most
P^{+}(d)
.

Proof.

(1) We first consider
J_{r}
.
Notice

k-\lambda_{r}=\frac{2(d-1)k-(2r+1)d}{2(d-1)}=\frac{h(k,r)}{2(d-1)}

where
h(k,r)
ranges over

W:=\{(k,r)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}|1\leq k\leq r\leq g-1\}
, whose convex hull has
vertices
\{(1,1),(1,g-1),(g-1,g-1)\}
.
When
d=2g+1
,
|h(k,r)|\leq d^{2}-4d+2
;
when
d=2g+2
, each
h(k,r)
is even, so each prime factor is bounded by

\max\{2,|h(k,r)/2|\}\leq(d^{2}-5d+2)/2
.
On the other hand, prime factors in the denominator of
k-\lambda_{r}
are bounded by

d-1\leq P^{+}(d)
.
This proves the statement for
J_{r}
.

(2) We now consider
R_{s}
.
Write its numerator and denominator
as quotients of products of integers:

\displaystyle\prod_{r=1}^{g-1}(s-\lambda_{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{r=1}^{g-1}((2s-2r-1)d-2s)}{(2(d-1))^{g-1}}

\displaystyle\binom{\frac{s}{d}-\frac{1}{2}}{s-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{2s-d}{2d}\cdot\frac{2s-d-2d}{2d}\cdot\frac{2s-d-4d}{2d}\cdot\cdots\frac{2s-d-2(s-2)d}{2d}}{(s-1)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{\ell=1}^{s-1}((2\ell-1)d-2s)}{(-1)^{s-1}(s-1)!(2d)^{s-1}}.

Taking their quotient, we have the following factorization:

R_{s}=\frac{2^{g-s}(d-1)^{g-1}}{(-1)^{s-1}(s-1)!d^{s-1}}R_{s}^{\sharp}

where

R_{s}^{\sharp}:=\frac{\prod_{\ell=1}^{s-1}((2\ell-1)d-2s)}{\prod_{r=1}^{g-1}((2s-2r-1)d-2s)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\star)}}{{=}}\frac{\prod_{\ell=1}^{s-1}h(s,\ell)}{\prod_{\ell=1}^{g-1}h(s,\ell)}.

The last equality
(\star)
is by
reindexing the numerator via
\ell\mapsto s-\ell
,
as it permutes
\{1,\ldots,s-1\}
.
The first factor of
R_{s}
contains no prime factor
>d
,
so it remains to consider
R_{s}^{\sharp}
.
The function
h(s,\ell)
is integer-valued,
its domain is either
U
or
V
depending on where
s
lies:

(case 1): Suppose
g\leq s\leq d-1
.
Then

R_{s}^{\sharp}=\prod_{\ell=g}^{s-1}h(s,\ell).

For
s=g
,
R_{s}^{\sharp}=1
, there are no remaining factors.
It remains to consider
g+1\leq s\leq d-1
. Hence the function
h(s,\ell)
ranges in the domain
A
. By Lemma
2.5
,
for
d=2g+1
, we have
|h(s,\ell)|\leq d^{2}-4d+2
;
for
d=2g+2
, the integer
h(s,\ell)
is even on the domain
U
,
so every odd prime factor already divides
\frac{h(s,\ell)}{2}
.
Hence each prime factor is bounded by
\max\{2,|\frac{h(s,\ell)}{2}|\}\leq(d^{2}-3d+2)/2

by the same lemma.

(case 2): Suppose
1\leq s<g
.
Then

R_{s}^{\sharp}=\prod_{\ell=s}^{g-1}h(s,\ell)^{-1}.

In this case the function
h(s,\ell)
has its domain
V
.
For
d=2g+1
, we have
|h(s,\ell)|\leq d^{2}-4d+2
;
For
d=2g+2
, the integer
h(s,\ell)
is even on its domain,
hence each odd prime factor divides
h(s,\ell)/2
and hence
every prime factor is at most

\max\{2,|h(s,\ell)/2|\}\leq(d^{2}-5d+2)/2
, by Lemma
2.5
.

In summary, we have shown that for
d=2g+1
, any prime factor

p\leq\max(d^{2}-4d+2,d)=P^{+}(d)
; for
d=2g+2
,
any prime factor
p\leq\max((d^{2}-3d+2)/2,d)=P^{+}(d)
.
∎

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
s_{1},\ldots,s_{g}\in\mathcal{A}
be distinct integers.
Each prime factor of
\Delta
is at most
P^{+}(d)
.
If
p>P^{+}(d)
, then
\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{(p)}
and

\Delta\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
.

Proof.

Since
1\leq s_{i},s_{j}\leq d-1
, we have
|s_{j}-s_{i}|\leq d-2<d.

The factors
s_{i}-s_{j}
and the prefactor
(-d/(d-1))^{G}
contribute only prime factors at most
d
.
The remaining factor is

\prod_{r=1}^{g-1}J_{r}\prod_{i=1}^{g}R_{s_{i}}
, whose prime
factors
are at most
P^{+}(d)
by Lemma
2.6
.
This proves the first statement.

For any
p>P^{+}(d)
, our first statement
implies that both the numerator and denominator of
\Delta

are
p
-adic units.
Thus
\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}^{*}
and
\Delta\not\equiv 0\bmod p
.
∎

3.
Hasse–Witt polynomial over
\mathbb{Z}

For the rest of the paper let
p
be a prime coprime to
2d
.
In this section, we define Hasse–Witt polynomial with integral coefficients in one or two variables, then show that they are nonzero
at primes lying over
p
when
p
is large enough.

We use
the following notation for the rest of the paper:

Q_{i}=\left\lfloor\frac{p\,i}{d}\right\rfloor,\quad s_{i}=[p\,i]_{d},\quad\mbox{ for $1\leq i\leq g$}.

Here
[N]_{d}
represents the least nonnegative residue
N\bmod d
.
Once
p
is given,
s_{i}
can be considered a function of
i
.

3.1.
Leading term in the coefficient polynomial

Lemma 3.1
.

Then
s_{1},\ldots,s_{g}
are distinct elements in
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Since
p
and
d
are coprime, the integers
s_{i}
’s are distinct and lie in
\{1,2,\ldots,d-1\}
for
i=1,\dots,g
.
It remains to show that for
d=2g+2
we have
s_{i}\neq d/2
.
Suppose there exists
1\leq i\leq g
such that
\frac{d}{2}=s_{i}
.
Write
a=(p\bmod d)
for the least nonnegative residue, so
a
is coprime to
d
hence there exists
1\leq b\leq d-1
such that
ab\equiv 1\bmod d
.
Since
d
is even,
a
and
b
are both odd.
On the other hand,
ai\equiv\frac{d}{2}\pmod{d}
. Multiplying
b
on both sides,

i\equiv b\frac{d}{2}\pmod{d}.

Since
b
is an odd integer, we have
(b-1)\frac{d}{2}\equiv 0\bmod d
, hence

i\equiv b\frac{d}{2}\equiv\frac{d}{2}\pmod{d}.

Since
1\leq i\leq g
, this congruence implies
i=\frac{d}{2}=\frac{2g+2}{2}=g+1
.
This contradicts that
1\leq i\leq g
. Therefore,
s_{i}\neq\frac{d}{2}
.
∎

For any integer
N
we define a finite set of lattice points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

\mathcal{T}(N)=\left\{(t_{0},t_{1},t_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{3}:dt_{d}+t_{1}=N,\quad t_{0}+t_{1}+t_{d}=\frac{p-1}{2}\right\}.

This set may be empty; for example, it is empty if
N<0
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume
\mathcal{T}(N)\neq\varnothing
. Then

\mathcal{T}(N)
contains a unique element
with the maximal
t_{0}
-coordinate, that is,

(t_{0},t_{1},t_{d})=(\frac{p-1}{2}-q-r,r,q)

where
q=\left\lfloor N/d\right\rfloor
and
r=[N]_{d}
.

Proof.

Write
N=qd+r
with
0\leq r<d
. Every integral solution of

t_{1}+dt_{d}=N
is of the form

t_{d}=q-\ell,t_{1}=r+d\ell

for some
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
t_{1}\geq 0
and
0\leq r<d
,
we must have
\ell\geq 0
. Moreover,

t_{0}=\frac{p-1}{2}-q-r-\ell(d-1).

Thus
t_{0}
strictly decreases as
\ell
increases. Since

T(N)\neq\varnothing
, some feasible
\ell\geq 0
exists; replacing
it by
\ell=0
increases both
t_{d}
and
t_{0}
, so
\ell=0

is feasible. It is therefore the unique value maximizing
t_{0}
.
∎

Set
\mathbf{1}_{\{j>s_{i}\}}\coloneqq 1
if
j>s_{i}
and
0
if
j\leq s_{i}
,
and set
\deg(0)=-\infty
for the rest of the paper.
Applying the above lemma to
N=pi-j
, we derive the following:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
1\leq i,j\leq g
.
If
\mathcal{T}(p\,i-j)\neq\varnothing
,
then it contains a unique element with maximal
t_{0}
-coordinate,
that is,

(t_{0},t_{1},t_{d})=\left(\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}-(s_{i}-j)-(d-1)\mathbf{1}_{\{j>s_{i}\}},s_{i}-j+d\mathbf{1}_{\{j>s_{i}\}},Q_{i}-\mathbf{1}_{\{j>s_{i}\}}\right).

Proof.

Division algorithm gives

pi-j=\begin{cases}Q_{i}d+(s_{i}-j)&\mbox{ if $s_{i}\geq j$}\\
(Q_{i}-1)d+(d+s_{i}-j)&\mbox{ if $s_{i}<j$}.\end{cases}

The rest follows from Lemma
3.2
.
∎

For each pair
(i,j)
with
1\leq i,j\leq g
,
we define a coefficient polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
:

(5)

\boxed{M_{ij}(X,Y)\coloneqq\sum_{(t_{0},t_{1},t_{d})\in\mathcal{T}(pi-j)}\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}}{t_{0},t_{1},t_{d}}X^{t_{1}}Y^{t_{0}},}

or equivalently,

M_{ij}(X,Y)=[x^{pi-j}]\bigl(x^{d}+Xx+Y\bigr)^{(p-1)/2}.

For each pair
(i,j)

we define two integers:

(6)

\displaystyle\eta_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}-(s_{i}-j)-(d-1){\bf 1}_{\{j>s_{i}\}}

(7)

\displaystyle b_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}}{Q_{i}-\mathbf{1}_{\{j>s_{i}\}}}\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}+\mathbf{1}_{\{j>s_{i}\}}}{s_{i}-j+d\mathbf{1}_{\{j>s_{i}\}}}

Lemma 3.4
.

We have
\mathcal{T}(p\,i-j)\neq\varnothing
if and only if
\eta_{ij}\geq 0
.
If
\mathcal{T}(p\,i-j)=\varnothing
then
M_{ij}(\alpha,Y)=0
;
If
\mathcal{T}(p\,i-j)\neq\varnothing
then

\deg(M_{ij}(\alpha,Y))=\eta_{ij},\qquad[Y^{\eta_{ij}}]M_{ij}(\alpha,Y)=\alpha^{s_{i}-j+d\mathbf{1}_{\{j>s_{i}\}}}b_{ij}.

In particular, in all cases,

\deg M_{ij}(\alpha,Y)\leq\eta_{ij}
with the convention

\deg 0=-\infty
.

Proof.

The first statement is clear by the above definition of
\mathcal{T}(p\,i-j)
.
By Lemma
3.3
,

[Y^{t_{0}}]M_{ij}(\alpha,Y)=\alpha^{t_{1}}\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}}{t_{0},t_{1},t_{d}}=\alpha^{t_{1}}\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}}{t_{d}}\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}-t_{d}}{t_{1}}.

According to (
5
), if
\mathcal{T}(p\,i-j)\neq\varnothing
,
the multinomial coefficient is a positive integer and
\alpha
is nonzero,
so this coefficient is nonzero.
Hence the degree is attained.
The values of
t_{1}
and
t_{d}
from Lemma
3.3
verify our claim directly.
If
\mathcal{T}(p\,i-j)=\varnothing
, then the sum defining
M_{ij}
is empty
and hence
M_{ij}(\alpha,Y)=0
.
∎

3.2.
Hasse–Witt matrix over
\mathbb{Z}

For our study of ordinarity, the
g\times g
coefficient matrix

(M_{ij}(X,Y))_{1\leq i,j\leq g}
, and especially its determinant, will be analyzed. This determinant lies in
\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
.

Definition 3.5
.

Define a
Hasse–Witt determinant polynomial
in variables
X
and
Y
:

H_{p}(X,Y):=\det_{1\leq i,j\leq g}M_{ij}(X,Y).

For a nonzero algebraic integer
\alpha
, define a
Hasse–Witt determinant polynomial

\boxed{H_{p,\alpha}(Y):=\det_{1\leq i,j\leq g}M_{ij}(\alpha,Y).}

When no confusion is possible, we call these
Hasse–Witt polynomials
.

Note that
H_{p}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
and
H_{p,\alpha}(Y)
lies in

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)}[Y]
.
We will see later in Proposition
3.8

that
H_{p,\alpha}(Y)
is nonzero when
p>P^{+}(d)
.

3.3.
Leading term in Hasse–Witt determinant polynomials

The Hasse–Witt determinant
polynomial
H_{p,\alpha}(Y)
is a polynomial in

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)}[Y]
. For the proof of our main theorem,
we need to examine its reduction mod
\mathfrak{p}
for primes

\mathfrak{p}
of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)}
.
There are two major steps.
We first study the leading term;
then we control the prime factors in the leading coefficient.

Let
S_{g}
be the symmetric group on
g
letters, and consider the subset

S_{g}^{\prime}:=\{\sigma\in S_{g}\mid\sigma(i)\leq s_{i}\mbox{ for $1\leq i\leq g$}\}.

Lemma 3.6
.

Then
S_{g}^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
.
For
\sigma\in S_{g}^{\prime}
,
the number
E\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{g}\eta_{i,\sigma(i)}

is constant.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
,
s_{1},\ldots,s_{g}
are distinct positive integers;
so these integers can be arranged in increasing order
1\leq s_{m_{1}}<s_{m_{2}}<\ldots<s_{m_{g}}
.
Define
\sigma^{\prime}(m_{k})=k
for every
k
. Since
k\leq s_{m_{k}}
, we see that

\sigma^{\prime}\in S_{g}^{\prime}
. Thus

S_{g}^{\prime}
is non-empty.
By (
6
),

E=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\left(\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}-s_{i}\right)+\frac{g(g+1)}{2}.

This is clearly independent of
\sigma
.
∎

Define a determinant:

(8)

\boxed{\Delta_{p}\coloneqq\det_{1\leq i,j\leq g}\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}}{s_{i}-j}.}

As
p
is odd,
\frac{p-1}{2}
is an integer.
Since
Q_{i}\leq\frac{p\,i}{d}<\frac{p}{2}=\frac{p-1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}
,
and
Q_{i}
is integer, we have

Q_{i}\leq\frac{p-1}{2}
. This implies
the binomial
\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}}{s_{i}-j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
hence
\Delta_{p}
is an integer too.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let

m=\sum_{i=1}^{g}s_{i}-\frac{g(g+1)}{2}
and
u_{p}=\prod_{i=1}^{g}\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}}{Q_{i}}
.
Then
m\geq 0
and
u_{p}
is a positive integer coprime to
p
.

(1)

If
E\geq 0
then

\deg(H_{p,\alpha}(Y))\leq E
and
[Y^{E}]H_{p,\alpha}(Y)=\alpha^{m}u_{p}\Delta_{p}
.

(2)

If
E<0
then
H_{p,\alpha}=0
and
\Delta_{p}=0
.

Proof.

Since
s_{1},\ldots,s_{g}
are distinct positive integers,
\sum_{i=1}^{g}s_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{g}i=\frac{g(g+1)}{2}
, hence
m\geq 0
.
The comment preceding the proposition shows

Q_{i}\leq\frac{p-1}{2}<p
so the binomial
\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}}{Q_{i}}
is a positive integer coprime to
p
,
as is their product
u_{p}
.

(1)
In this proof we write
M_{ij}=M_{ij}(\alpha,Y)
.
Set
e(\sigma)=\#\{i|\sigma(i)>s_{i}\}
.
By Lemma
3.4
,
for any
\sigma\in S_{g}
, if
\prod_{i=1}^{g}M_{i,\sigma(i)}\neq 0
then

\deg\prod_{i=1}^{g}M_{i,\sigma(i)}=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\eta_{i,\sigma(i)}=E-(d-1)e(\sigma).

Consequently,
\deg H_{p,\alpha}(Y)\leq E
, and only permutations in
S_{g}^{\prime}
can contribute to

[Y^{E}]H_{p,\alpha}
. For
\sigma\in S_{g}^{\prime}
, if
\prod_{i=1}^{g}M_{i,\sigma(i)}\neq 0
, then

(9)

[Y^{E}]\prod_{i=1}^{g}M_{i,\sigma(i)}=\alpha^{\sum_{i=1}^{g}s_{i}-\sigma(i)}\prod_{i=1}^{g}b_{i,\sigma(i)}=\alpha^{m}\prod_{i=1}^{g}b_{i,\sigma(i)}.

On the other hand, consider
\sigma\in S^{\prime}_{g}
with
\prod_{i=1}^{g}M_{i,\sigma(i)}=0
. In this case, for some
i
we have

M_{i,\sigma(i)}=0
. Hence by Lemma
3.4
, we have

\eta_{i,\sigma(i)}<0
, that is,

s_{i}-\sigma(i)>\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}
, this implies

\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}}{s_{i}-\sigma(i)}=0
and
b_{i,\sigma(i)}=0
.
Hence (
9
) remains valid. Therefore

\displaystyle[Y^{E}]H_{p,\alpha}(Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{m}\sum_{\sigma\in S^{\prime}_{g}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{g}b_{i,\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{m}\prod_{i=1}^{g}\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}}{Q_{i}}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{g}^{\prime}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{g}\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}}{s_{i}-\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{m}u_{p}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{g}^{\prime}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{g}\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}}{s_{i}-\sigma(i)}.

For each permutation
\sigma\in S_{g}\backslash S_{g}^{\prime}
,
there exists
i
such that
s_{i}-\sigma(i)<0
, hence

\prod_{i=1}^{g}\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}}{s_{i}-\sigma(i)}=0
.
Thus

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{g}^{\prime}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{g}\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}}{s_{i}-\sigma(i)}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{g}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{g}\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}}{s_{i}-\sigma(i)}=\Delta_{p}.

Therefore,

[Y^{E}]H_{p,\alpha}(Y)=\alpha^{m}u_{p}\Delta_{p}.

(2) Suppose
E<0
.
For every
\sigma\in S_{g}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{g}\eta_{i,\sigma(i)}=E-(d-1)e(\sigma)<0
.
If all
M_{i,\sigma(i)}\neq 0
, then
\eta_{i,\sigma(i)}\geq 0
for each
i
, so their sum is nonnegative, a contradiction.
Therefore each Leibniz term in the determinant vanishes, and
hence
H_{p,\alpha}=0
.

If
\sigma\notin S^{\prime}_{g}
, then one factor in the corresponding
term of
\Delta_{p}
has negative lower index and is zero. If

\sigma\in S^{\prime}_{g}
and all factors were nonzero, then

\eta_{i,\sigma(i)}=\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}-s_{i}+\sigma(i)\geq 0

for every
i
. Hence

E=\sum_{i}\eta_{i,\sigma(i)}\geq 0,

contrary to the assumption. Thus every term in the determinant
expansion of
\Delta_{p}
is zero.
∎

3.4.
Hasse–Witt determinant polynomial mod
p

For any prime
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
in the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)}
,
we write
\kappa(\mathfrak{p})=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)}/\mathfrak{p}
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\alpha
be a nonzero algebraic integer, and let
F=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
.
Let
p>P^{+}(d)
.

(1)

Then
H_{p,\alpha}(Y)
is nonzero in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)}[Y]
.

(2)

For any prime
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
, if

\alpha\not\in\mathfrak{p}
then
H_{p,\alpha}(Y)\bmod\mathfrak{p}

is nonzero in
\kappa(\mathfrak{p})[Y]
.

Proof.

Since
p>P^{+}(d)>d
, both

2
and
d
are invertible modulo
p
; thus for each
i
we have
the congruence in
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
:

\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}=\frac{p-1}{2}-\frac{p\,i-s_{i}}{d}\equiv\frac{s_{i}}{d}-\frac{1}{2}=\beta_{i}\pmod{p}.

If
0\leq s_{i}-j<d<p
, then
(s_{i}-j)!
is invertible modulo
p
,
and therefore in
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}
we have

\binom{\frac{p-1}{2}-Q_{i}}{s_{i}-j}\equiv\binom{\beta_{i}}{s_{i}-j}\pmod{p}.

If
s_{i}-j<0
, both corresponding binomial coefficients are zero, so the above congruence again holds.
Taking determinants gives

\Delta_{p}\equiv\Delta(s_{1},\ldots,s_{g})\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}

by Proposition
2.7
(since
p>P^{+}(d)
).
In particular,
\Delta_{p}\neq 0
in
\mathbb{Z}
.
Proposition
3.7
(2) therefore implies that
E\geq 0
, and
Proposition
3.7
(1) gives

[Y^{E}]H_{p,\alpha}=\alpha^{m}u_{p}\Delta_{p}.

Since
\alpha\neq 0
,
u_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, and
\Delta_{p}\neq 0
,
this coefficient is nonzero in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
. This proves part (1).

Now let
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
and
\alpha\not\in\mathfrak{p}
.
Then
\alpha\not\equiv 0\bmod\mathfrak{p}
.
By Proposition
3.7
,
u_{p}\not\equiv 0\bmod p
.
We also have
\Delta_{p}\not\equiv 0\bmod\mathfrak{p}
above.
Thus
[Y^{E}]H_{p,\alpha}=\alpha^{m}u_{p}\Delta_{p}\not\equiv 0\bmod\mathfrak{p}
, hence

H_{p,\alpha}(Y)\not\equiv 0\bmod\mathfrak{p}
, proving part (2).
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
p>P^{+}(d)
.
Then

H_{p}(X,Y)\bmod p

is a nonzero polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X,Y]
.
In particular,
H_{p}(X,Y)
is a nonzero polynomial over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

If
H_{p}(X,Y)\bmod p
is a zero polynomial then

H_{p,1}(Y)=H_{p}(1,Y)=0\bmod p
,
contradicting Proposition
3.8
.
∎

4.
Hyperelliptic families
with generically ordinary fibers

Let
\alpha
be a nonzero algebraic integer and let
F=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
.
The discriminant of
f(x)=x^{d}+\alpha x+t
is

D_{\alpha}(t)=\mathrm{disc}_{x}(f(x))=(-1)^{d(d-1)/2}(d^{d}t^{d-1}+(-1)^{d-1}(d-1)^{d-1}\alpha^{d}).

Let
p\nmid 2dN_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)
below.
For each prime
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
with
\kappa(\mathfrak{p})=\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p}

Let
\overline{\alpha}
denote
\alpha\bmod\mathfrak{p}
and

\overline{D}_{\overline{\alpha}}(t)
denote

D_{\alpha}(t)\bmod\mathfrak{p}
.
Since
\overline{D}_{\overline{\alpha}}(t)
is nonzero,
every fiber
\mathcal{S}^{\circ}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is a nonempty geometrically integral open

\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\kappa(\mathfrak{p})}
, defined by the principal open

D(\overline{D}_{\overline{\alpha}}(t))
.

Theorem 4.1

(Theorem
1.3
)
.

Let
d\in\{2g+1,2g+2\}
with
g\geq 2
.
Let
\alpha
be a nonzero algebraic integer.
For every prime

and

,
the family of genus-

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\circ}

is generically ordinary at each prime
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
in
\mathcal{O}_{F}
.

Proof.

Each reduction
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}\bmod\mathfrak{p}
has affine equation
y^{2}=\overline{f}(x)

where

\overline{f}(x)=x^{d}+\overline{\alpha}x+t
lies in
\kappa(\mathfrak{p})[t][x]
.
The generic fiber over
\kappa(\mathfrak{p})(t)
is ordinary if
and only if

\det_{1\leq i,j\leq g}(C_{ij})\neq 0,\qquad C_{ij}=[x^{p\,i-j}]\overline{f}(x)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}.

(We remark that the coefficient matrix
(C_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq g}

is the transpose of Hasse–Witt matrix.

[

]
for clarification in modern notations.)
From (

), we immediately conclude

Taking determinant, we have

\det_{1\leq i,j\leq g}(C_{ij})=\det_{1\leq i,j\leq g}(M_{ij}(\alpha,t)\bmod\mathfrak{p})=(H_{p,\alpha}(t)\bmod\mathfrak{p})

where

is the Hasse–Witt polynomial
introduced above in Definition

.
Write

Then the generic fiber over

.
Equivalently, the ordinary locus in

is

Note that

for every

(2),

for every

.

\mathcal{S}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\circ}
is

.
As noted before the theorem,
the ordinary locus on
\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\kappa(\mathfrak{p})}

is
D(\overline{D}_{\overline{\alpha}}\overline{H}_{p,\overline{\alpha}})
.
This proves that
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
is generically ordinary.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

d\in\{2g+1,2g+2\}

.

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

For the Katz family, take

∎

Consider the family
\mathcal{C}
of curves

According to

Corollary 4.3

Let
d\in\{2g+1,2g+2\}

Remark 4.4

Each curve
