Emergence of feline sporotrichosis with zoonotic risk in a municipality of the Eastern Amazon, Brazil
Authors: Paula Fernanda Morais de Sousa, Gabriel Soares Sodré, Raquel Mesquita Senado, José Roberto da Silva Filho, Simone Silveira da Costa, Bruna Thainá Sousa de Araújo, Talita Bandeira Roos, Daniela Isabel Brayer Pereira, Joelson Sousa Lima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sporotrichosis is a zoonotic implantation mycosis caused by dimorphic fungi of the genus Sporothrix. Transmission occurs through traumatic inoculation of the fungus and may involve different routes, including sapronotic transmission from contaminated soil, plants, and organic matter, zoonotic transmission mainly associated with infected felines, and, less frequently, contaminated fomites. The disease remained neglected for many years; however, several regions have recently reported outbreaks that are difficult to control. Thus, this study aimed to describe the emergence of sporotrichosis in the interior of the state of Pará. Twenty-three suspected cases were evaluated, of which 20 animals were confirmed with the disease in the municipality of Castanhal–PA, all presenting suggestive clinical signs and lesions. Diagnosis was confirmed by fungal culture. A higher incidence of the disease was observed in animals with access to the street and in regions with forest areas or recent occupation. Early diagnosis of sporotrichosis favors treatment, as localized lesions show a better response to drug therapy.

Introduction

Sporotrichosis is a chronic implantation mycosis caused by fungi of the genus Sporothrix. Sporothrix species can be divided into environmental and pathogenic clades. The pathogenic clade includes S. schenckii, S. brasiliensis, S. globosa, and S. luriei, which are associated with sporotrichosis in animals and humans, with S. brasiliensis showing the highest incidence in Brazil [1]. Sporothrix spp. are dimorphic, saprobiotic, aerobic, and cosmopolitan fungi found in nature, particularly in warm and humid environments and in soils with abundant decomposing organic matter. In the environment, they exist in a mycelial form; however, within the host (parasitic form) or when cultivated at temperatures of 35–37 °C, they transition to a yeast-like morphology [2].

Sporotrichosis is considered an emerging disease with global occurrence, showing higher prevalence in tropical and subtropical areas. Endemic and hyperendemic regions have been reported in several countries of Latin America, as well as in China, Japan, India, South Africa, and Australia. In contrast, Europe, North America, and the Middle East mainly report sporadic or imported cases. While sapronotic transmission predominates in most regions, Brazil has experienced a large zoonotic epidemic associated with feline transmission, which has contributed to the geographic expansion of the disease [3–6].

Initially, environmental sapronotic transmission through traumatic inoculation from contaminated soil, plants, and organic matter was considered the main route of infection. However, zoonotic transmission, especially involving infected felines, is currently recognized as having major epidemiological importance. In addition, recent evidence suggests that transmission may also occur through contaminated fomites and environmental surfaces. In this context, sporotrichosis is classified as a zoonotic implantation mycosis affecting humans and other quadrupeds, with felines acting as the main transmission agents in endemic areas. [7–9].

Among animals, felines are the most affected, in addition to acting as reservoirs and disseminators of the fungus to other animals and humans. The behavioral habits of cats largely explain this higher prevalence. The infectious agent shows no predilection regarding sex or age; however, non-neutered males, whether free-ranging or domiciled with outdoor access, play a significant role in the spread of the disease. Transmission of the fungus mainly occurs through traumatic inoculation caused by scratches and bites from infected felines during territorial or mating disputes, but may also occur through direct contact with exudates from lesions and contaminated environmental surfaces [9, 10].

Currently, in Brazil, sporotrichosis is considered a neglected and endemic mycosis, with Sporothrix brasiliensis as the main species involved [11]. Since the first reported cases, the disease has undergone changes in its distribution, evolving from sporadic occurrences to numerous outbreaks concentrated mainly in the South and Southeast regions. However, in recent years, zoonotic sporotrichosis has expanded to the North and Northeast regions of the country [12], intensifying its relevance as a public health problem and reinforcing the need for epidemiological investigation, as well as control and prophylaxis measures.

Against this backdrop, our study aims to officially report the emergence of feline sporotrichosis in a municipality in the interior of the state of Pará, in the Eastern Amazon.

Methods

In this observational and longitudinal epidemiological study, the occurrence of Sporothrix spp. infection in felines was evaluated between January 2023 and August 2024, in the municipality of Castanhal, located in the interior of the state of Pará. The study was conducted in partnership with the municipal Zoonosis Control Center, which was responsible for identifying, registering, and notifying suspected cases of the disease, as well as collecting the samples.

The animal’s history and physical examination were recorded on specific forms by the sanitary agency’s agents, and this information was sent along with the biological material to assist in conducting the laboratory diagnosis. In addition, epidemiological data were collected regarding the animals’ physical characteristics (age, sex, breed, and neutering status), behavioral habits, immune status, and demographics. Confirmed cases were subsequently followed to obtain information regarding clinical evolution and final outcomes, including survival or euthanasia, through records maintained by the municipal Zoonosis Control Center and communication with animal owners.

The inclusion criteria for animals in the study was the presence of characteristic lesions raising suspicion of subcutaneous mycosis, mainly in the face, paws, and ear tips, as well as a history of trauma resulting from fights or contact with other animals also suspected of infection. Furthermore, the notification sites of the cases were georeferenced for the preparation of a disease distribution map in the municipality. Spatial distribution analysis was performed based on the geographic location of confirmed case notifications to identify and visualize their occurrence and concentration within urban and peri-urban areas of Castanhal.

Clinical specimens for diagnosis were obtained from secretions and ulcerated lesions, collected using sterile swabs and Stuart transport medium for fungal culture, in addition to the imprint cytological technique on glass slides for direct examination. Animals were considered confirmed cases when compatible clinical findings and characteristic yeast-like cells were observed, with fungal culture used as the confirmatory diagnostic method. Samples were stored under refrigeration and sent to the Laboratory of Microbiology and Immunology of the Institute of Veterinary Medicine at UFPA–Castanhal, where they were processed as soon as possible after collection, not exceeding 24 hours.

In the direct mycological examination, the imprint slides were stained with hematological reagents (Quick Panoptic) to visualize characteristic yeast-like cells. Samples were also cultured on Mycosel® Agar, incubated for up to one month, and examined twice a week. Although Mycosel® agar is traditionally used for dermatophyte isolation, it was employed following previously published diagnostic protocols for sporotrichosis, as its selective composition helps suppress contaminating bacteria and rapidly growing environmental fungi that may interfere with isolation. Fungal dimorphism was evaluated by subculturing isolates on Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) agar at 37 °C, while Mycosel® agar was used for primary isolation and observation of the filamentous form at 25 °C [11].

After growth in culture media, the macro and micromorphological characteristics of the fungi were observed. Colonies were evaluated for type, size, margins, relief, texture, and pigmentation, observed on both the obverse and reverse of the plates. Fragments of the colonies were collected using an inoculation loop, fixed on slides, and stained with Lactophenol Cotton Blue. Under microscopy, the presence and characteristics of hyphae and reproductive structures were investigated; in some cases, subcultures and the slide culture technique were performed for better observation of the fungal architecture.

Results

In the present study, 23 suspected cases of the disease were recorded, of which 20 were confirmed as sporotrichosis through mycological diagnosis. All animals evaluated were symptomatic, presenting localized or disseminated lesions and, according to the epidemiological investigation, had access to outdoor environments—factors considered for the selection of the survey presented here. Follow-up information showed that 18 of the 20 confirmed cases progressed to euthanasia, whereas only two animals survived (cases 4 and 7). Most euthanized animals presented multiple or disseminated lesions, while the surviving animals received clinical management and follow-up.

When evaluating the age factor, no specific age range of higher disease occurrence was identified, with a range varying from 5 months to 7 years. Among animals with age information available, the median age was 4.5 years for females and 3.5 years for males.

Regarding symptomatology, the face regions—primarily the snout and ear tips—were the sites of highest lesion incidence, as these areas are most frequently involved in fungal inoculation [13]. Lesions presented in a localized or disseminated manner, and their number and classification directly influenced clinical manifestations. Discussion

The onset of sexual maturity in animals stands out—a period in which territorial and sexual disputes intensify, factors that may contribute to the dissemination of the fungus causing sporotrichosis [14]. In the present survey, six females and 14 males were evaluated. Although there is no definitive evidence of a direct association between Sporothrix spp. infection and the animal’s sex, male felines, especially non-neutered ones, tend to be more affected due to dominant and aggressive behavior, which was also observed in this study. Atavistic behaviors of felines, in both wild and urban environments—such as the habit of claw sharpening—favor hunting and defense, predisposing these animals to contamination with the fungus present in the soil and decomposing organic matter [15].

Felines have the habit of traveling long distances in search of territory, food, and mating. Fungal transmission occurs primarily during contact between healthy and infected animals, in situations of fights over territorial disputes, food, or during mating, which even allows for the contamination of females [16].

All evaluated animals, both positive and negative, had street access, with extensive roaming in outdoor environments, mainly in recently occupied suburban areas, with unoccupied lots, presence of residual forest, and abundance of organic matter in the peridomestic area. Access to these areas is one of the main risk factors for contamination, whether through fights or scratching habits in environments favorable to fungal proliferation. In the epidemiological survey, of the 20 positive animals evaluated, 15 were semi-domiciled and five were stray. Non-neutered free-ranging animals have a greater impact on the spread of sporotrichosis when compared to neutered and strictly domiciled felines [17].

In this context, long-distance roaming behavior, especially in free-ranging felines, increases contact between healthy and infected animals, favoring the spread of the disease [14]. With the increase in the number of stray animals, it becomes difficult to estimate the real dimension of sporotrichosis expansion in the region, as stray animals were reported in both the central area of the city and peripheral neighborhoods in the present study.

These findings highlight the need for public health and animal health policies aimed at controlling the spread of sporotrichosis. Educational campaigns, professional training for early recognition and diagnosis, improved access to diagnostic methods, responsible ownership practices, neutering programs, control of stray animal populations, and early treatment of infected animals are important measures to reduce transmission [18]. However, the implementation of such strategies is often limited in regions where the disease remains poorly documented. In this context, epidemiological studies such as the present one provide essential information for surveillance systems and may support the development of prevention, monitoring, and control policies for sporotrichosis in newly affected areas.

The pattern of lesions observed in the present study, with a predominance of involvement of the facial region, particularly the snout and ear tips, was consistent with previous reports in the literature, which describe these anatomical sites as the most frequently affected in feline sporotrichosis [13]. Additionally, animals presenting multiple and disseminated lesions exhibited more severe clinical conditions, suggesting a greater extent of disease involvement.

The dissemination of lesions beyond the site of inoculation is associated with the involvement of other organs, such as the respiratory system, leading to inappetence, apathy, and progressive weight loss [19]. The lack of early diagnosis, associated with the animal’s immune status at the time of infection, favors a more aggressive clinical course, hindering treatment and potentially leading to death [20]. Furthermore, the greater the number of lesions, the higher the fungal load and the risk of transmission to other animals and humans.

The unfavorable outcome observed in most cases reinforces the severity of feline sporotrichosis when diagnosis and treatment are delayed. Animals presenting disseminated lesions and possible systemic involvement tend to exhibit poorer prognoses, highlighting the importance of early detection, prompt therapeutic intervention, and responsible animal ownership as measures to reduce both animal mortality and zoonotic transmission.

The two surviving animals in the present study were diagnosed comparatively earlier and received appropriate veterinary management and follow-up, reinforcing the importance of timely diagnosis and treatment for improving clinical outcomes. In contrast, most of the remaining animals did not complete or receive antifungal therapy due to the advanced stage of the disease at presentation, financial constraints faced by owners, or concerns regarding zoonotic transmission, leading in many cases to euthanasia as the most feasible management option.

Current recommendations for feline sporotrichosis are based primarily on prolonged antifungal therapy, with itraconazole (100 mg) considered the main treatment option, often administered alone or in combination with potassium iodide (5 mg/Kg), depending on the clinical presentation and disease severity. Treatment generally requires several months of continuous administration, regular veterinary follow-up, and monitoring for potential adverse effects. Despite the availability of effective therapeutic protocols, access to treatment remains an important challenge in many regions, as prolonged therapy and repeated clinical evaluations may result in substantial financial costs for animal owners [11].

In municipalities where public treatment programs are unavailable, these barriers may contribute to treatment abandonment, delayed intervention, or even euthanasia of severely affected animals. In contrast, some Brazilian states have implemented public initiatives that provide diagnosis and treatment free of charge, demonstrating the potential impact of coordinated control policies on disease management. These findings reinforce the importance of expanding access to diagnosis, treatment, and responsible animal ownership programs as part of a comprehensive One Health strategy.

The present study evidences the emergence of sporotrichosis in a region where the disease had not yet been officially recorded. In the state of Pará, the first cases were described in the capital [21], and the detection of cases in distant and non-contiguous municipalities, such as Castanhal, indicates the spread of the disease within the state, representing an important alert for surveillance and public health agencies. The population’s lack of knowledge, combined with the absence of public control policies, may favor the transformation of isolated outbreaks into hyperendemic areas, as already observed in other Brazilian [22].

The municipality of Castanhal had no history of sporotrichosis notifications at the Zoonosis Control Center; however, the high number of cases diagnosed in a short period suggests previous underreporting, especially considering the number of affected stray animals.

Spatial analysis revealed that confirmed cases were distributed across several neighborhoods of the municipality, with a predominance in peripheral areas. Cases were concentrated in geographically close neighborhoods located mainly in the outskirts, where access to basic sanitation and sanitary surveillance programs is limited. These areas feature rudimentary vegetation, recently deforested soils for housing construction, and sites of improper waste disposal, characteristics that may favor environmental conditions associated with the persistence and spread of infectious agents.

The disordered growth of developing regions contributes to the deforestation of native vegetation areas, altering soil conformation and favoring the emergence of pathogens and new diseases [23]. The recent occupation of areas without urban planning and adequate public policies constitutes an important anthropogenic factor predisposing to the dissemination of zoonoses. According to data from Winck et al. [24], two-thirds of the Brazilian population are exposed to medium or high risk of zoonotic disease outbreaks, especially in regions with recent loss of native vegetation and socio-environmental vulnerability.

According to Fiocruz [25], the fungus has always been present in the environment, but the increase in the number of stray animals has favored the proliferation of sporotrichosis among felines. The areas of recent occupation in the studied municipality—associated with the presence of vacant lots, significant vegetation, organic-rich soils, and deforestation—may explain the recently diagnosed cases.

The increase in feline sporotrichosis cases may result in higher zoonotic transmission to the human population. In healthy (immunocompetent) individuals, cases tend to be moderate; however, in immunocompromised patients or those with comorbidities, the disease can progress to more severe conditions. Delay in seeking treatment, combined with a lack of knowledge about the disease, may worsen the clinical presentation, reinforcing the need for integrated One Health policies, surveillance actions, and research such as the study presented here.

Consistent with this concern, two owners developed lesions compatible with sporotrichosis after exposure to infected cats. However, these human cases were not investigated as part of the present study. The individuals were advised to seek medical evaluation through the public health system, and no laboratory results, treatment records, or clinical outcome data were available to the authors.

Nevertheless, the management of human sporotrichosis is well established. Itraconazole is considered the treatment of choice for cutaneous and lymphocutaneous sporotrichosis, with reported response rates ranging from 80% to 100%. Current guidelines recommend oral administration of 200 mg/day, generally maintained for 2–4 weeks after complete lesion resolution, resulting in treatment durations that commonly range from 3 to 6 months, depending on disease severity and clinical response [5].

These findings reinforce the importance of increasing awareness of sporotrichosis among both the general population and healthcare professionals. Sporotrichosis is still poorly known in the region, which may contribute to its spread among animals and humans, with potential for further zoonotic transmission events. Understanding the epidemiological patterns of the disease in the state of Pará assists Municipal and State Health Departments in identifying risk areas, supporting public prevention and control policies, and reinforcing the importance of raising public awareness about the risks of animals’ outdoor roaming.

A limitation of this study is that species-level identification was not performed. The diagnosis was based on the phenotypic characterization of Sporothrix spp., including direct examination, culture, and fungal dimorphism. Therefore, although S. brasiliensis is considered the most likely species based on its recognized predominance in the emergence of feline sporotrichosis in Brazil and previous reports from the state of Pará, its involvement could not be confirmed in the present study. Molecular methods, including PCR-based assays and gene sequencing, are currently available for species-level identification and may contribute to a more detailed understanding of the epidemiology, transmission dynamics, and geographic dissemination of Sporothrix species in future investigations.

Additionally, the relatively small number of confirmed cases and the descriptive nature of the available epidemiological data limited the application of inferential statistical analyses. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted primarily as a descriptive characterization of the emergence of feline sporotrichosis in the municipality, providing baseline information for future studies involving larger sample sizes and more comprehensive statistical approaches.

Conclusion

Of the 23 animals tested for sporotrichosis, 20 were diagnosed as positive. Access to outdoor environments proved to be a crucial factor for feline contamination, being considered a significant risk for contagion. Stray animals showed significant relevance in the pathogen’s dissemination, contributing to the occurrence of zoonotic infections.

The proximity between neighborhoods, associated with the characteristics of the predominant vegetation and areas of recent occupation, directly impacts the spread of the disease. The population’s lack of knowledge about the condition, as well as the scarcity of regional data on sporotrichosis, contributes to the illness remaining marginalized and neglected, favoring its spread and broadening the impact on animal, human, and environmental health.
