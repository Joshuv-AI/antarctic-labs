Effectiveness of health education interventions on breast cancer literacy and breast screening uptake in sub-Saharan Africa: a systematic review protocol

Published: 2026

Background

Breast cancer remains a global public health concern. In 2022 alone, 2.2 million new cases of breast cancer cases were reported worldwide [1]. Breast cancer is the number one cancer among women (23.8% of global cancer cases) and the second leading cancer in both sexes (12.4% of global cancer cases) [1]. With a 5-year prevalence of 72.2 per 100,000, breast cancer continues to be the leading cancer in Africa [1]. The available evidence also indicates that in 2022, nearly 91,252 deaths attributable to breast cancer were reported in Africa [1]. Studies show that the high breast cancer mortality in Africa is due to late diagnosis and detection uptake among women [2, 3]. For instance, Jedy-Agba et al. [3] report that about 80% of breast cancer cases are presented in advanced or metastatic phase of the disease which adversely impacts on survivorship. Data from the 2022 Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) shows differences in breast cancer incidence and mortality between SSA countries. For instance, Ghana recorded an age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) and mortality rate (ASMR) of 40.2 and 19.3 per 100,000 population, respectively [1]. Meanwhile, Lesotho, Tanzania, Kenya, and South Africa reported an ASIR of 18.9 per 100,000, 25.1 per 100,000, 40.8 per 100,000, and 47.8 per 100,000 population, respectively [1].

It must be noted that the late detection and diagnosis of breast cancer in Africa does not occur unexpectedly. Rather, it is due to the low rate of breast screening uptake and the non-availability of population-based screening services [4, 5]. In breast cancer prevention, there are three screening modalities: self-examination, clinical breast examination, and mammography [6]. Breast self-examination (BSE) is the easiest screening modality due to its non-invasiveness, cost-effectiveness and self-administration; yet only 44% and 17.9% had ever performed or regularly perform BSE [7]. Ayebeng et al. [5] also estimate that only 19.2% of women of reproductive age in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) have ever undergone clinical breast examination (CBE). The literature also shows that even though mammography is the gold standard, there is a low uptake of this screening modality when compared to CBE [8]. This is due to high cost involved in accessing mammography screening [8].

Previous studies conducted at the SSA sub-regional level reveal that a combination of health system, cultural norms and individual factors contribute to the low screening uptake [9–11]. For example, Magwesela et al. [9] report that across SSA, financial constraints, lack of social support, inaccessibility to breast cancer screening services, and previous negative experience with health professionals limited women’s screening uptake. Pierz et al. [10] also reported that across sub-Saharan Africa, inadequate training of health professionals and limited access to health facilities contribute substantially to low breast cancer screening uptake. Beyond these factors, the extant literature identifies low breast cancer literacy as a major reason for the low breast cancer screening uptake among women in SSA [9–12].

Breast cancer literacy refers to a situation where women have knowledge of breast cancer (i.e., knowledge on risk factors as well as suggestive signs and symptoms) and are aware of breast cancer screening (i.e., screening modalities and benefits of getting screened) [13]. Roh et al. [13] found that women who were breast cancer literate were 18 times more likely to undergo CBE than those who were not breast cancer literate. One systematic review found in East Africa also revealed that women who had poor knowledge and awareness of breast cancer were significantly less likely to undergo screening [9]. This means that implementing a health education intervention could impact positively on breast cancer literacy and screening uptake in Africa.

Recognizing the potential of health education interventions to improve breast cancer literacy, researchers in Africa have implemented varied interventions [14–16]. For example, Agide et al. [14] implemented a community health education intervention and found mammography use and comprehensive knowledge increased from baseline to endpoint. Similarly, Nsaful et al. [16] implemented a school-based educational intervention in Ghana that resulted in higher knowledge on breast cancer risk factors and screening knowledge. Thus, our argument is premised on the Health Belief Model which posits that individuals will take up preventive health interventions when there is high perceived susceptibility, high perceived benefits, low perceived barriers, and a high perceived self-efficacy [17]. We argue that by increasing knowledge of breast cancer risk factors, signs and symptoms, and available screening modalities, health education enhances perceived susceptibility and perceived severity while simultaneously clarifying the benefits of early detection.

Despite the available literature [14–16], evidence on the effectiveness of these interventions in SSA is limited and fragmented, with variations in educational strategies, delivery methods, and cultural contexts. A systematic review is needed to synthesize existing evidence, identify the most effective educational approaches, and guide the development of culturally appropriate interventions aimed at improving breast cancer outcomes in SSA. The primary aim of this review is to assess the effectiveness of health education interventions on breast cancer literacy and breast cancer screening uptake among women in sub-Saharan Africa. A secondary objective is to describe the methods used to deliver breast cancer education interventions within the region.

Methods

Study design

This study will be a systematic review of quantitative studies published in a single SSA country or across multiple SSA countries. The protocol for this review has been prospectively registered in Open Science Framework [18]. The protocol for this study is prepared in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines (see Supplementary file I) [19].

Eligibility criteria

Population

The review will include studies that have women aged 15 or older in an SSA country as their study population. We acknowledge that breast screening is usually not recommended for young women (15–25 years). However, the aim of this study goes beyond screening uptake to cover breast cancer literacy. That justifies the inclusion of younger women. Studies involving women who have already been diagnosed with breast cancer will not be included in this review. This criterion is included to identify the true impact of the interventions on breast cancer literacy and screening uptake. Ordinarily, women who have already been diagnosed with cancer would have frequent interaction with health professionals and the health system. As such, their knowledge about breast cancer would have improved through lived experience and information received from health professionals.

Interventions

In terms of the type of studies included, only randomized controlled trial and quasi experimental studies (i.e., pre-post interventional studies) will be included. Studies will be eligible for inclusion provided the aim of the intervention was to either improve knowledge, awareness, screening uptake or all three outcomes. This means that the review will include studies that have examined community-based, digital, mass media and facility-based interventions.

Comparison

Studies will be included in this review irrespective of whether they had a comparator group. It is envisioned that studies that have a comparator group will be women who receive no intervention or receive the standard care.

Outcomes

In this review, studies will be considered eligible only if they report on one or all of the following outcomes as a result of the implementation of an intervention: knowledge on breast cancer risk factors, knowledge on breast cancer suggestive signs and symptoms, awareness of breast cancer screening modalities (BSE, CBE or mammography), and screening uptake.

Exclusion criteria

Studies on quality of life, survivorship, lived experiences of breast cancer, and breast cancer referrals will be excluded (Table 1). Additionally, the study will exclude studies that were not written in English language, as well as non-primary studies (e.g., commentaries, scoping review, position papers, case reports, and conference abstracts). Qualitative and non-interventional quantitative studies (i.e., observational studies, descriptive studies, and cross-sectional studies), as well as studies published before January 1, 2015, will be excluded. The restriction to the most recent ten years is intended to capture evidence reflecting contemporary breast cancer screening interventions, delivery platforms, and health communication strategies that have evolved substantially in SSA over the past decade. This period encompasses major shifts in screening modalities, task shifting to community-based health workers, and increased use of mobile and digital health interventions. The inclusion of studies conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic is intentional, as pandemic-related disruptions and adaptations to screening services constitute an important component of the current implementation context. Studies not conducted in a SSA country will also be excluded.

Search strategy

Peer-reviewed published research articles will be searched from the following databases: PubMed, Web of Science, SCOPUS, PsychInfo and CINAHL [7, 20]. The literature search strategy will be developed using a combination of controlled vocabulary and free text keywords to ensure comprehensive and reproducible retrieval of relevant studies. In PubMed, Medical Subject Headings will be used alongside text word searches in titles and abstracts. Core concepts will include breast cancer, breast cancer screening and uptake, breast cancer knowledge, awareness and risk factors, and the target population of women in SSA. Specifically, MeSH terms such as Breast Neoplasms, Mass Screening, Early Detection of Cancer, Mammography, Breast Self-Examination, Health Knowledge, Attitudes, Practice, Health Literacy, Risk Factors, Women, and Africa South of the Sahara will be combined using Boolean operators with free text keywords including breast cancer, breast neoplasms, breast tumor or tumor, breast cancer screening, screening uptake or utilization, mammography, clinical breast examination, breast self-examination, knowledge, awareness, literacy, risk factors, and women or girls. Boolean operators AND/OR will be applied to combine concepts, and truncation will be used where appropriate to capture word variants. The search strategy will be adapted to the indexing systems of each database, with MeSH terms replaced by CINAHL Headings in CINAHL and by database-specific subject headings or text keywords in Scopus and Web of Science. The search will be limited to studies published between January 1, 2015 and March 1, 2025, with no language restrictions applied at the search stage (see Supplementary file II). The search process will last between April 1, 2025, and June 30, 2025.

Grey literature, specifically preprints of randomized or quasi-experimental studies, will be searched on ArXiv and PsyArXiv. Preprints will be included to capture emerging evidence that has not yet completed the peer review process, thereby enhancing the completeness of the evidence base. To ensure transparency, studies identified as preprints will be clearly labeled as such and their findings will be interpreted with caution. Also, an ascendency and descendancy approach will be adopted where we will check the reference lists of the included studies to identify additional potential studies for the review. Our search will not be repeated after the initial search, as this is not a living review. The review will be based on studies available at the time of the initial search, and no further updates are planned.

Study selection

After a successful search, the BibTex file of the search results will be exported into Zotero. In Zotero, the abstracts and titles of each study from each database will be screened. Following the screening process, all duplicates will be identified and removed. After this process, the finalized studies to be included in the review will be uploaded in Rayyan (an online systematic review system) where the detailed screening will be done. Following removal of duplicates, two reviewers (JO and BS) will independently screen titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Studies deemed potentially eligible will proceed to full-text review. Subsequently, all three reviewers (JO, BS, and PS) will independently assess full-text articles for eligibility. Where there are discrepancies in the decision taken, all authors will have a discussion to deliberate and come to a consensus on how to deal with the discrepancy.

Data extraction

Upon deciding on the final studies to include in the review, key data will be extracted using a tabular template. The lead author (JO) will be responsible for extracting the data. However, each step of the extraction process will be discussed with the other two team members. This template will include the following information: title, authors’ names, year of publication, publication language, journal, country, theory used, study design, type of intervention, outcome measure, sample size and analysis, key outcomes, and funding source (see Supplementary file III) [9, 21].

Data synthesis

The findings will be synthesized using narrative synthesis methods in accordance with guidance from Popay et al. [22]. Quantitative findings will be grouped and described according to intervention type, mode of delivery, study setting, and outcome category. Descriptive comparisons will be undertaken to identify patterns in intervention effectiveness across studies. If sufficient homogeneity exists among studies, a meta-analysis will be performed using a random-effects model. The effectiveness of educational interventions will be reported as pooled effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using the I2 statistic. Subgroup analyses will be conducted based on intervention type, delivery method, and country [20].

Risk assessment

The risk of bias for included studies will be assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Controlled Trials (RoB 2) [23] and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for non-randomized studies [24]. For RCTs, the Cochrane RoB 2 tool evaluates five domains of bias, including randomization, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results [23]. Each domain will be rated as “Low risk,” “Some concerns,” or “High risk,” and an overall risk of bias score will be assigned accordingly [23]. For non-randomized studies, the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist assesses key methodological domains including participant selection, identification and management of confounding factors, reliability and validity of outcome measurement, and appropriateness of statistical analysis [24]. Each item will be judged as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not Applicable.” Consistent with JBI guidance, the results of the appraisal will be reported descriptively, and an overall risk of bias judgment will be made qualitatively based on the pattern and severity of identified methodological limitations.

Review status

This review is currently ongoing. Searches across the various databases have commenced but not yet completed.

Discussion

This systematic review is necessary in light of the persistent public health challenge posed by late-stage breast cancer presentation in SSA [2, 3], despite increasing global and regional commitments to early cancer detection. Early detection through improved breast cancer literacy and screening uptake remains a central pillar of the World Health Organization breast cancer initiative [25]. However, in SSA, progress in this area has been slow, largely due to low awareness, limited access to screening services, and substantial contextual and health system constraints [9, 10, 12]. These challenges are further exacerbated by perceived potential harms associated with screening including false-positive findings, psychological distress, unnecessary diagnostic procedures, and overdiagnosis of lesions that may never progress to clinically significant disease [9]. Through the synthesis of evidence on the effectiveness of health education interventions, this review directly addresses a priority gap in implementation-focused evidence needed to strengthen early detection strategies in the region.

This review differs from previous reviews in several ways. Existing reviews conducted in sub-Saharan Africa have largely focused on prevalence of screening uptake [7], barriers and facilitators [9, 10], or descriptive assessments of breast cancer knowledge [11], with limited emphasis on intervention effectiveness. Other reviews conducted in low- and middle-income countries have combined heterogeneous study designs or examined multiple cancer types, limiting their applicability to breast cancer-specific policy and programming in SSA [6]. In contrast, the present review is restricted to randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies, allowing for a more robust assessment of causal effects. Additionally, this review focuses exclusively on health education interventions and synthesizes evidence within the sociocultural and health system contexts of SSA.

Anticipated strengths and limitations

This review has several anticipated strengths. The inclusion of only randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies enhances the internal validity of the evidence and allows for stronger inferences regarding the effectiveness of health education interventions. Also, the comprehensive search strategy across multiple bibliographic databases, grey literature sources, and reference lists increases the likelihood of capturing a broad range of relevant studies. Additionally, the use of established risk of bias assessment tools tailored to both randomized and non-randomized designs strengthens the transparency and credibility of the synthesis.

Nonetheless, certain limitations are anticipated. Substantial heterogeneity in intervention content, delivery modes, outcome definitions, and follow-up periods may limit the feasibility of meta-analysis and reduce comparability across studies. The restriction to English-language publications may result in the exclusion of relevant studies published in other languages. Additionally, variations in how breast cancer literacy and screening uptake are measured across studies may introduce measurement inconsistency.

Contribution to policy, practice, and research

From a policy perspective, the review will provide evidence to inform national cancer control programs and resource allocation decisions by identifying educational approaches that demonstrate measurable improvements in breast cancer literacy and screening uptake. It is also expected that the review will offer practical guidance for intervention design and scale-up in SSA. The review will also identify gaps in the existing evidence base, including under-researched populations, intervention components, and outcome measures. This will inform the design of future trials and implementation studies, promote standardization in outcome measurement, and strengthen the overall quality of research on breast cancer education and screening interventions in the region.
