Thermal-decoupled selenization enables kesterite solar cells with 15.3% certified efficiency
Authors: Zucheng Wu, Hao Wei, Zhipeng Shao, Dongxing Kou, Lijing Wang, Jia Jia, Yimeng Li, Changcheng Cui, Xiaopeng Feng, Shengren Xia, Luyao Lv, Ziye Liu, Yuehui Wang, Sixin Wu, Guanglei Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cu2ZnSn(S,Se)4 kesterite solar cells, while promising for sustainable photovoltaics, are constrained by undefined crystallization kinetics during selenization, which introduces bilayer crystallization with detrimental horizontal grain boundaries, voids, and secondary phases. This work traces this issue to early-stage Se-driven reaction at the back interface, which forms a low-melting-point Cu(S,Se) phase and triggers uncontrolled reverse crystallization. To address this, we propose a thermal-decoupled selenization strategy that creates a vertical Se concentration gradient in the initial stage. This approach decouples Se supply from the Cu(S,Se) formation temperature range, thereby suppressing bilayer crystallization. Consequently, it enables the growth of top-down columnar grains, which enhance carrier transport and suppress recombination, achieving a champion power conversion efficiency of 15.7% (certified 15.3%) in the resultant devices. This approach offers critical insights into crystallization kinetics and is also applicable to solution-processed Cu(In,Ga)Se2 solar cells, highlighting its great significance for diverse copper-based chalcogenides.

Introduction

Cu2ZnSn(S,Se)4 (CZTSSe) solar cells are considered a leading candidate for next-generation low-cost and environmentally benign photovoltaics, benefiting from their tunable bandgap, high theoretical efficiency, earth-abundant elements, and low toxicity1–3. Driven by advances in heterojunction engineering and bulk defect control strategies, their power conversion efficiency (PCE) has rapidly progressed over the past 5 years4–7. However, pervasive detrimental heterogeneous crystallization in the absorber layer constitutes a critical bottleneck that fundamentally limits key photovoltaic parameters such as the open-circuit voltage (VOC) and fill factor (FF)8–10. In contrast to the ideal top-down columnar growth, CZTSSe thin films usually exhibit upward growth of grains from the Mo back contact, which is termed reverse crystallization. This behavior drives the upward-growing grains to collide with the top-surface crystallization, thereby forming the reported bilayer structure4,10–13. The resulting detrimental horizontal grain boundaries are rich in voids, secondary phases, and defects, which serve as non-radiative recombination centers and increase the carrier transport barrier14,15.

To elucidate the mechanisms of heterogeneous crystallization, many investigations focusing on thermodynamic pathways and kinetic mechanisms have been conducted on kesterite absorbers10,16–19. Scragg et al. systematically elucidated the thermochemical reactions between CZTS and the Mo back electrode during annealing, which generate secondary phases such as Cu2S, ZnS, and SnS at the interface20. These reactions are widely recognized as linked to heterogeneous nucleation, void formation, the elemental diffusion and unbalance21,22. Several strategies, including alkali metal doping and precursor engineering, have been developed to improve the crystalline quality at the back interface. For instance, Li+ can adjust the lattice parameter by changing the ratio Li/(Li+Cu), thereby driving the growth of CZTSSe grains23. Modulating the oxidation states of elements in the precursor alters the intermediate phase evolution during selenization, which can suppress secondary phase formation and promote favorable bottom grain growth4,10,11. However, all these studies still face the issue of heterogeneous crystallization and generate detrimental horizontal grain boundaries. Therefore, elucidating the origin of reverse crystallization and eliminating the bilayer structure are essential to resolve the aforementioned back-interface challenges and break the performance bottleneck in such devices.

In this work, we reveal that reverse crystallization in CZTSSe originates from the formation of a low-melting-point Cu(S,Se) liquid phase at the Mo back contact. This phase forms when Se reacts with Cu2ZnSnS4 (CZTS) precursor and Mo at low temperature during initial selenization. Based on these findings, we present a thermal-decoupled selenization strategy that ensures Se supply reaches the back interface only after the formation temperature range of Cu(S,Se), guiding the back-interface growth along the high‑melting‑point Cu2(S,Se) solid phase pathway to integrate with the top-surface crystallization. This method effectively suppresses reverse crystallization and related derivative issues, promoting ideal top‑down columnar grain growth. These material improvements enhance carrier transport and suppress recombination losses, as evidenced by reduced defect density and enhanced carrier collection and extraction efficiencies. As a result, we achieve a marked improvement in VOC × FF and a champion PCE of 15.7% (certified 15.3%) for CZTSSe solar cells, demonstrating substantial progress in addressing a key performance limitation in CZTSSe photovoltaics.

Results

Thermal-decoupled selenization suppresses reverse crystallization

The conventional selenization process of CZTSSe film is carried out in a graphite chamber. Rapid heating causes solid Se to melt and vaporize quickly, reacting with the precursor film at high temperature. Owing to the high thermal conductivity of graphite, the system exhibits the same thermal-rate of Se source and precursor, resulting in thermal-coupled behavior. As this behavior fundamentally limits independent control of Se evaporation, we design a thermal-decoupled selenization strategy by selecting materials with different thermal conductivity as the Se source crucibles to change its heating rate, thereby achieving real-time control of Se evaporation time and evaporation amount, as shown in Fig. 1a. Experimental results demonstrate that the use of low-thermal-conductivity materials, such as boron nitride (CZTSSe-B) in place of graphite (CZTSSe-G), as the evaporation crucible leads to visible improvement in grain quality (Supplementary Fig. 1). Figure 1b presents the real-time temperature change curves and real-time Se mass loss curves of CZTSSe-G and CZTSSe-B during selenization, details of the temperature measurement method are provided in the Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2, Se mass loss is derived from quantitative analysis of the residual mass in the evaporation crucibles at various time points. The real-time temperature monitoring reveals a critical difference in thermal profiles at 60 s of selenization. Under the CZTSSe-G condition, both the sample and Se source reached 388.6 °C. In contrast, under the CZTSSe-B condition, the Se source temperature was only 258.0 °C, which was 130.6 °C below the sample temperature. This substantial thermal lag directly reduced the amount of evaporated Se from 0.34 g to 0.05 g. Consequently, the Se concentration for CZTSSe-B was significantly lower than for CZTSSe-G during the initial 60 s. These results demonstrate that modulating the evaporation kinetics under CZTSSe-B conditions effectively extends the Se saturation time.

In the conventional selenization process, the CZTSSe-G sample is quenched immediately at the initial stages of crystallization (520 °C, 60 and 120 s) to halt the reaction and examine the absorber morphology. Cross-sectional analysis reveals that nucleation and crystallization initiate nearly simultaneously at both the back and top interface (Fig. 1e), indicating that the early selenization stage critically determines the absorber’s final microstructure. To investigate the spatial distribution of Se at this early phase, time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF‑SIMS) and cross‑sectional energy‑dispersive X‑ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping is conducted on samples quenched at 60 s. The results confirm that in the CZTSSe‑G sample, rapid Se vaporization leads to uniform Se distribution throughout the film (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3). This high-concentration Se environment promotes crystallization at the back interface. In contrast, the CZTSSe-B sample exhibits a distinct gradient distribution, with the Se content markedly decreasing at the back interface. We further perform X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) on mechanically exfoliated interfaces at 60 s of selenization (exfoliation method according to our previous research)22. A distinct Se-3d signal is observed for CZTSSe-G, whereas it is undetectable for CZTSSe-B, confirming the complete absence of Se at the CZTS/Mo interface at this time point (Fig. 1d).

To track the dynamic progression, we also measure the Se distribution at two additional time points (90 and 120 s) during selenization (Supplementary Fig. 4). The CZTSSe-B sample requires 120 s to achieve uniform Se distribution, which represents a 60 s delay compared to the CZTSSe-G sample. The delayed Se exposure thus plays a decisive role in preventing reverse crystallization. Consequently, reverse crystallization initiates at approximately 90 s (Supplementary Fig. 5a), leading to bilayer structures in conventionally processed CZTSSe-G samples (Fig. 1e). By contrast, thermal-decoupled CZTSSe-B samples successfully suppress back-contact reverse crystallization and horizontal grain boundaries, thereby developing dense, top-down columnar grains (Supplementary Fig. 5b and Fig. 1f).

The calculation results reveal that, during the initial stage of selenization, CZTSSe follows distinct decomposition pathways at the back interface depending on the presence or absence of Se. In the presence of Se, the formation of low-melting-point Cu(S,Se) is thermodynamically favored23,24. Supplementary Fig. 6 and Previous studies have demonstrated that this low-melting-point Cu(S,Se) melts into a liquid phase under typical selenization temperatures, effectively promoting the grain growth of CZTSSe crystals17,25,26. Conversely, when thermal-decoupled selenization is employed to suppress Se participation within the Cu(S,Se) formation temperature range, the local environment only supports back-interface evolution along the thermally stable Cu₂(S,Se)-mediated pathway18,19, promoting it to integrate seamlessly with the top-down crystallization (Fig. 2b).

To verify the conclusions, we performed compositional and chemical-state analyses on mechanically exfoliated back-interface samples quenched at various stages of selenization. XPS data in Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 7 reveal an increased Cu binding energy in CZTSSe-G compared to CZTSSe-B, indicating the formation of Cu in a higher oxidation state17,27. This observation is further corroborated by X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 8), fluorescence-mode Cu K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra show a clear shift of the absorption edge to higher energy in CZTSSe-G relative to CZTSSe-B, consistent with the presence of a more oxidized Cu species28. Moreover, time-resolved X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the back-contact region at various selenization stages confirms the formation of a Cu(S,Se) phase, as illustrated in Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 917,24,25,27,29. Collectively, these results establish that the formation of Cu(S,Se) at the Mo/back-contact interface is the key driver of reverse (upward) crystallization in CZTSSe-G.

We further examine the mechanically exfoliated back-interface samples quenched at various stages of selenization using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis, with representative results presented in Fig. 2e, f. The HRTEM image reveals two distinct lattice structures in adjacent regions. Region 1, located within a hexagonal grain, exhibits lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.302 nm, corresponding to the (103) plane of Cu(S,Se). Region 2, shows a spacing of 0.324 nm, consistent with the (112) plane of kesterite CZTSSe. As shown in Fig. 2g, EDS point analysis further corroborates this nanoscale phase evolution. Region 1 displays negligible Sn and Zn signals and a Cu/(S+Se) atomic ratio close to the stoichiometry of Cu(S,Se), whereas region 2 exhibits Cu-rich characteristics but with significant incorporation of Zn and Sn, approaching the nominal composition of CZTSSe. Collectively, these observations provide direct evidence of a phase transformation pathway from Cu(S,Se) to CZTSSe at the back interface.

Concurrently, we detect Zn(S,Se) and Sn(S,Se) phases at the back interface of samples quenched at 60 s via HRTEM, FFT and EDS analyses (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b), confirming the phase information relevant to reaction (1). Subsequently, we prepare additional back-interface samples quenched at 90 s using the progressive annealing approach. HRTEM, FFT and EDS analyses of these samples (Supplementary Fig. 10c–f) further corroborate the products of reaction (1). At this stage, CZTS is almost completely consumed, with sulfur content dropping sharply to negligible levels below 4.5% and selenium content markedly increasing to above 62.5%. These results, together with the 60 s data, collectively reveal the full progression of Se-dominated reaction (1). Furthermore, we conduct Raman spectroscopy analyses at both 60 s and 90 s for additional validation (Supplementary Fig. 11). The Raman spectra of progressively annealed samples are fully consistent with TEM, EDS and XRD results (Fig. 2d–g, Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Fig. 11), confirming the presence of Cu(S,Se), Zn(S,Se) and Sn(S,Se)10,17. Notably, characteristic CZTS peaks persist at 60 s but disappear at 90 s, substantiating the stepwise completion of reaction (1).

To confirm the decisive role of Cu(S,Se) in driving reverse crystallization, control experiments are conducted. First, selenization on glass substrates (removing Mo interference) yields top-down columnar grains under standard conditions (Supplementary Fig. 12a). When CuS is intentionally placed at the precursor bottom interface, upward (reverse) grain growth is observed after selenization (Supplementary Fig. 12b). In contrast, introducing Cu2S at the same location under identical conditions does not induce reverse crystallization (Supplementary Fig. 12c). These results clearly demonstrate that Cu(S,Se) formation is critical for initiating reverse crystallization at the back-contact interface.

Based on the above computational and experimental results, The different phase evolution pathways at the back interface are driven by the presence or absence of Se at low temperature. When Se is abundant at the back interface, as in the CZTSSe-G condition, it leads to the formation of Cu(S,Se), which triggers reverse crystallization. In contrast, when Se is present at the back interface at high temperature, as in the CZTSSe-B condition, the system follows a different pathway where Cu2(S,Se) forms preferentially. This pathway suppresses reverse crystallization at the back interface and instead directs its evolution to integrate seamlessly with the top-down grain growth.

Properties of CZTSSe thin films

The reaction at back interface exerts a profound influence on the microstructure and optoelectronic properties of the final absorber layer. As discussed above, the CZTSSe-G shows a pronounced bilayer grain structure, whereas CZTSSe-B exhibits a uniform, single-layer, top-down columnar grain morphology (Fig. 1e, f). We further characterize the CZTSSe-B and CZTSSe-G absorber films after selenization. Line-scan and mapping EDS analyses (Fig. 3a, b, and Supplementary Fig. 13) further show significant elemental fluctuations at the horizontal grain boundaries between the bilayered large grains in CZTSSe-G. Specifically, reduced Cu, Sn, and Ag signals accompanied by enhanced Zn intensity, a signature of poor local contact and secondary phase formation. Subsequently, we perform mechanical exfoliation of the CZTSSe-G bilayer grain sample, characterizing the bottom grain layer (CZTSSe-G-Gap). As shown in Fig. 3c, SEM images reveal irregularly distributed fine particles at the horizontal grain boundaries and the morphological characteristics derived from the liquid phase15. EDS point analysis (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 14) indicates significant Zn enrichment in these particles, contrasting with the nearly ideal stoichiometric composition observed in the single-layer grain structure of the CZTSSe-B sample. This observation aligns well with the line-scan and mapping EDS data from cross-sectional analyses (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 13), further confirming the presence of substantial elemental segregation at the horizontal grain boundaries. XRD comparisons between CZTSSe-G-Gap and the intact CZTSSe-B absorber layer reveal multiple secondary phase diffraction peaks in the CZTSSe-G-Gap sample, including Cu(S,Se), Cu2(S,Se), Zn(S,Se), and Sn(S,Se) (Fig. 3e)10,17,25. In contrast, the CZTSSe-B sample exhibits a single-phase composition17,30,31. Raman spectroscopy further corroborates the presence of various secondary phases at the horizontal grain boundaries and detects residual CZTS signals (Fig. 3f), indicating incomplete phase transformation in these regions10,19.

CZTSSe-B sample not only facilitates carrier transport but also reduces non-radiative recombination compared to the CZTSSe-G sample, thereby enhancing overall device performance. The elimination of horizontal grain boundaries and secondary phases manifests in the optical characterization results as a marked suppression of non-radiative recombination. Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra (Fig. 3g) reveal nearly identical bandgaps for both samples, yet the CZTSSe-B sample exhibits significantly higher PL intensity. Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) decay measurements (Fig. 3h) further show that CZTSSe-B has a fluorescence lifetime of 8.9 ns which is substantially longer than the 3.3 ns observed from CZTSSe-G, indicating effectively suppressed carrier recombination32–35.

The carrier transport and recombination characteristics of the two samples are further investigated using electrical characterization techniques. Modulated electrical transient measurements are employed to probe charge transport and recombination dynamics under different bias voltages, quantifying both bulk and interfacial charge losses, thereby effectively evaluating the impact of thermal-decoupled processing during initial selenization on device performance. Figure 4a, b display the transient photocurrent (TPC) decay of CZTSSe devices under different bias voltages. The CZTSSe-B device exhibits obviously smaller decay time than the CZTSSe-G device, indicating that the CZTSSe-B device has better charge transport ability. The elimination of horizontal grain boundaries and their induced voids and poor contact is an important beneficial reason for this result. The charge extraction efficiency (ηE) and charge collection efficiency (ηC) are derived from the τTPC and τTPV results, respectively, as shown in Fig. 4d. With reverse crystallization suppressed via the thermal-decoupled selenization strategy, CZTSSe-B device exhibits markedly enhanced ηE and ηC. As the bias voltage increases, the ηE of CZTSSe-B device declines more slowly and remains higher than ~70% even at the maximum voltage, while the ηE of the CZTSSe-G device declines to ~30%. The ηC of CZTSSe-B device remains close to 100% at maximum voltage, while the CZTSSe-G device is below 90%. The improvement in ηE is consistent with reduced bulk defect density within the absorber layer, correlating with suppression of secondary phases and carrier recombination losses. The enhancement in ηC indicates diminished charge losses due to interfacial defects, correlating with elimination of poor contact horizontal grain boundaries37,39.

Furthermore, we characterize the bulk defect density by performing drive-level capacitance profiling (DLCP) measurements at varying frequencies (Fig. 4e, f)40–42. The difference in the drive-level defect densities (NDL) obtained at low (1 kHz) and high (200 kHz) frequencies reveals that the bulk defect concentrations in the CZTSSe-G and CZTSSe-B devices are 3.4 × 1015 cm−3 and 2.0 × 1015 cm−3. Obviously, the elimination of horizontal boundaries indeed helps reduce bulk defect concentration, finally leading to high-quality CZTSSe absorbers and high-performance photovoltaic devices.

Solar cell performance and characterizations

Finally, we fabricate solar cell devices based on CZTSSe-G and CZTSSe-B absorbers to evaluate their performance. The results demonstrate that devices prepared using CZTSSe-B samples exhibit significant enhancements in performance parameters, particularly in VOC and FF43, as shown in Fig. 5a. The champion CZTSSe-B device achieves a short-circuit current density (JSC) of 37.1 mA cm−2, a VOC of 582.1 mV, and a FF of 72.9%, resulting in a PCE of 15.7% (Fig. 5b), significantly higher than the 13.6% of CZTSSe-G. Based on the EQE spectra, the optical bandgap of both samples is calculated to be approximately 1.1 eV. The higher JSC of the CZTSSe‑B device correlates with its enhanced EQE response over the 600–1200 nm wavelength range, indicating improved collection of photogenerated carriers from the bulk absorber. The comprehensive comparison of previous published certified high-efficiency device data for CZTSSe solar cells with bandgaps around 1.1 ± 0.05 eV4,5,19,22,31,36,40,44–52 is provided in Fig. 5e with details in Supplementary Table 1, where a marked improvement in VOC × FF underscores our advance in overcoming the fundamental performance bottleneck in CZTSSe photovoltaics43.

Moreover, the effectiveness and broader applicability of the thermal rate decoupling strategy is established in Cu(In,Ga)Se2 (CIGS) solar cells, which employ a similar device architecture. Using an identical experimental approach, we control the evaporation rate of the Se source by employing crucibles made of different materials, denoted as CIGS-G and CIGS-B, respectively. The results reveal that CIGS-G has the same trend as CZTSSe-G, exhibiting a bilayered grain structure from reverse crystallization that severely impedes carrier transport. In contrast, thermal-decoupled CIGS-B suppresses this at the back contact, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 16. XPS analysis of mechanically exfoliated back-interface samples quenched at 60 s of selenization shows trends consistent with the CZTSSe study, in which Se participation in back-interface reactions during early selenization significantly increases copper binding energy (Supplementary Fig. 17). Concurrently, the PCE improves from 13.4% to 15.6% (Fig. 5f, g and Supplementary Fig. 18). Supplementary Fig. 19 presents the EQE spectra for cells fabricated from the CIGS-G and CIGS-B samples; the integrated current densities are 29.4 mA cm-2 and 30.8 mA cm-2, respectively, which are consistent with the J–V measurement results. The successful implementation of thermal-decoupled selenization strategy in CIGS solar cells demonstrates its generality and applicability to chalcogenide-based photovoltaic technologies.

Discussion

In summary, we identify the origin of reverse crystallization in CZTSSe absorbers as the formation of a low‑melting‑point Cu(S,Se) liquid phase at the Mo/back-contact interface at low temperature during initial selenization. This interfacial reaction drives anomalous upward grain growth, leading to horizontal grain boundaries riddled with voids, secondary phases, and defects that act as potent non-radiative recombination centers, thereby limiting overall device performance. To overcome this fundamental bottleneck, we present a thermal-decoupled selenization strategy that temporally separates Se supply from detrimental back-interface reactions at the relevant temperature range. This establishes a vertical Se concentration gradient that suppresses Se participation at the back interface at low temperature, promoting the back-interface evolution along the high‑melting‑point Cu2(S,Se) solid phase pathway and enabling seamless top-down columnar grain growth throughout the absorber. The resulting microstructure achieves the elimination of detrimental horizontal boundaries and related derivative issues, thereby enhancing carrier transport and suppressing recombination losses. As a result, we achieve a marked improvement in VOC × FF and a champion PCE of 15.7% (certified 15.3%) for CZTSSe solar cells. Looking forward, if the Se source and the precursor film can be fundamentally controlled with independent real-time temperature ramping within the same reaction vessel, we believe that further efficiency improvements can be achieved.

Methods

Materials

CuCl (99.999%, Alfa), Zn(Ac)2⋅2H2O (99%, Aladdin), SnCl4⋅5H2O (98%, Aladdin), AgCl (99.9%, Alfa), Tu (99%, Alfa), 2-methoxyethanol (99%, Alfa), CdSO4·8/3H2O (99.99%, Aladdin), ammonia solution (25%-28%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.). ZnO/ITO targets (99.99%, ZhongNuo Advanced Material (Beijing) Technology Co., Ltd.). CuSe (99.99%, Meryer), Cu2Se (99.99%, Meryer), CuS (99.99%, ZhongNuo Advanced Material (Beijing) Technology Co., Ltd.), Cu2S (99.99%, ZhongNuo Advanced Material (Beijing) Technology Co., Ltd.). All materials were used as received without further purification.

Device fabrication

Precursor solution preparation: The precursor system comprises CZTS and LZTS solutions. The CZTS solution is prepared by dissolving CuCl (0.3742 g), AgCl (0.0604 g), Zn(Ac)2·2H2O (0.6585 g), SnCl4·5H2O (0.8940 g), and Tu (1.5186 g) in 10 mL 2-methoxyethanol (MOE). The solution is stirred at 60 °C for 2 h to yield clear, colorless solutions.

Molybdenum (Mo)-coated soda-lime glass substrates were sequentially cleaned with detergent and absolute ethanol. A precursor solution was spin-coated onto the substrates at 3000 rpm for 25 s, followed by annealing at 280 °C for 2 min. This process was repeated until a CZTS precursor film with a thickness of approximately 1.8 µm was obtained.

The precursor film was placed in a graphite box along with evaporation crucibles of identical geometry, each loaded with Se pellets. Selenization was performed at 520 °C for 20 min in an argon-filled rapid thermal processing furnace to obtain the CZTSSe absorber. For the thermal-coupled condition, evaporation crucibles made of the same material as the graphite box were used. In contrast, for the thermal-decoupled condition, crucibles with thermal conductivity lower than that of graphite—such as silicon carbide, boron nitride, aluminum oxide, or quartz—were employed.

The furnace pressure was maintained at ambient atmospheric pressure (~101 kPa) throughout the selenization process. Real-time monitoring was performed using a digital pressure gauge (EYS 80, accuracy ±0.1%). A gas flow controller (PIPG SC 102) continuously supplied high-purity argon at 80 sccm, maintaining steady gas flow with the exhaust path open to ensure a stable pressure environment.

The absorber films were then immersed into a mixture of CdSO4·8/3H2O, Tu, and ammonia solution at 65 °C to complete the deposition of the CdS buffer layer (~50 nm). Next, the films were subjected to heterojunction annealing treatment at 90 °C for 5.5 h. Subsequently, i-ZnO (~50 nm) and ITO (~200 nm) were sequentially magnetron sputtered on the top of the buffer layer. Finally, the Ag grid electrode (~100 nm) and MgF2 (~100 nm) antireflection coating were sequentially thermal-evaporated onto the ITO layer to complete the fabrication of the CZTSSe solar cell device.

Characterization

The current density-voltage (J-V) curves of the solar cells were measured by using Oriel Sol3a, Class AAA, Newport, 100 mW/cm2. The active area of the device is 0.058 cm2 determined by a black metal mask. The EQE was tested using QE-R-RCG3018 (ENLITECH) and calibrated using Si and Ge diodes as references. DLCP measurements were carried out using AC amplitude varied from 40 mV to 140 mV. DC bias voltage sweeping from 1.0 V to 0.6 V. The Modulated transient photocurrent and photovoltage (M-TPV/TPC) measurements were performed in which the devices were excited with a 532-nm pulsed laser (Brio, 10 Hz, 4 ns), and the decay process was recorded by a digital oscilloscope (Tektronix DPO 7104). Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) examinations were performed using a Hitachi S4800 scanning electron microscope, which was equipped with an energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) attachment (EMAX). The X ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS, American Thermo Fischer, ESCALAB Xi+) was used for element analysis. XRD patterns were collected using an X-ray diffractometer with Cu Ka as the radiation source (SmartLab9KW, RIGAKU). Raman spectra were carried out on a Raman spectrometer (Lab-RAM HR Evolution, HORIBA) using 532 nm laser diodes as the excitation sources, respectively. KPFM images were obtained on a BRUKER Dimension Icon. Steady-state and time-resolved PL spectra were obtained on a PL spectrometer (NanoLog, HORIBA), excited with a picosecond-pulsed diode laser with a wavelength of 440 nm.
