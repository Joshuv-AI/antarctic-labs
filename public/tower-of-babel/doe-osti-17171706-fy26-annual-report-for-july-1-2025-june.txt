1 IDEAS-WATERSHEDS OVERVIEW

Phase 2 of the Interoperable Design of Extreme-scale Application Software – Watersheds (IDEAS-
Watersheds) project is addressing a scientific challenge common across multiple Environmental System
Science (ESS) projects—improving the representations of biogeochemical processes and their
hydrological controls in the upland areas, streams, floodplains, and river corridors of watersheds. To that
end, IDEAS-Watersheds will continue to advance an ecosystem of AI-assisted interacting software tools
and workflows that will be shared across projects and with the broader community. In addition, the
project will train a cohort of young computational scientists and provide them with the necessary skills to
nurture the emerging ecosystem of interoperable modeling software as part of interdisciplinary project
teams. Those outcomes of the project will significantly enhance our capabilities and scientific
productivity in computational watershed science, thus advancing ESS’s watersheds programs toward its
overarching goal of predictive understanding of watershed hydrobiogeochemical function.
IDEAS–Watersheds is organized around three research activities and four shared infrastructure activities,
and advances software development methodologies and engagement in the growing community-driven
software ecosystem. Research activities are pursued jointly with the multiscale watershed SFA
partnerships and strive to develop process-level understanding of watershed function. Shared
infrastructure activities provide cross cutting support for the research activities through the software
ecosystem, including enhancing sustainability with the Software Stewardship activity, developing
nationally consistent data sets in the Integrated Hydrologic Simulation Infrastructure (IHSI) activity,
addressing land surface model integration with Integrated Hydrology in the Land Model Interface (LMI)
activity, and training. Advances in AI/ML have figured prominently in all our activities, exploring best
practices and potential gains in ModEx, demonstrating gains in code development, and investigating the
community impact and potential acceleration of watershed science with agentic workflows.

2 REVIEW OF SCIENTIFIC PROGRESS

2.1 Research Activities
2.1.1 Watershed Function SFA Partnership: East River Use Case
Contributors (LBNL): Andrew Graus, Sergi Molins, Jinyun Tang, Chuyang Liu, Marshall Worsham
The partnership with the LBNL-SLAC’s Watershed Function SFA (WF-SFA) continued to develop and
demonstrate capability for advanced modeling of watershed hydrology coupled to ecohydrological
processes, using the East River watershed, CO as a use case. Our phased approach involves the
enhancement of ATS with the addition of a process kernel (PK) from EcoSIM, a comprehensive, process‐
rich ecohydrological simulator (Tang et al., 2024). This new PK computes mass and energy fluxes
associated with ecosystem processes and returns them as sources and sinks in the balance equations ATS
solves. We have completed the main phase by developing the interface between ATS and EcoSIM, and
implementing evapotranspiration (ET) and snow dynamics in the EcoSIM PK. As part of this work, we
have issued two pull requests, one that added EcoSIM as a third-party library to the Amanzi-ATS
framework (Graus, 2026a), which underwent a review and was approved, and a second that implemented
the EcoSIM PK in ATS (Graus, 2026b), which is under review. The interface to access EcoSIM
capabilities resides within the EcoSIM repository and is constantly upgraded via minor pull requests
following repository best practices.
The simulation of snowmelt dynamics at the Snodgrass catchment has been used as a benchmark to
evaluate the performance of the coupled capability (Figure 1), by building on earlier WF-SFA work
(Wang et al., 2025) that established a baseline before the EcoSIM PK was developed. In connection with
the 2025 NEON Airborne Observation Platform campaign, which included CHESS canopy data
collection, the Snodgrass simulations have also been used to inform input data formatting and to identify

work is underway to allow for multiple plant functional types (PFTs) per column connected to EcoSIM's

In our phased approach, we will move to adding additional flux contributions from EcoSIM processes to
the ATS mass balance calculations by extending the EcoSIM interface and PK in ATS. First,
biogeochemical (BCG) fluxes will be added to solute mass balance, and then vegetation dynamics and
plant hydrology will contribute to both water and solute mass balance. A manuscript describing the new
coupled model is planned that will include a technical description of the approach and the Snodgrass
simulations as a demonstration.
2.1.2 Watershed Dynamics and Evolution SFA Partnership: East Fork Poplar Creek Use Case
Contributors (ORNL): Bo Gao, Phong Le, Saubhagya Rathore, Ethan T. Coon, Scott L. Painter, J. Gomez-
Velez
The partnership with ORNL’s Watershed Dynamics and Evolution (WaDE) SFA continued to develop
and demonstrate capability for advanced modeling of watershed hydrology and whole-stream metabolism,
using East Fork Poplar Creek, TN, (EFPC) as a use case. We are using our ATS-based multiscale
framework for reactive transport in stream corridors (Painter, 2018; Jan et al., 2021), an IDEAS-
Watershed product that represents hyporheic exchange, transport along diverse flow paths within the
hyporheic zone, and reactions within the hyporheic zone and surface water. We published a journal article
(Gomez-Velez et al., 2026) on the multiscale model configured to represent gross primary productivity
(GPP) in the water column and ecosystem respiration (ER) in the hyporheic zone and water column with
rate-limited transfer between the domains. A manuscript that uses our high-resolution flow model of
EFPC to better understand stream network expansion and contraction is in review (Rathore et al., 2026).
Combining our high-resolution flow model of EFPC with our multiscale stream metabolism model, we
continued to refine a multiscale model for dissolved oxygen dynamics at full watershed scales.
Preliminary comparisons to dissolved oxygen measurements from the WaDE SFA show the simulations
reproduce overall magnitude and seasonality of dissolved oxygen concentrations (Figure 2). This first-of-
a-kind capability uses ATS’s capability to handle multiple interacting meshes, developed previously in
IDEAS (Garimella et al., 2014). In FY26, we implemented models (Savoy et al., 2021) in ATS to
estimate solar radiation incident on the stream surface, accounting for stream aspect, bank height, canopy
leaf area index, and vegetation height. Incident radiation is needed for surface energy balance in thermal
hydrology simulation and for stream metabolism modeling. This capability was previously implemented

2.1.3 River Corridor SFA Partnership: Yakima River Use Case
Contributors (PNNL): Xingyuan Chen, Glenn Hammond, Hoang Tran, Faria Tuz Zahara, Zhi Li
The partnership with PNNL’s River Corridor SFA started to explore a multi-agent framework that drives
process-based models, e.g., PFLOTRAN, to answer scientific questions posed in natural language.
PFLOTRAN remains as the core of the framework, ensuring scientific rigor, while a team of AI agents
orchestrates the workflow around them (see Figure 1): gathering information, designing numerical
experiments, executing them efficiently and reproducibly, and validating and interpreting the results. In
doing so, the framework makes traditional model experimentation more streamlined, efficient, and
reproducible, lowering the effort needed to move from a scientific question to a defensible answer. We
demonstrate its capabilities on questions ranging from simple to complex and show how it can orchestrate
weak coupling between ELM and PFLOTRAN, extending the framework from running individual models
to connecting them in order to address questions that require coupled models. The code is available at
https://github.com/hoangtv1899/multi-agent-framework.
We have also started to develop a PFLOTRAN MCP server. It now supports the full modeling lifecycle
through more than 40 tools, including input generation, ensemble execution, output analysis, and
parameter estimation using iterative ensemble smoother or neural network-based inverse mapping
workflows. In addition, the Lambda-PFLOTRAN pipeline has been integrated into the MCP server,
enabling dissolved organic matter from field FTICR-MS data to be translated into thermodynamically
binned, kinetically resolved biogeochemical reaction networks, while a differentiable JAX surrogate
reproduces Lambda kinetics at faster speed. Together, these capabilities enable scalable uncertainty
quantification, sensitivity analysis, and gradient-based inference for systems involving microbial
processes control mineral mobilization, sequestration, or recovery.

2.2 Shared Infrastructure Activities
2.2.1 Software Ecosystem and Software Stewardship Activity
Contributors (LLNL, ORNL, PNNL, LBNL)
Software Stewards for each of the primary codes in the software ecosystem continue to play a critical role
in ensuring its sustainability and effectiveness. The Stewards are Ethan Coon (ATS), Glenn Hammond
(PFLOTRAN), Sergi Molins (Alquimia, CrunchFlow), and Steven Smith (ParFlow/EcoSLIM), each
supported at approximately 0.2 FTE. They have continued to enhance the ecosystem for AI-enabled
scientific workflows and to leverage AI in software development practices. Alquimia’s CMake build
system was upgraded, a Dockerfile was implemented to support automated containerization, and a
persistent context markdown file was added to facilitate agentic development workflows. ParFlow began
using large language models (LLMs) as an additional code-review resource; notably, Claude was cited as
the third-highest pull request contributor to ParFlow from March through June 2026. The Stewards
continue to share AI best practices across the ecosystem to accelerate adoption of effective approaches.
Support for the ecosystem user community remains a central focus of the stewardship effort. Stewardship
responsibilities are being distributed more broadly to improve long-term sustainability, with ATS
stewardship now shared among several contributors at ORNL and ParFlow establishing a monthly
leadership and developer team meeting. The ATS and ParFlow mailing lists have grown to 156 and 162
members, respectively. Community participation continues to increase, with more user questions being
answered by community members, helping build a vibrant, self-sustaining ecosystem that continues to
expand within the BER project portfolio.
Software development and maintenance activities have remained active across the ecosystem, including
feature additions, bug fixes, and continuous integration testing. Watershed Workflow Version 2.0 was
released following a major refactor that modernized the software stack around GeoPandas and xarray

(partially supported by an ESS-DIVE community project), significantly improving interoperability with
tools such as Basin3D and other hydrologic data libraries. Alquimia received a community-contributed

enhancements to snow-process and transpiration modeling, added reservoir support, and completed
significant GPU and solver updates. The releases of the primary codes managed by the Software Stewards

Community support activities will continue to be a core focus of stewardship efforts, alongside
maintaining and enhancing ecosystem capabilities for the user community. We plan to expand the AI/ML
interfaces and data-exchange capabilities of the ecosystem codes. Given the rapid pace of change in
AI/ML technologies, individual code teams often struggle to keep pace; a shared software ecosystem is
essential for efficiently adopting and integrating new capabilities. AI/ML is also driving changes in
computing architectures, and the ecosystem will continue to share successful optimization strategies and
best practices across codes.
2.2.2 Land Model Interface (LMI) Activity
Contributors (LANL, LBNL, ORNL)
We continued to support our role in the development of integrated hydro-terrestrial models described in
the community white paper we led (Painter 2024), which not only motivated the need for two
complementary approaches, but also the broader benefits that these capabilities provide. Specifically, the
Watershed Function SFA (as described above) is coupling ATS with EcoSIM, a modular land surface
modeling library that incorporates detailed mechanistically-based process representations that can be
linked with measurable plant-soil-microbial traits at smaller scales. In addition, we continued to follow
the development in COMPASS-GLM of the coupling of ATS within ELM to replace ELM's column-
based hydrologic model with a 3D surface and subsurface flow and reactive transport model.

Within the Shared Infrastructure/LMI activity we will help play a coordinating role to facilitate the
deployment and support of these emerging capabilities for the ESS Community. Since COMPASS-GLM
is transitioning to a new project this fall, IDEAS-Watersheds is planning to continue to support the testing
and further development of the ELM-ATS coupling and associated AI enhanced workflow tools. We will
also explore the defining benchmark studies to compare and contrast its capabilities with ATS-EcoSIM.
2.2.3 Integrated Hydrologic Simulation Infrastructure (IHSI) Activity
Contributors (UA, Princeton, LLNL): Laura Condon, Reed Maxwell, Steve Smith
The focus of the IHSI activity is enhancing the Hydroshare national hydrologic data platform to provide
hydro-geo fabrics across modeling platforms and create tools that are AI-ready. The quality of the
national HydroData API, associated Python packages, and documentation demonstrated this AI readiness
when Ethan Coon used Claude Code to rapidly prototype an interface to Watershed Workflow making
our subsurface data available to the ATS. Also using Claude Code, we were able to quickly update, add
new tests, and merge an abandoned feature branch to support subsurface reactive transport in ParFlow
through the Alquimia interface. Despite the interest our users had in this capability the branch had been
abandoned when a postdoc left for industry, and without Claude it would not have been feasible to
recover it. Continued delays in the subcontract renewal caused other activities to be pushed to FY27.

We will integrate the previously developed national reservoir database into the national platform,
enabling this significant human-systems component to be represented in our models, and ensure all our
data is findable and available through EES-DIVE. We will work with the Watershed Workflow team to
complete testing of their interface and ensure all our data is available to ATS. In addition, we will update

2.2.4 Training, Community Building, and Outreach
Contributors: IDEAS-Watersheds Team
IDEAS-Watersheds continues to use a multi-layered approach to training, community building and
outreach. Within the project we continued our regular Modeling Case Studies and Software Best
Practices seminar series to help guide early career staff to adapt to the rapid growth of AI/ML capabilities
and encourage collaboration across the SFAs partnerships. For example, we hosted short presentations
and a panel discussion on advances and limitations of data driven AI models and the future role of
physics-based models in this new AI integrated paradigm for scientific discovery (Painter and Destouni,
2026; Ma et al., 2026; Condon, 2025). In addition, we held a demonstration and instructional session on
using Claude Code (Ethan Coon) to accelerate workflow development and model setup, in this case
prototyping enhancements to Watershed Workflow to use SCREAM output for atmospheric forcing. A
prototype that would have taken days, was accomplished in hours. We continued the Plenary seminar
series this year, also striving to bridge science with advances in AI/ML, such as Andrew Bennett's (U of
A) talk on emerging techniques in physics guided and physics inspired machine learning.
At the ESS community level, the IDEAS-Watersheds PI (David Moulton) and ESS-DIVE PI (Charu
Varadharajan) co-led the joint Cyberinfrastructure Working Groups (CIWGs) and ESS-DIVE Annual
Meeting designed to increase community awareness and enhance collaboration. The first day was
focused on the CIWGs and had over 100 virtual attendees, including 5 BER program managers. In
addition to the very popular flash talks we had two sessions in the afternoon to explore different aspects
of ModEx in the Era of AI. These sessions spanned the shifting role of models and the potential of
foundation models, best practices for LLM-assisted writing, the growing suite of tools that accelerate
code development, and how we can build Agentic workflows that will potentially make all our models
more accessible and easier to use. The discussions and feedback from this meeting are now helping drive
the activities of the working groups this summer, and its popularity has motivated a fall meeting that will
capture our progress and will welcome engagement with the new Genesis projects.
The project co-sponsored the second ATS Short Course September 8-10, 2025 including one day at
ORNL focused on developing Early Career researchers understanding of conceptual models and two days
in Knoxville, TN, focused on using ATS for hydro-bio-geochemical applications. The short course was
attended by approximately 30 people in person and another approximately 40 people virtually. Training
material and video recordings are published online, enabling new users to learn from this course’s
material. In addition, the IHSI team put on a ParFlow short course June 11-12, 2026 in conjunction with
the Groundwater Modeling and More conference at Princeton University. We had 10 in person attendees
and all materials have been published online for easy access from the broader ParFlow community.

We will continue to enrich this multi-layered approach in FY27. The popular internal and Plenary
seminar series will continue to provide a critical venue to share, learn, and develop best practices to help
the community use the rapidly growing AI/ML tools effectively for their science. We will continue to
enhance the AI readiness (e.g., documentation, testing, containerization, support for AI coding tools, and
more standardized output), and AI capabilities of the software ecosystem (e.g., Agentic workflows,
AI/ML model components) and develop short course material. For example, we will further develop the
ATS agentic workflow capabilities using the open-source URSA (Universal Research and Scientific
Agent) agentic harness which not only supports standard execution and visualization but also explores
allowing users to describe their conceptual model with natural language, identifying potential matches
with relevant demonstration templates, and assisting the users in modifying those demos to better fit their
specific needs.

Appendix 5: Codes in the Software Ecosystem

The IDEAS-Watersheds Software Ecosystem is comprised of Primary Codes and Primary Workflow Tools,
in which our team has an active development role, as well as Third-Party Codes and Third-Party Workflow
Tools, which our team are users. We note that Primary Codes include domain science components and
libraries (e.g., Alquimia).

Primary Codes
Alquimia
Alquimia is a biogeochemistry Application Program Interface and wrapper library that provides unified
access to biogeochemistry capabilities from mature geochemical codes, allowing any subsurface flow and
transport simulator to access a range of functionality. Alquimia is not an implementation of a
biogeochemistry reaction library and does not do any geochemical calculations. It provides unified data
structures and subroutine signatures so that existing geochemical codes perform these calculations.
Currently, Alquimia provides access to the geochemical codes PFLOTRAN and CrunchFlow and can be
used for the simulation of aqueous complexation reactions, radioactive decay, ion exchange, surface
complexation, and mineral dissolution-precipitation.

Amanzi
Amanzi provides a flexible and extensible flow and reactive transport simulation capability for
environmental applications. It includes general polyhedral mesh infrastructure, which leverages MSTK,
advanced discretizations of process models, including traditional finite volume schemes, mimetic finite
differences, and nonlinear finite volumes. In addition, it provides advanced nonlinear solvers, such as
Nonlinear Krylov Acceleration (NKA) and Anderson Acceleration, and leverages Trilinos-ML and Hypre
Algebraic Multigrid for scalable solvers. The multiphysics framework, Arcos, provides a flexibility for
hierarchical weak and strong coupling of processes with subcycling. Geochemistry support is provided
through the Alquimia interface library and can use the geochemistry engine from PFLOTRAN or
CrunchFlow. The code is parallel and leverages Trilinos and PETSc. of open-source parallel frameworks
such as Trilinos, PETSc. Amanzi is used to model contaminant migration at various DOE waste sites
(e.g., Hanford, and Savannah River), and is generally applicable to groundwater contaminant migration
under partially saturated, nonisothermal conditions and its interaction with surface water. Amanzi is
jointly developed by LANL, LBNL, and PNNL, and ORNL as an open source project under the three-
clause BSD license.

ATS
The Advanced Terrestrial Simulator is a code for solving ecosystem-based, integrated, distributed
hydrology. It builds on the multi-physics framework and toolsets (mesh infrastructure, discretizations,
solvers) provided by Amanzi and is a key driver of development of the flexible multiphysics framework
Acros. Capabilities are largely based on solving various forms of Richards equation coupled to a surface
flow equation, along with the needed sources and sinks for ecosystem and climate models. This can (but
need not) include thermal processes (especially ice for frozen soils), evapo-transpiration, albedo-driven
surface energy balances, snow, biogeochemistry, plant dynamics, deformation, transport, and much more.
It is unique in its thermal integrated hydrology capabilities, which includes thermal energy with freeze/thaw
processes in both the surface and subsurface water, and its reactive transport capabilities, which are also

IDEAS–Watersheds 18

coupled in both surface and subsurface water. ATS is jointly developed by LANL, LBNL, and ORNL as
an open-source project under the three-clause BSD license.

CrunchFlow
CrunchFlow is an open-source software package for simulating reactive flow and transport applied to a
variety of problems in the Earth and environmental sciences that can include an arbitrary number of
reactions, including mineral dissolution/precipitation, ion exchange, surface complexation, and microbially
mediated reactions (Steefel et al, 2015). Two approaches are available at runtime for coupling reactions and
transport: (1) a global-implicit approach that solves transport and reactions simultaneously, and (2) an
operator-splitting approach based on a sequential non-iterative solution.

EcoSLIM
EcoSLIM is a Lagrangian particle-tracking model designed to operate with integrated hydrologic models.
(Reed M. Maxwell et al., 2018) Particles are initialized in the subsurface or added at the surface via
precipitation fluxes. Spatially gridded velocity, pressure, and saturation fields are used to move particles
through the domain and particles can exit via overland flow or evapotranspiration. In addition to steady-
state simulations, EcoSLIM can also use transient model outputs to develop time-varying residence-time
distributions. The incorporation of precipitation sources from the land-surface model also allows for the
direct evaluation of source water contribution (e.g., rain versus snow). EcoSLIM has been demonstrated
using outputs from the ParFlow-CLM hydrologic model; however, the model can easily be combined with
other hydrologic tools that generate similar gridded outputs. In proof-of-concept simulations, EcoSLIM has
been used to explore spatial variability in plant water sources (i.e., old vs. young water), connections
between plan rooting depth and ET residence-time distributions, and seasonal variability in residence-time
distributions and source water partitioning. (Reed M. Maxwell et al., 2018)
modeling workflows.

ParFlow
The integrated watershed model ParFlow solves saturated and variably saturated flow in three dimensions
using either an orthogonal or a terrain-following, semi-structured mesh that enables fine vertical resolution
near the land surface and deep (~1 km) confined and unconfined aquifers. ParFlow models dynamic surface
and subsurface flow solving the simplified shallow water equations implicitly coupled to Richards’
equation; this allows for two-way exchanges and intermittency in stream and river flow. ParFlow uses
robust linear and nonlinear solution techniques and exhibits efficient parallel scaling to large processor
counts, more than 450K cores, enabling very large-extent simulations with fine spatial resolution.
In addition, ParFlow is coupled to the land-surface model CLM, providing a comprehensive representation
of vegetation, snow, and land-atmosphere water and energy fluxes. CLM is unique in the land surface
modeling community because it is a module that is called from within ParFlow. This modeling framework
has been shown to represent the observed range of temporal scales and non-stationary behavior making it
appropriate for the proposed work. A 1 km lateral resolution ParFlow-CLM model of CONUS has been
developed and used to evaluate large-scale controls on groundwater configuration and connections between
lateral groundwater flow and land-surface partitioning. ParFlow-CLM is currently distributed with ParFlow
through the GitHub repo. In the IDEAS project the CLM land model interface will be generalized and also
be made available as a separate library that other codes in the eco-system can connect use still within the
ParFlow GitHub repo.

PFLOTRAN
PFLOTRAN is an open-source, state-of-the-art massively parallel subsurface flow and reactive transport
code. PFLOTRAN solves a system of generally nonlinear partial differential equations describing
multiphase, multicomponent, and multiscale reactive flow and transport in porous materials. The code is
designed to run on massively parallel computing architectures as well as workstations and laptops.
PFLOTRAN is written in object-oriented, free-formatted FORTRAN 2003. The reactive transport
equations can be solved using either a fully implicit Newton-Raphson algorithm or the less robust operator-
splitting method. Geochemical capabilities include aqueous complexation, sorption, mineral precipitation
and dissolution, and microbially mediated reactions.

Primary Workflow Tools
Watershed Workflow
Watershed Workflow is a python library and collection of Jupyter notebooks enabling the discovery,
acquisition, curation, and integration of watershed geometry, land cover, soil properties, and meteorological
data for Integrated Hydro-Terrestrial Models. To do this, Watershed Workflow leverages open data
repositories from a variety of US governmental agencies, including USGS, USDA, DOE, and NFS.
Watershed Workflow queries REST APIs to determine available data, downloads that data from the
repository, then provides a variety of tools to manipulate and standardize that data to allow users to inform
complete simulations. Workflows are provided for generating a first ATS hydrologic simulation on any
Hydrologic Unit Code or watershed shapefile in the United States and generating river-routing networks
for use in river corridor models. Watershed Workflow is currently in use in a variety of DOE-led projects
to enable the rapid generation of data-informed simulations at complex, varied sites.

MAGIEC
The MAGIEC pipeline enables building metagenome-based reactive transport modeling through the
following steps. First, MAGIEC constructs and gapfills metabolic networks by metagenome annotations
using the KBase. Second, it generates a reduced-order flux balance analysis (FBA) model using artificial
neural networks (ANNs) for computational efficiency. Lastly, the ANN-bases surrogate model is coupled
with reactive transport models. As a core element for the last step, the MAGIEC pipeline identifies kinetics
from substrate chemical formulae using the thermodynamic theory known as the lamba model.

Third-Party Codes
EcoSIM
EcoSIM is a modular ecosystem model that simulates plant-soil-microbial interactions using a mechanistic
approach. It builds upon the ecosys code, which has a proven track record in model development and diverse
applications. EcoSIM stands out from other land biogeochemical models due to its comprehensive
representation of plant, soil, and microbial processes, as well as its ability to dynamically reassemble plant
and microbial communities in response to disturbances and ecosystem management. EcoSIM includes
detailed plant and soil dynamics, encompassing complete phenology cycles from seed germination to seed
reproduction, and detailed canopy structures. Root growth is fueled by carbon and nutrients, and fine roots
and mycorrhizae develop based on the availability of these nutrients, soil temperature, and moisture. The
model also represents microbial processes, including decomposition, organic matter transformation, and
microbial growth, with the growth of microorganisms formulated using the substrate uptake-driven Pirt-
model. The model integrates water dynamics, plant physiology, and biogeochemistry through close
ecohydrology coupling, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of ecosystem processes. Additionally,
EcoSIM enables consideration of disturbance and management scenarios.

ELM
The E3SM land model (ELM) predicts interactions of the land surface with the atmosphere, including
carbon, water and energy fluxes from natural vegetation, soil, snow cover, lakes, urban systems and
croplands. ELM has been included in fully coupled E3SM simulations both with prescribed static vegetation
and with interactive vegetation and soil biogeochemistry that includes nitrogen and phosphorus cycling and
a representation of non-structural carbohydrates. ELM vegetation and soil parameters are informed using
an uncertainty quantification framework, which allows for parameter sensitivity analysis and calibration
using model ensembles for selected grid cells. ELM is intended for large spatial scales with coarse
horizontal discretization and uses multiple non-interacting 1-D columns to represent soil hydrology with
parameterized runoff. Within E3SM, ELM is coupled with the Model for Scale Adaptive River Transport
(MOSART), which routes the runoff to predict river flow and freshwater input to the oceans. ELM also
includes representations of land use change and natural disturbance such as wildfire.

FATES
The Functionally Assembled Terrestrial Ecosystem Simulator (FATES) is a next-generation numerical
terrestrial ecosystem model that simulates and predicts growth, death, and regeneration of plants and
subsequent tree size distributions. When coupled into an earth system model, FATES allows representation
of potential ecosystem responses to environmental resource availability (water, light, and nutrients),
climate, and atmospheric compositional changes—and how ecosystem change alters earth system
dynamics.

SWAT
Soil & Water Assessment Tool. A watershed model developed for the USDA Agricultural Research
Service. SWAT can be used to predict the impact of land management practices on water, sediment and
agricultural chemical yields in large complex watersheds. It can deal with varying soils, land use and
management conditions over long periods of time.

Third-Party Workflow Tools
LaGriT/PyLaGriT
LaGriT is a software tool for generating, editing and optimizing multi-material unstructured finite element
grids; it also maintains the geometric integrity of complex input volumes, surfaces, and geologic data and
produces an optimal grid (Delaunay, Voronoi) elements. The data structures used in the code are compact
and powerful and expandable to include hybrid meshes (tet, hex, prism, pyramid, quadrilateral, triangle,
line), however the main algorithms are for triangle and tetrahedral meshes. The LaGriT tools are used in
many projects including ASCEM meshing for Amanzi, Discrete Fracture Networks (DFN), Arctic
Permafrost, and Subsurface Flow and Transport models using FEHM and PFLOTRAN. PyLaGriT provides
a python interface to LaGriT capabilities, making it easier to incorporate mesh generation in modeling
workflow.

TINerator
TINerator is a Python module for the easy creation of unstructured 3D and 2.5D meshes from GIS data
sources. Digital Elevation Maps (DEMs) can be quickly turned into unstructured triangulated meshes, and
then further refined by the import of flowline shapefiles or automatically through watershed delineation.
Advanced layering and attribute functions then allow for a complex subsurface to be defined. TINerator
comes with a host of 2D and 3D visualization functions, allowing the user to view the status of the mesh at

every step in the workflow. In addition, there are geometrical tools for removing triangles outside of a
polygon, generating quality analytics on the mesh, adding cell- and node-based attributes to a mesh, and
much more.
