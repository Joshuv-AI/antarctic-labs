Quantification of magnetic interactions in van der Waals heterostructures using Lorentz transmission electron microscopy and electron holography
Joachim Dahl Thomsen, Qianqian Lan, Nikolai S. Kiselev, Eva Duft, Arslan Rehmat, Zdeněk Sofer, Rafal E. Dunin-Borkowski

Abstract
Magnetic van der Waals (vdW) materials are promising for memory and logic applications because of their highly tunable magnetic properties and compatibility with vdW heterostructure devices. However, in conventional plan-view measurements, coupling between magnetic textures in stacked layers is difficult to resolve because the magnetic signal is integrated over the sample thickness. Here, these interactions are quantified in Fe 3 GeTe 2 (FGT)/graphite/FGT heterostructures using cross-sectional Lorentz transmission electron microscopy and electron holography, enabling reconstruction of the local magnetic field within and between the layers. Domain alignment weakens with increasing FGT separation, yielding a dipolar coupling length scale of λ = 34 ± 4 \lambda=34\pm 4 nm for the cross-sectional geometry studied here, corresponding to the average separation at which domain misalignment first emerges. This length scale coincides with an approximately 50% reduction in the interlayer magnetic field relative to bulk FGT. Surface effects result in canting of the magnetic moments away from the easy axis up to ∼ \sim 100 nm from a surface. Finally, the domain walls are narrow ( ∼ \sim 9 nm), while micromagnetic simulations reproduce the observed textures without invoking Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction. These results quantify the internal and stray fields in stacked vdW magnets and guide the design of devices that require controllable coupling between magnetic textures.

Another advantage of vdW magnets is that they can be assembled into heterostructures without lattice-matching constraints

Understanding the interactions between ferromagnetic layers, the resulting magnetic domain structures, and the effects of interfaces and surfaces on the local magnetization is therefore important for the development of new spintronic applications.

However, in vdW heterostructures imaged using conventional magnetic imaging methods in the plan-view geometry, it is difficult to determine which magnetic layer gives rise to a given signal, because the measured signal is integrated through the thickness of the heterostructure.

To address this challenge, we prepare cross-sectional lamellae of FGT/graphite/FGT heterostructures, allowing the vertical spacing between the FGT layers to be controlled using a graphite spacer. FGT is a metallic vdW ferromagnet with a bulk Curie temperature (
T_{\mathrm{c}}

\sim

7
;
5

\gtrsim
50 nm along the crystal
c

Using Lorentz transmission electron microscopy (LTEM) and off-axis electron holography (OAEH), we visualize the magnetic domain structure in each FGT layer, as well as the internal and stray magnetic fields. The cross-sectional geometry enables direct visualization of dipolar coupling and domain alignment as a function of FGT separation. Domain alignment decreases with increasing layer separation, yet interactions persist even at a separation of 110 nm. From one heterostructure, we determine a dipolar coupling length scale of
\lambda=34\pm 4
nm, corresponding to the average FGT spacing at which domain misalignments are first observed. OAEH further reveals that this spacing corresponds to an approximately 50% reduction in the interlayer magnetic field relative to the bulk FGT value, and that surface effects cause the local magnetic moments to cant away from the magnetic easy axis of FGT over distances of up to
\sim
100 nm from the surface.

Another important question concerns the domain-wall topology in FGT. Previous studies have shown that Fe-deficient and stoichiometric FGT host Néel-type and Bloch-type domain walls, respectively

3.31

31
;
24

36

35

31

Beyond their fundamental interest, these results provide design guidance and insight into surface effects on the local magnetization in devices that require controllable coupling between magnetic textures in vertically stacked vdW layers. More broadly, this cross-sectional imaging strategy enables high-resolution quantification of the magnetic field and specimen magnetization, and is applicable to a wide range of magnetic vdW heterostructures, including systems exhibiting proximity-induced phenomena arising from strong spin-orbit coupling, as well as systems with superconductor/ferromagnet interfaces.

II.1
Magnetic domain structures of FGT-graphite-FGT heterostructures

The magnetic domain structure and dipolar coupling length scales in FGT/graphite/FGT heterostructures were first determined using LTEM. In total, four vdW heterostructure lamellae were fabricated. The heterostructures were assembled from mechanically exfoliated FGT and graphite crystals, and the lamellae were subsequently prepared by focused ion-beam (FIB), see
Methods

The difference in

23

\mu

The reduced domain width could therefore also be understood in terms of additional stray-field energy associated with the side surfaces, which can be lowered by forming more domain walls. Regardless of the microscopic origin, these results show that FIB patterning alters the domain structure in FGT. This effect should therefore be considered when comparing lamella-based measurements with those from as-exfoliated flakes, although it does not affect the internal comparison between the heterostructures studied here.

With an appropriate spacer thickness relative to the FGT thickness, cross-sectional geometries therefore provide a direct means to investigate domain alignment and magnetic coupling in vertically stacked magnetic vdW layers.

II.2
Visualization of the magnetic field

II.2.1
Internal and stray magnetic fields

OAEH was then used to quantify the magnetic field within and between the FGT layers and to determine how surfaces affect the local magnetization. In all datasets, the electrostatic contribution to the phase shift was removed by subtracting the phase shift measured at room temperature, above the
T_{\mathrm{c}}
of FGT; see
Methods

Figure
4

II.2.2
Surface effects

Surface-related effects on the magnetization of the FGT layers are considered next. Figures
4
(c, d) show dome-shaped regions near the FGT surfaces, between magnetic domain walls, where
|\boldsymbol{B}_{\perp}(x,y)|\,t
is reduced compared with the interior of the FGT. The striped white arc in Fig.
4
(c) highlights one such near-surface region with reduced projected in-plane induction. These regions extend up to
\sim
100 nm into the FGT and are observed both at the outer surfaces of the vdW heterostructure and at the internal FGT/vacuum interface in Fig.
4
(c). However, they are less pronounced at the internal FGT/vacuum interface in Fig.
4
(d), where the two FGT layers are in closer proximity.

This surface effect may arise from several factors, including (1) demagnetizing fields near the specimen surface, (2) magnetization canting toward the electron-beam direction, which reduces the measured projected in-plane component, and/or (3) a reduced local magnetic moment near the FGT surface.

Because the reduced
|\boldsymbol{B}_{\perp}|\,t
near the free FGT surfaces persists after model-based reconstruction, the diminished projected in-plane induction is interpreted as magnetization canting relative to the electron-beam direction. This effect is less pronounced when the interlayer gap is small (Fig.

In summary, OAEH quantifies the magnetic field and interlayer dipolar coupling in magnetic vdW heterostructures. In the interior of FGT, projected in-plane induction values of
\sim
130–220 mT are extracted. Domain alignment between the top and bottom layers begins to break down when the field in the vacuum spacer is reduced by roughly 50% relative to the bulk value, which occurs at separations on the order of
\lambda
. In addition, surface effects reduce the measured induction up to
\sim
100 nm from a free FGT surface. Finally, signatures of partial flux closure near domain corners are observed, consistent with dipolar-field-driven canting despite the strong perpendicular anisotropy of FGT.

II.3
Magnetic domain walls in FGT

The domain wall topology in the FGT crystals is investigated in this section. The nature of domain walls in FGT–particularly whether they are Néel- or Bloch-type–remains under debate. In LTEM studies of vdW magnets imaged in plan view, domain wall contrast that appears only upon tilting the sample is often taken as evidence for Néel-type walls
25
;
31
;
24
. However, in principle, tilt-induced domain wall contrast is not a unique signature of Néel character. Similar behavior can, for instance, arise from narrow Bloch-type walls
29
or from Bloch-type walls in multilayer structures with alternating chirality in adjacent layers
36
;
35
.

A closely related question concerns the role of DMI in FGT. Because bulk FGT is centrosymmetric, it is not expected to host an intrinsic bulk DMI. Nevertheless, Néel-type walls are often interpreted as evidence for an effective DMI
32
;
48
;
1
. One possible origin is bulk defects
4
. For example, Fe deficiency has been reported to stabilize Néel-type walls in Fe
3-δ
GeTe
2
with
\delta\sim 0.3
, whereas crystals with
\delta\sim 0
exhibit Bloch-type walls
25
. Another possible origin is surface effects, such as oxidation, which have been reported to yield Néel-type domain walls
32
;
31
. However, it remains an open question how such surface effects influence the domain wall topology throughout the thickness of vdW magnets.

A more quantitative comparison between theory and experiment would require more accurate estimates of the material parameters and simulations of smaller samples, which are readily achievable within micromagnetic modeling.

Overall, domain-wall contrast is observed only upon tilting the sample in plan-view LTEM imaging, consistent with previous reports concluding that the domain-wall topology is Néel-type, and suggesting that Néel-type domain walls are favored in off-stoichiometric FGT. However, OAEH data from cross-sectional lamellae show that the domain walls are very narrow, in line with the large magnetic anisotropy of FGT. Hence, it is possible that the tilt-dependent LTEM contrast arises from the small domain-wall width. Finally, micromagnetic simulations reproduce the experimental findings and indicate that DMI is not required to reproduce the domain structure observed in our samples. These results highlight that additional experiments are needed to determine the domain-wall character unambiguously in FGT. One possible approach would be to apply an in-plane magnetic field during LTEM imaging of plan-view samples, since Bloch- and Néel-type walls are expected to respond differently to in-plane fields

.

III
Conclusions and outlook

We have quantified magnetic interactions between FGT layers in FGT/graphite/FGT vdW heterostructures with varying FGT separations by directly imaging the domain structure and measuring the magnetic field within and between the FGT layers. A characteristic dipolar-interaction length scale of
\lambda=34\pm 4
nm is extracted for the cross-sectional lamella geometry studied here, corresponding to the average separation at which the first misalignment between the layers is observed. In addition, magnetic induction measurements show that a separation on the order of
\lambda
corresponds to an approximately 50% reduction in the field within the spacer relative to that in the interior of the FGT. Together, these results connect a directly observed characteristic coupling length (
\lambda
) to a quantitative reduction in the local magnetic field within the vacuum spacer and demonstrate that the effective interaction strength between vertically stacked FGT layers can be tuned from strong to weak coupling through the interlayer separation and sample geometry.

Furthermore, surface effects lead to canting of the local magnetic moments and reduce the projected in-plane magnetic induction over length scales of up to
\sim
100 nm from a free FGT surface. Signatures of partial flux closure near domain corners are also observed, indicating dipolar-field-driven canting despite the strong perpendicular anisotropy of FGT. These findings suggest that surface effects can dominate the local magnetization in flakes with thicknesses
\lesssim
100 nm.

Considering the magnetic domain walls, previous reports have found that Fe-deficient FGT hosts Néel-type walls, whereas stoichiometric FGT hosts Bloch-type walls
25
. Our crystals are Fe-rich (Fe
3.31
GeTe
1.98
) and show tilt-dependent contrast in LTEM that is consistent with prior reports of Néel-type walls. This may indicate that Néel-type domain walls are favored in off-stoichiometric FGT. Nevertheless, we emphasize that the OAEH data show that the domain walls are very narrow (
\sim
9 nm wide), which could explain the tilt-dependent LTEM contrast without requiring Néel-type domain walls. Micromagnetic simulations show that DMI is not required to reproduce the experimentally observed domain structures in the heterostructures and confirm that Néel- and Bloch-type walls produce very similar contrast in LTEM and OAEH data. These results indicate that additional methods, such as application of in plane magnetic fields to plan-view samples
32
, are required to determine the domain-wall type unambiguously.

Overall, these results provide insight into interlayer magnetic coupling in magnetic vdW heterostructures, as well as into surface effects in device-relevant heterostructures for spintronic and quantum information applications. We anticipate that electron holography studies of cross-sectional specimens can be extended to probe proximity interactions between vdW ferromagnets and a wide range of other vdW materials, including materials with heavy elements that induce interfacial spin-orbit coupling, or superconductors, to investigate the interaction between magnetic domains and flux vortices
10
. Other techniques, such as vector field electron holography, can reconstruct the three-dimensional magnetic field in these structures and provide additional information on stray fields and surface effects
39
, and a method like phase-shifting electron holography can be used to increase the spatial resolution of electron holography data
51
. Finally, the response of these heterostructures to external stimuli, such as temperature variation or applied magnetic fields, could be directly visualized.
