Protocol for a randomised controlled trial on smartphone- and web-based app for precision dosing and monitoring of lithium («PharmOracle») for improving adherence to therapy in patients with bipolar affective disorder or schizoaffective disorder
Authors: Kirill Markin, Arslan Moritz, Ekaterina Gerasimchuk, Ksenia Romanova, Irina Shpakovskaya, Aleksandr Sinitca, Dmitriy Kaplun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLithium remains a first-line treatment for bipolar disorder but its use is declining owing to the need for intensive monitoring and concerns about tolerability. Digital decision-support tools that combine model-informed precision dosing, Bayesian therapeutic-drug-monitoring, and adherence support may facilitate safer, more effective lithium therapy and improve long-term adherence. We aimed to evaluate whether adding a mobile application with precision-dosing, monitoring reminders and medication-support functions («PharmOracle») to standard care reduces a composite measure of non-adherence and adverse outcomes over 12 months, compared with standard care alone. We planning an open-label, multicenter, randomized (1:1) superiority trial enrolling adults (≥18 years) with ICD-10 bipolar I/II or schizoaffective disorder who are initiating lithium carbonate. Planned sample size is 210 participants (105 per arm). The primary endpoint is time to first occurrence of a composite outcome: lithium discontinuation for any reason, hospitalization for lithium toxicity (serum lithium ≥1.2 mmol/L with symptoms), or unplanned psychiatric hospitalization/emergency visit for mood relapse assessed continuously and verified at 3, 6 and 12 months. Key secondary endpoints include changes in the Integral Diagnostic Index for Bipolar Disorder composite index for assessing symptoms of depression (HDRS), mania (YMRS), psychosocial functioning (PSP), and quality of life (WHOQOL), self-reported medication adherence (10-item Medication Adherence Report), adherence to recommended laboratory/instrumental monitoring, proportion of patients achieving therapeutic serum lithium (0.6-1.0 mmol/L), and safety outcomes. The intervention comprises a mobile/web platform performing model-informed individualized dose prediction, Bayesian dose refinement from measured troughs, automated monitoring reminders, adverse-event self-reporting, and clinician dashboards. Analyses will follow intention-to-treat principles using survival methods (Kaplan–Meier, log-rank, Cox regression) for the primary outcome and mixed models or appropriate nonparametric tests for longitudinal secondary outcomes. We anticipate that using of mobile application with precision-dosing, monitoring reminders and medication-support functions will reduce the incidence of the composite outcome by improving adherence and accelerating attainment of therapeutic lithium concentrations, while maintaining an acceptable safety profile.Keywords: Lithium, Bipolar disorder, Adherence, Mobile health, Model-informed precision dosing, Randomized controlled trial

Introduction

Lithium is one of the most effective drugs for treating bipolar disorder (BD) and is recommended as a first-line therapy in multiple clinical guidelines (Fountoulakis et al., 2017; Yatham et al., 2018; Hidalgo-Mazzei et al., 2023). Numerous studies have demonstrated that lithium significantly outperforms placebo in preventing both manic and depressive episodes (Miura et al., 2014). Furthermore, findings from large trials and a systematic review by the International Society for Bipolar Disorders have indicated an advantage of prophylactic monotherapy with lithium carbonate over monotherapy with other mood stabilizers or atypical antipsychotics (Kessing et al., 2018; Hayes et al., 2016; Fountoulakis et al., 2022).

Despite the well-established efficacy of lithium for the acute management and prophylaxis of affective episodes, its use by clinicians worldwide has gradually declined, amounting to approximately 35% of patients with BD (Kessing et al., 2018). This trend is likely attributable to the requirement for extensive monitoring associated with lithium prescribing and long-term use, due both to its narrow therapeutic window (the target concentration for maintenance therapy is approximately 0.6–1.0 mmol/L; lower targets are sometimes used in specific clinical situations and in elderly patients, whereas levels above 1.2 mmol/L are associated with a substantially increased risk of toxicity) (Nolen et al., 2019). In addition, a range of adverse effects, some of which may occur even at therapeutic concentrations, further complicates lithium treatment (Losenkov et al., 2020).

In routine psychiatric practice, however, the large volume of required diagnostic procedures and the subsequent performance and tracking of those investigations are often challenging (Singh et al., 2024). A retrospective cohort study of 861 respondents reported that approximately half of patients prescribed lithium discontinue treatment (in 60% of these cases due to adverse effects) (Öhlund et al., 2018a). Importantly, the majority of adverse effects are associated with nonadherence to the lithium dosing regimen and are, in many cases, relatively easily remediable (Gitlin, 2016). Meanwhile, the relatively low mean rate of recurrent lithium resistance (17.3%) suggests that previously discontinued lithium therapy can often be successfully reinitiated (Kupka et al., 2024).

To increase patient adherence to lithium therapy, several research initiatives have emerged, including the implementation of digital health technologies primarily for medication management and adverse event monitoring (Steinkamp et al., 2019; Gan et al., 2021; Parracha et al.), the individualization of therapy (Potanin et al., 2023), and the development of precision dosing regimens (Lereclus et al., 2024; Aurélie et al., 2023a). In addition, efforts are underway to develop noninvasive methods that simplify therapeutic drug monitoring (Amirghasemi et al., 2024; Islam et al., 2025; Sheikh et al., 2022). Although these directions are promising, their development and the supporting evidence base remain heterogeneous. Mobile applications for self-monitoring and medication reminders are abundant, yet their quality and conformity with clinical guidelines are frequently questionable, and robust evidence from rigorous trials demonstrating improved adherence is lacking. (Patoz et al., 2021; Eis et al., 2022). Recognising this gap, the ECNP Digital Network recently published expert consensus recommendations for the evaluation of mental health apps, identifying efficacy assessment via randomised controlled trials, usability, acceptability, user satisfaction, monitoring of adverse effects, drop-out tracking, and data security as minimum evaluation criteria (Bolea et al., 2026). The design of the present trial was explicitly informed by these recommendations."

One promising approach to improving lithium adherence is precision dosing based on population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) models, which enable individualized dose calculation (Potanin et al., 2023; Lereclus et al., 2024). Until recently, however, the superiority of such models over empirical dose selection has been limited because many models do not fully account for the full set of relevant factors (Sienaert et al., 2013). Meta-modelling can address this limitation by selecting the most influential covariates for inclusion in a meta-model, thereby improving dosing accuracy, hastening attainment of the target concentration, and accelerating the onset of therapeutic effect (Lereclus et al., 2024; Aurélie et al., 2023b; Millischer et al., 2022). Currently, comprehensive digital tools that combine a patient-friendly interface with a validated precision-dosing module for clinicians are virtually nonexistent. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) assessing the effectiveness of such integrated systems are scarce, creating a substantial gap in the evidence base (Patoz et al., 2021).

Objective

To assess whether the use of a mobile application incorporating precision-dosing functionality along with support for lithium administration and monitoring, in addition to standard care, is superior to standard care alone in improving treatment adherence, measured using a composite outcome (lithium discontinuation, hospitalization due to lithium toxicity, unplanned psychiatric hospitalization/emergency care visit) over 12 months of follow-up.

Primary hypothesis: Use of the application with precision-dosing and lithium-monitoring support will lead to a statistically significant reduction in the incidence of the composite outcome compared with standard care (superiority design).

Study design

This study protocol was developed in accordance with the SPIRIT-2025 checklist (Chan et al., 2025).

We plan to conduct an open-label, multicenter, randomized controlled trial with parallel groups (superiority design). Patients with BD type I or II and schizoaffective disorder who are prescribed lithium carbonate will be randomized 1:1 to receive either the «PharmOracle » mobile-application intervention or standard clinical care. The total duration of follow-up is 12 months.

Participants

The multicenter design involves recruitment at several psychiatric inpatient hospitals and outpatient clinics providing specialized mental healthcare. Both outpatients and inpatients who are newly prescribed lithium will be eligible. A total of 3–5 sites are expected to participate. Participant recruitment will be carried out by treating psychiatrists at participating centers at the time they decide to initiate lithium carbonate therapy. The planned start date for recruitment is September 2026. The recruitment procedure will be embedded in routine clinical practice. Treating psychiatrists will identify potentially eligible individuals at the time lithium initiation is considered. Centers are required to routinely prescribe lithium carbonate and have access to laboratory (lithium level, eGFR, Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone, electrolytes) and instrumental (ECG) assessments. The intervention («PharmOracle»), as well as standard-care lithium prescribing, may be delivered by psychiatrists experienced in managing patients with BD and schizoaffective disorder.

(2018) (Öhlund et al., 2018b), who reported a 54% lifetime lithium discontinuation rate in a large retrospective cohort. Since discontinuation is expected to be the dominant driver of our composite outcome, we consider 50% a conservative and clinically plausible estimate. The 35% intervention group estimate reflects a 30% relative risk reduction, consistent with the magnitude of effect reported in meta-analyses of digital health interventions for chronic conditions (Wicha et al., 2021). The multi-component nature of «PharmOracle » combining MIPD, Bayesian dose refinement, automated monitoring reminders, and adverse event self-reporting provides theoretical grounds for an effect at least comparable to single-component digital interventions.

Interventions

The intervention consists of adding the «PharmOracle » mobile application to standard clinical care (Group 1) versus standard clinical care alone (Group 2). A centralized web-based randomization system will be used. After confirming eligibility and obtaining informed consent, an authorized study investigator will enter a minimal dataset into the system, which will automatically assign the patient to one of the two groups. Block randomization with variable block sizes (4 and 6), stratified by study site, will ensure balanced allocation within each center.

Standard clinical care (Group 2) will follow routine outpatient or inpatient documentation practices. The initial lithium carbonate dose, monitoring intervals and frequency, and concomitant pharmacotherapy will be determined by the treating clinician according to local and national guidelines and the center's usual practice. All concomitant medications (including other mood stabilizers, antidepressants, and antipsychotics) as well as non-pharmacological interventions (e.g., psychotherapy) will be permitted in both groups at the clinician's discretion and will be thoroughly documented.

Age influences the model through two pathways. First, age is a required input for the CKD-EPI formula used to calculate eGFR, which is a direct covariate in the clearance equation. Second, age determines the individualized target serum lithium concentration (Ctaᵣ(get)): in patients aged ≥60–65 years, a lower target range (0.4–0.6 mmol/L) is applied in accordance with current clinical guidelines, reflecting increased sensitivity to lithium toxicity in older adults.

This approach represents an alternative to the traditional empirical strategy (“start low, go slow”), enabling personalized therapy from the outset. The algorithm estimates individual lithium clearance (CLlᵢ) and the recommended daily dose (Dose24h) required to achieve a target plasma concentration (Ctaᵣ(get)) using key patient parameters. The benefits include faster and safer attainment of the therapeutic window, reduced risk of both subtherapeutic and toxic concentrations during dose titration, and incorporation of individual metabolic characteristics (Millischer et al., 2022).

The trough correction factor R(τ) accounts for the difference between mean steady-state concentration (Caᵥg) and the 12-h trough concentration (Ctᵣoᵘᵏʰ) used in therapeutic drug monitoring. It is derived from the terminal rate constant β of the two-compartment pharmacokinetic model: R(τ) = (βτ)/(1 − exp(−βτ)), where τ = 12 h for twice-daily (BID) or 24 h for once-daily (QD) dosing. Omitting R(τ) results in systematic dose underestimation of approximately 25–38% for BID and 40–84% for QD regimens. The recommended lithium carbonate dose is rounded to the nearest available tablet strength (300 mg in the Russian Federation), with safety limits of 300–1800 mg/day.

Model-Informed Precision Dosing is increasingly recognized as a key tool for personalizing psychotropic therapy by accounting for interindividual variability in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, genetic factors, comorbidities, and drug–drug interactions (Darwich et al., 2021). However, despite notable progress, widespread adoption of Model-Informed Precision Dosing in psychiatry remains limited due to a lack of validated models, insufficient integration with electronic health record systems, limited large-scale clinical evidence, and the absence of standardized clinician training programs (Janković et al., 2025).

1.Dose adjustment using Bayesian adaptation

Dose adjustment using Bayesian adaptation

The application incorporates tools for a posteriori dose adjustment based on Bayesian updating of measured serum lithium concentrations (Methaneethorn et al., 2022;Turner et al., 2018; Wicha et al., 2021). Bayesian dose refinement is triggered under two conditions: (1) the measured concentration falls outside the absolute therapeutic range (<0.6 or >1.0 mmol/L); or (2) the concentration, while within the therapeutic range, deviates by more than ±0.1 mmol/L from the patient's individualized target. The updated dose is calculated as: Dt+1 = Dt × (Ctaᵣ(get)/Ct)ᵏ, where K = ω2/(ω2 + σ2) is the Bayesian weight (Kalman gain; ω2 = inter-individual variance of ln(CL), σ2 = total observation variance). Serum lithium is measured 7 days after treatment initiation or any dose change, and subsequently every 3 months during stable maintenance; each dose adjustment resets the monitoring cycle. All Bayesian recommendations require explicit clinician confirmation before implementation.

1.Automated reminders for recommended monitoring

Automated reminders for recommended monitoring

«PharmOracle » issues reminders for laboratory and physiological monitoring in accordance with the “Guidelines on Safe Lithium Prescribing and Shared Care” and the “Psychotropic Monitoring Guide” (Taylor et al., 2021).

Real-world studies highlight the importance of regular serum lithium monitoring for ensuring both efficacy and safety of treatment (Uskur et al., 2024). Nevertheless, real-world adherence to recommended monitoring remains suboptimal, with reported compliance rates of only 60–70%, and merely 10–15% of patients in high-income countries meeting full monitoring requirements (Inagaki and Takeshima, 2014; Nikolova et al., 2018; Parfitt et al., 2021; Nederlof et al., 2019). The feasibility of fulfilling the complete monitoring recommendations is debated, and alternative, more pragmatic monitoring frameworks have been proposed to improve adherence in resource-limited settings (Mannapperuma et al., 2019).

1.Medication reminders and assistance with tracking side effects and symptom changes

Medication reminders and assistance with tracking side effects and symptom changes

Studies comparing two multicenter cohorts, each comprising more than 300,000 patients, demonstrated that patients receiving app-based reminders adhered to recommendations 20% more frequently than those receiving traditional monthly feedback (Coma et al., 2019). Similar findings from Japanese research support the utility of electronic reminders embedded in medical records for lithium therapy, although such systems did not improve achievement of therapeutic serum lithium levels (Seki et al., 2019, 2023). Smartphone-based monitoring systems have demonstrated feasibility and high engagement in patients with severe mental illness, including those with affective and schizoaffective disorders (Machleid et al., 2025).

1.Recording and follow-up of adverse event reports

Recording and follow-up of adverse event reports

The application includes a self-report form with selectable symptoms, highlighting potentially dangerous adverse events for which automated recommendations to contact a clinician are triggered.

A comprehensive report generated for the clinician will include visualization panels summarizing dosing history, laboratory results, trough concentrations, and therapy-adjustment recommendations. «PharmOracle» is designed exclusively as a clinical decision-support tool. The clinician receives each model-generated dose recommendation and must explicitly confirm or override it within the application interface. Patients in the intervention group are shown only the clinician's final confirmed dose, not the model recommendation. The treating clinician retains full prescribing authority at all times. Clinician agreement with model recommendations will be tracked as a secondary descriptive outcome and used in the per-protocol sensitivity analysis.

Randomization and blinding

A centralized web-based randomization system will be used. After confirming eligibility and obtaining informed consent, an authorized study investigator will enter the minimal required patient data, and the system will automatically assign the patient to one of the two groups. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding is not feasible.

Baseline parameters

At the baseline visit, sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, education, marital and employment status) and clinical history (confirmed diagnosis of bipolar affective disorder type I/II or schizoaffective disorder according to ICD-10 validated via MINI, illness duration, number of previous hospitalizations, prior psychopharmacological treatment) will be collected through interview and review of medical records.

To assess factors influencing adherence to the digital intervention, patients’ readiness to use digital health technologies will also be evaluated. Anthropometric measurements (height, weight, BMI) and key laboratory parameters (eGFR, TSH, serum calcium, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio), required for the precision-dosing algorithm, will be recorded.

Treating clinicians at all participating sites will provide informed consent and attend a standardised onboarding session prior to patient recruitment. At onboarding, clinicians will complete a baseline assessment of attitudes toward digital health and model-informed precision dosing using adapted Technology Acceptance Model items. This will be paired with MARS evaluation and semi-structured interviews at study completion, enabling a pre–post assessment of clinician acceptance of the «PharmOracle » platform.

Planned statistical methods

Statistical analyses will be performed using Jamovi and/or R with additional packages for survival analysis and mixed-effects modeling. During the data preprocessing stage, descriptive statistics will be computed, normality will be assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances will be evaluated using Levene's test.

For the composite primary outcome (time to lithium discontinuation or hospitalization), survival analysis will be applied. Kaplan–Meier survival curves will be constructed and compared using the log-rank test. To assess the effect of the intervention while adjusting for covariates, Cox proportional hazards regression models will be used.

To evaluate changes in the IDI-BD composite index and its subscales across three timepoints (baseline, 6 months, 12 months), repeated-measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA) or Linear Mixed Models (LMM) will be applied, depending on whether model assumptions are met. Greenhouse–Geisser corrections will be used for RM-ANOVA when required, and random effects will be included in LMMs as appropriate. If normality assumptions are violated, the nonparametric Friedman test will be employed for within-group comparisons and the Kruskal–Wallis test for between-group comparisons, followed by Dwass–Steel–Critchlow–Fligner post-hoc tests. Categorical variables (e.g., proportion of patients within the therapeutic lithium range, frequency of adverse events) will be analyzed using chi-square tests (with Yates’ correction when applicable) or logistic regression.

Across all analyses, statistical significance will be set at p < 0.05, with appropriate corrections for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni or FDR).

Three pre-specified analysis populations will be used. The primary analysis follows intention-to-treat (ITT) principles, including all randomised participants regardless of clinician adherence to model recommendations. A per-protocol sensitivity analysis will be conducted in the subgroup where the clinician confirmed the model recommendation without modification. Additionally, clinician agreement rate (the proportion of model recommendations confirmed without modification, extracted from app-use logs) will be reported as a secondary descriptive outcome, calculated from app-use logs as the proportion of recommendations confirmed without modification.

Discussion

The rationale for the present trial rests on two converging bodies of evidence. First, lithium exhibits marked inter-individual pharmacokinetic variability driven by renal function, body composition, age, and drug interactions, which renders fixed-dose regimens inadequate for a substantial proportion of patients. While therapeutic drug monitoring has long formed the cornerstone of lithium management, standard TDM does not fully exploit the predictive information contained in patient-specific covariates. Model-informed precision dosing, which integrates population pharmacokinetic models with Bayesian updating of individual measurements, has demonstrated clinical benefit in oncology, infectious disease, and transplant medicine (Darwich et al., 2021; Wicha et al., 2021), but has not yet been evaluated within a prospective randomised framework in psychiatry.

Several methodological decisions warrant explicit justification. An open-label pragmatic design was selected because blinding is not feasible for a digital intervention, and pragmatic trials better reflect real-world implementation conditions. The composite primary endpoint captures the clinical spectrum of lithium treatment failure, with each component directly targeted by a distinct « PharmOracle » functionality. A 12-month follow-up was selected to capture the period of highest risk for early lithium discontinuation (Öhlund et al., 2018b).

To date, the closest comparable initiative is the SmartBipolar trial – RCT of smartphone-based monitoring in patients with bipolar disorder (Faurholt-Jepsen et al., 2023). However, SmartBipolar does not incorporate pharmacokinetic modelling or model-informed dose individualization. Existing mHealth applications for bipolar disorder focus predominantly on mood monitoring and psychoeducation (Eis et al., 2022), without addressing pharmacological precision of lithium dosing. The present trial therefore addresses an unmet gap in digital psychiatric pharmacotherapy.

If effective, «PharmOracle » has the potential to accelerate attainment of therapeutic serum concentrations, reduce avoidable hospitalisations, and improve patient engagement in shared decision-making. At the health system level, a reduction in unplanned admissions would carry meaningful economic implications. For clinicians, real-time dashboards and Bayesian recommendations may reduce the cognitive burden of lithium titration in complex patients.

Beyond the primary efficacy question, the trial will generate secondary contributions: a parallel external validation study of the Lereclus meta-model will provide prospective evidence on MIPD predictive accuracy (Aurélie et al., 2023a); clinician agreement rates, MARS ratings, and interview data will characterise barriers and facilitators to adoption. These initiatives position the current work as a foundational step toward broader integration of MIPD across psychopharmacology, with natural extensions to clozapine, valproate, and lamotrigine.

Key limitations

●Blinding is infeasible given the digital intervention; this introduces risk of performance and detection bias. Outcome verification by the research team and use of objective endpoints (hospitalization, serum levels) will mitigate but not eliminate bias.
