Concentration of the largest induced tree size of G n , p G_{n,p} around the standard expectation threshold
Jakob Hofstad

Abstract




Abstract

Let
T(G)
be the size of the largest induced tree of
G
, and let
G_{n,p}
be the binomial random graph. Kamaldinov, Skorkin, and Zhukovskii proved that
T(G_{n,p})
equals one of two consecutive values with high probability if
p
is constant, and more recently, Oropeza extended this result to include all vanishing
p
such that
p>n^{-\frac{e-2}{3e-2}+\epsilon}
, where
e
is Euler’s constant. We further extend this result to all vanishing
p
such that
p\gg n^{-1/2}\ln^{3/2}n
, and furthermore, we show that, for
p
such that
n^{-1}\ll p\ll n^{-1/2},\ T(G_{n,p})
cannot be concentrated at the standard expectation threshold.

1
Introduction

Define
T(G)
to be the maximum size of an induced tree of
G
. Let
G_{n,p}
be the binomial random graph; i.e., a graph on
n
vertices such that each pair of vertices is joined by an edge with probability
p
, and all
\binom{n}{2}
of these events are independent. We say that an event
\mathcal{E}_{n}
(dependent on
n
) happens “with high probability”, or “whp”, if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{n}]=1
. Finally, we say that a variable
X
defined on
G_{n,p}
is “concentrated on

k=k(n)
values” if there is a function
f(n)
such that
X\in\{f(n),\dots,f(n)+k-1\}
whp.

The study of
T(G_{n,p})
began with Erdős and Palka, who proved that, if
p
is a non-trivial constant,
T(G_{n,p})\approx 2\log_{1/(1-p)}n
whp
[
6
]
. Kamaldinov, Skorkin, and Zhukovskii later sharpened this result: they proved that, for constant
p
,
T(G_{n,p})
is concentrated on two values
[
9
]
. Oropeza then proved that two-point concentration of
T(G_{n,p})
also holds for all functions
p=p(n)
such that
p=o(1)
and
p>n^{-\frac{e-2}{3e-2}+\epsilon}
for any fixed
\epsilon>0

[
12
]
.

Closely related to the size of the maximum induced tree
T(G)
is the independence number
\alpha(G)
, at least with regard to their behavior in
G_{n,p}
.
The nature of
\alpha(G_{n,p})
has been long studied; for constant
p
, two-point concentration was established independently by Erdős and Bollobás
[
5
]
and by Matula
[
11
]
in the 1970’s, at the same asymptotic value of
2\log_{1/(1-p)}n
. In 1990, Frieze
[
7
]
used a combination of the second moment method and martingale inequalities to show that, if
p=o(1)
and
p=\omega(1/n)
, then whp

\alpha(G_{n,p})=\frac{2\ln(np)+2+o(1)}{p}.

Much more recently, in a series of two papers
[
3
,
4
]
, Bohman and the author proved that
\alpha(G_{n,p})
is concentrated on two values if
p=\omega(n^{-2/3}\ln^{2/3}n)
, and moreover, that this order of
p
is tight. Unlike the Erdős-Bollobás and Matula papers, these newer results required the use of a random variable that counts not independent sets themselves, but sets which are “almost” independent but contain many independent sets within them.

Also closely related to
T(G)
is
the maximum induced
forest
size
F(G)
; the study of
F(G_{n,p})
has many close parallels with that of
T(G_{n,p})
and
\alpha(G_{n,p})
.
Krivoshapko and Zhukovskii proved that, for constant
p
(
p\not=0
),
F(G_{n,p})
is concentrated on the
same
two values as
T(G_{n,p})

[
10
]
. Akhmejavona and Kozhevnikov
[
1
]
used techniques similar to the earlier result of Frieze to show that, if
p=o(1)
and
p=\omega(1/n)
, then whp

F(G_{n,p})=\frac{2\ln(np)+2+o(1)}{p}.

In this paper, we prove that 2-point concentration of
T(G_{n,p})
holds for a wide range of values of
p
. Defining
X_{k}
as the number of induced graphs of size
k
in
G_{n,p}
which are trees, we also show that, for slightly smaller
p
,
T(G_{n,p})

cannot
be concentrated around the value of
k
for which
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]
transitions from being
\omega(1)
to being
o(1)
, known as the “expectation threshold”.

Theorem 1
.

There exists an integer function
k_{0}=k_{0}(n,p)
such that, for all
p=p(n)
such that
\frac{1}{n}\ll p\ll\frac{1}{\ln^{2}n}
:

\I

The largest value of
k
such that
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]
is at least 1 is either
k_{0}
or
k_{0}+1
.

\ii

If
p=\omega(n^{-1/2}\ln^{3/2}n)
, then
T(G_{n,p})\in\{k_{0},k_{0}+1\}
whp.

\iii

If
p=o(n^{-1/2})
, then
T(G_{n,p})<k_{0}-1/(4np^{2})
whp.

The theorem is proved by using two other random variables
Y_{k}
and
W_{k}
. Both count induced trees of size
k
but with restrictions:
Y_{k}
counts the induced trees of size
k
where vertices outside the tree have at least three neighbors inside the tree, while
W_{k}
counts the induced trees of size
k
which are maximal induced trees. The first and second moment methods with
Y_{k}
are used to prove part (ii) of the theorem, and the first moment of
Y_{k}
is used for part (iii).

The structure of the paper is as follows. Part (i) and the upper bound for
T(G_{n,p})
in part (ii) will be proved in Section
2
. The lower bound for
T(G_{n,p})
in part (ii) will be proved in Section
3
. Part (iii) will be proved in Section
4
. Section
5
contains closing remarks. An appendix contains two technical lemmas used in Section
3
.

1.1
Key definitions and inequality

We close this section by defining some key notation and citing a strong form of Stirling’s Inequality with the gamma function
\Gamma
. For a positive integer
n
, let
[n]
the the set
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. For a graph
G
and set
A\subset V(G)
, let
G[A]
be the induced graph of
G
on vertex set
A
. For a vertex
v
in a graph
G
, let
\mathcal{N}(v)
the set of the neighbors of
v
in
G
.

A strong version of Stirling’s Inequality is as follows (see (2.2) and (3.9) in
[
2
]
): for all positive real
n
,

\sqrt{2\pi}n^{n+1/2}e^{-n}\leq\Gamma(n+1)\leq\sqrt{2\pi}n^{n+1/2}e^{-n+1/(12n)}.

(1)

We mostly use the inequality above in combination with the fact that
\Gamma(n+1)=n!
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
(see (2.6) in
[
2
]
), though it will also be used for half-integer values of
n
.

2
First moment calculation

Throughout the rest of the paper,
\frac{1}{n}\ll p\ll\frac{1}{\ln^{2}n}
always.

Recall that
X_{k}
is the number of induced subgraphs with size
k
that are trees. The expected value of
X_{k}
in
G_{n,p}
is

\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]=\binom{n}{k}k^{k-2}p^{k-1}(1-p)^{\binom{k}{2}-k+1}.

(2)

We shall define

k_{0}:=\max\{k:\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]>\ln(np)\};

this is certainly well-defined since
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]=n
and
k\leq n
. If
k=\Theta(1)
then (
2
) will be
\Omega(n)
, and if
k=\Theta(n)
(suppose
k>\varepsilon n
for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
) then

\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]\leq 2^{n}(\varepsilon np)^{\varepsilon n}e^{-pk(k-3)/2}\leq(2(\varepsilon np)^{\varepsilon}e^{-\varepsilon^{2}np+3\varepsilon p})^{n}=o(1).

Therefore, it is sufficient to just consider
k
which is both
\omega(1)
and
o(n)
. Using (
1
), taking the
k
th root of (
2
) gives

(\mathbb{E}[X_{k}])^{1/k}\approx enp(1-p)^{k/2}.

This expression will be
\Theta(1)
if and only if

k=2\log_{1/(1-p)}(enp)+o(p^{-1});

Since
p=o(\ln^{-2}n)
, the above is equivalent to

k=\frac{2\ln(np)+2+o(1)}{p}

(3)

and likewise equivalent to

(1-p)^{k/2}\approx\exp\{-kp/2\}\approx\frac{1}{enp}.

(4)

Moreover, a larger
k
will make
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]
be
o(1)
, and a smaller
k
will make
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]
be
\Omega(n)
, therefore
k_{0}
must satisfy (
3
) and (
4
). For such a range of
k
,

\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{k+1}]}{\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]}=(n-k)\left(\frac{k+1}{k}\right)^{k-2}p(1-p)^{k-1}\approx\frac{1}{enp},

(5)

therefore
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]=o(1)
for all
k\geq k_{0}+2
.

From this final statement, it follows that
k_{0}
satisfies part (i) of Theorem
1
. Also, by Markov’s Inequality on
X_{k_{0}+2}
,
T(G_{n,p})
is at most
k_{0}+1
whp, which is precisely the upper bound given in part (ii) of Theorem
1
.

3
Second moment calculation

The goal of this Section is to verify the lower bound of part (ii) in Theorem
1
; i.e., to show that
T(G_{n,p})\geq k_{0}
whp if
p=\omega(n^{-1/2}\ln^{3/2}n)
; hence, throughout this Section we assume that
p=\omega(n^{-1/2}\ln^{3/2}n)
.

We prove that
T(G_{n,p})\geq k_{0}
whp using the second moment method on the random variable
Y_{k}
that counts the number of induced trees of size
k
such that all vertices not in
Y
have at least 3 neighbors in
Y
. We have (using (
3
))

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}]}{\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\sum_{i=0}^{2}\binom{k}{\ell}p^{i}(1-p)^{k-\ell}\right)^{n-k}

\displaystyle=(1-O(k^{2}/n^{2}))^{n-k}

\displaystyle=1-O\left(\frac{k^{2}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=1-o(1).

Since
\mathbb{E}[X_{k_{0}}]=\omega(1)
by definition, then
\mathbb{E}[Y_{k_{0}}]=\omega(1)
also. Therefore, to prove that
T(G_{n,p})\geq k_{0}
whp, by Chebyshev’s Inequality, it is enough to prove the following Lemma, which we will do in the remainder of this Section:

Lemma 2
.

For
k
satisfying (
3
) and (
4
) such that
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]=\omega(1)
:

\frac{\Var[Y_{k}]}{\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}]^{2}}=o(1).

For ease of notation, we fix one such
k
and write
X_{k}
as
X
and
Y_{k}
as
Y
.

We start by expressing both
X
and
Y
as sums of indicator variables. For any set
A
with
|A|=k
, and if
T_{A}
is a tree on the vertices of
A
, define

\displaystyle X_{A,T_{A}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }G_{n,p}[A]=T_{A}\\
0\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle Y_{A,T_{A}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }G_{n,p}[A]=T_{A}\text{ AND }\forall v\not\in A,\mathcal{N}(v)\cap A\geq 3\\
0\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of all possible tuples
(A,T_{A})
for which
A\in\binom{[n]}{k}
ad
T_{A}
is a tree with vertex set
A
. Clearly,
\mathbb{E}[X]=|\mathcal{S}|\cdot\mathbb{E}[X_{A,T_{A}}]
and
\mathbb{E}[Y]=|\mathcal{S}|\cdot\mathbb{E}[Y_{A,T_{A}}]
for all
(A,T_{A})\in\mathcal{S}
. Furthermore, since
\mathbb{E}[Y]\approx\mathbb{E}[X]
(and hence
\mathbb{E}[Y_{A,T_{A}}]\approx\mathbb{E}[X_{A,T_{A}}]
), to prove Lemma
2
it is enough to show that

\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}}\sum_{(A,T_{A}),(B,T_{B})\in\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{E}[Y_{A,T_{A}}Y_{B,T_{B}}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{A,T_{A}}]\mathbb{E}[Y_{B,T_{B}}])=o(1).

(6)

As is standard with the second moment method, we will split the sum above based on the size of
|A\cap B|
. We will first consider the case where
|A\cap B|=0
, the only case for which the
-\mathbb{E}[Y_{A,T_{A}}]\mathbb{E}[Y_{B,T_{B}}]
in the summand is necessary to use. We make use of the fact that, if
|A\cap B|=0
, then
X_{A,T_{A}}
and
X_{B,T_{B}}
are independent events:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(A,T_{A}),(B,T_{B})\in\mathcal{S}:\\
|A\cap B|=0\end{subarray}}(\mathbb{E}[Y_{A,T_{A}}Y_{B,T_{B}}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{A,T_{A}}]\mathbb{E}[Y_{B,T_{B}}])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(A,T_{A}),(B,T_{B})\in\mathcal{S}:\\
|A\cap B|=0\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{A,T_{A}}Y_{B,T_{B}}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{A,T_{A}}]\mathbb{E}[Y_{B,T_{B}}]}{\mathbb{E}[X_{A,T_{A}}]^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(A,T_{A}),(B,T_{B})\in\mathcal{S}:\\
|A\cap B|=0\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{A,T_{A}}X_{B,T_{B}}]-(1-o(1))\mathbb{E}[X_{A,T_{A}}]\mathbb{E}[X_{B,T_{B}}]}{\mathbb{E}[X_{A,T_{A}}]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(A,T_{A}),(B,T_{B})\in\mathcal{S}:\\
|A\cap B|=0\end{subarray}}o(1)

\displaystyle=o(1)

.

Furthermore, the sum above with the restriction
|A\cap B|=k
instead is at most
\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y]}{\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}}=o(1)
, so it is enough to consider the case
|A\cap B|\in[k-1]
. Next, we define parameters to equal partial sums of (
6
) dependent not only on a parameter
\ell:=|A\cap B|
, but also on a parameter
m
, equal to the number of components of
T_{A}
(likewise
T_{B}
) induced on
A\cap B
(if they are not the same, then surely
\mathbb{E}[Y_{A,T_{A}}Y_{B,T_{B}}]=0
), which we simply write as
T_{A}\cap T_{B}
. Define

\displaystyle x_{\ell,m}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(A,T_{A}),(B,T_{B})\in\mathcal{S}:\\
|A\cap B|=\ell,\\
T_{A}\cap T_{B}\text{ has $m$ components}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}[X_{A,T_{A}}X_{B,T_{B}}]

(7)

\displaystyle y_{\ell,m}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(A,T_{A}),(B,T_{B})\in\mathcal{S}:\\
|A\cap B|=\ell,\\
T_{A}\cap T_{B}\text{ has $m$ components}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}[Y_{A,T_{A}}Y_{B,T_{B}}]

(8)

To prove Lemma
2
, it is enough to show that

\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}y_{\ell,m}=o(1).

(9)

Clearly
y_{\ell,m}\leq x_{\ell,m}
by definition of
X_{A,T_{A}}
and
Y_{A,T_{A}}
; the following lemma demonstrates where
y_{\ell,m}
significantly differs from
x_{\ell,m}
:

Lemma 3
.

Let
\ell\in[k-1]
and
m\in[\ell]
. If
(k-\ell)p<1
and
2(k-\ell)-m+1>0
, then

y_{\ell,m}\leq x_{\ell,m}\cdot((k-\ell)p)^{2(k-\ell)-m+1}.

Proof.

Suppose that we have fixed sets
A,B
and trees
T_{A},T_{B}
(which coincide on
A\cap B
) such that
|A\cap B|=\ell
and there are
m
components in
T_{A}\cap T_{B}
. We will condition
G_{n,p}
on the event
\mathcal{E}:=\{X_{A,T_{A}}X_{B,T_{B}}=1\}
. Note that
\mathcal{E}
only depends on the edges within
\binom{A}{2}\cup\binom{B}{2}
, so all pairs of vertices in
(A\backslash B,B\backslash A)
appear as edges in
G_{n,p}
(conditioned on
\mathcal{E}
) independently with probability
p
.

Next, suppose that the vertices of
A\backslash B
are
a_{1},\dots,a_{k-\ell}
, and
D(i):=\mathcal{N}(a_{i})\cap A\cap B
. Because
T_{A}[A\cap B]
has
m
components, there are at most
(k-\ell)+m-1
edges from
A\backslash B
to
A\cap B
, so
\sum_{i=1}^{k-\ell}D(i)\leq k-\ell+m-1
. If
Y_{B,T_{B}}=1
, then each vertex
a_{i}\in A\backslash B
has
at least
\min\{3-D(i),0\}
neighbors in
B\backslash A
which, by union bound, happens with probability at most
\min\{((k-\ell)p)^{3-D(i)},1\}
. Furthermore, these
k-\ell
events are all independent of each other and independent of
\mathcal{E}
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\Pr[Y_{A,T_{A}}Y_{B,T_{B}}=1\mid\mathcal{E}]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[Y_{B,T_{B}}=1\mid\mathcal{E}]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k-\ell}\min\{((k-\ell)p)^{3-D(i)},1\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{k-\ell}((k-\ell)p)^{3-D(i)},1\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\{((k-\ell)p)^{3(k-\ell)-(k-\ell+m-1)},1\}

\displaystyle=((k-\ell)p)^{2(k-\ell)-m+1}.

Therefore
\Pr[Y_{A,T_{A}}Y_{B,T_{B}}=1]\leq((k-\ell)p)^{2(k-\ell)-m+1}\Pr[X_{A,T_{A}}X_{B,T_{B}}=1]
, and since
(A,T_{A})
and
(B,T_{B})
were arbitrary such that
|A\cap B|=\ell
and
T_{A}\cap T_{B}
has
m
components, then by (
7
) and (
8
) the Lemma holds.

∎

3.1
A general upper bound for
x_{\ell,m}

First, for fixed sets
A
and
B
, we determine the number of possible trees
T_{A}
and
T_{B}
that coincide on
A\cap B
such that the number of components in this overlap is
m
. We count in the following way: suppose that the vertex sets of the components of
T_{A}\cap T_{B}
are
F_{1},\dots,F_{m}
with
f_{i}:=|F_{i}|
for all

. Then:

1.

The total number of possibilities for
T_{A}\cap T_{B}
is

\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}^{f_{i}-2}.

(10)

2.

The number of ways to complete the trees
T_{A}
and
T_{B}
, by Lemma
4
in the appendix, is

\left(\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}\right)k^{k-\ell-1}(k-\ell)^{m-1}\right)^{2}.

(11)

Therefore, by multiplying the expressions (
10
) and (
11
) with
\binom{\ell}{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}}
, the number of ways to arrange
F_{1},\dots,F_{m}
in
A\cap B
, then enumerating over all possible
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
, then finally dividing by
m!
to eliminate overcounting by permuting the labels of
F_{1},\dots,F_{m}
, we have, for fixed
A
,
B
with

:

\displaystyle|T_{A},T_{B}:T_{A}\cap T_{B}\text{ has $m$ components}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}:\\
f_{1}+\dots+f_{m}=\ell\end{subarray}}\frac{\ell!}{m!}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{f_{i}^{f_{i}}}{f_{i}!}\right)k^{2k-2\ell-2}(k-\ell)^{2m-2}.

Next, we use Lemma
5
from the appendix to give a useful upper bound of the above expression:

\displaystyle|T_{A},T_{B}:T_{A}\cap T_{B}\text{ has $m$ components}|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{e^{\ell}\ell^{(m-2)/2}}{2^{m/2}\Gamma(m/2)}\right)\frac{\ell!}{m!}k^{2k-2\ell-2}(k-\ell)^{2m-2}

\displaystyle=k^{2k-2\ell-2}\left(\ell^{(m-2)/2}\ell!e^{\ell}\right)(k-\ell)^{2m-2}\left(\frac{1}{2^{m/2}\Gamma(m/2)m!}\right).

Next, we multiply the above expression by
\binom{n}{k}\binom{k}{\ell}\binom{n-k}{k-\ell}
, the number of ways to choose sets
A
and
B
such that
|A\cap B|=\ell
:

\displaystyle|(A,T_{A}),(B,T_{B})\in\mathcal{S}:|A\cap B|=\ell,\text{ and $T_{A},T_{B}$ coincide on $A\cap B$ with $m$ components}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}\binom{n-k}{k-\ell}\frac{k!}{(k-\ell)!}k^{2k-2\ell-2}\left(\ell^{(m-2)/2}e^{\ell}\right)(k-\ell)^{2m-2}\left(\frac{1}{2^{m/2}\Gamma(m/2)m!}\right).

Next, suppose that
T_{A}
and
T_{B}
coincide on

; then
there are
2k-\ell+m-2

, hence

\mathbb{E}[X_{A,T_{A}}X_{B,T_{B}}]=\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^{2k-\ell+m-2}(1-p)^{2\binom{k}{2}-\binom{\ell}{2}}.

(12)

Putting everything together with (
7
) and recalling (

x_{\ell,m}\leq\frac{k!\binom{n-k}{k-\ell}}{(k-\ell)!\binom{n}{k}}k^{-2\ell+2}\left(\ell^{(m-2)/2}e^{\ell}\right)(k-\ell)^{2m-2}\left(\frac{1}{2^{m/2}\Gamma(m/2)m!}\right)\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^{-\ell+m}(1-p)^{-\binom{\ell}{2}}.

(13)

With a general upper bound of
x_{\ell,m}
, together with Lemma
3
, we now work to bound the sum (

). We consider two cases, depending on whether

.

3.2
Case 1:
\ell\leq k/2

Here we show

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}x_{\ell,m}=o(1);

indeed, it is sufficient to just use the parameter

instead of

.

First, note that

\binom{n-k}{k-\ell}\leq\binom{n}{k-\ell}=\binom{n}{k}\frac{k(k-1)\dots(k-\ell+1)}{(n-k+1)(n-k+2)\dots(n-k+\ell)}\leq\binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{k}{n-k}\right)^{\ell}.

Therefore, by (

\displaystyle x_{\ell,m}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{3e^{\ell p/2}}{np}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{2^{m/2}\Gamma(m/2)m!}\right)\left(2k^{2}p\ell^{0.5}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\left(\Theta(1)\frac{e^{\ell p/2}}{\ell}\frac{k^{2}}{n}\right)^{\ell}.

Since (by (

\displaystyle\ell<\frac{1}{p}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\frac{e^{\ell p/2}}{\ell}=O(1/\ell)=O(1)

then (by (

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}x_{\ell,m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\left(O(1+n^{0.5}p^{1.5})\frac{k^{2}}{n}\right)^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\left(O\left(\frac{k^{2}}{n}+\frac{k^{2}p^{1.5}}{n^{0.5}}\right)\right)^{\ell}

By (

\displaystyle x_{k-j,m}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[X]}\frac{k!\binom{n-k}{j}}{j!}k^{2j-k}\left(k^{(m-2)/2}e^{k-j}\right)j^{2m-2}\left(\frac{1}{2^{m/2}\Gamma(m/2)m!}\right)\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^{j+m-1}(1-p)^{\binom{k}{2}-\binom{k-j}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{(j!)^{2}}(1-p)^{(k-j)j+\binom{j}{2}}\right)\left(\frac{nk^{2}p}{e(1-p)}\right)^{j}\left(\frac{1}{2^{m/2}\Gamma(m/2)m!}\right)\left(\frac{k^{0.5}j^{2}p}{1-p}\right)^{m-1}\frac{k!e^{k}}{k^{k+0.5}}

\displaystyle=\left(\Theta(1)\frac{nk^{2}p(1-p)^{k}e^{jp/2}}{j^{2}}\right)^{j}\left(\Theta(1)\frac{k^{0.5}j^{2}p}{m^{1.5}}\right)^{m-1}\qquad\text{by (\ref{equ: Stirling's})}

\displaystyle=\left(\Theta(1)\frac{k^{2}e^{jp/2}}{npj^{2}}\right)^{j}\left(\Theta(1)\frac{k^{0.5}j^{2}p}{m^{1.5}}\right)^{m-1}\qquad\text{by (\ref{equ: k usefuler approx}).}

(14)

We now split into three subcases:

3.3.1

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor k^{-2}p^{-3}\rfloor}\sum_{m=1}^{2j}y_{k-j,m}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor k^{-2}p^{-3}\rfloor}\sum_{m=1}^{2j}\left(\Theta(1)\frac{k^{2}}{npj^{2}}\right)^{j}\left(\Theta(1)\frac{k^{0.5}j^{2}p}{m^{1.5}}\right)^{m-1}(jp)^{2j-m+1}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor k^{-2}p^{-3}\rfloor}\sum_{m=2j+1}^{k-j}x_{k-j,m}

\displaystyle=o(1).

3.3.3

\displaystyle\sum_{j=\lceil k^{-2}p^{-3}\rceil}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\sum_{m=1}^{k-j}x_{k-j,m}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=\lceil k^{-2}p^{-3}\rceil}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\sum_{m=1}^{k-j}\left(\Theta(1)\frac{k^{2}e^{(k/2)p/2}}{np(k^{-2}p^{-3})^{2}}\right)^{j}\left(\Theta(1)\frac{k^{0.5}j^{2}p}{m^{1.5}}\right)^{m-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=\lceil k^{-2}p^{-3}\rceil}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\sum_{m=1}^{k-j}\left(\Theta(1)\frac{k^{6}p^{5.5}}{n^{0.5}}\right)^{j}\left(\Theta(1)\frac{k^{1/3}j^{4/3}p^{2/3}}{m}\right)^{1.5(m-1)}

If

One could prove Lemma

be asymptotically equal to

4
Drift from the expectation threshold for small
p

Here, we consider a third random variable. Let
W_{k}
be the number of
maximal
induced trees with size
k
. An induced tree is a maximal induced tree if and only if no vertex outside has exactly one neighbor inside. Thus,

\frac{\mathbb{E}[W_{k}]}{\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]}=\left(1-kp(1-p)^{k-1}\right)^{n-k}.

When
p=o(n^{-1/2})
, this ratio is small enough that it can be used to prove part (iii) of Theorem
1
.

Proof of (iii) of Theorem
1
.

It is enough to show that whp
T(G_{n,p})\not\in[k_{0}-1/(4np^{2}),k_{0}+1]
, since we already noted that
T(G_{n,p})\leq k_{0}+1
by Markov’s Inequality using variable
X_{k_{0}+2}
in Section
2
. Note that, for all
k\in[k_{0}-1/(4np^{2}),k_{0}+1]
, it satisfies (
3
) and (
4
).

If
T(G_{n,p})=k
, then certainly
W_{k}\geq 1
. Therefore, by Markov’s Inequality, and using (
3
), (
4
), and (
5
):

\displaystyle\Pr[T(G_{n,p})\in[k_{0}-1/(4np^{2}),k_{0}+1]]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=\lceil k_{0}-1/(4np^{2})\rceil}^{k_{0}+1}\mathbb{E}[W_{k}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=\lceil k_{0}-1/(4np^{2})\rceil}^{k_{0}+1}\left(1-kp(1-p)^{k-1}\right)^{n-k}\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=\lceil k_{0}-1/(4np^{2})\rceil}^{k_{0}+1}\exp\{(-1+o(1))k_{0}e^{-2}n^{-1}p^{-1}\}\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]

\displaystyle=\exp\{(-1+o(1))k_{0}e^{-2}n^{-1}p^{-1}\}\mathbb{E}[X_{k_{0}+2}]\sum_{k=\lceil k_{0}-1/(4np^{2})\rceil}^{k_{0}+1}\prod_{j=k}^{k_{0}+1}\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]}{\mathbb{E}[X_{j+1}]}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\{(-1+o(1))k_{0}e^{-2}n^{-1}p^{-1}\}\left(3np\right)^{k_{0}-(k_{0}-1/(4np^{2}))+2}

\displaystyle=\exp\{(-1+o(1))k_{0}e^{-2}n^{-1}p^{-1}+(1+o(1))(k_{0}p/2)/(4np^{2})\}

\displaystyle=\exp\{(1/8-e^{-2}+o(1))k_{0}n^{-1}p^{-1}\}

\displaystyle=o(1).

∎

5
Closing remarks

To prove two-point concentration for smaller
p
, one would need to consider a random variable that does not simply count induced trees of size
k
with restrictions. Instead, the likely way forward would be to count sets with
k+\ell
vertices, called “clusters”, which contain multiple induced trees of size
k
inside. This kind of clustering technique was used by Bohman and the author
[
4
]
to prove two-point concentration of the independence number of
G_{n,p}
for values of
p
too small to just count independent sets. It appears that the ideal cluster structure for trees for
p
slightly smaller than
n^{-1/2}\ln^{3/2}n
is one whose 2-core, after contracting vertices of degree 2 (with respect to the
k
-core), is a random 3-regular multigraph, with a restriction for how many neighbors a vertex outside can have inside the cluster, as well as a restriction on what subset of the vertices of degree 3 in the 2-core can be cut vertices for the entire cluster. Even the first moment calculations needed here are surprisingly difficult.

One could also ask whether the above results can be modified to account for induced forests instead of induced trees. For constant
p
, the problem is simple because a significant proportion of the maximum induced forests are trees, such that one can perform second moment calculations on just trees and still derive 2-point concentration for forests; this is precisely what was done in
[
10
]
. However, if
p=o(1)
, then it can be shown that the typical maximum induced forest of size
k
contains a tree that covers all but roughly
1/p
of the vertices in the forest. The remainder of the forest will behave like
G(1/p,p)
conditioned on there being no cycles. Understanding the behavior of this remainder will likely involve a detailed analysis in the critical threshold of
G_{n,p}
where a giant component emerges; specifically, where
p=\frac{1}{n}+O(n^{-4/3})
. This is required just to perform the first-moment calculations.

Lemma 5

For all positive integers

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}:\\

By (

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}:\\

(16)

If

Next, we consider
