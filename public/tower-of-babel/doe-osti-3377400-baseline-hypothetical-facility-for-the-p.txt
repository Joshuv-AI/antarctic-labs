Executive Summary
This report describes a hypothetical facility for production of medical radioisotopes via activation
under the Proliferation Resistance and Optimization (PRO-X) program. The facility uses neutron
activation of non-special nuclear material (SNM) to produce the medical isotopes 131I and 99Mo at
a throughput of 60 Ci/week of 131I and 5 Ci/week of 99Mo.

The hypothetical design was carried out using a 10 MWt research reactor. The precursors used for
the activation process were TeO2 for 131I and MoO3 for 99Mo. The processes are performed in 3
hot cells used for target receipt, extraction, purification low specific activity (LSA) generator
introduction, and packaging. A fourth hotcell is used for waste processing. The hot cell processing
area takes up a footprint of 15.4 m2 with the total footprint of the facility, including space for
administrative offices, non-rad labs, quality assurance, and radiation buffer areas set at 763 m2.
Waste is produced at a weekly rate of 257.8 g low activity solid waste and 8032.7 mL of low
activity liquid waste, 8032 mL of which is water.

This baseline hypothetical facility for production of medical isotopes via activation was then
compared and contrasted to the hypothetical facility for production of medical isotopes via fission
products to show the differences in approach for the two production modes. The two production
modes had several highlighted differences including the overall facility and hot cell layout, the
type and amount of waste produced by the respective facilities, and economic factors impacting
production mode. Finally, a decision tree for which production mode might be more beneficial for
an entrant into medical isotope production was developed based on the differences examined and
the desired output of medical isotopes desired by the entrant.

1. Introduction
The Office of Material Management and Minimization (M3) Proliferation Resistance Optimization
(PRO-X) program establishes frameworks for evaluation of designs of research reactor (RR)
systems that are optimized in the intrinsic features of the design for both inherent proliferation
resistance and the performance of their stated, peaceful uses. The reduction of proliferation risk is
achieved via the minimization of special nuclear materials (SNM) (i.e. U, Pu) usage, SNM
production, and potential diversion pathways. PRO-RR designs are considered from the angle of
three overlapping technical areas: Core Design, Fuel Design and Fabrication, and Auxiliary
Capabilities (AUX). The generic scope of these three areas is described in Figure 1-1.

 Figure 1-1: Venn diagram of the scope of the three PRO-RR technical areas.

This report, as part of the PRO-RR AUX (Figure 1-2) technical area, develops and describes a
baseline (non-optimized) hypothetical facility design to produce two radioisotopes, 99Mo and 131I,

 1

from non-SNM targets via neutron activation. These two isotopes were selected due to their
common use in medicine and their typical production involves SNM. The facility design includes
elements auxiliary to the reactor for the processing and handling of the activated materials,
including chemical separations and packaging of the product materials.

  Figure 1-2: The PRO-X optimization strategy balances the performance of a research reactor
 with the proliferation resistance and enhanced safety of the design.

Prior work by PRO-RR AUX has investigated the production of these radioisotopes via fission-
based targets containing high-assay low-enriched 235U (HALEU).[1] Following an irradiation, the
targets are then dissolved and processed to harvest desired isotopes. While this production
technology yields large quantities of isotopes to meet global demands, it also incurs significant
quantities of processed special nuclear material with no identified disposition path.

An alternate mode of medical radioisotope production with irradiation targets, chemical processing
flowsheets, and chemical processing systems that are inherently proliferation-resistant to the
fission targets are those using activation targets as described herein. The prior work established a
framework for the radiological and chemical processes that would be utilized by a hypothetical
facility focused on the production of 131I and 99Mo.[2] The previous work included material
balances and radiological summaries that followed each process with activation targets. This report
utilizes the chemistry discussed in the prior reporting to establish a hypothetical auxiliary facility
to separate the medical isotopes from the activated targets. Included herein are details of the
required chemical processing, hot cells, equipment, and facility layout of an unoptimized
hypothetical production facility.

A comparison of the system chemicals, hot cells number and size and shielding, waste type and
volumes and overall footprint of the facilities for the fission target and the activation targets is
provided. A decision tree to aid a new entrant in selection of their production method is provided.

2. Design Process Overview

2.1 Design Basis
The process flow of the target activation and separation is based on the prior report which outlined
the processes a hypothetical facility producing 131I and 99Mo via activation would perform.[2] The
processing of activation-based 131I targets was based on the dry distillation method. The processes
to produce 99Mo from activation were based on work carried out by the Institute of Nuclear Physics
in Kazakhstan using the WWR-K research reactor, as well as work on the chemical processing of
irradiated Mo targets performed by Argonne National Laboratory.[3, 4]

The targeted production rates for the processes in the prior report, and for this hypothetical facility,
are 60 Ci/week of 131I and 5 Ci/week of 99Mo. The 60 Ci 131I /week target was selected to provide
a direct comparison for the processes designed for 131I production via fission as described in
additional prior work by the PRO-X program.[1] For the fission-based process, it was determined
that a facility capable of producing 60 Ci 131I/week from fission targets would also be able to
produce 750 Ci/week of 99Mo from these same targets. For the activation-based process, the 99Mo
production is now independent of the 131I production process, utilizing separate targets. The
significantly lower target of 5 Ci 99Mo/week for the activation-based process was selected due to
the lower activity of 99Mo that is produced via activation, often referred to as low specific activity
(LSA) 99Mo when compared to fission.

2.2 Target Irradiation

2.2.1 Reactor

Target irradiation conditions were pinned to the irradiation position of the 10 MW Materials Test
Reactor (MTR) design as presented in the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) TECDOC
233 “Research Reactor Core Conversion from the Use of Highly Enriched Uranium to the Use of
Low Enriched Uranium Fuels Guidebook.”[5] This design is representative of many MTR-style
plate fuel research reactors. The 10MW MTR was originally being used to demonstrate
methodologies for converting fuel from highly enriched uranium (HEU) to low-enriched uranium
(LEU). Reactor power is assumed to be 10 MW. The moderator and coolant of the reactor is pure
light water, and the reflectors are modeled as unclad graphite blocks. The target irradiation
(production/depletion of the isotopes) were calculated using an equilibrium core, the contents
(uranium, plutonium, americium, and samarium) of each fuel assembly are set to values that
approximate a core at the beginning of a cycle within an out-in fuel shuffle pattern.

2.2.3 Irradiation Calculations

The isotopic composition of the irradiated targets was calculated using the SCALE software
suite.[6] The TRITON sequence within SCALE was used to capture case-specific irradiation
conditions, including reactor and target geometry, as well as a neutron flux, and using the TRITON
sequence the rates of change in composition for the target nuclei were determined. These rates
were then used to determine the material composition as a function of irradiation time and post-
irradiation decay time using ORIGEN. ORIGEN is also used to calculate the activity of each
nuclide within the material at given times post-irradiation. This enables for the computation of
dose rates at those time steps. While ORIGEN does not take the chemical processes performed on
the materials in the time post-irradiation into account directly, these losses of specific isotopes and
elements via these processing steps can be calculated for each step to produce a mass balance that
can additionally be used to determine doses from the relevant radioisotopes at the individual steps
in the processing of the material. The steps involved in the irradiation calculations in ORIGEN for
both 131I and 99Mo can be seen below in Figure 2-2 and Figure 2-3, respectively.

 Figure 2-2: Process flow diagram for the ORIGEN irradiation calculations for 131I.

 6

 Figure 2-3: Process flow diagram for the ORIGEN irradiation calculations for 99Mo.

The compositions of the two targets are calculated in ORIGEN at specific points in the production
process at time steps relative to the end of irradiation (EOI) time for each target. The milestones
include are:

 1. EOI.
 2. EOI+1 day: the point, after a one-day cooldown period post-irradiation, at which
 processing can begin.
 3. EOI+6 days: the point at which the products are delivered.
 4. Waste Cut: The waste cut is tracked at both 1 and 5 years after EOI and represents the
 composition of the materials diverted to waste streams from the processing of the targets,
 allowing for tracking of radiological wastes within the hypothetical facility design. 5 years
 was chosen per US 10 CFR 61.55 that provides guidance on waste classification.[7]

3. Hot Cell Descriptions
This section discusses the processing of the activated targets after irradiation. The three hot cells
represent three phases of the post-irradiation handling and processing of the materials: a target
receipt stage, a processing (separations) stage, and a packaging stage.

 • The first hot cell is for target receipt and the storage of the activated targets for a specified
 decay time before further handling, as well as removal of the material from the target
 containers.
 • The second hot cell is where the chemical separations processes will separate the desired
 product materials from the rest of the target material.
 • In the third hot cell, the separated product material is packaged into its final form for
 delivery to the end users.

While the decay times, separations chemistry, and packaging strategy for the 99Mo and 131I differ,
at this scale, the two can undergo these steps in the same hot cell, with dividers providing additional
separation to mitigate concerns of potential cross-contamination. Each of these specific stages of
processing are discussed in the corresponding hot cell sections, which include process descriptions
and material balances assumed for each process, as well as a list of equipment needed for the
processes and a diagram of the proposed configuration of the hot cell.

3.1 Hot Cell 1: Target Receipt
The first hot cell is used for the receipt of the irradiated targets and provides a storage space for
these targets from EOI until a sufficient decay time has been achieved to begin processing, at which
point the irradiated target material is removed from the original target container and then relocated
to Hot Cell 2. The decay time of the 131I material is 6 hours post-irradiation, while the decay time
for the 99Mo targets is one day post-irradiation.

3.1.1 Process Description

Irradiated targets are initially transferred post-irradiation to Hot Cell 1, with the process diagram
outlined in Figure 3-1. They are stored in this hot cell for a determined period to decay prior to
processing. To meet a target production rate of 60 Ci 131I/week and 5 Ci 99Mo/week, 4 TeO2 targets
and 1 MoO3 target are needed per week. These 5 targets arrive in Hot Cell 1 post irradiation. The
TeO2 (131I) targets begin processing at EOI+6 hours, while the processing of MoO3 (99Mo) targets
begins at EOI+1 day. After their allotted decay time, the first step of processing each of the targets
is the removal of the target material from the containers. For the 131I targets, the lids are removed
from the containers in Hot Cell 1 at EOI+6 hours. These containers are then transferred to Hot Cell
2, while their lids are disposed of as solid waste. For the 99Mo target, the lid is removed from the
container at EOI+1 day, and the container is transferred to Hot Cell 2, while the lid is disposed of
as solid waste.

3.2 Hot Cell 2: Separations
The separation of the product materials from the containers and other undesired contents, including
impurities, for both the 131I and 99Mo targets occurs in Hot Cell 2. While the processing of these
two products is performed via liquid and gas processes that are contained within separate lines,
further mitigation of cross contaminating the two products is achieved via a physical divider within
the cell. For the 131I targets, the chemical separation is performed via a dry distillation process
where the materials are heated into a furnace, and the 131I is from there carried along a carrier gas
through a series of traps. The 99Mo separation process is comprised of a series of stages beginning
with caustic dissolution, followed by an iron precipitation stage, a pH adjustment, filtration, and
evaporation.

3.2.1 Process Description

In the 131I processing side of Hot Cell 2, the lidless containers containing the irradiated TeO2/131I
target material are first loaded into a furnace crucible, which is heated to 700 °C, and held at this
temperature for 60 minutes. The iodine in the target material sublimes into a carrier gas with a
flow rate of 25 mL/min. During this distillation, ~95% of the iodine is removed from the target.
The carrier gas then flows through a series of two bubbler traps, which each contain 8 mL of 0.1
 15

M NaOH solution, as well as 0.2 mg/mL Na2SO3 as a reducing agent. The primary bubbler trap
removes ~85% of the iodine from the carrier gas, and the remainder is collected in the secondary
bubbler trap. The two traps combined collect 99.99% of the iodine. The target containers and solid
furnace residual materials are collected from the furnace and disposed of as solid waste. The
product iodine solution is then transferred to Hot Cell 3 for packaging. The process flow for Hot
Cell 2 is shown in Figure 3-3.

 Figure 3-3: Process Flow Diagram for Purification of 131I.

In the 99Mo processing side of Hot Cell 2, the lidless container containing the irradiated
MoO3/99Mo material is emptied into the 99Mo reactor, where it will undergo three stages of
processing: caustic dissolution, iron precipitation, and pH adjustment (Figure 3-4). The emptied
lidless container is disposed of as solid waste. In the 99Mo reactor, 50 mL of 6 M NaOH dissolves
the MoO3 and forms sodium molybdate. 4 mL of 1 M iron(II) chloride solution is then added to
the reactor, where the salt reacts with the free caustic to form an iron hydroxide precipitate that
also captures the impurities from the target material. 1.76 mL of concentrated HNO3 is used to
adjust the pH of the solution. The solution from the 99Mo reactor is then passed through a Whatman
filter to remove the insoluble Fe(OH)2 salt, also removing the Nb, Tc, and Zr impurities present in
the irradiated molybdenum target material. The filtered solution then enters the evaporator, where
water is added to reduce the pH to 7. The solution is then heated in the evaporator to reduce the
 16

solution to a final concentration of 135 g Mo/L H2O. This solution is then transferred to Hot Cell
3 for packaging.

3.3.1 LSA Process Description

After recovery of Mo product solution, it is combined with a Low Specific Activity (LSA)

3.5.2 Hot Cell Shielding

The shielding requirement for each hot cell is taken to be the shielding required for the limiting
step performed in that hot cell. “+ 1 day” and “Cut” steps are before and after an (assumed
instantaneous) separation of the desired product material. The drop is due to the removal of waste
from the material. Shielding requirements for waste from a single target are given in the previous
section. Waste from multiple batches may require additional shielding. Guidelines on current Good
RadioPharmacy Practice (cGRPP) state that, where possible, each process should have a dedicated
area.[8] For Hot Cell 3, and possibly Hot Cells 1 and 2, this may require a divider wall or other
separation to achieve this requirement.

The main contributors to the TeO2 waste are Te activation products with half-lives under a year, so
the shielding requirement for extended operation past 5 years would not increase significantly. For
MoO3, the big contributor is 60Co (half-life 5.271 year) from cobalt impurities, which is only about
halfway to saturation at 5 years, so extended operation past 5 years may increase the shielding
requirement by 1 to 2 cm.

Shielding requirements are estimated based on the radiation source term associated with the
limiting material stream processed by each hot cell. ORIGEN calculations used to determine the
isotopic content of the material for each processing step are also utilized to produce a gamma ray
source term associated with each step. The 47-group “fine” energy bin structure is used for these
calculations. Material streams which pass from one hot cell to the next are attributed to both hot
cells for the purposes of determining the limiting stream, though in practice the initial stream

 28

entering the cell is the limiting stream for all processing hot cells. Processing steps are represented
in ORIGEN using idealized separation splits, resulting in a particular ORIGEN step applying to
one or more streams within the material balance calculations.

Detailed shielding design for the gloveboxes was not undertaken. Rather, the thickness of lead
required to reduce the dose rate at 30 cm to 5 mrem/h is used to give the “lead equivalent” required
shielding. This calculation is performed using MCNP6.2.[6] A spherically symmetric geometry is
used. The radioactive material is treated as a point source within a 0.038 cm radius aluminum
sphere. This radius was selected since it corresponds to a common aluminum cladding thickness
and is conservatively thin for any vessel likely to be used in processing. The lead shield inner
radius is set at 1 cm, assumed to be the closest that a source item would likely approach the inner
surface of the hot cell, given the finite dimensions of a real source. Lead thickness of 1 cm, 10 cm,
and multiples of 10 cm until sufficient shielding is achieved is used. A surface tally 30 cm outside
the lead shell is used with an appropriate response function to determine the dose rate. The final
lead thickness required is interpolated between the two adjacent dose points using the logarithm
of the dose rates. This is chosen over linear interpolation since the probability of transmission for
a gamma ray decreases exponentially with shielding thickness. The shielding requirements for
each hot cell is shown in Table 3-12.

4.1.1 Quality Control Laboratory

There are two types of Quality Control/Quality Assurance (QC/QA) samples and measurements
required for a radiopharmaceutical facility: Product Assurance (PA) and Operational Support (OS).
Each of these QC functions will need to have separate tools and instruments to perform the
appropriate analyses.

The products from the operation are drugs that will be either ingested, injected, or externally
applied to medical patients. Requirements for analytical procedures, facility cleanliness, and
analyses performed will be dictated by the governmental agency responsible for maintaining the
quality of the medicine. Sample collection will be performed in the different product packaging
hot cells in the main facility. The sample sizes are assumed to be an aliquot sized for a specific test
being run so that they can be removed from the hot cell line and analyzed in laboratory gloveboxes,
hoods, or benchtop facilities. Due to the need to potentially analyze the three products recovered
by this process at the same time, multiple analytical workspaces and equipment will be needed. At
a minimum, the PA QA facility will have:

 • 3 gloveboxes,
 • 3 fume hoods,
 • 3 analytical equipment set-ups.

The QC for the operation will be used to ensure that the process equipment and waste management

 • A single glovebox,

Within the processing facility, there will need to be areas for administration and logistic operations.
These areas include such functions as:

 • Production control office space,
 • Radiation protection monitoring,

 • Personal protective equipment storage, and

4.2 Off-Gas Management
The full facility will contain three zones of HVAC off-gas protection. All gases identified in
process flow sheets in Section 3 that are released from the system will go through the Zone 1
handling system and then are released into the Zone 2 (hot cell) atmosphere. The Zone 1 system
contains a series of demisters, pads, and HEPA filters to collect any radiological releases from the
purification process that are not caught by the process filters themselves. The potential to collect
higher activity off-gases from the process means that the equipment for Zone 1 is fully contained
within the hot cell structure and the filters changed regularly to ensure the radiological barrier is
maintained.

Zone 2 contains hot cells, gloveboxes and hoods within the facility. Zone 2 operates as backup for
Zone 1 processes, seeing all filtered exhaust from Zone 1. Zone 2 should not experience
radiological releases as part of normal operations.

The Zone 3 system is designed to handle all air flow that is used to maintain the negative pressure
of the entire facility. And gas released from Zone 2 is handled by the Zone 3 system, but this zone
is not intended to handle any radiological material.

5.2 Hot Cells
Hot cells are a significant portion of the design of a medical isotope facility. Each hot cell is
designed with an intended purpose and an expectation of the materials to be handled in the hot cell
due to the difficulty of modifying the cells after construction. As such, the shielding required for
each hot cell is based on the portion of the operations carried out in the hot cell. The need for the
hot cell to be customized to the specific work going on within it makes the hot side of the facility
a key point of difference between the hypothetical fission product and activation facilities.

Comparing the hot cells developed for the hypothetical fission product facility and the hot cell line
for activation immediately shows a difference in not only the amount of hot cells to complete the
production of medical isotopes, but the amount of steps necessary to complete the production of
medical isotopes. This is because the medical isotopes made using a fission product approach must
be first separated from the HALEU target before being purified and this process uses a different
set of consumables, a different set of equipment, and requires a higher level of shielding than the
purification step. This critical first step of fission product production does not have a clear parallel
in an activation approach, where purification and separation from the natural isotope is a relatively
straightforward process.

5.3 Waste Streams
Waste streams between the two hypothetical facilities are also a key comparison point. The most
notable difference is the generation of transuranic (TRU) waste (also known as high activity waste)
by the fission product facility while the activation production method does not generate TRU
waste. While TRU waste is not an identifier that is used outside of the US, it is useful to discuss

because of the amount of additional processes, complications, and hazards that are involved with
disposing of this type of waste. In particular, the storage, handling, and transport to a waste
handling facility is typically much more labor intensive and hazardous than handling even
intermediate activities that are not TRU, which incurs significant extra cost. Without access to
TRU or high activity waste processing, or enough running programs to support the movement of

5.4 Economics
Economic arguments between using a fission product production process versus an activation
production product focus on several main key points including:[9, 10]

 • Material costs of consumables and targets,
 • Production throughput and efficiency,
 • Ccalability of production vs operational costs.
 • Infrastructure requirements for the auxiliary facility, and
 • Waste handling.

Initial material costs are higher for HALEU targets than TeO2 and MoO3 targets in a gram-to-gram
comparison, and the separation and purification processes take more consumables than activation,
except for water. While costs of both HALEU and activation target materials will fluctuate and
increase as more actors seek to produce medical isotopes locally, the initial assessment of costs is
highly unlikely to change. With these key points in mind, a generalized representation of
associated costs associated with the methods vs medical isotope production amounts for activation
and fission product methods is presented in Figure 5-2. This generalized graph is meant to illustrate
the higher start-up costs of the fission product production method and the higher cost of
maintaining the facility and waste processing plant vs an activation production method. The
activation product approach, by comparison, is much lower in cost for the initial start-up costs and
as long as production remains low, this holds at a relative state. After the bottleneck for irradiation

 40

of the targets has been reached however, activation will require an additional reactor for more
irradiation space, significantly increasing costs to generate additional product isotopes. Based on
this analysis, fission product production becomes most economically attractive when irradiation
space for activation production is used up in a single reactor, otherwise activation production is
more the more economically attractive option for an aspiring entrant to domestic medical isotope
production seeking to only service their country or limited nearby neighbors.

Despite costs between the targets being higher for HALEU, HALEU produces more products that
can be used for medical purposes, such as 133Xe, in addition to more 99Mo. A greater activity from
a volume of target and from irradiation in a reactor favors fission product production of the
radioisotopes over activation production. Smaller production goals favor activation which
minimizes additional costs such as feed material, waste disposal, consumables, and facility
footprint.

Smaller facility footprints for activation production seem ideal then for starting, but economics of
scale favor fission product production due to having more production in a HALEU target. In
addition to the higher efficiency of HALEU targets, natural TeO2 and MoO3 are not produced as
target blades that are inserted into target channels but in test loops or beam ports, both of which
are limited for scaling purposes. This essentially imposes a bottleneck on the throughput of a single
research reactors output of activation based medical isotopes compared to the fission product
production method.

However, infrastructure and operating costs are higher for a fission product production facility
compared to an activation-based facility. This is due to higher shielding for a greater number of
high cells, more personnel required due to increased operational complexity, more personnel to
meet potential increased security requirements for HALEU, and more maintenance requirements
for the harsher chemistry involved in separations for HALEU.

A final consideration that affects the choice between fission product production and activation
production is access to a waste handling facility that’s capable of processing high activity/TRU

 41

waste. The costs and effort associated with having and maintaining a waste processing program if
one is not readily available in a nearby country is a complicated problem that falls outside of the
scope of this report but would add significant base costs for HALEU target processing that would
keep HALEU processing costs high over time despite having higher overall throughput.

5.5 Decision Tree Analysis
Entrant actors interested in the production of medical isotopes are faced with a series of decisions
that they must make before building an auxiliary facility suited to their needs. Typically, the
question of medical isotope production goals is the primary driving question for any actor
attempting to enter this field, with an overall production split either between domestic use or
exporting.

There are many factors that can influence factors that are made in this area that lay far outside of
technical hurdles. This decision tree analysis attempts to generate a generalized list of steps and
decisions every actor interested in building a medical isotope production facility would ask, no
matter what external factors may be for the actor. An important note is that this decision tree does
not factor in accelerator style activation processes which is outside the scope of the PRO-X RR
mission. The decision tree (Figure 5-3) contains the elements used in the previous table to compare
fission vs activation. All the discussion about the decision tree is distilled from prior reporting
delivered by the PRO-X AUX program.[11]

As shown above, medical isotope production needs including both domestic or export must be
balanced against not only the economics of starting up a medical isotope production facility, but
also against regulatory hurdles that may be in place for the actor, availability (and relative costs)
of target material, consumables, and access to high activity waste processing.

The production modes detail the basics of the decision tree for deciding between the two
hypothetical medical isotope production facilities. As outlined, fission product production
generates more product medical isotopes while having the disadvantage of using a U target,
generating high activity waste, and having more complications at a facility level, while activation
production produces much lower activity waste and does not require additional U for targets, but
produces less product overall.

Once the decision has been made between activation and production, separation and purification
processes can be further defined. For fission product production, this means deciding between acid
and caustic separation methods. Acid separation is a well-known process that provides a
straightforward route to dissolution of HALEU targets with an easy to neutralize solution but
requires acid-resistant equipment and tools, generates more waste volume due to the neutralization
steps, and has the potential to generate hydrogen containing gases. Caustic separation is another
well-known separation method that provides a more selective dissolution of HALEU targets which
generates less waste. Using caustic chemistry is also more complicated and slower due to its
selectivity.

 43

For the production of medical isotopes using the activation production mode, the decision to
recycle the material or not is one that is still being actively perused by academia. Recycling or
enriching natural targets may increase yield per amount of target material but adds complexity and
time to the process. Using natural targets lowers the time requirement and complexity of the
process but produces less material overall from the target before it is sent to waste. Due to the
complex nature of enrichment or recycling of target material, exact economic benefits from doing
so are not clear due to the increase in consumables required and potential increase in irradiation.

6. Conclusions
This report lays out prototypical design considerations for a hypothetical baseline facility for the
production of the medical radioisotopes 131I and 99Mo through neutron activation of non-SNM
TeO2 and MoO3 targets, respectively. The target designs and the processing schemes were take-
offs from methods described in the literature and in prior reporting of the chemistry processes
associated with each target. The throughput of the facility was set based on maintaining parity of
production of 131I with the hypothetical fission product production facility detailed in prior
reporting. The throughput of 60 Ci/wee of 131I resulted in a paired output of 5 Ci/week of 99Mo.

Hot cell groupings were developed based on specific process steps, footprint of processing
equipment, and shielding required for the steps. Sizing of the equipment used for the separations
was based on the throughput of 60 Ci a week of 131I. Using the hot cell and equipment sizes
determined by the material balances detailed in prior reporting, the baseline activation facility size
was determined by including supporting non-rad areas and waste processing areas. The final
footprint of the facility was determined to be roughly 763 square meters.

After the hypothetical facility for the production of medical isotope production via activation was
developed, a detailed comparison of this activation target facility to a previously developed similar
facility using fission targets was made. This comparison focused initially on technical facility-level
components including hot cells and then discussed waste streams produced by the facilities.
Economics of each process was discussed and a generalized statement on economics breakpoints
between using either facility was demonstrated. Finally, a decision tree was presented that might
inform an aspiring medical isotope producer to decide which production mode to use between
fission product production and activation production, and separation methods for each of the
production modes were presented. This decision tree represents a first attempt at a culmination of
reporting for baseline facilities and methodologies presented by the PRO-X AUX program. The
reduced number, size, and shielding for hot cells, and the exclusion of having actinide processing,
actinide waste in the activation target facility shows that clear distinction in intrinsic proliferation-
resistant design of the facility.
