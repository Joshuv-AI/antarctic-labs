EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
GTI Energy develops cost-effective technologies for producing diverse power sources.
Leveraging expertise in gas-to-liquids chemistry and strategic alliances, it recently partnered
with a leading US waste management company to convert tons of municipal landfill into purified
liquid fuel. This advancement, combined with processes customized for the aviation sector,
offers a viable pathway to producing drop-in quality jet fuel from non-recyclable solid waste.
Technoeconomic and life cycle analyses show this solution is on track to compete favorably with
industry benchmarks.
Non-recyclable municipal solid waste is a blend of materials such as plastics, fiber, rubber, glass,
and heavily soiled items. The project team that transformed this underutilized matter into a new
energy source undertook a research program with challenging targets set forth by the US
Department of Energy. The Idaho National Laboratory and the Georgia Institute of Technology
prepared and characterized vast amounts of waste material while nanoRanch/UHV Technologies
developed a novel artificial intelligence sorting algorithm, and GTI Energy designed and built a
customized kickdown pump for its U-GAS® conversion technology.
Results surpassed expectations, with a decontamination efficiency of 90% using an AI sorting
tool and a feedstock purity of 96% with >50% variation reduction. Additionally, the kickdown
pump, a newly developed and key component of this technology, successfully injected the
gasifier at the target pressure of 150 psig, advancing both tools to Technology Readiness Level
4, as determined by the US Department of Energy.
Technoeconomic analysis shows fuel production costs are about 9% lower with the kickdown
pump than with a conventional lock hopper feed system, with greater savings likely at scale. Life
cycle analysis finds a 7–9% reduction in carbon intensity compared to traditional jet fuel. Further
consideration of advanced carbon capture and sequestration reveals an improvement ranging
from 125% to 133%, resulting in net-negative emissions.
Following this successful proof-of-concept testing and laboratory validation, efforts now focus
on progressing the AI sorting tool and kickdown pump to Technology Readiness Level 6 through
pilot testing. The International Air Transport Association projected a $236 billion global fuel bill
for airlines in 2025, underscoring the potential of waste-to-jet fuel technology as a cost-efficient
energy solution. With patents pending for complementary processes in aviation fuel production
using other feedstocks, GTI Energy is positioning itself as a leading entity in this specialized field.

INTRODUCTION
Solutions which produce sustainable fuel and achieve economies of scale, such as innovative
gas-to-liquids systems, are vital to today’s national and global interests. GTI Energy has been at
the forefront of inventing such processes. Its proprietary technologies convert both organic and
non-organic material into synthetic liquid fuel in ways that reduce capital costs and are scaled
for competitive markets. The research performed and described within this report focuses on
overcoming challenges associated with the conversion of large quantities of non-recyclable
waste for the production of drop-in quality sustainable aviation fuel.

Background
In 2011, the Oak Ridge National Lab concluded that the United States has the potential to
generate over one billion tons of sustainable biomass annually and serve as a source for
renewable transportation fuels, chemicals, and power for the grid (1). It also suggested that
utilizing waste streams could increase this capacity further once issues of feedstock supply,
quality and processing were addressed.
Consequently, the US Department of Energy (DOE) invested in high-impact research and
development (R&D) aimed at producing energy from waste biogas, carbon dioxide, and plastics
- endeavors often deemed too risky by the private sector. These initiatives specifically targeted
the establishment of scientific and engineering collaborations capable of demonstrating high-
performing drop-in biofuels and other renewable chemicals at a cost of $3 per gasoline gallon
equivalent (GGE), with the additional objective of attaining a modeled mature biofuel minimum
fuel selling price below $2.50 per GGE.
A primary area of focus involved supporting the advancement of sophisticated sorting and
preprocessing methods for non-recyclable municipal solid waste (nMSW). These processes
would address material heterogeneity and variability and enable the production of high-purity
feedstocks. Within this framework, integration with U-GAS® conversion technology - a
commercially validated solution within the GTI Energy portfolio - was emphasized as critical for
facilitating large-scale synthetic fuels production.
The single-stage, fluidized-bed gasification process underlying U-GAS® operates with high
efficiency and low emissions, converting diverse feedstocks into low-to-medium heating value
Synthetic gas (syngas). This syngas serves as a foundational product for generating electricity,
hydrogen, renewable natural gas, transportation biofuels (gaseous and liquid), and chemicals.
Notably, this advanced gasification technology has been evaluated using more than 25 distinct
feedstocks, including peat, pulp mill residues, and various waste materials.
Specific elements critical to advancing this initiative included integration of an artificial
intelligence (AI) sorting tool and implementation of a solids pump designed to provide efficient
and dependable feeding technology for preprocessed nMSW. Such innovations would lay the
foundation for the next steps towards commercialization.

Project objectives
The primary goal of this project was to develop technology for converting nMSW into liquid
aviation fuel with a focus on nMSW preprocessing, the steps to decontaminate and size the
feedstock. The project aimed to establish cost-effective methods to turn nMSW into feedstock
with 1) chemical and mechanical properties suitable for thermal oxidation in the U-GAS®
gasifier, and 2) mechanical properties compatible with injection at the U-GAS® feed pressure of
150 psig using an adapted kickdown pump.
Requirements included processing nMSW to a minimum of 95% purity with a 50% reduction in
variation, achieving at least 80% decontamination efficiency, and maintaining preprocessing
costs below $30 per dry ton.
To achieve these objectives, the project was structured into three distinct task structures:
 1. Review and approve the project plan and technical baseline.
 2. Determine the mechanical and chemical material processing needed to meet conversion
 standards. Integrate an AI-based sorting approach to improve fractionation and
 decontamination. Evaluate pump components and analyze the effects on the injection
 system to assess the suitability of material fractions and blend designs. Develop a
 complete preprocessing system for use in subsequent technoeconomic and life cycle
 assessments.
 3. Validate and implement a preprocessing system. This included validating a sensor-based
 AI sorting algorithm and processing various nMSW samples into feedstock for
 laboratory-scale testing with a kickdown pump. The pump is intended to demonstrate
 injection at commercially relevant pressures for gasification. Data on preprocessing,
 pump performance, and conversion costs will inform the technoeconomic and life cycle
 analyses.

METHODS
The sections below describe the methodology adopted to preprocess nMSW and validate its
suitability for conversion to jet fuel. The approach included staged size reduction, AI-assisted
sorting to remove contaminants, physical and chemical characterization, and development of a
pressurized feeding system, followed by technoeconomic and life cycle assessments to evaluate
process feasibility and sustainability.

Technical approach
This section describes the processes used to preprocess nMSW and enable its conversion to jet
fuel. It includes staged size reduction, AI-assisted contaminant removal, and the development of
a novel kickdown pump. The approach also provided the basis for technoeconomic analysis
(TEA) and life cycle analysis (LCA) to evaluate cost competitiveness and sustainability.

Secondary size reduction was performed using 2, 4, and 6 mm heads (Crumbler® Rotary Shear
by Forest Concepts) respectively (Figure 5). A magnetic separator was installed above the
conveyor to remove residual ferrous contaminants. The feed rate was maintained at a slow
speed to avoid clogging in the rotors. The detailed process is described below: the <3” sample
was fed manually on a screw conveyer to 6 mm rotor set in a shredder. The sample between 6
mm and 3/32” screen (2.38 mm) screens was considered as 6 mm on-spec sample, while the
sample smaller than 2.38 mm was considered as fines (reject) from this process.
Subsequently, a portion of the 6 mm on-spec sample was fed manually on a screw conveyer and
reduced in size through the 4 mm rotor set, while the sample between 4 mm and 3/32” screens
was considered as 4 mm on-spec sample, and the sample under 2.38 mm was considered as
fines. Similarly, another portion of the 6 mm on-spec sample was also reduced in size through 2
mm crumble, while the sample between 2 mm and 20 mesh (0.85 mm) screens was considered
as 2 mm on-spec sample and 20 mesh was considered as fines. Oversized particles in above
steps were recirculated through the crumbles system to improve yield. As the samples were
processed, we randomly selected samples from each on spec and fine fractions for further
chemical and physical analyses.

• 6 mm fines: preprocessed nMSW with particle sizes smaller than 2.38 mm, obtained after

• 4 mm on-spec samples: material obtained by further processing the 6 mm on-spec samples
 using the 4 mm head, with particle sizes between 6 mm and 2.38 mm.
• 4 mm fines: material obtained by further processing the 6 mm on-spec samples using the 4

• 2 mm on-spec samples: material obtained by further processing the 6 mm on-spec samples

Characterize nMSW fractions
Characterization of nMSW fractions was conducted using first-pass and second-pass samples to
determine their physical, chemical, and flow properties relevant to thermochemical conversion
using the first-pass samples. The characterization included analyses of particle size distribution,
bulk density, and moisture content; chemical composition through proximate, ultimate, and
heating value determinations; and consolidation and permeability behavior under simulated
operating conditions.

Physical properties
The physical properties of the samples were determined through three main procedures:
• Particle size distribution (PSD) was analyzed following the ASAE standard method S319.3.
 After the sample was sieved for 10 minutes, the sieves were weighed to calculate the
 cumulative particle passing distributions.
• Bulk densities (loose and tapped) were measured using a modified ASAE standard method
 S269.4. The sample was poured into a cylindrical container, and the loose bulk density was
 calculated based on the mass and volume. Tapped bulk density was measured after
 dropping the container five times from a set height, then recalculating the density.

 Page 12

• Moisture content was determined by oven-drying the sample at 105°C for 24 hours.

Chemistry
Chemical characterization of nMSW fractions was conducted to evaluate their suitability for
thermochemical conversion and to support predictive modeling of feedstock behavior. Samples
were cryogenically ground to 2 mm using a Wiley mill with dry ice to prevent thermal
degradation and volatilization of organics. The ground material was dried at 40 °C, then split
using a riffle splitter to ensure homogeneity.
Proximate analysis was carried out in a LECO Thermogravimetric Analyzer 701 (St. Joseph,
Michigan) as per ASTM D 7582. Briefly, the sample was heated from room temperature to 107 °C
at 6 °C/min heating ramp and held until a constant mass was reached. The mass loss during this
time was considered as moisture content. The sample was further heated up to 950 °C at a ramp
of 50 °C/min and kept isothermal for 9 minutes. During these steps, an inert atmosphere was
maintained by flowing ultra-high purity (UHP) grade nitrogen at 10 L/min. The mass loss during
this time was considered as volatile matter (VM). The sample was then cooled down to 600 °C
and introduced oxygen (3.5 L/min) instead of nitrogen to allow the sample to be combusted.
The temperature was ramped to 750 °C at 13 °C/min and held until a constant mass was
reached for the sample. This remaining mass was considered as ash while the fixed carbon was
determined by subtracting volatile matter and ash percentages from 100%.
Ultimate analysis was carried out in an Elementar Vario EL cube (Ronkonhoma, New York) as per
American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) standard method D 3176-09. The analyzer
was calibrated with a certified Alfalfa standard. During a typical analysis, sample was combusted
at 1150 °C in presence of UHP oxygen at a flowrate of 38 mL/min along with a carrier gas (UHP
helium) at 230 mL/min. The combusted gas was analyzed using a thermal conductivity detector
to determine the elemental carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen. Oxygen content was calculated by
the difference method which is a common method.
Higher heating value (HHV) was determined as per ASTM method D 5865 using a LECO AC600
(St. Joseph, Michigan) isoperibolic system. In short, samples were combusted under 450 psi UHP
grade oxygen and all the calculations were conducted based on the methods stated in D 5865.
Each analysis was conducted in triplicate to ensure statistical robustness. The data were used to
assess compositional variability across fractions and to inform blending strategies for feedstock
optimization. These measurements also supported validation of AI-based sorting algorithms and
numerical models developed by project partners.

Permeability
The consolidation and permeability of particulated nMSW are essential to feedstock handling.
The way that nMSW particles pack together affects the jamming of these materials in various
feedstock handling equipment (such as hoppers and screw feeders), as well as heat transfer,
reaction uniformity, and overall efficiency during waste-to-energy conversion through
gasification, pyrolysis, or incineration. Permeability controls how gases (air, oxygen, steam,

 Page 13

syngas) flow through the waste bed. Low permeability can cause channeling, incomplete
combustion/gasification, or hot spots. Particularly for this project, consolidation and
permeability in particulated nMSW feedstock govern the solid pump design to ensure
continuous hopper flow and gas pressure induced feedstock densification in the solid pump (see
Appendix B for Hopper Flow simulation and validation).
After the customized permeability cell was assembled, tested nMSW materials were gradually
poured into the permeability cell (i.e., air pluviation) up to 45.72 cm (18 in.) in height. The total
mass of the sample was recorded to determine the bulk density and porosity of the tested
samples. Then the specimen was mechanically loaded with a constant strain rate of 2.54 cm/min
(1 in./min). At each desired height, gas permeability through the compressed specimen was
measured. This loading-permeability measurement process is repeated until a maximum loading
stress of 5.5 MPa is achieved. The maximum loading stress is chosen to encompass a broad
spectrum of stress conditions encountered in multiple industry feedstock handling equipment.
For gas permeability measurement, a pressure difference between the inlet and the outlet was
created, and meanwhile, the gas flowmeter monitored the flux through the tested specimens.

Adapt kickdown pump
To enable continuous, pressurized feeding of preprocessed nMSW into a pressurized gasification
system (such as GTI Energy U-GAS®), a kickdown pump concept – adapted in place of the
originally proposed dry solids pump due to cost constraints – was developed and tested. This
technology was designed to overcome the limitations of conventional lock-hopper systems by
providing a rapid, reliable, and cost-effective method for feedstock pressurization and delivery.
The following section describes the design, operation, and advantages of the kickdown pump.
The kickdown pump technology utilizes a unique two-stage ram assembly (Figure 6). The two-
stage ram consists of two rams that are nested, one inside the other. By filling the interior
volume of the primary cylinder with a pressurized medium, the primary ram can move itself and
the secondary ram forward to a first position. The space inside the primary ram can then be
energized (i.e. pressurized) and the secondary ram can then be moved to a second position. It
should be noted that the figure shows a system where hydraulic or pneumatic pressure is used
to actuate the rams, but the assembly could also be actuated via mechanical or even electrical
means, if desired.

 Page 14

Figure 6: Two-stage telescoping ram

The second-stage action of the telescoping ram then “kicks” the feedstock through the flapper
valve, after the feedstock has been conditioned and made suitable for introduction to the
pressurized feed hopper of the process.
One major advantage of the kickdown pump technology is that there are no large vessels
associated with this concept, nor are any large valves (with slow cycling times) required in order
to make it work. Since a vacuum can be drawn on the feedstock in the conditioning chamber in
the preferred embodiment of the technology in order to remove air and/or oxygen from the
feedstock before it is sent into the process, no compressed inert gas is required (though purging
with some type of inert gas may be preferred for certain applications). This will reduce the
CAPEX dramatically.
In addition, the receiving chamber can be brought up to near-process pressure with the same
gas (i.e. H2 or CO2) that comprises the process atmosphere. Thus, no additional flow of any gas
may be needed in order to operate the kickdown pump since the pump can potentially be used
to introduce some or all of the process gas that would be needed in order to operate the
process anyway (this is particularly true in the case of the GTI Energy IH2® process which requires
that a stream of make-up hydrogen be supplied to the first-stage reactor in order to carry out
the relevant process chemistry).
The components of the kickdown pump, including the parts of the two-stage ram, are
mechanically as simple as they can possibly be, meaning that minimal cost is associated with the
design and construction of these components.
As shown in Figure 7, a lab-scale kickdown pump was designed, built and tested through a GTI
Energy funded project, where repeated test cycles were carried out without pressure in the
receiving chamber on a manual operation basis. The kickdown pump integrated a series of
nested pistons, a live seal, a flapper valve and a pressurized process chamber. The loading

Develop and validate AI sorting algorithm
An AI sorting algorithm was developed and validated to enable accurate identification and
separation of plastic fractions derived from nMSW. This effort aimed to improve feedstock
quality for thermochemical conversion by removing chlorine-bearing and other contaminant
materials prior to gasification. The work was conducted in two phases – algorithm development
and system validation – progressing from controlled laboratory datasets to demonstrations
using representative mixed plastic streams under controlled conditions. The following sections
describe the development of machine learning models, feature optimization, and multi-sensor
integration that collectively advanced the AI sorting capability.

Process description
The nMSW delivered to the plant from a MRF facility enters a plastics decontamination section
where it is sorted by AI-assisted sorting equipment. Plastics such as polyvinyl chloride (PVC) that
are highly valuable and also unsuitable for gasification are removed. The sorted feedstock is
then processed in a solid feed preprocessing unit to remove non-convertible materials, such as
dirt, sand, glass, metals, etc., which is returned to landfill. The preprocessed material is then
crushed and densified into briquets before being fed into the gasifier through two kickdown
pumps working in parallel in Case 1 (or a traditional lock hopper feed system in Case 2).
The low bulk density of the nMSW (~80 – 90 kg/m3) is neither economical nor practical for
kickdown pumps or lock hopper feed system. Low bulk density materials tend to bridge in lock
hoppers which makes them less suitable for industrial processes; they also require bulky lock
hoppers or more kickdown pumps for same gravimetric throughput. The project hence
recommends and implements a densification step before feeding the nMSW to the kickdown
pumps (or lock hopper feed system). Commercially available densification process such as
biomass briquetting can densify the nMSW to a bulk density larger than 500 kg/m3, which is

 Page 28

used in the nMSW gravimetric throughput estimate. Accordingly, to deliver 1,000 tons per day
(TPD) of densified nMSW, a kickdown pump with a volumetric capacity of approximately 1,820
m³/day is required, or alternatively, two pumps rated at 910 m³/day each. To ensure sufficient
capacity and operational flexibility, the project team conservatively assumed a total volumetric
capacity of 2,000 m³/day, achieved using two kickdown pumps rated at 1,000 m³/day each, for
cost estimation and design extrapolation.
The nMSW is gasified in a U-GAS® gasifier using oxygen as oxidant and superheated steam as
the fluidization medium. The solid feed rate to the U-GAS® gasifier is 1,000 TPD. The
gasification occurs at 145 – 150 psig (10 barg) and 1,562 °F (850 °C). The syngas existing the
gasifier still contains some light hydrocarbons and higher hydrocarbons (tars), and hence, a
partial oxidation (POX) reactor is used to convert them to CO and H2. In the proposed process,
an R-GASTM style POX reactor is incorporated due to its robust design, compactness, high mixing
efficiency, lower CAPEX and OPEX, where the syngas produced by the U-GAS® gasifier is fed to
the top part of the POX reactor together with the required oxygen flow. Immediately
downstream of the POX reactor, a syngas cooler (which is mechanically integrated to the POX) is
utilized to recover the heat from the hot syngas while generating superheated steam. Portion of
the steam is sent back to the U-GAS® gasifier; another portion of steam is added to syngas
before the Water Gas Shift (WGS) reactor; the remaining steam is used to drive a turbine to
generate power. The ash exits the gasifier at its bottom and is then cooled and collected for
disposal.
The syngas is then filtered by a candle filter and passes through a syngas scrubber to remove
chlorides and carryover particulates. The syngas ratio (H2/CO) out of the scrubber is
approximately 1.3, which is insufficient for Fischer-Tropsch (FT) synthesis. Therefore, part of the
steam generated by the syngas cooler is added to adjust the H2O/CO ratio to optimize the water
gas shift reaction. The syngas, after adding steam, is passed through a Water Gas Shift unit to
shift part of CO to H2 so that the shifted syngas has a H2/CO ratio slightly above 2.
The resulting syngas contains approximately 27 vol% CO₂, which must be removed prior to
Fischer–Tropsch (FT) synthesis, as elevated CO₂ concentrations in the FT feed can adversely
affect hydrocarbon product selectivity. CO₂ removal is carried out in a conventional Acid Gas
Removal (AGR) unit. In Case 1, a portion of the separated CO₂ stream is recycled to the feed
system as transport gas, which enters the gasifier together with the solid feedstock, while the
remaining portion is routed to CO₂ sequestration. In Case 2, which employs a traditional lock-
hopper feed system, an additional portion of the captured CO₂ is utilized for lock-hopper
pressurization and depressurization operations. This step is unnecessary in Case 1, which uses a
kickdown pump for direct pressurized feeding. Consequently, Case 1 results in a greater amount
of CO₂ being sequestered than Case 2.
The syngas is then passed through a Sulfur Removal Unit, which consists of LO-CAT and ZnO
beds. Though sulfur is not considered in the composition, the average nMSW composition has
about 0.34% sulfur content (3,400 ppmw) (5). The LO-CAT is a liquid redox system that uses a
chelated iron system to reduce H2S to elemental sulfur. The sulfur content reduces to 10-20

 Page 29

ppm. ZnO beds reduce the sulfur content to <1 ppmv, which is a requirement for the
downstream Fischer-Tropsch catalysts.
The syngas, which now contains 62% H2, 31% CO, and 4% CO2, is then compressed to 435 psig
(30 barg) and fed to the FT synthesis unit. A single-pass CO conversion of 50% is used for the
Fischer-Tropsch reactor (6). This conversion requires a recycle to make the overall conversion
commercially feasible. A recycle compressor maintains flow in the recycle loop. The recycle ratio
is limited by the amount of CO2 in the FT reactor combined feed stream, which is 10%.

Carbon balance
As part of the LCA for the nMSW-to-jet-fuel pathway, a carbon balance was developed to ensure
the completeness, consistency, and accuracy of the modeled system by accounting for all
carbon inputs, outputs, and transformations within the defined system boundaries. The balance
serves as a mass conservation check to verify that the total carbon entering the system -
primarily from nMSW feedstock - is consistent with the total carbon leaving the system in the
form of jet fuel, gasoline, process emissions, and solid residues. Establishing a well-closed
carbon balance enhances data integrity, helps identify missing or inconsistent streams (e.g.,
untracked CO2 or carbon in reject fractions), distinguishes between biogenic and fossil-derived
carbon, and ensures all relevant process steps - from waste preprocessing and gasification
through Fischer-Tropsch synthesis and product upgrading - are accurately represented.
Ultimately, maintaining a closed carbon balance strengthens the credibility of the greenhouse

gas (GHG) results and increases confidence in the calculated carbon intensity of the nMSW-
derived jet fuel.

Following approval from the DOE, the operation of the kickdown pump was automated by

The original operation of the kickdown pump includes a series of manual switches to
energize/deenergize solenoid valves for each pneumatic step of the process. To implement a
system that could automate most of the procedural steps, a data acquisition cart and series of
controllable solenoid valves were introduced to the system. An Allen Bradley PLC was added to
control the solenoid valves to enable the automated operation of the kickdown pump.

Determined composition of as-received nMSW
Fractionation of representative primary size-reduced samples revealed the heterogeneous
nature of the as-received nMSW stream (Figure 26). The material was manually sorted into major
categories: plastics, paper, woody biomass, metals, foam/cotton/napkins, unsorted residuals,
and dirt/glass. Plastics constituted a significant portion of the mass, ranging from 7.6% to 39.3%
depending on the sample location (top, middle, or bottom of the super sacks where the material
was stored). Paper was the most abundant component, with values between 14.1% and 51.5%,
reflecting the high prevalence of packaging and printed materials in residential waste streams.
Woody materials and metals were present in smaller quantities, typically below 3%, while foam,
textiles, and napkins accounted for less than 5%. Notably, the unsorted and dirt/glass fractions
were substantial in some samples, reaching up to 40%, particularly in lower layers of the sacks,
indicating stratification during storage and handling.
“Unsorted” is listed as a separate category because the particles were too small to be manually
separated into distinct fractions. This category primarily consists of thin plastics, rigid plastics,
paper, cardboard, and foam/fabric. The reported average and standard deviation represent 15
measurements, providing a quantitative representation of variability within the stream.

Characterized chemistry of nMSW fractions
The proximate analysis, higher heating values (HHV), and ultimate analysis of the as-received
nMSW and its preprocessed fractions are presented in Table 10 and Table 11, respectively. Note:
each of the analyses were conducted multiple times for each sample to minimize the error limit.
The as-received nMSW exhibited high variability in composition, characteristic of heterogeneous
municipal solid waste. On average, it contained 73.47 wt% volatile matter, 5.65 wt% fixed
carbon, and 20.87 wt% ash, with an HHV of 21.47 ± 2.87 MJ/kg. The ultimate analysis showed
51.16 wt% carbon, 6.28 wt% hydrogen, 0.44 wt% nitrogen, 0.11 wt% sulfur, and 21.14 wt%
oxygen (all on a dry basis). Despite the elevated ash levels, the relatively high HHV indicates that
a substantial portion of the material possesses considerable energy potential, which can be
effectively recovered through proper fractionation and decontamination.
The large standard deviations observed in both proximate and ultimate analyses further confirm
the significant compositional variability among samples. This heterogeneity reflects the uneven
distribution of organic and inorganic constituents such as plastics, paper, food residues, dirt, and
glass. Consequently, preprocessing and size reduction are essential steps to improve feedstock
uniformity, reduce ash variability, and enhance the suitability of the material for gasification and
other thermochemical conversion processes.

On the other hand, the on-spec samples generated through successive size reduction using the
6 mm, 4 mm, and 2 mm heads exhibited consistent composition and stable energy content

Achieved decontamination efficiency at 90%
The AI sorting algorithm development phase demonstrated that AI-based classification of
nMSW fractions was feasible. After preprocessing the XRF datasets to remove noise and
selecting twelve key features, several machine learning algorithms were tested. CatBoost
consistently gave the most stable performance, while Random Forest was valuable for feature
importance analysis but showed fluctuations across runs. Other models such as XGBoost, SVM,
and AdaBoost produced mixed results, with some achieving strong accuracy for certain classes
but failing to generalize. These tests confirmed that chlorine could be detected at a limit of
detection of approximately 3% using optimized XRF settings, but they also highlighted that
classical machine learning methods alone could not reach the required sorting efficiency for
real-world applications.
During the AI sorting algorithm development phase, the algorithms were extended and
integrated into multi-sensor platforms at INL and UHV. Near-infrared and optical imaging
datasets were collected, and deep learning approaches were introduced to fully exploit the
complexity of the spectral data. Among the models tested, ResNet architectures (ResNet-18 and
ResNet-50) delivered the strongest performance. Trained on nMSW-specific datasets with data
augmentation, the ResNet models proved to be robust and accurate when deployed on the UHV
“blue plastics sorter.”
As shown in Table 12, controlled trials were conducted with 200 mixed nMSW pieces, consisting
of 20 PVC and 180 non-PVC items. The system successfully removed 18 PVC pieces and
mistakenly removed 3 non-PVC pieces, leaving 2 PVC items undetected. This corresponds to a
decontamination efficiency of 90% (surpassing the milestone target of ≥80%) and a stream
purity of 99%, indicating that the AI sorting process was highly effective at separating PVC from
non-PVC materials with minimal contamination. These results were further confirmed through
demonstrations on the INL sorter, where PVC was reliably separated from PET, HDPE, and PP
bottles using real-time inference.

Demonstrated injection at target U-GAS® pressure
The project team conducted a series of tests to demonstrate the ability of the kickdown pump
to inject solid feedstocks, including wood chips, processed nMSW, and pulverized powder, into a
receiving chamber pressurized at 150 psig, representative of commercial scale U-GAS® gasifier
operating conditions.
The project successfully adapted, automated, and demonstrated a laboratory-scale kickdown
pump prototype, advancing the technology from TRL 2 to TRL 4. Testing confirmed the ability to
inject diverse solid feedstocks, including wood chips, preprocessed nMSW, and fine powders,
into a chamber pressurized at 150 psig through repeated automated cycles. The system
achieved cycle times of 12–15 seconds, representing more than a 60-fold improvement over

 Page 56

conventional lock hopper trains, while maintaining feedstock integrity and reliable flap valve
reseating.

Wood chip testing
Prior to testing with the nMSW, commercially available Marth material was used to commission
the kickdown pump, which is a homogeneous, woody biomass feedstock that is commonly
found in industry. The average particle size of this material size is smaller than the nMSW, less
than 4mm particle size. No additional processing was conducted on this material prior to
kickdown pump testing. The purpose of this test is to calibrate pressures and troubleshooting
any issues with the kickdown pump hardware and control logic.
Kickdown pump operating procedures described above are followed during the testing and no
issues found and we successfully demonstrated the injection of the wood chips into the
receiving chamber pressurized at 150 psig during February 2025 with multiple testing cycles.
Strong “kick” was observed during the testing. No changes to its physical form were observed
after the kickdown pump testing, the optimal outcome for processing using the kickdown pump.

nMSW testing
Upon receiving the second-pass nMSW feedstock from INL (average particle size ~4 mm), the
material was conditioned to reduce the risk of damage to the aluminum kickdown pump from
severe erosion. Such damage typically results from fine, high-hardness particles – such as metal
and glass – scoring internal surfaces or entering sensitive areas of the pump assembly.
The conditioning process included: 1) manual inspection to remove glass or other hard objects –
materials exceeding 0.25 inches were rejected; 2) passing a magnet across small batches to
remove ferrous metals; 3) processing through a 3D-printed high-density separator to separate
problematic high-density materials from less dense nMSW; and 4) removing top layers and
loading them into corresponding sieves for 10 minutes using a Ro-Tap testing sieve shaker.
Material collected from sieves 0.223 through 0.066 inches was mixed and placed into baggies at
approximately 18 grams per bag. This weight was determined by testing different loading sizes
in the kickdown pump – 18 grams produced the best results.
A test conducted in April 2025 successfully completed 22 consecutive semi-automated cycles,
where the GTI conditioned nMSW was successfully injected into the receiving chamber
pressurized at 150 psig. The only manual operation was the operator loading the materials from
the bags into the kickdown pump; all other steps were controlled by the PLC controller. Testing
ended when the pressurized air cylinder – a 300-liter compressed air cylinder with a starting
pressure of 1500 psig, used to drive the pistons – dropped below 400 psig. The run results were
recorded in Table 17.

Fine powder testing
To broaden the application of the kickdown pump, the project team conducted pump testing
with pulverized torrefied forest residual (D50 ~150 microns) since fine powder material is
essential for entrained flow gasifiers such as R-GASTM gasifier. Similarly to the test with wood
chips and nMSW, the kickdown pump successfully injected the powder into the receiving
chamber pressurized at 150 psig.

Cycle time improvement
The capacity of the kickdown pump for a known material is reverse proportional to the cycle
time. The project team conducted additional tests to further reduce the cycle time of the
kickdown pump prototype. By optimizing the solenoid valve feedback loop, reducing the scan
time of the PLC software (the time the PLC takes to read inputs, execute the program logic,
update outputs, and perform internal diagnostics) and supplying sufficient pressure to the
prototype, 12-seconds (excluding material loading time) cycle time was demonstrated with

 Page 59

multiple tests conducted without feedstock. The project team considered the tests are enough
to demonstrate the capability of the kickdown pump prototype since there is no significant
difference in cycle time with and without materials observed.

Operating expenses
The operating expenses (OPEX) and costs associated with the plant include the following:
• Administrative and operating labor
• Maintenance – material and labor
• Consumables
• Fuel/feedstock
• Taxes and insurance
The operating costs of the plant are categorized into fixed operating costs and variable
operating costs. Fixed operating costs are those that remain constant regardless of the level of
jet fuel production, such as maintenance labor, supervision, insurance, and administrative
overhead. In contrast, variable operating costs are directly proportional to the quantity of jet fuel
produced and include expenses such as feedstock, utilities, catalysts, and consumables.
Quantities of major consumables – such as feedstock, process water, and utilities – were derived
from the heat and material balance. These data ensure consistency between the process model
and the economic evaluation. Operating labor requirements were estimated based on the
number of operators needed per shift, accounting for plant coverage, supervision, and support
personnel. The corresponding labor costs were determined using standard wage rates and
industry norms applicable to facilities of similar scale and complexity.

Combined, these offsets total approximately $40.77 million annually at 90% plant capacity,
equivalent to $95.55 per dry ton of nMSW. After applying these credits, the net preprocessing
cost becomes –$46.95 per dry ton, effectively turning preprocessing into a revenue-positive
operation and driving the overall system cost well below the DOE target of $30 per ton for
fractionation and decontamination.

Sensitivity analysis
A univariate sensitivity analysis was conducted for the kickdown pump configuration (Case 1) to
examine the influence of individual techno-economic parameters on the overall process
economics and the levelized cost of jet fuel. In this analysis, one variable was varied at a time
while holding all others constant at their base-case values to isolate its effect on fuel cost. The
parameters evaluated included the total plant cost (varied by ±25% of the base estimate),
discount rate (6%, 8%, and 10%), nMSW tipping fee ($65, $45, and $25 per ton), selling price of
the PVC by-product stream ($0.6, $0.4, and $0.2 per pound), plant capacity factor (95%, 90%,
and 85%), number of operational staff in the gasification island (10, 22, and 50 personnel),
maintenance cost (±25% of the base case), selling price of the ash stream from the U-GAS®
process ($15, $7.5, and –$45 per ton), and electricity price ($30, $40, and $50 per MWh). These
ranges were selected to represent reasonable variations in capital cost uncertainty, feedstock
supply agreements, by-product market volatility, and operational flexibility typical for early-
stage waste-to-jet-fuel projects. The results are presented in Figure 32, which illustrates the
relative influence of each variable on the levelized cost of jet fuel.

The primary focus of this LCA is to quantify the net greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions associated
with the nMSW-to-jet-fuel pathway. The analysis identifies relevant emission sources across the

kilogram of fuel as the functional unit. A 100-year time horizon Global Warming Potential (GWP)
is applied to calculate carbon dioxide equivalents (CO₂e) (22), and the gasoline co-product is
managed through system expansion (displacement).

(Rev. 1) (23). Key LCI data used in this study include:
 • GWPs from IPCC AR6, based on a 100-year time horizon.

DISCUSSION
This section provides an analysis of the technoeconomic and life cycle results for converting
nMSW to jet fuel via the U-GAS® pathway. It examines cost performance relative to DOE
targets, identifies key cost drivers, and evaluates GHG reduction potential under different
scenarios. The discussion also outlines strategies to reduce costs, optimize processes, and
increase revenue, forming the basis for recommendations to improve economic viability and
accelerate commercialization. A concluding summary is provided at the end of this section to
highlight key findings and future work.

Technoeconomic analysis
nMSW preprocessing cost
The current preprocessing cost for nMSW, excluding revenue offsets, is $48.60 per dry ton,
which exceeds the DOE target of $30 per ton. This cost comprises $27.93 per ton for plastics
decontamination and $20.67 per ton for solid feed preprocessing. When tipping fees and PVC
co-product sales are included, the economics shift dramatically, resulting in a net cost of –$46.95
per dry ton, effectively making preprocessing revenue-positive. However, if these offsets are
removed, significant cost reductions are required to meet DOE objectives. Analysis of cost
components reveals that labor dominates plastics decontamination, accounting for nearly 58%
of its annualized cost, while energy consumption is the largest contributor in solid feed
preprocessing, representing about 44% of its cost. Repairs, supplies, and maintenance also add
substantial overhead, and capital costs remain significant despite amortization.
Levelized cost of jet fuel
The TEA demonstrates that converting nMSW to jet fuel via the U-GAS® gasification pathway is
both technically feasible and economically promising, particularly when using the kickdown
pump feeding configuration. Compared to the lock hopper system, the kickdown pump design
reduces total plant cost by approximately 6%, lowers the total as-spent cost from $734 million
to $689 million, and decreases the levelized cost of jet fuel from $5.03 to $4.60 per GGE - a 9%
reduction. These cost advantages stem from system simplification, reduced mechanical
complexity, and lower installation and auxiliary equipment costs. Sensitivity analysis identified
total plant cost, discount rate, tipping fee, and PVC by-product price as the most influential
variables affecting fuel economics, emphasizing the importance of capital efficiency, stable
feedstock agreements, and by-product market optimization. While uncertainties remain due to
the inclusion of lower TRL subsystems, the results highlight clear opportunities to enhance
economic performance through larger-scale deployment, improved feedstock flexibility,
favorable financing, and optimized by-product utilization.

Life cycle analysis
Overall, the LCA results demonstrate that converting nMSW to jet fuel via the U-GAS® pathway
can achieve significant greenhouse gas (GHG) reductions compared to conventional petroleum-

 Page 78

based jet fuel. Without CCS, the process already provides a 7–9% improvement in carbon
intensity due primarily to avoided landfill emissions and biogenic carbon credits. When CCS is
incorporated, the pathway achieves net-negative emissions, improving the carbon intensity by
125–133% relative to the baseline. The combination of waste diversion benefits, utilization of
biogenic carbon, and carbon capture makes the nMSW-to-jet-fuel system a promising low-
carbon alternative to fossil-based jet fuel production.

Recommendations
nMSW preprocessing cost
To reduce costs without relying on revenue credits, the following strategies are recommended.
Automation of sorting and material handling can substantially lower labor expenses, which are
the single largest cost driver. Energy optimization through efficient grinders, briquetters, and
heat integration using waste heat from syngas cooling can reduce power costs. Predictive
maintenance programs will minimize repair and upkeep costs, while modular equipment design
can control capital investment and allow incremental scaling. Further savings can be achieved by
integrating drying and densification steps and co-locating preprocessing facilities near waste
sources to reduce handling and logistics costs. Implementing these measures could reduce
preprocessing costs by 40–50 percent, bringing them closer to the DOE $30 per ton target even
without revenue offsets. This improvement strengthens the economic viability of nMSW-to-jet-
fuel conversion and supports the pathway toward commercial deployment.
Levelized cost of jet fuel
The sensitivity analysis identifies several key variables that can be optimized to enhance overall
project economics and move toward the target levelized cost of jet fuel (LCOJ) of $2.5 per GGE.
These variables represent both technical and strategic opportunities for cost reduction, process
efficiency improvement, and revenue enhancement. The following recommendations summarize
potential pathways to achieve the target LCOJ.
 • Plant scale and location: Locating the facility in a region with an abundant and
 consistent nMSW supply would enable construction of a larger-scale plant, improve
 economies of scale and reduce the unit fuel cost. For example, Los Angeles County
 generated approximately 11 million tons of landfill waste in 2022, equivalent to roughly
 30,000 tons per day, which could readily support a commercial-scale facility.
 • Feedstock flexibility: Blending nMSW with other low-cost carbonaceous feedstocks,
 such as coal or biomass residues, could stabilize feedstock composition and enhance
 process throughput, leading to improved gasifier utilization and lower overall production
 cost. However, each type of feedstock carries its own greenhouse gas emissions, energy
 inputs, and waste outputs, and hence a separate evaluation is needed for these cases to
 assess the sustainability and economic viability.
 • Tipping fee structure: Selecting a project site with favorable tipping fees or negotiating
 long-term waste supply contracts with municipal or private partners can significantly

 Page 79

 improve project cash flow. Higher tipping fees directly translate into greater revenue for
 nMSW-based processes.
 • Financing terms: Securing a long-term, low-interest loan or other favorable financing
 mechanisms would reduce annual debt service requirements, thereby lowering the
 levelized fuel cost and improving the internal rate of return of the project.
 • By-product revenue optimization: Enhancing the purity and market value of the sorted
 plastic (PVC) stream through improved pre-processing and sorting technologies can
 yield additional revenue streams. Refining or pelletizing the plastic fraction for resale to
 recycling or chemical markets could further offset jet fuel production costs.
To achieve the target levelized cost of $2.50 per GGE, several pathways for improvement are
recommended. Scaling the plant in regions with abundant nMSW supply can enhance
economies of scale and significantly lower unit fuel cost. Securing higher and more stable
tipping fees or long-term waste supply agreements can improve project revenue stability.
Enhancing feedstock flexibility by co-feeding nMSW with other low-cost carbonaceous materials
such as coal or biomass residues could improve gasifier utilization and reduce cost, though
further evaluation of their life-cycle impacts is necessary. Accessing favorable financing terms -
such as low-interest loans or public-private partnerships - would lower capital recovery
requirements. Finally, increasing the purity and market value of the PVC co-product through
improved sorting and processing could further offset fuel costs. Collectively, these measures
would move the process closer to the target fuel cost while improving the economic resilience
and long-term sustainability of the nMSW-to-jet-fuel pathway.
Future work should focus on these areas to further enhance the financial viability and long-term
sustainability of nMSW based jet fuel production projects. Continued research and development
are needed to refine process integration, improve feedstock pre-processing, and optimize by-
product utilization to maximize overall value recovery. In parallel, efforts to establish favorable
policy mechanisms, secure low-cost financing, and develop stable markets for waste-derived
fuels and co-products will be essential to enable commercial deployment. Collectively, these
advancements will help reduce economic risk, attract investment, and accelerate the transition of
waste-to-jet-fuel technologies from pilot to commercial scale.

Conclusions
This project demonstrates that nMSW can be successfully preprocessed, decontaminated, and
converted into a high-quality feedstock suitable for U-GAS® gasification, enabling production
of drop-in jet fuel with strong economic and environmental performance. The integrated system
combining advanced AI-enabled sorting, staged size-reduction, densification, and a novel
kickdown pump feeding mechanism consistently met or exceeded DOE targets for purity,
variability reduction, PVC removal, and feed system performance. Technoeconomic analysis
shows that the system provides a clear pathway to further reduce production costs toward the
long-term target of $2.50 per GGE, with additional opportunities to improve both CAPEX and
OPEX at commercial scale. Life cycle analysis shows net-negative well-to-wheel emissions when

 Page 80

paired with carbon capture and sequestration, highlighting the substantial decarbonization
potential of nMSW-derived jet fuel.
The benefits of this integrated approach are most pronounced when the system leverages its full
suite of innovations – from high-efficiency AI decontamination to pressure-stable densified
feedstock and rapid-cycle pump injection – which together overcome long-standing challenges
associated with heterogeneous solid waste conversion. By achieving TRL 4 for both the AI
sorting tool and the kickdown pump, the project establishes a strong technical foundation for
pilot-scale TRL 6 demonstrations. With growing demand for sustainable aviation fuel and
increasing pressure to divert waste from landfills, the validated nMSW-to-jet-fuel pathway
provides a viable, scalable, and cost-competitive route for decarbonizing the aviation sector
while addressing waste management challenges. Continued development will allow stakeholders
to optimize cost, improve reliability, and accelerate commercialization of this emerging waste-
to-jet-fuel technology.

APPENDIX B: SIMULATE AND VALIDATE HOPPER FLOW
Efficient and reliable feeding of nMSW into pressurized gasification systems requires a clear
understanding of bulk solid flow behavior under various operating conditions. To support the

design and optimization of the nMSW feeding system, a comprehensive hopper flow study was
conducted combining physical experiments and numerical modeling. The study aimed to
characterize the flowability of individual nMSW fractions, their blends, and the second-pass
preprocessed feedstock, and to evaluate the influence of particle size, hopper geometry, and
outlet opening size on mass flow rate and jamming tendencies. Continuum-based hypoplastic
constitutive modeling (Gudehus–Bauer model) was employed within a coupled Eulerian–
Lagrangian (CEL) finite-element framework to simulate the flow behavior, with parameters
calibrated from laboratory tests such as oedometer and ring-shear measurements. The
experimental data obtained from dynamic hopper discharge tests provided essential validation
for the numerical simulations and offered insight into the key factors controlling the flow
consistency and feed stability of nMSW-derived materials.
Modeling
The hopper flow simulations use both 2D plane-strain slices of the wedge-shaped hopper and
3D models with geometries identical to that of the hopper used in physical testing (Figure 38).
The simulations use continuum models, such as the hypoplastic Gudehus–Bauer (G-B). To
address the mesh-tangling issue of the Finite Element Method (FEM) when simulating large
deformation, the coupled Eulerian–Lagrangian (CEL) FEM scheme was applied, which was
realized by two steps: a deformation step, where the material deformed with the mesh, followed
by a remapping step, where the deformed mesh was returned to its original position and the
material (deformed) properties were interpolated back to the “fixed” mesh. The inlet and outlet
boundaries are maintained at atmospheric pressure, and the impacts of different wall frictions
are investigated in the simulations.

Determine material parameters for the models
The hypoplastic Gudehus–Bauer model requires the input of eight material parameters, which
can be grouped into three categories: (1) the internal friction angle (ϕc) and exponent (α), which
dominate the shear behavior; (2) the granulate hardness (hs), exponent (n), and parameter (β),

which determine the compression behavior; and (3) the minimum, critical, and maximum void
ratios at zero pressure (ed0, ec0, and ei0), which define the range of the void ratio. These
parameters were calibrated using laboratory experiments and single-element FEM simulations,
with the detailed calibration procedure reported in our earlier publications.
As illustrated in Figure 39, ed0 and ec0 were obtained from the minimum/maximum density tests;
hs and n were derived from oedometer tests; and the ring-shear friction angle (ϕcrs) was
measured from Schulze ring-shear tests. Then, using single-element FEM simulations, n and ei0
were further calibrated against oedometer results, ϕc from the critical-state shear behavior, and
α from the peak-stress behavior observed in the ring-shear tests. Note that the internal friction
angle at the critical state (ϕc) was mapped from the ring-shear friction angle (ϕcrs) by numerically
determining the Lode angle.

Summary
The integrated experimental and modeling investigation successfully captured the complex
hopper discharge behavior of prepressed nMSW feedstocks. Results demonstrated strong
correlations between particle size, bulk density, and flowability, with smaller particle sizes (≤ 2
mm) showing stable mass flow and minimal jamming, while coarser fractions (≥ 6 mm) exhibited
intermittent or complete blockage. The hypoplastic Gudehus–Bauer model, calibrated with
laboratory derived material parameters, reproduced the observed flow trends and provided
predictive capability for evaluating hopper geometry and outlet size effects. These findings
provide a validated framework for designing and scaling up feed systems for heterogeneous
solid waste streams, supporting reliable operation of downstream gasification and conversion
processes.
Future efforts will focus on refining the hypoplastic material model to better represent the multi-
phase and compressible nature of nMSW derived feedstocks, including the effects of particle
shape irregularity, inter-particle cohesion, and moisture content. Additional large-scale
experiments under pressurized conditions will be performed to validate hopper flow at pilot
scale. Integration of real-time flow monitoring and machine-learning-based predictive control
will be explored to enhance feed consistency and prevent jamming events during continuous
operation.

APPENDIX C: AI-SORTER SCALABILITY
Following the successful validation of the AI-based sorting algorithms, a technoeconomic
analysis was conducted to assess the scalability and commercial viability of the system. The

analysis was based on the design and demonstration of a 2-meter wide AI-enabled plastics
decontamination system operating at belt speeds of 400–500 feet per minute. Under these
conditions, a single sorter is capable of processing approximately 20,000 metric tons per year of
nMSW when operated continuously at 24/7 with 80% uptime (Figure 47).
To reach industrial throughput levels, three parallel sorters were modeled, resulting in a
combined capacity of 60,000 metric tons per year. The total CAPEX for this configuration was
estimated at approximately $2.0 million, which included the cost of sorters, installation (vibratory
feeders and loaders), and facility upgrades such as power, water, and compressors. All costs
mentioned here is 2024$.

estimate the cost of the commercial scale kickdown pump. To contend with the throughput of

the current solid fuel handling technology used in the U-GAS® process, our upscaled kickdown
pump(s) must handle a minimum total gravimetric throughput of 1,000 short tons per day (TPD)
of nMSW, which is volumetric throughput multiplied by the bulk density of the feedstock.

commercial scale industrial piston-based pumps can achieve quicker cycle times (as low as a few

that cycle time would increase with bore size due to greater piston inertia, the cycle times of
larger kickdown pumps have been held constant at 15 seconds for the purpose of simplicity in
the preliminary throughput calculations. Loading length, or hopper window length, are held at
1m, but are eventually adjusted to match the specifications of industrial piston pumps gathered
during industry benchmarking stage. Table 30 lists the preliminary volumetric throughput for a

Density considerations/densification
The GTI processed nMSW used in kickdown pump testing has a bulk density of 86.76 kg/m3. By
multiplying volumetric throughput in Table 2 with bulk density, the gravimetric throughput of a
single 22” bore kickdown pump is found to only reach 135 TPD of nMSW. If the loading length
of a 22” bore kickdown pump is matched to that of a 22” bore piston pump found in industry
(92.5”), then gravimetric throughput becomes 316 TPD of nMSW. At this capacity, the U-GAS®
process would require four-multimillion-dollar machines to contend with the process’ current
solids handling technology.
The low bulk density of the nMSW is neither economical nor practical for kickdown pumps or
lock hoppers. Low bulk density materials are prone to bridge in lock hoppers which makes them
not preferable for industrial processes; they also require bulky lock hoppers or more kickdown
pumps for same gravimetric throughput. The project hence recommends a densification step
before feeding the nMSW to the kickdown pumps (as well as lock hoppers). Commercially
available densification process such as biomass briquetting can densify the nMSW to a bulk
density larger than 500 kg/m3, which is used in the nMSW gravimetric throughput estimate.
Therefore, in order to deliver 1,000 TPD densified nMSW, a kickdown pump with volumetric
capacity of 1,820 m3/day or two kickdown pumps with 910 m3/day each. On the safe side, the
project team used 2,000 m3/day or two kickdown pumps with 1,000 m3/day each to extrapolate
the kickdown pump cost.
CAPEX estimate – industry benchmarking
Instead of using a bottom-up estimate approach by designing a commercial scale kickdown
pump and soliciting fabrication quotes, a project team decides to determine the kickdown pump
cost by benchmarking the volumetric throughputs with similar industrial equipment – this
procedure is less time consuming and can produce more accurate estimates.
Due to higher fidelity of similarity, concrete pumps and industrial piston pumps designed to
handle solids and non-Newtonian fluids such as sewage, slurries, and landfill trash are used for
cost estimates. Most of the relevant piston pumps follow the design of a dual piston concrete
pump, which uses two hydraulically driven pistons side by side to continuously deliver material.
 D-2

In this design, the pistons are out of phase so that one is filled while the other pushes material
to the outlet of the machine. Besides this design difference, the price of the entire machine
should match that of a kickdown pump because the hydraulic system, PLC, materials of
construction, machine weight and skid requirements, and piston inertia are expected to match a

kickdown pump of the same bore size. The pistons of the kickdown pump pistons are nested,
but the hydraulic power requirements should be similar to the dual piston design because each
full cycle of the machine includes one full stroke from each piston. The cost of ancillary hydraulic
equipment used to switch the output between each piston in the dual piston design is assumed
to be offset by the cost of different ancillary equipment in the kickdown pump design.
The project team contacted two leaders of the hydraulic piston pump industry and obtained the
prices of a few commercially available standard models. The price versus the max volumetric
throughput of the machine is plotted in Figure 48 below. One datapoint represents the price of
an industrial machine and the calculated throughput of a kickdown pump having the same
delivery volume at a rate of 15s/cycle. Machine price as a function of the volumetric throughput
of the kickdown pump exhibits a linear relationship. In contrast to Table 2 above, the length of
the loading hopper matches the manufacturer specifications (the max volumetric throughput is
calculated from the bore diameter of the machine, the loading length and a cycle time of 15
seconds).

Figure 48: Price of a kickdown pump vs. maximum volumetric throughput

To allow some redundancy, two kickdown pumps, each having an 11.8” bore and 122” long
loading window, are selected. Each such pump can deliver 1,250 m3/day or 625 metric ton per

pumps combined can deliver 1,000 TPD densified nMSW with 30% surplus capacity. Cost of the
two pumps is $1.8 million dollars (2025 cost basis).

Power requirement estimate
Commercial scale piston pumps utilize hydraulic power packs ranging from 5.5 to 1600 kW, but
the hydraulic power pack size ultimately depends on the required output pressure and is
therefore application specific. The applications requiring the greatest hydraulic power are

typically concrete and mining because the pump must continually push material into a pipe that
has significant backpressure. The hydraulic power requirements of the kickdown pump are
expected to be lower than those of concrete pumps because densified nMSW is much lighter
than concrete (around 2,500 kg/m3), and comparatively, back pressure in a kickdown pump is
much less (150 psig required for U-GAS® gasifiers). Figure 49 shows data collected from two
major concrete pump vendors that present a specific application where one of the models of
interest is used. One datapoint represents the power requirement of an industrial machine and
the calculated throughput of a kickdown pump having the same delivery volume at a rate of
15s/cycle. Based on the linear trend, the hydraulic requirement of the configuration that
optimizes capital expenditure, two 11.8” bore size machines, would be less than or equal to 300
kW/machine or 600 kW.

Figure 49: Kickdown pump hydraulic power demand vs. maximum volumetric throughput

Scalability Summary
Following the successful laboratory-scale demonstration of the kickdown pump, the project
team conducted a commercial-scale scale-up and cost evaluation to assess its applicability for
nMSW feed delivery to U-GAS® gasifiers. Throughput modeling indicated that a commercial
system must handle a minimum of 1,000 short tons per day (TPD) of densified nMSW to match
current solid fuel handling performance. Benchmarking against industrial hydraulic piston and
concrete pumps revealed a linear cost correlation with volumetric throughput, leading to an
estimated total capital cost of $1.8 million (2025 $) for two 11.8-inch bore units with a combined
capacity exceeding 1,000 TPD. Power analysis further indicated that hydraulic requirements
would not exceed 300 kW per pump, significantly lower than comparable concrete applications.
 D-4

These results confirm that the kickdown pump technology is technically scalable and
economically competitive with existing solid-feed pressurization systems.
