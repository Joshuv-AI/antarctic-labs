Executive Summary
This report summarizes the performance limits of the LANSCE Coupled-Cavity Linac (CCL). These results
are captured or summarized directly from the references cited. This report was written in support of the
LANSCE Modernization Project (LAMP).
This brief report summarizes the emittance budget for the LANSCE Modernization Project (LAMP).
While the project Key Performance Parameters (KPPs) specify threshold and objective requirements for
charge delivered to each experimental area, no upper limits on beam emittances are specified. To maintain
low losses in the high-energy section of the LANSCE linac and hands-on maintenance, some upper limits
on beam emittance need to be specified for the new LAMP front-end performance.
The scope of the LAMP project replaces the injector section and drift-tube linac (DTL) up to 100 MeV of
the existing LANSCE linac. This new design replacement will be integrated with the remaining coupled
cavity linac (CCL) which makes up most of the accelerator at LANSCE and accelerates the beam to a final
energy of 800 MeV.
The initial approach that has been used to define an emittance budget for the new LAMP front end is based
on recent and historical measured beam emittances at 100 MeV for the three beam types accelerated at
LANSCE: H+ (protons for isotope production), LBEG (H- beam for delivery to proton radiography and to
the Lujan neutron spallation target, and MPEG (H- beam for delivery to the Weapons Neutron Research
facility). The emittance budget (upper limit) for each beam type has been selected to maintain the losses in
the CCL to a level approximately equivalent to those observed in present operations to first order. However,
the goal of the LAMP project is to improve the quality of the beams injected into the CCL, if possible, thus
allowing for higher average current operation while also lowering beam losses and activation at high beam
energies.
The Table 1 below summarizes the beam measurements evaluated and used to establish a conservative
emittance budget for LAMP based on known historical beam losses and activation. However, based on
estimates of the CCL admittance and the Isotope Production Facility (IPF) beamline acceptance, a
transverse emittance upper limit of 0.093 π-cm-mrad, rms has been established by the project for all
output beams from the DTL 100 MeV. This upper limit still meets the LAMP performance requirements
for charge delivery to each LANSCE experimental area and while maintaining hands-on maintenance. This
upper limit is supported by a recent analysis of operational data. Additionally, the present conceptual
LAMP front-end design meets this requirement.
A more detailed summary of the data evaluated is given in the sections below.

1 Introduction
The approach that has been used to define an emittance budget for the new LAMP front end is based on
recent and historical measured beam emittances at 100 MeV for the three beam types accelerated at
LANSCE: H+ (protons for isotope production), LBEG (H- beam for delivery to proton radiography and to
the Lujan neutron spallation target, and MPEG (H- beam for delivery to the Weapons Neutron Research
facility). The emittance budget (upper limit) for each beam type has been selected to maintain the losses in
the CCL to a level approximately equivalent to those observed in present operations to first order based on
available data.
Transverse beam size in a linac is directly proportional to the square root of the beam emittance for a given
beam current, a fixed focusing lattice, and transverse beam emittance. It is therefore assumed that to first
order if the beam emittances delivered from the new LAMP front end at 100 MeV can be maintained at
values equivalent to or less than the present front end, an equivalent level of beam loss and activation will
also follow. LAMP is expected to potentially deliver higher beam currents than the present front end,
however maintaining similar emittance upper limits even at higher beam currents should mitigate any
increase in beam losses at these higher beam currents.
Only a transverse emittance budget has been specified for LAMP. It is expected that the beam losses in the
CCL will be less sensitive to the longitudinal properties of the beams. It is also expected that the longitudinal
properties of the beam (energy spread and phase spread) into the CCL will be improved through the
combination of a Radio-Frequency Quadrupole (RFQ) accelerator and new DTL, as compared to the
existing injector.
It should be noted that the LANSCE CCL has known “hot spots” inherent to the design of the CCL. One is
at the CCL entrance at 100 MeV where the beam is injected into the CCL. Beam losses at this location
occur predominately due to the factor of 4 transition in accelerating structure frequency (201.25 MHz to
805 MHz); the RF bucket needed to capture the beam shrinks by this ratio. The second major beam loss
location is at 211 MeV (module 13) where the transverse focusing lattice changes and subsequently the
beam size increases by approximately a factor of 2. It is also at this location that low-energy beam that was
not captured at 100 MeV at the CCL entrance is further defocused and lost transversely. These inherent
features of the CCL will not be changed or improved as part of LAMP.
Data from long-term operation delivering 800-MeV, 80-kW H- beam (10 mA peak) indicates that the
integrated losses in the CCL do not exceed 200 nA [1]. The CCL is approximately 700 m in length [2].
Therefore, the beam loss per unit length is approximately 0.29 nA/m. Losses of H- beam are mostly due to
stripping of the H- ions by residual gas, intra-beam stripping, and Lorentz stripping. The H+ beam is not
affected by these loss mechanisms. The measured beam loss of the H+ beam is typically one order of
magnitude smaller than that of the H- beam at the same current [3]. Beam losses are monitored during
accelerator operations and are actively limited using activation protection (AP) detectors and hardware
transmission monitors (HWTMs). The integrated signal from each AP is monitored and beams are shut off
if the average current of 100 nA as measured by the AP is exceeded. The HWTM system measures the
beam current losses between current monitors and shuts the beam off if the difference in measured beam
current between HWTM monitors exceeds a set threshold value. LANSCE also incorporates a beam fast-
protect system that measures instantaneous beam loss at multiple locations to avoid high beam spill during
turn-on activities and system transients. These monitoring systems are used to limit beam losses to a level
that allows for “hands on” maintenance of the accelerator. The generally accepted value for beam loss to
maintain this level is 1.0 nA/m. Assuming beam losses increase linearly with peak beam current, the losses
in the CCL should remain well below these levels even for substantially increased peak beam currents of
up to a factor of two or more. This fact, along with the low estimated losses per unit length under the current

3 Beam Loss Simulation Results
Recent simulation studies were completed to estimate the transmission of the LANSCE H- beams from the
end of the present LANSCE DTL to the PSR stripper foil (LBEG) and WNR Target 4 (MPEG) [8]. The
LBEG beam has a high duty factor and therefore allows for continuous monitoring of its transmission
through the various stages of the accelerator. The MPEG beam, however, has widely spaced micropulses.
The lack of requisite sensitivity current monitors throughout the accelerator makes transmission
measurements of this beam extremely difficult and more uncertain. Therefore, beam-dynamics simulations
using HPSim [9] were used to estimate the MPEG beam transmission. The results, compared with
measurement data, are shown in Table 4 and Table 5 below. Table 5 shows a transmission comparison
between operation and HPSim results for the LBEG beam through Tanks 1-4 of the DTL, the Transition
Region (TR) and Tanks 5-48 of the CCL at various locations. Table 6 is a summary of CCL and HEBT
losses comparing measurements and HPSim simulation results for both LBEG and MPEG beams. These
transmission estimates are vital to determining the performance requirements of the new LAMP Front-End
design needed to meet the project KPPs.
The simulation results overestimate the beam losses in the LANSCE CCL and high-energy beam transport
(HEBT) to the PSR stripper foil and WNR Target 4. Beam loss simulations can be very sensitive to the
beam distribution assumed, the number of macroparticles, beam phase space matching conditions, etc.
Initial simulation results shown in Table 5 were initially in disagreement with the measurement data. An
optimization of the CCL and beam transport parameters was completed to improve the simulation results
following a process very similar to that performed by the operators in the Central Control Room (CCR).
This process has been shown to increase the beam transmission (lower initial beam losses) by approximately
5% in the simulations and up to a factor of 4000 from initial physics-based beam tune-up to production-

like beam operation for the real LANSCE beam. The simulation results shown in Table 5 have also
undergone this optimization process.
Although it is difficult to estimate absolute beam losses using simulation results, these kinds of simulations
can still be useful in determining the trend of beam losses as changes to the LAMP design are made and the
LAMP DTL output beam distribution and other physics details are better defined.

overly constraining.

5 IPF Beamline Acceptance
The acceptance of the IPF beam line has also been estimated [11]. Since the IPF beam line IPF does not
have a periodic lattice, the approach taken was to find the maximum emittance that will fit within the known
apertures while providing the desired beam spot at the target and keeping the same Twiss parameters at the
entrance of the beam line. Therefore, the Acceptance is defined as the maximum unnormalized transverse
emittance that will fit given these constraints. Additional assumptions are that the output beam from the
DTL has the same Twiss parameters, but larger emittance; the longitudinal emittance will be ignored, the
beam at the IPF target has the same size and phase-space slope, the beam size at target is σ ~ 6.6 mm as
determined from previous TRACE 3D modeling (P. Walstrom), and any dispersion requirements are
ignored.
The known beam pipe apertures are as follows:

 2” dia. pipe with 1/8” (assumed) wall, a = 0.875” from DTL exit to entrance of shield wall between TR
 and IPF
 4” dia. pipe from entrance of TR to IPF shield wall and IPEM01
 6” dia. pipe from IPEM01 to IPF target
 Raster magnets just downstream of IPQM07

Therefore, the minimum aperture that constrains the acceptance is the 2” diameter pipe from the DTL exit.
This value for a will be used to estimate the acceptance and aperture-to-rms rations for a range of n values
as previously determined for the CCL.

of the IPF beamline as previously generated by P. Walstrom (Figure 5). This TRACE 3D model is the basis
