1.0 Instrument Description
 The U.S. Department of Energy’s Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User Facility operates
 TSI aerodynamic particle sizer (APS) Model 3321 instruments in its Aerosol Observing System (AOS).
 The APS measures aerodynamic diameter and light-scattering intensity, providing number size
 distributions for particles from 0.5 to 20 µm.

 In the AOS, the APS characterizes coarse-mode aerosol size distributions and complements submicron
 sizing from the AOS scanning mobility particle sizer (SMPS). The AOS is designed to sample aerosols
 through a common inlet and flow distributor with a defined sampling conditioning, so APS results must
 be interpreted in the context of inlet, sample conditioning (RH/temperature), and sampling configuration.

1.2 Instrument/Measurement Theory
The APS 3321 measures aerodynamic particle size using a time-of-flight principle: the aerosol sample is
accelerated through a nozzle, and particles pass through two partially overlapping laser beams that create
a double-crested beam profile (see Figure 1). Larger particles accelerate more slowly due to inertia,
producing a longer peak-to-peak time of flight — resolved at 4 ns resolution — which is mapped to
aerodynamic diameter via an internal calibration curve. A valid single-particle event generates a signal
with exactly two crests; signals with only one crest (phantom particles) or more than two crests
(coincidence events) are excluded from size distribution calculations but logged separately for
post-analysis correction. Because coincidence increases with particle concentration, the manufacturer
provides maximum recommended concentration limits and coincidence thresholds; adherence to these
limits is necessary to ensure that reported number and mass size distributions reflect only
well-characterized single-particle events.

2.0 Data
 The APS 3321 measures aerodynamic diameter from 0.5 to 20 µm, binning size data into 52
 logarithmically spaced channels. It reports the particle size distribution, number concentration,
 aerodynamic diameter, and total count in real time. In the ARM AOS, raw 1 Hz records are collected over
 RS-232 by the ARM APS LabVIEW VI and archived in the aosaps datastream, from which the size
 distribution is derived downstream.

 2.1 Data Description
 Raw, unpolled records are acquired at 1 Hz over the RS-232 serial interface by the ARM APS LabVIEW
 VI, time-stamped, and archived. Aerodynamic sizing spans 0.5-20 µm across 52 logarithmically spaced
 channels (32 per decade); optical sizing is reported from 0.3-20 µm. Primary measured quantities are the
 number size distribution (dN/dlogDp), total concentration/count, and aerodynamic diameter. Secondary
 derived quantities (in the higher-order data product) include total volume and surface area.

 Each record also carries housekeeping and metadata used for quality screening: total, aerosol
 (inner-nozzle), and sheath flows; sample/inlet pressure (bpress); sheath- and total-pump voltages (spump,
 tpump); laser current and power (lcur, lpower); instrument temperatures (itemp, btemp, dtemp);
 event/status flags; and coincidence and phantom-event counters.

 All APS sizing is on an aerodynamic-diameter basis; merging with the SMPS (mobility diameter) or
 optical particle counter (OPC) (optical-equivalent diameter) requires assumptions about particle density
 and dynamic shape factor.

 2.1.1 APS Data in ARM Data Discovery

 APS data progress through three processing levels, each retrievable from ARM Data Discovery: raw (00),
 ingested (a0), and quality-controlled (b1). The distinction matters because automated quality control and
 the reported variables may differ among the different data levels.

 Raw (level 00). The APS streams unpolled records once per second over RS-232, which the LabVIEW VI
 logs as tab-separated (.tsv) files: a common metadata header — VI name/version/date, site/platform,
 instrument, system, mentor, and comment fields — followed by 1 Hz data rows. The VI writes three
 parallel files per interval. The main file records, each second, a timestamp (UTC), acquisition mode and
 status flags, sample and dead times, the Event 1, Event 3, and Event 4 counters with a total, and the raw
 counts across the 52 aerodynamic-diameter channels (the lowest being <0.523 µm), together with
 instrument housekeeping.

 Alongside the main file, the VI logs the two accumulator records defined in the TSI manual, each
 carrying the same timestamp, event counters, and totals as leading columns: the Accumulator
 (time-of-flight) Data Record (A), holding counts across the 1,024 time-of-flight bins (a1…a1024, 4 ns
 each), and the Side-Scatter Accumulator Data Record (B), holding counts across the 64 side-scatter
 channels (b1…b64). Each particle signal is classified as one of four event types. Only a two-crest "event
 2" yields a full aerodynamic size — for these the instrument records both the peak-to-peak time-of-flight
 and the side-scatter intensity, and they populate the sized channels. Single-crest "event 1" signals (the

coincidences (event 3, three or more crests) and timer over-range events (event 4) are tallied as counts but

2.2 Data Quality and Uncertainty
Flow accuracy is central to both sizing and concentration measurements in the APS: the aerosol sample
flow (1.0 ±0.05 L/min) sets the sampled volume from which number concentrations are derived, while the
sheath flow (4.0 ±0.05 L/min) governs the particle acceleration profile and therefore the
time-of-flight-to-diameter calibration mapping. Deviations in either flow from their nominal values
introduce systematic biases in both reported concentrations and aerodynamic diameters. Furthermore,
unit-to-unit variability is typically between 10% and 20% for particles in the range of 0.9 to 3 µm
(Pfeifer et al. 2016). This range is considered acceptable for atmospheric measurements, though users
should be aware that variability increases for particles outside this window. Particle counting accuracy is
additionally influenced by coincidence at high concentrations — typically above 1,000 particles/cm³ —
where coincident events are detected but not sized; these are flagged in the datastream and can be used to
apply concentration corrections in post-processing.

To ensure the APS is operating within nominal conditions, a tiered quality assurance approach is applied
in both laboratory and field settings.

2.2.1 Laboratory Data Quality Assessment

Tier 1 — Basic instrument status check: The first tier is instrument screening, which includes
verification of aerosol and sheath flow rates, zero particle counts on HEPA-filtered air, and review of
housekeeping variables including laser power, detector voltage, optics temperature, and coincidence event
rates — confirming that the instrument is operating within manufacturer-specified limits.

2.2.2 Data Quality Assessment in the Field

2.2.2.1 Daily Instrument Checks

Instrument operations are evaluated daily using the standard APS Daily Check Template, which tracks the
following instrument operational variables:
 • Status Lights: Power, flow, laser, and particle detection lights.
 • Operational Status: VI (Virtual Instrument) status.
 • Flow Rates: Total flow (tflow) and sample flow (sflow).
 • Pressure: Barometric/internal pressure (bpress).
 • Pump Operations: Sheath pump voltage and total pump voltage.

2.2.2.2 Weekly Instrument Checks

Weekly procedures focus on baseline validation of flow and noise, as well as physical maintenance of the
sampling path:
 • HEPA Zero Check: Verifies the leak integrity of the system and ensures the optical bench is free of
 background contamination.
 • Inner Nozzle Cleaning: Removes any particle deposition or debris that could constrict the sample
 flow.
 • Inner Nozzle Flow Verification: Ensures the sample air jet remains properly aligned and calibrated.
 This is also referred to as aerosol/sample flow.

2.2.2.3 Remote Mentor-Led Data Quality Assessment

The ARM DQ Explorer (dqexplorer.arm.gov) is used to review the aosaps datastream in real time to
identify suspicious, incorrect, or missing data. The tool generates near-real-time visualizations of the
aerodynamic particle sizer (APS) datastream as it is collected from the field. In coordination with the
ARM Data Quality (DQ) Office, automated flags are generated directly on these visualizations based on
mentor-supplied evaluation criteria. Flags are generated whenever measured data (primary output and/or
metadata such as instrument housekeeping) are above, below, or outside defined maximum threshold
ranges. Examples of these thresholds and ranges are listed below, intended to be illustrative rather than
definitive.
 • Sample Pressure (1000±30 hPa 1): depending upon the site ambient pressure reading, when sampling
 downstream of the inlet impactor, the sample pressure indicates the degree to which the impactor is
 being clogged by debris.
 • Status Flag (0 or 1): Provides a binary indication of key variables tracked by the TSI APS firmware.
 A value of 0 indicates a normal measurement, while a value of 1 indicates a suspicious measurement.

3.0 Historical Background
 ARM uses the TSI aerodynamic particle sizer (APS) Model 3321, a commercial time-of-flight
 aerodynamic spectrometer, as the standard coarse-mode sizing instrument for the AOS. The APS extends
 ARM’s in situ size coverage into the super-micron range and complements submicron sizing from the
 AOS SMPS. Coarse-mode aerosols — mineral dust, sea salt, bioaerosol, and other mechanically
 generated particles — dominate aerosol mass loading and contribute substantially to aerosol optical depth,
 yet have historically been less well characterized than fine mode.

 Until 2023, continuous APS operation within the AOS was limited to the SGP site and AMF1. Beginning
 in 2024, ARM expanded APS deployment to the second ARM Mobile Facility (AMF2), AMF3, ENA,
 and the North Slope of Alaska (NSA), establishing network-wide coarse-mode coverage. The current
 deployment status is summarized in Table 4 below.

4.2 Corrective Action
Corrective action follows a graded response keyed to the expected/warning/contact-mentor ranges in the
daily and weekly check tables. When a parameter falls in the warning range, the operator rechecks the
reading and inspects the affected subsystem; when it reaches the must-contact range or a check fails
outright, the operator notifies the instrument mentor before continuing.

 • Flow out of range: verify with a calibrated flow standard and, if confirmed, adjust the sheath
 (RA3) and aerosol (RA4) potentiometers per the flow-calibration procedure. Check PSL size after
 the flow check.

 • Failed HEPA zero (counts not at 0.0 #/cc): reclean the inner nozzle, confirm the inlet tube is
 seated and the knurled ring is hand tight, and rerun the zero; persistent counts may indicate a leak
 or contaminated optics.

 • Laser power: monitor the trend and schedule laser replacement before particle counting degrades;
 keep a spare laser available.

 • Repeated or persistent flags, or gaps in the VI/data ingest: check the serial communications and
 restart the VI; escalate to the mentor if unresolved.

4.3 Instrument Service Routine
Scheduled instrument service keeps the sample path clean and the flows stable. Service intervals and
responsibilities are summarized in the routine-check timetable below (Table 6); an active spare parts
inventory should be maintained to support these activities.
 • Weekly: clean the 1/8-inch inner nozzle (flush with clean dry air, or isopropyl alcohol if heavily
 soiled, then dry) and inspect the inner-nozzle O-ring.
 • As needed: inspect and clean the nafion dryer orifice and replace the orifice filter; check or replace
 the Perma Pure MD-700 (24-inch) nafion dryer.
 • As needed: inspect the Parker Balston pump filter if the pump voltages are noisy.
 • Periodic (quarterly/six-monthly): perform a full system zero check and send the unit for factory
 service per the routine-check timetable.

5.0 Calibration Plan
 APS calibration and validation follow a three-tier program applied at laboratory (at BNL/C3) and during
 field deployment as description in Section 2.2.1 and 2.2.2. Only onsite calibration/validation is scheduled
 per the routine-check timetable. Briefly:

 Tier 1 verifies instrument status, flows, optics, and the zero-count baseline; Tier 2 verifies sizing accuracy
 with PSL standards; and Tier 3 performs full characterization and size-distribution closure against the
 co-located SMPS and a reference OPC.

 Flow rates are verified against a NIST-traceable volumetric flow standard (e.g., Sensidyne Gilibrator 2.0).
 Sheath flow is set to 4.0 ± 0.05 LPM using the RA3 potentiometer and aerosol (inner nozzle) flow to
 1.0 ± 0.05 LPM using the RA4 potentiometer on the Power PC board, giving a total flow of 5.0 LPM. The
 sheath and aerosol adjustments are iterated until both are within tolerance.

 Sizing accuracy is verified by atomizing monodisperse PSL spheres into the dried sample line and
 comparing the measured modal diameter — taken from a lognormal fit rather than the raw histogram peak
 — against the certified size, with an acceptance criterion of within ±3–5% of nominal. Field validation
 typically uses 700 and 1,000 nm PSL from the calibration travelling kit, while laboratory characterization
 extends the check to larger sizes (e.g., 2,500 nm).

 Tier 3 closure experiments route the APS, SMPS, and reference OPC together and qualitatively compare
 size distributions in the overlapping size bins; deviations outside the agreement criteria are investigated.

6.0 User Notes and Known Issues
 Accurate characterization of the coarse-mode aerosol size distribution in ground-based sampling systems
 requires careful attention to inlet design, sample transport, conditioning, instrument performance, and
 calibration traceability.

 Each of these introduces biases and uncertainties in capturing the size and concentration of coarse-mode
 aerosols. The following subsections detail the principal challenges identified in the ARM AOS context.

 6.1 Sampling Configuration
 Need for a Standardized Sampling Configuration

 The APS draws from the main AOS inlet manifold via a vertical, dedicated sample line, with horizontal
 runs and bends kept to a minimum to limit gravitational and inertial particle losses. A Perma Pure
 MD-700 nafion dryer (24-inch active length with a crossflow purge) sits upstream of the APS, holding
 sample RH at ≤ 40% consistent with Aerosol, Clouds and Trace Gases Research Infrastructure
 (ACTRIS)/Global Atmosphere Watch (GAW) guidance (see Figure 9). RH and temperature can be
 monitored just upstream of the APS to verify dryer performance.

 While the current AOS configuration for the APS is constrained by available space within the rack, the
 eventual goal is to standardize a sampling configuration that minimizes losses by design. The observed
 bends in ENA and BNF may result in more losses than the configuration in AMF1 and SGP. However,
 only AMF1 transmission loss has been characterized (see Section 6.2) and transmission through the stack
 has not been quantified at each site. Site-specific transmission efficiency measurements are needed —
 comparing near-inlet and post-manifold size distributions against a collocated APS or OPC reference.

6.2 Sampling and Transmission Losses Inlet Loss
Super-micron transmission through aerosol sampling systems is inherently limited by gravitational
settling, inertial impaction at bends and constrictions, and turbulent deposition. These losses are
site-specific: tubing length, diameter, bend count, and orientation differ across ARM sites in AMF3 BNF,
SGP, ENA, NSA, and the mobile facilities (AMF1 and AMF2) as observed in Section 6.1. Without
site-specific transmission efficiency characterization, applying uniform corrections or comparing
coarse-mode data across sites carries large uncertainty.

AMF1 Inlet Stack Experiment in 2021

Kuang and Singh (2024) conducted a transmission efficiency (TE) characterization with the AMF1 AOS
prior to deployment in the ARM Tracking Aerosol Convection Interactions Experiment (TRACER) in
2021/2022 (see Figure 10). TE was estimated from an ensemble spanning 0.01-10 µm — four days of
SMPS data and nine days of OPC data. OPC estimates were restricted to periods when the sample RH of
OPC-1 (dried, via stack) and OPC-2 (undried, ambient) agreed within 5%, then averaged per day
(Figure 11). TE approached unity (±10-15% daily standard deviation) from ~0.02 to 4.0 µm, with 50%
transmission (TE50) at 0.016 µm and 6.2 µm at the lower and upper limits. The roll-off at these limits
indicates stack losses: diffusion at the smallest sizes, and gravitational settling and impaction at micron
sizes.

6.3 APS Instrument Performance
The APS 3321 measures aerodynamic diameter via particle time-of-flight between two laser beams.
While the technique is well established for super-micron particles, a couple of performance limitations are
relevant to ARM’s application:
 • Counting efficiency below 1 µm: The APS's lower detection limit is nominally 0.523 µm
 (aerodynamic), but measurement consistency declines significantly at sizes approaching this limit.
 Volckens and Peters (2005) found that for solid particles, individual APS 3321 units maintained
 counting efficiencies between 85% and 99% across the tested 0.8-10 µm range. However,
 Pfeifer et al. (2016) found that unit-to-unit variability in the reported number size distribution —
 using solid ammonium sulfate and ambient aerosol — grew to as much as 60% for particles below
 0.9 µm, compared to just 10-20% between 0.9 and 3 µm, likely reflecting differences in counting
 efficiency between individual units. This suggests that while a single well-characterized unit may
 count solid particles reliably near the lower size limit, results can vary substantially between different
 APS units without unit-specific calibration.
 • Unit-to-unit variability: A quick screening of multiple APS instruments with the ARM AOS
 suggested measurable differences in counting response among individual units, even after standard
 calibration — consistent with Pfeifer et al. (2016), who reported similar unit-to-unit variability in a
 15-unit APS intercomparison. This variability complicates inter-site comparisons and may warrant
 characterization through more systematic intercomparison campaigns.

 Operational Stability

 Long-term continuous operation of APS instruments presents several practical challenges that affect data
 continuity and quality, as detailed below.
  • Laser Operational Lifetime. The APS 3321 uses a diode laser for time-of-flight detection. ARM
 operational experience indicates typical lifetimes of 4,500-7,000 hours of continuous operation before
 laser power degrades below the threshold for reliable particle detection. At 24/7 operation, this
 corresponds to replacement intervals of approximately 6-10 months, requiring proactive monitoring
 and spare laser inventory management.
  • Flow and Pump Stability. The APS relies on a stable total flow of 5.0 L/min (1.0 L/min sample,
 4.0 L/min sheath) maintained by an internal pump. Pump wear, filter loading, and ambient
 pressure/temperature changes can cause flow drift that affects sizing accuracy. Weekly flow
 verification against a NIST-traceable flow standard is essential.
  • Data Acquisition and Communications. Data acquisition (DAQ) communication failures between
 the APS and the site data system have been documented at multiple AOS sites, resulting in
 intermittent data gaps. Implementing fail-safe watchdog protocols and automated data integrity
 checks can minimize these losses.

7.0 Frequently Asked Questions
 What size range does the APS 3321 measure?

 0.5-20 µm aerodynamic diameter (0.3-20 µm optical), reported in 52 logarithmically spaced channels at
 32 channels per decade.

 During field deployment, what are the daily and weekly check or validation performed on the APS?

 Daily checks on the instrument status, and operational variable, measured flow and zero check on the
 weekly basis.

 How often should the inner nozzle be cleaned?

 Weekly. Turn the instrument off, remove the inlet tube and the 1/8-inch inner nozzle, flush with clean dry
 air (isopropyl alcohol if heavily soiled), dry, inspect the O-ring, reassemble hand tight, and then run a
 HEPA zero check.

 Which PSL sizes are used to check sizing accuracy?

 The field travelling kit carries 700 and 1,000 nm PSL; laboratory commissioning adds larger sizes (e.g.,
 2,500 nm). The measured mode must be within ±3-5% of the certified size.

 When downloading the APS data from ARM Data Discovery, what should a user be aware of?

 Use b1 level with quality flagged applied. Data also has qc_embedded, so use data after filtering
 appropriate qc
