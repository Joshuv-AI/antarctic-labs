Asparaginyl-tRNA Synthetase (AsnRS) Is a Novel Vanadate Reductase from a Marine Bacterium
Authors: Dewi Yuliani, Romaidi, Tatsuya Ueki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Increasing environmental releases of vanadium (V) by industrial plants pose significant risks of soil, water, and ecosystem contamination. Microbes reduce this toxic compound by producing a vanadate reductase enzyme that converts V5+ to a less toxic compound, V4+. This study describes the cloning, expression, and characterization of an asparaginyl-tRNA synthetase (AsnRS) from the marine bacterium Shewanella sp. HU-ET00281. The Shewanella-AsnRS (Sh-AsnRS) recombinant protein reduced V5+ to V4+ under ambient conditions. The Km and Vmax values were 8.70 mM and 4.45 mol-V4+/min/mol-protein respectively. Activity was enhanced by up to 75% on addition of Zn2+ and Cu2+. Sh-AsnRS is a novel dual-function enzyme with AsnRS and V5+ reductase activities that will aid bioremediation and environmental detoxification.

Introduction

Vanadium (V) is a transition metal with unique properties that finds applications in the medical, photographic, chemical, electrochemical, refining, energy-storage, catalytic, and other fields, thereby playing essential roles in modern industries (Hu et al. 2018; Yan et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2020). The metallurgical industry uses V during production of steel and non-ferrous alloys (Ścibior et al. 2020; Dong et al. 2021). The demand for V is rising, and global production of the metal has approximately doubled over the past 15 years, to 120 kilotons in 2025 (Gustafsson 2019; Boni et al. 2023; Ibrahim et al. 2025). Environmental contamination has therefore become of concern. Most V is released by anthropogenic activities (Zhang et al. 2019b; Altaf et al. 2022; Sandanayake et al. 2022; Dinda et al. 2025). Above a certain concentration, V is toxic to microorganisms, plants, animals, and humans (Ghosh et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2020; Zheng et al. 2021). V is a redox-labile metal that exists in multiple oxidation states (+ 3, + 4, +5). However, most environmental V is V5+, the toxicity and mobility of which are higher than those of V4+ (De Souza et al. 2020; Fei et al. 2022; Abernathy et al. 2022; Wnuk 2023). Biological reduction of V may usefully remove/detoxify V (Wang et al. 2018; Gan et al. 2024; Kim et al. 2025).

Microbial V reduction is attractive in terms of resource availability and the environmental benefits afforded (Okibe et al. 2016; Xiao et al. 2024). Various bacteria reduce V5+ to the less toxic V4+. These include Pseudomonas (Antipov et al. 2000; He et al. 2023; Gan et al. 2024), Geobacter (Ortiz-Bernad et al. 2004; Li and Zhang 2020; Yan et al. 2022), Bacillus (Zhang et al. 2019b; Wang et al. 2020), Shewanella (Carpentier et al. 2003; Romaidi and Ueki 2016; Mohamed 2017; Jo et al. 2021), Lactococcus (Zhang et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2025), Vibrio (Yuliani et al. 2024), and Halomonas (Antipov and Khijniak 2016). Such diversity implies that microbial V reduction is common and may involve different enzymatic pathways (Rehder 2022).

Several in vitro and in silico studies have identified proteins/enzymes and other substances that facilitate V reduction. Membrane-associated proteins and extracellular polymeric substances (EPSs) play essential roles in terms of reducing V in extracellular compartments with electron transport chains that include a c-type tetraheme cytochrome (CymA) and certain outer membrane proteins (OmcB, OmcA, and MtrC) (Myers et al. 2004; Van Marwijk et al. 2009; Li and Zhang 2020) as revealed by retrieved metagenomic-assembled genomic and transcriptomic analyses (Sun et al. 2020; Gan et al. 2024). Scanning electron microscopy energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) have suggested the interaction of EPS and V5+ in a biofilm and vanadium and possible reduction of V5+ to V4+ by EPS (Lai et al. 2018; Zhou et al. 2022).

V bio-reduction also proceeds in the intracellular compartment (Zhang et al. 2014). Nitrate reductase (NarG) enzymatically reduces V (Antipov et al. 1998; Antipov and Khijniak 2016). Several vanadate reductases have been identified via analyses of gene and transcript abundance, and enzymatic activity. NarG aside, another nitrate reductase (NapA) and two nitrite reductases (NirS, NirK) catalyze V reduction well using the electron transport system (Zhang et al. 2019a, 2021; He et al. 2021). Yan et al. (2022) used in silico metagenomic binning to show that NarG and NapA engage in respiratory V reduction. Gan et al. (2024) employed a transcriptomic analysis to reveal that nitrate/nitrite reductase was involved in V reduction. Glutathione production was upregulated upon exposure to V5+, as was enzyme activity, implying involvement of the system in terms of reduction (Shi and Dalal 1990; Wang et al. 2024). V5+ bio-reduction is often accompanied by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH), implying a role for electron transfer (Zhang et al. 2021).

Many proteins/enzymes and other substances that reduce V have been described in recent years. Here, we focus on an enzyme that converts V5+ to the less toxic V4+. Following the study by Romaidi and Ueki (2016), we purified a 61-kDa vanadate reductase from the membrane fraction of Shewanella sp. strain S-RA-6 and characterized it via matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-MS). The enzyme was an asparaginyl-tRNA synthetase (AsnRS). Here, we isolated and characterized an AsnRS from a closely related marine bacterium, Shewanella sp. strain HU-ET00281. The results offer new insights into a novel enzyme with dual functions as an AsnRS and vanadate reductase that will aid bioremediation and environmental detoxification.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Strains and Plasmids

Shewanella sp. strain S-RA-6 (Romaidi and Ueki 2016) was unfortunately lost after purification and identification of the V5+ reductase. We sought similar bacteria elsewhere. Three candidate marine bacteria (strains HU-SA00079, HU-ET00281, and HU-ET01161) were identified based on 16 S rRNA analysis. These strains were isolated as part of Project JPNP20008 of the New Energy and Industrial Technology Development Organization (NEDO). HU-SA00079 was obtained from a poly(butylene succinate-co-adipate)) film and HU-ET00281 and HU-ET01161 were obtained from a polyethylene terephthalate film that had been immersed in the Mukaishima area of the Seto Inland Sea in Japan. Colony isolation and identification have been described (Miura et al. 2025). Marine and standard media with and without V were used for bacterial cultivation.

Escherichia coli DH5α and E. coli KRX were respectively used for cloning and gene expression. Competent cells were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium (0.5% yeast extract, 1% peptone, and 1% NaCl) with 50 µg/mL ampicillin. pBluescript II SK(+) was used for gene cloning and sequencing, and pMAL-c2x for gene expression.

Purification of a V5+ Reductase from Shewanella sp. S-RA-6

Bacterial cells (strain S-RA-6) were cultured in 400 mL of medium at 180 rpm and 20 °C for 24 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C and resuspended in 6 mL buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA and 0.8% Nonidet P40]. Resuspended cells were gently shaken at 4 °C for 1 h and then centrifuged at 100,000 rpm and 4 °C for 90 min. Extracted proteins were loaded onto a DEAE–Sephacel anion exchange chromatography column and stepwise-eluted in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) with 0, 200, 300, 400 and 500 mM NaCl. Protein purity and concentration were assessed via SDS-PAGE and the Bradford assay, respectively. Proteins in specific fractions were separated via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) on a C18 reverse-phase column operating in the water-acetonitrile gradient mode.

MALDI-TOF MS Protein Identification

Protein bands were excised from SDS-PAGE gels, destained in acetonitrile, and dried under vacuum. The dried gels were then exposed to reducing solution (10 mM dithiothreitol, 5 mM ammonium hydrogen carbonate) at 56 °C for 1 h. The proteins were alkylated using iodoacetamide and digested by trypsin (sequencing grade modified trypsin, Promega). Each extracted protein sample was mixed with a matrix (2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid, Sigma-Aldrich Merck Life Science) and dried. MALDI-QIT-TOF-MS analysis employed an AXIMA-OIT platform (Shimadzu Co. Ltd.) and Mascot Server 2.4 space software (Matrix Science Co. Ltd.).

Peptide mass fingerprint (PMF) data were identified via 1-D SDS PAGE and MALDI-TOF-MS. The peptides were compared to those in public and private databases. Protein identification employed the MS-Fit program (https://prospector.ucsf.edu/prospector/cgi-bin/msform.cgi?form=msfitupload). The searches permitted no more than one missed trypsin cleavage over a protein mass range (m/z) of 50,000–70,000 Da; the mass tolerance was 1.2 Da. The acceptable modifications included carbamidomethylation of cysteine (C) and oxidation of methionine (M). The number of protein hits was set to 5, and the mass type was “average”. The mass lists were created using the public Swiss-Prot (release 20210618) database, limited to the taxonomic name Shewanella. Strain S-RA-6 information was also analyzed based on its genomic sequence using a private protein database (Romaidi and Ueki 2016). Protein candidates were selected based on both MOWSE scores and consistency.

V Resistance and Accumulation Assays

The assays used were those of Yuliani et al. (2024) and Romaidi and Ueki (2016). Briefly, bacteria were inoculated onto standard and marine agar media supplemented with sodium orthovanadate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at 0, 5, and 10 mM and incubated overnight at 20 °C. The bacteria that grew were V-resistant. To assess V accumulation, bacteria were grown in standard and marine media with 0.5 mM V5+ or V4+. Cells were collected via centrifugation and washed three times in V-free media. The pellets were heat-dried and digested using nitric acid. Each sample was centrifuged to obtain a supernatant, and the V concentration in that supernatant was quantified via atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS) as nanograms per milligram of dry weight (ng/mg dw) of cells. All experiments were conducted at least three times.

Molecular Cloning and Sequencing

Bacteria were exposed to 100 µg/mL lysozyme at 37 °C for 1 h, 200 unit/mL achromopeptidase at 37 °C for 30 min, and 10 mg/mL proteinase-K at 55 °C for 2 h. Then, chromosomal DNA was isolated using the phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol (PCI) method (Sambrook and Russel 2001).

AsnRS genes were PCR-amplified using specific primers designed using AmplifX2 version 2.1.1 based on the DNA sequences of asnRS from Shewanella sp. strain S-RA-6. The primer pair for AsnRS amplification was asnRS-F (5’-GGATCCATGAGCATTACATCTGTCGC) and asnRS-R (5’- GTCGACTTAGAAGTTCGCTGATTTTGG). The forward primer contains a BamHI restriction site (underlined) and the reverse primer contains a SalI restriction site (underlined). PCR proceeded as follows: 30 cycles at 96 °C for 30 s, 63 °C for 40 s, and 72 °C for 2 min. The reaction mixture contained 1.0 µL DNA template (~ 20 ng), 1.0 µL 10-µM primer, 5.0 µL premixed KAPA2G HS ReadyMix (Sigma-Aldrich Merck Life Science), and 3.0 µL Milli-Q water. The PCR product (∼1,400 bp) in a gel was purified using a FastGene™ Gel/PCR Extraction Kit (Nippon Genetics Co. Ltd.), cloned into pBluescript II SK(+), and transformed into E. coli DH5α. The recombinant plasmid was isolated using a Nucleospin Plasmid EasyPure Kit (Sigma-Aldrich Merck Life Science). DNA sequencing analyses was performed at the Natural Science Center for Basic Research and Development (N-BARD) of Hiroshima University, Japan. The gene was named Sh-asnRS and its nucleotide sequence was submitted to the DDBJ/EMBL/GenBank databases under accession number LC929984. The amino acid sequence was aligned with known AsnRS proteins using ClustalW (https://www.genome.jp/tools-bin/clustalw). Alignment employed NCBI BLAST data and the information was visualized using Jalview ver. 2.11.2.0 (Waterhouse et al. 2005). The physical parameters of the Sh-asnRS amino acid sequence were obtained using Protparam tools (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The predicted protein structures were generated by Phyre2 (http://www.sbg.bio.ic.ac.uk/phyre2/html/page.cgi?id=help) and the various structures were superimposed using Chimera-X software (Pettersen et al. 2021). Putative metal ion-binding sites were predicted by Alphafold3 (Abramson et al. 2024). Conserved domains were predicted using CD-Search at NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi).

Preparation and Purification of Recombinant Protein

The Sh-asnRS gene was cloned in-frame into pMAL-c2x (New England BioLabs Japan Inc.) between the BamHI and SalI sites. The pMal-c2x vector contains a malE gene encoding maltose-binding protein (MBP), with which the recombinant gene was fused to form a fusion protein (FP). The plasmid was introduced into E. coli KRX for protein expression. Transformants grown on an LB agar plate supplemented with ampicillin (50 µg/mL) contained the Sh-asnRS gene as revealed by colony PCR using vector-specific primers.

FP induction and purification were performed as reported by Tri et al. (2022). Briefly, an overnight bacterial culture was added to 10 volumes of fresh LB medium with 0.5 mM IPTG and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in 10 mL 1x buffer with 8 M urea, and disrupted by sonication. The FP was purified on an amylose resin column following the manufacturer’s protocol. FP expression and purification were monitored via SDS-PAGE. Empty pMAL-c2x served as the control. The protein concentration was determined by the Bradford method with bovine serum albumin as a standard (Kruger 2009).

Aminoacyl-tRNA Synthetase Activity

Aminoacyl-tRNA activity was measured according to Cestari and Stuart (2013). Molecular-biology-grade ATP disodium salt was purchased from Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Japan. Inorganic pyrophosphatase (iPPase) was purchased from Takara Bio Inc., Japan. Reactions were composed of 0.3 µM purified FP or MBP, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 50 µg/mL total RNA isolated from Shewanella sp. strain HU-ET00281, 1 U/mL iPPase, 1 mM amino acids, and 200 µM ATP in reaction buffer (30 mM HEPES, 140 mM NaCl, 30 mM KCl, and 40 mM MgCl2) for a 50-µL total reaction volume. The reaction mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The amount of inorganic phosphate produced was measured by the molybdate-Brilliant Green method (Ishikuro et al. 1991).

Immobilized Metal Ion Affinity Chromatography (IMAC)

IMAC analysis was performed as described by Ueki et al. (2009), using 0.1 M solutions of copper chloride (Cu2+), cobalt sulfate (Co2+), magnesium chloride (Mg2+), vanadyl sulfate (VO2+), and zinc chloride (Zn2+), all 99.9% pure from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical. Chelating Sepharose FF resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to assess the metal-binding capacity of FP or MBP. The resin (0.4 mL) was washed with distilled water, exposed to the various metals, and equilibrated with binding buffer (100 mM NaCl, 20 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.0). Purified FP or MBP was added to the resin, followed by mixture rotation for 30 min at room temperature. Non-bound proteins were removed via centrifugation, and metal-binding proteins were eluted with chelate buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.3, 100 mM NaCl, 25 mM EDTA, pH 8.0). SDS-PAGE was used to reveal all non- and metal-binding proteins.

Vanadate Reductase Activity

The vanadate (V5+) reductase activities of FP and MBP were measured using the modified perphenazine (PP) method (Mohamed and El-Shahat 2000). This spectrophotometrically assesses the decrease in vanadate and formation of vanadyl ions. Each 750-µL reaction mixture, containing 5 µM FP, 5 mM V5+, 1 mM NADH, and 20 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.0), was incubated at 20 °C for 30 min followed by 25-fold dilution with 20 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.0) to a final V concentration of 200 µM. The diluted mixture (750 µL) was mixed with 2 M phosphoric acid and 1 mM perphenazine. Enzyme activity was measured via ultraviolet–visible (UV-vis) spectrophotometry at 532 nm after a 30-min incubation at 20 °C. Each sample absorbance was that after subtraction of the control absorbance. MBP was used to prepare a calibration curve from 0 to 5 µM V5+. The specific activity was the concentration of the V4+ product (mol) divided by the protein concentration (mol) per min. Vanadate reductase activity can be expressed as a single enzyme unit.

FP and MBP activities were kinetically studied using the Michaelis-Menten equation and the double-reciprocal (Lineweaver-Burk) plot at four different substrate concentrations in 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0). The V concentration was 0.625–12.5 mM. The Michaelis constant (Km) and maximum velocity (Vmax) were calculated via linear regression of the double-reciprocal plot derived from the Michaelis-Menten equation (Ueki et al. 2014; Paul et al. 2021).

Various divalent metal ions (Mg2+, Zn2+, Co2+, and Cu2+; all 5 µM) were added to the reaction medium and the assays proceeded as described above. A mixture without any metal ion served as a control. Again, MBP was used for comparative purposes. The specific activity was the V4+ product level (mol) divided by the protein concentration (mol).

We previously reported the isolation of V-accumulating bacteria from the intestine of a V-rich ascidian Ascidia sydneiensis samea (Romaidi and Ueki 2016). We found that one strain, Shewanella sp. S-RA-6, exhibited V reductase activity in a crude extract (Table 1). The highest activity was that of a membrane fraction treated by the detergent NP-40. We used anion exchange column chromatography to purify the putative reductase as a 61-kDa protein (Fig. 1).

V Resistance and Accumulation Assays for Shewanella sp. strain HU-ET00281

As mentioned in the Methods, the original Shewanella sp. strain S-RA-6 was lost, thus we sought similar bacteria. Three candidate marine strains were identified in a bacterial collection isolated from plastic films that had been immersed in the Mukaishima area of the Seto Inland Sea in Japan. We successfully isolated a gene fragment encoding the AsnRS protein from strain HU-ET00281. No genes encoding the AspRS protein were isolated from the bacterial strains examined.

We then confirmed that this strain was indeed similar to the original strain S-RA-6. Both strains were V-resistant to 10 mM and grew equally well on both standard and marine agar with or without V. HU-ET00281 accumulated up to 258 ng/mg dw V5+ and 60 ng/mg dw V4+ in standard medium. We thus decided to continue study of the V5+ reductase from this strain; the corresponding gene was named Sh-asnRS, as an AsnRS from a Shewanella sp.

Nucleotide and Amino Acid Sequences of Sh-AsnRS

The coding region of the Sh-asnRS gene was 1,398 bp in length, encoding 466 amino acids. The nucleotide sequence was registered in the DDBJ/NCBI/GenBank databases under accession number LC929984. The theoretical molecular mass was 52.4 kDa and the pI was 4.93. The calculated molecular weight differed from the apparent molecular mass of 61 kDa determined by SDS-PAGE; the reason is unclear.

The Sh-asnRS amino acid sequence was compared with those of other bacteria. The similarity was 99.78–32.87%. The new Sh-asnRS gene was very similar to those of strain S-RA-6 (99.78%; Romaidi & Ueki, unpublished) and Shewanella kaireitica (Accession WP_248975555.1). Sh-asnRS shared 77.90%, 77.68%, 58.26%, 57.27%, and 32.87% sequence identities with asnRS genes from Ent-AsnRS (Enterobacteriaceae, WP_000117881.1), Eco-AsnRS (Escherichia coli, WP_000117895.1), Ssa-AsnRS (Synechocystis salina, WP_194019526.1), Mae-AsnRS (Microcystis aeruginosa, WP_024969948.1), and Tht-AsnRS (Thermus thermophilus, WP_011172806.1), respectively (Fig. 2).

The three-dimensional structure of the Sh-AsnRS protein was predicted by Phyre2. The predicted structure was superimposed on that of the AsnRS of Elizabethkingia sp. (PDB ID 6pqh) (Fig. 3A). The superimposed structure exhibited structurally similar regions over 51% of the entire sequences. Sh-AsnRS contained two domains, a C-terminal catalytic domain, and an N-terminal anticodon-binding domain, connected by a small hinge. The C-terminal domain contained a cavity. Electrostatic potential analysis revealed that this cavity was more electronegative than the surrounding region (Fig. 3B).

Expression and Purification of Sh-AsnRS FP and MBP

The recombinant vector pMAL-c2x harboring the Sh-asnRS gene was transformed into E. coli KRX and the Sh-AsnRS protein was expressed as an FP with an N-terminal MBP-tag (Sh-AsnRS-MBP). The FP was expressed in—and purified from—the soluble intracellular fraction using an amylose resin column. SDS-PAGE revealed an approximate molecular weight of 97.4 kDa (Supplementary Figure S2). The MBP was that of the malE gene; the molecular weight was 45.0 kDa. Hence, the AsnRS molecular weight was 52.4 kDa, exactly as calculated.

Aminoacyl-tRNA Synthetase Activity of Sh-AsnRS

The aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase activities of Sh-AsnRS FP and MBP were spectrophotometrically determined (Fig. 4). Experiments were performed separately for Sh-AsnRS FP and MBP, and statistical significance was confirmed by the Tukey test for Sh-AsnRS FP and MBP, separately. Phosphate production was higher in experiments with Asn than in the no amino acid control, and significance was supported by the Tukey test. In experiments with Lys, phosphate production was higher than in the control, although significance was not supported by the Tukey test. Among the four experiments for MBP, significant production of phosphate was observed only in the presence of each amino acid as compared with the no amino acid control. These results imply that Sh-AsnRS can function as an AsnRS.

Metal-binding Ability of Sh-AsnRS

IMAC was used to investigate metal-binding affinity. Sh-AsnRS, a class II aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, would be expected to interact non-covalently with metal ions (Kaiser et al. 2020). We employed Cu2+, Co2+, Mg2+, Zn2+, and VO2+ ions in tests that compared the non-binding (F) and binding (B) fractions of FP and MBP. The FP bound strongly to Cu2+ and Co2+ ions, but weakly to Zn2+ ions (Fig. 5). MBP bound strongly to Cu2+ ions but weakly to Co2+ and Zn2+ ions. The FP did not interact with Mg or V ions.

Possible binding sites for divalent metal ions on Sh-AsnRS were predicted by Alphafold3 (Abramson et al. 2024) (Fig. 3C). If we assume that single ions of VO43−, Cu2+, Co2+, Mg2+, and Zn2+ coordinate simultaneously, VO43−, Mg2+, and Zn2+ ions bind to beta hairpins and connect end loops of the catalytic domain (pos 359–437), while Cu2+ and Co2+ ions bind to the joint region (pos 110–130) and helix-loop-helix structure (pos 312–349), respectively.

Vanadate Reductase Activity of Sh-AsnRS

The vanadate reductase activities of Sh-AsnRS FP and MBP were spectrophotometrically assayed using the perphenazine method. The V5+ concentration was 0.625–12.5 mM. The FP Michaelis-Menten kinetics and Lineweaver-Burk plots are shown in Fig. 6. The FP Km was 8.70 mM and the Vmax was 4.45 mol-V4+/min/mol-protein. The MBP values were Km 10.57 and Vmax 1.78 mol-V4+/min/mol-protein. The Km and Vmax values reflect the substrate binding affinity and the reduction rate, respectively. The FP exhibited both a higher substrate affinity and faster catalysis than the MBP control.

The V5+ reductase activities in the presence of various divalent metal ions are shown in Fig. 7. The specific activity was affected by Zn2+ and Co2+. That activity was 42.8 ± 9.6 mol product V4+/mol protein without any metal addition. The activity increased to 71.7 ± 14.2 and 60.6 ± 16.3 mol product V4+/mol protein on Zn2+ and Co2+ addition, respectively. No significant response was noted after Mg2+ and Cu2+ addition. The FP is sensitive only to certain metals. The MBP control activity was not significantly affected by any metal.

Discussion

Our long-term interest in vanadate reductases led to a promising discovery. These reductases, key players in terms of changing V5+ to the less toxic V4+, may significantly aid environmental bioremediation (Shi and Dalal 1990). Some studies have described V5+ reductases in V-reducing bacteria. Nitrate/nitrite reductase (NirG, NirS) exhibits such activity (Wang et al. 2024). However, these enzymes are primarily involved in reduction of nitrate (NO₃⁻) to nitrite (NO₂⁻) and then to nitrogenous compounds (NH4+, NO) (Maiti et al. 2025). The vanadate reductase activities of such enzymes from several bacteria, including Pseudomonas (Antipov et al. 1998), Halomonas sp. (Antipov and Khijniak 2016), Lactococcus (Zhang et al. 2021), and Shewanella (Wang et al. 2024), have been studied via metagenomic assembled-genomics and transcriptomic analyses (Sun et al. 2020; Yan et al. 2022; Gan et al. 2024).

Here, we show for the first time that AsnRS is a V5+ reductase. The AsnRS gene Sh-asnRS was isolated from the marine bacterium HU-ET00281, which tolerated V concentrations as high as 10 mM and accumulated V up to 258 ng/mg dw. 16 S rRNA analysis showed that the strain was a Shewanella, which tolerate, accumulate, and reduce V (Carpentier et al. 2003; Myers et al. 2004; Wang et al. 2017; Mohamed 2017).

AsnRS attaches the amino acid asparagine to the appropriate tRNA molecule. This is a crucial step in protein synthesis. Recognition of the correct tRNA ensures accurate protein translation (Rajendran et al. 2018). We here show that AsnRS also exerts non-translational functions, particularly metal reduction. AsnRS acts as a V5+ reductase; the product is V4+ (Fig. 6). Compared to other V5+ reductases, the binding affinity of Sh-AsnRS is lower than that of AsTrx1 (0.19 mM), while Sh-AsnRS requires a higher substrate concentration to attain the AsTrx1 activity level (Ueki et al. 2014). However, the reduction capacity is greater than that of AsTrx1 (0.066 mol-V4+/min/mol-Vanabin 2 protein). The kinetic parameters are affected by enzyme type and source (Alrumman et al. 2019; Qeshmi et al. 2022).

AsnRS, traditionally known for its role in protein synthesis, exerts other functions, including V5+ reduction, in turn aiding the stress response and adaptation (Lewis et al. 2024). The structural characteristics of AsnRS, an α-helical dimer, render the enzyme stable under different environmental conditions (Biswas et al. 2019). This may afford an evolutionary advantage. An organism may be able to adapt to varying physiological and ecological conditions.

The interactions of aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases—including AsnRS—with metal ions is both complex and crucial in terms of enzyme function and specificity. In previous studies, such enzymes interacted with Mg2+ (Thompson et al. 2006), as well as Cu2+, Zn2+, and Co2+ (Landro and Schimmel 1993; Rubio Gomez and Ibba 2020). Sh-AsnRS exhibits a distinct binding profile. Co2+ bound strongly but Zn2+ weakly (Fig. 5). Such interactions enhance catalytic V5+ reduction (Fig. 7). Putative binding sites for Co2+ and Zn2+ were predicted as the editing and catalytic domains, respectively (Fig. 3C), which are near the putative V5+ binding site and could therefore reasonably affect the V5+ reductase kinetics. The strong Co2+ binding implies that Co2+ may help the enzyme to assume an active conformation or enhance its capacity to catalyze V5+ reduction. By contrast, the weak Zn2+ binding may imply that Zn is not essential for activity, or that any role of Zn2+ is indirect, possibly affecting protein stability or structure, not catalysis.

Conclusion

AsnRS traditionally functions in protein synthesis, attaching asparagine to the appropriate tRNA. However, the enzyme exhibits other functions. We are the first to report a dual-function AsnRS isolated from the marine bacterium Shewanella sp. strain HU-ET00281, named Sh-AsnRS, that engaged in V5+ reduction. The Km value was 8.70 mM and the Vmax was 4.45 mol V4+/min/mol-protein. Zn2+ and Co2+ enhanced V5+ reduction by up to 75%. The enzyme may counter V contamination.
