Uniform-in-time diffusion approximations for multiscale stochastic systems Thanks: This work is supported by National Key R&D program of China (No. 2023YFA1010103) and NSFC (No. 12471140,12595282).
Longjie Xie and Xicheng Zhang

Abstract
. This paper establishes a quantitative, uniform-in-time diffusion approximation for the joint law of a broad class of fully coupled multiscale stochastic systems. We derive a precise characterization of the limiting joint distribution as a specific skew-product of the conditional equilibrium of the fast process and the homogenized law of the slow component, thereby providing a rigorous uniform-in-time formulation of the adiabatic elimination principle. The convergence rate explicitly separates the initial relaxation of the fast dynamics from the long-time homogenized evolution and depends only on the regularity of the coefficients in the slow variable. As a consequence, we obtain the first quantitative identification of the limiting stationary distribution of the original multiscale system and prove the commutativity of the limits ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 and t → ∞ t\to\infty for a large class of observables. Our framework accommodates unbounded and irregular coefficients, degenerate structures, and weakly mixing dynamics. We illustrate its scope with three applications: (i) a uniform-in-time averaging principle for fast-slow systems; (ii) a uniform Smoluchowski–Kramers approximation for degenerate Langevin systems, yielding convergence of the joint position-scaled velocity law and global-in-time asymptotics of key thermodynamic functionals (e.g., total energy, entropy production, free energy); and (iii) the first uniform-in-time periodic homogenization result for SDEs with distributional drifts. AMS 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 60H10, 60F17, 35B40, 35B30. Keywords: Diffusion approximation, uniform-in-time, averaging principle, Smoluchowski-Kramers approximation, periodic homogenization.

1.1.
Background and motivation

Multiscale stochastic differential equations (SDEs) provide a probabilistic counterpart to homogenization theory for partial differential equations (see e.g.
[
24
,
38
,
41
]
), and are essential for modeling real-world dynamical systems involving separated time scales, with a wide range of applications spanning planetary motion, molecular dynamics, climate science, neuroscience and financial mathematics. We refer the
readers to the books
[
22

42

for a comprehensive overview. In this paper, we consider the following general, fully coupled multiscale system on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{\vartheta}
:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} {\mathord{{\rm d}}}X^{\varepsilon}_{t}&=\varepsilon^{-2}b(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\varepsilon^{-1}c(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,\varepsilon^{-1}\sigma(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\quad X_{0}^{\varepsilon}=x,\\
{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}&=F(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\varepsilon^{-1}H(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,G(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\qquad\quad Y_{0}^{\varepsilon}=y,\end{aligned}\right.

where
W
is an
m
-dimensional Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, the coefficient

(b,c,\sigma):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{\vartheta}\to(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}),\qquad(F,H,G):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{\vartheta}\to(\mathbb{R}^{\vartheta},\mathbb{R}^{\vartheta},\mathbb{R}^{\vartheta}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m})

are measurable functions, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
quantifies the separation of time scales: the fast component
X^{\varepsilon}
evolves on the
\varepsilon^{-2}
scale, while the variable
Y^{\varepsilon}
is driven simultaneously by its own intrinsic dynamics and the rapid fluctuations of
X^{\varepsilon}
. This formulation is rather versatile. When
c\equiv H\equiv 0
, it reduces to the classical fast-slow setting.
More importantly, it encompasses the physically fundamental Langevin stochastic system, obtained by taking
F\equiv 0
,
G\equiv 0
,
H(x,y)=x
, and

b(x,y)=-A(y)x,\qquad c(x,y)=-\nabla_{y}U(y),\qquad\sigma(x,y)=\sigma(y),

where
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a matrix-valued friction coefficient and
U:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is a potential. This leads to

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} {\mathord{{\rm d}}}X^{\varepsilon}_{t}&=-\varepsilon^{-2}A(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})X^{\varepsilon}_{t}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t-\varepsilon^{-1}\nabla_{y}U(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,\varepsilon^{-1}\sigma(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\\
{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}&=\varepsilon^{-1}X_{t}^{\varepsilon}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t,\end{aligned}\right.

which is equivalent to the second-order stochastic system

(1.3)

\varepsilon^{2}\,\ddot{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=-\nabla_{y}U(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})-A(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})\dot{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}+\sqrt{2}\,\sigma(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})\dot{W}_{t}.

The limiting regime
\varepsilon\to 0

1.3

The classical diffusion approximation (or second-order averaging) provides an effective dynamics for the slow

Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
in (
1.1

\varepsilon\to 0
through an auxiliary Poisson equation involving the frozen fast dynamics. More precisely, for any fixed

y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
, let
X^{y}
solve the frozen equation

(1.4)

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}^{y}=b(X_{t}^{y},y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,\sigma(X_{t}^{y},y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\qquad X_{0}^{y}=x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

and denote by
\mu_{y}
its invariant measure. Under suitable assumptions, there exists a unique (centered) solution

\Phi(\cdot,y)
to the Poisson/cell equation

(1.5)

{\mathscr{L}}_{1}\Phi(x,y):=(\sigma\sigma^{*})(x,y):\nabla_{x}^{2}\Phi(x,y)+b(x,y)\cdot\nabla_{x}\Phi(x,y)=-H(x,y),

where
:
denotes matrix contraction. Define the homogenized drift and diffusion coefficients by

(1.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathcal{F}}(y)&:=\mu_{y}\big((F+\Gamma_{1})(\cdot,y)\big),\\
{\mathcal{G}}(y)&:=\mu_{y}\big(\big(GG^{*}+(\Gamma_{2}+\Gamma_{2}^{*})/2\big)(\cdot,y)\big),\end{split}

with

(1.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Gamma_{1}&:=(c\cdot\nabla_{x}+H\cdot\nabla_{y})\Phi+2\,\sigma G^{*}:\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}\Phi,\\
\Gamma_{2}&:=H\otimes\Phi+2\,(G\sigma^{*})\cdot\nabla_{x}\Phi.\end{split}

Here and throughout the paper, we employ the shorthand notation
\mu(f):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)
for the expectation of a function
f
with respect to a probability measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Then, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
converges weakly to
\bar{Y}_{t}
which solves the homogenized SDE

(1.8)

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\bar{Y}_{t}={\mathcal{F}}(\bar{Y}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,\bar{\Sigma}(\bar{Y}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}\bar{W}_{t},\qquad\bar{\Sigma}(y)\bar{\Sigma}(y)^{*}={\mathcal{G}}(y),

where
\bar{W}
is a
\vartheta

Despite the substantial literature, the available theory on diffusion approximation for fully coupled multiscale systems still exhibits several notable limitations:

(i)

Finite-time horizon:
Most convergence results are typically established only over finite time intervals
[0,T]
, leaving the crucial long-time behavior–particularly the stationary regimes–unclear. Even in the simpler averaging regime (i.e., when
c\equiv H\equiv 0
in (
1.1
)), uniform-in-time averaging principles have only been studied relatively recently under strong dissipativity and regularity assumptions on the coefficients
[
3
,
15
]
. Obviously, the uniform-in-time diffusion approximation for the general system (
1.1
) is substantially more delicate, as it requires controlling the accumulation of corrector terms and thus presents additional difficulties.

(ii)

Lack of joint law analysis:
Even within the averaging framework, prior work typically focuses solely on convergence of the marginal law of the slow variable
Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
. The limiting joint distribution of the slow and fast components, which is essential for understanding the full statistical physics of the coupled multiscale system–including key thermodynamic functionals and the structure of invariant measures (information that cannot be recovered from the marginal law of the slow variable alone)–has remained largely uncharacterized. In the specialized setting of S-K approximation, recent works
[
7
,
9
,
11
]
have studied the convergence of stationary measures, but still only for the marginal distribution of the slow component, leaving the full joint law unresolved.

(iii)

Restrictive coefficient assumptions:
Many existing proofs rely on strong boundedness, uniform ellipticity, or high regularity assumptions on the coefficients, thereby excluding important physical models such as degenerate Langevin dynamics or systems with growing coefficients and, most strikingly, highly irregular or even distributional drifts.

The first two points are particularly critical for understanding the stationary behavior of multiscale systems. As emphasized by Freidlin
[
20
]
:“while the classical result of diffusion approximation provides a good approximation to the
slow process
Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
of the multi-scale system, but how is the stationary distribution of the original system (
1.1
) related to that of the homogenized equation (
1.8
)? Statement of diffusion approximation says nothing on this relation.” To the best of our knowledge, this question has remained open in full generality.

1.2.
Main results and contributions

In this paper, we develop a
quantitative and uniform-in-time diffusion approximation for the joint law
of
(X_{t}^{\varepsilon},Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})
in the multiscale system (
1.1
) under a remarkably flexible and general framework that accommodates unbounded and irregular coefficients, degenerate structures, and weakly mixing dynamics. Our results represent a significant advance beyond classical finite-horizon theory and provide a unified route from transient homogenization to stationary asymptotics. Our core contributions are as follows:

•

Quantitative identification of the joint-law limit.
We prove that the joint law of
(X_{t}^{\varepsilon},Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})
converges uniformly in time to
a specific skew-product
\mu_{y}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x){\mathcal{L}}_{\bar{Y}_{t}}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)
(rather than an independent product), see
Theorem
1.1
. This limit rigorously captures the physical principle of adiabatic elimination: the fast component instantaneously equilibrates to its conditional distribution
\mu_{y}
given the current state of the slow variable, which itself evolves according to the homogenized dynamics
\bar{Y}_{t}
.
The explicit convergence rate comprises an initial-layer term describing the rapid relaxation of the fast motion and a main error term, which depends only on the regularity of coefficients in the slow variable. As far as we know, this constitutes the first uniform-in-time result for the joint law, even within the classical averaging regime. Moreover, this precise characterization enables the rigorous asymptotic analysis of many important physical observables that intrinsically depend on the full system state–such as total energy, entropy production rate, and free energy–globally in time (see Subsection 1.3.2).

•

Quantitative stationary-measure convergence.
As a direct consequence, we obtain a quantitative answer to the question mentioned by Freidlin
[
20
]
: the invariant measure
\nu_{\varepsilon}
of the original multiscale system converges weakly to the skew-product
\mu_{y}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\bar{\mu}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)
, where
\bar{\mu}
is the invariant measure of the homogenized equation, see
Theorem
1.4
. This provides a precise asymptotic description of the stationary regime for the multiscale system and, more importantly, establishes the commutativity of the limits
\varepsilon\to 0
and
t\to\infty
(see (
1.21
) below) for a broad class of observables. Consequently, it validates the use of the homogenized equation for long-time prediction and offers a firm foundation for multiscale numerical methods (e.g., the Heterogeneous Multiscale Method
[
19
]
) in the stationary regimes.

•

Weak regularity, mild dissipativity and degenerate structures.
Our assumptions are rather mild: coefficients may be unbounded and only weighted Hölder continuous, and crucially, no non-degeneracy of the full multiscale system is imposed, making the theory applicable to genuinely degenerate models arising in physical applications. Furthermore, our ergodicity hypotheses permit polynomial mixing rates. This is achieved through a novel set of hypotheses
(H)
that are both mathematically flexible and physically verifiable:

–

(H
0
)
basically ensures well-posedness and uniform moment bounds.

–

(H
1
)
guarantees ergodicity of the frozen fast dynamics with a weak mixing rate.

–

(H
2
)
assumes the homogenized system (
1.8
) is non-degenerate and ergodic.

A key feature of
(H
2
)
is that the non-degeneracy and dissipativity of the homogenized limit can be either inherited directly from the original slow dynamics
or transferred entirely from the corresponding properties of the fast motion via the corrector terms. This flexibility is crucial for applications where the slow dynamics alone are degenerate, see Section 6 for the degenerate Langevin system.

To state our main results and for notational convenience, we set
Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}:=(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})
and define, for
z=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,

B_{\varepsilon}(z):=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon^{-2}b(x,y)\\
F(x,y)\end{pmatrix},\quad D(z):=\begin{pmatrix}c(x,y)\\
H(x,y)\end{pmatrix},\quad\Theta_{\varepsilon}(z):=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}\sigma(x,y)\\
G(x,y)\end{pmatrix}.

Then the system (
1.1
) can be written compactly as

(1.9)

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\big[B_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{-1}D\big](Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,\Theta_{\varepsilon}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\quad Z^{\varepsilon}_{0}=z.

Notation: Polynomial weights and weighted Hölder spaces

To handle unbounded coefficients and capture moment growth, we introduce a class of polynomial weight functions. For
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define

(1.10)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{W}}_{N}:=\left\{\rho:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to(0,\infty):\ \exists\,r\in{\mathbb{R}},\,0<\inf_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\frac{\rho(x)}{1+|x|^{r}}\leqslant\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\frac{\rho(x)}{1+|x|^{r}}<\infty\right\}.

In words,
{\mathscr{W}}_{N}
consists of functions that exhibit at most polynomial growth or decay at infinity. Clearly,
{\mathscr{W}}_{N}
is closed under algebraic operations of addition, multiplication, and division: for any
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{N}
,

(1.11)

\displaystyle\rho_{1}+\rho_{2},\quad\rho_{1}\cdot\rho_{2},\quad\rho_{1}/\rho_{2}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{N}.

Furthermore, each
\rho\in{\mathscr{W}}_{N}
satisfies a local translation estimate: there exists a constant
C=C(\rho)\geqslant 1
such that for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
with
|y|\leqslant 1
,

(1.12)

C^{-1}\rho(x)\leqslant\rho(x+y)\leqslant C\rho(x).

Throughout the paper, weights in
{\mathscr{W}}_{d+\vartheta}
are denoted by
\varrho(z)
. Given
\rho\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}
or
\omega\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
,
we may view it as an element of
{\mathscr{W}}_{d+\vartheta}
via
\varrho(x,y)=\rho(x)
or
\varrho(x,y)=\omega(y)
. Conversely, for any

\varrho\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d+\vartheta}
there exist
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that

\varrho(x,y)\leqslant\rho(x)\,\omega(y),\quad(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}.

With polynomial weights at hand, we now introduce the corresponding anisotropic Hölder spaces.
Given
\alpha,\beta\geqslant 0
and weights
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
, we denote

{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}:={\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta})),\quad{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rm p}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\rm p}:=\bigcup_{\rho\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d},\ \omega\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}.

A precise definition and a discussion of the relevant properties of these spaces are provided in Section 2 below.

Hypotheses.

Our analysis is built upon three hypotheses concerning the multiscale system: the first ensures basic well-posedness and stability of the original system; the second guarantees the ergodicity of the frozen fast dynamics; and the third posits that the anticipated homogenized limit is itself a well-behaved, ergodic process. Each is formulated to be as weak as possible while still enabling a quantitative, uniform-in-time analysis.

We begin with the fundamental assumptions regarding the well-posedness and moment bounds for the original multiscale system (
1.9

(H
0
)

For each

(1.13)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}{\mathbb{E}}\,\varrho_{0}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}(z))\leqslant\varrho_{1}(z)<\infty,\quad z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
{\bf(H)}
hold. In addition, suppose that for some
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1]
,

(1.16)

\displaystyle b,\sigma,H\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rm p}{\mathbf{C}}^{1+\beta}_{\rm p},\quad c,F,G\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rm p}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\rm p}.

Then for any
\gamma\in(\beta,2)
and
(\rho_{0},\omega_{0})\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
, there exist
a weight
\varrho\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d+\vartheta}
such that for all
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}
,

\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
z=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,and every
t>0
,

(1.17)

\displaystyle\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}(z))]-{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{\varphi}(\bar{Y}_{t}(y))]\big|\leqslant\varrho(z)\Big(\varepsilon^{\beta}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}+\ell_{0}(t/\varepsilon^{2})\,\|\varphi-\bar{\varphi}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\omega_{0}}}\Big),

where
\bar{\varphi}(y):=\mu_{y}(\varphi(\cdot,y))
, with
\mu_{y}
the invariant measure of the frozen system (
1.4
),
\ell_{0}(t)
is given in (
1.14
), and
\bar{Y}_{t}(y)
solves the homogenized equation (
1.8
).

Remark 1.2
.

(i) (Convergence of the slow marginal).
If
\varphi(x,y)=\psi(y)
depends only on the slow variable, then
\bar{\varphi}=\psi
and the estimate (
1.17
) simplifies to

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\psi(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}(z))]-{\mathbb{E}}[\psi(\bar{Y}_{t}(y))]\big|\leqslant\varepsilon^{\beta}\,\varrho(z)\,\|\psi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

This provides a uniform-in-time diffusion approximation for the slow component, recovering and extending classical finite-horizon results. Notably, the coefficients can be unbounded and the convergence rate is independent of the regularity of the coefficients in the fast variable.

(ii) (Convergence of the fast marginal).
If
\varphi(x,y)=\psi(x)
depends only on the fast variable, then the result yields, for every
t>0
,

(1.18)

\displaystyle\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\psi(X_{t}^{\varepsilon}(z))]-{\mathbb{E}}[\mu_{\bar{Y}_{t}(y)}(\psi)]\big|\leqslant\varrho(z)\big(\varepsilon^{\beta}+\ell_{0}(t/\varepsilon^{2})\big)\,\|\psi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}}.

This implies that the law of
X_{t}^{\varepsilon}(z)
converges weakly to the mixture

{\mathbb{E}}[\mu_{\bar{Y}_{t}(y)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}}\mu_{\tilde{y}}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x){\mathcal{L}}_{\bar{Y}_{t}(y)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\tilde{y})

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. The presence of the term
\ell_{0}(t/\varepsilon^{2})
in (
1.18
) is natural and physically meaningful: it captures the rapid relaxation of the fast variable to its local equilibrium
\mu_{y}
on the
O(\varepsilon^{-2})
time scale, conditioned on the current state of the slow variable, which itself evolves according to the homogenized dynamics. Thus, the limit as well as the convergence rate (
1.18
) precisely describe this conditional equilibration at each macroscopic time
t
.

A standard approximation argument confirms that (
1.18
) indeed implies weak convergence for any bounded continuous function. Indeed, let
\psi
be a bounded continuous function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\psi_{n}
be the mollification of
\psi
. Then

\displaystyle\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\psi(X_{t}^{\varepsilon}(z))]-{\mathbb{E}}[\mu_{\bar{Y}_{t}(y)}(\psi)]\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\psi_{n}(X_{t}^{\varepsilon}(z))]-{\mathbb{E}}[\mu_{\bar{Y}_{t}(y)}(\psi_{n})]\big|

\displaystyle+\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\psi_{n}(X_{t}^{\varepsilon}(z))]-{\mathbb{E}}[\psi(X_{t}^{\varepsilon}(z))]\big|

\displaystyle+\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\mu_{\bar{Y}_{t}(y)}(\psi_{n})]-{\mathbb{E}}[\mu_{\bar{Y}_{t}(y)}(\psi)]\big|

\displaystyle=:I^{(1)}_{n,\varepsilon}+I^{(2)}_{n,\varepsilon}+I^{(3)}_{n}.

For
I^{(1)}_{n,\varepsilon}
, by what we have proved, for fixed
n
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}I^{(1)}_{n,\varepsilon}=0.

For
I^{(2)}_{n,\varepsilon}
, since
\psi
is continuous, we have for any
R>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{|x|\leqslant R}|\psi_{n}(x)-\psi(x)|=0.

Thus, by Chebyschev’s inequality,

\displaystyle I^{(2)}_{n,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{|x|\leqslant R}|\psi_{n}(x)-\psi(x)|+\|\psi\|_{\infty}({\mathbb{P}}(|X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(z)|>R)+{\mathbb{P}}(|X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(z)|>R))

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{|x|\leqslant R}|\psi_{n}(x)-\psi(x)|+\|\psi\|_{\infty}(2\,{\mathbb{E}}|X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(z)|^{2}/R^{2}),

which implies by (
1.13
) that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}I^{(2)}_{n,\varepsilon}=0.

For
I^{(3)}_{n}
, by the dominated convergence theorem, we clearly have

\lim_{n\to\infty}I^{(3)}_{n}=0.

Combining the above limits, we obtain

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\psi(X_{t}^{\varepsilon}(z))]-{\mathbb{E}}[\mu_{\bar{Y}_{t}(y)}(\psi)]\big|=0.

Remark 1.3
.

If one drops the ergodicity assumption
(c)
in
(H
2
)
and replaces the uniform moment bound (
1.13
) by a finite-time moment bound, the proof below can be adapted to yield a
finite-horizon convergence result. Specifically, for any fixed
T>0
, there exists a positive constant
C_{T}
such that for all
z=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
and
t\in[0,T]
,

\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}(z))]-{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{\varphi}(\bar{Y}_{t}(y))]\big|\leqslant C_{T}\cdot\varrho(z)\Big(\varepsilon^{\beta}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}+\ell_{0}(t/\varepsilon^{2})\,\|\varphi-\bar{\varphi}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\omega_{0}}}\Big).

As a direct and significant consequence of Theorem
1.1
, we obtain a quantitative resolution to Freidlin’s question regarding the asymptotic behavior of the stationary measures of multiscale systems. Namely, suppose that for each
\varepsilon>0
the original system (
1.9
) admits an invariant probability measure
\nu_{\varepsilon}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)=\nu_{\varepsilon}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)
such that for every
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}
and
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,

(1.19)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}(z))]-\nu_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\big|=0.

We show that
\nu_{\varepsilon}
converges weakly to the skew-product
\mu_{y}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\bar{\mu}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
\bar{\mu}
is the invariant measure of the homogenized system (
1.8
).

Theorem 1.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
and (
1.19
),
for each
\gamma\in(\beta,2)
and
(\rho_{0},\omega_{0})\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
,
there exists a
C>0
such that for every

\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

(1.20)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d+\vartheta}}\varphi(x,y)\,\nu_{\varepsilon}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varphi(x,y)\,\mu_{y}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\,\bar{\mu}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\bigg|\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\beta}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

Remark 1.5
.

(i) The skew-product structure of the limiting stationary measure is a precise realization of the adiabatic elimination principle in the stationary regime: the fast degrees of freedom have been completely integrated out, leaving a homogenized dynamics for the slow variables alone, but with statistical information about the fast scale encoded in the conditional measure
\mu_{y}
.

(ii) From a theoretical perspective, this result demonstrates the commutativity of the limits
\varepsilon\to 0
and
t\to\infty
for a large class of observables:

(1.21)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{t\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}(z))]=\lim_{t\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}(z))].

This property is far from obvious given the complex nature of the multiscale coupling. It thus validates the use of reduced models for long-time prediction, and provides a firm foundation for multiscale numerical methods, such as the Heterogeneous Multiscale Method (see e.g.
[
19
]
), when applied to systems in their stationary regime.

Proof.

Fix
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}
and let

\bar{\varphi}(y):=\mu_{y}(\varphi(\cdot,y))
.
For any
t>0
and
z=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
, we write

\displaystyle\big|\nu_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)-\bar{\mu}(\bar{\varphi})\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\big|\nu_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)-{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}(z))]\big|+\big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\bar{\varphi}(\bar{Y}_{t}(y))\big]-\bar{\mu}(\bar{\varphi})\big|

\displaystyle\quad+\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}(z))]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[\bar{\varphi}(\bar{Y}_{t}(y))\big]\big|.

By (
1.15
) and the uniform estimate (
1.17
), the second and third terms are bounded by

\bar{\ell}(t)\omega_{1}(y)\|\bar{\varphi}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\omega_{0}}}+\varrho(z)\Big(\varepsilon^{\beta}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}+\ell_{0}(t/\varepsilon^{2})\|\varphi-\bar{\varphi}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\omega_{0}}}\Big).

Taking the limit
t\to\infty
in the above estimate and using (
1.19
), together with the facts that
\bar{\ell}(t)\to 0
and
\ell_{0}(t/\varepsilon^{2})\to 0
, we obtain

\big|\nu_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)-\bar{\mu}(\bar{\varphi})\big|\leqslant\varrho(z)\,\varepsilon^{\beta}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

This proves (
1.20
). Indeed, it suffices to fix once and for all a reference point
z_{*}=(0,0)
at the beginning of the argument and then take
C:=\varrho(z_{*})
.
∎

1.3.
Applications

To further demonstrate the power and scope of our framework, we present several highly non-trivial applications, deriving explicit limiting dynamics and precise error bounds.

1.3.1.
Uniform-in-time averaging principle (Section 5)

In the classical averaging regime, where
c\equiv H\equiv 0
in (
1.1
), our results still provide a significant generalization. In this setting, the homogenization correction naturally vanishes, and the effective coefficients reduce to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}(y)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}GG^{*}(x,y)\,\mu_{y}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x),\quad{\mathcal{F}}(y)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}F(x,y)\,\mu_{y}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x).

Thus, the ellipticity of
{\mathcal{G}}
and the dissipativity of
{\mathcal{F}}
required in hypothesis
(H
2
)
follow directly from the corresponding properties of
G
and
F
in the original system. We immediately obtain a uniform convergence for the joint law of the system under general dissipativity and regularity assumptions, thereby superseding standard finite-horizon results that concern only the convergence of the slow marginal.

1.3.2.
Uniform Smoluchowski–Kramers approximation with state-dependent friction (Section 6)

A critical application of our joint-law analysis is the derivation of a uniform-in-time S-K limit for the degenerate Langevin system (
1.3
) with a state-dependent, possibly non-symmetric friction matrix
A(y)
. We overcome the strict degeneracy of the slow equation to prove that the joint position-scaled velocity system converges uniformly to a homogenized manifold with explicit, closed-form expressions for the effective macroscopic dynamics. Critically, the precise description of the joint law limit and the uniformity in time enable us, for the first time, to rigorously justify the asymptotic limits of key thermodynamic functionals globally in time. Specifically, we

•

provide an explicit formula for the limiting effective energy, demonstrating a generalized equipartition theorem in which the macroscopic kinetic energy is regulated by the friction matrix through the Lyapunov equation;

•

obtain an explicit limit for the scaled entropy production rate, revealing a universal macroscopic fluctuation-dissipation relation that no longer depends on the non-symmetric friction and is determined solely by the noise intensity;

•

give a formal asymptotic description of the effective free energy, where the correction is expressed by the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the local equilibrium and a reference Gaussian distribution. This clarifies how information carried by the fast degrees of freedom is renormalized at the macroscopic level.

Moreover, the above analysis extends naturally to a broader class of stochastic Hamiltonian systems with multiple time scales, where the fast dynamics retains a gradient-type or symplectic structure; see, e.g.,
[
5
,
7
]
.

1.3.3.
Periodic homogenization with distributional drifts (Section 7)

Finally, we apply our methodology to a genuinely highly singular regime: periodic homogenization where the fast drift is a distribution. Specifically, we consider the operator

{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}f(x)=\Delta f(x)+[\varepsilon^{-1}b+c](x/\varepsilon)\cdot\nabla f(x),

where
b\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
possesses strictly negative Hölder regularity
\alpha\in(-1,-1/2)
and is divergence-free (
\nabla\cdot b=0
). In this deeply singular regime, the product
b\cdot\nabla
is classically ill-defined via standard paraproducts.
By integrating a periodic Zvonkin-type transformation directly into our multiscale framework, we algebraically eliminate the singularity. We prove that the effective macroscopic drift and diffusion, computed for the regularized transformed system, accurately govern the deeply singular original process, yielding an explicit uniform-in-time convergence rate of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
. To our knowledge, this is the first uniform-in-time homogenization result for stochastic systems driven by distributional drifts.

1.4.
Overview of the proof and organization of the paper

The proof strategy is based on a novel decomposition of the joint-law error into three contributions: an effective slow-motion error, a conditional-equilibrium error for the fast component, and an initial-layer boundary term. Uniform-in-time control for the convergence of the slow process and for the deviation of the fast component from its local equilibrium relies on new regularity estimates for parameter-dependent Poisson equations and infinite-horizon Kolmogorov equations in weighted Hölder spaces, together with long-time decay estimates for derivatives of the associated semigroups (Theorems 3.2 and 3.4), which are of independent interest. These ingredients yield uniform fluctuation bounds for the original multiscale system (
1.1
) (Lemmas 4.2 and 4.3).
Moreover, in stark contrast to works that rely exclusively on exponential derivative decay to derive uniform-in-time convergence results in settings such as Euler schemes and averaging limits
[
3
,
15
,
16
,
35
,
36
]
, we show that algebraic (polynomial) decay already suffices for a uniform-in-time diffusion approximation.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce weighted Hölder spaces. Section 3 is devoted to parameter-dependent Poisson and Kolmogorov equations with unbounded coefficients. Section 4 contains the core proof of the main theorems. Sections 5, 6, and 7 present the three applications detailed above.

Throughout the paper,
C
denotes a generic constant whose value may change from line to line. We write
A\lesssim_{C}B
to mean
A\leqslant CB
for some inessential constant
C>0
.

2.
Preliminaries: Banach-valued weighted Hölder spaces

This section introduces the functional framework that supplies our analysis. To handle coefficients with polynomial growth and to quantify regularity in the fast and slow variables separately, we introduce the weighted anisotropic Hölder spaces. We define these spaces, establish their basic properties, and provide the necessary mollification estimates, which will be used for handling low-regularity coefficients in later sections.

Let
{\mathcal{O}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be an open set and
{\mathbb{B}}
a Banach space. For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we denote by
{\mathbf{C}}^{k}({\mathcal{O}};{\mathbb{B}})
the space of
k
-times Fréchet differentiable
{\mathbb{B}}
-valued functions on
{\mathcal{O}}
equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{k}({\mathcal{O}};{\mathbb{B}})}:=\|f\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{O}};{\mathbb{B}})}+\cdots+\|\nabla^{k}f\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{O}};{\mathbb{B}})}<\infty.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the Hölder space
{\mathbf{C}}^{k+\alpha}({\mathcal{O}};{\mathbb{B}})
is defined by

\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{k+\alpha}({\mathcal{O}};{\mathbb{B}})}:=\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{k}({\mathcal{O}};{\mathbb{B}})}+[\nabla^{k}f]_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}({\mathcal{O}};{\mathbb{B}})}<\infty,

where

[\nabla^{k}f]_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}({\mathcal{O}};{\mathbb{B}})}:=\sup_{x,y\in{\mathcal{O}},x\neq y}\frac{\|\nabla^{k}f(x)-\nabla^{k}f(y)\|_{\mathbb{B}}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}.

We also use
{\mathbf{C}}^{k+\alpha}_{\mathord{{\rm loc}}}({\mathcal{O}};{\mathbb{B}})
to denote the local Hölder space:

{\mathbf{C}}^{k+\alpha}_{\mathord{{\rm loc}}}({\mathcal{O}};{\mathbb{B}}):=\Big\{f:\ f\phi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{k+\alpha}({\mathcal{O}};{\mathbb{B}}),\ \forall\phi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\infty}_{c}({\mathcal{O}};{\mathbb{R}})\Big\}.

Recall the class
{\mathscr{W}}_{d}
in (
1.10
). For
\rho\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}
and
\alpha\geqslant 0
, we define the weighted Hölder space

{\mathbf{C}}_{\rho}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathbb{B}}):=\left\{f\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\mathord{{\rm loc}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathbb{B}}):\ \|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathbb{B}})}:=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}(B_{1}(x);{\mathbb{B}})}}{\rho(x)}<\infty\right\},

where
B_{1}(x)
is the Euclidean ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
centered at
x
with radius
1
.
When there is no confusion, we write

{\mathbf{C}}_{\rho}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{B}}):={\mathbf{C}}_{\rho}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathbb{B}}),\quad{\mathbf{C}}_{\rho}^{\alpha}:={\mathbf{C}}_{\rho}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathbb{R}}).

A key feature of this localization-based definition is its compatibility with difference operators. For
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, define the translation operator
\tau_{h}f(x):=f(x+h)
and the first-order difference

{\mathcal{D}}_{h}f(x):=\tau_{h}f(x)-f(x).

Then, for suitable
f,g
, we have the product rule

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}_{h}(fg)={\mathcal{D}}_{h}f\cdot\tau_{h}g+f\cdot{\mathcal{D}}_{h}g.

The following lemma provides a convenient characterization of the weighted Hölder norm via finite differences and establishes a product estimate.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Then

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\rho}^{k+\alpha}({\mathbb{B}})}\asymp\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\rho}^{k}({\mathbb{B}})}+\sup_{|h|\leqslant 1}\frac{\|{\mathcal{D}}_{h}\nabla^{k}f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\rho}^{0}({\mathbb{B}})}}{|h|^{\alpha}}.

Moreover, for any
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all

f\in{\mathbf{C}}^{k+\alpha}_{\rho_{1}}({\mathbb{B}})
and
g\in{\mathbf{C}}^{k+\alpha}_{\rho_{2}}({\mathbb{B}})
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{k+\alpha}_{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}({\mathbb{B}})}\leqslant C\,\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{k+\alpha}_{\rho_{1}}({\mathbb{B}})}\,\|g\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{k+\alpha}_{\rho_{2}}({\mathbb{B}})}.

Proof.

The equivalence (
2.2
) follows from the standard characterization of Hölder norms via difference quotients, applied to
\nabla^{k}f
and combined with the weight normalization over unit balls. The product estimate (
2.3
) is a consequence of the Leibniz rule for
\nabla^{k}(fg)
, the rule (
2.1
) for the difference operator, and the elementary weight bound
\rho_{i}(x+h)\lesssim\rho_{i}(x)
for
|h|\leqslant 1
(see (
1.12
)), where
i=1,2
.
∎

Due to the low regularity assumptions on the coefficients, we will need some smoothing arguments. Let
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be a symmetric probability density, and set

\phi_{n}(x):=n^{d}\phi(nx),\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

For a function
f\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})
, we define its mollification by

f_{n}(x):=f*\phi_{n}(x):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x-y)\phi_{n}(y)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y.

The following lemma quantifies the approximation and smoothing properties of this operation in the weighted setting.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant\gamma\leqslant 2
,
there is a constant
C=C(d,\alpha,\gamma,\phi)>0
such that for all
f\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|f_{n}-f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})}\leqslant Cn^{\alpha-\gamma}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})},

and for all
f\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\|f_{n}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})}\leqslant Cn^{\gamma-\alpha}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})}.

Proof.

We sketch the main ideas; the presence of the weight
\rho
is handled by using
\rho(x-y)\lesssim\rho(x)
for
|y|\leqslant 1
and the compact support of
\phi
.

Step 1: estimate in
{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})
.

By symmetry and the fact that
\int\phi_{n}=1
, we have

f_{n}(x)-f(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big(f(x-y)-f(x)\big)\phi_{n}(y)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y.

If
f\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})
with
\gamma\in(0,1]
, then

\|f(x-y)-f(x)\|_{\mathbb{B}}\lesssim|y|^{\gamma}\rho(x)\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})}
, hence

\frac{\|f_{n}(x)-f(x)\|_{\mathbb{B}}}{\rho(x)}\lesssim\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})}\int|y|^{\gamma}\phi_{n}(y)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y\lesssim n^{-\gamma}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})}.

For
\gamma>1
, one expands to order
\lfloor\gamma\rfloor
and uses the vanishing moments of the symmetric kernel up to order
1
(or, more generally, Taylor’s theorem plus the cancellation coming from
\int y\,\phi_{n}(y)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y=0
) to obtain the same scaling
n^{-\gamma}
in
{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho}
.

Step 2: estimate Hölder seminorms.

Using the commutation
{\mathcal{D}}_{h}f_{n}=({\mathcal{D}}_{h}f)*\phi_{n}
and the characterization (
2.2
) (with
k=\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
when
\alpha\notin{\mathbb{N}}
), one reduces (
2.4
) to the
{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho}
-bound applied to
\nabla^{k}{\mathcal{D}}_{h}f
:

\|{\mathcal{D}}_{h}\nabla^{k}(f_{n}-f)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})}\lesssim n^{-(\gamma-k)}\|{\mathcal{D}}_{h}\nabla^{k}f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma-k}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})}\lesssim n^{-(\gamma-k)}|h|^{\alpha-k}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})}.

Dividing by
|h|^{\alpha-k}
and taking
\sup_{|h|\leqslant 1}
yields
(
2.4
).

Step 3: smoothing estimate.

For (
2.5
), note that for any integer
m,k\geqslant 0
,

\nabla^{m+k}f_{n}=\nabla^{k}f*\nabla^{m}\phi_{n},\quad\|\nabla^{m}\phi_{n}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim n^{m}.

Thus
\|\nabla^{m+k}f_{n}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})}\lesssim n^{m}\|\nabla^{k}f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho}({\mathbb{B}})}
. For fractional
\gamma
, apply (
2.2
) to
\nabla^{\lfloor\gamma\rfloor}f_{n}
and use

{\mathcal{D}}_{h}\nabla^{\lfloor\gamma\rfloor}f_{n}=f*{\mathcal{D}}_{h}\nabla^{\lfloor\gamma\rfloor}\phi_{n},\quad\|{\mathcal{D}}_{h}\nabla^{\lfloor\gamma\rfloor}\phi_{n}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim(n|h|)^{\gamma-\lfloor\gamma\rfloor}\,n^{\lfloor\gamma\rfloor}.

Combining these bounds gives (
2.5
).
∎

Our analysis involves functions that depend on both the fast variable
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and the slow variable
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
, often with different regularity exponents in each direction. To capture this anisotropy, let
\omega\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
and
\beta\geqslant 0
. Taking
{\mathbb{B}}={\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta})
, we consider functions
f(x,\theta)
belonging to mixed Hölder spaces

{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}\big({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta})\big).

By swapping the order of variables and unfolding the definitions (all norms are taken on unit balls), we obtain an equivalent norm:

(2.6)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}\big({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta})\big)}\asymp\|\tilde{f}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}\big({\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta};{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\big)},\quad\text{where}\quad\tilde{f}(\theta,x):=f(x,\theta).

For brevity, we write

{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}:={\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}\big({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta})\big)={\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}\big({\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta};{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\big)={\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}.

In particular,
{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}
can be viewed as an anisotropic Hölder space on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
.

The following interpolation result is often useful. It shows that a function which is Hölder in each variable separately enjoys a mixed Hölder regularity of any intermediate order.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
and
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
. If

f\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\omega}\cap{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}
, then for any
\theta\in[0,1]
,

f\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\theta\alpha}_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}^{(1-\theta)\beta}_{\omega}.

Moreover, the mixed second-order increment satisfies that for all
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
|z|\leqslant 1
and
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
with
|h|\leqslant 1
,

\|\Delta_{z,h}f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho\omega}}\lesssim|z|^{\theta\alpha}|h|^{(1-\theta)\beta},

where

\Delta_{z,h}f(x,y):=f(x+z,y+h)-f(x+z,y)-f(x,y+h)+f(x,y).

Proof.

Fix
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
with
|z|\leqslant 1
and
|h|\leqslant 1
. Using the
y
-Hölder regularity (uniformly in
x
) and (
1.12
), we obtain

\displaystyle|\Delta_{z,h}f(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|f(x+z,y+h)-f(x+z,y)|+|f(x,y+h)-f(x,y)|

\displaystyle\lesssim|h|^{\beta}\rho(x+z)\omega(y)+|h|^{\beta}\rho(x)\omega(y)\lesssim|h|^{\beta}\rho(x)\omega(y).

Similarly, using the
x
-Hölder regularity (uniformly in
y
), we have

\displaystyle|\Delta_{z,h}f(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|f(x+z,y+h)-f(x,y+h)|+|f(x+z,y)-f(x,y)|

\displaystyle\lesssim|z|^{\alpha}\rho(x)\omega(y+h)+|z|^{\alpha}\rho(x)\omega(y)\lesssim|z|^{\alpha}\rho(x)\omega(y),

Combining these two estimates yields

|\Delta_{z,h}f(x,y)|\lesssim(|z|^{\alpha}\wedge|h|^{\beta})\,\rho(x)\omega(y).

Finally, for any
a,b\geqslant 0
and
\theta\in[0,1]
, we have the elementary inequality
{\mathord{{\rm min}}}(a,b)\leqslant a^{\theta}b^{1-\theta}
. Applying this with
a=|z|^{\alpha}
and
b=|h|^{\beta}
gives

|\Delta_{z,h}f(x,y)|\lesssim|z|^{\theta\alpha}|h|^{(1-\theta)\beta}\rho(x)\omega(y).

This yields the claimed mixed bound.
∎

3.
Parameter-dependent Poisson and Kolmogorov equations

This section develops the core analytic tools for our analysis: regularity estimates for Poisson and infinite-horizon Kolmogorov equations whose coefficients depend on a parameter. We establish two main results: (i) regularity of the solution to the Poisson equation with respect to the parameter (see Theorem
3.2
), and (ii) regularity and long-time decay estimates for the parameter-dependent infinite-horizon Kolmogorov equation (see Theorem
3.5
). These estimates are of independent interest and may find applications in other problems involving parameter-dependent stochastic dynamics, such as sensitivity analysis, numerical schemes or other limit theorems for multiscale SDEs.

3.1.
Parameterized SDE and standing assumptions

To develop a unified framework that captures the dependence on the parameter of the frozen system, we consider a family of SDEs indexed by a parameter
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X^{\theta}_{t}=b_{\theta}(X^{\theta}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_{\theta}(X^{\theta}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\quad X^{\theta}_{0}=x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

where
W_{t}
is an
m
-dimensional standard Brownian motion and

(b,\sigma):{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to\big({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\big)

are measurable functions of
(\theta,x)
.
Throughout this section, we impose the following set of assumptions.

(A
{}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
)

For some
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\beta\in(0,2)
, we have

b,\sigma\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\rm p}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rm p}={\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\rm p}({\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta};{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rm p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})).

Moreover, the following conditions hold:

(a)

For any
T>0
and
\rho_{0}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}
, there exist
(\rho_{1},\omega_{1})\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
and
(x,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\rho_{0}(X^{\theta}_{t}(x))\leqslant\omega_{1}(\theta)\rho_{1}(x).

(b)

There exist
\lambda,\Lambda\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta+d}
such that for all
(\theta,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\lambda_{\theta}(x)|\xi|^{2}\leqslant|\sigma^{*}_{\theta}(x)\xi|^{2}\leqslant\Lambda_{\theta}(x)|\xi|^{2},\quad\forall\,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(c)

For each
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
, the process
X^{\theta}_{t}(x)
admits a unique invariant probability
measure
\mu_{\theta}
. Furthermore, for any
\rho_{0}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}
, there exist

(\omega,\rho)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{d}
and a decreasing function
\ell_{0}

with
\int^{\infty}_{0}t\ell_{0}(t){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t<\infty

such that for any
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{0}}
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{t}\varphi-\mu_{\theta}(\varphi)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho}}\leqslant\ell_{0}(t)\,\omega(\theta)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{0}}},\ \ \forall(t,\theta)\in(1,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta},

where
{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{t}\varphi(x):={\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(X^{\theta}_{t}(x))].

From (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), we immediately obtain a bound on the invariant measure: for any
\rho_{0}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}
there exists
\omega\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that for any
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{0}}
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle|\mu_{\theta}(\varphi)|\leqslant\omega(\theta)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{0}}}.

The generator of (
3.1
) is given by

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}f=(\sigma_{\theta}\sigma_{\theta}^{*}):\nabla^{2}_{x}f+b_{\theta}\cdot\nabla_{x}f.

Since
\mu_{\theta}
is invariant for
{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}
, we have
{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}^{*}\mu_{\theta}=0
and hence

(3.5)

\mu_{\theta}({\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}\varphi)=0\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{2}_{\rho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\text{ and }\rho\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}.

3.2.
Poisson equation on the whole space with a parameter

In this subsection, we study the following Poisson equation on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
:

(3.6)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}u_{\theta}(x)=f_{\theta}(x)-\mu_{\theta}(f_{\theta})=:\widetilde{f}_{\theta}(x),

where
\theta
is a parameter, and the source term
f
belongs to an anisotropic Hölder space:
for some
\gamma\geqslant 0
and
(\rho_{0},\omega_{0})\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
,

(3.7)

\displaystyle f\in{\mathbf{C}}_{\omega_{0}}^{\gamma}{\mathbf{C}}_{\rho_{0}}^{\alpha},\quad\text{i.e.}\quad f_{\theta}(\cdot)\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}\ \text{and}\ \theta\mapsto f_{\theta}\ \text{is }\gamma\text{-H\"{o}lder in }{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}.

Under assumption
(A
{}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
)
and by
[
48
, Theorem 5.1]
, equation (
3.6
) admits a unique solution which is given by

(3.8)

\displaystyle u_{\theta}(x):=-\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{t}\widetilde{f}_{\theta}(x)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t.

We first recall the following Schauder estimate from
[
48
, Theorem 5.2]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume
(A
{}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
)
and (
3.7
) hold. Then there exist weights
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that for all
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\|u_{\theta}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha}_{\rho}}\leqslant\omega(\theta)\|f_{\theta}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}}.

We now prove the main result of this subsection: the regularity of
\theta\mapsto\mu_{\theta}(f_{\theta})
and
\theta\mapsto u_{\theta}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume
(A
{}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
)
and (
3.7
) hold. For any
(\rho_{0},\omega_{0})\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
and
\gamma\in[0,\beta]
,
there exist weights
\bar{\omega}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
and
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that

(3.10)

\displaystyle\|\mu_{\cdot}(f_{\cdot})\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\bar{\omega}}}\leqslant\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\omega_{0}}^{\gamma}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}},

and

(3.11)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega}{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha}_{\rho}}\leqslant\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\omega_{0}}^{\gamma}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}}.

Proof.

By interpolation, it suffices to consider
\gamma=\beta
. We split the proof into two cases.

(i) Case 1:
\beta\in(0,1]
.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}
denote the difference operator in the
\theta
-variable:

{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}g(\theta):=\tau_{h}^{\theta}g(\theta)-g(\theta),\quad\tau_{h}^{\theta}g(\theta):=g(\theta+h).

Applying
{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}
to (
3.6
) and using the product rule (
2.1
) (in the
\theta
-variable) yields

(3.12)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}\widetilde{f}_{\theta}={\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}({\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}u_{\theta})={\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta})+({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta})\,\tau^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}.

Integrating against
\mu_{\theta}
and using (
3.5
) gives

\mu_{\theta}({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}\widetilde{f}_{\theta})=\mu_{\theta}\big(({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta})\,\tau^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}\big).

Since
\widetilde{f}_{\theta}=f_{\theta}-\mu_{\theta}(f_{\theta})
, we obtain the key identity

(3.13)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}(\mu_{\theta}(f_{\theta}))=\mu_{\theta}({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}f_{\theta})-\mu_{\theta}\big(({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta})\,\tau^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}\big).

Moreover, note that

(3.14)

\displaystyle({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta})\,\tau^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}={\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}(\sigma_{\theta}\sigma_{\theta}^{*}):\nabla^{2}_{x}u_{\theta+h}+({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}b_{\theta})\cdot\nabla_{x}u_{\theta+h}.

Using
b,\sigma\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\rm p}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rm p}
, the difference characterization (
2.2
),
and the Schauder estimate (
3.9
), we obtain weights
(\rho_{1},\omega_{1})\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that for all
|h|\leqslant 1
and
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,

\|({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta})\,\tau^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{1}}}\leqslant|h|^{\beta}\,\omega_{1}(\theta)\,\|f_{\theta+h}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}}.

Inserting this bound into (
3.13
) and using (
3.4
) yields, for some
\bar{\omega}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
,

|{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}(\mu_{\theta}(f_{\theta}))|\leqslant|h|^{\beta}\,\bar{\omega}(\theta)\,\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}},

which is exactly (
3.10
) for
\gamma=\beta\in(0,1]
.

Next, from (
3.12
) we rewrite

(3.15)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta})={\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}\widetilde{f}_{\theta}-({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta})\,\tau^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}=:g^{h}_{\theta}.

By construction and (
3.5
),
\mu_{\theta}(g^{h}_{\theta})=0
.
Using again (
3.14
), (
2.2
), (
3.9
), (
3.10
) (already proved) and

b,\sigma\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\rm p}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rm p}
,
we obtain weights
(\rho_{g},\omega_{g})\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that
for all
|h|\leqslant 1
and
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,

\|g^{h}_{\theta}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{g}}}\leqslant|h|^{\beta}\,\omega_{g}(\theta)\,\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}}.

Applying (
3.9
) to the Poisson equation
{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}v=g^{h}_{\theta}
(with
\mu_{\theta}(g^{h}_{\theta})=0
) gives, for some
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
,

\|{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha}_{\rho}}\leqslant|h|^{\beta}\,\omega(\theta)\,\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}},

which yields (
3.11
) for
\gamma=\beta\in(0,1]
by the definition of
{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}
in the
\theta
-variable.

(ii) Case 2:
\beta\in(1,2)
.

Fix
i\in\{1,\dots,\vartheta\}
and let
e_{i}
be the
i
-th coordinate vector. Taking
h=\varepsilon e_{i}
in (
3.13
), dividing by
\varepsilon
,
and letting
\varepsilon\to 0
(the limit is justified by the continuity of
\theta\mapsto u_{\theta}
established in Case 1 and dominated convergence), we obtain

\partial_{\theta_{i}}\big[\mu_{\theta}(f_{\theta})\big]=\mu_{\theta}(\partial_{\theta_{i}}f_{\theta})-\mu_{\theta}\big((\partial_{\theta_{i}}{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta})u_{\theta}\big)=:\mu_{\theta}(g_{\theta}),

where

g_{\theta}:=\partial_{\theta_{i}}f_{\theta}-(\partial_{\theta_{i}}{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta})u_{\theta}.

Using
b,\sigma\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\rm p}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rm p}
and (
3.9
), we infer that

g\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta-1}_{\omega_{g}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{g}}
for some weights
(\rho_{g},\omega_{g})
, with

\|g\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta-1}_{\omega_{g}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{g}}}\leqslant\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}}.

Applying Case 1 to the map
\theta\mapsto\mu_{\theta}(g_{\theta})
(now with exponent
\beta-1\in(0,1]
) yields the

{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta-1}
-regularity of
\partial_{\theta_{i}}[\mu_{\theta}(f_{\theta})]
, hence
\mu_{\cdot}(f_{\cdot})\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}
and (
3.10
) follows.

For (
3.11
), as above, differentiate (
3.15
) in
\theta_{i}
to get

{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}(\partial_{\theta_{i}}u_{\theta})=\partial_{\theta_{i}}\widetilde{f}_{\theta}-(\partial_{\theta_{i}}{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta})u_{\theta}=:g_{\theta},\quad\mu_{\theta}(g_{\theta})=0,

and then repeat the difference-quotient argument of Case 1 with
\partial_{\theta_{i}}u_{\theta}
in place of
u_{\theta}
.
This gives
\partial_{\theta_{i}}u\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta-1}_{\omega}{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha}_{\rho}
, and therefore
u\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha}_{\rho}
, establishing (
3.11
).
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Theorem
3.2
provides a systematic regularity transfer mechanism: from the known regularity of the Poisson solution
u_{\theta}
in the
x
-variable (as provided by Lemma
3.1
), we deduce its regularity with respect to the parameter
\theta
, as well as the regularity of the averaged quantity
\mu_{\theta}(f_{\theta})
. Beyond the present multiscale context, such parameter-dependent estimates are valuable in sensitivity analysis for SDEs (e.g., computing derivatives of invariant measures with respect to parameters) and in the numerical analysis of multiscale methods, where the corrector must be approximated as a function of the slow variable.

3.3.
Infinite-horizon Kolmogorov equation with a parameter

We now turn to study the regularity of
{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{t}\varphi_{\theta}(x)
with respect to parameter
\theta
.
Note that the function
u_{\theta}(t,x):={\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{t}\varphi_{\theta}(x)
solves the following Kolmogorov equation on
[0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
:

(3.16)

\partial_{t}u_{\theta}(t,x)={\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}u_{\theta}(t,x),\quad u_{\theta}(0,x)=\varphi_{\theta}(x).

We first recall the following fundamental estimate from
[
48
, Theorems 1.5 and 4.11]
, which quantifies the decay of the semigroup in Schauder estimate.

Theorem 3.4
.

Under
(A
{}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
)
, for any

\gamma\in[0,\alpha]
,
\delta\in(\gamma,1]
and
\rho_{0}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}
, there exist

(\rho_{1},\omega_{1})\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
and
\ell
with
\int_{0}^{\infty}(1\vee t)\ell(t){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t<\infty

such that for all

\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,

(3.17)

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{t}\varphi-\mu(\varphi)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\gamma}_{\rho_{1}}}\leqslant\ell(t)\,\omega_{1}(\theta)\,\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}},\quad t>0,\ \theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}.

We now prove the
\theta
-regularity for
{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{t}\varphi_{\theta}
when
\beta\in(0,1]

and
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}
, which will be crucial in Section 4 for proving the convergence of the joint law.

Theorem 3.5
.

Assume
(A
{}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
)
holds with
\beta\in(0,1]
.
For any
\gamma\in[0,\alpha]
,
\delta\in(\gamma,1]
and
(\rho_{0},\omega_{0})\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
, there
exist weights
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that for all
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}
and all
t>0
,

(3.18)

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{T}}^{\cdot}_{t}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\cdot}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}},

where
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta}(x):=\varphi_{\theta}(x)-\mu_{\theta}(\varphi_{\theta})
, and

(3.19)

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{T}}^{\cdot}_{t}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\cdot}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\gamma}_{\rho}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Big(\ell(t/2)+\int_{t}^{\infty}\ell(s)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\Big)\,\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}},

where
\ell
is the same as in (
3.17
).

Proof.

In the following, for simplicity of notation,
we always use
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
to denote generic polynomial weights,
which may change from line to line.

For
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}
, set

u_{\theta}(t,x):={\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{t}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta}(x).

Using (
3.17
) with
\varphi=\varphi_{\theta}
, we obtain that for some
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
,

\|u_{\theta}(t)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\gamma}_{\rho}}\leqslant\ell(t)\,\omega(\theta)\,\|\varphi_{\theta}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}},\ \ t>0,\ \theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}.

Applying
{\mathcal{D}}_{h}^{\theta}
to (
3.16
), we have

\partial_{t}{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}-{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}=({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta})\,\tau^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}=:f^{h}_{\theta}.

Note that

({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta})\psi={\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}(\sigma_{\theta}\sigma_{\theta}^{*}):\nabla_{x}^{2}\psi+({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}b_{\theta})\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi.

By
b,\sigma\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\rm p}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rm p}
, the product rule,
and shrinking/enlarging weights if needed,
there exist weights
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}

such that for all
|h|\leqslant 1
,
t>0
and
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,

(3.20)

\displaystyle\|f^{h}_{\theta}(t)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}}\leqslant|h|^{\beta}\,\omega(\theta)\,\ell(t)\,\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}}.

By Duhamel’s formula,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}(t)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{t}({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta})+\int_{0}^{t}{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{t-s}f^{h}_{\theta}(s)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle=:{\mathscr{I}}_{1}(t)+{\mathscr{I}}_{2}(t)+{\mathscr{I}}_{3}(t),

where

\displaystyle{\mathscr{I}}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle:={\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{t}({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta})-\mu_{\theta}({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta}),

\displaystyle{\mathscr{I}}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle:=\int^{t}_{0}\Big({\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{t-s}f^{h}_{\theta}(s)-\mu_{\theta}(f^{h}_{\theta}(s))\Big){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

\displaystyle{\mathscr{I}}_{3}(t)

\displaystyle:=\mu_{\theta}({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta})+\int^{t}_{0}\mu_{\theta}(f^{h}_{\theta}(s)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

(i) Estimate of
{\mathscr{I}}_{1}(t)
.

From (
3.3
) and (
3.10
),

\|{\mathscr{I}}_{1}(t)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{1}}}\leqslant\omega(\theta)\,\|{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{0}}}\leqslant|h|^{\beta}\,\omega(\theta)\,\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}}.

Similarly, by (
3.17
) and (
3.10
),

\|{\mathscr{I}}_{1}(t)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\gamma}_{\rho_{1}}}\leqslant\ell(t)\,\omega(\theta)\,\|{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}}\leqslant|h|^{\beta}\,\ell(t)\,\omega(\theta)\,\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}}.

(ii) Estimate of
{\mathscr{I}}_{2}(t)
.

Using (
3.3
) and (
3.20
),

\displaystyle\|{\mathscr{I}}_{2}(t)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{1}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}\ell(t-s)\omega(\theta)\,\|f^{h}_{\theta}(s)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho}}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\leqslant|h|^{\beta}\,\omega(\theta)\,\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ell(s)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

Similarly, by (
3.17
),

\|{\mathscr{I}}_{2}(t)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\gamma}_{\rho_{1}}}\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}\ell(t-s)\,\omega(\theta)\,\|f^{h}_{\theta}(s)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho}}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\leqslant|h|^{\beta}\,\omega(\theta)\,\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}}\int_{0}^{t}\ell(t-s)\ell(s)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

Since
\ell
is non-increasing,

\int_{0}^{t}\ell(t-s)\ell(s)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=\left(\int^{t/2}_{0}+\int^{t}_{t/2}\right)\ell(t-s)\ell(s)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\leqslant 2\,\ell(t/2)\int_{0}^{\infty}\ell(s)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

and hence

\|{\mathscr{I}}_{2}(t)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\gamma}_{\rho_{1}}}\leqslant|h|^{\beta}\,\omega(\theta)\,\ell(t/2)\,\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}}.

(iii) Estimate of
{\mathscr{I}}_{3}(t)
.

Using the Poisson representation (
3.8
) with
f=\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta}
, i.e.,

{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}U_{\theta}=-\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta}\quad\text{with}\quad U_{\theta}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{s}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

and the identity (which is justified by (
3.3
))

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{s}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=\lim_{T\to\infty}\Big({\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{T}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta}-\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta}\Big)=-\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta}

we obtain

\mu_{\theta}({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\theta})=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu_{\theta}\big({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}({\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}u_{\theta}(s))\big)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu_{\theta}\big(f^{h}_{\theta}(s)\big)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

where we used
\mu_{\theta}({\mathscr{L}}_{\theta}({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}(s)))=0
and
({\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}{\mathscr{L}}_{\theta})\tau^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}(s)=f^{h}_{\theta}(s)
. Consequently,

{\mathscr{I}}_{3}(t)=-\int_{t}^{\infty}\mu_{\theta}\big(f^{h}_{\theta}(s)\big)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

and therefore, by (
3.20
),

|{\mathscr{I}}_{3}(t)|\leqslant\int_{t}^{\infty}|\mu_{\theta}(f^{h}_{\theta}(s))|\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\leqslant|h|^{\beta}\,\omega(\theta)\,\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}}\int_{t}^{\infty}\ell(s)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

Collecting the bounds for
{\mathscr{I}}_{1},{\mathscr{I}}_{2},{\mathscr{I}}_{3}
and dividing by
|h|^{\beta}
gives that for
some
\omega\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
and all
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
and
t>0
,

\sup_{|h|\leqslant 1}\|{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}(t)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{1}}}/|h|^{\beta}\leqslant\omega(\theta)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}},

and

\sup_{|h|\leqslant 1}\|{\mathcal{D}}^{\theta}_{h}u_{\theta}(t)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\gamma}_{\rho_{1}}}/|h|^{\beta}\leqslant\omega(\theta)\Big(\ell(t/2)+\int_{t}^{\infty}\ell(s)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\Big)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\delta}_{\rho_{0}}}.

These are exactly (
3.18
)–(
3.19
) by the characterization (
2.2
) in the
\theta
-variable.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Since
\int^{\infty}_{0}(1\vee t)\ell(t){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t<\infty
, by integration by parts it is easy to see that

\int^{\infty}_{0}\left(\ell(t/2)+\int_{t}^{\infty}\ell(s)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t<\infty.

This integrability will be crucial in Section 4 for controlling the relaxation of the fast variable to its conditional equilibrium.

4.
Uniform-in-time diffusion approximation for multi-scale systems

This section is devoted to the proof of our main result, Theorem
1.1
. The argument proceeds in two stages. First, we establish a uniform-in-time diffusion approximation for the slow variable
Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
alone (see Theorem
4.1
).
This step relies on regularity estimates for parameter-dependent Poisson equations in the fast variable developed in Subsection 3.2, together with long-time decay estimates for the semigroup corresponding to the homogenized equation. Second, we extend this convergence to the joint process

Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}=(X_{t}^{\varepsilon},Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})
by a novel decomposition, and carefully handle the initial layer–in which the fast variable relaxes to its local equilibrium–using the infinite-horizon parameter-dependent Kolmogorov equation estimates from Subsection 3.3.

Recall the compact form (
1.9
) of the multiscale system, and we always write
z=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
.
For a function
\psi:[0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that is
C^{1}
in time and
C^{2}
in space, and satisfies appropriate growth conditions, Itô’s formula yields

(4.1)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\,\psi(t,Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})=\psi(0,z)+\int^{t}_{0}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[(\partial_{s}\psi+{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}\psi)(s,Z^{\varepsilon}_{s})\Big]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

where

{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}\psi:=(B_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{-1}D)\cdot\nabla_{z}\psi+\Theta_{\varepsilon}\Theta^{*}_{\varepsilon}:\nabla^{2}_{z}\psi

is the generator of the joint process. Expanding the coefficients according to system (
1.1
), we decompose

(4.2)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-1}{\mathscr{L}}_{0}+\varepsilon^{-2}{\mathscr{L}}_{1}+{\mathscr{L}}_{2},

with

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{0}\psi

\displaystyle:=D\cdot\nabla_{z}\psi+2(\sigma G^{*}):\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}\psi,

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{1}\psi

\displaystyle:=(\sigma\sigma^{*}):\nabla_{x}^{2}\psi+b\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi,

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{2}\psi

\displaystyle:=(GG^{*}):\nabla_{y}^{2}\psi+F\cdot\nabla_{y}\psi.

We emphasize that
{\mathscr{L}}_{1}
acts only on the fast variable
x
, whereas
{\mathscr{L}}_{2}
acts only on the slow variable
y
.

Throughout the remainder of this section, we adopt the convention that
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
denote generic polynomial weights whose precise values may change from line to line. This convention applies to all lemmas and proofs that follow.

4.1.
Convergence of the slow variable

The main result of this subsection is the following uniform-in-time diffusion approximation for the slow component.

Theorem 4.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, for any
\omega_{0}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
and any
\gamma\in(\beta,1]
,
there exists a weight
\varrho\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d+\vartheta}
such that for every

\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}({\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta})
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and
z=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}(z))-{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(\bar{Y}_{t}(y))\Big|\leqslant\varrho(z)\varepsilon^{\beta}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}},

where
\bar{Y}_{t}(y)
solves the homogenized limit (
1.8
).

In the following, we always work under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
.

4.1.1.
Estimates for the homogenized coefficients and semigroup

We first prepare two preparatory results concerning the homogenized system (
1.8
). The following lemma establishes the basic regularity of the corrector and the effective coefficients.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{G}}
and
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
be defined by (
1.6
)-(
1.7
), respectively.
Then there exist weights
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega},\quad{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{G}}\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}),

and
{\mathcal{G}}(y)
is symmetric and positive semidefinite for every
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
.

Proof.

(i) regularity of
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
.

Recall that
\Phi
solves the Poisson equation (
1.5
).
By Theorem
3.2
and the equivalence (
2.6
), we have
\Phi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha}_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}^{1+\beta}_{\omega}

for some
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
.
By definition,

\Gamma_{1}=D\cdot\nabla_{z}\Phi+2\sigma G^{*}:\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}\Phi,\quad\Gamma_{2}=H\otimes\Phi+2G\sigma^{*}\cdot\nabla_{x}\Phi.

Using the assumptions on
(b,\sigma,H,c,F,G)
, the product rule, and the closure properties of polynomial weights in
{\mathscr{W}}

(cf. (
1.11
)), we can choose
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that

\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}.

(ii) regularity of
{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{G}}
.

By definition (
1.6
),

{\mathcal{F}}(y)=\mu_{y}\big((F+\Gamma_{1})(\cdot,y)\big),\quad{\mathcal{G}}(y)=\mu_{y}\Big(\big(GG^{*}+(\Gamma_{2}+\Gamma_{2}^{*})/2\big)(\cdot,y)\Big).

By the mapping property encoded in (
3.10
), the regularity of
F,\Gamma_{1},GG^{*},\Gamma_{2}
implies

{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{G}}\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta})
for some
\omega\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
.

(iii) symmetry and positivity of
{\mathcal{G}}
.

Symmetry is immediate. To show positivity, fix
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
and define

\Phi_{\xi}(x,y):=\langle\Phi(x,y),\xi\rangle,\quad G_{\xi}(x,y):=G(x,y)^{*}\xi,\quad H_{\xi}(x,y):=\langle H(x,y),\xi\rangle.

From the Poisson equation (
1.5
), we have
{\mathscr{L}}_{1}\Phi_{\xi}=-H_{\xi}
.
A direct computation gives

{\mathscr{L}}_{1}(\Phi_{\xi}^{2})=2\Phi_{\xi}\,{\mathscr{L}}_{1}\Phi_{\xi}+2|\sigma^{*}\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\xi}|^{2}=-2H_{\xi}\Phi_{\xi}+2|\sigma^{*}\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\xi}|^{2}.

Integrating against
\mu_{y}
and using
{\mathscr{L}}_{1}^{*}\mu_{y}=0
yields

0=\mu_{y}({\mathscr{L}}_{1}(\Phi_{\xi}^{2}))=-2\mu_{y}(H_{\xi}\Phi_{\xi})+2\mu_{y}(|\sigma^{*}\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\xi}|^{2}),

hence

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mu_{y}(H_{\xi}\Phi_{\xi})=\mu_{y}(|\sigma^{*}\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\xi}|^{2})\geqslant 0.

Now compute
\langle\xi,{\mathcal{G}}(y)\xi\rangle
using (
1.6
)–(
1.7
):

\langle\xi,{\mathcal{G}}(y)\xi\rangle=\mu_{y}(|G_{\xi}|^{2})+\mu_{y}(H_{\xi}\Phi_{\xi})+2\mu_{y}(\langle G_{\xi},\sigma^{*}\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\xi}\rangle).

Combining with (
4.3
) we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\xi,{\mathcal{G}}(y)\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=\mu_{y}(|G_{\xi}|^{2})+\mu_{y}(|\sigma^{*}\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\xi}|^{2})+2\mu_{y}(\langle G_{\xi},\sigma^{*}\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\xi}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\mu_{y}(|G_{\xi}+\sigma^{*}\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\xi}|^{2})\geqslant 0.

Therefore
{\mathcal{G}}(y)
is positive semidefinite.
∎

The next lemma, which is a direct consequence of
[
48
, Corollary 4.10]
, quantifies the smoothing effect of the homogenized semigroup

(4.4)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\varphi(y):={\mathbb{E}}\varphi(\bar{Y}_{t}(y)),\quad y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}.

Especially, the long-time estimates for the first and second derivative will be crucial for our uniform-in-time analysis.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
\gamma\in(\beta,2)
and
\omega_{0}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
,
there exist a decreasing function
\ell\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
and weights
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that for all
t>0
,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\|\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{1}}}\leqslant\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}},\quad\|\nabla_{y}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{2}}}+\|\nabla^{2}_{y}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{2}}}\leqslant\ell(t)\,\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

Remark 4.4
.

If
\gamma=\beta
, then the function
\ell(t)
behaves like
t^{-1}
as
t\to 0
and is therefore not integrable near zero; this explains the need for the strict inequality
\gamma>\beta
in our main results.

4.1.2.
A key uniform-in-time fluctuation estimate

The following lemma provides a uniform bound for integrals involving centered fluctuations of the multiscale system. This is the core technical estimate that allows us to control the error in the diffusion approximation.

Lemma 4.5
.

For
(\rho_{1},\omega_{1}),(\rho_{2},\omega_{2})\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
,
let
Q_{1}\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{1}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{1}}
and
Q_{2}\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{2}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{2}}

be
{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
-valued and
{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
-valued functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
, respectively, satisfying the centering condition

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mu_{y}(Q_{j}(\cdot,y))=0,\quad\forall\,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta},\ \ j=1,2.

For any
\omega_{0}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
and
\gamma\in(\beta,2)
,
there exist weights
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d+\vartheta}
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, all
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+\vartheta}
,
and all
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}
,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\left|{\mathbb{E}}\int^{t}_{0}(Q_{j}:\nabla^{j}_{y}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)(Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}(z)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right|\leqslant\varepsilon^{\beta}\varrho_{j}(z)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}},\quad j=1,2,

where
:
denotes the natural contraction between vectors (for
j=1
) or matrices (for
j=2
), and
\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\varphi
is given by (
4.4
).

Proof.

We provide the proof of (
4.7
) for
j=2
. The case
j=1
is completely analogous and in fact simpler.

Step 1: the Poisson corrector and mollification.

Let
U:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
solve the Poisson equation (in
x
with parameter
y
)

(4.8)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{1}U=Q_{2}.

By (
4.6
), (
2.6
) and Theorem
3.2
, there exist weights
(\rho,\omega)\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that

(4.9)

\displaystyle\|U\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha}_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}}\leqslant\|Q_{2}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{2}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{2}}}.

Fix
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}
and
t>0
.
For
s\in(0,t)
define

f(s,z):=U(z):\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi(y),\quad z=(x,y).

To justify the application of Itô’s formula (and the appearance of
\nabla_{y}^{3}
-terms below), we mollify in the
y
-variable.
Let
U_{n}
be the mollification of
U
in
y
and similarly for
\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi
.
We set

f_{n}(s,z):=U_{n}(z):(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}(y).

Since
\partial_{r}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{r}\varphi=\bar{\mathscr{L}}\,\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{r}\varphi
, we have

\partial_{s}(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)=-\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathscr{L}}\,\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi,

and therefore

\partial_{s}f_{n}(s,z)=-U_{n}(z):(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathscr{L}}\,\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}(y).

Step 2: applying Itô and isolating the main term.

Applying (
4.1
) to
\psi=f_{n}
and using the generator decomposition (
4.2
) we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}f_{n}(t,Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}(z))

\displaystyle=f_{n}(0,z)-{\mathbb{E}}\int^{t}_{0}\Big[U_{n}:(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathscr{L}}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}\Big](Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}(z)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

(4.10)

\displaystyle\quad+{\mathbb{E}}\int^{t}_{0}(\varepsilon^{-1}{\mathscr{L}}_{0}+\varepsilon^{-2}{\mathscr{L}}_{1}+{\mathscr{L}}_{2})f_{n}(s,Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}(z)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

Note that
{\mathscr{L}}_{1}
acts only in
x
, hence

{\mathscr{L}}_{1}f(s,z)=({\mathscr{L}}_{1}U)(z):\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi(y)=Q_{2}(z):\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi(y),

where we used (
4.8
).
Multiply (
4.1.2
) by
\varepsilon^{2}
and rearrange terms gives

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}\big(Q_{2}:\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi\big)(Z_{s}^{\varepsilon}(z)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\Big({\mathbb{E}}f_{n}(t,Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}(z))-f_{n}(0,z)\Big)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\int^{t}_{0}\Big[U_{n}:(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathscr{L}}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}\Big](Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}(z)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle-{\mathbb{E}}\int^{t}_{0}(\varepsilon{\mathscr{L}}_{0}+\varepsilon^{2}{\mathscr{L}}_{2})f_{n}(s,Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}(z)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}\int^{t}_{0}\big({\mathscr{L}}_{1}f-{\mathscr{L}}_{1}f_{n}\big)(s,Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}(z)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle=:{\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon,n}_{1}(t)+{\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon,n}_{2}(t)+{\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon,n}_{3}(t)+{\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon,n}_{4}(t).

Below, we bound each
{\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon,n}_{k}(t)
uniformly in
t
.

Step 3: estimate of
{\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon,n}_{1}(t)
(boundary terms).

By the mollifier estimate (
2.5
), (
4.9
) and (
4.5
), there exists
\varrho\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d+\vartheta}

and
\omega\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that

|U_{n}(z)|\lesssim\varrho(z),\quad|\nabla_{y}^{2}\psi_{n}(y)|\lesssim n^{2-\beta}\omega(y)\|\psi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}}\quad(\psi=\varphi\ \text{or}\ \psi=\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\varphi).

Consequently,

|f_{n}(t,z)|=|U_{n}(z):(\nabla_{y}^{2}\varphi)_{n}(y)|\leqslant n^{2-\beta}\varrho(z)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}},

and

|f_{n}(0,z)|=|U_{n}(z):(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\varphi)_{n}(y)|\leqslant n^{2-\beta}\varrho(z)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}}.

Using the uniform moment bound (
1.13
), we can choose
\varrho\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d+\vartheta}
so that
for all
t>0
,

|{\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon,n}_{1}(t)|\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}n^{2-\beta}\Big({\mathbb{E}}\varrho_{1}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}(z))+\varrho_{1}(z)\Big)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}}\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}n^{2-\beta}\varrho(z)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{0}}}.

Step 4: estimate of
{\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon,n}_{2}(t)
(time derivative term).

We first bound the factor involving
(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathscr{L}}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}
.
By (
2.5
),

\|(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathscr{L}}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\omega}}\lesssim n^{2-\beta}\|\bar{\mathscr{L}}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}}.

Using the coefficient regularity, (
2.3
) and (
4.5
),

\|\bar{\mathscr{L}}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}}\lesssim\|\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{2}}}\lesssim\ell(t-s)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

Therefore,

\|(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathscr{L}}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\omega}}\lesssim n^{2-\beta}\ell(t-s)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

Combining this with
|U_{n}|\lesssim\varrho_{1}
and (
1.13
), we obtain

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon,n}_{2}(t)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}n^{2-\beta}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}\int_{0}^{t}\ell(t-s){\mathbb{E}}\varrho_{1}(Z_{s}^{\varepsilon}(z)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}n^{2-\beta}\varrho(z)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}\int_{0}^{t}\ell(t-s){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}n^{2-\beta}\varrho(z)\|\ell\|_{L^{1}}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

Step 5: estimate of
{\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon,n}_{3}(t)
(terms involving
{\mathscr{L}}_{0}
and
{\mathscr{L}}_{2}
).

A direct computation of the derivatives of
f_{n}
gives that for
i=1,\dots,d
,

\partial_{x_{i}}f_{n}(s,z)=\partial_{x_{i}}U_{n}(z):(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}(y),

and for
j=1,\dots,\vartheta
,

\displaystyle\partial_{y_{j}}f_{n}(s,z)

\displaystyle=U_{n}(z):\partial_{y_{j}}(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}(y)+\partial_{y_{j}}U_{n}(z):(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}(y),

\displaystyle\partial_{y_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}f_{n}(s,z)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x_{i}}U_{n}(z):\partial_{y_{j}}(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}(y)+\partial_{y_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}U_{n}(z):(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}(y).

Using (
4.9
), (
2.5
), and (
4.5
), we get for some weights
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d+\vartheta}
,

\|\nabla_{z}f_{n}(s)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\varrho_{1}}}+\|\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}f_{n}(s)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\varrho_{1}}}\leqslant n^{1-\beta}\ell(t-s)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}},

and

\|\nabla_{y}^{2}f_{n}(s)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\varrho_{2}}}\leqslant n^{2-\beta}\ell(t-s)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

Recalling

{\mathscr{L}}_{0}f_{n}=D\cdot\nabla_{z}f_{n}+2(\sigma G^{*}):\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}f_{n}

and

{\mathscr{L}}_{2}f_{n}=GG^{*}:\nabla_{y}^{2}f_{n}+F\cdot\nabla_{y}f_{n},

we deduce (for suitable weights
\varrho_{3},\varrho_{4}
)

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{0}f_{n}(s)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\varrho_{3}}}\leqslant n^{1-\beta}\ell(t-s)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}},

and

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{2}f_{n}(s)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\varrho_{4}}}\leqslant n^{2-\beta}\ell(t-s)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

Therefore, using (
1.13
) exactly as in Step 4,

|{\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon,n}_{3}(t)|\leqslant(\varepsilon n^{1-\beta}+\varepsilon^{2}n^{2-\beta})\varrho(z)\|\ell\|_{L^{1}}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

Step 6: estimate of
{\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon,n}_{4}(t)
(mollification error in
{\mathscr{L}}_{1}
).

We estimate

{\mathscr{L}}_{1}f_{n}-{\mathscr{L}}_{1}f=({\mathscr{L}}_{1}U_{n}):(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}-({\mathscr{L}}_{1}U):\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi.

Split it as

\displaystyle|{\mathscr{L}}_{1}f_{n}-{\mathscr{L}}_{1}f|

\displaystyle\leqslant|{\mathscr{L}}_{1}U_{n}-{\mathscr{L}}_{1}U|\cdot|(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}|+|{\mathscr{L}}_{1}U|\cdot|(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}-\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi|.

By (
2.3
), (
2.4
) and the
y
-regularity in (
4.9
), we have

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{1}U_{n}-{\mathscr{L}}_{1}U\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{3}}{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\omega_{3}}}\leqslant\|U_{n}-U\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2}_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\omega}}\lesssim n^{-\beta}\|U\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2}_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{UU8}}}{{\leqslant}}n^{-\beta}\|Q_{2}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{2}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{2}}}.

Also, again by (
2.4
) and (
4.5
),

\|(\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi)_{n}-\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\omega}}\lesssim n^{-\beta}\|\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\omega_{2}}}\leqslant n^{-\beta}\ell(t-s)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

Combining these bounds yields, for some weight
\varrho_{0}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d+\vartheta}
,

|{\mathscr{L}}_{1}f_{n}(s,z)-{\mathscr{L}}_{1}f(s,z)|\lesssim n^{-\beta}\ell(t-s)\varrho_{0}(z)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

Integrating in
s
and using (
1.13
), we obtain

|{\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon,n}_{4}(t)|\lesssim n^{-\beta}\varrho(z)\|\ell\|_{L^{1}}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

Step 7: optimizing in
n
.

Combining Steps 3–6, we conclude that for all
t>0
,

\left|{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}\big(Q_{2}:\nabla_{y}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi\big)(Z_{s}^{\varepsilon}(z)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right|\leqslant\Big(\varepsilon^{2}n^{2-\beta}+\varepsilon n^{1-\beta}+n^{-\beta}\Big)\varrho(z)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}.

The optimal choice
n=\varepsilon^{-1}
balances the three error contributions and yields the desired
\varepsilon^{\beta}
rate.
∎

4.1.3.
The
H
-term and the effective operator

The next lemma handles the fluctuations involving the singular term
\varepsilon^{-1}H\cdot\nabla_{y}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi
, which is the most delicate part of the expansion.

Lemma 4.6
.

For
\omega_{0}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
and
\gamma\in(\beta,2]
,
there exists a weight
\varrho\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d+\vartheta}
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and all
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}
and
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,

(4.11)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\left|{\mathbb{E}}\int^{t}_{0}\left[\varepsilon^{-1}H\cdot\nabla_{y}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi-\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}\,\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi\right](Z^{\varepsilon}_{s}(z)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right|\leqslant\varepsilon^{\beta}\varrho(z)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}},

where, for
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
given by (
1.7
),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}f:=\mu_{y}(\Gamma_{1}(\cdot,y))\cdot\nabla_{y}f+\mu_{y}(\Gamma_{2}(\cdot,y)):\nabla^{2}_{y}f.

Proof.

The strategy parallels Lemma
4.5
, but now the corrector is the Poisson solution
\Phi
from (
1.5
).

Step 1: object decomposition.

Fix
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}
and
t>0
. For
s\in(0,t]
set

f(s,z):=\Phi(z)\cdot\nabla_{y}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t-s}\varphi(y),\quad z=(x,y),

and mollify in

4.1.4.
Proof of the slow convergence theorem

With the lemmas established above, we now proceed to give:

Proof of Theorem

Limit of the joint distribution

Proof of Theorem

5.
Uniform-in-time averaging principle

In this section, we demonstrate how our general framework applies to the classical averaging principle.
Consider the slow-fast stochastic system

\left\{\begin{aligned} {\mathord{{\rm d}}}X^{\varepsilon}_{t}&=\varepsilon^{-2}b(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,\varepsilon^{-1}\sigma(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\ &X^{\varepsilon}_{0}=x,\\
{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}&=F(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,G(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\ &Y^{\varepsilon}_{0}=y,\end{aligned}\right.

where
W
is an
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
-valued Brownian motion. This corresponds to the general framework (
1.1
) with
c\equiv 0
and
H\equiv 0
, so that the
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-1})
terms vanish in both the fast and the slow equations.

We impose the following assumptions on the coefficients.

(A
{}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
)
′

For some
\alpha,\beta\in(0,2]
, we have

b,\sigma,F,G\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rm p}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}_{\rm p}
.
Moreover, the following conditions hold.

(a)

(Fast component) Assume that one of the following holds.

(i) (Exponential ergodicity)
There exist
\lambda,\Lambda\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}
such that for all
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,

\lambda(x)|\xi|^{2}\leqslant|\sigma(x,y)\xi|^{2}\leqslant\Lambda(x)|\xi|^{2},\quad\forall\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

and for any
\kappa>0
, there exists constants
c_{\kappa},C_{\kappa}>0

\kappa|\sigma:\sigma|(x,y)+\langle x,b(x,y)\rangle\leqslant-c_{\kappa}|x|^{2}+C_{\kappa}.

(ii) (Polynomial ergodicity)
There exist constants
0<\lambda<\Lambda<\infty
such that for all
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,

\lambda|\xi|^{2}\leqslant|\sigma(x,y)\xi|^{2}\leqslant\Lambda|\xi|^{2},\quad\forall\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

and

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\sup_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}}\langle x,b(x,y)\rangle=-\infty.

(b)

(Slow component) Assume that one of the following holds.

(i) (Exponential ergodicity)
There exist
\lambda,\Lambda\in{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
such that for all
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,

\lambda(y)|\xi|^{2}\leqslant|G(x,y)\xi|^{2}\leqslant\Lambda(y)|\xi|^{2},\quad\forall\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

and for any
\kappa>0
, there exist constants
c_{\kappa},C_{\kappa}>0,m\geqslant 2
such that

\kappa|G:G|(x,y)+\langle y,F(x,y)\rangle\leqslant-c_{\kappa}|y|^{2}+C_{\kappa}(1+|x|^{m}).

(ii) (Polynomial ergodicity)
There exist constants
0<\lambda<\Lambda<\infty
such that for all
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
,

\lambda|\xi|^{2}\leqslant|G(x,y)\xi|^{2}\leqslant\Lambda|\xi|^{2},\quad\forall\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

and

\lim_{|y|\to\infty}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\langle y,F(x,y)\rangle=-\infty.

Under condition (a) of
(A
{}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
)
′
, for each
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\vartheta}
, the frozen fast equation (
1.4
)
admits a unique invariant probability measure
\mu_{y}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)

(see Examples 1.6 and 1.7 in
[
48
]
).
In this setting, the effective coefficients in (

) reduce to

{\mathcal{F}}(y)=\mu_{y}\big(F(\cdot,y)\big),\quad{\mathcal{G}}(y)=\mu_{y}\big((GG^{*})(\cdot,y)\big).

The averaged equation for the slow component is therefore

(5.1)

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\bar{Y}_{t}={\mathcal{F}}(\bar{Y}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,{\mathcal{G}}(\bar{Y}_{t})^{1/2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\bar{W}_{t},\qquad\bar{Y}_{0}=y,

where
\bar{W}
is a
\vartheta
-dimensional Brownian motion, and
{\mathcal{G}}^{1/2}
denotes the (symmetric) matrix square root.
As a result of Theorem
1.1
, we have the following uniform-in-time averaging result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume
(A
{}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
)
′
. Let
(\rho_{0},\omega_{0})\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{\vartheta}
,

,
z=(x,y)
and all

(5.2)

\displaystyle\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\varphi(X_{t}^{\varepsilon},Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})(z)\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[\bar{\varphi}(\bar{Y}_{t}(y))\big]\Big|\leqslant\varrho(z)\Big(\varepsilon^{\beta}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}+\ell_{0}(t/\varepsilon^{2})\|\varphi-\bar{\varphi}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\omega_{0}}}\Big),

where

,

solves the averaged equation (

), and the mixing rate

Remark 5.2
.

(i) Classical averaging principles are typically established on finite time intervals

(see, e.g.,
[

improves these results in several directions:

–

It allows for unbounded and irregular coefficients as well as test functions;

–

It requires only weak (polynomial) mixing properties for the dynamics;

–

It provides an explicit convergence rate that depends only on the regularity of the coefficients in the slow variable;

–

It establishes convergence for the joint law of

(ii) In the general diffusion approximation setting of Theorem

with

due to the need for solving the Poisson equation (

Proof.

We verify the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
. Under
(A
{}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
)
′

and polynomial decay under (a)

)

Moreover, under condition (b), one readily checks that either

together with the coercivity bound

Under (b)

Under (b)

6.
Uniform S-K approximation with state-dependent matrix friction

In this section, we study the small-mass limit for Langevin equations (
1.2
) with state-dependent, possibly non-symmetric friction matrices. Our goal is to derive a uniform-in-time convergence for the joint position-scaled velocity law as a direct application of Theorem
1.1
. We remark that the analysis developed below extends naturally to a broader class of stochastic Hamiltonian systems with multiple time scales, see e.g.
[
5
,
7
]
.

6.1.
The kinetic system and assumptions

We consider the second-order stochastic system

(6.1)

\varepsilon^{2}\ddot{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=-\nabla U(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})-A(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})\dot{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}+\sqrt{2}\,\sigma(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,\dot{W}_{t},\qquad(\dot{Y}_{0}^{\varepsilon},Y_{0}^{\varepsilon})=(v,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

where

U:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty),\qquad A,\sigma:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Introducing the scaled velocity

X_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon\,\dot{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon},

we rewrite (
6.1
) as the first-order system

(6.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} {\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}^{\varepsilon}&=-\varepsilon^{-2}A(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})X_{t}^{\varepsilon}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t-\varepsilon^{-1}\nabla U(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{2}\,\sigma(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\\
{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}&=\varepsilon^{-1}X_{t}^{\varepsilon}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t,\qquad(X_{0}^{\varepsilon},Y_{0}^{\varepsilon})=(\varepsilon v,y).\end{aligned}\right.

This is a particular case of the general model (
1.1
) with
\vartheta=d
and

G\equiv 0,\quad F\equiv 0,\quad H(x,y)=x,\quad b(x,y)=-A(y)x,\quad c(x,y)=-\nabla U(y).

Remark 6.1
.

Under the Smoluchowski–Kramers scaling, the natural initial datum for (
6.2
) is the scaled state

(X_{0}^{\varepsilon},Y_{0}^{\varepsilon})=(\varepsilon v,y).

Accordingly, when applying the abstract averaging theorem, the initial variable
z=(x,y)
should always be understood as the scaled initial state, namely

x=X_{0}^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon v.

We impose the following assumptions on the coefficients.

(A
{}^{\beta}_{\rm SK}
)

Let
\beta\in(0,1]
. Assume that

U,A,\sigma\in{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{p}}^{1+\beta}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.
Moreover, there exist constants
\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1},\lambda,\Lambda>0
such that for all
y,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

(6.3)

\kappa_{0}|\xi|^{2}\leqslant\langle A(y)\xi,\xi\rangle\leqslant\kappa_{0}^{-1}|\xi|^{2},\quad\lambda|\xi|^{2}\leqslant|\sigma(y)\xi|^{2}\leqslant\Lambda|\xi|^{2},\quad\|\nabla A(y)\|\leqslant\kappa_{1},

and there exist constants
c_{0},C_{U}>0
such that

(6.4)

\big\langle y,A(y)^{-1}\nabla U(y)\big\rangle\geqslant c_{0}|y|^{2}-C_{U}.

Furthermore, there exists a function
h\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};[0,\infty))
such that for some constants
\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3},C_{A},c_{1}>0
,

(6.5)

\kappa_{2}|y|^{2}\leqslant h(y)\leqslant\kappa_{3}(1+|y|^{2}),

and, with

{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y):=(A(y)^{-1})^{*}\nabla h(y)
,

(6.6)

\frac{|{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y)|}{1+|y|}+\|\nabla{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y)\|\leqslant C_{A},\qquad y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

and

(6.7)

\big\langle{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y),\nabla U(y)\big\rangle\geqslant c_{0}|y|^{2}+c_{1}U(y)-C_{U},\qquad y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Example 6.2
.

Let
\hbar:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
satisfy

\kappa_{0}\leqslant\hbar(s)\leqslant\kappa_{1},\qquad|\hbar^{\prime}(s)|\leqslant\kappa_{2},\qquad s\geqslant 0,

and set

A(y)=\hbar(|y|^{2})I_{d}.

Define

h(y):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{|y|^{2}}\hbar(s)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

Then conditions (
6.3
) and (
6.5
) hold, and we readily obtain

{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y)=y.

Hence, (
6.6
) is automatically satisfied, while (
6.7
) reduces to the standard coercivity condition

\langle y,\nabla U(y)\rangle\geqslant c_{0}|y|^{2}+c_{1}U(y)-C_{U}.

Example 6.3
.

Suppose
A(y)
satisfies (
6.3
) alongside the bound

\|\nabla A(y)\|\leqslant\frac{C}{1+|y|}.

Let
U(y)=\frac{1}{2}u(|y|^{2})
with

su^{\prime}(s)\geqslant c_{0}s+c_{1}u(s)-C_{u},\qquad s>0.

Then (
6.6
), (
6.4
), and (
6.7
) hold with the choice

h(y)=\frac{1}{2}|y|^{2}.

Indeed, observing that

\nabla h(y)=y,\qquad\nabla U(y)=u^{\prime}(|y|^{2})\,y,\qquad{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y)=(A(y)^{-1})^{*}y,

we immediately obtain

\|\nabla{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y)\|\leqslant\|(A(y)^{-1})^{*}\|+\|\nabla(A(y)^{-1})\|\,|y|\leqslant C_{A}.

Moreover, applying the uniform ellipticity condition,

\big\langle{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y),\nabla U(y)\big\rangle=u^{\prime}(|y|^{2})\big\langle A(y)^{-1}y,y\big\rangle\geqslant\kappa_{0}|y|^{2}u^{\prime}(|y|^{2}).

Thus, (
6.4
) and (
6.7
) are both satisfied.

6.2.
Frozen fast dynamics and homogenized limit

For each frozen configuration
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the frozen system (
1.4
) reduces to an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck equation

(6.8)

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}^{y}=-A(y)X_{t}^{y}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,\sigma(y)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\qquad X_{0}^{y}=x.

Lemma 6.4
.

Under
(A
{}^{\beta}_{\rm SK}
)
, for each
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, equation (
6.8
) admits the explicit solution

X_{t}^{y}(x)={\mathrm{e}}^{-tA(y)}x+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{-(t-s)A(y)}\sigma(y)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}.

The unique invariant probability measure is the Gaussian distribution

\mu_{y}={\mathscr{N}}(0,\Sigma(y)),

where
\Sigma(y)
solves the Lyapunov equation

(6.9)

A(y)\Sigma(y)+\Sigma(y)A(y)^{*}=2\,\sigma(y)\sigma(y)^{*}.

Equivalently,

(6.10)

\Sigma(y)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-sA(y)}\sigma(y)\sigma(y)^{*}{\mathrm{e}}^{-sA(y)^{*}}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

Moreover, for every polynomial weight
\rho_{0}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}
, there exist a polynomial weight
\rho_{1}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}
and a constant
C>0
such that

(6.11)

\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(X^{y}_{t}(x))]-\mu_{y}(\varphi)\big|\leqslant C{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa_{0}t}\rho_{1}(x)\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{0}}},\qquad t\geq 0,\ x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

for all
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{0}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. In particular, the centering condition holds:

\mu_{y}(H(\cdot,y))=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}x\,\mu_{y}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=0.

Proof.

The explicit formula follows from the variation-of-constants formula. By (
6.3
), the symmetric part of
A(y)
is uniformly coercive, which implies

\|{\mathrm{e}}^{-tA(y)}\|\leqslant{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa_{0}t},\qquad t\geq 0,\ y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

This guarantees the absolute convergence of the integral in (
6.10
) and the exponential stability of the frozen semigroup. The existence and uniqueness of the invariant Gaussian law are standard features of Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes, and its covariance is completely characterized by (
6.9
). Estimate (
6.11
) is a consequence of the explicit Gaussian transition formula, the exponential decay of the mean, and the uniform bounds on the covariance. The centering property holds trivially since
\mu_{y}
has zero mean.
∎

Let

{\mathscr{L}}_{1}:=(\sigma\sigma^{*})(y):\nabla_{x}^{2}-A(y)x\cdot\nabla_{x}

be the generator of the frozen SDE
6.8
.
We proceed to identify the corrector and the effective coefficients.

Lemma 6.5
.

Under
(A
{}^{\beta}_{\rm SK}
)
, the unique centered solution to the Poisson equation

{\mathscr{L}}_{1}\Phi(\cdot,y)=-H(\cdot,y)=-x

is given by

(6.12)

\Phi(x,y)=A(y)^{-1}x.

Consequently, the effective diffusion matrix and drift are

(6.13)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}(y)

\displaystyle=A(y)^{-1}\sigma(y)\sigma(y)^{*}(A(y)^{-1})^{*},

(6.14)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}(y)

\displaystyle=-A(y)^{-1}\nabla U(y)+\mathfrak{B}(y),

where
\mathfrak{B}(y)
is called the noise-induced term given componentwise by

(6.15)

\mathfrak{B}_{i}(y)=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\Sigma_{jk}(y)\,\partial_{y_{j}}(A(y)^{-1})_{ik},\qquad i=1,\dots,d.

Proof.

Since
{\mathscr{L}}_{1}x=-A(y)x
, the expression (
6.12
) is easily verified. For
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, define

\Phi_{\xi}(x,y):=\langle\Phi(x,y),\xi\rangle=\langle x,(A(y)^{-1})^{*}\xi\rangle.

Then

\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\xi}(x,y)=(A(y)^{-1})^{*}\xi,

and the abstract formula (
1.6
) for the effective covariance yields

\langle{\mathcal{G}}(y)\xi,\xi\rangle=\mu_{y}\big(|\sigma(y)^{*}\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\xi}(\cdot,y)|^{2}\big)=\big\langle A(y)^{-1}\sigma(y)\sigma(y)^{*}(A(y)^{-1})^{*}\xi,\xi\big\rangle,

which proves (
6.13
).
For the effective drift, recall that
D=(c,H)=(-\nabla U(y),x)
. A direct computation gives

\Gamma_{1}=D\cdot\nabla_{z}\Phi=c\cdot\nabla_{x}\Phi+H\cdot\nabla_{y}\Phi.

Since
\nabla_{x}\Phi=A(y)^{-1}
, the first component evaluates to

c\cdot\nabla_{x}\Phi=-A(y)^{-1}\nabla U(y).

Moreover,

\partial_{y_{j}}\Phi_{i}(x,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\partial_{y_{j}}(A(y)^{-1})_{ik}\,x_{k},

and hence

(H\cdot\nabla_{y}\Phi)_{i}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}x_{j}x_{k}\,\partial_{y_{j}}(A(y)^{-1})_{ik}(y).

Taking expectation with respect to
\mu_{y}={\mathscr{N}}(0,\Sigma(y))
, we obtain

\mu_{y}\big((H\cdot\nabla_{y}\Phi)_{i}\big)=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\Sigma_{jk}(y)\,\partial_{y_{j}}(A(y)^{-1})_{ik}.

This confirms (
6.14
) and (
6.15
).
∎

Consequently, the homogenized limit for the slow motion is described by the Itô SDE

(6.16)

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\bar{Y}_{t}={\mathcal{F}}(\bar{Y}_{t})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,{\mathcal{G}}(\bar{Y}_{t})^{1/2}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\bar{W}_{t},\qquad\bar{Y}_{0}=y.

Remark 6.6
.

If the friction matrix acts as a scalar multiplier, i.e.,
A(y)=h(y)I_{d}
, then (
6.9
) reduces symmetrically to

\Sigma(y)=\frac{\sigma(y)\sigma(y)^{*}}{h(y)},

and the effective coefficients simplify to

{\mathcal{G}}(y)=\frac{\sigma(y)\sigma(y)^{*}}{h(y)^{2}},\qquad{\mathcal{F}}(y)=-\frac{\nabla U(y)}{h(y)}-\frac{\sigma(y)\sigma(y)^{*}\,\nabla h(y)}{h(y)^{3}}.

This elegant reduction perfectly recovers the classical Smoluchowski–Kramers formulas characterizing the scalar-friction regime.

6.3.
Convergence of joint position-scaled
velocity law

Denote
Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=(X_{t}^{\varepsilon},Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
.
We can now state the main result of this section.

Theorem 6.7

(Uniform Smoluchowski–Kramers approximation)
.

Assume
(A
{}^{\beta}_{\rm SK}
)
. Let
\gamma\in(\beta,2)
,
\alpha\in(0,1]
, and let
(\rho_{0},\omega_{0})\in{\mathscr{W}}_{d}\times{\mathscr{W}}_{d}
. Then there exists a weight
\varrho\in{\mathscr{W}}_{2d}
such that, for every
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and all
t\geqslant 0
,

(6.17)

\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\varphi(Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}(z))\big]-\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\bar{\varphi}(y)\Big|\leqslant\varrho(z)\Big(\varepsilon^{\beta}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}_{\omega_{0}}}+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa_{0}t/\varepsilon^{2}}\|\varphi-\bar{\varphi}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\rho_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}^{0}_{\omega_{0}}}\Big),

where

\bar{\varphi}(y):=\mu_{y}(\varphi(\cdot,y)),\qquad\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\bar{\varphi}(y):={\mathbb{E}}\big[\bar{\varphi}(\bar{Y}_{t}(y))\big],

\mu_{y}={\mathscr{N}}(0,\Sigma(y))
is given by Lemma
6.4
, and
\bar{Y}
solves (
6.16
).

Proof.

We verify the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
.

(i) Verification of
{\bf(H_{1})}
.

Lemma
6.4
establishes the existence of the unique invariant measure
\mu_{y}
with exponential mixing rate (
6.11
), and confirms the centering condition

\mu_{y}(H(\cdot,y))=0.

The uniform ellipticity aspect of
{\bf(H_{1})}
is a direct consequence of (
6.3
).

(ii) Verification of
{\bf(H_{2})}
.

Lemma
6.5
identifies the corrector
\Phi
and the effective coefficients
{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{G}}
. Since
A,\sigma\in{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{p}}^{1+\beta}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
A
is uniformly invertible by (
6.3
), standard composition estimates imply that
A^{-1}
and
\Sigma
also belong to
{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{p}}^{1+\beta}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Consequently,
\mathfrak{B}
,
{\mathcal{F}}
, and
{\mathcal{G}}
share this regularity.
Moreover, by (
6.3
),

\kappa_{0}|\xi|\leqslant\big|A(y)^{-1}\xi\big|\leqslant\kappa_{0}^{-1}|\xi|,\qquad y,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

and combining this with (
6.13
), we obtain

\lambda\kappa_{0}^{2}|\xi|^{2}\leqslant\langle{\mathcal{G}}(y)\xi,\xi\rangle\leqslant\Lambda\kappa_{0}^{-2}|\xi|^{2},\qquad y,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Hence,
{\mathcal{G}}
is uniformly elliptic.

Next, define the Lyapunov function

W(y):=1+|y|^{2}.

Let
\bar{{\mathscr{L}}}
be the generator of (
6.16
), namely

\bar{{\mathscr{L}}}f={\mathcal{F}}\cdot\nabla f+{\mathcal{G}}:\nabla^{2}f.

Using (
6.14
) alongside (
6.4
) and the fact that
\mathfrak{B}
and
{\mathcal{G}}
are globally bounded (since
A^{-1}
,
\nabla A
, and
\Sigma
are globally bounded by our assumptions), we have for some constants
c,C>0
,

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathscr{L}}}W(y)

\displaystyle=-2\big\langle y,A(y)^{-1}\nabla U(y)\big\rangle+2\langle y,\mathfrak{B}(y)\rangle+2\operatorname{tr}{\mathcal{G}}(y)

\displaystyle\leqslant-2c_{0}|y|^{2}+2C_{U}+2|y|\|\mathfrak{B}(y)\|+2\operatorname{tr}{\mathcal{G}}(y)

\displaystyle\leqslant-c\,W(y)+C,

which implies the exponential ergodicity of the effective equation (
6.16
).

(iii) Uniform moment estimates for the slow variable.

Fix
\eta\in(0,1]
and define

V_{\varepsilon,\eta}(x,y):=\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(|x|^{2}+2U(y)\bigr)+\eta\varepsilon\langle x,{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y)\rangle+\eta h(y)+1,

where
{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y)
is given in (
6.6
).
Recall that the full generator decomposes as

{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-1}{\mathscr{L}}_{0}+\varepsilon^{-2}{\mathscr{L}}_{1},

where

{\mathscr{L}}_{0}=x\cdot\nabla_{y}-\nabla U(y)\cdot\nabla_{x},\qquad{\mathscr{L}}_{1}=(\sigma\sigma^{*})(y):\nabla_{x}^{2}-A(y)x\cdot\nabla_{x}.

We first compute the contribution from
{\mathscr{L}}_{0}
. Observe that

{\mathscr{L}}_{0}(\varepsilon^{2}|x|^{2})=-2\varepsilon^{2}\langle\nabla U(y),x\rangle,\qquad{\mathscr{L}}_{0}(2\varepsilon^{2}U(y))=2\varepsilon^{2}\langle x,\nabla U(y)\rangle,

so these two cross-terms cancel precisely. Furthermore,

{\mathscr{L}}_{0}\langle x,{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y)\rangle=(x\otimes x):\nabla{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y)-\langle{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y),\nabla U(y)\rangle,

and

{\mathscr{L}}_{0}(\eta h(y))=\eta\langle x,\nabla h(y)\rangle.

We next compute the contribution from
{\mathscr{L}}_{1}
. Noting that
{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y)=(A(y)^{-1})^{*}\nabla h(y)
, we have

\nabla_{x}V_{\varepsilon,\eta}=2\varepsilon^{2}x+\eta\varepsilon{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y),\qquad\nabla_{x}^{2}V_{\varepsilon,\eta}=2\varepsilon^{2}I_{d}.

This yields

(\sigma\sigma^{*})(y):\nabla_{x}^{2}V_{\varepsilon,\eta}=2\varepsilon^{2}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\sigma(y)\sigma(y)^{*}\bigr),

and

\displaystyle-A(y)x\cdot\nabla_{x}V_{\varepsilon,\eta}

\displaystyle=-A(y)x\cdot\Bigl(2\varepsilon^{2}x+\eta\varepsilon(A(y)^{-1})^{*}\nabla h(y)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=-2\varepsilon^{2}\langle A(y)x,x\rangle-\eta\varepsilon\langle A(y)x,(A(y)^{-1})^{*}\nabla h(y)\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\varepsilon^{2}\langle A(y)x,x\rangle-\eta\varepsilon\langle x,\nabla h(y)\rangle.

Therefore,

{\mathscr{L}}_{1}V_{\varepsilon,\eta}(x,y)=2\varepsilon^{2}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\sigma(y)\sigma(y)^{*}\bigr)-2\varepsilon^{2}\langle A(y)x,x\rangle-\eta\varepsilon\langle x,\nabla h(y)\rangle.

Combining both contributions yields

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}V_{\varepsilon,\eta}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-2\langle A(y)x,x\rangle+\eta(x\otimes x):\nabla{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y)

(6.18)

\displaystyle\quad-\eta\langle{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y),\nabla U(y)\rangle+2\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\sigma(y)\sigma(y)^{*}\bigr).

Crucially, the singular mixed term of order
\varepsilon^{-1}
cancels out exactly.

Now, (
6.3
) and (
6.6
) imply that

\big|(x\otimes x):\nabla{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y)\big|\leqslant C_{A}|x|^{2},

while (
6.7
) dictates

-\eta\langle{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y),\nabla U(y)\rangle\leqslant-\eta c_{0}|y|^{2}-\eta c_{1}U(y)+\eta C_{U}.

Substituting these bounds into (
6.18
), we find

{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}V_{\varepsilon,\eta}(x,y)\leqslant-\bigl(2\kappa_{0}-\eta C_{A}\bigr)|x|^{2}-\eta c_{0}|y|^{2}-\eta c_{1}U(y)+C.

By choosing
\eta>0
sufficiently small, we guarantee the existence of a uniform constant
c>0
such that

(6.19)

{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}V_{\varepsilon,\eta}(x,y)\leqslant-c\bigl(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}+U(y)\bigr)+C,

with constants strictly independent of
\varepsilon
.

By invoking Young’s inequality alongside (
6.6
) and (
6.5
), for
\eta>0
chosen small enough, there exist constants
0<c_{1}\leqslant c_{2}<\infty
, independent of
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, ensuring the equivalence:

(6.20)

c_{1}\bigl(1+|y|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}U(y)\bigr)\leqslant V_{\varepsilon,\eta}(x,y)\leqslant c_{2}\bigl(1+|y|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}U(y)\bigr).

Combining (
6.19
) and (
6.20
), we immediately deduce that

{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}V_{\varepsilon,\eta}(x,y)\leqslant-\delta V_{\varepsilon,\eta}(x,y)+C

for some
\delta,C>0
independent of
\varepsilon
.

Let
m\geqslant 1
. Applying the diffusion operator
{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}
to the
m
-th power yields

{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}(V_{\varepsilon,\eta}^{m})=mV_{\varepsilon,\eta}^{m-1}{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}V_{\varepsilon,\eta}+m(m-1)V_{\varepsilon,\eta}^{m-2}\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(V_{\varepsilon,\eta}),

where the
carré du champ
operator evaluates to

\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(V_{\varepsilon,\eta})=\varepsilon^{-2}\big\langle(\sigma\sigma^{*})(y)\nabla_{x}V_{\varepsilon,\eta},\nabla_{x}V_{\varepsilon,\eta}\big\rangle.

Since

\nabla_{x}V_{\varepsilon,\eta}=2\varepsilon^{2}x+\eta\varepsilon{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y),

we have

\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(V_{\varepsilon,\eta})\leqslant C\varepsilon^{-2}\bigl(\varepsilon^{4}|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}|{\mathcal{A}}_{h}(y)|^{2}\bigr)\leqslant C\bigl(\varepsilon^{2}|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}+1\bigr).

Exploiting (
6.6
) and (
6.20
), we infer that

\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(V_{\varepsilon,\eta})\leqslant C\,V_{\varepsilon,\eta}.

Consequently, utilizing Young’s inequality allows us to absorb lower-order terms, leading to

{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}(V_{\varepsilon,\eta}^{m})\leqslant-c_{m}V_{\varepsilon,\eta}^{m}+C_{m},

for suitable constants
c_{m},C_{m}>0
independent of
\varepsilon
. Dynkin’s formula and Gronwall’s lemma subsequently yield the uniform moment bounds

\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\sup_{t\geqslant 0}{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{\varepsilon,\eta}(Z_{t}^{\varepsilon})^{m}\big]\leqslant C_{m}\bigl(1+V_{\varepsilon,\eta}(z)^{m}\bigr).

In particular, this provides uniform moment bounds for the spatial slow variable:

(6.21)

\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\sup_{t\geqslant 0}{\mathbb{E}}\big[|Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{m}\big]\leqslant\widetilde{C}_{m}\bigl(1+|y|^{2}+|x|^{2}+U(y)\bigr)^{m/2}.

(iv) Uniform moment estimates for the fast variable.

To establish the uniform bounds for the fast variable
X_{t}^{\varepsilon}
, we define the unperturbed energy functional

V(x,y):=|x|^{2}+2U(y).

Applying
{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}
and utilizing the coercivity condition in (
6.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}V(x,y)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-2}\bigl(2\operatorname{tr}(\sigma(y)\sigma(y)^{*})-2\langle A(y)x,x\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant-2\kappa_{0}\varepsilon^{-2}|x|^{2}+C\varepsilon^{-2}

\displaystyle\leqslant-2\kappa_{0}\varepsilon^{-2}V(x,y)+\varepsilon^{-2}\bigl(C+4\kappa_{0}U(y)\bigr),

where we have used the definition of
V(x,y)
to substitute
|x|^{2}=V(x,y)-2U(y)
.

For
m\geqslant 1
, we apply the diffusion operator
{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}
to
V^{m}
:

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}V^{m}

\displaystyle=mV^{m-1}{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}V+m(m-1)V^{m-2}\varepsilon^{-2}\big\langle(\sigma\sigma^{*})(y)\nabla_{x}V,\nabla_{x}V\big\rangle

\displaystyle=mV^{m-1}{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}V+4m(m-1)V^{m-2}\varepsilon^{-2}\big\langle(\sigma\sigma^{*})(y)x,x\big\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant mV^{m-1}\varepsilon^{-2}\bigl[-2\kappa_{0}V(x,y)+C+4\kappa_{0}U(y)\bigr]+4m(m-1)\varepsilon^{-2}\Lambda|x|^{2}V^{m-2}

\displaystyle\leqslant-2\kappa_{0}m\varepsilon^{-2}V^{m}+\varepsilon^{-2}C_{m}V^{m-1}\bigl(1+U(y)\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant-c_{m}\varepsilon^{-2}V^{m}+\varepsilon^{-2}C_{m}^{\prime}\bigl(1+U(y)^{m}\bigr),

where we utilized
|x|^{2}\leqslant V(x,y)
to bound the second-order diffusion term, and applied Young’s inequality to absorb the mixed term
V^{m-1}U(y)
into
V^{m}
, producing strictly positive constants
c_{m},C_{m},C_{m}^{\prime}
independent of
\varepsilon
.

By Itô’s formula, taking expectations yields the differential inequality

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}}{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t}{\mathbb{E}}\big[V^{m}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}V^{m}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})\big]\leqslant-c_{m}\varepsilon^{-2}{\mathbb{E}}\big[V^{m}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})\big]+\varepsilon^{-2}C_{m}^{\prime}\bigl(1+{\mathbb{E}}\big[U(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})^{m}\big]\bigr).

Since
U\in{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{p}}^{1+\beta}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, it exhibits at most polynomial growth. By the uniform spatial moment bounds on
Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
established in (
6.21
), we deduce that
\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\sup_{t\geqslant 0}{\mathbb{E}}\big[U(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})^{m}\big]
is bounded by some polynomial weight
\widehat{C}_{K}\bigl(1+|y|^{2}+|x|^{2}+U(y)\bigr)^{K}
depending only on the initial state
z=(x,y)
. Substituting this uniform bound back into the differential inequality and integrating via Gronwall’s lemma gives

\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\sup_{t\geqslant 0}{\mathbb{E}}\big[V(Z_{t}^{\varepsilon})^{m}\big]\leqslant V(x,y)^{m}+\widetilde{C}_{m}\bigl(1+|y|^{2}+|x|^{2}+U(y)\bigr)^{K}.

Since the scaled initial state is
z=(x,y)
with
x=\varepsilon v
, the initial evaluation
V(Z_{0}^{\varepsilon})^{m}=(|x|^{2}+2U(y))^{m}=V(x,y)^{m}
exactly matches the initial energy, providing the required uniform bounds for the fast variable.

(v) Conclusion.

Combining
(iii)
and
(iv)
, we find that
{\bf(H_{0})}
is satisfied.
Thus, all assumptions of Theorem
1.1
are verified. By (
6.11
), the frozen semigroup converges to equilibrium at the exponential rate
{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa_{0}t}
, meaning the boundary-layer term in the abstract slow-fast estimate naturally takes the form

{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa_{0}t/\varepsilon^{2}}.

Applying Theorem
1.1
, we obtain the desired estimate (
6.17
).
∎

6.4.
Uniform-in-time thermodynamic approximations and physical implications

The quantitative description of the limit for the joint law of
(X_{t}^{\varepsilon},Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})
has important physical implications. Because the Smoluchowski–Kramers approximation established in Theorem
6.7
is
uniform-in-time
, it allows us to rigorously derive global-in-time asymptotics for key thermodynamic quantities such as total energy and entropy production rate, and to formulate a formal asymptotic description of the free energy. This provides, for the first time, a mathematically sharp version of adiabatic elimination for these observables beyond finite time intervals
[0,T]
and up to the limit
t\to\infty
.

To formalize this, recall that for any thermodynamic observable
\varphi(x,y)
with appropriate polynomial growth, Theorem
6.7
yields the global error bound:

(6.22)

\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\varphi(X_{t}^{\varepsilon},Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[\bar{\varphi}(\bar{Y}_{t})\big]\Big|\leqslant\varrho(z)\Big(\varepsilon^{\beta}\|\varphi\|+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa_{0}t/\varepsilon^{2}}\|\varphi-\bar{\varphi}\|\Big),\qquad\forall t\geqslant 0,

where
z=(v,y)
is the scaled initial state,
\bar{\varphi}(y)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varphi(x,y)\,\mu_{y}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)
is the local equilibrium expectation, and
\varrho(z)
is a polynomial weight.

6.4.1.
Total mechanical energy

Define the scaled mechanical energy (Hamiltonian) of the system as

{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(t):=\frac{1}{2}|X_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}+U(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}).

This quantity corresponds to
\varepsilon^{2}
times the physical kinetic energy plus the potential energy, which is purely mechanical and does not explicitly involve the friction matrix
A(y)
. The observable
\varphi(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}+U(y)
has quadratic growth, which is perfectly controlled by the uniform moment estimates established in Section
6
. Using the fact that the frozen equilibrium
\mu_{y}
is a centered Gaussian with covariance
\Sigma(y)
, the effective macroscopic energy evaluates to

\bar{{\mathcal{E}}}(y):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}+U(y)\right)\mu_{y}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\Sigma(y)\bigr)+U(y).

Theorem
6.7
then yields the following explicit uniform-in-time estimate.

Corollary 6.8

(Uniform energy approximation)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
6.7
, there exists a constant
C(z)>0
such that

(6.23)

\left|{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(t)]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[\bar{{\mathcal{E}}}(\bar{Y}_{t})\big]\right|\leqslant C(z)\Big(\varepsilon^{\beta}+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa_{0}t/\varepsilon^{2}}\Big),\qquad\forall t\geq 0.

Physical interpretation:

•

Kinetic thermalization (boundary layer):
For very short times
t={\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{2})
, the exponential term
{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa_{0}t/\varepsilon^{2}}
dominates the error. This characterizes the rapid initial transient phase where the fast velocity
X_{t}^{\varepsilon}
dynamically forgets its deterministic initial kinetic energy
\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}|v|^{2}
and thermalizes toward the local Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution conditioned on
Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
.

•

Uniform long-time tracking:
For macroscopic time
t\gg\varepsilon^{2}
, the transient boundary layer strictly vanishes. The macroscopic expected energy tracks the effective energy
\bar{{\mathcal{E}}}(\bar{Y}_{t})
uniformly up to
t\to\infty
with a steady, non-exploding accuracy of
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{\beta})
.

•

Generalized equipartition:
The effective kinetic energy
\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\Sigma(\bar{Y}_{t})\bigr)
demonstrates that the local friction matrix
A(y)
inherently regulates the macroscopic kinetic energy via the Lyapunov equation (
6.9
), providing a rigorous justification of the generalized equipartition theorem for state-dependent friction, see e.g.
[
33
]
and
[
50
, Chapter 1-2]
.

6.4.2.
Entropy production rate

For the Langevin dynamics (
6.1
) at unit temperature (
T=1
), the mean rate of entropy production in the medium (heat bath) is given by the average power dissipated by the friction force
[
14
,
44
,
45
]
:

\dot{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\varepsilon}_{\text{med}}(t):={\mathbb{E}}\big[\langle A(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})\dot{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon},\dot{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}\rangle\big].

Using the velocity scaling
\dot{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-1}X_{t}^{\varepsilon}
, we introduce the rescaled entropy production rate

\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\varepsilon}_{{\text{med}}}(t):=\varepsilon^{2}\dot{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\varepsilon}_{\text{med}}(t)={\mathbb{E}}\big[\langle A(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})X_{t}^{\varepsilon},X_{t}^{\varepsilon}\rangle\big],

which rescales the diverging physical entropy production rate to an
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
quantity that captures the macroscopic dissipation.
As a direct result of Theorem
6.7
, we have:

Corollary 6.9

(Uniform entropy production approximation)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
6.7
,

(6.24)

\left|\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\varepsilon}_{\text{med}}(t)-{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\operatorname{tr}\bigl((\sigma\sigma^{*})(\bar{Y}_{t})\bigr)\Big]\right|\leqslant\tilde{C}(z)\Big(\varepsilon^{\beta}+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa_{0}t/\varepsilon^{2}}\Big),\qquad\forall t\geqslant 0.

Proof.

Apply (
6.22
) to the quadratic observable
\varphi(x,y)=\langle A(y)x,x\rangle
, the local equilibrium expectation evaluates to

\bar{\varphi}(y)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\langle A(y)x,x\rangle\,\mu_{y}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(A(y)\Sigma(y)\bigr).

Taking the trace of the Lyapunov equation (
6.9
) and using the cyclic property of the trace (
\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma A^{*})=\operatorname{tr}(A\Sigma^{T})=\operatorname{tr}(A\Sigma)
since
\Sigma
is symmetric), we obtain the exact algebraic identity

2\operatorname{tr}\bigl(A(y)\Sigma(y)\bigr)=2\operatorname{tr}\bigl((\sigma\sigma^{*})(y)\bigr).

Thus,
\bar{\varphi}(y)=\operatorname{tr}\bigl((\sigma\sigma^{*})(y)\bigr)
.
∎

Physical interpretation:

•

The unscaled entropy production rate diverges as
\dot{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\varepsilon}_{\text{med}}(t)\sim{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-2})
for each fixed
t>0
, reflecting the intense thermal dissipation generated by the highly oscillatory fast velocities as mass vanishes.

•

Strikingly, (
6.24
) shows that the macroscopic rescaled dissipation
sheds its dependence on the non-symmetric friction matrix
A(y)
. After the thermalization layer
t\gg\varepsilon^{2}
, the steady dissipation is universally determined solely by the noise intensity
\operatorname{tr}(\sigma\sigma^{*})
, reflecting a robust energy balance in the homogenized dynamics.

6.4.3.
Commutativity of limits and steady-state thermodynamics

The uniformity in time has a profound mathematical consequence: it guarantees the commutativity of the limits
\varepsilon\to 0
and
t\to\infty
.
Suppose the homogenized diffusion
\bar{Y}_{t}
defined in (
6.16
) is ergodic with a unique invariant probability measure
\bar{\mu}
, and the original fully coupled system
Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}
admits an invariant measure
\mu_{\varepsilon}
. Taking the limit
t\to\infty
in (
6.23
), the exponential boundary layer strictly vanishes, leaving

\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\,\mu_{\varepsilon}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\bar{{\mathcal{E}}}(y)\,\bar{\mu}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\right|\leqslant{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{\beta}).

This establishes the rigorous relation

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{t\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(t)]=\lim_{t\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(t)].

Consequently, the steady-state thermodynamics of the fully coupled, highly singular multiscale system can be computed with strict bounds through the geometry of the reduced effective manifold. This provides an uncompromising theoretical justification for using macroscopic homogenized equations to predict long-time physical steady states.

6.4.4.
Asymptotic Gibbs–Shannon entropy and effective free energy

The uniform-in-time framework also provides deep insights into the structural renormalization of information. Let
\rho_{\varepsilon}(t,x,y)
denote the joint probability density of
(X_{t}^{\varepsilon},Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})
. The existence and regularity of such densities for degenerate Langevin systems have been extensively studied, we refer to
[
12
,
17
,
49
]
.
The Gibbs–Shannon information entropy is defined as
{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(t):=-{\mathbb{E}}\big[\log\rho_{\varepsilon}(t,X_{t}^{\varepsilon},Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})\big]
.

A formal free-energy discussion.

While
-\log\rho_{\varepsilon}
is a density-dependent observable and therefore does not strictly fall within the polynomially weighted test-function class of Theorem
6.7
, the uniform weak convergence established above strongly suggests the following asymptotic factorization of the density away from the initial boundary layer:

\rho_{\varepsilon}(t,x,y)\approx\mu_{y}(x)\,\bar{\rho}(t,y),\qquad t\gg\varepsilon^{2},

where
\bar{\rho}(t,y)
is the marginal density of the effective slow process
\bar{Y}_{t}
.
Under this factorization, the joint entropy formally splits into the marginal entropy of the slow variable
{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{Y}}(t):=-{\mathbb{E}}[\log\bar{\rho}(t,\bar{Y}_{t})]
and the conditional entropy of the frozen Gaussian fast variable
\mu_{y}={\mathscr{N}}(0,\Sigma(y))
. Calculating the exact differential entropy of the multidimensional Gaussian yields the asymptotic structural limit:

(6.25)

{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(t)\approx{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{Y}}(t)+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi\mathrm{e})+\frac{1}{2}\log\det\bigl(\Sigma(\bar{Y}_{t})\bigr)\right].

Defining the mean free energy as
{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(t):={\mathbb{E}}[{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(t)]-{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(t)
(see, e.g.,
[
29
,
50
]
), we combine the asymptotic expression (
6.25
) with the uniform energy approximation to find

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle\approx{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\Sigma(\bar{Y}_{t})\bigr)+U(\bar{Y}_{t})\right]-{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{Y}}(t)-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi\mathrm{e})+\frac{1}{2}\log\det\bigl(\Sigma(\bar{Y}_{t})\bigr)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathcal{F}}_{\bar{Y}}(t)+\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\Sigma(\bar{Y}_{t})\bigr)-\log\det\bigl(\Sigma(\bar{Y}_{t})\bigr)-d\right]-\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi),

where
{\mathcal{F}}_{\bar{Y}}(t):={\mathbb{E}}[U(\bar{Y}_{t})]-{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{Y}}(t)
is the standard macroscopic free energy of the reduced slow dynamics.

Physical interpretation (Kullback–Leibler renormalization):

Notice that the explicitly emergent penalty term in the free energy,

\frac{1}{2}\Big(\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\Sigma(\bar{Y}_{t})\bigr)-\log\det\bigl(\Sigma(\bar{Y}_{t})\bigr)-d\Big),

is exactly the Kullback–Leibler divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big({\mathscr{N}}(0,\Sigma(\bar{Y}_{t}))\parallel{\mathscr{N}}(0,I_{d})\big)\geqslant 0
. This reveals a deep principle: during adiabatic elimination, the statistical information of the fast degrees of freedom is not lost, but systematically renormalized into an emergent thermodynamic potential landscape characterizing the reduced macroscopic system globally in time.

7.
Periodic homogenization for SDEs with distributional drifts

In this section, we present an application of our abstract uniform-in-time diffusion-approximation framework to periodic homogenization. The key novelty of our result lies in that the highly oscillatory fast drift is allowed to be
distributional
(having strictly negative Hölder regularity), yet we rigorously extract an
explicit
convergence rate that is
uniform in time
for suitable macroscopic observables.

We first establish the geometric notation. Let
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}:={\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
denote the
d
-dimensional flat torus. For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we denote its equivalence class by

[x]:=x\pmod{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad[x]-x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.

Given a function
f:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we canonically extend it to
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by periodicity:

f(x):=f([x]),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

For
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we denote by
{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
the standard Hölder–Besov spaces defined via the Littlewood–Paley decomposition (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
).

7.1.
Periodic homogenization with Hölder coefficients

Before tackling the singular distributional regime, we first consider the setting where the coefficients are Hölder continuous. This will serve as the foundation for the more general result. Let
\varphi\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
be a bounded, measurable,
1
-periodic observable. Consider the Cauchy problem

(7.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)={\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x),\qquad(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\
&u_{\varepsilon}(0,x)=\varphi(x),\end{aligned}\right.

with
{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}
defined by

{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}f(x):=a\!\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right):\nabla_{x}^{2}f(x)+\Big[\varepsilon^{-1}b+c\Big]\!\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}f(x),\qquad a(x):=\big(\sigma\sigma^{*}\big)(x).

We impose the following structural assumptions on the periodic coefficients:

(A
α
)

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Assume that
\sigma,b,c\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
are
1
-periodic in each spatial coordinate. Furthermore, there exist constants
0<\lambda\leqslant\Lambda<\infty
such that

\lambda|\xi|^{2}\leqslant|\sigma^{*}(x)\xi|^{2}\leqslant\Lambda|\xi|^{2},\qquad\forall x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\ \forall\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Let
(X_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geqslant 0}
be the unique weak solution to the associated SDE:

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\Big[\varepsilon^{-1}b+c\Big]\!\left(\frac{X_{t}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,\sigma\!\left(\frac{X_{t}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\qquad X_{0}^{\varepsilon}=x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Then
{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}
is the generator of
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}
, and the solution to (
7.1
) admits the classical Feynman–Kac representation:

u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)={\mathbb{E}}\big[\varphi(X_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x))\big].

Consequently, studying the limiting behavior of
u_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
is equivalent to establishing the weak convergence of the stochastic process
X_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)
.

7.1.1.
Cell problem and uniform homogenization

Consider the auxiliary unperturbed fast SDE constrained to the torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
:

(7.2)

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=b(X_{t})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,\sigma(X_{t})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\qquad X_{0}=x,

whose generator is given by

{\mathscr{L}}_{0}f(x):=a(x):\nabla_{x}^{2}f(x)+b(x)\cdot\nabla_{x}f(x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}.

Under Assumption
(A
α
)
, the diffusion
X_{t}
associated with
{\mathscr{L}}_{0}
admits a unique invariant probability measure
\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)
on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and exhibits exponential ergodicity: there exist constants
C,\kappa>0
such that

(7.3)

\big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\psi(X_{t}(x))\big]-\mu(\psi)\big|\leqslant C{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa t}\|\psi\|_{\infty},\quad\forall t>0,\ \forall x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\ \forall\psi\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}).

Define the averaged drift
\bar{b}
and the centered fast drift
\widetilde{b}
by

\bar{b}:=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}b(x)\,\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\qquad\widetilde{b}(x):=b(x)-\bar{b}.

Let
\Phi:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be the unique zero-mean solution to the Poisson equation (also known as the cell problem in this setting):

{\mathscr{L}}_{0}\Phi(x)=-\widetilde{b}(x),\qquad\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\Phi(x)\,\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=0.

Standard Schauder estimates on the torus guarantee that
\Phi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, with norms depending only on
d,\alpha,\lambda,\Lambda
and the Hölder norms
\|a\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}},\|b\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}}
.
Denote by
\nabla_{x}\Phi(x)
denote the Jacobian matrix of the corrector and set

K(x):=I_{d}+\nabla_{x}\Phi(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}.

The effective macroscopic coefficients are then given by

(7.4)

{\mathcal{F}}:=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}K(x)c(x)\,\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\qquad{\mathcal{G}}:=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}K(x)a(x)K(x)^{*}\,\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}.

By uniform ellipticity,
{\mathcal{G}}
is symmetric and strictly positive definite.

Remark 7.1
.

The representation (
7.4
) provides an algebraically robust form of the effective diffusion matrix. Under additional smoothness assumptions,
{\mathcal{G}}
can be rewritten via integration by parts into several equivalent Eulerian forms (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
38
]
). For our purposes, (
7.4
) arises organically and requires no supplementary regularity.

To extract the macroscopic behavior, we define the centered slow variable:

(7.5)

Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=X_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\frac{\bar{b}}{\varepsilon}t.

The following result shows that
Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
converges uniformly in time to an effective Brownian motion with drift, with an explicit convergence rate of order
\varepsilon
.

Theorem 7.2

(Uniform-in-time periodic homogenization)
.

Assume
(A
α
)
and let
\gamma>1
. There exists a constant
C>0
(depending only on
d,\alpha,\gamma,\lambda,\Lambda
and the
{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}
-norms of
b,c,\sigma
) such that for any observable
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
, extended periodically,

(7.6)

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\varphi(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x))\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[\varphi(x+{\mathcal{F}}t+\sqrt{2{\mathcal{G}}}W_{t})\big]\Big|\leqslant C\,\varepsilon\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}.

Remark 7.3
.

Recall that
u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)={\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(X_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x))]
solves the Cauchy problem (
7.1
). Theorem
7.2
states that
u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)
satisfies, after shifting by the average drift
\bar{b}t/\varepsilon
,

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big|u_{\varepsilon}(t,x+\bar{b}t/\varepsilon)-\bar{u}(t,x)\big|\leqslant C\varepsilon\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})},

where
\bar{u}
solves the constant-coefficient homogenized PDE

\partial_{t}\bar{u}(t,x)={\mathcal{F}}\cdot\nabla_{x}\bar{u}(t,x)+{\mathcal{G}}:\nabla_{x}^{2}\bar{u}(t,x),\qquad\bar{u}(0,x)=\varphi(x).

Thus, the highly oscillatory operator
{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}
homogenizes to the constant-coefficient operator
\bar{\mathscr{L}}={\mathcal{F}}\cdot\nabla_{x}+{\mathcal{G}}:\nabla_{x}^{2}
, with an explicit
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
convergence rate uniform in time.

Proof.

Let us introduce the highly oscillatory rescaled fast variable
\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon^{-1}X_{t}^{\varepsilon}
. Then the pair
(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon},Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})
exactly satisfies the coupled system

\left\{\begin{aligned} {\mathord{{\rm d}}}\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon}&=\varepsilon^{-2}b(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\varepsilon^{-1}c(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{2}\,\sigma(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\qquad\widetilde{X}_{0}^{\varepsilon}=x/\varepsilon,\\
{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}&=c(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\varepsilon^{-1}\widetilde{b}(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,\sigma(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\qquad\quad\qquad Y_{0}^{\varepsilon}=x.\end{aligned}\right.

This corresponds to the general model (
1.1
) with

H(x,y)=\widetilde{b}(x),\qquad F(x,y)=c(x),\qquad G(x,y)=\sigma(x).

Since all coefficients are uniformly bounded and Hölder continuous on the compact state space
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
are satisfied. This yields the uniform convergence estimate (
7.6
) with rate
\varepsilon
(since
\beta=1
in this setting), the effective drift

{\mathcal{F}}=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\big(I_{d}+\nabla_{x}\Phi(x)\big)c(x)\,\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}K(x)c(x)\,\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x),

and the effective covariance matrix

{\mathcal{G}}=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\left(a+a(\nabla_{x}\Phi)^{*}+(\nabla_{x}\Phi)a+\frac{1}{2}\big(\widetilde{b}\,\Phi^{*}+\Phi\,\widetilde{b}^{*}\big)\right)\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x).

We now show that this expression reduces to (
7.4
). Applying the generator
{\mathscr{L}}_{0}
to
\Phi\Phi^{*}
and using
{\mathscr{L}}_{0}\Phi=-\widetilde{b}
yields

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{0}(\Phi\Phi^{*})

\displaystyle=({\mathscr{L}}_{0}\Phi)\Phi^{*}+\Phi({\mathscr{L}}_{0}\Phi)^{*}+2(\nabla_{x}\Phi)a(\nabla_{x}\Phi)^{*}

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{b}\,\Phi^{*}-\Phi\,\widetilde{b}^{*}+2(\nabla_{x}\Phi)a(\nabla_{x}\Phi)^{*}.

Integrating with respect to the invariant measure
\mu
and noting that
\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}{\mathscr{L}}_{0}f{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\mu=0
for any regular function
f
, we extract the exact cross-term relation:

\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\big(\widetilde{b}\,\Phi^{*}+\Phi\,\widetilde{b}^{*}\big)\,\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(\nabla_{x}\Phi)a(\nabla_{x}\Phi)^{*}\,\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x),

which simplifies
{\mathcal{G}}
to the desired form (
7.4
).
∎

7.1.2.
A functional CLT

Theorem
7.2
provides uniform-in-time estimates for one-dimensional distributions, but it does not capture the full path structure. We next strengthen this to weak convergence in the Skorokhod space
C({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, thereby establishing a functional central limit theorem for the centered process
Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
.

Let
{\mathbb{E}}^{0}
denote the expectation with respect to the law of the unperturbed diffusion
X_{t}
defined in (
7.2
). We first establish a standard 4-point correlation bound for the corresponding Markov semigroup
{\mathcal{T}}_{t}
.

Lemma 7.4

(Four-point correlation bound)
.

Let
f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
be centered such that
\mu(f)=0
. For any ordered times
0\leqslant r_{1}\leqslant r_{2}\leqslant r_{3}\leqslant r_{4}
, the unperturbed diffusion
X
satisfies

\big|{\mathbb{E}}^{0}\big[f(X_{r_{1}})f(X_{r_{2}})f(X_{r_{3}})f(X_{r_{4}})\big]\big|\leqslant C\|f\|_{\infty}^{4}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa(r_{2}-r_{1})}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa(r_{4}-r_{3})},

where
\kappa>0
is the spectral gap constant from (
7.3
).

Proof.

By the Markov property, conditioning on
{\mathcal{F}}_{r_{2}}
yields

{\mathbb{E}}^{0}\big[f(X_{r_{3}})f(X_{r_{4}})\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{r_{2}}\big]={\mathcal{T}}_{r_{3}-r_{2}}\big(f\,{\mathcal{T}}_{r_{4}-r_{3}}f\big)(X_{r_{2}})=:\Psi(X_{r_{2}}).

Because
\mu(f)=0
, the ergodicity bound (
7.3
) implies
\|{\mathcal{T}}_{r_{4}-r_{3}}f\|_{\infty}\leqslant C{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa(r_{4}-r_{3})}\|f\|_{\infty}
. Since
{\mathcal{T}}_{r_{3}-r_{2}}
acts as a contraction in
L^{\infty}
, we obtain the estimate

\|\Psi\|_{\infty}\leqslant\big\|f\,{\mathcal{T}}_{r_{4}-r_{3}}f\big\|_{\infty}\leqslant C{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa(r_{4}-r_{3})}\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}.

We now evaluate the full expectation. Since
X
is strictly stationary under the invariant measure
\mu
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{0}\big[f(X_{r_{1}})f(X_{r_{2}})\Psi(X_{r_{2}})\big]

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}f(x){\mathcal{T}}_{r_{2}-r_{1}}(f\Psi)(x)\,\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}f(x)\Big[{\mathcal{T}}_{r_{2}-r_{1}}(f\Psi)(x)-\mu(f\Psi)\Big]\,\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)+\mu(f)\mu(f\Psi).

Because
\mu(f)=0
, the second term identically vanishes. Applying (
7.3
) to the centered function
f\Psi-\mu(f\Psi)
, the remaining term is structurally bounded by

\|f\|_{\infty}\big\|{\mathcal{T}}_{r_{2}-r_{1}}\big(f\Psi-\mu(f\Psi)\big)\big\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2C\|f\|_{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa(r_{2}-r_{1})}\|f\Psi\|_{\infty}.

Using the triangle inequality bound
\|f\Psi\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|f\|_{\infty}\|\Psi\|_{\infty}\leqslant C\|f\|_{\infty}^{3}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa(r_{4}-r_{3})}
, we immediately conclude:

\big|{\mathbb{E}}^{0}\big[f(X_{r_{1}})f(X_{r_{2}})f(X_{r_{3}})f(X_{r_{4}})\big]\big|\leqslant C\|f\|_{\infty}^{4}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa(r_{2}-r_{1})}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\kappa(r_{4}-r_{3})},

completing the proof.
∎

The following result strengthens Theorem
7.2
by establishing weak convergence of the entire path of
Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
, which is essential for applications that depend on the full trajectory, such as the analysis of hitting times, occupation measures, and other path-dependent functionals, see e.g.
[
10
]
.

Theorem 7.5

(Functional CLT)
.

Assume
(A
α
)
. Then the centered process
(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geqslant 0}
defined in (
7.5
) converges weakly in the Skorokhod space
C({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
to the effective Brownian motion with drift
(x+{\mathcal{F}}t+\sqrt{2{\mathcal{G}}}W_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
.

Proof.

By the uniform convergence (
7.6
) and standard applications of the Markov property, the finite-dimensional distributions of
(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geqslant 0}
converge to those of the limiting macroscopic process. It therefore remains to prove tightness. Writing

Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}-Y_{s}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{s}^{t}c(\widetilde{X}_{r}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r+\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{s}^{t}\widetilde{b}(\widetilde{X}_{r}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r+\sqrt{2}\int_{s}^{t}\sigma(\widetilde{X}_{r}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{r},

we see that the regular drift term and the martingale term satisfy the required moment bounds immediately because
c
and
\sigma
are bounded. Thus the only nontrivial contribution is the singular centered-drift term. By Kolmogorov’s continuity criterion, it is enough to show that for any horizon
T>0
and all
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant T
,

(7.7)

\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}{\mathbb{E}}^{\varepsilon}\left[\Big|\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{s}^{t}\widetilde{b}(\widetilde{X}_{r}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\Big|^{3}\right]\leqslant C_{T}\,|t-s|^{3/2}.

Consider the macroscopically accelerated diffusion and scaled Brownian motion:

\widehat{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=\widetilde{X}_{\varepsilon^{2}t}^{\varepsilon},\qquad\widehat{W}_{t}:=\varepsilon^{-1}W_{\varepsilon^{2}t}.

The process
\widehat{X}^{\varepsilon}
solves the
\mathcal{O}(1)
-volatility SDE

(7.8)

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\widehat{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\big(b+\varepsilon c\big)(\widehat{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,\sigma(\widehat{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\widehat{W}_{t}.

Using the temporal change of variables
r=u\varepsilon^{2}
, the target integral maps to:

\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{s}^{t}\widetilde{b}(\widetilde{X}_{r}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r=\varepsilon\int_{s/\varepsilon^{2}}^{t/\varepsilon^{2}}\widetilde{b}(\widehat{X}_{u}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u.

Thus, verifying the tightness condition (
7.7
) is equivalent to showing

{\mathbb{E}}^{\varepsilon}\left[\Big|\int_{S_{0}}^{S_{1}}\widetilde{b}(\widehat{X}_{u}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u\Big|^{3}\right]\leqslant C_{T}\,\frac{|t-s|^{3/2}}{\varepsilon^{3}},

where
S_{0}=s/\varepsilon^{2}
and
S_{1}=t/\varepsilon^{2}
. Let
X
denote the unperturbed diffusion (
7.2
), with expectation operator
{\mathbb{E}}^{0}
. By Girsanov’s theorem, the law of (
7.8
) is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of
X
on
[0,S_{1}]
via the Radon–Nikodym density martingale

{\mathcal{E}}_{S_{1}}^{\varepsilon}=\exp\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2}}\int^{S_{1}}_{0}\big\langle(\sigma^{-1}c)(\widehat{X}^{\varepsilon}_{s}),{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\widehat{W}_{s}\big\rangle-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\int^{S_{1}}_{0}\big|(\sigma^{-1}c)(\widehat{X}^{\varepsilon}_{s})\big|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right\}.

Since
\sigma^{-1}
(by uniform ellipticity) and
c
are uniformly bounded, Novikov’s criterion holds uniformly in
\varepsilon
, ensuring that for every fixed
T>0
,
\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}{\mathbb{E}}^{0}[({\mathcal{E}}_{S_{1}}^{\varepsilon})^{4}]\leqslant C_{T}
. Applying Hölder’s inequality over the measure change yields

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{\varepsilon}\left[\Big|\int_{S_{0}}^{S_{1}}\widetilde{b}(\widehat{X}_{u}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u\Big|^{3}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\left({\mathbb{E}}^{0}\big[({\mathcal{E}}_{S_{1}}^{\varepsilon})^{4}\big]\right)^{1/4}\left({\mathbb{E}}^{0}\left[\Big|\int_{S_{0}}^{S_{1}}\widetilde{b}(X_{u})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u\Big|^{4}\right]\right)^{3/4}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{T}\left({\mathbb{E}}^{0}\left[\Big|\int_{S_{0}}^{S_{1}}\widetilde{b}(X_{u})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u\Big|^{4}\right]\right)^{3/4}.

Fix a coordinate
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
and set
f=\widetilde{b}_{i}
. Expanding the fourth moment algebraically yields:

{\mathbb{E}}^{0}\left[\Big|\int_{S_{0}}^{S_{1}}f(X_{u})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u\Big|^{4}\right]=4!\int_{S_{0}\leqslant r_{1}\leqslant\dots\leqslant r_{4}\leqslant S_{1}}{\mathbb{E}}^{0}\big[f(X_{r_{1}})\dots f(X_{r_{4}})\big]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r_{1}\cdots{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r_{4}.

Invoking Lemma
7.4
, the integrand decays exponentially in the time gaps. Integrating this bound over the ordered simplex yields an upper bound of order
{\mathcal{O}}((S_{1}-S_{0})^{2})
. Thus,

{\mathbb{E}}^{0}\left[\Big|\int_{S_{0}}^{S_{1}}\widetilde{b}(X_{u})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u\Big|^{4}\right]\leqslant C\,(S_{1}-S_{0})^{2}=C\left(\frac{t-s}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{2}=C\,\frac{|t-s|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}.

Substituting this kinematic bound directly back into the Hölder reduction, we obtain

{\mathbb{E}}^{\varepsilon}\left[\Big|\int_{S_{0}}^{S_{1}}\widetilde{b}(\widehat{X}_{u}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u\Big|^{3}\right]\leqslant C_{T}\left(\frac{|t-s|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right)^{3/4}=C_{T}\,\frac{|t-s|^{3/2}}{\varepsilon^{3}}.

This rigorously confirms the tightness condition (
7.7
).
∎

7.2.
Periodic homogenization with distributional fast drift

We now extend the analysis to the highly singular regime where the fast drift is
distributional
. For clarity of exposition, we take the diffusion coefficient to be the identity; the extension to uniformly elliptic
{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}
diffusions follows the same geometric transformation strategy.

We focus primarily on the deeply singular regime
\alpha\in(-1,-1/2)
. Consider the operator

{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}f(x)=\Delta_{x}f(x)+\Big[\varepsilon^{-1}b+c\Big]\!\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}f(x),

where
b
is a distribution and
c
is classically regular. We impose the following conditions:

(A
{}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
)
′′

Let
\alpha\in(-1,-1/2)
and
\beta\in(0,1)
. Assume that
b\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is divergence-free (
\nabla_{x}\cdot b=0
), and
c\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}({\mathbb{T}}^{d};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is
1
-periodic.

Remark 7.6
.

The restriction
\alpha\in(-1,-1/2)
places the system in the deeply singular regime. Here, the product
b\cdot\nabla_{x}\mathbf{u}
possesses a negative sum of regularities (
2\alpha+1-\theta\leqslant 0
) and is classically ill-defined via standard Bony paraproducts. One must exploit the divergence-free condition
\nabla_{x}\cdot b=0
to regularize the transport via commutator estimates (see, e.g.,
[
26
]
). This delicate cancellation justifies the fractional heat kernel bounds used below.

Let
(X_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geqslant 0}
be the diffusion process solving

(7.9)

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\Big[\varepsilon^{-1}b+c\Big]\!\left(\frac{X_{t}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\qquad X_{0}^{\varepsilon}=x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

The well-posedness of this singular SDE under
(A
{}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
)
′′
is established via a periodic Zvonkin transformation.

7.2.1.
Periodic Zvonkin transform

To eliminate the distributional singularity of
{\mathscr{L}}_{\varepsilon}
, we construct a steady-state Zvonkin-type spatial corrector. Consider the decoupled elliptic PDE system on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
:

(7.10)

\Delta_{x}u_{i}-\lambda u_{i}+b\cdot\nabla_{x}u_{i}=f_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,d,

where
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d})\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. In the application below we take
f=-b
, which is precisely the choice that cancels the singular drift after applying Itô’s formula.

Theorem 7.7
.

Let
\alpha\in(-1,-1/2)
and
\theta\in(0,1+\alpha)
. For any
f\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, there exists a threshold
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}(d,\alpha,\theta,\|b\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}})\geqslant 1
such that for all
\lambda\geqslant\lambda_{0}
, the elliptic equation (
7.10
) admits a unique weak solution
\mathbf{u}
satisfying

(7.11)

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha-\theta}}\leqslant C\lambda^{-\theta/2}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}},

where the constant
C
is independent of
\lambda
.

Proof.

It suffices to establish the a priori estimate (
7.11
). By Duhamel’s principle, the solution admits the resolvent representation:

\mathbf{u}(x)=\int^{\infty}_{0}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\lambda t}P_{t}\big(b\cdot\nabla_{x}\mathbf{u}+f\big)(x)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t,

where
P_{t}:={\mathrm{e}}^{t\Delta}
is the standard Gaussian heat semigroup.
Because
\alpha<-1/2
, the product
b\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}
has a negative sum of regularities and is classically undefined. However, exploiting the divergence-free condition
\nabla\cdot b=0
, the singular product is regularized via commutators. The fractional heat kernel estimates for singular divergence-free convective terms established in
[
26
, (2.16)]
give

\big\|P_{t}(b\cdot\nabla_{x}\mathbf{u})\big\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha-\theta}}\leqslant Ct^{-1+\theta/2}\|b\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha-\theta}},\qquad t>0.

Integrating against the resolvent yields

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha-\theta}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int^{\infty}_{0}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\lambda t}\Big(\big\|P_{t}(b\cdot\nabla_{x}\mathbf{u})\big\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha-\theta}}+\big\|P_{t}f\big\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha-\theta}}\Big)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t

\displaystyle\leqslant C\int^{\infty}_{0}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\lambda t}t^{-1+\theta/2}\Big(\|b\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha-\theta}}+\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}}\Big)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t

\displaystyle\leqslant C\lambda^{-\theta/2}\Big(\|b\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha-\theta}}+\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}}\Big),

where we used
\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\lambda t}t^{-1+\theta/2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t\lesssim\lambda^{-\theta/2}
. Choosing
\lambda_{0}
sufficiently large so that
C\lambda^{-\theta/2}\|b\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}}<1/2
, the term involving
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha-\theta}}
can be absorbed into the left-hand side, yielding the global contraction bound (
7.11
).
∎

Let
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})
be the unique solution to (
7.10
) with
f=-b
and with
\lambda
chosen large enough (using (
7.11
)) so that
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha-\theta}}\leqslant 1/2
.
Since
b
is periodic,
\mathbf{u}
inherits this periodicity. We construct the periodic Zvonkin map by

(7.12)

\Phi(x):=x+\mathbf{u}(x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Since
2+\alpha-\theta>1
,
\Phi(x)
generates a global
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism and satisfies

\frac{1}{2}|x-x^{\prime}|\leqslant|\Phi(x)-\Phi(x^{\prime})|\leqslant\frac{3}{2}|x-x^{\prime}|.

7.2.2.
Reduction to the Hölder regime

To bypass the distributional singularity, we introduce the macroscopically scaled process

\widehat{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon^{-1}X_{\varepsilon^{2}t}^{\varepsilon},\qquad\widehat{W}_{t}:=\varepsilon^{-1}W_{\varepsilon^{2}t}.

Under this scaling, (
7.9
) becomes

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\widehat{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\big(b+\varepsilon c\big)(\widehat{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\widehat{W}_{t}.

Let
\widehat{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=\Phi(\widehat{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon})
be the spatially transformed trajectory. Applying Itô’s formula and utilizing the cancellation identity
\Delta_{x}\mathbf{u}+b\cdot\nabla_{x}\mathbf{u}=\lambda\mathbf{u}-b
, we find that
\widehat{Y}^{\varepsilon}
solves the regularized SDE (see
[
26
, Lemma 4.5]
):

(7.13)

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\widehat{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\big(\widehat{b}+\varepsilon\widehat{c}\big)(\widehat{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\,\widehat{\sigma}(\widehat{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\widehat{W}_{t},

where

\widehat{b}(y):=\lambda\mathbf{u}\big(\Phi^{-1}(y)\big),\quad\widehat{c}(y):=\big((I_{d}+\nabla\mathbf{u})c\big)\big(\Phi^{-1}(y)\big),\quad\widehat{\sigma}(y):=\big(I_{d}+\nabla\mathbf{u}\big)\big(\Phi^{-1}(y)\big).

Crucially, these coefficients possess strictly positive Hölder regularity:

\widehat{b}\in{\mathbf{C}}^{2+\alpha-\theta}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}),\quad\widehat{c}\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta\wedge(1+\alpha-\theta)}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}),\quad\widehat{\sigma}\in{\mathbf{C}}^{1+\alpha-\theta}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}).

Set
\widehat{\alpha}:=\beta\wedge(1+\alpha-\theta)
. Since
\theta<1+\alpha
, we have
\widehat{\alpha}>0
. Thus, the highly singular problem is transformed into the regular Hölder framework
(A
{}^{\widehat{\alpha}}
)
of Subsection
7.1
.

7.2.3.
Homogenization

Let
\widehat{\mu}
be the unique invariant probability measure of the transformed fast diffusion governed by
\widehat{b}
and
\widehat{\sigma}
, and define its effective macroscopic drift

\bar{b}:=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\widehat{b}(x)\,\widehat{\mu}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x).

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{G}}
be the effective coefficients defined in (
7.4
), computed from the transformed regularized dynamics. By mapping our uniform bounds back through the bounded corrector geometry, we prove the main distributional homogenization theorem.

Theorem 7.8

(Periodic homogenization with distributional drift)
.

Assume
(A
{}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
)
′′
and let
\gamma>1
.
There exists a constant
C>0
(depending only on parameters and defined norms) such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and any periodic observable
\varphi\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
,

(7.14)

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\varphi(X_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)-\bar{b}\,t/\varepsilon)\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[\varphi(x+{\mathcal{F}}t+\sqrt{2{\mathcal{G}}}W_{t})\big]\Big|\leqslant C\,\varepsilon\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}.

Proof.

To satisfy the
1
-periodic observable requirement of the uniform homogenization framework, we consider the macroscopic process directly, rather than dynamically scaling the observable (which creates an
\varepsilon^{-1}
-periodic function and violates Theorem
7.2
). Define the macroscopic Zvonkin process:

Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon\widehat{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{t/\varepsilon^{2}}.

By applying the inverse Itô scaling to (
7.13
), and noting that
W^{\varepsilon}_{t}:=\varepsilon\widehat{W}_{t/\varepsilon^{2}}
has the same law as
W_{t}
, we find that
Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}
solves the standard
\varepsilon
-periodic multiscale SDE:

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\Big[\varepsilon^{-1}\widehat{b}+\widehat{c}\Big]\!\left(\frac{Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\widehat{\sigma}\!\left(\frac{Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t}.

Since
(\widehat{b},\widehat{c},\widehat{\sigma})
possess positive Hölder regularity, Theorem
7.2
applies to the centered macroscopic process
\bar{Z}_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\bar{b}t/\varepsilon
. Evaluating the original fixed
1
-periodic observable
\varphi
at the initial condition
Z_{0}^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\Phi(x/\varepsilon)=x+\varepsilon\mathbf{u}(x/\varepsilon)
, we obtain:

(7.15)

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\varphi(\bar{Z}_{t}^{\varepsilon})\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[\varphi(Z_{0}^{\varepsilon}+{\mathcal{F}}t+\sqrt{2{\mathcal{G}}}W_{t})\big]\Big|\leqslant C\varepsilon\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}.

Finally, we reconcile
\bar{Z}_{t}^{\varepsilon}
with the original state
X_{t}^{\varepsilon}
. Unrolling the macroscopic scaling and using the Zvonkin diffeomorphism (
7.12
), we have

Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\Phi\big(\widehat{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t/\varepsilon^{2}}\big)=\varepsilon\Phi\big(\varepsilon^{-1}X_{t}^{\varepsilon}\big)=X_{t}^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u}\big(\varepsilon^{-1}X_{t}^{\varepsilon}\big).

Subtracting the secular drift yields

\bar{Z}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\Big(X_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\frac{\bar{b}}{\varepsilon}t\Big)+\varepsilon\mathbf{u}\big(\varepsilon^{-1}X_{t}^{\varepsilon}\big).

Since
\varphi
is globally Lipschitz (as
\gamma>1
), the geometric error is bounded by

\Big|\varphi(\bar{Z}_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\varphi\Big(X_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\frac{\bar{b}}{\varepsilon}t\Big)\Big|\leqslant\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\varepsilon\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}\leqslant C\varepsilon\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}.

A similar Lipschitz estimate replaces the shifted initial condition
Z_{0}^{\varepsilon}=x+\varepsilon\mathbf{u}(x/\varepsilon)
by
x
in the Brownian expectation on the right-hand side of (
7.15
). Taking expectations and applying the triangle inequality against the uniform approximation bound (
7.15
) yields (
7.14
).
