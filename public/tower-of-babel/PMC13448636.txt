Ligand-controlled sequential activation enables nickel-catalysed alkyl–alkynyl coupling
Authors: Nayeong Kim, Hyeri Jeon, Seungwoo Hong, Won Jun Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCross-electrophile coupling between unactivated alkyl halides and alkynyl halides has long been challenged by reactivity mismatch and poor chemoselectivity. Here we report nickel-catalysed cross-electrophile coupling enabled by a tridentate ligand that controls the sequence of electrophile activation, allowing direct construction of alkynyl–alkyl bonds. This ligand-controlled strategy affords internal alkynes from unactivated primary, secondary, and tertiary alkyl halides with broad functional-group tolerance and enables reductive coupling of an unactivated tertiary alkyl electrophile with an alkynyl partner. Mechanistic studies reveal that the tridentate ligand enforces an unusual sequential activation pathway, in which the Ni(0) catalyst engages the alkyl halide prior to oxidative addition of the alkynyl partner. This alkyl-first activation mode ensures high chemoselectivity while suppressing homocoupling pathways, in contrast to conventional cross-electrophile coupling strategies that rely on rate-matching of electrophile reactivity. Overall, this study expands the scope of alkynyl–alkyl coupling and demonstrates how ligand control over activation sequence can enable challenging carbon–carbon bond-formation between mismatched electrophiles.Subject terms: Catalytic mechanisms, Homogeneous catalysis, Synthetic chemistry methodology

Introduction

Alkynes are ubiquitous functional groups and valuable building blocks in organic synthesis1, with broad applications in chemical biology2, medicinal chemistry3, and materials science4 (Fig. 1a). Traditional routes for alkyne installation include the Corey–Fuchs reaction5 and nucleophilic substitution (SN2) of primary alkyl halides using alkynyl anions derived from terminal alkynes (Fig. 1bi). Transition-metal catalysis has enabled efficient C(sp)–C(sp²) couplings (e.g. Sonogashira coupling)6, but analogous strategies for C(sp)–C(sp³) bond formation remain largely underdeveloped. Fundamental challenges—such as rapid β-hydride elimination and sluggish reductive elimination—have long impeded alkynyl–alkyl cross-coupling reactions7,8. Nonetheless, notable progress has been made using pre-functionalised organometallic reagents—for instance, Pd-catalysed oxidative couplings of alkylzinc reagents with alkynyl stannanes9 or terminal alkynes10, as well as Negishi-type reactions of alkylzinc reagents and bromoalkynes (Fig. 1bii)11. Ni catalysis has also enabled the decarboxylative alkynylation of activated alkyl electrophiles (via redox-active esters) using alkynylzinc partners (Fig. 1biii)12. However, these approaches rely on pre-functionalised organometallic intermediates, necessitating additional synthetic steps and limiting functional-group compatibility because of the high reactivity of such species.

Cross-electrophile coupling (XEC) has emerged as a powerful strategy to bypass organometallic intermediates by directly coupling two electrophiles, thereby streamlining synthesis and broadening substrate scope (Fig. 1c)13. A persistent challenge in XEC, however, is chemoselectivity: if two electrophiles have similar reactivities, the catalyst can indiscriminately activate both precursors, leading to homocoupling and other side reactions14. Weix and co-workers have made pioneering and continuing contributions to chemoselective aryl–alkyl coupling15,16, and independent advances by Gong17,18 and Reisman19,20 have broadened the scope of Ni-catalysed C(sp²)–C(sp³) coupling reactions. Further, theoretical studies by Leifert and Studer indicated that to achieve chemoselectivity, the rates for the formation of LNi(II)(Csp2)X (ra) and alkyl radical •C(sp3) (rb) are nearly equal, whereas the rate for both undesired homocoupling (rc) remains significantly lower than the rates of radical trapping (rd) and reductive elimination (re) (Fig. 1d)21. For C(sp²)–C(sp³) couplings, this kinetic balance has been achieved for several nickel catalytic platforms, which therefore deliver reliable chemoselectivity.

In contrast, alkynyl–alkyl XEC reactions remain underdeveloped because of the intrinsic reactivity mismatch between the two coupling partners. First, alkynyl electrophiles are significantly more reactive than aryl electrophiles (C(sp)–Br > C(sp²)–Br), disrupting the delicate kinetic balance: that is, the oxidative addition of an alkynyl halide to nickel (forming a Ni(II)–alkynyl species, rₐ′) can outpace alkyl radical (rb) generation, leading predominantly to 1,3-diyne homocoupling instead of the desired cross-coupling (Fig. 1e)22. Second, alkynes can act as strong π-acceptor ligands and radical traps, potentially deactivating the catalyst or scavenging radical intermediates23. Initial efforts to address these issues employed activated alkyl precursors to generate radicals more readily, thereby mitigating the mismatch. For example, Weix and co-workers reported the decarboxylative alkynylation of N-hydroxyphthalimide esters with alkynyl bromides24, whereas Han, Wang, Yan and co-workers reported the Ni-catalysed deaminative XEC of Katritzky pyridinium salts with alkynyl bromides25. In both cases, the use of an activated alkyl partner facilitates rapid radical generation, thus keeping pace with the oxidative addition of the alkynyl halides. Although highly effective in enabling C(sp)–C(sp³) bond formation, these strategies require additional steps to pre-install the sacrificial activating groups that are consumed during radical generation and are not ideal in terms of atom economy. More recently, Xu and co-workers developed a dual nickel/photoredox system to achieve enantioselective C(sp)–C(sp³) coupling with activated α-bromo phosphonates26 and α-trifluoromethylated alkyl bromides27. Although this photoredox-driven platform provides an effective enantioselective route, its reliance on activated alkyl halides limits its applicability.

We hypothesised that the mismatch of alkynyl–alkyl reactivity could be overcome by tailoring the catalyst and ligand to control the kinetics of each step. Here we report a nickel-catalysed alkynyl–alkyl cross-electrophile coupling that achieves high chemoselectivity and broad substrate scope from unactivated primary, secondary, and even tertiary alkyl halides (Fig. 1g). This one-step method directly constructs C(sp)–C(sp³) bonds from simple halides without any pre-functionalisation while enabling broad functional-group compatibility. Notably, this strategy demonstrates cross-electrophile coupling of unactivated tertiary alkyl electrophiles with alkynyl partners under reductive conditions.

In this work, we show that the ligand-controlled activation sequence reverses the usual activation order of alkyl and alkynyl electrophiles, resulting in a distinct reaction pathway. The use of a tridentate ligand promotes an alkyl-first activation pathway in which the nickel catalyst first activates the alkyl halide, forming an alkyl radical and a nickel–alkyl intermediate. The alkynyl halide then undergoes rapid oxidative addition to Ni(I), yielding a Ni(III) complex that undergoes reductive elimination to form the C(sp)–C(sp³) bond (Fig. 1h). This mechanistic divergence expands the scope of alkynyl–alkyl XEC and highlights ligand-controlled activation sequence as a strategy for overcoming reactivity barriers in C(sp)–C(sp³) bond formation.

Results

Reaction optimisation

We started our studies by examining the cross-electrophile coupling between alkyl iodide 1 and alkynyl bromide 2 as model substrates (Fig. 2). Applying the conditions reported by Weix et al. for a related decarboxylative coupling24, we obtained only a 6% yield of the desired alkynylated product 3. The crude reaction mixture was dominated by alkynyl–alkynyl and alkyl–alkyl homocoupling products, highlighting the difficulty of achieving chemoselective coupling with unactivated alkyl halides under these conditions. Systematic optimisation identified NiI₂ with tridentate ligand L1, in combination with ZnCl₂ as an additive and Mn as the reductant, as the optimal catalyst system. Under these conditions, the desired cross-coupled product 3 was obtained in 97% isolated yield with minimal formation of homocoupling byproducts, and no detectable olefinic products arising from β-hydride elimination (Fig. 2, entry 1). Adjusting the stoichiometry from a 2:1 to a 1:1 ratio of alkyl iodide to alkynyl bromide led to a modest decrease in yield; however, analysis of the crude mixture showed that chemoselectivity was largely preserved, with only trace amounts (~5%) of alkyl–alkyl and alkynyl–alkynyl homocoupling products detected (Fig. 2, entry 2). Alternative Ni(II) or Ni(0) precursors28 gave significantly lower yields of 3 (Fig. 2, entries 3–5). Using trimethylsilyl chloride (TMSCl) in place of ZnCl₂ also provided good reactivity, affording 3 in 79% yield (Fig. 2, entry 6). Other reductants such as Zn and tetrakis(dimethylamino)ethylene (TDAE) were much less effective than Mn (Fig. 2, entries 7 and 8). Notably, even at a lower catalyst loading (5 mol% Ni), coupling still afforded 3 in 90% yield (Fig. 2, entry 9), highlighting the efficiency of this XEC system. Further, control experiments confirmed that each component of the catalytic system was essential. Omission of ZnCl₂ led to a moderate decrease in the yield of 3 (Fig. 2, entry 10), although efficient product formation was still observed. This result indicates that ZnCl₂ is not strictly required for C(sp)–C(sp³) bond formation, but contributes to more efficient catalytic performance (see Supplementary Information for time-course data). In contrast, exclusion of either NiI₂ or Mn completely suppressed the reaction, with >95% of both electrophiles recovered unchanged (Fig. 2, entry 11), highlighting that both the nickel catalyst and the reductant are essential for productive cross-electrophile coupling. Similarly, in the absence of L1, the yield of 3 decreased sharply to 12%, with only minor amounts of alkyl–alkyl and alkynyl–alkynyl homocoupling byproducts detected (Fig. 2, entry 12). Finally, L1 proved to be uniquely effective; other tridentate ligands (L2–L4) or bidentate ligands (L5–L9) afforded substantially lower yields (Fig. 2, entries 13–20). These results indicate that the electronic and coordination properties of the tridentate nitrogen ligand are critical for enforcing efficient and chemoselective alkynyl–alkyl cross-electrophile coupling.

Substrate scope

Under the optimised conditions, we examined the scope of alkyl halide coupling partners (Fig. 3). The unactivated primary alkyl iodides bearing a wide range of functional groups were efficiently converted into the cross-coupled products. For example, substrates containing phthalimide (4), ether (5), acetoxy (6), acetal (7), cyano (8), ester (9), amide (10), or Boc-protected amine (11) groups all afforded the corresponding internal alkynes in high yields. Notably, functional groups that often challenge cross-coupling reactions—such as alkyl chlorides (12), terminal alkenes (13) or boronic esters (14)—were well tolerated, giving the desired products without the need for any protecting group. Even sterically hindered primary halides (15–18) were successfully coupled without significant loss in yield. Both cyclic (19–22) and acyclic (23–24) secondary alkyl electrophiles were also viable substrates, providing the corresponding alkynes in good yields. Furthermore, tertiary alkyl electrophiles—whose cross-couplings with alkynes have not been previously reported—could be coupled under our conditions, delivering products 26–28 in moderate yields, consistent with the increased steric congestion associated with tertiary alkyl electrophiles. Next, we explored the scope of alkynyl halide coupling partners. Both silyl- and alkyl-substituted alkynyl bromides were readily coupled with alkyl iodide 1 to deliver the corresponding alkynes 29–38 in good yields. Notably, an alkynyl bromide bearing a malonate diester moiety—typically incompatible with strongly basic conditions due to its acidic α-protons—underwent smooth coupling to furnish product 33.

Synthetic applications

The present method is applicable in late-stage functionalisation, as demonstrated by its successful use with complex molecular substrates (Fig. 4a). Alkyl halides derived from estrone, cholesterol, and α-tocopherol were viable partners, affording alkynylated products 39–41 in good yields. Similarly, alkynyl halides derived from estrone and nootkatone were also suitable substrates, providing the corresponding alkynylated products 42–43 in good yields. Moreover, even coupling between two bulky, complex partners—an alkyl halide derived from α-tocopherol and an alkynyl halide derived from estrone—was feasible, providing the cross-coupled product 44 in 53% yield. To further demonstrate the synthetic utility of this method, a series of bioactive intermediates were prepared, highlighting the practicality and versatility of the reaction platform (Fig. 4b). From coupling product 4, the catalytic hydrogenation of the alkyne delivered corresponding alkane 45 in 89% yield, whereas hydrazinolysis of the N-alkyl phthalimide afforded primary amine 46 in high yield without affecting the alkyne group. Desilylation of the triisopropylsilyl group with tetrabutylammonium fluoride (TBAF) furnished terminal alkyne 47 in 92% yield. Given the versatility of terminal alkynes, we further functionalised compound 47 as follows: partial hydrogenation with Lindlar’s catalyst produced alkene 48, and Sonogashira coupling provided internal alkyne 50 in 83% yield. Importantly, this strategy also enabled the efficient preparation of compound 51, which contains a key structural motif found in certain alkaloids and antibiotics29. In addition, pyrimidine derivative 52, which shows anticancer activity30, was obtained in good yield. Moreover, compound 49, containing both a phthalimide and 1,2,3-triazole moiety—a scaffold of interest for overcoming antimicrobial resistance—was accessed in 94% yield via a Cu-catalysed click reaction31. We next attempted a tandem sequence: the radical initiation of 53 followed by intramolecular cyclisation to form a cyclic alkyl radical (int 1), then Ni-catalysed C(sp)–C(sp³) coupling (Fig. 4c). This one-pot tandem process delivered bicyclic diether 54 in 78% yield with high diastereoselectivity, demonstrating the single-step assembly of a complex cyclic framework.

Mechanistic investigation

To probe the reaction mechanism, we considered two plausible scenarios: (i) in situ formation of organomanganese species (alkynyl–Mn or alkyl–Mn) followed by conventional nickel-catalysed coupling, and (ii) a cross-electrophile coupling mechanism—the oxidative addition of the alkynyl halide to give a Ni(II)–alkynyl species concomitant with alkyl radical formation, followed by radical capture to form a Ni(III) intermediate and subsequent reductive elimination. To distinguish between these possibilities, we designed a series of mechanistic experiments. First, each electrophile was examined under standard conditions in the absence of the Ni/L1 catalyst. Neither alkyl iodide 1 nor alkynyl bromide 2 showed detectable conversion without Ni/L1 (Fig. 5a), indicating that direct insertion of Mn into the C(sp³)–I or C(sp)–Br bond is not a productive pathway under our conditions.

a Control experiments showing no reaction in the absence of Ni/L1, indicating that Mn alone does not insert into C–I or C–Br bonds. b Testing a possible organometallic pathway: coupling with Mg as reductant vs a pre-formed Grignard reagent. c Radical clock experiment. d Use of a chiral secondary alkyl iodide under standard conditions. e Radical trapping with TEMPO. f Order-of-addition experiment. g Initial rate studies. h Effective magnetic moment (Evans method). ee enantiomeric excess, HRMS high-resolution mass spectrometry.

Thus, Mn primarily functions as a reductant for the nickel catalyst rather than as a source of organometallic nucleophiles. Consistent with this interpretation, although the Ni-catalysed coupling between 57 and 2 proceeded in 57% yield when Mg metal was used as the reductant, no cross-coupled product was obtained when alkynyl bromide 2 was treated with a pre-formed alkyl Grignard reagent under analogous conditions (Fig. 5b). Collectively, these results argue against mechanisms involving organometallic nucleophiles as productive intermediates in this system. Multiple experiments support the involvement of alkyl radical intermediates. Reaction of (iodomethyl)cyclopropane 59 furnished a ring-opened product, consistent with rapid cyclopropylmethyl radical rearrangement (Fig. 5c). Similarly, coupling of chiral secondary alkyl iodide 61 resulted in a racemic product, indicating a loss of stereochemistry, consistent with the formation of a free secondary alkyl radical (Fig. 5d). In addition, conducting the reaction in the presence of 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO) led to formation of alkyl–TEMPO adduct 63, while neither alkynyl–TEMPO adducts nor the desired cross-coupled product were detected (Fig. 5e). Together, these observations provide strong evidence for alkyl radical intermediacy under the catalytic conditions. We next examined the order in which nickel engages the two electrophiles using stoichiometric order-of-addition experiments (Fig. 5f). Exposure of the Ni–L1 complex to either electrophile resulted in the formation of distinct organonickel intermediates. Strikingly, subsequent reaction with the complementary electrophile afforded the cross-coupled product only when the alkyl-derived intermediate was generated first, whereas pre-formation of the alkynyl-derived intermediate led exclusively to unproductive pathways with no detectable C(sp)–C(sp³) bond formation. This behaviour reflects the pronounced kinetic preference for alkyl electrophile engagement observed under catalytic conditions (Fig. 6a, b). These results indicate that nickel first activates the alkyl halide, likely via the generation of an alkyl radical together with an LNi(I)X species, which rapidly recombine to form an LNi(II)(alkyl)X intermediate. We propose that this Ni(II)–alkyl intermediate is reduced by Mn to a more reactive alkyl–nickel species, which subsequently undergoes oxidative addition with the alkynyl bromide en route to C(sp)–C(sp³) bond formation. To further elucidate the electronic nature of the organonickel intermediates formed during alkyl-first activation, we investigated intermediates 65 (Ni–alkynyl) and 66 (Ni–alkyl) using solution magnetic susceptibility measurements (Evans method) (Fig. 5h)32,33. Both intermediates exhibit paramagnetic behaviour with integer-spin magnetic moments, indicative of two unpaired electron nickel species. In particular, intermediate 66 displays an effective magnetic moment (μeff ≈ 2.9 μB), most consistent with an S = 1 Ni(II) complex. Intermediate 65 likewise shows magnetic behaviour characteristic of an even-electron nickel species. Notably, both intermediates are EPR-silent upon initial formation, excluding a one-electron Ni(I) assignment at early reaction times. Over time, EPR-active Ni(I) species emerge, consistent with the gradual off-cycle reduction of these intermediates. Together, these data support assignment of 65 and 66 as transient, even-electron nickel intermediates formed during the catalytic cycle. To probe the behaviour of the Ni–alkyl intermediate under reducing conditions, we conducted separate stoichiometric experiments on intermediate 66 in the presence of Mn. Under these conditions, further reduction to EPR-active Ni(I) species was observed (Fig. 6d), with corresponding magnetic susceptibility data provided in Supplementary Information. This behaviour contrasts with aryl–alkyl XEC, in which aryl–Ni(II) intermediates can engage alkyl electrophiles productively, whereas pre-formed alkyl–Ni(II) species typically undergo alkyl–alkyl dimerisation34. In previous decarboxylative alkynylation reactions employing activated alkyl precursors, productive coupling could proceed from either alkynyl–Ni(II) or alkyl–Ni intermediates24. In contrast, in the present system involving unactivated alkyl halides, productive coupling occurs exclusively via an alkyl-first pathway. We attribute this distinct order of reactivity to two factors: (1) nickel-mediated alkyl radical generation is suppressed once nickel is bound as an LNi(II)(alkynyl)X complex, and (2) the tridentate coordination environment imposed by ligand L1 substantially retards oxidative addition of the alkynyl halide. Together, these effects enforce preferential engagement of the alkyl electrophile prior to alkynyl activation. Kinetic studies further clarify the turnover-limiting events in the catalytic cycle (Fig. 5g). Initial rate measurements reveal a first-order dependence on alkyl halide, nickel catalyst and Mn, and a zero-order dependence on the alkynyl halide. This rate law indicates that turnover is limited by a step occurring after alkyl electrophile activation but prior to alkynyl engagement. In line with the stoichiometric and spectroscopic data, we attribute the turnover-limiting event to the reduction of the alkyl–nickel intermediate to a more reactive state preceding oxidative addition of the alkynyl halide.

To probe the preference for alkyl- over alkynyl halide activation, we monitored the reaction using time-resolved UV–vis spectroscopy. The starting nickel complex, LNi(0) produced a strong characteristic absorption band at 890 nm (ε = ~5000 M−1 cm−1) in THF under an inert atmosphere (Fig. 6a). Upon the addition of stoichiometric alkyl iodide, this 890 nm band decayed rapidly, following pseudo-first-order kinetics kobs = 1.6 × 10−3 s−1 (Fig. 6a). In contrast, the same Ni(0) complex reacted with an alkynyl bromide approximately 20 times more slowly (kobs = 7.1 × 10−5 s−1), indicating that the oxidative addition of alkyl halides to Ni(0) is kinetically favoured (Fig. 6b). In addition, the 1H NMR spectrum obtained during the reaction between Ni(0) and the alkyl halide showed broad, paramagnetically shifted signals, consistent with the formation of a paramagnetic nickel species (Fig. 6c).

Having established that Ni(0) preferentially activates alkyl electrophiles, we sought direct evidence for the proposed Ni(I) and Ni(III) intermediates. The reduction of LNi(II)(alkyl)I with Mn as a reductant afforded a new absorption band at 930 nm (ε = ~700 M−1 cm−1) in the UV–vis spectrum, arising from the formation of LNi(I)(alkyl) intermediate (Fig. 6e)35. In addition, the EPR spectrum of LNi(I)(alkyl) displayed distinct new signals, having g values at 2.00 and 2.26, consistent with axial symmetry (Fig. 6d). Subsequent addition of alkynyl bromide to the reaction solution containing LNi(I)(alkyl) produced distinct absorption bands at 672 nm (ε = ~845 M−1 cm−1) and 808 nm (ε = ~525 M−1 cm−1), suggesting oxidative addition to give a high-valent nickel intermediate, most consistent with an LNi(III)(alkyl)(alkynyl)Br formulation, upon the oxidative addition of the alkynyl halide to a Ni(I) complex (Fig. 6f)36. Multiple attempts to obtain structural information about both Ni(I) and Ni(III) intermediates were unsuccessful, reflecting their intrinsic instability.

To substantiate the formation of the Ni(I) and Ni(III) intermediates, we first conducted density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Source Data). Geometry optimisation was performed for both the LNi(I)(alkyl) and LNi(III)(alkyl)(alkynyl)Br species (Fig. 6g, h). The Ni(I) complex was found to adopt a distorted square planar geometry, whereas Ni(III) had a distorted octahedron structure. Frontier molecular orbital analysis revealed metal-to-ligand charge-transfer characteristics in both complexes (Fig. 6i, j). Moreover, time-dependent (TD)-DFT calculations of the electronic absorption spectra agreed well with the experimental results (Fig. 6k, l); for the Ni(I) intermediate, the simulated electronic absorption bands at 630–690 nm and shoulder band at approximately 835–890 nm were well matched to the absorption bands observed during the reduction of LNi(II)(alkyl)I by Mn, whereas for the Ni(III) intermediate, strong absorption bands ranging from 470 to 600 nm and a weaker band at 714 nm are consistent with the experimental results. Together, the spectroscopic data and supporting calculations provide compelling evidence for the involvement of Ni(I) and Ni(III) intermediates in the catalytic cycle.

Based on these mechanistic studies, we propose the catalytic cycle shown in Fig. 7, which is supported by the combined spectroscopic, kinetic, and computational analyses. The LNi(0) complex chemoselectively activates an alkyl iodide (R–I) to generate an LNi(II)(alkyl)I complex. This Ni(II)–alkyl species is subsequently reduced by Mn to afford the active LNi(I)(alkyl) species, a step identified as the rate-determining step based on kinetic analysis. The alkynyl halide then undergoes oxidative addition to the Ni(I)–alkyl complex, forming a high-valent LNi(III)(alkyl)(alkynyl)X intermediate. Finally, rapid reductive elimination from the Ni(III) centre forms the C(sp)–C(sp³) bond, releasing the coupled alkyl–alkynyl product and regenerating a nickel species that is returned to the catalytically active Ni(0) state under the reductive reaction conditions. This tridentate–ligand-enabled pathway, involving sequential alkyl-first activation and Ni(I)/Ni(III) intermediates, diverges fundamentally from conventional cross-coupling mechanisms and is essential for achieving the observed reactivity and chemoselectivity with unactivated coupling partners.

Plausible catalytic cycle for alkyl–alkynyl cross-electrophile coupling.

The proposed cycle involves alkyl iodide activation by LNi(0), reduction to an alkyl–Ni(I) species, oxidative addition of the alkynyl halide to form a high-valent nickel intermediate, and reductive elimination to furnish the alkyl–alkynyl product.

In summary, we report a nickel-catalysed cross-electrophile coupling reaction for alkynyl bromides with unactivated alkyl halides that enables one-step access to internal alkynes without the need for any pre-functionalisation. This strategy accommodates a broad range of substrates and functional groups and demonstrates that cross-electrophile coupling can successfully unite tertiary alkyl electrophiles with alkynyl partners. Crucial to the success of this method is the incorporation of a tridentate ligand, which enforces an unconventional alkyl-first mechanism, unlike standard cross-coupling pathways. By engaging the alkyl halide prior to activating the alkynyl electrophile, the Ni catalyst forms an alkyl–Ni(I) intermediate that can subsequently capture the alkynyl fragment, thereby avoiding uncontrolled side reactions such as 1,3-diyne homocoupling. This mechanistic framework effectively overcomes the intrinsic reactivity mismatch between alkyl and alkynyl coupling partners. These findings highlight how mechanistic control can guide the development of cross-electrophile coupling strategies for constructing challenging carbon–carbon bonds in organic synthesis.

Methods

The general procedure for the alkynylation of unactivated alkyl halides with alkynyl halides was as follows. In a glovebox under an atmosphere of purified argon, NiI₂ (3.1 mg, 0.01 mmol, 10 mol%), L1 (4.0 mg, 0.01 mmol, 10 mol%), ZnCl₂ (2.7 mg, 0.02 mmol, 20 mol%), and manganese powder (16 mg, 0.3 mmol, 3 equiv.) were added to an oven-dried 4 mL vial equipped with a magnetic stir bar. Anhydrous THF (0.1 mL, 1.0 M), alkyl halide (0.2 mmol, 2 equiv.), and alkynyl halide (0.1 mmol, 1 equiv.) were then added. The vial was sealed with parafilm, and the reaction mixture was stirred outside of the glovebox at room temperature for 12 h. The reaction mixture was diluted with dichloromethane and filtered through a plug of silica gel and celite. The crude solution was removed under reduced pressure, and the residue was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel to afford the desired products. Detailed experimental procedures are provided in the Supplementary Information.
