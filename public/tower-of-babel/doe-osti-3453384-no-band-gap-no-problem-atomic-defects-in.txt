LDRD PROJECT TITLE: No Band Gap, No Problem:
Atomic defects in narrow bandgap materials via a band-avoiding
occupation-constrained density functional theory
PROJECT TEAM MEMBERS: Peter A. Schultz (PI) and Leopoldo Diaz

ABSTRACT:

This final report summarized the results of the Laboratory Directed Research and Development
Project No. 242427 (Exploratory Express). Development of new radiation-hard space-based
infrared imaging devices, and low power high-speed electronics based upon III-V semiconductor
materials is hampered by difficulties in identifying and mitigating the atomic defects responsible
for debilitating radiation sensitivities. Defect-discriminating experiments are exceedingly
challenging and, conversely, modeling that might provide usable insight for experimental studies
is also impaired. State-of-the-art density functional theory (DFT) to characterize radiation
defects is hampered by prohibitive computational cost and limited accuracy due to the DFT
“band gap problem”. This project developed a new method for defect calculations, a band-
avoiding occupation-constrained DFT – ba-occ-DFT – that prevents the spurious occupation of
band-edge states and enables total energy calculations of atomic defects uncontaminated by band
edges. Application of ba-occ-DFT to indium arsenide (InAs), a simple exemplar of a family of
narrow gap materials with a zero DFT gap, shows that a simple ba-occ-DFT is feasible, separates
the band gap problem from the calculations of defect electronic properties, enabling rigorous
calculations of defect levels (charge transition energies) in narrow-gap semiconductors despite a
DFT band gap that collapses to zero.

INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS:

Development of new radiation-hard space-based electronics based upon narrow band gap III-V
semiconductors, either for low-power high-speed applications or infrared imaging devices, is
hampered by difficulties in mitigating the atomic defects responsible for debilitating radiation
sensitivities. Defect-discriminating experiments to identify and characterize the radiation
vulnerabilities are exceedingly challenging in narrow gap semiconductors. Conversely, modeling
of atomic defects using state-of-the-art density functional theory (DFT), frequently partnered
with experiment to identify and characterize defects in semiconductors, is also hampered in
narrow band gap semiconductors. Density functional theory [1,2] is widely used with great
success for modeling structural properties of bulk materials and atomic defects in first-principles
simulations of materials, with typical accuracy of a couple tenths of an electron Volt (eV) over a
wide range of applications. DFT’s success in structural energetics contrasts sharply with DFT’s
inadequate description of the fundamental band gap in semiconductors and insulators. The
Kohn-Sham (KS) band gap, the difference between the computed KS eigenvalues for the highest

occupied valence band edge (VBE) state and the first unoccupied conduction band edge (CBE)
state greatly underestimates the experimental band gap [3-5]—the infamous “band gap problem”.
The band gap problem reaches its extreme in narrow-gap semiconductors such as indium
arsenide, the computed band gap goes to zero (the CBE collapses to the VBE), which seemingly
precludes calculations of defect levels inside the band gap. Using InAs as the exemplar, we
develop a band-avoiding occupation-constrained DFT (ba-occ-DFT) that enables predictive
calculations of defect levels despite a band gap problem, even in a semiconductor with a zero
DFT band gap.

InAs is a zinc-blende structure semiconductor with a 0.42 eV band gap (at (0K) [6-8] and high
electron mobility, a simple example of a family of narrow-gap III-V alloys {Al,Ga,In}-(P,As,Sb}
with great potential for low-power high-speed electronics and infrared imaging device. Atomic
displacement damage degrades electronic performance of devices deployed in the extreme
radiation environments of space, sparking extensive efforts to determine the atomic-scale cause
of the radiation sensitivities and thereby engineer effective mitigation strategies. Deep-level
transition spectroscopy (DLTS) is the experimental workhorse for defect characterization, but is
impaired in narrow-gap systems by leakage currents mediated by surface effects [9,10].
Simulations in InAs using DFT [11-18] are also impaired, by the band gap problem and by finite
model-size errors, with the consequence that no two previous studies appear to agree on almost
any defect physics in InAs. In previous work, we had demonstrated a total-energy-based DFT
method for charge defects [19,20,21] that reliably predicted energy levels in silicon [19] and
GaAs [22-25] to 0.1 eV accuracy, systems with a band gap problem, a KS gap smaller than
experiment, but not a zero gap. Hybrid functional DFT methods such as HSE06 [26,27] can fix
the band problem [26,27] but cannot match this accuracy and the most recent HSE06 study [28]
failed to predict even a single candidate for any radiation-induced defect level in GaAs [29].

Figure 1 illustrates the central issues addressed in this project. The HSE06 band structure has a
direct band gap of 0.54 eV at the G-point, in quantitative agreement with previous results [30].
Our PBE functional (standard DFT) collapses the gap at the G-point. The idealized defect state

is a single discrete energy in the gap (flat band) that runs through the band gap at G. Away from
G, the defect state is cleanly in the band gap even with standard DFT using the PBE functional.
Our hypothesis, depicted in Fig. 1(d) is that this localized defect state continues through the G-
point, remains distinct from band states, and can be selectively occupied to avoid occupation of
the spuriously lower-energy CBE state.

In this project, we adapted a scheme for computing excited states using ground-state DFT
machinery [31] to develop a ba-occ-DFT method to implement a band-avoiding occupation at
the G-point to obtain a clean (uncontaminated by band states) calculation of defect energies. Our
hypothesis was confirmed, the defect bands were indeed flat (for very large computational
models), the ba-occ-DFT implementation was able to sample the defect states through the G-
point, and yield successful, size-converged calculations of defect levels for simple intrinsic
defects in InAs that might be expected in radiation displacement damage (both antisites AsIn and
InAs, for single vacancies vAs and vIn, the divacancy vv, and both single interstitial defects, Asi
and Ini). The predictions were generally consistent with new experiments [10], validating the
approach. The method, results, and analysis were presented in a manuscript submitted for
publication (SAND2026-23880O) [32], included as an addendum to this Final Report.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT AND
METHODOLOGY:
The methods are largely described in a publication [32] (SAND2026-23880O) and are only
summarized in brief here. The defect calculations using the PBE functional [33] were
performed using the SEQQUEST electronic structure code [34]. To achieve and verify
computational model-size convergence, the defect calculations were performed in (NxNxN)
expansions of the conventional 8-atom cubic InAs cells, for N=2-5, resulting in supercell models
consisting of 64-, 216-, 512- and 1000-atom atoms, using 2x2x2 regular k-grids to converge
sampling the Brillouin Zone (3x3x3 for the 64-atom supercell). The lattice parameter was fixed
the optimized PBE lattice parameter (11.691 bohr ≅ 6.19 Å) but the internal atomic positions are
fully relaxed to achieve forces <0.0002 Ry/bohr. The local moment countercharge (LMCC)
method implemented into SEQQUEST is used to enforce rigorous boundary conditions for charge
defect calculations [20,21] to remove finite size effects [19]. Additional calculations using the
HSE06 hybrid functional [26,27], e.g. Fig. 1(a), were performed with the VASP code [36,37].

The new ba-occ-DFT was developed and implemented into the SEQQUEST code. The standard
DFT Aufbau occupation scheme for ground state DFT calculations occupies electronic states
starting with the deepest state and fills every electronic state up to a Fermi level defined by the
highest occupied state. Previously, we had developed an occupation-constrained DFT scheme to
compute excited states by imposing a single-particle excitation across the Brillouin zone, shifting
an electron from an occupied state into a higher-energy unoccupied state, which successfully

modeled photoluminescent spectra in GaN systems [31]. Here, we adapted this occ-DFT
machinery to specifically target the band gap problem at the G-point, skipping occupation of the
spurious CBE state and selectively occupying the localized defect state to compute a defect
energy uncorrupted by band states. The risks were that such a defect state could not be isolated
(it could be dissolved into the CB states), that this non-Aufbau occupation would not be stable
(that it would collapse to the DFT ground states), and that the resulting eigenstate degeneracies at
the VBE/CBE edge would lead to unstable self-consistency problem. None of these potential
issues (risks) proved to be a problem for our implementation of the ba-occ-DFT approach.

We applied this ba-occ-DFT approach to the simple intrinsic point defects in InAs that might
appear in the initial displacement damage after irradiation: the antisites (AsIn and InAs), the single
vacancies (vAs and vIn), the single interstitials (Asi and Ini), and the divacancy vv. We performed
a comprehensive search for stable charge states for each defect, and a full defect structural
optimization including comprehensive search for possible symmetry-lowering distortions, and
especially (for the vacancies) considered possible site-shifts.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION:
This section largely repeats and summarizes the analysis presented in our publication [32]
(SAND2026-23880), included as an addendum.

The initial trial calculations to test the first hypothesis (existence of a defect state above the CBE
state at G) were performed for the arsenic antisite AsIn. By analogy to the As antisite in GaAs
identified as the EL2 [29], the AsIn is expected to have two donor states, a (0/1+) and a (1+/2+)
transitions inside the band gap. Our off-G 2x2x2 k-grid found a stable neutral, positive, and
double-positive state cleanly within the gap at the off-G sampling points in the BZ, the salient
question becomes: what happens at the G-point? The computed band structures for the antisite in
the various supercells, from 64-atom to 1000-atom are presented in Fig. 2.

Figure 2. Computed band structure for the neutral As antisite (AsIn) defect in (a) 64-, (b) 216-, (c) 512-, and (d) 1000-atom
supercell models. The VBE is at energy=0, represented by a dashed black line in the figure, and the CBE marking and green
dashed line correspond to the experimental position of the CBE (0.42 eV). The black bands are fully occupied bulk band states.
The blue band represents the first state above the VBE, occupied in a standard Aufbau, and apparently a defect band. The green
bands above those are empty states that are nominally correspond to conduction bands. The red arrows represent the ba-occ-

DFT occupation adjustment, “exciting” an electron from the CBE state at G to the defect state. The full red dots correspond to
the position of the defect states at the special points (X,G,M,R) in the BZ.

The AsIn(0) band structure in the 64-atom supercell depicted in Fig. 2(a) shows a presumptive
defect band that strongly violates the premise of a flat defect band, the dispersion in the first
occupied band above the VBE, presumptively the defect state, is more that 0.5 eV—larger than
the experimental band gap. The dispersion of this first band in the 216-atom supercell in Fig.
2(b), while slightly less, still spans more than 0.3 eV, again strongly violating the premise of a
flat dispersionless defect band. The 512-atom and 1000-atom results do manifest a flattening
defect band, except at the G-point, where it is apparent that the CBE state and the localized
defect state have swapped bands: the state touching the VBE is the CBE, while state above it is
now the defect state. With this identification of the defect state (indicated by the full red circles
at the special points X, G, M, and R), the defect state dispersion systematically reduces to 0.02
eV in the largest 1000-atom supercell, now satisfying the requirement of a flat dispersionless
defect band to a good approximation, provided that a ba-occ-DFT (indicated by the red arrows)
promotes an electron at G from the spuriously occupied CBE state into the higher defect state.
Incidentally, the special points are precisely the sampling points of a G-centered 2x2x2 k-grid –
so that a flat localized defect band can be cleanly sampled within a standard k-grid.

This ba-occ-DFT calculation, performed on G-centered 2x2x2 k-grids for the As antisite in these
supercells, proved to be stable: there was no collapse to the Aufbau ground state, no self-
consistency problems engendered by the state degeneracy created at the VBE/CBE confluence at
G, and the higher state (higher than the vacated CBE state) at G was cleanly identified as the
defect state. This led to highly converged results as a function of supercell size, evidence of
consistency in defect state description across the different supercell sizes, and evidence of good
convergence to an infinite bulk limit with even the smallest 216-atom supercell computational
model size. This convergence had not been as evident in our earlier (unpublished) calculations
using off-G 2x2x2 k-grids.

This newly implemented ba-occ-DFT was applied to survey the properties of all the simple
primary displacement damage defects in InAs, using the following protocol. First, a standard
Aufbau occupation calculation would be performed for each defect in each of its stable charge
states. If inspection of the computed eigenvalue spectrum revealed a spuriously occupied CBE
state at G (not every defect, e.g. the AsIn(2+), occupies a state above the VBE), we applied a ba-
occ-DFT to empty the CBE state and populate the higher defect state at the G-point instead. The
total energies and ensuing charge transition energies (defect levels) are computed using the
LMCC-based finite defect model [19].

First, we note that we observed the same flattening of the defect band in the larger supercell for
all the defects we considered that we observed for the As antisite in Fig. 2. A second notable

observation is that every such stable defect that required a ba-occ-DFT to obtain a clean defect
state involved a defect state crossing above the CBE. Not a single stable defect dipped below the
VBE. The defect levels using the ba-occ-DFT G-centered k-grids were cleaner and easier to
distinguish from band states than in our previous (unpublished) results using standard Aufbau in
off-G k-grids and also exhibited improved convergence with supercell size, so that the results that
emerge with the new ba-occ-DFT methods are more physically reliable.

Figure 3. Predicted InAs defect level diagram using PBE+LMCC, and the ba-occ-DFT in the 1000-atom using G-2x2x2 k-grid.

The defect level diagram for InAs defects depicted in Fig. 3 presents the results of the ba-occ-
DFT calculations in the 1000-atom supercell using a G-centered 2x2x2 k-grid. As expected, in
analogy to GaAs, the AsIn antisite has two donor states in the gap, a (0/1+) transition at 0.35 eV
above the VBE and a (1+/2+) charge transition at 0.12 eV. The In antisite exhibited similar
parallels to the Ga antisite in GaAs: charge states ranging from (2+) through (2-), with similar
distortions. The other defects had greater differences. In InAs, only the neutral vv(0) is stable in
the gap. The single vacancies and interstitials do not have a stable charge state that is neutral or
negatively charged, in contrast to the analogous defects in GaAs. Surprisingly, the defect level
analyses are already well-converged in the small 216-atom supercell, to within 30 meV,
providing even further evidence that the new ba-occ-DFT results are reliable.

Ultimately, the meaningful arbiter of the quality of the results is validation against experimental
results. With InAs, this is challenging, because experiment has not (to date) been able to
definitively chemically identify a defect from the measurements. This can partially be attributed

to a lack of reliable theory to provide insight into the limited defect-sensitive experimental
observations that have been made. The new results we obtain with ba-occ-DFT suggest potential
reinterpretations of old experimental data, potential explanations of the shoulder consisting of
shallow donor states seen in recent data from the Air Force Research Laboratory [10], and,
perhaps most crucially, provide invaluable insight to help design and guide new experiments
intended to decipher the radiation-sensitivity in narrow gap materials due to radiation-induced
defects.

ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS:
.
The project has resulted in one manuscript submitted for publication in a high-profile journal,
entitled “No band gap, no problem: Defects in InAs using a band-avoiding occupied constrained
density functional theory”, let by the project PI and coauthored by two other Sandians and two
Air Force Research Laboratory (Kirtland) partners, focused on the notion of using the ba-occ-
DFT to avoid the band gap problem in narrow gap systems. A follow-up full-length manuscript
is in progress focused on implications for radiation effects of defect physics in InAs. The
tentative (speculative?) associations of predicted defect levels (e.g. for the As antisite) with
specific experimental observations has inspired plans for new experiments in III-V infrared
devices, to collect more and better targeted evidence of defect-specific phenomena in InAs
devices and strengthen (or refute) the tentative chemical defect identifications made by theory.

Establishing this new modeling capability for narrow-bandgap III-V materials begins a virtuous
cycle, where experiment discovers new phenomena and validates defect models proposed by
theory results, and theory identifies candidates for new phenomena, and makes predictions of
new defect physics that might (or might not) be found by new experiments. Progress in making
III-V devices less sensitive to radiation damage has been slow and hard-won, principally driven
by Edisonian experimental searches that lacked microscopic insight into measured behavior.
The most recent experiments in irradiated InAs [10] had observed an unprecedented (for any III-
V semiconductor material) a broad shoulder they postulated was indicative of a collection of
shallow states. This observation is now straightforwardly confirmed and explained by our new
modeling results predicting that all the simple displacement damage (Frenkel pair defects,
vacancies and interstitials) will have shallow donor (0/1+) levels. Our new modeling results, in
turn, make new predictions of defects that can be the target of more focused experiments. The
new experiment-theory partnership promises to accelerate the development and deployment of
next generation electronics for low-power high-speed applications and infrared imaging devices.

CONCLUSION:
We have successfully developed a band-avoiding occupations-constrained DFT that enables
rigorous total energy calculations that enable predictions of defect formation energies and defect

levels in systems with small or vanishing DFT band gaps. The results obtained for the exemplar
InAs system are consistent with what limited experimental data exists for InAs, indicating that
this breakthrough has the potential to extend the accuracy of DFT predictions into a new class of
semiconductor systems. The crucial feature is to strictly enforce occupation of localized defect
states and strictly exclude occupation of delocalized crystal band states, which can be
accomplished straightforwardly with a simple ba-occ-DFT. Herein, this was implemented into
the SEQQUEST code, but the ba-occ-DFT approach, in principle, could be straightforwardly
implemented into any DFT supercell code. The ba-occ-DFT extends the predictive accuracy of
the DFT+LMCC method into narrow-gap systems, where (currently) there are no good
alternatives.
