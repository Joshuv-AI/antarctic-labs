Evaluation of therapeutic effects of D‐limonene following orchiopexy in the rat model of cryptorchidism
Authors: Arman Norouzi‐Ghalehbala, Mohammadjavad Rezaei‐Varmaziar, Majid Taati, Ali Soleimanzadeh, Abbas Raisi, Farshid Davoodi, Amir Ali Shahbazfar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCryptorchidism impairs spermatogenesis, causes infertility, and increases malignancy risk. Orchiopexy, the standard treatment, often fails to fully restore testicular function. This study evaluated the adjunctive effects of D‐limonene in prepubertal rats with bilateral cryptorchidism.MethodsTwenty‐four male rats were allocated into four groups: sham‐operated (SH), cryptorchid control with orchiopexy (CT), and two orchiopexy groups receiving low‐dose (50 mg/kg, Lim‐50) or high‐dose (100 mg/kg, Lim‐100) D‐limonene. Assessments included oxidative stress markers in testicular tissue (total antioxidant capacity [TAC], malondialdehyde [MDA], superoxide dismutase [SOD], and glutathione peroxidase [GPx]), macroscopic indices (testicular index [TI], absolute testicular weight [TW], and testicular weight index [TWI]), histopathological parameters (Johnsen score [JS], epithelial height [EHS], and seminiferous tubule diameter [STD]), apoptosis‐related proteins (Bax, Bcl‐2, Caspase‐3, and TNF‐α), serum testosterone, and sperm parameters.ResultsD‐limonene improved several indices of testicular injury following orchiopexy in cryptorchid rats. Both treatment doses increased TAC, while the higher dose more effectively reduced MDA and improved SOD activity. Testicular morphometric and histopathological parameters, including TW, TI, TWI, JS, EHS, and STD, were significantly improved. D‐Limonene also decreased pro‐apoptotic markers (Bax and Cleaved Caspase‐3), increased anti‐apoptotic markers (Bcl‐2 and Pro‐Caspase‐3), reduced TNF‐α expression and apoptotic cell counts, and enhanced serum testosterone and sperm quality. However, full normalization of all parameters was not achieved.ConclusionAdjunctive D‐limonene administration following orchiopexy attenuated oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis, and partially restored testicular structure and function in experimental cryptorchidism. These findings support D‐limonene as a promising supplementary strategy with partial but meaningful protective effects.Keywords: cryptorchidism, D‐limonene, orchiopexy, oxidative stress, spermatogenesis, testicular function

INTRODUCTION

Cryptorchidism is one of the most common congenital abnormalities in the male reproductive system and represents an important global public health concern. It affects 2%–9% of full‐term male newborns and exceeds 30% in those born prematurely. 1 , 2 Often associated with other genital disorders such as hypospadias and infertility, this condition plays a critical role in diminishing the quality of life and contributes to both psychological and physical health challenges. 3 Research on the etiologies of cryptorchidism highlights complex mechanisms shaped by genetic, environmental, dietary, and mechanical influences. 4 Furthermore, its increasing incidence has been linked to modern lifestyle changes and rising levels of environmental pollution. 5

Cryptorchidism causes significant alterations in testicular structure and function, leading to profound disruptions in the spermatogenesis process. Elevated intra‐abdominal testicular temperature induces oxidative stress, which not only amplifies the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) but also diminishes the activity of antioxidant enzymes, resulting in lipid peroxidation. 6 , 7 Along with promoting germ cell apoptosis, this condition also leads to morphological abnormalities, including seminiferous tubule shrinkage and a reduction in the germ cell count. 8 , 9 Furthermore, heat induced stress is associated with genetic modifications and molecular pathway imbalances, ultimately undermining sperm motility and overall quality. 10 , 11 Eventually, these structural and functional deteriorations in the testicles heighten the likelihood of infertility and potentially pave the way for precancerous developments. 12

Several strategies have been proposed for managing cryptorchidism, including surgical, hormonal, and antioxidant therapies. Orchiopexy, regarded as the treatment of choice, involves repositioning the testicles into the scrotum, effectively reducing the risks associated with infertility and testicular neoplasms by mitigating elevated temperature and oxidative stress. This surgical intervention provides the best outcomes when performed within the first year of life. 13 , 14 Nevertheless, research indicates that orchiopexy alone cannot address all the repercussions of prepubertal cryptorchidism, such as damage to the germinal epithelium and disrupted spermatogenesis. 15 , 16

Complementary treatments have aimed at limiting oxidative damage and germ‐cell apoptosis, most commonly through antioxidant strategies. In experimental cryptorchidism models, antioxidants such as vitamin E and plant‐derived compounds (e.g., Moringa oleifera) have been associated with improvements in sperm quality. 17 , 18 Hormonal treatments employing gonadotropins have also been explored to facilitate testicular descent and fertility enhancement; however, results remain inconsistent, and in some cases may even worsen germ cell apoptosis. Together, these observations underscore a key gap: while orchiopexy remains the most effective intervention for cryptorchidism, adjunct therapies may be needed to better address persistent oxidative and apoptotic injury beyond surgical correction alone. 19 , 20

D‐limonene, a naturally occurring citrus monoterpene, demonstrates antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and anti‐apoptotic activities with favorable oral bioavailability. 21 , 22 Importantly, its multi‐pathway profile (oxidative stress–inflammation–apoptosis axis) provides a mechanistic rationale for its use as an adjunct following orchiopexy and can be contrasted with other antioxidant approaches previously tested in cryptorchidism models or related post‐surgical frameworks (e.g., melatonin‐based adjunct strategies and antioxidant paradigms). 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 In addition, preclinical evidence supports D‐limonene–mediated protection in testicular oxidative‐injury settings, including improvements in sperm parameters and restoration of histological architecture. 27 , 28 , 29 Therefore, the novelty of the present study lies in evaluating D‐limonene as an adjunct therapy following orchiopexy in a prepubertal rat model of cryptorchidism, with the hypothesis that adjunct D‐limonene could enhance post‐orchiopexy recovery by mitigating persistent oxidative stress and apoptotic signaling beyond surgical repositioning alone.

METHODS

Animals and groups

Ethics Committee for the Use and Care of Animals of Lorestan University (LU.ECRA.2024.85) approved the experiments and all employed methods in this study were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines of Lorestan University Animal Ethics Committee. Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) reporting guidelines were followed. Twenty‐four male Sprague–Dawley rats (3 weeks old and weighing 40–50 g) free from any signs of disease were sexed and weaned from other neonates. Each animal was housed in separate wire cages under appropriate conditions of temperature, light, and humidity (22–24°C in a fixed cycle of 12 h of light, 12 h of darkness, and an average humidity of 55% ± 1%) and had free access to water and a standard rodent pellet. All stages of production, maintenance, and surgery of rats were performed at the Laboratory Animal Care Center of Lorestan University of Medical Sciences, Khorramabad, Iran.

Animals were randomly assigned to the four experimental groups (n = 6 per group) using a simple computer‐generated randomization procedure as follows:

Sham (SH): In this experimental group, the abdominal region of the rats was opened and closed without additional manipulation at two time points (days 21 and 42). The sham group did not undergo cryptorchidism induction or orchiopexy. After day 42, rats received 3 weeks of oral gavage of 1.5 mL distilled water.

Sham (SH): In this experimental group, the abdominal region of the rats was opened and closed without additional manipulation at two time points (days 21 and 42). The sham group did not undergo cryptorchidism induction or orchiopexy. After day 42, rats received 3 weeks of oral gavage of 1.5 mL distilled water.

Control test (CT): Cryptorchidism was surgically induced in 21‐day‐old rats and orchiopexy was performed 3 weeks later (42‐day‐old rats) followed by 3 weeks of oral gavage of 1.5 mL distilled water.

Control test (CT): Cryptorchidism was surgically induced in 21‐day‐old rats and orchiopexy was performed 3 weeks later (42‐day‐old rats) followed by 3 weeks of oral gavage of 1.5 mL distilled water.

D‐limonene 50 mg/kg (Lim‐50): Cryptorchidism was induced in 21‐day‐old rats and orchiopexy was performed 3 weeks later. Thereafter, rats received D‐limonene (50 mg/kg/day; 8.18407.0100, Merck, Germany) by oral gavage for 3 weeks. The selected dose range was based on prior in vivo rat studies using 50–100 mg/kg oral D‐limonene and reporting antioxidant/anti‐inflammatory effects. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33

D‐limonene 50 mg/kg (Lim‐50): Cryptorchidism was induced in 21‐day‐old rats and orchiopexy was performed 3 weeks later. Thereafter, rats received D‐limonene (50 mg/kg/day; 8.18407.0100, Merck, Germany) by oral gavage for 3 weeks. The selected dose range was based on prior in vivo rat studies using 50–100 mg/kg oral D‐limonene and reporting antioxidant/anti‐inflammatory effects. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33

D‐limonene 100 mg/kg (Lim‐100): Cryptorchidism was surgically induced at 21 days of age followed by orchiopexy 3 weeks later. Thereafter, rats received D‐limonene (100 mg/kg/day) by oral gavage for 3 weeks. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33

D‐limonene 100 mg/kg (Lim‐100): Cryptorchidism was surgically induced at 21 days of age followed by orchiopexy 3 weeks later. Thereafter, rats received D‐limonene (100 mg/kg/day) by oral gavage for 3 weeks. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33

The selected doses (50 and 100 mg/kg/day) were based on previous experimental studies reporting significant antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory effects of D‐limonene within this range without evidence of systemic toxicity. These doses fall within the established therapeutic window in rodent models.

Body weight was recorded at days 21, 42, and 63 to monitor growth/tolerability, and no abnormal weight‐gain trends were observed in any group.

To model early surgical correction of cryptorchidism, we selected an experimental timeline that approximates the clinical window of early orchiopexy in human infants (commonly recommended at 6–12 months) to preserve testicular development and future fertility potential. 34 , 35 In rats, physiological testicular descent occurs around four weeks of age; therefore, inducing cryptorchidism at day 21 (weaning period with active testicular development) and performing orchiopexy at 42 spans a critical prepubertal‐to‐early pubertal window and has been utilized in experimental cryptorchidism/orchiopexy models assessing subsequent testicular development and spermatogenesis. 36 , 37 , 38 This design aims to reflect a clinically relevant “early‐correctable” scenario in which timely orchiopexy may allow partial recovery while residual impairments can persist, thereby enabling evaluation of adjunctive therapies.

All outcome assessments, including biochemical assays, Western blot analyses, histological scoring, and sperm evaluations, were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation to minimize measurement bias.

Surgical procedure

Cryptorchidism induction

Rats were anesthetized using ketamine and xylazine (80 and 10 mg/kg). After aseptic preparation, a longitudinal lower midline abdominal incision was made and the testes were exposed. To induce bilateral cryptorchidism, the anterior pole of each testis was secured to the abdominal wall near the ipsilateral kidney by placing a 5–0 nylon suture through the paratesticular fat and anchoring it to the abdominal wall. The linea alba and skin were then closed in routine layers, and animals were kept warm until full recovery.

Orchiopexy

Three weeks following cryptorchidism induction (42 days of age), rats were re‐anesthetized and prepared aseptically as described above. The previous abdominal incision was reopened and both testes were identified intra‐abdominally. The fixation suture anchoring each testis to the abdominal wall was carefully released. To prepare the scrotal pouch, the distal scrotal skin was gently grasped with forceps and temporarily inverted toward the abdominal cavity. A 5–0 nylon suture was then placed between the paratesticular fat of each testis and the inner surface of the scrotal wall. By gently applying traction to the scrotal forceps, each testis was guided through the inguinal canal into the scrotum and secured in its anatomical position. Testicular position was routinely monitored by scrotal palpation during daily handling and oral gavage procedures until euthanasia.

In the SH group animals in both surgical stages, after completing all preoperative preparation stages, only opening of the abdominal area and limited manipulation of the testes were performed, and then the abdominal wall was closed. To prevent infection after the surgeries, animals received oral antibiotics (enrofloxacin, 15 mg/kg) through drinking water for seven days. All animals recovered well, and no signs of local infections were observed.

No animals were excluded due to unsuccessful induction or postoperative complications.

Sampling

On day 63 after birth, all rats were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of ketamine and xylazine (300 and 30 mg/kg) after recording the weight and position of the testes. Cardiac blood samples were obtained from the animals and stored in serum‐separating tubes. After 30 min, serum was separated by centrifugation (15 min at 5000 rpm) and stored at −20°C for measuring levels of blood testosterone by ELISA. For histological and immunofluorescence evaluations, testicular tissue samples were initially fixed in 10% buffered formalin for 48 h. After fixation, each sample was bisected: one half was processed directly for paraffin embedding and used for immunofluorescence staining, whereas the other half was post‐fixed in Bouin's solution for 24 h, then paraffin‐embedded and used for H&E staining. Bouin's solution was used only for H&E processing. The remaining testicular tissues were stored at −80°C. Before performing biochemical tests to determine protein levels and enzyme activities, 500 mg of thawed tissues were homogenized with 500 μL of lysis buffer. The resulting sample was centrifuged at 4°C for 10 min at 12 000 rpm. The supernatant was extracted and stored in labeled plain sample tubes at −20°C.

Antioxidant capacity

The concentrations of total antioxidant capacity (TAC), malondialdehyde (MDA), and the activities of glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) enzymes in the supernatant were measured by the colorimetric method. The concentrations of TAC and MDA in rat testicular tissue were measured at wavelengths of 517 and 532 nm, respectively, using commercial kits (Zenbio, North Carolina, USA, Kit No. AOX‐3 and ZellBio, Berlin, Germany, Kit No. ZB‐MDA96, respectively).

The activities of SOD and GPx enzymes in rat testicular tissue were also measured at wavelengths of 550 and 412 nm, respectively, using commercial kits (Elabscience, Texas, USA, Kit No. E‐BC‐K019‐S and ZellBio, Berlin, Germany, Kit No. ZB‐GPX‐A48). All steps were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Histopathological parameters

Hematoxylin and eosin assessment

Histopathological parameters of the testicular tissue in the experimental groups were evaluated using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and three parameters were evaluated: Johnsen score (JS), epithelial height (EHS), and seminiferous tubule diameter (STD). For this purpose, 5 μm‐thick sections were made from the paraffin block using a microtome and placed on slides. After the deparaffinization process, hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed on the sections. Using a light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a micrometer plate, 50 perfectly round tubules were randomly selected at a magnification of ×40 from each slide and observed by a pathologist unaware of group assignments. Johnsen score (from 1 to 10 and based on the presence and maturity of germ cells), 39 , 40 epithelial height and seminiferous tubule diameter were measured in these tubules.

Immunofluorescence assessment

The assessment of apoptosis in different spermatogenic cell lines was performed using immunofluorescence staining of apoptosis‐related proteins. Initially, slides were prepared with 5 μm‐thick sections from paraffin embedded testicular tissue blocks, and the deparaffinization process was performed using xylene and hydration with serial concentrations of ethanol. To retrieve antigens, slides were heated with citrate buffer (pH = 6.2) for 20 min in a microwave oven (600 W). Subsequently, to reduce nonspecific binding of antibodies, samples were incubated for 2 h at room temperature in 10% normal serum or 1% BSA in TBS buffer. Samples were exposed to primary antibodies initially for 4 h at 37°C (turbocharged reaction) and overnight at 4°C. Primary antibodies and their dilution in buffer were as follows: Bax (sc‐7480, 1:300), Bcl‐2 (sc‐492, 1:300), Cleaved Caspase‐3 (sc‐22 171‐R, 1:300), purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Texas, USA. The slides were then exposed to secondary antibodies conjugated to fluorescent dyes (Goat Anti‐Rabbit IgG(H + L)(FITC conjugated), E‐AB‐1014, 1:60, and Goat Anti‐Mouse IgG(H + L)(CY3 conjugated), E‐AB‐1011, 1:75, purchased from Elabscience, Wuhan, China) for 10 min at room temperature. Between all the above steps, the slides were washed by gentle agitation in TBS plus 0.03% Triton X‐100. DAPI staining was performed to stain the nuclei of all cells and the slides were dehydrated using serial dilutions of ethanol and xylene. After mounting and coverslipping, the slides were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX50) and photographed using a camera (Olympus DP72).

For immunofluorescence TUNEL staining, the procedure was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (E‐CK‐A334, Elabscience, Wuhan, China). After fixation, the samples were exposed to a TUNEL reaction cocktail, containing the TdT enzyme and Enhanced Fluorescein Mix, at 37°C for 60 min to label fragmented DNA associated with apoptosis. Following washing, the samples were counterstained with DAPI, mounted, and analyzed using a fluorescence microscope. Antibody specificity was verified based on manufacturer validation data and previous peer‐reviewed studies reporting their use in rodent testicular tissue.

Western blot

The Western blot technique was used to measure the amount of proteins. For this purpose, the total protein concentration in each sample was initially obtained using the Bradford assay at a wavelength of 630 nm and was determined in mg/mL using a standard curve drawn with the BSA standard protein. Based on the average Bradford data for each sample, the amount of water and sample buffer required to equalize the samples was determined and this mixture was heated to 100°C for 5‐10 min. After preparing the upper and lower gels, 12 μL of the samples were added to each well and electrophoresis was performed on SDS‐PAGE gel for about 45 min at a voltage of 120 V. To identify the protein bands of interest, all the proteins separated in the gel were initially transferred to a PVDF membrane using a 120 V current of the Western blot device for 90 min. Before adding the primary antibody, PVDF membrane was shaken with a blocking solution (2% non‐fat dry milk in TBS‐T buffer) for 75 min to prevent unwanted reaction of the primary antibody. After the blocking step, each PVDF membrane was incubated with specific primary antibodies diluted in a blocking solution for 16–18 h. Primary antibodies and their dilution ratios in blocking solution are as follows: β‐Actin (sc‐517 582, 1:200), Bax (sc‐526, 1:200), Bcl‐2 (sc‐492 1:200), TNF‐α (sc‐133 192, 1:100) (purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Texas, USA) and Caspase‐3 Rabbit mAb (D3R6Y, 1:1000) (purchased from Cell Signaling Technology, Massachusetts, USA). Before and after incubation with secondary antibody (mouse anti‐rabbit IgG‐HRP, 1:1000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Texas, USA), PVDF membrane was washed three times consecutively for 15 min each time with TBS‐T buffer. Detection of the desired protein band was performed using the ECL advanced reagents Chemoluminescence kit and the ratio of solution (A/B Reagents and non‐fat milk) to the paper surface was considered to be 0.1 mL/cm. Finally, the PVDF membrane covered with cellophane was placed in the cassette with the film for 10 to 80 s (depending on the type of protein). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence and captured using a digital imaging system. Exposure times were optimized individually for each membrane to avoid signal saturation and ensure linear detection. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software, and target protein expression levels were normalized to the corresponding loading control (β‐actin). Relative protein expression was calculated as the ratio of target band intensity to the loading control band intensity for each sample. The specificity of each antibody was confirmed by detection of a single band corresponding to the predicted molecular weight of the target protein.

Serum testosterone level

Testosterone levels in serum samples were determined using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). All assay steps were performed using a commercial kit (55‐TESMS‐E01, ALPCO, New Hampshire, USA) and values were obtained as OD at 450 nm. A standard curve was used to convert absorbance to ng/mL.

Sperm evaluations

CASA analysis

The concentration and motility parameters were evaluated using a computer‐assisted sperm analysis system (CASA) (Test sperm 3.2, Videotest, St Petersburg, Russia), which was adjusted based on the characteristics of rat sperm. Calibration included verification of microscope stage temperature, optical focus, and frame capture rate. Sperm detection parameters were standardized across all samples, including minimum sperm head size, contrast threshold, and velocity thresholds for motility classification. The frame rate and number of frames analyzed per sample were kept constant to ensure consistent tracking of sperm trajectories. All CASA analyses were performed using identical threshold settings across groups to minimize analytical variability. For this purpose, 7 μL of sperm sample was placed on a slide at 37°C (to prevent heat shock to sperm) and a coverslip was placed over it. Sperm motility parameters such as percentage of total motility (TM) and percentage of progressive motility (PM) were evaluated using a fraction of the sperm sample. Sperm kinetic parameters such as curvilinear velocity (VCL), straight line velocity (VSL), average path velocity (VAP), amplitude of lateral head displacement (ALH), beat‐cross frequency (BCF), linearity (LIN, which is equal to dividing VSL by VCL) and straightness (STR, which is equal to the ratio of VSL to VAP) were also evaluated. The CASA system settings are listed in Table 1. In this evaluation, a dark‐field or phase‐contrast microscope (Olympus, BX41, Tokyo, Japan) was used to observe at least 500 sperm in five microscopic fields.

Eosin‐nigrosin staining

This study used eosin‐nigrosin staining to evaluate the percentage of sperm viability and morphology. With this method, cells that took on the eosin dye due to damaged membranes and turned pink were considered dead sperm. In addition, nigrosin stain, by creating contrast between sperm and the background, made it easier to detect morphological disorders in the head, middle part, and tail of sperm or the presence of cytoplasmic droplets, and sperm with morphological defects were reported as a percentage of healthy sperm. For this purpose, 20 μL of diluted sperm suspension was combined with 20 μL of eosin solution and after 30 s, 20 μL of nigrosin dye solution was added to it. After preparing a smear from the final solution and drying it, the slides were observed under a light microscope at ×400 magnification.

HOST staining

In this study, the hypoosmotic swelling test (HOST) was used to evaluate the efficiency of the sperm plasma membrane. This test aims to determine how the sperm membrane responds and tolerates osmotic changes. Accordingly, if the sperm shows signs such as swelling or tail curling after exposure to a hypoosmotic environment, it is considered to have a functional plasma membrane, and if it is observed as unchanged and with an elongated tail, it is considered to have a damaged plasma membrane. To perform this test, each sperm sample was mixed with a hypoosmotic solution (containing sodium citrate and fructose at concentrations of 0.735 and 1.351 g/L, respectively) in a ratio of 1:10 and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. After that, 5 μL of the solution with 5 μL of eosin dye was placed on a preheated slide, and 200 sperm from each sample were observed using a phase contrast microscope at ×400 magnification. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44

Acridine orange staining

In this study, acridine orange staining was used to evaluate sperm DNA. Sperm that show the most fluorescence when observed and are in the yellow to red color spectrum have the most damage to their double‐stranded DNA. On the other hand, sperm in the green color spectrum are considered to have intact DNA. After the sperm samples were washed three times in a row with PBS solution and finally concentrated with this solution, smears were prepared from all samples. The prepared slides were placed in Carnoy's fixative for 2 h to fix the cells and dried at room temperature. Staining was performed by placing the slides in acridine orange solution (1 mg acridine orange in 1000 mL water) for 7 min in the dark at 4°C. A fluorescent microscope (Model GS7, Nikon Co., Tokyo, Japan) with ×400 magnification and a 460 nm filter was used to observe the samples. Two hundred cells were counted per slide and cells with damaged DNA were reported as a percentage of healthy cells. 42 , 45 , 46

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 26). Normality was assessed using the one‐sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± SD and analyzed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD post‐hoc test. Since numerous biological parameters were evaluated, the potential for inflated Type I error is acknowledged, and data with marginally significant results should be interpreted cautiously. Non‐normally distributed variables (JS, EHS, and STD) were expressed as median (IQR) and analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test; when significant, pairwise comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni adjustment (Dunn–Bonferroni‐type procedure). A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Antioxidant capacity

The antioxidant capacity of rat testes was evaluated by measuring TAC, MDA levels, and the enzymatic activities of SOD and GPx, as illustrated in Figure 1. A significant reduction in TAC levels was observed in the CT group compared with the SH group (p ≤ 0.05). Administration of D‐limonene at both doses (Lim‐50 and Lim‐100) significantly enhanced TAC concentrations relative to the CT group (p ≤ 0.05); however, no significant differences were noted between the Lim‐50 and Lim‐100 treatments, nor between either treatment group and SH. MDA concentrations were significantly lower in SH compared with the experimental groups (p ≤ 0.001). Among the treatments, only the high dose of D‐limonene (Lim‐100) significantly decreased MDA levels relative to CT (p ≤ 0.05), indicating a dose‐related attenuation of lipid peroxidation without complete normalization to SH. SOD activity showed a notable decline in CT, Lim‐50, and Lim‐100 compared with SH (p ≤ 0.001 for CT and Lim‐50; p ≤ 0.05 for Lim‐100). Although the high dose of D‐limonene significantly improved SOD activity compared with CT (p ≤ 0.05), SOD in Lim‐100 remained significantly lower than SH, reflecting incomplete restoration of this enzymatic antioxidant defense. GPx activity was significantly higher in SH than in all other groups (p ≤ 0.001). While both doses of D‐limonene increased GPx activity numerically relative to CT, this improvement did not reach statistical significance in post‐hoc comparisons.

Collectively, these antioxidant measurements indicate that orchiopexy alone (CT group) does not restore redox balance in cryptorchid testes. Adjunct D‐limonene treatment improves oxidative status most consistently by restoring TAC and reducing MDA (particularly at the higher dose), whereas antioxidant enzyme activities—especially SOD and GPx—show incomplete recovery toward sham levels.

Spermatogenesis

The quality of spermatogenesis was evaluated both qualitatively and quantitatively through hematoxylin and eosin staining. Representative images of testicular tissue from experimental groups are presented in Figure 2. In contrast, the CT group, which experienced cryptorchidism without D‐limonene treatment, exhibited severe disruptions in the architecture of spermatogenic tissue. Both primary and secondary spermatocytes were highly disorganized, with notable structural irregularities. Neither spermatids nor spermatozoa were observed in this group, highlighting a profound impairment in the spermatogenic process.

The Lim‐50 group, treated with a low dose of D‐limonene alongside surgical intervention, showed moderate improvements in the structure of the seminiferous tubules. While spermatocyte alignment demonstrated considerable progress compared to the CT group, irregularities still persisted. Although spermatids and spermatozoa were present, specific areas of the germinal epithelium remained vacant and inactive, indicating incomplete tissue regeneration.

Significant tissue repair was observed in the Lim‐100 group, which received a higher dose of D‐limonene. Structural recovery of the seminiferous tubules was markedly improved, accompanied by enhanced spermatogenesis. Organized layers of spermatids and spermatozoa were clearly visible within the germinal epithelium. However, some residual signs of cellular damage, including pyknosis (nuclear shrinkage) and karyorrhexis (nuclear fragmentation), persisted as remnants of necrotic processes.

Quantitative data related to spermatogenesis, including Johnsen score (JS), epithelial height (EHS), and seminiferous tubule diameter (STD), are summarized in Table 2. The JS in the SH group was significantly higher than in the other three experimental groups (p < 0.05). Both Lim‐50 and Lim‐100 groups demonstrated significantly higher JS values compared to the CT group (p < 0.05), but no significant differences were observed between the two doses of D‐limonene. Epithelial height in the CT group was significantly lower than in the other groups (p < 0.05), with no significant differences detected among the remaining groups. Similarly, the STD in the CT group was markedly smaller than in the other groups (p < 0.05). Although no significant differences were observed between the high and low doses of D‐limonene, the tubule diameter was significantly reduced in the Lim‐100 group compared to the SH group (p < 0.05).

DISCUSSION

Cryptorchidism is a common congenital anomaly affecting 2%–9% of full term and over 30% of preterm infants, with most cases being unilateral. 1 , 2 The failure of testicular descent exposes germ cells to supraphysiologic abdominal temperatures, promoting oxidative stress‐driven injury that increases ROS, lipid peroxidation, and germ‐cell apoptosis, ultimately impairing fertility and elevating the risk of malignancy. 6 , 7 , 8 , 47 Although orchiopexy remains the standard treatment, accumulating evidence indicates that surgery alone rarely restores full spermatogenic and endocrine function, leaving persistent long term deficits. 13 , 15 , 16 , 48 This has encouraged interest in adjunctive antioxidant therapies. D‐limonene, a monoterpene with antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and anti‐apoptotic activity, has shown testicular protective effects, including reductions in ROS and enhanced histological architecture. 21 , 22 , 27 , 28 Our study therefore evaluated two doses of D‐limonene as adjuncts to orchiopexy in a juvenile rat bilateral cryptorchid model to mitigate oxidative injury and support testicular recovery. 49 , 50

In our experiment, no statistically significant differences emerged between the 50 and 100 mg/kg groups for several outcomes, suggesting a partial dose response plateau. This pattern implies that major antioxidant benefits may be achieved near 50 mg/kg, with higher doses providing limited incremental improvement, although the overall trend still favored the higher dose. The multiplicity of biochemical and functional endpoints increases the probability of Type I error; thus, borderline p‐values should be interpreted cautiously. Nonetheless, the absence of adverse effects supports the safety of the administered doses.

Oxidative stress is well recognized as a central mechanism of cryptorchid injury. Prior studies have shown heat induced ROS overproduction leading to elevated MDA and diminished SOD and catalase activities, 51 , 52 , 53 with similar observations regarding GPx reductions. 54 , 55 DNA damage has also been linked to impaired antioxidant activity. 56 In alignment with this literature, our CT group demonstrated increased MDA and reduced the levels of SOD and GPx. D‐limonene improved overall antioxidant capacity most consistently, as indicated by partial restoration of TAC and attenuation of lipid peroxidation, although MDA did not fully normalize. Enzymatic antioxidants responded less robustly; SOD increased but remained subnormal, and GPx improvement did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that D‐limonene more effectively enhances global redox capacity than fully restores all enzymatic defenses, consistent with previous evidence supporting multimodal antioxidant approaches. 17

D‐limonene's mechanistic relevance relates to its influence on oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis, paralleling reports on vitamin E, melatonin, and other adjunctive antioxidants in cryptorchidism. 23 , 24 , 25 , 57 Prior studies indicate that D‐limonene modulates redox inflammatory signaling pathways (NF κB/Nrf2) and reduces MAPK/NF κB mediated injury. 26 , 29 , 58 TNF α and ROS exhibit bidirectional amplification, further linking oxidative injury to inflammatory cascades. 59 Preservation of Leydig cell steroidogenesis represents an additional mechanism through which D‐limonene may support spermatogenesis, given the susceptibility of Leydig cells to heat induced oxidative damage and the critical role of testosterone in Sertoli cell function and germ cell development. 60 , 61 , 62

Morphological alterations in cryptorchidism commonly include reduced testicular weight, tubule diameter, and germ cell layer thickness. 63 , 64 Although orchiopexy improves morphology, complete restoration is rarely achieved. 15 , 48 , 65 Consistent with these observations, the CT group showed reduced TW, TWI, and TI, whereas D‐limonene improved these indices, particularly at the higher dose. These findings are in line with prior studies demonstrating the capacity of antioxidants to enhance structural recovery. 66 , 67 Similarly, histopathological analysis revealed severe disruption in cryptorchid testes, while adjunct D‐limonene treatment improved seminiferous architecture, though not completely, consistent with reports of partial but incomplete restoration. 63 , 64 , 68 , 69 , 70

In the present study, histopathological evaluations revealed that the SH group exhibited intact seminiferous tubules with well‐preserved spermatogenesis, whereas the CT group demonstrated severe structural disruption, characterized by reduced EHS, diminished STD, and an absence of mature germ cells. Complementary treatment with D‐limonene markedly improved tissue organization, with the high dose producing a marked recovery, as evidenced by an increased Johnsen score and enhanced tissue homogeneity, and the low dose resulting in moderate improvements despite some minor irregularities, findings that align with reports by Tekayev et al. 70 and Abd‐El‐Hafez et al., 71 which indicate that although antioxidant supplementation can significantly ameliorate the microscopic architecture of seminiferous tubules, complete restoration to normal histopathological conditions is not fully achieved. 55 , 67

Apoptosis plays a major role in cryptorchid testicular damage, extending beyond normal spermatogonial turnover to involve primary spermatocytes and round spermatids under heat stress. 72 , 73 , 74 Elevated ROS triggers both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways through Bax/Bcl‐2 imbalance and caspase activation. 75 , 76

In our study, cryptorchid testes displayed increased Bax and Cleaved Caspase‐3 with reduced Bcl‐2, consistent with prior findings. 18 , 77 The observed increase in pro‐apoptotic markers is consistent with the notion that exposure to elevated intra‐abdominal temperatures promotes mitochondrial dysfunction and triggers the intrinsic apoptotic cascade, ultimately leading to extensive germ cell loss and structural damage to the seminiferous tubules. 66 , 78 This shift not only impairs spermatogenesis but also contributes to the atrophy seen in the cryptorchid testis. Our results further reveal that treatment with D‐limonene produces a dose‐dependent protective effect against testicular apoptosis; specifically, D‐limonene supplementation led to a significant reduction in the levels of Bax and cleaved Caspase‐3 while simultaneously enhancing Bcl‐2 expression. These data support earlier findings by Kumagai et al. 77 and DeFoor et al., 79 and are further corroborated by the work of Kaur et al. 57 which collectively suggest that effective antioxidant interventions can attenuate the deleterious effects of heat‐induced oxidative stress in cryptorchid testes. 57 , 80 TNF α expression was also markedly reduced in the high dose group, suggesting attenuation of inflammatory apoptotic signaling. 81 Reports on testosterone levels in cryptorchidism are variable, with some noting preserved serum levels and others showing declines due to Leydig cell dysfunction. 82 , 83 , 84 , 85 In our study, testosterone decreased in the CT group but improved dose‐dependently with D‐limonene, nearing normalization at 100 mg/kg. This supports its protective effects on Leydig cells and endocrine function.

Finally, cryptorchidism disrupts spermatogenesis through thermal and oxidative stress, impairing sperm morphology, motility, and viability. 77 , 79 , 86 Consistent with prior research, our CT group displayed poor sperm parameters, while D‐limonene significantly enhanced count, motility, morphology, and viability in a dose‐dependent manner. These outcomes align with recent evidence demonstrating benefits of antioxidant supplementation alongside orchiopexy. 77

CONCLUSION

In summary, orchiopexy alone does not fully reverse oxidative, endocrine, or spermatogenic impairments associated with cryptorchidism. Adjunctive D‐limonene treatment, particularly at 100 mg/kg, effectively enhanced global antioxidant capacity, reduced lipid peroxidation, and partially improved enzymatic antioxidant defenses. These biochemical effects were accompanied by structural recovery, improved testosterone levels, and marked enhancement of sperm parameters, although complete normalization of all indices was not achieved. Collectively, these findings support D‐limonene as a promising supplemental strategy to mitigate testicular injury in cryptorchidism. Further studies exploring molecular pathways and clinical translation are warranted.
