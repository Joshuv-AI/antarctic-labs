Respiratory function profiling using non-invasive methods in children aged 7–18 years: a hospital based study from North India
Authors: Kashika Mendiratta, Smita Nair, Meenakshi Bothra, Aayushi Kalra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRespiratory health during childhood is crucial for optimal growth and long-term well-being. Early lung function impairment may remain clinically silent, yet predispose to chronic respiratory morbidity. While spirometry is the standard for assessing airway function, its routine use in children is often limited. Simple bedside tests such as Single Breath Count (SBC), Breath Holding Time (BHT), and Peak Expiratory Flow Rate (PEFR) are non-invasive, inexpensive, and feasible in outpatient and community settings, and may serve as practical screening tools for early detection of ventilatory dysfunction.MethodologyThis hospital-based observational study was conducted in the Department of Pediatrics at a tertiary care teaching hospital in North-Western Uttar Pradesh after ethical approval and informed consent. Children aged 7–18 years attending the outpatient department were enrolled; those with acute respiratory illness or severe systemic disease were excluded. Anthropometry was recorded, and respiratory function was assessed using SBC, BHT, and PEFR following standardized protocols. These respiratory parameters were analyzed in relation to age, height, and BMI using descriptive and inferential statistical methods.ResultsAmong 190 children (mean age 11.5 ± 2.1 years), mean SBC, BHT, and PEFR were 32.4 ± 12.9, 30.4 ± 10.9, and 170.8 ± 60.4 L/min, respectively. All respiratory parameters increased with age, with PEFR peaking at 13 years. SBC and BHT showed significant positive associations with PEFR; There was a statistically significant positive correlation between SBC, BHT, PEFR and anthropometric parameters. On regression analysis, age and height were found to significantly influence the PEFR reading.ConclusionBreath-holding time and height are significant predictors of PEFR in children. Simple bedside respiratory measures, SBC and BHT, may serve as practical adjuncts for assessing pulmonary function in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07138-1.

Introduction

Respiratory health is a vital component of childhood well-being and plays a central role in physical growth, cognitive development, and overall quality of life. The lungs undergo continuous growth and maturation throughout childhood and adolescence, making this period particularly sensitive to environmental exposures, infections, nutritional status, and chronic diseases. Despite this, early respiratory impairment in children often remains under recognized, as many affected individuals may not exhibit obvious clinical symptoms during the initial stages of the disease. Subtle abnormalities in lung function can precede overt respiratory illness by several years.

Early identification of these changes is therefore crucial, as it enables timely intervention, prevents deterioration, and reduces the likelihood of long-term respiratory morbidity. Chronic respiratory illnesses in children often manifest with airflow limitation of varying severity within the tracheobronchial tree, which can be picked up by pulmonary function testing [1].

Peak Expiratory Flow Rate (PEFR) is a simple, effort-dependent measure widely used to assess airway function and monitor respiratory disorders. Several Indian studies, including those by Dikshit MB and Chowgule RV, have established normative PEFR values in healthy children and demonstrated strong associations with height, age, and other anthropometric parameters [2, 3]. More recent regional data from North and East India further emphasize the need for population-specific reference standards [4, 5].

Spirometry remains the gold standard for lung function assessment, as highlighted by Liou TG; however, its availability is often limited in primary care and school settings due to equipment cost and the need for trained personnel [6]. In addition, younger children may have difficulty understanding or following the required instructions, leading to poor-quality or unreliable results. Consequently, simple non-invasive bedside measures such as Single Breath Counting (SBC) and Breath-Holding Time (BHT) have been explored as potential surrogate markers of pulmonary function. These measures are inexpensive, relatively easy to perform, require minimal equipment and can be conducted in a short period of time, making them particularly suitable for use in outpatient clinics, schools and community-based settings. Studies by Bartfield JM and Ali SS demonstrated correlations between SBC and spirometric indices, while Aggarwal V reported significant associations between BHT and spirometry parameters [7, 8, 9].

SBC is a simple test that reflects the individual’s ability to sustain phonation during a single expiration and indirectly assesses ventilatory capacity. BHT measures the duration for which a person can voluntarily hold their breath after maximal inspiration, providing an indirect estimate of respiratory muscle strength and lung capacity. PEFR especially in obstructive airway diseases assesses the maximum speed of expiration and is widely used to detect and monitor airflow limitation [2]. Although these tests do not replace comprehensive pulmonary function testing but they have the potential to serve as effective screening tools.

Despite these findings, limited data exist evaluating the combined relationship of SBC, BHT, PEFR, and anthropometric parameters in Indian school-aged children and adolescents. The present study aims to examine these associations and explore the utility of simple bedside respiratory measures as adjuncts to conventional lung function assessment. The primary objective of the present study was to assess the respiratory function amongst children aged 7–18 years using single breath count (SBC), breath holding time (BHT) and peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR). The secondary objectives were to study the correlation of PEFR with SBC and BHT and to study the association between non-invasive respiratory parameters (SBC, BHT and PEFR) and the anthropometric parameters (weight, height and age) of the enrolled children. We also intended to examine the relationship of PEFR with anthropometric variables, using regression analysis.

Methodology

Prior to initiation of the study, approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee. Participation was entirely voluntary, written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians of all enrolled children, and assent was taken from the children. The research was conducted as a hospital-based observational study in the Department of Pediatrics at a tertiary care teaching hospital in North-Western Uttar Pradesh. The study involved children aged 7 to 18 years who visited the pediatric outpatient department and fulfilled the eligibility criteria. Patients with respiratory complaints or fever, suffering from any critical systemic illness requiring PICU admission or with a history of ever being on mechanical ventilator for more than 1 week in the last 6 months were excluded from the study.

For the anthropometric assessments, Height was measured in centimeters using a stadiometer and Weight was taken in kilograms using a calibrated digital weighing scale. Measurements were recorded to the nearest whole number to ensure measurement accuracy.

Respiratory function was evaluated using following non-invasive bedside methods:

Single Breath Count (SBC) which was assessed by instructing the child to take a deep inspiration and then count aloud at a steady, standardized pace of two counts per second using metronome beats application without pausing for another breath. The test was repeated thrice with an interval of at least one minute, and the highest value was noted.Breath Holding Time (BHT) was measured by asking the participant to take a deep inspiration and hold their breath for as long as possible. The duration (in seconds) for which the breath could be held was recorded using a stopwatch. The test was performed twice and the higher reading was recorded.Peak Expiratory Flow Rate (PEFR) was measured using a peak flow meter. The participant was instructed to take a deep breath followed by a forceful and rapid expiration into the device. Three readings were taken with sufficient rest intervals and the highest value was noted.

Data were entered in Microsoft Excel and analysed in Python (version 3.x) in a Jupyter Notebook. Descriptive statistics, group comparisons, and correlation analyses were performed using the pandas and scipy libraries; regression modelling was performed using the statsmodels package; and figures were prepared using seaborn and matplotlib. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, median, interquartile range, and range. Pearson correlation coefficients with corresponding p-values and 95% confidence intervals (Fisher z transformation) were computed for the pairwise associations among SBC, BHT, and PEFR, and between each respiratory parameter and the anthropometric variables age, height, weight, and BMI. Differences between dependent correlations sharing a common variable (PEFR) were compared using Williams’s test.

A multivariable ordinary least squares (OLS) regression model was constructed to examine the relationship of PEFR with anthropometric variables. PEFR was selected as the dependent variable because, among the three bedside respiratory measures studied, PEFR is the most objective, instrumented, and clinically actionable indicator of expiratory airflow, and is the parameter for which population-specific prediction equations are most commonly required in pediatric practice. Candidate independent variables chosen a priori on the basis of established pediatric pulmonary physiology and consistency with published Indian PEFR prediction equations were height (cm) and age (years).

Variables were entered together in a single block; no stepwise or data-driven variable selection was applied, in order to avoid model overfitting and biased standard errors. Multicollinearity was assessed by the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF below 4 considered acceptable. Normality of residuals was assessed by visual inspection of the plot and homoscedasticity was assessed by inspection of residual-versus-fitted plots. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

This observational cross-sectional study included 190 children and adolescents from 7 to 18 years. The mean age of the study population was 11.5 ± 2.1 years and the range was 7 to 18 years. The maximum number of children enrolled were 11–12 years old, with the age distribution showing a bell-shaped curve, as seen in Fig. 1. The distribution of the SBC, BHT and PEFR among the study population has been shown in Table 1. The mean SBC was 32.4 ± 12.9, BHT was 30.4 ± 10.9 s and PEFR was 170.8 ± 60.5 L/min.

The OLS regression model with PEFR as the dependent variable and height and age as predictors is PEFR (L/min) = − 62.9 + 1.3 × Height (cm) + 5.3 × Age (years). This model was statistically significant (F(2, 187) = 21.48, p < 0.005), with R² = 0.187 and adjusted R² = 0.178, indicating that approximately 18% of the variance in PEFR was explained by these two anthropometric predictors.

For illustrative reference, this equation predicts a PEFR of approximately 135.5 L/min in a 8-year-old child, with a height of 120 cm, 177 L/min in an 11-year-old adolescent with a height of 140 cm, and 234 L/min in a 16-year-old adolescent, with a height of 170 cm.

The variance inflation factor was less than 4 for both predictors, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. Residuals were approximately normally distributed the residuals are homoscedastic as per the residual plot, depicted in supplementary Fig. 1. Discussion

The present study demonstrates significant positive associations between simple bedside respiratory measures (SBC and BHT) and PEFR, with height emerging as the strongest anthropometric predictor. The results reflected wide physiological variability expected in a pediatric population. This trend may be related to sample distribution in older age groups or variability in effort-dependent measurements. In the present study, PEFR showed an overall tendency to increase with age and height. The observed age-wise rise in PEFR and its peak during early adolescence are consistent with Indian pediatric reference studies by Gunasekaran A and Chowgule RV, who reported progressive increases in PEFR with growth and pubertal development [1, 3]. The study by Gunasekaran et al. show that with one centimeter increase in height there was 5.1% increase in PEFR [1]. However a study predicted equation for boys based on height alone was PEFR = 4.08 height (cm)--284.55 and for girls was PEFR = 3.92 height (cm)--277.01 [13]. It is also reported that the regression equation based on height for the combined Indian and Nepalese young adults is calculated to be: PEFR = 5.687 x Height (cm) − 495.787 [14]. Similar findings have been documented in South and North Indian cohorts by Budhiraja S and Parikh S, reinforcing the influence of somatic growth on lung function [4, 10].

Height showed the strongest correlation with PEFR in our cohort, aligning with classical Indian prediction models proposed by Dikshit MB and Raju PS [2, 11]. As reported by Syed Sameer Ali in 2011, the correlation (r) of SBC to PEFR was found to be 0.55 in the pediatric population, while another study conducted on adults predicted this correlation by r = 0.68 [7, 8]. Similarly, Breath Holding Time (BHT) showed weak-to-moderate positive correlation (r = 0.32) with PEFR., suggesting that breath-holding capacity reflects underlying respiratory efficiency, further demonstrated by a study of 2018 finding the correlation (r) of BHT to PEFR to be 0.378 [9]. Anthropometric parameters also showed evident association with respiratory variables, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.28 to 0.39 proven by various other authors as well [4]. Some studies also suggest that other anthropometric parameters like BSA, chest circumference, body fat also show a clear connection with lung functions [11].

Multivariate analysis confirmed height as an independent predictor, comparable to findings from Jharkhand and other regional datasets (Datta et al., Dey et al.), emphasizing the need for population-specific reference standards. The negative coefficient for weight in the combined model likely reflects multicollinearity with height and BMI rather than a true inverse physiological relationship [5, 12].

BHT showed a numerically higher correlation with PEFR than SBC (r = 0.32 vs. 0.17). A comparison of these two dependent correlations (Williams’s test) did not reach statistical significance (t(187) = 1.86, p = 0.06); therefore, BHT does not correlate statistically stronger with PEFR than SBC. This observation parallels findings by Aggarwal V, who reported significant correlations between BHT and spirometric indices, supporting its utility as a non–technician-dependent surrogate [9]. Studies by Bartfield JM and Ali SS also validated single-breath counting as a simple tool reflecting pulmonary reserve, though with modest explanatory power—consistent with our low R² values [7, 8].

The modest variance explained by SBC and BHT underscores that these measures cannot replace spirometry, the gold standard described by Liou TG [6]. However, in resource-limited or school-based settings where spirometry is not readily available, these bedside tools may provide pragmatic screening value.

Strengths of the present study include simple, non-invasive bedside tests to assess respiratory function, making the procedures safe, child-friendly and easily acceptable particularly in the areas where the patient load is more and the resources are limited. Assessment of multiple respiratory measures such as SBC, BHT and PEFR provide a comprehensive evaluation rather than reliance on a single parameter. Standardized techniques with repeated measurements have minimized observer and measurement bias. Correlation of respiratory indices with anthropometric parameters also strengthens interpretation. Finally, the findings support the feasibility of low-cost respiratory assessment methods in resource-limited pediatric settings. In primary healthcare centers and rural areas, where spirometry may not be readily available, these methods can aid in early identification of children who require further evaluation or referral.

The study has certain limitations like SBC and BHT are effort-dependent and influenced by patient cooperation which may introduce variability, particularly in younger children. Also the sample size was small due to which the findings need further evaluation. Moreover, the low R^2 values indicate that non-invasive bedside tests alone cannot adequately predict objective lung function. Overall, our findings corroborate Indian normative lung function data while highlighting BHT as a practical adjunct in pediatric respiratory assessment.

Conclusion

SBC, BHT and PEFR are simple bedside non-invasive tools which can be used as screening tools to suspect lung diseases in children in low and middle income countries. SBC and BHT show significant positive associations with PEFR in children however, considerable inter-individual variation was observed reflecting the multifactorial and effort-dependent nature of pediatric respiratory function. SBC and BHT are therefore insufficient, to predict PEFR with high accuracy.
