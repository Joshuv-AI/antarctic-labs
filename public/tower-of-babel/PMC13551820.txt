Lifestyle influence on burnout syndrome in religious leaders in Peru
Authors: Silla Mescouto-Conrado, Maryuri Abigail Perez-Coaquira, Cristian Edwin Adriano-Rengifo, Cesar Augusto Galvez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The objective of this study was to determine the influence of lifestyle on Burnout Syndrome (BS) among Peruvian religious leaders. Data were collected from a total of 205 participants. The study had a quantitative, non-experimental, explanatory, and cross-sectional approach. The proposed model was evaluated through structural equation modeling (SEM). The results revealed that lifestyle has a highly significant correlation with BS, however, no significant correlation is observed between Lifestyle and the Reduced Personal Accomplishment dimension of BS; Lifestyle showed a significant negative influence on the BS of religious leaders, (β = − 0.80; p < .001), with the model explaining 64% of the variance in the dependent variable (R2 = 0.64). The findings of this study suggest that some lifestyle habits such as social support, physical activity, sleep, and personality can protect against and/or mitigate the negative effects of BS.

Introduction

The importance of lifestyle for health and well-being is now widely recognized. Lifestyle has significant effects on physical health [1, 2]. Poor dietary habits, insufficient physical activity, and smoking are important. risk factors for diabetes and obesity and risk factors for death worldwide from NCD complications such as cardiovascular disease and cancer [1, 3].

For Roth [4], lifestyle is those individual behavioral habits that present a certain consistency over time, in more or less constant conditions, which can constitute risk or protective factors, being therefore ”a complex product, the result of personal, environmental and social factors that emerge from the present and from interpersonal history” (p. 38). The World Health Organization [5] defines lifestyle as the set of habits and customs that are influenced, modified, motivated, or inhibited by the prolonged process of socialization. Different kinds of research have linked lifestyle to mental illnesses such as depres- sion, anxiety, substance abuse, chronic stress, and Burnout Syndrome (BS) [6–8]. Merlo & Rippe [2] and Bonfim et al. [6] demonstrated that healthy lifestyle can act as a protective variable for mental health. Ptacek et al. [7], aiming to describe the levels of emotional exhaustion in 2394 Russian elementary school teachers, associating work stressors, lifestyle and BS, noted among their findings that healthier lifestyle is asso- ciated with lower rates of BS, and that teachers who devoted more time to family and personal interests had significantly lower rates of emotional exhaustion than teachers who did not.

The literature indicates that BS has been studied since the 1970s [9]. Currently, its incidence and study extended from social service professions [10] to a wide range of occupational occupations, such as: health professionals [2, 11, 12], teachers [7, 13–15], among others. In Latin America, Mendoza-Martínez, Castaño-Benjumea & Uribe-Prado [16], found that professional BS burnout occurred to a greater degree among Mexican professionals than among Colombian professionals; while García-Rivera, Mendoza-Martinez & Ramirez-Baron [17] concluded that transformational traits (idealized influence, individualized consideration) directly decrease emotional exhaustion and depersonalization among nurses and physicians at Baja California, México.

Recent research indicates high rates of chronic illness and burnout among religious leaders [14, 15, 18]. Upenieks [19], seeking to examine work-related stressors among 3,515 North American Catholic religious leaders, in order of magnitude, found: Feelings of disrespect in their role as a priest; thoughts of leaving the priesthood; feelings of overwork; and low levels of trust in superiors. Additionally, unrealistic expectations of membership, community conflicts, work-life boundaries, high levels of criticism from others, expectations of frontline mental health care provision, among others, often contribute to the prevalence of mental illness among religious leaders being three times higher than that of the general population [20, 21].

Studies suggest that some lifestyle habits may influence BS such as: maintaining a strong social support network with friends and family, vigorous and regular physical activity, healthy food intake, sleep, stress management, and positive personality traits. House [22] points out that social support or social relations is described as the functional aspect of relationships where resources, assistance, and help are exchanged or offered to an individual through family, friends, community groups, and government services. Some research with religious leaders suggests that social support from mem- bers, collaborators, colleagues, and superiors is a very important protective factor for the mental health of these professionals [21, 23]. On the influence of physical activity on physical and psychological burnout, pre- vious studies show physical activity as a means to prevent or alleviate job burnout. Dreher et al. [24] and Melguizo-Ib´an˜ez et al. [25] found that regular prac- tice of physical activity can help decrease BS symptoms in addition to preventing job burnout. Likewise, studies by Ruiz-Prada et al. [23] and Webb and Bopp [26] mention that frequent physical activity can positively influence the mood and occupational health of religious leaders.

Regarding diet and BS, Sanchez-Villegas [27], analyzing prospective cohort studies, observational studies, and experimental trials of primary prevention, indicated that both diet and dietary patterns are related to the development of mental illness. Neves et al. [28] emphasizes that the promotion of healthy eating habits can mitigate emotional work burnout, since the intake of some nutrients, such as fatty acids, omega-3, B vitamins, minerals, and amino acids are often effective in reducing the symptoms of patients with mental health problems.

In the relationship between stress, sleep, and BS, Song et al. [11] identified, among 1,013 Chinese nurses working in six public hospitals, that perceived stress not only had a positive relationship with BS, but could also have an indirect effect on BS through sleep quality. Although job burnout is strongly associated with poor sleep quality, there are several factors that could influence sleep quality such as fatigue, depression, chronic diseases, and lifestyle changes [12].

Regarding personality and job burnout, Angelini [29], in a systematic review investigating the role of personality in burnout, under the Five Factor theoretical approach, found that neuroticism, extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, and openness can be significantly related to the development of the syndrome. Similarly, the systematic review by Picornell-Gallar & Gonz´alez-Fraile [15], with the purpose of seeking and analyzing evidence related to burnout in religious leaders and possible modulating variables, finds that BS is also related to personality traits, where religious leaders with greater cognitive rigidity and lower emotional stability are more vulnerable to burnout than extroverts who are characterized by having a more flexible mind and more emotional stability. Finally, in their work to ensure the spiritual well-being of their congregations, religious leaders may neglect their own well-being as they face unique and ongoing stressors, which can increase risks for psychosocial and vocational vulnerabilities, resulting in BS, loss of motivation, underperformance, and absenteeism [14, 15, 18, 19].

So far, no study has been found on the influence of lifestyle on BS in religious leaders. Seeking to fill this gap in the scientific literature, this study used structural equation modeling (SEM) to determine the influence of lifestyle on BS in religious leaders of a religious organization in Peru. The objective of this study is to analyze the influence of lifestyle, here categorized into the dimensions of Physical Activity, Nutrition, Physical Activity, Sleep and Stress, Family and Friends, and Personality Type, on BS in Peruvian religious leaders.

Materials and methods

Design and participants

This study used an associative strategy within explanatory studies called latent vari- able design which specifies the relationships of the variables (observable and latent) [30]. Structural equation modeling based on the structural covariance (CB-SEM) was conducted to test the hypothesized relationships among the study variables.

The sampling was non-probabilistic with convenience sampling [31]. Religious leaders working as district pastors were included, excluding those working as chaplains in denominational institutions (schools, universities, and clinics) and leaders working in administrative positions. A district pastor is a full-time religious minister in charge of several churches in a particular geographical area.

The selection criteria of the religious organization were based on the adequate size of the church in the national level, a united and solid governance system, a corporate religious organization with well-educated pastors, the access granted to the research team, and the interest of the organization to serve their pastors by knowing their reality while accomplishing their mission. After the organization headquarter authorized the study, each of the seven missions/conferences opened their doors, and all pastors received the invitation by email. Of the 290 religious ministers who received the invitation, 212 completed the questionnaire. However, after data cleaning, 205 participants remained in the study.

Measuring instruments

Lifestyle assessment test – FANTASTIC

The FANTASTIC scale by Wilson et al. [32], the Colombian Spanish version [33], was used to measure lifestyle. The instrument consists of 25 items distributed in ten dimensions, which are: family and friends, physical activity, nutrition, tobacco use, alcohol consumption, sleep and stress, personality type, introspection, driving to work and use of other drugs. It has a three-point Likert response type ranging from 0 to 2 points. The Colombian version has adequate validity and reliability indicators [33].

In the present study, content validity was performed to verify the clarity and con- text of the items. The V-Aiken coefficients were greater than 0.70, considering the items to be adequate. In addition, the internal structure of the scale was verified by Con- firmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). After re-specifying the model, six items (15, 18, 19, 20 and 23) and the factors of tobacco, alcohol and drugs were discarded because they do not apply to the type of sample (religious leaders of a church that promotes healthy lifestyles). Thus, optimal fit indices were obtained (X2 = 40.46/df = 25, p<.05; CFI=0.97; RMSEA=0.05; SRMR=0.06). Reliability was obtained using the ordinal alpha coefficient due to the ordinal nature of the items, which achieved moderately acceptable reliability values for each of the factors: family and friends (α ordinal=0.78), physical activity (α ordinal=0.76), nutrition (α ordinal=0.50), sleep and stress (α ordinal=0.56), and person- ality (α ordinal=0.64). Additionally, the CR and AVE were obtained for each factor. Family and Friends and Physical Activity demonstrated adequate internal consistency (CR = 0.78–0.79) and satisfactory convergent validity (AVE ≥ 0.65). In contrast, Nutrition showed low reliability (CR = 0.57) and low convergent validity (AVE = 0.31), likely due to weak item loadings. Similarly, Sleep and Stress exhibited marginal reliability (CR = 0.59) and a suboptimal AVE (0.40), suggesting the presence of measurement error. Finally, Personality Type exhibited borderline acceptable reliability (CR = 0.64) and an AVE (0.48), which can be considered acceptable in applied research. Overall, while some constructs meet the recommended thresholds, others should be interpreted with caution due to their limited reliability and convergent validity. It is important to note that personality type in the FANTASTIC Test, refers to the traits of competitiveness, aggressive behavior, and positive thinking.

Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI)

BS was measured with the Maslach and Jackson [34] Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI). The Mexican version by Meda Lara et al. [35] was used. The scale is composed of 22 items distributed in three dimensions: emotional exhaustion (9 items), reduced personal accomplishment (8 items) and depersonalization (5 items). The scale presents seven response categories ranging from Never = 0 to Every day = 6 in a Lik- ert scale. This version presents adequate validity and reliability indicators [35]. The clarity and context of the items were also verified. The V- Aiken coefficients were greater than 0.70, considering the items to be adequate. The CFA presented an adequate three-factor model fit (X2 = 301.64 / df = 186, p<.001; CFI=0.91; RMSEA=0.05; SRMR=0.06). Reliability using the omega coefficient was adequate for the emotional exhaustion (ω = 0.85), depersonalization (ω = 0.70), and reduced personal accomplishment (ω = 0.85) factors. Additionally, the CR and AVE were obtained for each factor. Emotional Exhaustion (CR = 0.87) and Reduced Personal Accomplishment (CR = 0.87) showed high reliability. However, their AVE values (0.43 and 0.48, respectively) are slightly below the recommended threshold of 0.50, suggesting a moderate convergent validity. In contrast, Depersonalization showed lower reliability (CR = 0.68) and inadequate convergent validity (AVE = 0.31), probably due to lower factor loadings. This allowed for the exclusion of item 15 due to its low factor loading (0.39). Thus, the CR and AVE increased slightly (CR = 0.69; AVE = 0.35). Overall, while most dimensions showed acceptable reliability, the reduced AVE values and weaker performance of depersonalization indicate the presence of measurement errors, so the results related to this dimension should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, there is evidence of a bifactor structure of the MBI that can be used as a general factor [36].

Ethical procedures

This study had the approval of the Ethics Committee of the Universidad Peruana Uni´on Graduate School (UPeU Escuela de Posgrado) with resolution number 2022-CE-EPG-0000183. In addition, permission was requested from the administrators of the religious organization where the importance of the research, confidentiality, and anonymity of the data collected were highlighted. Religious leaders were invited to participate in the study through a virtual survey. Participation was voluntary, with the prior acceptance of informed consent to answer the questionnaires. In this way, the right to confidentiality, fairness, and the right to withdraw from the study at any time if they so wished, were assured. Data collection was carried out between February and April 2023.

Statistical analysis

First, outlier data were cleaned using the Mahalanobis test. No outliers were found in the database below the cutoff point (p < .001), however, missing data were found so seven participants had to be eliminated, leaving a sample of 205 participants for the subsequent analyses. A CFA was performed to verify the internal structure of each scale. To demonstrate an adequate adjustment of the internal structure of the scale, the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) was analyzed, which should be > 0.95. Likewise, the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) were used, which should be < 0.06 or 0.08 respectively, evidencing an admissible fit [37, 38]. A descriptive analysis of the model was performed by determining the univariate normality with skewness and kurtosis, which must be within the range ± 1.5 [39]. Covariance-Based Structural Equation Modeling (CB-SEM) was employed to test the hypothesized theoretical model. This approach was selected due to the confirmatory nature of the study and its strong theoretical grounding [40, 41], and applying the Maximum Likelihood Robust (MLR) estimator, which is appropriate in the presence of potential deviations from multivariate normality [42]. Reliability was determined by internal consistency with the ordinal alpha coefficient, whose value > 0.70 is adequate [43], and also by the omega coefficient, whose adequate value is ≥ 0.80 [44]. All data analysis was performed with the free software R and its interface RStudio.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

Of the 205 religious leaders who participated in the study, 91.7% were married with an average age of 42.6 years old (SD = 9.40). The majority of the participants came from the coast and highlands of Peru (42.4% and 43.4% respectively). Regarding education, 46.8% had a university Bachelor Degree. Regarding the Mission/Association of their field of work, the majority were from the Southern Peruvian Mission and the Lake Titicaca Mission (20.0% and 21.5% respectively). Finally, 47.8% of the religious leaders worked in an urban area (Table 1).

Explanatory analyses between the study variables

The empirical model was acceptably adjusted to the proposed theoretical model. The fit indices presented were optimal: X2/df (20.29/13) = 1.56; p = .08; CFI = 0.97; TLI = 0.95; RMSEA = 0.052; SRMR = 0.044. Complementary indices also indicated adequate fit (AGFI = 0.94, IFI = 0.97, PGFI = 0.45, PNFI = 0.58). Although the Relative Fit Index (RFI) value was slightly below the conventional cutoff (0.89), this index is known to be sensitive to model complexity; therefore, it should be interpreted cautiously and in conjunction with the remaining fit indices. Figure 1 shows that the lifestyle variable has a direct and significant effect on the BS of religious leaders, by presenting a high standardized regression coefficient (β= − 0.80; p < .001), suggesting that lifestyle explains 64% (R² = 0.64) of the self-rated BS by religious leaders.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to analyze the influence of lifestyle dimensions such as family and friends, physical activity, sleep and stress, nutrition, and type of personality, on BS among Peruvian religious leaders. However, the nutrition dimension of lifestyle was excluded from the structural model due to its inadequate reliability indicators, since measurement error in latent constructs can attenuate or, in some cases, distort structural parameter estimates [45]. In this regard, the structural model corresponding to the general hypothesis was tested using structural equation modeling (SEM). The model’s fit indices were optimal; similarly, the complementary indices indicated an adequate fit. Although the RFI value (0.89) was slightly below the conventional threshold (0.90), this index is known to be sensitive to the model’s complexity. Furthermore, the results revealed that lifestyle has a direct and significant effect on religious leaders’ BS with a high regression coefficient (β = − 0.80; p < .001), indicating that lifestyle explains 64% of the BS self-reported by religious leaders. These results suggest that healthier lifestyles are associated with lower levels of burnout among religious leaders. Similar results were found in other studies such as Bonfim et al. [6], who found that Brazilian public-school teachers with higher physical activity, social contact, and stress control have greater protection against BS. Likewise Ptacek et al. [7], conducted a study with 2394 elementary school teachers in the Czech Republic, finding that several lifestyle factors were significantly associated with BS in teachers; positive factors such as enough time with family, sufficient time in hobbies, quality and duration of sleep, and low alcohol consumption, and negative factors such as sweet and sugary drink intake, smoking, and feelings of guilty. In this study among Peruvian religious ministers who do not smoke or drink alcohol due to religious principles, and tend to take care of their lifestyle due to religious beliefs, the negative association between lifestyle and BS is highly significant. These findings suggest that certain healthy lifestyle habits may constitute personal resources associated with lower levels of burnout in this population.

Regarding the fit of the model for the specific hypotheses, it should be interpreted in a balanced manner. While some indices, such as the AGFI (0.88) and the RFI (0.89), fall slightly below conventional thresholds (0.90), other widely recommended indices indicate optimal fit (CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.95, IFI = 0.94, SRMR = 0.053, RMSEA = 0.034). Current SEM guidelines emphasize evaluating model fit by using multiple complementary indices, rather than relying on a single statistic, especially given the sensitivity of some indices (e.g., AGFI, RFI) to sample size and model complexity. In this context, the combination of acceptable incremental fit (CFI/IFI ≥ 0.90) and low residuals (SRMR ≤ 0.08), along with a reasonable RMSEA, supports the overall adequacy of the model [37, 44]. However, the suboptimal AGFI and RFI values suggest that the model fit should be interpreted with some caution.

In this regard, the findings of this study suggest that family and friends have a direct and significant effect on BS in religious leaders, with an acceptable standardized regression coefficient (β = -0.11). These data indicate that family and friends serve as a protective factor for mental health in this sample. Likewise, in a systematic review of 13 studies, social support was consistently associated with protection against depression in religious leaders [21]. Similarly, Ruiz-Prada et al. [23], in an exploratory review of the experience of stress and burnout syndrome among Catholic religious leaders, identified that social support (from members, family, colleagues, and superiors) was an important protective factor against burnout. However, the findings of the systematic review by Picornell-Gallar, González-Fraile [15], on burnout syndrome among Catholic religious leaders indicated a very weak correlation between social support and the dimensions of the MBI; therefore, further research is needed to explore the influence of social support on burnout syndrome in religious leaders.

Similarly, physical activity was found to have a direct and significant effect on work-related stress, with an acceptable standardized regression coefficient (β = -0.15). These data suggest that physical activity represents a protective factor for mental health among religious leaders. Previous studies are consistent with this result. Ruiz-Prada et al. [23] examined the experience of stress and work-related stress among religious leaders and found that frequent physical activity had a protective effect on work-related stress in these professionals. Similarly, an experimental case-control study among North American Catholic religious leaders found that regular physical activity is an excellent preventive measure against stress and its complications in that population [26].

It was also found that sleep and stress have a direct and significant effect on BS, with a standardized regression coefficient of β = -0.33, indicating that sleep deprivation and work-related stress play an important role in triggering BS. Consistent with these results, some studies emphasize that work-related stress is negatively associated with sleep quality [12]; sleep deprivation and work-related stress can precipitate BS, and perceived stress and poor sleep quality show strong positive associations with BS [11]; therefore, sleeping well can function as a regulator of daily stressful experiences and a buffer against the negative effects of stress.

Similarly, an adequate standardized regression coefficient (β = -0.34) was found for the personality dimension, implying that certain personality traits have a direct and significant effect on BS. This finding should be interpreted in light of the specific behavioral characteristics assessed by the FANTASTIC Lifestyle instrument, rather than general models of personality traits. The Personality dimension primarily assesses behaviors related to competitiveness, impatience, aggressive tendencies, stress proneness, and positive thinking. Consequently, individuals with lower levels of hostility and greater positive thinking may be better equipped to cope with workplace stressors, thereby reducing their susceptibility to burnout [15, 23]. Previous research has shown that hostility, chronic stress reactivity, and maladaptive coping behaviors are associated with poorer psychological adjustment and greater vulnerability to stress-related consequences, such as burnout and emotional exhaustion [46, 47]. In the context of religious leadership, where individuals are frequently exposed to high emotional demands, interpersonal responsibilities, and expectations of continuous service, positive thinking and adaptive stress management behaviors may represent important protective factors. Therefore, the observed relationship suggests that attitudinal and behavioral patterns associated with emotional self-regulation and constructive coping may contribute to lower levels of burnout among full-time religious leaders.

The present findings can be more comprehensively understood when interpreted through established theoretical frameworks, particularly the Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) model and health behavior theories. According to the JD–R model, burnout develops as a result of an imbalance between job demands and available resources [48, 49]. In the context of full-time religious leaders, job demands may include high emotional labor, role overload, and constant availability to congregants, all of which increase vulnerability to emotional exhaustion [50]. In contrast, several lifestyle dimensions identified in this study can be conceptualized as personal resources that may buffer these demands [48]. For instance, family and social support may serve as protective factors, while adaptive personality traits may enhance coping capacity. Consistent with this framework, the strongest effects observed in the model were related to sleep and stress management and personality, suggesting that these resources play a key role in mitigating burnout.

From a health behavior perspective, lifestyle dimensions can also be interpreted as self-regulatory behaviors that influence psychological well-being. Health behavior frameworks propose that behaviors such as sleep, nutrition, and stress management are shaped by cognitive and contextual factors and, in turn, affect health outcomes [51]. The present results align with this perspective by indicating that not all lifestyle behaviors contribute equally to burnout; rather, those directly linked to recovery processes—such as sleep and stress regulation—show stronger associations. This is consistent with evidence highlighting the role of recovery in preventing burnout and maintaining well-being [50].

These findings must be understood within the specific cultural and organizational context of religious leadership in Peru. Religious leaders often operate within value-driven systems that emphasize service, altruism, and commitment to community, which may intensify job demands while simultaneously providing meaning and purpose [50]. In Latin American contexts, where religion continues to play a central social role, ministers may experience heightened expectations of emotional availability and community engagement, increasing their risk of burnout. At the same time, strong communal ties and social support may function as key resources, particularly in dimensions such as family and interpersonal relationships [48]. Organizational factors within religious institutions may further shape these dynamics. Hierarchical structures, role expectations, and norms of self-sacrifice may influence both lifestyle practices (e.g., limited time for self-care, irregular sleep patterns) and stress levels. These contextual characteristics suggest that burnout among religious leaders is not solely an individual phenomenon but is embedded within broader cultural and organizational systems [49]. Consequently, the transferability of the findings should be considered with caution. The results are most applicable to full-time religious leaders engaged in community-based ministry within similar sociocultural contexts. They may not generalize to more secularized settings, institutional chaplaincy roles, or administrative leadership positions, where job demands and organizational expectations differ [48]. Future research should examine these relationships across diverse cultural and occupational contexts to better understand the generalizability of the proposed model.

This study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design allows only for the identification of associations between variables, which prevents the establishment of causal inferences. Longitudinal designs are recommended to better examine the temporal and causal relationships among the study variables. Second, the use of non-probabilistic convenience sampling may introduce selection bias, as participants who voluntarily enrolled in the study may systematically differ from those who did not (for example, in terms of motivation, workload, or participation in ministry activities). This may result in an overrepresentation or underrepresentation of specific subgroups, thereby limiting the representativeness of the sample. Consequently, the external validity of the findings is limited, and the results should be interpreted with caution. Specifically, the findings can be generalized primarily to full-time religious ministers who perform fieldwork providing spiritual support in multiple churches within a defined geographic area. However, the results should not be extrapolated to other groups, such as chaplains working in institutional settings (e.g., schools, universities, or clinics) or religious leaders with primarily administrative roles, as these contexts may involve different demands and psychosocial conditions. Another limitation concerns the reliability of some lifestyle dimensions, particularly sleep/stress (ordinal α = 0.56), which showed moderate internal consistency. Supplementary analyses such as CR and AVE revealed low values (CR ≥ 0.70; AVE ≥ 0.50) in some lifestyle dimensions. Similarly, in SB, most dimensions demonstrated adequate composite reliability, although the depersonalization dimension showed a lower AVE. Low reliability indicates a higher degree of measurement error, which may attenuate the strength of the observed relationships and reduce the stability of parameter estimates within the structural model. Despite this, the general pattern of results remained theoretically consistent. Future research should consider refining these subscales or employing alternative measures with more robust psychometric properties to improve the robustness of the findings. Finally, the use of self-report measures may introduce response biases, such as social desirability. Future studies could benefit from incorporating complementary data collection methods, such as semi-structured interviews or multi-informant approaches.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that lifestyle is significantly associated with burnout among full-time religious leaders in Peru. Specifically, family and social relationships, physical activity, sleep and stress management, and adaptive personality-related behaviors showed negative associations with burnout, with sleep/stress management and personality-related behaviors exhibiting the strongest effects within the model. These findings are consistent with theoretical frameworks such as the Job Demands–Resources model, which emphasizes the protective role of personal and behavioral resources in reducing burnout risk. However, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. The cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences, the non-probability sampling strategy limits generalizability, and some dimensions exhibited suboptimal psychometric properties, which may have introduced measurement error into the structural estimates. Therefore, the results should be understood as evidence of statistical associations rather than causal effects. The findings are most applicable to full-time religious leaders engaged in community-based ministry work within contexts similar to those examined in this study. Future longitudinal and multi-context investigations are needed to confirm the temporal stability, causal direction, and broader generalizability of the proposed model. Despite these limitations, the study contributes novel evidence regarding the relationship between lifestyle and burnout in an understudied occupational group and highlights potentially modifiable behavioral factors that may be relevant for burnout prevention and well-being promotion among religious leaders.
