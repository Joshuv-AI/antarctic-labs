Exciton dispersion fine structure and deep ultraviolet optical conductivity of freestanding two-dimensional h-BN
Authors: Jinhua Hong, Alberto Guandalini, Weibin Wu, Haiming Sun, Fuwei Wu, Shulin Chen, Chao Ma, Kazu Suenaga, Thomas Pichler, Francesco Mauri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExcitons govern the light-matter interaction in 2D gapped materials with intrinsically large binding energies. In spite of plentiful optical measurements in the visible for semiconducting transition-metal dichalcogenides, we still lack optical-absorption studies of the exciton structure of insulating 2D materials that requires ultraviolet (UV) light. Moreover, measurements of the momentum dispersion of excitons in the vicinity of optical limit are rare owing to low resolutions but hold the key to reveal quasiparticle interactions. To close this gap, we employ high momentum resolution electron energy loss spectroscopy (q-EELS) to explore exciton dispersions of mono- and few-layer hexagonal boron nitride. Surprisingly, we reveal a fine structure of the first bright exciton dispersion band composed by two features (A and A′), visible only at small momentum, not predicted by frozen-nuclei Bethe-Salpeter calculations. Introducing an optical conductivity approximation (OCA), we extract from the experimental q-EELS spectra deep-UV optical conductivity at zero momentum, σ(ω), and discuss the exciton fine structure in σ(ω), consistent with previous photoluminescence observations. Our findings establish a general methodology to probe the fine structure of exciton dispersions, providing new insights into exciton-phonon sidebands and eventually polarons in low-dimensional materials.Subject terms: Two-dimensional materials, Transmission electron microscopy

Introduction

Excitons play an essential role in the excitation properties of two-dimensional (2D) band insulators1 subject to external photons, electrons, or neutrons. Two-dimensional materials, such as hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN)2–5, transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs)6,7 and phosphorene8–11, preserve an intrinsic large exciton binding energy due to their weak screening in the atomic-scale thickness dimensionality12,13. This gives rise to sharp excitonic peaks followed by an absorption continuum14, typical in 2D excitonic systems. As an example, h-BN/TMDs/h-BN heterostructures have long been an ideal platform to explore the interlayer15, charge-transfer16, Moiré excitons17–19, trions20 and even higher-order charge complexes, building up a rich landscape of the excitonic physics in 2D semiconductors21. Also, exotic phases of matter such as exciton-mediated superconductivity, exciton condensates or insulators22, superfluidity23 are found in these semiconductor systems. However, in contrast with TMDs, the intrinsic exciton band structure of 2D UV-gapped h-BN is seldom explored24–27, but vital for the understanding of low-dimensional devices encapsulated by h-BN and for deep-UV optics applications.

In most optical experiments, the excitonic behavior involves mostly vertical electronic transition preserving negligible momentum transfer (q ~ 2πθ/λ ~ 10−4 Å−1). Thus, the degree of freedom of momentum is seldom involved, hindering a thorough measurement of the many-body interactions, such as the exciton band structures28,29 and their couplings with finite momentum phonons/photons. To access non-negligible momentum, inelastic X-ray/neutron scattering or electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) is required. For the former X-ray/neutron techniques30,31, the experimental challenge lies in the large required crystal size (mm), difficult to achieve in 2D, while for reflection EELS32 the need of substrates causes a strong quenching or decay of the excitonic peaks. Thus, EELS in a transmission electron microscope (TEM) is a feasible way to measure exciton band structures of 2D h-BN sheets, as it directly probes the longitudinal dielectric response resolved both in energy and momentum33,34 and may be applied to nanometer-scale and suspended samples35–37, provided that q resolution is sufficiently high to distinguish different q signals.

In theoretical calculations, exciton band structures and related properties are predicted starting from a GW band structure38–41 and solving the Bethe-Salpeter equation (BSE)38,42,43, eventually at finite momenta44. In particular, several works have investigated the excitonic band structure of h-BN45 and TMDs46,47, finding a double-degenerate optical exciton, one longitudinal and the other traverse (EELS bright and dark), with linear and parabolic dispersions, respectively. Excitonic effects have even been studied in materials without bounded excitons, such as graphene48,49. However, lattice vibrations and their interactions with excitons are usually neglected within the GW-BSE framework. Some pioneering works with included exciton-phonon coupling50–53, executed on top of frozen nuclei GW-BSE, or exciton-polaron54,55, demonstrated the importance of mixed exciton-phonon excitations in the description of the h-BN photoluminescence spectra. However, controversial results are obtained both in experiments24,56–58, probably due to screening effects of the substrate, and in theoretical simulations52,53, due to the different approximations involved.

In this work, we employ momentum (q) resolved EELS (q-EELS) in a TEM and GW-BSE calculations to study the exciton dispersions of mono- and few-layer h-BN, fully characterizing this UV-range excitonic system. We find a fine-structure composition of the lower-linear dispersive bright exciton (A-A′) with a splitting gap of Δ ~ 0.25 eV and a linear dispersive branch (B). The A-A′ splitting is not present in GW-BSE calculations, while the weighted average of the A-A′ fine structure and B exciton dispersions are instead in accordance with the calculated exciton dispersions. We ascribe the A′ absorption to be dominated by exciton-phonon coupling, as not found in calculations obtained with the frozen-nuclei approximation and already qualitatively described in previous works59.

Using an optical conductivity approximation (OCA) to model the q-EEL spectra, we are able to extract the optical conductivity σ(ω) and characterize the A′ satellite at q = 0. We point out the OCA extraction provides the experimental optical conductivity in the deep-UV regime, which is basically impossible to get accurately by UV optics, especially for freestanding mono and few-layer materials (see Supplementary Note 1). Finally, by checking the thickness dependence of the exciton fine structure, we find A′ satellite is manifested in the monolayer and becomes more and more invisible concomitant to the decreasing A-A′ gap as the thickness increases. This indicates the exciton-phonon coupling effect is strongest in the monolayer limit for the reshaping of phonon satellites of excitons. Our work offers a successful methodology to investigate the complicated exciton band structure in 2D materials with considerable exciton-phonon interaction, which can be generalized to other low-dimensional systems.

Then, we obtain the q-EEL spectra along Γ M and Γ K directions. Figure 1b shows in black an example of the spectrum at a fixed momentum (q = 0.036 Å−1) along the Γ M direction. We identify two excitonic branches: one composed by two peaks (A, A′) at ~ 6.5−7 eV and another composed of a single asymmetric peak (B) at around ~ 8 eV with a longer high-energy tail. To identify the maxima and oscillator strengths of all the excitations, we fit the EEL spectra with a four Lorentzian model (4LM, more details in Supplementary Figs. S9, S10), which accurately reproduces the raw experimental data.

Figure 1 c shows the q-E diagram of the low-energy electron excitations of monolayer h-BN within limited q < 0.3 Å−1 along the ΓM direction. Due to the detection sensitivity and decreasing signal-to-noise ratio, the exciton peaks can only survive within 1/5 of the Brillouin zone nearby the Γ point (∣ΓM∣ = 1.45 Å−1, ∣ΓK∣ = 1.67 Å−1). Within this range, the in-plane Γ M/Γ K anisotropy can be ignored (Supplementary Fig. S11), since the dispersion structure shows almost no difference. In Fig. 1c, the white dots correspond to the maxima found by the 4LM fitting of the spectra normalized to their maximum at each q (see Fig. 2c, d). The lower A exciton band starts at 6.6 eV at q = 0, which is ~ 0.5 eV higher than the reported optical measurements24–27. This effect is probably due to the different screening environments. The substrate presence reduces both the fundamental gap and the exciton binding energy, due to the added screening of electron-electron interaction. By looking at GW-BSE calculations as a function of the thickness of h-BN60, where similarly screening effects are enhanced with the layer number, the net effect is a redshift of the exciton position. The sideband A′ appears and gradually dominates the fine structure as q shifts away from the optical limit q = 0, while the lower A exciton is observed to survive within low q < 0.15 Å−1. Therefore, a high momentum resolution is very key to detect this exciton fine structure due to its low-momentum regime, not found previously61. Compared with A-A′ fine structure, the upper B exciton shows instead an almost linear dispersion with increasing relative intensity as q increases.

q-EEL spectra and deep-UV optical conductivity

In Fig. 2a-b, we show the experimental EEL spectra in black at increasing q of monolayer h-BN. One may note the q = 0.009 Å−1 and the q = 0 spectra are basically identical because they fall within experimental resolution, which is also averaging the higher q values. All peaks present a blue shifting as q increases along Γ M (see also Γ K direction and multi-layer in Supplementary Figs. S12 and a larger energy range ~ 20 eV shown in Supplementary Figs. S13, S14). The lowest exciton A shows a decreasing oscillator strength as q increases, while the excitons A′ and B show obvious increasing intensity as q shifts away from Γ point. The splitting of the A-A′ peak, for q < 0.15 Å−1 (far beyond a photon’s momentum 10−3 Å−1 for a polariton62), is clearly visible. For q > 0.15 Å−1, the two features merge into an asymmetric unique shape. We also note noisy features at energies lower than the A peak, while the low signal-to-noise ratio does not allow a scientific analysis.

Within the optical conductivity approximation (OCA), we assume that low-q EEL spectra are fully determined by the complex optical conductivity σ(ω) at q = 0 (see the Method section and Supplementary Figs. S15, S16) and that the q-dependence of position, shape and intensity of the spectral features are only due to the long-range component of the Coulomb interaction. We refer to the Method section for more details about the OCA and its error quantification. Taking advantage of this hypothesis, we have been able to extract from the low-q EEL spectra (q < 0.065 Å−1) the deep-UV real and imaginary parts of the optical conductivity, which is almost inaccessible from UV optics (see Supplementary Note 1). While it is not surprising that a double peak structure (A-A′) is present also in σ(ω), the significant advantage of the method resides in the quantitative identification of the energy and intensity of the q = 0 A′ satellite, not directly accessible with EELS, where the exact q = 0 signal is quenched63. In addition, q = 0 response functions are easier to analyze in terms of quasi-particles, and to the best of our knowledge, finite-momentum calculations of exciton-phonon coupling have not been implemented yet. Then, we compare the OCA σ(ω) with the GW-BSE one in Fig. 2c, d, respectively (see also Supplementary Fig. S17). We verify the accuracy of the extracted σ(ω) by comparing the OCA calculated spectra with the experimental q-EEL data for q < 0.13 Å−1 in Fig. 2a, b with a remarkable accuracy, in line with the OCA accuracy for GW-BSE calculations in Supplementary Fig. S15.

Extending our methods to all q range (Supplementary Figs. S9, S10), we plot the experimental dispersions for splitting excitons A and A′ as black dots, in comparison with the calculated curves in Fig. 3a. Compared with the less dispersive peak A′, the lower exciton A presents a more obvious linear relation with saturation, in contrast with the parabolic dispersions in TMDs37. According to our GW-BSE calculation, and previously discussed in the literature64, the exciton A is degenerate with a parabolic transverse (EELS dark) exciton band at Γ point (Supplementary Fig. S18), arising from the double degeneracy of K and K′ valleys of the electronic structure. Nevertheless, the parabolic band is not observed in the experimental spectra, owing to its dipole-forbidden rule predicted by GW-BSE calculations. The OCA predicted (in red curves) A-A′ fine structure in Fig. 3a shows a good accuracy with respect to the experimental dispersions, validating a posteriori the extraction procedure. By looking at Eq. (2) in the Methods part, we note the dispersion properties of the χ poles (thus of the EELS peaks) originate from the dispersion of the conductivity σ(q, ω), which include effects such as the dispersion of the electronic band structure and orbitals, and the dispersion of the macroscopic component of the Coulomb interaction v(q), describing low-dimensional dielectric effects. Within the OCA verified a posteriori, we may neglect the dispersion contribution from the conductivity [σ(q, ω) ≈ σ(ω)] in the studied momentum range, and then conclude that both the energy and intensity dispersions of the A peak and A′ satellite, are given by the divergent q−1 Coulomb interaction in Eq. (3). Similar splitting fine structure A-A′ can be found in ΓK direction in Supplementary Fig. S19.

Given a coarse accuracy or a lower energy resolution, we obtain a weighted-average exciton dispersion for the overall A+A′ structure, (EAIA+EA′IA′)/(IA+IA′), marked by the green dots in Fig. 3a. The A+A′ averaged dispersion is in good accuracy with GW-BSE calculations, which is taken as a linear dispersion by Liu et al.61. We thus conclude the linear exciton dispersion is consistent with a low-resolution picture, while an internal fine structure creates satellite peaks. Whether an exciton is energetically isolated from the others, it disperses linearly with a linear coefficient proportional to its q = 0 oscillator strength46,65. In the vicinity of the optical limit, the similarity between the weighted-average A+A′ dispersion (linear in q with linear coefficient proportional to IA+IA′, Supplementary Note 2) and the frozen-nuclei BSE A dispersion signifies IA+IA′≈IABSE. This is a hint that A′ is a satellite of A, due to total oscillator strength conservation, activated by a mechanism not present in the frozen-nuclei BSE calculation, as for example theoretically predicted in exciton-phonon coupling scattering mechanisms51–53 (for more details, see the assignment section). While in the intermediate q, deviation from linearity of the A and A′ dispersions occurs and signifies their hybridization at q ≠ 0.

For the high-energy peak B, its dispersion in Fig. 3b presents an almost linear relation, coinciding well with the GW-BSE calculated dispersions. Compared with exciton A, the linewidth of B is much larger, indicating the complicated exciton components. This is confirmed by GW-BSE calculations64, which predict the B peak to consist of several excitons with similar energies.

Within the A+A′ structure, we plot the ratio of the intensity of A′ to that of A+A′, namely IA′/(IA+IA′), as a function of q (Fig. 3c). We see an obvious increase behavior, that is, the sideband A′ plays a more and more dominant role in the exciton lineshape, indicating a more explicit phonon satellite as q shifts away from the optical limit.

Thickness dependent phonon satellite

We then discuss the excitonic properties of h-BN at increasing number of layers. Previously reported photoluminescence experiments24,56,57 show different spectra at different environments, indicating that environmental effects considerably modify the excitonic properties of monolayer h-BN. In Fig. 4a, we show some selected q-EEL spectra of mono-, bi- and three-layer h-BN. Both mono- and multi-layer h-BN show an obvious weight transfer from A+A′ excitons to the B excitons, as seen in Fig. 4a qualitatively and in Fig. 4c quantitatively. Here, frozen-nuclei GW-BSE calculations are not able to predict an accurate low-momentum intensity ratio of the monolayer h-BN. Increasing the number of layers, the peaks broaden due to the interaction with excitons localized in single layers60, and the intensity ratio (IA+IA′)/IB decreases at low momenta, which used to act as a fingerprint of the thickness identification66. The OCA correctly predicts this trend, indicating that it arises from the 2D dielectric properties of few-layer h-BN, dictated by the 2D Coulomb interaction.

Regarding the fine structure, in Fig. 4b we show the extracted σ(ω) of mono-, bi- and tri-layer, whose accuracy is demonstrated a posteriori from the ability of OCA to reproduce experimental spectra (see also Supplementary Fig. S12). As found from the EEL spectra, A′ satellite peak is clearly manifested in the monolayer limit (black curve in Fig. 4b). Unfortunately, such kind of fine structure becomes more and more invisible in the σ(ω) broadening. One possible explanation is Davydov’s splitting, for which exciton peaks of bi- and tri-layer h-BN split into replicas with ΔE ≈ 100 meV60 due to low interaction effects between the layers. As the broadening η ≈ 200–300 meV > ΔE, the net effect of Davydov’s splitting is to increase the broadening compatibly to what we measured. Supposing it will be possible to lower the peak damping in future studies, a comprehensive study about the possible Davydov’s splitting of the A-A′ peaks would be very interesting to complete the characterization of such exciton-phonon excitations.

Looking over the exciton fine structure, the splitting gap Δ also shows a thickness dependence: decreasing from 0.25 eV for monolayer, 0.20 eV for bilayer, then invisible for trilayer (Fig. S20-S21). The narrowing splitting gap, together with the broadening effect, make the sideband A′ submerge quickly as the thickness increases (see also Supplementary Fig. S22). This indicates the A-A′ pair has more almost-degenerate exciton structures, causing the broadening and invisibility of the satellite at increasing thicknesses.

Assignment of the A′ satellite

After an agnostic discussion of the fine-structure A-A′ exciton structure, we assign its nature by considering several possible mechanisms.

One may suppose that the A′ peak arises from defects and is thus not an intrinsic fine structure. To exclude this hypothesis, we perform beam irradiation test in Supplementary Figs. S23, S24, which shows the beam dose dependent defect creation does not change the spectrum line-shape and intensity (Supplementary Note 3). This means defects do not yield observable contribution in this deep-UV range of interest. To further exclude the defect origin, a slit-type q-EELS map in Supplementary Fig. S25, acquired parallel within 5 min at low magnification with substantially decreased dose to minimize irradiation effect, shows clearly the A′ peak is also present as an intrinsic excitation structure. Other alternative extrinsic explanations, such as exchange splitting, Davydov/interlayer effects, substrate environmental screening, can be excluded (Supplementary Figs. S2, S23 unambiguously confirm the monolayer nature), and we cannot have transverse (EELS dark) excitons.

The A′ peak in σ(ω), as not shown in the GW-BSE calculation within the frozen-nuclei approximation, may be due to exciton-phonon coupling, as described in ref. 53, where absorption contributions mediated by exciton-phonon coupling were derived and tested in the calculation of the optical conductivity of monolayer h-BN. In order to sustain such a thesis, we show in Fig. 5a EEL spectra obtained at q ≈ 0, at temperatures ranging from liquid nitrogen 113 K to heating at 773 K. It gives an initial rough indication about the phonon-related nature of the A-A′ branch besides its critical dependence on the temperature. We assign the peak positions as maxima of − ∂2I/∂E2, shown in Fig. 5b. Due to a clear increase of peak broadening with temperature, we have not been able to perform a reliable Lorentzian fit except for the T = 113 K case, thus we miss temperature-dependent intensity data. Increasing temperature, the A excitonic peak shows a redshift (see also Supplementary Figs. S26, S27), due to the temperature dependence of the band gap, observed both theoretically67 and experimentally68. Compared with the A and B peaks, the A′ remains stable, suggesting it is less sensitive to the lattice thermal expansion. In addition, the intensity ratio of A′ over A increases. As discussed in ref. 53, the A′ peak corresponds to the creation of an exciton at the Brillouin zone corner q = K plus the out-of-plane optical phonon (ZO), again at q = K, due to momentum conservation. We thus have EA′=EKexc.+EKph.. Its intensity is proportional to Nph.(T) + 1, where Nph.(T) = [2.3 × 10−5, 8.5 × 10−2, 0.14, 0.27] is the Bose population of the involved phonon for T = [113, 473, 573, 773] K, respectively. This is coherent with the increasing A′/A intensity ratio. We also find other minor excitation peaks at increasing temperatures in Fig. 5b, which may correspond to other exciton-phonon satellites. We set the ZO phonon energy as the one predicted by DFT, EKph.≈104 meV, as DFT calculations are usually accurate within 5–10% for such systems. Within the proposed interpretation, we find EKexc.≈6.77 eV, higher than EΓexc.≈6.62 eV, in contrast with previous theoretical calculations53,60. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first estimation of the energy difference of EKexc. and EΓexc. in freestanding monolayer h-BN, showing it could be a direct gap material.

Discussion

In summary, we employed q-EELS and GW-BSE calculations to study the fine structure of the exciton bands of freestanding h-BN at vanishing and finite q. We find the lower bright exciton is composed by two excitations, not found in the GW-BSE calculation, while their weighted average is consistent with GW-BSE predictions of linear dispersion. Separately, the A and A′ dispersions show instead a nonlinear dispersion due to their interaction at finite momenta. With the OCA, we extracted the optical conductivity σ(ω) of mono-, bi- and tri-layer. We identified the satellite peak A′ as due to exciton-phonon coupling, in analogy with previous calculations with electron-phonon coupling included53.

While previous photoluminescence (PL) studies have focused on substrate-bound samples24,56–58, EELS provides a robust alternative for investigating the intrinsic exciton-phonon properties of freestanding 2D materials. Specifically, EELS allows for the characterization of absorption features where the simultaneous creation of an exciton and a phonon results in an excitation peak at Eexc. + Eph.. Conversely, PL emission typically occurs via phonon-assisted decay at Eexc. − Eph.. A unified analysis using both EELS and PL thus offers a synergistic framework for elucidating these distinct exciton-phonon coupling mechanisms. The combination of EELS and OCA may be in principle applied to measure the optical conductivity of 2D freestanding materials in other contexts, for example, the infrared regime. In fact, EELS already proved its ability to reach the infrared energy scale69. Far-infrared (5 ~ 30 meV) spectroscopy, requiring a large sample size difficult to obtain in optics, will be accessible in the next generation TEMs and will work as a probe of several emergent quantum mechanisms, such as superconductivity70 and quantum spin-hall insulators71,72. The finding of this work may provide critical insights into exciton manipulation in the vicinity of the optical limit and into many-body effects such as exciton-polaron and electron-phonon coupling73 in low-dimensional systems.

Methods

Sample preparation and transfer

High-quality h-BN monolayers (provided by the group of Dr. Wang3) are grown on copper (110) foil by chemical vapor deposition through ammonia borane reduced under Ar/H2 gas flow. In this CVD growth, a continuous monolayer h-BN film will be formed, and occasionally a second layer or a third layer appears and covers a small part of the monolayer h-BN. A single-crystal 2D h-BN film is then transferred onto a TEM quantifoil grid using wet chemistry method. First, polycarbonate (PC) solution (2% in CHCl3) is first dropped onto the monolayer sample using spin coating of 2000 r/min, and then get dried in air for 10 min. Then a PC film is formed on top of h-BN with strong van der Waals interaction. Second, the sample is placed on the surface of H2O2:HCl = 1: 3 solution to dissolve the 25μm-thick Cu substrate within 30 min. Then PC film with h-BN thin layers get floated and then are moved into ultrapure water to wash away possible ions on the sample surface. Third, TEM grid is used to pick up the floating sample with the h-BN side closely contacting the holey carbon film of the grid. After natural drying in air, the TEM grid is immersed into CHCl3 to remove the PC assisted layer, and then into acetone and isopropanol to remove possible organic residues on the h-BN surface. Finally, the TEM grid is placed into a CVD furnace at low pressures and then annealed at 300 ∘C for 6h to remove most surface contaminations. Before the TEM characterization, the samples are also annealed in the chamber at 300 ∘C to prevent carbon deposition.

q-EELS measurements

All the q-EEL spectra are acquired in diffraction mode using parallel beam in a TEM (JEOL Triple C2) at 60 kV. The TEM is equipped with a double Wien filter monochromator, providing an electron beam with energy resolution of 40 ~ 50 meV. The smallest spectrometer entrance aperture in a diameter of 1 mm is used at a camera length of 120 cm, to achieve a momentum resolution of ~ 0.02 Å−1. The q-serial valence spectra are collected along a specific in-plane orientation Γ M or Γ K. In the spectrometer, a dispersion of 0.01 eV/channel is set for the spectrum acquisition. Dual EELS mode is used to exclude the zero-loss peak (ZLP) shift, then dwell time of 1 μs ~ 0.1 s is used for the ZLP, and 0.1 s ~ 10 s is used for the valence spectra of interest. Also, time series method eliminates the impact of ZLP vibration, especially for the high q range taking ~ 40 min for each spectrum. All the EEL spectra are calibrated using the ZLP, and the flat background is removed to normalize the acquired low-loss spectra and to do quantitative analysis.

ADF-STEM imaging

Atomically resolved ADF-STEM imaging is achieved on an aberration corrected TEM (JEOL Triple C3) equipped with a cold field emission gun at 60 kV. A probe current of 20 pA and an acquisition time per pixel of 60 μs is used for imaging. For ADF-STEM imaging, the convergence angle is set to ~ 35 mrad, and the acceptance angle is 70 ~ 200 mrad.

GW-BSE calculation

DFT calculations were performed using a plane wave basis set as implemented in the Quantum ESPRESSO package74, with the PBE approximation75 and adopting a kinetic energy cutoff of 60 Ry for the wavefunctions and norm-conserving pseudopotentials for electron-ion interaction. The Yambo code76,77 has been adopted to compute the quasi-particle band structure, within the G0W0 approximation, and the BSE to obtain zero and finite-momentum EEL spectra and the optical conductivity of monolayer h-BN. The adopted supercell includes a vacuum region along the perpendicular direction of 7 Å, and a 2D slab Coulomb cutoff78,79 has been used to avoid spurious interactions. For what concerns the calculation of the screening interaction W, we used a cutoff of 10 Ry for the size of the dielectric matrix, including up to 400 states in the sum-over-state of the response function. For GW calculations, we adopted the plasmon-pole approximation in the Godby-Needs scheme80. The quasi-particle Dyson equation has been solved with a linearization of the G0W0 self energy, and we included 400 states in the sum-over-state of the non-interacting Green’s function. In the solution of the BSE, we included the lower energetic 10 bands (4 valence and 6 conduction). We found an artificial broadening of 250 meV provides the best match with experiments. We are aware such a high broadening has probably an extrinsic component, thus in principle it could be possible to reduce it. However, a comprehensive study about the nature of such a high broadening in experiments is out of the scope of this work due to its complexity, and will be left for future studies. We carefully verified that a 48 × 48 Monkhorst-Pack grid converges both the DFT, GW and BSE calculations. With the parameter discussed so far, we find the first bright exciton at q = 0 to be at 5.3 eV, very similar to the value 5.4 eV found in previous works52,53. To fix the discrepancy from the experimental peak position, located at ≈ 6.62 eV, we blue-shifted all GW-BSE EEL spectra by 1.32 eV. This energy is larger than the shift employed by previous works52,53 because they use photoluminescence spectra of h-BN over a substrate, where the first exciton is located at ≈ 6.1 eV.

The OCA can thus be used to extract the deep-UV range of the optical conductivity, following the fitting procedure described in Supplementary Fig. S16. We note σ(ω) is in general a complex quantity, and the EEL spectra depend on both Re[σ] and Im[σ] through the denominator in the right side of Eq. (3). The OCA is exact at q = 0, and there is always at least a vicinity around q = 0 for which it is justified. For the specific case of monolayer h-BN, we tested the range of validity of the OCA by comparing ab-initio GW-BSE calculations obtained by Eq. (2) with OCA calculations, obtained via Eq. (3), starting from a GW-BSE optical conductivity. Testing the OCA with finite-q GW-BSE calculations (see Supplementary Fig. S15), we find the A exciton position with an error ≈ 14 meV at q = 0.06 Å−1. This can be estimated as the maximum local error for spectra at q ≤ 0.06 Å−1, as the OCA error is monotonically increasing with q. We note, within the OCA, we are including the dispersion mechanism originated from the highly dispersive Coulomb interaction, while instead neglecting dispersion effects originated from the electronic band structure. This approximation is mainly justified if the exciton is composed by electronic transitions between a valence band maximum and conduction band minimum, where first-order electronic band corrections are null, as for the h-BN case.
