Unentangled stoquastic Merlin–Arthur proof systems: the power of unentanglement without destructive interference
Yupan Liu, Pei Wu

Abstract
Stoquasticity, originating in physical systems free of the so-called sign problem and avoiding destructive interference from sign cancellations, gives rise to 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 {\sf StoqMA} , introduced by Bravyi, Bessen, and Terhal (2006) , a quantum-inspired intermediate class between 𝖬𝖠 {\sf MA} and 𝖠𝖬 {\sf AM} . Unentanglement similarly gives rise to 𝖰𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 2 ) {\sf QMA}(2) , introduced by Kobayashi, Matsumoto, and Yamakami (CJTCS 2009) , which generalizes 𝖰𝖬𝖠 {\sf QMA} to two unentangled proofs and still has only the trivial 𝖭𝖤𝖷𝖯 {\sf NEXP} upper bound. In this work, we initiate a systematic study of the power of unentanglement without destructive interference via 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 2 ) {\sf StoqMA}(2) , the class of unentangled stoquastic Merlin–Arthur proof systems. Although 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 {\sf StoqMA} is semi-quantum and may collapse to 𝖬𝖠 {\sf MA} , 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 2 ) {\sf StoqMA}(2) turns out to be surprisingly powerful. We establish the following results: • 𝖭𝖯 ⊆ 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 2 ) {\sf NP}\subseteq{\sf StoqMA}(2) with O ~ ​ ( n ) \widetilde{O}(\sqrt{n}) -qubit proofs and completeness error 2 − polylog ⁡ ( n ) 2^{-{\rm polylog}(n)} , paralleling the best known 𝖰𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 2 ) {\sf QMA}(2) lower bounds except for perfect completeness. On the upper-bound side, 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 2 ) ⊆ 𝖤𝖷𝖯 {\sf StoqMA}(2)\subseteq{\sf EXP} via the Sum-of-Squares algorithm due to Barak, Kelner, and Steurer (STOC 2014) . Our tightened SoS upper bound shows the essential optimality of the parameters in both our protocol and the BKS algorithm under the exponential-time hypothesis (ETH). • 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 ​ ( 2 ) 1 ⊆ 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 {\sf StoqMA}(2)_{1}\subseteq{\sf PSPACE} , and the containment holds with completeness error 2 − 2 poly ⁡ ( n ) 2^{-2^{\operatorname{poly}(n)}} . • 𝖯𝗋𝖾𝖼𝗂𝗌𝖾𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 2 ) {\sf PreciseStoqMA}(2) , a variant of 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 2 ) {\sf StoqMA}(2) with exponentially small promise gap, cannot achieve perfect completeness unless 𝖤𝖷𝖯 = 𝖭𝖤𝖷𝖯 {\sf EXP}={\sf NEXP} . In contrast, 𝖯𝗋𝖾𝖼𝗂𝗌𝖾𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 {\sf PreciseStoqMA} achieves perfect completeness, since 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 ⊆ 𝖯𝗋𝖾𝖼𝗂𝗌𝖾𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 1 {\sf PSPACE}\subseteq{\sf PreciseStoqMA}_{1} . • When the completeness error is negligible, 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( k ) = 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 2 ) {\sf StoqMA}(k)={\sf StoqMA}(2) for k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 . Our lower bounds are obtained by stoquastizing the short-proof 𝖰𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 2 ) {\sf QMA}(2) protocols via distribution testing techniques. Our upper bounds for the nearly perfect completeness case are proved via our rectangular closure testing framework, a new combinatorial technique tailored to 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 ​ ( 2 ) 1 {\sf StoqMA}(2)_{1} .

Introduction

The notion of
stoquasticity
was first introduced in
[
BDOT08
]
to describe Hamiltonians whose
off-diagonal matrix elements are real and non-positive
, a condition that naturally connects the model to stochastic processes such as Markov chains and random walks. Motivated by this connection, Bravyi, Bessen, and Terhal
[
BBT06
]
introduced the complexity class
StoqMA
, for which the stoquastic local Hamiltonian problem is complete.

A useful way to understand
StoqMA
is to compare it directly with
MA

[
Bab85
]
, the class of Merlin–Arthur proof systems and the standard randomized generalization of
NP
. Indeed,
MA
admits a natural “quantum” formulation
[
BDOT08
]
: the verifier uses classical reversible circuits built from Toffoli, CNOT, and X gates, together with the usual
\ket{0}
ancillary qubits and
\ket{+}
ancillary qubits that provide randomness, and then measures a designated output qubit in the
computational
basis. Allowing the witness to be quantum does not increase the power of this model, as quantum witnesses offer
no
advantage over classical ones.

By contrast, in
StoqMA
the only formal difference is that the designated output qubit is measured in the
Hadamard
basis instead. Consequently,
StoqMA
naturally contains
MA
.
This apparently minor syntactic change has important structural consequences. By the Perron–Frobenius theorem in linear algebra, the acceptance probability of a
StoqMA
verifier is always maximized by a witness state whose amplitudes are
non-negative
in the computational basis,
1
1

1

In this work, we refer to such states as
non-negative states
.
and this structural property makes the underlying optimization substantially easier than in
QMA
, the fully quantum analogue of
NP
.

Whereas the local Hamiltonian problem, which asks one to approximate the minimum eigenvalue of an underlying Hermitian matrix, is complete for
QMA
, and
QMA
is thus at least contained in
PSPACE

[
Kit99
,
KSV02
]
, the stoquastic local Hamiltonian problem can be reformulated as the problem of estimating the trace of a power of an entrywise non-negative matrix with efficiently computable local contributions. Using the techniques in
[
BGM06
,
GS89
]
, this places
StoqMA
at least inside
AM

[
BBT06
]
, and hence inside the polynomial-time hierarchy (
PH
)
[
Bab85
]
. In this sense,
StoqMA
is a
semi-quantum
class: it is quantum enough to go beyond
MA
, yet still structured enough to admit unexpectedly classical upper bounds, unlike
BQP
, for which even proving containment in
PH
is technically difficult in light of oracle separations
[
RT22
]
.

Further perspectives on
StoqMA
.

Beyond serving as a natural quantum-inspired intermediate class between
MA
and
AM
,
StoqMA
admits several further perspectives:

Physical perspective:

Traditionally,
StoqMA
corresponds to Hamiltonians without the

: a regime with no destructive interference from sign cancellations, where random-walk-based Monte Carlo simulations become applicable. A standard example is stoquastic
k
-
SAT
, equivalently the perfect-completeness version of
StoqMA
(
\textnormal{{StoqMA}}_{1}
), which is contained in
MA

[
BBT06
,
BT10
]
. More recently, this containment was extended slightly to
StoqMA
with negligible completeness error
[
AGL25
]
.
2
2

2

This improvement is achieved via a careful analysis of the underlying random walk for
yes
instances in
[

, Section 5]
, together with a probabilistic argument for the lazy random walk, such as
[
ST13
, Proposition 2.5]
.
Beyond being a formal model,
StoqMA
also captures physically meaningful Hamiltonians, such as the transverse-field Ising model whose associated Hamiltonian problem is
StoqMA
-complete
[
BH17
]
and is closely related to adiabatic quantum computation
[
AL18
]
.

Complexity-theoretic perspective:

From a more modern viewpoint,
StoqMA
occupies a distinguished place in the classification of local Hamiltonian problems. In the qubit
2
-local setting, every problem falls into one of four categories: in
P
,

-complete,
StoqMA
-complete, or
QMA
-complete
[

]
, yielding a quantum analogue of Schaefer’s dichotomy theorem
[
Sch78
]
.
3
3

3

Other examples include local Hamiltonian problems with symmetric
2
-local interactions and no 1-local terms, which are either
QMA
-complete or in
StoqMA

[
PM17
]
, as well as a recent classification of EPR Hamiltonians
[
MS26
]
, whose levels are
QMA
-complete,
StoqMA
-complete,
NP
-complete, and reducible to the
\textsc{EPR}^{*}
problem conjectured to be in

.
Under derandomization assumptions implying
\textnormal{{AM}}=\textnormal{{NP}}

[

,

]
, the intermediate

-complete level would disappear. Likewise, under the standard derandomization assumption that
\textnormal{{MA}}=\textnormal{{NP}}
, proving
\textnormal{{StoqMA}}=\textnormal{{MA}}
would eliminate the same level.

Distribution-testing perspective:

More recently, an instinctive connection between
distribution testing
and the final Hadamard-basis measurement in a
StoqMA
verifier has been identified in
[
Liu21
]
. Roughly speaking, the verifier’s final measurement computes a Hellinger-affinity-type quantity between two induced distributions that are determined jointly by the verification circuit and the witness. This perspective led to an alternative proof that
\textnormal{{StoqMA}}_{1}\subseteq\textnormal{{MA}}

[
Liu21
]
, via distribution-testing techniques
[
CR14
]
, as well as to parallel repetition for
StoqMA

[
Liu21
]
, which immediately implies error reduction for
StoqMA
with negligible completeness error.

Unentangled Merlin–Arthur proof systems and their motivations.

Parallel to the development of stoquastic Merlin-Arthur proof systems, the notion of
unentanglement
gives rise to the class
QMA
(2) introduced by Kobayashi, Matsumoto, and Yamakami
[
KMY09
]
, in which the verifier has full quantum power but receives two unentangled quantum proofs, or equivalently the witness is required to be a separable state across two registers.
4
4

4

See also
[
JLW26
]
for a comprehensive survey about
QMA
(2).
This viewpoint was then substantially clarified by Harrow and Montanaro
[
HM13
]
, who showed that
QMA
(
k
), for up to polynomially many provers, collapses to
QMA
(2). Their work established error reduction for
QMA
(2), showing the class is robust under the usual choice of constant completeness and soundness parameters.

On the upper-bound side, however,
QMA
(2) is notoriously difficult. The underlying optimization is over separable states and is therefore
more subtle
than that over arbitrary quantum states, which obstructs many of the techniques available for
QMA
. For the general class, the trivial upper bound of
NEXP
remains the best known. Nevertheless, several restricted settings are better understood. For example, when the verifier’s induced measurements are restricted to Bell or one-way LOCC, the corresponding subclasses are contained in
QMA

[
BCY11
,
BH13
,
LS15
]
. Several further perspectives on
QMA
(2) are as follows:

Power of short unentangled proofs:

QMA
(
k
) with short proofs already captures
NP
in striking ways.
Blier and Tapp
[
BT12
]
first established that

admits two
O(\log n)
-qubit quantum proofs, with a
vanishing

[
BT12
]
. A scaled-up version of this idea later implied that
\mathsf{PreciseQMA(2)=\textnormal{{NEXP}}}

[
Per12
]
, and the soundness analysis was subsequently improved in
[
Bei10
,
CF13
,
LNN12
]
.
Later work focused on obtaining a
constant
gap, a goal first realized in
[
ABD+09
]
, and later refined to use two
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{n})
-qubit quantum proofs by combining PCP machinery with prover-elimination techniques from
[
HM13
]
. This line of work also yields conditional hardness of
QMA
(2) under the exponential-time hypothesis (ETH). Moreover, the measurement underlying this
QMA
(2) protocol can be relaxed further, even to Bell measurements
[
CD10
]
, with subsequent improvements in
[

,
NZ23

. More recently, imposing a

Optimization over the separable states:

A central way to understand
QMA
(2) is as tensor optimization in disguise: the verifier’s maximum acceptance probability is obtained by optimizing over the separable states, rather than by estimating a top eigenvalue. This connects
QMA
(2) to a broader landscape of tensor optimization and hardness of approximation, including themes around the Unique Games Conjecture
[
BBH+12
]
.
This viewpoint has led to several distinct algorithmic approaches. One sophisticated line of developments includes semidefinite-programming hierarchies arising based on relaxations of separable states, such as PPT and extendibility
[
DPS04
,
NOP09
,
HNW17
,
FF21
]
.
The convergence rate of the corresponding SDP hierarchies is often guaranteed by quantum information-theoretical or de Finetti type arguments asserting that symmetric states must be close to separable. In restricted measurement regimes such as one-way LOCC, such SDP hierarchy yields quasipolynomial-time algorithms
[
BCY11
,
BH13
,
LS15
]
. Another line of attacks includes smarter search algorithms exploring the set of quantum states such epsilon-net methods
[
SW15
,
BH15
]
, Sum-of-Squares based algorithms
[
BKS14
,
BKS17
]
.
More recently, algebraic-geometry based approaches have been explored
[
HNW17
,
KR26
]
.
Altogether, this optimization perspective places
QMA
(2) at the interface of separability testing, convex optimization, and classical hardness of approximation.

Physical and information-theoretic perspective:

One canonical
QMA
(2)-complete problem is
Separable Sparse Hamiltonian

[
CS12
]
. By contrast,
Separable Local Hamiltonian
, though once conjectured to be

(2)-complete, is in fact in

, and the hardness techniques of
[

apply only in the

(2)-complete problem is

]
. Interestingly,

also serves as a starting point for proving that

From stoquastic to unentangled stoquastic.

Since stoquasticity originates from
sign-problem-free
physical systems, which avoid destructive interference from sign cancellations, our model
StoqMA
(2) naturally combines the two themes above and captures the power of
unentanglement without destructive interference
. Algebraically, this absence of destructive interference is reflected by
non-negativity
. This interaction leads to the following two questions:

(a)

Can unentanglement remain computationally powerful even without destructive interference?
More concretely, can
StoqMA
(2) recover some known
QMA
(2)-type lower bounds?

Prior work suggests that the ability to perform distribution-testing-type tasks makes
StoqMA
appear more powerful than
MA
, with this extra power closely tied to non-negativity
[
Liu21
]
; moreover, non-negativity can dramatically strengthen unentanglement
[
JW23
]
. Together, these results support the intuition behind Question
(a)
.

(b)

Can stoquasticity impose new computational limitations on the unentangled proof systems? More precisely, does
StoqMA
(2)
admit upper bounds beyond
NEXP
, still out of reach for general
QMA
(2)?

Question
(b)
reflects the viewpoint that the intrinsic non-negativity of
StoqMA
makes its convex structure easier to exploit and therefore imposes extra structure, as supported by the best known upper bounds:
\textnormal{{QMA}}\subseteq\textnormal{{SBQP}}

[
Vya03
,
MW05
,
Kup15
]
, whereas
\textnormal{{StoqMA}}\subseteq\textnormal{{SBP}}

[
BBT06
]
, where
SBQP
and
SBP
are variants of
BQP
and
BPP
, respectively, with a completeness-to-soundness ratio at least
2
.

We provide surprisingly sharp answers to these questions.

Lower bounds via distribution testing.

The connection between distribution testing and

\frac{1+\langle R_{0}(\Psi)|R_{1}(\Psi)\rangle}{2},\quad\text{where }\quad\ket{R_b(\Psi)}\coloneqq R_{b}\ket{\Psi}\ket{\bar{0}}\ket{\bar{+}},\;\forall b\in\{0,1\}.

(1.2)

We extend this viewpoint to the

Consequently, our lower-bound constructions can be viewed as coherent analogues of distribution testing. In a classical distribution-testing algorithm, one draws independent samples from an unknown distribution. In

Upper bounds via rectangular closure testing.

To derive upper bounds for
\textnormal{{StoqMA}(2)}_{1}
, a natural starting point is to extend the known approaches behind
\textnormal{{StoqMA}}_{1}\subseteq\textnormal{{MA}}
to the unentangled setting. Both approaches crucially rely on the local optimality phenomenon of stoquastic
k
-
SAT
. The random-walk approach
[
BBT06
,
BT10
]
uses this structure
dynamically
: perfect completeness can be interpreted as the existence of a clean connected component in the configuration graph of a stoquastic
k
-SAT instance, and a random walk from a single seed string suffices to test this component. By contrast, the easy-witness approach
[
Liu21
]
uses the same structure
algebraically
: under perfect completeness, the local optimality conditions allow the verifier to efficiently evaluate the relevant amplitude ratios, enabling the distribution testing techniques of
[
CR14
]
.

However, this local structure does not directly extend to
StoqMA
(2). Given a pair of reversible circuits
(R_{0},R_{1})
,
Equation

1.2
allows us equivalently to consider the circuit pair
(\Gamma,I)
, where
\Gamma=R_{0}^{\dagger}R_{1}
. Consequently, for any
2\ell
-qubit non-negative witness state
\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\ket{\psi_2}
, perfect completeness yields the following identity:

\Gamma\left\lparen\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\ket{\psi_2}\right\rparen\ket{\bar{0}}\ket{+}^{\otimes r}=\left\lparen\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\ket{\psi_2}\right\rparen\ket{\bar{0}}\ket{+}^{\otimes r}.

(1.3)

Since
\Gamma
is a bijective permutation,
Equation

1.3
implies
support invariance
. More precisely, if
\ket{\psi_1}
and
\ket{\psi_2}
have supports
S
and
T
, respectively, then the joint support is the rectangle
S\times T
; moreover, one may take the corresponding product subset state
\ket{S}\otimes\ket{T}
, where the subset state of
S\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\ell}
is denoted by
\ket{S}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|S|}}\sum_{j\in S}
.
This rectangular structure is
global
and therefore breaks the direct
local
optimality analogy with
\textnormal{{StoqMA}}_{1}
: a clean connected component may be highly non-rectangular and hence may not support any product witness, while the sets
S
and
T
need not have efficient membership tests, so an easy witness is no longer guaranteed.

Nevertheless, our combinatorial approach recovers a
global
analogue of
local
random-walk exploration by testing a property we call
rectangular closure
. The key observation from
Equation

1.3
is that, whenever
(s,t,u)
satisfies
s\in S
,
t\in T
, and
u\in U\coloneqq\{0,1\}^{r}
, every transition of
\Gamma
from
(s,t,\bar{0},u)
to
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime},a_{0},u^{\prime})
must satisfy
a_{0}=\bar{0}
and
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime},u^{\prime})\in S\times T\times U
. In other words, a closed rectangle never sends a valid configuration outside the zero-ancilla sector. Our rectangular closure testing algorithm
\mathcal{T}
formalizes this idea: starting from a seed pair
(s_{0},t_{0})
, it repeatedly closes the current rectangle under all transitions of
\Gamma
, adding every pair
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
forced by the transition relation underlying
\Gamma
. This
deterministic
process tests not only pairs reached by an ordinary walk, but also all crossed pairs required by separability.

The resulting algorithm is therefore
global
rather than
local
. For
yes
instances,
Equation

1.3
guarantees that a seed inside a closed rectangle never changes the zero-ancilla register. For
no
instances, the soundness promise implies that every non-closed rectangle has noticeable escaping mass; by reversibility, this escaping mass forces multiplicative growth of the rectangular closure. If
\gamma
denotes the rejection probability for
no
instances, which is comparable to the promise gap
\Delta
, then it suffices to run
L=\Omega\left\lparen\ell/\gamma\right\rparen
closure rounds so that every seed encounters a nonzero ancilla transition. Implementing our
L
-round rectangular closure testing algorithm recursively yields a
PSPACE
upper bound for inverse-polynomial gaps, while an explicit-table implementation yields an
EXP
upper bound for exponentially small gaps. Consequently,

\textnormal{{StoqMA}(2)}_{1}\subseteq\textnormal{{PSPACE}}\quad\text{and}\quad\textnormal{{PreciseStoqMA}(2)}_{1}\subseteq\textnormal{{EXP}}.

Finally, by replacing exact invariance (
Equation

1.3
) with an approximate version, one can extend our upper bounds to the nearly perfect-completeness regime. Combining our upper bounds with our prover-compression technique (
Section

1.2
) establishes
Theorem

1.2
.

A general upper bound via Sum-of-Squares.

The upper bound of
\textnormal{{StoqMA}({k})}\subseteq\textnormal{{EXP}}
is a corollary of the Sum-of-Squares algorithm of Barak, Kelner, and Steurer
[
BKS14
]
.

After passing to the symmetric form of the protocol, the acceptance probability can be written as a tensor optimization over the unit sphere

\max_{\|x\|=1}\Tr M(xx^{T})^{\otimes t},

where
M
acting on
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes t}
is the entrywise non-negative matrix induced by the stoquastic verifier,
t
is the tensor order corresponding to the number of provers, and
d=2^{\ell}
is the dimension of each proof. The BKS algorithm relaxes this optimization to a bounded-degree pseudoexpectation and rounds it using the non-negative vector

x_{i}^{*}=\sqrt{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[x_{i}^{2}]}.

The role of non-negativity is crucial here: it allows the rounded vector to be compared directly with the pseudoexpectation without cancellations from signs or phases.

At a high level, the analysis views the pseudoexpectation
as defining a joint exchangeable distribution on random variables
A_{1},\ldots,A_{t}
. If these variables are close to independent, then the above direct rounding already gives a good approximation.
If they are noticeably correlated, the BKS conditioning step reweights the pseudo-distribution by a square monomial, which decreases an entropy potential. Since the entropy is bounded by
\log d
, only finitely many conditioning steps are possible before rounding succeeds. BKS’s analysis yields a degree-
O(t^{3}\log d)
SoS upper bound, which corresponds to a SDP of size
\exp(O(t^3\log^2 d))
.

We slightly tighten BKS’s analysis, exploiting the symmetry in the entropy argument, avoiding a wasteful triangle inequality, yielding an improved degree-
O(t^{2}\log d)
SoS upper bound. Although the improvement looks quantitatively modest, it makes the parameter
dependence
tight
. Combined with

Theorem

1.1
, the degree-
O(t^{2}\log d)

SoS gives rise to a
SAT
algorithm which matches the ETH barrier up to logarithmic and gap-dependent factors:
an improvement to
\exp((t\log d)^{2-\Omega(1)})
time of the SoS algorithm, or any improvement of our protocols
Theorem

1.1
with shorter proofs or smaller number of provers would yield a subexponential-time
algorithm for
SAT
.

Fidelity.

Let
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
be quantum states in
\mathcal{D}(N)

The (Uhlmann) fidelity and the squared fidelity between

and

\mathrm{F}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\coloneqq\Tr\absolutevalue{\sqrt{\rho_0}\sqrt{\rho_1}}=\Tr\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho_{0}}\rho_{1}\sqrt{\rho_{0}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{F}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\coloneqq\mathrm{F}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{2}.

When one of the states is

and

\mathrm{F}^{2}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi},\rho)=\bra{\psi}\rho\ket{\psi}.

(2.1)

When both states are pure, the following identity holds; see, e.g.,

\mathrm{T}(\outerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0},\outerproduct{\psi_1}{\psi_1})=\sqrt{1-\mathrm{F}^{2}\left\lparen\outerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0},\outerproduct{\psi_1}{\psi_1}\right\rparen}.

(2.2)

By the data-processing inequality for the trace distance, e.g.,

{lemma}
[Measurement bound for trace distance]

Let

3.6

For every entrywise non-negative square Hermitian matrix

Proof.

For simplicity, we present the proof only for the case

Let

\ket{\psi_1^\star}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in[d_{1}]}a_{i}\ket{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\ket{\psi_2^\star}\coloneqq\sum_{j\in[d_{2}]}b_{j}\ket{j}

be two pure states that achieve the optimum stoquastic separable value. Accordingly, define the corresponding non-negative states

\ket{\psi_1^+}\coloneqq\sum_{i}\absolutevalue{a_i}\ket{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\ket{\psi_2^+}\coloneqq\sum_{j}\absolutevalue{b_j}\ket{j}.

Since

is entrywise non-negative and

is non-negative, we obtain:

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d_{1},d_{2}\right\rparen}(M)

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi_1^\star}\bra{\psi_2^\star}M\ket{\psi_1^\star}\ket{\psi_2^\star}

(3.3a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\operatorname{Re}\left\lparen a_{i}^{*}b_{j}^{*}a_{i^{\prime}}b_{j^{\prime}}\right\rparen\bra{i}\bra{j}M\ket{i'}\ket{j'}

(3.3b)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\absolutevalue*{a_i^*b_j^*a_{i'}b_{j'}}\bra{i}\bra{j}M\ket{i'}\ket{j'}

(3.3c)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{a_i^*}\absolutevalue{b_j^*}\absolutevalue{a_{i'}}\absolutevalue{b_{j'}}\bra{i}\bra{j}M\ket{i'}\ket{j'}

(3.3d)

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi_1^+}\bra{\psi_2^+}M\ket{\psi_1^+}\ket{\psi_2^+}\leq h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d_{1},d_{2}\right\rparen}(M).

(3.3e)

Here, the fourth line follows from the triangle inequality.
Therefore, the non-negative states

and

also achieve the desired optimum stoquastic separable value.

, it suffices to consider only non-negative quantum states.

The lower bound is straightforward. This is because there exist non-negative states
\ket{\psi_1}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}\lparen d_{1}\rparen
,
\ket{\psi_2}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}\lparen d_{2}\rparen
,
\ket{\psi'_1}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}\lparen d^{\prime}_{1}\rparen
, and
\ket{\psi'_2}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}\lparen d^{\prime}_{2}\rparen
such that the following holds:

\displaystyle\Tr\left\lparen M_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}\left\lparen\outerproduct{\psi_1}{\psi_1}_{\mathsf{A}}\!\otimes\!\outerproduct{\psi_2}{\psi_2}_{\mathsf{B}}\right\rparen\right\rparen

\displaystyle=h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d_{1},d_{2}\right\rparen}(M),

\displaystyle\Tr\left\lparen M^{\prime}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\left\lparen\outerproduct{\psi'_1}{\psi'_1}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\!\otimes\!\outerproduct{\psi'_2}{\psi'_2}_{\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\right\rparen\right\rparen

\displaystyle=h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d^{\prime}_{1},d^{\prime}_{2}\right\rparen}(M^{\prime}).

Then, there exist non-negative states
\ket{\phi_1}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\coloneq\ket{\psi_1}_{\mathsf{A}}\!\otimes\!\ket{\psi'_1}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(d_{1}d^{\prime}_{1})
and
\ket{\phi_2}_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\coloneq\ket{\psi_2}_{\mathsf{B}}\!\otimes\!\ket{\psi'_2}_{\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(d_{2}d^{\prime}_{2})
, which imply the desired lower bound:

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d_{1}d^{\prime}_{1},d_{2}d^{\prime}_{2}\right\rparen}(M\!\otimes\!M^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\Tr\left\lparen\left\lparen M_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}\!\otimes\!M^{\prime}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\right\rparen\left\lparen\outerproduct{\phi_1}{\phi_1}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\!\otimes\!\outerproduct{\phi_2}{\phi_2}_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\right\rparen\right\rparen

\displaystyle=h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d_{1},d_{2}\right\rparen}(M)\cdot h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d^{\prime}_{1},d^{\prime}_{2}\right\rparen}(M^{\prime})

To establish the upper bound, let
\ket{\phi_1}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(d_{1}d^{\prime}_{1})
and
\ket{\phi_2}_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(d_{2}d^{\prime}_{2})
be non-negative states that attain the maximum separable value of
M\!\otimes\!M^{\prime}
. Decompose these states in the computational basis of their first register,
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
, respectively:

\ket{\phi_1}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\ket{i}_{\mathsf{A}}\!\otimes\!\ket{\eta_i}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\quad\text{and}\quad\ket{\phi_2}_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\ket{j}_{\mathsf{B}}\!\otimes\!\ket{\vartheta_j}_{\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}.

(3.4)

A direct calculation implies the following normalization equalities:

\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\langle\eta_{i}|\eta_{i}\rangle=1\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\langle\vartheta_{j}|\vartheta_{j}\rangle=1.

(3.5)

Now we expand
h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d_{1}d^{\prime}_{1},d_{2}d^{\prime}_{2}\right\rparen}(M\!\otimes\!M^{\prime})
using the decompositions in
Equation

3.4
:

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d_{1}d^{\prime}_{1},d_{2}d^{\prime}_{2}\right\rparen}(M\!\otimes\!M^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bra{\phi_1}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\bra{\phi_2}_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\left\lparen M_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}\!\otimes\!M^{\prime}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\right\rparen\ket{\phi_1}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\ket{\phi_2}_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}

(3.6a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,\hat{i},\hat{j}}\bra{i}_{\mathsf{A}}\!\bra{\eta_i}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\!\bra{j}_{\mathsf{B}}\!\bra{\vartheta_j}_{\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\left\lparen M_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}\!\otimes\!M^{\prime}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\right\rparen\ket{\hat{i}}_{\mathsf{A}}\!\ket{\eta_{\hat{i}}}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\!\ket{\hat{j}}_{\mathsf{B}}\!\ket{\vartheta_{\hat{j}}}_{\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}

(3.6b)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j,\hat{i},\hat{j}}\bra{i}_{\mathsf{A}}\!\bra{j}_{\mathsf{B}}M_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}\ket{\hat{i}}_{\mathsf{A}}\!\ket{\hat{j}}_{\mathsf{B}}\cdot\absolutevalue*{\bra{\eta_i}_{\sfA'}\!\bra{\vartheta_j}_{\sfB'} M'_{\sfA'\sfB'} \ket{\eta_{\hat{i}}}_{\sfA'}\!\ket{\vartheta_{\hat{j}}}_{\sfB'}}.

(3.6c)

Here, the last line follows from the fact that
M
is entrywise non-negative.

Note that the entrywise non-negativity of
M^{\prime}
implies that all entries of
M^{\prime}
are real. Since
M^{\prime}
is positive semi-definite, it follows that
M^{\prime}
is real symmetric. Consequently,
M^{\prime}
can be viewed as a Gram matrix, and we may therefore apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the positive semi-definite bilinear form induced by
M^{\prime}
. In particular, for every
i\in[d_{1}]
,
j\in[d_{2}]
,
\hat{i}\in[d_{1}]
, and
\hat{j}\in[d_{2}]
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\absolutevalue*{\bra{\eta_i}_{\sfA'}\!\bra{\vartheta_j}_{\sfB'} \!M'_{\sfA'\sfB'}\! \ket{\eta_{\hat{i}}}_{\sfA'}\!\ket{\vartheta_{\hat{j}}}_{\sfB'}}

(3.7a)

\displaystyle\leq\penalty\

\displaystyle\sqrt{\bra{\eta_i}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\!\bra{\vartheta_j}_{\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\!M^{\prime}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\!\ket{\eta_i}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\!\ket{\vartheta_j}_{\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}}\cdot\sqrt{\bra{\eta_{\hat{i}}}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\!\bra{\vartheta_{\hat{j}}}_{\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\!M^{\prime}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\!\ket{\eta_{\hat{i}}}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\!\ket{\vartheta_{\hat{j}}}_{\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}}

(3.7b)

\displaystyle\leq\penalty\

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d^{\prime}_{1},d^{\prime}_{2}\right\rparen}(M^{\prime})\cdot\sqrt{\langle\eta_{i}|\eta_{i}\rangle}\cdot\sqrt{\langle\vartheta_{j}|\vartheta_{j}\rangle}\cdot\sqrt{\langle\eta_{\hat{i}}|\eta_{\hat{i}}\rangle}\cdot\sqrt{\langle\vartheta_{\hat{j}}|\vartheta_{\hat{j}}\rangle}

(3.7c)

Plugging
Equation

3.7
into
Equation

3.6
, we obtain the desired upper bound:

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d_{1}d^{\prime}_{1},d_{2}d^{\prime}_{2}\right\rparen}(M\!\otimes\!M^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\penalty\

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d^{\prime}_{1},d^{\prime}_{2}\right\rparen}(M^{\prime})\cdot\sum_{i,j,\hat{i},\hat{j}}\bra{i}_{\mathsf{A}}\!\bra{j}_{\mathsf{B}}M_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}\ket{\hat{i}}_{\mathsf{A}}\!\ket{\hat{j}}_{\mathsf{B}}\cdot\sqrt{\langle\eta_{i}|\eta_{i}\rangle}\cdot\sqrt{\langle\vartheta_{j}|\vartheta_{j}\rangle}\cdot\sqrt{\langle\eta_{\hat{i}}|\eta_{\hat{i}}\rangle}\cdot\sqrt{\langle\vartheta_{\hat{j}}|\vartheta_{\hat{j}}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\penalty\

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d^{\prime}_{1},d^{\prime}_{2}\right\rparen}(M^{\prime})\cdot\bra{\xi}_{\mathsf{A}}\bra{\varsigma}_{\mathsf{B}}M_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}\ket{\xi}_{\mathsf{A}}\ket{\varsigma}_{\mathsf{B}}

\displaystyle\leq\penalty\

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d^{\prime}_{1},d^{\prime}_{2}\right\rparen}(M^{\prime})\cdot h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d_{1},d_{2}\right\rparen}(M).

Here,
\ket{\xi}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\sqrt{\langle\eta_{i}|\eta_{i}\rangle}\ket{i}
and
\ket{\varsigma}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\sqrt{\langle\vartheta_{j}|\vartheta_{j}\rangle}\ket{j}
are non-negative states, where the normalization is ensured by
Equation

3.5
.
∎

Stoquastic separable values of product Hermitian matrices.

However, this multiplicative property only holds for Hermitian matrices in
product
form:

{lemma}

[Stoquastic separable values of product Hermitian matrices are multiplicative]

Let
M
and
M^{\prime}
be entrywise non-negative product Hermitian matrices of dimensions
\prod_{i\in[k]}d_{i}
and
\prod_{i\in[k]}d^{\prime}_{i}
, respectively, and suppose that these matrices admit the product decompositions

M=M_{1}\!\otimes\!\cdots\!\otimes\!M_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad M^{\prime}=M^{\prime}_{1}\!\otimes\!\cdots\!\otimes\!M^{\prime}_{k},

(3.8)

where each factor
M_{i}
and
M^{\prime}_{i}
is Hermitian and entrywise non-negative for
i\in[k]
. Then the stoquastic separable value is multiplicative under tensor products, namely

h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d_{1}d^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,d_{k}d^{\prime}_{k}\right\rparen}\left\lparen M\!\otimes\!M^{\prime}\right\rparen=h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}\right\rparen}(M)\cdot h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,d^{\prime}_{k}\right\rparen}(M^{\prime}).

Proof.

For simplicity, we prove only the case where
k=2
, and the same argument directly extends to general
k
. Similar to
Proposition

3.6
, it suffices to consider only non-negative quantum states. Also, the lower bound is straightforward, following the same argument as that used for the corresponding part of the proof of
Section

3.1
.

We now establish the upper bound. Let the operator
M
act on quantum registers
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
, and let the operator
M^{\prime}
act on quantum registers
\mathsf{A}^{\prime}
and
\mathsf{B}^{\prime}
.
After regrouping the registers as
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}
and
\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}
, we obtain

M_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}\otimes M^{\prime}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}=(M_{1}\otimes M^{\prime}_{1})_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\otimes(M_{2}\otimes M^{\prime}_{2})_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}

from
Equation

3.8
. Then it follows that:

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d_{1}d^{\prime}_{1},d_{2}d^{\prime}_{2}\right\rparen}(M\!\otimes\!M^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\penalty\

\displaystyle\max_{\ket{\phi_1}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(d_{1}d^{\prime}_{1}),\,\ket{\phi_2}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(d_{2}d^{\prime}_{2})}\bra{\phi_1}(M_{1}\otimes M^{\prime}_{1})\ket{\phi_1}\cdot\bra{\phi_2}(M_{2}\otimes M^{\prime}_{2})\ket{\phi_2}

\displaystyle\leq\penalty\

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(M_{1}\otimes M^{\prime}_{1})\cdot\lambda_{\max}(M_{2}\otimes M^{\prime}_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\penalty\

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(M_{1})\cdot\lambda_{\max}(M_{2})\cdot\lambda_{\max}(M^{\prime}_{1})\cdot\lambda_{\max}(M^{\prime}_{2})

\displaystyle=\penalty\

\displaystyle\max_{\ket{\psi_1},\ket{\psi_2}}\bra{\psi_1}\!\otimes\!\bra{\psi_2}\left\lparen M_{1}\!\otimes\!M_{2}\right\rparen\ket{\psi_1}\!\otimes\!\ket{\psi_2}\cdot\max_{\ket{\psi'_1},\ket{\psi'_2}}\bra{\psi'_1}\!\otimes\!\bra{\psi'_2}\left\lparen M^{\prime}_{1}\!\otimes\!M^{\prime}_{2}\right\rparen\ket{\psi'_1}\!\otimes\!\ket{\psi'_2}

\displaystyle=\penalty\

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d_{1},d_{2}\right\rparen}(M)\cdot h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen d^{\prime}_{1},d^{\prime}_{2}\right\rparen}(M^{\prime}).

Here, the fourth line follows from Theorem 3 in
[
Gan00
, Section VIII.3]
, which states that the absolute value of each eigenvalue of an entrywise non-negative matrix
A
is at most the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}(A)
. The fifth line uses the fact that
\lambda_{\max}(A)=\max_{\ket{\psi}}\bra{\psi}A\ket{\psi}=\bra{\psi_\star}A\ket{\psi_\star}
for some non-negative unit vector
\ket{\psi_\star}
that attains the optimum.
∎

3.7
Stoquastic separable values of product-convex Hermitian matrices are not multiplicative.

Similar to the notion of
separable
in
[
HM13
]
, one may wonder whether
Section

3.1
extends to convex combinations of such matrices, which we refer to as
product-convex Hermitian
matrices. The following simple example shows that multiplicativity fails in this more general setting.
Consider the product-convex Hermitian matrix
M=\outerproduct{00}{11}+\outerproduct{11}{00}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen X\otimes X+(-Y)\otimes Y\right\rparen
, where
X=\outerproduct{0}{1}+\outerproduct{1}{0}
and
Y=-\mathrm{i}\outerproduct{0}{1}+\mathrm{i}\outerproduct{1}{0}
.
A direct calculation shows that
h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen 2,2\right\rparen}(M)=1/2
.
12
12

12

For any non-negative product state
\ket{a}\otimes\ket{b}
of the form
\ket{a}\coloneqq a_{0}\ket{0}+a_{1}\ket{1}
and
\ket{b}\coloneqq b_{0}\ket{0}+b_{1}\ket{1}
, the AM–GM inequality together with
\langle a|a\rangle=1
and
\langle b|b\rangle=1
gives

\bra{a}\!\otimes\!\bra{b}M\ket{a}\!\otimes\!\ket{b}=2a_{0}a_{1}b_{0}b_{1}\leq\left\lparen a_{0}^{2}+a_{1}^{2}\right\rparen b_{0}b_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen a_{0}^{2}+a_{1}^{2}\right\rparen\left\lparen b_{0}^{2}+b_{1}^{2}\right\rparen=\frac{1}{2}=h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen 2,2\right\rparen}(M)
.
The optimum is attained at
\ket{a}=\ket{b}=\ket{+}
.

However, one can check that
h_{\mathrm{Sep}\left\lparen 4,4\right\rparen}(M\otimes M)\geq\bra{\psi}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\bra{\psi}_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\left\lparen M_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}\otimes M_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\ket{\psi}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\ket{\psi}_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\right\rparen=1/2
, which implies the desired contradiction.

3.2
Two
StoqMA
(
k
)-complete problems from the class definition

We introduce two
StoqMA
(
k
)-complete problems that arise directly from the class definition, and each admits an interpretation in terms of property testing over different objects. Our second complete problem (
\textnormal{{SepRCD}}_{k}
) is directly analogous to the
StoqMA
-complete problem
Reversible Circuit Distinguishability
(
RCD
) introduced in
[
Liu21
, Section 4]
.

3.2.1
Separable Stoquastic Close Image to Uniform

We start with an observation due to
[
Kob03
, Theorem 9]
: deciding whether
\Tr(\rho_0\rho_1)
is at least
2/3
or at most
1/3
for
single-qubit
states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
, whose purification can be prepared efficiently, is
BQP
-complete. This observation suggests that the acceptance probability of quantum computation can be interpreted as a closeness testing problem. Moreover, when neither
\rho_{0}
nor
\rho_{1}
admits such a measurement, we can instead implement the measurement induced by the SWAP test for mixed states
[
BCWdW01
,
KMY09
]
, which accepts with probability
(1+\Tr(\rho_0\rho_1))/2
.

Since the SWAP test can be viewed as a stoquastic verifier, as implicitly observed in
[
Liu21
, Section 4]
, we introduce our first
StoqMA
(
k
)-complete problem
\textnormal{{SepStoqCIU}}_{k}
:

13
13

13

This
StoqMA
-complete variant,
Stoquastic Close Image to Uniform
(
StoqCIU
), is obtained by removing the tensor-product promise; that is, in the promise, replacing the maximization over
\ket{\psi_1}\ket{\psi_2}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}\left\lparen 2^{\ell}\right\rparen
with the maximization over
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}\left\lparen 2^{2\ell}\right\rparen
.

3.8
k
-
Separable Stoquastic Close Image to Uniform
,
\textnormal{{SepStoqCIU}}_{k}
.

Let
R
be a polynomial-size classical reversible circuit consisting only of Toffoli, CNOT, and X gates. Suppose that
R
acts on an
m(n)
-qubit input state of the form

\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_k}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes m_{0}}\otimes\ket{+}^{\otimes m_{+}},

where each
\ket{\psi_j}
is an
\ell(n)
-qubit non-negative state for every
j\in[k]
, and
k\ell+m_{0}+m_{+}=m
. Assume that
R
has
r(n)
designated output qubits, where
1\leq r(n)\leq m(n)
and
r=r_{0}+r_{+}
. Let
\Phi_{R}(\cdot)
denote the quantum channel obtained by applying
R
and then tracing out all non-output qubits.
14
14

14

Notably, the underlying quantum channel belongs to a subclass of
completely positive trace-preserving maps
introduced and studied in
[
JS22
]
, which preserve non-negative amplitudes in both input and output states.
The promise problem
\textnormal{{SepStoqCIU}}_{k}[\alpha(n),\beta(n)]
is to decide whether one of the following holds:

•

Yes
:

\max\limits_{\ket{\psi_1},\cdots,\ket{\psi_k}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(2^{\ell})}\mathrm{F}^{2}\left\lparen\Phi_{R}\left\lparen\bigotimes_{j\in[k]}\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}\right\rparen,\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes r_{0}}\!\otimes\!\outerproduct{+}{+}^{\otimes r_{+}}\right\rparen\geq\alpha(n)
.

•

No
:

\max\limits_{\ket{\psi_1},\cdots,\ket{\psi_k}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(2^{\ell})}\mathrm{F}^{2}\left\lparen\Phi_{R}\left\lparen\bigotimes_{j\in[k]}\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}\right\rparen,\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes r_{0}}\!\otimes\!\outerproduct{+}{+}^{\otimes r_{+}}\right\rparen\leq\beta(n)
.

When
k=2
, we simply write this problem as
\textnormal{{SepStoqCIU}}[\alpha(n),\beta(n)]
.

We then establish our first
StoqMA
(
k
)-complete problem:

{lemma}

[
\textnormal{{SepStoqCIU}}_{k}
is
StoqMA
(
k
)-complete]

For all efficiently computable functions
\alpha(n)
and
\beta(n)
, and every efficiently computable positive integer-valued function
k(n)\leq\operatorname{poly}(n)
, such that
0\leq\beta(n)<\alpha(n)\leq 1
and
\alpha(n)-\beta(n)\geq 1/\!\operatorname{poly}(n)
, the following holds:

(1)

\textnormal{{SepStoqCIU}}_{k}[\alpha,\beta]
is in
\textnormal{{StoqMA}}\left\lparen k,\frac{1+\alpha}{2},\frac{1+\beta}{2}\right\rparen
.

(2)

\textnormal{{SepStoqCIU}}_{k}[\alpha,\beta]
is
\textnormal{{StoqMA}}\left\lparen k,\alpha,\beta\right\rparen
-hard.

Proof.

We prove only the case where
k=2
for simplicity, and the same argument directly extends to more general
k
.
To establish
Item

(1)
, we simply consider the SWAP test
[
BCWdW01
]
with two quantum states

\rho_{0}\coloneqq\Phi_{R}(\outerproduct{\psi_1}{\psi_1}\otimes\outerproduct{\psi_2}{\psi_2})\quad\text{and}\quad\rho_{1}\coloneqq\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes r_{0}}\otimes\outerproduct{+}{+}^{\otimes r_{+}},

which directly constitutes a
StoqMA
(2) verifier given the witness state
\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\ket{\psi_2}
. Noting that
\rho_{1}
is a pure state, it follows that
\mathrm{F}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=\Tr(\rho_0\rho_1)
. Consequently, the acceptance probability of the SWAP test for mixed states
[
KMY09
, Proposition 9]
implies the desired threshold bound.

To prove
Item

(2)
, we begin with a stoquastic verifier
V_{\mathcal{I}}
corresponding to the promise problem
\mathcal{I}\in\textnormal{{StoqMA}}_{\ell}(2)
. For any
x\in\mathcal{I}
, we fix the stoquastic verifier to be
V_{x}\coloneqq V_{\mathcal{I}}(x)
.
For any non-negative product state witness
\ket{\psi_1}\!\otimes\!\ket{\psi_2}
, the acceptance probability of
V_{x}
is

\displaystyle\Pr[V_{x}\text{ accepts}\ket{\psi_1}\!\otimes\!\ket{\psi_2}]

\displaystyle=\norm*{\ketbra{+}{+}_{\sfO} V_x \rbra*{\ket{\psi_1}\ket{\psi_2}\ket{\bar{+}}\ket{\bar{0}}}}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Tr\left\lparen\outerproduct{+}{+}_{\mathsf{O}}\Phi_{R}(\outerproduct{\psi_1}{\psi_1}\!\otimes\!\outerproduct{\psi_2}{\psi_2})\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\mathrm{F}^{2}(\outerproduct{+}{+},\Phi_{R}(\outerproduct{\psi_1}{\psi_1}\!\otimes\!\outerproduct{\psi_2}{\psi_2})).

Here, the third line follows from the identity in
Equation

2.1
.
Therefore, we complete the proof by taking
\Phi_{R}
to be the channel defined by

\forall\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{+}\big\lparen 2^{2\ell}\big\rparen,\quad\Phi_{R}(\rho)\coloneqq\Tr_{\overline{\mathsf{O}}}\big\lparen R\left\lparen\rho\otimes\outerproduct{\bar{0}}{\bar{0}}\otimes\outerproduct{\bar{+}}{\bar{+}}\right\rparen R^{\dagger}\big\rparen,

where
R=V_{x}
is the required classical reversible circuit.
∎

3.2.2
Separable Reversible Circuit Distinguishability

Next, we present our second
StoqMA
(
k
)-complete problem
\textnormal{{SepRCD}}_{k}
, which is a direct generalization of
[
Liu21
, Section 4]
:

3.9
k
-
Separable Reversible Circuit Distinguishability
,
\textnormal{{SepRCD}}_{k}
.

Let
R_{0}
and
R_{1}
be polynomial-size classical reversible circuits consisting only of Toffoli, CNOT, and X gates. Suppose that both
R_{0}
and
R_{1}
act on an
m(n)
-qubit input state of the form
\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_k}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes m_{0}}\otimes\ket{+}^{\otimes r}
, where each
\ket{\psi_j}
is an
\ell(n)
-qubit non-negative state for every
j\in[k]
and
k\ell+m_{0}+r=m

\ket{R_b}

\ket{R_b}\coloneqq R_{b}\left\lparen\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_k}\otimes\ket{\bar{0}}\otimes\ket{\bar{+}}\right\rparen

•

\langle R_{0}|R_{1}\rangle\leq\beta(n)

When

, we simply write this problem as

\textnormal{{SepRCD}}_{a,b}(\ell,m_{0},r)

It is worth noting that, when
k=1
, the promise problem
\textnormal{{SepRCD}}_{1}
coincides with the
StoqMA
-complete problem

introduced in
[

, Section 4.1]
.

{lemma}

[
\textnormal{{SepRCD}}_{k}
is
StoqMA
(
k
)-complete]

For all efficiently computable functions
\alpha(n)
and
\beta(n)
, and every efficiently computable positive integer-valued function
k(n)\leq\operatorname{poly}(n)
, such that
0\leq\beta(n)<\alpha(n)\leq 1
and
\alpha(n)-\beta(n)\geq 1/\!\operatorname{poly}(n)
, we have

\textnormal{{SepRCD}}_{k}[\alpha,\beta]\text{ is }\textnormal{{StoqMA}}\left\lparen k,\frac{1+\alpha}{2},\frac{1+\beta}{2}\right\rparen\text{-complete}.

In particular, for any stoquastic verification circuit
V_{x}
, the hard instance is given by the reversible circuit pair

We now prove the soundness:

{lemma}
[Soundness of
Algorithm

5.1

The soundness of the branch-local verifier

is at most
\varepsilon
.

If

\delta

.
Hence, assume from now on that

Define the following sets

\absolutevalue{L}<2\delta n;

and analogously because every

Therefore,

\absolutevalue{V\setminus G}<3\delta n.

For each vertex

and the

\iota^{*}:V\to\Sigma

For vertices with

Thus

iff the label at

We split the soundness analysis into two cases.

(a)

There is substantial ambiguity on good vertices. Then the consistency test is likely to see a same vertex sampled twice with different labels by the birthday paradox, and thus rejects.

(b)

The label is almost deterministic on a good fraction of vertices. Then the consistency test is likely to see adjacent vertices with unsatisfying labels by the birthday paradox, and thus rejects.

The following generalized birthday paradox, whose proof is deferred to the appendix, captures the birthday-paradox estimate required by the two cases:

{lemma}
[A generalized birthday paradox]

Let
\Omega
be a finite set of size
n
,
\mu
be a probability distribution on
\Omega
, and
B\subseteq\Omega\times\Omega
be a symmetric relation representing the “birthday collisions.”
For some subset
\Omega_{0}\subseteq\Omega
, if for some parameters
\alpha,\beta>0
and some integer

D>0
, the following are true

(1)

\mu(x)\leq\alpha/n
for every
x\in\Omega_{0}
;

(2)

for every
x\in\Omega_{0}
, the number of
y\in\Omega_{0}
with
(x,y)\in B
is at most

D
;

(3)

the bad-pair weight on
\Omega_{0}
satisfies

\lambda\coloneqq\sum_{(x,y)\in B\cap(\Omega_{0}\times\Omega_{0})}\mu(x)\mu(y)\geq\frac{\beta}{n}.

Then for any constant
\varepsilon>0
, there is a large enough
C
such that the
following holds for all sufficiently large
n
:
Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{K}
be i.i.d. samples from
\mu
with
K=C\sqrt{n}
,

\Pr\Bigl[\exists i<j\text{ such that }(X_{i},X_{j})\in B\cap(\Omega_{0}\times\Omega_{0})\Bigr]\geq 1-\varepsilon.

With this generalized birthday paradox at our disposal, we prove the promised soundness of our protocol by considering the aforementioned two cases:

Case
(a)
:
There is substantial ambiguity among good vertices.
Suppose

\sum_{v\in G}q(v)b_{v}\geq\kappa

for
\kappa>0
, whose exact choice
\kappa
is irrelevant in this case.
To apply the birthday paradox, define the sample space

\Omega\coloneqq V\times\Sigma\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(v,a)\coloneqq p(v,a),

and define

\Omega_{0}^{\mathrm{ver}}\coloneqq G\times\Sigma\subseteq\Omega.

Let
B_{\mathrm{ver}}\subseteq\Omega\times\Omega
be the symmetric bad-pair relation consisting of all ordered pairs

((v,a),(v,a^{\prime}))\quad\text{with }v\in G\text{ and }a\neq a^{\prime}.

For
x=(v,a)\in\Omega_{0}^{\mathrm{ver}}
, we have

\mu(x)=p(v,a)\leq q(v)\leq\frac{2}{n}.

Moreover, every
x\in\Omega_{0}^{\mathrm{ver}}
has at most
|\Sigma|-1
neighbors inside
\Omega_{0}^{\mathrm{ver}}
with respect to
B_{\mathrm{ver}}
. The bad-pair weight equals

\lambda_{\mathrm{ver}}\coloneqq\sum_{(x,y)\in B_{\mathrm{ver}}}\mu(x)\mu(y)=\sum_{v\in G}q(v)^{2}\sum_{a\neq a^{\prime}}r_{v}(a)r_{v}(a^{\prime})

Since
r_{v}(\iota^{*}(v))=1-b_{v}
and

, one has the elementary bound

\sum_{a\neq a^{\prime}}r_{v}(a)r_{v}(a^{\prime})\geq 2b_{v}(1-b_{v})\geq\frac{2}{|\Sigma|}b_{v}.

Also, for

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{ver}}\geq\sum_{v\in G}\frac{q(v)}{2n}\cdot\frac{2}{|\Sigma|}b_{v}=\frac{1}{|\Sigma|n}\sum_{v\in G}q(v)b_{v}\geq\frac{\kappa}{|\Sigma|n}.

Applying the generalized birthday paradox (
Section

5.1.1
) with

\Omega_{0}=\Omega_{0}^{\mathrm{ver}},\qquad\alpha=2|\Sigma|,\qquad\beta=\kappa,\qquad D=|\Sigma|-1,

we conclude that for

with

Case

Since we are in a

We begin by describing the

6.3
Perfectly agreeing pair.

For any
SepRCD

(R_{0},R_{1})
, we say that
(R_{0},R_{1})
is a

if there exists a non-negative product state
\ket{\psi}
such that

\langle R_{0}(\psi)|R_{1}(\psi)\rangle=1,\quad\text{where }\ket{R_z(\psi)}\coloneqq R_{z}\ket{\psi}\ket{0}^{\otimes m_{0}}\ket{+}^{\otimes r}\text{ for each }z\in\{0,1\}.

(6.1)

Furthermore, we say that
(R_{0},R_{1})
is an
\varepsilon
-near perfectly agreeing pair
if the condition in

is relaxed to
\langle R_{0}(\psi)|R_{1}(\psi)\rangle\geq 1-\varepsilon

{lemma}

[Perfectly agreeing pairs admit product subset states]

For every perfectly agreeing pair
(R_{0},R_{1})
corresponding to a
yes
instance of
\textnormal{{SepRCD}}_{1,\beta}(\ell,m_{0},r)
, let
\ket{\psi^\star}\coloneqq\ket{\psi^\star_1}\otimes\ket{\psi^\star_2}
be a non-negative state that satisfies
\langle R_{0}(\psi^{\star})|R_{1}(\psi^{\star})\rangle=1
. Then there exist non-empty subsets

S_{1}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\ket{\psi^\star_1})\quad\text{and}\quad S_{2}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\ket{\psi^\star_2})

such that the product subset state
\ket{\phi}\coloneqq\ket{S_1}\otimes\ket{S_2}
satisfies
\langle R_{0}(\phi)|R_{1}(\phi)\rangle=1
.

Proof.

Following
Section

3.2.2
, it suffices to consider a
SepRCD
instance
(\Gamma,I)
such that
\langle R_{0}(\psi)|R_{1}(\psi)\rangle=\bra{\psi}\Gamma\ket{\psi}
for every non-negative state
\ket{\psi}
. We now write

\ket{\psi^\star_1}=\sum_{x_{1}}\alpha^{(1)}_{x_{1}}\ket{x_1}\text{ and }\ket{\psi^\star_2}=\sum_{x_{2}}\alpha^{(2)}_{x_{2}}\ket{x_2},\quad\text{where }\alpha^{(1)}_{x_{1}}\geq 0\ \forall x_{1}\text{ and }\alpha^{(2)}_{x_{2}}\geq 0\ \forall x_{2}.

For the non-negative states
\ket{\psi^\star_1}
and
\ket{\psi^\star_2}

S_{1}\coloneqq\left\{x_{1}\,\middle|\,\alpha^{(1)}_{x_{1}}>0\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{2}\coloneqq\left\{x_{2}\,\middle|\,\alpha^{(2)}_{x_{2}}>0\right\}.

Thus, the support of the initialized state is the product set
K\coloneqq S_{1}\times S_{2}\times\{0^{m_{0}}\}\times U
. Since
\bra{\psi^\star}\Gamma\ket{\psi^\star}=1
, a direct calculation shows that

\norm*{ \ket{\psi^\star} - \Gamma\ket{\psi^\star} }^{2}_{2}=\langle\psi^{\star}|\psi^{\star}\rangle+\bra{\psi^\star}\Gamma^{\dagger}\Gamma\ket{\psi^\star}-2\bra{\psi^\star}\Gamma\ket{\psi^\star}=2-2\bra{\psi^\star}\Gamma\ket{\psi^\star}=0.

Consequently,
\Gamma\ket{\psi^\star}=\ket{\psi^\star}
.
Since
\Gamma
is a permutation matrix, any basis vector with non-negative amplitude cannot be sent outside the support of non-negative amplitudes. Thus, we obtain

\Gamma(K)\subseteq K\quad\text{and}\quad\absolutevalue{\Gamma(K)}=\absolutevalue{K},

(6.2)

where the second identity holds because
\Gamma
is bijective. Therefore,

6.2
implies that

\Gamma(K)=K\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma\ket{K}=\ket{K}.

(6.3)

Here,

corresponds to the set

Testing rectangular closure.

With this rectangular structure of non-negative product states established in

To formalize this perspective, we need to introduce some additional notation. Specifically, we refer to a Cartesian product

of two sets

•

Initialized sector

•

Good and bad transitions

\Gamma(a,b,0^{m_{0}},u)=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},z^{\prime},u^{\prime}).

\Gamma_{\bar{0}}(a,b,u)=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}).

Next, we define the notions of

and

and
T^{+}

\Gamma

\Gamma(K(S,T))\subseteq K(S,T)

Equivalently, this condition means that every transition that starts in the

\displaystyle S_{t+1}

\displaystyle T_{t+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq T_{t}\cup\left\{b^{\prime}\,\middle|\,\exists a\in S_{t},\ b\in T_{t},\ u\in U,\ \Gamma_{\bar{0}}(a,b,u)=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right\}.

Analysis of Algorithm
6.1

We will analyze the correctness of Algorithm

separately for

and

Completeness.

We begin with the perfect completeness case as a warm-up:

{lemma}
[Perfectly agreeing pairs imply rectangular closure]

Let
(\Gamma,I)
be a perfectly agreeing pair, viewed as a
yes
instance of
\textnormal{{SepRCD}}_{1,\beta}(\ell,m_{0},r)
. Then, there exists a starting pair whose rectangular closure never encounters a bad round.

Proof.

By utilizing
Section

6.1
, there exist non-empty sets
S,T\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\ell}
such that
\Gamma\ket{K(S,T)}=\ket{K(S,T)}
, where the initialized sector
K(S,T)=S\times T\times\{0^{m_{0}}\}\times U
and
U=\{0,1\}^{r}
.
Since
\Gamma
is a permutation, it follows that

\Gamma(K(S,T))=K(S,T)

(6.6)

Next, pick any starting pair
(a_{0},b_{0})\in S\times T
. We will prove by induction that

S_{t}\subseteq S\;\text{and}\;T_{t}\subseteq T\text{ holds for all rounds }t,

and hence no round is bad. It is easy to see that the induction hypothesis holds at round
t=0
, because
S_{0}=\{a_{0}\}
and
T_{0}=\{b_{0}\}
.

Now assume that the induction hypothesis holds at round
t
, equivalently, it holds that:

\text{If }a\in S_{t}\subseteq S,\;b\in T_{t}\subseteq T,\;\text{and}\;u\in U,\quad\text{then }(a,b,0^{m_{0}},u)\in K(S,T).

According to
Equation

6.6
, the image of
\Gamma
lies again in
S\times T\times\{0^{m_{0}}\}\times U
, and thus the transition in this round is not bad. Therefore, any newly added coordinates
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
satisfy
a^{\prime}\in S
and
b^{\prime}\in T
, which establishes the induction hypothesis at round
t+1
via

S_{t+1}\subseteq S\quad\text{and}\quad T_{t+1}\subseteq T.\qed

Next, we relax the perfect completeness requirement in
Section

6.3
and extend the result to the setting in which the completeness error is sufficiently small relative to the number of rounds:

{lemma}

[Near perfectly agreeing pairs imply rectangular closure in bounded rounds]

Let
(\Gamma,I)
be an
\varepsilon
-near perfectly agreeing pair, viewed as a
yes
instance of
\textnormal{{SepRCD}}_{1-\varepsilon,\beta}(\ell,m_{0},r)
.
If the completeness error satisfies the upper bound

\varepsilon\leq\tau^{2}_{L}/2^{r+3},\quad\text{where }\tau_{0}\coloneqq 2^{-\ell}\;\text{and}\;\tau_{t+1}\coloneqq\tau^{2}_{t}/16,

then there exists a starting pair whose rectangular closure does not encounter a bad round up to round
L
.

Proof.

Let
\ket{\psi_1}\ket{\psi_2}
be a non-negative product state whose initialized full state
\ket{\Omega}
satisfies

\bra{\Omega}\Gamma\ket{\Omega}\geq 1-\varepsilon.

(6.7)

Let
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
be probability distributions induced by the states
\ket{\psi_1}
and
\ket{\psi_2}
, respectively, defined by

\forall x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell},\quad p_{1}(x)\coloneqq\absolutevalue*{\innerprod{x}{\psi_1}}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad p_{2}(x)\coloneqq\absolutevalue*{\innerprod{x}{\psi_2}}^{2}.

Since both distributions
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are defined over
[2^{\ell}]
, there exist
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
such that

p_{1}(a_{0})\geq 2^{-\ell}=\tau_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad p_{2}(b_{0})\geq 2^{-\ell}=\tau_{0}.

We use this
(a_{0},b_{0})
as the starting pair.

We first bound the global near-invariance error. Since
\Gamma
is a permutation and
\langle\Omega|\Omega\rangle=1
, together with
Equation

6.7
, we obtain

\norm*{\Gamma\ket{\Omega}-\ket{\Omega}}_{2}^{2}=\bra{\Omega}\Gamma^{\dagger}\Gamma\ket{\Omega}+\langle\Omega|\Omega\rangle-2\bra{\Omega}\Gamma\ket{\Omega}=2-2\bra{\Omega}\Gamma\ket{\Omega}\leq 2-2(1-\varepsilon)=2\varepsilon.

Consequently, for every coordinate
y\in\{0,1\}^{2\ell+m_{0}+r}
, it follows that

\absolutevalue*{ \bra{y}\Gamma\ket{\Omega} - \innerprod{y}{\Omega}}\leq\sqrt{2\varepsilon}.

(6.8)

Using the assumed threshold on
\varepsilon
and the fact that
\tau_{t}\geq\tau_{L}
for every
t\leq L
, we obtain

\forall t\in[0,L],\quad\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\tau^{2}_{L}}{4\cdot 2^{r}}}=\frac{\tau_{L}}{2\cdot 2^{r/2}}\leq\frac{\tau_{t}}{2\cdot 2^{r/2}}.

(6.9)

Next, we prove by induction that

S_{t}\subseteq\left\{a\,\middle|\,p_{1}(a)\geq\tau_{t}\right\}\;\text{and}\;T_{t}\subseteq\left\{b\,\middle|\,p_{2}(b)\geq\tau_{t}\right\}\text{ for all rounds }t\leq L,

and hence no round before round
L
is bad. The induction hypothesis holds at round
t=0
, as
S_{0}=\{a_{0}\}
and
T_{0}=\{b_{0}\}
.

Now assume that the induction hypothesis holds at round
t<L
. Then, if
a\in S_{t}
,
b\in T_{t}
, and
u\in U
, and we write
x\coloneqq(a,b,0^{m_{0}},u)
, we have

\langle x|\Omega\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{p_{1}(a)p_{2}(b)}{2^{r}}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\tau^{2}_{t}}{2^{r}}}=\frac{\tau_{t}}{2^{r/2}}.

(6.10)

Let
y\coloneqq\Gamma x
, which implies
\bra{y}\Gamma\ket{\Omega}=\langle x|\Omega\rangle
. Consequently, combining the bounds in
Equations

6.8
and
6.9
yields that

\absolutevalue*{\innerprod{x}{\Omega}-\innerprod{y}{\Omega}}=\absolutevalue*{\bra{y}\Gamma\ket{\Omega}-\innerprod{y}{\Omega}}\leq\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\leq\frac{\tau_{t}}{2\cdot 2^{r/2}}.

(6.11)

We then show the transition in the current round is not bad by contradiction. More precisely, if
y
were outside the initialized sector, i.e. if
z\neq 0^{m_{0}}
, then
\langle y|\Omega\rangle=0
, and hence
Equation

6.10
would imply

\absolutevalue*{ \innerprod{x}{\Omega} - \innerprod{y}{\Omega} }=\langle x|\Omega\rangle\geq\frac{\tau_{t}}{2^{r/2}},

which contradicts
Equation

6.11
, as desired.

To establish the induction hypothesis at round
t+1
, we write
y=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},0^{m_{0}},u^{\prime})
. Then, combining
Equations

6.11
and
6.10
and the triangle inequality, it follows that:

\sqrt{\frac{p_{1}(a^{\prime})p_{2}(b^{\prime})}{2^{r}}}=\langle y|\Omega\rangle\geq\langle x|\Omega\rangle-\absolutevalue*{\innerprod{x}{\Omega} - \innerprod{y}{\Omega}}\geq\frac{\tau_{t}}{2^{r/2}}-\frac{\tau_{t}}{2\cdot 2^{r/2}}=\frac{\tau_{t}}{2\cdot 2^{r/2}}.

(6.12)

Since
p_{1}(a^{\prime})\leq 1
and
p_{2}(b^{\prime})\leq 1
holds for all
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, squaring
Equation

6.12
gives:

p_{1}(a^{\prime})\geq\frac{\tau_{t}^{2}}{4}\geq\frac{\tau_{t}^{2}}{16}=\tau_{t+1}\quad\text{and}\quad p_{2}(b^{\prime})\geq\frac{\tau_{t}^{2}}{4}\geq\frac{\tau_{t}^{2}}{16}=\tau_{t+1}.

Therefore, we establish the induction hypothesis by concluding that all newly inserted coordinates are heavy at threshold
\tau_{t+1}
, and no bad transition occurs in this round.
∎

Soundness.

We now prove the soundness part:

{lemma}

[Rectangle size growth for
no
instances before encountering a bad round]

Let
(\Gamma,I)
be a
no
instance of
\textnormal{{SepRCD}}_{\alpha,1-\gamma}(\ell,m_{0},r)
for some
\gamma>0
. Then, for every starting pair and every round
t
, one of the following holds: either round
t
is bad, or the rectangle size grows by a multiplicative factor of
1+\gamma
:

\absolutevalue*{S_{t+1}\times T_{t+1}}\geq(1+\gamma)\absolutevalue*{S_t \times T_t}.

(6.13)

Consequently, no starting pair can avoid encountering a bad round before round

L\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{2\ell\ln{2}+1}{\ln(1+\gamma)}\right\rceil.

Proof.

Fix a starting pair and a round
t
.
Assume that round
t
is not bad. It therefore suffices to prove that
Equation

6.13
. To do so, we show that the rectangle size grows exponentially even when one restricts attention to product subset state witnesses.

Consider a valid product subset witness state
\ket{S_t}\ket{T_t}
, whose initialized full state is uniform on
K(S_{t},T_{t})=S_{t}\times T_{t}\times\{0^{m_{0}}\}\times U
. A direct calculation shows that

\displaystyle\bra{K(S_t,T_t)}\Gamma\ket{K(S_t,T_t)}

(6.14a)

\displaystyle=\penalty\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|K(S_{t},T_{t})|}\absolutevalue*{\set{y\in K(S_t,T_t)}{\Gamma y \in K(S_t,T_t)}}

(6.14b)

\displaystyle=\penalty\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{r}|S_{t}\times T_{t}|}\absolutevalue*{\set{(a,b,u)\in S_t\times T_t\times U}{\GammaZero(a,b,u) \in S_t\times T_t}}.

(6.14c)

By combining the soundness promise
\bra{K(S_t,T_t)}\Gamma\ket{K(S_t,T_t)}\leq 1-\gamma
with
Equation

6.14
, we obtain a lower bound on the number of pairs escaping from
S_{t}\times T_{t}\times U
:

\mathrm{Esc}(S_{t},T_{t},U)\coloneqq\absolutevalue*{\set{(a,b,u)\in S_t\times T_t\times U}{\GammaZero(a,b,u) \notin S_t\times T_t}}\geq\gamma 2^{r}\absolutevalue*{S_t\times T_t}.

(6.15)

Because
\Gamma
is injective, these leaving triples
(a,b,u)
have distinct outputs
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=\Gamma_{\bar{0}}(a,b,u)
. For each output pair
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
, there are at most
|U|=2^{r}
possible choices of output random strings
u^{\prime}
.
Consequently, the number of distinct new output pairs outside
S_{t}\times T_{t}
is at least
\mathrm{Esc}(S_{t},T_{t},U)/|U|
. Since all these new output pairs are included in
S_{t+1}\times T_{t+1}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\absolutevalue*{S_{t+1}\times T_{t+1}}

\displaystyle\geq\absolutevalue*{S_{t}\times T_{t}}+\frac{\mathrm{Esc}(S_{t},T_{t},U)}{|U|}

\displaystyle\geq\absolutevalue*{S_{t}\times T_{t}}+\frac{\gamma 2^{r}\absolutevalue*{S_t\times T_t}}{2^{r}}=(1+\gamma)\absolutevalue*{S_t\times T_t}.

Here, the second line uses
Equation

6.15
, thereby establishing
Equation

6.13
.

If a starting pair were to reach round
L
without encountering a bad round, then combining
Equation

6.13
with
\absolutevalue*{S_0\times T_0}=1
would yield

\absolutevalue*{S_L \times T_L}\geq(1+\gamma)^{L}\absolutevalue*{S_0\times T_0}=(1+\gamma)^{L}>2^{2\ell}.

(6.16)

Here, the last inequality follows from the definition of
L
. Since
S_{L}\times T_{L}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\ell}\times\{0,1\}^{\ell}
,
Equation

6.16
leads to a contradiction. Therefore, no starting pair can reach round
L
without encountering a bad round, as desired.
∎

Next, we analyze two deterministic implementations of Algorithm

:

Recursive implementation.

For every fixed starting pair
(a_{0},b_{0})
, define indicator predicates

\forall t\in\{0,1,\cdots,L\},\qquad\chi_{S_{t}}(a)\coloneqq\mathbb{I}[a\in S_{t}]\quad\text{and}\quad\chi_{T_{t}}(b)\coloneqq\mathbb{I}[b\in T_{t}].

Initially, it holds that
\chi_{S_{0}}(a)=\mathbb{I}[a=a_{0}]
and
\chi_{T_{0}}(b)=\mathbb{I}[b=b_{0}]
.

For each round
t<L
, we first test whether round
t
is bad by evaluating the predicate

\mathrm{Bad}_{t}\coloneqq\exists a,b,u\ \mathbb{I}[\chi_{S_{t}}(a)\wedge\chi_{T_{t}}(b)\wedge\Gamma(a,b,0^{m_{0}},u)\notin A\times B\times\{0^{m_{0}}\}\times U].

If
\mathrm{Bad}_{t}
is false, then the next-round predicates are simultaneously given by

\displaystyle\chi_{S_{t+1}}(a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\chi_{S_{t}}(a^{\prime})\ \vee\ \exists a,b,b^{\prime},u\ \mathbb{I}\left[\chi_{S_{t}}(a)\wedge\chi_{T_{t}}(b)\wedge\Gamma_{\bar{0}}(a,b,u)=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right],

\displaystyle\chi_{T_{t+1}}(b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\chi_{T_{t}}(b^{\prime})\ \vee\ \exists a,a^{\prime},b,u\ \mathbb{I}\left[\chi_{S_{t}}(a)\wedge\chi_{T_{t}}(b)\wedge\Gamma_{\bar{0}}(a,b,u)=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right].

A depth-first deterministic evaluation stores only one recursion path of length at most
L
, together with a constant number of strings of total length
O(\lambda)
per level. Hence, the space is
O(L\lambda+S_{\Gamma})
.
There are
2^{2\ell}
starting pairs, and at each level the deterministic search enumerates at most
2^{2\ell+r}
triples. Thus, the running time is bounded by

(2^{2\ell+r})^{O(L)}\cdot\operatorname{poly}(L,2^{\ell},2^{r})\cdot T_{\Gamma}.

For each starting pair, Algorithm

stores

and

as bit tables of length

, and the whole implementation is still deterministic. During one round it scans all triples in

, or equivalently scans
A\times B\times U
and ignores triples outside the current rectangle. One round therefore takes time
O(2^{2\ell+r}T_{\Gamma})
. Consequently, over all

O(L2^{2\ell+r}T_{\Gamma})
.

There are
\absolutevalue{A\times B}=2^{2\ell}
starting pairs, so the total time is
O(L2^{4\ell+r}T_{\Gamma})
. The space consists of a constant number of bit tables for
S_{t}
and
T_{t}
, the current basis strings and counters, and the workspace for evaluating
\Gamma
, namely
O(2^{\ell}+\lambda+S_{\Gamma})
. ∎

\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim X}[x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}]\leq\Big(\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim X}[x_{\alpha}^{2}]\,\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim X}[x_{\beta}^{2}]\Big)^{1/2}=y_{\alpha}y_{\beta}.

\nu\leq\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim X}[M(x)]\leq\sum_{\alpha,\beta}M_{\alpha\beta}y_{\alpha}y_{\beta}=\langle y,My\rangle.

On the other hand, by our rounding and the choice of
z
, we have

M(x^{*})=\langle z,Mz\rangle.

So for symmetric
M
,

\nu-M(x^{*})\leq\langle y,My\rangle-\langle z,Mz\rangle=\langle y-z,M(y+z)\rangle\leq\norm{M}\,\norm{y-z}\,\norm{y+z}.

Since
y
and
z
are unit vectors,
\norm{y+z}\leq 2
; and
\|M\|\leq 1
, one concludes that the direct rounding is worse than the SDP value by an additive term
2\|y-z\|
:

\nu-M(x^{*})\leq 2\|y-z\|

(B.3)

The Hellinger distance is the bridge from
\|\cdot\|
to an entropic argument. In particular,

\displaystyle\norm{y-z}^{2}

\displaystyle=2-2\sum_{\alpha}\sqrt{\Pr[(A_{1},\dots,A_{t})=\alpha]\,\Pr[\{A_{1}\}\cdots\{A_{t}\}=\alpha]}

\displaystyle=2\,d_{\mathrm{H}}\big(\{A_{1}\cdots A_{t}\},\{A_{1}\}\cdots\{A_{t}\}\big)^{2}.

\{X\}
denotes the law of
X
, and
d_{\mathrm{H}}
is the Hellinger distance.
So the above analysis on the direct rounding can be summarized below:

{lemma}
[Direct rounding]
Let
X
be a level-
2t
pseudodistribution satisfying (
B.1
)-(
B.2
). If

d_{\mathrm{H}}\big(\{A_{1}\cdots A_{t}\},\{A_{1}\}\cdots\{A_{t}\}\big)\leq\varepsilon,

then the unit vector
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

x_{i}^{*}\coloneqq\Big(\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim X}[x_{i}^{2}]\Big)^{1/2}

satisfies

M(x^{*})\geq\nu-2\sqrt{2}\,\varepsilon.

Reweighting.

If direct rounding fails to provide a good approximation, then

d_{\mathrm{H}}\big(\{A_{1}\cdots A_{t}\},\{A_{1}\}\cdots\{A_{t}\}\big)\gtrsim\varepsilon.

Hence the variables
A_{i}
are somewhat correlated, and one can pin some variables to make the remaining variables more deterministic. Updating the pseudodistribution by reweighting (conditioning on the pinnings) iteratively decreases the entropy of the variables
A_{i}
. Since entropy is bounded, within a bounded number of iterations, the Hellinger distance must be small and at that time the direct rounding would work.
This is the part where we tighten the analysis in BKS exploiting the chain rule and symmetry bypassing a wasteful triangle inequality.

{lemma}
[Entropy decrement per conditioning round]
If

d_{\mathrm{H}}\big(\{A_{1}\cdots A_{t}\},\{A_{1}\}\cdots\{A_{t}\}\big)>\varepsilon,

then

\mathrm{H}(A_{t}\mid A_{1},\dots,A_{t-1})\leq\mathrm{H}(A)-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{t}.

(B.4)

Proof.

Let
P\coloneqq\{A_{1}\cdots A_{t}\}
and
Q\coloneqq\{A_{1}\}\cdots\{A_{t}\}
.
Using
Section

2.1
, we obtain

\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)\geq 2\,d_{\mathrm{H}}(P,Q)^{2}>2\varepsilon^{2}.

(B.5)

By the chain rule for relative entropy,

\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mathrm{I}(A_{j};A_{1},\dots,A_{j-1})\leq t\cdot\mathrm{I}(A_{t};A_{1},\dots,A_{t-1})

(B.6)

The inequality holds, because
(A_{1},\dots,A_{t})
is exchangeable with marginal
A
, and for every
j
,

\mathrm{I}(A_{j};A_{1},\dots,A_{j-1})\leq\mathrm{I}(A_{t};A_{1},\dots,A_{t-1})=\mathrm{H}(A)-\mathrm{H}(A_{t}\mid A_{1},\dots,A_{t-1}).

Then (
B.4
) in the statement follows by combining the above inequalities.
∎

{corollary}

[BKS conditioning succeeds when direct rounding fails]
If

d_{\mathrm{H}}\big(\{A_{1}\cdots A_{t}\},\{A_{1}\}\cdots\{A_{t}\}\big)>\varepsilon,

then there exist
i_{1},\dots,i_{t-1}\in[d]
with

\Pr[A_{1}=i_{1},\dots,A_{t-1}=i_{t-1}]>0

such that

\Psi(X_{i_{1},\dots,i_{t-1}})\leq\Psi(X)-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{t}.

Proof.

Lemma
B.1
says that the average, over
(A_{1},\dots,A_{t-1})
, of the conditional entropy

\mathrm{H}(A_{t}\mid A_{1},\dots,A_{t-1})
is at most
\mathrm{H}(A)-2\varepsilon^{2}/t
. Hence there exists a particular

(i_{1},\dots,i_{t-1})
of positive probability such that

\mathrm{H}(A_{t}\mid A_{1}=i_{1},\dots,A_{t-1}=i_{t-1})\leq\mathrm{H}(A)-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{t}.

It remains to identify this conditional law with
A(X_{i_{1},\dots,i_{t-1}})
. For every
i\in[d]
,

\displaystyle\Pr[A_{t}=i\mid A_{1}=i_{1},\dots,A_{t-1}=i_{t-1}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim X}[x_{i}^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{t-1}x_{i_{j}}^{2}]}{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim X}[\prod_{j=1}^{t-1}x_{i_{j}}^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim X_{i_{1},\dots,i_{t-1}}}[x_{i}^{2}]=\Pr[A(X_{i_{1},\dots,i_{t-1}})=i].

Therefore

\Psi(X_{i_{1},\dots,i_{t-1}})=\mathrm{H}(A(X_{i_{1},\dots,i_{t-1}}))=\mathrm{H}(A_{t}\mid A_{1}=i_{1},\dots,A_{t-1}=i_{t-1}),

and the claim follows.
∎

Putting things together.

Set

\delta\coloneqq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad R\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{t\log d}{2\delta^{2}}\right\rceil+1.

We now imitate the BKS combining algorithm, but with the corrected entropy decrement.

Start with
X^{(0)}\coloneqq X
. At stage
s
, let
A^{(s)}(X)
,
A_{1}^{(s)},\dots,A_{t}^{(s)}
, and
\Psi(X^{(s)})
denote the
objects associated with the current pseudodistribution
X^{(s)}
.
If

d_{\mathrm{H}}\big(\{A_{1}^{(s)}\cdots A_{t}^{(s)}\},\{A_{1}^{(s)}\}\cdots\{A_{t}^{(s)}\}\big)\leq\delta,

then Lemma
B.1
yields a unit vector
x^{*}
with

M(x^{*})\geq\nu-2\sqrt{2}\,\delta\,\norm{M}\geq\nu-\varepsilon\norm{M},

and we stop.
Otherwise, direct rounding fails at stage
s
, so Corollary
B.1
gives a tuple

i_{1}^{(s)},\dots,i_{t-1}^{(s)}\in[d]
such that

\Psi\big(X^{(s)}_{i_{1}^{(s)},\dots,i_{t-1}^{(s)}}\big)\leq\Psi(X^{(s)})-\frac{2\delta^{2}}{t}.

We then set

X^{(s+1)}\coloneqq X^{(s)}_{i_{1}^{(s)},\dots,i_{t-1}^{(s)}}.

If direct rounding were to fail for every stage
s=0,1,\dots,R-1
, then repeated application of
Corollary
B.1
would give

\Psi(X^{(R)})\leq\Psi(X^{(0)})-R\cdot\frac{2\delta^{2}}{t}\leq\log d-R\cdot\frac{2\delta^{2}}{t}<0,

which is impossible because entropy is always nonnegative. Hence some stage must succeed, and the resulting
vector
x^{*}
satisfies

M(x^{*})\geq\nu-\varepsilon.

Each conditioning step reweights by the square monomial
\prod_{j=1}^{t-1}x_{i_{j}}^{2}
, whose degree is

2(t-1)
. Hence after
s
rounds, the total degree loss is
2s(t-1)
. To keep direct rounding available at
stage
s
, we still need level at least
2t
at that stage. Therefore it is enough to start with

D\geq 2t+2(t-1)R.
By our choice of
R
,

D=O\left(\frac{t^{2}\log d}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

Thus, we have completed the proof of
Theorem

7.1
.

Appendix D
Omitted proof of the generalized birthday paradox

See
5.1.1

Proof.

Let
I_{ij}
be the indicator that
(X_{i},X_{j})\in B\cap(\Omega_{0}\times\Omega_{0})
, and

Z\coloneqq\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}I_{ij}.

Then
\E[Z]=\binom{K}{2}\lambda.

Because
\lambda\geq\beta/n
and
K=C\sqrt{n}
, we have

\E[Z]\geq\beta C^{2}/3

for all sufficiently large
n
.

We claim that
\E[Z^{2}]=O(1)+O(\E[Z]^{2})
, with constants depending only on

\alpha,\beta,D,C
.
Expand

Z^{2}=\sum_{i<j}I_{ij}+2\sum_{(i,j)<(k,\ell)}I_{ij}I_{k\ell}.

(D.1)

There are three kinds of terms in the above equation:

Diagonal terms.

These contribute exactly
\E[Z]
.

Disjoint pairs of pairs.

If
\{i,j\}\cap\{k,\ell\}=\varnothing
, then
I_{ij}
and
I_{k\ell}
are independent, so

\E[I_{ij}I_{k\ell}]=\lambda^{2}.

The total contribution of all disjoint pairs of pairs is therefore
O(K^{4}\lambda^{2})
, which is

O(\E[Z]^{2})
.

Overlapping pairs of pairs.

It remains to bound terms such as
\E[I_{ij}I_{ik}]
.
Define

s(x)\coloneqq\sum_{y\in\Omega_{0}:(x,y)\in B}\mu(y)\leq\frac{D\alpha}{n},

where the inequality is due to our assumption (i) and (ii).
Hence

\displaystyle\E[I_{ij}I_{ik}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Omega_{0}}\mu(x)s(x)^{2}\leq\frac{D\alpha}{n}\sum_{x\in\Omega_{0}}\mu(x)s(x)=\frac{D\alpha}{n}\lambda.

Because
\lambda\leq D\alpha/n
as well, this contribution is
O(1/n^{2})
.
There are only
O(K^{3})=O(n^{3/2})
overlapping pairs of pairs, so their total contribution is

O(K^{3}/n^{2})=o(1)
.

Combining all contributions,

\E[Z^{2}]\leq\E[Z]+O(\E[Z]^{2})+o(1).

Choose
C
large enough so that
\E[Z]\geq 1
for all sufficiently large
n
.
Then, for large
n
,

\E[Z^{2}]\leq A\E[Z]^{2}

for some constant
A=A(\alpha,\beta,D,C)>0
.
Paley–Zygmund now yields

\Pr[Z>0]\geq\frac{\E[Z]^{2}}{\E[Z^{2}]}\geq\frac{1}{A}.

To further amplify the above probability to
1-\varepsilon
, we increase the number of samples by a factor of
\log(1/\epsilon)
. More precisely, we repeat the experiment in
O(\log(1/\epsilon))
independent blocks and accept if any block finds a bad pair; this procedure reduces the failure probability to at most
\varepsilon
. For constant
\varepsilon
, this procedure only changes the absolute constant
C
. This gives the desired conclusion.
∎
