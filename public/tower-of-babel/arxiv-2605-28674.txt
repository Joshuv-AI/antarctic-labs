Disjunctive Sum of Squares
Amir Ali Ahmadi, Sanjeeb Dash, Yixuan Hua, Bartolomeo Stellato

Abstract
We introduce the concept of disjunctive sum of squares for certifying nonnegativity of polynomials. Unlike the popular sum of squares approach where nonnegativity is certified by a single algebraic identity, the disjunctive sum of squares approach certifies nonnegativity with multiple algebraic identities which can be found in parallel. Our main result is a disjunctive Positivstellensatz proving that we can keep the degree of each algebraic identity as low as the degree of the polynomial whose nonnegativity is in question. Based on this result, we construct a semidefinite programming based converging hierarchy of lower bounds for the problem of minimizing a polynomial over a compact basic semialgebraic set, where the size of the largest semidefinite constraint is fixed throughout the hierarchy. We further prove a second disjunctive Positivstellensatz which leads to an optimization-free hierarchy for polynomial optimization. We specialize this result to the problem of proving copositivity of matrices. Finally, we describe how the disjunctive sum of squares approach can be combined with a branch-and-bound algorithm and we present numerical experiments on polynomial, copositive, and combinatorial optimization problems.

The problem of optimizing over the intersection of the cone of nonnegative polynomials of a given degree with an affine subspace is fundamental to optimization and algebraic geometry, with broad applications in polynomial optimization

A polynomial

p(x)=\sum_{i}q_{i}^{2}(x)
for some polynomials

A sum of squares decomposition of a polynomial provides a single algebraic identity that certifies its nonnegativity.
Moreover, as is well known by now, such a decomposition (assuming it exists) can be obtained by solving a semidefinite program (SDP)

Throughout the paper, we denote the set of nonnegative polynomials and sos polynomials in
n

It has been known since the seminal work of Hilbert

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n,d}

M(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}^{4}x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{4}-3x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}+1.

(1)

Despite the existence of such examples, it turns out that a proof of nonnegativity based on a single algebraic identity is always possible though at the expense of increasing the degree of the sos polynomials that appear in the identity. More specifically, it follows from Artin’s solution to Hilbert’s 17th problem
[
4

that for every nonnegative polynomial

, such that the product
pq
is sos (and hence nonnegativity of

(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})M(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}^{2}(1-x_{2}^{2})^{2}+x_{2}^{2}(1-x_{1}^{2})^{2}+x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-2)^{2}.

(2)

By utilizing Artin’s theorem, one can design a complete SDP-based hierarchy for proving nonnegativity of a polynomial
p
. In level

With this context in mind, the central question that motivates our research is the following:
“Can one lower the complexity of a sum of squares based proof of polynomial nonnegativity by constructing several algebraic identities instead of a single one?”
Our primary notion of complexity here is the degree of the sos polynomials that appear in the proof of nonnegativity. However, our results also simplify the structure of these sos polynomials making the search for them amenable to cheaper classes of conic programs, such as linear programs. As an example, consider the following proof of nonnegativity of the Motzkin polynomial using two degree-6 algebraic identities:

\displaystyle M(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=(1-x_{1}x_{2})^{2}+(x_{1}^{2}x_{2}-x_{1}x_{2}^{2})^{2}+2x_{1}x_{2}(1-x_{1}x_{2})^{2}

(3)

\displaystyle=(1+x_{1}x_{2})^{2}+(x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{2}^{2})^{2}-2x_{1}x_{2}(1+x_{1}x_{2})^{2}.

Indeed, for every point

or
x_{1}x_{2}<0

follows from the first identity. Similarly, when

, nonnegativity of

follows from the second identity. We call a proof of this type a

From a computational standpoint, this means that the size of the semidefinite constraint underlying the second proof is smaller.

We recall that, imposing an sos constraint on a polynomial in

Our main contribution in this paper is to formalize the concept of disjunctive sum of squares proofs of nonnegativity and show that

1.1
Organization and Main Contributions

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we define the notion of a disjunctive sum of squares proof of nonnegativity of a polynomial.
We also show that a natural alternating maximization approach based on SDP can find low-degree disjunctive sum of squares proofs of nonnegativity of several classical non-sos polynomials.

In Section

introduces an SDP-based hierarchy, with semidefinite constraints of

In Section
4
, we prove a second disjunctive Positivstellensatz, which shows that a proof of nonnegativity of a polynomial can be given by simply checking nonnegativity of the coefficients of the same polynomial under several linear changes of coordinates. These linear transformations are explicit and derived from disjunctions based on simplicial partitions of the Euclidean space. An upper bound on the number of linear transformations is provided.

In Section
5
, we demonstrate how our disjunctive sum of squares approach can be applied to constrained polynomial optimization problems.
In Section
5.1
,
using a reduction from
[

]
, we construct a semidefinite programming based converging hierarchy of lower bounds for the general problem of minimizing a polynomial over a compact basic semialgebraic set, where the size of the largest semidefinite constraint is fixed throughout the hierarchy.
In Section
5.2
,
we focus on the case where the constraint set is the unit simplex, and we provide a specialized disjunctive Positivstellensatz for certifying copositivity of matrices.

In Section
6
, we show how one can more practically search for disjunctive sum of squares proofs by dynamically partitioning the Euclidean space through a
spatial branch-and-bound framework. As examples,
we propose branch-and-bound algorithms for minimizing a homogeneous polynomial over the unit sphere and for certifying copositivity of matrices. In Section

Finally, Section

concludes our paper and highlights potential future directions.

2
SOS with Respect to an Algebraic Disjunction

In this section, we introduce the concept of sum of squares with respect to an algebraic disjunction, which is central to our framework.
Throughout the paper, we denote the degree of a polynomial
p
by
\deg p
.

Definition 1

(Algebraic Disjunction)
.

Let
r,n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}
be positive integers. We say that a collection of polynomials
\mathcal{D}=\{\{q_{k,j}\}_{j=1,\ldots,n_{k}},\quad k=1,\ldots,r\}
forms an
algebraic disjunction
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if

\mathbb{R}^{n}=\bigcup\limits_{k=1}^{r}\Omega_{k},\ \text{where }\Omega_{k}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid q_{k,j}(x)\geq 0,\quad j=1,\ldots,n_{k}\}.

Definition 2
.

Let
p
be a polynomial in
n
variables and degree at most
d
,
\bar{d}\geq d
be an integer, and
\mathcal{D}=\{\{q_{k,j}\}_{j=1,\ldots,n_{k}},\quad k=1,\ldots,r\}
be an algebraic disjunction of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We say that
p
is
sos of order
\bar{d}
with respect to
\mathcal{D}

(or
p
is sos
{}^{\bar{d}}_{\mathcal{D}}
)
if there exist sos polynomials
s_{k,j}
for
k=1,\ldots,r
and
j=0,1,\ldots,n_{k}
, such that

p(x)=s_{k,0}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}s_{k,j}(x)q_{k,j}(x),\quad k=1,\ldots,r,

(4)

where
\deg s_{k,0}\leq\bar{d}
and
\deg(s_{k,j}q_{k,j})\leq\bar{d}
for
k=1,\ldots,r
and
j=1,\ldots,n_{k}
.
We denote the set of sos
{}^{\bar{d}}_{\mathcal{D}}
polynomials in
n
variables and degree at most
d
as
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n,d}^{\bar{d}}(\mathcal{D})
.

Observe that for any algebraic disjunction
\mathcal{D}
and integer
\bar{d}\geq d
, the set
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\bar{d}}_{n,d}(\mathcal{D})
is a convex cone and lies between
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n,d}
and
P_{n,d}
.
Indeed, by setting
s_{k,j}=0
for
k=1,\ldots,r
and
j=1,\ldots,n_{k}
, we note that
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n,d}\subseteq\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\bar{d}}_{n,d}(\mathcal{D})
. This inclusion can be strict even when
\bar{d}=d
; e.g., the Motzkin polynomial in (
1
) belongs to
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2,6}^{6}(\{\{+x_{1}x_{2}\},\{-x_{1}x_{2}\}\})
, as shown by the identities in (
3
).
To see that
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\bar{d}}_{n,d}(\mathcal{D})\subseteq P_{n,d}
, note that for any point
x\in\Omega_{k}
, we have
q_{k,j}(x)\geq 0
for all
j=1,\ldots,n_{k}
, and therefore, in view of (
4
),
p(x)\geq 0
.
Consequently,
p
is nonnegative over each subregion
\Omega_{k}
, and thus nonnegative over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We call the
r
algebraic identities in (
4
) a disjunctive sos proof of nonnegativity of
p
.

From a computational standpoint, for any fixed
\bar{d}
, linear optimization over the intersection of an affine subspace with the cone
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n,d}^{\bar{d}}(\mathcal{D})
can be carried out by an SDP of size polynomial in
n
. We also note that for implementation purposes, there is no need to include
s_{k,0}
as a decision variable; instead, one can impose the constraint that
{p(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}s_{k,j}(x)q_{k,j}(x)\in\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n,\bar{d}}}
for
k=1,\ldots,r
.

Unlike the well-known hierarchies for proving nonnegativity of a polynomial (e.g., based on the Positivstellensätze of Artin
[
4
]
, Reznick
[
43
]
, Stengle
[
49
,
40
]
, or Putinar
[
42
,
28
]
) that increase the degrees of the sos polynomials involved in the proof at each level of the hierarchy, our approach keeps the degrees of these sos polynomials fixed (and low).
We instead increase the number of subsets in the algebraic disjunction
\mathcal{D}
(see Theorem
1
and Theorem
8
).

A natural question is how to search for disjunctive proofs of nonnegativity of a polynomial
p
; i.e., for the identities in (
4
). Note that as written, the joint search for the polynomials
q_{k,j}
that define the algebraic disjunction
\mathcal{D}
and the sos multipliers
s_{k,j}
cannot be formulated as a convex optimization problem. An obvious workaround to this issue is to alternate the search for these decision variables. The details of this approach are described in the rest of this section together with some examples. In Section
3
and Section
4
, we present a more theoretically sound approach to finding disjunctive proofs of nonnegativity. We show that by appropriately fixing a family of algebraic disjunctions, we can always construct low-degree proofs of nonnegativity using convex optimization.

An alternating maximization algorithm.

In the joint search for the polynomials
q_{k,j}
and the sos multipliers
s_{k,j}
in (
4
), a challenge is to ensure that the polynomials
q_{k,j}
form a valid algebraic disjunction (see Definition
1
).
A simple way to guarantee this is to take
\ell
polynomials
h_{1},\ldots,h_{\ell}
, let
r=2^{\ell}
,
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{r\times\ell}
with all rows of
\varepsilon

q_{k,j}=\varepsilon_{k,j}h_{j},\quad k=1,\ldots,r,\quad j=1,\ldots,\ell.

With this construction, it is easy to see that the union of the corresponding
r
subregions
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{r}
as defined in Definition
1
is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Building on this, given a polynomial
p
of degree
d
and an integer
\bar{d}\geq d
, the problem boils down to finding polynomials
h_{j}
and sos multipliers
s_{k,j}
such that

p(x)=s_{k,0}(x)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\varepsilon_{k,j}h_{j}(x)s_{k,j}(x),\quad k=1,\ldots,r,

(5)

where
\deg s_{k,0}\leq\bar{d}
,

\deg(h_{j}s_{k,j})\leq\bar{d}
for
k=1,\ldots,r
and
j=1,\ldots,\ell
.
To approach this problem using convex optimization, we alternate between the search for the polynomials
h_{j}
and the sos multipliers
s_{k,j}
.
To track progress across iterations, we always maximize a lower bound on

that can be certified with identities of the form (

More concretely, we alternate between the following two steps, which can both be reformulated as a semidefinite program:

1.

Given polynomials
h_{j}^{*}
for

, solve the SDP

\begin{array}[]{cl}\max\limits_{\gamma,s_{k,j}}&\gamma\\
\text{s.t.}&p(x)-\gamma=s_{k,0}(x)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\varepsilon_{k,j}h_{j}^{*}(x)s_{k,j}(x),\quad k=1,\ldots,r\\
&\deg s_{k,0}\leq\bar{d},\ \deg(h_{j}^{*}s_{k,j})\leq\bar{d},\quad k=1,\ldots,r,\ j=1,\ldots,\ell,\\
&s_{k,j}\text{ is sos},\quad k=1,\ldots,r,\ j=0,\ldots,\ell.\end{array}

2.

Given sos polynomials
s_{k,j}^{*}
for
k=1,\ldots,r
and
j=1,\ldots,\ell
, solve the SDP

\begin{array}[]{cl}\max\limits_{\gamma,h_{j},s_{k,0}}&\gamma\\
\text{s.t.}&p(x)-\gamma=s_{k,0}(x)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\varepsilon_{k,j}h_{j}(x)s_{k,j}^{*}(x),\quad k=1,\ldots,r\\
&\deg s_{k,0}\leq\bar{d},\ \deg(h_{j}s_{k,j}^{*})\leq\bar{d},\quad k=1,\ldots,r,\ j=1,\ldots,\ell,\\
&s_{k,0}\text{ is sos},\quad k=1,\ldots,r.\end{array}

To run Step 1, we let the polynomials

for

be arbitrary (e.g., chosen at random) if we are in the first iteration, or an optimal solution to Step 2 if we are in subsequent odd iterations. Similarly, to run Step 2, we let

for

3
A Disjunctive Positivstellensatz

In this section, we introduce our main result in Theorem
1
which shows that one can a priori fix a family of low-degree algebraic disjunctions with respect to which there always exists a low-degree disjunctive sos proof of nonnegativity.

Let us establish some basic notation related to
forms
, i.e., homogeneous polynomials, which will be useful in the remainder of this paper.
We denote the set of forms, nonnegative forms, and sum of squares forms in
n
variables and degree
d
by
H_{n,d},\widehat{P}_{n,d},
and
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,d}
respectively.
Observe that
\widehat{P}_{n,d}=P_{n,d}\cap H_{n,d}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,d}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n,d}\cap H_{n,d}
.
Additionally, we say that a form
p
in
n
variables is
positive definite
if
p(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
x\neq 0
.
We are now ready to present our main result.

Theorem 1

(Disjunctive Positivstellensatz)
.

For any fixed dimension
n
and even degree
d
, there exists an explicit family of algebraic disjunctions

\mathcal{D}^{m}
, indexed by
m
, such that for any positive
definite form
p\in H_{n,d}

Our proof in fact provides several constructions: the forms in the algebraic disjunction may be chosen to have any even degree between 2 and

We prove Theorem

In Section

In Section

3.1
Local Positivstellensätze

For a polynomial

in
n

m\geq M(p)
. In particular,
\left(1+2M(p)\right)^{n}

Proof.

Recall the definition of the algebraic disjunction

\mathcal{D}_{d}^{m}=\big\{\{(x^{T}x^{i})^{d}-t_{m}h_{d}(x;x^{i})\},\quad i=1,\ldots,r_{m}\big\},

. For each

where

is defined as in (

Rewriting gives

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{p(x^{i})}{2}(x^{T}x^{i})^{d}+\sigma_{i}(x)-T(p;x^{i})h_{d}(x;x^{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{p(x^{i})}{2}\left((x^{T}x^{i})^{d}-t_{m}h_{d}(x;x^{i})\right)+\left(\sigma_{i}(x)+\left(\frac{p(x^{i})}{2}t_{m}-T(p;x^{i})\right)h_{d}(x;x^{i})\right).

Note that

Since

is a net such that for any

\displaystyle\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=2-2\langle x,y\rangle\leq 2\left(1-\frac{m}{\sqrt{m^{2}+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\sqrt{m^{2}+1}-m)}{\sqrt{m^{2}+1}}=\frac{2}{(\sqrt{m^{2}+1}+m)\sqrt{m^{2}+1}}

∎

3.3
An Algorithm Based on Spherical-Cap Subregions

Building on Theorem
1
, we propose an algorithm to approximate the minimum value
p_{\min}
of a form

over the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. This quantity is also equal to the optimal value of the following optimization problem:

p_{\min}=\;\begin{array}[t]{cl}\max\limits_{\gamma\in{\scriptsize\mathbb{R}}}&\gamma\\
\text{s.t.}&p(x)-\gamma\|x\|_{2}^{d}\geq 0,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{array}

(17)

For any even degree

, we can present a complete SDP-based hierarchy of lower bounds on

. At level
m
of this hierarchy, we construct a net
\mathcal{N}_{m}
of the unit sphere
6
6

6

There are different techniques for constructing such a net; see, e.g.,
[
32

12
]
.
and the corresponding algebraic disjunction

p^{(m)}\coloneq\begin{array}[t]{cl}\max\limits_{\gamma\in{\scriptsize\mathbb{R}}}&\gamma\\

(18)

The next theorem utilizes Theorem

Theorem 7
.

Let

and

\mathcal{D}^{m}_{d}

Proof.

Let

for all

\displaystyle T^{*}(p-\epsilon q)

\displaystyle=\sup_{U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:U^{T}U=I_{n}}\left(\frac{n}{2(p_{\min}-\epsilon)}\|(p-\epsilon q)(Ux)\|_{\infty}^{2}+\|(p-\epsilon q)(Ux)\|_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:U^{T}U=I_{n}}\left(\frac{n}{p_{\min}-\epsilon}\left(\|p(Ux)\|_{\infty}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\|q(Ux)\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)+\|p(Ux)\|_{1}+\epsilon\|q(Ux)\|_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:U^{T}U=I_{n}}\left(\frac{n}{p_{\min}-\epsilon}\left(\|p(Ux)\|_{\infty}^{2}+c_{\infty}^{2}\epsilon^{2}\right)+\|p(Ux)\|_{1}+c_{1}\epsilon\right)

4
An Optimization-Free Disjunctive Positivstellensatz

In this section, we introduce a new disjunctive approach to certify polynomial nonnegativity. Unlike our previous approach, which relies on sos polynomials and solutions to SDPs, the new approach only requires checking nonnegativity of the coefficients of some polynomials we can explicitly write down. In this framework,
checking
polynomial nonnegativity does not require any optimization solver at all, and
imposing
a nonnegativity constraint on a polynomial leads to a linear program. Our main result in this section is an optimization-free disjunctive Positivstellensatz (Theorem
8
), analogous to Theorem
1
.

4.1
Simplicial Disjunctions and Optimization-Free Certificates

In this section, we work with algebraic disjunctions where the corresponding subregions of the space are polyhedral cones.
Recall that a polyhedral cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be represented as a conic combination of a finite number of vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell}\}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x=\alpha_{1}v_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{\ell}v_{\ell}\;\text{ for some }\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}\geq 0\}.

We say that a polyhedral cone is simplicial if it is generated by linearly independent vectors.

Definition 3
.

Let
r
be a positive integer. We say that a collection of
n\times n
invertible matrices
\mathcal{V}=\{V_{k}\}_{k=1,\ldots,r}
forms a
simplicial disjunction
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if

\mathbb{R}^{n}=\bigcup\limits_{k=1}^{r}\Omega_{k},\quad\text{where }\quad\Omega_{k}=\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V_{k}\}\coloneq\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{v_{k,1},\ldots,v_{k,n}\},

and
v_{k,j}
is the
j
-th column of
V_{k}
.

Note that any vector
x\in\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V_{k}\}
can be written as
x=V_{k}z
for some elementwise nonnegative vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
V_{k}
is invertible, this implies that

\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V_{k}\}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid V_{k}^{-1}x\geq 0\}.

Therefore, the simplicial disjunction
\mathcal{V}
can be viewed as a special case of an algebraic disjunction introduced in Definition
1
with
n_{k}=n
for
k=1,\ldots,r
, and polynomials
q_{k,j}(x)=c_{k,j}^{T}x
for
k=1,\ldots,r,j=1,\ldots,n
, where
c_{k,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the columns of
V_{k}^{-T}
.
We now explain how simplicial disjunctions can help certify polynomial nonnegativity. Because of the homogenization argument given in Section
3.1
, we restrict attention to forms.

Definition 4
.

A polynomial
p\in H_{n,d}
is said to
have nonnegative coefficients with respect to a simplicial disjunction

\mathcal{V}=\{V_{k}\}_{k=1,\ldots,r}
if
p(V_{k}x)
has nonnegative coefficients for all
k=1,\ldots,r
. We denote the set of such polynomials by
\text{NC}_{n,d}(\mathcal{V})
.

Analogous to the cone
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n,d}^{\bar{d}}(\mathcal{D})

in Definition
2
, the set
\text{NC}_{n,d}(\mathcal{V})
is a convex cone.
Moreover, any polynomial
p\in\text{NC}_{n,d}(\mathcal{V})
has an explicit disjunctive proof of nonnegativity. Indeed, for
k=1,\ldots,r
, let
\sigma_{k}(x)\coloneq p(V_{k}x)
, which, by definition, has nonnegative coefficients. We can therefore write

p(x)=\sigma_{k}(V_{k}^{-1}x),\quad k=1,\ldots,r.

Since
\sigma_{k}
has nonnegative coefficients, we have the implication

V_{k}^{-1}x\geq 0\ \Rightarrow\ \sigma_{k}(V_{k}^{-1}x)\geq 0,

for all
k=1,\ldots,r
. Equivalently,
x\in\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V_{k}\}\ \Rightarrow\ p(x)\geq 0
. Since
\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V_{k}\}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
by the definition of a simplicial disjunction,
p
is nonnegative over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We now establish the following optimization-free disjunctive Positivstellensatz, which runs parallel to the disjunctive Positivstellensatz in Theorem
1
. It shows that every positive definite form has a disjunctive proof of nonnegativity of the above type.

Theorem 8

(Optimization-Free Disjunctive Positivstellensatz)
.

For any fixed dimension
n
and degree
d
, there exists an explicit family of simplicial disjunctions

\mathcal{V}^{m}
, indexed by
m
, such that for any positive
definite form
p\in H_{n,d}
, we have
p\in\text{NC}_{n,d}(\mathcal{V}^{m})
for some
m
.

4.2
A Local Optimization-Free Positivstellensatz

To prove Theorem
8
, we first establish a local optimization-free Positivstellensatz in Theorem
9
, which runs parallel to Theorem
2
. Recall that
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the largest coefficient in absolute value of a polynomial in the standard monomial basis. For an
n
-dimensional multi-index
\alpha
with
|\alpha|=d
, we define

\binom{d}{\alpha}\coloneq\frac{d!}{\alpha_{1}!\ldots\alpha_{n}!}.

We denote the
n
-dimensional vector of all ones by
\mathbf{1}
.

Theorem 9
.

For a polynomial
p\in H_{n,d}
with
d
even, if
p(x^{*})>0
for some
x^{*}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, then there exists
\delta>0
such that for any matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
\|V-x^{*}\mathbf{1}^{T}\|_{F}\leq\delta
, the polynomial
p(Vx)
has nonnegative coefficients.
In particular, the above statement holds for any

\delta\leq\delta(p;x^{*})\coloneq\sup_{\epsilon>1/p(x^{*})}\min\left\{\frac{(p(x^{*})\epsilon)^{1/d}-1}{(p(x^{*})\epsilon)^{1/d}+1},\frac{1}{(1+\eta(p)\epsilon)n}\right\},

where

\eta(p)\coloneq\sup_{U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:U^{T}U=I_{n}}\|p(Ux)\|_{\infty}.

Remark 4
.

The polynomial
p(Vx)
having nonnegative coefficients implies that
p(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V\}
. In particular, when
V
is invertible,
\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V\}
is simplicial and full-dimensional, which provides a proof of nonnegativity of
p
over a full-dimensional neighborhood around
x^{*}
.

Proof.

We first consider the special case where
x^{*}=e_{1}
. Let
p(x)=\sum_{\alpha:|\alpha|=d}p_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}
. For each multi-index
\beta
with
|\beta|=d
, we estimate the coefficient of the monomial
x^{\beta}
in the expansion
p(Vx)=\sum_{\alpha:|\alpha|=d}p_{\alpha}(Vx)^{\alpha}
.
Let
c(\alpha,\beta)
denote the coefficient of
x^{\beta}
arising from the expansion of
(Vx)^{\alpha}
. Then, the coefficient of
x^{\beta}
in
p(Vx)
is given by

\sum_{\alpha:|\alpha|=d}p_{\alpha}c(\alpha,\beta).

In the case where
\alpha\neq de_{1}
(i.e.,
x^{\alpha}\neq x_{1}^{d}
), we bound
c(\alpha,\beta)
by first noting that

(Vx)^{\alpha}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}(V_{k1}x_{1}+\ldots+V_{kn}x_{n})^{\alpha_{k}}.

Since
\|V-e_{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\|_{F}\leq\delta
, it follows that
V_{1j}\in[1-\delta,1+\delta]
for
j=1,\ldots,n
, and
V_{kj}\in[-\delta,\delta]
for
k=2,\ldots,n
and
j=1,\ldots,n
. Hence,
\max_{1\leq j\leq n}|V_{1j}|\leq 1+\delta
and
\max_{1\leq j\leq n}|V_{kj}|\leq\delta
for
k=2,\ldots,n
.
Therefore, recalling the multinomial theorem, we have

\displaystyle|c(\alpha,\beta)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma_{1}+\ldots+\gamma_{n}=\beta\\
|\gamma_{i}|=\alpha_{i},\;i=1,\ldots,n\end{subarray}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq n}|V_{kj}|\right)^{\alpha_{k}}\binom{\alpha_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}

(19)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\delta)^{\alpha_{1}}\delta^{d-\alpha_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma_{1}+\ldots+\gamma_{n}=\beta\\
|\gamma_{i}|=\alpha_{i},\;i=1,\ldots,n\end{subarray}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\binom{\alpha_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}

\displaystyle=(1+\delta)^{\alpha_{1}}\delta^{d-\alpha_{1}}\binom{d}{\beta},

The last equality follows from matching the coefficients of
x^{\beta}
on both sides of the identity
(x_{1}+\dots+x_{n})^{d}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}(x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n})^{\alpha_{k}}
and employing the multinomial theorem.

We now turn to the case
\alpha=de_{1}
, where we have
(Vx)^{\alpha}=(V_{11}x_{1}+\ldots+V_{1n}x_{n})^{d}
. For any
\delta\leq\delta(p;e_{1})
, we have
\delta<1/n\leq 1
. Thus,
\min_{1\leq j\leq n}V_{1j}\geq 1-\delta\geq 0
, and

c(\alpha,\beta)\geq\left(\min_{1\leq j\leq n}V_{1j}\right)^{d}\binom{d}{\beta}\geq(1-\delta)^{d}\binom{d}{\beta}.

(20)

Combining the bounds (
19
) for
\alpha\neq de_{1}
and (
20
) for
\alpha=de_{1}
, the coefficient of
x^{\beta}
in
p(Vx)
can be bounded from below as

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha:|\alpha|=d}p_{\alpha}c(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=p_{de_{1}}c(de_{1},\beta)+\sum_{\alpha:|\alpha|=d,\alpha\neq de_{1}}p_{\alpha}c(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=p(e_{1})c(de_{1},\beta)+\sum_{l=0}^{d-1}\sum_{\alpha:|\alpha|=d,\alpha_{1}=l}p_{\alpha}c(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\geq\binom{d}{\beta}\left(p(e_{1})(1-\delta)^{d}-\|p\|_{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{d-1}\sum_{\alpha:|\alpha|=d,\alpha_{1}=l}(1+\delta)^{\alpha_{1}}\delta^{d-\alpha_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\binom{d}{\beta}\left(p(e_{1})(1-\delta)^{d}-\|p\|_{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{d-1}n^{d-l}(1+\delta)^{l}\delta^{d-l}\right)

\displaystyle=\binom{d}{\beta}\left(p(e_{1})(1-\delta)^{d}-\|p\|_{\infty}\left(\frac{1+\delta}{n\delta}-1\right)^{-1}\left((1+\delta)^{d}-n^{d}\delta^{d}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\binom{d}{\beta}\left(p(e_{1})(1-\delta)^{d}-\|p\|_{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{n\delta}-1\right)^{-1}(1+\delta)^{d}\right)

where the second inequality follows from the fact that the number of distinct
n

\alpha

|\alpha|=d
and
\alpha_{1}=l

\binom{d-l+n-2}{n-2}\leq n^{d-l}
.

For any
\epsilon>1/p(e_{1})>0

\delta\leq\frac{1}{(1+\eta(p)\epsilon)n}\leq\frac{1}{(1+\|p\|_{\infty}\epsilon)n},

we have

This expression is nonnegative because

\delta\leq\frac{(p(e_{1})\epsilon)^{1/d}-1}{(p(e_{1})\epsilon)^{1/d}+1}.

Therefore, the coefficient of
x^{\beta}
in

remains nonnegative for every multi-index
\beta
with
|\beta|=d
whenever
\delta\leq\delta(p;e_{1})
.
Therefore, the theorem follows for the case where
x^{*}=e_{1}
.

For a general point
x^{*}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, there exists an orthogonal matrix

, such that

. Let
p_{U}(x)=p(Ux)

\tilde{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

\|U^{T}V-e_{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\|_{F}=\|U(U^{T}V-e_{1}\mathbf{1}^{T})\|_{F}=\|V-x^{*}\mathbf{1}^{T}\|_{F}\leq\delta(p_{U};e_{1}).

This implies that
p(Vx)=p(U(U^{T}V)x)
has nonnegative coefficients. Finally, observe that
\eta(p)
is unitarily invariant, i.e.,

\displaystyle\delta(p_{U};e_{1})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\epsilon>1/p_{U}(e_{1})}\min\left\{\frac{(p_{U}(e_{1})\epsilon)^{1/d}-1}{(p_{U}(e_{1})\epsilon)^{1/d}+1},\frac{1}{(1+\eta(p_{U})\epsilon)n}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\epsilon>1/p(x^{*})}\min\left\{\frac{(p(x^{*})\epsilon)^{1/d}-1}{(p(x^{*})\epsilon)^{1/d}+1},\frac{1}{(1+\eta(p)\epsilon)n}\right\}

4.3
Proof of the Optimization-Free Disjunctive Positivstellensatz

With the theorem from the previous subsection in place, we are now ready to prove Theorem

Proof of Theorem
8
.

We construct

The columns of the matrix

\alpha/m

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\alpha}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}-\frac{\beta}{\|\beta\|_{2}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}+\beta\left(\frac{1}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}-\frac{1}{\|\beta\|_{2}}\right)\right\|_{2}

(21)

Analogous to Algorithm
1

an optimization-free algorithm for certifying polynomial nonnegativity. In practice, instead of uniformly refining each simplicial cone as in the proof, it is more efficient to adaptively refine these cones within a spatial branch-and-bound framework. Even with such a modification, we expect the optimization-free approach to require a significantly larger number of regions to obtain a disjunctive certificate of nonnegativity compared to the disjunctive sum of squares approach based on semidefinite programming (i.e., the approach of Section
3
). Studying these tradeoffs computationally is a direction for future research.

5
Extensions to Constrained Optimization Problems

In this section, we extend the disjunctive sum of squares method from certifying global nonnegativity of polynomials to certifying nonnegativity over a closed basic semialgebraic set, i.e., a subset of the Euclidean space defined by finitely many polynomial inequalities.
We present two approaches. The first, discussed in Section
5.1

[
2

that converts constrained polynomial optimization problems (POPs) into unconstrained ones, to which our disjunctive sum of squares method then applies. The second, given in Section

so that the subregions defined by the algebraic disjunction cover the feasible set of interest rather than all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We illustrate this approach for the problem of testing matrix copositivity, which reduces to minimizing a quadratic form over the nonnegative orthant.

5.2
An Application to Copositive Programming

In this section, we give an example of how the disjunctive sum of squares approach can be adapted to certify nonnegativity over a subset of the Euclidean space. We take this subset to be the nonnegative orthant, which arises naturally in copositive programming.

A symmetric matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is said to be
copositive
if
x^{T}Qx\geq 0
for all
x\geq 0
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{n}
denote the cone of
n\times n
copositive matrices. A copositive program is a linear optimization problem over the intersection of
\mathcal{C}_{n}
with an affine subspace.
In recent years, copositive programming has found many applications in both discrete and continuous optimization; see, e.g.,
[
11
,
18
]
.
Since checking copositivity of a given matrix is NP-hard
[
38
]
, tractable inner approximations to
\mathcal{C}_{n}
are of interest in most applications of copositive programming.

A well-known SDP-based sufficient condition for copositivity is the so-called “
P+N
” criterion. For a symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, let us use the standard notation
A\succeq 0
(resp.
A\geq 0
) to denote that
A
is positive semidefinite (resp. elementwise nonnegative). Observe that if a matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
can be written as
Q=P+N
, where
P\succeq 0
and
N\geq 0
, then
Q
is copositive.
This condition is known to also be necessary for copositivity if
n\leq 4

[
17
]
. For
n\geq 5
, however, there are copositive matrices that do not admit a
P+N
decomposition. A classical example is the Horn matrix
[
17
]
:

H=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}1&-1&\phantom{-}1&\phantom{-}1&-1\\
-1&\phantom{-}1&-1&\phantom{-}1&\phantom{-}1\\
\phantom{-}1&-1&\phantom{-}1&-1&\phantom{-}1\\
\phantom{-}1&\phantom{-}1&-1&\phantom{-}1&-1\\
-1&\phantom{-}1&\phantom{-}1&-1&\phantom{-}1\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(25)

To bridge the gap between the
P+N
criterion and copositivity, a complete SDP-based hierarchy of sufficient conditions
for copositivity was introduced in
[
40
]
.
Let
x.^{2}\coloneq(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2})
. At level
m
of this hierarchy, one checks if the polynomial
(x.^{2})^{T}Q(x.^{2})\cdot(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2})^{m}
is sos, and if so, copositivity is certified. The zeroth level of this hierarchy recovers exactly the
P+N
criterion described above
[
40
]
.
Moreover, this hierarchy is complete: for any strictly copositive matrix
Q
(i.e., a matrix
Q
satisfying
x^{T}Qx>0
for all
x\geq 0
with
x\neq 0
), there exists a level
m
at which copositivity is certified
[
40
,
43
]
.
However, the size of the semidefinite constraint at level
m
of this hierarchy grows as
\binom{n+m+2}{n}\times\binom{n+m+2}{n}
. While one can reduce this size using symmetry reduction techniques
[
20
, Section 8.1]
, the higher levels of this hierarchy often remain too expensive to solve.

Here, we utilize the disjunctive sos approach to propose an alternative SDP-based complete hierarchy for certifying copositivity of an
n\times n
matrix, where the size of the semidefinite constraint remains
n\times n
throughout the hierarchy. To this end, we extend the notion of a simplicial disjunction in Definition
4
and require the subregions to cover only the nonnegative orthant rather than all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 5
.

Let

be a positive integer. We say that a collection of

invertible matrices

forms a

of a region
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
if

\Omega\subseteq\bigcup\limits_{k=1}^{r}\Omega_{k},\quad\text{where }\quad\Omega_{k}=\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V_{k}\}\coloneq\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{v_{k,1},\ldots,v_{k,n}\},

is the

Definition 6

A symmetric matrix

Observe that for any simplicial disjunction

.

Let
q(x)\coloneq x^{T}Qx
.
Since
q
is homogeneous and
Q
is strictly copositive, we have
q(x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|_{2})>0
for all
x^{*}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Delta^{n-1}}
. Letting
\delta(\cdot;\cdot)
be defined as in Theorem
9
, it follows that
\delta(q;\,x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|_{2})>0
for all

x^{*}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Delta^{n-1}}
. It is straightforward to check that
\delta_{\min}\coloneq\inf_{x^{*}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Delta^{n-1}}}\|x^{*}\|_{2}\,\delta(q;x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|_{2})>0
.
For any integer
m\geq\lceil\sqrt{2n}/\delta_{\min}\rceil
and any matrix
V\in\mathcal{V}^{m}
,
\operatornamewithlimits{conv}\{V\}
is a polytope with edge length
\sqrt{2}/m
, so for any column
v\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Delta^{n-1}}
of
V
, we have

\|V-v\mathbf{1}^{T}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{n}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{m}\leq\delta_{\min}\leq\|v\|_{2}\,\delta(q;v/\|v\|_{2}).

Thus,
\left\|V/\|v\|_{2}-(v/\|v\|_{2})\mathbf{1}^{T}\right\|_{F}\leq\delta(q;v/\|v\|_{2})
, and by Theorem
9
, the polynomial
q(Vx/\|v\|_{2})=x^{T}V^{T}QVx/\|v\|_{2}^{2}

has nonnegative coefficients, hence
V^{T}QV\geq 0
.
Since the same argument holds for each matrix in
\mathcal{V}^{m}
, we conclude that

Q\in\mathcal{PN}_{n}(\mathcal{V}^{m})
.
∎

Remark 5
.

We make two observations regarding Theorem
11
and Definition
6
:

(i)

By the construction in the proof of Theorem
11
, the first simplicial disjunction
\mathcal{V}^{1}
contains only the identity matrix,
and hence checking membership in
\mathcal{PN}_{n}(\mathcal{V}^{1})
reduces exactly to the
P+N
test.
Moreover, the tests for copositivity in the higher levels of the hierarchy all dominate the
P+N
test. Indeed, consider an arbitrary integer
m\geq 1
. If
Q=P+N
with
P\succeq 0
and
N\geq 0
, then for each matrix
V
in
\mathcal{V}^{m}
,
V^{T}QV=V^{T}PV+V^{T}NV
, where
V^{T}PV\succeq 0
, and
V^{T}NV\geq 0
as the entries of
V
are nonnegative by construction. Hence,
Q\in\mathcal{PN}_{n}(\mathcal{V}^{m})
.

(ii)

Setting
P_{k}=0
in Definition
6
reduces the condition

V_{k}^{T}QV_{k}=P_{k}+N_{k}
to
V_{k}^{T}QV_{k}~\geq~0
,
which coincides with the sufficient condition for copositivity in
[
9
, Theorem 1]
.
However, by doing so, no level of the resulting hierarchy would dominate the
P+N
test. See also Section
6.3.2
for some related comparisons in numerical experiments.

6
A Spatial Branch-and-Bound Framework

While the Positivstellensätze in previous sections show that one can always find low-degree disjunctive sos proofs of nonnegativity of a polynomial by uniformly dividing the space, in practice it is more efficient to perform this division
adaptively
. In this section, we describe how this can be done by integrating our framework into a spatial branch-and-bound (SBB) algorithm.
We present SBB algorithms to certify polynomial nonnegativity and matrix copositivity in Section
6.1
and Section
6.2
, respectively, and evaluate their numerical performance in Section
6.3
.

6.1
A SBB Framework for Polynomial Minimization

In this section, we apply a branch-and-bound algorithm to approximate the minimum value
p_{\min}
of a form
p\in H_{n,d}
over the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
by adaptively dividing the sphere into subregions using simplicial disjunctions.
We use simplicial disjunctions because it is straightforward to partition a simplicial cone into two smaller ones.

We consider two choices for the initial simplicial disjunction:

(1)

2^{n-1}
matrices, each of the form
8
8

8

Since even-degree homogeneous polynomials are symmetric with respect to the origin, it suffices to certify nonnegativity on a hemisphere.

\begin{bmatrix}\pm e_{1}&\ldots&\pm e_{n-1}&e_{n}\end{bmatrix}
.

(2)

n+1
matrices, constructed as follows: Consider
n+1
vertices of a regular
n
-simplex

c_{i}=\sqrt{1+n^{-1}}e_{i}-n^{-3/2}(\sqrt{n+1}-1)\mathbf{1},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,\quad c_{n+1}=-n^{-1/2}\mathbf{1}.

Each matrix is formed by taking
n
of the
n+1
vectors above as its columns.

Bounding.

Each subregion of the sphere takes the form
\mathcal{Q}=\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V\}\cap\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
for some invertible
matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. We compute a lower bound on
p
over
\mathcal{Q}
by solving

\phi(V)\coloneq\begin{array}[t]{cl}\max\limits_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}&\gamma\\
\text{s.t.}&p(V(x.^{2}))-\gamma\|V(x.^{2})\|_{2}^{d}\in\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n,2d}.\end{array}

(26)

Note that any feasible
\gamma
in problem (
26
) is a valid lower bound on
p
over
\mathcal{Q}
.
Indeed, any point

can be written as
z=V(x.^{2})
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so the constraint in (
26

p(z)-\gamma\|z\|_{2}^{d}\geq 0
for all
z\in\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V\}
. In particular,
p(z)-\gamma\geq 0
for all
z\in\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V\}\cap\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

To obtain an upper bound on
p
over
\mathcal{Q}
, we run
K
projected gradient descent steps (where
K
is chosen by the user): starting from a column of
V
, we iterate

y^{k}=\Pi_{\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V\}}(x^{k}-\beta\nabla p(x^{k})),\quad x^{k+1}=\frac{y^{k}}{\|y^{k}\|_{2}},\quad k=0,\ldots,K-1,

(27)

where
\Pi_{\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V\}}
denotes the projection onto
\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V\}
and
\beta>0
is a chosen stepsize.

Branching.

At each iteration, we select a subregion

\mathcal{Q}=\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V\}\cap\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

with the smallest lower bound, where
V=[v_{1}\,\ldots\,v_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is some invertible matrix. Letting
(i,j)\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{1\leq k,l\leq n}\|v_{k}-v_{l}\|_{2}
, we
set
w=(v_{i}+v_{j})/\|v_{i}+v_{j}\|_{2}
and form two further subregions

\mathcal{Q}^{+}=\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V^{+}\}\cap\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\quad\mathcal{Q}^{-}=\operatornamewithlimits{cone}\{V^{-}\}\cap\mathbb{S}^{n-1},

where
V^{+}=[v_{1}\,\ldots\,v_{i-1}\,v_{i+1}\,\ldots\,v_{n}\,w]
and
V^{-}=[v_{1}\,\ldots\,v_{j-1}\,v_{j+1}\,\ldots\,v_{n}\,w]
.

6.2
A SBB Framework for Matrix Copositivity

In this section, we focus on the problem of testing copositivity of a matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, which reduces to computing the minimum value
p_{\min}
of a quadratic form
p(x)=x^{T}Qx
over the unit simplex
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Delta^{n-1}}
.
We develop a branch-and-bound algorithm analogous to Algorithm
2
, tailored to the unit simplex. This algorithm can be used more generally to solve copositive programs. We initialize the algorithm with a single subregion, the unit simplex
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Delta^{n-1}}=\operatornamewithlimits{conv}\{I_{n}\}
, corresponding to the simplicial disjunction
\{I_{n}\}
.

and
\mathcal{Q}^{-}
.

Bounding.

Each subregion of the unit simplex takes the form
\mathcal{Q}=\operatornamewithlimits{conv}\{V\}
for some invertible
matrix
V~=~[v_{1}\,\ldots\,v_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Note that the minimum of
p
over
\mathcal{Q}
equals the optimal value of

\begin{array}[]{cl}\max\limits_{t\in\mathbb{R}}&t\\
\text{s.t.}&V^{T}(Q-tJ_{n})V\in\mathcal{C}_{n},\end{array}

&N\geq 0.\end{array}

(28)

over
\mathcal{Q}

(29)

Here,
\Pi_{\operatornamewithlimits{conv}\{V\}}
denotes the projection onto
\operatornamewithlimits{conv}\{V\}

and
\beta>0
is the stepsize.

Branching.

At each iteration, we select a subregion with the smallest lower bound and split it by bisecting its longest edge. More specifically, if

\mathcal{Q}=\operatornamewithlimits{conv}\{V\}

denotes the selected subregion, where
V=[v_{1}\,\ldots\,v_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is some invertible matrix, we let
(i,j)\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{1\leq k,l\leq n}\|v_{k}-v_{l}\|_{2}
, and set
w=(v_{i}+v_{j})/2
, forming two further subregions

\mathcal{Q}^{+}=\operatornamewithlimits{conv}\{V^{+}\},\quad\mathcal{Q}^{-}=\operatornamewithlimits{conv}\{V^{-}\},

where
V^{+}=[v_{1}\,\ldots\,v_{i-1}\,v_{i+1}\,\ldots\,v_{n}\,w]
and
V^{-}=[v_{1}\,\ldots\,v_{j-1}\,v_{j+1}\,\ldots\,v_{n}\,w]
.

Computing the clique number of a graph.

A clique in a graph is a set of pairwise adjacent vertices. The clique number of a graph
G
, denoted by
\omega(G)
, is the size of a maximum
clique in
G
.
By a theorem of Motzkin and Straus
[
36
]
, we have

\frac{1}{\omega(G)}=\min\limits_{x\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Delta^{n-1}}}x^{T}(I_{n}+\bar{A})x,

where
\bar{A}
is the adjacency matrix of the complement graph
\bar{G}
; i.e., the graph obtained by swapping the edges and non-edges of
G
. Equivalently, the clique number can be written as the optimal value of the copositive program

\displaystyle\omega(G)=

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\ k

(30)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\ k(I_{n}+\bar{A})-J_{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}.

To estimate the clique number by Algorithm
3
, at each node of the branch-and-bound tree, we perform 10 projected gradient descent steps in (
29
) with
Q=I_{n}+\bar{A}
to obtain a lower bound on
\omega(G)
, and apply our inner approximation of
\mathcal{C}_{n}
to (
30
) to obtain an upper bound on
\omega(G)
.
Since the clique number is an integer, the algorithm terminates when the ceiled lower bound and the floored upper bound coincide.

Conclusions and Future Directions

In this paper, we introduced the disjunctive sum of squares method for certifying nonnegativity of polynomials.
Unlike the classical sum of squares approach, which certifies nonnegativity via a single algebraic identity, our method constructs multiple algebraic identities, which together certify nonnegativity.
We showed that by doing so, the degrees of the sum of squares polynomials involved in the proof (and hence the size of the underlying semidefinite constraints) can be kept low and fixed. We proposed algorithms that can search for disjunctive proofs of nonnegativity, and studied extensions of our method to copositive programs and polynomial optimization problems.

This work leaves several interesting directions for future research. While we proposed a general reduction from constrained polynomial optimization problems to unconstrained ones in Section
5.1
, future work could tailor the design of algebraic disjunctions to the geometry of specific feasible sets (as we did, e.g., in the case of the nonnegative orthant in Section
5.2
).
Another interesting direction is to develop more efficient implementations of the disjunctive sum of squares approach that in addition to utilizing spatial branch-and-bound (cf. Section
6
), potentially exploit the natural parallelism across subregions.
Along these lines, it would be interesting to combine the disjunctive sum of squares method with techniques for exploiting sparsity
[
54
,
56
,
55
]
and symmetry
[
20
,
46
]
in sum of squares programs. Another avenue to study is the dual side of our framework through the theory of moments
[
28
,
27
]
, which has proven useful for extracting solutions in polynomial optimization problems
[
24
]
.
Finally, a promising direction is the design of instance-specific algebraic disjunctions that require fewer subregions than the universal constructions provided in this paper (cf. Theorems
1
and
8
). The branch-and-bound framework of Section
6
constructs such disjunctions adaptively, but there could be techniques that in addition exploit algebraic properties of the given problem instance.

We end with an open problem of theoretical interest: does there exist a nonnegative form that admits no disjunctive sum of squares proof of nonnegativity with any number of identities? This question has two natural variants: one where the degree of the sum of squares proof is required to be the same as that of the given form, and one where it is allowed to be larger. By Theorem
1
, any such form must have a zero.
A natural candidate would be a polynomial with a “bad point”; see, e.g.,
[
45
,
5
]
and references therein.
An example of such a polynomial is Delzell’s polynomial
D
(see
[
16
]
)

D(x_{1},\ldots,x_{4})=x_{1}^{4}x_{2}^{2}x_{4}^{2}+x_{2}^{4}x_{3}^{2}x_{4}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}x_{3}^{4}x_{4}^{2}-3x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}x_{3}^{2}x_{4}^{2}+x_{3}^{8},

which was also a test case for us in Section
6.3.1
. It is known that while
D
is nonnegative,
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{4}x_{i}^{2}\right)^{k}D(x_{1},\ldots,x_{4})
is not sos for any integer
k\geq 0
, which implies that the hierarchy of SDPs based on Reznick’s Positivstellensatz
[
43
]
cannot certify nonnegativity of
D
.
It turns out, however, that the disjunctive sum of squares approach can produce a low-degree proof of nonnegativity of
D
only with two subregions:

\displaystyle D(x_{1},\ldots,x_{4})

\displaystyle=(x_{1}^{2}x_{2}x_{4}-x_{1}x_{3}^{2}x_{4})^{2}+(x_{2}^{2}x_{3}x_{4}-x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4})^{2}+(x_{3}^{4})^{2}+2x_{1}x_{2}(x_{1}x_{3}x_{4}-x_{2}x_{3}x_{4})^{2}

\displaystyle=(x_{1}^{2}x_{2}x_{4}+x_{1}x_{3}^{2}x_{4})^{2}+(x_{2}^{2}x_{3}x_{4}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4})^{2}+(x_{3}^{4})^{2}-2x_{1}x_{2}(x_{1}x_{3}x_{4}+x_{2}x_{3}x_{4})^{2}.
