Antenatal depression and associated obstetric and psychosocial factors among pregnant women attending the public health clinics in Malaysia
Authors: Siti Sabrina Kamarudin, Nor Asiah Muhamad, Nur Hasnah Maamor, Noor Harzana Harrun, Tengku Puteri Nadiah Tengku Baharudin Shah, Nik Athirah Farhana Nik Azhan, Nurul Hidayah Jamalluddin, ‘Izzah ‘Athirah Rosli, Fatin Norhasny Leman, Nur Atirah Syahira Rosli, Nur Safawati Abd Manab, Norhafizah Mohd Noor, Norli Abdul Jabbar, Nurashikin Ibrahim, The My Perinatal Depression Team
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntenatal depression is a common but under reported complication of pregnancy and childbirth, particularly in low and middle-income settings. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of antenatal depressive symptoms and identify associated obstetric and psychosocial factors among antenatal women attending public health clinics.MethodsA multi-centre cross-sectional study was conducted in public health clinics. Participants completed self-administered questionnaire on sociodemographic characteristics, obstetric history and psychosocial factors. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) with a score ≥ 12 was used to indicate probable antenatal depression. Data were analysed using R Software version 4.5.2.ResultsA total of 572 women were included in the analysis, and prevalence of antenatal depression was 9.6%. In the multiple logistic regression adjusted for age, education, race and household income, antenatal depression was independently associated with unplanned pregnancy ( aOR 2.20, 95% CI 1.03 – 5.02), exposure to stressful event ( aOR 2.99, 95% CI 1.42 – 6.27), present of depressive symptoms in the past two weeks ( aOR 8.25, 95% CI 3.47 – 19.94) and self-perceived present of high worries ( aOR 4.56, 95% CI 1.84 – 13.18). Perceived maternal emotional support during upbringing was protective ( aOR 0.16, 95% CI 0.05 – 0.53).ConclusionAround one in ten women were screened positive for probable antenatal depression which was associated with unplanned pregnancy, stressful life events, recent depressive symptoms and high worry, while maternal emotional support from upbringing was protective. Finding support routine brief mental health screening practices that also considers other symptoms such as anxiety to improve case-finding and guide targeted support for women at higher risk, alongside interventions that build supportive relationships and break intergenerational cycles of distress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09573-5.Keywords: Depression, Antenatal Depression, Pregnancy, Mental Health

Introduction

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual Disorders (Fifth Edition), uses the specifier “with peripartum onset” to describe depressive episodes in which mood symptoms begins during pregnancy or within four weeks after delivery [1]. In broader obstetric and public health literature, including guidance from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists, the term perinatal depression is commonly used to describe depressive symptoms occurring during pregnancy or within the first year after childbirth [2, 3]. It is characterized by persistent low mood, loss of interest, and impaired functioning that could affect the mother, the future development of the child and the risk of suicide [4, 5]. Globally, the pooled estimates suggest that about one in four women experience a depressive episode during the perinatal period, with recent meta-analyses reporting antenatal depression prevalence 28% and postnatal depression around 27%, although estimates vary depending on setting and measurement tools [6]. The burden is consistently reported as significant in lower to middle income countries where prevalence commonly falls in the 15–20% range [7].

In Malaysia, awareness about perinatal mental health has grown and available evidence suggest a meaningful burden. A recent meta-analysis reported a pooled perinatal depression prevalence of 12.7%, with higher prevalence of antenatal depression at about 17% compared to postnatal depression of around 10% [8]. The 2022 national survey data also showed postnatal depression affecting approximately one in ten women [9]. However, estimates and associated factors differ across populations, settings, and measurement tools. Much of the available evidence is either older, drawn from single facilities, or centred mainly on prevalence, with less emphasis on an integrated set of clinically useful correlates that can guide care in everyday practice.

Clinic-based antenatal studies from different regions report comparable or higher prevalence, including 20% among women attending public antenatal clinics in the state of Penang [10], 13.8% in a large cohort of pregnant women in the state of Sabah [11] and 30.2% in late pregnancy in the state of Kelantan [12]. Estimates from perinatal psychiatric clinic settings in both the east and west coasts of Peninsular Malaysia, including Selangor, are around 12% [13, 14].

Furthermore, routine case-finding in the public health clinics often begins with very brief tools such as the 2-item Whooley questions or Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) [15]. Those are efficient but may not fully reflect psychosocial vulnerability, including prominent anxiety and worry symptoms that commonly co-occur during pregnancy [16]. Psychosocial stressors and support structures vary across communities and can shift over time with relationship dynamic, changing family arrangements, economic pressures and differing cultural and religious understandings [17–19]. This may alter risk profiles even when overall prevalence appears similar.

Given that routine antenatal care in Malaysia is delivered through the public health clinics, locally derived clinic-based estimates and identification of risk markers of antenatal depression are needed to guide local screening and intervention strategies particularly in high-volume settings such as the state of Selangor [20]. Therefore, this study aims to determine the prevalence of antenatal depressive symptoms and determine its associated obstetric and psychosocial factors among pregnant women attending public health clinics in Selangor, Malaysia.

Methods

Ethical approval and funding

Ethical approval for the study was obtained by the Medical Research Ethics Committee (MREC) (NMRR ID-24–01019-GZX Reference No:24–01019-GZX dated 12 June 2024). This study was funded by the Ministry of Health Malaysia through its administrative budget. No specific grant number was assigned. The funder had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, interpretation of findings, decision to publish, or preparation of the manuscript.

Study population

We conducted a multi-centre cross-sectional study among antenatal women attending the public health clinics in Selangor, Malaysia, from 1 st February 2025 to 31 st September 2025. Selangor is a highly urbanized state in Peninsular Malaysia comprising nine districts (Gombak, Hulu Langat, Hulu Selangor, Klang, Kuala Langat, Kuala Selangor, Petaling, Sabak Bernam and Sepang) and is the most populous state in the country. The sampling frame consisted of pregnant women attending government health clinics in Selangor during the study period. There are 85 government health clinics across the nine districts. One clinic per district was selected using simple random sampling which yielded nine participating clinics.

Sample size

The sample size was determined based on expected antenatal depression prevalence of 20% [12], a precision of 0.05, a 95% confidence level and a 20% allowance for non-response, the minimum required sample size for estimating prevalence was approximately 308. To account for the clustered sampling design, this figure was further adjusted using a design effect of 1.5. This resulted in a final minimum required sample size of approximately 500 participants.

Study participants

Participants were recruited via systematic random sampling. At each participating clinic, a random starting point was selected from the first two eligible antenatal attendees, after which every second eligible woman were approached until the target sample size was achieved. Participants were included if they were aged 18 years or older, able to communicate in the Malay or English and on antenatal follow-up. Women identified by medical staff as medically or psychologically unfit to take part were not recruited into the study. Medical Unfit refers to impaired consciousness and is defined by the Glasgow Coma Scale score of less than 15. Psychologically unfit refers to present of acute psychiatric symptom at the time of recruitment which include psychosis, delusional symptoms, severe agitation, disorientation and inability to coherently communicate based on clinical judgement of the medical staff. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

Measures

Questionnaire administration

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire, completed with guidance from trained research team members. The questionnaire comprised study-specific sociodemographic and obstetric items, psychosocial items adapted from the published Antenatal Risk Questionnaire (ANRQ) [21], and the published EPDS [22–24] to assess the depressive symptoms (See Supplemental Material Questionnaire).

Obstetric characteristics

Obstetric and pregnancy-related characteristics included parity, number of living children, pregnancy intention (planned or unplanned), trimester at enrolment, the presence of pregnancy complications, and perceived support from the husband during pregnancy. All items were collected as dichotomous items (Yes/No), with responses reported as frequencies and column percentages.

Assessment of psychosocial factors

Psychosocial factors were assessed using items adapted from the published Antenatal Risk Questionnaire (ANRQ) [21] developed by Austin et al. 2013, to assess early and chronic psychosocial stressors that are associated with increased risk for perinatal mental health problems. Adapted items covered depressive symptoms lasting two weeks or longer, history of other mental health problems, perceived emotional support from partner, stressful events or losses in the past 12 months, tendency to worry, distress when lacking order in daily life, perceived availability of practical support with infant care, history of emotional abuse during upbringing, and perceived maternal emotional support during upbringing. The ANRQ abuse item was operationalised as two separate questions on sexual abuse and physical abuse for reporting and analysis. The items were translated into Malay using forward translation and back-translation by bilingual members of the research team. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus to ensure conceptual equivalence and cultural appropriateness. The pre-final Malay version was pilot-tested among pregnant women in Malaysia to assess clarity and acceptability, and minor wording refinements were made before the main study was conducted.

Likert-type responses (for example, from “not at all” to “very much”) were dichotomised for analysis for example: “No” for “not at all” and “Yes” for any response from “a little” to “very much”. Items were analysed as individual psychosocial risk indicators rather than scored as the original ANRQ summary measure. Dichotomisation also reduced sparse cell counts across multiple response categories and improved the stability of logistic regression estimates, particularly given the relatively small number of participants with probable antenatal depression.

Assessment of antenatal depression

Depression was assessed using the published Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) by Cox et al. [22]. It is a widely used screening tool for depressive symptoms during pregnancy and postpartum period. The EPDS is widely used internationally and has been translated and validated in various language including Malay with good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha approximately 0.77–0.86), concurrent validity with other depression measures, and an optimal cut-off score of 11.5 with sensitivity 72.7% and specificity 92.6% for detecting postnatal depression [23–25]. Participants rated how they had felt over the past week, and item scores were summed to give a total score (range 0–30). In this study, an EPDS score of ≥ 12 was used to indicate probable antenatal depression, and depression status was analysed as a binary outcome (Yes = EPDS ≥ 12; No = EPDS < 12).

Analysis

Data were analysed using R software version 4.5.2. Participant characteristics and factors were summarised using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Antenatal depression was defined as EPDS score ≥ 12. Bivariable (unadjusted) associations between antenatal depression status and factors were examined using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Variables for multivariable modelling were selected based on bivariable significance at p < 0.05.

Multivariable logistic regression was then performed using a forced-entry (enter) approach to identify independent factors associated with antenatal depression. A priori confounders were included in all models (age, education, race, and household income). Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% Cis) were reported, and Wald z statistics. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Complete-case analysis was applied for the variables included in the final model (n = 572; depression cases = 55). Model fit was assessed using Akaike information criterion (AIC) and McFadden’s pseudo-R2. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the ROC curve (AUC, with 95% CI) and calibration using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (g = 10). Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values > 10 considered suggestive of problematic collinearity.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted using stepwise model selection based on AIC, with the same complete-case dataset and outcome definition. Forward and backward stepwise procedures were run within a pre-specified scope, forcing the same confounders (age, education, race, household income) to remain in the model. The selected predictor sets and estimates from forward and backward approaches were compared to assess robustness of the final model specification.

Result

A total of 614 patients were approached and 572 responded, giving a response rate of 93.16%. Table 1 summarizes the patients sociodemographic characteristic. Overall, a total of 572 women were included in the analysis, of whom 55 (9.6%) had clinical symptoms of antenatal depression and 517 (90.4%) did not. Just over half were aged above 30 years old (53.1%), and almost all were married (99.5%). The majority of women and their husbands had attained higher education, were employed, and reported a higher household income of more than RM3,000 (≈ US$670 in Jan 2025). Most participants were Malay, with smaller proportions of Chinese, Indian and other ethnicities. Third trimester participants made up almost half of the participants (49.1%).

There was no statistically significant difference found between the groups in the gestational age category, history of miscarriage, number of children, and presence of pregnancy complications (all p > 0.05). In contrast, psychosocial exposures were reportedly significant. Women with antenatal depression had more frequently reported at least one stressful event (58.2% vs 19.1%, p < 0.001) and lack of support from their in-laws (36.4% vs 17.2%, p < 0.001). They were also more likely to endorse symptoms of low mood in the past two weeks (40.0% vs 5.8%, p < 0.001), major stressors/changes/losses in the past 12 months (18.2% vs 9.1%, p = 0.032), identifying themselves as a worrier (89.1% vs 54.0%, p < 0.001), and a history of emotional abuse growing up (18.2% vs 3.3%, p < 0.001).

In the multiple logistic regression (Table 3) adjusted for age, education, race and household income, several factors remained independently associated with antenatal depression (EPDS ≥ 12). Women with unplanned pregnancy had higher odds of antenatal depression compared to those with planned pregnancy (adjusted OR 2.20, 95% CI 1.03–5.02, p = 0.048). Those reporting at least 1 or more stressful life event were also associated with higher odds of depression (aOR 2.99, 95% CI 1.42–6.27), p = 0.004).

Among the psychosocial factors, women who has prior symptoms of depression within the past 2 weeks had the strongest association with antenatal depression (aOR 8.25, 95% CI 3.47–19.94, p = < 0.001). Those who considered themselves a worrier were had a higher odd of antenatal depression (aOR 4.56, 94% CI 1.84–13.18, p = 0.002). In contrary, present of perceived maternal emotional support during upbringing were found to be protective (aOR 0.16, 95% CI 0.05–0.53, p = 0.002). The model was deemed acceptable with AIC = 282.10 and McFadden R2 = 0.298. Good discrimination of AUC – 0.853, 95% CI 0.797–0.909, adequate calibration with Hosemer-Lemeshow χ2 (8) = 8.50, p = 0.386) and acceptable multicollinearity of VIF ranging from 1.07–1.54. To explore the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses using backward and forward regression (AIC) also produced a similar pattern of findings (See Supplemental Material Table 4). Similar key associated factors remained which are unplanned pregnancy, stressful events, present of depressive symptoms, worries and emotional support during upbringing. Effect estimates were comparable in magnitude and direction thereby supporting the robustness of the main model.

Discussion

The prevalence of probable antenatal depression of 9% among the antenatal women attending government clinic in Selangor is comparable to studies by Elias & Sulaiman, 2020 & Nasreen et al., 2018 and of the recent national morbidity and health survey 2022 [9]. However, it is lower than that reported prevalence among the antenatal population in the Malaysia states of Kelantan 30.2% [12] and Penang (20%) (Rashid & Mohd, 2017). Differences across Malaysian regions likely reflect variations in population characteristics and study methods, including income definitions, urban rural context, social support and stress exposures, and differences in screening tools and timing of assessment. Kelantan’s higher estimate may relate to greater socioeconomic disadvantage, while the relatively high prevalence in Penang suggests that factors beyond income, such as urban stressors and contextual pressures, may also play a role [26].

Unplanned pregnancy was a significant risk factor in our sample, with 2.2-fold higher odds of getting antenatal depression. In Malaysia, unintended pregnancies may be partly driven by gaps in reproductive health services, including unmet need for family planning. The National Health Morbidity Survey 2022 reported an unmet need for family planning is at 26.7% which may be contribute to reduced pregnancy preparedness and greater psychosocial strain when pregnancies occur unexpectedly [27]. Therefore, unplanned pregnancy may act as a marker which heightens vulnerability through pathways such as financial pressure, relationship stress, or reduced perceived control during pregnancy.

Younger maternal age was associated with antenatal depression in our univariate analysis. Younger women may face greater reproductive and social vulnerability, including higher unmet need for contraception compared to older age groups, which an contribute to unplanned pregnancy [28]. This aligns with evidence that young motherhood is often marked by a cluster of vulnerabilities, including lower educational attainment, low income or welfare dependency, limited social support, less developed parenting skills, higher parenting stress and a greater burden of mental health problems [29, 30]. In our study, experiencing at least one stressful life event was independently associated with almost three-fold higher odds of antenatal depression. This further supports the need for routine antenatal assessment of current stressors, alongside clear pathways for counselling, social welfare support, and safeguarding where relevant particularly for the younger age group.

Notably, the impact of stressful life events on antenatal depression may be buffered or even exacerbated by the availability of supportive relationships. In our study, having maternal emotional support while growing up were protective in developing antenatal depression. Evidence have shown that women who report emotional neglect, low maternal warmth or other forms of childhood adversity are more likely to experience antenatal and postpartum depression, even after adjusting for current socio-economic status, with proposed mechanisms including insecure attachment, altered stress responses and difficulties in emotion regulation [31]. These findings support the idea of a life-course and intergenerational lens for perinatal mental health. Screening that captures current stressors and support, alongside brief indicators of earlier adversity, may help identify women who would benefit from more proactive psychosocial support and referral pathways during antenatal care [32].

Two brief psychosocial indicators in our study were strongly associated with antenatal depression. Women reporting depression symptom in the past two weeks had markedly higher odds of antenatal depression even after adjustment. This suggests that a simple symptoms screen may help identify those with ongoing or recurrent vulnerability that becomes more apparent as pregnancy-related demands increase. In addition, women who described themselves as a “worrier” had substantially higher odds of antenatal depression. This aligns with evidence that antenatal depression frequently overlaps with anxiety-related vulnerability, where traits worry and heightened stress reactivity have been linked with depressive symptoms [33].

While very brief depression screens are useful for routine antenatal care, tools such as two Whooley Questions or PHQ-2 mainly focus on low mood and anhedonia and do not explicitly probe persistent worry or anxiety-related traits. In our study, additional worry item may therefore add value by flagging women whose distress is expressed more through anxious rumination rather than classic depressive symptoms alone. This may be of particular value in contexts where mixed anxiety and depression presentations are common and where a brief add-on question could improve case-findings without substantially increasing burden on staff. This is important as evidence have shown that anxious self-expectations and perceived pressure around motherhood have been described as prominent concerns among local pregnant women and may offer practical entry-point for early support [34].

Our findings suggest that antenatal depression clusters around pregnancy related vulnerability and psychosocial strain, particularly unplanned pregnancy and exposure to stressful life events. Relationship factors and early life emotional support potentially may shape resilience and how symptoms developed. These patterns matter because many of these stressors do not resolve at delivery. For some women they intensify during the postpartum period when sleep disruption, caregiving demands, financial strain, and relationship pressures become more immediate and lack of positive coping mechanism will leave these women vulnerable [34]. In this context, anxiety and maladaptive emotion regulation or coping may amplify distress and sustain symptoms, supporting the value of considering anxiety alongside depression during assessment and follow-up [35].

In Malaysia, initiatives such as the TIARA MURNI project demonstrate that perinatal mental health support can be delivered through health care workers, but this approach still depends heavily on staffing capacity and service availability [34, 36]. At the same time, women’s mental health support does not have to sit only in antenatal and postnatal clinics. There is real room to extend prevention and early support through broader touchpoints such as LPPKN services, community-based programmes, education system, workplace and faith-based networks, and adolescent and preconception platforms where relationship stress, contraception counselling, and psychosocial strain often surface earlier. Alongside this, there remains a notable gap in pushing for digital mental health solutions for postpartum mothers, particularly in Asian settings where resources and access may be uneven. Therefore, this highlights a practical opportunity for more scalable, low-intensity digital interventions that can complement routine care by offering flexible access, privacy, and continuity of support [37, 38].

Limitation

This study was conducted in multi-centre public health clinics with relatively large. We used validated screening tool for depression (EPDS), together with a structured questionnaire covering socio-demographic, obstetric and psychosocial factors. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design prevents any firm conclusions about causality or the temporal sequence between risk factors and depression. Antenatal depression was identified using a screening tool rather than clinical diagnostic interviews, so some misclassification is possible. All exposures were self-reported and may be affected by recall or social desirability bias, particularly for sensitive issues such as relationship difficulties or past emotional neglect. Some Likert-type psychosocial items were dichotomised to indicate whether each risk indicator was present or absent. While this improved interpretability and model stability, it may have reduced response variability and limited assessment of dose–response relationships with antenatal depression. Residual confounding is still possible and relevant factors were measured using brief single items. Finally, the sample was restricted to women who attended public health clinics and may not fully represent those seeking care in the private clinics or women who do not attend antenatal or postnatal services. Hence, generalisability to more remote or marginalised populations is therefore limited.

Conclusion

Probable antenatal depression was identified in around one in ten pregnant women attending public antenatal clinics, with prevalence comparable to other Malaysian and regional studies. Unplanned pregnancy, stressful life events, current depressive symptoms, high worry, and limited emotional support in childhood were associated with higher odds of antenatal depression. These findings support routine antenatal mental health screening in primary care, with attention to both depressive symptoms and anxiety. This should be supported by community-based and educational pathways for early identification and timely psychosocial support, particularly for younger women and those experiencing high stress or long-standing relationship difficulties, within a life-course approach.
