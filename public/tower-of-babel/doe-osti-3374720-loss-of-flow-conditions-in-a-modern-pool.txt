 Loss of Flow Conditions in a Modern Pool-type SFR and In-Pile
 Experiment Design

 SUMMARY
 The metallic fuel safety performance under unprotected design-basis
transients is a key consideration for the deployment of advanced sodium
fast reactors (SFRs). Reliable data are needed to validate advanced safety
codes, reduce uncertainty in cladding failure thresholds, and strengthen
confidence in licensing approaches. To address this need, this report
develops blueprints for a conceptual sodium loss-of-flow (LOF)
experiment in the Mk-IIIR loop at the Transient Reactor Test Facility
(TREAT), providing the technical foundation for future integral testing.
 The PRISM MOD B reactor system was adopted as a reference
configuration, evaluated in both its original U-19Pu-10Zr form and a
modified U-10Zr variant. Bounding analyses using SAS4A/SASSYS-1
and RELAP5-3D were performed to characterize steady-state and
unprotected transient behavior. Results for bounding fuel pins indicated
cladding loading and thermal excursions, providing a technical basis for
defining experimental boundary conditions. Analyses of Unprotected
Transient OverPower (UTOP) and Unprotected Station Blackout (USBO)
scenarios highlighted the role of passive feedbacks in stabilizing reactor
power and confirmed that, even when uncertainties are considered, coolant
temperatures remain below sodium boiling.
 Building on these system-level analyses, a preliminary test matrix was
assembled to span power histories and flow coast-downs consistent with
plant relevant LOF and flow-blockage events. Independent optimization
studies with EBR-II X430 fuel pins identified combinations of power and
flow that reproduce the desired cladding temperature histories within the
TREAT energy limits. Preliminary SAS4A/SASSYS-1 and RELAP5-3D
simulations produced consistent cladding temperature predictions
reinforcing confidence in the feasibility of the blueprints.
 This integrated effort establishes a pathway from reference reactor
analysis to in-pile experiment definition. The resulting blueprints provide
the groundwork for TREAT LOF testing that will generate high-value
validation data to reduce uncertainties in metallic fuel performance models
and provide critical support for the safety case of advanced pool-type
SFRs.

 Loss of Flow Conditions in a Modern Pool-type SFR and In-Pile
 Experiment Design

 1. Introduction and Purpose
 Metallic fueled sodium-cooled fast reactors (SFR) are inherently well-suited to achieve passive safety
strategies and removes any credible pathway for severe accident conditions such as hypothetical core
disruptive accidents (HCDA). Significant RD&D has been achieved to prove this statement. Still,
transient behaviors are still design dependent and individual designs must demonstrate their performance.
With the restart of the Transient Reactor Test Facility (TREAT) and revival accompanying sodium test
devices, a significant opportunity exists today to bolster the metallic-fueled SFR safety case by reducing
uncertainties related to relevant accident events.
 The purpose of this document is to establish relevant conditions for an experimental design for testing
the most limiting design basis conditions relevant to fuel/core performance.
 1.1. SFR accident conditions and relevance
 SFRs are advanced reactor systems that rely on passive safety features and inherent reactivity
feedback mechanisms to achieve stable behavior under a wide range of operating and off-normal
conditions. Their use of liquid sodium as coolant allows for low operating pressure, high thermal
conductivity, and strong natural circulation potential, all of which contribute to their resilience during
transient scenarios. With proper design, a metallic-fueled SFR can achieve large safety margins and the
magnitude of thermal excursions is dampened such that design basis accidents may have minimal to no
meaningful damage to the fuel.
 Nevertheless, the safety evaluation of SFRs requires detailed understanding of system response under
conditions that may challenge these inherent safety features. Accident scenarios such as loss of flow, loss
of heat sink, or transient overpower can result in significant fuel and cladding temperature excursions, all
dependent on specific design considerations and specific accident severity. These conditions may lead to
cladding strain, chemical interaction between fuel and cladding, fission gas release, and in severe cases,
mechanical failure of the fuel pin.
 Accurate modeling of such phenomena is essential for confirming the reliability of SFR designs and
for meeting safety requirements under licensing frameworks. While system-level codes can simulate the
overall plant behavior, their predictive capability depends on the fidelity of models used to represent fuel
behavior at the pin level during transients. Experimental validation remains a key part of this effort,
particularly for anticipated transients without scram (ATWS) and other events that involve coupled
thermal, mechanical, and chemical processes in the core.
 1.2. Objective
 The objective of this work is to support the design of transient experiments in the TREAT facility that
can generate high-value validation data for U-(Pu)-Zr metallic fuel alloy with stainless-steel HT9
cladding behavior in SFRs under accident conditions. The effort focuses on identifying key transient
degradation mechanisms that occur during unprotected scenarios and designing experiments that can
meaningfully replicate those phenomena at the single-pin level.
 To this end, a reference SFR core was evaluated under representative ATWS conditions using the
SAS4A/SASSYS-1 [1] and RELAP5-3D [2] codes. The analysis identified dominant thermal and
mechanical responses of the fuel and cladding, including conditions leading to significant strain, eutectic
interaction, or potential failure. These insights were used to guide the development of a TREAT flow loop
model that can reproduce the most relevant aspects of in-core thermal and hydraulic conditions.

 The resulting experiment design recommendations aim to support validation of transient fuel behavior
models by replicating key degradation pathways observed in system-level simulations. The proposed
configurations are optimized to isolate and study specific fuel pin failure modes, while remaining
compatible with TREAT’s operational capabilities and diagnostic systems.
 1.3. Current Understanding of LOF
 1.3.1. Basic description of ATWS events of primary interest
 ATWS events are those in which the reactor fails to shut down automatically following a disturbance,
due to malfunction of the reactor protection system. These events are considered BDBA due to the very
low probability of occurrence for double-fault events (~ <10-6/ reactor yr), with failure of two primary
safety systems. These scenarios are particularly important for advanced pool-type SFRs, where passive
reactivity feedback mechanisms and decay heat removal systems are expected to maintain safety without
operator action or active control. Furthermore, the positive void reactivity of most SFR designs has
traditionally driven to consider these beyond design basis accidents with higher rigor than BDBA in other
reactor systems due to the potential resulting risks of reaching such a condition. The predicted response of
a given SFR design under even low probability BDBA conditions is a crucial consideration for all
stakeholders. Many SFR designs, physical and conceptual, emphasizing metal-fueled designs, have
demonstrated excellent performance to mitigate some of these primary concerns.
 In ATWS scenarios, the core’s passive negative reactivity feedback mechanisms, such as Doppler
broadening, fuel axial expansion, and core radial expansion, act to varying degrees to counter power
excursions. Their promptness and strength are essential to mitigating transient progression.
 The primary ATWS events of interest are:
 • Unprotected Loss of Flow (ULOF): A scenario in which forced coolant flow is lost due to pump
 coast down or trip, and no reactor scram occurs. The reduction in flow results in a rapid rise in
 fuel and coolant temperatures. ULOF is of particular interest due to the potential for localized
 overheating and cladding degradation under stagnant or low-flow conditions.
 • Unprotected Loss of Heat Sink (ULOHS): In this event, the ability to reject heat through the
 intermediate or secondary systems is lost while primary flow continues. This causes a slower but
 sustained increase in system temperature. Although the temperature evolution is more gradual
 than in ULOF, failure to remove decay heat can still lead to eutectic formation between fuel and
 cladding, cladding ballooning, flow blockages, and creep-rupture of the cladding.
 • Unprotected Station Blackout (USBO): A case involving total loss of offsite and onsite AC
 power, combined with failure to scram. Both forced cooling and heat rejection are lost, leaving
 decay heat removal to rely entirely on natural circulation, heat conduction, and structural heat
 capacity. USBO represents a limiting case that combines features of ULOF and ULOHS.
 • Unprotected Transient Overpower (UTOP): A scenario involving unintended positive reactivity
 insertion, such as uncontrolled control rod withdrawal or a fault in the reactivity control system.
 Without scram, the reactivity insertion leads to a significant power increase. Depending on the
 rate and magnitude of reactivity insertion, fuel temperature and mechanical loading can rise to the
 point of cladding strain or failure.
 • Unprotected Local Flow Blockage (ULFB): A scenario in which flow is obstructed in one or
 more subassemblies due to debris, oxide buildup, or structural deformation, and no scram is
 initiated. The resulting local flow reduction can lead to severe temperature rise in affected fuel
 pins, while neighboring subassemblies remain largely unaffected. ULFB challenges the ability of
 the system to mitigate highly localized overheating. This scenario is particularly relevant for
 studying localized cladding degradation mechanisms such as eutectic interaction, strain
 accumulation and creep rupture under asymmetric cooling conditions. If the fuel melting and

 13

 molten fuel relocation occur, it becomes essential to demonstrate the damage propagation is
 limited and safe shutdown remains viable.

 1.3.2. Experimental data
            1.3.2.1.     WPF
 The whole pin furnace (WPF) tests, developed at Argonne National Laboratory, were designed to
examine the behavior of full-length irradiated metallic fuel pins under slow transient conditions, such as
those representative of loss-of-flow (LOF) or loss-of-heat-sink (LOHS) events [3][4]. These experiments
address a critical gap between rapid transient overpower tests (e.g., in TREAT M-Series [5]) and lower-
temperature integral demonstrations (e.g., EBR-II SHRT [6]), by focusing on the progressive thermal
degradation of metallic fuel over timescales of minutes to hours.
 Each test was conducted in an evacuated, inert environment using a programmable infrared furnace
capable of reaching and maintaining controlled temperature profiles. Irradiated U-Zr and U-Pu-Zr pins
from EBR-II were enclosed in sealed test chambers instrumented with thermocouples, and monitored for
pressure increases associated with cladding breach. The design allowed for investigation of integral pin
behavior while isolating key thermomechanical and chemical effects in the absence of coolant or neutron
flux.
 Key phenomena investigated in the WPF tests included:
 • Eutectic interaction between fuel and cladding, particularly the formation of low-melting
 intermetallic phases at the fuel/HT9 steel interface
 • Cladding wastage and thinning due to localized chemical attack initiated at elevated
 temperatures
 • Thermal creep and strain accumulation in the cladding, driven by internal fission gas pressure
 • Creep rupture and breach onset
 • Fission gas behavior, including bubble coarsening, morphology development, and
 redistribution within the fuel
 While the WPF tests did not simulate fuel relocation or mechanical disruption, they provided essential
data on the progression of cladding degradation under slow transients. Post-test examination further
enabled detailed assessment of damage modes and fission gas evolution.
 These results are valuable for informing and validating mechanistic models of fuel failure used in
advanced fast reactor safety codes. The WPF data have contributed to a more complete understanding of
metallic fuel performance and margin to failure under off-normal conditions in sodium-cooled fast
reactors.
            1.3.2.2.     EBR-II SHRT
 The Shutdown Heat Removal Tests (SHRT) conducted in the Experimental Breeder Reactor II (EBR-
II) between 1984 and 1986 provide a valuable data set for validating system-level response of SFRs under
protected and unprotected transient conditions [6]. These tests were designed to demonstrate the
effectiveness of passive safety mechanisms and provide benchmark-quality data for safety code
validation.
 Among the SHRT series, two experiments stand out with detailed documentation and wide usage in
model validation: SHRT-17 and SHRT-45R. SHRT-17 was a protected loss-of-flow (PLOF) test in which
both primary and intermediate coolant pumps were tripped simultaneously with a full control rod scram.
It demonstrated the reactor’s capability to safely transition to decay heat removal via natural circulation.
SHRT-45R, in contrast, was an unprotected loss-of-flow (ULOF) test where the plant protection system

 14

was disabled to suppress automatic scram. This test showed the reactor’s ability to shut down passively
due to negative reactivity feedback mechanisms.
 The insights gained from SHRT-17 and SHRT-45R serve as a valuable precedent for the design and
interpretation of future transient experiments, such as those planned at the TREAT facility. In particular,
these historical tests provide critical validation anchors for evaluating passive safety behavior in proposed
advanced reactor concepts. They must also still be considered in the context of code validation supporting
evaluation of different reactor designs.

        1.3.3. Data Needs
 Accurate prediction of fuel pin behavior during unprotected transients is critical for evaluating the
safety performance of SFRs. While most metal fuel pins are expected to remain intact during anticipated
ATWS scenarios, bounding cases with degraded cooling or extended heat removal times can subject pins
to conditions near their failure thresholds. Understanding this margin to failure is essential for validating
safety models and ensuring robust design margins.
 The dominant cladding failure mechanism during slow transients with metallic fuel is creep rupture,
driven by elevated cladding temperature, increasing plenum pressure, and localized weakening from
eutectic interaction between fuel and cladding. While past experiments, such as the TREAT M-Series,
EBR-II SHRT, and WPF studies, have provided important benchmarks, they do not fully represent
transient conditions that match the heating rates and time scales of ATWS events, particularly with
irradiated metallic fuel.
 Uncertainties remain in material property data at high temperature, fission gas-driven deformation,
and the interplay between eutectic attack and cladding thermal creep. Experimental benchmarks under
realistic transient conditions are needed to constrain these uncertainties and improve the predictive
fidelity of safety codes such as SAS4A/SASSYS-1 [1] and RELAP5-3D [2].
 The key data needs include:
 • Transient experiments with irradiated metallic fuel under flow-limited conditions
 representative of ATWS
 • Reproduction of relevant time scales, thermal boundary conditions, and mechanical loading
 • Observation of degradation behaviors to failure such as cladding creep strain, creep rupture,
 ballooning, cladding wastage, fuel axial expansion, and molten fuel relocation
 • Evaluation of specific fuel design impacts such as prototypic length fuel pins, different fuel or
 cladding compositions, burnup > 10 at%, minor actinide-bearing fuel, etc.
 • Data to inform and validate creep rupture and eutectic interaction models in fuel performance
 codes such as MFUEL module of SAS4A/SASSYS-1 and BISON
 Addressing these data needs through carefully designed TREAT flow loop experiments will help
reduce model uncertainties, establish validated failure thresholds, and strengthen confidence in the
passive safety performance of advanced SFR designs. These experiments should be developed in concert
with other testing in the TREAT static sodium capsule and hot-cell furnace testing, each lacking the
loop’s more prototypic boundary condition.

 2. Approach
 The overall approach of this work is to evaluate representative transient scenarios in a reference SFR
core to inform the design of fuel behavior experiments in the TREAT facility. The study focuses on

 15

ATWS and similar slow-developing accident conditions that could lead to fuel pin degradation via
cladding creep rupture and fuel/cladding chemical interactions.
 To accomplish this, high-fidelity system analyses were conducted using two independent tools:
SAS4A/SASSYS-1 and RELAP5-3D. These codes were applied to simulate plant-scale thermal and
mechanical responses under bounding unprotected scenarios and to identify thermal histories and
mechanical loading conditions most relevant to individual fuel pin behavior. Use of two independent
codes and users is a strategic opportunity to better ensure quality results, as the development of the
models and results, provided frequent opportunity for dialogue and crosschecking assumptions and
results. Conveniently, the full plant model already existed for the SAS4A/SASSYS-1 code, while the
TREAT sodium loop has a detailed RELAP5-3D model. Each was used to develop a sister model in the
other code.
 The analysis results were used to extract enveloping conditions—such as heating rates, peak cladding
temperatures, and plenum pressure rise—that represent the most limiting fuel pin behavior without
assuming generalized core failure. These bounding fuel responses were then used to define realistic and
achievable test conditions for single-pin transient experiments in the TREAT facility.
 A TREAT flow loop model was developed and evaluated to determine the feasible range of thermal
and flow conditions that could be achieved in irradiation experiments. The objective was to ensure the
experiment design could replicate the key degradation mechanisms predicted in system-level simulations,
within the constraints of TREAT hardware, diagnostics, and safety limits.
 This integrated approach provides a direct link between full-core transient simulation, fuel pin
response modeling, and the design of targeted experiments that can deliver valuable validation data for
advanced SFR safety codes.

 2.1. Modeling Approach
        2.1.1. SAS4A/SASSYS-1/RELAP5-3D
 SAS4A/SASSYS-1
 SAS4A/SASSYS-1 (SAS) is an integrated system analysis code developed and maintained by
Argonne National Laboratory for the thermal-hydraulic and safety analysis of liquid-metal cooled fast
reactors [1]. It is designed to simulate a wide range of transient and accident scenarios, including both
design-basis and beyond-design-basis events.
 The code supports the following major capabilities:
• Assessment of safety margins during operational transients and anticipated events,
• Evaluation of severe accident consequences, including coolant boiling, fuel melting, and cladding
 failure,
• High-fidelity simulation of fuel behavior and cladding failures under normal and off-normal
 conditions,
• System-level modeling of the primary, intermediate, and balance-of-plant circuits, including pumps,
 heat exchangers, and control systems.
 SAS4A/SASSYS-1 models plant-wide dynamics while allowing for localized analysis of core
behavior using detailed multi-channel representations. Each thermal-hydraulic channel simulates an
individual fuel pin (or group of pins), its cladding, coolant, and a portion of the duct wall. Structural
features such as wire wraps or spacers are treated within the structural field. The model assumes one-
dimensional axial flow and two-dimensional radial-axial heat conduction in solid and fluid regions.

 16

Channels can be configured to represent average or peak power pins, control or blanket elements, or other
distinct zones in the core.
 The axial extent of each channel spans the entire subassembly length, including the active core, axial
blankets, fission gas plenum, and extension regions. Multiple channels are used to capture radial,
azimuthal, and burnup-dependent variations in power and coolant conditions across the core.
 Figure 1 provides a schematic of a representative pool-type SFR, illustrating the major components
modeled in SAS4A/SASSYS-1. The primary system includes the reactor core (S1), intermediate heat
exchangers (S2/S3), and primary sodium pumps. Heat is transferred from the core to the intermediate heat
transport system via the IHX, then to the decay heat removal system (e.g., DHX shown in S8). Control
volumes (CV1–CV5) are used to define key fluid regions in the core and surrounding structures, enabling
full-loop simulation of flow and temperature evolution during transient scenarios.

 Figure 1. Schematic of a representative pool type sodium fast reactor

 The SAS4A/SASSYS-1 code has been validated using a comprehensive set of separate effects tests
(SETs) and integral effects tests (IETs) from multiple experimental facilities. The validation matrix
includes only those experiments with well-documented conditions and corresponding SAS4A/SASSYS-1
analyses. Key facilities and test series include:
 • EBR-II: Balance of Plant (BOP), Shutdown Heat Removal Tests (SHRT), and Plant Inherent
 Control Tests (PICT) [6]
 • FFTF: Loss of Flow Without Scram (LOFWOS) and Cycle 8A reactivity feedback tests [7]
 • Phénix: Natural Circulation [8] and Dissymmetric [9] tests
 • TSTF: Thermal stratification tests [10]
 • UIUC: Single-phase natural circulation tests [11]
 • NCSHR (1980s): Natural convection shutdown heat removal tests [12]

 17

 • THETA: Thermal hydraulic stratification tests [13]
 These experiments span a broad range of phenomena critical to safety analysis, including natural
circulation, thermal stratification, and reactivity feedback, and provide high-confidence validation of the
code’s predictive capabilities.

 2.1.2. Fuel behavior – MFUEL
 MFUEL is the metallic fuel performance module integrated within SAS4A/SASSYS-1 [1][14][15]. It
provides a mechanistic, physics-based framework for simulating the behavior of metallic fuel pins during
both steady-state operation and transient scenarios. The model captures the coupled thermal, mechanical,
and chemical processes that influence fuel performance and safety margins, with specific emphasis on
fuel melting, cladding failure, and the associated reactivity feedback effects under accident conditions.
 Using user-defined inputs for operating history, initial fuel geometry, fuel composition, cladding type,
and the temperature distributions calculated by SAS, MFUEL simulates the time-dependent behavior of
both the fuel and cladding. As shown in Figure 2, the MFUEL driver manages a suite of interrelated
submodels that evaluate the evolution of key physical phenomena at each time step.
 The MFUEL framework determines thermo-mechanical behavior, fuel phase transitions,
redistribution of uranium, plutonium, and zirconium constituents, fission gas release and swelling,
formation of fuel porosity, and anisotropic axial growth of the fuel column. It also accounts for sodium
bond infiltration into the fuel porosity and its axial migration, along with cladding thermal and irradiation
creep. Once fuel-cladding contact occurs, additional models are activated to represent cladding wastage,
lanthanide and actinide diffusion into the cladding, iron migration from cladding to fuel, interfacial
mechanical stresses, and eutectic formation at the fuel-cladding interface. These interactions directly
influence fuel pin geometry, affect the local reactivity feedback, and impact coolant flow characteristics.
In scenarios involving eutectic formation and localized melting, MFUEL captures the experimentally
observed enhanced axial expansion of the fuel column.
 Figure 2 illustrates the integrated structure of MFUEL’s modeling approach. The processes enclosed
in oval shapes are handled by the core SAS models, while the rectangular elements are evaluated by the
MFUEL driver. This separation allows for efficient and accurate coordination between the fuel behavior
models and the larger plant simulation framework within SAS4A/SASSYS-1.
 MFUEL has been extensively validated against experimental data from fast reactor programs
including EBR-II, FFTF, and Phénix [15]. The validation portfolio encompasses a wide range of
operating conditions and fuel designs, with burnups between 1.9 and 19.3 at%, and plutonium contents of
0, 8, and 19 wt%. Cladding materials covered in the validation set include HT9, D9, 316SS, and 15-15Ti.
While EBR-II provided most of the data for short fuel columns, FFTF included full-length irradiated pins.
In contrast, the METAPHIX experiments conducted in the Phénix reactor introduced top-peaked axial
power profiles and long-length pins with 15-15Ti cladding, thereby adding further diversity to the
validation base. In addition to steady-state performance, MFUEL has also been validated against transient
experiments including the TREAT M-series and in-pile furnace tests [14]. These studies demonstrated the
model’s robustness in predicting fuel reactivity feedback, fission gas behavior, fuel pin dimensional
evolution, and the timing and location of cladding failure during transients.

 RELAP5-3D
 RELAP5-3D is system thermal-hydraulic code [2] developed and maintained by Idaho National
Laboratory (INL). It was initially developed for Light Water Reactor analysis. During the past years,
RELAP5-3D capabilities have been greatly augmented for modeling advanced nuclear systems, including
sodium fast reactors. Dedicated fluid property files are available for modeling nuclear systems using
sodium, sodium-potassium eutectic, lead, lead-bismuth as coolants. The thermal hydraulics of the main
circuits can be modeled with a significant level of detail, including three-dimensional (3D) modeling.
 Dedicated correlations allow the simulation of the convective heat exchange for wire-wrapped fuel
bundles and components in the liquid metals. Conduction, convection and radiation heat transfer can be
modeled via heat structures components. The neutronics can be simulated through a zero-dimensional
(0D) model using appropriate control variables which models the different reactivity coefficients of the
SFR. Since RELAP5-3D is not a severe-accident code, it does not have two-phase modeling capabilities
for the liquid metals; therefore, any liquid metal systems analysis should be terminated at the onset of the
coolant boiling to avoid numerical errors.

 3. Development of Reference Transient Conditions
 3.1. Selection of Representative SFR Design
 The selection of a representative sodium fast reactor (SFR) design is a critical step in defining
realistic and meaningful transient conditions for safety analysis and experimental planning. The selected
design must reflect modern design practices, incorporate credible operating parameters, and align with
fuel types and system characteristics that are relevant for current and future deployment scenarios of
advanced fast reactors. Furthermore, the chosen reactor must be compatible with existing modeling tools
and validation data and allow sufficient flexibility to examine the behavior of different fuel compositions.
 In this study, the Power Reactor Innovative Small Module (PRISM) Mod B design is adopted as the
reference SFR for transient simulations, as a publicly available representation of relevant modern SFR
designs [20][21][22]. PRISM Mod B is a pool-type sodium-cooled fast reactor concept that has been
extensively studied and modeled within Argonne National Laboratory. It features a thermal power of 840
MW and employs passive safety features enabled by the metal fuel characteristics and inherent reactivity
feedback mechanisms. The main features are given in Table 1. The reactor configuration includes a
compact core layout, internal heat exchangers, and primary pumps located within a sodium pool, making
it representative of modern fast reactor design principles.
 The original PRISM Mod B design incorporates high-plutonium-content metallic fuel, approximately
U-19Pu-10Zr, with fuel pins arranged in a tight lattice to support high power density and efficient
breeding characteristics. For this study, the core has been converted to a U-10Zr configuration. This
adaptation allows the analysis to extend to a broader class of SFRs, particularly in the context of fuel
cycle flexibility, supply chain considerations, and proliferation resistance. The U-10Zr core maintains key
features of metallic fuel performance while enabling a wider range of experimental relevance.
 The choice of PRISM Mod B provides a practical and well-characterized basis for transient safety
analysis using SAS4A/SASSYS-1 as provided in Refs [20][21][22] . Detailed core design data, thermal-
hydraulic configurations, and feedback coefficients are available from prior Argonne-developed models,
facilitating accurate simulation of accident conditions such as UTOP and ULOF/USBO events. This
foundation ensures consistency in core physics and system behavior modeling, while enabling systematic
exploration of bounding test conditions for future experimental campaigns at TREAT.

 3.2. Reference Plant Description
 3.2.1. Design Assumptions Impacting Reactivity Feedback
 The reference PRISM Mod B core model used in this study was initially developed at Argonne
National Laboratory and includes pre-defined reactivity feedback coefficients embedded within the
SAS4A/SASSYS-1 input files [21][22]. These feedback components represent core-level responses to
temperature-driven phenomena such as Doppler broadening, thermal expansion of fuel and structures, and
coolant density changes. The original model was based on a high-plutonium-content metallic fuel (U-
16Pu-10Zr inner core and U-22Pu-10Zr outer core), and associated reactivity feedback components
reflected the strong resonance absorption characteristics and thermal expansion behavior of that
composition.
 To enable analysis of reactor designs using U-10Zr fuel, the core was converted from the original
configuration using neutronics data derived from Versatile Test Reactor (VTR)-based studies [23]. The
updated composition resulted in modified reactivity feedback components, particularly in Doppler, fuel
axial expansion, core radial expansion and sodium void worth, which were applied by scaling or
substituting the appropriate coefficients in the SAS input. These updated values reflect the softer neutron
spectrum, reduced Pu content, and different expansion behavior of the U-10Zr system. Note that this
approach is rather approximate here and should be verified in future studies.
 The following reactivity feedback mechanisms were considered and either directly modeled or
adjusted during the core conversion:
 • Doppler feedback represents the increase in resonance absorption with fuel temperature rise.
 This mechanism is regarded as having low uncertainty. However, the fuel temperature
 distribution that drives Doppler effect depends on thermal conductivity and heat transfer
 pathways. This adds moderate uncertainty in the effective Doppler response during transients. For
 the U-10Zr core, the magnitude is reduced relative to the original high-Pu core.
 • Fuel and cladding axial expansion feedback components were retained structurally but scaled
 to reflect the lower reactivity worth of U-10Zr pins. Typically, thermal expansion coefficients are
 well-characterized. However, if the transients involve significant fuel swelling, the effective
 feedback can depend on whether fuel-cladding contact is hard or soft and on the presence of
 eutectic formation, introducing a moderate degree of uncertainty.
 • Coolant density (sodium void) feedback was recalculated based on the altered neutron
 spectrum of the U-10Zr core.v Since the operating and transient conditions considered do not
 involve bulk sodium boiling, the dominant effect is spectrum hardening from density rather than
 full void formation. The underlying cross-section data and neutronics response are well
 established, so the basic uncertainty is relatively low.
 • Core radial expansion feedback was retained structurally but scaled to reflect the altered
 reactivity worth of the U-10Zr core. It arises from the thermal expansion of hexagonal duct
 structures and support grids, which causes individual fuel assemblies to bow outward in a
 "flowering" pattern. Structure expansion itself is predictable but uncertainties in coupled
 neutronics-mechanical interaction, bundle-to-duct interactions, bowing distribution, and timing of
 deformation introduce moderate uncertainty in its relative worth.

 21

 Control rod driveline expansion reactivity feedback is ignored in this study due to high uncertainties
and added conservatism. The updated feedback coefficients were implemented using the
SAS4A/SASSYS-1 reactivity feedback structure, which allows feedback to be tied to key thermal and
mechanical parameters such as fuel temperature, coolant temperature, core inlet/outlet flow, and structure
displacement. The impact of these feedback components is particularly relevant in unprotected transient
simulations (e.g., UTOP, USBO), where system response is dominated by the interplay of reactivity
effects and passive feedback mechanisms.

 3.2.2. Fuel design considerations
 This study considers two metallic fuel configurations for the PRISM Mod B core: the original U-
19Pu-10Zr design and a converted U-10Zr variant. Both configurations use HT9 cladding with a sodium-
bond between fuel and cladding. The analyses were performed for both fuel types to compare transient
behavior and capture a broader range of advanced fast reactor concepts. The core layout, channel
configuration, and thermal-hydraulic boundary conditions remain consistent between the two cases, with
only fuel composition and associated reactivity feedback parameters being modified.
 A bounding fuel pin was selected for detailed modeling, representing the highest power-to-flow ratio
within the core. This pin is assumed to reach a peak burnup of approximately 15 atom percent, consistent
with typical end-of-life conditions for metallic fuel. The peak linear heat generation rate is 36 kW/m, and
the fuel experiences a peak cladding temperature of approximately 582°C with a peak fast neutron
damage dose of 200 displacements per atom (dpa).
 The modeled pin features a fuel slug radius of 3.11 mm, surrounded by a sodium bond within HT9
cladding. The cladding has an inner radius of 3.59 mm and an outer radius of 4.15 mm, giving a pitch-to-
diameter ratio of 1.2. The active fuel height is 975 mm, and the fission gas plenum height above the fuel
column is 1400 mm. Coolant enters the core at 360°C and exits at 510°C under nominal conditions. The
sodium bond is modeled to allow infiltration into porosity and axial migration during transients, both of
which influence fuel swelling and mechanical interaction with the cladding.
 A bounding fuel hydraulic channel is defined for core inner and outer regions to characterize hot pins
operating with power-to-flow ratio in a limiting condition.
 The design and operating conditions ensure that key failure margin parameters such as cladding
wastage, thermal creep, and margin to creep rupture remain within acceptable limits for the bounding pin
throughout normal operation. These considerations establish a credible baseline for evaluating the fuel’s
thermal-mechanical performance and failure thresholds under unprotected accident conditions.

 Primary and Secondary Loop Models
 The PRISM MOD B reactor system is modeled using a network of compressible volumes and
segments and liquid elements to represent the thermal-hydraulic behavior of both
the primary and intermediate (secondary) sodium loops, as illustrated in Figure 3.
 In this modeling framework, compressible volumes represent key hydraulic regions such as plena,
manifolds, and pools, while segments define the flow paths and thermal connections between them.
Segments may contain one or more thermal-hydraulic elements and are used to simulate pressure drops,
heat transfer, and momentum changes throughout the system.
 In the primary loop, cold sodium enters the inlet plenum below the core and flows vertically upward
through the core, where nuclear heat generation occurs. The heated sodium exits the core into the hot
pool, then flows laterally into the intermediate heat exchangers (IHXs). Within the IHXs, heat is
transferred from the primary to the intermediate sodium as it flows through the shell side. The primary
sodium then turns downward toward the pump inlet manifold, where it is circulated by a set of primary
pumps. The pump discharge returns the sodium to the cold pool, completing the loop.
 The intermediate loop begins with cold intermediate sodium entering the IHX tube side, where it
receives heat from the primary side. The heated sodium exits the IHX and flows through hot leg piping to
the steam generator, where it transfers thermal energy to water/steam. After leaving the steam generator,
the cooled intermediate sodium flows through cold leg piping, passes through the intermediate loop
pump, and returns to the IHX, completing the cycle.
 This nodalized approach enables simulation of dynamic thermal-hydraulic behavior across both
loops, including heat transport, pressure evolution, and system response under transient conditions. The
model captures key physical phenomena and supports safety evaluations by accurately representing heat
exchange and flow redistribution in the PRISM system.

 3.4. Reference Accident Fuel Response
        3.4.1. UTOP
 A transient analysis was performed for both the U-10Zr and U-19Pu-10Zr (inner core is U-16Pu-10Zr
an outer core is U-22Pu-10Zr) cores to evaluate their response to an Unprotected Transient Overpower
(UTOP) event. The event was initiated by inserting 40 cents (0.40 $) of reactivity into the core without
invoking any external reactivity control systems. The analysis considers fuel behavior for bounding pins
at three burnup levels: 0.5, 7.5, and 15 at%, with plots in Figure 5 through Figure 12 provided for the
high-burnup (15 at%) cases. Table 6 summarizes the fuel behavior results predicted by MFUEL.
 Following the reactivity insertion, both cores experienced a prompt rise in power. In the U-10Zr core,
the normalized power peaked at approximately 2.1X before gradually declining due to the action of
inherent negative reactivity feedback mechanisms. The U-19Pu-10Zr core exhibited a less pronounced
power excursion, with a peak closer to 1.3X, indicating a more moderated transient response. This
difference is largely attributed to the stronger Doppler and structural feedback mechanisms in the higher-
plutonium-loaded core.
 Coolant flow remained nearly constant throughout the transient in both cores, consistent with the
assumption of fixed pump operation during unprotected transients. The temperature response reflects the
observed power excursion trends. In the U-10Zr core, the peak cladding and fuel temperatures at 15 at%
burnup reached 830°C and 951°C, respectively. In contrast, the U-19Pu-10Zr core peaked at 649°C for
cladding and 828°C for fuel. These differences underscore the role of feedback strength and plutonium
content in shaping transient behavior.
 The reactivity feedback breakdown illustrates that Doppler, fuel axial expansion, and core radial
expansion provide the primary negative reactivity responses in both cores. In the U-10Zr core, the
combined effect of these feedback effects is sufficient to reduce the net reactivity to near-zero after the
initial insertion. In the U-19Pu-10Zr core, stronger Doppler feedback acts more rapidly to limit the
severity of the transient.
 Fuel performance results during the transient are summarized in Table 6. The margin to cladding
failures remains high for low and medium burnup core. At 15 at% burnup, the U-10Zr core exhibited a
plenum pressure of 7.80 MPa and a cladding thermal strain of 1.05%. The U-19Pu-10Zr core, while still
experiencing significant loading, showed lower corresponding values of 6.96 MPa plenum pressure and

 29

0.31% thermal creep. These results reflect the lower temperature and mechanical stress imposed by the
milder power excursion in the Pu-bearing core.
 While both designs remain within acceptable limits for most performance metrics, the MCF reached
1.0 in the U-10Zr core at 15 at%, indicating cladding failure is predicted to occur at approximately 353
seconds into the transient. This is the point where the combined effects of internal pressure and thermal
creep reach critical levels. Cladding wastage also increases during the transient, reaching 139 µm in the
U-10Zr core and 119 µm in the U-19Pu-10Zr core, which are reasonable in such transient conditions.
 Overall, the results demonstrate that the U-19Pu-10Zr core offers a more favorable response under
UTOP conditions due to its stronger inherent feedback mechanisms and lower thermal loading. The
bounding pin in the U-10Zr core, particularly at high burnup, approaches cladding failure limits and
would require careful margin assessment in safety evaluations. Note that the reactivity feedback
approximations assumed for U-10Zr based on a previous VTR study [23] carry higher uncertainty and
likely conservatism. Future additional neutronics studies could reduce these uncertainties and provide
useful refinement to predictions. Table 6 and the accompanying figures provide detailed comparison of

                        3.4.2. USBO
 An unprotected station blackout (USBO) transient was simulated for the PRISM MOD B core designs
using U10Zr and U19Pu10Zr fuel. The scenario assumes a complete loss of off-site and internal power,
resulting in the loss of both primary and intermediate loop pump operation. Additionally, the steam line is
considered non-functional, eliminating heat extraction from the steam generator. The transient is entirely
mitigated by passive feedback mechanisms, without operator action or active system intervention.
 The simulation results computed by SAS are given in Table 7 and in Figure 13 through Figure 20.
Immediately after the blackout, both power and coolant flow rates decline sharply as forced convection is
lost. Normalized coolant flow drops below 10% of nominal within the first 50 seconds, and reactor power
decreases to approximately 10% of initial levels as negative reactivity feedback mechanisms rapidly

 34

activate. In both core designs, net reactivity becomes negative early in the transient and remains so,
ensuring passive stabilization.
 A small increase in normalized coolant flow rate is observed near 200 seconds in both cores. This
feature corresponds to the onset of weak natural circulation following the pump trip. As the hot pool and
connected components continue to heat up, while the cold pool and intermediate heat exchangers remain
relatively cooler, buoyancy forces develop that momentarily enhance the upward flow. This transient
recirculation response arises from the interplay of pool heating, exchanger inertia, and density-driven
circulation. The resulting change in the power-to-flow ratio directly impacts the cladding thermal
response. In particular, the rapid flow reduction after the pump trip produces a sharp increase in cladding
temperature, while brief recovery around 200 seconds provides temporary mitigation, slightly flattening
the peak cladding temperature profile.
 The thermal response of the cores reflects these dynamics. At 15 at% burnup, the U10Zr core
experiences a peak cladding temperature of 836°C and a peak fuel temperature of 852°C. In the
U19Pu10Zr core, the corresponding values are slightly lower at 773°C and 801°C, respectively. The
difference in behavior is mainly due to reactivity feedback inputs which should further be evaluated in
future neutronics studies.
 Fuel performance results from the high-burnup cases show that mechanical degradation is more
severe in the U-10Zr core. At 15 at% burnup, thermal creep strain reaches 1.0%, and the CDF rises to
0.11. While these values are still within the limits used for cladding integrity assessment, they are toward
the upper end of the acceptable range and indicate significant stress accumulation. The MCF reaches 0.22,
which approaches the threshold for failure probability, particularly under longer-duration transients. In
contrast, the U19Pu10Zr core exhibits lower degradation, with 0.31% thermal creep, and a CDF of 0.022.
 Plenum pressure increases with burnup, peaking at 7.42 MPa in the U10Zr core and 7.50 MPa in the
U-19Pu-10Zr core due to continued fission gas release and thermal pressurization. Cladding wastage also
progresses with burnup, reaching 122 µm and 131 µm respectively, though it does not appear to
compromise integrity in either case during this passive transient.
 Overall, the USBO results confirm that both the U-10Zr and U-19Pu-10Zr cores can passively
withstand station blackout conditions without operator action. However, the U-10Zr core exhibits greater
mechanical loading and cladding degradation at high burnup, operating closer to design limits. The U-
19Pu-10Zr core benefits from stronger feedback and lower cladding temperature, suggesting a wider
margin under severe passive transients. These outcomes demonstrate the critical role of feedback strength
and core composition in ensuring passive safety.
 For comparison, RELAP5-3D results for the U-19Pu-10Zr are also reported, confirming the peak
temperature trend predicted by SAS4A/SASSYS-1. The RELAP5-3D analysis given in Figure 21 through
Figure 23 shows the reactivity components trend, the fuel centerline and internal cladding temperature of
the inner core hot channel.

 3.4.3. Sensitivities & Uncertainties in USBO Event
 A parametric sensitivity study was conducted to assess the impact of uncertainties in key reactivity
feedback and thermal-hydraulic parameters on fuel performance during the USBO scenario. The study
involved varying five input parameters by ±25% from their nominal values: fuel Doppler reactivity
feedback, fuel axial expansion, core radial expansion, coolant reactivity, and pump inertia. These
variations were selected based on engineering judgment to conservatively bound modeling and physical
uncertainties typical in fast reactor transient analyses. The analysis methodology follows standard
deterministic practices for sensitivity and uncertainty quantification as described in Refs. [26][27]. Each
case was run individually while holding the other parameters constant.
 The primary goal was to examine how each parameter affects key safety-relevant fuel behavior
metrics: peak cladding temperature, fuel temperature, cladding thermal creep strain, CDF, MCF, and
cladding wastage.

 3.4.4. Discussion (key experiment considerations)
 The results of these analyses provide data for establishing applicable boundary conditions in potential
corresponding experiments to study fuel performance. The fuel composition dependencies indicate more
limiting conditions apply to the binary alloy due to inherent characteristics for generally equal
applications. The results for UTOP and USBO provide bounding conditions for overpower and
undercooling events, which would be considered applicable to design basis considerations. In either
scenario, the results show worst case peak cladding temperatures reaching near 850°C, with the high
temperature transient lasting for approximately 200-300 seconds. A primary distinction is the peak
centerline temperature reaches nearly 950°C in the overpower condition while maintaining full flow

 42

conditions in the UTOP. In the USBO, the fuel centerline is not much higher than the cladding
temperature, but the flow is also significantly reduced to near 10% of the initial value.
 The results indicate fuel temperatures remain below coolant boiling point, even considering the
sensitivity study performed above. A given reactor design should be assessed for this margin as it will be
design dependent. This threshold is potentially crucial for specifying applicable experiment conditions
since a typical SFR design will also have a positive void coefficient. Activating this reactivity feedback
mechanism would likely have significant implications on applicable boundary conditions for experiment
and model assessments. Based on these analyses and the benefit of metal fuel transient performance,
sodium boil up is not considered for conservative design basis assessments for detailed R&D study.
However, data from potential experiments could potentially also serve to reduce uncertainty on important
feedback mechanisms to further strengthen the case for exclusion.

 4. Fuel Experiment Design
 The experimental studies on transient fuel behaviors typically includes a combination of approaches.
Several capabilities exist or are planned to support detailed studies, similar to historical approaches for
metal fuel (including some described in Section 1.3.2) and those used on other fuel systems today.
Experimental capabilities include hot-cell furnaces of various scales to test small disk sections to full-
length EBR-II pins. These are generally most applicable for separate effects testing such as eutectic and
for relatively slow transients with at longer time at temperature considerations without thermal-hydraulic
boundary conditions. Over the past several years, a static sodium test capsule called the Temperature
Heatsink Overpower Response (THOR) device has been established for testing segments to full-length
EBR-II pins in the TREAT facility. Soon a flowing sodium device called the Mk-IIIR loop is planned for
deployment in TREAT enabling full forced flow conditions.
 In general, Phenomena-Identification and Ranking Table-like evaluations are useful to identify and
prioritize testing needs. In some cases, integral experiments also serve a similar function to reveal
phenomena. For the metal fuel system, fuel damage mechanisms specified in the prediction of fuel
behavior in plant transients in the previous sections, such as cladding strain and stress and fuel-cladding
eutectic interaction, are of dominant focus. It also includes behaviors impacting core reactivity such as
fuel expansion or relocation and post-failure considerations such as propensity to form blockages. Most
behaviors are heavily time-at-temperature dependent, with some potential concern for temperature change
rate dependency. Others are also strongly linked to thermal hydraulic boundary conditions such as axial
effects in the fuel column and post failure evolution. Therefore, specific targeted phenomena should be
considered in the compressive testing strategy.
Here, we focus primarily on the application of the sodium loop where its forced convection capabilities
are most applicable. Capturing thermal hydraulic effects is most desirable for flow transients such as
ULOF or USBO. In this section, modern transient testing capabilities are introduced. A proposed test
matrix is presented with specific data objectives to support metal fuel technology needs. The conceptual
design of a TREAT experiment device is provided for application to flow transient events.
 4.1. Proposed Experiment Test Matrix
 As described in Section 1.3.2, impressive whole core EBR-II and hot-cell furnace experiments were
performed to demonstrate and study metal fuel LOF performance. The hot-cell experiments were
designed to explore modern limiting transient conditions on fuel pins. Historically, LOF experiments
were performed on oxide fuel pins including TREAT and the CABRI facility. The design approach to
these experiments was largely based on considerations specific to oxide-fueled SFRs, which
predominantly combined LOF-TOP conditions. Metal fuel pins were tested in TREAT historically only

 43

under TOP conditions, with some preliminary planning for LOF studies which were never completed by
the termination of the Integral Fast Reactor program in 1994.
 The objective of a modern in-pile LOF test plan is to measure fuel behavior in a bounding flow
transient condition to demonstrate and provide validation data to fuel behavioral models supporting
reactor safety analysis.
 Specific goals of the testing are to:
 • Quantify fuel damage during a bounding flow transient including fuel eutectic and cladding
 deformation mechanisms.
 • Measure fuel expansion during the duration of the transient event and evaluate potential for
 fuel relocation through eutectic formation.
 • Experimentally evaluate the propensity for fuel blockage formation.
 • Measure fission product behavior and transport through fission gas release, potential
 expulsion, and coolant.

 power levels (higher burnup will be lower power) with the same flow conditions. The results will
 show the fuel performance impacts for limiting fuel and flow transient conditions, expecting
 minimal fuel degradation and no failure.
 • The third experiment in the series is planned to show the margin to cladding failure through
 driving to higher temperature (through flow and/or power adjustment) relative to the previous
 test. Due to limited fuel pins of a single type, the X441A fuel pins (assuming near two nearly
 identical pins) could be good candidate options having high burnup and small plenum, and thus
 relatively high plenum pressure to help drive to cladding rupture. Driving at least one pin to
 failure is a primary objective of the test to measure a failure threshold while maintaining the
 second pin slightly cooler through flow tube inlet orifices. The option to terminate the test upon
 cladding breach in the first pin is available but would be considered against monitoring post-
 failure evolution and potential relocation with the TREAT hodoscope.
 • The fourth test proposes to evaluate the condition of a postulated fuel assembly blockage. This
 case is not reflected in the analysis shown previously but has been reported preliminarily by
 separate analysis performed using the SAS4A/SASSYS-1 code. This case likely provides a severe
 accident condition, beyond fuel performance considerations. This scenario is postulated and
 credibility of conditions leading to it should be carefully evaluated. The data from a test could aid
 in validating predictions related to fuel relocation behavior and corresponding reactivity effects.
 The preliminary analysis showed fuel reactivity changes are limited to about 100s after pin failure
 with minimum negative reactivity occurring about 15s after failure. Flow tube integrity would
 likely be a key design challenge to address.
 • The final test in the matrix puts forth a small bundle test on metallic fuel, a first of a kind
 opportunity. Historically, oxide fuel tests were performed in similar loops in 7-pin configurations.
 This test would be most useful if post-failure phenomena are prioritized after the initial testing.
 The test may utilize irradiated EBR-II fuel pins – along with some fresh pins – to create a fully
 prototypic boundary condition around the center pin. Therefore, the test should be designed to fail
 the center pin. This configuration would especially focus on pin-pin interactions such as failure
 propagation, fuel relocation, and the propensity for fuel blockage.
 Several important details will be considered in the design of these experiments (some mentioned
above). Full accounting of these aspects is not provided here but a few of these considerations include:
 • Radial peaking effects – TREAT’s thermal neutron spectrum will induce strong radial
 peaking in the test fuel. Historically, successful compensation was made through scaling
 adjustments of power and flow to create a desired prototypic temperature profile in the fuel.

 45

 Such methods may be useful here, but flow transients are also likely not as exacerbated
 compared to faster power ramp experiments of the past.
 • Flow tube orifices – The sodium loop design includes the option of differential flow throttling
 in multi-flow tube configurations. This approach was used historically to successfully impose
 differential cladding heating scenarios in a given test. The benefit was having pins tested
 under nearly identical conditions but with slightly different cooling and resulting damage
 levels for comparison.
 • Reactor control feedback – TREAT provides options to use experiment information to trigger
 reactor control in a desired manner. Historically, this capability was used to launch reactivity
 insertion upon sodium boiling detection or to shut down the reactor upon cladding failure
 detection. In the M-series tests, this latter capability was used with flow tube orifices to
 obtain just-failed pins with nearly-failed pin in the same test.
 • Loop pressure – Loop overpressure is a useful tool for controlling sodium coolant saturation
 temperature. In some tests, maintaining single phase flow may be desired to evaluate pure
 thermomechanical response of the fuel. The third test in the series above is an example where
 this may be useful. In other tests, having prototypic, near atmospheric pressure may be more
 appropriate.
 • TREAT hodoscope – The TREAT hodoscope will provide crucial data related to fuel
 reactivity behavior. Establishing testing design and protocols to maximize its performance
 and better reduce uncertainty in data interpretation will be paramount.

 4.2. Preliminary TREAT Loop LOF Design
 4.2.1.Mk-IIIR Sodium Loop
 The Mk-IIIR sodium loop is a system for conducting liquid metal cooled, fuel safety, irradiation
experiments in TREAT. Its design is based on historical sodium loops used successfully in TREAT in the
1970’s and 1980’s. The Mk-IIIR sodium loop provides prototypic, liquid metal cooled reactor thermal-
hydraulic conditions. It supports test trains of various configurations from single up to seven pins of
uranium- or plutonium-bearing fuel with custom instrumentation options. The loop can house test pins up
to the size of an FFTF fuel pin (0.9m fuel length and 2.4 m overall length). The device is capable of
handling fresh or previously irradiated fuel pins through complete engineering of hot cell handling and
loading capabilities. It provides heating and sodium re-melt capability and containment for associated test
hazards.
 The Mk-IIIR loop consists of two parallel, Inconel-625 pipes with upper and lower bends. The pipes
are referred to as the test leg and the pump leg. Figure 24 shows a schematic of the primary features of the
Mk-IIIR sodium loop. Sodium flows in the clockwise direction around the Mk-IIIR loop.

 47

Figure 24. Mk-IIIR sodium loop rendering showing primary piping. Insulation, wires, outer containment,
 etc., are not pictured.

 The test leg houses fueled test trains. Figure 25 shows the test train location inside of the Mk-IIIR
sodium loop. In this image, the sodium loop is shown inside the experiment containment. A Grayloc
flange provides access to insert and remove test trains and seals the sodium boundary. Test trains seat on
the bottom taper of the Mk-IIIR test leg. A gas plenum below the Grayloc flange provides a volume for an
inert cover gas, an expansion volume, and a volume to regulate experiment test pressure. A burst disc
connected to this gas plenum provides overpressure protection without draining sodium during off-
nominal scenarios. Two thermowells in the plenum above the upper bend allow detection of sodium level
in the loop.
 The pump leg provides return flow in the downward direction. The pump leg houses equipment used
to establish the hydraulic test conditions in the loop. The annular linear induction pump (ALIP) surrounds
a segment of the pump leg piping and provides sodium flow at up to 20 gpm at a discharge head of 50
psig. Pressure transducers monitor loop pressure at the pump inlet and outlet. Redundant, permanent-
magnet flowmeters at the bottom of the pump leg control flow during the experiment. Historically in
TREAT sodium loops, flowmeters were also used to detect test fuel pin failure and are therefore
considered a candidate for the same purpose today.

 48

 The thermal conditions in the loop are controlled via resistive heaters, using thermocouples mounted
on the outside of the loop piping as process variables, and insulation wrapped around the primary piping
to limit thermal losses. The heaters, thermocouples, and insulation are not shown for clarity, but they can
be seen in detail in INL Drawing 1010896.
 The primary piping of the Mk-IIIR loop is surrounded by the Big-Broad Use Specimen Transient
Experiment Rig (Big-BUSTER). The long length of the loop requires special additions to Big-BUSTER
including the Big-BUSTER extension, the top hat extension, and the experiment closure plate, the latter
being a common feature of all Big-BUSTER experiments. This equipment surrounds the Mk-IIIR sodium
loop piping assembly and establishes the experiment safety containment boundary. Big-BUSTER is a
thick-wall, zirconium-niobium alloy, pipe weldment that extends from the TREAT grid plate at the
bottom of the reactor core reflector region to above the reflector region on the top of the TREAT core.
The zirconium-niobium alloy is used to improve neutron efficiency during transient testing. The Big-
BUSTER extension and remaining equipment extend the containment boundary above the TREAT core
due to the overall length of the Mk-IIIR sodium loop. The above core equipment is fabricated from
austenitic stainless steel and Inconel. The Big-BUSTER pressure boundary is filled with inert gas during
the experiment to prevent oxygen ingress into the sodium boundary and subsequent sodium reaction in
the event of a leak from the sodium boundary. If the Mk-IIIR loop is overpressurized in what would
typically be an unlikely condition, the burst disc discharges directly into the Big-BUSTER containment
boundary.

            4.2.1.1.     LOF Test Train
 The pre-conceptual test train design is based on successful, legacy, test train designs operated in
TREAT using the Mk-II and Mk-III sodium loops. The test train provides support for the test fuel pins
and instrumentation and directs the flow of sodium around the test fuel pins. Test trains are approximately
128 inches long x 1.4 inches in diameter. Historically, test trains were fabricated from austenitic stainless
steel and Inconel. Material selection depends on the planned temperatures and pressures during the
experiment. The test train consists of three sub-assemblies: the hanger rod assembly, gas jacket assembly,
and the sodium inlet assembly. Figure 26 shows views of the test train for a configuration consisting of
two test pins in separate individual flow tubes.
 The sodium inlet assembly (see Figure 26) interfaces with the tapered experiment leg of the Mk-IIIR
sodium loop while supporting the test fuel pins and directing sodium from the Mk-IIIR into the separate
fuel pin flow channels. The preliminary test pins described in Section 4.1 are from EBR-II with a
relatively large diameter. The loop is sized to allow any fuel pins up to FFTF length (~1m fueled length,
~2.4m overall length) to be placed into the test section. Orifice plates may be used to control differential
flow between the flow channels as necessary. The pin extensions attach the fuel pins to the sodium inlet
assembly and control the axial height of the fuel pins relative to the TREAT core. The sodium inlet
assembly attaches to the gas jacket assembly via a specialized nut due to the limited radial clearance with
the Mk-IIIR piping.
 The gas jacket assembly (see Figure 26) provides an inert gas environment surrounding individual
sodium flow tubes. The gas jacket insulates the Mk-IIIR sodium loop piping from the elevated
temperature of the fuel pins and provides an insulated, inert gas environment for test instrumentation.
Bellows assemblies allow differential thermal expansion between the flow tubes and gas jacket. At the
sodium outlet, a perforated strainer is optional to collect fuel debris in the event of meaningful dispersal
from a failed fuel pin. The strainer increases the available surface area for debris collection and mitigates
the obstruction of flow channels from potential dispersed particulate material. Borated, stainless-steel flux
collars are attached on the outside of the gas jacket. The boron concentration and thickness of the flux
collars are selected to shape the axial flux profile on the test fuel pins.
 Instrumentation (not shown in Figure 26 but listed in Table 10), will be based on the data collection
and control signals necessary to operate the experiment and requested in-situ data. Typical options for
experiment instrumentation are thermocouples, miniature flowmeters, acoustic sensors, and pressure
transducers. Instrument selection will begin in the conceptual design phase of the experiment. Instrument
leads are routed through tubing and sealed in the gas plenum region of the experiment to minimize the
potential for sodium contact with the leads and sodium ingress into the gas jacket.
 The experiment hanger assembly (see Figure 26) forms the mechanical seal with the Mk-IIIR
assembly and provides axial support for the experiment. Below the flange are a series of Belleville
springs that permit differential thermal expansion between the test train and the Mk-IIIR sodium loop. All
test train instrument leads are sealed through the containment boundary via the experiment closure plate.
 The design includes a challenging requirement for assembly and disassembly using remote handling
facilities at the Hot Fuel Examination Facility (HFEF). The loop has been engineered with custom
designed facilities uniquely enabling assembly of the heavily instrumented thermal-hydraulic sodium
system. This aspect of the design creates significant risk and cost, which has been exercised successfully
for water and sodium capsule experiments in the past several years.

 4.3. Preliminary Experiment Predictions
 The objective of this modeling effort is to design a TREAT experiment that replicates key cladding
thermal responses observed in loss-of-flow events, specifically focusing on conditions that may lead to
thermal creep and potential cladding rupture. While the unprotected station blackout (USBO) scenario
serves as reference for the target thermal response, the experiment is not intended to reproduce the full
reactor-scale transient. Instead, the modeling aims to replicate the approximate power-to-flow ratio
evolution that drives the rise and fall of peak cladding temperature, the primary parameter controlling
time-dependent cladding deformation and fuel-cladding chemical interactions.
 Because the TREAT facility imposes a strict energy generation limit of approximately 1500 MJ for
this design configuration, the simulated transient must occur at reduced power and flow levels. This
constraint requires scaling down both power and flow to achieve thermal exposure comparable in
magnitude and timing to USBO transients. The goal is not to match timing precisely but to reproduce
the shape and magnitude of cladding temperature rise and decline, particularly in the region where
thermal creep becomes active.
The SAS4A/SASSYS-1 and RELAP5-3D codes are employed to model the thermal and mechanical
behavior of a representative fuel pin subjected to the designed experimental conditions. Reactivity
feedback effects are neglected in this simulation, and both power and flow are imposed as external inputs.
This approach enables fine control over test conditions and isolates thermal effects from system-level
feedback behaviors, ensuring relevance for validating fuel performance under controlled, high-
temperature excursions.

 4.3.1. Model Descriptions
            4.3.1.1.    RELAP5-3D
 A detailed model of the TREAT sodium test loop has been developed using the RELAP5-3D code.
The scope of this RELAP5-3D Mk-IIIR model is to provide steady-state and transient thermal-hydraulic
parameters for the safety analysis and experiment evaluation [28]. The model includes:
 • Two flow tubes, each one containing one fuel X430 fuel pin (see Table 11)
 • The gas jacket
 • The stagnant sodium outside the gas jacket
 • The descending leg, including ALIP pump boundary conditions and the bottom curve
 • The upper part of the test section and the gas zone.
 Loop mass flow is imposed as function of time using a time-dependent junction. Active (fuel pins)
and passive heat structures (ALIP and pipes/tubes walls) have been modeled. Power generation in the
active heat structure is obtained using axial peaking factors and time-dependent general tables. Ten axial
layers have been used to model the X430 fuel pins. The passive heat structures account for the thermal
effects of the thermal insulator. An adiabatic boundary conditions is imposed on the outer surface of the
thermal insulator material, to represent the negligible heat transfer of the loop in the BIG BUSTER
containment. A radiation heat transfer scheme has been implemented to allow the code to simulate the
energy transfer between the gas jacket wall and the test train wall at high temperatures. A scheme of the
RELAP5-3D Mk-IIIR loop model is reported in the following Figure 27.

            4.3.1.2.    SAS4A/SASSYS-1
 Figure 28 shows the simplified TREAT test loop representation used in the SAS4A/SASSYS-1
model. TREAT flow loop input model for SAS has been developed based on RELAP input described in
Section 4.3.1.1. The test section consists of two fuel pins from an irradiated X430 bundle previously
operated in EBR-II, placed in separate flow tubes. The model simulates two pins to capture bounding
behavior. The flow path includes lower and upper plenum regions, a test section, and a cover gas region
above the assembly. The cold leg contains a pump used to impose a time-dependent flow profile
consistent with a loss-of-flow transient.
 The SAS model includes seven axial thermal elements to resolve the fuel and cladding temperature
distributions, and four compressible control volumes representing the lower plenum, test section, upper
plenum, and cover gas. Each loop segment is assigned specific wall mass, geometry, and thermal

 53

properties, including material-specific heat capacities. System pressure is held below 0.8 MPa throughout
the transient to prevent sodium boiling.
 Both power and flow histories are directly imposed as user inputs. The power profile reflects the
peaking behavior of a high-power bundle but is constrained to remain within TREAT’s total energy
generation limit. The flow rate is defined with a programmed coastdown shape to mimic pump inertia
effects and enables cladding heat-up consistent with creep rupture initiation thresholds.
 No reactivity feedback mechanisms (e.g., Doppler, axial or radial expansion, coolant density) are
included, as the objective is to isolate thermal and mechanical behavior. Thermal interaction between pins
is neglected, and radial heat loss is assumed to be minimal. Key outputs from the model include peak
cladding temperature, cladding thermal creep strain, MCF, and CDF, which are compared against
structural limits to evaluate the severity of the test.
 The test section includes two irradiated fuel pins from the X430 bundle previously irradiated in EBR-
II. The pins are inserted into separate flow tubes within the test train, and their thermal and mechanical
characteristics were explicitly defined based on measured post-irradiation data. Table 11 summarizes the
relevant parameters of the irradiated X430 pins and the corresponding flow tubes, including burnup,
dimensions of fuel and cladding, and overall pin geometry. These characteristics were used as input to the
SAS model to accurately represent the thermal response of the test fuel during transient operation.

        4.3.2. Results
 An optimization study was conducted to identify a power-flow history that yields peak cladding
temperature behavior similar to that observed in the reference USBO scenario, while satisfying the energy
generation constraint of the TREAT facility (limited to about 1500 MJ in this proposed design
configuration). Given this constraint, the simulation strategy focused on operating at low power and low
flow conditions extended over a longer duration (~1000 s), in order to emulate key aspects of the transient
thermal response relevant to creep-rupture behavior.
 The SAS and the RELAP5-3D models were run iteratively, adjusting both the power profile and the
inlet flow rate to each pin, aiming to reproduce a similar peak cladding temperature variation as observed
in the USBO case. The final selected conditions resulted in a decaying power profile shown in Figure 29,
corresponding peak linear heat rate in Figure 30, and the inlet coolant flow rate per pin in Figure 31.
While the power and flow histories differ significantly from the reactor-scale USBO transient, the ratio
between power and flow—and its variation with time—were tuned to reproduce a comparable thermal
response in the fuel.
 Power coupling factors were used to convert the SAS-predicted pin-level power to the total required
TREAT power. These coupling factors were approximated using prior experimental benchmarks (e.g.,
THOR), and will be refined as part of future experiment definition.
 To ensure credibility of the SAS predictions, selected cases were cross-checked using the RELAP5-
3D simulations. Figure 32 and Figure 33 shows excellent agreement between SAS and RELAP5-3D
results for cladding temperature evolution, reinforcing confidence in the simplified model’s ability to
capture dominant thermal-hydraulic trends. This validation supports the use of SAS for further design
optimization. Figure 34 and Figure 35 show RELAP5-3D results for maximum sodium temperature and
loop structural components, respectively, for the experiment.
 The results demonstrate that it is feasible to recreate the desired thermal transient behavior, including
the evolution of cladding temperature over time, within the TREAT energy budget. This enables
investigation of cladding creep behavior under representative thermal conditions, by navigating the
constraints of the test facility. The control combination of power and flow provides flexibility to consider
a range of conditions. Beyond these results, corresponding with the first two tests in Table 9, these
models will be used to consider more detailed and expanded test development.

 5. Conclusions
 This study has established a framework for designing integral transient experiments that can generate
high-value validation data for metallic fuel behavior in SFRs. Identifying the conditions most relevant to
fuel pin degradation during loss-of-flow type transients is essential for validating advanced safety codes
and fuel performance models, and for reducing uncertainties in cladding failure thresholds. By linking
plant-scale simulations with in-pile experimental design, this work bridges system-level accident response
and single-pin failure mechanisms in a way that can directly support licensing and advanced reactor
deployment.
 The PRISM MOD B design was adopted as a reference configuration, both in its original U-19Pu-
10Zr form and in a modified U-10Zr variant to broaden relevance to different fuel cycle options.
Bounding analyses were performed using SAS4A/SASSYS-1 and RELAP5-3D, encompassing steady-
state and unprotected operation, while unprotected cases produce cladding loading and thermal excursions
approaching creep, eutectic and fission-gas-driven stress thresholds for the high burnup bounding fuel
pins. These simulations provided a rigorous technical basis and blueprints for defining experimental
boundary conditions and highlighted the specific degradation pathways that merit direct validation.
 Analyses of UTOP and USBO scenarios underscored the central role of inherent reactivity feedback
mechanisms in stabilizing reactor power. The predicted responses of U-19Pu-10Zr and U-10Zr cores were
consistent with expectations, though the magnitude of the differences indicates that refined neutronics
data will be needed before drawing strong conclusions about relative margins. Both configurations
ultimately stabilized without scram, confirming the general resilience of metallic fuel SFRs under
bounding cases while also emphasizing the importance of improved feedback characterization for safety
analysis. When considered alongside the sensitivity and uncertainty evaluations, these results indicate that
even under bounding assumptions the system remains below sodium boiling, reinforcing the confidence
in the conservatism of the simulations.
 To translate the bounding analyses into practical validation opportunities, a conceptual design of a
sodium LOF experiment was developed that couples a pair of representative metallic fuel pins to MK-

 59

IIIR loop at TREAT. A preliminary test matrix was assembled to span a range of power histories and flow
coast-downs representative of unprotected loss-of-flow and flow blockage type transients. These planned
experiments emphasize conditions most relevant to the cladding degradation under thermal-hydraulic
environments designed to mirror plant-scale power-to-flow ratios to support the licensing, validation, and
model improvements.
 The experiment design defined the test train and loop configuration to deliver controlled boundary
conditions while remaining compatible with TREAT operational constraints. In parallel, independent
loss-of-flow optimization studies were carried out using X430 fuel pins from EBR-II to establish
conditions that would reproduce desired peak cladding temperature histories. By iteratively adjusting
power and flow, a combination was identified that matched the bounding plant-scale thermal response
while remaining within the energy limits of TREAT. These limits necessitated the use of reduced linear
heat rates and flow rates, but optimization preserved a consistent power-to-flow ratio, ensuring fidelity to
plant relevant thermal-hydraulic conditions. SAS4A/SASSYS-1 and RELAP5-3D produced very similar
predictions for the cladding temperature evolution, providing confidence in the ability of the simplified
models to capture the dominant thermal-hydraulic trends. This consistency reinforces the credibility of the
conceptual design and supports its role in generating integral data to validate fuel performance and safety
analyses.
 6. Next Steps
 The results presented in this report are planned to next be used to develop a detailed fuel performance
assessment. Further analysis may evolve the proposed test matrix and help further refine the experiment
design strategy. In addition, a detailed mechanical design and supporting analysis will need to be
completed to make proposed tests a reality. These results will also be used to support other testing in
TREAT capsules, hot-cell furnaces, and out-of-pile testing for fuel and cladding. Distinct analysis could
also be done to provide further refinement and confirmation of key model assumptions related to core
behavior during the transient events.
