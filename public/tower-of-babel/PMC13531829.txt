Technical feasibility of hand-meshed autologous dermal grafts in immediate prepectoral breast reconstruction: a preliminary case series
Authors: Habibollah Mahmoodzadeh, Sayyed Mohammad Reza Hakimian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrepectoral breast reconstruction offers reduced morbidity and improved aesthetic outcomes compared to traditional approaches. However, access to cost-effective and biologically compatible tissue for implant support remains limited. This case series explores the use of hand-meshed autologous dermal grafts harvested from patients as a readily available and cost-effective alternative for implant coverage.MethodsBreasts underwent immediate prepectoral implant-based reconstruction using hand-meshed autologous dermal grafts, harvested from the lower abdomen, the ipsilateral breast, or the contralateral breast, in a prepectoral breast reconstruction technique referred to as harvested corium-covered implant (HACCIM). However, minor superficial mastectomy skin flap wound events were common, indicating a clinically relevant complication burden. Background

Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy among women globally, with a rising incidence in both developed and developing countries [1, 2]. Despite advancements in screening and treatment, mastectomy often remains a necessary part of oncologic management, particularly in high-risk or locally advanced cases [3]. While effective in disease control, mastectomy can significantly impact a woman’s body image, sexuality, emotional health, and social well-being [4]. The psychological effects may include anxiety, depression, and loss of self-esteem, often interfering with interpersonal relationships and quality of life [5]. In this context, breast reconstruction is not simply an aesthetic option but a key component of recovery, contributing to psychosocial rehabilitation and improving long-term patient satisfaction [6, 7].

Implant-based breast reconstruction remains the most commonly performed method of post-mastectomy reconstruction due to its relatively lower complexity, shorter operative time, and faster postoperative recovery [8, 9]. However, achieving stable and long-lasting results requires adequate tissue coverage to support the implant, prevent extrusion, reduce wound healing complications, and improve aesthetic outcomes [10, 11]. In contrast to subpectoral approaches, where the pectoralis major muscle offers inherent soft tissue coverage over the implant, prepectoral techniques place the implant entirely over the muscle, making the need for reliable and supplementary coverage more critical [12].

The use of acellular dermal matrices (ADMs) in implant-based breast reconstruction has become increasingly widespread, particularly in prepectoral techniques [13]. ADMs, derived from human, bovine, or porcine dermis, may offer better aesthetic outcomes and reduced risk of implant-associated complications such as capsular contracture [14, 15]. However, breast reconstructions using ADMs are associated with a wide range of complications, such as infection, seroma formation, red breast syndrome, and delayed wound healing [16, 17]. In addition, the use of ADMs substantially increases the overall cost of breast reconstruction. This factor must be carefully considered, particularly in low- and middle-income (LMICs) countries where healthcare systems operate under constrained financial resources [18]. Therefore, the exploration and utilization of alternative coverage materials with lower complication profiles and reduced financial burden are of significant relevance.

The use of autologous dermal grafts in implant-based breast reconstruction has emerged as a viable alternative to ADMs. These grafts, typically harvested from the patient’s own abdominal or redundant skin during mastectomy or simultaneous procedures, provide a cost-effective and biologically compatible scaffold for lower pole support, facilitating stable implant positioning and minimizing soft tissue complications [19–22]. Several retrospective series and small prospective trials have reported favorable surgical and aesthetic outcomes, with reduced rates of seroma, infection, and capsular contracture in selected patient populations [19, 21, 23, 24]. However, the current evidence remains limited, and more clinical data are needed to better understand the specific indications and limitations of this technique.

This case series presents our experience with autologous dermal grafts in immediate prepectoral breast reconstruction, with a focus on surgical technique, feasibility, and complication rates.

Methods

Patients and study design

This case series was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and its protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran (No: IR. TUMS. IKCH.REC. 1404. 518). Patients who underwent immediate, single-stage, prepectoral implant-based breast reconstruction following mastectomy were considered eligible for inclusion, regardless of cancer stage, tumor type, prior treatments, or whether the procedure was therapeutic, prophylactic, or for recurrent disease, except for inflammatory and locally advanced cases. In clinical practice, selection for this reconstructive approach was primarily based on the operating surgeon’s intraoperative clinical assessment of adequate mastectomy flap perfusion and viability. Prepectoral breast reconstruction was chosen as the preferred approach to minimize muscle disruption and associated morbidity. The surgical technique and its potential benefits and limitations were thoroughly discussed with all patients during the preoperative consultation. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before surgery.

Data collection

Data were collected using a researcher-made checklist, including demographic variables (age, sex, and body mass index (BMI)), smoking status, relevant medical history (including chronic diseases, prior chemotherapy, or radiotherapy), operative details, and clinical outcomes. Operative variables recorded included the indication for surgery (therapeutic vs. prophylactic), type of mastectomy (nipple-sparing or skin-sparing), and implant characteristics (volume and shape).

Outcomes of interest included the mean time required for dermis harvesting and donor site repair, as well as postoperative complications. Minor complications were defined as wound-related issues (such as dehiscence, superficial mastectomy skin flap wound events (e.g., superficial epidermolysis and more superficial skin flap necrosis), seroma, hematoma, delayed wound healing, and infectious events) managed conservatively. Major complications were defined as any adverse event necessitating unplanned reoperation, hospital readmission, or resulting in implant failure.

Surgical technique and follow-up

Mastectomies included both nipple-sparing and skin-sparing approaches, depending on oncologic and anatomical considerations. All mastectomy and subsequent immediate breast reconstruction procedures were performed at two surgical centers located in Isfahan and Tehran, Iran.

Simultaneously with mastectomy or immediately following its completion, harvesting of the free autologous dermal graft was initiated. Depending on case-specific factors, dermal grafts were harvested either from the lower abdomen, the ipsilateral breast, or the contralateral breast, particularly in patients undergoing simultaneous reduction mammaplasty. The thickness of the harvested dermis varied according to the donor site and intrinsic tissue characteristics, typically ranging from approximately 0.5 to 3 millimeters, with careful intraoperative selection to ensure adequate strength and pliability. The graft was de-epithelialized in situ using a scalpel, then excised and manually defatted. To enhance its flexibility and expand its usable area, the graft was meshed by making a series of parallel cuts measuring 2 to 4 centimeters in length and spaced approximately 4 to 5 millimeters apart. This meshing technique expanded the surface area of the graft by approximately three- to fourfold. The prepared graft was then preserved in sterile normal saline until it was implanted.

In all cases, a prepectoral reconstructive approach was employed, wherein the implant was placed directly over the pectoralis major muscle in the subcutaneous plane. This technique, referred to as the harvested corium-covered implant method (HACCIM) here, involves complete coverage of the implant using a meshed dermal (corium) graft, with the superficial dermal surface facing upward (Fig. 1). Mastectomy flap thickness and perfusion were evaluated intraoperatively based on the surgeon’s clinical judgment and experience; no formal objective measurement tools were used. The graft was precisely sutured to the pectoralis major muscle using 2 − 0 polydioxanone sutures to secure the implant position and ensure stable fixation. Two closed-suction Jackson-Pratt drains were placed for each breast. Drains were removed within 1 to 2 weeks postoperatively, once daily output had decreased to less than 30 mL. No topical antibiotics or povidone-iodine solutions were applied to the implant surface. Instead, all patients received intravenous prophylactic antibiotics initiated before surgery and continued for 24 h, followed by oral antibiotics until drain removal. The oral regimen consisted of cephalexin 500 mg every 6 h; in patients with a reported cephalexin allergy, clindamycin 300 mg every 8 h was planned as an alternative. Postoperative follow-up was conducted over a period ranging from 6 to 12 months and included clinical examination and photographic documentation at each visit to assess reconstructive outcomes and identify any complications (Fig. 2).

Findings

A total of 16 patients underwent implant-based breast reconstruction using autologous hand-meshed dermal grafts. Of these, 5 patients underwent bilateral procedures, resulting in a total of 21 reconstructed breasts. The mean age of the patients was 40.87 years (range: 32–60 years), and the mean BMI was 26.31 kg/m² (range: 20.80–30.86 kg/m²). A family history of breast cancer in first-degree relatives was documented in 4 patients (25.0%), while the remaining 12 patients (75.0%) reported no family history of cancer. None of the patients had a history of smoking or chronic comorbid conditions at the time of surgery. Regarding oncologic indications, 12 out of 21 breasts (57.14%) were reconstructed following newly diagnosed breast cancer, 7 (33.33%) were prophylactic mastectomies, and 2 (9.52%) were performed due to cancer recurrence. In terms of oncologic treatment history, 2 patients (12.5%) had received prior radiotherapy, and 4 (25.0%) had undergone chemotherapy before the reconstructive surgery.

Skin-sparing mastectomy (SSM) was performed in 19 breasts (90.48%), while nipple-sparing mastectomy (NSM) was applied in 2 cases (9.52%). Postoperative chemotherapy and radiotherapy were administered in 10 (47.6%) and 7 (33.3%) breasts, respectively. All reconstructions were completed using round implants. Implant volumes ranged from 170 cc to 650 cc, with a mean volume of 433.57 cc. The dermal grafts used in the reconstruction were harvested from the abdominal region in 15 cases (71.43%), from the ipsilateral breast in 5 cases (23.81%), and from the contralateral breast in 1 case (4.76%). The average duration of dermis harvesting and donor site repair was 36 min (range: 18–60 min).

No cases of minor seroma, hematoma, or surgical site infection were observed during the short-term postoperative period. However, superficial mastectomy skin flap wound events along the suture line occurred in 10 of the 21 reconstructed breasts (47.62%), and among these cases, 4 breasts (19.05%) also showed small wound dehiscence. Importantly, all minor complications were effectively managed with outpatient conservative treatment and required no hospital admission.

In total, 2 out of 21 reconstructed breasts (9.5%) experienced major complications, resulting in implant loss. In the first case, breast reconstruction was performed following neoadjuvant chemotherapy (TCHP: docetaxel, carboplatin, trastuzumab, and pertuzumab; six cycles administered every 21 days). Postoperative radiotherapy was initiated 4 weeks after breast reconstruction, during which the patient developed a large radiation-induced skin ulcer that subsequently led to cellulitis, wound dehiscence, and eventual implant loss. The short interval between surgery and postoperative oncologic treatments likely played a significant role in the unfavorable outcome. In the second case, the patient had undergone chest wall radiotherapy within 18 months before breast reconstruction. The postoperative course was complicated by extensive skin flap necrosis occurring early after surgery, which progressed to wound dehiscence and ultimately resulted in implant loss. The relatively recent history of radiation exposure may have contributed to increased tissue fragility and impaired healing.

Discussion

The present study reports the outcomes of prepectoral breast reconstruction using a hand-meshed autologous dermal graft. Minor complications, particularly superficial mastectomy skin flap wound events, occurred frequently and represent a clinically significant finding, even though most cases were managed conservatively in the outpatient setting. Major complications occurred in two patients who had received recent pre- or postoperative radiotherapy, resulting in implant loss. In this small series, no major complications were observed among patients without a history of radiation.

Given the critical role of breast reconstruction in improving women’s quality of life following mastectomy, identifying accessible and reliable reconstructive options is essential [25]. Hand-meshed free dermal autografts offer a practical and cost-effective alternative to ADMs, which remain limited by high cost and lack of FDA approval [18, 26]. In addition, previous studies have reported a higher rate of complications such as infection, dehiscence, seroma, necrosis, and implant failure in breast reconstruction using ADM [27, 28]. Our technique provides a valuable reconstructive option by avoiding some of these barriers while still offering biologically compatible soft-tissue support, particularly in settings with limited financial resources or in situations where ADM use is not feasible.

Some previous studies have investigated the use of autologous dermal grafts in breast reconstruction across different surgical modalities [19]. In these studies, dermal tissue was typically used as a vascularized dermal flap or sling to support a tissue expander or implant, most commonly in subpectoral or dual‑plane reconstruction. More recently, several studies have also explored the use of autologous dermal slings for implant coverage in pre‑pectoral breast reconstruction [29–31]. For example, a recent case series of 23 breasts describes a meshed dermal sling for complete prepectoral breast reconstruction [31]. In that series, no major complications were reported, and minor complications were observed in only 2 of 23 breasts. However, that technique differs from HACCIM in several important respects. The authors used a vascularized pedicled dermal sling, with meshing applied only to the distal portion, whereas HACCIM is based on a free, non-vascularized hand-meshed dermal autograft. Moreover, their sling was derived from de-epithelialized tissue of the operated breast, while HACCIM allows separate graft harvest from the lower abdomen, ipsilateral, or contralateral breast tissue. Another important difference relates to intraoperative decision-making; their reconstruction strategy was guided by indocyanine green angiography to assess mastectomy-flap perfusion, whereas in our series, flap viability was assessed by clinical intraoperative judgment. In contrast, reports describing the use of free dermal autografts remain relatively limited. For instance, Zingaretti et al. described the use of a machine-meshed autologous dermal graft to provide inferior pole coverage of a subpectoral implant [21]. In contrast to their approach, our technique utilizes a hand‑meshed free dermal graft specifically in a direct‑to‑implant pre‑pectoral setting. In the above-mentioned studies on breast reconstruction using dermal grafts, the reported overall complication rates ranged from approximately 7% to 59% [19, 29–31]. These differences likely reflect variations in patient populations, surgical approaches, and follow-up durations. In the present case series, complications occurred in 57% of breasts (12 out of 21 breasts comprising 10 minor complications and 2 major complications). This rate falls within the range previously reported, as noted above, in studies of breast reconstruction using free dermal autografts.

Although superficial mastectomy skin flap wound events were managed successfully with conservative measures, they occurred relatively frequently in this series. Several factors associated with this complication have been demonstrated in previous studies, including patient BMI, comorbidities, prior radiotherapy, and poor intraoperative perfusion [32, 33]. In our series, intraoperative assessment of flap perfusion and thickness was based solely on subjective clinical judgment, without objective adjuncts such as perfusion imaging, which may have contributed to the observed complication rate. To identify determinants of this complication, well-designed interventional studies with larger sample sizes are required. Therefore, further studies are needed to obtain more robust and generalizable evidence.

Previous studies of ADM-assisted prepectoral breast reconstruction reported a range of complications, including seroma, implant loss, partial nipple–areola complex necrosis, and mastectomy flap necrosis occurring at overall rates of 5–19% [34, 35]. However, direct comparison of complication rates between breast reconstruction using ADM and dermal autografts requires comparative studies. Some prior comparative studies have evaluated dermal autografts versus ADMs in the context of breast reconstruction. In a study by Lynch et al. involving 48 patients who underwent tissue expander-based breast reconstruction with either ADM or dermal autografts, patients in the dermal autograft group demonstrated significantly lower rates of delayed wound healing (14.8% vs. 4.8%) and major complications (18.5% vs. 0.0%) compared with those in the ADM group. Furthermore, histological evaluations comparing ADM and dermal autografts revealed a greater degree of vascularization within dermal autografts, suggesting improved tissue integration [36]. Nevertheless, another publication based on the same patient cohort reported no significant differences between the two groups regarding long-term outcomes, including aesthetic outcome scores and capsular contracture grades [24]. A 2012 study involving 16 patients reported that tissue expander–based breast reconstruction using dermal autografts harvested from preexisting abdominal scars resulted in low complication rates (19.0%) with no implant losses, offering outcomes comparable to ADM-assisted reconstruction but at a lower cost, without a significant increase in operative time. However, none of the patients included in this study had a history of radiotherapy [23]. These findings suggest that autologous dermal grafts can serve as a feasible and cost-effective alternative to synthetic meshes in breast reconstruction.

In the present series, both cases of implant loss occurred in patients with a history of radiotherapy. Based on this descriptive observation and previous evidence, we hypothesize that radiotherapy may be associated with implant loss in these cases. In support of this interpretation, a previous systematic review by Lee and Mun involving 20 studies demonstrated a higher risk of implant failure (relative risk: 2.58; 95% CI: 1.86–3.57) in patients who had received pre-reconstruction radiotherapy [37]. Similarly, multiple subsequent comparative studies have reported an increased rate of implant failure among patients who underwent pre-mastectomy radiotherapy, as compared with those who received post-mastectomy or no radiation, across various reconstructive modalities, including prepectoral breast reconstruction [38–40]. Furthermore, multiple systematic reviews have reported higher rates of complications, such as implant loss, reoperation, wound infection, and capsular contracture, in reconstructed breasts of patients who received post-mastectomy radiotherapy [41–43]. However, given the descriptive nature of our study and the limited number of events, this hypothesis should be interpreted with caution, and no definitive conclusion can be drawn. Further studies are needed to better evaluate outcomes in irradiated patients undergoing prepectoral breast reconstruction using autologous dermal grafts.

This study is among the few investigations evaluating the feasibility and safety of a hand-meshed autologous dermal graft in prepectoral breast reconstruction. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the short follow-up period (6–12 months) restricts the ability to assess long-term outcomes, including complications such as capsular contracture, rippling, implant malposition, aesthetic changes, and patient-reported outcomes; these outcomes were not collected in the present study, and interpretations are therefore restricted to short-term postoperative outcomes and feasibility. Moreover, the absence of a control group, such as ADM-assisted prepectoral reconstruction, limits the ability to directly compare outcomes and precludes any interpretation regarding comparative safety or effectiveness. Another limitation is that intraoperative assessment of mastectomy flap perfusion and thickness was based on subjective clinical judgment, without the use of objective tools such as perfusion imaging. This limitation may partly explain the relatively high rate of superficial mastectomy skin flap wound events. Another limitation of the present study is the absence of radiological or histological assessment of graft integration; therefore, future studies incorporating objective evaluation methods may provide further insight into the biological behavior of autologous dermal grafts. Future studies with larger cohorts, longer follow-up periods, and comparative study designs are needed to validate these results and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the long-term safety and effectiveness of this reconstructive approach.

Conclusion

In this preliminary case series, hand-meshed free dermal autograft was technically feasible in immediate prepectoral implant-based breast reconstruction. Nevertheless, superficial mastectomy skin flap wound events occurred frequently, reflecting a clinically meaningful complication burden. Implant loss, the major complication observed in this series, occurred in patients with recent pre- or postoperative radiotherapy exposure. Given the limited sample size and descriptive design of the study, these observations should be interpreted with caution, and larger prospective studies with longer follow-up periods and comparative designs are needed to define better the safety, complication profile, long-term outcomes, and clinical role of this technique.
