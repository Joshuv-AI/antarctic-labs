An overview of circular RNAs in the regulation of autophagy in lung cancer
Authors: Mokhtar Rejili, Kasim Sakran Abass, Farid Hashemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCircular RNAs (circRNAs) are a class of stable non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) that regulate cellular processes such as proliferation, apoptosis, and stress responses. Autophagy is an essential catabolic process that maintains cellular homeostasis by degrading damaged proteins and organelles. Increasing evidence suggests that circRNAs can influence tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic response in lung cancer through the modulation of autophagy. In this narrative review, we summarize current findings on the role of circRNAs in regulating autophagic signaling pathways in lung cancer. We also discuss the potential clinical significance of autophagy-related circRNAs, including their role in tumor cell survival, proliferation, and sensitivity to chemotherapy. Although emerging evidence highlights their promise as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers as well as therapeutic targets, further experimental and clinical validation is needed. This review provides an overview of current knowledge and highlights future research directions to improve lung cancer management.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Circular RNAs, Autophagy, Lung cancer, Molecular mechanisms, Biomarker

Background

Lung cancer remains a significant public health issue globally, owing to its widespread incidence and high fatality rates [1]. Late-stage diagnosis in many patients limits available treatment options, often resulting in poor clinical outcomes. Histopathologically, lung cancer is categorized into two main subtypes: non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC). NSCLC approximately 85% of all new lung cancer diagnoses and encompasses adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma [2]. The worldwide incidence of lung cancer is rising, particularly in female groups [1, 3].

Autophagy is a lysosomal degradation mechanism that has been conserved through evolution and is important for cellular homeostasis. It has been getting more attention for its function in lung cancer that depends on the situation [4]. In the initial phases, cytotoxic autophagy acts as a tumor suppressor by removing damaged organelles, proteins, and DNA, therefore inhibiting tumor formation. Although by providing cancer cells with energy and nutrients, protective autophagy in advanced lung cancer may accelerate tumor development [5, 6]. In spite of the role of autophagy is still debated, there is a growing number of clinical strategies to modulate this process in lung cancer therapy [7, 8].

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are a special type of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) that are widely found in various cell types [9, 10]. Their unique circular shape comes from a process called back-splicing of precursor messenger RNAs (pre-mRNAs). Researchers have discovered them in many different types of eukaryotic organisms [11, 12]. Since their first identification nearly half a century ago, circular RNAs were predominantly dismissed as splicing artifacts without function. Growing research now shows their critical roles in multiple physiological pathways, including autophagy [13], apoptosis, cell proliferation [14–16], inflammation and immune system regulation [17]. Dysregulated expression levels of circRNAs have been seen in many types of cancers, including glioma, breast cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma and prostate cancer [18, 19]. Notably, the expression profiles of these circRNAs are frequently associated with patients’ clinical outcomes and pathological features. Due to their unique closed-loop structure, circRNAs are more resistant to degradation by exonucleases than linear RNAs, which makes them more stable [20, 21]. Moreover, they can also be found in body fluids like saliva, urine, and blood, making them attractive candidates for non-invasive cancer tests [22]. These characteristics suggest that circRNAs could play an important role in diagnosing, predicting outcomes, and developing targeted treatments for cancer. Recent studies have demonstrated that diverse ncRNAs, particularly circRNAs, possess potential therapeutic value in various cancers, including lung cancer [23, 24]. Their biological functions are exerted through the regulation of multiple cellular processes, such as autophagy [25]. While some emerging evidence has highlighted ncRNAs molecules suppress autophagosome formation and trigger cells apoptosis, others promote cytotoxic autophagy, resulting in autophagic cell death. Currently, a detailed comprehensive review elucidating these structures and their complex mechanisms remains lacking.

An increasing number of studies revealed that circRNAs, along with other ncRNAs such as miRNAs, can regulate autophagy through influencing autophagy-related genes (ATGs) at different stages of gene expression [26]. At first, miR-30a was found to be an important regulator of autophagy. Subsequent research discovered that many miRNAs influence autophagy by interacting with specific regions of the mRNAs involved in this process [27]. Furthermore, beyond miRNAs, other ncRNA like circRNAs have also been implicated in influencing key components of the cell’s autophagic pathways [28, 29]. In pathological states, dysregulation of circRNA expression may interfere with normal autophagic mechanisms, promoting tumor growth, angiogenesis and resistance to therapeutic interventions by altering autophagy pathways [30–32].

More analyses are required to shed light on the dark pathways and mechanisms connecting circRNAs and autophagy in lung cancer. A group of recent evaluations has provided valuable findings; circRNAs appear to play fundamental roles in regulating autophagy in this disease. In this context, the present review aims to summarize recent outcomes concerning the role of circRNAs in autophagy regulation during lung cancer development and to underline their potential capacities in both diagnosis and treatment.

Methods

A structured literature search was conducted using the MEDLINE/PubMed and Scopus databases to identify studies investigating the regulatory roles of circular RNAs (circRNAs) in autophagic signaling pathways associated with lung cancer. The search strategy was developed using combinations of the following keywords: “lung cancer”, “non-small cell lung cancer”, “small cell lung cancer”, “circular RNAs”, “circRNAs”, “autophagy”, “autophagic signaling”, “tumor progression”, “drug resistance”, “diagnosis”, and “therapy”. Additional keyword combinations, including “lung cancer AND circRNAs” and “circRNAs AND autophagy”, were also applied to improve the relevance of the retrieved studies.Original studies relevant to the role of circRNAs in the regulation of autophagy and lung cancer pathogenesis were included. The final selection focused on studies addressing the molecular interplay between circRNAs and autophagic pathways, as well as their diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic implications in lung cancer progression and treatment response. Given the emerging and evolving nature of this field, the present study was designed as a narrative review emphasizing mechanistic insights, translational relevance, and current research gaps.

Formation, characteristics circRNAs

CircRNA generation: shaping the loop

Emerging evidence indicates that circRNAs can originate from nearly all genomic regions and are generated through multiple circularization mechanisms, resulting in substantial diversity in sequence composition and length [33–35]. Circular RNAs are mainly produced from pre-mRNAs through a special type of splicing called back-splicing [36]. Most circRNAs are derived from pre-mRNAs via back-splicing, a noncanonical splicing process in which a downstream 5′ splice site is joined to an upstream 3′ splice site, forming a covalently closed single-stranded RNA loop characterized by a unique 3′–5′ junction [12, 37, 38]. This spliceosome-dependent process also enables the production of multiple circRNA isoforms from the same precursor sequence through alternative back-splicing events [39, 40].

Based on their origin and structural features, circRNAs can be grouped into several main categories: exonic circRNAs (ecircRNAs), which consist only of exons; exonic-intronic circRNAs, which consist of both exons and introns (ElciRNAs); intronic circRNAs (ciRNAs), which consist of introns; and tRNA-intronic circRNAs (tricRNAs), which consist of tRNA introns [45]. A new class of mitochondrially encoded circRNAs, called mecciRNAs, has been identified. These molecules act as molecular chaperones and direct nuclear-encoded proteins into mitochondria [46]. Despite significant advances, the precise molecular mechanisms governing circRNA biogenesis remain incompletely elucidated. Ongoing research endeavors aim to further decipher these underlying processes.

Features of circRNAs

CircRNAs are a major subgroup of ncRNAs that have been studied extensively and show key structural features (Fig. 1). Unlike linear RNAs, circRNAs form closed loops without free 5′ or 3′ ends, and they lack the usual 5′-to-3′ polarity and polyadenylated tails. These unique ncRNAs help them avoid degradation by exonucleases, which makes them more stable compared to linear RNAs [47, 48]. The structural stability of circRNAs enables their robust detection and accurate quantification, frequently at abundances surpassing those of their linear counterparts. This property underscores their potential as highly reliable biomarkers for disease diagnostics [49, 50].

Studies show that most of them are localized in the cytoplasm, though some are present in the nucleus [41]. CircRNA expression is highly specific, changing with development, tissue type, and pathological conditions [51, 52].With the growth of RNA sequencing technologies, circRNAs are widely present and abundant in many human tissues [53]. They appear throughout the body but are especially abundant in the brain [54]. In human fibroblasts, researchers have identified more than 25,000 unique circRNAs, about ten times more than their linear counterparts [44, 55, 56]. These circular structures influence gene regulation at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels [57]. Both circRNAs and linear RNAs originate from precursor messenger RNA(pre-mRNA); however, circRNAs are predominantly produced via a non-canonical back-splicing mechanism. This process is different from the usual splicing that produces linear transcripts [44].

Biological roles of circRNAs: from sponges to scaffolds

Functionally, circRNAs participate in numerous biological processes across a wide spectrum of human tumors. As summarized schematically in (Fig. 1), circRNAs are generated through back-splicing events and exert their functions through several well-characterized mechanisms, including acting as miRNA sponges, interacting with proteins, regulating gene transcription or alternative splicing, encoding peptides or proteins, and modulating epigenetic states. Among these mechanisms, the most extensively studied and predominant function of circRNAs is their role as miRNA sponge [58–60]. Normally, miRNAs inhibit the translation of target mRNAs by binding to the 3′ untranslated regions (UTR) [61]. This affects how stable the mRNA is and can also change gene expression in the nucleus by interacting with promoters.

CircRNAs can bind to and sequester miRNAs, reducing their activity and thereby modulating target gene expression at the molecular level [62, 63]. Distinct circular RNAs exert either tumor-suppressive or oncogenic effects through their capacity to act as molecular sponges for various microRNAs. The ultimate biological outcome is contingent upon the functional role of the targeted miRNAs and the downstream signaling pathways they regulate [64, 65]. For example, circTAB2 has been characterized as a tumor-suppressive circRNA that exhibits significantly reduced expression in lung cancer tissues and suppresses cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. CircTAB2 functions as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) by sequestering miR-3142. This interaction relieves the repression on GLIS2 and modulates downstream AKT signaling, contributing to its tumor-suppressive effects [66]. In contrast to circTAB2, which suppresses lung cancer progression, circSWT1 exerts oncogenic effects by promoting invasion, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in NSCLC. Findings from Long et al. indicate that circSWT1 acts as a ceRNA that sponges miR-370-3p, thereby relieving inhibition of the transcription factor SNAIL, a pivotal regulator of EMT [67]. Interestingly, numerous circRNAs that do not bind miRNAs still carry out a wide array of biological activities, which implies they have regulatory functions independent of miRNA interaction.

Studies have found that circRNAs can interact with RBPs or other proteins, creating circRNA-protein complexes. These interactions have the potential to impact on the function and mechanistic pathways of action of binding proteins [68–70]. CircDCUN1D4 binds to the RNA-binding protein HuR and drives its movement into the cytoplasm. This circRNA acts as a scaffold, facilitating the interaction of HuR with thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) mRNA. Ultimately, circDCUN1D4 inhibit metastasis and glycolysis in lung adenocarcinoma through a TXNIP-dependent mechanism [71]. cIARS (also known as hsa_circ_0008367) binds to the RNA-binding protein ALKBH5, which normally suppresses autophagic flux in hepatocellular carcinoma cells. Through this interaction, cIARS modulates autophagy, ferritinophagy, and the ferroptotic response to sorafenib [72].

In addition to their previously described roles, circRNAs have been demonstrated to influence gene transcription and regulate alternative splicing [73, 74]. Additionally, several circRNAs have been detected in the cytoplasm, suggesting the possibility that these molecules could be translated into proteins. In study by Zheng et al., a circular RNA called circPPP1R12A, found mostly in the cytoplasm of colon cancer cells, was reported to contain an open reading frame encoding a small protein of 73 amino acids. This protein promotes tumorigenesis and metastasis by activating of the Hippo–YAP signaling pathway [75].

Autophagy: from pathways to impact

Autophagy is an evolutionarily preserved intracellular degradation mechanism that enables cells to break down and recycle damaged proteins, organelles, and other cytoplasmic materials through lysosomal digestion [76]. It operates at a basal level to maintain cellular homeostasis and cytoplasmic quality control, but is strongly upregulated during nutrient deprivation, metabolic stress, or cellular damage to preserve energy balance and cell survival [77, 78]. Autophagy exists in both non-selective and selective forms [79, 80]. Selective autophagy specifically targets substrates through receptors that bind LC3/Atg8 proteins. Major forms include mitophagy, ribophagy, ER-phagy, and pexophagy. These processes contribute to development, tissue maintenance, immunity, and aging, and are closely linked to diseases such as cancer, cardiovascular disease, neurodegeneration, and infections [81, 82]. In tumors, autophagy may either facilitate cancer progression by providing nutrients and supporting metastasis or act as a protective mechanism that limits tumor development [83, 84].

Autophagy activation occurs through a regulated multistep cascade:

Initiation: Cellular stress activates the ULK1/2 complex (ULK1, ULK2, ATG13, ATG101, and FIP200). AMPK stimulates ULK1 and promotes autophagy, whereas mTORC1 inhibits ULK1 and blocks autophagy initiation [85, 86].

Autophagy paradox: suppression versus promotion

In normal cells, autophagy is considered a tumor-suppressing mechanism. Last research reveals that autophagy can play a paradoxical role in cancer, functioning both to suppress and promote tumor development depending on the cellular context and disease stage [32, 76]. Once tumors are established, cancer cells often exploit autophagy to survive stress. These stresses include nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, and therapeutic pressure. By providing metabolic flexibility, autophagy helps tumor cells grow and survive. It also contributes to tumor progression and the development of more aggressive phenotypes [77, 78]. Although several autophagy modulators have been investigated as therapeutic strategies, the inherent duality of autophagy remains a significant obstacle, often contributing to limited efficacy and therapeutic failure. Here, we briefly describe the two opposing roles of autophagy in different studies.

Tumor-suppressive autophagy

In physiological conditions and during the initial stages of cancer, autophagy functions as a critical safeguard, protecting cells from harmful stimuli and reducing the risk of malignant transformation [79]. Autophagy protects DNA integrity and preserves genomic stability by reducing the harmful effects of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [80, 81]. The significance of autophagy is highlighted in cervical cancer cells lacking this mechanism, which have significantly elevated ROS formation [82]. Similarly, in immortalized mouse kidney (iBMK) cells under metabolic stress, p62 accumulates with damaged mitochondria and ER chaperones, suggesting a deficiency in protein clearance. Accumulated p62 enhances ROS generation, triggering DNA damage responses that contribute to tumorigenesis [83, 84]. Autophagy restrains tumor progression by removing toxic mutagens and limiting the accumulation of genetic alterations [85]. This process additionally limits excessive inflammation and promotes cellular senescence, thereby restricting tumor cell growth [85]. In fetal human colon cells, autophagy also attenuates inflammation through negative regulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [87]. Moreover, its protective role is evident in renal cell carcinoma (RCC), where reduced autophagy is associated with increased cell proliferation [86].

Genomic investigations have shown that essential autophagy-related genes, including as Beclin-1, ATG5, and ATG7, are directly involved in tumor suppression. Mutations in these genes increase spontaneous cancer formation, indicating the tumor-suppressive role of autophagy [88–90]. Beclin-1, an essential regulator of autophagy, is affected by miR-140-3p in gastric cancer, where its activation inhibits cell survival, invasion, and EMT [91]. ATG5 and ATG12 similarly prevent metastasis in breast cancer by promoting the degradation of the NBR1 protein [92]. Altogether, these findings support the view that autophagy is an important barrier to tumor formation in the early stages of cancer progression.

Tumor-promoting autophagy

In the development and progression of the tumor, autophagy functions as a crucial regulator that supports cancer cell growth and survival. Autophagy contributes to tumor progression through enhancing the migratory and invasive potential of cancer cells. In hepatocellular carcinoma, autophagy has been identified as a essential mediator of invasion by promoting of EMT via activation of the TGF-β/Smad3 signaling pathway. Starvation-induced autophagy suppressed epithelial markers and increased mesenchymal markers. It also upregulated MMP-9, hence promoting cellular invasiveness. Inhibition of autophagy or inhibition of TGF-β/Smad3 diminished EMT and decreased invasion [93]. Autophagy in HCC has also been reported to support pulmonary metastasis by promoting anoikis resistance and colonization [94]. Autophagy induction promoted migration and invasion of bladder cancer cells by facilitating EMT via the TGF-β pathway [95].

Studies have shown that autophagy enhances the ability of cancer cells to tolerate stress following exposure to radiotherapy, chemotherapy, hypoxia, or hypothermia, thereby promoting their survival. Notably, elevated basal autophagy levels are detected across various cancer cell lines. Conversely, downregulation of autophagy helps to reduce cancer cell viability. However, when considering autophagy as a pro-tumorigenic mechanism, it is important to note that its activation is highly dependent on tumor type, stage, and the patient’s genetic background [96]. Given the increased metabolic demands of rapidly proliferating cancer cells, autophagy enables their survival under unfavorable conditions by modulating tumor metabolism. Furthermore, in RCC, reduced mitochondrial metabolism is associated with autophagy activation, heightened proliferation, and enhanced cellular migration [97].

Autophagy in lung cancer

Peng et al. reported that human lung adenocarcinoma samples exhibited elevated autophagy levels compared with matched normal lung tissues [98]. In lung cancer cells, silencing PITX2B induces autophagy and concurrently activates apoptosis, resulting in decreased tumor cell proliferation. These effects were reversed when autophagy was blocked with lysosomal inhibitors [99]. These results highlight the role of PITX2B in lung cancer and suggest that targeting autophagy could be a potential therapeutic strategy in tumors with elevated PITX2B expression [99]. Evidence also indicated that the up- or down-regulation of miRNAs may influence drug resistance in lung cancer cells [100]. One study demonstrates that varying miRNA expression levels can modulate the sensitivity of lung cancer cells to conventional therapy via autophagy regulation. For instance, transfection with miR-101-3p reduced cisplatin resistance and suppressed autophagy, whereas inhibiting miR-101-3p activated autophagy and decreased cisplatin sensitivity [101]. Autophagy levels in lung cancer cells were found to be closely linked with the tumor microenvironment, which includes stromal compartments such as non-tumor cells and fibroblasts [102]. In a model mimicking this microenvironment, proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-8 acted as strong inducers of autophagy, supporting tumor growth. IL-6 or IL-8 increased ATG5/12 conjugation and reduced p62, indicating cytokine-induced autophagy. Importantly, inhibition of autophagy in this context suppressed tumor growth [102].

CircRNAs at the crossroads of autophagy

Accumulating evidence indicates that circRNAs display duality role in autophagy regulation. For instance, circRHOBTB3 enhances autophagy via the circRHOBTB3/miR-600/NACC1 axis, thereby promoting proliferation in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [103]. In breast cancer, circ-Dnmt1 promotes autophagy by interacting with p53 and AUF1 to induce their nuclear translocation, thereby enhancing tumor proliferation and xenograft growth [104]. Similarly, circCDYL activates autophagy by sponging miR-1275 to increase ATG7 and ULK1 expression, linking its overexpression to poor prognosis and reduced therapeutic response [105]. In renal clear cell carcinoma, circ-0035483 promotes autophagy under gemcitabine treatment. Mechanistically, circ-0035483 acts as a miR-335 sponge to upregulate CCNB1 expression, enhancing autophagy and contributing to gemcitabine resistance [106]. Circ-0085131 enhances autophagy in NSCLC cells by competing with miR-654-5p and upregulating ATG7. Its overexpression promotes autophagy and chemoresistance, while silencing this axis reduces proliferation and restores chemosensitivity [107]. In contrast, in bladder cancer, circHIPK3 functions as a suppressor of autophagy. Its overexpression prevents Valosin-containing protein (also known as P97) from engaging with the Beclin 1 complex, establishing a regulatory axis through which circHIPK3 restrains autophagic activity [108]. Circ-0000515 inhibits autophagy in cervical cancer cells, while its silencing enhances both autophagy and apoptosis. Its oncogenic role involves sponging miR-326 to upregulate ELK1, resulting in reduced LC3 levels [109]. Zhang et al. indicated that circSEPT9 suppresses autophagy in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells. Silencing circSEPT9 induces autophagy, apoptosis, and cell cycle arrest, whereas its oncogenic effect involves sponging miR-637 to upregulate LIF and activate STAT3 signaling [110].

With the rapid advancement of sequencing technologies and bioinformatics, an increasing number of circRNAs regulating autophagy in cancer are expected to be identified. Investigating whether autophagy can, in turn, modulate circRNA expression remains a critical question. Elucidating how circRNAs regulate autophagy and how this regulation impacts tumor initiation and progression is essential for developing circRNA-based therapeutic strategies in cancer.

CircRNAs often act through miRNAs, which have recently emerged as their major downstream effectors in multiple signaling cascades. CircPLCE1

CircPLCE1 exemplifies an emerging class of circRNAs whose function is regulated not primarily tthrough miRNA sequestration, but through stress-responsive RNA modification and protein turnover. In lung cancer, circPLCE1 is consistently downregulated in tumors and correlates with advanced stage and poor prognosis, suggesting a tumor-suppressive role [111]. Mechanistically, its regulation departs from canonical epitranscriptomic models and its stability is controlled by oxidative stress–induced 8-oxoguanine modification, linking circRNA fate directly to the tumor redox environment [111]. Unlike conventional RNA modifications mediated by dedicated “writers,” o8G is generated through ROS attacking guanine bases, thereby altering base pairing and influencing RNA fate [112–115]. In line with previous findings in miRNAs [116–118], o8G modification led to altered RNA stability. This feature aligns circPLCE1 with a broader theme in cancer biology whereby RNA molecules act as sensors of oxidative stress, yet whether such modification is selective or broadly affects circRNAs remains unresolved. circPLCE1 inhibits chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) by destabilizing HSC70, a core regulator acting with LAMP2A, both frequently overexpressed in lung tumors and linked to poor prognosis [111]. Loss of HSC70 stability reduces CMA activity. This suppression disrupts proteostasis that favors tumor progression [111, 120, 121]. Reduced CMA elevates ATG5 protein levels, thereby promoting ATG5-dependent autophagosome formation and increasing autophagic activity [122, 123]. Therefore, circPLCE1 modulates autophagy through destabilization of HSC70, thereby suppressing CMA while indirectly enhancing Macroautophagy via ATG5 accumulation (Fig. 3). This mode of regulation contrasts with many reported circRNAs that influence autophagy by altering transcriptional programs or miRNA availability, and instead highlights a post-translational, proteostasis-centered mechanism. Notably, the opposing regulation of CMA and macroautophagy underscores the complexity of autophagic crosstalk in cancer, where inhibition of one pathway may activate compensatory mechanisms with distinct tumor consequences [111]. While this study positions circPLCE1 as a nodal regulator linking oxidative stress, circRNA turnover, and autophagy balance, key questions remain regarding the generalizability of this mechanism across tumor types and circRNA species [111]. Future work will be required to determine whether redox-sensitive circRNA decay represents a common regulatory layer in cancer and whether targeting such pathways offers therapeutic selectivity.

From a therapeutic perspective, the reciprocal regulation of CMA and macroautophagy by circPLCE1 highlights a major pharmacological challenge in targeting autophagy in cancer. Inhibition of CMA, whether through circPLCE1-mediated HSC70 destabilization or pharmacological blockade, can trigger compensatory activation of ATG5-dependent macroautophagy, potentially preserving tumor cell survival under stress. This adaptive plasticity suggests that effective therapeutic strategies may require coordinated suppression of both CMA and macroautophagy to prevent pathway bypass. Combination approaches targeting HSC70–LAMP2A–mediated CMA alongside core macroautophagy components such as ATG5 may therefore be necessary to overcome compensatory autophagic flux in lung cancer. Importantly, the redox-sensitive regulation of circPLCE1 further implies that such combinatorial interventions may need to be tailored to the oxidative state of the tumor microenvironment to achieve maximal selectivity and efficacy.

Hsa_circ_0085131

Hsa_circ_0085131, derived from exons 11–15 of PABPC1, has been implicated in NSCLC progression and cisplatin resistance, with elevated expression correlating with tumor recurrence and poor survival outcomes. While these associations suggest an oncogenic role, mechanistic insights remain largely derived from a single experimental framework [107]. Functionally, hsa_circ_0085131 was shown to enhance autophagic activity through a ceRNA mechanism involving miR-654-5p–mediated derepression of ATG7, leading to increased expression at both mRNA and protein levels [107]. As a key autophagy-related protein, ATG7 catalyzes the conjugation of ubiquitin-like proteins, including ATG12 and ATG8, driving autophagosome formation and maintaining autophagic flux [124, 125]. Elevated ATG7 expression has been linked to cisplatin resistance in NSCLC [126]. More experiments have demonstrated clinical samples from recurrent NSCLC patients showed increased ATG7 levels, reinforcing its role in chemoresistance [107]. Hsa_circ_0085131 enhances ATG7-dependent autophagy, which in turn protects NSCLC cells from cisplatin-induced apoptosis and facilitates their survival under chemotherapeutic stress, suggesting that circ_0085131 may act to amplify an already primed survival pathway rather than initiate autophagy de novo. However, the functional interpretation of this axis warrants caution. Autophagy plays a context-dependent role in NSCLC, with evidence supporting both cytoprotective and cytotoxic effects depending on treatment duration and genetic background. The reliance on chloroquine to reverse chemoresistance supports autophagy involvement but does not distinguish between bulk and selective autophagy, nor does it exclude autophagy-independent functions of ATG7 [107]. Moreover, whether circ_0085131 regulates additional targets beyond miR-654-5p remains unexplored, raising the possibility that its contribution to chemoresistance extends beyond canonical ceRNA activity [107, 127].

Collectively, these findings position hsa_circ_0085131 as a modulator of autophagy robustness rather than a singular driver, highlighting a broader theme whereby circRNAs reinforce stress-adaptive pathways in drug-resistant NSCLC. Future studies integrating multi-omics data and comparative circRNA profiling will be essential to determine whether this regulatory pattern represents a shared resistance mechanism or a context-specific adaptation.

Circ_0020123

Circ_0020123, located at chr10:119042605–119,049,859 and derived from the PDZD8 gene, represents another circRNA implicated in the regulation of autophagy in NSCLC. It functions as a molecular sponge for miR-193a-3p, thereby acting as a ceRNA. Through this mechanism, circ_0020123 relieves the post-transcriptional repression of interferon regulatory factor 4 (IRF4) [128], this distinguishes circ_0020123 from circRNAs that directly modulate autophagy genes such as ATG7 or Beclin-1, suggesting that circRNAs can influence autophagy at multiple regulatory tiers. IRF4, a member of the IRF transcription factor family, plays a crucial role in immune regulation [129] and has been identified as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in various hematological malignancies [130]. In NSCLC, IRF4 promotes tumor cell proliferation and survival by activating oncogenic pathways such as NOTCH-AKT signaling [131].

LC3-II, p62 (SQSTM1), Beclin-1, ATG5, and ATG7 are among the key molecular markers and core components commonly used to evaluate autophagy. LC3-II represents the lipidated form of LC3-I (ATG8) and is associated with autophagosome membrane formation, and is widely used as an indicator of autophagy activation. p62 (SQSTM1) functions as a selective autophagy adaptor that binds ubiquitinated cargo and LC3 and is degraded during autophagy; therefore, its accumulation is generally indicative of impaired autophagic degradation, whereas its reduction reflects active autophagic flux. Beclin-1 is an essential autophagy-related protein involved in the initiation of autophagosome formation through its role in the class III PI3K complex, and it is closely associated with regulation of autophagy initiation. ATG5 and ATG7 are core autophagy machinery proteins required for autophagosome biogenesis. ATG7 acts as an E1-like activating enzyme in ubiquitin-like conjugation systems that include the ATG12–ATG5 pathway as well as LC3 lipidation, thereby contributing to autophagosome formation and maturation [132, 133]. Yang et al. shown that Silencing circ_0020123 increased autophagic activity, indicated by elevated Beclin-1 levels and reduced accumulation of p62/SQSTM1, and simultaneously reduced NSCLC cell proliferation, migration, and glycolysis. this study also represented that in xenograft models, circ_0020123 knockdown constrained tumor growth [128]. These findings imply that IRF4-dependent autophagy suppression may support a metabolically aggressive tumor phenotype and this pattern contrasts with circRNAs that promote chemoresistance by enhancing cytoprotective autophagy, underscoring the context-dependent role of autophagy in NSCLC progression.

However, several mechanistic questions remain unresolved. It is unclear whether IRF4 directly regulates autophagy-related gene transcription or whether autophagy modulation is secondary to metabolic reprogramming. Additionally, reliance on canonical markers limits insight into the qualitative nature of autophagy induced by circ_0020123 silencing. Given IRF4’s established immunological functions, whether this axis also influences tumor–immune interactions in vivo warrant further investigation. Collectively, circ_0020123 represented how circRNAs can suppress autophagy indirectly by reinforcing oncogenic transcriptional networks, highlighting mechanistic diversity beyond the conventional ceRNA–ATG paradigm.

CircRNA_100565

CircRNA_100565 has emerged as a recurrent oncogenic regulator in NSCLC, particularly in the context of chemoresistance, but the available studies also reveal important nuances in its functional repertoire. Initial clinical profiling showed that circRNA_100565 is significantly overexpressed in cisplatin-resistant NSCLC tissues and cell lines, and that its high expression correlates with poor overall survival, supporting its value as an independent prognostic indicator [134], these clinical associations suggest a role for circRNA_100565 beyond tumor initiation, implicating it in disease progression and therapy response. Mechanistically, early work demonstrated that circRNA_100565 promotes NSCLC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion through sequestration of miR-506-3p, leading to derepression of HMGA2 [135]. This axis aligns with established oncogenic pathways, as HMGA2 is a well-characterized driver of epithelial–mesenchymal transition and metastatic behavior in lung cancer [136].

Subsequent studies expanded this view by identifying an alternative ceRNA network in which circRNA_100565 sponges miR-337-3p, thereby enhancing expression of ADAM28 [137]. ADAM28, a disintegrin and metalloproteinase family member involved in cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions [138], has been reported to be upregulated in NSCLC, where it is associated with tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and enhanced proliferation [139]. Its oncogenic functions have also been documented in multiple other cancer types [140, 141], this broader oncogenic relevance supports the biological plausibility of circRNA_100565 as a multifunctional regulator rather than a pathway-specific effector. Importantly, the miR-337-3p/ADAM28 axis connects circRNA_100565 to autophagy modulation, a process increasingly recognized as a key adaptive mechanism underlying cisplatin resistance. Silencing circRNA_100565 sensitized NSCLC cells to cisplatin by suppressing proliferation, inhibiting autophagic flux, and promoting apoptosis [134]. Silencing circRNA_100565 sensitized NSCLC cells to cisplatin by impairing proliferation, suppressing autophagy, and inducing apoptosis. At the molecular level, these effects were reflected in autophagy markers. The LC3-II/I ratio and Beclin-1 expression decreased, while p62 levels accumulated, indicating autophagy inhibition [134]. Notably, this autophagy-centered mechanism was not addressed in the earlier miR-506-3p/HMGA2 study, suggesting that circRNA_100565 may engage distinct downstream pathways depending on cellular context or selective pressure.

Taken together, these studies converge on the concept that circRNA_100565 functions as an oncogenic hub capable of regulating multiple miRNA–mRNA axes, with overlapping but non-identical phenotypic consequences. While both reported mechanisms support tumor progression, only the miR-337-3p/ADAM28 pathway has been explicitly linked to autophagy-dependent chemoresistance. This raises unresolved questions regarding whether circRNA_100565 simultaneously regulates multiple ceRNA networks within the same tumor cells, or whether these interactions are context-dependent, such as differing between treatment-naïve and drug-resistant states. Further uncertainties remain concerning upstream regulators of circRNA_100565, the relative dominance of its competing miRNA interactions, and whether its autophagy-modulating effects are ADAM28-dependent or involve additional downstream effectors. Addressing these gaps will be essential for determining whether circRNA_100565 represents a viable therapeutic target or biomarker in NSCLC. Nevertheless, the convergence of clinical prognostic data with mechanistic evidence supports the emerging view that circRNA_100565 is not merely an NSCLC-associated circRNA, but a dynamic regulator of tumor aggressiveness and chemotherapy response, with potential implications for improving cisplatin efficacy in NSCLC [137].

CircFLNA

Accumulating evidence implicates circRNA filamin A (CircFLNA) as a regulator of autophagy-associated tumor progression in NSCLC, although current conclusions rely largely on a limited number of mechanistic studies [142, 143]. Gong et al. identified a CircFLNA-centered regulatory axis involving miR-486-3p and its downstream targets CYP1A1 and XRCC1, demonstrating that CircFLNA knockdown suppresses proliferation, migration, and invasion of NSCLC cells [143]. Mechanistically, CircFLNA functions as a competing endogenous RNA that sequesters miR-486-3p, thereby relieving post-transcriptional repression of CYP1A1 and XRCC1 and sustaining autophagic flux. This autophagy-supportive role appears particularly relevant in the context of chemoresistance, as enhanced autophagy enables cisplatin-resistant A549/DDP cells to evade apoptosis [143].

Therapeutically, the triterpenoid compound Ganoderic acid A (GA-A) has been shown to disrupt this regulatory network by downregulating CircFLNA, thereby increasing miR-486-3p levels and reducing CYP1A1 and XRCC1 expression. Functional assays confirmed that GA-A treatment inhibits autophagy, as evidenced by decreased LC3-II/I and Beclin-1 levels and increased p62 accumulation, which in turn enhances apoptosis and restores cisplatin sensitivity in resistant NSCLC cells. Overexpression of CircFLNA reverses these effects, and autophagy inhibition with 3-methyladenine (3MA) further confirms that GA-A’s pro-apoptotic and chemo sensitizing actions are mediated via suppression of CircFLNA-dependent autophagy [143]. Importantly, the use of both CircFLNA overexpression and pharmacologic autophagy inhibition (3-methyladenine) strengthens the causal link between CircFLNA, autophagy suppression, and chemo sensitization. Nevertheless, these interventions do not fully disentangle whether CircFLNA primarily regulates autophagy initiation, autophagosome maturation, or autophagy-related stress responses, leaving the precise hierarchical position of CircFLNA within the autophagy machinery unclear.

Collectively, available data suggest that CircFLNA contributes to NSCLC progression by coordinating autophagy, DNA damage tolerance, and drug resistance through a miRNA-mediated regulatory network [142, 143]. However, the current evidence base is dominated by in vitro models and a single signaling axis, raising important questions regarding clinical relevance, pathway redundancy, and tumor heterogeneity. Future studies comparing CircFLNA function across different NSCLC genetic backgrounds, treatment modalities, and autophagy states will be essential to determine whether CircFLNA represents a broadly actionable therapeutic target or a context-specific modulator of therapy resistance.

CircHIPK3

Several studies have confirmed the crucial role of circular RNA homology domain interacting protein kinase 3 (circHIPK3) in the pathogenesis of various diseases [144–146]. This RNA molecule is derived from the second exon of the HIPK3 gene, which is located on chromosomal region 11p13 [56]. Notably, circHIPK3 acts as an oncogenic factor in multiple cancer types, including NSCLC [147]. Previous studies have demonstrated that circHIPK3 promotes cell proliferation in liver, colon, and cervical cancers by sponging multiple miRNAs [148–151]. This highlights its potential role as a central regulator of cellular growth pathways. Moreover, circHIPK3 expression has been linked to unfavorable clinical outcomes. Elevated levels of this circRNA correlate with enhanced tumor aggressiveness and reduced survival, particularly in advanced stages of NSCLC [152].

Chen et al. reported that circHIPK3 regulated macroautophagy through a ceRNA network [147]. In STK11-mutant lung cancer cell lines (A549 and H838), silencing of circHIPK3 was found to induce autophagy via the miR124-3p/STAT3/PRKAA (AMPKα) axis. In their study, exact molecular mechanisms showed that circHIPK3 acted as a sponge for miR124-3p, reducing its availability to inhibit target mRNAs such as STAT3 and IL6R. Consequently, loss of circHIPK3 elevated miR124-3p activity, which suppressed phospho-STAT3 and enhanced phospho-PRKAA levels, thereby inducing autophagy [147]. Moreover, silencing of circHIPK3 enhanced autophagic activity and suppressed NSCLC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. STAT3 inhibition produced similar effects, indicating the involvement of the STAT3–PRKAA pathway in circHIPK3-mediated autophagy [147].

Taken together, these findings indicate that circHIPK3 acts as an upstream regulator of autophagy in NSCLC via the MIR124-3p/STAT3/PRKAA (AMPKα) axis. It represents a potential therapeutic target, where silencing its expression or disrupting its ceRNA network could enhance autophagy and improve clinical outcomes.

CircEHD2

CircEHD2 has emerged as a putative biomarker across distinct malignancies, including RCC and NSCLC, suggesting a broader association with tumor aggressiveness rather than a tumor-type–restricted function [153, 154]. However, while its diagnostic and prognostic relevance has been proposed in RCC [153], mechanistic insights are currently derived almost exclusively from NSCLC models, highlighting an important gap between clinical association and functional validation. In NSCLC, Zhang et al. observed elevated expression of circEHD2, accompanied by a decline in miR-3186-3p and positioning circEHD2 within the increasingly recognized class of circRNAs that reprogram tumor metabolism via miRNA sequestration [154]. Functionally, circEHD2 promoted glycolysis while concomitantly suppressing autophagy and apoptosis, reinforcing the concept that metabolic activation and autophagy inhibition frequently coexist in rapidly proliferating cancer cells. This dual phenotype aligns with broader oncogenic paradigms in which enhanced glycolytic flux supports biosynthetic demands, while autophagy suppression prevents growth-limiting catabolic processes. Mechanistically, these effects were attributed to the circEHD2/miR-3186-3p/FOXK1 regulatory axis [154]. FOXK1, a proto-oncogene in multiple cancers including breast, liver, and ovarian malignancies [155–157], facilitated glycolytic activity and acted as a suppressor of autophagy. Notably, FOXK1 silencing phenocopied circEHD2 knockdown, restoring autophagic markers (LC3BII, Beclin-1) and reducing glycolytic enzyme expression (HK-2, GLS1), thereby strengthening the causal link between circEHD2-mediated miRNA sequestration and metabolic reprogramming [154]. These findings situate circEHD2 within a conserved oncogenic circuitry in which circRNAs act as upstream modulators of transcriptional programs governing metabolism and autophagy.

Collectively, current evidence positions circEHD2 as a metabolic gatekeeper that couples enhanced glycolysis with restrained autophagic flux in NSCLC cells [154]. However, the heavy reliance on a single investigative framework underscores the need for comparative studies across cancer types, therapeutic contexts, and disease stages. Such efforts will be essential to determine whether circEHD2 represents a broadly actionable regulatory node or a context-specific metabolic modulator.

CircFAM190B

The circFAM190B axis represents a regulatory paradigm that extends well beyond the classical miRNA sponging model and instead exemplifies master regulation at the protein and pathway level. As described by Chen et al., circFAM190B is markedly upregulated in lung cancer tissues and cell lines and positively correlates with tumor stage and size. Functional assays further demonstrate that circFAM190B promotes tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo [158]. However, its mechanistic significance lies not merely in its oncogenic association, but in the hierarchical level at which it exerts control.

Unlike circRNAs that primarily influence gene expression indirectly via miRNA sequestration, circFAM190B directly interacts with the signaling protein stratifin (SFN/14-3-3σ). This interaction prevents SFN ubiquitination, thereby inhibiting its proteasomal degradation and resulting in sustained accumulation of SFN protein [158]. Importantly, circFAM190B does not regulate SFN at the transcriptional or translational level; instead, it controls SFN protein stability, positioning circFAM190B upstream of post-translational proteostasis mechanisms. This mode of action qualifies circFAM190B as a molecular scaffold that stabilizes a key signaling hub, rather than a passive RNA decoy.

Stabilized SFN subsequently exerts broad downstream effects by reshaping the mTOR–ULK1 signaling axis, a central regulator of cellular metabolism and autophagy. SFN binds to mTOR within the mTORC1 complex and enhances the interaction between mTOR and ULK1 This interaction results in inhibitory phosphorylation of ULK1 and disrupts formation of the ULK1–ATG13 complex, which is essential for autophagy initiation [158–160]. Consequently, autophagy is suppressed at an early and decisive checkpoint. Because mTOR and ULK1 function as core signaling nodes, circFAM190B-mediated SFN stabilization effectively locks the autophagy pathway in an inactive state, favoring anabolic growth and tumor cell survival [158].

Notably, this regulatory cascade is further reinforced by epitranscriptomic control. Chen et al. demonstrated that circFAM190B carries 5-methylcytosine (m5C) modifications primarily mediated by NSUN2. m5C modification enhances circFAM190B stability in an NSUN2-dependent manner, thereby sustaining its intracellular abundance [158]. NSUN2 itself is known to regulate RNA stability, nuclear export, and non-coding RNA maturation, underscoring its broad impact on RNA biology [161]. Through this epitranscriptomic mechanism, circFAM190B maintains prolonged SFN stabilization and persistent suppression of the mTOR/ULK1-driven autophagy program.

Collectively, these findings establish circFAM190B as a central oncogenic circRNA that integrates multiple regulatory layers: epitranscriptomic modification (m5C), post-translational protein stabilization (inhibition of SFN ubiquitination), and pathway-level signaling control (mTOR/ULK1-mediated autophagy inhibition). This multi-tiered regulatory architecture enables circFAM190B to function as a master regulator of cellular fate, coordinating protein stability and metabolic signaling to sustain tumor growth and survival. Thus, circFAM190B exemplifies how circRNAs can orchestrate complex regulatory networks beyond miRNA sponging, acting instead as structural and functional hubs within oncogenic signaling pathways.

Hsa_circ_0096157

In NSCLC, hsa_circ_0096157 was identified as a critical circRNA contributing to cisplatin resistance. In cisplatin-resistant NSCLC models, Lu et al. identified hsa_circ_0096157 as an oncogenic circRNA that enhances proliferation and suppresses apoptosis while sustaining antioxidant defenses through the Nrf2/ARE pathway [162]. This observation aligns with a growing body of evidence positioning Nrf2 as a central node in tumor survival, particularly under therapeutic stress, through its transcriptional control of cytoprotective genes such as HO-1 and NQO1 [163, 164]. In NSCLC, hyperactivation of Nrf2 through mutational and epigenetic mechanisms supported tumor progression and survival under stress conditions [165].

Within this broader framework, hsa_circ_0096157 functions as a competing endogenous RNA by sequestering miR-142-5p and miR-548n, thereby relieving repression of Nrf2 expression. While this miRNA-sponging mechanism mirrors that reported for several other circRNAs in lung cancer, the biological significance here lies less in the novelty of the interaction and more in its downstream consequences: sustained Nrf2 signaling promotes autophagic competence during cisplatin exposure. This reinforces the emerging concept that oxidative stress responses and autophagy are not independent survival programs but are functionally intertwined. Indeed, activation of the Nrf2/ARE axis has been shown to enhance autophagic flux in cisplatin-treated NSCLC cells, suggesting a feed-forward loop that buffers redox imbalance while facilitating metabolic adaptation. Experimental validation in A549/DDP cells further supports this integrated model. Cisplatin-induced upregulation of hsa_circ_0096157 coincided with increased LC3-II/I and Beclin-1 levels and reduced p62 accumulation, indicating enhanced autophagic turnover. Conversely, circRNA silencing impaired autophagy, reduced proliferation, and partially restored cisplatin sensitivity. Pharmacological modulation of autophagy—either inhibition or induction—confirmed that autophagic activity is not merely correlative but functionally required for hsa_circ_0096157-mediated chemoresistance. Rescue experiments using Nrf2 overexpression further placed this transcription factor downstream of the circRNA–miRNA axis, reinforcing its role as a key effector rather than a parallel pathway [162]. Taken together, hsa_circ_0096157 should be viewed not as an isolated example but as part of a broader regulatory motif in which circRNAs stabilize redox homeostasis and autophagic capacity to sustain malignant phenotypes under chemotherapeutic stress. As noted in the text, most of the autophagy-related circRNAs currently identified in lung cancer exhibit oncogenic properties, while only a few, such as CircPLCE1, have been reported to have tumor suppressive effects. This imbalance may partly reflect the biological role of autophagy in lung tumors, where increased autophagy activity often enhances tumor cell survival, metabolic adaptation, and therapeutic resistance. However, publication bias and methodological bias may also play a role, as transcriptional studies often prioritize highly upregulated circRNAs with prominent carcinogenic phenotypes. Therefore, other tumor suppressor circRNAs that regulate autophagy in lung cancer may not yet be identified.

Combined analysis of multiple circRNAs

A recent study identified six circRNAs as potential regulators in NSCLC patients undergoing radiotherapy. These included hsa_circ_0019709, hsa_circ_0040569, hsa_circ_0081983, hsa_circ_0098966, hsa_circ_0112354, and hsa_circ_0135500. Identification of these circRNAs in NSCLC patients undergoing radiotherapy points to a convergent regulatory strategy whereby CircRNAs collectively fine-tune autophagy through shared miRNA pools and overlapping downstream targets. Notably, these circRNAs do not converge on a single autophagy-related gene (ARmRNAs), but instead regulate distinct ARmRNAs—SESN3, ELAPOR1, and SNCA—that are associated with different autophagy subtypes, suggesting a layered and potentially compensatory control of autophagic flux [167]. These circRNAs were found to interact with miRNAs such as miR-194-3p, miR-205-5p, and miR-26a-1-3p, which in turn target ARmRNAs including SESN3, ELAPOR1, and SNCA. Through this ceRNA mechanism, circRNAs can modulate autophagic processes, potentially affecting how tumor cells respond to radiation [167]. A common theme emerging from this network is the preferential targeting of stress-adaptive autophagy pathways. SESN3, which is induced under cellular stress and positively regulates macroautophagy [168] and EIG121, which is associated with endosome–lysosome dynamics and may promote cytoprotective autophagy [169]. both align with a model in which circRNA-mediated miRNA sequestration enhances tumor cell survival during radiotherapy. In contrast, SNCA negatively regulates CMA [170], introducing an apparent discrepancy: circRNA-driven upregulation of SNCA could suppress selective protein degradation while macroautophagy remains active. This divergence raises the unresolved question of whether radiotherapy-resistant NSCLC cells benefit more from bulk autophagy than from CMA, or whether suppression of CMA serves to stabilize oncogenic proteins under treatment-induced stress. Bioinformatics analyses have indicated differential expression of autophagy-related genes (ARGs) and miRNAs between radiotherapy-resistant and radiotherapy-sensitive NSCLC patients. Using nine ARmRNAs/miRNAs, an autophagy-related radiosensitivity risk signature (ARRS) was constructed, achieving an estimated AUC of 0.8854 (95% CI: 0.8131–0.9576). Additionally, a three-gene autophagy-related overall survival risk signature (AROS) was developed, with an AUC of 0.7901 (95% CI: 0.7168–0.8685). These signatures correlated with clinicopathological factors, short-term radiotherapy response, overall survival, and immune characteristics, including immune checkpoint expression and tumor-infiltrating immune cells [167].

Collectively, this study supports a model in which circRNAs participate in a distributed regulatory network that modulates multiple autophagy subtypes under radiotherapy stress, rather than functioning as isolated molecular switches. However, discrepancies in the directionality of autophagy regulation (macroautophagy versus CMA), the lack of functional validation for individual circRNAs, and the unclear contribution of immune-related effects highlight critical gaps. Addressing these questions will be essential to determine whether circRNA-mediated autophagy represents a tractable therapeutic target or primarily a biomarker of radiotherapy response in NSCLC.

Autophagy-associated circular RNAs as emerging therapeutic targets in lung cancer

Recent research has highlighted a strong association between circRNAs and autophagy in lung cancer. Autophagy-regulating circRNAs influence several major signaling pathways that control tumor growth, apoptosis, metastasis, and resistance to therapy [171]. Beyond tumor tissues, circRNAs can also be detected in biological fluids, including blood, serum, saliva, and urine. Due to their high availability and stability, autophagy-related circRNAs have attracted attention as potential biomarkers for assessing tumor progression, predicting treatment outcomes, and detecting disease recurrence [22]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that specific circRNAs display distinct expression profiles in lung cancer patients compared with healthy individuals, and these alterations are frequently associated with tumor stage, metastatic potential, and patient survival. Among these molecules, circPLCE1 has emerged as an important autophagy-associated circRNA in lung cancer. Its expression is markedly reduced in tumor tissues relative to adjacent normal tissues, with the decrease becoming more pronounced in advanced disease stages (II–IV). In addition, circPLCE1 levels are inversely related to tumor size (r = − 0.3630, P = 0.0017). Clinically, reduced circPLCE1 expression has been linked to shorter overall survival, while receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated favorable prognostic capability (AUC = 0.7808, P < 0.001). These observations suggest that circPLCE1 may serve as both a prognostic indicator and a treatment-related biomarker in lung cancer [111]. Another circRNA of interest is circ-FOXM1, which has been characterized as an oncogenic regulator in NSCLC. Evidence indicates that circ-FOXM1 enhances autophagy and accelerates tumor progression through suppression of miR-149-5p, thereby increasing the expression of the autophagy-related gene ATG5. Elevated ATG5 promotes autophagosome formation, supporting cancer cell survival and proliferation. Consequently, circ-FOXM1 contributes to aggressive tumor behavior and unfavorable clinical outcomes in patients with lung cancer [172]. Similarly, circ_0003028 has been recognized as a cancer-promoting circRNA in NSCLC. Studies revealed elevated expression of both circ_0003028 and glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase 2 (GOT2) in NSCLC tissues and cell lines, whereas miR-1298-5p expression was significantly reduced. Functional investigations demonstrated that silencing circ_0003028 suppressed cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, colony formation, and angiogenesis, while simultaneously promoting apoptosis and autophagy. Enhanced autophagy was evidenced by increased Beclin-1 expression and a higher LC3-II/LC3-I ratio. Mechanistically, circ_0003028 acts as a sponge for miR-1298-5p, thereby weakening its inhibitory effect on GOT2. Through regulation of the miR-1298-5p/GOT2 signaling axis, circ_0003028 facilitates malignant progression in NSCLC. In vivo experiments further confirmed that knockdown of circ_0003028 significantly inhibited tumor growth, underscoring its contribution to NSCLC development and autophagy regulation [171]. In addition, Chen and colleagues demonstrated that circFAM190B expression is substantially elevated in lung cancer tissues compared with adjacent non-cancerous samples. Clinical analyses showed a positive association between circFAM190B levels, tumor size, and advanced disease stage, indicating its involvement in tumor progression. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis further revealed that patients with high circFAM190B expression experienced significantly poorer overall survival. ROC curve analysis also supported its diagnostic relevance, with an AUC value of 0.7928 (P < 0.001). So circFAM190B is possible as a clinically significant autophagy-related circRNA with both diagnostic and prognostic potential in lung cancer [158]. Taken together, these findings suggests that autophagy-associated circRNAs may become valuable tools in precision medicine. Their detection through minimally invasive approaches could help clinicians personalize therapeutic strategies, improve treatment selection, and enhance patient outcomes in lung cancer.

Chemotherapy is a common treatment for cancer, but drug resistance often prevents chemotherapy from being effective, making it difficult to treat tumors [173]. Cisplatin is one of the most widely used chemotherapeutic agents for the treatment of lung cancer, but cisplatin resistance often develops during treatment [174, 175]. Studies have reported that autophagy-related circRNAs can be used as targets to reduce drug resistance in cancer therapy. circRNA_100565 plays an important role in cisplatin resistance in NSCLC. CircRNA_100565 promotes autophagy and inhibits apoptosis through the regulation of the miR-337-3p/ADAM28 signaling axis. Increased autophagy enables cancer cells to survive chemotherapy-induced stress, thereby contributing to drug resistance [137]. hsa_circ_0085131 is another circRNA implicated in cisplatin resistance in NSCLC cells. It has been shown that hsa_circ_0085131 expression is significantly increased in cisplatin-resistant NSCLC cells compared with sensitive cells. Functional analyses revealed that silencing hsa_circ_0085131 reduced cell viability and increased sensitivity of cisplatin-resistant NSCLC cells. In contrast, overexpression of hsa_circ_0085131 increased chemotherapy resistance and supported malignant cell behaviors. Mechanistic studies revealed that hsa_circ_0085131 plays a role in chemotherapy resistance by modulating autophagy-related pathways. Inhibition of hsa_circ_0085131 suppressed autophagy activity, as evidenced by altered expression of autophagy markers, including decreased LC3-II levels and increased p62 accumulation, indicating impaired autophagy [107]. In another study, hsa_circ_0096157 was identified as one of the molecules involved in cisplatin resistance in lung cancer. Mechanistic investigations revealed that hsa_circ_0096157 activated autophagy through activation of the Nrf2/ARE pathway, thereby maintaining cell survival under cisplatin treatment and enhancing resistance to chemotherapy. These findings suggested that autophagy activation plays a protective role in resistant NSCLC cells by enhancing cell survival during cisplatin exposure [162]. Therefore, combining circRNA-targeted therapies with conventional chemotherapy may enhance therapeutic efficacy and overcome resistance mechanisms.

Despite significant advances in this field, several important obstacles must be addressed before circRNA-based strategies can be implemented in clinical settings. One major limitation is that the biological functions and regulatory mechanisms of many circRNAs remain insufficiently characterized. In addition, the development of efficient, targeted, and safe delivery systems for circRNA-modulating agents is still in its early stages, limiting their therapeutic applicability. Another complication arises from the dual and context-dependent role of autophagy in cancer biology, as both excessive activation and inhibition of this process may lead to undesirable or even counterproductive outcomes. Furthermore, robust validation through large-scale, multi-center clinical trials is essential to confirm the reliability of autophagy-related circRNAs as diagnostic or therapeutic biomarkers in patients with lung cancer [176].

Conclusions and perspectives

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related incidence and mortality worldwide. Targeting autophagy, despite being controversial, is increasingly explored as a therapeutic strategy. Current research focuses on small molecules that exploit the dual role of autophagy. Identifying reliable compounds capable of modulating key components of autophagy is essential to better understand the consequences of its pharmacological regulation and to translate these findings into clinical applications.

CircRNAs are involved in autophagy regulation through multiple signaling pathways. circRNA-mediated autophagy influences various aspects of tumor progression, showing both pro- and anti-tumor effects depending on the context. Understanding how circRNA-mediated autophagy affects tumorigenesis and cancer progression is crucial for developing novel circRNA-based therapeutic strategies. Evidence suggests that specific circRNAs regulate autophagy in lung cancer, affecting chemo- and radio-resistance. Most studies indicate that circRNAs modulate autophagy primarily by sponging miRNAs and interacting with RNA-binding proteins. This circRNA–autophagy axis exhibits context-dependent effects, which can influence treatment outcomes. Therefore, targeting this axis may improve the efficacy of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and new antineoplastic drugs when used in combination.

Future research should aim to identify the circRNAs that are most critically involved in autophagy-related resistance mechanisms in lung cancer and evaluate their potential as diagnostic markers, prognostic indicators, or therapeutic targets. Particular attention should be directed toward circRNAs associated with major autophagy-regulating pathways such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR, AMPK, MAPK, and HIF-1α signaling. Although several circRNAs have already been implicated in resistance to cisplatin, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and radiotherapy, the molecular basis of their actions and their clinical significance are still not fully understood. Consequently, comprehensive studies involving large and well-characterized patient cohorts are required to determine the relationship between circRNA expression patterns, histological subtype, disease progression, therapeutic response, and overall survival. In parallel, integrating multi-omics technologies, including transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and single-cell analyses, may provide a more complete understanding of how circRNAs interact with autophagy pathways and the tumor microenvironment during lung cancer progression.

The application of advanced experimental models will be essential for enhancing the clinical relevance of future investigations. Experimental systems including patient-derived organoids, xenograft models, and genetically engineered mouse models are expected to more accurately reflect tumor heterogeneity and provide improved platforms for studying the influence of circRNAs on autophagy and therapeutic responsiveness in vivo. Although circRNA-based therapeutic strategies have attracted increasing attention, several barriers still hinder their translation into clinical practice. Many circRNAs are expressed at relatively low levels, which complicates their reliable detection and selective functional inhibition. Furthermore, the lack of unified methodologies for circRNA isolation, quantification, and validation contributes to inconsistencies across studies. Additional challenges include limited understanding of circRNA degradation dynamics and stability, together with difficulties in achieving precise and efficient delivery to tumor tissues. These limitations are especially significant in the setting of therapeutic resistance, a major factor contributing to treatment failure in lung cancer. Cisplatin resistance, in particular, has been linked to circRNAs such as hsa_circ_0085131 and hsa_circ_0096157, which may enhance cancer cell survival by regulating autophagy-associated pathways. Because resistance to cisplatin is commonly associated with disease recurrence, metastasis, and unfavorable prognosis, targeting circRNA-mediated autophagic signaling could provide new therapeutic opportunities. Among the proposed approaches, antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) and CRISPR/Cas-based technologies have emerged as promising strategies for circRNA targeting. ASOs are short synthetic nucleic acid sequences capable of binding specifically to circRNAs, leading either to their degradation or to the disruption of their interactions with miRNAs and RNA-binding proteins. Through these mechanisms, ASOs may inhibit signaling pathways involved in autophagy and chemoresistance while maintaining high target specificity and minimizing off-target effects on linear RNAs. Gene-editing technologies based on CRISPR/Cas9 may also interfere with circRNA production by modifying splice sites or intronic complementary regions necessary for RNA circularization. In parallel, RNA-targeting systems such as CRISPR/Cas13 offer the possibility of selectively degrading circRNAs without introducing permanent genomic alterations. Collectively, these approaches may suppress autophagy-related mechanisms that contribute to cisplatin resistance in lung cancer. Despite their therapeutic potential, efficient and safe delivery of ASOs or CRISPR components to lung tumors remains a critical challenge. To overcome this limitation, several delivery systems are currently under investigation, including inhalable lipid nanoparticles, polymer-based nanocarriers, viral vectors, and engineered exosomes, all of which may facilitate localized delivery to pulmonary tissues while reducing systemic adverse effects. Continued advances in nanotechnology, exosome engineering, and gene-editing platforms may therefore accelerate the clinical development of circRNA-directed therapies. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of the interplay between circRNAs and autophagy could support the development of personalized therapeutic approaches aimed at overcoming treatment resistance and improving outcomes for patients with lung cancer.
