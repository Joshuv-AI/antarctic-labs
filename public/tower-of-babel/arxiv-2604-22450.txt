Exploring Cosmic Evolution in Rényi Entropic Cosmology with Constraints from DESI DR2 BAO and GW Data
Rajdeep Mazumdar, Kalyan Malakar, Kalyan Bhuyan

Abstract
We explore a cosmological model based on Rényi entropic corrections to the Friedmann equations and constrain it using DESI, P-BAO, CC, and gravitational-wave observations. Unlike earlier works, we obtain a direct and stringent constraint on the Rényi parameter λ \lambda from late-time cosmic acceleration, with the resulting value even satisfying recent Big Bang Nucleosynthesis and baryogenesis bounds. The model predicts values of H 0 H_{0} and Ω m ​ 0 \Omega_{m0} that are consistent with current observational data. It provides a successful description of late-time acceleration with a quintessence-like behavior, smoothly approaching the Λ \Lambda CDM limit without crossing the phantom divide. Furthermore, the statical comparisons along with the evaluation of energy conditions and stability analyses reinforce its viability as a robust alternative to the standard cosmological model.



Keywords:
Rényi Entropy, gravity-thermodynamics conjecture, cosmic acceleration; observational constraints

I
Introduction

The Universe is currently undergoing an accelerated phase of expansion, as demonstrated by measurements from Type Ia supernovae (SN Ia)
riess1998
;
perlmutter1999
, the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)
bennett2003
;
spergel2003
, Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO)
eisenstein2005
;
percival2010
, and Large-Scale Structure (LSS) surveys, among other recent developments in observational cosmology. Dark energy (DE), an enigmatic component that makes up around 70% of the universe’s total energy density, is frequently attributed as a cause for this late-time acceleration. Despite its remarkable success in explaining observational data, the fundamental nature and origin of dark energy remain elusive from a theoretical perspective. The cosmological constant (
\Lambda
), which serves as the foundation for the conventional
\Lambda
CDM model, is the most straightforward and commonly recognised candidate for dark energy. Excellent agreement with a wide variety of cosmological observations from various eras has been shown by this hypothesis. Nevertheless, the
\Lambda
CDM framework has a number of serious conceptual problems even with its practical effectiveness. The most well-known of these is the cosmological constant issue, which results from the huge difference between the observed and theoretically expected levels of vacuum energy density
weinberg1989
;
Martin2012
. Further challenging our comprehension are the fine-tuning and cosmic coincidence problems
weinberg1989
;
carroll1992
;
sahni2000
, which ask why the current value of dark energy density is so perfectly tuned and why its dominance corresponds with the current period of cosmic evolution. Such theoretical challenges shows the need for the exploration of other theoretical possibilities causing the late-time cosmic acceleration, outside the domain of standard
\Lambda
CDM paradigm. Broadly, such approaches can be classified into two main categories. The first involves introducing dynamical dark energy components with evolving equations of state, including models such as quintessence, phantom fields, k-essence
ArmendarizPicon2000
, tachyon fields
Sen2002
, chameleon fields
Khoury2004
, and Chaplygin gas models
Kamenshchik2001
;
Bento2002
. The second approach focuses on modifying the underlying theory of gravity itself, leading to various extended or alternative gravitational frameworks that aim to naturally explain the observed acceleration without invoking an explicit dark energy component.

In recent years due to insights from black hole physics, an alternative and conceptually intriguing approach has emerged from the interplay between gravity and thermodynamics. Since Bardeen
et al.
developed black hole mechanics and introduced Bekenstein–Hawking entropy
bardeen1973
;
bekenstein1973
;
hawking1975
, the relationship between spacetime dynamics and thermodynamic principles has been more apparent. Concepts like the holographic principle, gauge/gravity duality, and quantum information-theoretic approaches to gravity have further reinforced this connection
tHooft1993
;
susskind1995
;
maldacena1999
;
almheiri2013
.
Jacobson’s groundbreaking study showed that the first rule of thermodynamics applied to local Rindler horizons can yield Einstein’s field equations
jacobson1995
, indicating that gravity itself might be an emergent phenomena. Further, the Friedmann equations can be found by applying thermodynamic rules to the apparent horizon of a Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) universe
cai2005
;
akbar2006
. Such a thermodynamic approach has been effectively extended to a broad class of modified gravity theories
sheykhi2018
;
nojiri2017
and offers a coherent framework in which cosmic dynamics are guided by horizon thermodynamics. Modifications to the entropy–area relation naturally result in different cosmic dynamics under this thermodynamic framework. A number of generalised entropy formalisms, including Tsallis, Rényi, Barrow, Kaniadakis, and Sharma–Mittal entropies, as well as corrections inspired by loop quantum gravity
tsallis1988
;
renyi1961
;
barrow2020
;
kaniadakis2002
;
sharma1975
;
rovelli1996
, have been proposed in response to the long-range nature of gravity and potential quantum gravitational effects. These generalised entropies capture non-extensive and information-theoretic aspects of gravitational systems and, in suitable situations, reduce to the standard Bekenstein–Hawking entropy. They result in modified Friedmann equations and effective energy components that can drive acceleration in both early and late times when applied to the thermodynamics of cosmic horizons. These modifications typically take the shape of logarithmic and power-law changes. Specifically, the effects of long-range correlations are captured by power-law corrections that result from the entanglement of quantum fields both inside and outside the horizon
x13
;
x14
. However, the uncertainty principle, quantum fluctuations, and thermal equilibrium fluctuations in loop quantum gravity naturally lead to logarithmic corrections
x16
;
x21
. In quantum information and communication theory, the Shannon entropy, which is a member of the class of logarithmic entropies, is essential and meets a number of crucial operational requirements
x22
. However, Shannon entropy might not be sufficient to describe non-asymptotic regimes, where the rule of big numbers is not applicable. Alternative entropy metrics, including collision entropy and other generalised non-extensive formulations, become more significant in these situations. Motivated by these considerations, Rényi
renyi1961
introduced a generalized entropy formalism that unifies various entropy measures within a single consistent framework, where the entropy associated with the horizon can be expressed as
renyi1961
:

S_{R}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\left(1+\lambda S_{BH}\right),

(1)

where,
S_{BH}
is the standard Bekenstein–Hawking entropy, and
\lambda
is the Rényi parameter that characterises deviations from the standard entropy. The conventional thermodynamic description is recovered when the Rényi entropy drops to the standard Bekenstein–Hawking entropy in the limiting scenario
\lambda\to 0
.

Rényi entropy
renyi1961
, originally introduced by Alfréd Rényi, serves as a one-parameter generalization of the Boltzmann–Gibbs entropy and provides a consistent framework to incorporate non-extensive and information-theoretic effects into gravitational dynamics. A number of studies have explored its implications across different cosmic epochs. For instance, investigations of Rényi entropy in Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN) and baryogenesis
n1
have placed important constraints on the Rényi parameter, demonstrating its viability in describing early Universe thermodynamics without conflicting with primordial element abundances. Furthermore, works on Rényi entropy-corrected expanding universe models
n2
have shown that such corrections can significantly affect the evolution of the Hubble parameter and deceleration parameter, offering distinguishable signatures from standard
\Lambda
CDM cosmology. In addition, Rényi entropy has been explored in the framework of inflationary cosmology
n3
, where it provides alternative mechanisms for realizing inflationary dynamics consistent with observational constraints. The connection between Rényi entropy and holographic dark energy models
n4
has also been widely studied, particularly in flat spacetime scenarios, where it modifies the infrared cutoff and leads to improved agreement with observational data. More recently, Rényi entropy has been proposed as a tool to quantify cosmic homogeneity
n5
, linking information-theoretic measures with large-scale structure formation. These diverse applications collectively highlight that Rényi entropy not only offers a unifying thermodynamic description but also serves as a powerful phenomenological framework to probe deviations from standard cosmology across both early- and late-time regimes.

Despite these promising developments, a comprehensive assessment of Rényi entropic cosmology, together with direct and stringent constraints on the Rényi parameter
\lambda
in light of the latest high-precision observational data remains relatively unexplored. Recent advances in observational cosmology have significantly strengthened constraints on theoretical models of cosmic acceleration. In particular, the DESI DR2 dataset
ref90
provides some of the most stringent bounds through its high-precision Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO) measurements, and has been widely employed to test extensions beyond the
\Lambda
CDM paradigm, including dynamical dark energy, early dark energy scenarios, scalar field models, and modified gravity frameworks
refk58
;
refk65
;
refk68
;
refk69
. Moreover, gravitational wave (GW) observations
g1
;
g2
offer an independent and complementary probe of the cosmic expansion history through the standard siren approach. The GW dataset, compiled from detections by LIGO and Virgo across multiple observing runs (O1, O2, and O3), provides direct measurements of luminosity distance from compact binary mergers extending up to redshift
z\sim 0.9
. Motivated by this, it is both timely and essential to investigate the viability of Rényi entropic cosmology at late times and to place stringent observational constraints on its parameters using these recent high-precision datasets. Such an analysis can provide deeper insights into the role of entropy-based modifications in cosmic evolution and assess their potential as viable alternatives to the standard
\Lambda
CDM framework.

II
Rényi Entropic Cosmology

We consider a spatially homogeneous and isotropic Universe described by the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) metric
renyi1961
;
n1
,

ds^{2}=h_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}+R^{2}\left(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2}\right),

(2)

here,
R=a(t)r
,
x^{0}=t
,
x^{1}=r
, with the two-dimensional metric taking the form
h_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{diag}\left(-1,\frac{a^{2}}{1-kr^{2}}\right)
. The spatial curvature parameter
k=-1,0,+1
represents open, flat, and closed geometries, respectively. And, radius of the apparent horizon can be expressed as
renyi1961
;
n1
;
n2
:

R=\frac{1}{\sqrt{H^{2}+\frac{k}{a^{2}}}},

(3)

where
H=\dot{a}/a
denotes the Hubble parameter. And, the corresponding temperature, given in terms of the surface gravity, is expressed as:

T_{h}=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi}=-\frac{1}{2\pi R}\left(1-\frac{\dot{R}}{2HR}\right).

(4)

In the quasi-static limit
\dot{R}\ll 2HR
, one recovers
T_{h}=1/(2\pi R)
. We assume that the cosmic fluid is described by the perfect fluid energy–momentum tensor,

T_{\mu\nu}=(\rho+p)u_{\mu}u_{\nu}+pg_{\mu\nu},

(5)

here the the energy density and pressure of the cosmic fluid are
\rho
and
p
respectively. Conservation of energy–momentum
renyi1961
;
n1
,

\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=0
, leads to the continuity equation:

\dot{\rho}+3H(\rho+p)=0.

(6)

The work density is defined as:

W=-\frac{1}{2}T^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}(\rho-p).

(7)

Applying the first law of thermodynamics on the apparent horizon
renyi1961
;
n1
;
n2
,

dE=T_{h}dS_{h}+WdV,

(8)

where
E=\frac{4\pi}{3}\rho R^{3}
and
V=\frac{4\pi}{3}R^{3}
, we obtain:

dE=4\pi R^{2}\rho\,dR-4\pi HR^{3}(\rho+p)\,dt.

(9)

To incorporate non-extensive effects, we consider the Rényi entropy (Eq.
1
) associated with the horizon, given up to leading order by
renyi1961
;
n1
:

S_{h}=S-\frac{\lambda}{2}S^{2},

(10)

where
S=\frac{A}{4G}=\frac{\pi R^{2}}{G}
. Its differential form is

dS_{h}=(1-\lambda S)\,dS,

(11)

with

dS=\frac{2\pi R\dot{R}}{G}dt.

(12)

Substituting the above relations into the first law and simplifying, we obtain
renyi1961
;
n1
:

(1-\lambda S)\frac{dR}{R^{3}}=4\pi GH(\rho+p)\,dt.

(13)

Using the continuity equation, this can be rewritten as
renyi1961
:

-\frac{2\,dR}{R^{3}}\left(1-\alpha R^{2}\right)=\frac{8\pi G}{3}d\rho,

(14)

where
\alpha=\frac{\lambda\pi}{G}
. Integrating, we obtain

\frac{1}{R^{2}}-\alpha\ln\left(\frac{1}{R^{2}}\right)=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho+\frac{\Lambda}{3},

(15)

where
\Lambda
is an integration constant. Substituting
R^{-2}=H^{2}+\frac{k}{a^{2}}
, the modified Friedmann equation becomes:

H^{2}+\frac{k}{a^{2}}-\alpha\ln\left(H^{2}+\frac{k}{a^{2}}\right)=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho+\frac{\Lambda}{3},

(16)

with now
\Lambda
being nothing but the cosmological constant of the standard
\Lambda
CDM model. Where, for
\alpha\to 0
the above equations reduces to the standard form. For a flat Universe
(k=0)
with the absence of the cosmological constant
(\Lambda=0)
, this reduces to
n1
:

H^{2}-\alpha\ln H^{2}=\frac{8\pi G}{3}(\rho).

(17)

Using Eqs. (
6
) & (
17
) the second Friedmann equation follows as
n1
:

\dot{H}\left(1-\alpha H^{-2}\right)=-4\pi G(\rho+p).

(18)

Literature shows that the modified Friedmann equation given by Eq. (
17
) can be recast as:

\left(\frac{H}{H_{0}}\right)^{2}=\Omega_{m0}(1+z)^{3}+\Omega_{r0}(1+z)^{4}+\frac{8{\pi}^{2}\lambda\ln H^{2}}{{H_{0}}^{2}}

(19)

where, we consider
8\pi G=1
. With the present value of the Hubble parameter given by
H_{0}
,and the present values of the radiation and matter density parameters are denoted by
\Omega_{r0}
and
\Omega_{m0}
, respectively.

Employing the condition
H(z)=H_{0}
at
z=0
in Eq. (
19
), we can obtain the parameter
\lambda
as:

\lambda=\frac{{H_{0}}^{2}\left(1-\Omega_{m0}-\Omega_{r0}\right)}{8\pi^{2}\ln{H_{0}}^{2}}

(20)

So, using Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
) we obtain:

\left(\frac{H(z)}{H_{0}}\right)^{2}=\Omega_{m0}(1+z)^{3}+\Omega_{r0}(1+z)^{4}+\ln\left(H^{2}-{H_{0}}^{2}\right)\left(1-\Omega_{m0}-\Omega_{r0}\right),

(21)

Equation (
21
) is an implicit equation for
H(z)
. Therefore, we calculate this equation numerically at each redshift
z_{i}
for given model parameters
\theta=\{\Omega_{m0},H_{0}\}
in order to derive the theoretical Hubble values
H_{th}(z_{i})
. These values are then utilised to calculate the required observables, which are then compared with observational datasets to determine the best fitting parameter values for the specified model using the previously stated
\chi^{2}
functions and the MCMC approach.

III
Observational Data And Methodology for Constraints

In this study, we use a combination of cosmic chronometer (CC), Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO), and gravitational-wave (GW) standard siren datasets to constrain the free parameters of the proposed cosmological model. The most recent DESI DR2 dataset
ref90
, which offers high-precision measurements, and an earlier compilation (P-BAO) comprising observations from SDSS, WiggleZ, DES, and related surveys
d113
–
d79
, are the sources of the BAO data. The CC data provide a direct probe of the expansion history by providing separate estimations of the Hubble parameter H(z) based on the differential ages of passively evolving galaxies
x38
. Additionally, gravitational-wave observational provide an independent and complementary probe through standard sirens. We use publicly accessible data for the O1, O2, and O3 observing runs from the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA catalogues (GWTC-1, GWTC-2, GWTC-2.1, and GWTC-3)
g1
;
g2
. Together, these datasets allow for a reliable reconstruction of the history of cosmic expansion over a large redshift range. To assess the performance of the given cosmological model, we perform three independent analyses:

•

DESI,

•

P-BAO,

•

DESI + P-BAO + CC + GW.

For the BAO analysis, we employ the observables such as Hubble parameter
H(z)
, the Hubble distance
D_{H}(z)
, the transverse comoving distance
D_{M}(z)
, and the volume-averaged distance
D_{V}(z)
. These quantities are normalized with respect to the sound horizon at the drag epoch
r_{d}
, which is given as:

r_{d}=\int_{z_{d}}^{\infty}\frac{c_{s}(z^{\prime})}{H(z^{\prime})}\,dz^{\prime},

(22)

where
c_{s}(z)
denotes the sound speed of the baryon–photon fluid. The relevant distance measures are given by

D_{H}(z)=\frac{c}{H(z)},

(23)

D_{M}(z)=c\int_{0}^{z}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{H(z^{\prime})},

(24)

and

D_{V}(z)=\left[z\,D_{M}^{2}(z)\,D_{H}(z)\right]^{1/3}.

(25)

Baryon Acoustic Oscillations are caused by sound waves propagating in the closely linked photon–baryon plasma before the drag epoch, leaving a distinctive comoving scale (
r_{d}
) imprinted in the Cosmic Microwave Background and the Universe’s large-scale structure. BAO observations are reliable standard rulers for examining the history of cosmic expansion because of their geometric nature and low sensitivity to astrophysical systematics. For convenience, we limit our study to the previously described datasets, even though additional probes, such Type Ia supernovae, can potentially offer useful restrictions.

For the GW analysis, we employ luminosity distance measurements derived from the GWTC catalogs, adopting median values along with their corresponding
1\sigma
uncertainties obtained through Bayesian inference. The redshift information is either obtained from electromagnetic counterparts or statistically inferred for binary black hole mergers. In this framework, GW observations act as distance–redshift probes, with the theoretical luminosity distance given by

d_{L}(z)=(1+z)\int_{0}^{z}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{H(z^{\prime})}.

(26)

The corresponding distance modulus is defined as

\mu=5\log_{10}d_{L}(z)+\mu_{0},

(27)

where

\mu_{0}=5\log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{H_{0}\,\mathrm{Mpc}}\right)+25,

(28)

and
H_{0}
denotes the present Hubble parameter.

III.1
CC Dataset

The cosmic chronometer (CC) data provide direct measurements of the Hubble parameter
H(z)
with uncorrelated Gaussian uncertainties. The corresponding chi-square function is defined as

\chi^{2}_{\rm CC}(\theta)=\sum_{j}\frac{\left[H^{\rm obs}(z_{j})-H^{\rm th}(z_{j},\theta)\right]^{2}}{\sigma_{H,j}^{2}},

(29)

where
H^{\rm obs}(z_{j})
and
\sigma_{H,j}
denote the observed Hubble parameter and its uncertainty at redshift
z_{j}
, respectively, while
H^{\rm th}(z_{j},\theta)
represents the theoretical prediction of the model with parameter set
\theta
.

III.2
DESI Dataset

There is a correlation between the BAO observables in the DESI DR2 dataset. As a result, a complete covariance matrix treatment is necessary. Hence, the chi-square function is defiend for it as:

\chi^{2}_{\rm DESI}(\theta)=\sum_{i,j}\left(X^{\rm obs}_{i}-X^{\rm th}_{i}(\theta)\right)C^{-1}_{ij}\left(X^{\rm obs}_{j}-X^{\rm th}_{j}(\theta)\right),

(30)

where
X^{\rm obs}=\{D_{M}(z)/r_{d},\,D_{H}(z)/r_{d},\,D_{V}(z)/r_{d}\}
denotes the observed BAO quantities,
X^{\rm th}
represents the corresponding theoretical predictions, and
C^{-1}_{ij}
is the inverse covariance matrix provided by the DESI collaboration.

III.3
P-BAO Dataset

In contrast to the DESI DR2 dataset, the previous BAO (P-BAO) compilation utilised in this study consist of measurements from multiple surveys. At different effective redshifts the data points a are treated as independent, but the quantities specifically
D_{M}/r_{d}
and
D_{H}/r_{d}
measured at the same effective redshift are generally correlated. For such cases, the corresponding covariance matrices provided in the original analyses are incorporated into the likelihood construction
w56
;
w57
;
w55
;
w60
. Accordingly, the total chi-square for the P-BAO dataset is given by

\chi^{2}_{\rm P\mbox{-}BAO}(\theta)=\sum_{i}\Delta\mathbf{D}_{i}^{T}\,\mathbf{C}_{i}^{-1}\,\Delta\mathbf{D}_{i}+\sum_{j}\frac{\left[X^{\rm obs}_{j}-X^{\rm th}_{j}(\theta)\right]^{2}}{\sigma_{X,j}^{2}},

(31)

where the data vector for correlated measurements is defined as

\Delta\mathbf{D}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{D_{M}^{\rm obs}(z_{i})}{r_{d}}-\dfrac{D_{M}^{\rm th}(z_{i},\theta)}{r_{d}}\\
\dfrac{D_{H}^{\rm obs}(z_{i})}{r_{d}}-\dfrac{D_{H}^{\rm th}(z_{i},\theta)}{r_{d}}\end{bmatrix},

(32)

and
\mathbf{C}_{i}
denotes the covariance matrix associated with the correlated pair
\left(D_{M}/r_{d},\,D_{H}/r_{d}\right)
at redshift
z_{i}
. For the uncorrelated data points, we consider
X^{\rm obs}_{j}=\left\{\frac{D_{M}(z_{j})}{r_{d}},\,\frac{D_{H}(z_{j})}{r_{d}},\,\frac{D_{V}(z_{j})}{r_{d}},\,H(z_{j})\right\}
,
where
X^{\rm th}_{j}(\theta)
represents the corresponding theoretical predictions, and
\sigma_{X,j}=\left\{\sigma_{D_{M}/r_{d},j},\,\sigma_{D_{H}/r_{d},j},\,\sigma_{D_{V}/r_{d},j},\,\sigma_{H,j}\right\}
denotes the associated observational uncertainties.

III.4
GW Dataset

The gravitational-wave (GW) data are incorporated via luminosity distance measurements, expressed in terms of the distance modulus. The chi-square function is given by

\chi^{2}_{\rm GW}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm GW}}\frac{\left[\mu^{\rm obs}(z_{i})-\mu^{\rm th}(z_{i};\theta)\right]^{2}}{\sigma_{\mu,i}^{2}},

(33)

where
\mu^{\rm obs}(z_{i})
and
\sigma_{\mu,i}
denote the observed distance modulus and its uncertainty for each GW event, respectively, and
\mu^{\rm th}(z_{i};\theta)
is the corresponding theoretical prediction.

Based of above definitions the total chi-square for each analysis is given by:

\chi^{2}_{\rm tot}=\begin{cases}\chi^{2}_{\rm DESI},&\text{DESI},\\[6.0pt]
\chi^{2}_{\rm P\mbox{-}BAO},&\text{P-BAO},\\[6.0pt]
\chi^{2}_{\rm DESI}+\chi^{2}_{\rm P\mbox{-}BAO}+\chi^{2}_{\rm GW}+\chi^{2}_{\rm CC},&\text{DESI + P-BAO + GW + CC}.\end{cases}

(34)

Based on the
\chi^{2}
functions and datasets described above, a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method is employed to explore the parameter space and obtain the posterior distributions of the model parameters
\Omega_{m0}
, and
H_{0}
. We adopt uniform priors given by
H_{0}\in[60,80]
, and
\Omega_{m0}\in[0,1]
. The MCMC chains are initialized at
(H_{0},\Omega_{m0})=(70,0.3)
. For simplicity, the present radiation density parameter is fixed throughout the analysis to
\Omega_{r0}=0.0003
. To assess the goodness of fit and compare the proposed model with the standard
\Lambda
CDM cosmology, we employ several statistical estimators, including the minimum chi-square (
\chi^{2}_{\min}
), the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The AIC and BIC are defined as

\mathrm{AIC}=\chi^{2}_{\min}+2k,\qquad\mathrm{BIC}=\chi^{2}_{\min}+k\ln N,

(35)

where
k
is the number of free parameters and
N
is the total number of data points. Lower values of AIC, BIC, and
\chi^{2}_{\min}
, along with higher
R^{2}
, indicate a better fit. While
\chi^{2}_{\min}
and
R^{2}
quantify the fit quality, AIC and BIC further penalize model complexity, with BIC imposing a stronger penalty for larger datasets. To compare the give cosmological model against
\Lambda
CDM, we define the differences:

\Delta X=X_{\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}}-X_{\mathrm{model}},\quad X\in\{\mathrm{AIC},\mathrm{BIC}\}.

(36)

The strength of evidence is interpreted as follows:
0\leq\Delta X\leq 2
indicates weak evidence,
2<\Delta X\leq 6
suggests positive evidence, and
\Delta X>6
corresponds to strong evidence in favor of the model with lower information criterion values. Using these diagnostics, we constrain the model parameters and examine the resulting cosmological dynamics with the datasets described above.

Deceleration Parameter

IV.6
Stability Analysis

In this section, we investigate the dynamical stability of the proposed model under homogeneous linear perturbations. Following standard approaches, we introduce first-order perturbations in the Hubble parameter and energy density as:

H^{*}(t)=H(t)\,[1+\delta(t)],\qquad\rho^{*}(t)=\rho(t)\,[1+\delta_{m}(t)],

(48)

where
\delta(t)
and
\delta_{m}(t)
denote the fractional perturbations. The background evolution satisfies the continuity equation,

\dot{\rho}+3H(\rho+p)=0,

(49)

\frac{d}{dt}=-(1+z)\,H(z)\,\frac{d}{dz}.

(50)

Substituting the perturbed quantities and linearizing, we obtain:

\dot{\delta}_{m}+3H(1+\omega_{\rm eff})\,\delta=0,\qquad 2\,\delta=\delta_{m}.

(51)

Transforming to redshift space yields:

-(1+z)\,\frac{d\delta_{m}}{dz}+\frac{2}{3}\left(1+\omega_{\rm eff}(z)\right)\delta_{m}=0,

(52)

where

\omega_{\rm eff}(z)=-1+\frac{2(1+z)}{3H(z)}\frac{dH}{dz}.

(53)

Using the best-fit parameters we numerically solve the above equation to trace the evolution of
\delta_{m}(z)
and
\delta(z)

This damping behavior indicates the absence of growing modes or late-time instabilities, confirming that the model remains dynamically stable throughout cosmic evolution. The consistency of this behavior across all datasets further reinforces the robustness and physical viability of the proposed cosmological scenario.

Conclusion

In this work, we adopt the thermodynamic interpretation of gravity, wherein modifications to the cosmological field equations arise from corrections to the entropy associated with the apparent horizon. In particular, we consider the Rényi entropy, which represents a non-extensive generalization of the standard Boltzmann–Gibbs entropy. Within this framework, deviations from standard cosmology naturally emerge as corrections to the Friedmann equations. At the relativistic level, the application of the first law of thermodynamics on the apparent horizon leads to modified Friedmann equations containing additional terms governed by the Rényi parameter
\lambda
. These corrections encode the non-extensive thermodynamic effects and can be interpreted as geometric modifications to the underlying gravitational dynamics. Here, the model’s cosmological dynamics were explored based on the best fit values of the parameters, which were constrained using recent observational datasets, including DESI, P-BAO, CC, and GW data, through a comprehensive MCMC analysis.
