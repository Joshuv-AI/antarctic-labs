Introduction

Patient safety is a global issue throughout all tiers of the healthcare system, primarily aimed at reducing hazards to patients during healthcare services delivery. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommended that its member nations enact systematic reforms to promote safety and improve the health care quality [1]. Despite technological improvements and a focus on enhancing safety culture, healthcare institutions continue to face substantial challenges in mitigating medical errors. Healthcare-related harm and mortality are significant global burdens, especially in developing countries. According to the World Health Organization, 134 million adverse incidents occur yearly, leading to 2.6 million fatalities. This emphasizes the pressing need to enhance patient safety systems, particularly in developing nations like Egypt, which confront significant challenges in reducing preventable harm, costing an estimated $1 trillion to $2 trillion socially [2].

Patient safety includes structured activities that support cultures, processes, procedures, behaviors, technologies, and environments within healthcare. these activities aim to consistently and sustainably mitigating risks, diminishing the incidence of preventable harm, minimizing the likelihood of errors, and minimizing their impact when they arise [3]. A commonly used definition of safety culture is “the collective attitudes, beliefs, values, and assumptions that influence individuals’ perceptions of behavioral patterns in safety performance within their organization.” [4].

Patient Safety Culture (PSC) indicates the extent to which an organization’s culture support and enhances safety of patient. It shows the values, beliefs, and norms collectively held by healthcare practitioners and other personnel in the organizations that shape their actions and behaviors [5].

In Egypt, patient safety received increased attention under Vision 2030, as the Ministry of Health and Population (MoHP) has initiated a national initiative aimed at enhancing healthcare quality and minimizing unnecessary injury. The establishment of the General Authority for Healthcare Accreditation and Regulation (GAHAR) and the initiation of a Patient Safety Department reflect significant developments in the standardization of safety protocols [6]. Recent studies at Egyptian hospitals reveal deteriorating safety culture, particularly in the high-risk areas, including intensive care units [7].

A 2025 national multicenter study observed that while job satisfaction among ICU staff was relatively high, dimensions such as teamwork climate, stress recognition, and safety climate were substantially lower than international benchmarks difficulties in mitigating medical errors [8].

Healthcare providers’ burnout represents a major risk to patient safety. Burnout (including emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and decreased personal accomplishment) has repeatedly been associated with more medical errors and nosocomial infections, patient falls, and adverse events. A recent meta-analysis conducted in 2024, including 85 studies and over 288,000 nurses has found that burnout has a meaningful decremental effect on safety climate and patient satisfaction as well as quality care [9]. The effect is driven by age, gender, and geography, underscoring the threat that burnout poses to healthcare systems across the board.

Workplace safety, which includes physical, psychological, and organizational factors that impact the ability of HCPs to work safely, is, however, equally relevant. A US study in 2024 in 28 hospitals also confirmed similar findings and showed a strong association between workplace safety measures and PSC measures with standardized beta coefficients of up to 0.95 for hospital administration support [10]. When workers are safe, supported, and empowered, they are more likely to report incidents, engage in safety behaviors, and contribute to developing a culture of care.

Providers’ attitudes regarding patient safety culture are affected by various aspects, including leadership, communication, team culture, and the structure of organizational learning. These perceptions are not static and change over time with experience, exposure to risk, and institutional investment. Comprehending these perceptions is necessary for successful development interventions to foster a safety and resilience culture [11].

Methodology

Study design and setting

Study design A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted to assess healthcare workers’ perceptions of patient safety culture (PSC) across three public hospitals in Beni-Suef City, Egypt the survey continued for three months.

Location and duration of the study

The study was done at Beni-Suef City, Egypt, within three different health sectors (Beni-Suef University Hospital, the general hospital, and the health insurance hospital). The survey continued from May 2025 to Augst 2025 after approval of Ethical committee.

Study population

Healthcare workers (physicians, nurses, pharmacists, technicians, and administrative staff) from the selected hospitals who were willing to participate.

Inclusion criteria

Healthcare workers with at least one month of experience at the current facility and who provided consent.

Healthcare workers with at least one month of experience at the current facility and who provided consent.

The healthcare worker who accepted to participate.

The healthcare worker who accepted to participate.

Exclusion criteria

Newly employed staff with less than one month of experience.

Newly employed staff with less than one month of experience.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated by Epi-Info 7, based on a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and an assumed population proportion of 50%. The required sample was 382. We increased the sample size to 497 to promote the study statistical power and to prevent any non-response or dropouts.

Sampling technique

The study used convenience sampling to select respondents.

Data collection methods and tools

A semi-structured interview questionnaire was distributed to health care workers who were willing to participate. The questionnaire was given via two ways: in-person interviews and an online Google form to reach as many participants as possible through social media groups (such as WhatsApp and Facebook because most of the HCWs were busy during their work and they suggested sending it in social media groups to fill it out) for HCWs who were working in these hospitals; this mixed type was done to collect the sample size. A questionnaire was used to evaluate healthcare workers’ perceptions of patient safety culture across three sectors: Beni-Suef University Hospital, a general hospital, and a health insurance hospital. This assessment drew on findings from two research papers, one regarding Moroccan primary health care and the other addressing the link between patient safety culture and workplace safety culture in hospitals [5,11]. A pilot study of 30 health care workers was conducted to ensure the clarity and usability of the questionnaire, with no edition done and not included in the sample.

A questionnaire is classified as part 1: A 9-item question; study participants’ socio-demographic characteristics and background information included age, gender, and residence.

Academic level, Occupational, Workplace, work experience, work hours per week, and direct interaction or contact with patients.

Part 2

A 27-item question measures patient safety, with the number of items including Participant Unit and Work Area (14); Supervisor, Manager, or Clinical Leader Support for Patient Safety (2); Communication About Error and Communication Openness (4); Reporting Patient Safety Events (3); Patient Safety Rating (1); and Hospital Management Support for Patient Safety (3).

Part 3

A 4-item question; measures workplace safety, including protection from workplace hazards; moving, transferring, or lifting patients; hospital management support for workplace safety; and work stress/burnout.

Patient safety culture (PSC) scores were computed by summing participants’ responses to items across six dimensions: “Patient Safety in Your Unit/Work Area,” “Your Supervisor, Manager, or Clinical Leader,” “Communication,” “Reporting Patient Safety Events,” “Patient Safety Rating,” and “Your Hospital.” Responses were rated on a five-point Likert scale, where agreement-based items ranged from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), and frequency-based items ranged from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Negatively worded items were reverse-coded to ensure that higher scores consistently reflected more positive perceptions of patient safety. These included items 3, 5, 6, 7, 9, 11, 13, and 14 in the “Patient Safety in Your Unit/Work Area” section; item 1 in “Your Supervisor, Manager, or Clinical Leader”; item 4 in “Communication”; and items 2 and 3 in “Your Hospital.” Workplace safety scores were calculated similarly by summing responses to items under “Protection from Workplace Hazards,” “Moving, Transferring, or Lifting Patients,” and “Hospital Management Support for Workplace Safety,” using the same Likert scale without reverse coding. Burnout was assessed using a single-item measure with five response options, scored from 1 (no symptoms of burnout) to 5 (completely burned out and may need help), reflecting increasing severity. For all domains, higher scores indicated more favorable perceptions of safety and lower burnout. The percentage of positive responses (scores of 4 or 5) was calculated for each dimension and used as the primary outcome measure, with reverse-coded items adjusted accordingly to maintain interpretive consistency.

Data management and analysis

All collected questionnaires were reviewed for completeness and logical consistency. To ensure maximum data quality, questionnaires with significant missing data or incomplete primary outcomes were excluded at the point of collection and replaced by recruiting additional participants. This process continued until a final sample of 497 complete cases was achieved. Data were managed and analyzed by using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0. Sociodemographic characteristics and scores of patient safety culture were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and quantitative variables were described with means, standard deviations (SD), medians, minimums-maximums, and interquartile ranges (IQR). The normality of quantitative variables was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and all variables have normal distribution.

For inferential statistics, independent-samples t-tests were conducted to compare means between two independent groups, and one-way ANOVA was used to compare means across more than two groups with Bonferroni post-hoc tests comparing pairwise differences. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the relationships between continuous variables, such as patient safety and workplace safety scores and burnout. Correlation strength was interpreted as weak (r ≤ 0.3), moderate (r = 0.31–0.69), or strong (r ≥ 0.7). To identify independent predictors of patient safety culture, Multivariable linear regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of patient safety culture, workplace safety, and burnout scores. To evaluate the explanatory power of the regression models, the coefficient of determination (R squared) was utilized. It represents the proportion of variance in the dependent variable that is predictable from the independent variables. In accordance with the established benchmarks, it is interpreted as follows: R² < 0.01 (Very Small), 0.01 ≤ R² < 0.09 (Small), 0.09 ≤ R² < 0.25 (Medium), and R² ≥ 0.25 (Large) [12].

It also showed that nearly 56% of participants were females and living in urban areas. More than half of participants had a bachelor’s degree. More than one-third of participants were physicians. Nearly 70% of participants worked at Beni-Suef University Hospital. As regards experience years, nearly one-third of participants had 1 to 5 experience years. Nearly another one-third had less than 1 year. Regarding working hours, more than half of participants worked 30–40 h per week. Nearly 90% of participants had direct contact with patients (Table 1).

Discussion

Demographic and workload influences on patient safety culture

Research examining PSC among healthcare workers reveals significant demographic influences on safety perceptions and behaviors. The mean age of the participants was (28.98 ± 6.70), and over 40% of the participants were aged between 26 and 30. This indicates that the working-age population is young. Younger nurses in a Palestinian university hospital tended to have a higher PSC compared with older nurses [13].

These results suggest that younger caregivers may be more receptive to safety laws and training, consequently promoting safety culture in an effective institution-building manner. Key determinants affecting patient safety culture encompass teamwork, organizational learning, staffing ratios, and work experience, with younger demographics constituting a substantial segment of the healthcare workforce necessitating focused safety culture initiatives [14].

Investigations on healthcare worker schedules and patient safety indicate substantial issues with working hours and their effect on care quality. Research indicates that extended working hours, frequent night shifts, and inadequate days off correlate with a detrimental patient safety culture [15]. Working over 12 h a workday or 40 h weekly may be associated with poorer patient outcomes [16].

Occupational and institutional factors shaping patient safety culture

This study’s findings provide statistically significant evidence of correlations in understanding and perceptions among health workers regarding patient safety culture. It is interesting that nurses reported the highest mean patient safety scores, followed by administrative staff. These findings are in contrast with to those of, Ming et al. 2023 and Malinowska-Lipień Iwona 2021 who found that safety perceptions were lower for nurses than for supervisors and doctors, respectively [17,18].

The current findings are consistent with AL-Mugheed et al. 2022, in that nurses had positive perceptions of job satisfaction and stress recognition [19]. These variations could be due to contextual variances in healthcare systems, organizational backing, and the role of nurses in delivering patient care.

The workplace environment also had a large effect, and participants working in government hospitals were more likely to report higher patient safety scores than those working in university hospitals. This is supported by Brubakk Kirsten AND Svendsen who underscored the importance of having supportive hospital environments in creating a healthy safety climate [20]. Public hospitals may have more defined protocols, better reporting systems, or more teamwork dynamics, which can result in improved safety perceptions.

Working hours emerged as another influential factor. Participants working 30–40 h per week had significantly higher safety scores than those working less than 30 h. This finding is consistent with Hayashi et al. 2020, who highlighted that excessive working hours and irregular shifts negatively impact safety culture [15]. Interestingly, in our study, fewer working hours were associated with lower safety scores, which may suggest that part-time or less engaged staff have limited exposure to safety practices or organizational culture.

Direct patient contact was also associated with higher safety scores, reinforcing the findings of Badr et al. 2017 who identified direct communication with patients as a key determinant of safety culture [21]. Healthcare workers with direct patient interaction may be more attuned to safety protocols and more invested in maintaining high standards of care.

The role of assistive devices and psychological stress in enhancing patient safety

Assistive devices showed a statistically significant association with greater patient safety scores in this study, supporting the notion that more access to assistive devices is related to better patient safety outcomes. This aligns with Mali Namrata and Restrepo 2023, which [22] indicated that the injurious possesses of equipment by the elderly is frequently associated with devices with defects, stressing the quality and ubiquity of assistance technologies [22].

In addition, we found that participants with stress in the absence of burnout had the highest levels of patient safety scores, as compared to those with no burnout, who also had higher scores than those with severe burnout, such that moderate stress is potentially associated with increased vigilance or performance. This complex interrelationship coincides with that of Montgomery et al. 2022 who reported on a study in which all nurse burnout subdimensions were associated with poorer patient safety grades, while better work environments were associated with better safety ratings [23].

Negative correlations of nurse burnout were also found with patient safety management activities, and compassion satisfaction partially mediated this relationship Ryu et al. 2021 Collectively, these results illustrate the multi-factorial nature of patient safety and that both organizational resources and individual psychological states are critical to the formation of outcomes [24].

Multivariable analysis of organizational and environmental factors influencing patient safety scores

This study sought common factors that were predictive of patient safety scores via multivariate linear regression analysis. It is interesting to note that employment at a nonteaching hospital, working 30 h or more per week, and limited direct contact with patients were all significantly associated with higher patient safety scores. Additionally, for each 1-unit increase in workplace safety score, there was an associated 1.99- point increase in PS. The findings are in accordance with the recent information regarding the degree of importance of job conditions with respect to safety performance. Brubakk Kirsten AND Svendsen found that a supportive working environment in the hospital was significantly associated with the patient safety climate, including teamwork, creativity, and harmful practice reporting [20].

Hesgrove et al. 2024 found that there were strong relationships between work safety climate and patient safety, particularly if supported by the hospital’s top management [11]. Pittman et al. 2025 analyzed the effect of activity hours and reported that the increased proportion of time in overtime and use of agency nurses’ hours all resulted in increased pressure areas and other adverse outcomes [25].

This reflects the need for having a good nurse staffing policy for our own protection. Kang et al. 2024 demonstrated that safety-critical events and stress at work negatively impact nurses’ work-life balance and performance; consequently, organizations should help assistance to avoid burnout while keeping safety [26]. Association 2025 has advocated that a strong culture of health and safety in the workplace is associated with better patient care on several of the care units in the hospital [27]. Taken together, these studies support our findings and show that improvements in the work environment, proactive leadership, and safety climate are key factors for improving healthcare safety.

Multivariate predictors of workplace safety perception among healthcare professionals”

Results The multivariable linear regression identified multiple predictors of healthcare workers’ perceived workplace safety. Interestingly, female sex was associated with a greater workplace safety score. This corresponds with recent reports that females tend to follow precautions more carefully (have higher compliance for personal protective equipment (PPE) use) mainly due to increased perception of safety [28].

On the other hand, living in rural areas was associated with lower scores for workplace safety. This could be reflective of disparities in healthcare infrastructure, availability of staff, and safety resources, urban-rural wide. Rural health facilities may have scarce resources, fewer training opportunities, and higher workloads that impair the safety cultures [29].

Scores for safety were low among HCWs with more than five years of experience, which could be explained by more contact with systemic problems, burnout, or disbelief in safety practices with time [30]. Equally, having a job for ≥ 30 h/wk was inversely associated with perception of safety, which is supported by research portraying long shifts as being associated with fatigue, lack of alertness, and higher error rates [31].

Finally, the moderate to strong positive relationship between patient safety culture and workplace safety score highlights the interconnectedness of these areas. The positive patient safety environment might also mirror more general organizational support for staff wellbeing, communication and leadership engagement [11].

These results suggest that interventions that focus on addressing gender-specific issues, supporting rural healthcare, improving role-based training, and managing workload for a stronger safety culture are necessary.

Burnout determinants among healthcare workers: hospital type and patient contact

Two significant predictors for burnout in healthcare workers emerged from the present study: working in a hospital other than the university hospital and having direct exposure to the patients. These results are in line with the recent insights, which point to the complex interaction between institutional contexts, job profiles, and emotional demands in influencing the levels of burnout.

For non-university hospitals, higher burnout scores were better. This could be due to variations in organizational support, staffing, and access to training. A study by Maunder et al. 2021 reported that hospital workers, especially those working in the intensive care unit and the emergency department, have suffered from a significant rise in burnout as a result of a lack of staffing and a great deal of work during the COVID-19 pandemic [32]. Similarly, Appelbom et al. 2024 indicated that symptoms of burnout persisted in workers in general hospitals during the pandemic, particularly among those made to work in roles with high demand, such as below-normal staffing levels, without enough psychological support [33].

Direct patient care was also linked to higher burnout, consistent with COVID-19 patient healthcare carriers who exhibited significantly more stress, anxiety, and burnout negatively affecting their quality-of-life Çelmeçe et al. 2020 Stress and fear of infection and moral distress from continuous exposure to pain and suffrage raises this risk [34]. also found that working in COVID-19 units and being reassigned to unfamiliar areas were the most powerful predictors of burnout, particularly among younger staff and those with a prior mental health problem [35].

These results highlight the importance of interventions to reduce burnout, particularly for those working in non-academic hospitals and front-line staff. Organizational approaches should involve adequate staffing, psychological support, and leadership presence. In addition, both the removal of unnecessary administrative burden and clarity of roles can contribute to reduction of stress and increase of resilience among health care professionals.

Conclusion

This study suggests that patient safety culture is shaped by a combination of demographic, occupational, and organizational factors. Higher scores among younger healthcare workers, particularly nurses, and in governmental hospitals may reflect differences in engagement, organizational structure, and leadership. The observed association between workplace safety and patient safety culture highlights the importance of integrated approaches that support both staff and patient well-being. In the Egyptian context, strengthening patient safety culture may benefit from targeted training, leadership support, and burnout mitigation strategies. These findings should be interpreted considering the study limitations, and further research is warranted to confirm these associations and explore causal relationships.

Strengths

This study benefits from a large, diverse sample (n = 497) across three hospital types, enhancing generalizability. The use of both paper and electronic surveys improved response rates and data completeness. Including workplace safety items enriched the assessment of safety culture.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias, as the sample may not be fully representative of all healthcare workers. Second, the reliance on self-reported data may be subject to response bias, including social desirability bias. Third, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causal relationships between variables. Finally, the use of mixed data collection methods (online and in-person) may introduce mode bias, potentially affecting the consistency of responses.

Recommendations

Healthcare organizations are encouraged to strengthen workplace safety measures, including ensuring adequate staffing, adherence to protective protocols, and access to assistive devices. Optimizing working conditions, particularly workload and working hours, is also recommended to support patient safety performance. Additionally, efforts should be made to reduce disparities across healthcare settings, especially in non-university hospitals, through improved resource allocation and standardization of safety practices.

Practical implications

The findings suggest that improving workplace safety, reducing burnout, and enhancing staff engagement may support better patient safety outcomes. Integrating patient safety into training programs and adopting standardized safety frameworks could further strengthen safety culture across healthcare settings.
