Learning to Decide with AI Assistance under Human-Alignment
Nina Corvelo Benz §, Eleni Straitouri †, Manuel Gomez-Rodriguez †

Abstract
It is widely agreed that when AI models assist decision-makers in high-stakes domains by predicting an outcome of interest, they should communicate the confidence of their predictions. However, empirical evidence suggests that decision-makers often struggle to determine when to trust a prediction based solely on this communicated confidence. In this context, recent theoretical and empirical work suggests a positive correlation between the utility of AI-assisted decision-making and the degree of alignment between the AI confidence and the decision-makers’ confidence in their own predictions. Crucially, these findings do not yet elucidate the extent to which this alignment influences the complexity of learning to make optimal decisions through repeated interactions. In this paper, we address this question in the canonical case of binary predictions and binary decisions. We first show that this problem is equivalent to a two-armed online contextual learning problem with full feedback, and establish a lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( | ℋ | ⋅ | ℬ | ⋅ T ) \Omega(\sqrt{|\mathcal{H}|\cdot|\mathcal{B}|\cdot T}) on the expected regret any learner can attain, where ℋ \mathcal{H} and ℬ \mathcal{B} denote the sets of human and AI confidence values. We then demonstrate that, under perfect alignment between AI and human confidence, a learner can attain an expected regret of 𝒪 ⁡ ( | ℋ | ⋅ T ​ log ⁡ T ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{|\mathcal{H}|\cdot T\log T}) and, when | ℋ | = 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ T ) \sqrt{|\mathcal{H}|}=\mathcal{O}(\log T) and ℬ \mathcal{B} is countable, a non-trivial generalization of the Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz inequality improves the regret bound to 𝒪 ⁡ ( T ​ log ⁡ T ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T\log T}) . Taken together, these results reveal that alignment can reduce the complexity of learning to make decisions with AI assistance. Experiments on real data from two different human-subject studies where participants solve simple decision-making tasks assisted by AI models show that our theoretical results are robust to violations of perfect alignment.

2.2
Complexity of Learning to Decide with AI Assistance

Our starting point is the realization that, for binary predictions and binary decisions, the problem of learning to decide with AI assistance through repeated interactions is equivalent to a two-armed online contextual learning problem with full feedback
(
37
)
.
In particular, at each time step
t
, the confidence values
(h_{t},b_{t})
correspond to the context, the decisions
a
corresponds to the arms, the utility
u(a_{t},y_{t})
corresponds to the reward, and we have full feedback because, given the label of interest
y_{t}
, the decision maker can compute both
u(a_{t},y_{t})
and
u(1-a_{t},y_{t})
.

Consequently, similarly as elsewhere in the online learning literature
(
37
)
, we can measure to which extent a decision maker succeeds at efficiently finding the optimal decision policy by analyzing the expected cumulative regret.
More formally, the expected cumulative regret
\mathbb{E}[R(T)]

is defined as

\mathbb{E}[R(T)]=\mathbb{E}_{h_{t},b_{t}\sim P(H,B)}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mu(\pi^{*}(h_{t},b_{t})\,|\,h_{t},b_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{a_{t}\sim P(A_{t}\,|\,h_{t},b_{t})}[\mu(a_{t}\,|\,h_{t},b_{t})]\right)\right],

(3)

where
P(H,B)
denotes the distribution of the decision maker’s and the classifier’s confidence, which implicitly depends on the feature distribution
P(X,V)
, and
P(A_{t}\,|\,h_{t},b_{t})
denotes the distribution of decisions induced by the decision policy
\pi_{t}
used at time step
t
.

In what follows, we establish a lower bound on the expected regret using a non-trivial extension of a classical result for contextual bandits with partial feedback from
1

Theorem 1
.

For any choice of decision policies
\pi_{t}
based on historical observations, there exists a problem instance such that
\mathbb{E}[R(T)]=\Omega(\sqrt{|\mathcal{H}|\cdot|\mathcal{B}|\cdot T})
.

The above result reveals that, in general, the complexity of learning the optimal decision policy through repeated interactions depends on the size of the sets of possible human and AI confidence values,
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{B}
.
6
6

6

Our lower bound is only meaningful whenever both
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{B}
are finite discrete sets.
Note that, without further assumptions on the decision policy, learning is impossible under infinite context
(
36
)
.

Importantly, in the next section, we will show that, under perfect alignment between AI and human confidence, this dependence vanishes, reducing the overall complexity.

3
Learning to Decide with AI Assistance under Perfect Alignment

In this section, we first show that, under perfect alignment between AI and human confidence, the optimal decision policy
\pi^{*}
is a threshold function defined over the AI confidence, with a threshold that depends on the human confidence.
Next, we present a simple online algorithm that, by utilizing this structure, is guaranteed to attain an expected regret of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{|\mathcal{H}|\cdot T\log T})
in learning the optimal decision policy
\pi^{*}
.
Finally, when
\sqrt{|\mathcal{H}|}=\mathcal{O}(\log T)
and
\mathcal{B}
is countable, we improve the regret bound to
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T\log T})
.
In Appendix
B
, we further show that, whenever perfect alignment does not hold, the degree of alignment bounds the gap between the expected utility achieved by the decision policy found by our algorithm and the optimal policy.

3.1
Optimal Decision Policy Under Perfect Alignment

There is perfect alignment between AI and human confidence if, for any
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
and
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
h<h^{\prime}
and
b<b^{\prime}
, we have that
(
8
)

~P(Y=1\,|\,H=h,B=b)-P(Y=1\,|\,H=h^{\prime},B=b^{\prime})\leq 0.

(4)

Moreover, if there is perfect alignment, it is easy to see that, for any
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
b<b^{\prime}
and any
h\in\mathcal{H}
, it holds that

\mu(1|h,b)\leq\mu(1|h,b^{\prime})\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu(0|h,b)\geq\mu(0|h,b^{\prime}),

(5)

where
\mu(a\,|\,h,b)
is the conditional expected utility achieved by decision
a
given the human and AI confidence
H=h
and
B=b
, defined in Eq.
2
.

As an immediate consequence, for any
h\in\mathcal{H}
and
b\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\pi^{*}(h,b)=1
, it must hold that
\pi^{*}(h,b^{\prime})=1
for any
b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
b^{\prime}>b
and, for any
h\in\mathcal{H}
and
b\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\pi^{*}(h,b)=0
, it must hold that
\pi^{*}(h,b^{\prime})=0
for any
b^{\prime}<b
.
Further, this implies that, for each

such that

\pi^{*}(h,b)=0

Theorem 2
.

Under perfect alignment, for any

is given by a threshold function
\pi^{*}(h,b)=\mathbb{I}[b>b^{*}(h)]

b^{*}(h)=\sup\left\{b\in\mathcal{B}\ \middle|\ P(Y=0\,|\,H=h,B=b)\geq\frac{u(1,1)-u(0,1)}{u(1,1)-u(1,0)+u(0,0)-u(0,1)}\right\}.

Importantly, the above result reveals that learning the optimal policy essentially reduces to learning

\forall h\in\mathcal{H}

,
\forall h\in\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{B}
and, when

3.2
An Efficient Algorithm for Learning to Decide Under Perfect Alignment

Our starting point is the realization that the problem of learning
b^{*}(h)
,
\forall h\in\mathcal{H}
through repeated interactions is equivalent to
|\mathcal{H}|
independent multi-armed online learning problems with full feedback—one per value of
h
.
For each of these problems, at each time step
t
, the (candidate) thresholds
b\in\mathcal{B}
correspond to the arms, the utility
u(\mathbb{I}[b_{t}>b],y_{t})
corresponds to the reward, and we have full feedback because, given the label of interest
y_{t}
, the decision maker can compute the utility
u(\mathbb{I}[b_{t}>b],y_{t})
for all thresholds
b\in\mathcal{B}
.

To solve the above multi-armed online learning problems, our online algorithm leverages the following key theorem:

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mu(b\,|\,h)
be the conditional expected utility achieved by the decision policy
\pi
defined by a threshold function with threshold
b
given the human confidence
H=h
,
i.e.
,

\mu(b\,|\,h)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mu(1\,|\,H,B)\cdot\mathbb{I}[B>b]\,|\,H=h\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\mu(0\,|\,H,B)\cdot\mathbb{I}[B\leq b]\,|\,H=h\right],

(6)

where
\mu(a\,|\,H,B)
is the conditional expected utility achieved by decision
a
given the human and AI confidence
H
and
B
, defined in Eq.
2
.
Then, it holds that
b^{*}(h)=\arg\max_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\mu(b\,|\,h)
.

In a nutshell, our online algorithm proceeds as follows.
At each time step
t
, it first computes an unbiased estimate of the conditional expected utility
\mu(b\,|\,h_{t})
for each candidate threshold
b\in\mathcal{B}
,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}_{t}(b\,|\,h_{t})=\frac{1}{n_{t}(h_{t})}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t:h_{t^{\prime}}=h_{t}}\left(\mathbb{I}[y_{t^{\prime}}=0,b_{t^{\prime}}>b]\cdot(u(1,0)-u(1,1))\right.

\displaystyle+\mathbb{I}[y_{t^{\prime}}=0,b_{t^{\prime}}\leq b]\cdot(u(0,0)-u(0,1))

\displaystyle+\left.\mathbb{I}[b_{t^{\prime}}\leq b]\cdot(u(0,1)-u(1,1))+u(1,1)\right).

(7)

where
n_{t}(h_{t})
is the number of observations with human confidence equal to
h_{t}
until time step
t
.
Then, it picks the threshold
\bar{b}_{t}(h_{t})
with the highest estimated conditional expected utility
\bar{\mu}_{t}(b\,|\,h_{t})
.
Finally, it takes decision
a_{t}=1
if
b_{t}>\bar{b}_{t}(h_{t})

a_{t}=0

1

|\mathcal{H}|

The following theorem bounds the expected regret attained by our algorithm:

Theorem 4

The proof of the above theorem relies on two key steps.
First, we show that, under perfect alignment, our algorithm’s expected regret on the

2.2

7

7

Second, we use the DKW inequality

Theorem 5

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}_{t}(\ell)=P_{t}(Y=0,B>\ell(H))\cdot(u(1,0)-u(1,1))

5
Discussion and Limitations

In this section, we highlight several limitations of our work, discuss its broader impact, and point out avenues for future research.

Monotonicity of the optimal decision policy.

In our work, we have shown that, under perfect alignment, a decision-maker can learn the optimal decision policy more efficiently by focusing only on the set of decision policies monotone in the AI confidence.
However,
8
have shown that, under perfect alignment, the optimal decision policy lies within the set of policies monotone in both the human and the AI confidence, which is a subset of the set of decision policies monotone in the AI confidence.
Therefore, it would be very interesting to investigate whether, under perfect alignment, a decision-maker can learn the optimal decision policy even more efficiently by focusing on this latter set.

Decision making setting.

We have considered AI-assisted decision making processes where the label
y
is always observable independently of the decision taken—there is full-feedback. However, there exist decision making tasks in which the label
y
is only realized whenever the decision
a=1
. For example, we can only observe if a treatment is eventually beneficial or not if the patient is treated, or we can only observe if a customer does or does not default on a loan if the loan is granted. Our lower bound on the expected regret can be easily extended to such partial-feedback setting using a classical result for contextual bandits by
1
.
However, investigating the extent to which a decision-maker can leverage the alignment between AI and human confidence to learn more efficiently under partial-feedback is significantly more challenging, and it is left for future work.
Further, in our setting, we have assumed that the AI model and the degree of alignment are fixed.
It would be interesting to extend our analysis to a setting where both the AI model and the degree of alignment can change over time. To this end, recent work on online calibration
(
20
)
and online multicalibration
(
13
;
14
)
may be proven useful.

Broader impact.

AI promises to enhance decision making in a variety of high-stakes domains; however, empirical evidence suggests that decision-makers often struggle to make optimal decisions under AI assistance.
Through theoretical analysis, our work suggests that increasing the alignment between the AI confidence and the decision maker’s confidence on their own predictions may help decision-makers learn to make optimal decisions through repeated interactions.

6
Conclusions

We have theoretically shown that, when AI models assist decision-makers by predicting a binary outcome of interest, perfect alignment between the AI confidence and the decision-makers’ confidence in their own predictions can be used to learn the optimal decision policy more efficiently.
Moreover, using real data from two human-subject studies, we have empirically demonstrated that our theoretical results are robust to certain violations of perfect alignment—a learner who assumes perfect alignment can achieve lower expected regret than one making no such assumption, provided that a weaker notion of alignment holds.

Then,

Corollary 9
.

Given a natural number

, let
Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{n}
be
real-valued independent and identically distributed random variables with cumulative distribution function

. Let

F^{+}(\cdot)=P(Z>z)
and
F^{+}_{n}
denote the associated empirical distribution function defined by

F^{+}_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}[Z_{i}>z]\quad\text{for}\quad z\in\mathbb{R}

Then,

P\left(\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\ \ |F^{+}_{n}(z)-F^{+}(z)|>\epsilon\right)\leq 2\exp^{-2n\epsilon^{2}}

Proof.

First, note that,

|F^{+}_{n}(z)-F^{+}(z)|=|F^{+}_{n}(z)+1-1-F^{+}(z)|=|-(1-F^{+}_{n}(z))+1-F^{+}(z)|=|-F_{n}(z)+F(z)|=|F_{n}(z)-F(z)|

(19)

Substituting the above in Theorem

(DKW inequality), we get that

P\left(\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\ \ |F^{+}_{n}(z)-F^{+}(z)|>\epsilon\right)=P\left(\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\ \ |F_{n}(z)-F(z)|>\epsilon\right)\leq 2\exp^{-2n\epsilon^{2}}

∎

Lemma 3

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad P\left(\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\ |P(Y=0,B>b\,|\,H=h)-P_{n}(Y=0,B>b\,|\,H=h)|\right.

Lemma 4
.

Under perfect alignment, consider a selection algorithm that for confidence values

\bar{B}_{t}(H_{t})\in\mathcal{B}

By the law of total expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R(T)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{h_{t},b_{t}\sim P(H,B),a_{t}\sim P(A_{t}\,|\,H_{t},B_{t})}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu(\pi^{*}(h_{t},b_{t})\,|\,h_{t},b_{t})-\mu(a_{t}\,|\,h_{t},b_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{h_{t}\sim P(H)}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{b_{t}\sim P(B\,|\,H=h_{t}),a_{t}\sim P(A\,|\,H=h_{t},B=b_{t})}\left[\mu(\pi^{*}(h_{t},b_{t})\,|\,h_{t},b_{t})-\mu(a_{t}\,|\,h_{t},b_{t})\right]\right]

\bar{b}

A_{t}

\bar{b}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{b_{t}\sim P(B\,|\,H=h_{t})}

\displaystyle\left[\mu(A_{t}\,|\,H_{t}=h_{t},B_{t}=b_{t})\,|\,\bar{B}_{t}=\bar{b}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{b_{t}\sim P(B\,|\,H=h_{t})}\left[\mathbb{E}[\mu(1\,|\,h_{t},b_{t})\cdot\mathbb{I}[b_{t}>\bar{b}]]+\mathbb{E}[\mu(0\,|\,h_{t},b_{t})\cdot\mathbb{I}[b_{t}\leq\bar{b}]]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bar{b}_{t}\sim P(\bar{B}_{t}(H_{t})\,|\,H_{t}=h_{t})}[\mathbb{E}_{b_{t}\sim P(B\,|\,H=h_{t})}\left[\mu(A_{t}\,|\,h_{t},b_{t})\,|\,\bar{B}_{t}(H_{t})=\bar{b}_{t}(h_{t})\right]

The VC dimension

of

is defined as the largest integer

of

\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 13

(Envelope function

)

An envelope function of a class
\mathcal{F}
of functions is any function

such that

\displaystyle=n\sup_{\Delta\in\mathcal{D}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\Delta(K_{i},X_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{K,X\sim P(K,X)}(\Delta(K,X))\right|

To show statement

Appendix B
Learning to Decide with AI Assistance under Imperfect Alignment

In this section, we show that, under imperfect alignment, the decision policy found by Algorithm
1
is near-optimal, and the degree of alignment, as measured by the maximum alignment error (MAE), bounds the near-optimality gap.

To this end, we begin with the observation that the expected utility achieved by the decision policy
\pi(h,b)=\mathbb{I}[b>b^{*}(h)]
, defined in Theorem
2
, matches that achieved by the optimal decision policy
\pi^{*}_{\texttt{mono}}
within the set of decision policies monotone in the AI confidence.
9
9

9

A decision policy
\pi
is monotone in the AI confidence if, for any
h\in\mathcal{H}
and any
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
b\leq b^{\prime}
, it holds that
\pi(h,b)\leq\pi(h,b^{\prime})
.

This is because any policy
\pi_{\texttt{mono}}
monotone in the AI confidence can be expressed as a threshold function
\pi_{\texttt{mono}}(h,b)=\mathbb{I}[b>b(h)]
, as shown elsewhere
(
7
)
.

Based on the above observation and Theorem 5 in
8
,
which characterizes the maximum gap between the expected utility of the optimal decision policy
\pi^{*}
and the optimal decision policy within the set of decision policies monotone in both the human and AI confidence
10
10

10

A decision policy
\pi
is monotone in the human and the AI confidence if, for any
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
and
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
h\leq h^{\prime}
and
b\leq b^{\prime}
, it holds that
\pi(h,b)\leq\pi(h^{\prime},b^{\prime})
.
,
it readily follows that

~\mathbb{E}_{H,B\sim P(H,B)}[\mu(\pi^{*}(H,B)\,|\,H,B)]-\mathbb{E}_{H,B\sim P(H,B)}[\mu(\pi^{*}_{\texttt{mono}}(H,B)\,|\,H,B)]\\
\leq\text{MAE}\cdot\left[u(1,1)-u(0,1)+\frac{3}{2}(u(0,0)-u(0,1))\right],

(53)

because the set of decision policies that are monotone with respect to the AI confidence includes the set of decision policies that are monotone with respect to the human and the AI confidence.

Finally, since Algorithm
1
is guaranteed to learn
\pi^{*}_{\texttt{mono}}
as
T
grows, as shown in Theorems
4
and
6
, we can conclude that Algorithm
1
is near-optimal under imperfect alignment.
