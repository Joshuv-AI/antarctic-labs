Adaptive Fourier decomposition-based time-frequency analysis of COVID-19 epidemic trends and policy interventions in Washington D.C., with comparative validation in London
Authors: Kerui Cen, Xuanfeng Li, Guibin Lu, Yiping Li, Yangqianxi Wang, Zhiqi Zeng, Zifeng Yang, Xiaoyan Deng, Chitin Hon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpidemic trends respond dynamically to public health interventions, and retrospective evaluation of these responses is important for future preparedness. However, conventional epidemiological and time-series approaches often have limited ability to resolve the multi-scale and non-stationary fluctuations of epidemic data, which may obscure temporal structures associated with intervention timing. This study aimed to apply adaptive Fourier decomposition (AFD) to characterize coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) epidemic dynamics and examine their temporal associations with public health policy interventions.MethodsDaily confirmed COVID-19 case data for Washington D.C. were collected through a multi-step source verification process using public data repositories and official local surveillance records. The archived New York Times COVID-19 GitHub repository was used as the initial structured dataset, and the Washington D.C. case series was cross-checked and corrected against the official District of Columbia government COVID-19 surveillance data. Public health intervention data were collected primarily from official mayoral orders and related policy announcements issued by the District of Columbia government. The epidemic time series and policy timeline were then aligned chronologically, and the Washington D.C. study period was divided into five epidemic waves. For comparative validation, the London daily COVID-19 case series was obtained from the authors of a published AFD-based study. AFD was applied to decompose the epidemic time series into interpretable time-frequency components, and newly constructed components were further generated for correlation analysis. The decomposition results were compared with those obtained using empirical mode decomposition (EMD) and variational mode decomposition (VMD).ResultsThe third-order AFD components showed the strongest correlations with the original epidemic signal and effectively captured both long-term trend variation and short-term local fluctuations. In Washington D.C., the third-order components performed particularly well during the Omicron-dominated fifth wave, when major policy adjustments were implemented. Compared with EMD and VMD, AFD provided clearer multi-scale structures and stronger statistical associations with observed epidemic trajectories. Similar decomposition patterns and correlation results were observed in the London comparison, supporting the robustness of the proposed framework in a comparative urban setting.ConclusionsAFD provides a useful framework for analyzing non-stationary epidemic time series and for retrospectively examining temporal relationships between epidemic waves and public health interventions. In this study, the low-frequency and third-order AFD components captured the major epidemic trend more effectively than the comparison methods, suggesting that AFD may help identify meaningful shifts in epidemic structure under changing intervention conditions. These findings indicate that AFD may complement conventional epidemic surveillance by supporting interpretation of multi-scale epidemic fluctuations, improving assessment of intervention timing, and providing an additional analytical reference for future mitigation planning and monitoring of respiratory infectious disease outbreaks.

Key findings

• Adaptive Fourier decomposition (AFD) effectively captures both short- and long-term fluctuations in coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) daily infection data.

• The third-order AFD components show the highest correlation with real infection trends, especially during the Omicron-driven fifth wave.

• AFD provided clearer time-frequency structures and stronger statistical associations than empirical mode decomposition (EMD) and variational mode decomposition (VMD) in revealing epidemic trends aligned with policy interventions.

What is known and what is new?

• Existing time-frequency methods like EMD and VMD have been used to analyze epidemic trends but lack mathematical rigor and often produce scale aliasing.

• This study applied AFD to COVID-19 case data from Washington D.C. and used London as a comparative validation setting, showing that AFD can provide a mathematically grounded and interpretable framework for epidemic trend analysis under changing policy contexts.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• AFD may serve as a useful supplementary tool for retrospective assessment of epidemic intervention timing and epidemic trend evolution.

• In future respiratory epidemic surveillance, AFD-based signal analysis may be incorporated into monitoring and early-warning frameworks alongside conventional epidemiological methods.

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has imposed substantial burdens on public health systems, social life, and economic activity worldwide. In densely populated urban settings, epidemic dynamics are shaped not only by viral evolution and population mobility, but also by the timing, intensity, and continuity of public health interventions. Daily epidemic case series therefore often exhibit nonlinear, non-stationary, and multi-scale fluctuations. Understanding these temporal structures is important for retrospective policy evaluation and for improving preparedness for future large-scale respiratory infectious disease outbreaks (1-3).

Washington D.C. is a particularly relevant urban setting for such analysis because of its high population density, policy visibility, and marked social and health disparities (1). From March 2020 onward, the city experienced multiple epidemic waves associated with repeated transitions in restrictions, reopening measures, masking requirements, vaccination campaigns, and booster promotion (2-8). These repeated intervention shifts provide an informative setting for examining the temporal relationship between epidemic fluctuations and public health measures. In addition, London was included in this study as a comparative validation setting to assess whether the proposed analytical framework could capture similar epidemic structures under a different urban and policy environment (9).

A wide range of methods has been used to analyze epidemic dynamics. Traditional epidemiological models, such as susceptible-infected (SI), susceptible-infected-susceptible (SIS), susceptible-infected-recovered (SIR), and susceptible-exposed-infected-recovered (SEIR) models, are useful for mechanistic descriptions of disease transmission (10,11). However, these approaches mainly focus on population transition processes and are less suitable for directly characterizing the multi-scale time-frequency structures of observed daily case signals under changing intervention conditions. To address the complexity of non-stationary epidemic series, signal decomposition methods have also been applied. EMD has been used in COVID-19 time-series analysis; for example, Lu et al. demonstrated in their study the results of applying EMD to epidemic time-series analysis (12). Nevertheless, EMD may suffer from mode mixing, scale aliasing, and limited mathematical interpretability, which can affect the physical interpretation of instantaneous frequency and decomposition results (13-15). Variational mode decomposition (VMD) provides a variational optimization framework with improved mode separation under some conditions, but it generally requires predefined mode numbers and parameter settings, which may affect robustness and interpretability when the underlying epidemic structure is complex (16).

Adaptive Fourier decomposition (AFD) is a mathematically rigorous signal decomposition framework developed in Hardy space that adaptively selects basis elements according to signal characteristics (17-20). Compared with empirical decomposition approaches, AFD provides stronger theoretical support, improved convergence properties, and clearer interpretability for non-stationary signal analysis (17,19,20). It has also been successfully applied in biomedical signal processing, including lung-heart sound separation and electrocardiogram denoising (21-23). More recently, AFD has been used to analyze severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) epidemic waves in London, suggesting its potential value in policy-related epidemic signal analysis (9). However, the application of a mathematically rigorous adaptive decomposition framework to characterize intervention-related COVID-19 fluctuations in urban comparative contexts remains limited. This represents an important knowledge gap, because policy-related epidemic signals require decomposition methods that are both adaptive and mathematically well grounded. The present study is intended to support population-level retrospective policy analysis rather than patient-level causal or clinical inference.

Therefore, this study applied AFD to daily COVID-19 case data from Washington D.C. to characterize epidemic trends and examine their temporal associations with major public health interventions. We further compared AFD with EMD and VMD to evaluate their relative abilities to represent epidemic fluctuations and policy-related temporal structures. London was included as a comparative validation case to assess the robustness of the proposed framework across different urban policy settings.

Methods

Data sources for epidemic case series

Daily confirmed COVID-19 case data for Washington D.C. were collected through a multi-step source verification process. Several public data sources were initially reviewed, including the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), COVID Act Now, USAFacts, the District of Columbia government COVID-19 surveillance website, and the Johns Hopkins University Coronavirus Resource Center (24-29). However, some of these sources provided weekly aggregated indicators rather than daily case counts, while others reported percentage-based metrics that were not suitable for the present time-frequency decomposition analysis.

To obtain a continuous daily case series, we used the archived New York Times COVID-19 GitHub repository as the initial structured dataset (28). The Washington D.C. records extracted from this dataset were then cross-checked against the official case information published by the District of Columbia government (27). During the verification process, discrepancies were identified and corrected according to the official government records. After correction, the resulting daily case series was used as the final epidemic dataset for the Washington D.C. analysis. Johns Hopkins University data were also reviewed as an auxiliary reference but were not selected as the primary analytical dataset because of inconsistencies with the official local reports (30).

For comparative validation, the London daily COVID-19 case series used in this study was provided by the authors of Liu et al. (9) upon request. This dataset corresponded to the daily COVID-19 infections in London from January 31, 2020 to February 24, 2022 reported in their published study. London was included solely as a comparative validation setting. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Collection of policy intervention data

Public health intervention data for Washington D.C. were collected primarily from official mayoral orders and related policy announcements issued by the District of Columbia government during the COVID-19 pandemic (31). These official documents provided detailed information on the issue date, duration, extension, and modification of intervention measures, including restrictions, mask mandates, reopening policies, vaccination requirements, and related control measures.

To facilitate interpretation and timeline construction, supplementary policy summaries from the Johns Hopkins University Coronavirus Resource Center and other policy-tracking websites were also reviewed (32-34). These secondary sources were used only as supportive references for summarizing policy content and organizing the timeline. In cases of discrepancy, the official government legal and administrative documents were treated as the reference standard.

Alignment of epidemic time series and intervention periods

After the epidemic case data and policy data were collected, the two datasets were aligned chronologically to examine the temporal correspondence between daily COVID-19 trends and intervention periods. A policy timeline was first established according to the official issue dates and effective durations of the mayoral orders. The daily epidemic case series was then matched to this intervention timeline for subsequent AFD-based time-frequency analysis.

Because no official wave classification specifically for Washington D.C. was identified, epidemic wave division was determined by combining the local epidemic trend with the broader wave framework commonly used for the United States during the same period (4-7). Considering that many U.S. jurisdictions relaxed major restrictions in 2022 and that later Omicron-related waves were generally managed under substantially different policy contexts, the present study focused on the earlier policy-intensive phase. Accordingly, the primary Washington D.C. analysis was restricted to the period ending on March 17, 2022, corresponding to the last mayoral order specifically related to epidemic prevention included in this study. Based on the alignment between the Washington D.C. epidemic trend, policy transitions, and the commonly referenced five-wave U.S. epidemic framework, the Washington D.C. epidemic period analyzed in this study was divided into five waves.

Scope of analysis and data limitation

This study was based on publicly available aggregated epidemic surveillance data rather than individual-level clinical records. Therefore, baseline clinical variables such as age stratification, comorbidities, and individual vaccination status were not available for inclusion in the decomposition analysis. The purpose of the present study was to characterize population-level temporal patterns and their associations with intervention periods, rather than to establish patient-level mechanistic or causal inferences.

In addition, the observed temporal correspondence between decomposed epidemic components and intervention periods may also be influenced by factors such as reporting delays, testing practices, and incubation-related lags. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as population-level time-frequency associations under changing policy contexts.

AFD

Empirical mode decomposition (EMD) has been widely applied for signal decomposition; however, it lacks a rigorous mathematical foundation, making the interpretation of its results challenging in practical applications (13,21). By contrast, AFD provides a mathematically rigorous framework for adaptive signal decomposition (17-20). AFD constitutes an advanced signal processing methodology extending the conventional Fourier analysis techniques. Unlike the conventional Fourier Transform, which dissects signals into a predetermined ensemble of sinusoidal basis functions encompassing sine and cosine functions across varying frequencies, AFD dynamically adjusts its basis functions in accordance with the specific characteristics of the signal under examination (13,22). This adaptability makes AFD particularly effective for analyzing non-stationary signals whose spectral composition changes over time. AFD decomposes a signal into a sequence of orthogonal components under an energy distribution framework. By proficiently partitioning a signal into a sequence of orthogonal components, AFD ensures each component is delineated by solely non-negative analytic phase derivatives. These components are systematically arranged in a hierarchy ranging from high-energy modes to low-energy modes. This feature provides AFD with a remarkable edge, especially when dealing with signals marked by intersecting frequencies, thus setting it apart from other methods of decomposition. Given that daily COVID-19 case data are nonlinear, AFD serves as an effective tool for decomposing infection trends under different policy contexts.

To evaluate the epidemiological relevance of the decomposed signals, Pearson correlation analysis was subsequently performed between the newly constructed components and the original daily COVID-19 case series. The resulting correlations were then compared across different decomposition orders and further contrasted with the corresponding results obtained from EMD and VMD in later analyses.

Statistical analysis

Because this study was based on publicly available aggregated epidemic surveillance data rather than individual-level clinical records, patient-level baseline characteristics and clinical subgroup comparisons were not applicable. The statistical analysis therefore focused on evaluating the association between decomposed time-frequency components and the original epidemic time series. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the newly constructed AFD components and the original daily COVID-19 case series. The same correlation analysis was also applied to components generated by EMD and VMD to enable comparison across decomposition methods. Correlation coefficients and corresponding P values were reported. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and P<0.01 was considered highly significant where applicable. All results were interpreted as population-level temporal associations rather than causal or patient-level effects.

The first wave (March 2020 to July 2020) corresponded to the initial outbreak period, during which strict emergency control measures were introduced, including gathering restrictions, school closure, stay-at-home requirements, and mask-related measures. The second wave (July 2020 to October 2020) was characterized by relatively moderate fluctuations under the continued public emergency framework and the maintenance of remote educational measures. The third wave (October 2020 to July 2021) was associated with a substantial winter increase in infections and renewed restrictions on reopening and indoor activities. Component analysis

Figure 1 combines daily infection data with government measures. The red boxes represent anti-epidemic policies, the green boxes represent reopening measures, and the blue boxes represent vaccination policies. A comparison between the first six components in Figure 3 and the original daily infection data in Figure 1 shows that these components broadly reflect the trend of the fifth wave of infections in Washington, D.C. This wave was driven by the rapid spread of the Omicron B.1.1.529 variant. From the sixth component, severe fluctuations can be observed from the fifth wave. Meanwhile, the extension of the COVID-19 public emergency and mask mandate, alongside vaccination campaigns, was temporally associated with a subsequent reduction in the epidemic signal in the District of Columbia. The results of the seventh component show the first, third, and fourth trends of waves (Table 1), which reflect the relatively medium and long-term number of infections (31-34). These intervention periods may reflect the influence of measures such as restaurant capacity limits, masking requirements, and vaccination promotion on the observed epidemic fluctuations, although the relationships should be interpreted cautiously. The severe fluctuations can be observed from the fifth wave, reflecting the short-term local fluctuations during the shock. The findings of the eighth component exhibit pronounced fluctuations since the year 2020. While the second fluctuation may not be as readily apparent, a slight arch can be discerned in the curve. This pattern may be associated with the period during which self-isolation and distance education measures were in place. The pronounced fluctuations in the fourth and fifth waves show epidemics from the Delta variant and Omicron B.1.1.529 variant. The observed reduction in epidemic intensity may be associated with the implementation of control measures, including booster vaccination campaigns and proof-of-vaccination requirements in public settings. Although the seventh and eighth components also display fluctuations after April 2022, these were excluded from analysis because the government had ceased updating epidemic policies and the Omicron variants during this period were characterized by mild symptoms, rapid spread, and influenza-like patterns.

Connection between AFD decompositions and government measures

The Omicron variant (B.1.1.529) of SARS-CoV-2 was initially brought to the attention of the World Health Organization (WHO) by the Network for Genomics Surveillance in South Africa on November 24, 2021 (40,41). Originating in Botswana, it has subsequently disseminated to attain predominance among circulating variants globally. The Omicron variant, despite its lower severity, exhibits high levels of contagiousness, resulting in unprecedented rates of infection across all geographical regions (42). Because of this contagiousness, the relationship between immunization policies and daily infections at this stage can be better studied. We use the 3rd-order AFD new construction components result and the daily COVID-19 infection count under epidemic prevention policies to segmentation and combination.

Figure 5A-5C is different kinds of construction of the AFD. Table 4 is the correlations between components of distinct new construction components and original infections of the fifth wave. Compared with based AFD and second-order AFD, the 3rd-order AFD has the highest Pearson correlation coefficient and the lowest P value, which means the 3rd-order AFD is closer to the original trend and has better statistical significance. Table 5 is the correlations between components of different methods and original infections of the fifth wave. Compared with EMD and VMD, the 3rd-order AFD has the highest Pearson correlation coefficient and the lowest P value at the first component. In this research, the first component of the 3rd-order AFD new construction components result will be used to study the relationship between the overall trend of daily infections and epidemic prevention policies for the 5th wave in the District of Columbia. Figure 5C is the 3rd-order AFD new construction components. The first component appeared to capture the overall trend of daily infections in the District of Columbia more closely during the fifth epidemic wave. Figure 6 is different kinds of construction of the AFD of the 5th wave. Figure 6A shows the first component of 3rd-order AFD is better than EMD’s in Figure 6B and VMD’s in Figure 6C. Figure 6 combines the original daily infection for the 5th wave, the first component of the 3rd-order AFD for the 5th wave, and the corresponding policies. As shown in the Figure 7, after the fourth wave, the government of the District of Columbia announced on November 19 that fully vaccinated individuals were exempt from the masking requirement. It is a policy of relaxation, and it may lead to an increase in infections by the Omicron variant, as a specific booster vaccine targeting Omicron has not yet been developed. When daily infections are increasing quickly, the government posted a new mayor’s order to reduce the infection. Following the issuance of the mayor’s directive to reimpose the indoor mask mandate, the government declared a state of emergency. In compliance with this declaration, it mandates that both business and governmental personnel receive booster vaccinations and furnish evidence thereof. Additionally, individuals are required to present proof of vaccination for admittance into public venues, effective from December 22nd onwards. The relationship between these two policies is about the mask order and proof of vaccination. Therefore, the mask requirement and proof-of-vaccination policies may have been temporally associated with the major epidemic fluctuations observed during this period, although these associations should not be interpreted as direct mechanistic evidence.

Comparison with the Omicron wave in London

COVID-19 infections in London showed a trend similar to that in the District of Columbia. In England, a series of distinct epidemiological waves have been observed, characterized by shifts in dominant viral variants. The initial wave spanning from January to July 2020 coincided with the prevalence of the wild type variant. Subsequently, a second wave ensued from August 2020 to May 2021, marked initially by the wild type variant and later compounded by the emergence of the Alpha variant. Following this, a third wave transpired between May and December 2021, predominantly attributed to the Delta variant. Presently, the ongoing fourth wave is predominantly propelled by the Omicron variant, with discernible subdivisions denoted by two distinct sub-waves: BA.1 spanning from December 2021 to March 2022, succeeded by BA.2 from March 2022 onwards (43). However, from the policy perspective, England experienced only three national lockdowns before March 2021 (30).

Owing to the absence of well-defined parameters and regulatory frameworks delineating the phases of the delta variant and its subsequent iterations throughout the course of infection, a methodological approach has been adopted to facilitate a more comprehensive comparison of infection those observed in the District of Columbia. Specifically, the commencement of the initial relaxation of restrictions subsequent to the third wave of lockdown is identified as the baseline, while the conclusion preceding the emergence of the Omicron BA.2 variant is designated as the reference period for the fourth wave in London (44). The other reason is the Prime Minister announced the lifting of all COVID-19 legal restrictions on February 24, 2022 (45). Therefore, the fourth wave in London is from July 9 2021 to February 18 2022. Figure 8 is daily infection in London from February 3 2020 to July 3 2023. The fourth wave in London includes the waves of Delta and Omicron, which correspond to the fourth and fifth waves of the epidemic in the District of Columbia, so it is about four months longer than the fifth wave of the Washington, D.C., epidemic. Therefore, the first component of the third-order AFD may provide a useful summary of the overall epidemic trend in London and may support comparative interpretation of policy-related temporal patterns.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically examined changes in daily COVID-19 infection rates in the District of Columbia and assessed the public health measures implemented by the government. Our objective was to evaluate the effectiveness of these interventions in mitigating the impact of the pandemic. The epidemic prevention strategies adopted in Washington, D.C. served as a key focus of our analysis. We used AFD to break down the daily infection data in relation to government policies. Additionally, we combined various elements from the decomposition results to identify trends at different levels. Our analysis identified five distinct phases of significant change in infection rates, with particular emphasis on the fifth phase driven by Omicron, where mask mandates and proof-of-vaccination requirements played a central role. Additionally, we examine the fourth wave of the epidemic in London during the corresponding time frame and observe that the decomposition outcomes and Pearson correlation coefficients of the AFD closely mirror those of the fifth wave in the District of Columbia, suggesting the reliability and validity of the findings.

Wavelet-based methods are also widely used for non-stationary signal analysis and may provide strong time localization for abrupt transients. This characteristic can be advantageous when the main objective is to detect short-lived local spikes or abrupt reporting shocks in epidemic data. However, the present study focused on decomposing daily COVID-19 case series into interpretable multi-scale components under evolving public health intervention contexts. In this setting, AFD was selected because it provides a mathematically rigorous adaptive decomposition framework with clear basis selection, favorable convergence properties, and convenient interpretation across different frequency scales. Therefore, the use of AFD in this study reflects its suitability for structured multi-scale epidemic trend analysis rather than a claim of universal superiority over all other non-stationary signal methods.

In addition, the present framework did not explicitly model epidemiological delays such as incubation periods, testing delays, or reporting lags. Therefore, the observed temporal relationships between AFD components and policy implementation dates should not be interpreted as instantaneous causal effects. Instead, they should be understood as population-level temporal associations under potentially lagged epidemic responses. In practical terms, policy-related changes in the decomposed epidemic signal may reflect delayed responses rather than same-day effects, and this issue should be considered when interpreting the correspondence between intervention periods and epidemic fluctuations.

This study also has several limitations. First, the analysis was based on publicly available aggregated epidemic surveillance data rather than individual-level clinical records. As a result, baseline variables such as age stratification, comorbidities, and individual vaccination status were not incorporated into the decomposition model. Second, the Washington D.C. wave classification used in this study was based on alignment between local epidemic trends, intervention timelines, and the broader U.S. wave framework, rather than on an officially defined local wave system. Third, the London dataset was used only for comparative validation, and its role was supportive rather than equivalent to the primary Washington D.C. analysis.

Conclusions

Our analysis of newly constructed composite components across different scales revealed a positive correlation between AFD component frequencies and daily infection rates under policy interventions, as indicated by elevated Pearson coefficients. This correlation evolved over time, gradually shifting from high- to low-frequency AFD components, indicating that they captured the major epidemic trend more effectively. A comparative assessment of AFD against EMD and VMD demonstrated AFD’s superior capability in capturing essential temporal and frequency domain insights pertaining to epidemic prevention strategies derived from the trio of methodologies.
