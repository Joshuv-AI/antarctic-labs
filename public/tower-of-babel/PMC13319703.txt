High hydrostatic pressure–induced structural remodeling of walnut meal protein and its functional implications: Integrated experimental and molecular dynamics insights
Authors: Weiqian Wang, Guoli Yan, Jianbing Li, Yang Tian, Feng Zhang, Muhammad Aaqil, Yuwei Guo, Jingchuan Zheng, Lijun You, Cunchao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Walnut meal protein (WMP) represents an underutilized plant protein resource with considerable application potential. However, the molecular basis underlying its structural responsiveness to high hydrostatic pressure (HHP) remains insufficiently elucidated. This study systematically investigated the effects of HHP (100–600 MPa, 5–20 min) on the structural, physicochemical, and functional properties of WMP by integrating spectroscopic analyses with molecular dynamics simulations. HHP markedly reduced solubility (48.4% to 4.6% at 600 MPa, 20 min) while increasing turbidity and particle size, indicating pressure-induced aggregation. Surface hydrophobicity and absolute zeta potential increased, whereas free sulfhydryl content decreased by up to 64.81%, reflecting unfolding followed by intermolecular association. Circular dichroism revealed reduced α-helix content with increased β-sheet and random coil structures, and fluorescence spectroscopy confirmed tertiary structural perturbation and enhanced hydrophobic exposure. These structural transitions significantly improved functional performance, with emulsifying activity index reaching 33.9 m2/g, foaming ability 47.52%, and marked increases in water- and oil-holding capacities. Molecular dynamics simulations corroborated experimental observations, demonstrating pressure-dependent conformational destabilization, increased solvent-accessible surface area, reduced radius of gyration, and a roughened free-energy landscape. Collectively, these findings elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying HHP-induced structural reorganization of WMP and provide a rational basis for tailoring plant protein functionality through non-thermal processing.

Introduction

Protein is essential for growth, metabolic regulation, and immune function; however, modern dietary transitions have contributed to nutritional imbalance and increased chronic disease incidence, with excessive animal protein consumption linked to elevated cardiovascular risk (Yao et al., 2024). In response, plant proteins have gained increasing attention as sustainable and health-promoting alternatives due to their nutritional adequacy, economic accessibility, and lower environmental burden. Beyond their nutritional role, plant proteins also exhibit valuable techno-functional properties, including emulsification, foaming, viscosity enhancement, and water- and oil-holding capacities (Baskıncı & Gul, 2023). Consequently, recent research has increasingly focused on elucidating the physicochemical, nutritional, and functional characteristics of emerging plant protein sources to expand their applicability in food systems. In this context, the valorization of agro-industrial by-products as alternative protein resources represents a sustainable and economically advantageous strategy for addressing the rising global demand for high-quality protein ingredients.

Walnut is widely cultivated in China, where production increased from 829,600 tons in 2008 to 4,795,970 tons in 2020, making it the world's leading producer. Walnut meal, a by-product of oil extraction, retains substantial nutritional components and contains over 40% protein. However, it is largely used as animal feed or discarded, leading to underutilization of this valuable protein resource (Wang et al., 2023). Walnut meal protein (WMP) comprises albumins, globulins, prolamins, and glutenins, containing 18 amino acids, including eight essential amino acids that meet FAO/WHO adult dietary recommendations. Compared with commonly consumed plant proteins such as wheat, soybean, and corn proteins, WMP contains a higher proportion of essential amino acids and exhibits a more balanced amino acid profile, making it a promising plant-based source of essential amino acids for adults. In addition, WMP is relatively enriched in branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) and neutral amino acids, representing a compositional feature that distinguishes it from several conventional plant proteins (Wen, Zhang, Cao, Liu, Liang, Liu, et al., 2023). Since BCAAs are generally highly hydrophobic, their relatively high abundance contributes to the hydrophobic characteristics of WMP (Brosnan & Brosnan, 2006). This chemical profile may facilitate the adsorption of WMP at the oil-water interface, thereby providing a structural basis for its potential application in emulsion systems, interfacial stabilization, and protein-based delivery platforms (Li et al., 2022). Therefore, WMP represents a high-quality plant protein with strong potential for food applications (Liu, Zhang, Shang, Li, Xia, Tian, et al., 2024). Nevertheless, the broader utilization of plant proteins is often constrained by suboptimal functional performance, including limited foaming and emulsifying capacities and sensitivity to variations in pH, ionic strength, and temperature (Gül et al., 2025). These limitations restrict the incorporation of plant proteins into complex food systems, making targeted structural modification essential to optimize the functionality of WMP and enhance its techno-functional performance. Protein modification involves structural alteration or introduction of functional groups through physical, chemical, or biological approaches. However, chemical methods such as phosphorylation, oxidation, and glycosylation are often constrained by regulatory and safety concerns, while biological strategies including enzymatic hydrolysis and polymerization may compromise nutritional or sensory quality (Kong et al., 2026). In contrast, physical modification techniques including ultrasound, thermal processing, and high-pressure processing offer advantages such as operational simplicity, efficiency, and the generation of non-toxic products (Zhang et al., 2025). Among physical modification strategies, high hydrostatic pressure (HHP) has emerged as a scalable, cost-effective non-thermal technology compatible with clean-label demands and widely applied in the food industry as a sustainable and energy-efficient approach to protein modification (W. Wang, Yang, Rao, Zhao, Wu, Wang et al., 2022).

Extensive studies have demonstrated that HHP can significantly enhance protein functionality, including improvements in water-holding capacity, foaming behavior, and emulsifying performance (Dehnad et al., 2024). For example, Deng et al. (2025) reported that HHP treatment (100–500 MPa) markedly improved water- and oil-holding capacities, foaming properties, and emulsifying characteristics of pepper seed protein isolate. Similarly, Hall and Moraru (2021) observed enhanced foaming capacity and stability in pea, lentil, and faba bean proteins treated at 600 MPa for 4 min. Despite these advances, comprehensive mechanistic investigations into the physicochemical and functional modifications of WMP induced by HHP remain scarce, partly due to the intrinsic variability of plant protein systems and the complexity of pressure–time interactions.

The functional impact of HHP is highly dependent on pressure and treatment time, as excessive exposure may induce irreversible denaturation or aggregation. Conventional experiments also struggle to isolate pressure-specific effects from environmental and energy-related influences (Zhang et al., 2025). In contrast, computational techniques, particularly molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, enable precise control of thermodynamic parameters and provide molecular-level insight into conformational transitions. Yang et al. (2024) employed molecular dynamics simulations to elucidate pressure-induced alterations in the tertiary structure and residue interactions of ovalbumin, demonstrating the utility of computational approaches for mechanistic interpretation. Zhou et al. (2022) also combined molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with spectroscopic experiments to elucidate, at the molecular level, the mechanisms by which high-pressure processing induces conformational changes in polyphenol oxidase, including alterations in its active center, substrate channel, and binding capacity. Han et al. (2022) employed MD simulations to investigate the effect of ultrasound on the adsorption of phenol by single-walled carbon nanotubes, using positive and negative square waves to mimic ultrasound-induced pressure fluctuations. Building on these studies, the integration of in vitro experiments with molecular simulations provides an effective auxiliary and complementary strategy for elucidating the pressure-induced structural remodeling mechanism of WMP. However, to date, no comprehensive study has systematically integrated experimental characterization with molecular dynamics simulations to elucidate the pressure-induced structural remodeling mechanisms of walnut meal protein. In particular, the molecular basis linking HHP-triggered conformational destabilization, aggregation behavior, and functional enhancement in WMP remains unclear.

This study systematically investigates the influence of HHP treatments on the structural, physicochemical, and functional properties of WMP. By integrating comprehensive experimental characterization with molecular dynamics simulations, this work aims to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying pressure-induced conformational reorganization and aggregation of WMP. The findings provide a mechanistic foundation for optimizing HHP-based modification strategies and advancing the high-value utilization of walnut meal protein in food processing applications.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Walnut meal was obtained from Anyang Guanghua Grain and Oil Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). Ellman's reagent was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The Bradford protein assay kit was supplied by Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Reagents and materials for protein characterization, including precast SDS–PAGE gels, 4× SDS–PAGE loading buffer, Coomassie Brilliant Blue rapid staining solution, and molecular weight markers, were procured from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All additional chemicals and reagents employed in this study were of analytical grade and used without further purification.

Preparation of WMP

The WMP was extracted following a modified procedure described by Li et al. (2025). Walnut meal was finely ground, sieved (500 μm), and defatted with hexane (2:1, w/v) through five successive extractions until no visible lipid residue remained, followed by overnight drying. The defatted powder was dispersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 1 mmol/L, pH 7.2) at a 1:10 (w/v) ratio and adjusted to pH 8.5. After stirring at room temperature for 4 h, the mixture was centrifuged (10,000 ×g, 20 min, 4 °C). The supernatant was adjusted to pH 4.5 with 2 mol/L HCl to precipitate proteins, stirred for 1 h, and centrifuged under the same conditions. The precipitate was redispersed in deionized water (1:3, w/v), neutralized to pH 7.0 with 2 mol/L NaOH, dialyzed overnight against deionized water (7 kDa cutoff; MD44–7, Viskase Inc.), and lyophilized to obtain WMP powder.

HHP processing of WMP

The HHP treatment was performed following the procedure described by Yang et al. (2025). Pressure levels of 100–600 MPa were selected to encompass mild to intensive conditions commonly employed for plant protein modification, with treatment durations of 5–20 min reflecting industrially relevant processing times. The temperature was maintained at 25 °C to minimize thermal effects. WMP solutions (10 mg/mL in PBS) were degassed, sealed in polyethylene bags, and subjected to 0.1 MPa (control), 100, 200, 400, or 600 MPa at 25 °C for 5, 10, or 20 min using an SHPP-2 L high-pressure processing system (Shanxi Lidefu Technology Co., Ltd., Shanxi, China). Following treatment, samples were immediately frozen, lyophilized, and stored at −20 °C until further analysis.

Structural analysis of WMP

Zeta potential, average particle size and polydispersity index (PDI)

The zeta potential, average particle size, and PDI of WMP were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK) according to a slightly modified method described by Deng et al. (2025). WMP solutions (1 mg/mL) were centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 15 min, and the resulting supernatant was subjected to analysis. Measurements were performed at 25 °C with the refractive index set to 1.330. Data were recorded and processed using the instrument's associated software.

Free sulfhydryl content

The free sulfhydryl content of WMP was determined based on the method described by He et al. (2025). WMP solutions (2 mg/mL) were prepared in Tris–Glycine buffer and centrifuged at 8000 ×g for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was reacted with Ellman's reagent (5 mg/mL) and incubated in the dark for 15 min. Absorbance was subsequently measured at 412 nm using a spectrophotometer. The free sulfhydryl content was calculated according to the following equation:(1)−SHμmolg=73.53×A412C

Where A412 is the absorbance at 412 nm, and C denotes the concentration of the anopine solution.

Surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity was evaluated using a slightly modified method based on that reported by Huang et al. (2022). WMP solutions were diluted to 5 mg/mL in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.02 mol/L, containing 0.6 mol/L NaCl, pH 6.0). Subsequently, 20 μL of bromophenol blue (BPB, 1 mg/mL) was added to each sample and mixed thoroughly. The mixtures were centrifuged at 8500 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were subjected to a second centrifugation under the same conditions. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm using PBS as the blank. The amount of BPB bound to WMP was taken as an index of surface hydrophobicity and calculated according to the following equation:(2)BPB=20μg×Acontrol−AsampleAcontrol

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The SEM was conducted following the method established by He et al. (2025). WMP samples were uniformly mounted onto conductive adhesive stubs and coated with a thin layer of gold by ion sputtering under an argon atmosphere. Microstructural images were captured using a Phenom Pro scanning electron microscope (Phenom-World, Netherlands) at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV and a magnification of 500 × .

Electrophoretic analysis

The structural alterations of WMP induced by HHP were analyzed by SDS–PAGE according to the procedure described by Yang et al. (2025). WMP solutions (10 mg/mL, 20 μL) were mixed with 4× loading buffer (5 μL) containing 5% (w/v) β-mercaptoethanol and denatured at 100 °C for 5 min. After cooling, protein marker (6 μL) and sample aliquots (10 μL) were loaded onto polyacrylamide gels consisting of a 12% resolving gel and a 5% stacking gel. Electrophoresis was performed using a vertical system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA) at 80 V for 20 min, followed by 120 V for 60 min. The gels were subsequently stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue Rapid Stain for 1 h, and images were acquired using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Fusion Solo6s, Vilber, France).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The structural characteristics of WMP were characterized by FTIR spectroscopy using a PerkinElmer Spectrum Two spectrometer (PerkinElmer, USA), following the method reported by Chen et al. (2023) with minor modifications. FTIR spectra of untreated (control) and HHP-treated samples were recorded over the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1. Each spectrum was obtained from 28 consecutive scans, and the averaged spectra were used for subsequent analysis.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy

The HHP-induced changes in the secondary structure of WMP were analyzed by CD spectroscopy using a Chirascan spectropolarimeter (Applied Photophysics, UK), following the protocol described by Liu et al. (2025). WMP solutions (0.1 mg/mL, 200 μL) were placed in a 1 mm quartz cuvette, and spectra were recorded from 190 to 260 nm at a scanning speed of 100 nm/min with a bandwidth and resolution of 1 nm. Measurements were performed in triplicate, and averaged spectra were obtained after subtraction of the blank and baseline. Secondary structural components (α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil) were estimated using the DichroWeb online analysis platform.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy

The Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy was conducted according to the method described by He et al. (2025). WMP samples were diluted in PBS to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. Emission spectra were recorded using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (FL-6500, PerkinElmer Inc., USA) with an excitation wavelength of 290 nm. Spectra were collected over the range of 300–500 nm at a scanning rate of 200 nm/min, with both excitation and emission slit widths set to 5 nm.

Functional properties of WMP

Solubility

The solubility of HHP-treated WMP was determined according to the method described by Xu et al. (2023). WMP samples (protein content: 83.25 ± 2.07%) were prepared at 2.0 mg/mL and centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 15 min. The protein concentration in the supernatant was quantified using the Bradford assay following the manufacturer's instructions. Solubility (%) was calculated as the ratio of protein content in the supernatant to the initial total protein content × 100.

Turbidity

The turbidity was determined according to the method described by Xu et al. (2023). WMP solutions (2.0 mg/mL in PBS, 200 μL) were dispensed into a 96-well microplate, and absorbance was measured at 600 nm using a multi-mode microplate reader.

Emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsifying stability index (ESI)

The EAI and ESI were determined following the method described by He et al. (2025). Briefly, 15 mL of 1% (w/v) WMP solution was mixed with 5 mL of rapeseed oil and homogenized at 12,000 rpm for 1 min to form an emulsion. Immediately after homogenization and after 30 min of standing, 50 μL of the emulsion was diluted with 10 mL of 0.1% (w/v) SDS solution and thoroughly vortexed. Absorbance was measured at 500 nm. EAI and ESI were calculated according to the following equations:(3)EAIm2/g=2×2.303×A0×DFC×∅×θ×1000(4)ESImin=A0ΔA

In these equations, DF represents the dilution factor, ∅ is the oil volume fraction (0.25), C refers to the sample concentration (mg/mL), θ is the path length, and ΔA denotes the difference in absorbance between the two time points.

Foaming ability index (FAI) and foaming stability index (FSI)

The FAI and FSI were determined according to the method described by He et al. (2025). Briefly, 0.2 g of WMP powder was dispersed in 40 mL of deionized water (initial volume, V₁) and homogenized at 10,000 rpm for 3 min to generate foam. The total foam volume (V₂) was recorded immediately after whipping. Following standing at room temperature for 30 min, the remaining foam volume (V₃) was measured. FAI and FSI were calculated using the following equations:(5)FAI%=V2×V1V1×100(6)FSI%=V3×V1V1×100

Water holding capacity (WHC) and oil holding capacity (OHC)

The WHC and OHC of WMP were determined according to the method described by Zhang et al. (2018). Briefly, 10 mg of WMP was separately dispersed in 0.50 mL of distilled water or soybean oil. The mixtures were vortexed for 60 s, incubated at room temperature for 90 min, and centrifuged at 8000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. WHC and OHC were expressed as grams of water or oil retained per gram of protein.

Molecular dynamics simulations (MDS)

The three-dimensional structure of WMP (PDB ID: A0A2I4F8Z7) was obtained from the Universal Protein Resource and further optimized using AlphaFold 2, following the approach reported by Zhang et al. (2025). All molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the Amber99SB force field with explicit solvent representation. Each system was solvated in a cubic simulation box containing TIP3P water molecules, and counterions were added to ensure charge neutrality. Energy minimization was conducted for 20,000 steps using the conjugate gradient algorithm to achieve structural stabilization. Subsequently, the system was equilibrated for 500 ps under constant temperature (300 K, NVT ensemble) and constant pressure (1 atm, NPT ensemble) conditions. Production simulations were then carried out for 100 ns per system. Pressure coupling was implemented using the Parrinello–Rahman algorithm at 0.1 MPa (control), 100 MPa, 200 MPa, 400 MPa, and 600 MPa, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences among groups were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan's multiple range test using SPSS 19.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results and discussion

Average particle size and polydispersity index (PDI)

The average particle size and PDI are important indicators of protein aggregation behavior following HHP treatment. Native WMP exhibited an average particle diameter of approximately 324 nm and a PDI of 1.00, indicating a highly polydisperse system with heterogeneous size distribution. Such heterogeneity may adversely affect functional performance and practical application of the protein (Danaei, Dehghankhold, Ataei, Hasanzadeh Davarani, Javanmard, Dokhani, et al., 2018). With increasing pressure intensity and treatment duration, the average particle size increased significantly, accompanied by a marked reduction in PDI relative to the untreated control (Fig. 2A–2B). These results suggest that HHP promoted the association of smaller particles into larger and more uniformly distributed aggregates, thereby enhancing dispersion homogeneity.

Similar trends have been reported by Khati et al. (2024), who observed pressure-induced changes in particle characteristics of potato protein. Aggregation behavior under HHP has also been documented in other plant-derived proteins, including soy, kidney bean, and cowpea proteins (Queiros et al., 2018). To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying these pressure-induced alterations in particle size distribution, subsequent analyses focused on changes in zeta potential, free sulfhydryl content, and surface hydrophobicity.

Zeta potential

The absolute value of the zeta potential reflects the magnitude of electrostatic interactions among protein molecules. Higher absolute zeta potential values are generally associated with enhanced electrostatic stabilization, whereas lower values indicate a greater propensity for aggregation, including coagulation or flocculation (Zho et al., 2018). As shown in Fig. 2C, the absolute zeta potential of WMP increased progressively with rising pressure and treatment duration compared with the control, suggesting enhanced electrostatic stabilization of the dispersed fraction.

This trend is consistent with the reduced PDI observed in Fig. 2B, indicating a narrower particle size distribution following HHP treatment. The elevated absolute zeta potential may be attributed to pressure-induced conformational alterations that expose ionizable residues at the protein surface, thereby increasing surface charge density. Enhanced electrostatic repulsion would favor dispersion stability and uniformity. Nevertheless, despite the increased surface charge, HHP simultaneously promoted the formation of larger aggregates, as evidenced by the increased particle size (Fig. 2A). These findings suggest that HHP modulates the surface charge characteristics of WMP in a pressure- and time-dependent manner, influencing the dynamic balance between electrostatic stabilization and pressure-driven aggregation. Comparable increases in absolute zeta potential following HHP treatment have been reported for litchi thaumatin-like proteins.

Free sulfhydryl content

Free sulfhydryl content is closely associated with protein aggregation behavior. As shown in Fig. 2D, HHP treatment resulted in a significant reduction in free sulfhydryl content, with decreases ranging from 22.22% to 64.81% relative to the untreated control as pressure intensity and treatment duration increased. This pressure- and time-dependent decline indicates reduced accessibility of sulfhydryl groups in the treated samples.

The decrease is consistent with HHP-induced conformational unfolding followed by structural rearrangement, which may limit sulfhydryl exposure. Under elevated pressure, increased exposure of hydrophobic regions can enhance intra- and intermolecular hydrophobic interactions, potentially embedding sulfhydryl residues within aggregated structures and thereby reducing their measurable content (Liu et al., 2025). Concurrently, strengthened hydrophobic interactions may further accelerate aggregation (C.-Y. Wang et al., 2016) in agreement with the increased particle size observed under more intense treatment conditions. Similar trends have been reported for buckwheat silkworm pupae protein subjected to pressures ranging from 100 to 600 MPa (Yang et al., 2025).

Surface hydrophobicity(H0)

Surface hydrophobicity (H₀) reflects the extent of exposure of nonpolar amino acid residues on the protein surface (Dnyaneshwar Patil et al., 2024). As illustrated in Fig. 2E, all HHP treatments significantly increased H₀ relative to the untreated control, with the magnitude of enhancement generally intensifying as pressure and treatment duration increased. These findings indicate that HHP promoted the exposure of hydrophobic domains that were less accessible in native WMP.

The elevated H₀ values support the occurrence of pressure-induced surface remodeling and help explain the concurrent changes in particle size and dispersion behavior. Increased hydrophobic exposure enhances protein adsorption and interfacial film formation, consistent with the improved foaming and emulsifying properties observed after HHP treatment (Figs. 1C–1F). However, greater exposure of hydrophobic regions in aqueous environments also strengthens the driving force for protein–protein interactions, thereby promoting aggregation and contributing to the observed increase in particle size and reduction in solubility. Overall, HHP treatment generated more hydrophobic and interface-active WMP surfaces while simultaneously favoring the formation of larger aggregates. Microstructural analysis (SEM)

The untreated WMP exhibited a plate-like morphology characterized by smooth, intact surfaces and well-defined edges without visible fissures (Fig. 3). With increasing pressure (100–600 MPa) and treatment duration (5–20 min), surface cracking became evident, and the plate-like structures progressively fragmented into smaller flakes. These structural disruptions resulted in increased surface roughness, accompanied by protrusions and the formation of particulate features. The fragments subsequently associated to form denser, network-like assemblies with contracted yet discernible microporous characteristics (Pan, Li, Tuersuntuoheti, Wang, Zheng, Fang, et al., 2023). However, the morphological differences among treatments were relatively subtle and did not permit clear discrimination between specific pressure–time combinations at this magnification. Therefore, SEM was primarily employed to qualitatively visualize overall microstructural alterations, whereas pressure-dependent effects were interpreted principally based on quantitative physicochemical measurements and spectroscopic analyses presented in other sections.

Effect of HHP on the structural characteristics of WMP

SDS–PAGE analysis

SDS–PAGE analysis indicated that HHP treatment altered the subunit distribution of WMP. As pressure intensity and treatment duration increased (Fig. 4), the intensity of bands corresponding to approximately 15 kDa and 40 kDa gradually diminished, while new bands appeared at higher apparent molecular weights, indicating the formation of high-molecular-weight protein aggregates. The generation of these aggregates may be associated with the reduced availability of free sulfhydryl groups, which can facilitate intermolecular interactions and cross-linking (Yin et al., 2022). Consistent with these findings, Deng et al. (2025) reported similar aggregation phenomena in pepper seed protein isolates subjected to HHP, further highlighting the propensity of high-pressure treatment to promote the formation of large molecular assemblies.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis

Five characteristic absorption bands were identified in the FTIR spectra. The amide A band at 3270–3280 cm−1 was attributed to N—H stretching vibrations. The absorption band at 2930–2970 cm−1 was assigned to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of CH₂ and CH₃ groups in aliphatic side chains. These changes suggest disruption of intramolecular hydrogen-bonding networks and enhanced exposure of hydrophobic residues, thereby facilitating hydrophobic-driven aggregation. Similar intensity variations were observed in the amide I and amide II regions, indicating substantial alterations in secondary structure induced by HHP processing. The observed spectral shifts further imply incorporation of functional groups into aggregated protein structures during association (Liu et al., 2023c; Tan et al., 2024). Collectively, these FTIR results demonstrate that HHP significantly perturbs non-covalent interactions within WMP, leading to conformational destabilization and aggregation.

Circular dichroism CD spectra

The CD spectroscopy was employed to evaluate HHP-induced changes in the secondary structure of WMP(Fig. 5C–5D). As shown in Figs. 5E–5F, HHP treatment resulted in a reduction in α-helix content accompanied by an increase in random coil structures, indicating protein unfolding and subsequent aggregation (Panick et al., 1999). In addition, the progressive increase in β-sheet content with increasing pressure and treatment duration suggests the formation of intermolecular β-sheet networks during aggregation (Yin et al., 2022). Collectively, these results demonstrate that HHP markedly altered the secondary structural composition of WMP, facilitating aggregate formation. Similar structural transitions have been reported by Khati et al. (2024), who observed a decrease in α-helix content along with concomitant increases in β-sheet and random coil structures in potato protein aggregates subjected to HHP.

Fluorescence spectra

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy was employed to evaluate HHP-induced alterations in the tertiary structure of WMP, primarily through monitoring tryptophan and tyrosine residues. This technique is widely recognized as a sensitive indicator of conformational changes, reflecting variations in the polarity of the microenvironment surrounding tryptophan residues (Yang et al., 2024). Following HHP treatment, the maximum emission peak of WMP exhibited a blue shift, suggesting increased exposure of previously buried hydrophobic groups and a corresponding decrease in environmental polarity. Such a reduction in polarity is typically associated with protein relaxation or unfolding (Liu et al., 2023a). In addition, fluorescence intensity decreased markedly in HHP-treated samples compared with the untreated control and progressively declined with increasing pressure and treatment duration (Fig. 5G–5H). The attenuation of fluorescence intensity may result from conformational unfolding that enhances solvent exposure of tryptophan residues, thereby promoting fluorescence quenching. Exposure to pressures ranging from 100 to 600 MPa induced structural unfolding and increased surface hydrophobicity, potentially involving dissociation and reorganization of disulfide bonds under high-pressure conditions. This process may facilitate migration of previously buried thiol groups toward the protein surface, followed by aggregation or recombination of exposed hydrophobic regions, leading to the formation of more stable protein aggregates (Yang et al., 2024). These findings are consistent with those reported by Yang et al. (2025), who observed reduced fluorescence intensity and enhanced aggregation in silkworm pupae proteins subjected to HHP. Collectively, the fluorescence results corroborate the structural alterations identified by FTIR and CD analyses, confirming that HHP induces conformational remodeling and promotes aggregation of WMP.

Effect of HHP on the functional properties of WMP

Solubility and turbidity

Native WMP exhibited moderate solubility, as shown in Fig. 1A. However, solubility decreased significantly following HHP treatment (Fig. 1A), declining from 48.4% in the control to 4.6% at 600 MPa for 20 min. The marked reduction in soluble protein content suggests the formation of larger protein aggregates. This phenomenon may be attributed to HHP-induced enhancement of intermolecular interactions, promoting the assembly of dense, high-molecular-weight complexes that subsequently precipitated from solution (Wang et al., 2008). These results are consistent with previous reports by Deng et al. (2025) and Yang et al. (2025), who observed progressive aggregation and reduced solubility in pepper seed and silkworm pupae protein isolates with increasing HHP intensity.

Turbidity is widely recognized as an effective indicator of protein aggregation (Zhao et al., 2023c). As depicted in Fig. 1B, turbidity increased significantly after HHP treatment compared with the untreated control, further supporting the occurrence of aggregation. The increase in turbidity corresponded closely with the reduction in solubility. Notably, decreased solubility was accompanied by elevated turbidity (Fig. 1B) and increased particle size (Fig. 2A), while SDS–PAGE analysis revealed the emergence of higher-molecular-weight species (Fig. 4). Emulsifying activity and stability index analysis

The EAI and ESI are key parameters for evaluating the emulsifying capacity and stability of proteins (Yildiz et al., 2017). As illustrated in Fig. 1C and D, HHP treatment significantly increased both EAI and ESI compared with the control. EAI values ranged from 18.5 to 33.9 m2/g, while ESI values varied between 20.4 and 38.9 min. The most pronounced improvements were observed at 100–200 MPa. At higher pressures (≥400 MPa), further enhancement was limited, suggesting that emulsifying performance approached a plateau.

Notably, at 400 MPa, EAI exhibited a non-monotonic trend, with a lower value at 10 min compared to 5 and 20 min. This condition also corresponded to the highest turbidity (Fig. 1B), indicating intensified aggregation. Excessive aggregation may reduce the availability of surface-active protein and hinder diffusion and interfacial rearrangement, thereby diminishing EAI despite continued protein unfolding. In general, moderate unfolding enhances emulsifying performance by exposing hydrophobic residues and increasing interfacial flexibility, whereas extensive aggregation under higher pressure conditions may offset these beneficial effects (Liu et al., 2023b). These findings are consistent with the surface hydrophobicity variations discussed in Section 3.5. Similarly, Yildiz (2025) reported improved emulsifying properties in melon seed protein isolates following HHP treatment.

Foaming properties of WMP

Fig. 1E and F illustrate the effects of HHP on the foaming properties of WMP. Both the FAI and FSI increased significantly following HHP treatment. FAI exhibited marked enhancement at 100–200 MPa, whereas higher pressures and prolonged treatment times resulted in only marginal additional improvements. For instance, FAI increased from 24.10% in the control to 47.52% at 600 MPa for 5 min. FSI also improved relative to the untreated sample, although the magnitude of enhancement was comparable across different HHP conditions. The highest FSI value (52.21%) was observed at 400 MPa for 5 min.

From a practical perspective, treatment at 100 MPa for 5–20 min appears sufficient to achieve substantial improvement in foaming performance, particularly in terms of foam stability. The enhancement may be attributed to HHP-induced conformational relaxation, which promotes exposure of hydrophobic residues and facilitates rapid adsorption at the air–water interface, enabling the formation of cohesive viscoelastic films. Additionally, pressurization may provide the mechanical energy required for bubble formation during whipping, contributing to increased FAI (Zhao et al., 2023a). Concurrently, pressure-induced aggregation may reinforce interfacial film thickness and cohesion surrounding gas bubbles, thereby enhancing FSI. Comparable trends have been reported for soybean protein isolates subjected to increasing pressure (Speroni et al., 2024).

Water- and oil-holding capacities of WMP

The WHC reflects the ability of proteins to absorb and retain water under centrifugal or compressive forces, thereby influencing food texture, juiciness, and shelf-life. The OHC similarly represents the capacity of proteins to bind and retain oil, contributing to the sensory and structural properties of food systems. As shown in Fig. 1G and H, HHP treatment significantly enhanced both WHC and OHC of WMP, with improvements generally increasing with pressure intensity and treatment duration. Native WMP exhibited a WHC of 3.54 g/g, which increased markedly to 6.81 g/g following treatment at 200 MPa for 10 min. This enhancement may be attributed to HHP-induced structural rearrangement, including partial unfolding of protein chains and increased exposure of hydrophilic groups, thereby improving water-binding capacity and stabilization of hydration layers within the protein matrix (Deng, Du, Liu, Xiong, Wang, Rao, et al., 2025). These findings are consistent with those of Peyrano et al. (2016), who reported significant increases in WHC of cowpea protein isolates subjected to 200–600 MPa.

In terms of OHC, native WMP exhibited a value of 2.17 g/g, which increased to 4.42 g/g after treatment at 400 MPa for 20 min. The improvement in OHC is likely associated with enhanced exposure of hydrophobic residues under HHP conditions, facilitating stronger interactions between protein molecules and oil phases. Comparable results were reported by He et al. (2014) who observed significant enhancement of OHC in peanut protein isolate with increasing pressure from 50 MPa to 200 MPa, exceeding that of commercial soy protein isolate.

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis

Root mean square deviation (RMSD) was used to quantify conformational deviation of WMP from its initial structure during simulation (Pan, Li, Zhao, Tuersuntuoheti, Mehmood, Zhou, et al., 2021). Higher RMSD values indicate greater structural divergence. As shown in Fig. 6A, RMSD increased progressively with rising pressure (100–600 MPa) relative to the control, suggesting enhanced conformational perturbation and reduced structural stability under HHP conditions.

Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) was calculated to assess residue-level flexibility by measuring the displacement of each Cα atom relative to its initial position, with higher RMSF values corresponding to increased molecular mobility (Borgohain & Paul, 2018). As illustrated in Fig. 6B, RMSF values increased steadily with pressure, indicating enhanced residue flexibility and dynamic instability. These findings are consistent with the experimentally observed increase in random coil content following HHP treatment.

The radius of gyration (Rg), which reflects the spatial distribution of atoms relative to the molecular center of mass and serves as an indicator of protein compactness (N. Zhang, Cui, Li, Fan, Liu, Wang et al., 2022), decreased under pressures of 100–600 MPa (Fig. 6C). This reduction suggests the formation of more compact conformations, particularly at 600 MPa where the lowest Rg values were observed. Such compact structural rearrangement may facilitate aggregation (Yang et al., 2024). Hydrogen bonding plays a critical role in maintaining secondary structure and stabilizing non-covalent interactions (Mehranfar et al., 2013). While the control system exhibited a gradual increase in hydrogen bond formation over time, reflecting structural adaptation in aqueous solution, elevated pressure conditions resulted in a marked reduction in hydrogen bond numbers. This decrease correlated with the increased RMSD values, further supporting pressure-induced structural destabilization.

Solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) provides insight into the degree of solvent exposure and is commonly used to assess hydrophobic exposure (Shrake & Rupley, 1973). As shown in Figs. 6E–6F, both total SASA and hydrophobic SASA increased with pressure, indicating enhanced exposure of hydrophobic residues due to structural unfolding. These computational findings are consistent with the experimentally observed increase in surface hydrophobicity and support the occurrence of aggregation driven by intensified hydrophobic interactions.

Free-energy landscape analysis further illustrated the impact of pressure on conformational stability. Structurally stable proteins typically exhibit a single, well-defined energy minimum, whereas unstable systems display multiple irregular energy clusters. As depicted in Fig. 7A, the control system presented a smooth and dominant energy basin, indicative of structural stability. In contrast, HHP-treated systems exhibited increasingly fragmented and rugged energy landscapes, reflecting reduced stability and greater conformational heterogeneity with increasing pressure.

Structural superposition analysis using PyMOL (Fig. 7B) revealed progressive unfolding of WMP under elevated pressure conditions, with greater conformational disorder observed at higher pressures. The extent of unfolding correlated positively with applied pressure. Collectively, these molecular dynamics results are in strong agreement with the experimental findings from CD and fluorescence analyses, confirming that HHP induces conformational destabilization, enhanced hydrophobic exposure, and subsequent aggregation of WMP.

Mechanism of HHP-induced WMP unfolding and aggregation

The HHP disrupted pressure-sensitive non-covalent interactions, including hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic microenvironments, thereby inducing conformational rearrangement of WMP. Overall, the results suggest a pressure-dependent transition from initial unfolding and surface reorganization to subsequent protein association and aggregate growth as pressure intensity and holding time increased. Spectroscopic analyses provided clear evidence of conformational destabilization. CD measurements demonstrated a reduction in α-helix content accompanied by increases in random coil and β-sheet structures, indicating structural unfolding and the formation of intermolecular β-sheet networks during aggregation. Intrinsic fluorescence analysis revealed a blue shift in the emission maximum along with a progressive decrease in fluorescence intensity as pressure and treatment duration increased. These findings are indicative of tertiary structural disruption and enhanced exposure of hydrophobic domains. The observed increase in surface hydrophobicity further supports this interpretation, suggesting greater hydrophobic exposure that may enhance interfacial activity while simultaneously promoting protein–protein interactions in aqueous environments.

Notably, the absolute zeta potential increased progressively with pressure and treatment time. This trend is consistent with pressure-induced exposure of ionizable residues at the particle surface, resulting in enhanced surface charge and stronger electrostatic repulsion within the dispersed fraction. The reduced PDI following HHP treatment further indicates a more uniform particle size distribution and improved dispersion homogeneity. However, despite enhanced electrostatic stabilization, aggregation was not prevented under high-pressure conditions. Instead, the final structural state appears to reflect a dynamic balance between electrostatic repulsion and pressure-driven association mediated primarily by hydrophobic interactions and conformational reorganization.

Multiple lines of evidence support aggregate formation and growth. Increased turbidity, decreased solubility, and enlarged particle size collectively confirm pressure-induced aggregation. Concurrently, enhanced hydrophobic exposure and strengthened intermolecular interactions were associated with improvements in functional properties, including foaming performance, emulsifying activity, WHC, and OHC.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that high hydrostatic pressure (HHP) serves as an effective strategy for tailoring the structural and functional properties of walnut meal protein (WMP). HHP induced pressure-dependent conformational remodeling characterized by partial unfolding, redistribution of surface charge, increased hydrophobic exposure, and modulation of sulfhydryl accessibility. These molecular-level alterations collectively promoted controlled protein association, leading to the formation of compact aggregates with enhanced interfacial activity. Functionally, moderate HHP treatments improved emulsifying and foaming performance, as well as water- and oil-holding capacities, whereas excessive pressure intensified aggregation and reduced solubility. The integrated experimental analyses and molecular dynamics simulations consistently revealed that disruption of non-covalent interactions under pressure governs the balance between structural destabilization and aggregation-driven reorganization. Importantly, the combined computational–experimental framework provided mechanistic insight into pressure-induced protein remodeling, enabling rational interpretation of structure–function relationships. These findings establish a theoretical and technological basis for optimizing HHP processing conditions and advancing the high-value utilization of walnut meal protein as a functional plant-based ingredient in food systems.
