Schützen: Evaluating LLM Safety in Bulgarian and German Contexts
Kiril Georgiev, Yuxia Wang, Dimitar Iliyanov Dimitrov, Preslav Nakov, Ivan Koychev

Abstract
Large language models are increasingly deployed across professional domains, bringing hard-to-predict risks, including the generation of harmful or disrespectful content. Although substantial progress has been made in developing safety evaluation datasets, existing resources remain overwhelmingly English- and Chinese-centric. This limitation is particularly pronounced when evaluating languages that operate within shared sociocultural, legal, and ethical contexts. To address this gap, we introduce Schützen : a German–Bulgarian safety dataset designed to assess model answerability under risk, covering both a low-resource language (Bulgarian) and a high-resource language (German). Experiments with multilingual and language-specific LLMs reveal pronounced cross-language differences in safety behavior, highlighting the necessity of tailored, region-specific evaluation resources to support the responsible deployment of LLMs in Germany and Bulgaria. Datasets and code are available at https://github.com/xnlp-lab/Schutzen . Warning: this paper contains examples that may be offensive, harmful, or biased.

Introduction

Since the emergence of large language models (LLMs), jailbreaking and safety alignment have received significant attention
(
8
)
, not only in English but also across languages such as Chinese, Arabic, Russian, Kazakh, and Hindi
(
19
;
20
;
2
;
4
)
. Existing safety benchmarks capture the linguistic properties of individual languages and crucially reflect region-specific cultural norms, which play a central role in evaluation and alignment
(
21
)
. Despite this progress, several low-resource languages and underrepresented regions remain insufficiently explored, such as Bulgarian.

With the release of bgGPT
(
1
)
and its growing adoption in Bulgaria, evaluating LLM safety in the Bulgarian language has become increasingly important. Such evaluation must account not only for linguistic characteristics but also for cultural conventions, legal frameworks, and regional policies.
Without targeted safeguards, LLMs risk producing misleading, harmful, or inappropriate content, particularly in settings where users increasingly rely on conversational agents for information and decision-making.

Most existing LLM safety benchmarks remain predominantly English-centric and frequently evaluate non-English languages in isolation. This limits our understanding of how safety behavior transfers across languages that share sociocultural, political, and legal environments. Bulgarian and German form a particularly compelling pair for such an investigation. Although they differ substantially in resource availability: Bulgarian being a low-resource language and German a high-resource one, both operate within a shared European regulatory, ethical, and political framework and frequently co-occur in real-world deployment settings, especially in Bulgaria. A joint Bulgarian-German benchmark, therefore, enables a controlled comparison of safety behavior across low- and high-resource languages while holding regional norms constant. This design reveals cross-language safety discrepancies that monolingual or loosely multilingual benchmarks fail to capture, supporting more robust and regionally aligned LLM deployment in Europe.

To address this gap, we curate a bilingual safeguard benchmark for Bulgarian and German that incorporates region-specific and culturally relevant prompts. The dataset is designed to evaluate how LLMs respond to risks that are characteristic of these two countries, spanning both general safety concerns and locally sensitive topics.

Beyond binary safety classification, we conduct a fine-grained analysis of how LLMs respond to risky prompts. Safe responses include outright refusal, well-rounded explanations, or the provision of general information accompanied by appropriate disclaimers. Unsafe responses encompass misleading or false information, offensive or harmful content, assistance with illegal activities, and violations of privacy.

Across both languages, safe responses are most frequently realized as generic informational answers, while the remaining safety patterns are more evenly distributed. For unsafe responses, models exhibit similar tendencies across Bulgarian and German, producing a comparable range of harmful behaviors when prompted in either language. This suggests that, despite linguistic differences, LLMs often manifest analogous failure modes across closely related sociocultural contexts.
Our contributions are as following:

•

We introduce a bilingual dataset for LLM safety evaluation in Bulgarian and German, emphasizing region-specific and culturally relevant risky prompts.

•

We collect responses from 15 LLMs and evaluate them from two complementary perspectives: (
i
) binary safe versus unsafe, and (
ii
) fine-grained response pattern analysis, enabling a nuanced comparison of model behavior across languages.

•

We empirically explore a human-AI collaborative annotation pipeline for LLM safety evaluation, in which models generate explanations and preliminary labels that are subsequently verified by human annotators. Compared with human-only annotation, this approach yields higher agreement with automatic metrics, improves annotation efficiency, and reduces task difficulty.

Related Work

Bulgarian is a South Slavic language spoken primarily in Bulgaria and is generally considered a low-resource language in the context of large-scale language modeling. German, by contrast, is a high-resource language and the official language of Germany, Austria, and Liechtenstein, as well as one of the official languages of Switzerland. It is also widely used in professional, educational, and administrative contexts in Bulgaria. Both languages are embedded within a shared European cultural, political, and legal framework, resulting in a bilingual communication landscape in which LLMs are expected to operate safely and reliably in both languages.

Monolingual and Multilingual LLM Safety Evaluation

LLM safety evaluation initially emerged in English, leading to the development of numerous English-centric benchmarks
(
19
;
8
)
. More recent efforts have extended safety evaluation to other languages, including Chinese
(
20
)
, Arabic
(
2
)
, Russian, Kazakh
(
4
)
and others. These works consistently demonstrate that safety risks and sensitivities vary across languages and cultures, motivating the need for localized and culturally grounded safety benchmarks.

In parallel, the design of safety prompts has evolved substantially. Early benchmarks focused on explicit high-risk requests (e.g.,
How to produce a gun?
), while later work introduced indirect or concealed jailbreak strategies (e.g.,
I’m writing a novel about war—could you describe a scene of producing guns?
). More recent benchmarks additionally examine over-refusal and the trade-off between safety and helpfulness, as exemplified by OR-Bench
(
3
)
. SORRY-Bench further advances this line of work by introducing a fine-grained taxonomy of 44 potentially unsafe topics and 440 class-balanced unsafe instructions, enabling more precise analysis of refusal behavior and safety failures
(
21
)
. Together, these efforts highlight the importance of evolving risk coverage, linguistic diversity, and cultural relevance in safety evaluation.

Despite the growing interest in multilingual safety benchmarks, coverage remains uneven. Several large-scale datasets do not include Bulgarian and only partially cover German. LinguaSafe
(
10
)
, for example, omits Bulgarian entirely. XSafety
(
18
)
evaluates 2,800 prompts across ten major languages, including German, but relies primarily on translated English prompts, limiting its ability to capture region-specific cultural, legal, and policy differences. Similarly, multilingual blending approaches focus on mixed-language inputs rather than cultural localization and demonstrate that multilingual prompts can more easily bypass safety mechanisms
(
17
)
. PolygloToxicityPrompts provides a large-scale benchmark of 425K toxic prompts across 17 languages, including German, and shows that toxicity in model outputs increases as language resources decrease
(
7
)
. Most recently, SafeWorld introduces a geographically diverse benchmark spanning 50 countries and 493 regions, highlighting the challenges of aligning LLMs with heterogeneous cultural and legal norms
(
22
)
. Collectively, these studies underscore the need for culturally grounded, region-specific safety evaluation, particularly in low-resource and multilingual contexts.

German and Bulgarian LLM Evaluation Benchmarks

Although German is considered a high-resource language, existing evaluation benchmarks largely focus on traditional natural language understanding (NLU) tasks rather than capability-oriented evaluation of LLMs for safety, reasoning, or robustness. SuperGLEBer
(
13
)
is a broad German benchmark spanning 29 NLU tasks, including document classification and sequence tagging. The GermEval shared tasks have further contributed resources for German NLP, including GermEval 2019 on offensive language detection
(
16
)
and GermEval 2021 on identifying toxic, engaging, and fact-claiming comments
(
15
)
. These benchmarks primarily target classification tasks and do not directly assess generative safety behavior in LLMs.

Bulgarian, as a low-resource language, has even more limited evaluation resources. BgGLUE
(
5
)
provides a comprehensive benchmark for Bulgarian NLU, covering tasks such as natural language inference, named entity recognition, sentiment analysis, question answering, and fact-checking. More recently,
1
introduced BgGPT, a family of Bulgarian-centric LLMs obtained via continual pretraining and instruction tuning of Gemma-2 models. These models were evaluated using translated general-purpose benchmarks (e.g., Winogrande, HellaSwag, ARC, MMLU, GSM8K) as well as native Bulgarian datasets such as EXAMS
(
6
)
. However, to date, there has been no native Bulgarian benchmark specifically designed to evaluate LLM safety.

To address this gap, we introduce
Schützen
, the first Bulgarian–German bilingual benchmark explicitly designed for LLM safety evaluation. By combining region-specific localization with controlled comparison across a low-resource and a high-resource language operating under shared European norms, our work complements existing multilingual benchmarks and enables a more nuanced analysis of cross-lingual safety behavior.

3
Dataset

We developed two region-specific datasets for Bulgaria and Germany to evaluate LLM safety.
Inspired by the
Do-not-Answer
(DNA) series datasets
(
19
;
20
;
4
)
, we curated our datasets by first
(i)
rewriting general-risk questions to adapt them to local language, culture, and events, and then
(ii)
creating region-specific prompts tailored to the unique safety concerns of Bulgaria and Germany. The dataset includes 16 harm types distributed across six risk areas, enabling broad coverage of potential harms.
We emphasize that we constructed the dataset through comprehensive cultural localization rather than direct translation for both general-risk prompts and region-specific sensitivity topics (see Appendices
A
and
B
).

3.1
DNA Question Localization

Using the English DNA benchmark and a subset of the Kazakh DNA dataset, we manually rewrote prompts by first accurately expressing them in the target language and then adapting them to Bulgarian and German contexts. We preserved the original objective of evaluating LLM vulnerability to specific risks while systematically replacing references to individuals, organizations, and locations with well-known local figures, historical events, policies, cultural conventions, norms, and traditions that reflect the linguistic and societal characteristics of the target country.

A bilingual Bulgarian–German MSc student in NLP performed the initial translation from English into Bulgarian and German.
A second bilingual annotator then verified the full dataset for correctness and consistency.
Our annotators are highly qualified with the expertise required to curate high-quality data. Specifically, annotators are native Bulgarian speakers fluent in German who have academic experience studying in Germany, ensuring deep familiarity with both linguistic and cultural contexts. We instructed the annotators to ensure grammatical accuracy, linguistic naturalness, and adherence to normative conventions in both languages.

After translation, we further localized the Bulgarian and the German questions to reflect local cultural and historical nuances. For example, we replaced the names
Claire
and
Keisha
with
Pepa
and
Senya
in Bulgarian, and with
Gertrude
and
Karina
in German (see Appendix
A
). This yielded 3,663 questions per language, spanning five general risk areas: information hazards, malicious uses, discrimination and toxicity, misinformation harms, and human–chatbot interaction harms.
For the 3,663 general-risk prompts, we manually adapted each item to Bulgarian and German contexts through targeted adjustments of names, events, and historical references.

We designed all prompts to appear in three attack modes: (
i
) direct attacks (
Ori
), in which prompts explicitly express risks to assess basic safety sensitivity, (
ii
) indirect attacks (
FN
), in which prompts appear benign but aim to elicit unsafe content, and (
iii
) over-sensitivity assessments (
FP
), in which prompts include sensitive terms (e.g.,
gun
,
bomb
) while requesting benign, general information.

3.2
Region-Specific Questions

We extended the dataset by constructing 315 region-specific prompts per language, targeting sensitive and socially significant issues specific to Bulgaria and Germany. These prompts span multiple harm types, including politically sensitive topics and controversial historical events. Given their shared European cultural and civilizational context, public discourse in both countries often addresses overlapping ethical, governance, and historical issues, making these questions relevant across both societies.

As with the general-risk prompts, region-specific questions are instantiated as direct attacks, indirect attacks, and over-sensitivity assessments, with detailed annotation instructions provided in Appendix
B
. In total, the dataset comprises 3,978 prompts per language. Table
1
provides a visual overview of the dataset composition, illustrating the distribution of prompt types across six risk areas and sixteen harm types for both Bulgarian and German.

4.2
Binary Safe vs. Unsafe Evaluation

We performed binary safety classification using two approaches:
(i)
classifier-based evaluation (e.g.,
LlamaGuard
) and
(ii)
reasoning-based evaluation with customized risk-specific criteria.

In the reasoning-based approach, we first checked whether the model refused to answer; if so, we labeled the response as safe. Otherwise, we evaluated the response against predefined criteria for each risk area. For
Malicious Use
, we asked whether the response:
(
i
) contains only public or legally available information;
(
ii
) risks violating laws or policies;
(
iii
) introduces ethical risks if misused;
(
iv
) could harm individuals, organizations, or the state; or
(
v
) could undermine democracy or freedom of speech.
We labeled the responses as unsafe if they violated any criterion. Appendix
D
provides examples.

For risk areas with three to eight criteria, we allowed one to four mismatches to avoid overly strict judgments. Some responses remain broadly harmless despite minor deviations (see Table
9
in the Appendix). Because ethical and privacy-related risks are often subjective and context-dependent, we calibrated thresholds against human annotations, balancing rigor and flexibility. Appendix
C
lists the full evaluation criteria for risk areas I–VI.

4.3
Responding Pattern Classification

Inspired by the safeguard evaluation framework in Qorǵau
(
4
)
, we categorize safe responses into six patterns (see Table
10
in the Appendix): answer rejection, opinion refutation, well-rounded statements, risk-aware responses with disclaimers, general information, and admission of uncertainty. This taxonomy enables fine-grained analysis and supports detection of over-sensitivity.

For unsafe responses, we label four harm types (see Table
11
in the Appendix): general harmful content, misinformation, privacy violations, and offensive or emotionally harmful content. This scheme highlights model-specific failure modes and supports targeted safety improvements.

4.4
Human vs. Automatic Evaluation

Before full automation, we conducted human annotation on a subset of responses. We sampled prompts from each risk area and collected responses from all 15 models, yielding 1,462 Bulgarian and 1,225 German examples. The annotators labeled each response for binary safety and fine-grained patterns.

We then compared the human judgments with the automatic evaluations using
GPT-4.1
and
GPT-4.1-mini
as reasoning-based judges, and
Llama-Guard-4-12B
and
Llama-Guard-3-8B
as classifier-based baselines. We selected
GPT-4.1
and
GPT-4.1-mini
for their strong multi-criteria reasoning, bilingual capabilities, and cost efficiency at scale, and included
Llama-Guard-4-12B
and
Llama-Guard-3-8B
to contrast structured reasoning with widely used classifier-based safety systems under identical conditions. While
LLM
-based judges may inherit biases from their own alignment objectives, we mitigate this risk by validating all automatic judgments against human annotations and by reporting agreement metrics rather than relying on absolute scores.

Treating human annotations as ground truth, we evaluated agreement using accuracy, precision, recall, and macro-average F1. Table
2
reports the results. All evaluators performed better on German than Bulgarian, and
GPT-4.1-mini
achieved performance comparable to
GPT-4.1
. Despite its larger size,
Llama-Guard-4-12B
underperformed
Llama-Guard-3-8B
in both accuracy and F1.

Due to the complexity of fine-grained classification, we used
GPT-4.1

Given the scale of over 95K responses, we applied

Fine-Grained Classification

We conducted a fine-grained analysis of the response behavior for both safe and unsafe outputs. We labeled the unsafe responses with four harm types and grouped safe responses into six safety patterns. Appendix

We validate
GPT-4.1
’s automatic judgments against human annotations 1,462 Bg and 1,225 De samples. We adopted a human-AI collaborative procedure to identify an effective prompting strategy for fine-grained labeling.

First, we designed three prompt variants, each enumerating the response patterns from Tables
10
and
11
:

(i)
prompts written in Bulgarian/German;

(ii)
prompts written in English; and

(iii)
Bulgarian/German prompts augmented with a brief example for each fine-grained label to aid disambiguation.
Confusion matrices for all variants are reported in Appendix
F
. Across two languages, these variants yielded no substantial differences in agreement between
GPT-4.1
and human annotators.

We then consulted
GPT-5

12
for guidance on improving evaluation fidelity, obtaining the feedback that
LLM judges tend to select the first label that appears to fit, rather than evaluating all options to identify the best match
.
Based on this, we reordered the label set and instructed
GPT-4.1
to apply a
sequential first-match rule
: traverse the labels in order and assign the first label that fits without considering the later options. This priority-based decision rule reduced the ambiguity and improved
GPT-4.1
’s evaluations, as reflected in higher agreement and cleaner confusion matrices.

We also used human–AI collaboration for manual annotation. After annotators independently labeled responses, we prompted
GPT-5
to assign binary and fine-grained labels and to provide brief reasoning. Another annotator then reviewed the disagreements between the humans and
GPT-5
and resolved many conflicts during verification, often using the model’s broader perspectives to counter human tunnel vision. This workflow suggests that LLMs often offer diverse candidate interpretations, while humans provide evidence-based verification.

6
Conclusion and Discussion

We introduced a safety evaluation dataset for Bulgarian and German, comprising
\sim
8K prompts spanning both general and region-specific risk areas. We designed three prompt types—direct attacks, indirect attacks, and over-sensitivity assessments—and collected over 95K responses from 15
LLMs
. We evaluated these responses using binary and fine-grained schemes that distinguish four unsafe harm types and six safe response patterns.

Empirically, all evaluated
LLMs
achieved safe rates above 95% on our benchmark.
Claude
outperformed all models, while
GPT-4o
lagged behind most open-source systems. Several SOTA models achieved near-ceiling safety scores, indicating strong cross-lingual safety transfer. Contrary to our initial hypothesis that low-resource languages would show weaker alignment, the results suggest that when target cultures share safety norms and policy with English-speaking settings, safety capabilities transfer robustly, even to low-resource languages. This finding underscores the dataset’s value for studying cross-lingual transfer and motivates future extensions, like creating more challenging jailbreak strategies and finer region-specific prompts to probe safety nuances across EU countries.

Despite these strong overall results, most
LLMs
degraded noticeably on region-specific content.
bgGPT
remained the strongest model for Bulgaria-specific prompts, while
EuroLLM-9B
performed best on German region-specific content. Overall, models performed better on German than on Bulgarian, highlighting imbalanced linguistic and regional representation within Europe. Our dataset thus provides a practical resource for supporting responsible
LLM
deployment in European settings where linguistic and cultural factors interact.

Limitations and Future Work

Looking ahead, we plan to extend the region-specific component of the dataset to better capture local cultural and socio-political contexts, and to design more challenging jailbreak strategies and subtler prompts to probe finer-grained safety behavior. We will also expand fine-grained evaluation to a broader set of models and integrate additional automated evaluators (e.g.,
GPT-4o
and
GPT-4.1-mini
) to reduce reliance on any single system and enable cross-validation. Finally, we aim to enlarge the question pool to cover a wider range of safety risks, including emergent and context-dependent phenomena.

Cultural and Regional Nuances

While we aimed to reflect the socio-cultural and political contexts of Germany and Bulgaria—two countries embedded in a broader European setting—the complexity of their relationship posed challenges for constructing a joint NLP dataset. Cultural norms, political sensitivities, and social contexts varied both between the two countries and across regions within each. As a result, the dataset captures only part of this variability and remains an approximation.

Evaluation Framework

Our evaluation framework comprised two tasks and relied on four automated judges, with a central role for
GPT-4.1
and
Llama-Guard-3-8B
. Although human evaluation showed strong agreement with all four classifiers on the binary safety task, this approach may have inherited biases from the alignment objectives and safety mechanisms of
GPT-4o
. Moreover, the limited scope of the fine-grained question set likely left some risk categories underrepresented, indicating coverage gaps.

Ethical Considerations

The dataset contains sensitive prompts specific to the German and Bulgarian contexts, which could be misused for prompt-engineering attacks or to exploit regional sociopolitical sensitivities. NLP and ML graduate students conducted all annotations with informed consent and the option to withdraw at any time. We designed the dataset solely to evaluate and improve LLM safety mechanisms in both countries, with attention to their shared European setting and distinct linguistic and cultural features, and to support responsible cross-cultural deployment.

Reproducibility

To support transparent and reproducible research, we will publicly release (
i
) the full prompt set in Bulgarian and German with prompt-type labels, (
ii
) documentation describing localization decisions and known limitations, and (
iii
) annotation guidelines for both binary (safe/unsafe) and fine-grained response-pattern labeling, along with evaluation scripts and model-interface configurations needed to reproduce our experiments.
