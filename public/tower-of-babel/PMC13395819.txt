Abortion caused by hemolytic disease associated with co-infection by Anaplasma marginale and Babesia spp. in dairy cattle
Authors: Ícaro Guilherme dos Santos, Letícia Perri Almeida Luciano, João Paulo da Silva Cavasani, Marlon Ribeiro, Rafaela Mallmann Bohn, Bernardo Nassau de Souza, João Fabio Soares, Bernardo Coelho de Carvalho, Emílio César Martins Pereira, Marcos de Almeida Souza, Edson Moleta Colodel, Caroline Argenta Pescador
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bovine anaplasmosis and babesiosis are important tick-borne diseases associated with significant economic losses, although fetal infection and abortion are rarely described. This study reports a bovine abortion associated with co-infection by Anaplasma marginale, Babesia bovis and Babesia bigemina. in the Midwestern region of Brazil. Fetal necropsy was performed, followed by cytological, histopathological, immunohistochemical, and molecular analyses. Gross findings included jaundice, splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, and renal discoloration, consistent with severe hemolytic disease. Blood smear evaluation revealed intraerythrocytic inclusions compatible with (A) marginale and Babesia-like merozoites, and PCR confirmed (B) bovis and B. bigemina, and Anaplasma spp. infection was confirmed by sequencing. Histologically, lesions consisted of erythrophagocytosis, hemosiderosis, hemoglobin casts, and marked intravascular leukocytostasis in multiple organs. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated accumulation of macrophages and T lymphocytes, suggesting activation of a fetal inflammatory response. These findings emphasize the importance of including hemoparasitic infections in the differential diagnosis of bovine abortion and support a role for fetal immune responses in lesion development.

Introduction

Bovine anaplasmosis and babesiosis are important tick-borne diseases caused by the intraerythrocytic rickettsial bacterium Anaplasma marginale and the apicomplexan protozoa Babesia bigemina and Babesia bovis, respectively, and are widely distributed in cattle-producing regions worldwide (Bock et al., 2004; Marques et al. 2020; MacGregor et al. 2021). In Brazil, transmission occurs primarily through bites of Rhipicephalus (Boophilus) microplus ticks, although mechanical transmission of Anaplasma spp. by tabanid flies and blood-contaminated instruments has also been reported (Kocan et al. 2010). The epidemiology of these diseases is strongly influenced by climatic conditions, varying across tropical, subtropical, and temperate regions. In subtropical areas, enzootic instability may predispose herds to outbreaks, whereas in tropical regions such as Mato Grosso, where climatic conditions favor continuous tick exposure, infections are typically considered endemic (Almeida et al. 2006; Lucena et al. 2010; Mello et al. 2017; Henker et al. 2020).

Clinically, bovine anaplasmosis and babesiosis are characterized by fever, anemia, weakness, anorexia, dehydration, jaundice, and reduced productivity, and severe cases may progress to death. Infections caused by B. bovis may additionally result in neurological signs due to sequestration of parasitized erythrocytes in cerebral capillaries (Bock et al., 2004). Pregnant cows may also develop reproductive disorders, including abortion (Richey and Palmer 1990). Transplacental transmission of A. marginale and Babesia bovis has been described mainly during the second and third trimesters of gestation (Ribeiro et al., 1995; Henker et al. 2020); however, fetal infection and abortion are considered uncommon outcomes, particularly when concurrent infection with Babesia spp. is involved (Correa et al. 1978; Henker et al. 2020).

Although both pathogens are endemic in many cattle-producing areas, reports describing bovine abortion associated with concurrent anaplasmosis and babesiosis, confirmed through integrated pathological and molecular approaches, remain scarce in the literature. In addition, the characterization of fetal lesions associated with these co-infections and their differential diagnoses is still limited, which hampers definitive etiological interpretation in abortion investigations.

Therefore, the present study describes a case of bovine abortion associated with co-infection by (A) marginale, (B) bovis and B. bigemina in the Midwestern region of Brazil, based on gross, histopathological, immunohistochemistry and molecular findings.

Case presentation

Reproductive failure occurred in a herd of 50 Holstein × Gir crossbred dairy cows belonging to the School Farm of the Federal University of Mato Grosso (UFMT), located in the municipality of Santo Antônio de Leverger, Mato Grosso, Brazil. Animals were maintained on improved pastures (Brachiaria brizantha and Brachiaria humidicola) with free access to mineral supplementation and water. During the dry season, cows received corn silage twice daily after milking. The herd was routinely vaccinated against Brucella abortus, bovine herpesvirus type 1 (BoHV-1) and bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV). Artificial insemination (AI) was used as the standard reproductive method, and pregnancy was diagnosed by ultrasonography 30 days after AI.

A 5/8 Girolando bovine fetus at approximately eight months of gestation was submitted to the Veterinary Pathology Laboratory at UFMT for postmortem examination. The period leading up to the abortion was marked by unstable weather and atypical rainfall. The dam was a seven-year-old cow with no additional clinical signs at the time of abortion; however, this was the second abortion recorded for this animal.

At necropsy, the fetus measured 74 cm crown-to-rump, compatible with approximately eight months of gestation. Gross findings included mild jaundice of the mucous membranes and carcass, moderate accumulation of light-yellow fluid within the thoracic cavity, and pulmonary collapse. The liver was markedly enlarged with diffuse moderate yellow-to-orange discoloration (Fig. 1A). Moderate splenomegaly was observed (Fig. 1B), and the kidneys showed diffuse moderate dark-red discoloration (Fig. 1C). Hepatic and perirenal lymph nodes were enlarged, with firm to smooth consistency and blackish-brown coloration (Fig. 1D). Multifocal hemorrhages were present on the parietal surface of the thymus. Blood smears were prepared during necropsy and stained with Panótico Rápido® (Laborclin, Brazil) for Cytological evaluation. Cytological evaluation of blood smears revealed intraerythrocytic basophilic peripheral pinpoint structures measuring approximately 1 μm in diameter, consistent with A. marginale (Fig. 1E), as well as intraerythrocytic structures morphologically compatible with Babesia-like merozoites (Fig. 1F).

Microscopic lesions were most pronounced in the liver, kidneys, spleen, lymph nodes and lungs. The liver showed abundant golden-yellow pigment within bile ducts, hepatic sinusoids and hepatocytes (Fig. 2A). Renal lesions consisted of brownish pigment interpreted as hemoglobin casts within proximal tubular epithelial cells and tubular lumina (Fig. 2B). The spleen exhibited diffuse marked erythrophagocytosis associated with hemosiderosis. In the lungs there are macrophages with moderately abundant, occasionally vacuolated cytoplasm and are distributed within the sinuses and interstitium. Moderate amounts of brownish intra-alveolar material interspersed with amorphous eosinophilic structures morphologically compatible with meconium and keratin were observed. Leukocytostasis was present within vessels of the lungs (Fig. 2C), liver, kidneys, spleen, and lymph nodes. The lymph nodes there is marked vascular and sinusoidal congestion, with multiple distended vascular spaces filled with erythrocytes. There is expansion of the lymphatic sinuses by an abundant predominantly mononuclear cellular infiltrate composed of macrophages (Fig. 2D) and lymphocytes, associated with a moderate diffuse increase in parenchymal cellularity. Discussion

Maternal infection by A. marginale can lead to progressive hemolytic anemia, which may culminate in fetal hypoxia, fetal death and abortion (Fowler and Swift 1975). Reduced oxygen delivery secondary to maternal anemia has been proposed as a major mechanism of fetal compromise, resulting from decreased fetal oxygen tension (pO₂) and increased carbon dioxide levels (pCO₂). Although the mechanisms involved in transplacental transmission of A. marginale remain incompletely understood, dissemination may occur during an extraerythrocytic phase or through the passage of infected erythrocytes across the placenta without significant pathogen amplification (Zaugg 1985; Aubry and Geale 2011). Additional factors, including immunosuppression, may favor subclinical maternal infection and increase the likelihood of vertical transmission (Silva and Fonseca 2014). In this case, the abortion was likely caused by fetal hemolytic anemia resulting from a co-infection with Anaplasma spp. (probably (A) marginale), (B) bovis and B. bigemina; since the cow showed no apparent clinical signs other than the abortion, she may have had a mild or subclinical infection, which allowed for vertical transmission.

The gross findings observed in this fetus including jaundice, splenomegaly and darkened kidneys are consistent with severe hemolytic disease and corroborate previous descriptions of fetal anaplasmosis and babesiosis (Fowler and Swift 1975; Henker et al. 2020). The identification of intraerythrocytic structures compatible with A. marginale and Babesia sp. in blood smears, together with PCR and sequencing results, provided strong etiologic evidence supporting co-infection as the cause of abortion. These findings reinforce the importance of integrating cytological, molecular and pathological data in abortion investigations, particularly in endemic regions where multiple hemoparasites circulate simultaneously.

Abortions and neonatal losses associated with anaplasmosis and babesiosis have been reported in different regions of Brazil (Costa et al. 2016; Henker et al. 2020; Andrade et al. 2024); however, the vascular leukocytostasis observed in the lungs, liver, kidneys, spleen and lymph nodes in the present case represents an unusual finding in bovine fetuses. This lesion may reflect an active fetal inflammatory response triggered by acute intrauterine infection. The intravascular accumulation of leukocytes observed in the pulmonary vessels may reflect local inflammatory responses triggered by hemoparasitic infection. Microvascular alterations and local hypoxia caused by erythrocyte destruction can promote endothelial activation and leukocyte adhesion, while parasite-derived and host inflammatory mediators may contribute to chemotactic recruitment and leukostasis. Together, these mechanisms suggest that the pulmonary lesions observed in this case are compatible with a systemic inflammatory process associated with intraerythrocytic infection (Adams and Lloyd 1997).

The fetus in this case was in the late stage of gestation, a period in which the bovine immune system reaches greater functional maturity. Fetal immune competence progressively develops throughout gestation, and phagocytic activity becomes more efficient in the final months due to maturation of cellular immune components and antigenic stimulation (Ahmed 2002). Experimental studies have demonstrated that bovine fetuses are capable of mounting cell-mediated immune responses, including IFN-γ production, during mid-to-late gestation, indicating progressive maturation of fetal immunocompetence (Andrianarivo et al. 2001; Rosbottom et al. 2008).

In agreement with this hypothesis, immunohistochemistry demonstrated prominent intravascular increase of MAC387-positive cells, indicating the participation of myeloid-lineage cells such as monocytes and macrophages. These findings suggest that the inflammatory response induced by (A) marginale. and Babesia sp. in the fetus may resemble mechanisms described in adult cattle, where acute infection by (B) bovis triggers strong cell-mediated immunity associated with erythrocyte destruction (Homer et al. 2000). The presence of CD3-positive further supports activation of the adaptive immune response, indicating that fetal immunity may play a more relevant role in hemoparasitic pathogenesis than previously recognized.

The predominance of intravascular monocyte–macrophage populations and marked leukostasis observed in the lungs may reflect a systemic fetal inflammatory response analogous to fetal inflammatory response syndrome (FIRS), which is characterized by systemic cytokine activation, multiorgan fetal inflammation, and adverse neonatal outcomes in human medicine (Jung et al. 2020).

The microscopic lesions observed including erythrophagocytosis, hemosiderosis, bilirubin accumulation and hemoglobin casts in renal tubules are consistent with severe hemolysis. Both intravascular and extravascular mechanisms likely contributed to erythrocyte destruction. Parasite replication inside erythrocytes and subsequent membrane rupture lead to intravascular hemolysis, while opsonization and macrophage-mediated phagocytosis contribute to extravascular hemolysis in the spleen and liver (Rimal et al. 2025). The resulting hemolytic process explains the observed jaundice, pigment accumulation and widespread macrophage activation.

Epidemiological factors may also have contributed to disease occurrence in this case. The Midwest region of Brazil is characterized by enzootic stability, where continuous exposure to ticks favors maintenance of hemoparasite circulation. Climatic instability and unseasonal rainfall recorded during the period preceding the abortion may have increased humidity and favored proliferation of Rhipicephalus microplus populations, consequently increasing infection pressure in the herd. Additionally, the genetic composition of the herd, which included animals with Bos taurus taurus ancestry, may have increased susceptibility to tick infestation compared with Bos indicus breeds, known for greater tick resistance (Bock et al. 1997; Brito et al. 2011). It is worth highlighting that the farm has a history of high calf mortality associated with this coinfection. Management factors, such as possible iatrogenic transmission through contaminated needles during oxytocin administration, should also be considered as potential contributors to pathogen dissemination (Kessler 2001).

Overall, this case highlights the multifactorial nature of bovine fetal losses associated with tick-borne pathogens. Environmental conditions, host susceptibility and pathogen burden likely interacted to enable transplacental infection and fetal disease. Importantly, the presence of marked intravascular leukocyte accumulation suggests that fetal immune responses may contribute significantly to lesion development, supporting the need for further studies investigating inflammatory pathways and immune activation in bovine fetuses affected by hemoparasitic infections.

Conclusion

This report describes a case of bovine abortion associated with co-infection by (A) marginale, (B) bovis and B. bigemina., confirmed by integrated cytological, molecular, and pathological findings. The gross and microscopic lesions were consistent with severe fetal hemolytic disease and the marked intravascular accumulation of leukocytes suggests that inflammatory responses may play an important role in lesion development and fetal death. Environmental conditions, host susceptibility and potential management-related factors likely contributed to disease occurrence in this endemic region. These findings highlight the importance of including tick-borne hemoparasites in differential diagnoses of bovine abortion and emphasize the need for further studies to better characterize fetal immune responses and inflammatory mechanisms associated with intrauterine hemoparasitic infections.
