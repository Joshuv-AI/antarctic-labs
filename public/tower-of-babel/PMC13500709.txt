Variety-seeking in joint decisions: the role of reciprocity
Authors: Nuno José Lopes, Ignacio Rodríguez-Carreño, Cristina Etayo, Elena Reutskaja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConsumers typically switch between different alternatives when choosing multiple times from the same product category. Although this phenomenon of variety-seeking has received considerable attention, literature has primarily focused on decisions made by only one person. However, consumers frequently choose jointly with their peers. This situation often implies different decision processes and outcomes compared with individual choices. We address this gap in two studies. The participants chose either in groups of two people (i.e., dyads) or alone. We found that dyads exhibited a higher level of variety-seeking than individuals, with reciprocity working as a key driver of dyadic variety-seeking. Furthermore, the variety behavior of a dyad decreased when the favorite alternatives of both dyad members were the same, positioning preference alignment as a moderator. Finally, the analysis of impression management concerns showed that it was the difference in scores between dyad members, rather than their individual levels, that significantly affected dyadic variety-seeking.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55593-8.Keywords: Variety-seeking, Joint decisions, Dyads, Reciprocity, Impression management concernsSubject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Variety-seeking behaviors are ever-present. Spanning widely different choice domains, such as snacks, songs, fruits, and restaurants1–3, variety-seeking is defined as the tendency to switch among different alternatives of products, services, or activities4,5. Considered as a determining factor in buying decisions6, its importance goes beyond the consumer realm as an essential principle of human behavior7,8. Variety-seeking can be found in the dietary preferences of children at the age of two9 and is documented in the habits of many animal species7,10.

Despite the extensive literature on this phenomenon (for a review, see ref.11), variety-seeking has thus far been characterized essentially in the context of individual choice (e.g., ref. 12). Yet, many choices are group choices. Recurrent decisions, such as picking a restaurant or holiday destination, are often made jointly by groups13,14. Nonetheless, how variety-seeking operates in these settings is mostly unknown and challenging to predict, as group decision-making is not the mere averaging of the members’ preferences15,16. In their literature review, Sevilla and colleagues11 specifically identified the need for more research on the social components of variety-seeking. We address this call by investigating variety-seeking in joint decisions, and more precisely, the Case 1 from Gorlin and Dhar17, in which multiple group members jointly choose a product to be consumed by all of them together. Therefore, we compare the level of variety-seeking in choices made jointly by two people for their shared consumption with those made by individuals consuming alone. Additionally, we investigate the possible causes and moderators of variety-seeking in dyadic decisions, with a special emphasis on the role of reciprocity.

When choosing jointly, the best-case scenario is when both dyad members’ preferences coincide on the favorite alternative, as the group can select an alternative that maximizes the individual utility of every person. However, this ideal scenario of matching preferences is not that frequent18, making disagreement recurrent in joint consumer decisions19,20.

On the other hand, cooperation can substantially help to overcome this problem of preference mismatch. Cooperation, which is regarded as a fundamental human trait21, is widespread in every society22 because of its capacity to support social interaction. Furthermore, the ability to cooperate is considered to provide an evolutionary advantage23,24, with the social brain hypothesis positing that the sophisticated human brain has been evolving to support a rich and sizable level of social interaction25. All in all, cooperation is recognized as an embedded element of human behavior22. Additionally, it is important to highlight that joint consumer decisions typically occur within lasting relationships26 such as with family, friends, or colleagues. Since in these relations people expect to interact in the future, cooperation is quite the norm here27, because partners find it critical not to undermine their relationships28.

Disagreements can be resolved through compromise29,30. That is, all group members sacrifice a share of their utility or liking and converge into an option that maximizes joint utility31. Although compromise allows to overcome the problem of preference mismatch, this decision strategy also requires sacrifices from all parties involved, which implies a cost to everyone32.

As discussed earlier, since joint consumer decisions usually occur within enduring relationships, people expect to choose together on multiple occasions. Then, the repetition of these interactions over time creates the conditions for cooperation to develop into something bigger called reciprocity: I make a sacrifice to help you now and expect you to do the same afterwards24,33. Reciprocity, which can be found recurrently in all human societies34, is of central importance in dyads’ well-being and satisfaction35, and greatly influences joint decisions. Thus, rather than compromising in every choice, a dyad may overcome disagreement by reaching overall equity through reciprocity in a set of choices across time26. More specifically, one dyad member picks their favorite option in a first selection, and the other person picks their own favorite on a subsequent occasion.

Overall, whereas a dyad typically needs to balance its members’ different preferences in a reciprocal way, an individual, on the other hand, can focus solely on their favorite alternative. Therefore, we predict that dyads would show a higher level of variety-seeking than individuals. Furthermore, we suggest that reciprocity is a strong and positive driver of dyadic variety-seeking, since the literature claims it is a very common decision strategy26 because it overcomes, in a cooperative way, the recurrent mismatch of preferences between dyad members18, and reduces the sacrifice of every person in at least one of the joint selections36. However, despite the significant influence of reciprocity, it is also necessary to acknowledge that when both dyad members coincide with their favorite alternative, the preference mismatch problem disappears. Then, the natural strategy should be to select the common top choice instead of taking turns reciprocally. Consequently, we expect variety-seeking to decrease when dyad members’ preferences are aligned.

To better understand the role of reciprocity, it is necessary to examine its preconditions, among which, the number of interactions is particularly important24. To begin with, reciprocity within a dyad can only emerge if there are at least two interactions between members; otherwise, the exchange of favors is naturally impossible. Furthermore, when interactions are so rare that dyad members lose track of who obtained the relatively preferred option in past decisions, their motivation to reciprocate should diminish23. Therefore, the repetition of joint decisions and their time distance constitute a boundary condition of our research, as reciprocity, and its association with dyadic variety-seeking, are unlikely to emerge in contexts characterized by one-off or infrequent choices.

As mentioned earlier, the central goal of this research is to investigate variety-seeking in choices made by dyads. In this context, we propose that reciprocity is a key determinant of dyadic variety-seeking because of its importance in group interactions34. Nevertheless, as we are also interested in comparing dyads with individuals, and given the substantial importance of impression management concerns for individuals’ variety-seeking37,38, we consider it worthwhile to examine this factor in a dyadic context as well. Therefore, we examine impression management concerns as an additional driver of variety-seeking in dyadic choice. However, because we expect its role in dyadic variety-seeking to be less important than that of reciprocity, our analysis of impression management concerns is essentially exploratory.

Choosing jointly conveys a twofold message39. First, this decision process is essentially public because when a consumer shares their preferences with others, they reveal not only their taste, but ultimately, they also offer a portrait of themselves. Typically, a person wants to make a good impression on their peers40, which can be achieved by choosing a wide assortment of products, as variety-seeking is associated with being a more desirable and interesting person37,41. Second, in situations of joint decision-making, a person needs to interact with other people, and the way this interaction evolves often reveals their feelings towards their companions. For instance, incorporating others’ preferences into the discussion and eventually choosing them is a well-perceived and frequently desired behavior in dyadic choice26,42.

Previous research has found that variety-seeking increases when individuals choose in the presence of others43 or select products that will be consumed by or with another person1,44. This happens because people believe that others appreciate variety37; therefore, when they know that their choices will be public, they usually select more varied outcomes to make a good impression. Moreover, this effect is stronger for those individuals who are more concerned with how others perceive them38. Since in joint decision-making, both the process and outcome are quite visible to others, we expect dyad members with higher impression management concerns to push for more dyadic variety to positively impress their companions.

The singularity of joint choice implies that the decision process is often influenced not only by each member’s individual level on a particular construct but also by the differences among these members on that construct16,45. Therefore, when investigating the role of impression management concerns in dyadic variety-seeking it is worthwhile to explore whether, in dyads where one member has a stronger concern to impress than the other, the former will have more room to push the dyad for higher variety because he or she assumes that this distinctive behavior will give them a better image. Therefore, we suggest that the greater the difference in impression management concerns between members of the same dyad, the greater variety-seeking that dyad will exhibit.

It is important to note that both individual decisions made in the presence of others and joint decisions involve a significant public component. Therefore, we predict that choices made in these two situations are likewise influenced by impression management concerns, as discussed above. However, it is necessary to emphasize that choosing jointly entails further constraints and dynamics. For instance, since members’ preferences often do not align, dyads need to solve this additional problem. Because maintaining the relationship is considered central in joint consumer decisions27, this gives rise to reciprocity, which, we argue, not only dramatically influences the decision process and outcome in dyads, but furthermore, is naturally absent in individual decision-making. This additional factor represents a key difference between the drivers of variety-seeking in dyadic versus individual choices. Consequently, we predict it will lead dyads to exhibit a higher level of variety than individuals, even when the latter are in a public context.

In two studies, we investigated the phenomenon of variety-seeking in joint choices for shared consumption. Study 1 aimed to compare the level of variety-seeking between dyads and individuals and explored its drivers. In this Study, we asked participants to independently sample and rate seven chocolates, followed by three sequential choices made by either dyads or individuals. In Study 2, we only investigated dyadic choices. Participants rated 18 different chocolates and ranked their favorite five. Based on this information, we then paired participants and created a customized 7-alternative choice set for each dyad so that the favorite chocolate of both dyad members was either the same or different. By manipulating the level of preference alignment between dyad members, we could facilitate or constrain the emergence of reciprocity and analyze its effect on variety-seeking. Study 2 also examined impression management concerns in joint decisions.

We contribute to the literature on variety-seeking in several important ways. First, we documented this important phenomenon in a setting that, despite ubiquitous in the consumer arena, has not yet been studied: joint choice for shared consumption. We revealed that dyads exhibited a higher level of variety-seeking when compared to individuals. Second, by studying a novel decision unit (i.e., dyads), we were able to uncover a new driver of variety-seeking: reciprocity. Third, we identified the dyad members’ preference alignment as a moderator of the effect of reciprocity on dyadic variety. Fourth, we highlighted the uniqueness of joint decisions, by revealing that impression management concerns’ influence on dyadic variety-seeking was limited to the differences among dyad members, and not by the individual scores of each person, likewise was found in previous studies on individual decisions.

Study 1

Study 1 compared the level of variety-seeking between dyads and individuals and investigated the influence of reciprocity and compromise on dyadic variety.

Methods

This Study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the University of Navarra. It followed all relevant guidelines and regulations and presented no harm to the human participants. Participation was voluntary, and all participants gave their informed consent to take part in it.

Participants and design

One hundred and thirteen undergraduates (Mage = 20.7; SD = 0.92; 46% female) participated as a class exercise. Seven students did not complete the procedure, preventing us from collecting their data. This resulted in a valid sample of 106 participants. They were randomly assigned to either the dyad or individual condition. The dyads comprised students who had worked together during that semester.

Procedure and materials

As Fig. 1 shows, initially, every participant independently sampled and rated seven different chocolates on liking and familiarity through an increasing 9-point scale, followed by reporting their own demographics. A few minutes later, the dyads and individuals were presented with a choice set with the same seven chocolates tasted before, and selected the alternative they wanted to eat. Participants in the dyadic condition were asked to work jointly for the remainder of the Study. They could discuss freely while making their choices and share their preferences with one another. Additionally, both dyad members were required to choose the same chocolate. In order to increase the validity of the selection task, all participants had to eat the chocolate selected by them.

Procedure of study 1. The individual condition is represented in brown, and the dyadic condition in blue. Blue dotted line denotes the task was performed by dyad members independently, whereas blue continuous line means the task was done jointly as a dyad.

The selection and eating tasks were repeated every 25 min, a total of three times3. As in real-life consumer decisions, participants were aware that they had to choose multiple times but did not know the exact number. This procedure aimed to prevent strategic choice, particularly in the case of dyads, where knowing the number of interactions in advance affects reciprocity23. Across all three selection rounds, the 7-alternative choice sets remained constant, and participants could choose the same chocolate in different rounds if they wanted to.

Measures

Hereafter, variety-seeking was measured as the total number of different alternatives selected3,46, ranging from a minimum of one (always choosing the same alternative) to a maximum of three (always choosing a different alternative). Due to the discrete nature and limited range of this variable, which favors a skewed distribution, a Shapiro-Wilk test verified whether it departed from normality in each Study.

The chocolate liking values reported during the tasting allowed us to build several measures, such as the liking of the alternative selected (increasing 9-point scale). In the dyadic condition, we built the reciprocity construct by assigning “1” to those dyads where one dyad member got an alternative that they liked more than their partner, and the reverse occurred in at least one of the two remaining selections; if these two requirements did not hold, reciprocity was assigned “0”26. We note that, although both variety-seeking and reciprocity derive from the choices made by dyads, they are conceptually distinct, as they reflect different phenomena and are operationalized based on different aspects of those choices. Furthermore, we verified that they were not significantly correlated, which could have caused endogeneity issues. Accordingly, we used the Spearman rank correlation because variety-seeking may have a skewed distribution and reciprocity is a binary variable.

Additionally, if the selected alternative had the highest aggregate liking for that dyad, it was considered the compromise option (0 = No, 1 = Yes29. Finally, in the individual condition, when the selected alternative had the highest liking value for the participant, it was considered the best for the individual (0 = No, 1 = Yes). The Online Supplementary Information presents all the materials and measures.

Results

A Shapiro-Wilk test confirmed that the distribution of variety-seeking departed from normality (W(70) = 0.84, p < .001), demanding a non-parametric test to statistically compare variety-seeking between dyads and individuals. We found that dyads (Mdyad = 2.92, SD = 0.28) showed a higher level of variety-seeking than did individuals (Mind. = 2.41, SD = 0.74) when choosing from a set of three sequential choices, with the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test indicating that the variety-seeking distributions of dyads and individuals were significantly different (z = 3.49, p < .001, r = .42). For more details see the Online Supplementary Information.

Furthermore, we explored possible predictors of variety-seeking. To ensure that endogeneity did not bias our dyadic model, we checked the correlation between variety-seeking and reciprocity. Because the distribution of variety-seeking deviates from normality, as shown earlier, we used the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, which indicated no evidence of endogeneity (r = .24, p = .158). Additionally, owing to the hierarchical structure of our data45, the three choice trials were nested within participants (Level 1). In the collective condition, the participants were also nested within the dyad (Level 2). We ran hierarchical linear models (HLM) separately for dyad members and individuals.

Model 1 from Table 1 shows that reciprocity was the strongest predictor of dyadic variety-seeking among the factors tested in Study 1 (b = 0.15, SE = 0.04, p < .001). That is, choosing in a reciprocal manner along a set of sequential decisions significantly increased variety-seeking in the collective condition. Additionally, if the selected alternative represented the best possible outcome for the dyad, it also significantly influenced variety-seeking. However, this driver had a lower effect than reciprocity and a negative impact (b = -0.09, SE = 0.04, p = .023). Specifically, selecting a compromise option reduces dyadic variety-seeking. Finally, Model 2 did not reveal any significant predictors of variety-seeking behavior for individuals.

Discussion

Study 1 revealed that dyads exhibit a higher level of variety-seeking than individuals. Specifically, choosing jointly usually leads to the selection of a more diverse set of alternatives than choosing alone, because dyads repeat the same alternative less often than individuals do. Additionally, we show that reciprocity strongly drives dyadic variety-seeking. This finding provides a new implication of reciprocity, which previous literature26 found to be a typical response used by dyads to overcome the characteristic preference mismatch of its members. Furthermore, we found that compromise also influences dyadic variety-seeking. However, its lower predictive power highlights the importance of reciprocity in joint consumer decision-making.

As mentioned earlier, it is important to acknowledge that, because reciprocity and variety-seeking are derived from the same sequence of choices, this may raise the question of whether their association reflects a meaningful conceptual relationship or a measurement artifact. We argue it is the former. Reciprocity, as operationalized here, reflects a pattern in which dyad members take turns getting their preferred option (obtained from their liking intensity) across rounds. This pattern is theoretically distinct from variety-seeking, which simply counts the number of distinct alternatives selected, regardless of the social dynamic behind those selections. We suggest that this overlap is not a measurement artifact, but it reflects our core argument: dyads seek more variety precisely because they take turns getting their preferred option. Study 2 provides a more direct test of this argument by manipulating preference alignment exogenously, thereby isolating the conditions under which reciprocity and variety-seeking is prevented or facilitated in dyadic decisions.

Study 1 was conducted without manipulating the level of preference alignment within the dyads. Consequently, a mismatch of preferences between the dyad members naturally emerged, as is usually the case in joint consumer decisions. However, it follows that if the level of preference alignment increases, that is, if both dyad members favor the same alternative, compromise should gain more prominence, and consequently, dyadic variety-seeking should decrease. This was examined in Study 2.

Study 2

Study 2 focused on joint decision-making. The level of preference alignment between dyad members was manipulated to prevent or facilitate the emergence of reciprocity and verify whether it affects dyadic variety-seeking. Moreover, by experimentally manipulating preference alignment, Study 2 provides greater causal leverage than Study 1, directly addressing concerns about the overlap between reciprocity and variety-seeking.

Additionally, previous literature has found that individual consumers who are more concerned about impression management typically increase their variety-seeking behavior when their choices are visible1,37. Therefore, owing to the inherently observable nature of joint decisions, in Study 2, we explored the role of impression management on variety-seeking in dyadic choices for shared consumption.

Methods

Study 2 was approved by the Ethical Committee of the University of Navarra. It was performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and involved no harm. Participation in the Study was voluntary and all participants gave their informed consent to take part in it.

The Study involved 331 undergraduate participants (Mage = 19.5; SD = 1.31; 40% female) in a two-session class exercise over one month. The participants’ first task consisted of rating 18 chocolates on liking and familiarity (0-100 increasing scale), whose pictures were shown on their digital devices. They were then asked to rank their top five chocolates. Additionally, participants reported how frequently they eat chocolate (0–10 increasing scale) and their demographics. Each participant performed these tasks independently. Based on the chocolate information disclosed by the participants and the experimental conditions to which they were randomly assigned, we then paired them and created a customized choice set of seven chocolates for each dyad (a procedure similar to ref. 47). Half of the dyads were in the aligned preferences condition. Here, the chocolate with the highest liking in the choice set for each dyad member was the same to discourage reciprocity. The other half, which were in the non-aligned preferences condition, received a choice set in which the chocolate with the highest liking for each dyad member was not the same, although both members still exhibited a liking for these two chocolates, to favor reciprocity.

One month later, in class, dyads received their customized choice sets by email and had the opportunity to disclose their preferences while jointly selecting one chocolate for shared consumption every 30 min, for a total of three rounds. The number of choice trials was not revealed in advance. After the last selection, participants independently filled a questionnaire assessing their impression management concerns (9-point scale; 2-item, α = 0.731 and how familiar they were with their dyad partner (9-point increasing scale). Subsequently, there was a lottery, and ⅓ of the participants ate the chocolate selected by them in one of their selections. As several participants did not appear in class on the day of the selection task, our final sample had 52 valid dyads in the aligned condition and 47 in the non-aligned condition. The members of the dyad were familiar with each other. The Online Supplementary Information presents all materials and measures.

Results

In Study 2, the Shapiro-Wilk test showed that variety-seeking did not depart from a normal distribution (W(99) = 0.98, p = .145). Therefore, differences in variety-seeking between the two experimental conditions were tested using a two-tailed t-test. Confirming our expectation, dyads whose preferences were aligned (Maligned = 1.50, SD = 0.64) exhibited less variety than those whose preferences were not aligned (Mnon−aligned = 1.89, SD = 0.79; t(97) = -2.74, p = .007, d = -0.53). Additionally, we ran a two-level HLM model for each experimental condition to explore the predictors of dyadic variety-seeking (see Table 2). Regarding which decision strategies affect joint variety-seeking, we found that when both dyad members coincided with their favorite alternative (Model 3), both reciprocity (b = 0.80, SE = 0.08, p < .001) and compromise (b = -0.14, SE = 0.05, p = .003) influenced variety. More specifically, when preferences were aligned, reciprocity was the main driver, with compromise also being significant, but with a weaker and negative effect. On the other hand, when preferences did not coincide (Model 4), reciprocity emerged as the only significant decision strategy (b = 1.28, SE = 0.05, p < .001), as compromise no longer affected joint variety-seeking (b = -0.01, SE = 0.02, p = .517).

Furthermore, our exploratory analysis on the role of impression management concerns in dyadic variety-seeking, found that a dyad member’s individual level of impression management concerns did not significantly predict dyadic variety regardless of whether preferences were aligned (b = -0.01, SE = 0.01, p = .315) or not (b = 0.00, SE = 0.01, p = .636). However, when we changed the perspective of the analysis from the individual level to the differences within the dyad, impression management significantly affected dyadic variety-seeking. More precisely, when preferences were aligned, larger differences between the two dyad members significantly reduced joint variety-seeking (b = -0.06, SE = 0.02, p < .001). This result suggests that when one member had a stronger willingness to impress and both members preferred the same alternative, making a favorable impression meant showing enthusiasm for the shared option, which resulted in less variety. Conversely, when preferences were not aligned, the larger the intra-dyad difference in impression management concerns, the more variety the dyad exhibited (b = 0.04, SE = 0.01, p = .001). This possibly happened because, making a good impression in this context of preferences mismatch implied demonstrating personal uniqueness in taste, and pushing for greater variety of choice. Discussion

This Study showed that the central role of reciprocity in joint decisions pushes dyads towards variety-seeking behaviors. In fact, Study 2 revealed that this decision strategy was the main driver of dyadic variety-seeking in both experimental conditions. However, this Study also identified a situation in which this tendency can be substantially reduced. Specifically, when choosing from a set in which both dyad members have the same favorite alternative, it is logical for the dyad to choose the same option several times. Then, since compromise naturally becomes more prevalent when preferences are aligned, collective variety-seeking diminishes. On the contrary, when each dyad member has their own favorite alternative, that is, their preferences are not aligned, the compromise loses its raison d’être and reciprocity emerges as the only sound decision strategy. Consequently, a dyad’s variety behavior increases. This suggests that the alignment of preferences is a moderator of dyadic variety-seeking.

Additionally, Study 2 unearths some novel implications of impression management for variety-seeking. Whereas previous literature on individual choices witnessed by other people demonstrated that the level of impression management concerns increases variety-seeking1,37, our exploratory analysis found no such effect for dyadic choices, even though joint decisions are also visible. Instead, we found that it is the magnitude of the differences within the dyad that has a significant effect on dyadic variety-seeking. That is, the influence of impression management on variety-seeking is limited to situations in which one dyad member has a greater desire to impress than the other. Furthermore, the direction of this effect is not universal, but depends on whether both dyad members’ preferred option is the same. More specifically, when preferences are aligned, greater differences in impression management concerns within the dyad reduce variety-seeking, as compromising on the shared option becomes the socially desirable strategy. On the other hand, when preferences are not aligned, wider differences in impression management concerns increase variety-seeking, because causing a good impression in this context requires advocating for one’s own preferences. Overall, these findings highlight the need to investigate joint decisions because they have their own processes and dynamics that go beyond the mere replication of individual choices.

General discussion

In two studies, we documented the occurrence of variety-seeking behavior in choices made jointly by two people for their shared consumption and investigated its potential drivers and moderators. We found that dyads exhibit more variety-seeking than individuals when making a set of sequential choices. Furthermore, we revealed reciprocity is an important driver of dyadic variety-seeking behavior. More specifically, as dyad members often do not coincide with their favorite alternative, they try to overcome their disagreements by having one member choose their preferred option in one decision, and then the other person chooses their preferred item in a subsequent decision. Conversely, we showed that selecting the alternative that maximizes the dyadic aggregate liking, the compromise option, reduces joint variety-seeking. However, this effect is weaker than that of reciprocity. Furthermore, we demonstrated that dyadic variety behaviors decrease when dyad members coincide with their favorite alternative, because in this situation, there is less need to reciprocate and more room for compromise. This sets preference alignment as a moderator of dyadic variety-seeking. Finally, our research showed that larger within-dyad differences in impression management concerns significantly reduce (increase) dyadic variety behaviors when the dyad members’ preferences are (not) aligned.

Our work extends the literature on variety-seeking in several ways by investigating it in an important social context that has not been examined yet: joint choices for shared consumption. In a seminal literature review5, and based on previous work48, it has already been suggested that multiple users might choose multiple objects. Although this theoretical hypothesis was proposed long ago, it has remained untested. Not surprisingly, a new literature review on variety-seeking made almost four decades later11, drew the attention to the necessity of conducting more research on situations that involve consumption with others. Our work partially answers this call by examining the first of the several cases of joint decision-making17.

We make four important contributions. First, by studying variety in joint choices for shared consumption, we are, as far as we know, the first to empirically test Laurent’s hypothesis48. Our research confirms that a decision unit with multiple users typically chooses a greater variety of alternatives than an individual consumer. This considerably enriches the consumer behavior literature by documenting the important phenomenon of variety-seeking in a frequent consumer situation such as joint decision-making.

Second, we reveal two decision strategies that significantly drive dyadic variety-seeking: reciprocity (positive factor) and compromise (negative factor), with the former having a stronger impact than the latter. A rich anthropological literature showed reciprocal behaviors to be present in all human societies34, and to reach such a level of sophistication that humans are labeled as “champions of reciprocity”49. We extend the influence of the essential mechanism of reciprocity to a setting of human behavior that, at first sight, might not be as obvious: joint consumer decisions. More specifically, we demonstrate that when two people jointly choose multiple times, they typically balance their different preferences reciprocally. This causes consumer dyads to exhibit more varied choice behaviors than individual consumers.

Additionally, although our research shows that joint consumer decisions typically result in higher levels of variety-seeking, we also reveal a circumstance in which dyads’ variety behavior decreases. More specifically, when both dyad members coincide with their favorite alternative, there is less need to reciprocate, and therefore, variety-seeking is reduced. Then, our third contribution is positioning preference alignment as a moderator of dyadic variety-seeking.

Fourth, previous literature showed that when individual decisions have a strong social dimension, such as when choices are public37 or made on behalf of other consumers39, the level of impression management concerns of the individual decision-maker influences variety-seeking. Nonetheless, our research reveals that in joint decisions for shared consumption, it is exclusively the magnitude of the differences between dyad members in impression management concerns that significantly affects variety-seeking, rather than the individual scores of the decision-makers. This emphasizes the importance of considering intra-dyad differences when investigating joint decisions, as they incorporate the dynamics emerging from members’ interdependencies, something naturally absent in earlier research on variety-seeking as it focused on individual decisions only.

Our research is not free of limitations. First, we restricted our study to groups of two people. Although dyads make many joint consumer decisions, larger groups are also present (e.g., a group of friends choosing which movie to watch). Therefore, it would be interesting to verify whether, in larger decision units, where it is necessary to balance the preferences of more people, joint variety-seeking behavior increases, or if managing a wider range of preferences implies that sacrificing in favor of a specific member becomes so challenging that groups prefer to compromise, which then reduces variety-seeking.

Second, our empirical studies required dyads to make only three choices. Although this limited number of choice trials was sufficient for reciprocity to emerge and increase variety-seeking, we cannot be certain on how these effects would operate if the number of joint decisions were to vary. More specifically, because the benefits of cooperation—and thus the motivation to reciprocate—increase with repeated interaction23, the number of joint decisions may moderate the relationship between reciprocity and dyadic variety-seeking. That is, in dyads that expect to make only a small number of joint decisions, reciprocity might be less prevalent and, consequently, variety-seeking may decrease. On the other hand, when dyads engage in a large number of shared decisions, reciprocity is likely to increase and, subsequently, variety should grow as well. Future research should examine that, thereby strengthening our understanding of the role of reciprocity in dyadic variety-seeking.

Third, our investigation of variety-seeking in joint decisions was restricted to situations in which dyads jointly choose a product to be consumed by all of them together (Case 1 from Gorlin and Dhar17. Therefore, our findings are specific to this type of joint decision. In cases where group members decide jointly but consume separately, the dynamics of reciprocity and variety-seeking may operate differently, representing an important avenue for future investigation.

Fourth, and following a typical practice in variety-seeking research50, we used a low-involvement product (i.e., chocolate) as a stimuli in our two empirical studies. Investigating decisions regarding such products is important because most everyday consumer decisions involve this type of choices. However, as variety behaviors fluctuate across product categories6, it would be worthwhile for future studies to confirm whether our results hold in joint decisions of high-involvement products (e.g., houses, cars), as the lower buying frequency of these products and the higher costs of sacrificing might hinder the occurrence of reciprocity.

An additional issue related to the use of chocolate as a stimuli was the fact that certain alternatives contained traces of alcohol. While this aspect did not constitute a concern in our sample, given that none of the participants came from cultural or religious backgrounds that restrict alcohol consumption, it may nevertheless represent a potential source of bias in more heterogeneous populations. Future research should therefore account for participants’ sociocultural and religious characteristics when designing studies involving products that could contain alcohol.

To conclude, this work investigates the phenomenon of variety-seeking in an important but under-researched setting: joint choices for shared consumption. We show that a group of two people typically exhibits more variety-seeking than a single person choosing alone. This happens because when two consumers choose together, their preferences do not necessarily coincide. To address this problem, dyads allow each member to pick their favorite on different occasions, such that in the end, everyone gets pleased. This mechanism of reciprocity, which is naturally absent in individual choices, makes variety-seeking higher in dyadic than in individual choices. However, when members’ preferences are aligned, compromise gains more room, and dyadic variety diminishes. Finally, our research also revealed that the influence of impression management concerns on dyadic variety-seeking arises from intra-dyad differences and not from the level of each person involved in the selection, as documented in the previous literature on individual choice.

We trust our work will encourage more research on joint choices, a type of decision that, despite being ubiquitous and unique, is often neglected in the literature. In particular, we hope to have created the foundations for future research on variety-seeking in joint decisions, so that, the call made by Sevilla and colleagues11 starts to be finally addressed in a substantial and rich manner.
