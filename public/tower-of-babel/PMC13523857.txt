A research agenda for aging and climate change: recommendations from a research-to-practice consensus workshop
Authors: Karl Pillemer, Chloe Shores, Timothy J Mateer, Nancy M Wells, Matthew Luebke, Maeve Givens, Andrea Cianfrani, Allison Nickerson, Emily A Gadbois, Mark S Lachs, Michelle Putnam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough climate change poses disproportionate health risks to older adults, studies addressing this critical problem remain scarce and fragmented. As research on the intersection of population aging and climate change effects is increasing, guidance is needed regarding key research priorities for the field. To inform future inquiry, a research-to-practice consensus workshop was convened with 44 multidisciplinary participants, including researchers, practitioners, and policymakers. Using structured small-group discussions and an established priority-setting process, participants critiqued existing research, identified knowledge gaps, and achieved consensus on a research agenda for aging and climate change. Fourteen research priorities included quantifying economic impacts of climate change on older populations, developing evidence-based climate communication strategies, and addressing program access barriers. Additional priorities encompassed healthcare integration, emergency protocols, and disparities research. These findings provide a practice-driven, evidence-informed framework for prioritizing research topics, intervention development, and funding priorities addressing the intersection of aging and climate change.Keywords: Climate resilience, Heat vulnerability, Translational research priorities, Health equity, Extreme weather events

This article presents the first consensus research agenda addressing climate change impacts on older adults. Using an innovative research-to-practice consensus workshop model, we engaged 44 researchers, practitioners, and policymakers to identify critical knowledge gaps. The resulting 14 research priorities emphasize actionable knowledge over descriptive research—prioritizing economic analysis, program accessibility, misinformation mitigation, and multi-hazard preparedness strategies. This practice-driven agenda differs markedly from existing academic emphases, providing concrete direction for future research that can inform the development of interventions, policies, and systems changes.

Climate change is one of the most pressing global challenges of our time, and older adults are especially vulnerable to its impacts. Older people, as a group, face increased health risks during and after extreme weather events due to a combination of physical and social factors. Their risk is heightened by underlying health conditions, mobility limitations, caregiver dependence, and social isolation, among others (Harrington & Otto, 2023; Prina et al., 2024). Older individuals are also especially vulnerable to the effects of air pollution, vector-borne illnesses, forced relocation, and interruptions in the availability of food, services, and medical care resulting from climate change events (Carnes et al., 2014; McDermott-Levy et al., 2019). Mental health effects from climate change can also be devastating for older adults, exacerbating vulnerabilities (Ayalon et al., 2021).

Although the general elevated risk that climate change presents has been well documented, the heterogeneity of the older population also must be taken into consideration (Tipaldo et al., 2024). Older adults’ vulnerability to climate change is shaped by the intersection of age with race, income, disability status, living situation, cognitive capacity, geographic location, and other factors. This heterogeneity means that climate risks and appropriate interventions differ across subgroups of older people (Prina et al., 2024). Further, national and regional contexts are also heterogenous, with low-resource countries in the Global South at particular risk of heat events and droughts, while also having fewer resources to promote resilience (Harrington & Otto, 2023).

Older adults’ greater vulnerability across these heterogeneous contexts is evident in climate-related disasters that have occurred over the past three decades. In every case where data are available, older people make up most deaths, vastly disproportionate to their percentage of the general population (Liao & Hu, 2025). These statistics underscore why protecting older populations is one of the greatest needs in preparing for the effects of climate change. Although climate change narratives frequently emphasize impacts on future generations, older people are already being adversely affected by intensifying extreme weather events and other climate-related impacts.

Therefore, research is needed to promote older people’s climate resilience: that is, the capacity of older adults, caregiving systems, and communities to anticipate, prepare for, respond to, and recover from climate-related threats in ways that preserve health, functioning, and quality of life (cf., Ebi & Semenza, 2008; Haq & Gutman, 2022). Climate change increases the frequency, intensity, and duration of extreme weather events, including heat waves, hurricanes, floods, wildfires, and droughts, as well as slower-onset changes such as rising baseline temperatures, sea level rise, and degraded air quality (IPCC, 2021). Both research and interventions are needed to foster resilience among the older population in the face of these effects.

Recently, the fields of gerontology, geriatrics, public health, medicine, and environmental science have begun to respond to the urgent need for knowledge. Scientific publications on the topic of climate and aging have increased in recent years (Prina et al., 2024; Tohit et al., 2026). This academic interest is mirrored by policymakers’ increased attention to the intersection of aging and climate change. Older people are increasingly likely to be identified as an at-risk population in state climate action plans (Carlson et al., 2024). Aging-related organizations in the United States (Kaldes, 2022) and internationally (Charveriat et al., 2023; World Health Organization, 2022) recently have identified climate change as both a critical challenge to the well-being of older people and an opportunity for their active engagement.

The development of this rapidly emerging field of inquiry and policy provides a unique opportunity to intentionally consider the most pressing research priorities. However, no research agenda focused specifically on older people and climate change has been published to date. Further, there is a need to incorporate research priorities identified by service providers, practitioners, and policymakers. Without systematic input from experts working directly with older populations, research risks overlooking critical implementation barriers and practical solutions. The disproportionate vulnerability of the older population to climate hazards demands that research priorities be grounded in the lived experiences and practice-based wisdom of individuals confronting such challenges.

In this article, we present findings from the “Research-to-Practice Consensus Workshop on Climate and Aging,” which convened an interdisciplinary group of 44 researchers, policymakers, and frontline practitioners to define shared priorities for future study. Following principles of community-engaged and participatory research, the workshop aimed to ensure that individuals who work directly with and for older adults, in areas such as healthcare, housing, emergency management, and community services, played a leading role in shaping the research agenda. This workshop produced a set of action-oriented research priorities designed to accelerate the development of climate resilience and engagement strategies for older adults.

Method

The research-to-practice consensus workshop model

The Research-to-Practice Consensus Workshop (RPCW) model is an evidence-based methodology for bridging the gap between research and community practice through structured engagement (Pillemer et al., 2011, 2015; Riffin et al., 2022; Sabir et al., 2009; Trevino et al., 2018; 2020; Wethington et al., 2018). Developed by the Cornell Institute for Translational Research on Aging, the model adapts traditional scientific consensus conferences by incorporating the community-based participatory research (CBPR) approach to ensure that research priorities align with real-world practice needs (Sabir et al., 2006). The model has demonstrated utility across diverse aging-related topics, including palliative care, elder mistreatment prevention, social isolation interventions, mobile health technologies, dementia caregiving, and cancer care.

As described in the studies cited above, the RPCW model addresses a critical challenge in translational research: the disconnect between academic research priorities and the practical needs of frontline service providers. Traditional consensus conferences typically involve only scientific experts, potentially overlooking crucial practice-based perspectives. In contrast, the RPCW model incorporates several CBPR principles: (1) co-learning and capacity building between researchers and practitioners; (2) equitable participation of community partners in all phases of the process; (3) emphasis on community-identified priorities; and (4) commitment to translating findings into action (Cargo & Mercer, 2008; Israel et al., 1998; Sabir et al., 2006). Therefore, the RPCW model deliberately includes practitioners and policymakers as equal partners with researchers, fostering multidirectional knowledge exchange that enhances both research relevance and practice effectiveness.

Key distinguishing features of the RPCW model include (1) the use of accessible, non-technical language to facilitate meaningful dialogue across disciplines; (2) structured facilitation ensuring all voices are heard; and (3) explicit focus on generating actionable recommendations. The model thus promotes practice-based evidence (Green, 2006) alongside evidence-based practice, recognizing that practitioners possess valuable insights about implementation barriers and facilitators that may not be captured in traditional research.

The RPCW model follows six structured steps (Pillemer et al., 2011, 2015; Sabir et al., 2009; Wethington et al., 2018). Across RCPWs, these steps include (1) topic selection by a community advisory committee based on practice relevance and existing research gaps; (2) preparing a nontechnical background paper synthesizing current scientific evidence; (3) identifying a panel of expert discussants representing both research and practice perspectives; (4) recruiting workshop participants to ensure a diverse, multidisciplinary group of researchers, practitioners, and policymakers; (5) convening a structured workshop featuring group discussions and prioritizing to generate recommendations; and (6) conducting a follow-up roundtable to refine and finalize recommendations. The Cornell University Institutional Review Board (IRB) determined that this project did not constitute human participant research and therefore did not require IRB review. In this section, we briefly describe five of the six steps in the process, as dissemination components are currently underway.

Topic selection

Topic selection was determined through an existing research-practice partnership comprising Cornell University, LiveOn NY, and the New York Academy of Medicine. These organizations represent academic research, an organization representing several hundred aging service agencies, and public health and environmental expertise, respectively. To supplement this partnership input, LiveOn NY convened a small group of member organization representatives, including senior center directors and aging service providers, to provide frontline perspective on the relevance and framing of the issue. This two-level structure, which combines core partnership decision-making with frontline practitioner consultation, ensured that diverse types of community partners shaped the topic selection. Through discussion among the partner organizations, consensus emerged around the need for actionable knowledge about the dual issues of climate change risk prevention and older adult engagement.

Preparation of a nontechnical background paper

Members of the project team prepared a nontechnical background paper that reviewed major issues at the intersection of aging and climate change and summarized recommendations for research priorities from the scientific community. The goal of the background paper was to provide scientific information to practitioners in nontechnical language, thereby reducing the knowledge gap with researchers. In the RPCW model, the background paper is not intended as a formal or comprehensive review of the literature. Rather, its purpose is to synthesize existing knowledge in accessible, nontechnical language, serving as a springboard for workshop discussion and ensuring that practitioners enter the deliberations with a shared grounding in the current science, without placing undue demands on their time.

The primary basis for the background paper was a forthcoming edited handbook on aging and climate change (Pillemer & Ayalon, in press), which includes 20 chapters written by scholars from diverse disciplines and world regions. The volume covers key aspects of aging and climate change, including heat, flooding, air quality, mental health, social isolation, long-term care, emergency preparedness, equity, and climate activism. Importantly, contributors include researchers from the Global South, expanding the review beyond the geographic bias that characterizes most existing literature on aging and climate change, which draws on studies from North America, Australia, and Western Europe (Prina et al., 2024). Each chapter was specifically structured to include the authors’ recommendations for major research priorities in the field. Therefore, the handbook is an unusually well-suited resource for the purposes of the RPCW.

The research recommendations from the 20 chapters were reviewed and synthesized, allowing the background paper to reflect current knowledge through June 2025. This primary synthesis was supplemented by consulting available reviews of the aging and climate change literature. At the time the background paper was prepared, no comprehensive scoping or systematic reviews of the general field had been published, although targeted systematic reviews on specific subtopics were consulted (Ayalon et al., 2022; Lima et al., 2024; Pinna et al., 2024).

This background paper was reviewed by all participants in advance of the workshop, providing a basis for the group’s deliberations. In preparation for the RPCW, participants were asked to consider the following questions:

Identify a panel of expert discussants

The RPCW convened two expert panels to examine aging and climate resilience from complementary perspectives (Trevino et al., 2020). Each panel consisted of four participants (eight total). The research panel included representatives from Cornell University, a federal public health agency, an academic medical center, and a research-focused environmental organization. The practice panel included representatives from a governmental agency focused on aging, a major philanthropic foundation focused on aging, a senior services network, and a community-based climate advocacy organization. Research panelists were recruited through the project team’s professional networks and the Special Interest Group on Aging and Climate Change of the Gerontological Society of America. Panelists were asked to review the background paper in advance, and each offered a 5-min response to the paper. A major objective of the initial presentations was to stress the equal value of the contributions from both researchers and clinicians, serving to effectively demonstrate an environment of constructive and professional exchange between these two groups.

Recruit workshop participants

Workshop participants were purposively selected to convene a diverse, multidisciplinary group of thought leaders and frontline experts whose expertise and experiences were essential to defining a comprehensive and practical research agenda. Because aging and climate change research is still relatively new, the project team intentionally brought together participants from both domains, including some with deep knowledge in one area but limited exposure to the other. Practitioners were identified through the project’s network of community partners under the guidance of LiveOn NY, whereas researchers were again recruited from academic and professional networks, including the Special Interest Group on Aging and Climate Change.

The conference brought together 44 participants representing diverse professional roles and expertise areas. Nearly half of the participants (45%) were practitioners working directly in aging services and community-based organizations, while 39% were researchers from academic and research institutions, and 16% were policy professionals from government agencies and advocacy organizations. Selection criteria for workshop participation were (1) professional expertise in aging services, aging-related research, public health, environmental policy, emergency management, healthcare, or related fields; (2) willingness to engage across disciplinary and sectoral boundaries; and (3) availability for the full day-long workshop and follow-up roundtable. We attempted to ensure representation across sectors, levels of experience, and degrees of familiarity with either or both of the aging and climate domains.

Participants’ areas of focus reflected the interdisciplinary nature of aging and climate change, with aging services being the most represented category (32%), followed by climate and public health (16%), health policy (11%), medicine (11%), gerontology (9%), and environmental policy (9%). The work settings of participants were distributed relatively evenly across academic institutions (27%), human service agencies (27%), community-based organizations (23%), government agencies (14%), and medical care facilities (9%), ensuring that multiple perspectives, from research and policy development to direct service delivery, were represented in the discussions. Most participants had more than 15 years of professional experience, bringing seasoned insight to the deliberations. This range of expertise provided a solid foundation for generating recommendations that reflect both scientific evidence and practice wisdom.

Conduct RPCW

To develop the recommendations, a day-long in-person workshop was convened in New York City. The session began with 5-min responses from each of eight experts in research and practice. Participants then joined five breakout groups to identify major research directions with practical and policy implications. The breakout groups were intentionally constructed to include a mix of practitioners and researchers, as well as individuals from different sectors. Facilitators, who were members of the project organizing team, opened the discussion by asking: “What knowledge is needed to design policies and interventions that protect and support older adults facing climate change?” After one hour, facilitators reported back 8–10 priorities from each group for further dialogue. A total of 47 individual recommendations were proposed across the breakout groups.

The investigators and facilitators synthesized the recommendations through a rapid, two-step process of consolidation and categorization (Sabir et al., 2009). This work was performed immediately between the small-group presentations and the subsequent plenary session. They reduced the original 47 recommendations to 23 by eliminating duplicate recommendations and grouping several overlapping recommendations. In the final plenary session of the RPCW, the 23 recommendations were presented.

To achieve consensus on the workshop recommendations, the RPCW used two techniques: voting for preliminary prioritization followed by iterative consensus deliberation to determine the final set of recommendations.This hybrid approach addresses the limitations of each method alone, recognizing that voting efficiently reduces options while deliberation ensures quality and genuine consensus (Jones & Hunter, 1995; Sutherland et al., 2011). In the RPCW model, achieving consensus is critically important, as voting alone could highlight differences and mask common priorities between researchers and practitioners.

Therefore, multi-voting for preliminary prioritization preceded iterative consensus deliberation (Jones & Hunter, 1995). Multi-voting is designed to narrow down long lists through structured allocation of votes, allowing participants to distribute votes across options based on preference intensity. This method allows a group to reduce large sets of possibilities before detailed deliberation and achieving consensus (Sutherland et al., 2011). In the RCPW, dot voting (a version of multi-voting) was used. For dot voting, all recommendations were listed on flipcharts. Ten dots were distributed to each participant to register their votes. Participants then placed their dots next to recommendations they supported. They were able to choose how to distribute their dots, ranging from placing all 10 on a single option to placing one dot on 10 different recommendations.

After the voting, the participants discussed and interpreted the results, resulting in consensus on the preliminary list of recommendations. Following the workshop, the project team reviewed the priorities for redundancy, clarity, and relevance. The results of the multi-voting process were used to help guide this subsequent review. Community advisors were consulted to create the next draft of recommendations to ensure that any changes were consistent with the group discussion during the RPCW.

Follow-up roundtable

Approximately 1 month later, all workshop participants were invited to a virtual follow-up roundtable (approximately 50% attended). Prior to the meeting, all participants were sent the revised list of priorities resulting from the workshop and project team review. This session focused on additional clarification of the recommendations, grouping overlapping ideas, and refining the list as needed. As a result of the follow-up roundtable, the list of recommendations was reduced from 23 to 14, primarily through consolidation of several redundant recommendations and further review of voting results. Roundtable participants also recommended that the report from the conference organize the recommendations into general thematic areas, and they provided recommendations for this organization. After the roundtable, the resulting set of priorities was sent to all participants for their approval. No substantive suggestions were made for changes in the priorities, and no dissents to the final list of priorities were registered, establishing the list as the final product of the consensus workshop.

Results

Following the suggestion of follow-up roundtable participants, the research team organized the 14 priorities into four thematic categories (these groupings were not part of the consensus workshop process itself and do not represent a separate analytic finding). The categories are (1) “Systems and Policy,” which includes recommendations for studies that address macro-level factors such as economic issues, the role of influence groups, and health and long-term care systems. (2) “Communication and Education,” addresses information dissemination, communication, and training priorities. (3) “Equity and Access” recommendations focus on addressing inequity in climate preparedness and response and overcoming structural barriers to adaptation and resilience. (4) “Individual Resilience and Support” includes recommendations related to assessing and addressing vulnerability and using social networks and technology to develop and enhance support systems. Research priorities for aging and climate change.

Systems and policy

This category addresses the need for systemic changes in how institutions respond to climate change and its corresponding impact on older adults. These priorities emphasize transforming economic frameworks, healthcare systems, long-term care facilities, and policy processes to embed climate preparedness as a standard component of aging services. Research recommendations in this area focuses on building the economic case for climate resilience investments, integrating climate considerations into routine healthcare delivery, understanding policy influences, and strengthening infrastructure in settings serving older populations.

Research the societal economic impacts of climate change in relation to aging

Practitioners and policymakers were concerned about how to motivate government and organizations to acknowledge and act on the elevated risks for older people. The frustration with inaction stems from their contact with people who have age-related physiological changes, chronic health conditions, and social vulnerability to climate change. Workshop members asserted that research on the economic costs of inaction, and the return on investment of protective interventions, are essential to securing the resources needed to prevent excess mortality and morbidity. Participants offered examples of how such economic analysis would allow them to make a case for funding resources, such as cooling centers, improved emergency response, and information systems.

Participants emphasized the critical need for a convincing economic case to be made for investing in climate resilience for older adults. Efforts should include quantifying the economic impacts of climate threats and developing effective strategies for demonstrating the costs and benefits of interventions for the older population. Specific recommendations included investigating the cost of inaction (e.g., increased hospitalization, health care utilization, recovery after disaster), as well as the cost savings and return-on-investment of resilience, adaptation, and prevention interventions.

Research innovative strategies for health care systems to embed climate preparedness, risk conversations, and planning

As climate change accelerates, older adults will face heightened acute and chronic health risks. Healthcare systems are uniquely positioned to help them prepare for these challenges. Participants recommended researching strategies to systematically integrate climate considerations into routine healthcare delivery. Such efforts should include embedding climate preparedness conversations with older patients into annual wellness visits, developing protocols for providers to discuss heat safety and emergency planning with patients, and creating referral pathways to connect older people with community resources, such as cooling centers and weatherization programs. Research should also examine how to build provider capacity through training, develop clinical workflows that normalize climate risk assessment, and identify system-level changes that make climate preparedness a standard component of care.

Examine how special interest groups influence climate and aging efforts

Participants asserted that understanding the influence of special interest groups is essential for developing effective, equitable climate resilience policies for older adults. Research should examine both beneficial and potentially harmful influences, including how fossil fuel industry lobbying may delay climate action, how insurance industry practices affect access to climate adaptation resources, and how pharmaceutical and medical device companies shape healthcare system responses to climate threats. This research should also investigate how advocacy organizations and grassroots movements can counterbalance corporate influence and amplify the voices of older people in policy discussions. Researchers can help identify who has power in the community (“power mapping”). They can also inform policy analysis regarding whose interests are being prioritized in climate adaptation funding, program design, and regulatory frameworks, helping to ensure that policies serve the needs of vulnerable older populations rather than narrow economic interests.

Research ways to address climate risks in long-term care and senior living environments

Long-term care facilities and senior living communities face unique climate vulnerabilities that demand targeted research and intervention. Residents often have multiple chronic conditions, cognitive impairments, and mobility limitations that make emergency evacuations challenging. Power outages pose life-threatening risks when medical equipment, temperature control, and medication refrigeration are compromised. Research should examine how to strengthen infrastructure resilience in these settings, including backup power systems, cooling capacity, and water security. Additionally, studies can investigate workforce preparedness because undertrained staff may struggle to implement emergency protocols effectively. Research must also address equity issues, as lower-income facilities and those serving racial and ethnic minority populations often have fewer resources for climate adaptation, potentially widening existing disparities in care quality and resident safety.

Communication and education

Effective communication is essential for preparing older people and those who support them with climate-related challenges. This category encompasses research on developing evidence-based messaging strategies, countering misinformation, disseminating emergency information through appropriate channels, and building capacity among formal and informal caregivers. These priorities recognize that information flow, through both formal communication systems and informal social networks, plays a critical role in climate resilience.

Develop, evaluate, and disseminate evidence-based communication strategies to improve public understanding of climate change and to counter misinformation

Participants emphasized that support for climate change initiatives hinges on acceptance of scientifically accurate information. Research is needed to support the development and testing of evidence-based, culturally appropriate, and generationally tailored communication strategies that improve older adults’ awareness and engagement around climate change. These efforts should include identifying trusted and unbiased messengers, addressing misinformation, and creating public awareness campaigns tailored to the older population.

Participants also asserted that research on communication strategies must take place in collaboration with older people and community-based organizations that serve them. Their view acknowledged that older adults possess valuable lived experience and insights about which messengers are trusted and which communication channels are most effective. Research should adopt participatory approaches that position older individuals as active partners in designing communication interventions, ensuring relevance and cultural appropriateness while enhancing acceptability and uptake of climate messaging.

Research dissemination methods and platforms for reaching older adults across all stages of climate emergency management

Older adults face higher risks and casualties during natural disasters, relying heavily on systems (e.g., transportation, utilities, health care) that may fail (Liao & Hu, 2025; McDermott-Levy et al., 2019). Evacuations are especially difficult because of mobility issues and medical equipment needs, and power outages further threaten access to life-sustaining devices. Research is needed to develop communication methods that support safe evacuation and response, particularly for older persons who are isolated, cognitively impaired, or have limited mobility. For example, research could address how to ensure that emergency alerts reach older individuals through multiple channels, accounting for hearing and vision impairments as well as limited digital literacy. Studies are also needed to determine how best to maintain communication between older adults and their support systems when traditional infrastructure fails, including during extended power outages. Special attention is required for the specific information needs of family and professional in-home caregivers who may be making decisions remotely during an emergency.

Develop and evaluate training for paid and unpaid caregivers to address climate risks

Caregivers (e.g., home health staff, family members, long-term care personnel) play a critical role in protecting older people from climate-related risks. Participants emphasized the need to equip both paid and unpaid caregivers with training and tools to recognize climate risks, plan for emergencies, and support health and safety. Research is needed to develop, implement, and evaluate effective, scalable caregiver training. Priority research includes developing and testing training curricula that address climate-specific risks such as heat illness recognition, medication storage during power outages, and evacuation planning for persons with mobility limitations. Studies can also address how best to integrate climate preparedness content into existing caregiver training programs to ensure systematic uptake without creating an additional burden on caregivers.

Equity and access

The workshop asserted that climate change disproportionately affects older people who already face marginalization due to race, income, housing status, disability, or other factors. This priority highlights research on how structural inequities, program design flaws, and power dynamics create differential vulnerability, with the goal of developing interventions that reduce rather than perpetuate disparities.

Examine barriers for older adults’ access to climate change resilience and adaptation programs

Participants emphasized the need to improve access to existing climate-related programs. Many older people face significant barriers to utilizing available resources, including transportation challenges, unequal geographic distribution of resources, and language or communication obstacles. Research should identify and test strategies to reduce these barriers and expand program reach. Community-engaged approaches and ongoing needs assessments were recommended to ensure that climate resilience programs are genuinely accessible and responsive to the diverse needs of older populations, particularly those who are currently underserved.

Research the impacts of climate change on marginalized older adults, considering differences by age, race, gender, income, housing status, health, and other social factors

Participants agreed that equity should serve as the guiding principle at every stage of research, from study design through dissemination. Climate vulnerabilities are amplified by intersecting social determinants of health. Research must not only document these disparities but also assist in addressing them through targeted interventions and policy research that confront structural barriers. This equity-centered approach should inform recruitment strategies, data collection methods, analysis frameworks, and translation of findings into practice.

Workshop participants called for research that quantifies the public health impact of these intersecting factors. For example, low-income older people living in substandard housing without air conditioning face compounded risks during heat waves and live in neighborhoods that experience higher exposure to urban heat islands and pollution. Research must not only document these disparities but also examine the structural and systemic factors that create and perpetuate differential vulnerability. This information will be essential for the development of both general and targeted interventions.

Examine how structural barriers impact older adults’ ability to avoid or adapt to climate risks

Structural barriers disproportionately affect older persons from marginalized communities, perpetuating patterns of historical disinvestment and systemic inequality. Factors such as inadequate housing, limited transportation access, and lack of nearby resources (e.g., cooling centers) directly compound climate vulnerabilities and limit adaptive capacity. This situation is exacerbated for people with low incomes, mobility limitations, or who live alone. Future research must examine these accumulated disadvantages to inform policy recommendations that address the root causes of differential risk.

Individual resilience and support

Although systemic change is essential, individual-level tools and support are also necessary for protecting older people from climate risks. This category focuses on developing practical assessment instruments, leveraging technology appropriately, understanding the role of social connections, and addressing psychological impacts.

Create validated screening tools and protocols to assess climate vulnerability in older adults

Participants recommended developing and validating screening protocols that assess key vulnerabilities associated with housing conditions, mobility limitations, cognitive status, heat sensitivity, and availability of social support. Such tools could help healthcare providers, social service organizations, and emergency management teams better provide interventions and resources to those at greatest risk.

These screening tools should be designed for systematic integration into existing frameworks and workflows. By embedding climate vulnerability assessment into routine processes—such as Medicare annual wellness visits, long-term care facility admissions, and social service intake procedures—screening for climate preparedness can become a routine component of aging services. This embedded approach can ensure that identifying at-risk older adults becomes a standard component of quality care and service delivery.

Advance understanding of how social networks affect older adults during both short-term climate events and long-term adaptation and resilience efforts

Social networks serve as infrastructure for climate resilience among older people, yet they remain understudied in climate adaptation research. During acute climate events such as heat waves or hurricanes, participants noted that strong social connections may mean the difference between life and death. Social network resources include friends and neighbors who check in, family members who facilitate evacuation, and community organizations that provide cooling centers or emergency supplies. Research should examine how distinct types of social ties (e.g., family, friends, neighbors, faith communities, service organizations) contribute to preparedness and response to climate emergencies, as well as how digital and in-person networks complement each other. Critically, research must address how social isolation compounds climate vulnerability, especially among individuals with disabilities, limited English proficiency, or those living in under-resourced communities.

Develop and leverage technology to promote climate adaptation and resilience in older adults

Participants recognized technology’s significant potential to enhance climate resilience for older persons. Smart home systems can monitor indoor temperatures and air quality, wearable devices can detect heat stress, and mobile applications can deliver real-time emergency alerts. However, research must address the substantial barriers that prevent many older adults from benefiting from these innovations, including cost, digital literacy, lack of internet connectivity, visual or cognitive impairments, and limited technical support. Research should adopt co-design approaches that position older people as partners in developing age-friendly interfaces and features. Further, participants stressed that technology research should promote equitable interventions and ensure innovations reach those who are most vulnerable.

Research the ongoing psychological and mental health impacts of climate change

Participants supported researching the psychological toll climate change has on the older population. Negative effects include disaster trauma, chronic stress, anticipatory anxiety, grief, and existential distress. Research should examine how climate impacts intersect with existing mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety, dementia, and how trauma from extreme weather events may trigger or exacerbate PTSD. Further, studies must explore protective factors and sources of resilience, including community connection, exposure to nature, meaningful climate action, features of the built environment, and culturally responsive mental health interventions. Research on these psychological dimensions should focus on the development of mental health services, peer support programs, and disaster recovery approaches that address the impacts of climate change on older adults’ emotional well-being and quality of life.

Discussion

The RPCW process engaged a group representing multiple professional sectors (academia, community-based services, government, philanthropy, and clinical medicine), varied types of disciplinary expertise, and a range of direct experience with older adult populations and climate-related work. RCPW participants generated 14 recommendations for future research by aligning the practitioner and policymaker lens with the scientific priorities of researchers. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first consensus research agenda created on climate and aging, as well as the first to rely on researcher–policymaker–practitioner dialogue as a method for generating research priorities. These recommendations are a promising starting point for translational research on the needs and contributions of older adults in the face of a changing climate.

Taken together, the recommendations represent a comprehensive agenda that balances macro-level policy and systems reform with micro-level attention to individual vulnerability and the social contexts that shape climate resilience among older adults. It is noteworthy that a number of the recommendations from the RPCW differ from dominant research paradigms in the academic literature. In this context, it is illuminating to examine how the research priorities from the RPCW differed from those proposed in the researcher-generated background paper. This comparison revealed important distinctions between academic and practice-oriented perspectives.

First, there is one particularly clear difference between current emphases in the field and the RPCW recommendations. Existing research on aging and climate change is heavily concentrated in public health epidemiology (Montoro-Ramírez et al., 2025). Studies have focused on identifying heat and air pollution vulnerability zones (Prina et al., 2024), documenting mortality during specific extreme weather events (Liao & Hu, 2025), and quantifying the health impacts of hazards, especially heat waves and disasters (Figueiredo et al., 2024).

Although this hazard-specific approach has established the disproportionate risk that older adults face during climate events, workshop practitioners deemphasized single-hazard investigations in favor of comprehensive preparedness strategies. Rather than calling for dedicated research on extreme heat, floods, or other individual threats, participants prioritized approaches that address the vulnerability of older adults across multiple, often cascading climate risks. These risks range from heat and floods to power outages and supply chain disruptions.

This distinction reflects a difference in perspective: whereas epidemiological research typically isolates specific hazards for investigation, practitioners working directly with older clients need integrated frameworks that support multi-hazard preparedness planning. The practitioner view acknowledges the reality that older adults and the systems serving them must simultaneously prepare for compound and intersecting climate threats rather than addressing each hazard separately. The RPCW findings suggest future research should center on developing holistic resilience strategies applicable across diverse climate scenarios. Further, given the abundance of extant evidence, practice, and policy, participants suggested that additional studies demonstrating vulnerability to climate change effects are of lower priority than prevention and intervention research.

It should also be noted that the four organizational themes employed in this article are deeply interconnected in practice. For example, structural barriers to program access (Equity and Access) are directly shaped by policy frameworks and resource allocation decisions (Systems and Policy). Communication strategies (Communication and Education) must be developed with equity considerations in mind to be effective for the most vulnerable older adults. Individual resilience (Individual Resilience and Support) is fundamentally constrained by the systems and structural conditions within which older adults live. This interconnectedness suggests that research and intervention in this area should adopt cross-cutting, system-oriented approaches rather than treating these domains in isolation.

Second, economic analysis emerged as a top RPCW priority. Participants emphasized the critical need to quantify costs of inaction and demonstrate return on investment for climate resilience interventions, a framing that was absent from the background paper’s health-focused approach. They see economic evidence for taking climate action as a key way to leverage resources for these efforts. Practitioners’ focus on demonstrating economic value reflects an understanding that quantified impacts carry significant weight in securing policy support and funding. Further, the workshop’s call for economic evidence aligns with broader environmental policy practice, where cost-benefit analysis has become central to decision-making (OECD, 2018).

Third, countering misinformation was a high-priority concern. Although prior research has addressed communication, RPCW participants elevated evidence-based communication strategies and addressing misinformation to a top priority specifically for older people. It is likely that this emphasis emerged from practitioners’ encounters with older clients exposed to climate misinformation. Research demonstrates that older adults are especially vulnerable to misinformation in general, and there is extensive research on climate misinformation broadly. However, with very few exceptions (e.g., Ayalon, 2024) the specific intersection of these two areas—that is, older adults’ specific vulnerability to climate misinformation—remains largely unexplored in the academic literature. Practitioners urge attention to remedying this gap.

Fourth, program accessibility emerged as a distinct priority. Practitioners identified barriers preventing older adults from utilizing existing climate programs as requiring immediate research attention. Prior studies have focused more on developing new interventions than addressing implementation barriers to program use. However, the limited research on the topic demonstrates such barriers. For example, a study found that although heat-related hospitalizations were higher among older adults, barriers to cooling center access among older people included lack of transportation and awareness of the location of centers (Mallen et al., 2022). The workshop suggests a shift in research priorities to understanding why older adults cannot use services that are currently available.

Finally, academic investigators have emphasized basic research on the distribution of climate change risks and large-scale studies of effects on health. Practitioners were less interested in basic research on the extent of the problem and large-scale health impacts associated with climate change (e.g., extreme heat). Instead, they strongly advocated for actionable knowledge that can be integrated into current educational and healthcare practice and used to inform policy decisions. Although recognizing that a basis of epidemiological and public health research is needed to understand the problem, their concerns focused on knowledge that will be useful in the real-world situations they encounter.

Limitations

The RPCW shares a limitation common to many consensus workshops: invited researchers and practitioners were primarily from one geographical region (New York State). However, drawing on locally and regionally accessible experts is standard practice in consensus workshops, as the resource demands of convening face-to-face conferences make geographically concentrated recruitment a practical necessity across contexts (McGlynn et al., 1990; McMillan et al., 2016). However, to enhance the generalizability of the findings, we purposely recruited six participants from outside New York State, including representatives from a federal public health agency and nationally oriented research and advocacy organizations, all of whom brought explicitly national perspectives to the discussions.

Further, the scope of a research agenda is determined more by the nature of the questions addressed than by the geographic residence of participants. Examining the 14 priorities makes clear that none is specific to New York State or any regional context; rather, they reflect national and international knowledge gaps, consistent with recent reviews in the field (Montoro-Ramírez et al., 2025; Patrick et al., 2025; Prina et al., 2024). Nonetheless, additional RPCWs in other geographic, demographic, and national contexts are needed to extend and confirm these findings.

Second, although the workshop included practitioners who work directly with older adults and policymakers who represent their interests, the absence of older adults’ own voices is a meaningful constraint. This limitation is especially important given the CBPR principles guiding the model. However, a mechanism for including older adults presented a distinct challenge for the workshop. Unlike populations defined by a shared experience, such as patients with a specific medical condition, older adults are an extraordinarily diverse group, and no small sample could adequately represent this diversity. We therefore recommend that future RPCWs on this topic be paired with complementary methods such as focus groups or community advisory mechanisms designed specifically to capture older adult perspectives.

Despite these limitations, this RPCW can inform the study of climate change and the older population. As an emerging and rapidly growing field, the agenda proposed here identifies ways to merge scientific knowledge with real-world impact. Practice-based research recommendations differ from current research emphases in several ways. In particular, they suggest that researchers move beyond repetitively affirming the greater risk of older people to climate change, which has been the subject of much demographic and public health epidemiology research, and move toward informing the development of economic models, scalable interventions, screening tools, and similar initiatives. It is to be hoped that robust partnerships among researchers, practitioners, and policymakers can be created to move these research priorities forward.
