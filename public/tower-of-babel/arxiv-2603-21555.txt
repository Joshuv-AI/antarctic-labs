On the series expansion of the secondary zeta function about s = 1 s=1 and its coefficients
Artur Kawalec

Abstract
The secondary zeta function is defined as a generalized zeta series over the imaginary parts of non-trivial zeros assuming (RH). This function admits Laurent series expansion at the double pole at s = 1 s=1 . In this article, we derive a new formula for the expansion coefficients of the regular part, which is similar to the Stieltjes constants formula for the Riemann zeta function. We also numerically verify and compute the new formula to high precision for several test cases. Lastly, we also apply the Brent’s (BPT) Theorem for improving convergence of the main formula.



1
Introduction

Let
\gamma_{n}
denote the positive imaginary parts of non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function, written as
\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}+i\gamma_{n}
on the critical line assuming (RH), where the first few values are
\gamma_{1}=14.13472514\ldots
,
\gamma_{2}=21.02203963\ldots
,
\gamma_{3}=25.01085758\ldots
, and so on. The secondary zeta function is defined as a generalized zeta series over these imaginary parts of non-trivial zeros as

Z(s)=\sum_{\gamma}\frac{1}{\gamma^{s}}

(1)

which converges absolutely for
\Re(s)>1
. The secondary zeta function extends to
\mathbb{C}\backslash\{1,-1,-3,-5,\ldots\}
with a double pole at
s=1
and simple poles at negative odd integers. The Laurent series expansion about
s=1
is given by

Z(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi(s-1)^{2}}-\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2\pi(s-1)}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}\frac{(s-1)^{n}}{n!}

(2)

as derived by Ivi
\acute{c}
[14][6]. This series has radius of convergence
R=2
due to being limited by the next closest pole at
s=-1
from the center at
s=1
. In this article, we derive a new formula for these expansion coefficients as

Theorem 1
.

C_{n}=\lim_{T\to\infty}(-1)^{n}\Bigg\{\sum_{\gamma<T}\frac{\log^{n}(\gamma)}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2\pi(n+1)(n+2)}\log^{n+1}(T)\log\left(\frac{T^{n+1}}{(2\pi)^{n+2}}\right)\Bigg\}

(3)

for
n\geq 0
.

We first review some relevant sources on this subject in the References Section. Chakravarty first wrote down the principal part of (2), and coined the term secondary zeta function [7][8][9]. And Ivi
\acute{c}
derived the main Laurent series expansion with regular part in (2). Hassani in [12][13] proved the limit (3) for the
n=0
case, and who also first computed the constant
C_{0}
to
5
decimal places. More developments by Brent (BPT) [3][4][5], who developed better error bounds for the sums over non-trivial zeros, and who improved computation of Hassani constant
C_{0}
to
19
decimal places [4, p.64]. A key development by Arias De Reyna [2][15] is the (ADR) algorithm for high precision computation of
Z(s)
for any complex
s
, which we used to compute
C_{n}
to high precision in [16] and published a reference table of
C_{n}
coefficients to
50
accurate digits (see also Table
1
below for
C_{n}
coefficients). Also in [17], we published an unprocessed list of over
100
coefficients, which took over
1
month to compute on a fast workstation. We believe that many of these coefficients should be accurate to more than
100
digits after the decimal place, but more analysis is still needed.

In this article, based on these results, we derive a general limit formula (3) for
C_{n}
for
n\geq 0
and connect it to the series expansion (2).

2
Proof of Theorem 1

To show that, let us recap some basic definitions. The non-trivial zero counting function up to height
T
(assuming
T\neq\gamma
) is defined by

N(T)=\sum_{0<\gamma<T}1

(4)

assuming (RH) and the Riemann-von Mangoldt asymptotic formula is

N(T)=L(T)+Q(T)

(5)

where the dominant term is

L(T)=\frac{7}{8}+\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\left(\frac{T}{2\pi}\right)-\frac{T}{2\pi}

(6)

and the lower order term

Q(T)=S(T)+f(T)

(7)

where

S(T)=\frac{1}{\pi}\arg\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+iT)

(8)

defined as in [14]. One also has the bounds

S(T)=O(\log T),\quad f(T)=O(\frac{1}{T}),\quad Q(T)=O(\log T).

(9)

Also, the
m^{th}
derivatives of (1) are given by

(-1)^{m}Z^{(m)}(s)=\sum_{\gamma}\frac{\log^{m}(\gamma)}{\gamma^{s}}

(10)

for
\Re(s)>1
. So we proceed by modifying the main derivation by Brent (BPT) [4, p.61] by inserting
\log^{m}(t)
factor (setting
s=1
), hence we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma<T}\frac{\log^{m}(\gamma)}{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\log^{m}(t)}{t}dN(t)=\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\log^{m}(t)}{t}dL(t)+\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\log^{m}(t)}{t}dQ(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\log^{m}(t)\log(t/2\pi)}{t}\,dt+\left[\frac{\log^{m}(t)}{t}Q(t)\right]_{1}^{T}-\int_{1}^{T}\frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{\log^{m}(t)}{t}\right]Q(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=A(T)+\frac{\log^{m}(T)}{T}Q(T)+B_{m}+\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\log^{m}(t)-m\log^{m-1}(t)}{t^{2}}Q(t)\,dt

(11)

Here we note there is a subtle detail concerning the lower integration variable constant
B_{m}
defined by

B_{m}=\begin{cases}-Q(1)&\text{if }m=0\\
0&\text{if }m>0\end{cases}

(12)

which only appears for
m=0
case. The reason is that it is assumed the convention that
0^{0}=1
, which implies
[\log(1)]^{0}=1
, thus yielding a nonzero term, otherwise for
m>1
, we have
[\log(1)]^{m}=0^{m}=0
. Next, following this result, this integral identity can be generated as

\displaystyle A(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\log^{m}(t)\log(t/2\pi)}{t}\,dt

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi(m+1)(m+2)}\log^{m+1}(T)\log\left(\frac{T^{m+1}}{(2\pi)^{m+2}}\right)

by a repeated integration by parts for
m\geq 0
. Then, it is readily seen that the limit

\sum_{\gamma<T}\frac{\log^{m}(\gamma)}{\gamma^{s}}-A(T)=(-1)^{m}C_{m}+O\left(\frac{\log^{m+1}(T)}{T}\right)

(14)

exists as
T\to\infty
and converges to a constant

(-1)^{m}C_{m}=B_{m}+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{m}(t)-m\log^{m-1}(t)}{t^{2}}Q(t)\,dt

(15)

for
m\geq 0
, since
Q(T)=\log(T)
. Now the next step is to connect this constant to Laurent Series expansion (2). Ivi
\acute{c}
in [14] essentially derived this result, so we reproduce it as follows:

\displaystyle Z(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{s}}\,dN(t)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{s}}\Biggl[\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\left(\frac{t}{2\pi}\right)\Biggr]dt\,+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{s}}dQ(t)

(16)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Biggl[t^{1-s}\log\left(\frac{t}{2\pi}\right)\Biggr]_{1}^{\infty}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{1-s}}{1-s}\cdot\frac{1}{t}\,dt\,+

\displaystyle+\Biggl[\frac{1}{t^{s}}Q(t)\Biggr]_{1}^{\infty}+s\int_{1}^{\infty}Q(t)t^{-s-1}\,dt

by Stieltjes integration, which results in

Z(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi(s-1)^{2}}-\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2\pi(s-1)}+B_{0}+s\int_{1}^{\infty}Q(t)t^{-s-1}\,dt

(17)

where
B_{0}=-Q(1)
as in (12). Since the integral in (17) converges for
\Re(s)>0
, it analytically continues
Z(s)
in (17) to
\Re(s)>0
. It now remains to show that this integral admits the series expansion about
s=1
. This analysis is carried out by Bondarenko-Ivi
\acute{c}
-Saksman-Seip in [6, p.4-5] so we reproduce it here. We first consider an integral

\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-s-1}Q(t)\,dt

(18)

which converges for
\Re(s)>0
assuming (RH). One then generates the exp-log expansion as

\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-2}e^{-(s-1)\log t}Q(t)\,dt=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}\frac{(s-1)^{j}}{j!}

(19)

where its expansion coefficients can be read off as

c_{j}=(-1)^{j}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{j}(t)}{t^{2}}Q(t)\,dt

(20)

but such a series is only limited to
|s-1|<1
domain. However, on writing integral term in (17) in
s-1
domain, then one needs to consider this form instead

\displaystyle s\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-s-1}Qt)\,dt

\displaystyle=(s-1)\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-s-1}Q(t)\,dt+\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-s-1}Qt)\,dt

(21)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}[(s-1)t^{-s-1}Q(t)+t^{-s-1}Q(t)]\,dt

where we can generate a new series expansion as

\displaystyle s\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-s-1}Q(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)c_{j}\frac{(s-1)^{j+1}}{(j+1)!}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}\frac{(s-1)^{j}}{j!}

(22)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}mc_{m-1}\frac{(s-1)^{m}}{m!}+c_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}c_{m}\frac{(s-1)^{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle=c_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left[mc_{m-1}+c_{m}\right]\frac{(s-1)^{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}y_{j}\frac{(s-1)^{j}}{j!}

by combining the expansions in (21). Here we’ve shifted index variable
m=j+1
in first series in (22) and re-labeled
m=j
in second series in (22). The new expansion coefficients are then

y_{m}=\begin{cases}c_{0}&\text{for }m=0\\
mc_{m-1}+c_{m}&\text{for }m\geq 1\end{cases}

(23)

where they are expressed by
c_{n}
coefficients, and by (20) these coefficients can be expressed by the integral

y_{m}=(-1)^{m}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{m}(t)-m\log^{m-1}(t)}{t^{2}}Q(t)\,dt

(24)

which matches the series in (15). And we note that for
m=0
case we simplify (24) to get

y_{0}=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{Q(t)}{t^{2}}\,dt

(25)

whereby adding the constant
B_{0}
from (12) (and as it appears in (17)), we get the formula

C_{0}=-Q(1)+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{Q(t)}{t^{2}}\,dt

(26)

And for the
m\geq 1
case we have
C_{m}=y_{m}
. Also, we note the value of this constant

\displaystyle-Q(1)

\displaystyle=L(1)

(27)

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{8}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\right)-\frac{1}{2\pi}

\displaystyle=0.42333783699382573900\ldots

Hence this completes the proof.

3
Numerical Computation

In this section we explore numerical computation of this formula. To check the
n=0
case, the formula (3) simplifies to

C_{0}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\Bigg\{\sum_{\gamma<T}\frac{1}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\log(T)\log\left(\frac{T}{4\pi^{2}}\right)\Bigg\}.

(28)

This limit formula was first studied by Hassani [12][13] who first computed it accurately to
5
decimal places. Hassani used a small number of non-trivial zeros and applied other approximation methods, such as averaging and curve fitting, to extract a few more digits. Later, Brent (BPT) computed this constant to
19
decimal places [4, p. 64], using a database of
10^{10}
non-trivial zeros together with (BPT) method, a new technique to improve convergence of sums over non-trivial zeros (Theorem 2 in [3]). And in our previous article [16], with our best computation effort we computed

\displaystyle C_{0}=

\displaystyle 0.25163675131270596653346632934264537551475958738

(29)

\displaystyle 3654550533059356530585960570182311791574050852516

\displaystyle 937760994148142\ldots

to
111
decimal places using the Arias De Reyna algorithm [2]. But our result was offset by
\log^{2}(2\pi)/(4\pi)
factor. And in Table
1
, we computed
C_{n}
to
50
digits for
0\leq n\leq 50
. We reproduce Table 1 here for reference.

So to verify equation (3) directly, we have a database of
2
million non-trivial zeros from which we can easily compute such sums over non-trivial roots. We have
N(T)=2\times 10^{6}
with

T=1131944.47182486226849153321\ldots

(30)

So to test the
n=0
case, we directly compute the harmonic sum

\sum_{\gamma<T}\frac{1}{\gamma}=11.63680321239784824535\ldots

(31)

which results in calculation

C_{0}\approx 0.2516{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\underline{3}}729326778528275\ldots

(32)

which is accurate to
5
decimal places as it’s shown by last correct digit underlined in blue. We did not employ any additional approximation techniques, as our goal is to just compute equation (3) directly. And similarly for
n=1
case the formula yields

C_{1}=\lim_{T\to\infty}-\Bigg\{\sum_{\gamma<T}\frac{\log(\gamma)}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{12\pi}\log^{2}(T)\log\left(\frac{T^{2}}{8\pi^{3}}\right)\Bigg\}

(33)

we compute the sum

\sum_{\gamma<T}\frac{\log(\gamma)}{\gamma}=115.40475869669330537244\ldots

(34)

which results in computing the constant

C_{1}\approx-0.130{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\underline{0}}05204046642585705\ldots

(35)

which is accurate to
4
decimal places as shown by last correct digit underlined in blue, as compared in the reference Table
1
. And similarly for
n=2
we have

C_{2}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\Bigg\{\sum_{\gamma<T}\frac{\log^{2}(\gamma)}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{24\pi}\log^{3}(T)\log\left(\frac{T^{3}}{16\pi^{4}}\right)\Bigg\}

(36)

and we compute the sum

\sum_{\gamma<T}\frac{\log^{2}(\gamma)}{\gamma}=1238.24307097390760286254\ldots

(37)

and the constant

C_{2}\approx 0.08{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\underline{2}}52679757366788286\ldots

(38)

which matches to
3
decimal places.

We note that as the accuracy starts dropping, and so more non-trivial roots are needed in computation to increase the accuracy.

4
Application of (BPT) for improving error bound in (3)

In this Section, we will apply the work of Brent (BPT) Theorem to improve the error term in (14), which we rewrite as

\sum_{\gamma<T}\frac{\log^{m}(\gamma)}{\gamma^{s}}-A(T)=(-1)^{m}C_{m}+E(T)

(39)

where

E(T)=O\left(\frac{\log^{m+1}(T)}{T}\right)

(40)

as obtained earlier. This error is consistent with computations we did in Section
2
. Now applying (BPT) equations (1-3) [3, p.2] and taking
\phi(t)=\log^{m}(t)/t
, yields

E(T)=-\frac{\log^{m}(T)}{T}\Bigg[N(T)-L(T)\Bigg]+E_{2}(T)

(41)

where the improved error term is

E_{2}(T)<2(A_{0}+A_{1}\log T)\bigg|\frac{m\log^{m-1}T}{T^{2}}-\frac{\log^{m}T}{T^{2}}\bigg|+(A_{1}+A_{2})\frac{\log^{m}T}{T^{2}}

(42)

where the constants are
A_{0}=2.067
,
A_{1}=0.059
and
A_{2}=0.007
. Hence

E_{2}(T)=O\left(\frac{\log^{m+1}(T)}{T^{2}}\right)

(43)

a nice improvement by
O(1/T)
factor from (40).

We summarize these results in the next Theorem:

Theorem 2
.

\displaystyle C_{n}=\lim_{T\to\infty}

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\Bigg\{\sum_{\gamma<T}\frac{\log^{n}(\gamma)}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2\pi(n+1)(n+2)}\log^{n+1}(T)\log\left(\frac{T^{n+1}}{(2\pi)^{n+2}}\right)+

(44)

\displaystyle-\frac{\log^{n}(T)}{T}\Bigg[N(T)-\frac{7}{8}-\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\left(\frac{T}{2\pi}\right)+\frac{T}{2\pi}\Bigg]+E_{2}(T)\Bigg\}

for
n\geq 0
.

If we apply this Theorem to the parameters from the previous Section
2
with
N=2\times 10^{6}
and
T
as in (27), we recompute

C_{0}\approx 0.25163675131{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\underline{2}}53386814\ldots

(45)

which is now accurate to
12
decimal places as it’s shown by last correct digit underlined in blue, and the computed error
E_{2}(T)<4.5614\times 10^{-12}
. And similarly, recomputing

C_{1}\approx 0.130044485{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\underline{9}}0966041896\ldots

(46)

which matches to
10
decimal places, and the computed error
E_{2}(T)<5.9073\times 10^{-11}
. And the next one is

C_{2}\approx 0.082421491{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\underline{2}}2637581623\ldots

(47)

which matches to
10
decimal places, and the computed error
E_{2}(T)<7.6058\times 10^{-10}
.

This demonstrates that by adding a simple extra term from (BPT), we can get a big improvement in convergence. We also numerically verified this formula for higher
n
, and it is valid as expected.
