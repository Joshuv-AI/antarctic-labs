INHAND: Non-proliferative and proliferative lesions of fish – Chapter 14. Fish special senses
Authors: Wes Baumgartner, Ute Bach, James Baily, Christine Ruehl-Fehlert, Stacey Fossey, Satoshi Furukawa, Yuki Kato, Victoria Laast, Emily Meseck, Heike Schmidt-Posthaus, Helmut Segner, Shiva Kumar Shanmukhappa, Jan Spitsbergen, Jeffrey Wolf
Published: 2026

Introduction

This document is organized to provide introductory material that reviews comparative interspecies differences in anatomy, followed by a list of microscopic findings in a standardized format. Throughout the document, comparisons are made with respect to similar lesions that may occur in laboratory rodents or humans. A high diversity of structural differences between species complicates the interpretation.

The special senses of teleost species include ocular (eye), otic (labyrinth), tactile (lateral line system), olfactory, and gustatory senses. There are notable similarities and differences between the teleost and vertebrate special senses that are discussed in literature1,2,3,4.

1. Eye

The teleost eye is morphologically and functionally similar to vertebrate eye, however there are some notable exceptions. One significant difference is the spherical shape of lens as opposed to lenticular shape in other higher vertebrates (Figs. 14.1 and 14.2Fig. 14.1.Diagrammatical vertical section of generalized teleost eye reprinted from Walls (1942)18. Some structures shown may not be present in some eyes. For example, either lentiform body and falciform process or hyaloid vessels are present18, 19.Fig. 14.2.Large Mouth Bass. Eye. Normal. H&E, 20×. Image courtesy of Heike Schmidt-Posthaus.). The teleost eye usually protrudes out and has a short focal length that results in larger field of view and greater depth of focus. Accommodation is accomplished by backward movement of the lens through the retractor lentis muscle5. The pupil of fish is immobile and there is no iris for protection against excess rays of light6. At the iridocorneal angle, zebrafish have an annular ligament that is not analogous to the trabecular meshwork in mammals; anatomical differences in the eyes of fish compared to those in mammals have been described in detail elsewhere7,8,9.

Cellular organization of the retina is also similar to the mammalian retinas. As in all vertebrates, there are differences in rod, cone, and rhodopsin composition based on the visual environment of the fish, like the depth at which they normally reside; this may further vary seasonally or during migration4, 10. The zebrafish retina has remarkable regenerative capacities. The Müller glial cells of zebrafish retinas are able to differentiate into different retinal cell types in response to injury11, 12. Many ocular adnexa present in mammals are absent in fish, including eyelid, nasolacrimal duct, vomeronasal organ, septal organ of Masera, Meibomian gland, Harderian gland, and intra-or extraorbital lacrimal glands. The vasculature of fish is specially adapted to support visual acuity and vascular needs of the retina.

The retina of fish is generally thicker than that of mammals (Fig. 14.3Fig. 14.3.Large Mouth Bass. Eye. Normal. H&E. Image courtesy of Heike Schmidt-Posthaus.). Most teleost retinae are avascular13. This varies by species; notably the retinae of zebrafish and rainbow trout have superficial hyaloid or vitreal derived vasculature14, 15. Three ocular vascular structures not present in mammals supply oxygen to the retina in fish: the choroid rete mirabile (choroid body), the lentiform body (also a rete), and the falciform process16. The choroid rete mirabile is a large horseshoe-shaped, glandlike structure located around the optic nerve in the choroid layer of the eye of many species of fishes. This rete consists of closely packed and parallel arterial and venous capillaries with a countercurrent flow of blood. The lentiform body is a smaller, globular rete. The falciform process is a vascularized projection extending into the vitreous. The presence or absence of these structures varies by species17. There is a tapetum in the eye of certain species of fish.

Large Mouth Bass. Eye. Normal. H&E. Image courtesy of Heike Schmidt-Posthaus.

2. Olfactory, tactile, otic, electric, gustatory organs

The olfactory organ is located on the snout as a paired organ with two openings, the anterior and posterior, which are separated by a bridge of skin (Fig. 14.4Fig. 14.4.Zebrafish. Olfactory organ. Normal. Decalcified section. H&E, 100×. Image courtesy of Jeffrey Wolf.). Olfactory epithelium is composed of ciliated columnar epithelium that lines many finger-like projections over the fibrovascular stroma4. These paired peripheral olfactory organs, or rosettes, located in the nasal cavity connect to the olfactory bulbs, which constitute the rostral-most forebrain region. The sensory epithelium consists of olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs) that extend axonal projections to the olfactory bulbs, forming a fasciculated connecting fiber known as the olfactory nerve. OSN axons reaching the olfactory bulbs form discrete structures known as glomeruli, where they form synapses with bulbar mitral cells. These neurons extend axons to the forebrain in bundles known as olfactory tracts, where they relay signals to telencephalic olfactory-processing areas consisting of: the posterior zone of the dorsal telencephalon; the ventral nucleus of the central telencephalon; the posterior tuberculum; and the right habenula20.

Zebrafish. Olfactory organ. Normal. Decalcified section. H&E, 100×. Image courtesy of Jeffrey Wolf.

Functional and structural organization of the olfactory system, as well as olfactory transduction mechanisms, have remained evolutionarily conserved in vertebrates. The zebrafish (Danio rerio) has emerged as a valuable and relevant model for studying human physiology, disease, and toxicity. Olfactory organs are directly exposed to the milieu and thus are particularly vulnerable to damage by environmental pollutants and toxicants, such as heavy metals, pesticides, and surfactants, among others20.

Octavolateralis (acousticolateralis) system: This system is composed of the lateral line system and inner ear (statoacoustic organ) of fish. The lateral line system detects particle velocities and accelerations with frequencies below 100 Hz while the auditory system detects pressure fluctuations with frequencies above 100 Hz21, 22. In addition to the auditory and vestibular system, fish have mechanosensory and electrosensory systems in the skin23, 24.

Free neuromasts/lateral line: The mechanosensory system in bony fishes is composed of neuromasts arranged freely in the epidermis, or alternatively in lateral line canals that occur prominently on the head, trunk and caudal peduncle (Fig. 14.5Fig. 14.5.Catfish. Lateral line. Normal. Decalcified section. H&E, 100×.)25,26,27,28. Neuromasts are discrete clusters of sensory hair cells and supporting cells with an apical, gelatinous cupula that extends into the water; hair cells resemble those in mammalian inner ears. Kinocilia and stereocilia in each sensory cell are directionally polarized and embedded in the cupula, allowing the detection of fine change in fluid movement29, 30.

Catfish. Lateral line. Normal. Decalcified section. H&E, 100×.

Embryonic neuromasts are distributed over the body in specific patterns within the epidermis. Formation of the adult pattern occurs in two processes; superficial neuromasts form rows via budding (stitches) while remaining neuromasts become incorporated into epithelium-lined canals in the dermis (lateral line system)31, 32. The lateral line primordium is a cluster of cells that migrates along species-specific routes in the skin during development, depositing clusters of cells at regular intervals, each of which will develop into neuromasts33. Subsequently, the canal system forms as ridges of intramembranous ossification which envelop existing neuromasts, fusing into a tubular system interrupted by epithelial pores and tubed scales34,35,36. The lateral line canals are enveloped in bone, whether from the scale or otherwise (see comment in ‘scale’ in the skin section), and their formation is dependent on the lateral line cells35. The canal neuromasts are similar to free neuromasts, but have distinctive cell biology features, including continuous growth during development29,32. Labyrinth organ/statoacoustic organ: The labyrinth organ is composed of an otolith organ and three fibrous semilunar tubes that form a complex sensory organ associated with equilibrium (vestibular) and hearing (auditory). The otolith organ is composed of three interconnecting sections: utriculus, sacculus and lagena. In most teleosts, the utricle primarily functions as a vestibular organ, the saccule as an auditory organ, and the lagena in both the auditory and vestibular systems40,41,42. These chambers are filled with endolymph, contain otoliths and are lined by sensory cells in an area referred to as the macula, that is similar to lateral line neuromasts. The macula also contains non-sensory supporting cells interspersed between sensory hair cells. Hair cells project into the lumen via ciliary bindles with one kinocilium and multiple stereocilia to contact the otolith43,44,45. Three semilunar tubes are also similarly filled by endolymph and insert into the labyrinth cavity and utriculus. The labyrinth cavity also contains sensory hair cells that are similar to lateral line neuromasts. In some ostariophysian fish, these tubes are attached from their posterior end to Weberian ossicles. The Weberian apparatus consists of four small bones and connects the swim bladder to the labyrinth organ. It is the analog to the mammalian middle ear acting as an accessory hearing structure transmitting vibrations from swim bladder to inner ear42,46-48. This transmission of sound is well developed in the fathead minnow49.

There are two types of teleost hair cells, Type I and Type II. Type I hair cells contain both afferent and efferent innervation while Type II hair cells contain afferent innervation and are located extrastriatally50,51,52. Hair cells of teleost maculae are found as patches of fairly equally spaced hair cells surrounded by supporting cells in contrast to the precise rows in mammalian cochlea53.

Electrical sensory system

Some species of fish have an additional sensory modality related to electric currents, with electrogenesis (production) and electroreception elements23. They are best known in the elasmobranchs, but are present in eels, catfishes, and knifefishes54, 55.

Gustatory system

Taste buds/solitary chemosensory cells: In addition to their presence in the mouth, taste buds, paraneurons (solitary chemosensory cells & Merkel cells), and nerves occur widely in the skin1, 56,57,58,59,60,61,62. Merkel cells and other paraneuron cell types are associated with the nerves in the epidermis, and produce serotonin, among other substances58. Epidermal sensory axons in the zebrafish are invested by keratinocytes and not a basal lamina63.

Necropsy and Trimming

The histological preparation of special sense organs is in many ways comparable to methods used for mammals. The eyes of large fish, in which the heads cannot fit into standard tissue cassettes, are often excised in toto, accompanied by a small portion of the optic nerve if possible. Because in certain species the sclera may contain bony plates, the eyes may have to be decalcified prior to routine processing. For small aquarium species, the eyes may be examined in whole head or whole-body sections. If the eye is known to be a target tissue, or if ocular lesions are identified macroscopically, transverse sections through the head are often preferred, because they provide a superior viewpoint for assessing ocular structures and allow for bilateral comparisons within the same section. Otherwise, the eyes may be evaluated individually in parasagittal whole-body sections. As in mammals, initial fixation of eyes in modified Davidson’s, Bouin’s solutions or zinc formalin may provide superior results when compared to neutral buffered formalin.

The olfactory and statoacoustic organs are also demonstrable in parasagittal sections, albeit at different distances from the midline, and preparation of these structures for histopathological examination usually requires decalcification. In most teleosts used in toxicologic studies, the lateral line is visible macroscopically and is best visualized in transverse sections through the lateral body musculature. It is not readily evident in sagittal/parasagittal sections, and special muscle sections containing the lateral line are not collected routinely; therefore, this structure is not examined in most toxicology studies. When there is a macroscopic lesion, care should be taken to collect and section the tissue so that the finding is optimally available for examination.

Morphology, Physiology, Nomenclature, Diagnostic Criteria, and Differential Diagnosis

1. Eye

Morphologically evident ocular findings related to treatment are generally rare in fish toxicological studies. Background findings tend to be more common, such as traumatic lesions related to confinement, manifestations of infectious disease, hereditary or nutritional cataract formation, or neoplasms in aged individuals. Exceptions include, for example, corneal erosion or edema in response to ocular irritation in fish exposed to caustic chemicals by water bath, retinal apoptosis in juvenile fish caused by radiation treatment, and questionable claims of dichlorvos as a cause of cataract development in farmed Atlantic salmon64.

The nomenclature associated with neoplastic findings in fish eye (and nervous system) in the literature has been inconsistent and insufficient information is available for the authors to be didactic in this publication. Therefore, in some instances, the same term is listed as other term, to indicate that published literature may use any of these terms, as well as a differential diagnosis, with information to assist with differentiation, reflecting the authors’ professional experience.

Eye – Congenital Lesions

Congenital lesions of the eye are uncommon and may be described as “anatomical region, malformation”. Cysts and developmental anomalies may potentially be present but have not been reported in literature. Lesions may be reported following INHAND guidance for non-piscine species.

Persistent hyaloid vessels, vitreous, eye

Comments

Hyaloid vasculature morphogenesis in zebrafish has been described (Hartsock, 2014). Hyaloid vasculature networks in the vhl−/− zebrafish model of Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) syndrome are improperly formed, and this phenotype is concomitant with development of an ectopic intraretinal vasculature65. Sunitinib malate (a multi tyrosinase kinase inhibitor market-authorized for cancer treatment) reversed the ocular, behavioral and morphological phenotypes observed in vhl−/− zebrafish.

Retinal rosettes retina, eye

Other Terms

• Retinal dysplasia; linear retinopathy; retinochoroidal degeneration; retinal dystrophy; retinal anomaly

Pathogenesis

• Usually related to anomaly in cell signaling associated with cell differentiation, migration during development66

• Non-specific response to diverse stimuli that affects retinal cell differentiation during development

Diagnostic Features

• Focal to multifocal rosette-like and tubular structures expanding and distorting the inner and outer nuclear layers

• Rosette-like and tubular structures can present variable morphology with a combination of the following features:

○ One to multiple layers of cells with nuclear profiles similar to neurons of the inner and outer nuclear layers

○ Nuclei polarized away from the center or “lumen” of the rosette

○ Eosinophilic linear structures resembling photoreceptors inner segments and/or ciliated cells from primitive neuroepithelial origin may be observed in the inner layer of the rosette

○ Presence of a basement membrane-like structure near the center of the rosette resembling the external limiting membrane of the retina

○ RPE-like cells or macrophages may be in the center of the rosette

Differential Diagnosis

• Retinal Folds: Inwards projection of retina usually without the central tubular structure

• Processing artifact: Suboptimally preserved, collected, embedded, and/or sectioned eyes can have distorted structural features

Comments

Retinal rosettes are a focal or multifocal disorganization of the sensory retina due to faulty development. Rosettes have been reported in zebrafish with N-cadherin loss-of-function mutant parachutepaR2.10 (pacpaR2.10)67. Mutant retinae display a severe lamination defect with rosette formation (mostly islands of plexiform layer tissue surrounded by inner nuclear layer or photoreceptor cells). C(2)H(2) zinc finger proteins Nlz1 and Nlz2 function in normal fissure closure. Gene knockdown of C(2)H(2) zinc finger proteins Nlz1 and/or Nlz2 in zebrafish leads to a failure of the optic fissure to close, a phenotype which closely resembles that seen in human uveal coloboma66.

Pigment eye: Pigments in fish are more complex compared to mammals. Alterations in pigment is defined as a fish having a different color than as observed in controls.

Pigment, alteration, eye

Modifiers/Locators

Corneal, Conjunctive, Iris, Retinal Pigmented Epithelium

Other Terms

Discoloration

Pathogenesis/Cell of Origin:

• Endogenous chromatophore alteration or pigment deposition or loss

• Change in number or size of chromatophores within the stroma or the quantity of pigment granules within the chromatophores

Diagnostic Features

• Variably sized, shaped and colored aggregates of material, increased, decreased or altered compared with concurrent or historical controls or normal animals

• Located in cytoplasm or extracellular

• Increased numbers or size of chromatophores in the affected eye structure or component (e.g. iris stroma)

• May be composed of intracellular or extracellular punctate, granular or amorphous material deposited within the cells, stroma or epithelium of the eye or its component structures (e.g. cornea or conjunctiva)

• Alterations in pigment, including increase or decrease may result from alteration in number of chromatophores within the tissue, or alteration in the amount of pigment within the affected cell populations or resident chromatophores

Differential Diagnosis

Cornea, conjunctiva, uvea (ciliary body, iris) choroid

• Mineralization

• Hemosiderin pigment accumulation following hemorrhage.

Retinal pigmented epithelium

• RPE hypopigmentation: Failure to produce or insufficient production of melanin; associated with genetic abnormalities of pigmentation

• Melanin granules are present but poorly pigmented

• Relative decrease in pigmentation associated with RPE hypertrophy

• Other forms of RPE degeneration

• Inclusions, RPE

• RPE or melanotic neoplasia

Comments

Pigment may be endogenous (e.g. melanin), occur from hemorrhage (hemosiderin, hemoglobin), or derive from exogenous sources (e.g. foreign body). In fish, pigments are largely contained within cells called chromatophores. In some fishes, chromatophores derived from neural crest are responsible for generating skin and eye color68. Colors produced by chromatophores derive from melanin, pteridines, carotenoids, and purines, among others.

Increased pigmentation associated with an increase in pigment granules can result from chronic irritation or trauma. Pigment granules can be found intracellularly within intraepithelial melanocytes (which may increase in number following injury) or corneal epithelial cells (thought to acquire melanin from melanocytes), or extracellular deposits.

See Skin chapter for expanded discussion of chromatophores.

Comments

‘Degeneration’ may be used as an aggregate finding to include many of the nonspecific changes observed in the lens fibers. In advanced examples, the cortical fibers are completely liquefied and lost, and the nucleus of the lens is free-floating in the lens capsule. Cataract is a clinical diagnostic term used to describe lens opacities and should not be used as a microscopic finding.

Lens fiber degeneration is common in Atlantic Salmon due to sub-optimal environmental conditions and nutritional deficiencies while zebrafish seems to be resistant to lens fiber degeneration69. Cataracts and traumatic lesions were reported in aquarium kept Monodactylus argenteus70. In that investigation, dietary amino acid content and aqueous humor osmolality imbalances were not a likely cause of the cataracts. However, differences in lens amino acid concentrations between affected and control fish may be related to cataract formation70.

Atrophy, retina, eye – Similar to rodents

Modifier/Locator

Inner retina, outer retina, global or diffuse

Comments

Atrophy is the result of a degenerative process of the retina resulting in cell loss and collapse of the retinal layers. Atrophy may be specific to the ganglion cell layer and/or inner nuclear layer, or outer retinal layer due to the susceptibility and sensitivity of the cell populations in those areas to the inciting cause, or to both (global). Global atrophy can also be an end-stage lesion associated with secondary degeneration of retinal cells that progress from one cell layer to the next. Retinal atrophy may be further defined by specifying the cell layer affected.

Retinal atrophy in fish may be due to variety of causes including high intensity light in Atlantic salmon, Atlantic cod and European sea bass71. Mutant zebrafish are useful models for investigation of the pathogenesis of retinal degeneration and regeneration72.

Atrophy of the inner retina (inner nuclear, inner plexiform and ganglion cell layers) may be secondary to increased intraocular pressure, compressive lesions of the optic nerve, or direct toxicity resulting in degeneration of retinal cells and cell loss.

Outer retinal atrophy or degeneration is a broad term that indicates loss of photoreceptors. Conditions resulting in atrophy of the outer retinal layers (outer plexiform and photoreceptor layers) include hereditary or direct photoreceptor injury, and/or RPE toxicity. Injury may arise from a variety of causes that include aging, toxicity, phototoxicity, nutritional deficiencies, retinal detachment, and inflammation.

Global retinal atrophy is an end stage retinal lesion with involvement of all cell layers, regardless of the initial cause. It is characterized by a complete collapse of retinal architecture and replacement of the retinal tissue by a fibrous layer with glial cells and occasional neurons.

Octavolateralis/Lateral Line System – Proliferative Neoplastic

Nerve sheath tumors (B/M), lateral line

Other Terms

• Schwannoma

• Neurilemmoma

• Neurinoma

• Peripheral nerve sheath tumor (PNST)

Diagnostic Features

• Neoplastic spindle cells are arranged in a storiform pattern with bundles, palisades, and whorls; the hallmark of PNSTs in humans is the classic Antoni A pattern with Verocay bodies; this is rare in tumors of domestic species

• Antoni A pattern is characterized by elongate cells arranged in short interlacing streams and bundles in areas of moderate to high cellularity within a scant eosinophilic matrix

• Antoni B pattern is characterized by elongate cells arranged in a loose meshwork in less densely cellular areas within abundant myxomatous matrix

• Verocay bodies are characterized by stacked parallel rows of elongate cells with palisading nuclei (nuclear regimentation) and anuclear eosinophilic fibrillar material

Differential Diagnosis

• Fibroma/Fibrosarcoma: Less cellular, lack palisading and whorling

• Neurofibromatosis: Described in Bicolor Damselfish with interest as potential model for neurofibromatosis 1 in humans. Grossly, tumors are multicentric and track along cutaneous nerves on the body and fins, as well as spinal nerves. Histologically tumors contain invasive proliferations of spindle cells in various patterns, consistently associated with peripheral nerves80

• Leiomyoma: Immunohistochemically positive for Desmin and negative for S-100

• Melanoma: Histologically, cells may contain melanin granules and contain melanosomes ultrastructurally

Comments

Goldfish and zebrafish are predisposed to develop nerve sheath neoplasia of peripheral and cranial nerves. Inactivating mutations in tumor suppressor genes (tp53, mlh1, msh2, msh6, and ribosomal genes) results in induction of PNSTs in zebrafish77. Epizootic sarcomas and nerve sheath tumors of the lateral line were reported in lake trout (Salvelinus namaycush)81.
