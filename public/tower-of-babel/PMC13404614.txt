Paternal parenting styles, smartphone addiction and the mediating role of self-efficacy in adolescents
Authors: Kaize Yang, Ranran Gong, Xinyun Lin, Daosen Wang, Yijin Wu, Xiuhong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough the global risks of adolescent smartphone addiction (SA) and the effects of parenting styles are widely recognised, research has predominantly focused on maternal or both parents’ roles. Paternal parenting styles and their potential mediating pathways for SA remain understudied. This study aimed to examine associations between paternal parenting styles and SA among Chinese adolescents as well as the potential mediating role of self-efficacy of mental addiction which refers to an individual’s confidence in their ability to resist, control or avoid engaging in potentially addictive behaviours, particularly SA.MethodsWe conducted a 1-year longitudinal study using stratified cluster random sampling to recruit 8825 adolescent students in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province of China. Data were collected through self-administered questionnaires in October 2022 and October 2023. The associations between paternal parenting styles and SA were analysed using generalised estimating equation models. Then the mediation effects of self-efficacy of mental addiction between paternal parenting styles and SA were tested through bootstrapping analyses (5000 resamples). Finally, subgroup analysis tested the robustness of the results.ResultsPaternal rejection (OR=1.13, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.14) and overprotection (OR=1.09, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.10) were significantly associated with increased risk of SA, while paternal emotional warmth (OR=0.94, 95% CI 0.93 to 0.95) and self-efficacy of mental addiction (OR=0.70, 95% CI 0.69 to 0.71, all p<0.001) were significantly associated with decreased risk of SA with per point increase. Notably, self-efficacy of mental addiction mediated 62.5% (95% CI 42.1% to 100.0%), 48.9% (95% CI 35.5% to 70.0%) and 29.8% (95% CI 17.9% to 45.0%, all p<0.001) of the total effects of paternal emotional warmth, rejection and overprotection on the SA, respectively.ConclusionOur findings indicate paternal parenting styles are independently associated with SA in Chinese adolescents. Self-efficacy of mental addiction mediated the associations.

Introduction

Smartphone addiction (SA) has become a growing global public health concern among adolescents. SA is generally defined as a pattern of excessive, compulsive and uncontrolled smartphone use that leads to functional impairment in daily life, including disruptions to social relationships, academic performance and psychological well-being, often accompanied by withdrawal symptoms when access is restricted.1 A meta-analysis of 24 countries indicates that SA prevalence among adolescents ranges from 10% to 30%,2 with Chinese adolescents reporting rates of 28.3% and showing an upward trend in recent years.3 Beyond its high prevalence, SA poses significant risk to adolescent development, including myopia, impaired sleep quality, increased anxiety and depression, compromised academic performance and deteriorated social functioning.4 5 Besides, in studying the formation of SA, it has been well documented that both environmental factors and individual factors jointly contribute to behavioural addiction, including SA.6 In addition, parenting continues to shape and affect the behaviour of adolescents.7

Parenting style refers to the kind of physical and emotional care that parents provide to their children.8 With sociocultural changes in China, the traditional mother-dominated parenting model has shifted toward a more collaborative approach,9 with the importance of paternal parenting being increasingly emphasised.10 Although existing research has confirmed the correlation between parenting styles and SA, these studies have predominantly focused on maternal parenting or parents’ combined influence, with limited specific exploration of fathers’ unique roles as fathers increasingly assume more influential parenting roles in recent years.11–13 Among these styles, paternal rejection, paternal emotional warmth and paternal overprotection—defined within Rohner’s Parental Acceptance-Rejection Theory as the absence of, or reduced warmth, affection and care on the part of fathers—warrants particular attention.14

It is important to situate these findings within the cultural context of contemporary China, where paternal parenting roles have changed considerably over time. Traditionally, Chinese fatherhood followed the Confucian ‘strict father, kind mother’ model, in which fathers were mainly responsible for discipline and moral education, while emotional care was largely left to mothers.15 However, rapid urbanisation, rising educational pressures, the lasting effects of the one-child policy and growing exposure to Western parenting values have together driven a gradual shift toward more emotionally engaged fathering.16 Despite this shift, many Chinese fathers may still navigate a tension between authoritarian tendencies, such as rejection or overprotection, and expectations of emotional involvement, which may amplify the influence of paternal parenting on adolescent psychological outcomes, including SA.17 Accordingly, the present study employs a validated, culturally adapted measure of paternal parenting styles to ensure cross-cultural applicability in interpretation.

Furthermore, the mechanisms through which paternal parenting influences adolescent SA remain insufficiently understood. Self-efficacy, as originally conceptualised by Bandura, refers to an individual’s belief in their own capacity to execute the behaviours necessary to produce specific outcomes in a given situation.18 In the context of SA and other health-related behaviours among Chinese adolescents, researchers have further proposed the concept of adolescent health behaviour self-efficacy—defined as adolescents’ confidence in their ability to adopt beneficial behaviours or discontinue harmful ones.19 Specifically, self-efficacy of mental addiction, as a subtype of this construct, refers to an individual’s confidence in their capacity to resist or regulate addictive behaviours associated with mental addiction activities, such as excessive internet or smartphone use.20

According to Bandura’s social cognitive theory, paternal parenting styles may influence adolescent self-efficacy through four primary sources: mastery experiences, vicarious learning, verbal persuasion and physiological and emotional states.21 Paternal emotional warmth may support these processes by providing opportunities for autonomy, positive behavioural modelling, affirming feedback and emotional security. In contrast, paternal rejection and overprotection may constrain these pathways by limiting autonomy, providing maladaptive models, offering critical feedback and generating anxiety or emotional disconnection.18 Therefore, lower self-efficacy, as may be fostered by less supportive paternal parenting, may reduce adolescents’ capacity to regulate addictive behaviours, increasing their susceptibility to persistent SA in the context of paternal rejection, overprotection or other psychosocial stressors.

The present study therefore pursued two primary objectives: (1) to examine the associations between paternal parenting styles (specifically paternal rejection) and SA among Chinese adolescents; and (2) to investigate whether self-efficacy of mental addiction mediates this relationship. By examining the associations between paternal parenting and adolescent SA, this study may contribute to a more nuanced theoretical understanding of father-specific mechanisms in adolescent SA, and provide preliminary insights for the development of family-based intervention strategies targeting paternal parenting behaviours and adolescents’ self-efficacy of mental addiction.

Methods

Patient and public involvement

The participants aged 11–19 years were recruited using cluster sampling from selected schools in a district of Shenzhen, China. Data collection was conducted through web-based surveys via the Wenjuanxing platform (Changsha Haoxing Information Technology, Hunan, China). The instruction was to fill the surveys only once by the participant. Two waves of cross-sectional surveys were conducted in October 2022 and October 2023, covering 27 full-time junior high schools and three senior high schools in the district of Shenzhen, Guangdong Province. The survey procedure is shown in figure 1. To minimise reverse causality in the subgroup analysis, we restricted the sample to participants without SA. We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline to draft this manuscript,22 and the STROBE reporting checklist when editing, included in online supplemental file A.

Flow chart of the longitudinal survey.

Measures

Demographic characteristics

Participants provided information regarding their age, sex, school grade, family structure (status of siblings), parents’ marital status, paternal and maternal educational level. Paternal and maternal education level was classified as either ‘high school or below’ or ‘above high school’. Parental marriage was coded as married, divorced or single, with married as the reference group.

Smartphone addiction

SA was measured using the Chinese version of the Smartphone Addiction Scale-Short Version (SAS-SV).23 The SAS-SV is a 10-item self-report scale that assesses symptoms of SA, with items rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). The total score ranges from 10 to 60, with higher scores indicating greater levels of SA. The eighth item of the original SAS-SV was modified by replacing ‘Twitter or Facebook’ with ‘WeChat, micro-blog, or QQ’ to make it culturally relevant for Chinese adolescents. Based on previously established cut-off values, SA was defined as score ≥32.24 The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency in the current sample (Cronbach’s α=0.92).

Parenting styles

Parenting styles were assessed using the Short-Form Egna Minnen av Barndoms Uppfostra for Children-Chinese version.25 This self-report measure evaluates adolescents’ perceptions of their parents’ child-rearing behaviours across three dimensions: rejection, emotional warmth and overprotection. Each dimension is assessed separately for fathers and mothers, resulting in six subscales: paternal rejection (six items), paternal emotional warmth (seven items), paternal overprotection (eight items), maternal rejection (six items), maternal emotional warmth (seven items) and maternal overprotection (eight items). Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 4 (always), with higher scores indicating greater levels of the respective parenting style. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency in the current sample (Cronbach’s α=0.89).

Self-efficacy of mental addiction

Health-related behavioural self-efficacy was measured using the Adolescent Health-related Behavior Self-efficacy Scale.19 This scale assesses adolescents’ confidence in their ability to engage in health-promoting behaviours and resist health-risk behaviours. For this study, we focused on the subscales most relevant to SA: self-efficacy of mental addiction (seven items, eg, ‘When I’m bored, I can resist the urge to surf the internet’). Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 4 (always), with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy. The subscales demonstrated acceptable to good internal consistency in the current sample (Cronbach’s α=0.83).

Statistical analysis

Prior to formal analysis, data were examined for missingness, normality and outliers. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for the primary outcome was calculated using a null mixed-effects model, yielding an ICC of 0.015, indicating negligible between-school variance; a random-effects model was therefore not required. We analysed a two-wave longitudinal generalised estimating equation models (GEE) with repeated measures collected from each adolescent at two time points to examine the associations of specific paternal parenting styles with SA and self-efficacy of mental addiction with SA. In this step, three models were tested: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for demographic family characteristics) and Model 3 (building on Model 2, adjusted for maternal parenting styles). Next, we employed a mediation analysis to explore the potential mediating roles of self-efficacy of mental addiction and obtained the 5000 bootstrapping 95% CIs for these mediation tests.

In order to further verify the directionality of the correlation, we performed a subgroup analysis to test the robustness of the associations between paternal parenting styles, self-efficacy of mental addiction and SA among adolescents without SA at 2022. Given that this subgroup comprised a single follow-up observation per participant, the binary logistic regression model was used in place of GEE, with the same three-model adjustment sequence applied as in the primary analysis. To control for Type I error due to multiple testing, the Bonferroni correction was applied with a significance threshold of p<0.0167 (0.05/3) for all statistical tests.

We performed an additional sensitivity analysis to test the robustness of the associations between paternal parenting styles, self-efficacy of mental addiction and SA total scores as a continuous outcome.

Population characteristics

As presented in table 1, a total of 8825 students were included in this study, of whom 2358 (26.7%) reported SA behaviour. Students with SA tended to be older students (15–19 years: 25.9% vs 16.7%, p<0.001), female (50.8% vs 44.6%, p<0.001), high school students (25.4% vs 16.6%, p<0.001), siblings (84.9% vs 82.6%, p=0.009), having not married parents (5.6% vs 3.6%, p<0.001) and lower parental education levels (father: 73.1% vs 66.3%, p<0.001; mother: 73.3% vs 67.6%, p<0.001) compared with those without SA. Sensitivity analysis

As a sensitivity analysis, the SAS-SV total score was modelled as a continuous outcome using GEE with a Gaussian family and identity link, across the same three-model adjustment sequence as the primary analysis. As shown in online supplemental table S2, all associations remained statistically significant and directionally consistent with the primary binary findings: paternal rejection and overprotection were positively associated with SA total score, while paternal emotional warmth and self-efficacy of mental addiction were inversely associated, all p<0.001. These results support the robustness of the primary findings.

Discussion

In this longitudinal study of adolescents followed over a 1-year period, we investigated the relationship between paternal parenting styles and SA with maternal parenting styles controlled, as well as self-efficacy of mental addiction as a potential mediator.

Key findings

We examined paternal parenting styles—rejection, emotional warmth and overprotection—in relation to adolescent SA and tested the mediating role of self-efficacy mental-addiction. Higher paternal rejection and overprotection were associated with increased risk, whereas greater paternal emotional warmth and higher self-efficacy of mental addiction were associated with reduced risk. These associations remained significant after full adjustment. While self-efficacy of mental-addiction mediated 48.9%, 62.5% and 29.8% of the total effects for paternal rejection, emotional warmth and overprotection, respectively. Even after excluding the adolescents with SA at 2022, the association between paternal emotional warmth, paternal overprotection and SA, and the mediation effect of self-efficacy remained robust. Only adjusting for demographic, familial and maternal parenting styles confounders, the association between paternal rejection and SA would be not significant.

Comparison with prior studies and interpretations

Consistent with previous studies,26 our findings revealed that paternal emotional warmth was negatively associated with SA, whereas paternal rejection and overprotection were positively associated with SA. Paternal emotional warmth may buffer adolescents against SA by providing affective security and positive reinforcement, reducing the appeal of smartphone use as a means of emotional compensation. Conversely, paternal rejection may heighten adolescents’ negative emotional arousal, increasing their reliance on smartphones as a maladaptive coping strategy. Paternal overprotection elevates SA risk through a distinct mechanism: by inadvertently restricting adolescents’ opportunities for independent outdoor activities and peer interaction, overprotective fathers limit access to real-world stimulation and social engagement. This constraint on offline opportunities effectively channels adolescents’ need for stimulation, social connection and autonomy expression toward screen-based alternatives, creating a substitution pattern whereby smartphones become the primary outlet for meeting these developmental needs.27 Over time, repeated addiction on smartphones to fulfil these functions may gradually escalate into addictive use patterns, as the virtual environment offers readily accessible rewards with minimal barriers to engagement—a particularly potent risk factor for adolescents who lack adequate offline alternatives and may develop heightened psychological addiction on smartphone. SA has also been associated with tension in the parent–adolescent relationship; conflicts over smartphone use frequency and boundaries may interfere with communication quality and relational trust.28 This pattern may in turn reinforce SA, suggesting the value of longitudinal research to examine these reciprocal dynamics.

However, the present findings also diverge from previous studies in certain respects. Compared with previous studies, our observed prevalence of SA (26.7–28.1%, online supplemental table S1 in online supplemental file B) in 1-year period exceeds several regional estimates in China and approaches reported surveys in other different countries,29 but considering heterogeneity in measurement tools, sampling frames and time periods, comparisons should be interpreted cautiously. Notably, the behavioural profile associated with SA—including attentional difficulties, impulsivity and hyperactivity—closely resembles the symptom presentation of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, raising important concerns regarding potential misdiagnosis or symptom exacerbation in vulnerable adolescents.30

Our findings suggested that paternal parenting styles could independently influence SA.10 13 31 Even after adjusting for maternal parenting styles, most paternal parenting dimensions remained significantly associated with SA—with the exception of paternal rejection. This finding may be partially explained by the traditional Chinese cultural value of ‘strict father, warm mother’,32 whereby paternal rejection and emotional distance are considered normative and expected within Chinese family structures, distinguishing fathers’ roles from those of mothers. When maternal parenting is considered, the independent effect of paternal rejection on SA may be attenuated, as adolescents in this cultural context may be more responsive to maternal influence.

In addition, according to our mediating results, paternal rejection and overprotection emerged as risk factors that restrict or deprive adolescents’ self-efficacy of mental addiction, ultimately elevating the risk of SA.27 33 34 Some researchers have proposed that fathers play a unique role in shaping adolescents’ self-efficacy, which in turn is associated with SA risk, as lower self-efficacy has been identified as a predictor of SA.35 Adolescents with lower self-efficacy may show reduced self-regulatory capacity, less persistence and less effective coping strategies when facing temptations, potentially contributing to greater reliance on immediately reinforcing activities such as SA.36

In closing, our study conceptually integrates parenting research with a core cognitive determinant—self-efficacy—with particular attention to the often-neglected role of paternal parenting. In the digital age, parents are encouraged to take greater responsibility for managing adolescents’ SA, rather than defaulting to rejection or overprotection.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine longitudinal associations of three paternal parenting styles with SA while adjusting for maternal parenting styles, addressing a critical confound overlooked in prior research. The study employed a two-wave longitudinal design with a large, multischool student sample (N=8825), which enhances statistical power to detect meaningful effect sizes and strengthens causal inference relative to longitudinal approaches. The large sample size and diverse school recruitment also improve the generalisability and representativeness of findings across urban Chinese adolescent populations. The robustness of our findings was further supported by two additional analyses: a sensitivity analysis in which the SA outcome was remodelled as a continuous variable using a Gaussian GEE framework, yielding results consistent in direction and significance with the primary binary analyses; and a subgroup analysis restricted to adolescents free of SA at baseline, which allowed for a more direct examination of the prospective associations between paternal parenting styles and incident SA, thereby reducing the influence of reverse causation. This study also has the following limitations: (1) parenting styles were assessed exclusively through adolescent self-report, which may introduce recall bias, social desirability bias and other response biases that could affect measurement accuracy and validity. While adolescents’ perceptions of parenting serve as proximal psychological influences on behavioural outcomes including SA,37 future research using objective or multimethod measurement approaches (eg, observational coding, experience sampling methods) would help validate these findings and mitigate potential biases inherent to self-reported data. (2) The sample was drawn from a single district in Shenzhen, which may limit generalisability to broader populations. Notably, the majority of parents in this sample had relatively low educational attainment (high school or below: father 68.1%, mother 69.1%), which may have contributed to the higher prevalence of paternal rejection observed, given that lower parental education has been associated with reduced warmth and increased strictness in parenting.31 Future nationwide multicentre studies are warranted. (3) Self-efficacy of mental addiction only mediated partial effects between paternal parenting styles and SA. Future work should clarify the mechanisms linking parenting to SA and examine the other pathways. (4) The quantitative findings also point to directions for future research. Qualitative methods—such as interviews or focus groups with adolescents and their fathers—may help clarify how cultural norms, family dynamics and parenting practices interact to shape SA, and could inform the development of family-based interventions.

Implications

These findings have practical implications for SA prevention in adolescents. Interventions may benefit from incorporating paternal parenting components—specifically reducing rejection and overprotection while promoting emotional warmth—alongside strategies to support adolescents’ self-efficacy of mental addiction, which may represent a pathway linking paternal parenting to SA risk.

Conclusion

Different paternal parenting styles independently associated with SA risk. Paternal parenting styles affected adolescents’ SA through indirect effects of self-efficacy of mental addiction.
