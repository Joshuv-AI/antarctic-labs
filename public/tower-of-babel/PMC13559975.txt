Impacts of Global Cannabis Policy Changes on Substance Use: A Systematic Review of Quasi‐Experimental Studies
Authors: SARAH B WINDLE, PETER M SOCHA, JASLEEN ARNEJA, SAM HARPER, GENEVIEVE GORE, GENEVIÉVE GARIÉPY, EVA GRAHAM, ANDREA BENEDETTI, ARIJIT NANDI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Policy PointsThe liberalization of medical and recreational cannabis policies may increase cannabis initiation across age groups, with increased prevalence of cannabis use among young adults and adults and weaker effects among youth. Evidence also suggests increases in cannabis‐related health care utilization among adults.Alcohol use may decrease following medical legalization (suggesting use of cannabis in place of alcohol), but it may increase among young adults following recreational legalization (suggesting co‐consumption).Evidence suggests a potential decrease in tobacco use across age groups and cannabis policy changes, and most studies found no change or a decrease in opioid‐related outcomes, although evidence was less consistent for opioid‐related mortality specifically. ContextSubstantial changes in cannabis policies are occurring globally; however, the impacts of these reforms on cannabis use, as well as downstream effects on other substance use, remain unclear.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review to identify studies examining the impact of a cannabis policy change on substance use. We searched Embase, Medline, PsycINFO, the Web of Science Core Collection, and the Criminal Justice Database, as well as gray literature sources including the Social Science Research Network. The included studies employed a quasi‐experimental approach and were assessed for risk of bias using a modified version of ROBINS‐I to facilitate the assessment of these designs. The findings were synthesized using a descriptive, analytical approach.FindingsThe included reports (N = 176) were published between 1993 and 2024, with approximately two‐thirds published after 2019. Most were peer‐reviewed publications (n = 148) applying a difference‐in‐differences (n = 129) or interrupted time series (n = 42) approach to data from the United States (n = 141). Medical and recreational cannabis legalization may increase cannabis initiation across age groups, with increased prevalence of cannabis use among young adults and adults and inconsistent evidence among youth. The impacts on alcohol use were differential by policy and age group, with potential decreases following medical legalization and potential increases following recreational legalization among young adults. Evidence from a limited number of studies suggested potential decreases in tobacco use among all age groups. Most studies found no change or a decrease in opioid‐related outcomes, although evidence was less consistent for opioid‐related mortality. We found few studies examining cannabis decriminalization, with no clear evidence of changes in cannabis or other substance use.ConclusionsOverall, these findings suggest that medical and recreational legalization increase cannabis use, with weaker effects among youth. In addition, potential substitution effects were identified for other substances (e.g., alcohol, tobacco, opioids), excepting complementary alcohol use among young adults following recreational cannabis legalization.Keywords: systematic review, policy, cannabis, alcohol, tobacco, opioids, substance use

The past several decades have seen substantial changes in cannabis policies globally, notably the legalization of cannabis for medical and recreational purposes across much of North America, as well as in Uruguay and more recently in parts of Europe and Australia. 1 , 2 These changes reflect a shift in attitudes toward cannabis use, as well as efforts to reduce harms from criminalization. 1 , 2 Governments considering cannabis legalization highlight potential risks, including increased cannabis use among vulnerable populations such as youth and impacts on other substance use. 3 Tobacco and alcohol combined account for approximately 95% of substance‐related deaths (direct and indirect) worldwide. 4 Therefore, if the liberalization of cannabis policies increases or decreases other substance use, this would have substantial implications for population‐level morbidity and mortality.

Although an association between cannabis and other substance use has been established, the question of whether cannabis use causally contributes to the initiation, prevalence, and/or frequency of other substance use is unclear. 5 Cannabis policy changes represent a unique opportunity to further our understanding of this relationship by evaluating the impacts of externally driven changes in cannabis use on other substance use. We hypothesized that policy changes which substantively increase access to legal cannabis will increase the prevalence and/or frequency of adult cannabis use. We would then anticipate downstream effects among adults (e.g., increases in cannabis‐related health care utilization), as well as impacts on other substance use outcomes as a result of complementary use (co‐consumption) or substitution (use of cannabis in place of other substances). These effects may be heterogenous across substances and age groups. Lastly, we considered whether expanded access to legal cannabis among adults alters cannabis and other substance use among youth (e.g., as a result of changes in general cannabis availability, including among older peers and other adults).

Studies examining the impact of cannabis policy changes on cannabis and other substance use have found mixed results, with some studies finding increases, decreases, or no changes in use, even for similar populations. 6 , 7 These conflicting results may be attributable to challenges in policy evaluation, such as accounting for temporal trends in substance use and confounding due to differences between jurisdictions that choose to liberalize cannabis policies and those that do not. In contrast to other observational studies that rely only on adjustment for measured covariates, quasi‐experimental approaches (e.g., interrupted time series, difference‐in‐differences, regression discontinuity, synthetic control) account for some sources of unmeasured confounding through their design and are therefore well‐suited to estimate the causal effects of policy changes. 8 Therefore, we conducted a systematic review of quasi‐experimental studies assessing the impact of global cannabis policy changes on cannabis and other substance use.

Methods

Our systematic review protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024547798), and our findings were reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. 9

Search Strategies

We searched the Embase, Medline, and PsycINFO databases via Ovid, as well as the Web of Science Core Collection and the Criminal Justice Database (ProQuest) from the date of inception through July 10, 2024. The subject area and textword terms consisted of three groups combined with AND: (1) “cannabis” or “marijuana” in the title; (2) policy terms in the title, abstract, or descriptors; and (3) substance use terms in the title, abstract, or descriptors (see Appendix A for complete search strategies). No restrictions were placed on date or language of publication, nor were restrictions were placed on study design, as the terminology for quasi‐experimental designs is broad, inconsistent, and poorly indexed in databases. 8 , 10 Our search strategies were peer‐reviewed by a librarian according to the peer review of electronic search strategies (PRESS) statement. 11 Gray literature sources included a preprint repository (Social Science Research Network), the National Institute on Drug Abuse International Research Abstract Database, and relevant conference websites (Cannabis Research Conference, Conference of the International Society for the Study of Drug Policy, Lisbon Additions Conference 2019, Cannabis Clinical Outcomes Research Conference, Society for Epidemiologic Research).

Study Selection

Search results were uploaded to Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for deduplication and screening. Two reviewers independently screened all unique titles/abstracts. Any citation considered potentially eligible by either reviewer was moved to full‐text screening (likewise conducted in duplicate). Disagreements were resolved by consensus or a third reviewer, if necessary.

Inclusion/Exclusion

Eligible studies examined the impact of a change in cannabis policy (e.g., depenalization, decriminalization, medical or recreational legalization) or policy feature (e.g., changes to the specific conditions of legalization, such as the allowance of retail sales) on an outcome related to use of cannabis or another substance (e.g., alcohol, tobacco, opioids, prescription drugs). Possible outcomes included, but were not limited to, self‐reported substance use, substance‐related health care utilization, substance use disorders, mortality attributable to substance use (e.g., opioid overdose), drug prescriptions, and sales/spending related to drugs or substances (e.g., at the individual, household, or corporate/governmental levels; excluding cannabis supply measures, which reflect changes in sourcing of cannabis, rather than cannabis use itself). Outcomes that were likely to be impacted by the policy change directly, rather than mediated primarily by changes in substance use, were excluded (e.g., cannabis legalization reducing cannabis‐related arrests due to changes in legality rather than cannabis use; cannabis legalization increasing policing for substance‐impaired driving). No restrictions were placed on study location, as our goal was to provide a comprehensive overview of global cannabis policy impacts.

The studies included were quasi‐experimental, defined as studies that aimed to approximate an experiment with clearly defined treatment groups, and accounted for some unobservable factors (e.g., unmeasured confounding) by design. We excluded studies that used approaches relying entirely on conditioning (e.g., regression adjustment, stratification, matching) on measured covariates to estimate treatment effects. Eligible designs included, but were not limited to, interrupted time series, difference‐in‐differences, instrumental variable, regression discontinuity, and synthetic control. Reports had to include the findings of an original study (e.g., systematic reviews were excluded). Conference proceedings and abstracts were eligible for inclusion, provided eligibility could be determined on the basis of available information. If a conference abstract and full text reported the same findings, only the full text was included.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted into a pilot‐tested form in Excel by one reviewer, with validation by a second reviewer. Extracted data included publication characteristics, policy characteristics, study population, methodology, and relevant findings.

Risk of Bias

We used the Risk Of Bias In Non‐Randomized Studies ‐ of Interventions (ROBINS‐I) Tool (V2), 12 the only tool recommended by the Cochrane Handbook for risk of bias assessment of non‐randomized studies. 13 ROBINS‐I involves assessing the risk of bias in seven domains: (1) confounding, (2) selection of participants into the study, (3) classification of interventions, (4) deviations from intended interventions, (5) missing data, (6) measurement of outcomes, and (7) selection of the reported results. Modifications applied to facilitate the assessment of quasi‐experimental designs are described in Appendix B. 8 Assessment was completed in duplicate by two independent reviewers and resulted in a classification of low, moderate, serious, or critical for each of the seven domains. An overall assessment corresponded to the highest assessed risk of bias in any domain or a judgment that the risk of bias was compounded across domains, (e.g., multiple serious assessments could be considered a critical overall risk of bias).

Data Synthesis

Extracted data were synthesized using a descriptive, analytical approach to assess the impact of each cannabis policy or feature on the outcomes of interest and to identify gaps in knowledge. Impacts were synthesized by age group as possible. Age group definitions varied by study; however, youth typically included teens up to the age of majority, young adults included individuals aged approximately 18 to 24 years, and older adults often included individuals aged either 55 years and older or 65 years and older. We also considered other sociodemographic groups (e.g., sex/gender, race/ethnicity), as well as reported groups with distinct sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., prescription outcomes by health care payer type, as group selection is impacted by characteristics such as employment, age, income, and disability). We reported measures of effect as they were reported by study authors. If multiple measures were available, we prioritized absolute measures (prevalence differences) as the most relevant and interpretable for policy impacts, followed by prevalence ratios, and then odds ratios. Assessed risk of bias was taken into consideration when drawing conclusions. Although our protocol planned for meta‐analysis of some policy‐outcome pairs, we determined there to be limited added value from statistical pooling owing to heterogeneity in study and population characteristics. Instead, we considered the impacts of heterogeneity in the descriptive synthesis and discussion.

Overall Results

Search Results

Database searches yielded 11,060 unique records, of which 10,665 were excluded based on title/abstract and 395 were retrieved for full‐text screening (Figure 1). We retrieved an additional 34 reports from gray literature sources, for a total of 429 full texts screened, of which 176 satisfied the inclusion/exclusion criteria. The included reports (Appendix C) were published between 1993 and 2024, with approximately two‐thirds (n = 113) published after 2019 and nearly 90% (n = 155) published after 2016. Most were peer‐reviewed publications (n = 148), with the remaining reports from preprints (n = 14), conference abstracts (n = 8), or dissertations (n = 6). Most studies applied a difference‐in‐differences approach (n = 129). Others used interrupted times series analysis (n = 42), synthetic control (n = 3), and difference‐in‐discontinuities (n = 1). The vast majority of studies used data from the United States (n = 141), with the remaining studies using data from Canada (n = 23), Europe (n = 6), Uruguay (n = 4), or Australia (n = 2). The reported findings used data from the United States unless otherwise specified.

Policy Changes

The included studies examined the impacts of a range of cannabis policy changes, the most liberal of which was the legalization of recreational cannabis with retail sales and/or home cultivation (n = 61), in which adult possession and use of cannabis is permitted for any purpose and the policy provides for access to legal cannabis. Other studies examined the legalization of recreational cannabis, irrespective of provisions for access (n = 80). Similarly, the included studies examined the legalization of medical cannabis with retail sales and/or home cultivation (n = 42) and the legalization of medical cannabis irrespective of provisions for access (n = 77), in which policies allow for cannabis use among individuals with qualifying medical conditions. Nine of the included studies evaluated the impacts of decriminalization, in which criminal penalties for personal possession were replaced by civil penalties (e.g., fines). A total of 13 studies examined other global cannabis policies, including more permissive policies such as depenalization (reduction but not elimination of criminal penalties) or legalization of products with low psychoactive potential (n = 8) and less permissive policies such as repenalization (increase in criminal penalties), bans on synthetic cannabis products, increases in the minimum legal age of cannabis use, and varied local restrictions (n = 4), as well as other intervention definitions that combined heterogenous policies (n = 3).

Assessed Risk of Bias

Many reports examined multiple eligible policy changes and outcomes; we assessed the risk of bias of each policy‐outcome pair separately; therefore, risk of bias could vary within each study (Appendix D). The lowest overall risk of bias (of any policy‐outcome pair) within the included studies was low (n = 30), moderate (n = 55), serious (n = 77), or critical (n = 14). The number of serious assessments reflects our conservative approach to risk of bias within this group of generally well‐conducted quasi‐experimental studies. Policy‐outcome pairs at low, moderate, or serious risk of bias were considered informative and were included in the narrative synthesis. In cases where the findings were inconsistent across studies, we considered whether the assessed risk of bias among the informative results could explain the observed heterogeneity of effects. Only results at critical risk of bias were considered uninformative; these results were not included in the narrative synthesis, but are available in the relevant appendices.

The confounding domain considered design‐specific assumptions and vulnerabilities to bias. Elevated risk of bias in this domain partly reflects recent developments in difference‐in‐differences methodologies, such that studies that pooled treated groups without considering heterogeneous treatment timing (which may bias difference‐in‐differences estimates if earlier‐treated groups serve as controls for later‐treated groups in the context of dynamic treatment effects) were assessed as moderate, unless they demonstrated that it was unnecessary to do so (e.g., via Goodman‐Bacon decomposition). 14 However, a number of studies did not address other design‐specific assumptions (e.g., examining pretreatment trends to add credibility to the assumption that postintervention trends in the control group are similar to what would have happened in the treated group, had they not been treated). Co‐interventions were a common reason for receiving a critical assessment for confounding (e.g., policy changes simultaneous with the Covid‐19 pandemic). Classification of interventions was typically low risk of bias, except for studies that used averaged multiyear outcome data obscuring the pre‐ and postintervention periods. Selection of participants was assessed to be low risk of bias in the setting of universal or near‐universal outcome data, with survey data often receiving a moderate assessment (e.g., no information about demographic shifts pre‐post intervention). Most studies received low for the remaining risk of bias domains, with the exception of selection of reported results. As written, ROBINS‐I resulted in an assessment of at least moderate risk of bias in the absence of a publicly available protocol. However, registration of quasi‐experimental studies is not standard (only one included study had a protocol available); therefore, we did not consider the availability of a protocol when determining overall risk of bias, although other factors that contributed to elevated risk in the selection of reported results domain (e.g., selective reporting of analyses) were included.

Recreational Cannabis Retail Sales and/or Home Cultivation

A total of 61 included studies examined recreational cannabis retail sales (n = 37) or retail sales and/or home cultivation (n = 3) in the United States or impacts of Canada's 2018 recreational cannabis legalization with the simultaneous allowance of retail sales (n = 22) (Appendix E). The studies employed difference‐in‐differences approaches (n = 33), including two with a synthetic control, 15 , 16 and uncontrolled interrupted time series analysis (n = 28).

Adults and the General Population

Cannabis: Prevalence and Frequency of Use

Four studies examined changes in cannabis use among adults following the allowance of retail sales or open dispensaries, finding increases in the prevalence of cannabis use among young adults (14.6%‐14.8%) 17 and adults (2.2 percentage points [standard error {SE}, 0.7]; 24.8%‐33.5%), 17 , 18 , 19 past two‐day vaped cannabis use, 19 and initiation of cannabis use among young adults 17 and adults. 19 , 20 One study found an increase in positive workplace drug tests following open dispensaries, 17 and another found no clear change in the cessation of cannabis use. 19

Cannabis Use Disorder

Six studies reported cannabis use disorder outcomes, suggesting no change or an increase in cannabis use disorders among adults and pregnant individuals in Quebec; 21 , 22 very small but precise increases in cannabis use disorder among veterans in the United States; 23 , 24 an increase in admissions for cannabis‐related substance use treatment among adults in pooled US states; 18 and an increase in the proportion of maternal hospitalizations involving cannabis use disorder in the United States. 25

Cannabis‐Related Health Care Utilization

Three studies found increases in poison‐control reports in pooled US states, 26 California, 27 and Washington state. 28 One study found the increases to be greater for males and intentional exposures. 26 The 13 studies reporting other health care utilization outcomes exclusively examined the impact of Canada's simultaneous legalization and allowance of retail sales, with mixed directionality of findings. Whereas one study found no change or a decrease in cannabis‐attributable hospitalizations across five pooled provinces, 29 other studies found increases in cannabis‐attributable emergency department (ED) visits among adults in individual provinces, 30 , 31 with no change or an increase in cannabis‐attributable hospitalizations. 32 Two studies suggesting a possible decrease in cannabis‐attributable ED visits were imprecise. 33 , 34 Findings for cannabis‐induced psychosis were inconsistent. 35 , 37 Studies found no change in ED visits attributable to anxiety with cannabis involvement 38 or cannabis hyperemesis syndrome, 39 as well as unclear changes in ED visits or hospitalizations for cannabis use in pregnancy 29 or self‐harm. 40

Alcohol

Following open dispensaries, one study found no change or a small increase in the prevalence of alcohol use among young adults (1.2% [SE, 2.1]) and adults (0.8% [SE, 1.9]) in pooled US states, 17 as well as no change in excessive drinking among adults in pooled US states (−0.1 percentage points; SE, 0.5) 18 and in Colorado and Washington state (−0.7% to 0.5%). 41 No change was found in the frequency of alcohol use in pooled US states or in adjacent states following the allowance of retail sales. 42

Four studies found no change or an increase in alcohol‐related health care utilization in Ontario or Alberta, although the estimates were imprecise, 35 , 38 , 40 , 43 and one study found no clear change in the diagnosis of alcohol‐related disorders among pregnant individuals in Quebec. 22 Other studies found no change in maternal hospitalizations involving alcohol use disorder or fetal alcohol syndrome in the United States, 25 as well as no clear change in admissions for alcohol‐related substance use treatment or alcohol‐related mortality. 18 One study found that household expenditures on alcohol increased in pooled US states following open dispensaries, 44 whereas another study found a decrease in beer, liquor, and wine sales in Washington state. 45 The study at lowest risk of bias (low) found an immediate decrease in beer and spirit sales in Canadian provinces, followed by monthly decreases. 46

Tobacco

Following open dispensaries, the studies found no clear change or a decrease in tobacco prevalence, including among young adults (−1.8% [SE, 3.2]) 17 and adults (−1.3 to 0.8 percentage points; −5.9% [SE, 2.2]); 17 , 19 a decrease in vaping that was greater among males; 19 no change in the initiation of cigarette use or vaping (−0.7 to 0.5 percentage points); 19 and no change or a possible increase in the cessation of cigarette use or vaping (1.7‐2.1 percentage points). 19 One study found no change in maternal hospitalizations involving tobacco use disorder. 25 Another study found a decrease in cigarette sales in Washington state but no change in sales of non‐cigarette tobacco products. 45

Opioids: Prescribing

Seven studies found no change or a decrease in opioid prescribing outcomes, including among all payers 47 or Medicaid enrollees 48 in pooled US states and among all payers in Colorado counties following open dispensaries, 49 for orthopedic‐related prescriptions filled by Medicare Part D enrollees following the allowance of home cultivation, 50 among commercial insurance enrollees following the allowance of home cultivation and/or active dispensaries, 51 and in Canada following the simultaneous legalization and allowance of retail sales. 52

Opioid Use Disorder

Studies found no change in admissions for opioid‐related substance use treatment among young adults in Colorado and Washington state 53 or among adults in pooled US states. 18 One study found no change in maternal hospitalizations involving opioid use disorder. 25

Opioid‐Related Health Care Utilization

Two studies examined opioid‐related health care utilization outcomes following open dispensaries, finding a decrease in opioid‐related hospitalizations in Colorado counties 49 and a decrease in opioid‐attributable ED visits in Ontario and Alberta. 43

Opioids: Mortality

Eight studies examined opioid‐related mortality, with most point estimates (including the studies at lowest risk of bias) suggesting a decrease in overall opioid‐related mortality following the allowance of retail sales or open dispensaries (−23% to −5%) 15 , 47 , 54 , 55 , 56 and the allowance of home growing and/or open dispensaries (rate ratio [RR], 0.81 [95% credible interval {CrI}, 0.75‐0.88]), 54 although two studies at serious risk of bias found no change 18 , 57 and another found an increase following the allowance of retail sales (range of logged percentage changes, 16.0%‐22.4%). 58 Findings were inconsistent for mortality related to natural and semisynthetic opioids, 54 , 58 heroin, 18 , 47 , 54 , 58 and synthetic opioids, although most estimates suggested a decrease. 18 , 47 , 54 , 55 , 58 No change or a decrease was found in mortality related to prescription opioids. 47 , 55

Youth

Cannabis: Prevalence and Frequency of Use

Following the allowance of retail sales or open dispensaries, the evidence for changes in the prevalence (n = 7) (−2.6 to 2.3 percentage points; 10.1%‐14.5%; range of ORs, 0.78‐1.40) 17 , 59 , 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 or frequency 42 , 59 , 60 , 61 of cannabis use among youth in the United States and Canada was inconsistent. No change or an increase was found in the initiation of cannabis use in the past 1 to 2 years (n = 5). 17 , 20 , 61 , 63 , 64 One study found no clear change in the average age of first cannabis use among youth aged 15 to 16 years in Canada, whereas the average age of first cannabis use increased by 4.1 months (95% CI, 2.7‐5.5) among youth aged 17 to 18 years. 64

Cannabis Use Disorder

One study found no change or an increase in cannabis use disorder among youth in Ontario. 61 No clear change was found in admissions for cannabis‐related substance use treatment among youth in Colorado and Washington state. 65

Cannabis‐Related Health Care Utilization

One study found increases in poison‐control reports in California, which were greater among children than other age groups. 27 Two studies found increases in cannabis‐attributable ED visits among children 35 and/or youth 31 , 35 in Alberta and Ontario following Canada's simultaneous legalization and allowance of retail sales. One study found an increase in ED visits for pediatric cannabis exposures in Ontario. 66

Other Substances

Following open dispensaries, one study found no change or a small increase in the prevalence of alcohol use (3.4% [SE, 4.3]) and no clear change in the prevalence of tobacco use (1.1% [SE, 9.6]) among youth in pooled US states. 17 Another study found no change in admissions for opioid‐related substance use treatment among youth in Colorado and Washington state. 53

Recreational Cannabis Legalization

A total of 80 studies included in this review examined recreational cannabis legalization (Appendix F). All publications used data from the United States, except for four publications using data from Uruguay. 67 , 68 , 69 , 70 Most studies employed difference‐in‐differences approaches, including three with synthetic controls. 16 , 71 , 72 Other studies used uncontrolled interrupted time series analysis (n = 11), 27 , 28 , 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 , 78 , 79 , 80 , 81 synthetic control (n = 3), 67 , 68 , 69 controlled interrupted time series analysis (n = 2), 82 , 83 or difference‐in‐discontinuities (n = 1). 84

Adults and the General Population

Cannabis: Prevalence and Frequency of Use

Among young adults, all studies at lowest risk of bias (moderate) found an increase in past‐month cannabis use in pooled US states (1.2‐1.6 percentage points; 16.4% [SE, 4.3]; OR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.19‐1.28]), 17 , 18 , 19 , 85 whereas one study suggested a decrease (OR, 0.79 [95% CI, 0.64‐0.96]), 86 and another found no clear change (OR, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.86‐1.08]). 87 One study in Washington state found an immediate level change of −1.4 percentage points (P = .002); however, the difference between the pre‐ and post‐legalization slopes was +11.5 percentage points (P < .001). 82 Another study found a decrease in past‐month and past‐year cannabis use in Uruguay (−5.8 to −4.9 percentage points), 70 although differing directions of effect were observed for past‐month (−3.5 percentage points [95% CI, −6.8, −0.2]) and past‐year (7.4 percentage points [95% CI, −1.2‐16.1]) risky cannabis use, as measured by the Cannabis Abuse Screening Test. 70 All studies found an increase in the initiation of cannabis use in the past 1 to 2 years or in the prevalence of lifetime cannabis use. 17 , 20 , 82 One study found increased odds of frequent use, 85 whereas another study found imprecise changes in frequent use in Uruguay. 70

Among adults, all studies (n = 6) found an increase in past‐month cannabis use (1.5‐4.1 percentage points; 20.1% [SE, 4.7]; range of ORs, 1.13‐1.28) 17 , 18 , 19 , 86 , 88 , 89 or past two‐day vaped cannabis use (0.8 percentage points [SE, 0.3]). 19 Among individual states, two studies found increases in past‐month cannabis use among adults in Oregon, Washington, Colorado, and Alaska (1.8‐4.4 percentage points), with no clear changes in California or Massachusetts (0.1‐0.5 percentage points). 18 , 19 One study found no change in past‐month cannabis use following legalization in a bordering state (−0.3 percentage points [SE, 0.2]), 18 whereas another study found a 2.5 percentage point (SE, 0.9) increase in past‐month cannabis use in Washington border counties following Oregon's legalization. 84 In addition, increases in past‐year cannabis use among adults (n = 6) were found in pooled US states (2.0‐4.7 percentage points; 12.7% [SE, 4.2]), 17 , 19 , 88 , 89 , 90 as well as in states bordering Washington and Colorado (1.2‐3.5 percentage points). 91 All studies found an increase in the initiation of cannabis use in the past 1 to 2 years or in the prevalence of lifetime cannabis use. 17 , 19 , 89 One study found no change in past‐year frequency of use, 88 whereas two other studies found increases in past‐month frequent cannabis use. 88 , 92 One study found a possible decrease in the cessation of cannabis use, although the estimates were imprecise. 19 Two studies found increases in positive drug urine screening, including in workplaces 93 and during ED visits. 17 Evidence was imprecise and/or inconsistent in direction among studies examining sociodemographic effect measure modifiers, including sex/gender (n = 7), 19 , 79 , 85 , 86 , 87 , 92 , 94 race/ethnicity (n = 6), 19 , 79 , 86 , 87 , 92 , 94 marital status (n = 4), 86 , 87 , 92 educational attainment (n = 4), 19 , 86 , 87 , 92 and income (n = 3). 86 , 87 , 92

Cannabis Use Disorder

Two studies found small but precise increases in past‐year cannabis use disorder among veterans or adults (0.04‐0.4 percentage points), 24 , 95 particularly among the oldest veterans (65 to 75 years) and those with chronic pain. 24 Most studies found no change in admissions for cannabis‐related substance use treatment, including among young adults 18 , 96 and adults 18 , 89 , 96 in pooled US states, although sex/gender may be an effect measure modifier, with one study finding an increase among women 18 and another study finding an increase in maternal hospitalizations or delivery encounters involving cannabis use disorder. 25

Cannabis‐Related Health Care Utilization

Three studies found increases in cannabis‐related poison‐control reports, including in Washington state, 28 California, 27 and pooled US states. 26 Two studies reported increases in hospital admissions for presumed cannabis hyperemesis syndrome 97 or psychosis with cannabis use disorder. 81

Alcohol: Prevalence and Frequency of Use

Among young adults, all studies found no change or an increase in the prevalence of alcohol use in the United States or Uruguay (1.1‐3.9 percentage points). 17 , 69 , 98 One study found an increase in binge drinking (1.3 percentage points [95% CI, 0.0‐2.7]), 98 whereas other studies found no clear change (−0.4 percentage points [SE, 0.6]; range of ORs, 0.94‐0.97). 18 , 99 One study found no change in the frequency of alcohol use. 98

Among adults, most studies (including all studies at lowest risk of bias [moderate]) found no clear change or a decrease in the prevalence of alcohol use in pooled US states (−0.0 to 1.0 percentage points; −0.34% [SE, 2.1]), 17 , 88 , 89 , 98 in Washington counties bordering Oregon (−2.3 percentage points [SE, 1.4]), 84 and in Uruguay (−22.2% [uncertainty estimate not reported]). 69 One study found a small increase among older adults in pooled US states (1.5 percentage points [95% CI, 0.5‐2.5]). 98 This study also found potentially greater increases among non‐Hispanic Black individuals, men, and adults with any college education, although there was substantial overlap in confidence intervals. 98 One study found a small increase in past‐month binge drinking among men (0.9 percentage points [95% CI, 0.2‐1.6]) but not women (0.0 percentage points [95% CI, −0.6‐0.5]). 98 Other studies found no clear change in past‐month binge drinking among adults (−0.9 to 0.4 percentage points). 18 , 41 , 100 One study found no change in heavy drinking in the past month, 98 whereas other studies found an increase in the prevalence of frequent alcohol use 88 and in the frequency of alcohol use. 88

Alcohol: Sales/Spending

Studies found no change or an increase in household expenditures on alcohol or alcohol sales tax revenue in pooled US states. 44 , 89 , 101 The study at lowest risk of bias (low) found an increase in tax revenue from liquor sales in Colorado, 74 whereas another study (moderate risk of bias) found a possible decrease in alcohol sales in Colorado, a possible increase in Washington state, and inconsistent estimates depending on sample selection in Oregon. 102

Alcohol Use Disorder

One study, at low risk of bias, found no change in admissions for alcohol‐related treatment (point estimates suggested decreases in most states, although the estimates were imprecise), 18 whereas another study, at serious risk of bias, found a small increase in alcohol use disorder among adults in pooled US states. 88 One study found no change in maternal hospitalizations or delivery encounters involving alcohol use disorder and no change in newborn hospitalizations for fetal alcohol syndrome. 25

Alcohol‐Related Mortality

One study, at low risk of bias, found no change in alcohol‐related mortality; point estimates suggested decreases in most states, although the estimates were imprecise. 18 No change was found in alcohol‐related mortality in bordering states. 18

Tobacco: Prevalence and Frequency of Use

Among young adults, most estimates suggested no change or a small decrease in prevalence of tobacco use (−0.8 to −0.2 percentage points; −0.8% [SE, 2.3]; range of ORs, 0.97‐1.07). 17 , 19 , 99 One study also found a reduction in tobacco use among youth and young adults aged 15 to 25 years in Uruguay (−6.9 percentage points [uncertainty estimate not reported]). 67 , 69 Among adults, most studies found no change or a decrease in tobacco use in pooled US states (−1.3 to 0.4 percentage points), 17 , 19 , 89 in individual states (−1.7 to −0.4 percentage points), 19 and in Uruguay (−0.2 percentage points [uncertainty estimate not reported]). 69 One study reported no clear change in past‐year initiation of cigarette use, cigar use, or vaping. 19 The same study found no clear change in the cessation of cigarette use or vaping, but a possible increase in the cessation of cigar use. 19

Tobacco Use Disorder

Opioids: Prevalence and Frequency of Use

Discussion

Our systematic review was designed to provide a comprehensive overview of the impacts of global cannabis policies on cannabis and other substance use, using evidence from quasi‐experimental studies. The overall findings were generally consistent across categories of cannabis legalization, retail sales, and/or home cultivation; therefore, we discuss these collectively as “medical legalization” and “recreational legalization”. Among young adults and adults, the findings suggested increased initiation of cannabis use, as well as increased prevalence of cannabis use (particularly following recreational legalization), with likely downstream impacts on cannabis use disorder and cannabis‐related health care utilization. Following medical legalization, a potential decrease was found in the prevalence and frequency of alcohol use, as well as binge drinking, among young adults and adults, suggesting a possible net substitution effect for these groups. Following recreational legalization, a potential increase was found in the prevalence of alcohol use and binge drinking among young adults (suggesting complementary use), whereas evidence was less consistent for adults in general across alcohol use outcomes, suggesting weaker population‐level effects. Interestingly, several studies suggested a possible increase in alcohol use among older adults following both medical and recreational legalization. We identified potential substitution effects for other substances, including no change or a decrease in tobacco outcomes and non‐opioid prescribing outcomes. Most studies also found no change or a decrease in opioid‐related outcomes, although evidence for opioid‐related mortality was less consistent. Relatively few studies examined findings for non‐opioid illicit drugs, with no clear overall evidence of change.

Although youth did not gain legal access to cannabis, they may have been indirectly impacted by legalization through an increase in the availability of cannabis generally. The findings suggested a possible increase in the initiation of cannabis use among youth following medical and recreational legalization; however, we found that cannabis initiation did not necessarily correlate with more regular cannabis use or other distal outcomes observed among adults. If there is a credible increase in cannabis use among youth (see the following discussion), it may explain potential decreases in the initiation, prevalence, and frequency of alcohol use, as well as in binge drinking, among youth following medical legalization. Evidence was less consistent among youth following recreational legalization, although most evidence suggested no change or a decrease in alcohol‐related outcomes. In addition, a possible decrease was found in tobacco outcomes among youth; however, the presence of a substitution effect is likewise contingent on an increase in cannabis use, which remains unclear.

A number of recent systematic reviews examined the impacts of cannabis policies on substance use, most of which focused on the impacts of recreational legalization on cannabis and were not restricted to quasi‐experimental study designs. Our findings are largely consistent with those reported by previous reviews, including increases in cannabis use among young adults 164 , 165 , 166 , 167 and adults 164 , 167 , 168 following recreational legalization and heterogeneous evidence for cannabis use among youth following recreational legalization. 164 , 165 , 166 , 167 , 168 One review similarly found increases in cannabis‐related health care utilization following recreational legalization, as well as an increase in alcohol‐related health care utilization for which our review found few eligible studies. 169 Other reviews found inconsistent evidence for alcohol use and limited evidence for changes in tobacco use following recreational legalization; 164 , 167 however, these reviews identified fewer studies reporting these outcomes than our review. Likewise, reviews including opioid outcomes also identified few studies, with similar findings of no change or decreases in opioid‐related outcomes following recreational or medical legalization. 167 , 170 , 171 One review examining medical legalization in the United States concluded that there was no change in cannabis use among youth and young adults, 166 whereas we found the available literature to be inconsistent among youth and to suggest no change or an increase among young adults.

Unclear evidence for changes in cannabis use among youth is a common conclusion from the literature. 164 , 165 , 166 , 168 Although restricting our review to studies employing quasi‐experimental approaches provided more homogeneity across designs than in previous reviews, we similarly found that the evidence for youth was less consistent than that for other age groups, which may be explained by smaller sample sizes (with greater imprecision), heterogeneity in population characteristics, and policy maturity. The use of a school‐based versus a general population‐based sampling frame could explain some heterogeneity. For example, among the included studies examining cannabis use among youth following recreational legalization, 8 of the 10 studies finding no change or a decrease used school‐based surveys, 37 , 59 , 68 , 70 , 86 , 87 , 94 , 113 , 114 , 115 compared to 3 of the 9 studies finding an increase. 17 , 72 , 78 , 79 , 90 , 91 , 96 , 115 , 116 Timing of data collection may also explain some heterogeneity. Among studies that found no change or a decrease in cannabis use among youth, 7 of the 10 studies had data collection periods ending prior to 2018, compared with 3 of the 9 studies that found an increase. We did not find any indication of heterogeneity by assessed risk of bias (studies with moderate risk of bias comprised about half of each group). Overall, these findings suggest that an increase in cannabis use may be more likely among the general population of youth, compared to youth who are in school, and that cannabis use among youth may increase to reflect greater availability of cannabis following the earlier years of recreational legalization (e.g., as legal cannabis sources become established).

Another area in which we found substantial heterogeneity was the impacts of cannabis policies on opioid‐related mortality. Given the severity of the opioid crisis in North America, the impact of cannabis policies on opioid use has been a topic of much interest. 172 , 173 , 174 We found no change or decreases in varied opioid‐related health care utilization outcomes and opioid sales following medical and recreational legalization, and most studies suggested no change or a decrease in opioid prescribing outcomes. These findings were similar across study periods. Changes in opioid‐related mortality were less consistent, although the studies at the lowest assessed risk of bias within each policy type suggested no change or decreases. However, for medical legalization, analyses at the lowest risk of bias predominantly examined study periods beginning in 1999 and ending before 2018; during this period, very few of the analyses included in this review found an increase in opioid‐related mortality. In contrast, almost all analyses examining periods ending in 2019 or 2020, although much smaller in number, found an increase in opioid‐related mortality following medical legalization. It is unclear whether this observation can be attributed to a greater risk of bias in this latter group or to other sources of heterogeneity (e.g., the inclusion in pooled estimates of late‐adopting US states with new or expanded access to medical cannabis, 175 which may differ in their policy environments and mediators of the relationship between medical cannabis legalization and opioid‐related mortality). We did not observe any consistent pattern of directionality by study period for recreational legalization (for which most study periods ended in 2019 or 2020).

Inconsistencies in the impacts of cannabis policies on opioid‐related mortality may be explained by methodological challenges specific to opioids, which are also variable by jurisdiction. These include (1) phases of the opioid epidemic, impacting the most common types of opioids used, 176 and (2) impacts of other interventions such as policies and programs targeting opioid use and overdose risk, including prescription drug monitoring programs, overdose education and naloxone distribution, and drug testing (e.g., for fentanyl), or otherwise impacting opioid prescribing (e.g., Medicaid expansion). 110 , 177 The quasi‐experimental approaches most commonly employed by studies included in our review (difference‐in‐differences and interrupted time series analysis) are vulnerable to time‐varying confounding (as are most other observational studies), which may have resulted in residual confounding for some studies. Our recommendation for researchers and policymakers is that the strongest evidence of credible causal effects will be found by conducting separate evaluations of individual jurisdictions of interest, allowing for careful identification of and accounting for these jurisdiction‐specific time‐varying confounders.

Lastly, we identified a number of knowledge gaps that would benefit from further research. First, evidence from a limited number of studies suggests that there may be a decrease in tobacco use across age groups attributable to cannabis policy liberalization, which could impact population‐level tobacco‐related morbidity and mortality. There are several possible mechanisms by which cannabis could reduce tobacco use, including cannabis‐induced inhibition of nicotine metabolism and alleviation of withdrawal symptoms, 178 as well as replacement of behavioral cues (e.g., smoking cannabis mimicking smoking cigarettes). Second, limited evidence was found suggesting increases in the prevalence of alcohol use among older adults following medical cannabis legalization (in contrast to decreases among all other age groups). Alcohol use is known to be increasing among older adults compared to younger age groups; 179 therefore, the question of whether our observed increase reflects a true effect or residual confounding from secular trends should be explored. Third, limited evidence was found concerning the impact of policies other than medical or recreational legalization (e.g., decriminalization, depenalization, policy features other than retail sales or home cultivation), using data from outside the United States or for non‐opioid illicit drug use. Lastly, although substance use may differ by sociodemographic characteristics, few studies addressed potential effect measure modifiers other than age (e.g., gender, racial identity, socioeconomic status).

Limitations

Our systematic review has a number of limitations. First, for feasibility, we restricted inclusion to studies using quasi‐experimental approaches. Although the intention was to focus inclusion on a group of studies well‐suited to address challenges in policy evaluation, other credible designs exist. Second, we assessed many policy‐outcome pairs within included studies to be at serious risk of bias. However, this often reflected a failure to fully demonstrate that the study met all of the causal assumptions needed for its design (i.e., indicating risk of bias, rather than observed bias). These studies (unlike critical risk of bias) are still informative, particularly as our approach to risk of bias assessment was conservative. Third, differential measurement error is a potential threat to internal validity across studies evaluating impacts of legalization on cannabis use, owing to possible changes in willingness to report following legalization; however, changes in reporting are likely to be small relative to actual changes in cannabis consumption. Fourth, our findings for non‐cannabis substance use reflect population‐level changes, which may be the result of individuals choosing both substitution and complementary use in varying degrees. For example, cannabis may be used in place of alcohol (substitution) or in conjunction with alcohol (complementary use); 180 therefore, our findings indicate a net effect (e.g., an overall increase in alcohol use after recreational legalization indicates that more young adults used alcohol as a complement to cannabis, even though some individuals may have used cannabis in place of alcohol). Fifth, most included studies used data from the United States, which may or may not be generalizable to other countries and contexts.

Conclusions

The legalization of medical and recreational cannabis may increase cannabis initiation across age groups, with increased prevalence of cannabis use among young adults and adults and weaker potential effects among youth. Evidence also suggests downstream impacts on cannabis use disorder and cannabis‐related health care utilization among adults. Impacts on alcohol use appear to be differential by policy, with potential decreases following medical legalization and increases following recreational legalization in some groups. Evidence from a small number of studies suggests potential decreases in tobacco use among all age groups. Most studies found no change or a decrease in opioid‐related outcomes, although evidence was less consistent for opioid‐related mortality and these outcomes may be particularly vulnerable to time‐varying confounding. Overall, these findings suggest that cannabis legalization increases cannabis use, with weaker effects among youth. We identified potential substitution effects for other substances (e.g., alcohol, tobacco, opioids), excepting complementary alcohol use among young adults following recreational cannabis legalization.
