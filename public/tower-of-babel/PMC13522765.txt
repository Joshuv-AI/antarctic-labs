Combination of exosome vaccines, lenvatinib, and anti-PD-1 eradicates tumor in autochthonous hepatocellular carcinoma mice
Authors: Bingfeng Zuo, Xuening Liu, Zheng Zhang, Zhenqiang Xu, Yanzhi Zhong, Yang Zhang, Qian Yang, Zezhen Yu, Kangjie Zhao, Yali Zhang, Yiqi Seow, HaiFang Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUnresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) demands more effective therapeutics to overcome its dismal prognosis. Dendritic cell (DC)-derived exosomes (DEXs) modified with CP05-anchored HCC-targeting peptide (P47-P), DC-recruiting and -activating high-mobility group nucleosome-binding protein 1 (HMGN1) (N1ND-N), and lymphotactin domains (XCL1-X) make DEXPNX. In situ antigen release by oral lenvatinib (LEN) with intravenous DEXPNX (DEXPNX/LEN) synergistically promotes intratumoral DC recruitment and activation. This enhances tumor neoantigen cross-presentation, de novo T cell responses, and durable immunological memory against rechallenge in orthotopic HCC mice. Furthermore, DEXPNX/LEN suppresses tumor growth and increases tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells in diethyl-nitrosamine (DENA)-induced and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis-associated HCC mice. Crucially, combining DEXPNX/LEN with anti-PD-1 eradicates established tumor and boosts intratumoral CD8+ effector T cell expansion in autochthonous HCC mice. Our study demonstrates a generalizable strategy to elicit personalized antitumor responses by leveraging DEX vaccines to activate tumoral DCs against LEN-released antigens, strengthened by anti-PD-1 blocking T cell exhaustion. This represents a clinically viable approach to improve HCC outcomes.

Introduction

With increasing clinical intervention options for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), ongoing clinical trials indicate a landscape shift from single to multidrug regimens such as combining immune checkpoint antibodies with multi-kinase inhibitors as first-line treatments for HCC.1,2 However, a recent phase 3 trial for lenvatinib (LEN), a multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor, and pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) conducted in a larger cohort of patients with unresectable HCC and multiple global sites revealed no significant improvement in overall and progression-free survival versus LEN alone,3 though earlier trials in smaller cohorts showed promising outcomes.4 This highlights the complexity and heterogeneity of HCC and the necessity to develop new approaches or combination therapies for HCC.

Inspired by the immunogenic cell death (ICD) induced by LEN,5 we hypothesized that the combination of in situ antigen release of LEN with a vaccine that enables targeted recruitment and activation of various endogenous dendritic cell (DC) subsets to the tumor can augment cross-presentation of tumor antigens and generate tumor-specific antigen-agnostic immune responses against any tumor antigen including neoantigen, as opposed to delivering specific antigens in vaccines as we did previously.6 This strategy would achieve personalized immunotherapy without the need to characterize (neo)antigens for each patient. For HCC, the low mutational burden and heterogeneity pose practical hurdles to neoantigen identification and personalized cancer vaccines.7,8,9

To this end, we designed a vaccine by modifying DC-derived exosomes (DEXs), nanovesicles bearing high biocompatibility and low or no immunogenicity,10,11,12 with (1) an HCC-targeting peptide-P47 (P),13 (2) the N terminus of high-mobility group nucleosome-binding protein 1 (HMGN1) (N1ND-N) capable of promoting recruitment and activation of myeloid DCs,6,14,15 and (3) XC motif chemokine ligand 1 (XCL1-X), lymphotactin that attracts potent cross-presenting XC motif chemokine receptor-positive (XCR1+) conventional type 1 DC (cDC1) populations via an exosomal anchor peptide CP0516 to form DC-mobilizing DEX vaccines (DEXPNX). XCL1 was chosen because XCR1+cDC1 populations recruited by XCL1 play a critical role in regulating tumor-specific antitumor immunity and the effective response to immune checkpoint blockade.17,18 Theoretically, DEXPNX would enable tumor-targeted delivery of N1ND and XCL1 and promote recruitment and activation of endogenous DCs, particularly XCR1+cDC1 subpopulations excelling at antigen cross-presentation,17 in synergy with in situ antigen release of LEN in tumor, resulting in enhanced cross-presentation of tumor antigens and tumor-specific T cell responses, in which tumor-targeted recruitment of XCR1+ cDC1 is important for T cell infiltration and effector function within tumor as these DCs secrete chemokines such as C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9) that function as stimulators of T cell recruitment and clonal expansion.18

Here, we demonstrate that DEXPNX in synergy with LEN (DEXPNX/LEN) specifically recruited and activated DCs, with XCR1+CD11c+, particularly potent cross-presenting XCR1+CD103+ and XCR1+CD8α+ DCs,19,20 significantly enriched in tumor of orthotopic HCC mice after intravenous administration of DEXPNX with oral LEN. Recruited DCs cross-presented tumor antigens and triggered antigen-specific de novo immune responses, resulting in significant tumor suppression in orthotopic HCC mice bearing large established tumors, with prolonged protective T cell memory against tumor rechallenge. DEXPNX/LEN effectively suppressed tumor growth in diethyl-nitrosamine (DENA)-induced and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)-associated HCC mice. Importantly, complete tumor eradication was achieved in DENA-induced autochthonous HCC mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN when supplemented with an immune checkpoint programmed cell death 1 inhibitor (anti-PD-1), with intratumoral CD8+ effector T cell expansion. These findings demonstrate that the combination of DEXPNX vaccines with oral LEN and intraperitoneal anti-PD-1 triggers tumor-specific immune responses in each individual and realizes individualized immunotherapy without complicated antigen characterization, thus providing a generalizable strategy for personalized immunotherapy of HCC and beyond.

Results

DEXPNX/LEN promote tumor-targeted DC recruitment, activation, and neoantigen cross-presentation and elicit de novo T cell responses in orthotopic HCC mice

To design DC-mobilizing DEX vaccines, we modified DEXs with an HCC-targeting peptide P47 (P),6,13 N1ND (N),14 and the N terminus (1–25 aa) of XCL1 (X)20 via the exosomal anchor peptide CP0516 to form DEXPNX. DEXPNX had a triple loading efficiency of 58.4% (Figure 1A) and sauce-cup shape typical of DEXs21 (Figure S1). The N terminus (1–25 aa) of XCL1 was identified as crucial for XCR1 binding by mapping XCL1 fragments'22 ability to induce intracellular calcium release in lymphocytes (Figures S2A and S2B), chemotactic activities for DCs (Figure S2C), and antitumor effects in subcutaneous HCC mice (Figure S2D). To further illustrate whether XCL1 activates DCs through XCR1,23 we treated lymphocytes isolated from tumor-bearing HCC mice with DEXX in the absence or presence of N1ND in vitro. Significantly elevated expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) I, CD86, and CCR7, surface biomarkers for DC activation,24 was detected in XCR1+CD11b−CD11c+ (cDC1)25 treated with DEXX or DEXN and to a much greater extent with DEXNX. Conversely, DEXX did not increase the expression of biomarkers in XCR1−CD11b+CD11c+ DCs (cDC2)26 (Figure S2E), supporting that XCL1 primarily activates DCs through XCR1. Intravenous administration of DiR-labeled DEXPNX or DEXs into day-21 orthotopic HCC mice resulted in increased fluorescence in tumor 2 h post-injection compared to DEX and PBS controls (Figure 1B), demonstrating the tumor-specific delivery of DEXPNX. The intrinsic tropism of DEXs for normal liver tissue was also evident from the fluorescence (Figure 1B). Oral administration of LEN in subcutaneous HCC mice at a single dose of 30 mg/kg resulted in significantly higher serum levels of antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns released by ICD,27 including α-fetoprotein (AFP), α-interferon (α-IFN), and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) compared to oxaliplatin (Figures S3A–S3C), peaking at 3 days post-administration (Figure S3D), demonstrating LEN-induced ICD. Notably, single or triple oral administration of LEN did not impact the proportion of major immune cells such as DCs and T cells in tumor from treated orthotopic HCC mice (Figures S4A–S4D), although three administrations significantly improved antitumor effects compared to a single administration (Figure S4E), suggesting that antitumor effects achieved by LEN were primarily attributed to anti-angiogenesis. When oral LEN was combined with DEXs, a stronger synergistic effect was observed with DEXPNX compared to DEXPN and DEXPX, reflected by significantly greater tumor inhibition (Figure S5A), increased numbers of XCR1+CD11c+ DCs (Figure S5B), and CD8+ T cells (Figure S5E) in tumor from treated orthotopic HCC mice. To illustrate the effect of DEXPNX/LEN on endogenous DC subpopulations, we administered DEXPNX at a single dose of 120 μg intravenously in day-21 orthotopic HCC mice bearing tumors expressing ovalbumin (OVA) neoantigen with a single oral administration of LEN at 30 mg/kg 3 days prior to DEXPNX (Figure 1C). This time point was chosen as it takes 3 days for in situ antigen release to peak (Figure S3D). As expected, significantly increased numbers of CD11c+ DCs (Figure S6A) including T helper-activating cDC226 (Figure S6B) and cytotoxic T cell-priming cDC125 DCs (Figure 1D) were found in tumor from orthotopic HCC mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN compared to DEXPNX, DEX, and PBS controls. Migratory XCR1+CD103+ and resident XCR1+CD8α+ DCs, two major DC subsets excelling in the priming and cross-presentation of cell-associated antigens to CD8+ T cells,19,20 also increased (Figure 1E). Concordantly, elevated expression of MHC I, CD86, and CCR7 was detected in tumor-infiltrating total XCR1+ (Figure 1F) and XCR1+CD103+ and XCR1+CD8α+ DCs (Figure 1G) from mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN, compared to DEXPNX, DEX, and PBS. These indicate the potent DC recruitment and activation ability of the DEXPNX/LEN combination. Weaker DC recruitment and activation of DEXPNX than DEXPNX/LEN (Figures 1D–1G) underscores the importance of LEN-mediated antigen release for DC mobilization mediated by DEXPNX. Considering that the CXCL9 secreted by tumor DCs is important for T cell infiltration, particularly C-X-C chemokine receptor 3 (CXCR3)-dependent effector and memory T cell trafficking to tumor,28,29 we measured and detected elevated CXCL9 levels in tumor-infiltrating total XCR1+ and XCR1+CD103+ and XCR1+CD8α+ DCs from mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN compared to DEXPNX, DEX, and PBS (Figures 1H and 1I).

To assess neoantigen cross-presentation and de novo host antitumor responses, we employed OVA as a model neoantigen and detected whether DEXPNX/LEN could trigger anti-OVA de novo immune responses. Strikingly, significantly more OVA-derived SIINFEKL-H2kb complexes were presented on total XCR1+ and XCR1+CD103+ and XCR1+CD8α+ DCs in tumor and spleen of orthotopic HCC mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN than in DEXPNX-, DEX-, and PBS-treated mice (Figures 2A–2C). Correspondingly, significantly greater T cell responses specific for the OVA peptide-SIINFEKL were induced in tumor (Figure 2D), resulting in significant tumor inhibition (Figure 2E) of orthotopic HCC mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN compared to DEXPNX, DEX, and PBS. Consistent with DC recruitment and activation results, increases in antigen cross-presentation, T cell response, and tumor suppression were also found in mice treated with DEXPNX compared to DEX and PBS groups but much weaker than those in mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN (Figures 2A–2E), strengthening the importance of antigen-releasing LEN in DEXPNX-mediated antitumor immunity. Given that XCL1 exclusively recruits XCR1+cDC1, we compared the capacity of DEXPNX/LEN and DEXPN/LEN to recruit XCR1+cDC1 and induce corresponding activation, cross-presentation, T cell responses, and antitumor effects. Corroborating the aforementioned synergistic effects of N1ND and XCL1, incorporation of XCL1 into DEXPN/LEN further boosted recruitment of XCR1+ cDC1, particularly XCR1+CD103+ and XCR1+CD8α+ DC subsets (Figures S6C and S6D). The synergistic DC recruitment resulted in stronger antitumor immune responses, reflected by significantly enhanced activation and OVA cross-presentation on tumor total XCR1+, XCR1+CD103+, and XCR1+ CD8α+ DCs (Figures S6E and S6F), de novo T cell response (Figure S6G), and significant tumor retardation (Figure S6H) in mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN compared to DEXPN/LEN, confirming the strong synergistic effects on DC mobilization of DEXPNX/LEN conferred by N1ND and XCL1. DEXPNX/LEN induce tumor-specific immune responses and tumor suppression in orthotopic HCC mice bearing large established tumors

To assess the antitumor immunity, we administered DEXPNX at 120 μg/week for 3 weeks intravenously in orthotopic HCC mice bearing large established tumors15 with oral LEN at the dose of 30 mg/kg/week for 3 weeks 3 days prior to DEXPNX (Figure 3A). A robust tumor regression was observed in mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN compared to LEN and PBS, with one mouse being tumor-free (Figure 3B). Similar to the aforementioned observations, CD11c+ DCs, particularly XCR1+CD11c+ DCs, were substantially increased in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes from mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN compared to LEN and PBS (Figures 3C and 3D). Significantly larger numbers of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells (Figure 3E), T cells specific for the GSMLNEHVMAFP peptide and the AMFKNNYPSL glypican 3 (GPC3) peptide30 (Figure 3F), and fewer Foxp3+CD4+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) (Figure 3G) were detected in blood from DEXPNX/LEN-treated mice than in LEN and PBS controls, indicating generation of de novo T cell responses to tumor epitopes. Just to reiterate, the specific tumor antigens were not delivered by DEXPNX as DEXPNX was designed to enhance the response against tumor antigens released by LEN-induced ICD. Consistently, tumor-infiltrating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Figure 3H) and AFP+CD8+ and GPC3+CD8+ T cells (Figure 3I) significantly rose, Tregs significantly declined (Figure 3J), immunostimulatory interferon-γ (IFN-γ) significantly increased, and immunosuppressive transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) significantly decreased in blood (Figure S7A) and in tumor (Figure S7B) from mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN compared to LEN and PBS controls. DEXPNX/LEN also curtailed lung metastasis in mice bearing metastatic HCC nodules in the lungs (Figure S8A), reflected by significantly decreased lung weight and tumor nodules (Figure S8B) and increased CD8+ T and IFN-γ-expressing CD8+ T cells in tumor after DEXPNX/LEN treatment compared to LEN and PBS (Figures S8C and S8D). DEXPNX/LEN trigger long-lasting antitumor immunity against tumor rechallenge

Given the striking effect of DEXPNX/LEN, we explored the long-term antitumor immunity in day-21 orthotopic HCC mice bearing large established tumors (Figure 4A). Remarkably, half of the mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN became tumor-free on day 40 after tumor implantation (Figure 4B), with greater than an 87.5% of survival rate on day 65, whereas no mice survived in LEN and PBS groups (Figure 4C). Significantly greater numbers of circulating CD8+ T cells were detected in mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN than LEN and PBS (Figure 4D). To determine whether DEXPNX/LEN enable the generation of long-term host memory antitumor responses, we analyzed memory T cells31 in blood from mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN at 40 days after initial priming. The results revealed significantly larger populations of circulatory CD44hiCD8+ memory T cells (Figure 4E), particularly CD62LlowCD44hi effector memory T cells (TEM)31,32 (Figure 4F) in mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN than LEN and PBS controls. To investigate immune memory, we rechallenged the tumor-free survivors with 2 × 105 Hepa1-6 cells subcutaneously on day 60 after initial tumor implantation (Figure 4A). Strikingly, 100% of mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN rejected tumor rechallenge for the first 2 weeks and remained significantly suppressed at 52 days after cessation of treatment (Figure 4G). Real-time monitoring of circulating CD44hiCD8+ memory T cells and TEM in mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN at different time points exhibited a relatively stabilized pool of memory T cells, with TEM cells boosted on day 74 after exposure to antigens and sharply dropped on day 90 to the level similar to that before rechallenge (Figures 4H and 4I).32 To verify memory CD8+ T cell-mediated long-term antitumor immunity, we treated day-7 subcutaneous HCC mice with DEXPNX/LEN under the identical dosing regimen as used for orthotopic HCC mice and administered anti-CD8α on day 30 after tumor implantation when memory CD8+ T cells were substantially induced (Figures 4J and S9). DEXPNX/LEN suppress tumor growth in DENA-induced autochthonous HCC mice

To better mimic clinical settings of HCC patients, we adopted DENA-induced autochthonous HCC mouse models with treatment initiated at 7 months after DENA induction when the tumor nodules appeared and employed the identical dosing regimen as before (Figure 5A). Remarkable tumor retardation was attained with DEXPNX/LEN in autochthonous HCC mice compared to LEN and PBS, shown by significantly decreased tumor weight and nodule numbers (Figure 5B). Consistently, significant rises in circulating CD8+, PD-1+CD8+, and CD4+ T cells (Figures 5C and 5D) and a drastic decline in circulating Foxp3+CD4+ Tregs (Figure 5E) were detected in mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN compared to LEN and PBS controls. Considering that intratumoral host DC-derived CXCL9 is important for chemotaxis of cytotoxic CXCR3+CD8+ T cells,34,35 we analyzed CXCR3+CD8+ T cells in autochthonous HCC mice. Dramatically increased numbers of circulating CXCR3+CD8+ and CXCR3+CD4+ T cells and intratumoral CXCR3+CD8+ T cells were found in DEXPNX/LEN-treated mice compared to LEN and PBS (Figures 5F and 5G). Accordingly, IFN-γ-expressing, PD-1+, and AFP+CD8+ effector T cells also significantly rose (Figures 5H–5J), while Foxp3+CD4+ Tregs drastically dropped (Figure 5K) in tumor from DEXPNX/LEN-treated mice compared to LEN and PBS, indicating enhanced tumor infiltration of effector T cells. DEXPNX/LEN effectively curb tumor growth in NASH-HCC mice

Considering that NASH-HCC represents a growing clinical burden and is often characterized by an immunosuppressive microenvironment and poor response to immunotherapy,37,38 to investigate whether DEXPNX/LEN exert tumor inhibitory effects in NASH-HCC mice, we established NASH-HCC mouse models with typical steatosis, hepatocellular injury (ballooning), and inflammation (Figure S11A) with the commonly used STAM protocol.39,40 Dramatically reduced numbers of tumor nodules and liver weight were attained in NASH-HCC mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN compared to LEN and PBS (Figures 6A and 6B), while no significant alteration in the levels of serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (Figure S11B) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (Figure S11C), indices for liver and kidney damage,41 respectively, was observed among the three groups. Corroborating the biochemical parameters, no evident morphological change was observed in liver, kidney, heart, spleen, and lung from mice treated with either DEXPNX/LEN or LEN compared with PBS controls (Figure S11D), confirming no detectable drug-associated toxicities. Concurrently, tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells and PD-1+CD8+ T cells significantly rose (Figures 6C and 6D), though expression of T cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (Tim 3), a marker of T cell exhaustion,42 and lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (Lag 3), a negative regulator for activated T cells,43 significantly declined in PD-1+CD8+ T cells from NASH-HCC mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN compared to LEN and PBS (Figures 6E and 6F), indicating the less exhausted status of activated T cells elicited by DEXPNX/LEN than by LEN. Combination therapy eradicates tumor in DENA-induced autochthonous HCC mice

As it was shown that tumor infiltration of CXCR3+CD8+ and PD-1+CD8+ effector T cells is an indicator of positive responsiveness to anti-PD-1 treatment,34 we incorporated anti-PD-1 into the current dosing regimen in DENA-induced autochthonous HCC mice (Figure 7A). Strikingly, significantly decreased tumor nodules and liver weight were detected in autochthonous HCC mice treated with the combination of DEXPNX/LEN and anti-PD-1, with one mouse being tumor-free, compared to DEXPNX/LEN, LEN, and PBS (Figure 7B). Consistent with previous reports,37,44 incorporation of anti-PD-1 failed to enhance the antitumor effect of DEXPNX/LEN in NASH-HCC mice under an identical dosing regimen (Figure S12), confirming the non-responsiveness of anti-PD-1 in NASH-HCC mice. However, DEXPNX effectively repressed tumor growth, even to a much greater extent than LEN, in NASH-HCC mice (Figure S12), indicating the capacity and broad applicability of DEXPNX to elicit strong antitumor immune responses in HCC patients irrespective of etiologies. Discussion

Neoantigen-based personalized immunotherapy has advanced rapidly with promising clinical outcomes reported for tumors with high mutational burdens.45,46,47 However, for tumors bearing low mutations such as HCC, identification of neoantigens has been extremely challenging with only one clinical trial showing encouraging results.7 Here, we demonstrate that a DC-mobilizing DEX vaccine combined with LEN enables tumor-targeted DC recruitment, activation, and cross-presentation of an array of tumor antigens and drives robust antitumor responses and even tumor eradication in DENA-induced autochthonous HCC mice when supplemented with anti-PD-1, by actively engaging the strong endogenous DC-T interaction, to achieve individualized immune response without the requirement of characterizing tumor antigens. This generalizable strategy combines three functional arms with (1) DC-mobilizing DEX vaccines empowered with immunoadjuvants, (2) in situ antigen release of LEN, and (3) anti-PD-1 against T cell exhaustion enabling mounting of potent tumor-specific immunity in different HCC mouse models. It represents a viable and generalizable approach for HCC personalized immunotherapy by circumventing tumor antigen identification and potentially tumor escape caused by vaccines targeting specific tumor antigens. Importantly, this approach is highly adjustable for different tumors with high heterogeneity by switching targeting moieties and clinical ICD drugs and thus can be fast-tracked to the clinic.

Although DEXs show potential as cell-free surrogates for DC vaccines,48 DEXs loaded with single epitopes failed to prime effective T cell responses in earlier clinical trials.12,49 To enhance the immunogenicity of DEX vaccines, we previously loaded DEXs with full-length AFP alone (DEXAFP) or AFP, HCC-targeting moiety, and N1ND (DEXP&A&N).6,50 Although active engagement of endogenous DCs, particularly CD103+CD11c+ DCs, mediated by N1ND on DEXP&A&N substantially boosted the antitumor immunity compared to DEXAFP, the employment of a single antigen is only applicable to a subpopulation of HCC patients with potential immune escape. To increase the diversity of tumor antigens, we chose clinically approved LEN as a trigger for in situ release of tumor antigens in the present study. Also, we noticed that incorporation of Fms-related tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (Flt3L), a major cytokine in stimulating proliferation and differentiation of immune cell progenitors, particularly for DCs,51 to DEXP&A&N vaccines significantly boosted the DC pool and greatly enhanced the overall antitumor immunity in our previous study.6 Thus, to enlarge endogenous DC populations, particularly XCR1+cDC1, key orchestrators for antitumor immunity,26 we incorporated chemotactic XCL1 to DEX vaccines for selective recruitment of XCR1+cDC126 in our current study. Importantly, we mapped the functional core of XCL1 and identified that the first 25 aa of the N terminus of XCL1 is critical for XCR1 binding and systemically verified the potent functionality of this fragment in mobilizing XCR1+ cDC1. As expected, total CD11c+ DCs were significantly boosted in tumor with DEXPNX vaccines in synergy with LEN, without any impact on other immune cells including plasmacytoid DCs, macrophages, B cells, and monocytes (data not shown). The proportions of intratumoral XCR1+cDC1, particularly potent cross-presenting XCR1+CD103+ and XCR1+CD8α+ DC subsets, were significantly enlarged in tumor of HCC mice treated with DEXPNX compared to DEXPN or DEXPX combined with LEN, resulting in amplified cross-presentation and de novo T cell responses in HCC mice, supporting the notion that boosting and activating tumor DCs enable potent antitumor immunity. Also, our preliminary test showed an effective antitumor effect of DEXPNX/LEN in HCC patient-derived tumor-like cell clusters (PTCs) in vitro (data not shown), though a more comprehensive study is warranted. Notably, besides XCR1+cDC1, T helper-driving XCR1−CD11b+ DCs (cDC2)26 were also increased in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes from mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN, indicating the strong capacity of DEXPNX to mobilize a large variety of DCs conferred by XCL1 and N1ND. Similar to previous observations,6 XCR1+CD103+ and XCR1+CD8α+ DCs were shown to be largely responsible for the potent antitumor immunity elicited by DEXPNX; thus, we did not dive into further mechanistic exploration, although a completely different strategy was implemented in the current study. However, given the complex tumor microenvironment and tenacity of HCC,52 studies on DEX vaccines armed with more functional moieties to engage a larger variety of immune cells are warranted in future studies.

As CXCR3 is predominantly expressed on activated T cells and NK cells,28 besides T cells, we found increased CXCR3+ NK cells in tumor from autochthonous HCC mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN. Given that CXCL9 produced by XCR1+CD103+ DCs is critical for tumor chemotaxis and reinvigoration of cytotoxic CXCR3+CD8+ T cells,53,54 we focused on CXCR3+CD8+ T cells in the present study. Indeed, the increase in tumor XCR1+CD103+ DCs corresponds to elevated CXCL9 levels and numbers of CXCR3+CD8+ T cells34 in tumor of DENA-induced autochthonous HCC mice treated with DEXPNX/LEN, further substantiating the major contribution of the CXCL9-CXCR3 axis in DEXPNX/LEN-mediated anti-HCC effects. We cannot rule out the involvement of other chemokines, such as C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5), CXCL10, and CXCL11,53 though characterization is beyond the scope of this study. Unlike CXCL9 and CXCL10, CXCL11 was shown to promote tumor progression in HCC mice,55 and CCL5 was reported to promote HCC progression and metastasis.56 Given the striking antitumor effect of DEXPNX/LEN, comprehensive efficacy evaluation and immune microenvironment mapping including spatial transcriptomics and single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to decode the underlying mechanism in humanized HCC mice are warranted in future studies. Notably, complete tumor eradication was achieved in autochthonous HCC mice with the combination of DEXPNX/LEN and anti-PD-1, which has not been possible with current immunotherapeutic approaches,57 highlighting the strong synergistic potency of DEXPNX in combination with clinically approved drugs and the potential to be fast-tracked to the clinic. However, the percentage of tumor-free remains low; thus, strategies aiming to engage multiple immune responses including adaptive and innate immunity need to be developed in the future. Although incorporation of anti-PD-1 failed to enhance the antitumor effect of DEXPNX/LEN in more complicated NASH-HCC mice as expected,37,44 DEXPNX/LEN or DEXPNX induced strong antitumor immunity in NASH-HCC mice, further demonstrating the broad applicability of DEXPNX as an antitumor vaccine in HCC patients with different backgrounds. Nevertheless, DEXPNX/LEN-mediated active recruitment and activation of larger numbers of endogenous DCs within tumor enhanced the overall efficacy of DEX vaccines in solid tumors such as HCC, further underscoring the potency of the combination of DEXPNX/LEN and anti-PD-1 for treating HCC.

In summary, our study demonstrates a generalizable strategy by incorporating a potent DC-mobilizing DEX vaccine functionalized with an HCC-targeting ligand and peptide immunoadjuvants, enhanced by clinically approved LEN and anti-PD-1 drugs, enabling active engagement of DC-T interaction and triggering tailored tumor-specific immune responses to each individual, resulting in tumor eradication in DENA-induced autochthonous HCC mice without the requirement to identify tumor antigens, thus opening a new avenue for personalized tumor immunotherapy of HCC and beyond.

Limitations of the study

While our study demonstrates the clinical potential and efficacy of DEXPNX/LEN across diverse HCC mouse models, several limitations remain. First, comprehensive efficacy studies in humanized HCC models, particularly patient-derived xenograft (PDX) mice, are required prior to clinical deployment. Second, although we focused on the CXCL9-CXCR3 axis, the exhaustive mechanisms underlying DEXPNX/LEN’s functionality require further elucidation. Finally, while addition of anti-PD-1 to DEXPNX/LEN enhanced the antitumor efficacy in DENA-induced autochthonous HCC, it failed in NASH-HCC mice despite significantly reduced CD8+ T cell exhaustion markers in DEXPNX/LEN-treated NASH-HCC mice. Future studies should investigate the impact of other immunosuppressive cells such as Tregs, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) on DEXPNX/LEN in NASH-HCC mice.

Experimental model details and study participant details

Cell line

Murine DC2.4 cell line (ATCC CRL-2254; kindly provided by Dr. De Yang, Center for Cancer Research, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, US) was cultured as described previously.6 Briefly, DCs were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 1% penicillin and streptomycin (PS), 1% glutamine, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol, and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) obtained by centrifugation at 100,000 g for 1 h to remove possible FBS-containing exosomes. Murine Hepa1-6 cell line (CSTR:16397.09.0F01000916‌‌; derived from C57BL/6 mice, H-2Kb) was purchased from Boster Biological Technology Ltd (Wuhan, China) and cultured as described previously.6 Ovalbumin (OVA)-expressing Hepa1-6 cells were generated by our lab and kept in house as reported previously.6 All cells except for DCs were grown at 37°C in 5% CO2 in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% PS as described above and were tested for mycoplasm contamination with the MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detector D101 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). All cell cultures for collecting exosomes were conducted without bacterial, fungal or mycoplasma contamination.

In vivo animal models

Male mice of C57BL/6 (H-2Kb) and female mice of C3H (H-2Kk) (6-8-week-old, purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center Inc., China) were used in all experiments (number of animals used in each group was specified in corresponding figure legends). All animal experiments were carried out in the animal unit of Tianjin Medical University (Tianjin, China) according to procedures authorized and specifically approved by the institutional ethical committee (permit No. SYXK 2023–0004). All the animals were maintained and bred at Specific Pathogen Free (SPF) animal facilities with individually ventilated cages under a 12 h dark/12 h light cycle, an ambient temperature of 22 ± 2°C, and 40–60% humidity. The animals were group-housed in individually ventilated cages (maximum of 5 mice per cage) and provided with ad libitum access to a standard rodent diet and sterilized drinking water. For intravenous injections, various amounts of DEX, DEXPN, DEXPX or DEXPNX dissolved in (80 μg for subcutaneous HCC and 120 μg for orthotopic or DENA-induced autochthonous HCC or NASH-HCC mice) 200 μL phosphate buffered saline (PBS) solutions were injected into tail vein of different tumor-bearing mice. Lenvatinib (GlpBio, CA, US) was administered to tumor-bearing mice at 30 mg/kg for once or three times at weekly interval as specified in corresponding figure legends via oral gavage.58 Anti-mouse PD-1 antibody (BioXcell, WestLebanon, NH, US) was injected intraperitoneally into tumor-bearing mice at the dose of 100 μg/mouse on different timepoints as specified in the dosing scheme.59 Mice were killed by terminally anesthesia with isoflurane or 4% chloral hydrate. Tissues were harvested for isolation of living cells or fixed with Bouin’s solution (Sigma, US) and embedded with paraffin for histological studies.

Method details

Preparation and purification of exosomes

Exosomes from cell culture medium was isolated and purified as before.6 Briefly, cell culture medium was sequentially centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min, followed by 10,000 g for 30 min. The supernatant from different cells was collected and filtered with a 0.22-μm filter (Millex, Germany), followed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 1 h to pellet exosomes. Exosome pellets were washed in a large volume of PBS and recovered by centrifugation at 100,000 g for 1 h. Exosomes were further purified with sucrose gradient ultracentrifugation (Sigma, China) as previously described.16 Exosome pellets were dissolved in PBS and the total protein concentration of exosomes was quantified by the Bradford assay (Sangon Biotech, US), and stored at −80°C.

Characterization of exosomes

Exosome morphology was visualized using a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (TEM, Hitachi HT7700, Tokyo, Japan) as described previously.16 Briefly, exosomes were resuspended and diluted into PBS (1 μg/μL) and mixed with an equal volume of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), followed by adsorption onto a glow-discharged, carbon-coated formvar film attached to a metal specimen grid. Excess solution was blotted off and the grid was immersed with a small drop (50 μL) of 1% glutaraldehyde for 5 min, followed by washing with 100 μL distilled water for 8 times (2 min each time). Subsequently, the grid was transferred to 50 μL uranyl-oxalate solution (pH 7.0) for 5 min and then 50 μL methyl cellulose-uranyl acetate (100 μL 4% uranyl acetate and 900 μL 2% methyl cellulose) for 10 min at 4°C. The excess solution was blotted off and the sample was dried and examined with TEM. The size of exosomes was measured by Nano Particle Tracking and Zeta potential distribution analyzer (ParticleMetrix (PMX), Germany).

Peptide binding assay

N1ND-CP05(MPKRKVSSAEGAAKEEPKRRSARLSAKPPAKVEAKPKKAAAKD KSSDKKVQTCRHSQMTVTSRL),14,16 XCL1-CP05 (VGTEVLEESSCVNLQTQRL PCQKIKGGGGSCRHSQMTVTSRL)20 and P47-CP05 (SQDIRTWNGTRSCRHSQ MTVTSRL)13 were purchased from China peptide Ltd. (Suzhou, China) with >95% purity. For the peptide binding assay,6 briefly, AF750-labeled N1ND-CP05 (20 μg) (provided by Professor Qibing Zhou, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China), FAM-labeled P47-CP05 (20 μg) and 5′-TAMRA-labeled XCL1-CP05 (20 μg) (purchased from China Peptide Ltd., Shanghai, China) were incubated with DEX (DC-derived exosomes) (10 μg) overnight at 4°C, followed by washing with PBS for five times in 2-mL ultracentrifuge tubes and filtered with 100-kDa diafiltration tube (Millipore, US) to remove unbound peptides. The binding of peptide-exosome complexes was measured by flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa cell analyzer, US).

Establishment of different HCC mouse models

Subcutaneous, orthotopic HCC mouse models bearing large tumor and DENA-induced autochthonous HCC mice were established as previously reported.6,50 The NASH-HCC mouse model was established with the STAM protocol.39,40 Briefly, C57BL/6 male pups were subcutaneously injected with 200 μg of streptozotocin (STZ, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, US) at two days after birth for once, followed by feeding with high-fat diet (HFD) containing 60 kcal% fat (Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ, US) started at the age of 4 weeks old continuously until the end of this study. For HCC mice bearing large established tumor, when tumors reached 10–20 mm3, tumor-bearing mice were treated with PBS, DEX, DEXPN, DEXPX or DEXPNX intravenously at the dose of 120 μg/mouse/week for 1 week or 3 weeks, respectively. For mapping XCL1, XCL1N1-N25 (VGTEVLEESSCVNLQTQRLPCQKIK) and XCL1N1-N35 (VGTEVLEESSCVNLQTQRLPCQKIKTYIIWEGAMR) were synthesized by China peptide Ltd. (Suzhou, China) with >95% purity and subcutaneously injected into day-7 HCC mice bearing subcutaneous tumor at the dose of 200 μg/week for 3 weeks. Lenvatinib (30mg/kg) was orally administered via gavage as aforementioned. Mice were monitored during the treatment and sacrificed on different time-points as specified in corresponding figure legends. The length (L) and width (W) of tumors were measured with a caliper. Tumor size was calculated by the formula: (L×W2)/2 as previously described.6 Number of tumor nodules and liver (tumor) weight were measured for autochthonous HCC and NASH-HCC mice. For HCC mice bearing lung metastasis, Hepa1-6 cells (106) were intravenously injected to C57BL/6 mice for 21 days.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) assessment

The magnetic resonance images of orthotopic HCC mice were acquired as described previously6 using the same T2 propeller sequence and parameters with a small animal coil in Tianjin Medical University General Hospital. During the examination, mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium and fixed to minimize body motion, and respiration rate was monitored and body temperature was maintained to be at 37°C using warm airflow.

Tissue distribution

To examine the biodistribution of DEXPNX in orthotopic HCC mice, DiR-labeled DEXPNX or DEX (80 μg) was administered into day-21 orthotopic HCC mice for single intravenous injection as described previously.6 Perfusion was performed 2 h after injection with 50 mL cold PBS to wash out DEX in circulation. Heart, quadriceps, liver, tumor, lung, mesenteric lymph node, inguinal lymph node, kidney and spleen were harvested for imaging with IVIS spectrum (PE, Waltham, MA, US).

Lymphocyte isolation

Isolation of lymphocytes from blood or different tissues was adopted from previous studies.6 Briefly, blood from treated HCC mice was collected with 1% heparin and added gently on the top of equal volume of Lymphoprep (STEMCELL Technologies Inc., Canada) and centrifuged at 3,000 g for 5 min, followed by lysis with the ammonium chloride-Potassium (ACK) lysis buffer for 5 min at room temperature (RT) to generate lymphocyte suspensions. To isolate lymphocytes from spleen or draining lymph nodes, tissues were harvested and washed 3 times with PBS containing 2% FCS and 2% PS. Single lymphocyte cell suspensions were obtained by rubbing and pressing lymph nodes on 70-μm nylon sieve (BD Biosciences, Belgium) using a pair of sterile forceps and syringe handle. Subsequently, cells were re-suspended in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FCS, 2 mM L-glutamine, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol and 1% PS. For isolation of lymphocytes from tumor tissues, tumor tissues were minced into small pieces with surgical scissors, followed by dissociation into single cell suspensions with mechanical dissociation and enzymatic degradation of the extracellular matrix with Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) as per manufacturer’s instructions. After dissociation, the sample is applied to 70-μm cell strainer to remove any remaining larger particles from the single cell suspension. The extract was centrifuged at 528 g for 10 min and the supernatant was removed. The mixture was re-suspended with 10 mL 40% percoll (Pharmacia, Sweden), followed by centrifugation at 850g for 30 min at 22°C to remove the supernatant. Cell pellets were resuspended in ACK lysis buffer to remove red blood cells and the rest cells were used for antibody staining.

In vitro DC activation with XCL1

Lymphocytes isolated from tumor-bearing mice were plated in 24-well plates (2×105 cells) and DEX, DEXN, DEXX or DEXNX (10 μg/mL) were added to lymphocytes and incubated for 48 h at 37°C and 5% CO2 in T cell culture medium (RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% PS and 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol). Subsequently, lymphocytes were collected, washed and stained with APC-Cy7-anti-mouse CD45 (1:500, eBioscience, US), PE-anti-mouse CD11c (1:500; Biolegend, US), BV421-anti-mouse-CD11b (1:1000, Biolegend, US), PerCP-Cy5.5-anti-mouse-XCR1 (1:500; Biolegend, US), FITC-anti-mouse MHC-I (1:250, eBioscience, US), APC-anti-mouse CD86 (1:250, eBioscience, US), PE-Cy7-anti-mouse CCR7 (1:250, Biolegend, US) at 4°C for 30 min, respectively. Flow cytometric data were acquired by BD FACS Verse or LSR Fortessa (BD, US) and analyzed with FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC).

Flow cytometry and intracellular staining

For detecting mouse DC surface markers, DCs were washed in the FACS buffer (PBS containing 0.5% BSA and 0.05% NaN3), stained with a combination of FITC-, PE-cy7- or PE-anti-mouse IgG2b Isotype (1:500, eBioscience, US), APC-anti-mouse IgG2b Isotype (1:500; Biolegend, US); FITC- or APC-cy7-anti-mouse CD45 (1:1000, Biolegend, US), PE-anti-mouse CD11c (1:500, Biolegend, US), BV421-anti-mouse CD11b (1:1000, Biolegend, US), BV605-, APC- or Percp-cy5.5-anti-mouse CD8α (1:500, Biolegend, US), APC-cy7-anti-mouse MHC-I (1:250, eBioscience, US), APC-anti-mouse CD86 (1:250, eBioscience, US), APC-anti-mouse CCR7 (1:250, Biolegend, US), BV421-anti-mouse-XCR1(1:500; Biolegend), APC-anti-mouse CD103 (1:250; Biolegend, US) on ice for 30 min prior to flow cytometry. For detection of NK cells and T lymphocytes, single cell suspensions were stained with FITC- or APC-cy7-anti-mouse CD45 (1:500, Biolegend, US), FITC-, APC- or PE-anti-mouse CD3 (1:500, eBioscience, US), APC-anti-mouse NK1.1 (1:500, eBioscience, US), APC- or PE-anti-mouse CD4 (1:500, eBioscience, US), BV421-anti-mouse-Foxp3(1:500; Biolegend), APC- or Percp-cy5.5-anti-mouse CD8α (1:500, Biolegend, US), PE-anti-mouse CD62L (1:500, eBioscience, US), FITC-anti-mouse CD44 (1:500, eBioscience, US), APC-cy7-anti-mouse-PD-1 (1:500; Biolegend, US), PE-Cy7-anti-mouse-Tim3 (1:500; Biolegend, US), PerCP-Cy5.5-anti-mouse-Lag3 (1:500; Biolegend, US) or PE-anti-mouse-CXCR3 (1:500; eBioscience, US) antibodies. After staining, cells were washed with FACS buffer for 3 times, followed by flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa cell analyzer, US). All data analyses were performed using FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc., Ashland, OR, US). Intracellular staining of IFN-γ, CXCL9 and Granzyme B was performed as per manufacturer’s instructions (BD Biosciences, US). Briefly, lymphocytes were collected from spleen, tumor tissues or peripheral blood of treated mice and transferred into U-bottom 96-well plates in 200 μL T cell culture medium (RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% PS and 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol), followed by addition of Ionomycin (400 ng/mL, Cayman, US) and Golgi Plug Protein Transport Inhibitor (1.5 μg/mL, Gogi stop, BD Biosciences, US) for 6 h to block cytokine secretion. Then, T cells and DCs were collected, washed and stained with FITC-anti-mouse CD3 (1:500, eBioscience, US) and Percp-cy5.5-anti-mouse CD8α (1:500, Biolegend, US) at 4°C for 30 min, respectively. Subsequently, cells were washed and fixed with Cytofix (BD Biosciences, US), followed by permeabilization with the permeabilization solution (BD Bioscences, US) for another 20 min at 4°C. Permeabilized cells were further stained with PE-cy7-anti-mouse IFN-γ antibody (1:500, eBioscience, US), PE-anti-mouse-CXCL9 (1:500; eBioscience, US) or APC-anti-mouse- Granzyme B (1:500; eBioscience, US), respectively, as per manufacturer’s instructions. Flow cytometric data were acquired by BD FACS Verse or LSR Fortessa (BD, US) and analyzed with FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC).

Cytokine release assay in vitro

Immune cytokines were detected by ELISA as described previously.6 Briefly, mouse blood or tumor tissues were harvested from treated orthotopic HCC mice 48 h after last immunization. Blood was centrifuged at 3,000 g for 30 min at RT, followed by analysis of IFN-γ and TGF-β with ELISA kits (eBioscience, US) as per manufacturer’s instructions, respectively. Tumor tissues (150 mg) were homogenized with the homogenizer and then subjected to centrifugation at 3,000 g for 30 min at 4°C, followed by analysis of IFN-γ and TGF-β with ELISA kits as described above.

Tetramer staining

Mouse CD8+ T cells were stained with antigen-specific tetramers as described previously.6 Briefly, AFP-, GPC3- and OVA-specific tetramers were generated as per manufacturer’s instructions (the QuickSwitchTM Quant H-2Kb tetramer kit, TB-7400 -K1, MBL, US) with AFP212 (GSMLNEHVM),60 GPC3 (AMFKNNYPSL)30 and OVA (SIINFEKL)61 (10 μM) epitopes, respectively. Lymphocytes derived from treated mice were treated with 50 nM dasatinib (HY-10181, MCE, US) for 30 min at 37°C, followed by washing with 1× washing buffer and stained with AFP-H-2Kb-tetramer-PE, GPC3-H-2Kb-tetramer-PE or OVA-H-2Kb-tetramer-PE (2 μg/mL) for 60 min at 4°C. Subsequently, tetramer-stained lymphocytes were counterstained with APC-cy7-CD45 (eBioscience, US), FITC-anti-mouse CD3 (eBioscience, US) and APC-anti-mouse CD8α (Biolegend, US) antibodies. Stained cells were subjected to flow cytometric analysis with BD LSRFortessa (BD, US) and analyzed by FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC).

Antigen cross-presentation assay

Measurement of antigen cross-presentation via MHC I on DCs was conducted as described previously.18 Briefly, immunocytes were harvested from spleen or tumor tissue of treated OVA-expressing orthotopic HCC mice 48 h after last injection. Immunocytes were washed with the FACS buffer (PBS containing 1% FBS and 2% EDTA) for 3 times, followed by staining with antibodies specific for APC-SIINFEKL-H-2kb including APC-cy7-anti-mouse CD45 (1:1000, Biolegend, US), PE-anti-mouse CD11c (1:500; Biolegend, US), FITC-anti-mouse-CD11b (1:1000, Biolegend, US), APC-Cy7-anti-mouse-CD8α (1:500; Biolegend, US), BV421-anti-mouse-XCR1 (1: 500; Biolegend, US), PerCP-Cy5.5-anti-mouse-CD103(1:250; Biolegend, US). Flow cytometric analysis was performed with LSRFortessa Cell Analyzer (BD Biosciences, US) and analyzed by FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC).

Antibody blocking

Day-7-subcutaneous HCC mice were treated with PBS or DEXPNX/LEN at the dose as described above. Mice were treated intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 200 μg per mouse of anti-CD8α (Bio-X-Cell, Lebanon, US) on days 31, 34, 36, 38, 41 and 44 post tumor inoculation. T cells were collected from peripheral blood, and CD8+ T cells, CD44+ or CD44+CD62L+ memory T cell subsets were measured on day 30 prior to antibody blocking and day 46 after antibody blocking post tumor inoculation by flow cytometry as described above.

ELISA for ICD effects

Serum was collected from eye fundus of subcutaneous HCC mice treated with single administration of Lenvatinib (30 mg/kg, oral gavage) or oxaliplatin (meilunbio, Dalian, China; 30 mg/kg; intraperitoneally)62 at day 0, 1, and 3 post-administrations. Serum was centrifuged at 3,000 g for 30 min at 4°C as described above. Levels of serum AFP, HMGB1 or α-IFN was assessed with mouse AFP ELISA kit (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), mouse HMGB1 ELISA Kit (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland) or mouse IFNα ELISA Kit (eBioscience, San Diego, US), respectively, as per manufacturer’s instructions.

Ca2+ flux assay

Ca2+ flux assay was performed as reported previously.23 Briefly, to isolate lymphocytes, inguinal lymph nodes of wild-type C57BL/6 mice were cut into small pieces and digested with Collagenase D (500 mg/mL) and DNase I (20 mg/mL, Roche, Mannheim, Germany) for 25 min at 37°C in RPMI 1640 containing 2% FBS (low endotoxin, Biochrom, Berlin, Germany). EDTA (10 mM) was added the digestion buffer for additional 5 min and cells were filtered through a 70-μm nylon sieve (BD Falcon). Lymphocytes were plated in 24-well plates (2 × 105 cells) and XCL1 recombinant protein (500 ng/mL; Peprotech, New Jersey, US), XCL1N1-7 (VGTEVLE), XCL1N1-25 (VGTE VLEESSCVNLQTQRLPCQKIK), XCL1N26-51(TYIIWEGAMRAVIFVTKRGLKICA DP) and XCL1N52-76 (EAKWVKAAIKTVDGRASTRKNMAET) (10 μg/mL) were added to lymphocytes, respectively, with Ionomycin (2 μg/mL; Cayman, US) and incubated for 6 h at 37°C and 5% CO2 in enzyme-free cell dissociation buffer (Thermo Fisher, MT, US) and resuspended in assay buffer (1xHBSS, 20 mM HEPES (pH7.4), and 0.1% BSA). Cells were incubated with 3 μM fura 2-AM fluorescence dye (Santa Cruz biotechnology, Texas, US) for 30 min at 37°C and 5% CO2, followed by washing with PBS for 2 times, and detection with flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa cell analyzer, US). Flow cytometric data were analyzed with FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC).

Chemotaxis assay

Chemotaxis assay was performed as reported previously.20 Briefly, lymphocytes (1 × 106) suspended in 100 μL chemotaxis medium (RPMI 1640, 1% BSA, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol, 100 mg/mL penicillin/streptomycin) were placed into the upper chamber of a 24-Transwell system (6.5 mm diameter, 5 μm pore polycarbonate membrane, Corning Costar Co., Acton, MA, US). The lower chamber was filled with chemotaxis medium containing different XCL1 peptides (10 μg/mL) or XCL1 recombinant protein (500 ng/mL) and cells were incubated for 2.5 h at 37°C and 5% CO2. Subsequently, cells in the lower chamber were analyzed by flow cytometry for the expression of CD45 and CD11c (DCs). The total cell number of migrated cells or the percentage of migrated DCs were determined by counting cells in a defined volume via a flow cytometer.

Toxicity assessment

Biochemical and histopathological analysis were performed as reported previously.41 Serum levels of alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine (SCR) were measured by automatic biochemistry analyzer (Sysmex BX-4000, Kobe, Japan). Heart, lung, liver, spleen or kidney were collected for histological examination and were fixed in 4% PFA and embedded in paraffin. Sections were subsequently prepared and stained with Hematoxylin-Eosin stain kit (#G1120, Solarbio, Beijing, China).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data are reported as mean values ±SEM. Statistical differences between treated and control groups were evaluated by SigmaStat (Systat Software, London, UK). Both parametric and nonparametric analyses were applied as specified in figure legends. Sample size was determined by G∗Power 3.1.7 (Power analysis and Sample size). Significance was determined based in p < 0.05.
