Co-creating the clinical pathway for Test it, a web-accessed sexually transmitted infections testing service
Authors: Ethan T Cardwell, Lauren Ware, Christopher K Fairley, Adrian Bickerstaffe, Danny Hancock, Rashidur Rahman, Fabian YS Kong, Jason J Ong, Jane Tomnay, Shanton Chang, Megan SC Lim, Jane S Hocking, Teralynn Ludwick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSexually transmitted infections (STIs) are continuing to rise in high-income countries, particularly among young adults. Increasing access to timely STI/HIV testing and treatment is key to reducing transmission. Web-accessed STI testing services have emerged as a pragmatic solution to improving access and uptake. However, these web-accessed alternatives do not always provide equivalent quality to in-person services. The scientific body of evidence around their development is limited and often not reported in detail, offering little guidance about design best practice. Translating a traditionally in-person service to a web-accessed one is a complex process that requires input from multiple stakeholders, including end users, clinical experts, and software developers, to support user acceptability, clinical safety, and technical feasibility. This manuscript outlines the evidence-based approach for establishing the clinical pathway for Test it, a statewide, publicly funded, web-accessed STI testing service targeting 16- to 29-year-olds in Victoria, Australia. We present the ten steps of the web-accessed clinical pathway and describe the iterative consultation and problem-solving process used to define them.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are a major public health concern globally, with more than one million curable infections acquired each day1. In many high-income countries, including Australia, STI notifications have risen substantially over the past decade. In Australia, gonorrhoea and syphilis diagnoses have more than doubled, and chlamydia remains the most commonly notified infection (>100,000 cases annually)2. While rates remain high among young people aged 15–29 years, increases are also evident in the broader population2. Recent epidemiological trends indicate persistently high rates of gonorrhoea and syphilis among men who have sex with men (MSM), alongside rising transmission within heterosexual populations2,3. Although significant progress has been made in reducing HIV transmission among men who have sex with men through pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) in Australia, rates have not reduced among heterosexual populations3. In response, STI/HIV guidelines in Australia and elsewhere now recommend comprehensive screening (chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis and HIV) for anyone requesting an STI test; previously, asymptomatic women and heterosexual men were routinely tested only for chlamydia and gonorrhoea4.

Health systems in Australia and elsewhere, however, are not well equipped to meet this growing demand. Access is constrained by limited specialist capacity, especially in rural and regional areas, and by barriers to seeking STI testing at mainstream primary care (general practice), including cost, privacy concerns, and anticipated stigma5,6. Because most STIs are asymptomatic, diagnosis depends on proactive health-seeking; embarrassment and confidentiality concerns and lack of understanding—particularly in rural and regional areas—deter many people, especially young people, from testing, leading to suboptimal testing coverage7,8.

Web-accessed STI testing services have emerged in high-income settings as a pragmatic response9. By enabling users to access testing outside traditional clinical settings, these services can reduce stigma and improve convenience and have been shown to increase testing uptake and case detection, particularly among younger populations10–13. These services are typically delivered via three main modalities: i) self-testing using point-of-care tests, where users collect their own specimens and interpret the result (analogous to COVID-19 rapid tests), is available in the United States and other countries, although regulatory approval varies across jurisdictions14–16; ii) mailed self-sampling kits, where users collect specimens and return them to a laboratory (typically by post), as used throughout the United Kingdom17,18 or iii) self-navigated pathology, where users receive an electronic pathology form to take to a pathology collection centre, as used in Canada’s GetCheckedOnline19,20. Each modality involves trade-offs in diagnostic accuracy, reach, cost, and integration with clinical care, with some modalities better suited to reaching large mainstream populations and others better at targeting harder-to-reach population groups.

Despite their promise, these services raise concerns about equity, quality, and continuity of care. Access may be limited for people with low digital or health literacy, poor internet access, or competing social disadvantages21–27. While web-accessed testing can reduce interpersonal stigma, it may introduce new concerns related to privacy, self-sampling, and managing results independently. Critically, linkage to treatment and follow-up remains challenging28. Although some web-accessed services offer an entirely online pathway, including for treatment and management (e.g., via mailing antibiotics to patients)29, many web-accessed services still require some in-person care, particularly for injections and the complex management of infections such as syphilis and HIV20.

The rapid expansion of web-accessed services, especially in the private sector, has outpaced regulatory and governance frameworks, leading to variable quality30,31. Using established appraisal tools (e.g. HONcode) to assess online information and alignment with national guidelines, Cardwell et al. found that many Australian web-accessed services prescribe inappropriate STI/BBV test panels, provide inadequate health education or counselling compared with an in-person consultation, and apply costs and eligibility criteria that limit equity, with private providers typically receiving lower assessments of quality31. These findings highlight the importance of robust clinical governance, alongside data security and quality management systems, to ensure that web-accessed services meet accepted standards of care.

In Australia, most web-accessed STI testing services are privately operated, and do not always provide comprehensive, guideline-concordant testing31. Public sexual health services are at capacity and not available in many areas. In Victoria (population ~7 million, ~5 million living in greater metropolitan region of Melbourne), rising notifications, changing epidemiology, and updated guidelines have intensified demand for accessible, comprehensive STI testing. To address these gaps, a publicly funded web-accessed STI/BBV testing service (Test it) is being developed by the Melbourne Sexual Health Centre (MSHC), Victoria’s only government-funded specialist sexual health clinic, using a self-navigated pathology model integrated with existing clinical systems. Test it aims to expand access to high-quality testing across metropolitan and regional settings while maintaining clinical oversight and continuity of care.

Although evidence supports the effectiveness and acceptability of web-accessed STI testing9,18, there is little guidance on how to systematically design and implement services that meet expected standards of care. Delivering care equivalent to in-person services is complex, requiring integration of clinical best practice, technical development and cybersecurity, and user-centred design. Studies from other fields highlight the importance of engaging end-users and other stakeholders to co-create digital health interventions32–34. However, existing literature focuses on outcomes rather than development processes, providing little guidance on how to integrate clinical, technical, regulatory, and user-centred requirements into a coherent model of care, constraining the ability to produce, evaluate, and scale high-quality digital health interventions.

In this study, we describe our structured approach to developing a web-accessed STI/BBV testing service (Test it) within the Australian public health system. We outline the development of the clinical pathway and the research, co-design, and stakeholder engagement processes used to ensure that the service is clinically appropriate, user-centred, and technically feasible. By making this process explicit, we aim to provide practical guidance for the development of high-quality, web-accessed health services and contribute to a more systematic evidence base for the development of digital STI testing interventions. Our future work will evaluate the effectiveness of the service in terms of user uptake and cost-effectiveness.

Results

Test it aims to provide best-practice STI/BBV testing and management for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis, HIV, and Hepatitis C to anyone 16 years of age or older, living in Victoria, Australia, and will use a ‘self-navigated pathology’ model for STI/BBV testing in which the user is issued with an electronic pathology form that they take to a pathology collection centre for testing. Users with the most common STIs (chlamydia, gonorrhoea) will be referred to local services for treatment and management of any STI/BBV detected, while the management of STIs requiring more complex care will be facilitated by MSHC. While anyone aged 16 years or older living in Victoria will be eligible to use Test it, we have initially designed the user interface to target young adults, aged 16 to 29 years, particularly those living in rural and regional areas of Victoria because of their high STI burden and well-documented barriers to accessing services8. This paper describes the co-creation of the client (user) interface. In parallel, we also developed an integrated client record management system to support seamless management of Test it and in-person clients at MSHC; however, this process is not reported here, as the focus of this manuscript is the user interface.

Defining the functional units of the clinical pathway for Test it

A traditional in-person clinical pathway involves numerous steps, from presentation at a clinic through to consultation with a clinician, investigation, management, and follow-up. We utilised Gibbs et al.’s eClinical Care Pathway Framework35 to guide the translation of the in-person service to a digital one, requiring a fundamental resequencing of these ‘steps’ (known as ‘functional units’). We broke down the in-person clinical pathway into eight discrete steps (functional units). In translating the functional units of the in-person clinical pathway to a web-accessed one, we needed to adapt the sequencing of the units and add two additional functions to support the overall process. Figure 1 illustrates how the functional units of an in-person clinical pathway align with the functional units of the Test it web-accessed clinical pathway. It is important to note that both web-accessed and in-person clinical consultations are captured within a single electronic patient record at the MSHC, a major strength of the service as it reduces fragmentation of care and supports integrated care between online and clinic-based service provision.

What each service provides is equivalent, however the steps vary between the two pathways. The major difference in the pathways is sequencing, particularly in relation to health promotion and user feedback. The dotted arrows illustrate where in-person clinical pathway units occur in Test it.

As a web-accessed service, several functional units were fully automated. For example, in functional unit 3— consultation—the user’s relevant health history is obtained via an online, self-administered questionnaire, and a computer algorithm uses this information to determine which STI/BBV tests are ordered. The relevant health history information also determines which health promotion messages are displayed online for the user. We included two new functional units (eligibility, user feedback) for the Test it clinical pathway, and in contrast with the traditional care pathway, we integrated the ‘health promotion’ function and the ‘user feedback’ functions at various points throughout the clinical pathway. Partner notification is included in Test it as part of the health promotion functional unit which provides individualised recommendations on sexual health, including resources on how to notify sexual contacts.

Determining the scope and specifications for each functional unit

The scope and specifications for each functional unit were defined in ongoing consultation with our expert working groups of end-users, clinicians, informatics specialists, and software developers, in an iterative process in which we re-visited design decisions for each functional unit and engaged our stakeholders numerous times over the 3-year period of development. We consulted these stakeholders to find a balance between achieving high accessibility and acceptability for end users, while considering the regulatory and clinical requirements. In addition to clinical requirements, we had to ensure Test it was compatible with the existing infrastructure and software development capabilities at MSHC. The decision-making and relative contributions of each working group are described in the sections below, with an overview presented in Table 1.

Functional unit 1: Eligibility

The function of the Eligibility unit is to determine if potential users are eligible to use the service before registering for an account.

Input from stakeholders

Rather than beginning the process by registering (creating) a Test it account as we had originally envisaged, end users recommended the service include a new functional unit on ‘eligibility’ as the first step. Users wanted to determine if they were eligible to use the service, so that they could avoid wasting time on registering if they were ineligible.

Originally, our service was intended for asymptomatic users only, as is common with many online services. Symptoms such as rashes, lumps, sores, and pain are hard to assess without clinical examination and may require investigation. Consequently, users with these symptoms are typically ineligible to use web-accessed services and are recommended to consult a doctor. However, the clinical working group noted that reporting any ‘discharge’ (which is common for a range of reasons) would unwarrantedly screen out a large percentage of potential users. They recommended that Test it could support wider access, particularly for rural and regional users, by allowing those experiencing vaginal or urethral/penile discharge to use the service, without compromising medical responsibility.

Outcome

Several screening questions are included before registration to determine the user’s eligibility, including establishing whether the user: i) resides in the service catchment area (Victoria or bordering towns with reciprocal health funding arrangements); ii) meets the age requirements (16+), and iii) does not have complicated symptoms necessitating a clinical examination. These screening questions precede registration so that no client data are captured until after the user registers, supporting privacy and reducing the volume of data captured from incomplete accounts. Our service will include tools to locate clinical services, should users be ineligible for Test it. Figure 2 provides an example of the digital screens users are shown to determine eligibility.

These are prototype mockups of the screens shown to users to determine Test it eligibility.

Questions will be shown to users one at a time with built-in logic determining if they are eligible only after they have completed all questions. Users will first be asked if they are experiencing any of the more complicated symptoms of an STI (e.g., rash, lumps or sores, anal soreness, pelvic pain) and if yes, they will not be able to use the service. Next, users will be asked if they are experiencing the less complicated symptom of urethral/vaginal discharge. They will then be asked about their age and current state residence.

Functional Unit 2: account creation and consent

The function of the Account creation and consent unit is to capture relevant data required to: i) create a unique user record, and ii) obtain user consent to collect and use personal data.

Input from stakeholders

Given that this is a web-accessed service, user consent needs to be officially recorded. In the workshops with young people, there was concern about privacy and confidentiality, which encouraged us to provide clear statements around privacy and data usage as part of the consent process. Creating a client record with MSHC was deemed important by the clinical working group to support integrated record keeping for clients who use both online and in-person services at MSHC, and by the technical working group to avoid issues with duplication and fragmented medical history records. Importantly, because of past research highlighting that users prefer not to have sexual health apps installed on their phones, the clinical working group recommended that Test it be a web-accessed application (accessed via a browser), rather than a downloadable application (‘app’).

Outcome

Before registering an account, users must indicate their consent to the creation of a client record at MSHC and the use of deidentified data for evaluation and research purposes. Mobile phone number and date of birth will be used as unique identifiers for the purposes of user login. These fields will be cross-checked with MSHC data management systems to reduce duplication of existing records. Users’ mobile number will be used for multi-factor authentication to gain access to their account. We considered other options for unique identification, like email, but ultimately decided to use mobile number because it was an essential element of the service for communicating notifications and results by SMS.

Functional Unit 3: consultation

The function of this unit is to obtain sexual and relevant health history for each user. Users complete an online, self-administered questionnaire to determine which STI/BBV tests should be prescribed and to provide individualised health promotion messages.

Input from stakeholders

As this is not an in-person service, and because there will be no opportunity for follow-up clarification questions, we needed to make sure we asked questions in a way that was clear, concise and unambiguous. In the workshops, young people expressed they were happy to answer any necessary questions (though sensitive), but wanted more information about why each question was being asked. The clinical working group raised the importance of identifying specific exposures among end-users that may place them at increased risk of HIV or syphilis (to offer prophylactic treatment), as well as to screen for Hepatitis C (which we had not originally included in tests). Both the clinical and end-user working groups raised concerns that online services can miss opportunities for health promotion and linking to other appropriate services and resources.

Outcome

The online consultation includes questions related to sex, gender, sexual activity (including anal sex) and relevant health history, including injection drug use, to determine which STI/BBV tests should be prescribed for each user. STI/BBV tests include non-genital specimens, i.e., oral and anorectal swabs. All tests prescribed will be consistent with the Australian STI Management Guidelines36. In addition, the consultation will ask questions about specific exposures, for example, to syphilis and HIV, and questions around topics such as vaccines, contraception, and use of prevention methods like HIV PrEP to provide the user with tailored recommendations and health promotion messages (Functional Unit 9). To address end-users’ appetite to understand why some questions were asked, we will also include a ‘tool tip’ (a clickable icon) for each question, providing explanatory information in a graphical overlay.

Functional Units 4 and 5: electronic pathology test request and sample collection

The function of the Electronic pathology test request and Sample collection units is to provide users with an individualised, electronic, pathology test request form to be taken to a pathology collection centre in person, where samples will be collected for testing at an accredited laboratory. The prescribed tests are generated based on answers to questions in Unit 3: Consultation.

Input from stakeholders

End users requested further information about how to find a pathology collection centre and what they could expect when attending the centre, including how specimens will be collected. The clinical working group suggested that we provide education and training to pathology centre staff to increase their understanding about STI/BBV testing and reduce any barriers that users might face, such as stigma and discrimination,

Outcome

Based on their answers to the online consultation, users will be provided with a downloadable, electronic pathology request form that orders appropriate STI/BBV tests. We will provide users with an online search tool to identify the location and operating hours of pathology collection centres throughout Victoria. On the Test it website, we will develop an instructional video and provide information about what happens at a collection centre and how to collect specimens. Future work, outside the scope of this development process, may involve working with pathology providers to develop resources to support their staff in serving clients who present for STI testing.

Functional Unit 6: results

The function of the Results unit is to process the STI/BBV test results, communicate them to users and arrange re-testing for those with invalid or unsatisfactory test results. All STI/BBV test results will be sent electronically from the pathology laboratory to MSHC. Users view their results by logging in to Test it.

Input from stakeholders

In the end-user workshops, young people raised concerns about the possibility of bystanders inadvertently seeing results messages if communicated by SMS. Separately, the clinician working group raised the importance of minimising public records where HIV status is stored to ensure user safety and confidentiality. The need for a process to manage invalid results (typically due to errors in sample collection or laboratory processing) was also raised by the clinician working group.

Outcome

Users will be sent a generic SMS notifying them to log into their Test it account. No information about test results will be provided in the SMS. Once logged in, users will see their results for all tests (except HIV) posted as follows: pending, detected, not detected, or needs repeating. No result other than ‘not detected’ will be displayed for HIV. As communication of STI results can be sensitive, different communication channels have been developed in consultation with the clinical working group’s advice and best practice. Given the clinical significance and complexity of managing a syphilis, Hepatitis C or HIV infection, users with one of these infections detected will be contacted directly by an MSHC nurse via a phone call, who will facilitate treatment and case management. Chlamydia and gonorrhoea test results (detected and not detected) will be communicated to users via the web-accessed portal. In cases where invalid results are returned, users will receive a link to a new electronic pathology request to repeat that specific test. Repeating the specific test is designed to minimise wasteful ordering, an important principle encouraged by the clinical working group. For most results, users will receive an automated SMS message notifying them that the results are available.

When users log into their Test it account, they will be able to see a full list of results for all tests conducted and view treatment options for those detected. A clinic nurse will notify users with a positive syphilis or HIV result by phone call.

Functional Unit 7: treatment

The function of the Treatment unit is to support users in navigating options for treatment.

Input from stakeholders

While digital or e-prescriptions were considered a user-friendly means to reduce barriers to accessing treatment, they were not possible due to regulatory constraints around issuing electronic prescriptions in the absence of a ‘real-time’ doctor consultation. During the workshops, end users noted that many young people may be unsure about where to get treatment and suggested we provide tools on how to locate providers. As this is a web-accessed service that provides access to testing across Victoria, users may not be able to attend MSHC for treatment. Therefore, Test it will need to assist users in identifying local services to access treatment. The clinical working group noted the importance of supporting client choice, minimising time-to-treatment, and supporting rational distribution of care between specialist and generalist providers in different geographic locations. This group recommended providing weblinks to a network of primary care clinics that have received sexual health training. The training is part of a Victorian state government-funded initiative to expand the availability of sexual healthcare across Victoria.

Outcome

Test it will provide users with a range of options for obtaining treatment and issue them with a referral letter to bring to a provider of their choice. Treatment options depend on the infection detected, based on the relative simplicity or complexity of diagnosis and treatment:

Chlamydia detected: users will be provided with a downloadable referral letter. They will have two options to access treatment: i) use Test it to access a telehealth provider who can provide an e-prescription for chlamydia treatment, ii) use Test it to locate a suitable health service for an in-person consultation to obtain a prescription for chlamydia

Chlamydia detected: users will be provided with a downloadable referral letter. They will have two options to access treatment: i) use Test it to access a telehealth provider who can provide an e-prescription for chlamydia treatment, ii) use Test it to locate a suitable health service for an in-person consultation to obtain a prescription for chlamydia

Gonorrhoea detected: user receives a downloadable referral letter and use Test it to locate a suitable health service for an in-person consultation to obtain treatment

Gonorrhoea detected: user receives a downloadable referral letter and use Test it to locate a suitable health service for an in-person consultation to obtain treatment

Syphilis, Hepatitis C or HIV detected: MSHC will telephone users to arrange in-person treatment, as it is particularly important that these STIs be managed sensitively and in the case of syphilis and Hepatitis C, to determine if the detected result is based on a current or past infection

Syphilis, Hepatitis C or HIV detected: MSHC will telephone users to arrange in-person treatment, as it is particularly important that these STIs be managed sensitively and in the case of syphilis and Hepatitis C, to determine if the detected result is based on a current or past infection

To assist users in locating a service for treatment, we will link users to a state-managed sexual health service locator tool and provide links to primary care clinics participating in a government-funded initiative to provide sexual health services in rural, regional and disadvantaged areas. These clinics have been trained to provide safe, non-judgemental service to clients—a concern especially noted in rural and regional areas. We worked with these clinics to develop the downloadable referral letters for clients requiring treatment. The letters can be used at any clinic and contain sufficient clinical information to allow the clinicians to provide the appropriate treatment and management based on the referral letter alone.

Functional Unit 8: follow-up and reminder system

The function of the Follow-up Unit is to provide a systematic reminder and recall process for users with positive results who require further action, including re-testing.

Input from stakeholders

For Test it, we needed to determine the optimal way to adhere to clinical recommendations for re-testing. Some in the clinical group raised concerns about over-testing by sending non-guideline adherent testing reminders, leading to health resource wastage, while young people in the end-user workshops expressed concern that reminder messages could be perceived as an annoyance and/or invasion of privacy. At the same time, this group was concerned that for STIs where treatment needs to be self-navigated (namely chlamydia and gonorrhoea) supplementary efforts may be needed to contact and alert Test it users of positive results, if these individuals fail to log back into the system to view their results after being tested.

Outcome

To be consistent with the Australian STI management guidelines, only individuals who self-identify their sex as female and receive a chlamydia detected test result will be offered SMS reminders for retesting using an opt-in check box. In addition, we developed a quality assurance mechanism in which reminder SMS messages are sent to users who have not logged in to Test it to view their positive chlamydia or gonorrhoea results, and for clinical nurses to follow up with these individuals if their results continue to go unopened for a specified period of time.

Functional Unit 9: health promotion and partner notification

The function of the Health promotion and partner notification unit is to provide users with health information and recommendations, including partner notification, both general and individualised.

Input from stakeholders

Young people and clinicians raised a key gap in the clinical pathway—the lack of opportunity for health education and health promotion that is normally conducted as part of an in-person consultation. They strongly recommended that the service provide health recommendations and resources for users. Clinicians noted that the consultation also serves as a point to check-in with broader issues affecting a user’s health and well-being.

Outcome

Users will receive health promotion messages or recommendations at multiple points in the clinical pathway, and these will be personalised based on their responses to the online consultation (Functional Unit 3). We will provide three different types of health information i) time-sensitive information about prophylactic treatment available with a specified timeframe of a recent exposure (e.g. recommendations to consider HIV PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis for HIV) or emergency contraception for an individual with female anatomy); ii) health recommendations related to vaccines (Hepatitis A, B), contraception, and HIV PrEP; iii) links to mental health, sexuality, domestic violence, and other relevant support services, and iv) links to online partner notification services to inform sexual partners when an infection is detected. An example of a health promotional message provided to the user is shown in Fig. 4. Given that the MSHC already hosts several web resources for supporting partner notification, links to these existing resources were included.

At multiple points in the clinical pathway, users will be provided with health promotion messaging.

These messages can be general or tailored to the individual. Some messages, like for emergency contraception, are time sensitive and will be displayed as urgent to users.

Functional Unit 10: user feedback and outcomes

This User feedback and outcomes unit serves to provide data on user experience (including capturing negative experiences), and data for research and evaluation purposes.

Input from stakeholders: Both the technical and clinical working groups raised the importance of collecting data from users about the quality and acceptability of Test it, noting the failure of many publicly provided health services to provide an accessible avenue to actively solicit user feedback. Both groups also raised the importance of decoupling service records from user feedback responses for confidentiality purposes.

Outcome: Anonymous, optional, user feedback surveys will be disseminated by SMS at multiple points along the clinical pathway to capture a range of monitoring, evaluation, and research objectives. This includes collecting feedback on the digital interface, understanding users’ experiences (including negative experiences), and capturing user treatment outcomes. These satisfaction surveys will be used throughout the evaluation process to monitor the effectiveness of the service and inform future modifications.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the utility of using a framework to guide a systematic approach for translating an in-person client pathway to a web-accessible one. It highlights the value of diverse stakeholder input, including from end-users, clinicians, and informatics specialists, in contributing to design decisions. Developed over a 3-year period, the key features of our online STI testing service, Test it, include 10 functional units: 1) eligibility, 2) account creation and consent, 3) consultation, 4) electronic pathology request, 5) sample collection, 6) results, 7) treatment and 8) follow up, with 9) health promotion and partner notification and 10) user feedback and outcomes woven throughout the pathway. Test it differs from the traditional composition and sequencing of functional units in an in-person clinical pathway by including two additional functional units (eligibility, user feedback surveys), which perform the functions of pre-screening those eligible to use the service and collecting feedback. These are crucial elements to capture information about harms and negative experiences while guiding the future refinement of the service24. Our inclusion of diverse stakeholders contributed to improved design, including modifications to support ease-of-use, clinical best practice, integration with an in-person clinical service (MSHC), and data security.

Although the steps in Gibbs et al.’s eClinical Care Pathway Framework are presented sequentially, suggesting a linear process, we employed a highly iterative process in which we re-visited design decisions for each functional unit and engaged our stakeholders numerous times over the 3-year period. Engaging with users and other stakeholders at multiple points allowed us to affirm changes we made, including when changes in one functional unit affected design decisions in another unit. The iterative approach also helped us ensure best clinical practice and data practice were upheld as adjustments were made. Our stakeholder engagement process led to several important design choices and changes including: the addition of pre-screening eligibility questions and service locator tools to improve useability and accessibility; the recommendation for a more expansive approach to the provision of sexual health promotion messages; the decision to expand the service’s scope to include some symptomatic individuals and testing for Hepatitis C to improve access and comprehensiveness; and the inclusion of security measures like multi-factor authentication to ensure privacy for users.

Actively engaging users and stakeholders in the design and development of digital health services can improve its acceptability and usability32–34. However, it is important to acknowledge that policy and regulatory requirements, and other contextual factors, may limit design options. In many cases, there may be a need to place boundaries around what features can be considered. For example, we initially considered using postal kits rather than requiring users to attend a pathology collection centre for specimen collection. This was not feasible in our setting due to regulations on the posting of biological specimens, lack of accredited tests in Australia for some self-collected blood specimens, lower return rates, and the postal cost15,18. Additionally, we had hoped to include automatically generated, electronic scripts for individuals testing positive for chlamydia. However, during the Test it design period, new Australian guidelines were introduced prohibiting the issuing of electronic scripts in the absence of a real-time consultation with a clinician. As such, we pivoted to referring users for treatment to other services, either in person or via telehealth.

It is important to note that not all elements of service provision within a digital service can be automated, and that a clinic-based workforce is still required to deliver some components of the service. Where it is feasible to offer web-accessed treatment options, we have done so to streamline management for both users and the MSHC. For example, chlamydia, which can be effectively treated with oral antibiotics, is well suited to telehealth, allowing an electronic prescription to be issued without the need for an in-person visit. However, management of syphilis, gonorrhoea and HIV requires treatment administration in person by a health professional. Given the clinical significance of HIV and syphilis, clients testing positive for these infections will be contacted by MSHC who will facilitate access to treatment. In contrast, those testing positive for gonorrhoea will be provided with referral letters and links to primary care clinics providing sexual health services where they can seek treatment. We acknowledge that there is a risk that these individuals may not access treatment, but Test it will use a system of text messages to remind clients of the importance of being treated. Test it will use an electronic patient record that is integrated with MSHC capturing the user’s interactions with Test it and MSHC. The integrated client record will be particularly valuable for clients who use both Test it and MSHC—a group expected to grow as asymptomatic clients will be redirected from MSHC to Test it in the future— and will facilitate smooth transitions between the two services.

Our design, like other publicly funded projects, needed to be executed within a defined budget. This places constraints on the scope of Test it’s features and capabilities. The first iteration of Test it will only be available in English and targeted to those aged 16-29. This will likely have an impact on equity, although anyone aged 16 years or older can use the service. We do not have the resources to develop alternative public-facing websites for different demographic groups in the first instance. Websites with in-language resources are important for attracting those born overseas, while the inclusion of artwork designed by and for Australian Aboriginal people is known to be important for engendering trust and a sense of safety in health services37,38. We acknowledge the implications of these design limitations on the potential for equitable Test it access. Literature also indicates that digital health services can further exacerbate inequities among socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals, those with disabilities, cultural or ethnic minorities with lower levels of English proficiency, and those with low digital and health literacy, thus limiting access to Test it by these groups21–27. While Test it is likely to improve access to STI testing and management for many individuals living in rural and regional areas and will meet the testing needs of men who have sex with men who are already familiar with STI testing, it may not be appropriate for all those at risk of STIs. Additional strategies will be required to support certain population groups for whom STIs are of increasing concern, such as, for example, syphilis control in the subset of at-risk pregnant women who have limited contact with the health system39.

It is also worthy of comment that there is a growing debate about the frequency of asymptomatic STI screening, particularly gonorrhoea and chlamydia, in response to concern about antimicrobial resistance40, and some may question whether it is appropriate to make it easier to have asymptomatic STI testing where there is less opportunity to inform clients about antimicrobial resistance. However, the need for increased availability of syphilis and HIV testing will continue regardless of whether or not asymptomatic chlamydia and gonorrhoea testing is recommended.

In light of the limited literature describing the design and development of web-accessed STI testing services, this manuscript describes how we developed the eClinical Pathway for Test it, rather than presenting the impact of Test it on testing rates and positive diagnoses. We anticipate implementing Test it in late 2026 and a future manuscript will describe our evaluation protocol. Our evaluation will capture both qualitative and quantitative data, and will explore benefits (e.g., acceptability, test uptake [implications for equity and inclusion], infections detected and cost-effectiveness).

While Test it design decisions are tailored for the Australian context, our process of using the eClinical Care Pathway Framework combined with diverse stakeholder engagement provides a universal scaffolding upon which other web-accessed STI testing services can be designed and developed. This scaffolding will help produce services that are supported by end-users, clinically sound, and technically feasible to implement in the context for which they are intended.

Methods

Overview of the development of the clinical pathway

Developing a web-accessed service that provides best practice clinical care consistent with traditional, in-person services can be challenging. Innovative strategies are required to ensure the web-accessed service offers equivalent quality. Given the complexity of ensuring end-user acceptability, clinical safety, service quality, and technical feasibility, we used the eClinical Care Pathway Framework developed by Gibbs and colleagues to guide our approach35. The framework provides a comprehensive, nine-step structure for translating a traditional clinical pathway into a web-accessed one (Table 2). Although the Gibbs et al. framework is presented as a sequence of discrete steps, our use of it was highly iterative and not always linear. Figure 5 illustrates how we used the framework. Feedback from stakeholders and the consultative processes described in later sections led us to move back and forth between steps, revisiting and modifying earlier steps (for example, revising consultation questions and interface design) as new insights emerged.

Formative research

We conducted several formative research studies to understand limitations in current web-accessed services; youth preferences for online STI testing; suitability of a self-navigated pathology service for specimen collection (particularly for those in rural and regional communities); and clinician requirements to be part of a decentralised treatment pathway for online clients who test positive for STIs. Our studies included:

A systematic review of existing web-accessed STI services in Australia, in which we developed a de novo framework for assessing service quality, service accessibility (cost, time from testing to treatment) and website reliability31. We found gaps in service quality, including not obtaining a full client history, offering tests that are not recommended by Australian STI management guidelines36, and limited accessibility in rural and regional areas. Overall, online services using a self-navigated pathology model tended to be higher quality and more accessible31.

Establishing working groups–stakeholder engagement

We identified three key stakeholder groups to engage—end-users (young people), clinical experts, and technical experts—and established working groups based on these stakeholder groups. The working groups were consulted throughout the development process. The research team facilitated all working group meetings to ensure continuity across service design and share insights between groups. The Clinical Working Group comprised sexual health nurses, physicians, and clinical researchers, representatives from public health networks (government-funded networks providing strategic support and oversight of local public health priorities across Victoria), and representatives from those with experience developing web-accessed STI testing services in other contexts. Representatives were recruited from across Victoria, including those based in rural/regional areas. This group met regularly to provide feedback on the service’s scope and services to be offered, strategies to support best clinical practice, quality and safety in service delivery, and processes to manage access to treatment. The group made key contributions to steps 1–4 of the eClinical Care Pathway Framework, including establishing the necessary functional units for the service and reviewing the questions to be included as part of the online consultation.

The User Engagement Working Group consisted of researchers, a third-party graphic design company, and representatives of non-government and government organisations focusing on youth and equity, including regional youth, culturally and linguistically diverse youth, and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander youth. Contributing primarily to Steps 1-4 of the eClinical Care Pathway Framework, this working group met frequently during the early stages of project development to inform inclusive, youth-oriented design (see section on Workshops with young people). This working group was crucial in developing workshop materials, informing strategies to help us recruit a diverse range of young people to participate in co-creation workshops. The working group also helped create proposals for inclusive language and connections to broader health promotion materials.

The Technical Working Group included the third-party software developer, a range of informatics specialists, data business analysts, technology managers and cyber security analysts from the research team and Melbourne Sexual Health Centre. This group was responsible for specifying the software build requirements (Steps 7 and 8) and ensuring that the technical design was compatible with end-user preferences, clinical requirements, and cybersecurity best practices. More specifically, this group was responsible for i) translating the clinical pathway into technical requirements; ii) developing the backend logic and algorithms (the data and infrastructure to make the web-accessed application function) to trigger Test it functionality including generating the electronic pathology test request, SMS test result prompts and reminders and information ‘pop-ups’ (notifications that automatically appear on the users screen); iii) ensuring best practice for data security including user privacy and confidentiality, and; iv) working with the clinical working group to ensure Test it integrates seamlessly with the MSHC client management software. Members of this working group attended meetings from the other working groups on demand, ensuring that technical feasibility was considered throughout the design process, rather than solely at the end phase.

Workshops with young people

End-users (youth aged 18–9) were engaged in a set of three workshops held across three geographic locations, facilitated by a specialist design firm (PaperGiant.com.au). Details about the workshop methodology have been previously published45, but in brief, these workshops aimed to work with young people to design the Test it interface and service functionality. The workshops explored branding and messaging (e.g., tone of voice, imagery) and key service functions (e.g., account creation, online questionnaire, treatment pathways) using interactive activities. Based on the workshops, the design firm constructed wireframes, i.e., a basic skeleton or visual blueprint of a digital product’s layout, functionality and structure. One-on-one interviews with 10 individuals were then conducted to gain feedback on the wireframes (step 6), including feedback on the wording and sequencing of the questions that formed the clinical consultation (online questionnaire) to determine which STI/HIV tests should be prescribed. Using an iterative process, feedback from the workshops and interviews was conveyed to the working groups for refinement of steps 2–3. While aesthetics and messaging fall outside the scope of Gibb’s 9-step framework, we note the important addition of this contribution to the larger design process and its importance in creating a usable product.

Process for implementing insights and recommendations

Working groups were the primary forums for decision-making about Test it’s design. A core team from the research group and MSHC facilitated the development process, with members embedded in all working groups, involved in each research study, and present at the youth workshops. This positioning enabled them to play a strategic role in synthesising and sharing insights from all consultative activities, supporting real-time decisions within each working group, shaping practical implementation strategies, and iteratively refining the service as new considerations emerged.
