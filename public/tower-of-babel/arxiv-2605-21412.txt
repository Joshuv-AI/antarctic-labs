A biquaternionic reformulation of Maxwell’s equations via Fourier analysis
Aarón Guillén-Villalobos, Briceyda B. Delgado, Héctor Vargas Rodríguez

Abstract
We analyze the parabolic Dirac operator D ± i ∂ t D\pm i\partial_{t} in a biquaternionic setting, characterizing its kernel via generalized div-curl systems and Cauchy-Riemann-type relations between the real and imaginary parts. Using the machinery provided by the Fourier transform, we fully characterize the Fourier transform of a fundamental solution of this operator and construct a well-defined right inverse operator. As an application, we derive explicit vectorial solutions to the time-dependent Maxwell system, extending prior biquaternionic approaches. These tools offer analytical efficiency for complex electromagnetic problems.



Keywords:
Maxwell’s system, Fourier transform, wave equation, parabolic Dirac

operators.

MSC Classification:
42A38, 47S02, 35Q61, 30G35

Introduction

In this work, we employ the following factorization of the wave operator

-\Delta+\partial_{tt}=(D+i\partial_{t})(D-i\partial_{t})=(D-i\partial_{t})(D+i\partial_{t}),

where
i
is the imaginary unit and
D
is the Moisil-Teodorescu operator. We work in a biquaternionic setting and follow closely the previous results documented in the monograph
[
18
]
. Other first-order perturbed Dirac operators have been reported in the literature for analyzing and factorizing the wave equation within Clifford algebra frameworks; see, for instance,
[
12
,
10
,
11
,
21
]
. In
[
17
]
, the authors showed that any solution of the Klein-Gordon equation can be represented through two different solutions of parabolic Dirac equations with the same mass, and in
[
3
]
, the authors studied a general scheme for factorizing second-order time-dependent operators of mathematical physics. In both works, the key point is the reduction of second-order equations into first-order biquaternionic equations.

In this manuscript, we develop fundamental properties of the parabolic Dirac operators
D\pm i\partial_{t}
. Instead of seeking a fundamental solution
\Phi_{\pm}
of
D\pm i\partial_{t}
, we fully characterize its Fourier transform through the exponential quaternionic function. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left(\Phi_{\pm}\right)(k,t)=\exp\left(\mp 2\pi kt\right)=\cos\left(2\pi|k|t\right)\mp\frac{k}{|k|}\sin\left(2\pi|k|t\right),

for all
(k,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, using properties of the Fourier transform and the quaternionic exponential function, we propose a right inverse operator for these parabolic Dirac operators. As an application, we analyze the time-dependent Maxwell system. Quaternionic and biquaternionic analysis has been previously applied to such physical systems. For the time-harmonic Maxwell system, a series of works
[
19
,
5
,
6
]
adopt a biquaternionic approach. In particular,
[
5
,
6
]
employ classical integral operators in quaternionic analysis, along with a completion monogenic process that generalizes the classical harmonic conjugate concept from complex analysis.

The Maxwell equations hold importance across many fields of science and applied mathematics. For example,
[
23
,
22
]
use invariants of the electromagnetic
2
-form to classify electromagnetic fields and study their propagation, while
[
25
]
examines stationary electromagnetic fields with angular momentum via the Poynting vector.

Fourier analysis serves as a powerful tool not only in physics but also in digital image processing, signal processing, and cryptography. For instance,
[
4
]
applies it as an alternative method for solving the heat equation by replacing the spatial derivatives with Fourier-space variables, thereby transforming the partial differential equation into a simpler linear differential equation. In
[
1
]
, the authors use the Fourier transform to recast Maxwell’s equations into a system of dot and cross products, which changes both the space and time partial derivatives to the Fourier space variables.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we recall definitions of biquaternionic algebra along with basic properties of the Dirac operator
D
and the Moisil–Teodorescu system. In Section
3
, we derive preliminary results characterizing the kernel of
(D+i\partial_{t})
. Proposition
4
establishes that the kernel of
D\pm i\partial_{t}
corresponds to a generalized div-curl system:
w=u+iv=(u_{0}+\vec{u})+i(v_{0}+\vec{v})\in\text{Ker}(D\pm i\partial_{t})
if and only if

\begin{array}[]{rcl}-\mbox{div}\,(\vec{u})&=&\pm\partial_{t}v_{0},\\
\nabla u_{0}+\mbox{curl}\,(\vec{u})&=&\pm\partial_{t}\vec{v},\\
-\mbox{div}\,(\vec{v})&=&\mp\partial_{t}u_{0},\\
\nabla v_{0}+\mbox{curl}\,(\vec{v})&=&\mp\partial_{t}\vec{u}.\end{array}

Meanwhile, Proposition
5
shows that if
u
is a complex-valued function satisfying the wave equation, we can construct a quaternion-valued function

such that
u\pm iv
solves
(D\pm i\partial_{t})
. In Section
4
, we construct a right inverse operator for
D+i\partial_{t}
(see Theorem
10

L^{2}(\mathscr{C},\mathbb{B})
. Finally, in Section
5
, we apply the right inverse of the perturbed Dirac operators
D\pm i\partial_{t}
to solve the time-dependent Maxwell system in differential form and Cartesian coordinates. In Subsection
5.1
, we generalize the results obtained in previous sections. We do a similar analysis for the operator
D\pm i\lambda\partial_{t}
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

Preliminaries

Let us consider the algebra of biquaternions and quaternions, denoted by
\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{H}
, respectively. Let
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{B}
, we will denote by
u_{i},v_{i}\in\mathbb{H}
to their respective real and imaginary parts,
i=1,2
. That is,

w_{1}=u_{1}+iv_{1},\hskip 10.00002ptw_{2}=u_{2}+iv_{2}.

Following the classical complex rule multiplication, then

\displaystyle w_{1}w_{2}

\displaystyle=(u_{1}+iv_{1})(u_{2}+iv_{2})=u_{1}u_{2}-v_{1}v_{2}+i(u_{1}v_{2}+v_{1}u_{2}),

where
u_{1}u_{2},v_{1}v_{2},u_{1}v_{2}
and
v_{1}u_{2}
follow the classical quaternionic multiplication.

Let us denote the scalar part and vector part of
u
by
u_{0}=\mbox{Sc}\,{u}
and
\vec{u}=\mbox{Vec}\,{u}
, respectively. Let
w=u+iv\in\mathbb{B}
, then

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mbox{Sc}\,(w)&=&\mbox{Sc}\,(u)+i\mbox{Sc}\,(v)&=&u_{0}+iv_{0},\\
\mbox{Vec}\,(w)&=&\mbox{Vec}\,(u)+i\mbox{Vec}\,(v)&=&\vec{u}+i\vec{v}.\end{array}

Now, let us denote by
\overline{w}
the quaternionic conjugation, and
w^{*}
stands for the usual complex conjugation. Indeed,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\overline{w}=(u_{0}-\vec{u})+i(v_{0}-\vec{v})=(u_{0}+iv_{0})-(\vec{u}+i\vec{v}),\quad w^{*}=(u_{0}+\vec{u})-i(v_{0}+\vec{v}).\end{array}

From now on,
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
will be a bounded domain. We will denote the space-time domain by
\mathscr{C}:=\Omega\times(0,\infty)
,
C^{2,2}(\mathscr{C},\mathcal{B})
will be the space of functions with continuous second-order spatial and temporal partial derivatives defined in
\mathscr{C}
and taking values on
\mathcal{B}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{C}^{3},\mathbb{H},\mathbb{B}\}
, and
C^{1,1}(\mathscr{C},\mathcal{B})
will denote the space of functions whose first-order spatial and temporal partial derivatives are continuous. Throughout this work, we will work with biquaternionic-valued functions. That is,
w:\mathscr{C}\to\mathbb{B}
, and
w
has the form

w=\sum_{k=0}^{3}e_{i}w_{i}=\underbrace{u}_{\text{Re}\left(w\right)}+i\underbrace{v}_{\text{Im}\left(w\right)},

(1)

where
w_{i}:\mathscr{C}\to\mathbb{C}
,
i=0,1,2,3
, are complex-valued functions, and
u,v:\mathscr{C}\to\mathbb{H}
are quaternionic-valued functions.

Recall that the definition of a
L^{p}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is defined as all the set of functions
w
with domain
\mathcal{A}
and taking values on
\mathcal{B}
, where
\mathcal{A}\in\{\mathscr{C},\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty)\}
and
\mathcal{B}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{C}^{3},\mathbb{H},\mathbb{B}\}
, such that every component of
w
is integrable over
\mathcal{A}
, for
p
in
[1,\infty)
, also recall the Sobolev space
W^{k,p}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
defined as the subspace of all the integrable functions in
L^{p}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
such that every component has
k
weak derivatives, for
k
a positive integer. Now, we define the
Moisil–Teodorescu differential operator
as follows

D=\sum_{i=1}^{3}e_{i}\partial_{i},

(2)

where
\partial_{i}
represents the partial derivative with respect to the variable
x_{i}
, for each
i=1,2,3
. In the literature, operator (
2
) is also known as the
Dirac operator
.

If the operator
D
acts over functions in the set
C^{1,1}(\Omega,\mathbb{B})
, which is the set of
C^{1,1}
-functions defined on
\Omega
and taking values in
\mathbb{B}
, then

Dw=\sum_{i=1}^{3}e_{i}\partial_{i}w,

(3)

for every
w\in C^{1,1}(\Omega,\mathbb{B})
. In particular, when
w=u+iv\in C^{1,1}(\Omega,\mathbb{B})
, then (
3
) reduces to

Dw=Du+iDv,

(4)

where the action over every quaternion-valued functions
u=u_{0}+\vec{u}
and
v=v_{0}+\vec{v}
is given by the classical rule

Du=-\mbox{div}\,\vec{u}+\nabla u_{0}+\mbox{curl}\,\vec{u},\hskip 20.00003ptDv=-\mbox{div}\,\vec{v}+\nabla v_{0}+\mbox{curl}\,\vec{v}.

(5)

Notice that
Du,Dv
are expressed in terms of the gradient
\nabla
, the divergence
div
, and the curl (or rotational
curl
). This particular form of decomposing the Dirac operator for biquaternionic-valued functions will be useful in the development of the present work.

A function
w\in C^{1,1}(\Omega,\mathbb{B})
is called
left-monogenic
in
\Omega
when
Dw=0
in
\Omega
. Respectively, it is called
right-monogenic
when
wD=0
in
\Omega
. Some basic properties of the Moisil-Teodorescu operator and left-monogenic functions can be found in references
[
2
,
18
,
13
]
. By (
4
)-(
5
), we can observe that
w:\Omega\to\mathbb{B}
is left-monogenic in
\Omega
if and only if
u,v
simultaneously satisfy in
\Omega

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mbox{div}\,\vec{u}&=&0,&&\mbox{div}\,\vec{v}&=&0\\
\mbox{curl}\,\vec{u}&=&-\nabla u_{0}&&\mbox{curl}\,\vec{v}&=&-\nabla v_{0}\end{array}

(6)

The system (
6
) is called the
Moisil–Teodorescu system
. It is worth mentioning that it can be considered as the most natural generalization of the Cauchy–Riemann system in three dimensions
[
18
]
.

Let us consider the
Cauchy kernel

E(x)=\frac{-x}{4\pi|x|^{3}},\hskip 20.00003ptx\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}.

(7)

It is well-known that the Cauchy kernel is a fundamental solution of the Moisil-Teodorescu operator
D
. For each
w\in L^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{B})
, the
Teodorescu transform
of
w
is defined as

T_{\Omega}[w](x)=-\int_{\Omega}E(y-x)w(y)\,dy\hskip 20.00003ptx\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(8)

where
E
is the Cauchy kernel defined in (
7
).
In fact, the Teodorescu transform is a right inverse of
D
in
L^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{B})
, for
p>1
. More precisely,
DT_{\Omega}[w]=w
, for every
w\in L^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{B})
.

We will consider the
wave operator
,
-\Delta+\partial_{tt}
, acting over functions in
C^{2,2}(\mathscr{C})
, it acts naturally component-wise. We say that
w
satisfies the homogeneous wave equation if

(-\Delta+\partial_{tt})w=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ in }\Omega.

(9)

Background on the Fourier transform

Let
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Following
[
20
]
, we define the Fourier transform of a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{B}
, denoted by
\mathcal{F}\left(f\right)
as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left(f\right)(k)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp(-2\pi i\left\langle k,x\right\rangle)f(x)\,dx=\sum_{i=0}^{3}e_{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp(-2\pi i\left\langle k,x\right\rangle)f_{i}(x)\,dx,

(10)

for all
k\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where

\displaystyle\left\langle k,x\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}x_{i},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{ and }\hskip 20.00003ptf=\sum_{i=0}^{3}f_{i}e_{i},

where
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}
,
i=0,1,2,3.
We usually work with functions defined in
\mathscr{C}=\Omega\times(0,\infty)
, by convention we will extend the function
f
by defining
f(x,t)=0
for all
x
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. Despite being functions defined in a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, we define the Fourier transform of
f
at the point
(k,t)\in\mathscr{C}
as in (
10
). Indeed,

\mathcal{F}\left(f\right)(k,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp(-2\pi i\left\langle k,x\right\rangle)f(x,t)\,dx.

(11)

Some fundamental properties of the Fourier transform are collected below.
In particular, the map
f\mapsto\mathcal{F}\left(f\right)
is invertible, with inverse
operator denoted by
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(f\right)
.

Theorem 1
.

[
20
, Th. 5.5]

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
we define

\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(f\right)(x):=\mathcal{F}\left(f\right)(-x).

(12)

Then

f=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{F}\left(f\right)\right).

(13)

Let
f,g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, the convolution of
f
and
g
, denoted by
f*g
, is defined as follows

(f\ast g)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x-y)g(y)\,dy.

Most of the time, we will work with convolutions in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
unless otherwise specified; for instance, we will denote by
f\ast_{(x,t)}g
the convolution in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
.
One important property of the Fourier transform is that it converts differentiation and convolution into multiplication operations, indeed

Theorem 2
.

[
20
, Th. 5.8]

Let
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
g\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and let
1+\tfrac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}
. Suppose that
1\leq p,q
and
r\leq 2
, then

\mathcal{F}\left(f\ast g\right)(k)=\mathcal{F}\left(f\right)(k)\mathcal{F}\left(g\right)(k).

(14)

Theorem 3
.

[
20
, Th. 7.9]

Let
f
be an
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
function with Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}\left(f\right)
. Then
f
is in
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
if and only if the function
k\to|k|\mathcal{F}\left(f\right)(k)
is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. If it is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, then

\mathcal{F}\left(\nabla f\right)(k)=2\pi ik\mathcal{F}\left(f\right)(k),

(15)

Moreover,

\left|\left|f\right|\right|^{2}_{W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\mathcal{F}\left(f\right)(k)|^{2}(1+4\pi^{2}|k|^{2})\,dk.

It is worth mentioning that Theorems
1
-
3
are enunciated for
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, but indeed they are valid for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in general.

3
The parabolic Dirac operator

In this section, we are going to analyze the following
parabolic Dirac operators
in a biquaternionic framework

D\pm i\partial_{t},

(16)

where
D
is the Dirac operator defined previously in (
2
) and
i
is the imaginary unit in
\mathbb{C}
. Following
[
17
,
18
]
, we employ the following factorization of the wave operator in terms of the parabolic Dirac operators
D\pm i\partial_{t}

-\Delta+\partial_{tt}=(D+i\partial_{t})(D-i\partial_{t})=(D-i\partial_{t})(D+i\partial_{t}).

(17)

The above factorization will be the cornerstone of our work. We proceed now to investigate the kernel of
D\pm i\partial_{t}
. Consider a function
w:\mathscr{C}\to\mathbb{B}
, which belongs to the kernel of the parabolic Dirac operator
D\pm i\partial_{t}
in
\mathscr{C}
. As a consequence of the factorization (
17
), we can easily see that
w
is a solution of the wave equation (
9
) component-wise in
\Omega
. As a consequence, it becomes important to have a complete characterization of
\text{Ker }(D\pm i\partial_{t})
. From now on, we are going to use the convention for biquaternionic-valued functions (
1
). Thus,
w\in\text{Ker }(D\pm i\partial_{t})
if and only if
w
satisfies a parabolic div-curl system as follows

Proposition 4
.

Let
w:\mathscr{C}\to\mathbb{B}
be a
C^{1,1}(\mathscr{C},\mathbb{B})
function. Then
(D\pm i\partial_{t})w=0
in
\mathscr{C}
if and only if

\begin{array}[]{rlc}-\mbox{div}\,\vec{u}&=&\pm\partial_{t}v_{0},\\
\nabla u_{0}+\mbox{curl}\,\vec{u}&=&\pm\partial_{t}\vec{v},\\
-\mbox{div}\,\vec{v}&=&\mp\partial_{t}u_{0},\\
\nabla v_{0}+\mbox{curl}\,\vec{v}&=&\mp\partial_{t}\vec{u},\\
\end{array}

(18)

where
w=u+iv=(u_{0}+\vec{u})+i(v_{0}+\vec{v})
.

Proof.

Let
w:\mathscr{C}\to\mathbb{B}
be a continuously differentiable function, then

\displaystyle(D\pm i\partial_{t})w=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow Du\mp\partial_{t}v+i(Dv\pm\partial_{t}u)=0.

Therefore,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}Du\mp\partial_{t}v&=&0,\\
Dv\pm\partial_{t}u&=&0.\end{array}\right.

(19)

Using the action (
5
) of the Dirac operator
D
and separating the scalar and the vector parts, we can directly verify that (
19
) and (
18
) are equivalent systems.
∎

Given the above equations, we can see a certain similarity with the Cauchy-Riemann equations, so we want to generalize the harmonic conjugation process. Based on the equivalence between
\text{Ker }(D\pm i\partial_{t})
and system (
19
) and the properties of the Teodorescu operator
T_{\Omega}
defined in (
8
), we are able to construct the imaginary part of solutions in the kernel of
D\pm i\partial_{t}
when the real part is known, and it is a solution of the wave equation (
9
). That is,

Proposition 5
.

Let
u:\mathscr{C}\to\mathbb{H}
be a
C^{2,2}(\mathscr{C},\mathbb{H})
function and
u\in\text{Ker }(-\Delta+\partial_{tt}).
Then we can construct
v:\mathscr{C}\to\mathbb{H}
such that
u\pm iv\in\text{Ker }(D\pm i\partial_{t})
as follows

v(x,t):=\int_{0}^{t}Du(x,s)\,ds-T_{\Omega}[\partial_{t}u](x,0)+\nabla\varphi(x),

(20)

where
T_{\Omega}
is the Teodorescu operator and
\varphi
is any harmonic function independent of time.

Proof.

Let
u
be a solution for the wave equation (
9
). By the proof of Proposition
4
we only need to verify that (
19
) holds, then

\displaystyle\pm\partial_{t}v(x,t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\left(\int_{0}^{t}Du(x,s)\,ds-T_{\Omega}[\partial_{t}u](x,0)+\nabla\varphi(x)\right)

\displaystyle=Du(x,t).

Now, using that
DT_{\Omega}=I
in
\Omega
, we get that

\displaystyle Dv(x,t)

\displaystyle=\pm D\left(\int_{0}^{t}Du(x,s)\,ds-T_{\Omega}[\partial_{t}u](x,0)+\nabla\varphi(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\pm\left(\int_{0}^{t}D^{2}u(x,s)\,ds-DT_{\Omega}[\partial_{t}u](x,0)-\Delta\varphi(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\pm\left(\int_{0}^{t}-\Delta u(x,s)\,ds-\partial_{t}u(x,0)\right)

\displaystyle=\pm\left(\int_{0}^{t}-\partial_{s}^{2}u(x,s)\,ds-\partial_{t}u(x,0)\right)

\displaystyle=\pm(\partial_{t}u(x,0)-\partial_{t}u(x,t)-\partial_{t}u(x,0))

\displaystyle=\mp\partial_{t}u(x,t).

Therefore,
w:=u\pm iv
is a solution of
(D\pm i\partial_{t})w=0
as we desired.
∎

Example 6
.

Let
u(x,t)=x+t
, then
(-\Delta+\partial_{tt})u=0
.
Using that
T_{\Omega}[1]=-\frac{1}{3}x
(see
[
14
, Appendix]
) and by (

, namely

as follows

\displaystyle v(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}Du(x,s)\,ds-T_{\Omega}[\partial_{t}u](x,0)+\nabla\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}-3\,ds-T_{\Omega}[1](x,0)+\nabla\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=\left(-3t-T_{\Omega}[1](x,0)+\nabla\varphi(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-3t+\frac{1}{3}x+\nabla\varphi(x)\right).

Therefore,

w(x,t)=x+t\pm i\left(\frac{1}{3}x-3t+\nabla\varphi(x)\right)\in\text{Ker }(D\pm i\partial_{t}),

where
\varphi
is an arbitrary harmonic function in
\Omega
.

Let
u_{0}\in C^{2,2}(\mathscr{C},\mathbb{C})
, let us define the following operator

\mathscr{U}[u_{0}](x,t):=i\int_{0}^{t}\nabla u_{0}(x,s)\,ds-iT_{\Omega}[\partial_{t}u_{0}](x,0).

(21)

Observe that
\mathscr{U}[u_{0}]\in C^{1,1}(\mathscr{C},\mathbb{C}^{3})

. Moreover, if

is a scalar solution of the wave equation (

), then
u_{0}\pm\mathscr{U}[u_{0}]
belongs to the kernel of the parabolic Dirac operator (

). Indeed,

Proposition 7
.

Let
u_{0}:\mathscr{C}\to\mathbb{C}
in
C^{2,2}(\mathscr{C},\mathbb{C})
. If
u_{0}\in\text{Ker }(-\Delta+\partial_{tt}),
then
u_{0}\pm\mathscr{U}[u_{0}]\in\text{Ker }(D\pm i\partial_{t})
. Moreover, for any scalar harmonic function independent of time

, we have that
u_{0}\pm\mathscr{U}[u_{0}]+\nabla h\in\text{Ker}(D\pm i\partial_{t})
.

Proof.

The proof is based on a direct computation

\displaystyle(D\pm i\partial_{t})\left(u_{0}\pm\mathscr{U}[u_{0}]\right)(x,t)

\displaystyle=(D\pm i\partial_{t})\left(u_{0}(x,t)\pm i\left[\int_{0}^{t}\nabla u_{0}(x,s)\,ds-T_{\Omega}[\partial_{t}u_{0}](x,0)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla u_{0}\pm i\int_{0}^{t}-\Delta u_{0}(x,s)\,ds\mp i\partial_{t}u_{0}(x,0)\pm i\partial_{t}u_{0}(x,t)-\nabla u_{0}

\displaystyle=\pm i\int_{0}^{t}-\partial_{s}^{2}u_{0}(x,s)\,ds\mp i\partial_{t}u_{0}(x,0)\pm i\partial_{t}u_{0}(x,t)

\displaystyle=0.

∎

.

4
Inverse of the parabolic Dirac operator
D\pm i\partial_{t}
.

One advantage of the factorization (
17
) is that fundamental solutions of the first-order operators
D\pm i\partial_{t}
can be derived directly from a fundamental solution of the wave operator. However, after an exhaustive review of the literature, we opt against using the classical fundamental solutions of the wave operator; instead, we propose to work with a full characterization of the image of a fundamental solution of
D\pm i\partial_{t}
under the classical Fourier transform, and we find an unexpected relation with the quaternionic exponential function.

Let us consider
\Phi_{\pm}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty))
be a fundamental solution of the parabolic Dirac operator
D\pm i\partial_{t}
, i.e.

(D\pm i\partial_{t})\Phi_{\pm}(x,t)=0,\hskip 10.00002ptx\neq 0,\,t\neq 0.

(22)

Applying the Fourier transform defined in (
11
) to (
22
) and using the property (
15
), we get that

2\pi ik\mathcal{F}\left(\Phi_{\pm}\right)(k,t)\pm i\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}\left(\Phi_{\pm}\right)(k,t)=0.

(23)

Using that
\overline{k}=-k
, it is easy to verify that a solution of (
23
) is

\mathcal{F}\left(\Phi_{\pm}\right)(k,t)=\exp\left(\mp 2\pi kt\right)=\cos\left(2\pi|k|t\right)\mp\frac{k}{|k|}\sin\left(2\pi|k|t\right),

(24)

where the right-hand side is a quaternionic-valued exponential function defined for all
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}
, see for instance
[
13
, Theorem 11.19]
.
In particular, we consider
t>0
, we additionally suppose that
\mathcal{F}\left(\Phi_{\pm}\right)(k,t)=0
for all
t<0
.

So the next natural step is to find
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{F}\left(\Phi_{\pm}\right)\right)
, but this can be rather difficult, so instead we should use the properties (
12
)-(
14
) of the Fourier transform and the complete characterization of
\mathcal{F}\left(\Phi_{\pm}\right)
given in (
24
). Observe that

\displaystyle(\Phi_{\pm}*_{(x,t)}w)(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Phi_{\pm}(x-y,t-s)w(y,s)\,dyds

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{s}(x-y)g_{s}(y)\,dyds

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{s}*g_{s})(x)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{F}\left(f_{s}*g_{s}\right)\right)(x)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{F}\left(f_{s}\right)\mathcal{F}\left(g_{s}\right)\right)(x)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\mathcal{F}\left(f_{s}\right)(k)\mathcal{F}\left(g_{s}\right)(k)\,dkds.

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\mathcal{F}\left(\Phi_{\pm}\right)(k,t-s)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,dkds.

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(\mp 2\pi k(t-s)\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,dkds,

where
f_{s}(x)=\Phi_{\pm}(x,t-s)
and
g_{s}(x)=w(x,s).

After the above discussion, we define the
parabolic Teodorescu transform
acting on integrable biquaternionic functions
w\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{B}
as follows

\displaystyle T_{\mathscr{C},\pm}[w](x,t):=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(\mp 2\pi k(t-s)\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,dk\,ds,

(25)

for all
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty)
. From now on, when we work with the parabolic Teodorescu transform
T_{\mathscr{C},\pm}
acting on functions in
L^{2}(\mathscr{C},\mathbb{B})
, we will extend by zero on
(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega})\times(0,\infty)
. Moreover,

Proposition 9
.

Let
w\in L^{2}(\mathscr{C},\mathbb{B})
. Then
T_{\mathscr{C},\pm}[w](x,t)
is well defined for almost all
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

If
w\in L^{2}(\mathscr{C},\mathbb{B})
, then
w(\cdot,t_{0})\in L^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{B})
and extending by zero on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
, then
\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)
is well-defined. And now given
t>0
then for all
0<s<t
we have the following

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\exp\left(\mp 2\pi(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)|^{2}\,dk\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)|^{2}\,dk\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|{w}(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2},

where the last equality comes from Plancherel’s theorem. But given that
\left|\left|\cdot\right|\right|
is continuous, then is bounded over
[0,t]
. Therefore,
\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\mp 2\pi(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,ds
is in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{B})
meaning it has a Fourier inverse. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\mp 2\pi(t-s)\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,ds\right)(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\mp 2\pi(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,ds\,dk

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(\mp 2\pi(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,dk\,ds.

\displaystyle=T_{\mathscr{C},\pm}[w](x,t),

is well-defined for almost all
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty)
as we desired.
∎

Now, we enunciate the main result of this Section, which establishes that up to a multiplicative factor, the operator
T_{\mathscr{C},\pm}
defined in (
25
) is a right inverse operator of the parabolic Dirac operator
D\pm i\partial_{t}
.

Theorem 10
.

Let
w\in L^{2}(\mathscr{C},\mathbb{B})
then the operator
T_{\mathscr{C},\pm}[-i\text{ }\cdot]
is a right inverse of
D\pm i\partial_{t}
. That is,

(D\pm i\partial_{t})T_{\mathscr{C},\pm}[\mp i\,w](x,t)=w(x,t),\hskip 20.00003pt\forall(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty).

(26)

Proof.

First, by convention, we will extend
w
by zero on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. Notice that

\displaystyle\partial_{j}T_{\mathscr{C},\pm}[w](x,t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{j}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(\mp 2\pi(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,dk\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}2\pi ik_{j}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(\mp 2\pi(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,dk\,ds.

Then,

DT_{\mathscr{C},\pm}[w](x,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}2\pi ik\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(\mp 2\pi(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,dk\,ds.

We now compute the partial derivative with respect to
t
, applying the Leibniz integral rule.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}T_{\mathscr{C},\pm}[w](x,t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(\mp 2\pi(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,dk\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,t)\,dk

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{t}\left[\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(\mp 2\pi(t-s)k\right)\right]\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,dk\,ds

\displaystyle=w(x,t)\mp\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}2\pi k\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(\mp 2\pi(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,dk\,ds

\displaystyle=w(x,t)\pm iDT_{\mathscr{C},\pm}[w](x,t)(x,t).

Therefore, we can easily verify that

(D\pm i\partial_{t})T_{\mathscr{C},\pm}[w](x,t)=\pm iw(x,t),\hskip 20.00003pt\forall(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty),

which in turn implies (
26
) as we desired.
∎

A straightforward consequence of Theorem
10
and of the factorization (
17
) is

Corollary 11
.

Under the same hypothesis than Theorem
10
. The scalar part of
T_{\mathscr{C},\pm}[{\mp iw}]
is solution of the wave equation (
9
) if and only if

\displaystyle\mbox{div}\,\vec{w}\pm i\partial_{t}w_{0}=0.

(27)

Proof.

Just notice the following equalities

\displaystyle(-\Delta+\partial_{tt})T_{\mathscr{C},\pm}[{\mp iw}]

\displaystyle=(D\mp i\partial_{t})(D\pm i\partial_{t})T_{\mathscr{C},\pm}[{\mp iw}]

\displaystyle=(D\mp i\partial_{t})w

\displaystyle=(D\mp i\partial_{t})(w_{0}+\vec{w})

\displaystyle=-\mbox{div}\,{\vec{w}}\mp i\partial_{t}w_{0}+\nabla w_{0}+\mbox{curl}\,{\vec{w}}\mp i\partial_{t}\vec{w}.

Taking the scalar part, it yields the desired result.
∎

Observe that in the time-independent case, the condition (
27
) reduces to the class of solenoidal vector fields
[
8
, Prop. 3.1]
required in the construction of the solution of the inhomogeneous div-curl system provided in
[
8
]
.

5
Solution of Maxwell’s equations

A historical account of how Maxwell’s equations were discovered can be found in
[
15
,
24
]
, while a rigorous and self-contained introduction to these four fundamental laws of electricity and magnetism is presented in
[
16
]
. These laws (Gauss’s law for electricity, the absence of free magnetic monopoles, Faraday’s law of induction, and Ampère’s law) govern the behavior of classical electromagnetic fields. Throughout this work, we consider Maxwell’s equations in Gaussian units

\begin{cases}\mbox{div}\,\vec{E}&=4\pi\rho,\\
\mbox{div}\,\vec{B}&=0,\\
\mbox{curl}\,\vec{E}&=-\partial_{t}\vec{B},\\
\mbox{curl}\,\vec{B}&=4\pi\vec{\jmath}+\partial_{t}\vec{E},\end{cases}

(28)

where
\rho
is the electric charge density,
\vec{\jmath}
is the current density,
\vec{E}
is the electric field, and
\vec{B}
is the magnetic field, respectively. Notice that these equations are written in units where the speed of light is one, meaning
c=1.

It is crucial to note the compatibility condition linking the divergence
of the current density to the time derivative of the electric charge density

\operatorname{div}\vec{\jmath}+\partial_{t}\rho=0,

(29)

which expresses the
law of conservation of electric charge
.
This equation is not an external constraint but an intrinsic consistency
condition of Maxwell’s system. In integral form, it states that the rate of change of total charge
enclosed in any volume equals the net inward flux of current through its
boundary. From the perspective
of PDE analysis, (
29
) is a necessary condition for the
system to be well-posed: the source terms
\rho
and
\vec{\jmath}
cannot
be prescribed independently. This constraint will play a central role in
Theorem
13
, where it enables a reformulation
of Maxwell’s equations admitting purely vectorial solutions.

The following Proposition, established in
[
18
, Sec. 3.1.1]
for Maxwell’s equations
in a homogeneous and isotropic medium, will be central to our analysis.

Proposition 12
.

Let
\rho
and
\vec{\jmath}
be continuously differentiable functions with domain
\Omega\times[0,\infty)
and values in
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, respectively. Then
(\vec{E},\vec{B})
is a solution of the Maxwell system (
28
) if and only if the biquaternionic function
\vec{\varphi}=\vec{E}+i\vec{B}
satisfies

(D-i\partial_{t})\vec{\varphi}=-4\pi(\rho-i\>\vec{\jmath}).

(30)

Proof.

We proceed with a direct verification.

\displaystyle(D-i\partial_{t})\vec{\varphi}=(D-i\partial_{t})(\vec{E}+i\vec{B})

\displaystyle=-\mbox{div}\,(\vec{E})+\mbox{curl}\,(\vec{E})-i\partial_{t}\vec{E}-i\mbox{div}\,(\vec{B})+i\mbox{curl}\,(\vec{B})+\partial_{t}\vec{B}

\displaystyle=-4\pi(\rho-i\>\vec{\jmath}).

Separating into the scalar real, scalar imaginary, vector real, and vector imaginary parts, we obtain that (
30
) and (
28
) are equivalent systems.
∎

Now, combining the previous equivalence with the ideas of construction of purely vectorial solutions of generalized div-curl systems from
[
8
,
9
,
5
,
7
,
6
]
, we provide an explicit solution of Maxwell’s system (
28
).

Theorem 13
.

Let
\rho
and
\vec{\jmath}
be continuously differentiable functions with domain
\Omega\times[0,\infty)
and values in
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, respectively. Suppose that
\rho
and
\vec{j}
satisfy the compatibility condition (
29
). Then a solution
(\vec{E},\vec{B})
of the Maxwell’s system (
28
) is as follows

\displaystyle\vec{E}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\text{Re }\left(\mbox{Vec}\,\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(2\pi(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(-4\pi(\rho-i\>\vec{\jmath})\right)(k,s)\,dkds\right)\right.

\displaystyle-\text{Im }\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{x}u_{0}(x,s)\,ds-T_{\Omega}[\partial_{t}u_{0}](x,0)\right\}+\nabla h_{1}(x),

\displaystyle\vec{B}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\text{Im }\left(\mbox{Vec}\,\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(2\pi(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(-4\pi(\rho-i\>\vec{\jmath})\right)\,dkds\right)\right.

\displaystyle+\text{Re }\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{x}u_{0}(x,s)\,ds-T_{\Omega}[\partial_{t}u_{0}](x,0)\right\}+\nabla h_{2}(x),

where
u_{0}:=\text{Sc }T_{\mathscr{C},-}\left[-4\pi i(\rho-i\>\vec{\jmath})\right]
,
h_{1},h_{2}
are harmonic functions independent of time.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we extend
\rho
and
\vec{\jmath}
as zero scalar and vector functions whenever
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. By Theorem
10
, we have that

(D-i\partial_{t})T_{\mathscr{C},-}\left[-4\pi i(\rho-i\>\vec{\jmath})\right]=-4\pi(\rho-i\vec{j}).

By the compatibility condition (
29
) and Corollary
11
, we have that
u_{0}:=\text{Sc }T_{\mathscr{C},-}\left[-4\pi i(\rho-i\>\vec{\jmath})\right]
is a solution of the wave equation (
9
). As a consequence, by Proposition
7
, we have that
u_{0}-\mathscr{U}[u_{0}]
belongs to the kernel of
D-i\partial_{t}
. Thus, adding this biquaternionic function, we do not affect the system (
30
). That is,

\displaystyle\vec{\varphi}=T_{\mathscr{C},-}\left[-4\pi i(\rho-i\>\vec{\jmath})\right]-(u_{0}-\mathscr{U}[u_{0}])=\text{Vec }T_{\mathscr{C},-}\left[-4\pi i(\rho-i\>\vec{\jmath})\right]+\mathscr{U}[u_{0}]

is a purely vectorial solution of (
30
). Finally, by Proposition
12
we get that
\vec{E}:=\text{Re }\vec{\varphi}
,
\vec{B}:=\text{Im }\vec{\varphi}
solve Maxwell’s system (
28
) as we desired.
∎

5.1
Maxwell’s equations in homogeneous media

Similarly to what we did in Section
4
, we analyze the slightly modified parabolic Dirac operator
D\pm i\lambda\partial_{t}
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. We look for a fundamental solution in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty))
to the equation

(D\pm i\lambda\partial_{t})\Phi_{\lambda,\pm}(x,t)=0.

Using the Fourier transform, we similarly get that the quaternionic exponential function
\mathcal{F}\left(\Phi_{\lambda,\pm}\right)(x,t)=\exp\left(\mp 2\pi\lambda^{-1}tk\right)
solves

2\pi k\mathcal{F}\left(\Phi_{\lambda,\pm}\right)(x,t)\pm i\lambda\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}\left(\Phi_{\lambda,\pm}\right)(x,t)=0.

Consequently, we define the
\lambda
parabolic Teodorescu transform
as follows

T_{\mathscr{C},\pm,\lambda}[w](x,t):=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(\mp 2\pi\lambda^{-1}(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(w\right)(k,s)\,dsdk,

(31)

for all
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty)
. Similarly to Proposition
9
and Theorem
10
, the operator
T_{\mathscr{C},\pm,\lambda}
is well-defined and serves as a right inverse of
D\pm i\lambda\partial_{t}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty)
. Moreover,

Corollary 14
.

Let
w:\mathscr{C}\to\mathbb{B}
be a differentiable function. Then

(D\pm i\lambda\partial_{t})w=0\Leftrightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}-\mbox{div}\,(\vec{u})&=&\pm\lambda\partial_{t}v_{0},\\
\nabla u_{0}+\mbox{curl}\,(\vec{u})&=&\pm\lambda\partial_{t}\vec{v},\\
-\mbox{div}\,(\vec{v})&=&\mp\lambda\partial_{t}u_{0},\\
\nabla v_{0}+\mbox{curl}\,(\vec{v})&=&\mp\lambda\partial_{t}\vec{u},\\
\end{array}\right.

where

w=u+iv=(u_{0}+\vec{u})+i(v_{0}+\vec{v}).

Let us consider Maxwell’s equations in a homogeneous and isotropic medium in SI units

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mbox{div}\,{\vec{E}}&=&\dfrac{\rho}{\varepsilon},\\
\mbox{div}\,{\vec{B}}&=&0,\\
\mbox{curl}\,{\vec{E}}&=&-\partial_{t}\vec{B},\\
\mbox{curl}\,{\vec{B}}&=&\mu\left(\vec{\jmath}+\varepsilon\partial_{t}\vec{E}\right),\end{array}\right.

(32)

where
\varepsilon
is the absolute permittivity and
\mu
is the absolute permeability. From now on,
we will suppose that
\varepsilon,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. Following
[
18
, Section 3.1.1]
, the author show that
the operator
c^{-1}\partial_{t}\pm iD
factorizes the wave operator as follows

\frac{1}{c^{2}}\partial_{t}-\Delta=\left(\frac{1}{c}\partial_{t}+iD\right)\left(\frac{1}{c}\partial_{t}-iD\right),

where
c
is the speed of propagation of electromagnetic waves in the medium, given by
c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}}
. Moreover,
(\vec{E},\vec{B})
solves (
32
) if and only if the biquaternionic function
\sqrt{\varepsilon}\vec{E}+i\sqrt{\mu}\vec{B}
satisfies

\left(\frac{1}{c}\partial_{t}+iD\right)(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\vec{E}+i\sqrt{\mu}\vec{B})=-\left(\sqrt{\mu}\;\vec{\jmath}+\frac{i\rho}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right).

Or equivalently, if
\sqrt{\varepsilon}\vec{E}+i\sqrt{\mu}\vec{B}
satisfies

\left(D-\frac{i}{c}\partial_{t}\right)\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\vec{E}+i\sqrt{\mu}\vec{B}\right)=-\left(\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}-i\sqrt{\mu}\;\vec{\jmath}\right).

(33)

As in the previous results, solving (
32
) is equivalent to finding a purely vectorial solution to (
33
). Again, the proof relies on the properties of the Fourier transform, the
\lambda
parabolic Teodorescu transform
T_{\mathscr{C},\pm,\lambda}
defined in (
31
), and taking
\lambda=\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}
. For the completion process illustrated in Proposition
7
, in this case, it is necessary to do a re-scaling with the constant
\lambda
, recall the operator
\mathscr{U}
defined in (
21
), then

Corollary 15
.

Let
u_{0}:\mathscr{C}\to\mathbb{C}
in
C^{2,2}(\mathscr{C},\mathbb{C})
. If
u_{0}\in\text{Ker }(-\Delta+\lambda^{2}\partial_{tt}),
then
u_{0}\pm\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathscr{U}[u_{0}]\in\text{Ker }(D\pm i\lambda\partial_{t})
. Moreover, for any scalar harmonic function independent of time
h
, we have that
u_{0}\pm\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathscr{U}[u_{0}]+\nabla h\in\text{Ker}(D\pm i\lambda\partial_{t})
.

The novelty in this Subsection
5.1
is to provide an explicit solution of Maxwell’s system (
32
).

Theorem 16
.

Let
\rho
and
\vec{\jmath}
be continuously differentiable functions with domain
\Omega\times[0,\infty)
and values in
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, respectively. Suppose that
\rho
and
\vec{j}
satisfy the compatibility condition

\mbox{div}\,\vec{\jmath}+\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}\partial_{t}\rho=0.

(34)

Let
\lambda=\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}
. Then a solution
(\vec{E},\vec{B})
of the Maxwell’s system (
32
) is as follows

\displaystyle\vec{E}(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\text{Re }\left(\mbox{Vec}\,\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(2\pi\lambda^{-1}(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}-i\sqrt{\mu}\;\vec{j}\right)(k,s)\,dkds\right)\right.

\displaystyle+\lambda^{-1}\text{Im }\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{x}u_{0}(x,s)\,ds-T_{\Omega}[\partial_{t}u_{0}](x,0)\right\}+\nabla h_{1}(x),

\displaystyle\vec{B}(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\text{Im }\left(\mbox{Vec}\,\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle x,k\right\rangle\right)\exp\left(2\pi\lambda^{-1}(t-s)k\right)\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}-i\sqrt{\mu}\;\vec{j}\right)(k,s)\,dkds\right)\right.

where

,

6
Conclusions

In this work, we analyzed the parabolic Dirac operator
D\pm i\partial_{t}
and characterized its kernel via generalized div-curl systems and Cauchy-Riemann-type relations between the real and imaginary parts. Using Fourier analysis, we constructed a right inverse operator and applied it to obtain purely vectorial solutions for the time-dependent Maxwell system (see Theorems
13
and
16
).

These results highlight the synergy of Fourier transforms and quaternionic analysis for tackling complex PDE systems in electrodynamics. Future directions include extensions to nonlinear perturbations, relativistic frameworks, and numerical implementations.
