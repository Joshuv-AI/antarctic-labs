Compound heterozygous TECRL variants in an adolescent: Early electrocardiographic abnormalities and sudden cardiac arrest
Authors: Ellie K. Bruce, Alexander J. Hutchinson, Charles C. Anderson
Published: 2026

Key Teaching Points •Homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations in the trans-2,3-enoyl-CoA reductase-like (TECRL) gene may cause a rare genetic arrhythmia syndrome with overlapping features of both catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia and certain subtypes of long QT syndrome. The clinical phenotype is often severe, including sudden cardiac arrest because of ventricular fibrillation.•We describe the clinical features, diagnostic evaluation, and long-term outcome of an adolescent boy with compound heterozygous mutations in the TECRL gene, who presented with a family history of sudden cardiac death, borderline QT prolongation, atrial fibrillation, and ventricular fibrillation leading to sudden cardiac arrest despite β-blocker therapy.•Propafenone in combination with β-blocker therapy appears to be effective in controlling atrial and ventricular tachyarrhythmias in this patient’s specific TECRL mutations and may be effective antiarrhythmic therapy in patients with TECRL-related disease.

Introduction

Homozygous and compound heterozygous mutations in the trans-2,3-enoyl-CoA reductase-like (TECRL) gene are emerging as a rare cause of severe cardiac arrhythmias and sudden death. Located on the long arm of chromosome 4 (4q13), TECRL encodes a 363-amino acid protein that is expressed in the sarcoplasmic reticulum of the cardiomyocyte and appears to play a pivotal role in calcium homeostasis.1 Mutations in the TECRL gene may cause a phenotype of catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia type 3 (CPVT3), which manifests as abnormal calcium handling, diastolic instability, and delayed afterdepolarizations.1, 2, 3 Although the mechanism by which TECRL gene mutations cause arrhythmogenicity is still poorly understood, it appears that cardiomyocytes expressing TECRL mutations exhibit longer action potential duration, lower concentrations of calcium within the sarcoplasmic reticulum, and higher diastolic calcium concentration within the cytoplasm, perhaps because of downregulation of the ryanodine receptor 2 protein (RYR2) and calsequestrin 2 protein (CASQ2). Beyond that, there may be deeper perturbations in cellular energetics of the cardiomyocyte related to altered fatty acid oxidation in TECRL mutations. Affected cardiomyocytes are thus vulnerable to delayed afterdepolarizations, which can trigger ventricular tachyarrhythmias. In CPVT, severe ventricular tachyarrhythmias are often triggered by catecholamine release during exercise or emotional stress. Genetically speaking, TECRL-related disease is more similar to CPVT2, which is caused by CASQ2 mutations, typically inherited in an autosomal recessive manner or as compound heterozygous mutations, whereas CPVT1, caused by mutations in RYR2, is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner.4,5 Although helpful in providing clinical context for diagnosis and family screening, the inheritance patterns of these conditions do not necessarily reflect the exact mechanisms of arrhythmogenesis.

There is little available literature describing the clinical features of patients with TECRL-related disease.1 Here, we describe a case of TECRL-related disease in an adolescent boy referred for electrophysiology evaluation after sudden cardiac death (SCD) of his brother.

Case report

A 14-year-old adolescent boy underwent cardiac electrophysiology evaluation after SCD of his brother, who collapsed unexpectedly during a motocross competition. An Automated External Defibrillator used on the scene did not advise a shock, but analysis of data later retrieved from the Automated External Defibrillator indicated fine ventricular fibrillation (VF). Autopsy revealed no structural abnormalities, but no molecular autopsy was performed. Both parents had normal electrocardiograms (ECGs) and no personal cardiac history. Echocardiogram and Holter monitoring recording were normal. Exercise treadmill testing provoked several seconds of self-limited atrial fibrillation (AF) near peak exercise with a maximum heart rate of 240 beats/min and slight QRS aberrancy. There was no ventricular ectopy, and the QT interval did not significantly change during exercise, peak, or recovery. The patient was then initiated on metoprolol succinate 50 mg daily. Although the underlying diagnosis was not known at this time, metoprolol was indicated because of the patient’s borderline QT prolongation and family history of exercise-related SCD. The goal of this therapy was to decrease myocardial sympathetic stimulation and decrease the likelihood of arrhythmias. Serial ECGs over the following year continued to show similar findings with corrected QT intervals up to 470 ms.

A diagnostic electrophysiology study (EPS) was performed after a period of metoprolol washout. Ventricular extrastimulus testing with single, double, and triple extrastimuli at 3 drive cycle lengths from 2 right ventricular sites was conducted with and without isoproterenol. During EPS, the baseline corrected QT interval of 390 ms increased by 28% to 495 ms on isoproterenol infusion. QT intervals during EPS were measured in lead II, corrected using the Bazett formula, and averaged over 3 consecutive beats. U waves were excluded. No ventricular arrhythmias were induced. High-output atrial burst pacing caused sustained but self-terminating AF. There was no evidence of an accessory pathway.

Genetic testing was recommended at the time, but was not covered by the patient’s insurance and was not performed until years later.

Over the course of follow-up, the metoprolol succinate dose was reduced to 25 mg daily because of poor patient tolerance. Then at age 16, while standing at the free throw line in a basketball game, the patient felt dizzy and lightheaded, had blurred vision, and then collapsed owing to cardiac arrest. He was successfully defibrillated from coarse VF on the first shock. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging early postarrest showed a mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction of 50% and no delayed contrast enhancement. His corrected QT interval in the 24 hours postarrest peaked at 470 ms and normalized to 425 ms by hospital discharge.

Before hospital discharge postarrest, a dual-chamber transvenous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was placed, and his metoprolol succinate dose was increased back to 50 mg daily. Over the next 2 years, the patient experienced several inappropriate ICD shocks for AF and supraventricular tachycardia. Digoxin was added, but it did not reduce the patient’s heart rate during AF and was discontinued. Propafenone was then added, and metoprolol dose was increased further, leading to resolution of arrhythmias and inappropriate shocks. A repeat EPS was performed with epinephrine infusion up to a maximum dose of 0.3 μg/(kg⋅min), resulting in a maximum corrected QT interval of 525 ms, 3-dimensional right ventricular voltage mapping showed no areas of scarring, and again there were no inducible ventricular arrhythmias with programmed ventricular stimulation.

Several years later at age 31, the patient has remained free of significant cardiac events and inappropriate shocks on the combination of metoprolol succinate and propafenone. Genetic testing (RhythmNext, Ambry, Aliso Viejo, CA) was completed and revealed 2 heterozygous variants in the TECRL gene—p.G246∗ (c.736G>T) and p.L271del (c.812_814delTGT)—1 variant inherited from each parent.

Discussion

TECRL mutations are a rare but increasingly recognized cause of disease, which exhibit some phenotypic similarities to CPVT and to a lesser extent specific subtypes of long QT syndrome (LQTS) displaying diastolic instability, although the presentation may vary significantly from person to person. Moreover, TECRL-related disease is more genotypically complex than most cases of LQTS and CPVT, with the possibility of manifesting severe arrhythmias when inherited in either a homozygous or a compound heterozygous manner, as in our patient’s case.6 Since the first case reported in 2016, fewer than 20 symptomatic individuals have been described in the literature. Thus, the clinical presentation and standard treatment of TECRL-related disease remain incompletely understood.

The TECRL gene product appears to play a critical role in lipid and fatty acid metabolism, which influences levels of proteins that manage calcium homeostasis.1 A study using human-induced pluripotent stem cell–derived cardiomyocytes from affected individuals showed that TECRL homozygous variants are associated with reduced intracellular calcium transient amplitude and elevated diastolic calcium levels, which contribute to prolonged action potential duration and increased susceptibility to triggered activity in response to adrenergic stimulation.1

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a patient with compound heterozygosity for both p.G246∗ and p.L271del variants in TECRL, thus expanding knowledge surrounding the possible phenotypes of specific mutations. Initially designated as variants of uncertain significance, these were later reclassified as pathogenic and likely pathogenic, respectively. This patient’s variants were classified by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics on the basis of changes in DNA, how these changes are predicted to influence protein functionality, and variation rarity. This patient’s clinical course is consistent with these classifications, but was not used to determine them. The patient’s first TECRL variant, p.G246∗ (c.736G>T), is a nonsense mutation resulting in premature truncation of the protein. The second, p.L271del (c.812_814delTGT), is an in-frame deletion. The p.G246∗ variant is classified as pathogenic, as it meets criteria such as Pathogenic Very Strong 1 (PSV1), indicating loss of function in a protein implicated in TECRL-related disease, and Pathogenic Moderate 2 (PM2), indicating rarity in population databases. The p.L271del variant’s likely pathogenic classification derives foremost from PM4, indicating that the in-frame deletion of conserved amino acids is likely to disrupt protein function. Other criteria include PM2, which is rarity in population databases, and Pathogenic Supporting 3 (PP3), which is in silico evidence. To date, no functional studies have been done on nonsense mutations or in-frame deletions, so their effects can be inferred on the basis of similarity to other loss-of-function mutations and predicted impact.6, 7, 8, 9

Similar to this patient, other compound heterozygous variants with different alleles have been described. These include the splice-site variant c.918+3T>G and the missense mutation p.Arg196Gln, as well as c.331+1G>A.1,8 c.331+1G>A is a splice-site mutation characterized in multiple studies and was one of the first mutations characterized along with p.Arg196Gln in TECRL-related disease.1 Additional loss-of-function mutations include p.Tyr305Ter and p.Pro290Ser.2,8 While the specific mutation in this patient has not previously been reported, the biallelic loss-of-function mutations leading to impaired calcium handling and a CPVT-like phenotype is consistent in the literature.

This case is also unique in the patient’s presentation with a borderline prolonged QT interval and exercise stress test–induced AF, occurring before manifestation of any ventricular arrhythmias. Our patient’s presentation also differs from a previous case report published by Ebrahim and colleagues10 in that no ventricular arrhythmias were induced with catecholamine stimulation during EPS. In addition, the progression to cardiac arrest despite β-blocker therapy underscores the rapid, progressive, and variable nature of TECRL-related disease.

This patient’s treatment deviates from previously reported TECRL-related disease management, with a combination of metoprolol and propafenone leading to resolution of cardiac events and inappropriate ICD shocks. Metoprolol was initially chosen because of the patient’s family history of exertion-related cardiac death and personal history of exertion-related arrhythmia in the absence of a specific genetic diagnosis. While metoprolol does decrease sympathetic stimulation, it does not directly affect calcium handling and thus inadequately addresses the underlying mechanism of TECRL-related disease. Previous case studies more frequently describe the use of β-blockers alone or in combination with flecainide, which is also a common treatment of CPVT without features of LQTS.1 Class 1c antiarrhythmics primarily affect fast sodium channels, thus reducing intracellular sodium levels. This indirectly increases sodium-calcium exchanger activity and decreases calcium levels in cardiac myocytes, thus suppressing delayed afterdepolarizations and diseases mechanistically similar to CPVT, such as TECRL-related disease. This patient’s favorable response to a combination of metoprolol and propafenone supports the importance of this mechanism in TECRL-related disease. Although controversial, direct RYR2-mediated effects of Class 1c antiarrhythmics are considered less significant than the primary action of the fast sodium channel blockade.11

Conclusion

We report a case of TECRL-related disease caused by inheritance of compound heterozygous TECRL mutations presenting in an adolescent boy with a family history of SCD in his brother, manifested by borderline prolongation of the QT interval and prominent U waves, exercise-induced AF, and sudden cardiac arrest caused by VF, responsive to combination metoprolol succinate and propafenone therapy. Other noteworthy features of this case include the absence of ventricular arrhythmias triggered during exercise testing or programmed ventricular stimulation, but a significant increase in the corrected QT interval in response to both isoproterenol and epinephrine infusions.

Establishment of a multicenter registry for TECRL-related disease may provide a more accurate and complete understanding of the prevalence of individual TECRL variations and more evidence-based diagnosis and management of patients with this life-threatening condition.
