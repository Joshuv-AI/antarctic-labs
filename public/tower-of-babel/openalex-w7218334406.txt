Abstract
Anthracnose, caused by Colletotrichum lindemuthianum (Sacc. & Magnus) Briosi & Cavara, is one of the most
damaging diseases affecting common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.), often transmitted through infected seeds. While
fungicide-based chemical control is widely used, it poses risks such as the emergence of resistant strains and negative
environmental impacts. In this context, microwave radiation has emerged as a promising physical method for
sustainable seed disinfection. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of microwave radiation on the incidence of C.
lindemuthianum and on the physiological quality of artificially inoculated bean seeds. Two frequencies (2450 and
1225 MHz) and three exposure times (30, 45, and 60 seconds) were tested, along with an inoculated and a noninoculated control, both untreated. Results showed that longer exposure times, particularly at 2450 MHz, significantly
reduced pathogen incidence - most notably in the 60-second treatment, which brought infection rates below 2%.
However, these same conditions resulted in total seed mortality and compromised germination. In contrast, the
treatment at 1225 MHz for 30 seconds achieved a more favorable balance, reducing fungal incidence while
maintaining germination rates above 70%. These findings indicate that although microwave radiation is highly
effective in suppressing C. lindemuthianum, careful optimization of treatment parameters is essential to preserve seed
viability and ensure practical application.
Keywords: Seed disinfection; Seed-borne pathogens; Physiological viability; Electromagnetic radiation; Alternative
control.

1. Introduction
Common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) cultivation plays a crucial role in Brazilian agriculture and food culture and
represents a key source of nutrition and income. In the 2024/2025 season, Brazil allocated about 2.86 million hectares to beans,
with an average yield of 1,157 kg/ha and total production reaching 3.31 million tons (Conab, 2025). These figures highlight the
crop’s enduring economic relevance and its role as a staple in the Brazilian diet. However, the productivity and quality of this
essential crop are frequently threatened by various diseases, among which anthracnose stands out.
Anthracnose, caused by the fungus Colletotrichum lindemuthianum (Sacc. & Magnus) Briosi & Cavara, is one of the
most widespread and economically important diseases affecting common beans. Under favorable climatic conditions, it can
cause yield losses of up to 90% due to severe infections in seedlings, leaves, stems, and pods (Mohammed, 2013). The
pathogen is seed-borne, and the use of infected seeds can lead to yield losses ranging from 80% to 100% (Sharma et al., 2008;
Padder et al. 2017). To reduce the initial inoculum and minimize disease outbreaks, farmers commonly adopt fungicide
applications through foliar sprays or seed treatments (Lamichhane et al., 2020; Oliveira et al., 2016).
While chemical control remains a common strategy against anthracnose, the repeated use of commercial fungicides
can lead to the emergence of resistant strains of C. lindemuthianum, which compromises these products effectiveness over time
(Haq et al., 2020). Amid these concerns, alternative and more sustainable approaches have been explored to reduce reliance on
chemical control.
Microwave irradiation has emerged as a potential non-chemical strategy for enhancing seed quality and managing
plant pathogens, drawing increasing interest within the context of sustainable agriculture and integrated pest management.
Short-duration microwave exposure (20–30 seconds) significantly suppressed soil-borne pathogens such as Macrophomina
phaseolina, Rhizoctonia solani, and Fusarium spp., while also promoted plant growth in mash bean, chickpea, and sunflower

Controlled microwave exposure (8–10 hours) has been shown to improve germination indices, biochemical
composition, and growth-related gene expression in okra seedlings (Iwuala et al., 2021). Similarly, microwave treatment
enhanced germination, vigor, and the accumulation of bioactive compounds in tomato seedlings, with Fourier-transform
infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy confirming structural changes in treated tissues (Kumari et al., 2018). When precisely regulated,
microwave irradiation can serve as an effective tool for enhancing seed performance and suppressing certain pathogens,
although careful management of treatment duration and intensity is essential to avoid detrimental effects.
Despite growing interest in microwave irradiation as a non-chemical seed treatment, little is known about its effects
on common bean seeds infected with C. lindemuthianum. This gap underscores the need for innovative, sustainable alternatives
to chemical control, particularly amid concerns over fungicide resistance and environmental impact. This study aimed to
evaluate the effects of microwave radiation on the incidence of C. lindemuthianum and on the physiological quality of
artificially inoculated bean seeds. It evaluates the effects of microwave irradiation on artificially inoculated bean seeds, testing
two frequencies (2450 and 1225 MHz) and three exposure times (30, 45, and 60 seconds).

2. Methodology
An experimental, laboratory-based research was carried out using a quantitative approach (Pereira et al., 2018;
Risemberg et al., 2026) and statistical analysis (Banzatto & Kronka, 2013; Vieira, 2022).
2.1 Pathogen inoculation
Pérola common bean seeds were artificially inoculated with C. lindemuthianum by using the water restriction method
adapted from Carvalho (1999). The fungal isolate was grown on potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium amended with a mannitol
solution at -0.1MPa. After complete pathogen growth over the medium, seeds were distributed in Petri dishes and kept in
contact with the fungal colony for 24 hours. Before and after inoculation, seeds underwent surface disinfection using a sodium
hypochlorite solution, followed by air-drying under ambient laboratory conditions. The working sample consisted of a mix of
inoculated seeds and seeds placed on potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium amended with mannitol solution without fungal
inoculum, yielding an estimated infection rate of 20%.
2.2 Microwave treatment
Microwave irradiation was applied using a standard household microwave oven. Prior to treatment, the glass turntable
was sterilized and covered with sterile filter paper. Seeds were evenly distributed on the paper surface and subjected to one of
six microwave treatments, which varied by frequency and exposure time, as follows: (1) 2450 MHz for 30 seconds, (2) 2450
MHz for 45 seconds, (3) 2450 MHz for 60 seconds, (4) 1225 MHz for 30 seconds, (5) 1225 MHz for 45 seconds, and (6) 1225
MHz for 60 seconds. Two control treatments were included: one consisting of inoculated seeds (7) and the other of noninoculated seeds (8), neither of which was treated with radiation. All treatments were conducted under ambient temperature
and humidity conditions.

2.3 Experimental design
The experiments were carried out using a completely randomized design with eight treatments. Each treatment
included 20 replicates for the seed health assay and four replicates for the germination assay. For each variable, the experiment
was repeated twice.
2.4 Seed health assessment
Seed health was evaluated using the filter paper method (blotter test) (BRASIL 2009a). For each treatment, 200 seeds
were assessed, arranged in 20 Petri dishes with 10 seeds per plate. Seeds were individually placed on moistened sterile filter
paper composed of three overlapping filter discs, ensuring a spacing of 1–2 cm between seeds to prevent contact and crosscontamination. Incubation was performed in BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand) chambers under a 12-hour photoperiod at
20 ± 2 °C for 7–8 days. After incubation, seeds were examined individually under a stereomicroscope at 30–80× magnification.
The presence of C. lindemuthianum was confirmed by observing typical fungal structures such as acervuli, setae and
conidiophores with conidia. Results were expressed as the incidence of infection, calculated as the percentage of seeds
presenting fungal structures per plate.
2.5 Germination assay
Germination was assessed using the rolled paper towel method (BRASIL 2009b). Fifty seeds per replicate were
placed between two sheets of moistened germination paper, wrapped into rolls, and incubated in a BOD chamber at 25 °C.
After nine days of incubation, the number of normal seedlings, abnormal seedlings, and dead seeds was recorded. Results were
expressed as percentages for each category.
2.6 Statistical analysis
All percentage data were transformed using the arcsine square root transformation prior to statistical analysis.
Analysis of variance (ANOVA) were performed using the F-test, and treatment means were compared using Tukey’s test at a
5% significance level. All analyses were conducted using the AgroEstat statistical software.

3. Results
The results clearly demonstrate the significant effect of microwave radiation on the incidence of C. lindemuthianum in
common bean seeds, as well as on the physiological performance of the resulting seedlings.
As shown in Table 1, there was a marked reduction in pathogen incidence in seeds treated with microwave radiation
compared to the inoculated control, which exhibited the highest infection rates in both experiments (42.50% and 42.00%). The
most effective treatments in reducing fungal incidence were those with longer exposure times. The pathogen incidence rates in
treatments 3 (2450 MHz for 60 seconds) and 6 (1225 MHz for 60 seconds) were statistically similar to those observed in the
non-inoculated control, in both experiments. This indicates the high effectiveness of radiation treatment in inactivating the
pathogen. Statistically significant differences were observed among treatments, according to Tukey’s test at the 5% probability
level.

4. Discussion
Overall, the data indicate that while microwave thermal treatment holds promise as an effective seed disinfection
method, its success depends on balancing pathogen control with the preservation of seed physiological quality. Lower
frequencies and shorter exposure times may offer a viable strategy to reduce pathogen load while minimizing impacts on seed
vigor.
Given its simplicity, ease of use, and minimal spatial requirements, microwave radiation emerges as a practical and
scalable tool for seed sanitation, with potential integration into agronomic practices aimed at improving seed health and food
security (Kumari et al. 2018). However, its effectiveness and safety are highly dependent on treatment parameters, as
variations in frequency, power, and duration can either preserve or severely compromise seed viability.
Positive outcomes have been reported for tomato and okra seeds treated with 9.3 GHz microwave radiation for
extended periods (4–10 hours), which led to increased production of flavonoids, chlorophyll, and carotenoids, thereby
enhancing germination (Iwuala et al. 2021; Kumari et al. 2018). In maize, seed treatment with microwave (200W/20 seconds)
was effective in increasing the germination index and biomass parameter, and improving its nutricional value (Lu et al. 2024).
In contrast, our findings show that common bean seeds suffered near-total loss of viability after just 45 seconds of exposure at
2450 MHz or 1225 MHz. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in seed characteristics, exposure conditions, and
equipment precision. The high-frequency, gradual treatments applied in shielded environments differ markedly from the lowfrequency, high-penetration exposures used in our study, which lacked thermal control and likely caused rapid internal heating.
Furthermore, bean seeds’ larger size and higher moisture content increase their vulnerability to microwave-induced damage.
Our results align with previous studies reporting that dry bean seed germination begins to decline after approximately
40 seconds of microwave exposure (Friesen et al. 2014a, 2014b, 2017). The germination of Trifolium repens, Portulaca
oleracea and Solanum nigrum seeds was also affected by the microwave treatment, which combined two power levels (480
and 900 W) with two exposure times (10 and 30 seconds) (Fornaciari et al. 2025). The species T. repens and S. nigrum
exhibited similar behavior regarding the effect on germination, whose values were significantly lower under the higher-power
treatment. The germination of P. oleracea was not affected by the low-power treatment, but was drastically reduced at
maximum power for both exposure periods. These consistent findings reinforce the need for precise control of exposure
parameters, considering species and seed characteristics.
Regarding pathogen control, we observed a clear reduction in C. lindemuthianum load with increased exposure time.
However, these conditions were also harmful to seed viability. A similar trade-off was observed by Szopińska and Dorna
(2021), who noted that longer exposure times reduced Alternaria spp. infestation on carrot seeds but also compromised
germination.
Additional support for the antifungal potential of microwave treatment comes from Kanwal et al. (2018), who
demonstrated that short-term exposure (10–30 seconds) to microwave radiation significantly reduced root rot fungi such as
Macrophomina phaseolina, Rhizoctonia solani, and Fusarium spp. in multiple crop seeds, while also promoting plant growth
in chickpea, sunflower, and mash bean under controlled conditions. Similarly, Mattner et al. (2026) observed that microwave
treatment considerably reduced M. phaseolina and Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. fragarie viability in strawberry crowns residues

5. Final Considerations
Taken together, these findings highlight the dual nature of microwave radiation: it is both a promising method for
controlling seed-borne pathogens and a potential risk to seed viability. To harness its full potential, future studies must focus
on optimizing treatment protocols specific to each crop, considering variables such as seed size, water content, and pathogen
pressure. Such tailored approaches are essential for developing safe and sustainable microwave-based seed sanitation strategies
that do not compromise germination or seedling vigor.
