Fully GMP-complient radiosynthesis of [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 using the miniAiO synthesizer for clinical translation
Authors: David Kryza, Pierre Le Fur, Jean Noël Badel, Clément Morgat, Fouzi Khayi, Anne-Laure Giraudet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTerbium‑161 (161Tb) is an emerging radionuclide for targeted radionuclide therapy that combines β⁻ emissions with short‑range conversion and Auger electrons, increasing its efficacy against micrometastatic and low‑volume prostate cancer. This study aimed to develop, optimize, and validate a fully automated good manufacturing practice (GMP)-compliant radiosynthesis of [161Tb]Tb-PSMA‑1 for clinical application. Radiolabeling parameters were systematically investigated via different buffer systems (acetate, ammonium acetate, and ascorbate), labeling temperatures (24–95 °C), and ethanol supplementation. Process validation was performed on three independent batches produced under routine conditions (7.4–11.5 GBq) via a Trasis MiniAiO synthesizer. Comprehensive quality control included radionuclidic identification, radiochemical purity (RCP), pH, sterility, endotoxin testing, and container–content interaction assessment.ResultsEfficient radiolabeling was achieved under all the tested conditions, with RCPs consistently exceeding 97%. Optimal performance was observed when ascorbate buffer was used at 95 °C, yielding approximately 99% RCP at the end of synthesis. The addition of 40% (V/V) ethanol improved both the labeling efficiency and radiochemical stability, enabling high RCP even at lower temperatures. The final formulation demonstrated excellent stability, maintaining RCP ≥ 97.5% up to 48 h post-synthesis. All validation batches met predefined release criteria in accordance with Ph. Eur. Monograph radiopharmaceutical preparations. The total synthesis time was less than 30 min.ConclusionA robust, rapid, and fully automated GMP-compliant method for [161Tb]Tb-PSMA‑1 production was successfully established. The high radiochemical purity, stability, and reproducibility support its suitability for routine clinical use and decentralized distribution. This work represents a key step toward the clinical translation of [161Tb]Tb-labeled PSMA radioligands for improved management of advanced prostate cancer.Keywords: [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1, Prostate cancer, Radiolabeling optimization, MiniAio synthesizer, Terbium-161

Background

Prostate cancer remains one of the most common cancers and the cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among men worldwide, and its incidence continues to increase in elderly patients (Zhang et al. 1990; Wang et al. 2022; Sung et al. 2021). Over the past decade, prostate–specific membrane antigen (PSMA) has emerged as an important molecular target in oncologic imaging and therapy. PSMA is a type II transmembrane glycoprotein that is highly expressed in prostate cancer cells—especially in advanced, metastatic, and castration-resistant stages—whose expression is limited in most benign tissues (Eder et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2025; Nauseef et al. 2021). This differential expression has driven the rapid development of PSMA-targeted radiopharmaceuticals (Jafari et al. 2020; Maes et al. 2024; Kranzbühler et al. 2018). Clinically, [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 and [18F]F-PSMA-1007 have changed disease staging through highly sensitive detection of biochemical recurrence and metastases (Hoffmann et al. 2022; Kuten et al. 2020). Following this diagnostic advancement, the introduction of β−-emitting therapeutic radioligands such as [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-617 (vipivotide tetraxetan, Pluvicto™) has provided an effective systemic therapy option for patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (Sartor et al. 2021; Murthy et al. 2024). Despite its therapeutic success, β⁻-particle irradiation alone may be insufficient for eradicating low-volume lesions, disseminated micrometastases, or single malignant cells—disease categories that remain clinically challenging (Hindié et al. 2016; Karimi et al. 2026; O’Donoghue et al. 1995).

Terbium-161 (161Tb) represents a promising next-generation therapeutic radionuclide that may address these limitations (Buteau et al. 2025; Xiao et al. 2025; Lee and Manning 2026). The development of several new radiopharmaceuticals with terbium-161 is actively ongoing (Trejtnar et al. 2025). While its β− emission energies are close to those of lutetium-177 (mean β− energy is 133.3 keV for lutetium-177 vs 154.3 keV for terbium-161), terbium-161 also releases low-energy conversion electrons and Auger electrons. These particles have a very short range, typically on the nanometer to micrometer scale, and deposit their energy with extremely high linear energy transfer (LET). This results in the formation of dense ionization clusters capable of inducing complex, and often irreparable, DNA double-strand breaks [Lee et al. 2026; Van Laere et al. 2024; Bernhardt et al. 2021]. This unique emission profile suggests that terbium-161 may be able to achieve enhanced cytotoxicity, particularly in small-volume and disseminated disease, thereby improving the radiobiological effectiveness of PSMA-targeted therapies (Bernhardt et al. 2021; Xiao et al. 2026a). From a chemical and radiopharmaceutical perspective, terbium-161 behaves similarly to other trivalent lanthanides (Ln3+) such as Lu3+, which allows it to form stable coordination complexes with commonly used macrocyclic chelators such as DOTA or DOTAGA (Ntihabose et al. 2025; Favaretto et al. 2023). These complexes are known for their thermodynamic stability and kinetic inertness under physiological conditions, which are essential properties for ensuring safe in vivo application of therapeutic radiometals. [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-1 is a radioligand therapy using PSMA-1 (Glu-CO-Lys[(Sub)DLys-DPhe-DTyr(3I)-DOTAGA]) indicated for the treatment of metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer. Building on established onsite experience with [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-1 radiolabeling (Kryza et al. 2024), the present work focuses on translating 161Tb radiolabeling into a reliable, GMP-oriented automated synthesis process in order to ensure security, consistency and compliance with regulatory standards. Automated manufacturing presents several advantages for clinical radiopharmaceutical production (Ko et al. 2025). It ensures the standardization of critical steps—including pH adjustment, thermal control, and reagent transfer—thereby minimizing operator–dependent variability (Schulte and Rosenberg 2025). Automation, when implemented using a closed and validated system within an appropriate GMP environment, supports aseptic processing and reduces the risk of contamination, reduces operator radiation exposure in accordance with ALARA principles, and enhances reproducibility for GMP compliance.

In this study, we report a reproducible and fast fully automated synthesis of [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 using the Trasis MiniAiO synthesizer. Specifically, we (1) evaluated the optimized radiochemical reaction parameters—including buffer strength (commonly used for lutetium-177 radiolabeling), and radiolabeling temperature,—to obtain efficient radiolabeling with high radiochemical purity; (2) validated the synthesis process via the manufacture of three consecutive and independent batches; and (3) evaluated the stability for clinical application. Overall, this work aims to facilitate the advancement of [161Tb]Tb-based PSMA radioligands toward routine clinical application.

Materials and methods

Reagents and apparatus

All reagents employed in this study were high‑purity commercial grade and were used without any additional purification. Radioactivity measurements were carried out with a VDC‑606 dose calibrator operating with the IBC Lite software integrated into the ELENA isolator (Comecer, Castel Bolognese RA, Italy).

Radiosyntheses were performed on a mini AiO module (Trasis, Ans, Belgium), and all spikes, manifolds, and disposable cassettes were sourced from the same manufacturer. The PSMA‑1 ligand (115 µg) was supplied by ITM Isotope Technologies Munich SE. Sodium acetate trihydrate (Emsure®), ultrapure water (UPW, Lichrosolv®), acetonitrile (Lichrosolv®), glacial acetic acid (Emsure®) and ammonium acetate (Emprove®) were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Sodium ascorbate and ascorbic acide were purchased from Cooper (Lyon, France). A 0.22‑µm Millipore membrane filtration unit was obtained from Merck‑Millipore (Molsheim, France). Trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, Reagent‑Plus®) was acquired from Sigma‑Aldrich (Saint‑Quentin‑Fallavier, France). No-carrier-containing terbium 161 solutions were obtained from Terthera (Bréda, the Netherlands) as a solution in 0.05 M HCl with a radioactive concentration of 15.5 GBq/mL.

To reduce the risk of metal contamination, silicone needles (Sterican®, BBraun Melsungen, Germany) and polypropylene syringes (BD Plastipak®, Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) were systematically used. Aeration needles, 0.9% sodium chloride solution, and water for injection were provided by BBraun Medical. Acetate buffers (1 M and 2 M, pH 5.5) were prepared by dissolving sodium acetate trihydrate (136.1 g·L−1 for 1 M; 272.2 g·L−1 for 2 M) in umltrapure water, followed by adjustment of the pH to 5.5 using glacial acetic acid. Ammonium acetate buffers (1 M and 2 M, pH 5.5) were prepared by dissolving ammonium acetate (77.1 g·L−1 for 1 M; 154.2 g·L−1 for 2 M) in water and adjusting the pH to 5.5 with acetic acid as required. The ascorbate buffer was freshly prepared by dissolving 160 mg of sodium ascorbate and 40 mg of ascorbic acid in 2 mL of deionized water, yielding a reducing buffer system.

For quality‑control procedures, pH values were determined via Duotest indicator strips (pH 3.5–6.8) from Macherey‑Nagel (GmbH & Co. KG, Düren, Germany). Thin‑layer chromatography (TLC) was performed using an eluent composed of ammonium acetate (77 g/L) / methanol (1:1) mixture purchased from Trasis (Ans, Belgium), together with ITLC Biodex tec-control black plates from Biodex Medical Systems (Shirley, NY, USA). Chromatographic plates were evaluated via a ScanRam 2 radio‑TLC system (LabLogic Systems Ltd., Sheffield S10 2QJ, UK) equipped with a PS plastic/PMT detector, and the data were processed via Laura v4.2.50 SP3 software.

Analytical radio‑HPLC was conducted on a Dionex rapid‑separation liquid chromatography system (Voisin‑le‑Bretonneux, France) equipped with an autosampler, UV detector, and gamma flow detector (Bioscan, Washington DC, USA). Separation was carried out on a Poroshell 120 EC‑C18 column (3 × 150 mm, 4 µm; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Data acquisition and processing were performed via the Chromeleon chromatography 6.8 software package.

Quality control methods

Several quality control procedures, adapted from the European Pharmacopoeia monograph for [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T (i.e. [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-1) for injection (pharmacopoeia) (EDQM 2025) and and the general radiopharmaceutical preparation monograph (0125), were implemented to verify the product’s quality and to ensure that no impurities were present in the final vial.

The pH was assessed via indicator strips, with an acceptable range between 4 and 7. Thin-Layer chromatography (TLC) was performed to determine the proportion of 161Tb-species using methanol/1 M ammonium acetate (1:1 v/v) as the mobile phase. Under the selected conditions, terbium-161 species remained at the origin, whereas [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 migrated with the solvent front, with an Rf value greater than 0.7. Radiochemical purity was calculated as the fraction of [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 radioactivity relative to the total radioactive species detected on the TLC chromatogram.

For radio-HPLC characterization, [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 was analyzed on an Poroshell 120 EC‑C18 column (3 × 150 mm). The mobile phase was delivered at 0.6 mL·min⁻1, starting with 85% phase A (A: 0.1% TFA in water; B: 0.1% TFA in acetonitrile) from 0 to 1 min, followed by a gradient to 20% A between 1 and 3 min. This was followed by an isocratic step with 80% B for 3 min, and then a gradient returning to 85% A over 6 min (Fig. 3). A 40 µL aliquot of the preparation was injected for analysis. Under these conditions, the retention times were 11.5 min for [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 and 1.6 min for free 161Tb.

Gradient profile used in the radio-HPLC method. Grey (0.1%TFA in acetonitrile) %; blue (0.1%TFA in water)%

Before batch validation, the radio-HPLC analytical method was validated according to ICH guideline Q2(R2) with respect to specificity, linearity, repeatability, limit of detection (LOD), and limit of quantification (LOQ). Specificity was assessed by analyzing terbium-161 species and [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 to verify the absence of interfering peaks at the retention time of the analyte. Linearity was evaluated by constructing calibration curves over the studied concentration range (n = 10). The peak area was plotted against concentration, and linear regression analysis was performed to determine the slope, intercept, and coefficient of determination (r2). Repeatability (intra-day precision) was assessed by analyzing replicate injections (n = 10) of the same sample, and results were expressed as the coefficient of variation (CV%). The LODs and LOQs were calculated according to ICH Q2(R1) from the standard deviation of the response (SD) and the slope of the calibration curve (S), using the equations LOD = 3.3 × SD/S and LOQ = 10 × SD/S. The SD was obtained from the residual standard deviation of the regression. Validation results were considered acceptable when: r2 ≥ 0.997 (linearity), CV ≤ 2% (repeatability) and resolution between terbium-161- species and [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 ≥ 1.5.

Radiochemical purity was also assessed at 24 and 48 h after radiosynthesis to evaluate radiochemical stability under clinical relevant conditions, although longer stability may be expected given the physical half‑life of terbium‑161. The radionuclide was identified by evaluating its radioactive half-life through measurement of the decay curve via a VDC-606 activimeter (Comecer), calibrated for terbium-161. In addition, gamma spectrometry was performed using a CAMBERRA detector to confirm the main characteristic photopeaks (49 and 75 keV). Sterility was assessed by inoculating samples into appropriate culture media immediately after injection during 14 days. Anaerobic microorganisms were tested via incubation of thioglycolate broth at 33 °C, whereas aerobic bacteria and filamentous fungi were tested via incubation of tryptic soy broth at 23 °C. The minimum inoculation volume was 1 mL.

Bacterial endotoxins were quantified via the Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) assay with an ENDOSAFE® portable reader (Charles River). The endotoxin testing method followed Method D of the European Pharmacopoeia monograph 2.6.14. Measurements were performed via a PTS Nexgen® reader (Charles River, Wilmington, MA, USA). The samples were diluted 1:10, resulting in a detection limit of 0.5 IU/mL under these conditions. Bioburden was assessed prior to the final sterilizing filtration step using standard microbiological methods (Direct Growth Agar Test (DGAT), and Direct Membrane Liquid Test (DMLT). Samples (1 mL) were tested under aseptic conditions, and microbial growth was evaluated after appropriate incubation.

At the end of each clinical batch radiosynthesis, a bubble point test was performed to assess the integrity of the filter.

A container–content interaction (CCI) study was performed to evaluate the compatibility between the radiopharmaceutical formulation and the primary container, with particular focus on the potential adsorption of the active compound onto the inner surface of the vial.

To maximize contact between the solution and the inner surface, vials were stored in an inverted position throughout the study. Three independent batches of [161Tb]Tb‑PSMA‑1 were transferred into 25 mL glass vials (Huayi Isotopes Co., China) and stored upside down at 25 ± 3 °C for 24 h. At predefined time points (T0, 2 h, 5 h, and 24 h), samples were withdrawn and the radioactivity in the solution was measured. All measured activities were decay-corrected to the initial time point (T0) to account for radioactive decay. Adsorption to the container surface was assessed by comparing the decay-corrected volumic activity at each time point to the initial volumic activity. A decrease in volumic activity over time would indicate potential adsorption of the radiopharmaceutical onto the container surface. The absence of significant container–content interaction was concluded if the decay-corrected volumic activity remained within ± 10% of the initial value (i.e., between 90 and 110% of the initial volumic activity). Minor deviations within this range were considered acceptable and attributed to analytical and sampling variability.

Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Given the limited sample size (n = 3), no formal statistical hypothesis testing was performed. Comparisons between experimental conditions are described qualitatively based on observed trends.

Results

Influence of the buffer and its concentration on [161Tb]Tb‑PSMA‑1 radiolabeling

For each experimental condition (n = 3), the radiolabeling reactions were carried out at room temperature, 40 °C or 95 °C for 20 min using a fixed PSMA-1 amount of 0.013 µg/MBq. Table 1 presents the different radiolabeling conditions investigated and the corresponding radiochemical outcomes are summarized in Table 3. Regardless of whether acetate (1 M or 2 M) or ammonium acetate (1 M or 2 M) was used as the buffer, the radiochemical purity consistently exceeded 97%, with no specific difference. A representative radio-HPLC chromatogram of [1⁶1Tb]Tb‑PSMA‑1 is presented in Fig. 4. The highest RCPs were observed by using ascorbate buffer and heating at 95 °C for radiolabeling, which achieved approximately 99% radiochemical purity at end of synthesis (EOS). At each time point (T = 0, 24 h and 48 h), the use of ascorbate buffer at 95 °C resulted in higher efficiency compared to that of acetate and ammonium acetate buffers at both 1 M and 2 M, with the strongest differences observed after 48 h.

Microbiological evaluation

The level of bacterial endotoxins measured in the [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 preparation via the efficient radiosynthesis process (i.e., ascorbate buffer at 95 °C) was less than 7.95 IU/mL, fully complying with the European Pharmacopoeia requirement, which sets a maximum limit of 2.5 IU/kg. Sterility testing was performed over 14 days in accordance with Ph. Eur. 2.6.1. In addition, microbiological stability of [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 after preparation was assessed over 24 h. The integrity of the final sterilizing filter was also verified via a bubble-point test, and in each experiment, the obtained values met the manufacturer’s specified criteria. A bioburden test was performed using a 1 mL test sample. DGAT (Direct Growth Agar Test) and DMLT (Direct Membrane Liquid Test) bioburden tests revealed no detectable microbial growth. The results were below the limit of quantification (< 0.1 CFU/mL), demonstrating an extremely low bioburden level and compliance with applicable acceptance criteria.

Analytical method validation

Before performing batch validations, HPLC analytical methods should be validated. The parameters analyzed to validate the radio-HPLC method were specificity, linearity, repeatability, limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) in accordance with selected principles of the ICH guidelines “validation of analytical procedures validation Q2(R2)”. The analytical method demonstrated satisfactory performance for all evaluated validation parameters. The method linearity was excellent across the tested concentration and volumic activity range (5–100 µg/mL for UV detection and 0–1 GBq/mL for radiometric detection) with a determination coefficient (r2) of 0.9998. The LOD assessed by the UV channel was estimated at 0.85 µg/mL, whereas radiometric detection achieved an LOD below 1% of the total activity. The repeatability also met the validation requirements, with very low variability observed for both UV (CV = 0.18%) and radiometric detection (CV = 0.65%). The LOQ by UV was determined to be 2.55 µg/mL, and radiometric quantification was achieved below 2% of the total activity. Overall, all the validation criteria were successfully fulfilled, confirming the suitability of the method for reliable analysis.

Batch evaluation and validation of the manufacturing process

Process validation was conducted by the manufacture of three consecutive and independent batches of [161Tb]Tb‑PSMA‑1 under conditions representative of routine production and within the activity range (7.4–11.5 GBq) in a 22 mL volume specified in the predefined acceptance criteria using condition 3 at 95 °C for radiolabeling. The results of the three finished product validation batches ([161Tb]Tb‑PSMA‑1) obtained using the GMP PSMA‑1 precursor are presented in Table 2. The whole radiosynthesis time was less than 30 min and the radiochemical yield was over 92%. The specifications were established in accordance with the European Pharmacopoeia general monograph for radiopharmaceutical preparations (0125) and adapted from the monograph for [177Lu]Lu‑labelled PSMA compounds (Ko et al. 2025). All process validation batches successfully complied with the quality control release acceptance criteria.

Container interaction studies

In this test, three batches of [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 collected in 25 mL glass vials were placed upside down for approximately 24 h. The measured activities were decay-corrected and compared with the initial radioactivity concentration of the [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 stock solution to assess the extent of radioactivity adsorption by the container. The measured sample activity remained largely within 10% (0.23%, 0.01% and 0.05% for the three test batches) of the initial value, indicating the absence of any container–content interaction.

Discussion

The present work demonstrates the development, optimization, and validation of automated GMP radiosynthesis of [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 via the Trasis MiniAiO synthesizer. Given the growing interest in the use of terbium‑161 as a next‑generation therapeutic radionuclide, this study aims to address a critical translational gap by establishing a robust, reproducible, and clinically implementable manufacturing process suitable for the routine production of [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1. Owing to the strong interest in the evaluation of terbium 161 in radionuclide therapy (Ji et al. 2026), we investigated the influence of several key parameters (buffer, labeling temperature) that are able to optimize radiochemical purity and stability via the use of a synthesized radiolabeling process. High radiochemical purities were achieved across a broad range of buffer systems and concentrations. Both acetate and ammonium acetate buffers supported efficient complexation of terbium-161 with PSMA‑1, yielding radiochemical purities exceeding 97% under standard heating conditions without the need for a solid-phase extraction (SPE) purification process for removing unreacted free 161Tb (TC18 Sep Pak or Oasis HLB® cartridges) as observed for [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-617 (Uygur et al. 2024). This robustness, reflected by the consistently high radiochemical purity obtained across the different radiolabeling conditions tested is particularly relevant from a GMP perspective, as it provides operational flexibility and reduces vulnerability to batch failure caused by minor variations in reagent preparation. Among the evaluated conditions, the ascorbate buffer produced the highest initial radiochemical purities (≈99%), likely reflecting its dual role as a buffering agent and radiolytic stabilizer (Iorio et al. 2022). The inclusion of ascorbate may mitigate oxidative degradation pathways that are particularly relevant for radionuclides emitting high‑LET Auger and converting electrons, such as terbium-161 (Bolcaen et al. 2023).

Temperature was confirmed to be a critical parameter influencing labeling kinetics, yet the data indicate that efficient radiolabeling can also be achieved at room temperature when ethanol is included in the reaction mixture. The presence of 40% ethanol during radiolabeling markedly improved radiochemical purity and stability (Ji et al. 2026). At both 24 °C and 40 °C, ethanol-containing formulations presented consistently high RCP values (> 98.8%) at all time points, with minimal degradation occurring over 48 h.

Notably, even when radiolabeling was performed at 40 °C, ethanol effectively mitigated the decline in RCP observed under aqueous conditions, indicating a protective effect against temperature-induced radiochemical instability during labeling. While elevated temperatures are advantageous for shortening reaction times (Larenkov et al. 2024), the successful labeling observed under milder conditions highlights the favorable coordination chemistry of terbium with DOTAGA chelates and decreases the amount of radiochemical impurities in our experiments at later time points (24 and 48 h post EOS). This observation may prove valuable for future optimization efforts aimed at further simplifying synthesis workflows or reducing thermal stress on more labile targeting vectors. Although 40% ethanol could be use during the radiolabeling to increase stability, the [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 solution should be dilute with 0.9% NaCl to ensensure compliance with pharmacopoeial requirements. A gas chromatography method should be used to evaluate the ethanol concentration in radiopharmaceutical preparations within 10% (v/v) in radiopharmaceutical formulations (International Council for Harmonization. Impurities: guideline for residual solventsQ3C(R8) 2021).

Importantly, the radiochemical stability of [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 remained excellent for all the tested formulations, with radiochemical purity consistently maintained above 97% for up to 48 h post‑synthesis. This stability profile compares favorably with published data for 177Lu‑labeled PSMA inhibitors and is particularly encouraging given the additional low‑energy electron emissions associated with terbium-161, which could theoretically exacerbate radiolysis. The demonstrated 24‑hour stability under routine storage conditions directly supports clinical feasibility, including delayed administration and regional distribution possibilities.

The analytical and microbiological evaluations further confirmed the readiness of the process for clinical deployment. The validated radio‑HPLC method met all the ICH Q2(R2) performance criteria, providing reliable discrimination between [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1, free terbium-161, and potential radiochemical impurities. Microbiological testing consistently demonstrated sterility, extremely low bioburden, and endotoxin levels far below pharmacopoeial limits. Together with successful filter integrity testing and container–content interaction studies, these results highlight the overall pharmaceutical quality and safety of the final formulation.

Process validation via three consecutive GMP-grade batches confirmed excellent batch‑to‑batch reproducibility across all critical quality attributes, including activity yield, radionuclidic identity, radiochemical purity, and sterility. The alignment of these results with established European Pharmacopoeia expectations for therapeutic radiopharmaceuticals further strengthens the clinical translatability of this work. Notably, the synthesis framework, which could be achieved in less than 30 min, is readily compatible with activity ranges relevant to patient dosing (up to 11.5 GBq of 161Tb (Buteau et al. 2025)) with a specific activity of around 82 MBq/µg, reinforcing its applicability beyond proof‑of‑concept production.

From a broader perspective, this study contributes practical manufacturing evidence supporting the clinical advancement of 161Tb‑labeled PSMA inhibitors. While the enhanced radiobiological effectiveness of terbium-161 has been demonstrated primarily in preclinical settings (Xiao et al. 2026b), the availability of a reliable, automated, and GMP‑compliant production process is a prerequisite for meaningful clinical evaluation. Due to their similar coordination chemistry as trivalent lanthanides, terbium‑161 and lutetium‑177 exhibit comparable radiolabeling behavior ans quality control approaches, thereby facilitating the adoption of terbium‑based therapies in clinical centers. Although developed on a Trasis MiniAiO platform, the process relies on standard radiopharmaceutical operations and could be adapted to other automated synthesizers with appropriate optimization, supporting feasibility of technology transfer.

Conclusion

In this study, we report a fully automated GMP radiosynthesis of [161Tb]Tb-PSMA-1 using a MiniAiO synthesizer that meets all the requirements for clinical radiopharmaceutical production. The method enures a high level of radiochemical purity, remarkable stability, and adherence to both pharmacopeial and regulatory quality standards, all while being suitable for standard hospital manufacturing procedures.

The robustness of the synthesis is supported by the use of ascorbate buffer and established quality control protocols, which contribute to high radiochemical purity and stability. The successful validation of multiple GMP batches, together with demonstrated stability over 24 h, supports its suitability for clinical use. This also enables potential decentralized distribution. Overall, this work supports the clinical translation of [161Tb]Tb‑PSMA‑1 for prostate cancer treatment, particularly for micrometastatic and low‑volume disease.
