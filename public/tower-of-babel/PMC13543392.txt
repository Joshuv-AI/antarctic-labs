Impact of vitamin D deficiency on hyperlipidemia and hyperuricemia in pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus in Bangladesh
Authors: Mohammad Abul Hasnat, Mosammat Halima Khanam, Syeda Ummul Khair Fatima, Zafrul Hasan, Md. Waseque Mia, Jubayer Khan, Md. Mostafij Talukder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVitamin D (Vit-D) deficiency is considered a path to metabolic disturbance during pregnancy, especially among women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). This study aimed to investigate the impact of Vit-D status on hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, and hypocalcemia in pregnant women with GDM in Bangladesh.Materials and methodsSerum biochemical parameters were measured in 335 pregnant women using an automated chemistry and immunoassay analyzer. The associations among these parameters were investigated using the IBM SPSS statistical package. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsAmong the population, 80.30% and 19.70% were without GDM and GDM, respectively. 30.45%, 48.36%, and 21.19% of the participants were identified as Vit-D sufficient (> 30 ng/mL), insufficient (20–30 ng/mL), and, deficient (< 20 ng/mL) respectively, with a higher prevalence of Vit-D deficiency and insufficiency in the second trimester during pregnancy. Serum Vit-D levels were significantly positively associated with calcium and HDL levels, whereas negative associations were observed with uric acid, fasting blood sugar (FBS), cholesterol (Cho), triglycerides (TG), and LDL (p < 0.05). Remarkably, significantly (P < 0.01) increased levels of uric acid, Cho, TG, LDL, and FBS along with decreased Ca and HDL were observed in the Vit-D-deficient plus GDM group, compared to Vit-D-sufficient plus without GDM group.ConclusionPregnant women with GDM, particularly those with Vit-D deficiency, are significantly associated with a high risk of hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, and hypocalcemia. The outcomes of this study emphasize the importance of routine Vit-D screening during pregnancy to minimize the threat of hostile metabolic outcomes during pregnancy and the postpartum period.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

The vitamin D[25(OH)D] (Vit-D) deficiency has emerged as a global public health concern, predominantly among pregnant women, because of its potential implications for maternal and fetal health [1]. Despite increased physiological demands during pregnancy, Vit-D insufficiency and deficiency remain prevalent even in regions with abundant sunlight, dietary insufficiencies, and increased metabolic needs [1, 2]. In addition to its classical role in calcium (Ca) and bone homeostasis, Vit-D has been recognized as a vital modulator of lipid and glucose metabolism, inflammation, and oxidative stress [3, 4]. In particular, Vit-D deficiency has been implicated in the pathogenesis of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) [5, 6], a condition characterized by impaired glucose tolerance that is first detected during pregnancy [7, 8]. Its incidence is noticeably increasing globally, posing significant short- and long-term health risks to both the mother and the fetus [9, 10], including the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), metabolic disorders, and kidney and cardiovascular diseases even later in life [11, 12].

Recent studies have suggested that hypovitaminosis-D is associated with the dysregulation of lipid metabolism, elevated insulin resistance, and increased oxidative stress, all of which may contribute to the pathophysiological cascade of GDM [13, 14]. Vit-D deficiency may increase metabolic complications, including hyperlipidemia and hyperuricemia, which increase the risk of cardiovascular diseases and kidney diseases with systemic inflammation in uncontrolled T2DM patients in addition to women with GDM [15, 16]. Elevated uric acid concentrations have been linked to endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and increased cardiovascular risk in pregnant women [17, 18].

Currently, hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, and hypocalcemia are very prevalent physiological abnormalities that very frequently occur in pregnant women. Vit-D deficiency and GDM may play pivotal roles in these metabolic abnormalities. These triadic correlations among serum Vit-D status, uric acid levels, and lipid profiles in pregnant women with GDM remain poorly understood in Southeast Asian regions, particularly in Bangladesh. Most Bangladeshi studies have looked at Vit-D and GDM separately (e.g., lower Vit-D in GDM vs. controls) [19]. Some work has also evaluated serum uric acid in GDM mothers, but not in conjunction with Vit-D and detailed lipid parameters. There is no comprehensive dataset that examines all three serum biomarkers, Vit-D, uric acid, and lipid profile from the same population at the same time point [19, 20]. These interrelationships among the considered biochemical parameters could offer novel insights into metabolic disturbances in pregnant women with Vit-D deficiency and GDM and potentially lead to early intervention policies. This study aims to explore the associations of serum Vit-D levels with the levels of serum uric acid, Ca, and lipids that cause hyperuricemia, hypocalcemia, and hyperlipidemia, respectively, in pregnant women diagnosed with and without GDM, with the goal of contributing to pregnant women as well as to the growing body of evidence regarding micronutrient status and metabolic complications during pregnancy.

Methods

Study place, population, duration, and approval

This study was carried out in the Department of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology (BMB) of Shahjalal University of Science and Technology (SUST), Sylhet-3114, Bangladesh, from May 2024 to August 2025. The technical support was obtained from a well-known diagnostic centre named Medinova Medical Services Ltd. Sylhet, Bangladesh.

Pregnant women attending the Biochemistry and Immunology Laboratory of Medinova Medical Services Ltd. were initially screened for pregnancy using a rapid HCG pregnancy test cassette (Lot: HCG-1240705, Econ Diagnostics Ltd., UK). Women with confirmed pregnancy were invited to participate in the study. After explaining the objectives of the study, written informed consent was obtained from those who agreed to participate.

A total of 335 pregnant women out of 511 (response rate = 65.5%) who met the inclusion criteria and provided consent were enrolled in the study. Pregnant women with pre-existing type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus, renal disease, thyroid disorders, chronic inflammatory conditions, or those undergoing treatment for Vit-D deficiency, hypocalcemia, or lipid-lowering medications were excluded.

Demographic information and routine biochemical test results were obtained from the laboratory records of Medinova Medical Services Ltd. with permission from the institution and the participants. Blood samples had already been collected by the diagnostic centre as part of routine clinical investigations. For the purposes of this study, portions of the same collected samples were obtained from the laboratory before routine disposal and used for additional biochemical analyses that were not performed at the diagnostic centre. These supplementary biochemical estimations were subsequently carried out at the Clinical Laboratory of the Department of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology, Shahjalal University of Science and Technology (SUST), Sylhet, Bangladesh.

Pregnant women were classified as Vit-D deficient (< 20 ng/mL), insufficient (20–30 ng/mL), or sufficient (> 30 ng/mL) [18]. Additionally, pregnant women were classified into the first, second, and third trimesters based on their gestational age (weeks) and β-hCG levels. On the basis of fasting blood sugar (FBS) levels, pregnant women were categorised into without GDM and with GDM (FBS ≥ 6.5 mmol/L) groups [19, 20].

Specimen collection, preparation, and preservation

The required samples, including urine and blood, from pregnant women were withdrawn following standardized protocols [21, 22]. For the rapid pregnancy test, approximately 3–4 mL of the first morning urine in a sterile glass tube, for fasting blood sugar (FBS) estimation, approximately 3 ml of fasting venous blood in sodium fluoride containing gray-cap blood collection tube, and for the estimation of other pre-specified biochemical parameters such as Ca, uric acid, and lipid profile, β-hCG, and Vit-D levels, approximately 3 + 3=6 mL of venous blood in two different clot activator containing red-cap tubes were collected from each participant [23, 24]. The serum was separated from the withdrawn blood after centrifugation at 4000 rpm and 37 °C for 20 min, after which biochemical assays were performed. In the case of biochemical assays, we collected some estimated clinical data that aligned with our study from that diagnostic laboratory; then, we collected samples (serum) from that specific individual and preserved them in a -80 °C freezer following a standard protocol [21, 22] to determine the remaining clinical parameters of the participant at the Clinical Laboratory of the Department of BMB, SUST, Bangladesh.

Biochemical assays

The uric acid, Ca, lipid profile (cholesterol, TG, HDL, and LDL), and FBS levels were estimated via an automatic chemistry analyser (Dimension RxL Max Integrated Chemistry System, SIEMENS, USA) at the Biochemistry and Immunology Laboratory, Medinova Medical Services Ltd., Sylhet as well as via a semiautomatic biochemistry analyser (Humalyzer 3000, USA) in the Clinical Laboratory at the Department of BMB, SUST, Sylhet, Bangladesh. The β-hCG and vitamin D (25-hydroxyvitamin D) levels of the pregnant women were detected via the “ARCHITECT i1000SR immunoassay analyser”. The accuracy of all the biochemical analyses was confirmed through standard calibration and running quality control regularly following a standardized protocol.

Statistical analysis

All collected demographic and clinical data as well as estimated values of targeted parameters were analysed via IBM SPSS version − 27. The associations of serum Vit-D levels with the levels of serum uric acid, Ca, FBS, and lipid profiles (Cho, TG, HDL, and LDL) were initially investigated using simple linear regression analysis. The correlations among all the biochemical parameters in pregnant women were examined by Pearson correlation analysis. Occurrence of various disease conditions due to abnormal levels of relevant biochemical parameters in pregnant women with Vit-D deficiency and GDM were analysed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was performed to compare the levels of all studied biochemical parameters between various groups of participants. In addition, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate the independent associations between serum Vit-D levels and metabolic biomarkers (uric acid, Ca, FBS, and lipid profile parameters) after adjusting for potential confounding variables, including age, pregnancy week, region, education, job status, vitamin D supplement use, and betel-nut consumption. For non-glucose outcomes, GDM status was additionally included as a covariate, whereas it was not included in the FBS model due to definitional overlap. These multivariable analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.11, Python Software Foundation, USA) with the statsmodels library (version 0.14.0). Model assumptions were assessed prior to analysis. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance, whereas a p value ≥ 0.05 was considered not significant (NS).

Results

Prevalence of Vit-D and GDM

Among the study population, 30.45%, 48.36%, and 21.19% of pregnant women were classified as vitamin D sufficient, insufficient, and deficient, respectively (Fig. 1A). Assessment of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) status revealed that 80.30% of participants were non-GDM, while 19.70% were diagnosed with GDM (Fig. 1B). Moreover, the pregnancy trimesters of the pregnant women were evaluated (Fig. 1C), and the prevalence of Vit-D deficiency and insufficiency was examined by trimester. Notably, the prevalence of Vit-D insufficiency and deficiency was higher during the second and third trimesters (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, the incidence of GDM was markedly greater among women with Vit-D deficiency and insufficiency compared to those who were Vit-D sufficient (Fig. 1D).

Associations of Vit-D with uric acid, Ca, lipid profile, and FBS

The associations between serum Vit-D levels and metabolic biomarkers, including uric acid, Ca, FBS, and lipid profiles parameters (Cho, TG, HDL, and LDL) were investigated.

Fig. 2Association of Vit-D with serum uric acid (A), Ca (B), FBS (C), and with lipid profile (D, E, F, and G) by Linear regression analysis

Association of Vit-D with serum uric acid (A), Ca (B), FBS (C), and with lipid profile (D, E, F, and G) by Linear regression analysis

Statistically significant positive correlations were observed between serum Vit-D concentrations and serum calcium (Fig. 2B) as well as HDL levels (Fig. 2F), indicating that lower Vit-D concentrations were associated with reduced Ca and HDL levels among pregnant women. Conversely, significant negative associations were identified between serum Vit-D levels and uric acid, FBS, Cho, TG, and LDL concentrations (Fig. 2A, C–E, and G). These inverse relationships suggest that decreasing serum Vit-D concentrations are associated with increased levels of these metabolic risk markers, potentially contributing to the development of adverse metabolic conditions during pregnancy. These associations were further evaluated using multivariable linear regression analysis to account for potential confounding factors (Table 3; Fig. 3).

Correlation analysis of biochemical parameters

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships among the levels of Vit-D, uric acid, Ca, the lipid profile (Cho, TG, HDL, and LDL), and FBS in pregnant women. According to this correlation analysis method, Vit-D levels were significantly (p < 0.05) and positively correlated with serum Ca (p = 0.006) and HDL (p < 0.001) in pregnant women. In contrast, significant inverse correlations were observed between Vit-D levels and serum uric acid, Cho, TG, LDL, and FBS (all p < 0.05), consistent with the findings of the linear regression analysis. As presented in Table 2, the FBS level was significantly (p < 0.05) and positively correlated with the levels of serum uric acid (p < 0.001), Cho (p = 0.014), TG (p = 0.001), and LDL (p = 0.003).

On the other hand, the levels of serum Ca and HDL in pregnant women were significantly (p < 0.05) and negatively correlated with their FBS concentrations. Adjusted multivariable associations of serum Vit-D with metabolic biomarkers

To strengthen the analysis beyond unadjusted comparisons, multivariable linear regression models were fitted to examine the association of serum Vit-D with FBS, uric acid, calcium, cholesterol, TG, HDL, and LDL after adjustment for the measured covariates available in the dataset. In these adjusted models, higher Vit-D remained significantly associated with lower FBS (beta = -0.028, 95% CI: -0.040 to -0.016), lower uric acid (beta = -0.014, 95% CI: -0.018 to -0.009), lower cholesterol (beta = -0.479, 95% CI: -0.901 to -0.056), and lower TG (beta = -0.737, 95% CI: -1.019 to -0.455), as well as with higher HDL (beta = 0.076, 95% CI: 0.011 to 0.142). The association with calcium was weaker and borderline (beta = 0.006, 95% CI: -0.000 to 0.012), whereas the association with LDL was not clearly supported after adjustment (beta = -0.132, 95% CI: -0.299 to 0.034). These adjusted findings indicate that the associations of Vit-D with several metabolic biomarkers remained present even after accounting for measured covariates, particularly for FBS, uric acid, TG, and HDL (Table 3; Fig. 3). Clinical abnormalities linked to Vit-D deficiency and GDM in pregnant women, as assessed through biochemical parameters with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA)

The ANOVA table (Table 4) clearly present the prevalence of various disease conditions within two distinct groups of pregnant women: those with Vit-D deficiency and with GDM. The analysis revealed for both groups that all investigated biochemical parameters highly significantly varied, indicating a strong likelihood that these conditions are prevalent in the populations studied.

Abnormalities due to Vit-D deficiency

In the Vit-D-deficient subgroup, consistently high F-statistics were observed across all evaluated parameters, indicating obvious between-group variability relative to within-group variance. Notably, the highest F-statistic values were recorded for serum Ca (F = 1092.298) and uric acid (F = 999.2255) (Table 4, upper panel), reflecting substantial group-level differences in these biomarkers, indicating that Vit-D deficiency disrupts calcium homeostasis, promotes oxidative stress due to the elevation of uric acid, suggesting an increased risk for bone and cardiovascular complications respectively. The corresponding p-values of F-statistic values of Ca and uric acid were extremely small and significant, with values of 4.94E-44 and 8.84E-43 respectively (Table 4, upper panel), providing strong statistical evidence against the null hypothesis. These findings indicate that the observed variations among the groups are highly significant.

Abnormalities due to GDM

In the GDM subgroup, the ANOVA results were similarly robust, with F-statistics for all evaluated parameters exceeding those observed in the Vit-D-deficient group. The highest F-statistic values were recorded for uric acid (F = 1897.013) and LDL (F = 1684.878) (Table 4, lower panel), indicating pronounced between-group variability for these biomarkers. The corresponding p-values were extremely small and significant, including 2.77E-49 for uric acid and 1.08E-47 for LDL (Table 4, lower panel), providing strong statistical evidence against the null hypothesis. These findings indicate that the observed differences in these biochemical parameters among the groups are exceedingly significant.

Assessment of the relationships of biochemical parameters with Vit-D and GDM status

Relationships among serum uric acid, Ca, and FBS levels

Serum levels of uric acid, Ca, and FBS were analyzed according to the status of Vit-D and GDM as well as their combined effects (Fig. 4). Serum uric acid concentrations were significantly (p < 0.001) higher in the Vit-D-insufficient and Vit-D-deficient groups compared with the Vit-D-sufficient group (Fig. 4A). In addition, women with GDM exhibited significantly (p < 0.01) elevated uric acid levels relative to those without GDM (Fig. 4B). The highest uric acid concentrations were observed among women with concurrent GDM and Vit-D deficiency, who showed markedly (p < 0.001) higher levels than women without GDM and with sufficient Vit-D (Fig. 4C).

Serum Ca levels differed significantly (p < 0.01) across Vit-D categories, with both the Vit-D-insufficient and Vit-D-deficient groups showing reduced Ca concentrations compared with the Vit-D-sufficient group (Fig. 4D). Women with GDM also exhibited significantly (p < 0.05) lower Ca levels than those without GDM (Fig. 4E). The most pronounced reduction in Ca levels was observed in women with both GDM and Vit-D deficiency, who had significantly (p < 0.001) lower Ca concentrations than women without GDM and with sufficient Vit-D (Fig. 4F).

Comparative analysis of FBS levels revealed a significant (p < 0.05) increase in the Vit-D-insufficient and Vit-D-deficient groups relative to the Vit-D-sufficient group (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, FBS concentrations were markedly (p < 0.001) higher in women with GDM than in those without GDM (Fig. 4H). The highest FBS levels were detected among women with coexisting GDM and Vit-D deficiency, which were significantly (p < 0.001) greater than those observed in women without GDM and with sufficient Vit-D (Fig. 4I).

Serum TG levels were significantly increased in the Vit-D-insufficient and Vit-D-deficient groups (Fig. 5D). Women with GDM also showed significantly (p < 0.01) higher TG concentrations compared with those without GDM (Fig. 5E). The most evident elevation in TG levels was observed in women with both GDM and Vit-D deficiency, who exhibited significantly (p < 0.001) higher values than women without GDM and with sufficient Vit-D (Fig. 5F).

In contrast, serum HDL levels were significantly reduced in the Vit-D-insufficient and Vit-D-deficient groups (Fig. 5G). The highest LDL levels were observed among women with both GDM and Vit-D deficiency, who demonstrated a highly significant (p < 0.001) increase compared with women without GDM and with sufficient Vit-D (Fig. 5L).

Discussion

This study examined the significant relationship between Vit-D deficiency, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), and the development of metabolic issues such as hyperuricemia, hypocalcemia, and hyperlipidemia in pregnant women in Bangladesh. The findings highlight the increased metabolic stress faced by women with GDM, who also have vitamin D deficiency or insufficiency. This emphasizes the importance of early screening and timely intervention.

Among the study population, 19.70% of pregnant women were diagnosed with GDM, which accords with the increasing global prevalence, especially in South Asian regions, where rates ranging from 10 to 35% depending on population demographics and diagnostic criteria have been reported [25, 26]. Moreover, the observed Vit-D statuses, i.e., the rates of Vit-D insufficiency and deficiency in pregnant women, were 48.36% and 21.19%, respectively, which are alarming because adequate Vit-D status is essential for maternal and fetal well-being. Almost similar occurrence patterns of Vit-D status among pregnant women were observed with 47.2% insufficiency and 17.3% deficiency in Bangladesh [27]. Other studies in South Asian countries, including India [28], Nepal [29], and Sri Lanka [30], have reported inadequate (combining Vit-D deficiency and insufficiency) Vit-D levels exceeding 60%, which are closely similar to our study, 64.5%.

Based on Vit-D status (i.e., deficiency, insufficiency, and sufficiency), several significant relationship were found with participants job status, region, pregnancy period (e.g., trimester) FBS, uric acid, calcium, lipid profile irrespective with their education level and betel nut consumption status, which are aligns with previous studies [27, 31, 32]. Remarkably, trimester-based analysis revealed a greater frequency of Vit-D insufficiency and deficiency during the second and third trimesters, which are in good agreement with others, suggesting an increased maternal Vit-D demands [33–35]. Notably, a higher incidence of GDM was more common between Vit-D-deficient and Vit-D-insufficient participants (Fig. 1D), which also supports earlier reports that low Vit-D status may contribute to impaired glucose metabolism and insulin resistance during pregnancy [36].

One of the core objectives of this study is to comprehensively examine the associations between serum Vit-D levels and a wide range of biochemical parameters, including uric acid, Ca, FBS, and lipid profiles such as Cho, TG, HDL, and LDL. The results revealed that serum Vit-D levels were significantly and positively correlated with Ca and HDL levels, indicating a potential relationship, although the strength of association appeared variable across analyses.This suggests that Vit-D insufficiency or deficiency in pregnant women may contribute to decreased Ca and HDL, resulting in adverse conditions like hypocalcemia and dyslipidemia [37–39]. Although a few studies have reported no significant link between Vit-D and HDL may be due to sampling criteria and inclusion method [40].

Conversely, the present study identified significant negative associations between Vit-D and levels of uric acid, FBS, Cho, TG, and LDL (Table 2), suggesting broad metabolic involvement in unadjusted analyses. This pattern is strongly supported by other studies [15, 39], indicating that lower serum Vit-D are associated with increased levels of these metabolic markers during pregnancy. As such elevations of these markers may elevate the risk of several diseases, including hyperuricemia [16], gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) [4, 5, 15], and hyperlipidemia [40]. Overall, these findings highlight the multifaceted influence of Vit-D on key biochemical parameters relevant to maternal health, emphasizing the importance of maintaining adequate Vit-D levels during pregnancy for metabolic balance and disease prevention.

Importantly, to account for potential confounding effects, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed (Table 3; Fig. 3). After adjustment, serum Vit-D levels remained significantly and inversely associated with FBS, uric acid, cholesterol, and TG, while a positive independent association was observed with Ca and HDL (Table 3; Fig. 3). These findings indicate that the relationships between Vit-D and several metabolic biomarkers are not solely explained by demographic or clinical factors. However, the association with LDL was attenuated and no longer statistically significant after adjustment, suggesting that this relationship may be influenced by confounding variables. Similarly, the association with Ca was borderline, indicating a comparatively weaker independent effect. Overall, these adjusted analyses strengthen the robustness of the observed associations, particularly for glucose metabolism, lipid regulation, and uric acid homeostasis during pregnancy.

Besides, pregnant women with Vit-D deficiency and GDM were significantly associated with Ca and uric acid levels, while LDL showed variability in unadjusted comparisons but a less consistent association after adjustment, which indicates increased occurrence rate of hypocalcemia, hyperuricemia, and hyperlipidemia, respectively. In addition, Ca, uric acid, and LDL levels are mostly affected by F-statistics (Fig. 4) highlight the role of Vit-D in calcium homeostasis, bone, altered oxidative stress and lipid metabolism. These abnormalities are linked to adverse outcomes during pregnancy especially an increase risk for bone, renal and cardiovascular complications [1, 12, 41]. In women with GDM, disturbances were more pronounced with hyperuricemia and higher LDL level. These findings also align with present studies where altered mineral metabolism and hyperlipidemia are well-known features driven by pregnant women with GDM [42].

Thus, our study demonstrates that Vit-D abnormalities (i.e., insufficient and deficiency) is strongly associated with elevated serum uric acid and lower Ca levels, especially in pregnant women with GDM. Women with both Vit-D deficiency and GDM exhibited a higher prevalence of hyperuricemia and hypocalcemia, reflecting a pattern of metabolic disturbances that increase the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. Furthermore, uric acid showed a positive correlation with TG and a negative correlation with HDL, supporting the link between hyperuricemia, dyslipidemia, and endothelial dysfunction reported in previous studies [43]. Impaired calcium homeostasis and elevated uric acid levels, driven by Vit-D deficiency, contribute to gestational metabolic complications and may negatively affect both maternal and fetal health [37, 44, 45]. The relationship between Vit-D status and lipid metabolism has been previously reported, with hyperlipidemia, lipogenesis, lipid oxidation, and insulin signalling [46], and our results indicating that Vit-D deficiency, especially when coupled with GDM (Fig. 5), may increase hyperlipidemia during pregnancy, potentially increasing the risk of adverse neonatal outcomes [47]. Specifically, TG levels were consistently elevated in the Vit-D-deficient and GDM groups, whereas LDL levels showed an increasing trend in unadjusted comparisons but were not independently associated after adjustment. (Fig. 5D-F, J-L). These outcomes strongly support the earlier evidence that inadequate Vit-D levels are associated with hyperlipidemia, particularly through increased TG levels, while the contribution of LDL appears less consistent after accounting for confounding factors [48]. In the context of cholesterol and HDL, this study revealed that pregnant women with Vit-D deficiency, GDM, or both presented significantly increase the levels of cholesterol (Fig. 5A-C) and significantly lower the HDL concentration (Fig. 5G-I). Our findings align with those of other scientific studies elsewhere [39, 49], with the exception of reversal and/or unclear relationships [50]. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, we suggest that Vit-D deficiency is associated with, but not necessarily a cause of metabolic abnormalities. Reverse causality and residual confounding may influence this relationship. For instance, individuals with poor health, unhealthy diets, obesity, or sedentary lifestyles may have both dyslipidemia and lower Vit-D levels [1, 2]. Limited outdoor activity, common in those with metabolic issues, can also reduce sunlight exposure and Vit-D synthesis [2, 13, 43]. Therefore, Vit-D deficiency may serve as a marker of poor metabolic health rather than a direct cause.

Overall, our findings strongly suggest that Vit-D deficiency not only contributes to increasing hyperuricemia and hyperlipidaemia but also disrupts glucose metabolism and calcium homeostasis in pregnant women, particularly those with GDM. These metabolic influences are remarkably triggered by both Vit-D deficiency and GDM in pregnant women.

These multiple impacts of inadequate Vit-D levels may have important implications for both maternal and fetal health. The crucial strength of this study is its comprehensive assessment across multiple metabolic axes, such as lipids, uric acid, calcium, and glucose regulation of pregnant women in Bangladesh.

However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Information on key lifestyle-related confounding factors-such as dietary habits, smoking status, and physical activity-could not be comprehensively collected due to participants’ reluctance to disclose personal information, which is often considered sensitive within the sociocultural context of the study population. Consequently, the potential influence of these variables on the observed associations could not be fully evaluated. In addition, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes any inference of causality and raises the possibility of residual confounding. Therefore, future longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to better elucidate the causal relationship and to determine whether correction of Vit-D deficiency can improve metabolic outcomes among pregnant women in Bangladesh.

Conclusion

The results of our demographic and estimated data analysis support the hypothesis that Vit-D deficiency is a critical modifiable risk factor contributing to metabolic dysfunction, particularly in individuals with hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, and calcium homeostasis during pregnancy with both Vit-D deficiency and GDM. Our results highlight the importance of regular Vit-D screening and promising supplementation from the beginning of pregnancy, particularly for pregnant women who are at risk or are diagnosed with GDM. Further longitudinal studies and interventional trials are needed to determine whether correcting Vit-D deficiency can mitigate the associated metabolic disturbances during and after pregnancy and improve maternal-fetal outcomes.
