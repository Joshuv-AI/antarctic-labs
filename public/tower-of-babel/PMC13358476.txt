Ultra-low versus low tidal volume ventilation in acute respiratory distress syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ya Wang, Nickjaree Songsangvorn, Jianchun Li, Xiaoli Chen, Jinquan Xie, Yu Pi, Xinxin Yang, Quanzhen Liu, Xiaoqing Liu, Haibo Zhang, Yonghao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow tidal volume (LTV) ventilation improves outcomes in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), whereas ultra-low tidal volume (ULTV) ventilation may further reduce lung stress but often requires extracorporeal life support (ECLS) to maintain gas exchange. Existing studies differ in ARDS severity, ECLS modality, and indications for ECLS initiation. We aimed to compare ULTV versus LTV ventilation in patients with ARDS.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis of studies published up to April 2024 was conducted using PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane, and Web of Science databases. Studies comparing ULTV (<4 mL/kg) with LTV (4–8 mL/kg) in ARDS patients were included.ResultsSix studies involving 1,102 patients met inclusion criteria. The included studies varied in ARDS severity, ECLS modality, and indications for ECLS initiation. Five studies utilized ULTV with ECLS, and one applied ECLS only when PaO2/FiO2 was below 60 mmHg in prone patients. ULTV was associated with lower driving pressure than LTV [standardized mean difference (SMD) =−1.61, 95% confidence interval (CI): −2.62 to −0.60, P<0.01] but not with a statistically significant reduction in mortality [odds ratio (OR) =0.99, 95% CI: 0.77 to 1.26, P=0.92]. Subgroup analyses suggested non-significant trends toward lower mortality in severe ARDS (OR =0.74, CI: 0.49 to 1.11, P=0.15), patients on ECLS (OR =0.79, 95% CI: 0.52 to 1.18, P=0.25), and those with baseline driving pressure >15 cmH2O (OR =0.69, P=0.11). ULTV was associated with longer hospital (SMD =0.35, 95% CI: 0.11–0.59, P<0.01) and ICU stays (SMD =0.35, 95% CI: 0.14–0.56, P<0.01). Bleeding events were more frequent in the ULTV groups in studies involving ECLS (OR =3.60, 95% CI: 1.30 to 10.01, P=0.01).ConclusionsULTV is associated with lower driving pressure, but current heterogeneous evidence does not demonstrate a statistically significant mortality benefit. Length-of-stay and bleeding findings should be interpreted cautiously because of differences in illness severity and ECLS exposure. Further adequately powered trials are needed.Keywords: Driving pressure, mechanical ventilation, intensive care unit length of stay (ICU length of stay), bleeding complications, mortality rates

Highlight box

Key findings

• This meta-analysis of six studies involving 1,102 acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) patients found that ultra-low tidal volume ventilation (ULTV; <4 mL/kg) was associated with lower driving pressure than conventional low tidal volume ventilation (LTV; 4–8 mL/kg). However, current evidence did not show a statistically significant reduction in mortality. ULTV was associated with longer intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital stays and bleeding events were more frequent in studies involving extracorporeal life support (ECLS).

What is known and what is new?

• LTV ventilation is the standard ventilatory strategy for ARDS to prevent ventilator-induced lung injury. ULTV may further reduce lung stress, but it often requires ECLS to maintain gas exchange.

• This meta-analysis quantitatively summarizes the available comparative evidence and highlights the dissociation between lower driving pressure and uncertain patient-centered benefit. The findings also emphasize that ECLS modality, ECLS indication, illness severity, and survival time may influence clinical outcomes and complication estimates.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Current evidence does not support routine use of ULTV with ECLS in unselected ARDS patients. ULTV-based strategies may warrant further evaluation in selected patients, such as those with severe ARDS or persistently high driving pressure, but this hypothesis requires confirmation. Future trials should focus on selected populations, ECLS modality, ECLS indication, and ECLS-related complications.

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a severe clinical syndrome characterized by the acute hypoxemia and bilateral pulmonary infiltrates, often resulting from direct or indirect insults such as pneumonia, sepsis, and trauma (1-3). Despite advances in critical care, ARDS remains a major clinical challenge due to its high mortality rates and complex pathophysiology (4). Effective management of ARDS relies on mechanical ventilation, which provides life-sustaining respiratory support (5). However, mechanical ventilation can also exacerbate lung injury through mechanisms collectively termed ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI), including barotrauma, volutrauma, and atelectrauma (6,7).

To mitigate VILI, lung-protective ventilation strategies have been developed, focusing on limiting tidal volumes and plateau pressures (8,9). Low tidal volume (LTV) ventilation, typically 6 mL/kg of predicted body weight (PBW), has become the standard of care and is associated with improved survival in ARDS patients (8). However, despite the widespread adoption of LTV, mortality rates in ARDS remain unacceptably high (4). Moreover, some patients may continue to experience excessive lung stress under conventional LTV, particularly when respiratory system elastance or driving pressure remains high.

Ultra-low tidal volume (ULTV) ventilation has therefore been proposed as a strategy to further reduce mechanical stress and strain on the injured lung. This approach employs tidal volumes as low as 4 mL/kg PBW or lower (10), aiming to minimize overdistension and limit the mechanical forces associated with VILI (11). However, further reductions in tidal volume may lead to challenge such as hypercapnia and acid-base disturbances due to reduced CO2 elimination. As a result, extracorporeal life support (ECLS), including extracorporeal CO2 removal (ECCO2R) or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), is often used to facilitate ULTV while maintaining adequate gas exchange (12,13). Importantly, ECLS is not merely a technical adjunct; it also introduces risks related to anticoagulation, vascular access, extracorporeal circuit exposure, and device-related complications.

Studies investigating ULTV have yielded mixed results. Bein et al. (12) demonstrated a potential benefit of combining ULTV strategy (3 mL/kg) with ECCO2R in moderate to severe ARDS, suggesting a trend towards lower mortality compared to conventional LTV. Conversely, Tongyoo et al. (13) reported no significant difference in mortality rates between patients treated with venovenous ECMO (VV-ECMO) combined with ULTV and those managed with standard mechanical ventilation. These inconsistent findings may reflect differences in ARDS severity, study design, ECLS modality, indications for ECLS initiation, and baseline respiratory mechanics.

Previous expert reviews have outlined the physiological rationale and potential risks of ultra-lung-protective ventilation, particularly in the context of extracorporeal support (14). Although driving pressure and respiratory system elastance may help identify patients most likely to benefit, further reduction in ventilatory intensity must be balanced against ECLS-related complications. However, quantitative synthesis of comparative studies evaluating ULTV versus LTV in ARDS remains limited. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare ULTV with LTV in patients with ARDS, focusing on driving pressure, mortality, length of stay, bleeding events, and heterogeneity related to ECLS modality and indication. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0573/rc) (15).

Methods

The review protocol was registered in International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD42024550428).

Search strategy

A comprehensive search of four major databases, PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Controlled Register of Trials (CENTRAL), and Web of Science, was conducted up until April 4, 2024. A broad range of search terms related to ARDS and ventilation strategies were used, including “acute respiratory distress syndrome”, “ARDS”, “ventilation”, “ultraprotective ventilation”, “conventional protective ventilation”, “low tidal volume”, and “lung protective ventilation”. The detailed search strategy for each database is provided in Table S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: (I) patients diagnosed with ARDS; (II) patients receiving mechanical ventilation; (III) studies comparing ULTV (≤4 mL/kg) with LTV (4–8 mL/kg) (10); (IV) studies reporting outcome measures such as mortality rate, hospital length of stay, and complications. Exclusion criteria included: (I) review articles; (II) conference abstracts; (III) case reports; (IV) non-full-text articles; and (V) studies lacking extractable data.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers (Y.W. and J.L.) assessed the eligibility of the studies by screening titles and abstracts. Full texts of potentially relevant studies were then thoroughly evaluated. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer. When consensus could not be reached, adjudicated by a third reviewer (Y.X.). The selection process was documented using a PRISMA flow diagram.

Relevant data were extracted from each study, including study design, sample size, patient demographics, intervention for both experimental and control groups, tidal volume, driving pressure (∆P), and other outcomes of interest. The tidal volume and ∆P values reported represent mean values averaged over the duration of mechanical ventilation rather than measurements taken at specific time points. This approach was chosen to better reflect the overall ventilatory management applied in each study. To assess the risk of bias of the included studies, two reviewers utilized the Cochrane ROB-2 tool for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (16), while the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies-of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was applied to non-randomized studies (NRS) (17). This approach ensures a comprehensive evaluation of bias across both study types. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the Cochrane Collaboration Review Manager (RevMan version 5.3) (18) and Stata Version 12.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) (19). Continuous data were compared using standardized mean differences (SMD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), while categorical data were analyzed using odds ratios (ORs). Random-effects models were used for the main analyses because substantial clinical heterogeneity was anticipated. Fixed-effect models were used only in sensitivity or secondary analyses when heterogeneity was low (I2<50%), as appropriate. This approach was selected to provide more conservative estimates when synthesizing data from diverse populations and interventions.

Subgroup analysis was conducted based on study design (RCTs and NRS), ARDS severity, type of extracorporeal life support, ∆P in the control group (less than 15 cmH2O vs. more than 15 cmH2O). Since extracorporeal life support (ECLS) may be an important confounder, we analyzed ULTV with ECLS vs. LTV without ECLS after excluding Richard 2023 (which ECLS were used conditionally in both ULTV and LTV groups) and Combes 2018 (rescue ECMO was used in LTV group). To assess the robustness of the findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed using the leave-one-out approach, systematically excluding each to evaluate its impact on the overall results.

Publication bias was assessed both visually through funnel plot inspection and statistically using Egger’s test. Egger’s test was performed only when the number of studies included in the analysis exceeded ten, as recommended (20).

Certainty of evidence (GRADE)

We assessed the certainty of evidence for the main outcomes (all-cause mortality, driving pressure, bleeding complications, ICU length of stay) using the GRADE approach. The following domains were considered: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. For each outcome, two reviewers (Y.W. and J.L.) independently rated the certainty and resolved disagreements by consensus. A “Summary of Findings” table (SoF) summarizing the effect estimates and GRADE ratings is presented in the main text (Table S2).

Results

Study identification

The initial search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, and Web of Science databases identified 1,780 records. After removing 234 duplicates, 1,546 records were screened by title and abstract. A total of 1,145 records were excluded for reasons such as meeting abstracts (n=315), review articles (n=400), editorials (n=73), case reports (n=152), letters (n=90), meta-analyses (n=20), and other irrelevant articles (n=95). This left 401 articles for further review. Of these, 390 were excluded due to irrelevant outcomes or participant populations, resulting in 11 articles for full-text review. Further exclusions included one study lacking full text (21), three studies that did not define ULTV (22-24), and one duplicate study (25), leaving six studies included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Notably, no study directly compared ULTV and LTV without the use of ECLS in ARDS.

Characteristics of included studies

The meta-analysis included six studies published between 2013 and 2023 (10-13,26,27), encompassing four RCTs, one prospective observational study, and one retrospective study. The studies were conducted in Thailand, England, Germany, and France. Sample sizes ranged from 30 to 202 patients in the ULTV groups and from 34 to 210 patients in the LTV groups (Table 1). All included studies enrolled adult patients with ARDS and used the Berlin definition. The mean or median age of participants ranged from 49.3 to 68.0 years, and the proportion of female patients ranged from 26% to 65.6% across studies.

The included studies differed substantially in ARDS severity, underlying etiology, ECLS modality, and indications for ECLS initiation. Three studies focused on severe ARDS (PaO2/FiO2 <100) (11,13,26), while the remaining three enrolled patients with moderate to severe ARDS (PaO2/FiO2 <150) (10,12,27). Two studies specifically included COVID-19-related ARDS, whereas the others included ARDS from various etiologies. ECLS use was not uniform across studies. Three studies used VV-ECMO (11,13,26) and two used ECCO2R (12,27). One study (Richard 2023) applied ECMO conditionally in both intervention and control groups when the PaO2/FiO2 ratio was <60 mmHg in the prone position; however, the number of patients supported by ECMO in each group was not reported (10). In Combes et al. (26), ECMO was permitted as rescue therapy in the control group for refractory hypoxemia [oxygen saturation (SaO2) <80% for >6 hours despite adjunctive therapies]. Thus, ECLS should be interpreted as a variably used adjunct and an important source of clinical heterogeneity rather than as a mandatory inclusion criterion.

Ventilatory strategies also varied across studies. Tidal volume targets in the ULTV groups ranged from approximately 1 to 4 mL/kg predicted body weight, whereas LTV groups generally received tidal volumes between 5.8 and 6 mL/kg predicted body weight. Driving pressure was reported in all studies; however, the timing and methods of reporting differed. When available, the extracted driving pressure values represented post-intervention or averaged values during mechanical ventilation rather than measurements at a uniform time point. Other ventilatory and physiologic variables, including respiratory rate, pH, PaCO2, and PaO2/FiO2, are summarized in Table 1. These differences in study design, ARDS severity, ECLS exposure, and ventilatory reporting were considered when interpreting the pooled estimates.

Risk of bias assessment

We assessed risk of bias for randomized controlled trials using RoB 2 and for non-randomized studies using ROBINS-I. Overall, among the four RCTs, two trials were judged at low risk of bias for the primary outcome (randomization, allocation concealment and outcome ascertainment), one trial had some concerns related to incomplete outcome data, and one trial had high risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions and selective reporting (Figure S7A). For the two non-randomized studies, ROBINS-I judgments ranged from moderate to serious risk of bias, primarily due to confounding by indication (patients selected for ULTV + ECLS tended to be more severely ill), lack of prospective adjustment for baseline severity, and potential selection bias (Figure S7B). Importantly, non-randomized studies were at risk of confounding by indication (patients receiving ULTV + ECLS often had greater baseline severity), and some trials had concerns about deviations from intended interventions. These issues reduce confidence in pooled effect estimates and are considered in the “Discussion” and “GRADE” assessments.

Meta-analysis of mortality rates

All six studies reported mortality rates. For statistical power, 90-day and in-hospital mortality were initially combined as all-cause mortality. The pooled OR for all-cause mortality rate was 0.99 (95% CI: 0.77 to 1.26, P=0.92), and the analysis did not show a statistically significant difference between the ULTV and LTV groups (Figure 2A). Subgroup analyses of mortality type showed similar non-significant results for 90-day mortality (OR =0.99, 95% CI: 0.74 to 1.33, P=0.95) and in-hospital mortality (OR =0.97, 95% CI: 0.44 to 2.13, P=0.93) (Figure 2B). Sensitivity analysis confirmed these findings (Figure S1).

Further subgroup analyses explored factors influencing mortality. In the RCT subgroup, the analysis did not show a statistically significant difference in mortality between the ULTV and LTV groups (OR =0.97, CI: 0.74 to 1.28, P=0.84) (Figure 2C). Among patients with severe ARDS, the mortality estimate numerically favored ULTV, but the difference was not statistically significant (OR =0.74, CI: 0.49 to 1.11, P=0.15) (Figure 2D). Similarly, studies using ECMO showed a non-significant numerical trend towards lower mortality with ULTV (OR =0.79, 95% CI: 0.52 to 1.18, P=0.25) (Figure 2E). In studies in which the control group had ∆P >15 cmH2O, the mortality estimates also numerically favored ULTV but did not reach statistical significance (OR =0.69, 95% CI: 0.44 to 1.09, P=0.11) (Figure 2F).

Meta-analysis of ∆P

All six studies compared ∆P between ULTV and LTV groups. The pooled SMD was −1.61 (95% CI: −2.62 to −0.60, P<0.01), indicating significantly lower ∆P in the ULTV group (Figure 3). Across the six included studies, mean driving pressure in the LTV groups ranged from 10.0 to 17.9 cmH2O. In two studies, mean driving pressure in the LTV group exceeded 15 cmH2O [Combes 2018 (28): 16.0 cmH2O; Tongyoo 2020 (13): 17.9 cmH2O]. In the ULTV groups, mean driving pressure ranged from 5.0 to 14.0 cmH2O and remained below 15 cmH2O in all included studies.

Meta-analysis of mechanical ventilator duration

Three studies reported the duration of mechanical ventilation. Low heterogeneity (I2=0.0%, P=0.56) allowed a fixed-effect model. The pooled SMD was 0.09 (95% CI: −0.08 to 0.25, P=0.32), indicating no significant difference between groups (Figure S2).

Meta-analysis of hospital and ICU length of stay

Five studies reported hospital length of stay. Due to significant heterogeneity (I2=68%, P=0.01), a random-effects model was used, revealing that ULTV was associated with longer hospital stays (SMD =0.35, 95% CI: 0.11–0.59, P<0.01) (Figure S3). Four studies reported ICU length of stay, with moderate heterogeneity (I2=45%, P=0.14). A random-effects model indicated significantly longer ICU stays in the ULTV group (SMD =0.35, 95% CI: 0.14 to 0.56, P<0.01) (Figure S4).

Meta-analysis of ventilator-free days

Five studies compared ventilator-free days. No significant differences were observed between ULTV and LTV groups for 28-day (SMD =−0.14, 95% CI: −0.42 to 0.14, P=0.32), 60-day (SMD =0.07, 95% CI: −0.86 to 1.00, P=0.89), or 90-day ventilator-free days (SMD =0.00, 95% CI: −0.477 to 0.47, P>0.99) (Figure S5).

Meta-analysis of complications

Two studies (26,27) reported bleeding events, both involving ECLS. Substantial heterogeneity (I2=71.5%, P=0.06) prompted a random-effects model, showing that bleeding events were more frequent in the ULTV groups in studies involving ECLS (OR =3.60, 95% CI: 1.30 to 10.01, P=0.01) (Figure 4).

Qualitative analysis of other complications revealed specific adverse events. In one study (12), arteriovenous ECCO2R complications included transient ischemia (7.5%) and false aneurysms. Another study (13) reported ECMO-related events, including intracranial hemorrhage, gastrointestinal bleeding, cardiac arrhythmia, thrombosis, and hemolysis.

Quality assessment and publication bias

Visual inspection of funnel plots indicated no publication bias (Figure S6). Methodological quality was high across studies, with a low risk of bias, supporting the reliability of these findings (Figure S7).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis included six studies involving 1,102 patients with ARDS and compared ULTV with LTV across heterogeneous clinical settings. Overall, ULTV was associated with lower driving pressure than LTV, but current evidence did not show a statistically significant reduction in all-cause mortality. ULTV was also associated with longer ICU and hospital stays, and bleeding events were more frequent in studies involving ECLS. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the included studies differed substantially in ARDS severity, ECLS modality, indications for ECLS initiation, and risk of bias. Therefore, the observed reduction in driving pressure should be regarded as an expected physiological finding rather than definitive evidence of improved clinical outcomes.

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis by Zhen et al. confirmed that ECCO2R effectively facilitates ultra-low tidal volume ventilation in ARDS, improving respiratory mechanics (29). However, it also reported notable device-related complications, including bleeding (15%) and circuit clotting (19%), reinforcing that the bleeding signal observed in our study likely reflects ECLS exposure rather than tidal volume reduction per se.

∆P is an important determinant of VILI and ARDS outcomes and has been shown to predict mortality more accurately than tidal volume or plateau pressure (30). In our analysis, ULTV was associated with a lower ΔP than LTV. However, this expected physiological effect should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of improved lung protection or clinical benefit. Mechanical power, which integrates several of these variables, may therefore better reflect the total mechanical energy delivered to the respiratory system than any single ventilatory parameter (6). However, mechanical power was not consistently reported in the included studies, precluding a pooled analysis of this variable. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as showing that ULTV lowers ΔP, rather than proving that it reduces the overall mechanical burden of ventilation. Consistent with this caution, Guervilly et al. reported that the application of an ultra-lung protective strategy was not associated with a significant reduction in biotrauma (31). Moreover, the lack of a clear mortality benefit in our findings suggests that lowering ΔP alone may be insufficient to improve survival outcomes. The recent ECMOVENT study compared two ultra-protective ventilation strategies during VV-ECMO in 121 ARDS patients and found no significant difference in 90-day survival or ECMO weaning time (32). This suggests that once gas exchange is fully supported by ECLS, the choice of specific ventilation strategy may have limited incremental impact on clinical outcomes.

Several factors may explain why the reduction in ΔP was not accompanied by a statistically significant mortality reduction. First, a threshold effect of ΔP may exist, whereby once ΔP is reduced below a critical level, further reductions confer nonadditional survival benefit (33). In this context, reducing ΔP from a potentially injurious range to a safer range may be more relevant than pursuing the lowest possible ΔP in all patients. Consistent with this concept, the recent ECMOVENT study reported that, among patients with severe ARDS receiving VV-ECMO, a ΔP-targeted ultraprotective strategy (P =8 cmH2O, PEEP 14 cmH2O and respiratory rate 10 breaths/min) improved decarboxylation and higher static elastic mechanical power compared with a quasi-apneic VT 1 mL/kg strategy, without significant differences in 90-day survival or time to successful ECMO weaning (32). These findings support the need to balance ∆P reduction against gas-exchange targets and the overall mechanical burden of ventilation, rather than focusing solely on achieving the lowest possible ΔP.

Second, implementation of ULTV frequently requires adjunctive VV-ECMO or ECCO2R to manage the inevitable hypercapnia and acid-base disturbances (28). While these interventions enable the physiological feasibility of ULTV, they also introduce risks related to bleeding, thrombosis, and device-related complications (34). Therefore, the clinical effects observed in ULTV-based strategies may reflect the combined influence of ΔP reduction and extracorporeal support rather than the independent effect of ULTV itself. This is particularly important when interpreting bleeding events and length-of-stay outcomes.

Longer ICU and hospital stays should not be interpreted solely as a harmful effect of ULTV. These findings may reflect survival bias, because patients who survive longer have more opportunity to accumulate ICU and hospital days. They may also reflect greater baseline illness severity, prolonged ECLS exposure, delayed recovery, or differences in discharge and rehabilitation practices across centers. Because the available aggregate data did not permit adjustment for survival time or baseline severity, length-of-stay findings should be interpreted as resource-use signals rather than direct evidence of harm from tidal volume reduction.

Similarly, bleeding events should not be attributed directly to ULTV. In the included studies, bleeding occurred in the context of ECLS and may have been influenced by anticoagulation exposure, vascular access, ECLS duration, circuit-related factors, illness severity, and survival time. Survival bias may also contribute, because patients who survive longer or remain on ECLS for longer periods have more opportunity to experience bleeding events. Therefore, bleeding findings are best interpreted as ECLS-related safety signals associated with ULTV-based strategies requiring extracorporeal support, rather than as direct effects of tidal volume reduction itself.

Patient heterogeneity may also modify the potential effect of ULTV. Subgroup analyses suggested a non-significant trend toward lower mortality among patients with higher baseline ΔP under conventional LTV. However, this trend did not reach statistical significance, underscoring the importance of individualized ventilation strategies. These findings highlight the complex interplay between mechanical ventilation parameters, ECLS modality, and patient-specific factors. While ULTV might reduce ΔP, its independent clinical value remains uncertain, particularly when implemented as part of an ECLS-supported strategy.

The application of ULTV in routine clinical practice faces several challenges. First, the need for ECLS limits its feasibility in resource-constrained settings (35). Even in high-resource environments, the complications associated with ECLS, such as bleeding and thrombosis, necessitate vigilant monitoring and expertise (36). Second, the longer ICU and hospital length of stay observed in the ULTV group may have implications for resource utilization, although these findings cannot be interpreted as a direct adverse effect of ULTV. Finally, the underreporting of patient-centered outcomes, including ventilator-free days, quality of life and long-term functional recovery, further limits assessment of the broader clinical value of ULTV based strategies.

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, only six studies were included, which limited statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses and mortality outcomes. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant mortality difference should not be interpreted as evidence of no treatment effect. Second, the included studies differed substantially in study design, ARDS severity, underlying etiology, ECLS modality, indications for ECLS initiation, and timing of ventilatory measurements. This clinical and methodological heterogeneity limits the generalizability of the pooled estimates and requires cautious interpretation (37). Third, most included studies were not specifically designed to isolate the effect of ULTV versus LTV; in several studies, ventilatory settings were part of broader ECLS-based management strategies or secondary to other trial interventions. As a result, the independent effect of tidal volume reduction could not be clearly separated from ECLS exposure, anticoagulation, vascular access, and device-related complications. Fourth, confounding by indication is likely, especially in non-randomized studies, because patients receiving ULTV with ECLS may have had greater baseline severity or refractory respiratory failure. Fifth, several clinically relevant variables were inconsistently reported, including mechanical power, ECLS duration, anticoagulation intensity, timing of driving pressure assessment, ventilator-free days, quality of life, and long-term functional outcomes. Finally, although trial sequential analysis was considered, it was not performed because of the small number of studies, limited event counts, and heterogeneous mortality definitions. Taken together, our findings should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence for clinical decision-making.

Future research should focus on adequately powered multicenter trials in clearly defined ARDS populations. These trials should stratify patients by ARDS severity, baseline ∆P, respiratory system mechanics, and ECLS modality, and should clearly distinguish whether ECLS is used as a protocolized adjunct, conditional support, or rescue therapy. Future studies should also systematically assess mechanical power, ECLS duration, anticoagulation exposure, bleeding and thrombotic events, ventilator-free days, quality of life, and long-term functional recovery.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that ULTV is associated with lower driving pressure than LTV in patients with ARDS, but current heterogeneous evidence does not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in mortality. Findings of longer ICU/hospital stays and more frequent bleeding events should be interpreted cautiously because they may reflect differences in ECLS exposure, anticoagulation, baseline illness severity, and survival time rather than the ventilation strategy itself. Further adequately powered trials are needed to determine whether selected patients, particularly those with severe ARDS or persistently high driving pressure despite conventional LTV, may benefit from ULTV-based strategies.
