Stability analysis of the primary reflector of the 500 mm aperture ultra-lightweight millimeter wave satellite antenna for observing solar flare
Authors: Jian Pei, Lei Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe primary reflector is the core component of the large-aperture millimeter wave sat-ellite antenna for observing solar flare, and its mechanics-thermology stability directly affects the working performance of the antenna. To make the antenna resistant to the harsh mechanical vibration environment during rocket launches and to keep the small surface error in the thermal environment of space, this thesis focuses on the objectives of ultra-lightweight, high stiffness, and minor surface error of the antenna. First, the material and ply angle used for the primary reflector are studied, and the scheme of M40 high modulus carbon fiber material and [0°/60°/− 60°]4 ply angle is selected by mechanics-thermology simulation and analysis comparison. Then, high stiffness is achieved by structural optimization of the primary reflector. Simulation results show that the first-order modal frequency of the antenna is about 119.94 Hz, and the RMS value of the primary reflector surface error is less than 1/100 wavelength under extreme thermal environment. Finally, the vibration tests are designed to verify the simulation results. The test results show that most of the test data have the error of less than 10%, which proves that the modeling approach in this thesis is reasonable and the analysis results are accurate.Keywords: Millimeter wave antenna, Mechanics-thermology stability, Surface error, Ultra-lightweight, High stiffness structureSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Solar flares are among the most intense activities on the solar surface, releasing enormous energy and high-frequency electromagnetic radiation, which severely disturb the space environment, particularly posing threats to spacecraft and communication systems1–4. To accurately capture the evolution of solar flares and enable real-time monitoring, the development of novel and high-efficiency millimeter-wave antennas is urgently required. Against this background, this paper proposes a design method for the primary reflector of a 500 mm-aperture ultra-lightweight millimeter-wave spaceborne antenna for solar flare observation. Through optimized design, this study aims to improve the mechanical stability and surface accuracy of the antenna, so as to meet the stringent requirements of spaceborne antennas for minor surface error and high structural stiffness.

In existing research, the design of millimeter-wave antennas has mainly focused on applications in lower frequency bands and small-aperture configurations. Although several studies have addressed antenna designs operating in the 50–70 GHz millimeter-wave band in recent years, numerous challenges still exist in the design and implementation of large-aperture ultra-lightweight antennas. Most conventional antenna designs conduct independent analyses of material selection and structural optimization, lacking investigations on integrated optimization design frameworks that synergistically consider structure, material, and ply layup. This gap is especially prominent in achieving lightweight design and high stiffness while maintaining thermo-mechanical stability and minor surface error.

To address the aforementioned issues, this paper presents an integrated optimization design method covering material selection, ply layup, and structural configuration. Combining M40 high-modulus carbon fiber reinforced polymer composites with a [0°/60°/− 60°]₄ stacking sequence, the proposed method achieves the desired lightweight characteristics and high stiffness of the antenna. This approach effectively integrates material selection and ply design to ensure the thermo-mechanical stability of the antenna under harsh space environments, thereby guaranteeing minor surface error.

Furthermore, this study adopts a closed-loop verification strategy combining numerical simulations and vibration tests. The mechanical responses of the designed antenna obtained from experimental tests are consistent with simulation results, which validates the rationality and engineering feasibility of the proposed optimization method. Through the proposed design methodology, this paper makes innovative contributions to the field of large-aperture ultra-lightweight millimeter-wave antenna design, provides a systematic design framework, and offers a valuable reference for future high-precision antenna development.

Antenna design inputs and surface error evaluation methods

The satellite antenna has no active thermal control system and always aims at the sun. Temperature variations in extreme environments cause increased surface errors in the primary reflector. The primary reflector surface error significantly impacts antenna efficiency and signal accuracy when the antenna structure receives high-energy electromagnetic waves from the sun. The larger surface error of the primary reflector will lead to phase errors in the aperture field, causing degradation of the electromagnetic performance, such as gain degradation, beam width increase, and beam pointing shift5,6. In addition, to adapt to the limitations of the small satellite platform on the equipment mass, the antenna must have an overall mass of < 1.5 kg and resist the violent vibration environment during the rocket launch (≥ 100 Hz)7–9.

In order to avoid the whole antenna frequency dropping below 100 Hz after the assembly of the secondary reflector, the frequency design of the primary reflector is carried out following 1.2 times safety redundancy. The weight breakdown shows that the primary reflector weighs no more than 1 kg. The operating frequency determines the design requirements for the primary reflector surface error. The higher the operating frequency, the more stringent the requirements for surface error. Generally, the antenna primary reflector surface error is required to be less than or equal to 1/16–1/32 of the operating wavelength. Considering the antenna machining and thermal deformation errors, this thesis increases the design index of surface error by 2–3 times. Considering all these factors, the following design requirements are proposed for the primary reflector of the antenna.

The RMS value of the primary reflector surface error is less than 1/100 wavelength under extreme environments from -70 to 120 °C.

Small aperture antenna primary reflector surface error is mainly caused by machining; for large aperture antenna, this error primarily comes from structural load deformation. The structural deformation of the large aperture antenna is greater for the same design indicators. The aperture of the antenna studied in this thesis is as high as 500 mm, so the primary reflector surface error caused by the deformation of structural loads is mainly analyzed. The operating wavelength is calculated at 70 GHz, and the design requires the error less than 1/100 wavelength, which is 0.0428 mm. According to Ruze tolerance theory this specification not only satisfies the structural accuracy requirement but also ensures that the antenna gain and beam performance are not significantly degraded10,11.

Selection of primary reflector material

According to the design requirements, the weight of the primary reflector needs to be less than 1 kg. Among the commonly used metal materials, the density of titanium alloy is about 4.51 g/cm3, aluminum alloy is about 2.63–2.85 g/cm3, carbon fiber is about 1.5–2 g/cm3, and glass fiber is about 2.1–2.7 g/cm3. When a metal material makes a 500 mm aperture primary reflector, a thickness of > 3 mm is usually required if the surface error is guaranteed to be less than 1/100 wavelength. Moreover, the primary reflector must be constructed with reinforcing ribs to avoid large machining deformations. A comprehensive estimate of the mass of the primary reflector developed using titanium and aluminum alloys was 4.6 kg and 3 kg, which exceeded the mass limit, so fiber materials were chosen. The honeycomb sandwich structure is mainly used to develop large antennas to reduce the mass. This structure has less impact on the mass of the antenna in this thesis. Different materials in the honeycomb sandwich structure have different coefficients of thermal expansion, and uneven surface changes when subjected to heat will lead to larger surface error and even structural damage14,15. Therefore, fiber composite laminate with higher strength, specific stiffness, and designability are selected to make the primary reflector.

Material and ply angle are essential factors in determining the mechanical properties of laminate. Carbon Fibre-reinforced polymer have the advantages of lightweight and high stiffness, and the materials used as reinforcement are carbon fiber and glass fiber. M40 high-modulus carbon fiber exhibits an elastic modulus of up to 210 GPa, together with a low coefficient of thermal expansion and high specific stiffness. Compared with high-strength carbon fibers such as T300 and T700, it can better satisfy the high-stiffness requirements of the antenna. Meanwhile, in contrast to higher-grade M50 series materials, M40 achieves a more favorable balance between performance and cost, and is therefore selected as the preferred material for the primary reflector16–18.

Structural design of the primary reflector

The primary reflector of the millimeter wave satellite antenna developed in this thesis is made of Carbon Fibre-reinforced polymer, which has a low stiffness due to its small thickness and mass. In this thesis, the structure of the primary reflector is optimized and designed to increase the stiffness so that the antenna can resist the harsh mechanical vibration environment during rocket launch. The vibration frequency of the rocket launch is generally ≤ 100 Hz, leaving the 20% design margin. The first-order modal frequency of the antenna is designed to be greater than 120 Hz. Pasting reinforcing ribs on the back of the primary reflector is a common way to improve its stiffness, mainly divided into radial and circumferential direction reinforcing ribs. According to the different shapes of the long trusses, the reinforcing ribs can be generally categorized into T-type, L-type, I-type, and Hat-type19,20. In this thesis, from the perspective of symmetry, lightweight, and low cost of the primary reflector, radial and circumferential direction reinforcing ribs are added to its backside to improve its stiffness further. The T-type reinforcing ribs, which have the advantages of high specific stiffness, close fit, and easy integrated molding, are arranged in a radioactive structure on the back of the primary reflector. The design objectives were achieved by optimizing the number of radial direction reinforcing ribs and the height and thickness of circumferential reinforcing ribs.

Study of the number of radial direction reinforcing ribs

Considering the symmetry of the antenna structure, 3, 4, 6, and 8 evenly distributed reinforcing ribs are added to the back of the primary reflector. Different materials deform to varying degrees during temperature changes, producing large surface errors and stresses. For this purpose, the reinforcing ribs are made of the same M40 carbon fiber composite material as the primary reflector, with a thickness of 1 mm.

In the following, the finite element model of the primary reflector is constructed to analyze the primary reflector modal of the four radial direction reinforcing ribs quantity schemes and compare the modal to determine the optimal quantities. Due to the small thickness of the primary reflector and reinforcing ribs, the way of dividing the surface mesh is chosen here, and the analysis results are shown in Fig. 5.

From Table 3, the first-order modal frequency decreases and then increases when the number of radial direction reinforcing ribs rises from 3 to 8. The frequency increase is minor when the number is increased from 6 to 8. This phenomenon mainly stems from the coupled competing relationship between the increase in structural stiffness and the increase in mass. When the number of reinforcing ribs is increased from 3 to 4, the improvement in structural symmetry and load transfer paths is limited, while the mass increases significantly, resulting in a decrease in natural frequency. When increased from 4 to 6, structural symmetry is significantly improved and edge stiffness is enhanced; the overall stiffness increase plays a dominant role, leading to a rise in natural frequency. When further increased to 8, the stiffness improvement tends to saturate, whereas the mass continues to increase, and thus the natural frequency only rises slightly. Figure 5 shows that the edge displacement of the primary reflector varies more, proving that the edge stiffness is lower. From the cost point of view, six radial direction reinforcing ribs are installed, and a ring of circumferential reinforcing rib is added at the edge of the primary reflector to increase its stiffness.

When the surface RMS error is less than λ/100, the antenna gain loss at an operating frequency of 70 GHz (λ ≈ 4.28 mm) is limited to approximately 0.1 dB, and the beamwidth and beam pointing are hardly affected. In this design, the surface error is far below the conventional criterion of λ/16 to λ/32. Furthermore, the field test at 50–54 GHz shows no obvious degradation in signal quality, which further validates the rationality of the structural design.

Constrained by experimental conditions, the antenna gain and signals across the full operating band were not directly measured before and after vibration in this paper; instead, signal tests were mainly conducted within the 51–54 GHz band. Experimental results indicate that no obvious degradation in signal quality was observed before and after vibration within this frequency range. Mechanistically, the influence of vibration on antenna performance primarily stems from geometric deviations induced by structural deformation. Since such deviations are small relative to the order of magnitude of the operating wavelength, their impact on electrical performance varies moderately within a certain frequency range.

In addition, manufacturing errors, including ply deviation, thickness error and residual stress, exert an influence on surface accuracy, thereby further affecting the electromagnetic performance of the antenna. Manufacturing errors have been taken into consideration in the design phase of this study, and sufficient margin has been reserved by raising the surface accuracy specification (better than 1/100 wavelength) to compensate for the effects caused by manufacturing imperfections.

In future work, full-band signal testing, gain measurement and quantitative analysis of manufacturing errors will be further carried out to improve the experimental validation.

Conclusions

In this paper, a comprehensive stability analysis and design optimization of a 500 mm aperture ultra-lightweight millimeter-wave satellite antenna primary reflector has been carried out.

Firstly, the effects of material properties and laminate configurations on the mechanical–thermal performance of the reflector were systematically analyzed. The M40 carbon fiber composite with a [0°/60°/− 60°]₄ layup was identified as the optimal solution, achieving excellent specific stiffness and minimal thermal deformation.

Secondly, the structural stiffness of the reflector was significantly improved through the optimization of reinforcing ribs configurations. By introducing a combination of radial and circumferential reinforcing ribs, the first-order modal frequency was increased to approximately 120–130 Hz, ensuring sufficient margin against launch vibration environments.

Furthermore, the mechanical–thermal coupling behavior of the reflector was analyzed using finite element methods, demonstrating that the surface error RMS remains below 1/100 wavelength under extreme temperature conditions. This confirms the ability of the proposed design to maintain high surface accuracy in harsh space environments.

Finally, vibration tests were conducted to validate the simulation results. The comparison shows that most deviations between simulation and experiment are within 10–14%, indicating good agreement and confirming the reliability of the modeling approach. Although local discrepancies exist (e.g., edge response amplification), the overall structural behavior is accurately captured.

Compared with existing studies, this work not only provides an integrated design methodology combining material, laminate, and structural optimization, but also establishes a simulation–experiment verification framework, which enhances its engineering applicability.
