Introduction

Ovarian cystadenomas are among the most common benign epithelial ovarian tumors and are primarily classified into serous and mucinous subtypes. At the molecular level, serous cystadenomas typically lack mutations in Kirsten Rat Sarcoma Viral Oncogene Homolog (KRAS) and B-Raf Proto-Oncogene, Serine/Threonine Kinase (BRAF), whereas mucinous cystadenomas have been reported to harbor KRAS mutations at a considerable rate [1]. These findings suggest distinct molecular pathways in the pathogenesis of serous and mucinous ovarian tumors.

Ovarian cystadenomas are often asymptomatic and are frequently detected incidentally during routine gynecological examination or imaging studies. However, in some cases, patients may present with abdominal pain, progressive distension, or symptoms mimicking an acute abdomen. Such presentations may require a multidisciplinary evaluation involving both gynecology and general surgery teams.

If left untreated, these cysts may reach massive dimensions and lead to serious complications. Reported complications include abdominal compartment syndrome [2], ovarian torsion [3], and cyst rupture [4], all of which may necessitate urgent surgical intervention.

In this report, we present the clinical course and surgical management of a patient diagnosed with a giant mucinous ovarian cystadenoma.

Case presentation

A 45-year-old woman was admitted to the Internal Medicine Department with complaints of progressive dyspnea, dyspepsia, and marked abdominal distension. Physical examination revealed significant abdominal enlargement with a palpable mass. Computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a giant cystic lesion measuring approximately 45 × 20 cm, occupying nearly the entire abdominal cavity.

Given the suspected gynecological origin of the mass, the patient was referred to the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology. Based on clinical and radiological findings, a benign ovarian cyst, particularly a serous cystadenoma, was considered preoperatively.

Venous blood samples were obtained preoperatively. Serum CA-125, CA19-9, CA15-3, AFP, and CEA levels were analyzed, and all tumor markers were within normal limits. On ultrasound, the maximum diameter of the mass was greater than 400 mm, it did not contain solid particles or papillary particles, there was no ascites in the abdominal cavity, no acoustic shadow, and it was a single locular cystic mass.

The patient was evaluated preoperatively according to the International Ovarian Tumor Analysis (IOTA) ADNEX model criteria. Based on calculations using the patient’s age, whether our clinic was a tertiary gynecology referral center, maximum lesion diameter, maximum diameter of the largest solid fragment, number of cyst segments, number of papillary segments, presence of acoustic shadow, presence of ascites in the abdominal cavity, and a serum CA-125 value, the probability of the mass being a benign ovarian tumor was calculated as 98%.

Surgical treatment was planned, and the patient underwent total abdominal hysterectomy, bilateral salpingectomy, and left oophorectomy (Fig. 1). Intraoperatively, a 42 cm mucinous cyst filling the abdominal cavity and compressing the diaphragm and stomach was identified and excised (Fig. 2). Adhesions involving the bowel and bladder were carefully dissected. The procedure was completed without complications. Estimated blood loss was approximately 150 mL, and no intraoperative adverse events occurred.

Intra-abdominal cyst and uterus

Preoperative ultrasonography findings were benign, intraoperative assessment showed the mass was not invading surrounding tissues and had regular borders. Moreover, according to the IOTA ADNEX scoring performed preoperatively, the mass was considered to be very likely (98%) benign. For all these reasons, and considering the risk of cyst rupture, frozen section analysis was deemed unnecessary.

The patient underwent hysterectomy due to abnormal uterine bleeding and the presence of both anterior and posterior uterine fibroids. The posterior fibroid is demonstrated in Fig. 2.

Radiological findings

Abdominal CT revealed a giant cystic lesion measuring approximately 45 × 20 cm in the axial plane, occupying a substantial portion of the abdominal cavity. The mass extended from the pelvic region to the substernal (epigastric) level. The lesion appeared thin-walled with internal septations. No obvious solid component was identified. Based on the imaging findings, a serous cystadenoma was initially suspected; however, further imaging with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was recommended to exclude the possibility of cystadenocarcinoma in the differential diagnosis. Additionally, a 3 cm intramural uterine fibroid located at the left side of the uterine fundus, along with multiple nodular lesions, was observed.

Discussion

Mucinous ovarian cystadenomas are benign epithelial neoplasms that are commonly encountered in women of reproductive and perimenopausal age [5]. Epithelial ovarian tumors account for approximately 60% of all ovarian neoplasms, with serous and mucinous subtypes representing a significant proportion [6]. Mucinous tumors are typically unilateral and present as multilocular, large-volume cystic masses containing gelatinous material [7].

Giant mucinous cystadenomas, such as in the present case, are relatively rare in modern clinical practice. This decline is largely attributed to routine gynecological examinations and the widespread use of imaging modalities. However, delayed access to healthcare, nonspecific symptoms, and the slow-growing nature of these tumors may contribute to delayed diagnosis [8]. Cases of ovarian cysts reaching 30–50 cm in diameter have been reported in the literature, with abdominal distension, early satiety, dyspepsia, and respiratory distress being the most common presenting symptoms [9, 10]. Dyspnea due to diaphragmatic compression is particularly expected in cases involving massive intra-abdominal masses.

Radiological evaluation plays a crucial role in differential diagnosis. Although ultrasonography is the first-line imaging modality, CT and MRI provide more detailed information regarding anatomical extension in large masses [11]. Thin-walled, well-defined cystic lesions without solid components generally suggest benign pathology; however, thick septations, papillary projections, or solid areas increase suspicion for malignancy [5, 8]. Nevertheless, definitive diagnosis is established through histopathological examination. At the molecular level, KRAS mutations have been frequently identified in mucinous ovarian tumors, whereas serous tumors are associated with different molecular pathways [12]. These findings further support the distinct biological behavior and pathogenesis of mucinous and serous ovarian neoplasms.

The most significant clinical risks associated with giant masses include torsion, rupture, infection, and, rarely, abdominal compartment syndrome [10, 13]. Such complications may result in an acute abdomen requiring emergency surgical intervention. In the present case, despite significant compressive symptoms, no acute complication developed, allowing the procedure to be performed electively.

The choice of surgical approach should be individualized based on patient age, fertility expectations, and the presence of concomitant uterine pathology. Fertility-sparing surgery may be considered in women of reproductive age, whereas hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is often preferred in perimenopausal patients or those with associated uterine disease [6, 14, 15]. In this case, considering the patient’s age and the presence of a uterine fibroid, definitive surgical management was undertaken. Careful adhesiolysis and controlled dissection minimized intraoperative risks.

Conclusion

In conclusion, although giant mucinous ovarian cystadenomas are rare, they may lead to significant compressive symptoms and potential complications. Malignancy must be carefully excluded in the differential diagnosis, and surgical planning should follow a multidisciplinary approach. Early diagnosis and appropriate surgical management significantly reduce morbidity and improve patient quality of life.
