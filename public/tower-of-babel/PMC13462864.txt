The endoscopic-assisted triple-plane technique: a technical refinement for complete autologous prosthesis coverage in subpectoral implant-based breast reconstruction
Authors: Wenfei He, Yue He, Mingzhi Xia, Zhihua Liu, Zhaoyang Zhu, Zhaoyun Wu, Zhijie Gao, Xiaobo Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The triple-plane technique offers complete autologous prosthesis coverage in subpectoral breast reconstruction without requiring mesh. Its integration with endoscopic assistance allows for precise dissection through a concealed incision. Achieving complete implant coverage without mesh in subpectoral reconstruction remains a technical challenge. This study evaluates the feasibility and early outcomes of this novel endoscopic-assisted triple-plane technique. In this prospective case series, we describe and evaluate an integrated approach utilizing a single axillary incision under endoscopic visualization to dissect three anatomical planes: the subpectoral plane (pectoralis major, A-plane), the serratus anterior fascia (B-plane), and the deep superficial fascia (C-plane). These layers are combined to create a mesh-free pocket ensuring complete autologous prosthesis coverage. In our preliminary series, the procedure was successfully performed in 89 patients. The mean operative time was 90.28±14.95 minutes, with an average blood loss of 44.21±13.40 mL. The mean incision length was 5.47±0.52 cm, and the average hospital stay was 7.56±1.78 days. Postoperative breast appearance was rated as excellent in 86 patients (96.63%) and good in 3 patients (3.37%). All patients reported high satisfaction, with BREAST-Q (a validated patient-reported outcome measure for breast surgery) scores ranging from 80 to 100 and a mean score of 87.28. No major perioperative complications, such as prosthesis loss or total flap necrosis, were recorded. The endoscopic-assisted triple-plane technique is a safe and reproducible mesh-free method that combines the benefits of complete autologous coverage with the advantages of minimally invasive surgery. This integrated approach appears to be a promising refinement for subpectoral breast reconstruction, supported by favorable early aesthetic outcomes and high patient satisfaction. These preliminary findings warrant further validation in comparative studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up.

Introduction

Implant-based breast reconstruction is a pivotal component of restoring quality of life and body image for patients following mastectomy (1-3). The evolution of techniques continually seeks to balance optimal aesthetic outcomes with minimal complications and functional morbidity (4). Currently, the dominant paradigms are the prepectoral and the subpectoral approaches, each with distinct advantages and limitations. The prepectoral approach, positioning the implant entirely anterior to the pectoralis major muscle, has gained popularity for its potential to avoid animation deformity and reduce postoperative pain (5,6). However, its success is often contingent upon sufficient soft tissue coverage and frequently necessitates the use of acellular dermal matrices (ADMs) or synthetic meshes to mitigate risks such as implant visibility, rippling, and capsular contracture (7,8). The substantial cost associated with these adjunct materials can limit their accessibility, particularly in resource-conscious healthcare settings (9).

Conversely, traditional subpectoral reconstruction, in which the implant is placed partially beneath the pectoralis major, provides reliable upper pole coverage and has been the most prevalent method in many regions, including China (10). Nevertheless, this approach is hampered by significant drawbacks. The requisite dissection and release of the pectoralis major muscle can lead to denervation, atrophy, functional impairment, and the characteristic animation deformity—a dynamic distortion of the breast during muscle contraction (11,12). Furthermore, the limited native capacity of the subpectoral pocket often proves inadequate for complete inferolateral implant coverage, especially with larger prostheses. This frequently mandates the use of meshes to define the inframammary fold and ensure stability, thereby reintroducing cost concerns and potential mesh-related complications (13).

To address these specific limitations of conventional subpectoral reconstruction, our team previously developed and reported the open triple-plane technique (14). This method innovatively constructs a complete, vascularized autologous tissue envelope for the prosthesis by strategically utilizing three anatomical planes: the pectoralis major muscle (A-plane) medially, a pedicled flap of the serratus anterior fascia (B-plane) laterally, and the deep layer of the superficial thoracic fascia (C-plane) inferiorly. This structured coverage theoretically reduces direct implant-skin contact, lowers complication rates, and crucially, eliminates the dependency on costly biologic or synthetic meshes (14).

Parallel to this, endoscopic-assisted surgery has established its role in breast surgery, valued for providing magnified visualization, enabling precise dissection in confined spaces, and allowing access through remote, inconspicuous incisions—most commonly in the axilla (15-17). Its application in nipple-sparing mastectomy and immediate reconstruction has demonstrated benefits in reducing incision-related complications and improving cosmetic outcomes by concealing surgical scars (18-20). While endoscopic techniques have been independently described for both prepectoral and subpectoral implant placement (21,22), their systematic integration with a structured, multi-layered autologous coverage technique like the triple-plane method has not yet been reported.

We hypothesize that the synergistic combination of endoscopic precision and the robust architectural support of the triple-plane anatomy can optimize subpectoral breast reconstruction (Figure 1). This integration aims to harness the advantages of minimally invasive surgery—improved cosmesis and potentially reduced tissue trauma—while retaining and potentially enhancing the fundamental benefits of the triple-plane technique: complete mesh-free autologous coverage and preservation of muscular function. This technical report details the procedural steps of the endoscopic-assisted triple-plane technique and presents an evaluation of its early feasibility and clinical outcomes. We present this article in accordance with the SUPER reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2026-0224/rc).

Schematic illustration of the endoscopy-assisted triple-plane technique for breast reconstruction. The three circles denote the three anatomical planes: the pectoralis major muscle plane (A-plane); the serratus anterior fascia plane lateral to the pectoralis major (B-plane); and the deep layer of the superficial fascia inferior to the pectoralis major (C-plane). Both the lateral (B-plane) and inferior (C-plane) components are connected to the pectoralis major through the deep layer of the superficial fascia, allowing for expansion of the pocket volume based on the size of the prosthesis as required.

Preoperative preparations and requirements

This prospective, single-arm, technical feasibility study was conducted between January 2025 and December 2025. No formal sample size calculation was performed; the sample size represents a consecutive series of eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria and underwent the procedure during the study period. The primary objective was to demonstrate the feasibility and safety of the endoscopic-assisted triple-plane technique. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Cancer Hospital (No. SBQLL-2025-391). All procedures were performed in a dedicated operating theatre with standard sterile conditions at a tertiary cancer center. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for publication of this article and any accompanying images.

A total of 89 female patients with breast cancer, who opted for implant-based reconstruction following mastectomy, were recruited. The inclusion criteria were: (I) histopathologically confirmed breast cancer; (II) age between 25 and 75 years; (III) no evidence of distant metastasis; and (IV) planned for total mastectomy with immediate implant-based reconstruction. Exclusion criteria comprised: (I) active smoking or smoking history within 4 weeks before surgery; (II) recent chemotherapy (within 4 weeks); (III) prior radiotherapy to the breast region; (IV) immunocompromised status or immunodeficiency disorders; (V) uncontrolled diabetes mellitus; (VI) body mass index (BMI) ≥35 kg/m2; (VII) planned postoperative radiotherapy after reconstruction; and (VIII) presence of surgical contraindications, severe impairment of vital organ function, or active severe infection.

All patients underwent immediate reconstruction using the endoscopic-assisted triple-plane technique, performed by the same senior surgical team. The procedures were performed by two experienced breast surgeons (W.H. and Y.H.) who had each performed >50 endoscopic breast surgeries prior to this study. No specific additional training was required beyond standard endoscopic skills. Prior to mastectomy, preoperative markings were made, indicating the sternal midline, bilateral breast contours, and inframammary folds. Standard measurements, including breast base width, skin and subcutaneous tissue thickness, and key distances for symmetry assessment, were documented.

All patients underwent nipple-sparing mastectomy (NSM) when oncologically appropriate [no tumor involvement of the nipple-areola complex, tumor distance >2 cm from the nipple-areola complex (NAC), and no Paget’s disease]. Skin-sparing mastectomy was performed in 8 patients (9.0%) where NSM was contraindicated. Sentinel lymph node biopsy was performed in 75 patients (84.3%); if intraoperative frozen section of the sentinel node was positive, axillary lymph node dissection was performed (14 patients, 15.7%). Implant selection was based on approximately 75% of the resected breast tissue volume, combined with preoperative measurements of breast base width, projection, and height, as well as soft tissue envelope quality. All prostheses were textured surface silicone implants. Closed-suction drains were placed in all patients (one axillary, one chest wall) and were maintained until output was <30 mL per 24-hour period. Postoperative restrictions included no arm abduction >90 degrees for 4 weeks and no heavy lifting for 6 weeks. No patients required conversion to open surgery or intraoperative modification of the planned technique.

The final analyzed cohort consisted of 89 patients. The mean age was 44.43±8.64 years, and the average BMI was 22.82±3.09 kg/m2. The mean mastectomy specimen volume was 350.11±119.61 mL, and the mean implant volume used was 268.92±78.65 mL. Pathological staging distribution was as follows: stage 0 (n=16, 17.98%), stage IA (n=31, 34.83%), stage IB (n=22, 24.72%), and stage IIA (n=20, 22.47%). In total, 53 patients (59.55%) were diagnosed with luminal A/B subtype, 26 (29.21%) with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive subtype, and 10 (11.24%) with triple-negative subtype.

Step-by-step description

Endoscopic retropectoral pocket creation

Following the induction of general anesthesia, the patient was placed in a supine position with the ipsilateral upper limb abducted to 90 degrees. A single 5-cm incision was made along the natural skin creases of the axilla. Under direct vision, the lateral border of the pectoralis major muscle was identified. A preliminary subcutaneous and pre-pectoral dissection was performed to accommodate a wound retractor. A disposable wound protector was then inserted, and an endoscopic operative space was established using a modified surgical glove with one observational and two working ports. The cavity was continuously insufflated with carbon dioxide to maintain a stable pressure of 10 mmHg.

Under magnified endoscopic visualization, the avascular plane between the pectoralis major and pectoralis minor muscles was identified and entered. An ultrasonic dissector was used to systematically develop this plane. The inferior costal attachments of the pectoralis major were released. Dissection proceeded caudally along the superficial layer of the deep fascia, extending 2–5 cm beyond the native inframammary fold, with the precise distance determined by the planned implant size. The dissection continued laterally along the same fascial plane toward the pre-marked lateral breast contour, carefully preserving the continuity between the fascia and the pectoralis major muscle. This sequence established the foundational retropectoral pocket and initiated the elevation of the lateral fascial flap (B-plane) (Figure 2A,2B).

Intraoperative endoscopic views of endoscopy-assisted triple-plane breast reconstruction. (A,B) Creation of the retropectoral pocket. (C) Dissection of the retromammary space. (D,E) Elevation of subcutaneous flaps. (F-H) Peripheral fascial expansion to establish B-plane and C-plane coverage.

Endoscopic dissection of the retromammary space

Using an electrosurgical device with a hook attachment, the pectoralis major fascia was carefully dissected to expose the retromammary space (Figure 2C). The dissection boundaries extended superiorly to approximately 2 cm below the clavicle, medially to the parasternal line, inferiorly to the inframammary fold, and laterally to 1–2 cm medial to the lateral border of the pectoralis major muscle. The deep layer of the superficial fascia was meticulously preserved beyond the pectoral border, corresponding to the pre‑marked breast footprint on the pectoralis major surface. The parasternal ligaments and the triangular converging ligaments were carefully retained to maintain medial breast contour and stability.

Flap elevation and management of the NAC

Using an electrosurgical device, the skin flaps were raised along the superficial layer of the superficial fascia to a uniform thickness of approximately 0.5 cm (Figure 2D,2E). After complete mobilization of the entire breast skin envelope, the glandular tissue was circumferentially separated along the circummammary ligaments. The whole breast specimen was then removed and immediately weighed, and its volume was measured.

When dissection reached the area directly beneath the NAC, a scalpel was used for meticulous sharp dissection to excise the major lactiferous ducts while preserving the subdermal vascular plexus. A thickness of approximately 0.3 cm was maintained for the sub-areolar tissue, and the nipple skin was preserved at about 0.5 cm in thickness. A biopsy specimen from the NAC base was sent for intraoperative frozen section analysis.

Peripheral fascial expansion (creation of B-plane and C-plane)

After removal of the breast specimen, endoscopic re-inspection of the cavity was performed for final pocket adjustment. To create the lower pole coverage (C-plane), the preserved deep layer of the superficial fascia at the inferior border of the pectoralis major was dissected caudally to connect seamlessly with the previously established retropectoral pocket, thereby forming a continuous sling for the lower pole of the prosthesis. To establish the lateral coverage (B-plane), the superficial fascia preserved along the lateral border of the pectoralis major was mobilized outward to join the lateral extent of the retropectoral dissection (Figure 2F-2H). This maneuver completed the lateral fascial flap, ensuring that the prosthesis would be fully enveloped inferolaterally by vascularized autologous tissue.

Implant placement and closure

After achieving meticulous hemostasis, the pocket was copiously irrigated with dilute povidone-iodine followed by sterile saline. Surgical gloves and instruments were changed. The prosthesis, coated with antibiotic powder, was introduced into the pocket and positioned deep to the pectoralis major. A textured surface silicone implant was oriented so that its lower pole marker aligned with the 6 o’clock position of the breast mound, ensuring complete coverage by the pectoralis major superomedially and the mobilized fascial flaps inferolaterally. After confirming satisfactory symmetry, the capsule was closed with interrupted 3-0 absorbable sutures. Closed-suction drainage tubes were placed in the axilla and along the chest wall, followed by standard layered wound closure.

Postoperative considerations and tasks

Postoperatively, all patients received standardized care, which included prophylactic intravenous antibiotics administered every 8 to 12 hours for 24–48 hours. Surgical drains were maintained until the daily output was less than 30 mL. A comprehensive set of perioperative data was recorded, including operative time, intraoperative blood loss, total drainage volume within 7 days postoperative, time to drain removal, and total hospitalization duration. Patients were enrolled in a structured follow-up protocol with evaluations scheduled at 3, 6, 12, and 18 months after surgery. The primary outcomes—aesthetic results and BREAST-Q scores—were evaluated at the 6-month follow-up visit, at which time all 89 patients (100%) completed the assessment. The 12- and 18-month visits were used for monitoring long-term complications and aesthetic stability; completion rates were 95.5% (85/89) at 12 months and 91.0% (81/89) at 18 months. Losses to follow-up (n=8 by 18 months) were due to patients relocating (n=3), non-compliance with scheduled visits (n=3), or transfer of care to other institutions (n=2). Surgical safety was rigorously monitored through the meticulous documentation of complications. Major complications of primary interest were implant loss/exchange and implant exposure. Other monitored complications included capsular contracture (graded using the Baker classification system), skin flap necrosis requiring surgical intervention (e.g., debridement), hematoma and seroma (confirmed ultrasonographically), subcutaneous effusion (identified by clinical examination and needle aspiration), and incision-related adverse events (e.g., redness, delayed healing, or infection).

Patient-reported outcomes were assessed using the BREAST-Q reconstruction module, which was a validated, procedure-specific patient-reported outcome measure developed by Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center to assess satisfaction and health-related quality of life following breast surgery. This instrument measures health-related quality of life and satisfaction across six key domains: psychosocial well-being, physical well-being, sexual well-being, satisfaction with breasts, satisfaction with outcome, and satisfaction with care. Aesthetic outcomes were independently evaluated by two plastic surgeons who were blinded to the surgical technique used. They applied the standardized criteria reported by Ueda, assessing the reconstructed breast from three standard views (one frontal and two oblique). Specific attention was paid to the breast mound, nipple-areola complex, and inframammary fold. Scores were categorized as excellent (≥9 points), good (7–8 points), fair (5–6 points), or poor (≤4 points). Health economic indicators were also collected, focusing on the direct medical costs associated with the initial reconstruction procedure and the management of any related complications. This comprehensive assessment framework ensured the consistent monitoring of objective surgical outcomes, patient perspectives, and economic factors throughout the recovery and follow-up period, providing robust data for a holistic analysis of the technique’s effectiveness.

Tips and pearls

Several technical refinements are pivotal for optimizing outcomes. Primarily, precise identification and preservation of the deep layer of the superficial fascia form the cornerstone for developing the C-plane and ensuring adequate lateral fascial extension. Implant placement prior to finalizing the C-plane dissection is advised, as this permits direct visualization of the required fascial expansion and facilitates optimal lower pole coverage. Magnified endoscopic visualization is indispensable for identifying and preserving the perforating vessels along the lateral pectoral border. These vessels are critical for maintaining axial perfusion of the serratus anterior fascial flap (B-plane). Furthermore, fastidious hemostasis combined with ultrasonic dissection is paramount for maintaining visual clarity and achieving precision within the constrained endoscopic working space. In case of uncontrolled bleeding or inadequate endoscopic visualization, conversion to open surgery through the same axillary incision or an additional inframammary incision should be considered. To prevent bottoming-out deformity, several technical maneuvers are critical: (I) meticulous preservation of the circummammary ligament during mastectomy; (II) creation of a continuous fascial sling (C-plane) by dissecting the preserved deep layer of the superficial fascia inferiorly to connect with the retropectoral pocket; and (III) interrupted capsular closure with 3-0 absorbable sutures to reinforce the lower pole.

Data analysis

This study employed R (version 4.3.1) software for data processing and analysis. Continuous variables, such as patient age and operation time, were calculated and statistically described as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables, such as pathological stage and complication rates, were expressed as numbers (n) and percentages (%).

The endoscopic-assisted triple-plane procedure was successfully performed in all patients in the experimental group (Figure 3). Surgical safety

All predefined complications were systematically monitored. No cases of implant loss/exchange, implant exposure, capsular contracture (Baker grade ≥ III), skin flap necrosis requiring debridement, hematoma requiring surgical evacuation, seroma requiring aspiration, or incision delayed healing (>21 days) occurred. The only recorded complication was wound infection in 4 patients (4.49%), defined as clinical signs (erythema, warmth, purulent discharge) requiring antibiotics. These infections occurred on postoperative days 7–14 and were successfully treated with oral antibiotics (n=3) or intravenous antibiotics (n=1). No reoperation, implant exchange, or readmission was required. All patients resolved without sequelae.

Patient-reported and aesthetic outcomes

Aesthetic outcomes and patient-reported outcomes were assessed at the 6-month postoperative visit, with a 100% completion rate (89/89 patients). Aesthetic outcomes were independently evaluated by two plastic surgeons who were not involved in the surgical procedures. They were blinded to patient clinical data (including complications and hospital course) but not to the surgical technique itself, as all patients underwent the same endoscopic-assisted triple-plane procedure. Inter-rater agreement was excellent, and any disagreements (n=3) were resolved by consensus discussion. The postoperative breast appearance was rated as excellent by 86 (96.63%) patients and good by 3 (3.37%) patients. None of the subjects rated their results as fair or poor. During the follow-up period, the shape and size of all reconstructed breasts were similar and symmetrical to the contralateral side, with no obvious deformity observed. The validated Chinese version of the BREAST-Q reconstruction module (Version 2.0, Q-ScoreTM platform, Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center) was administered at the 6-month follow-up visit by an independent research coordinator. The instrument measures six official domains: satisfaction with breasts, satisfaction with outcome, satisfaction with care, psychosocial well-being, physical well-being, and sexual well-being. Scores were transformed to a 0–100 scale according to the official scoring manual; higher scores indicate greater satisfaction or well-being. No missing data were present for the completed questionnaires. Preoperative BREAST-Q scores were not available, as reconstruction was performed immediately following mastectomy; therefore, the results are presented as postoperative descriptive satisfaction data without direct preoperative comparison. According to the BREAST-Q scores, the patients’ scores for psychosocial well-being, sexual health, breast appearance, clothing, chest activity, and overall BREAST-Q score were 99.43±2.56, 74.24±3.91, 77.56±5.85, 97.15±4.90, 88.03±4.16, and 87.28±2.24, respectively, indicating a high overall satisfaction with the reconstruction process and results. In a few patients, very mild lower pole descent was observed during follow-up; the degree was clinically minimal and did not require reoperation or additional intervention.

Discussion

This study establishes that the integration of endoscopic assistance with the triple-plane technique constitutes a viable and refined approach to subpectoral implant-based breast reconstruction. The principal innovation resides in the synergistic combination of two advanced concepts: the complete autologous coverage afforded by the triple-plane anatomy and the minimally invasive precision conferred by endoscopic visualization.

In China, subpectoral implant-based breast reconstruction remains the most prevalent method (9). The primary challenge in conventional subpectoral techniques is achieving adequate inferolateral prosthesis coverage without resorting to costly meshes or complex autologous flaps (14). While meshes can enhance lower pole contour and reduce displacement (7,8), their substantial cost presents a significant economic barrier to widespread adoption, particularly in resource-conscious healthcare environments (23). The triple-plane technique was originally conceived to address this very issue by creating a mesh-free, vascularized tissue envelope (14,24). Our findings indicate that the endoscopic approach not only preserves this fundamental advantage but potentially augments it. Compared with our previously reported open triple-plane technique (14), the endoscopic-assisted approach achieved similar rates of complete autologous coverage (100% vs. 100%) and favorable major complication profiles (0% vs. 1.04%), while offering additional advantages including a shorter incision (5.47±0.52 vs. 12.24±4.01 cm) and a well-concealed axillary scar. Additionally, the magnified endoscopic view facilitates precise dissection and reliable preservation of the perforating vessels supplying the fascial flap, which is more challenging in the open approach. When compared with conventional subpectoral reconstruction reported in the literature (9,11), which is associated with a higher risk of animation deformity and frequent mesh usage, our technique eliminated mesh dependency and, theoretically, the risk of animation deformity, although formal assessment of animation deformity was not performed in this study. The magnified endoscopic view allows for meticulous dissection and preservation of the critical perforating vessels pedicled to the serratus anterior fascia flap (B-plane), which is paramount for ensuring its vascularity and long-term viability. This enhanced precision under direct vision likely contributes to the low incidence of major complications observed in our preliminary series.

The endoscopic component addresses several limitations inherent to both traditional open surgery and the blind dissection of fascial planes. First, it enables the creation of a well-defined prosthetic pocket with unparalleled accuracy, as the surgeon can visually confirm the extent of dissection in all three planes (A, B, and C) and ensure hemostasis in real-time. Second, the single axillary incision offers a superior cosmetic outcome by concealing the surgical scar, a significant concern for many patients (15,16,25). Furthermore, the minimally invasive nature of the approach, characterized by a smaller skin incision and potentially reduced tissue trauma, may contribute to the favorable postoperative recovery metrics observed, such as the shorter hospitalization duration in our cohort compared to traditional open reconstructions in China. The minor complication rate of 4.49% (4/89, all wound infections) in our series is within the lower range of reported complication rates for conventional subpectoral reconstruction, which range from 10% to 25% and include hematoma and implant exposure (11,12). This rate is also comparable to the 1.04% major complication rate reported for the open triple-plane technique (14); the difference reflects our inclusion of minor wound infections as complications, whereas the prior study reported only major events requiring surgical intervention.

When compared to other reconstructive options, the endoscopic-assisted triple-plane technique occupies a unique position. It avoids the donor site morbidity and functional sacrifice associated with the latissimus dorsi flap (26,27). Simultaneously, it circumvents the issues of animation deformity and persistent pain linked to traditional subpectoral dissection with partial muscle coverage (11,12). The endoscopic-assisted triple-plane technique theoretically preserves pectoralis major function, which may reduce the risk of animation deformity compared with conventional subpectoral dissection; however, formal assessment of animation deformity was not performed in this study, and this potential advantage remains speculative. The high patient satisfaction scores across various BREAST-Q domains in our study underscore the technique’s success in meeting patient expectations for both aesthetic and psychosocial outcomes. We attribute the low complication rate (4.49% minor complications, no major complications) to several synergistic factors. First, the endoscopic approach provides magnified visualization, reducing surgical blind areas, enabling precise dissection and reliable preservation of the perforating vessels that supply the serratus anterior fascial flap (B-plane), thereby maintaining robust tissue vascularity. Second, complete autologous coverage of the prosthesis eliminates direct implant-skin contact, potentially reducing the risk of exposure, rippling, and capsular contracture. Third, the single axillary incision (5 cm) minimizes skin flap trauma and devascularization compared with traditional inframammary fold incisions (typically 10–15 cm). These anatomical and technical advantages, rather than the endoscopic approach alone, collectively contribute to the favorable safety profile.

The low rate of capsular contracture in our series, with no cases reaching Baker grade III or higher, is another noteworthy finding. We postulate that the vascularized fascial coverage, precisely mobilized under endoscopic guidance, creates a more favorable biological environment around the prosthesis. This viable tissue interface may mitigate chronic inflammation and foreign body reaction, which are key drivers of capsular formation (7,24). However, the follow-up duration was relatively short, as capsular contracture typically develops over 1–3 years. Whether the vascularized fascial coverage reduces the risk of capsular contracture remains a hypothesis that requires validation in long-term studies (≥3 years).

Notwithstanding these encouraging results, certain limitations and technical considerations warrant acknowledgment. The learning curve associated with mastering endoscopic breast reconstruction is considerable, demanding proficiency in both endoscopic technique and a profound comprehension of the three-plane surgical anatomy. The integrity of the fascial flap blood supply is dependent on the lateral thoracic perforators; therefore, patients with a history of axillary lymph node dissection require careful preoperative evaluation (24,28). Another important limitation of this study is the strict patient selection criteria. The exclusion of patients with active smoking, uncontrolled diabetes, BMI ≥35 kg/m2, prior or planned radiotherapy, recent chemotherapy (within 4 weeks), or immunocompromised status created a relatively low-risk cohort. While this selection was appropriate for an initial feasibility study, it likely contributed to the favorable complication profile observed. Therefore, the outcomes may not be directly generalizable to higher-risk patient populations, and the results should be interpreted in the context of this selection bias. Additionally, the initial investment in specialized endoscopic equipment may be a barrier for some institutions. Most importantly, the single-arm design of this study limits the strength of our conclusions. The favorable outcomes, while promising, were not compared directly with a concurrent control group undergoing alternative techniques. The observed advantages in recovery and satisfaction, though plausible, require validation through future comparative studies, ideally randomized controlled trials.

In conclusion, the endoscopic-assisted triple-plane technique constitutes a refined and mesh-free approach in the armamentarium of breast reconstructive surgery. In this single-arm feasibility study, the technique demonstrated promising integration of the principles of anatomical stability and functional preservation with the documented benefits of minimally invasive surgery, including a favorable safety profile, high patient satisfaction. Mesh avoidance may also offer potential material cost savings, although formal cost-effectiveness analysis was not performed in this study. However, these preliminary findings require confirmation in comparative trials before definitive conclusions can be drawn. Future prospective, randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up are warranted to validate its long-term benefits, particularly regarding capsular contracture rates and aesthetic longevity, and to definitively establish its clinical value and position in the reconstructive algorithm.

Conclusions

In this prospective series, the endoscopic-assisted triple-plane technique appears to be a significant advance in subpectoral implant-based breast reconstruction. It effectively integrates the principle of complete autologous prosthesis coverage with the precision and cosmetic benefits of minimally invasive endoscopic surgery. These preliminary findings demonstrate a favorable safety profile, achieve high patient satisfaction and excellent aesthetic outcomes, while effectively obviating the need for costly biologic or synthetic meshes. As a reproducible and mesh-free alternative, the endoscopic triple-plane approach promises to expand reconstructive options for breast cancer patients. However, as this was a single-arm feasibility study without a control group, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Prospective randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up are warranted to confirm these findings and to compare this technique with conventional approaches.
