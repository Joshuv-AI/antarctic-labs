Interleukin‐40 in Systemic Sclerosis: A Treatment‐Sensitive Marker of Immune Modulation Rather Than Disease Activity
Authors: İbrahim Vasi, Emin Ümit Bağrıaçık, Rıza Can Kardaş, Nihan Örüklü, Melek Yaman, Derya Yıldırım, Burcugül Kaya, Rahime Duran, İbrahim Karaduman, İbrahim Yahya Çakır, Hamit Küçük, Berna Göker, Abdulsamet Erden, Mehmet Akif Öztürk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInterleukin‐40 (IL‐40) is a recently identified cytokine primarily produced by activated B cells and implicated in immune regulation across several autoimmune diseases. Although B cell–driven immune dysregulation plays a central role in systemic sclerosis (SSc), the circulating behavior and clinical relevance of IL‐40 in this disease remain poorly defined. To characterize serum IL‐40 levels in patients with SSc and to explore their associations with immunosuppressive treatment exposure, disease subsets, clinical manifestations, and disease activity. Serum IL‐40 levels were measured by ELISA in 41 patients with SSc and 29 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls. Patients were stratified according to immunosuppressive treatment status at the time of serum sampling. Clinical features, organ involvement, and disease activity were recorded. Serum IL‐40 levels differed significantly among immunosuppressed SSc patients, untreated SSc patients, and healthy controls (p < 0.001). Patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy exhibited markedly lower IL‐40 concentrations compared with both untreated SSc patients and healthy controls. In contrast, untreated SSc patients showed IL‐40 levels comparable to controls. Serum IL‐40 levels were not significantly associated with cutaneous disease subtype, interstitial lung disease, dysphagia, or disease activity status. Stratified analyses demonstrated that lower IL‐40 levels in treated patients were observed irrespective of disease activity. Lower circulating IL‐40 levels were observed in immunosuppressed patients irrespective of disease phenotype, organ involvement, or disease activity. These findings support an association between treatment exposure and circulating IL‐40 levels, although longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causality.Keywords: disease activity, IL‐40, systemic sclerosis

Introduction

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by a pathogenesis that begins with inflammation, especially in the early stages, and can subsequently progress to vasculopathy and fibrosis [1]. Clinically, SSc is classified into diffuse, limited, and sine subsets based on the extent of skin involvement [2]. In parallel, molecular profiling studies have recognized distinct transcriptional signatures reflecting heterogeneous immune and fibrotic pathways, highlighting the biological diversity of the disease [1].

Cytokines play a central role in directing immune responses and modulating interactions between immune cells and target tissues [3]. One of the more recently identified cytokines is interleukin‐40 (IL‐40), first described by Catalan et al. in 2017. IL‐40 is primarily produced by activated B cells and is essential in B cell homeostasis and humoral immune responses [4]. Subsequent studies have demonstrated that IL‐40 expression is altered in several autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and primary Sjögren's syndrome, suggesting a broader role in immune‐mediated inflammation [5, 6, 7].

Importantly, accumulating evidence indicates that IL‐40 expression may be highly context‐dependent. In RA, elevated IL‐40 levels have been reported in early disease and shown to decrease following immunomodulatory therapy, particularly B cell–targeted treatments [5]. In SLE, serum IL‐40 levels do not consistently reflect disease manifestations, while tissue‐level expression appears more pronounced in organ‐specific involvement such as lupus nephritis, supporting the concept of compartmentalized IL‐40 activity [7]. Similarly, in primary Sjögren's syndrome, IL‐40 has been linked to immune activation and tissue inflammation, although its circulating levels may be influenced by disease stage and treatment exposure [6].

B cells are increasingly recognized as major contributors to SSc pathogenesis [8]. Molecular signatures of B cell activation have been identified in skin biopsies, particularly in early disease, and B cell–directed therapies have demonstrated clinical efficacy in selected patient subsets [9]. Activated B cells may contribute to fibrosis through the production of profibrotic cytokines, including transforming growth factor‐β and interleukin‐6, and through interactions conducted by B cell–activating factor (BAFF) [10]. Given the central role of B cell–driven immune dysregulation at the intersection of inflammation, vasculopathy, and fibrosis in SSc, cytokines selectively produced by activated B cells may represent critical yet underexplored mediators of disease heterogeneity. IL‐40, a recently identified B cell–associated cytokine implicated in several autoimmune diseases, represents a plausible immunological mediator in SSc. However, to date, no studies have evaluated IL‐40 in systemic sclerosis, and its circulating behavior and clinical relevance in this disease remain incompletely defined.

We aimed to characterize serum interleukin‐40 levels in patients with systemic sclerosis and to explore their potential clinical relevance across disease subsets and immunological phenotypes. We evaluated differences in circulating IL‐40 levels between patients with systemic sclerosis and healthy individuals and examined their associations with disease subtype, major clinical manifestations, disease activity, and exposure to immunosuppressive therapy.

Methods

Study Cohorts

This study included 41 SSc patients aged over 18 years who were diagnosed with SSc according to the 2013 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) classification criteria [11]. Patients were primarily stratified according to immunosuppressive treatment status at the time of serum sampling. Of the 41 patients with systemic sclerosis, 25 were not receiving immunosuppressive therapy, whereas 16 were receiving at least one immunosuppressive agent at the time of serum sampling. Disease activity (active disease or remission) was subsequently recorded within each treatment group and used for stratified analyses. Twenty‐nine age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls were also included. Participants were recruited between November 2024 and January 2025 from the Rheumatology Unit at the Gazi University Hospital. Exclusion criteria were concurrent malignancy, pregnancy, evidence of infection, other rheumatologic diseases such as autoinflammatory diseases, seronegative spondylarthritis, and patient refusal.

Clinical and Laboratory Data Collected

Demographic data were recorded, including sex, age, and disease duration. The disease duration was defined as the time from the initial onset of the first non‐Raynaud symptoms.

Clinical assessments were performed in all patients, including symptoms of the disease course, such as Raynaud's phenomenon, sclerodactyly, telangiectasia, sicca syndrome, arthralgia, myositis, digital ulcers, esophageal dysphagia, interstitial lung disease, pulmonary arterial hypertension, and scleroderma renal crisis.

Serological data related to SSc diagnosis, such as antinuclear antibody, anti‐topoisomerase I antibody, and anticentromere antibody, were collected and recorded.

Disease activity status was assessed independently by two experienced rheumatologists at the time of serum sampling. Clinical disease activity was initially evaluated based on combined clinical and laboratory assessment, including newly developed or progressive digital ulcers, worsening skin fibrosis, progression of pulmonary fibrosis, active inflammatory arthritis, newly developed or progressive myositis, scleroderma renal crisis, or elevated acute‐phase reactants. These domains were selected in accordance with manifestations incorporated into the European Scleroderma Study Group Activity Index (EScSG‐AI) [12]. To further standardize disease activity assessment, EScSG‐AI scores were retrospectively calculated for all patients. Patients with an EScSG‐AI score ≥ 3 were classified as having active disease, whereas those with a score < 3 were classified as being in remission. Immunosuppressive treatment status was also recorded at the time of serum sampling by two rheumatologists. Patients were classified as receiving immunosuppressive therapy if they were actively treated with at least one immunosuppressive agent. The type of immunosuppressive agents was recorded, and patients were classified according to exposure to conventional and/or biologic immunosuppressive therapies.

Measurement of IL‐40 Levels

Serum samples of patients with SSc and healthy controls were collected consecutively at the Gazi University Hospital. All serum samples were stored at −80°C until analysis. The serum levels of human IL‐40 were assessed using a commercial ELISA kit (Abbexa, Cat. No: abx508530, Cambridge, United Kingdom) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, serum samples were added to the wells of ELISA plates were incubated at room temperature for the time instructed in the test procedures. The plate was washed three times using a microplate washer (Tecan, HydroFlex plus, Switzerland) after each step in the method. Antibody conjugate was added to the wells of microplates for the enzymatic reaction. Absorbance values were measured at 450 nm by a plate reader (Biotek, Synergy HT, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics of the study population. Categorical variables are presented as absolute frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using visual inspection and distributional characteristics. As serum IL‐40 levels showed a non‐normal distribution with marked right skewness, data are presented as median (IQR), and non‐parametric statistical methods were applied throughout the analyses.

Patients with systemic sclerosis were primarily stratified according to immunosuppressive treatment status at the time of serum sampling (receiving IS vs. not receiving IS). Healthy controls were included as an independent comparison group. Differences in serum IL‐40 levels among SSc patients receiving IS, SSc patients not receiving IS, and healthy controls were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When a statistically significant overall difference was detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing.

Comparisons between two independent patient subgroups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. These analyses included comparisons according to cutaneous disease subtype (diffuse vs. limited systemic sclerosis), interstitial lung disease status (presence vs. absence), and dysphagia status (presence vs. absence).

To account for potential confounding, an exploratory multivariable linear regression analysis was performed with serum IL‐40 level as the dependent variable. Age, disease duration, cutaneous subset, anti‐Scl‐70 positivity, and immunosuppressive treatment exposure were included as independent variables. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors.

All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using RStudio.

Ethics Statement

Consent was obtained from all participants, and the ethics committee approved the study protocol (Ankara Training and Research Hospital Ethics Committee, date 06.11.2024, number E‐24‐234). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Study Population and Immunosuppressive Treatment Profile

A total of 70 individuals were included in the study, comprising 41 patients with systemic sclerosis and 29 healthy controls. Among the 41 patients with SSc, 16 were receiving immunosuppressive therapy at the time of the study, whereas 25 were not.

When comparing the two SSc subgroups, patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy were younger, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (mean age 50.9 ± 11.3 vs. 58.2 ± 15.8 years). Disease duration was comparable between the groups (8.1 ± 3.3 vs. 9.8 ± 5.8 years).

Evaluation of clinical manifestations among patients with SSc showed similar proportions of diffuse and limited cutaneous subtypes. Interstitial lung disease was present in 68.3% of patients, and esophageal dysphagia was observed in 43.9%. Among patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy, 11 patients were treated with mycophenolate mofetil, 4 with rituximab, and 1 with methotrexate. Serum IL‐40 Levels in SSc Subgroups and Healthy Controls

Patients with SSc not receiving immunosuppressive therapy had the highest serum IL‐40 levels, with a median of 1.40 ng/mL (IQR, 1.15). In contrast, patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy demonstrated substantially lower IL‐40 concentrations, with a median of 0.51 ng/mL (IQR, 0.34) and a narrower distribution (0.27–0.97 ng/mL). Healthy controls showed intermediate serum IL‐40 levels, with a median of 1.14 ng/mL (IQR, 1.75).

Overall, serum IL‐40 levels differed significantly among the three groups (Kruskal–Wallis test, H = 17.09, df = 2, p < 0.001). Post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction revealed that patients with SSc receiving immunosuppressive therapy had significantly lower IL‐40 levels than both healthy controls (adjusted p = 0.006) and untreated patients with SSc (adjusted p < 0.001). In contrast, no significant difference was observed between healthy controls and untreated SSc patients (adjusted p = 0.652). Serum IL‐40 Levels According to Clinical Involvement

When stratified according to clinical involvement, serum IL‐40 concentrations did not show significant differences. Comparable IL‐40 levels were observed between diffuse and limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis subtypes (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, p = 0.411). Similarly, no significant differences in serum IL‐40 levels were detected between patients with and without interstitial lung disease (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, p = 0.435) or between those with and without dysphagia (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, p = 0.314). Serum IL‐40 Levels Stratified by Immunosuppressive Treatment and Disease Activity

When serum IL‐40 levels were stratified by immunosuppressive treatment status and disease activity, lower concentrations were observed among patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy, both in those with active disease and in those in remission. In contrast, patients not receiving immunosuppressive therapy exhibited higher and more variable IL‐40 levels, irrespective of disease activity status (Figure 3). No statistically significant differences were observed according to disease activity status within treatment strata. To validate disease activity assessment, the EScSG‐AI was retrospectively calculated for all patients. Patients classified as having active disease exhibited significantly higher EScSG‐AI scores (scores > 3) than those in remission (median 4.0 [IQR 1.0] vs. 0.0 [IQR 2.0], p < 0.001).

In exploratory multivariable linear regression analysis, immunosuppressive treatment exposure remained independently associated with lower serum IL‐40 levels after adjustment for age, disease duration, cutaneous subset, and anti‐Scl‐70 positivity (standardized β = −0.521, p = 0.001). Increasing age was also independently associated with lower IL‐40 concentrations (standardized β = −0.430, p = 0.007). Disease duration (p = 0.600), cutaneous subset (p = 0.886), and anti‐Scl‐70 positivity (p = 0.300) were not significant predictors. The overall model was statistically significant (adjusted R 2 = 0.303, p = 0.003), with no evidence of relevant multicollinearity.

Discussion

This study provides a comparative analysis of serum interleukin‐40 levels between patients with systemic sclerosis and healthy individuals and examines their associations with exposure to immunosuppressive treatment, clinical manifestations, organ involvement, and disease activity. The findings demonstrate a strong association between serum IL‐40 concentrations and exposure to immunosuppressive treatment, whereas disease subtype, organ involvement, and disease activity do not appear to be major determinants of circulating IL‐40 levels in systemic sclerosis. These findings suggest that circulating IL‐40 may primarily reflect treatment exposure rather than disease phenotype.

Association of Serum IL‐40 With Immunosuppressive Treatment Exposure

A marked reduction in serum IL‐40 levels was observed in patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy, irrespective of disease activity status. In contrast, patients not exposed to immunosuppressive treatment exhibited higher, more heterogeneous IL‐40 concentrations, comparable to those observed in healthy controls. Taken together, these findings suggest that circulating IL‐40 is more closely associated with treatment exposure than with disease phenotype. Notably, serum IL‐40 levels in untreated SSc patients were comparable to those observed in healthy controls, suggesting that circulating IL‐40 may not simply reflect disease presence but rather immunological modulation. Although major clinical characteristics were broadly comparable between treated and untreated patients, residual confounding related to treatment indication, prior disease course, or unmeasured factors cannot be excluded.

Importantly, the association between immunosuppressive treatment exposure and lower serum IL‐40 levels persisted in exploratory multivariable analysis after adjustment for key sources of systemic sclerosis heterogeneity, including age, disease duration, cutaneous subset, and anti‐Scl‐70 positivity. This supports the interpretation that reduced IL‐40 levels in treated patients are not solely explained by differences in disease duration, skin phenotype, or autoantibody profile. Nevertheless, given the limited sample size, this model should be considered exploratory and requires validation in larger cohorts.

In our cohort, nearly all patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy received mycophenolate mofetil or rituximab. These agents are well known to exert substantial effects on lymphocyte activation, cytokine production, and immune homeostasis in systemic sclerosis and other autoimmune diseases [13, 14]. Although our study was not designed to evaluate drug‐specific effects, lower IL‐40 levels were consistently observed among patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy. This association is biologically plausible, given that both mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab directly or indirectly target pathways involved in B cell activation and differentiation, which have been implicated in IL‐40 biology. However, the small numbers of patients receiving individual immunosuppressive agents, particularly rituximab and methotrexate, precluded meaningful comparisons between treatment classes. Furthermore, because of the cross‐sectional design, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct treatment‐induced reduction in circulating IL‐40 levels.

Associations Between Serum IL‐40 and Clinical Phenotype, Organ Involvement, and Disease Activity

In our cohort, serum IL‐40 levels did not show clear or consistent associations with major clinical phenotypes, including cutaneous disease subtype, interstitial lung disease, dysphagia, or overall disease activity. Notably, serum IL‐40 levels were not significantly associated with disease activity as assessed by the validated EScSG Activity Index. While lower IL‐40 concentrations were consistently observed among patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy, IL‐40 levels did not correlate with EScSG‐AI scores, suggesting that circulating IL‐40 may be more closely associated with treatment status than with contemporaneous disease activity. Nevertheless, the relatively small sample size and substantial variability in IL‐40 concentrations, particularly among untreated patients, may have limited our ability to detect modest associations with disease activity. These findings contrast with previous reports suggesting potential associations between IL‐40 and disease activity or organ involvement in systemic sclerosis [15]. Therefore, larger prospective studies are required to further clarify the relationship between circulating IL‐40 levels and disease activity in systemic sclerosis.

Comparison With IL‐40 Biology in Other Autoimmune Diseases

Findings from other autoimmune diseases provide useful context for interpreting our results. In early seropositive rheumatoid arthritis, serum IL‐40 levels have been reported to decrease rapidly after treatment initiation, in parallel with clinical improvement and reductions in inflammatory markers [16]. This pattern resembles the lower IL‐40 levels observed among treated patients in our cohort. In contrast, studies in primary Sjögren syndrome have described associations between serum IL‐40 levels and disease activity, together with functional effects on immune cells [6]. Differences between these findings and our results in systemic sclerosis may be related to differences in disease mechanisms and, importantly, to differences in treatment exposure. In patient groups with limited or no immunosuppressive treatment, IL‐40 levels may show a closer relationship with disease activity, whereas in treated populations this relationship may be less apparent.

Studies in systemic lupus erythematosus, particularly lupus nephritis, further illustrate this complexity. In these settings, serum IL‐40 levels do not consistently reflect disease involvement, while increased IL‐40 expression has been observed within affected tissues [17]. This observation suggests that measurements in the circulation may not fully represent IL‐40 activity occurring at the tissue level.

In addition, IL‐40 production is not restricted to a single immune cell type. Reports from rheumatoid arthritis and primary Sjögren syndrome indicate that IL‐40 can be produced by several immune cell populations, which may vary depending on the disease context [5, 6]. This cellular diversity may contribute to variability in circulating IL‐40 levels across different autoimmune conditions.

Tissue–Serum Dissociation and Treatment Effects on IL‐40

Taken together, our findings suggest that serum IL‐40 levels in systemic sclerosis are more closely associated with immunosuppressive treatment status than with clinical measures of disease activity or organ involvement and may not fully reflect immune activity occurring within affected tissues. The mechanisms underlying this association remain speculative. IL‐40 has been described as a B cell‐associated cytokine, suggesting that therapies targeting B cell function may influence its production. Rituximab induces B cell depletion, whereas mycophenolate mofetil suppresses lymphocyte proliferation and may indirectly reduce B cell activation and differentiation. However, the small numbers of patients receiving individual immunosuppressive agents, particularly rituximab and methotrexate, precluded meaningful comparisons between treatment classes. Further mechanistic studies incorporating tissue‐level analyses and longitudinal sampling will be required to clarify the role of IL‐40 in systemic sclerosis pathogenesis and its relationship with immunosuppressive treatment exposure. Observations from osteoarthritis and other inflammatory joint diseases support this view, as IL‐40 has been reported to accumulate locally in affected tissues without corresponding increases in circulation, where it may promote local inflammation and tissue damage [18].

Future studies should therefore prioritize longitudinal evaluations before and after initiation of immunosuppressive therapy, investigations in early or treatment‐naïve SSc populations, and tissue‐based or paired serum–tissue analyses to better define the pathogenic relevance of IL‐40 in systemic sclerosis.

Several limitations of this study should be considered. First, the cross‐sectional design does not allow causal inferences regarding the relationship between immunosuppressive treatment exposure and circulating IL‐40 levels. In addition, because treatment allocation was not randomized, confounding by indication remains an important consideration. Patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy may differ from untreated patients in ways not fully captured by the variables assessed in this study, including previous disease severity, treatment indications, cumulative disease burden, and other unmeasured factors. Therefore, the observed association between treatment exposure and lower IL‐40 levels should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a direct treatment effect. Second, the relatively limited sample size may have reduced the statistical power to detect more subtle associations with clinical phenotypes or organ involvement. In addition, the diversity of immunosuppressive regimens used in our cohort precluded meaningful drug‐specific analyses. Nevertheless, the consistent association between immunosuppressive treatment status and lower serum IL‐40 levels across multiple analyses supports the robustness of our primary observations.

In conclusion, circulating IL‐40 levels in systemic sclerosis were more closely associated with immunosuppressive treatment status than with disease subtype, organ involvement, or clinical activity. Lower IL‐40 levels were consistently observed among treated patients irrespective of disease activity. However, given the cross‐sectional design and the potential for confounding by indication, longitudinal studies are required to determine whether immunosuppressive therapies directly modulate circulating IL‐40 concentrations.
