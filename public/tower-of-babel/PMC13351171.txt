Clinical Characteristics and Molecular Epidemiology of Invasive Nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae at a Tertiary Medical Center in Washington, DC
Authors: Jose Lucar, Stephen Stone, Matthew Spence, Marc O Siegel, Afsoon Roberts, Tara N Palmore, Maria Elena Ruiz, Huma Saleem, Sarah Lohsen, Charlotte J Royer, Lauren F Collins, Rebecca Yee, Sarah W Satola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe observed a sharp rise in invasive nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae disease at a tertiary medical center in Washington, DC. Nine adults presented over 9 months with severe illness, with both clonal and nonclonal strains identified by genome sequencing. These findings suggest emerging transmission patterns and the need for expanded surveillance.Keywords: invasive bacterial infections, molecular epidemiology, nontypeable haemophilus influenzae, people with HIV, whole-genome sequencing

Nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae (NTHi) has become the predominant cause of invasive H. influenzae disease in the United States following widespread type b vaccination, which has dramatically reduced disease caused by encapsulated strains [1–4]. U.S. surveillance data have reported increased NTHi incidence across multiple age groups, and international studies similarly highlight a predominance of NTHi among invasive isolates [1, 2, 4–6].

Recent population-based studies have also identified distinctive demographic patterns among adults with invasive NTHi. In metropolitan Atlanta, clusters disproportionately involved Black men, people with HIV (PWH), and men who have sex with men (MSM), with whole-genome sequencing (WGS) confirming repeated involvement of 2 clonal lineages termed cluster 1 (C1) and cluster 2 (C2) [7, 8]. These strains were associated with musculoskeletal infections and geographic proximity, suggesting that interconnected social networks may influence transmission.

Beginning in late 2022, clinicians at our tertiary medical center in Washington, DC noted an unexpected increase in adult invasive NTHi cases, involving demographic groups similar to those previously reported in Atlanta [7, 8]. In response, we conducted a retrospective assessment to describe the clinical presentations, outcomes, and molecular characteristics of invasive NTHi identified at our institution.

METHODS

We conducted a retrospective review of adults hospitalized with invasive NTHi at the George Washington University Hospital in Washington, DC, between December 2022 and August 2023. Cases were identified through microbiology laboratory records. Invasive disease was defined as isolation of NTHi from a normally sterile site or clinical presentations indicating infection extending beyond the respiratory tract or skin. Haemophilus influenzae was isolated from patient specimens using standard culture methods on chocolate agar (Thermo Scientific Remel, Waltham, MA, USA) incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2, and species identification was performed using the Bruker MALDI Biotyper MBT Smart MS instrument (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) with the FDA-approved database. β-Lactamase production was assessed using BD BBL Cefinase β-Lactamase Detection Discs (BD Diagnostics, Sparks, MD, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Data abstracted from the electronic medical record included demographics, comorbidities, HIV status, antiretroviral therapy use, CD4 cell count, HIV viral load, and documented sexual behavior when available. Clinical manifestations, microbiologic findings, antimicrobial therapy, length of hospital stay, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and all-cause in-hospital mortality were recorded.

Frozen stocks of patient isolates were sent to the Georgia Emerging Infections Program laboratory (Atlanta, Georgia) for whole-genome sequencing. Upon receipt, frozen stocks were plated onto BD BBL Chocolate II Agar (BD Diagnostics, Sparks, MD, USA) and incubated overnight at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cluster designation (C1, C2, or noncluster) was assigned according to previously established genomic criteria [7, 8]. Laboratory personnel were blinded to all clinical information.

Continuous variables were summarized as medians or means with ranges, and categorical variables as counts with percentages. No inferential statistics were performed due to small sample size. The George Washington University Institutional Review Board determined that the study met criteria for exemption.

Bioinformatics Methods

Primary analytics, including read preparation, genome assembly, and MLST, were conducted using Bactopia v3.1.0 as described previously [8, 9]. Patient H. influenzae assemblies generated by Bactopia were aligned with Parsnp v2.1.5 [10]. This alignment included the complete C1 and C2 genomes published in 2025 (GA-C1 and GA-C2, respectively) for comparison, with GA-C2 used as the reference genome for alignment [7, 8]. ClonalFrameML v1.13 [11] was used to generate a core genome–masked alignment, and the resulting phylogeny was visualized using FigTree v1.4.4 [12]. The tool snp-dists v1.2.0 [13] was used to produce an all-versus-all matrix of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), which was visualized using Python 3.12 with the packages pandas [14], Seaborn [15], and matplotlib [16].

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics, Clinical Manifestations, and Outcomes

Nine adults with invasive NTHi were identified. The median age was 55 years (range 32–76). All individuals were male, and 8 (89%) were Black. Six (67%) identified as MSM. Five patients (56%) were PWH receiving antiretroviral therapy; the median CD4 count among PWH was 348 cells/mm3 (range 32–984), and 4 were virologically suppressed. Musculoskeletal disease was common: 5 patients presented with septic arthritis and/or osteomyelitis. Other presentations included bacteremia without a clear focal source and mixed invasive syndromes. Empiric therapy consisted primarily of ceftriaxone-based regimens tailored to final culture results. All 9 isolates tested negative for beta-lactamase production. The mean hospital stay was 11 days (range 3–28). Three patients required ICU admission, all of whom died during hospitalization.

Genomic Characterization

Seven of the 9 patients' isolates were available for whole-genome sequencing. One isolate belonged to C1 (PT-2), 3 to C2 (PT-5, PT-6, PT-8), and 3 were noncluster (PT-1, PT-3, PT-7), demonstrating cocirculation of both known clonal lineages and unrelated strains during the study period (Figure 1). Characterization revealed C1 and C2 samples were closely related, forming distinct lineages (Figure 1A), with comparatively few SNPs between lineage members (Figure 1B). C1 (PT-2) and C2 (PT-5, PT-6, PT-8) isolates belong to ST164 and ST1714, respectively. The noncluster isolates from PT-1 and PT-3 belong to ST436 and ST44 while the isolate from PT-7 did not match any known ST. These findings are consistent with previously described characteristics of the C1 and C2 lineages [7, 8].

DISCUSSION

This case series describes a notable increase in invasive nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae (NTHi) among adults hospitalized at a tertiary medical center in Washington, DC over a 9-month period. The cohort was composed predominantly of Black men, many of whom were PWH and/or MSM. Musculoskeletal infections, including septic arthritis and osteomyelitis, were frequent, and clinical severity was substantial, with all intensive care unit admissions resulting in death. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) demonstrated both recognized clonal clusters (C1/C2) and unrelated lineages, indicating that multiple transmission patterns were likely operating simultaneously.

These findings align with U.S. epidemiologic trends demonstrating an increase in invasive NTHi across all age groups following widespread type b vaccination [1–4]. The overrepresentation of Black men, PWH, and MSM closely parallels observations from metropolitan Atlanta, where clusters of invasive NTHi were linked to C1 and C2 lineages and associated with both social networks and musculoskeletal disease [7]. The detection of a mix of clonal and nonclonal isolates in our cohort also mirrors broader U.S. molecular surveillance data, which report heterogeneous strain populations that include both sporadic and cluster-associated infections [1]. International surveillance reinforces the predominance of NTHi among invasive H. influenzae isolates and highlights increasing antimicrobial resistance, an area of emerging concern [5, 6].

The strengths of this study include the use of detailed clinical data, integration of molecular characterization through WGS, and the identification of a demographic signal consistent with previously described risk networks. The ability to compare clinical and genomic patterns strengthens the relevance of the findings to both clinical practice and public health response. However, several limitations warrant consideration. The sample size was small, limiting generalizability and precluding statistical inference. Only 7 of 9 isolates were sequenced due to nonviability of the other 2. Behavioral information may have been under-documented in clinical notes, resulting in potential misclassification. As a single-center, retrospective analysis, the study may not reflect broader regional trends.

In conclusion, we identified a short-interval rise in invasive NTHi among adults in Washington, DC, marked by a demographic pattern similar to prior U.S. cluster-associated outbreaks and characterized by severe presentations, frequent musculoskeletal involvement, and mixed clonal and nonclonal strains. These findings underscore the need for expanded genomic epidemiologic surveillance to better understand transmission networks and guide prevention strategies for invasive NTHi in adult populations.
