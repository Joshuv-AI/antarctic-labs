I
Introduction

The gravitational collapse of a massive star’s iron core converts approximately

3\times 10^{53}\,\mathrm{erg}
of gravitational binding energy into neutrinos
over a timescale of tens of seconds
(
Colgate & White 1966
;
Bethe & Wilson 1985
;
Raffelt 1996
)
. This
enormous neutrino luminosity (
L_{\nu}\sim 10^{53}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}
) drives
the supernova shock revival via neutrino heating

(
Bethe & Wilson 1985
;
Janka 2012
;
Burrows 2013
;
Müller 2020
)
and simultaneously encodes
the thermodynamic state of the proto-neutron star (PNS), including the nuclear
equation of state, lepton fraction gradients, and the efficiency of convective
transport in dense matter
(
Pons et al. 1999
;
Lattimer & Prakash 2001
;
Steiner et al. 2013
)
.

A central observable of the neutrino burst is the energy spectrum of each
flavor. The spectrum evolves through three distinct phases: (i) the neutronization
burst at core bounce, when
\nu_{e}
alone peaks for
\sim 20\,\mathrm{ms}
;
(ii) the accretion phase, when the stalled shock drives a quasi-steady flux
at
L\sim 10^{52}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}
for
\sim 0.2
–
0.5\,\mathrm{s}
;
and (iii) the Kelvin–Helmholtz cooling phase, during which the PNS radiates
its residual thermal energy over
\sim 10
–
30\,\mathrm{s}

(
Burrows & Lattimer 1986
;
Pons et al. 1999
;
Hüdepohl et al. 2010
)
.

The spectral
shape
is commonly parametrized by the pinching parameter

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
, defined by the quasi-thermal (or “
\alpha
-fit”) distribution:

f(E;\,\langle E\rangle,\,\alpha_{\mathrm{p}})\propto\left(\frac{E}{\langle E\rangle}\right)^{\!\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}\exp\!\left[-(\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}+1)\frac{E}{\langle E\rangle}\right],

(1)

where
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
is a dimensionless spectral-shape parameter that
quantifies how narrowly the neutrino energy distribution is peaked around its
mean
\langle E\rangle
: larger
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
corresponds to a more
sharply concentrated (“pinched”) spectrum with suppressed high- and
low-energy tails, while smaller
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
corresponds to a broader,
more non-thermal distribution with enhanced tails.
Concretely,
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}=2
recovers a Maxwell–Boltzmann spectrum,

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\approx 2.3
corresponds to a Fermi–Dirac distribution
with zero chemical potential, and
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}>2.3
(“pinching”)
signals a spectrum narrower than Fermi–Dirac, as arises when the
neutrinosphere opacity sharpens the decoupling layer

(
Keil et al. 2003
;
Tamborra et al. 2012
)
. Values
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}<2
(“anti-pinching”)
indicate a distribution broader than Maxwell–Boltzmann, as can occur when a
hard non-thermal tail from late-time accretion contributes to the spectrum.
In core-collapse supernovae,
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
evolves from moderately
pinched values (
\approx 2.3
–
3.5
) during the accretion phase toward
lower values during Kelvin–Helmholtz cooling
(
Hüdepohl et al. 2010
;
Mirizzi et al. 2016
)
.

The physical importance of
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
is threefold.
First, the inverse-beta-decay event rate at water-Cherenkov detectors scales
as
\langle E^{2}\rangle\propto(\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}+2)/(\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}+1)
,
so a shift
\Delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\approx 0.3
alters predicted rates by

\sim\!4
–
6\%
—hundreds of events at Hyper-Kamiokande for a Galactic
supernova at
10\,\mathrm{kpc}
—directly affecting equation-of-state and
neutron-star-mass inference
(
Strumia & Vissani 2003
;
Li & Beacom 2023
)
.
Second, the boundaries of collective-oscillation spectral swaps and the
onset of fast-flavor instabilities are controlled by the spectral widths of

\nu_{e}
and
\bar{\nu}_{e}
, so
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
sets the post-oscillation
spectra reaching terrestrial detectors and governs the detectability of the
neutrino mass ordering
(
Duan et al. 2010
;
Dasgupta et al. 2010
;
Serpico et al. 2012
)
.
Third, charged-current captures in the neutrino-driven wind depend on the
high-energy spectral tail; spectral pinching suppresses this tail, reducing
the electron fraction
Y_{e}
and shifting conditions for
r
-process
nucleosynthesis
(
Qian & Woosley 1996
;
Hüdepohl et al. 2010
;
Roberts et al. 2012
)
.
The
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
floor derived in this work therefore provides a
simulation-motivated anchor for all three of these applications.

The
\sim\!24
events from SN 1987A remain the only direct measurement of
the supernova neutrino burst, and subsequent analyses have constrained

\langle E_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}\rangle
and
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
through fits to the
Kamiokande-II, IMB, and Baksan data
(
Raffelt 1996
;
Kachelriess et al. 2005
)
. Those
fits are degenerate between spectral mean and shape, and the adopted prior
on
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
materially affects the inferred mean energy and total
radiated energy. The 3D floor established here provides a simulation-motivated
prior for such analyses. For the next Galactic event, next-generation detectors
will accumulate
\mathcal{O}(10^{4})
events enabling per-bin spectral
measurements, so accurate spectral templates with realistic
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}

uncertainties will be essential for equation-of-state constraints and tests
of non-standard neutrino physics
(
Dasgupta et al. 2010
;
Li & Beacom 2023
)
.

Despite this importance, no systematic study of
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}(t,M)
across
a large, homogeneous ensemble of
3D
simulations has been performed. 1D
models
(
Hüdepohl et al. 2010
;
Fischer et al. 2010
;
Pons et al. 1999
)
predict a cooling-phase floor near

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\approx 2.0
–
2.3
. In 2D, convection inside the PNS
slightly broadens spectra
(
Marek & Janka 2009
;
Müller et al. 2012
)
. In 3D, additional
symmetry-breaking instabilities — the standing accretion shock instability

(
Tamborra et al. 2013
, SASI;)
and the lepton-number emission self-sustained
asymmetry
(
Tamborra et al. 2014a
;
O’Connor & Couch 2018
, LESA;)
— a persistent
hemispheric asymmetry in
\nu_{e}
/
\bar{\nu}_{e}
emission quantified by its
dipole amplitude
\varepsilon
(defined precisely in
Eq.
11
) — modulate spectral moments at the
\lesssim 10\%

level, but the secular time- and angle-averaged spectral evolution across a
wide progenitor mass range remains uncharacterized.

With 25 models spanning
8.1
to
100\,M_{\odot}
and simulation durations reaching
up to
8.47\,\mathrm{s}
post-bounce, the Princeton
Fornax
dataset

(
Coleman et al. 2022
;
Vartanyan et al. 2023
)
provides the homogeneous, long-duration 3D
ensemble needed to characterize the mass and time dependence of
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
.
Choi et al. 2025
analyzed luminosities,
mean energies, and multi-detector event rates from this dataset, but did not
study
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
. In the present work we analyze the 25 models for
which full angle-resolved output is available, spanning

8.1
–
100\,M_{\odot}
; all spectral quantities are derived directly from the
12-bin spectral data rather than from the angle-averaged moment files.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
describes the
simulations and data. Section
III
defines
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
and
the analysis procedure. Section
IV
presents the results.
Section
V
discusses observational prospects.
Section
VI
interprets results and places them in context.
Section
VII
presents our conclusions.

II
Simulations and Data

II.1
The Princeton
Fornax
Code

All simulations were performed with
Fornax
, a multidimensional
radiation-hydrodynamics code developed at Princeton University

(
Skinner et al. 2019
;
Radice et al. 2017
)
. The code solves the
two-moment
(M1)
neutrino transport equations coupled to compressible hydrodynamics on a
spherical-polar grid.

Fornax
solves the two-moment (M1) neutrino transport equations for three
species (
\nu_{e}
,
\bar{\nu}_{e}
,
\nu_{x}
) coupled to compressible hydrodynamics
on a spherical-polar grid. The system is closed by the
Minerbo 1978

maximum-entropy prescription, whose Eddington factor interpolates between the
diffusion (
\mathsf{f}=1/3
) and free-streaming (
\mathsf{f}=1
) limits.
The M1 scheme overestimates the tangential radiation pressure in the
semi-transparent regime relative to full Boltzmann transport
(
Just et al. 2015
)
,
but reproduces angle-averaged spectral moments to
\lesssim 5\%
accuracy
compared to variable-Eddington-factor codes during the accretion phase

(
Nagakura et al. 2021
)
.

Gravity employs the pseudo-Newtonian effective potential of
Marek et al. 2006
,
which reproduces the GR deepening of the gravitational well critical for accurate
PNS cooling
(
Marek & Janka 2009
;
Burrows et al. 2019
)
; the approximation becomes inaccurate
only in the final
\lesssim 10\,\mathrm{ms}
before BH formation, well before
the spectral features we characterize (Section
IV.4
). Interaction
rates include charged-current absorption and emission for
\nu_{e}
and

\bar{\nu}_{e}
; neutral-current scattering off nucleons, nuclei, and electrons;
thermal pair processes; and nucleon–nucleon bremsstrahlung

(
Bruenn 1985
;
Hannestad & Raffelt 1998
;
Burrows et al. 2006
)
. The nuclear EOS is SFHo

(
Steiner et al. 2013
)
(
n_{0}=0.1595\,\mathrm{fm^{-3}}
,
K=245\,\mathrm{MeV}
,

R_{1.4}=11.9\,\mathrm{km}
,
M_{\mathrm{max}}\approx 2.05\,M_{\odot}
).
Full algorithmic details are given in
Skinner et al. 2019
.

\xi_{2.5}=0.458

discuss additional high-mass models (e.g.,
40

and

12.25
and

reach BH

without achieving a sustained explosion.

Progenitors were drawn from
Sukhbold et al. 2016
(solar metallicity,

9
–
100\,M_{\odot}
) and
Woosley & Heger 2015
(low-mass models
8.1
and

9.6\,M_{\odot}
).
Simulation durations range from

(

to
8.47\,\mathrm{s}
(
18\,M_{\odot}
).

II.3
Data Products

For each model and species
s\in\{0:\nu_{e},\;1:\bar{\nu}_{e},\;2:\nu_{x}\}
, the
spectral data files
(
Vartanyan et al. 2023
)
provide time series of:
the post-bounce time
t
[s]; the angle-averaged luminosity

L_{s}
[
\times 10^{51}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}
]; the mean energy

\langle E_{s}\rangle
[MeV]; the RMS energy

E_{\mathrm{rms},s}=\sqrt{\langle E_{s}^{2}\rangle}
[MeV]; and a skewness
energy
E_{\mathrm{skw},s}
[MeV]. The
\nu_{x}
entries represent the sum
over all four heavy-lepton flavors (
\nu_{\mu}
,
\nu_{\tau}
and their
antiparticles). The median temporal resolution is
\sim 1\,\mathrm{ms}
.
In this work,
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
is computed directly from the angle-averaged
12-bin spectral moments (Eq.
4
), with no
quasi-thermal approximation. Appendix Table
2
demonstrates this
for representative models (
11
,
17
,
18.5\,M_{\odot}
): the moment-based value
is exact (0% difference) since both use the same spectral bins.

The pinching parameter computed via Eq. (
4
) is an

effective
spectral-shape measure derived from only the first two energy
moments (
\langle E\rangle
and
E_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}\equiv\langle E^{2}\rangle
).
It is
not
a best-fit parameter to the full binned spectrum.
Specifically,
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}^{\mathrm{mom}}
would reproduce the true
best-fit
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
exactly if and only if the spectrum belongs
strictly to the quasi-thermal family (Eq.
3
).
For real neutrino spectra with non-thermal contributions
(e.g., hard-tail accretion luminosity), the two can differ; in particular,
anti-pinching (
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}<2
) in the BH-forming models manifests
as a genuine increase in
E_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}/\langle E\rangle^{2}
regardless
of whether the underlying distribution is strictly quasi-thermal.
M1 spectral moments agree with variable-Eddington-factor moments at
the
\lesssim 5
–
10\%
level during the accretion phase

(
Tamborra et al. 2013
)
; systematic differences in the cooling phase remain
to be quantified.

III
Methods

III.1
The Quasi-Thermal Spectral Parametrization

We employ the quasi-thermal family introduced by
Keil et al. 2003
and widely
used in detection and oscillation studies
(
Tamborra et al. 2012
;
Serpico et al. 2012
;
Li & Beacom 2023
)
:

f(E;\,\langle E\rangle,\,\alpha_{\mathrm{p}})=\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{E}{\langle E\rangle}\right)^{\!\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}+1)\,E}{\langle E\rangle}\right],

(3)

where
\mathcal{N}
is fixed by
\int_{0}^{\infty}f(E)\,\mathrm{d}E=1
.

The
n=2
moment of distribution (
3
) gives

\langle E^{2}\rangle=[(\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}+2)/(\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}+1)]\langle E\rangle^{2}
.
Defining
E_{\mathrm{rms}}\equiv\sqrt{\langle E^{2}\rangle}
(used interchangeably
with
E_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}\equiv\langle E^{2}\rangle
throughout), inverting yields:

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}=\frac{2\langle E\rangle^{2}-E_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}}{E_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}-\langle E\rangle^{2}},

(4)

valid for any distribution with
E_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}>\langle E\rangle^{2}
.
The normalized variance
\sigma_{E}^{2}\equiv(\langle E^{2}\rangle-\langle E\rangle^{2})/\langle E\rangle^{2}=1/(\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}+1)

(
Keil et al. 2003
)
; Appendix Table
3
lists reference values.

The physical interpretation of
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
is the following.
A value
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}=2
corresponds to a Maxwell–Boltzmann spectrum;
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\approx 2.3
to a Fermi–Dirac distribution with zero chemical potential; and
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}>2.3
(“pinching”) to spectra narrower than Fermi–Dirac, as expected when neutrinos decouple from a hot, optically thick neutrinosphere where thermalization is efficient.
Values
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}<2
(“anti-pinching”) correspond to distributions broader than Maxwell–Boltzmann, indicative of a hard non-thermal tail from accretion or of a flattened temperature gradient at the decoupling surface.
In core-collapse supernovae,
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
is important because it governs the cross-section-weighted event rate at terrestrial detectors (through the
\sigma\propto E^{2}
weighting), sets the effective spectral input for flavor-transformation calculations, and encodes the thermodynamic state of the PNS interior: the degree of spectral pinching is set by the temperature gradient at the neutrinosphere, making
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
a sensitive probe of PNS cooling physics
(
Pons et al. 1999
;
Keil et al. 2003
)
.

III.2
Quality Cuts and Smoothing

Raw
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}(t)
traces exhibit short-timescale (
\lesssim 10\,\mathrm{ms}
) fluctuations driven by convective turnover inside the PNS
(overturn timescale
\tau_{\mathrm{conv}}\sim 20\,\mathrm{ms}
) and by
numerical noise at the level of
|\delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}|\lesssim 0.1
.
We apply a
25\,\mathrm{ms}
boxcar rolling mean, which preserves features
on timescales
\gtrsim 50\,\mathrm{ms}
while suppressing shot noise.
The window corresponds to approximately twice the SASI turnover timescale
(
\tau_{\mathrm{SASI}}\sim 10
–
20\,\mathrm{ms}
at early times, rising to

\sim 50\,\mathrm{ms}
during the cooling phase;
Tamborra et al. 2013
),
ensuring that random-phase SASI fluctuations are averaged over without
erasing the secular spectral trends we characterize. We discard samples where

E_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}-\langle E\rangle^{2}<0.01\,\mathrm{MeV}^{2}
or

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}<-0.5
(less than
0.4\%
of all time steps).
These cuts are triggered almost exclusively during the neutronization burst
(
t\lesssim 10\,\mathrm{ms}
, where the
\nu_{e}
spectral variance temporarily
collapses as the burst narrows the spectrum) and at the final time step of the
two BH-forming models immediately before the luminosity cutoff. No cut is
triggered at any epoch relevant to the pre-BH anti-pinching trajectory
(
t=0.3
–
2.7\,\mathrm{s}
), confirming that the quality filter does not
affect the BH spectral evolution (Section
IV.4
).

Smoothing convention.
Unless stated otherwise, all scalar time-series quantities quoted in this
paper (spectral survey curves, Spearman correlations, floor estimate,
Spearman
r_{S}
values, and
f_{+}
) are computed on the smoothed series.
The late-time floor value (Section
IV.2
) is computed as the
mean of per-model time-averages over
t=3.0\,\mathrm{s}
–
t_{\mathrm{max}}

after smoothing, restricted to the
N=13
models with

t_{\mathrm{max}}>3.5\,\mathrm{s}
; the Spearman coefficients use single smoothed values at the
target epoch; and
f_{+}
uses a 250 ms boxcar applied after the primary
25 ms smoothing. The viewing-angle percentile bands in
Section
IV.6.2
are computed directly from the unsmoothed
sky map at a single snapshot epoch.

III.3
Progenitor-Mass Interpolation and Total Energy

To connect individual model values at a fixed epoch into a continuous function
of progenitor mass, we fit a degree-
k
B-spline (
k=\min(2,N-1)
) on the

\log_{10}(M/M_{\odot})
axis
(
Dierckx 1993
)
; the 9a/9b duplicate is averaged
before spline construction. The total radiated energy per species is

E_{s}=\int_{0}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}}L_{s}\,\mathrm{d}t
(trapezoidal rule, native

\sim\!1\,\mathrm{ms}
grid), and
E_{\mathrm{tot}}=E_{\nu_{e}}+E_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}+E_{\nu_{x}}

with
E_{\nu_{x}}
summing all four heavy-lepton flavors.

IV.3
Neutrino Energy Hierarchy and Late-Time Reversal

The standard energy hierarchy
\langle E_{\nu_{x}}\rangle>\langle E_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}\rangle>\langle E_{\nu_{e}}\rangle
is set by the opacity-dependent decoupling
radii. For free-streaming neutrinos above the neutrinosphere at radius

r_{\nu}^{(s)}
, the mean energy scales as
\langle E_{s}\rangle\approx 3.15\,T(r_{\nu}^{(s)})
(Fermi–Dirac approximation), where
T(r)
is the
matter temperature at the decoupling surface. Since charged-current opacity
for
\nu_{e}
and
\bar{\nu}_{e}
scales as
\sigma\propto E_{\nu}^{2}\,Y_{e}\,\rho

while neutral-current opacity
\propto E_{\nu}^{2}\,\rho
,
\nu_{x}
decouple
at deeper, hotter radii during the accretion phase, giving

\langle E_{\nu_{x}}\rangle>\langle E_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}\rangle>\langle E_{\nu_{e}}\rangle

(
Raffelt 2012
;
Ott et al. 2008
;
Bruenn et al. 2016
)
.

The mean energy evolution and hierarchy difference are shown in
Fig.
4
for models running past
5\,\mathrm{s}
.
Throughout this section,
\langle E_{\nu_{x}}\rangle
denotes the

per-flavor
mean energy. In the
Fornax
spectral data files, the

\nu_{x}
luminosity
L_{\nu_{x}}
is the
sum
over all four heavy-lepton
flavors, but the mean energy
\langle E_{\nu_{x}}\rangle
is the
energy-weighted spectral mean, which equals the per-flavor value by symmetry
(all four heavy-lepton species carry identical spectra). Thus comparing

\langle E_{\nu_{e}}\rangle
with
\langle E_{\nu_{x}}\rangle
directly —
without a factor of four — gives the physically meaningful hierarchy comparison.

IV.4.1
Pre-Collapse Anti-Pinching Mechanism

A natural candidate mechanism for the anti-pinching observed in BH-forming
models is the growing accretion luminosity component. As the stalled shock
retreats, the ram pressure of infalling material would force the neutrinosphere
to larger radii at lower optical depth. In this regime, the emerging spectrum
could be a superposition of a thermal core component (contributing to the peak)
and a hard non-thermal tail from downscattering, giving a broader-than-thermal
distribution with
\sigma_{E}^{2}>1/3
, i.e.,
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}<2
.
Whether this mechanism is the dominant cause, or whether spectral transport
approximations in the
Fornax
code contribute, requires confirmation with
independent neutrino transport schemes
(
Mirizzi et al. 2016
)
.

Quantitatively, the accretion contribution to the total luminosity fraction

\epsilon_{\mathrm{acc}}\equiv\frac{L_{\mathrm{acc}}}{L_{\mathrm{tot}}}\approx\frac{G\,M_{\mathrm{PNS}}\,\dot{M}}{R_{\mathrm{PNS}}\,L_{\mathrm{tot}}}

(8)

grows as the accretion rate
\dot{M}
increases (for fixed
R_{\mathrm{PNS}}

and
M_{\mathrm{PNS}}
). In the
14\,M_{\odot}
model,
\epsilon_{\mathrm{acc}}

rises from
\sim 0.3
at
t=0.5\,\mathrm{s}
to
\gtrsim 0.6
at

t=1.0\,\mathrm{s}
, consistent with the observed drop in
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
.

The anti-pinching deficit at

t=0.5\,\mathrm{s}
is
\Delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\approx 0.66
for the

12.25\,M_{\odot}
model (
\xi_{2.5}=0.458
; angle-averaged
\bar{\nu}_{e}
:

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}=1.77
vs
\approx 2.43
for the
11\,M_{\odot}
successful
model) and
\Delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\approx 0.66
for the
14\,M_{\odot}
model
(
\xi_{2.5}=0.528
;
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}=1.73
). Both BH-forming models
therefore show an essentially identical deficit at this early epoch despite
their differing compactness values (
\Delta\xi_{2.5}=0.07
), suggesting
that the
onset
of anti-pinching near
t\approx 0.3
–
0.5\,\mathrm{s}

is not strongly differentiated by compactness within the narrow range probed
here. The two models do differ in their subsequent evolution: the
14\,M_{\odot}

model (
\xi_{2.5}=0.528
) reaches
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\lesssim 0.9
earlier
and shows a larger total deficit (

to collapse) than the
12.25\,M_{\odot}
model
(
\Delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}^{\rm tot}=0.70
), consistent with the expectation
that higher compactness drives faster mass accretion and a more rapid spectral
evolution. A broader sample of BH-forming models spanning a wider compactness
range is needed to establish a quantitative
\xi_{2.5}
–onset relationship.

IV.4.2
Critical Luminosity and BH Formation

The critical condition for shock revival in the neutrino-driven mechanism can
be written as
(
Burrows 2013
;
Müller et al. 2012
)

L_{\nu_{e}}\langle E_{\nu_{e}}\rangle^{2}+L_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}\langle E_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}\rangle^{2}>C(M_{\mathrm{PNS}},\,\dot{M},\,R_{\mathrm{PNS}}),

(9)

where
C
is a function of the mass accretion rate and PNS properties. Failed
models remain below this threshold until
\dot{M}
drives the PNS mass past the
maximum stable mass of the SFHo EOS. The spectral pinching enters through the cross-section-weighted second moment.
For a spectrum with fixed
\langle E\rangle
, a lower
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}

(broader spectrum)
increases

\langle E^{2}\rangle
and hence

\langle\sigma E^{2}\rangle\propto\langle E^{3}\rangle/\langle E\rangle

(since
\sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}}\propto E^{2}
). However, the relevant quantity
for the critical luminosity condition is
L\langle E\rangle^{2}
, not

\langle\sigma E^{2}\rangle
; at fixed total luminosity, a broader spectrum
concentrates energy in the tail at the expense of the peak, reducing the

number
flux at the gain radius. In the accretion-dominated regime where

L
is set by
\dot{M}
rather than diffusion, lower
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
is
associated with a softer effective spectral peak and a
lower
net
heating rate per unit luminosity
(
Müller et al. 2012
;
Burrows 2013
)
.
This creates a positive feedback: growing
\dot{M}
drives down

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
, which reduces the heating efficiency per unit
luminosity, which allows
\dot{M}
to grow further.

IV.6.2
Viewing-Angle Spread of
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}

Although
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
is not directly detected, it can be inferred
from the energy distribution of detected events at terrestrial neutrino
observatories (Section
V.4
); each direction on the sky
yields a different effective spectrum, and hence a different inferred

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
.

Because the spectral pinching parameter
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
is derived
from number-weighted energy moments, it depends on the local spectrum

L(E,\hat{n})
seen by each distant observer. For each angular pixel

\hat{n}
we compute

\langle E\rangle(\hat{n})=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i}L_{i}(\hat{n})\,\Delta E_{i}}{\displaystyle\sum_{i}\bigl[L_{i}(\hat{n})\,\Delta E_{i}/E_{i}\bigr]},\quad\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}(\hat{n})=\frac{2\langle E\rangle^{2}-E_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}}{E_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}-\langle E\rangle^{2}},

(12)

where the sum runs over the 12 energy bins.

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}(\hat{n})

In contrast, the successful

We quantify the contributions of the dipole (
\ell=1
) and quadrupole
(
\ell=2
) to
\sigma_{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}^{\rm geom}
using the full

128\times 256
sky maps for all 25 models. The LESA dipole amplitude

\varepsilon
directly measures the
\ell=1
fractional asymmetry in
lepton-number emission. To quantify the
\ell=2
contribution — driven
primarily by SASI spiral modes and convective overturn — we compute the
normalized quadrupole amplitude
Q_{2}
of the
\bar{\nu}_{e}
luminosity sky map.

Q_{2}
measures how much of the total sky variation is organized into a
quadrupolar (
\ell=2
) pattern; unlike the LESA dipole, which is tied to the

\nu_{e}/\bar{\nu}_{e}
lepton flux asymmetry,
Q_{2}
captures the contribution of
SASI-driven and convective asymmetries to the total spectral spread seen by
distant observers. Knowing both
\varepsilon
and
Q_{2}
allows us to
estimate what fraction of
\sigma_{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}^{\rm geom}
is
attributable to large-scale modes versus small-scale turbulent structure.
Explicitly:

Q_{2}\equiv\left[\sum_{m=-2}^{2}|a_{2m}|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\!\!\Big/\,\sqrt{4\pi}\,\bar{L},

(14)

where
a_{2m}=\int[L(\hat{n})-\bar{L}]\,Y_{2m}(\hat{n})\,d\Omega
,

\bar{L}
is the sky-averaged
\bar{\nu}_{e}
luminosity, and the
Y_{2m}

are real spherical harmonics.

\varepsilon(t)
and
Q_{2}(t)
for all 25 models is shown overlaid in Fig.

\approx 0.04
–
0.43

near

where SASI activity is strongest, and is

\varepsilon
in the
same models.

throughout
its evolution — suppressed by a factor
\sim 3
–
5
relative to successful
models at the same epoch, mirroring the LESA suppression. The
14\,M_{\odot}

model reaches
Q_{2}\lesssim 0.21
, moderately suppressed relative to the
upper end of the successful-model distribution but less extreme than the

12.25\,M_{\odot}
case; its somewhat higher
Q_{2}
reflects persistent SASI
activity even as the accretion rate grows.

Using the empirical sensitivity

\partial\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}/\partial(\delta L/\bar{L})\approx 1.5

(estimated from the per-pixel
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
scatter), the quadrupole
contribution to the
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
sky variance is

\delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}^{(\ell=2)}\approx 1.5\,Q_{2}\approx 0.06
–

,

\sim 15
–

of the total

\sigma_{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}^{\rm geom}\approx 0.4
–
0.75
at any epoch.
The
\ell=1

\delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}^{(\ell=1)}\approx 1.5\,\varepsilon\lesssim 0.3
–
0.5
(using
\varepsilon\lesssim 0.23
).
Since
\delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}^{(\ell=1)}+\delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}^{(\ell=2)}

accounts for most but not all of the observed sky variance, small-scale
convective turbulence (
\ell\geq 3
) must also contribute. The viewing-angle
systematic therefore does not rest on the LESA dipole alone; both
\ell=1
and

\ell=2
modes independently drive the variance above the statistical floor, as
shown in Fig.
7
(a). A complete
a_{\ell m}
power spectrum is
deferred to a dedicated follow-up analysis.

The LESA dipole direction defines the axis of maximum
\nu_{e}/\bar{\nu}_{e}

contrast; an observer along this axis experiences the largest departure from
the angle-averaged spectrum, making the LESA orientation a key prior for
spectral inversion.

The geometric uncertainty
\sigma_{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}^{\rm geom}\approx 0.4
–
0.75

exceeds the statistical precision
\sigma_{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}^{\rm stat}\approx 0.12
(Eq.
18
,
N_{\rm eff}\sim 1000
) by
a factor of
3
–
6
, so the geometric term dominates the total
uncertainty budget for any single-direction measurement. A meaningful
spectral inversion therefore requires either multi-detector triangulation
or a prior on the LESA orientation. The BH-forming models (12.25 and

14\,M_{\odot}
) are again outliers: their narrow, low-
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}

distributions (
\alpha_{\mathrm{p,med}}\approx 1.1
,

\Delta\alpha_{68}<0.3
) reflect the nearly isotropic, pinched spectra
of the accretion-dominated phase at which they terminate
(Section
IV.4.1
).

V.1
Detector Overview

We consider four detectors:
Hyper-Kamiokande (Hyper-K,
374\,\mathrm{kt}
water Cherenkov,

\bar{\nu}_{e}
via IBD;
Hyper-Kamiokande Collaboration 2018
);
DUNE (
40\,\mathrm{kt}
liquid argon,
\nu_{e}
via CC-Ar;

DUNE Collaboration 2020
;
Capozzi et al. 2020
);
JUNO (
20\,\mathrm{kt}
liquid scintillator,
\bar{\nu}_{e}
via IBD;

JUNO Collaboration 2016
); and
IceCube (
\sim\!650\,\mathrm{Mt}
effective water equivalent,

Abbasi et al. 2011
): the
\sim\!5160
deployed optical modules detect
supernova neutrinos collectively as a rate increase above the dark-noise
baseline. IceCube cannot reconstruct individual event energies but provides
very high total statistics (
\sim\!10^{6}
events for a Galactic supernova at

10\,\mathrm{kpc}
), enabling high-time-resolution luminosity monitoring.
We include a brief estimate of IceCube’s
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
sensitivity in
Section
V.4
.

V.2
Cross Sections

For IBD at Hyper-K and JUNO, we use the
Strumia & Vissani 2003
cross section:

\sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}}(E)=9.52\times 10^{-44}\left(\frac{E_{e}}{1\,\mathrm{MeV}}\right)^{\!2}\,\mathrm{cm}^{2},

(15)

where
E_{e}=E_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}-\Delta_{np}\approx E_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}-1.806\,\mathrm{MeV}
is the positron kinetic energy. We apply a detector energy threshold
E_{\mathrm{th}}=5\,\mathrm{MeV}

(neutrino energy). The 5 MeV threshold corresponds to the practical
detection threshold of both detectors: below this energy, backgrounds from
reactor antineutrinos, spallation products, and solar neutrinos overwhelm
the supernova signal at both Hyper-K and JUNO
(
Hyper-Kamiokande Collaboration 2018
;
JUNO Collaboration 2016
)
. Radiative corrections at the

\sim 0.3\%
level are included in the prefactor. The total cross section
scales as
\sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}}\propto E^{2}
above threshold, making
Hyper-K and JUNO strongly energy-dependent probes of the spectral shape.

For DUNE, the
\nu_{e}+{}^{40}\mathrm{Ar}\to e^{-}+{}^{40}\mathrm{K}^{*}

cross section is approximated by
(
Capozzi et al. 2020
)
:

\sigma_{\mathrm{CC}}(E)\approx 3.0\times 10^{-42}\left(\frac{E-5.0\,\mathrm{MeV}}{20\,\mathrm{MeV}}\right)^{\!2.5}\mathrm{cm}^{2},\quad E>5\,\mathrm{MeV}.

(16)

The steeper energy dependence (
\propto E^{2.5}
) compared to IBD reflects
the nuclear structure of argon and makes DUNE a sensitive probe of the
high-energy tail. We caution that the
\nu_{e}
–Ar CC cross section carries
a factor-of-two theoretical uncertainty at supernova energies

(
Kolbe et al. 2003
;
Suzuki et al. 2018
;
Nikolakopoulos et al. 2022
)
; the DUNE event rates reported
here should be regarded as order-of-magnitude estimates pending improved
nuclear-structure calculations or direct cross-section measurements. This
\times 0.5
–
\times 2

uncertainty applies
uniformly
to all models, and thus does not affect
the relative comparisons between mass-ordering scenarios or progenitor models
presented in this work.

The
E_{\mathrm{th}}=5\,\mathrm{MeV}
threshold removes
\sim\!5
–
15\%

of the number flux for the cooling-phase spectra
(
\langle E_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}\rangle\approx 8
–
12\,\mathrm{MeV}
), biasing the
reconstructed
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
upward by
\delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\lesssim 0.15
(Eq.
4
). Since all rate comparisons
use the same threshold, this bias cancels in ratios; it should be corrected
in detailed spectral inversion analyses.

V.3
Event Rate and Total Event Count

The event rate is
\dot{N}(t)=N_{\mathrm{target}}\int_{E_{\mathrm{th}}}^{\infty}\Phi_{s}(E,t)\sigma(E)\,dE
,
where the number flux
\Phi_{s}=L_{s}f(E)/(4\pi d^{2}\langle E_{s}\rangle)
uses the normalized
quasi-thermal spectrum (Eq.
3
). We use the quasi-monochromatic
approximation
(
Kachelriess et al. 2005
)
corrected by the analytic spectral-shape factor

(\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}+3)(\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}+2)/(\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}+1)^{2}
(unity at

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}=2
,
\pm 15\%
across
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\in[1.0,3.5]
);
residual approximation error
\lesssim 1.5\%
.
For
d=10\,\textrm{kpc}
, the peak Hyper-K rate is

\sim 5\times 10^{3}
–
2\times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
during accretion,
declining to
\sim 100
–
500\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
during cooling.

The smoothed rates for all 25 models at all three detectors, colored by progenitor mass, are shown in Fig.

V.4
Measuring
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
from Event Distributions

For IceCube, the lack of individual event energy resolution means that

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
cannot be measured from spectral moments, but the overall
rate can bound the total
\bar{\nu}_{e}
luminosity and provide complementary
timing information on the BH-formation cutoff
(
Dighe & Smirnov 2000
;
Abbasi et al. 2011
)
.

Given
N
detected events in a time bin
\Delta t
,
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
can be
reconstructed from the first two moments of the detected energy distribution,
accounting for the
\sigma\propto E^{2}
cross-section weighting
(
Serpico et al. 2012
)
.
From
N
detected events with energies
\{E_{i}^{\mathrm{det}}\}
reconstructed
in a time window
\Delta t
, the two-moment estimator of
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
is

\hat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}=\frac{2\langle E^{\mathrm{det}}\rangle^{2}-\langle(E^{\mathrm{det}})^{2}\rangle}{\langle(E^{\mathrm{det}})^{2}\rangle-\langle E^{\mathrm{det}}\rangle^{2}},

(17)

where
\langle E^{\mathrm{det}}\rangle=N^{-1}\sum_{i}E_{i}^{\mathrm{det}}
and

\langle(E^{\mathrm{det}})^{2}\rangle=N^{-1}\sum_{i}(E_{i}^{\mathrm{det}})^{2}
are
the sample moments. For IBD, the detected energy
E^{\mathrm{det}}\approx E_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}-1.3\,\mathrm{MeV}
(positron kinetic energy) and the
cross-section weighting
\sigma\propto E^{2}
biases
\langle E^{\mathrm{det}}\rangle
upward relative to the source
\langle E\rangle
; this bias must be
unfolded to recover
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
of the source
(
Serpico et al. 2012
)
.
The precision from a two-moment analysis is

(
Minakata et al. 2008
;
Serpico et al. 2012
)
:

\sigma_{{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}}\approx\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}+2}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}},

(18)

where
N_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 0.7\,N
for realistic detector response and
energy-bin thresholds
(
Li & Beacom 2023
)
. This formula applies to the ideal case
where
\langle E\rangle
is known
a priori
. In practice,
\langle E\rangle

must also be estimated from the same event sample, which inflates

\sigma_{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}
by a factor
\sim 1.3
from the Fisher-matrix
estimate of
Serpico et al. 2012
at
N_{\mathrm{eff}}\sim 1000
; the working
precision is therefore
\sigma_{{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}}\approx 0.12\times 1.3\approx 0.15
. Additional contributions from energy-scale uncertainty
(
\delta E/E\approx 2
–
3\%
) contribute
\delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\lesssim 0.15
.
For Hyper-K with
N_{\mathrm{eff}}\sim 1000
events in a
1\,\mathrm{s}

cooling window at
10\,\textrm{kpc}
, the effective
\sigma_{{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}}\approx 0.15
, still sufficient to resolve the floor (Eq.
7
)
from any 1D model prediction at
>2\sigma
.

The expected significance for detecting a spectral change

|\Delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}|
between two time windows is

\mathcal{S}=\frac{|\Delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}|}{\sigma_{{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}},1}}\oplus\sigma_{{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}},2}}}=\frac{|\Delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}|\sqrt{N_{1}N_{2}/(N_{1}+N_{2})}}{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}+2},

(19)

where
N_{1,2}
are the event counts in the two windows and
\oplus
denotes
quadrature addition. For the BH-forming signature (
|\Delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}|\approx 1.3
, comparing the last
0.3\,\mathrm{s}
before collapse to the
preceding
1\,\mathrm{s}
), this yields
\mathcal{S}\approx 4.2\sigma
at
Hyper-K for
d=10\,\textrm{kpc}
.

The dominant systematic is energy-scale uncertainty (
\delta E/E\sim 2
–
3\%
at Hyper-K;
Hyper-Kamiokande Collaboration 2018
),
which biases
\delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\lesssim 0.15
, comparable to the floor
scatter. Backgrounds (
\lesssim 1\,\mathrm{event\,ms^{-1}}
) and detector
deadtime (
\lesssim 1\%
) are negligible against the SN signal.

V.5
Oscillation-Corrected Detection Rates

The detection rates discussed in Section
V.3
and
Figure
10
use the unoscillated source spectra. In practice,
MSW flavor transformation in the stellar envelope modifies the
\bar{\nu}_{e}

flux reaching the detector. In the adiabatic approximation

(
Wolfenstein 1978
;
Mikheyev & Smirnov 1986
)
, the observed
\bar{\nu}_{e}
number flux at Earth is

\Phi_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}^{\oplus}=\bar{p}\,\Phi_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}^{0}+(1-\bar{p})\,\Phi_{\nu_{x}}^{0},

(20)

where
\bar{p}
is the
\bar{\nu}_{e}
survival probability and

\Phi_{\nu_{x}}^{0}=L_{\nu_{x}}/(4\times 4\pi d^{2}\langle E_{\nu_{x}}\rangle)

is the single-flavor heavy-lepton number flux. We consider two mass-ordering scenarios.
In the Normal Mass Ordering (NMO),
\bar{p}=0
: the

\bar{\nu}_{e}
flux undergoes complete adiabatic H-resonance conversion
and is fully replaced by
\nu_{x}
.
In the Inverted Mass Ordering (IMO),
\bar{p}=\cos^{2}\theta_{12}\approx 0.693
(partial conversion; PDG 2024 value

\sin^{2}\theta_{12}=0.307
;
Particle Data Group 2024
).

The resulting Hyper-K IBD event rates for both orderings, along with their ratio, for all 25 models are shown in Fig.

.

scatter
\sigma_{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}=0.12
(Eq.
7
) applies
to the unoscillated source spectra; the Earth-arriving mixture has an
effective
\bar{p}
-weighted
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
that is model- and
ordering-dependent.

Because the
\nu_{x}
spectrum is harder than
\bar{\nu}_{e}
during the accretion
phase (
\langle E_{\nu_{x}}\rangle>\langle E_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}\rangle
), the
NMO rate — dominated by
\Phi_{\nu_{x}}^{0}
— exceeds the IMO rate. During
late-time cooling, the hierarchy reversal
(Section
IV.3
) causes the energy advantage of
\nu_{x}
to
diminish, compressing the NMO/IMO ratio toward unity. This interplay between
spectral pinching evolution and flavor transformation makes
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}(t)

a key input for oscillation-sensitive analyses at Hyper-K.

V.6
Oscillation and Detector Assumptions

For clarity we state the key assumptions and their limitations:

MSW (adiabatic, no collective oscillations).
Equation (
20
) assumes fully adiabatic MSW conversion in the
H-resonance and L-resonance layers of the stellar envelope. This is
expected to be an excellent approximation for typical stellar density profiles
and the neutrino energies and luminosities relevant here

(
Wolfenstein 1978
;
Mikheyev & Smirnov 1986
;
Dighe & Smirnov 2000
)
.
Collective

\nu
–
\nu

forward-scattering oscillations
(
Duan et al. 2010
;
Mirizzi et al. 2016
)
are

not
included. Their effect is uncertain and potentially large
during the early accretion phase (
t\lesssim 0.5\,\mathrm{s}
, where
the net lepton-number flux is high), but suppressed or negligible during the
Kelvin–Helmholtz cooling phase (
t\gtrsim 1\,\mathrm{s}
) where the

\nu_{e}
–
\bar{\nu}_{e}
asymmetry is small
(
Zaizen & Nagakura 2023
)
.
We additionally note that
fast-flavour instabilities
(FFI), driven by
angular crossings in the neutrino distribution function rather than the net
lepton flux, are not modelled here. FFI can persist at residual levels even
when the bulk
\nu_{e}
–
\bar{\nu}_{e}
asymmetry is small

(
Fiorillo et al. 2025
)
, and their impact on the late-time pinching spectrum
is an open question; the quoted 8–12% NMO/IMO rate asymmetry should be
regarded as subject to this uncertainty.
The results of Section
V.5
focus on the cooling phase,
so the neglect of synchronized collective oscillations is unlikely to affect
the bulk NMO/IMO discrimination, but FFI effects remain unquantified.

Practical discriminability.
The 8–12% NMO/IMO asymmetry in IBD rate is the dominant spectral
discriminant in the cooling phase. However, the viewing-angle dispersion in

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
(Section
IV.6.2
,

\sigma_{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}^{\rm geom}\approx 0.4
–
0.75
) means that
for an unknown source direction, the effective
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
entering
the MSW rate formula (Eq.
22
) is uncertain at the

\Delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\sim 0.5
level, suppressing the apparent rate
asymmetry. Multi-detector triangulation
(
Brdar et al. 2018
)
or an independent
determination of the LESA dipole direction would reduce this systematic.
The DUNE cross-section factor-of-two uncertainty is orthogonal to the
NMO/IMO comparison (it affects DUNE absolute rates, not the NMO/IMO ratio),
but limits the combined DUNE+Hyper-K spectral analysis.

VI
Discussion

VI.1
Implications for Neutrino Flavor Transformation

The spectral shape determines the post-oscillation observable spectrum. In the
MSW
(
Wolfenstein 1978
;
Mikheyev & Smirnov 1986
)
adiabatic approximation for the normal mass
ordering (NMO), the
\bar{\nu}_{e}
survival probability at the H-resonance is

\bar{p}\approx 0
(complete conversion), so the detected
\bar{\nu}_{e}
at Earth
is

\phi_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}^{\oplus}(E)=\bar{p}\,\phi_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}^{0}(E)+(1-\bar{p})\,\phi_{\nu_{x}}^{0}(E)\xrightarrow{\bar{p}=0}\phi_{\nu_{x}}^{0}(E),

(21)

while in the inverted mass ordering (IMO),
\bar{p}\approx\cos^{2}\theta_{12}\approx 0.68
. The observable event rate difference between NMO and IMO is

\frac{\dot{N}_{\mathrm{NMO}}-\dot{N}_{\mathrm{IMO}}}{\dot{N}_{\mathrm{IMO}}}\approx\frac{\langle\sigma E^{2}\rangle_{\nu_{x}}-\langle\sigma E^{2}\rangle_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}}{\langle\sigma E^{2}\rangle_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}}\,\cos^{2}\theta_{12},

(22)

which depends directly on the spectral shapes through the cross-section-weighted
second moments. For
\Delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}(\bar{\nu}_{e},\nu_{x})\approx 0.4

during accretion (Fig.
3
), the mass-ordering asymmetry
is
\sim 8\text{--}12\%
in IBD rates at Hyper-K, potentially detectable at

\sim 2
–
3\sigma
for a galactic event
(
Dighe & Smirnov 2000
;
Serpico et al. 2012
)
.

Collective neutrino oscillations
(
Duan et al. 2010
;
Mirizzi et al. 2016
)
depend on the
flavor-flux asymmetry
\Phi_{\nu_{e}}-\Phi_{\bar{\nu}_{e}}
, which is modulated by

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
at a level comparable to the species mean-energy difference.
Our
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}(t)
maps supply the spectral inputs for
time-resolved collective-oscillation calculations.

VI.2
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
as a PNS Thermometer

In the 1D diffusion limit,
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
is related to the logarithmic
temperature gradient at the neutrinosphere: a steeper gradient (hotter, more
compact interior) gives
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}>2

(
Pons et al. 1999
;
Keil et al. 2003
)
, while a
In 3D, convective plumes and angle-dependent fluctuations break the spherical
symmetry of the 1D formula, so the mapping from
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
to PNS
temperature is qualitative only; quantitative inference awaits models with
full Boltzmann transport and comprehensive reaction rates
(
Mirizzi et al. 2016
)
.

VII
Conclusions

We have performed a systematic analysis of
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}(t)
across 25
3D CCSN simulations from the Princeton
Fornax
code
(
8.1
–
100\,M_{\odot}
, up to
8.47\,\mathrm{s}
post-bounce).

The
\bar{\nu}_{e}
pinching converges to a late-time floor of

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}^{\bar{\nu}_{e}}=1.92\pm 0.10
for
t_{\mathrm{pb}}>3\,\mathrm{s}
.
The floor is measured from the
N=13
models with
t_{\mathrm{max}}>3.5\,\mathrm{s}
,
the subset that has evolved far enough into the Kelvin–Helmholtz cooling phase
for
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
to have converged; the remaining 10 successful models
terminate too early to sample the secular cooling floor.

Both BH-forming models (
12.25
,
14\,M_{\odot}
,
\xi_{2.5}>0.45
) show
anti-pinching (
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\lesssim 0.9
) in the
\sim 0.3\,\mathrm{s}

before collapse, with a deficit
\Delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\approx 0.65
already
present at
t=0.5\,\mathrm{s}
. This early anti-pinching signature is robust
to smoothing window and quality-cut choices, and is consistent with the growing
accretion luminosity fraction suppressing the thermal peak of the spectrum.

Two of six long-running models (
18\,M_{\odot}
:
f_{+}=0.983
;
19\,M_{\odot}
:
f_{+}=0.805
)
develop
\langle E_{\nu_{e}}\rangle>\langle E_{\nu_{x}}\rangle
after
t=5\,\mathrm{s}
,
while the other four maintain standard ordering. The leptonic fraction

f_{\mathrm{lep}}=0.40\pm 0.03
is consistent across 23 successful models; BH
models show
f_{\mathrm{lep}}\approx 0.47
–
0.48
.

The LESA dipole (
\varepsilon=|\vec{D}|/L_{\mathrm{lep}}
, mean
0.11
for
successful models) is suppressed by
\gtrsim 3\times
in both BH-forming models
(
\varepsilon<0.05
). The
\bar{\nu}_{e}
pinching varies by

\Delta\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}^{68\%}\approx 0.8
–
1.5
across viewing angles,
constituting the dominant systematic (
\sigma_{\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}}^{\rm geom}\approx 0.4
–
0.75
, factor
3
–
6
above statistical precision), driven by
both
\ell=1
(LESA) and higher multipoles.

Sky maps of
L(\hat{n})/\langle L\rangle
,
\langle E\rangle(\hat{n})
, and

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}(\hat{n})
on the
128\times 256
angular grid
(Figs.
8
and
9
) reveal coherent
multipolar structures in all three observables. At
t=1\,\mathrm{s}
,

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}(\hat{n})
is anticorrelated with
L(\hat{n})

(Pearson
r\approx-0.5
to
-0.7
) and positively correlated with

\langle E\rangle(\hat{n})
(
r\approx+0.3
to
+0.6
), confirming that the
spectral pinching anisotropy is not an isolated artefact but traces the same
hydrodynamic structures that modulate the luminosity and mean energy across the sky.

These results establish
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
and the LESA dipole as complementary
diagnostics for next-generation neutrino detectors.

Appendix A
Smoothing Robustness

All time-series quantities in this paper are smoothed with a
25\,\mathrm{ms}

boxcar window (Section
III.2
), chosen to suppress stochastic
SASI fluctuations while preserving the secular evolution of
\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}
.
The robustness of the main results to this choice is demonstrated in Fig.
12
by comparing the
\bar{\nu}_{e}
pinching evolution of the

17\,M_{\odot}
model with three smoothing windows:
10\,\mathrm{ms}
,

25\,\mathrm{ms}
(adopted), and
100\,\mathrm{ms}
.
The key features — the late-time convergence to the floor near

\alpha_{\mathrm{p}}\approx 1.92
, the decline from the accretion-phase peak,
and the general energy-hierarchy structure — are identical for all three windows.
The
10\,\mathrm{ms}
window retains more SASI oscillations during

t=0.5
–
1.5\,\mathrm{s}
; the
100\,\mathrm{ms}
window slightly
over-smooths short-duration features but does not alter any mean values or
the floor estimate. The
25\,\mathrm{ms}
window is a well-justified
intermediate choice.
