Single-cell mitophagy patterns dictate intercellular crosstalk in the tumor microenvironment to promote osteosarcoma progression
Authors: Wei Shi, Lei Zhang, Liying Mei, Chengqi Zhang, Lei Liu, Nan Zhao, Qingyu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteosarcoma (OS) is a highly aggressive primary bone malignancy in adolescents, with poor prognosis due to limited diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of cancer, and mitophagy, the selective clearance of damaged mitochondria, critically maintains cellular homeostasis. However, the specific role of mitophagy in shaping the OS tumor microenvironment (TME) at single-cell resolution remains poorly understood. This study aims to systematically characterize mitophagy patterns within the OS TME and investigate their impact on intercellular communication, tumor progression, and patient prognosis.MethodsWe analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data from OS samples using non-negative matrix factorization to cluster cells based on mitophagy-related genes. We characterized distinct mitophagy-associated subtypes of TME cells. Pseudotime trajectory, cell-cell communication), gene regulatory network, and functional enrichment analyses were performed. Prognostic significance was evaluated using GSVA and Cox regression in bulk RNA-seq cohort. Immunotherapy response was predicted using the TIDE algorithm.ResultsWe identified diverse mitophagy-activated cellular subtypes within the TME. Mitophagy-active CAFs and macrophages exhibited enhanced angiogenic signaling to endothelial cells. Mitophagy-associated CD8+ T cells displayed marked exhaustion features, while macrophages showed metabolic reprogramming. Clinically, higher infiltration of these mitophagy-related subtypes was consistently associated with poorer overall survival. TIDE analysis indicated that mitophagy patterns potentially correlate with immune checkpoint blockade response.ConclusionsOur findings reveal that mitophagy drives complex intercellular crosstalk in the OS TME, promoting angiogenesis and immunosuppression. Mitophagy-related signatures serve as robust prognostic biomarkers. These insights suggest that targeted inhibition of mitophagy, rather than activation, represents a promising therapeutic strategy, providing a novel framework for improving OS patient outcomes.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is an aggressive primary malignant bone tumor with high malignancy and poor prognosis, which is the main cause of cancer-related death and disability in children and adolescents (1). Although the combination of surgical resection and systemic chemotherapy has improved the overall survival of OS patients to a certain extent, the high rates of local recurrence and distant metastasis still plaguing clinical treatment (2,3). Hence, exploring the underlying molecular mechanisms of OS initiation and progression is critical to developing novel diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets.

During tumor development, cancer cells exhibit aberrant metabolic reprogramming (4) and disrupted organelle homeostasis (5). Mitochondrial dysfunction is a well-established hallmark of malignancy, leading to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria that contribute to oxidative stress, genomic instability, and dysregulation of signaling pathways essential for tumor proliferation and survival (6-8). Mitophagy, a selective form of autophagy, plays a critical role in clearing dysfunctional mitochondria and serves as a key quality control mechanism that preserves cellular metabolic balance and mitigates carcinogenic stress (9). Beyond maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, mitophagy dynamically modulates how tumor cells adapt to environmental stressors, influences chemoresistance, and affects metastatic potential (10). Accumulating evidence indicates that the targeted modulation of mitophagy could disrupt key tumor-promoting pathways, positioning it as a promising therapeutic strategy in oncology (11-13).

OS is characterized by a highly complex tumor microenvironment (TME), which comprises diverse infiltrating cell types and intricate intercellular communications (14,15). A growing body of evidence underscores the critical role of the TME in both tumor initiation and metastatic progression (16). Key cellular components of the TME include cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) (17), tumor associated macrophages (TAMs) (18), T cells (19) and B cells (20). Bidirectional interactions between malignant cells and these TME constituents have been shown to significantly promote tumor progression through multiple mechanisms (21,22). Despite extensive research into the OS TME (23,24), the specific role of mitophagy and its interplay with the TME in regulating OS aggressiveness remains largely unexplored. A deeper investigation into how mitophagy interfaces with TME dynamics could provide valuable insights into the pathogenesis of OS and uncover novel therapeutic opportunities.

In this study, we utilized single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to investigate how mitophagy influences functionally distinct subpopulations within the OS TME. Through non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) clustering of mitophagy-related genes, we identified distinct mitophagy activation patterns associated with patient prognosis. Our results reveal previously unrecognized roles for mitophagy in modulating intercellular communication networks within the TME and in shaping OS progression. This detailed map of mitophagy-TME interactions offers a foundational framework for the development of novel therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting mitochondrial homeostasis in OS.

Methods

Experiments design, data acquisition and cell landscape of OS

The GSE162454 dataset, comprising samples from six patients with OS, was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Following sample integration and batch-effect correction, a preliminary gene expression matrix containing 31,465 cells was constructed. In addition, messenger RNA (mRNA) sequencing data and corresponding clinical information for a total of 125 OS patients were collected from the UCSC Xena and GEO databases (Table S1). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. All datasets used in this study were derived from publicly available sources or previously published research. As such, no ethical approval was required for the use of these data.

scRNA-seq data were processed using the Seurat package in R (version 4.2.3). Quality control thresholds were set as nFeature_RNA > 200 & nFeature_RNA <4,000 & percent.mt <10. Subsequently, the “LogNormalize” function was used for normalization with a scale factor of 10,000. The top 2,000 genes exhibiting the highest variability were selected, followed by batch effect correction with Harmony (1.2.0) (15,25,26). Dimensionality reduction and visualization were subsequently performed using t-SNE and UMAP. Cell clusters were annotated based on the expression of canonical marker genes (Table S2) (15,27,28). Annotation and visualization of these cell types were carried out using functions implemented in the Seurat package (4.4.0).

Cell-cell communication analysis

To investigate potential intercellular communication networks, we employed the CellChat package (1.6.1) (29) to analyze cell–cell interactions. We performed all analyses using the standard pipeline (https://github.com/sqjin/CellChat). Ligand-receptor pairs involved in secreted signaling were extracted from the CellChatDB database (database: human) and used to infer communication patterns among distinct cell populations. We filtered out the cell-cell communication if there are only fewer than 10 cells in certain cell groups and we will only consider the cell communication to be meaningful when the threshold is ≤0.05. These ligand–receptor interactions were subsequently integrated into protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks, enabling the quantification of interaction strength based on computed probabilities of cell-cell communication. Using this framework, we systematically characterized molecular interaction networks involving key cell types—including macrophages, CAFs, T cells, endothelial cells, B cells—with a particular focus on communication pathways between endothelial cells and mitophagy-associated cell subsets.

NMF of mitophagy-related genes in OS TME cells

To explore the influence of mitophagy on distinct cellular subsets within the TME, we first performed dimensionality reduction on individual cell populations. The expression patterns of 32 mitophagy-related genes (Table S3) were then examined across major TME cell types. Subsequently, we applied NMF (0.27) to delineate functionally distinct subtypes within these cell populations based on mitophagy gene expression profiles. Based on the single-cell RNA (scRNA) expression matrix, different NMF cell subtypes were determined. The FindAllMarkers function was used, and the characteristics of each cluster were determined by setting logfc.threshold =1. If the log fold change (FC) value was the maximum and the p_val was less than 0.05, the characteristic genes were mitophagy-related genes. In this case, we defined this subgroup with this gene. The execution method of all these steps was similar to that of previous studies (30,31).

Pseudotime trajectory analysis of mitophagy gene for OS TME cells

To investigate pseudotime trajectories and the associated dynamics of mitophagy regulators, we applied the Monocle2 R package (version 2.30.0) (32) to analyze the developmental progression of NMF-defined subtypes within the OS TME. Highly variable genes were selected based on the following criteria: mean expression ≥0.1 and empirical dispersion ≥0.1 × dispersion fit. The ‘plot_pseudotime_heatmap’ function was then used to generate heatmaps illustrating the temporal expression patterns of mitophagy-related genes across different TME cell types along the inferred pseudotime trajectory.

The identification for mitophagy-related OS TME subcelltypes

After defining the target cell populations, we identified differentially expressed genes within each NMF cluster using the FindAllMarkers function. Genes with a log2 fold change greater than 1 were considered cluster-specific marker genes, and the top-ranked gene in each cluster was designated as its representative marker. The expression levels of these top markers across clusters were visualized using the DotPlot function in the Seurat package.

NMF mitophagy-related subtypes functional enrichment analysis

Based on marker genes associated with distinct mitophagy-related TME cell types, we performed functional enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler R package, querying both the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) pathway databases. Enrichment results were visualized using heatmaps and bar plots. Only gene sets with an adjusted P<0.05 were considered statistically significant. Our analysis highlights the most significantly enriched pathways across mitophagy-defined clusters.

Single-Cell rEgulatory Network Inference and Clustering (SCENIC) analysis of NMF mitophagy-related subtypes

Gene regulatory network analysis was performed on OS scRNA-seq data using the SCENIC pipeline. Transcription factor (TF) binding motif ranking—specifically hg19-tss-centered-10kb and hg19-500bp-upstream—were obtained from the RcisTarget database to support the identification of transcription start sites and regulatory interactions. TFs with an adjusted P<0.05 were selected for further analysis.

Survival analyses with mitophagy-related signatures in public bulk RNA-sequence datasets

Mitophagy-related gene signatures were first identified for each NMF cluster using the FindAllMarkers function (the mean expression of the top marker genes that logfc.threshold ≥1 and p_val <0.05 from each NMF cluster), and the average expression of these signatures was then computed for major cell types in the scRNA-seq data. After this, we identified the characteristic markers for each of the mitophagy-related subgroups and grouped them into gene sets (Table S4). Signature scores were subsequently calculated in bulk OS transcriptomic datasets using the GSVA (1.48.3) (33) function with method=’ssgsea’, kcdf=‘Gaussian’. Associations between mitophagy-related signatures and patient overall survival were evaluated using log-rank tests and Cox proportional hazards regression. Optimal cutoff values for stratifying patients based on signature scores were determined with the survminer R package, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated accordingly.

Immunotherapy analysis

OS samples were obtained from the UCSC Xena and GEO databases. First, we normalized bulk RNA-seq expression matrices and subsequently submitted to the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Rejection (TIDE) web platform (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) (34) to predict patient responses to immunotherapy. Subsequently, we conducted a logistic regression based on the output file. The threshold for differentiating responders from non-responders. Subsequently, we used the GSVA (1.48.3) function with method=’ssgsea’, kcdf=‘Gaussian’ to predicted immunotherapy outcomes and mitophagy-related NMF features. The statistical association between mitophagy-related signature scores and TIDE-predicted response was assessed using logistic regression.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.3). Survival analyses were conducted using the KaplanMeier method and log-rank tests, with optimal cutoff values determined by the survminer package. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to evaluate the prognostic value of mitophagy-related signatures, with results reported as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). For cell-cell communication, interaction significance was determined using a permutation test with a threshold of P<0.05. Differential gene expression between NMF clusters was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with genes considered significant if |log2 fold change| >1 and adjusted P<0.05. Association between signature scores and predicted immunotherapy response was evaluated using logistic regression.

To investigate cellular heterogeneity within the OS TME, we performed scRNA-seq on samples from six patients. After quality control and batch effect removal, 31,456 cells were retained and clustered into 20 distinct populations, visualized using UMAP and t-SNE (Figure S1A-S1C). Based on canonical marker genes, we annotated eight major cell types: T cells, B cells, macrophages, endothelial cells, monocytes, plasma cells, CAFs, and osteoblastic OS cells (Figure 1A,1C). Representative markers for each cell type are shown in Figure 1D. Cell type proportions varied considerably across individual samples, with monocytes, macrophages, and osteoblastic OS cells comprising the largest fractions (Figure 1B).

Intercellular communication analysis revealed extensive signaling networks among OS-resident cell populations (Figure 1E; Figure S1D,S1E), with macrophages exhibiting the highest incoming and outgoing interaction strengths (Figure 1F). To assess mitophagy activity across cell types, we applied multiple scoring methods (AUcell, Ucell, singscore, ssGSEA, and AddModuleScore). Mitophagy scores were elevated in most OS cell populations, with CAFs showing the highest enrichment, while monocytes displayed comparatively lower scores (Figure 1G-1I). Examination of individual mitophagy-related gene expression revealed broad abundance of genes such as RPS27A, UBB, and UBC across cell types, alongside cell-type-specific expression patterns (Figure 1J).

Following cell type annotation, we extracted 341 CAFs from the OS scRNA for further analysis. After quality control, dimensionality reduction, and NMF, three distinct CAF subtypes were identified (Figure S2A). Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed that mitophagy-related genes were dynamically expressed throughout CAF differentiation and appeared to play a regulatory role in this process (Figure 2B,2C). Based on marker gene expression, the subtypes were designated as SQSTM1_CAFs, TOMM20_CAFs, and non-Mit CAFs (Figure 2A). Cell-cell communication analysis showed that mitophagy-associated CAFs engaged in ligand-receptor interactions with endothelial cells to varying degrees (Figure 2E).

Notably, endothelial cells received more signals from mitophagy-active CAFs, particularly through angiogenic pathways such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), angiopoietin-like protein (ANGPTL), and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) (Figure 2D; Figure S2B,S2C). Among the subtypes, TOMM20_CAFs exhibited the strongest outgoing signaling via VEGF and ANGPTL (Figure 2F; Figure S2D). Gene regulatory network analysis revealed distinct TF activity across CAF subtypes. Among 51 differentially regulated TFs, the majority were upregulated in TOMM20_CAFs and downregulated in non-Mit CAFs (Figure 2H). When mapped to previously defined pan-CAF signatures, non-Mit CAFs showed negative correlations with all known CAF subtypes, whereas SQSTM1 and TOMM20_CAFs correlated broadly with multiple CAF populations (Figure 2G). Pathway-level analysis further highlighted these distinctions: genes involved in matrix remodeling, including multiple MMPs, were predominantly downregulated in non-Mit CAFs but upregulated in the mitophagy-associated subtypes (Figure 2I).

Mitophagy-related macrophages resembled classical features

After initial clustering, 11,000 macrophages were identified. Following quality control and exclusion of potential contaminating cell types, 7,630 macrophages were retained for downstream analysis. NMF was applied, yielding nine initial clusters (Figure S3A-S3E), which were subsequently consolidated into five subtypes: VDAC1_Mac, UBE2L3_Mac, TOMM20_Mac, VDAC2_Mac, and non-Mit Mac (Figure 3A). Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed that mitophagy-related genes were primarily active during both early and late stages of macrophage differentiation (Figure 3B,3C). Similar to observations in CAFs, mitophagy-associated macrophage subtypes—particularly VDAC1_Mac—displayed frequent intercellular communication with tumor endothelial cells (Figure 3D). Analysis of signaling patterns further highlighted that mitophagy-active macrophages exhibited stronger outgoing angiogenic signals, including VEGF pathway activity, compared to their non-mitophagy counterparts (Figure 3E). To assess potential metabolic reprogramming in mitophagy-associated macrophages, we performed KEGG enrichment analysis of metabolic pathways. VDAC1_Mac showed marked upregulation of multiple metabolic processes, including glucose metabolism, fatty acid oxidation, and cholesterol metabolism (Figure 3F). Finally, using the AddModuleScore function, we found that mitophagy-related macrophages were significantly associated with a pro-inflammatory gene signature (Figure 3G).

The contribution of mitophagy-related B cell subtypes in OS TME

From the scRNA-seq data, 431 B cells were identified and further clustered using NMF, resulting in five initial clusters (Figure S4A-S4E). These were subsequently consolidated into three distinct subtypes: UBE2D3_B cells, MAP1LC3B_B cells, and non-Mit B cells (Figure 4A). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that mitophagy-associated B cell subtypes exhibited enhanced oxidative phosphorylation and activation of inflammatory pathways, including the NF-κB signaling cascade (Figure 4B). GO enrichment analysis further supported these findings, indicating more active biological processes in mitophagy-related B cells compared to non-Mit B cells (Figure 4C). Cell-cell communication analysis between B cells and endothelial cells demonstrated that mitophagy-associated B cells engaged in more frequent interactions with endothelial cells than their non-mitophagy counterparts (Figure 4D). The signaling output from mitophagy-active B cells was primarily mediated by MIF and GALECTIN pathways (Figure 4E). Although the number of ligands-receptor pairs varied across B cell subtypes, the dominant communication direction was from endothelial cells to B cells. Notably, the NAMPT-(ITGA5 + ITGB1) and MIF-(CD74 + CXCR4) ligand-receptor interactions were markedly upregulated in these mitophagy-associated clusters (Figure 4F,4G).

From the scRNA data, 4,948 T cells were identified and further classified into CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells (Figure S5A-S5F). CD8+ T cells were selected for in-depth analysis. NMF of these cells yielded 15 initial clusters, which were subsequently consolidated into eight subtypes: SQSTM1_CD8+ T, OPTN_CD8+ T, TOMM20_CD8+ T, non-Mit CD8+ T, UBE2D2_CD8+ T, MAP1LC3B_CD8+ T, UBE2L3_CD8+ T, and UBE2D3_CD8+ T (Figure 5A). Pseudotime trajectory analysis delineated seven differentiation stages (Figure 5B), with mitophagy-related genes primarily active during the later phases (Figure 5C).

Cell-cell communication analysis revealed extensive interactions between CD8+ T cell subsets and endothelial cells, with mitophagy-associated subtypes showing heightened communication frequency (Figure 5D). Most CD8+ T cells received MIF, GALECTIN, and MK signals from endothelial cells. Notably, mitophagy-active CD8+ T subsets also exhibited outgoing signaling via PARs and VISFATIN pathways (Figure 5E; Figure S5G,S5H).

Gene regulatory network analysis uncovered distinct TF activity across CD8+ T cell subtypes (Figure 5F). Evaluation of immune modulatory genes showed differential expression of co-stimulatory and co-inhibitory molecules among the subtypes (Figure 5H). Furthermore, T cell exhaustion and cytotoxicity scores varied significantly: SQSTM1, UBE2L3, and OPTN_CD8+ T cells displayed prominent exhaustion signatures, whereas non-Mit CD8+ T cells were characterized by higher cytotoxicity scores (Figure 5G).

Mitophagy-related TME patterns indicated the prognosis and immunotherapy of OS

Within the OS TME, we observed complex intercellular communication among mitophagy-associated cell populations (Figure S6A-S6C). To further characterize these cell types, we quantified the expression of mitophagy-related genes in both bulk RNA-seq and single-cell RNA-seq data. Mitophagy activity scores were calculated using the GSVA method and subsequently evaluated for their prognostic significance in OS patients. For Cox regression analysis, scores were dichotomized into high and low groups (table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2026-0735-1.xlsx). Notably, higher infiltration levels of mitophagy-associated TME cell types were consistently associated with poorer patient outcomes (Figure 6A). These findings were partially validated in an independent GEO cohort (Figure 6B; Figure S6D). To identify specific mitophagy-related signatures associated with prognosis, we performed univariate Cox regression analysis (table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2026-0735-1.xlsx). Among the 15 mitophagy-related characteristics, TOMM20_CD8+ T cell signature [hazard ratio (HR) =426.64, 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.87–63,430.99, P=0.02] and MAP1LC3B_B cell signature (HR =100,315.73, 95% CI: 3.18×109–3.16×109, P=0.03) were significantly associated with poorer overall survival. The remaining 13 characteristics did not reach statistical significance (all P>0.05). Due to the limited sample size (37 patients, 10 events), the confidence intervals for these estimates are wide; therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and require validation in larger cohorts.

We also assessed the potential of mitophagy‑related TME signatures to predict immunotherapy response. OS transcriptomic data from the Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET) and GEO cohorts were submitted to the TIDE platform to compute predicted responses to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). Logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate associations between each mitophagy‑associated cell subtype and TIDE‑predicted responder status (table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2026-0735-1.xlsx). Among the evaluated signatures, VDAC1_Mac showed a trend toward positive association with predicted response (OR =1.536, 95% CI: 0.0002–5,048.10); however, this did not reach statistical significance due to the wide confidence interval, likely attributable to the limited sample size. No other signatures reached statistical significance. OS transcriptomic data were submitted to the TIDE platform, and logistic regression analysis revealed that mitophagy-associated subtypes were significantly correlated with predicted response to immune checkpoint blockade (Figure 6C). Consistent results were observed in both TARGET and GEO datasets (Figure 6D). The limited efficacy of current immunotherapies in OS may be partly attributable to mitophagy-driven immunosuppression within the TME.

Discussion

Mitophagy dysfunction has been implicated in a range of human diseases, including inflammatory disorders, degenerative conditions, and various cancers (9). Impaired clearance of damaged mitochondria disrupts cellular metabolism and contributes to pathological cellular function (35). Recent studies have reinforced the clinical and biological relevance of mitophagy in OS (36). Mechanistically, mitophagy promotes metastasis (37), invasion (38), tumorigenesis (39) and is coactivated with metabolic pathways (40) across OS. Functionally, mitophagy drives M2like macrophage polarization and increased chemoresistance and tumor growth (41), and immunosuppressive TME remodeling. Prognostically, mitophagyrelated gene signatures predict survival (42) and progression (43). Although previous studies have established a link between mitophagy and the pathogenesis of OS (44) and our findings confirm elevated mitophagy activity in most OS cells, the specific impact of mitophagy on the TME at single-cell resolution remains underexplored. In this study, we systematically characterized mitophagy-associated cellular subtypes within the OS TME and uncovered distinct intercellular communication patterns—particularly with endothelial cells—using scRNA. This integrative approach provides new insights into how mitophagy shapes the TME and influences OS progression.

OS exhibits substantial histological heterogeneity, including osteoblastic, chondroblastic, and fibroblastic subtypes (28), which may differentially shape the TME and influence therapeutic response. Increasingly, studies have focused on the dynamic remodeling of the OS TME. For instance, chemotherapy has been shown to modulate immune composition by reducing regulatory T cells and activated CD8+ T cells while enhancing CD8+ effector T cell population (45). M2 macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) collectively predict poor metastasis-free survival, with M2-derived MIP-1α promoting pulmonary metastasis (46). Spatial transcriptomic analysis of OS lung metastases further reveals a granuloma-like immunosuppressive niche characterized by lymphocyte exclusion and peritumoral macrophage accumulation, with CXCR4 identified as a spatially heterogeneous therapeutic target (47). These findings underscore the critical role of intercellular interactions in shaping the OS TME. They are also consistent with our observation results, which indicate that macrophages with mitochondrial autophagy activity exhibit stronger inflammatory and angiogenic signal transduction, and there are extensive interactions between mitochondrial autophagy-related TME cells and endothelial cells. Moreover, our research results also supplement these studies, proving that mitochondrial autophagy plays a functional regulatory role in the immune suppression, metabolic reprogramming and intercellular communication of the TME, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

Tumor endothelial cells play a critical role in multiple aspects of cancer progression, including tumor growth, invasion, metastasis, therapeutic resistance, and immune evasion (48). In OS, angiogenesis driven by endothelial cell infiltration is closely associated with poor prognosis (48), underscoring the importance of understanding how other TME cells influence endothelial function. In this study, we investigated the effects of mitophagy-associated subtypes of CAFs, macrophages, CD8+ T cells, and B cells on endothelial cells. Our findings reveal that mitophagy-mediated intercellular communication contributes to the activation of pro-angiogenic signaling in endothelial cells, highlighting the involvement of mitophagy-defined TME subsets in regulating angiogenesis in OS.

In complex TME, CAFs are key contributors to angiogenesis and extracellular matrix remodeling. Previous studies have demonstrated that CAFs promote tumor vascularization largely through the secretion of VEGF (49,50). Consistently, our scRNA analysis revealed robust communication networks between CAFs and endothelial cells involving multiple pro-angiogenic signaling pathways, further supporting the role of CAFs in tumor angiogenesis. Specifically, mitophagy-associated CAF subtypes exhibited enhanced outgoing signaling via classical angiogenic factors such as VEGF and ANGPTL. The activation and reciprocal reinforcement of these signals may facilitate vascular network remodeling within the TME, potentially contributing to tumor progression, therapeutic resistance, and metastasis (51,52). Compared to non-mitophagy CAFs, mitophagy-active subtypes displayed more extensive signaling toward endothelial cells. This may reflect mitophagy-induced metabolic and functional reprogramming in CAFs (53,54), which could partially explain the association between mitophagy-associated CAF subtypes and poorer clinical outcomes in OS.

Based on established molecular and functional classifications, CAFs have been categorized into several subtypes, including pan-myCAFs, pan-dCAFs, pan-iCAFs, pan-nCAFs, and pan-pCAFs (17,55-57). In this study, we observed that mitophagy-associated CAFs displayed broader functional characteristics compared to their non-mitophagy counterparts, suggesting a possible link between mitophagy activation and stem-like properties (58). Correlation analysis further delineated the functional relationships between our mitophagy‑defined CAF subtypes and established pan‑CAF classifications. TOMM20_CAFs showed strong positive correlations with pan‑dCAF and pan‑pCAF signatures and exhibited pronounced enrichment of matrix‑remodeling factors (e.g., MMP9, MMP11), closely resembling the matrix‑remodeling phenotype of mCAFs. SQSTM1_CAFs correlated positively with pan‑iCAF signatures and displayed elevated expression of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL6, IL7, CCL2) along with activation of the ANGPTL pathway, consistent with the inflammatory CAF (iCAF) phenotype. In contrast, non‑Mit CAFs showed consistently negative correlations with all pan‑CAF subtypes, suggesting a quiescent or functionally suppressed state. Collectively, these results indicate that while mitophagy status does not define mutually exclusive CAF lineages, it may serve as a functional modulator that potentiates the pro‑angiogenic or pro‑inflammatory capacities of CAFs.

Further analysis revealed that mitophagy-active CAFs exhibited elevated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL6, IL7), matrix remodeling factors (e.g., MMPs), and neurovascular regulators (e.g., EGFL6, VEGFA). The upregulation of inflammatory mediators may result from enhanced protein turnover associated with clearance of damaged mitochondria (59,60), which in turn could amplify angiogenic and matrix-remodeling processes, establishing a pro-tumorigenic feedback loop (61,62). These features help explain the heightened intercellular communication between mitophagy-associated CAFs and endothelial cells, as well as their multifunctional nature. Such activity may also contribute to extracellular matrix degradation, distant metastasis, and ultimately poor prognosis in OS. Consistent with these functional changes, mitophagy-related CAFs exhibited increased transcriptional activity, with upregulation of TFs potentially linked to mitochondrial biogenesis and repair following mitophagy (63). However, sustained activation of factors such as JUN and FOSB may drive excessive intracellular signaling, potentially undermining the homeostatic benefits of mitophagy and contributing to malignant progression.

Recent studies have increasingly highlighted the role of immune cell regulation and reprogramming in tumor progression. Through NMF clustering, we identified extensive intercellular communication between mitophagy-associated macrophage subtypes and endothelial cells. Most mitophagy-active macrophages exhibited efferent signaling via CCL, SPP1, TNF, and VEGF pathways, suggesting a pro-inflammatory phenotype. However, contrary to the expected anti-tumor effects of classically activated inflammatory macrophages (64,65), these subtypes were not associated with favorable prognosis in OS. This discrepancy implies that mitophagy-driven macrophages may engage in additional physiological processes that counterbalance their pro-inflammatory and tumor-suppressive functions. From a metabolic perspective, mitophagy-associated macrophages displayed heterogeneous metabolic reprogramming. Among them, the VDAC1 subtype stood out with marked activation of the TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, and glycolysis. Previous research has linked transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ) signaling to metabolic reprogramming in macrophages (66,67). Consistently, our cell communication analysis revealed that VDAC1_Mac exhibited strong TGFβ outgoing signaling, which may underlie their enhanced metabolic activity and functional divergence from other mitophagy-related subtypes.

In addition to macrophages, our analysis revealed that mitophagy-associated CD8+ T cells and B cells also formed intricate communication networks with endothelial cells. CD8+ T cells with elevated mitophagy activity exhibited pronounced exhaustion features, including higher exhaustion scores and reduced expression of co-inhibitory molecules, suggesting that mitophagy may compromise their cytotoxic function and promote an immunosuppressive microenvironment (68,69). Consistently, survival analysis indicated that these mitophagy-related CD8+ T cell subsets were associated with poorer patient outcomes. Meanwhile, mitophagy-associated B cells showed enrichment of stress and apoptosis-related signaling pathways, which may further reinforce immunosuppression (70,71). Together, these findings suggest that mitophagy-active lymphoid subsets are poorly suited to support coordinated anti-tumor immunity and may contribute to the limited efficacy of immunotherapy observed in OS patients.

Based on these findings, we hypothesize that mitophagy-associated subtypes contribute to the remodeling and reprogramming of the TME, fostering an immunosuppressive milieu that adversely affects patient prognosis. The establishment of such an environment may be partially driven by mitophagy activity. Notably, the close intercellular communication between mitophagy-related TME cells and endothelial cells suggests a potential mechanism by which mitophagy promotes immunosuppression and vascular network remodeling—processes that may ultimately facilitate distant metastasis in OS. Based on the original findings, we also conducted immune predictions for these cell subtypes that affect prognosis. However, disappointingly, it seems that immunotherapy does not provide significant benefits to patients. While TIDE analysis provides computational predictions of immunotherapy response, its clinical relevance in OS remains to be established. ICB monotherapy has shown limited efficacy in OS patients, and the TIDE framework was primarily developed on adult solid tumors. Therefore, our TIDE-based findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than clinically definitive. Future studies incorporating prospective immunotherapy cohorts are needed to validate the predictive utility of mitophagy-related signatures.

Conclusions

Using NMF factorization applied to scRNA sequencing data, we identified distinct mitophagy‑related subtypes within the OS TME. Our findings highlight the role of mitophagy‑associated intercellular communication in modulating vascular network maintenance and tumor immune regulation. Notably, elevated mitophagy activity in TME cells, including CAFs, macrophages, and CD8+ T cells, was consistently associated with poorer patient prognosis, enhanced angiogenic signaling, and an immunosuppressive phenotype. These observations suggest that inhibition of mitophagy, rather than its activation, represents the desirable therapeutic directionality for improving OS outcomes. Pharmacologically, this could be pursued using existing autophagy inhibitors. However, given the cell‑type‑specific roles of mitophagy, systemic inhibition may cause unwanted side effects; therefore, cell‑type‑restricted delivery strategies just like nanoparticle‑encapsulated inhibitors or combination with conventional chemotherapies should be explored in preclinical models.

In summary, this study provides a high‑resolution atlas of mitophagy patterns in the OS TME, establishes a prognostic model based on mitophagy‑related signatures, and offers a rationale for developing mitophagy‑targeted interventions. Future work should focus on validating the therapeutic efficacy of mitophagy inhibition in orthotopic OS models and elucidating the optimal timing and combination regimens for clinical translation.
