Expression of miR-185-5p, miR-146b-5p, miR-320b, and miR-21-5p in Eosinophil Subtypes in Allergic Asthma Patients
Authors: Eglė Vasylė, Andrius Januskevicius, Paulina Malakauskaitė, Airidas Rimkūnas, Jolita Palacionyte, Kęstutis Malakauskas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAllergic asthma (AA) is a common type 2 eosinophilic asthma phenotype. Inflammatory (iEOS) and lung-resident (rEOS) eosinophil subtypes may contribute to its underlying inflammatory processes. The biological functions of these eosinophils are tightly regulated by gene expression, with micro ribonucleic acids (miRNAs) emerging as key modulators and potential biomarkers.PurposeTo investigate the expression of miR-185-5p, miR-146b-5p, miR-320b, and miR-21-5p in eosinophil subtypes and relate with their plasma levels in AA patients.Patients and MethodsThe study included 13 AA patients and 16 healthy subjects (HS). All AA patients underwent bronchial allergen challenge (BAC) with Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus and blood samples were re-taken after 24 hours. Total blood eosinophils were isolated by high-density centrifugation and magnetic separation, phenotyped using CD62L-conjugated magnetic beads. miRNAs expression quantified by reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR).ResultsBefore BAC, miR-185-5p and miR-146b-5p expression were elevated in both eosinophil subtypes relative to HS, with miR-185-5p showing higher expression in iEOS-like cells than in rEOS-like cells within the AA group. miR-320b was elevated only in iEOS-like cells relative to HS and remained higher than in rEOS-like cells within the AA group, whereas miR-21-5p was decreased only in iEOS-like cells and remained lower than in rEOS-like cells. After BAC, miR-146b-5p expression decreased in iEOS-like cells but increased in rEOS-like cells. Plasma analysis revealed elevated levels of all investigated miRNAs in AA patients relative to HS before BAC. However, after BAC, miR-185-5p and miR-146b-5p remained elevated, whereas miR-320b and miR-21-5p decreased to levels not significantly different from HS.ConclusionElevated miR-185-5p and miR-320b expression in iEOS-like cells compared to rEOS-like cells from AA patients highlights differences in their biological properties. Following the BAC, miR-146b-5p expression decreased in iEOS-like cells but increased in rEOS-like cells. Moreover, miR-185-5p and miR-146b-5p remained elevated in plasma, suggesting their potential to reflect chronic inflammation rather than acute response to allergen.Keywords: asthma, eosinophil subtypes, allergen, miRNAs, biomarkers

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic respiratory disease marked by airway inflammation, which leads to symptoms such as wheezing, breathlessness, chest tightness, and coughing. The disease presents with a variety of phenotypes, which are often driven by distinct inflammatory pathways.1 Eosinophils, a type of white blood cell that typically take part in allergic reactions, play a significant role in the pathogenesis of asthma by contributing to airway inflammation and influencing the frequency of exacerbations.2 Building upon this understanding, research has identified distinct eosinophil subtypes with unique gene expression profiles, representing a major advancement in the field.3,4 Particularly, the differentiation of eosinophils into iEOS and rEOS subtypes has been a critical development, with each subtype playing distinct roles in the pathogenesis and progression of asthma.5–7 While these subtypes differ functionally (rEOS are mainly involved in tissue maintenance and immune regulation, while iEOS display a pro-inflammatory profile characterized by enhanced activation, degranulation, and cytokine release), they are phenotypically distinguished by surface markers: rEOS are characterized by high expression of L-selectin (CD62L+), whereas iEOS exhibit low or absent expression (CD62L-).4 Consequently, they are thought to play distinct roles in airway inflammation.

miRNAs have gained attention in the field of asthma research due to their ability to regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level.8 These small, non-coding RNA molecules play a crucial role in various biological processes, including immune response regulation and inflammation, which are central to asthma pathogenesis. The study of miRNAs in asthma has revealed insights into their potential as biomarkers for disease severity and treatment response, as well as their role in modulating the function of key immune cells, including eosinophils.9–12

While studies have begun to characterize the miRNA expression profiles in eosinophils,13 a complete understanding of miRNA dynamics, especially in the distinct eosinophil subtypes in the context of allergic eosinophilic asthma remains unexplored. This gap in knowledge presents an opportunity to delve deeper into the molecular mechanisms of asthma, particularly how these miRNA profiles may differ between the eosinophil subtypes and contribute to the various asthma phenotypes. Investigating the differential expression of miRNAs in eosinophil subtypes could pave the way for more targeted and effective therapies, since miRNAs are implicated in the regulation of processes involved in asthma pathogenesis.14 In addition to their role in cellular function, miRNAs are also known for their stability in body fluids, such as blood plasma and serum, making them attractive candidates as non-invasive biomarkers for asthma diagnosis and monitoring.15 This aspect is particularly promising in the context of personalized medicine, where identifying specific miRNA patterns may help stratify asthma patients according to inflammatory phenotype, eosinophilic activity, or disease severity and support treatment tailoring to individual patients’ needs.

For this study, we specifically selected patients with AA who are sensitive to dust mites, as AA is the most prevalent phenotype, and dust mite allergens are among the most common triggers worldwide with 45% of sensitization rate reported among local sensitized individuals.16 We focused on four miRNAs (miR-185-5p, miR-146b-5p, miR-320b, and miR-21-5p), selected based on prior evidence identifying them as eosinophil-associated miRNAs that distinguish asthmatic from healthy individuals.13 Moreover, miR-185-5p was chosen for its strong diagnostic performance, outperforming established biomarkers such as serum periostin, while miR-21-5p and miR-146b-5p were included due to their increased expression in asthmatic airways, and miR-320b was selected for its demonstrated utility in differentiating asthma phenotypes.13,17,18 Given these findings, we analysed the expression of these miRNAs in eosinophil subtypes and plasma, examined the expression of their predicted gene targets in eosinophil subtypes, and evaluated the influence of allergen challenge to better understand miRNA-mediated mechanisms in eosinophils and their potential utility as biomarkers and and inform future studies exploring their therapeutic potential in AA management.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

The study group consisted of 13 AA patients and 16 HS. Recruitment of AA participants was conducted through the Pulmonology Department at the Lithuanian University of Health Sciences Kaunas Clinics. AA was diagnosed based on persistent asthma-related symptoms together with confirmed airway hyperresponsiveness to methacholine. Eligible patients were required to have non-severe asthma corresponding to GINA treatment Steps 1–2. Furthermore, to avoid potential confounding effects related to corticosteroid exposure, inhaled corticosteroid use was not permitted within one month before study. The HS was formed from volunteers devoid of any pulmonary conditions or allergic histories. Whole subjects were non-smokers. All participants underwent a structured study visit schedule. The HS completed two hospital visits: an initial screening visit followed by the primary experimental visit (V1), scheduled 7 to 21 days later. AA participants were scheduled for three visits: an initial screening, an experimental visit (V1), and a follow-up visit 24 h after BAC with Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus (V2). At the screening visit, preliminary inclusion and exclusion criteria were first assessed. Written informed consent was then obtained prior to any study-related procedures. Subsequently, screening procedures were performed in the following order: spirometry, methacholine challenge testing (for all AA participants), and skin prick testing. During V1, peripheral blood sampling for complete blood count and total IgE analysis was performed first, followed by FeNO measurement, spirometry, and finally BAC with Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus in the AA group. For AA participants, V2 included repeat peripheral blood collection, FeNO assessment, and spirometry in the same order. Outcome Measures

The primary outcome of this study was the expression of miR-185-5p, miR-146b-5p, miR-320b, and miR-21-5p in iEOS-like and rEOS-like cells from AA patients compared with eosinophils from HS. Secondary outcomes included BAC-induced changes in miRNA expression in eosinophil subtypes, plasma levels of the selected miRNAs before and after BAC, and the expression of selected miRNA target genes in eosinophil subtypes.

Confirmation of Participation in the Study

The study’s protocol was approved by the Regional Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of the Lithuanian University of Health Sciences (LUHS) (approval number: P3-BE-2-58/2020). All participants provided written informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study. The study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki. For privacy protection, each participant’s data was assigned a unique identification number. The study is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT04542902). The study was conducted between October 1, 2021, and October 1, 2023.

Peripheral Blood Count and Immunoglobulin E

Blood samples from participants were drawn into dipotassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (K2EDTA)-coated vacutainers (BD Vacutainer®, Becton Dickinson UK Ltd., Wokingham, UK) for analysis. The automated hematology analyzer XE-5000™ (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan) was utilized for the complete blood count, and the automated immunoassay analyzer AIA-2000 (Tosoh Bioscience, South San Francisco, CA, USA) measured the immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels.

Pulmonary Function Evaluation

FeNO Measurement

FeNO levels were assessed for all individuals participating in the study. Measurements for the AA group were taken both before and after 24 hours of undergoing a BAC with Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus. These evaluations were conducted using the Vivatmo-me device (Bosch Healthcare Solutions, Waiblingen, Germany), with adherence to the provided guidelines, and each assessment was performed at least two times for accuracy.

Spirometric Assessment

Spirometry was administered to all individuals involved in the study, with a minimum of three attempts being standard practice. An ultrasonic spirometer (Ganshorn Medizin Electronic, Niederlauer, Germany) was used for lung function tests. A comprehensive description of the spirometry technique is available in reference.5

Methacholine Challenge Test

All AA participants underwent methacholine challenge testing during the screening visit. The test was administered using a ProvoX dosimeter (Ganshorn Medizin Electronic, Niederlauer, Germany), with lung function assessments conducted bi-minutely following methacholine inhalation, completing when a 20% reduction in FEV1 was observed. The methodology is detailed in reference.5

Bronchial Allergen Challenge with Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus

For all participants in the AA group, a BAC was conducted using the inhaled Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus allergen (DIATER, Madrid, Spain). Inhalation of the allergen was facilitated through a dosimeter (ProvoX; Ganshorn Medizin Electronic, Niederlauer, Germany), and the challenge was concluded when there was a 20% reduction in FEV1 from the baseline. Comprehensive descriptions of this method are documented in reference.5

Skin Prick Testing

Skin testing for allergies was conducted with an array of standardized extracts (Stallergenes, S.A., France), including cat and dog dandruff, birch pollen, five mixed grass pollen, mugwort, Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus, Dermatophagoides farinae, Aspergillus, Alternaria, and Cladosporium. This testing aimed to identify sensitivities, employing saline as the negative and histamine hydrochloride (10 mg/mL) as the positive control. Results were evaluated 15 minutes post-application, with reactions exceeding 3 mm in wheal diameter deemed positive for allergy. The study included only those AA patients who showed a sensitivity to Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus.

Isolation of Eosinophils from Peripheral Blood and Eosinophil Subtyping

Approximately 30 mL of blood was collected from each participant into K2EDTA-coated vacutainers. The eosinophil isolation and subtyping process utilized magnetic beads and antibodies against CD62L (Miltenyi Biotec, Somerville, MA, USA), targeting an adhesion molecule highly expressed (CD62L+) on rEOS-like cells but not or low (CD62L-) on iEOS-like cells.4 The total amount and viability of eosinophil subtypes were assessed using the ADAM automatic cell counter (NanoEnTek Inc, Mountain View, CA, USA), while a FacsCalibur flow cytometer (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was used to confirm the separation of the eosinophil subtypes based on CD62L surface expression as described in reference.19 The eosinophil isolation kit ensures over 96% efficiency in eosinophil isolation while preserving cell viability. Detailed methodology is outlined in reference.5

RNA Isolation from Eosinophils

To isolate total RNA (including small RNAs) from eosinophils, the procedure began with cell lysis and homogenization, utilizing the miRNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The methodology was rigorously followed according to the manufacturer’s guidelines to maintain RNA integrity and quality, vital for precise miRNA expression analysis. The purified RNA was stored at −80 °C until use.

RNA Isolation from Plasma

The process for total RNA (including small RNAs) isolation from blood plasma involved an initial centrifugation step at ≤ 1300 × g for 10 minutes to obtain clear plasma free of cellular components. Subsequent RNA purification from the plasma was carried out using the miRNeasy Mini Kit. (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), applying the manufacturer’s protocol tailored for RNA purification from body fluids. To initiate, 1 mL of the sample was mixed with 1 mL of QIAzol Lysis Reagent in a 1:1 ratio, vigorously shaken, and incubated for 5 minutes at room temperature. Following this, 25 fmol of synthetic spike-in cel-miR-39 (TaqMan™ MicroRNA Assay, Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific), used as a control for RNA purification, cDNA synthesis, and quantitative PCR efficiency assessment, and 200 μL of chloroform were added. The mixture was shaken vigorously, incubated for 2–3 minutes at room temperature, and then centrifuged for 15 minutes at12000× g at 4 °C. From this point, RNA purification followed the manufacturer’s guidelines: the aspirated upper phase was combined with 1.5 times its volume of 96% ethanol, mixed, and then applied to a column in several steps. It was washed once with 700 μL of Buffer RWT and twice with 500 μL of Buffer RPE, centrifuging for 15 seconds at10000× g after each wash. Elution was performed directly on the column with nuclease-free water, and the purified RNA was stored at −80 °C until use.

Extracted RNA Quality Assessment

The quality and concentration of total RNA (including small RNAs) extracted from eosinophils and blood plasma were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

miRNA Selection and Target Prediction

For the initial selection of microRNAs, we conducted a comprehensive literature review to identify miRNAs previously described in studies that are associated with asthma and specifically with eosinophils. Using PubMed and Web of Science with keywords “asthma,” “eosinophils,” and “miRNA,” we focused on miRNAs that showed differential expression in individuals with asthma compared to healthy controls.12,13 Considering availability with some miRNA primers from suppliers, we selected four key miRNAs for our study: miR-185-5p, miR-146b-5p, miR-320b, and miR-21-5p. To identify the potential target genes of these selected miRNAs, bioinformatics resources, such as TargetScan (release 8.0) and miRDB, were utilized.20,21 These tools allowed us to examine specific gene sequences, like ENSG00000173198.6 for miR-185-5p, aiming to pinpoint targets relevant to asthma pathology and eosinophil function.

We opted not to analyze gene targets for miR-320b due to significant challenges. The current literature offers only a limited number of experimentally validated miRNA targets, making it difficult to confidently identify and analyze potential targets, especially within the specific context of asthma and eosinophil function. This challenge is further intensified by the limited sequence complementarity between animal miRNAs and their targets, meaning miRNA-target interactions in animals rely on a shorter sequence match. In these interactions, miRNAs do not perfectly match across their entire length with the sequences on their target mRNAs.21–26 This complexity complicates predictions of which mRNAs a given miRNA will regulate and significantly hampers the development of highly specific target prediction models. Consequently, for miR-320b, bioinformatics tools failed to identify any potential gene targets.

cDNA Synthesis and Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)

Extracted RNA from eosinophils and blood plasma was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and miRNA stem-loop primers from the TaqMan MicroRNA Assays for miR-185-5p (ID: 002271), miR-146b-5p (ID: 474220_mat), miR-320b (ID: 002844), miR-21-5p (ID: 000397), and the spike-in control cel-miR-39 (ID: 000200), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

Each RT reaction was performed in a volume of 15 µL, including 5 ng of total RNA. The reaction setup included 7 µL of the RT reaction mix (100mM dNTPs (with dTTP), MultiScribe™ Reverse Transcriptase, 50 U/µL, Reverse Transcription Buffer, RNase Inhibitor, 20 U/µL, Nuclease-free Water), 5 µL of total RNA, and 3 µL of the miRNA-specific RT primers. The RT reaction conditions were set as follows: incubation at 16 °C for 30 minutes, followed by 42 °C for 30 minutes, and at 85 °C for 5 minutes. The cDNA products are immediately utilized for subsequent research stages.

The miRNA abundance was assessed using RT-qPCR in a 20 µL reaction volume that included 2× TaqMan Universal Master Mix II, no UNG (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), the respective 20× TaqMan MicroRNA Assay (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), 1.33 µL of the cDNA template, and RNase-free water. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate to ensure data accuracy. The qPCR cycling conditions were as follows: enzyme activation at 95 °C for 10 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 seconds and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 minute on the 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied BiosystemsTM, Foster City, CA, USA).

TaqMan Array Analysis of miRNA Target Genes

For the analysis of miRNA target genes related to eosinophil functions in asthma, our study utilized TaqMan Array custom format plates (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Endogenous controls including 18S, GAPDH, GUSB, and HPRT1 were employed to ensure the reliability of our gene expression data. The target genes for miR-185-5p were PRG2 (Assay ID: Hs00794928_m1) and CYSLTR1 (Assay ID: Hs00272624_s1), for miR-146b-5p was RANTES (Assay ID: Hs00982282_m1), and for miR-21-5p were FOXO3 (Assay ID: Hs00818121_m1) and IL1B (Assay ID: Hs01555410_m1). RNA extracted from eosinophil samples underwent cDNA synthesis using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The resulting cDNA was then mixed with TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and applied to the wells of the TaqMan Array Plate for each specified target gene. Quantification of miRNA target genes was performed on the 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System. The thermal cycling protocol included an enzyme activation at 95°C for 20 seconds, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 3 seconds and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 seconds.

Gene Expression

miRNA expression in eosinophils and plasma was normalized against the expression of all detected miRNAs using a global normalization approach. For blood plasma samples, an additional technical normalization step was performed using the exogenous spike-in control cel-miR-39 to minimize variability associated with RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qPCR amplification efficiency. The expression of miRNA target genes in eosinophil subtypes was normalized against the endogenous reference genes 18S, GAPDH, GUSB, and HPRT1, which served as internal controls for correction of sample-to-sample variation. Data normalization and analysis were conducted using GenEx software version 7.0 (MultiD Analysis AB, Gothenburg, Sweden). Subsequently, relative expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method, in which the mean normalized ΔCt value of the HS group was applied as the calibrator. Results are presented as log2,2−ΔΔCt representing relative upregulation or downregulation in comparison with the HS group.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software version 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As most variables did not follow a normal distribution, non-parametric tests were applied. Differences between two independent study groups for clinical characteristics were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Since HS served as the calibrator group for ΔΔCt normalization, comparisons of miRNA and gene expression values in AA patients against the HS reference level (zero line) were performed using the one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Paired biological comparisons, including iEOS-like versus rEOS-like cells within the same subject and V1 versus V2 comparisons within the same subject, were assessed using the Wilcoxon matched-pair signed-rank test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In addition, exploratory associations between BAC-induced changes in miRNA expression in eosinophil subtypes and plasma and BAC-induced changes in FEV1% predicted were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation test.

Results

Study Subject Characteristics

Our study included 13 AA and 16 HS. The AA group exhibited significantly higher blood eosinophil count, elevated FeNO levels, and increased total IgE levels, compared to the lower values observed in the HS group, p < 0.05. While no substantial differences in FEV1 (L) measurements were found initially, the AA group showed a lower percentage of predicted FEV1, compared to HS, p < 0.05. Following BAC, there was a marked decrease in FEV1 for the AA group, both in absolute terms and as a percentage of the predicted value, highlighting the allergen’s adverse effect on their respiratory function. Relative expression of miR-185-5p, miR-146b-5p, miR-320b, and miR-21-5p was evaluated in blood iEOS-like and rEOS-like cells from AA patients before BAC (V1) and 24 h after challenge (V2), with expression values presented as fold changes relative to HS (Figure 2).

Analysis revealed that miR-185-5p expression in both iEOS-like and rEOS-like cells from AA patients was elevated relative to the HS reference level, showing 4.29 ± 0.49-fold and 2.52 ± 0.31-fold increases, respectively, p < 0.05. Additionally, miR-185-5p expression was higher in iEOS-like cells compared to rEOS-like cells within the AA group, p < 0.05.

For miR-146b-5p, increased expression was also observed in both iEOS-like and rEOS-like cells, with iEOS-like cells showing a 1.26 ± 0.38-fold increase and rEOS-like cells a slightly lower 0.75 ± 0.28-fold increase relative to the HS reference level, p < 0.05.

Furthermore, miR-320b expression in iEOS-like cells from AA patients was increased by 1.48 ± 0.21-fold relative to the HS reference level, p < 0.05. Additionally, this expression was also higher compared to rEOS-like cells within the AA group, p < 0.05, underscoring a subtype-specific expression pattern.

Conversely, miR-21-5p expression in iEOS-like cells from AA patients exhibited a notable −1.02 ± 0.19-fold decrease relative to the HS reference level, p < 0.05. This reduction was more pronounced when compared with rEOS-like cells in the AA group, p < 0.05, highlighting differential regulation within eosinophil subtypes. However, no significant differences were observed in miR-21-5p and miR-320b expression in rEOS-like cells relative to the HS reference level at V1, p > 0.05.

After BAC, miR-185-5p expression did not change in both eosinophil subtypes, but remained elevated relative to the HS reference level, with a 4.57 ± 0.42-fold increase in iEOS-like cells and a 2.72 ± 0.36-fold increase in rEOS-like cells, p < 0.05. Furthermore, 24 h after BAC, the distinction between eosinophil subtypes persisted, with miR-185-5p expression remaining significantly higher in iEOS-like cells compared to rEOS-like cells within the AA group, p < 0.05.

In the case of miR-146b-5p, a differential pattern was observed after BAC. miR-146b-5p expression in iEOS-like cells decreased to −1.24 ± 0.33-fold at V2, showing lower expression relative to the HS reference level and a significant reduction compared with iEOS-like cells at V1, p < 0.05. Conversely, in rEOS-like cells, miR-146b-5p expression increased from V1 to V2, reaching a 1.46 ± 0.18-fold increase, which was higher than the HS reference level, p < 0.05. Additionally, a difference between the eosinophil subtypes emerged within the AA group, with miR-146b-5p expression being higher in rEOS-like cells compared to iEOS-like cells at V2, p < 0.05. This variance underscores the distinct regulatory adaptations to allergen exposure that may be inherent to each subtype.

miR-320b expression in eosinophil subtypes from AA patients showed distinct patterns after BAC. In iEOS-like cells, miR-320b expression remained upregulated relative to the HS reference level, showing a 1.30 ± 0.25-fold increase at V2, p < 0.05. In contrast, rEOS-like cells demonstrated a significant increase in miR-320b expression at V2, reaching a 0.55 ± 0.19-fold increase relative to the HS reference level, p < 0.05. Moreover, within the AA group, the difference in miR-320b expression between the eosinophil subtypes persisted at V2, with iEOS-like cells exhibiting higher expression than rEOS-like cells, p < 0.05.

For miR-21-5p, in the AA group, the pattern observed before BAC remained consistent after BAC. In iEOS-like cells, miR-21-5p expression was decreased at V2, showing a −1.24 ± 0.30-fold change relative to the HS reference level, p < 0.05. In contrast, rEOS-like cells did not show a significant difference in miR-21-5p expression at V2, although there was a slight 0.40 ± 0.43-fold increase relative to the HS reference level, p > 0.05.

Relative Plasma miRNA Expression in Allergic Asthma Patients Before and After Bronchial Allergen Challenge

Relative plasma expression of miR-185-5p, miR-146b-5p, miR-320b, and miR-21-5p was evaluated in AA patients before BAC (V1) and 24 h after challenge (V2), with expression values presented as fold changes relative to HS (Figure 3).

Relative plasma miRNA expression of miR-185-5p, miR-146b-5p, miR-320b, and miR-21-5p in AA patients before (V1) and 24 h after (V2) BAC, presented as log2 fold changes relative to the HS reference level (0 after normalization).

Notes: AA patients, n = 11; HS, n = 16. Asterisk (*) indicates p < 0.05 versus the HS reference level. Horizontal brackets indicate p < 0.05 for paired comparisons between V1 and V2 within the AA group. Results are presented as mean ± standard error. Statistical analysis: one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test and Wilcoxon matched-pair signed-rank test.

Abbreviations: AA, allergic asthma; BAC, bronchial allergen challenge; HS, healthy subjects; V1, visit 1 (before BAC); V2, visit 2 (24 h after BAC).

Analysis of plasma samples from AA patients revealed significant differences in miRNA expression relative to the HS reference level. Specifically, miR-185-5p plasma levels were increased at V1, showing a 3.52 ± 0.65-fold increase, p < 0.05. Although a slight decrease was observed at V2, miR-185-5p levels remained significantly higher than the HS reference level, with a 2.45 ± 0.88-fold increase, p < 0.05, indicating sustained elevation despite the allergen challenge.

miR-146b-5p plasma levels in the AA group were significantly higher at both time points relative to the HS reference level, with a 2.07 ± 0.30-fold increase at V1 and a 2.08 ± 0.23-fold increase at V2, p < 0.05; however, no significant difference between visits was observed.

For miR-320b, plasma levels at V1 were elevated relative to the HS reference level, showing a 5.93 ± 1.33-fold increase (p < 0.05). However, after BAC, miR-320b levels showed a marked decrease compared with V1 and were no longer significantly different from the HS reference level at V2, p > 0.05.

Similarly, miR-21-5p plasma levels also exhibited a significant change. At V1, plasma miR-21-5p levels in AA patients were significantly increased relative to the HS reference level, showing a 6.23 ± 1.39-fold increase, p < 0.05. After BAC, miR-21-5p levels significantly decreased compared with V1 and were no longer significantly different from the HS reference level, p > 0.05, highlighting a pronounced BAC-associated reduction in circulating expression.

Correlation Between Bronchial Allergen Challenge-Induced miRNA Changes and Lung Function Decline

To investigate whether BAC-induced molecular alterations were associated with the magnitude of airway functional impairment, exploratory Spearman correlation analyses were performed between changes in miRNA expression in eosinophil subtypes and plasma and changes in FEV1% predicted. No statistically significant correlations were observed (p > 0.05). However, a moderate inverse trend was observed between changes in iEOS-like miR-146b-5p expression and FEV1% predicted; although, this association did not reach statistical significance (r = −0.55, p = 0.085).

Relative Expression of Eosinophil Subtype-Specific miRNA Target Genes Before and After Bronchial Allergen Challenge

To identify potential target genes of the selected miRNAs, bioinformatics tools were utilized. Based on this analysis, several genes were selected for further investigation to identify targets relevant to asthma pathology and eosinophil function. Relative expression of PRG2, CYSTLR1, FOXO3, IL-1β, and RANTES was evaluated in iEOS-like and rEOS-like cells from AA patients before BAC (V1) and 24 h after challenge (V2), with expression values presented as fold changes relative to HS (Figure 4). As this was a pilot study, the analysis provides preliminary insights into target gene expression.

Relative target gene expression in blood iEOS-like and rEOS-like cells from AA patients before (V1) and 24 h after (V2) BAC, presented as log2 fold changes relative to the HS reference level (0 after normalization).

Notes: AA patients, n = 6; HS, n = 6. Asterisk (*) indicates p < 0.05 versus the HS reference level. Horizontal brackets indicate p < 0.05 for paired comparisons between eosinophil subtypes or between visits within the AA group. Results are presented as mean ± standard error. Statistical analysis: one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test and Wilcoxon matched-pair signed-rank test.

Abbreviations: AA, allergic asthma; BAC, bronchial allergen challenge; HS, healthy subjects; iEOS-like cells, inflammatory-like eosinophil cells; rEOS-like cells, resident-like eosinophil cells; V1, visit 1 (before BAC); V2, visit 2 (24 h after BAC).

We found that in AA patients, the expression of the PRG2 gene, a potential target of miR-185-5p, was increased by 1.79 ± 0.38-fold in iEOS-like cells relative to the HS reference level, p < 0.05. In contrast, PRG2 expression was decreased by −2.78 ± 0.40-fold in rEOS-like cells relative to the HS reference level, p < 0.05. After BAC, PRG2 expression in iEOS-like cells decreased, showing a −1.47 ± 0.60-fold reduction relative to the HS reference level, p < 0.05. Additionally, in rEOS-like cells, PRG2 expression increased compared to V1 but remained lower than the HS reference level, showing a −2.18 ± 0.27-fold decrease, p < 0.05.

For the miR-185-5p target gene CYSTLR1, no significant differences in expression were observed between eosinophil subtypes or between visits in AA patients. However, CYSTLR1 expression remained consistently lower relative to the HS reference level, with the following reductions: in iEOS-like cells at V1, −1.07 ± 0.36-fold; in rEOS-like cells at V1, −1.56 ± 0.46-fold; in iEOS-like cells at V2, −1.11 ± 0.33-fold; and in rEOS-like cells at V2, −0.82 ± 0.19-fold, p < 0.05.

Similarly, FOXO3, a target of miR-21-5p, showed reduced expression across both eosinophil subtypes and both visits in AA patients. No differences between eosinophil subtypes were observed within the AA group. The reductions were as follows: in iEOS-like cells at V1, −3.24 ± 0.59-fold; in rEOS-like cells at V1, −2.80 ± 0.38-fold; in iEOS-like cells at V2, −3.14 ± 0.46-fold; and in rEOS-like cells at V2, −3.22 ± 0.68-fold, all relative to the HS reference level, p < 0.05.

IL1B, another potential target of miR-21-5p, showed a −1.85 ± 0.33-fold decreased expression solely in rEOS-like cells of AA patients relative to the HS reference level, p < 0.05. In contrast, IL1B expression in iEOS-like cells did not differ from the HS reference level, indicating a difference between eosinophil subtypes within the AA group, p < 0.05. However, after BAC, IL1B expression in iEOS-like cells decreased significantly from V1, showing a −1.58 ± 0.21-fold decrease, p < 0.05. After BAC, IL1B expression in rEOS-like cells did not differ from the HS reference level.

Lastly, expression changes of RANTES, a potential miR-146b-5p target, were examined. A notable decrease was observed in both iEOS-like and rEOS-like cells of the AA group at V1, showing −1.30 ± 0.65-fold and −3.74 ± 0.37-fold reductions, respectively, relative to the HS reference level, p < 0.05. At V2, the decrease in RANTES expression persisted in both subtypes, with iEOS-like cells showing a −1.89 ± 0.33-fold reduction and rEOS-like cells showing a −3.17 ± 0.34-fold reduction relative to the HS reference level, p < 0.05. This reduction in RANTES gene expression was more pronounced in rEOS-like cells than in iEOS-like cells within the AA group at both V1 and V2, p < 0.05.

Discussion

miRNAs are important post-transcriptional regulators of gene expression involved in inflammatory and immune pathways in asthma.27 Previous studies have shown that more than 100 miRNAs are differentially expressed in asthma and may serve as potential biomarkers,28 while allergen-driven asthma is also associated with altered circulating miRNA profiles.29 In addition, 24 miRNAs have been reported to be differentially expressed in eosinophils from asthmatic patients compared to healthy controls.13 Understanding miRNA expression in blood eosinophils during AA can provide important insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of this disease. However, miRNA expression in circulating eosinophil subtypes remains poorly characterized. Therefore, in this study we characterized miRNA expression changes characteristic of blood eosinophil subtypes in AA, highlighting differences between iEOS-like and rEOS-like cells and their alterations after BAC, which mimics acute asthma exacerbation. In addition, circulating miRNAs were detected in the plasma of AA patients and showed differences relative to HS. Finally, we evaluated the expression of selected asthma-related target genes of the investigated miRNAs in eosinophil subtypes. Taken together, these findings suggest that eosinophil-associated miRNA regulation in AA is subtype-dependent and dynamically influenced by allergen exposure. The subtype-dependent differences were most evident for miR-185-5p and miR-320b, which were higher in iEOS-like than rEOS-like cells, for miR-21-5p, which was reduced mainly in iEOS-like cells, and for miR-146b-5p, which showed opposite changes between the two eosinophil subtypes after BAC.

miR-185-5p has been associated with asthma pathogenesis through regulation of Th1/Th2 imbalance, inflammatory cytokine production, periostin secretion, and airway smooth muscle contraction.14,30,31 Our findings revealed significant overexpression of miR-185-5p in both blood eosinophil subtype-like cells in asthma, with higher expression in iEOS-like cells compared to rEOS-like cells. This persistent predominance in iEOS-like cells suggests that miR-185-5p may be more closely related to the inflammatory eosinophil phenotype than to the resident-like phenotype. Notably, BAC had no additional effect on miR-185-5p expression in either eosinophil subtype. Similarly, miR-185-5p levels in plasma were elevated in AA patients compared to HS before and after BAC. BAC had no additional effect that may be linked to its consistent expression in eosinophil subtypes, where it also remained unchanged despite allergen challenge. The observed post-BAC stability of miR-185-5p in both eosinophils and plasma could primarily be supported by the unchanged miR-185-5p expression observed in eosinophil subtypes. However, an additional contributing factor may be rapid kinetics of miRNA production and secretion,32 characterized by dynamic fluctuations that can occur within hours of an inflammatory trigger. Since blood samples were collected 24 hours post-BAC, any transient changes in miR-185-5p levels may have normalized by this time point, leading to the observed stability.

miR-146b-5p is linked to asthma-associated eosinophilic inflammation and has been reported to be elevated both in eosinophils and in airway structural cells during asthma.13,18 In agreement with these observations, in our study both iEOS-like and rEOS-like cells from AA patients exhibited overexpression of miR-146b-5p before BAC compared to HS. This may be relevant because miR-146 family members are known to regulate inflammatory signaling, including IRAK/TRAF6- and NF-κB-related pathways involved in cytokine production.33–37 Interestingly, the mature products of miR-146a and miR-146b differ by only two nucleotides in their 3′ regions, yet they share functional similarities. miR-146b can also repress the reporter constructs of the untranslated regions of IRAK1 and TRAF6, indicating its potential to control similar inflammatory pathways.33 In this context, miR-146b-5p plays a significant role in mitigating excessive inflammation and consequent immunopathology and may contribute to the regulation of inflammatory responses in asthma, although its therapeutic relevance requires further investigation. In line with this regulatory role, after BAC, miR-146b-5p expression increased in rEOS-like cells but significantly decreased in iEOS-like cells. This opposite direction of change represents the clearest BAC-induced differential response between eosinophil subtypes in our study. This differential response may be related to the distinct roles these cells play in the immune system. rEOS-like cells, which are primarily involved in maintaining local homeostasis, upregulate miR-146b-5p to suppress inflammatory cytokines and prevent excessive tissue damage. In contrast, iEOS-like cells are actively engaged in inflammatory responses, and the decrease in miR-146b-5p expression following BAC treatment likely reflects their need to potentiate the inflammatory reaction during asthma exacerbations. This downregulation in iEOS-like cells enables a robust immune response, crucial for immediate defence, even at the cost of increased inflammation.

In plasma, we demonstrated that miR-146b-5p levels are elevated in asthma patients, aligning with studies in murine models that have reported increased miR-146b-5p levels in both serum and lung samples.38 Additionally, several studies have observed increased miR-146b-5p levels in murine models of allergic inflammation and in asthma patients.13,39,40 These findings further support its role as a potential asthma biomarker, particularly given its association with eosinophilic activity in the disease. After BAC, miR-146b-5p levels in asthma patients blood remained elevated. These findings agree with P. Cay et al, who identified increased miR-146b-5p levels in the serum of mice exposed to house dust mite.38

Information about the role of miR-320b in asthma remains limited; however, it has been found that miR-320b might be part of a panel of circulating miRNAs that could serve as biomarkers for asthma.13 This aligns with our findings, where plasma levels of miR-320b were higher in the asthma group compared to HS. This increase may be linked to iEOS-like cells, as miR-320b expression in this subtype was significantly higher compared to rEOS-like cells. After BAC, plasma miR-320b levels significantly decreased to values comparable with HS. This reduction may partly reflect the previously observed decrease of circulating iEOS after allergen challenge.5 At the cellular level, miR-320b expression remained elevated in iEOS-like cells, whereas its expression increased in rEOS-like cells after BAC. Such findings suggest that miR-320b may participate in dynamic eosinophil adaptation during allergen exposure rather than represent a stable chronic inflammatory marker. Notably, increased expression of miR-320 family members has been reported in patients with complete asthma remission compared with persistent asthma, suggesting potential anti-inflammatory or disease-resolving properties.41 This may indicate that the post-BAC redistribution of miR-320b between plasma and eosinophil subtypes reflects its involvement in regulatory processes associated with eosinophil trafficking and inflammatory resolution.

miR-21-5p is involved in the regulation of inflammatory signaling, immune responses, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition, thereby contributing to airway remodeling processes relevant to asthma pathogenesis.42–44 Also, exosome-derived miR-21-5p might differentiate asthma severity and is related to type 2 high atopic asthma.44 In our study, decreased miR-21-5p expression was observed selectively in iEOS-like cells, whereas rEOS-like cells showed no significant difference relative to HS, indicating another subtype-dependent miRNA pattern in AA. This selective reduction in the more inflammation-associated iEOS-like subtype may suggest altered regulatory control of inflammatory signaling in these cells. In contrast, plasma miR-21-5p levels were elevated before BAC but decreased to values comparable with HS after allergen challenge. Since no parallel increase was observed in eosinophil subtypes, these findings suggest that circulating miR-21-5p is not solely derived from blood eosinophils and may reflect a broader systemic response involving additional inflammatory or structural airway cells. To obtain preliminary insight into the possible functional relevance of the investigated miRNAs, we additionally evaluated the expression of selected asthma-related target genes in eosinophil subtypes. Overall, the target gene analysis suggested that altered miRNA expression was accompanied by dysregulation of several genes involved in eosinophil activation, bronchoconstrictive signaling, chemotaxis, and inflammatory control, although these relationships were not uniformly direct. The subtype-related differences in PRG2, IL1B, and RANTES expression further support that iEOS-like and rEOS-like cells respond differently at both miRNA and gene-expression levels.

For miR-185-5p, PRG2 and CYSTLR1 were selected as potential asthma-related target genes. PRG2, encoding eosinophil major basic protein, showed a subtype-dependent pattern, being reduced in rEOS-like cells before BAC and in iEOS-like cells after BAC. In parallel, CYSTLR1 expression was downregulated in both eosinophil subtypes without an additional BAC effect. These findings may suggest that increased miR-185-5p expression is accompanied by suppression of selected pro-inflammatory eosinophil effector pathways.

Similarly, persistent downregulation of RANTES, a chemotactic mediator strongly associated with eosinophilic airway inflammation, was observed in both eosinophil subtypes. This pattern was generally consistent with the elevated miR-146b-5p expression detected in eosinophils, supporting the possibility that miR-146b-5p participates in the control of eosinophil-associated chemokine signaling. However, because RANTES remained suppressed despite the post-BAC decrease of miR-146b-5p in iEOS-like cells, additional regulatory mechanisms are likely involved.

In the case of miR-21-5p, both FOXO3 and IL-1β also demonstrated reduced expression, although not in a completely parallel manner with miR-21-5p itself. FOXO3 was decreased in both eosinophil subtypes, whereas IL-1β showed subtype- and challenge-dependent downregulation. These non-uniform relationships indicate that the observed target gene changes cannot be explained solely by individual miRNA shifts and likely reflect broader inflammatory regulatory networks active in AA.

This study has several potential limitations. Firstly, the relatively small sample size (13 AA patients and 16 healthy subjects) restricts the generalizability of the results. Future studies with larger cohorts are necessary to validate the findings and ensure their applicability to a broader population. Secondly, the study focused on a limited number of miRNAs, potentially overlooking other significant miRNAs involved in eosinophil regulation and asthma pathogenesis. A more comprehensive miRNA profiling using, for example, next-generation sequencing, could provide a more complete understanding. Thirdly, the reliance on computational predictions for miRNA target genes may not fully capture the complexity of miRNA-mRNA interactions, necessitating experimental validation to confirm these targets’ biological relevance. Lastly, although the study measured miRNA expression before and after 24 hours after BAC, providing some temporal data, it does not capture long-term changes and trends in miRNA expression and asthma progression. Furthermore, measuring miRNA expression at shorter time intervals is essential to better understand the immediate and dynamic changes in miRNA regulation, which may provide critical insights into early response mechanisms. Additionally, gene expression does not always reflect protein levels, which also need to be measured. Therefore, longitudinal studies following patients over extended periods are needed to fully understand the dynamics of miRNA expression in relation to asthma exacerbations and remissions.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight the distinct roles of miRNAs in AA and their potential as complementary tools for understanding disease mechanisms. The elevation of miR-185-5p and miR-320b in iEOS-like cells compared to rEOS-like cells suggests that these miRNAs may serve as biomarkers for distinguishing eosinophil subtypes. Moreover, BAC reduced miR-146b-5p expression in iEOS-like cells but increases in rEOS-like cells. Additionally, expression of miR-185-5p, miR-146b-5p, miR-320b, and miR-21-5p in plasma indicate their potential utility in characterizing AA, although further validation is needed to distinguish disease-related changes from normal biological variability. In particular, miR-185-5p and miR-146b-5p demonstrate stability post-BAC, suggesting their potential to reflect chronic inflammation rather than acute inflammatory responses. These findings may contribute to the development of more personalized and effective asthma management strategies, refining our approach to managing this chronic respiratory disorder.
