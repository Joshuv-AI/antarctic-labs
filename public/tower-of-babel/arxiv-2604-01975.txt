Efficient generation and explicit dimensionality of Lie group-equivariant and permutation-invariant bases
Eloïse Barthelemy, Geneviève Dusson, Camille Hernandez, Liwei Zhang

Abstract
In this article, we propose a practical construction of Lie group-equivariant and permutation-invariant functions of N {N} variables from the knowledge of a one-particle basis that is stable with respect to the group action. The construction is generic for any linear Lie group and relies on building a matrix constructed from the Lie algebra whose kernel is spanned by a group-equivariant and permutation-invariant basis. In particular, this construction does not require the knowledge of Clebsch–Gordan coefficients and instead directly builds generalized Clebsch–Gordan coefficients. For specific groups such as S ​ O ​ ( 3 ) SO(3) and S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) , we exploit the Lie algebra structure to simplify the matrix, which then allows us to derive an explicit formula for the exact dimension of the group-equivariant and permutation-invariant space. Numerical simulations are provided to show that the proposed method scales linearly instead of exponentially for existing methods in the literature. We also show that for large values of N {N} , the number of rotation-equivariant and permutation-invariant basis functions is of a comparable order as the number of permutation-invariant basis functions, while pre-asymptotically, a large gain can be achieved by explicitly enforcing rotation-equivariance on top of permutation-invariance.

Introduction

Symmetric functions are fundamental across diverse scientific fields, from physics and chemistry to materials science. They are especially crucial for studying interacting particle systems, where identical particles give rise to permutation-invariant functions.
Often an extra symmetry arises from the considered model, which can be mathematically formalized as being equivariant with respect to a specific Lie group.
This can be e.g.
O(3)
in atomistic modeling,
SO^{+}(1,3)
in high-energy physics, or
SU(2N)
in quantum mechanics.
When approximating such functions, constructing a parametrization that respects the inherent symmetries is essential. This approach typically reduces the number of required parameters, making the approximation more data-efficient.

For
SO(3)
and
O(3)
, many parametrizations have been recently proposed in chemistry and materials science to obtain accurate approximations of symmetric functions of interest. They appear in e.g. the construction of interatomic potentials
[
23
,
7
,
6
,
5
]
, Hamiltonians
[
31
,
15
,
33
,
24
,
21
]
, or density matrices
[
27
,
13
,
30
]
.
Most of these works build on the construction of so-called many-body equivariant features which consist of linearly independent basis functions equivariant
under specific group representations.
When considering functions of
{N}
variables satisfying rotation-equivariance and no permutation-invariance, linearly independent equivariant features correspond to so-called generalized Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, already defined in 1962
[
29
, Chapter 2]
. A practical method for computing these coefficients was e.g. proposed in
[
8
]
related to the Atomic Cluster Expansion (ACE).
However, an extra difficulty arises when permutation-invariance is enforced. For example, in ACE
[
8
]
, the systematic construction of generalized Clebsch–Gordan coefficients involves evaluating a Gramian to remove linear dependencies,
which requires performing a sum over permutations leading to an exploding cost when the number of variables
{N}
grows. Alternative methods have been proposed such as
[
18
,
11
,
10
]
for
SO(3)/SU(2)
and
[
4
]
in the larger framework of reductive Lie groups but to the best of our knowledge, the computational cost always scales exponentially with respect to the number of particles
{N}
and therefore the construction becomes costly and even unfeasible for large particle numbers.
In general, the equivariance can be similarly dealt with for Lie groups, which is our motivation for the focus of this work, e.g. other symmetries and computation of generalized Clebsch–Gordan coefficients beyond
SO(3)
, including the group
SO^{+}(1,3)

[
17
]
appearing in particle physics.

In this article, we propose a systematic construction of generalized Clebsch–Gordan coefficients (also called coupling coefficients) both for group-equivariant (GE) and GE-Permutation-Invariant (GE-PI) functions based on the Lie algebra of the considered Lie group.
To do so, we build, similarly to
[
4
]
, a linear system that has to be satisfied by the coupling coefficients. Differentiating this linear system with respect to the generators of the group gives rise to another, way simpler linear system involving the Lie algebra: coupling coefficients are in one-to-one correspondence with the basis of a matrix kernel (see Proposition
2.4
and Theorem
2.14
).
When considering only the group-equivariance, the method leads to similar results as what was obtained in
[
8
,
4
]
. But when permutation-invariance is also enforced, the linear system can be expressed independently of permutations, which significantly reduces the computational cost.
Note that, compared to other methods, such as
[
18
]
, we do not need Clebsch–Gordan coefficients as a prerequisite to compute generalized Clebsch–Gordan coefficients.

For the groups
SO(3)
and
SU(2)
, the Lie algebra has a specific structure leading to a particularly sparse matrix (see Figure
1
) for which we need to find the kernel.
As a byproduct, we obtain the dimensionality of the GE spaces and GE-PI spaces for generic representations, similarly to
[
32
,
26
,
19
]
in the case of identical spin systems.
We are then able to provide asymptotic estimates of GE and GE-PI basis dimensions, from which we show that asymptotically, the number of PI basis functions is comparable to the number of GE-PI functions, completing the theoretical approximation result
[
2
]
restricted to the PI case. However preasymptotically, which is of practical interest, the number of PI and GE functions is way larger than the number of GE-PI functions.

The outline of the article is as follows.
In section
2
we present two methods for obtaining a basis of GE and GE-PI functions: a direct and a recursive one. We then apply the method to
SO(3)
and
SU(2)
groups and extend it to
O(3)
. We provide explicit dimensionality as well as asymptotics in this case in section
3
. We then provide numerical results in section
4
, showcasing that the proposed method is more efficient than existing methods from the literature.
We also provide an analysis and comparison of GE and GE-PI dimensionalities.
Finally we present concluding remarks in section
5

and gather the proofs of the main results in section
6
.
To facilitate the reading of the article, we add a list of notation in Appendix
A
.

2
Generating equivariant bases using the Lie Algebra

In this section, we provide preliminary definitions and a set of assumptions before determining group-equivariant basis functions via
the kernel of a specific matrix
based on the Lie algebra of the group.

2.1
Preliminary

Let
G
be a linear connected
Lie group and denote by
e
its neutral element. Let
\mathbb{F}
be a vector space over a field
\mathbb{K}
, typically
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
in the examples below.
Let
\rho
be a continuous representation of
G
of dimension
K
on
\mathbb{K}
.
Let
\Omega
be a spatial domain and let
\cdot
be a group action for
G
on
\Omega
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\forall{\bm{r}}\in\Omega,g\in G,&g\cdot{\bm{r}}\in\Omega\\
\forall{\bm{r}}\in\Omega,&e\cdot{\bm{r}}={\bm{r}}\\
\forall g_{1},g_{2}\in G,&(g_{1}g_{2})\cdot{\bm{r}}=g_{1}\cdot(g_{2}\cdot{\bm{r}}).\end{array}\right.

For any
{N}\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the corresponding group action over
\Omega^{N}
, which is still denoted by
\cdot
,
as

\forall{\bm{R}}=({\bm{r}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{r}}_{N})\in\Omega^{N},\;g\in G,\quad g\cdot{\bm{R}}=(g\cdot{\bm{r}}_{1},\ldots,g\cdot{\bm{r}}_{N}).

We now define group-equivariant (GE) and group-equivariant permutation invariant (GE-PI) functions.

Definition 2.1

(Group-equivariant (GE) function)
.

A function
F:\Omega^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{K}
is group-equivariant for the representation
\rho
if

\forall{\bm{R}}\in\Omega^{N},\;g\in G,\quad F(g\cdot{\bm{R}})=\rho(g)\;F({\bm{R}}).

(2.1)

Definition 2.2

(Group-equivariant and permutation invariant (GE-PI) function)
.

A function
F:\Omega^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{K}
is group-equivariant for the representation
\rho

and permutation-invariant if

\forall{\bm{R}}\in\Omega^{N},\;g\in G,\;\sigma\in S_{N},\quad F(g\cdot{\bm{R}}_{\sigma})=\rho(g)\;F({\bm{R}}),

(2.2)

where
S_{N}
denotes the symmetric group of degree
{N}
, and

\forall{\bm{R}}=({\bm{r}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{r}}_{N})\in\Omega^{N},\quad{\bm{R}}_{\sigma}=({\bm{r}}_{\sigma_{1}},\ldots,{\bm{r}}_{\sigma_{N}}).

Remark 2.3
.

In some context, e.g. for functions defined on multisets, the permutation invariance of the functions is hidden in the domain of definition. Precisely, a function
F:\textup{MS}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{F}^{K}
satisfying

\forall{\bm{R}}\in\textup{MS}(\Omega),\;g\in G,\quad F(g\cdot{\bm{R}})=\rho(g)\;F({\bm{R}}),

for the representation
\rho
with dimension
K
is also said to be GE-PI, where
\textup{MS}(\Omega)
is the union of multisets of
\Omega
with arbitrary multiplicity, that is

\textup{MS}(\Omega)=\bigcup_{n=1}^{+\infty}\Big\{\left\{{\bm{r}}_{1},{\bm{r}}_{2},\ldots,{\bm{r}}_{n}\right\},{\bm{r}}_{1},{\bm{r}}_{2},\ldots,{\bm{r}}_{n}\in\Omega\Big\}.

The aim of this manuscript is to provide an efficient construction of a basis spanning the space (or a finite-dimensional subspace thereof) of the above GE and GE-PI functions, and to understand the basic mathematical properties of the resulting space.
We first define one-particle basis functions. Given a spatial domain
\Omega
, we consider functions defined on
\Omega
in a space endowed with a scalar product denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, typically
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
, that satisfy (
2.1
) or (
2.2
).
The one-particle function space is indexed by parameter
l
in an index set
\mathcal{L}
,
which is typically countable and totally ordered (e.g.
\mathbb{N}_{0}
for the group
SO(3)
, see Section
3.2
for more examples),
and is stable with respect to the considered group action.
That is, for
l\in\mathcal{L}
, we consider

V^{l}={\rm Span}\{\phi^{l}_{m},\;m\in\mathcal{M}_{l}\},

where
\mathcal{M}_{l}
denotes a finite, totally ordered, index set depending on
l
, typically
\{1,2,\ldots,|{\mathcal{M}_{l}}|\}
.
For
m\in\mathcal{M}_{l}
,
\phi^{l}_{m}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{F}

are one-particle basis functions and assumed for simplicity to be orthonormal, i.e.,

\forall l,l^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L},\;m,m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{l},\quad\langle\phi^{l}_{m},\phi^{l^{\prime}}_{m^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{ll^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}.

(2.3)

Since
V^{l}
is stable with respect to the group action, we have

\forall g\in G,\quad{\bm{r}}\mapsto\phi^{l}_{m}(g\cdot{\bm{r}})\in V^{l}.

This implies that there exists a representation of
G
of dimension
|{\mathcal{M}_{l}}|
, denoted by
\rho^{l}
, such that

\forall g\in G,\;{\bm{r}}\in\Omega,\quad\phi^{l}_{m}(g\cdot{\bm{r}})=\sum_{m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{l}}\rho^{l}_{m,m^{\prime}}(g)\phi^{l}_{m^{\prime}}({\bm{r}}).

(2.4)

Indeed, by the uniqueness of the coordinates in the basis

\{\phi^{l}_{m}\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{l}}
, we have

\rho^{l}(e)=I_{\mathcal{M}_{l}}
, and

\forall g_{1},g_{2}\in G,\quad\rho^{l}(g_{1}g_{2})=\rho^{l}(g_{1})\rho^{l}(g_{2}).

We call
\{\phi^{l}_{m}\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{l}}
a compatible basis of
V^{l}
corresponding to the representation
\rho^{l}
.

Then, for a given vector
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
, we restrict ourselves to consider the tensor product space

V^{{\bm{l}}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}V^{l_{i}},

and we aim to characterize a basis of GE and GE-PI functions, whose components are in this space.
An orthonormal basis for the space
V^{{\bm{l}}}
is

\left\{\phi^{\bm{l}}_{\bm{m}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\phi^{l_{i}}_{m_{i}},\quad{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}\right\},\quad\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}:=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{M}_{l_{i}},

(2.5)

where the corresponding scalar product on
\Omega^{N}
is defined as

\forall\phi^{\bm{l}}_{\bm{m}}\in V^{\bm{l}},\;\phi^{{\bm{l}}^{\prime}}_{{\bm{m}}^{\prime}}\in V^{{\bm{l}}^{\prime}},\quad\langle\phi^{\bm{l}}_{\bm{m}},\phi^{{\bm{l}}^{\prime}}_{{\bm{m}}^{\prime}}\rangle=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\langle\phi^{l_{i}}_{m_{i}},\phi^{l^{\prime}_{i}}_{m^{\prime}_{i}}\rangle=\delta_{{\bm{l}}{\bm{l}}^{\prime}}\delta_{{\bm{m}},{\bm{m}}^{\prime}}.

Due to (
2.4
),
V^{{\bm{l}}}
is stable by group action.

To obtain such a GE or GE-PI basis, we derive a linear system based on (
2.1
) or (
2.2
) that has to be satisfied for any element of the group
G
, and using the fact that
G
is a Lie group, we differentiate this linear system with respect to the generators of the group at the neutral element. To do so, we need to introduce a few additional objects. We denote by
N_{\rm{dim}}
the dimension of the Lie group
G
, and we consider a set of generators in the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of the group
G
denoted by
g_{d}\in\mathfrak{g},{d}=1,\ldots,N_{\rm{dim}}
.
Since
G
is a connected Lie group, the continuous representations
\rho^{l}
are differentiable
[
12
, Corollary 3.50]

and the partial derivative of
\rho^{l}
with respect to
g_{d}
is defined as

d\rho^{l}(g_{d})=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\rho^{l}(\exp(tg_{d})).

Therefore, for
d=1,\ldots,N_{\rm{dim}}
,
l\in\mathcal{L}
, and
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{l}
,
we denote the derivatives of
\rho^{l}_{m,m^{\prime}}
with respect to the generators
g_{d}
at the neutral element
e
by

\varrho^{l,{d}}_{m,m^{\prime}}:=[d\rho^{l}(g_{d})]_{m,m^{\prime}},

and we define the corresponding gradient as

\nabla\rho^{l}_{m,m^{\prime}}(e):=\left[\varrho^{l,{d}}_{m,m^{\prime}}\right]_{{d}=1,\ldots,N_{\rm dim}}.

2.2
Group-equivariant basis

In this section, we provide the necessary and sufficient conditions for characterizing a basis of GE functions for a representation
\rho^{L}
in the space
[V^{\bm{l}}]^{{{\rm dim}}(\rho^{L})}
for a given
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
.
Unless otherwise specified, we assume
L\in\mathcal{L}
, so that
\mathcal{M}_{L}
is well-defined and
{{\rm dim}}(\rho^{L})=|{\mathcal{M}_{L}}|
. We denote the space of the desired GE functions by
V^{{\bm{l}},L}
, and characterize a basis of this space in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.4

(Basis of the GE space
V^{{\bm{l}},L}
)
.

Let
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
,
L\in\mathcal{L}
, and let

M^{{\bm{l}},L}=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}\\
M_{2}\\
\vdots\\
M_{N_{\rm{dim}}}\end{pmatrix},

(2.6)

where for
{d}=1,\ldots,N_{\rm{dim}}
,
the elements of the submatrices

M_{d}
are defined for
({\bm{m}},k),\;({\bm{m}}^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}\times\mathcal{M}_{L}
as

M_{d}[({\bm{m}},k),({\bm{m}}^{\prime},k^{\prime})]=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varrho^{l_{j},{d}}_{m_{j},m_{j}}-\varrho^{L,{d}}_{k,k}\qquad&\textup{if}\;\;{\bm{m}}={\bm{m}}^{\prime},\;\;k=k^{\prime},\\
-\varrho^{L,{d}}_{k,k^{\prime}},\qquad&\textup{if}\;\;{\bm{m}}={\bm{m}}^{\prime},\;\;k\neq k^{\prime},\\
\varrho^{l_{j},{d}}_{m^{\prime}_{j},m_{j}},\qquad&\textup{if}\;\;\forall s\in\{1,\ldots,{N}\}\neq j,\;m_{s}=m^{\prime}_{s},\;\;m_{j}\neq m^{\prime}_{j},\;\;k=k^{\prime},\\
0,\qquad&\textup{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

(2.7)

Then, a basis of the GE space
V^{{\bm{l}},L}

is given by

b^{{\bm{l}},L}_{i}=\left[\sum_{{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}}c_{{\bm{m}},k,i}^{{\bm{l}},L}\phi^{\bm{l}}_{\bm{m}}\right]_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{L}},\;\;i=1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}\big(\ker(M^{{\bm{l}},L})\big),

(2.8)

where
{\bm{c}}^{{\bm{l}},L}_{i}=\{c_{{\bm{m}},k,i}^{{\bm{l}},L}\}_{{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}},k\in\mathcal{M}_{L}},\;i=1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}\big(\ker(M^{{\bm{l}},L})\big)
is a basis of
\ker(M^{{\bm{l}},L})
.
Thus,

{{\rm dim}}(V^{{\bm{l}},L})={{\rm dim}}\big(\ker(M^{{\bm{l}},L})\big).

We sometimes refer to the coefficients in
{\bm{c}}^{{\bm{l}},L}_{i}
as coupling coefficients. The proof of Proposition
2.4
can be found in Section
6.1
. For ease of notation, the superscript is sometimes omitted from the matrix
M^{{\bm{l}},L}
when the associated
{\bm{l}}
and
L
are clear from the context.
By definition of the matrix
{M}
(
2.6
), its kernel is the intersection of the kernels of its submatrices, that is,

{\rm Ker}(M)=\bigcap_{d=1}^{N_{\rm dim}}{\rm Ker}(M_{d}).

(2.9)

Note that
M_{d}
can be written as a sum of Kronecker products,

M_{d}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\bigotimes_{s=1}^{j-1}I_{|{\mathcal{M}_{l_{s}}}|}\otimes\varrho^{l_{j},{d}}\bigotimes_{s=j+1}^{{N}}I_{|{\mathcal{M}_{l_{s}}}|}\right]\bigotimes I_{|{\mathcal{M}_{L}}|}-\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}I_{|{\mathcal{M}_{l_{j}}}|}\otimes(\varrho^{L,{d}})^{T},

(2.10)

and its kernel can be related to the tensor products of eigenvectors of matrices
\varrho^{l,{d}}
, as presented in the next proposition.

Proposition 2.5
.

For any
l\in\mathcal{L}
,
d\in\{1,\ldots,N_{\rm dim}\}
, let
(\epsilon^{l,d}_{j},u^{l,d}_{j})\in\mathbb{F}\times\mathbb{F}^{|\mathcal{M}_{l}|}
be the eigenpairs of the matrices
\varrho^{l,{d}}
for
j\in\mathcal{M}_{l}
. Then the kernel of the matrix
M_{d}
defined in (
2.7
) is spanned by all

u^{l_{1},d}_{j_{1}}\otimes u^{l_{2},d}_{j_{2}}\otimes\ldots\otimes u^{l_{N},d}_{j_{N}}\otimes u^{L,d}_{j},

such that

\epsilon^{l_{1},d}_{j_{1}}+\epsilon^{l_{2},d}_{j_{2}}+\ldots+\epsilon^{l_{N},d}_{j_{N}}-\epsilon^{L,d}_{j}=0.

Proof.

By using the expression of the matrices
M_{d}
in (
2.10
), the eigenvectors of the matrices
M_{d}

are
u^{l_{1},d}_{j_{1}}\otimes u^{l_{2},d}_{j_{2}}\otimes\ldots\otimes u^{l_{N},d}_{j_{N}}\otimes u^{L,d}_{j}

for all possible indices
j_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{l_{i}}
for
i=1,\ldots,{N}
and
j\in\mathcal{M}_{L}
,
with corresponding eigenvalues

\epsilon^{l_{1},d}_{j_{1}}+\epsilon^{l_{2},d}_{j_{2}}+\ldots+\epsilon^{l_{N},d}_{j_{N}}-\epsilon^{L,d}_{j}
.
Therefore the kernel of
M_{d}
corresponds to the space spanned by eigenvectors with zero eigenvalues.
∎

Note that in principle, one could determine the kernel either using Proposition
2.4

or Proposition
2.5
. In practice, we mostly use Proposition
2.4
to numerically determine the coupling coefficients. However, Proposition
2.5
could be useful to obtain some explicit dimensionality of GE spaces.

We end this section with a remark providing a link with Clebsch–Gordan coefficients.

Remark 2.6
.

In the case where
{\bm{l}}=(l_{1},l_{2})
, the coupling coefficients correspond to the well-known Clebsch–Gordan coefficients. For larger
{\bm{l}}
’s, these coupling coefficients are sometimes called generalized Clebsch–Gordan coefficients
[
8
,
4
]
.

2.3
Group-equivariant and permutation invariant basis

We now turn to characterize GE-PI functions (see Definition
2.2
).
This is essential in practice, since incorporating the permutation invariance into modeling can significantly reduce the dimension of the targeted function space, especially when high-dimensional problems are considered.
As a first result, we provide a basis for PI functions in
V^{\bm{l}}
for some
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
.
To this end, we define

\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{l}}}=\{\pi\in S_{N}\;|\;\pi({\bm{l}})={\bm{l}}\}\subset S_{N},

and

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}:=\{\overline{{\bm{m}}}\;|\;{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{{\bm{l}}}\},

(2.11)

where
\overline{{\bm{m}}}
stands for the equivalent class of
{\bm{m}}
with the equivalent relation
\sim
being defined as

{\bm{m}}\sim{\bm{m}}^{\prime}\Leftrightarrow\exists\;\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{l}}},\quad{\bm{m}}^{\prime}=\pi{\bm{m}}.

For simplicity of notation, and as it is often clear from the context, we still denote the classes in
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
by
{\bm{m}}
.

Proposition 2.7

(Basis of PI functions in
V^{\bm{l}}
)
.

Let
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
.
A basis of PI functions belonging to
V^{{\bm{l}}}
consists of

\left\{\displaystyle\bar{\psi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}:=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\phi^{\bm{l}}_{\bm{m}}\circ\sigma,\quad{\bm{m}}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}\right\}.

(2.12)

In particular, the dimension of the space of PI functions in
V^{\bm{l}}
is
|{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}}|
.

The proof can be found in Section
6.1
.

Since the evaluation of basis functions defined in (
2.12
) scales exponentially with
N

due to the sum over permutations,
a density projection trick was proposed
[
7
,
8
]
.
We define the corresponding one-particle functions

\forall l\in\mathcal{L},\;m\in\mathcal{M}_{l},\quad\bar{\phi}^{l}_{m}:{\bm{R}}=({\bm{r}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{r}}_{N})\in\Omega^{N}\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi^{l}_{m}({\bm{r}}_{i}),

(2.13)

which are clearly PI and depend on
{N}
variables. The considered PI function space is then

{\bar{V}}^{l}={\rm Span}\{\bar{\phi}^{l}_{m},\;m\in\mathcal{M}_{l}\}.

Similar to
V^{{\bm{l}}}
, the space of PI functions
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}}
for
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
can be defined by forming tensor products of
{\bar{V}}^{l_{i}}
, as

{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}}:=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}{\bar{V}}^{l_{i}}={\rm Span}\left\{\bar{\phi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\phi}^{l_{i}}_{m_{i}},\;{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}\right\},\quad\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}:=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{M}_{l_{i}}.

Note that definition (
2.13
) can naturally be extended to an arbitrary number of variables by defining
\bar{\phi}^{l}_{m}:{\bm{R}}\in\textup{MS}(\Omega)\mapsto\sum_{{\bm{r}}\in{\bm{R}}}\phi^{l}_{m}({\bm{r}})
.
We can therefore also account for multiset functions in this setting.
Also, the evaluation cost of the
\bar{\phi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}
scales linearly with respect to the number of variables in the multiset.
Similarly as with
\bar{\psi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}
, some functions in this set are identical. We therefore restrict
{\bm{m}}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
, and obtain the following result.

Proposition 2.8

(Basis of PI space
{\bar{V}}^{\bm{l}}
)
.

Let
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
. A basis of
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}}
consists of the functions

\left\{\bar{\phi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}},\;{\bm{m}}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}\right\}.

In particular, the dimension of
{\bar{V}}^{\bm{l}}
is
|{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}}|
.

We defer the proof of this proposition to Section
6.1
.

Remark 2.9

(Group action)
.

Note that
{\bar{V}}^{\bm{l}}
is stable with respect to the group action, and the latter acts in the same way over the functions
\phi^{\bm{l}}_{\bm{m}}
,

\bar{\psi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}
and
\bar{\phi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}
. Indeed, since

\bar{\phi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}({\bm{R}})=\sum_{1\leq n_{1},\ldots,n_{N}\leq{N}}\phi^{\bm{l}}_{\bm{m}}({\bm{r}}_{n_{1}},\ldots,{\bm{r}}_{n_{N}}),

we have that for any
g\in G

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}(g\cdot{\bm{R}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq n_{1},\ldots,n_{N}\leq{N}}\phi^{\bm{l}}_{\bm{m}}(g\cdot({\bm{r}}_{n_{1}},\ldots,{\bm{r}}_{n_{N}}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{m}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}}\rho^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}},{\bm{m}}^{\prime}}(g)\sum_{1\leq n_{1},\ldots,n_{N}\leq{N}}\phi^{\bm{l}}_{{\bm{m}}^{\prime}}({\bm{r}}_{n_{1}},\ldots,{\bm{r}}_{n_{N}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{m}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}}\rho^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}},{\bm{m}}^{\prime}}(g)\bar{\phi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}^{\prime}}({\bm{R}}).

It can be easily checked that the same property holds for
\phi^{\bm{l}}_{\bm{m}}
and
\bar{\psi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}
.

Definition 2.10

(Scalar product on
{\bar{V}}^{\bm{l}}
)
.

As
\left\{\bar{\phi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}},\;{\bm{m}}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}\right\}
is a basis of
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}}
, we can define an inner product on
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}}
by the relation

\langle\bar{\phi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}},\bar{\phi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{{\bm{m}},{\bm{m}}^{\prime}}.

We now turn to characterizing GE-PI functions with components in
{\bar{V}}^{\bm{l}}
, starting by introducing some notation.
For
{\bm{l}}
’s containing a single value
l
, i.e.,

{\bm{l}}=(l,l,\ldots,l)\in\mathcal{L}^{N},

the classes defined in (
2.11
) are in one-to-one correspondence with ordered
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}
, which we use by default as a representative for the corresponding class.
Furthermore, the classes
{\bm{m}}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
are also in one-to-one correspondence with their Parikh vectors or count vectors

\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}}
(see e.g.
[
14
, Supplementary lecture H]
), which are defined as the vectors containing the number of occurrences of the elements in
\mathcal{M}_{l}
. For consistency, we denote by
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}}
the count vector of
{\bm{m}}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
. We give an explicit example to better illustrate this concept.

Example 2.11
.

Assume
l=1
,
\mathcal{M}_{l}=\{-1,0,1\}
and
{\bm{l}}=(l,l,l,l,l)
, then the class
{\bm{m}}=\overline{(-1,-1,0,1,1)}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
has a count vector

\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}}=(2,1,2)
, whose components stand for the two occurrences of
-1\in\mathcal{M}_{l}
, one occurrence of
0\in\mathcal{M}_{l}
and two occurrences of
1\in\mathcal{M}_{l}
, respectively.

Note that the sum of the elements in the count vector is equal to the length of
{\bm{l}}
,
{N}
, and we can identify
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
with the set

\Lambda_{{\bm{l}}}:=\{\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathcal{M}_{l}}:\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{l}}\bm{\lambda}_{i}={N}\}.

(2.14)

For a general
{\bm{l}}
, the classes of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
are in one-to-one correspondence with
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}
ordered within each subset of indices corresponding to identical
l
’s. To simplify the presentation, but without loss of generality, we assume that
{\bm{l}}
is ordered.
The index
{\bm{l}}
can be divided into a set of minimal length
{N_{\rm blocks}}
of sub-indices
\{{\bm{l}}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}
of length
|{\bm{l}}^{(j)}|
, so that all elements in
{\bm{l}}^{(j)}
are identical, that we call minimal partition as defined below.

Definition 2.12

(Minimal partition)
.

Let
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
be ordered, we say that
({\bm{l}}^{(j)})_{i=1}^{{N_{\rm blocks}}}
is the minimal partition of
{\bm{l}}
if each block
{\bm{l}}^{(j)}
has a single repeated value and that for all
j,j^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,{N_{\rm blocks}}\},i\neq j,\;{\bm{l}}^{(j)}\cap{\bm{l}}^{(j^{\prime})}=\varnothing
.

Then it immediately follows that

\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{l}}}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}S_{|{\bm{l}}^{(j)}|},\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{l}}^{(j)}}.

For
{\overline{{\bm{m}}}}=({\overline{{\bm{m}}}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\overline{{\bm{m}}}}^{({N_{\rm blocks}})})\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
, we use
{\bm{m}}=({\bm{m}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bm{m}}^{({N_{\rm blocks}})})
as its representative, where
{\bm{m}}^{(j)}
are ordered, for all
j
.
Thus, for a general ordered
{\bm{l}}
,
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
is in one-to-one correspondence with

\displaystyle\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}\Lambda_{{\bm{l}}^{(j)}}
,
with
\Lambda_{{\bm{l}}^{(j)}}
defined in (
2.14
). For simplicity, we also denote this set by
\Lambda_{{\bm{l}}}
, which will not cause confusion since when
{\bm{l}}
contains only identical values, this definition is consistent with (
2.14
).
For any
{\bm{m}}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
, we denote its corresponding element in
\Lambda_{{\bm{l}}}
by
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}}=(\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}^{(1)}},\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}^{(2)}},\ldots,\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}^{({N_{\rm blocks}})}})
. Similarly, for any
\bm{\lambda}\in\Lambda_{\bm{l}}
, we denote the associated element in
\mathcal{M}_{{\bm{l}}}
by
{\bm{m}}_{\bm{\lambda}}
.

We now define the set of interacting classes as follows, which will be key in constructing a GE-PI basis.

Definition 2.13

(Interacting classes)
.

For a given
{\bm{m}}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
, we define the set of its interacting classes
\mathcal{N}_{\bm{m}}\subset\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
as

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{\bm{m}}:=\Bigg\{{\bm{m}}_{(j,p,q)}:=&\;\;{\bm{m}}_{\bm{\lambda}_{(j,p,q)}},\quad j\in\{1,2,\ldots,{N_{\rm blocks}}\},\;\;p,q\in\mathcal{M}_{{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}},\;\;p\neq q,\\
&\textup{where }\quad\bm{\lambda}_{(j,p,q)}=(\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}^{(1)}},\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}^{(2)}},\ldots,\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}^{(j-1)}},\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}^{(j)}}-{\bf e}_{p}+{\bf e}_{q},\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}^{(j+1)}},\ldots,\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}^{({N_{\rm blocks}})}})\\
&\textup{if $\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}^{(j)},p}>0$, otherwise $\bm{\lambda}_{(j,p,q)}$ is discarded }\Bigg\},\end{split}

(2.15)

where
{\bf e}_{k}
denotes the
k
-th vector of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{M}_{{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}}}
,
{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}
is the first component of
{\bm{l}}^{(j)}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}^{(j)},p}
the
p^{\textup{th}}
component of
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}^{(j)}}
.

The following theorem provides a basis for GE-PI functions for the representation
\rho^{L}
in the space
\left[{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}}\right]^{|{\mathcal{M}_{L}}|}
, whose span we denote by
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L}
, by analogy with the GE case.

Theorem 2.14

(Basis of the GE-PI space
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L}
)
.

Let
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
,
L\in\mathcal{L}
, and let

M^{{\bm{l}},L}=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}\\
M_{2}\\
\vdots\\
M_{N_{\rm{dim}}}\end{pmatrix},

(2.16)

where for
{d}=1,\ldots,N_{\rm{dim}}
,
the elements of the matrices
M_{d}
read for
({\bm{m}},k),({\bm{m}}^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}\times\mathcal{M}_{L}
as

M_{d}[({\bm{m}},k),({\bm{m}}^{\prime},k^{\prime})]=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varrho^{{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1},{d}}_{m_{j},m_{j}}-\varrho^{L,{d}}_{k,k}\qquad&\textup{if}\;\;{\bm{m}}={\bm{m}}^{\prime},\;\;k=k^{\prime},\\
-\varrho^{L,{d}}_{k,k^{\prime}},\qquad&\textup{if}\;\;{\bm{m}}={\bm{m}}^{\prime},\;\;k\neq k^{\prime},\\
\bm{\lambda}_{{{\bm{m}}^{\prime}}^{(j)},q}\;\varrho^{{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1},{d}}_{q,p},\qquad&\textup{if}\;\;{\bm{m}}^{\prime}={\bm{m}}_{(j,p,q)}\in\mathcal{N}_{{\bm{m}}},\;\;k=k^{\prime},\\
0,\qquad&\textup{otherwise,}\\
\end{cases}

(2.17)

with
\mathcal{N}_{\bm{m}}
defined in (
2.15
).
Then a basis of
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L}

is given by

b^{{\bm{l}},L}_{i}=\left[\sum_{{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}}c_{{\bm{m}},k,i}^{{\bm{l}},L}\bar{\phi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}\right]_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{L}},\;\;i=1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}\big(\ker(M^{{\bm{l}},L})\big),

where

{\bm{c}}^{{\bm{l}},L}_{i}=\{c_{{\bm{m}},k,i}^{{\bm{l}},L}\}_{{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}},k\in\mathcal{M}_{L}},\;i=1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}\big(\ker(M^{{\bm{l}},L})\big)
is a basis of
\ker(M^{{\bm{l}},L})
.
Thus,

{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L})={{\rm dim}}\big(\ker(M^{{\bm{l}},L})\big).

The proof of this theorem can be found in Section
6.1
.
As with the GE case, the superscript of the matrix
M^{{\bm{l}},L}
is sometimes omitted when the associated
{\bm{l}}
and
L
are clear. Compared to Proposition
2.4
that deals with the GE case, the only two differences are: (1) the set
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}
being replaced by the set of classes
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
, an even smaller set compared with
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}
; and (2) the presence of the

\bm{\lambda}_{{{\bm{m}}^{\prime}}^{(j)},q}\;\;\varrho^{{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1},{d}}_{q,p},

instead of
\varrho^{l_{j},{d}}_{m^{\prime}_{j},m_{j}}
.
Therefore, our construction of the GE-PI basis is almost as efficient as that of the GE basis. To the best of our knowledge, this has not been the case in any of the existing work, where the presence of permutation invariance often incurs substantial additional costs
[
4
,
8
,
10
]
.

We now provide a similar result when the basis
\bar{\psi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}
is used.

Corollary 2.15
.

Let
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
,
L\in\mathcal{L}
.
Let
{\bm{c}}^{{\bm{l}},L}_{i}=\{c_{{\bm{m}},k,i}^{{\bm{l}},L}\}_{{\bm{m}}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}},k\in\mathcal{M}_{L}},i=1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}(\ker(M^{{\bm{l}},L}))
be a basis of
\ker(M^{{\bm{l}},L})
.
A basis of GE-PI
functions for the representation
\rho^{L}
in the space
\left[V^{\bm{l}}\right]^{|{\mathcal{M}_{L}}|}
is
given by

b^{{\bm{l}},L}_{i}=\left[\sum_{{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}}c_{{\bm{m}},k,i}^{{\bm{l}},L}\bar{\psi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}\right]_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{L}},\;\;i=1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}\big(\ker(M^{{\bm{l}},L})\big).

Proof.

Since the group action on
G
acts the same over the functions
\bar{\psi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}
and
\bar{\phi}^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}}}
, Proposition
2.7
combined with an adapted proof of Theorem
2.14
easily provides the result.
∎

2.4
Recursive construction of GE and GE-PI bases

In this section, we show how the GE and GE-PI bases can be constructed recursively. This also offers a way to obtain an exact recursive formula for the dimensionality of the considered GE and GE-PI spaces.
Indeed, a priori it is not so clear whether the tensor product of two GE sub-bases is a basis of the final space or an overcomplete set of functions. To obtain these results, we need a few additional assumptions on the considered group and the representations. We assume that the group
G
is compact, so that the Haar measure is well-defined
[
12
, Chapter 4]
, and we consider a complete set of mutually inequivalent irreducible representations
\rho^{l}
for
l\in\mathcal{L}
with corresponding space
V^{l}
.
Under these assumptions, the spaces
V^{\bm{l}}
and
{\bar{V}}^{\bm{l}}
for
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
can respectively be decomposed into the direct sum of GE and GE-PI spaces.

Proposition 2.16

(Direct sum of spaces with GE functions)
.

Suppose
G
is a
compact Lie group,

{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
and

V^{\bm{l}}
is stable by group action.
There holds

V^{{\bm{l}}}=\bigoplus_{L\in\mathcal{L}}\;\bigoplus_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{L}}[V^{{\bm{l}},L}]_{k},

(2.18)

where
[V^{{\bm{l}},L}]_{k}
denotes the space spanned by the
k
-th component of the functions in
V^{{\bm{l}},L}
.

Proof.

Since the space
V^{\bm{l}}
is stable by group action, the group action defines a representation
\rho^{{\bm{l}}}
, which can be blockdiagonalized with irreducible representations
\rho^{L}
,
L\in\mathcal{L}
and corresponding basis functions
v^{L}_{i,k}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,d_{L}\}
and
k\in\mathcal{M}_{L}
, where
d_{L}
is the number of copies of
\rho^{L}
in the blockdiagonalization.
Noting that

V^{{\bm{l}},L}={\rm Span}\{[v^{L}_{i,k}]_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{L}},i\in\{1,\ldots,d_{L}\}\}
, the result follows.
∎

Proposition 2.17

(Direct sum of spaces with GE-PI functions)
.

Suppose
G
is a compact Lie group,

{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
and

{\bar{V}}^{\bm{l}}
is stable by group action.
There holds

{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}}=\bigoplus_{L\in\mathcal{L}}\;\bigoplus_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{L}}[{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L}]_{k},

(2.19)

where
[{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L}]_{k}
denotes the space spanned by the
k
-th component of the functions in
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L}
.

Proof.

Since the group action is identical on
V^{l}
and
{\bar{V}}^{l}
for
l\in\mathcal{L}
using Remark
2.9
, a similar proof as Proposition
2.16
applies in this case.
∎

We now provide a recursive construction of GE bases and provide a recursive formula for the dimensionality of the GE spaces.

Proposition 2.18

(GE basis based on recursion)
.

Let
N,N_{1},N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
with
N_{1}+N_{2}=N
.
Let
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
and
L\in\mathcal{L}
. Suppose
{\bm{l}}=({\bm{l}}^{(1)},{\bm{l}}^{(2)})\in\mathcal{L}^{{N}_{1}}\times\mathcal{L}^{{N}_{2}}
, then a basis for
V^{{\bm{l}},L}
is given by

b^{{\bm{l}},L}_{(L_{1},i_{1},L_{2},i_{2},j)}=\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{L_{1}}\\
k_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{L_{2}}\end{subarray}}c_{(k_{1},k_{2}),k,j}^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L}[b^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}}_{i_{1}}]_{k_{1}}[b^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}}_{i_{2}}]_{k_{2}}\right]_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{L}},\quad\begin{array}[]{l}L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L},\\
i_{1}\in\{1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}(V^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}})\},\\
i_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}(V^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}})\},\\
j\in\{1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}(V^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L})\},\end{array}

where
\{b^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}}_{i_{1}}\}_{i_{1}=1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}(V^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}})}
,

\{b^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}}_{i_{2}}\}_{i_{2}=1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}(V^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}})}
are bases of
V^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}}
and
V^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}}
, respectively, and

\{c_{(k_{1},k_{2}),k,j}^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L}\}_{j=1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}(V^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L})}
is a basis of
\ker(M^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L})
with
M^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L}
being defined in (
2.6
).
Moreover, there holds

{{\rm dim}}(V^{{\bm{l}},L})=\sum_{L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}{{\rm dim}}(V^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L})\;{{\rm dim}}(V^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}})\;{{\rm dim}}(V^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}}).

(2.20)

The proof can be found in Section
6.1
.
Note that,
as mentioned in Remark
2.6
,
the coupling coefficients
c_{(k_{1},k_{2}),k,j}^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L}
are called Clebsch–Gordan coefficients.

We then provide a result that can be used to compute a GE-PI basis recursively. In the following, we say that
{\bm{l}}^{(1)}
and
{\bm{l}}^{(2)}
do not intersect if
{\bm{l}}^{(1)}_{i}\neq{\bm{l}}^{(2)}_{j}
for all
i,j
.
In this case, we write
{\bm{l}}^{(1)}\cap{\bm{l}}^{(2)}=\varnothing
.

Proposition 2.19

(GE-PI basis based on recursion)
.

Let
N,N_{1},N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
with
N_{1}+N_{2}=N
.
Let
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
and
L\in\mathcal{L}
. Suppose
{\bm{l}}=({\bm{l}}^{(1)},{\bm{l}}^{(2)})\in\mathcal{L}^{{N}_{1}}\times\mathcal{L}^{{N}_{2}}
, then a spanning set for
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L}
is

b^{{\bm{l}},L}_{(L_{1},i_{1},L_{2},i_{2},j)}=\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{L_{1}}\\
k_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{L_{2}}\end{subarray}}c_{(k_{1},k_{2}),k,j}^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L}[b^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}}_{i_{1}}]_{k_{1}}[b^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}}_{i_{2}}]_{k_{2}}\right]_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{L}},\quad\begin{array}[]{l}L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L},\\
i_{1}\in\{1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}})\},\\
i_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}})\},\\
j\in\{1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}(V^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L})\},\end{array}

where
\{b^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}}_{i_{1}}\}_{i_{1}=1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}})}
and

\{b^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}}_{i_{2}}\}_{i_{2}=1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}})}
are bases of
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}}
and
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}}

respectively, and

\{c_{(k_{1},k_{2}),k,j}^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L}\}_{j=1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L})}
is a basis of
\ker(M^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L})
with
M^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L}
being defined in (
2.6
).

Moreover, if
{\bm{l}}^{(1)}\cap{\bm{l}}^{(2)}=\varnothing
, this family is a basis of
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L}
and there holds

{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L})=\sum_{L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}{{\rm dim}}(V^{(L_{1},L_{2}),L})\;{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}})\;{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}}).

(2.21)

Proof.

The proof for the non-intersecting case is similar to the proof of Proposition
2.18

noting that if
{\bm{l}}^{(1)}\cap{\bm{l}}^{(2)}=\varnothing
,
then
{\bar{V}}^{\bm{l}}={\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)}}\otimes{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)}}
, and a basis of
{\bar{V}}^{\bm{l}}
is

([b^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}}_{i_{1}}]_{k_{1}}[b^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}}_{i_{2}}]_{k_{2}})_{L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L},\;i_{1}\in\{1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}})\},\;i_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}})\}},

) instead of (

([b^{L_{1}}_{{\bm{l}}^{(1)}i_{1}}]_{k_{1}}[b^{L_{2}}_{{\bm{l}}^{(2)}i_{2}}]_{k_{2}})_{L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathbb{N},i_{1}\in\{1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(1)},L_{1}})\},i_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}}^{(2)},L_{2}})\}}

.

∎

Note that in the case of intersecting
{\bm{l}}^{(1)},{\bm{l}}^{(2)},
we can numerically obtain a basis by, for example, performing a singular value decomposition on the spanning set given in Proposition
2.19
.

In practice, it is not always clear which construction is more efficient between the direct and recursive constructions. However, with the result on GE-PI functions for
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
with minimal partition
{\bm{l}}=({\bm{l}}^{(1)},{\bm{l}}^{(2)},\ldots,{\bm{l}}^{({N_{\rm blocks}})})
, we expect it to be efficient to first compute basis functions for the different blocks
{\bm{l}}^{(j)}
of identical values, and then assemble them using Proposition
2.19
, especially for large

.

3
Application to some rotation groups

In this section, we apply our construction to the groups
SU(2)
and
SO(3)
, which naturally appear in quantum mechanics, molecular mechanics, and computer graphics. We show how the matrices (
2.6
) and (
2.17
) simplify for these specific groups, and investigate the algebraic properties of the GE and GE-PI bases. In particular, we provide their exact dimensionality and prove asymptotic estimates.

3.1
Representations and Lie algebra

We first introduce the explicit expression of the Wigner-D matrices with respect to Euler angles, which are irreducible representations of
SU(2)
and
SO(3)

[
28
]
. As it is standard, we denote the Wigner-D matrices by
D^{\ell}
instead of
\rho^{\ell}
.

Definition 3.1

(Wigner-D matrix)
.

Let
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in[0,4\pi)\times[0,\pi)\times[0,4\pi)
for
SU(2)

or

[0,2\pi)\times[0,\pi)\times[0,2\pi)

for
SO(3)

be the three Euler angles that parametrize an element
Q
in either the group
SU(2)
or
SO(3)
under the ZYZ convention. Then for
\ell=0,\;\frac{1}{2},\;1,\;\frac{3}{2},\;\ldots
, the Wigner-D matrix
D^{\ell}
is defined as a
(2\ell+1)\times(2\ell+1)
matrix, whose elements read

D^{\ell}_{\mu m}(Q)=D^{\ell}_{\mu m}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\mu\alpha}d^{\ell}_{\mu m}(\beta)e^{-\mathrm{i}m\gamma},

(3.1)

for
\mu,m\in\{-\ell,-\ell+1,\ldots,\ell-1,\ell\}
, where

d^{\ell}_{\mu m}(\beta)=[(\ell+m)!(\ell-m)!(\ell+\mu)!(\ell-\mu)!]^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{s=\max(0,\mu-m)}^{\min(\ell+\mu,\ell-m)}\frac{(-1)^{s}(\cos\frac{\beta}{2})^{2l+\mu-m-2s}(\sin\frac{\beta}{2})^{m-\mu+2s}}{(\ell+\mu-s)!s!(m-\mu+s)!(\ell-m+s)!}.

In particular, the irreducible representations of
SU(2)
are the Wigner-D matrices for

\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{SU(2)}
defined as

\mathcal{L}_{SU(2)}:=\left\{\frac{\ell}{2}:\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\},

(3.2)

while the irreducible representations of
SO(3)
correspond to Wigner-D matrices for

\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{SO(3)}:=\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
To unify the notation and to emphasize the dependency of the index sets for the irreducible representations on the group
G
, we denote the sets as
\mathcal{\mathcal{L}_{\rm G}}
, particularly for
G=SU(2)
and
G=SO(3)
, in this section. To apply Proposition
2.4
and Theorem
2.14
, we need the partial derivatives of the Wigner-D matrices with respect to the Euler angles at the origin, which corresponds to the neutral element of the group (identity denoted by
I
). For notational convenience, we define for
\ell\in\mathcal{\mathcal{L}_{\rm G}}
the set

\mathcal{M}_{\ell}=\{-\ell,-\ell+1,\ldots,\ell-1,\ell\},\quad\text{ with }\quad|{\mathcal{M}_{\ell}}|=2\ell+1.

(3.3)

3.2
One-particle compatible bases

We now introduce some possible one-particle compatible bases for
SU(2)
and
SO(3)
. Although a large freedom exists in the choice of the bases that are stable by group action, we focus on compatible bases with respect to the irreducible representation
D^{\ell}
for
\ell\in\mathcal{\mathcal{L}_{\rm G}}
. Specifically, we focus on the bases that are frequently used in real implementations.
For each case, we need to define the considered spatial domain
\Omega
, the chosen
\mathbb{F}
and
\mathbb{K}
, the considered group action, and the one-particle compatible bases.

Wigner-D matrices

First, for all
\ell\in\mathcal{\mathcal{L}_{\rm G}}
, elements in each column of
D^{\ell}
are functions from
\Omega:=G
to
\mathbb{F}:=\mathbb{C}
, and form a compatible basis with respect to the representation
D^{\ell}
itself. This corresponds to
\mathbb{K}:=\mathbb{C}
.
Precisely, let
l=\ell
,
\mathcal{M}_{l}=\mathcal{M}_{\ell}
, then for any index
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell}
, the space

\displaystyle V^{l}_{\mu}=\textup{Span}\big\{D^{\ell}_{m\mu}:G\to\mathbb{C},\;m\in\mathcal{M}_{l}\big\}

is stable under the group action defined by matrix multiplication as

\forall Q,{\bm{r}}\in G,\quad Q\cdot{\bm{r}}:=Q{\bm{r}},

since

\forall Q\in G,\;{\bm{r}}\in G,\qquad D^{\ell}_{m\mu}(Q\cdot{\bm{r}})=[D^{\ell}(Q)\cdot D^{\ell}({\bm{r}})]_{m\mu}=\sum_{m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell}}D^{\ell}_{mm^{\prime}}(Q)D^{\ell}_{m^{\prime}\mu}({\bm{r}}).

Hence, columns of Wigner-D matrices
\{D^{\ell}_{m\mu}:G\to\mathbb{C}\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell}}
form compatible bases for the representations
D^{\ell}
.

Spherical harmonics

In particular, for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\mu=0\in\mathcal{M}_{l}
, and for
Q\in SO(3)
represented by Euler angles
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
, there holds

D^{\ell}_{m0}(Q)=D^{\ell}_{m0}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=e^{-\textup{i}m\alpha}\cdot(-1)^{m}\sqrt{\frac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}}P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos\beta),

where
P_{\ell}^{m}
is the associated Legendre polynomial. Clearly,
D^{\ell}_{m0}
is independent of
\gamma
, and is proportional to the complex spherical harmonics of order
\ell
and angular momentum quantum number
m
:
Y_{\ell}^{m}(\alpha,\beta)
.
In other words, the spherical harmonics
\{Y_{\ell}^{m}\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell}}
form a compatible basis corresponding to
D^{\ell}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the irreducible representations of
SO(3)
. Thus, defining
\Omega:=S^{2}
the 2-dimensional unit sphere,
\mathbb{F}:=\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{K}:=\mathbb{C}
, and the group action as matrix-vector multiplication

\forall Q\in SO(3),\;{\bm{r}}\in S^{2},\quad Q\cdot{\bm{r}}:=Q{\bm{r}},

the space for
l:=\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
defined by

V^{l}:=\textup{Span}\big\{Y_{l}^{m}:S^{2}\to\mathbb{C},\;m\in\mathcal{M}_{l}\big\}

is stable under group action. Note that we could also take
\mathbb{F}:=\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathbb{K}:=\mathbb{R}
by considering real spherical harmonics, although another set of irreducible representations would have to be taken into account in this case.

One-particle basis on
\mathbb{R}^{3}

In practice, it is of particular interest to investigate a compatible basis on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This is often done by padding a 1D radial basis to spherical harmonics.
Precisely speaking, we take the spatial domain
\Omega
as
\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
in this case,
and let
\mathbb{F}:=\mathbb{C}
, and
\mathbb{K}:=\mathbb{C}
. Then for
{\bm{r}}=|{\bm{r}}|\hat{{\bm{r}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
with
|{\bm{r}}|>0
and

\hat{{\bm{r}}}\in S^{2}
, we define the group action as

\forall Q\in SO(3),\;{\bm{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\},\quad Q\cdot{\bm{r}}:=|{\bm{r}}|(Q\hat{{\bm{r}}}).

Taking
\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, an orthogonal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we
define for
l=(n,\ell)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}=:\mathcal{L}
that
\mathcal{M}_{l}:=\mathcal{M}_{\ell}
, and for
m\in\mathcal{M}_{l}
that

\phi^{l}_{m}=\phi^{(n,\ell)}_{m}:{\bm{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\mapsto P_{n}(|{\bm{r}}|)Y_{\ell}^{m}(\hat{{\bm{r}}}).

Then,
\big\{\phi^{l}_{m}\big\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{l}}

forms a compatible basis of
SO(3)
corresponding to representation
D^{\ell}
, as the radial function
P_{n}
remain invariant under
SO(3)
rotations. The space

V^{l}=\textup{Span}\big\{\phi^{l}_{m},\;m\in\mathcal{M}_{l}\big\}

is therefore stable under the defined group action.

One-particle basis on higher-dimensional spaces

In fact, any invariant transformation with respect to rotations
has no impact on the stability of the corresponding basis. One can take into account higher-dimensional spaces
\Omega=W\times S^{2}
, with
W
independent of rotations.
For example, a particle in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is often described not only by its position, but also by other properties, such as its chemical species. In this case, one could, e.g., take

W=\mathbb{N}\times\big(\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}\big),\quad\Omega=\mathbb{N}\times\big(\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\big),\quad\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C},\quad\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C},

with the group action being defined as

\forall Q\in SO(3),\;(z,{\bm{r}})\in\Omega,\quad Q\cdot(z,{\bm{r}}):=(z,Q{\bm{r}}).

Hence, we may write for
l=(n_{1},n_{2},\ell)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}=:\mathcal{L}
the basis index set
\mathcal{M}_{l}:=\mathcal{M}_{\ell}
, and the compatible basis

\{\phi^{l}_{m}:(z,{\bm{r}})\in\mathbb{N}\times\big(\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\big)\mapsto F_{n_{1}}(z)P_{n_{2}}(|{\bm{r}}|)Y_{\ell}^{m}(\hat{\bm{r}})\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{l}},

where
F_{n_{1}}
are basis functions depending on the chemical species (and possibly other parameters independent of rotations),
which are usually chosen as
\delta
-functions (also called one-hot embedding in some contexts),

P_{n_{2}}
are radial functions as stated above.
Thus, the
corresponding function space

V^{l}=\textup{Span}\big\{\phi^{l}_{m},\;m\in\mathcal{M}_{l}\big\}

is stable for the representation
D^{\ell}
.

In some cases that should be clear from the context, when it comes to operations on

In the GE case, we have an explicit formula for the dimensionality, as presented below.

Proposition 3.10

(Exact GE dimensionality)
.

For

and

{{\rm dim}}(V^{{\bm{l}},L})=\sum_{{\mathbf{S}}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,N\}}(-1)^{\left(1+\sum{\mathbf{S}}\right)}\;\binom{L+\sum{\bm{l}}-\sum_{s\in{\mathbf{S}}}(2l_{s}+1)+N-1}{N-2}.

(3.22)

Proof.

By definition of
\mathcal{M}_{{\bm{l}},L}

|{\mathcal{M}_{{\bm{l}},L}}|
is the coefficients of the term

in the polynomial

\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{m=-l_{i}}^{l_{i}}x^{m}\right).

Therefore, by the binomial theorem,

|{\mathcal{M}_{{\bm{l}},L}}|=\sum_{{\mathbf{S}}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,N\}}(-1)^{\sum{\mathbf{S}}}\binom{L+\sum{\bm{l}}-\sum_{s\in{\mathbf{S}}}(2l_{s}+1)+N-1}{N-1}.

From (
3.21
), the dimensionality of the GE basis is,

\displaystyle{{\rm dim}}(V^{{\bm{l}},L})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{S}}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,N\}}(-1)^{(1+\sum{\mathbf{S}})}\left(\binom{L+\sum{\bm{l}}-\sum_{s\in{\mathbf{S}}}(2l_{s}+1)+N-1}{N-1}-\binom{L+\sum{\bm{l}}-\sum_{s\in{\mathbf{S}}}(2l_{s}+1)+N}{N-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{S}}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,N\}}(-1)^{(1+\sum{\mathbf{S}})}\binom{L+\sum{\bm{l}}-\sum_{s\in{\mathbf{S}}}(2l_{s}+1)+N-1}{N-2},

using the binomial formula
\binom{n}{k}+\binom{n}{k+1}=\binom{n+1}{k+1}

∎

Example 3.19
.

In the specific case where
{\bm{l}}=(1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
, we have for

that

|{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{l}},L}}|=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lfloor\frac{{N}-L}{2}\rfloor+1&\textup{if }0\leq L\leq{N}\\

and as a consequence,

{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\text{if }L\leq{N}\text{ and }{N}+L\text{ is even},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Indeed, the elements
{\bm{m}}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}

have coordinates either -1, 0 or 1 and are thus uniquely determined by their count vectors
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{m}}}=(\bm{\lambda}^{-1}_{{\bm{m}}},\bm{\lambda}^{0}_{{\bm{m}}},\bm{\lambda}^{1}_{{\bm{m}}})
with
\bm{\lambda}^{i}_{{\bm{m}}}\geq 0
and
\bm{\lambda}^{-1}_{{\bm{m}}}+\bm{\lambda}^{0}_{{\bm{m}}}+\bm{\lambda}^{1}_{{\bm{m}}}={N}

\sum{\bm{m}}=L
if and only if
\bm{\lambda}^{-1}_{{\bm{m}}}+L=\bm{\lambda}^{1}_{{\bm{m}}}
. Thus,
{\bm{m}}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{l}},L}
if and only if
2\bm{\lambda}^{-1}_{{\bm{m}}}+\bm{\lambda}^{0}_{{\bm{m}}}+L={N}
. If

, this equation has no solution. If

, there are
\lfloor\frac{{N}-L}{2}\rfloor+1
possible values of
\bm{\lambda}^{-1}_{{\bm{m}}}
such that

and for each possible value of
\bm{\lambda}^{-1}_{{\bm{m}}}
, there is only one possible value for
\bm{\lambda}^{0}_{{\bm{m}}}
, which is

.

3.18
.

Conjecture 3.21

.

Let
{\bm{l}}=(l,\ldots,l)\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
containing identical nonzero values
l
,
L\in\mathcal{L}_{G}
such that
L+\sum{\bm{l}}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Define

\textup{Var}_{\bm{l}}=\frac{Nl(N+2l+1)}{6}.

Then for
L\ll\sqrt{\textup{Var}_{\bm{l}}}
,

{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L})=\binom{N+2l}{2l}\left(\frac{2L+1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}(\textup{Var}_{\bm{l}})^{3/2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{{N}^{5/2}}\right)\right).

(3.39)

Note that an asymptotic estimate can be obtained for
|{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{l}},L}}|

[
25
, (45)]
as

\lim_{{N}\to+\infty}\left(\frac{|{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{l}},L}}|}{\binom{N+2l}{2l}}-\frac{\exp{(-L^{2}/(2\textup{Var}_{\bm{l}}))}}{\sqrt{2\pi\textup{Var}_{\bm{l}}}}\right)=0,

from which we deduce that

{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L})=\binom{N+2l}{2l}\left(\frac{2L+1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}(\textup{Var}_{\bm{l}})^{3/2}}+o\left(\frac{1}{{N}^{3/2}}\right)\right).

However, estimating the remainder as in (
3.39
) is beyond the scope of this manuscript.

When extending to the multi-block case, we have that for general
{\bm{l}}
,
|{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{l}},L}}|
is the coefficients of the term
x^{L+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}N_{j}{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}}
in the following

\prod_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}\left[\begin{matrix}N_{j}+2{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}\\
2{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}\end{matrix}\right]_{x}=\prod_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}\prod_{i=1}^{2{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}}\frac{1-x^{N_{j}+i}}{1-x^{i}},\quad x\neq 1.

In addition, the sums of the blocks can be considered as independent variables. Hence, by using a similar strategy as shown in the proof of Proposition
3.13
, we have the following estimation with the increase of
{N_{\rm blocks}}
.

Proposition 3.22

(Asymptotic dimensionality for general
{\bm{l}}
)
.

For
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
,
L\in\mathcal{L}
,
L+\sum{\bm{l}}\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
{\bm{l}}=({\bm{l}}^{(j)})_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}
is the minimal partition of
{\bm{l}}

with
N_{j}
being the length of its
j
-th block, define

\textup{Var}_{\bm{l}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}\frac{N_{j}{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}(N_{j}+2{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}+1)}{6}.

If there exist
\kappa_{\min},\kappa_{\max}>0
independent of
{N_{\rm blocks}}
such that

\forall j=1,\ldots,{N_{\rm blocks}},\quad\kappa_{\min}\leq N_{j}{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}\leq\kappa_{\max},\;

(3.40)

then for
L\ll\sqrt{\textup{Var}_{\bm{l}}}
, there holds for
{N_{\rm blocks}}
sufficiently large

{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L})=\prod_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}\binom{N_{j}+2{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}}{2{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}}\left(\frac{2L+1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}(\textup{Var}_{\bm{l}})^{3/2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{{N_{\rm blocks}}^{5/2}}\right)\right).

(3.41)

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of Proposition
3.13
, using the variance given in
[
25
, (43)]
, and the fourth-order cumulants

C_{4{\bm{l}}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}\frac{N_{j}{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}(N_{j}+2{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}+1)(N_{j}^{2}+2N_{j}{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}+N_{j}+4{{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}}^{2}+2{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1})}{60}.

∎

As Conjecture
3.21
and Proposition
3.22
suggest, when the order of equivariant
L
is not to large, we can estimate
{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L})
by

{{\rm dim}}^{\textup{est}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L})=\prod_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}\binom{N_{j}+2{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}}{2{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}}\left(\frac{2L+1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}(\textup{Var}_{\bm{l}})^{3/2}}\right)

in the asymptotic regime. The dimensionalities of the GE-PI spaces scale linearly with respect to
L
.

Let us mention that when
N_{j}=1,\;\forall j\in\{1,2,\ldots,{N_{\rm blocks}}\}
, Proposition
3.22
degenerates to Proposition
3.13
.

3.5
Extension to
O(3)

In a variety of physical implementations, the
O(3)
symmetry is of greater interest than the
SO(3)
one described above, i.e., considering not only rotation equivariance, but also reflection equivariance. Whereas
O(3)
is not a connected group, our approach can still be applied to its two connected components separately.

First, since
O(3)=\{\pm 1\}\times SO(3)
, each element
R\in O(3)
can be written as
R=sQ
where
s:=\det(R)\in\{\pm 1\}
and
Q\in SO(3)
.
The irreducible representations of
O(3)
are then defined for
R=sQ\in O(3)
as

D^{\ell,+}(R)=D^{\ell}(Q),\qquad D^{\ell,-}(R)=sD^{\ell}(Q),

for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, where
D^{\ell}
stands for the Wigner-D matrix of degree
\ell
(see Definition
3.1
).
Given
l\in\mathcal{L}
with component
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, all the compatible bases
\{\phi^{l}_{m}\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{l}}
mentioned in Section
3.2
corresponding to the representation
D^{\ell}
satisfy

\phi^{l}_{m}(R\cdot{\bm{r}})=\phi^{l}_{m}(sQ\cdot{\bm{r}})=s^{\ell}\phi^{l}_{m}(Q\cdot{\bm{r}})=s^{\ell}\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{l}}D^{\ell}_{\mu m}(Q)\phi^{l}_{m}({\bm{r}}).

(3.42)

Therefore, they are also compatible bases of the group
O(3)
corresponding to the representation
D^{\ell,+}
when
\ell
is even, and
D^{\ell,-}
when
\ell
is odd. While other compatible bases may exist, we restrict ourselves to these specific bases.

The GE functions in
\left[V^{\bm{l}}\right]^{2L+1}
and the GE-PI functions in
\left[{\bar{V}}^{\bm{l}}\right]^{2L+1}
for the representation
D^{L,\pm}
are strongly connected to the equivariant functions for the representation
D^{L}
. For notation simplicity, we denote by
V^{{\bm{l}},L,\pm}
the space for GE functions in
\left[V^{\bm{l}}\right]^{2L+1}
corresponding to
D^{L,\pm}
. Likewise, we write
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L,\pm}
for the spaces of the GE-PI functions in
\left[{\bar{V}}^{\bm{l}}\right]^{2L+1}
for the representations
D^{L,\pm}
.

Proposition 3.23
.

Let
{\bm{l}}\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
,
L\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. If
\sum{\bm{l}}
is even, then

V^{{\bm{l}},L,+}=V^{{\bm{l}},L},\quad{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L,+}={\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L},

and

{{\rm dim}}(V^{{\bm{l}},L,-})={{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L,-})=0.

Similarly, if
\sum{\bm{l}}
is odd, then

V^{{\bm{l}},L,-}=V^{{\bm{l}},L},\quad{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L,-}={\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L},

and

{{\rm dim}}(V^{{\bm{l}},L,+})={{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L,+})=0.

Proof.

We prove only for the GE case, and for the case where
\sum{\bm{l}}
is even. The proofs for all other situations are similar.
On the one hand, for all
F\in V^{{\bm{l}},L,\pm}
, if we restrict the group action to elements of
SO(3)
only, there holds

\forall{\bm{r}}\in\Omega,\;Q\in SO(3),\quad F(Q\cdot{\bm{r}})=D^{L}(Q)\cdot F({\bm{r}}),

which indicates that
F\in V^{{\bm{l}},L}
. Hence,
V^{{\bm{l}},L,\pm}\subset V^{{\bm{l}},L}
.
On the other hand, for all
F\in V^{{\bm{l}},L}
, we have by definition that

\forall{\bm{r}}\in\Omega,\;Q\in SO(3),\quad F(Q\cdot{\bm{r}})=D^{L}(Q)\cdot F({\bm{r}}).

Furthermore, let
\{b^{{\bm{l}},L}_{i}\}_{i}
be a basis of
V^{{\bm{l}},L}
given in Proposition
3.9
, then there exists
\mathbf{f}=\{f_{i}\}_{i}\subset\mathbb{F}
such that

F=\sum_{i}f_{i}\cdot b^{{\bm{l}},L}_{i}=\sum_{i}f_{i}\cdot\left[\sum_{{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{{\bm{l}},K}}c_{{\bm{m}},i}\phi^{\bm{l}}_{\bm{m}}\right]_{K\in\mathcal{M}_{L}}.

The last equality is due to (
3.20
). Using the equivariant property (
3.42
) of the one particle compatible basis
\phi^{\bm{l}}_{\bm{m}}
, we have

\forall{\bm{r}}\in\Omega,\quad F(-I\cdot{\bm{r}})=(-1)^{\sum{\bm{l}}}\cdot F({\bm{r}}).

Thus, when
\sum{\bm{l}}
is even, we have immediately

\forall{\bm{r}}\in\Omega,\;R=sQ\in O(3),\quad F(R\cdot{\bm{r}})=s^{\sum{\bm{l}}}\cdot D^{L}(Q)\cdot F({\bm{r}})=D^{L}(Q)\cdot F({\bm{r}})=D^{L,+}(R)\cdot F({\bm{r}}).

Therefore,
F\in V^{{\bm{l}},L,+}
, and thus
V^{{\bm{l}},L,+}=V^{{\bm{l}},L}
. In addition,
F
can be in
V^{{\bm{l}},L,-}
only when
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{0}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition
3.23
implies that the spaces of the GE and GE-PI functions for the
O(3)
representations
D^{L,\pm}
,
V^{{\bm{l}},L,\pm}
and
{\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L,\pm}
, are either spanned by basis functions given in Propositions
3.9
and
3.18
, or are trivial zeros, depending on the parity of
\sum{\bm{l}}
.

4.1
Efficiency

We first illustrate the efficiency gain in obtaining the equivariant bases using our method, which is mainly due to the chosen approach to dealing with the permutations, and the full exploitation of the specific structure of the matrix M defined in (

4.1.1
Complexity of the algorithm

Figure 2:
Breakdown of construction time (ms) of the GE-PI bases versus the number of classes in the matrix
{{M}}^{\textup{up}}
(
3.28
), or the product of the number of classes and the number of basis functions.

To compute the coupling coefficients, three main steps have to be performed: 1) constructing the set
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}
(GE case) or the set of classes
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{l}}
(GE-PI case), 2) building the matrix
{{M}}^{\textup{up}}
, and 3) finding its kernel. In practice, it appears that the first step is typically negligible, which amounts to less than 2% of the overall cost. For instance, in the GE-PI case, when
{\bm{l}}=(8,8,8,8,8,8)
,
L=20
, generating the classes takes around 2
ms
, constructing the matrix
{{M}}^{\textup{up}}
takes 84
ms
, and finding the kernel of
{{M}}^{\textup{up}}
takes 74
ms
.
We therefore focus below on the complexity of the last two steps only, and for simplicity only in the GE-PI case, the GE case being similar.

First, to build
{{M}}^{\textup{up}}
, one needs to loop over the row indices
{\bm{m}}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{l}},K}
for
K\in\mathcal{M}_{L+1}\backslash\{-L-1,L\}
. Noting that the maximum number of non-zero elements in the row corresponding to
{\bm{m}}
is
|{\mathcal{N}_{\bm{m}}}|+1\leq 2\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}+1
, the whole cost of step 2) is bounded by

\text{{cost}}_{\rm 2}\lesssim\left(2\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}+1\right)\sum_{K\in\mathcal{M}_{L+1}\backslash\{L,L+1\}}|{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{l}},K}}|.

(4.1)

The computational cost of step 3) depends on the structure of the matrix
{{M}}^{\textup{up}}
. As can be seen on Figure
1
(c),
{{M}}^{\textup{up}}
is a supertriangular block matrix, that is, a block matrix with non-zero blocks only on the main diagonal and the super-subdiagonal. Moreover, the first
2L
diagonal blocks are scaled identities, and the last block
B_{L}^{+}
can in fact be made upper triangular, up to some proper convention of the ordering of columns. Therefore, the complexity of obtaining the kernel of
{{M}}^{\textup{up}}
, using a back-substitution approach, is bounded by

\text{{cost}}_{3}\lesssim{{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L})\left(2\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}+1\right)\sum_{K\in\mathcal{M}_{L+1}\backslash\{L,L+1\}}|{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{l}},K}}|.

(4.2)

Consequently, the total cost to generate a basis of GE-PI functions, neglecting step 1), is bounded by

\text{{cost}}^{\rm GE-PI}\lesssim\left({{\rm dim}}({\bar{V}}^{{\bm{l}},L})+1\right)\left(2\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm blocks}}{\bm{l}}^{(j)}_{1}+1\right)\sum_{K\in\mathcal{M}_{L+1}\backslash\{L,L+1\}}|{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{l}},K}}|.

We provide in Figure
2
a breakdown of the computational cost as a function of the number of classes (left) for a large variety of choices for parameters
{\bm{l}}=(l,\ldots,l)
of length
{N}
and equivariance order
L
. On the right of Figure
2

we plot the cost of step 3) for the same parameters as a function of the product of the number of classes and the dimensionality, from which we observe a linear complexity, as expected from (
4.1
) and (
4.2
).

{\bm{l}}=(l,l,\ldots,l)\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
: (a)
N=6
; (b)
N=20
; (c)
N=40
; and (d)

. For

As can be seen from Figure

, the space without any symmetry and that with both symmetries exhibit the largest and smallest dimensions, respectively. When the correlation order is small, GE turns out to be of equal importance, if not more, than PI for dimension reduction. However, as
{N}
increases, the PI dimension becomes increasingly closer to the GE-PI dimension, making PI the dominating source of dimension reduction.
Tables exhibiting precise dimensionality examples are given in Appendix
D
.
These results suggest that it is in general important for dimensionality reduction to include both permutation and group-equivariance symmetry, starting from permutation invariance.

5
Conclusion

In this article, we have provided a direct numerical method to efficiently construct group-equivariant and permutation-invariant basis sets for arbitrary linear Lie groups. For compact Lie groups, we have also proposed an alternative recursive construction. These methods are based on the use of Lie algebra to derive a linear system which has to be satisfied by GE and GE-PI functions. Using the specific form of the representation derivatives for
SO(3)
and
SU(2)
, the linear system is further simplified, and can be solved very efficiently exploiting sparsity.
Indeed, our method scales almost linearly with respect to the number of basis functions, largely outperforming existing packages in the literature, which typically scale exponentially with respect to the number of basis functions.
On top of this, we manage to provide the explicit dimensionality of the spaces of interest.
Comparing GE and GE-PI dimensionality shows how important it is in practice to consider the permutation-invariance to reduce the computational cost of approximating GE-PI functions.
It is interesting to note that the obtained structures for the linear systems are very similar in the GE and GE-PI cases, showcasing that imposing PI on top of GE using our method does not require extra computational cost; indeed the GE case can be seen as a particular case of GE-PI, in the extreme scenario that compatible bases remove the permutation-invariance constraint.
As an immediate application, this method can be used
as a GE-PI feature generator for a broad range of existing approaches, such as interatomic potentials, extending its utility beyond linear models to frameworks like the equivariant neural networks E3NN
[
10
]
and MACE
[
5
]
.

Appendix B
Analysis on Lie groups

Definition B.1
.

A
linear Lie group
is a subgroup of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
which is also a closed subset of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Let
G
be a linear Lie group. The derivative of a continuous representation
\rho
, denoted d
\rho
, is defined by

d\rho(g)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\rho(\exp(tg)).

We denote by
\textup{diff}_{g}\rho
, the
differential of
\rho
in
g
.

Proposition B.2
.

The derivative of a representation is its differential at the neutral element
e
. In other words

d\rho=\textup{diff}_{e}\rho.

Proof.

\displaystyle\rho(\exp(tg))-\rho(e)

\displaystyle=\rho\left(\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(tg)^{k}}{k!}\right)-\rho(e)

\displaystyle=\rho\left(e+t\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{t^{k-1}g^{k}}{k!}\right)-\rho(e)

\displaystyle=t\textup{diff}_{e}\rho\left(\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{t^{k-1}g^{k}}{k!}\right)+\underset{t\to 0}{o}\left(t\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{t^{k-1}g^{k}}{k!}\right)

\displaystyle=t\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{t^{k-1}}{k!}\textup{diff}_{e}\rho(g^{k})+\underset{t\to 0}{o}(t)

\displaystyle=t\textup{diff}_{e}\rho(g)+\underset{t\to 0}{o}(t).

Hence,
d\rho(g)=\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\rho(\exp(tg))-\rho(e)}{t}=\textup{diff}_{e}\rho(g)
.
∎

Theorem B.3
.

Let
G
be a linear Lie group, then

\forall g\in G,\;\textup{diff}_{g}\rho=\rho(g)\textup{diff}_{e}\rho.

Proof.

For
h\in G
, since
\rho
is a representation,

\rho(h)-\rho(g)=\rho(g)(\rho(hg^{-1})-\rho(e)).

Moreover, the inverse in the group is a homeomorphism, so
h\to g
if and only if
hg^{-1}\to e
. Thus,

\rho(h)-\rho(g)=\rho(g)\left(\textup{diff}_{e}\rho(hg^{-1})+\displaystyle\underset{h\to g}{o}(hg^{-1})\right).

Therefore,
\textup{diff}_{g}\rho=\rho(g)\textup{diff}_{e}\rho
.
∎

Therefore, it is sufficient to know the differential in
e
.

Corollary B.4
.

Assume that
G
is a connected linear Lie group and that
\rho=\displaystyle\sum_{{\bm{m}},{\bm{m}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\bm{l}}}c_{{\bm{m}},{\bm{m}}^{\prime}}\rho^{{\bm{l}}}_{{\bm{m}},{\bm{m}}^{\prime}}
is such that
\nabla\rho(e)=0
. Then
\rho
is constant.

Proof.

The assumption on
\rho
means that
\textup{diff}_{e}\rho=0
. However, by the previous theorem, for any
g\in G
we have that
\textup{diff}_{g}\rho=\rho(g)\textup{diff}_{e}\rho=0
. Since
G
is connected, we can thus conclude that
\rho
is constant.
∎

Proposition B.5
.

If
G
is a linear Lie group then the Lie algebra of
G
is

\textup{Lie}(G)=\{x\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{C}):\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\;\exp(tx)\in G\}.

Remark B.6
.

Any compact Lie group is isomorphic to a linear Lie group as their Lie algebras are isomorphic. Therefore, we can do the same computations in a compact Lie group.

Proof of Proposition

\frac{\partial D^{l}_{\mu m}}{\partial\alpha}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=-\mathrm{i}\cdot me^{-\mathrm{i}m\alpha}d^{l}_{\mu m}(\beta)e^{-\mathrm{i}\mu\gamma},

and

\frac{\partial D^{l}_{\mu m}}{\partial\gamma}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=-\mathrm{i}\cdot me^{-\mathrm{i}m\alpha}d^{l}_{\mu m}(\beta)e^{-\mathrm{i}\mu\gamma}.

Inserting
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(0,0,0)
and noticing that
d_{\mu m}^{l}(0)=\delta_{\mu m}
, we arrive at (
3.4
) and (
3.6
).

Differentiating the formula for the Wigner-D matrix with respect to
\beta
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial D^{l}_{\mu m}}{\partial\beta}

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=e^{-\mathrm{i}(m\alpha+\mu\gamma)}[(l+m)!(l-m)!(l+\mu)!(l-\mu)!]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\frac{(-1)^{s}}{2}\frac{(\cos\frac{\beta}{2})^{2l+\mu-m-2s-1}(\sin\frac{\beta}{2})^{m-\mu+2s-1}[(-2l-\mu+m+2s)(\sin\frac{\beta}{2})^{2}+(m-\mu+2s)(\cos\frac{\beta}{2})^{2}]}{(l+\mu-s)!s!(m-\mu+s)!(l-m-s)!}.

Let
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(0,0,0)
, we see that the above derivative can be non-zero only when
m-\mu+2s-1=0
or
m-\mu+2s+1=0
. Since
s\in\{\max(0,\mu-m),\ldots,\min(l+\mu,l-m)\}
, the latter is never true since its left hand side is always positive. In addition, the previous conditions can be fulfilled only when
|m-\mu|=1
. Otherwise, the derivative with respect to
\beta
at the origin will be zero.

If
m-\mu=1
, then only
s=0
can contribute to the summation. Thus

\displaystyle\frac{\partial D^{l}_{\mu m}}{\partial\beta}(I)

\displaystyle=[(l+m)!(l-m)!(l+\mu)!(l-\mu)!]^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{(-1)^{0}}{2}\frac{1}{(l+\mu)!0!1!(l-m)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{[(l+m)!(l-m)!(l+m-1)!(l-m+1)!]^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(l+m-1)!(l-m)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{[(l+m-1)!(l+m)(l-m)!(l+m-1)!(l-m)!(l-m+1)]^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(l+m-1)!(l-m)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{[(l+m)(l-m+1)]^{\frac{1}{2}}(l+m-1)!(l-m^{\prime}-1)!}{(l+m-1)!(l-m^{\prime}-1)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[(l+m)(l-m+1)]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Similarly, when
m-\mu=-1
, only
s=1
contributes to the summation and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial D^{l}_{\mu m}}{\partial\beta}(I)

\displaystyle=[(l+m)!(l-m)!(l+\mu)!(l-\mu)!]^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{(-1)^{1}}{2}\frac{1}{(l+\mu-1)!1!0!(l-m-1)!}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{[(l+m)!(l-m)!(l+m+1)!(l-m-1)!]^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(l+m)!(l-m-1)!}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{[(l+m)!(l-m-1)!(l-m)(l+m)!(l+m+1)(l-m-1)!]^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(l+m)!(l-m-1)!}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}[(l-m)(l+m+1)]^{\frac{1}{2}},

which completes the proof of (
3.5
).
∎
