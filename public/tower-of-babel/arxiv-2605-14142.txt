To discretize continually: Mean shift interacting particle systems for Bayesian inference
Ayoub Belhadji, Daniel Sharp, Youssef Marzouk

Abstract
Integration against a probability distribution given its unnormalized density is a central task in Bayesian inference and other fields. We introduce new methods for approximating such expectations with a small set of weighted samples—i.e., a quadrature rule—constructed via an interacting particle system that minimizes maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) to the target distribution. These methods extend the classical mean shift algorithm, as well as recent algorithms for optimal quantization of empirical distributions, to the case of continuous distributions. Crucially, our approach creates dynamics for MMD minimization that are invariant to the unknown normalizing constant; they also admit both gradient-free and gradient-informed implementations. The resulting mean shift interacting particle systems converge quickly, capture anisotropy and multi-modality, avoid mode collapse, and scale to high dimensions. We demonstrate their performance on a wide range of benchmark sampling problems, including multi-modal mixtures, Bayesian hierarchical models, PDE-constrained inverse problems, and beyond.

Introduction

The need to compute expectations with respect to a target probability distribution
\pi
is ubiquitous in statistical inference and machine learning.
Most distributions, however, do not admit any closed-form numerical integration technique. This situation often arises in Bayesian inference, where posterior
quantities of interest are expressed as integrals over a distribution whose density is only known up to normalization; similar situations arise in computational chemistry and other physical sciences.

Recent work has approached integral discretization through gradient flows on the space of probability measures
[
71
,
21
]
.
In this framework, a discretization is identified with a probability measure,
often an empirical measure supported on finitely many particles,
and is characterized as the solution of a variational problem. One then constructs a gradient flow to minimize the chosen discrepancy between this measure and the target distribution
\pi
. Such formulations naturally give rise to partial differential equations (PDEs) which may be discretized via systems of interacting particles
[
59
,
32
,
33
,
17
,
63
,
76
]
.

Crucially, the most useful gradient flow methods have dynamics invariant to the unknown normalizing constant of the target density; this feature is usually due to the choice of discrepancy, e.g., Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence,
\chi^{2}
divergence, or kernelized Stein discrepancy (KSD).
Designing particle systems as discretizations of gradient flows leads to algorithms with strong theoretical guarantees largely concerned with the mean-field limit, i.e., when the number of particles goes to infinity. The practical, non-asymptotic regime (with finite particles) still presents numerous challenges, especially for target distributions exhibiting strong non-Gaussianity, local anisotropy, and multi-modality.

In this paper, we study integration through the lens of
quadrature rules
. A quadrature rule approximates the expectation of a function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
by a weighted average of function evaluations,

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f(Y)]=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(y)\approx\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}f(y_{i}),

(1)

where the weights
w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and the nodes
y_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
. From this perspective, most interacting particle systems use
uniform
weights, a design choice that constrains their effectiveness as numerical integration schemes. We instead develop
weighted
quadrature rules for essentially arbitrary unnormalized densities. This task is nontrivial, as classical quadrature constructions rely on estimating high-order moments
[
39
,
34
]
, which is generally intractable for unnormalized densities.

We show that effective quadrature rules for unnormalized densities can be designed by minimizing a regularized maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) associated with the squared exponential kernel.
This result is unexpected: MMD minimization is typically viewed as intractable for high-dimensional unnormalized densities.
Our approach circumvents this obstacle by introducing a damped fixed-point iteration whose dynamics are invariant, by construction, to the unknown normalizing constant.
Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We extend an optimal quantization method from the setting of empirical target distributions to continuous target distributions known through their unnormalized densities, as in sampling and Bayesian inference.

•

We introduce finite-particle dynamics for MMD minimization in this setting, and prove that they are invariant to the normalization of the target despite the fact that MMD itself depends on this constant.

•

We show that certain regularization techniques needed for stability of our kernel implementation are cleanly interpretable as optimization of a
regularized
MMD.

•

We introduce multiple
gradient-free
and
gradient (score)-informed
formulations of our algorithm, suitable for a broad range of application domains.

•

We demonstrate that our approach is scalable to high dimensions and to diverse, geometrically challenging (e.g., strongly multi-modal and anisotropic) target distributions.

For a matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with elements
[\bm{A}]_{ij}=a_{ij}
, we write the matrix slice as
[\bm{A}]_{i,:}\coloneqq(a_{i1},a_{i2},\ldots,a_{in})
. Throughout the paper, we consider a domain of interest
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a density
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Then, we use a set of particles
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\}\subset\mathcal{X}
in a configuration denoted
Y\in\mathcal{X}^{M}
with
[Y]_{i,:}:=y_{i}
.
For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, we use the notation
\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
to denote the diagonal matrix whose
(i,i)
-th entry is
x_{i}
.
Given a symmetric positive-definite kernel
\kappa:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we define the kernel matrix
\bm{K}:\mathcal{X}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
via
[\bm{K}(Y)]_{ij}=\kappa(y_{i},y_{j})
. We write the
kernel mean embedding

\bm{v}_{0}:\mathcal{X}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
of a density

as

v_{0}(y)=\int\kappa(x,y)\,\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x),\quad[\bm{v}_{0}(Y)]_{i}=v_{0}(y_{i}).

(2)

Similarly, we define the
kernel embedding of the first moment

\bm{v}_{1}:\mathcal{X}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
as

v_{1}(y)=\int x\kappa(x,y)\,\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x),\quad[\bm{v}_{1}(Y)]_{i,:}=v_{1}(y_{i}).

(3)

Theory and background

We contextualize our approach by reviewing literature on integration given an unnormalized density.

2.1
From MCMC methods and Langevin dynamics to interacting particle systems

The overdamped Langevin diffusion admits an invariant law with density
\pi
. Discretizing this diffusion leads to Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms such as ULA and MALA
[
12
,
69
]
, which incorporate gradients of the log-density and enable more efficient exploration. In the latter case, Metropolization
[
65
,
68
]
ensures that the target distribution is preserved after discretization. There are of course myriad other sampling algorithms using Markov chains for Bayesian inference and generative modeling, including both gradient-free
[
40
,
42
]
and gradient-based
[
27
,
48
]
approaches. Yet these algorithms often struggle on the geometrically complex target distributions described in §
1
.

51
interpret the Langevin dynamics as the gradient flow of the KL divergence in the Wasserstein geometry. The gradient flow is expressed as a PDE governing the evolution of a probability density
\rho_{t}
for which
\mathrm{KL}(\rho_{t}\|\pi)
decreases with
t
.
This variational point of view has since been extended to alternative geometries and divergences.
These variational approaches emphasize
using an
ensemble of interacting particles
instead of independent Markov chains.

Stein variational gradient descent

SVGD is derived as a gradient flow of the KL divergence under the Stein geometry
[
28
]
, which is induced by a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
[
59
]
. At each iteration, a nonlinear map defined by the kernel of the RKHS is applied to the set of particles. This algorithm has since been the subject of extensive theoretical investigation.
Convergence of mean-field, i.e., infinite-particle, SVGD dynamics in various divergences and discrepancies has been discussed thoroughly
[
60
,
56
,
20
,
70
,
74
,
28
,
45
]
. More recently,
[
72
,
61
,
24
,
6
]
obtain convergence guarantees for the finite-particle SVGD algorithm.
Despite this theory, standard SVGD empirically suffers from mode collapse, often failing to cover all modes of multi-modal target distributions
[
78
]
.

Interacting particle Langevin samplers

Inspired by methods for Bayesian filtering
[
30
]
, ensemble Kalman approaches have been introduced for gradient-free sampling in the Bayesian setting, particularly with Gaussian observation models. In the mean-field limit, certain ensemble Kalman samplers (EKS) realize gradient flows of the KL divergence with respect to the so-called Kalman–Wasserstein geometry. The resulting PDE is a Fokker–Planck equation preconditioned by the covariance matrix of the evolving density
[
32
]
. EKS also admits affine-invariant variants, e.g., ALDI
[
33
]
, a crucial property for highly anisotropic target densities where standard Langevin dynamics converge slowly.

Kernel Fisher–Rao flow

Instead of using the Wasserstein geometry as in
[
51
]
, a few recent algorithms propose using gradient flow in the
Fisher–Rao
geometry
[
76
,
63
]
. These methods follow a log-geometric path
\log\pi_{\tau}:=(1-\tau)\log\pi_{0}+\tau\log\pi_{1}
,
\tau\in[0,1]
, which connects an initial
\pi_{0}
and a target
\pi_{1}
. While such bridges between prior and posterior have been used in Bayesian inference for many years
[
37
]
, schemes in
[
76
,
63
]
implement either gradient-free and gradient-informed estimators of particle transport using kernel methods, and allow practitioners to choose a time integration scheme over
\tau
. The theory of Fisher–Rao gradient flows has also seen much recent development
[
19
,
77
,
14
]
.

Consensus-based methods

Consensus-based sampling (CBS) comprises a system of interacting particles designed to sample from an unnormalized density
[
17
]
; it builds on consensus-based optimization methods for gradient-free non-convex optimization
[
67
]
and relates to collective behavior modeling
[
54
,
26
,
75
,
23
,
16
]
. At each iteration, particles are driven toward a consensus point, a weighted average favoring lower objective values, while exploring the space through additive noise. CBS is amenable to theoretical study from the mean-field perspective
[
15
,
49
,
36
,
57
]
, with explicit convergence rates, though these guarantees are generally limited to unimodal densities
[
17
,
63
]
.

2.2
MMD–optimizing methods

MMD is widely used in numerical analysis to assess quadrature rules
[
46
,
4
,
64
,
52
]
.
Using the reproducing kernel
\kappa
for the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
, the MMD is given by

\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mu,\mu}[\kappa(X,X^{\prime})]-2\mathbb{E}_{\mu,\nu}[\kappa(X,Y)]+\mathbb{E}_{\nu,\nu}[\kappa(Y,Y^{\prime})]\,.

(4)

Unlike the KL divergence, MMD is well defined even for mutually singular measures, as when comparing a continuous distribution to a discrete empirical distribution. Indeed, for a quadrature rule expressed as a discrete measure
{p}(\bm{w},Y)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\delta_{y_{i}}
, the worst-case integration error over the unit ball of the RKHS, given by

\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1}\bigg|\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)\mathrm{d}\pi(x)-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}f(y_{i})\bigg|,

coincides with
\mathrm{MMD}(\pi,{p}(\bm{w},Y))
.
This correspondence has motivated minimizing
\mathrm{MMD}(\pi,{p}(\bm{w},Y))
using different approaches, including ridge leverage score sampling
[
5
]
, determinantal point processes
[
7
,
11
]
, continuous volume sampling
[
8
]
, Fekete points
[
53
]
, recombination algorithms
[
43
,
44
]
,
and gradient flows
[
2
,
38
]
. These methods rely on symmetric kernels and, unfortunately,
assume a normalized density. Using (
4
) for density
\tilde{\pi}=Z_{\tilde{\pi}}\pi
, we see

\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(\tilde{\pi},{p}(\bm{w},Y))=C_{\tilde{\pi}}-2Z_{\tilde{\pi}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\kappa(X,y_{i})\right]+\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}w_{i}w_{j}\kappa(y_{i},y_{j}),

(5)

for some constant
C_{\tilde{\pi}}
independent of
p
. This affine relationship of the squared MMD with
Z_{\tilde{\pi}}
makes the former infeasible to directly estimate, let alone minimize, for a general, unnormalized target.

The kernel Stein discrepancy (KSD), proposed for assessing sample quality
[
58
,
22
,
41
]
, is a kernelized semi-metric invariant to this normalization and is equivalent to the MMD associated with a
\pi
-induced Stein kernel. KSD minimization has been used in the design of quadrature rules
[
18
,
55
]
. By construction, however, first-order minimization of the KSD requires second-order information from the target density
\pi

[
55
]
, and thus it is often difficult or impossible to perform this minimization without resorting to greedy methods
[
18
]
or otherwise using methods that scale poorly in dimension.

3
Main results

As discussed above, the minimization of KL divergence (and related functionals) underlies many methods for sampling from unnormalized densities, in large part because the associated objectives admit gradient-based optimization schemes that are invariant to the unknown normalizing constant. In contrast, the minimization of MMD (excluding Stein kernels) is typically seen as infeasible here. We challenge this notion and show that
MMD minimization is achievable for unnormalized densities.

Recalling the definition of
{p}(\bm{w},Y)
, consider the function
F^{\pi}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathcal{X}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
given by

F^{\pi}(\bm{w},Y):=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{MMD}^{2}\left(\pi,{p}(\bm{w},Y)\right).

(6)

Observe that any critical point
(\bm{w}^{\ast},Y^{\ast})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathcal{X}^{M}
of
F
is defined via

\nabla_{\bm{w}}F^{\pi}(\bm{w}^{\ast},Y^{\ast})=0,\quad\nabla_{y_{i}}F^{\pi}(\bm{w}^{\ast},Y^{\ast})=0.

(7)

We use conditions (
7
), which are necessary for optimality, as the desiderata for our quadrature rule. Recalling the notation
v_{0}
and
v_{1}
from (
2
) and (
3
), introduce the squared exponential kernel
\kappa(x,y)=\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{-2}\|x-y\|^{2}\bigr)
to express (
7
) as

\bm{K}(Y^{\ast})\bm{w}^{\ast}=\bm{v}_{0}(Y^{\ast}),\quad\bm{W}^{\ast}\bigl(\bm{K}(Y^{\ast})\bm{W}^{\ast}\ Y^{\ast}-\bm{v}_{1}(Y^{\ast})\bigr)=\bm{0},

(8)

where
\bm{W}^{\ast}:=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{w}^{\ast})
is the diagonal weight matrix. For a given
Y
, the first equation is linear in
\bm{w}^{\ast}
; we thus let the function
\bm{w}^{\ast}:\mathcal{X}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
describe the weights for configuration
Y
as

\bm{w}^{\ast}(Y)=\bm{K}(Y)^{-1}\bm{v}_{0}(Y),

(9)

and define
\bm{W}^{\ast}(Y)=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{w}^{\ast}(Y))
.

The second condition in (
8
) is more challenging. A strategy to satisfy the condition is to alternate between solving the linear system (
9
) for
\bm{w}^{\ast}
and updating the configuration
Y
through the iteration

Y^{(t+1)}=(1-\eta)Y^{(t)}+\eta\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP}}(Y^{(t)}).

(10)

Here
\eta>0
is a step size, and the function
\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP}}:\mathcal{X}^{M}\to\mathcal{X}^{M}
is defined by

\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP}}(Y):=\bm{W}^{\ast}(Y)^{-1}\bm{K}(Y)^{-1}\bm{v}_{1}(Y).

(11)

This algorithm, known as
mean shift interacting particles
(MSIP), was introduced in
[
9
]
to minimize the MMD with respect to a probability measure
\pi
. In particular, it was proven that the dynamics (
10
) correspond to a preconditioned gradient descent of the function
F^{\pi}_{M}:\mathcal{X}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
defined as

F^{\pi}_{M}(Y)=\inf_{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}F^{\pi}(\bm{w},Y)\,.

(12)

The implementation of MSIP, however, was previously restricted to the “data-driven” setting where
\pi
is available as an empirical distribution, as this input enables direct evaluation of the functions
v_{0}
,
v_{1}
.

In this work, we overcome this restriction and adapt MSIP to the case of an unnormalized density
\tilde{\pi}
.
Our approach builds on the observation that the MSIP map
\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP}}
, unlike
F^{\pi}
itself, is invariant to the normalizing constant. Unfortunately, this property alone does not suffice for a systematic implementation of MSIP in the unnormalized setting. Two more challenges must be addressed: numerical stability and reliance of the map
\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP}}
on the embeddings
v_{0}
and
v_{1}
.

3.1
Regularized mean shift interacting particles

We first grapple with extending the definition of the MSIP map to arbitrary particle configurations
Y
.
The problem of conditioning primarily stems from the explicit inversion of the kernel matrix
\bm{K}(Y)
. A classical remedy for such numerical issues is to replace
\bm{K}(Y)
with its regularized counterpart
\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y):=\bm{K}(Y)+\lambda\bm{I}_{M}
for some
\lambda>0
.
We thus use a
regularized
MSIP map
\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}
defined as

\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}(Y):=\bm{W}^{\ast}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\bm{v}_{1}(Y)

(13)

where
\bm{W}^{\ast}_{\lambda}(Y)=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{w}_{\lambda}^{\ast}(Y))
, with

\bm{w}_{\lambda}^{\ast}(Y)=\bm{K}_{\lambda}({Y})^{-1}\bm{v}_{0}({Y}),

(14)

which is a regularization of the optimal weights defined by (
9
). This regularized map provides significantly more numerical stability—the matrix
\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)
is nonsingular for any configuration
Y
—but, crucially, still yields preconditioned gradient descent of a penalized MMD, as we show below. Define the penalized objective function
F^{\pi}_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathcal{X}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
as

F^{\pi}_{\lambda}(\bm{w},Y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{MMD}\left(\pi,{p}(\bm{w},Y)\right)^{2}+\lambda\|\bm{w}\|^{2}=F^{\pi}(\bm{w},Y)+\lambda\|\bm{w}\|^{2},

(15)

and consider the function
F^{\pi}_{M,\lambda}:\mathcal{X}^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

F^{\pi}_{M,\lambda}(Y)\coloneqq\inf_{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}F^{\pi}_{\lambda}(\bm{w},Y).

(16)

Theorem 3.1
.

For the squared exponential kernel, we have

\nabla F^{\pi}_{M,\lambda}(Y)=\sigma^{-2}\bm{W}^{\ast}_{\lambda}(Y)\Big(\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\bm{W}^{\ast}_{\lambda}(Y)Y-\bm{v}_{1}(Y)\Big),

(17)

where
\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y):=\bm{K}(Y)+\lambda\bm{I}_{M}
, and
\bm{W}^{\ast}_{\lambda}(Y)=\textup{diag}(\bm{w}_{\lambda}^{\ast}(Y))
, with
\bm{w}_{\lambda}^{\ast}(Y)
from (
14
).

Due to
Theorem

3.1
,
we can see that the regularized dynamics

Y^{(t+1)}=(1-\eta)Y^{(t)}+\eta\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}(Y^{(t)}),

(18)

for nonzero weights
\bm{\hat{w}}_{\lambda}
leads to

\displaystyle Y^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=Y^{(t)}-\eta P_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\nabla F^{\pi}_{M,\lambda}(Y),

(19)

\displaystyle\text{with preconditioner }P_{\lambda}(Y)

\displaystyle:=\sigma^{-2}\bm{W}^{\ast}_{\lambda}(Y)\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\bm{W}^{\ast}_{\lambda}(Y).

(20)

In other words,
\lambda
-regularization mollifies
the target functional while ensuring that
\bm{K}_{\lambda}
is invertible.

3.2
Numerical implementations

Implementing the iteration (
18
) requires the kernel mean embedding
\bm{v}_{0}
and the kernelized first moment
\bm{v}_{1}
for any set of particles
Y
. Unlike the data-driven setting where
\pi
is an empirical measure, these functions do not admit closed-form expressions. In what follows, we propose computationally tractable estimators when using the squared exponential kernel. First, note that

\sigma^{2}\nabla\log v_{0}(y)=\frac{\sigma^{2}\nabla v_{0}(y)}{v_{0}(y)}=\frac{v_{1}(y)-yv_{0}(y)}{v_{0}(y)}=\frac{v_{1}}{v_{0}}(y)-y.

(21)

Therefore, we can consider the primitive quantities to be the pair of functions
(v_{0},v_{1})
or the pair
(\log v_{0},\,\sigma^{2}\nabla\log v_{0})
with some equivalence.
Our estimators rely on the following observation:

\displaystyle v_{0}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\pi(x)\mathrm{d}x=\omega_{\sigma,d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\pi(y+\sigma\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\xi),

(22)

where
\rho
is the standard multivariate Gaussian distribution and
\omega_{\sigma,d}:=\big(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma\big)^{d}
. Similarly,

\displaystyle v_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=\omega_{\sigma,d}\int(y+\sigma\xi)\pi(y+\sigma\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\xi)

(23)

\displaystyle=yv_{0}(y)+\sigma\omega_{\sigma,d}\int\xi\pi(y+\sigma\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\xi).

(24)

The problem of estimating
v_{0}
and
v_{1}
over the unnormalized density
\pi
thus reduces to computing integrals with respect to a multivariate Gaussian distribution. We propose to estimate these integrals via an inner quadrature rule. Suppose
(u_{q},\xi_{q})_{q=1}^{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{X}
satisfies

\int\varphi(\xi)\mathrm{d}\rho(\xi)\approx\sum\limits_{q=1}^{Q}u_{q}\varphi(\xi_{q}),

(25)

where
\varphi
is a real-valued function defined on
\mathcal{X}
.
A first choice for this
inner
quadrature rule is Monte Carlo, which involves sampling
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{Q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\rho
with weights
u_{q}=Q^{-1}
. A second choice uses spherical radial quadrature rules
[
10
,
1
]
. A third option is a simple one-point quadrature rule, i.e., using
Q=1
, with
u_{1}=1
and
\xi_{1}=0
.
Using the discretization in (
25
) for the integrand
\xi\mapsto\pi(y+\sigma\xi)
is straightforward
and yields an approximation of
v_{0}
, denoted by
\hat{v}_{0}
and defined by

\hat{v}_{0}^{Q}(y):=\omega_{\sigma,d}\sum\limits_{q=1}^{Q}u_{q}\pi(y+\sigma\xi_{q}).

(26)

Since
v_{1}
has multiple representations (
23
)–(
24
), strategies for approximating
v_{1}
are discussed below.

Gradient-free estimator

Applying the quadrature (
25
) to the first integral representation of
v_{1}
(
23
) leads to a gradient-free approximation,

\hat{v}_{1}^{\mathrm{gf}}(y):=\omega_{\sigma,d}\sum\limits_{q=1}^{Q}u_{q}(y+\sigma\xi_{q})\pi(y+\sigma\xi_{q}).

(27)

Stein estimator

Using Stein’s identity
[
73
]
simplifies the representation in (
24
) of
v_{1}
:

v_{1}(y)=y\,v_{0}(y)+\sigma^{2}\omega_{\sigma,d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla\pi(y+\sigma\xi)\mathrm{d}\rho(\xi).

(28)

Applying the quadrature (
25
) to (
28
), we derive the Stein estimator:

\hat{v}^{SQ}_{1}(y)=y\,\hat{v}^{Q}_{0}(y)+\sigma^{2}\omega_{\sigma,d}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}(u_{q}\pi(y+\sigma\xi_{q}))\,\nabla\log\pi(y+\sigma\xi_{q}),

(29)

noting that
\nabla\pi=\pi\nabla\log\pi
, where
\nabla\log\pi
is often referred to as the (Stein) score.
For the one-point inner quadrature rule, the Stein estimator reduces to

\hat{v}_{0}^{1}(y)=\omega_{\sigma,d}\pi(y),\quad\hat{v}_{1}^{\mathrm{S1}}(y)=\omega_{\sigma,d}\,\pi(y)\bigl(y+\sigma^{2}\nabla\log\pi(y)\bigr).

(30)

In particular, we see
\sigma^{2}\nabla\log\pi(y)
corresponds to
\hat{v}_{1}^{\mathrm{S1}}(y)/\hat{v}_{0}^{1}(y)-y
; this closely echoes
\nabla\log v_{0}
in (
21
).
Moreover, the one-point estimators can recover a Gaussian deconvolution of
\pi
: see Proposition
B.1
for details. For this reason, we consider
\hat{v}_{0}^{1}
and
\hat{v}_{0}^{\mathrm{S1}}
to be
Fredholm
estimators.

3.3
MSIP invariance to normalization

Now that we have shown strategies for practically implementing MSIP, the regularized MSIP dynamics can be implemented using the following iteration:

Y^{(t+1)}=(1-\eta)Y^{(t)}+\eta\hat{\Psi}_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}(Y^{(t)}),

(31)

where
\hat{\Psi}_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}
is obtained by replacing
v_{0}
and
v_{1}
by
\hat{v}_{0}
and
\hat{v}_{1}
respectively in (
13
).

\hat{\Psi}_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}(Y)=\bm{W}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\hat{\bm{v}}_{1}(Y).

(32)

We detail this method in
Algorithm

1
. Next, we show that this iteration is invariant to the normalizing constant of a target density
\pi
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}(\cdot\,;\pi)
be the regularized MSIP map for density
\pi
. For any
Y\in\mathcal{X}^{M}
,

\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}(Y;\pi)=\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}(Y;\;C\,\pi)\ \ \forall C>0.

(33)

The invariance of
\hat{\Psi}_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}
to normalization reasons similarly to Proposition
3.2
.
In essence, this invariance comes from the preconditioner
P_{\lambda}
in (

3.4
Related kernelized methods

With details of our method in hand, we contrast it with other recent kernel methods used in sampling and optimization.
One line of work
considers interacting particle methods for Bayesian neural networks.
In these approaches, repulsion and diversity are enforced at the level of network predictions rather than directly on the weights; see, e.g.,
[
29
]
. Understanding the precise relationship between such function-space interactions and parameter-space interacting particle systems such as MSIP lies outside the scope of the present work.
Polarized CBS
[
13
]
enriches classical CBS with interaction mechanisms designed to improve exploration of multi-modal distributions. While polarized CBS shares certain similarities with our gradient-free MSIPGF, the two approaches arise from fundamentally different principles: the former remains centered around consensus-driven dynamics, whereas MSIPGF constructs particle interactions through kernelized approximations of nonlocal quantities such as
v_{0}
and
v_{1}
. The theoretical analysis in
[
13
]
is primarily carried out in the large-
M
regime.
Drifting
[
25
]
is a recent kernelized approach for generative modeling that constructs a vector field from the discrepancy between mean-shift maps associated with the target samples and the particle distribution. In contrast to MSIP, drifting is designed for sample-based generative modeling rather than for sampling from unnormalized densities.

We observe that the different variants of MSIP consistently achieve either the lowest KSD values or values extremely close to the best-performing method across essentially all benchmark distributions. In contrast, competing approaches exhibit substantially less robustness across geometries and dimensions. In particular, SVGD frequently underperforms relative to MSIP variants, not only on challenging targets such as funnel, joker, and Himmelblau distributions, but also on several Gaussian mixture models, especially when the evaluation bandwidth matches the characteristic scale used to construct the targets. These observations also highlight a limitation of KSD-based evaluation: on multi-modal distributions, small KSD values do not necessarily imply accurate global exploration or faithful mode allocation. This phenomenon appears sharply when examining visualizations of the two-dimensional distributions, as in
Section

B.4
. Similarly, ALDI performs strongly on funnel distributions, where its affine-invariant structure is advantageous, but is generally less competitive on the remaining targets.
By contrast, the gradient-free variant MSIP-GF remains remarkably competitive across nearly all synthetic experiments, including difficult high-dimensional settings. Overall, the MSIP family is the only class of methods in this benchmark that consistently maintains strong and stable performance simultaneously across multi-modal, anisotropic, heavy-tailed, and high-dimensional targets.

5
Conclusion

We have shown that MSIP is an effective new approach for producing
weighted quadratures
of distributions known only through their unnormalized density.
To our knowledge, it is the most scalable method for MMD minimization in this setting.
Our experiments demonstrate that MSIP algorithms enjoy robust performance over a very broad range of target distributions, achieving approximation quality that is consistently competitive with—and often superior to—current state-of-the-art sampling approaches.
A key strength of MSIP is that its dynamics balance systematic modal coverage (i.e., quickly finding all modes of a target) with accurate representation of the shape and extent of each mode.

Underlying MSIP is the internal quadrature rule, a distinctive degree of freedom not afforded in typical approaches. Through the quantities
v_{0}
and
v_{1}
, this quadrature highlights non-local information, in lieu of dynamics driven by the score function alone. Our experiments suggest that accurately approximating these global kernel-based quantities can lead to substantial improvements in performance, particularly for challenging multi-modal and anisotropic targets.

Future work will focus on establishing theoretical guarantees for MSIP, including convergence and approximation results, as the nonconvex optimization at the core of the method remains challenging analytically. We also plan to advance systematic and adaptive strategies for selecting the internal quadrature rule and associated kernel hyperparameters.

A.2
MSIP for mixtures of Gaussians

Consider a mixture of Gaussians

\pi(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_{k}\mathcal{N}(x;\,\mu_{k},\,\Sigma_{k})

(34)

with

\mathcal{N}(x;\,\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k}):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma_{k}|^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x-\mu_{k})^{T}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}(x-\mu_{k})\right)

(35)

We consider the kernel
\kappa_{\sigma}(x,y)=\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x-y\|^{2})=Z_{\sigma}\mathcal{N}(x;\,y,\sigma^{2}I)
for
Z_{\sigma}=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{d/2}
, and we obtain

\displaystyle v_{0}(y)=\int\kappa_{\sigma}(x,y)\pi(x)\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle Z_{\sigma}\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_{k}\mathcal{N}(y\mid\mu_{k},\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k})

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{\sigma}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{m_{k}}{|\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}|^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(y-\mu_{k})^{T}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(y-\mu_{k})\right)

(37)

where
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}=\Sigma_{k}+\sigma^{2}I
.
Now, let
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}=L_{k}L_{k}^{\top}
be the Cholesky decomposition of
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}
and let
z_{k}:=L_{k}^{-1}(y-\mu_{k})
. We have
\log|\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}|^{1/2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\log[L_{k}]_{jj}
and, thus,

\log v_{0}(y)=\log\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\left(\log m_{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\log[L_{k}]_{jj}-\frac{1}{2}\|z_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\right)+d\log\sigma

(38)

Therefore,

\nabla\log v_{0}(y)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}(y)\left(-\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(y-\mu_{k})\right),

(39)

where

r_{k}(y):=\frac{m_{k}\,\mathcal{N}(y;\,\mu_{k},\,\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k})}{\sum_{i=1}^{K}m_{i}\,\mathcal{N}(y;\,\mu_{i},\,\widetilde{\Sigma}_{j})}

(40)

Using the Cholesky factors,
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(y-\mu_{k})=L_{k}^{-\top}z_{k}
, we get

\nabla\log v_{0}(y)=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}(y)L_{k}^{-\top}z_{k}.

(41)

This last identity is purely for computational convenience.

\hat{\bm{w}}(Y)=\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\bm{v}_{0}(Y)
, we get

\nabla_{ij}\hat{\bm{w}}(Y)=\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\nabla_{ij}\bm{v}_{0}(Y)-\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\,\nabla_{ij}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\,\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\bm{v}_{0}(Y),

thus

\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\nabla_{ij}\hat{\bm{w}}(Y)=\nabla_{ij}\bm{v}_{0}(Y)-\nabla_{ij}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\,\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),

so that

\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\ \bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\nabla_{ij}\hat{\bm{w}}(Y)\rangle=\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\ \nabla_{ij}\bm{v}_{0}(Y)\rangle-\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\ \nabla_{ij}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\hat{\bm{w}}(Y)\rangle.

(46)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}2\nabla_{ij}F_{M,\lambda}(Y)&=-\,2\Big(\,\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,\nabla_{ij}\bm{v}_{0}(Y)\rangle-\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,\nabla_{ij}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\,\hat{\bm{w}}(Y)\rangle\,\Big)\\
&\quad\>-\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,\nabla_{ij}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\,\hat{\bm{w}}(Y)\rangle\\
&=-2\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,\nabla_{ij}\bm{v}_{0}(Y)\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,\nabla_{ij}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\,\hat{\bm{w}}(Y)\rangle.\end{split}

(47)

Now recall that

[\nabla_{ij}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)]_{m_{1},m_{2}}=\nabla_{ij}\kappa(y_{m_{1}},y_{m_{2}}),

for all
m_{1},\,m_{2}\in[M]
; the regularization parameter
\lambda
disappears here as it is not a function of
Y
. Therefore, for the squared-exponential
\kappa
with
\bar{\kappa}(x,y):=\sigma^{-2}\kappa(x,y)
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{ij}\kappa(y_{m_{1}},y_{m_{2}})

\displaystyle=\delta_{i,m_{1}}(y_{m_{2}j}-y_{ij})\bar{\kappa}(y_{i},y_{m_{2}})+\delta_{i,m_{2}}(y_{m_{1}j}-y_{ij})\bar{\kappa}(y_{m_{1}},y_{i})

(48)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}\Big(\delta_{i,m_{1}}(y_{m_{2}j}-y_{ij})\kappa(y_{i},y_{m_{2}})+\delta_{i,m_{2}}(y_{m_{1}j}-y_{ij})\kappa(y_{m_{1}},y_{i})\Big)

(49)

First, we denote
[\bm{a}\otimes\bm{b}]_{ij}=a_{i}b_{j}
as the outer product between vectors
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
with possibly different dimensions. We also denote
\bm{a}\odot\bm{b}
as the elementwise (i.e., Hadamard) product between
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
with identical dimensions. Then, we have

\nabla_{ij}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)=\bm{b}_{ij}\otimes\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{i}\otimes\bm{b}_{ij},

where
\bm{e}_{i}
is the
i
th elementary vector, i.e.,
[\bm{e}_{i}]_{m}=\delta_{im},
and we use the expression of the squared exponential kernel
to get definition

\bm{b}_{ij}:=\sigma^{-2}(Y_{j}-y_{ij}\bm{1})\odot\bm{K}_{i}=\sigma^{-2}\Big(Y_{j}\odot\bm{K}_{i}-y_{ij}\bm{K}_{i}\Big),

with
Y_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the vector containing the
j
-th entry of each
y_{i}
, identical to the
j
th column of the matrix
Y
, and
\bm{K}_{i}=\bm{K}(Y)\bm{e}_{i}
. Then,

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,\nabla_{ij}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\,\hat{\bm{w}}(Y)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,(\bm{b}_{ij}\otimes\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{i}\otimes\bm{b}_{ij})\hat{\bm{w}}(Y)\rangle

\displaystyle=2\hat{w}_{i}(Y)\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,\bm{b}_{ij}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\sigma^{-2}\hat{w}_{i}(Y)\Big(\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,Y_{j}\odot\bm{K}_{i}-y_{ij}\bm{K}_{i}\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle=2\sigma^{-2}\hat{w}_{i}(Y)\Big(\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,Y_{j}\odot\bm{K}_{i}\rangle-y_{ij}\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,\bm{K}_{i}\rangle\Big)

(50)

For
m\in[M]
, we recall that
\nabla\kappa
is bounded on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
to prove

\displaystyle\nabla_{ij}\bm{v}_{0}(Y)

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla_{ij}v_{0}(y_{i})\right)\bm{e}_{i}

\displaystyle\nabla_{ij}v_{0}(y_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla_{ij}\kappa(x,y_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{j}-y_{ij})\bar{\kappa}(x,y_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}\Big(\int_{\mathcal{X}}x_{j}\kappa(x,y_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x)-y_{ij}v_{0}(y_{i})\Big),

where
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
is the
j
th entry of integration variable
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and we recall that
v_{0}(y)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\kappa(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x)
.
This equivalence is precisely due to the fact that
\kappa\propto\bar{\kappa}
. For derivative kernel
\bar{\kappa}
as discussed in
[
9
]
, this identity would not hold. We then have

\nabla_{ij}\bm{v}_{0}(Y)=\sigma^{-2}\left([v_{1}(y_{i})]_{j}-y_{ij}v_{0}(y_{i})\right)\bm{e}_{i}.

Thus,

\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,\nabla_{ij}\bm{v}_{0}(Y)\rangle=\sigma^{-2}\hat{w}_{i}(Y)\left([v_{1}(y_{i})]_{j}-y_{ij}v_{0}(y_{i})\right).

(51)

Combining (
47
), (
B.1
) and (
51
), we obtain

\displaystyle 2\nabla_{ij}F_{M,\lambda}(Y)

\displaystyle=2\sigma^{-2}\hat{w}_{i}(Y)\left(-[v_{1}(y_{i})]_{j}+y_{ij}v_{0}(y_{i})+\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,Y_{j}\odot\bm{K}_{i}\rangle-y_{ij}\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,\bm{K}_{i}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=2\sigma^{-2}\hat{w}_{i}(Y)\left(-[v_{1}(y_{i})]_{j}+y_{ij}v_{0}(y_{i})-y_{ij}\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,\bm{K}_{i}\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,Y_{j}\odot\bm{K}_{i}\rangle\right)

(52)

Now,

\displaystyle y_{ij}v_{0}(y_{i})-y_{ij}\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,\bm{K}_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=y_{ij}\left(-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\hat{w}_{m}(Y)\kappa(y_{i},y_{m})+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\kappa(x,y_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\hat{w}_{i}(Y)y_{ij}

(53)

\displaystyle=\big[\lambda\bm{W}(Y)Y\big]_{i,j}.

(54)

The identity used in (
53
) is precisely a rearrangement of the fact that
\bm{K}(Y)\hat{\bm{w}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{w}}=\bm{v}_{0}(Y)
.
Using the symmetry of
\kappa
,

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,Y_{j}\odot\bm{K}_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{w}_{m}(Y)y_{mj}\kappa(y_{m},y_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\kappa(y_{i},y_{m})\hat{w}_{m}(Y)y_{mj}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{e}_{i},\,\bm{K}(Y)\bm{W}(Y)Y_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left[\,\bm{K}(Y)\bm{W}(Y)Y\right]_{i,j}

(55)

where
\bm{W}(Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
satisfies
[\bm{W}(Y)]_{i,m}=\hat{w}_{i}(Y)\delta_{i,m}
. Combining (
B.1
) and the fact that
[v_{1}(y_{i})]_{j}
is the
(i,j)
-th entry of the matrix
\bm{v}_{1}(Y)
, we conclude that

\left(-[v_{1}(y_{i})]_{j}+y_{ij}v_{0}(y_{i})-y_{ij}\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,\bm{K}_{i}\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{w}}(Y),\,Y_{j}\odot\bm{K}_{i}\rangle\right)

is the
(i,j)
-entry of the matrix
-\bm{v}_{1}(Y)+\bm{K}(Y)\bm{W}(Y)Y+\lambda\bm{W}(Y)Y=-\bm{v}_{1}(Y)+\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\bm{W}(Y)Y
. Thus, (
B.1
) is
the
(i,j)
-entry of the matrix

2\sigma^{-2}\bm{W}(Y)\left(\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\bm{W}(Y)Y-\bm{v}_{1}(Y)\right).

(56)

In other words,

\displaystyle\nabla F_{M}(Y)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}\bm{W}(Y)\left(\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\bm{W}(Y)Y-\bm{v}_{1}(Y)\right).

(57)

When the weights
\bm{W}(Y)
are nonzero, we thus have

\nabla F_{M}(Y)=\sigma^{-2}\bm{W}(Y)\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)\bm{W}(Y)\left(Y-\bm{W}(Y)^{-1}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\bm{v}_{1}(Y)\right).

(58)

We remark that
\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)
is invertible by construction for any
\lambda>0
.
Finally, observe that the function
\bm{\Psi}_{\lambda,\mathrm{MSIP}}
defined by (
13
) satisfies

\bm{\Psi}_{\mathrm{MSIP}}(Y)=\bm{W}(Y)^{-1}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\bm{v}_{1}(Y).

(59)

B.2
Proof of
Proposition

3.2

First, fix
C>0
and define scaled density
\tilde{\pi}=C\pi
for convenience. Then, given
Y\in\mathcal{X}^{M}
, we have

\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}(Y;\pi)=\bm{W}^{\ast}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\bm{V}_{0}(Y)r(Y),

(60)

where
\bm{V}_{0}(Y)=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{v}_{0}(Y))
, and
r:\mathcal{X}^{M}\to\mathcal{X}^{M}
has row
i
defined by
v_{1}(y_{i})/v_{0}(y_{i})
.
Fix
Y\in\mathcal{X}^{M}
, then define the functions
v_{0}^{C}
and
v_{1}^{C}
for
\tilde{\pi}
, corresponding to
v_{0}
and
v_{1}
, respectively. Remarkably, we observe

\displaystyle v_{0}^{C}(y)=C\int\kappa(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x)=Cv_{0}(y),

(61)

\displaystyle v_{1}^{C}(y)=C\int x\kappa(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x)=Cv_{1}(y).

(62)

This identity also holds for any of our estimators
\hat{v}_{0}
and
\hat{v}_{1}
. Thus,

\frac{v_{1}^{C}(y)}{v_{0}^{C}(y)}=\frac{v_{1}(y)}{v_{0}(y)}.

(63)

Stated plainly, the vector of ratios
r(Y)
is invariant to the value of
C
. Moreover, the weights
\hat{\bm{w}}_{\lambda}^{C}
for
\tilde{\pi}
satisfy

\bm{w}_{\lambda}^{{}^{\ast}C}(Y):=\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\bm{v}_{0}^{C}(Y)=C\bm{w}_{\lambda}^{\ast}(Y),

(64)

so that the product
\bm{W}^{\ast}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\bm{K}_{\lambda}(Y)^{-1}\bm{V}_{0}(Y)
is invariant to the value of
C
. Therefore,
\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}(Y;C\cdot\pi)
is invariant to the value of
C
.

B.3
One-point MSIP map as a GMM approximation

Proposition B.1
.

Suppose the target density
\pi
is in RKHS
\mathcal{H}
with squared-exponential kernel. Evaluate
\widehat{\Psi}_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}
using one-point estimators
\hat{v}_{0}^{1}
and
\hat{v}_{1}^{S1}
in (
30
). If there exists a measure
\mu
with

\pi(x)=\int\kappa(x,y)\ \mathrm{d}\mu(y),

(65)

then any fixed point of
\widehat{\Psi}_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}
is a critical point of
F_{M,\lambda}^{\mu}
. Further, we have the identity

F^{\mu}_{M,\lambda}(Y)\equiv\min_{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\left\|\pi-\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\mathcal{N}(\cdot;y_{i},\ \sigma^{2}I)\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\bm{w}\|^{2}.

Proof.

We remark that the MSIP map
\Psi_{\mathrm{MSIP},\lambda}
defined using
\bm{v}_{0}
and
\bm{v}_{1}
for the measure
\mu
precisely aligns with the practical calculation of
\hat{v}_{0}^{1}
and
\hat{v}_{1}^{S1}
. Therefore, we apply
Theorem

3.1
to arrive at the first half of the result.

Now, consider a configuration of points and weights
Y,\bm{w}
. Let
f_{i}(x)=\mathcal{N}(x;y_{i},\sigma^{2}I)
and note that

\displaystyle\pi(x)-\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}f_{i}(x)=\left\langle\kappa(x,\cdot),\mu-\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\delta_{y_{i}}\right\rangle=\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}\left[\mu-\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\delta_{y_{i}}\right](x),

where
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}
is the feature map of RKHS
\mathcal{H}

[
64
]
Therefore, we see that

\left\|\pi-\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\mathcal{N}(\cdot;y_{i},\ \sigma^{2}I)\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}=\left\|\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}\left[\mu-\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\delta_{y_{i}}\right]\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}=\mathrm{MMD}^{2}\left(\mu,\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\delta_{y_{i}}\right).

The result thus follows from the definition of
F^{\mu}_{M,\lambda}
.
∎
