Beta-Blocker Discontinuation in Acute Heart Failure
Authors: Nima Alaeiilkhchi, Wade Thompson, Lisa M. McCarthy, William McDonald, Raha Eskandari, Yanrong Maggie Yang, Douglas M. Salzwedel, Parag Goyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhether beta-blockers should be continued during hospitalization for acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) remains uncertain.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to conduct a systematic review on continuation vs discontinuation of beta-blockers during ADHF hospitalization (PROSPERO CRD42024557727).MethodsWe searched MEDLINE, Embase, and CENTRAL from inception to September 11, 2025. We included randomized and observational studies comparing beta-blocker discontinuation with continuation during hospitalization for ADHF and grouped studies by timing of discontinuation. We conducted meta-analysis of observational studies using generic inverse-variance random-effects models, prioritizing adjusted estimates.ResultsThirteen studies met the eligibility criteria: 1 randomized trial (n = 147) and 12 cohort studies (n = 14,654). The associated risk of in-hospital mortality was uncertain for beta-blocker discontinuation vs continuation (pooled OR: 5.20; 95% CI: 0.24-113.06; I2 = 91%, 3 studies). Short-term discontinuation might be associated with higher risk of mortality up to 3 months (pooled HR: 2.30; 95% CI: 1.23-4.29; I2 = 0%, 2 studies). One study reported an adjusted estimate for long-term mortality (HR: 1.55; 95% CI: 0.69-3.51). The single randomized controlled trial showed an uncertain effect for in-hospital mortality (risk ratio: 1.77; 95% CI: 0.16-19.1) and short-term mortality (risk ratio: 0.88; 95% CI: 0.30-2.62). Findings on rehospitalization were inconsistent and did not favor either approach. All findings were low or very low certainty.ConclusionsAvailable evidence on beta-blocker discontinuation in ADHF is of very low certainty and findings of possible harm may reflect confounding by indication and failure to restart therapy rather than causal harm. Further high-quality randomized controlled trials and nonrandomized studies may resolve uncertainty.

Acute decompensated heart failure (HF) (ADHF) is a leading cause of hospital admissions worldwide, contributing substantially to morbidity, mortality, and health care expenditures.1 In chronic HF, guideline-directed medical therapy typically includes beta-blockers because of their proven benefits in reducing mortality and hospitalizations.2,3 Beta-blockers are used in many patients with HF.4, 5, 6 When patients are hospitalized, clinicians are faced with the dilemma of continuing beta-blockers or discontinuing them due to concerns about blood pressure, heart rate, and cardiac output, which may be abnormal during an episode of ADHF.7

In real-world practice, beta-blocker therapy is frequently held during ADHF episodes—reported discontinuation rates are up to 70% in some cohorts.8,9 However, there are potential risks to discontinuing beta-blockers. Abrupt withdrawal of a beta-blocker can precipitate adverse rebound effects such as tachycardia,10,11 ischemia,12 arrhythmias,13 and sudden cardiac death.14 Over the longer term, patients whose beta-blockers are stopped in hospital may never have them reinitiated, thereby losing potential benefits of beta-blockade (especially in HF with reduced ejection fraction [EF] [HFrEF]15, 16, 17). However, continuing beta-blockers during acute decompensation might be deleterious in hemodynamically unstable patients.7,18 Current HF guidelines provide limited specific direction on beta-blocker management during ADHF. Observational studies suggest that abruptly withdrawing beta-blockers in ADHF may increase arrhythmogenic risk,19 whereas a 2015 systematic review found an association between beta-blocker discontinuation in ADHF and higher mortality risk.20 However, that review did not formally assess the certainty of evidence (eg via Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation [GRADE]) and its search is now over a decade old.20 Therefore, a rigorous and up-to-date synthesis of available evidence is needed regarding the effect of beta-blocker discontinuation on clinical outcomes in ADHF. We conducted a systematic review on the benefits and harms of discontinuing beta-blocker therapy among adults hospitalized for ADHF.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.21 The protocol was registered prospectively (PROSPERO CRD42024557727). The registered protocol encompassed 2 related systematic reviews (1 focusing on ADHF and another on postmyocardial infarction/acute coronary syndromes); here we report the findings specific to the ADHF.

This systematic review and meta-analysis used published, deidentified aggregate data; institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies met the following criteria:•Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or observational studies (cohort or case-control)•Enrolled adults (≥18 years old) hospitalized for ADHF•Patients were receiving beta-blocker therapy before or during hospitalization•The study compared beta-blocker discontinuation vs continuation during the acute hospitalization or at hospital discharge•Reported at least one clinical outcome of interest (eg, in-hospital mortality, short-term ≤3 months, long-term >3 months mortality, or rehospitalization)

Studies were excluded if they focused only on chronic HF without an acute decompensation episode, or if they were published only as conference abstracts without a full manuscript. No language limitation was applied.

Information sources and search strategy

A medical librarian (D.S.) performed the search. Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) were searched from inception through September 11, 2025. We searched Epistemonikos, Google Scholar, and ClinicalTrials.gov for any relevant studies and reviewed reference lists of pertinent articles and prior reviews to identify further citations. The search strategy is provided in the Supplemental methods.

Study selection

References were imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation) for deduplication and screening. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, as well as full text articles. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data from each included study using a standardized data collection form. Any discrepancies or errors in data extraction were resolved by consensus through re-examination of the original article. Extracted information included study characteristics (first author, year, design, setting, location, sample size) and patient demographics (mean age, sex, baseline left ventricular EF [LVEF]). We recorded how discontinuation was defined in the study, the timing of beta-blocker withdrawal or resumption, the number of patients in each group, and all reported outcomes. The outcomes of interest were in-hospital mortality, short-term mortality (within 3 months), long-term mortality (beyond 3 months), rehospitalization, and length of hospital stay.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias for each included study. For RCTs, we used the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0.22 For observational (nonrandomized) studies, we used the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I tool).23 Any differences in risk-of-bias judgments between the 2 reviewers were resolved by discussion and, if needed, by involving a third reviewer to reach consensus. We also evaluated the overall certainty of evidence for each outcome using the GRADE approach,24 considering factors such as study limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Per GRADE guidance, RCT evidence started as high certainty and observational evidence as low certainty and then were downgraded or upgraded based on the identified factors. Summary-of-finding tables were prepared to present the GRADE assessments.

Data synthesis and analysis

We prespecified outcomes by timing: in-hospital; short-term (≤3 months postdischarge); or long-term (>3 months postdischarge). For nonrandomized studies, direct pairwise adjusted effect estimates were prioritized for quantitative synthesis. Time-to-event mortality outcomes were synthesized using HRs when available; adjusted ORs were synthesized separately and served as supportive analyses when HRs were unavailable. We did not combine HRs, ORs, and risk ratios (RRs) within the same meta-analysis. The most fully adjusted direct estimate from each study was extracted for the primary adjusted synthesis. When adjusted direct estimates were unavailable or when only indirect/nonpairwise adjusted contrasts were reported, crude event-count syntheses using RRs were undertaken as secondary analyses. For continuous outcomes (such as length of stay [LOS]), we pooled mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs. If a study reported continuous data as medians with IQRs or ranges, we converted these to means and SDs using established methods recommended in the Cochrane Handbook.25

Adjusted estimates were pooled on the log scale using generic inverse-variance random-effects models. For crude dichotomous outcomes, we calculated log RRs and corresponding SEs from raw event counts. Random-effects models used restricted maximum likelihood to estimate between-study variance (tau2). CIs for pooled effects used the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman method for syntheses with 3 or more studies. When only 2 studies were available, Wald-type CIs were used because Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman can be unstable with very small meta-analyses. We report tau2, I2, and 95% prediction intervals for pooled models with 3 or more studies and interpreted prediction intervals cautiously because most analyses included few studies. We conducted subgroup analyses by LVEF (HFrEF-only vs mixed/unreported populations) and by exposure definition (in-hospital withholding vs discharge withdrawal) when data permitted, along with leave-one-out and intensive care unit/exposure-restriction sensitivity analyses. We used crude outcome data for subgroup and sensitivity analyses as these analyses were not possible using adjusted estimates. Meta-regression was not performed because all meta-analyses included fewer than 10 studies. Funnel plots were visually inspected for pooled observational analyses with at least 3 studies and interpreted cautiously because all analyses included fewer than 10 studies. Studies with zero events in both comparison groups were excluded from dichotomous meta-analyses. All meta-analyses, forest plots, and funnel plots were generated in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) using the meta package.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The search yielded 4,103 unique records. After title/abstract screening, and full-text review of 108 articles, 13 studies met the inclusion criteria (Supplemental Figure 1). These included 1 open-label randomized controlled trial (B-CONVINCED, n = 78 discontinuation and n = 69 continuation) and 12 cohort studies (n = 2,817 discontinuation and n = 11,837 continuation). Studies were conducted between 2003 and 2019 in Europe, North America, and the Middle East. Across the included studies, the mean patient age was 55 to 79 years, and most participants were males (Table 1). Four of 13 studies specifically enrolled patients with HFrEF (Table 1). The mean/median LVEF was reported in 6 studies and was <40% in all these studies. Four studies provided proportions of participants with different LVEF (most patients had LVEF <40% in these studies, see Table 1); 1 study did not report LVEF data for beta-blocker continuers/discontinuers separately but in the overall cohort the LVEF was 43% whereas another study reported that 89% of patients were hospitalized with left ventricular systolic dysfunction but did not clearly report LVEF; finally, 1 study did not report LVEF at all (Table 1). Studies examined outcomes among patients who were receiving beta-blockers before admission and had them discontinued in hospital during hospitalization compared to those who continued beta-blockers. Beta-Blocker Discontinuation in Acute Heart Failure

For adults hospitalized with ADHF, discontinuation of beta-blockers might be associated with higher postdischarge mortality up to 3 months though we are very uncertain about these findings. Findings on in-hospital mortality and long-term mortality were uncertain. RCT = randomized controlled trial

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

The 12 observational studies were judged to be at serious risk of bias on ROBINS-I, with confounding by indication being the most important limitation (patients with hypotension, shock, bradycardia, or need for inotropes may have been more likely to have beta-blockers withheld and to experience adverse outcomes) (Supplemental Figure 2). The RCT was judged to be at high risk of bias due to missing outcome data, open-label design, and small sample size. Using GRADE, certainty of evidence was low or very low for all outcomes (Supplemental Table 1). Given few eligible studies, inspection of funnel plots did not reveal any clear patterns.

In-hospital mortality

Five observational studies8,9,26,33,35 reported all-cause in-hospital mortality (Table 2). No studies reported time-to-event analyses. Three studies8,26,33 reported adjusted pairwise estimates (reporting OR) comparing beta-blocker discontinuation with continuation and the association with all-cause mortality (Supplemental Table 2). Pooling these adjusted ORs yielded OR 5.20 (95% CI: 0.24-113.06; I2 = 91%) (Figure 1A), indicating extreme imprecision and substantial heterogeneity. One additional observational study35 reported an adjusted comparison against a “not treated” reference group rather than continuation and was therefore not eligible for the adjusted pairwise meta-analysis (Supplemental Table 2). Secondary crude event-count pooling of 4 studies8,26,33,35 was directionally similar but highly heterogeneous (RR, 5.31; 95% CI: 0.93-30.41; I2 = 94%) (Supplemental Figure 3A). In the RCT,31 in-hospital mortality was 2.6% (2/78) with discontinuation vs 1.4% (1/69) with continuation (RR, 1.77; 95% CI: 0.16-19.1). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses generated imprecise estimates (Supplemental Table 3). Short-term mortality (≤3 months)

Five observational studies8,26,27,29,30 reported mortality outcomes within 3 months after the index hospital discharge (Table 2). Two studies27,29 reported adjusted time-to-event estimates (Supplemental Table 2) and formed the primary short-term mortality analysis. Pooling these adjusted HRs showed higher short-term mortality associated with discontinuation vs continuation (HR: 2.30; 95% CI: 1.23-4.29; I2 = 0%) (Figure 1B). Two additional studies8,26 reported adjusted ORs and yielded a directionally consistent pooled estimate (OR: 1.93; 95% CI: 1.30-2.88; I2 = 0%) (Figure 1C). Secondary crude event-count pooling of 5 studies8,26,27,29,30 was also similar in direction and magnitude (pooled RR: 2.35; 95% CI: 1.69-3.27; I2 = 0%) (Supplemental Figure 3B). The RCT31 showed no difference in mortality at 3 months: 6/78 (7.7%) in the discontinuation arm vs 6/69 (8.7%) in the continuation arm (RR: 0.88; 95% CI: 0.30-2.62) (Table 2). Notably, 76% of patients randomized to discontinuation were restarted on beta-blockers by 3 months compared with 90% in the continuation group. Subgroup analyses with crude data were directionally similar when restricted to HFrEF studies and when stratified by exposure definition (Supplemental Table 3). The certainty of evidence was very low for the observational studies and very low for the RCT (Supplemental Table 1).

Long-term mortality (>3 months)

Four observational studies26, 27, 28,32 evaluated mortality beyond 3 months (from 6 months up to 1 year) (Table 2). Only 1 study27 reported an adjusted estimate for long-term mortality (see Supplemental Table 4), precluding meta-analysis. This study27 found no association between beta-blocker discontinuation and long-term mortality compared with continuation (HR: 1.55; 95% CI: 0.69-3.51). Secondary analysis of crude outcome data from 4 studies26, 27, 28,32 suggested possible higher long-term mortality associated with beta-blocker discontinuation (pooled RR: 1.68; 95% CI: 1.19-2.36; I2 = 0%) (Supplemental Figure 3C). Subgroup analysis using crude outcome data restricting to 3 studies26,27,32 with HFrEF populations found a pooled RR of 1.67 (95% CI: 0.93-3.02) associated with beta-blocker discontinuation compared to continuation (Supplemental Table 3). The certainty of evidence was rated as very low for the observational studies (Supplemental Table 1).

Length of hospital stay

Five observational studies9,26,28,29,33 reported index-hospital LOS (Supplemental Table 4). One study (Abi Khalil et al9) was excluded from the quantitative synthesis because it reported medians with extremely wide ranges (1-451 days), precluding reliable conversions to a mean and SD. Based on meta-analysis of 4 studies,26,28,29,33 beta-blocker discontinuation was associated with a longer LOS compared with continuation (mean difference 1.45 days; 95% CI: 0.05-2.86; I2 = 0%) (Figure 2). The corresponding prediction interval crossed no difference (Supplemental Table 5). The RCT reported similar mean stays in the groups (discontinuation: 10.4 vs continuation: 11.5 days; P = 0.2). Definitions of rehospitalization varied across studies (eg, all-cause, HF-specific, cardiovascular in general, arrhythmia-related; and follow-up intervals ranged from 60 days to 1 year), which precluded any quantitative synthesis. Discussion

Our systematic review identified 12 cohort studies and 1 RCT that examined clinical outcomes associated with discontinuation of beta-blockers during hospitalization for ADHF. Most evidence comes from individuals with LVEF <40%, thus our findings mainly apply to this population. Our findings suggested that beta-blocker discontinuation might be associated with an increased risk of short-term mortality compared with continuation. Findings on in-hospital mortality and long-term mortality were uncertain due to considerable imprecision and heterogeneity, with sensitivity analyses demonstrating fragility of the pooled estimate. Adjusted estimates and secondary syntheses of crude outcome data were generally directionally consistent, although crude syntheses are more vulnerable to confounding by indication and should be interpreted as supportive evidence only. The risk of rehospitalization following beta-blocker discontinuation was unclear due to differences in reporting. Nevertheless, available studies consistently suggested there might be no meaningful difference in rehospitalization rates between discontinuation and continuation. Beta-blocker discontinuation might be associated with a slightly longer index hospital LOS in observational studies, but this modest increase may not be clinically significant given the wide CI and the lack of a difference in LOS in the RCT. Given predominantly observational evidence and concerns around risk of bias, our confidence in our findings is very low. In particular, our findings may reflect confounding by indication (high-risk phenotypes having beta-blockers stopped) or reflect failure to restart therapy rather than causal harm from discontinuation of beta-blockers in ADHF.

Although a prior systematic review20 similarly concluded that withdrawal may worsen outcomes, it predated several large registry analyses now included in our synthesis. By incorporating newer observational data, performing quality assessment, subgroup and sensitivity analyses, and distinguishing time-specific mortality windows, our review provides the most current and methodologically rigorous summary to date. Although our findings are very low certainty, and we cannot infer causality, findings generally support for the 2022 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Failure Society of America guideline on HF management and the 2021 European Society of Cardiology guidance indicating that patients experience worse outcomes when guideline-directed therapies (such as beta-blockers) are withheld in ADHF3,36 and recommend continuation in the absence of shock or severe hypotension.3,36 Nevertheless, real-world practice does not mirror these recommendations.8,9 Our findings reinforce practical points already embedded in guidelines,3,36 which highlight that individualizing therapy is important. For example, continuation of beta-blockers may be reasonable to avoid potential rebound sympathetic activation37 and/or loss of antiarrhythmic benefit,19,38 whereas temporary discontinuation is generally appropriate in cases of hypotension, bradycardia, and/or cardiogenic shock.39, 40, 41

Calculating the risk-benefit ratio of continuing (vs discontinuing) beta-blocker can be challenging. Although hypotension and bradycardia are clinically evident and easy to recognize, cardiogenic shock can present in subtle ways. Physicians must make difficult in-the-moment decisions about beta-blocker therapy for patients hospitalized for ADHF. Prior studies confirm that clinicians frequently opt to discontinue beta-blockers in this scenario.8 Based on our findings, it was unclear whether possible harm is a result of beta-blocker discontinuation during hospitalization or because beta-blockers may not be restarted after hospitalization. Our study could prompt the development of interventions focused on examining rates of inappropriately discontinued beta-blockers, and potentially strategies to reinitiate beta-blockers where appropriate either later in the hospitalization or soon-after hospital discharge. This stresses the importance of continuity of care with care transitions. Whether promoting beta-blocker dose reduction (instead of complete discontinuation) improves outcomes may be worth investigating formally, as observational data would indicate that this may be reasonable.42

Deprescribing has emerged as a useful tool for clinicians to improve outcomes in patients.43 The potential benefits of deprescribing beta-blockers in select scenarios have become an area of interest. Given the very low certainty of the available evidence, further research is needed to clarify uncertainties in evidence. The conduct of a large, pragmatic RCT with the following key design elements would be ideal: 1) broad inclusion criteria spanning but also differentiating between LVEF phenotypes; 2) randomization for situations where there is equipoise with withdrawal; 3) ascertainment of and stratification by hemodynamic status; and 4) inclusion of patient-centered outcomes (mortality, arrhythmia, and quality of life) with standardized outcome definitions (eg, time-to-first HF readmission). Until such trials are completed, high-quality observational studies using advanced causal-inference methods (eg, target-trial emulation) may add to the evidence base, particularly those that can avoid or mitigate confounding by indication and misclassification, which is a common challenge in observational studies of medication discontinuation.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this review include an a priori registered protocol, extensive searches, ROBINS-I and Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 appraisal, Hartung-Knapp random-effects pooling, and transparent GRADE assessments. We conducted several analyses to examine robustness of our findings, including providing parallel summaries of adjusted estimates as well as subgroup analyses by LVEF and exposure definition. The limitations in our review mirror limitations of observational studies on medication discontinuation, notably potential for residual confounding and confounding by indication (in particular, beta-blockers being discontinued because people had worse health or clinical status). There was minimal information on why a beta-blocker was being discontinued, along with potential for misclassification in exposure status. Many included studies did not report or stratify outcomes by LVEF, limiting our ability to assess whether the impact of beta-blocker withdrawal differs between HF with preserved EF or HFrEF. When we only analyzed studies among patients with HFrEF, results were similar to our main analysis; however, there were few data available for HFpEF. Overall, we cannot draw any conclusions around whether the effects of discontinuation differ according to EF. Moreover, we could not determine whether there is any difference based on exposure definition (ie, temporary inpatient holds vs permanent discontinuation at discharge). A short-term interruption with prompt reinitiation of beta-blockers may carry less risk than complete cessation; however, most studies did not report whether or when beta-blockers were restarted after the acute phase. We also lacked detailed data on background guideline-directed medical therapy in the observational studies. It was generally unclear whether patients who continued beta-blockers were receiving more comprehensive HF therapy over time than those who discontinued. Any imbalance in concurrent therapies could introduce confounding. Although 1 RCT was available, the event rates were low, limiting the ability to draw meaningful conclusions around in-hospital mortality due to imprecision. In this RCT, most patients randomized to discontinuation resumed beta-blockers after hospitalization, which also limited the ability to compare longer term clinical outcomes following discontinuation.

Conclusions

Our review provides a contemporary, rigorous, and up-to-date synthesis of available evidence related to discontinuation of beta-blockers during ADHF. Available evidence is of very low certainty and comes primarily from observational studies, precluding firm conclusions around the effects of beta-blocker discontinuation in ADHF. We found imprecision in estimates for in-hospital mortality and long-term mortality, and found beta-blocker discontinuation may be associated with higher risk of short-term mortality up to 3 months. However, findings of possible harm may reflect confounding by indication and failure to restart therapy, rather than causal harm from discontinuation during ADHF. Until additional higher certainty data become available, this synthesis of evidence can be combined with clinical judgment to guide decision-making in practice.PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Based on primarily observational studies of adults hospitalized for ADHF, the effects of beta-blocker discontinuation are not clear.
