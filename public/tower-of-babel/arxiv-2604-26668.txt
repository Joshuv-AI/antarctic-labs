Nonlinear Probabilistic Forecast Reconciliation Journal: International Journal of Forecasting
Anubhab Biswas, Lorenzo Zambon, Lorenzo Nespoli, Giorgio Corani

Abstract
Forecast reconciliation adjusts independently generated forecasts so that they satisfy some known constraints. While probabilistic forecast reconciliation is well established for linear constraints, some practical forecasting problems involve nonlinear relationships among variables. In this paper, we address probabilistic forecast reconciliation with nonlinear constraints for the first time. We extend both reconciliation via projection and conditioning to the case of nonlinear constraints. The projection approach reconciles forecast samples by mapping them onto the nonlinear coherent manifold. The conditioning approach adopts a sampling algorithm inspired to the Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF). We evaluate both methods on synthetic and real datasets. Empirically, both reconciliation approaches generally improve forecast accuracy. The UKF-based approach achieves the best overall performance while being substantially faster than the projection one.

Introduction

Time series are often related by linear constraints of aggregation. For example, for a certain store,
consider forecasting both the sales of each item and the total sales.
The forecast of the total sales of the store should be equal to the sum of the forecast of the sales of each item. If forecasts satisfy these constraints they are
coherent
, while if they fail to satisfy the constraints they are
incoherent
.
Base forecasts
, which are created independently for each time series, are generally incoherent.
Forecast reconciliation
adjusts the base forecasts making them coherent.

The first methods for forecast reconciliation were based on projection
(
1
;
9
)
. In particular,
20
obtained in closed form the projection that minimizes the expected squared error of the coherent forecast. Such methods can be interpreted as projecting the base point forecast onto the linear coherent subspace
(
16
)
.

While the above methods refer to point forecast reconciliation, the most advanced approaches perform linear probabilistic reconciliation yielding a probability density on the linear coherent subspace.
Earlier studies include
18
, where an empirical copula based approach for reconciliation which uses permutation of the samples was studied.

There are two main approaches to probabilistic forecast reconciliation. The first one is the extension of the
reconciliation via projection
method to the probabilistic case
(
17
;
21
)
.
The second approach is
reconciliation via conditioning
where the incoherent density of the base forecast is sliced on the coherent subspace. This approach has been extensively studied for continuous, discrete and mixed-type of linearly constrained time series
(
23
;
22
;
24
)
. Also the probabilistic approaches of
3
;
4
have been shown to be equivalent to
reconciliation via conditioning

(
22
)
.

Several applications in multiple time series forecasting include nonlinear constraints between the time series, such as socio-demographic ratios like mortality or unemployment rates
(
6
)
.
Very recently the projection-based reconciliation approach has been extended to obtain coherent forecasts for time series with nonlinear constraints
(
14
;
6
)
; however, these works are limited to point forecasts.

We address probabilistic forecast reconciliation under nonlinear constraints for the first time.
We extend both reconciliation via
projection
and via
conditioning
to the case of time series with nonlinear constraints.
The projection-based reconciliation projects each sample point from the cloud of the base forecast distribution to points on the manifold. However, it has the shortcoming of requiring a numerical optimization for each sample to be reconciled, leading to heavy computation times.

reconciliation via conditioning
, with a sampling strategy inspired by the Unscented Kalman Filter
(

3
Probabilistic Methods for Nonlinear Reconciliation

Two principled approaches for linear probabilistic reconciliation are projection
(
17
)
and conditioning
(
23
;
3
)
. Here we extend both to the case of nonlinear constraints.

3.1
Reconciliation via Projection

In the linear case,
17
defined probabilistic forecast reconciliation through the push-forward of measures. Given a base forecast distribution
\widehat{\nu}
on the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the reconciled measure
\widetilde{\nu}
is defined as the push-forward
\widetilde{\nu}=\psi_{\#}\widehat{\nu}
under a reconciliation map
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathcal{S}
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the coherent subspace: for any measurable set
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, the reconciled probability is given by
\widetilde{\nu}(\mathcal{B})=\widehat{\nu}(\psi^{-1}(\mathcal{B}))
.

In the linear literature,
\psi
is commonly defined as an orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{S}
with respect to some distance on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Given a positive definite matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the distance is derived from the induced norm
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\mathbf{W}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{W}^{-1}\,\mathbf{x}}
.
Then,
\psi
maps each point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to its nearest point on
\mathcal{S}
:

\psi(\widehat{\mathbf{y}})=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}}\,\,\|\widehat{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{x}\|_{\mathbf{W}}
.
When
\mathbf{W}
is an estimate of the covariance matrix of the base forecast errors, this mapping corresponds to the Minimum Trace (MinT) projection.

14
and
6
reconcile point forecasts satisfying nonlinear constraints by projecting them as the nearest-point mapping onto the manifold
\mathcal{M}
.
Since
\mathcal{M}
is not a linear subspace, this mapping is nonlinear.
We extend this approach to probabilistic reconciliation by defining the reconciled distribution as the push-forward of the base forecast distribution under the nonlinear nearest-point map.

Definition 2

(
Reconciled distribution via projection
)
.

Given a norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{W}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
induced by a positive definite matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathcal{M}
be the nearest-point projection map:

\psi(\widehat{\mathbf{y}})=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{M}}\,\,\|\widehat{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{x}\|_{\mathbf{W}}.

(5)

The reconciled distribution via projection is defined as
\widetilde{\nu}=\psi_{\#}\widehat{\nu}
, the push-forward of the base forecast distribution
\widehat{\nu}
via
\psi
, specified by

\widetilde{\nu}(\mathcal{B})=\widehat{\nu}(\psi^{-1}(\mathcal{B})),\quad\text{for any measurable }\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{M}.

(6)

As in the linear case
(
17
)
, we obtain samples from the reconciled distribution
\widetilde{\nu}
by mapping samples drawn from the base distribution
\widehat{\nu}
via
\psi
:

\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}^{(i)}=\psi\left(\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{(i)}\right),\quad\text{for all }i=1,\dots,M

where
\left(\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{(i)}\right)_{i=1\dots M}\sim\widehat{\nu}
.
If
\mathcal{M}
is a non-linear manifold, the map
\psi
does not admit a closed-form expression.
Hence, for each sample
\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{(i)}
we numerically solve Eq. (
5
) via optimization,
using the Newton-Raphson algorithm as in
14
.
A drawback of this approach is thus its computational cost, which linearly increases with the number of samples.

We should note that although both the mentioned works on nonlinear reconciliation for point forecasts obtain guarantees in reduction of Euclidean distance-based scores depending on local curvature conditions, we do not study how it could correspond to the probabilistic case in our experiments.

We consider 3 specifications for the matrix
\mathbf{W}
, each corresponding to a different projection.

1.

OLS
: we set
\mathbf{W}
as the identity matrix; this choice corresponds to the Euclidean distance in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
However, the underlying assumption of isotropic error is inappropriate the time series have different scales.

2.

WLS
: we set
\mathbf{W}
as a diagonal matrix, whose entries are the estimated variances of the base forecast errors.
This defines an anisotropic distance that accounts for the influence of the different scales present in the time series.

3.

Full
: we set
\mathbf{W}
as the full covariance matrix of the base forecast errors, estimated via shrinkage as in
(
20
)
.
This choice accounts for both heteroscedasticity and the correlation structure between base forecasts. This approach has optimal properties in the linear framework
(
21
)
, but no optimality guarantee currently exists in the nonlinear case.

3.2
Reconciliation via conditioning

This approach to probabilistic reconciliation conditions the joint base forecast distribution on the hierarchical constraints
(
23
)
.
We generalize it to the nonlinear case.
In the following, we assume that
\widehat{\nu}
is a continuous distribution, and we denote by
\widehat{\pi}
its density.

Definition 3

(
Reconciled distribution via conditioning
)
.

The reconciled distribution via conditioning of the free time series is defined as the probability distribution
\widetilde{\nu}_{B}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{b}})
with density

\widetilde{\pi}_{B}(\mathbf{b})\propto\widehat{\pi}(f_{u}(\mathbf{b}),\,\mathbf{b}).

(7)

The reconciled distribution via conditioning
\widetilde{\nu}
for the entire set of time series is then defined as the pushforward of
\widetilde{\nu}_{B}
via the FTA map:

\widetilde{\nu}=f_{\#}\widetilde{\nu}_{B}.

(8)

We now give the intuition behind this definition, leaving the details in
B
.
Let
{\widehat{\mathbf{Y}}=\left[\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime},\,\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{\prime}\right]^{\prime}}
be a random vector distributed as the base forecast distribution
\widehat{\nu}
.
Eq. (
7
) provides the density of the conditional distribution of
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
, given the constraints:

\widehat{\mathbf{B}}\;\Big|\left(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}-f_{u}(\widehat{\mathbf{B}})=0\right)\;\;\sim\;\;\widetilde{\nu}_{B}.

(9)

Then, Eq. (
8
) follows directly from
the definition of coherent forecast distribution (Def.
1
).
Given samples for the free time series
\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}^{(i)}\sim\widetilde{\nu}_{B}
, we can thus obtain coherent samples for all the series by applying the FTA map to each sample:
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}^{(i)}=f\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}^{(i)}\big)
.
As in the linear case, reconciliation via conditioning can be interpreted as slicing the incoherent joint density over the coherent manifold
\mathcal{M}
(Fig.
3
).
Since the density in Eq. (
7
) is generally not available in closed-form, we need to resort to sampling techniques.
We propose an algorithm inspired by the Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF)
(
12
)
.

Unscented Transform

3
showed the link, in the linear case, between probabilistic forecast reconciliation and the update step of the Kalman filter.
We extend this idea to nonlinear reconciliation, proposing an algorithm based on the Unscented Transform used by the Unscented Kalman Filter (
UKF
)
(
12
;
19
)
. We thus use the UKF as a numerical method for approximating the conditional distribution induced by the nonlinear constraint rather than as a dynamical filtering procedure. The basic idea is to approximate the probability distribution of the base forecast of the free time series by replacing it with a small deterministic set of
sigma points
whose mean and covariance coincide with those of the original distribution. These points are then propagated through the nonlinear map
f_{u}(.)
, and the mean and covariance of the transformed cloud are used to approximate the transformed distribution. The resulting transformed points are then summarized to obtain the quantities needed for a Kalman-style update, which produces the reconciled mean and covariance of the free-level forecasts.

We approximate both the incoherent and the reconciled distribution of the free time series with Gaussian distributions. We then treat the base forecasts of the constrained time series as noisy observations of the free time series.

\hat{\mathbf{U}}=f_{u}(\mathbf{B})+\varepsilon^{u},

(10)

where
\varepsilon^{u}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\hat{\Sigma}_{U})
represents uncertainty in the constrained forecasts.

We assume the joint predictive distribution of the free-level base forecasts to be Gaussian

p(\mathbf{B})\equiv\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mathbf{b}},\hat{\Sigma}_{B}),

(11)

where
\hat{\Sigma}_{B}
is estimated from in-sample residuals, analogously to
minT

(
20
)
. Under this assumption, the Unscented Transform yields a Gaussian approximation to the joint distribution of the free and constrained base forecasts:

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}\\
\hat{\mathbf{U}}\end{bmatrix}\approx\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{b}}\\
\mathbf{u}^{-}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\Sigma}_{B}&P_{b,u}\\
P_{b,u}^{T}&S_{u}\end{bmatrix}\right),

where
\mathbf{u}^{-}
is the transformed mean of the sigma points,
P_{b,u}
is the approximate cross-covariance between
\mathbf{B}
and
\hat{\mathbf{U}}
, and
S_{u}
is the approximate covariance of
\hat{\mathbf{U}}
. The reconciled distribution for the free time series is then obtained by conditioning this approximate joint Gaussian on the observed upper-level base forecasts
\hat{\mathbf{U}}=\hat{\mathbf{u}}
:

p(\mathbf{B}\mid\hat{\mathbf{U}}=\hat{\mathbf{u}}),

(12)

This leads to a Gaussian approximation of the reconciled distribution for the free-level forecasts
\tilde{\nu}_{b}
given by a
\mathcal{N}(\tilde{b},\tilde{\Sigma}_{B})
with,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}=\hat{\mathbf{b}}+K(\hat{\mathbf{u}}-\mathbf{u}^{-})

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{B}=\hat{\Sigma}_{B}-KS_{u}K^{T}

The detailed derivation and the description of the UKF algorithm are provided in

and Algorithm
1
.
We eventually sample from
\tilde{\nu}_{b}
and apply the function
f(.)
to each sample to obtain the reconciled samples for the whole set of time series.
In

3.3
Limitations

We acknowledge that the proposed methods are not suitable for all scenarios. In particular, reconciliation via projection is mainly designed for continuous forecast distributions, while discrete and mixed-type time series with nonlinear constraints remain more challenging. As seen in the linear case by
25
, modifying projection to work with discrete time series requires ad hoc choices and results in computationally expensive algorithms. Conditioning has instead been applied, in the linear case, to reconcile constraints related to intermittent, discrete or mixed time series
(
4
;
23
;
24
)
.
However, our specific algorithm based on UKF is unsuitable to reconcile intermittent or discrete time series with nonlinear constraints due to its underlying Gaussian assumption. We leave this problem for future research.

Both the projection (FULL) approach and the reconciliation via conditioning (UKF) can model the dependence between the free and constrained time series using linear measures of correlation; however this is not fully appropriate and better approaches to account for nonlinear dependencies should be tested in the future.

Evaluation metrics

We use the
energy score
to score the joint predictive distributions, which is a proper scoring rule
(
7
)
. If
F
is the multivariate predictive distribution and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the observed ground truth, then we estimate the energy score for
M
samples
x^{(j)}
from
F
as

\text{ES}(F,y)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\|x^{(j)}-y\|_{2}-\frac{1}{2M^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\|x^{(j)}-x^{(k)}\|_{2}.

where
\|.\|_{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm.
The Energy Score is scale-dependent and hence it is generally affected by the time series with the largest values (for example there is a huge difference in scale for the variables of the Swiss demography dataset as seen in Fig.
5
). We also report a score based on the geometric mean of the relative CRPS across time series:

\mathrm{RelCRPS}_{\text{method}}=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\mathrm{CRPS}_{j,\text{method}}}{\mathrm{CRPS}_{j,\text{base}}}\right)^{1/n},

where

\mathrm{CRPS}_{j,\text{method}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{CRPS}\!\left(\hat{F}^{\,\text{method}}_{j,t},\,y_{j,t}\right),

and analogously for
\mathrm{CRPS}_{j,\text{base}}
.
Here,
T
denotes the number of rolling windows (or evaluation time steps),
\hat{F}^{\,\text{method}}_{j,t}
is the predictive distribution produced by the considered method for series
j
at time step
t
, and
y_{j,t}
is the corresponding observed value
(
5
)
.
For a univariate predictive distribution
F
and the observed ground truth
y\in\mathbb{R}
we compute the CRPS as
7
:

{\text{CRPS}}(F,y)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}|x^{(j)}-y|-\frac{1}{2M^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}|x^{(j)}-x^{(k)}|.

For the simulations we also consider the relative Energy score:

\text{RelES}_{method}=\frac{\text{ES}_{method}}{\text{ES}_{base}}

5
Conclusions

We studied nonlinear probabilistic forecast reconciliation for the first time,
extending (i) reconciliation
via projection
and (ii)
via conditioning
to the nonlinear setting.

Reconciliation in general improves the accuracy compared to both the base forecast and probabilistic bottom-up. However, we conclude that these results are still empirical, and we have not explored the connection between the improvement guarantees that were obtained using the projection method for the point forecasts
(
14
;
6
)
and our probabilistic setting.

In our experiments, the UKF-based reconciliation via conditioning outperforms, slightly but consistently, any other method in terms of probabilistic scores.
It also provides a faster alternative to the projection method, since it does not need to go through the optimization iterations. In particular we observe faster computation in 3-4 orders of magnitudes on a single time step as shown in Tab.
4
, Tab.
6
, and Tab.
8
.

These methods, however come with some limitations. As mentioned above, the
UKF
might fail if the assumption of the free-level time series being Gaussian does not hold true, since that is the core idea of that approach. The projection-based method is dependent on numerical methods for projecting the samples onto the nonlinear manifold, and it might sometime have the issue of divergence or slow convergence which we did not improve upon. Although we mention the Frechèt mean as a probable new point forecast, we do not explore it much in this paper and we keep that as a future research direction.

Although we use relative versions of the CRPS and Energy Scores for our experiments both for the simulations and the case studies, sometimes using these Euclidean-distance based scores might lead to misleading results as shown by
14
. Developing a new geodesic-distance based proper scoring rule to evaluate the reconciled forecast distribution, which is more meaningful on the nonlinear manifold, is thus important and can be considered as another future research direction that we keep out of this paper.

Appendix A
Reconciliation via UKF computation

Computation of
\sigma
-points

Consider the assumptions given in
3.2
. To propagate the free state through a nonlinear function, we first generate a set of
sigma points
that represent the uncertainty of the state. The Unscented Transform (UT) is used for this purpose, which approximates the mean and covariance of a nonlinear transformation of a Gaussian random variable. The prior state mean corresponds to the mean of the free-level base predictive distribution
\hat{b}_{t+h}
. The prior state covariance is estimated from the Shaffer-Strimmer covariance of the fitted in-sample residuals as
\hat{\Sigma}_{B,h}
. The
\sigma
-points are computed as
\chi_{j}
’s,
j=0,\dots,2m
-
m
being the number of free time series in the following way:

\displaystyle\chi_{0}

\displaystyle=\hat{b}_{t+h},\quad

\displaystyle\chi_{i}

\displaystyle=\hat{b}_{t+h}+\gamma.\sqrt{\lambda+m}.[\sqrt{\hat{\Sigma}_{B,h}}],\quad i=1,\dots,m

\displaystyle\chi_{m+i}

\displaystyle=\hat{b}_{t+h}-\gamma.\sqrt{\lambda+m}.[\sqrt{\hat{\Sigma}_{B,h}}],\quad i=1,\dots,m

Here
\lambda=\alpha^{2}(m+\kappa)-m
;
\alpha
controls the dispersion of the
\sigma
-points and
\gamma=\sqrt{m+\lambda}
. The notation
[\sqrt{A}]
denotes the Cholesky decomposition of
A
.

Computation of weights

Each sigma point is assigned a weight for the transformed mean and covariance computations. These weights are used to combine the sigma points and obtain the transformed mean and covariance after applying the nonlinear function
f
. Denoting the weights for the mean and covariance as
W_{m}
and
W_{c}
, they are computed as

\displaystyle W^{0}_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{m+\lambda},

\displaystyle W^{0}_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{m+\lambda}+(1-\alpha^{2}+\beta),

\displaystyle W^{i}_{m}

\displaystyle=W^{i}_{c}=\frac{\lambda}{2(m+\lambda)},\quad i=1,\dots,2m.

Here,
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
all are hyper-parameters that control the spread of the
\sigma
-points and their contribution to the mean and the covariance.

Obtaining the Kalman Gain

The Kalman Gain
K
is used to combine the prior distribution and the measurement in a way that minimizes the posterior uncertainty. It essentially determines how much weight should be given to the prediction versus the observation. It is computed as

K=P_{b,u}S^{-1}_{u}.

(14)

Let us denote the transformed sigma points in the observation space as,
z_{j}=f(\chi_{j})
. Let us also denote the mean and the estimated covariance of the constrained base predictive distribution as
\hat{u}_{t+h}
and
\hat{\Sigma}_{U,h}
respectively. The predicted mean of the transformed observations is computed as
u^{-}_{t+h}=\sum_{i=0}^{2m}W_{m}^{i}z_{i}
. Then
P_{b,u}
is the cross-covariance between the sigma points in the free state space and the observation space, which is computed as:

P_{b,u}=\sum_{i=0}^{2m}W_{c}^{i}.(\chi_{i}-\hat{b}_{t+h})(z_{i}-\hat{u}_{t+h})^{T}

S_{u}
is the covariance of the transformed observations, computed as:

S_{u}=\hat{\Sigma}_{U,h}+\sum_{i=0}^{2m}W_{c}^{i}.(z_{i}-u^{-}_{t+h})(z_{i}-u^{-}_{t+h})^{T}

Computing the reconciled distribution

After obtaining the Kalman Gain, we update the mean and covariance of the state estimate based on the observed data. The reconciled free-level posterior distribution
p(B_{t+h}|U_{t+h}=\hat{u}_{t+h})
is updated as a Gaussian with the posterior mean
\tilde{b}_{t+h}
and posterior covariance
\tilde{\Sigma}_{B,h}
computed as:

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{t+h}

\displaystyle=\hat{b}_{t+h}+K(\hat{u}_{t+h}-u^{-}_{t+h})

(15)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{B,h}

\displaystyle=\hat{\Sigma}_{B,h}-KS_{u}K^{T}

(16)

Appendix B
Derivation of the reconciled distribution via conditioning

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{Y}}=\left[\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime},\,\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{\prime}\right]^{\prime}
be a random vector distributed as the base forecast distribution
\widehat{\nu}
.
We specify the reconciled distribution
\widetilde{\nu}_{B}
of the free time series as the distribution of

\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}\;\>\sim\;\;\widehat{\mathbf{B}}\;\Big|\left(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}-f_{u}(\widehat{\mathbf{B}})=\mathbf{0}\right).

(17)

Let
\widehat{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
be the base forecast distribution;
we assume that
\widehat{\nu}
is absolutely continuous, and we denote by
\widehat{\pi}
its density.
Following
23
, we define
\mathbf{Z}=\hat{\mathbf{U}}-f_{u}(\hat{\mathbf{B}})
. The joint density function of
(\mathbf{Z},\hat{\mathbf{B}})
can be computed as:

\pi_{(\mathbf{Z},\hat{\mathbf{B}})}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{b})=\hat{\pi}(\mathbf{z}+f_{u}(\mathbf{b}),\mathbf{b})

and the conditional density of
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}\;|\;\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{0}
is the density of the reconciled forecast distribution of the free series:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}_{B}(\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{(\mathbf{Z},\widehat{\mathbf{B}})}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{b})}{\int\pi_{(\mathbf{Z},\widehat{\mathbf{B}})}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\pi}(f_{u}(\mathbf{b}),\mathbf{b})}{\int\hat{\pi}(f_{u}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\propto\hat{\pi}(f_{u}(\mathbf{b}),\mathbf{b}).

Appendix C
UKF as reconciliation via conditioning

The LHS of Eq. (
7
) is the conditional density of
\hat{\mathbf{B}}|\mathbf{Z}=\textbf{0}
, where
\mathbf{Z}
is defined in the same way as in
B
. This can be written as

\displaystyle p[\hat{\mathbf{B}}|\mathbf{Z}=\textbf{0}]=p[\hat{\mathbf{B}}|\hat{\mathbf{U}}-f_{u}(\hat{\mathbf{B}})=\textbf{0}]

with change of variables.

\implies p[\hat{\mathbf{B}}|\mathbf{Z}=\textbf{0}]=p[\hat{\mathbf{B}}|\hat{\mathbf{U}}=f_{u}(\hat{\mathbf{B}})]

(18)

UKF approximates the RHS of Eq. (
18
) with the Gaussian distribution and has an additional Gaussian assumption on the free time series. So, UKF is a Gaussian approximation to the original definition of reconciliation via conditioning, with
p[\hat{\mathbf{B}}|\hat{\mathbf{U}}=f_{u}(\hat{\mathbf{B}})]
being approximated by
p[\hat{\mathbf{B}}|\hat{\mathbf{U}}=f_{u}(\hat{\mathbf{B}})+\varepsilon^{u}]
.
