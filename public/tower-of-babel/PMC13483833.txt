Ecological barrier crossing strategies in longitudinal migrants: a case study of Mongolian gulls (Larus mongolicus) distributed in East Asia
Authors: Jing Zhang, Lixia Chen, Ge Sun, Yiwen Chen, Yang Wu, Hongxing Jiang, Jiamei Xu, Lixin Wu, Xitao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEcological barriers are major factors shaping the migratory strategies of birds. The Mongolian Plateau, with sparse and fragmented wetlands, provides limited stopover for waterbirds. Recent studies revealed that several bird species migrate longitudinally between their breeding sites in central Asia and the coasts of East Asia, thereby crossing the Mongolian Plateau. However, how these birds adapt their migratory strategies to cope with the environmental constraints imposed by the Mongolian Plateau remains poorly understood.MethodsTo address this gap, we focused on the Mongolian gull (Larus mongolicus), a representative longitudinal migrant that migrates along the East Asian coast and crosses the Mongolian Plateau. Using satellite tracking data from 29 coastal and 30 inland migration events in spring, and 34 coastal and 33 inland migration events in autumn between 2018 and 2025, we analyzed the difference in their migration duration, stopover duration, travel duration, migration distance, number of stopover sites, daily travel speed, straightness index and flight altitude relative to the ground between coastal and inland migration stage.ResultsWe found that, in both spring and autumn, inland migration across the Mongolian Plateau covered significantly longer distance than coastal migration and was associated with higher daily travel speed. Migration duration, stopover duration, and number of stopover sites were all markedly lower during inland migration than during coastal migration, particularly in autumn. Flight altitude relative to the ground was significantly higher during inland migration compared to coastal migration across both seasons. Furthermore, flight altitude relative to the ground exhibited a pronounced diel pattern across inland and coastal migration stage, with birds flying at lower altitudes at night, increasing altitude during the day, and decreasing altitude toward dusk.ConclusionsMongolian gulls adopt a strategy of rapidly crossing the Mongolian Plateau and adjacent arid and semiarid areas by minimizing stopover use, increasing daily travel speed, and flying at higher altitudes. These findings highlight the Mongolian Plateau as an important ecological barrier shaping migration behavior in this species and, together with growing evidence from other tracked and banded species migrating across this region, suggest its broader ecological and conservation significance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40462-026-00673-7.

Background

Migratory birds often travel thousands of kilometers annually between breeding and nonbreeding areas, visiting a variety of distant landscapes and exploiting seasonally available resources to maximize fitness [1]. Such migrants can exhibit pronounced spatiotemporal flexibility in their migration strategy, as they are influenced by ecological barriers [2], habitat connectivity [3, 4], and climatic change encountered along the migration routes [5]. These factors shape the selection of migratory routes and timing, regulate energy expenditure and refueling, and modify the risks encountered during migration, ultimately influencing individual fitness [1].

Ecological barriers are geographical features, such as deserts, mountains, boreal forests or seas, which impose adverse conditions on bird migration strategy because they provide rare food resources, nowhere to land, or harsh climatic conditions [6–8]. Previous studies have shown that migratory birds can adopt a variety of behavioral strategies to cope with the effects of ecological barriers. For example, within the Palearctic–African migratory system, passerines cope with the extreme conditions of the Sahara Desert by adjusting their diel migration rhythms and increasing flight altitude [2, 7, 9]. In Central Asia, Bar-headed Geese (Anser indicus) adopt a “roller coaster” flight strategy to cross the Tibetan Plateau, with a mean elevation exceeding 4,500 m [10, 11]. In East Asia, geese and Siberian Cranes (Leucogeranus leucogeranus) employ a nonstop flight strategy to cross the boreal forests, which span more than 2,000 km in a north to south direction, from northeastern China to their arctic breeding grounds [6, 12]. These studies demonstrate that migrants can cope with different types of ecological barriers by adjusting migration schedules, modifying flight altitude, and optimizing migration routes, thereby mitigating the energetic costs and survival risks.

The Mongolian Plateau, one of the major plateaus located in the arid and semiarid areas of East Asia, has a mean elevation of roughly 1,580 m and spans approximately 2,380 km from east to west. Its eastern margin connects to lowland plains and marine environments, which are characterized by semihumid and semiarid climates. Toward the west, elevation increases and precipitation decreases, gradually transitioning into the more arid regions of Central Asia [13–15]. This region is characterized by sparsely distributed wetlands with pronounced seasonal dynamics, an overall arid climate with low precipitation, high evaporation rates, and pronounced interannual variability, as well as generally low habitat suitability [16]. Such harsh environmental conditions may substantially increase the energetic costs and migratory risks for longitudinal migrants, and consequently lead to the adoption of migration strategies similar to those used when crossing other ecological barriers like Sahara Desert and northern boreal forests.

In recent years, satellite tracking studies have shown that several waterbirds wintering along the eastern Asian coast, such as the Mongolian gull (Larus mongolicus), Dalmatian Pelican (Pelecanus crispus), and Mute Swan (Cygnus olor), migrate westward in spring across the Mongolian Plateau and the adjacent arid and semiarid areas to their inland breeding ground [17–19]. The Mongolian gull specifically shows a particularly stage-structured migratory pattern. Populations wintering along the eastern Asian coast initially migrate northward along the coastline in early spring. Then, they turn westward from the Yellow Sea toward inland, crossing the Mongolian Plateau and continuing toward breeding areas in the Altai Mountains and northwestern Mongolia [18, 20]. This marked shift from coastal migration to westward inland travel makes the Mongolian gull an excellent study species for exploring the influence of the Mongolian Plateau on the migratory strategies of longitudinal migrants in East Asia.

In this study, we used telemetry data from individually tracked Mongolian gulls to investigate variation in migratory behavior between the coastal and inland migration stages involving the crossing of the Mongolian Plateau. In particular, because the Mongolian Plateau is more likely to be less suitable for stopover and energy refueling compared to coastal areas, we hypothesized that, during inland crossings, Mongolian gulls would exhibit significantly fewer and shorter stopovers, higher daily migration speeds, and higher flight altitudes than during coastal migration, as an adaptive response to the environmental constraints of the Mongolian Plateau.

Method

Birds capture and satellite logger deployments

Twelve adult Mongolian gulls were captured during the spring of 2018 at Yancheng Wetland National Nature Reserve (120.50°E, 33.61°N), one of the most important wintering and stopover sites for this species located in Jiangsu Province, China [21]. After weighing and measuring the length of culmen, head, tarsus, wing, tail and body, birds were ringed with metal and plastic leg bands, and fitted with solar-charged GPS/GSM loggers (HQPG3621S, 23 g; Hunan Global Messenger Technology, China), which were attached on the back with leg-loop harness by using 2 mm nylon rope [22]. The weight of these devices ranged from 1.85% to 2.3% of th gull’s body mass (Table S1), which is light enough to avoid significant effects on the bird’s normal activities. The entire process of measuring the gulls and attaching the loggers typically took no more than 10 min. Following attachment, the gulls were observed for 30 min in the aviary and released if no abnormalities were observed.

Data acquisition frequency ranged from 40 min to 2 h depending on battery charge levels, generating 15–28 fixes per day (Table S1). The records included information on time, geographic coordinates, flight altitude relative to sea level, position accuracy and others. Only the fixes with high positioning accuracy (< 20 m) were included in this study. Although the nominal altitudinal accuracy of the loggers was ± 30 m, this precision was adequate for large-scale analyses of the flight altitude of migratory birds [23].

Of the 12 tracked Mongolian gulls, nine provided complete migration data for at least one migration season, and the other three individuals returned no data. Furthermore, we did not consider the spring migration parameters of 2018, except for the arrival date to breeding site, since the individuals captured in Yancheng did not return to their capture site for the following winter, indicating that the capture site was not their wintering grounds. Finally, 29 complete coastal migrations and 30 inland migrations were recorded in spring, 34 coastal migrations and 33 inland migrations were obtained in the autumn, based on a total of 222,920 fixes from 2018 to 2025.

Defining migration parameters

To segment movement tracks and identify stopover sites of each Mongolian gull, we followed the methods described by Wang et al. (2018). The wintering area was defined as the site where the gulls did not venture any further south during their migration along the coast [24]. The breeding/summering area was defined as the main places where the gulls remained largely stationary from late April to August following westward migration across the Mongolian Plateau. Additionally, to investigate the gulls’ behavioral differences between inland (crossing the Mongolian Plateau) and coastal regions, we divided the spring and autumn migration periods using the first location at which individuals departed from and/or arrived at the Yellow Sea. The two phases were defined as the “coast” and the “inland” migratory stages, respectively. We identified the departure date as the date of the first position when a gull left the wintering/summering/breeding/coastal/inland area, judged as the last position acquired from the logger assigned to “non-flight” status using the segmentation method of [12]. The arrival date was defined as the date of the first position when a gull arrived at its wintering/summering/breeding/coastal/inland area, judged as the first position acquired from the logger assigned to “non-flight” status after a protracted period of flight.

To examine differences in flight altitude of Mongolian gulls across migration seasons, migration stages, and diel periods, we extracted all locations classified as “flight” during the spring and autumn migration periods and calculated their flight altitude relative to the ground (hereafter: relative altitude). The classification of daytime and nighttime at each location was determined using the “maptools” package in R [25]. The relative altitude was calculated as the difference between the altitude recorded by the logger and the elevation at each location. To minimize the influence of measurement errors on the variation in relative altitude, we excluded records with values lower than 0 m. Elevation data were derived from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission digital elevation model at a spatial resolution of 30 arc-seconds (SRTM30) [26]. Based on the above definitions, and using the “dplyr” and “geosphere” packages [27] in R to extract, we calculated eight migration parameters for each tracked individual during spring and autumn, as well as for the coastal and inland migratory stages, as follows:

Statistical analysis

We used linear mixed models (LMMs) to examine which factors accounted for the difference in migration parameters using the ‘lme4’ R package. Eight migration parameters, as described above, were entered into models as the response variable, with stage (inland and coast), season (spring and autumn) and their interaction as explanatory variables, and individual as a random intercept to account for repeated measures. Furthermore, the relative altitude difference between daytime and nighttime flight was also analyzed. The significance of explanatory variables was defined as a 95% confidence interval (CI) that does not cross zero. Furthermore, ‘emmeans’ R package (V. 1.10.3) was used for post-hoc pairwise comparisons of the LMMs in the interaction term when the CI does not cross zero, applying a separate Tukey adjustment to the P values [33]. The relative altitude changes of the Mongolian gull were quantified using Generalized Additive Models (GAM) with the ’mgcv’ package in R [34]. Independent sample t-tests were used to compare the seasonal differences of the eight migration parameters after assessing normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. When the data did not meet the assumption of normality, log-transformed (log(x + 1)) were applied; if normality could not be achieved, Wilcoxon tests were used for the comparisons. As the sample sizes in each year were relatively small, we did not consider interannual variation in the analyses. All statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were conducted in R v4.3.2 and ArcGIS Pro v3.0.2. Data are presented as mean ± SD.

Results

Migration routes and critical stopover sites

Based on the tracking data, we found the individuals started the spring migration from the eastern coast of China around March 19 (± 15 days, n = 29). They left the Yellow Sea around April 1 (± 7 days, n = 30), then turned westward to cross the Mongolian Plateau, and arrived at the breeding grounds around April 16 (± 12 days, n = 30, Table S2). A total of 80 stopover sites were recorded during spring migration, including 36 sites during the coastal migration and 44 sites during the inland migration. Major stopover sites were distributed in the western coast of the Yellow Sea. Major inland stopover sites included Dalinor Lake, the northern part of the Yellow River in China, the Tuul River and Kherlen River basins north of Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia, as well as other small lakes (Fig. 1a, Table S3).

Mongolian gulls started the autumn migration from late July to August (13 Aug. ± 15 days, n = 33). They crossed the Mongolian Plateau eastward and reached the Yellow Sea between late August to early September (3 Sep. ± 13 days, n = 33). The migration corridor was generally similar to that used in spring. They then gradually arrived at the wintering grounds from late September to early January of the following year (17 Nov. ± 63 days, n = 34, Table S2). A total of 192 stopover sites were recorded during autumn migration, of which 54 occurred during the inland migration and 138 others during the coastal migration. Major inland stopover sites included Buir Lake, as well as the basin of Selenga river, Orkhon river and Kherlen river in Northern Mongolia, whereas major coastal stopover sites were concentrated along the northern and western coasts of the Yellow Sea (Fig. 1b, Table S3).

Difference in migration parameters between coast and inland

The migration distance (CI: 1195.62, 1875.45) and daily travel speed (CI: 52.97, 199.46) of Mongolian gulls during inland migration was significantly larger than during coastal migration in both spring and autumn (Table 1; Fig. 2, Table S2). However, there were no significant differences in the migration duration (coast: 15.42 ± 14.23 day, inland: 14.55 ± 7.32 day), stopover duration (coast: 8.67 ± 12.46 day, inland: 6.97 ± 7.07 day), travel duration (coast: 6.75 ± 5.74 day, inland: 7.58 ± 2.26 day), number of stopover sites (coast: 1.17 ± 1.34, inland: 1.13 ± 0.94) and straightness index (coast: 0.78 ± 0.22, inland: 0.82 ± 0.09) between the coast and inland migration in spring (Table 1; Fig. 2, Table S2).

Changes in relative altitude

Overall, the relative altitude of Mongolian gulls showed significant differences between seasons (CI: -67.71, -36.89), stages (CI: 175.22, 208.94) and diel period (CI: -135.75, -114.62, Table 1). In both spring and autumn, the relative altitude along the inland was significantly higher than that along the coast (Ps < 0.001, Table 1; Fig. 2h). During all seasons, stages and diel periods, the Mongolian gull primarily flew below 500 m, relative to the ground (Fig. 3, Fig. S1, Fig. S2, Table S2). However, they occasionally flew higher in different seasons or stages, such as reaching altitudes as high as 2,589 m along the coast and 6,042 m inland during spring migration; and in autumn, the highest altitude reaches 1,508 m along the coast and 3,619 m inland (Fig. S1, Fig. S2).

Across all seasons and migration stages, Mongolian gulls flew at significantly higher relative altitude during the day than at night (Ps < 0.001; Fig. 4, Table S2). Relative altitude also exhibited clear diel periodicity, with birds flying at relatively low altitudes at night, gradually increasing altitude during the day, and then decreasing again toward dusk. Notably, the magnitude of the diurnal increase in flight altitude was significantly greater during the inland migration than during the coast (Fig. 5). During the spring coastal migration, Mongolian gulls flew at a mean relative altitude of 63 ± 187 m at night, which increased to 117 ± 286 m during the day (edf = 2.78, F = 12.15, P < 0.001; Figs. 4a and 5a). In contrast, during the inland migration stage, mean relative altitude increased from 132 ± 225 m at night to 415 ± 511 m during the day (edf = 2.99, F = 220.5, P < 0.001; Figs. 4a and 5b). A similar pattern was observed in autumn. During the coastal migration, mean relative altitude increased from 21 ± 55 m at night to 45 ± 103 m during the day (edf = 2.90, F = 48.52, P < 0.001; Figs. 4b and 5c), whereas during the inland, it increased from 179 ± 377 m at night to 459 ± 538 m during the day (edf = 2.93, F = 108, P < 0.001; Figs. 4b and 5d).

Difference in seasonal migration parameters

The migration parameters of the Mongolian gull differed notably between seasons. Compared with autumn, the spring migration was characterized by a much shorter migration distance (nearly 460 km less), as well as a significantly shorter migration duration, stopover duration, travel duration, as well as fewer stopovers (Ps < 0.05). In contrast, spring migration showed a more time-efficient and direct strategy: migration routes were significantly straighter, daily travel speed was markedly higher, and relative altitude was significantly higher than in autumn (Ps < 0.05; Table 2).

Discussion

We found that the Mongolian gull exhibited pronounced spatiotemporal shifts in its migration duration, travel duration, number of stopover sites and other migratory behaviors when switching from the East Asian coastal region to migration across inland arid and semiarid areas, like the Mongolian Plateau. As predicted, the inland migration of the Mongolian gull was characterized by longer migration distance, fewer stopover times, shorter stopover duration, higher daily travel speed and higher flight altitude above ground than coastal migration.

Fewer stopover sites and shorter stopover duration during inland migration

We found the number of stopover sites and stopover duration of Mongolian gulls varied across seasons and stages, whereas their migration routes were similar between seasons. During autumn migration over the Mongolian Plateau, gulls made very few stopovers and stayed for short periods, with each individual stopping once on average with the stopover duration being only about one-sixth of that in coastal migration. This pattern is consistent with findings for the sympatric migrating Whooper Swans (Cygnus cynus) [17], the western population of the Swan Geese (A. cygnoides) [37], and other Mongolian gulls tracked in northern Mongolia [18], resembling strategies observed in latitudinal migrants when crossing the ecological barriers [9]. Studies of latitudinally migrating landbirds and waterbirds have shown that when crossing ecological barriers such as the Mediterranean-Sahara Desert system [38] or the boreal forest [12], birds often adopt strategies involving very few stopovers or even nonstop flights because of the scarcity of suitable habitats. European populations of Black-headed Gulls (Chroicocephalus ridibundus) have also been reported to perform nocturnal flights when crossing ecological barriers such as the Wadden Sea [39]. The Mongolian Plateau is characterized by sparse wetlands, extensive east–west extent, and generally unsuitable environmental conditions for migratory waterbirds [14–16]. In addition, climate change and human activities (e.g., resource extraction) have led to significant declines in both the number and area of wetlands in this region [16]. In fact, the natural wetlands suitable for prolonged stopovers and effective refueling of migratory waterbirds have gradually diminished in this region [40], which may contribute to Mongolian gulls rarely stopover in inland areas.

Mongolian gulls adopted a contrasting strategy of prolonged stopovers during autumn migration in the Yellow Sea. Some individuals arrived at the northern coast of the Yellow Sea in late August, then gradually moved along the coast with multiple long stopovers for 34–110 days. This pattern indicates that the coast of the Yellow Sea is the crucial refueling area for Mongolian gulls after long inland migrations. The extensive distribution of intertidal mudflats, estuarine wetlands, and aquaculture ponds along the Yellow Sea’s coast [36, 41] provides abundant and stable food resources and suitable habitats for Mongolian gulls and other waterbirds. Unlike the Mongolian Plateau, where wetlands are scarce and highly fragmented, coastal wetlands have higher habitat connectivity and more predictable resources [42], allowing Mongolian gulls to adopt a strategy of “long stopover with less migration”. This striking contrast between inland and coastal stopover behavior and refueling strategies reflects the gulls’ high adaptability to different ecological conditions and highlights the strong ecological constraints imposed by the Mongolian Plateau on longitudinal migrants. Furthermore, the long stopovers along the coast of the Yellow Sea emphasize the importance of conserving these wetlands for Mongolian gulls and other endangered species that cross the Mongolian Plateau.

Faster flight over inland

Our results also show that in both spring and autumn, Mongolian gulls increased their daily travel speed over inland areas, thereby covering longer distances in shorter periods and minimizing exposure to unfavorable environments. This “fast migration with few stopovers” strategy is closely linked to the lack of suitable stopover habitats and limited opportunities for refueling on the Mongolian Plateau, forcing birds to increase migratory intensity to overcome ecological constraints. This pattern aligns with migration strategies documented in other birds facing ecological barriers [9]. Migratory birds often shift from a “stopover for refueling” tactic to “rapid crossing” tactic when habitats along migration routes are scarce. For example, landbirds crossing the Sahara Desert typically extend flight duration and adjust daily flight schedules to reduce exposure to extreme heat and limited resources areas [7]. Similarly, Bar-tailed Godwits (Limosa lapponica) crossing the Pacific Ocean undertake continuous flights lasting 7–9 days to move directly from breeding to wintering grounds [8]. Thus, whether crossing deserts, oceans, or arid and semi-arid plateaus, rapid crossing rather than frequent stopovers appears to be the optimal adaptive strategy when habitats are unavailable, inhospitable or highly fragmented.

Faster migration in spring and slower in autumn

Mongolian gulls showed a clear seasonal contrast, with longer durations, greater migration distances, and more stopovers during autumn migration. Similar seasonal patterns have been reported in sympatric species breeding at similar latitudes, including Swan Geese (A. cygnoides) [43], Greylag Geese (A. anser) [44], Taiga bean Geese (A. fabalis middendorffii) [40], as well as in closely related species such as the Lesser Black-backed Gulls (L. fuscus) [31], Brown-headed Gulls (C. brunnicephalus) [24], and other migratory landbirds [45]. This strategy is generally attributed to differences in life-history pressures between seasons: birds are thought to minimize time during spring migration and minimize energy expenditure during autumn migration [46]. In spring, Mongolian gulls are under strong pressure to reach breeding grounds early to secure resources and breeding opportunities, which drives them to increase flight speed and reduce stopover duration despite experiencing less favorable wind conditions [20]. This may explain why migration duration, stopover duration, flight duration, and number of stopover sites were similar between coastal and inland stages in spring. In autumn, although favorable tailwinds can facilitate rapid movement cross the Mongolian Plateau [20] the release from breeding pressure allows gulls to adopt a prolonged stopover strategy in the Yellow Sea, accumulating energy reserves and reducing migration related mortality.

Cross the inland with higher flight altitude

According to our data, the Mongolian gulls flew at significantly higher altitudes above ground during inland migration in both seasons, with flight altitude showing a characteristic increase, followed by a decrease in relation to the time of day. Previous studies have demonstrated that flight altitude is influenced by wind conditions, solar radiation, temperature, and humidity, and varies when birds cross different geographic barriers. Dufour et al. (2026) showed that some landbirds fly at very low altitudes over the Mediterranean Sea but substantially increase flight altitude when crossing the Sahara Desert [7]. Studies on Oriental Reed Warbler (Acrocephalus orientalis) found that the flight altitude increased from 2,394 m at night to 5,367 m during daytime [2]. Galtbalt et al. (2021) also reported distinct differences in flight altitudes over land and sea in Far Eastern Curlew (Numenius madagascariensis) and Whimbrel (N. phaeopus) [23]. The Mongolian Plateau lies at the intersection of the arid and semiarid area and the monsoon wind, where precipitation, solar radiation, wind speed, and tailwind conditions vary strongly with altitude. As a diurnal migrant, the Mongolian gull may increase flight altitude to reduce thermal stress and exploit more favorable wind at higher altitude, thereby enhancing migration efficiency and producing the observed daily pattern of rising and declining flight altitude. In coastal regions, suitable temperature, humidity, and wind conditions are typically concentrated at lower altitude [23], making low altitude flight more advantageous.

Additionally, differences in life-history objectives between seasons may also contribute to variation in flight altitude [46]. During autumn coastal migration, Mongolian gulls mainly forage and move among feeding sites in the Yellow Sea, where maintaining low altitudes may help conserve energy and locate food. In spring, however, the primary goal is to reach breeding grounds as quickly as possible. Higher flight altitudes may allow gulls to utilize more favorable winds, increase migration speed and avoid predators, enhancing overall migration success and breeding opportunities. Future research examining migratory survival and reproductive success would help to substantiate the adaptive advantages of this behavior.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that landscape features along migration corridors and seasonal-specific demands significantly affect the migration strategy of Mongolian gulls, causing pronounced differences between coastal and inland longitudinal migration stages. When crossing ecological barriers, such as the Mongolian Plateau, Mongolian gulls adopt a strategy characterized by few stopovers, fast migration, and high flight altitudes relative to the ground in both spring and autumn. In contrast, in the coastal wetlands of the Yellow Sea, gulls migrate rapidly in spring under breeding pressure, but adopt an energy accumulation strategy in autumn with more stopovers, slower migration, and lower flight altitudes.

With an increase of tracking and banding research, more migratory species that travel longitudinally through the Mongolian Plateau and the surrounding arid areas have been identified, including Whooper Swan, Relict Gull (Ichthyaetus relictus) [47], Common Swift (Apus apus pekinensis) [48], Yellow-breasted Bunting (Emberiza aureola) [49], and other migrants (https://migrationatlas.org/) [49–52]. These findings suggest that this region functions as a multi-species longitudinal migration corridor and is broadly ecologically significant. Therefore, the Mongolian Plateau and adjacent arid areas should be recognized as a critical longitudinal flyway, and future studies should integrate multi-species and multi-scale approaches to investigate migration behavior, energy strategies, and environmental drivers. Furthermore, the prolonged stopovers of Mongolian gulls in coastal and summering areas highlight the critical importance of these habitats for the gulls and other migratory birds that cross this ecological barrier. Strengthening conservation and management of these areas will be essential to ensure sufficient energy supply and population safety for these species.
