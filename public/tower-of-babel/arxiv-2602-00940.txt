A Baire Category Approach to Besicovitch’s Theorem and Measure Regularity
Emma Gruner, Jan Reimann

Abstract
. By reformulating the classical proof as a Baire Category argument, we show that Besicovitch’s Theorem in Cantor space is provable in ACA 0 \text{ACA}_{0} , and additionally that the witnessing subset is computable from one jump of the original set. We show that the necessary formulation of Baire Category, which we call Baire Category Theorem for Closed Sets (BCTC), is equivalent to ACA 0 \text{ACA}_{0} , contrasting with previous results on the reverse math strength of Baire Category variants. We also examine the implications of BCTC for more general monotone functions on closed sets, and explore how changing the representation of a closed set affects the reverse math strength of its measure regularity properties.

03B30

1.
Introduction

Since its inception in the latter half of the twentieth century, the reverse mathematics research program has been used to analyze theorems across a wide range of disciplines. Real analysis and point-set topology have been some of the most popular areas of study, since many of the objects involved (real numbers, separable metric spaces, continuous functions, open sets, etc.) have “essentially countable” structures which allows them to be coded naturally as elements of
2^{\omega}
. Thus, these fields can be meaningfully discussed in the context of second-order arithmetic.

Geometric measure theory, on the other hand, is almost entirely unexplored from the reverse mathematics framework. Pauly and Fouché
9
studied Frostman’s Lemma in the context of Weihrauch degrees (see also
10
for effective aspects of Frostman’s Lemma). Another foundational result of geometric measure theory is a theorem proven by Abram Besicovitch in 1952
1
, which states that any closed subset in Euclidean space having infinite Hausdorff measure contains a closed subset with positive finite Hausdorff measure. Sets of positive finite measure have many convenient local properties (see
6
for an extensive treatment), so the ability to “pass” to such a subset is highly useful. Later in 1952
4
, Roy Davies strengthened Besicovitch’s result to the case where the given set is analytic. We will refer to the combined result as the
Besicovitch-Davies Theorem
.

Besides its importance in geometric measure theory as a whole, there are a few reasons why the reverse mathematical content of this theorem is of interest. For one, the original form of the theorem proven by Besicovitch only concerns closed sets. When we work over Cantor space
2^{\omega}
, the tree structure and compactness properties of these sets, particularly with regard to the definition of Hausdorff measure, are very convenient for formalization in second-order arithmetic.

Additionally, the set existence axioms required to prove Besicovitch’s theorem are not obvious from the structure of the original argument. If
F
represents the original closed subset, then the witnessing subset
E
is defined as the infinite intersection of nested decreasing closed subsets
\{E_{k}\}_{k\in\omega}
. By the properties of the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff
\delta
-measure on compact sets, one can verify that
E_{0}
has an arithmetic definition from
F
, and each subsequent
E_{k+1}
can be defined similarly from
E_{k}
. Thus, while the existence of each set
E_{k}
in the approximating sequence is provable in
\text{ACA}_{0}
, the same may not be true for the infinite intersection
E=\bigcap_{k\in\omega}E_{k}
.

In this paper, we were able to impose a topology on the space of closed subsets of our given set
F
, and relate the Hausdorff
\delta
-measures of these sets to certain open and closed sets within this topology. By exploiting this topological structure, we could use a variant of the Baire Category Theorem to significantly decrease the arithmetic complexity of the final witnessing subset
E
. This brings us to the following main theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

In Cantor space
2^{\omega}
, Besicovitch’s Theorem is provable in
\text{ACA}_{0}
.

In fact, from a computability standpoint, Besicovitch’s Theorem only requires a small amount of
\text{ACA}_{0}
’s full power, in the following sense:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be a closed subset with infinite
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure, and suppose
Z_{F}\in 2^{\omega}
is a code for its tree. Then we can find a closed subset
E\subseteq F
with positive finite Hausdorff measure whose code
Z_{E}
is computable from
Z_{F}^{\prime}
(the first Turing jump of
Z_{F}
).

Besicovitch’s original argument suggests that infinitely many jumps of the original closed set
F
may be required to compute the witnessing subset
E
. The theorem above also stands in contrast to a result by Reimann and Kjos-Hanssen (2014) regarding
G_{\delta}
sets
8
. By the full Besicovitch-Davies Theorem, any
G_{\delta}
set of infinite Hausdorff measure contains a closed subset of positive measure, but the authors exhibited a lightface
\Pi^{0}_{2}
subset of
2^{\omega}
whose witnessing closed subsets could not be computed from any hyperarithmetic real.

Whether Besicovitch’s Theorem is equivalent to
\text{ACA}_{0}
remains open. However, we did show that Besicovitch implies the axioms of
\text{WWKL}_{0}
, or even
\text{WKL}_{0}
if we impose additional structure on the witnessing subset
E
. (See Section 4.2 for details). Therefore, we at least know that Besicovitch is not provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
.

While Besicovitch’s theorem provided the motivation for this project, the measure theoretic and topological techniques developed along the way led to interesting avenues of study in their own right. For one, the definition of
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure involves taking the limit of a sequence of outer measures, called
\delta
-measures. If we fix one of these outer measures, we can ask a variety of questions related to its regularity properties and their reverse mathematical strength. These investigations also have implications for the standard Lebesgue measure on
2^{\omega}
, since any of these
s
-dimensional
\delta
-measures reduces to the Lebesgue measure when
s=1
.

Other research projects on the reverse mathematics of measure theory have already explored many of its technicalities. One of the first extensive treatments was in Xiaokang Yu’s Ph.D. thesis (1987) and a subsequent joint paper with Stephen Simpson (1990), where it was demonstrated that
\text{ACA}_{0}
was necessary to prove that the Lebesgue measure of every open set exists, and that
\text{WWKL}_{0}
was necessary to prove that the Lebesgue measure is countably additive
14
,
13
. A 2009 paper by Simpson investigated the reverse math strength of the well-known regularity result that any Borel set contains an
F_{\sigma}
-subset of the same Lebesgue measure
11
. Surprisingly, Simpson showed that this result is independent of many standard subsystems of second-order arithmetic, as one can find models of
\text{RCA}_{0}
where this regularity property holds, but where the axioms of
\text{WWKL}_{0}
,
\text{WKL}_{0}
, and
\text{ACA}_{0}
may either hold or fail.

In this paper, we restrict our attention to closed sets, and to questions that are some variation of the following: given a closed subset
F
, how hard is it to construct a closed subset
E
of some desired
s
-dimensional
\delta
-measure? In particular, we explore how the answer to this question may change depending on how the closed sets are coded; we introduce the notions of “standard closed” and “pruned closed” to describe differences in the structure of these sets’ representative trees. Other variables that affect the reverse math strength are the precision with which we specify the target measure of our set
E
, and whether the measure of our starting set
F
exists in the space.

Preliminaries regarding the representations of closed sets and the Hausdorff
\delta
-measure can be found in Sections 2.2 and 2.3 respectively, and various regularity results for these measures are proven in Section 4.1. A few contrasting results of note are given below:

Proposition 1.3
.

The following is provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
: If
F\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
is a nontrivial standard closed set with tree code
Z_{F}
, and
c
is such that
0\leq c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
, then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a nontrivial standard closed set
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies
c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})<c+\epsilon
.

Proposition 1.4
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, the following is equivalent to
\text{WKL}_{0}
: If
F\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
is a nontrivial standard closed set with tree code
Z_{F}
, and
c
is such that
0\leq c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
, then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a nontrivial pruned closed set
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies
c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})<c+\epsilon
.

Proposition 1.5
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, the following is equivalent to
\text{WKL}_{0}
: If
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is a nontrivial standard closed set with tree code
Z_{F}
, and there exists a real number
d
such that
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})=d
, then there exists a nontrivial pruned closed set
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})=H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})=d.

Proposition 1.6
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, the following is equivalent to
\text{ACA}_{0}
: If
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is a nontrivial standard closed set with tree code
Z_{F}
, then there exists a nontrivial pruned closed set
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})=H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
.

Finally, we wanted to explore the particular variant of the Baire Category Theorem needed for the
\text{ACA}_{0}
-proof of Besicovitch’s Theorem. Classically, the Baire Category Theorem states that in a complete metric space, the intersection of countably many dense open sets is dense, and its reverse mathematical strength has already been extensively studied. In its most straightforward formalization, Baire Category is actually a famously “simple” result, being one of few theorems in classical analysis that is provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
. (See
12
for a proof).

However, it was later discovered that the exact manner in which the open sets in the statement are coded is important. Besides the standard coding of an open set as a countable union of basic sets, one can also represent an open set by specifying a countable dense sequence of points in its closed complement. Such a representation is called a “separably open” set, and was explored by Douglas Brown in a 1990 paper
3
. In this work, Brown showed that
\Pi^{1}_{1}\text{-CA}
is required to prove the equivalence of these two representations for arbitrary metric spaces, although
\text{ACA}_{0}
suffices for compact spaces. In a 1993 paper
2
, Simpson and Brown showed that replacing the standard open set representations with separably open sets yields a version of Baire Category which is not provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
or even
\text{WKL}_{0}
. However, this new Baire Category variant did not require the full power of
\text{ACA}_{0}
, since it could be proven in a strictly weaker subsystem that the authors called
\text{RCA}_{0}^{+}
.

In this paper, we will interpret the Baire Category Theorem in the context of a closed subset of Cantor space. Since a closed subset of a complete metric space is itself complete under the same metric, the Baire Category Theorem applies when working with open sets in the subspace topology. We call this variant “Baire Category Theorem for Closed Sets”, or BCTC, and can formally state it as follows:

“
Let
X
be a complete metric space, and let
F\subseteq X
be a nonempty closed set. Suppose
\{U_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
is a sequence of nonempty open sets in
X
such that each
U_{n}
is dense in
F
(that is, if
N\subseteq X
is a basic open set such that
N\cap F\neq\emptyset
, then there exists some
x\in N\cap F\cap U_{n}
). Then
\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}
is also dense in
F
.
”

Like the classical Baire Category Theorem, we must be explicit about how the open and closed sets in the statement are coded in order to analyze the reverse mathematical strength. The most natural interpretation for Cantor space, and that which is necessary for the proof of Besicovitch’s Theorem, is to code the open sets
U_{n}
as unions of cylinder sets and the closed set
F
as a tree. In this manner, the classical proof of Baire Category in
\text{RCA}_{0}
does not translate, since for a given basic open set
N
, there is no computable way to determine whether
N\cap F
is nonempty. However, given the previous result of Brown and Simpson regarding an “altered” version of Baire Category, one might conjecture that BCTC is still relatively simple from a reverse math perspective. However, we were able to prove the following equivalence:

Theorem 1.7
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, BCTC is equivalent to
\text{ACA}_{0}
.

However, this equivalence only holds in the case where the closed set
F
is “standard closed.” Taking
F
to be separably closed or pruned closed set gives a version of BCTC which is still provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}.
See Section 3.1 for details.

In Section 3.2, we explore how the closed version of Baire Category Theorem has some interesting implications regarding certain “monotone” functions defined on closed subsets of Cantor space, particularly those which satisfy a certain density property regarding their behavior around their infimum. We define the “Dense Monotone Minimum Principle” (or DMMin) to be the statement that a function with this property always realizes its infimum. We analyze the reverse math strength of this principle, along with a related statement we call “Separated Monotone Minimum” (SMMin), in order to prove the following:

Theorem 1.8
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, both SMMin and DMMin are equivalent to
\text{ACA}_{0}
.

One of the most natural examples of a monotone function with this density property is precisely the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff
\delta
-measure, when restricted to the set of closed sets whose measure satisfies some fixed lower bound. So besides being an interesting result in its own right, DMMin also serves an important role in the intermediate steps of the Besicovitch Theorem proof.

2.
Background and Preliminaries

2.1.
Subsystems of Second Order Arithmetic

We assume some standard background in reverse mathematics and second-order arithmetic; in particular, we will assume that the reader is familiar with the definitions and key properties of the subsystems
\text{RCA}_{0},

\text{WKL}_{0}
, and
\text{ACA}_{0}
. More information can be found in any standard text on reverse mathematics (see, for example,
12
and
7
).

However, we will briefly review some well-known equivalences for the above systems that we will need for this analysis. We will also review the definition of the subsystem Weak Weak König’s Lemma, or
\text{WWKL}_{0}
, since this system is not as well-known.

First, over
\text{RCA}_{0}
the system
\text{WKL}_{0}
is equivalent to several important compactness principles, of which we give two below. See
12
for a proof.

Proposition 2.1
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, the system
\text{WKL}_{0}
is equivalent to the following statements:

(1)

In any metric space, any open covering of a compact set has a finite subcovering (Heine-Borel theorem).

(2)

In any metric space, any nested decreasing sequence of nonempty compact sets has nonempty intersection.

For the system
\text{ACA}_{0}
, one of the most important equivalences involves the existence of ranges for functions on the natural numbers. In fact, in our analysis, we will almost exclusively use this equivalence when verifying that a given statement proves the axioms of
\text{ACA}_{0}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, the system
\text{ACA}_{0}
is equivalent to the following statement: every one-to-one function
f:\omega\to\omega
has a range, i.e. there exists a set
X\subseteq\omega
such that for all
n\in\omega

n\in\omega\iff\exists k\in\omega\>f(k)=n.

The system
\text{ACA}_{0}
is also equivalent to various statements regarding sequence convergence. We state two such statements below, since they will be of use to us later.

Proposition 2.3
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, the system
\text{ACA}_{0}
is equivalent to the following:

(1)

In a compact metric space, every sequence of points has a convergent subsequence.

(2)

Every bounded monotone sequence of real numbers converges.

As with the
\text{WKL}_{0}
equivalences, proofs of the above results can be found in
12
.

Between the subsystems of
\text{RCA}_{0}
and
\text{WKL}_{0}
in reverse mathematical strength is the system
\text{WWKL}_{0}
, whose definition we give below.

Definition 2.1
.

We define
Weak Weak König’s Lemma
to be the following axiom: if
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
is a tree with the property that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|\{\sigma\in T:|\sigma|=n\}|}{2^{n}}>0,

then
T
has an infinite path.
The formal system
\text{WWKL}_{0}
consists of the axioms of
\text{RCA}_{0}
plus Weak Weak König’s Lemma.

It is clear that
\text{WWKL}_{0}
can be at most as strong as
\text{WKL}_{0}
, since the statement of Weak Weak König’s Lemma is just the standard Weak König’s Lemma restricted to a certain class of infinite trees. One can also demonstrate a computable infinite tree which meets the conditions of Weak Weak König’s Lemma but which does not have a computable path, proving that
\text{WWKL}_{0}
is strictly stronger than
\text{RCA}_{0}
. Showing that
\text{WWKL}_{0}
lies strictly below
\text{WKL}_{0}
requires constructing a more sophisticated model.
While
\text{WWKL}_{0}
is not one of the standard “Big Five” systems, it has been shown to be equivalent to several theorems of measure theory and algorithmic randomness.

2.2.
Closed Sets and their Representations

In this paper, we will concern ourselves with measure and topological results in Cantor space, or
2^{\omega}
. In this topology, we will use the notation
N_{\sigma}
to denote the basic open set

N_{\sigma}=\{X\in 2^{\omega}:X\supseteq\sigma\},

where
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
.

It is likely that many of the results in this work can be generalized to arbitrary compact metric spaces, although the details have not been formalized.

Since we cannot speak of general subsets of
2^{\omega}
within second-order arithmetic, we need a way to code a closed subset
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
as a subset of
\omega
. To do this, we will almost exclusively use trees.

Definition 2.2
.

A
tree
over
\{0,1\}
is a subset
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
with the property that if
\sigma\in T
and
\tau\subseteq\sigma
, then
\tau\in T
as well. This property is called being
closed under initial segments
.

A sequence
X\in 2^{\omega}
is a
path through T
if the length-
n
initial segment
X\upharpoonright_{n}
is an element of
T
for all
n\in\omega
. The set of all paths through a tree
T
is denoted by
[T]
. We will call a set
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}

standard closed
if there exists a tree
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
with
F=[T].

Classically, any closed set
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
can be represented by some tree
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
. Unless otherwise specified, when we are working in second-order arithmetic and we speak of the existence of a closed set in
2^{\omega}
, we implicitly mean a tree which represents that closed set. However, we will also discuss two other ways we can represent closed sets.

Definition 2.3
.

A
pruned tree
is a tree
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
with the additional property that for every
\sigma\in T
, we have at least one of
\sigma^{\frown}0\in T
or
\sigma^{\frown}1\in T
.

As with a standard tree, we associated a pruned tree
T
with the closed set
[T]
in
2^{\omega}
, the set of all paths through
T
. We will call a set
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}

pruned closed
if there exists a pruned tree
T
with
F=[T].

Certainly any closed set which has a pruned tree representation also has a standard tree representation, since a pruned tree is just a specific type of tree. However, depending on the axiom system being used, the converse does not always hold; we will explore the reverse math strength of the existence of pruned tree representations later in this section. Note that unlike the standard tree representation, the pruned tree representation for a closed set
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is unique, consisting of exactly the strings
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
which are initial segments of some element
X\in F
.

Also, later in this work it will be convenient to have a name for closed subsets whose corresponding tree representations are infinite. If we are working in an axiom system equivalent to or stronger than
\text{WKL}_{0}
, this is equivalent to the closed set being nonempty. However, to avoid any ambiguity, we make the following definition.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
F
be a standard closed or pruned closed set in
2^{\omega}.
We call
F

nontrivial
if its corresponding tree (or pruned tree) in
2^{<\omega}
is infinite.

An alternative way to code a closed set is by specifying a countable sequence of elements of
2^{\omega}
which are dense in that set, in the following sense:

Definition 2.5
.

Let
S=\langle X_{n}\rangle_{n\in\omega}
be a sequence of elements of
2^{\omega}
. The set
\bar{S}
consists of all
X\in 2^{\omega}
with the property that

\forall k\in\omega\>\exists n\in\omega\>\forall i<k\>X_{n}(i)=X(i).

A set
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is called
separably closed
if it is empty, or if there exists a sequence
S
of elements of
2^{\omega}
such that
F=\bar{S}
.

When it comes to representing open sets in
2^{\omega}
, we will use the notion defined below. This corresponds naturally to the idea of an open set as a union of cylinder sets.

Definition 2.6
.

A set
U\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is called
open
if there exists a set
V\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
with the property that for all
X\in 2^{\omega}

X\in U\iff\exists\sigma\in V\>\sigma\subseteq X.

Such a set
V
is called a
code
for
U
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
V
is closed upward under extensions: that is, if
\sigma\in V
and
\tau\supseteq\sigma
, then
\tau\in V
.

Observe that if an open code
V\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
is nonempty, then there will necessarily be an element
X
in our model which lies in the corresponding open set
U
; we can (for example) just take any string
\sigma\in V
and extend with infinitely many 0’s. Thus, we will freely use the term “nonempty open set” to refer to an open set
U
whose corresponding code
V
is nonempty, without any danger of ambiguity.

It is straightforward to see that, given an open code
V\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
(closed under extensions), then
T=2^{<\omega}\setminus V
is a tree, and vice versa. From this fact, it follows that a set
U\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is open if and only if its complement
F=2^{\omega}\setminus U
is standard closed. While we will not work with this notion in this paper, one can also represent an open set as the complement of a separably closed set. This notion has been previously studied in the context of Baire Category, as mentioned in the introduction.

It turns out that the ability to translate between different representations of the same closed set (or open set) is not trivial, as the results below will demonstrate. In the statements of these results, we will abuse language slightly for the sake of efficiency, as we will conflate the existence of closed subsets with the existence of their appropriate representations. For example, the statement “Every separably closed set is pruned closed” is technically shorthand for “For every countable sequence
S
of elements of
2^{\omega}
, there exists a pruned tree
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
such that for all
X\in 2^{\omega}
,
X\in\bar{S}
if and only if
X\in[T]
.”

First, we note that a pruned tree representation is the strongest in a sense, since it can generate both of the remaining representations.

Proposition 2.4
.

The following are provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}:

(1)

Every pruned closed set in
2^{\omega}
is standard closed.

(2)

Every pruned closed set in
2^{\omega}
is separably closed.

Proof.

First, we note that (i) is trivial; as a pruned tree is just a special type of tree, any pruned closed set is automatically standard closed as well.
For (ii), let
T
be a pruned tree. If
T
is finite, then the corresponding closed set
F=[T]
is empty, so
F
is separably closed trivially. Otherwise, let
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
be an enumeration of the elements of
T
. Define a sequence
S=\langle X_{n}\rangle_{n\in\omega}
in
2^{\omega}
by setting
X_{n}
to be the leftmost extension of the string
\sigma_{n}
through
T
.
More precisely,
X_{n}
is defined recursively by

X_{n}(m)=\begin{cases}\sigma_{n}(m),&\text{if }m<|\sigma|\\
0,&\text{if }m\geq|\sigma|\text{ and }X_{n}\upharpoonright_{m}^{\frown}0\in T\\
1,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that the above construction is (uniformly) computable from
\sigma_{n}
, so the existence of the overall sequence
S
is provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
. Also, since
T
is pruned, every string
\sigma\in T
satisfies at least one of
\sigma^{\frown}0\in T
or
\sigma^{\frown}1\in T
. Therefore, each sequence
X_{n}
defined in the above manner will be an element of
[T]
.

We claim that
\bar{S}
and
[T]
define the same closed set. Indeed, if
X\in[T]
, then all initial segments
X\upharpoonright_{k}
(for
k\in\omega
) are elements of
T
, so will appear as strings in the enumeration
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}.
For each
X\upharpoonright_{k}
, the corresponding element of the sequence
S
will agree with
X
through the first
k
entries, so
X
satisfies the conditions for being in
\bar{S}
.

On the other hand, suppose
X\in\bar{S}
. We wish to show that for each
k\in\omega
, the initial segment
X\upharpoonright_{k}
is an element of
T
.
First, note that for each
k
, there must be some
N_{k}\in\omega
such that in our enumeration
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
of
T
, all strings
\sigma_{n}
with
n\geq N_{k}
have
|\sigma_{n}|\geq k
. This implies that for all
X_{n}
in the sequence
S=\langle X_{n}\rangle_{n\in\omega}
with
n\geq N_{k}
, the length-
k
initial segment of
X_{n}
will be an element of
T
(as
T
is closed under initial segments).
Since
X\in\bar{S}
, we know there is some
X_{n}
in
S
such that
X
agrees with
X_{n}
for the first
k
entries. If we can find such an
X_{n}
at or after index
N_{k}
, then this implies
X\upharpoonright_{k}=X_{n}\upharpoonright_{k}
is an element of
T
.

If not, then for all
m\geq k
, the witnessing element of
S
which agrees with
X
through the first
m
entries must occur before position
N_{k}
. By the infinite pigeonhole principle, one of these elements
X_{n}
must agree with
X
in all entries, i.e.
X_{n}=X
. By our earlier discussion, all
X_{n}
in the sequence
S
are elements of
[T]
, so in particular the string
X\upharpoonright_{k}=X_{n}\upharpoonright_{k}
is in
T
as desired.
∎

In contrast to the result above, obtaining a pruned tree representation from a standard or separately closed set, or translating between the latter two representations, requires much more axiomatic strength. As mentioned in the introduction, variations of items (iii) and (iv) of the theorem below have been proven
before for arbitrary compact metric spaces: see
3
. However, we include the proofs anyway for the sake of completeness and consistency with our terminology in Cantor space.

Theorem 2.5
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, the following statements are all equivalent to
\text{ACA}_{0}
:

(1)

Every standard closed set in
2^{\omega}
is pruned closed.

(2)

Every separably closed set in
2^{\omega}
is pruned closed.

(3)

Every standard closed set in
2^{\omega}
is separably closed.

(4)

Every separably closed set in
2^{\omega}
is standard closed.

Proof.

We first show that
\text{ACA}_{0}
is sufficient to prove both (i) and (ii). In light of the previous proposition, this would also imply that
\text{ACA}_{0}
can prove (iii) and (iv).

Claim 1:

\text{ACA}_{0}\implies(i)

Proof of Claim 1:

Let
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
be a tree. Using arithmetic comprehension, we can define the tree
\tilde{T}
by

\tilde{T}=\{\sigma\in T:\forall n\geq|\sigma|,\exists\tau\supseteq\sigma\text{ such that }|\tau|=n\text{ and }\tau\in T\}.

Note that by the pigeonhole principle and closure under initial segments,
\tilde{T}
consists precisely of the strings in
T
which have infinitely many extensions which are also in
T
. (Note that
\tilde{T}
could potentially be empty.) We can then deduce that
\tilde{T}
is pruned; if
\sigma
has infinitely many extensions in
T
, then by the pigeonhole principle again, at least one of
\sigma^{\frown}0
or
\sigma^{\frown}1
must also have infinitely many extensions in
T
.

It is then straightforward to verify that
[T]
and
[\tilde{T}]
represent the same closed set. As
\tilde{T}
is a subset of
T
, certainly any
X\in[\tilde{T}]
is also an element of
[T]
. On the other hand, if we have an element
X\in[T]
, then any initial segment
\sigma\subseteq X
has infinitely many extensions in
T
(namely, all finite initial segments
\tau\subseteq X
with
|\tau|\geq|\sigma|
), and thus
X\in[\tilde{T}]
as well. This completes the proof of (i) under
\text{ACA}_{0}.

\square

Claim 2:

\text{ACA}_{0}\implies(ii)

Proof of Claim 2:

If we have a separably closed set which is empty, then the empty tree
T
is trivially a pruned tree representing the same closed set. So assume our separably closed set
F
is nonempty, and let
S=\langle X_{n}\rangle_{n\in\omega}
be a sequence representing
F
. Using arithmetic comprehension, we can define a pruned tree
T
by collecting all the finite initial segments of the elements in the sequence
S
. That is,

T=\{\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}:\exists n\in\omega\text{ such that }\sigma\subseteq X_{n}\}.

It is clear that
T
is closed under initial segments, and that any
\sigma\in T
has infinitely many extensions in
T
(namely, all sufficiently long initial segments of its witnessing element
X_{n}
). Thus,
T
represents a pruned tree.

We can also check that
[T]
represents the same closed set as
\bar{S}
. If
X\in\bar{S}
, then for each
k\in\omega,
there is some
X_{n}
in
S
for which
X_{n}\upharpoonright_{k}=X\upharpoonright_{k}
. By definition,
X_{n}\upharpoonright_{k}=X\upharpoonright_{k}
is an element of
T
, which since

is arbitrary proves that

. Conversely, if
X\in[T]
, then for each initial segment
X\upharpoonright_{k}
, there exists some element of

which shares that same initial segment. This is precisely what it means to be an element of
\bar{S}
.
\square

We now wish to show that each of statements (i) - (iv) is sufficient to prove the axioms of
\text{ACA}_{0}
. Again, we will use the previous proposition to consolidate this argument. Under
\text{RCA}_{0}
, statement (i) implies statement (iii) and statement (ii) implies statement (iv). Therefore, it suffices to demonstrate a reversal argument for the weaker statements (iii) and (iv).

Claim 3:

(iii)\implies\text{ACA}_{0}

Proof of Claim 3:

Recall that by Theorem
2.2
, it suffices to show that under the assumed statement, every one-to-one function on the natural numbers has a range. Therefore, assume statement (iii) (every standard closed set in
2^{\omega}
is separably closed), and let
f:\omega\to\omega
be a one-to-one function.

For each
n\in\omega
, let
\tau_{n}
denote the length-
(n+1)
string consisting of
n

0
’s followed by a single
1
. Under
\text{RCA}_{0}
, we may define a tree
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
by setting
\sigma\in T
if and only if either:

(1)

\sigma
consists entirely of 0’s

(2)

\sigma
extends
\tau_{n}
for some
n\in\omega
, and there exists no
k<|\sigma|
with
f(k)=n
.

Note that
T
does define a tree. If
\sigma\in T
according to condition (1) - i.e.
\sigma
consists entirely of 0’s - then certainly any prefix of
\sigma
will consist entirely of 0’s as well. If
\sigma\in T
according to condition (2), then any prefix of
\tau\subseteq\sigma
will either consist entirely of 0’s, or will have the same corresponding prefix
\tau_{n}
as
\sigma
. In the latter case, if no
k
with
f(k)=n
has been found before input
|\sigma|
, then certainly no witness will be found before input
|\tau|
either. Thus,
\tau\in T
as well.

Now, if we are assuming statement (iii), then there must be some sequence
S=\langle X_{k}\rangle_{k\in\omega}
in
2^{\omega}
such that
\bar{S}=[T]
. We claim that
S
and

together can be used to compute the range of

, which is a consequence of the following fact:

n\notin\text{ran}(f)\iff\exists k\in\omega,X_{k}\supseteq\tau_{n}.

To understand this correspondence, assume first that
n\notin\text{ran}(f)
. It follows that any infinite sequence
X
which extends
\tau_{n}
will be an element of
[T]
, since we will never locate a

for which

. Such an

will be an element of
\bar{S}
as well, which means there must be some
X_{k}
in the sequence

which agrees with

, as desired.

Conversely, if there is some
X_{k}
in the sequence

which extends
\tau_{n}

must necessarily be an element of
\bar{S}=[T].
From this, it follows that

Now that we have this correspondence, we can use both the function

\square

Claim 4:

(iv)\implies\text{ACA}_{0}

Once again, let

, let

denote the (computable) infinite sequence with a 1 in position

Assuming statement (iv), there must be a tree

such that

. We claim that

has the following property:

Indeed, if

, then no element of the sequence

Thus, the tree

. In combination with the

∎

While we will briefly look at separably closed sets in the context of the Baire Category theorem (see Section 3.1), most of this paper will be concerned with standard and pruned closed sets (i.e. those which are represented by trees). These representations provide a natural way of approximating the sets’ measure, as we will demonstrate in the following subsection. If we are given a particular tree representation for a standard/pruned closed set
F
, we would like a way to code the set of all nontrivial closed subsets of
F
. It turns out that this can also be done using a tree.

Before we introduce this representation, we observe that there is a standard (computable) bijection between
\omega
and
2^{<\omega}
which enumerates the elements of
2^{<\omega}
first by length and then lexicographically. For example, the first few elements of this enumeration are

\langle\rangle,0,1,00,01,10,11,000,001,\dots

Throughout the rest of this paper, we freely identify the elements of
2^{<\omega}
with their counterparts in
\omega
under this bijection, letting the meaning be clear from context. In light of this bijection, we also introduce some useful notation.

Definition 2.7
.

Given an element
X\in 2^{\omega}
and a natural number
n\in\omega
, we let
X^{\leq n}
denote the initial segment of
X
with length
2^{n+1}-1
. If a finite string
\nu\in 2^{<\omega}
is such that
|\nu|\geq 2^{n+1}-1
, then we use the notation
\nu^{\leq n}
similarly.

This notation is convenient since
X^{\leq n}(\sigma)
will be defined for a particular
\sigma
(under the bijection mentioned above) if and only if
|\sigma|\leq n
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
be a tree. Let
S_{T}\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
be the tree consisting of all
\nu\in 2^{<\omega}
which satisfy the following properties:

(1)

For all
\sigma<|\nu|
with
\nu(\sigma)=1
, we also have
\nu(\tau)=1
for all
\tau\subseteq\sigma
.

(2)

For all
\sigma<|\nu|
with
\nu(\sigma)=1
, we have
\sigma\in T
.

(3)

For all
n\in\omega
such that
\nu^{\leq n}
is defined, at least one
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
of length
n
satisfies
\nu(\sigma)=1
.

If we have an element
Z\in[S_{T}]
, then we can identify
Z
with the subset
T_{Z}\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
consisting of exactly the strings
\sigma
for which
Z(\sigma)=1
; we will say that
Z
represents or codes the tree
T_{Z}
. Property (1) in the definition of
S_{T}
guarantees that
Z
does indeed code a tree, property (2) guarantees that this tree will be a subset of
T
, and property (3) guarantees that this tree will be infinite. Thus, the closed set
[T_{Z}]
will be a nontrivial closed subset of the closed set
[T]
. (Assuming we have access to Weak König’s Lemma, this also means that
[T_{Z}]
will be nonempty.) Likewise, if we have an arbitrary nontrivial standard/pruned closed subset of
[T]
, we can find a tree corresponding to that set which will be an element of
[S_{T}]
.

At times, it will be convenient to work with a variant of
S_{T}
whose infinite paths all correspond to pruned trees. Such a variant is defined below.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
be a tree. Let
\tilde{S}_{T}\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
be the tree consisting of all
\nu\in 2^{<\omega}
which satisfy the conditions for
S_{T}
in the previous definition, as well as the following additional condition:

(1)

For all
\sigma<|\nu|
such that
\nu(\sigma)=1
and such that
\nu(\sigma^{\frown}0)
and
\nu(\sigma^{\frown}1)
are both defined, we have at least one of
\nu(\sigma^{\frown}0)=1
or
\nu(\sigma^{\frown}1)=1
.

In light of these results, we can impose a completely metrizable topology on the space of nontrivial closed subsets of a particular standard/pruned closed set
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
. If
T
is a tree with
F=[T]
, then we can simply restrict the usual topology and metric on
2^{\omega}
to the closed subset
[S_{T}]
or
[\tilde{S}_{T}]
.

2.3.
Hausdorff Measure and its Representations

The notion of
Hausdorff measure
on a metric space is a generalization of Lebesgue measure. This measure is the main object of study in the subfield of analysis known as geometric measure theory, of which a standard reference is
6
.

The classical concept of Hausdorff measure is defined as a limit of a sequence of intermediate outer measures, which can be defined in the following way for Cantor space.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
E\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be a set, let
\delta
be a positive real number, and
s
a nonnegative real number. We call a countable or finite collection of cylinder sets
\{N_{\sigma_{i}}\}_{i<N}
a
\delta
-cover
for
E
if
E\subseteq\bigcup_{i<N}N_{\sigma_{i}}
and each
\sigma_{i}
satisfies
2^{-|\sigma_{i}|}\leq\delta
. (Here,
N
can either be a finite positive integer or
\omega
.) The
s-dimensional Hausdorff
\delta
-measure

\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta}(E)
is the quantity

\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta}(E)=\inf\left\{\sum_{i<N}2^{-s|\sigma_{i}|}:\{N_{\sigma_{i}}\}_{i<N}\text{ is a $\delta$-cover for }E\right\}.

Observe that if
s=1
, then
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta}(E)
is simply the standard Lebesgue measure
\mu(E)
regardless of the value of
\delta
. This is because any cover
\{N_{\sigma_{i}}\}_{i<N}
of
E
by cylinder sets can be converted to a
\delta
-cover of
E
without changing the value of
\sum_{i<N}2^{-|\sigma_{i}|}
. Any cylinder
N_{\sigma}
for which
2^{-|\sigma|}>\delta
can just be replaced with its two “halves”
N_{\sigma^{\frown}0}
and
N_{\sigma^{\frown}1}
, and the process can be repeated as many times as necessary until all cylinders have measure at most
\delta
.

On the other hand, if
s<1
, then different values of
\delta
can potentially yield different values of
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta}(E)
. However, note that if
\delta_{0}>\delta_{1}
, then the collection of
\delta_{1}
-covers for a set
E
will be a subset of the collection of
\delta_{0}
-covers, which implies that
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta_{0}}(E)\leq\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta_{1}}(E)
. Therefore, the limit in the definition below is (classically) well-defined, although it can potentially be infinite.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
E\subseteq 2^{\omega}
, and let
s
be a nonnegative number. Then the
s-dimensional Hausdorff measure

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)
is defined by

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta}(E).

The fact that the definition of Hausdorff measure involves checking infinitely many collections of open sets makes it somewhat nonconstructive and difficult to work with in a reverse math context. However, we can define a more explicit measure function in the case where
E
is closed, which will also correspond nicely to a more general discussion of “monotone functions” in Section 3.2. As we show below, this function will be equivalent to the usual definition of Hausdorff measure as long as we are in a sufficiently strong axiom system.

First, we introduce some preliminary notation.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
V\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
, and let
s\geq 0
. Define the
s
-weight
of
V
by

W_{s}(V)=\sum_{\sigma\in V}2^{-s|\sigma|}

with
W_{s}(\emptyset)
defined to be 0.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
S\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
denote the set
S_{T}
from Definition
2.8
, where
T
is taken to be all of
2^{<\omega}
. Suppose
s\geq 0
and
n\in\omega
. We define a function
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}:S\to\mathbb{R}
as follows.

Given
\nu\in S
of length at least 1, we first find the largest integer
m
for which
|\nu|\geq 2^{m+1}-1
, i.e. for which
\nu^{\leq m}
is defined. (We handle the case where
\nu
is the empty string
\langle\rangle
separately). We then proceed in cases depending on the value of
m
.

•

Case 1:
If
m\geq n
, we define

\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu)=\min\{W_{s}(V)\},

where the minimum is taken over all
V\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
with the following properties:

(1)

For all
\sigma\in V
,
n\leq|\sigma|\leq m
.

(2)

For all
\sigma
such that
|\sigma|=m
and
\nu(\sigma)=1
, there is some
\tau\in V
with
\tau\subseteq\sigma
.

•

Case 2:
If
m<n
, or if
\nu=\langle\rangle
, we define

\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu)=2^{n}\cdot 2^{-sn}=2^{(1-s)n}.

Using this function
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}
, we can then define a function
H^{s}_{n}:[S]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

H^{s}_{n}(Z)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Z\upharpoonright_{k}).

Observe that if
\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\in S
and
\nu_{0}\subseteq\nu_{1}
, then
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu_{0})\geq\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu_{1})
(which is relevant to the existence of the above limit). For one, the value of
m
for
\nu_{0}
will be at most that of the value for
\nu_{1}
. If both strings fall under Case 1 of the definition, then whichever set
V_{0}
realizes the minimum
W_{s}(V)
for
\nu_{0}
will still be considered among the sets
V
for
\nu_{1}
, due to the “closure under initial segments” property for the strings in
S
. Therefore, the minimum in the definition for
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu_{1})
cannot increase from its value for
\nu_{0}
.

Similarly, the set
V
which consists of all length-
n
strings in
2^{<\omega}
will always be under consideration in the Case 1 definition, and
W_{s}(V)
for this
V
is precisely
2^{(1-s)n}
. Thus, the desired monotonicity still holds in the case where
\nu_{0}
falls under Case 2 while
\nu_{1}
falls under Case 1. If both strings fall under Case 2, then the claim is obvious.

Under classical, non-restricted mathematics, the function
H^{s}_{n}
is well-defined and is precisely the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff
2^{-n}
-measure. However, given the properties of compactness and convergence necessary for the proof to proceed, we must be a bit careful in weak subsystems of second order arithmetic. Thus, we break the proof into stages.

Proposition 2.6
.

The following is provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
: Let
E\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be a standard closed set, let
Z\in[S]
be a code for its tree, let
s\geq 0
, and let
n\in\omega
. Then for any initial segment
\nu\subseteq Z
, there is some
2^{-n}
-cover
\{N_{\sigma_{i}}\}_{i<N}
of
E
for which

\sum_{i<N}2^{-s|\sigma_{i}|}\leq\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu).

This implies

\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}(E)\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z),

assuming that both quantities exist.

Proof.

Given the monotonicity of the function
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}
, it suffices to only consider
\nu\subseteq Z
which have the form
Z^{\leq m}
for some
m\geq n
. For such a string
\nu
, let
V\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
be the set realizing the minimum
s
-weight, according to the definition of
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}
, and consider the cover
\{N_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma\in V}.
Since all strings in
V
have length at least
n
by condition a., this generates a
2^{-n}
-cover. (Note also that this cover must exist in the model since it can be specified by only finitely many strings). Additionally, for any
X\in E
, all initial segments
\sigma
must satisfy
Z(\sigma)=1
, and thus
\nu(\sigma)=1
as long as
\nu
is defined there. If we consider the initial segment
\sigma=X\upharpoonright_{m}
, then condition b. of the definition of
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}
guarantees that
\sigma
has some initial segment
\tau\in V
, which implies that
X
is an element of the corresponding cylinder
N_{\tau}
in our cover.

Thus,
\{N_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma\in V}
is indeed a
2^{-n}
-cover for
E
, and we have

\sum_{\sigma\in V}2^{-s|\sigma|}=W_{s}(V)=\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu),

as desired. This implies

\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}(E)\leq\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu),

(assuming the left-hand quantity exists), and thus

\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}(E)\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z),

since the initial segment
\nu
of
Z
was arbitrary.

∎

Interestingly, if we reverse the roles of
H^{s}_{n}
and
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}
in the above argument, we obtain a much stronger result, reverse mathematically.

Proposition 2.7
.

The following is equivalent to
\text{WKL}_{0}
: Let
E\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be a standard closed set, let
Z\in[S]
be a code for its tree, let
s\geq 0
, and let
n\in\omega
. Then for any
2^{-n}
-cover
\{N_{\sigma_{i}}\}_{i<N}
of
E
, there exists some initial segment
\nu\subseteq Z
such that

\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu)\leq\sum_{i<N}2^{-s|\sigma_{i}|}.

Proof.

We begin by showing that the desired statement is provable in
\text{WKL}_{0}.

Since
E
is a closed subset of the compact space
2^{\omega}
,
E
itself is compact. Since we have access to
\text{WKL}_{0}
, for any given
2^{-n}
-cover
\{N_{\sigma_{i}}\}_{i<N}
, we may apply the Heine-Borel Theorem (see Theorem
2.1
) to take a finite subcover
N_{\sigma_{0}},\dots,N_{\sigma_{N}}
for

. Let

denote the open set
U=\bigcup_{i\leq N}N_{\sigma_{i}}
, which by assumption contains

as a subset.

For each
k\in\omega
, define
E_{k}\subseteq E
to be the clopen set consisting of all cylinders

such that
Z(\tau)=1
(i.e.
\tau
is in the tree for
E
) and
\tau
has length
k
. It is clear that the sets
E_{k}
are nested decreasing (by the initial segment closure property of trees), and that

E_{k}
for which
E_{k}\subseteq U
.

Indeed, for each
k\in\omega
we can define the set
C_{k}=E_{k}\setminus U
, which is compact as the intersection of two compact sets. Formally, the set
C_{k}
has a tree consisting of all strings
\tau
which are compatible with some length-

string in the tree for
E
, but which are not compatible with any string
\sigma_{i}
represented in
U
.
Additionally, the sets
C_{k}
are nested decreasing, as they inherit this property from the sets
E_{k}
, and we have
\bigcap_{k\in\omega}C_{k}=E\setminus U=\emptyset
.

Again, using compactness properties which are provable in
\text{WKL}_{0}
, we can conclude that some

must be empty, implying that
E_{k}\subseteq U
. In fact, all

are empty for sufficiently large

since the sets are nested.

So choose such a
k
for which
E_{k}\subseteq U
, and assume that
k
is longer than the maximum length of the strings
\sigma_{0},\dots,\sigma_{N}
corresponding to the cover for
U
. Then we see that the set
V=\{\sigma_{0},\dots,\sigma_{N}\}
will be considered in the definition for
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Z^{\leq k}).
Since the original collection
\{N_{\sigma_{i}}\}_{i<N}
was a
2^{-n}
-cover, it follows that all
\sigma_{i}\in V
have length at least
n
, meeting condition a. Additionally, the strings
\sigma
considered in condition b. of the definition are precisely the strings whose associated cylinder sets comprise
E_{k}
. As
E_{k}\subseteq U=\bigcup_{i\leq N}N_{\sigma_{i}}
, any infinite sequence in the model extending one of these
\sigma
(which certainly exist, since we can, for instance, just extend
\sigma
with an infinite string of 0’s) must have an initial segment among
V
. This initial segment must also then be a prefix of
\sigma
since we assumed that all strings in
V
had length less than
k
.

Therefore, since
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Z^{\leq k})
computes the minimum
s
-weight over all valid sets of strings, we must have

\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Z^{\leq k})\leq W_{s}(V)=\sum_{i\leq N}2^{-s|\sigma_{i}|}\leq\sum_{i<N}2^{-s|\sigma_{i}|},

as desired. This completes the proof that
\text{WKL}_{0}
implies the desired statement.

For the opposite implication, assume that the statement holds, and let
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
be an infinite tree with associated code
Z\in 2^{\omega}
We wish to show that
E=[T]
contains an infinite path.

If this were not the case, then the empty set
\emptyset
would be a valid
2^{-n}
-cover for the set
E
, whose associated “weight” (that is, the sum of all quantities
2^{-s|\sigma|}
for
\sigma
in the cover) would be zero.

However, if
T
is infinite, then for all initial segments
Z^{\leq k}
for
k\geq n
, the quantity
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Z^{\leq k})
must be positive. This is certainly the case if
Z^{\leq k}
falls under case (2) of the definition. If instead
Z^{\leq k}
is sufficiently long to fall under case (1), then since there will always be at least one
\sigma\in T
of length
k
, all covering sets
V
under consideration will be nonempty. All these sets
V
will then have positive values of
\text{W}_{s}(V)
, and therefore the minimum weight computed by
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Z^{\leq k})
is positive too. We can then conclude the same about all values of
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu)
be monotonicity.

This contradicts the desired statement when applied to the empty cover, which means
E=[T]
must be nonempty, proving Weak König’s Lemma.
∎

From the above results, we can conclude that as long as we are working in a system at least as strong as
\text{WKL}_{0}
, our two notions of Hausdorff
2^{-n}
-measure agree.

Corollary 2.8
.

The following is provable in
\text{WKL}_{0}
: Let
E\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be a standard closed set, let
Z\in[S]
be a code for its tree, let
s\geq 0
, and let
n\in\omega
. Then

H_{n}^{s}(Z)=\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}(E),

assuming both quantities exist.

Proof.

From the previous result, we can deduce that for any
2^{-n}
-cover
\{N_{\sigma_{i}}\}_{i<N}
of
E
, we have

H_{n}^{s}(Z)\leq\sum_{i<N}2^{-s|\sigma_{i}|}.

As the cover was arbitrary, we must have

H_{n}^{s}(Z)\leq\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}(E),

assuming both quantities exist.

Combining this inequality with
2.6
, we obtain

H^{s}_{n}(Z)=\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}(E)

over
\text{WKL}_{0}
, as claimed.
∎

By this corollary, in
\text{WKL}_{0}
and stronger systems, we must also have

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}(E)=\lim_{n\to\infty}H^{s}_{n}(Z),

assuming that all relevant quantities exist. However, if we are in a situation where the distinction matters, we will use the notation
H^{s}(Z)
to refer to the quantity

\lim_{n\to\infty}H^{s}_{n}(Z).

3.
The Baire Category Theorem

As discussed in the introduction, the motivation behind this paper was to analyze the reverse math strength of the Besicovitch Theorem in Cantor space, which initiated an investigation into general “measure regularity” results for closed sets.
It turns out that by using an appropriate topology on the space of closed sets (as represented by trees or pruned trees), one can view the construction of these subsets as a type of Baire Category Argument. Before we see how this theorem can be applied, we wish to analyze the reverse mathematics strength of several Baire Category variants.

3.1.
Baire Category Theorem Formulations

The Baire Category Theorem of classical analysis already has a few equivalent forms, but one standard way it is often presented is as follows:

“In any complete separable metric space, the intersection of countably many dense open sets is dense.”

Of course, if we have a closed subset of a complete separable metric space, then the induced subspace is itself a complete separable metric space. Therefore, if we have a countable sequence of dense open sets in this subspace topology, the Baire Category Theorem applies. By using the definition of the subspace topology, we can formulate this corollary of the Baire Category theorem in the following way:

Theorem 3.1

(Baire Category Theorem for Closed Sets, or BCTC)
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space, and let
F\subseteq X
be a nonempty closed set. Suppose
\{U_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
is a sequence of nonempty open sets in
X
such that each
U_{n}
has the following property: if
N\subseteq X
is a basic open set such that
N\cap F\neq\emptyset
, then there exists some
x\in N\cap F\cap U_{n}
.
Then
\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}
also has this property: that is, if
N
is a basic open set such that
N\cap F\neq\emptyset
, then there exists
x\in N\cap F\cap\left(\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}\right)
.

It turns out that the reverse mathematics strength of the theorem above depends on how the open and closed sets are coded. As discussed in the introduction, other mathematicians have already studied the effect of varying the representation of the sequence of open sets, so we focus on the representation of the closed set
F
.

We restrict our attention to Cantor space. When we speak of an open set in
2^{\omega}
, we assume that it is coded in the manner outlined in Section 2.2: i.e. as a subset of
2^{<\omega}
which is closed upward under taking extensions. We also introduce some terminology to make our theorems more concise.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be a nonempty closed set, and let
A\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be any set. We say that
A
is
dense in

F
if for every string
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
such that
N_{\sigma}\cap F\neq\emptyset
, there exists some
X\in N_{\sigma}\cap F\cap A
.

Proposition 3.2

(BCTCI)
.

Let
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be a standard closed set. Suppose
\{U_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
is a sequence of nonempty open sets in
2^{\omega}
such that each
U_{n}
is dense in
F
. Then
\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}
is dense in
F
.

Proposition 3.3

(BCTCII)
.

Let
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be a separably closed set. Suppose
\{U_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
is a sequence of nonempty open sets in
2^{\omega}
such that each
U_{n}
is dense in
F
. Then
\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}
is dense in
F
.

Proposition 3.4

(BCTCIII)
.

Let
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be a pruned closed set. Suppose
\{U_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
is a sequence of nonempty open sets in
2^{\omega}
such that each
U_{n}
is dense in
F
. Then
\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}
is dense in
F
.

We first consider the second and third formulations, since these are the versions which require the least axiomatic power to prove.

Proposition 3.5
.

The theorems BCTCII and BCTCIII are provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
.

Proof.

We will actually begin with BCTCIII, since this is the argument which most closely resembles the classical proof of the Baire Category Theorem. Since
F
is pruned closed, let
\tilde{T}\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
denote the pruned tree which represents
F
. Additionally, let
\{V_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
be the sequence of subsets of
2^{<\omega}
whose members code the respective open sets of
\{U_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
.

Suppose
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
is such that
N_{\sigma}\cap F\neq\emptyset
. Note that this is equivalent to
\sigma\in\tilde{T}
, since
\tilde{T}
is pruned. We wish to construct an element
X\in N_{\sigma}\cap F\cap\left(\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}\right)
. We do this by defining a procedure for recursively constructing an increasing sequence of strings
\sigma_{0}\subsetneq\sigma_{1}\subsetneq\sigma_{2}\subsetneq\dots
with the following properties:

(1)

\sigma_{0}\supseteq\sigma

(2)

\sigma_{n}\in V_{n}
for each
n\in\omega

(3)

\sigma_{n}\in\tilde{T}
for each
n\in\omega

If we set
X\in 2^{\omega}
to be the unique limit of the sequence
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
, then it is clear from properties (1) and (2) that
X\in N_{\sigma}\cap\left(\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}\right).
The fact that
X\in F
follows from
X
having arbitrarily long initial segments in
\tilde{T}
(by property (3)), and from
\tilde{T}
being a tree.

So let
n\in\omega
, and suppose inductively that
\sigma_{n-1}
has already been defined. In the case of
n=0
, we consider
\sigma_{-1}
to be our given string
\sigma
. Start enumerating the strings in
2^{<\omega}
which properly extend
\sigma_{n-1}
, and define
\sigma_{n}
to be the first string found which is an element of both
V_{n}
and
\tilde{T}
.

We know this must happen eventually by the density of
U_{n}
in
F
. Since
\sigma_{n-1}\in\tilde{T}
by our inductive assumption, we know that
N_{\sigma_{n-1}}\cap F
is nonempty. Therefore, there must exist some element
Y\in U_{n}\cap F
which extends
\sigma_{n-1}.
We know all initial segments of
Y
will be in
\tilde{T}
, and all sufficiently long initial segments will be in
V_{n}
. Thus, if we search through the proper extensions of
\sigma_{n-1}
, we will eventually find a sufficiently long initial segment of
Y
lying in both
\tilde{T}
and
V_{n}
. This completes the argument for BCTCIII.

For BCTCII, we use the same notation for representing the open sets, but now we have a countable sequence
S=\langle Z_{k}\rangle_{k\in\omega}
representing the separably closed set
F
. However, the argument proceeds in a similar way.

First, we observe that for a string
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
, we have:

N_{\sigma}\cap F\iff\exists k\in\omega\>Z_{k}\supseteq\sigma.

As with BCTCIII, we will construct our desired
X\in 2^{\omega}
as the limit of an increasing sequence
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
whose elements satisfy three properties. Properties (1) and (2) will be the same as in the previous proof, while the new property (3) will be that for all
n\in\omega
, there exists some
k\in\omega
such that
Z_{k}\supseteq\sigma_{n}
.

Note that since our final sequence
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
will be strictly increasing, for any
m\in\omega
we can find an element
Z_{k}
which agrees with our limit element
X\in 2^{\omega}
up to position
m
. Thus,
X\in F=\bar{S}
.

To inductively construct the element
\sigma_{n}
, we fix an enumeration of all pairs
(\tau,k)
such that
\tau\supsetneq\sigma_{n-1}
and
k\in\omega
. For each pair, we check if
\tau\in V_{n}
and
\tau\subseteq Z_{k}
, and once we find such a pair we set
\sigma_{n}=\tau
. Again, we know such a pair must be encountered eventually because of density. Our inductive assumption on
\sigma_{n-1}
means that
N_{\sigma_{n-1}}\cap F
is nonempty, so there is some
Y\in N_{\sigma_{n-1}}\cap U_{n}\cap F
. Thus, we just need to consider an initial segment
\tau\subseteq Y
sufficiently long for both
\tau\supsetneq\sigma_{n-1}
and
\tau\in V_{n}
, and then consider
k\in\omega
such that
Z_{k}
agrees with
Y
up through the length of
\tau
.
∎

We finally turn to the complexity of BCTCI, which we will henceforth refer to as just BCTC. One might start by attempting to carry out a proof in
\text{RCA}_{0}
by following the structure of the argument for BCTCIII. However, the fact that the tree
T
representing
F
is not necessarily pruned causes issues with the inductive construction. Naturally, each
\sigma_{n}
in that construction needs to have infinitely many extensions in the tree in order for a limiting element
X
to be well-defined, yet we cannot determine in a computable way whether a given string
\sigma
in
T
has that property.

Of course, if we have access to
\text{ACA}_{0}
we can just replace the tree
T
with a pruned subtree representing the same closed set
F
, and the BCTCIII proof goes through. But considering that other versions of the Baire Category theorem are provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
, one might conjecture that BCTC is still relatively “simple” from a reverse math perspective, even if its power does reach beyond
\text{RCA}_{0}
. However, it turns out that
\text{ACA}_{0}
is in fact necessary, as the next result shows. However, first we introduce some notation.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\langle\rangle:\omega^{2}\to\omega
denote a fixed pairing function. Assume that
\langle\rangle
has the property that for all
n,m\in\omega
:

•

\langle n,m\rangle<\langle n+1,m\rangle

•

\langle n,m\rangle<\langle n,m+1\rangle

For
X\in 2^{\omega}
and
n\in\omega
, let
\langle X\rangle_{n}\in 2^{\omega}
be defined by

\langle X\rangle_{n}(m)=X(\langle n,m\rangle),

for all
m\in\omega
. We call
\langle X\rangle_{n}
the
n
-th column
of
X
.
We can generalize this definition to finite strings
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
. Here, the
n
-th column
\langle\sigma\rangle_{n}
will be another finite string (possibly empty), since for a fixed
n\in\omega
,
\sigma(\langle n,m\rangle)
will be undefined for all but finitely many
m\in\omega
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, BCTC is equivalent to
\text{ACA}_{0}.

Proof.

To show that
\text{ACA}_{0}
implies BCTC, let
T
be a tree representing the closed set
F
from the statement of BCTC. Under
\text{ACA}_{0}
, we can find a pruned tree
\tilde{T}\subseteq T
such that
[\tilde{T}]=[T]=F
(see Theorem
2.5
). Using
\tilde{T}
, we can then carry out the proof of BCTCIII, which does not require any axioms beyond
\text{RCA}_{0}
.

For the opposite implication, we will use BCTC to show that every one-to-one function has a range. Let
f:\omega\to\omega
be a one-to-one function, and consider the
f
-computable tree
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
defined as follows.
For
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
, we set
\sigma\in T
if and only if for all
n\in\omega
such that
\langle\sigma\rangle_{n}
is nonempty,

\langle\sigma\rangle_{n}\text{ contains a }0\implies\text{there exists no }k<|\sigma|\text{ such that }f(k)=n.

We first observe that this does indeed define a tree. If
\sigma\in T
and
\tau\subseteq\sigma
, then the properties of the pairing function guarantee that
\langle\tau\rangle_{n}\subseteq\langle\sigma\rangle_{n}
for all
n\in\omega
. If
n
is such that
\langle\tau\rangle_{n}
contains a 0, then so will
\langle\sigma\rangle_{n}
, which if
\sigma\in T
means that there is no
k<|\sigma|
with
f(k)=n.
Thus, we will not find a witnessing
k<|\tau|
either, which means
\tau\in T
since this applies to all
n
.

Let
F=[T]
denote the closed set corresponding to
T
. Note that
F
is nonempty, since it (for example) contains the infinite sequence consisting entirely of 1’s.

Claim 1:
If
X\in F
, then any
n\in\omega
for which
\langle X\rangle_{n}
contains a 0 is not in the range of
f
.

Proof of Claim 1:
Suppose there did exist some
k\in\omega
for which
f(k)=n
. If
\langle X\rangle_{n}
contains a 0 (say at position
m
) we can choose
\tau\subseteq X
to be a finite initial segment long enough for
|\tau|>k
and for
\langle\tau\rangle_{n}(m)=\langle X\rangle_{n}(m)=0
to be defined. By the definition of
T
,
\tau
cannot be an element of this tree, but this contradicts the fact that
X
was in
[T]=F
.
\square

Next, we define a sequence of subsets
\{V_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
of
2^{<\omega}
, which will code a sequence of open sets
\{U_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
in
2^{\omega}
. For each
n\in\omega
, we set
\sigma\in V_{n}
if and only if at least one of the following conditions holds:

(1)

There exists
k<|\sigma|
with
f(k)=n
.

(2)

\langle\sigma\rangle_{n}
contains a 0.

Note that each
V_{n}
is closed upward under taking extensions.

Claim 2:
For every
n\in\omega
, the open set
U_{n}
is dense in
F
.

Proof of Claim 2:
Let
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
be such that
N_{\sigma}\cap F
is nonempty, and let
X
be an element of
N_{\sigma}\cap F
. We wish to show that
N_{\sigma}\cap F
contains an element of
U_{n}
.

Suppose first that
n\in\text{ran}(f)
. If
k\in\omega
is such that
f(k)=n
, and
\tau\subseteq X
is a finite initial segment with length
|\tau|>k
, then we see that
\tau\in V_{n}
by condition (1). Thus, the sequence
X
itself is already an element of
U_{n}
, and we are done.

On the other hand, suppose
n\notin\text{ran}(f)
. Let
m\in\omega
be the least index for which
\langle\sigma\rangle_{n}(m)
is undefined. Define a sequence
Y\in 2^{\omega}
by setting
\langle Y\rangle_{n}(m)=Y(\langle n,m\rangle)=0
, and by setting all other entries of
Y
to agree with the respective entries of
X
. (Note that if
\langle X\rangle_{n}(m)
was already equal to 0, then we just have
Y=X
). Observe that this sequence
Y
must exist in the model, since it is computable from the element
X
.

We first note that
Y
is an element of
N_{\sigma}
, since we didn’t change any entries of
X
which agreed with the defined entries of
\sigma
. We also observe that
Y
is in
U_{n}
, since we can take an initial segment
\tau\subseteq Y
sufficiently long that
\langle\tau\rangle_{n}(m)=\langle Y\rangle_{n}(m)=0
, putting
\tau\in V_{n}
by condition (2).

Finally, we observe that
Y
is an element of
F
. For suppose
\tau\subseteq Y
is a finite initial segment, and
i\in\omega
is such that
\langle\tau\rangle_{i}
is nonempty. If
i\neq n
, then
\langle\tau\rangle_{i}=\langle\tau^{\prime}\rangle_{i}
, where
\tau^{\prime}
is the initial segment of
X
with the same length as
\tau
. As
X
was an element of
F
, we know that
\tau^{\prime}\in T
, and thus
\langle\tau^{\prime}\rangle_{i}=\langle\tau\rangle_{i}
containing a 0 means that there is no
k<|\tau^{\prime}|=|\tau|
with
f(k)=i
.

For
i=n
, we know that there is no
k<|\tau|
with
f(k)=n
since we assumed that
n\notin\text{ran}(f).
This means that regardless of the entries of
\langle\tau\rangle_{n}
,
\tau
meets the conditions for being in
T
, so
Y\in[T]=F
as desired.
\square

Having shown that the relevant hypotheses hold, we can now apply BCTC to deduce that
\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}
is dense in
F
. In particular, there must exist an element
Z\in F\cap\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}
, since
F
is nonempty. We claim that
\text{ran}(f)
is computable from
Z
and
f
.

Indeed, consider the algorithm that, on input
n
, searches through the initial segments of
Z
until a
\sigma
is found such that either (1) there exists
k<|\sigma|
with
f(k)=n
, or (2)
\langle\sigma\rangle_{n}
contains a 0. Since we know
Z
is an element of
U_{n}
, such an initial segment must be located eventually, as these are just the conditions for entering the coding set
V_{n}
. If the string
\sigma
meets the first condition, then clearly
n\in\text{ran}(f)
. If
\sigma
meets the second condition, then we know that
\langle Z\rangle_{n}
contains a 0, which means
n\notin\text{ran}(f)
since
Z\in F
. (See the previous Claim 1.) Since our algorithm can distinguish between these two conditions, we can determine whether
n
is in
\text{ran}(f)
, as desired. (Formally, one could argue that
\text{ran}(f)
exists by
\Delta^{0}_{1}
-comprehension with parameters
f
and
Z
, similar to the proof of Theorem
2.5
).

∎

3.2.
Monotone Functions on Closed Sets

In this section, we introduce a new context in which we can consider BCTC and its reverse mathematical strength. This context involves the behavior of certain “monotone” functions defined on closed sets.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
be a tree, and let
\tilde{f}:T\to\mathbb{R}
be a function.
We say that
\tilde{f}
is
monotone decreasing
if
\tilde{f}(\sigma)\geq\tilde{f}(\tau)
whenever
\sigma\subseteq\tau
. Similarly, we say that
\tilde{f}
is
monotone increasing
if
\tilde{f}(\sigma)\leq\tilde{f}(\tau)
whenever
\sigma\subseteq\tau
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
be a tree, and let
F=[T]
be the corresponding closed set. If
\tilde{f}:T\to\mathbb{R}
is a function, we define the partial function
f:F\rightharpoonup\mathbb{R}
by

f(X)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f(X\upharpoonright_{n}),

if the quantity on the right exists.

Observe that if
\tilde{f}
is a monotone increasing function which is bounded above, or a monotone decreasing function which is bounded below, then the limit in the definition above always exists “classically.” However, this limiting value may not exist in the model for all
X\in F
if we are working in a system strictly weaker than
\text{ACA}_{0}
.

That said, we will sometimes abuse notation in the following way. If
\tilde{f}
is a monotone decreasing function on a tree
T
,
X
is an element of
[T]
, and
c
is a real number, then we may write
f(X)\geq c
to mean that
\tilde{f}(X\upharpoonright_{n})\geq c
for all
n\in\omega
, even if the limiting value for
f(X)
doesn’t exist in the model. Similarly, writing
f(X)<c
is understood to mean that there exists some
n\in\omega
for which
\tilde{f}(X\upharpoonright_{n})<c
. We use similar notation with the opposite inequalities for a monotone increasing function.

With this broadening of notation, the following definition is intuitive.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
be a tree with corresponding closed set
F=[T]
, let
\tilde{f}:T\to\mathbb{R}
be a monotone decreasing function, and let
f:F\rightharpoonup\mathbb{R}
be the induced function. We say that a real number
\alpha
is an
infimum
of
f
over
F
if the following conditions hold:

(1)

For all
X\in F
,
f(X)\geq\alpha
.

(2)

For all
\epsilon>0
, there exists some
X\in F
such that
f(X)<\alpha+\epsilon
.

The notion of
supremum
for a monotone increasing function is defined similarly.

It turns out that the realization of infimums (or supremums) of certain types of functions gives equivalent characterizations of
\text{ACA}_{0}
, which can be realized by considering their similarities to BCTC (recall that the equivalence of these statements was proven in the last section).

First, suppose that we have a tree
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
with corresponding closed set
F=[T]
and a function
\tilde{f}:T\to\mathbb{R}
which is monotone decreasing. Let
f:F\rightharpoonup\mathbb{R}
be the induced function, and suppose there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\alpha
is an infimum of
f
over
F.
Under what conditions is there an element
X\in F
for which
f(X)=\alpha
? (Of course, we could also ask the symmetric question involving a monotone increasing function and a supremum.)

We observe that the conditions mentioned above are not sufficient to guarantee that the infimum is realized, in any axiom system.

Proposition 3.7
.

There exists a tree
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
, a monotone decreasing function
\tilde{f}:T\to\mathbb{R}
, and a real number
\alpha
such that
\alpha
is an infimum of the induced function
f
over
F=[T]
, but there is no
X\in F
for which
f(X)=\alpha
.

Proof.

Let
T
be all of
2^{<\omega}
(meaning that
F
is all of
2^{\omega}
). We define a monotone decreasing function
\tilde{f}:2^{<\omega}\to\mathbb{R}
by setting
\tilde{f}(\sigma)=1
if
\sigma
consists entirely of 0’s (or if
\sigma
is the empty string), and otherwise setting
\tilde{f}(\sigma)=2^{-k}
, where
k
is the position of the first 1 in
\sigma
.

It is clear that
\tilde{f}
is monotone decreasing. We also see that
0
is an infimum of the induced function
f
over
2^{\omega}
, yet there is no
X\in 2^{\omega}
for which
f(X)=0
. If
X
is the sequence consisting entirely of 0’s, then
f(X)=1
. Otherwise,
f(X)=2^{-k}>0
, where
k
is the position of the first 1 in
X
.

∎

However, there are more restricted types of monotone decreasing functions that do realize their infimums. To describe them, we introduce some terminology. For brevity, we assume going forward that
T,F,\tilde{f},f,
and
\alpha
are defined as in Definition
3.5
.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
X
be an element of
[T]=F
. We say that
X
is a
limit point for

\alpha
if for every
\sigma\subseteq X
and every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
Y\in F
such that
\sigma\subseteq Y
and
f(Y)<\alpha+\epsilon
.

In other words, if
X
is a limit point, then we can find points which are arbitrarily close to
Y
(in the usual metric on
2^{\omega}
) which get mapped arbitrarily close to
\alpha
.

The following definitions describe two “extreme” properties that a function can have regarding limit points.

Definition 3.7
.

We say that
f
is
dense above

\alpha
if every
X\in F
is a limit point for
\alpha
.

Definition 3.8
.

We say that
f
is
separated from

\alpha
if the only limit points above
\alpha
are those points
X\in F
for which
f(X)=\alpha
(if they exist).

Despite describing very different types of functions, both of these extreme behaviors allow the infimum
\alpha
to be realized “classically.” These principles are summarized in the following principles.

Proposition 3.8

(Dense Monotone Minimum (DMMin))
.

If
f
is dense above
\alpha
, then there exists
X\in F
for which
f(X)=\alpha
.

Proposition 3.9

(Separated Monotone Minimum (SMMin))
.

If
f
is separated from
\alpha
, then there exists
X\in F
for which
f(X)=\alpha
.

Given the “density” properties displayed by the notion of limit point, it is not terribly surprising that the Dense Monotone Minimum principle is related to BCTC. In fact, the two statements are equivalent, which, by the result in the previous section, implies DMMin is equivalent to
\text{ACA}_{0}
. We can also demonstrate an equivalence directly between
\text{ACA}_{0}
and SMMin, which gives the following four-fold equivalence theorem:

Theorem 3.10
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\text{ACA}_{0}

(2)

BCTC

(3)

SMMin

(4)

DMMin

Proof.

We have already shown the equivalence of (i) and (ii) in Theorem
3.6
. We will complete the chain by proving the equivalences
\text{(ii)}\iff\text{(iv)}
and
\text{(i)}\iff\text{(iii)}
.

Claim 1
:
(ii)\implies(iv)
, i.e.
BCTC
implies
DMMin
.

Proof of Claim 1:
Let
T,F,\tilde{f},f,\alpha
be as in the statement of Dense Monotone Minimum.
For each
n\in\omega
, define the set

V_{n}=\{\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}:\sigma\notin T\text{ or }\tilde{f}(\sigma)<\alpha+2^{-n}\}.

Since
T
is a tree and
\tilde{f}
is monotone decreasing, each set
V_{n}
is closed under taking extensions, so corresponds to an open set
U_{n}\subseteq 2^{\omega}.
Furthermore, each set
U_{n}
is dense in
F
. Indeed, suppose
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
is such that
N_{\sigma}\cap F\neq\emptyset
, and let
X
be an element of
F
extending
\sigma
. Since
f
is dense above
\alpha
, we know that
X
is a limit point for
\alpha
. Therefore, there must be some
Y\in F
for which
\sigma\subseteq Y
and
f(Y)<\alpha+2^{-n}
. The latter statement then implies that there is some initial segment
\tau\subseteq Y
for which
\tilde{f}(\tau)<\alpha+2^{-n}
, and thus
Y\in U_{n}
.
We can now apply BCTC to conclude that
\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}
is dense in
F
. In particular, this means that the intersection
\left(\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}\right)\cap F
is nonempty (as the existence of a finite infimum implies that
F
itself is nonempty). Taking
X_{0}
to be an element of this intersection, we observe that we must have

f(X_{0})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{f}(X_{0}\upharpoonright_{n})=\alpha,

since
X_{0}
is in
F=[T]
yet has initial segments in every set
V_{n}
. This completes the proof of Claim 1.
\square

Claim 2:

(iv)\implies(ii)
, i.e. DMMin implies BCTC.

Proof of Claim 2:
Assume that
F
is a standard closed set, and
\{U_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
is a sequence of open sets in
2^{\omega}
such that each
U_{n}
is dense in
F
. We wish to show that the intersection
\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}
is dense in
F
. So let
\nu\in 2^{<\omega}
be such that
N_{\nu}\cap F
is nonempty. Let
F^{\prime}
denote the closed set
N_{\nu}\cap F
, and let
T^{\prime}
be a tree representing
F^{\prime}
. (Note that we can obtain this computably from our tree representing
F
just by taking all sequences which are compatible with
\nu
).
Additionally, let
V_{n}
denote the set of strings representing each open set
U_{n}
.

We define a monotone decreasing function
\tilde{f}:T^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
by setting
\tilde{f}(\sigma)=2^{-k-1}
, where
k
is the largest index with
0\leq k<|\sigma|
such that
\sigma\in V_{i}
for all
i
with
0\leq i\leq k
; if no such

exists, we set
\tilde{f}(\sigma)=1
. Given that the sets
V_{n}
are all closed under taking extensions, it is straightforward to check that
\tilde{f}
is monotone decreasing.

Additionally, we see that
0
is the infimum of the induced function
f
over
F^{\prime}
. One one hand, 0 is certainly a lower bound for
f
, since
\tilde{f}
only outputs positive values. To see that
0
is the greatest lower bound, let
\epsilon>0
be arbitrary, and let
n\in\omega
be such that
2^{-n-1}<\epsilon.

Since each of
U_{0},U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
is dense in the original set
F
, we can construct an element
X_{n}\in F^{\prime}\cap(\bigcap_{i=0}^{n}U_{i})
as follows. First, choose
X_{0}\in F^{\prime}\cap U_{0}
, and let
\tau_{0}\subseteq X_{0}
be sufficiently long that
\nu\subsetneq\tau_{0}
and
\tau_{0}\in V_{0}
. By density of
U_{1}
, we can then choose
X_{1}\in N_{\tau_{0}}\cap F\cap U_{1}
and select an initial segment
\tau_{1}\subseteq X_{1}
with
\tau_{0}\subsetneq\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{1}\in V_{1}
. We can continue inductively in this manner until we have obtained a sequence of strings

\nu\subsetneq\tau_{0}\subsetneq\tau_{1}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq\tau_{n},

and a corresponding sequence of element
X_{0},X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
with the property that each
\tau_{i}\in V_{i}
and each
X_{i}\in N_{\tau_{i}}\cap F.
Note also that since our sequence of
\tau_{i}
’s is strictly increasing in length, each
\tau_{i}
must have length greater than
i
.

For our final element
X_{n}\in F
, we observe that we must have
f(X_{n})\leq 2^{-n-1}<\epsilon
. Our inductive construction, and the fact that the sets
V_{i}
are closed under taking extensions, means that the initial segment
\tau_{n}\subseteq X_{n}
must be an element of each of
V_{0},V_{1},\dots,V_{n}
. Thus,
\tilde{f}(\tau_{n})\leq 2^{-n-1}
as desired.

Finally, we observe that every
X\in F^{\prime}
is a limit point over
0
, meaning that
f
is dense above 0. Again, this comes from the density of each set
U_{n}
in our original set
F
. Let
\sigma\subseteq X
and
\epsilon>0
be arbitrary. To construct an element
Y\in F^{\prime}
with
Y\supseteq\sigma
and
f(Y)<\epsilon
, we essentially follow the same procedure used in verifying that
0
was the infimum of
f
over
F^{\prime}
. We just start by choosing an
X_{0}
in
F\cap N_{\sigma}\cap N_{\nu}\cap U_{0}
, rather than one which is only in
F\cap N_{\nu}\cap U_{0}=F^{\prime}\cap U_{0}
.

At this point, we can use DMMin to conclude that there is some
Z\in F^{\prime}=N_{\nu}\cap F
for which
f(Z)=0
. We can then check that this
Z
must be in
\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}
. Indeed, for any
n\in\omega
, we can choose an initial segment
\tau\subseteq Z
for which
\tilde{f}(\tau)\leq 2^{-n-1}.
From the definition of
\tilde{f}
, we must have
\tau
in each of
V_{0},V_{1},\dots V_{n}
, meaning that
Z\in U_{0},U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
. This completes the proof of Claim 2, implying the full equivalence of statements (ii) and (iv).
\square

Claim 3:

(i)\implies(iii)
, i.e.
\text{ACA}_{0}
implies SMMin.

Proof of Claim 3:
Let
T,f,\tilde{f},f,
and
\alpha
be as in the statement of Separated Monotone Minimum. First, by Theorem
2.5
we may use
\text{ACA}_{0}
to obtain a pruned tree representation
\tilde{T}
of
F=[T]
. Observe that
F
must be nonempty since
f
has a finite infimum over
F
, and thus
\tilde{T}
is infinite.

We define a sequence
\langle X_{n}\rangle_{n\in\omega}
uniformly in

as follows. For each
n\in\omega
, we enumerate the elements of
\tilde{T}
with length at least
n
until we find a string
\sigma
for which
\tilde{f}(\sigma)<\alpha+2^{-n}.
By the definition of infimum, we know this must happen eventually. Indeed, there must be some
X\in F=[\tilde{T}]
and some initial segment
X\upharpoonright_{k}
for which
\tilde{f}(X\upharpoonright_{k})<\alpha+2^{-n}
, and since
\tilde{f}
is monotone decreasing, we may assume that this initial segment has length at least
n
.
Once this
\sigma
has been identified, we then define
X_{n}
to be the leftmost path through
\tilde{T}
above
\sigma
, which we can construct in a computable way since
\tilde{T}
is pruned.

At this point, we have constructed a sequence of elements in a compact subspace
F
of
2^{\omega}
. Using
\text{ACA}_{0}
again, we can find a subsequence
\langle X_{k_{i}}\rangle_{i\in\omega}
which converges to some

(see Theorem
2.3
). We claim that this

satisfies
f(Y)=\alpha
.

For suppose not; then since
f
is separated from
\alpha
, we could conclude that
Y
was not a limit point for
\alpha
. Thus, there would be some initial segment
\tau\subseteq Y
and some

such that all
X\in F
which extend
\tau
would yield

. But now we could choose an element
X_{k_{i}}
sufficiently far along in our subsequence such that
X_{k_{i}}
agrees with

\tau
, yet the index
n
of
X_{k_{i}}

. This last would imply that

from the construction of

.

Therefore, we must have

, completing the proof of Claim 3.
\square

Claim 4:

(iii)\implies(i)
, i.e. SMMin implies
\text{ACA}_{0}
.

Proof of Claim 4:
As in previous proofs, we will use SMMin to prove that every one-to-one function has a range. The setup used is somewhat similar to the proof that BCTC implies
\text{ACA}_{0}
, as seen in Theorem
3.6
.

Let
g:\omega\to\omega
be an arbitrary one-to-one function. Define a tree
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
by setting
\sigma\in T
if and only if for all
n<|\sigma|
,

\sigma(n)=0\implies\text{there exists no }k<|\sigma|\text{ with }g(k)=n.

It is straightforward to verify that
T
is a tree; let
F=[T]
denote the corresponding closed set. We also observe that for any
X\in 2^{\omega},
we have

X\in F\iff\forall n\in\omega\>(X(n)=0\rightarrow n\notin\text{ran}(g)).

The proof of this correspondence is also straightforward and is similar to the argument used for the analogous statement from the proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Now, we define a function
\tilde{f}:T\to\mathbb{R}
as follows. First, for any
\sigma\in T
we use the notation
A[\sigma]
to denote the set of all
n<|\sigma|
for which either
\sigma(n)=0
, or
\sigma(n)=1
and there exists
k<|\sigma|
with
g(k)=n
. Note that
A[\sigma]
is computable from
\sigma
. We then set

\tilde{f}(\sigma)=1-\left(\sum_{n\in A[\sigma]}2^{-n-1}\right).

Note that
\tilde{f}
is monotone decreasing, which follows from the easy observation that
\sigma\subseteq\tau
implies
A[\sigma]\subseteq A[\tau].
We also claim that
\tilde{f}
has an infimum of 0. As
\tilde{f}
can only output positive values (the sum being subtracted is always less than 1), 0 is certainly a lower bound for
\tilde{f}
. To see that 0 is the greatest lower bound, let
\epsilon>0
be arbitrary, and choose
n\in\omega
so large that

2^{-n}=1-\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}2^{-i-1}\right)<\epsilon.

Let
\sigma
be the length-
n
string such that for all
0\leq k<n
, we have
\sigma(k)=1
if
k\in\text{ran}(g)
and
\sigma(k)=0
otherwise. Then define a string
\tau\supseteq\sigma
by extending
\sigma
with a sufficiently long string of 1’s such that any
k<n
for which
\sigma(k)=1
has a witness
m<|\tau|
with
g(m)=k
. Observe that for this
\tau
, the set
A[\tau]
will contain all natural numbers
k
from
0
to
n-1
, and thus

\tilde{f}(\tau)=1-\left(\sum_{i\in A[\tau]}2^{-i-1}\right)\leq 1-\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}2^{-i-1}\right)<\epsilon.

Extending
\tau
by an infinite string of 1’s will then yield an element
X\in F
with
f(X)<\epsilon,
as desired.

Finally, we claim that
f
is separated from 0. For suppose
X\in F
is such that
f(X)>0
(or
f(X)
is undefined). In this case, there must be some
k\in\omega
for which
X(k)=1
but
n\notin\text{ran}(g)
. Otherwise, for any
\epsilon>0
we could find an initial segment
\tau\subseteq X
for which
\tilde{f}(\tau)<\epsilon
; the argument is similar to that used above in verifying that 0 was the infimum of
f
.

With that in mind, choose such a witnessing
k
for which
X(k)=1
but
k\notin\text{ran}(g),
and let
\sigma\subseteq X
be long enough that
\sigma(k)=X(k)
is defined. Then we see that for any
Y\in F
which extends
\sigma
and any initial segment
\tau\subseteq Y
, we will never have
k\in A[\tau]
. Thus, the sum being subtracted in the definition of
\tilde{f}(\tau)
will always be missing the term
2^{-k-1}
, meaning that the difference will always be at least
2^{-k}.
Therefore, if
f(Y)
is defined, it must be the case that
f(Y)\geq 2^{-k}>0
, proving our desired separation property.

At this point, we can finally use SMMin to conclude that there is some
X\in F
for which
f(X)=0
. We claim that for this
X
, we have
X(n)=1
if and only if
n\in\text{ran}(g)
, which tells us the range of
g
exists in our model. On one hand, we’ve already mentioned that for any
X\in F
,
X(n)=0
implies that
n\notin\text{ran}(g)
. On the other hand, our separation argument showed that any
n
for which
X(n)=1
must satisfy
n\in\text{ran}(g)
. If there were an
n
for which this failed, then we could take an initial segment
\tau\subseteq X
long enough that any
Y\in F
extending
\tau
would give
f(Y)\geq 2^{-n},
contradicting our assumption that
f(X)=0
. This completes the proof of Claim 4, and of the entire theorem.
\square

4.
Measure Regularity Results

If we return to the formalization of Lebesgue and Hausdorff measure for closed sets that was discussed in the introduction, we see that this function represents a natural example of a monotone decreasing function on Cantor space. Therefore, the results from the previous section can help us deduce the reverse mathematical strength of some measure regularity results. These same techniques will also aid us in the analysis of the Besicovitch Theorem for Hausdorff measure in the final section.

4.1.
Regularity of
H^{s}_{n}
measure

In this section, we assume that we have some fixed
s\geq 0
and

, and we will work with the
H^{s}_{n}
measure as defined in Section 2.3. For these results, we assume that we start with some (standard or pruned) closed set
F
, and we seek to construct a closed subset
E\subseteq F
with some specified measure.
The complexity of this construction varies depending on how the closed sets are represented as well as on how precisely the measures are specified.

Note that if we consider the specific measure
H^{1}_{1}
, we obtain a version of the standard Lebesgue measure. This will be equivalent to the normal definition of Lebesgue measure in
\text{WKL}_{0}
and stronger systems, but in any system it still provides a well-defined function on the space of “tree codes.”
While the case of Lebesgue measure may hold the most interest, we decided to work with the general measure

(for

and

As in the section on general monotone functions, we will abuse notation slightly in writing inequalities involving

, since the limiting value of

itself may not exist for every tree code

without the axioms of

. (See the paragraph before Definition

First, we demonstrate how closed sets which satisfy a desired measure bound can be coded topologically.

Definition 4.1
.

Let

be a tree coding

. Recall from Section 2.2 that

Let

, and let

\mathcal{S}^{c}_{F}\subseteq 2^{\omega}

\{\nu\in S_{T_{F}}:\tilde{H^{s}_{n}}(\nu)\geq c\}.

Note that the monotonicity of the function
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}
guarantees that the set above is indeed a tree.
We define
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{c}_{F}

Lemma 4.1
.

The following is provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
: Let
F
be a nontrivial closed set, and let
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{F}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{c}_{F}

\mathcal{S}^{c}_{F}

(respectively
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{c}_{F}

For ease of notation, we will use the set

over
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{F}

First, by extending

(1)

If

(2)

Since

Given the fact that

for which
Y_{0}(\sigma)=1
, since each of the
K
-many length-
n^{\prime}
strings represented in
Z
will either have no extensions represented in
Y_{0}
, or exactly one. If we take
U
to consist of exactly these extensions, then
W_{s}(U)\leq K\cdot 2^{-sm}<c+\epsilon
by our choice of
m
. Thus, any initial segment
\nu\subseteq Y_{0}
for which
\nu\supseteq Y_{0}^{\leq m}
will satisfy
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu)<c+\epsilon
, and thus we have
H^{s}_{n}(Y_{0})<c+\epsilon
when we pass to the limit. Notice also that
Y_{0}\supseteq\tau
, since
Y_{0}
agrees with
Z
for all
\sigma
of length at most
n^{\prime}
.

If
Y_{0}=Z
, then
Y_{0}
itself is our witnessing element of
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{F}
(respectively,
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{c}_{F}
). Otherwise, there must be at least one length-
m

\sigma
for which
Y_{0}(\sigma)=0
but
Z(\sigma)=1
. Let
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{t}
enumerate all of the length-
m
strings with this property. Inductively define elements
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{t}
in
2^{\omega}
as follows:

•

If
Y_{i-1}(\sigma)=1
, set
Y_{i}(\sigma)=1
.

•

If
Y_{i-1}(\sigma)=0
, set
Y_{i}(\sigma)=1
if and only if
\sigma
is compatible with
\sigma_{i}
and
Z(\sigma)=1
.

Again, one can check that these do indeed code subtrees of
T_{F}
(pruned subtrees if necessary), that they each extend the string
\tau
, and that their existence is provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
. (In particular, each
Y_{i}
is computable from
Z
and any
Y_{j}
of lower index.)

Note also that for all strings
\sigma
of length at least
m
, we will have
Y_{t}(\sigma)=Z(\sigma)
. This is because any such string
\sigma
for which
Z(\sigma)=1
must either satisfy
Y_{0}(\sigma)=1
, or else must extend one of
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{t}
. In particular, this means that
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Z^{\leq k})=\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{t}^{\leq k})
for all
k\geq m
, and thus
H^{s}_{n}(Y_{t})\geq c.

We claim that some
Y_{i}
must satisfy
c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Y_{i})<c+\epsilon.
For consider any
k\geq m
, and consider the values of

\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{0}^{\leq k}),\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{1}^{\leq k}),\dots,\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{t}^{\leq k}).

We already know that
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{0}^{\leq k})<c+\epsilon
and
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{t}^{\leq k})\geq c
by previous remarks. Additionally, we observe that each
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i}^{\leq k})
must satisfy

\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i-1}^{\leq k})\leq\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i}^{\leq k})<\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i-1}^{\leq k})+\epsilon.

The first inequality follows from the fact that
Y_{i-1}(\sigma)=1
implies
Y_{i}(\sigma)=1
for all
\sigma
. Thus any valid cover for computing
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i}^{\leq k})
is also valid for computing
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i-1}^{\leq k})
. For the second inequality, we observe that if
U
is the minimal cover whose weight realizes
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i-1}^{\leq k})
, then adding
\sigma_{i}
to this cover gives one which is valid when computing
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i}^{\leq k})
, since the only strings
\sigma
which differ between
Y_{i}
and
Y_{i-1}
are those compatible with
\sigma_{i}
. (Note also that
|\sigma_{i}|=m
is in the appropriate range of
n
to
k
) By considering the weight of this new cover, we deduce that

\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i}^{\leq k})\leq\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i-1}^{\leq k})+2^{-sm}<\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i-1}^{\leq k})+\epsilon

by our choice of
m
.

It follows that as we enumerate through the values

\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{0}^{\leq k}),\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{1}^{\leq k}),\dots,\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{t}^{\leq k})

we must at some point land in the range
[c,c+\epsilon)
, since we start at a value less than
c+\epsilon
, we end at a value at least
c
, and we cannot “jump” by more than
\epsilon
. Let
Y^{(k)}
denote the first element
Y_{i}
for which
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i}^{\leq k})\in[c,c+\epsilon)
. Since we only have finitely many
Y_{i}
to consider, at least one
Y_{i}
must appear as
Y^{(k)}
for infinitely many
k
by the infinite pigeonhole principle.

In that case, we certainly have
H^{s}_{n}(Y_{i})<c+\epsilon
, since any of these
k
will work as a witness for
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i}^{\leq k})<c+\epsilon
, at which point we can apply monotonicity and pass to the limit. But since we can find arbitrarily large values of
k
for which
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Y_{i}^{\leq k})\geq c
, it follows by monotonicity again that we must in fact have
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu)\geq c
for all
\nu\subseteq Y_{i}
, and thus
H^{s}_{n}(Y_{i})\geq c
. As long as
c
is positive (which forces
Y_{i}
to code a nontrivial closed set), this proves that
Y_{i}
is our witnessing element of
\mathcal{S}_{F}^{c}
(or
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{F}^{c}
), as desired.

Finally, we consider the case when
c=0
. As before, choose an arbitrary
Z\in\mathcal{S}_{F}^{c}
, an arbitrary initial segment
\tau\subseteq Z
, and an arbitrary
\epsilon>0
. If
H^{s}_{n}(Z)=0
, then
Z
itself works as our witnessing element
X\in\mathcal{S}_{F}^{c}
with
0\leq H^{s}_{n}(X)<\epsilon.

Otherwise, choose
d>0
such that
d<\epsilon
and
d\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z)
. Then our
Z
will be an element of
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{F}
, so we can apply the argument above with respect to this positive
d
, using the same
Z
and
\tau
but with
\delta=\epsilon-d
in place of
\epsilon
. This will yield an element
X\supseteq\tau
of
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{F}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{0}_{F}
which satisfies

0<d\leq H^{s}_{n}(X)<d+(\epsilon-d)=\epsilon.

∎

From the proposition above, we deduce that if we start with a closed set
F
and we wish to find a subset
E
of an approximate measure, then the construction can be carried out computably as long as
F
and
E
are of the same type (either both standard closed or both pruned closed).

Proposition 4.2
.

The following is provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
: If
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is a nontrivial standard closed (respectively, pruned closed) set with tree code
Z_{F}
, and
c
is such that
0\leq c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
, then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a nontrivial standard closed (respectively, pruned closed) set
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies
c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})<c+\epsilon
.

Proof.

Note that if
F
is nontrivial and and its code
Z_{F}
satisfies
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})\geq c
, then
\mathcal{S}_{F}^{c}
will be nonempty since it will at least contain the element
Z_{F}
. If
F
happens to be pruned closed, then
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{F}^{c}
will also be nonempty for the same reason.
This corollary is then immediate from Lemma
4.1
, by applying the definition of infimum.

∎

If
F
is standard closed but we want the subset
E
to be pruned closed, then the analog to the above result is equivalent to
\text{WKL}_{0}
instead.

Proposition 4.3
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, the following is equivalent to
\text{WKL}_{0}
:
If
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is a nontrivial standard closed set with tree code
Z_{F}
, and
c
is such that
0\leq c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z)
, then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a nontrivial pruned closed set
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies
c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})<c+\epsilon
.

Proof.

To show that
\text{WKL}_{0}
is sufficient to prove the statement, we note that since
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})\geq c
, the tree corresponding to
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{c}_{F}
must be infinite. Indeed, consider the initial segment
Z_{F}^{\leq k}
for any
k\in\omega
, and define a string
\nu^{k}
of the same length by setting
\nu^{k}(\sigma)=1
if and only if
Z_{F}^{\leq k}(\sigma)=1
, and for all lengths
\ell
with
|\sigma|<\ell\leq k
there exists at least one
\tau\supseteq\sigma
with
|\tau|=\ell
and
Z_{F}^{\leq k}(\tau)=1
. Note that this string
\nu^{k}
will meet the conditions for being in
\tilde{S}_{T_{F}}.

We also claim that
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu^{k})\geq c
. If we take a cover
V\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
whose weight realizes
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu^{k})
, then the corresponding union of cylinders would form a
2^{-n}
-cover for the set
F
. (Note that every infinite sequence
X\in F
will have its length-
k
initial segment
\sigma
satisfying
Z^{\leq k}_{F}(\sigma)=\nu^{k}(\sigma)=1
). By Proposition
2.7
(which is provable in
\text{WKL}_{0}
), there must be some initial segment
\nu^{\prime}\subseteq Z_{F}
for which

\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu^{\prime})\leq\sum_{\sigma\in V}2^{-s|\sigma|}=W_{s}(V)=\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu^{k}).

Thus, if
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu^{k})
were strictly less than
c
, we would contradict the assumption that
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})\geq c
.

Therefore, each of the infinitely many sequences
\nu^{k}
will be in the tree for
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{c}_{F}
. By
\text{WKL}_{0}
, this tree must have at least one infinite path, so Lemma 3.1 applies and allows us to find a code
Z_{E}
for a nontrivial pruned closed set
E\subseteq F
which satisfies
c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})<c+\epsilon,
for any
\epsilon>0
.

For the reversal, consider any infinite tree
T
, and let
Z\in 2^{\omega}
and
F=[T]
be the corresponding code and closed set respectively. Note that
F
is nontrivial since
T
is infinite. Choose any
c\geq 0
such that
0\leq c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
, any
\epsilon>0
, and apply the statement to choose a nontrivial pruned closed
E\subseteq F
whose code
Z_{E}
satisfies
c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})<c+\epsilon
. As
E
is represented by an infinite pruned tree, we can just take its leftmost path
X
, which can be defined in a computable way. Since
E\subseteq F=[T]
, we have
X\in[T]
which proves
\text{WKL}_{0}
.
∎

In fact, we can actually obtain an equivalence with
\text{WKL}_{0}
just by requiring the existence of pruned closed subset with exactly the same measure as the original, under the constraint that this original measure is actually defined in our model.

Corollary 4.4
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, the following is equivalent to
\text{WKL}_{0}
:
If
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is a nontrivial standard closed set with tree code
Z_{F}
, and there exists a real number
d
such that
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})=d
, then there exists a nontrivial pruned closed set
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})=H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})=d
.

Proof.

If
\text{WKL}_{0}
holds, then we can choose any
\epsilon>0
and apply the previous corollary to
c=d
to obtain a nontrivial pruned closed set
E\subseteq F
whose code
Z_{E}
satisfies
d\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})<d+\epsilon.

We can also use the equivalence of
H^{s}_{n}
and
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}
in
\text{WKL}_{0}
(see Corollary
2.8
) to conclude that
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}(F)=H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})=d
. This means that given any
\delta>0
, there is some valid
2^{-n}
-cover of
F
with weight less than
d+\delta
. As
E
is a subset of
F
, this cover would be valid for
E
as well, so we can conclude that
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})<d+\delta
as a consequence of Proposition
2.7
. As
\delta>0
was arbitrary, we can combine this observation with the previous inequality of
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})\geq d
to conclude that
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})=d
as desired.

The reversal is the same as for the previous corollary, again making use of the fact that we can computably find an infinite path through any infinite pruned tree.

∎

Consider now the assertion that we can obtain a closed set with an
exact
positive measure, although unlike the previous result, this measure may be strictly less than that of the original set. We again break this down into cases depending on the representations of the sets involved.

Proposition 4.5
.

(Reg-S) If
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is a nontrivial standard closed set with tree code
Z_{F}
, and
c
is such that
0\leq c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
, then there exists a nontrivial standard closed set
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})=c
.

Proposition 4.6
.

(Reg-SP) If
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is a nontrivial standard closed set with tree code
Z_{F}
, and
c
is such that
0\leq c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
, then there exists a nontrivial pruned closed set
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})=c
.

Proposition 4.7
.

(Reg-P) If
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is a nontrivial pruned closed set with tree code
Z_{F}
, and
c
is such that
0\leq c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
, then there exists a nontrivial pruned closed set
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})=c
.

Proposition 4.8
.

(Reg-PS) If
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is a nontrivial pruned closed set with tree code
Z_{F}
, and
c
is such that
0\leq c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
, then there exists a nontrivial standard closed set
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})=c
.

We first verify that
\text{ACA}_{0}
is sufficient to prove all of these results.

Proposition 4.9
.

Each of Reg-S, Reg-SP, Reg-P, and Reg-PS is provable in
\text{ACA}_{0}.

Proof.

Note that it suffices to show that at least one of these regularity statements is provable in
\text{ACA}_{0}
. By Theorem
2.5
, standard and pruned closed sets are equivalent over
\text{ACA}_{0}
. Also, as
\text{ACA}_{0}
can prove any statement which holds in
\text{WKL}_{0}
, we can conclude from Corollary
2.8
that for any (standard or pruned) closed set
F
with associated code
Z_{F}
, we have
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})=\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}(F)
. The latter measure only depends on the elements in the closed set
F
, not on the specific tree we are using to code it. Taken together we can conclude that each of Reg-S, Reg-SP, Reg-P, and Reg-PS are pairwise equivalent under the axioms of
\text{ACA}_{0}
. We choose to show that
\text{ACA}_{0}
proves Reg-S.

To that end, we use the fact that
\text{ACA}_{0}
is equivalent to the Dense Monotone Minimum principle (see Theorem
3.10
). Let
F
and
c
be as in the statement of Reg-S. By Lemma
4.1
, the function
H^{s}_{n}
is dense above its infimum
c
over the nonempty closed set
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{F}
. Therefore, by DMMin there must be some
Z\in\mathcal{S}^{c}_{F}
for which
H^{s}_{n}(Z)=c
. The corresponding closed set
E_{Z}\subseteq F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
serves as our desired witness.

∎

Obtaining lower bounds for the complexity of these results is more difficult. In fact, Reg-PS could still potentially be provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
, although the author suspects that this is unlikely. However, we do have some results regarding the other three; in particular, none of them hold computably.

First, we will prove a lemma that we will need for the complexity lower bounds of Reg-S and Reg-P.

Lemma 4.10
.

The following is provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
: Let
E\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be a nontrivial standard closed set, and let
Z_{E}
be a code for its tree. Suppose there exists a real number
c>0
such that
H^{1}_{0}(Z_{E})=c
. Then there must exist an element
X\in E
.

Proof.

Let
T_{E}
denote the tree representing the closed set
E
. Given
\tau\in 2^{<\omega}
and
m\geq|\tau|
, we define the following notation:

W_{m}[\tau]=2^{-m}\cdot|\{\sigma\in T_{E}:|\sigma|=m\wedge\sigma\supseteq\tau\}|

W_{m}[\neg\tau]=2^{-m}\cdot|\{\sigma\in T_{E}:|\sigma|=m\wedge\sigma\nsupseteq\tau\}|

Note that these quantities are both computable from
T_{E}
, and that they are monotonically decreasing in
m
.

We will now inductively define an element
X\in E
. Since the procedure will be computable, the existence of
X
is provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}.
Assume for
k\geq 0
that
X\upharpoonright_{k}\in T_{E}
has already been defined, and that
W_{M}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}]<c
for some sufficiently large
M\geq k
. When
k=0
, we just set
X\upharpoonright_{0}
to be the empty string
\langle\rangle
; this is certainly an element of
T_{E}
if this tree is infinite, and as every string in
T_{E}
extends
\langle\rangle
, we will have
W_{m}[\neg\langle\rangle]=0<c
for all
m\geq 0
, so our inductive hypothesis is satisfied.

To define the value of
X(k)
, enumerate through the integers
m\geq k+1
until we find a value of
m
for which one of
i=0
or
i=1
satisfies

W_{m}[X\upharpoonright_{k}^{\frown}i]+W_{m}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}]<c.

We will then set
X(k)=1-i
for this value of
i
.

Note that so long as
m\geq k+1
, we will have

W_{m}[X\upharpoonright_{k}^{\frown}0]+W_{m}[X\upharpoonright_{k}^{\frown}1]+W_{m}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}]=2^{-m}\cdot|\{\sigma\in T_{E}:|\sigma|=m\}|.

Consider the initial segment
Z_{E}^{\leq m}
. One can check that the set

\{\sigma\in T_{E}:|\sigma|=m\}

gives the minimum weight across all covers considered in the computation of
\tilde{H}^{1}_{0}(Z_{E}^{\leq m})
; this is because for any cover under consideration, we can replace each string with the set of its length-
m
extensions in
T_{E}
and obtain a still-valid cover with equal or lesser weight.

By the assumption that
H^{1}_{0}(Z_{E})=c
, we can conclude that

2^{-m}\cdot|\{\sigma\in T_{E}:|\sigma|=m\}|=\tilde{H}^{1}_{0}(Z_{E}^{\leq m})\geq c.

Thus, since our value of
m
satisfies

W_{m}[X\upharpoonright_{k}^{\frown}i]+W_{m}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}]<c,

it must be the case that

W_{m}[X\upharpoonright_{k+1}]=W_{m}[X\upharpoonright_{k}^{\frown}(1-i)]>0.

Therefore,
X\upharpoonright_{k+1}
must have at least one length-
m
extension in
T_{E}
, which means
X\upharpoonright_{k+1}
itself is an element of
T_{E}
by initial segment closure. Also, since

W_{m}[X\upharpoonright_{k}^{\frown}i]+W_{m}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}]=W_{m}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k+1}],

we have
W_{m}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k+1}]<c
for this value of
m
, satisfying the second condition of our inductive hypothesis.

It remains to show why such a value of
m
must exist. Suppose for contradiction that for every
m\geq k+1
, we have both

W_{m}[X\upharpoonright_{k}^{\frown}0]+W_{m}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}]\geq c,

W_{m}[X\upharpoonright_{k}^{\frown}1]+W_{m}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}]\geq c.

Adding these two inequalities and rearranging would give

W_{m}[X\upharpoonright_{k}^{\frown}0]+W_{m}[X\upharpoonright_{k}^{\frown}1]+W_{m}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}]=2^{-m}\cdot|\{\sigma\in T_{E}:|\sigma|=m\}|\geq 2c-W_{m}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}].

Recall by our inductive assumption that there is some
M\in\omega
for which

W_{M}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}]<c
. Let
\epsilon=c-W_{M}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}]>0
; note that we will have

c-W_{m}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}]\geq\epsilon
for all
m\geq M
, since the quantity
W_{m}[\neg\tau]
is monotone decreasing in
m
.

Since
H^{1}_{0}(Z_{E})=c
, we must have
\tilde{H}^{1}_{0}(Z_{E}^{\leq m})<c+\epsilon
for all sufficiently large
m\in\omega
. In particular, we can choose such an
m\geq M
, which yields

\tilde{H}^{1}_{0}(Z_{E}^{\leq m})=2^{-m}\cdot|\{\sigma\in T_{E}:|\sigma|=m\}|<c+\epsilon.

Combining with our inequality from earlier, we have

2c-W_{m}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}]\leq 2^{-m}\cdot|\{\sigma\in T_{E}:|\sigma|=m\}|<c+\epsilon.

Subtracting
c
from both sides implies that

c-W_{m}[\neg X\upharpoonright_{k}]<\epsilon,

a contradiction to our choice of
\epsilon
.

This completes the inductive step, and in the limit of this construction we will have obtained an element
X\in[T_{E}]=E
as desired.

∎

We will now prove lower bounds for the complexity of Reg-SP, Reg-S, and Reg-P.

Proposition 4.11
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, we have the following:

(1)

Reg-SP
\implies\text{WKL}_{0}

(2)

Reg-S
\implies\text{WWKL}_{0}

(3)

Reg-P
\implies\text{WWKL}_{0}

Proof.

Proof of (i):
Observe that Reg-SP is simply a strengthening of the statement in Proposition
4.3
, which we have already shown is equivalent to
\text{WKL}_{0}
.
\square

Proof of (ii):
We wish to show that Reg-S implies
\text{WWKL}_{0}
. Let
T\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be a tree satisfying the hypothesis of
\text{WWKL}_{0}
, i.e. a tree with the property that

\lim_{k\to\infty}2^{-k}\cdot|\{\sigma\in T:|\sigma|=k\}|>0.

(Technically, we should understand the above to mean that the terms in our sequence are uniformly bounded away from zero, since formally the limit may not exist without
\text{ACA}_{0}.
) We wish to use Reg-S to show that this tree has an infinite path.

First, observe that if
Z\in 2^{\omega}
is the code for
T
, then the above condition on
T
implies that
H^{1}_{0}(Z)>0
, using the same considerations as discussed in the proof of the previous lemma.

With that in mind, we can set
F=[T]
, we can choose
c>0
such that
H^{1}_{0}(Z)\geq c
, and we can apply Reg-S to choose a nontrivial standard closed subset
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies
H^{1}_{0}(Z_{E})=c
. From the previous lemma, we can deduce that there is an element
X\in E\subseteq F=[T]
, serving as our witness to
\text{WWKL}_{0}
.
\square

Proof of (iii):
Finally, we wish to show that Reg-P implies
\text{WWKL}_{0}.
As in the proof of the previous item, we will let
T\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be an infinite tree satisfying the hypothesis of
\text{WWKL}_{0}
. We will define a new tree
T^{\prime}\subseteq 2^{\omega}
by setting
\sigma\in T^{\prime}
if and only if
\sigma
satisfies one of the following conditions:

(1)

\sigma\in T

(2)

There exists
\tau\subseteq\sigma
such that
\tau\in T
, neither of
\tau^{\frown}0
or
\tau^{\frown}1
are in

\sigma(i)=0

One can verify that

First, since the tree coded by

H^{1}_{0}(Z_{E})\leq H^{1}_{0}(Z_{E}^{\prime})=c

Suppose for contradiction that

\tilde{H}^{1}_{0}(Z_{E}^{\leq m})=2^{-m}\cdot|\{\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}:Z_{E}(\sigma)=1\wedge|\sigma|=m\}|<c.

For this choice of

Z_{E}(\sigma)=0

However, since

For this

But now suppose we define

V_{k,E}=\{\sigma\in V_{k}^{\prime}:Z_{E}(\sigma\upharpoonright_{m})=1\}.

Then we will have

\displaystyle W^{1}(V_{k,E})

\displaystyle=2^{-k}\cdot|\{\sigma\in V_{k}^{\prime}:Z_{E}(\sigma\upharpoonright_{m})=1\}|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-m}\cdot|\{\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}:Z_{E}(\sigma)=1\wedge|\sigma|=m\}|

\displaystyle=\tilde{H}^{1}_{0}(Z_{E}^{\leq m})

We will now briefly examine the relationship of these four regularity results to one another. Given that any pruned closed set is standard closed, it is clear that Reg-SP implies both Reg-S and Reg-P, and likewise that both Reg-S and Reg-P imply Reg-PS. Beyond that, it is unclear whether any of these implications can be reversed using only the axioms of
\text{RCA}_{0}
. However, we can show that any differences between these results disappear by the time we have
\text{WKL}_{0}

Corollary 4.12
.

Over
\text{WKL}_{0}
, the regularity results of Reg-S, Reg-SP, Reg-P, and Reg-PS are all pairwise equivalent.

Proof.

By the discussion in the preceding paragraph, it suffices to prove that under
\text{WKL}_{0}
, Reg-PS (the weakest statement) implies Reg-SP (the strongest statement).

Therefore, assume we have a nontrivial standard closed set
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
with tree code
Z_{F}
, and let
c
be such that
0\leq c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F}).
Consider any positive
\epsilon>0
. By Proposition
4.3
in
\text{WKL}_{0}
, we may choose a nontrivial pruned closed set
F^{\prime}\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{F}^{\prime}
satisfies
c\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F}^{\prime})<c+\epsilon
.

Now, use Reg-PS to find a nontrivial standard closed set
E^{\prime}\subseteq F^{\prime}
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}^{\prime}
satisfies
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E}^{\prime})=c
. Then use Corollary
4.4
in
\text{WKL}_{0}
to find a nontrivial pruned closed set

whose associated tree code

∎

So far, we have not yet demonstrated any measure regularity results which are demonstrably strong enough to prove
\text{ACA}_{0}
. As discussed in previous sections, one difficulty with using measure theoretic results in reverse mathematics comes from the fact that in weaker subsystems of second order arithmetic, the quantity
H^{s}_{n}(Z)
may not actually exist for all tree codes
Z
. In fact, by modifying Yu’s result for open sets mentioned in the introduction, one can show that

\text{ACA}_{0}
is actually required to obtain a well-defined Lebesgue measure for all closed sets.

That said, we can still meaningfully compare the measure of a (standard or pruned) closed set with a potential subset if we allow for some minor abuse of notation. If we have two closed sets
E
and
F
with respective tree codes
Z_{E}
and
Z_{F}
respectively, we will write
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
to mean the following:

\forall\epsilon>0,\exists k\in\omega,\forall m\in\omega,\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Z_{E}^{\leq k})<\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Z_{F}^{\leq m})+\epsilon.

It is straightforward to show that if both quantities
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})
and
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
exist, then the above statement is equivalent to the interpretation of
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
as a normal inequality of real numbers. Naturally, we will write
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})=H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
to mean that we have both
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
and
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})\leq H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})
.

This allows us to finally obtain a regularity result equivalent to
\text{ACA}_{0}
: passing from a standard closed to a pruned closed subset of the same measure.

Proposition 4.13
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, the following is equivalent to
\text{ACA}_{0}
: if
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is a nontrivial standard closed set with associated tree code

, then there exists a nonempty pruned closed set
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies
H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})=H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})
.

Proof.

The fact that
\text{ACA}_{0}
proves the stated result is straightforward, since standard and pruned closed sets are equivalent. Therefore, given a nontrivial standard closed set
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
with tree code
Z_{F}

Z_{F}^{\prime}
for a pruned tree representing the same set. Applying the equivalence of the
H^{s}_{n}
and
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}

\text{ACA}_{0}
, we can conclude that

H_{n}^{s}(Z_{F}^{\prime})=\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}(F)=H_{n}^{s}(Z_{F}).

As is typical for
\text{ACA}_{0}
reversals, we will show that the given statement is sufficient to prove that every one-to-one function
f:\omega\to\omega
has a range.
Using the given function

, we will define a code
Z_{F}
for the same tree

which was used in the proof of (iii)
\implies\text{ACA}_{0}
in Proposition
2.5

consists of all

such that either

(1)

(2)

for some

for all

,

where

represents the length-

H^{1}_{0}(Z_{E})=H^{1}_{0}(Z_{F})
.

We claim that for this tree code

and for all

if and only if

. This gives us a means of computing the range of

-comprehension in
\text{RCA}_{0}

To see this correspondence, suppose first that
n\in\omega

with

Suppose instead that

Z_{E}(\tau_{n})=0

By monotonicity, we may assume that

2^{-k}\cdot|\{\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}:Z_{F}(\sigma)=1\wedge|\sigma|=k\}|<2^{-k}\cdot|\{\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}:Z_{E}(\sigma)=1\wedge|\sigma|=k\}|+\epsilon,

|\{\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}:Z_{F}(\sigma)=1\wedge|\sigma|=k\}|<|\{\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}:Z_{E}(\sigma)=1\wedge|\sigma|=k\}|+\epsilon\cdot 2^{k}.

Note that any string

Thus, we can deduce from the above inequality that the number of length-

But now observe that since

∎

Compare the result from this proposition to that of Corollary

, where the measure of the original closed set

As an aside, this pair of results is somewhat analogous to some prior results concerning maxima/minima for continuous functions on compact sets. If the supremum of the function’s values on the set exists, as a real number, then the existence of a point in the set realizing that supremum is equivalent to
\text{WKL}_{0}
; otherwise, the existence is equivalent to

. See

4.2.
The Besicovitch Theorem

In this final section, we will use the techniques and results developed thus far to analyze the strength of the following regularity result for the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure, first proven by Abram Besicovitch in 1952. For a reproduction of Besicovitch’s proof (albeit for Euclidean space), see
5
. While the particular formulation of the proof given in this section is original, we give due credit to Besicovitch for the overall structure and key measure theoretic insights.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
s\geq 0
, and let
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be a closed set with
\mathcal{H}^{s}(F)=\infty
. Then for any real number
c>0
, there exists a closed subset
E\subseteq F
with
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=c
.

Our main goal is to verify that this theorem is provable in
\text{ACA}_{0}
. As discussed in the introduction, this fact is not obvious from Besicovitch’s original argument. Fortunately, with the axioms of
\text{ACA}_{0}
, the various ways of coding closed sets are all equivalent, as are the different ways of representing the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff
2^{-n}
-measure. Thus, the provability of Besicovitch’s theorem in
\text{ACA}_{0}
does not depend on the precise way in which the theorem is formalized. We choose to prove the formulation below; this version is slightly stronger than the original statement, in that it does not require the original set’s Hausdorff measure to be infinite, nor the witnessing subset’s measure to be positive.

Theorem 4.15
.

(Besicovitch)
Let
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be a nontrivial standard closed set with tree code
Z_{F}
, and suppose that
c,s\geq 0
are real numbers such that

c\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}H^{s}_{2^{-n}}(Z_{F}).

Then there exists a nontrivial standard closed set
E\subseteq F
whose associated tree code
Z_{E}
satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})=c.

We will derive this result as a consequence of BCTC, which is equivalent to (and therefore provable in)
\text{ACA}_{0}
by Theorem
3.6
. First, we introduce some notation. We assume throughout the remainder of this section that our parameter
s\geq 0
and our given closed set
F\subseteq 2^{\omega}
are fixed, as are the corresponding tree
T_{F}\subseteq 2^{\omega}
and tree code
Z_{F}\in 2^{\omega}
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
c\geq 0
be a real number, and let
n\in\omega
. Define the tree
T^{c}_{n}
by

T^{c}_{n}=\{\nu\in S_{T_{F}}:\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(\nu)\geq c\},

and let
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n}=[T^{c}_{n}]
denote the corresponding closed set. In other words,
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n}
consists of all
Z\in[S_{T_{F}}]
for which
H^{s}_{n}(Z)\geq c
.

We will let
\mathcal{U}^{c}_{n}
denote the open complement
2^{\omega}\setminus\mathcal{S}^{n}_{c}
.

Note that the definition of
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n}
is essentially the same as that in Definition
4.1
. However, in that section we assumed that the index
n
was fixed, so we did not need to keep track of it. Here, we will be working with changing values of
n
so we include that parameter as part of the notation.

Our BCTC argument hinges on the following result, which we will call
Besicovitch-Density
or
BD
.

Proposition 4.16
.

(BD) Let
n_{0}\in\omega
be such that
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}
is nonempty. If
d>c
, then for all
n\in\omega
, the open set
\mathcal{U}^{d}_{n}
is dense in the closed set
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}
.

If this proposition holds in
\text{ACA}_{0}
, then if we choose a sufficiently large
n_{0}\in\omega
, and a decreasing sequence
(d_{n})_{n\in\omega}
of real numbers converging downward to
c
, then BCTC will allow us to obtain an element
Z\in\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}\cap\bigcap_{n\in\omega}\mathcal{U}^{d_{n}}_{n}
. This element
Z
will
code a closed subset
E_{Z}\subseteq F
satisfying
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E_{Z})=c
. (We will provide the details at the end of this section).

The proof of the Besicovitch Density result is quite technical and requires multiple lemmas. First, we verify that the proposition holds in the case where the indices
n
agree.

Lemma 4.17
.

The following is provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
: Let
n\in\omega
be fixed, and assume
c\geq 0
is such that
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n}
is nonempty. If
d>c
, then the open set
\mathcal{U}^{d}_{n}
is dense in the closed set
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n}
.

Proof.

This result is essentially just a reformulation of Lemma
4.1
. Indeed, suppose
\tau\in 2^{<\omega}
is such that
N_{\tau}\cap\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n}\neq\emptyset
. We wish to show that there exists some
Y\in\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n}\cap\mathcal{U}^{d}_{n}
with
Y\supseteq\tau.
By Lemma
4.1
, (after accounting for the change in notation), we know that the function
H^{s}_{n}
is dense above
c
over
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n}
. Therefore, if we choose any
Z\in\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n}
, we know
Z
is a limit point with respect to
c
and
H^{s}_{n}
. If we take
\epsilon=d-c>0
, then there is some
Y\in\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n}
with
Y\supseteq\tau
and
H^{s}_{n}(Y)<c+\epsilon=d
, the last of which implies that
Y\in\mathcal{U}^{d}_{n}
as desired.

∎

Note that the previous lemma actually shows that
\mathcal{U}_{k}^{d}
is dense in
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{c}
for all

. For such a

, the closed set
E_{Y}
corresponding to the witness

from the above proof will satisfy

\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-k}}(E_{Y})\leq\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2^{-n}}(E_{Y})<d

by the definition of the Hausdorff

-measure. Therefore,

as well.

Returning to the proposition BD, by induction it now suffices to prove that if
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{d}
is dense in
\mathcal{S}_{n_{0}}^{c}
for some

, we also must have
\mathcal{U}_{n+1}^{d}

\mathcal{S}_{n_{0}}^{c}
. Note that we must be somewhat careful, since
\text{ACA}_{0}
technically only allows us to apply induction over statements which are arithmetic. However, this does not present an issue, since as long as we have
\text{WKL}_{0}
, the existence of an infinite path through a tree is equivalent to the existence of infinitely many strings in that tree. In particular, for any
\nu\in 2^{<\omega}
we can express the statement

as

\forall m\geq|\nu|,\exists\tau\supseteq\nu,\tau\in T^{c}_{n}\wedge|\tau|=m.

Likewise, the entire statement “
\mathcal{U}^{d}_{n}

is dense in

\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}
” can be expressed in an arithmetic way by noting that

\forall\nu\in 2^{<\omega},N_{\nu}\cap\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}\neq\emptyset\implies\exists Y\in N_{\nu}\cap\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}\cap\mathcal{U}^{d}_{n_{0}}

is equivalent to

\forall\nu\in 2^{<\omega},N_{\nu}\cap\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}\neq\emptyset\implies\exists\tau\supseteq\nu,N_{\tau}\cap\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}\neq\emptyset\wedge\tau\notin T^{d}_{n}.

Before we proceed to the main proof, let us briefly unpack the inductive hypothesis that
\mathcal{U}^{d}_{n}
is dense in
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}
, where
n\geq n_{0}
.
Suppose we have string
\tau\in 2^{<\omega}
such that

, and we have used this density assumption to obtain an element

. Consider an optimal covering
U
witnessing that
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Z^{\leq m})<d
for some

. By the definition of these covers, we know that all strings in

have length at least

. If all of these strings had length strictly greater than

would be considered in the calculation of

, meaning that

would also be in
\mathcal{U}^{d}_{n+1}
and we would be done.

Of course, this may not be the case. However, the next best option would be to know that, in a sense, any length-

strings in the cover were unnecessary. One way to interpret this is that each length-

string can be replaced by a set of proper extensions which still “cover” the appropriate portion of

but which don’t increase the original cover’s weight - or at least, that we can ensure that these increases can be made arbitrarily small.

This idea motivates the following definitions.

Definition 4.3
.

Suppose
Z\in[S_{T_{F}}]
, and
\tau\in 2^{<\omega}.
Let
Z_{\tau}\in[S_{T_{F}}]
denote the sequence defined by
Z_{\tau}(\sigma)=1
iff

is compatible with

and
Z(\sigma)=1
.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
Z\in[S_{T_{F}}]
, let
n\in\omega
, and let
\tau\in 2^{<\omega}
have length
n
. Define
Z
to be
n-thin for

\tau
if we have

H^{s}_{n+1}(Z_{\tau})\leq 2^{-ns}.

If
Z
is
n
-thin for all
\tau\in 2^{<\omega}
with length

, then we simply say

is
n-thin
.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
denote the set of all
n
-thin sequences in
[S_{T_{F}}]
.

As suggested by the informal discussion above, these

-thin sequences are sufficient to guarantee robustness when passing from
\mathcal{U}^{d}_{n}
to
\mathcal{U}^{d}_{n+1}.

Lemma 4.18
.

The following is provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
: For all

, we have
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{d}\cap\mathcal{T}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{U}^{d}_{n+1}.

H^{s}_{n}(Z)<d

Let
U=\{\tau_{0},\dots,\tau_{k-1}\}
be an optimal cover for

\tilde{H}^{s}_{n}(Z^{\leq m})<d
for some

.

If all strings in

\tilde{H}^{s}_{n+1}(Z^{\leq m})<d

\tilde{H}^{s}_{n+1}(Z_{\tau_{i}}^{\leq m^{\prime}})<2^{-ns}+\tfrac{\epsilon}{t}.

For each

, let
V_{i}\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
be a witnessing cover with

and let

be the cover obtained from

by replacing each string
\tau_{i}
with the strings from

. It is straightforward to verify that
U^{\prime}
is a valid cover for computing
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n+1}(Z^{\leq m^{\prime}})
. Finally, we note that

\displaystyle W_{s}(U^{\prime})

\displaystyle=W_{s}(V_{0})+\dots+W_{s}(V_{t-1})+2^{-s|\tau_{t}|}+\dots+2^{-s|\tau_{k-1}|}

\displaystyle<(2^{-ns}+\tfrac{\epsilon}{t})+\dots+(2^{-ns}+\tfrac{\epsilon}{t})+2^{-s|\tau_{t}|}+\dots+2^{-s|\tau_{k-1}|}

\displaystyle=2^{-s|\tau_{0}|}+\dots+2^{-s|\tau_{t-1}|}+2^{-s|\tau_{t}|}+\dots+2^{-s|\tau_{k-1}|}+t(\tfrac{\epsilon}{t})

\displaystyle=W_{s}(U)+\epsilon

\displaystyle<(d-\epsilon)+\epsilon

\displaystyle=d.

This proves that

is an element of
\mathcal{U}_{n+1}^{d}
as desired.

∎

All that remains to show now is that the

-thin elements are sufficiently numerous within each nonempty set
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{n_{0}}.
To that end, we prove the following:

Lemma 4.19
.

The following is provable in
\text{ACA}_{0}
: Let

and let

be such that
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}
is nonempty. Then for all

, the set
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is dense in
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}

Proof.

Let
N_{\nu}
be such that

Y\in N_{\nu}\cap\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}\cap\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

Choose an element

. By extending
\nu
along

if necessary, we may assume that
\nu
is long enough for
\nu^{\leq n}
to be defined. For each
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
of length
n
, let
\nu_{\sigma}
denote the finite string with
|\nu_{\sigma}|=|\nu|
and which has
\nu_{\sigma}(\tau)=1
if and only if
\nu(\tau)=1
and
\tau
is compatible with
\sigma
. Note that for each such
\sigma
, we have
Z_{\sigma}\supseteq\nu_{\sigma}
.

For ease of reading, we split the proof into two main steps.

Step 1:
Define an element
Y\in N_{\nu}\cap\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

We will define an element
Y\in[S_{T_{F}}]
by specifying
Y_{\sigma}
for each
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
of length
n
, and ensuring that each
Y_{\sigma}
extends
\nu_{\sigma}
. In this way, the final sequence
Y
will be well-defined and will extend
\nu
. If
\tau\in 2^{<\omega}
is in the domain of
\nu
, then
\tau
is also in the domain of
\nu_{\sigma}
for all length-
n
strings
\sigma
. Thus for any length-
n

\sigma_{i}
which is compatible with
\tau
(either a prefix or extension, depending on the length of
\tau
), we will have

Y(\tau)=Y_{\sigma_{i}}(\tau)=\nu_{\sigma_{i}}(\tau)=\nu(\tau).

If
\tau\in 2^{<\omega}
is not in the domain of
\nu
, then we must have
|\tau|>n
, so the value of
Y(\tau)
will be determined uniquely by
Y_{\tau\upharpoonright_{n}}(\tau)
.

It is straightforward to show that
Y
will be an element of
[S_{T_{F}}]
as long as each
Y_{\sigma}
is. Additionally, as long as
\text{ACA}_{0}
can prove the existence of each
Y_{\sigma}
, we can also deduce the existence of the overall element
Y
, which is computable from the (finite) collection of all
Y_{\sigma}
.

We will define the elements
Y_{\sigma}
according to two cases. Suppose first that
\sigma
is such that
Z
is
n
-thin for
\sigma
, i.e.
H^{s}_{n+1}(Z_{\sigma})\leq 2^{-ns}
.
Define
Y_{\sigma}=Z_{\sigma}
for all such
\sigma
. Note that
\nu_{\sigma}\subseteq Z_{\sigma}
so our desired condition is satisfied.

For all other
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
of length
n
, it must be the case that
H^{s}_{n+1}(Z_{\sigma})>2^{-ns}
, which implies that
Z_{\sigma}
is an element of
\mathcal{S}_{n+1}^{2^{-ns}}.
By Lemma
4.1
, we know that the function
H^{s}_{n+1}
is dense above
2^{-ns}
over
\mathcal{S}_{n+1}^{2^{-ns}}
. As
\mathcal{S}_{n+1}^{2^{-ns}}\cap N_{\nu_{\sigma}}
is nonempty (it at least contains
Z_{\sigma}
), we can actually conclude that
H^{s}_{n+1}
is dense above
2^{-ns}
over the closed set
\mathcal{S}_{n+1}^{2^{-ns}}\cap N_{\nu_{\sigma}}
as well. (The proof is just a matter of working through the definitions).
Since we have access to
\text{ACA}_{0}
, we may apply the Dense Monotone Minimum principle (see Theorem
3.10
) to find an element
Y_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n+1}^{2^{-ns}}\cap N_{\nu_{\sigma}}
which satisfies
H^{s}_{n+1}(Y_{\sigma})=2^{-ns}
.

It remains to observe that by its construction, the sequence
Y
extends the string
\nu
and is
n
-thin.

Step 2:
Show that
Y
is an element of
\mathcal{S}_{n_{0}}^{c}
.

By the monotonicity of the function
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n_{0}}
, it suffices to show that
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n_{0}}(Y^{\leq m})\geq c
for all
m\geq n\geq n_{0}
. Let such an
m
be arbitrary, and suppose
U
is a cover for computing
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n_{0}}(Y^{\leq m})
. We wish to show that
W_{s}(U)\geq c
.

Note that if we delete a string which properly extends another element of
U
, we have a still-valid cover of smaller weight, so we may assume that
U
is prefix-free. We wish to replace
U
with a cover
U^{\prime}
which is valid for computing
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n_{0}}(Z^{\leq m})
and for which
W_{s}(U^{\prime})\leq W_{s}(U).
Since
Z
was an element of
\mathcal{S}_{n_{0}}^{c}
, this would yield
W_{s}(U)\geq W_{s}(U^{\prime})\geq c
. This would imply that
Y\in\mathcal{S}_{n_{0}}^{c}
since the cover
U
was arbitrary.

Suppose we have a string
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
of length
n
for which
H^{s}_{n+1}(Z_{\sigma})>2^{-ns}
. If
\sigma
has no proper extensions in
U
, then we do nothing. Otherwise, delete all of these extensions and replace them with
\sigma
.

We claim that this action will not increase the
s
-weight of the cover. Indeed, let
V_{\sigma}
denote the (nonempty) set of proper extensions of
\sigma
in
U
. Note that all strings in
V_{\sigma}
have length between
n+1
and
m
, inclusive. Also, if
\tau
is such that
|\tau|=m
and
Y_{\sigma}(\tau)=1
, then
\tau
must extend some element of
V_{\sigma}
. Given that the original set
U
was a cover for
Y
, we know that
\tau
extends some element
\mu\in U
, which must be compatible with
\sigma
. If
\mu\subseteq\sigma
, then
\mu
would be a proper prefix of any element in
V_{\sigma}
, contradicting the fact that
V_{\sigma}
was nonempty and
U
was prefix-free. Therefore, we must have
\mu\supsetneq\sigma
, and thus
\mu\in V_{\sigma}.

Taken together, we see that
V_{\sigma}
is a valid cover for computing
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n+1}(Y_{\sigma}^{\leq m})
. Since
\sigma
was such that
H^{s}_{n+1}(Z_{\sigma})>2^{-ns}
, the construction of
Y
yields that
H^{s}_{n+1}(Y_{\sigma})=2^{-ns}
, and thus
W_{s}(V_{\sigma})\geq 2^{-ns}
. Therefore, replacing the strings in
V_{\sigma}
with a string
\sigma
having length
n
cannot increase the
s
-weight of
U.

Let
U^{\prime}
be the cover obtained from applying this replacement process to all length-
n
strings
\sigma
for which
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n+1}(Z_{\sigma})>2^{-ns}
. It remains to verify that
U^{\prime}
is a valid cover for computing
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n_{0}}(Z^{\leq m})
. For the length condition, note that we only added strings of length
n
, and we assumed that
n_{0}\leq n\leq m
. Thus our string lengths remain within the proper bounds.

For the second condition, suppose we have a string
\tau
with
|\tau|=m
and
Z(\tau)=1
. Let
\sigma
be the unique length-
n
prefix of
\tau
. If
\sigma
satisfies
H^{s}_{n+1}(Z_{\sigma})\leq 2^{-ns}
, then from our construction we know that
Y_{\sigma}=Z_{\sigma}
, and thus
Y_{\sigma}(\tau)=Z_{\sigma}(\tau)=1
. As the original cover
U
was valid for computing
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n_{0}}(Y^{\leq m})
, this implies that
U
contains some prefix of
\tau
, which will be compatible with
\sigma
. As
\sigma
was not one of the strings considered in our replacement process, this compatible prefix
\tau
will still be in
U^{\prime}
.

Suppose instead that
\sigma
satisfies
H^{s}_{n+1}(Z_{\sigma})>2^{-ns}
. Once again, we know that the original cover
U
was valid for computing
H^{s}_{n_{0}}(Y^{\leq m})
, and thus for computing
H^{s}_{n_{0}}(Y_{\sigma}^{\leq m})
as well. Given that
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n+1}(Y_{\sigma}^{\leq m})\geq 2^{-ns}
(from the construction of
Y
) there must be at least one
\tau^{\prime}
of length
m
for which

, so this
\tau^{\prime}
has a prefix

, compatible with
\sigma
. If
\mu\subseteq\sigma
, then
\mu
is also a prefix of
\tau
and was not removed in passing to

. If instead
\mu\supseteq\sigma
, then
\mu
was removed and replaced with

. Either way, we determine that

contains a prefix of
\tau
, which completes the argument that
U^{\prime}
is a valid cover for computing
\tilde{H}^{s}_{n_{0}}(Z^{\leq m})
, as desired.

∎

We are now ready to complete the proof of our desired density result.

Proposition 4.20
.

Proposition
4.16
(BD) is provable in
\text{ACA}_{0}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.17

\mathcal{U}_{n}^{d}

\text{RCA}_{0}

Let

First, we may apply the inductive hypothesis to choose

While we have already outlined the argument, we will now formally demonstrate the proof of Besicovitch’s theorem (according to the formulation in Theorem
4.15
) in
\text{ACA}_{0}
.

Theorem 4.21
.

Theorem
4.15
(Besicovitch) is provable in
\text{ACA}_{0}
.

Proof.

First, if
\mathcal{H}^{s}(F)=\lim_{n\to\infty}H^{s}_{n}(Z_{F})=c
, then the result is trivial, since we can just take our witnessing subset
E
to be
F
itself (with appropriate codes). So assume that
\mathcal{H}^{s}(F)
(which may be infinite) is strictly greater than the target measure
c
. In that case, there must be some
n_{0}\in\omega
for which
H^{s}_{n_{0}}(Z_{F})\geq c
. In that case, the closed set
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}
will be nonempty, since it will at least contain the element
Z_{F}
.

Now, let
(d_{n})_{n\in\omega}
be a strictly decreasing sequence of real numbers which converges to
c
. (For example, we may take
d_{n}=c+2^{-n}
.) As each
d_{n}
is strictly greater than
c
, it follows from Proposition
4.20
that each open set
\mathcal{U}^{d_{n}}_{n}
is dense in
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}
, and that this fact is provable in
\text{ACA}_{0}
. We can then apply BCTC (also provable in
\text{ACA}_{0}
) to obtain an element
Z\in\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}\cap\bigcap_{n\in\omega}\mathcal{U}^{d_{n}}_{n}
. We claim that for the associated closed set
E_{Z}\subseteq F
, we have

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E_{Z})=\lim_{n\to\infty}H^{s}_{n}(Z)=c,

implying that
E_{Z}
is our desired witnessing subset.

First, since
Z\in\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}
, we know that
H^{s}_{n_{0}}(Z)\geq c
, and thus
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E_{Z})\geq c
since the quantity
H^{s}_{n}(Z)
is monotonically increasing in
n
. To prove the opposite inequality, let
\epsilon>0
be arbitrary, and choose
N\in\omega
so large that
d_{n}<c+\epsilon
for all
n\geq N
. For all such
n
, we know that
Z\in\mathcal{U}^{d_{n}}_{n}
, and thus

H^{s}_{n}(Z)<d_{n}<c+\epsilon.

As this bound applies to all
n\geq N
, we can conclude that

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E_{Z})=\lim_{n\to\infty}H^{s}_{n}(Z)\leq c+\epsilon.

But then since
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary, we must in fact have
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E_{Z})\leq c
, and therefore
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E_{Z})=c
when combined with our earlier inequality. This completes the proof.
∎

Having shown that
\text{ACA}_{0}
implies Besicovitch’s theorem, it is natural to wonder whether the converse is true. After all, the BCTC result was integral to this particular proof, and BCTC itself is equivalent to
\text{ACA}_{0}
as shown in Section 3.1. While we have not yet determined whether this is the case, the results in the previous section at least demonstrate that Besicovitch - as formalized in Proposition
4.15
- does not hold in
\text{RCA}_{0}
. We can obtain similar lower bounds by changing the representations of the closed sets involved.

Proposition 4.22
.

Over
\text{RCA}_{0}
, Theorem
4.15
(Besicovitch) implies
\text{WWKL}_{0}
, as does the analog of Theorem
4.15
where the sets
F
and
E
are pruned closed. If
F
is standard closed but
E
is required to be pruned closed, then the theorem implies
\text{WKL}_{0}
.

Proof.

Assume that Theorem
4.15
holds (or one of its variants), and suppose we apply it in the case where
s=1
. In this case,
H^{1}_{n}
will compute the same function for every
n\in\omega
, so for any closed set
F
with tree code
Z_{F}
, we will just have
\mathcal{H}^{1}(F)=H^{1}_{0}(Z_{F})
. We can then apply the same reversal proofs as were used for Reg-S, Reg-P, and Reg-SP in Proposition
4.11
.
∎

We conclude this section with an examination of Besicovitch’s theorem from the perspective of computability theory. One well-known property of
\text{ACA}_{0}
is that its
\omega
-models are precisely those which are closed under Turing reducibility (downwards), the Turing join, and the Turing jump. Given any real
A\in 2^{\omega}
, we can construct an
\omega
-model of
\text{ACA}_{0}
containing
A
by defining its second order part to be the collection

\{X\in 2^{\omega}:X\leq_{T}A^{(n)}\text{ for some }n\in\omega\}.

Here,
A^{(n)}
represents the
n
-th iterate of the Turing jump of
A
.

Suppose
Z_{F}\in 2^{\omega}
is any tree code representing a nontrivial standard closed set, and suppose that our real number parameter
s
and our target measure
c
are both computable. Consider the model of
\text{ACA}_{0}
above with
A=Z_{F}
, whose second-order part would contain (codes for) each of
Z_{F},c,
and
s
. As Besicovitch’s theorem holds in this model, we can conclude that there exists a witnessing subset
E\subseteq F
whose tree code
Z_{E}
is computable from
Z_{F}^{(n)}
for some
n\in\omega
. If we could place some lower bound on the value of this
n
, then we could gain some understanding about the computability-theoretic complexity of Besicovitch’s theorem.

Since Besicovitch’s theorem is not provable in
\text{RCA}_{0}
, we know there are at least some tree codes
Z_{F}
for which the witnessing subset code
Z_{E}
is not computable from
Z_{F}
itself; this would mean that the necessary jump iterate
n
is at least 1. However, by examining the computability theoretic techniques used in previous arguments, we can deduce that a single jump is always sufficient, as formalized below.

Theorem 4.23
.

Let
Z_{F}\in 2^{\omega}
be a tree code representing a nontrivial standard closed set
F
, and suppose that
c,s\geq 0
are computable real numbers such that

c\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}H^{s}_{2^{-n}}(Z_{F}).

Then there exists a tree code
Z_{E}\in 2^{\omega}
for a nontrivial standard closed subset
E\subseteq F
such that
Z_{E}\leq_{T}Z_{F}^{\prime}
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})=c.

Proof.

Let the closed set
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}
be defined as in the proof of Theorem
4.21
. Observe that the corresponding tree

T^{c}_{n_{0}}=\{\nu\in S_{T_{F}}:\tilde{H}^{s}_{n_{0}}(\nu)\geq c\}

is computable from
Z_{F}
(i.e.
T_{F}
). Therefore, the first jump
Z_{F}^{\prime}
is sufficient to compute the pruned subtree

\tilde{T}^{c}_{n_{0}}=\{\sigma\in T^{c}_{n_{0}}:\forall m\geq|\sigma|,\exists\tau\supseteq\sigma\text{ with }|\tau|=m\text{ and }\tau\in T^{c}_{n_{0}}\},

since
\tilde{T}^{c}_{n_{0}}
is
\Pi^{0}_{1}
-definable from
Z_{F}
. Note that
\tilde{T}^{c}_{n_{0}}
still represents the same closed set
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}
, which we can now think of as pruned closed with this new tree code.

Now, consider the sequence of open sets
\{\mathcal{U}_{n}^{d_{n}}\}_{n\in\omega}
from the proof of Theorem
4.21
. Each set
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{d_{n}}
has an open set code
V_{n}
defined by
2^{\omega}\setminus T^{d_{n}}_{n}
, so these are all (uniformly) computable from
Z_{F}
. We know from Proposition
4.20
that each open set
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{d_{n}}
is dense in
\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}
. By following the proof of BCTCIII as given in Proposition
3.5
, we can, in a computable way, construct an element
Z\in\mathcal{S}^{c}_{n_{0}}\cap\bigcap_{n\in\omega}\mathcal{U}_{n}^{d_{n}}
using the pruned tree
\tilde{T}^{c}_{n}
and the open codes
\{V_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
. This element
Z
is thus computable from
Z_{F}^{\prime}
, and by the argument given in the proof of Theorem
4.21
, the corresponding closed set will satisfy

\lim_{n\to\infty}H^{s}_{n}(Z_{E})=c,

as desired.

∎

As discussed in the introduction, the witnessing subset constructed in the original proof would require infinitely many iterates of the original set’s jump, were it to be constructed precisely as Besicovitch described. Thus, the existence of a subset that is relatively simple from a computability perspective provides a rather surprising conclusion to this project.
