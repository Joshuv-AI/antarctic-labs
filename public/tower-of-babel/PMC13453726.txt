Case Report: Cerebrospinal fluid cytology as a diagnostic clue in hemorrhagic glioblastoma mimicking hypertensive intracranial hemorrhage
Authors: Yuanyang Ye, Yuan Chen, Chunxia Huang, Lina Cheng, Shaoqiang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioblastoma (GBM) rarely presents with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) as the initial symptom. A massive hematoma can completely obscure the underlying tumor on imaging, leading to misdiagnosis and treatment delay. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytology is simple and inexpensive, but is underutilized in the diagnostic workup of atypical ICH.Case presentationA 60-year-old man with poorly controlled hypertension presented with sudden left-sided weakness and vomiting. CT showed a large right basal ganglia hemorrhage (87.1 mL, 72.8 HU) with a midline shift of 17.8 mm. After endoscopic hematoma evacuation, hypertensive ICH was diagnosed. However, two months after discharge, the patient was readmitted with bone-flap bulging and elevated intracranial pressure. Follow-up MRI showed only a cystic lesion, interpreted as postoperative or inflammatory change. Lumbar puncture was performed for CSF cytology. Three consecutive punctures all revealed numerous atypical cells with high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios, pseudopod-like protrusions, and atypical mitotic figures. Pathology later confirmed IDH1-wildtype glioblastoma (WHO grade IV) with leptomeningeal spread.ConclusionWhen a large hematoma masks an underlying tumor on imaging and atypical imaging features or abnormal clinical recovery are present, CSF cytology can provide a crucial diagnostic clue. This case highlights that CSF cytology should be actively pursued in patients with atypical ICH, especially when follow-up imaging shows only non-specific changes.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive primary brain tumor, with a median survival of less than two years (1, 2). Approximately 2.5% of cases present with intracranial hemorrhage as the initial symptom, but the hematoma often masks the underlying tumor, leading to misdiagnosis and treatment delay (3).

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytology is simple, inexpensive, and considered one of the “gold standards” for diagnosing leptomeningeal metastasis (4). A meta−analysis including 9, 493 patients with central nervous system lymphoma showed that the positive detection rate of CSF cytology was 17%, but only 8% of patients were diagnosed by CSF analysis, indicating that positive cytology findings are often not fully utilized in routine diagnostics (5).

Here we report a case of hemorrhagic GBM in which the tumor was completely masked by a large hematoma, and imaging provided no definitive diagnosis. In this difficult situation, CSF cytology revealed malignant cells, suggesting a tumor, which was eventually confirmed by pathology as glioma. This case emphasizes that for atypical intracerebral hemorrhage on imaging, CSF cytology is an underutilized but indispensable diagnostic tool.

Case presentations

A 60−year−old man presented to our hospital with sudden left−sided weakness and vomiting. Emergency head CT showed a large right basal ganglia hemorrhage (87.1 mL, CT value 72.8 HU) with a midline shift of 17.8 mm to the left (Figure 1A). At the time of initial ICH diagnosis, no contrast-enhanced head MRI was performed because the patient’s critical condition (brain herniation) required immediate surgical intervention. The patient had no personal history of malignancy and no family history of cancer. The patient had an 8−year history of poorly controlled hypertension (admission blood pressure 213/131 mmHg) and long−term antiplatelet use. After initial treatment with furosemide and nitroglycerin at another hospital, his symptoms did not improve and he developed altered consciousness. Then he underwent emergency endoscopic hematoma evacuation and decompressive craniectomy on the day of admission in our emergency department. Postoperative CT showed complete hematoma resolution and midline restoration (Figure 1B). Based on the history of hypertension and the basal ganglia location, hypertensive intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) was diagnosed, and the patient was discharged after hematoma absorption.

Two months after discharge, he was readmitted due to bone−flap bulging, altered mental status, and elevated intracranial pressure (290 mmH2O). Head CT revealed a low−density lesion (12.3 HU), and MRI showed an abnormal cystic lesion in the right frontal lobe with cranial bulging, initially interpreted as post−inflammatory or postoperative change (Figures 1C, D). No definite tumor was identified on imaging.

Because elevated intracranial pressure had been documented at the other hospital, a lumbar puncture was performed for CSF cytology after admission to investigate the cause of the elevated intracranial pressure. Three punctures (performed on days 1, 3, and 7 after admission) all revealed numerous atypical cells (Table 1; Figures 2A, B): large cell size, high nuclear−to−cytoplasmic ratio, pseudopod−like protrusions, and atypical mitotic figures. CSF routine showed a mild increase in nucleated cells; CSF biochemistry demonstrated markedly elevated protein (7.03 g/L), elevated lactate dehydrogenase (79.9 U/L), and decreased glucose (0.9 mmol/L), supporting a malignant process and making infection unlikely (Table 2). Tumor markers in CSF and serum (AFP, CEA, NSE, HCG, SCC, CA199, TPSA) were all negative, excluding adenocarcinoma. These findings collectively pointed toward a neoplastic etiology rather than infection, prompting a multidisciplinary discussion.

After multidisciplinary discussion, a tumor was strongly suspected. The patient underwent craniotomy and tumor resection after providing written informed consent. Pathology revealed a high−grade glioma, WHO grade IV, IDH1−wildtype glioblastoma with leptomeningeal spread (Figure 2C). Postoperative MRI (Figure 1E) later revealed abnormal enhancement along the surgical margin and adjacent dura mater, extensive linear leptomeningeal enhancement indicative of cerebrospinal fluid dissemination. An Ommaya reservoir was placed to relieve hydrocephalus, but the patient refused further radiotherapy and chemotherapy. He died approximately one and a half months after discharge (approximately three months after tumor resection, and approximately six months from the initial hemorrhage), due to recurrent intracerebral hemorrhage with a total survival of 6 months from the initial hemorrhage. Rapid leptomeningeal spread was observed after surgery.

Discussion

High−grade gliomas can present as spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) (6). The underlying mechanisms include pathological angiogenesis (VEGF overexpression leading to disorganized neovasculature and fragile vessel walls), direct vascular invasion by tumor cells, and intratumoral arteriovenous fistulas (7–9). On imaging, key differentiating features exist between tumor−related ICH and hypertensive ICH. The former is predominantly lobar, with irregular shape, heterogeneous density, and perilesional edema disproportionate to the stage of the hematoma. The latter typically occurs in the basal ganglia, thalamus, or pons, with a regular shape, homogeneous density, and edema proportional to the volume and timing of bleeding (6). In our patient, emergency CT showed a large right basal ganglia hemorrhage (87.1 mL), but the hematoma was irregular, heterogeneous, and accompanied by edema inconsistent with the absorption stage, features that should have raised strong suspicion of tumorous stroke. Nevertheless, the patient had an 8−year history of poorly controlled hypertension (admission blood pressure 213/131 mmHg), long−term antiplatelet therapy, and the hemorrhage was located in the basal ganglia. Clinicians prematurely diagnosed hypertensive ICH based on the clinical context, and the imaging abnormalities were overshadowed by the patient’s history, leading to initial misdiagnosis.

Such over−reliance on clinical history at the expense of imaging clues is not uncommon in the literature. Leclerc et al. reported 14 glioma patients, three of whom presented with ICH; two were diagnosed within 3 months, but the third was not diagnosed until 9 years later (10). Turkkkan et al., in a retrospective analysis of 55 patients with brain metastases presenting as ICH, found that the most common symptom was elevated intracranial pressure and the most frequent location was the temporal lobe (11). The common lesson from these cases is that relying solely on the patient’s history while ignoring abnormalities in hematoma shape, density, and edema pattern carries a high risk of missing an underlying tumor.

A large hematoma can completely mask the underlying tumor on CT/MRI through signal evolution and mass effect, even with contrast−enhanced studies. In the acute stage, deoxyhemoglobin causes T2 hypointensity; in the early subacute stage, intracellular methemoglobin leads to T1 shortening. The mass effect compresses and obscures the enhancing lesion, while persistent tumor oozing further delays methemoglobin conversion. Consequently, contrast−enhanced MRI may not clearly depict the tumor−hematoma interface until partial hematoma absorption has occurred. In our patient, the three−month follow−up MRI showed only a cystic lesion, which was misinterpreted as postoperative change or an inflammatory process, a direct consequence of this masking effect. Although guidelines recommend repeating contrast−enhanced MRI after hematoma resolution (approximately 2−4 weeks) (12). our patient did not undergo this examination during the initial admission because the postoperative CT showed complete hematoma resolution and clinical improvement, leading to a diagnostic delay.

When imaging falls into a blind spot due to hematoma masking, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytology can provide a crucial alternative diagnostic clue. The anatomical basis is that the hematoma is confined to the brain parenchyma or ventricles, whereas CSF circulates freely in the subarachnoid space and ventricular system. Tumor cells that have invaded the subarachnoid space or ependyma can shed into the CSF, and lumbar puncture directly samples this compartment, completely bypassing the physical obscuration caused by the hematoma. In our patient, three lumbar punctures were performed for CSF cytology (Romanowsky staining combined with cytocentrifugation), which clearly revealed numerous atypical cells (high nuclear−to−cytoplasmic ratio, pseudopod−like protrusions, and atypical mitotic figures). Concurrent CSF biochemistry showed markedly elevated protein (7.03 g/L), very low glucose (0.9 mmol/L), and elevated LDH (79.9 U/L). Bacterial culture, tuberculosis/cryptococcal tests, and tumor markers were all negative, ruling out infectious meningitis and metastatic adenocarcinoma. It should be noted that the positivity rate of a single lumbar puncture is only 25−45%, but the sensitivity increases to 86% after three consecutive punctures (13, 14).

Safety consideration for lumbar puncture: Although CSF cytology provides critical diagnostic information in patients with atypical intracerebral hemorrhage, lumbar puncture should only be performed after careful assessment of intracranial pressure and exclusion of significant mass effect or impending herniation on imaging. In our patient, elevated intracranial pressure (290 mmH2O) was documented, but brain CT showed no ventricular compression or midline shift at the time of readmission, making lumbar puncture safe. The procedure was performed using a 22-gauge needle with slow drainage of CSF (approximately 2 mL collected for analysis); the opening pressure was measured before fluid withdrawal, and no post-procedural complications (e.g., herniation, new neurological deficits) occurred.

Based on our experience with this case, we propose the following clinical diagnostic pathway: (1) Identification of high−risk patients: those with an irregular hematoma shape, heterogeneous density, or edema disproportionate to the bleeding stage; or those with an abnormal clinical recovery (delayed neurological improvement, persistently elevated intracranial pressure, or recurrent neurological symptoms); or those in whom follow−up imaging shows only non−specific cystic changes. (2) Imaging follow−up: contrast−enhanced MRI should be routinely performed after hematoma resolution (approximately 2−4 weeks). (3) Safety assessment: before lumbar puncture, confirm absence of significant mass effect, ventricular compression, or brain herniation on recent imaging. (4) Timing for CSF cytology: if contrast−enhanced MRI remains non−diagnostic or shows only cystic changes, lumbar puncture for CSF cytology should be performed promptly. If the initial cytology is negative but clinical suspicion remains high, the puncture should be repeated (up to three times), combined with CSF biochemical parameters (protein, glucose, LDH) for comprehensive assessment.

It is important to emphasize that at the time of diagnosis, our patient already had leptomeningeal spread (LMS). The median survival for LMS is only 2−5 months (15); our patient survived 6 months from the initial hemorrhage, slightly better than the literature average. Risk factors for LMS include an infratentorial or periventricular tumor location and intraoperative ventricular opening (6, 16). Although our patient was 60 years old, the tumor was adjacent to the lateral ventricle, and the rapid postoperative progression was consistent with these high−risk features. This dismal prognosis further underscores the importance of early diagnosis. Therefore, incorporating CSF cytology into the routine evaluation of patients with atypical imaging findings after hematoma resolution may be a key strategy to break the diagnostic deadlock and improve prognosis.

The patient ultimately died of recurrent intracerebral hemorrhage. Based on the rapid tumor progression and extensive leptomeningeal dissemination documented during hospitalization, we consider this fatal hemorrhage to be tumor-related intratumoral hemorrhage due to glioblastoma progression, rather than uncontrolled hypertension alone. After pathological confirmation, the patient and his family declined radiotherapy and chemotherapy, receiving only supportive care. Tumor progression eventually led to fatal intratumoral hemorrhage, consistent with the natural history of high-grade glioma.

Regarding whether the glioma already existed at the time of the initial hypertensive hemorrhage, we strongly incline to an affirmative judgment. The initial CT showed a large basal ganglia hemorrhage (87.1 mL), but the hematoma was irregular in shape, heterogeneous in density, and accompanied by edema disproportionate to the bleeding volume, features more consistent with tumor-related hemorrhage than with pure hypertensive bleeding. However, the patient’s blood pressure was 213/131 mmHg and the hemorrhage was located in the typical site for hypertensive hemorrhage, leading clinicians to favor that diagnosis. The patient recovered well after the first surgery but was readmitted only about two months later due to bone-flap bulging and mental status changes, progression far exceeding the expected course of post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus. Subsequent MRI and CSF cytology confirmed extensive leptomeningeal dissemination, which is unlikely to have developed from zero to a detectable level within only a few months. Literature reports that approximately 2.5% of gliomas present with intracranial hemorrhage as the initial symptom, and glioblastoma is known to induce fragile neovasculature through VEGF overexpression, making intratumoral hemorrhage a recognized complication. Therefore, the initial hemorrhage most likely represented spontaneous intratumoral hemorrhage from glioblastoma, with hypertension as a contributing factor.

Regarding why the tumor was not identified during the first surgery, the patient presented with cerebral hemorrhage and brain herniation in a life-threatening emergency. The sole surgical goal was rapid hematoma evacuation, intracranial pressure reduction, and life preservation. The surgeon’s entire focus was on hematoma removal; it was not feasible to actively explore or identify tumor tissue within the hematoma cavity during active bleeding. Preoperative CT could not effectively distinguish tumor from hematoma due to the massive hemorrhage, and intraoperative endoscopic findings were dominated by hematoma and the hematoma cavity, with surrounding edematous brain tissue lacking identifiable tumor features. Postoperative CT confirmed satisfactory hematoma clearance with midline restoration, and no early follow-up contrast-enhanced MRI was arranged, this represents a point of reflection for this case and explains why the tumor was initially missed.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a single case report, the findings may not be generalizable. Second, CSF cytology was performed only after the patient’s clinical deterioration; its diagnostic value in the acute phase remains unclear. Third, although we have proposed a clinical diagnostic pathway, this algorithm has not yet been prospectively validated. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm the routine value of CSF cytology in such patients.

Conclusions

For high-risk patients with large intracerebral hemorrhage showing atypical imaging features or abnormal clinical recovery, CSF cytology can serve as an important supplement to imaging. Especially when follow-up imaging shows only non-specific cystic changes, CSF cytology should be actively pursued to avoid diagnostic delay.
