SIRT6 enhances the therapeutic potential of extracellular vesicles in mitigating osteoarthritis in rat models
Authors: Guihua Yang, Su Liu, Huitian Han, Xueyan Yang, Feng Li, Qiong Wu, Zhiyu Zhao, Fei Liu, Zhifa Zheng, Guangxi Gao, Wenjing Ma, Lina Zhao, Baohua Liu, Zhihong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSCs) have emerged as promising therapeutic candidates for osteoarthritis (OA). Sirtuin 6 (SIRT6), a class III histone deacetylase, exerts protective effects by preventing cellular senescence, reducing inflammation, and promoting longevity. This study aims to generate immortalized hUCMSCs with SIRT6 and telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) overexpression and investigate the therapeutic potential of their secreted EVs (EVs@SIRT6) in OA.MethodshUCMSCs were genetically engineered using lentiviral transduction to overexpress SIRT6 and TERT, referred to as SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs. Natural EVs and EVs@SIRT6 were isolated via differential ultracentrifugation and characterized by morphology, size distribution, marker expression, and proteomic signature. Their effects on IL-1β stimulated chondrocytes were assessed in vitro, including cell viability, apoptosis, scratch closure, inflammatory cytokine secretion, and oxidative stress. Transcriptomic alterations were analyzed by RNA sequencing. Therapeutic efficacy in vivo was evaluated in a rat anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) model via micro-computed tomography, histological analyses, and immunohistochemistry.ResultsSIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs preserved mesenchymal identity, trilineage differentiation potential, and sustained proliferative capacity up to passage 60, accompanied by longer telomeres and reduced senescence compared to parental cells. EVs@SIRT6 maintained typical EV features but were enriched in SIRT6 protein and displayed a distinct proteomic signature, comprising 1,440 unique proteins associated with nuclear/chromatin repair. In vitro, EVs@SIRT6 more effectively enhanced chondrocyte viability and wound healing while reducing apoptosis compared with natural EVs. EVs@SIRT6 also markedly alleviated inflammatory response, promoted anabolism, suppressed catabolism, and mitigated oxidative stress in chondrocytes. The protective effects of EVs@SIRT6 on chondrocytes were associated with the suppression of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway. Studies in a rat ACLT model further confirmed that EVs@SIRT6 outperformed natural EVs in attenuating subchondral osteosclerosis, reducing osteophyte formation, and mitigating cartilage damage.ConclusionsEngineered EVs@SIRT6 outperform natural EVs in preserving chondrocytes homeostasis and reducing OA progression, establishing an efficient platform for preparing engineered EVs for clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05112-3.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Mesenchymal stem cells, Extracellular vesicles, SIRT6

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative joint disorder characterized by progressive articular cartilage degradation, subchondral bone remodeling, and synovial inflammation [1]. OA impacts hundreds of millions of individuals globally and represents a leading cause of disability [2]. Current treatments, including pharmacological interventions for pain relief and surgical procedures for joint repair or replacement, primarily aim to alleviate symptoms and slow disease progression; however, they generally fail to preserve joint homeostasis or halt the structural deterioration inherent in OA [3, 4].

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as promising therapeutic agents in OA [5–7], largely via paracrine function that involves extracellular vesicles (EVs) [8]. EVs act as bioactive molecular carriers including proteins and miRNAs, and antagonize OA by inhibiting inflammation and chondrocyte apoptosis [9]. Compared with MSCs, EVs offer advantages in safety, industrial preparation and long-term storage. Nevertheless, their therapeutic application remains constrained by the intrinsic biological characteristics of the parent MSCs, including replicative senescence and limited capacity for large-scale expansion [10, 11].

Sirtuin 6 (SIRT6), a protein deacylase, regulates DNA repair, metabolic homeostasis, aging, and longevity [12, 13]. Here, we aimed to develop an improved EV-based strategy for OA by engineering human umbilical cord–derived MSCs (hUCMSCs) to overexpress SIRT6 and telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), thereby overcoming senescence-related barriers to therapeutic. We hypothesized that EVs derived from SIRT6- and TERT-overexpressing hUCMSCs (EVs@SIRT6) would enhance therapeutic efficacy. We systematically examined the biological properties of SIRT6/TERT-engineered hUCMSCs, the proteomic profiles of EVs@SIRT6, and their functional impacts on chondrocytes both in vitro and in vivo. This study provides a rationale for improving EV-based therapy for mitigating OA progression, highlighting its potential for clinical translation via intra-articular delivery for early intervention.

Materials and methods

Isolation and characterization of hUCMSCs

Human umbilical cord tissue was obtained from one consenting donor with approval from the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (Ethical Approval Number: JS-2613). Samples were aseptically collected and transported to the laboratory under cold chain conditions, maintained at a temperature of 4–10 °C, and processed within 48 h. Upon arrival, sample containers were disinfected with 75% ethanol and subsequently placed in a biosafety cabinet. Umbilical cords were alternately rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 75% ethanol to isolate Wharton’s jelly tissue. The Wharton’s jelly was then sectioned into 1 mm³ pieces and positioned at 5 mm intervals on the bottom of a petri dish. Cells were cultured in α-MEM complete medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and the culture was maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Cells were passaged at a 1:2 ratio upon reaching 80–90% confluence.

Cell phenotyping was performed in accordance with the standards of the International Society for Cell Therapy (ISCT) [14]. Passage 3 cells were harvested and incubated with FITC-conjugated mouse anti-human monoclonal antibodies against positive markers (CD73, CD90, CD105) and negative markers (CD34, CD45, CD14, CD19, HLA-DR) at room temperature for 30 min. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with PE-labeled secondary antibody for an additional 30 min and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Flow cytometry analysis showed that > 95% of cells expressed positive markers, whereas < 2% expressed negative markers, consistent with ISCT standard.

Trilineage differentiation potential of hUCMSCs

For chondrogenic differentiation, hUCMSCs at approximately 80% confluence were detached with 0.25% trypsin, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in α-MEM containing 10% FBS at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. 0.5 mL cell suspension was transferred to 15-mL centrifuge tubes, pelleted at 300 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Cells were resuspended in 1 mL chondrogenic differentiation medium and cultured under static conditions at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Half of the medium was replaced (0.5 mL fresh induction medium) every 2 days for 21–28 days. Cartilage pellets were washed three times with DPBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 60 min, and washed twice. Chondrogenic differentiation was assessed by overnight Alcian blue staining, followed by three acidified solution washes to visualize chondrocyte-specific blue matrix under microscopy.

For osteogenic differentiation, cells at 80% confluence were trypsinized, centrifuged, and resuspended in α-MEM to 2–4 × 10⁴ cells/mL. Cells (1 mL/well) were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured to 80% confluent before replacement to osteogenic differentiation medium. Medium was replaced every 3 days for 2–4 weeks. Cells were washed twice with DPBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, and rinsed three times. Mineralized matrix formation was evaluated by Alizarin red staining (0.5 mL/well for 5 min). Red mineralized deposits indicating calcium nodules were observed under microscopy.

For adipogenic differentiation, cells at 80% confluence were trypsinized, centrifuged, and resuspended in α-MEM to 2–4 × 10⁴ cells/mL. Cells (1 mL/well) were seeded in 24-well plates and induced with adipogenic differentiation medium upon reaching 80% confluence, with fresh medium replacement every 2–3 days for 12–21 days until cytoplasmic lipid droplets were evident. Cells were washed twice with DPBS, fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 30 min, treated with 60% isopropanol to enhance staining, and stained with Oil Red O to visualize intracellular lipid accumulation.

Establishment of SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs

Third-passage hUCMSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at 2 × 10⁴ cells per well and subjected to antibiotic selection using gradient concentrations of puromycin (0, 1, 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10 µg/mL) or blasticidin S HCl. The culture medium supplemented with these antibiotics was replaced every two days. The optimal screening concentration was defined as the minimum dose eliminating 50–70% of the cells within 5 days and all untransfected cells within 2 weeks. Cells were then plated in 6-well plates at varying densities (2.0 × 10⁵, 3.0 × 10⁵, 4.0 × 10⁵, 5.0 × 10⁵ cells per well) and cultured in the optimal antibiotic concentration, with viability monitored every two days. A confluency of 80–90% prior to marked cell death (approximately 5 days) was selected as the optimal condition for subsequent experiments. Cells were then transduced with TERT-expressing lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 80, with a titer of at least 1 × 10 transducing units per milliliter (TU/mL), followed by puromycin selection. Monoclonal TERT-hUCMSCs were isolated by limiting dilution and subsequently transduced with a SIRT6-expressing lentivirus (MOI = 80) and a viral titer of at least 1 × 10⁸ TU carrying a blasticidin resistance cassette. Following a 48 h antibiotic selection, SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs were isolated and subsequently cultured at a passage ratio of 1:5 until proliferation ceased.

Identification of SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs

Cell surface marker profiles were analyzed by flow cytometry using the same protocol as described for parental hUCMSCs. Chondrogenic, osteogenic, and adipogenic differentiation potentials were evaluated using identical induction protocols. Differentiation outcomes were assessed by Alcian blue, Alizarin red, and Oil Red O staining, with specific matrix deposition visualized by light microscopy.

Total RNA was extracted from SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs using RL solution reagents (Beibei Biotechnology, Henan, China). cDNA synthesis was performed with the PrimeScript RT Kit (Takara, Liaoning, China), followed by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) using SYBR PreMix Ex Taq II (Takara, Liaoning, China) on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche). The thermal cycling protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s (annealing/extension). Reactions were set up in 10-µL volumes, with GAPDH serving as the housekeeping gene for normalization using the 2-∆∆Ct method. Primer sequences were as follows: TERT, 5’-AGACCTCCAGCCGTACATGC-3’ (forward) and 5’-AGACGTCGAAGAGGCCACTG-3’ (reverse); SIRT6, 5’-CCCACGGAGTCTGGACCAT-3’ (forward) and 5’-CTCTGCCAGTTTGTCCCTG-3’ (reverse); GAPDH, 5’-GCCTCAAGATCATCAGCAATGC-3’ (forward) and 5’-TGGTCATGAGTCCTTCCACGAT-3’ (reverse).

For immunofluorescence staining, hUCMSCs and SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs (8 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded in 24-well plates until adhesion and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBST. Cells were incubated with rabbit anti-human SIRT6 primary antibody at room temperature for 1 h, followed by goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody in the dark for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min, and slides were mounted with anti-fluorescence quenching medium before imaging via fluorescence microscopy.

Cumulative proliferative capacity assay

Third-passage hUCMSCs and SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 10⁴ cells/well and serially passaged upon reaching ≥ 90% confluence. Live cells were counted via 0.4% Trypan Blue dye exclusion using a hemocytometer. Population doublings (PDs) were calculated as PD = log2 (Nf) -log2 (Ni), where Nf represents the total cell number post-passage and Ni the initial seeding density. PD values were recorded for each passage, and a cumulative proliferation curve was generated from three independent biological replicates. During long-term culture, when parental hUCMSCs exhibited severely limited proliferative capacity, cells were continuously maintained by routine dissociation and reseeding procedures for analyses at corresponding culture time points relative to engineered hUCMSCs undergoing normal serial passaging.

Telomere length detection

Third-passage hUCMSCs and SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs in the logarithmic growth phase were used for genomic DNA extraction with the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS, lysed in cell lysis buffer, digested with proteinase K, and centrifuged. DNA was precipitated with absolute ethanol, dissolved in TE buffer, and stored at -20 °C. Telomere length was measured using the Telomere Length Detection Kit (ELK Biotechnology, Wuhan, China). The 20 µL reaction mixture included 10 µL DNA template, 0.4 µL telomere/single-copy gene-specific primers, and 10 µL 2× PCR Mix. Amplification was performed on a LightCycler 480 real-time PCR system with the following program: predenaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 35–40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s (denaturation), 55 °C for 30 s (annealing), and 72 °C for 45 s (extension), ending with melting curve analysis. Each sample was assayed in triplicate for technical repeats. Relative telomere length was calculated using the 2-∆∆Ct method, normalizing to single-copy gene expression as an internal control.

Karyotype analysis

Karyotype analysis was conducted using the GENMED Blue Fluorescent Chromosomal Karyotyping Kit (Genmed Scientifics, Shanghai, China). Briefly, SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs at passages 10, 20, 40, and 60 were seeded in 6-well plates. Upon reaching 70–80% confluence, cells were treated with a prewarmed (37 °C) mitotic inhibitor and incubated for 4 h to arrest cells at metaphase. Cells were then detached with 0.25% trypsin, collected by centrifugation, and subjected to hypotonic treatment with prewarmed hypotonic solution to facilitate chromosome dispersion. After centrifugation, cells were fixed with cold fixative added dropwise along the tube wall. This fixation step was repeated 2–3 times until cell pellets appeared white and homogeneous. The fixed cell suspension was filtered through a 200-mesh nylon filter, and 100 µL aliquots were dropped onto prechilled (-20 °C) slides. Slides were air-dried, stained with 100 µL DAPI fluorescent dye for 15 min at room temperature, and mounted with anti-fluorescence quenching medium. Fluorescent images were captured and converted to black-and-white G-band mode. 20 metaphase cells per sample were randomly selected to assess chromosome number and structure.

β-Galactosidase staining

hUCMSCs and SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs at passage 60 were seeded in 24-well plates at 2 × 10⁴ cells/well in α-MEM complete medium containing 10% FBS at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 24 h. Upon reaching 60–70% confluence, the medium was removed and cells were washed once with 1 mL DPBS. Fixation was performed with 0.5 mL 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min. Cells were then rinsed three times with DPBS (1 min each) to remove residual fixative and subsequently incubated with 50 µL β-galactosidase staining solution (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA). Staining was conducted overnight at 37 °C in a humidified incubator. β-galactosidase–positive cells were visualized and imaged using bright-field microscopy, and the percentage of positive cells was quantified.

Isolation of EVs and EVs@SIRT6

FBS was depleted of endogenous extracellular vesicles by ultracentrifugation at 120,000 × g for 12 h at 4 °C. The upper 90% supernatant was collected, aliquoted, and stored frozen for later use as EV-depleted serum. When hUCMSCs and SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs reached 70% confluence, cells were washed with PBS and cultured in α-MEM supplemented with EV-depleted FBS. After 72 h of continuous culture, hUCMSCs or SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs were dissociated with trypsin, quantified using a hemocytometer, and evaluated for viability using trypan blue exclusion. Only cultures with viability > 95% were included for subsequent EV isolation. The final cell number at harvest was approximately (10 ± 1.8)×106 cells. Supernatants were subsequently collected and sequentially centrifuged at 2,000×g for 10 min and 10,000×g for 30 min to remove cellular debris and apoptotic bodies. The clarified supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 110,000×g for 70 min to pellet EVs. Pellets were resuspended in sterile PBS and subjected to a second ultracentrifugation under the same conditions to reduce soluble protein contaminants. EVs were finally resuspended in PBS and stored at -80 °C until further use.

Characterization of EVs and EVs@SIRT6

Morphological analysis of EVs and EVs@SIRT6 was performed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Freshly isolated samples (20 µL) were deposited onto formvar/carbon-coated copper grids and fixed with 1% glutaraldehyde in PBS (pH 7.4). Following fixation, grids were negatively stained with 3% aqueous phosphotungstic acid for 5 min at room temperature in the dark. After air-drying for 10 min, EVs were imaged using a Tecnai G2 Spirit TEM (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) operated at 120 kV. Size distribution was evaluated using a Zeta View nanoparticle tracking analyzer (NTA) (Particle Metrix, Meerbusch, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were diluted to an appropriate concentration in sterile PBS and loaded into the instrument via a sterile syringe for analysis.

Identification of EVs and EVs@SIRT6

Western blot analysis was performed to characterize surface markers of EVs and EVs@SIRT6. Total proteins from hUCMSCs, EVs, and EVs@SIRT6 were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China), and protein concentrations were quantified with a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equal protein amounts were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD63, CD9, TSG101, ALIX, Calnexin, and GAPDH. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using a super-enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit and quantified with ImageJ software.

Proteomic analysis

Proteomic profiling was conducted by BIOMS (Beijing, China). Proteomic analysis was performed using three biological replicates per group on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Peptide fragmentation was automatically selected using the data-dependent acquisition (DDA) strategy. Raw MS files were searched against the UniProt human database using Spectronaut Pulsar software for protein identification. The false discovery rate (FDR) was set at 1% at the PSM, peptide, and protein group levels, with a maximum of two missed cleavages allowed. Missing values were imputed using the sequential K-nearest neighbor method. Differential protein selection criteria were defined as |log2(EVs@SIRT6/EVs)| ≥1 and FDR ≤ 0.05. Volcano plots and Venn diagrams were generated from the differential protein list to visualize significantly altered proteins. Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) categorization (biological processes, cellular components, molecular functions) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, were performed using the clusterProfiler package in R. An FDR threshold of ≤ 0.05 was applied to identify significantly enriched categories.

Extraction and identification of chondrocytes

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wangjing Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (WJEC-KT-2021-035-P003), and all patients provided written informed consent for the use of joint cartilage samples obtained from joint replacement tissues. Specimens were collected within 8 h post-surgery and transported to the laboratory at 4–10°C. Cartilage tissue was trimmed into 1-mm-thick slices using sterile scalpels, minced into fragments with ophthalmic scissors, and subjected to two-step enzymatic digestion: 0.25% trypsin at 37°C for 1–2 h, followed by 2 mg/mL type II collagenase with gentle shaking overnight at 37°C. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min to obtain single cells. Pellets were resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 15–20% FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution, then cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, chondrocytes were detached using 0.25% trypsin and passaged. Chondrocyte identity was characterized by toluidine blue staining (Beyotime, China) to assess glycosaminoglycan synthesis, a marker of chondrogenic phenotype. Chondrocytes from each donor were isolated, cultured, and used separately without pooling. Therefore, primary chondrocytes used in each experiment originated from a single donor source, thereby minimizing inter-donor variability.

Cellular uptake of EVs and EVs@SIRT6

Chondrocytes were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well and cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 24 h to facilitate adhesion. EVs and EVs@SIRT6 were labeled with PKH67 fluorescent dye using the PKH67 Cell Membrane Labeling Kit (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 10 µL of vesicles were mixed with 4 µL PKH67 dye and 0.5 mL Diluent C, incubated for 4 min, and the reaction was terminated by adding 1 mL 0.5% BSA. Labeled vesicles were purified by ultracentrifugation at 120,000×g for 70 min to remove free dye, and protein concentration was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For uptake assays, 4 µg of labeled EVs or EVs@SIRT6 were added to each well and co-cultured with chondrocytes in the dark for 24 h. Cells were then washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and stained with DAPI to label nuclei and phalloidin to visualize the actin cytoskeleton for 20 min. Endocytic efficiency and cellular morphology were visualized using confocal fluorescence microscopy (Leica TCS SP8), with green fluorescence indicating PKH67-labeled vesicles, blue fluorescence indicating nuclei, and red fluorescence indicating actin filaments.

Cell viability assay

Chondrocyte viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Beyotime, China). Cells were seeded at 5 × 10³ cells per well in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h, followed by stimulation with DMEM containing 10% FBS and 10 ng/mL interleukin-1β (IL-1β) for 24 h. Cultures were then treated with PBS, EVs, or EVs@SIRT6 for 24, 48, and 72 h. At each time point, 10 µL CCK-8 reagent (diluted 1:10 in culture medium) was added, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured after 1 h of incubation using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA).

Apoptosis assay

Chondrocytes were seeded at 1 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6-well plates and stimulated with 10 ng/mL IL-1β for 24 h, followed by treatment with PBS, EVs, or EVs@SIRT6 for an additional 24 h; unstimulated cells served as controls. Cells were trypsinized, collected by centrifugation (300 g, 5 min), and washed twice with PBS. Pellets were resuspended in 1× binding buffer (2–5 × 10⁶ cells/mL), stained with 6 µL Annexin V-PE and 10 µL 7-AAD (20 µg/mL) in the dark for 5 min, and analyzed by flow cytometry immediately.

Scratch assay

Confluent (90%) chondrocytes (5 × 10⁴/well in 24-well plates) were scratched using a 200-µL pipette tip to create uniform wounds (0.5 cm spacing). Non-adherent cells were removed by washing three times with PBS, and cultures were stimulated with 10 ng/mL IL-1β for 24 h before treatment with PBS, EVs, or EVs@SIRT6. Images of scratch gaps were captured at 0 and 24 h using bright-field microscopy, and scratch closure distances were quantified with ImageJ software.

Anabolism/catabolism detection

For WB and RT-qPCR, chondrocytes seeded in 10-cm dishes were stimulated with 10 ng/mL IL-1β for 24 h, followed by treatment with PBS, EVs, or EVs@SIRT6 for an additional 24 hours. Total protein and RNA were extracted using standard protocols. WB analyzed ACAN, COL2A1, and MMP13 expression (GAPDH as loading control), while RT-qPCR measured mRNA levels of ACAN, COL2A1, MMP13, and ADAMTS-5 (GAPDH as internal reference). Primer sequences were as follows: ACAN, 5’-TGGAGACAAGGATGAGTTTCC-3’ (forward) and 5’-GGCGAAGCAGTACACATCATA-3’ (reverse); COL2A1, 5’-TGGACGATCAGGCGAAACC-3’ (forward) and 5’-GCTGCG-GATGCTCTCAATCT-3’ (reverse); MMP13, 5’-TGGCATTGCTGACATCATGA-3’ (forward) and 5’-GCCAGAGGGCCCATCAA-3’ (reverse); ADAMTS-5, 5’-TACTTGGCCTCTCCCATGAC-3’ (forward) and 5’-TTTGGACCAGGGCTTAGATG-3’ (reverse). WB and qPCR were performed as previously described.

Enzyme-like immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Supernatants from IL-1β stimulated (10 ng/mL, 24 h) or unstimulated chondrocytes (1 × 10⁵/well in 6-well plates) were collected following treatment with PBS, EVs, or EVs@SIRT6 for 24 h. Levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin-10 (IL-10) were measured using ELISA kits (R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Optical density at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay

ROS production was detected using 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, 10 µM). Chondrocytes (1 × 10⁵/well) were stimulated with IL-1β (10 ng/mL, 24 h), treated with PBS, EVs, or EVs@SIRT6 for 24 h, and incubated with DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were washed three times with PBS, and fluorescence images were captured using a Leica fluorescence microscope. Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified with Image-Pro Plus software.

Antioxidant enzyme assay

Chondrocytes treated with IL-1β and EVs as described above were harvested, washed with ice-cold PBS, and lysed. Catalase (CAT) activity, glutathione (GSH) content, and total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD) activity were measured using commercial kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

RNA sequencing

Chondrocytes stimulated with IL-1β from three biological replicates and subjected to subsequent treatments were collected, washed twice with PBS, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. RNA extraction and sequencing were performed by Novogene (Beijing, China) according to standard procedures. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the Bioconductor package DESEQ2 with a p-adj value < 0.05 and a |log2 fold change| ≥1.

Animal model

A rat knee OA model was established via anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) [15]. Briefly, rats were anesthetized using an induction chamber with 2–3% isoflurane mixed with oxygen at a flow rate of 1.5–2 L/min. Anesthesia depth was maintained by continuous mask inhalation of 1.5–2.5% isoflurane, ensuring absence of pain reflexes. The right knee region was shaved and disinfected with iodophor solution, and a longitudinal incision (0.5–3 cm) was made along the medial knee to incise skin and subcutaneous tissue. Muscles were bluntly dissected to expose the patellar ligament and joint capsule. The patella was realigned, and the knee flexed to 90° to fully expose the joint cavity. The ACL was completely transected using microscissors, and successful transection was confirmed by a drawer test showing tibial displacement > 2 mm. The joint capsule and skin were sutured in layers. Postoperatively, penicillin (50,000 units/kg) was administered intramuscularly for three consecutive days to prevent infection. All animal experiments were reported in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines 2.0.

Animal grouping

Sprague-Dawley (SD) male rats (8 weeks old, SPF-grade) were obtained from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All animal procedures were performed in accordance with guidelines approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital. After a 1-week adaptive feeding period, 12 rats were randomly assigned to the Sham group, which underwent joint cavity exposure without ACL transection. The remaining rats underwent ACLT surgery and, one week later, were randomly divided into three groups (n = 12 per group): PBS, EVs, and EVs@SIRT6. EVs and EVs@SIRT6 were intra-articularly injected at a dose of 100 µg/50 µL per rat, with the PBS group receiving an equal volume of PBS. Injections were administered weekly for a total of four times. At 4 and 8 weeks after the first injection, six rats from each group were randomly selected for endpoint analyses. Rats were euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO₂) overdose using a gradual fill method in accordance with institutional guidelines. Death was confirmed by respiratory arrest and lack of a pedal reflex.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis

Following sacrifice, rat hindlimbs were harvested, soft tissues dissected, and samples fixed in 10% formalin for 48 h before µCT scanning using an animal imaging system (VivaCT 80; SCANCO Medical, Switzerland). Scanning parameters included 70 kV, 114 µA, 8 W power, and a voxel size of 7.8 μm. Three-dimensional reconstructions and quantitative analyses were performed on regions of interest (ROI) using SkyScan CT Analyzer (CTan software). Structural parameters evaluated included tibial subchondral bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), subchondral bone plate thickness (SCBP), osteophyte volume, and maximum osteophyte area [16, 17].

Histological analysis

At 4 and 8 weeks post-treatment, animals were sacrificed, and right hind limbs were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 48 h. Samples were decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 2 weeks, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5-µm-thick slices for histological processing. Safranin O-fast green (SO/FG) and toluidine blue (TB) staining were performed to visualize proteoglycan content in articular cartilage. OA severity was evaluated using the modified Mankin scoring system, which assesses cartilage degeneration, cellularity, and matrix integrity. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was conducted to examine general tissue morphology, with quantitative analysis of tibial plateau cartilage area and calcified cartilage area performed using image analysis software [18, 19]. All histological sections were imaged using a Zeiss Axio Imager optical microscope.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis

Knee joint sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a gradient of ethanol (100%, 95%, 85%, 70%). After washing three times with PBS, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) for 15 min. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 min in a microwave, followed by cooling to room temperature. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Aggrecan, matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-13, TNF-α, and IL-6. After washing, sections were treated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-mouse/anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (MXB Biotechnologies, Fuzhou, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein localization was visualized using 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen, and reactions were terminated by washing with distilled water. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min, and slides were dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped. Digital images were captured using a Zeiss Axio Imager microscope. Quantitative analysis of positive staining intensity and cell density was performed with Image-Pro Plus software, normalized to the total tissue area.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 24.0 (IBM) and GraphPad Prism 10 software. Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett’s multiple comparison tests were applied for multi-group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

Results

Engineered SIRT6/TERT overexpression ameliorates senescence in hUCMSCs

HUCMSCs were isolated and cultured according to standard procedure [20] that fulfilled the MSCs ISCT criteria including morphology, surface marker profile, and trilineage differentiation capacity (Figure S1A-G). Cells were genetically modified by lentiviral transduction to co-express SIRT6 and TERT, generating SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that engineered hUCMSCs retained typical MSC phenotypes, with high expression of CD73 (98.92%), CD90 (99.33%), and CD105 (98.56%), and low expression of hematopoietic and leukocyte markers, including CD14 (0.04%), CD19 (0.07%), CD34 (0.12%), CD45 (0.09%), and HLA-DR (0.07%) (Fig. 1A). These findings indicate that the lentiviral transduction did not alter the fundamental characteristics of hUCMSCs. Consistently, trilineage differentiation assays showed little difference in chondrogenic, osteogenic, or adipogenic potential between SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs and parental hUCMSCs (Fig. 1B-D). RT-qPCR revealed significant upregulation of TERT and SIRT6 mRNAs in SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs compared to parental hUCMSCs (Fig. 1E, F). Immunofluorescence staining revealed robust nuclear SIRT6 protein expression in SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs (Fig. 1G), confirming efficient transgene overexpression without compromising phenotypic stability.

We next examined whether SIRT6/TERT overexpression mitigated cell senescence. SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs underwent continuous expansion up to 60 passages. Normal diploid karyotypes were confirmed by karyotype analysis (Fig. 1J and 46,XY/46,XX, ≥ 20 metaphase spreads/passage cells). Cumulative proliferation assays showed significantly enhanced growth of SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs compared with parental hUCMSCs (Fig. 1H). The relative telomere length at passage 5 was markedly increased in SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs (Fig. 1I). A significantly lower proportion of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) positively stained cells was noticed in P60 SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs compared to parental hUCMSCs, the latter of which exhibited classic senescent phenotypes such as enlarged cell volume and cytoplasmic vacuolation (Fig. 1K). Collectively, these results demonstrate that SIRT6/TERT overexpression confers enhanced proliferative capacity and resistance to senescence without compromising genomic integrity.

EVs@SIRT6 exhibit distinct proteomic signatures

We next cultured hUCMSCs and SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs in EV-free medium and collected EVs according to standard protocols [21], referred to as EVs and EVs@SIRT6 respectively. TEM revealed that both vesicle populations exhibited typical cup-shaped or spherical bilayer structures (Fig. 2A), satisfying the morphological criteria outlined by the ISEV. Nanoparticle tracking analysis revealed highly overlapping size distributions between EVs and EVs@SIRT6, ranging from 30 to 150 nm, consistent with the size profile of small EVs (sEVs) (Fig. 2B). Comparable particle-to-protein ratios (2–4 × 109 p/µg) were observed between EVs and EVs@SIRT6. Western blotting analysis found robust expression of transmembrane proteins (CD63, CD9), cytoplasmic markers (TSG101, Alix), and absence of endoplasmic reticulum marker Calnexin in both vesicle types (Fig. 2C). Together, EVs@SIRT6 aligned with the MISEV2018 Guidelines’ triple verification requirements for EVs, exhibiting comparable morphology, biophysical properties, and marker expression to control EVs. We next analyzed proteomic composition of EVs and EVs@SIRT6 by high-resolution liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). PCA revealed high intra-group reproducibility and clear separation between two EV populations, indicating distinct proteomic signatures (Fig. 2D). Volcano plot analysis identified 2,035 significantly upregulated and 746 significantly downregulated proteins in EVs@SIRT6 compared to control EVs (Fig. 2E). Comparative proteomic analysis revealed that 2,166 proteins were shared between the two EV populations, while 1,440 proteins were unique in EVs@SIRT6 (Fig. 2F). SIRT6 was revealed in EVs@SIRT6 and confirmed by western blotting (Fig. 2G). GO analysis showed that proteins enriched in control EVs were primarily cytoplasmic components, whereas proteins enriched in EVs@SIRT6—particularly the unique subset—were mainly nuclear components. Markedly, KEGG analysis revealed that EVs@SIRT6-specific proteins were predominantly enriched in chromatin remodeling and DNA base excision repair pathways (Fig. 2H-I), which are impaired and thus accelerating aging in Sirt6−/− mice [17]. These data suggest that EVs@SIRT6 encapsulate components associated with genomic integrity, which may potentially contribute to nuclear-associated functions (Figure S2).

EVs@SIRT6 attenuates IL-1β-induced chondrocyte damage

We next assessed the impacts of EVs@SIRT6 on chondrocytes. EVs and EVs@SIRT6 were labeled with green fluorescence dye PKH67 and incubated with chondrocyte cultures at logarithmic growth phase. After 24 h, green fluorescence was observed in the cytoplasm of nearly all chondrocytes (Fig. 3A). IL-1β treatment induced chondrocyte apoptosis, determined by Annexin V-PE/7-AAD dual staining and flow cytometry (Fig. 3B). Treatment with EVs or EVs@SIRT6 significantly reduced apoptosis, withEVs@SIRT6 showed more pronounced decrease. Similarly, both EVs and EVs@SIRT6 reversed inhibition of chondrocyte viability by IL-1β, as determined by CCK-8 assays, with EVs@SIRT6 showing a more substantial effect (Fig. 3C). After 24-h treatment with EVs or EVs@SIRT6, enhanced scratch closure was observed in IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes, while EVs@SIRT6 achieved a healing rate exceeding 60% and the EVs showed only 50% wound healing (Fig. 3D).

IL-1β stimulation significantly downregulated expression of anabolic gene ACAN and COL2A1, while upregulated expression of catabolic gene MMP13 and ADAMTS5. Treatment with EVs or EVs@SIRT6 increased ACAN/COL2A1 expression and decreased MMP13/ADAMTS5 expression, with EVs@SIRT6 demonstrating a more pronounced effect (Fig. 3E). Western blotting data corroborated these findings, showing that IL-1β-mediated reduction in ACAN/COL2A1 protein and elevation in MMP13 protein were ameliorated by EVs and EVs@SIRT6, particularly EVs@SIRT6 (Fig. 3F). These results indicate that EVs@SIRT6 outperformed EVs in promoting chondrocyte anabolism and inhibiting catabolism in an inflammatory metabolic disorder model.

IL-1β stimulation increased pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, and TNF-α etc.), while decreasing anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10 etc.) in chondrocytes. Treatment with equivalent doses of EVs or EVs@SIRT6 modulated cytokine imbalance by decreasing IL-6 and TNF-α levels and increasing IL-10 expression, with EVs@SIRT6 showing superior efficacy (Fig. 3G). Chondrocytes generate excessive ROS under inflammatory conditions. IL-1β markedly elevated intracellular ROS levels, as detected using the DCFH-DA probe (Fig. 3H). Treatment with EVs or EVs@SIRT6 substantially reduced ROS level, with EVs@SIRT6 producing a greater reduction. Activities of T-SOD, CAT, and GSH levels were measured to study the antioxidant defense mechanism. IL-1β stimulation decreased T-SOD, CAT, and GSH levels in chondrocytes, which were restored by EVs and EVs@SIRT6 treatment, with EVs@SIRT6 promoting a stronger recovery (Fig. 3I). These findings suggest that EVs@SIRT6 mitigates ECM remodeling, inflammatory response, as well as oxidative stress in inflamed chondrocytes.

Subsequently, the effects of EVs@SIRT6 on chondrocyte gene expression were further investigated by RNA sequencing. The Venn diagram revealed that the IL-1β + EVs@SIRT6 group shared 11,768 genes with the control, IL-1β, and IL-1β + EVs groups, while containing 102 uniquely expressed genes (Fig. 3J). The volcano plot identified 232 differentially expressed genes between the IL-1β and IL-1β + EVs@SIRT6 groups (Fig. 3K). Among these, several pro-inflammatory genes (CXCL8, ICAM1, and GDF15, etc.) were significantly downregulated in the IL-1β + EVs@SIRT6 group, whereas anti-inflammatory genes such as IGFBP3 and SRSF1 were significantly upregulated (Fig. 3L). GO results showed that these differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in inflammation-related pathways, including cytokine regulation, the JAK–STAT signaling pathway, and leukocyte migration (Fig. 3M). These findings further suggest that EVs@SIRT6 may exert potential anti-inflammatory effects on inflamed chondrocytes.

EVs@SIRT6 promotes superior articular cartilage protection

An ACLT rat model was established and treated with EVs or EVs@SIRT6. Knee cartilage damage and degeneration were assessed at week 4/8 post-treatments by histological evaluation (H&E, SO/FG, and TB staining). At week 4, the Sham group (without damage) exhibited smooth cartilage surfaces with uniform ECM distribution, while the PBS group showed rough surfaces, prominent fissures, and uneven matrix distribution (Fig. 4A). Both EV and EVs@SIRT6 treatments significantly reduced cartilage wear compared to PBS, with EVs@SIRT6 exhibiting more homogeneous ECM organization. Moreover, the most severe cartilage injury was found in PBS group according to the Modified Mankin scoring, which was significantly lower in EVs group, and lowest in EVs@SIRT6 (Fig. 4C). Quantitative analysis of tibial plateau cartilage and calcified cartilage areas revealed that PBS-treated group had decreased cartilage area but increased calcified cartilage compared to Sham group, indicative of cartilage loss and calcification (Fig. 4B). Both EV and EVs@SIRT6 treatments increased cartilage area compared with PBS. Notably, EVs@SIRT6 reduced calcified cartilage area, while EVs showed no obvious effect. At week 8, histological and scoring results mirrored those observed at week 4, with EVs@SIRT6 consistently outperforming EVs in mitigating cartilage loss and calcification (Fig. 4D).

Additionally, aggrecan (AGC) expression reduced and MMP13, IL-6, and TNF-α expression elevated in the PBS group after 4 weeks, reflecting impaired anabolism, enhanced catabolism, and intensified inflammation (Fig. 5A). Both EVs and EVs@SIRT6 upregulated AGC level and decreased MMP13/IL-6/TNF-α levels, with EVs@SIRT6 exerting stronger effects (Fig. 5B), and these trends persisted until week 8 (Fig. 5C, D). Thus, EVs@SIRT6 exhibited superior therapeutic efficacy in attenuating cartilage damage by promoting anabolism, inhibiting catabolism, and reducing inflammation.

EVs@SIRT6 inhibits subchondral osteosclerosis and osteophyte formation

Micro-CT was used to assess subchondral bone remodeling and osteophyte formation. At 4 weeks post-treatments, PBS-treated rats exhibited significantly higher subchondral bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and subchondral bone plate thickness (SCBP.Th), along with lower trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) compared to the Sham group, indicative of subchondral osteosclerosis (Fig. 6A, B). Osteophyte volume and maximum osteophyte area were also markedly increased in the PBS group, confirming successful induction of knee OA characteristics. Treatment with EVs or EVs@SIRT6 attenuated these changes. Both EV groups had lower BV/TV than the PBS group at week 4, with EVs@SIRT6 inducing a more pronounced reduction. The EVs@SIRT6-treated rats exhibited significantly decreased Tb.Th and increased Tb.Sp compared to both PBS and EVs treated groups, indicating superior relief of subchondral osteosclerosis. Both EV groups reduced osteophyte volume and area versus PBS, withEVs@SIRT6 demonstrating stronger inhibition. No significant differences in SCBP.Th were observed among groups at week 4.

By week 8, the PBS group showed further progression of OA, with elevated BV/TV, Tb.Th, SCBP.Th, osteophyte volume, and maximum osteophyte area relative to Sham (Fig. 6C, D). EVs@SIRT6 continued to outperform EVs, which had the lowest BV/TV and maximum osteophyte area, followed by EVs and PBS. Tb.Th and osteophyte volume were significantly reduced in EVs@SIRT6 versus PBS, with no difference from EVs. EVs also reduced Tb.Th compared to PBS, but Tb.Sp and SCBP.Th remained unchanged across groups. Collectively, EVs@SIRT6 effectively alleviated subchondral osteosclerosis and inhibited osteophyte formation, displaying superior therapeutic effects than EVs.

Discussion

OA, a prevalent degenerative joint disease, presents significant treatment challenges. MSC-derived EVs have been emerging as a forefront therapy for OA due to their immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and tissue repair capabilities [22]. However, the clinical application of hUCMSCs is hindered by two bottlenecks. First, their in vitro proliferative capacity is limited, often entering senescence after 10–15 passages as a result of telomere shortening, leading to reduced EVs production and batch-to-batch instability [23]. Second, donor-dependent heterogeneity in EV composition and functional activity contributes to inconsistent therapeutic efficacy [24]. In this study, we engineered hUCMSCs (SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs) capable of stable passage beyond 60 generations through the synergistic overexpression of TERT and SIRT6. EVs@SIRT6 retained the characteristic morphology and marker protein expression of natural EVs, with multidimensional enhancements in the treatment efficacy of OA.

TERT overexpression addresses the challenge of senescence and functional decline during the in vitro expansion of primary MSCs [25]. SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs maintained stable trilineage differentiation potential and EV secretion functionality over extended passages. Achieving immortalization while preserving the characteristics of primary cells may represent a promising therapeutic strategy. In agreement with Brancolini et al., a single donor source was used to minimize donor-to-donor variability, thereby facilitating greater consistency and standardization of EV production for potential therapeutic applications [26]. Furthermore, the selection strategy for a monoclonal SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSC cell line may reduce heterogeneity that can arise during polyclonal immortalization of primary cells [27]. EVs@SIRT6 were enriched with proteins associated with nuclear/chromatin repair, with SIRT6 protein levels threefold higher than in natural EVs. This engineering transformation not only overcomes the limitation of insufficient primary EVs yield but also enhances their anti-apoptotic, proliferative, and pro-migratory capabilities of EVs@SIRT6 through SIRT6-mediated signaling [28], providing a standardized approach to addressing the heterogeneity issue in clinical applications.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that TERT-immortalized cells generally retain primary characteristics similar to those of their parental cells [29–31]. Recent research has further shown that TERT-overexpressing immortalized MSCs preserve the therapeutic potential of their derived EVs, including anti-inflammatory and wound-healing activities [26]. In contrast, cartilage-specific SIRT6 deficiency has been reported to accelerate chondrocyte senescence and OA progression [17]. In our study, EVs@SIRT6 exhibited superior therapeutic effects compared with EVs, including suppression of inflammation, oxidative stress, and ECM dysregulation in chondrocytes, as well as attenuation of subchondral osteosclerosis and osteophyte formation in rats. Therefore, we hypothesize that the therapeutic efficacy of EVs@SIRT6 may be attributable to the transcellular delivery of the SIRT6 and its associated proteome via EVs. Previous studies have reported that SIRT6 participates in the regulation of immune responses, cellular metabolism, and ECM homeostasis, thereby potentially influencing the pathological microenvironment of OA [32, 33]. For example, SIRT6 markedly reduces the expression of IL-1β induced pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, while upregulating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, likely by inhibiting the nuclear translocation of the NF-κB subunit p65 and the phosphorylation of JAK2 kinase within the JAK-STAT pathway, thereby restoring the “anti-inflammatory-repair” balance [34–36]. In case of metabolic regulation, SIRT6 may enhance mitochondrial complexes I and IV activity, suppress ROS production, downregulate key glycolytic enzymes such ashexokinase 2 (HK2) and phosphofructokinase platelet (PFKP), and improves the chondrocyte energy metabolism [37, 38]. This intervention delays glycolytic dependence and matrix degradation induced by inflammation. Regarding ECM homeostasis, SIRT6 has been shown to directly interact with the SOX9 gene promoter, facilitating the synthesis of COL2A1 and ACAN, and simultaneously inhibiting the expression of MMP13, ADAMTS5, and other catabolic enzymes, thereby preventing the degradation of the cartilage matrix [39]. Based on these previous reports, the protective effects of EVs@SIRT6 in OA may be mediated through the regulation of the osteoblast-osteoclast balance or the subchondral bone microenvironment. Further investigation will be necessary to elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms of EVs@SIRT6 in OA and to clarify the specific contributions of SIRT6 through the inclusion of EVs derived from TERT-only hUCMSCs as additional controls.

While TERT overexpression offers a reliable source for large-scale EVs production, the safety issue requires thorough evaluation. SIRT6/TERT-hUCMSCs preserved a normal 46, XY (or 46,XX) diploid karyotype across 60 passages, exhibiting no chromosomal abnormalities or structural rearrangements, and their tri-lineage differentiation potential remained comparable to that of parental cells. Nevertheless, the potential links between telomerase hyperactivation and tumorigenesis require attention [40, 41]. It is imperative to investigate whether TERT can indirectly influence EVs components by modulating autophagy, vesicular secretion pathways, or metabolic phenotypes.

From a translational perspective, this study presents several unresolved questions. Regarding the mechanism of action, a translational perspective is highly expressed in EVs@SIRT6 and effectively endocytosed by chondrocytes, it remains uncertain whether it is delivered with intact enzymatic activity. The specific deacetylation modification targets within the receptor cells, such as histone H3K9 and H3K56, and the downstream signaling cascades, including the regulation of the p16INK4a/p21CIP1 pathway, require precise elucidation [42–44]. In addition, current in vivo pharmacokinetic properties of EVs@SIRT6, including joint cavity retention time and the efficiency of capsule/cartilage enrichment, remain unclear. Advanced imaging techniques, such as near-infrared fluorescence or PET combined with targeted ligands, could facilitate quantification of EV biodistribution, enabling tissue-specific delivery and minimizing systemic exposure could facilitate the development of tissue-specific delivery systems, thereby enhancing treatment precision and minimizing systemic side effects. At the level of clinical transformation, rigorous quality controls should be established to encompass the particle size distribution of EVs, marker protein expression, and biological activity. Further, toxicology experiments adhering to Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) standards should be conducted. Future emphasis should be placed on evaluating safety, batch-to-batch consistency, and potential long-term organ effects.

This study underscores the scientific significance of a “donor cell gene transfer-EVs enhancement” strategy. The EVs@SIRT6 exhibited multiple therapeutic benefits both in vitro and in vivo, confirming the multifaceted role of SIRT6 as a druggable target in OA therapy. The approach established a comprehensive pathological intervention network addressing synovial inflammation inhibition, chondrocyte metabolism regulation, ECM homeostasis, and overall joint protection. More broadly, our findings introduce a novel concept for disease-modifying strategy in the treatment of age-related diseases: enhancing donor cell functionality through gene transfer to subsequently augment EV therapeutic efficacy. Future research could explore combining EVs@SIRT6 with injectable hydrogels [45], biological scaffolds [46], and other materials to prolong therapeutic effects within the joint cavity by establishing a sustained-release microenvironment. Moreover, CRISPR-mediated knockout of pro-inflammatory genes and overexpression of reparative factors may enable the development of next-generation engineered EVs with enhanced efficacy. Given the global rise in aging populations, OA has emerged as a significant cause of disability. Our findings provide a robust foundation for safe and effective EVs-based therapeutics, potentially facilitating the transition of related treatments from the laboratory to clinical settings, offering more precise and long-lasting treatment options, thereby improving the quality of life for patients.

Conclusions

EVs@SIRT6 outperforms EVs in the treatment of OA, exhibiting enhanced efficacy through multi-pathway regulation, including the inhibition of inflammation, cartilage protection, and ECM remodeling. These data provide novel insights into the pathogenesis of OA and establish an efficient platform for engineered EV production, thereby laying a crucial foundation for precise EV-based therapy.
