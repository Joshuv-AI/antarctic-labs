Storage temperature modulates fruit quality, volatile compound profiles, and primary metabolite dynamics in ‘Shijizhixing’ peach
Authors: Yunxiao Feng, Nan Zhang, Jingang He, Yudou Cheng, Junfeng Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Storage temperature critically governs the metabolome and postharvest quality dynamics of fruits. In this study, ‘Shijizhixing’ peach (Prunus persica L. cv. Shijizhixing) fruits were stored at 0, 10, and 20 °C to investigate the temperature-dependent changes in physiology and metabolomics. Storage at 0 °C significantly attenuated the decreases in peel lightness (L*), firmness, juice yield, and decay incidence. In contrast, 10 and 20 °C promoted fruit softening, accompanied by an increase in water-soluble and chelate-soluble pectin while a decrease in covalently bound pectin. GC–MS identified 29 volatiles, among which aldehydes such as (E)-2-hexenal and hexanal were the predominant components at harvest, whereas fruity aroma compounds, including (Z)-3-hexenyl acetate and γ-undecalactone, accumulated markedly during storage at high temperatures. Primary metabolomic profiling revealed that lipid-derived metabolites, notably lysophosphatidylcholine and lysophosphatidylethanolamine, were strongly positively correlated with ester and lactone biosynthesis. Overall, storage temperature markedly influences postharvest quality and aroma by coordinating cell-wall disassembly and lipid-mediated volatile biosynthesis.

Introduction

Peach (Prunus persica L.) is a highly valued fruit prized by consumers for its rich nutritional profile, intense aroma, balanced sweetness and acidity, and juicy texture. However, peach fruits rapidly ripen and deteriorate during ambient temperatures storage and are highly susceptible to microbial infection (Kong et al., 2023). Most peach cultivars have a short shelf life of only 3–5 days. Low-temperature storage is one of the most widely applied strategies for maintaining postharvest peach quality. Nevertheless, peach fruits are sensitive to low temperatures, for example, long-term cold storage may induce chilling injury (CI), such as internal browning, mealiness, juice loss, and flavor deterioration (Lurie, 2021).

Fruit flavor is a critical quality attribute that influences consumer preferences and market competitiveness. The flavor of fruits is determined primarily by volatile aroma compounds. More than 100 volatile aroma compounds, including esters, lactones, alcohols, ketones, aldehydes, and terpenoids, have been identified in peach fruits (Liu, Bi, & Chen, 2025; Liu, Guo, et al., 2025; Liu, Zhang, et al., 2025). C6 aldehydes, including hexanal, (Z)-3-hexenal, and (E)-2-hexenal, contribute to green aromas. Esters are associated with fruity aromas, whereas lactones contribute to peach-like and sweet flavors (Liu, Bi, & Chen, 2025; Liu, Guo, et al., 2025; Liu, Zhang, et al., 2025). Further studies indicated that esters were the predominant volatile compounds among 42 peach cultivars (Mohammed et al., 2021), whereas β-ionone was identified as the major contributor to the aroma profiles of minority peach fruits (Zhang, Guo, et al., 2022; Zhang, Liu, et al., 2022). Moreover, numerous studies have demonstrated that storage temperature significantly influences fruit flavor. In ‘Red Haven' peach fruit, cold storage reduces the accumulation of lactones, esters, and terpenoids, thereby suppressing aroma development (Farcuh & Hopfer, 2023). Melting peach flesh showed lower levels of volatile compounds, especially esters and lactones, during storage at 5 °C than at 8 °C (Zhang et al., 2011).

Metabolites play a pivotal role in shaping fruit taste and flavor. Sugars and organic acids serve as the primary determinants of sweetness and acidity, respectively, whereas fatty acids and amino acids act as key precursors for volatile aroma compounds, which define fruit flavor characteristics (Zhang, Bai, et al., 2024; Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2024). Metabolomics, a discipline employing high-throughput chemical analysis techniques, enables the comprehensive qualitative and quantitative assessment of small-molecule metabolites within biological cells or tissues. This powerful approach has been extensively applied in research on the metabolism, stress response, and postharvest physiology of fruits and vegetables. In honey peach, stimulated carbohydrate metabolism, degradation of cell wall components, and accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) might be the key factors contributing to peach softening (Kong et al., 2023).

‘Shijizhixing’ peach (Prunus persica L. cv. Shijizhixing), a cold-tolerant cultivar widely cultivated in northern China, was derived from a hybrid cross between ‘Jiucui’ and ‘21Shiji’ (Xia et al., 2024) and exhibited a uniquely intense fruity-floral aroma profile, with odor activity values (OAVs) that surpassed those of either parent (Hao et al., 2022). This study aimed to investigate the effects of different storage temperatures on fruit quality, volatile aroma profiles, and primary metabolite dynamics in ‘Shijizhixing’ peach, thereby providing theoretical support for selecting appropriate storage temperatures and developing effective postharvest management strategies to preserve fruit flavor and quality. To achieve this goal, we employed gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) and metabolomics to study the physiological and biochemical alterations during fruit storage at three temperatures (0, 10, and 20 °C). Furthermore, correlation analysis was performed to reveal the potential metabolic basis underlying aroma changes during storage. The findings provide new insights into the mechanisms regulating postharvest changes of quality and volatile compound in peach fruit and offer theoretical guidance for optimizing storage strategies to preserve fruit flavor and quality.

Materials and methods

Materials and treatments

‘Shijizhixing’ peach fruits were harvested in Shenzhou, Hebei Province (latitude 38.00° N, longitude 115.56° E) on August 28, 2022. After being transported to the laboratory, the fruits were kept overnight at room temperature (25 ± 1 °C). On the following day, fruits of uniform size, maturity, free from mechanical damage, pests, and diseases, were selected and randomly divided into three groups (150 fruits per group) and stored at 0 ± 0.5 °C, 10 ± 0.5 °C, and 20 ± 0.5 °C, respectively. Fruit quality was evaluated at three-day intervals. Each sampling was performed in triplicate, with ten fruits per replicate. The flesh samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Fruit quality analysis

Basic fruit quality: Fruit color was measured at two symmetrical points on the fruit equator via a colorimeter (CR-400, MINOLTA, Japan) and recorded as the CIE L*, a*, b* color space. The fruit firmness was measured at two symmetrical positions along the suture line after peel removal via a fruit hardness tester fitted with a 7.9-mm diameter probe (Model GY-4, Zhejiang Top Cloud-Agri Technology, China). The fruit firmness was represented as ‘N'. The soluble solids content (SSC) was measured via a digital hand-held refractometer (Model PAL-1, ATAGO, Japan) on juice hand-pressed from two opposing slices each fruit.

Juice yield determination: The peach fruits were cored, sliced into small pieces, and juiced using a juicer (HU24FR3L, Hurom, China). The juice yield was calculated via the following formula:Juice yield%=juice weight÷fruit weight×100

Decay measurement: The decay incidence was calculated as the percentage of decayed fruits relative to the total number of fruits in each treatment group.

Pectin content determination

The pectin content was determined according to the method described by Fishman et al. (1993). Briefly, 5 g of flesh powder was homogenized with 12 mL of 80% (v/v) ethanol, incubated at 80 °C for 20 min, and then centrifuged at 8000g for 10 min. The residue was subsequently treated with 12 mL of a methanol: chloroform mixture (1:1, v/v) to eliminate proteins and lipids. After centrifugation, the residue was washed sequentially with 80% ethanol and aqueous acetone and dried at 45 °C in a hot-dry oven to a constant weight. The dried cell wall material was then subjected to isolation of different pectin fractions. First, water-soluble pectin (WSP) was extracted by incubating the sample with 10 mL of distilled water at 30 °C for 2 h, followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant solution. Next, chelate-soluble pectin (CSP) was obtained by extracting the residue with 10 mL of 50 mmol L-1 1,2-cyclohexylenedinitrilotetraacetic acid (CDTA) buffer (pH 6.5) for 6 h. Finally, sodium carbonate-soluble pectin (SSP) was extracted by treating the remaining residue with 10 mL of 50 mmol L-1 Na₂CO₃ containing 2 mmol L-1 CDTA at 4 °C for 12 h, with the supernatant collected after centrifugation. The pectin content was determined via the colorimetric carbazole method (Bitter & Muir, 1962), and the results were expressed as mg/g fresh weight.

Analysis of volatile compounds

Volatile compounds were extracted via solid-phase microextraction (SPME) (Liu et al., 2021). Approximately 4 g of frozen flesh powder was placed into a 15 mL headspace vial and mixed with 6 mL of saturated NaCl solution along with 0.2 mL of 1 mg/L 3-nonanone (internal standard). A SPME fiber coated with 65 μm PDMS/DVB (Supelco Co., Bellefonte, Pennsylvania, USA) was selected to extract volatile compounds according to the method (Xi et al., 2012). Before analysis the fiber was preconditioned at 240 °C for 7 min. Samples were initially equilibrated at 40 °C for 30 min, followed by headspace extraction with the SPME fiber for an additional 30 min at the same temperature. Subsequently, the fiber was desorbed in the GC–MS injector port (TQ8040, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) at 250 °C for 1 min in splitless mode. Volatile analysis was performed via an SH-Rxi-5Sil MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm) with helium as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 6 mL/min. The optimized oven program was as follows: the initial temperature was 40 °C (held for 1 min), the temperature was increased to 100 °C at 2 °C/min (held for 2 min), and then the temperature was increased to 250 °C at 4 °C/min (held for 5 min). The MS instrument was operated in electron ionization mode at 70 eV, with scanning in full-scan mode (m/z 35–500). The interface temperature was maintained at 250 °C. The volatile compounds were tentatively identified by matching their mass spectra against the NIST 17.0 database.

Semi-quantification of volatile compounds was performed using 3-nonanone as an internal standard (Liu et al., 2021). The relative concentration of volatile was calculated based on the peak area of each compound to the internal standard, based on the extracted ion chromatogram of the quantifying ion.

Primary metabolome analysis

A total of 50 mg of frozen flesh powder was extracted with 1200 μL of prechilled 70% methanol via vortex mixing and centrifuged at 12,000g for 3 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane prior to analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed on a UPLC-MS/MS system (ExionLC™ AD GX, SCIEX) equipped with an Agilent SB-C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm). The mobile phases consisted of (A) 0.1% formic acid in water (v/v) and (B) 0.1% formic acid in methanol (v/v). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–9 min, 95–5% A; 9–10 min, 5% A; 10–11.1 min, 5–95% A; and 11.1–14 min, 95% A. An injection volume of 2 μL with a 0.35 mL min-1 flow rate was used.

The MS conditions were as follows: source temperature, 500 °C; ion spray voltage, 5500 V (positive mode)/−4500 V (negative mode); ion source gas I, gas II, and curtain gas, 50, 60, and 25 psi, respectively; CAD, high; and detection in MRM mode.

Statistical analysis

All the data are presented as the means ± SD, with a minimum of three independent replicates. Statistical analyses were performed via one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's multiple range test (SPSS 16.0 software), with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) for metabolomics data was applied using online platforms (https://cloud.metware.cn/). MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) was used for performing metabolomic pathway analysis. The differentially abundant metabolites were selected via a combination of fold change (FC) and variable importance for projection (VIP) values obtained from OPLS-DA, and the metabolites were considered to be differentially expressed if they met the following criteria: FC > 2 or FC < 0.5 and VIP > 1. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships between the metabolite content and volatile aroma compounds across all the samples. An interaction relationship with a correlation coefficient > |0.9| and p < 0.05 was defined as significant and was visualized via Cytoscape software (3.10.2).

Results

Changes in peel color, SSC, juice yield, and decay incidence during storage

A higher L* value corresponds to a brighter color. During storage, the L* value decreased across all the treatments, with the most rapid decrease observed at 10 °C and 20 °C (Fig. 1A). In contrast, no significant changes were observed in the a* and b* values (Fig. 1B and C). With respect to the SSC, a slight decrease was observed on days 3 and 6, followed by an increase on days 9 and 12 in the fruits stored at 0 °C, but no significant changes were observed in those stored at 10 and 20 °C (Fig. 1D). The juice yield of the fruit stored at 10 and 20 °C increased during storage, whereas no significant change was detected in those stored at 0 °C (Fig. 1E). After 12 days of storage, decay was detected in the fruits stored at 10 °C and 20 °C, with decay rates reaching 23.33% and 40.74%, respectively (Fig. 1F). Effects on fruit firmness and pectin content during storage

A sharp decline in firmness was observed on day 3 in the fruits stored at 10 °C and 20 °C, whereas a small decline was observed only on day 12 at 0 °C (Fig. 2A). The WSP content increased during storage in all the treatments. However, the fruits stored at 0 °C presented obviously lower WSP contents than those stored at 10 °C and 20 °C (Fig. 2B). The CSP content increased gradually with prolonging storage time in the fruits stored at 10 °C and 20 °C, whereas it changed slightly at 0 °C (Fig. 2C). Effects of storage temperature on volatile compounds

The storage temperature significantly influenced both the composition and abundance of volatile compounds. For volatile contents, (E)-2-hexenal (B2), hexanal (B1), benzaldehyde (B3), and decane (O3) were the predominant components on day 0 (Table 1 and Fig. 3C). In fruits stored at 0 °C, the relative abundance of esters increased during storage (Fig. 3A). At 10 °C, the total volatile content markedly increased on day 12 (Fig. 3B). The relative content of alcohols first increased but then decreased, whereas the levels of lactones and esters, such as γ-unsecalactone, δ-decalactone, methyl benzoate, and methyl 2-methyl butyrate, gradually increased during storage (Fig. 3A and Table 1). In the fruits stored at 20 °C, the total aroma content markedly increased on day 3 and then remained relatively high level, in which the relative contents of lactones and esters increased gradually, but those of alcohols and aldehydes, including (E)-2-hexenal, hexanal, and 1-hexanol, decreased. Effects of storage temperature on primary metabolites

A total of 655 metabolites, including amino acids, nucleotides, lipids, sugars, vitamins, and organic acids, were identified in the flesh of ‘Shijizhixing’ peach fruit. PCA revealed that PC1 and PC2 accounted for 26.74% and 10.62% of the variance in the peach fruit datasets, respectively (Fig. 5A). The samples selected on days 3, 6, 9, and 12 from the fruits stored at 0 °C were clustered together (Fig. 5A), suggesting a similar metabolic profile to that of the low-temperature storage samples. However, the selected samples were scattered among the fruits stored at 10 °C and 20 °C, suggesting greater metabolic variation during storage. To further visualize the variance and clustering patterns within each treatment, separate PCA score plots were generated for fruit stored at 10 °C and 20 °C (Supplementary Fig. S1 and S2). At 10 °C, samples collected at 3 and 9 d of storage were grouped relatively closely, while those at 12 d were distinctly separated, indicating that substantial metabolic changes occurred during the late stage of storage. A total of 255 differentially abundant metabolites were identified in all the storage groups compared with the 0 °C group, and the number of differentially accumulated metabolites in the three different temperature storage groups tended to increase with prolonged storage (Fig. 5B). As shown in Fig. 5C, lipids accounted for the largest proportion of differentially abundant metabolites (31.76%), followed by amino acids, nucleotides, organic acids, and sugars, each comprising more than 10% of the total. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed that the differentially abundant metabolites in all the storage groups compared with those in the 0 °C group were enriched mainly in pathways involved in purine metabolism; valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis; butanoate metabolism; alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism; phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis; isoquinoline alkaloid biosynthesis; riboflavin metabolism; pentose and glucuronate interconversions; cysteine and methionine metabolism; and ascorbate and aldarate metabolism (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

Fruit quality

The decline in peel lightness (L*) reflected ripening, senescence, and water loss. In the present study, peel lightness decreased more slowly in fruit stored at 0 °C, in agreement with the results for yellow peach and ‘Venus’ nectarine (Shinya et al., 2014; Zhang, Guo, et al., 2022; Zhang, Liu, et al., 2022), indicating that low-temperature storage effectively maintained fruit freshness. The SSC slightly fluctuated, suggesting a balance between sugar accumulation and consumption during low-temperature storage. Notably, decay was observed on day 12 in fruit stored at 10 °C and 20 °C, whereas no visible decay occurred in fruit stored at 0 °C. This phenomenon coincided with the relatively stable primary metabolite profiles observed at 0 °C, while fruit stored at higher temperatures exhibited more pronounced metabolic alterations. These results suggest that maintaining metabolic homeostasis under low-temperature storage may contribute to delayed quality deterioration and extending postharvest storage life.

Firmness decreased more rapidly in fruits stored at 10 °C and 20 °C, accompanied by an increase in juice yield, indicating accelerated cell wall degradation. Fruit softening during ripening is generally accompanied by the degradation of protopectin and an increase in soluble pectin (Jiang, Xu, et al., 2024; Jiang, Ye, et al., 2024). Previous studies have indicated that WSP is loosely cross-linked in the cell wall by van der Waals forces, making it susceptible to solubilization and degradation (Wang, Li, et al., 2023; Wang, Lyu, et al., 2023). In contrast, CSP and SSP are bound to the cell wall by ionic and covalent bond, respectively (Wang, Li, et al., 2023; Wang, Lyu, et al., 2023). In this study, the decline in SSP content in the fruit stored at 10 °C and 20 °C likely accelerated pectin release from the primary cell wall, weakening the cell wall structure and thereby reducing firmness (Jiang, Xu, et al., 2024; Jiang, Ye, et al., 2024). In contrast, the CSP and SSP levels remained significantly unchanged at 0 °C. This result is consistent with findings in ‘Friar’ plums, in which low temperatures suppressed the conversion of insoluble SSP into soluble WSP, thereby helping to maintain fruit firmness (Pan et al., 2018).

Volatile aroma compound profiles

Aroma is an essential component of fruit sensory quality. Hundreds of volatile compounds, such as aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, esters, and lactones, have been detected in peach fruit (Liu, Bi, & Chen, 2025; Liu, Guo, et al., 2025; Liu, Zhang, et al., 2025). In general, aldehydes such as hexanal and (E)-2-hexenal are typically abundant in freshly harvested fruit and are associated with green aromas (Xiao et al., 2023). Esters and lactones, known for their fruity and floral aromas, tend to accumulate when fruits are stored at relatively high temperatures (Zhou et al., 2019). In the present study, the aldehyde and alcohol contents decreased during storage, whereas the ester, lactone, and ketone contents increased, reflecting the transition from a “green aroma” to a “fruity aroma”. Both ‘Shijizhixing’ and its parent cultivar ‘Jiucui’ contained abundant linalool, benzaldehyde, nonanal, (Z)-3-hexenyl acetate, and methyl octanoate. However, ‘Shijizhixing’ presented significantly higher levels of lactones, including γ-undecalactone, γ-caprolactone, and δ-decalactone, which were not detected in ‘Jiucui’ peach. This contributed to the more pronounced peach-like and sweet aroma profile of ‘Shijizhixing’ peach (Li et al., 2025). Additionally, honey peach cultivars such as ‘Chiyue’ and ‘Hujingmilu’ are characterized by key components such as butanal, isobutyl acetate, n-hexanol, and limonene (Cui et al., 2025).

The volatile profile of ‘Shijizhixing’ peach was markedly affected by storage temperature. The total aroma content remained low at 0 °C, suggesting that the formation of “fruity aroma” compounds, such as esters and lactones, was delayed and suppressed. This result is consistent with previous findings on Zhang et al. (2011) which reported that cold storage at 5 °C resulted in a low volatile aromatic content and suppressed the expression of genes involved in the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway in melting peach. Similarly, Li et al. (2025) reported that, compared with room temperature storage, low-temperature storage reduced volatile levels in ‘Jiucui’ peach. In contrast, esters and lactones such as (Z)-3-hexenyl acetate, γ-undecalactone, and γ-caprolactone noticeably increased during storage at 10 °C and 20 °C. This indicates a temperature-induced shift in volatile biosynthesis, likely through lipid-derived pathways (Li et al., 2024). Furthermore, Zhang, Bai, et al. (2024) and Zhang, Zhang, et al. (2024) reported that γ-decalactone and γ-dodecalactone are important contributors to the peach-like profile of honey peaches.

Connections between primary metabolism and volatile formation

Primary metabolites such as sugars, amino acids, organic acids, and lipids serve as precursors for aroma biosynthesis and reflect fruit physiological status (Yang et al., 2024). During storage, the volatile aroma compounds of peach fruit are generated primarily through four metabolic pathways: fatty acid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, phenylpropanoid/benzenoid biosynthesis, and terpenoid biosynthesis (Liu, Bi, & Chen, 2025; Liu, Guo, et al., 2025; Liu, Zhang, et al., 2025).

Fatty acid metabolism is a major source of fruity esters and lactones in peach fruit. C6 aldehydes, alcohols, and lactones are synthesized from linoleic and linolenic acids via the LOX pathway (Zhang et al., 2010). Initially, LOX converts these fatty acids into hydroperoxide isomers, which are subsequently cleaved by hydroperoxide lyase (HPL) to produce hexanal and hexenal. These aldehydes can then be reduced to the corresponding C6 alcohols. The final step is catalyzed by alcohol acyltransferase (AAT), which links an acyl moiety with an alcohol to form esters and is therefore directly responsible for ester production (Li et al., 2023). In this study, several alcohols, esters, and lactones, such as (E)-2-hexen-1-ol, hexanol, hexyl acetate, γ-caprolactone, and γ-octalactone, were detected, all of which contributed to fruity and peach-like aromas. These volatiles strongly positively correlated with lipid metabolites, including lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LysoPC) and lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LysoPE), suggesting a close association between lipid catabolism and aroma compound biosynthesis. The increased abundance of these lipid precursors (LysoPC and LysoPE) at 10 °C and 20 °C was consistent with the increased activity of the lipolytic and β-oxidation pathways, which provide essential substrates for lactone formation (Liu, Bi, & Chen, 2025; Liu, Guo, et al., 2025; Liu, Zhang, et al., 2025). These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that temperature-dependent variation in lactone production is likely regulated by alterations in the activity of long-chain acyl-CoA oxidase (ACX) and the expression of PpACX1 in peach fruit (Xi et al., 2012).

Phenylpropanoid and benzenoid metabolism are responsible for the synthesis of aromatic aldehydes and alcohols, such as benzaldehyde and methyl benzoate. These compounds are derived from phenylalanine via phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL)-mediated deamination (Huang et al., 2022). In this study, the higher level of phenylalanine observed under low-temperature storage may reflect a reduced metabolic flux through phenylpropanoid/benzenoid biosynthesis, suggesting that this pathway could be suppressed by cold storage. Consequently, the floral and almond-like aroma features contributed by benzaldehyde and methyl benzoate are altered.

Linalool is derived from the terpenoid pathway, in which glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and pyruvate serve as precursors for terpene synthase–mediated reactions (Pu et al., 2021). Although linalool is a minor contributor to the overall aroma profile of ‘Shijizhixing’ peach fruit, it is particularly abundant in nectarines and some juicy honey peach cultivars (Wang, Li, et al., 2023; Wang, Lyu, et al., 2023). Previous studies have identified linalool as a key aroma compound in Yulu honey peach fruits (Cui et al., 2025). Li et al. (2015) reported that linalool decreased from the commercial harvest maturity stage to the full maturity stage in ‘Hu Jing Mi Lu’ peach. Consistent with these findings, we observed a decline in linalool at 20 °C, whereas its level increased at 10 °C on days 3 and 6. These results suggest that storage at 10 °C may represent an optimal condition for linalool accumulation, thereby enhancing floral, sweet, and nectarine-like aroma characteristics.

Conclusion

Low-temperature storage (0 °C) maintained firmness, peel brightness, and primary metabolites while reduced the accumulation of fruity esters and lactones, likely by suppressing fatty acid-related metabolism. Higher storage temperatures (10 and 20 °C) accelerated pectin solubilization and lipid metabolism, therefore promoted the fruit softening, juicy and accumulation of aroma-active compounds. Overall, these findings provide guidance for optimizing postharvest storage strategies to balance aroma development with quality retention in peach fruit.
