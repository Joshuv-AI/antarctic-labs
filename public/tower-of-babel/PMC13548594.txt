SinTransNet: an EEG-based deep learning framework for infantile epileptic spasms syndrome detection
Authors: Junyuan Feng, Zhenzhen Liu, Linlin Shen, Xiaoling Luo, Yan Chen, Lin Li, Tian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infantile Epileptic Spasms Syndrome (IESS) represents a severe form of developmental epileptic encephalopathy in infancy, characterized by clusters of spasms and hypsarrhythmia patterns on electroencephalogram (EEG), which often lead to long-term neurodevelopmental impairments if not diagnosed promptly. The inherent non-stationarity and polymorphic complexity of EEG signals complicate interpretation, resulting in time-consuming and error-prone diagnostics that hinder timely therapeutic interventions. To address these challenges, we propose SinTransNet, an innovative EEG-based deep learning framework that combines multi-band signal decomposition, adaptive sinusoidal convolutions, and Transformer-based attention mechanism. This architecture decomposes EEG into five key frequency bands (δ, θ, α, β, γ) to isolate oscillatory features such as spike-and-wave complexes, employs sinusoidal convolutions for frequency-adaptive feature extraction, and utilizes Transformer attention to capture inter-band correlations and long-range dependencies essential for accurate IESS detection. Evaluated on a proprietary dataset comprising 129 EEG recordings with 1,941 epileptic spasm events, SinTransNet demonstrates superior performance with average accuracy of 85.69%, sensitivity of 80.55%, and specificity of 90.76%. By providing an automated, efficient tool for IESS identification, SinTransNet holds promise for enhancing clinical workflows and supporting early interventions in pediatric neurology.

Introduction

Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS), a severe developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, presents with epileptic spasms in infants aged 1 to 24 months, typically peaking between 3 and 12 months. The most frequently observed ictal behaviors in infantile spasms include symmetric flexion, extension, or a combination of both [1, 2]. During ictal events, electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings typically exhibit fast activity resembling spindles, high-voltage spikes, spike-and-wave complexes, slow waves, and attenuation [1, 2]. The interictal EEG is characterized by hypsarrhythmia, an abnormal pattern comprising disorganized, high-voltage slow waves and spikes [3]. The subtle, brief spasms and complex EEG patterns, including transient spike waves and cross-band interactions, pose unique challenges for IESS detection, necessitating early diagnosis to mitigate severe developmental consequences. Traditionally, IESS detection relies on labor-intensive visual EEG inspection, which is time-consuming, costly, and prone to human error. The implementation of automated IESS detection system holds the potential to significantly enhance the diagnostic process for clinicians, thereby improving diagnostic efficiency and accuracy.

Early studies on EEG-based epilepsy and IESS detection primarily relied on traditional machine learning (ML) frameworks, where handcrafted features were extracted from EEG recordings and subsequently classified using conventional classifiers such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forests (RF), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) [4–6]. Typical feature extraction strategies included time-domain statistical descriptors, spectral power analysis, entropy measures, and wavelet-based time-frequency representations. For example, Subasi et al. combined Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) with SVM optimized using Genetic Algorithms and Particle Swarm Optimization for seizure classification [7]. Aayesha et al. introduced Fuzzy Rough Nearest Neighbors to address EEG nonlinearity [8]. Omidvar et al. further employed multi-level Db4 wavelet decomposition for multi-scale EEG analysis [9], while Pattnaik et al. utilized Tunable-Q Wavelet Transform to improve time-frequency resolution [10]. In addition, Melek demonstrated that frequency-domain representations outperform purely time-domain features in KNN-based epilepsy classification, highlighting the importance of rhythmic EEG activity analysis [11]. Although these approaches achieved promising results in controlled settings, they heavily depend on manual feature engineering and expert prior knowledge. Consequently, they often struggle to capture the highly nonlinear, non-stationary, and cross-band coupling characteristics of IESS EEG signals, thereby limiting their generalization capability and clinical robustness.

The advent of deep learning has catalyzed progress in epilepsy detection, with Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) automating feature extraction from raw EEG data to discern intricate patterns [12, 13]. Wei et al., for example, devised a multi-view CNN incorporating Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN) for interictal epileptiform discharge detection [14], and Pan et al. fused raw EEG with FFT, STFT, and DWT in a hybrid time-frequency CNN for multi-domain integration [15]. Shanmugam et al. augmented CNN with LSTM for temporal modeling [16], while Liu et al.’s CosCNN employed cosine-based kernels to mitigate overfitting in lightweight designs [17]. Nonetheless, CNNs’ constrained local receptive fields impede their grasp of expansive seizure dynamics, limiting efficacy in capturing the global context vital for IESS.

Transformer architectures have recently surged as efficacious alternatives for EEG tasks, harnessing self-attention to model long-range dependencies and surmount CNN limitations [18–21]. He et al. proposed a sparse Transformer encoder to alleviate computational demands [22], Zhao et al. introduced CwA-T with channel-wise compression for efficient feature handling [23], and Yan et al.’s DARNet fused spatio-temporal convolutions and dual attention for bolstered dependency capture [24]. Yet, these models frequently overlook inter-band oscillatory linkages in brain rhythms, compromising performance in nuanced seizure scenarios for epileptic spasms.

Although general epilepsy research flourishes, IESS-targeted investigations are sparse. Das et al. applied SVM to differentiate IESS from akin disorders [25], Fan et al. automated the BASED scoring via multi-layer CNN [26], and Zheng et al. constructed PLV-based networks from five EEG sub-bands, training ResNet18 for detection [27]. These tailored efforts, while promising, often neglect seamless fusion of frequency-specific oscillations with global modeling, yielding suboptimal accuracy and robustness in clinical IESS contexts.

To address these challenges, we introduce SinTransNet, a novel deep learning framework designed to meet the unique demands of IESS detection in pediatric EEG analysis. Tailored to the neurophysiological complexity of IESS, SinTransNet integrates multi-band decomposition, sinusoidal convolution, Transformer-based attention, and a lightweight classifier, harmonizing local oscillatory signatures with global seizure dynamics. EEG signals are decomposed into five sub-band rhythms for capturing the diverse oscillatory patterns inherent to spasms. Sinusoidal convolutions, parameterized by amplitude, frequency, phase, and bias, adaptively extract band-specific features, addressing the non-stationary nature of EEG signals. These features are then processed as tokens by a Transformer-based attention mechanism, which models cross-band interactions and long-range dependencies critical for delineating IESS-specific patterns. Finally, a lightweight classifier generates the diagnostic output. Methodology

Overall architecture

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the proposed SinTransNet is a hierarchical deep learning architecture. The framework encompasses five interconnected modules: signal preprocessing, multi-band decomposition, sinusoidal convolution, Transformer encoding, and classification, forming a seamless pipeline for robust feature extraction and decision-making.Fig. 1The framework of SinTransNet, including a signal preprocessing module, a multi-band decomposition module, a sinusoidal convolution module, a Transformer encoding module, and a classification module

The framework of SinTransNet, including a signal preprocessing module, a multi-band decomposition module, a sinusoidal convolution module, a Transformer encoding module, and a classification module

The preprocessing module filters, re-references, and standardizes raw EEG inputs to ensure high-quality data. Subsequently, the multi-band decomposition module partitions the signals into five brain wave sub-bands, aligning with the frequency-specific manifestations of IESS. The sinusoidal convolution module then processes each sub-band adaptively, extracting nuanced oscillatory characteristics that reflect the dynamic nature of epileptic spasms. These localized features are tokenized and fed into the Transformer encoding module, which employs multi-head self-attention to model cross-band fusions and long-range dependencies, yielding comprehensive global representations. Finally, the classification module delivers a binary output, enabling reliable IESS identification suitable for clinical integration.

Signal preprocessing module

In this section, some steps are used to preprocess the raw EEG data to mitigate noise, enhance spatial patterns, and standardize amplitudes. First, a band-pass filtering in the range of 0.3 to 70 Hz is applied to remove low-frequency artifacts and high-frequency noise, while preserving the neurophysiological components relevant to IESS [28].

Next, the average reference montage reduces common-mode signals across channels, thereby enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio of localized brain activity. Multi-band decomposition module

The human brain consists of multiple specialized regions that interact to produce distinct brain wave rhythms [29], reflecting activity in functional areas. We decompose EEG signals into five frequency sub-bands: δ (0.3–4 Hz), θ (4–8 Hz), α (8–13 Hz), β (13–30 Hz), and γ (30–70 Hz). This is achieved using finite impulse response (FIR) band-pass filters, which provide linear phase response and precise frequency selectivity without phase distortion, preserving temporal patterns in IESS-related signals.

This balances sharpness in the transition band and computational efficiency while minimizing ripples in passband and stopband. Sinusoidal convolution module

Traditional convolution kernels lack explicit frequency-dependent training parameters, making it difficult to adapt to the non-stationary oscillations of EEG. The sinusoidal convolution module extracts localized oscillatory features from each sub-band of the EEG signal, tailored to the neurophysiological characteristics of IESS. Our design incorporates four learnable parameters, amplitude, frequency, phase offset, and bias, for superior adaptability to dynamic EEG patterns in pediatric epilepsy.

Transformer encoding module

The global patterns in EEG with IESS, such as hypsarrhythmia, a hallmark of abnormal brain activity [30], demand models that transcend local receptive fields to capture long-range dependencies and inter-band interactions [31]. The Transformer encoding module fulfills this by leveraging multi-head self-attention (MHSA) to fuse sub-band features into cohesive global representations, drawing from neural network dynamics to mirror epileptic propagation across frequencies.

Features from the sinusoidal convolution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times K \times C_{\mathrm{out}}}$$\end{document}) are restructured into a token sequence for efficient processing. For each sub-band k, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_k \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{out}}}$$\end{document} is divided into S=4 non-overlapping segments, each linearly projected to a token \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{k,s} \in \mathbb{R}^{E}$$\end{document} (E=32), reducing computational load while preserving oscillatory essence. The encoder comprises two stacked layers, each integrating MHSA and a feed-forward network (FFN) with layer normalization (LN) and residual connections for stable gradient flow. MHSA employs H=8 heads; for head h, scaled dot-product attention computes: 11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Attention}_h(Q_h, K_h, V_h) = \mathrm{softmax} \left( \frac{Q_h K_h^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}} \right) V_h,$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_h, K_h, V_h \in \mathbb{R}^{(1 + K \cdot S) \times d_h}$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_h = E/H = 4$$\end{document}) are projections. The concatenated MHSA output is: 12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{MHSA}(T\prime) = \mathrm{Concat}(\mathrm{Attention}_1, \ldots, \mathrm{Attention}_H) W^O,$$\end{document}

with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W^O \in \mathbb{R}^{E \times E}$$\end{document}. The sub-layer result is: 13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T^{(1)} = \mathrm{LN} \left( T\prime + \mathrm{MHSA}(T\prime) \right),$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{LN}(x) = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \cdot \gamma + \beta$$\end{document} normalizes embeddings (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu, \sigma$$\end{document}: mean/std; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma, \beta$$\end{document}: learnable).

The FFN applies two linear layers with LeakyReLU: 14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{FFN}(x) = \mathrm{LeakyReLU} \left( x W_1 + b_1 \right) W_2 + b_2,$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{E \times D}$$\end{document}, D=128; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times E}$$\end{document}; biases \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_1, b_2$$\end{document}), yielding: 15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T^{(2)} = \mathrm{LN} \left( T^{(1)} + \mathrm{FFN}(T^{(1)}) \right).$$\end{document}

Post two layers, the refined CLS token \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{\mathrm{cls}}\prime\prime \in \mathbb{R}^{E}$$\end{document} is extracted for downstream classification.

Classification module

The classification module decodes the CLS token, a comprehensive feature representation, to produce the final classification result for IESS detection.

The module receives the CLS token \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{\mathrm{cls}}\prime\prime \in \mathbb{R}^{E}$$\end{document}, where E=32 is the embedding dimension. A linear transformation maps it to classification logits: 16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z = t_{\mathrm{cls}}\prime\prime W + b,$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W \in \mathbb{R}^{E \times N}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$$\end{document}, N=2 is the number of classes (IESS or non-IESS), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times N}$$\end{document} contains the logits for each sample in the batch B. The softmax function converts logits to probabilities: 17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{b,i} = \mathrm{softmax}(z)_{b,i} = \frac{\exp(z_{b,i})}{\sum_{j=1}^N \exp(z_{b,j})},$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_{b,i}$$\end{document} is the logit for sample b and class i, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{b,i} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times N}$$\end{document} is the predicted probability for sample b and class i, and the predicted label is the class with the highest probability. The cross-entropy loss is used to train the SinTransNet framework: 18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^B \sum_{i=1}^N \hat{y}_{b,i} \log(y_{b,i}),$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_{b,i} \in \{0, 1\}$$\end{document} is the ground-truth label for sample b and class i.

Evaluation metrics

For evaluation, four metrics are computed: accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified samples: 19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Accuracy} = \frac{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{TN} + \mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{FN}},$$\end{document}

where TP, TN, FP, and FN are the true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively. Sensitivity (or recall) quantifies the true positive rate for the IESS class: 20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Sensitivity} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}}.$$\end{document}

Specificity measures the true negative rate for the non-IESS class: 21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Specificity} = \frac{\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TN} + \mathrm{FP}}.$$\end{document}

The AUC represents the area under the ROC curve, which plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate across varying classification thresholds. It quantifies the model’s ability to distinguish IESS from non-IESS samples by ranking their predicted probabilities, with a value of 1 indicating perfect discrimination and 0.5 indicating no discriminative ability.

Dataset

Data source

The proprietary dataset was collected by Shenzhen Children’s Hospital, specifically designed for IESS detection in pediatric patients. Data were recorded in a controlled hospital EEG monitoring ward using a Compumedics Greal EEG system with dry disk electrodes. The study received ethical approval from the Shenzhen Children’s Hospital Ethics Committee (approval number 202305802), with informed consent obtained from legal guardians.

The dataset comprises 129 multi-session EEG recordings from 10 pediatric subjects diagnosed with IESS based on EEG and clinical features. While the subject size is limited, this reflects the rare nature of IESS, with an incidence of approximately 2 per 10,000 infants [32]. The specialized clinical requirements and strict diagnostic criteria further constrain data availability. Nevertheless, the comprehensive multi-session recordings per subject provide sufficient data for developing clinically meaningful detection algorithms. EEG analysis identified background activity, interictal epileptiform discharges, and ictal patterns. Clinically, subjects exhibited epileptic spasms and psychomotor developmental delay (PDD), with additional comorbidities such as CDKL5 deficiency disorder (CDD), glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency (G6PD), or neonatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), confirmed by pediatric neurologists. Data segmentation and splitting

To ensure robust model training and evaluation, the dataset was preprocessed by the signal preprocessing module, subjected to segmentation into decision windows, and subsequently split into training, validation, and test sets. Considering the predominantly brief duration of most seizure episodes, to augment seizure samples, 0.5-second decision windows with 0.1-second overlapping sliding were applied specifically to seizure segments within each EEG recording. Concurrently, an equivalent number of non-overlapping 0.5-second non-seizure decision windows were selected to balance the dataset. To prevent data leakage, the dataset was partitioned by subject into training, validation, and test sets in a ratio of 7:1:2, yielding 24,128 training windows, 3,484 validation windows, and 8,244 test windows.

Results

Implementation details

The model was implemented in Python 3.8 with PyTorch 1.9.0, with training accelerated through an NVIDIA V100 GPU (32GB VRAM). The Adam optimizer was adopted with learning rates of 1e-4. Meanwhile, a batch size of 128 and a maximum of 50 training epochs were configured, and the early stopping strategy is adopted. The training will stop if the loss function value on the validation set does not decrease for 10 consecutive epochs, and the model parameters corresponding to the lowest validation loss will be restored.

To further demonstrate the training stability and robustness of SinTransNet, we present the training and validation accuracy curves in Fig. 2. Discussion

The development and evaluation of SinTransNet demonstrate the potential of IESS-related spasm detection using EEG signals. By integrating multi-band EEG decomposition, the sinusoidal convolution module, and the Transformer encoder module, SinTransNet is able to capture both local oscillatory features and global seizure patterns in non-stationary and polymorphically complex EEG signals, providing an assistive approach for supporting neurologists in reviewing complex pediatric EEG recordings.

Compared to previous approaches, SinTransNet addresses several key limitations in the literature. While conventional CNN-based methods (e.g., CosCNN [17], LMDA-NET [21]) are effective at local feature extraction, they lack the capability to model long-range dependencies and cross-frequency interactions essential for hypsarrhythmia patterns. Existing Transformer-based models (e.g., EEG Conformer [19], DARNet [24]) capture global context but often overlook the frequency-specific non-stationarity inherent in IESS signals. In contrast, our method combines multi-band decomposition, a sinusoidal convolution module with jointly learnable amplitude, frequency, phase, and bias parameters for frequency-adaptive local modeling, and Transformer attention for global integration. This design enables more flexible characterization of non-stationary oscillatory patterns while preserving physiologically meaningful sub-band structures.

From a clinical perspective, balancing sensitivity and specificity is particularly important in automated IESS detection. SinTransNet achieved a sensitivity of 80.55% and a specificity of 90.76%, indicating its potential as an assistive screening and decision-support tool rather than a standalone diagnostic system. Higher sensitivity may help reduce missed spasms, which is critical in pediatric neurology, while high specificity can reduce unnecessary alarms and manual EEG review burden. Compared with PLV-ResNet18 [27], which incorporates phase-locking information but exhibits lower sensitivity, SinTransNet showed improved detection performance for subtle epileptic spasms while maintaining a relatively low false-positive rate.

The interpretability analysis further supports the clinical plausibility of the proposed framework. Grad-CAM results indicate that SinTransNet exhibited stronger activations in the low-frequency delta and theta bands for IESS samples, with relatively sparse responses in the beta and gamma bands. This pattern is consistent with reported EEG characteristics of IESS, including slow-wave abnormalities and transient epileptiform discharges. Such interpretability may facilitate clinical adoption, as decision-support systems should not only provide predictions but also offer evidence that can be reviewed by clinicians.

The proprietary IESS dataset provides a robust foundation for training and evaluating automated detection algorithms. Unlike public datasets such as CHB-MIT [36] and Bonn [37], which are limited by low sampling rates and lack annotations for epileptic spasms. The proprietary dataset includes 1,941 epileptic spasm events with high density and high precision annotations, capturing the transient and subtle nature of IESS seizures. The high resolution of this dataset and the focus on IESS-specific characteristics enable robust training and evaluation, rendering the dataset suitable for developing IESS-related spasm detection algorithms and supporting clinical decision making.

Nevertheless, several limitations remain. Although SinTransNet achieves a favorable efficiency profile with only 0.616 M parameters and an average inference time of 4.57 ms per 0.5-s window, the Transformer-based architecture still incurs relatively higher computational cost compared to purely convolutional models. This may limit its deployment in extremely resource-constrained bedside or portable EEG devices for prolonged continuous monitoring. In addition, the relatively small patient cohort may restrict generalization to broader populations. Future work will explore multi-center external validation and subject-level evaluation strategies such as leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) to further assess robustness. To improve generalization under limited data conditions, knowledge-guided representation learning and multimodal fusion with complementary physiological signals (e.g., EMG or video) will also be investigated. Finally, model compression techniques, including pruning and quantization, along with data augmentation strategies, offer promising directions for even more efficient and clinically deployable IESS detection systems.

Conclusion

This study presents SinTransNet, a deep learning framework for automated detection of IESS. By combining sinusoidal convolution for adaptive extraction of local oscillatory features with Transformer-based attention for modeling global seizure dynamics, SinTransNet effectively addresses the non-stationarity and polymorphic complexity of pediatric EEG. Validated on a clinically annotated proprietary dataset, it outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, demonstrating strong potential to reduce diagnostic delays and enable timely therapeutic interventions. By bridging local and global EEG representations, SinTransNet enhances diagnostic precision and offers a practical foundation for integrating automated EEG analysis into pediatric clinical workflows.
