Finite Sentence-Interface Control for Learning Bounded-Fan-Out Linear MCFGs under Fixed Monoid Typing
Takayuki Kuriyama

Abstract
We study positive-data learning of bounded-fan-out linear multiple context-free grammars under a fixed explicit finite monoid homomorphism h h . The main obstacle beyond the context-free case is that an MCFG nonterminal derives a tuple whose components may be placed in a surrounding sentence in different orders. We introduce sentence-interface types as finite external control objects for such tuple occurrences. A type records the permutation of tuple components in the final sentence together with the h h -values of the boundary intervals between them. For reduced working binary linear nondeleting MCFG presentations whose string languages satisfy ( f , h ) (f,h) -tuple substitutability, we build a typed refinement, a finite characteristic sample, and a canonical positive-data learner. Once the sample contains this characteristic sample and remains contained in the target language, the learner reconstructs the language exactly. Consequently, for fixed fan-out bound f f and fixed explicit h h , the resulting class is identifiable in the limit from positive data. Moreover, the hypothesis associated with any given finite sample is constructible in polynomial time for fixed f f and fixed h h , including output size. Thus sentence-interface control is the finite mechanism that lifts fixed- h h distributional reconstruction from context-free grammars to bounded-fan-out linear MCFGs. Keywords. grammatical inference, multiple context-free grammars, linear multiple context-free grammars, finite monoids, sentence-interface types, identification in the limit

Introduction

Learning grammars from positive data alone requires finite information that
can eventually be observed in every complete text for the target language.
Gold’s theorem rules out broad language classes in this setting

[
9
]
, while query-learning models such as Angluin’s regular-set
algorithm obtain power from active information
[
3
]
. A
standard positive-data strategy is therefore to restrict the distributional
behavior of substrings or tuples so that finitely many observed contexts are
enough to determine the whole language. This perspective underlies the
Clark–Eyraud line for context-free grammars
[
4
]
,
Yoshinaka’s substitutability-based work for context-free and mildly
context-sensitive languages
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
,
and later developments for multiple context-free grammars with Clark

[
5
,
6
]
. Kanazawa’s monograph on
learnable classes of categorial grammars provides another background example
in which restrictions on grammatical structure lead to positive-data
learnability through finite witnessing information, compatible with the
characteristic-sample viewpoint used here
[
11
]
.

The present paper asks what finite local information is needed when the
grammar formalism is a bounded-fan-out linear MCFG. MCFGs are a standard
mildly context-sensitive formalism
[
14
,
15
,
13
,
10
]
; their nonterminals derive tuples rather
than single strings. Thus the external environment of a nonterminal
occurrence is not just a two-sided context. The tuple components may be
scattered through the final sentence, and their left-to-right placement can
change from one occurrence to another. Componentwise information such as

h(w_{1}),\ldots,h(w_{d})
is therefore not enough to describe the role of a

d
-tuple occurrence in a sentence.

Our finite control object is the
sentence-interface type
. For an
arity-
d
sentence context, it records the permutation in which the

d
holes appear and the
h
-values of the
d+1
boundary intervals
between and around them. Because both the fan-out bound
f
and the
finite monoid
M
are fixed, the resulting interface space

S_{d}\times M^{d+1}
is finite for each
d\leq f
. The permutation
component is essential for the reconstruction proof: it records how a
parent rule routes a child tuple into the surrounding sentence, while the
boundary entries provide the finite monoid data needed for substitutability.

The semantic distributional assumption is
(f,h)
-tuple substitutability.
It compares tuples only when they have the same componentwise
h
-type;
under that equality, sharing one accepting sentence context forces equality
of all accepting sentence contexts. Yoshinaka’s multidimensional
substitutability is stronger as a distributional implication because it does
not require equality of componentwise
h
-types. The fixed-
h
setting
therefore gives a different reconstruction problem: the learner is not
trying to infer the finite monoid, but to use the prescribed finite monoid
as external control for typed reconstruction.

Technically, the paper proceeds from finite interface control to exact
reconstruction. Given a reduced working binary linear nondeleting MCFG
presentation of fan-out at most
f
, we refine each nonterminal by both
its componentwise output
h
-type and its sentence-interface type. The
transport maps induced by binary rule templates determine the interface of
each child occurrence from the parent interface and the sibling output type.
After trimming to productive and reachable typed copies, we extract a finite
characteristic sample exposing every surviving typed state and every
realized typed rule. The canonical learner rebuilds rules from observed
typed tuples and concrete binary witnesses. Soundness is supplied by

(f,h)
-tuple substitutability; completeness is supplied by the exposed
typed refinement.

This paper is related to the author’s fixed-
h
reconstruction for CFGs

[
1
,
2
]
, but the present contribution is
not merely a notational lift. In the CFG case an occurrence has an ordinary
two-sided context. In the MCFG case the external state must additionally
control the sentence-level placement of tuple components. Sentence-interface
types are introduced to isolate precisely this new finite object.

The theorem should be read as a fixed-parameter positive-data
reconstruction result. The fan-out bound
f
, the explicit finite
homomorphism
h\colon\Sigma^{*}\to M
, and the working binary linear
nondeleting presentation discipline are fixed in advance. Within this
regime, the learner reconstructs the target language once a finite
characteristic sample has appeared and constructs the current hypothesis in
polynomial time from the finite sample at hand. The point is therefore not
to infer the finite monoid or to normalize arbitrary MCFG presentations, but
to show that sentence-interface types provide enough finite external control
for exact reconstruction under the fixed-
h
discipline.

The contributions are as follows.

(i)

We introduce sentence-interface types
S_{d}\times M^{d+1}
as finite
external control states for tuple occurrences in bounded-fan-out linear
MCFGs under fixed monoid typing.

(ii)

We define rule-template transport maps that propagate these types from

h
-type.

(iii)

We construct the typed refinement and prove the associated typing

(iv)

We extract a finite characteristic sample exposing typed states and
realized typed rules, and define a canonical positive-data learner.

(v)

We prove exact reconstruction, identification in the limit, and
polynomial-time construction of the hypothesis from a given finite sample
for fixed
f
and fixed explicit
h

2
Main Results

This section records the theorem package before the technical development.
The definitions needed for the fully formal statements are introduced in the
next sections, but the hypotheses and the strength of the conclusions are
already fixed here.

Theorem 2.1

(Finite sentence-interface control, informal)
.

Fix a fan-out bound

h

The formal form of this statement is Proposition
7.11

(Exact reconstruction, preview)

Fix

. Let

The fully formal statement is Theorem

For fixed

For fixed

3
Related Work and Positioning

The present paper belongs to the positive-data branch of grammatical
inference. Gold’s theorem shows that broad language classes cannot be
identified from positive data alone
[
9
]
, while Angluin’s
regular-set algorithm illustrates the additional power obtained from active
queries
[
3
]
. In the positive-data setting, a common way to
recover learnability is to impose a distributional or structural restriction
that yields finite tell-tales or characteristic samples; see also de la
Higuera’s general treatment of this viewpoint
[
7
,
8
]
.

For context-free grammars, the distributional-learning line of Clark and
Eyraud
[
4
]
and Yoshinaka’s substitutability-based work

[
16
]
show how restrictions on contexts can support
positive-data reconstruction. Yoshinaka later developed multidimensional
substitutability for mildly context-sensitive formalisms

[
17
,
18
]
, and Clark–Yoshinaka studied related
learning problems for multiple context-free grammars

[
5
,
6
]
. The comparison with these
works is a comparison of both distributional assumptions and reconstruction
mechanisms. Yoshinaka’s untyped multidimensional implication compares any
two tuples sharing an accepting context. The present
(f,h)
-tuple
substitutability condition compares tuples only after equality of their
componentwise
h
-types has been fixed, and the learner uses the prescribed
finite monoid as external control rather than attempting to infer it.

The main new object introduced here is the sentence-interface type. In the
CFG case, the exterior of an occurrence is an ordinary two-sided context;
under a fixed homomorphism
h
, its finite record is essentially the pair
of boundary values
(h(u),h(v))
. In an MCFG derivation, however, a
nonterminal derives a tuple, and the tuple components may appear in the final
sentence in different left-to-right orders. The sentence-interface type
records this placement permutation together with the boundary
h
-values.
This finite placement record is not present in the standard untyped
substitutability framework and is the additional control needed for the typed
refinement and rule-transport construction below.

Kanazawa’s monograph on learnable classes of categorial grammars

[
11
]
is relevant as another example in which restrictions on
grammatical structure lead to positive-data learnability through finite
witnessing information. The present paper follows the same broad
tell-tale/characteristic-sample pattern, but applies it to fixed-
h

interface-controlled reconstruction for bounded-fan-out linear MCFGs.

Multiple context-free grammars and their equivalents are standard mildly
context-sensitive formalisms
[
14
,
15
,
13
,
10
]
.
The theorem below is not a normal-form theorem for arbitrary MCFGs. It is a
reconstruction theorem for languages having a reduced working binary linear
nondeleting presentation and satisfying the language-level
(f,h)
-tuple
substitutability condition.

Preliminaries and the Target Class

4.1
Learning from positive data

Definition 4.1

(Text and learner)
.

A
text
for
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is an infinite sequence

(w_{1},w_{2},\dots)
from
L
in which every element of
L
appears. A

learner
is a computable function mapping each finite prefix

(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
of a text to a hypothesis grammar. A learner

\mathcal{A}

identifies in the limit from positive data
a class

\mathcal{C}
if for every
L\in\mathcal{C}
and every text for
L

there exists
n_{0}
such that
L(\mathcal{A}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n}))=L
for all

n\geq n_{0}
.

The learners below are order-independent, so we identify a finite text
prefix with the corresponding finite sample
K=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\}\subseteq L
.

Definition 4.2

(Characteristic sample)
.

For a learner
\mathcal{A}
and
L\in\mathcal{C}
, a finite set

S_{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq L
is a
characteristic sample
if

L(\mathcal{A}(K))=L
holds for every finite
K
with

S_{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq K\subseteq L
.

This is the standard finite tell-tale, or characteristic-sample, pattern
in positive-data identification; see de la Higuera
[
7
,
8
]

for general learning-theoretic background.

Definition 4.3

(Positive sample size)
.

For a finite sample
K\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
, define

\|K\|_{+}:=\sum_{w\in K}\max(1,|w|).

The use of
\max(1,|w|)
avoids degenerate counting issues if the empty
word occurs.

Lemma 4.4

(Finite sufficient samples imply identification)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a computable order-independent learner.
Suppose that for each target language
L\in\mathcal{C}
,
there exists a finite set
S_{L}\subseteq L
such that

S_{L}\subseteq K\subseteq L\implies L(\mathcal{A}(K))=L

for every finite sample
K
.
Then
\mathcal{A}
identifies
\mathcal{C}
in the limit from positive data.

Proof.

Let
(w_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be a text for
L
. Since
S_{L}
is finite and every
word of
L
appears in the text, there is
n_{0}
such that

S_{L}\subseteq\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\}
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Every prefix sample
is contained in
L
. Hence the displayed implication gives

L(\mathcal{A}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n}))=L
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
∎

4.2
Finite monoids and fixed-
h
control

Definition 4.5

(Fixed homomorphism)
.

A
monoid homomorphism

h\colon\Sigma^{*}\to M

Finite monoids and syntactic morphisms are standard tools in automata theory;

Definition 4.6

(Componentwise

.

For

h^{(d)}(\vec{w}):=(h(w_{1}),\dots,h(w_{d}))\in M^{d}

4.3
The grammar formalism

Definition 4.7

(Working binary linear nondeleting MCFG)
.

A
working binary linear nondeleting multiple context-free grammar

is a tuple
G=(V,\Sigma,P,S,\mu)
, where
V
is a finite nonterminal set,

\Sigma
a finite terminal alphabet,
S\in V
the start symbol with

\mu(S)=1

\mu\colon V\to\mathbb{N}_{>0}
the fan-out map. Every

P
has one of the following forms: (1) a
start rule

S\to A
with
A\neq S
and
\mu(A)=1
; (2) a
terminal rule

A\to(a)
with
A\neq S
,
a\in\Sigma
, and
\mu(A)=1
; (3) a

binary rule

\rho\colon A\to(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{e})(B,C)
, where
A\neq S
,

e=\mu(A)
,
\mu(B)=d_{B}
,
\mu(C)=d_{C}
, each
\alpha_{i}
is a word over

\Sigma\cup\{x^{1},\dots,x^{d_{B}},y^{1},\dots,y^{d_{C}}\}
, and every
variable
x^{1},\dots,x^{d_{B}},y^{1},\dots,y^{d_{C}}
occurs

exactly once
in the whole tuple
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{e})
.
In addition, the presentation is assumed to be
start-separated
:
no terminal rule or binary rule has left-hand side
S
, and
S
does
not occur as a child nonterminal on the right-hand side of any binary
rule. Equivalently, the only rules with left-hand side

are start
rules

, and the start symbol is never used as a non-start child.

Remark 4.8

(Start-separated convention)
.

The start-separated convention makes the external start step explicit:
every successful derivation begins with one rule
S\to A
. In the typed
refinement this step is represented by
\widetilde{S}\to A_{(p)}^{\tau_{\mathrm{st}}}
, where
\tau_{\mathrm{st}}=(\mathrm{id}_{1};1_{M},1_{M})
. If
S

were also allowed as a child of non-start rules, typed copies of
S
with
interfaces other than
\tau_{\mathrm{st}}
would have to be treated separately.
Thus start-separation is part of the working-presentation discipline in the
definition of
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mcf}}_{f,h}
. This convention can always be enforced at
the level of the start symbol by introducing a fresh start nonterminal

S^{\prime}
with the single rule
S^{\prime}\to S_{0}
, where
S_{0}
is the original
start nonterminal; the old start symbol then becomes an ordinary
nonterminal. This observation only addresses start separation. The binary
linear nondeleting working discipline itself remains part of the presentation
hypothesis of this paper.

The semantics of a binary rule
\rho
is simultaneous substitution:
if
\vec{u}\in L_{B}(G)
and
\vec{v}\in L_{C}(G)
, then
A
derives

\rho(\vec{u},\vec{v})
by replacing each
x^{i}
by
u_{i}
and each

y^{j}
by
v_{j}
. Thus, for

\rho\colon A\to(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{e})(B,C)
, we write

\rho(\vec{u},\vec{v})
for the tuple obtained by simultaneously
substituting
u_{i}
for
x^{i}
and
v_{j}
for
y^{j}
in the template tuple

(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{e})
.

Remark 4.9

(Relation to the standard MCFG formalism)
.

Definition
4.7
is a binary linear nondeleting working form
inside the standard MCFG framework of Seki et al.
[
13
]
; every
grammar in the present sense is a standard MCFG. The theorem is stated

Remark 4.10

(Scope of the working-form assumption)
.

The working form is an assumption on a chosen presentation of the target
language. Its role is proof-theoretic: because each child variable occurs
exactly once, the child sentence-interface type is computed from the parent
interface, the rule template, and the sibling componentwise
h
-type. The
typed refinement and the characteristic sample depend on the chosen
presentation, although
(f,h)
-tuple substitutability is a language-level
condition. No normal-form theorem for arbitrary standard MCFG presentations
is claimed here.

Remark 4.11

(The empty word)
.

Terminal rules produce one terminal symbol and binary rules are nondeleting,
so the generated string languages in this working formalism do not contain

\varepsilon
. A separate start rule
S\to\varepsilon
could be added
with minor changes, but it is not treated in this paper.

Definition 4.12

(Tuple language and reduced grammar)
.

The
tuple language

L_{A}(G)\subseteq(\Sigma^{*})^{\mu(A)}
is the
set of all tuples derivable from
A
;
L(G):=L_{S}(G)
. A grammar is

and

4.4
Tuple distributions and the class
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mcf}}_{f,h}

Definition 4.13

(Sentence context of arity
d
)
.

A
sentence context of arity

is a word

E=u_{0}\,\square_{\sigma(1)}\,u_{1}\cdots\square_{\sigma(d)}\,u_{d},

where
\sigma
is a permutation of

and

, in which each hole symbol

.

Remark 4.14

(Permutation convention)
.

In the display

E=u_{0}\square_{\sigma(1)}u_{1}\cdots\square_{\sigma(d)}u_{d}
,

\sigma(i)
is the name of the
i

maps positions to hole names, not hole names

Definition 4.15

(Language-level tuple distribution)
.

Let
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
. For

\vec{x}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{d}
, define the arity-
d
tuple distribution

\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(d)}(\vec{x}):=\{E\mid E\text{ is a sentence context of arity }d\text{ and }E[\vec{x}]\in L\}.

When the arity is clear, we write simply

.

Definition 4.16

(
(f,h)
-tuple substitutability)
.

Let

be an explicit finite monoid
homomorphism. A language

is

-tuple-substitutable

A grammar is called fixed-

-tuple-substitutable for the
fan-out bound under discussion.

Example 4.18

(A unary example showing that nontrivial

Let

Remark 4.19

(Position of the MCFG formalism)

The bounded-fan-out and linearity assumptions should be read in the standard

Remark 4.20

4.5
Position of the class

The class
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mcf}}_{f,h}
is a fixed-parameter reconstruction class rather
than a normal form for all MCFG languages. This subsection records three
basic facts needed to calibrate the definition. First, familiar regular
examples are included once the finite monoid type is chosen appropriately.
Second, Yoshinaka-style untyped multidimensional substitutability implies the
present fixed-
h
condition. Third, the converse implication fails already
at fan-out two: the language
L_{3}=\{a^{n}b^{n}c^{n}\mid n\geq 1\}
belongs to a
natural fixed-
h
class while failing untyped arity-two tuple
substitutability.

Remark 4.22

(Regular and finite languages)
.

Let

For every

.
In particular, every finite language not containing

(Yoshinaka-substitutable languages are included)

Remark 4.25

(Technical and motivating examples)
.

The language

is used only to calibrate the fixed-

distributional
condition. The interface construction itself is motivated more generally by
MCFG placement phenomena, including crossing dependencies such as

\{a^{n}b^{m}c^{n}d^{m}\mid n,m\geq 1\}
, where the order in which tuple
components are inserted into the final sentence is part of the finite
structural information that must be controlled.

5
Why Sentence Interfaces Are Needed

5.1
From two-sided contexts to tuple placement

For CFG occurrences, a fixed finite monoid type can record the two exterior
pieces of a context: if a nonterminal derives a string
w
inside
uwv
,
the boundary data
(h(u),h(v))
are the natural finite record. For an MCFG
occurrence of arity
d
, the derived object is a tuple

(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})
. A surrounding sentence context has
d
named holes,
and those holes can occur in any left-to-right order. Thus the external
state of a tuple occurrence must remember not only the monoid values of the
boundary intervals, but also the placement order of the tuple components in
the final sentence.

5.2
Why componentwise type is not enough

Componentwise
h
-type records
(h(w_{1}),\ldots,h(w_{d}))
, but it does not
record whether
w_{1}
appears before
w_{2}
, after
w_{2}
, or separated by
other material in a sentence. This distinction is invisible in the tuple
itself and in its componentwise type, yet it matters for typed rule incidence:
a binary rule can route the same child tuple into different positions of the
parent sentence. The permutation component of a sentence-interface type is
introduced exactly to keep this finite incidence information available for
the typed refinement. Section
6.1
gives the formal definition, and
Example
6.3
gives the minimal fan-out-two pattern.

Remark 5.1

(Role of the permutation component)
.

The permutation component is not introduced to classify concrete contexts up
to equality. It is used to make typed rule incidence finite and stable: for a
fixed linear nondeleting binary rule, the parent sentence-interface and the
sibling output type determine the child sentence-interface. This is what
allows the typed refinement to route each child occurrence through a finite
state space.

6
Sentence-Interface Types and Transport

The purpose of this section is to construct the finite state space that
will later drive the typed refinement. A state is not merely a
componentwise
h
-type of a tuple. It must also remember how the tuple
is placed in a surrounding sentence. Sentence-interface types provide
a finite external state sufficient for the typed-refinement and
reconstruction argument developed here, and the transport maps below
show how binary MCFG templates propagate such states from parent
occurrences to child occurrences.

Throughout this section, let
G=(V,\Sigma,P,S,\mu)
be a reduced
working binary linear nondeleting MCFG and let
h\colon\Sigma^{*}\to M
be an
explicit finite monoid homomorphism.

6.1
Sentence-interface types

Definition 6.1

(Sentence-interface type)
.

Let

E=u_{0}\,\square_{\sigma(1)}\,u_{1}\cdots\square_{\sigma(d)}\,u_{d}

be a sentence context of arity

-sentence-interface

is

\tau_{h}(E):=(\sigma;\,h(u_{0}),\,h(u_{1}),\,\dots,\,h(u_{d}))\;\in\;S_{d}\times M^{d+1}.

Define

in a sentence context, this order is well-defined even when some boundary

Remark 6.2

(Sufficiency, not completeness, of sentence-interface types)
.

A sentence-interface type is not a complete invariant of concrete sentence
contexts. Its role is narrower: for a fixed binary linear nondeleting rule,
the parent interface together with the sibling componentwise
h
-type
determines the child interface. The soundness proof below still uses a
concrete shared sentence context whenever
(f,h)
-tuple substitutability is
invoked.

Since

recorded. For

parameters

and

. The next example shows why the permutation
component must be retained in the reconstruction mechanism.

(Not a general separation claim)

Example

We now define the decorated traces from which the child-interface

Let

Definition 6.6

(Decorated

For

Lemma 6.7

(
B
-trace normal form)
.

There exist a unique permutation
\kappa_{B}\in S_{d_{B}}
and unique words

\mathrm{red}(\Gamma_{B}^{\rho}(\tau,\mathbf{r}))=\beta_{0}\,x^{\kappa_{B}(1)}\,\beta_{1}\cdots x^{\kappa_{B}(d_{B})}\,\beta_{d_{B}}.

Consequently, for each

Proof.

By Definition
4.7
, each variable

occurs exactly
once in the whole tuple
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{e})
.
The word
\Gamma_{B}^{\rho}(\tau,\mathbf{r})

Hence, by cutting the word at the occurrences of the

Here

is the permutation describing the left-to-right order in

Proposition 6.11

(Finiteness and effective computability of transport)
.

For every binary rule
\rho
, the maps
\mathrm{out}_{\rho}
,
\mathrm{IT}_{B}^{\rho}
, and

\mathrm{IT}_{C}^{\rho}
are well-defined and effectively computable from the rule
template and the multiplication table of
M
.

Proof.

We first consider
\mathrm{out}_{\rho}
.
Each
\alpha_{i}
is a finite word over the finite alphabet

\Sigma\cup\{x^{1},\dots,x^{d_{B}},y^{1},\dots,y^{d_{C}}\}
.
Thus, for any
\mathbf{q}\in M^{d_{B}}
and
\mathbf{r}\in M^{d_{C}}
,
we may scan
\alpha_{i}
from left to right, replace each terminal
a
by

h(a)
, each variable
x^{k}
by
q_{k}
, and each variable
y^{j}
by
r_{j}
,
and then multiply the resulting elements according to the multiplication
table of
M
.
This uniquely determines

\mathrm{ev}_{\rho}(\alpha_{i};\mathbf{q},\mathbf{r})\in M
.
Hence
\mathrm{out}_{\rho}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{r})
is well-defined and can be
computed by a finite scan and table lookup.

Next consider
\mathrm{IT}_{B}^{\rho}
.
Take as input

\tau=(\sigma;m_{0},\dots,m_{e})\in\mathcal{I}_{h}(e)
and

\mathbf{r}\in M^{d_{C}}
.
Each word
\alpha_{i}^{B}(\mathbf{r})
is uniquely obtained by finitely many
symbol replacements, and hence

\Gamma_{B}^{\rho}(\tau,\mathbf{r})
is uniquely determined as a finite word.
The reduction
\mathrm{red}
is applied by scanning this word from left to
right and replacing each maximal consecutive
\underline{M}
-block by the
single symbol representing its monoid product; this is a single finite
pass. This left-to-right scan is an algorithmic implementation of the
canonical block-contraction operation
\mathrm{red}
defined above: equivalently,
one decomposes the trace into maximal consecutive
\underline{M}
-blocks
between visible variables and replaces each such block by the single
underlined symbol representing its monoid product. If one instead realizes
the same operation by iterated adjacent-pair contraction inside a maximal

\underline{M}
-block, each such contraction
replaces two consecutive
\underline{M}
-symbols by one and strictly reduces
length, so a block of length
t
is reduced after at most
t-1
such
contractions. Associativity makes the resulting product independent
of parenthesization. Therefore
\mathrm{red}(\Gamma_{B}^{\rho}(\tau,\mathbf{r}))

is uniquely determined.

By Lemma
6.7
, this reduced word can be written
uniquely in the form

\mathrm{red}(\Gamma_{B}^{\rho}(\tau,\mathbf{r}))=\beta_{0}\,x^{\kappa_{B}(1)}\,\beta_{1}\cdots x^{\kappa_{B}(d_{B})}\,\beta_{d_{B}}

for a unique permutation
\kappa_{B}
and unique interval words

.
Thus each

is uniquely determined, and

\mathrm{IT}_{B}^{\rho}(\tau,\mathbf{r})=(\kappa_{B};n_{0},\dots,n_{d_{B}})

is well-defined.

The computation is as follows:

(1)

construct each
\alpha_{i}^{B}(\mathbf{r})
;

(2)

concatenate these words with

\underline{m_{0}},\dots,\underline{m_{e}}
to obtain

\Gamma_{B}^{\rho}(\tau,\mathbf{r})
;

(3)

reduce each maximal consecutive
\underline{M}
-block to the
single symbol representing its monoid product;

(4)

read off
\kappa_{B}
from the order of the remaining
x
-variables
and cut out the intervening blocks

;

(5)

compute each

using the multiplication table.

All these are finite operations, and they require only the rule template,
the letter values of
h
, and the multiplication table of
M
.
Hence
\mathrm{IT}_{B}^{\rho}
is effectively computable.
The argument for
\mathrm{IT}_{C}^{\rho}
is completely symmetric.

6.4
Summary as finite local control

The preceding definitions can be summarized as the following finite
local control principle. For fixed
f
and fixed explicit
h
, a
typed copy
A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\tau}
of a nonterminal
A
of fan-out

d\leq f
is specified by
\mathbf{p}\in M^{d}
and

\tau\in\mathcal{I}_{h}(d)=S_{d}\times M^{d+1}
. Hence, over one fixed
underlying nonterminal, the number of possible typed copies is

|M|^{d}\cdot|S_{d}|\cdot|M|^{d+1}=d!\,|M|^{2d+1}.

Moreover, given a binary template

\rho\colon A\to(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{e})(B,C),

child output types
\mathbf{q}\in M^{\mu(B)}
and

\mathbf{r}\in M^{\mu(C)}
, and a parent interface type

\tau\in\mathcal{I}_{h}(e)
, the corresponding typed production is determined
as

A_{\mathrm{out}_{\rho}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{r})}^{\tau}\to(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{e})\bigl(B_{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathrm{IT}_{B}^{\rho}(\tau,\mathbf{r})},C_{\mathbf{r}}^{\mathrm{IT}_{C}^{\rho}(\tau,\mathbf{q})}\bigr).

Here
\mathrm{out}_{\rho}
computes the output type of the parent from the
output types of the two children, while
\mathrm{IT}_{B}^{\rho}
and

\mathrm{IT}_{C}^{\rho}
compute the interface types of the two child occurrences
from the parent interface type and the output type of the sibling
occurrence.

Thus this construction should not be read as a transition from one
state to another single state. Rather, it is a finite local
rule-typing scheme that simultaneously makes compatible the typed state
of the parent occurrence and the typed states of the two child
occurrences. Consequently, under fixed
f
and fixed explicit
h
,
only finitely many output/interface states can occur in the typed
refinement, and the typing of each rule template is controlled by
finitely many local schemes.

.

The rules are as follows.

(i)

Typed copies that are unproductive in any successful typed derivation,

(ii)

of

(iii)

Definition 7.6

(Interface realization for typed occurrences)

Definition 7.8

(Trimmed typed refinement)
.

A typed nonterminal of

Lemma 7.9

Every typed nonterminal of

Each retained typed nonterminal occurs in a successful derivation tree
from

∎

Lemma 7.10

(A successful outside context for surviving typed nonterminals)
.

7.4
Typing invariants and language equivalence

Proposition 7.11

(Typing invariants and language equality)
.

The following statements hold.

(i)

Type invariant.
If
A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\tau}\Rightarrow^{*}_{\widetilde{G}^{\mathrm{full}}}\vec{u}
,
then
h^{(\mu(A))}(\vec{u})=\mathbf{p}
. Moreover, forgetting type
annotations yields a valid derivation
A\Rightarrow_{G}^{*}\vec{u}
.

(ii)

Lifting.
If
\vec{u}
has a grammar-level successful occurrence at
A
with
interface type
\tau
, and
\mathbf{p}=h^{(\mu(A))}(\vec{u})
, then
this occurrence lifts to a successful occurrence of the typed copy

A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\tau}
in
\widetilde{G}^{\mathrm{full}}
.

(iii)

Survival.
Every typed nonterminal and every typed rule arising in the lifted
successful derivation tree of part
(ii)
survives in the trimmed
typed refinement
\widetilde{G}
. In particular, the typed copy

A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\tau}
itself survives.

(iv)

Language equality.

L(\widetilde{G})=L(G)
.

Proof.

We prove the four assertions in order.

Part (i): Type invariant.

The assertion concerns derivations rooted at typed copies of original
nonterminals. The fresh typed start symbol
\widetilde{S}
is not one
of these typed copies, and it does not occur on the right-hand side of
any rule of
\widetilde{G}^{\mathrm{full}}
.

We induct on the height of the typed derivation tree. For a node labelled
by
A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\tau}
, with
d=\mu(A)
, suppose that the subtree
rooted at that node derives
\vec{u}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{d}
. We maintain two
assertions:
h^{(d)}(\vec{u})=\mathbf{p}
, and forgetting type
annotations from the subtree gives a valid derivation

A\Rightarrow_{G}^{*}\vec{u}
.

If the subtree consists of a typed terminal rule
A_{(h(a))}^{\tau}\to(a)
,
then
\vec{u}=(a)
and
\mathbf{p}=(h(a))
, so the type assertion is
immediate. Forgetting the type annotation gives the terminal rule

A\to(a)
of
G
.

For the binary step, suppose the root rule is

A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\tau}\to\rho\bigl(B_{\mathbf{q}}^{\tau_{B}},C_{\mathbf{r}}^{\tau_{C}}\bigr),

where the underlying rule of
G
is

\rho\colon A\to(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{e})(B,C)
. Let

d_{B}=\mu(B)
and
d_{C}=\mu(C)
, and suppose that the two child
subtrees derive
\vec{u}_{B}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{d_{B}}
and

\vec{u}_{C}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{d_{C}}
. Then

\vec{u}=\rho(\vec{u}_{B},\vec{u}_{C})
. By the induction hypothesis,

h^{(d_{B})}(\vec{u}_{B})=\mathbf{q}
and

h^{(d_{C})}(\vec{u}_{C})=\mathbf{r}
, and the two child subtrees forget to
valid
G
-derivations from
B
and
C
.

By Definition
6.5
,
\mathrm{ev}_{\rho}(\alpha_{i};\mathbf{q},\mathbf{r})

is obtained by replacing each terminal
a
by
h(a)
, each variable

x^{k}
by
q_{k}
, and each variable
y^{j}
by
r_{j}
, and then
multiplying in
M
. Since
h
is a monoid morphism and

\rho(\vec{u}_{B},\vec{u}_{C})_{i}=\alpha_{i}[\vec{u}_{B}/x,\vec{u}_{C}/y]
,
we have
\mathrm{ev}_{\rho}(\alpha_{i};\mathbf{q},\mathbf{r})=h(\rho(\vec{u}_{B},\vec{u}_{C})_{i})
. Hence

\mathrm{out}_{\rho}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{r})=h^{(e)}(\rho(\vec{u}_{B},\vec{u}_{C}))=h^{(e)}(\vec{u})
. Since the typed rule has

\mathbf{p}=\mathrm{out}_{\rho}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{r})
by
Definition
7.4
(iii), the type assertion follows.
For the forgetting assertion, forgetting the annotations from the root
rule gives the original rule
\rho
, and combining it with the forgotten
child derivations gives
A\Rightarrow_{G}^{*}\rho(\vec{u}_{B},\vec{u}_{C})=\vec{u}
. This proves part (i).

Part (ii): Lifting.

Fix a grammar-level successful occurrence of
\vec{u}
at
A
with
interface type
\tau
. Thus there are a successful derivation tree

D
of
G
from
S
, with yield
w
, and a node
\nu_{*}
of

D
labelled by
A
, such that the subtree rooted at
\nu_{*}

derives
\vec{u}
, and the grammar-level outside context of this
occurrence has interface type
\tau
. We construct a successful
derivation tree of
\widetilde{G}^{\mathrm{full}}
with the same underlying tree
and the same yield
w
.

For each non-start node
\nu
of
D
, let its label be
P
, let

d=\mu(P)
, and let
\vec{u}_{\nu}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{d}
be the tuple
derived by the subtree rooted at
\nu
. Let
E_{D,\nu}
be the
grammar-level outside context of this occurrence. Then

E_{D,\nu}[\vec{u}_{\nu}]=w
by definition, with empty components and
hole order interpreted as part of the concrete occurrence. Define

\mathbf{p}_{\nu}:=h^{(d)}(\vec{u}_{\nu})
and

\tau_{\nu}:=\tau_{h}(E_{D,\nu})
, and annotate
\nu
by

P_{\mathbf{p}_{\nu}}^{\tau_{\nu}}
. The root is annotated by

\widetilde{S}
.

It remains to check that every local rule of this annotated tree belongs
to
\widetilde{G}^{\mathrm{full}}
. For the start step, the start-separated
form gives a rule
S\to A_{0}
, whose unique child is some node

\nu_{0}
. Here
\vec{u}_{\nu_{0}}=(w)
, the outside context is

\square_{1}
,
\tau_{\nu_{0}}=\tau_{\mathrm{st}}
, and

\mathbf{p}_{\nu_{0}}=(h(w))
. Hence the typed start rule

\widetilde{S}\to(A_{0})_{(h(w))}^{\tau_{\mathrm{st}}}
belongs to

\widetilde{G}^{\mathrm{full}}
by Definition
7.4
(i).

For a terminal node
\nu
with underlying rule
P\to(a)
, we have

\vec{u}_{\nu}=(a)
and
\mathbf{p}_{\nu}=(h(a))
. Hence

P_{(h(a))}^{\tau_{\nu}}\to(a)
belongs to
\widetilde{G}^{\mathrm{full}}
by
Definition
7.4
(ii).

For a binary node
\nu
, write its underlying rule as

\rho\colon P\to(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{e})(B,C)
, and let its children
be
\nu_{B}
and
\nu_{C}
. Put
\mathbf{q}:=\mathbf{p}_{\nu_{B}}
and

\mathbf{r}:=\mathbf{p}_{\nu_{C}}
. By construction,

\mathbf{q}=h^{(\mu(B))}(\vec{u}_{\nu_{B}})
and

\mathbf{r}=h^{(\mu(C))}(\vec{u}_{\nu_{C}})
. Since

\vec{u}_{\nu}=\rho(\vec{u}_{\nu_{B}},\vec{u}_{\nu_{C}})
,
Definition
6.5
and the homomorphism property of

give
\mathbf{p}_{\nu}=\mathrm{out}_{\rho}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{r})
.

We next compare the child interfaces. By the definition of outside
context, the outside context of the
B
-child is the child context
obtained from the parent outside context by expanding
\rho
, filling
the
C
-components by the concrete tuple
\vec{u}_{\nu_{C}}
, and
leaving the
B
-components as named holes. Hence, in the notation of
Definition
6.12
,

E_{D,\nu_{B}}=E_{B}^{\rho,E_{D,\nu},\vec{u}_{\nu_{C}}}
. Lemma
6.13

gives
\tau_{\nu_{B}}=\mathrm{IT}_{B}^{\rho}(\tau_{\nu},\mathbf{r})
. Similarly,

E_{D,\nu_{C}}=E_{C}^{\rho,E_{D,\nu},\vec{u}_{\nu_{B}}}
, and
Lemma
6.13
gives

\tau_{\nu_{C}}=\mathrm{IT}_{C}^{\rho}(\tau_{\nu},\mathbf{q})
. Therefore the typed
binary rule

P_{\mathbf{p}_{\nu}}^{\tau_{\nu}}\to(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{e})\bigl(B_{\mathbf{q}}^{\tau_{\nu_{B}}},C_{\mathbf{r}}^{\tau_{\nu_{C}}}\bigr)

belongs to
\widetilde{G}^{\mathrm{full}}
by
Definition
7.4
(iii).

All local rules of the annotated tree have now been checked. A bottom-up
structural induction on subtrees therefore shows that the annotated tree
is a successful derivation tree of
\widetilde{G}^{\mathrm{full}}
from

\widetilde{S}
with yield
w
. At the distinguished node
\nu_{*}
,
we have
\mathbf{p}_{\nu_{*}}=h^{(\mu(A))}(\vec{u})=\mathbf{p}
and

\tau_{\nu_{*}}=\tau
, so the annotation is precisely

A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\tau}
. Thus the given successful occurrence lifts to
a successful occurrence of
A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\tau}
in

\widetilde{G}^{\mathrm{full}}
. This proves part (ii).

Part (iii): Survival.

The lifted tree constructed in part (ii) is a successful
derivation tree of
\widetilde{G}^{\mathrm{full}}
from
\widetilde{S}
.
By Definition
7.8
, every typed nonterminal and
every typed rule that occurs in such a successful derivation tree is
retained in
\widetilde{G}
. Hence all typed nonterminals and typed
rules arising in the lifted tree survive in
\widetilde{G}
. In
particular, the distinguished typed copy
A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\tau}

survives.

Part (iv): Language equality.

We prove both inclusions. First let
w\in L(\widetilde{G})
. A
successful derivation of
\widetilde{G}
from
\widetilde{S}
begins
with a typed start rule

\widetilde{S}\to(A_{0})_{\mathbf{p}_{0}}^{\tau_{\mathrm{st}}}
, whose
underlying original start rule is
S\to A_{0}
. This start rule forgets
by definition to
S\to A_{0}
. Below it, there is a typed derivation

(A_{0})_{\mathbf{p}_{0}}^{\tau_{\mathrm{st}}}\Rightarrow_{\widetilde{G}}^{*}(w)
, which is also a derivation in

\widetilde{G}^{\mathrm{full}}
. By part (i), this subderivation
forgets to a valid derivation
A_{0}\Rightarrow_{G}^{*}(w)
. Prepending

S\to A_{0}
gives
S\Rightarrow_{G}^{*}w
, so
w\in L(G)
. Hence

L(\widetilde{G})\subseteq L(G)
.

Conversely, let
w\in L(G)
, and choose a successful derivation tree

D
of
G
with yield
w
. By part (ii),
D
lifts to a
successful derivation tree
\widetilde{D}
of
\widetilde{G}^{\mathrm{full}}

from
\widetilde{S}
with the same yield. By part (iii), all
typed nonterminals and typed rules appearing in
\widetilde{D}
survive
in
\widetilde{G}
. Therefore
\widetilde{D}
is also a valid
derivation tree of
\widetilde{G}
, and
w\in L(\widetilde{G})
. Hence

L(G)\subseteq L(\widetilde{G})
.

The two inclusions give
L(\widetilde{G})=L(G)
.
∎

Throughout Sections

8.2
Anchors and exposing contexts

Fix once and for all an effective total order on tuples of strings, for
example by total length and then lexicographic order, and an effective
total order on sentence contexts, first by terminal length and then
lexicographically in a fixed encoding with named holes.

Definition 8.2

(Anchors and exposing contexts)
.

For each typed nonterminal
X=A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\tau}
of
\widetilde{G}
,
let
\omega(X)
be the least tuple in
L_{X}(\widetilde{G})
under the fixed
tuple order. Let
\chi(X)
be the least sentence context
E
such that

\tau_{h}(E)=\tau(X)=\tau\qquad\text{and}\qquad E[\omega(X)]\in L.

Equivalently,
\chi(X)[\omega(X)]\in L(G)
and

\tau_{h}(\chi(X))=\tau
. Such a context exists by
Lemma
8.1
.

Remark 8.3

(Existence of the minimum exposing context)
.

The minimum defining
\chi(X)
is well-defined because the relevant
context set is nonempty by Lemma
8.1
. For
fixed arity
d
and fixed total terminal length
T
, an arity-
d

sentence context is obtained by choosing a permutation in
S_{d}
, choosing
a split of
T
terminal positions among the
d+1
boundary strings, and
choosing the terminal word of length
T
. Thus there are at most

d!\binom{T+d}{d}|\Sigma|^{T}
such contexts, hence only finitely many at
each terminal length. Since terminal length ranges over
\mathbb{N}
and
is well-ordered, and the fixed lexicographic tie-breaker orders the finite
set at the least length, the minimum exists and is unique.

Lemma 8.4

(Anchor distribution equivalence)
.

Let
X=A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\tau}
be a typed nonterminal of

\widetilde{G}
. For every tuple

\vec{u}\in L_{X}(\widetilde{G})
, we have

h^{(\mu(A))}(\vec{u})=\mathbf{p}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{D}_{L(G)}(\vec{u})=\mathcal{D}_{L(G)}(\omega(X)).

Proof.

The equality
h^{(\mu(A))}(\vec{u})=\mathbf{p}
follows from
Proposition
7.11
. The same proposition also
gives

h^{(\mu(A))}(\omega(X))=\mathbf{p},

since
\omega(X)\in L_{X}(\widetilde{G})
by definition of the anchor.

We now prove equality of tuple distributions. By
Lemma
8.1
, choose a successful typed
occurrence of
X
in a derivation of
\widetilde{G}
, and let

E_{X}
be the concrete sentence context obtained by replacing that
occurrence by named holes. This is a concrete sentence context:
replacing the subtree rooted at the chosen occurrence of
X
changes only
the tuple inserted into the named holes, and leaves all surrounding
terminal material and the left-to-right order of those holes unchanged.
The lemma gives

E_{X}[\vec{v}]\in L(G)\qquad\text{for every }\vec{v}\in L_{X}(\widetilde{G}).

In particular,

E_{X}[\omega(X)]\in L(G)\qquad\text{and}\qquad E_{X}[\vec{u}]\in L(G).

Thus the same concrete sentence context
E_{X}
belongs to

\mathcal{D}_{L(G)}(\omega(X))\cap\mathcal{D}_{L(G)}(\vec{u}).

Moreover,

h^{(\mu(A))}(\vec{u})=h^{(\mu(A))}(\omega(X))=\mathbf{p}.

Here
E_{X}
has arity
d=\mu(A)
, and
d\leq f
because the target
grammar has fan-out at most
f
. Since
L(G)
is

(f,h)
-tuple-substitutable, the shared concrete context
E_{X}
,
together with equality of componentwise
h
-types, implies

\mathcal{D}_{L(G)}(\vec{u})=\mathcal{D}_{L(G)}(\omega(X)).

Notice that this argument does not use
\chi(X)
as a successful
derivational context. The exposing context
\chi(X)
is used only
after the distribution equality has been obtained.
∎

Lemma 8.5

(Exposing contexts apply to all tuples of the same typed symbol)
.

For every typed nonterminal
X
and every

\vec{u}\in L_{X}(\widetilde{G})
,

\chi(X)[\vec{u}]\in L.

Proof.

By definition of
\chi(X)
, we have

\chi(X)\in\mathcal{D}_{L}(\omega(X))
. Lemma
8.4

gives

\mathcal{D}_{L}(\vec{u})=\mathcal{D}_{L}(\omega(X)).

Therefore
\chi(X)\in\mathcal{D}_{L}(\vec{u})
, which is exactly

\chi(X)[\vec{u}]\in L
.
∎

Lemma 8.6

(Start-child exposing context)
.

If
\widetilde{S}\to X
is a surviving typed start rule of

\widetilde{G}
, then for every
u\in L_{X}(\widetilde{G})
,

u\in L
and
\square_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(1)}(u)
. In particular

\chi(X)=\square_{1}
.

Proof.

A typed start rule has the form

\widetilde{S}\to X
with
\tau(X)=\tau_{\mathrm{st}}=(\mathrm{id}_{1};1_{M},1_{M})
.
If
u\in L_{X}(\widetilde{G})
, then

\widetilde{S}\Rightarrow X\Rightarrow^{*}u,

so
u\in L(\widetilde{G})
.
By Proposition
7.11
(iv),
u\in L
. Therefore
the empty unary context
\square_{1}=\varepsilon\square_{1}\varepsilon
belongs
to
\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(1)}(u)
and has interface
\tau_{\mathrm{st}}
. Its terminal length is

|\varepsilon|+|\varepsilon|=0
, which is the minimum possible terminal
length of any unary context
u_{0}\square_{1}u_{1}
. Since the context order
compares terminal length first and then uses a fixed tie-breaker, the
empty unary context realizes the least possible length among contexts
satisfying the defining conditions for
\chi(X)
. Hence

\chi(X)=\square_{1}
.
∎

8.3
Symbol observations and rule observations

Definition 8.7

(Symbol and rule observations)
.

For a typed nonterminal
X
, define its
symbol observation
by

\mathrm{obs}_{\mathrm{NT}}(X):=\chi(X)[\omega(X)].

For a non-start rule
R
of
\widetilde{G}
, define
\mathrm{obs}_{\mathrm{Rule}}(R)

as follows.

(i)

Terminal rule.
If
R
is a typed terminal rule
X\to(a)
, set

\mathrm{obs}_{\mathrm{Rule}}(R):=\chi(X)[(a)].

(ii)

Binary rule.
If
R\colon X\to\rho(Y,Z)
is a typed binary rule, where

\rho\colon A\to(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{e})(B,C)
is the underlying
template, define

\vec{z}_{R}:=\rho\!\left(\omega(Y),\,\omega(Z)\right)

and set

\mathrm{obs}_{\mathrm{Rule}}(R):=\chi(X)[\vec{z}_{R}].

8.4
The characteristic sample

Definition 8.8

(Characteristic sample)
.

The target-dependent finite set

\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})=\{\mathrm{obs}_{\mathrm{NT}}(X):X\in\mathrm{NT}(\widetilde{G})\}\cup\{\mathrm{obs}_{\mathrm{Rule}}(R):R\text{ is a non-start rule of }\widetilde{G}\}

is the characteristic sample associated with the trimmed typed
refinement. It explicitly contains symbol observations for every
surviving typed nonterminal; these are not derived from rule
observations.

Remark 8.9

(Multiple exposures in one sample word)
.

A single word of the positive sample may expose more than one typed symbol or
more than one typed rule instance. No information is lost in this case: the
learner enumerates all tuple occurrences, all admissible segmentations, and
all concrete sentence contexts occurring in the sample words. Thus the use
of a set
K
of sample words is compatible with multiple observation roles
played by the same word.

Lemma 8.10

(Positivity of the characteristic sample)
.

The set
\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})
is finite and satisfies

\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})\subseteq L
.

Proof.

The grammar
\widetilde{G}
is finite, so the displayed union is finite.
For a symbol observation,

\mathrm{obs}_{\mathrm{NT}}(X)=\chi(X)[\omega(X)]\in L
by the definition of
\chi(X)
.

For a terminal rule
R:X\to(a)
, the tuple
(a)
belongs to

L_{X}(\widetilde{G})
. Lemma
8.5
gives

\chi(X)[(a)]\in L
.

For a binary rule
R:X\to\rho(Y,Z)
, the tuple

\vec{z}_{R}=\rho(\omega(Y),\omega(Z))
is in
L_{X}(\widetilde{G})
because

\omega(Y)\in L_{Y}(\widetilde{G})
and

\omega(Z)\in L_{Z}(\widetilde{G})
. Lemma
8.5

gives
\chi(X)[\vec{z}_{R}]\in L
.
∎

8.5
Canonical learner

We now define the learner used in the reconstruction theorem. Its
nonterminals are observed typed tuples, and its rules are those witnessed
by positive examples through concrete shared sentence contexts. The word
“concrete” is important: equality of sentence-interface type is not used
as a substitute for a shared context when soundness invokes

(f,h)
-tuple substitutability.

8.6
Observed typed tuples and learner rules

Definition 8.11

(Tuple occurrence in a sample word)
.

An arity-
d
tuple occurrence in
w\in K
is a pair
(E,\vec{x})
with

E
a concrete arity-
d
sentence context,
\vec{x}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{d}
,
and
E[\vec{x}]=w
. Equivalently, choose
d
named slots in
w
: a
nonempty slot is a positive-length interval, while an empty slot is a cut
position. Positive-length slots are disjoint, and empty slots sharing a cut
carry a local left-to-right tie order. The induced left-to-right order of
slots gives the order of the holes in
E
.

Remark 8.12

(Tie-order convention for empty occurrences)
.

When several empty slots are assigned to the same cut position, a

tie-order
is a total order on the corresponding slot labels at that
cut. The occurrence data include these tie-orders. Thus two tuple
occurrences are identical only when they have the same positive-length
intervals, the same cut positions for empty slots, the same named slot
labels, and the same tie-orders at every cut. This convention is used
unchanged for zero-length variable intervals in binary witnesses.

Definition 8.13

(Observed typed tuple)
.

A pair
[\vec{x}:\tau]
, where
\vec{x}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{d}
with

1\leq d\leq f
and
\tau\in\mathcal{I}_{h}(d)
, is
observed in
K

if there is a concrete arity-
d
sentence context
E
such that

E[\vec{x}]\in K
and
\tau_{h}(E)=\tau
. The learner uses one
nonterminal
[\vec{x}:\tau]
for each observed typed tuple, together
with a fresh start symbol
\widehat{S}
.

The contexts in Definition
8.13
are concrete
sentence contexts. No semantic quotienting of contexts is used. In
particular, equality of sentence-interface type is not treated as equality
of concrete contexts.

Definition 8.14

(Binary witness)
.

A
binary witness
in
K
consists of the following data.

(i)

a parent tuple occurrence
(E,\vec{z})
in a word of
K
, where

\vec{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{e})
and
e\leq f
;

(ii)

two observed typed tuples
[\vec{x}:\tau_{B}]
and

[\vec{y}:\tau_{C}]
, where
\vec{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d_{B}})
,

\vec{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d_{C}})
, and
d_{B},d_{C}\leq f
;

(iii)

for each parent component
z_{i}
, a segmentation into terminal
gaps and variable intervals labelled by distinct variables from

\{x^{1},\dots,x^{d_{B}},y^{1},\dots,y^{d_{C}}\}
, such that every variable in
this set is used exactly once in the whole tuple of segmentations;

(iv)

the terminal gaps are the exact terminal substrings of the
corresponding component
z_{i}
, and each variable interval labelled

x^{j}
is equal to
x_{j}
, while each variable interval labelled

y^{k}
is equal to
y_{k}
.

Empty child components are represented by zero-length variable intervals
with the tie-order convention of Remark
8.12
: at
each cut position, the witness data include a total order on the zero-length
variable labels placed at that cut. Two binary witnesses are identical only
when they agree on the parent occurrence, the two child typed tuples, all
component segmentations, and all such tie-orders. Multiple equal substrings
at different positions are treated as distinct occurrence choices; this
over-enumeration is harmless for fixed fan-out.

The segmentations determine a concrete binary linear nondeleting template

\rho\colon\bullet\to(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{e})(\bullet,\bullet)
such
that
\vec{z}=\rho(\vec{x},\vec{y})
. Put

\tau=\tau_{h}(E)
,

\tau_{B}^{\prime}=\mathrm{IT}_{B}^{\rho}(\tau,h^{(d_{C})}(\vec{y}))
, and

\tau_{C}^{\prime}=\mathrm{IT}_{C}^{\rho}(\tau,h^{(d_{B})}(\vec{x}))
. The witness is

typed
if
\tau_{B}^{\prime}=\tau_{B}
and
\tau_{C}^{\prime}=\tau_{C}
.

Definition 8.15

(Learner grammar)
.

The
learner grammar

\hat{G}(K)
is the extended grammar with
fresh start symbol
\widehat{S}
, nonterminals the observed typed tuples

[\vec{x}:\tau]
, and the following rules.

(i)

Start rules.
For every word
w\in K
such that
[(w):\tau_{\mathrm{st}}]
is observed via
the empty unary context
\square_{1}
, add the rule

\widehat{S}\to[(w):\tau_{\mathrm{st}}]
.

(ii)

Terminal rules.
For every observed typed tuple
[(a):\tau]
with
a\in\Sigma
, add
the rule
[(a):\tau]\to(a)
.

(iii)

Binary witness rules.
For every typed binary witness with parent occurrence
(E,\vec{z})
,
child typed tuples
[\vec{x}:\tau_{B}]
and
[\vec{y}:\tau_{C}]
, and
template
\rho
, add the rule

[\vec{z}:\tau_{h}(E)]\longrightarrow\rho\bigl([\vec{x}:\tau_{B}],[\vec{y}:\tau_{C}]\bigr).

(iv)

Typed substitution rules.
For every ordered pair of observed typed tuples
[\vec{x}:\tau]
and

[\vec{y}:\tau]
of the same arity
d
, add the unit rule

[\vec{x}:\tau]\to[\vec{y}:\tau]
, provided that

h^{(d)}(\vec{x})=h^{(d)}(\vec{y})
and there exists a concrete
arity-
d
sentence context
E
with
\tau_{h}(E)=\tau
such that

E[\vec{x}]\in K
and
E[\vec{y}]\in K
. The pair is ordered: when
the conditions hold in both directions, both directed unit rules are
included.

Remark
.

The shared context required here is a concrete sentence context, not
merely equality of sentence-interface type. Thus

E[\vec{x}],E[\vec{y}]\in K\subseteq L
gives

E\in\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(d)}(\vec{x})\cap\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(d)}(\vec{y})
, which is exactly
the nonempty-intersection hypothesis needed for
(f,h)
-tuple
substitutability. Equality of interface type alone would not supply
such a common concrete context.

By construction,
\hat{G}(K)
depends only on the finite sample
K
, the
fixed fan-out bound
f
, and the fixed explicit homomorphism
h
. It does
not receive the target grammar
G
, the typed refinement
\widetilde{G}
,
or the characteristic sample
\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})
as input. These objects
enter only in the correctness proof, where
\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})
serves as a
finite existence witness ensuring eventual stabilization on every complete
positive text.

The grammar
\hat{G}(K)
is called extended because it may contain unit
rules. The polynomial-time construction and the standard elimination of
unit rules are treated in Section
10
; no polynomial
time-and-data bound is asserted here.

9
Exact Reconstruction

9.1
Distributional equivalence and learner soundness

Definition 9.1

(Typed distributional equivalence)
.

Let
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
. For tuples

\vec{u},\vec{x}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{d}
, write

\vec{u}\equiv_{L}^{d}\vec{x}
if

h^{(d)}(\vec{u})=h^{(d)}(\vec{x})
and

\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(d)}(\vec{u})=\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(d)}(\vec{x})
. When the arity is clear,
we write simply
\vec{u}\equiv_{L}\vec{x}
. For each fixed arity

d
, this is an equivalence relation, since it is defined by equality
of componentwise
h
-types and equality of concrete tuple
distributions.

Lemma 9.2

(Shared-context substitutability)
.

Let
L
be
(f,h)
-tuple-substitutable. Let

\vec{x},\vec{y}\in(\Sigma^{*})^{d}
with
d\leq f
. If

h^{(d)}(\vec{x})=h^{(d)}(\vec{y})
and there is a concrete
arity-
d
sentence context
E
such that
E[\vec{x}]\in L
and

E[\vec{y}]\in L
, then
\vec{x}\equiv_{L}^{d}\vec{y}
.

Proof.

The concrete context
E
belongs to

\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(d)}(\vec{x})\cap\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(d)}(\vec{y})
. Since
d\leq f
and

h^{(d)}(\vec{x})=h^{(d)}(\vec{y})
, the
(f,h)
-tuple
substitutability of
L
gives

\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(d)}(\vec{x})=\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(d)}(\vec{y})
. Together with the
assumed
h
-type equality, this is exactly

\vec{x}\equiv_{L}^{d}\vec{y}
.
∎

Lemma 9.3

(Observed substitution preserves distributional equivalence)
.

Let
L
be
(f,h)
-tuple-substitutable, and let
K\subseteq L
.
If
\hat{G}(K)
contains a typed substitution rule

[\vec{x}:\tau]\to[\vec{y}:\tau]
of arity
d
, then

\vec{x}\equiv_{L}^{d}\vec{y}
.

Proof.

By Definition
8.15
(iv), the rule is added only when

h^{(d)}(\vec{x})=h^{(d)}(\vec{y})
and there is a concrete
arity-
d
context
E
such that
E[\vec{x}]\in K
and

E[\vec{y}]\in K
. Since
K\subseteq L
, the same concrete context
places both tuples in
L
. Lemma
9.2

therefore gives
\vec{x}\equiv_{L}^{d}\vec{y}
.
∎

Lemma 9.4

(Witnessed composition preserves distributional equivalence)
.

Let
L
be
(f,h)
-tuple-substitutable. Let

\rho\colon A\to(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{e})(B,C)
be a binary linear
nondeleting template with parent arity
e\leq f
and child arities

d_{B},d_{C}
. Let
E
be a concrete arity-
e
sentence context such
that
E[\rho(\vec{x},\vec{y})]\in L
. If

\vec{u}\equiv_{L}^{d_{B}}\vec{x}
and

\vec{v}\equiv_{L}^{d_{C}}\vec{y}
, then

\rho(\vec{u},\vec{v})\equiv_{L}^{e}\rho(\vec{x},\vec{y})
.

Proof.

Put
\vec{z}=\rho(\vec{x},\vec{y})
and

\vec{r}=\rho(\vec{u},\vec{v})
. First we compare their
componentwise
h
-types. Since
\rho
is a binary linear
nondeleting template, each child variable occurs exactly once in the
whole tuple of right-hand sides, and the componentwise output

h
-type is computed by the finite output map
\mathrm{out}_{\rho}
. Hence

h^{(e)}(\vec{r})=\mathrm{out}_{\rho}(h^{(d_{B})}(\vec{u}),h^{(d_{C})}(\vec{v}))=\mathrm{out}_{\rho}(h^{(d_{B})}(\vec{x}),h^{(d_{C})}(\vec{y}))=h^{(e)}(\vec{z}).

It remains to obtain a shared concrete parent context. Since

E[\rho(\vec{x},\vec{y})]\in L
, Lemma
6.14

gives the induced
B
-side concrete context

E_{B}^{\rho,E,\vec{y}}
with

E_{B}^{\rho,E,\vec{y}}[\vec{x}]=E[\rho(\vec{x},\vec{y})]
. Thus

E_{B}^{\rho,E,\vec{y}}\in\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(d_{B})}(\vec{x})
. Because

\vec{u}\equiv_{L}^{d_{B}}\vec{x}
, the same concrete context belongs to

\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(d_{B})}(\vec{u})
. By Lemma
6.14
, applied
with the
B
-child tuple
\vec{u}
and the sibling
\vec{y}
, we have

E_{B}^{\rho,E,\vec{y}}[\vec{u}]=E[\rho(\vec{u},\vec{y})]
. Hence

E[\rho(\vec{u},\vec{y})]\in L
.

Now form the induced
C
-side concrete context
E_{C}^{\rho,E,\vec{u}}
.
The preceding paragraph says that this context accepts
\vec{y}
.
Since
\vec{v}\equiv_{L}^{d_{C}}\vec{y}
, it also accepts
\vec{v}
.
Using the filling identity once more, we obtain

E[\rho(\vec{u},\vec{v})]\in L
. Thus the same concrete parent
context
E
accepts both
\vec{z}
and
\vec{r}
. Together with
the
h
-type equality proved above, Lemma
9.2

gives
\vec{r}\equiv_{L}^{e}\vec{z}
.

No step in this argument replaces equality of concrete contexts by
equality of sentence-interface types. The interface data are part of the
finite learner rule construction; the application of substitutability is
justified by the concrete context
E
.
∎

Lemma 9.5

(Learner derivations preserve distributional equivalence)
.

Let
L
be
(f,h)
-tuple-substitutable, and let
K\subseteq L
. If

[\vec{x}:\tau]\Rightarrow_{\hat{G}(K)}^{*}\vec{u}
, then

\vec{u}\equiv_{L}^{d}\vec{x}
, where
d
is the arity of

\vec{x}
.

Proof.

We induct on the number of rules used in a derivation from

[\vec{x}:\tau]
to
\vec{u}
.

If no rule is applied, then
\vec{u}=\vec{x}
, and

\vec{u}\equiv_{L}^{d}\vec{x}
holds trivially.

If the first rule is the terminal rule
[(a):\tau]\to(a)
, then

\vec{x}=(a)=\vec{u}
, so the assertion is immediate.

Suppose the first rule is a typed substitution rule

[\vec{x}:\tau]\to[\vec{y}:\tau]
, followed by a derivation

[\vec{y}:\tau]\Rightarrow_{\hat{G}(K)}^{*}\vec{u}
. By induction,

\vec{u}\equiv_{L}\vec{y}
. By
Lemma
9.3
,
\vec{y}\equiv_{L}\vec{x}
.
Since
\equiv_{L}
is an equivalence relation,
\vec{u}\equiv_{L}\vec{x}
.

Finally suppose the first rule is a binary witness rule

[\vec{x}:\tau]\to\rho([\vec{y}:\tau_{B}],[\vec{z}:\tau_{C}])
, and the
children derive
\vec{u}_{B}
and
\vec{u}_{C}
. The induction
hypotheses give
\vec{u}_{B}\equiv_{L}^{d_{B}}\vec{y}
and

\vec{u}_{C}\equiv_{L}^{d_{C}}\vec{z}
. By the definition of a binary
witness rule, the displayed rule comes from a concrete parent occurrence

(E,\vec{x})
in
K
, and the witness template satisfies

\vec{x}=\rho(\vec{y},\vec{z})
. Thus

E[\rho(\vec{y},\vec{z})]=E[\vec{x}]\in K\subseteq L
.
Lemma
9.4
applies and gives

\rho(\vec{u}_{B},\vec{u}_{C})\equiv_{L}\rho(\vec{y},\vec{z})=\vec{x}
.
This is the desired assertion for the tuple produced by the binary step.
∎

Lemma 9.6

(Start-symbol soundness)
.

Let
L
be
(f,h)
-tuple-substitutable, and let
K\subseteq L
.
Then
L(\hat{G}(K))\subseteq L
.

Proof.

Let
w\in L(\hat{G}(K))
. A derivation from
\widehat{S}
begins with
a start rule
\widehat{S}\to[(x):\tau_{\mathrm{st}}]
, where
x\in K
is
observed via the empty unary context
\square_{1}
. Thus

\square_{1}[x]=x\in K\subseteq L
, so
\square_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(1)}((x))
.
If
[(x):\tau_{\mathrm{st}}]\Rightarrow_{\hat{G}(K)}^{*}(w)
, then
Lemma
9.5
gives
(w)\equiv_{L}^{1}(x)
. Hence

\square_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(1)}((w))
. Since

\square_{1}[w]=\varepsilon w\varepsilon=w
by the definition of context
filling, this means
w\in L
.
∎

9.2
Exposure simulation

For each typed nonterminal
X
of the trimmed typed refinement

\widetilde{G}
, fix the exposing context
\chi(X)
, exposing tuple

\omega(X)
, and interface type
\tau(X)
from
Section
8
. Define the
exposure map
by

\eta(X):=[\omega(X):\tau(X)].

No injectivity or surjectivity of
\eta
is asserted.

Definition 9.7

(Rule simulation)
.

Assume that
\eta
maps typed nonterminals of
\widetilde{G}
to
nonterminals of
\hat{G}(K)
. We say that
\eta
is a

rule simulation
from
\widetilde{G}
to
\hat{G}(K)
if the
following two conditions hold: for every typed terminal rule
X\to(a)

of
\widetilde{G}
, one has

\eta(X)\Rightarrow_{\hat{G}(K)}^{*}(a)
; and for every typed binary rule

X\to\rho(Y,Z)
of
\widetilde{G}
, one has

\eta(X)\Rightarrow_{\hat{G}(K)}^{*}\rho(\eta(Y),\eta(Z))
. Start rules
are not included in this definition; they are handled separately by
start compatibility.

Lemma 9.8

(Exposure symbols are observed)
.

Assume
\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})\subseteq K
. For every typed nonterminal

X
of
\widetilde{G}
, the symbol
\eta(X)
is a nonterminal of

\hat{G}(K)
.

Proof.

By definition of the characteristic sample,

\mathrm{obs}_{\mathrm{NT}}(X)=\chi(X)[\omega(X)]\in\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})\subseteq K
.
The exposing context
\chi(X)
is a concrete sentence context of arity

d=\mu(A)\leq f
, and by Definition
8.2
it satisfies

\tau_{h}(\chi(X))=\tau(X)
. Therefore
\chi(X)
is a concrete witness
for the observation of
[\omega(X):\tau(X)]
in the sense of
Definition
8.13
. Hence
[\omega(X):\tau(X)]

is a nonterminal of
\hat{G}(K)
.
∎

Lemma 9.9

(Exposure simulates terminal rules)
.

Assume
\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})\subseteq K\subseteq L
. If
X\to(a)
is a
typed terminal rule of
\widetilde{G}
, then

\eta(X)\Rightarrow_{\hat{G}(K)}^{*}(a)
.

Proof.

The symbol observation
\mathrm{obs}_{\mathrm{NT}}(X)=\chi(X)[\omega(X)]
and the rule
observation
\mathrm{obs}_{\mathrm{Rule}}(X\to(a))=\chi(X)[(a)]
both belong to
K
.
By Proposition
7.11
(i), applied to the derivation

X\Rightarrow^{*}\omega(X)
, the tuple
\omega(X)
has componentwise

h
-type equal to the output type of
X
. Since

is a typed
terminal rule, Definition

says that the same
output type is
(h(a))=h^{(1)}((a))

and

Lemma 9.10

(Exposure simulates binary rules)
.

Assume
\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})\subseteq K\subseteq L
. If

R:X\to\rho(Y,Z)
is a typed binary rule of
\widetilde{G}
, then

\eta(X)\Rightarrow_{\hat{G}(K)}^{*}\rho(\eta(Y),\eta(Z)).

Proof.

Let
\rho\colon A\to(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{e})(B,C)
be the underlying
binary template, and put
\vec{z}_{R}=\rho(\omega(Y),\omega(Z))
. The
symbol observation
\chi(X)[\omega(X)]
and the rule observation

\chi(X)[\vec{z}_{R}]
both belong to
K
. Since

\vec{z}_{R}\in L_{X}(\widetilde{G})
, Proposition
7.11
(i)
gives
h^{(e)}(\vec{z}_{R})=h^{(e)}(\omega(X))
. The tuples

\omega(X)
and
\vec{z}_{R}
are observed in the same concrete

\chi(X)
, with interface type
\tau(X)
. Hence the learner
adds the typed substitution rule
\eta(X)\to[\vec{z}_{R}:\tau(X)]
.

It remains to see that the learner adds the binary rule

[\vec{z}_{R}:\tau(X)]\to\rho(\eta(Y),\eta(Z))
. By
Lemma
9.8
, the child symbols
\eta(Y)

and
\eta(Z)
are observed typed tuples of
\hat{G}(K)
. The parent
occurrence is the concrete occurrence of
\vec{z}_{R}
inside

\omega(Y)
and

\omega(Z)
, and the segmentations are the template-induced

\rho

\rho(\omega(Y),\omega(Z))_{i}=\alpha_{i}[\omega(Y)/x,\omega(Z)/y]
contains
exactly
\omega(Y)_{j}
at the position of the variable

, and
exactly
\omega(Z)_{k}
at the position of

is
linear and nondeleting, each child variable determines exactly one

\vec{z}_{R}

8.12

8.14
.

It remains to check that the witness is typed. Since

\widetilde{G}

survivor of a rule introduced by Definition

. By

\tau(Y)=\mathrm{IT}_{B}^{\rho}(\tau(X),h^{(d_{C})}(\omega(Z))),\qquad\tau(Z)=\mathrm{IT}_{C}^{\rho}(\tau(X),h^{(d_{B})}(\omega(Y))).

These are exactly the interface checks required in

[\vec{z}_{R}:\tau(X)]\to\rho(\eta(Y),\eta(Z))
.
Combining this rule with the substitution rule

∎

Remark 9.16

(Presentation dependence of the characteristic sample)
.

The set
\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})
depends on the chosen witnessing working
presentation
G
. This does not weaken the Gold-style identification
claim. For a language
L\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mcf}}_{f,h}
, the definition of the class
provides at least one reduced working binary linear nondeleting
presentation
G
satisfying the required assumptions and
L(G)=L
.
Choosing such a presentation yields one finite sufficient sample

\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})\subseteq L
. The learner is not given
G
,

\widetilde{G}
, or
\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})
; this set is used only as an
existence witness in the proof that every text for
L
eventually
contains enough positive data for exact reconstruction. Thus

\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})
is a target-dependent existence witness, not an
oracle input to the learner. The result is a Gold-style finite-locking
theorem together with polynomial-time construction of the current
hypothesis from a given finite sample.

10
Polynomial-Time Construction from a Given Sample

This section gives the algorithmic part of the main theorem. For fixed
fan-out bound
f
and fixed explicit homomorphism
h
, the interface
spaces, componentwise
h
-type spaces, and bounded rule-template choices
are finite sets depending only on these fixed parameters. The learner can
therefore enumerate observed typed tuples, concrete shared contexts, binary
witnesses, and typed substitution rules from the finite sample
K
. We
prove that the resulting hypothesis
\hat{G}(K)
is constructible in time
polynomial in
\|K\|_{+}
, including output size. More specifically, the
construction below yields
O_{f}(\|K\|_{+}^{2f})
nonterminals and

O_{f}(\|K\|_{+}^{4f+2})
typed substitution rules, with the remaining rule
families also polynomially enumerable for fixed
f
and
h
. This is a
polynomial-time hypothesis-construction theorem from the sample at hand;
it is not a claim that every target has a polynomial-size characteristic
sample.

Lemma 10.1

(Observed tuple enumeration)
.

For fixed
f
and
h
, the set of observed typed tuples in
K
can be
enumerated in time polynomial in
\|K\|_{+}
. Each concrete context key is
stored as a string with named holes of length at most the length of the
sample word from which it is obtained.

Proof.

For a word
w
of length
n
and arity
d\leq f
, a tuple
occurrence is determined by at most
2d
interval endpoints or cut
positions, together with the names of the slots. Positive-length slots
are required to be pairwise disjoint. Empty slots may share the same cut
position; when several empty slots are placed at one cut, their local
left-to-right tie order is part of the occurrence data. Since

d\leq f
, the number of possible local tie orders contributes only a
constant factor depending on
f
. Thus the number of tuple occurrences
is still
O_{f}(n^{2d})
, hence polynomial for fixed
f
.

For each occurrence, the concrete context string and the tuple strings
have total length
O(n)
, and
\tau_{h}(E)
is computed by scanning the
boundary strings and multiplying in the fixed monoid. Summing over

w\in K
gives a polynomial bound in
\|K\|_{+}
. Hashing or comparing
concrete context strings, including the named holes and any empty-slot
tie-order data, is included in this bound.
∎

Lemma 10.2

(Binary witness enumeration)
.

For fixed
f
and
h
, all typed binary witnesses in
K
can be
enumerated, and the corresponding binary witness rules can be output, in
time polynomial in
\|K\|_{+}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
10.1
, there are polynomially many observed
typed tuples and polynomially many parent tuple occurrences. Fix a
parent tuple
\vec{z}
of arity
e\leq f
and two observed child tuples
of arities
d_{B},d_{C}\leq f
. A concrete template witnessing

\vec{z}=\rho(\vec{x},\vec{y})
is determined by choosing, inside the
components of
\vec{z}
, one occurrence interval for each of the

d_{B}+d_{C}\leq 2f
child strings, assigning the distinct variables

x^{1},\dots,x^{d_{B}},y^{1},\dots,y^{d_{C}}
to these intervals, and taking the
remaining substrings as terminal gaps. The number of such choices is
bounded by a polynomial in the total length of
\vec{z}
for fixed
f
,
times the constant
(2f)!
. Each candidate is checked by string
comparison and by verifying that every variable is used exactly once.
Zero-length intervals at cut positions account for empty child
components, and equal substrings at different positions are kept as
different occurrence choices; both conventions only multiply the count by
a polynomial factor for fixed
f
. When several zero-length variable
intervals are placed at the same cut position, their left-to-right order
is treated as part of the finite segmentation data. Since at most
2f

variables are involved, this introduces only a constant-factor multiplier
depending on the fixed fan-out bound
f
. The transported child
interfaces are computed using the fixed finite monoid. Therefore the number
of witnesses and their total output size are polynomial in
\|K\|_{+}
.
∎

Lemma 10.3

(Enumeration of typed substitution rules)
.

Let
K\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
be a finite positive sample and let

N:=\|K\|_{+}
. For fixed fan-out bound
f
and fixed explicit finite
monoid homomorphism
h\colon\Sigma^{*}\to M
, the typed substitution rules
of
\hat{G}(K)
introduced by clause
(iv)
of
Definition
8.15
can be enumerated in time polynomial in

N
, and their total number is polynomial in
N
.

Proof.

We work in three steps: counting observed concrete context/tuple pairs,
grouping them to identify candidate substitution rules, and deduplicating
the output. Throughout, we distinguish
observed typed tuples

[\vec{x}:\tau]
, whose count was bounded in Lemma
10.1
,
from
observed concrete pairs

(E,\vec{x})
, which record the
concrete sentence context
E
together with the tuple
\vec{x}
such
that
E[\vec{x}]\in K
. Clause (iv) of Definition
8.15

requires a
shared concrete
sentence context
E
, not merely
equality of sentence-interface types; concrete contexts with equal
interface type are not interchangeable for this purpose.

Step 1: Counting observed concrete pairs.

Fix
w\in K
of length
n
. An arity-
d
concrete tuple occurrence

(E,\vec{x})
with
E[\vec{x}]=w
can be specified by choosing at
most
2d
cut positions in
\{0,\ldots,n\}
, together with the
left-to-right order and the names of the
d
components. This gives
at most
O_{f}(n^{2d})
choices for fixed
d\leq f
. Zero-length
components and multiple zero-length intervals at the same cut position
are handled by including their left-to-right order and component names
in the finite occurrence data; this changes the bound only by a
constant depending on
f
. Summing over
1\leq d\leq f
gives

O_{f}(n^{2f})
observed concrete pairs per word. Summing over all

w\in K
and bounding by
|K|\cdot\max_{w\in K}|w|^{2f}\leq N^{2f+1}
,
the total number of observed concrete pairs across all arities and all
words is
O_{f}(N^{2f+1})
.

For each observed concrete pair
(E,\vec{x})
of arity
d
, the
interface type
\tau_{h}(E)\in\mathcal{I}_{h}(d)
is computed by scanning the

d+1
boundary strings of
E
and multiplying in the fixed finite
monoid
M
. The componentwise
h
-type
h^{(d)}(\vec{x})
is
computed by scanning the
d
component strings and applying
h

letterwise. Since
M
is a fixed finite monoid with an explicitly
given multiplication table, each computation takes time
O(n)
. Hence
all observed concrete pairs, together with their interface types and
componentwise
h
-types, can be enumerated and stored in time polynomial
in
N
for fixed
f
.

Step 2: Grouping and identifying candidate rules.

By clause (iv) of Definition
8.15
, a typed substitution
rule
[\vec{x}:\tau]\to[\vec{y}:\tau]
is added precisely when there
exists a concrete arity-
d
sentence context
E
such that

E[\vec{x}]\in K
,
E[\vec{y}]\in K
,

\tau_{h}(E)=\tau
, and
h^{(d)}(\vec{x})=h^{(d)}(\vec{y})
. The
shared concrete context
E
is a necessary part of this condition:
equality of interface type
\tau_{h}(E_{1})=\tau_{h}(E_{2})
between two
distinct concrete contexts
E_{1}\neq E_{2}
does not suffice.

We therefore group observed concrete pairs by the explicit key

\bigl(E,\;h^{(d)}(\vec{x})\bigr),

where
E
is stored as the concrete string with named holes obtained
from
w
by cutting at the chosen positions, and
h^{(d)}(\vec{x})

is the componentwise
h
-type vector. Concretely, contexts are encoded as
strings over the extended alphabet

\Sigma\cup\{\square_{1},\ldots,\square_{f}\}
, where the hole symbols are
distinct from terminals and from one another. Equality of concrete
contexts is therefore ordinary equality of such encoded strings, decidable
by a linear-time string comparison. Two observed concrete pairs

(E,\vec{x})
and
(E,\vec{y})
with the same key

\bigl(E,h^{(d)}(\vec{x})\bigr)=\bigl(E,h^{(d)}(\vec{y})\bigr)

satisfy all conditions of clause (iv), and the learner adds the unit
rule

[\vec{x}:\tau_{h}(E)]\to[\vec{y}:\tau_{h}(E)].

The number of ordered pairs
(\vec{x},\vec{y})
within any single
group is at most the square of the group size. Since the total number
of observed concrete pairs is
O_{f}(N^{2f+1})
, the total number of
candidate ordered pairs across all groups is bounded above by the square
of this quantity, namely

O_{f}(N^{2(2f+1)})=O_{f}(N^{4f+2})
.
Checking key equality requires comparing a concrete context string and
a monoid-valued vector; both comparisons run in polynomial time since
context strings have length at most
\max_{w\in K}|w|\leq N
and

h^{(d)}
-type vectors have fixed size
d\leq f
.

Step 3: Deduplication and output.

The same typed substitution rule
[\vec{x}:\tau]\to[\vec{y}:\tau]

may arise from more than one shared concrete context. Duplicate rules
are identified and removed by sorting or hashing all candidate rules on
an explicit encoding of the rule template, which has length
O(N)
per
rule. After deduplication, the number of distinct typed substitution
rules is
O_{f}(N^{4f+2})
and their total encoding length is polynomial
in
N
.

Conclusion.

For fixed
f
and fixed explicit
h
, the entire enumeration of typed
substitution rules runs in time polynomial in
N
and produces

O_{f}(N^{4f+2})
distinct rules. No claim about the size of the
characteristic sample is made or required.
∎

Lemma 10.4

(Unit elimination)
.

Let
H
be a finite extended grammar obtained from a working binary linear
nondeleting MCFG by adding unit productions only between nonterminals of the
same fan-out. There is a polynomial-time construction of an equivalent
grammar without unit productions. The number of produced non-unit rules is
polynomial in
|H|
.

Proof.

In the application to
\hat{G}(K)
, all unit rules arise from typed
substitution rules
[\vec{x}:\tau]\to[\vec{y}:\tau]
between observed
typed tuples of the same arity. Hence unit edges always connect
nonterminals of the same fan-out. The unit-closure construction below
therefore preserves fan-out throughout.

Let
N_{H}
be the number of nonterminals of
H
, and let

P_{\mathrm{nu}}
be the set of non-unit rules of
H
. Let
U
be
the directed graph on nonterminals whose edges are the unit productions.
Compute the reflexive transitive closure
U^{*}
in polynomial time. For
every pair
A\,U^{*}\,B
and every non-unit rule
B\to\theta
, add the
rule
A\to\theta
. Then delete all unit productions.

Language preservation is the standard unit-closure argument. In any
derivation tree that derives a terminal tuple, a maximal block of unit
steps cannot be the final part of the derivation: unit rules lead from one
nonterminal to another, whereas a terminal tuple contains only symbols from

\Sigma
. Hence every maximal unit block in such a derivation is
followed by a terminal or binary non-unit step. Thus every maximal block
of unit steps

A=A_{0}\Rightarrow A_{1}\Rightarrow\cdots\Rightarrow A_{m}=B

followed by a non-unit step
B\to\theta
is replaced by the single
added rule
A\to\theta
. Conversely, every added rule
A\to\theta

is simulated in
H
by a unit path
A\Rightarrow^{*}B
followed by
the original non-unit rule
B\to\theta
. Hence the unit-free grammar
is language-equivalent to
H
.

The number of pairs
(A,B)
in
U^{*}
is at most
N_{H}^{2}
. Therefore
the construction adds at most
N_{H}^{2}\cdot|P_{\mathrm{nu}}|
non-unit
rules. Each added rule reuses the right-hand side of an existing
non-unit rule. Thus the number of produced rules and the total encoding
size are polynomial in
|H|
, and the construction is polynomial-time.
∎

Theorem 10.5

(Polynomial-time hypothesis construction)
.

For fixed
f
and fixed explicit
h
, the learner grammar
\hat{G}(K)
is
constructible in time polynomial in
\|K\|_{+}
, including output size.
After the unit-elimination construction of Lemma
10.4
,
an equivalent unit-free hypothesis grammar is also obtainable in
polynomial time.

Proof.

We verify each clause of Definition
8.15
in turn, then
combine the bounds.

Observed typed tuples and context keys.

By Lemma
10.1
, all observed typed tuples

[\vec{x}:\tau]
and their associated concrete context keys can be
enumerated in time polynomial in
\|K\|_{+}
. The number of observed
typed tuples is
O_{f}(\|K\|_{+}^{2f})
, and each concrete context key is
a string of length at most the length of the sample word from which it
is drawn.

Start rules (clause (i)).

For each
w\in K
, the pair
(\square_{1},(w))
is an observed concrete
pair of arity
1
with interface type

\tau_{\mathrm{st}}=(\mathrm{id}_{1};1_{M},1_{M})
. The start rule

\widehat{S}\to[(w):\tau_{\mathrm{st}}]
is added immediately. The number of
start rules is at most
|K|\leq\|K\|_{+}
.

Terminal rules (clause (ii)).

For each observed typed tuple
[(a):\tau]
with
a\in\Sigma
, the
terminal rule
[(a):\tau]\to(a)
is added. The number of such rules
is at most the number of observed typed tuples, hence

O_{f}(\|K\|_{+}^{2f})
.

Binary witness rules (clause (iii)).

By Lemma
10.2
, all typed binary witnesses
and the corresponding binary witness rules can be enumerated in time
polynomial in
\|K\|_{+}
, and their total number is polynomial in

\|K\|_{+}
.

Typed substitution rules (clause (iv)).

By Lemma
10.3
, the typed
substitution rules required by clause (iv) can be enumerated in time
polynomial in
\|K\|_{+}
and their total number is

O_{f}(\|K\|_{+}^{4f+2})
, hence polynomial in
\|K\|_{+}
for fixed
f

and fixed explicit
h
. The key point is that clause (iv) requires a

concrete
shared sentence context
E
with
E[\vec{x}]\in K

and
E[\vec{y}]\in K
, not merely equality of sentence-interface
types; Lemma
10.3
explicitly counts
observed concrete context/tuple pairs and correctly accounts for this
requirement, distinguishing them from the separately bounded observed
typed tuples.

Total grammar size and construction time.

Combining the four clauses, the extended grammar
\hat{G}(K)
has

O_{f}(\|K\|_{+}^{2f})
nonterminal symbols and

O_{f}(\|K\|_{+}^{4f+2})
rules in total. Each rule template is encoded
using substrings of sample words and fixed monoid values, so the total
encoding length is polynomial in
\|K\|_{+}
. The entire construction
runs in time polynomial in
\|K\|_{+}
for fixed
f
and fixed explicit

h
.

Unit elimination.

Applying Lemma
10.4
to
\hat{G}(K)
eliminates
all unit rules, including the typed substitution rules. The extended
grammar
\hat{G}(K)
has polynomial size in
\|K\|_{+}
by the preceding
bounds, and the unit-elimination lemma is polynomial in the size of the
input grammar. Hence the resulting equivalent unit-free grammar has
polynomial size and is obtained in polynomial time in
\|K\|_{+}
for
fixed
f
and fixed explicit
h
.
∎

10.1
Exposure size and the polynomial-data boundary

The preceding theorem is a polynomial-time hypothesis-construction
result from a given finite sample. It does not by itself imply a
polynomial-size characteristic sample for the full class

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mcf}}_{f,h}
. The following parameter isolates exactly where a
polynomial-data theorem would require additional structure.

Definition 10.6

(Exposure size)
.

Let
G
be a target grammar and let
\widetilde{G}
be its trimmed
typed refinement. Define the exposure size
B(G,h)
to be the maximum
positive length of the words

\mathrm{obs}_{\mathrm{NT}}(X),\qquad\mathrm{obs}_{\mathrm{Rule}}(R),

where
X
ranges over typed nonterminals of
\widetilde{G}
and

R
ranges over realized non-start typed rules of
\widetilde{G}
.

Proposition 10.7

(Characteristic sample size under an exposure bound)
.

Let
N_{\mathrm{NT}}
be the number of typed nonterminals of

\widetilde{G}
, and let
N_{\mathrm{Rule}}
be the number of realized
non-start typed rules of
\widetilde{G}
. Then

|\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})|\leq N_{\mathrm{NT}}+N_{\mathrm{Rule}}

and

\|\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})\|_{+}\leq(N_{\mathrm{NT}}+N_{\mathrm{Rule}})B(G,h).

Consequently, any subclass for which
N_{\mathrm{NT}}
,
N_{\mathrm{Rule}}
, and

B(G,h)
are polynomially bounded in the chosen target presentation
size has polynomial-size characteristic samples.

Proof.

By definition,
\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})
consists of one symbol observation
for each typed nonterminal and one rule observation for each realized
non-start typed rule. Hence its cardinality is at most

N_{\mathrm{NT}}+N_{\mathrm{Rule}}
, allowing for possible coincidences among
observation words. Each word appearing in these observations has
positive size at most
B(G,h)
. Summing over all observations gives

\|\mathrm{CS}(\widetilde{G})\|_{+}\leq(N_{\mathrm{NT}}+N_{\mathrm{Rule}})B(G,h).

The final statement is immediate.
∎

This proposition does not assert a polynomial exposure bound for the full
class
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mcf}}_{f,h}
. Its purpose is to isolate the exact point at
which a polynomial-data theorem would require additional structure. The
present paper proves finite exact reconstruction and polynomial-time
hypothesis construction from a given sample; polynomial-size
characteristic samples are obtained for subclasses in which the exposure
size is polynomially controlled.

11
Discussion and Scope

The result proved here is a fixed-parameter reconstruction theorem. The
fan-out bound
f
, the explicit finite homomorphism

h\colon\Sigma^{*}\to M
, and the working binary linear nondeleting
presentation discipline are part of the setting; they are not inferred from
positive data. The theorem is not a normal-form theorem for arbitrary MCFG
presentations. It applies to languages admitting a reduced working
presentation of the specified kind and satisfying the language-level

(f,h)
-tuple substitutability condition.

The typed refinement and the characteristic sample are constructed from a
chosen witnessing presentation. This presentation dependence is harmless for
identification in the limit, because the characteristic sample is only an
existence witness for convergence and is not supplied to the learner. The
polynomial result of Section
10
likewise has a specific scope:
for fixed
f
and fixed explicit
h
, it constructs
\hat{G}(K)
from a
given finite sample
K
in polynomial time, including output size. It is
not a polynomial time-and-data theorem; such a statement would require
uniform polynomial bounds on characteristic samples, as indicated by
Proposition
10.7
.

The comparison with Yoshinaka’s multidimensional substitutability and the
Clark–Yoshinaka MCFG line is a comparison of assumptions and reconstruction
mechanisms, not a simulation or subsumption claim. Yoshinaka’s condition is
an untyped implication, whereas the present theorem compares tuples only
after their componentwise
h
-types agree and uses the fixed monoid together
with sentence-interface transport as finite external control. Natural
extensions left open here include inferring a suitable finite congruence
instead of fixing
h
, treating copied or deleted variables beyond the
linear nondeleting discipline, and identifying subclasses with
polynomial-size characteristic samples.

12
Conclusion

This paper introduced sentence-interface types as finite external control
objects for tuple occurrences in bounded-fan-out linear MCFGs under a fixed
explicit finite monoid homomorphism. The point beyond the CFG case is that
componentwise
h
-type alone does not record how tuple components are placed
in the surrounding sentence. By recording both the placement permutation and
the boundary
h
-values, sentence-interface types make interface transport
through binary rule templates finite and computable. Using this control, we
constructed a typed refinement, a finite characteristic sample, and a
canonical positive-data learner. For every target satisfying

(f,h)
-tuple substitutability, the learner reconstructs the language
exactly once the sample contains the characteristic sample and remains inside
the target language. Hence
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mcf}}_{f,h}
is identifiable in the limit from
positive data for fixed
f
and fixed explicit
h
, and
\hat{G}(K)
is
constructible in polynomial time from each finite sample
K
. Thus
sentence-interface control isolates the finite mechanism by which fixed-
h

distributional reconstruction extends from CFGs to bounded-fan-out linear
MCFGs.
