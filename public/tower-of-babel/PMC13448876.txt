The Impact of Missed Doses on Pharmacokinetic Concentrations for Oral Semaglutide in Comparison to Other Glucagon‐Like Peptide‐1‐Based Therapies
Authors: Rune Viig Overgaard, Henrik Agersø, Christian Hollensen, Naveen Rathor, Eric M Bomberg
Published: 2026

Background

Obesity, a chronic, multifactorial disease estimated to affect more than a billion people worldwide, is associated with numerous adverse health sequelae including metabolic disease, several cancers and mental health disorders [1]. Semaglutide, a glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist (GLP‐1 RA), is now approved for the treatment of obesity and Type 2 diabetes (T2D) as both a once‐weekly subcutaneous injection and a once‐daily oral formulation [2]. In the OASIS 4 trial including adults with obesity (or overweight with ≥ 1 obesity‐related comorbidity), treatment with oral semaglutide 25 mg was shown to reduce body weight by 13.6% (estimated 11.4% placebo‐subtracted difference) for the treatment policy estimand (effect regardless of treatment discontinuation or use of rescue medication, such as other obesity medications), and 16.6% for the trial product estimand (assuming that the drug or placebo was taken without discontinuation or use of rescue medication) [3]. Several other GLP‐1 RA‐based therapies are also available or in development for the treatment of obesity and/or T2D including liraglutide [4], tirzepatide [5] and orforglipron [6].

Missing doses of prescribed medications is common in real‐world settings, and may result from both personal and healthcare/insurance‐related causes [7]. The recommended dosing regimen for GLP‐1 RAs is important [2] as disruptions to the prescribed dosing regimen may alter treatment efficacy and tolerability [7]. Indeed, inconsistent use of GLP‐1 RA‐based therapies can be challenging because tolerance is commonly developed to their known gastrointestinal side effects with regular use. This is also why all GLP‐1 RA‐based therapies, including oral semaglutide, have guidance on resumption of therapy after missed doses [8].

With the array of injectable and oral medication options available, each with unique dosing regimens, it is important to understand the differences between how missed doses affect the drug concentrations achieved via once‐daily and once‐weekly dosing schedules. As deviating from optimal dosing conditions can result in decreased exposure [7], obesity medications that do not substantially vary in efficacy or safety following a missed dose may be preferred by patients and healthcare providers compared with medications that are more sensitive to missed doses. Here, we compare the pharmacokinetic (PK) profile of oral semaglutide 25 mg with subcutaneous semaglutide 2.4 mg, liraglutide 3 mg and tirzepatide 15 mg, and with oral orforglipron 36 mg, all following missed doses. We also indirectly compare previously published efficacy and safety data to determine whether the efficacy and the low rates of adverse event‐related discontinuations seen in oral semaglutide trials are due to a low sensitivity to missed doses.

Methods

This analysis simulated maximum plasma concentrations of once‐daily oral semaglutide 25 mg, once‐weekly subcutaneous semaglutide 2.4 mg, once‐daily subcutaneous liraglutide 3 mg, once‐weekly subcutaneous tirzepatide 15 mg and once‐daily oral orforglipron 36 mg using published population PK models [9, 10, 11, 12, 13].

Each molecule was simulated up to steady state and concentrations were normalised with the maximum concentration (Cmax) to allow for a comparison between the peak and trough. By normalising to Cmax, the relative exposure becomes identical across dose levels, allowing for a comparison of concentrations between the different compounds following a delay of 1, 3 or 7 missed days. PK simulations for each molecule were performed in a White, non‐Hispanic, female participant without T2D, aged 45 years and with a baseline body weight of 105 kg and body mass index of 45 kg/m2. This reference participant was selected as these characteristics are representative of participants in randomised controlled trials of obesity medications among adults [3, 14].

Results

Normalised concentrations for oral semaglutide; subcutaneous semaglutide, liraglutide and tirzepatide; and oral orforglipron are presented in Figure 1. For oral semaglutide, a meaningful decline in exposure of ≥ 50% was observed following 7 days of missed doses (Figure 1a). This pattern was similar for subcutaneous semaglutide (Figure 1c). However, for orforglipron, tirzepatide and liraglutide (Figure 1b,d,e), a meaningful decline (≥ 50%) in exposure was observed following a single missed dose.

Figure 1 shows that PK exposure increases when treatment is reinitiated after missed doses. The long half‐life of oral semaglutide stabilises the PK so that following reinitiation of treatment after 1–7 missed doses, exposure increases in gradual increments. Conversely, following reinitiation of liraglutide or orforglipron, more pronounced relative increases of approximately 400%–700% in exposure were observed after 3 days of missed doses. Conclusions

In this analysis, PK models demonstrated that oral orforglipron, and subcutaneous tirzepatide and liraglutide, show greater sensitivity to missed doses with greater reductions in PK following a missed dose and substantial PK increases following reinitiation, compared with oral and subcutaneous semaglutide. The large changes in PK following reinitiation after missed doses may lead to reductions in efficacy and increases in tolerability issues.

The impact of missing doses depends on a medication's half‐life, and previous clinical trials (Table 1) of orforglipron and liraglutide, with the shortest half‐lives, demonstrated reductions in body weight ranging from 7.8%–12.4%; whereas subcutaneous semaglutide, with a longer half‐life, demonstrated greater body weight reductions ranging from 16.9%–20.7%. When indirectly compared with subcutaneous semaglutide, tirzepatide at the highest dose (15 mg) was associated with greater efficacy and a similar rate of discontinuations due to adverse events; however, these outcomes may have been driven by the action of tirzepatide on both the glucagon‐like peptide‐1 and gastric inhibitory polypeptide receptors, rather than being related to half‐life. Additionally, GLP‐1 RA molecules are not pharmacologically identical and may have different effects on receptor activation.

As PK normalised to Cmax is dose proportional, the impact of missed doses on the relative exposure will be the same across different dose levels and different medications. Therefore, the data in the present analysis are applicable across the various doses of each GLP‐1 RA‐based therapy. As such, the observed greater sensitivity to missed doses for some GLP‐1 RA‐based therapies, likely due to shorter half‐lives, may correspond to a greater reduction in treatment efficacy and increased tolerability issues in a real‐world setting following deviation from recommended dosing. Indeed, all GLP‐1 RA‐based therapies have recommendations for reinitiation following missed doses, and those taking liraglutide and orforglipron are instructed to reinitiate at lower doses following 3 and 7 missed doses, respectively [2, 19, 21]. However, for oral semaglutide there is no recommendation to reinitiate treatment at a lower dose [2], potentially due to its extended half‐life.

Although GLP‐1 RA‐based therapies are generally well tolerated with relatively low treatment discontinuation due to adverse events in clinical trials, discontinuation increases with higher doses [3, 5, 6]. In separate clinical trials, discontinuations due to adverse events, when compared against placebo, were greater for orforglipron and liraglutide compared with oral semaglutide. However, it should be noted that reasons for discontinuation are not as well understood in real‐world compared with clinical trial settings. Due to the long half‐life and once‐daily dosing of oral semaglutide, the return to steady state concentration following reinitiation of the drug was more gradual compared with both liraglutide and orforglipron. Both subcutaneous semaglutide and tirzepatide had similar changes in concentrations following a missed dose, although these were more pronounced with tirzepatide, and similar times for return to steady‐state levels following reinitiation. Indeed, the effect of 7 days of missed doses for oral semaglutide was more similar to subcutaneous semaglutide and tirzepatide, compared with liraglutide and orforglipron. Therefore, these data indicate that oral semaglutide may be more practical in a real‐world setting compared with other once‐daily formulations as it would be less affected by lack of adherence.

Although our PK results are considered robust, and the potential impact on efficacy and safety is well established, the GLP‐1 RA‐based therapies we assessed are inherently different. It should also be noted that real‐world effects of missed doses cannot be established from this analysis of clinical trial data, and data should therefore be interpreted with some caution. Moreover, this analysis did not assess covariates that may contribute to individual causes of PK variability following a missed dose such as sex, body weight, diabetes status or the gut microbiome [22, 23]. In a recently published modelling analysis, it was demonstrated that baseline body weight, sex and diabetes status were the covariates that had the greatest impact for oral semaglutide exposure [22]. Therefore, it will also be important to evaluate how such covariates impact the PK response to missed doses in future studies.

Overall, these findings suggest that the efficacy of oral and subcutaneous semaglutide and subcutaneous tirzepatide may be less affected by deviations from the prescribed dosing regimen, with potentially fewer adverse events following reinitiation of treatment, compared with other GLP‐1 RA‐based therapies. This indicates that oral semaglutide may have more clinical practicality in a real‐world setting due to its lack of sensitivity to missed doses and relatively longer half‐life compared with other once‐daily GLP‐1 RA‐based therapies.
