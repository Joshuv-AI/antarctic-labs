Intrinsic motivation to study and amotivation for most frequently performed activities: Association with increased non‐specific psychological distress
Authors: Reiko Shiba, Yuki Ueno, Shuntaro Ando, Hironori Nakatani, Shinsuke Koike
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimNon‐specific psychological distress is closely associated with the risk of depression. Accordingly, assessing motivation on this distress can provide important information for preventing depression. In this study, we investigated the relationship between motivational state and non‐specific psychological distress in participants with a 1‐year follow‐up.MethodsTwo hundred and sixty‐eight participants (117 men, 15.3–57.7 years, mean age = 23.7 years, SD = 7.8) were publicly recruited and administered a self‐reported questionnaire. The 135 of all participants completed a 1‐year follow‐up. We translated and adapted the Perceived Locus of Causality Questionnaire (PLOCQ) into Japanese to be applicable to motivation for “study” (PLCQ‐J‐study) and for “most frequently performed activities” (PLCQ‐J‐time) and used them in the baseline survey. The Kessler 6 (K6), a 6‐item scale assessing non‐specific psychological distress, was administered in both surveys.ResultsIn a hierarchical multiple regression analysis, K6 scores at 1‐year follow‐up were predicted by intrinsic motivation in the PLCQ‐J‐study (B [SE] = 0.259 [0.103], t = 2.51, p < 0.05), amotivation in the PLCQ‐J‐time (B [SE] = 0.287 [0.086], t = 3.33, p < 0.01), and K6 score at baseline (B [SE] = 0.436 [0.090], t = 4.83, p < 0.001).ConclusionWe identified the relationship between motivation in multiple domains as well as in non‐specific psychological distress. Understanding multiple perspectives on motivation could reveal a new understanding of non‐specific psychological distress.Keywords: amotivation, intrinsic motivation, longitudinal study, non‐specific psychological distress, Perceived Locus of Causality Questionnaire

INTRODUCTION

Markedly diminished interest or pleasure in activities and psychomotor retardation are known to be core features of major depressive disorder (MDD), as described in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders‐5‐Text Revision (DSM‐5‐TR). 1 Decreased motivation can be a result of depressive symptoms; this has been proven by theoretical studies, 2 physiological experiments that measured the responses of 40 adults to rewards, 3 and a questionnaire‐based cohort study of 319 individuals in their early 20s. 4 In a comparative experiment between 22 MDD patients and 27 healthy participants, motivation‐based interventions improved cognitive abilities more than a non‐motivational control condition did. 5 Furthermore, in a questionnaire‐based cohort study of 228 Italian university students, high motivation was a good predictor of low depression 1 year after the study. 6 However, a cohort study of 510 college students in the United States (n = 279) and the United Kingdom (n = 231) reported that high conditioned goal setting was uniquely correlated with high depression. 7 Furthermore, a cohort study of 537 college students in China reported a weaker negative association between intrinsic motivation to learn and depression in participants with higher levels of interpersonal conflict. 8 Although the states of motivation and depression are considered interrelated, several unknowns exist regarding their direct relationship. By clarifying how they are related, information on motivational state may provide useful information for the prevention of depression.

Motivation is a process of initiating and maintaining purpose‐oriented behavior and controlling one's activity patterns. 9 , 10 Motivational research covers a wide range—from physiological to complex social demands—and several studies on motivation have been conducted on the participant so far. 11 , 12 Several motivational theories have been constructed focusing on different aspects of psychology. A review summarized five major theories for motivational studies within education over the past 20 years. 9 According to this review of major theories for motivational studies within education, self‐determination theory (SDT) provides the most comprehensive theoretical framework. SDT focuses on variables related to intrinsic motivation and various forms of extrinsic motivation. 13 , 14

In SDT, categories of motivation include “amotivation,” representing no motivation at all, “extrinsic motivation,” which is produced by external forces, and “intrinsic motivation,” which prompts a person to act on their own internal desires. Additionally, these categories fall on an axis from non‐self‐determined to self‐determined. 13 According to SDT, amotivation is defined as the state of lacking intention to act. The path of internalization from extrinsic motivation to intrinsic motivation is explained by satisfying the three basic needs for competence, 15 relatedness, 16 and autonomy. 17 , 18 Organic integration theory was introduced as a sub‐theory within SDT to break extrinsic motivation into four consecutive contextual factors, namely, “external regulation,” “introjected regulation,” “identified regulation,” and “integrated regulation.” 13 , 19 Ryan and Connell created the self‐regulation questionnaire (SRQ) based on the concept of perceived locus of causality (PLOC). 20 The SRQ consists of four subscales: “external,” “introjection,” “identification,” and “intrinsic.”

Numerous studies have been conducted in various fields, from education to medicine, and scales created to measure motivation and its effects. Based on the SRQ, some scales have been created to measure motivation for school physical education. 21 , 22 Subsequently, most motivational studies focused on a specific single activity such as physical education, 23 , 24 study, 25 , 26 appetite, 27 and addiction. 28 Some studies have also examined the relationship between motivation and other factors that influence each other, such as satisfaction, frustration, resilience, and self‐esteem. 24 , 29 To analyze motivation from multiple perspectives, it is important to measure and compare motivations in multiple domains. However, few studies have measured motivation in multiple domains simultaneously and analyzed them from multiple angles.

Research suggests that motivational factors related to frequently performed activities can affect one's mental health. For example, recent reports have shown that patients with depression rely on cell phones to relieve negative emotions and spend considerable time on communication activities available on mobile phone, such as texting and social media, without specific and clear goals in mind. 30 Additionally, excessive or continuously watching a series of TV programs in isolation can lead to additional feelings of isolation and loneliness. 31 Such behavior has a statistically significant positive relationship with depression, 32 and increases the likelihood of symptoms of depression, loneliness, and anxiety. 33 , 34 Accordingly, examining motivation related to one's most frequently performed activities may provide valuable insights, even considering their variability.

To address these issues, we selected “learning” and “most frequently performed activities” as two distinct yet complementary domains of daily life. Learning is a fundamental activity—not only for students but also for adults engaging in continuous self‐improvement—and has been shown to support psychological well‐being across the lifespan. 35 Conversely, “most frequently performed activities” encompass the primary behaviors that occupy a significant portion of an individual's daily life, whether they are leisure‐based or mandatory, such as work. By examining both a focused, goal‐oriented domain (learning) and a broader, routine‐based domain simultaneously, we aimed to capture a broader spectrum of motivational profiles and their associations with mental health.

In this study, we developed two motivational questionnaires. Specifically, we modified the Perceived Locus of Causality Questionnaire (PLOCQ) to create two new scales: one to assess motivation to “study” (PLCQ‐J‐study) and one to assess motivation related to “most frequently performed activities” (PLCQ‐J‐time). We then examined whether these scales could predict non‐specific psychological distress at a 1‐year follow‐up. The PLOCQ is based on SDT. Previous studies have analyzed motivation in a single domain. Here, we aimed to analyze the relationship between motivation and non‐specific psychological distress from a new perspective. Additionally, we analyze new relationships by surveying and analyzing motivation for multiple domains simultaneously.

METHODS

Participants and procedure

Two hundred and sixty‐eight participants (117 men, 15.3–57.7 years, mean age = 23.7 years, SD = 7.8) were categorized into three groups based on their recruitment source and timing. Group 1 (n = 167) served as the primary cohort and was recruited through a job recruitment website and high school bulletin boards. To ensure an adequate sample size for factor analysis and to conduct test–retest reliability assessments, Group 2 (n = 57) and Group 3 (n = 44) were subsequently recruited. Group 2 consisted of individuals recruited from university lectures and laboratory‐affiliated networks, whereas Group 3 was recruited via email from a pre‐existing research participant pool, with the survey administered by mail. Data from all three groups were combined for the initial factor structure analysis. For the longitudinal study, changes in non‐specific psychological distress were evaluated using a subset of Group 1 at follow‐up. Meanwhile, test–retest reliability was established through follow‐up data from subsets of Groups 2 and 3. Detailed demographic characteristics for all groups are summarized in Table 1.

All surveys were conducted using anonymous questionnaires at the University of Tokyo or by mail. The questionnaire for the first survey of Group 1 contained the PLCQ‐J‐study, PLCQ‐J‐time, and assessing non‐specific psychological distress (Kessler 6; K6) and was conducted between May and June, 2017. As confounding factors, participants' age, gender, estimated IQ, and socioeconomic status (SES) were collected. For the second survey, conducted approximately 55 weeks (SD = 3.2 weeks) after the first one, 135 participants in Group 1 (70 men and 65 women, 15–57 years, mean age = 23.8 years, SD = 8.7) completed the K6. There were no significant differences in the demographic variables and PLCQ scores between those who either did or did not participate in the second survey (all p > 0.05).

Data from 45 participants (20 men, 20–51 years old, mean age = 25.6 years, SD = 8.6) in Groups 2 and 3 who responded twice to the PLCQ‐J‐study, PLCQ‐J‐time, and the Japanese Self‐Determination Theory‐based Measure of Academic Motivation (SAMJ 36 ) were used for validity and reliability analyses, including criterion‐related validity and test–retest reliability. The first survey was conducted between September 2018 and May 2019 (Group 2) and between June and July 2019 (Group 3); the second survey was conducted 6.6 weeks after the first survey (SD = 0.52). This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee, the University of Tokyo (approval nos 22‐397 and 19‐27). Informed consent was obtained from all participants in written form prior to their inclusion in the study.

Questionnaires

Assessing non‐specific psychological distress

The K6 is a 6‐item short‐form of the K10 for the US National Health Interview Survey, used to assess non‐specific psychological distress. 37 Each item assesses how frequently the respondent experienced each symptom of psychological distress in the last 30 days, using a five‐point Likert scale (0—“None” to 4—“All of the time,” range: 0–24). 37 The K6 score is used to screen for non‐specific psychological distress, depending on the cut‐off score. The scale has been used in various countries as a brief, effective screening tool 38 , 39 , 40 and in the WHO World Mental Health Surveys. 41 In this study, we used the Japanese version of the K6, which has confirmed validity and reliability for assessing non‐specific psychological distress. 42

The PLOCQ for study and time in Japanese

We translated the PLOCQ 22 into Japanese and modified it to assess the non‐specific psychological distress indicators examined in this study. The PLOCQ consists of 20 items using a 7‐point Likert scale (1—“Not true at all” to 7—“Very true”), and includes five factors (amotivation, external regulation, introjected regulation, identified regulation, and intrinsic motivation) for the locus of causality for physical education. 22 We translated the PLOCQ into Japanese and modified it into the PLCQ‐J‐study and PLCQ‐J‐time to assess motivation for “study” and “most frequently performed activities,” respectively (Table S1). To ensure conceptual equivalence, the original PLOCQ was first translated into Japanese by a bilingual researcher and then back‐translated into English by an independent bilingual researcher blinded to the original version to verify the accuracy of the psychological nuances. The two Japanese versions, PLCQ‐J‐study and PLCQ‐J‐time, are structurally identical in terms of their item content and factor structure. The only modification was the adaptation of the target behavioral domain within the instructions and item stems (i.e., specifying the target as either “studying” for PLCQ‐J‐study or “the thing I spend the most time on besides studying now identified by each participant” for PLCQ‐J‐time).

We obtained 15 types of responses from 268 participants for the PLCQ‐J‐time item, “What do you spend the most time doing, except for study” (Table S2). Using these responses, we conducted confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and validation of these questionnaires.

Other questionnaires

The SAMJ is a short Japanese version of the SRQ that was developed and confirmed to measure autonomous motivation for study. 36 It consists of 20 items using a four‐point scale, ranging from 1 “Not true at all” to 4 “Very true.” The items are grouped into four factors: external regulation, introjected regulation, identified regulation, and intrinsic regulation, which are grounded in SDT. 13 , 19 We used the SAMJ to confirm the external validity of the PLCQ‐J‐study and PLCQ‐J‐time. To control for confounding factors, the Japanese Adult Reading Test (JART‐25; abridged version) was used as the Japanese version of the National Adult Reading Test (NART), 43 with 25 Japanese irregularly pronounced Kanji words as a measure of estimated IQ. 44 For SES, the higher final educational status of the participant's parents with a seven‐category scale was used, ranging from 0 “elementary school” to 7 “postgraduate doctoral degree.” Parental educational attainment was used as an indicator for SES. While SES has traditionally been indicated using indicators such as education, occupation, and income (e.g., the Hollingshead Scale), parental educational attainment is stable among these and is recognized as a reliable indicator for household economic status. 45 Accordingly, we adopted a simplified SES scale developed for the Japanese population, which quantifies SES based solely on educational background. 46

Statistical analysis

First, a CFA was conducted to examine the validity of the PLCQ‐J‐study and PLCQ‐J‐time scales in Japanese with a five‐factor model consisting of 20 items defined in the original PLOCQ. The obtained data analyses were performed in R (version 3.5.1, the R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). A good model–data fit is indicated by comparative fit index (CFI) values around 0.95 or higher, standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) values around 0.08 or lower, and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) values around 0.06 or lower. However, it is difficult to make a uniform judgment because the values vary depending on the conditions. An acceptable fit is indicated by CFI values between 0.90 and 0.94, SRMR values between 0.09 and 0.10, and RMSEA values between 0.07 and 0.10. 47 To verify the internal consistency of each constituent factor, Cronbach's alpha coefficient was calculated using the R “psych,” “psy,” and “lavaan” packages. A Cronbach's alpha value of greater than 0.80 was used as the cut‐off criterion.

If the models did not fit well according to the original factor structure, we performed an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with each questionnaire to find a factor structure that was equivalent in both. Maximum likelihood and varimax rotation were used. An eigenvalue > 1 was used as the criterion for factor selection, 48 and the items that composed common factors between both questionnaires were extracted. A CFA was conducted to examine the reliability of the selected factors. To examine the criterion‐related validity, Pearson's correlation analysis was performed between the PLCQ‐J‐study, PLCQ‐J‐time, and SAMJ. Test–retest reliability was also examined with the PLCQ‐J‐study, PLCQ‐J‐time, and SAMJ using Pearson's correlation analysis.

To test the relationship between confirmed motivation indices and non‐specific psychological distress, Pearson's correlation analysis and a series of hierarchical multiple regression analyses were performed. To assess the longitudinal progression over 1 year, the “change” in distress was operationalized as the baseline‐adjusted change, rather than a simple difference score. This approach allowed us to assess each participant's progression relative to their own starting point, effectively isolating the “independent change” by statistically removing the influence of baseline scores. Specifically, by entering the baseline K6 score as a covariate in the first step of the hierarchical multiple regression analyses, we statistically controlled for initial distress levels. This procedure allowed us to identify the independent contribution of baseline motivation to the subsequent status of psychological distress at the 1‐year follow‐up. Under this analytical framework, hierarchical multiple regression analyses were performed using the centered value of each factor and the R “psych” package to determine the most suitable model. Statistical significance was set at 0.05. We compared five models to explain the effects on the K6 score in the 1‐year follow‐up survey. Model 1 examined the effects of age, gender, JART‐25 score, and SES as confounding factors. Models 2 and 3 examined the effects of PLCQ‐J‐study or PLCQ‐J‐time added to Model 1, respectively. Model 4 examined the effects of both PLCQ‐J‐study and PLCQ‐J‐time added to Model 1. Model 5 examined the effects of the interaction factors of PLCQ‐J‐study Intrinsic and PLCQ‐J‐time amotivation, two of the PLCQ‐J‐study and PLCQ‐J‐time factors added to Model 4 that showed significant effects. The effectiveness of the added variable was determined by the amount of change in the coefficient of determination R 2 when the variable of interest was added. Analysis of variance among the coefficient of determination R 2 was performed to determine the most suitable model.

RESULTS

Confirmation and validation of the PLCQ‐J‐study and PLCQ‐J‐time

Reliability and validity of the reconstructed five‐factor model

The CFI value of the PLCQ‐J‐study was 0.80, and the RMSEA value was 0.12, while those for the PLCQ‐J‐time were 0.66 and 0.16, respectively. The validation factor analysis with CFA did not fit well. In addition, Cronbach's alpha coefficients for most subscores were below 0.80. Therefore, the five‐factor model did not fit well to our questionnaires.

Reliability and validity of the reconstructed three‐factor model

We performed an EFA to find a factor composition that was equivalent in both questionnaires. Scree plots suggested a three‐factor structure for the PLCQ‐J‐study and a six‐factor structure for the PLCQ‐J‐time (Table S3). Finally, 12 common items with 3 factors (amotivation [Items 1–4], External regulation [Items 6, 10 –12], intrinsic motivation [Items 13, 18–20]; Tables 1 and S1) were obtained from both questionnaires. CFAs were carried out for the three‐factor models of the PLCQ‐J‐study and PLCQ‐J‐time. The model for the PLCQ‐J‐study had a good fit (CFI = 0.97, SRMR = 0.055, RMSEA = 0.065 [90% confidence interval = 0.048–0.082], Figure 1a) and an acceptable fit for the PLCQ‐J‐time (CFI = 0.94, SRMR = 0.081, RMSEA = 0.087 [90% confidence interval = 0.071–0.103], Figure 1b). Cronbach's alpha coefficient was above 0.80 for all subscores (Table 2).

Hierarchical multiple regression analysis showed significant differences between Model 1 and Models 4 (F(1,6) = 2.483, p = 0.027; Table 4) and 5 (F(1,7) = 2.280, p = 0.03235) but not between Model 1 and Model 2 (F(1,3) = 0.916, p = 0.436) or Model 3 (F(1,3) = 2.1666, p = 0.095). There was no significant difference between Models 4 and 5 (F(1,1) = 1.054, p = 0.307). The hierarchical multiple regression analysis results showed that Model 4 with both questionnaires added to the confounders had an adjusted R 2 = 0.306, residual standard error = 5.300 (df = 123), F‐statistic = 6.383 (df = 11; 123), p < 0.001, and the largest change in the coefficient of determination R 2 when the target variable was added. This suggested that the model was the most suitable one. In the results of Model 4, main effects of K6 score at baseline (B = 0.436, t = 4.834, p < 0.001), PLCQ‐J‐study intrinsic motivation (PL_study_Int; B = 0.259, t = 2.511, p < 0.05), and PLCQ‐J‐time amotivation (PL_time_Amo; B = 0.287, t = 3.328, p < 0.01) were significant (Table 4). The value of the constant was almost zero, because the multiple regression analysis used centered values. The variance inflation factors for all variables were less than 2, confirming that there were no collinearity problems.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we constructed the PLCQ‐J‐study and PLCQ‐J‐time scales as versatile questionnaires assessing motivation to investigate the relationship of motivation for study and most frequently performed activities with non‐specific psychological distress at a 1‐year follow‐up. The results of exploratory and CFA of both questionnaires showed that the three‐factor model consisting of amotivation, External regulation, and intrinsic motivation had a good fit and was internally consistent. Hierarchical multiple regression analysis showed that the model including both questionnaires best predicted the K6 score at the 1‐year follow‐up, and PLCQ‐J‐time amotivation and PLCQ‐J‐study intrinsic motivation at baseline were both positively associated with the change in non‐specific psychological distress at the 1‐year follow‐up. These results suggest that intrinsic motivation in the PLCQ‐J‐study and amotivation in the PLCQ‐J‐time are independently and differentially related to change in non‐specific psychological distress after 1 year. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to predict non‐specific psychological distress at a 1‐year follow‐up with both measures of motivation to study and for most frequently performed activities, and to find that each may have unique effects on depression.

First, we adapted the PLCQ‐J‐study and PLCQ‐J‐time from the PLOCQ and confirmed their reliability and validity. The five‐factor classification originally envisioned for the PLCQ‐J‐study and PLCQ‐J‐time did not fit well. EFA and CFA suggested a three‐factor structure. The original questionnaire, the PLOCQ, was designed to investigate motivation for physical education. 22 However, the modified questionnaires are versatile for the purpose of investigating various target domains. While previous studies have focused on specific single activities such as physical education, 23 , 24 study, 25 , 26 appetite, 27 and addiction, 28 the wide range of target domains in the present study made it difficult to categorize their motivation into five factors in detail. Here, we could obtain a consistent three‐factor structure for both the PLCQ‐J‐study and PLCQ‐J‐time. “amotivation” is an independent component in the original motivation questionnaire, and we were able to extract it independently in both questionnaires as well. The four items of external–intrinsic motivation were reduced to two this time, but since this was originally a continuum, it was thought that the general framework remained consistent. However, if it is necessary to extract the specific four factors, we would need to focus on a single target domain, as in the existing questionnaire.

In the results of a hierarchical multiple regression analysis, the best fitting Model 4 suggested that the PLCQ‐J‐study factor “intrinsic motivation” and the PLCQ‐J‐time factor “amotivation” at baseline were associated with the change in non‐specific psychological distress at the 1‐year follow‐up. The positive correlation between lack of motivation and depressive symptoms has been widely reported, 2 , 3 , 4 , 49 and is consistent with our findings that amotivation for “what I spend most of my time doing, apart from studying” is a significant positive predictor of non‐specific psychological distress change in a prospective survey.

However, we need to consider the reasons the PLCQ‐J‐study factor “intrinsic motivation” at baseline affects the increase in non‐specific psychological distress at 1‐year follow‐up independently from the PLCQ‐J‐time factor “amotivation.” While we have already discussed the positive effects of motivated learning, 6 several reports on the risks of motivated learning exist. 7 Students with higher intrinsic motivation to get things done were more stressed. 50 Furthermore, it has been reported that students with high academic self‐expectations and high academic expectations from others, such as parents and teachers, experience chronic stress. 51 Academic stress has also been reported to be caused by inappropriate workloads, assignments, exams, and inappropriate treatment by teachers. 52 Exposure to high levels of chronic academic stress causes “burnout syndrome,” a state of physical and emotional exhaustion and feeling overwhelmed that exceeds the individual's internal and external resources to cope with the situation. 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 While burnout is classified in ICD‐11 as an occupational phenomenon (ICD‐11 code: QD85), the framework also recognizes exhaustion in other domains, such as caregiver burnout (ICD‐11 code: QF27), supporting its application to demanding study contexts. 57

Burnout syndrome is considered to be a widespread occupational disease, 58 affecting professionals such as physicians, 59 , 60 , 61 nurses, 62 and teachers. 63 More recently, similar symptoms have been observed in younger students, such as medical students, 64 university students, 55 and high school students. 56 Burnout syndrome is also associated with low personal achievement, 60 higher risk of depression, 65 lower self‐esteem, 66 and higher risk of suicide. 55 , 67 Factors that have been reported to predispose to burnout syndrome include a perfectionist personality. 68 , 69 , 70

Notably, while burnout is conceptually distinct from depression, meta‐analytic evidence indicates a significant positive correlation between the two, sharing a common aspect of emotional and physical exhaustion. 71 Furthermore, burnout has been reported to be closely associated with depressive symptoms, with a high degree of co‐occurrence observed in epidemiological studies. 72 While burnout provides a plausible framework for interpreting our findings, it remains a hypothetical explanation that warrants further empirical validation. In the future, other potential risk factors will also need to be examined against various indicators.

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small for a study that employed self‐reported questionnaires. In particular, different age groups and genders may demonstrate specific relationships between motivation and non‐specific psychological distress. In addition, the responses regarding the most frequently performed activity varied across participants; however, we were unable to examine the effect of the items on the motivation scores and their relationships with non‐specific psychological distress. Second, because this survey was conducted based on recruitments from the general population and self‐reported questionnaires, it is unclear whether it can be applied to clinical patients with depressive symptoms. Third, a wide range of age groups were included in this experiment. Older people are less likely have extrinsic motivation, 73 and the fact that the factor analysis produced three factors may have been influenced by the age of the participants. Especially, the wide range of ages covered would be expected to yield a greater variety of kinds of “study.” Whereas “study” at a relatively young age would imply schoolwork, at an older age, it would imply a wider range of contexts. The possibility of analyzing a wide variety of participants was both an advantage and a limitation of this questionnaire. Furthermore, it is important to note that this study did not account for the specific occupational status of the participants (e.g., high school/university students, employees, or homemakers). Motivation for academic study and time management may manifest differently depending on these roles; for instance, students might be influenced by more formal educational structures, whereas employees might be driven by professional demands. The lack of subgroup analysis by occupation may limit the generalizability of our results. Future research should collect detailed occupational data to examine how these contextual factors influence the relationship between motivation and non‐specific psychological distress.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, we examined the relationship of the PLCQ‐J‐study and the PLCQ‐J‐time, scales derived from a Japanese translation and modification of the PLOCQ, with the non‐specific psychological distress indicators at a 1‐year follow‐up. Of the three factors of the PLCQ‐J‐study and the PLCQ‐J‐time—“amotivation,” “external regulation,” and “intrinsic motivation,”—“amotivation” of the PLCQ‐J‐time and the “intrinsic motivation” factor of the PLCQ‐J‐study were shown to positively predict the non‐specific psychological distress indicators at the 1‐year follow‐up. The results suggest that capturing motivation from multiple perspectives may reveal new relationships with non‐specific psychological distress. Further investigation of the relationships between motivation and psychiatric disorders is needed as this index is further utilized for various motivational factors.
