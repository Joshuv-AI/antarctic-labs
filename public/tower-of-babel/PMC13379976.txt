Hemoglobin glycation index may contextualize the association between time-varying stress hyperglycemia ratio and mortality in critically ill patients with heart failure: a multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yecheng Chen, Yuzhou Cai, Yanming Sha, Qing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStress hyperglycemia is associated with adverse outcomes in critically ill patients, yet the optimal metric and the influence of chronic glycemic background remain unclear. The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) adjusts acute glucose for chronic glycemic status, but whether the prognostic association of time-varying SHR differs across hemoglobin glycation index (HGI)-defined glycation phenotypes in critically ill heart failure patients remains unclear.ObjectivesTo evaluate the association between time-varying SHR and mortality across HGI tertiles in critically ill heart failure patients.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective cohort study included 2,488 adult heart failure patients from MIMIC-III-CareVue (n = 307), MIMIC-IV v3.1 (n = 1,654), and NWICU v0.1.0 (n = 527). HGI was calculated as the residual from regressing HbA1c on admission glucose within each database, and patients were stratified into tertiles. SHR was modeled as a time-varying exposure using extended Cox proportional hazards models with progressive covariate adjustment. Dose–response relationships were examined using restricted cubic splines, and incremental predictive value was assessed via time-dependent ROC analysis.ResultsIn fully adjusted models (hazard ratios per 0.1-unit increase in SHR), time-varying SHR was significantly associated with 28-day mortality in MIMIC-III (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.06–1.21) and MIMIC-IV (HR 1.10, 95% CI 1.07–1.13; both P < 0.001), and with in-hospital mortality across all three cohorts (all P ≤ 0.002). Twenty-eight-day mortality was not available in NWICU. HGI-stratified analyses revealed a numerical gradient in MIMIC-IV (T1: HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.08–1.17; T3: HR 1.06, 95% CI 1.01–1.12), though SHR × HGI interactions were not significant in individual databases. Restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated J-shaped dose–response curves in larger analyses, with significant nonlinearity in MIMIC-IV and the pooled in-hospital mortality cohort. Adding mean SHR to clinical models modestly improved discrimination (ΔAUC = 0.029–0.032, P ≤ 0.019). Subgroup analyses identified significant effect heterogeneity by age, sex, and Charlson index (all P-interaction < 0.05). Eight sensitivity analyses corroborated the robustness of primary findings.ConclusionsTime-varying SHR was independently associated with mortality in critically ill patients with heart failure, with a numerical tendency toward stronger associations among patients with low HGI phenotype. These hypothesis-generating findings suggest that dynamic SHR assessment combined with HGI-informed interpretation may potentially aid individualized glycemic risk assessment in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03262-5.

Introduction

Heart failure is a common high-risk condition among critically ill patients, characterized by substantial short-term mortality, complex cardiometabolic disturbance, and rapidly changing clinical status. Although established ICU severity scores remain central to prognostic assessment, many widely used scoring systems are anchored to physiologic derangements measured during the early phase of critical illness and may not fully capture evolving metabolic stress over subsequent ICU days [1, 2]. In parallel, accumulating evidence has emphasized the prognostic relevance of insulin resistance-related metabolic indices in cardiovascular disease, with the triglyceride-glucose index showing broad diagnostic and prognostic value across multiple cardiometabolic outcomes [3]. In patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, several insulin resistance surrogates have been independently associated with adverse outcomes and have improved risk stratification beyond conventional clinical prediction tools [4]. More importantly, emerging longitudinal evidence suggests that the prognostic relevance of metabolic dysfunction may depend not only on its baseline level but also on its temporal pattern, as triglyceride-glucose index trajectories have been associated with worsening heart failure and overall mortality in elderly patients with chronic heart failure and type 2 diabetes [5]. These findings support a transition from single-time-point metabolic assessment toward dynamic, phenotype-informed risk evaluation in critically ill cardiovascular populations.

Among metabolic perturbations during critical illness, stress hyperglycemia has attracted particular attention because it reflects acute neurohormonal activation, inflammatory stress, insulin resistance, and impaired glycemic resilience. However, absolute glucose concentration alone is difficult to interpret in patients with heterogeneous chronic glycemic backgrounds, since the same glucose level may represent acute metabolic decompensation in one patient but habitual hyperglycemia in another. The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), also referred to as relative hyperglycemia, was proposed to quantify acute glycemic elevation relative to chronic glycemic exposure by indexing measured glucose against HbA1c-derived estimated average glucose [6]. The estimated average glucose component is commonly derived from the A1c-Derived Average Glucose equation [7]. Recent high-quality cardiovascular evidence suggests that SHR provides prognostic information beyond absolute glucose across major cardiovascular presentations, high-acuity cardiovascular settings, and heart failure-related phenotypes, including critically ill atrial fibrillation, cardiac surgery, coronary artery bypass grafting, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and physical frailty in HFpEF [8–13].

A further consideration is that acute glycemic stress in the ICU is not only determined by a single glucose value but also by glycemic variability, treatment exposure, and the patient’s chronic glycemic phenotype. In critically ill cardiovascular populations, glycemic variability has been associated with mortality, supporting the clinical importance of dynamic glycemic assessment rather than static glucose evaluation alone [14]. Nevertheless, the interpretation of HbA1c-adjusted glycemic stress may still be complicated by interindividual heterogeneity in hemoglobin glycation. HbA1c does not simply mirror ambient glucose exposure; for a given glucose level, individuals may exhibit systematically higher or lower HbA1c values, reflecting variation in glycation tendency, erythrocyte biology, and other non-glycemic determinants. The hemoglobin glycation index (HGI), defined as the difference between observed HbA1c and glucose-predicted HbA1c, was developed to quantify this individual deviation and has been proposed as a phenotype marker that complements conventional glycemic indices [15]. Therefore, HGI may provide a clinically relevant framework for interpreting whether elevated SHR represents acute stress hyperglycemia superimposed on a relatively low chronic glycemic background or a different metabolic state influenced by long-term glycemic exposure and individual glycation phenotype.

Despite these advances, several important knowledge gaps remain. First, most previous studies evaluated SHR or HGI as baseline or single-time-point markers, whereas glycemic stress in critically ill patients is inherently dynamic and may fluctuate substantially during the early ICU course. Second, whether the prognostic association of time-varying SHR differs across HGI-defined glycation phenotypes has not been investigated in critically ill patients with heart failure. Third, evidence from external critical care databases remains limited, restricting the generalizability of existing findings. To address these gaps, we conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study using three publicly available ICU databases to evaluate the association between time-varying SHR and mortality in critically ill patients with heart failure. We further examined whether this association differed across HGI tertiles, characterized dose–response relationships using restricted cubic splines, and assessed the incremental predictive value of SHR beyond established clinical severity scores. We hypothesized that higher time-varying SHR would be independently associated with increased mortality and that this association might differ across HGI-defined glycation phenotypes.

Materials and methods

Study design and data sources

This was a multicenter retrospective cohort study using data from three publicly available critical care databases. The MIMIC-III-CareVue database (v1.4) contains clinical data collected between 2001 and 2008 from a single tertiary care center in the United States ( https://physionet.org/content/mimic3-carevue/1.4/)[16]. The MIMIC-IV database (v3.1) encompasses data from 2008 to 2022 at the same institution, representing the most recent iteration of the MIMIC series ( https://physionet.org/content/mimiciv/3.1/)[17]. The Northwestern ICU (NWICU) database (v0.1.0) contains data from 2020 to 2022 collected at Northwestern Medicine, with a structure harmonized to the MIMIC framework (https://physionet.org/content/nwicu-northwestern-icu/0.1.0/). As MIMIC-III-CareVue and MIMIC-IV were derived from the same institution across different time periods, they do not constitute fully independent external validation cohorts. Accordingly, all primary analyses were presented separately for each database, and pooled cohort results were reported as supplementary evidence. All databases were accessed through PhysioNet and required completion of recognized human research ethics training. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The reporting of this study followed the Strengthening Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study population

Adult patients (aged > 18 years) admitted to the ICU with a primary or secondary diagnosis of heart failure were identified from each database. Exclusion criteria were: non-first hospital admissions and multiple ICU admissions within a single hospitalization, age ≤ 18 years, ICU length of stay less than 24 h, and missing stress hyperglycemia ratio data within ICU days 0 through 7. Only the first ICU admission per patient was retained to ensure independence of observations.

Exposure variables

The hemoglobin glycation index (HGI) was calculated as the residual from a linear regression of admission glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) on admission blood glucose, fitted separately within each database. This approach quantifies the individual deviation of observed HbA1c from the value predicted by ambient glucose concentration. Patients were stratified into tertiles (T1, T2, T3) based on database-specific HGI distributions, representing low, medium, and high hemoglobin glycation phenotypes respectively. Tertile stratification was chosen to balance adequate sample size within each subgroup with the ability to detect a graded association across HGI levels, while avoiding the loss of information inherent in dichotomization and the potential for residual confounding associated with modeling HGI as a continuous interaction term in relatively small cohorts.

The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) served as the time-varying exposure variable, calculated at each ICU day as the ratio of the daily mean blood glucose to expected glucose derived from the ADAG formula (expected glucose [mg/dL] = 28.7 × HbA1c [%] − 46.7). When multiple glucose measurements were available within a single calendar day, the arithmetic mean was used to represent that day's glucose level, as this approach reduces the influence of isolated extreme values and better reflects the overall glycemic exposure within each 24 h period. SHR values were calculated daily from ICU day 0 (the calendar day of ICU admission) through ICU day 7, defining an 8 day observation window for the time-varying exposure. This metric captures acute glycemic derangement relative to chronic glycemic background, thereby distinguishing stress-induced hyperglycemia from pre-existing diabetic hyperglycemia.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome was 28 day all-cause mortality, defined as death occurring within 28 days of ICU admission. The secondary outcome was in-hospital all-cause mortality. Survival time was derived from ICU and hospital length of stay records converted from hours to days. Twenty-eight-day mortality was available in MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV but not in NWICU; therefore, 28 day mortality analyses were restricted to these two databases, and NWICU contributed only to in-hospital mortality analyses.

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and established associations with mortality in critically ill heart failure patients. Three adjustment models were constructed with progressive covariate inclusion: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, and body mass index), and Model 3 (fully adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score, Charlson comorbidity index, mechanical ventilation use, vasopressor use, and insulin administration within the first 24 h).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using medians with interquartile ranges for non-normally distributed continuous variables, means with standard deviations for normally distributed continuous variables, and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Core variables including age, gender, HbA1c, admission glucose, and HGI had no missing data. For baseline covariates with missing values, multiple imputation was performed. For time-series variables with missing values during the ICU course, temporal imputation methods were applied. The missing proportions of all key variables are reported in Supplementary Table 1. Between-group comparisons across HGI tertiles were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, one-way analysis of variance, or chi-square test as appropriate.

Longitudinal SHR trajectories across HGI tertiles were compared using group-level mean values with 95% confidence intervals at each ICU day, with between-group differences assessed by the Kruskal–Wallis test at each time point.

The association between time-varying SHR and mortality was evaluated using extended Cox proportional hazards models with a counting process (start-stop) data structure, allowing SHR to update at each ICU day. Hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals were estimated per 0.1-unit increase in SHR across the three adjustment models within the overall population and within each HGI tertile. Potential effect heterogeneity across HGI tertiles was assessed through likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without the SHR × HGI interaction term. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residual tests.

The dose–response relationship between SHR and mortality was examined using restricted cubic splines with 3 to 5 knots (selected based on event count) within the fully adjusted extended Cox framework. The reference value was set at the median SHR in each analysis. Overall association and departure from linearity were tested separately.

The incremental predictive value of SHR for 28 day mortality was assessed using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analysis. Three predictive models were compared: mean SHR alone (averaged over ICU days 0–7), a clinical model (SOFA plus SAPS II scores), and the combined clinical plus SHR model. Area under the curve differences were tested using the method implemented in the timeROC framework.

Subgroup analyses for in-hospital mortality were conducted in the pooled cohort across eight prespecified clinical variables: age (< 65 vs ≥ 65 years), sex, body mass index (< 30 vs ≥ 30 kg/m2), SOFA score (< 4 vs ≥ 4, dichotomized at the pooled median of 4), Charlson comorbidity index (< 7 vs ≥ 7, dichotomized at the pooled median of 7), mechanical ventilation, insulin use, and vasopressor use. In each subgroup model, the stratification variable itself was excluded from covariates to avoid collinearity. Interaction P-values were derived from likelihood ratio tests.

Eight sensitivity analyses were performed in MIMIC-IV to test robustness: (1) relaxing inclusion to patients with glucose data available on days 0–3 only; (2) excluding extreme SHR values below the 1st and above the 99th percentiles; (3) excluding patients receiving insulin; (4) excluding patients with extreme HbA1c values (< 5% or > 15%); (5) replacing time-varying SHR with day-0 SHR in a standard Cox model; (6) additionally adjusting for time-varying insulin and corticosteroid use as time-dependent covariates; (7) recalculating HGI using ICU day 0 mean glucose instead of admission glucose to reduce the potential influence of acute stress physiology on the glycation index; and (8) a landmark analysis excluding patients who died within 24 h of ICU admission to evaluate potential survival bias.

All statistical tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3) with the following key packages: survival for Cox regression, rms for restricted cubic spline modeling, timeROC for time-dependent ROC analysis, forestploter for forest plot generation, tableone for baseline characteristics, and flextable and officer for document formatting.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 7,265 heart failure patients were identified from MIMIC-III-CareVue, 26,005 from MIMIC-IV, and 2,908 from NWICU. After sequential exclusion of non-first hospital admissions and multiple ICU admissions (n = 2,724, 16,386, and 575), patients aged ≤ 18 years (n = 16, 3, and 1), ICU stays shorter than 24 h (n = 456, 1,631, and 435), and patients with missing SHR data within ICU days 0–7 (n = 3,762, 6,331, and 1,370), the final analytic cohorts comprised 307, 1,654, and 527 patients from MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV, and NWICU, respectively (Fig. 1). Longitudinal SHR trajectories across HGI tertiles

Given the observed baseline differences across HGI tertiles, we next examined the longitudinal trajectories of SHR over ICU days 0–7. Patients were stratified into HGI tertiles within each database: in MIMIC-III, T1 ≤ − 0.54, T2 − 0.54 to − 0.16, T3 > 0.16; in MIMIC-IV, T1 ≤ − 0.49, T2 − 0.49 to − 0.14, T3 > 0.14; and in NWICU, T1 ≤ − 0.47, T2 − 0.47 to − 0.16, T3 > 0.16. The database-specific linear regression equations for predicted HbA1c (MIMIC-III: predicted HbA1c = 3.888 + 0.0175 × glucose, R2 = 0.365; MIMIC-IV: predicted HbA1c = 4.128 + 0.0153 × glucose, R2 = 0.307; NWICU: predicted HbA1c = 4.332 + 0.0138 × glucose, R2 = 0.279) and full model statistics are provided in Supplementary Table2. Across all three databases and the pooled cohort, SHR levels were consistently stratified by HGI tertile throughout the observation period, with all between-group differences reaching statistical significance at every time point (all P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A–D). Association between time-varying SHR and mortality

Building on the observed SHR stratification across HGI groups, we evaluated the association between time-varying SHR and mortality using extended Cox models. All hazard ratios are reported per 0.1-unit increase in SHR.

In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), higher SHR was significantly associated with increased 28-day mortality in the overall population of both MIMIC-III (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.06–1.21, P < 0.001) and MIMIC-IV (HR 1.10, 95% CI 1.07–1.13, P < 0.001). For in-hospital mortality, consistent associations were observed across all three cohorts: MIMIC-III (HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.07–1.24, P < 0.001), MIMIC-IV (HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.06–1.12, P < 0.001), and NWICU (HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.03–1.14, P = 0.002). NWICU was not included in 28-day mortality analyses because this outcome was not available in this database.

Within HGI-stratified analyses, a gradient of association was observed across tertiles. For 28-day mortality in MIMIC-IV, the association was strongest in the low HGI group (T1: HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.08–1.17, P < 0.001), intermediate in the medium HGI group (T2: HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.05–1.17, P < 0.001), and attenuated in the high HGI group (T3: HR 1.06, 95% CI 1.01–1.12, P = 0.026). A similar pattern was observed in MIMIC-III, where the association was significant in the low HGI group (T1: HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.07–1.30, P = 0.001) but not in the medium (T2: HR 1.13, 95% CI 0.97–1.33, P = 0.116) or high HGI group (T3: HR 1.07, 95% CI 0.92–1.25, P = 0.397). For in-hospital mortality, this gradient was consistent across cohorts: in NWICU, the association was significant in the low HGI group (T1: HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.07–1.21, P < 0.001) but not in the medium (T2: HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.97–1.33, P = 0.109) or high HGI group (T3: HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.90–1.14, P = 0.843). Dose–response relationship between SHR and mortality

To further characterize the shape of the SHR–mortality association, restricted cubic spline analyses were performed within the fully adjusted extended Cox framework. The overall association between time-varying SHR and 28-day mortality was significant in MIMIC-III (P-overall = 0.002) and MIMIC-IV (P-overall < 0.001). Because 28 day mortality was not available in NWICU, no three-database pooled RCS analysis was performed for 28-day mortality. In MIMIC-III, the dose–response curve displayed a monotonically increasing pattern with no evidence of nonlinearity (P-nonlinear = 0.289), whereas MIMIC-IV exhibited a J-shaped relationship with mortality risk elevated at both low and high SHR values, and significant departure from linearity was confirmed (P-nonlinear < 0.001) (Fig. 4A, B). For in-hospital mortality, the overall association was significant across all cohorts: MIMIC-III (P-overall = 0.002), MIMIC-IV (P-overall < 0.001), NWICU (P-overall = 0.005), and the pooled cohort (P-overall < 0.001). The curve shape in MIMIC-III and NWICU was approximately linear and monotonically increasing (P-nonlinear = 0.280 and 0.510, respectively), while MIMIC-IV and the pooled cohort demonstrated J-shaped curves with significant nonlinearity (P-nonlinear = 0.032 and 0.008, respectively) (Fig. 4C–F). Incremental predictive value of SHR for 28-day mortality

Beyond establishing the dose–response relationship, we assessed the incremental predictive value of SHR using time-dependent ROC analysis. Eight sensitivity analyses in MIMIC-IV supported the robustness of the primary findings (Supplementary Table 3). The overall association between SHR and 28-day mortality remained significant across all alternative analytic approaches, with HRs ranging from 1.08 (95% CI 1.05–1.11) in both the fixed day-0 SHR exposure analysis and the model additionally adjusted for time-varying insulin and corticosteroid use, to 1.11 (95% CI 1.08–1.15) after excluding insulin-treated patients. The HGI-stratified gradient persisted in all scenarios, with the strongest associations consistently observed in the low HGI group (T1) and attenuated estimates in the high HGI group (T3). Notably, the association in T3 became non-significant only in the fixed-exposure analysis (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.99–1.10, P = 0.078). Adjusting for time-varying insulin and corticosteroid use did not materially alter the results (overall HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.05–1.11). Recalculating HGI using ICU day-0 mean glucose instead of admission glucose yielded similar findings (overall HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.06–1.12), and the landmark analysis excluding deaths within 24 h of ICU admission also confirmed the primary results (overall HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.06–1.12).

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study of critically ill patients with heart failure from three large critical care databases, we found that time-varying SHR was independently associated with increased mortality. This association was observed for 28-day mortality in MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV and for in-hospital mortality across MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV, and NWICU. When patients were stratified by HGI tertiles, the magnitude of the SHR–mortality association showed a generally graded pattern in effect estimates, with numerically strongest estimates observed among patients with low HGI and attenuated estimates among those with high HGI. Restricted cubic spline analyses further demonstrated significant dose–response relationships between SHR and mortality, with J-shaped patterns in larger cohorts. In addition, adding mean SHR to conventional clinical severity models modestly but significantly improved discrimination for 28-day mortality. These findings suggest that dynamic SHR may serve as a clinically informative marker of acute glycemic stress in critically ill patients with heart failure, and that HGI-defined glycation phenotype may potentially help contextualize the prognostic meaning of SHR, although formal interaction tests were not consistently significant across individual databases.

Our findings are broadly consistent with high-quality evidence supporting SHR as a prognostic marker across cardiovascular and critical care populations. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that elevated SHR was associated with increased all-cause mortality across major cardiovascular presentations, supporting the prognostic relevance of relative hyperglycemia beyond absolute glucose alone [8]. Similar prognostic relevance has been observed in high-acuity cardiovascular and cerebrovascular settings, including critical cerebrovascular disease, ischemic stroke, coronary artery bypass grafting, cardiac surgery, and critically ill atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease populations [10–12, 18, 19]. Beyond acute cardiovascular illness, SHR has also been associated with adverse outcomes in broader cardio-kidney-metabolic contexts, including cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic syndrome and chronic kidney disease or diabetic nephropathy, suggesting that relative hyperglycemia may capture systemic vulnerability across overlapping metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular disease states [20, 21]. Our study extends these observations in three important ways. First, rather than relying on admission or baseline SHR, we modeled SHR as a time-varying exposure during the early ICU course, thereby better reflecting dynamic glycemic stress. Second, we focused specifically on critically ill patients with heart failure across multiple ICU databases. Third, by stratifying patients according to HGI-defined glycation phenotype, our findings raise the possibility that the prognostic meaning of elevated SHR may vary according to chronic glycemic-glycation background.

The observed gradient of SHR-associated risk across HGI tertiles may reflect important heterogeneity in the biological meaning of stress hyperglycemia. Patients with low HGI have lower HbA1c than expected for their glucose level, suggesting that an elevated SHR in this group may represent a more abrupt increase in acute glucose relative to their chronic glycemic-glycation background. In contrast, patients with high HGI may have a greater chronic glycation tendency or long-term glycemic burden, such that elevated glucose during critical illness may be less specific for acute metabolic decompensation. Importantly, an ACCORD trial analysis suggested that HGI may identify subgroups with different responses to intensive glycemic treatment, reinforcing the concept that glycation phenotype may influence the clinical interpretation of glucose-related biomarkers [22]. In the present study, the SHR–mortality association showed numerically strongest estimates in the low-HGI group and attenuated estimates in the high-HGI group, suggesting that HGI may help contextualize whether elevated SHR primarily reflects acute stress hyperglycemia or a metabolic state shaped by chronic glycemic-glycation background. Moreover, a methodological consideration is that HGI and SHR share mathematical components, as HGI is derived from the residual of regressing HbA1c on admission glucose, while SHR is calculated using measured glucose divided by HbA1c-derived expected glucose. This mathematical coupling may partly contribute to the observed separation of SHR trajectories across HGI tertiles and should be considered when interpreting the apparent HGI-related heterogeneity. However, because formal SHR × HGI interactions were not consistently significant in individual databases, this phenotype-dependent gradient should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and requires further validation.

The restricted cubic spline analyses provided additional insight into the shape of the SHR–mortality association. In larger analyses, particularly MIMIC-IV and the pooled in-hospital mortality cohort, the relationship appeared J-shaped, suggesting that both markedly elevated and relatively low SHR values may be associated with increased mortality risk. The adverse association at higher SHR levels is biologically plausible, as pronounced relative hyperglycemia may reflect severe acute stress, heightened inflammatory activation, catecholamine excess, insulin resistance, and impaired metabolic adaptation during critical illness. By contrast, the elevated risk observed at lower SHR values may represent a distinct high-risk metabolic state, potentially related to reduced nutritional reserve, impaired gluconeogenic capacity, hypoglycemia tendency, or advanced disease severity with limited metabolic flexibility [23]. This interpretation is consistent with emerging evidence that dynamic glycemic and metabolic trajectories may provide prognostic information in critically ill cardiovascular populations beyond isolated single-time-point values [24]. More broadly, longitudinal evidence has linked SHR and related cardiometabolic trajectories to incident cardiometabolic disease and adverse cardiovascular outcomes, supporting the concept that metabolic risk should be interpreted dynamically rather than as a static exposure [25]. Therefore, SHR should not be interpreted as a simple linear therapeutic target; rather, it may function as a dynamic risk marker reflecting both acute metabolic stress and the patient’s capacity to maintain glycemic homeostasis during critical illness.

The time-dependent ROC analyses further suggested that SHR may provide incremental prognostic information beyond conventional severity scores. In both MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV, adding mean SHR to a clinical model incorporating SOFA and SAPS II yielded a modest improvement in discrimination for 28-day mortality that reached statistical significance, though the confidence intervals of the combined model overlapped with those of the clinical model alone. However, the magnitude of AUC improvement was modest, indicating that SHR should not be regarded as a replacement for established severity scores. Instead, dynamic SHR may serve as a complementary marker for refining risk stratification, particularly among patients with similar organ dysfunction severity but different degrees of acute glycemic stress. From a clinical perspective, serial SHR assessment may help identify patients who warrant closer metabolic monitoring, individualized nutritional assessment, and cautious interpretation of glucose-lowering therapy. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence supporting aggressive glucose normalization, because prior randomized evidence in critically ill patients has shown that intensive glucose control may increase harm compared with conventional management [26]. Rather, SHR and HGI may be most useful as risk-phenotyping tools that contextualize glycemic abnormalities within the broader trajectory of critical illness.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, we used three large critical care databases, allowing the association between time-varying SHR and mortality to be evaluated across different ICU populations and data structures. Second, SHR was modeled as a time-varying exposure using an extended Cox framework, which better reflects the dynamic nature of glycemic stress during the early ICU course than a single admission measurement. Third, we incorporated HGI to account for interindividual variation in hemoglobin glycation, thereby providing a more individualized framework for interpreting HbA1c-adjusted glycemic stress. Fourth, the robustness of the findings was supported by multiple complementary analyses, including restricted cubic splines, time-dependent ROC analysis, subgroup analyses, and sensitivity analyses.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective observational design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding remains possible despite multivariable adjustment. Second, the primary outcome was all-cause mortality rather than cardiovascular or heart failure–specific mortality. Although cause-specific mortality would provide greater etiological specificity, reliable cause-of-death adjudication is not feasible in retrospective ICU databases such as MIMIC and NWICU, which lack standardized death certificate or adjudication committee data. All-cause mortality remains the most robust and least biased endpoint in this setting, though it may dilute associations that are primarily driven by metabolic or cardiovascular mechanisms. Third, the requirement for available HbA1c and serial glucose measurements may have introduced selection bias, as patients with more complete glycemic monitoring may differ systematically from those without sufficient data. In particular, serial glycemic data during ICU days 0–7 may preferentially capture patients who survived long enough or received more intensive metabolic monitoring. A landmark analysis excluding patients who died within 24 h of ICU admission yielded consistent results, partially mitigating this concern, but survival bias related to the requirement for extended glucose monitoring cannot be entirely ruled out. Fourth, ICU glucose measurements were not obtained under standardized fasting conditions and may have been influenced by nutrition, insulin therapy, corticosteroids, vasopressors, renal replacement therapy, and illness severity, raising the possibility of time-varying treatment-related confounding. Although a sensitivity analysis additionally adjusting for time-varying insulin and corticosteroid use did not materially change the results, the possibility of residual treatment-related confounding cannot be fully excluded. HGI was calculated using database-specific regression models based on admission glucose and HbA1c; because admission glucose may itself be affected by acute stress physiology, HGI partly captures acute illness severity rather than purely reflecting a chronic glycation phenotype. Although a sensitivity analysis using ICU day-0 mean glucose instead of admission glucose yielded similar results, pre-admission or outpatient glucose measurements were not available in the databases studied. In addition, HbA1c may be influenced by anemia, transfusion, renal dysfunction, and altered erythrocyte turnover, and therefore HGI values in critically ill patients should be interpreted cautiously. Fifth, the resulting HGI tertiles may not be directly generalizable to other clinical settings. Sixth, although HGI-stratified analyses showed a gradient in effect estimates, formal SHR × HGI interactions were not consistently significant across individual databases; therefore, the potential role of HGI in contextualizing the SHR–mortality association should be interpreted cautiously. Seventh, MIMIC-III-CareVue and MIMIC-IV were derived from the same medical center across different time periods, and therefore do not represent fully independent external validation cohorts. In addition, 28-day mortality analyses were available only in MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV, whereas NWICU mainly contributed to analyses of in-hospital mortality. Finally, important heart failure–specific variables, including ejection fraction, natriuretic peptide levels, echocardiographic parameters, hemodynamic profiles, and detailed guideline-directed medical therapy, were incompletely available in structured database fields.

Conclusions

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study of critically ill patients with heart failure, time-varying SHR was independently associated with increased mortality across multiple ICU databases. The association tended to be numerically more pronounced among patients with low HGI phenotype and attenuated among those with high HGI phenotype, although formal interaction tests were not consistently significant across individual databases. These findings suggest that chronic glycation background may potentially help contextualize the prognostic meaning of acute stress hyperglycemia, but this observation remains hypothesis-generating. SHR also provided modest but statistically significant incremental predictive value beyond conventional severity scores. These findings support the potential role of dynamic SHR assessment and HGI-informed interpretation as complementary tools for individualized glycemic risk stratification in critically ill patients with heart failure. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to determine whether SHR- and HGI-based risk phenotyping can improve clinical decision-making.
