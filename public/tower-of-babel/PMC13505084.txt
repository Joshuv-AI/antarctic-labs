Genetic evidence elucidates the mediating role of sex hormone-binding globulin in the causal relationship linking insomnia to carpal tunnel syndrome
Authors: Junming Huang, Mingxing Chen, Song Zhou, Jun Liu, Jiangxi Li, Shanhu Huang, Zhiyuan Zou, Kui Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the most prevalent type of entrapment neuropathy and is frequently associated with sleep disturbances. However, the directionality of this association remains ambiguous, necessitating an exploration of potential mediators. Sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) may affect the risk of CTS by modulating the sex hormone milieu within the carpal tunnel. This study seeks to elucidate the causal relationship between sleep disturbances and CTS and to assess the mediating role of SHBG.MethodComprehensive summary-level data on sleep traits and CTS from Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) were gathered in this study. The causal analysis between sleep traits and CTS was carried out using two-sample MR. Additionally, two-step MR analysis was employed to study the mediating effect of SHBG on the causal relationship between sleep traits and CTS.ResultsWe examined the genetic causal relationships between nine sleep traits and CTS. Only insomnia genetically predicted the increased risk of CTS. The backward MR analyses showed no causal associations of CTS with any sleep traits. Exploratory mediation analysis suggested that SHBG may represent a putative partial explanatory pathway between insomnia and CTS, accounting for approximately 2.1% of the total association.ConclusionThis genetic evidence demonstrates that insomnia is an independent risk factor for CTS, with SHBG acting as a putative hormonal mediator or genetically correlated pathway in this relationship. The findings highlight sleep management as a potential preventive strategy for CTS and warrant mechanistic and clinical validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-026-01006-w.

Introduction

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is characterized by abnormal sensory and motor functions in the region innervated by the median nerve, resulting from compression of the nerve as it traverses the carpal tunnel. It represents the most prevalent peripheral nerve entrapment syndrome encountered in clinical practice, constituting approximately 90% of all such syndromes [1]. Clinically, CTS typically presents with tingling or numbness in the radial three and a half digits innervated by the median nerve. In the initial stages, pain predominantly occurs at night and progressively extends into daytime hours. In advanced cases, pain may radiate from the wrist to the forearm, upper arm, and even the shoulder, accompanied by persistent sensory disturbances. Additionally, atrophy of the thenar eminence may develop, leading to diminished thumb abduction and adduction strength, as well as impaired dexterity in fine motor tasks involving the fingers. This condition is linked to substantial functional impairment and a reduced quality of life, leading to a considerable socio-economic burden. With the advancement of contemporary lifestyles, particularly the increased use of smartphones and computers, CTS has emerged as a more prevalent issue [2].

The influence of CTS on the quality of life for patients extends significantly beyond mere impairments in hand function. Nighttime symptoms, particularly frequent awakenings, have been consistently reported as prevalent concerns among these patients. Evidence indicates that the predominant complaint among individuals with CTS is sleep disruption caused by hand pain and numbness [3]. It is estimated that up to 80% of CTS patients experience sleep disturbances due to these nocturnal symptoms, making sleep-related issues the primary motivation for seeking medical intervention [4, 5]. Nevertheless, the sleep disturbances associated with CTS have not been extensively studied or addressed in the literature.

Sleep is a complex, multidimensional concept that includes various characteristics such as sleep patterns, early awakening, sleep duration, snoring, and insomnia. It is essential for maintaining bodily homeostasis and is vital for numerous physiological processes that support physical health. Sleep regulates metabolic functions by influencing energy balance and modulating hormone levels that control appetite, thereby reducing the risk of obesity and metabolic disorders [6]. It also enhances immune function by facilitating the production of cytokines, which are essential for combating infections, and it supports cardiovascular health by regulating blood pressure and reducing the likelihood of hypertension [7]. Moreover, sleep is integral to cognitive performance, memory consolidation, and emotional regulation, with its various stages, particularly rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, being vital for brain health. Additionally, sufficient sleep aids in the regulation of inflammatory processes, thereby decreasing the risk of chronic diseases [8]. In the domain of CTS investigating, sleep disturbances are predominantly regarded as concomitant symptoms. However, there is a lack of studies establishing causal relationships between CTS and sleep.

Sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) is a glycoprotein predominantly synthesized in the liver, which plays a pivotal role in modulating the concentrations of free sex hormones in the bloodstream. Reduced levels of SHBG result in increased availability of free testosterone and estrogen, which can exert effects on neural tissues and the microenvironment of the carpal tunnel [9, 10]. Notably, fluctuations in sex hormone levels during various life stages in women, such as pregnancy and menopause, are correlated with alterations in SHBG levels and may contribute to the heightened susceptibility of females to CTS [11, 12]. Pregnancy is associated with a high incidence of CTS, with prevalence ranging from 7% to 43% [1], particularly during the third trimester due to hormonal changes and fluid retention [13]. However, regarding menopause, the impact of exogenous hormones remains controversial; while a nationwide cohort study by Tang et al. reported a positive association between hormone replacement therapy (HRT) and CTS risk [11], the Women’s Health Initiative randomized trial by Al-Rousan et al. indicated a protective effect [12]. In contrast to the extensive research in women, the role of sex hormones in men remains largely unexplored. Although SHBG regulates testosterone bioavailability and a experimental study have indicated that testosterone promoted perineural fibrosis and nerve tethering [9], there is a lack of clinical studies examining the association between SHBG, testosterone, and CTS risk in the male population. This represents a significant gap in the literature, particularly given that genetic datasets often include male participants. Interestingly, several studies have indicated an association between sleep disorders and decreased SHBG levels [10, 14–16]. Consequently, this raises the question of whether SHBG may have a regulatory function in the relationship between sleep disorders and CTS.

Mendelian randomization (MR), similar to a randomized controlled trial, is widely used in epidemiology to study causes [17]. The key benefit of MR is use of germline genetic variants, which are randomly assigned at conception and unaffected by environmental factors, reducing the risk of reverse causation. These variants serve as instrumental variables, enhancing the assessment of causal mediation effects and minimizing confounding and bias compared to traditional observational methods [18].

This study sought to elucidate the causal relationship between sleep disturbances and CTS utilizing a two-sample MR analysis. Additionally, we examined the potential mediating role of SHBG within the causal pathways linking sleep disturbances and CTS. Through this investigation, we aim to deepen our understanding of the intricate interactions among these variables and inform future therapeutic strategies for CTS.

Materials and methods

Study design

In this study, we employed MR analysis and mediation analysis to explore the mediating mechanisms of the causal relationship between sleep characteristics and CTS. The primary exposures were sleep characteristics such as morning/evening person (chronotype), snoring and sleeplessness/insomnia, outcome was CTS, and potential mediator was SHBG. Firstly, the public GWAS summary data of sleep characteristics, CTS and SHBG were downloaded from public databases. Secondly, bidirectional MR analysis was used to investigate the causality between sleep characteristics and CTS. Finally, multivariate MR analyses and mediation analysis were conducted to identify and calculate the potential mediation effect of SHBG in the causal connection between insomnia and CTS.

Data sources

The data for insomnia, CTS and SHBG were obtained from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) Open Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The IEU OpenGWAS project acts as a comprehensive repository that curates and harmonizes summary-level genetic association data from a wide range of large-scale biobanks and published studies worldwide. For the specific datasets selected in this study, the underlying population was primarily derived from the UK Biobank cohort, ensuring a large sample size of European ancestry.

The characteristics of data sources were summarized in Supplementary table S1. For exposures, the dataset of morning/evening person dataset (id: ukb-b-4956) contains 413,343 participants. Snoring (id: ukb-b-17400) dataset consists of 430,438 cases. The dataset of insomnia (id: ukb-b-3957) includes 9,851,867 SNPs from 462,341 European samples. The ukb-b-5776, ukb-b-4956, ukb-b-17,400, ukb-b-4424 and ukb-b-3957 all contain 9,851,867 SNPs. The other insomnia dataset (id: ukb-a-13) consists of 336,965 cases and 10,894,596 SNPs. The sleep-related phenotypes used in this study were derived from GWAS datasets based primarily on self-reported traits in the UK Biobank, including sleeplessness/insomnia symptoms, snoring, and chronotype-related measures, rather than clinically confirmed sleep disorder diagnoses. Therefore, the MR estimates should be interpreted as the effects of genetically predicted lifelong liability to these sleep-related traits on CTS risk, rather than the effects of short-term sleep disturbances or clinically diagnosed sleep disorders. For outcomes, CTS dataset (id: ebi-a-GCST90018813) contains 24,181,062 SNPs and 480,201 participants. For potential mediation, the SHBG statistics datasets (id: ieu-b-4870 and ieu-b-4871) were obtained from 214,989 female cases and 185,221 male cases, respectively. Both of the above datasets included 12,321,875 SNPs.

The deadline for data collection is June 1, 2025. The GWAS datasets included in this study were all sourced from public databases. The participants have signed the informed consent form in the original studies.

Instrumental variable selection

In this study, the selected instrumental variable (IV) satisfies the following three main assumptions: (1) IV should have a direct correlation with exposure; (2) IV must be independent of other confounding factors that may affect the relationship between exposure and outcome; (3) IVs themselves do not directly affect the outcome unless they are associated through exposure [19].

Although P < 5 × 10⁻⁸ is conventionally regarded as the genome-wide significance threshold, it yielded an insufficient number of SNPs for some analyses, which reduced the feasibility and stability of several MR estimators. Therefore, we used a relaxed threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻⁶ to increase the number of available instruments and improve analytical feasibility. This strategy has been adopted in previous MR studies when genome-wide significant instruments were insufficient [20, 21]. However, the relaxed threshold may increase the probability of including weaker or less exposure-specific variants. To reduce this concern, we assessed instrument strength using F-statistics and performed multiple sensitivity analyses. The effect sizes and alleles of SNP between exposure and outcome data were harmonized. Through the clumping process, the influence of linkage disequilibrium was eliminated, and independent SNPs were selected (r2 = 0.001, distance = 10,000 kb) [22]. The F-statistic was conducted to assess the strength of chosen SNPs, and F-value > 10 indicated have robust strength [23]. Any outlier SNPs was removed using the Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) method, these SNPs were summarized in Supplementary table S2.

Statistical analysis

The univariate MR analysis was applied to evaluate the causal relationship between sleep characteristics and CTS. Due to the advantage of providing the highest statistical power, inverse variance weighting (IVW) was selected as the main analysis method [24]. MR Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods were used as supplementary evidence for IVW results. These methods have their unique characteristics. For example, in the MR-Egger method, the included IVs may possess targeted gene pleiotropy [25]. The weighted median method can yield reliable estimates when more than 50% of the information comes from valid variable [26]. When at least PIVW <0.05, the result of this MR analysis was positive. The analysis results of the above MR methods were presented using the scatter plots.

In sensitivity analysis, MR-Egger analysis was used to detect the horizontal pleiotropy. MR-Egger method can explore situations that violate the IV assumption and estimate the effect values that are not affected by these violations [27]. When the intercept P > 0.05, it indicates that there is no horizontal pleiotropy. Cochran’s Q statistic in IVW and MR-Egger allowed for the detection of heterogeneity. P < 0.05 indicates the presence of heterogeneity [28]. The leave-one-out analysis assessed the robustness of the research results by sequentially eliminating each individual SNP.

To detect whether CTS had a causal effect on sleep characteristics, we conducted a reverse MR analysis. CTS was regarded as exposure, sleep characteristics as outcome, and CTS-related SNPs as IVs. The analysis process and methods were the same as those of MR analysis.

Two-step Mendelian randomization was used to explore the mediation effect. This method relies on several strong assumptions: (1) the genetic variants effectively instrument the exposure; (2) the mediator causally affects the outcome; (3) there is no unmeasured confounding between the mediator and outcome; and (4) the exposure affects the outcome solely through the mediator (no horizontal pleiotropy). Multivariate MR analysis was conducted to quantify the direct effects of SHBG and insomnia on CTS [29] and to specifically correct for potential pleiotropy arising from the correlation between these traits [30]. The IVW method was utilized within the multivariate framework to disentangle the independent causal effects of insomnia and SHBG on CTS. Heterogeneity and directional pleiotropy were rigorously evaluated using Cochran’s Q statistic and the MR-Egger intercept test, respectively, to ensure the validity of the mediation estimates. In mediation analysis, firstly, the causality between insomnia and SHBG was analyzed to obtain β1. Then the causal relationship between SHBG and CTS was valued to get β2. The formula β1 × β2 was utilized to calculate the mediation effect. The total effect of insomnia on CTS was obtained from the above MR analysis (β_all), and (β_all - β1 × β2) was the direct effect. The mediation proportion was calculated as (mediation effect / total effect) × 100%. The confidence interval (CI) for mediation proportion are evaluated via the delta method [31].

Finally, we assessed the statistical power of our analysis using the mRnd method. For the outcome of CTS (N = 480,201, cases = 3.9%), our study possessed > 80% power to detect a causal effect of OR ≥ 1.24 [32]. For the reverse MR analysis where insomnia was the outcome (prevalence ~ 29%), we had > 80% power to detect an OR ≥ 1.15. Additionally, for the continuous mediator (SHBG), the large sample sizes (> 180,000) provided sufficient power to detect small magnitude effects. These calculations suggest that the non-significant associations observed in the reverse and male-specific analyses likely reflect a genuine absence of causality. The “TwoSampleMR” (version 0.5.9) and MR-PRESSO (version 1.0) packages in R software (version 4.3.2) was applied to conduct statistical analyses.

Results

Bidirectional MR analysis

We conducted bidirectional MR analyses of sleep characteristics including morning/evening person (chronotype), snoring and sleeplessness/insomnia with CTS (Supplementary Table S3). Based on the results, there was a causal relationship between insomnia (ukb-b-3957) and CTS.

In ukb-b-3957, after rigorous screening, a total of 205 SNPs related to insomnia were ultimately included in the analysis (Supplementary Table S4). All F-statistics values > 10 indicated no weak instrument bias in this analysis. IVW, as the primary analysis method, indicated that insomnia and CTS had a significant causal relationship (Odds ratio [OR] = 2.326, 95% CI = 1.894–2.857, P < 0.001, Table 1). T In the sensitivity analyses for the effect of insomnia on CTS, we observed a consistent direction and magnitude of effect across several robust methods. The Weighted Median (OR = 2.192, 95% CI: 1.632–2.944, P < 0.001) and Weighted Mode (OR = 2.070, 95% CI: 1.022–4.193, P = 0.045) confirmed the significant positive association observed in the IVW analysis. Although the IVW estimate indicated a strong positive association between genetically predicted insomnia liability and CTS risk, the MR-Egger estimate was smaller and statistically non-significance (OR = 1.214, P = 0.584). This pattern may partly reflect the lower statistical power and wider uncertainty of MR-Egger regression, but it also introduces uncertainty regarding the precise magnitude of the association and the possibility of residual pleiotropy. The weighted median estimate was directionally consistent with the IVW result, supporting a potential positive association; however, the effect size should be interpreted cautiously (Table 1; Fig. 1A).

Mediation analysis

First, we investigated the causal relationship between insomnia and CTS, insomnia and SHBG, SHBG and CTS, respectively (Supplementary Table S6-S8). We utilized sex-stratified SHBG datasets (ieu-b-4871 for males and ieu-b-4870 for females) to account for sexual dimorphism in hormone regulation. In the male SHBG dataset, insomnia was not significantly associated with SHBG in the IVW analysis (OR = 0.867, 95% CI: 0.710–1.060, P = 0.164), whereas male SHBG was significantly associated with CTS (IVW OR = 0.907, 95% CI: 0.857–0.959, P < 0.001). MR-Egger intercept tests showed no evidence of directional pleiotropy for insomnia→male SHBG or male SHBG→CTS (P = 0.522 and P = 0.984, respectively), although heterogeneity was present. Because the insomnia→male SHBG association was not statistically significant, the male SHBG dataset did not support a complete insomnia→male SHBG→CTS mediation pathway and was not used for mediation proportion estimation. Consequently, we focus on female dataset (ieu-b-4870), where significant associations were observed, for the subsequent investigation.

In the Multivariable MR adjusting for SHBG, the direct effect of insomnia on CTS remained significant (OR = 2.200, 95% CI = 1.768–2.738, P < 0.001, Fig. 3A). Both insomnia-SHBG and SHBG-CTS also had significant statistical causal correlation (OR = 0.899, 95% CI = 0.822–0.983, P = 0.019; OR = 0.855, 95% CI = 0.805–0.907, P < 0.001. Figure 3B, C). It could be further observed that an increase in the duration of insomnia would reduce the level of SHBG, and an increase in the level of SHBG would reduce the risk of CTS, respectively. The MR effect sizes of each SNP for insomnia on CTS, insomnia on SHBG and SHBG on CTS were presented in the form of forest plots (Fig. 4). In the sensitivity analysis, there was no heterogeneity found in the causal relationship between insomnia and CTS using MR-Egger or IVW method (P > 0.05, Table 2). MR-PRESSO analysis did not identify any directional pleiotropy (P > 0.05, Table 2). In the causal correlation between insomnia and SHBG, although there was heterogeneity (P < 0.05, Table 2), no directional pleiotropy was found (P > 0.05, Table 2). In the causal correlation between SHBG and CTS, there was both heterogeneity and directional pleiotropy (P < 0.05, Table 2). Sensitivity analysis indicated evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy in the SHBG→CTS pathway, as shown by a significant MR-Egger intercept (intercept = − 0.004, SE = 0.002, P = 0.006). We therefore performed MR-PRESSO analysis for the SHBG→CTS pathway. The MR-PRESSO global test was significant (P < 0.001), and three outlier SNPs were identified: rs28929474, rs62033400, and rs7194995. After removing these outliers, evidence of directional pleiotropy was attenuated and no longer statistically significant according to the MR-Egger intercept test (P = 0.0819). The IVW estimate remained essentially unchanged after outlier correction (OR = 0.856, P = 5.24 × 10⁻⁶). The funnel plots of MR analysis were relatively symmetrical (Fig. 5). The leave-one-out analyses revealed the impact of a single SNP on the stability of MR estimation results (Fig. 6).

Lastly, we performed mediation analysis to explore whether the effect of insomnia on CTS was mediated by SHBG. In the mediation analysis, we obtained the intermediary effect (0.017, 95% CI = 0.001–0.032), direct effect (0.772, 95% CI = 0.552–0.991) and total effect (0.788, 95% CI = 0.570–1.007). The proportion of the genetically predicted effect of insomnia on CTS mediated by SHBG was 2.124% (95% CI: 0.145% − 4.104%). Furthermore, we conducted MR-PRESSO outlier correction and repeated the analysis after excluding three outlier SNPs. The corrected SHBG→CTS estimate was nearly identical to the original estimate, and the mediation proportion remained approximately 2.1%. Given this pleiotropy, the mediation estimates relying on the SHBG-CTS pathway should be interpreted as a putative mediator or partial explanatory factor rather than a confirmed causal mediator, as the precise magnitude of the mediation effect may be influenced by these pleiotropic variants.

Discussion

In our present work, a large-scale GWAS summary data was used to explore the genetically predicted causal relationship between insomnia and CTS. The results from univariable MR analyses revealed a significant association between insomnia and an increased risk of CTS. Furthermore, a two-step MR analysis suggested a potential mediating role of SHBG in the association between insomnia and CTS. These findings on the causal relationships among insomnia, SHBG, and CTS provide new insights into the etiology of CTS.

Sleep is a fundamental physiological process, occupying approximately one-third of the human lifespan [33]. It serves as a critical period for rest and recovery, during which the body undertakes tissue repair and regeneration, fortifies the immune system, consolidates memory, and replenishes both mental and physical energy. In patients diagnosed with CTS, sleep disturbances, particularly insomnia, are commonly reported as a comorbid condition, primarily attributed to hand pain and numbness. It is hypothesized that improper wrist positioning during sleep may acutely exacerbate CTS symptoms by elevating pressure within the carpal canal. Moreover, certain sleep studies involving CTS patients have shown significant improvements in nocturnal movements and nighttime awakenings following carpal tunnel release surgery [34, 35]. These findings provide robust evidence for the tight association between CTS and sleep disturbances. However, the causal relationship between the two conditions is still not clear. Though a two-sample MR study, we examined the relationship between sleep traits and CTS, and surprisingly, identified virtually genetic causal link that insomnia previously overlooked as a concomitant symptom of CTS, was identified as a risk factor for CTS.

Insomnia, the most common sleep disorder among CTS patients, is characterized by difficulties in initiating and maintaining sleep, as well as frequent awakenings during the night and early morning [36, 37]. Sleep issues are linked to the onset of hypertension, metabolic dysregulation leading to diabetes and obesity, increased mortality risk from cardiovascular diseases, exacerbation of chronic pain, and cognitive impairment and depression [5]. However, existing studies have not specifically investigated the direct impact of insomnia on CTS itself, which is the main objective of our study. Because our MR findings suggest a potential association between genetically predicted lifelong liability to insomnia symptoms and increased CTS risk, rather than a confirmed clinical effect of insomnia, endocrine dysregulation and related hormonal changes may partly explain this relationship.

Among these changes, we found that SHBG serves a crucial mediating role in this causal pathway through two-step MR investigation. SHBG, synthesized primarily in the liver, is regulated by insulin, thyroid hormones, and sex hormones, the appropriate secretion of which is also regulated by sufficient sleep. Insomnia-induced endocrine system dysregulation may affect SHBG levels. For instance, insulin resistance and reduced testosterone can reduce SHBG concentrations [38, 39]. As a protein binding and carrying sex hormones, low SHBG levels increase free testosterone and estrogen, which can cause fluid retention and swelling in soft tissues. Thus, SHBG may raise pressure on the median nerve and contribute to CTS development. Furthermore, estrogen receptors (ER) and progesterone receptors have been found in the transverse carpal ligament and flexor tenosynovium [40]. A study found an increase of the expressions of ER in tenosynovial tissue histologically and immunohistochemically in patients with CTS [41]. Finally, SHBG has been found to influence the production of certain inflammatory cytokines, which are closely associated with the pathogenesis of CTS [42, 43]. By regulating these cytokines, SHBG may indirectly impact the inflammatory response within the carpal tunnel, thereby influencing the progression of CTS. However, given the initial evidence of directional pleiotropy in SHBG→CTS, even though MR-PRESSO correction yielded stable estimates, SHBG should be interpreted as a putative mediator rather than a confirmed causal mediator. Furthermore, the biological coherence of this model is limited by a sex-discrepancy in our data: while the insomnia and CTS cohorts are sex-mixed, the mediation effect was only statistically viable in the female-stratified SHBG dataset (the male-stratified analysis yielded null results). This disconnect implies that any putative mediating role of SHBG is likely sex-specific and cannot be universally generalized to the broader CTS population. Importantly, SHBG should not be interpreted as an isolated causal mediator. Because metabolic dysfunction may precede changes in SHBG, the observed SHBG-related pathway may reflect a broader metabolic-endocrine process rather than an independent hormonal mechanism.

In addition to hormonal mechanisms, insomnia may contribute to CTS through various alternative pathways. Individuals suffering from insomnia often experience heightened stress and muscle tension during sleep, particularly affecting the upper limbs and wrist muscles [44]. Sustained muscle tension can diminish blood flow to the carpal tunnel, resulting in local ischemia and hypoxia, thereby damaging the median nerve. Moreover, insomnia frequently leads to poor sleep quality and frequent awakenings, which may cause individuals to adopt unfavorable wrist positions, such as prolonged flexion or extension, during sleep. These positions can elevate carpal tunnel pressure and mechanically compress the median nerve [45]. Furthermore, insomnia may compromise the immune system, impairing its ability to repair nerve damage and rendering the median nerve more vulnerable to compression injury [46]. However, owing to the absence of experimental validation, the aforementioned mechanisms warrant cautious consideration, and additional research is imperative.

Intriguingly, in the reverse MR analyses, we found almost no causal effect of CTS on sleep disturbances, which contradicts traditional perceptions. Our interpretation of these findings suggests that pathological alterations within the carpal tunnel are not directly responsible for sleep disturbances. Instead, the primary contributors to sleep disorders are the pain and numbness in the hand resulting from nerve compression. This is a significant reason why patients with carpal tunnel syndrome experiencing sleep disturbances pursue medical intervention.

This study possesses several notable strengths. Foremost, it constitutes the inaugural investigation to delineate a direct genetic correlation between insomnia, SHBG, and CTS, thereby elucidating the independent influence of insomnia on CTS development and the mediating role of SHBG in the effect of insomnia on CTS. The employment of genetic instrumental variables enhances causal estimation by reducing bias, including confounding factors and reverse causality. To enhance the precision of causal inferences, a comprehensive and rigorous analysis employing bidirectional and multivariate MR techniques was undertaken. Furthermore, a series of sensitivity analyses were conducted to verify the robustness and consistency of the causal relationships.

While this study possesses notable strengths, it is crucial to acknowledge its limitations. Firstly, the datasets employed were exclusively derived from individuals of European ancestry. Given that genetic architecture, allele frequencies, and linkage disequilibrium patterns can vary significantly across populations, the results of this analysis may not be directly applicable to other ethnic groups. Consequently, future research involving more diverse populations is necessary to validate and extend these findings. Second, the lack of data stratification by sex and age, two factors known to influence the prevalence of CTS, constitutes a significant limitation of the study. Given that both conditions are more prevalent among women and older individuals, and that SHBG levels are inherently sex-dimorphic, the inability to perform sex-stratified analyses represents a critical limitation. Investigating whether the SHBG-mediated pathway is specific to, or stronger in, females is essential for clinical translation. However, such analyses were not feasible due to the unavailability of publicly accessible stratified GWAS summary data. Future research incorporating stratified datasets will be crucial for validating and extending our findings. Third, the CTS definition in the outcome GWAS relied heavily on ICD codes and self-reports rather than gold-standard nerve conduction studies. This lack of strict clinical confirmation may introduce phenotypic misclassification and reduce diagnostic specificity. Fourth, MR relies on the assumption of a linear relationship between exposure variables, such as insomnia, and disease outcomes, such as CTS. If this relationship is nonlinear, it could undermine the validity of using this method in the analysis. Fifth, the CTS GWAS meta-analysis includes participants from the UK Biobank, which was also the source for sleep-trait GWAS datasets, raising the possibility of sample overlap. Although all selected SNPs reported F-statistics > 10 to ensure robust instrumentation. Additionally, the MR-Egger intercept P-value (0.057) approached the significance threshold, suggesting that residual directional pleiotropy cannot be fully excluded. The MR-Egger causal estimate was also smaller and non-significant compared with the IVW estimate, which weakens confidence in the precise magnitude of the association. Therefore, the IVW estimate should be interpreted as the effect of genetically predicted lifelong liability to insomnia symptoms, rather than evidence that clinically diagnosed insomnia directly doubles short-term CTS risk. The magnitude of this association may be inflated because of possible residual pleiotropy, sample overlap, phenotype misclassification, and the relaxed SNP threshold. Future studies using fully independent datasets and clinically defined sleep phenotypes are needed to validate this association. Sixth, the SHBG-related mediation result should be interpreted cautiously because SHBG is closely intertwined with metabolic traits, including body mass index, insulin resistance, and diabetes. A plausible alternative pathway is that insomnia contributes to metabolic dysfunction, which then lowers SHBG levels and increases CTS risk. In this scenario, SHBG may function as a downstream biomarker or partial explanatory factor rather than an independent causal mediator. We did not perform MVMR adjustment for body mass index or diabetes because these traits may lie upstream of SHBG in the causal pathway; conditioning on them would estimate a controlled direct effect independent of metabolic pathways and may represent overadjustment rather than a straightforward sensitivity analysis. Therefore, the present study cannot distinguish whether SHBG itself has a causal role or whether it reflects insomnia-related metabolic dysfunction. Seventh, our primary analysis utilized a relaxed instrumental variable selection threshold (P < 5 × 10⁻⁶) to maximize the number of SNPs available for comprehensive sensitivity analyses. While all selected variants possessed F-statistics > 10, indicating sufficient instrument strength, employing this liberal threshold carries the inherent risk of inadvertently including pleiotropic variants, which could potentially inflate the estimated causal effect sizes. Importantly, when we restricted our sensitivity analyses strictly to the 39 SNPs meeting standard genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸), more conservative models, such as the Simple and Weighted Mode methods, lost statistical significance. Consequently, while our primary IVW estimates remained robust, the magnitude of the causal associations observed in this study should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Finally, regarding the mediation analysis, we identified heterogeneity and directional horizontal pleiotropy in the causal pathway linking SHBG to CTS. While we employed robust estimators to mitigate these biases, the presence of pleiotropy suggests that the estimated proportion of mediation should be interpreted with caution. Although we conducted supplementary analyses to explore the role of SHBG in the development of CTS associated with insomnia, there remains a paucity of direct experimental or histological studies that specifically demonstrate the causal pathway from insomnia to CTS. This constrains the depth of mechanistic inference possible from the current data and underscores the need for future prospective, histopathological, and biomechanical studies to elucidate causal pathways.

In conclusion, our study provides evidence supporting a genetically predicted causal association between insomnia, SHBG, and CTS. We demonstrate that insomnia is positively correlated with an elevated risk of developing CTS, with SHBG acting as a mediating factor in this association. These findings underscore that insomnia, traditionally viewed as a concomitant symptom of CTS, also constitutes a risk factor for the condition. Moreover, this study introduces a novel research avenue for clinicians to explore the pathophysiological mechanisms and interactions between insomnia and CTS, with the objective of improving the management of these conditions.
