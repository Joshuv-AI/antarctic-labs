Mimicking mammalian hibernation to lock cancer cells in safe quiescence
Authors: Amirsajad Jafari, Fatemeh Shams, Elina Khanehzar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Relapse in cancer is often driven by disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) that enter a dormant, non-proliferative state and later reactivate. Conventional pharmacology primarily focuses on eradicating dividing cells but lacks tools to induce and maintain a benign dormant state. Mammalian hibernation offers a natural, reversible model of systemic cellular quiescence mediated by dynamic epigenetic reprogramming. Here, we propose that studying and pharmacologically mimicking the epigenetic landscape of hibernators may provide a novel class of agents, “dormostatics”, to lock DTCs into safe dormancy. We discuss key epigenetic features of torpor (DNA methylation shifts, histone modifications, sirtuin and SUMO pathways), their analogues in cancer cell biology, and articulate a translational roadmap toward epigenetic dormancy pharmacology. Finally, we highlight major outstanding questions and key clinical considerations.

Introduction

Cancer relapse remains one of the most difficult problems in oncology [1]. Even after apparently successful treatment, some tumor cells can persist in a dormant state, only to return later with renewed growth [2]. Most existing drugs are designed to destroy dividing cells, yet few can safely place malignant ones into a reversible pause. The field needs a shift in focus, from killing to controlled suspension of activity.

In the natural world, this strategy already exists. Hibernating mammals such as the thirteen-lined ground squirrel (Ictidomys tridecemlineatus) enter seasonal torpor in which body temperature, metabolism, and cell proliferation fall sharply. Still, tissue integrity is preserved, and full recovery follows [3]. Recent molecular studies reveal that this condition depends on flexible molecular regulation (epigenetic and transcriptomic) [4–6]. These insights point toward an intriguing idea: adapting the epigenetic/post-translational regulation logic of hibernation to place cancer cells into a therapeutic form of dormancy.

Characteristics of dormant cancer cells: therapy evasion, metabolic reprogramming, and epigenetic/post-translational control

Dormant cancer cells (DCCs) are a subpopulation of malignant cells that have entered a reversible, non-proliferative state (G0/G1 arrest), remain viable for extended periods, and resist conventional therapies while retaining the capacity to later reactivate and cause relapse [7]. DCCs are not merely passive; they actively reprogram their metabolism and transcriptional programs to survive without proliferating [8]. Characteristic features include reversible cell cycle arrest in G0/G1 phase, resistance to apoptosis, a shifted balance between stress-activated (p38 MAPK) and proliferation-promoting (ERK) signaling, and upregulation of autophagy and unfolded protein response pathways [9, 10]. Recent work indicates they shift away from glycolysis toward oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, and upregulate stress-adaptive pathways (e.g., p38 MAPK) to withstand chemotherapy [9, 11]. They also display enhanced expression of anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., BCL-2, BCL-XL), downregulated death receptors, and increased activity of DNA repair mechanisms [12–14]. Furthermore, the tumor microenvironment can either promote or prevent dormancy, with immune cells such as NK cells playing a critical role in maintaining or losing control over DTCs [15]. Dormant DTCs frequently reside in specialized niches (e.g., bone marrow, perivascular sites) that provide survival signals (CXCL12, TGF-β2, GAS6) while restricting proliferation [16, 17].

Critically, the ability to enter, maintain, and exit dormancy is not only a permanent genetic mutation, but also a dynamic epigenetic/post-translational reprogramming [16, 17]. Dormant cancer cells exhibit distinct transcriptional and epigenetic profiles characterized by the downregulation of growth-promoting pathways and the induction of autophagy, under the control of factors such as p53 [18]. Moreover, recent research has uncovered that quiescent DTCs can be primed by oncogenic enhancers that establish a “transcriptional memory,” reinforcing dormancy and actively helping cells evade NK cell-mediated immune surveillance [19]. These observations implicates the epigenetic machinery as a key determinant of long-term DTC persistence and resistance.

Epigenetic/post-translational regulation in Hibernation

Epigenetic/post-translational regulation in hibernating mammals is highly dynamic, enabling reversible transcriptional suppression during torpor and rapid restoration during arousal [4].

Across multiple hibernating species, including Ictidomys tridecemlineatus, both global and locus-specific DNA methylation undergo coordinated shifts during torpor and arousal. Alvarado et al. (2015) demonstrated that skeletal muscle exhibits an approximately 16% reduction in total methylation during late torpor, whereas liver methylation increases transiently during interbout arousals. These dynamics are accompanied by stage-specific adjustments in DNA methyltransferases (DNMT1, DNMT3B) and methyl-binding proteins (MBD1, MBD2, MBD3, MeCP2) [4], indicating a regulated methylation landscape rather than passive drift.

Epigenomic remodeling extends beyond DNA methylation to include broad reconfiguration of histone modifications. Tessier et al. (2017) demonstrated that phosphorylation, acetylation, and methylation patterns of histone or DNA in skeletal muscle undergo systematic shifts across the torpor–arousal cycle, promoting a transcriptionally repressed yet rapidly reversible chromatin state. Using mass spectrometry, they further identified previously unrecognized acetylation sites on histone H3 (K19, K24) and H2B (K6, K47, K110, K117) [6], underscoring the fine-tuned nature of chromatin adaptation during metabolic suppression.

A later proteomic analysis by Carter et al. (2025) broadened this view by surveying 24 histone post-translational modifications in the liver during deep torpor compared to interbout arousal and basal (summer) active states. Their findings revealed a coordinated reduction in activating acetyl marks (e.g., H2BK5ac, H3K18ac, H3K23ac, H3K27ac), transcription-linked methyl marks (H3K4me2, H3K4me3), and certain regulatory mono- and symmetric dimethyl marks (H4K20me1, H4R3me2s) during torpor, alongside decreased overall abundance of histones H2B and H4. In contrast, the linker histone H1.0 was the only variant to increase, consistent with a shift toward a more compact chromatin architecture [20].

Finally, broader comparative surveys indicate that additional reversible epigenetic-post translational regulation control, such as SUMOylation of histones (post-translational conjugation of small ubiquitin-like modifier proteins to target proteins) and sirtuin activation (sirtuins SIRT1–7 are a family of NAD⁺-dependent histone deacetylases), also contribute to the metabolic and transcriptional plasticity characteristic of torpor states [21–24].

Crosstalk between signaling pathways, epigenetic reprogramming, and the tumor microenvironment in dormancy

While hibernation provides a systemic model of quiescence, the regulation of cancer cell dormancy involves intricate crosstalk between key signaling pathways and the epigenetic machinery, further modulated by the tumor microenvironment (TME). Emerging evidence indicates that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK/ERK cascades, which are central to cell growth and proliferation, are dynamically suppressed in dormant disseminated tumor cells (DTCs), whereas stress-activated pathways such as p38 MAPK are upregulated [9, 25]. This shift in signaling balance is not independent of epigenetic control; rather, these pathways reciprocally influence and are influenced by DNA methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin remodeling [26]. For example, PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling can regulate the expression and activity of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs), while the MAPK/ERK pathway interacts with chromatin modifiers to control the expression of cell-cycle genes [9, 27]. Conversely, epigenetic modifiers such as the H3K9 methyltransferase G9a cooperate with hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) under low-oxygen conditions to enforce quiescence. This bidirectional crosstalk creates a flexible epigenetic landscape that allows DTCs to adapt to stressful microenvironments without undergoing permanent genetic change [28–31].

The TME itself is a critical regulator of dormancy. Hypoxia, a common feature of metastatic niches such as the bone marrow, stabilizes HIF-1α, which in turn induces cell-cycle inhibitors (p21, p27) and cooperates with G9a to promote repressive chromatin marks, thereby locking cells in a non-proliferative state [32, 33]. Immune modulation also plays a decisive role: natural killer (NK) cells can enforce dormancy through interferon-γ signaling, but DTCs can evade this control through epigenetic mechanisms [15, 19, 34]. Notably, oncogenic enhancers establish a “transcriptional memory” that reinforces quiescence while simultaneously suppressing NK-mediated immune surveillance. Additionally, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling influences dormancy–reactivation transitions; high ECM stiffness upregulates the protein TNNT1, which awakens dormant DTCs and promotes outgrowth [35]. These TME factors do not act in isolation but integrate with signaling pathways and epigenetic regulators to determine whether DTCs remain dormant, die, or proliferate. Importantly, many of these features, reduced mTOR signaling, enhanced stress resistance, and immune adaptation, have parallels in hibernating mammalian tissues, further strengthening the rationale for pharmacologically mimicking the hibernation epigenetic program to induce safe, reversible dormancy in cancer cells.

Mechanistic parallels between hibernation and cancer dormancy

Dormant cancer cells and hibernating tissues share hallmark features: non‑proliferation, low metabolic activity, stress resistance, and later re‑exit to a proliferative state [36]. At the epigenetic level [25, 36–41].

The RB/E2F/DREAM complex in quiescence parallels hibernator disengagement of cell‑cycle transcription

The RB/E2F/DREAM complex is a multi-protein assembly that integrates retinoblastoma protein (RB) with E2F transcription factors and the DREAM (DP, RB-like, E2F4, MuvB) complex to repress cell-cycle-promoting genes, thereby inducing cellular quiescence [42]. This complex also recruits epigenetic modifiers such as histone deacetylases (HDACs) and methyltransferases to establish repressive chromatin marks (e.g., H3K9me3, H3K27me3) at target gene promoters, linking transcriptional repression directly to heritable epigenetic silencing [42, 43]. This non-proliferation mechanism is conserved in hibernating animals during torpor, enabling metabolic conservation and stress resistance [44]. Pathologically, DTCs co-opt this same network to enter dormancy, facilitating chemoresistance and survival in harsh microenvironments [45]. Notably, this complex interacts with circadian clock proteins (e.g., PER2 stabilizing p53), suggesting clock-mediated epigenetic control in both states [46, 47].

Repressive chromatin marks (H3K9me3, H3K27me3) are enriched in dormant DTCs and abundant in torpor states

Repressive histone modifications, notably H3K9me3 and H3K27me3, establish a compact heterochromatic state essential for gene silencing [48]. This epigenetic mechanism is conserved in both hibernating animals during torpor and in dormant DTCs [20, 49–51]. For instance, in a prostate cancer model of DTCs, silencing of the Mer tyrosine kinase (MERTK) directly increases H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 levels, induces G1/G0 arrest, and prolongs metastasis-free survival in xenografts, demonstrating a functional role for these repressive marks in establishing and maintaining dormancy [49]. Conversely, MERTK overexpression triggers the loss of H3K27me3 and reactivates proliferation, underscoring the dynamic reversibility of this epigenetic state. In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), high H3K9me3 expression correlates with therapy-induced senescence and improved patient survival, while its absence is linked to more aggressive, relapse-prone disease [52]. These studies illustrate that H3K9me3- and H3K27me3-mediated heterochromatin formation can lock tumor cells in a non-proliferative state, directly supporting the dormostatic hypothesis.

DNA methylation changes in cancer cells’ dormancy mimic the remodeling seen in hibernators

Dormant cancer cells and hibernating mammals use similar DNA methylation strategies to enter and maintain a dormant state. In both cases, changes such as global DNA methylation suppress non-essential processes (like the cell cycle in cancer) to conserve energy and promote survival [6, 24, 25]. This allows for rapid reactivation later, which in cancer leads to relapse. In both cases, changes such as global DNA methylation suppress non-essential processes, like the cell cycle in cancer, to conserve energy and promote survival. For example, NR2F1, a master regulator of cellular quiescence, is epigenetically activated via DNA demethylation in DTCs and has been shown to induce global chromatin repression and maintain dormancy in prostate and head and neck cancers [53]. Similarly, the promoter of the myocyte enhancer factor 2 C (Mef2c) gene, a key regulator of metabolism, undergoes dynamic DNA methylation changes in the skeletal muscle of hibernating ground squirrels, highlighting a conserved epigenetic mechanism for metabolic suppression in both systems [4]. Furthermore, large-scale epigenomic analyses in aggressive breast cancers have identified over 150 genes, including the tumour suppressor GATA3, that are silenced by DNMT3B-driven promoter hypermethylation and are associated with high relapse risk, mirroring the methylation-mediated suppression of non-essential gene programs seen during hibernation [54].

Both torpor–arousal and cancer cells’ dormancy–reactivation cycles are circadian clock‑sensitive

Both torpor–arousal and cancer cells’ dormancy–reactivation cycles are circadian clock-sensitive, with clock proteins (e.g., BMAL1, PER, CRY) undergoing post-translational modifications (e.g., SIRT1-mediated deacetylation and SUMOylation) that influence quiescence maintenance [47, 55–58].

Similarly, in cancer dormancy, the reactivation of DTCs is often aligned with a state of circadian dysregulation. The core clock transcription factors BMAL1 and CLOCK are crucial in this context; while they typically control cell cycle progression, DNA repair, and metabolism, their aberrant expression in tumors can either suppress or promote malignancy depending on the cancer type [59, 60]. For instance, the Dec2 gene, a circadian regulator, has been shown to enforce pancreatic cancer dormancy by downregulating surface MHC-I on DTCs, allowing them to evade T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity. This provides a direct mechanistic link where a clock-controlled gene actively maintains the dormant state by enabling immune evasion [61]. Conversely, the disruption of normal circadian oscillations can fuel malignant progression. For example, a compromised circadian clock has been shown to accelerate cancer metastasis by promoting an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [61]. These findings underscore a critical relationship: a functioning circadian rhythm helps maintain DTCs in a stable, immune-controlled dormancy, whereas its breakdown creates a permissive environment that can drive a synchronized, and often aggressive, reactivation.

Evidence, parallels to cancer dormancy, and potential pharmacological mimics

We propose a new category: dormostatics: pharmacologic agents designed to enforce or maintain a safe, reversible dormant state in cancer cells. Candidate target axes include SUV39H1/H3K9me3, DNMT3A/B, sirtuin activation, SUMOylation networks, and chronopharmacology of epigenetic agents (Fig. 1).

Clinician’s corner

Adjuvant therapy now aims to clear residual disease. A dormostatic approach would pivot toward containment and long-term upkeep. Potential biomarkers include circulating chromatin fragments, patterns of histone marks, and DNA methylation states. Safety remains central; dormostatic agents should permit immune clearance or senescence while minimizing the chance of a durable escape from dormancy. Several of the pharmacological mimics proposed as dormostatics in Table 1 have already advanced into clinical trials, including direct evaluations in cancer patients. Table 2 summarizes key examples from sirtuin activators (e.g., SRT2104), BET inhibitors (e.g., birabresib, molibresib), and NAD⁺ boosters (e.g., nicotinamide mononucleotide) as well as some others. However, the reversible nature of induced dormancy necessitates strategies to mitigate risks of therapy interruption or resistance, which could precipitate synchronized relapse. Long-term adherence, toxicity management, and biomarkers for early escape detection will be critical. The endgame—whether indefinite containment or deliberate reactivation followed by eradication—should be tailored to disease context, patient factors, and emerging resistance patterns.

Pitfalls and drawbacks

Fundamental differences between physiological hibernation and pathological cancer dormancy

While the epigenetic mechanisms reviewed above reveal intriguing parallels between natural hibernation and cancer cell dormancy, critical differences must be emphasized. Hibernation is a highly orchestrated, evolutionarily conserved physiological program executed in genetically stable, non-transformed cells to protect the whole organism during metabolic stress. In contrast, cancer dormancy emerges in cells harboring genomic instability, accumulated mutations, defective DNA repair pathways, and clonal heterogeneity. These pathological features render cancer cells fundamentally dysregulated. Attempting to pharmacologically impose hibernation-inspired epigenetic controls onto such “chaotic” genomes, therefore, carries inherent risks. Cancer cells may not interpret dormostatic signals in the predictable, reversible manner observed in healthy hibernator tissues. Instead, partial or aberrant activation of quiescence pathways could trigger alternative survival mechanisms, incomplete metabolic suppression, or novel forms of therapy resistance. For example, cells with compromised DNA repair or altered chromatin architecture might enter unstable intermediate states that ultimately favor relapse rather than long-term containment. Moreover, drugs that effectively induce torpor-like states in healthy hibernating tissues may not translate directly to cancer cells. Hibernation involves coordinated, systemic changes in hormone levels (e.g., thyroid hormone, leptin), temperature regulation, and metabolic rate that are not present in isolated DTCs within a normothermic patient. A drug that increases SIRT1 activity or enhances SUMOylation in a ground squirrel liver may produce off-target effects, incomplete pathway engagement, or outright resistance in a genetically heterogeneous tumor cell population. Conversely, cancer cells may have evolved to co-opt dormancy pathways for survival under chemotherapy, potentially rendering them refractory to agents that merely mimic the hibernation epigenome. Therefore, positive results in hibernation studies should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than predictive of efficacy in cancer. Rigorous preclinical testing in authentic DTC dormancy models—including patient-derived xenografts, minimal residual disease (MRD) models, and co-culture systems that recapitulate the metastatic niche—is essential before any clinical translation of dormostatics can be considered. These risks underscore the need for cautious translational development, rigorous preclinical testing in models that recapitulate genomic instability, and close monitoring for unintended adaptive responses in early clinical studies.

Therapeutic endgame: managing reversibility and achieving elimination

A core feature of hibernation, and thus a potential limitation of dormostatic approaches, is its inherent reversibility, designed for eventual arousal. Pharmacologically induced dormancy in cancer cells would similarly preserve viable “seeds” (DTCs) that retain the capacity for reactivation. Therapy interruption (e.g., due to toxicity or non-compliance) or evolution of resistance could therefore trigger a synchronized “awakening,” potentially leading to aggressive, uniform relapse rather than staggered regrowth [82]. This risk underscores the need for continuous monitoring (e.g., via circulating biomarkers) and combination strategies to prevent escape [83, 84].

Two complementary endgame strategies emerge. First, chronic containment: dormostatics could maintain long-term quiescence as a management approach, akin to adaptive therapy paradigms that prioritize tumor control over eradication by exploiting competition between sensitive and resistant clones or stabilizing burden through dose modulation [85, 86]. This might require indefinite treatment, like antiviral therapy in HIV, but could extend progression-free survival in MRDs settings.

Second, planned elimination via synchronized reactivation: after prolonged dormancy (e.g., years), targeted withdrawal of dormostatics or addition of reactivation triggers (e.g., modulators of p38/ERK signaling, autophagy inhibitors, or lncRNA pathways) could force uniform exit from quiescence, rendering previously resistant DTCs proliferative and vulnerable to cell-cycle-specific therapies (chemotherapy, CDK inhibitors in reversal). This “wake-and-kill” approach leverages the reversible nature of hibernation-like states to create a therapeutic window [87]. Preclinical models and early adaptive therapy trials support feasibility, though clinical translation requires identifying safe reactivation cues that avoid uncontrolled relapse.

Selectivity challenges: achieving tumor-specific effects while minimizing systemic toxicity

The epigenetic mechanisms targeted by dormostatics, such as reduced histone acetylation (via sirtuin activation or HAT inhibition), enhanced SUMOylation, and RB-mediated quiescence, are fundamental to cellular homeostasis across all tissues, not exclusive to cancer cells [88, 89]. Systemic administration, therefore, risks inducing partial “torpor-like” suppression in healthy proliferating compartments, including hematopoietic stem cells (bone marrow), intestinal crypt cells (gut), and epidermal progenitors (skin), potentially causing myelosuppression, gastrointestinal toxicity, or impaired wound healing.

Clinical experience with candidate agents illustrates these concerns. CDK4/6 inhibitors (e.g., palbociclib, ribociclib), which activate RB to enforce quiescence, frequently cause neutropenia and leukopenia due to reversible cytostatic effects on bone marrow progenitors; the most common dose-limiting toxicity [90, 91]. Sirtuin activators (e.g., SRT2104) and NAD⁺ precursors have shown favorable safety profiles in early trials, with minimal hematologic toxicity [92, 93]. SUMOylation enhancers remain preclinical or early-stage, with limited human data, though related inhibitors (e.g., TAK-981) have reported manageable toxicities [94, 95].

Evidence gaps: from preclinical quiescence induction to clinical dormostatic validation

The dormostatic hypothesis posits that certain epigenetic modulators, particularly those activating RB-mediated repression (e.g., CDK4/6 inhibitors), may enforce a safe, reversible quiescent state in DTCs akin to hibernation torpor [95]. However, no clinical trials to date have directly tested this mechanism; existing studies of candidate agents (e.g., CDK4/6 inhibitors in Table 2) primarily evaluate progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival, or tumor shrinkage, endpoints reflecting cytostasis in proliferating tumors rather than dormancy induction in MRDs.

Preclinically, CDK4/6 inhibitors robustly induce RB-dependent G1 arrest, often transitioning to quiescence or a senescence-like state, with evidence of prolonged cell-cycle withdrawal even after drug removal in some models [96–98]. Continued inhibition in resistant models delays cycle re-entry, consistent with enforced quiescence. For other candidates (e.g., sirtuin activators, BET inhibitors), preclinical links to dormancy are emerging but indirect, via epigenetic silencing or stress resistance pathways [99–101].

Clinically, adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibitors reduce early recurrences in high-risk HR+ breast cancer, potentially by constraining MRD, but late relapses persist, and mechanisms remain unconfirmed. Promisingly, trials like PALAVY (NCT04841148) explicitly target dormant MRD with palbociclib (combined with immunotherapy or autophagy modulation) to prevent reactivation, providing a direct test of dormancy-focused therapy. Future validation will require dormancy-specific endpoints: persistence of quiescent DTCs (via bone marrow aspirates), epigenetic biomarkers (e.g., H3K27me3 enrichment), or ctDNA dynamics post-treatment.

It is important to emphasize that the epigenetic modulators proposed as dormostatics in Table 1 are derived from literature-based correlations between hibernation biology and cancer cell quiescence. To date, none of these agents have been systematically tested for their ability to induce or maintain a safe, reversible dormant state specifically in DTCs. The proposed mechanisms, such as RB-mediated G1 arrest via CDK4/6 inhibitors, SIRT activation, HDAC inhibition, or SUMOylation enhancement, are supported by indirect evidence from cancer cell lines, drug-tolerant persister models, or hibernation studies, but direct functional validation in authentic DTC dormancy models (e.g., bone marrow-homed DTCs or in vivo MRD models) is lacking. Therefore, the dormostatic concept remains a speculative but testable hypothesis. Thus, the dormostatic lens represents a reinterpretation of existing agents’ effects, supported by mechanistic parallels but awaiting dedicated clinical evidence.

Tumor heterogeneity and microenvironmental interference

Two additional limitations merit careful consideration. First, tumor heterogeneity, both genetic and epigenetic, poses a substantial challenge to dormostatic strategies [102]. Even within a single patient, DTCs exhibit clonal diversity, including variations in cell-cycle regulators, chromatin modifying enzymes, and signaling pathway activation states [103]. This heterogeneity may result in variable or incomplete responses to any given dormostatic agent. Some clones may fail to enter quiescence entirely, whereas others might enter an unstable intermediate state that predisposes to later relapse. Moreover, epigenetic plasticity itself can generate resistant subpopulations that acquire alternative survival mechanisms, such as upregulation of parallel stress-response pathways or autophagy escape routes. Consequently, a “one-size-fits-all” dormostatic regimen is unlikely to be universally effective, and combination strategies or biomarker-driven patient selection will likely be necessary.

Second, the TME provides dynamic and sometimes opposing signals that may override or destabilize pharmacologically induced epigenetic states. For instance, hypoxia can activate HIF-dependent transcriptional programs that counteract repressive histone marks, while inflammation-associated cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) may reactivate proliferation through NF-κB signaling [9]. Extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, particularly increased stiffness, has been shown to awaken dormant DTCs via mechanotransduction pathways that upregulate cell-cycle genes [35]. These TME cues do not operate in isolation; they often converge on the same epigenetic regulators targeted by dormostatics (e.g., HDACs, histone methyltransferases), potentially competing with or reversing the drug-induced quiescent state [104]. Therefore, successful clinical translation will require not only effective dormostatic compounds but also strategies to stabilize the TME or block its pro-reactivation signals, possibly through combination therapies that target both the cancer cell epigenome and the surrounding niche.

Drug resistance to dormostatics: evolutionary and epigenetic escape pathways

An additional concern is that cancer cells may eventually evolve resistance to dormostatic agents, just as they do to conventional chemotherapies. Because dormostatics are designed to enforce quiescence rather than kill cells, the selective pressure is fundamentally different: cells that manage to bypass or break free from the induced dormant state would gain a proliferative advantage, potentially leading to aggressive relapse. Several resistance mechanisms can be envisioned. First, genetic mutations in the target pathways, for example, RB1 loss-of-function mutations rendering CDK4/6 inhibitors ineffective, or DNMT3A alterations that abolish methylation maintenance could allow cells to ignore dormostatic signals [105, 106]. Second, epigenetic plasticity may enable cells to rewire their chromatin landscape, upregulating compensatory survival pathways (e.g., alternative stress kinases, autophagy enhancers) or reactivating cell-cycle genes via enhancer reprogramming [107]. Third, microenvironmental adaptation might occur, wherein DTCs alter their niche interactions to receive pro-proliferative signals that override drug-induced quiescence [108]. Fourth, heterogeneity within the DTC pool means that pre-existing resistant subclones could be selected during dormostatic therapy, leading to outgrowth of cells that never properly entered dormancy or that exit it prematurely [109]. Importantly, because dormostatics are envisioned for long-term maintenance (potentially years), the risk of acquired resistance is non-trivial. Monitoring strategies, such as serial liquid biopsies for emerging mutations or epigenetic biomarkers, and rational combination therapies (e.g., dual targeting of parallel quiescence pathways) will be essential to mitigate this risk. Preclinical models that simulate prolonged drug exposure and allow for resistance evolution are urgently needed to inform clinical trial design.

Why dormostatics?

The final question is why one would pursue dormancy induction rather than directly attempting to eliminate all malignant cells. Conventional oncology prioritizes maximal tumor cell killing, often with curative intent. However, in settings where eradication is unattainable such as minimal MRDs after therapy, DTCs in sanctuary sites, or metastatic disease resistant to standard regimens, a purely cytotoxic approach may be futile or even harmful. Dormostatics offer a fundamentally different philosophy: containment rather than elimination. By locking DTCs into a reversible, non-proliferative state, dormostatics aim to prevent relapse without imposing the selective pressure that drives resistance to cytotoxic agents. This approach is conceptually related to, but distinct from, adaptive therapy [86, 110].

Adaptive therapy dynamically adjusts drug doses to maintain a stable tumor burden by exploiting competitive interactions between sensitive and resistant clones, rather than attempting maximal kill [85, 111]. Dormostatics, in contrast, directly enforce cellular quiescence through epigenetic/post-translational reprogramming, independent of evolutionary competition. The two strategies are complementary: adaptive therapy manages proliferating tumor populations, while dormostatics target the non-proliferating DTC reservoir. In practice, a combined approach could be envisioned; using adaptive therapy to control bulk disease while administering dormostatics to suppress DTC reactivation.

When is dormostatic containment preferable to attempted eradication? Several scenarios argue for this shift: (i) when no curative option exists (e.g., metastatic pancreatic cancer, late-stage relapse) [112]; (ii) when eradication requires toxic therapies that compromise quality of life, especially in elderly or comorbid patients; (iii) when DTCs are known to persist after conventional treatment (e.g., bone marrow DTCs in breast cancer); and (iv) when resistance to cytotoxic agents has already emerged, leaving no effective killing strategy. In such contexts, turning cancer into a manageable chronic condition, analogous to HIV management with antiretroviral therapy, may be preferable to repeated cycles of toxic, ineffective treatment [113]. Dormostatics thus represent a paradigm shift from “kill at all costs” to “pause safely,” acknowledging that for some patients, long-term containment may offer better outcomes than futile attempts at eradication.

Future directions and conclusion

Oncology may move from “kill more” to “pause wisely.” The epigenetic program of mammalian hibernation offers a latent playbook for therapeutic dormancy. Comparative epigenomics in hibernators and dormant cancer cells is now within reach. Early trials could explore NAD⁺ enhancers or sirtuin activators in patients with MRDs. Additionally, future strategies could incorporate dormostatics, contrasting with conventional chemotherapy’s focus on eliminating rapidly proliferating cells. After a prolonged hibernation-like dormancy, inducing a synchronized reactivation could render these cells more vulnerable, enhancing the efficacy of standard therapies upon their reawakening. Also, The identification of novel dormostatic compounds may be accelerated by next-generation computational pipelines [114, 115].

In sum, hibernating mammals model a reversible, whole-body quiescent state. Converting their epigenetic toolkit into pharmacologic dormostatics could reshape how we prevent relapse.
