Universal Adaptive Proximal Gradient Methods via Gradient Mapping Accumulation
Zimeng Wang, Alp Yurtsever

Abstract
We propose an adaptive proximal gradient method for minimizing the sum of two functions, where one is a simple convex function, and the other belongs to one of the three classes: nonconvex smooth, convex nonsmooth, or convex smooth. The key feature of the method is an adaptive step size that accumulates historical gradient mapping norms in the denominator. Without any modification or knowledge of problem parameters, the method converges across all three problem classes under mild bounded-iterates and bounded-variance assumptions, with rates matching those of the proximal gradient method up to logarithmic factors, in both deterministic and stochastic settings. For the convex setting, we further propose an accelerated variant. It retains a similar near-optimal convergence rate for the nonsmooth case and achieves an improved rate of order O ~ ​ ( 1 / t 2 + σ / t ) \widetilde{O}\big(1/t^{2}+\sigma/\sqrt{t}\big) for the smooth case, which is optimal up to logarithmic factors. Notably, we develop new techniques for controlling the effect of stochastic noise, which are applicable across all three problem classes in the stochastic setting and enable simplified analysis.



1
Introduction

We study the following nonsmooth composite optimization problem:

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(x):=f(x)+h(x),

(1.1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is either nonconvex smooth or convex (possibly nonsmooth), and
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is convex, proper, and lower semicontinuous, but possibly nonsmooth. Problems of this form are ubiquitous in machine learning, signal processing, and statistics. Representative examples include LASSO
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
, support vector machine
(
Cortes and Vapnik 1995
)
, regularized logistic regression
(
Berkson 1944
)
, and trace-norm matrix completion
(
Candes and Recht 2012
)
. In the stochastic setting, we are particularly interested in the case
f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\in\mathcal{D}}[\ell(x;\xi)]
, where
\ell(\cdot;\xi):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the loss function associated with the sample
\xi
drawn from the underlying data space
\mathcal{D}
.

A widely used first-order algorithm for solving (
1.1
) is the proximal gradient (PG) method, also known as the forward-backward splitting algorithm
(
Lions and Mercier 1979
;
Tseng 2000
)
. With the notation of proximal operator
\operatorname{prox}_{h}(\cdot)
(see (
2.1
) for its definition), PG iteratively updates:

x_{k+1}=\operatorname{prox}_{\eta_{k}h}(x_{k}-\eta_{k}\nabla f(x_{k}))=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big\{\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x\rangle+\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}+h(x)\Big\},

(PG)

where
\eta_{k}>0
is a step size and
\nabla f(x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the gradient of
f
at
x_{k}
(or a subgradient if
f
is non-differentiable).
When
f
is
L
-smooth, PG with
\eta_{k}=1/L
achieves
O(1/t)
stationary convergence rate for nonconvex
f

(
Ghadimi et al. 2016
)
, and a rate of
O(1/t)
on the objective gap for convex
f
; accelerated variants
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
;
Nesterov 1983
;
Nesterov 2013
)
improve the convex rate to the optimal
O(1/t^{2})
.
When
f
is convex and
G
-Lipschitz, PG with
\eta_{k}=O(1/\sqrt{k})
achieves the optimal
O(1/\sqrt{t})
rate
(
Nesterov 2003
)
. Nevertheless, these results reveal a central drawback of PG: the step size must be tuned to the function class and relevant parameters (e.g., the smoothness constant
L
), which could be impractical since such information is often lacking in real applications.

A principled remedy is to use adaptive step sizes that automatically adjust based on information accumulated during the implementation, without requiring prior knowledge of the problem class or relevant parameters. AdaGrad
(
Duchi et al. 2011
)
pioneers this approach by setting
\eta_{k}=O\Big(1/\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|\nabla f(x_{i})\|^{2}}\Big)
, and has given rise to a rich family of adaptive methods
(
Kingma and Ba 2015
;
Reddi et al. 2018
;
Tieleman and Hinton 2012
;
Zeiler 2012
)
. The key mechanism is that the denominator implicitly tracks the cumulative gradient magnitude: for smooth objectives where
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
diminishes, the denominator grows slowly and the step size stays large; for nonsmooth objectives where
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
remains bounded away from zero, the step size decreases as
O(1/\sqrt{k})
. In the non-composite setting (
h=0
), AdaGrad-type step sizes have been rigorously analyzed for both convex
(
Ene et al. 2021
;
Kavis et al. 2019
;
Levy 2017
;
Levy et al. 2018
)
and nonconvex objectives
(
Attia and Koren 2023
;
Kavis et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023a
;
Ward et al. 2020
)
, and are shown to automatically match the convergence rates of gradient methods in each setting without prior knowledge of the problem class or smoothness constants.

While adaptive step sizes have been well-studied for non-composite problems, their extension to the composite setting (
1.1
) is nontrivial and remains largely unexplored, especially for nonconvex objectives. A fundamental difficulty is that gradient norms are no longer appropriate progress measures in the composite setting since the gradient
\nabla f(x^{*})
at a stationary point
x^{*}
of (
1.1
) is generally nonzero. Consequently, accumulating
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}
in an AdaGrad manner would drive the step sizes to zero even for smooth objectives, which is inconsistent with the standard constant step size on the order of
1/L
. This motivates the following question:

Can we design a universal (in the sense of
Nesterov 2015
) adaptive proximal gradient method for solving (
1.1
) that automatically achieves convergence rates matching those of PG for
f
being nonconvex smooth, convex smooth, or convex nonsmooth, without any prior knowledge of the problem-related parameters?

1.1
Our Contributions

We answer the above challenge affirmatively by proposing and studying Algorithm
1
. The key design insight is to replace the accumulated squared gradient norms as used in the AdaGrad denominator with accumulated squared norms of the
gradient mapping

\mathcal{G}_{k}
(see (
2.9
) for its definition), which vanishes at stationary points of (
1.1
). We show that Algorithm
1
is
universal
in the sense that it simultaneously applies to three different problem classes without any modification or prior knowledge of relevant parameters (e.g., smoothness constant, diameter
D
, or noise level
\sigma
), as summarized below (see also Table
1
for an overview).

•

For nonconvex and smooth
f
, we establish stationary convergence rate
O(1/t)
for Algorithm
1
in the deterministic setting. Additionally, in the stochastic setting with bounded variance
\sigma^{2}
and batch sizes
\{b_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, Algorithm
1
achieves the stationary convergence rate
\widetilde{O}\big(\frac{1}{t}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}\big)
with high probability, where
\widetilde{O}(\cdot)
hides logarithmic factors. To the best of our knowledge, this seems to be the first convergence analysis of AdaGrad-type method in a nonconvex composite setting, and the established rates match the best known results of PG in nonconvex settings
(
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
;
Ghadimi et al. 2016
)
up to logarithmic factors.

•

The same algorithm adapts to convex settings without requiring any modification. Specifically, when
f
is Lipschitz continuous, the averaged iterate of Algorithm
1
with unit batch size converges towards an optimal solution at a rate of
\widetilde{O}\big((1+\sigma)/\sqrt{t}\big)
. When
f
is smooth, it achieves an improved rate
\widetilde{O}\big(\frac{1}{t}+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{t}}\big)
. The corresponding deterministic rates follow directly by setting
\sigma=0
.

•

For convex
f
, we develop an accelerated variant (Algorithm
2
) that achieves better convergence rates when the diameter
D
is known. In particular, Algorithm
2
preserves a similar
\widetilde{O}\big((1+\sigma)/\sqrt{t}\big)
convergence rate as Algorithm
1
for Lipschitz continuous
f
, and achieves an accelerated rate of order
\widetilde{O}\big(\frac{1}{t^{2}}+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{t}}\big)
for smooth
f
. These rates are optimal in both regimes up to logarithmic factors
(
Lan 2012
)
.

A key technical challenge in establishing the results in stochastic settings is that the adaptive step sizes used in Algorithm
1
become random variables as opposed to predetermined ones, making it difficult to bound terms involving their products with other random variables (see Section
3.2
for details).
We overcome this difficulty by carefully estimating cumulative inner products between the stochastic noise and iterative differences (see Lemma
7
), which enables us to establish convergence with simplified analysis across different problem classes.
Another advantage of our analysis is that it removes the restrictive assumption that the stochastic gradients are uniformly bounded almost surely, which is frequently imposed in related literature (see, e.g.,
(
Kavis et al. 2019
;
Kavis et al. 2022
;
Levy 2017
;
Levy et al. 2018
;
Ward et al. 2020
)
).

1.2
Related Work

Proximal Gradient Methods.

The proximal gradient (PG) method in the form of (
PG
) traces back to the splitting algorithms of
Lions and Mercier 1979
and
Passty 1979
, and is surveyed comprehensively in
Parikh and Boyd 2014
. Depending on
h
, the proximal operator can admit a closed-form solution, yielding a variety of well-known special cases. For instance, when
h
is the indicator function of a convex closed set, (
PG
) reduces to the projected gradient method
(
Levitin and Polyak 1966
)
; when
h
is an
\ell_{1}
regularizer, it becomes the iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm (ISTA), along with its accelerated variant FISTA
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
)
. For convex smooth
f
, the
O(1/t)
rate is classical and can be accelerated to the optimal
O(1/t^{2})
via Nesterov-type momentum
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
;
Nesterov 1983
;
Nesterov 2013
)
. For convex nonsmooth
f
, PG achieves the optimal
O(1/\sqrt{t})
rate
(
Nesterov 2003
)
. In the stochastic setting,
Lan 2012
establishes optimal rates for stochastic convex composite objectives. These results are later extended to nonconvex cases by
Ghadimi et al. 2016
and
Ghadimi and Lan 2016
. Nesterov’s universal gradient method
(
Nesterov 2015
)
achieves near-optimal rates for both smooth and nonsmooth convex objectives without prior knowledge of the smoothness constant, providing an important precursor to adaptive approaches. Nevertheless, this method employs a line search at every iteration, which is unsuitable for handling noisy gradients in the stochastic setting.

Adaptive Gradient Methods.

AdaGrad
(
Duchi et al. 2011
)
introduced per-coordinate adaptive step sizes by accumulating squared gradient norms, with popular practical variants including Adam
(
Kingma and Ba 2015
)
and AMSGrad
(
Reddi et al. 2018
)
. In the non-composite setting (
h=0
), AdaGrad achieves a rate of
O(1/\sqrt{t})
for convex nonsmooth objectives and
O(\log t/t)
for convex smooth objectives
(
Duchi et al. 2011
)
. Building on this,
Ene et al. 2021
;
Kavis et al. 2019
;
Levy 2017
;
Levy et al. 2018
develop universal accelerated methods that achieve near-optimal rates simultaneously for smooth and nonsmooth convex objectives. For nonconvex objectives,
Kavis et al. 2022
;
Ward et al. 2020
provide sharp high-probability convergence guarantees for AdaGrad under an additional bounded-gradient assumption.
Attia and Koren 2023
;
Faw et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023a
provide refined analyses under a relaxed affine-noise variance assumption and completely remove the bounded-gradient assumption.
Adapting these methods to the composite setting (
1.1
) is less studied and has mainly focused on the convex regime. While the original AdaGrad
(
Duchi et al. 2011
)
already handles the composite structure, it may converge slowly, as it still accumulates squared gradient norms in the denominator. This issue is later addressed by
Joulani et al. 2020
and
Rodomanov et al. 2024
, which accumulate norms of gradient differences instead. Nevertheless, these works are still restricted to the convex regime and require knowledge of the diameter. For nonconvex composite problems, we are only aware of
Yun et al. 2021
, which proposes a general adaptive PG framework. However, they only provide rough convergence analysis for non-increasing step sizes, which requires prior knowledge of the smoothness constant, making the method not fully adaptive.
A separate line of adaptivity, pioneered by
Malitsky and Mishchenko 2020
, uses step sizes based on local gradient variation, achieving adaptivity to local smoothness without line search. This approach is later extended to the composite setting
(
Latafat et al. 2024
;
Latafat et al. 2025
;
Malitsky and Mishchenko 2024
)
.

Problem Setup

In this section, we summarize some notations and definitions, present auxiliary lemmas, and introduce the main algorithm.

2.1
Notations and Definitions

For a vector
v:=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\|v\|
denote its Euclidean norm, i.e.,
\|v\|:=\big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}^{2}\big)^{1/2}
. For a positive integer
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, define
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For
a\in\mathbb{R}
, let
[a]_{+}:=\max\{0,a\}
be its positive part. We also denote by
\log b
the natural logarithm of
b>0
, i.e., the logarithm with base
e
.

Let (
\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathrm{P})
be the underlying probability space. We denote by
\left\{x_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
the sequence of iterates generated by a stochastic algorithm, driven by a sequence of independent noise variables
\left\{\xi_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 1}
. Let
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 0}
be the natural filtration of this process, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\sigma\left(x_{0},\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{k}\right)
represents the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
x_{0}
and
\left\{\xi_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{k}
. We denote the conditional expectation given
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
by
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]
, which represents the expectation given all information available at step
k
.
Below we recall the definitions of convexity, Lipschitz continuity, and smoothness.

Definition 1

(Convexity, Lipschitz continuity, and Smoothness)
.

Let the function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be continuously differentiable. Let
\mu\geq 0,G>0
, and
L>0
.

1).

We say that
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex if

f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^{2},~\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

When the above inequality holds with
\mu=0
, we say that
f
is convex.

2).

We say that
f
is
G
-Lipschitz continuous if

|f(y)-f(x)|\leq G\|y-x\|,~\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

3).

We say that
f
is
L
-smooth if its gradient is
L
-Lipschitz continuous, i.e.,

\|\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)\|\leq L\|y-x\|,~\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

A useful property of an
L
-smooth function
f
is that

f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^{2},~\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For a proper, lower-semicontinuous, and convex function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, we denote by
\dom(h):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:h(x)<+\infty\}
its (effective) domain and by
\operatorname{prox}_{h}(\cdot)
its proximal operator defined as

\operatorname{prox}_{h}(x):=\underset{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\argmin}~\left\{h(z)+\frac{1}{2}\|z-x\|^{2}\right\},~\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.1)

The proximal operator
\operatorname{prox}_{h}(\cdot)
shares the property of nonexpansiveness:

\|\operatorname{prox}_{h}(x)-\operatorname{prox}_{h}(y)\|\leq\|x-y\|,~\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.2)

Additionally, we denote by
\partial h(x)
the set of all subgradients of
h
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,

\partial h(x):=\big\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:h(y)-h(x)-\langle u,y-x\rangle\geq 0,\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\big\}.

Throughout the paper, we always assume that the objective function
F
in (
1.1
) is bounded from below, and we denote
F^{*}:=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(x)
. If
F
is convex, we also assume that it attains a global minimizer, which we denote by
x^{*}\in\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(x)
and hence
F^{*}=F(x^{*})
. Consequently, it is natural to measure the suboptimality of a point
x
by the objective gap
F(x)-F^{*}
in the convex setting. When
f
is differentiable but nonconvex, the first-order optimality condition of (
1.1
) implies that any stationary point
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
should satisfy
\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x^{*})=0
for any
\eta>0
, where
\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(\cdot)
is known as the gradient mapping defined by

\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x):=\frac{1}{\eta}\big(x-\operatorname{prox}_{\eta h}(x-\eta\nabla f(x))\big).

(2.3)

Similar to existing works on nonconvex composite optimization (e.g.,
(
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
;
Ghadimi et al. 2016
)
), we measure the suboptimality using the norm of the gradient mapping.

2.2
Auxiliary Lemmas

Next, we introduce the following three elementary lemmas, which solve inequalities in specific formats.

Lemma 1
.

Let
a,b\geq 0
. If
x^{2}\leq ax+b
, then
x^{2}\leq a^{2}+2b
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
a,x>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
. If
x\leq a\log x+b
, then
x\leq 2a\log 2a-2a+2b.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

Applying the basic inequality
\log u\leq u-1
with
u=x/(2a)
gives

\log x\leq\frac{x}{2a}+\log 2a-1,

which together with
x\leq a\log x+b
implies that

x\leq\frac{x}{2}+a\log 2a-a+b.

We obtain the desired result after rearranging the terms.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
a,x>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
. If
x\leq a\sqrt{\log x}+b
, then
x\leq 2a\sqrt{\log(a^{2}+1)}+2b+1
.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

Plugging
u=x/a^{2}
in
\log u\leq u-1
gives

\log x\leq\frac{x}{a^{2}}+\log a^{2}-1\leq\frac{x}{a^{2}}+\log(a^{2}+1),

applying which on
x\leq a\sqrt{\log x}+b
and noting that
\sqrt{A+B}\leq\sqrt{A}+\sqrt{B}
for any
A,B>0
further implies

x\leq a\sqrt{\frac{x}{a^{2}}+\log(a^{2}+1)}+b\leq\sqrt{x}+a\sqrt{\log(a^{2}+1)}+b.

Solving the above inequality with Lemma
1
gives the desired result.
∎

The next lemma provides lower and upper bounds for specific forms of summations, which shall be used frequently in our analysis.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\{a_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be a nonnegative sequence and define
A_{k}=A_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}
with
A_{0}>0
.
Suppose there exists
C>0
such that
a_{k}\leq CA_{k-1}
for all
k\geq 1
. Then the following inequalities hold for all
t\geq 1
:

2(\sqrt{A_{t}}-\sqrt{A_{0}})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{a_{k}}{\sqrt{A_{k-1}}}\leq(1+\sqrt{1+C})(\sqrt{A_{t}}-\sqrt{A_{0}}).

(2.4)

\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k-1}}\leq(1+C)\log\frac{A_{t}}{A_{0}}.

(2.5)

Proof of Lemma
4
.

Firstly, we know from
A_{0}>0
that the two summations in (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) are well-defined.
Using the identity
a_{k}=A_{k}-A_{k-1}=(\sqrt{A_{k}}+\sqrt{A_{k-1}})(\sqrt{A_{k}}-\sqrt{A_{k-1}})
and
\sqrt{A_{k}}\geq\sqrt{A_{k-1}}
, we obtain

\frac{a_{k}}{\sqrt{A_{k-1}}}=\Big(1+\frac{\sqrt{A_{k}}}{\sqrt{A_{k-1}}}\Big)(\sqrt{A_{k}}-\sqrt{A_{k-1}})\geq 2(\sqrt{A_{k}}-\sqrt{A_{k-1}}).

(2.6)

On the other hand, under the assumption
a_{k}\leq CA_{k-1}
, we have

\frac{\sqrt{A_{k}}}{\sqrt{A_{k-1}}}=\sqrt{1+\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k-1}}}\leq\sqrt{1+C},

which implies that

\frac{a_{k}}{\sqrt{A_{k-1}}}\leq(1+\sqrt{1+C})(\sqrt{A_{k}}-\sqrt{A_{k-1}}).

(2.7)

Taking summations for both (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) over
k=1,\dots,t
gives (
2.4
).

We proceed to prove (
2.5
). Firstly, it follows from
a_{k}\leq CA_{k-1}
that

A_{k}=A_{k-1}+a_{k}\leq(1+C)A_{k-1},

which implies that

\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k-1}}\leq(1+C)\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k}}

(2.8)

Since the function
\log x
is concave, we know that

\log A_{k}-\log A_{k-1}\geq\log^{\prime}(A_{k})(A_{k}-A_{k-1})=\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k}}.

Applying the above inequality on (
2.8
) and summing over
k=1,\dots,t
leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k-1}}\leq

\displaystyle(1+C)\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k}}\leq(1+C)\sum_{k=1}^{t}\bigl(\log A_{k}-\log A_{k-1}\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+C)(\log A_{t}-\log A_{0})=(1+C)\log\frac{A_{t}}{A_{0}}.

The proof is complete.
∎

2.3
Algorithm Design

Recall that AdaGrad
(
Duchi et al. 2011
)
employs the adaptive step size
O\big(1/\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|\nabla f(x_{i})\|^{2}}\big)
. The motivation is that
\nabla f(x_{k})
is the stationarity measure when
h=0
since one has
\nabla f(x^{*})=0
for any stationary point
x^{*}
. Consequently, for smooth
f
, the accumulated norms
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|\nabla f(x_{i})\|^{2}
grow slowly as
\nabla f(x_{k})
diminishes, making the step size approximately constant. For nonsmooth
f
, the gradients stay bounded away from zero, leading to decreasing step sizes
O(1/\sqrt{k})
.

However, this strategy does not directly extend to the composite setting. The reason is that the gradient
\nabla f(x^{*})
at a stationary point
x^{*}
of (
1.1
) is generally nonzero since the first-order optimality condition merely requires
-\nabla f(x^{*})\in\partial h(x^{*})
. Hence, accumulating
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}
would drive the step size to zero even for smooth
f
, which is undesirable. The appropriate stationarity measure in the composite setting should be the gradient mapping
\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(\cdot)
(
2.3
), which reduces to
\nabla f
if
h=0
and satisfies
\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x^{*})=0
at any stationary point
x^{*}
of (
1.1
).
Moreover, the update rules in the form of (
PG
) provide direct access to the gradient mapping
\mathcal{G}_{k}:=\mathcal{G}_{\eta_{k}}(x_{k})
due to

\mathcal{G}_{k}=\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\big(x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\eta_{k}h}(x_{k}-\eta_{k}\nabla f(x_{k}))\big)=\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}(x_{k}-x_{k+1}).

(2.9)

This motivates us to accumulate
\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}
in the denominator of step sizes, which yields the following adaptive scheme at iteration
k
:

\eta_{k}=\frac{\eta}{S_{k}},\quad S_{k}^{2}=\gamma^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\|\mathcal{G}_{j}\|^{2},

(2.10)

where
\gamma,\eta>0
are hyperparameters. Different from the standard AdaGrad, we accumulate up to index
k-1
instead of
k
because
\mathcal{G}_{k}=(x_{k}-x_{k+1})/\eta_{k}
is not available until the
k
-th iteration is performed. Note that the step size (
2.10
) is different from the ones in
Joulani et al. 2020
and
Rodomanov et al. 2024
, which accumulate squared norms of gradient differences to handle the composite structure.

Applying (
2.10
), we obtain the adaptive proximal gradient method for solving (
1.1
), as summarized in Algorithm
1
. Note that the update of
S_{k+1}^{2}
in Algorithm
1
is equivalent to (
2.10
) in the deterministic setting because

S^{2}_{k+1}=S^{2}_{k}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}\Big)=S^{2}_{k}+\frac{1}{\eta_{k}^{2}}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}=S^{2}_{k}+\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}.

For stochastic settings, we define the stochastic gradient mapping as

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}:=\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\big(x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\eta_{k}h}(x_{k}-\eta_{k}g_{k})\big)=\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}(x_{k}-x_{k+1}).

(2.11)

Then we similarly have

S_{k}^{2}=\gamma^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{j}\|^{2},

(2.12)

Algorithm 1

Adaptive Proximal Gradient Method for Solving (
1.1
)

0:

initial point
x_{1}\in\dom(h)
; hyperparameters
\gamma,\eta>0
; batch sizes
\{b_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
(only needed for stochastic settings).

Initialize
S_{1}:=\gamma
.

for

k=1,2,\dots

do

if

\nabla f
is accessible
then

Compute full gradient
g_{k}:=\nabla f(x_{k})
.

else

Sample
\{\xi_{k_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{b_{k}}\subset\mathcal{D}
uniformly at random.

Compute gradient approximation
g_{k}:=\frac{1}{b_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{b_{k}}\nabla\ell(x_{k};\xi_{k_{j}})
.

end

if

Generate the next iterate
x_{k+1}
via

x_{k+1}:=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big\{\langle g_{k},x\rangle+\frac{S_{k}}{2\eta}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}+h(x)\Big\}.

Update
S^{2}_{k+1}=S^{2}_{k}\big(1+\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}/\eta^{2}\big)
.

end

for

To conduct convergence analysis on Algorithm
1
, we make the following boundedness assumption on the iterates
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
. Similar boundedness assumptions are widely adopted for analysis of AdaGrad-type methods in the literature (see, e.g.,
(
Duchi et al. 2011
;
Ene et al. 2021
;
Joulani et al. 2020
;
Kavis et al. 2019
)
). In the context of the composite problem (
1.1
), it can be satisfied if, e.g.,
\dom(h)
is bounded or the operator
\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\{\langle u,x\rangle+h(x)\}
is bounded for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(
Ghadimi and Lan 2016
, Lemma 2)
.

Assumption 1
.

There exists a constant
D>0
such that
\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|\leq D
for all
k\geq 1
. Moreover, if
F
is convex, we also assume that
\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|\leq D
for all
k\geq 1
.

Note that while Assumption
1
is used in the analysis, knowledge of the diameter
D
is not required for implementing Algorithm
1
.

3
Convergence Analysis in the Nonconvex Setting

In this section, we study the convergence of Algorithm
1
when
f
is
L
-smooth but possibly nonconvex. We first analyze the simpler deterministic case in Section
3.1
and then move on to the stochastic setting in Section
3.2
.

Before proceeding, we first introduce the following classical descent lemma for PG-type updates, which serves as a starting point for our analysis in this section. Its proof can be easily found in related literature (see, e.g.,
(
Parikh and Boyd 2014
)
). We provide a proof here for completeness.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose
f
is
L
-smooth. Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be the sequence generated by the iterative scheme (
PG
). Then for any
k\geq 1
, it holds that

F(x_{k+1})\leq F(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k})-g_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle-\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{L}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

From the optimality condition of (
PG
), we have

0\in g_{k}+\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})+\partial h(x_{k+1}),

which, together with the convexity of
h
, implies that

h(x)\geq h(x_{k+1})+\Big\langle g_{k}+\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}(x_{k+1}-x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x\Big\rangle,~\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(3.1)

Setting
x=x_{k}
in (
3.1
) and rearranging the terms gives

h(x_{k+1})\leq h(x_{k})-\langle g_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle-\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

(3.2)

On the other hand, it follows from the
L
-smoothness of
f
that

f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

(3.3)

Combining (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) leads to the desired result, hence completes the proof.
∎

3.1
Deterministic Setting

We first consider the deterministic setting where we use full gradient
g_{k}:=\nabla f(x_{k})
in Algorithm
1
.
When
f
is possibly nonconvex, the objective function
F
in (
1.1
) may also be nonconvex. Finding a minimizer of
F
is therefore difficult. Fortunately, we can show that the objective values at the iterates produced by Algorithm
1
are uniformly upper bounded, as indicated in the following lemma.
For notational convenience, we denote
\Delta_{t}:=F(x_{t})-F^{*}
.

Lemma 6
.

Suppose
f
is
L
-smooth and Assumption
1
holds. Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be the iterates generated by Algorithm
1
with full gradient
g_{k}:=\nabla f(x_{k})
and any
\gamma,\eta>0
. Then for any
t\geq 1
, we have

\Delta_{t}\leq M:=\Delta_{1}+\Big[L(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\log\frac{L\eta}{2\gamma}+\frac{LD^{2}}{2}\Big]_{+}.

Proof of Lemma
6
.

Firstly, since
g_{k}=\nabla f(x_{k})
and
\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|=\eta_{k}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|
, we obtain from Lemma
5
that

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq F(x_{k})-\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{L}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=F(x_{k})-\eta_{k}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{L\eta_{k}^{2}}{2}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}.

(3.4)

To proceed, summing (
3.4
) over
k=1,\ldots,t
and subtracting both sides by
F^{*}
yields

\Delta_{t+1}\leq\Delta_{1}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Big(\frac{L}{2}-\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\Big)\eta_{k}^{2}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}.

(3.5)

We consider two cases. Firstly, if
S_{1}=\gamma>\frac{L\eta}{2}
, then it follows that

\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}=\frac{S_{k}}{\eta}\geq\frac{S_{1}}{\eta}>\frac{L}{2},~\forall k\geq 1,

which implies that
\Delta_{t+1}\leq\Delta_{1}\leq M
for all
t\geq 1
. Otherwise if
\gamma\leq\frac{L\eta}{2}
, then
t^{*}:=\max\{k\in[t]:L/2\geq 1/\eta_{k}\}
is well-defined and hence
S_{t^{*}}\leq\frac{L\eta}{2}
.
For
k>t^{*}
, we know from the definition of
t^{*}
that
L/2\leq 1/\eta_{k}
. Therefore, it follows from (
3.5
) that

\Delta_{t+1}\leq\Delta_{1}+\sum_{k=1}^{t^{*}}\eta_{k}^{2}\Big(\frac{L}{2}-\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\Big)\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}\leq\Delta_{1}+\frac{L}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t^{*}}\eta_{k}^{2}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}=\Delta_{1}+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t^{*}}\frac{\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}.

(3.6)

From Assumption
1
, we know that

\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}=\frac{\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}}{\eta_{k}^{2}}\leq\frac{D^{2}}{\eta_{k}^{2}}=\frac{D^{2}}{\eta^{2}}S_{k}^{2},~\forall k\geq 1.

Applying (
2.5
) in Lemma
4
with
a_{k}:=\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}
,
A_{k}:=S_{k+1}^{2}
, and
C:=D^{2}/\eta^{2}
, we derive from (
3.6
) that

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}\leq

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{t^{*}-1}\frac{\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}+\frac{\|\mathcal{G}_{t^{*}}\|^{2}}{S_{t^{*}}^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\Big(1+\frac{D^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\Big)\log\frac{S_{t^{*}}^{2}}{S_{1}^{2}}+\frac{LD^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}+L(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\log\frac{L\eta}{2\gamma}+\frac{LD^{2}}{2},

where the last inequality holds from
S_{t^{*}}\leq\frac{L\eta}{2}
and
S_{1}=\gamma
.
The proof is thus complete.
∎

Using Lemma
6
, we can establish
O(1/t)
stationary convergence rates for Algorithm
1
, as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the same setting as in Lemma
6
. Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be the iterates generated by Algorithm
1
with full gradient
g_{k}:=\nabla f(x_{k})
and any
\gamma,\eta>0
. Then for any
t\geq 1
, we have

\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{t}\Big[\Big(\frac{M}{\eta}+L\eta+\frac{LD}{2}\Big)^{2}+\frac{2M\gamma}{\eta}\Big].

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We start by rearranging (
3.4
) to obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}(F(x_{k})-F(x_{k+1}))+\frac{L}{2}\eta_{k}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}(\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1})+\frac{L}{2}\eta_{k}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}.

(3.7)

Summing (
3.7
) over
k=1,\ldots,t
leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\eta_{1}}+\sum_{k=2}^{t}\Big(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{k-1}}\Big)\Delta_{k}-\frac{\Delta_{t+1}}{\eta_{t}}+\frac{L}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{\eta_{1}}+M\sum_{k=2}^{t}\Big(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{k-1}}\Big)+\frac{L}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{\eta_{t}}+\frac{L}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}=\frac{M}{\eta}S_{t}+\frac{L\eta}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}},

(3.8)

where we used
\Delta_{k}\leq M
and
\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{k-1}}\geq 0
since
\eta_{k}
is non-increasing.
Applying (
2.4
) in Lemma
4
with
a_{k}:=\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}
,
A_{k}:=S_{k+1}^{2}
, and
C:=D^{2}/\eta^{2}
, we further derive from (
3.8
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{\eta}\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}}+\frac{L\eta}{2}\Big(1+\sqrt{1+D^{2}/\eta^{2}}\Big)\Bigg(\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}}-\gamma\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M\gamma}{\eta}+\frac{M}{\eta}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}}+\frac{L\eta}{2}\big(2+D/\eta\big)\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{M\gamma}{\eta}+\Big(\frac{M}{\eta}+L\eta+\frac{LD}{2}\Big)\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}},

(3.9)

where we used the basic inequality
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
to obtain the second inequality. Solving (
3.9
) with Lemma
1
gives

\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}\leq\Big(\frac{M}{\eta}+L\eta+\frac{LD}{2}\Big)^{2}+\frac{2M\gamma}{\eta},

which completes the proof after dividing by
t
.
∎

3.2
Stochastic Setting

In this subsection, we proceed to investigate the convergence of Algorithm
1
when only stochastic gradients are accessible at each iteration, i.e.,
g_{k}:=\frac{1}{b_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{b_{k}}\nabla\ell(x_{k};\xi_{k_{j}})
for some batch size
b_{k}\geq 1
.
To this end, we make the following standard bounded-variance assumption on the stochastic gradient
\nabla\ell(x_{k};\xi)
.

Assumption 2
.

The stochastic gradient
\nabla\ell(x_{k};\xi)
is unbiased, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\nabla\ell(x_{k};\xi)]=\nabla f(x_{k})
for all
k\geq 1
, where the expectation is taken w.r.t. the randomness of
\xi
. Moreover, there exists
\sigma>0
such that

\mathbb{E}_{k}[\|\nabla\ell(x_{k};\xi)-\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}.

For notational convenience, we denote the stochastic noises as

\delta_{k,j}:=\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla\ell(x_{k};\xi_{k_{j}}),\quad\delta_{k}:=\frac{1}{b_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{b_{k}}\delta_{k,j}=\nabla f(x_{k})-g_{k}.

Then it follows from the independence among the batch samples
\{\xi_{k_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{b_{k}}
and Assumption
2
that

\mathbb{E}_{k}\bigg[\bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{b_{k}}\delta_{k,j}\bigg\|^{2}\bigg]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{b_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{k}\big[\langle\delta_{k,i},\delta_{k,j}\rangle\big]=\sum_{j=1}^{b_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{k}\big[\|\delta_{k,j}\|^{2}\big]\leq\sigma^{2}b_{k},

where to obtain the second equality, we used
\mathbb{E}_{k}\big[\langle\delta_{k,i},\delta_{k,j}\rangle\big]=\big\langle\mathbb{E}_{k}\big[\delta_{k,i}\big],\mathbb{E}_{k}\big[\delta_{k,j}\big]\big\rangle=0
for
i\neq j
due to the independence between
\delta_{k,i}
and
\delta_{k,j}
.
This further implies that

\mathbb{E}_{k}\big[\|\delta_{k}\|^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}_{k}\bigg[\bigg\|\frac{1}{b_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{b_{k}}\delta_{k,j}\bigg\|^{2}\bigg]=\frac{1}{b_{k}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{k}\bigg[\bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{b_{k}}\delta_{k,j}\bigg\|^{2}\bigg]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b_{k}}.

(3.10)

Note that the analysis of Algorithm
1
in such a stochastic setting is more challenging compared to the deterministic case, because the adaptive step sizes
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
become random variables. A direct consequence is that our previous analysis relying on bounding objective gaps is not directly applicable here. To see this, while we may still show that the objective gaps
\{\Delta_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
are uniformly bounded in expectation, it is hard to bound

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=2}^{t}\Big(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{k-1}}\Big)\Delta_{k}\bigg]

due to the coupling between the two random variables
\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{k-1}}
and
\Delta_{k}
, which is crucial for establishing Theorem
1
. This issue regarding randomness in
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
is also identified as the main bottleneck for nonconvex analysis of AdaGrad in non-composite settings
(
Attia and Koren 2023
;
Faw et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023a
;
Ward et al. 2020
)
. Most of these works handle it by introducing surrogates of step sizes that are measurable given all historical iterates, but result in highly involved analyses.
Besides, the composite structure of (
1.1
) introduces additional challenges for the analysis because in this case

\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle=-\eta_{k}\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\rangle\neq-\eta_{k}\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),g_{k}\rangle,

which makes existing nonconvex analysis for non-composite settings inapplicable.

To overcome this difficulty, we develop a new approach by carefully estimating the sum of inner products between the stochastic noises and iterative differences, which allows us to bound
\mathbb{E}[S_{t}]
without introducing new surrogates, leading to substantially simplified analysis. We present this technical ingredient in the following lemma. Note that it does not rely on any conditions on
f
and hence is applicable across different settings of
f
, as we shall demonstrate later.

Lemma 7
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be the iterates generated by Algorithm
1
with batch sizes
\{b_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
and any
\gamma,\eta>0
. Then for any
t\geq 1
, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle\Big]\leq\sigma\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]},

Proof of Lemma
7
.

Firstly, note from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}
(
2.11
) that

\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\delta_{k}\|\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|=\eta\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\delta_{k}\|\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|}{S_{k}}.

Taking expectations on both sides of the above inequality and applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for expectations
|\mathbb{E}[XY]|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]}
, we further obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle\Big]\leq

\displaystyle\eta\sum_{k=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\delta_{k}\|\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|}{S_{k}}\Big]\leq\eta\sum_{k=1}^{t}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|\delta_{k}\|^{2}]\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}\Big]}

(3.11)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sigma\eta\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{b_{k}}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}\Big]}\leq\sigma\eta\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}\Big]},

where we used (
3.10
) to obtain the third inequality and the last one follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Applying (
2.5
) in Lemma
4
with
a_{k}:=\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}
,
A_{k}:=S_{k+1}^{2}
, and
C:=D^{2}/\eta^{2}
to bound
\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}
, we derive from (
3.11
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle\Big]\leq

\displaystyle\sigma\eta\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\sqrt{\Big(1+\frac{D^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\Big)\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\frac{S_{t+1}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\Big]}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sigma\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]},

where the last inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality applied to the concave function
\log(\cdot)
. The proof is now complete.
∎

Leveraging Lemma
7
, we can establish high-probability convergence results to stationary points for Algorithm
1
, as summarized in the upcoming theorem. For ease of presentation, we use
\lesssim
to hide constants depending only on fixed quantities (e.g.,
L,\gamma,\eta,D
, and
\Delta_{1}
) hereafter, i.e.,
A\lesssim B
if there exists a constant
c>0
such that
A\leq cB
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
f
is
L
-smooth and Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be the iterates generated by Algorithm
1
with batch sizes
\{b_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
and any
\gamma,\eta>0
. Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Then for every
t\geq 1
, with probability at least
1-\epsilon
, we have

\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{t\epsilon^{2}}\Bigg[\Bigg(1+\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}\Bigg)\Bigg(1+\log^{2}\Bigg(1+\sigma\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\Bigg)\Bigg)\Bigg].

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We start by applying Lemma
5
and
\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|=\eta_{k}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|
to obtain

F(x_{k+1})\leq F(x_{k})+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle-\eta_{k}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{L\eta_{k}^{2}}{2}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}.

(3.12)

Subtracting both sides by
F^{*}
in (
3.12
) and rearranging the terms, we have

\eta_{k}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}\leq\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1}+\frac{L\eta_{k}^{2}}{2}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle.

(3.13)

Summing (
3.13
) over
k=1,\ldots,t
yields

\displaystyle\eta\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}}\leq

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{t+1}+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}+\frac{L}{2}(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\log\frac{S_{t+1}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle,

(3.14)

where the second inequality follows by applying (
2.5
) in Lemma
4
with
a_{k}:=\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}
,
A_{k}:=S_{k+1}^{2}
, and
C:=D^{2}/\eta^{2}
.

Next, we bound
\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]
. Applying (
2.4
) in Lemma
4
on the LHS of (
3.14
) gives

2\eta(S_{t+1}-\gamma)\leq\eta\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}}\leq\Delta_{1}+L(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\log\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle.

(3.15)

Taking expectation on both sides of (
3.15
) and applying Lemma
7
, we derive after rearranging the terms that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\Delta_{1}}{2\gamma\eta}+\frac{L}{2\gamma\eta}(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\mathbb{E}\bigg[\log\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]+\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma\eta}\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]}

(3.16)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\Delta_{1}}{2\gamma\eta}+\frac{L}{2\gamma\eta}(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]+\frac{\sigma}{4\gamma\eta}\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\bigg(1+\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]\bigg)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\Delta_{1}}{2\gamma\eta}+\frac{\sigma}{4\gamma\eta}\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}+\Bigg(\frac{L}{2\gamma\eta}(\eta^{2}+D^{2})+\frac{\sigma}{4\gamma\eta}\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\Bigg)\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg],

where the second inequality follows from the concavity of
\log(\cdot)
and the basic inequality
\sqrt{x}\leq\frac{1}{2}(1+x)
.
Solving (
3.16
) via Lemma
2
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]\leq

\displaystyle\Bigg(\frac{L}{\gamma\eta}(\eta^{2}+D^{2})+\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma\eta}\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\Bigg)\log\Bigg(\frac{L}{\gamma\eta}(\eta^{2}+D^{2})+\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma\eta}\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle+2+\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\gamma\eta}-\frac{L}{\gamma\eta}(\eta^{2}+D^{2})

which leads to the following after omitting the constants
\{\gamma,\eta,D,L,\Delta_{1}\}
:

\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]\lesssim 1+\Bigg(1+\sigma\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\Bigg)\log\Bigg(1+\sigma\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\Bigg).

(3.17)

From the nonexpansiveness of the proximal operator (
2.2
), we have

\|\mathcal{G}_{k}-\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{\eta_{k}^{2}}\big\|\operatorname{prox}_{\eta_{k}h}(x_{k}-\eta_{k}g_{k})-\operatorname{prox}_{\eta_{k}h}\big(x_{k}-\eta_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\big)\big\|^{2}\leq\|g_{k}-\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}=\|\delta_{k}\|^{2}.

(3.18)

Therefore, we obtain from the basic inequalities
\|u+v\|^{2}\leq 2\|u\|^{2}+2\|v\|^{2}
and
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
, together with (
3.18
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}}\Bigg]\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sqrt{2\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}+2\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}-\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}\Bigg]\leq\sqrt{2}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}\Bigg]+\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sqrt{2\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\delta_{k}\|^{2}}\Bigg]

(3.19)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]+\sqrt{2\sum_{k=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\|\delta_{k}\|^{2}]}\leq\sqrt{2}\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]+\sigma\sqrt{2\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}},

where we used (
2.12
) and the concavity of
\sqrt{\cdot}
to derive the third inequality, and the last one follows from (
3.10
).

Finally, we establish the high-probability bound through Markov’s inequality
\mathbb{P}(X\geq a)\leq\mathbb{E}[X]/a
for any nonnegative random variable
X
and
a>0
, which is equivalent to

\mathbb{P}\big(X^{2}\leq a^{2}\big)\geq 1-\mathbb{E}[X]/a.

Given an
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, then it follows by setting
a:=\mathbb{E}[X]/\epsilon
in the above inequality that

\mathbb{P}\big(X^{2}\leq(\mathbb{E}[X])^{2}/\epsilon^{2}\big)\geq 1-\epsilon.

(3.20)

Let
X:=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}}
in (
3.20
), then it follows from (
3.19
) that with probability at least
1-\epsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{G}_{k}\|^{2}}\Big]\Big)^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\leq\frac{4\big(\mathbb{E}\big[S_{t+1}\big]\big)^{2}+4\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}{\epsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\Bigg[1+\log^{2}\Bigg(1+\sigma\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\Bigg)+\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}\Bigg(1+\log^{2}\Bigg(1+\sigma\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}}\Bigg)\Bigg)\Bigg],

where we used (
3.17
) and the basic inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
to obtain the last inequality. The proof is thus complete.
∎

Remark 1
.

With unit batch size
b_{k}\equiv 1
, the bound in Theorem
2
reduces to
\widetilde{O}\big(\frac{1}{t}+\sigma^{2}\big)
, which only ensures convergence to a neighborhood of a stationary point. This marks a key difference from AdaGrad analysis in the non-composite setting, where unit batch size suffices for convergence to a stationary point
(
Attia and Koren 2023
;
Faw et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023a
;
Ward et al. 2020
)
. Such behavior is not an artifact of our analysis, as it matches the behavior of PG in the stochastic nonconvex setting
(
Ghadimi et al. 2016
)
, whose convergence bound also contains a residual term proportional to
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{b_{k}}
. To ensure convergence to a stationary point, one may take increasing batch sizes, e.g.,
b_{k}=\Omega(\sigma\sqrt{k})
, which yields the rate
\widetilde{O}\big(\frac{1}{t}+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{t}}\big)
, matching the result of
Ghadimi et al. 2016
.

4
Convergence Analysis in Convex Setting

In this section, we study the convergence of Algorithm
1
under the condition that
f
is convex (possibly nonsmooth). We show in the upcoming two subsections that, for any choice of initial point and hyperparameters, Algorithm
1
achieves convergence rates matching those of PG for both Lipschitz continuous and smooth
f
, respectively.

For the case of Lipschitz continuous
f
, we do not require
f
to be differentiable. With slight abuse of notation, we use
\nabla f
to denote a subgradient of
f
. For brevity, we focus on stochastic settings, while results in the deterministic case follow directly by setting the variance
\sigma^{2}=0
. Moreover, we fix the batch size
b_{k}\equiv 1
throughout this section (i.e.,
g_{k}=\nabla\ell(x_{k};\xi_{k})
), and the results extend to general mini-batch versions through (
3.10
). Then it follows directly from Assumption
2
that

\mathbb{E}_{k}[g_{k}]=\nabla f(x_{k})\in\partial f(x_{k}),\quad\mathbb{E}_{k}[\|\delta_{k}\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{k}[\|g_{k}-\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}.

The following lemma provides descent properties for PG-type algorithms under the convexity of
f
, which serve as starting points for our analysis in the upcoming subsections. Recall that
\Delta_{k}:=F(x_{k})-F^{*}
.

Lemma 8
.

Suppose
f
in (
1.1
) is convex. Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be the sequence generated by the iterative scheme (
PG
). Then for any
k\geq 1
, we have the following results:

(i).

If
f
is
G
-Lipschitz continuous, then

\displaystyle\Delta_{k+1}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}\big(\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}\big)-\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+2G\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|

(4.1)

\displaystyle+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle.

(ii).

If
f
is
L
-smooth, then

\displaystyle\Delta_{k+1}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}\big(\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\Big(L-\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\Big)\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

(4.2)

\displaystyle+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle.

Proof of Lemma
8
.

We start by using the convexity of
f
to obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle=f(x_{k})-f(x^{*})+h(x_{k})-h(x^{*})

(4.3)

\displaystyle\leq\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle+h(x_{k})-h(x^{*})

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle+\langle g_{k},x_{k+1}-x^{*}\rangle+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x^{*}\rangle+h(x_{k})-h(x^{*}).

We first prove the Lipschitz continuous case (i). To this end, we bound the first term on the RHS of (
4.3
) via the convexity of
f
and
\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\|\leq G
as follows

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle+\langle\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k+1}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle

(4.4)

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})+\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k+1})\|\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})+2G\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|.

For the second term in (
4.3
), plugging
x=x^{*}
in (
3.1
) and rearranging the terms yields

\displaystyle\langle g_{k},x_{k+1}-x^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\langle x_{k}-x_{k+1},x_{k+1}-x^{*}\rangle+h(x^{*})-h(x_{k+1})

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}\big(\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}\big)+h(x^{*})-h(x_{k+1}),

where we used the identity
2\langle a-b,b-c\rangle=\|a-c\|^{2}-\|a-b\|^{2}-\|b-c\|^{2}
for the equality.
To bound the third term in (
4.3
), we decompose

\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x^{*}\rangle=\langle\delta_{k},x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle.

(4.6)

Applying (
4.4
), (
4.5
), and (
4.6
) on (
4.3
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}\big(\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}\big)-\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+2G\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|

\displaystyle+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle+\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1},

which simplifies to (
4.1
).

We proceed to prove (ii) for
L
-smooth
f
. In this case, we can obtain a tighter bound for the first term on the RHS of (
4.3
) using

\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle\leq f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})+\frac{L}{2}\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}.

(4.7)

Then (
4.2
) follows after plugging (
4.7
), (
4.5
), and (
4.6
) on (
4.3
). The proof is now complete.
∎

4.1
Lipschitz Continuous Case

We establish the following convergence result for the averaged iterate of Algorithm
1
when
f
is convex and
G
-Lipschitz continuous.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
f
is convex and
G
-Lipschitz continuous, and Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be the iterates generated by Algorithm
1
with
b_{k}\equiv 1
and any
\gamma,\eta>0
. Denote
\bar{x}_{t}:=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{t}x_{k+1}
. Then for any
t\geq 1
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{x}_{t})-F^{*}]\lesssim\frac{(1+\sigma)\sqrt{\log\big((1+\sigma^{2})t\big)}}{\sqrt{t}}.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

We first establish a bound for
\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]
. To this end, note from the
G
-Lipschitz continuity of
f
that

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})+G\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|,

\displaystyle-\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|\leq G\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|.

Summing up the above two inequalities gives

f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle+2G\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|.

(4.8)

Combining (
4.8
) and (
3.1
) with
x=x_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})=f(x_{k+1})+h(x_{k+1})

(4.9)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f(x_{k})+h(x_{k})-\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+2G\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F(x_{k})-\eta_{k}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}+2G\eta_{k}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle,

where we used
\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|=\eta_{k}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|
to obtain the last equality. Rearranging the terms in (
4.9
) and noting
\eta_{k}=\eta/S_{k}
gives

\eta\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}}\leq F(x_{k})-F(x_{k+1})+2G\eta\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|}{S_{k}}+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle.

(4.10)

Summing (
4.10
) over
k=1,\ldots,t
and applying (
2.4
) in Lemma
4
yields

\displaystyle 2\eta(S_{t+1}-\gamma)\leq\eta\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq F(x_{1})-F(x_{t+1})+2G\eta\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|}{S_{k}}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle

(4.11)

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{1}+2G\eta\sqrt{t}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle,

where the last inequality follows from
F(x_{t+1})\geq F^{*}
and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
Taking expectation on both sides of (
4.11
) and using the concavity of
\sqrt{\cdot}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\Delta_{1}}{2\gamma\eta}+\frac{G\sqrt{t}}{\gamma}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}}\Bigg]+\frac{1}{2\gamma\eta}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle\Big]

(4.12)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\Delta_{1}}{2\gamma\eta}+\frac{G\sqrt{t}}{\gamma}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}\bigg]}+\frac{1}{2\gamma\eta}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle\Big].

Applying (
2.5
) in Lemma
4
and Lemma
7
with
b_{k}=1
on (
4.12
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\Delta_{1}}{2\gamma\eta}+\frac{G\sqrt{t}}{\gamma}\sqrt{\Big(1+\frac{D^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\Big)\mathbb{E}\bigg[\log\frac{S_{t+1}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\bigg]}+\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma\eta}\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})t}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\Delta_{1}}{2\gamma\eta}+\frac{2G+\sigma}{2\gamma\eta}\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})t}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]},

where the last inequality follows from the concavity of
\log(\cdot)
.
Solving the above inequality via Lemma
3
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]\leq\frac{2G+\sigma}{\gamma\eta}\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})t}\sqrt{\log\bigg(\frac{(2G+\sigma)^{2}(\eta^{2}+D^{2})t}{2\gamma^{2}\eta^{2}}+1\bigg)}+3+\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\gamma\eta},

which leads to the following after omitting the constants
\{\gamma,\eta,D,G,\Delta_{1}\}
:

\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]\lesssim 1+(1+\sigma)\sqrt{t\log\big((1+\sigma^{2})t\big)}\lesssim(1+\sigma)\sqrt{t\log\big((1+\sigma^{2})t\big)}.

(4.13)

Next, we estimate
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Delta_{k+1}]
by using the bound on
\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]
. Firstly, by omitting the negative term in (
4.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{k+1}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}\big(\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}\big)+2G\eta\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|}{S_{k}}+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle+\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle.

(4.14)

Summing (
4.14
) over
k=1,\ldots,t
and noting that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}\big(\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}\big)

(4.15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|x_{1}-x^{*}\|^{2}}{2\eta_{1}}+\sum_{k=2}^{t}\Big(\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}-\frac{1}{2\eta_{k-1}}\Big)\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\frac{\|x_{t+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}}{2\eta_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta_{1}}+D^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{t}\Big(\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}-\frac{1}{2\eta_{k-1}}\Big)=\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta_{t}},

we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Delta_{k+1}\leq\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta}S_{t}+2G\eta\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|}{S_{k}}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle.

(4.16)

Taking expectation on both sides of (
4.16
) and noting that
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\langle\delta_{k},x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle]=0,~\forall k\geq 1
due to unbiasedness of
g_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Delta_{k+1}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta}\mathbb{E}[S_{t}]+2G\eta\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|}{S_{k}}\bigg]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle\Big]

(4.17)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta}\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]+2G\eta\sqrt{t}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}}\bigg]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta}\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]+2G\eta\sqrt{t}\sqrt{\Big(1+\frac{D^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\Big)\mathbb{E}\bigg[\log\frac{S_{t+1}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\bigg]}+\sigma\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})t}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta}\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]+(2G+\sigma)\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})t}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]},

where the second inequality holds from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and concavity of
\sqrt{\cdot}
, the third one follows from (
2.5
) in Lemma
4
and Lemma
7
with
b_{k}\equiv 1
, and the last one holds due to concavity of
\log(\cdot)
.
Finally, applying (
4.13
) on (
4.17
) and hiding fixed constants, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Delta_{k+1}\Big]\lesssim

\displaystyle(1+\sigma)\sqrt{t\log\big((1+\sigma^{2})t\big)}+(1+\sigma)\sqrt{t\log\Big((1+\sigma)\sqrt{t\log\big((1+\sigma^{2})t\big)}\Big)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(1+\sigma)\sqrt{t\log\big((1+\sigma^{2})t\big)},

where the last inequality follows due to that
\log\big((1+\sigma^{2})t\big)\leq(1+\sigma^{2})t-1\leq(1+\sigma)^{2}t
.
This together with the convexity of
F
implies that

\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{x}_{t})-F^{*}]\leq\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Delta_{k+1}\Big]\lesssim\frac{(1+\sigma)\sqrt{\log\big((1+\sigma^{2})t\big)}}{\sqrt{t}}.

The proof is now complete.
∎

By setting
\sigma=0
in the analysis of Theorem
3
, we can derive a convergence rate of order

in the deterministic setting, as summarized in the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose
f
is convex and
G
-Lipschitz continuous, and Assumption
1
hold. Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be the iterates generated by Algorithm
1
with full gradient
g_{k}=\nabla f(x_{k})
and any
\gamma,\eta>0
. Denote
\bar{x}_{t}:=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{t}x_{k+1}
. Then for any
t\geq 1
, it holds that

F(\bar{x}_{t})-F^{*}\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\log t}}{\sqrt{t}}.

In this subsection, we consider the case where

.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
f
is convex and
L
-smooth, and Assumptions
1
and
2

\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be the iterates generated by Algorithm

\gamma,\eta>0
. Denote
\bar{x}_{t}:=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{t}x_{k+1}
. Then for any
t\geq 1
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{x}_{t})-F^{*}]\lesssim\frac{1+\log\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big)}{t}+\frac{\sigma\log\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big)}{\sqrt{t}}.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

We start by summing (
4.2
) over
k=1,\dots,t
and noting
\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|=\eta\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|/S_{k}
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Delta_{k+1}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}\big(\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}\big)+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Big(L-\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\Big)\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}

(4.18)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta}S_{t}+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Big(L-\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\Big)\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle,

where the second inequality follows from (
4.15
).

To bound the second term on the RHS of (
4.18
), we consider two cases. Firstly, if
S_{1}:=\gamma>L\eta
, then it follows that

\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}=\frac{S_{k}}{\eta}\geq\frac{S_{1}}{\eta}>L,~\forall k\geq 1,

which further implies

\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Big(L-\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\Big)\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}\leq 0.

Otherwise if
\gamma\leq L\eta
, then
t^{*}:=\max\{k\in[t]:L\geq 1/\eta_{k}\}
is well-defined. Since
\eta_{k}=\eta/S_{k}
is non-increasing, we have
L\geq S_{k}/\eta
for all
1\leq k\leq t^{*}
and hence
S_{t^{*}}\leq L\eta
. For
k>t^{*}
, we know from the definition of
t^{*}
that
L\leq 1/\eta_{k}
. Therefore, by applying (
2.5
) in Lemma
4
and noting that
\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|\leq DS_{k}/\eta
for all
k\geq 1
, we obtain

\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Big(L-\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\Big)\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}\leq\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{t^{*}-1}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}+\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{t^{*}}\|^{2}}{S_{t^{*}}^{2}}\bigg)\leq\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\Big(1+\frac{D^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\Big)\log\frac{L^{2}\eta^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}+\frac{LD^{2}}{2}.

Combining the above two cases, we have

\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Big(L-\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}\Big)\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}\leq\Big[L(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\log\frac{L\eta}{\gamma}+\frac{LD^{2}}{2}\Big]_{+}.

(4.19)

To proceed, we take expectation on both sides of (
4.18
) and apply
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\langle\delta_{k},x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle]=0
for all
k\geq 1
, (
4.19
), and Lemma
7
with
b_{k}=1
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Delta_{k+1}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta}\mathbb{E}[S_{t}]+\Big[L(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\log\frac{L\eta}{\gamma}+\frac{LD^{2}}{2}\Big]_{+}+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle\delta_{k},x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle\bigg]

(4.20)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta}\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]+\Big[L(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\log\frac{L\eta}{\gamma}+\frac{LD^{2}}{2}\Big]_{+}+\sigma\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})t}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]}.

Moreover, we have established from the analysis in the nonconvex smooth case (cf. (
3.17
)) and
b_{k}=1
that

\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]\lesssim 1+\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big)\log\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big).

(4.21)

Plugging (
4.21
) into (
4.20
) and using
\sqrt{x}\leq\frac{1}{2}(1+x)
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Delta_{k+1}\Big]\lesssim

\displaystyle 1+\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big)\log\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big)+\sigma\sqrt{t}\sqrt{\log\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big)\log\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big)+\frac{\sigma\sqrt{t}}{2}\Big(1+\log\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 1+\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big)\log\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big),

which together with the convexity of
F
implies that

\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{x}_{t})-F^{*}]\leq\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Delta_{k+1}\Big]\lesssim\frac{1+\log\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big)}{t}+\frac{\sigma\log\big(1+\sigma\sqrt{t}\big)}{\sqrt{t}}.

The proof is now complete.
∎

\displaystyle\frac{\|z_{1}-x^{*}\|^{2}}{2\eta_{1}}+\sum_{k=2}^{t}\Big(\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}-\frac{1}{2\eta_{k-1}}\Big)\|z_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\frac{\|z_{t+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}}{2\eta_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta_{1}}+D^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{t}\Big(\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}-\frac{1}{2\eta_{k-1}}\Big)=\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta_{t}}\leq\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta}S_{t+1},

we further obtain

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}^{2}\Delta_{t+1}\leq

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}^{2}\Delta_{1}-\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{z,k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}}+\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta}S_{t+1}+2G\eta\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{z,k}\|}{S_{k}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}\langle\delta_{k},z_{k+1}-z_{k}\rangle+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}\langle\delta_{k},z_{k}-x^{*}\rangle.

To proceed, from the definition of

) in Lemma

Additionally, since

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}^{2}\Delta_{1}+\gamma\eta+2G\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}^{2}}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]}+\sigma\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}^{2}}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}^{2}\Delta_{1}+\gamma\eta+(2G+\sigma)\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}^{2}}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]},

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}\langle\delta_{k},z_{k+1}-z_{k}\rangle+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}\langle\delta_{k},z_{k}-x^{*}\rangle.

Taking expectations on both sides of (

\displaystyle\Big(\eta-\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta}\Big)\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]\leq

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}^{2}\Delta_{1}+\gamma\eta+2G\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sqrt{\log\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}}\Bigg]+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}\langle\delta_{k},z_{k+1}-z_{k}\rangle\bigg]

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}^{2}\Delta_{1}+\gamma\eta+(2G+\sigma)\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}^{2}}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]}.

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}\Delta_{1}}{\gamma\eta\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho}+\frac{2G+\sigma}{\gamma\eta\rho}\sqrt{2(\eta^{2}+D^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}^{2}}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]}.

which leads to the following after omitting the constants

\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]\lesssim 1+(1+\sigma)\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}^{2}}\sqrt{\log\Big((1+\sigma^{2})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}^{2}\Big)}\lesssim(1+\sigma)t^{3/2}\sqrt{\log\big((1+\sigma^{2})t^{3}\big)}.

(5.27)

Finally, applying (

\alpha_{k}\leq k+1

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t+1}]

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+(1+\sigma)\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k}^{2}}\sqrt{\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{S_{t+1}}{\gamma}\bigg]}

(5.28)

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+\sigma)t^{3/2}\sqrt{\log\Big((1+\sigma)t^{3/2}\sqrt{\log\big((1+\sigma^{2})t^{3}\big)}\Big)}

∎

Theorem 6

Multiplying both sides of the above inequality by

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}^{2}\Delta_{t+1}\leq

Remark 2
.

While we establish improved convergence results of Algorithm
2
for convex
f
, it is unclear whether it can find stationary points for nonconvex problems similar to Algorithm
1
. Intuitively, this seems unlikely to happen if we consider the special case

and

. Then it follows from (
5.3
) that
z_{k+1}=z_{k}-\alpha_{k}\eta_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})
, which together with (
5.5
) implies

S_{t}^{2}=\gamma^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{t-1}\alpha_{k}^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}.

Suppose that
\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}=O(1)
(which is the best possible rate for nonconvex and
L
-smooth
f
), then we know from
\alpha_{k}=O(k)
that
S_{t}^{2}=O(t^{2})
and hence
\eta_{t}=\eta/S_{t}=O(1/t)
, which is decreasing and inconsistent with the standard constant step size choice on the order of
1/L
. It is thus interesting to design an algorithm that is both universal and optimal across all three settings (nonconvex smooth, convex nonsmooth, and convex smooth). We leave it as a future direction.

7
Conclusion

We proposed an adaptive proximal gradient method for solving nonsmooth composite optimization problems, where one component is a simple convex function and the other belongs to one of three classes: nonconvex smooth, convex nonsmooth, or convex smooth. The method is characterized by an adaptive step size that accumulates historical gradient mapping norms in the denominator, replacing the gradient norms used in standard AdaGrad to better accommodate the composite structure. We showed that the method converges across all three problem classes without any modification or knowledge of problem-related parameters, achieving rates that match those of proximal gradient methods up to logarithmic factors in both deterministic and stochastic settings. For the convex case, we further developed an accelerated variant that retains near-optimal rates for convex nonsmooth objectives and achieves an improved rate of
\widetilde{O}(1/t^{2}+\sigma/\sqrt{t})
for convex smooth objectives. To handle the randomness of the adaptive step sizes in the stochastic setting, we developed new technical tools to bound cumulative inner products between the stochastic noise and iterative differences, which are applicable across all three problem classes and enable simplified convergence analyses. Our experiments on both nonconvex and convex composite problems validate the theoretical findings and demonstrate the practical competitiveness of the proposed algorithms.

Several directions remain open. The logarithmic factors in our convergence rates arise from bounding the growth of the denominator in the adaptive step sizes, and it is unclear whether they can be removed by a sharper analysis or a modified algorithm.
Another challenging open direction is to develop an adaptive accelerated method that simultaneously achieves optimal convergence rates across all three problem classes without modifications or knowledge of problem-related parameters, thereby unifying the universality of the first algorithm we proposed with the accelerated rates of the second.
