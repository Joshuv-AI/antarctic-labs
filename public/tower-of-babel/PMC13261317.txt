Association of Motoric Cognitive Risk Syndrome and Its Components with Health Indicators in Community-dwelling Older Adults: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Takafumi Kinoshita, Kensuke Matsuda, Takuro Ikeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:This study aimed to examine the associations between motoric cognitive risk syndrome (MCR), its component subjective cognitive decline (SCD), and health-related indicators among community-dwelling older adults.Methods:This cross-sectional study included community-dwelling older adults. MCR was defined as the presence of SCD and slow gait speed. Health-related indicators included the Questionnaire for Medical Checkup of the Old-Old (QMCOO), physical frailty, and social frailty. Participants were divided into three groups according to MCR components: a healthy group, a group with SCD, and an MCR group. Basic characteristics and health-related indicators of the groups were compared. Multivariate regression analyses were performed with QMCOO, physical frailty, and social frailty as dependent variables and MCR and SCD as independent variables.Results:Among the 138 participants included in the analysis, 41 (29.7%) belonged to the healthy group, 82 (59.4%) to the SCD group, and 15 (10.9%) to the MCR group. In the multivariate regression analysis, QMCOO was significantly associated with MCR (β = 0.217, P = 0.011) and SCD (β = 0.190, P = 0.022). MCR was significantly associated with physical frailty (odds ratio =19.495, P <0.001) but showed no significant association with social frailty. In contrast, SCD was not significantly associated with either frailty type.Conclusions:MCR and SCD were significantly associated with existing health-related indicators, suggesting their potential utility as markers for early detection of health deterioration in older adults.

INTRODUCTION

More than 55 million people worldwide were living with dementia in 2023, and this number is projected to increase to 152 million by 2050.1) This rapid increase in the prevalence of dementia places significant strain on healthcare and long-term care systems, increases the burden on family caregivers, and contributes to social isolation. These factors have a substantial impact on society. Therefore, preventing the onset of dementia and implementing early intervention have become even more important.2) In particular, international attention has focused on risk assessment and the identification of modifiable factors during the “preclinical stage” before dementia onset.3)

Motoric Cognitive Risk syndrome (MCR) has been proposed as a preclinical stage preceding dementia and mild cognitive impairment (MCI).4) MCR is defined as a condition in which subjective cognitive decline (SCD) and slow gait speed (SG) coexist. It is characterized by the ability to screen individuals simply and noninvasively without requiring neuropsychological testing.5) The international prevalence of MCR varies from 2% to 27%; however, its combined prevalence is reported to be approximately 9.7%.6) SCD, a component of MCR, is considered a preliminary stage of Alzheimer’s disease and the earliest subjective change to appear in cognitive decline.7) Therefore, MCR and SCD have become the focus of recent prevention and treatment research as “intervention windows” that could serve as indicators for early prediction and preventive intervention in dementia.8,9) Furthermore, MCR has been reported to be associated with multifaceted vulnerability, encompassing not only cognitive aspects but also physical frailty,10) sarcopenia,11) psychological factors such as depression and anxiety,12) and social factors such as diminished social interaction.13) MCR has gained attention as a health indicator that can be modified to comprehensively reflect the physical, cognitive, and psychosocial aspects of older adults. Indeed, it has been reported that approximately 20% of older individuals diagnosed with MCR transitioned to non-MCR during follow-up studies,14) suggesting that MCR is a reversible indicator that can be improved through appropriate intervention.

MCR has high clinical significance, and studies conducted in multiple countries have reported that it is a useful indicator for predicting health impairments, such as dementia onset, falls, and physical function decline.15,16) Japan is experiencing accelerated population aging.17) Comprehensive and early evaluation of the health status of older adults has been supported by the introduction of evaluation tools such as the Questionnaire for Medical Checkup of Old-Old (QMCOO),18) the Kihon Checklist (KCL), and the Japanese version of the Cardiovascular Health Study (J-CHS). These indicators are useful for assessing the health status of older adults, but it has been pointed out that QMCOO and KCL are self-reported and susceptible to subjective influences,19) whereas the J-CHS focuses specifically on physical functioning and does not adequately reflect psychosocial vulnerability.10)

Against this backdrop, MCR and its component, SCD, are noninvasive, easily assessed indicators that simultaneously capture both physical and cognitive frailty and have been reported to correlate with psychosocial factors. Therefore, if MCR and SCD are found to be significantly associated with existing assessment metrics, it will be suggested that they can be used as screening tools to more comprehensively evaluate the health status of older adults. This study aimed to examine the relationship between MCR and SCD in a cross-sectional manner based on the hypothesis that these indicators show significant associations with existing assessment indicators and are useful as modifiable indicators for the early detection of health status in older adults.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This cross-sectional study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement, which provides guidelines for reporting observational studies.20)

Participants and Settings

The participants were community-dwelling older adults residing in the study area who attended senior citizen programs operated by the Elderly Welfare Division of Kudamatsu City Hall, Yamaguchi Prefecture, between January and September 2025. All participants provided consent to participate. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals capable of arriving at the evaluation venue independently, regardless of whether a walking aid was used, and (2) individuals capable of understanding the survey content and following examination instructions. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals diagnosed with dementia or Parkinson’s disease by a doctor, (2) individuals certified as requiring support or nursing care under Japan’s public long-term care insurance system, (3) individuals with impaired cognitive function that made conversation or responses to self-administered questionnaires difficult, and (4) individuals with missing data from self-administered questionnaires or physical function assessments. Cognitive impairment was assessed using the Japanese version of the Ascending Dementia 8 tool (AD8-J), for which a score of 2 or higher indicates impairment.21) Among the 164 participants who agreed to participate in the study, 26 were excluded (2 had been diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease, 3 were certified as requiring support or nursing care, 4 had AD8-J scores of 2 or higher, and 17 had missing physical function assessment data). Statistical analysis included data from 138 participants (Fig. 1).

Data Collection

Collected demographic and health status data included age, sex, educational history, medical history (hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, heart failure, diabetes mellitus, osteoporosis, and lung disease), and Body Mass Index (BMI). Additionally, MCR, QMCOO, physical and social frailty, and physical function [grip strength, one-leg standing test with eyes open (OLST), and Timed Up-and-Go Test (TUG)] were assessed. Depressive status, anxiety, and sleep disorders were also assessed. MCR was evaluated based on criteria consistent with those used in previous studies.22) Physical frailty was assessed using the J-CHS criteria,10) whereas social frailty was evaluated using a questionnaire developed by Makizako et al.23) Depressive and anxiety disorders were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4).24,25) The PHQ-4 consists of two items on depressive symptoms and two items on anxiety disorders. A total score of 3 or higher on each scale indicated the presence of the disorder. Sleep disorders were assessed based on the presence of three criteria: (1) difficulty falling asleep, (2) difficulty returning to sleep, and (3) use of sleep medications. The participants were diagnosed with a sleep disorder if they met one or more of these criteria.26,27)

MCR Assessment

MCR was defined as cases meeting the criteria for both SCD and SG.22) SCD was assessed using five questions on subjective memory, and a diagnosis of SCD was made if one or more of the five items applied. Walking speed was measured by using a 2-m buffer zone at both ends of a flat 5-m walking path and determining comfortable gait speed (m/s). SG was defined as a gait speed more than one standard deviation (SD) below the mean gait speed of the entire study population. In the present sample, the mean gait speed was 1.28 ± 0.23 m/s, resulting in a cutoff speed of 1.05 m/s. This population-based −1 SD approach has been adopted in previous epidemiological studies of MCR in Japan and Asia19); however, alternative definitions using fixed cutoffs (<1.0 m/s) or age- and sex-stratified thresholds have also been reported.14)

QMCOO Assessment

The QMCOO18) is a questionnaire used to assess multidimensional vulnerability in older adults. The QMCOO consists of 15 items covering the ten domains of health status, mental health, dietary habits, oral function, weight loss, exercise, falls, cognitive function, smoking status, social participation, and social support. A higher total score (0–15 points) indicates greater multifaceted vulnerability.

Physical Frailty Assessment

Physical frailty was assessed using the J-CHS criteria.28) The J-CHS consists of five items: (1) weight loss, (2) physical activity, (3) exhaustion, (4) muscle weakness, and (5) walking speed. When none of these items applied to the participant, the individual was assessed as “physically robust.” When one or two items applied, participants were assessed as “physical pre-frailty.” When three or more items applied, participants were assessed as “physical frailty.” To address potential definitional overlap between MCR and physical frailty, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a modified J-CHS definition excluding the gait speed component. In this modified definition, physical frailty was defined as the presence of three or more of the four remaining components (weight loss, physical activity, exhaustion, and muscle weakness); the presence of zero, one, or two components was classified as nonfrail.

Social Frailty Assessment

Social frailty was assessed using a questionnaire proposed by Makizako et al.23) Evaluation was based on the responses to five questions that assessed the participant’s social engagement: (1) frequency of going out compared to the previous year, (2) visiting friends’ homes, (3) feeling useful to friends or family, (4) living alone, and (5) having daily conversations with someone. When none of these five items flagged social isolation, the participant was assessed as “socially robust.” When one item applied, participants were assessed as having “social pre-frailty.” When two or more items were applied, participants were assessed as having “social frailty.”

Physical Performance Assessment

Grip strength was measured twice for each hand using the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 criteria. The maximum value of all measurements was adopted.29) The OLST stance was measured by raising one leg while keeping the eyes open and maintaining the stance. The test was performed for each leg for a maximum of 60 s, and the result with the longer hold time was used.15) The TUG measured the time required to rise from a seated position, walk 3 m at a comfortable pace, and return to the chair in one trial.15)

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany).30) In a multiple regression model with an effect size of 0.15 (moderate), a significance level of 0.05, a power of 95%, and five explanatory variables, the required sample size was calculated to be 138 subjects. Considering the prevalence of exclusion criteria and MCR (approximately 10%), the final target sample size was set at 170 subjects.

Statistical Analysis

The participants were classified into three groups: healthy, SCD, and MCR, and the relationships between these groups were examined. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used when continuous variables followed a normal distribution; otherwise, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables. Post-hoc tests using the Bonferroni method were performed on items that showed significant differences among the three groups. Furthermore, multivariate regression analyses were conducted using QMCOO scores, the presence of physical frailty, and the presence of social frailty as dependent variables, whereas MCR and SCD were used as independent variables. Logistic regression analyses were performed using both the original J-CHS definition and the modified definition excluding gait speed as part of the sensitivity analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using R software for Windows (ver. 4.4.2). The significance level was set at 5%.

Ethical Considerations

The participants were informed verbally and in writing about the purpose and objectives of the study, and that participation was voluntary. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the International University of Health and Welfare (Approval Number: 24-KS-013).

RESULTS

Table 1 shows the characteristics of the participants and the results of the three-group comparisons. The SG cutoff speed used to determine MCR was set at 1.05 m/s. The prevalence of MCR was 10.9%. The participants were classified into three groups: healthy (n = 41, 29.7%), SCD (n = 82, 59.4%), and MCR (n = 15, 10.9%). The mean age was significantly higher in the MCR group than in the SCD group. The gait speed was significantly lower in the MCR group than in the healthy and SCD groups. The TUG results showed that the MCR group had significantly higher values than the healthy and SCD groups. Additionally, the MCR group showed significantly shorter OLST times than the healthy and SCD groups. The prevalence of physical frailty was significantly higher in the MCR group (66.7%) than in the healthy group (7.3%) and the SCD group (6.1%). The QMCOO scores were significantly higher in the MCR group, followed by the SCD and then the healthy control groups. The overall distribution of QMCOO scores exhibited adequate variability (median [interquartile range]: 2 [1–3], range: 0–11), with no apparent concentration at the upper limit. In addition, QMCOO scores did not significantly differ between participants aged 65–74 years and those aged 75 years and older (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, W = 987, P = 0.237). The results of the multivariate regression analysis showed that in the model with QMCOO scores as the dependent variable, MCR [B = 1.514, β = 0.217, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.359–2.669, P = 0.011] and SCD (B = 0.906, β = 0.190, 95% CI: 0.138–1.673, P = 0.022) showed significant associations (Table 2). In the model with physical frailty as the dependent variable, MCR showed a significant association [odds ratio (OR) = 19.495, 95% CI: 5.246–83.893, P <0.001), whereas SCD did not show a significant association (Table 3). In a sensitivity analysis using a modified four-component frailty definition (frailty defined as three or more of four components, excluding gait speed), MCR remained significantly associated with modified physical frailty (age- and sex-adjusted OR = 17.333, 95% CI: 4.195–82.751, P <0.001). In this analysis, 8 of 15 participants with MCR met the modified frailty criterion, compared with 7 of 130 participants without MCR. In contrast, in the model with social frailty as the dependent variable, neither MCR nor SCD showed a significant association (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This cross-sectional study examined the association between MCR, SCD, and existing health-related indicators among community-dwelling older adults. The results showed that MCR and SCD were significantly associated with these indicators. These findings suggest that MCR and its component, SCD, may serve as simple and clinically useful markers for the early identification of older adults at an increased risk of progressing to frailty.

MCR prevalence in this study was 10.9%, which is generally consistent with but slightly higher than the findings of previous studies.6) Because SG was defined using a cohort-specific criterion of 1.05 m/s (−1 SD), which is slightly higher than the commonly used threshold of 1.0 m/s, the number of individuals classified as having SG may have been modestly increased. In an additional analysis applying the 1.0 m/s cutoff speed, the prevalence of MCR decreased from 10.9% to 9.4% (13 of 138 participants). Although this indicates some sensitivity to threshold selection, the difference was small, and both estimates remained within internationally reported ranges. Therefore, the impact of cutoff selection on prevalence appears to be limited. Factors contributing to this may include the high average age of the participants and the criteria for setting the SG. The mean age of the MCR group in this study was 83.7 ± 4.2 years, which may have contributed to the increase in MCR prevalence.31) Although age- and sex-stratified definitions are often recommended because of the influence of demographic factors on gait speed,8,19) the population-based −1 SD method used in this study has been used in prior community-based studies. However, the distribution of gait speed in the present sample may have been influenced by the characteristics of the study population, including a relatively small proportion of healthy participants (29.7%). Because the cohort included older adults with SCD and MCR, the overall gait speed distribution may differ from that of age- and sex-stratified normative populations. Therefore, the derived cutoff speed (1.05 m/s) should be interpreted within the context of this specific population and may not be directly comparable with thresholds derived from externally stratified reference samples. Consequently, the derived cutoff speed (1.05 m/s) may differ from age- and sex-adjusted normative thresholds. This methodological characteristic should be considered when interpreting prevalence estimates and when comparing results with studies using different definitions of SG.32)

This study showed that MCR is an important factor in determining physical frailty. MCR is characterized by physical and cognitive frailty. Given that MCR includes SG among its components, it conceptually overlaps with physical frailty.7) However, although the J-CHS is limited to physical functioning, MCR has been reported to be a comprehensive indicator of cognitive and psychosocial frailty. Even when frailty was defined using a stricter four-component criterion (three or more of four components) that excluded gait speed, the association between MCR and physical frailty remained statistically significant. This finding suggests that the observed relationship is not solely attributable to the shared gait component. Therefore, MCR can serve as a clinically useful assessment tool that complements the J-CHS and enables the early identification of multivariate vulnerability in older adults. In addition, significant differences in gait speed and TUG performance were observed among the groups. Because the MCR group was significantly older than the other groups, age-related decline may have partially contributed to these differences. Accordingly, these performance differences should be interpreted with caution.

Notably, in this study, MCR and SCD were significantly associated with QMCOO. Furthermore, QMCOO scores did not significantly differ between participants aged 65–74 years and those aged 75 years or older, suggesting that the measure demonstrated comparable score distributions across age groups. These findings support the applicability of QMCOO in this broader older adult population. The QMCOO is an indicator used to assess the multivariate vulnerability of older adults, and higher scores indicate deteriorating health status. These findings suggest that MCR and SCD may reflect broader multidimensional vulnerability encompassing physical and cognitive domains. This study could not determine the independent contributions of the physical and cognitive components of the QMCOO score, which should be examined in future research. In addition, the modest explanatory power of the model (adjusted R2 = 0.12) indicates that other unmeasured factors likely contribute to QMCOO scores; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as indicative of associations rather than robust predictive performance. Previous studies have reported that MCR is independently associated with QMCOO scores in community-dwelling older adults.19) This finding supports the possibility that MCR serves as an early indicator of health vulnerability in older adults. Furthermore, longitudinal studies have reported that higher frailty indices are associated with an increased risk of MCR. This finding suggests that shared biological mechanisms, such as chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, may underlie this association.33,34) In addition, SCD has been reported to emerge at an earlier stage than MCR.16) This finding suggests its potential utility as an indicator for the early detection of health decline. The QMCOO includes cognition-related items common to SCD, and this structural similarity may reinforce the association between SCD and QMCOO. MCR and SCD are comprehensive indicators that include physical, cognitive, and psychosocial aspects. These results are also consistent with the multidimensional assessment of vulnerability provided by the QMCOO. Therefore, these indicators are considered useful assessment tools for the early detection of health conditions in older adults and for supplementing the QMCOO.

In the present study, no significant association with social frailty was observed for MCR or SCD. Previous studies have yielded conflicting results, with some supporting a relationship and others showing no association.14,35,36) Previous studies conducted in community-dwelling older adults in Japan have reported social frailty prevalence rates ranging from 10% to 30%.37) In contrast, the prevalence observed in the present study ranged from 30.5% to 53.3% across groups, suggesting that our estimates may be relatively high compared with those of population-based samples. This discrepancy may partly reflect differences in recruitment methods and sample characteristics. Because participants were recruited from older adults enrolled in municipal welfare programs rather than from a population-based sampling frame, the study population may not fully represent the broader community-dwelling older population. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the external validity of these findings. Whereas MCR indicates physical and cognitive aspects, social frailty indicates social parameters, such as reduced social interaction and reduced frequency of going out. This difference in constituent elements is one factor that may explain why no statistical association was observed. Furthermore, it has been reported that MCR and its component SCD may appear at an early stage, indicating that they could precede the onset of social frailty. However, because this was a cross-sectional study, causal relationships could not be definitively established.11) Therefore, further longitudinal studies involving diverse groups are warranted.

The significance of this study lies in examining the relationship between MCR and SCD, which can be assessed noninvasively and simply using existing health-related indicators. MCR and SCD are clinically significant for the early detection of changes in the health status of older adults, because they are modifiable indicators that capture both physical and cognitive vulnerability. SCD can also be used to identify the early stages of cognitive decline and frailty. It is expected to be a useful screening tool for preventive interventions.

This study has some limitations. First, because of its cross-sectional design, causal relationships could not be inferred, and it remains unclear whether MCR contributes to the development of physical frailty or whether both conditions arise from shared underlying pathophysiological factors. Second, caution is needed when generalizing the findings because the study population was drawn from a limited geographic area. Given that participants were recruited from municipal welfare program attendees rather than from a population-based sampling frame, the observed prevalence of social frailty may not be fully generalizable to the broader community-dwelling older population. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the external validity of these findings. Third, in the logistic regression analysis with physical frailty as the dependent variable, insufficient Events Per Variable were identified; therefore, the Firth correction was applied to adjust for estimation bias. Because the number of MCR cases was small (n = 15), the number of covariates in the multivariable logistic regression models had to be limited to avoid overfitting and unstable estimates. Therefore, adjustment was restricted to age and sex as fundamental demographic confounders. The absence of additional variables such as educational attainment and comorbidities may have resulted in residual confounding. Future studies with larger samples are needed to allow more comprehensive adjustment.

CONCLUSION

The results of this study suggest that MCR and SCD are useful assessment tools that show significant associations with existing health-related indicators. MCR and SCD are modifiable indicators of physical and cognitive frailty in the early stages. Using these indicators in the initial phase of community support may help prevent dementia onset and address frailty.
