Design, synthesis, and antibacterial activity study of pyrroloquinazoline diamine derivatives
Authors: Muchun Cheng, YaJing Tian, JieYa Shang, Weiqing He, Ying Zheng, Can Yan, Xinyu Chen, Jiawei Li, YuanYing Jiang, Qiang Fu, Xianqing Deng, Xunli Xiao, Mingxia Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo combat antimicrobial resistance (AMR), a novel series of pyrrolo[3,2-f]quinazoline-diamine (PQD) derivatives (5a–5z) featuring a flexible benzyl-oxo-benzyl side chain were designed and synthesised via structural optimisation of lead compound IRS-16. All compounds exhibited potent dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) inhibition. Compound 5a was most notable, showing exceptional enzymatic inhibition (IC50 = 0.92 nM), surpassing both IRS-16 and trimethoprim. 5a demonstrated broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against Gram-positive (e.g., E. faecalis) and Gram-negative strains (e.g., E. coli), as well as clinical isolates of S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, and uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC), with MIC values as low as 0.5 μg/mL. It displayed rapid bactericidal action, low resistance propensity, and inhibited biofilm formation by >90% at 0.5 μg/mL. Additional mechanisms included disruption of bacterial membrane integrity, causing leakage of cellular contents. These results identify 5a as a promising lead for developing novel, broad-spectrum DHFR inhibitors with a low resistance risk.Keywords: Antimicrobial activity, cytotoxicity, DHFR, docking, quinazoline diamine

Introduction

The rapid emergence and global spread of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) represent a critical threat to public health, severely compromising the efficacy of conventional antibiotic therapies. The proliferation of multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) bacteria has led to prolonged hospitalisations, increased healthcare costs, and rising mortality rates due to dwindling treatment options1. A comprehensive analysis published in The Lancet identified AMR as a leading cause of death worldwide, with an associated mortality burden exceeding that of HIV/AIDS or malaria2. In 2019, drug-resistant infections were directly responsible for 1.27 million deaths and contributed to an additional 4.95 million fatalities. Without effective interventions, AMR could claim up to 39 million lives annually by 2050, equating to three deaths per minute2.

In response to this crisis, antibiotics such as carbapenems (broad-spectrum) and polymyxins (targeting Gram-negative bacteria) are often reserved as last-resort therapies3. However, the emergence of resistance mechanisms against these final-line agents—including carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE)—signals an alarming trajectory4. The erosion of these therapeutic bulwarks threatens to return us to a pre-antibiotic era, where common infections could again become fatal. This urgent scenario underscores the critical need to develop novel antibacterial agents, an endeavour that is both a scientific priority and a global health imperative.

The bacterial folate metabolism pathway presents a promising target for antibacterial development. Folate plays an essential role in synthesising DNA, RNA, and amino acids, and unlike humans, bacteria rely on de novo biosynthesis to meet their metabolic demands5,6. Two key enzymes in this pathway—dihydropteroate synthase (DHPS) and dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR)—serve as attractive targets for antimicrobial intervention. DHFR catalyses the NADPH-dependent reduction of dihydrofolate (DHF) to tetrahydrofolate (THF), a reaction essential for maintaining pools of one-carbon donors required for nucleotide biosynthesis and cell division5,6. Selective inhibition of bacterial DHFR disrupts THF regeneration, halting DNA synthesis and ultimately leading to bacterial death7.

Trimethoprim (TMP), a clinically validated DHFR inhibitor, features a 2,4-diaminopyrimidine core that forms key hydrogen bonds with active-site residues (e.g., Asp27), while its benzyl group enhances binding through hydrophobic interactions8,9. Although TMP exhibits broad-spectrum activity and oral bioavailability, its efficacy as monotherapy is limited, leading to its frequent use in combination with sulphonamides. This dual-blockade strategy synergistically inhibits consecutive steps in folate biosynthesis10. Structural optimisation of TMP’s benzyl side chain yielded iclaprim, a next-generation pyrimidine-based inhibitor with approximately 20-fold greater potency against DHFR and retained activity against TMP-resistant strains11. Iclaprim has completed Phase II/III trials for complicated skin and skin structure infections (cSSSIs) and is administered as monotherapy, avoiding sulfonamide-associated adverse effects12,13. Another promising candidate, Aditoprim, demonstrates high selectivity for bacterial DHFR, with IC50 values of 47 nM and 520 nM against DHFR from Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Lactobacillus casei, respectively, and is currently under development for veterinary applications14 (Figure 1).

Chemical structure of representative DHFR inhibitors as antibacterial agents.

Despite these advances, the clinical utility of DHFR inhibitors remains constrained by emerging resistance, suboptimal pharmacokinetics, and insufficient investment in antibacterial development15. Consequently, current research focuses on structural optimisation of existing scaffolds to enhance potency, broaden spectrum, and overcome resistance, or on discovering novel chemotypes targeting DHFR.

IRS-16, a DHFR inhibitor featuring a 1,3-diamino-7H-pyrrolo[3,2-f]quinazoline core, has been identified as a potential candidate for novel antibacterial development due to its broad-spectrum activity and exceptionally low resistance incidence16 (Figure 1). This molecule was designed by replacing the 2,4-diaminopyrimidine core of trimethoprim with the 1,3-diamino-7H-pyrrolo[3,2-f]quinazoline scaffold and substituting the side chain with a biphenyl moiety. In this structure, the 1,3-diamino-7H-pyrrolo[3,2-f]quinazoline group serves as the key pharmacophore responsible for binding to DHFR and inhibiting its activity. The biphenyl side chain not only enhances molecular affinity for DHFR via hydrophobic interactions but also possesses an additional function—disrupting bacterial membrane integrity—thereby acting synergistically with DHFR inhibition to exert antibacterial effects.

To develop DHFR inhibitors with superior antibacterial potency and an improved safety profile, this study focuses on further structural optimisation of IRS-16. We performed molecular docking of IRS-16 into the DHFR active site to analyse its interactions and guide subsequent structural modifications (Figure 2). The docking analysis revealed that the diaminopyrimidine moiety of IRS-16 forms stable interactions with E. coli dihydrofolate reductase (ecDHFR): hydrogen bonds with Ile94, Ile5, and Asp27. Concurrently, the 1,3-diamino-7H-pyrrolo[3,2-f]quinazoline core engages in a π–π stacking interaction with Phe31. The biphenyl group of the IRS-16 side chain interacts hydrophobically with Asn18 and Asn23. Notably, the biphenyl group is positioned very close to Asn18 and Asn23 (at distances of 2.3 Å and 3.5 Å, respectively), creating steric hindrance that negatively impacts binding.

Molecular docking models with ecDHFR. (a) Lead compound IRS-16, (b) The target molecule (5a), (c) Overlay diagram of IRS-16 (yellow) and 5a (green) docked with ecDHFR.

Guided by this analysis and inspired by the design strategy that led from trimethoprim to Iclaprim, we sought to modify the side chain while preserving the critical pyrrolo[3,2-f]quinazoline-diamine (PQD) scaffold responsible for DHFR inhibition. We introduced an oxygen atom into the biphenyl linker of IRS-16 to increase the flexibility of the side chain. This modification was intended to allow the terminal phenyl ring to adopt a comfortable position to interact with Asn18 and Asn23, thereby potentially enhancing the compound’s binding affinity for ecDHFR. The feasibility of this design was confirmed by molecular docking of the target compound into the DHFR binding site (PDB: 6CQA) (Figure 2). The docking results showed that the PQD cores of IRS-16 and the target compound overlap perfectly. However, the side chain of the target compound is shifted into a more favourable conformation, with increased distances from Asn18 and Asn23. This optimised interaction reduces the binding free energy and improves the docking score (from −8.692 kcal/mol to −8.838 kcal/mol).

Based by these structural insights, we designed and synthesised a novel series of pyrrolo[3,2-f]quinazoline-diamine (PQD) derivatives (5a–5z) through systematic modification of the lead compound IRS-16. The key structural optimisation involved the replacement of the original biphenyl side chain with a flexible benzyl-oxo-benzyl linker and the introduction of diverse substituents on the terminal phenyl ring. This rational design strategy aimed to enhance binding affinity for DHFR, ultimately pursuing antibacterial agents with improved potency and safety profiles. All target compounds were characterised by spectroscopic analysis. For biological profiling, the antibacterial activities were evaluated against a panel of standard and clinically relevant bacterial strains to determine the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs). Furthermore, the inhibitory potency against ecDHFR was quantified using in vitro enzymatic assays to obtain the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values. The selected compound 5a was subjected to further investigation of its bactericidal potential, resistance-suppressing potential, antibiofilm activity, and bacterial membrane-disruptive potential.

Results and discussion

Chemistry

The synthesis of target compounds 5a–5z was accomplished as outlined in Scheme 1. The route commenced with the reaction of 1H-indol-5-amine and sodium dicyanamide in DMF catalysed by TsOH, affording (Z)-2-cyano-1-(1H-indol-5-yl)guanidine (1). Subsequent cyclisation of 1 using boron trifluoride diethyl etherate in DME yielded the key intermediate 7H-pyrrolo[3,2-f]quinazoline-1,3-diamine (2). In a parallel sequence, p-fluorobenzaldehyde was coupled with various substituted phenols in DMSO using Cs2CO3 as a base to provide intermediates 3a–3z, which were then reduced with NaBH4 and chlorinated with SOCl2 to furnish compounds 4a–4z. Finally, nucleophilic substitution of intermediate 2 with chlorides 4a–4z produced the target PQD derivatives 5a–5z. All final compounds were fully characterised by 1H-NMR,1³C-NMR, and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS).

Preparation route of the target compounds 5a–5z.

The inhibitory rates of the target compounds at a concentration of 1 μM are presented in Table 1. As shown, most of the synthesised compounds exhibited substantial affinity for ecDHFR, effectively inhibiting the folate reduction reaction catalysed by the enzyme. Specifically, compounds 5a, 5f, 5j, 5k, 5 l, 5 m, and 5o demonstrated pronounced inhibition, with rates exceeding 95%. Among them, compound 5a displayed the highest potency, achieving an inhibition rate of 98.12%.

Antibacterial activity against standard strains

To validate the design strategy for enhancing antibacterial activity, we conducted in vitro antibacterial activity assessments using a serial dilution method to determine the MICs of new compounds 5a–5z against three standard Gram-positive bacterial strains (S. aureus ATCC 6538 and CMCC 25923, and E. faecalis CMCC 29212), and three standard Gram-negative bacterial strains (E. coli CMCC 25922 and CMCC 44568, and P. aeruginosa CMCC 10104). Norfloxacin, penicillin, TMP, and IRS-16 were tested under the same conditions as the reference agents.

Overall, the antibacterial activity of compounds 5a–5z against the tested Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains exhibited distinct strain selectivity and substituent dependence (Table 2).

Against the three Gram-positive strains, the compounds showed the most pronounced inhibition against E. faecalis CMCC 29212. Among the 26 derivatives, 18 exhibited MIC values ≤ 4 μg/mL. Compound 5k (R = 4-Br) was the most potent (MIC = 1 μg/mL), demonstrating activity comparable to penicillin (MIC = 1 μg/mL) and superior to IRS-16 (MIC = 8 μg/mL) and TMP (MIC = 32 μg/mL). Compounds 5a (R = H), 5g (R = 3-Cl), and 5h (R = 4-Cl) also showed good activity (MIC = 2 μg/mL). In contrast, activity against S. aureus ATCC 6538 and CMCC 25923 was generally weaker. Only compound 5v (R = 4-OCH3, MIC = 0.5 μg/mL) against S. aureus CMCC 25923 matched the potency of penicillin (MIC = 0.5 μg/mL) and was higher than IRS-16 (MIC = 8 μg/mL). The MIC values for the remaining compounds against these two S. aureus strains mostly ranged from 2 to 32 μg/mL, which were generally higher (i.e., less active) than those of the positive controls norfloxacin (MIC = 2 μg/mL) and penicillin (MIC = 1 μg/mL for ATCC 6538; MIC = 0.5 μg/mL for CMCC 25923).

Among the three Gram-negative strains, the compounds showed relatively better activity against E. coli CMCC 44568 and CMCC 25922, while their activity against P. aeruginosa CMCC 10104 was mostly weak. Specifically, against E. coli CMCC 44568, compound 5a (R = H) was the most potent (MIC = 0.5 μg/mL), showing activity close to that of norfloxacin, and higher activity than IRS-16 (MIC = 4 μg/mL). Against E. coli CMCC 25922, compounds 5b (R = 2-F), 5d (R = 4-F), and 5e (R = 2,4-(F)2) exhibited outstanding activity (MIC = 1 μg/mL), equating to IRS-16 (MIC = 1 μg/mL), outperforming TMP (MIC = 8 μg/mL) and showing a significant advantage over penicillin (MIC = 32 μg/mL). In contrast, against P. aeruginosa CMCC 10104, only 5a (MIC = 2 μg/mL) and 5b (MIC = 4 μg/mL) retained medium activity, while the majority of other compounds had MICs ≥16 μg/mL or >32 μg/mL.

In summary, compound 5a emerged as the most promising derivative within the series. It exhibited potent activity against both S. aureus CMCC 25923 (MIC = 2 μg/mL) and E. faecalis CMCC 29212 (MIC = 2 μg/mL). It was also one of the most active derivatives against S. aureus ATCC 6538 (MIC = 4 μg/mL). Its activity against E. coli CMCC 44568 (MIC = 0.5 μg/mL) and CMCC 25922 (MIC = 0.5 μg/mL) was superior to that of the reference drugs IRS-16, TMP, and penicillin. Its potency against P. aeruginosa CMCC 10104 was comparable to norfloxacin and superior to IRS-16, TMP, and penicillin.

Analysis of the structure-activity relationship (SAR) revealed that small halogen substituents generally maintained potency, whereas increasing the substituent bulk (e.g., in compounds 5q–5u) led to a marked reduction in antibacterial activity. This suggests that excessively large substituents hinder optimal fitting within the DHFR active site, resulting in diminished binding affinity.

Correlation analysis between DHFR enzymatic inhibition and antibacterial activity revealed a consistent trend. Compounds demonstrating high ecDHFR inhibition (> 95%), namely 5a, 5f, 5j, 5k, 5 l, 5 m, and 5o, also exhibited favourable antibacterial potency (MIC range: 0.5–16 μg/mL). Notably, compound 5a, which showed the best antibacterial activity, also possessed the highest ecDHFR inhibition rate (98.12%). Conversely, compounds with lower enzymatic inhibition, such as 5s (50.77%), 5v (53.33%), and 5w (46.50%), generally displayed correspondingly weaker antibacterial activity (mostly MIC >32 μg/mL). This strong correlation confirms that the primary mechanism of antibacterial action for this series is the disruption of bacterial folate metabolism via DHFR inhibition.

Antibacterial activity against clinical strains

Clinically isolated strains often exhibit varying degrees of drug resistance. In 2023, the national average detection rate of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) in Chinese hospitals was 29.1%, reaching over 50% in some regions17. These clinical isolates possess biological characteristics relevant to real infection scenarios. Beyond the previously mentioned resistance conferred by genetic determinants, they may also feature mechanisms such as overexpression of efflux pumps and loss of outer membrane porins. Evaluating compound activity against these strains validates their real-world efficacy under “non-ideal,” clinically relevant conditions18,19. Therefore, to better assess the antibacterial potential of our compounds, we evaluated their activity against five clinical strains. The selected panel included one Gram-positive strain (S. aureus U4373) and four Gram-negative strains (K. pneumoniae U639, U4515, U4727, and Uropathogenic E. coli U899), covering species commonly associated with clinical infections.

As summarised in Table 3, the activity of the compounds demonstrated significant strain selectivity and substituent dependence. The target compounds exhibited notable activity against S. aureus U4373. Nineteen of the 26 compounds showed MICs ≤8 μg/mL against this S. aureus strain. Among them, compounds 5a (R = H), 5c (R = 3-F), 5d (R = 4-F), and 5f (R = 2-Cl) were particularly potent (MIC = 1 μg/mL), performing significantly better than the positive controls norfloxacin (MIC = 8 μg/mL), penicillin (MIC = 8 μg/mL), and TMP (MIC >64 μg/mL).

K. pneumoniae is a prevalent Gram-negative pathogen in clinical settings, with extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing and carbapenem-resistant (CRKP) strains being major concerns for resistance control. The activity of the target compounds varied markedly across the three K. pneumoniae isolates. They were most active against K. pneumoniae U639, with sixteen compounds (5a–5p) exhibiting MICs ≤ 2 μg/mL. Compounds 5a (R = H), 5i (R = 2,4-(Cl)2), 5j (R = 3-Br), and 5m (R = 2-CH3) were the most effective (MIC = 0.5 μg/mL), surpassing all positive control drugs. In contrast, most compounds showed poor activity against K. pneumoniae U4515, with only 5a–5f, and 5m retaining weak activity (MIC = 8–64 μg/mL). Notably, all target compounds, like the positive controls, were inactive against K. pneumoniae U4727 (MIC > 64 μg/mL).

Uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) is the primary causative agent of human urinary tract infections (UTIs), such as cystitis and pyelonephritis, accounting for 70–95% of community-acquired UTIs. A subset of our compounds (5a–5g, 5r) demonstrated good antibacterial activity against UPEC strain U899, with MICs ≤8 μg/mL. Compounds 5a and 5c were the most potent, with MICs of 0.5 μg/mL, significantly outperforming the positive controls norfloxacin (MIC = 32 μg/mL), penicillin (MIC >64 μg/mL), and TMP (MIC >64 μg/mL).

The influence of substituents on activity was also evident in the MIC data against clinical strains. Compounds with smaller substituents generally possessed better antibacterial activity. An increase in substituent bulk (e.g., in 5q–5z) led to an immediate loss of activity against clinical strain U639 (MIC >64 μg/mL).

Based on this comprehensive evaluation against clinical isolates, compound 5a again emerged as the optimal candidate. It demonstrated excellent activity against four strains: U4373 (1 μg/mL), U639 (0.5 μg/mL), U4515 (8 μg/mL), and U899 (0.5 μg/mL). It was the only compound to achieve broad coverage encompassing Gram-positive bacteria, susceptible Gram-negative bacteria, and a uropathogenic strain, solidifying its status as a promising broad-spectrum antibacterial candidate for further development.

Time–kill curve of compound 5a

The time–kill assay is a standard method for evaluating the bactericidal activity of antimicrobial agents. The resulting kill curves allow for direct comparison of the killing rate and efficacy between different agents or across different concentrations of the same agent20. In this experiment, S. aureus CMCC 25923 and E. coli CMCC 25922 were treated with compound 5a or norfloxacin at concentrations of 1×, 2×, 4×, and 8× the respective MIC. Bacterial counts were monitored over 12 h to generate the time–kill curves for both agents (Figure 4).

The bactericidal activities of compound 5a and norfloxacin against S. aureus CMCC 25923 are shown in Figure 4(a). Both agents exhibited concentration-dependent killing. At equivalent multiples of the MIC, compound 5a demonstrated a faster killing rate and achieved a greater final reduction in the bacterial population than norfloxacin, indicating its superior bactericidal efficacy against this staphylococcal strain. It is worth mentioning that compound 5a exhibited a rebound in its bactericidal effect against S. aureus CMCC 25923 at the 1-h time point. This phenomenon may be attributed to persister cell formation at supralethal concentrations, or to the protective film formed by intracellular substances following rapid damage to the bacterial cell membrane.

The kill curves for E. coli CMCC 25922 are presented in Figure 4(b). Norfloxacin showed negligible bactericidal activity at its 1× MIC. As the concentration increased to 2×, 4×, and 8× MIC, its killing rate progressively accelerated, resulting in complete bacterial eradication (a reduction to zero CFU/mL) within the 12-h test period. In contrast, compound 5a was less effective than norfloxacin at the lower concentrations (1×, 2×, and 4× MIC). However, at 8× MIC, compound 5a surpassed norfloxacin’s performance, achieving complete bacterial killing at an earlier time point (6 h vs. 8 h).

Propensity to induce bacterial resistance of compound 5a

A critical metric in developing new antibacterial agents is a low propensity to induce bacterial resistance. This propensity can be assessed by serially passaging microorganisms in the presence of sub-inhibitory concentrations of the antimicrobial agent21. Studies have shown that long-term exposure of bacteria to antibiotics at sub-MIC concentrations is common in natural environments, host infection sites, and clinical therapy. Such selective pressure induces adaptive mechanisms, including altered gene expression, biofilm formation, and changes in motility, which in turn accelerate the evolution of drug resistance. Therefore, the bacterial response and resistance evolution under sub-MIC concentrations can more authentically reflect the developmental trend of drug resistance as well as the clinical application potential of the agent.

In our initial experiments, compound 5a demonstrated potent inhibitory activity against both standard and clinical strains of E. coli. Therefore, we evaluated the resistance development trend in E. coli CMCC 44568 when exposed to compound 5a, using norfloxacin as a control. The bacteria were serially passaged for 22 days in media containing 1/2× MIC of either compound 5a or norfloxacin, with MIC values monitored throughout this period.

As shown in Figure 5(a), bacteria (E. coli CMCC 44568) subjected to serial exposure exhibited markedly reduced susceptibility to both norfloxacin(MIC increased 128-fold, from 0.125 to 16 μg/mL) and the lead compound IRS-16 (MIC increased 16-fold, from 4 to 64 μg/mL). In stark contrast, the antibacterial activity of compound 5a remained largely unchanged. After 21 days, the MIC of compound 5a increased only marginally from 0.5 μg/mL to 1 μg/mL. The MIC increase of compound 5a was substantially lower than that of the lead compound IRS-16 and norfloxacin against E. coli CMCC 44568.

Resistance development of compound 5a, IRS-16 and norfloxacin against (a) E. coli CMCC 44568 and (b) S. aureus CMCC 25923. Bacteria were continuously cultured at sub-MIC concentration for 21 days, and the MIC values of the compound 5a and IRS-16 against this strain were determined daily. The MIC for 5a, IRS-16 and norfloxacin was plotted against days to give the propensity of bacterial resistance development.

As shown in Figure 5(b), bacteria (S. aureus CMCC 25923) subjected to continuous exposure did not exhibit significant differences in resistance development among compound 5a, IRS-16, and norfloxacin. The MIC of compound 5a increased 4‑fold, from 2 to 8 μg/mL. The MIC of norfloxacin increased 2‑fold, from 2 to 4 μg/mL. For the lead compound IRS‑16, the MIC increased 4‑fold from 8 μg/mL to 32 μg/mL. Collectively, these data demonstrate that compound 5a possesses a negligible propensity for inducing resistance.

Biofilm inhibition

Biofilms are structured communities of bacterial cells enclosed within a self-produced polymeric matrix, providing an ideal environment for bacterial proliferation. The formation of biofilms is associated with a substantial proportion of bacterial infections in humans, including infectious endocarditis, periodontitis, and chronic lung infections in cystic fibrosis patients22,23. Furthermore, bacteria within biofilms exhibit significantly enhanced resistance to conventional antibiotics due to the inherent diffusion barrier posed by the extracellular matrix, which also fosters acquired resistance following prolonged antimicrobial intervention24. Consequently, the development of novel antibiotics with potent antibiofilm activity is crucial for advancing healthcare outcomes.

In this study, the inhibitory effect of compound 5a, on biofilm formation by E. coli in the logarithmic growth phase, was assessed using the crystal violet staining assay. The results are presented in Figure 6, where the curve depicts the concentration-dependent inhibition of biofilm formation by compound 5a, and the vertical gray line indicates its minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). Compound 5a inhibited over 90% of biofilm formation at a concentration of 0.5 μg/mL. Notably, even at a substantially lower concentration of 0.03125 μg/mL, it still suppressed more than 60% of biofilm formation. A similar pattern was observed for norfloxacin, which also inhibited over 90% of biofilm formation at its MIC. These findings confirm that, like norfloxacin, compound 5a effectively inhibits E. coli biofilm formation at low concentrations, demonstrating its promising potential as a candidate for combating biofilm-associated infections.

Intracellular DNA and protein leakage assay

The cell membrane serves as a critical barrier for maintaining cellular homeostasis and integrity. Its disruption leads to the leakage of intracellular components—such as proteins, nucleic acids, sugars, and ions—resulting in loss of cytoplasmic homeostasis and ultimately, bacterial cell death25. To investigate the effect of compound 5a on the integrity of the E. coli cell membrane, we measured the leakage of intracellular DNA and proteins, using colistin as a positive control.

As shown in Figure 7, negligible leakage of DNA and proteins was detected in the bacterial culture after 6 h of incubation without antibiotic treatment. In contrast, the addition of either colistin or compound 5a resulted in significant, concentration-dependent leakage of these cellular components, indicating substantial compromise of membrane integrity. These results clearly demonstrate that compound 5a exerts a significant disruptive effect on the E. coli cell membrane.

The protein and DNA leakage of E. coli CMCC 44568 after treatment with compound 5a. (a) Protein leakage and (b) DNA leakage. Samples were analysed after incubation at 37 °C for 6 h. All samples were analysed in triplicate.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a key structural component of the outer membrane in Gram-negative bacteria, is anchored via its lipid A moiety in the outer leaflet. Exogenous LPS competitively binds cationic antimicrobial compounds, such as polymyxin or peptide-based antibiotics, thereby neutralising their charge and blocking their interaction with target LPS on the bacterial membrane. Divalent cations (e.g., Mg2+, Ca2+ mediate electrostatic cross-linking between adjacent LPS molecules, forming a dense barrier that limits permeation of hydrophobic antimicrobial agents. The addition of exogenous Mg2+ and Ca2+ stabilises the negatively charged LPS layer and enhances membrane integrity, thereby reversing the disruptive effects of the antimicrobial agent. These mechanism significantly impair the ability of such agents to insert into and disrupt the membrane26,27. To further elucidate the antibacterial mechanism of compound 5a, experimental groups involving exogenous supplementation of LPS and Ca2+ were established. As shown in Figure 8, the exogenous addition of either LPS or Ca2+ to the culture medium significantly reduced the compound 5a-induced leakage of DNA and proteins, indicating that these components could reverse the damaging effect of compound 5a on the cell membrane. The results indicate that exogenously added LPS competitively binds to antimicrobial agents targeting the outer membrane layer, while Ca2+ contributes to stabilising the negatively charged LPS layer and enhances membrane integrity. Therefore, the observed reduction in leakage can be directly attributed to the protective effect of these two components on the cell membrane, thereby providing reverse confirmation of the target site of compound 5a.

In vitro cytotoxicity of compound 5a

Antifolate agents hold a significant position in cancer therapy due to their cytotoxicity against tumour cells28. Methotrexate (MTX) was the first anticancer drug developed to function by inhibiting dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR). The toxic effects of MTX on normal cells can be mitigated by the administration of calcium folinate (leucovorin). Consequently, despite the inherent cytotoxicity of folate-based compounds, novel antifolate drugs such as pemetrexed, pralatrexate, and calcium levofolinate continue to be developed for the treatment of various cancers. To evaluate the potential antitumor activity of our target compounds, we investigated the growth inhibitory effects of compound 5a on human colon carcinoma cells (SW620), human breast cancer cells (MCF-7), and human gastric adenocarcinoma cells (AGS). The toxicity of compound 5a against normal cells (LO2) was also determined for comparison. As anticipated, treatment with compound 5a led to a significant reduction in tumour cell viability. As shown in Figure 9, compound 5a exhibited IC50 values of 4.92 μM, 5.29 μM, and 1.52 μM against SW620, MCF-7, and AGS cells, respectively, indicating considerable antitumor potential. Compound 5a gave an IC50 value of 7.03 μM against normal LO2 cells. This indicates that compound 5a possesses certain in vitro antitumor selectivity, albeit with moderate selectivity. Therefore, in future drug development, its toxicity against normal cells should be taken into consideration.

Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully developed a novel series of pyrrolo[3,2-f]quinazoline-diamine (PQD) derivatives as potent DHFR inhibitors through rational structural optimisation, replacing the biphenyl side chain of the lead compound IRS-16 with a benzyl-oxo-benzyl moiety. Structure-activity relationship (SAR) analysis established that small substituents on the terminal phenyl ring are crucial for maintaining excellent enzymatic inhibition and antibacterial potency. The optimised compound 5a, not only demonstrated broad-spectrum and potent antibacterial activity against a diverse panel of standard strains and clinically resistant isolates but also exhibited a dual mechanism of action involving DHFR inhibition and disruption of bacterial membrane integrity. Compound 5a exhibits higher DHFR inhibitory activity and stronger antimicrobial activity than the lead compound IRS-16, demonstrating the rationality of the present structural optimisation. Furthermore, 5a displayed rapid bactericidal kinetics, significant antibiofilm activity, and a low propensity for resistance development, collectively underscoring its considerable potential as a promising candidate to address the current AMR crisis. Additionally, the observed in vitro antitumor activity of 5a provides a preliminary rationale for its repurposing as an antifolate agent in oncology, thereby broadening its therapeutic applicability.

Assay of DHFR inhibition activity

The enzyme activity of dihydrofolate reductase (FolA) was determined using the Dihydrofolate Reductase Assay Kit (Sigma CS0340) on a QuantaMaster 40 Spectrophotometer (Photon Technology International Inc., Edison, NJ). This assay kit measures the activity of FolA by monitoring the absorbance change at 340 nm due to NADPH consumption dependent on FolA. In a 1 ml quartz cuvette, sequentially add 780 μL of Tris-HCl (50 μM, pH 7.4), 100 μL of NADPH (60 μM, Sigma-Aldrich, China), 10 μL of DHFR (0.02 U, Cusabio, China), and 10 μL of the test compound solution in DMSO, achieving a final compound concentration of 1 μM. After thorough mixing, incubate the mixture at room temperature for 2 min. Then, initiate the reaction by adding 100 μL of dihydrofolic acid (100 μM, Sigma-Aldrich, China), and immediately record the absorbance change at 340 nm using a UV spectrophotometer. Readings are taken every 30 s for a total duration of 5 min. The experiment is performed in triplicate. The slope of the absorbance versus time plot is obtained by linear fitting and used to represent the reaction rate. A blank control is measured under the same conditions without the test compound to obtain the slope of the blank reaction curve. Evaluation of anti-bacterial activity in vitro

Six standard strains namely S. aureus ATCC 6538, CMCC 25923, E. faecalis CMCC 29212, E. coli CMCC 25922, CMCC 44568, and CMCC 10104 were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (Purchased via BeNa Culture Collection) and National Centre for Medical Culture Collections (CMCC). Five clinical strains, namely S. aureus U4373, K. pneumoniae U639, U4515, U4727, and uropathogenic E. coli U899, were isolated from patients of the Affiliated Hospital of Jinggangshan University, and were identified. Bacterial suspension was prepared by diluting log-phase cultures with broth medium to approximately 105 CFU/mL. A 96-well transparent plate was used, with each well containing 100 μL of broth medium. Subsequently, 4 μL of compound in DMSO was added to the first row to achieve a final concentration of 32 μg/mL, followed by twofold serial dilution across the plate. Then, 100 μL of bacterial suspension was inoculated into each well, with the final row reserved as a blank control. The initial absorbance (0 h) was measured at 650 nm using a microplate reader. The plate was incubated statically at 37 °C for 15 h, after which the absorbance was measured again (15 h). The bacterial growth was indicated by the difference between the two absorbance readings. The inhibition rate was calculated as: Inhibition (%) = [1-(ODSample (15h–0h)/ODControl (15h–0h))], and the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was defined as the lowest concentration resulting in 90% inhibition.

Time–kill

Staphylococcus aureus CMCC 25923 and Escherichia coli CMCC 25922 were cultured with shaking at 37 °C for 15 h, then adjusted to a density of 105 CFU/mL. One millilitre of this diluted bacterial suspension was incubated for 2 h, followed by exposure to the test compound or positive agents at concentrations of 1, 2, 4, and 8 times the MIC. At time points of 0 h, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, 6 h, 8 h, and 12 h, 10 μL aliquots were withdrawn, serially diluted 10- or 100-fold, and spread onto culture plates. After 24 h of incubation, colony counts were recorded for each plate, and a time–kill curve was constructed.

Propensity of bacterial resistance development

The initial MIC values of 5a, IRS-16 and norfloxacin (Sigma-Aldrich, China) were determined against the E. coli CMCC 44568 and S. aureus CMCC 25923. Following the initial MIC determination, serial passage experiments were carried out. Bacterial cultures grown in the presence of the test compound or reference antibiotic at half the MIC (MIC/2) were transferred to fresh medium, and the MIC was measured again. After 15 h of incubation, bacteria that grew at the sub-inhibitory concentration (MIC/2) were once more transferred for a subsequent MIC assay. This cycle was repeated for a total of 21 passages. The MIC values of compound 5a and control agents were plotted against the passage number (days) to evaluate the tendency for resistance development.

Evaluation of antibiofilm activity

An overnight culture of E. coli was diluted 1:100 with Mueller–Hinton broth and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow biofilm formation. After discarding the planktonic bacterial suspension, the test compounds were added and incubated with the established biofilms at 37 °C for another 24 h. The supernatant was then removed, and the wells were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline to eliminate non-adherent bacteria. Subsequently, 0.1% crystal violet solution was added to each well to stain the biofilm biomass. Following a 30‑min staining period, the wells were washed again and air-dried. The ability of the compounds to remove E. coli biofilms was evaluated relative to the negative control (vehicle) using a colorimetric method, with absorbance measured at 600 nm on a microplate reader.

DNA and protein leakage assay

E. coli suspension (approximately 108 CFU/mL) was co-incubated with varying concentrations (0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 × MIC) of either compound 5a or colistin (Sigma-Aldrich, China). A bacterial suspension treated with an equal volume of 0.01 M PBS served as the control. Following a 6‑h incubation at 37 °C, each mixture was passed through a 0.22‑μm microporous membrane. The filtrate was then analysed for protein and DNA content using an ultra‑micro ultraviolet spectrophotometer. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Exogenous LPS and Ca2+ reverse bacterial DNA and protein leakage

Exogenous LPS and CaCl2 were separately added to the culture medium to prepare solutions with final concentrations of 100 μg/mL LPS and 15 mM Ca2+. The prepared LPS- or Ca2+-containing media were co-cultured with E. coli suspension (approximately 108 CFU/mL) and different concentrations (0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 × MIC) of compound 5a or colistin (Sigma-Aldrich, China). An equal volume of 0.01 M PBS was added to the bacterial suspension as a control. After 6 h of incubation at 37 °C, the mixture was filtered by a 0.22 μm microporous membrane, and then the protein concentration and DNA concentration of the sample were measured with an ultra-micro ultraviolet spectrophotometer. All samples were performed in triplicate.

Evaluation of cytotoxicity activity in vitro

Human breast cancer cell line MCF-7 (ATCC HTB-22, purchased from Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China), human gastric cancer cell line AGS (ATCC CRL-1739, purchased from Shanghai Bohu Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China), human colon cancer cell line SW620 (ATCC CCL-227, purchased from Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China), and human normal liver cell line LO2 (ATCC HL-7702, purchased from Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into a 96-well plate. When the cell confluency reached approximately 50%, different concentrations of the 5a solution (0.03 μM, 0.1 μM, 0.3 μM, 1 μM, 3 μM, 10 μM, 30 μM, 100 μM) were added to the wells for cell treatment. After incubation for 24 h, the medium was replaced, and CCK-8 reagent (Glpbio, China) was added to each well. A microplate reader was used to measure the absorbance at a wavelength of 450 nm. Three replicate wells were set up for each concentration. Cell viability and IC50 values were calculated based on the absorbance readings and the corresponding compound concentrations.
