Comprehensive evaluation of capsicum genotype-environment interactions utilizing AMMI and GGE biplot models across staggered transplanting environments
Authors: Jasdeep Kaur, Sonia Sood, Nikhil Thakur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genotype × environment interaction (GEI) poses a major challenge to the identification of stable, high-yielding capsicum genotypes under heterogeneous agro-climatic conditions. The present study evaluated 96 diverse capsicum (Capsicum annuum L.) genotypes across four staggered transplanting environments during 2022 and 2023 in the North-western Himalayas to dissect GEI for fruit yield and its component traits. Combined analysis of variance revealed highly significant (p ≤ 0.01) effects of genotype, environment, and GEI for all traits, indicating substantial genetic variability and differential environmental responses. AMMI analysis effectively partitioned interaction effects, with the first two interaction principal component axes (79–95%) explaining a large proportion of GEI for most traits. GGE biplot analysis provided clear visualization of genotype performance and stability. Genotypes G5 and G6 consistently exhibited superior marketable fruit yield with wide adaptability, while several genotypes showed specific adaptation to individual environments. The GGE biplot interpretation revealed that environment E3 was the most representative for selecting stable genotypes, while E2 demonstrated high discriminative ability among genotypes. The integrated AMMI-GGE approach facilitated the identification of promising genotypes under the evaluated environments at the study location, demonstrating its utility for stability assessment in capsicum.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-47846-3.

Introduction

Capsicum (Capsicum annuum L.) is a high-value horticultural crop of global significance, cultivated across a wide spectrum of agro-ecological niches characterized by pronounced spatial and temporal environmental heterogeneity1. Yield realization in capsicum is inherently complex, as it arises from the integrated expression of multiple morpho-phenological and fruit-specific component traits whose expression is strongly modulated by environmental conditions2–4. Consequently, phenotypic performance often reflects the combined influence of genetic potential and environmental modulation, resulting in genotype × environment interaction (GEI), which complicates the identification of consistently superior genotypes in breeding programs5. GEI can reduce selection efficiency by altering genotype rankings across environments and by inflating experimental variance, thereby obscuring stable genetic performance6. In capsicum, yield is governed by several environmentally responsive and genetically inter-related traits such as fruit number, fruit size, fruit weight, pericarp thickness, and harvest duration. The collective response of these traits to environmental variation often generates complex performance patterns that cannot be adequately interpreted using simple mean-based approaches. Therefore, robust multivariate analytical models are required to partition genotype and interaction effects and to interpret stability across variable environments78.

The Additive Main Effects and Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI) model integrates analysis of variance with principal component analysis to partition GEI into orthogonal interaction principal component axes, thereby improving interpretation of genotype responses across environments9,10. Similarly, the Genotype plus Genotype × Environment (GGE) biplot approach focuses on genotype evaluation by combining genotype main effects with GEI, enabling graphical visualization of genotype adaptability, stability, and test environment relationships11. The combined application of AMMI and GGE biplot analyses has proven useful for interpreting GEI patterns and identifying stable and high-performing genotypes in multi-environment trials12,13. In North-western Himalayan region, capsicum production is frequently constrained by fluctuating temperature regimes, erratic rainfall, and relatively short growing seasons. To cope with these constraints, farmers often adopt staggered transplanting schedules to optimize crop establishment and extend the harvesting window. Such temporal variation in transplanting time can create substantial environmental heterogeneity within a single location, thereby intensifying GEI and influencing genotype performance. However, systematic evaluation of GEI in capsicum under staggered transplanting environments remains limited14. Importantly, the environments considered in the present study represent temporal environmental variation generated by combinations of years and transplanting dates (year × transplanting date), rather than geographically distinct test locations. These temporally structured environments provide an opportunity to examine genotype responses to seasonal variability under hill production conditions.

Therefore, the present investigation hypothesizes that significant genotype × environment interaction exists among capsicum genotypes across staggered transplanting environments, and that multi-variate GEI models such as AMMI and GGE biplot can effectively identify stable and high-performing genotypes along with informative test environments. Accordingly, AMMI and GGE biplot models are employed to quantitatively characterize genotype × environment interaction for yield and its contributing traits in Capsicum annuum. The specific objectives were to evaluate genotype stability across temporally defined environments (year × transplanting date combinations), identify superior genotypes with consistent performance, and assess the discriminative ability and representativeness of the tested environments. By integrating multivariate GEI modelling with trait-level resolution, this study aims to provide analytical insights for genotype evaluation and selection under variable hill production conditions15.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The experimental material consisted of 96 capsicum (Capsicum annuum L.) genotypes (Table 1) sourced from diverse national and international institutions to capture broad genetic variability representing exotic introductions, indigenous germplasm, local cultivars, released varieties, advanced breeding lines, and MAGIC (Multi-parent Advanced Generation Inter-Cross) lines. All accessions were open-pollinated or stabilized breeding materials. These included accessions from the World Vegetable Center, Taiwan (2); Kyungpook National University, Daegu, Republic of Korea (1); National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources (NBPGR), New Delhi, India (5); ICAR–Indian Institute of Horticultural Research (IIHR), Hesaraghatta, India (2); ICAR–Indian Institute of Vegetable Research (IIVR), Varanasi, India (2); ICAR–Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI) Regional Station, Katrain, India (3); Punjab Agricultural University (PAU), Ludhiana, India (1); Dr. Y.S. Experimental site

The Experimental Farm is situated in the mid-hill zone of Himachal Pradesh (Zone-II) at an elevation of 1290.8 m above mean sea level, lying between 32o6′ N latitude and 76o3′ E longitude. The soil is silty clay loam, having an acidic pH (5.0 to 5.6). The climate is predominantly humid sub-temperate, with an average annual rainfall of about 2500 mm, nearly 80 per cent of which occurs between June to September. Experimental layout

Field evaluation trials were conducted during the summer–rainy seasons of 2022 and 2023 at the Experimental Farm of CSK Himachal Pradesh Krishi Vishvavidyalaya, Palampur. Seedlings were raised in plastic pro-trays under protected conditions using staggered sowing dates (5 January and 10 February in both years) following seed treatment with Bavistin and Dithane M-45.Transplanting was carried out on four dates to create distinct test environments: E1 (15 March 2022), E2 (15 April 2022), E3 (15 March 2023), and E4 (15 April 2023). Staggered transplanting dates were employed to deliberately expose genotypes to contrasting thermal and moisture regimes, thereby enhancing the detection and interpretation of genotype × environment interactions.

The experiment was laid out in an alpha-lattice design (12 × 8) with three replications in each environment. Each genotype was planted in a single row of ten plants at a spacing of 60 × 45 cm. The field was prepared by disc ploughing followed by rotavating, and fertilization was applied at 20 t ha⁻¹ farmyard manure and 90:75:50 kg N: P₂O₅:K₂O ha⁻¹ as per recommended practices. One-third of nitrogen and full doses of phosphorus and potassium were applied basally, while the remaining nitrogen was top-dressed in two equal splits at 30 and 45 days after transplanting.

Data observations

Observations were recorded on five randomly selected plants per genotype per replication, excluding border plants. Days to 50% flowering and days to first picking were noted as days from transplanting, while plant height (cm) was measured at final harvest. Harvest duration (days) was calculated as the interval between the first and last harvest. Fruit traits, including number of lobes per fruit, fruit length (cm), fruit width (cm), pericarp thickness (mm), average fruit weight (g), number of marketable fruits per plant, and marketable fruit yield per plant (g), were recorded from cumulative harvests; fruit shape index was calculated as the ratio of fruit length to fruit width. Total soluble solids (°Brix) were measured using a digital refractometer, and ascorbic acid content (mg 100 g⁻¹ fresh weight) was estimated by the 2,6-dichlorophenol indophenol titration method.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R16 software (version 4.2.1). Fruit yield and related traits were subjected to combined analysis of variance (ANOVA) to test the significance of genotype (G), environment (E), and genotype × environment interaction (GEI). In the ANOVA model, genotypes were considered as fixed effects, whereas environments (year × transplanting date combinations) were treated as random effects, following the standard framework for multi-environment trials aimed at genotype evaluation. Each year-transplanting combination was treated as an independent environment.

AMMI analysis was performed using the agricolae package17. The significance of interaction principal component axes (IPCAs) was evaluated using Gollob’s F-test at the 5% probability level, and IPCAs were sequentially tested to determine the number of statistically significant interaction components retained in the AMMI model. Only significant IPCAs representing meaningful interaction patterns were retained for interpretation, while non-significant components were considered as noise and excluded from further analysis. This approach enabled effective separation of signal from noise in the GEI structure, thereby improving the reliability of genotype stability assessment.

GGE biplots were generated using the GGEBiplotGUI package18–20. Environment-centered data (centering = 2) were used to remove environmental main effects, while scaling was set to 0 (no scaling) to preserve the original variance structure of the dataset. Singular value partitioning was genotype-focused (SVP = 1) to facilitate genotype comparison across environments. The “which-won-where” polygon view of the GGE biplot was used to identify winning genotypes and delineate possible mega-environment patterns, while the average environment coordination (AEC) view was employed to evaluate genotype mean performance and stability across environments. Additionally, the discriminative ability and representativeness of test environments were assessed using the environment vector view of the GGE biplot.

Results

Combined analysis of variance

Pooled ANOVA revealed highly significant (p < 0.01) effects of genotype (G), environment (E), and genotype × environment interaction (G×E) for all traits studied (Table 2). The presence of significant genotypic variation provided a robust basis for subsequent GEI analysis and the identification of stable, high-performing genotypes for yield and related traits in capsicum. Partitioning of total variation revealed that genotypic effects contributed the largest proportion of variance for most of the traits studied, particularly pericarp thickness (99.01%), fruit width (97.84%), average fruit weight (96.98%), fruit shape index (96.88%), fruit length (95.30%), harvest duration (91.60%), marketable fruits per plant (98.93%), marketable fruit yield per plant (81.37%), plant height (69.84%), days to first picking (61.22%), and days to 50% flowering (63.53%), indicating strong genetic control and substantial variability among the evaluated genotypes. Genotype × environment interaction (GEI) accounted for a relatively greater proportion of variation for ascorbic acid (18.91%), days to 50% flowering (13.23%), marketable fruit yield per plant (10.78%), days to first picking (10.56%), plant height (7.38%), harvest duration (3.58%), fruit length (1.79%), average fruit weight (1.51%), fruit shape index (1.15%), fruit width (1.04%), marketable fruits per plant (0.83%), pericarp thickness (0.29%), lobes per fruit (4.04%), and total soluble solids (0.59%), suggesting differential genotype responses across the evaluated environments. Environmental effects were comparatively more pronounced for days to first picking (13.15%) and plant height (7.64%), reflecting the influence of temporal environmental conditions on these traits.

AMM I biplot analysis for marketable fruit yield per plant

AMMI 1 biplot analysis

The AMMI1 biplot revealed a pronounced genotype × environment (G×E) interaction for marketable fruit yield per plant, with the first interaction principal component axis (IPCA1) accounting for 45.4% of the total interaction variance (Fig. 3). Genotypes G4, G5, G6, G8, and G9, positioned towards the positive end of the x-axis, exhibited higher mean yield across environments, indicating superior yield potential. In contrast, genotypes G35, G46, G57, G68, and G69, clustered close to the origin, displayed minimal interaction effects, reflecting greater yield stability across environments. Environmental differentiation was evident, with E3 showing a strong positive IPCA1 score, indicative of favourable conditions for yield expression and enhanced genotype discrimination. Conversely, E2, located in the negative IPCA1 direction, exerted a restrictive influence on yield performance for most genotypes. Genotypic proximity to the IPCA1 zero line indicated stability, whereas increasing deviation reflected greater environmental sensitivity.

AMMI 2 biplot analysis

The AMMI 2 biplot, integrating IPCA1 (45.4%) and IPCA2 (34.3%), cumulatively explained 79.7% of the total G×E interaction for marketable fruit yield per plant. Genotypes G12, G39, G41, G56, G78, G79, and G89, located near the origin, exhibited limited interaction with environments, confirming their broad adaptability and consistent yield performance. The polygon view of the biplot enabled clear identification of environment-specific winners, with G86 in E1, G48 and G52 in E2, G23 and G34 in E3, and G88 in E4, demonstrating specific adaptation to respective environments. The relative length of environmental vectors reflected differential interaction intensities, highlighting the heterogeneity among test environments. Collectively, the AMMI2 biplot complemented the AMMI1 results by distinguishing genotypes with wide adaptation from those with pronounced environment-specific yield advantages.

AMMI biplot interpretation of phenological, morphological, yield-component and quality traits

AMMI1 biplot analysis across phenological traits revealed a moderate to high magnitude of genotype × environment (G×E) interaction, with IPCA1 explaining 59.5% and 64.9% of the interaction variance for days to 50% flowering and days to first picking, respectively. Early flowering and early maturity were consistently associated with negative IPCA1 scores, with genotypes G4, G5, G6, G20, G29, and G37 exhibiting early flowering, while G4, G5, G6, G17, G18, and G62 were early for first picking. Genotypes G14, G24, G59, G61, G75 (flowering) and G41, G44, G66, and G76 (first picking), positioned close to the IPCA1 origin, demonstrated greater phenological stability. AMMI2 biplots cumulatively explained 89.2% and 93.0% of the interaction variance, respectively, and identified environment-specific winners, notably G52 (E1), G25 and G56 (E2), G55 and G92 (E3), and G4 (E4) for flowering, and G71 (E1), G40 (E2), G75 (E3), and G30 and G59 (E4) for first picking, confirming the presence of crossover interaction (Fig S1). The identification of “winning” genotypes in the AMMI2 polygon view is based on graphical inference from the AMMI Model.

For vegetative growth and harvest traits, AMMI1 biplots indicated substantial interaction effects, with IPCA1 accounting for 52.3% of the variance for plant height and 51.0% for harvest duration. Taller plant stature was associated with genotypes G18, G42, G85, G88, and G91, whereas G12, G15, G43, G44, and G45 showed shorter stature. Genotypes G8, G9, G40, and G60 combined moderate height with stability. Extended harvest duration was observed in G85, G87, G91, G93, G94, and G95, while G2, G31, G33, G40, and G51 displayed stable, moderate harvest duration. AMMI2 biplots explaining 83.6% (plant height) and 82.6% (harvest duration) of the interaction variance identified G87 (E1), G47 (E2), G18 and G78 (E3), and G50 (E4) as winning genotypes for plant height, and G94 (E1), G48 (E2), G85 and G96 (E3), and G36 and G57 (E4) for harvest duration, demonstrating strong environment-specific adaptation.

Fruit morphology and yield-component traits exhibited comparatively stronger interaction effects. IPCA1 explained 78.7% of the interaction for lobes per fruit, 55.5% for fruit length, 59.3% for fruit width, 82.6% for fruit shape index, 80.2% for pericarp thickness, 46.3% for average fruit weight, and 47.4% for marketable fruits per plant. High lobe number was associated with G17, G20, G28, G29, G38, G39, G52, and G93, whereas G46, G53, G70, G79, and G90 were stable across environments. Longer fruits were observed in G65, G81, G82, G83, and G85, while G13, G14, G27, G42, and G57 showed stability. Wider fruits were linked to G7 and G10, with G41, G85, G90, and G93 being stable. Elongated fruit shape was most pronounced in G76, G77, G78, G81, G94, and G95, while G10, G26, G42, and G72 exhibited stable expression. Thicker pericarp was recorded in G3, G15, G69, G70, G89, and G93, whereas G47, G56, G60, G86, and G91 showed minimal interaction. AMMI2 polygon views (cumulative variance 79.1–95.8%) consistently identified environment-specific winners, including G49, G31, G47, G17 (lobes), G54, G65, G12, G83 (fruit length), G36, G66, G26, G5 (fruit width), G81, G26, G77, G94 (fruit shape index), G16, G92, G3, G19 (pericarp thickness), G7, G1 and G11, G69, G14 and G17 (average fruit weight), and G95, G7 and G51, G94, and G84 (marketable fruits per plant) Fig S2.

Quality traits displayed the strongest interaction structure. For total soluble solids, IPCA1 alone explained 97.6% of the interaction, while ascorbic acid showed 79.7% contribution from IPCA1. High TSS expression was observed in G40, G79, and G81, whereas G12, G48, G50, G54, and G90 were stable across environments. For ascorbic acid, G21, G22, G61, and G78 recorded higher values, while G51, G55, G79, and G85 exhibited stability. AMMI2 biplots (cumulative variance 95.0–99.4%) identified G1 (E1), G39 and G63 (E2), G88 and G85 (E3), and G81 (E4) as winners for TSS, and G6 (E1), G32 (E2), G15 and G57 (E3), and G66 (E4) for ascorbic acid, highlighting pronounced environment-specific expression of biochemical traits Fig S3.

GGE biplot of marketable fruit yield per plant (g)

GGE biplot analysis for marketable fruit yield per plant across four environments revealed pronounced genotype × environment interaction (Fig. 4). The first two principal components explained 99.62% of the total variation, with PC1 accounting for 99.04% and PC2 for 0.58%.

The environment vector view showed wide dispersion of environments along PC1, reflecting substantial differences in their discriminating ability. Environments clustered into two groups, with E1 and E4 forming one group and E2 and E3 forming another. Acute angles (< 90°) among all environment vectors indicated positive correlations, suggesting that the environments were comparable while still effective in differentiating genotypic performance. The close proximity of E2 and E3 vectors further indicated a high degree of similarity between these environments.

In the discriminativeness versus representativeness view, E2 exhibited the longest vector length and was identified as the most discriminative environment. In contrast, E3 showed minimal deviation from the average environment axis, indicating high representativeness and suitability as an ideal test environment for selecting stable genotypes.

The mean performance versus stability biplot identified G5 as the highest-yielding genotype, followed by G6, G4, G8, and G9, all positioned far along the positive PC1 axis. Genotypes G1, G41, G43, G56, and G83 were located close to the biplot origin, indicating minimal interaction effects and superior yield stability across environments.

However, only four sectors contained test environments, indicating four environment-specific winning genotypes. Genotype G5 was the winning genotype in E1, G9 in E2, and G6 in both E3 and E4, demonstrating distinct patterns of specific adaptation across environments.

GGE biplot analysis of phenological, morphological, yield components, and quality traits

GGE biplot analyses across phenological, morphological, yield-component, and quality traits revealed that the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) together accounted for more than 90% of the total variation for all traits, confirming the robustness of the model and the reliability of graphical interpretations (Fig S4). Across traits, environments exhibited predominantly acute angles among their vectors, indicating positive correlations and consistent ranking of genotypes, though their discriminating ability and representativeness varied substantially. Environment E2 emerged as highly discriminative for several phenological and quality traits, while E3 was generally the most representative, making it a reliable test environment for identifying broadly adapted and stable genotypes.

For flowering and earliness traits, environments clustered into two major groups, with E3 and E4 showing strong similarity. Genotypes G1, G5, G6, G20, and G80 combined early flowering with high stability, whereas genotypes such as G24, G51, G61, G78, and G79 exhibited minimal G×E interaction. For days to first picking, genotypes G5, G17, G18, and G93 consistently showed early maturity, while G2, G25, G45, G62, and G76 were among the most stable across environments. Plant height was strongly influenced by environmental effects, with E4 being the most discriminative environment; genotypes G42, G48, G85, G88, and G91 were superior for this trait, whereas G8, G9, G40, G60, and G94 exhibited high stability.

Harvest duration displayed pronounced G×E interaction, with E3 showing the greatest discriminating power and E1 being most representative. Genotypes G85, G87, G93, G95, and G96 recorded extended harvest duration, while G16, G31, G33, G40, and G82 remained stable across environments. For fruit morphology traits, including lobes per fruit, fruit length, fruit width, fruit shape index, and pericarp thickness, PC1 and PC2 jointly explained more than 99% of total variation. E2 and E4 were consistently the most discriminative environments, whereas E3 was generally the most representative. Genotypes G7, G65, G81, G82, G83, and G85 showed superior performance for fruit size and shape attributes, while genotypes such as G12, G13, G23, G29, G39, G41, G43, G66, and G72 demonstrated stable expression with low interaction effects.

For average fruit weight and marketable fruits per plant, E3 exhibited the highest discriminating ability, while E4 and E1 were more representative. Genotypes G1, G5, G8, G23, and G69 recorded higher fruit weight, whereas G25, G29, G35, G53, and G57 were highly stable. Genotypes G86, G87, G93, G94, and G96 were superior for marketable fruits per plant, with several showing specific adaptation to individual environments.

Quality traits showed contrasting patterns of environmental sensitivity. Total soluble solids exhibited a relatively higher G×E interaction effect, with E4 being the most discriminative and E3 the most representative environment. Genotypes G21 and G22 consistently recorded higher TSS, while G14, G22, G60, G64, and G78 showed stable performance. For ascorbic acid content, E2 emerged as the most discriminative environment and E1 as the most representative. Genotypes G23, G48, G79, G81, and G84 exhibited higher ascorbic acid concentration, whereas G7, G11, G14, G38, and G73 showed greater stability across environments.

Overall, the GGE biplot analyses effectively distinguished between broadly adapted, stable genotypes and those with specific environmental adaptation across yield, yield-contributing, and quality traits, highlighting the potential of staggered transplanting environments for dissecting genotype × environment interactions in capsicum.

Discussion

The highly significant effects of genotype, environment, and their interaction for all studied traits clearly demonstrate the complex and non-additive nature of yield expression and associated characters in capsicum across staggered transplanting environments21, 22. Although a high proportion of genotypic variance (> 90%) was observed for several traits, these estimates should be interpreted with caution, as they may be influenced by the specific experimental conditions and relatively limited environmental variation. Such high values do not necessarily indicate strong genetic control in the absence of supporting genetic parameters such as heritability or repeatability. Therefore, these results should be considered indicative rather than conclusive for genetic determination of the traits. The magnitude of the G×E interaction observed in the pooled ANOVA underscores the necessity of employing multivariate stability models such as AMMI and GGE biplot analysis, as conventional mean-based comparisons alone would inadequately capture genotype performance under variable agro-temporal conditions23. Such pronounced interaction effects are typical for capsicum, a crop known for its sensitivity to temperature, photoperiod, and moisture fluctuations during flowering and fruit development24,25.

The relative contribution of genotypic effects exceeding environmental and G×E components for most quality and yield-contributing traits26. This indicates strong prospects for selection and genetic improvement. Conversely, the predominance of environmental effects for traits such as days to first picking and plant height highlights their plastic response to transplanting time-induced climatic variation, emphasizing the importance of environment-specific management rather than genetic selection for these attributes27–30. The high proportion of interaction variance captured by IPCA1 for total soluble solids should be interpreted with caution when the overall genotype × environment interaction is very small. In such situations, the percentage explained by the first interaction component is calculated relative to a very small interaction sum of squares, which can lead to apparently inflated values. Therefore, a large contribution of IPCA1 does not necessarily indicate strong or complex interaction effects. Instead, it suggests that the limited interaction variance present can be adequately summarized by a single interaction axis in the AMMI model, reflecting a relatively simple interaction structure rather than pronounced genotype-specific environmental responses31. Although PC2 was used to describe genotype stability, its contribution to the interaction variance was relatively small for certain traits (e.g., marketable fruit yield). Therefore, interpretations based on PC2 should be considered primarily descriptive and interpreted with caution. In such cases, greater emphasis may be placed on PC1, which accounts for the major proportion of the interaction variation.

The GGE biplot analysis for marketable fruit yield per plant partitioned genotype main effects and G×E interaction, revealing distinct adaptation patterns among the tested genotypes32–35. The first two principal components explained a very high proportion of the variation and PC2 for only 0.58%. While this indicates that most of the variation is captured by the first principal component, the extremely small contribution of PC2 suggests that interpretations of stability based on PC2 should be made cautiously. In such situations, the biplot largely reflects differences in mean genotype performance rather than strong differential stability patterns among genotypes. Accordingly, the graphical interpretation should be considered primarily descriptive of genotype performance across transplanting environments rather than definitive evidence of complex stability relationships. The dispersion of environments along the PC1 axis indicates differences in their discriminating ability, suggesting that staggered transplanting dates generated varying test conditions capable of eliciting differential genotypic responses36,37. However, it should be noted that all environments represent different transplanting dates within the same experimental location, and therefore the observed variation primarily reflects temporal climatic differences rather than spatial environmental heterogeneity.

The positive correlations among environments, as evidenced by acute angles between environment vectors, suggest a degree of consistency in genotype ranking despite differences in discriminative power38. This indicates that while the environments varied in their ability to expose genetic differences, they were not antagonistic, thereby enabling reliable identification of broadly adapted genotypes. The close alignment of E2 and E3 further implies similarity in prevailing climatic conditions during critical crop growth phases, resulting in comparable genotype responses39–41. The discriminativeness versus representativeness view provided insight into the utility of individual environments for selection. The high discriminating ability of E2 suggests its effectiveness in revealing genotypic differences, making it a useful environment for identifying high-performing genotypes. In contrast, the strong representativeness of E3, as indicated by its proximity to the average environment axis, suggests that it may serve as a suitable environment for selecting genotypes with relatively stable performance across transplanting windows. These interpretations should be considered cautiously because the environments differ only temporally within a single research location rather than representing independent agro-climatic zones42,43.

Genotypes positioned far along the positive PC1 axis with minimal projection onto PC2 combined high mean yield with acceptable stability, indicating their potential suitability for cultivation across the tested transplanting environments. In contrast, genotypes clustered near the origin exhibited minimal interaction effects, reflecting consistent performance but relatively moderate yield potential44. This distinction is important from a breeding perspective, as it enables breeders to select either broadly adapted stable genotypes or specifically adapted high-yielding ones depending on breeding objectives. The which-won-where pattern revealed the presence of crossover interactions, with different genotypes emerging as winners in specific transplanting environments45. However, although the polygon structure delineates several geometric sectors, only a subset of these sectors contains test environments. Therefore, the interpretation should be limited to identifying environment-specific winning genotypes. As all environments represent staggered transplanting dates at a single location, the results reflect temporal adaptation patterns rather than true mega-environment delineation46.

Overall, the integration of AMMI and GGE biplot analyses provided a comprehensive framework for examining genotype performance, stability, and adaptation under staggered transplanting environments47,48. Nevertheless, the present study was conducted at a single research farm, and therefore the findings should be interpreted as location-specific responses to temporal environmental variation. Additional multi-location testing across the northwestern Himalayan region would be required before making broader regional recommendations. These findings nevertheless highlight the usefulness of multivariate GEI analysis in identifying promising genotypes and understanding their responses to varying transplanting schedules under changing agro-climatic conditions49,50.

Conclusion

The present investigation highlights the pronounced influence of genotype × environment interaction on yield and its attributing traits in capsicum evaluated under staggered transplanting environments at Palampur. Highly significant effects of genotype, environment, and their interaction across all traits confirm the complex and non-additive nature of trait expression, emphasizing the limitations of selection based solely on mean performance. The combined use of the AMMI Model and GGE Biplot Analysis effectively dissected GEI patterns, enabling the identification of both widely adapted and environment-specific genotypes. Genotypes such as G5 and G6 exhibited consistently high yield and broad adaptability, while several others showed specific superiority under transplanting environments, supporting targeted genotype deployment under staggered transplanting conditions. Among environments, E3 was identified as the most representative and suitable for selecting stable genotypes, whereas E2 was highly discriminative for differentiating genotypic performance. Overall, this study demonstrates that integrating AMMI and GGE analyses provides a robust framework for genotype evaluation and environment characterization under staggered transplanting conditions at Palampur. These findings may guide future capsicum breeding programmes and multilocation trials aimed at validating genotype stability and refining selection strategies for staggered transplanting systems.
