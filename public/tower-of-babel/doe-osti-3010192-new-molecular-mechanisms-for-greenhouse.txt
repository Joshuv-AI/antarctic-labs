1.0 Executive Summary

Separations involving gaseous small molecules, including acid gases, nitrous oxide, and
hydrofluorocarbons, are vital to U.S. energy and technological dominance. However, many current
separations technologies are too energy-intensive for broad deployment. Sorptive separations, in which a
mixture of gases is contacted with a porous solid material, leading to separation based on the differing
affinity of the gases for the solid, promise lower energy requirements compared to many existing
technologies. However, selectivity remains a major challenge for many of these materials. In this work,
we took a molecular-level viewpoint to this problem by aiming to embed reactive and selective motifs for
different small molecule gases. In particular, we developed hydroxide- and amine N-oxide based materials
that show unmatched oxidative stability and high affinity for carbon dioxide, even from air (manuscripts
published in Nature, Journal of the American Chemical Society, and Angewandte Chemie, among others).
We also systematically investigated the ability of metal sites within porous materials to bind and activate
nitrous oxide, a byproduct of the agricultural industry with untapped potential as a chemical oxidant
(manuscript published in Journal of Materials Chemistry C). Last, we also designed materials capable of
recovering fluorinated greenhouse gases from the emissions of refrigerators and Teflon manufacturing
facilities (manuscripts published in Journal of the American Chemical Society and Chemical Science),
allowing them to be potentially recovered and employed as building blocks for the synthesis of fluorinated
pharmaceuticals and agrochemicals. These achievements were realized by embedding precise arrays of
functional groups within porous, crystalline materials known as metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), which
allow us to probe sorption mechanisms using a combination of X-ray diffraction, solid-state nuclear
magnetic resonance (SSNMR), infrared spectroscopy, and computational modeling. Together, our
findings shed important insights into reducing the energy costs associated with chemical separations,
which are currently responsible for 15% of all global energy use.

2.0 Project Objectives

Our project consisted of three main objectives:

 1) Embed hydroxide sites within porous MOFs to evaluate as sorbents for acid gas separations
 2) Understand how activation at open metal sites in porous MOFs can be used to strongly bind and
 activate nitrous oxide
 3) Employ halogen bonding interactions within MOFs to strongly bind fluorinated greenhouse gases
 such as fluoroform, a byproduct of Teflon manufacturing

3.0 Description of Activities Performed

Acid gas separations.
 We commenced our studies with cyclodextrin-based MOFs, which are known to contain a high
density of hydroxide anions and had previously been shown to strongly bind the acid gas carbon dioxide.
We developed a new solvothermal method for preparing a range of such MOFs with different cations
(sodium, potassium, rubidium), and anions, including both hydroxide and other basic anions (bicarbonate
and carbonate) as well as non-basic anions, including chloride and acetate. Carbon dioxide sorption
isotherms supported that only the MOFs made with non-basic anions exhibit strong affinity towards
carbon dioxide at low pressures, indicative of a reversible chemical reaction (also known as

chemisorption). Solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopic studies in conjunction with infrared
(IR) spectroscopy confirmed that this selectivity is due to reversible bicarbonate formation at the
hydroxide sites. These findings were supported by density functional theory (DFT) calculations as well.
We identified the MOF prepared from potassium bicarbonate as a promising material for further
evaluation due to its stability and low cost. This material could be cycled 100 times in a simulated
separation without significant degradation. Breakthrough measurements confirm its ability to separate
gases under realistic conditions. This work was published in Angewandte Chemie International Edition
(Mary E. Zick, Suzi M. Pugh, Jung-Hoon Lee, Alexander C. Forse, Phillip J. Milner*. “Carbon Dioxide
Capture at Nucleophilic Hydroxide Sites in Oxidation-Resistant Cyclodextrin-Based Metal–Organic
Frameworks.” Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2022, 61, e202206718. Selected as “Hot Paper.”). Subsequent
SSNMR studies further supported the proposed mechanism of carbon capture in this material (Benjamin
J. Rhodes, Lars L. Schaaf, Mary E. Zick, Suzi M. Pugh, Jordon S. Hilliard, Shivani Sharma, Casey R.
Wade, Phillip J. Milner, Gábor Csányi, Alexander C. Forse*. “17O NMR Spectroscopy Reveals CO2
Speciation and Dynamics in Hydroxide-Based Carbon Capture Materials.” ChemPhysChem 2024,
e202400941).

 Building upon this work, we identified the rubidium-based cyclodextrin MOF as a promising
material for acid gas separations at ultra-low concentrations, such as carbon dioxide removal from air.
This is due to the soft-soft match of the soft rubidium cation with the soft bicarbonate anion, which
strengthens the material’s affinity for carbon dioxide. We were able to optimize the synthesis of this
material and showed that it indeed is able to remove carbon dioxide from air using gas sorption isotherms
and thermogravimetric analysis measurements. When cycling the material, we identified an unusual
“switch” in the sorption mechanism, from reversible at low temperatures to irreversible at high
temperatures. In-depth SSNMR, IR, and DFT studies support that this switch occurs due to a change in
mechanism from bicarbonate to carbonate, the first time this switch in mechanism had been reported in a
hydroxide-based sorbent. This work lays the foundation for future work with these promising materials.
This work was published in Journal of the American Chemical Society (S. Alexandra Lim‡, Mary E.
Zick‡, Jaehwan Kim, Benjamin J. Rhodes, Joharimanitra Randrianandraina, Tristan A. Pitt, Ronald T.
Jerozal, Jung-Hoon Lee, Alexander C. Forse, Phillip J. Milner*. “Carbon Capture from Natural Gas Flue
Emissions and Air via (Bi)Carbonate Formation in a Cyclodextrin-Based Metal-Organic Framework.” J.
Am. Chem. Soc. 2025, 147, 25715–25726, ‡contributed equally).
 We were interested in determining whether hydroxide anions can be incorporated into even
cheaper sorbent supports, allowing for access to a low-cost material for direct air capture of carbon dioxide
and other acid gases. Working with Dr. Alexander Forse at the University of Cambridge, we demonstrated
that hydroxide anions can be electrochemically charged into inexpensive porous carbon supports to

produce “charge sorbents.” These ultra-stable materials (stable to oxidation at up to 150 C) are still capable
of removing carbon dioxide from air, making them a good platform for harvesting carbon dioxide from
air to use as a starting point for making chemical fuels and other commodity chemicals. This work was
published in Nature (Huaiguang Li, Mary E. Zick, Teedhat Trisukhon, Matteo Signorile, Xinyu Liu, Helen
Eastmond, Shivani Sharma, Tristan Spreng, Jack Taylor, Jamie Gittins, Cavan Farrow, S. Alexandra Lim,
Valentina Crocellà, Phillip J. Milner, Alexander C. Forse*. “Capturing Carbon Dioxide from Air with
Charged Sorbents.” Nature, 2024, 630, 654–659). In subsequent work, we demonstrated that these
materials can be regenerated easily using an electrical current (via Joule Heating) instead of requiring
heating like traditional sorbents, saving on energy costs for operation in a separation (Kepler Petzall,
Benjamin J. Rhodes, Yongqiang Wang, Teehat Trisukhon, Hari Kukreja, S. Alexandra Lim, Phillip J.
Milner, Alexander C. Forse*. “Developing Joule-Heating Regeneration of Carbon-Based Adsorbents for
Direct Air Capture.” Chem. Eng. J. 2025, 524, 169247).

 One of our fundamental motivations has been to identify materials that exhibit good oxidative
stability, as this is a fundamental challenge of the current leading sorbents for acid gas separations, known
as amines. We hypothesized that oxidized amines might still be capable of acid gas capture if they were
correctly functionalized. In this vein, we identified that tertiary amine N-oxides, which are non-toxic,
thermally stable, and oxidatively robust small molecules (they are already oxidized), might be able to
strongly bind carbon dioxide at their nucleophilic oxygen sites. These molecules are already used
industrially as part of the Lyocell process, underpinning their potential for real-world utility. Indeed,
extensive NMR and DFT studies support that tertiary amine-N-oxides can capture carbon dioxide via
reversible bicarbonate formation, despite being non-basic, by strongly activating water via hydrogen
bonding. The materials were stable to heating in air or real flue gas from Cornell University’s power plant
for a week and could be employed to remove carbon dioxide from the flue gas as well, as supported by in
situ IR cycling measurements. This work was published in Journal of the American Chemical Society
(Sijing Meng, Tristan H. Lambert*, Phillip J. Milner*. “Harnessing Oxidized Amines as Robust Sorbents
for Carbon Capture.” J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2025, 147, 6786–6794).

Nitrous oxide activation.
 Prior to our work, not much was known about the adsorption of nitrous oxide in porous materials.
We investigated the binding of this inert gas at open metal sites into different families of MOFs, one based

 Page 3 of 4

on oxygen ligands and one based on nitrogen ligands. We identified that the nature of the metal did not
have much impact on nitrous oxide binding in the latter materials, but it had a significant impact in the
former. Building upon these findings, we were able to identify record-setting materials for both the
adsorption enthalpy (or affinity) and the adsorption capacity for nitrous oxide in a porous material. We
employed DFT calculations to understand the origins of these differences. Our work lays the groundwork
for identifying better sorbents for nitrous oxide capture in porous materials. This work was published in
Journal of Materials Chemistry C (Tristan A. Pitt, Haojun Jia, Tyler J. Azbell, Mary E. Zick, Aditya
Nandy, Heather J. Kulik, Phillip J. Milner*. “Benchmarking Nitrous Oxide Adsorption and Activation in
Metal–Organic Frameworks Bearing Coordinatively Unsaturated Metal Centers.” J. Mater. Chem. C,
2024, 12, 3164–3174). This work also sparked a collaboration with Prof. Heather Kulik at MIT, which
continues to this day and has led to four co-authored manuscripts already, broadening the computational
and engineering expertise of the PI’s research group.

Hydrofluorocarbon capture.
 Hydrofluorocarbons are important refrigerants and starting materials for agrochemical and
pharmaceutical synthesis, but their separations remain understudied. We collaborated with Prof. Brandon
Barnett at the University of Rochester to investigate the binding of fluorinated gases like fluoroform and
tetrafluoromethane in MOFs (Bevan S. Whitehead, William W. Brennessel, Shane S. Michtavy, Hope A.
Silva, Jaehwan Kim, Phillip J. Milner, Marc D. Porosoff, Brandon R. Barnett*. “Selective Adsorption of
Fluorinated Super Greenhouse Gases within a Metal–Organic Framework with Dynamic Corrugated
Ultramicropores.” Chem. Sci. 2024, 15, 5964–5972). We hypothesized though that the key to strong
hydrofluorocarbon gas uptake in MOFs is to carefully tune the pore environment to maximize interactions
between the gas and the pores of the material. We systematically studied the adsorption of the fluorinated
gas fluoroform (a byproduct of Teflon manufacturing that is of interest for pharmaceutical synthesis)
within Zr-based MOFs, which shed valuable insight into the role of linker functionalization on gas
binding. We ultimately found that a bromofumarate linker is ideal to maximize the strength of the
fluorinated gas binding interactions, leading to record-setting selectivity and uptake at low pressures for
fluoroform in a MOF. These findings could be extended to the important refrigerant difluoromethane as
well. The synergistic combination of hydrogen-bonding interactions that is responsible for this strong
binding was elucidated using a combination of SSNMR and DFT studies. This work was ultimately
published in Journal of the American Chemical Society (Ronald T. Jerozal, Jaehwan Kim, Carolyn Ma,
Tristan A. Pitt, Jung-Hoon Lee, Phillip J. Milner*. “Enhancing Selective Hydrofluorocarbon Greenhouse
Gas Capture via Halogenation of Metal-Organic Frameworks.” J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2025, 147, 7127–
7136). After publication of this work, a company approached us about utilizing these findings to capture
and functionalize hydrofluorocarbon gases, which remains an ongoing collaboration sparked by this
project.
