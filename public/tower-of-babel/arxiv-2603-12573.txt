Pointwise mutual information bounded by stochastic Fisher information
Pedro B. Melo

Abstract
We derive general upper bounds to pointwise mutual information in terms of stochastic Fisher information and show these bounds average to known results in the literature for bounds to mutual information in terms of Fisher information. These results deepen the connection between information-theoretical quantities and are shown to hold in general cases. We test the bounds in classical systems and provide a quantum generalization. Our results are useful for stochastic dynamics, quantum sensing and quantum communication, providing a less costly way to realize and saturate the bounds.

INTRODUCTION

Parameter estimation is a fundamental task in physics

, where information-theoretic quantities like mutual information (MI) and Fisher information (FI) are utilized to quantify how much a measurement reveals about an unknown parameter

. Traditionally, these quantities are treated as ensemble-averaged metrics.
However, the inherent fluctuations of physical processes often prevent a direct local-global relation in standard parameter estimation

;

. In fields like stochastic thermodynamics and continuous quantum monitoring, systems are fundamentally characterized by single realizations or trajectories

;

;

. At this single-trajectory level, the local information gained from a specific measurement outcome is captured by the pointwise mutual information (PMI)

;

;

. Correspondingly, the local sensitivity to the parameter is governed by a state-dependent quantity, the stochastic Fisher information (SFI)

;

Recently, it has been shown that a fundamental bound relates the average MI to the FI

18

These bounds can be tighten on quantum systems via the CQFI on trajectory level
18
. By spectral decomposition, it decomposes into three distinct components, an incoherent term arising from population changes, a coherent term arising from unitary rotations of the eigenbasis, and a cross-term representing interference between the incoherent and coherent channels. This cross-term can be negative, meaning there is destructive interference between the incoherent weight of probabilities and the basis rotation relative to the probe state. The destructive interference during continuous quantum measurements can saturate bounds for single-trajectories.

This work is divided as follows: In section II, we introduce the bounds on PMI given by the probability distributions, the stochastic entropy and the stochastic Fisher information. In section III, we show that these bounds are related to the average ones, for the mutual information

In section V, we calculate the proposed bounds for some examples, such as an overdamped Langevin dynamics, single qubit phase estimation, real-time adaptive metrology, and information thermodynamics at trajectories level. In section VI, we discuss the results and present the conclusions.

II
BOUNDS ON PMI

To formally establish these trajectory-level limits, we first define our core quantities. Consider an unknown parameter
\theta
drawn from a prior distribution
p(\theta)
, and a measurement outcome
x
governed by a conditional probability
p(x|\theta)
. The local information gained from a single realization is quantified by the PMI
4
, which compares the joint probability to the product of marginals

i(x,\theta)=\log\frac{p(x|\theta)}{p(x)}.

(1)

Simultaneously, the local sensitivity of the trajectory to the parameter
\theta
is captured by the Stochastic Fisher Information (SFI)
16
;
17
, defined as

\iota(x,\theta)=(\partial_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta))^{2}.

(2)

Building upon the definitions of the PMI and the SFI, we establish upper bounds for the PMI that depend directly on the local stochastic fluctuations of the system. It has been shown
11
that the average mutual information follows a bound given by the Fisher information and the prior probability distribution
p(\theta)
. Here, we show a generalization to the trajectory level, relating the PMI to the SFI.

Theorem 1.
If an unknown parameter
\theta
is guaranteed to lie in a finite size set, such that
\text{supp }p(\theta)\subseteq[a,b]
, then the PMI is bounded by:

i(x,\theta)\leq\log\left(\frac{p(x|a)+p(x|b)}{p(x)}+\int_{a}^{b}d\theta\left|\frac{p(x|\theta)}{p(x)}\right|\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}(x,\theta)}\right)

(3)

where
\Lambda_{1}(x,\theta)=\iota(x,\theta)
.

Theorem 2.
For an arbitrary differentiable probability distribution of the parameter
p(\theta)
, we have that:

i(x,\theta)\leq\log\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\theta\left|\frac{p(x|\theta)}{p(x)}\right|\sqrt{\Lambda_{2}(x,\theta)}\right)+s(\theta)

(4)

where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}(x,\theta)

\displaystyle=\iota(x,\theta)p(\theta)^{2}+\dot{p}(\theta)^{2}+2\partial_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta)\dot{p}(\theta)p(\theta)

(5)

Here,
\dot{p}(\theta)=dp(\theta)/d\theta
and the final term
s(\theta)
acts as an upper bound for the stochastic entropy. It is important to note that this bound is not parameter invariant, and does not depend on
X
in principle, highlighting its sensitivity to the chosen parameter.

Details of PMI bounds.
To derive eqs. (
3
) and (
4
), we introduce an arbitrary, non-negative function
f(\theta)
such that
\text{supp}f(\theta)\supseteq\text{supp}p(\theta)
. We rewrite the PMI algebraically as

i(x,\theta)=\log\frac{p(x|\theta)f(\theta)}{p(x)}-\log f(\theta).

(6)

Assuming
f(\theta)
vanishes at infinity, we can bound the argument of the first logarithm using the fundamental theorem of calculus by integrating the absolute value of its derivative over all parameter space

\frac{p(x|\theta)f(\theta)}{p(x)}\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\theta^{\prime}\left|\frac{d}{d\theta^{\prime}}\left(\frac{p(x|\theta^{\prime})f(\theta^{\prime})}{p(x)}\right)\right|.

(7)

Expanding the derivative inside the absolute value using the product rule, and recognizing that probability distributions are strictly non-negative, we can factor out
p(x|\theta^{\prime})
. Utilizing the identity
\frac{\partial p(x|\theta)}{\partial\theta}=p(x|\theta)\partial_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta)
, we express the remaining terms inside a square root. This naturally introduces the stochastic Fisher information,
\iota(x,\theta)=(\partial_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta))^{2}
. Let us define the general term inside the square root as

\Lambda(x,\theta)\equiv\iota(x,\theta)f(\theta)^{2}+\dot{f}(\theta)^{2}+2\partial_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta)f(\theta)\dot{f}(\theta).

(8)

Substituting this expansion back into our original PMI expression yields the generalized bound

i(x,\theta)\leq\log\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\theta^{\prime}\frac{p(x|\theta^{\prime})}{p(x)}\sqrt{\Lambda(x,\theta^{\prime})}\right)-\log f(\theta).

(9)

From this generalized integral bound, our two theorems naturally emerge through specific choices of
f(\theta)
.

For Theorem 1, the parameter lies in a finite-size set
\text{supp}p(\theta)\subseteq[a,b]
, and we choose
f(\theta)
to be a boxcar function over
[a,b]
. To accommodate the step function inside the integral, we note the subadditivity property
\sqrt{y+z}\leq\sqrt{y}+\sqrt{z}
, which allows us to bound the generalized integral by decoupling the stochastic Fisher information from the absolute derivative
|\dot{f}(\theta)|
. The absolute derivative yields Dirac delta functions at the boundaries, extracting the specific boundary probability conditions
p(x|a)
and
p(x|b)
. Within the open interval
(a,b)
,
f(\theta)=1
and
\dot{f}(\theta)=0
, which simplifies the remaining functional to
\Lambda_{1}(x,\theta)=\iota(x,\theta)
. Finally, because
f(\theta)=1
inside the interval, the penalty term
-\log f(\theta)
vanishes. For Theorem 2, we set the arbitrary function to be the exact prior probability distribution,
f(\theta)=p(\theta)
. Substituting this into the general functional immediately recovers
\Lambda_{2}(x,\theta)
. The penalty term
-\log f(\theta)
becomes
-\log p(\theta)
, which defines the stochastic entropy
s(\theta)
. The proofs are discussed in more detail in Appendix
A
.

III
Recovering the average bounds

Having established the state-dependent bounds for the PMI, we now demonstrate how these local limits relate to the global bounds for the MI
11
.

The MI is defined as the ensemble average of the PMI over the joint probability distribution
p(x,\theta)=p(x|\theta)p(\theta)
, such that
I(X,\Theta)=\langle i(x,\theta)\rangle
. To find the average bound, we take the expectation value of the generalized bound from Eq. (
9
).

Averaging over the joint distribution yields

\displaystyle I(X,\Theta)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x}\int d\theta~p(x,\theta)

\displaystyle\times\left[\log\left(\int d\theta^{\prime}\left|\frac{p(x|\theta^{\prime})}{p(x)}\right|\sqrt{\Lambda(x,\theta^{\prime})}\right)-\log f(\theta)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}p(x)\log\left(\int d\theta^{\prime}\frac{p(x|\theta^{\prime})}{p(x)}\sqrt{\Lambda(x,\theta^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle-\int d\theta p(\theta)\log f(\theta).

(10)

Notice that the second term immediately recovers the penalty term associated with the arbitrary function
f(\theta)
found in standard MI bounds.

To evaluate the first term, we utilize Jensen’s inequality. Since the logarithm is a strictly concave function, the average of the logarithm is bounded by the logarithm of the average:
\sum_{x}p(x)\log(A_x)\leq\log(\sum_x p(x) A_x)
. Applying this to the sum over
x
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{x}p(x)\log\left(\int d\theta^{\prime}\frac{p(x|\theta^{\prime})}{p(x)}\sqrt{\Lambda(x,\theta^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(\sum_{x}p(x)\int d\theta^{\prime}\frac{p(x|\theta^{\prime})}{p(x)}\sqrt{\Lambda(x,\theta^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\int d\theta^{\prime}\sum_{x}p(x|\theta^{\prime})\sqrt{\Lambda(x,\theta^{\prime})}\right).

(11)

We apply Jensen’s inequality a second time to the sum inside the integral,
\sum_{x}p(x|\theta^{\prime})\sqrt{\Lambda(x,\theta^{\prime})}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{x}p(x|\theta^{\prime})\Lambda(x,\theta^{\prime})}
. We now evaluate the expectation value of our functional
\Lambda(x,\theta)
with respect to the conditional probability

\displaystyle\sum_{x}p(x|\theta)\Lambda(x,\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}p(x|\theta)\left[\iota(x,\theta)f(\theta)^{2}+\dot{f}(\theta)^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+2\partial_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta)f(\theta)\dot{f}(\theta)\right].

(12)

By definition, the average of the stochastic Fisher information (SFI) is the classical Fisher information (FI),
\sum_{x}p(x|\theta)\iota(x,\theta)=F(\theta)
. Furthermore, the cross-term vanishes because the sum of the derivative of a probability distribution is zero

\displaystyle\sum_{x}p(x|\theta)\partial_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\frac{\partial p(x|\theta)}{\partial\theta}=0.

(13)

Consequently, the expectation of the functional simplifies purely to ensemble quantities

\sum_{x}p(x|\theta)\Lambda(x,\theta)=F(\theta)f(\theta)^{2}+\dot{f}(\theta)^{2}.

(14)

Substituting this back into our averaged bound, we arrive at

\displaystyle I(X,\Theta)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(\int d\theta\sqrt{F(\theta)f(\theta)^{2}+\dot{f}(\theta)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle-\int d\theta p(\theta)\log f(\theta).

(15)

This successfully recovers the functional form of the mutual information bound provided by Ref.
11
, proving that our SFI-based pointwise limits are the fundamental, trajectory-level building blocks of the average information bounds.

IV
QUANTUM GENERALIZATION

To extend these trajectory-level bounds to quantum systems, we map classical conditional probabilities to quantum measurements. In the quantum regime, the conditional probability
p(x|\theta)
is determined by the Born rule,
p(x|\theta)=\text{Tr}(\Pi_{x}\rho_{\theta})
, where
\{\Pi_{x}\}
represents an arbitrary Positive Operator-Valued Measure (POVM) and
\rho_{\theta}
is the parameter-dependent density matrix.

While classical average bounds utilize the standard Fisher Information, mapping the classical Stochastic Fisher Information (SFI),
\iota(x,\theta)
, to the quantum trajectory level requires careful consideration. This trajectory-level quantum analogue was recently formalized as the Conditional Quantum Fisher Information (CQFI). For a specific measurement outcome
x
, the CQFI is defined via the Symmetric Logarithmic Derivative (SLD) operator
L_{\theta}
as

f_{Q,x}(\theta)=\frac{\text{Tr}(\Pi_{x}L_{\theta}^{2}\rho_{\theta})}{\text{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}\Pi_{x})}

(16)

Unlike the standard QFI, which provides a global bound averaged over all possible measurement outcomes, the CQFI is a random variable that fluctuates along individual quantum trajectories.

By applying the mapping
\iota(x,\theta)\rightarrow f_{Q,x}(\theta)
, we can directly extend the structure of our classical theorems to single-shot quantum measurements. For instance, the functional
\Lambda_{1}(x,\theta)
in Theorem 1 naturally generalizes to its quantum counterpart when we substitute the classical SFI with the CQFI. This establishes that the local pointwise information gain (PMI) is strictly limited by this localized quantum metric. Because negative interference contributions reduce the instantaneous value of
f_{Q,x}(\theta)
, our upper bound on the PMI can dynamically tighten for specific quantum measurement outcomes. This tightening is a single-trajectory feature; when averaged over the ensemble to calculate the standard MI, the cross-term vanishes completely, thereby masking this fundamental, non-classical limit on single-shot information extraction.

Theorem 3.
Given a family of quantum states
\rho_{\theta}
(differentiable with respect to
\theta
), appearing with a prior probability distribution
p(\theta)
. For a measurement performed using a set of Positive Operator-Valued Measures (POVMs)
\{\Pi_{x}\}
, that yields a specific trajectory outcome with conditional probability
p(x|\theta)=\text{Tr}(\Pi_{x}\rho_{\theta})
.

If the parameter is guaranteed to lie inside a finite-size set, namely
\text{supp }p(\theta)\subseteq[a,b]
, the quantum PMI for the outcome
x
is bounded by

\displaystyle i(x,\theta)\leq

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{p(x|a)+p(x|b)}{p(x)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\int_{a}^{b}d\theta\left|\frac{p(x|\theta)}{p(x)}\right|\sqrt{\Lambda_{1,Q}(x,\theta)}\right),

(17)

where the functional is defined using the Conditional Quantum Fisher Information (CQFI) as
\Lambda_{1,Q}(x,\theta)=f_{Q,x}(\theta)
.

For an arbitrary differentiable probability distribution
p(\theta)
with infinite support, the quantum PMI is bounded by

i(x,\theta)\leq\log\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\theta\left|\frac{p(x|\theta)}{p(x)}\right|\sqrt{\Lambda_{2,Q}(x,\theta)}\right)+s(\theta),

(18)

where the functional is
\Lambda_{2,Q}(x,\theta)=f_{Q,x}(\theta)p(\theta)^{2}+\dot{p}(\theta)^{2}+2\partial_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta)\dot{p}(\theta)p(\theta)
and
s(\theta)=-\log p(\theta)
is the stochastic entropy of the prior.

Proof.
The proof follows directly from substituting the classical SFI
\iota(x,\theta)
with the CQFI
f_{Q,x}(\theta)
in the classical derivations of Theorems 1 and 2, justified by the saturation of the Cauchy-Schwarz bound applied to the Symmetric Logarithmic Derivative (SLD) operator
L_{\theta}
for a specific measurement outcome
x
, such that
\iota(x,\theta)\leq f_{Q,x}(\theta)
.

V
EXAMPLES

To illustrate the derived bounds, we apply our results to four prototypical systems: a classical stochastic process, a single qubit phase estimation, a real-time adaptive metrological system, and a quantum Maxwell demon under continuous monitoring.

V.1
Overdamped Langevin Dynamics

Consider a Brownian particle trapped in a one-dimensional harmonic potential, a standard model in stochastic thermodynamics. The system is governed by the overdamped Langevin equation

\dot{x}=-\theta x+\sqrt{2D}\xi(t)

(19)

where
D
is the diffusion coefficient,
\xi(t)
is white noise, and the unknown parameter
\theta
represents the trap stiffness. We assume the system has reached a steady state, yielding a Gaussian conditional probability distribution

p(x|\theta)=\sqrt{\frac{\theta}{2\pi D}}\exp\left(-\frac{\theta x^{2}}{2D}\right)

(20)

Because the stiffness
\theta
can take a continuous range of positive values, we apply Theorem 2. We define a differentiable prior distribution for the parameter,
p(\theta)
, such as a Gamma or Gaussian distribution restricted to
\theta>0
.

The stochastic Fisher information (SFI) for this trajectory state is calculated directly from its definition

\iota(x,\theta)=\left(\frac{\partial\log p(x|\theta)}{\partial\theta}\right)^{2}=\left(\frac{1}{2\theta}-\frac{x^{2}}{2D}\right)^{2}

(21)

Applying Theorem 2, the PMI
i(x,\theta)
is bounded by the integral of the SFI-dependent functional
\Lambda_{2}(x,\theta)

\Lambda_{2}(x,\theta)=\iota(x,\theta)p(\theta)^{2}+\dot{p}(\theta)^{2}+2\partial_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta)\dot{p}(\theta)p(\theta)

(22)

This demonstrates that for any single measurement of the particle’s position
x
, the information gained about the trap stiffness
\theta
is strictly constrained by the stochastic fluctuations of the system, represented by
\iota(x,\theta)
, and the sharpness of our prior knowledge
\dot{p}(\theta)
.

V.2
Single Qubit Phase Estimation

For a quantum generalization, consider a sensing protocol where a single qubit is initialized in the state
|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
and undergoes a unitary evolution
U=e^{-i\theta\sigma_{z}/2}
. The resulting density matrix is
\rho(\theta)=|\psi(\theta)\rangle\langle\psi(\theta)|
, where the unknown phase
\theta
is guaranteed to lie in a finite interval
[0,\pi/2]
, allowing us to directly apply the finite-support conditions of Theorem 1.

If we perform a projective measurement using a set of POVMs
\{\Pi_{x}\}
, the conditional probability of outcome
x
is given by the Born rule,
p(x|\theta)=\text{Tr}(\Pi_{x}\rho(\theta))
. Rather than relying on the ensemble Quantum Fisher Information (QFI), we evaluate the local sensitivity for a specific measurement outcome
x
using the Conditional Quantum Fisher Information (CQFI)

f_{Q,x}(\theta)=\frac{\text{Tr}(\Pi_{x}L_{\theta}^{2}\rho_{\theta})}{\text{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}\Pi_{x})},

(23)

where
L_{\theta}
represents the Symmetric Logarithmic Derivative (SLD) operator.

Because the system remains in a pure state undergoing unitary evolution, the incoherent (population-based) contribution to the CQFI strictly vanishes. The local parameter sensitivity is therefore driven entirely by the coherent rotation of the state vector and its interference with the chosen measurement basis
\Pi_{x}
.

By substituting the CQFI into the functional
\Lambda_{1}(x,\theta)
, Theorem 1 bounds the quantum PMI
i(x,\theta)
for a specific measurement outcome
x
. Crucially, because the CQFI is a trajectory-dependent random variable, it is susceptible to the interference cross-terms inherent to continuous quantum measurements. If a specific measurement outcome
x
results in destructive interference between the unitary evolution and the measurement basis, the CQFI dips.

Consequently, the upper bound on the quantum PMI
i(x,\theta)
tightens for that specific trajectory. This showcases a possible quantum advantage, at the level of individual quantum trajectories, destructive interference can restrict the single-shot extraction of classical information about the phase
\theta
to limits much tighter than those predicted by standard ensemble ones
18
.

VI
Possible applications

We now discuss some potential applications of the proposed bounds. One for real-time adaptive metrology and other for information thermodynamics at trajectory level.

VI.1
Real-Time Adaptive Metrology

To demonstrate the operational significance of the trajectory-level bounds, we consider a real-time adaptive quantum metrology protocol inspired by the framework of continuous environmental monitoring
1
;
5
. In continuous-variable phase estimation, a standard homodyne detection yields a stochastic photocurrent
I(t)
, which acts as our trajectory record
x(t)
. The measurement is defined by a set of POVMs
\Pi_{x(t)}(\Phi)
that depend on a controllable local oscillator phase
\Phi(t)
.

At any instant
t
, the amount of information gained about the unknown parameter
\theta
is bounded by Eq. (
4
), which relies on the instantaneous CQFI

f_{Q,x(t)}(\theta;\Phi)=f_{Q,x(t)}^{IC}(\theta)+f_{Q,x(t)}^{C}(\theta;\Phi)+f_{Q,x(t)}^{X}(\theta;\Phi).

(24)

Without feedback, the stochastic nature of the photocurrent causes the measurement basis to randomly drift relative to the optimal sensing direction. Consequently, the interference cross-term
f_{Q,x(t)}^{X}(\theta;\Phi)
will frequently become negative, denoting destructive interference between the unitary parameter encoding and the measurement back-action. This reduces the CQFI, thereby sharply tightening the upper bound on the PMI
\Lambda_{2,Q}(x,\theta)
and capping the local information extraction.

However, our bound provides a optimization metric for adaptive feedback. By utilizing the photocurrent
x(t)
to continuously update the local oscillator phase such that
\Phi_{opt}(t)=\arg\max_{\Phi}f_{Q,x(t)}(\theta;\Phi)
, the experimentalist can actively rotate the measurement basis to ensure the cross-term remains strictly positive (
f_{Q,x(t)}^{X}\geq 0
). In doing so, constructive interference is maintained along the entire single-shot trajectory, dynamically maximizing the trajectory-level bound on the PMI and allowing the estimator to approach the ultimate Heisenberg scaling even in the presence of stochastic unravelling.

VI.2
Information Thermodynamics at the Trajectory Level

The connection between our trajectory-level bounds and stochastic thermodynamics is made explicit by considering a continuously monitored quantum Maxwell’s demon
7
. Consider a quantum system in contact with a thermal bath at inverse temperature
\beta=(k_{B}T)^{-1}
, driven by a protocol parameterized by
\theta
. A demon performs continuous weak measurements, generating a stochastic trajectory
x
, and applies feedback to extract work
W_{ext}(x)
from the system.

According to the generalized Sagawa-Ueda fluctuation theorem
21
;
22
, the second law of thermodynamics at the single-trajectory level is modified by the information acquired by the demon. The extractable work is strictly bounded by the free energy change
\Delta F
and the PMI

\beta(W_{ext}(x)-\Delta F)\leq i(x,\theta).

(25)

By substituting our quantum generalization of Theorem 1 into this thermodynamic inequality, we establish a fundamental physical limit on the demon’s efficacy

\displaystyle\beta(W_{ext}(x)-\Delta F)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(\frac{p(x|a)+p(x|b)}{p(x)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\int_{a}^{b}d\theta\left|\frac{p(x|\theta)}{p(x)}\right|\sqrt{\Lambda_{1,Q}(x,\theta)}\right)

(26)

where
\Lambda_{1,Q}(x,\theta)=f_{Q,x}(\theta)
.

This result proves that the maximum thermodynamic work a Maxwell’s demon can extract during a specific quantum fluctuation is strictly constrained by the Conditional Quantum Fisher Information (CQFI) of that trajectory. If the demon’s continuous measurement induces destructive interference (a negative cross-term
f_{Q,x}^{X}(\theta)<0
), the upper bound on the PMI tightens, physically reducing the amount of work the demon can extract. Thus, our trajectory-level Fisher information metric acts not only as a bound for metrological precision but as a fundamental thermodynamic constraint on open quantum systems.

VII
Discussions and conclusions

In conclusion, we have presented two bounds for the PMI, in terms of the SFI. These results deepen the connection between information-theoretic quantities and stochastic dynamics, showing that the local information gain for a specific measurement outcome is constrained by the local parameter sensitivity. This trajectory-level perspective is vital because the inherent fluctuations of stochastic processes often prevent a direct local-global relation in standard parameter estimation.

By mapping the classical conditional probabilities to the Born rule, we established that the quantum analogue of the PMI is bounded by the CQFI. Unlike the standard QFI, which averages over all possible POVM outcomes to yield global limits like the quantum Cramér-Rao, the CQFI is a random variable evaluated for the specific instantaneous trajectory state.

Implementing the CQFI unveils trajectory-level quantum advantages in the information bounds. The spectral decomposition of the trajectory-level sensitivity has a cross-term contribution from the interference between population shifts and basis rotations, which can be negative. This signals interference that can tighten the upper bound on the extractable PMI for specific measurement outcomes. At the single-shot level, information extraction can achieve limits tighter than those predicted by ensemble averages, where this term strictly vanishes.

Experimental implementation is still an open question. Due to the definition of Eq. (
16
) being in terms of the ensemble state, the decomposition without the need of full state tomography is still unknown. Dynamically monitoring the CQFI may provide a blueprint for real-time adaptive metrology, allowing protocols to actively rotate measurement bases to suppress destructive interference and maximize the instantaneous PMI bound, if there is a way to compute the CQFI decomposition without full state tomography. In stochastic thermodynamics, where the surprisal is equivalent to the stochastic entropy, our trajectory-level bounds physically constrain the extractable work in single-shot quantum Maxwell’s demon scenarios.

Future works will aim to establish the tightest possible saturation of these bounds under specific continuous-monitoring dynamic constraints, and to avoid the costly tomography of states to estimate the CQFI by feedback measurement schemes.

Appendix A
Proof of PMI bounds

We begin by expressing the PMI using an arbitrary non-negative function
f(\theta)
such that
\text{supp }f(\theta)\supseteq\text{supp }p(\theta)
. The PMI can be written algebraically as

i(x,\theta)=\log\frac{p(x|\theta)f(\theta)}{p(x)}-\log f(\theta).

(27)

To bound the first term, we evaluate the argument of the logarithm. By imposing the boundary condition that
f(\theta)\rightarrow 0
for
\theta\rightarrow\pm\infty
, we can express the quantity at a specific
x
as bounded by the integral of its absolute derivative via the fundamental theorem of calculus

\frac{p(x|\theta)f(\theta)}{p(x)}\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\theta^{\prime}\left|\frac{d}{d\theta^{\prime}}\left(\frac{p(x|\theta^{\prime})f(\theta^{\prime})}{p(x)}\right)\right|.

(28)

We now expand the derivative inside the absolute value using the product rule. Recognizing that probability distributions are strictly non-negative, we can factor out
\frac{p(x|\theta^{\prime})}{p(x)}
. Utilizing the identity
\frac{\partial p(x|\theta^{\prime})}{\partial\theta^{\prime}}=p(x|\theta^{\prime})\partial_{\theta^{\prime}}\log p(x|\theta^{\prime})
and the definition of the stochastic Fisher information (SFI)
\iota(x,\theta^{\prime})=(\partial_{\theta^{\prime}}\log p(x|\theta^{\prime}))^{2}
, the exact absolute variation is determined by the square root of a functional

\left|\frac{d}{d\theta^{\prime}}\left(\frac{p(x|\theta^{\prime})f(\theta^{\prime})}{p(x)}\right)\right|=\left|\frac{p(x|\theta^{\prime})}{p(x)}\right|\sqrt{\Lambda(x,\theta^{\prime})},

(29)

where

\Lambda(x,\theta)\equiv\iota(x,\theta)f(\theta)^{2}+\dot{f}(\theta)^{2}+2\partial_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta)f(\theta)\dot{f}(\theta).

(30)

Substituting this back into our original PMI expression yields the generalized bound:

i(x,\theta)\leq\log\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\theta^{\prime}\left|\frac{p(x|\theta^{\prime})}{p(x)}\right|\sqrt{\Lambda(x,\theta^{\prime})}\right)-\log f(\theta).

(31)

A.1
Proof of Theorem 1

If the parameter is guaranteed to lie in a finite-size set such that
\text{supp }p(\theta)\subseteq[a,b]
, we evaluate the integral over this specific domain by choosing
f(\theta)
to be a boxcar function over
[a,b]
, such that
f(\theta)=1
for
\theta\in[a,b]
and
0
elsewhere.

To evaluate the generalized bound without squaring the derivative of a step function, we apply the triangle inequality
|A+B|\leq|A|+|B|
directly to the expanded absolute derivative prior to integration. This subadditivity allows us to decouple the stochastic Fisher information from the boundary evaluations:

\left|\frac{\partial p(x|\theta^{\prime})}{\partial\theta^{\prime}}f(\theta^{\prime})+p(x|\theta^{\prime})\dot{f}(\theta^{\prime})\right|\leq p(x|\theta^{\prime})\sqrt{\iota(x,\theta^{\prime})}f(\theta^{\prime})+p(x|\theta^{\prime})|\dot{f}(\theta^{\prime})|.

(32)

The absolute derivative of the step function yields Dirac delta functions at the boundaries,
|\dot{f}(\theta^{\prime})|=\delta(\theta^{\prime}-a)+\delta(\theta^{\prime}-b)
. When integrating this absolute variation over all space, these delta functions extract the exact boundary probability conditions
\frac{p(x|a)}{p(x)}+\frac{p(x|b)}{p(x)}
.

Within the open interval
(a,b)
, the derivative
\dot{f}(\theta^{\prime})
vanishes, and
f(\theta^{\prime})=1
. Evaluating the functional in this region isolates the stochastic Fisher information, yielding

\Lambda_{1}(x,\theta)=\iota(x,\theta).

(33)

Integrating the absolute variation across the entire domain, including the boundaries at
a
and
b
, we recover the properly bounded terms. Finally, because
f(\theta)=1
for
\theta\in[a,b]
, the penalty term
-\log f(\theta)
vanishes. This yields

i(x,\theta)\leq\log\left(\frac{p(x|a)+p(x|b)}{p(x)}+\int_{a}^{b}d\theta\left|\frac{p(x|\theta)}{p(x)}\right|\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}(x,\theta)}\right).

(34)

A.2
Proof of Theorem 2

Theorem 2 follows directly from the generalized bound by choosing the arbitrary function to be the exact prior probability distribution,
f(\theta)=p(\theta)
. Substituting
f(\theta)=p(\theta)
into our definition of
\Lambda(x,\theta)
immediately recovers

\Lambda_{2}(x,\theta)=\iota(x,\theta)p(\theta)^{2}+\dot{p}(\theta)^{2}+2\partial_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta)\dot{p}(\theta)p(\theta).

(35)

The second term of the bound,
-\log f(\theta)
, becomes
-\log p(\theta)
, which is by definition the surprisal or stochastic entropy
s(\theta)
. Therefore, for an arbitrary differentiable prior
p(\theta)
, the bound simplifies exactly to Theorem 2:

i(x,\theta)\leq\log\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\theta\left|\frac{p(x|\theta)}{p(x)}\right|\sqrt{\Lambda_{2}(x,\theta)}\right)+s(\theta).
