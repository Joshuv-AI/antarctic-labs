FKBP8 connects the Hsp70-Hsp90 chaperone machinery to the folding of membrane proteins
Authors: Man-Xi Ge, Ming-Zhi Wu, Jia Ji, Zhao-Peng Li, Zhongjian Bai, Jieyan He, Josefa Chuh, Yixiao Zhang, Jing Li, Zai-Rong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe folding of membrane protein cytoplasmic domains on the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) surface, and their coordination with transmembrane and exoplasmic regions, remains poorly understood. Through a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screen, we identified the ER-anchored FK506 binding protein 8 (FKBP8) as a chaperone essential for membrane protein folding and assembly. Using ABC transporters as model substrates, we show that FKBP8 cooperates with Hsp70-Hsp90 machinery to remodel nascent or misfolded cytosolic domains into their native conformations. Cryo-EM analysis reveals that FKBP8 employs a conserved hydrophobic ϕ94ϕ96ϕ97/ϕ158ϕ162 cluster to help form a large client-binding cavity within the FKBP8–Hsp90 complex that captures folding intermediates. FKBP8 deficiency, disruption of this cluster, or disease-associated mutations within FKBP8 abolish substrate maturation, leading to ER retention and degradation. Reconstitution with purified components demonstrates that FKBP8 and Hsp40-Hsp70-HOP-Hsp90 constitute a minimal machinery capable of restoring the native structure of a misfolded ABC transporter. These findings uncover a dedicated folding module at the ER-cytosol interface that bridges cytosolic chaperones with membrane protein quality control, suggesting a broad role for FKBP8 in safeguarding the biogenesis of complex membrane proteins.Subject terms: Endoplasmic reticulum, Chaperones, Cryoelectron microscopy

Introduction

Membrane proteins, including ion channels, transporters, receptors, and intramembrane proteases, are essential to cellular life, yet their activity depends on high-fidelity folding and assembly of complex polypeptides at the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)1. The main steps of membrane protein folding involve inserting transmembrane domains to establish correct topology, folding cytosolic and exoplasmic domains into their native conformations, and, for approximately half of membrane proteins, assembling into functional oligomers. While luminal chaperones like BiP and the calnexin/calreticulin cycle are well-characterized, the mechanisms governing the folding of the extensive cytosolic regions of membrane proteins remain elusive.

The cytosolic face of the ER is not simply an extension of the bulk cytosol, but a constrained folding environment shaped by membrane anchoring and ribosome density. High ribosome density and ribosome-ribosome cooperation accelerate local translation rates2–4, creating a crowded, two-dimensional protein synthesis surface5,6. For nascent membrane proteins, this environment is particularly challenging. Because nascent cytosolic domains are tethered to the membrane by transmembrane segments, their conformational search space is restricted, and their effective diffusion radius is significantly reduced compared to soluble proteins. This spatial confinement limits access to diffusely distributed cytosolic chaperones, such as Hsp70 and Hsp90, thereby increasing the risk of non-productive collapse or kinetic trapping during folding and domain assembly.

The constraints of the ER surface necessitate a mechanism to locally recruit and concentrate folding machinery at the membrane interface. While the insertion of transmembrane helices via the Sec61 translocon and associated factors is well-delineated1,7–10, we still lack a clear understanding of how the cell coordinates helix packing with the folding of membrane-anchored soluble domains9,11,12. Studies of several model substrates have shown that folding often proceeds cotranslationally through stepwise maturation and assembly of individual domains, frequently involving interactions with cytosolic chaperone systems13–15. However, how cytosolic chaperones are locally recruited to assist folding remains poorly understood. In addition, it remains unclear whether the ER employs dedicated, membrane-bound factors to orchestrate folding and assembly events or to refold proteins that have already succumbed to misfolding.

ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters exemplify this problem14,16–18. These proteins require the coordinated folding and assembly of cytosolic nucleotide-binding domains with transmembrane segments19–21. Studies of the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) have shown that folding proceeds through hierarchical domain maturation and interdomain assembly, and that disruption of these steps often results in misfolding and ER retention. While cytosolic systems, including Hdj222, Hsp7022–24, Hsp9017,25, and Hsp11026. are known to regulate CFTR folding and quality control27, mechanisms that coordinate their activity at the ER membrane remain insufficiently understood. This gap in knowledge extends to other critical transporters, such as ABCG2 (breast cancer resistance protein) and ABCG5-ABCG8. ABCG2 exports a broad range of chemotherapeutic agents and contributes to multidrug resistance in cancers21,28–32. Thus, ABCG2 is prioritized by both the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency for drug-drug interactions33 and has emerged as a therapeutic target for overcoming chemotherapy resistance. ABCG2 also mediates urate and itaconate excretion, and genetic mutations in ABCG2 underlie hyperuricemia and gout34–36. Likewise, the ABCG5-ABCG8 heterocomplex mediates sterol excretion and is essential for cholesterol homeostasis; mutations in either gene cause sitosterolemia and predispose individuals to cardiometabolic diseases20,37–39. Despite advances in structural characterization of these transporters, the mechanisms underlying their folding and dimeric assembly at the ER membrane remain poorly understood. Whether membrane-anchored chaperones participate in the folding and maturation of these ABC transporters has not been established.

The tail-anchored pseudo-peptidyl prolyl isomerase FKBP8 has been ascribed diverse and sometimes conflicting functions, including anti-apoptotic signaling40,41, mTOR inhibition42,43, mitophagy regulation44, and roles in ER-mitochondria contact sites45,46. It also associates with Hsp9017,47 and has previously been implicated in protein quality control48, but the physiological significance of this interaction has remained enigmatic due to the absence of well-defined client proteins.

In this work, we identify FKBP8 as a membrane-anchored chaperone that cooperates with the Hsp90/HOP/Hsp70/Hsp40 machinery to promote folding, refolding, and complex assembly of ABC transporters, including ABCG2 and ABCG5–ABCG8. We demonstrate that FKBP8 deficiency or disease-associated mutations49 disrupt ABC transporter maturation, leading to their misfolding and degradation. Our findings reveal an ER-anchored chaperone system that bridges cytosolic folding machinery with nascent membrane proteins, providing a mechanistic framework for understanding how the ER surface manages protein folding and quality control. By defining FKBP8’s role, this study uncovers a key pathway for maintaining membrane protein homeostasis and offers insight into diseases linked to transporter misfolding and ER stress.

Results

Genome-wide screening identified FKBP8 as a factor required for the biogenesis of ABCG2 and ABCG5-ABCG8

ABC transporters demand precise, domain-spanning folding and are acutely vulnerable to folding perturbations, positioning them as an ideal system to interrogate membrane protein biogenesis and quality control mechanisms. The homodimeric ATP-binding cassette transporter G2 (ABCG2, Fig. 1A) functions as an exporter for various chemotherapy drugs and metabolites. We performed a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screen (Figs. 1A, B) to identify genes involved in EGFP-ABCG2 biogenesis (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Data 1). The top hit, mannosyl-oligosaccharide glucosidase (MOGS; glucosidase I)—the first enzyme in the N-linked glycan processing pathway and essential for folding and trafficking of N-glycosylated proteins—validated the fidelity of the screen. To further evaluate candidate genes, we individually knocked out 26 selected hits. This analysis identified the tail-anchored peptidyl-prolyl isomerase (PPIase) FKBP8 as a critical factor for ABCG2 maturation (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Loss of FKBP8 abolished the mature, Endo H–resistant form of ABCG2 (Fig. 1D), leaving only an Endo H–sensitive, ER-localized immature species across multiple cell types (Supplementary Figs. 1B–D). In contrast, knockout of other candidates did not affect ABCG2 maturation and was therefore not pursued further. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that depletion of FKBP8 abolished plasma membrane-localized, mature ABCG2 and arrested it in the ER (Fig. 1E). When FKBP8 was re-expressed in FKBP8KO cells, we observed the reappearance of the Endo H-resistant ABCG2 population, indicating the rescued production of plasma membrane-localized, mature ABCG2. The function of FKBP8 is specific, as other PPIases such as FKBP51, FKBP52, FKBPL, and AIP failed to compensate for the FKBP8 knockout, even when integrated into the membrane via a tail-anchored transmembrane domain (TMD) (Supplementary Figs. 1E, F). Together, these experiments validated the screening result, demonstrating that FKBP8 is strictly and specifically required for ABCG2 biogenesis and maturation.

We examined whether FKBP8 is also involved in the biogenesis of other membrane proteins. We found that the ABCG8-ABCG5 heterodimeric complex relied on FKBP8 for biogenesis and trafficking to the plasma membrane, as FKBP8 depletion diminished both mature and immature forms of ABCG8 and substantially decreased the mature population of ABCG5 (Fig. 1F). Additionally, re-expressing FKBP8 in FKBP8KO cells restored the production of the mature ABCG5-ABCG8 complex (Fig. 1G). Collectively, these results indicate that FKBP8 is essential for the biogenesis of the ABCG5-ABCG8 complex at an early stage. However, FKBP8 is not required for the maturation of other tested membrane proteins, including ATP1A1 (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 1B), BAP31 (Supplementary Figs. 1B, C, E), LAT1 (Supplementary Fig. 1G), CD98hc (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Figs. 1D, G), as well as hERG, ABCC7/CFTR and ABCB1 (Supplementary Fig. 1H), suggesting substrate specificity.

Since FKBP8 had been identified as a Hsp90 co-chaperone interacting with hepatitis C protein NS5A47, we wondered whether it also plays a role in the maturation of ABC transporters together with FKBP8. First, we confirmed the interaction between FKBP8 and Hsp90 by co-immunoprecipitation and showed that it was disrupted by point mutations in the FKBD and TPR domains, consistent with earlier structural studies50 (Fig. 1H). To test whether Hsp90 activity is involved in the biogenesis of ABCG2 and ABCG5-ABCG8 complexes, we treated cells with the Hsp90 inhibitors geldanamycin, 17-AAG and ganetespib. We found that the ER-localized, immature ABCG2 species disappeared after treatment (Fig. 1I). Reduced expression of mature ABCG2 was also observed when cells were treated for 12–48 h with inhibitors (Fig. 1J). Flow cytometry analysis of plasma membrane localized ABCG2 after Hsp90 inhibition confirmed the reduced expression of mature ABCG2 in both A549 and JIMT cell lines (Fig. 1K and Supplementary Fig. 1I). Reduction of Hsp90 levels with short-interference RNA also resulted in defects in maturation (Supplementary Fig. 1J). Similar effects on expression and trafficking were observed for ABCG5-ABCG8 when Hsp90 was inhibited, albeit less pronounced compared to ABCG2 (Fig. 1L). Importantly, accumulation of non-glycosylated ABCG5 and ABCG8 species occurred after prolonged treatment, indicating severe misfolding of nascent polypeptides. Taken together, these results demonstrate that FKBP8 and Hsp90 are required for proper folding, trafficking and maturation of newly synthesized ABCG2, ABCG5, and ABCG8.

FKBP8 promotes drug excretion and confers multidrug resistance to cancer cells

Our discovery that ABCG2 biogenesis strictly depends on FKBP8 suggests that FKBP8 could be a potential target for overcoming ABCG2-mediated MDR. To explore this possibility, we generated a stable HEK293T cell line overexpressing ABCG2 to mimic cancer cells with elevated ABCG2 levels (Fig. 2A). We then knocked out FKBP8 in these cells and examined the excretion of mitoxantrone (MX), a chemotherapeutic drug with intrinsic blue fluorescence, allowing for flow cytometry analysis. To measure the total intracellular MX, cells were co-treated with MX and ko143, an ABCG2 inhibitor that blocks MX efflux (Fig. 2B, blue curve). In wild-type cells, MX fluorescence intensity was significantly lower in the absence of ko143 (Fig. 2B, WT, red curve), indicating efficient MX excretion by functional ABCG2. However, in FKBP8 knockout cells, fluorescence intensity remained high even without ko143 (Fig. 2B, bottom), suggesting a substantial reduction in MX excretion. These results demonstrate that FKBP8 knockout markedly impairs drug efflux.

Furthermore, we assessed the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of MX in both parental and ABCG2-overexpressing wild-type cell lines. Overexpression of ABCG2 increased MX resistance by approximately 40-fold (Figs. 2C, D). However, when FKBP8 was knocked out, drug resistance returned to levels observed in parental cells lacking ABCG2 overexpression. A similar effect was observed in HeLa cells, which naturally express endogenous ABCG2: FKBP8 knockout reduced drug efflux (Supplementary Fig. 2A), leading to increased sensitivity to MX (Supplementary Figs. 2B, C).

We further tested the drug response in tumor spheroids, a three-dimensional cell culture model that more accurately mimics the structure and function of real tissues or tumors than traditional two-dimensional cell cultures. Compared to wild-type tumor spheroid, the FKBP8 knockout spheroid is more sensitive to MX treatment, consistent with what was observed in 2D culture (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Interestingly, FKBP8 knockout cells exhibited a slower aggregation rate at both low and high seeding densities (Figs. 2E, F), suggesting that FKBP8 deficiency may alter the pattern of cell-cell interactions. To further understand this phenotype, we set up a cell migration assay to investigate the effects of FKBP8 knockout. The results indicated that FKBP8KO impairs cell migration compared to the wild-type cells (Supplementary Figs. 2E, F). In addition, we found that knockout of FKBP8 in HeLa cells significantly reduced their growth rate (Fig. 2G) and increased cellular sensitivity to hyperoxide treatment (Fig. 2H), suggesting that FKBP8 protects cells from oxidative stress.

Taken together, these findings suggest that FKBP8 is a crucial factor in ABCG2-mediated drug resistance and may serve as a potential therapeutic target for overcoming tumor MDR. In addition, cell growth, cell migration and spheroid formation rates were significantly slower in FKBP8-knockout cells compared to wild-type cells, suggesting that FKBP8 also plays an important role in tumor growth.

FKBP8 and Hsp90 are essential for the correct folding and dimerization of ABCG2 and ABCG5-ABCG8

To determine whether FKBP8 promotes the proper folding of ABCG2, we used nanobodies specific to the native conformation of ABCG2’s cytosolic domain, including the monomer-specific nanobodies Nb8 and Nb96, as well as the dimer-specific Nb1751 (Fig. 3A). These nanobodies were incubated with total lysate from wild-type or FKBP8KO HeLa cells expressing endogenous ABCG2. In wild-type cells, nearly all ABCG2, including both plasma membrane- and ER-localized populations, was recognized by these nanobodies (Fig. 3B, red arrowheads), demonstrating that ABCG2 is present in the native, dimeric conformation. However, when FKBP8 was knocked out, ABCG2 species remained in the unbound fraction, indicating that they failed to bind both monomer- and dimer-specific nanobodies (Fig. 3B, blue arrowheads). This result strongly suggests that the ER-arrested ABCG2 species adopt a non-native, monomeric conformation. Consistent with the notion of a misfolded conformation of these arrested species, we could show that these ER-localized ABCG2 species were rapidly degraded through the p97- and proteasome-dependent ER-associated degradation (ERAD) pathway (Supplementary Fig. 3A). In addition, the bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) experiment confirmed that ABCG2 was exclusively monomeric in FKBP8 knockout cells (Supplementary Fig. 3B).

To assess the conformational state of ABCG2’s extracellular domain (ECD), we utilized the monoclonal 5D3 antibody, which specifically recognizes the dimeric conformation of the ECD30,52 for affinity purification (Fig. 3C). These experiments showed that when FKBP8 was knocked out, nearly all ER-arrested immature ABCG2 species were in the unbound fraction (Fig. 3D, blue arrowheads), indicating little to no binding to 5D3 due to a non-native, monomeric ECD conformation. Given that both cytosolic and extracellular domains exhibited monomeric, misfolding conformations in our analysis, it is unlikely that the transmembrane domain remained in a dimeric state. Therefore, we conclude that FKBP8 is essential for the proper folding and dimerization of ABCG2’s cytosolic, extracellular, and transmembrane domains.

To assess the folding and assembly states of the ABCG8-ABCG5 complex, we used two conformation-specific monoclonal antibodies, 2E10 and 11F453, targeting ABCG8’s cytosolic domain. Affinity purification revealed that in FKBP8KO cells, ABCG8 was expressed at low levels, and was barely recognized by 2E10 and 11F4 (Fig. 3E, lanes 1–4 and 9–12), indicating impaired folding and rapid degradation (Supplementary Fig. 3C). In contrast, in the presence of FKBP8, ABCG8 levels increased (Fig. 3E, lanes 5 and 13) and mature ABCG8-ABCG5 complex appeared (Fig. 3E, lane 13), demonstrating successful conformational maturation, complex assembly and plasma membrane trafficking. Of note, the ER-localized ABCG5 population co-precipitated with ABCG8-specific 2E10 and 11F4 antibodies, suggesting formation of the ABCG5-ABCG8 complex at the ER (Fig. 3E, lanes 15 and 16).

Not surprisingly, inhibition of Hsp90 activity led to ER-trapped misfolded ABCG2 species, which were detected in the unbound fraction of the conformation-specific nanobody Nb17 (Fig. 3F, red arrowheads). A non-glycosylated species of ABCG2 also accumulated when the p97 inhibitor CB5083 was co-administered to inhibit the degradation of misfolded ER proteins, further supporting failed ABCG2 biogenesis (Fig. 3F, black arrowheads). Together, these results suggest that FKBP8 and Hsp90 together play a critical role in promoting the folding and quaternary organization of newly synthesized membrane proteins.

The FKBP8 function requires stable interaction with Hsp90 and transient client engagement

FKBP8 consists of an N-terminal unstructured region (residues 1–100), an FK506-binding domain (FKBD), a tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) domain with an additional helix 7 (H7) and its extension (H7e), and a tail-anchored TMD that anchors it to membrane (Fig. 4A). Truncation mutants expressed in FKBP8 knockout cells revealed that ABCG2 remained in a core-glycosylated, immature form when FKBP8 lacked residues 1–100, the FKBD, TPR, or H7 (Fig. 4B). Thus, these domains are all required for proper folding and dimerization of ABCG2. Compared with wild-type FKBP8, the TMD-deletion mutant restored approximately 64% of mature ABCG2 (Fig. 4B). Notably, the reduction in activity became more pronounced at lower expression levels (Supplementary Figs. 4A, B), implying that membrane localization is not strictly required for FKBP8 function but contributes to maximal folding efficiency.

FKBP8 variants are risk factors for diseases such as spina bifida, a developmental disorder that occurs when the neural tube fails to close properly during early embryogenesis. Consistently, we observed defects in ABCG2 maturation in the spina bifida-associated A251G variant49, as well as in the A241T FKBP8 variant identified in an individual with marfanoid habitus and intellectual disability, as reported in ClinVar (SCV000992651.1) (Figs. 4C, D). In addition, although FKBP8 exhibits a low level of Ca2+-dependent peptidyl-prolyl isomerase activity compared to FKBP1254, inhibition with GPI1046 did not impair ABCG2 maturation (Supplementary Fig. 4C), suggesting that its enzymatic activity is not required for this process.

Co-immunoprecipitation experiments revealed that FKBP8 mutants defective in chaperone activity co-precipitated with ER-trapped, misfolded ABCG2, whereas wild-type FKBP8 did not form stable complexes with ABCG2, regardless of its maturation or glycosylation status (Figs. 1H and 4E, blue arrowheads). These results suggest that FKBP8 interacts transiently with ABCG2 during the folding process and dissociates once maturation is complete. Among FKBP8 mutants unable to support ABCG2 folding, the mutant lacking residues 1–100 interacted with misfolded, core-glycosylated ABCG2 (Fig. 4E). Truncation mutants lacking FKBD, TPR, H7, or H7e failed to bind Hsp90, as did the point mutants D149N/D151N and A241T (Fig. 1H). Notably, the ΔTPR mutant failed to interact with both Hsp90 and ABCG2, whereas the ΔFKBD mutant bound misfolded ABCG2 but not Hsp90. These observations indicate that TPR, H7, and H7e domains are critical for FKBP8 interaction with Hsp90 and its substrates. Because Hsp90 ATPase activity is essential for ABCG2 maturation (Figs. 1I–K), we investigated whether FKBP8 modulates Hsp90 ATPase activity. However, recombinant FKBP8 neither stimulated nor inhibited Hsp90 ATPase activity, suggesting that FKBP8 promotes client folding without directly regulating the Hsp90 ATPase cycle (Supplementary Figs. 4D, E).

Cryo-EM structure of Hsp90-FKBP8 complex reveals a substrate-engaging cavity with a hydrophobic cluster crucial for chaperone activity

Since FKBP8 can modestly promote ABCG2 maturation without its TMD, we truncated the C-terminal region and identified residues 93–372 as the minimal functional FKBP8 construct (mFKBP8, Supplementary Fig. 5A). To gain structural insight into the mFKBP8–Hsp90 complex, we purified the complex and determined its structure by cryo–electron microscopy (cryo-EM) (Supplementary Figs. 5B–G and Supplementary Table 1). Local resolution analysis revealed that the Hsp90 dimer is well resolved at 3.5–4.5 Å (Supplementary Figs. 5H, I), whereas mFKBP8 appears more dynamic, with an average local resolution of ~6 Å (Fig. 5A). The AlphaFold-predicted structures of Hsp90 and FKBP8 can be well docked into the cryo-EM map separately (Fig. 5B). The overall arrangement of FKBP8 relative to Hsp90 closely resembles that observed in Hsp90 complexes with FKBP5255 or XAP256 (Supplementary Fig. 5J). mFKBP8 interacts with the C-terminal domains of both Hsp90 protomers through its helix H7e (Fig. 5C). However, the interface involving Hsp90’s C-terminal MEEVD motif and the FKBP8 TPR domain50 is poorly resolved, likely due to conformational flexibility.

Consistent with the structural analysis, mutation of the MEEVD-binding basic groove in TPR domain (K308A/R312A; Fig. 1H, lane 9), deletion of H7e (ΔH7e; Fig. 4E, lane 14), or deletion of the entire TPR domain (ΔTPR, Fig. 4E, lane 12) severely impaired or abolished FKBP8 interaction with Hsp90 and consequently blocked ABCG2 folding and maturation in cells (Fig. 1H, lane 4; Fig. 4B, lanes 8; Fig. 4E, lanes 5 and 7; Supplementary Fig. 6A). These findings indicate that the Hsp90-FKBP8 interaction is required for FKBP8 function in cells. To further examine this requirement, we tested whether mFKBP8 alone could promote ABCG2Cyt folding and solubility when co-expressed in E. coli, where the bacterial Hsp90 homolog HtpG did not interact with mFKBP8 (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Under these conditions, mFKBP8 increased the soluble fraction of ABCG2Cyt only modestly (Supplementary Fig. 6C, compare lanes 6 and 9), whereas the majority (~90%) of ABCG2Cyt accumulated inclusion bodies (Supplementary Fig. 6C, arrowhead), even though mFKBP8 was expressed at much higher level than ABCG2Cyt (Supplementary Fig. 6C, lanes 7–9). Notably, the soluble ABCG2Cyt fraction produced in the presence of wild-type FKBP8 bound Nb8 or Nb96, indicating that it had adopted a native conformation (Supplementary Fig. 6D). Together, these results indicate that FKBP8 alone exhibits only minimal chaperone activity in the absence of mammalian Hsp90. The Hsp90–FKBP8 complex forms a large client-binding cavity that is sealed at the FKBP8–Hsp90 interface but open toward the N- and M-domains of Hsp90, and is noticeably larger than cavities observed in Hsp90–FKBP52 or Hsp90–XAP2 complexes (Supplementary Fig. 5J). Within this space, side chains of FKBP8 residues L94, I96, L97, and hydrophobic residues L158 and L162 form a hydrophobic cluster facing the cavity interior (Fig. 5D), suggesting a potential role in substrate interaction or stabilization of folding intermediates. This idea is consistent with the result showing that the FKBP8 truncation mutant lacking residues 1-100, which include L94, I96 and L97, failed to rescue ABCG2 maturation (Figs. 4B, E).

Sequence analysis revealed that FKBP8’s residues 92–100 are highly conserved among different species (Fig. 5E), but unique among its human paralogs (Supplementary Fig. 6E). Truncation analysis found that residues 95–97 are critical for FKBP8’s activity, as truncating 1–95, 1–96, or 1–97 markedly impaired or abolished ABCG2 maturation (Fig. 5F, arrowheads). While single mutations of Leu94 or Asp95 did not affect ABCG2 maturation (Fig. 5G), Ile96 mutations (I96G or I96N) slightly reduced FKBP8’s ability to promote ABCG2 folding. Notably, double mutations at positions 94 and 96 (to Ala, Thr, Asn, Arg, or Glu) reduced FKBP8 function, whereas substitution with hydrophobic residues (Ile, Leu, or Met) preserved activity (Figs. 5G, H). In addition, substitution of Leu97 with Val did not affect ABCG2 maturation, whereas substitution with Trp or Asn caused a modest reduction in activity (Fig. 5I). In contrast, substitution with Gly, Glu, or Lys nearly abolished FKBP8 function (Figs. 5I, J), even though these mutants retained binding to Hsp90 and misfolded ABCG2, which remained trapped in the ER and was rapidly degraded (Supplementary Fig. 6F). Together, these findings suggest that the total hydrophobicity at positions 94, 96, and 97 (ϕ94ϕ96ϕ97) is crucial for FKBP8’s chaperone activity in promoting ABCG2 folding and maturation.

We next examined the role of L158 and L162, two hydrophobic residues positioned adjacent to the L94/I96/L97 stretch (Fig. 5D), as well as the nearby Y177. Substitution of L158 or L162 to hydrophilic residues markedly impaired ABCG2 maturation, similar to mutations at L94, I96, and L97, whereas mutation of Y177 had little effect (Fig. 5K). These results identify a contiguous hydrophobic surface formed by L94, I96, L97, L158, and L162 (designated as the ϕ94ϕ96ϕ97/ϕ158ϕ162) that is critical for FKBP8 function, as substitution of these residues with hydrophilic residues severely compromises its activity.

To determine whether this surface directly engages unfolded ABCG2 intermediates, we employed site-specific photo-crosslinking to map the client-contacting sites on FKBP8. Because the interaction between wild-type FKBP8 and ABCG2 is highly transient, we used the substrate-trapping mutant FKBP8-L97K (or FKBP8-I94R/L96R) to stabilize the complex. Individual FKBP8 variants containing a single amber codon were co-expressed with an engineered BpaRS and its cognate suppressor tRNACUA, enabling site-specific incorporation of photoreactive p-Benzoyl-L-phenylalanine (Bpa). Following UV irradiation, FKBP8 was immunoprecipitated under denaturing conditions and analyzed by immunoblotting to detect cross-linked ABCG2 species. Crosslinked ABCG2 products were detected at positions I96 or L97 (Fig. 6A, lanes 14 and 24), two residues within the hydrophobic cluster, indicating that the surface directly contacts unfolded ABCG2. Additional crosslinking sites were identified at R116, V118, K119, G148, C150, D151, and K225 (Figs. 6A–C). These residues are located on loops and helical surfaces facing the substrate-binding cavity and the Hsp90 lumen (Fig. 6D). However, mutation of these positions individually had little or only marginal effects on ABCG2 folding and maturation (Fig. 6E), suggesting that client binding alone is insufficient to support productive folding.

Together, these results suggest that FKBP8 engages client proteins through multiple contacts, but that the ϕ94ϕ96ϕ97/ϕ158ϕ162 hydrophobic cluster provides a critical substrate-holding surface. This hydrophobic patch may stabilize partially folded intermediates—by increasing residence time or hydrophobic contact area—thereby enabling efficient conformational sampling during client folding. Thus, while other sites contribute auxiliary contacts, the hydrophobic cluster appears to form the primary productive client-binding site required for FKBP8 chaperone activity.

mFKBP8 promotes folding of the ABCG2 cytosolic domain in vitro

To investigate FKBP8’s functions in vitro, we purified recombinant mFKBP8 from bacteria (Supplementary Figs. 7A, B). We then tested its ability to promote structural maturation of newly synthesized ABCG2Cyt using a rabbit reticulocyte lysate (RRL)-based in vitro translation (IVT) system which does not contain endogenous FKBP8 (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. 7C). Without mFKBP8 or in the presence of mFKBP8 mutant L97K, ABCG2Cyt was produced but failed to bind to any of the nanobodies tested (Supplementary Fig. 7D, blue arrowheads), suggesting improper folding. In contrast, ABCG2Cyt translated in the presence of wild-type mFKBP8 was efficiently captured by Nb8 or Nb96, but not Nb17 (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Fig. 7D, red arrowheads), indicating successful folding and conformational maturation, but unsuccessful dimerization. These results suggest that FKBP8 promotes the folding but not dimerization of ABCG2Cyt. Notably, ABCG2Cyt (Supplementary Fig. 7E) and the transmembrane domain (ABCG2TMD, residue 370–655, Supplementary Fig. 7F) expressed in cells failed to form dimers, indicating that while the transmembrane domain is essential for dimerization, it is not sufficient on its own.

To determine whether an additional oligomerization event downstream of the ABCG2 cytosolic domain is required for its quaternary structure formation, we fused a homo-dimerizing coiled-coil (CC) motif of FLICE-associated huge protein (FLASH)57 to the C-terminus of ABCG2Cyt, generating ABCG2Cyt-CC (Fig. 7C). We translated ABCG2cyt-CC in RRL containing either wild-type or L97K mFKBP8 and assessed its dimerization. Affinity purification demonstrated that the dimer-specific antibody Nb17 efficiently depleted ABCG2Cyt-CC from RRL supplemented with wild-type mFKBP8, whereas depletion was not observed with the L97K mutant (Fig. 7D). This strongly suggests that the native, dimeric conformation of ABCG2Cyt was successfully formed. Collectively, these results indicate that full-length ABCG2 dimerization requires the interactions downstream of the cytosolic domain, i.e., the transmembrane and extracellular domains.

Additionally, we found that pre-treating RRL with geldanamycin, an Hsp90 inhibitor, prevented ABCG2Cyt folding and dimerization, even in the presence of the CC motif and wild-type mFKBP8 (Fig. 7E and Supplementary Fig. 7G). This suggests that Hsp90 activity is required for the proper folding of ABCG2Cyt.

Next, we investigated whether ABCG2Cyt can be folded post-translationally. To this end, we inhibited translation in the IVT system using cycloheximide, ensuring that nascent, unfolded ABCG2Cyt polypeptide was produced in the absence of FKBP8 (Fig. 7F). We then incubated the reaction mixture with recombinant mFKBP8 at either 0 °C or 30 °C for different time intervals and assessed ABCG2Cyt maturation via nanobody binding. At 0 °C, ABCG2Cyt failed to bind the nanobodies Nb8 or Nb96, indicating that FKBP8-mediated folding is temperature-dependent (Fig. 7G). At 30 °C, approximately half of ABCG2Cyt bound to Nb8 within 10 min, and after 30 min, complete conformational maturation was observed. Furthermore, we demonstrated that nascent ABCG2Cyt produced in the E. coli PURExpress system could be folded post-translationally by RRL containing mFKBP8. (Fig. 7H). Taken together, these results strongly support that the nascent ABCG2Cyt polypeptide can be folded into native structure post-translationally, with FKBP8 and Hsp90 playing critical roles in this process.

Recombinant FKBP8-Hsp90 repairs terminally misfolded full-length ABCG2 into native, dimeric structure in the membrane

To systematically identify chaperones involved in FKBP8/Hsp90-mediated protein folding, we performed affinity purification–mass spectrometry in both HEK293T and HeLa cells to identify factors associated with FKBP8 (Supplementary Fig. 8A). In addition to Hsp90, the chaperones Hsc70, Hsp70-1B and the Hsp70 cochaperone BAG2 ranked among the top hits in both cell types (Supplementary Fig. 8B). Because the E47A mutation stabilizes the closed conformation of Hsp90 and its associated chaperone-substrate complex58,59, we next used two-step immunoprecipitation to isolate the FKBP8-Hsp90-E47A complex and examine whether it interacts with additional chaperones (Supplementary Fig. 8C). The purified complex associated with Hsc70, Hsp40 (DNAJA1 and DNAJB1), and HOP, indicating that FKBP8-Hsp90 assembles into a multi-chaperone complex with these components (Supplementary Fig. 8D). This is consistent with the established model in which Hsp90 receives clients from the Hsp70-Hsp40 system via the adapter HOP60–62. Notably, HOP knockout markedly impaired endogenous ABCG2 level, confirming that HOP is required for efficient ABCG2 biogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 8E). Furthermore, in FKBP8 knockout cells, we observed markedly increased association of several co-chaperones with Hsp90, including p23, FKBP51, and AHA1 (Supplementary Fig. 8F), whereas the HOP–Hsp90 interaction remained unchanged. This pattern suggests a compensatory redistribution of Hsp90 co-chaperones in the absence of FKBP8 or an increased folding burden that enhances Hsp90 engagement with co-chaperones and client proteins. The unchanged HOP-Hsp90 interaction is consistent with HOP acting upstream of FKBP8, mediating substrate transfer from Hsp70 to Hsp9060,61.

We next tested whether the misfolded ABCG2Cyt can be handled by Hsc70-Hsp40. When expressed in bacteria, most ABCG2Cyt was found in inclusion bodies (Supplementary Fig. 6C). This allowed us to purify the urea-denatured, 6xHis-tagged ABCG2Cyt, followed by urea removal through dialysis. After centrifugation to eliminate aggregates, we obtained misfolded but solubilized ABCG2Cyt (Fig. 8A). We first incubated misfolded ABCG2Cyt with Hsc70 and DNAJB1 and heat-shocked it at 42 °C. Affinity purification of ABCG2Cyt revealed co-precipitated Hsc70 and DNAJB1 (Fig. 8B), indicating the generation of an ABCG2Cyt-loaded Hsc70- DNAJB1 complex60,61.

We next examined whether the misfolded ABCG2Cyt can be refolded into its native conformation by incubating the complex with RRL. When supplemented with mFKBP8, RRL promoted the formation of monomeric, folded ABCG2Cyt, as evidenced by binding to the nanobodies Nb8 and Nb96, but not to the dimer-specific nanobody Nb17 (Fig. 8C). In contrast, folding failed in the absence of mFKBP8 (Fig. 8C) or when Hsc70-K71E, an ATPase-deficient mutant, was used instead of wild-type Hsc70 (Supplementary Fig. 8G). These results indicate that FKBP8 and the Hsp70 ATPase activity are required for the refolding of misfolded ABCG2Cyt into its native monomeric state.

In FKBP8KO cells, full-length ABCG2 was misfolded, monomeric, and rapidly degraded by ERAD (Figs. 3B, D and Supplementary Fig. 3A). To test whether FKBP8 could correct the structure of misfolded ABCG2 containing its transmembrane domain, we isolated semi-permeabilized cells (SPC) and ER-derived rough microsomes (RM) from FKBP8KO cells, both of which contained misfolded ABCG2 (Fig. 8D). When these preparations were incubated with RRL supplemented with mFKBP8, folded ABCG2 with a mature dimeric cytosolic domain was detected, as evidenced by core-glycosylated ABCG2 binding to Nb17 (Fig. 8E, Supplementary Figs. 8H, I, arrowhead).

We then tested whether purified molecular chaperones were capable of refolding and dimerization of full-length ABCG2. We found that incubation with Hsc70, DNAJB1, HOP, Hsp90, and mFKBP8 in the presence of ATP was sufficient to re-fold and dimerize the cytosolic moiety of full-length membrane-bound ABCG2, albeit less efficiently than RRL with mFKBP8 (Figs. 8F, G and Supplementary Fig. 8J, arrowheads). Of note, DNAJA1 also facilitated the folding of ABCG2, suggesting that DNAJA1 and DNAJB1 have similar functions (Supplementary Fig. 8K). Importantly, no folded ABCG2 was detected in the absence of ATP or when Hsp90-E47A, an ATPase-deficient mutant, was used (Fig. 8H), implying that ATP hydrolysis of both Hsp70 and Hsp90 is essential for the folding process.

The successful folding and dimerization of the cytosolic moiety of full-length ABCG2 strongly suggest that its transmembrane regions also achieve a native, dimeric structure. To test this, we used the 5D3 antibody, which specifically recognizes a dimeric epitope in the ER luminal domain (Fig. 3C). Following incubation with the chaperone cascade (Hsc70, DNAJB1, HOP, Hsp90, and mFKBP8), or with RRL and mFKBP8, a substantial amount of core-glycosylated ABCG2 was recognized by 5D3 (Fig. 8I), confirming the formation of the luminal dimeric epitope. The maturation of both the cytosolic and luminal domains suggests the successful assembly of ABCG2’s transmembrane and extracellular regions. Taken together, our results indicate that the Hsc70-Hsp40-Hop-Hsp90-FKBP8 chaperone machinery is sufficient to promote folding and assembly of dimeric ABCG2 at the ER membrane.

Discussion

Through genome-wide screening and multiple conformations as well as dimer-specific antibodies, we uncovered a bipartite chaperone function of FKBP8 in anchoring Hsp90 at the ER membrane surface and facilitating the folding and assembly of two substrates, ABCG2 and ABCG8-ABCG5. Our in vitro reconstitution assays further demonstrate that the FKBP8-Hsp90 complex can repair misfolded cytosolic domains of ABCG2, thereby enabling the proper assembly of transmembrane and extracellular domains into native dimeric structures. These findings establish a role for the FKBP8-Hsp90 complex in de novo protein folding and complex assembly, as well as correcting misfolding at the ER membrane for a specific group of substrates (Fig. 8J). Given the essential roles of ABCG2 and ABCG8-ABCG5 in exporting urate, itaconate, chemotherapeutic drugs, and sterols, our study highlights the physiological importance of FKBP8 and Hsp90 in metabolite excretion and in drug metabolism and resistance in cancer cells.

During biogenesis, the N-terminal, soluble cytosolic domain of ABCG2 (ABCG2Cyt) is synthesized before the first transmembrane domain (TMD), which later serves as the signal sequence that targets the ribosome-nascent chain complex to the ER membrane. Thus, cytosolic chaperones, including Hsp40/Hsp70/HOP/Hsp90, together with soluble FKBP8 lacking the TMD, could assist the folding of ABCG2Cyt prior to ER targeting. Consistent with this idea, we found that soluble mFKBP8 facilitates conformational maturation of ABCG2Cyt in the fully soluble RRL system (Fig. 7). After ER targeting, however, most ABCG2 mRNAs become associated with ER-bound ribosomes through the Sec61 translocon, restricting translation of ABCG2Cyt to the cytoplasmic surface of the ER membrane. Anchoring FKBP8 to the ER via its C-terminal TMD likely increase its proximity to nascent ABCG2Cyt, thereby promoting more efficient engagement and folding (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Figs. 4A, B).

Our data suggest that FKBP8 facilitates substrate folding and refolding when interacting with Hsp90. Since Hsp90 contains two client-binding sites within its dimeric cleft63, FKBP8’s hydrophobic cluster might provide a third polypeptide binding site, enhancing conformational sampling efficiency. A recent cryo-EM structure of the FKBP52–Hsp90 complex revealed that FKBP52 promotes GR maturation by inducing an approximately 45° rotation of the ligand-bound GR, which may facilitate subsequent dimerization of the ligand-binding domain (LBD)55. While the FKBP8-Hsp90 complex shares a similar overall architecture of Hsp90 and FKBP8 subunits with other FKBP-Hsp90 complexes, we propose that FKBP8 possesses a distinct chaperone or protein-folding activity. First, the expression of many FKBP proteins in FKBP8 knockout cells failed to restore ABCG2 maturation (Supplementary Figs. 1E, F). Second, FKBP8 exhibited chaperone activity, although weak, even in the absence of Hsp90 (Supplementary Figs. 6C, D). The complete disruption of ABCG2 conformation upon FKBP8 deficiency indicates its vital role in the folding process (Supplementary Figs. 1B, C, D, G). In contrast, GR is in a folded and ligand-bound state even prior to its transfer to the Hsp90-FKBP5255, suggesting little folding activity of FKBP52. Therefore, we propose that FKBP8 exhibits chaperone activity distinct from that of other FKBP family proteins. Mechanistically, we speculate that FKBP8’s FKBD and TPR contribute to substrate holding, likely stabilizing partially folded cytosolic domains. However, further structural studies, such as cryo-EM analysis for substrate-engaged Hsp90-FKBP8 complex, are needed to determine the mechanism.

Beyond ABCG2 and ABCG5/ABCG8, FKBP8-Hsp90 may have other substrates. Although we screened additional ABC transporters and ion channels, including CFTR and hERG (Supplementary Fig. 1H), none of them required FKBP8 for biogenesis in our cultured cells, in contrast with earlier results17,64–66. However, given that FKBP8 depletion slows cell growth and migration (Fig. 2G and Supplementary Figs. 2E, F), and that cardiac-specific FKBP8 depletion leads to accumulation of misfolded protein aggregates and elevated ER stress markers48, FKBP8 may play a broader role in folding proteins localized at the ER. Further studies are needed to elucidate the full substrate spectrum of FKBP8.

Our findings indicate that ABCG2’s cytosolic domain must fold correctly before its transmembrane and extracellular domains can assemble. However, the cytosolic domain alone cannot dimerize without its transmembrane region or an appended dimeric coiled-coil motif (Figs. 7B, D). This suggests that complex assembly is a cooperative process requiring tripartite interactions among cytosolic, transmembrane, and extracellular domains. Given that purified Hsp40, Hsc70, HOP, Hsp90, and FKBP8 can refold misfolded ABCG2 cytosolic domains and facilitate transmembrane and extracellular domain assembly within microsomes in an ATP-dependent manner, it will be important to investigate whether dedicated chaperones or assembly factors facilitate assembly events within the lipid bilayer and in the ER.

Hsp90 and its bacterial homolog HtpG have been shown to promote Hsp70/Hsp40/NEF-mediated refolding of luciferase62,67. Our study expands this role by identifying both de novo folding and refolding functions of Hsp90-FKBP8 in processing physiologically relevant substrates, including ABCG2, a key transporter upregulated in many cancers in response to chemotherapy29 and associated with poor clinical outcomes68. The rapid proliferation of cancer cells imposes a heavy burden on the protein folding machinery, increasing the risk of a proteotoxic crisis69. Cancer cells thus depend on robust proteostasis mechanisms70. Our discovery that Hsp90-FKBP8 can refold misfolded ABCG2 (Fig. 8) suggests that this chaperone complex may contribute to multidrug resistance in cancer (Fig. 2). Since both protein synthesis and degradation are energy- and time-intensive, the ability of Hsp90-FKBP8 to refold misfolded proteins not only alleviates proteotoxic stress but also enhances proliferation efficiency, particularly in rapidly dividing cancer cells. In summary, our study identified a concept for the folding of ABC transporters at the ER membrane in which a PPIase links the chaperone machinery to the membrane and the client protein to allow spatially restricted folding and assembly to occur.

Protein expression and purification

Recombinant human mFKBP8, Hsp90α, Hsp90α-E47A, Hsc70, Hsp40 (DNAJB1 and DNAJA1), HOP and p23 containing a N-terminal 6xHis tag were expressed using E. coli BL21(DE3) (Tiangen Biotech, Cat#CB105). Cells were lysed using a French Press (JN-02C, JNBIO), and insoluble debris was separated by centrifugation at 4 °C for 30 min at 21,000 × g. The supernatant was incubated end-over-end with 5 mL Ni Sepharose (Cytiva, Cat#17526801) for 30 min at 4 °C. The protein was eluted using IMAC-B buffer (40 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 300 mM imidazole). Eluted Hsc70, Hsp40 and p23 were directly dialyzed against IMAC-A buffer. mFKBP8 was diluted fivefold using SP-A buffer (30 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 5% glycerol, 10 mM β-ME) and loaded onto a 1 mL IexCap SP 6FF column (Smart-Lifescience, Cat#SI003C11). mFKBP8 was eluted with a linear gradient of SP-B buffer (30 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 1 M KCl, 5% glycerol, 10 mM β-ME). Buffer exchange was performed on a HiTrap 5 mL Desalting Column (Cytiva, Cat#71715400) equilibrated in storage buffer (30 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 100 mM KCl, 5% glycerol). Hsp90 and HOP were diluted tenfold using Q-A buffer (40 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 5% glycerol, 10 mM β-ME) and loaded onto a 1 mL IexCap Q 6FF column (Smart-Lifescience, Cat#SI001C11), followed by elution with a linear gradient of Q-B buffer (40 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 1 M KCl, 5% glycerol, 10 mM β-ME). The concentrations of all purified recombinant proteins were determined using A280 and their respective extinction coefficients.

To generate Hsp90α(E47A)-mFKBP8 complex, Hsp90-E47A and mFKBP8 (containing a C-terminal TwinStrepⅡ tag) were further purified using Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) separately. For complex formation, 10 µM Hsp90α (E47A) and 30 µM mFKBP8 were mixed in buffer containing 500 mM KCl, 2 mM ATP, 5 mM MgCl₂, and 5 mM β-ME, and incubated on ice for 1 h. The mixture was subsequently incubated with Streptactin Beads 4FF (Smart-Lifesciences Cat# SA053005) at 4 °C for 2 h. The complex was eluted using size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) buffer (30 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 100 mM KCl, 2 mM β-ME) supplemented with 10 mM desthiobiotin (DSB). The eluted product was concentrated to 500 µL and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) to remove unbound proteins. The fractions containing the Hsp90α(E47A)-mFKBP8 complex were collected for negative-stain EM and cryo-EM sample preparation.

Nanobodies and antibodies in this study were purified using Expi293F cells. Briefly, Expi293F cells were transfected with plasmids encoding nanobodies or antibodies. Sodium butyrate was added to cells 16–24 h post-transfection to enhance expression, and cells continued to grow for 3–5 days at 30 °C. The cell culture medium was collected and filtered through a sterile 0.45 μm filter. For nanobody purification, the culture medium was pre-incubated with BioLock to block biotin, then incubated with Strep-Tactin XT Sepharose (Cytiva, Cat#29401324) and washed with PBS. For antibody purification, the culture medium was incubated with Protein G Resin (Genscript, Cat#L00209) and washed with 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, followed by washing with 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0. The nanobody- and antibody-conjugated beads were finally stored in PBS at 4 °C.

EM sample preparation and data collection

First, the homogeneity of the Hsp90α(E47A)-mFKBP8 complex negatively stained with 0.2% (w/v) uranyl acetate was verified using a Tecnai T12 Transmission Electron Microscope (FEI). For cryo-EM, 4 µL of the sample, measured at a concentration of 3.9 mg/mL by NanoDrop, was applied to a glow-discharged 200 mesh R2/1 UltrAuFoil grids (Quantifoil). After 5 s incubation, the grids were blotted for 2 s with a blot force of −2 in the Vitrobot Mark IV chamber (Thermo Fisher Scientific) set at 4 °C and 100% humidity, then rapidly plunged into pre-cooled liquid ethane.

The cryo-EM data were collected using a 300 kV Titan Krios G4 microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Biocontinuum K3 direct electron detector and a GIF filter (Gatan). Movies were recorded at a nominal magnification of ×81,000, with a calibrated pixel size of 1.055 Å per pixel. The defocus range was set between −1.2 and −2.4 µm. Beam-image shift in the EPU software package was employed to accelerate data acquisition. The exposure time was 2.2 s, fractionated into 40 frames (55 ms per frame), with a dose rate of 25 electrons per pixel per second (~1.235 electrons/Å² per frame) and a total accumulated dose of 49.41 electrons/Å².

Cryo-EM image processing and reconstruction

The collected movie stacks were gain-normalized, dose-weighted, and motion-corrected using MotionCor274. Contrast Transfer Function (CTF) parameters were estimated with CTFFIND475. A total of 320,536 particles from 400 movies were picked with Gautomatch (https://github.com/JackZhang-Lab/Gautomatch) and extracted with a box size of 256 pixels in RELION76. Following three rounds of 2D classification, 113,043 particles were subjected to ab-initio reconstruction in CryoSPARC77. 71,339 particles from the ab-initio class with well-defined features were selected and reconstructed using Non-Uniform Refinement. 3D Classification and local refinement were then performed to identify particles with stronger mFKBP8 density. To further improve the density of mFKBP8, an additional 2000 movie stacks were collected and processed using the same RELION workflow, yielding 340,643 particles. These particles, together with previously refined 41,814 particles, were subjected to Heterogenous Refinement. Particles from the classes displaying strong mFKBP8 density were further refined using Non-Uniform Refinement, resulting in a 3.86 Å map. To enhance the mFKBP8 density, three repeats of 3D Classification were performed with a local mask on mFKBP8. A total of 67,866 particles were finally selected from these classifications and subjected to Non-Uniform Refinement, producing an Hsp90α(E47A)-mFKBP8 complex map at a resolution of 4.09 Å. A local filter was subsequently applied to improve the map quality.

Model building and refinement

The initial structure of Hsp90α(E47A) was predicted using AlphaFold378, and the mFKBP8 structure was obtained from the AlphaFold database entry AF-Q14318-2-F1. The Hsp90α(E47A) model was fitted into the Hsp90α(E47A)-mFKBP8 density map using UCSF Chimera79. The residues were manually adjusted in Coot80 based on the density map, and ATP ligands were added. The structure was then refined through real-space refinement in PHENIX81. Following several iterative cycles of refinement in Coot and PHENIX, the structure of Hsp90α(E47A) exhibited satisfactory validation statistics. For mFKBP8, the predicted structure was fitted into the map and merged with the refined Hsp90α(E47A) structure. Model-building and refinement statistics of Hsp90α(E47A)-mFKBP8 complex are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Protein folding and assembly at semi-permeabilized cells (SPC)

FKBP8KO HEK293T cells were split into 6-well plates and transfected with 3xFLAG-ABCG2. After incubation, cells were spun at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C using a tabletop centrifuge. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet (semi-permeabilized cells) was resuspended in KHM buffer (30 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 110 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2).

For in vitro folding, 10 μL SPCs were incubated with 25 μL of RRL or the indicated proteins at 30 °C for 1 h, with a total reaction volume of 50 μL. After incubation, SPCs were spun again at 20,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was saved for western blot analysis, and the pellet was resuspended in 500 μL of NHM buffer containing 1% DDM and incubated on ice for 15 min. Cell debris was cleared by centrifugation, and the supernatant was added to ABCG2 nanobodies for immunoprecipitation.

Protein folding and assembly in rough microsomes (RM)

The FKBP8KO HEK293T cells expressing 3xFLAG-ABCG2 in a 15 cm dish (90% confluency) were collected by scraping and pipetting in 2 mL ice cold PBS and transferred to 15 mL conical tubes. Cells were washed again with ice-cold PBS and resuspended in 2 mL hypotonic buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 10 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2) containing 1x protease inhibitor cocktail. After incubating on ice for 10 minutes, cells were lysed by 20 passages through a 25-gauge needle using a 2 mL syringe. The cell lysate was then centrifuged at 3500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C twice to remove nuclei. The resulting post-nuclear supernatant was transferred to Beckman tubes for ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g (with Beckman TLA100.3 rotor) for 60 min. After removing the top cytosolic fraction thoroughly, the microsome pellet was resuspended in KHM (30 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 110 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2) buffer. Microsomes were incubated with RRL or recombinant proteins at 30 °C for 1 h. The remaining steps of in vitro folding are the same as those for SPC.

Affinity-coupled mass spectrometry sample preparation

This mass spectrometry experiment was performed once (biological replicate n = 1). For each condition (empty vector or Myc-FKBP8), three independent technical IP replicates were analyzed, resulting in a total of six MS samples.

For each condition, cells from two 10 cm dishes of FKBP8KO HEK293T or FKBP8KO HeLa cells expressing either empty vector or Myc-tagged FKBP8 were harvested and combined prior to lysis. Cells were lysed in 2 mL of 1% TX-100 NHM buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl₂) supplemented with 1× protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were incubated on ice for 15 min and clarified by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C using a tabletop centrifuge.

The clarified lysate was equally divided into three aliquots, which were independently subjected to anti-Myc immunoprecipitation using anti-Myc affinity gel (Beyotime Biotechnology, Cat#P2285) for 2 h at 4 °C with end-over-end rotation, generating three technical replicates per condition. Beads were washed three times with NHM buffer containing 0.1% TX-100, and bound proteins were eluted with 30 μL 1.5× SDS-PAGE sample buffer.

The entire 30 μL immunoprecipitated eluate was resolved on 4–20% SDS-PAGE gels using an EPS601 electrophoresis system (GE Healthcare) at 180 V for 45 min. The entire gel lane corresponding to each sample was excised for in-gel digestion. Protein bands were visualized by Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining to guide gel processing.

Excised gel pieces were destained with 30% acetonitrile (ACN) in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate until clear. Gel pieces were then dehydrated with 100% ACN and dried by vacuum centrifugation. For reduction, samples were incubated with 10 mM DTT at 37 °C for 60 min. After removal of the reducing solution, alkylation was performed using 60 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature in the dark for 60 min. Gel pieces were subsequently washed twice with 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate for 15 min each, followed by dehydration with 100% ACN and vacuum drying.

Proteins were digested in-gel with trypsin (10 ng/μL) at 37 °C for approximately 20 h. The resulting peptide solution was collected, and residual peptides were extracted from gel pieces using extraction buffer (60% ACN/0.1% TFA) with sonication for 15 min. Extracts were combined with the initial digest supernatant and dried by vacuum centrifugation.

Peptides were reconstituted in 2% formic acid in water (200 μL), desalted, and lyophilized. Dried peptides were finally reconstituted in 20 μL of 0.1% formic acid prior to LC–MS/MS analysis. Peptide concentrations were estimated by absorbance at 280 nm using a Nanodrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Indexed retention time (iRT) calibration peptides were spiked into each sample before LC–MS/MS analysis.

LC–MS/MS acquisition

Peptides were analyzed using an Evosep One liquid chromatography system (Evosep Biosystems, Denmark) coupled to a timsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) operated in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. Ion mobility MS spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 100–1700. Up to four isolation windows were defined for each 100 ms TIMS scan across the m/z–ion mobility plane. During PASEF-MS/MS acquisition, collision energy was linearly ramped from 20 eV at 1/K0 = 0.85 Vs/cm² to 59 eV at 1/K0 = 1.30 Vs/cm².

Mass spectrometry data processing and statistical analysis

DIA data were analyzed using Spectronaut version 19.7 (Biognosys AG, Switzerland). A spectral library generated from the corresponding experimental dataset was used for protein identification and quantification. Dynamic iRT-based retention time prediction, MS2-level interference correction, and cross-run normalization were enabled. Up to two missed cleavages were allowed. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, while methionine oxidation and protein N-terminal acetylation were set as variable modifications. Protein and peptide identifications were filtered at a false discovery rate (FDR) of ≤1%.

Protein abundance values generated by Spectronaut were used as relative quantitative measurements for comparisons within the same experimental batch, and missing values were not imputed. Fold change was calculated as the ratio of the mean protein abundance in the Myc-FKBP8 group to that in the empty vector control group. P values between groups were calculated using two-sided Student’s t tests based on technical replicate measurements and were used for descriptive comparison rather than inference across biological replicates. Proteins with an enrichment fold change > 1.5 and a P < 0.05 were considered candidate FKBP8-interacting proteins.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Western blot band intensities were quantified by densitometry using Image J. Data from at least three independent experiments are presented as mean ± standard deviation or mean ± standard error of the mean as indicated in the figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 or Microsoft Excel.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times with similar results. Representative images from independent repeats are shown in the figures.
