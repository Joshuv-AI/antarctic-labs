Right Ventricular Diastolic Function Predicts Adverse Outcomes After Pulmonary Valve Replacement in Patients With Repaired Tetralogy of Fallot
Authors: Takashi Kido, Yosuke Kugo, Motoki Komori, Hidekazu Ishida, Jun Narita, Hibiki Mima, Fusako Sera, Takayoshi Ueno, Shigeru Miyagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal timing of pulmonary valve replacement (PVR) in repaired tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) remains debated. We examined the influence of right ventricular (RV) diastolic dysfunction on post-PVR outcomes and investigated whether age ≥35 years at PVR is universally associated with adverse outcomes or represents a risk factor only in specific patient subgroups.Methods and ResultsWe reviewed 81 patients who underwent PVR for repaired TOF at Osaka University between 2002 and 2022. Adverse outcomes were defined as catheter ablation or pacemaker implantation for arrhythmia, or unplanned hospitalization for heart failure. RV diastolic dysfunction was defined as RV end-diastolic pressure ≥12 mmHg and/or a right atrial (RA) volume index ≥83 mL/m2. Cox regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with adverse outcomes, with subgroup analyses stratified by the presence or absence of RV diastolic dysfunction. Adverse outcomes occurred in 24 patients (30%). The RA volume index was the only independently significant predictor of adverse outcomes. Age ≥35 years at PVR was significantly associated with reduced freedom from adverse events, but only in patients with RV diastolic dysfunction.ConclusionsRV diastolic dysfunction was significantly associated with post-PVR clinical outcomes. Age ≥35 years at PVR was a significant risk factor only in patients with RV diastolic dysfunction, but not in those without, suggesting that advanced age alone does not uniformly confer increased risk.

Central Figure

Pulmonary valve replacement (PVR) is commonly performed in adults with repaired tetralogy of Fallot (TOF), but the optimal timing of PVR remains uncertain. Since the Amsterdam group proposed a threshold of right ventricular (RV) dilatation predictive of RV normalization after PVR,1 RV volume on cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging has become a central criterion for deciding when to intervene.2 Several recent studies have reported novel and clinically useful measures of RV function and the degree of pulmonary regurgitation,3,4 but even so, the broader impact of PVR on clinical outcomes has not been fully established.

Early reintervention on the RV outflow tract (RVOT) in patients with TOF may help prevent downstream complications and preserve RV function. A multicenter study from the UK reported that undergoing a first PVR after the age of 35 years is associated with higher mortality rates.5 However, operating too early risks multiple reinterventions for management of structural valve deterioration.6 Thus, the question that remains is the optimal timing for PVR, particularly for asymptomatic young patients.

Several previous studies, including our own, have questioned the reliance on RV volume alone and emphasized the importance of RV diastolic function in determining when PVR should be performed.7–13 RV diastolic dysfunction, marked by increased myocardial stiffness, leads to elevated RV end-diastolic pressure and right atrial (RA) enlargement. Patients with repaired TOF often have complex RVOT pathologies (pulmonary regurgitation, stenosis, and a combination of both), that can produce diverse patterns of myocardial injury, promote restrictive RV physiology, and ultimately result in diastolic dysfunction. RV diastolic dysfunction may therefore serve as an integrated parameter for assessing the need for PVR across a heterogeneous group of patients.

In this study, we examined the influence of RV diastolic function on clinical outcomes after PVR in patients with repaired TOF. We further investigated the factors associated with RV diastolic dysfunction (i.e., elevated RV end-diastolic pressure and increased RA volume), and assessed the impact of age at PVR within subgroups defined by the presence or absence of restrictive RV physiology.

Methods

Ethical Considerations

The Institutional Review Board of the University of Osaka, Graduate School of Medicine approved this study and waived the need for informed consent for the retrospective analyses (Approval no. 23135(T3)-3, Date of approval: 22 August 2023).

Definition of RV Diastolic Dysfunction

In this study, RV diastolic dysfunction was defined as an RV end-diastolic pressure ≥12 mmHg and/or a RA volume index (RAVI) ≥83 mL/m2, based on previously published criteria.7,8,14

Patients

We reviewed the records for all patients who underwent PVR after TOF repair at the University of Osaka between 2002 and 2022.

Surgical Procedure

The indication for PVR during the study period was the presence of symptoms or signs of RV failure, including palpitation, pedal edema, or arrhythmia. In asymptomatic patients, we scheduled PVR when the RV end-diastolic volume index on CMR imaging or computed tomography (CT) approached 150 mL/m2 or a pressure gradient across the RVOT >50 mmHg. PVR was performed via median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass under mild hypothermia. Cardiac arrest was induced if a residual left-to-right shunt was present or if concurrent surgery for left-sided cardiac pathology was needed. A longitudinal incision was made into the pulmonary trunk or a previously placed patch. When indicated, reduction plasty of the infundibulum and complete removal of the previous patch were performed. PVR was completed using either a biological valve or a decellularized homograft. Concomitant procedures included tricuspid annuloplasty in selected patients with more than moderate tricuspid regurgitation (TR), a right-sided Maze procedure for patients with atrial arrhythmia, and aortic valve replacement in those with more than moderate aortic regurgitation.

Adverse Outcomes

Adverse outcomes after PVR were defined as composite endpoints, including catheter ablation or pacemaker implantation for arrhythmia, or unplanned hospitalization for heart failure.

Study Methods

Preoperative RA volume was assessed using CT in all patients. End-diastolic and end-systolic RV volumes were measured by CMR imaging or by CT when CMR was contraindicated. RV volumes were evaluated at 2 time points: before PVR and 1 year after PVR. Detailed methods for RA and RV volume assessment have been described previously.7 The RA volume was calculated at the ventricular end-systolic phase, which was identified at 40% of the R-R interval. Preoperative RV end-diastolic pressure and the pressure gradient in the RVOT were measured by cardiac catheterization.

We first evaluated overall outcomes, including survival, reoperation, and adverse events, using medical records. We then identified factors associated with adverse outcomes. Next, we examined factors related to elevated RV end-diastolic pressure (≥12 mmHg) and increased RAVI ≥83 mL/m2. Finally, we assessed the influence of undergoing PVR after the age of 35 years on post-PVR outcomes in patients with and without RV diastolic dysfunction.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are presented as the absolute numbers and percentages. Continuous variables are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). The date of PVR was considered time zero for analysis of freedom from adverse outcomes. The Kaplan-Meier estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify factors associated with adverse events after PVR and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% CIs were estimated. Selection of a priori variables was based on previous literature and included the presence of greater-than-moderate TR, serum B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) level, RAVI, RV end-diastolic volume index, RV ejection fraction (RVEF), pressure gradient through the RVOT, and RV end-diastolic pressure greater than 12 mmHg. Variables included in the multivariate model were selected based on their established relevance to RV function. Factors associated with RV diastolic dysfunction were identified using a logistic regression with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs reported. Variables with a P value <0.05 in the univariate analyses were included in the multivariate models when appropriate. Analyses were performed using JMP Student Edition 18 (SAS institute, Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

Results

Patients’ and Operative Data

During the study period, 81 patients underwent PVR for TOF. The median age at PVR was 38 years (IQR 27–48). Of them, 16 patients had been hospitalized for arrhythmia or heart failure before PVR, at a median interval of 37.7 years (IQR 31.1–41.2) after initial repair, and 8 of those patients had undergone catheter ablation. A total of 19 patients had undergone prior cardiac surgery before PVR: aortic valve replacement with RVOT surgery in 3, RVOT surgery in 12, mitral valvuloplasty in 2, and ventricular septal defect enlargement in 1. RVOT procedures included transannular patch in 11 patients, a valved pericardial roll in 1, and patch enlargement in 3. The primary indication for PVR was clinical symptoms in 39 patients, significant pulmonary regurgitation with RV dilatation in 34, and significant pulmonary stenosis in 8. The median preoperative serum BNP was 38.8 pg/dL (IQR, 23.2–73.4). The median RV end-diastolic volume index and the RVEF were 140 mL/m2 (IQR, 114–170) and 40% (IQR, 34–49) %, respectively. The median RAVI and RV end-diastolic pressure were 70 mL/m2 (IQR 49–92) and 10 mmHg (IQR 8–12), respectively. The patients’ characteristics and operative data are summarized in Table 1. Outcomes

Follow-up was completed in all patients. The median follow-up period after PVR was 9.6 years (IQR 6.6–14.4). During that time, 2 patients died of accidental drowning and hepatic carcinoma at 6 and 8 years after PVR, respectively. On CMR imaging performed 1 year after PVR, the median RV end-diastolic volume index was 105 mL/m2 (IQR 81–121), and normalization of the RV end-diastolic volume index (<108 mL/m2) was achieved in 44 patients (54%). Surgical reintervention on the pulmonary valve was performed in 17 patients at a median 9.5 years (IQR 6.3–10.8) after the initial PVR. Adverse outcomes occurred in 24 patients (hospitalization due to heart failure in 9, pacemaker implantation for bradyarrhythmia in 6, catheter ablation for supraventricular arrhythmia in 6, and ventricular arrhythmia in 3). The rate of freedom from adverse events was 95% at 1 year, 83% at 5 years, and 68% at 10 years after PVR (Figure 1). Figure 1.Freedom from adverse events after pulmonary valve replacement (PVR). Factors Associated With Adverse Events

In the univariate Cox analysis, adverse events after PVR were associated with age at PVR, serum BNP level, RV end-diastolic volume index, RVEF, RAVI, and RV end-diastolic pressure ≥12 mmHg, but not with age at PVR ≥35 years. In the multivariate model, RAVI remained the only factor independently associated with adverse events (HR, 1.01, 95% CI, 1.00–1.03, P=0.0075) (Table 2). The previously reported threshold of 83 mL/m2 effectively predicted freedom from adverse events in our cohort, whereas normalization of RV end-diastolic volume did not (Figures 2,3). Among the 16 patients hospitalized for arrhythmia and/or heart failure before PVR, all were aged ≥35 years at the time of surgery; 10 (63%) had RAVI >83 mL/m2, 11 (68%) had an RV end-diastolic pressure >12 mmHg, and 11 (68%) experienced adverse events after PVR (Supplementary Table 1). Among the 17 patients who underwent re-PVR, 8 experienced an adverse event within 2 years of reintervention. All but 3 were aged ≥35 years and had either RV end-diastolic pressure >12 mmHg or RAVI >83 mL/m2. Of the 9 patients younger than 35 years at the time of their first PVR, 6 had no symptoms or RV diastolic dysfunction (defined as RV end-diastolic pressure ≥12 mmHg or RAVI ≥83 mL/m2) (Supplementary Table 2). Factors Associated With RV Diastolic Dysfunction

Factors related to RV diastolic dysfunction (defined as RV end-diastolic pressure ≥12 mmHg or RAVI ≥83 mL/m2) were examined using logistic regression analysis. In the univariate analysis, serum BNP level was significantly associated with both increased RAVI (≥83 mL/m2) and elevated RV end-diastolic pressure (≥12 mmHg) (OR 1.02, P<0.0001; and OR 1.00, P=0.027, respectively). Additional factors significantly associated with elevated RV end-diastolic pressure (≥12 mmHg) included greater-than-moderate TR (OR 3.61, P=0.0068) and RVEF (OR 0.94, P=0.0086) (Table 3). Impact of Age at PVR in the Subgroup Analysis

The influence of undergoing PVR after the age of 35 years was evaluated in subgroups defined by the presence or absence of RV diastolic dysfunction (either RV end-diastolic pressure ≥12 mmHg or RAVI ≥83 mL/m2). In patients with RV diastolic dysfunction, age ≥35 years at PVR was significantly associated with lower freedom from adverse events. Discussion

The main findings of this study were as follows. First, a high RAVI was significantly associated with adverse events after PVR, whereas normalization of RV end-diastolic volume did not meaningfully reduce adverse events. Second, RAVI ≥83 mL/m2, and RV end-diastolic pressure ≥12 mmHg could be predicted by the serum BNP level and the presence of greater-than-moderate TR. Third, the impact of undergoing PVR after 35 years of age was significant in patients with RV diastolic dysfunction, but not in those without it.

RV diastolic dysfunction, often interpreted as restrictive RV physiology, reflects maladaptive RV remodeling with increased fibrosis and considered a marker of poor clinical prognosis.15 Egbe et al. reported the value of RA pressure as a surrogate for RV diastolic function, showing that elevated RA pressure was associated with reduced exercise capacity and an increased risk of cardiovascular events (including sustained ventricular tachycardia and sudden cardiac death) in adults with repaired TOF.16 Our prior work similarly demonstrated the predictive value of RV diastolic dysfunction for adverse cardiovascular outcomes after PVR in patients with repaired TOF and significant pulmonary regurgitation.8

In this study, which included patients with repaired TOF across a spectrum of RVOT pathologies, markers of RV diastolic dysfunction (increased RA volume and elevated RV end-diastolic pressure) were significantly associated with adverse cardiovascular events after PVR, independent of preoperative RVOT pathology. By contrast, normalization of RV volume after PVR was not associated with improved outcomes. Given the heterogeneity of RVOT lesions in this population, integrated parameters of RV function are needed to guide the timing of PVR. Increased RA volume has been linked to RV diastolic dysfunction and is a useful predictor of atrial arrhythmias after PVR.7 The present findings further underscore the clinical value of RA volume assessment in the long-term management of adults with repaired TOF.

Several prior studies have attempted to define RV diastolic dysfunction in this population. In a large multicenter study, Aboulhosn et al. assessed tricuspid Doppler parameters, including E/A ratio, E/e’, and deceleration time, to characterize RV diastolic function.17 End-diastolic forward flow (EDFF), a well-established marker of restrictive RV physiology, reflects antegrade flow into the pulmonary artery during atrial contraction and indicates impaired RV filling in late diastole. Our previous work showed that persistent EDFF after PVR is associated with worse prognosis and increased arrhythmia burden.17 EDFF is typically diagnosed by detecting antegrade flow >30 cm/s in the main pulmonary artery during atrial contraction using pulsed-wave Doppler.18,19 Although these echocardiographic markers are useful, they are not routinely evaluated. In this study, the serum BNP level, the presence of greater-then-moderate TR, and the RVEF were significantly associated with both RA enlargement and elevated RV end-diastolic pressure. These factors should prompt careful assessment for RV diastolic dysfunction when deciding whether to pursue advanced testing or to consider PVR in patients with repaired TOF.

Cardiac events before PVR occured in 16 patients older than 35 years, and 13 of them had RV diastolic disfunction. Earlier PVR might be warranted in these individuals. Meanwhile, 6 patients who underwent re-PVR were younger than 35 years at the time of their first PVR and had no symptoms or evidence of RV diastolic disfunction, highlighting the complexity of determining the optimal timing of initial intervention.

Because patients typically require multiple reinterventions after their first PVR, choosing the appropriate timing for the initial procedure is crucial. Our findings show that age ≥35 years at PVR does not significantly affect outcomes in the absence of RV diastolic disfunction. Taken together, these results support incorporating RV diastolic dysfunction into decision-making for PVR, particularly in young, asymptomatic patients with repaired TOF.

Study Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective nature and small sample size. A significant limitation is the definition of adverse outcomes, which encompassed heterogeneous events (heart failure, supraventricular arrhythmias, and ventricular arrhythmias) of varying severity. Furthermore, the definition of RV diastolic dysfunction adopted in this study, based on previously published criteria, has not been universally validated, and a consensus definition remains to be established. Further studies with larger patient cohorts are warranted to identify factors associated with each individual adverse event and to refine the definition of RV diastolic dysfunction in this population.

Conclusions

RV diastolic dysfunction, indicated by an elevated RAVI and increased RV end-diastolic pressure, was significantly associated with clinical outcomes after PVR. Age ≥35 years at PVR was associated with adverse events after PVR only in patients with RV diastolic dysfunction.
