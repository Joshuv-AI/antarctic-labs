A scoping review of obesity policies and reimbursement landscape: global insights and lessons for Thailand
Authors: Petch Rawdaree, Nisita Jirawutkornkul, Parnnaphat Luksameesate, Dilok Piyayotai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity is one of the world’s leading health burdens. The prevalence of overweight and obesity continues to rise globally, creating significant healthcare and socioeconomic challenges. Effective strategies are essential to address this crisis.ObjectiveTo thematically map global obesity management strategies and policies, focusing on policy- and system-level features relevant to national implementation, to inform context-sensitive policy considerations in Thailand and similar health systems.MethodsA scoping review was conducted to map global obesity management policies and identify key themes, challenges, success factors, and future directions. Electronic databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus, were searched for relevant documents published between January 1, 2004, and June 30, 2024. Background

Obesity has been recognized as a global medical problem, with a steadily increasing prevalence worldwide for over two decades. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines overweight as a body mass index (BMI) of 25–29.9 kg/m2 and obesity as a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 [1]. Being overweight or obese is a major contributor to chronic diseases, particularly cardiovascular conditions such as heart disease and stroke, which are leading causes of death worldwide. Additionally, obesity affects public health by reducing life expectancy, impairing quality of life, and contributing to economic and social challenges. By 2035, an estimated 3.3 billion adults will have a high BMI, increasing from 2.2 billion in 2020, reflecting a rise in prevalence from 42% to over 54% of the global adult population [2].

The economic burden of obesity is expected to increase. In 2019, obesity-related costs accounted for 1.8% of GDP, with projections reaching 3.6% by 2060. A study by A. Okunogbe indicated that reducing obesity prevalence could significantly lower economic costs [3]. According to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), various factors contribute to obesity, including poor eating behaviors, physical inactivity, stress, medical conditions, genetics, and environmental factors [4, 5]. Thailand is facing an increasing obesity burden, similar to many countries around the world. Over the past two decades, the prevalence of childhood obesity has increased from 5.8% to 15%, while adult obesity has risen to as high as 42%. This growing burden has significant economic and health impacts, with an estimated cost of approximately 4,000 baht per capita in 2019. Moreover, obesity has contributed to a rising burden of obesity-related noncommunicable diseases, leading to significant challenges to the health system [6].

A modeling study in Australia, South Korea, and Thailand analyzed the financial burden of obesity on healthcare systems in the Asia-Pacific (APAC) region. The findings indicate that direct costs to public health insurance systems are exceptionally high. Without proactive measures to address obesity and noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) in the next decade, significant funding shortages could occur. The study also revealed that a 10% reduction in body weight could decrease disease incidence, potentially saving up to 3 billion USD in healthcare costs [7].

A 2024 study reported significant disparities in obesity prevalence, with higher rates among racial and ethnic minorities, particularly non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic adults, and those with lower socioeconomic status than White and higher-income adults [8]. Factors contributing to these disparities in access to quality of care include limited access to healthcare, fewer opportunities to consult specialists, and living in obesogenic environments. Ongoing exposure to discrimination and racial bias can also cause chronic stress, further increasing the risk of obesity [9].

Numerous policies have been implemented to reduce obesity prevalence, with a key strategy focused on changing the obesogenic environment. Health promotion efforts include encouraging healthy eating, limiting unhealthy foods, and increasing physical activity. Another important measure is to manipulate food prices through regulation or taxation to promote healthier eating [10]. Each country adopts different strategies, tailored to its specific context and resources. As a result, approaches to tackling obesity vary based on income, economic factors, social conditions, and lifestyles.

Although recent narrative reviews have summarized global obesity policy responses and challenges [11], systematic mapping, categorization, or comparison of global obesity management strategies, especially policy- and system-level implementation features such as reimbursement mechanisms, has not been thoroughly conducted across countries. Within this policy landscape, reimbursement mechanisms are one illustrative dimension through which national policies may affect access to obesity-related interventions, including pharmacological treatments. Thailand, an upper-middle-income country with universal health coverage, is presented as a contextual example to illustrate how obesity policies intersect with reimbursement structures within publicly financed health systems.

The objective of this scoping review is to thematically map global obesity management strategies and policies, focusing on policy- and system-level features relevant to national implementation, to inform context-sensitive policy considerations in Thailand and similar health systems.

Methods

A scoping review was conducted following the Arksey and O’Malley framework, with refinements from the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis (2020) and Levac et al. This approach systematically explored the scope and diversity of obesity management policies. It aimed to identify key themes, challenges, success factors, and future policy directions. Strategies and policies for global obesity management were reviewed by comparing and categorizing them across countries.

In this review, obesity policies were conceptualized in alignment with the WHO Global Action Plan (2013) [12] and the WHO Acceleration Plan to Stop Obesity (2023) [13] as government-endorsed strategies to prevent and control obesity. Policy implementation was defined as system-level arrangements that enable the translation of policy into national action, rather than operational enforcement or compliance processes.

The protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF; identifier: https://osf.io/pwcjr). The study adhered to the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) to ensure a comprehensive approach to comparing obesity policies across countries (Supplementary Table 1) [14].

Search strategy and study selection

The search strategy was developed in two parts. First, we reviewed global obesity management policies, followed by an examination of the reimbursement of obesity medications. To review global policies, we searched three electronic databases—PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus—for relevant documents published between January 1, 2004, and June 30, 2024. The starting date was selected to reflect the period following the WHO Global Strategy on Diet, Physical Activity and Health (2004), which marked a shift toward comprehensive, system-level policy responses to obesity [15]. Keywords, including “obesity,” “policy,” “nation,” “implementation,” and “management,” were used to identify articles related to national-level obesity policies. Following scoping review methodology, no predefined framework limited policy domains. Instead, policy themes and features were inductively identified through thematic analysis. The search strategy was designed to capture policy- and system-level structures; operational terms such as enforcement or compliance were not explicitly included. The search strategy is detailed in Supplementary Table 2. Two reviewers (NJ and PR) independently screened the titles and abstracts for relevance after removing duplicates. Full-text articles were independently assessed for eligibility by both reviewers, with any discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer (PL).

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were developed in accordance with the JBI framework for scoping reviews, addressing the population, concept, and context (PCC) [16]. The population comprised national governments, public health authorities, and health systems involved in implementing obesity-related policies. The concept encompassed national obesity management strategies and policies, with particular emphasis on system-level and regulatory components. The context was limited to policies implemented at the national or state/provincial level in federal systems. Policies implemented exclusively at local, hospital-based, or community levels were excluded.

Eligible sources included peer-reviewed journal articles, government reports, policy documents, NGO publications, theses, dissertations, and conference papers focused on obesity policies. Perspective articles, commentaries, editorials, and other opinion-based publications were excluded. Articles were required to be in English or a translatable language. Articles lacking full-text access or without a specific country identified were excluded.

The gray literature was used to explore the reimbursement status of obesity medications across countries. Countries with health insurance systems were selected based on the results of the scoping review. A list of obesity medications was compiled from guidelines and government websites to determine reimbursement status. Two reviewers (NJ and PR) independently extracted the data, and discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer (PL).

Data extraction and analysis

Data extraction was conducted in two parts. First, global policies were reviewed and categorized into two groups: 1) study characteristics, including author, year of publication, and document type, and 2) policy details, which included policy type, country (where the policies were implemented), income level, target population, policy implementation year, objectives, outcome evaluations, barriers, and success factors. Two reviewers (NJ and PR) independently reviewed the extracted data to ensure accuracy and consistency. Unresolved disagreements were referred to a third reviewer (PL) for final resolution.

A thematic analysis was conducted to identify recurring themes and patterns across studies. The data were organized into tables aligned with key policy themes, which were then classified into main strategies based on their significance to stakeholders. The thematic coding and categorization were independently performed by two reviewers (NJ and PR). The third reviewer (PL) made a final decision if consensus could not be reached. Finally, cross-country comparisons of key policy themes were undertaken to identify similarities and contextual variations. In the second part, the reimbursement status for obesity medications was reviewed using a targeted approach to compare countries based on eligibility for reimbursement, conditions, special programs, or lack of reimbursement under national health insurance schemes.

Results

Literature search

The initial search across PubMed (n = 2,695), EMBASE (n = 2,514), and Scopus (n = 3,568) yielded 8,777 records. After removing duplicates, 6,444 records remained. Title and abstract screening narrowed this number to 459. Of these, 15 could not be retrieved, and the remaining 444 reports underwent full-text screening, after which 163 records were included. The exclusion criteria were lack of country specification or focus on local policy (n = 149), insufficient policy details (n = 40), no mention of policy implementation (n = 30), conference abstracts (n = 61), and one retracted article (n = 1). Data characteristics and strategy framework development

The final dataset comprised 163 studies covering 65 countries, representing diverse geographic distributions: 63% examined policies in high-income countries (n = 41), 25% in middle-income countries (n = 16), and 12% in low-income countries (n = 8). We developed a comprehensive obesity management strategies framework that encompasses public policy, food security and healthy environment, and healthcare system support (Fig. 2) to synthesize and categorize obesity-related policies across varying socioeconomic contexts.Fig. 2Comprehensive obesity management strategies framework

Comprehensive obesity management strategies framework

This framework is grounded in the principles of multisectoral collaboration, which are central to broader public health policies. While the strategic domains were derived through thematic analysis, they align with the coordinated, cross-sectoral approaches emphasized in global policy discussions. For example, the Lancet Commission (2019) advocates for systemic and coherent actions to address the interconnected challenges of obesity, undernutrition, and climate change [17]. Our framework applies these principles specifically to the domain of obesity management, offering insights relevant to both community- and national-level strategies aimed at achieving sustainable health outcomes.

The included studies provide valuable insights into obesity policy across different regions and economic levels. High-income countries had a greater proportion of policies focused on comprehensive healthcare system support, food security, and a healthy environment, as well as public awareness campaigns. In contrast, low-income countries often face resource constraints that limit policy scope and implementation. The economic status of a country significantly influences its capacity to develop, implement, and sustain effective obesity management policies.

Building on this framework, the following sections elaborate on the three strategic domains identified across the included studies.

Public policy strategies focus on raising public awareness about obesity prevention and management. Policies include taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) and foods high in salt or fat, advertising restrictions, and initiatives to promote physical activity. Examples include campaigns, public media messaging, and urban planning measures such as green spaces and bike lanes, all aimed at creating environments that support sustainable healthy behaviors.

Food security and healthy environment strategies aim to improve access to nutritious food and create supportive environments for healthy living. Governments implement subsidies for low-income populations and school-aged children, regulate food pricing, and collaborate with the manufacturing sector to reformulate products for improved nutrition. The industrial and agricultural sectors contribute through nutrition labeling and the development of healthier food formulations. Healthy environment strategies also include investments in walkable infrastructure—such as bike lanes, parks, and footpaths—to promote daily physical activity.

Healthcare system support strategies address treatment access through insurance schemes to ensure equitable care. These strategies include health education, nutrition counseling, appropriate medications, clinical guidelines, and provider training. Some high-income countries reimburse bariatric surgery or weight-loss medication under certain conditions. Although primarily treatment-focused, these strategies often include preventive components such as early risk management and patient education, contributing to sustainable reductions in national obesity-related healthcare costs.

Distribution of national obesity policy themes across countries

High-income countries generally adopt a broader range of policies, reflecting a more comprehensive approach to obesity management. Australia and the United States (U.S.) implemented all 19 policy themes, followed by the United Kingdom (U.K.) and Brazil. Conversely, upper- and lower-middle-income countries showed more limited policy coverage, potentially due to economic or resource constraints. This table provides valuable insights into global trends in obesity-related policy, illustrating common themes and identifying gaps in policy implementation.

Policy themes

Advertising promotion

To combat obesity, many countries have adopted advertising strategies to increase public awareness and encourage healthier behaviors. For example, companies are encouraged to allocate at least 50% of their advertising toward nutritious foods targeted at children under 12 years of age. Public awareness is further promoted through billboards, social media, and family-oriented activities that aim to reduce screen time and encourage healthy lifestyles. Practical guidelines for maintaining a healthy weight also support these efforts, fostering long-term behavioral change [18–21].

Advertising restriction

Policies limiting advertisements for unhealthy foods—especially those targeting children—aim to reduce harmful consumption behaviors. Examples include bans on using cartoon characters in ads for high-sugar, high-fat, and high-salt foods, as well as restrictions on such ads during peak children’s viewing hours. These measures help reduce exposure and may discourage unhealthy food choices [22]. However, industry groups have raised concerns about the potential economic impacts of these restrictions.

Financial support

Financial support is fundamental to implementing health policy initiatives effectively. Funding may come from governments, the private sector, or nonprofit organizations. The goal is to prevent and manage obesity and other NCDs, facilitating public access to essential health services, such as obesity treatment through health insurance or reduced medical costs for low-income groups. Many governments also allocate budgets for health promotion efforts, such as school nutrition programs or community-based fitness initiatives. This study categorizes financial support into three areas:

Campaign and reward approach

These programs encourage healthy behaviors through public campaigns and financial incentives. For example, the U.S. Healthy Incentives Pilot provides cashback credits for purchasing fruits and vegetables. Programs such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children expand access to healthy foods for vulnerable populations. These financial incentives promote healthier eating on a large scale [23, 24].

Food security initiatives

These initiatives improve access to nutritious food, particularly for children and low-income families. For example, China provides daily food subsidies for children in poor areas and upgrades school cafeterias [21], while the U.K.’s School Fruit and Vegetable Scheme offers free produce to schoolchildren [25]. Similarly, Brazil’s Policy for Food and Nutritional Security Program promotes healthy eating habits and access to affordable, nutritious food [26].

Healthcare research funding

Investment in obesity-related research—including treatment, nutrition, and prevention—supports the development of evidence-based policies. Research in medical technologies, disease surveillance, and healthy lifestyle interventions helps inform strategies that reduce obesity. High-income countries, particularly in Europe and North America, have well-established funding mechanisms for such research [25, 27, 28].

These examples demonstrate that financial support is essential for sustaining health initiatives and ensuring equitable public benefits.

Agricultural support

This policy promotes the production and accessibility of high-quality foods to improve public health and reduce obesity. In the U.S., the Child Nutrition Act of 2010 connects local farmers with food banks and workplaces through programs such as “Thumbs Up for Healthy Choices” and “Farm to Work” to increase access to fruits and vegetables [29]. In Australia, fresh produce is promoted in suburban areas, and farmland near urban centers is protected to support local agriculture. To further increase access and affordability, governments provide subsidies or low-interest loans to farmers [30–33]. These policies increase food availability and affordability, support healthier diets, contribute to improved public health, and reduce obesity rates.

Food reformulation policy

This voluntary policy encourages collaboration between governments and industry to improve the nutritional quality of processed foods by reducing unhealthy ingredients such as sugar, salt, and fat, while increasing beneficial ingredients such as fiber, vitamins, and minerals. Commonly reformulated products include sausages, bread, cereals, cakes, and sauces [18, 22]. In Norway, the Salt Partnership program aims to reduce salt content across food categories. An impact assessment reported that approximately 40–50% of food categories met the established salt content targets; however, changes in population-level salt intake have not yet been directly measured [34]. In Thailand, the government collaborates with manufacturers to reduce the levels of saturated fat, trans fat, sugar, and salt [35]. Thailand also implemented a mandatory ban on partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs)—the primary source of trans fats—through the Ministry of Public Health Notification No. 388 (2018), which prohibits their production, import, and sale in all food products, including exports. This regulation aligns with national efforts to reduce diet-related risks for obesity and cardiovascular disease. However, effective monitoring and evaluation remain challenging, underscoring the need for continuous oversight and stricter enforcement.

Food price control policy

These policies use pricing strategies—such as subsidies and restrictions—to influence dietary choices and reduce obesity. For example, the U.K. restricts promotional programs for unhealthy foods and sugary drinks to reduce consumer demand [22]. In New Zealand, the government subsidizes fruits and vegetables and distributes healthy food vouchers to improve affordability [36]. Brazil’s PNAN policy supports local farmers in supplying fresh produce to schools and markets, representing a structural success at the food system level. This policy has enabled school meal programs to ensure that at least 70% of foods provided are fresh or minimally processed, thereby strengthening access to nutritious foods within institutional settings [37]. By applying price controls to both healthy and unhealthy products, these policies aim to shape consumer behavior and align eating habits with broader health and lifestyle goals.

Health education policy

These policies equip individuals with knowledge and practical skills for self-care and healthy living, especially in obesity prevention. Unlike public awareness campaigns, health education emphasizes structured training and personalized support—often delivered in schools, hospitals, and community centers. Furthermore, health education is fundamental for developing comprehensive national health policies and ensuring equitable access to health information and preventive services [32, 38, 39].

Public awareness policy

These policies aim to educate communities about the health risks associated with poor diets and physical inactivity, promoting healthier lifestyles through antenatal care, breastfeeding support, food security initiatives, and exercise. Public campaigns use media channels—TV, social media, and billboards—and expert-led community activities to empower individuals to make informed health choices. Examples include calorie education and breastfeeding support programs [21, 40–45], promoting physical activity and fruit and vegetable consumption [46], and early identification of at-risk children through family- and provider-based interventions [25]. Governments worldwide implement awareness strategies tailored to different age groups to strengthen public health and reduce the prevalence of obesity.

School education

Addressing childhood obesity requires the establishment of healthy behaviors early in life. School-based education helps reduce long-term obesity risk by teaching nutrition and encouraging physical activity. Many countries incorporate lessons on healthy living, nutrition, and exercise into their core curricula and have increased physical education class time as part of national strategies [47–50]. Studies have shown that school programs emphasizing proper nutrition and regular exercise positively influence student behaviors and contribute to reducing long-term obesity risk.

School regulation

Given the significant amount of time children spend in school, school policies are key to fostering environments that support healthy behaviors. Measures include regulating the sugar and fat content of meals, promoting the consumption of fruits and vegetables, and integrating physical activity into lessons and breaks [25, 32, 51, 52]. Countries such as the U.S. and the Bahamas have adopted these strategies. Additional policies include zoning restrictions on fast-food outlets near schools to limit access to unhealthy food [24, 48] and routine health screenings, with high-BMI students enrolled in weight management programs with ongoing support [53–55].

Workplace regulation

These policies support employee health by promoting active lifestyles and informing food choices. Providing facilities, such as showers, gyms, and bicycle storage, encourages physical activity [30]. Some offices offer standing desks and recreational areas to reduce sedentary time [56]. Nutritional information on menus empowers employees to make healthier choices [57]. In addition, workplace policies that support breastfeeding, such as providing designated nursing or lactation spaces, are associated with an increased continuation of breastfeeding among working women [30, 58]. These measures collectively address obesity while enhancing employee well-being and workplace productivity.

Infrastructure support

These policies aim to create environments that promote physical activity. Examples include building sidewalks, bike paths, and parks to encourage daily movement. In the U.S., the SAFETEA-LU Act funds safe walking and cycling routes to promote 30 minutes of daily exercise, whereas the National Safe Routes to School program encourages active commuting among children [59]. Countries such as the U.S., Mexico, Ireland, the Netherlands, Singapore, and Australia have increased access to free, clean drinking water through public dispensers to encourage healthier consumption habits [24, 25, 30, 59–61]. Australia also promotes physical activity by expanding green spaces, opening school facilities for public use outside school hours, and investing in community infrastructure such as sports fields, pools, parks, and fitness centers [30]. These initiatives reduce obesity risk factors and contribute to long-term improvements in public health.

Nutrition labeling control

Nutrition labeling enables consumers to make informed dietary choices by displaying caloric, sugar, fat, and sodium contents on packaging. Effective labeling reduces misinformation and encourages better food selection. In Australia, the ‘Daily Intake Labelling’ system is used to display per-serving nutrition, and is considered ‘Traffic Light Labelling’—a color-coded system endorsed by the U.K. Food Standards Agency and NICE—to help consumers easily interpret nutritional information [62]. Labeling policies also extend to restaurant menus and school cafeterias. Studies have shown that mandatory labeling is more effective than voluntary options in reducing obesity and cardiovascular mortality. Effectiveness is further enhanced when applied across all food business sizes, not just large manufacturers, helping to reduce misinformation and promote healthier food choices [63–65].

Taxation policy

Taxation policies aim to reduce obesity by discouraging the consumption of unhealthy foods, such as sugary drinks, high-calorie snacks, and saturated fats [46, 56, 66]. Some countries also tax imported high-sugar products, using revenue to subsidize healthier options and invest in public spaces for physical activity [67]. Taxes on personal vehicle fuel encourage the use of public transportation and promote more active lifestyles, such as walking and cycling [30, 68]. Therefore, these policies can influence consumer behavior and fund health initiatives. However, these measures must be carefully designed to avoid disproportionate impacts on low-income populations.

Obesity management guideline

Obesity guidelines are essential for addressing obesity-related health issues and associated chronic conditions. Global organizations, such as the WHO, have developed guidelines that focus on prevention, treatment, and rehabilitation through healthy eating and physical activity [69–73]. Professional bodies, such as the American College of Cardiology (ACC) and the American Heart Association (AHA), provide guidelines for adult obesity management [74]. In Thailand, clinical practice guidelines are developed based on current evidence, national policies, and local context [75]. These guidelines integrate with national health strategies, enabling effective obesity management and promoting sustainable public health improvements.

Healthcare system

Health insurance policies should ensure access to both preventive and treatment services for obesity, including weight loss medications and surgery. Both public and private insurance systems play key roles in reducing obesity and improving long-term health outcomes. Health literacy promotion—through school curricula, community programs, and public campaigns—is also vital for empowering individuals to adopt healthier behaviors.

Public health insurance policy

In the U.S., the Treat and Reduce Obesity Act (TROA) of 2021 aims to expand Medicare coverage for obesity medications and provide compensation to healthcare providers [25]. Thailand’s Universal Health Coverage (UHC) system enhances health access and infrastructure, including free screenings for childhood obesity [76]. Countries such as Australia, Belgium, Thailand, and the U.S. offer bariatric surgery coverage under specific conditions [77–79].

Private health insurance policy

Under the Affordable Care Act (ACA), obesity screening and counseling are covered for both children and adults [20]. To address rising childhood obesity, countries like Malta, Malaysia, the U.S., and the U.K. mandate BMI screening and annual exercise programs for children whose BMI are outside the normal range. [53, 54, 80].

Interagency collaboration policy

These policies involve coordination across local, regional, and national levels, including public-private partnerships. Countries such as the U.S., the U.K., Australia, Germany, Mexico, Argentina, Iran, and Brazil have established task forces with ministries of health, education, and agriculture. U.S. agencies developed initiatives such as Accountable Care Organizations and Medicare Shared Savings Programs [80], while Germany unites over 70 health-focused organizations through platforms like the ‘German Forum Prevention and Public Health Promotion,’ engaging the government, academia, and industry to promote nutrition policies [25, 27].

Collaborative goals vary. Poland partners with education and sports ministries to raise awareness and promote physical activity [81]. Norway works with industry to reformulate foods by reducing salt, sugar, and unhealthy fats [34]. In the U.K., efforts include regulating high-fat, high-sugar, and high-salt food advertising and encouraging voluntary calorie reductions [22]. Effective interagency collaboration relies on shared goals, improved access to healthy food, education, and ongoing evaluation to ensure long-term impact.

Reimbursement status for weight-loss medications across countries

This study compares weight-loss medication reimbursement across countries with established obesity management policies, representing diverse regions and income levels. Medications were selected based on clinical guideline listings, and reimbursement status was color-coded (green, yellow, red).

The findings indicated that glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), such as liraglutide and semaglutide—originally for type 2 diabetes—are reimbursed for obesity treatment in some high-income countries, including Denmark and the U.K. In upper-middle-income countries, coverage is more limited and typically restricted to diabetes care. In Thailand, these drugs are reimbursed only for diabetes under the Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme (CSMBS).

Other reimbursed anti-obesity drugs include mazindol, naltrexone/bupropion, and orlistat, which are limited in availability in countries such as the U.K., Finland, Japan, and the Netherlands. Lessons learned from successful policy implementation

Obesity management is a global public health challenge requiring comprehensive policy measures beyond health education. Success depends on supporting sustainable healthy lifestyles through policies beginning in prenatal stages, preventing obesity early, and promoting lifelong healthy behaviors in nutrition and physical activity. For example, the U.K.’s sugar tax reflects efforts to reduce sugar content in soft drinks, particularly among youth [101, 102]. Similarly, Thailand introduced a tiered sugar-sweetened beverage tax in 2017 [103], based on sugar content per 100 mL. This policy has driven product reformulation, reduced sugar intake, and significantly lowered sales of high-sugar drinks. Revenue from the tax supports health promotion activities. In some countries, social media campaigns and restrictions on online content have also effectively influenced consumer behavior, enhancing policy success.

The study also highlights the necessity of consistent and comprehensive policy enforcement and monitoring to achieve long-term effectiveness. A strong governmental commitment, particularly to research on consumer behavior and the establishment of socially relevant goals, has demonstrated a greater policy impact. For example, in Mexico, regulatory efforts to restrict food additives and ban trans fats in restaurants have resulted in a reduction in trans-fat consumption [104]. Similarly, salt reduction guidelines for processed foods have led to significant declines in salt intake in countries such as China [105], Finland, France, Ireland [106], South Africa [107], and the U.K. [102]. These proactive regulatory measures have contributed to measurable improvements in public health. Furthermore, strategic financial planning and inclusive, cross-sectoral collaboration, particularly through private-sector engagement, enable the development of integrated systems. Poland’s school-based physical activity promotion program exemplifies this approach, as public-private resource sharing strengthens program sustainability and ensures access to quality educational materials and support [108]. A holistic strategy that integrates regulation, research, financing, and collaboration is essential to achieving sustained progress in obesity prevention and management.

Key issues and challenges in policy implementation

Despite global efforts, several obstacles continue to hinder the effective implementation of obesity-related policies. These challenges can be categorized into five key areas (1) limited financial resources; (2) weak policy enforcement; (3) inadequate intersectoral coordination; (4) limited public unawareness and industry influence; and (5) cultural, social, and uncontrollable factors.

A major issue identified in numerous studies is the limited availability of financial resources, particularly in middle-income countries compared with high-income nations. As a result, policy implementation in these settings tends to be less comprehensive. For example, constrained budgets for healthy food subsidy programs may limit their reach, often benefiting only specific groups such as school-aged children or low-income families.

The second issue is weak policy enforcement, which significantly undermines policy effectiveness and allows industries to exploit regulatory gaps. Many governments face challenges, including inadequate monitoring systems and a lack of dedicated regulatory bodies. Additionally, corporate lobbying and public resistance often weaken enforcement, especially when policies lack clear implementation plans. For example, under voluntary food reformulation initiatives, manufacturers may resist reducing sugars, salts, and fats due to concerns over product taste. Similarly, restaurant menu labeling remains inconsistent when participation is nonmandatory, as some establishments doubt its influence on consumer behavior.

The third challenge is intersectoral coordination. Effective obesity policies require collaboration across the health, education, and agriculture sectors, yet governments often struggle to align their actions. For example, the Ministry of Public Health may focus on treatment, while the Ministry of Agriculture promotes food production without fully considering health impacts. At the same time, food pricing policies may not sufficiently incentivize healthier options, making products such as organic foods less affordable for many households.

Another challenge is limited public unawareness and industry influence. Behavioral change is difficult, particularly when individuals lack awareness of the health risks associated with unhealthy foods. Misleading nutrition information and aggressive marketing of unhealthy products contribute to confusion and resistance to change. Food advertising often appeals to consumers while obscuring health risks. Although some countries have implemented policies to restrict advertising targeting children, Thailand currently lacks such regulations. In some democratic contexts, such restrictions are challenged as infringements on consumer rights.

Finally, social and cultural factors significantly influence policy effectiveness. In some high-income settings, including parts of North America and Europe, prevailing dietary patterns characterized by high intake of energy-dense, high-fat, and refined-carbohydrate foods may pose challenges for obesity-reduction efforts [109]. Historically, excess weight has been perceived as a symbol of wealth and prosperity, and such perceptions may continue to influence attitudes in certain cultural contexts [110]. Similarly, in Thailand, the cultural notion that “a savory meal without a sweet dessert is uncivilized” reflects deeply rooted dietary norms [111]. In communities where obesity is common, it may not be viewed as a health concern, reducing motivation for weight management. These cultural perceptions can lead to poor public engagement with obesity prevention policies. Moreover, uncontrollable factors such as adolescent behavior and lack of discipline pose challenges to implementing effective childhood obesity interventions, potentially limiting the success of school-based regulations.

The obstacles discussed above may stem from social, economic, or political factors, as well as from a limited understanding of the policies themselves. These challenges may influence policy effectiveness and highlight the importance of ongoing adaptation and refinement of strategies. Effective implementation requires careful prioritization and responsiveness to each country’s unique context and constraints.

From global evidence to local action: implementation considerations for Thailand

To further interpret the relevance of the identified strategies, we conceptually referenced the Implementation Outcomes Framework proposed by Proctor et al. (2011) [112]. By distinguishing key implementation outcomes, including acceptability, feasibility, adoption, and sustainability, we reflected on how globally identified obesity policies may translate within the Thai context. Importantly, this framework was applied to guide the discussion of implementation considerations rather than to evaluate implementation effectiveness or outcomes.

Public policy strategies involve population-level interventions designed to influence obesogenic environments, including public awareness campaigns, school and workplace regulations. Feasibility and adoption may depend on governmental stability and sustained political commitment, while acceptability is likely to vary across population groups due to differences in knowledge, social norms, and cultural contexts. Sustainability may be enhanced when these strategies are embedded in routine institutional practices and supported by consistent governance and long-term public investment.

Food security and healthy environment strategies focus on improving access to nutritious food through mechanisms such as food price regulation, food reformulation policies, and taxation. Effective implementation of these strategies often requires substantial collaboration with the private sector, particularly the food industry. Acceptability may be difficult since healthier food options are usually more costly, and most households face a high cost-of-living burden, with expenditures accounting for approximately 80% of household income in Thailand [113]. Consequently, public acceptance of these policies may be limited unless complementary measures such as price controls for healthy foods or broader cost-of-living support are introduced to reduce financial constraints on households.

Healthcare system support strategies focus on improving access to obesity prevention and treatment services through clinical practice guidelines, healthcare service delivery, and health insurance coverage. From an access-to-care perspective, Thailand has a comparative advantage due to its strong and comprehensive health insurance system, which covers the majority of the population. Nevertheless, notable disparities persist across the three national insurance schemes, resulting in unequal access to certain health services. Ongoing efforts have been undertaken to reduce disparities through adjustments to benefit packages and financing mechanisms [114], although such reforms may require time to achieve meaningful change.

Overall, the successful implementation of these policies depends on sustained political stability, adequate government resource allocation, and effective intersectoral coordination among relevant ministries and government agencies, including those responsible for health, education, and urban planning, particularly for infrastructure- and education-related policies.

Policy implications for Thailand

In Thailand, policy priorities should focus on increasing public awareness of obesity as a serious medical condition. Despite existing efforts, obesity is often not perceived as a health concern. The term is frequently used in an affectionate or casual manner, diminishing its perceived severity. To address this cultural barrier, national strategies must emphasize public education to shift perceptions and promote timely treatment. An integrated approach that combines prevention, education, infrastructure development, and clinical care is urgently needed. Furthermore, effective obesity management requires intersectoral collaboration beyond the health sector, particularly with the food and agriculture sectors, to support a coordinated policy response.

Moreover, systemic policy gaps remain in Thailand’s healthcare system. While bariatric surgery is reimbursable under specific conditions, weight-loss medications are not currently reimbursable. Despite the effectiveness of bariatric surgery, it carries risks including gastroesophageal reflux, nutrient malabsorption, and other gastrointestinal complications, which may affect quality of life [114–116]. In contrast, GLP-1 RAs provide a lower-risk, clinically beneficial, medication-based alternative [117]. The evidence suggests that using GLP-1 RAs before or after surgery can increase weight loss, prevent weight regain, and improve long-term outcomes and quality of life [118, 119]. A cost-effectiveness analysis by Sanchez et al. also revealed that combining GLP-1 RAs with bariatric surgery is more cost-effective than surgery alone [120]. This combined approach may represent a promising and economical strategy for obesity management in Thailand, although further research is needed to evaluate its feasibility in the local healthcare context.

Further studies are recommended to evaluate the impact of medication reimbursement policies in Thailand. A holistic approach should also include public health measures such as regulating food advertising aimed at children, following successful international examples. Additionally, cultural dietary practices must be considered. Effective obesity policies should address public attitudes, including the cultural norm of consuming sweets after meals. These insights underscore expert recommendations for improving policies, integrating cultural awareness, and encouraging both healthcare providers and the public to recognize obesity as a serious condition requiring urgent intervention.

Limitations of the study

This study faced several limitations. First, data availability varied across countries, resulting in incomplete information in some cases. Second, access to relevant documents was restricted, and language barriers limited the inclusion of non-English sources, affecting the comprehensiveness of cross-country comparisons. Third, database constraints may have resulted in the omission of pertinent literature. Finally, differences in economic, social, and cultural contexts across countries may affect the generalizability of findings, as policies effective in one setting may not be applicable elsewhere. These limitations suggest the need for further research to enhance global obesity policy analysis.

Conclusions

High-income countries generally implement more comprehensive obesity-related policies, emphasizing prevention across 19 policy themes. In contrast, upper-middle- and lower-middle-income countries face resource constraints and enforcement difficulties despite adopting similar frameworks. Addressing obesity effectively requires integrated strategies that combine prevention and treatment, supported by strong multisectoral collaboration. Effective implementation also depends on joint efforts to increase public awareness and drive sustainable behavior change. Crucially, tailoring policies to each country’s social, cultural, and economic context is essential to ensure long-term sustainability and impact.
