Layer-by-layer shear densification for multiscale hierarchical alignment in bulk hydrogels
Authors: Sen Wang, Senxuan Tang, Tianqi Fu, Yirong Jiang, Yun Lin, Lianlian Fu, Ronghui Wu, Youhui Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Natural structural tissues achieve exceptional performance through precisely aligned hierarchical architectures that extend across multiple length scales. However, realizing such multiscale long-range alignment in synthetic bulk hydrogels remains challenging because of the difficulty in constructing a uniformly dense and highly oriented structure that extends throughout the full bulk matrix. Here, we introduce a scalable and versatile Layer-by-Layer Shear Densification (LBSD) strategy that integrates flocculation-induced aggregation with shear-driven progressive alignment, precisely driving the architectural evolution toward compact and uniformly ordered lamellar structures across multiscales. The resulting poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) hydrogels with a hierarchical network exhibit a Herman’s orientation factor of 0.91, surpassing previously reported values for bulk hydrogels. The structural orientation enables the hydrogel to exhibit excellent mechanical properties, including a tensile strength of 41.29 ± 2.10 MPa and toughness of 159.37 ± 28.15 MJ·m⁻³. To demonstrate the versatility, this strategy is further used to fabricate gelatin hydrogels, resulting in a 32-fold enhancement in toughness. Anisotropic thermal conductivity, another representative physical property originating from molecular-level alignment, is also demonstrated. This work establishes a generalizable technology for developing high-performance bulk polymeric materials through molecular-level engineering, offering substantial potential for applications in load-bearing components, bioelectronic devices, thermal management systems, etc.

Introduction

In nature, load-bearing biological tissues such as tendons, muscles, nacre, and wood exhibit extraordinary combinations of strength, toughness, and durability1–4. These properties arise from intricately organized hierarchical architecture across multiple scales, ranging from well-defined crystalline structures at the molecular level to oriented packing at the macroscale5,6. Such multilevel alignment enables efficient load redistribution, promotes energy dissipation, and enhances crack resistance, allowing these bulk materials to withstand large deformations while maintaining structural integrity7,8. Beyond mechanical performance, structural alignment also plays an impressive role in governing phonon conduction9,10, ion transport11,12, charge mobility13,14, and other key physical properties15. These capabilities have long motivated researchers to biomimic the structure for synthetic systems, particularly polymeric hydrogels with inherent softness, high water content, and excellent biocompatibility16–19. The multiscale reconstruction strategy could potentially enable hydrogels to match or even surpass the performance of their natural counterparts, opening opportunities for creating excellent load-bearing components20,21, artificial muscle tissues1,22, bioelectronic devices23, and advanced thermal management systems24. However, realizing such highly coordinated multiscale hierarchical organization within bulk polymeric hydrogels remains a significant challenge, limiting their property development and wide employment.

Considerable effort has been devoted to structural engineering strategies that introduce hierarchical organization into hydrogels. Directional freeze-casting is widely employed to fabricate anisotropic hydrogels7,25,26. Despite the advantages of the controlled solidification and compatibility with broad polymer systems, the ice-templated channels typically exhibit weak interlayer cohesion and limited alignment fidelity, so additional post-processing is often needed to improve performance. Strain-aligned hydrogels exhibit substantially enhanced strength and fracture toughness relative to homogeneous tough hydrogels and can induce partial ordering of molecular chains27,28. Moreover, the resulting alignment is often nonuniform, with a highly oriented central region but poorly aligned peripheral zones. In addition, because the process relies on pre-stretching, the overall deformability of the material is intrinsically compromised. Shear-flow-induced alignment allows continuous processing and can achieve high orientation, yet it is typically effective for thin films or microfibers8,29. Extending this approach to bulk hydrogels remains difficult because shear forces decay rapidly toward the material interior (Supplementary Fig. 1). Therefore, a generalizable method capable of constructing compact and uniformly aligned hierarchical structures across multiple length scales throughout bulk volumes is important and nontrivial.

To overcome these limitations, we developed a layer-by-layer shear densification (LBSD) strategy to construct highly ordered bulk hydrogels through a flocculating–freezing–rolling (FFR) process. In this method, controlled flocculation first transforms the polymer solution into soft, deformable aggregates that can be readily reconfigured. Subsequent freezing establishes a semi-solid framework that stabilizes the evolving structure while preserving sufficient plasticity, followed by the subsequent rolling step, which is the key driver of the architectural evolution. Under the cooperative action of shear and compressive forces during sequential rolling, each layer is progressively oriented and densified. In this process, shear more directly initiates polymer-chain alignment along the rolling direction, whereas compression primarily promotes structural densification and strengthens the aligned lamellar packing. In contrast to conventional shear alignment, where orientation is confined near the surface, the LBSD process facilitates the propagation of orientation from one layer to the next, ultimately generating a densely packed lamellar network with long-range alignment. The resulting FFR hydrogels exhibit a high orientation factor (f = 0.91), surpassing previously reported values for anisotropic polymeric hydrogels. They also display exceptional mechanical properties, including a tensile strength of 41.29 ± 2.10 MPa, toughness of 159.37 ± 28.15 MJ·m−3, and fracture energy of 97.94 ± 14.67 kJ·m−2. Moreover, the highly aligned lamellar structure significantly enhances in-plane thermal conduction, demonstrating that mechanical robustness and thermal transport can be simultaneously optimized through hierarchical design. This work establishes a scalable and versatile approach for constructing bioinspired anisotropic polymeric materials and provides a mechanistic framework that links external mechanical fields to multiscale structural organization and functional property enhancement.

Results

Formation of hierarchical-structured hydrogels under shear

Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) hydrogel was selected as a representative example due to its high hydrophilicity and programmable properties. A transparent sodium citrate solution was added dropwise to an aqueous PVA solution, which was continuously stirred until flocculation occurred, resulting in a white, soft, plasticine-like hydrogel (Fig. 1a, i and ii). Phase separation from the original homogeneous phase is attributed to the influence of kosmotropic salt ions, which promote the formation of numerous hydrogen bonds between PVA chains, leading to the development of abundant crystalline domains and enhanced chain entanglement. Importantly, unlike conventional post-immersion salting-out processes that rapidly lock polymer chains into a rigid gel, the present approach introduces concentrated salt directly into the polymer solution at elevated temperature. This allows phase separation to occur while retaining sufficient chain mobility, thereby generating a soft and reconfigurable flocculated state that can be readily used for subsequent alignment. The hydrogel was then shaped, frozen, and subjected to rolling pressure to progressively orient the layers under shear force (Fig. 1a, iii). During the initial stage of rolling, the outer regions of the frozen PVA gel, located near the roller heating layer, first experienced stronger shear force and compressive pressure. At the same time, partial water evaporation from the outermost layer increased the local polymer concentration and facilitated the formation of pore walls composed of tightly aligned polymer chains. Moreover, the salting-out effect continued to promote PVA aggregation and crystallization through enhanced phase separation. As a result, compact and highly aligned toughness layers were preferentially formed near the outer surface, while the inner region remained relatively soft and less oriented (Supplementary Fig. 2). Finite-element simulation further suggests that the rolling-induced shear and compressive stresses are not confined to the immediate surface, but can be transmitted into the bulk with gradual attenuation from the outer toward the inner, thereby enabling continued structural evolution in the inner region (Supplementary Fig. 3). Once the outer layers become densified and structurally stabilized through alignment and crystallization, further deformation of these regions is largely suppressed. With continued rolling, the initially soft inner layers progressively undergo alignment and densification under the transmitted stresses, subsequently evolving into new toughness layers. Through repeated rolling cycles, this outer-to-inner structural evolution proceeds until a completely aligned and compact architecture is established across the entire thickness, with the overall orientation of the FFR hydrogel gradually maturing from the exterior to the interior. This process resulted in the formation of a well-ordered 2D lamellar structure, with the alignment evolving continuously along both the X and Y axes. Here, the X direction refers to the rolling direction (longitudinal direction), whereas the Y direction refers to the radial direction perpendicular to rolling. The final anisotropic hydrogel, produced through the flocculating–freezing–rolling (FFR) process, combines softness with high strength, toughness, and crack resistance (Fig. 1b(i-iii)). For instance, the FFR hydrogel can lift a bucket weighing more than 15,000 times its own weight (Fig. 1b(iv)). In contrast, conventional PVA hydrogels treated with freezing-thawing (FT) typically exhibit brittle and fragile characteristics due to weak polymer chain bonding, limiting their applicability as engineering materials. The tensile strength of the FFR hydrogel increased by ~129,000% compared to the FT hydrogel (Fig. 1c), rising from 0.03 ± 0.01 MPa to 41.29 ± 2.10 MPa. Structure characterization and mechanical performance

To elucidate the structural evolution and mechanical properties of the FFR hydrogels, a series of microscopic and macroscopic characterizations was conducted. Cross-sectional observations reveal the progressive structural evolution during the rolling process, where the outer layers gradually transform into densely aligned toughness layers, while the inner regions initially retain a softer, more porous structure (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 4). Wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) patterns together with scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images further confirm the transition from loosely packed to tightly packed lamellar structures induced by rolling pressure (Fig. 2b). For comparison, randomly distributed PVA hydrogels prepared using the freezing-thawing (FT) and flocculating-freezing-thawing (FF) methods were used as control samples. Notably, we also attempted to compare samples prepared using freezing-rolling pressure; however, in the absence of flocculation, achieving proper shaping proved challenging, which led us to abandon the comparison (Supplementary Fig. 5). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images visually provide a detailed comparison of the surface morphology between FFR and FF hydrogels (Figs. 2c, 2d, and Supplementary Fig. 6). The FFR hydrogel exhibits a highly ordered structure with uniform alignment, as evidenced by clear, parallel ridges and grooves across the observed area. These features reflect the shear-induced PVA chain orientation achieved during the rolling pressure process. In contrast, the FF hydrogel displays a disorganized surface topology with uneven, randomly distributed features and significantly greater roughness. As illustrated in Fig. 2e, the SEM images of FFR hydrogels reveal a compact, lamellar structure with distinct anisotropy. The cross-section cut parallel to the rolling direction is referred to as the L-view (longitudinal view), which reveals the highly aligned microstructure (Fig. 2e, i and ii). Moreover, the cross-section observed perpendicular to the rolling direction is referred to as the R-view (radial view), which further proves the structural alignment. Brittle fracture upon exposure to liquid nitrogen indicates the presence of strong intermolecular forces between the stacked layers, which are attributed to the rolling pressure treatment (Fig. 2e, iii-iv). In contrast, when a precut crack is introduced perpendicular to the ordered orientation, the lamellar layers are forcibly separated under tensile fracture (Fig. 2e, v-vi). Such morphological features verify the formation of a lamellar architecture, which is essential for imparting pronounced anisotropy and mechanical robustness to the FFR hydrogels. However, there is no orientation observed in either the FT or FF hydrogels (Supplementary Fig. 7). Confocal microscopy further demonstrates the well-aligned morphology of the FFR hydrogel (Supplementary Fig. 8). Moreover, the FFR hydrogel exhibits strong birefringence under a polarizing optical microscope (POM), indicating the formation of an anisotropic structure. Maximum brightness is observed at 45° and minimal brightness at 90° with respect to the analyzer, whereas the FT and FF hydrogels show no discernible brightness variation (Supplementary Fig. 9). This anisotropic ordered structure significantly enhances the mechanical properties. Correspondingly, the FFR hydrogels showed distinct mechanical properties along the longitudinal (∥) and radial (⊥) directions, such as 2.51-fold difference in strength and 5.63-fold in toughness, whereas the randomly distributed hydrogels showed no significant differences (Fig. 2f and g). Although such pronounced anisotropy is advantageous for directional load bearing, it also makes the mechanical performance direction-dependent, with lower toughness and strength in the radial direction. As shown in Fig. 2h and i, both the FT and FF hydrogels exhibited lower Young’s modulus, strength, and toughness. In contrast, the FFR hydrogels demonstrate an ultrahigh Young’s modulus of 73.61 ± 8.29 MPa when stretched parallel to the alignment, along with tensile strength of 41.29 ± 2.10 MPa and toughness of 159.37 ± 28.15 MJ·m−3, without significant loss in tensile strain. Notably, although the FT, FF, and FFR hydrogels initially differ in water content, we conducted additional mechanical tests with all samples adjusted to comparable hydration levels. Under these conditions, the FFR hydrogel still exhibited a strength approximately 13-fold higher than FT and about 3-fold higher than FF (Supplementary Fig. 10). Overall, as shown in the comparison chart of Fig. 2j and Supplementary Table 1, the FFR hydrogels surpass all other reported tough hydrogels in terms of ultimate toughness and tensile stress3,7,8,30–38. Moreover, when subjected to cyclic loading with a tensile strain of 100% over fifty cycles, the pronounced hysteresis in the first cycle indicates initial irreversible network rearrangement, characteristic of a Mullins-type effect. To verify the broad compatibility of the LBSD strategy across different polymer systems, we further applied it to fabricate anisotropic gelatin hydrogels. Although untreated gelatin hydrogels exhibit intrinsically low mechanical strength, the LBSD-gelatin samples demonstrated an increase in toughness of more than an order of magnitude (Supplementary Fig. 12). This result demonstrates that the LBSD strategy is not restricted to PVA and is readily extendable to diverse polymer networks for fabricating bulk hydrogels that possess well-organized hierarchical structures.

Molecular dynamics simulations of shear-induced orientation of PVA chains

To further elucidate the effects of shear force on the orientation of PVA chains in this work, molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using a coarse-grained model. These simulations revealed the orientation and distribution of PVA chains under shear force, quantified by changes in the end-to-end distance and chain distribution. At a polymer concentration of 25 wt% and a shear rate of 45 mm·s−1, the end-to-end distance of the PVA chains along the x-axis (shear direction) increased steadily over time, while minimal changes were observed along the y- and z-axes (Fig. 3a). Initially, PVA chains adopted a randomly coiled conformation, with a narrow chain length distribution (0–22 nm), indicating little alignment (Fig. 3c). As shear time progressed, the PVA chains began to elongate and align along the shear direction, with chain lengths extending to the 43–65 nm range (Fig. 3d). Eventually, the chains exhibited significant stretching and alignment along the x-axis. The distribution shifts further, with a considerable proportion of chains extending into the 65–108 nm range, indicating a shear-induced transformation to a highly ordered state (Fig. 3e). These results demonstrate that shear force promotes chain elongation and preferential alignment along the shear direction. Additionally, Fig. 3b shows a clear correlation between shear rate and chain alignment: as the shear rate increased, the chain lengths along the x-axis extended significantly. This result indicates that higher shear rates lead to faster and more pronounced chain extension and alignment. Furthermore, the influence of polymer concentration on PVA chain alignment was also examined (Supplementary Fig. 13). At a fixed shear rate, higher polymer concentrations also led to greater alignment. Overall, the simulations suggest that chain extension and alignment are more sensitive to shear rate than to polymer concentration.Fig. 3Molecular dynamics simulations reveal shear-induced orientation behavior of PVA chains.a Evolution of end-to-end distance and chain extension along the x-, y-, and z-axes at 25 wt% polymer concentration and 45 mm/s shear rate. b Heatmap showing the increase in x-axis chain length as a function of shear rate and time, indicating enhanced alignment with increasing shear rate. c–e Snapshots of individual PVA chains (i), simulated chain configurations (ii), and corresponding chain length distributions along three axes (iii) at 0 μs (c), 4 μs (d), and 8 μs (e), demonstrating progressive chain stretching and alignment along the shear (x) direction.

Nanostructure characterization of FFR hydrogels

To understand the synergistic effects of flocculation and rolling pressure in this combined tactic, the nanostructures of the PVA hydrogels prepared using different methods were explored by small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) and wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS). The 2D SAXS patterns clearly indicate the crystalline morphology and allow for the analysis of the average distance between neighboring crystalline domains. In contrast to the nearly uniform intensity ring observed in the FT and FF hydrogels, the ellipsoidal SAXS pattern of the FFR hydrogels indicates the highly directional alignment of the polymer crystalline structure (Fig. 4a-c). Furthermore, the scattering vector of the PVA hydrogels is observed at the peak position, denoted as qmax, which corresponds to the radius of the scattering ring. Notably, the FFR hydrogels exhibit the largest qmax. This is further supported by the 1D SAXS profiles (Fig. 4d). To better highlight the peak positions, we multiplied the y-axis of the 1D SAXS profiles for different PVA hydrogels by q² (Fig. 4h). Based on the Bragg equation (D = 2π/qmax), it is evident that the average distance between neighboring nanocrystalline domains (D) in the FFR hydrogels gets smaller with the cooperative treatments of flocculating and rolling pressure. This indicates that the aligned PVA chains promote the formation of additional crystalline domains within the FFR hydrogel3. The 2D WAXS patterns further support this hypothesis (Fig. 4e-g). According to the WAXS results, no significant crystalline domains were observed in the FT hydrogels, and no alignment of crystalline domains was noted in the FF hydrogels. In contrast, the FFR hydrogels displayed significant crystalline domains with a highly oriented microstructure, achieving a Herman’s orientation factor (f) of 0.91 (Fig. 4j and Supplementary Table 2). WAXS 1D patterns of the FFR hydrogel, compared with those of the FT and FF hydrogels with the same polymer content, along with the corresponding integrated scattering intensity as a function of the scattering vector q, are shown in Fig. 4i. The sharpness and intensity of the peak at q = 1.35 Å−1 are significantly enhanced in the FFR hydrogel. This observation suggests that the FFR strategy promotes the formation of abundant crystalline domains by shear-induced chain alignment and facilitates hydrogen bonding between PVA chains. Moreover, in situ SAXS analyses elucidate the mechanisms underlying the exceptional stretchability and toughness of the FFR hydrogel. As tensile strain increases, the diffraction peaks of 2D SAXS become more pronounced along the horizontal axis (Supplementary Fig. 14). This sharpening of peaks signifies a heightened alignment of polymer chains consequent to mechanical stretching. The 1D SAXS patterns further reveal that the average spacing between neighboring crystalline domains increases from 12.8 nm in the unstressed state to 17.4 nm under 80% strain, reflecting the extension of amorphous interstitial chains bridging adjacent nanocrystalline regions. However, the calculated stretch ratio of these chains (e.g., (17.4−12.8)/12.8) is much smaller than the macroscopic applied stretch (e.g., 0.8). Such a discrepancy suggests that, in addition to chain extension, lateral sliding between lamellae may also contribute to the overall deformation process28. It is worth noting that the WAXS patterns exhibit negligible changes with increasing strain (Supplementary Fig. 15). This invariance can be ascribed to the already high degree of orientation in the pristine FFR hydrogels, as reflected by a Herman’s orientation factor of 0.91. Such a high f value indicates that the crystalline domains are densely packed and highly aligned from the initial state, and further stretching produces only minimal structural evolution, which is scarcely reflected in the WAXS patterns. Excess chemical cross-links were introduced to inhibit further crystallization before the DSC measurement. As a result, the calculated crystallinity of hydrogels exhibited a distinct uptrend. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to investigate the intermolecular interactions within PVA hydrogels (Fig. 4n). The analysis of band shape and wavenumbers revealed that the primary functional groups remained unaffected. Specifically, the broad absorption peak at 3275 cm⁻¹, associated with the stretching vibration of intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonds (─OH), exhibited a significant red shift upon treatment with FF and FFR methods. This shift suggests the formation of additional hydrogen bonds, implying enhanced molecular interactions. The peaks at 2936 cm−1 and 1420 cm−1 are associated with the stretching vibrations of C─H and CH241. The peak at 1142 cm⁻¹ serves as a distinctive marker of the crystallinity in polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), corresponding to the C─C stretching vibration. This vibration is coupled with the stretching of the C─O bond, specifically the symmetric stretching of the O─C─C configuration. In contrast, the peak observed at 1093 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the C─O stretching vibration, which is associated with the antisymmetric stretching of the O─C─C bond. Notably, not only does the intensity of this peak increase, but it also undergoes a noticeable red shift. This shift is indicative of a decrease in the water content, which in turn facilitates stronger interactions between the PVA chains in the amorphous region. This phenomenon suggests two key outcomes: firstly, the FFR process significantly enhances the crystallinity of PVA, and secondly, it substantially increases the density of the amorphous regions within the polymer network, further influencing the overall structure and properties of the material.

Multiscale toughening and crack resistance mechanisms in FFR hydrogels

Finite-element analysis (FEA) simulations were conducted to investigate the theoretical fracture behavior of traditional FT isotropic hydrogel and FFR anisotropic hydrogel (Fig. 5a). Predefined cracks were strategically introduced into the hydrogels before the application of tensile forces. The simulation results revealed that the inherently aligned lamellar architecture at the notched tip effectively prevents crack propagation, displaying a large yielding zone and a butterfly-shaped crack pattern. This configuration endows the FFR hydrogel with a damage-tolerant characteristic, thereby enhancing its crack resistance. On the contrary, the crack rapidly propagated within the isotropic hydrogel. Figure 5b presents a POM image illustrating crack propagation in the FFR hydrogel under applied stress. While localized stress concentration occurs at the crack tip, the highly aligned polymer chains effectively inhibit crack propagation, resulting in a more pronounced birefringence pattern. Consequently, the anisotropic lamellar hydrogel achieved a fracture energy of 97.94 ± 14.67 kJ·m−2 along the longitudinal direction, demonstrating an extraordinary enhancement of approximately 653-fold relative to the FT hydrogel and a 13-fold increase compared to the FF hydrogel (Fig. 5c, d, and Supplementary Fig. 16). Comprehensive analyses integrating both experimental data and computational simulations revealed that the FFR hydrogel simultaneously achieved excellent mechanical properties and damage-tolerant architectures. This achievement is primarily attributed to its unusual structure, which spans processes at the micrometre, nanometre, and molecular levels, engaging multiple fracture mechanisms (Fig. 5e). At the micrometre level, the FFR hydrogel consists of tightly aligned lamellar walls, which exhibit a flattened polygonal morphology (Supplementary Fig. 17). Under tensile loading, the densely packed and strongly bonded lamellae suppress interlamellar sliding at low strain, forcing the structure to deform primarily through collective lamellar stretching. With increasing strain, sliding and local separation are gradually activated, together with partial lamellar fracture, which collectively dissipate energy (Fig. 5e(i)). Meanwhile, the compressed three-dimensional lamellar configuration enables efficient stress transmission between the neighboring lamellae, thereby preventing premature structural failure38. At the nanometre level, a high degree of entanglement among aggregated PVA chains promotes the formation of nanocrystalline domains42. The compact network structure and enhanced crystallinity are directly correlated with the excellent mechanical properties (Fig. 4m). During mechanical deformation, certain polymer chains within the hydrogel may rupture due to relatively weak bonding, while those with crystalline domains maintain the material’s high strength. At the molecular level, the FFR fabrication process induces the formation of a substantial hydrogen bond network (Fig. 4n). These hydrogen bonds function as sacrificial bonds; their rupture during the fracture process effectively dissipates significant amounts of energy. This energy dissipation mechanism is crucial for maintaining the hydrogel’s integrity, thereby ensuring both high strength and exceptional stretchability. Consequently, the synergistic interplay between the compact lamellar configuration and the robust cross-linked network underpins the mechanical performance of FFR hydrogels. Crack propagation in FFR hydrogels is mitigated by fatigue-resistant mechanisms that operate across multiple hierarchical scales (Fig. 5e(ii)). In short, at the micrometre level, the pullout and fracturing of lamellar structures serve to delay the advancement of cracks by effectively dissipating mechanical energy. Moving to the nanometre scale, breaking the densely packed and highly aligned polymer network necessitates significantly higher energy per unit area compared to the fracturing of disordered amorphous polymer chains. This breakdown facilitates the absorption of additional energy, thus ensuring the hydrogel’s high fatigue threshold. On the molecular level, the presence of high-functionality crosslinking and the phenomenon of crack pinning act as robust barriers against catastrophic failure, thereby reinforcing the overall mechanical stability of the FFR hydrogel. Enhanced thermal conductivity of FFR hydrogel and heat dissipation demonstrations

The ongoing miniaturization, increased integration, and higher power density in electronic devices have amplified the challenges associated with effective thermal management. Metals, known for their high thermal conductivity, have traditionally been regarded as primary candidates for heat dissipation materials. Nevertheless, they are encumbered by several notable disadvantages, including high weight, susceptibility to corrosion, and environmental impact, all of which limit their application in precision electronic devices. In particular, their high electrical conductivity requires additional insulating layers, making integration complicated and raising reliability concerns. By contrast, polymers offer inherent electrical insulation, light weight, and facile processability, making them indispensable in electronic packaging. To assess the heat transfer efficiency of various hydrogel frameworks, an infrared imaging system was employed to monitor and record the temporal changes in surface temperature of vertically positioned hydrogels on a controlled heating stage. During the heat transfer process, the FFR hydrogel along the longitudinal direction (||) exhibited a significantly higher heat transport rate compared to the FT, FF, and FFR hydrogel along the radial direction (⊥) (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 18). This can be attributed to the fact that, in conventional isotropic polymers, the random orientation of crystalline domains leads to frequent phonon collisions during the thermal transfer process. This disordered spatial arrangement substantially reduces the mean free path of phonons, thereby enhancing phonon scattering and diminishing heat conduction efficiency. Additionally, the amorphous arrangement of polymer chains leads to a loose structure accompanied by numerous air bubbles, which further compromises thermal conductivity. Conversely, ordered composites with regularly aligned crystals facilitate the efficient propagation of phonons along the direction of the crystalline structure. This organized arrangement minimizes phonon scattering, significantly improving the material’s heat transfer efficiency. The anisotropic alignment of crystals forms continuous thermal pathways that allow phonons to traverse the composite along the shortest path, leading to higher thermal conductivity compared to isotropic counterparts43. Besides, in the case of FFR hydrogel, the tightly aligned polymer chains eliminate the impact of air bubbles, further enhancing thermal conductivity (Fig. 6b). These factors collectively lead to a maximum temperature difference of 12.7 °C on the top surface between FFR|| and FT within 300 s (Fig. 6c). To further clarify the anisotropic thermal conduction behavior, schematic illustrations were used to compare heat transport along the in-plane and cross-plane directions of the FFR hydrogel (Fig. 6d). In the in-plane direction, the highly aligned lamellar structures provide continuous phonon pathways, enabling rapid and efficient heat flux. By contrast, cross-plane transport is impeded by interfacial gaps and misalignment between adjacent lamellae, leading to much higher thermal resistance. Consistent with this structural anisotropy, quantitative measurements reveal a striking disparity in thermal conductivity: the in-plane value of the FFR hydrogel reaches 2.18 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹, approximately 3.6-fold higher than its cross-plane counterpart (0.60 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹) (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Table 3). Moreover, it is about 11.5-fold greater than that of the neat PVA matrix10 (0.19 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹). Such a high anisotropy ratio underscores the effectiveness of lamellar alignment in guiding phonon transport and highlights the potential of FFR hydrogels for thermal management. Non-equilibrium molecular dynamics (NEMD) simulations were conducted to elucidate the influence of shear-induced alignment on the thermal conductivity of the hydrogel44,45 (Supplementary Fig. 19). The simulations clearly demonstrate that shear enhances thermal transport efficiency along the shear direction (Supplementary Table 4). To demonstrate the feasibility of using the FFR hydrogel for efficient thermal management in electronic devices, the FFR hydrogel incorporated with graphene oxide (GO) was applied as a chip heat sink in an audio amplifier system (Supplementary Fig. 20). Each FFR hydrogel was securely bonded through layered welding with a PVA solution (Supplementary Fig. 21). For comparison, a commercial radiator was also tested under identical conditions. When a voltage of 24 V was applied for 360 s, the surface temperature of the chip without any heat sink increased significantly, surpassing 45 °C. In contrast, utilizing either the commercial radiator or FFR hydrogel as a heat spreader effectively dissipated the substantial heat generated during chip operation (Supplementary Fig. 22). The FFR hydrogel reduced chip temperature by up to 8.8 °C compared to the blank control group, demonstrating the heat-dissipating capability that outperforms the commercial radiator (Supplementary Fig. 23). Although a small amount of GO was incorporated, it was uniformly dispersed throughout the polymer matrix without forming continuous conductive pathways. Consequently, GO serves merely as a thermally conductive additive, facilitating heat transfer while maintaining excellent electrical insulation. As a result, the electrical conductivity of the FFR heat sink is 4.01 × 10⁻6 S·m−1, significantly lower than that of commercial metal-based heat sinks (7.04 × 102 S·m⁻¹, Supplementary Fig. 24). Discussion

In conclusion, we have demonstrated a generalizable Layer-by-Layer Shear Densification (LBSD) strategy capable of constructing bulk anisotropic hydrogels with precisely aligned hierarchical architectures. This structural engineering yields PVA hydrogels with exceptional mechanical performance, including a tensile strength of 41.29 ± 2.10 MPa and a toughness of 159.37 ± 28.15 MJ·m⁻³. Crucially, the fracture energy of the PVA hydrogel is over 600-fold higher than that of conventional freeze-thawed hydrogels. These enhancements are attributed to the formation of highly organized lamellar structures, increased crystallinity, and robust sacrificial hydrogen bond networks, which collectively mitigate crack propagation and enable efficient energy dissipation. The broad applicability of this strategy was confirmed by its successful extension to gelatin hydrogels. Furthermore, the enhancement of thermal conductivity along the alignment direction indicates the functional advantage endowed by the highly ordered structure. By overcoming the persistent challenge of achieving compact and uniformly aligned hierarchical structures throughout bulk hydrogel volumes, the LBSD strategy offers a general and scalable platform for engineering polymeric materials with precisely tailored multiscale architectures. We envision that these materials hold great promise for a wide range of advanced applications, including high-performance load-bearing components, artificial muscle tissues, bioelectronic devices, and advanced thermal management systems, thereby offering innovative solutions for both structural materials and diverse engineering challenges.

Methods

Materials

Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA, M.W. 146,000–186,000, 99+% hydrolyzed) was purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. Sodium citrate was purchased from Zancheng (Tianjin) Technology Co., Ltd., graphene oxide (GO) dispersion was obtained from Knano Graphene Technology Co., Ltd., and glutaraldehyde (50% in H₂O) was supplied by Aladdin. All chemicals were used as received without further purification. Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm, Millipore Co., USA) was used in all experiments.

Preparation of PVA solution

The 15 wt% PVA aqueous solution was prepared by dissolving PVA powder in deionized water under vigorous stirring at 90 °C until fully dissolved. The solution was subsequently degassed by ultrasonication for 1 h to remove trapped air bubbles, yielding a homogeneous transparent solution.

Preparation of salt solution

The 1.5 M sodium citrate solution was obtained by dissolving anhydrous sodium citrate in deionized water under magnetic stirring, followed by ultrasonication for 10 min to ensure complete dissolution and clarity.

Fabrication of anisotropic PVA hydrogels (FFR hydrogels)

To fabricate anisotropic hydrogels, 40 mL of the PVA precursor solution was kept at 95 °C under continuous stirring. Then, 12 mL of 1.5 M sodium citrate solution was slowly added dropwise into the PVA solution to induce flocculation. After complete formation of PVA floccules, the resulting plasticine-like gel was transferred into a Teflon mold with inner dimensions of 200 mm × 10 mm × 5 mm (length × width × thickness). The surface was leveled with a glass plate, and the sample was frozen at −20 °C for 30 min. The frozen sample was then subjected to repeated rolling-pressure treatment using a custom-built roller system with a roller diameter of 100 mm. The rolling temperature was set at 60 °C and the rolling speed was fixed at 6 rpm. The initial gap between the two rollers was set to 4 mm. After 20 rolling passes, the gap was reduced by 1 mm, followed by another 20 rolling passes. This procedure was repeated until the roller gap reached 1 mm. The sample was then further rolled at a gap of 0.5 mm for 50 additional passes. The resulting sample was denoted as the FFR hydrogel.

Fabrication of isotropic PVA hydrogels (FT and FF hydrogels)

For the FT hydrogels, the PVA precursor solution was poured into Teflon molds and frozen at −20 °C for 12 h, followed by thawing at room temperature for 3 h. For the FF hydrogels, 40 mL of PVA precursor solution was mixed with 12 mL of 1.5 M sodium citrate solution at 95 °C under stirring. After degassing, the PVA floccules were cast into Teflon molds, frozen at −20 °C for 12 h, and subsequently thawed at room temperature for 3 h to obtain FF hydrogels.

Fabrication of anisotropic FFR-GO hydrogels

The 10 mg·mL⁻¹ graphene oxide (GO) dispersion (16 mL) was diluted with deionized water to 40 mL, followed by ultrasonication to obtain a uniformly dispersed 4 mg·mL⁻¹ GO solution. Subsequently, 7.059 g of PVA powder was gradually added to the GO dispersion and heated at 90 °C under continuous stirring until the polymer was completely dissolved. The mixture was further sonicated for 1 h to ensure uniform dispersion of GO within the polymer matrix, forming a well-mixed PVA–GO composite precursor solution. The resulting composite solution was then processed using the FFR strategy described above to prepare anisotropic FFR–GO hydrogels.

SEM characterization

To investigate the internal structure and surface morphology of the hydrogels, samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently freeze-dried. For the FFR hydrogels, the lyophilized specimens were sectioned along directions parallel and perpendicular to the alignment layers to reveal the lamellar structure. The lyophilized samples were sputter-coated with gold and examined using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SIGMA HD, ZEISS) operated at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV.

Confocal fluorescence imaging

To visualize the aligned microstructure, 0.1 wt% fluorescein sodium salt was incorporated into the PVA precursor solution as a fluorescent tracer, followed by hydrogel fabrication using the same FFR procedure described above. The samples were imaged under a Leica DMi8 confocal microscope using a 488 nm laser excitation channel.

AFM imaging

The surface topography of lyophilized FF and FFR hydrogels was analyzed using atomic force microscopy (AFM, tapping mode). The cantilever tip gently scanned the surface, and the height variations were recorded to obtain 2D and 3D surface morphology maps.

POM imaging

Polarized optical microscopy (POM) was conducted using an Olympus BX53 microscope equipped with crossed polarizers. The hydrogel specimens were placed on glass slides and observed while gradually rotating the stage relative to the analyzer to evaluate birefringence and orientation contrast. All micrographs were captured under identical illumination and exposure conditions to ensure consistent qualitative comparison among samples.

FTIR characterization

The specific chemical groups of FT, FF, and FFR hydrogels were analyzed using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Nicolet iN510, Thermo Fisher Scientific). All the hydrogel samples were cut into 1-mm-thick films and then tested in the range of wavenumber from 4000 to 400 cm⁻¹.

X-ray diffraction characterization

FT, FF, and FFR hydrogel samples were characterized using an X-ray diffractometer (D8-A25, Bruker) with Cu Kα radiation. Diffraction patterns were collected over a 2θ range of 5–35° at a scanning rate of 5°·min⁻¹.

X-ray scattering characterization

The wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) measurements were conducted using a Xeuss 2.0 SAXS/WAXS system (Xenocs) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm). The small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) measurements were performed at beamline BL16B1 of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) using an X-ray wavelength of 0.124 nm. The sample-to-detector distance was 2188 mm, and the exposure time was 10 s. The data were analyzed using FIT2D software.

Mechanical measurements

The mechanical properties of the hydrogels were evaluated using a universal tensile testing machine (JHY-5000). Measurement of crystallinity

The crystallinity of the hydrogels was evaluated using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC3, Mettler). To suppress secondary crystallization during air-drying, the samples were chemically crosslinked before measurement. The hydrogels were immersed in 100 mL of a solution containing 10 mL of glutaraldehyde (50% in H₂O) and 1 mL of hydrochloric acid (36.5–38 wt%) for 12 h, followed by rinsing in deionized water for 12 h to remove excess reagents. The treated samples were subsequently dried in an incubator at 40 °C for 6 h. For DSC analysis, the air-dried hydrogels were weighed and sealed in Tzero aluminum pans, then heated from 50 °C to 300 °C at a rate of 5 °C·min⁻¹ under a nitrogen atmosphere. The DSC thermograms exhibited a broad endothermic peak between 60 °C and 180 °C, which was attributed to the evaporation of residual water. Following a previously reported method28, the mass of residual water mresidual was determined using the relation mresidual = minitial · Hresidual/H0water, where minitial is the sample mass before DSC testing, and H0water = 2260 J·g−1 is the latent heat of water evaporation. In addition, the distinct endothermic peak observed at 200–250 °C corresponds to the melting of PVA crystalline domains. The mass of the PVA crystalline domains mcrystalline can be calculated as mcrystalline = minitial · Hcrystalline/H0crystalline, where H0crystalline = 138.6 J g−1 represents the enthalpy of fusion for fully crystalline PVA at its equilibrium melting point. Accordingly, the crystallinity can be estimated by Xdry = mcrystalline/(minitial - mresidual).

Thermal management test

The thermal conductivity (k) of the hydrogels was determined based on the relation k = α×ρ×Cp, where α, ρ, and Cp represent the thermal diffusivity, density, and specific heat capacity of the hydrogel, respectively. The thermal diffusivity was measured at 30 °C using a laser flash analyzer (LFA 457, NETZSCH). The thermal management performance of the chip system was evaluated using an audio amplification device equipped with different heat sinks. An infrared thermal imaging camera (Ti480U, Fluke) was employed to record the real-time evolution of surface temperature during operation.

Measurement of resistance

The electrical resistance of the heat sink samples was determined by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) using an electrochemical workstation (CHI660E, Shanghai Chenhua). The measurements were conducted within potential ranges of −2 to 2 V and −0.5 to 0.5 V. The resistance (R) was obtained from the slope (k) of the current–voltage (I–V) curve according to R = 1/k.
