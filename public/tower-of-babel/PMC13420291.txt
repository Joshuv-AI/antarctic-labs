Feasibility and Safety of Median Sternotomy Combined With Hemi‐Clamshell Incisions for the Resection of Extremely Large Tumors in the Thoracic Cavity: A Case Series
Authors: Xiaoyun Li, Mei Xu, Mingyu Fan, Xiaojun Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo evaluate the feasibility and safety of median sternotomy combined with hemi‐clamshell thoracotomy for resection of extremely large intrathoracic tumors. The perioperative data of three patients with large tumors in the thoracic cavity from January 2021 to December 2024 were collected and analyzed retrospectively, and median sternotomy combined with the hemi‐clamshell approach to achieve tumor resection was described. All these patients underwent tumor resection and pneumonectomy. Two patients developed postoperative complications but recovered well with effective treatment, and the other patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery without any major complications.Keywords: enormous tumor, hemi‐clamshell, median sternotomy, resection, thoracic

Introduction

Currently, there are no universally accepted standards for the clinical diagnosis of massive thoracic tumors [1]. Generally, tumors with a diameter exceeding 10 cm or occupying more than 40% of the hemithorax are referred to as massive thoracic tumors [2, 3]. These tumors are infrequently observed in adults and are predominantly benign, including solitary fibromas, teratomas, thymomas, and neurofibromas [1]. Surgical resection remains the primary treatment modality [4]. To expose the tumor adequately and resect it successfully, the surgical approach is crucial [5]. Traditional surgical approaches include posterior lateral incisions, clamshell incisions, and midline incisions [4, 6]. However, in patients with massive tumors that occupy the hemithorax, particularly those with chest wall involvement or severe intrathoracic adhesions, traditional incisions may hinder safe tumor resection. In this study, we successfully employed a combined median sternotomy and hemi‐clamshell incision to perform tumor resection and pneumonectomy and analyzed the feasibility and safety of this surgical approach.

Case History/Examination

This study collected and analyzed three patients with extremely large tumors in the thoracic cavity who were treated from January 2021 to December 2024. There were two males and one female. The median age was 27 years (range, 20–56 years). The clinical symptoms included cough, dyspnea, chest pain, hemoptysis, weakness, and chest tightness. None of those patients had any comorbidities before surgery. All of those patients had extremely large tumors, and the sizes of those tumors were 20 × 15 × 10, 18.5 × 15.4 × 31.8, and 18.6 × 16.1 × 21.2 cm3, respectively. Among those patients, lymphangioma was diagnosed in one patient, a germ cell tumor was diagnosed in one patient, and a solitary fibrous tumor was diagnosed in one patient. The patient with a germ cell tumor received four cycles of chemotherapy, but the therapeutic evaluation was PD (progressive disease) according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors, version 1.1. The other two patients did not receive any therapy before surgery.

All patients underwent preoperative examinations, including lung function, heart function, contrast‐enhanced brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), whole‐body bone scanning, and contrast‐enhanced chest and abdominal CT scanning, to exclude distant metastasis and to ensure that patients could tolerate surgery. Contrast‐enhanced chest CT revealed that all the tumors were located fully in the hemithorax cavity and that the lung tissue was invaded by the tumor (Figure 1). In addition, the hilus pulmonary structures and heart were compressed and pushed to the contralateral hemithorax cavity without invading mediastinal structures such as the superior vena cava (SVC), aorta, or trachea (Figure 1). Moreover, the pulmonary artery in the pericardium is not invaded, indicating a high probability that it can be dissected from its origin in the pericardium (Figure 1).

Surgical manipulations and procedures from one typical case. Patient 1 (Figure 1) was a 17‐year‐old male who presented with severe cough and dyspnea. He was diagnosed with a mixed germ tumor that was not sensitive to chemotherapy, and the tumor progressed after four cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The chest CT revealed an extremely large mass of approximately 20 × 15 × 10 cm3 located fully in the left thoracic cavity, and the lung was invaded by the tumor. Therefore, en bloc resection of the tumor and pneumonectomy were needed. Given that the tumor occupies the whole thoracic cavity, it is extremely difficult to expose the tumor adequately via a posterior lateral incision, especially when the tumor strongly adheres to the chest wall, and it is nearly impossible to expose the hilum of the lung through such an incision. Therefore, median sternotomy was used to perform surgery on the basis of previous experience [7].

The patient was initially placed in the supine position, and general anesthesia was administered via a double‐lumen endobronchial tube. The patient underwent a median sternotomy, and then the pericardium was opened to expose the left pulmonary artery trunk, which could be freed and dissected (Figure 2A). Then, the pulmonary veins in the pericardium were also removed and dissected (Figure 2B). Anatomically, the left main bronchus was behind the pulmonary artery and veins; therefore, the left main bronchus could also be dissected easily after previous procedures (Figure 2C). The hilum structures of the lung, including the pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, and main bronchus, were dissected via median sternotomy, and the next step involved removing the tumor. Owing to the large size of the tumor, achieving adequate exposure through a midline incision is challenging, which compromises surgical visibility. A good surgical field is particularly critical, especially when the apex of the thoracic cavity is manipulated, where insufficient exposure can increase the risk of damage to the subclavian artery and vein, leading to life‐threatening hemorrhage. Therefore, a hemi‐clamshell incision along the fourth rib is essential (Figure 3). One 28Fr silicone chest drain was placed at the eighth intercostal space along the posterior axillary line on the surgical side. This approach allows for complete exposure of the tumor, particularly to expose the apex of the thoracic cavity and the diaphragmatic surface of the tumor (Figure 4B,C), thereby enhancing surgical visibility and minimizing the risk of bleeding.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

All patients underwent extensive tumor resection and pneumonectomy successfully via median sternotomy combined with hemi‐clamshell thoracotomy. Among them, two patients received right pneumonectomy, and the other patient received left pneumonectomy. The median operation time was 6 h (range, 5–7.5 h), and the median blood loss volume was 2500 mL (range, 1500–4000 mL). All patients received transfusions, and the median transfusion volume was 2300 mL (range, 1200–3100 mL). Postoperatively, all patients were admitted to the ICU for further supportive care; the median in‐ICU stay of those patients was 4 days (range, 3–15 days), and the median in‐hospital stay of those patients was 14 days (range, 12–21 days). The postoperative pathologic diagnosis was yolk sac sarcoma in one patient, and the other two patients were diagnosed with fibroma and lymphangioma. The postoperative course was uneventful for one patient. One patient developed acute liver and kidney failure and was successfully cured with continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), one patient developed chylothorax and heart failure with stable hemodynamics, and the patient's condition was effectively resolved by noninvasive ventilation, enhancement of cardiac contractility, appropriate reduction in systolic blood pressure, and diuresis to reduce the blood volume load and a fat‐free diet. We conducted our last follow‐up in December 2024, and all of those patients were alive without disease recurrence. The median overall survival time was 20 months (range, 8–34 months) (Tables 1 and 2).

The combination of median sternotomy with a hemi‐clamshell incision for the resection of thoracic tumors, including complete lung resection, is both safe and feasible for certain patients. The median incision facilitates the dissection of pulmonary structures such as the pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, and main bronchus, whereas the hemi‐clamshell incision provides sufficient exposure of the tumor and a good surgical field. However, our study is based on small retrospective samples, and future research with larger sample sizes is necessary to substantiate the clinical feasibility and safety of this surgical approach.

Discussion

The incidence of large thoracic tumors is relatively low, and such cases are rare in clinical practice. Typically, these tumors grow slowly, allowing the lungs sufficient time to compensate, and patients may remain asymptomatic. However, when the tumor reaches a significant size, it can cause a shift in the affected area, compressing the heart, lungs, and major blood vessels, and leading to the manifestation of corresponding symptoms [8]. The patients presented in this study had tumors occupying the hemithorax cavity and invading lung tissues, leading to compression of healthy lung tissue and resulting in symptoms such as chest tightness, cough, dyspnea, and hemoptysis. Large tumors in the hemithorax cavity threaten patients' lives, and surgery is the primary modality of treatment because the administration of internal medications for the management of large thoracic tumors is often proven to be ineffective [4]. The selection of an appropriate surgical incision is paramount to ensure optimal exposure of the tumor and to achieve complete resection. Traditional incisions include posterior lateral incisions, clamshell incisions, and median incisions [4, 6, 8, 9]. However, for large tumors occupying the hemithorax cavity, especially those with chest wall involvement or severe adhesions, traditional surgical incisions have difficulty providing adequate tumor exposure and optimal surgical visualization. In such cases, lateral thoracic incisions frequently do not penetrate the pleural cavity effectively and fail to adequately expose the hilus pulmonis and the tumors. The traditional clamshell incision requires horizontal sternotomy, resulting in a large surgical field and potential complications such as sternal displacement or separation postoperatively. Simultaneously, the clamshell incision entails the sacrifice of the internal mammary arteries, thereby limiting future options for coronary revascularization [10]. Median sternotomy has traditionally been the accepted approach for dealing with mediastinal tumors or bilateral pulmonary disease, but exposure to the lower lobes and access to tumors with extensive involvement of the hemithorax are often difficult [8]. Studies on the combined use of median sternotomy and hemi‐clamshell incisions for the resection of large thoracic tumors are rare, and their safety and feasibility require further investigation. In our study, the tumors in all patients occupied the hemithorax cavity fully and invaded the lung tissue, causing severe compression of the pulmonary and mediastinal structures. However, owing to the protective nature of the pericardium, tumors typically do not invade structures within it. In the majority of cases, the pulmonary artery and pulmonary veins within the pericardium are not directly invaded by the tumor, indicating a high probability that it can be dissected from its origin in the pericardium. Therefore, it is feasible to dissect the pulmonary artery, pulmonary vein, and main bronchus via a median incision. However, it is difficult to achieve en bloc resection of large tumors due to inadequate exposure, and a hemi‐clamshell incision through the fourth intercostal space is needed to expose the tumor adequately, providing a good surgical view. Considering the large size of the tumors and potential severe adhesions in the thoracic cavity, as well as the need for extensive exposure of pulmonary structures, we ultimately opted for the combined surgical approach of median sternotomy and hemi‐clamshell incision.

This surgical approach has several advantages. First, median sternotomy allows surgeons to free out and expose vital mediastinal structures such as the azygos vein, SVC, ascending aorta, brachiocephalic arteries, and subclavian arteries; therefore, we can preblock the proximal part of vessels such as the left subclavian arteries and azygos vein to avoid life‐threatening bleeding caused by accidental vessel damage, especially when the apex of the thoracic cavity is freed. Second, opening the pericardium can expose the left pulmonary artery trunk and veins within the pericardium, and we can also free out and expose the right pulmonary trunk between the SVC and the ascending aorta (Figure 5); therefore, we can ligate and dissect the pulmonary artery and veins within the pericardium. Third, further opening the pericardium behind the right pulmonary artery trunk can expose the trachea and the left and right main bronchi (Figure 5), allowing for precise bronchial management and bronchial carina lymph node removal. Moreover, the subsequent hemi‐clamshell incision at the fourth intercostal space provides good surgical visibility when retracted by a rib spreader.

The right pulmonary artery trunk behind the SVC and the ascending aorta and the bronchial carina behind the pulmonary artery trunk are also shown.

Since the pulmonary structures (pulmonary arteries, pulmonary veins, and main bronchi) have already been dissected via median sternotomy, the risk of major bleeding during tumor resection is reduced, thereby increasing surgical safety. The supine position of the patient allows full exposure of major vascular structures through median sternotomy. In the event of accidental damage to critical vessels such as the pulmonary arteries or veins and leading to life‐threatening bleeding, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) can be established easily if necessary to ensure hemostasis and surgical safety. Furthermore, a large tumor mass can lead to significant compression of the trachea and heart, which can be more severe in the lateral position. The supine position helps avoid further compression during surgery, reducing the risk of intraoperative hemodynamic instability.
