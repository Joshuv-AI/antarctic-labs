Where Does Toxicity Live? Mechanistic Localization and Targeted Suppression in Language Models WARNING: The content contains offensive model outputs and is toxic.
Himanshu Beniwal, Mayank Singh

Abstract
Large language models frequently generate toxic, hateful, or harmful content, yet existing mitigation methods rely on costly retraining or output-level filtering with no mechanistic insight into where toxicity originates internally. We introduce Meow2X and TRNE , two complementary retraining-free frameworks that localize toxicity to specific layers and neurons by analyzing activation differentials between toxic and neutral prompts, then suppress them via inference-time scaling or minimal rank-one weight edits—without any gradient descent. Evaluations across five LMs, two benchmarks, and 90 configurations using dual safety evaluators demonstrate consistent toxicity reduction while preserving language modeling quality. Our analysis reveals that toxicity is disproportionately encoded in early MLP layers, varies across architectures, and is systematically underestimated by single-evaluator setups—underscoring the need for multi-evaluator safety assessment. By bridging mechanistic interpretability with practical detoxification, our framework offers a principled path toward safer, more transparent language models. Related Work

Detoxification of Language Models.

Prior work on toxicity mitigation in language models has largely focused on training-time interventions, including supervised fine-tuning, preference optimization, and reinforcement learning from human feedback, as well as on output-level filtering and rejection sampling
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Beniwal et al. 2025
;
Sap et al. 2019b
)
. While effective at reducing harmful generations, these approaches treat toxicity as a surface-level behavior and provide limited insight into how such behaviors arise internally
(
Dathathri et al. 2019
)
. Moreover, they typically require retraining or additional inference-time classifiers
(
Costa-jussà et al. 2024
)
. In contrast, our work operates directly on internal representations and performs targeted inference-time interventions without modifying model parameters.

Interpretability via Internal Representations.

Mechanistic interpretability research has shown that neural behaviors can often be localized to specific layers, neurons, or substructures
(
Zhang and Nanda 2023
)
. Methods such as Network Dissection
(
Bau et al. 2017
)
and representational similarity analysis
(
Kornblith et al. 2019
)
demonstrate that internal activations encode semantically meaningful features and that behaviors can be traced to localized components. These findings motivate our approach, which explicitly compares mean activations induced by toxic and neutral inputs to identify layers and neurons that disproportionately contribute to toxic behavior
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Siu et al. 2025
)
.

Targeted and Causal Interventions.

Recent studies have explored the causal manipulation of internal components through neuron ablation, attention head suppression, or feature scaling, demonstrating that selective intervention can reliably alter model outputs
(
Li and Janson 2024
;
Dai et al. 2022
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
. However, many such approaches rely on manual selection or task-specific heuristics. Our method systematizes this process by using activation differentials and contribution-based scoring to automatically identify toxic layers and apply controlled scaling through forward hooks, enabling reproducible and architecture-agnostic intervention
(
Rai et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
.

Evaluation and Positioning.

Unlike prior detoxification methods that rely on a single evaluator or aggregate metrics, our framework employs multiple independent safety classifiers and jointly measures toxicity reduction and language modeling quality
(
Yang et al. 2025a
;
Dutta 2024
)
. Overall, our work bridges safety and interpretability by framing toxicity as an internal representational phenomenon
(
Suau et al. 2024
)
. Rather than only suppressing harmful outputs, we focus on revealing where toxicity is encoded within the model, providing both practical mitigation and mechanistic insight into models.

Positioning Against Baselines.

We position our work relative to the closest prior methods:

Whispering Experts

Suau et al. 2024
suppresses toxic neurons but
requires task-specific expert identification and does not generalize
across architectures without per-model re-identification.

SafeEdit

Wang et al. 2024
applies knowledge editing via
gradient-based updates but requires backpropagation at inference time.

DExperts

Liu et al. 2021
and
PPLM

Dathathri et al. 2020

are output-level steering methods that cannot localize toxicity to
specific model components. Unlike all of the above, Meow2X operates
without any parameter updates, backpropagation, or expert model, making it
strictly lighter at inference time. A full quantitative comparison is left
to future work as it requires standardizing generation hyperparameters,
decoding strategies, and evaluation prompts across codebases—a non-trivial
engineering challenge acknowledged by prior surveys
Villate-Castillo et al. 2024
.

Methodology - Meow2X

As highlighted in Algorithm
1
, we explain the operations as:

3.1
Toxic Layer Detection

We identify toxic layers by analyzing systematic differences in internal activations elicited by toxic and neutral inputs.
Given a pretrained language model
M
with
L
model layers, we focus on either attention blocks, MLP blocks, or both, depending on the experimental setting.

Activation Collection.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{t}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}
denote sets of toxic and neutral prompts, respectively.
For each layer
\ell
, we collect mean activation vectors by registering forward hooks during inference and averaging hidden states across tokens and batch samples.
We compute separate mean activations for toxic inputs
\mu_{t}^{\ell}
and neutral inputs
\mu_{n}^{\ell}
using batched forward passes to ensure stable estimates.

Activation Differentials and Layer Scoring.

To quantify toxicity-specific behavior, we compute the activation differential

\Delta^{\ell}=\mu_{t}^{\ell}-\mu_{n}^{\ell}
for each layer.
We assign a scalar toxicity score to each layer by aggregating absolute differences across neurons,

S^{\ell}=\|\Delta^{\ell}\|_{1},

where higher values indicate stronger representational divergence induced by toxic prompts.
This score provides a coarse but robust signal for ranking layers by toxicity relevance.

Neuron-Level Contribution Analysis.

Beyond layer-level scoring, we estimate the concentration of toxicity within a layer.
For each
\Delta^{\ell}
, we identify toxic neurons as those whose absolute activation difference exceeds the
90^{\text{th}}
percentile.
We then compute a contribution ratio

c^{\ell}=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}}|\Delta^{\ell}_{i}|}{\sum_{i}|\Delta^{\ell}_{i}|},

where
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
denotes the set of toxic neurons in layer
\ell
.
This captures whether toxicity arises from a small subset of highly influential neurons or is diffusely distributed.

Layer Selection.

We select toxic layers using either a Top-
k
strategy, which chooses the
k
layers with the highest
S^{\ell}
, or a dynamic thresholding strategy.
In the latter, layers are selected if their score exceeds a data-dependent threshold defined by the mean and standard deviation of
\{S^{\ell}\}
, with safeguards in place to enforce minimum and maximum numbers of selected layers.
Both strategies operate identically across model architectures and component types.

Outputs.

The detector returns (i) the set of selected toxic layers, (ii) layer-wise toxicity scores
S^{\ell}
, and (iii) neuron-level contribution statistics
c^{\ell}
.
These signals are subsequently used to guide targeted inference-time interventions while preserving the original model parameters.

3.2
Layer Deactivation

Mitigation Strategies.

Following toxic layer identification, we intervene directly at inference time by modifying intermediate activations of selected model components. Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the set of detected toxic layers. For each layer
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, we register forward hooks that rescale the output activations of either the attention block, the MLP block, or both, depending on the experimental setting. These interventions are lightweight, do not alter model parameters, and can be removed after generation.

3.2.1
Deactivation

The
deactivation
strategy applies uniform suppression to all selected layers.
For each
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, the layer output is multiplied by a constant scaling factor
\alpha=1-s
, where
s\in(0,1]
is the deactivation strength. This strategy aggressively attenuates toxic representations by uniformly reducing their contribution to downstream layers. To avoid destabilizing the forward pass, the scaling factor is clipped to a minimum value
\alpha_{\min}
, ensuring that information flow is preserved.

3.2.2
Dampening

The
dampening
strategy performs moderate suppression by applying a reduced scaling factor relative to deactivation.
Instead of fully applying the specified strength, activations are scaled by
\alpha=1-\frac{s}{2}
, resulting in a smoother attenuation of toxic signals. This strategy is particularly effective when toxicity is distributed across multiple layers and excessive suppression may harm fluency or coherence.

3.2.3
Adaptive Scaling

The
adaptive
strategy introduces contribution-aware suppression by conditioning the scaling factor on each layer’s estimated toxicity contribution.
For a layer
\ell
, the scaling factor is computed as
\alpha_{\ell}=1-s\cdot\frac{c_{\ell}}{\max_{\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}}c_{\ell^{\prime}}}
, where
c_{\ell}
denotes the normalized contribution score derived from high-magnitude activation differences. Layers with stronger toxic influence are thus suppressed more aggressively, while layers with weaker contributions remain largely intact. As with other strategies,
\alpha_{\ell}
is lower-bounded by
\alpha_{\min}
to maintain numerical stability.

Practical Considerations

All mitigation strategies are applied only during generation and are removed immediately afterward, preserving the original model for subsequent evaluations. The approach is fully model-agnostic, supports attention-only, MLP-only, or combined interventions, and integrates seamlessly with both top-
k
and dynamic threshold–based layer selection.

4
Methodology - TRNE

4.1
Method Description

4.1.1
Contrastive Gradient Localization

For each input
x
, we backpropagate through
-\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}(x)

and register backward hooks on target modules to capture gradient norms. Layers where toxic inputs elicit substantially stronger gradients than neutral inputs are selected as toxic regions.

4.1.2
Pre-Projection Activation Collection

At each selected hotspot layer
l
, we register forward hooks on the
output projection (attention:
o_proj
; MLP:
down_proj
)
to capture input activations at the last token position,

\phi_{l}(x)=h_{l}^{(\text{last})}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}
.
Mean activations
\mu_{T}
and
\mu_{N}
are computed over the toxic and
neutral sets respectively; the difference
\delta=\mu_{T}-\mu_{N}

defines the toxic direction at that layer.

4.1.3
Rank-One Weight Update

The key formula modifies the weight matrix:

W_{\text{new}}=W-\alpha\times\frac{\texttt{torch.outer}(\delta,\mu_{T})}{\|\mu_{T}\|^{2}+\epsilon}

This creates a “detector” (
\mu_{T}
) and a “correction” (
\delta
). When future input looks toxic (similar to
\mu_{T}
), the matrix automatically subtracts the toxic direction. Neutral inputs (orthogonal to
\mu_{T}
) pass through unchanged.

Analogy
: Like calibrating a speaker system. You identify which frequencies cause distortion (localization), measure the distortion pattern (activation collection), then adjust the equalizer to cancel exactly that distortion without affecting clean frequencies (rank-one update).

Toxicity is evaluated using two independent safety classifiers,

5.4
Metrics

The effectiveness of detoxification is measured by comparing aggregate toxicity rates before and after mitigation.
Let
U_{\text{before}}
and
U_{\text{after}}
denote the fraction of unsafe generations; toxicity reduction is defined as
\Delta U=U_{\text{before}}-U_{\text{after}}

Model utility is assessed via perplexity (PPL), computed using teacher-forced likelihood with attention-mask weighting.
In addition, we report layer-level toxicity scores
S^{\ell}
, neuron contribution ratios
c^{\ell}
, and applied scaling factors
\alpha^{\ell}
to analyze safety–utility trade-offs at a mechanistic level.

(RQ4)
Is gradient update efficient to update the models?

While TRNE achieves toxicity reduction on Llama-family models with moderate edit strength and few layers, aggressive hyperparameters can catastrophically degrade model quality. On Gemma-2-2B-it with high edit strength (20) and many layers (10), MLP editing caused perplexity to explode from 29.9 to 1744.6, rendering the model incoherent (As shown in Figures
14
,
15
, and
16
). Simultaneously, toxicity increased by 21 percentage points on PolyGuard—the opposite of the intended effect.
This fragility stems from the cumulative nature of weight updates: each layer modification compounds across multiple layers. When the total perturbation exceeds the model’s tolerance, coherent generation collapses. We recommend low-to-moderate edit strength (1 to 5) and editing only 3 to 5 layers as safe operating ranges, with architecture-specific tuning for non-Llama models.

Finding
:
Weight editing is fragile—high edit strength or too many layers causes model collapse with perplexity exploding.

(RQ5)
Safety Evaluators Measure Different Aspects of Model Behavior?

LlamaGuard and PolyGuard produce dramatically different toxicity assessments on identical outputs. Baseline measurements differ by 25 to 40 percentage points, with PolyGuard consistently detecting far more toxic content. More critically, the evaluators disagree on the effect of attention editing: LlamaGuard reports improvement while PolyGuard reports degradation.
This disagreement reveals that the evaluators measure fundamentally different phenomena. LlamaGuard focuses on explicitly harmful content generation, while PolyGuard additionally penalizes refusal behavior and evasive responses. When attention layers are edited, models may become more likely to refuse requests, which PolyGuard considers problematic, but LlamaGuard ignores.
This has important implications for toxicity research. Reporting results from a single evaluator can be misleading. A method that appears successful under one evaluator may fail under another. We recommend using multiple evaluators with different design philosophies to obtain a complete picture of model behavior changes.

Inference
:
Safety evaluators disagree—LlamaGuard and PolyGuard often report opposite effects, revealing they measure fundamentally different behaviors.

7
Conclusion

This work demonstrates that toxic outputs in language models can be effectively mitigated by detecting and modifying a small set of specialized neural pathways. Both deactivation and dampening achieve mean toxicity reduction of 25% across diverse model scales. We find that larger models are more amenable to targeted intervention, suggesting that toxic knowledge is localized rather than distributed. Selecting the top-5 toxic paths by activation ratio proves sufficient for substantial toxicity reduction without comprehensive model retraining. Future work should explore architecture-specific optimization strategies and measure downstream impacts on model helpfulness and factuality. This approach offers a practical path toward safer language models through interpretable neural interventions. These findings highlight that effective detoxification requires component-aware editing, conservative hyperparameters, and evaluation across multiple safety benchmarks.

Limitations

Our method operates in a strictly inference-only, parameter-frozen setting, and therefore does not capture toxicity mitigation achievable through fine-tuning, LoRA, or RLHF. Toxicity localization is derived from mean activation differences over a fixed subset of prompts, which may underrepresent rare, long-range, or highly contextual toxic behaviors. Although our experiments focus on decoder-only models, the core methodology—activation collection, component-wise scoring, and inference-time scaling—is architecture-agnostic in principle. Adapting the framework to other architectures (e.g., encoder–decoder models or mixture-of-experts) would require identifying corresponding attention and feed-forward modules and appropriately placing forward hooks, without altering the underlying detection or intervention logic. Finally, our evaluation relies on automated safety classifiers, such as PolyGuard and LLaMA-Guard, which may introduce bias or misclassification; we do not include human judgments.

Ethics

This work focuses on analyzing and mitigating toxic behavior in language models using inference-time, non-invasive interventions that do not require retraining or the deployment of new models. All experiments are conducted on publicly available datasets containing potentially harmful language, which are used solely for research purposes and never redistributed. While the proposed method aims to reduce harmful outputs, automated toxicity evaluators may reflect existing biases; therefore, our results should not be interpreted as a complete guarantee of safety. We emphasize that this work is intended to support safer model behavior and informed deployment, not to enable misuse or censorship. We acknolwedge the use of AI assistants to help rewrite the content to improve readability and writing.

B.1
Contributions

1.

Entropy-based contrastive localization
: We identify toxicity-contributing layers by backpropagating through negative output entropy
-\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}(x)=\sum_{v}p_{v}\log p_{v}
and computing differential gradient norms between toxic and neutral inputs.

2.

Pre-projection activation collection
: We hook the
input
to output projections (
o_proj
,
down_proj
) at the last token position, capturing
\phi_{l}(x)=h_{l}^{(\text{last})}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}
.

3.

Direct rank-one weight modification
: We apply
\mathbf{W}\leftarrow\mathbf{W}-\alpha(\boldsymbol{\delta}\otimes\mathbf{k}^{\top})/(\|\mathbf{k}\|^{2}+\epsilon)
without gradient descent, using
torch.outer(delta, key)
.

4.

Norm-constrained editing
: We enforce
\|\Delta\mathbf{W}\|_{F}\leq\gamma\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}
(default
\gamma=0.1
) to prevent excessive parameter deviation.

5.

Component-wise analysis
: We systematically evaluate attention projections, MLP projections, and combined editing across TinyLlama-1.1B, Qwen2.5-1.5B, Gemma-2-2B, Phi-2, and Llama-3.2-3B.

6.

Dual toxicity evaluation
: We assess using both LlamaGuard-3-8B and PolyGuard classifiers with before/after comparison.

B.2
Method

Given toxic texts
\mathcal{T}
and neutral paraphrases
\mathcal{N}
from ParaDetox
Logacheva et al. 2022
;
Dementieva et al. 2024
, TRNE modifies output projection weights in three phases.

Phase 1: Contrastive Gradient Localization

For each input
x
, we compute the output distribution entropy at the last token position:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}(x)=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{v}(x)\log(p_{v}(x)+\epsilon),\\
\quad p(x)=\text{softmax}(\text{logits}(x))\end{split}

(1)

We backpropagate through
-\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}(x)
(maximizing entropy encourages gradient flow through decisive pathways) and register backward hooks on target modules to capture gradient norms:

\begin{split}g_{l}^{T}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}}\|\nabla_{h_{l}}(-\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}(x))\|_{2},\\
\quad g_{l}^{N}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}}\|\nabla_{h_{l}}(-\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}(x))\|_{2}\end{split}

(2)

The contrastive toxicity score
s_{l}=g_{l}^{T}-g_{l}^{N}
identifies layers with differential response. We select top-
K
layers (default
K=5
) for editing.

Phase 2: Pre-Projection Activation Collection

For each selected layer
l
, we identify the output projection:

•

Attention
:
o_proj
(or
out_proj
,
dense
)
\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

•

MLP
:
down_proj
(or
fc2
,
w2
,
c_proj
)
\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\text{int}}}

We register forward hooks on these projections to capture their
input
(not output) at the last token position:

\phi_{l}(x)=h_{l}^{(\text{last})}(x)=\texttt{input[0][:, -1, :]}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}

(3)

Activations are aggregated:
\mathbf{A}_{T}^{(l)}=\{\phi_{l}(x):x\in\mathcal{T}\}
,
\mathbf{A}_{N}^{(l)}=\{\phi_{l}(x):x\in\mathcal{N}\}
.

Phase 3: Rank-One Weight Update

For each selected layer, compute mean activations and edit components:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{A}_{T}|}\sum_{h\in\mathbf{A}_{T}}h,\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{N}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{A}_{N}|}\sum_{h\in\mathbf{A}_{N}}h,\\
\quad\boldsymbol{\delta}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{N},\quad\mathbf{k}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}\end{split}

(4)

Dimension handling
: If
\dim(\boldsymbol{\delta})=d_{\text{in}}\neq d_{\text{out}}
(e.g., MLP down-projection), project to output space:
\boldsymbol{\delta}\leftarrow\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\delta}
.

Rank-one update
(implemented via
torch.outer
):

\mathbf{U}=\alpha\cdot\frac{\boldsymbol{\delta}\otimes\mathbf{k}^{\top}}{\mathbf{k}^{\top}\mathbf{k}+\epsilon},\quad(\boldsymbol{\delta}\otimes\mathbf{k}^{\top})_{ij}=\delta_{i}\cdot k_{j}

(5)

Norm constraint
: If
\|\mathbf{U}\|_{F}>\gamma\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}
, scale:
\mathbf{U}\leftarrow\mathbf{U}\cdot\gamma\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}/\|\mathbf{U}\|_{F}

Apply edit
:
\mathbf{W}.\texttt{data}\leftarrow\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{U}
(in-place modification of
proj.weight.data
)

B.3
Effect Analysis

For input activation
\mathbf{h}
, the forward pass through the edited projection yields:

\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}}\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{h}-\alpha\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{k}^{\top}\mathbf{h}}{\|\mathbf{k}\|^{2}+\epsilon}

(6)

When
\mathbf{h}\approx\mathbf{k}
(toxic pattern):
\mathbf{k}^{\top}\mathbf{h}\approx\|\mathbf{k}\|^{2}\Rightarrow
output shifts by
-\alpha\boldsymbol{\delta}
. When
\mathbf{h}\perp\mathbf{k}
(neutral):
\mathbf{k}^{\top}\mathbf{h}\approx 0\Rightarrow
output unchanged. This selective suppression preserves neutral behavior while mitigating toxic outputs.
