The NIE is a classified document published by the NIC that assesses a national issue and provides information on likely future events.

Through a formalized process, <a href="https://www.amazon.com/US-Intelligence-Community-Jeffrey-Richelson/dp/0813345111">the NIC produces NIEs</a> to project existing military, political, and economic trends into the future and to estimate for policymakers the likely implications of those trends.

Nominally, NIEs are perhaps the most authoritative written judgements of the IC, but other intelligence products, like the <a href="https://www.intelligence.gov/publics-daily-brief/presidents-daily-brief">President’s Daily Briefs</a> (PDBs) are generally considered to be more influential on policy decisions.</p><p>In January 1999, the NIC published its first environmentally focused National Intelligence Estimate, which assessed that Russia would be “unable to deal effectively with the formidable environmental challenges posed by decades of Soviet and post-Soviet environmental mismanagement.” (Document 1) The estimate—while not referencing climate change—highlights pollution, hazardous waste, and ecological degradation as pressing issues that could destabilize the region, a departure from the IC’s usual focus on adversarial threats.Through a formalized process, <a href="https://www.amazon.com/US-Intelligence-Community-Jeffrey-Richelson/dp/0813345111">the NIC produces NIEs</a> to project existing military, political, and economic trends into the future and to estimate for policymakers the likely implications of those trends.

This report signaled the beginning of a broader understanding of environmental factors as relevant national and regional security concerns.</p><p>Shortly after, the NIC prepared its “Global Trends 2015” report, the second publication of its kind, which provided policymakers and citizens with a long-range view of an array of possible futures.<a href="#_edn9" name="_ednref9">[9]</a> (Document 2) Rather than make specific predictions about the world, Global Trends reports are designed to provide an analytic framework for policymakers as they craft national security strategy and navigate uncertain futures.

The hearing allowed Democrats and Republicans to question Fingar and other witnesses on the newly completed, but still classified, NIA.</p><p>Domestically, Fingar warns of severe water scarcity in the Southwest, increasingly frequent wildfires, and powerful storms on the East and Gulf coasts, which could threaten nuclear power plants, oil refineries, and U.S. military installations. The testimony also discusses potential impacts on the military, which Fingar notes would find its capacity overstretched from disease outbreaks, food scarcity, and land clashes, and having to help alleviate increasingly common humanitarian emergencies around the world.</p><p>According to Fingar’s testimony, the NIC would plan to analyze three subtopics in greater detail: the security risks of climate change for individual countries; cooperation and competition among the world’s great powers, including the United States, Russia, China, and India; and the linkage between national security and possible climate change mitigation strategies.</p></div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>In 2021, the National Intelligence Council released what it called its first-ever National Intelligence Estimate on Climate Change which asserts that climate change will exacerbate long-standing threats to global security. The assessment shows a deepening concern within the U.S. security apparatus that shifts unleashed by climate change could reshape U.S. strategic interests, offer new opportunities to rivals such as China, and increase instability in nuclear states such as North Korea and Pakistan.</p><p>The assessment predicts that, by 2040, climate change will have significant destabilizing effects worldwide, influencing international conflict, migration patterns, and resource availability. Key findings of the NIE emphasize that climate change will: exacerbate geopolitical tensions; drive increased migration; challenge military operations; and catalyze new security risks, such as how insurgent groups or criminal organizations might exploit climate-related vulnerabilities.</p></div>
