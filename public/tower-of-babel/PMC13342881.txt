A comprehensive and standardized dataset on school feeding activities worldwide: The Global Survey of School Meal Programs
Authors: Arlene Mitchell, Ayala Wineman, Heidi Kessler, Yale Warner, Shivani Gharge, Emily Fredenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSchool meal programs—in which students receive meals, snacks, or take-home rations—reach over 400 million children throughout the world. They aim to address multiple objectives, most commonly to improve children’s nutrition and health and to meet education goals by facilitating and incentivizing children’s school attendance and learning. Yet despite their prevalence and evidence of impact, the data available on large-scale school meal programs have historically been fragmented and inconsistent, making it difficult to discern trends over time or compare school meal activities across different settings. Advocates, policy makers, analysts, and practitioners have all confronted the same challenge: a scarcity of comprehensive and standardized information on school meal programs. The Global Child Nutrition Foundation (GCNF)—supported by an array of international partners and partially funded by the U.S. Department of Agriculture—has sought to address this oversight through the Global Survey of School Meal Programs©. The Global Survey was launched in 2019 to gather information about school meal programs from national governments in every country in a standardized manner, and it has been repeated every 2–3 years. The survey is designed to be completed by a country “focal point” who is officially appointed by their government to liaise with the necessary entities to gather information for the survey and government approval for the information submitted. This survey dataset spans a broad set of topics of relevance to school feeding, including coverage/reach, targeting of beneficiaries, menu composition, food procurement and distribution, sources of funding, program governance, links to local agriculture and the private sector, and health and sanitation complementary services. It also tracks impacts over time, including the effects of shocks such as the COVID-19 pandemic, to support comparison across countries, inform investments, and enable research and advocacy. The dataset enriches the broader literature on school feeding and provides a starting point for further research on this topic.Keywords: Child nutrition, Diet quality, Food policy, Food systems transformation, Hunger, Social safety net

Background

School meal programs1 reach over 400 million children throughout the world. In 2018, the Global Child Nutrition Foundation (GCNF) undertook a systematic effort to gather information on school feeding in each country worldwide. GCNF surveyed both the academic and grey literature to understand the “landscape” of school meal programs, inclusive of their level of coverage (number of beneficiaries), food basket contents, and complementary programs, among other topics. From this exercise, GCNF confirmed that the information available was fragmented and inconsistent across countries and even across programs operating within the same country. Moreover, information was not collected or published regularly, making comparisons across settings or the identification of trends over time impossible. This revealed a need for a global database that periodically gathers standardized information on all school-based feeding activities—an observation echoed in the literature [5]. The Global Survey of School Meal Programs© was launched in 2019 to fill this gap. The survey collects key information on school feeding in each country and has been repeated every 2–3 years, documenting school meal programs worldwide in a consistent and comprehensive manner.

The survey dataset has been used in various peer-reviewed papers, demonstrating its applicability for wide-ranging analyses of school feeding programs. Studies drawing on these data have examined trends in school meal funding [6]; the multi-sectoral implications of universal school feeding [7]; the landscape of school meal programs on a regional scale [8]; and the role of particular commodities within school menus [9]. The data from the Global Survey of School Meal Programs have served as a foundation for the World Food Program’s periodic State of School Feeding Worldwide report (2020, 2022, 2024) [10], as well as other databases. Together with recent reviews of the evolving evidence around school meal programs—which highlight persistent gaps regarding school feeding costs and the role of school meals in food systems transformation, among other topics [11,12]—these studies underscore both the value of the dataset and the continued need for robust, comparable data in this field.

Data Description

Data coverage

The Global Survey of School Meal Programs was first launched in 2019 with participation from 105 countries [13]. It was conducted a second time in 2021 with data from 139 countries [14] and was repeated a third time in 2024 with data from 144 countries [15]. Altogether, this forms a global database with information on school feeding from 167 countries to date [16].

The percentage of participating countries varies by region, with the greatest participation rate seen in Sub-Saharan Africa (with 94% of countries participating) and the lowest rate seen in the Middle East/North Africa (Table 1). Across income groups, the rate of participation varies within a range of 81–92% of countries, with the highest rates seen among low-income and lower middle-income countries. In total, 91% of the world’s population resides in the 167 countries that participated in at least one round of the survey. Again, this rate varies across region, with nearly 100% of the population of Sub-Saharan Africa residing in the 45 countries that participated, and 90% of the population of Latin America & the Caribbean residing in the 27 countries that participated in the survey.

Many countries have participated in the survey multiple times. In fact, 81 countries have so far participated three times, and 142 countries have participated at least twice.

Reference years

The survey is retrospective, ensuring that governments can report information that is complete, vetted, and ready for release. Thus, the first round of the survey, conducted in 2019, captured information on the most recently completed school year in each country. For most countries, this was the 2017–18 school year. The second survey round, conducted in 2021, captured information on the school year that began in 2020, and the third round, conducted in 2024, captured information on the school year that began in 2022.

Survey structure

In each round of the survey, the questionnaire has been structured approximately as shown in Fig. 1, which is drawn from the 2024 survey round. The survey asks about national or large-scale school feeding programs, including those that are managed or administered by the national, regional, or local government, or large-scale school-based feeding programs that are managed by a non-governmental entity but in coordination with the national government. It also includes programs that do not involve the government but reach a substantial share of students in the country.

Survey structure.

The survey includes 10 sections. Three sections contain national-level questions that are completed just once for each country. The remaining seven sections contain program-level questions, meaning they are completed separately for each large-scale school meal program that is operating in the country.

The survey contains some questions that are binary (yes/no), categorical, or numeric, and also contains some that are open-ended (text-based). Questionnaires are made available to the public along with the dataset.

Glossary

A key challenge in the field of school feeding is a lack of a shared vocabulary among stakeholders. Terms such as “decentralized management”, “geographic targeting”, or “implementing partner” may be understood differently in different regions of the world or by different types of school feeding stakeholders. GCNF has therefore developed a detailed glossary of terms associated with school meals to accompany the questionnaire.

Country reports

The survey has been used to create a set of concise, colorful country reports (similar to fact sheets) that document, in a standard format, the status of school meal programs in each country. These reports are available on the GCNF website (at https://gcnf.org/country-reports/), and their content is also made available in the form of analyzed data (as described below).

Data folders

The 2021 and 2024 survey data are made available in both Stata and Excel formats. There are three folders: (1) Survey Data - Excel Format; (2) Survey Data - Stata Format; (3) Country Report Data - Excel and Stata Format

Sub-folders

Raw data (Survey data folders)

The data file named “Survey Status” includes all 194 countries. This specifies whether each country has detailed data in the database (for the reference school year); or responded to the survey by indicating that they had no large-scale school feeding programs in the reference school year; or does not have data in the survey database.

The data file named “country_level” includes variables from the survey that are at the country level. The data file named “country_program_level” includes variables that are at the program level. Each program has a unique program ID within a given survey round.

The other data files with names such as “table_A2” and “table_D4” correlate with these tables in the survey questionnaire. In Stata format, many variables have detailed variable labels. In Excel format, these variable labels are in the first row of each data file.

Among the files is a document titled “Orientation to Data Set” which includes detail on the structure, content, and interpretation of each data file.

Analyzed data (Country report data files)

The Excel file named “Global Survey of School Meal Programs - Country Reports - Survey Waves 1–3 –[date]” contains preconstructed variables, as used in the country reports, from all survey waves that have been conducted so far.

These variables have been carefully pre-constructed from the survey data by GCNF. This file includes the survey waves conducted in 2019, 2021, and 2024. A sample of these pre-constructed variables with brief descriptions is presented in Table 2. The file contains sheets for each survey wave, as well as a sheet that reports only the most recent information available for each country. Description of select variables.

Merging/linking data files

Merging together the country-level and program-level files is straightforward. At the country level, the unique identifier is “Country Code” or “Country”. At the program level, the unique identifier within each survey round is “Program ID”, and there is also a variable for “Country”. Users can merge together program-level files using the program ID variable and can merge together the program and country-level files using the country code or country name.

Missing values

In Stata, all missing values have been coded as “.” Values may be missing because of skip rules in the survey, or because the information was not provided (i.e., the survey was incomplete). Users should apply careful discretion when determining whether to leave missing values as-is or to interpret them according to the logic of skip rules.

Types of variables

As noted earlier, variables are alternately numeric, categorical, or string. The survey included some open-ended questions with text entries. These narratives, as entered by the survey respondents, are included in the data set as string variables. The text has sometimes been translated to English using an automated translator (particularly for surveys submitted in Arabic, Russian, or Chinese), though in some cases, the text has been retained in the language in which it was originally written.

Child population and student enrollment numbers

The survey asks respondents to report on the child population and student enrollment in the country during the reference school year. When respondents cannot provide this information themselves, these numbers are drawn from the UNESCO Institute of Statistics database [17]. In these cases, the numbers reflect the year 2017 (for the 2019 survey wave), 2020 (for the 2021 survey wave), and 2022 (for the 2024 survey wave).

In a small number of cases, survey respondents prefer that population numbers not be published if the original source of the numbers could not be confirmed or because the topic can be sensitive. In those cases, the population numbers are not published within this dataset.

It is possible for the number of enrolled students in a given school level (e.g., primary) to be greater than the number of children of school age. This situation can arise for various reasons. If children begin their schooling at an advanced age, they may be enrolled in a given school level even if they are outside the range of ages expected for that school level. In addition, population numbers may be extrapolated from the most recent census, which can lead to imprecision. Finally, different numbers may have been submitted by different offices/ministries with sets of information that have not been reconciled.

Exchange rates and monetary variables

Monetary variables are reported in the raw data files in various currencies, denoted using three-letter currency codes. Next to these variables, GCNF has inserted an exchange rate that can be used to convert the values to U.S. dollars (USD). This exchange rate is the average of the exchange rate that prevailed at the beginning and end of the reference school year in each country. Monetary variables in the “Global Survey of School Meal Programs - Country Reports - Survey Waves 1–3 – [date]” Excel file are reported in USD.

Study Design, Materials and Methods

Inclusive process of survey design

In the course of developing the survey tool in each survey wave, GCNF solicited input on the proposed topics, questions, and survey design. For example, in the first round, GCNF received comments from around 25 experts representing various institutions. For the second and third survey round, GCNF embarked on an even more inclusive process of refining the survey questionnaire and the methodology of data collection. Toward this end, GCNF circulated an online feedback form and held (virtual) stakeholder consultations with individuals from all over the world who had engaged with the survey in previous years, whether as survey respondents, data users, or school feeding advocates. The input received through this process is used, in each survey round, to improve the survey and ensure it meets the needs of its many users.

Survey pilot

Prior to the first survey round, four countries (Benin, Nepal, São Tomé and Príncipe, and the United States) volunteered to participate in a pilot exercise. The governments of these countries completed a pilot version of the survey, after which some revisions were made and the survey was finalized.

Data collection period

Data collection for the first survey round took place throughout 2019, with priority directed to low-income and lower middle-income countries. As noted earlier, the 2019 survey asked for data from “the most recently completed school year”, which for most countries was the 2017–18 school year. Data collection for the second survey wave took place from July 2021 to March 2022, with the survey capturing information for the school year that began in 2020. Data collection for the third survey round took place from November 2023 to July 2024, gathering information on the school year in each country that began in 2022.

Topics covered in the surveys

School meal programs are associated with a range of health, nutrition, and education-related outcomes for children [18]. As reflected in Fig. 1, the Global Survey of School Meal Programs collects data on coverage of school feeding; characteristics of beneficiaries; menu and food sources; funding; program management and implementation; health and nutrition; infrastructure; agriculture, employment, and community participation; monitoring and evaluation; program sustainability; and successes and challenges of school feeding programs. In the second survey round, a module was included to capture the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and understand how school meal programs responded to this crisis.

The third survey round also included several new features. This round had a greater focus on topics related to the environment, reflecting a growing interest in the potential for school meals to catalyze food systems transformation [19]. The module on the contents of the school food basket was revised so that the food categories are mostly aligned with those used in the Global Diet Quality Score (GDQS) [20]. The GDQS is novel in the manner in which it accounts for both healthy and unhealthy food consumption.

Although there are some differences across survey rounds, the Global Survey has emphasized continuity and comparability across years to enable the tracking of trends.

Data collection process

GCNF maintains a survey team, with each team member assigned a set of countries in accordance with their language skills. These individuals are positioned throughout the world and are responsible for outreach to country governments to secure their cooperation.

The surveys are made available as a fillable PDF form, a Word document, and an online survey. Overwhelmingly, the fillable PDF format has been preferred. The survey has been made available in English and six or seven other languages, including Arabic, Chinese, French, Portuguese, Russian, and Spanish (with Japanese included in the third survey round).

In each survey round, an invitation to participate in the survey is extended to all national governments. These include all countries with a seat in the United Nations, as well as those with observer status, summing to 194 countries. These invitations are issued through public calls for participation (via GCNF’s newsletter and website, as well as other networks) and through individual outreach to contacts in each government.

If a country’s government wants to participate in the survey, they convey an official commitment to complete a survey. GCNF requests that each government designate a “focal point”, an individual who is knowledgeable about school feeding activities in the country and/or is well-connected and able to gather information from others to complete the survey. The focal point often needs to communicate with various departments/ministries and program implementers to coordinate the survey submission. Throughout this process, the GCNF survey team member responsible for a given country is on hand to answer any questions and provide guidance through the survey process (see Fig. 2). The focal point is then responsible for obtaining government clearance for the survey results to be included in the public-facing global database.

Once the focal point has submitted a survey, the GCNF survey team reviews the submission for any points that are not clear, possibly returning to the focal point with questions of clarification. When all points are clarified to the extent possible, the survey is considered final, and the country’s submission is added to the global database on school meal programs.

Steps taken to ensure data quality

A frequent concern regarding the Global Survey relates to data quality and how information gathered at the country level can be vetted. GCNF follows a series of steps to validate the data received through the Global Survey of School Meal Programs.

First, the survey is designed to address key topics multiple times. This survey structure presents an opportunity to verify that the survey is internally consistent and to probe the focal point’s understanding of a given topic from multiple angles. In reviewing the data, the survey team also checks for outliers (unusually large, small, or odd values) relative to the global distribution of responses. Values that may potentially be typos or misunderstandings are confirmed with the focal point in a collegial dialogue before the survey is finalized.

The longitudinal nature of the Global Survey has resulted in a reference library of historical information. This enables the survey team to compare information received in a given survey round to earlier responses submitted by the same country. Altogether, this ensures that the ‘story’ makes sense.

Where feasible, information is triangulated with secondary sources of information, such as the monitoring reports of the EU School Fruit, Vegetables, and Milk Scheme. As noted earlier, each focal point is appointed by their government and expected to clear the final submission with their respective ministry/office, in accordance with protocols (including data quality checks) within their own institutions.

Finally, a “country report” (similar to a fact sheet) based on each survey submission is created and shared with the focal point. This serves as another opportunity for the government to review their responses and possibly amend the information submitted.

Limitations

The survey data are self-reported by national governments through designated focal points. Although efforts have been made to ensure clarity in survey responses and to document and explain discrepancies with each country’s survey submission, it is not possible to independently verify the accuracy of all responses received. It is also important to acknowledge that the responses to some questions may reflect the focal points' access to information, as well as the priorities or interpretations of the original sources of information (e.g., ministries, agencies, or program implementers). Likewise, it is possible (though not verifiable) that some submitted responses are shaped by political sensitivities or perceived international expectations, as focal points necessarily operate within social and institutional environments that can influence what information is made accessible or visible. While this data set is the most comprehensive resource on school food worldwide, and the underlying survey process is intentionally designed to promote government ownership of the information gathered, the inherent limitations of sourcing information from national governments should be noted.

In addition, due to the comprehensive nature of the survey and the unavailability of certain data, not all questions are answered by all countries. Consequently, the dataset is not universal, and analysis based on this data resource is necessarily limited to the countries and/or programs for which responses are provided for each question.

Ethics Statement

The first round of the Global Survey of School Meal Programs was reviewed by the University of Washington Institutional Review Board and was deemed to be exempt from further review, as this study does not constitute human subject research. The names and contact information of focal points are removed prior to dissemination of the data set, and no private information is gathered. The survey instructions included the following statement: “The focal point is responsible for gaining any necessary approval for the information to be made available in a public database. Submission of a completed survey is understood to indicate that government approval has been received.”
