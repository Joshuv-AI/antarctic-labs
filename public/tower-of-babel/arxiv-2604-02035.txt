Reinforcement Learning for Speculative Trading under Exploratory Framework
Yun Zhao, Alex S.L. Tse, Harry Zheng

Abstract
We study a speculative trading problem within the exploratory reinforcement learning (RL) framework of Wang et al. 2020 . The problem is formulated as a sequential optimal stopping problem over entry and exit times under general utility function and price process. We first consider a relaxed version of the problem in which the stopping times are modeled by the jump times of Cox processes driven by bounded, non-randomized intensity controls. Under the exploratory formulation, the agent’s randomized control is characterized via the probability measure over the jump intensities, and their objective function is regularized by Shannon’s differential entropy. This yields a system of the exploratory HJB equations and Gibbs distributions in closed-form as the optimal policy. Error estimates and convergence of the RL objective to the value function of the original problem are established. Finally, an RL algorithm is designed, and its implementation is showcased in a pairs-trading application.



Keywords:

Reinforcement learning; sequential optimal stopping; exploratory framework; entropy regularization; stochastic control; relaxed control; entry-and-exit strategies; pairs-trading

Introduction

Machine learning in finance is a rapidly growing field that is gradually transforming how financial institutions operate and make decisions. One of its most promising sub-fields is the use of deep and reinforcement learning (RL) for quantitative finance applications, where examples include derivatives pricing, portfolio optimization and algorithmic trading. RL adopts a model-free perspective: agents choose actions in an unknown environment by learning from interactions, and they optimally balance the exploration of the environment and the exploitation of the learned knowledge. RL has shown great success in various fields, including playing games such as Go
[
Silver et al. 2016
;
Silver et al. 2017
]
, training large language models
[
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2022
;
Guo et al. 2025
]
, controlling robotics
[
Levine et al. 2016
;
Haarnoja et al. 2019
]
, managing energy
[
Chen et al. 2022
]
, and many more.

Central to RL is the balance between curiosity and greediness: if the agent explores too much, they may never exploit the learned knowledge and in turn fails to consistently perform the action that delivers the highest reward; if the agent exploits too much, they may never sufficiently explore the unknown environment and thus may fail to discover a state or an action that gives better rewards. It is therefore crucial to develop a systematic way to balance exploration and exploitation in RL algorithms. In discrete time, entropy-regularized RL formulations address this by adding the entropy of the exploration strategy to the objective, with a temperature parameter governing the importance of exploration
[
Ziebart et al. 2008
;
Nachum et al. 2017
;
Fox et al. 2017
;
Neu et al. 2017
]
.
With continuous time, state and action space,
Wang et al. 2020
introduce an exploratory formulation of a stochastic control framework for RL in which Shannon’s entropy quantifies the cost of exploration. The policy is a probability measure over the action space and the reward is penalized by the exploration cost. A distributionally dispersed policy yields higher entropy (less penalty) which encourages exploration, whereas a deterministic policy (i.e., a Dirac measure) incurs infinite negative entropy and thus is always sub-optimal.

Wang et al. 2020
focus on the linear-quadratic (LQ) setting and show that the optimal policy is Gaussian, aligning with the widespread use of Gaussian exploration in RL algorithms.
The exploratory framework has since been extended to general exploratory HJB equations
[
Tang et al. 2022
]
,
policy gradient and Q-learning algorithms
[
Jia and Zhou 2022
;
Jia and Zhou 2023
]
, mean-variance portfolio selection
[
Wang and Zhou 2020
]
, mean-field games
[
Guo et al. 2022
]
,
jump diffusions
[
Gao et al. 2025
]
, and optimal stopping problems
[
Dong 2024
;
Dai et al. 2026
]
, among others.

RL is well suited to algorithmic trading because it can learn trading strategies from data without strong assumptions on the market dynamics.
This paper extends the framework of
Wang et al. 2020
to a sequential stopping problem in the context of speculative trading. In such problem, the agent optimally times the entry and exit of an investment opportunity and their goal is to maximize the expected utility associated with the round-trip profit net of any transaction costs. Our setup can encapsulate many different trading applications. One example is the model of
Tse and Zheng 2023
, in which the agent needs to decide the optimal time to first purchase and then to sell an indivisible risky asset to maximize their expected utility under Prospect Theory preference. Another example is pairs-trading (e.g.,
Leung and Li 2015
) where the price spread of two strongly co-moving stocks can be described by some mean-reverting process, and the agent needs to strategically enter and exit the trade to maximize profit.

In a nutshell, this paper presents a theoretical and methodological framework featuring the following three elements simultaneously: (i) a dynamic optimization problem involving sequential (rather than one-off) stopping; (ii) a continuous-time, exploratory RL framework under general price dynamics and utility functions which bypasses modeling assumptions; and (iii) a suitable formulation of the randomized policy that captures the spirit of exploratory RL in a classical sense. More specifically, our work contributes to the growing literature of continuous-time RL approach for optimal stopping on both the theoretical and computational front, as summarized below.

•

Theory.
We extend the continuous-time RL framework in
Wang et al. 2020
,
Dong 2024
and
Dai et al. 2026
to a sequential (entry and exit) optimal stopping problem under general diffusion dynamics and utility functions. Following the randomization idea of
Dong 2024
, we model the stopping times via the jump times of two Cox processes driven by deterministic intensity processes. Upon introducing two new auxiliary variables which respectively describe the current trading regime (before entry, after entry but before exit, and after exit) and the reference entry price of an existing open position, we reformulate the sequential stopping problem as a relaxed Markovian control problem over two intensities processes bounded above some constant
M
.

While the stopping times are randomized, the corresponding controlled intensities are still deterministic. To encourage exploration, we propose a regularized version of the problem where the control is now replaced by the probability measure over the intensities and the agent’s reward is penalized by Shannon’s differential entropy governed by a temperature parameter
\eta
. We then derive a system of HJB equations for three value functions, one for each possible trading regime. We also obtain the optimal exploration densities in closed-form as Gibbs distributions, which parameters are driven by the economic advantage of opening or closing the position.

Furthermore, we establish error bounds for the RL objective relative to the original value function in terms of the intensities bound
M
and the temperature parameter
\eta
, and prove convergence when
M\to\infty
and
\eta\downarrow 0
with
M^{2}\left|\eta\ln\eta\right|\to 0
. Another novelty of our approach concerns the definition of the entropy considered. Instead of taking entropy with respect to product Lebesgue measure as in
Wang et al. 2020
, we take the entropy relative to the uniform distribution, so it is nonpositive and gives monotone convergence of the value function. Our analysis relies on probabilistic techniques, in contrast to the PDE-based arguments in the extant literature such as
Dong 2024
;
Dai et al. 2026
.

•

Numerics.

We demonstrate the methodologies developed using a concrete example of pairs-trading. The underlying price dynamics is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process that represents the stochastic spread between two risky assets. After solving the HJB system by finite differences method iteratively, we validate the impact of
M
and
\eta
on our error bounds. Our numerical results confirm the distinctive regularization effects of
M
and
\eta
for the learning problem. A low
M
prevents the agent from entering or exiting the trade in time to capture the profit, while a high
\eta
induces excessive exploration and weak exploitation.

We further investigate the optimal entry and exit decision via examining the densities of the stopping intensities to illustrate the trading behaviors of the agent. We also quantify the effect of mean reversion speed and volatility: strong mean-reversion is economically (dis)advantageous if the spread is far from (close to) its long-run mean, while higher volatility generally increases the expected trading profit.

Finally, we design an offline, model-free RL algorithm with policy iteration and deep neural network value approximation, and we demonstrate that it closely matches the benchmark solution from the HJB solver.

We conclude the introduction by discussing some related works.
Optimal stopping has a long and rich history in both the areas of stochastic analysis and financial mathematics. See for example
Peskir and Shiryaev 2006
for an overview of the solution techniques, which typically involve characterizing the value function via a variational inequality and/or a free boundary problem.
The classical solution methods tend to suffer from the curse of dimensionality. As a result, there has now been a large literature on the use of deep learning to solve an optimal stopping problem.
Sirignano and Spiliopoulos 2018
;
Zhao and Zheng 2025
solve variational inequalities for high-dimensional optimal stopping via deep neural networks.
Becker et al. 2019
;
Becker et al. 2021
approximate the optimal stopping rule in high dimensions using backward induction or forward schemes.
Wang and Perdikaris 2021
;
Reppen et al. 2025
estimate the free boundaries with neural networks. These methods however have limitations, including for example the reliance on specific state dynamics assumptions to deduce the variational inequalities to be solved, time-discretization errors that arise from approximating the stopping time over a discrete time-grid, and the strong structural/regularity assumptions imposed on the stopping region.

In terms of model-free RL approach,
Dong 2024
is the first to apply the exploratory formulation of
Wang et al. 2020
to stopping problems.
They randomize the stopping time using Bernoulli variables, so that the policy is an intensity process that continuously modulates the probability of stopping.
This yields a continuous control formulation and an offline RL algorithm.
Their convergence analysis, however, is restricted to American put pricing (equivalent to a specific piecewise linear utility function) under geometric Brownian motion (GBM). Furthermore, the policy is a non-randomized intensity process: the only source of randomness is an exogenous Bernoulli variable, so the agent is not choosing a distribution over actions and hence it does not constitute exploration in the traditional sense of RL. In contrast, we consider sequential stopping under general diffusion dynamics and utility functions, and we randomize the intensity itself using a probability measure and penalize exploration via Shannon’s differential entropy. The agent determines the optimal sampling strategy over the candidate policies by directly balancing the exploration-exploitation tradeoff, which aligns better with the exploratory spirit of RL. We also cap the intensity by a constant
M
to limit over-exploitation. With this approach, we get a numerically more stable HJB equation system because the source term is log-exponential, not exponential in
Dong 2024
.

Another related work is
Dai et al. 2026
. They start with a penalized PDE associated with the variational inequality and reformulate the problem as a binary-action stochastic control problem. Then an exploratory formulation with Bernoulli entropy regularization is applied to the latter where the control is now a probability measure over the binary action. There are some important differences between their approach and ours. First, we consider sequential (entry and exit) stopping rather than one-off stopping. Second, the parameterization of the stopping strategy is different. In
Dai et al. 2026
, the reformulated stochastic control problem yields a binary optimal control that partitions the state space into the stopping set and the continuation set. In turn, the randomized policy obtained in their exploratory formulation can be viewed as the classification probability of whether a given state is in the stopping set or not. Such classification probability nonetheless does not explicitly suggest how exploration shall be performed in the context of the original optimal stopping task. Meanwhile, our stopping times are parameterized by controlled intensities of Cox processes, and our exploration policy is a probability measure over the capped intensity range rather than a classification probability. Since the intensity directly corresponds to the stopping rate, our formulation naturally and directly results in an exploratory stopping rule for the original problem. Although the state and policy space are now higher-dimensional, we are still able to obtain a closed-form optimal policy and the resulting HJB system is decoupled such that the computational cost remains comparable. More generally, echoing Footnote 2 of
[
Dai et al. 2026
]
, the practical relevance of our approach via stopping intensity might become even more significant for a continuous-time optimal stopping problem under which a randomized stopping time is optimal (without entropy regularization). A prime example of such setup is optimal stopping under probability weighting, where
Henderson et al. 2017
;
Henderson et al. 2018
characterize the optimal strategy via non-degenerate stopping intensity functions.

Speculative trading and intensity-relaxed problem

2.1
Original problem - three stage optimal stopping

Let
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P}\right)
be a filtered probability space satisfying the usual conditions and
W=(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a standard Brownian motion adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
The underlying signal
P
follows a general one-dimensional diffusion process:

dP_{t}=\mu(P_{t})dt+\sigma(P_{t})dW_{t},\quad t>0,\quad P_{0}=p.

(1)

In the speculative trading problem, the agent seeks the optimal time to enter and then exit a trading opportunity. The entry time
\tau
and exit time
\nu
are stopping times in
\mathcal{T}:=[0,\infty]
. When the trade is initiated at time
\tau
, a cost proportional to
P_{\tau}
is paid. When the trade is closed at time
\nu
, a payoff proportional to
P_{\nu}
is received. The goal of the agent is to find the optimal pair
(\tau,\nu)
to maximize their discounted expected utility over the round-trip profit net of any possible transaction costs. Mathematically, we define the value function at inception (i.e., before the entry of the trade) as

V_{\text{orig}}(p)=\sup_{\tau,\nu\in\mathcal{T}:\tau\leq\nu}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\rho\nu}U(\gamma P_{\nu}-\iota P_{\tau}-\Psi-R)\mathds{1}_{\{\nu<\infty\}}\,\middle|\,P_{0}=p\right],

(2)

where
U:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous increasing utility function,
\rho>0
is a subjective discount rate, and
\gamma,\iota,\Psi,R
are constants.

Remark 2.1
.

\gamma,\iota
can be viewed as some scaling parameters that describe the possible mismatches between the signal values and the actual traded prices. For example, if
P>0
represents the price of some asset, we can set
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
\iota\geq 1
to represent the proportional transaction costs on sale and purchase respectively. Meanwhile,
\Psi\geq 0
can be viewed as the fixed transaction costs in a round-trip trade, and
R\in\mathbb{R}
can be viewed as some reference point against which the profit is evaluated (c.f. a Prospect Theory preference model). One could also set
R=-w_{0}
where
w_{0}
represents the agent’s initial wealth. Then
\gamma P_{\nu}-\iota P_{\tau}-\Psi-R=\gamma P_{\nu}-\iota P_{\tau}-\Psi+w_{0}
represents the agent’s total wealth when the trade is closed.

Remark 2.2
.

The signal process
P
may take negative values.
For example, under a pairs-trading strategy, the signal can be
P_{t}=S^{A}_{t}-S^{B}_{t}
, where
S^{A},S^{B}
are the price processes of two stocks.
Then, opening position at time
\tau
refers to buying
S^{A}
and selling
S^{B}
, and closing position at time
\nu
refers to liquidating the trade via selling
S^{A}
and buying
S^{B}
.
If proportional transaction costs
\gamma
and
\iota
are present, then
the payoff shall be
\gamma(S^{B}_{\tau}+S^{A}_{\nu})-\iota(S^{A}_{\tau}+S^{B}_{\nu})
.
This cannot be correctly captured by our one-dimensional model because
\gamma P_{\nu}-\iota P_{\tau}=\gamma(S^{A}_{\nu}-S^{B}_{\nu})-\iota(S^{A}_{\tau}-S^{B}_{\tau})
.
The problem of pairs-trading with proportional transactions cost would require us to consider a two-dimensional model to describe the joint dynamics of
(S^{A},S^{B})
. When we study a pairs-trading application in
Section

4
, we focus on the special case of
\gamma=\iota=1
such that it becomes sufficient to model the one-dimensional process
P_{t}=S^{A}_{t}-S^{B}_{t}
.

Remark 2.3
.

We require
\rho>0
to be sufficiently high to ensure the problem yields a finite value function. See
1
and
Section

2.1
below. Our theoretical analysis depends heavily on the assumption of large positive
\rho
. More generally, consideration of zero discount rate will lead to two fundamental subtleties. First, it is no longer priori clear whether the value function is finite without further assumptions on
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
U(\cdot)
. Second, from an economic modeling viewpoint one needs to carefully distinguish the payoff to the agent in the cases of
\tau=\nu=\infty
and
\tau<\infty=\nu
, which can arise as an optimal solution. It is because trading losses can no longer be infinitely deferred and discounted away such that an agent might not be willing to enter the trade at all if the investment opportunity is bad. In the model of
Tse and Zheng 2023
with zero discount rate,
the objective is defined as

\sup_{\tau,\nu\in\mathcal{T}:\tau\leq\nu}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{U}(\tau,\nu)\,\middle|\,P_{0}=p\right],

where

\mathcal{U}(\tau,\nu)=\begin{cases}U(\gamma P_{\nu}-\iota P_{\tau}-\varPsi-R),&\tau<\infty,\;\nu<\infty,\\
U(-R),&\tau=\nu=\infty,\\
U(-\iota P_{\tau}-\varPsi-R),&\tau<\infty,\;\nu=\infty.\end{cases}

In particular, the fixed transaction cost
\Psi
only materializes if the agent decides to enter a trade (i.e.,
\tau<\infty
) while the reference point
R
is always present as a part of the agent’s preference specification. When discounting is introduced, the payoff in the second and the third case shall be replaced by zero to capture the behaviors that utility value realized in the distant future should have zero net present value. This inspires our definition of the objective as per
2
.

2.2
Intensity-relaxed problem

To facilitate the connection to a standard exploratory RL framework, we first convert the entry and exit decisions into continuous controls. Given
M>0
, define
\mathbb{M}:=[0,M]
.
Choose two processes
\bm{\alpha}:=(\alpha_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
\bm{\beta}:=(\beta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that
\alpha_{t},\beta_{t}\in\mathbb{M}
.
Denote
u_{t}:=(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t})
and
\bm{u}:=(u_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, which will be our control processes.

Starting from the signal process, we augment it to the controlled process
X^{\bm{u}}=(X_{t}^{\bm{u}})_{t\geq 0}
where
X_{t}^{\bm{u}}=(P_{t},J_{t}^{\bm{u}},B_{t}^{\bm{u}})\in\mathbb{R}\times\{0,1,2\}\times\mathbb{R}
. Economically,
J_{t}^{\bm{u}}\in\{0,1,2\}
is an indicator representing the current stage of the speculative trading problem:
J_{t}^{\bm{u}}=0
indicates that the agent has not initiated the position yet;
J_{t}^{\bm{u}}=1
indicates that the agent is holding the position and has not closed the trade yet; and
J_{t}^{\bm{u}}=2
indicates that the agent has already completed the round-trip transaction (and there is no further decision to be made thereafter).
B_{t}^{\bm{u}}
records the signal value when the position is initiated (we manually set
B_{t}^{\bm{u}}=0
if never opening). The initial state of the problem is
X_{0}^{\bm{u}}=x:=(p,0,0)
.

X^{\bm{u}}
is controlled in the following way. When
J_{t}^{\bm{u}}=0
, the agent can choose to enter the market by setting an intensity
\alpha_{t}\in\mathbb{M}
, and the probability of entering the market in the time interval
[t,t+dt)
is
\alpha_{t}dt
. When
J_{t}^{\bm{u}}=1
, the agent can choose to exit the market by setting an intensity
\beta_{t}\in\mathbb{M}
, and the probability of exiting the market in the time interval
[t,t+dt)
is
\beta_{t}dt
. When
J_{t}^{\bm{u}}=2
, the round-trip trade has already been completed where the agent cannot re-enter the market again and there is no further decision to be made.
To formally model the events of entry and exit,
we enlarge the probability space to support two independent exponential random variables
E^{a},E^{b}\sim\text{Exp}(1)
, with
W,E^{a},E^{b}
mutually independent.
These serve as the canonical randomness sources for entry and exit timing.
Define the unit-rate Poisson seeds as

\Pi^{a}(t):=\mathds{1}_{\{E^{a}\leq t\}},\quad\Pi^{b}(t):=\mathds{1}_{\{E^{b}\leq t\}}.

(4)

The triple
(W,E^{a},E^{b})
constitutes the primitive randomness of the model. The control
\bm{u}
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-predictable process that determines the intensity but does not introduce additional randomness. It enters the state dynamics only through the cumulative effective intensities used to time-change the fixed seeds.
Define the effective intensities
\lambda^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}
and
\lambda^{\bm{\beta}}_{t}
as

\lambda^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}:=\alpha_{t}\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=0\}},\quad\lambda^{\bm{\beta}}_{t}:=\beta_{t}\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=1\}},

(5)

i.e., the entry intensity
\lambda^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}
is effective (strictly positive) only if the agent has not yet opened the position (
J_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=0
), and the exit intensity
\lambda^{\bm{\beta}}_{t}
is effective (strictly positive) only if the agent is currently holding an open position (
J_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=1
).
The cumulative effective intensities
\Lambda^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}
and
\Lambda^{\bm{\beta}}_{t}
are defined as

\Lambda^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{\bm{\alpha}}_{s}ds,\quad\Lambda^{\bm{\beta}}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{\bm{\beta}}_{s}ds,

(6)

which are non-decreasing and absolutely continuous processes that accumulate the effective intensities up to time
t
, acting as the random clocks for time-change.
The Cox counting processes
N^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}
and
N^{\bm{\beta}}_{t}
are constructed by time-change of the unit-rate Poisson seeds:

N^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}:=\Pi^{a}(\Lambda^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}),\quad N^{\bm{\beta}}_{t}:=\Pi^{b}(\Lambda^{\bm{\beta}}_{t}).

(7)

There are two compensated martingales defined as

M^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}:=N^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{\bm{\alpha}}_{s}ds,\quad M^{\bm{\beta}}_{t}:=N^{\bm{\beta}}_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{\bm{\beta}}_{s}ds.

State dynamics.
The state dynamics are given by

\displaystyle dP_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu(P_{t})dt+\sigma(P_{t})dW_{t},

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle=p,

(8)

\displaystyle dJ_{t}^{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=0\}}dN^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}+\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=1\}}dN^{\bm{\beta}}_{t},

\displaystyle J_{0}^{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle dB_{t}^{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=P_{t}\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=0\}}dN^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t},

\displaystyle B_{0}^{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=0.

In the above, the indicator
J_{t}^{\bm{u}}
jumps from 0 to 1 when the agent opens the position, and jumps from 1 to 2 when the agent closes the position. The record
B_{t}^{\bm{u}}
is updated to the current signal
P_{t}
at the moment that the position is opened, and remains unchanged otherwise.

Remark 2.5
.

For any admissible control
\bm{u}
, the state process
X^{\bm{u}}
is constructed from the same primitive randomness
(W,E^{a},E^{b})
. Different controls yield different state trajectories by time-changing the same Poisson seeds with different cumulative effective intensities. In particular, for two controls
\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2}
, the corresponding state process
X^{\bm{u}_{1}},X^{\bm{u}_{2}}
live on the same probability space and can be compared pathwise. This is the standard setup in stochastic control and analogous to different controls sharing the same Brownian motion.
This common-seed construction is necessary to compare different admissible controls pathwise on the same probability space. It does not restrict the marginal law of any single controlled system. For a fixed admissible control, its law is the same as in the standard Cox construction with an independent exponential seed.

Reward defined with instantaneous utility.
The reward function is given by the instantaneous utility derived when the position is closed. Specifically, when the agent closes the position at time
t
, the realized utility is
U(\gamma P_{t}-\iota B_{t}^{\bm{u}}-\Psi-R)
. This reward materializes only when
J_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=1
and
J_{t}^{\bm{u}}=2
(i.e., the moment that
N^{\bm{\beta}}_{t}
jumps). Since
J^{\bm{u}}
jumps from
1
to
2
over the time interval
[t,t+\Delta t)
with probability
\beta_{t}\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=1\}}\Delta t
, the expected instantaneous reward rate at time
t
is
\beta_{t}U(\gamma P_{t}-\iota B_{t}^{\bm{u}}-\Psi-R)\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=1\}}
.
Following this idea, define

G(p,b):=U(\gamma p-\iota b-\Psi-R),\quad p,b\in\mathbb{R},

and

r(x,u):=r((p,j,b),(\alpha,\beta)):=\beta G(p,b)\mathds{1}_{\{j=1\}},\quad x=(p,j,b)\in\mathbb{R}\times\{0,1,2\}\times\mathbb{R},\quad u=(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{M}^{2}.

(9)

The instantaneous reward rate is given by

r_{t}^{\bm{u}}:=r(X_{t-}^{\bm{u}},u_{t})=r((P_{t-},J_{t-}^{\bm{u}},B_{t-}^{\bm{u}}),(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t}))=\beta_{t}G(P_{t-},B_{t-}^{\bm{u}})\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=1\}},\quad t\geq 0,

where
X_{0-}^{\bm{u}}=X_{0}^{\bm{u}}
by convention and thus
r_{0}^{\bm{u}}=0
.
Since
P
and
U(\cdot)
are continuous, and
B_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=B_{t}^{\bm{u}}
if
J_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=1
, we have

r_{t}^{\bm{u}}=\beta_{t}G(P_{t},B_{t}^{\bm{u}})\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{u}}=1\}}.

The first form of value function with intensity control is defined as

\displaystyle V_{\text{inte,1}}(p)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{M}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}r_{t}^{\bm{u}}dt\,\middle|\,P_{0}=p\right].

Reward defined with two stopping times.
Alternatively, we can define the reward function by two stopping times
\tau^{\bm{\alpha}}
and

Remark 2.11
.

In the proof of
2.9
, we only use the property that the auxiliary entry and exit seeds are exogenous and independent of Brownian motion. The global common-seed setup across different admissible controls is not needed for this theorem.

When the intensity cap
M
tends to infinity, the value function of the intensity-control problem converges to that of the original sequential stopping problem. This observation is not surprising. By standard theories of optimal stopping of Markovian processes, the state space can be partitioned into a continuation set and a stopping set, and the optimal strategy is to stop when the process first enters the stopping set. A non-degenerated randomized stopping rule generally results in worse value because any linear combination of the stopped and continuation values is weakly dominated by the maximum of these two values. Under the intensity formulation, the agent would therefore want to exert infinite stopping rate whenever the current state is in the stopping set in order to stop with certainty. The introduction of an upper cap
M
precludes infinite stopping rates or equivalently stopping with certainty. This restriction lowers the value function, but its performance gap against the original value function will vanish as the bound
M
is relaxed to infinity. In the context of RL, one could view a finite value of
M
as a mechanism to enforce exploration such that the agent cannot simply use a greedy strategy of stopping immediately whenever they are in the stopping set.

N_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\pi}}:=\Pi^{a}(\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha}}),\quad N_{t}^{\bm{\beta},\bm{\pi}}:=\Pi^{b}(\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\beta}}).

With the above formulations, the exploratory state process
X^{\bm{\pi}}=(X_{t}^{\bm{\pi}})_{t\geq 0}=(P_{t},J_{t}^{\bm{\pi}},B_{t}^{\bm{\pi}})_{t\geq 0}
has the following dynamics:

\displaystyle dP_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu(P_{t})dt+\sigma(P_{t})dW_{t},

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle=p,

(14)

\displaystyle dJ_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}}=0\}}dN_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\pi}}+\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}}=1\}}dN_{t}^{\bm{\beta},\bm{\pi}},

\displaystyle J_{0}^{\bm{\pi}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle dB_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}

\displaystyle=P_{t}\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}}=0\}}dN_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\pi}},

\displaystyle B_{0}^{\bm{\pi}}

\displaystyle=0.

Remark 3.1
.

The same unit-rate Poisson seeds
\Pi^{a},\Pi^{b}
from
4
are used for all randomized controls. This is consistent with the construction in
Section

2.2
. The seeds are part of the probability space, and the randomized control
\bm{\pi}
affects the state dynamics only through the average intensities
\bar{\alpha}_{t},\bar{\beta}_{t}
that determine the time-change.

3.2
Exploratory value function

We define previously in
9
the reward function as
r(x,u)=r((p,j,b),(\alpha,\beta))=\mathds{1}_{\{j=1\}}G(p,b)\beta
, for
x=(p,j,b)\in\mathbb{R}\times\{0,1,2\}\times\mathbb{R}
and
u=(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{M}^{2}
.
Under a pair of probability measures
\pi=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{ac}}(\mathbb{M})\times\mathcal{P}_{\text{ac}}(\mathbb{M})
, the expected reward is

\iint_{\mathbb{M}^{2}}r((p,j,b),(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}))\pi_{1}(\lambda_{1})\pi_{2}(\lambda_{2})d\lambda_{1}d\lambda_{2}=\mathds{1}_{\{j=1\}}G(p,b)\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi_{2}(\lambda)d\lambda.

Therefore, we define

\displaystyle\tilde{r}(x,\pi)=\tilde{r}((p,j,b),(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})):=\mathds{1}_{\{j=1\}}G(p,b)\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi_{2}(\lambda)d\lambda,

\displaystyle x=(p,j,b)\in\mathbb{R}\times\{0,1,2\}\times\mathbb{R},\quad\pi=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{ac}}(\mathbb{M})\times\mathcal{P}_{\text{ac}}(\mathbb{M}).

The instantaneous reward is now given by

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}

\displaystyle:=\tilde{r}(X_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}},\pi_{t})=\tilde{r}((P_{t-},J_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}},B_{t-}^{\pi}),(\pi_{t}^{\bm{\alpha}},\pi_{t}^{\bm{\beta}}))=\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}}=1\}}G(P_{t-},B_{t-}^{\pi})\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi_{t}^{\bm{\beta}}(\lambda)d\lambda.

Since
P
is continuous, and
B_{t-}^{\pi}=B_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}
if
J_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}=\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}}=1\}}G(P_{t},B^{\bm{\pi}}_{t})\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi_{t}^{\bm{\beta}}(\lambda)d\lambda.

Define the entropy of a pair of probability measures
\pi=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{ac}}(\mathbb{M})\times\mathcal{P}_{\text{ac}}(\mathbb{M})
as

\mathcal{H}(\pi)=\mathcal{H}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}):=-\iint_{\mathbb{M}^{2}}\pi_{1}(\lambda_{1})\pi_{2}(\lambda_{2})\ln\left(M^{2}\pi_{1}(\lambda_{1})\pi_{2}(\lambda_{2})\right)d\lambda_{1}d\lambda_{2}\leq 0.

With simple computations, the entropy can be decomposed as

\mathcal{H}(\pi)=-\int_{\mathbb{M}}\pi_{1}(\lambda_{1})\ln(M\pi_{1}(\lambda_{1}))d\lambda_{1}-\int_{\mathbb{M}}\pi_{2}(\lambda_{2})\ln(M\pi_{2}(\lambda_{2}))d\lambda_{2}.

The entropy-regularized value function is defined as

V_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(p):=\sup_{\bm{\pi}\in\mathcal{A}(p)}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(\tilde{r}_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}+\eta\mathcal{H}(\pi_{t})\right)dt\,\middle|\,P_{0}=p\right],

with the admissible control set defined as

\mathcal{A}(p):=\left\{\bm{\pi}=(\pi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t},\pi^{\bm{\beta}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}:\pi_{t}^{\bm{\alpha}},\pi_{t}^{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{ac}}(\mathbb{M}),\text{and \lx@cref{refnum}{item:A-predictability}, \lx@cref{refnum}{item:A-well-posedness}, \lx@cref{refnum}{item:A-reward-integrability} and\lx@nobreakspace\lx@cref{refnum}{item:A-entropy-finiteness-integrability} below hold}\right\},

(15)

where

(A0)

(Predictability) For each bounded Borel measurable function
\varphi:\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}
, the processes
t\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{M}}\varphi(\lambda)\,\pi_{t}^{\bm{\alpha}}(d\lambda)
and
t\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{M}}\varphi(\lambda)\,\pi_{t}^{\bm{\beta}}(d\lambda)
are
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-predictable.

(A1)

(Well-posedness) The state dynamic (
14
) of
X^{\bm{\pi}}
admits a unique strong solution.

(A2)

(Reward integrability)

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left|\tilde{r}_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}\right|dt\,\middle|\,P_{0}=p\right]<\infty
.

(A3)

(Entropy finiteness and integrability)
\mathcal{H}(\pi_{t})\in\mathbb{R}
a.e.
(t,\omega)
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left|\mathcal{H}(\pi_{t})\right|dt\,\middle|\,P_{0}=p\right]<\infty
.

Remark 3.2
.

Different from
Wang et al. 2020
, we evaluate the entropy relative to the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{M}^{2}
instead of the unnormalized product Lebesgue measure. Since these two definitions differ only by an additive constant
-2\ln M
, they lead to the same optimizer. The advantage of our normalization is that it yields
\mathcal{H}(\pi)\leq 0
with equality attained only when
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are both uniform distributions on
\mathbb{M}
, according to Gibbs’ inequality in continuous case.
Consequently, this allows us to show that the entropy-regularized value function converges monotonically upward as
\eta\downarrow 0
, which is convenient for tuning the exploration parameter in practice.

3.3
System of HJB equations for the entropy-regularized value function

The HJB equation approach requires us to identify the value function not only at the initial time but also at all possible states. Therefore, we augment the initial state
p
to

x=(p,j,b)\in\mathbb{R}\times\{0,1,2\}\times\mathbb{R}.

On the augmented state space, we use
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(x)
instead of
V_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(p)
to denote the value function, with the following definition:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(x):=\sup_{\bm{\pi}\in\mathcal{A}(x)}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(\tilde{r}_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}+\eta\mathcal{H}(\pi_{t})\right)dt\,\middle|\,X_{0}^{\bm{\pi}}=x\right],\quad x=(p,j,b)\in\mathbb{R}\times\{0,1,2\}\times\mathbb{R}.

The admissible control set
\mathcal{A}(x)
has the same conditions as
\mathcal{A}(p)
but with the initial state
p
replaced by
x
.
For convenience, we use the short-hands

\mathcal{V}_{0}(p):=\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(p,0,0),\qquad\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b):=\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(p,1,b),\qquad\mathcal{V}_{2}(p,b):=\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(p,2,b)=0.

Note that
\mathcal{V}_{2}\equiv 0
since the state of
j=2
corresponds to the case that the agent has already completed the round-trip transaction and the problem has terminated.

From the dynamic programming principle and generalized Itô’s lemma,
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(x)
satisfies the HJB equation

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sup_{\pi^{\bm{\alpha}},\pi^{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathcal{A}(x)}\left\{\eta\mathcal{H}(\pi)-\rho\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(x)+\mathcal{L}_{P}\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(x)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\mathds{1}_{\{j=0\}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi^{\bm{\alpha}}(\lambda)d\lambda\right)\left(\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,p)-\mathcal{V}_{0}(p)\right)+\mathds{1}_{\{j=1\}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi^{\bm{\beta}}(\lambda)d\lambda\right)\left(G(p,b)-\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)\right)\right\},

for all
x=(p,j,b)\in\mathbb{R}\times\{0,1,2\}\times\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathcal{L}_{P}
is the infinitesimal generator of the process
P
,

\mathcal{L}_{P}v:=\mu(p)\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(p)\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial p^{2}}.

When
j=2
, the equation holds trivially since
\mathcal{V}_{2}\equiv 0
.
When
j=1
,

\displaystyle\rho\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)-\mathcal{L}_{P}\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\pi^{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathcal{A}(x)}\left\{-\eta\int_{\mathbb{M}}\pi^{\bm{\beta}}(\lambda)\ln(M\pi^{\bm{\beta}}(\lambda))d\lambda+\left(\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi^{\bm{\beta}}(\lambda)d\lambda\right)(G(p,b)-\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b))\right\}

\displaystyle=\eta\ln\left(\frac{e^{y}-1}{y}\right),\quad y:=\frac{G(p,b)-\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)}{\eta}M,

(16)

with the optimal density being a Gibbs distribution, given by

\pi^{\bm{\beta},*}(\lambda;p,b)=\frac{e^{\frac{G(p,b)-\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)}{\eta}\lambda}}{\int_{0}^{M}e^{\frac{G(p,b)-\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)}{\eta}\lambda}d\lambda}=\frac{\frac{G(p,b)-\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)}{\eta}e^{\frac{G(p,b)-\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)}{\eta}\lambda}}{e^{\frac{G(p,b)-\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)}{\eta}M}-1},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{M},

and the optimal average intensity is

\int_{0}^{M}\lambda\pi^{\bm{\beta},*}(\lambda;p,b)d\lambda=\frac{M}{1-e^{-y}}-\frac{\eta}{G(p,b)-\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)}.

When
j=0
,

\displaystyle\rho\mathcal{V}_{0}(p)-\mathcal{L}_{P}\mathcal{V}_{0}(p)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\pi^{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathcal{A}(x)}\left\{-\eta\int_{\mathbb{M}}\pi^{\bm{\alpha}}(\lambda)\ln(M\pi^{\bm{\alpha}}(\lambda))d\lambda+\left(\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi^{\bm{\alpha}}(\lambda)d\lambda\right)(\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,p)-\mathcal{V}_{0}(p))\right\}

\displaystyle=\eta\ln\left(\frac{e^{z}-1}{z}\right),\quad z:=\frac{\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,p)-\mathcal{V}_{0}(p)}{\eta}M.

(17)

The optimal density
\pi^{\bm{\alpha},*}(\lambda;p)
is similar to
\pi^{\bm{\beta},*}(\lambda;p,b)
but with
G(p,b)-\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)
replaced by
\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,p)-\mathcal{V}_{0}(p)
.

3.4
Error estimate of the entropy-regularized problem

It is important to know whether the RL objective would yield a solution that is close to that of the original sequential stopping problem.
We first analyze the error of the entropy-regularized problem compared to the intensity-relaxed one in terms of
\eta
and
M
.
Then, together with
2.9
, we show that as
\eta\downarrow 0
and
M\to\infty
along a specific path, it converges to the original value function.

We first show the intensity-relaxed value function is equal to the value function with randomized intensity but without entropy regularization.
Under the augmented state space, for the intensity-relaxed problem,
define the performance functional and value function as

J(x,\bm{u}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\,r_{t}^{\bm{u}}dt\,\middle|\,X_{0}=x\right],\qquad\mathcal{V}_{\text{inte}}(x):=\sup_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}(x)}J(x,\bm{u}),

where
\mathcal{U}(x)
has the same conditions as
\mathcal{U}(p)
but with the initial state
p
replaced by
x
.
Next, consider the problem with randomized intensities, but without entropy regularization. Define

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}(x,\bm{\pi}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\tilde{r}_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}dt\,\middle|\,X_{0}=x\right],\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}(x):=\sup_{\bm{\pi}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)}\widetilde{J}(x,\bm{\pi}),

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)
consists of measures in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{M})
such that
(A0)
,
(A1)
and
(A2)
in
15
hold. Note that without the requirement
(A3)
, singular measures are contained in the set
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

\mathcal{V}_{\text{inte}}(p,j,b)=\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}(p,j,b)
for all
(p,j,b)\in\mathbb{R}\times\{0,1,2\}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

For any
\mathcal{V}_{\text{inte}}
-admissible control
\bm{u}=(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, let
\bm{\pi}^{\bm{u}}=(\delta_{\alpha_{t}},\delta_{\beta_{t}})_{t\geq 0}
, where
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac delta function at
x
.
Then,
\bm{\pi}^{\bm{u}}
is an admissible randomized control for
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
, and the corresponding exploratory state process
X^{\bm{\pi}^{\bm{u}}}
has the same distribution as
X^{\bm{u}}
.
Therefore,
J(\bm{u})=\widetilde{J}(\bm{\pi}^{\bm{u}})
, and we have

\mathcal{V}_{\text{inte}}\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}.

Conversely, for any
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
-admissible control
\bm{\pi}=(\pi_{t}^{\bm{\alpha}},\pi_{t}^{\bm{\beta}})_{t\geq 0}
, let
\bm{u}^{\bm{\pi}}=(\alpha_{t}^{\bm{\pi}},\beta_{t}^{\bm{\pi}})_{t\geq 0}
, where
\alpha_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}:=\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi_{t}^{\bm{\alpha}}(d\lambda)
,
\beta_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}:=\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi_{t}^{\bm{\beta}}(d\lambda)
.
Then,
\bm{u}^{\bm{\pi}}
is an admissible control for
\mathcal{V}_{\text{inte}}
, and the corresponding state process
X^{\bm{u}^{\bm{\pi}}}
has the same distribution as
X^{\bm{\pi}}
.
Therefore, we have

\mathcal{V}_{\text{inte}}\geq\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}.

Combining the above two inequalities, we obtain
\mathcal{V}_{\text{inte}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
.
∎

For the entropy-regularized problem, define, with
\eta>0
,

\displaystyle J^{\eta}(x,\bm{\pi}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(\tilde{r}_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}+\eta\mathcal{H}(\pi_{t})\right)dt\,\middle|\,X_{0}=x\right],

so
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(x)=\sup_{\bm{\pi}\in\mathcal{A}(x)}J^{\eta}(x,\bm{\pi})
.
By construction,
\mathcal{A}(x)\subsetneq\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)
.
The inclusion is strict since
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)
allows singular measures:
for example the Dirac control
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}=\delta_{a}
,
\pi^{\bm{\beta}}_{t}=\delta_{b}
(
a,b\in\mathbb{M}
)
lies in
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)
but not in
\mathcal{A}(x)
since it is not absolutely continuous, and
\mathcal{H}(\delta_{a},\delta_{b})=-\infty
.
We will show the error estimate between
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
and
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}
because they have similar forms. The technical difficulty mainly comes from the difference between the admissible sets.

Assumption 2
.

Assume that

\displaystyle\exists\zeta>0,r>2:K_{r}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\rho-\zeta)t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{t}\right|^{r}\,\middle|\,P_{0}=p\right]dt<\infty.

(18)

Remark 3.4
.

Since
K_{r}
is increasing as
\zeta
increases, we can always choose
\zeta
such that
0<\zeta<\rho
. For
0\leq p<q
, we can prove
K_{p}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\rho-\zeta)t}dt+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\rho-\zeta)t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{t}\right|^{q}\,\middle|\,P_{0}=p\right]dt
. Combining the two properties, if
K_{q}<\infty
, then
K_{p}\leq C+K_{q}<\infty
. It means for all
r\in[0,2]
,
K_{r}<\infty
is implied by
18
.

Remark 3.5
.

Consider the set

\displaystyle\Omega:=\{A>0:

\displaystyle\exists C>0\text{ independent of $p$ and $t$ s.t. }

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{t}\right|^{r}\,\middle|\,P_{0}=p\right]\leq C(1+p^{2})e^{At}\text{ for some $r>2$ and any $t\geq 0$}\},

which is non-empty by
Section

2.3
. Then, a sufficient condition for
2
to hold is
\rho>\inf\Omega
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
M\geq 1
,
0<\eta<1
and
\frac{2r}{(r-2)\kappa}\geq-\frac{\ln M}{\ln\eta}
, where
r>2
is a constant associated with Condition
18
and
\kappa\in(0,1]
is the Hölder exponent of
U(\cdot)
as per
3
.
Under
1
and
2
,
it holds that

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathcal{V}_{\text{inte}}(p,0,0)-\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(p,0,0)\leq CM^{2}\left|\eta\ln\eta\right|,\quad\forall p\in\mathbb{R},

for some constant
C>0
independent of
\eta
and
M
but depends on
p
. In particular, the following monotone convergence holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(p,0,0)\uparrow\mathcal{V}_{\text{inte}}(p,0,0)\text{ as }\eta\downarrow 0.

Proof.

From
Section

3.4
, we have
\mathcal{V}_{\text{inte}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
.
Thus, it suffices to prove the convergence of
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}
to
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
as
\eta\downarrow 0
.
The rough idea of the proof is to start with an
\varepsilon
-optimal control for
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
and modify it to be
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}
-admissible. The modification precludes singular measures but also introduces additional errors. The estimation of the error is the main technical challenge and summarized in
3.6
.

In the following, we do not explicitly write the initial state
x=(p,0,0)
in conditional expectations or
\widetilde{J}
for simplicity.

For any
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}
-admissible randomized control
\bm{\pi}=(\pi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(\tilde{r}_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}+\eta\mathcal{H}(\pi_{t})\right)dt\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\tilde{r}_{t}^{\bm{\pi}}dt,

since
\mathcal{H}(\pi_{t})\leq 0
for all
t\geq 0
.
Taking expectation and supremum over
\mathcal{A}(x)
, we obtain

\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}.

Moreover, when
\eta_{1}\geq\eta_{2}
, we have
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta_{1}}\leq\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta_{2}}
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}
is increasing as
\eta\downarrow 0
and is bounded above by
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}=\mathcal{V}_{\text{inte}}
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
-admissible randomized control
\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}
such that

\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}-\varepsilon\leq\widetilde{J}(\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon})\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}.

As we mentioned before, the admissible control set for
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
is broader than that of
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}
because the former includes atomic measures. To ensure
\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}
is also admissible for
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}
, we can modify it according to the following procedure.
Consider a class of probability density functions on
\mathbb{M}
denoted as
\Phi_{m,\delta}
for some
m\in[0,M]
and
\delta\in(0,1/2)
.
m
is the approximate mean of the density function, and
\delta
controls the spread of the density function.
Define

\Phi_{m,\delta}(\lambda):=\begin{cases}\frac{M-m}{\delta M^{2}},&\lambda\in[0,\delta M],\\
\frac{m}{\delta M^{2}},&\lambda\in[M-\delta M,M],\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that
\Phi_{m,\delta}
is a probability density function on
\mathbb{M}
with mean

m+\frac{\delta(M-2m)}{2}
,
which gives

\left|\int_{0}^{M}\lambda\Phi_{m,\delta}(\lambda)d\lambda-m\right|\leq\frac{\delta M}{2}.

(19)

The entropy of
\Phi_{m,\delta}
is

-\int_{0}^{M}\Phi_{m,\delta}(\lambda)\ln(M\Phi_{m,\delta}(\lambda))d\lambda=\ln\delta-\frac{M-m}{M}\ln(\frac{M-m}{M})-\frac{m}{M}\ln(\frac{m}{M}).

Since
-w_{1}\ln w_{1}-w_{2}\ln w_{2}\in[0,\ln 2]
for any
w_{1},w_{2}\geq 0
with
w_{1}+w_{2}=1
, we have

-\int_{0}^{M}\Phi_{m,\delta}(\lambda)\ln(M\Phi_{m,\delta}(\lambda))d\lambda\in[\ln\delta,\ln(2\delta)].

(20)

For
\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}=(\widetilde{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\alpha}}_{t},\widetilde{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\beta}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, denote their means as
\widetilde{m}_{t}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\alpha}}
and
\widetilde{m}_{t}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\beta}}
respectively.
Then, define the modified probability density functions

\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}:=\Phi_{\widetilde{m}_{t}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\alpha}},\delta(\eta)},\quad\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\beta}}:=\Phi_{\widetilde{m}_{t}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\beta}},\delta(\eta)},

where
\delta(\eta)
will be chosen later such that

\delta(\eta)\downarrow 0
as
\eta\downarrow 0
.
Let
\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}=(\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}},\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\beta}})_{t\geq 0}
with average intensities denoted as
m_{t}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}
and
m_{t}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\beta}}
.
Then,
\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}
is a
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}
-admissible randomized control.
We have the following result about the approximation error due to modified controls:

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
M\geq 1
and
M\delta(\eta)\leq 1
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{J}(\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta})-\widetilde{J}(\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon})\right|\leq CM^{2}\delta(\eta)^{\kappa-\kappa/r}+CM^{1+\kappa/2-\kappa/r}\delta(\eta)^{\kappa/2-\kappa/r},

for some finite constant
C>0
depending only on
(\rho,\mu,\sigma,G)
and the initial condition.

Proof.

See
Section

A.3
.
∎

By the entropy bound of
\Phi_{m,\delta}
in
20
, for all
t\geq 0
,
\mathcal{H}(\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,\eta})\geq 2\ln\delta(\eta)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\eta\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mathcal{H}(\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,\eta})dt\right]\geq 2\eta\ln\delta(\eta)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}dt=\frac{2}{\rho}\eta\ln\delta(\eta).

Putting the above together with
3.6
, when
M\delta(\eta)\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} J^{\eta}(\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta})&=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(\tilde{r}_{t}+\eta\mathcal{H}(\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,\eta})\right)dt\right]\\
&=\widetilde{J}(\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta})+\eta\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mathcal{H}(\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,\eta})dt\right]\\
&\geq\widetilde{J}(\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon})-CM^{2}\delta(\eta)^{\kappa-\kappa/r}-CM^{1+\kappa/2-\kappa/r}\delta(\eta)^{\kappa/2-\kappa/r}+\frac{2}{\rho}\eta\ln\delta(\eta).\end{aligned}

By the
\varepsilon
-optimality of
\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}
,

\widetilde{J}(\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon})\geq\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}-\varepsilon
. Hence,

\displaystyle J^{\eta}(\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta})\geq\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}-\varepsilon-CM^{2}\delta(\eta)^{\kappa-\kappa/r}-CM^{1+\kappa/2-\kappa/r}\delta(\eta)^{\kappa/2-\kappa/r}+\frac{2}{\rho}\eta\ln\delta(\eta).

Taking supremum over
\mathcal{A}(x)
, and since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}\geq\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}-CM^{2}\delta(\eta)^{\kappa-\kappa/r}-CM^{1+\kappa/2-\kappa/r}\delta(\eta)^{\kappa/2-\kappa/r}+\frac{2}{\rho}\eta\ln\delta(\eta).

The error bound above could be further optimized with respect to
\delta=\delta(\eta)
. Now we specialize to
\delta(\eta):=\eta^{\beta}
for some
\beta>0
such that
\delta(\eta)\downarrow 0
as
\eta\downarrow 0
. Recall that
M\delta(\eta)\leq 1
which implies the restriction
\beta\geq-\ln M/\ln\eta
.
Consider the minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\beta\geq-\ln M/\ln\eta}\left\{CM^{2}\eta^{\beta(\kappa-\kappa/r)}+CM^{1+\kappa/2-\kappa/r}\eta^{\beta(\kappa/2-\kappa/r)}-\frac{2}{\rho}\beta\eta\ln\eta\right\}.

We can now take
\beta=\frac{2r}{(r-2)\kappa}\geq-\frac{\ln M}{\ln\eta}
so that
\beta(\kappa-\kappa/r)>\beta(\kappa/2-\kappa/r)=1
.
Since the convergence speed of
\eta\ln\eta
is faster than any
\eta^{k}
for
k\in(0,1)
, and slower than any
\eta^{k}
for
k\geq 1
, we get that for sufficiently small
\eta
and
M\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}-\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\eta\ln\eta\right|\left(CM^{2}+CM^{1+\kappa/2-\kappa/r}+C\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\eta\ln\eta\right|\left(CM^{2}+C\right)\leq CM^{2}\left|\eta\ln\eta\right|.

∎

Remark 3.8
.

From
3.6
, we see that the choice of
M
affects the convergence rate of
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}
to
\mathcal{V}_{\text{inte}}
as
\eta\downarrow 0
.
A larger
M
enlarges the action space
\mathbb{M}=[0,M]
for exploration, which can potentially lead to better performance in the speculative trading problem.
However, it also slows down the convergence rate, as indicated by the term
M^{2}\left|\eta\ln\eta\right|
in the error bound.
Combining
2.9
and
3.6
, the overall error between
V_{\text{orig}}
and
\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}
can be bounded by

\displaystyle 0\leq V_{\text{orig}}(p)-\mathcal{V}_{\text{ent}}^{\eta}(p,0,0)\leq C\left(M^{2}\left|\eta\ln\eta\right|+M^{-\kappa/2}\right),

for some constant
C>0
independent of
\eta
and
M
.
Therefore, to ensure that the original problem can be well approximated with randomized intensities and entropy regularization, we need to choose
M
and
\eta
such that both terms on the right-hand side are small.
In particular, we need to jointly choose
(M,\eta)
such that
M^{2}\left|\eta\ln\eta\right|\to 0
as
M\to\infty
and
\eta\downarrow 0
.

4
A pairs-trading example

4.1
Model setup

Following
Section

2.1
, we choose
\gamma=\iota=1
while set
\Psi,R\geq 0
. The signal
P
is assumed to follow an OU process:

\displaystyle dP_{t}=\theta(\bar{p}-P_{t})dt+\sigma dW_{t},\qquad P_{0}=p_{0}\in\mathbb{R},

(21)

where
\theta,\sigma>0,\bar{p}\in\mathbb{R}
are constants. As introduced earlier,
P
can represent the price spread of two risky assets such that
\tau
and
\nu
respectively correspond to the time to initiate and to close the pairs-trading opportunity.

We can explicitly verify
2
. The OU process admits an explicit solution

P_{t}=\bar{p}+(p_{0}-\bar{p})e^{-\theta t}+\sigma\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}dW_{s}.

Hence,
P_{t}
is Gaussian with mean
\mathbb{E}\left[P_{t}\right]=\bar{p}+(p_{0}-\bar{p})e^{-\theta t}
and variance

\mathrm{Var}\left[P_{t}\right]=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\theta}\left(1-e^{-2\theta t}\right)
.
Since
\left|\mathbb{E}\left[P_{t}\right]\right|\leq\left|\bar{p}\right|+\left|p_{0}-\bar{p}\right|
and
\mathrm{Var}\left[P_{t}\right]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\theta}
, it follows that
\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{t}\right|^{r}\right]<\infty
for any
r>0
.
Hence,
2
is satisfied for any
\zeta\in(0,\rho)
.
Finally, we consider the S-shaped power utility

\displaystyle U(x)=\begin{cases}x^{\varpi},&x\geq 0,\\
-k\left|x\right|^{\varpi},&x<0,\end{cases}

(22)

where
\varpi\in(0,1],k>0
are constants. The value
\varpi
represents the degree of risk aversion and risk seeking over the gain and loss regimes, while
k
captures the agent’s loss aversion level. With the above choices,
1
is satisfied.

4.2
The system of HJB equations

Under
21
, the infinitesimal generator of
P
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{p}v=\theta(\bar{p}-p)\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial p^{2}}.

With
22
and
\gamma=\iota=1
, the realized utility as a function of current signal
p
and past signal
b
is

\displaystyle G(p,b)=U(p-b-\Psi-R)=\begin{cases}(p-b-\Psi-R)^{\varpi},&p-b-\Psi-R\geq 0,\\
-k\left|p-b-\Psi-R\right|^{\varpi},&p-b-\Psi-R<0.\end{cases}

Following the previous notations, we use
\mathcal{V}_{0},\mathcal{V}_{1}
and
\mathcal{V}_{2}
to denote the value functions in the three stages of the sequential trading problem (before entry, after entry but before exit, and after exit). Denote
\Delta_{1}(p):=\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,p)-\mathcal{V}_{0}(p)
and
\Delta_{2}(p,b):=G(p,b)-\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)
as the advantages (i.e., the marginal values in certainty equivalent terms) for market entry and exit, respectively.
Let
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}(\cdot\ ;\omega)
,
\pi^{\bm{\beta}}_{t}(\cdot\ ;\omega)\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{ac}}(\mathbb{M})
,
t\in[0,\infty)
,
\omega\in\Omega
,
be the exploration densities for market entry and exit, respectively.
From
Section

3.3
, the HJB system for
\mathcal{V}_{0}(p)
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\rho\mathcal{V}_{0}(p)-\mathcal{L}_{p}\mathcal{V}_{0}(p)=\eta\ln\left(\eta\frac{\exp\left(M\Delta_{1}(p)/\eta\right)-1}{M\Delta_{1}(p)}\right),\\
\rho\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)-\mathcal{L}_{p}\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)=\eta\ln\left(\eta\frac{\exp\left(M\Delta_{2}(p,b)/\eta\right)-1}{M\Delta_{2}(p,b)}\right).\end{cases}

When
\Delta_{1}(p)
or
\Delta_{2}(p,b)
equals zero, the corresponding right-hand side is defined as zero by continuity.
When
\eta\downarrow 0
, the right-hand side converges to
M\Delta_{1}(p)^{+}
or
M\Delta_{2}(p,b)^{+}
, respectively, recovering the HJB system for the exploration problem without entropy regularization.
With
P_{t}(\omega)=p
and
B_{t}(\omega)=b
,
the optimal exploration densities are given by

\displaystyle\pi_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},*}(\lambda;\omega)=\pi^{\bm{\alpha},*}(\lambda;p)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(\lambda\Delta_{1}(p)/\eta\right)}{\int_{\mathbb{M}}\exp\left(\tilde{\lambda}\Delta_{1}(p)/\eta\right)d\tilde{\lambda}}=\frac{\Delta_{1}(p)}{\eta}\frac{\exp\left(\lambda\Delta_{1}(p)/\eta\right)}{\exp\left(M\Delta_{1}(p)/\eta\right)-1},

\displaystyle\pi_{t}^{\bm{\beta},*}(\lambda;\omega)=\pi^{\bm{\beta},*}(\lambda;p,b)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(\lambda\Delta_{2}(p,b)/\eta\right)}{\int_{\mathbb{M}}\exp\left(\tilde{\lambda}\Delta_{2}(p,b)/\eta\right)d\tilde{\lambda}}=\frac{\Delta_{2}(p,b)}{\eta}\frac{\exp\left(\lambda\Delta_{2}(p,b)/\eta\right)}{\exp\left(M\Delta_{2}(p,b)/\eta\right)-1}.

When
\Delta_{1}(p)
or
\Delta_{2}(p,b)
equals zero, the corresponding optimal density coincides with the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{M}
by continuity. When
\eta\downarrow 0
, the optimal densities converge to delta measures:
\pi^{\bm{\alpha},*}(\lambda;p)
converges to
\delta_{M}(\lambda)\mathds{1}_{\{\Delta_{1}(p)>0\}}+\delta_{0}(\lambda)\mathds{1}_{\{\Delta_{1}(p)<0\}}+\frac{1}{M}\mathds{1}_{\{\Delta_{1}(p)=0\}}
, and
\pi^{\bm{\beta},*}(\lambda;p,b)
converges to
\delta_{M}(\lambda)\mathds{1}_{\{\Delta_{2}(p,b)>0\}}+\delta_{0}(\lambda)\mathds{1}_{\{\Delta_{2}(p,b)<0\}}+\frac{1}{M}\mathds{1}_{\{\Delta_{2}(p,b)=0\}}
.
The optimal intensities follow from their mean values as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi^{\bm{\alpha},*}(\lambda;p)d\lambda

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{M}{1-\exp\left(-M\Delta_{1}(p)/\eta\right)}-\frac{\eta}{\Delta_{1}(p)},&\Delta_{1}(p)\neq 0,\\
\frac{M}{2},&\Delta_{1}(p)=0,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi^{\bm{\beta},*}(\lambda;p,b)d\lambda

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{M}{1-\exp\left(-M\Delta_{2}(p,b)/\eta\right)}-\frac{\eta}{\Delta_{2}(p,b)},&\Delta_{2}(p,b)\neq 0,\\
\frac{M}{2},&\Delta_{2}(p,b)=0.\end{cases}

When
\eta\downarrow 0
, the optimal intensities converge to
M\mathds{1}_{\{\Delta_{1}(p)>0\}}+\frac{M}{2}\mathds{1}_{\{\Delta_{1}(p)=0\}}
and
M\mathds{1}_{\{\Delta_{2}(p,b)>0\}}+\frac{M}{2}\mathds{1}_{\{\Delta_{2}(p,b)=0\}}
, respectively.

4.3.2
Offline Policy Iteration

Since the optimal control has a closed-form expression in terms of the value functions, this can be conveniently estimated via an offline policy iteration algorithm. The idea is to use deep neural networks to parameterize the value functions
\mathcal{V}_{0}(p)
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)
, compute the optimal exploration policies based on the closed-form feedback control, and then update the value functions based on the TD errors.

We discretize time as
t_{l}=l\Delta t
for
l=0,1,\dots,L
with some
L,\Delta t>0
, and work on one time step
[t_{l},t_{l+1})
. This time discretization is only used for the numerical algorithm, and the value function remains independent of time. On each interval, we freeze the policy and assume the regime can only change at most once at the end point
t_{l+1}
.

Suppose we are given an offline signal path
\{P_{t_{l}}\}_{l=0}^{L}
. A key difference of our problem is that we need to augment the given paths to include the regime process
J
and reference process
B
. To do it, initialize
J_{0}=0
and
B_{0}=0
. For each step
l=0,\dots,L-1
:

•

If
J_{t_{l}}=0
(before entering the market), let the entry policy be
\pi_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\cdot)=\pi^{\bm{\alpha},*}(\cdot\ ;P_{t_{l}})
. Denote the mean intensity as
\bar{\lambda}_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\lambda)d\lambda
and the probability of market entry as
q^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t_{l}}:=1-\exp{\{-\bar{\lambda}_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\alpha}}\Delta t\}}
.
Draw
Y^{\bm{\alpha}}_{l}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(q^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t_{l}})
.
If
Y^{\bm{\alpha}}_{l}=1
, set
J_{t_{l+1}}=1
and
B_{t_{l+1}}=P_{t_{l+1}}
; otherwise set
J_{t_{l+1}}=0
and
B_{t_{l+1}}=B_{t_{l}}
.

•

If
J_{t_{l}}=1
(in the market), let the exit policy be
\pi_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\beta}}(\cdot)=\pi^{\bm{\beta},*}(\cdot\ ;P_{t_{l}},B_{t_{l}})
. Denote the mean intensity as
\bar{\lambda}_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\beta}}=\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\beta}}(\lambda)d\lambda
and the probability of market exit as
q^{\bm{\beta}}_{t_{l}}:=1-\exp{\{-\bar{\lambda}_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\beta}}\Delta t\}}
.
Draw
Y^{\bm{\beta}}_{l}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(q^{\bm{\beta}}_{t_{l}})
.
If
Y^{\bm{\beta}}_{l}=1
, set
J_{t_{l+1}}=2
and
B_{t_{l+1}}=B_{t_{l}}
; otherwise set
J_{t_{l+1}}=1
and
B_{t_{l+1}}=B_{t_{l}}
.

•

If
J_{t_{l}}=2
(after exit), keep
J_{t_{l+1}}=2
and
B_{t_{l+1}}=B_{t_{l}}
.

For each offline signal path, we repeat this simulation
I
times to obtain multiple
(J,B)
trajectories, which reduces Monte Carlo variance.
Denote the entropy cost as

\displaystyle c_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\alpha}}:=\eta\left(\int_{\mathbb{M}}\pi_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\lambda)\ln\left(M\pi_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\lambda)\right)d\lambda\right),\quad c_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\beta}}:=\eta\left(\int_{\mathbb{M}}\pi_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\beta}}(\lambda)\ln\left(M\pi_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\beta}}(\lambda)\right)d\lambda\right).

With samples
(P_{t_{l}},J_{t_{l}},B_{t_{l}},P_{t_{l+1}},J_{t_{l+1}},B_{t_{l+1}})=(p_{l},j_{l},b_{l},p_{l+1},j_{l+1},b_{l+1})
,
the TD errors in different regimes are given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta_{0,l}&=\mathds{1}_{\{j_{l}=0\}}\left(-c_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\alpha}}\Delta t+e^{-\rho\Delta t}\left(\mathds{1}_{\{j_{l+1}=0\}}\mathcal{V}_{0}(p_{l+1})+\mathds{1}_{\{j_{l+1}=1\}}\mathcal{V}_{1}(p_{l+1},b_{l+1})\right)-\mathcal{V}_{0}(p_{l})\right),\\
\delta_{1,l}&=\mathds{1}_{\{j_{l}=1\}}\left(-c_{t_{l}}^{\bm{\beta}}\Delta t+e^{-\rho\Delta t}\left(\mathds{1}_{\{j_{l+1}=1\}}\mathcal{V}_{1}(p_{l+1},b_{l+1})+\mathds{1}_{\{j_{l+1}=2\}}G(p_{l+1},b_{l+1})\right)-\mathcal{V}_{1}(p_{l},b_{l})\right).\end{aligned}

(23)

We will sum up and take average of both TD errors over all realized paths.
The algorithm is summarized in
Algorithm

1
.
The value functions
\mathcal{V}_{0}(p)
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b)
are parameterized using two-layer neural networks with 32 hidden units and ReLU activation. The time step size is set as
\Delta t=0.1
, and the total number of time steps is
100
.

Remark 4.1
.

In practice, when we simulate
I

(J,B)

Conclusion

In this paper, we study a speculative trading problem under a continuous-time exploratory RL framework. Starting from a general diffusion signal process and utility function, we first convert the sequential stopping problem into an optimal control problem over the (deterministic) bounded intensities processes of two Cox processes. To encourage exploration, we further allow randomized policies where the agent chooses probability measure over the intensities and the reward function is regularized by Shannon’s entropy. We then derive the HJB system for the value function and the optimal exploratory control associated with the entropy-regularized problem, and we show that the value function converges to that of the original sequential stopping problem when the intensity cap and the exploratory parameters degenerate jointly in a specific manner. Our setup covers many financial applications. We illustrate the numerical implementation and study the comparative statics for a pairs-trading problem featuring OU signal process as well as Prospect Theory preference. Finally, we introduce a model-free RL algorithm which gives very satisfactory performance when benchmarked against the ground-truth solution obtained by the finite difference method.
Future work includes extending the model to multiple round-trip trades where one needs to analyze a more general optimal switching problem.

\displaystyle P_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\leq t<\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}\}}-P_{\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}\leq t<\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\}}+(P_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}}-P_{\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}})\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}\leq t\}},

we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{t}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}-B_{t}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}\right|\right]dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\leq t<\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}\}}\right|\right]dt}_{I_{2.1}}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}\leq t<\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\}}\right|\right]dt}_{I_{2.2}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}}-P_{\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}\right|\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}\leq t\}}\right]dt}_{I_{2.3}}.

We can bound the first two terms using the earlier probability estimates. Take the first term as an example,

\displaystyle I_{2.1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\leq t\}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\leq t<\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}\}}\right|\right]dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}}\right|^{r}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\leq t\}}\right]\right)^{1/r}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\leq t<\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}\right)\right)^{1-1/r}dt

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{s}\right|^{r}m_{s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}\exp{\left(-\int_{0}^{s}m_{u}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}du\right)}\right]ds\right)^{1/r}\left(M\delta(\eta)t\right)^{1-1/r}dt

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(e^{(\rho-\xi)t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(\rho-\xi)s}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{s}\right|^{r}\right]ds\right)^{1/r}M^{1/r}\left(M\delta(\eta)t\right)^{1-1/r}dt

\displaystyle\leq CK_{r}^{1/r}M\delta(\eta)^{1-1/r}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}e^{(\rho-\xi)t/r}t^{1-1/r}dt\leq CM\delta(\eta)^{1-1/r},

where we used
\mathbb{P}\left(\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\in dt\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)=m_{t}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}\exp{\left(-\int_{0}^{t}m_{s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}ds\right)}dt
. Similarly,
I_{2.2}\leq CM\delta(\eta)^{1-1/r}
.
For
I_{2.3}
,

\displaystyle I_{2.3}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left|P_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}}-P_{\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}\right|dt\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\rho(\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon})}\left|P_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}}-P_{\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{1}{\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\rho(\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon})}\left|\int_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}^{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}\mu(P_{t})dt\right|\right]}_{I_{2.3.1}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\rho(\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon})}\left|\int_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}^{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}\sigma(P_{t})dW_{t}\right|\right]}_{I_{2.3.2}}.

Denote
A_{t}:=\{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\leq t<\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}\}\cup\{\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}\leq t<\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\}
. We have
\mathbb{P}\left(A_{t}\right)\leq M\delta(\eta)t
.
For
I_{2.3.1}
,

\displaystyle I_{2.3.1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}^{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}e^{-\rho t}\left|\mu(P_{t})\right|dt\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left|\mu(P_{t})\right|\mathds{1}_{A_{t}}dt\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mu(P_{t})\right|^{r}\right]\right)^{1/r}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(A_{t}\right)\right)^{1-1/r}dt

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(1+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{t}\right|^{r}\right]^{1/r}\right)\left(M\delta(\eta)t\right)^{1-1/r}dt

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{\rho}\left(M\delta(\eta)\right)^{1-1/r}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}t^{1-1/r}dt+D_{r}\sup_{t\geq 0}(t^{1-1/r}e^{-\zeta t})\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(M\delta(\eta)\right)^{1-1/r},

where we used the integrability result
26
.
For
I_{2.3.2}
,
consider
Y_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(P_{s})\mathds{1}_{A_{s}}dW_{s}
. We have
Y_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}=0
and
Y_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}=\int_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}^{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}\sigma(P_{s})dW_{s}
.
Applying Itô’s lemma on
e^{-2\rho t}Y_{t}^{2}
,

d(e^{-2\rho t}Y_{t}^{2})=-2\rho e^{-2\rho t}Y_{t}^{2}dt+e^{-2\rho t}\sigma(P_{t})^{2}\mathds{1}_{A_{t}}dt+2e^{-2\rho t}Y_{t}\sigma(P_{t})\mathds{1}_{A_{t}}dW_{t}.

Using standard localization argument, Fatou’s Lemma and monotone convergence as in the proof of
Section

2.1
, while integrating from
0
to
\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}
and taking expectation,

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-2\rho(\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon})}Y_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-2\rho(\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon})}\left(\int_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}^{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}\sigma(P_{t})dW_{t}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}^{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}e^{-2\rho t}\sigma(P_{t})^{2}dt\right].

Therefore,

\displaystyle I_{2.3.2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-2\rho(\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon})}\left(\int_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}^{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}\sigma(P_{t})dW_{t}\right)^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}^{\tau^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vee\tilde{\tau}^{\varepsilon}}e^{-2\rho t}\sigma(P_{t})^{2}dt\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2\rho t}\sigma(P_{t})^{2}\mathds{1}_{A_{t}}dt\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2\rho t}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\sigma(P_{t})\right|^{r}\right]\right)^{2/r}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(A_{t}\right)\right)^{1-2/r}dt\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2\rho t}\left(1+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{t}\right|^{r}\right]\right)^{2/r}\right)\left(M\delta(\eta)t\right)^{1-2/r}dt\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{\rho}\left(M\delta(\eta)\right)^{1/2-1/r}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2\rho t}t^{1-2/r}dt+E_{r}\sup_{t\geq 0}(t^{1-2/r}e^{-\zeta t})\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(M\delta(\eta)\right)^{1/2-1/r},

where we used the integrability result
28
.
Combining the estimates for
I_{2.3.1}
and
I_{2.3.2}
, we have
I_{2.3}\leq C(M\delta(\eta))^{1/2-1/r}+C(M\delta(\eta))^{1-1/r}
.
Combining the estimates for the three terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{t}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}-B_{t}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}\right|\right]dt\leq CM\delta(\eta)^{1-1/r}+C(M\delta(\eta))^{1/2-1/r}+C(M\delta(\eta))^{1-1/r}.

Apply Jensen’s inequality on
(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
and
([0,\infty),\rho e^{-\rho t}dt)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{t}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}-B_{t}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}\right|^{\kappa}\right]dt

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{\kappa-1}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{t}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}-B_{t}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}\right|\right]dt\right)^{\kappa}

\displaystyle\leq CM\delta(\eta)^{\kappa-\kappa/r}+C(M\delta(\eta))^{\kappa/2-\kappa/r}+C(M\delta(\eta))^{\kappa-\kappa/r}.

When
M\delta(\eta)\leq 1
, the bound simplifies to
CM\delta(\eta)^{\kappa-\kappa/r}+C(M\delta(\eta))^{\kappa/2-\kappa/r}
.

∎

A.3.4
Proof of
34

Proof.

Since both controls operate on the same probability space, the regime processes
J_{t}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}
and
J_{t}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}
are driven by the same exponential variables
E^{a}
and
E^{b}
, see discussions in
Sections

2.2
and
3.1
.
The entry (resp. exit) events of each process are determined by whether
E^{a}
(resp.
E^{b}
) has been exceeded by the respective cumulative effective intensity. A regime mismatch can only happen when
E^{a}
(resp.
E^{b}
) falls in the gap between two cumulative effective intensities.
Following
13
, denote their cumulative effective intensities as

\displaystyle\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},1}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi_{s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}(\lambda)d\lambda\mathds{1}_{\{J_{s-}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}=0\}}ds,\quad\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\beta},1}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\pi_{s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\beta}}(\lambda)d\lambda\mathds{1}_{\{J_{s-}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}=1\}}ds,

\displaystyle\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},2}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\widetilde{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\alpha}}_{s}(\lambda)d\lambda\mathds{1}_{\{J_{s-}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}=0\}}ds,\quad\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\beta},2}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{M}}\lambda\widetilde{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\beta}}_{s}(\lambda)d\lambda\mathds{1}_{\{J_{s-}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}=1\}}ds.

Denote the maximum and minimum
between
J_{t}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}
and
J_{t}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}
as

\displaystyle J_{t}^{\max}:=\max\{J_{t}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}},J_{t}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}\},\quad J_{t}^{\min}:=\min\{J_{t}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}},J_{t}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}\}.

Similarly, for the cumulative effective intensities, denote

\displaystyle\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\alpha},\max}_{t}:=\max\{\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},1},\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},2}\},\quad\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\alpha},\min}_{t}:=\min\{\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},1},\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},2}\},

\displaystyle\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\beta},\max}_{t}:=\max\{\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\beta},1},\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\beta},2}\},\quad\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\beta},\min}_{t}:=\min\{\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\beta},1},\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\beta},2}\}.

One can get

\{J_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}\neq J_{t-}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}\}=\{J_{t-}^{\min}=0,J_{t-}^{\max}\geq 1\}\cup\{J_{t-}^{\min}=1,J_{t-}^{\max}=2\}
.
We have the following bounds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(J_{t-}^{\min}=0,J_{t-}^{\max}\geq 1\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\alpha},\min}_{t}<E^{a}\leq\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\alpha},\max}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\alpha},\min}_{t}<E^{a}\leq\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\alpha},\max}_{t}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\alpha},\min}_{t})-\exp(-\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\alpha},\max}_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\alpha},\max}_{t}-\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\alpha},\min}_{t}\right|\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},1}-\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},2}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left|m_{s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}-\widetilde{m}_{s}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\alpha}}\right|ds\right]\leq\frac{M\delta(\eta)}{2}t,

where
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},1}-\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},2}\right|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left|m_{s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}-\widetilde{m}_{s}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\alpha}}\right|ds\right]
follows from the four cases:

1.

If
J_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}=0
and
J_{t-}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}=0
, it is obvious.

2.

If
J_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}=1
and
J_{t-}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}=1
, both
\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},1}
and
\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},2}
are equal to the exponential variable
E^{a}
.

3.

If
J_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}=0
and
J_{t-}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}=1
, then
\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},1}<E^{a}\leq\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},2}
. Thus,
\left|\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},1}-\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},2}\right|=\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},2}-\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},1}=\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},2}-\int_{0}^{t}m_{s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}ds\leq\int_{0}^{t}\widetilde{m}_{s}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\alpha}}ds-\int_{0}^{t}m_{s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}ds\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left|m_{s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}-\widetilde{m}_{s}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\alpha}}\right|ds
.

4.

If
J_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}=1
and
J_{t-}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}=0
, then
\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},2}<E^{a}\leq\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},1}
. Thus,
\left|\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},1}-\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},2}\right|=\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},1}-\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},2}=\bar{\Lambda}_{t}^{\bm{\alpha},1}-\int_{0}^{t}\widetilde{m}_{s}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\alpha}}ds\leq\int_{0}^{t}m_{s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}ds-\int_{0}^{t}\widetilde{m}_{s}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\alpha}}ds\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left|m_{s}^{\varepsilon,\eta,\bm{\alpha}}-\widetilde{m}_{s}^{\varepsilon,\bm{\alpha}}\right|ds
.

Similarly,
\mathbb{P}\left(J_{t-}^{\min}=1,J_{t-}^{\max}=2\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\beta},\min}_{t}<E^{b}\leq\bar{\Lambda}^{\bm{\beta},\max}_{t}\right)\leq\frac{M\delta(\eta)}{2}t.

Combining the two bounds, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(J_{t-}^{\bm{\pi}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}\neq J_{t-}^{\widetilde{\bm{\pi}}^{\varepsilon}}\right)\leq M\delta(\eta)t
.
∎

Appendix B
Algorithm

Algorithm 1

Offline Policy Iteration

Require:
Offline data
\{P_{t_{l}}^{n}\}_{l=0,\dots,L}^{n=1,\dots,N}
, parameters
(\rho,\theta,\bar{p},\sigma,M,\eta)
, initial value function parameters
\Theta^{(0)}_{0}
,
\Theta^{(0)}_{1}
, learning rate
lr
, batch size
M_{\text{batch}}
, time step size
\Delta t
, max iterations
K_{\max}
, simulations per path
I
.

Initialize:
value function approximators
\mathcal{V}_{0}(p;\Theta^{(0)}_{0})
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b;\Theta^{(0)}_{1})
.

For

k=0,1,\dots,K_{\max}-1

do
:

1.

Simulate
\{J_{t_{l}}^{n,i,k}\}_{l=0,\dots,L}^{n=1,\dots,N;i=1,\dots,I}
and
\{B_{t_{l}}^{n,i,k}\}_{l=0,\dots,L}^{n=1,\dots,N;i=1,\dots,I}
from Bernoulli random variables with success probabilities given by the optimal policies derived from
\mathcal{V}_{0}(p;\Theta^{(k)}_{0})
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b;\Theta^{(k)}_{1})
.

2.

Compute TD errors
\{\delta_{0,l}^{n,i,k},\delta_{1,l}^{n,i,k}\}_{l=0,\dots,L-1}^{n=1,\dots,N;i=1,\dots,I}
using
23
with value functions
\mathcal{V}_{0}(p;\Theta^{(k)}_{0})
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b;\Theta^{(k)}_{1})
.

3.

Sum all TD errors to get the average loss

\displaystyle\texttt{loss}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\mathds{1}_{\{J_{t_{l}}^{n,i,k}\in\{0,1\}\}}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\left((\delta_{0,l}^{n,i,k})^{2}+(\delta_{1,l}^{n,i,k})^{2}\right).

4.

Update value function parameters using gradient descent and get
\Theta^{(k+1)}_{0}
and
\Theta^{(k+1)}_{1}
.

Output:
learned value functions
\mathcal{V}_{0}(p;\Theta^{(K_{\max})}_{0})
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}(p,b;\Theta^{(K_{\max})}_{1})
.
