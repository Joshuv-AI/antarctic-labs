Optimizing 3D-printed core-shell hydrogel system for probiotic protection: stability under in vitro digestion conditions and during storage
Authors: Que-Anh Truong-Le, Sun-Ok Lee, Ali Ubeyitogullari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to develop a novel pH-sensitive core-shell delivery system to improve probiotics’ viability during simulated digestion and storage. A 10-strain premix of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium was encapsulated within a pregelatinized starch-based gel core, while alginate/pectin (Al/P) gel was employed as the shell using a 3D food printing (3DFOODP) process. The survival rates indicated that the 3DFOODP process did not compromise probiotic viability. To evaluate efficacy, a long-term storage stability study was conducted over a 33-day period under 4 °C and 23 °C, and at 50 °C for 2 h. Storage at 4 °C was most favorable, maintaining a survival rate of 64.37% after 33 days. In contrast, unencapsulated probiotics exhibited significantly reduced viability, falling below 5% by day 18 at 4 °C (3.55%) and by day 15 at 23 °C (2.75%), and reaching negligible survival by the end of the 33–day storage period. Under simulated digestion, the retention of encapsulated probiotics was 97.22% in the oral phase, 83.33% under gastric conditions, and 82.70% in the intestinal phase. Conversely, unencapsulated probiotics showed 93.62%, 10.92%, and 6.30% retention, respectively. Introduction

In recent years, functional foods have attracted substantial research interest, largely driven by growing consumer awareness of the role of diet in health promotion and disease prevention [1, 2]. The growing interest in healthier eating habits has been supported by scientific evidence, demonstrating that food enriched with vitamins, fiber, probiotics, and antioxidants can have beneficial effects on human health. Among the diverse categories of functional foods, probiotics are the most widely studied and consumed. Probiotics are live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, provide health benefits by supporting gastrointestinal (GI) homeostasis and improving the intestinal microbiota balance [3, 4]. Their consumption has surged rapidly as public understanding of their health-promoting properties increases. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species are among the most utilized strains [5, 6]. Extensive research and clinical trials have demonstrated their effectiveness in maintaining a balanced gut microbiota by promoting the growth of beneficial microbes and inhibiting harmful pathogens [7–10]. To deliver these intended health benefits, probiotic products must contain viable cell counts exceeding 109 CFU/mL or g at the time of consumption [11]. However, maintaining the survival and viability of these microorganisms during processing, storage, and passage through the GI tract remains a major challenge.

To enhance probiotic viability and functional performance, various micro- and nano-encapsulation techniques have been employed, including spray drying, freeze-drying, emulsification, and complex coacervation [12–16]. Among these techniques, spray drying microencapsulation is the most widely used for its high efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and low energy requirements [15, 17]. However, this technique can damage cell integrity by increasing membrane permeability and damaging surface structures due to heat [18, 19]. In contrast, spray-coating encapsulates probiotics with protective polymer layers at low temperatures, improving stability and viability, though it is less scalable and more costly [20, 21]. Emulsion-based encapsulation produces microcapsules by dispersing probiotic cells within oil-in-water or water-in-oil emulsions, but the use of organic solvents or emulsifiers can pose safety concerns, and the stability of such systems during storage may be limited [22, 23]. Additionally, the narrow range of encapsulating materials classified as generally considered safe (GRAS) restricts the selection of appropriate polymers or carriers for probiotic encapsulation [24]. These limitations have motivated the exploration of advanced probiotic delivery systems designed to encapsulate and protect probiotics, ensuring their targeted release in the colon.

In this context, 3D food printing (3DFOODP) has emerged as a promising technology for fabricating functional foods with customized structures and controlled spatial distribution of bioactive compounds [25, 26]. This technology offers several advantages, including the ability to customize food structures, deliver personalized nutrition, and simplify supply chains [27, 28]. Interest in probiotic foods developed through 3DFOODP has notably increased in recent years. While several studies have examined 3DFOODP and the general use of probiotics, targeted research specifically addressing the integration of probiotics into 3D-printed food matrices and encapsulated delivery systems is still in its early stages [29–31]. Existing studies can generally be categorized into two approaches: (i) direct incorporation of probiotics into printable food matrices and (ii) incorporation of pre-encapsulated probiotics into printing formulations. Although these approaches have demonstrated the feasibility of producing probiotic-enriched 3D-printed foods, they generally provide limited control over probiotic protection and targeted release. In particular, probiotic viability can be adversely affected by extrusion pressure, shear stress, temperatures, and post-printing conditions [32–34]. Moreover, printing materials must simultaneously satisfy printability requirements, food safety regulations, and the ability to protect probiotics during processing, storage, and digestion.

Coaxial 3DFOODP enables the fabrication of core–shell structures in a single step and has shown promise for probiotic encapsulation and targeted delivery by isolating probiotics within a protective, pH-responsive shell. Compared with conventional encapsulation approaches, which require separate encapsulation and food-fabrication steps, coaxial 3DFOODP enables in-situ probiotic encapsulation during structure fabrication. Furthermore, although coaxial extrusion and core–shell hydrogel systems have been investigated for controlled delivery applications, most studies have focused on model compounds or single-strain probiotics rather than commercial multi-strain probiotic formulations.

In our previous study, we employed 3DFOODP to develop a pH-sensitive core-shell hydrogel delivery system, incorporating pregelatinized starch as the core and an alginate/pectin (Al/P) gel as the shell. This system, characterized by its stimuli-responsive behavior and porous structure, exhibited significant potential for encapsulation and targeted delivery applications. We initially used this system to encapsulate Bifidobacterium bifidum and successfully confirmed its ability to maintain high probiotic viability during processing and under simulated GI fluids [35]. However, the previous study focused on a single probiotic strain and did not evaluate storage stability under different temperature conditions, thermal resistance, or simulated digestion. Building on this foundation, the present study aimed to expand the applicability of the system by encapsulating a 10-strain premix comprising Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species. The novelty of the present study lies in combining in-situ probiotic encapsulation during coaxial 3DFOODP with a commercially relevant multi-strain probiotic formulation and a comprehensive evaluation of probiotic viability during printing, storage, thermal treatment, and simulated gastrointestinal digestion. Printing parameters and material concentrations were optimized to achieve core-shell structures with high dimensional accuracy and robust structural integrity. Probiotic viability was assessed throughout the printing process, simulated digestion, and storage under different temperature conditions to assess the stability, thermal resistance and practical applicability of the developed hydrogel system.

Results and discussion

Dimensional accuracy in 3DFOODP

To achieve a smooth and stable 3DFOODP process, several key printing parameters were optimized. In our previous study, material concentration, air pressure, and temperature were identified as critical factors, and optimized for the best 3D printability [35]. For the core composition, a 9 wt% starch gel supplemented with 4 wt% trehalose was used, and printed at 10 psi and 35 °C. The shell composition consisted of 2 wt% Al/P gel solution with 0.02 M CaCl2, printed at 30 psi and 23 °C. The presence of Ca2+ increased the cohesiveness and hardness, thereby improving the shell stability [36]. Trehalose (4 wt%) functioned as a cryoprotectant in the core, preserving probiotic viability during freezing by mitigating ice crystal damage [37]. All the food ink’s rheological properties were characterized by apparent viscosity, frequency sweep, and recovery behavior, confirming their desirable extrudability, self-supporting structure, flowability, and printability to provide a stable and well-defined core-shell structure [35]. Additional parameters such as printing speed and nozzle height were also optimized to improve overall structural stability. A nozzle-platform gap of 1 mm was maintained to support accurate spiral cube deposition, while a printing speed of 8 mm/s was selected based on preliminary trials to ensure consistent sample formation. Additionally, to enhance structural integrity during and after printing, a spiral cube geometry (5 layers, 30 × 30 mm design) was selected as the digital model. This design minimized the risk of drooping and collapse, allowing for successful fabrication over time. Figure 1A illustrates the shape of a 3D-printed starch-Al/P hydrogel system encapsulating a 10-strain premix of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, confirming a well-defined multilayer architecture with minimal collapse. The smooth filament deposition and consistent layer adhesion suggest that the rheological compatibility between the starch core and Al/P shell contributed to stable interfacial bonding and structural integrity during printing. The absence of visible defects or phase separation further indicates homogeneous distribution of the shell material around the probiotic-loaded core, which is important for establishing a continuous protective barrier against environmental stress.

(A) Optical image of 3D-printed starch–Al/P core–shell hydrogel encapsulating a 10-strain premix of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium. (B) Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) image of the same probiotic premix treated with SYTO9/PI, illustrating bacterial viability and distribution within the hydrogel matrix

Figure 1B demonstrates the CLSM image of the 10-strain premix of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium encapsulated in starch gel. Since SYTO9 stains all cells while propidium iodide (PI) penetrates only membrane-compromised cells, the limited red fluorescence suggests minimal membrane disruption during extrusion and post-processing [38]. In Fig. 1B, the encapsulated probiotics predominantly display green fluorescence, indicating that most cells maintained intact membranes. This suggests high viability and low lethality of the encapsulated probiotics within the starch gel system. This observation indicates that the selected printing pressures, temperatures, and hydrogel compositions effectively minimized shear- and stress-induced cell damage. In addition, the dispersed distribution of fluorescent cells throughout the matrix suggests relatively uniform incorporation of probiotics within the hydrogel network, which may contribute to consistent protection and release behavior throughout the printed structure.

However, it is important to note that the CLSM analysis in this study was primarily qualitative. The CLSM observations represent localized imaging regions and may not fully reflect the distribution or viability of probiotics throughout the entire printed construct. Therefore, while the images provide supportive visual evidence of probiotic survival and distribution, more comprehensive quantitative imaging analyses are required in future studies to better characterize probiotic localization, membrane integrity, and structural heterogeneity within the core–shell hydrogel system.

Cell viability in 3D-printed constructs

In this study, a 10-strain premix of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium was encapsulated in the matrix of starch and Al/P core-shell structure using 3DFOODP technology. The encapsulation strategy involved embedding the probiotic mixture in the core while simultaneously forming a protective outer shell to enhance structural integrity and cellular protection. This core-shell structure significantly improved the retention of probiotic viability under the mechanical and thermal stresses commonly encountered during 3DFOODP, such as temperature, high air pressure, and shear forces.

Probiotic cell viability was assessed before and after the 3DFOODP process to evaluate the impact of printing on cell survival and to determine the potential of 3DFOODP for probiotic delivery. Figure 2 illustrates the viability of the encapsulated probiotics at three key stages: before printing, immediately after printing, and following freeze-drying. The initial viable cell count was 1.33 × 109 CFU/g. After the 3D printing process, no significant difference was observed in cell viability between the control and trehalose-supplemented samples, both maintaining counts above 1.26 × 109 CFU/g. This result suggests that the selected printing conditions, including extrusion pressure, temperature, and nozzle movement, imposed minimal irreversible damage to probiotic membranes. In addition, high viability further indicates that the rheological behavior of the starch and Al/P hydrogels reduced shear stress during extrusion by enabling smooth and continuous material flow. In addition, the shell layer may have acted as a physical buffer that partially dissipated mechanical stresses during coaxial extrusion.

(A) Viable cell counts and (B) survival rate during 3DFOODP stages (before printing, after printing, and after freeze-drying). Mean values were presented in bars with error bars representing the standard deviations (n = 3). Bars with different letters are significantly different (p < 0.05)

Viable cell counts were also assessed following the freeze-drying process, which was considered a preparatory step for further analysis. A comparison between the control and trehalose-supplemented samples was conducted to evaluate the protective effect of trehalose. The trehalose-containing formulation exhibited a high survival rate of 90.00 ± 6.08%, corresponding to 1.1 × 109 CFU/g, confirming its effectiveness as a cryoprotectant. In contrast, the control sample showed a substantial reduction in viability, with cell counts decreasing to 3 × 108 CFU/g (equivalent to 24.39 ± 2.24% survival rate). This value falls below the generally accepted consensus of 109 CFU/g required for probiotic efficacy. These findings align with previous reports, highlighting trehalose’s protective role during freeze-drying [35, 39, 40]. Trehalose stabilizes phospholipid membranes during drying by replacing water molecules, thereby preserving membrane integrity. In addition, by increasing the glass transition temperature through vitrification between the cell wall and membrane, trehalose reduces mechanical damage caused by ice crystal formation [41]. The severe reduction observed in the control sample suggests that dehydration-induced membrane destabilization and ice crystal formation caused substantial cellular injury in the absence of cryoprotection. Currently, encapsulation combined with freeze-drying represents the standard approach in commercial probiotic production, with trehalose widely employed as a cryoprotectant [37, 42, 43]. The high viability observed in this study demonstrates that our system effectively preserves probiotic cells against damage in frozen environments, supporting its potential for industrial applications.

Survival of probiotics under in vitro simulated digestion

Upon exposure to the oral phase, the free probiotic powder dissolved rapidly, forming a homogeneous solution. In contrast, the 3D-printed freeze-dried gel powder exhibited minimal change during the oral phase, indicating its structural stability. Following gastric digestion, the 3D-printed sample remained largely intact and undissolved in the simulated gastric fluid (SGF), further contributing to the enhanced protection of the probiotics. The dense network surrounding the cells likely acted as an effective physical barrier against the harsh acidic environment and gastric enzymes. During the intestinal phase, the 3D-printed gel powder gradually dissolved, forming a uniform solution upon mixing with the simulated intestinal fluid (SIF). This dissolution behavior suggested that the hydrogel system effectively protected the probiotics through the upper GI tract and facilitated their targeted release in the intestinal environment.

Probiotic viability upon oral administration is critical for achieving targeted delivery and functional efficacy. The human GI tract presents significant challenges due to variations in pH, digestive enzymes, bile salts, and other factors. Many studies reported that encapsulation provides effective protection for probiotics during GI transit [31, 44–46]. In this study, viability testing of probiotics was assessed in simulated conditions of the GI tract to analyze the survival and activity of these beneficial bacteria. Viability values (CFU/g) and survival rates (%) for different stages of in vitro digestion are presented in Fig. 3 for unencapsulated and encapsulated probiotics within the hydrogel system. The initial viable probiotics were 1.1 × 109 CFU/g. After 30 s of exposure to oral phase conditions (pH 7.0), both unencapsulated and encapsulated probiotics appeared to be relatively stable, with survival rates of 93.62 ± 6.81% and 97.22 ± 3.44%, respectively. This result suggests that the relatively mild oral conditions caused minimal membrane disruption or osmotic stress, and that the hydrogel matrix remained structurally stable without premature probiotic release.

(A) A schematic diagram of the sequential simulated digestion. (B) Viable cell counts and (C) Survival rate after simulated oral, gastric, and intestinal digestions. Mean values were presented in bars with error bars representing the standard deviations (n = 3). Bars with different letters are significantly different (p < 0.05)

However, after 2 h of gastric digestion (pH 3.0), major differences in viability became apparent. The survival rate of unencapsulated probiotics sharply declined to 10.92 ± 0.68%, while encapsulated probiotics retained a high viability of 83.33 ± 6.30%, suggesting that the encapsulation could improve the probiotics’ gastric stability. The dramatic loss of free probiotic viability indicates severe acid-induced membrane damage. In contrast, the core–shell hydrogel system likely reduced direct diffusion of hydrogen ions into the probiotic-loaded core, thereby slowing acid penetration and minimizing membrane destabilization. The dense ionic crosslinking between alginate and Ca2+, together with pectin incorporation, may have further decreased matrix porosity and proton transport within the shell layer. In a previous study, Chávarri et al. developed chitosan-coated alginate beads containing B. bifidum via extrusion-based microencapsulation and observed a rapid loss of free B. bifidum in SGF to less than 10 CFU/mL within just 1 h, compared to a survival rate above 85% for encapsulated cells [47]. Similarly, Xu et al. found no viable cells in free probiotic samples after 1 h of gastric digestion, while encapsulated probiotics maintained viable counts of 6.96 log CFU/g from an initial 8.0 log CFU/g.

During the intestinal phase (SIF containing bile salts and pancreatin, pH 7.0), further stress led to additional viability loss for unencapsulated probiotics, with the survival rate declining to just 6.30 ± 0.07%. This reduction likely resulted from bile salt-induced membrane solubilization and enzymatic degradation of cellular components. In contrast, encapsulated probiotics maintained significantly higher viability, with survival rates of 82.70 ± 1.92%. The limited reduction observed after transition from gastric to intestinal phases suggests that the hydrogel matrix maintained structural integrity during gastric exposure while enabling controlled swelling under neutral intestinal pH. Such pH-responsive behavior may have reduced sudden probiotic exposure to bile salts and digestive enzymes, thereby moderating transport of harmful molecules into the core region.

The protective effect of the core–shell system can be attributed to multiple structural and physicochemical mechanisms. First, the alginate/pectin shell formed a compact ionically crosslinked network that acted as a diffusion barrier against gastric acid, bile salts, and digestive enzymes [48, 49]. Second, the starch-based core likely provided an additional hydration layer surrounding the probiotics, reducing direct environmental contact and buffering local osmotic stress. Third, the multilayer core–shell configuration spatially separated probiotics from the external environment, thereby reducing cumulative mechanical and chemical damage during digestion. These mechanisms are consistent with the crystalline characteristics previously observed by XRD and the compact morphology identified by SEM analysis in our earlier work [35], which together indicate a tightly packed network capable of restricting the diffusion and penetration of bile salts and digestive enzymes. Consequently, their interaction with the encapsulated probiotics was minimized, preserving cell viability [50].

Building on conventional methods, recent studies have explored the potential of 3DFOODP to incorporate probiotics into 3D-prints. Previously, Yoha et al. and Rosas-Val et al. encapsulated probiotics using conventional methods, such as spray drying, freeze-drying, and spray-freeze drying [30, 51]. Next, they used the encapsulated probiotics as part of the formulation for 3D printing. However, the encapsulation was achieved via conventional methods, where 3D printing was used to print a probiotics-containing formulation with minimal control over the distribution of probiotics in the matrix. The use of conventional approaches as a pre-encapsulation step, subsequent drying, and blending into a printable matrix increase processing time, complexity, and risk of probiotic viability loss due to multiple handling and environmental stressors. In contrast, the current approach simultaneously achieved probiotic encapsulation and structure fabrication during coaxial extrusion, eliminating separate pre-encapsulation and blending steps. This integrated process reduces processing complexity, minimizes handling-induced stress, and enables more uniform spatial distribution of probiotics within the printed matrix.

To the best of our knowledge, our study is one of the first to implement an in-situ encapsulation strategy during 3DFOODP, where encapsulation and structure fabrication are seamlessly integrated into a single, continuous process. The combination of controlled structural fabrication, diffusion-limiting shell protection, and high probiotic survival under simulated GI conditions highlights the potential of this system as an advanced platform for targeted probiotic delivery and functional food applications.

Storage stability

Probiotics must be maintained at a level of 109 CFU/g to exert their host health benefits [52]. Therefore, probiotics must achieve high survival rates not only during processing but also throughout storage. The viability of probiotics during storage is highly dependent on storage temperature, which plays a critical role in determining their stability and functionality over time [53]. In this study, we evaluated the viability of probiotics stored under different temperature conditions (4 °C and room temperature 23 °C). Figure 4A–D shows the changes in cell viability stored at these temperatures over 33 days. The printed core–shell samples exhibited excellent shape fidelity during storage, with no observable changes in geometry, surface integrity, or structural features over the storage period. Across all tested conditions, encapsulated probiotics consistently exhibited superior survival compared to unencapsulated cells. By the end of the storage period, only probiotics encapsulated within the core-shell hydrogel system retained viability levels above the recommended threshold for health benefits.

Survival rates and viable cell count of (▪) unencapsulated and (●) 3D-printed probiotics during storage at (A, B) 4 °C, (C, D) 23 °C, and (E, F) 50 °C. Error bars represent standard deviations (n = 3). * indicates a significant decrease compared to the initial (Day 0–0 min) baseline (p < 0.05)

For unencapsulated probiotics, viability declined rapidly during storage, with viable cell counts falling below 5% before the 33-day period ended. Specifically, at 4 °C, viability decreased to 3.55 ± 0.33% after 18 days, while at 23 °C, viability dropped to 2.75 ± 0.14% after just 15 days (Fig. 4B, D). The rapid decline observed in free probiotics resulted from direct exposure to oxygen, moisture fluctuations, and environmental stressors, which can accelerate membrane oxidation, protein denaturation, and loss of cellular integrity during storage. In contrast, encapsulated probiotics demonstrated significantly enhanced stability, with viability levels of 64.37 ± 1.25% at 4 °C and 16.63 ± 1.51% at 23 °C after 33 days. Although the viability also decreased quickly at 23 °C, the viable counts became stable after 12 days of storage and ended with 1.9 × 108 (CFU/g) (Fig. 4C). The improved stability suggests that the core–shell hydrogel matrix acted as a diffusion barrier that limited oxygen penetration, moisture migration, and environmental fluctuations surrounding the probiotic cells. Previously, Rosas-Val et al. fabricated probiotic-loaded printlets by incorporating sodium caseinate-chitosan microparticles into a polyvinylpyrrolidone–glycerol matrix and shaping them via extrusion-based 3DFOODP to enhance the survival of Lactobacillus acidophilus at 4 °C and room temperature up to 12 months [51]. Kuo et al. applied a 3D-printed alginate/gelatin hydrogel to boost the cell viability of Bifidobacterium lactis and Lactobacillus acidophilus at room temperature for up to 12 weeks [31]. These studies confirmed enhanced probiotic survival and highlighted how different encapsulating matrices and storage conditions can be tailored to optimize long-term stability.

To further assess thermal resistance, we investigated probiotic survival at 50 °C (Fig. 4E, F). This temperature was selected to simulate severe thermal exposure within a shortened experimental period (i.e., accelerated shelf life test) and to evaluate the protective capability of the encapsulation system under elevated temperature conditions (i.e., food processing conditions). No significant difference in viability was observed between encapsulated and unencapsulated cells after 10 min; however, after 20 min, the survival rate of unencapsulated cells decreased to 23.82 ± 1.13%, whereas encapsulated cells retained a significantly higher viability of 59.58 ± 3.14%. This finding aligned with Wang et al., who reported that heat adaptation at 50 °C for 15 min reduced probiotic viability loss [54]. Despite these initial protective effects, after 120 min at 50 °C, the viability of encapsulated probiotics decreased to 20.83 ± 1.90%. The loss of viability under prolonged heating resulted from cumulative thermal denaturation of membrane proteins, increased membrane permeability, and structural relaxation of the hydrogel matrix at elevated temperatures. While the core-shell starch–Al/P matrix slowed moisture migration and oxygen penetration, it did not provide absolute thermal insulation to prevent heat-induced denaturation. Therefore, the core-shell system may offer short-term protection during processing steps, such as pasteurization or brief heating.

At 4 °C, the degradation of unencapsulated probiotics was best described by the zero–order kinetic model (R2 = 0.976 ± 0.001), with a high-rate constant (k = 5.856 ± 0.032), indicating rapid and constant loss of viable cells over time regardless of concentration. This behavior suggests that cell inactivation was primarily governed by external environmental factors during refrigerated storage, such as oxidative stress and gradual membrane damage. In contrast, for encapsulated probiotics at 4 °C, both the zero-order (R2 = 0.937 ± 0.024) and first-order (R2 = 0.930 ± 0.021) models provided a similarly good fit, while the second-order model (R2 = 0.916 ± 0.022) was slightly inferior. This transition suggests that the core–shell hydrogel matrix partially limited direct environmental exposure and created a diffusion barrier that slowed moisture (pH impact) and oxygen transport into the probiotic-loaded core. As a result, degradation became increasingly dependent on the number of surviving cells rather than occurring at a constant rate.

At room temperature (23 °C), the zero-order model remained the best fit for unencapsulated probiotics (R2 = 0.989 ± 0.004), with an even higher degradation rate (k = 6.641 ± 0.126), reflecting accelerated viability loss. However, for encapsulated probiotics, the first-order model provided the best fit (R2 = 0.977 ± 0.009, k = 0.024 ± 0.001), indicating a shift toward concentration-dependent degradation kinetics. This shift toward first-order behavior suggests that the protective effect of the encapsulation system became more pronounced as the viable population decreased. The hydrogel matrix may have preferentially protected surviving cells by reducing exposure to environmental stressors and slowing mass transfer of oxygen and moisture into the core region.

At 50 °C, kinetic behavior became more complex due to severe thermal stress. For unencapsulated probiotics, the first-order model provided the best fit (R2 = 0.951 ± 0.004, k = 0.015 ± 0.001), while the second-order model best described the degradation of encapsulated probiotics (R2 = 0.929 ± 0.003, k = 0.001 ± 0.001). The transition to second-order kinetics may indicate that degradation was influenced not only by the remaining probiotic concentration but also by interactions between probiotics and the surrounding hydrogel matrix. Under elevated temperatures, partial structural relaxation and moisture redistribution within the starch–alginate/pectin network may alter heat and mass transfer behavior, resulting in cooperative degradation mechanisms. This behavior suggests that the encapsulation matrix dynamically responded to thermal stress and temporarily delayed heat-induced damage rather than acting as a passive physical barrier alone.

Overall, encapsulation consistently reduced the degradation rate of probiotics across all tested conditions, as evidenced by markedly lower k values compared to unencapsulated counterparts. Moreover, encapsulation modulated the degradation kinetics, often changing the best-fit model. While unencapsulated probiotics followed zero-order kinetics at lower temperatures, indicating a constant degradation rate independent of viable cell concentration, and first-order kinetics at higher temperatures, where the degradation rate became proportional to the remaining viable cells, encapsulated probiotics exhibited more complex behavior [55]. The best-fit model for encapsulated probiotics varied between first order and second order depending on temperature, reflecting the influence of the protective matrix on degradation dynamics. The shift to first-order kinetics suggested that the encapsulation structure introduced concentration-dependent protection, where viability loss slowed as fewer cells remained. In contrast, the shift to second-order kinetics at elevated temperatures indicated that the degradation rate became dependent on interactions between remaining viable probiotics and the matrix or between the cells themselves, pointing to a more complex, cooperative loss mechanism under thermal stress. These transitions suggest that the hydrogel matrix progressively changed the dominant degradation mechanism from direct environmental inactivation toward transport-controlled and matrix-mediated degradation behavior. Nevertheless, the present analysis remains empirical, as quantitative mechanistic studies involving diffusion coefficient determination and mass transfer modeling were not included in this work. Future studies incorporating these analyses would provide deeper understanding of the transport and degradation mechanisms governing probiotic stability within the core–shell hydrogel system.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed an innovative 3D-printed, pH-responsive core-shell hydrogel system designed for enhanced protection and targeted delivery of a 10-strain mixture of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium. Incorporation of trehalose as a cryoprotectant during freeze-drying further improved probiotic survival, preserved cell membrane integrity, and thereby cell viability was maintained above 90%, with counts above 109 CFU/g, the minimum threshold required to confer health benefits. In vitro digestion studies revealed that the encapsulated probiotics retained high viability throughout oral and gastric phases, with a survival rate of 83.33 ± 6.30% after 2 h in simulated gastric fluid, compared to a dramatic reduction to 10.92 ± 0.68% for unencapsulated controls. Long-term storage stability analysis confirmed that refrigeration at 4 °C provided the most favorable conditions for maintaining probiotic viability, achieving a survival rate of 64.37 ± 1.25% after 33 days, while unencapsulated probiotics lost viability within 18 days. Kinetic modeling further highlighted the protective effect of encapsulation, which slowed viability loss and shifted degradation behavior from zero-order to concentration-dependent kinetics.

It is important to note that this study was primarily designed to evaluate the feasibility and protective performance of the coaxial 3D-printed pH-responsive core-shell hydrogel system. Although quantitative mechanistic analyses, such as diffusion coefficient determination and swelling kinetics, were beyond the scope of this work, the main conclusions are supported by direct experimental evidence, including probiotic viability, storage stability, thermal resistance, and survival during simulated gastrointestinal digestion. Future studies incorporating quantitative transport and release analyses would provide further insight into the mechanisms governing probiotic protection and release behavior. In addition, probiotic viability was evaluated based on total viable counts of the 10-strain formulation, and individual strain-level survival was not quantified. Therefore, while the results demonstrate that the encapsulation system effectively preserves the overall viability of the multi-strain formulation, strain-specific survival and potential dominance of individual strains cannot be determined. Future studies incorporating strain-specific identification and quantification methods (e.g., selective plating, qPCR, or sequencing approaches) are warranted to further investigate population dynamics within multi-strain systems, as some variations in the specific growth rates and doubling times are expected.

Furthermore, probiotic survival and release behavior were evaluated using a static in vitro gastrointestinal digestion model, which does not fully reproduce the dynamic physiological conditions of the human gastrointestinal tract. Consequently, the observed protective effects should be interpreted as preliminary indicators of in vivo performance. Future studies should incorporate dynamic gastrointestinal digestion models, strain-specific analyses, and in vivo evaluations to further validate the efficacy of the developed pH-responsive core-shell delivery system.

From an industrial perspective, the combination of coaxial 3D food printing and freeze-drying offers a promising strategy for the development of customized probiotic delivery systems with controlled architecture and targeted release behavior. The use of food-grade, commercially available biopolymers and mild processing conditions supports potential applicability in functional food and nutraceutical products. However, further studies are needed to evaluate large-scale manufacturing feasibility, production throughput, and cost-effectiveness, particularly regarding printing speed, material consumption, and energy requirements associated with freeze-drying. Optimization of processing efficiency and scalability will be essential for future industrial implementation.

Overall, our findings demonstrate the potential of 3DFOODP as a platform for improving the overall stability and delivery of multi-strain probiotics. Importantly, this study does not claim that all individual strains were equally preserved; rather, it demonstrates the effectiveness of the encapsulation strategy in maintaining total probiotic viability under simulated gastrointestinal and storage conditions.

Experimental section

Materials

Alginic acid sodium salt with medium viscosity (approximately 600–900 cps) was obtained from MP Biomedicals (CA, USA). Low methoxyl pectin (Pectin LMA) with a degree of methoxylation (DM) between 27% and 33% was purchased from TIC Gums Inc. (MD, USA). Pregelatinized corn starch (Anova®) was obtained from Onuva Starch Ltd. (Turkey).

A 10-strain premix of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium freeze-dried probiotic powder, including Lactobacillus acidophilus, L. casei, L. rhamnosus, L. plantarum, L. reuteri, L. helveticus, L. salivarius, Bifidobacterium longum, B. lactis, and B. bifidum, was sourced from Creative Enzymes (NY, USA). MRS broth, MRS agar, and potato-derived peptone were purchased from MilliporeSigma (MA, USA). Amano lipase A from Aspergillus niger (≥ 120 000 U/g), bile extract, pepsin from porcine gastric mucosa (≥ 3200 units/mg of protein), and pancreatin from porcine pancreas (neutral protease: 212 United States Pharmacopeia (USP) units/mg of solid; α-amylase, 215 units/mg of solid; and lipase, 40.1 USP units/mg of solid) were all purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (MO, USA). The enzyme activities were provided by their manufacturers. All other chemicals were of analytical grade.

Gel formulations

Alginate and pectin gel solutions were prepared by suspending 2 wt% of each powder in DI water and stirring at 1100 rpm for 2.5 h at room temperature (23 °C) (Cimarec, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) [36]. The solutions were then stored at 4 °C overnight for stabilization. Following our previous study, the alginate and pectin solutions were combined at an 80:20 (w/w) ratio to form the Al/P gel. A 0.1 M CaCl2 solution was added at a 1:4 (w/w) ratio to improve the gel’s stability and printability [36]. The final mixture was then kept overnight at 4 °C before use.

The pregelatinized starch gel was made following our previous study [35]. Starch powder (9 wt%) was dispersed in DI water and stirred at 1100 rpm for 2 h. The resulting starch gel was then left to rest in a beaker for an additional 2 h to ensure full water absorption and the formation of a uniform gel.

Probiotic loading

A 10-strain premix of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium (1.5 g; equivalent to 4 × 109 CFU) was incorporated into the starch gel. The mixture was gently stirred using a low-shear stirrer at 200 rpm to preserve cell integrity, continuing until a uniform and homogenous gel was obtained. The mixing process was conducted at 35 ± 2 °C to maintain probiotic viability. To protect the probiotic cells from environmental stress during subsequent processing, trehalose (4 wt%) was added as a cryoprotectant [35]. The final mixture was then used for 3DFOODP.

Core-shell gel system construction by 3DFOODP

A 3D food printer (Allevi 2, 3D Systems, Inc., PA, USA) equipped with a coaxial nozzle setup of 0.718 mm ID (1.27 mm OD) was employed to fabricate the Al/P core-shell gel system [36, 56]. The core syringe was loaded with the starch-probiotic gel, while the shell syringe contained the Al/P + CaCl2 solution (shell composition). Printing conditions were set at 10 psi and 35 °C for the core and 30 psi and 23 °C for the shell, with a consistent printing speed of 8 mm/s [35]. The structure was designed as a spiral cube (30 × 30 × 1 mm, 5 layers) using Tinkercad software (Autodesk Inc., CA, USA). All samples were printed onto Petri dishes.

Post-printing, the samples were sealed and stored at 4 °C for further analysis, or were frozen at -50 °C for 24 h, then freeze-dried for approximately 24 h using a freeze-dryer (Labconco Corporation, MO, USA) [36].

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

The shape and integrity of the bacterial membrane were assessed using the BacLight™ stain kit. With this kit, bacteria with intact membranes exhibited green fluorescence (SYTO9), while bacteria with damaged membranes showed red fluorescence (propidium iodide, PI) [57, 58]. For CLSM analysis, only a thin layer of starch gel (approximately 0.2 g) was used to facilitate optical penetration and to enable clear visualization of bacterial distribution and membrane integrity within the gel matrix. This qualitative imaging approach was not intended for quantitative analysis of bacterial load. Prior to staining, the starch gel was washed with 1X PBS and then stained with 3 µL BacLight™ solution (1.5 µL of SYTO 9 and 1.5 µL of PI) for 30 min at room temperature (23 °C) in the dark. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images were captured using a Leica Stellaris 8 WLL spectral confocal microscope (Mannheim, Germany). The Green SYTO9 fluorescence was measured with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 500–520 nm, and red PI fluorescence was measured by excitation at 561 nm and emission at 610–630 nm. Images were acquired using 10× dry, 20× water, and 63× water immersion objectives to examine the samples’ overall morphology and finer structural features. The system was operated with Leica LAS-X software for image acquisition and processing. CLSM imaging allowed for detailed visualization of bacterial distribution and membrane integrity within the starch matrix.

Assessment of probiotic viability

Probiotic viability was assessed using the standard plate count method. Either crude or encapsulated probiotic samples (1.5 g) were transferred into sterile tubes to mix with sterilized 0.1% (w/v) peptone diluent at room temperature (23 °C) [59, 60]. To ensure complete solubilization of the encapsulated probiotics, a sterile sodium citrate solution was added to facilitate the disruption of the hydrogel matrix. The mixture was then thoroughly homogenized via vortexing to yield a clear, uniform suspension prior to serial dilution. The dilution process was subsequently prepared in sterile microtubes. For each dilution, 1 mL was plated on MRS agar modified with 0.5% (w/v) L-cysteine and 0.5% (w/v) lactose [31]. The probiotic-agar mixture was gently spread by sliding the Petri dishes to ensure even distribution. A control plate containing only the peptone diluent was included to check for contamination. All samples were plated in triplicate and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 h using the BD GasPak™ EZ Anaerobe Container System. Probiotic viability was expressed as colony-forming units per gram (CFU/g), and only plates containing 25–250 colonies were used for calculation [31, 61]. In vitro digestion

Simulated GI digestion of unencapsulated and encapsulated probiotics was performed following the method of Minekus et al. [62]. Sequential digestion, including oral, gastric, and intestinal digestion, was performed (Fig. 5). Samples were taken after each digestion phase for analysis.

Oral phase

Oral phase digestion was performed according to Ubeyitogullari et al. [63], which was developed based on Minekus et al. [62]. Simulated salivary fluid (SSF) stock solution was prepared according to the composition listed in Table 2, with the pH adjusted to 7.0 using 1 M HCl and 1 M NaOH. To prepare the working solution, 3.5 mL of SSF, 12.5 µL of 0.3 M CaCl2, and 0.9875 mL of DI water were mixed. Then, the mixture was put into a water bath (PA, USA) at 37 °C with constant agitation at 150 rpm. To achieve a final α-amylase activity of 75 U/mL, 0.5 mL of α-amylase stock solution (750 U/mL) was added to the mixture, followed by the sample addition (0.125 g). The oral phase was maintained for 30 s, after which the enzymatic reaction was terminated by placing some of the samples on ice.

Gastric phase

Simulated gastric fluid (SGF) was added following the oral phase digestion. SGF was prepared with its composition detailed in Table 2, and the pH was adjusted to 3.0 using 1 M HCl [62, 64]. The mixture obtained after oral digestion was mixed with 3.25 mL of SGF, 2.5 µL of 0.3 M CaCl2, and 0.9 mL of DI water. Subsequently, 1 mL of porcine pepsin solution (20,000 U/mL) and 0.5 mL of fungal lipase were added. Then, the mixture was incubated in the water bath for 2 h at 37 °C at 100 rpm to simulate gastric digestion. The enzymatic reaction was terminated by placing some of the samples on ice.

Intestinal phase

Following gastric digestion, simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) was added, and the pH of the resulting mixture was adjusted to 7.0 with 1 M NaOH [62, 64]. A total of 6.125 mL of SIF, 20 µL of 0.3 M CaCl2, and 1.98 mL of DI water were combined with the gastric chyme. Based on the activity of α-amylase, 1.25 mL of a pancreatin solution (initial activity: 1600 U/mL) was added to the mixture. Subsequently, 0.625 mL of bile extract solution was incorporated. The final mixture was incubated in a shaking water bath at 37 °C and 100 rpm for 2 h to simulate intestinal digestion.

Investigation of long-term stability under different storage conditions

The final freeze-dried samples loaded with probiotics were placed in sterile Petri dishes and sealed using Parafilm® M to prevent moisture absorption [65, 66]. Control samples consisted of freeze-dried, unencapsulated probiotics. All samples were stored at 4 °C and 23 °C for up to 33 days, or until the probiotic survival rate dropped below 5%, a cutoff selected because probiotic effectiveness is negligible at very low viability, and plate count accuracy decreases near the detection limit. In addition, samples were exposed to 50 °C for 120 min to assess the thermal resistance of probiotics. Viability was assessed as previously described using the plate count method.

Statistical analysis

All experiments and measurements were conducted in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Prior to statistical analysis, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated. Statistical significance among groups was assessed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s HSD test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 2025, Posit PBC).
