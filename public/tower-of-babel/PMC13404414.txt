From diagnosis to daily life: a qualitative investigation into patients’ lived experiences of pre-diabetes diagnosis and care in a primary care setting in Singapore
Authors: P V Asharani, Roystonn Kumarasan, Madhumitha Ramu, Brian Tan Yeow Wee, Fiona Devi, Iskandar Shah, Peizhi Wang, Alvin Lum Wai Mun, Sum Chee Fang, Eng Sing Lee, Siow Ann Chong, Mythily Subramaniam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe prevalence of pre-diabetes mellitus (pre-diabetes, pre-DM) is increasing globally, with 5%–10% of adults with pre-DM progressing to type 2 DM (T2DM) annually. Despite the availability of national screening programmes and lifestyle interventions, uptake remains suboptimal and the lived experiences of individuals navigating pre-DM diagnosis and care in Singapore remain poorly understood. Understanding these experiences is critical, as patient perspectives can reveal gaps in care delivery, particularly in a multiethnic population where cultural, social and systemic factors intersect in complex ways. The current study explores the barriers and facilitators in detecting and managing pre-DM in Singapore’s multiethnic population.MethodsThis qualitative study employed purposive sampling and a phenomenological approach to capture the experiences of those living with pre-DM (n=13). Participants were recruited from National Healthcare Group polyclinics through clinician referrals and through self-referrals. Eligible participants were aged 21 and above, Singapore citizens or permanent residents, diagnosed with pre-DM and conversant in at least one local language, while those with T2DM or type 1 DM were excluded. One-to-one in-depth interviews were conducted between August 2023 and February 2024, which were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim and analysed through reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsSix overarching themes were identified: a) pathways to pre-DM diagnosis (detection during management of chronic conditions, self-initiated detection through health screening (HS)), b) dealing with diagnosis: navigating shock, acceptance and worry about the future, c) life before diagnosis: living in comfort and the absence of consequences (dietary habits and lifestyle choices before diagnosis, when not knowing felt safer: the reasons for not doing HS), d) life after diagnosis: reconfiguring daily life (lifestyle changes: renegotiating diet and exercise habits, alternative therapies: integrating traditional approaches), e) pre-DM care (challenges and enablers of pre-DM management) and f) reimagining care: suggestions for improving pre-DM care. The main barriers were i) personal factors: the struggle to sustain behavioural changes (such as lack of awareness, difficulty with dietary and physical activity changes, lack of self-discipline), ii) navigating social and environmental barriers to lifestyle change, iii) the financial burden of lifestyle changes, iv) work-related factors: competing priorities and time constraints,v) feeling unheard: the healthcare team-related barriers to care and vi) gaps in the systems: structural barriers to care (such as long waiting times, clinician rotation, short consultations). Enablers included i) fear to action: personal drivers of pre-DM self-management (such as personal factors including fear of developing T2DM), ii) family, peers and domestic help as daily support, iii) feeling heard and cared for: healthcare team-related factors, iv) seamless care through operational excellence and v) knowledge about pre-DM: sources and information seeking.ConclusionsOur findings suggest the need for multilevel collaboration to promote effective strategies to manage pre-DM in the community. This requires interventions to promote early screening, healthcare reforms to reduce waiting times and clinician rotation, and sustaining lifestyle modifications. Interventions should leverage the enablers identified in the study while addressing the barriers to ensure healthcare reforms. The insights derived from this study can directly inform locally contextualised interventions and policy reforms to strengthen pre-DM care delivery within a multiethnic healthcare setting.

Introduction

Pre-diabetes mellitus (pre-DM) is characterised by a blood glucose level higher than normal but below the threshold for a diagnosis of type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM).1 The global burden of pre-DM is on the rise, with the prevalence of impaired fasting glucose (IFG) estimated at 5.8% in 2021, which is projected to increase to 6.5% in 2045.1 The prevalence of impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) was 9.1% in 2021 and is estimated to reach 10% in 2045.1 Nearly half of those with pre-DM will progress to T2DM within a 5-year span.2 Those with pre-DM are at a higher risk of premature mortality3 with a meta-analysis identifying a 6%–101% increase in all-cause mortality and incidence of adverse health outcomes such as cardiovascular complications, stroke, kidney diseases, cancer and dementia3 4 among this group. Given that pre-DM is reversible, it presents a critical phase for prevention of diabetes.5

Quantitative evidence on pre-DM interventions and risk factors is substantial. Studies have identified various risk factors that predispose individuals to pre-DM, including obesity, being overweight, sedentary lifestyle and family history of diabetes.6 Research has demonstrated a significant reduction in diabetes risk (6.2 cases per 100 person-years) through lifestyle modifications involving dietary changes and physical activity, pharmacological interventions, alternative medicines, and self-care practices.3 7 8 A meta-analysis of 47 studies examining various interventions, particularly dietary changes and increased physical activity, provided strong evidence for the effectiveness of lifestyle modifications in reversing pre-DM.9 These lifestyle interventions focusing on diet and exercise habits reduced the risk of developing diabetes by 36%–54% in individuals with pre-DM.10 Ali et al11 demonstrated that for every one kilogramme of weight loss, diabetes incidence decreased by 16%. A 20-year longitudinal study revealed that individuals who achieved normal glucose levels following intervention had lower diabetes and cardiovascular incidence rates compared with those who remained in pre-DM or diabetes states.12 13

Despite the implementation of national policies introducing risk assessment tools and population-level health screening (HS) for diabetes prevention, awareness of these programmes and uptake remain low.14 15 This was also evidenced by a systematic review of 148 studies revealing that only 27% of individuals with pre-DM accepted lifestyle interventions.16 Quantitative evidence has largely focused on lifestyle interventions and risk factors while providing limited insights into the lived experiences of individuals, that shape engagement with care and sustained management of the condition. A meta-synthesis of 20 qualitative studies has identified three analytical themes underpinning perceived barriers and facilitators for lifestyle change: the individual’s evaluation of the importance of initiating lifestyle change, strategies and coping mechanisms for sustaining those changes, and the significance of supportive relationships and environments.17 Importantly, the majority of these studies were conducted in the context of structured community-based lifestyle intervention programmes, with only seven examining experiences through routine healthcare consultations without a formal intervention. This distinction is significant, as the barriers and facilitators experienced by individuals managing pre-DM outside of structured programmes—as is common in primary care settings—may differ considerably. Furthermore, the studies included in this meta-synthesis predominantly reflect Western populations, where cultural norms, food environments and healthcare structures differ markedly from those in multiethnic Asian settings such as Singapore. Culturally embedded dietary practices, family-oriented health motivations, and the specific constraints of Singapore’s primary care infrastructure are unlikely to be fully captured by this existing body of evidence. These findings were further corroborated by Teoh et al,18 who documented significant knowledge deficits and suboptimal practices among the population regarding pre-DM management. The complexity of pre-DM management is illustrated by multiple interconnected barriers operating at various levels, as demonstrated by Jokar et al19, include individual (knowledge, motivation, self-efficacy), interpersonal (healthcare relationships and social support), organisational (healthcare system constraints), and community-level factors (environmental and cultural influences), all affected by policy implementation. The healthcare provider perspective presents additional challenges, with studies demonstrating limited active involvement in screening and referral of eligible patients.20 This reduced engagement is primarily attributed to unclear referral pathways, despite evident provider motivation.21 Furthermore, the increasing prevalence of diabetes and escalating demand for T2DM care have resulted in overwhelmed healthcare systems, leading to the deprioritisation of pre-DM management.22 Therefore, understanding these complex barriers and facilitators in the local context is crucial for developing targeted interventions to improve pre-DM care.

While T2DM prevention and treatment has been the focus of anti-diabetes campaigns, pre-DM occupies an ambiguous space, with many healthcare providers reporting suboptimal knowledge on pre-DM screening, diagnostic criteria and management plans.23 A study conducted among patients and healthcare workers showed that only 30% of patients and 72% of clinicians had heard of pre-DM.24 This ambiguity is not merely a knowledge gap, but an indication of how the condition is experienced and perceived by both the patients and care providers. Pre-DM is often acknowledged as an intermediary phase between a healthy state and T2DM that is reversible. This could affect how people respond to the diagnosis. Other qualitative studies have noted that those diagnosed with pre-DM were often unaware of the seriousness of their condition, their risk for T2DM and how to manage it.25 26 This lower risk perception and limited pre-DM knowledge can delay engagement with care and reduce motivation for lifestyle changes, potentially leading to persistent hyperglycaemia and diabetes-related complications that often remain undiagnosed.27 Pre-DM is a time-sensitive window where progression to T2DM can still be averted, yet, knowledge, perceived severity and engagement with services remains lower than for T2DM.26 27 Behavioural change is the cornerstone of pre-DM management as decisions are influenced by emotional, perceptual and social factors rather than clinical factors alone.27 These factors are not captured through quantitative data but are better illustrated through patient experience studies. Examining the lived experiences of those with pre-DM provides insights into gaps in awareness, barriers and enablers to care and management, which will help inform the development of timely interventions.

A growing body of qualitative research has begun to characterise the lived experiences of those with pre-DM and noted the diagnostic moment as central to the pre-DM experience. The diagnostic experience is often shaped by healthcare, personal and community factors, with clinician communication identified as the strongest influence.28 Responses to diagnosis varied from shock and distress to indifference to viewing it as an opportunity for change, and the experience had lasting effects throughout their lives.29 30 When combined with clear dietary advice and lifestyle support, it opened a window of opportunity for some participants.30 Self-management of pre-DM involved struggles due to work pressure, cultural factors, insufficient knowledge and communication breakdown between healthcare teams, whereas family, peer-led programmes and online platforms facilitated the process.31 Collectively, these findings underscore that risk perception and treatment engagement are shaped through social interaction and how individuals personally interpret their diagnosis.

However, this evidence represents Western settings with limited representation of multiethnic Asian primary care populations. Previous studies in Singapore have addressed discrete behaviours and educational needs of patients rather than their experiences with pre-DM.32 Lim et al32 have shown that only 26.6% of patients received pre-DM education in primary care in Singapore and many were not referred to the programme. How individuals from Singapore’s diverse Chinese, Malay and Indian communities navigate a pre-DM diagnosis, engage with screening and follow-up care, and manage the tensions between recommended lifestyle changes and deeply embedded cultural and family practices remains insufficiently explored, which is a gap this study seeks to address.

Singapore is a city-state with a population of 6.04 million. Its multiethnic population comprises 75.6% Chinese, 15.1% Malay, 7.6% Indian and 1.7% other ethnic groups.33 The healthcare infrastructure consists of an extensive primary care network with over 2000 private general practitioner (GP) clinics and 26 polyclinics (government services offering subsidised care). Secondary and tertiary care is provided through 11 public hospitals (encompassing acute general hospitals, a women’s and children’s hospital and a tertiary psychiatric facility) and 10 national specialty centres (providing specialised care in cancer, cardiac, eye, skin, neuroscience, infectious diseases and dental care). Community care is supported by 223 active ageing centres and 15 000 home care places.34 35

Given that healthcare structures, policies and population characteristics vary significantly across different national and cultural contexts, understanding patient experiences with pre-DM management in the Singapore context is crucial for improving care delivery and T2DM prevention. Patient narratives provide comprehensive insights into challenges and unmet needs, which can inform timely interventions for T2DM prevention. This study aims to explore the barriers and facilitators in detecting and managing pre-DM in Singapore’s multiethnic population, providing a novel perspective that extends beyond the commonly studied barriers and facilitators to post-diagnosis management.

Methods

Design

This study employed a phenomenological approach to explore the lived experiences of individuals with pre-DM. This approach was chosen to capture participants’ perceptions and experiences across various stages of their pre-DM journey, enabling the identification of barriers and facilitators in both diagnosis and management processes. The study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (online supplemental table S1).

Participants and setting

Participants were recruited through multiple pathways to ensure adequate enrolment. Primary recruitment was conducted through the National Healthcare Group polyclinics, where attending clinicians identified and referred eligible patients to the study coordinator. On referral, pre-DM diagnoses were confirmed with the referring clinicians prior to enrolment. Study brochures outlining the study objectives and eligibility criteria were disseminated to clinicians across all participating institutions to facilitate referrals. In addition, self-referrals were invited through institutional portals of all participating institutions, where study brochures were made available to prospective participants. Individuals who expressed interest through self-referral were subsequently screened for eligibility, and their pre-DM diagnoses were verified with their attending clinicians before enrolment. Participants who were aged 21 years or older, citizens or permanent residents of Singapore, with a diagnosis of pre-DM as determined by the clinician based on fasting plasma glucose levels of 6.1–6.9 mmol/L (IFG) or a 2-hour post-load glucose of 7.8–11.0 mmol/L (IGT),36 conversant in at least one of the local languages (English, Chinese, Malay or Tamil) were included in the study. Participants were required to be willing to be audio-recorded and provide written informed consent. Those with T2DM and type 1 DM (T1DM) were excluded. Quotas were set for different ethnicities and gender to achieve a maximum variation sample, targeting approximately equal gender distribution (approximately 1:1 male to female ratio) and a comparable ethnic distribution reflective of Singapore’s multiethnic population, including Chinese, Malay, Indian and other ethnic groups.

Procedure

13 participants were enrolled in the study between August 2023 and February 2024, and all completed the interview. None withdrew from the study. All interviews were conducted by PVA, MS, PW, RK, FD, IS, BTYW and MR. Participants and interviewers had no prior relationship before the commencement of the study.

Study materials were translated by professional translation firms into three local languages (Mandarin, Malay and Tamil). Bilingual research team members conducted quality checks to ensure both linguistic accuracy and cultural sensitivity. All participants completed a sociodemographic questionnaire that included questions about their age, gender, ethnicity, income, education, marital status and housing type. Additional information regarding awareness of follow-up care was captured using the following questions: ‘Are you aware of the diabetes risk assessment tool in HealthHub (a national health portal)?’ and ‘Are you aware of the Screen for Life programme (national level subsidised screening)?’ Binary outcomes (Yes or No) were captured for both questions. Participants were also asked if they were currently on regular follow-up with their clinician for their pre-DM care. One-to-one in-depth interviews were conducted either face-to-face or through online platforms such as Microsoft (MS) Teams. The mode, time and place of the interview were arranged at the participant’s convenience. The interview guide was developed based on feedback from experts (diabetologists, GPs, nurses and policy makers; see online supplemental table S3). The guide was tested and modified after the initial two interviews, which mainly involved language changes and additional probes where needed. The interviews were conducted by trained qualitative researchers and lasted 60–90 min. Privacy and confidentiality were ensured by conducting the interviews strictly in clinic meeting rooms or at participants’ homes, according to participant preference. No personal identifiers were collected, and all study-related documents were stored securely. The interviewer and note takers introduced themselves, explained the purpose of the study, data usage and other key procedures, and reminded participants about the audio recording.

Characteristics of the study team, triangulations and reflexivity

The study team included qualitative researchers, diabetologists and primary care physicians who contributed to the development of the interview guides and the conduct of interviews. Further details and credentials of the study team members are indicated in the online supplemental table S2. All interviewers had mental health or biomedical backgrounds, with none involved in diabetes care or having a diagnosis of pre-DM. The interviewers were aware of pre-DM and T2DM and expanded their knowledge through discussions and literature reviews prior to the interviews. Several steps ensured minimal bias in the interviews and analysis. Prior training on the interview guide and interview skills emphasised the need to remain neutral, non-judgmental and empathetic, and to perform bracketing. Prior to data collection, interviewers engaged in bracketing exercises, explicitly surfacing and documenting their assumptions and preconceptions about pre-DM and lifestyle management during team discussions. During these discussions, interviewers openly acknowledged their assumptions and made conscious efforts to approach each interview with openness. A note taker was involved in monitoring the progression of the interview and identifying potential bias. Interviews opened with a broad introductory question about participants’ experience of receiving their pre-DM diagnosis, allowing them to lead the narrative before more specific probes were introduced. Interviewers used open-ended probing techniques throughout, including elaboration probes and clarification probes, with silent pauses used to encourage deeper reflection. Interviews were closed by inviting participants to share any final thoughts. The final interview guide is provided in the supplementary file (online supplemental table S3). The note taker captured points requiring attention from senior interviewers for further discussion and training. Debriefing sessions were conducted after the first three interviews and thereafter at the 5th, 7th, 10th and 12th interviews. The team discussed methodological shortcomings, potential revisions to the guide, and follow-up actions during these sessions. While none of the interviewers were employees of the diabetes clinic, all were healthcare staff, some of whom were users of healthcare services, and all had their own assumptions. The team acknowledged their role as healthcare researchers and remained reflexive throughout data collection and analysis to ensure that institutional perspectives did not unduly shape the findings. Continued discussions with the study team ensured that these assumptions and perspectives did not interfere with data collection or analysis. The study team maintained an encouraging attitude and assured participants that their opinions would not be shared with the healthcare team, maintaining confidentiality.

The study employed methodological, investigator and data triangulation. Maximum variation sampling contributed to data triangulation by capturing diverse perspectives across age, gender and ethnicity. The use of hybrid interview modes (both face-to-face and online interview formats), alongside field notes and structured debriefing sessions, addressed methodological triangulation. For investigator triangulation, interviews were conducted by pairs comprising a primary interviewer and note taker from multidisciplinary backgrounds, with ethnic matching of interviewer and participant carried out where feasible. The multidisciplinary composition of the team spanning qualitative research, psychology and biomedical sciences was deliberately leveraged during analysis, with differing interpretive perspectives discussed and reconciled through consensus to enrich the analytical process.

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim. Translations and transcriptions were completed by professional translation firms and cross-checked by native speakers in the study team. None of the transcripts were returned to the participants. A reflexive thematic analysis was employed using an inductive coding approach. The first five transcripts were distributed among the interviewers (PVA, MS, PW, RK, FD, IS, BTYW and MR), who familiarised themselves with the content and developed preliminary codes. These preliminary codes were combined, revised, or removed through a series of discussions. Codes were consolidated into themes as the team continuously reviewed the ongoing interviews. Key concepts noted during interviews were shared during debriefing sessions. Interviewers probed additional context where needed. Code saturation, defined as the point at which no new codes emerged from the data, was achieved at the ninth interview, while meaning saturation, defined as the point at which sufficient richness and depth of understanding was attained to fully explain the phenomenon, was achieved at the twelfth interview. This is consistent with Hennink et al37 demonstration that code saturation can be reached with as few as nine interviews, while meaning saturation requires a larger number to capture the nuanced and contextual dimensions of experience, supporting the adequacy of the current sample size. A total of 13 participants completed the interviews, with no dropouts. No repeat interviews were conducted. A codebook was generated with definitions, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and examples of each code. PVA and MR achieved a Cohen’s kappa interrater reliability of 0.76 and coded all transcripts using NVivo V.14 (QSR International).

Pathways to pre-DM diagnosis

Detection during management of chronic conditions

Pre-DM was detected during routine treatment for pre-existing chronic conditions for nearly half of the participants. All these participants had hypertension and/or hyperlipidaemia for which they were seeking treatment at the polyclinic. For these participants, the diagnosis was unexpected as they had presented for the management of known conditions and had not anticipated an additional diagnosis. The narratives showed that the healthcare team communicated to the participants their increased risk of diabetes with age, or with other risk factors such as family history which prompted regular screening. The incidental nature of these diagnoses meant that many participants had little psychological preparation for the news, which shaped their initial emotional responses and engagement with subsequent care. All diagnoses were made in the polyclinic setting. In particular, for many participants diagnosis unfolded across multiple visits with gradual escalation of hyperglycaemia over time. Clinicians played a key role by monitoring the family history, age and other risk factors and conducting timely blood tests while communicating the risk to the participants. This risk stratification was central to facilitating timely detection in this subgroup.

Self-initiated detection through HS

More than half of the participants were diagnosed through self-initiated or workplace-initiated HS. Awareness of anti-diabetes campaigns, known risk of diabetes (eg, family history), desire to stay healthy, accepting HS as a necessary step in old age, company sponsorship, advice from family members and the desire to track health status to detect any illness at early stages to reduce its impact motivated the participants to take up HS. For many, the decision to screen was not driven by symptoms or clinical urgency but by a proactive orientation towards health, often shaped by personal experience of diabetes within the family. For many, family history functioned as a ‘wake up call’ where they watched relatives develop diabetes or suffer complications that promoted proactive screening. Likewise, structured self-assessment tools at the government websites and media campaigns played a role in converting latent awareness into action. Many opted for polyclinics because of the subsidised screening and workplace regulations that accept polyclinic over private panel doctors, the latter of which tend to be more costly. The workplace reimbursement policies favoured polyclinics over private care providers, further channelling screening behaviour towards subsidised public facilities. Affordability, convenience and accessibility emerged as key factors for the screening uptake. Narratives also showed that many of the participants had been doing regular HS annually either at the workplace or in the polyclinics. This suggests that structural factors such as cost and proximity were as influential as personal motivation in determining who came forward for screening.

Dealing with diagnosis: navigating shock, acceptance and worry about the future

Participants’ responses to diagnosis varied from shock to acceptance. Those who perceived themselves as healthy and as following a healthy lifestyle felt shocked and confused, having assumed their habits would protect them from diabetes. The diagnosis disrupted their self-perception, particularly among those who had considered themselves health-conscious, creating a sense of disbelief that their efforts had not been sufficient. Some expressed disbelief at the diagnosis because they had been conscientious about their diet, with one participant exclaiming “Cannot be ah doctor, I always watch out for my meals” and another reflecting “I really do not know how I developed this” given their perceived healthy diet. Younger participants, in particular, worried about progressing to diabetes and developing complications such as amputations and kidney disease, with one participant expressing fear about kidney failure and another simply stating, “I feel very sad because I am young and I get diabetes”. This fear was often vivid and personal, shaped by witnessing the consequences of diabetes in family members or peers, making the diagnosis feel less like an abstract risk and more like a foreseeable trajectory. Some participants were also worried about lifelong medication dependence, drawing on their experience with other chronic conditions to anticipate what a diabetes diagnosis might mean for their autonomy and quality of life. As time progressed, participants showed increasing acceptance. Those who reported responding calmly to the diagnosis attributed their reaction to understanding that diabetes risk increases with age and that pre-DM can be managed through diet and exercise. Their narratives demonstrated awareness of both pre-DM and diabetes with some describing the diagnosis as a ‘wake up call’ rather than crisis and were determined to manage their condition positively. Those with a family history of diabetes appeared to accept the diagnosis more readily as an anticipated outcome of their genetic predisposition.

Life before diabetes: living in comfort and the absence of consequences

Dietary habits and lifestyle choices before diagnosis

Many participants described leading a relaxed lifestyle before diagnosis, with a high-carbohydrate diet including white rice and sweet drinks, and minimal exercise. Some reported unhealthy habits such as consuming sugary foods, drinking alcohol and smoking. These behaviours were not simply the result of ignorance but reflected a lived reality in which food was deeply tied to pleasure, culture and social connection with participants describing deep rooted cultural influence in consuming local delicacies such as kueh-kueh, kopi with sugar, white rice with friends and family without any dietary concerns. The absence of symptoms further reinforced this, underpinning a belief that the absence of symptoms constituted good health and that there was therefore no reason to change. While a few participants made careful lifestyle choices, such as taking daily walks and reducing carbohydrate intake, many engaged in heavy consumption of sweet foods such as cakes and canned drinks. These habits persisted due to a lack of motivation for stricter control and a misperception that their lifestyle was healthy, a perception that the diagnosis would later challenge.

When not knowing felt safer: the reasons for not doing HS

Many participants avoided regular HS due to diagnosis anxiety and low risk perception. The avoidance was not always passive. For some, it reflected a conscious choice to preserve a sense of normalcy, where not knowing meant not having to confront the possibility of illness or the lifestyle changes that a diagnosis might demand. This was at times a deliberate act of emotional self-protection, with some participants describing a pattern of self-denial in which the prospect of receiving a diagnosis felt more threatening than the condition itself, as one participant described: “I would rather not test it because once it is tested and I know that I’m diabetic…”. Other deterrents included lack of awareness about subsidised screening programmes and their importance, worry about screening outcomes, unclear information about the screening process, competing priorities, time constraints, and resistance to potential lifestyle changes following diagnosis. Many participants were unaware of the Screen for Life programme, while others felt that their existing polyclinic appointments were sufficient. For some, additional barriers included ineligibility for subsidised screening, caregiving responsibilities, and the competing demands of work. Taken together, these narratives suggest that avoidance of screening was as much an emotional and psychological response as a practical one.

Life after diagnosis: reconfiguring daily life

Lifestyle changes: renegotiating diet and exercise habits

Following diagnosis, all participants-initiated lifestyle changes through dietary modifications, physical activity, or both. Based on self-acquired knowledge and the healthcare team’s advice, they controlled portion sizes, reduced white rice intake, increased vegetable consumption and avoided sugary and fried foods. Many participants switched from white rice to brown rice and reduced or completely stopped their consumption of sugary drinks, while others tried intermittent fasting or skipping meals to reduce their carbohydrate intake. The process of change was rarely straightforward—participants described a period of trial and error as they navigated clinical recommendations and the practical realities of their daily lives, including family food preferences, cultural eating norms, and the social dimensions of mealtimes. Dietary changes also extended to reducing alcohol intake and smoking for some. Many adopted moderate to vigorous exercise routines such as brisk walking, jogging, swimming and weight training, and followed dietitians’ recommendations for healthy cooking and eating habits. For some, these changes represented not just a shift in behaviour but a broader reorientation of how they related to their bodies and their health.

Alternative therapies: integrating traditional approaches

Alongside conventional medical management, participants described incorporating alternative therapies into their pre-DM care. These choices revealed the deep personal and culturally embedded nature of their health-seeking behaviour. Narratives showed adoption of alternative therapies such as Ayurveda, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) and other health supplements. Bitter gourd was a popular choice that was perceived to be harmless and to have hypoglycaemic effects. For many participants, turning to remedies rooted in their cultural heritage provided a sense of continuity and control at a time when diagnosis had disrupted their sense of normalcy. Some experimented with other health supplements driven by curiosity, peer recommendations, and the hope of finding additional means of managing their condition. Most participants were aware of the risks associated with alternative therapies and believed that evidence-based Western medicine was more effective for managing their pre-DM. The decision to adopt alternative therapies did not stem from a rejection of Western medicine but rather reflected an effort to take ownership of their condition. Cultural knowledge and traditional practices played a significant role in shaping these choices, offering participants a sense of agency at a time when the demands of lifestyle change alone felt overwhelming and insufficient. Pre-DM care

Challenges of pre-DM management

Personal factors: the struggle to sustain behavioural changes

Lifestyle modifications emerged as a significant challenge for many participants. They struggled to change long-established preferences, particularly giving up favourite foods for healthier alternatives that were perceived as less satisfying. Certain habits, such as traditional foods, fast foods, and a sedentary lifestyle were deep-rooted in their lives, as part of their self-identity, and such changes were viewed as disruptive rather than beneficial. Those who adopted healthier diets often experienced cravings for sweet foods. Some participants described sudden cravings for chocolate and sugary items when they felt tired, which they thought was due to over-restriction on sugar or simply a regular craving. The cumulative weight of these sacrifices gradually eroded their resolve and motivation. The narratives indicated a fundamental struggle to cope with the demands of pre-DM management and their sense of what constituted a meaningful and enjoyable life. Lifestyle recommendations were not simply experienced as inconvenient but as threatening to everyday pleasures and personal identity, with non-adherence reflecting a conflicted prioritisation of present quality of life over future health outcomes. This tension was particularly evident in social settings, where participants felt unable to impose their dietary restrictions on friends and family, or to opt out of shared meals without disrupting group dynamics.

Personal barriers, such as self-reported low motivation, lack of willpower, prior injuries, and time constraints, further compounded their pre-DM management. Many relied on hawker centres (food courts selling local food) due to busy schedules, despite knowing it was unhealthy. Physical injuries, such as knee problems, limited the types of exercise participants could engage in. Additionally, for some participants, absence or loss of a companion challenged the motivation.

Limited knowledge about pre-DM management, which participants attributed to their educational background and age-related challenges, led to misconceptions about the effectiveness of lifestyle changes and reduced their commitment to maintaining them. Several participants expressed confusion about what they were and were not allowed to eat and described making dietary decisions based on their current understanding rather than clear guidance. Some did not know how to seek help or navigate the healthcare system and felt unsupported between clinical appointments. This suggests that the struggle to sustain behavioural changes was shaped not only by motivation but also by the degree to which participants understood why change mattered. Some participants downplayed the significance of their pre-DM diagnosis, perceiving it as a mild or borderline condition that did not yet require serious action, which in turn reduced their motivation to engage with recommended lifestyle changes. These findings suggest that effective interventions need to move beyond motivational framing and address how individuals can integrate health-promoting behaviours into a life that still feels meaningful and enjoyable. Approaches that position lifestyle modification as compatible with personal identity and cultural practices, rather than in opposition to them, may better support sustained change than those centred on restriction and discipline alone.

Navigating social and environmental barriers to lifestyle change

Social factors significantly impacted adherence. Social gatherings presented challenges as participants felt obligated to consume unhealthy foods to maintain cultural etiquette and social harmony. The pressure to eat at social occasions was experienced not merely as temptation but as a conflict between individual health goals and deeply held values around hospitality, belonging and cultural participation, making refusal feel socially costly. Those with young families struggled to follow dietary restrictions without limiting their family’s choices. Social media advertisements of appetising foods further challenged their self-discipline.

Environmental factors such as frequent rain and hot weather discouraged exercise. Singapore’s climate meant that outdoor physical activity, often the most accessible and affordable option, was frequently disrupted, leaving participants without viable alternatives. Participants also reported uncertainty in self-managing their pre-diabetes between appointments, particularly regarding food choices and portion control. Some reported experiencing dizziness due to complete elimination of carbohydrates or following unbalanced diets. Older participants found it especially challenging to make significant lifestyle changes and understand written health information due to limited health literacy, age-related difficulties in comprehension and language barriers.

The financial burden of lifestyle changes

Cost emerged as a significant barrier in managing pre-DM. Participants chose polyclinics over private GPs and panel doctors due to lower fees, but remained concerned about expenses for regular blood tests, medications and potential specialist care. Many perceived healthier food options as both expensive and less appealing than their usual choices. Wholegrain bread, lean meats, fish and vegetables were seen as considerably more costly than carbohydrate-heavy food, making it harder for participants to justify the switch, particularly when the healthier options were also considered less tasty. Additional healthcare support, such as consultations with dietitians and diabetes nurses, was often declined due to cost concerns when they learnt that it is not subsidised and reasoned that they had sufficient knowledge to manage the pre-DM on their own. Participants also raised issues about the high cost of glucometers and related supplies for those who were keen to monitor their glucose levels regularly. For some, the perceived high cost of pre-DM services was discouraging and led them to avoid appointments, especially when they felt that their sugar levels were not improving.

Work-related factors: competing priorities and time constraints

Work–life imbalance significantly impacted participants’ ability to manage pre-DM. Long, stressful workdays extending into late nights left little time for exercise, with weekends reserved for household duties and family time. Physical exhaustion discouraged them from exercising or preparing healthy meals at home. Shift workers particularly struggled, as irregular hours disrupted their sleep, diet, and exercise routines. Work commitments interfered with grocery shopping and meal preparation, while after-hours obligations undertaken to maintain job security further limited healthy lifestyle choices. The pressure to extend working hours beyond the standard workday was a recurring concern, with some participants feeling that they had to prove their worth to avoid losing their jobs. Job roles also influenced dietary habits, with sales professionals citing frequent meals with clients as a barrier to maintaining healthy eating patterns. Certain job roles, such as shift work, presented unique challenges for managing pre-DM. Manpower shortages and the demanding nature of such roles made it difficult for participants to take timely breaks, which was a particular concern for those trying to manage their blood glucose levels.

Feeling unheard: the healthcare team-related barriers to care

Some participants preferred GP clinics for their personalised care and rapport, which they found lacking in polyclinics. They attributed this difference to polyclinics’ rotating clinician system, where they saw different doctors at each visit. In contrast, GPs’ long-term familiarity with patients and their families was highly valued, a level of connection that was not noted for other primary care settings. Some participants also felt that information was not always communicated effectively. For instance, one participant only became aware of the importance of foot and eye care after a follow-up visit with a different doctor. Others noted that interactions with polyclinic staff could at times feel impersonal or transactional, which affected their overall experience of care. One participant described receiving inconsistent explanations from different doctors over follow-up visits, which left them confused about their condition. Another highlighted that doctors did not proactively review their health history during visits probably due to the busy schedule, and that patients needed to be very proactive in raising their own concerns.

Gaps in the system: structural barriers to care

Healthcare operational issues emerged as primary barriers, particularly the frequent change of doctors and long waiting times at the polyclinics. Participants expressed discomfort with seeing different physicians at each visit, concerned that this prevented building therapeutic relationships and comprehensive understanding of their health history. They worried that in the limited consultation time, they had to repeatedly explain their conditions, and that important information might not be fully documented or accessible across different healthcare providers.

The frequent change of doctors, combined with long queues, led to what participants described as rushed ‘touch-and-go’ appointments. One participant noted that a single visit could involve multiple separate queues, covering registration, medication collection, payment and booking the next appointment, which meant that each visit could take up a significant portion of the day. Many reported cutting their consultations short when noticing long queues, leaving important questions unasked despite having concerns. While most found polyclinic waiting times excessive, some noted significant improvements in newer facilities.

The appointment booking system presented additional challenges, with inflexible scheduling options and inability to reflect real-time availability from cancellations. Securing appointments in advance was often difficult, with some participants finding that booking online the night before was the most reliable way to get a slot. Rescheduling could also be challenging, as changes sometimes resulted in significantly longer waits for the next available appointment. Older participants struggled with healthcare apps due to technical limitations or lack of digital literacy, whereas the system assumed that all the patients have access to the latest technology and were well-versed in its usage. Participants also found healthcare advice too standardised, lacking personalisation and follow-up monitoring of compliance, which contributed to them reverting to previous lifestyle habits. They expressed a desire for more personalised care and structured follow-up.

Enablers of pre-DM management

From fear to action: personal drivers of pre-DM self-management

Fear of developing diabetes emerged as a key motivator, driven by witnessing complications in friends and family members, as well as media portrayals. This fear was not abstract as participants drew on vivid, personal memories of watching relatives undergoing amputations, dialysis or losing their independence, and these images became powerful reference points that sustained their motivation to change. This fear, combined with the desire not to burden family and the knowledge that pre-diabetes is reversible, motivated participants to take action. The desire not to become a burden was a particularly strong and recurring theme, reflecting the relational and familial dimensions of health motivation in this population. Personal factors enabling successful management included health consciousness, desire for healthy ageing, self-discipline in diet and exercise, and strong self-motivation to maintain changes. Those with pre-existing healthy habits found the transition easier. For these participants, diagnosis served as a consolidating moment that reinforced and gave new urgency to practices already underway, rather than requiring a wholesale change in lifestyle. Several participants also reframed the diagnosis positively, with one describing the pre-diabetes result as a ‘warning sign’ that motivated them to establish a more regular exercise routine.

Taking ownership of their condition, following medical advice, and maintaining a positive attitude facilitated pre-DM management. A strong sense of personal responsibility ran through participants’ accounts, with many expressing that health management was ultimately an individual endeavour: “I have to take care of myself. It’s my health. What happens to me, happens to me, not to somebody else.” External motivators proved helpful, such as supermarket vouchers tied to physical activity goals and cultural practices like fasting. Participants’ confidence in the health benefits of lifestyle changes, supported by healthcare team guidance, further reinforced their commitment.

Family, peers and domestic help as daily support

Social support played a crucial role in pre-DM management, with family serving as the primary source of assistance. Peers, friends, family and domestic helpers were identified as sources of support. Family members helped by purchasing groceries, monitoring blood glucose levels, accompanying patients to appointments, preparing healthy meals and encouraging adherence to dietary and exercise routines. Domestic helpers contributed by preparing healthy meals, while peer support in exercise classes provided additional motivation, the gym environment itself was noted as a subtle form of social accountability, with one participant observing that exercising alongside others made it harder to quit: “When we are going to the gym, this environment is there. So, then we cannot skip.”

Feeling heard and cared for: healthcare-team related factors

Healthcare providers’ interpersonal skills emerged as important enablers, particularly GPs’ empathy, friendliness and personalised approach. Doctors who patiently addressed questions provided clear guidance and avoided creating anxiety about glucose readings enhanced patient engagement. Staff experience in managing workflow and wait times, combined with clear communication, constant encouragement and in-depth medical knowledge, strengthened patient commitment to follow-up care.

Seamless care through operational excellence

Participants valued their pre-DM care journey across various healthcare professionals. They benefited from dietitians’ and doctors’ guidance on food choices, weight management, exercise options and clarification of dietary myths. The healthcare system’s operational features enhanced their experience, including real-time queue tracking apps, appointment reminders and educational content displayed during wait times. The convenient location of polyclinics further improved accessibility.

Participants appreciated the structured workflow that integrated doctor consultations, ancillary services (podiatrists, dietitians), screening for comorbidities and follow-up care. The availability of test results through apps like HealthHub and visit-based follow-up scheduling proved beneficial. Being able to review their own health trends through HealthHub before appointments gave some participants a sense of ownership over their health trajectory, which appeared to support continued engagement with self-management. Many noted significant operational improvements in newer polyclinics compared with older ones, including reduced waiting times, flexible scheduling and home medication delivery, which reduced the practical burden of sustained engagement with care.

Knowledge about pre-DM: sources and information seeking

Pre-DM knowledge came primarily from healthcare professionals, with additional information sourced from family, friends, media and the internet. Following diagnosis, participants’ curiosity and concerns led them to seek information through YouTube, social media, newspapers, books and television, which influenced their lifestyle choices and supplement use. Yet access to information did not uniformly translate into accurate understanding, with some participants drawing on lay theories that only partially reflected clinical reality.

Healthcare professionals served as the most reliable information source. Doctors provided fundamental knowledge about the condition, including blood test interpretation, potential impacts, treatment goals and risk reduction strategies. Nutritionists offered detailed guidance on dietary choices, portion control and exercise regimens.

Family history of diabetes provided many participants with baseline knowledge about the condition. Those who had family members and friends with diabetes benefited from shared experiences and practical management advice. While clinical consultations and online resources provided factual grounding, it was often the informal knowledge gained through close proximity to a family member living with diabetes that proved most influential. This firsthand exposure contextualised risk in ways that resonated more deeply than clinical information, and was frequently the catalyst that moved participants from awareness to action.

Reimagining care: suggestions for improving pre-DM care

While participants appreciated the government’s efforts to combat diabetes, they suggested several improvements. At the community level, they recommended group exercise programmes for pre-DM patients to share success stories of their lifestyle and learn from others. They suggested engaging activities like tai chi, Zumba, cooking classes and jogging through community centres, alongside comprehensive community programmes covering all aspects of pre-DM management. Mobile diabetes screening units serving all housing estates were also proposed.

Participants emphasised the importance of broader public education and awareness. They advocated for early education starting from childhood about pre-DM and screening, supported by door-to-door campaigns in multiple languages targeting all age groups. They suggested intensive media campaigns through public transport, workplaces and public spaces, along with enhanced communication about subsidised screening programmes. Age-specific programmes and social support for older adults were recommended, as was direct messaging to at-risk populations. Many supported stronger nutritional labelling with prominent warning messages similar to cigarette packaging, emphasising the need for continuous reinforcement of health messages for lasting impact. Beyond availability, participants stressed that activities needed to be enjoyable and socially engaging to hold people’s interest over time.

For healthcare settings, recommendations included dedicated doctors to build trust and rapport, similar to GP care but in polyclinic settings. Participants suggested promoting HS during all medical visits and reducing costs for healthy food and diabetes care. They recommended SMS lifestyle reminders between follow-up appointments and improved polyclinic operations with shorter waiting times. Real-time patient volume tracking, workload management, increased medical staffing and direct access to specialists for lifestyle-related queries between appointments were also proposed. Participants highlighted that health messaging would be more effective if delivered within the settings people already frequent such as workplaces, schools and community centres, rather than through one-off roadshows or passive media campaigns that risk missing those most in need.

Additional suggestions focused on enhancing accessibility and engagement through increased subsidies and rewards for regular screening. While acknowledging existing public gyms, participants recommended more community sports facilities catering to varied interests like tennis, soccer, basketball and badminton, along with innovative approaches to overcome financial barriers. Tiered reward systems tied to exercise milestones were seen as effective not just financially, but in fostering a sense of progress and momentum that helped participants maintain healthy behaviours beyond initial motivation. Discussion

The qualitative exploration identified crucial events in the pre-DM journey. Participants were diagnosed mostly at polyclinic settings either during routine care or during regular HS. The diagnosis resulted in shock and worry among younger participants while older participants who were primed towards chronic conditions in old age were able to accept it better. Various personal, social and organisational level barriers and enablers of pre-DM care were identified.

Two different pathways to pre-DM diagnosis were noted in the study: through regular HS and routine clinical screening as part of managing other conditions. Those who self-initiated the HS had higher risk perception due to family history, older age or took part in workplace screening. Adams et al38 showed that workplace HS nearly tripled the rate of screening in their study and recommended that a collaborative approach between healthcare system and workplace could improve the uptake of diabetes HS. In Singapore, the government has leveraged the workplace setting for innovative health promotion activities that include lunchtime talks on healthy lifestyle, subsidised fruit stalls, fruit distribution, programmes to support physical and mental well-being, group physical activities, etc.39 However, the awareness and uptake towards subsidised screening programmes (Screen for Life) was suboptimal. This is particularly notable given that 10 out of 13 participants in our study were unaware of the Screen for Life programme, suggesting that even among those already engaged with the healthcare system through polyclinic attendance, awareness of subsidised screening remains a significant gap. This is in agreement with previous reports where only 42.4% of the population attended regular diabetes HS.15 Those who did not take up regular HS had lower risk perception, diagnosis anxiety and fear of screening outcomes, competing priorities, were unaware of the process and were resistant to potential lifestyle changes. Notably, screening avoidance among participants was not always passive. For some, it represented a deliberate choice to maintain a sense of normalcy where remaining undiagnosed meant avoiding the emotional weight of illness and the lifestyle demands that a diagnosis would bring. This psychological dimension of avoidance, driven by a need to preserve one’s existing sense of self and routine, goes beyond the practical barriers that are commonly documented in quantitative literature. Similar factors were noted by Chien et al40 in a community based cross-sectional study conducted in rural and urban settings in Taiwan. Allowing walk-ins and waiving the co-payment options could contribute to higher uptake as observed by Adams et al.38

Many of the participants followed a sedentary lifestyle and were not careful with their diet, which are known risk factors of pre-DM.41 While none of the patients underwent lifestyle interventions that included dietary advice and physical activity, they still self-initiated healthy diet and exercise regimens. A meta-analysis of 16 randomised controlled trials showed that lifestyle interventions targeting diet and physical activities were not only efficacious in preventing progression to T2DM and in achieving weight loss but also were cost-effective and safe in those with pre-DM.10 Sandforth et al42 demonstrated that weight loss-induced remission reduced T2DM risk by 73% at 2 years post intervention, through decreased visceral adipose tissue and improved insulin sensitivity. What our data add to this evidence base is an understanding of why self-initiated lifestyle change, even when motivated, is difficult to sustain in the Singapore context. This is not due to lack of motivation but rather to a food environment where hawker centres are deeply embedded in Singapore’s social and cultural fabric that made it structurally difficult to make consistently healthy choices. Unlike findings from Western settings where dietary barriers are primarily framed around convenience and cost, our participants described hawker centre dining as simultaneously a cultural obligation, a social ritual and an affordable necessity, making it qualitatively distinct from the food environment barriers reported elsewhere. Alternative therapies such as Ayurvedic medicine, health supplements and TCM were also adopted by a few. While some studies have shown evidence to support these as promising treatment options for reversing pre-DM and for improving complications,43 44 caution needs to be exercised as many of the products contain adulterated materials that can be toxic.45 A key finding that distinguishes our data from existing literature is the motivation behind alternative therapy use. Participants did not turn to traditional remedies out of distrust of Western medicine or as a form of non-adherence. Rather, these choices reflected a search for cultural familiarity and a sense of control at a time when diagnosis had unsettled their daily lives. Most participants held conventional medicine and traditional remedies in parallel, viewing them as complementary rather than competing. For clinicians, this has practical implications. Conversations about alternative therapy use may be better received when framed around safety and informed integration into care, rather than outright discouragement. Participants often seek alternative therapies due to perceived safety and family recommendations45 46 but remain unaware of their ingredients and adverse effects,47 leading to potential toxicity and variable treatment responses. Hence, routine care should create awareness on alternative therapies.

The response to pre-DM diagnosis varied from worry to stress to acceptance. While worries originated from future complications acceptance came from the knowledge that pre-DM is reversible and risk increases with ageing. The emotional trajectory following diagnosis appeared to be shaped considerably by participants’ prior exposure to diabetes within their families and communities. Those who had watched relatives undergo amputations, dialysis or lose their independence carried these experiences as vivid and lasting reference points, making the diagnosis feel immediate and personally relevant in ways that clinical risk statistics alone could not achieve. This points to a practical consideration for risk communication in clinical settings; drawing on patients’ own lived experience of diabetes complications may prove more meaningful than numerical risk framing in motivating engagement with care. Similar responses were observed in other studies; however, different responses emerged when the diagnosis was presented positively with a message that pre-DM is reversible.19 28 The attitude of healthcare professionals, diabetes knowledge, having friends or family with diabetes and normalisation of diabetes within the community also lessened the impact of the diagnosis.28

Several barriers to pre-DM care were identified across personal factors to sustain behavioural change, financial burden, social and environmental, work-related and healthcare-system related domains. Personal barriers (Personal factors: the struggle to sustain behavioural changes) included resistance to lifestyle changes, lack of willpower, cravings and insufficient knowledge, which were similarly observed in other studies.19 48–50 Research suggests these challenges stem from the complex relationships among self-regulation, self-control and autonomy.19 48 These findings add nuance beyond existing literature. Lifestyle recommendations were not merely inconvenient. They felt threatening to participants’ sense of self, as dietary habits and daily routines were deeply tied to cultural identity, family traditions and personal enjoyment. Non-adherence therefore reflected a genuine tension between present quality of life and future health, rather than a simple lack of willpower. Interventions designed to work within participants’ cultural and personal frameworks, rather than against them, may achieve more sustained behaviour change than those built around restriction alone. In Singapore specifically, social gatherings and traditional celebrations posed significant challenges to maintaining healthy lifestyles, indicating the need for family-based dietary interventions.51

Work–life balance emerged as a main barrier in our study. A systematic review of 14 studies identified long working hours, shift work, work stress, heavy workload and low staff numbers as barriers to healthy eating, which corroborates our results.52 These factors led to poor dietary choices and reduced physical activity, resulting in increased sugar intake and nutritional deficiencies.53 Beyond general work-related barriers, participants consistently deprioritised health self-management against the demands of long hours, job commitments and after-hours obligations. The ready availability of affordable but nutritionally suboptimal hawker centre meals further compounded this, creating a convergence of occupational and environmental factors that are particularly relevant to the local context. Healthcare-related barriers noted in the study, such as insufficient support from healthcare teams, non-tailored repetitive standard advice, lack of systematic follow-up, busy schedules of doctors that restricted knowledge transfer and rapport, short consultations and long waiting times, were observed in other studies as well.19 54 55 These issues stemmed from low patient-to-care provider ratios amid high demand for diabetes care.55 The limited availability and high cost of healthy food options, along with unfavourable weather conditions for outdoor activities, were identified as additional barriers in both our study and others.17 Singapore has a hot and humid weather with occasional rains throughout the year which was also noted as a barrier in previous studies.51

Many changed their lifestyles following diagnosis, which was motivated by a desire to reverse pre-DM and to avoid complications, along with a sense of personal responsibility for self-care and not to burden family, as noted in other studies.17 56 The desire not to burden family members was a strong and recurring motivator in participants’ narratives, reflecting the relational dimensions of health motivation within Singapore’s family-oriented cultural context. Health promotion messaging that incorporates family and collective responsibility, alongside individual health outcomes, may therefore resonate more meaningfully with this population. However, participants often struggled with self-criticism, guilt and low confidence.17 Wallace et al57 found that stress from lifestyle changes led to negative emotional states affecting cognition and decision-making. These stresses impacted lifestyle sustainability through multiple mechanisms: increased insulin resistance,57 58 altered metabolism,59 impaired psychological responses60 and triggered unhealthy behaviours such as sedentary lifestyle and poor dietary choices.61

Several facilitators were noted in the current study including social factors (family, peers and domestic help as daily support) such as family and friends, pre-DM knowledge, personal factors (self-motivation, self-determination, desire to keep healthy, fear of complications and developing T2DM), healthcare team (feeling heard and cared for: healthcare team related factors) and system-specific factors (seamless care through operational excellence; friendly, empathetic and professional team members and improved workflow and patient management). Similar factors were noted in other studies.17 19 56 Healthcare teams provided essential information and guidance for lifestyle management,17 while supportive relationships enabled better adherence to dietary changes and physical activity.56 Family members played crucial roles as shared decision-makers and active participants in exercise and meal preparation, helping sustain lifestyle changes.17 In the Singapore context, domestic helpers emerged as an additional and underreported source of practical support, contributing to meal preparation and daily health routines in ways that are specific to households in this setting and not captured in studies from other countries. The motivation to reverse pre-DM and achieve health goals was consistently reported across studies.19 56

The study has several strengths and limitations. This is one of the few studies that examined the lived experiences of those with pre-DM that looked at various aspects in their life since diagnosis. The multi-ethnic maximum variation sample and interviews offered in native languages ensured representation of the population perspectives. Additionally, triangulation approaches involving investigator (ethnically and linguistically matched interviewer-participant pairs), methodological (online and face-to-face modes, field notes and debriefing sessions), and data (maximum variation) triangulation added to the methodological rigour. The main limitation of the study is that the participants were selected through clinician referral from the National Healthcare Group polyclinics or self-referral, and it is possible that the experiences of those seeking treatment at GP, other healthcare clusters or private clinics could have been missed.

Conclusions

The study revealed suboptimal awareness of subsidised screening programmes among pre-DM patients, with diagnoses primarily occurring through routine healthcare visits and workplace screening. Screening avoidance was driven not only by lack of awareness and cost concerns, but also by a deliberate choice among some participants to avoid confronting a potential diagnosis and its lifestyle implications. While participants initially expressed concerns about their diagnosis, all attempted lifestyle changes despite early apprehensions, though many struggled to sustain them over time. The sustainability of lifestyle changes was undermined by the deeply cultural and identity-based dimensions of dietary habits, the ready availability of hawker centre food, work-related time pressures and social obligations at communal gatherings. The study identified various barriers and facilitators including personal factors (motivation, self-determination, cravings), affordability, social-cultural influences, workplace conditions and healthcare system factors, which could inform future intervention strategies. Use of alternative therapies reflected cultural familiarity and a desire for personal agency rather than rejection of conventional medicine, underscoring the need for open clinical conversations around such practices. The desire not to burden family was a key motivator for self-management, suggesting that health promotion strategies incorporating family and collective responsibility may resonate well with this population. Although participants acknowledged existing government measures for pre-DM management, they suggested improvements including innovative awareness programmes, door-to-door campaigns, warning labels on sugary foods and beverage items, increased treatment subsidies and healthcare system enhancements such as reduced clinician rotation, shorter waiting times and more personalised follow-up care to meet growing diabetes care demands.
