Peroxide‐Assisted Solvate Engineering Enables Record‐High Birefringence in a Solar‐Blind Transparent Crystal
Authors: Yang Li, Congcong Jin, Kang Min Ok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDeveloping birefringent materials that simultaneously exhibit short ultraviolet (UV) cutoff edges and large birefringence is highly desirable but remains challenging. Here, we report a peroxide‐containing solvate crystal, 4HP·H2O2 (4HPO2, 4HP = 4‐hydroxypyridine), obtained by incorporating H2O2 into the 4HP lattice through a mild aqueous‐solution method. 4HPO2 exhibits a solar‐blind UV cutoff edge at 278 nm together with a giant experimental birefringence of 0.609@546 nm, representing an approximately 14‐fold enhancement compared with pristine 4HP. Structural analysis reveals that H2O2 induces a pronounced reorganization of the 4HP packing, converting a nearly orthogonal arrangement into an ordered, nearly parallel alignment through strengthened hydrogen‐bonding interactions. Combined experimental and theoretical analyses indicate that the aligned 4HP sublattice serves as the dominant source of optical anisotropy, whereas H2O2 mainly acts as a hydrogen‐bond‐directed structural modulator with a secondary direct optical contribution. The reordered packing also creates an anisotropic local‐field environment that further amplifies the molecular polarizability anisotropy (Δα). These findings highlight peroxide‐assisted solvate engineering as an effective strategy for tuning packing and optical anisotropy and provide a promising design principle for developing ultraviolet birefringent materials.

Introduction

Solvates, formed through intermolecular interactions between solute and solvent molecules within crystal lattices, have attracted increasing attention in fields such as pharmaceuticals, catalysis, gas storage, and optical materials [1, 2, 3, 4]. Variations in the presence, type, and stoichiometry of solvent molecules can give rise to distinct pseudopolymorphic phases with markedly different physical properties [5, 6]. In optical materials, such solvent‐mediated structural modulation provides an effective strategy for tuning crystal packing and, consequently, optical anisotropy [7, 8]. To date, H2O has been the most widely explored solvent in birefringent systems, and numerous hydrate crystals have demonstrated that hydrogen‐bonding interactions can strongly influence the arrangement of birefringence‐active groups (BAGs) and the resulting optical properties. Representative examples include BiF3 and BiF3·H2O, [C(NH2)3]2PO3(OH)·H2O and [C(NH2)3]2PO3(OH), as well as C3H8N6(BF4)2 and α‐/β‐C3H8N6(BF4)2·H2O [9, 10, 11]. In contrast, non‐hydrated solvates involving other solvent molecules remain far less explored in birefringent systems, with only a few reported examples such as SbOCl·DMSO and CO(NH2)2·H2O2 [12, 13].

Birefringent materials are indispensable components of modern optical systems because of their unique ability to modulate light polarization [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. In particular, materials that combine large birefringence with short ultraviolet cutoff edges are highly desirable for deep‐ultraviolet (DUV) and solar‐blind optical applications [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. However, achieving these two properties simultaneously remains a long‐standing challenge. Large birefringence generally requires highly anisotropic electronic distributions, whereas short cutoff edges typically demand wide‐band‐gap building units. Considerable efforts have therefore been devoted to identifying functional units capable of balancing optical anisotropy with ultraviolet transparency [25, 26, 27, 28].

Among the structural motifs explored in recent years, BAGs with highly anisotropic electronic structures have played a critical role in the design of high‐performance birefringent crystals [29, 30, 31]. Several borate‐ and phosphate‐based anionic groups have been shown to generate strong optical anisotropy while preserving relatively short cutoff edges, which has stimulated extensive exploration of related systems [32, 33]. Heterocyclic π‐conjugated units also represent promising candidates because their anisotropic electron distributions can contribute significantly to birefringence [34, 35, 36]. The 4‐hydroxypyridine (4HP) moiety is one such attractive functional unit (Figure S1), as it combines a conjugated six‐membered ring (6‐MR) with hydrogen‐bond donor and acceptor sites, making it favorable for constructing ordered packing motifs with potentially enhanced optical anisotropy [15, 27, 37, 38, 39].

Hydrogen bonding plays a particularly important role in molecular birefringent crystals because it can regulate both the orientation and spatial distribution of BAGs within the crystal lattice [40, 41]. Previous studies on hydrate‐based birefringent materials have demonstrated that H2O can effectively mediate molecular arrangements and thus influence macroscopic optical properties. These observations suggest that hydrogen‐bonding solvent molecules may serve not only as lattice guests but also as structure‐directing components for tuning crystal packing and optical anisotropy. Nevertheless, most reported examples remain limited to hydrates, whereas other hydrogen‐bonding solvents capable of producing distinct packing modes have rarely been explored in this context.

In this regard, H2O2 represents an intriguing yet largely overlooked candidate. Compared with H2O, H2O2 possesses a nonplanar H─O─O─H geometry with flexible dihedral angles and bifunctional hydrogen‐bonding capability, features that may enable more diverse intermolecular interactions and packing arrangements. At the same time, peroxide‐containing birefringent crystals remain extremely rare, and their potential for regulating optical anisotropy has not been systematically explored [42, 43, 44]. This raises an interesting question as to whether H2O2 can function as an effective solvate component for reorganizing molecular packing and enhancing birefringence in ultraviolet‐transparent crystals.

Here, we address this question by introducing H2O2 into the 4HP lattice and obtaining a new peroxide‐containing solvate crystal, 4HP·H2O2 (4HPO2), through a mild aqueous‐solution route. 4HPO2 exhibits a solar‐blind UV cutoff edge of 278 nm and a giant experimental birefringence of 0.609@546 nm, corresponding to an approximately 14‐fold enhancement relative to pristine 4HP [45]. Structural analysis reveals that the incorporation of H2O2 reorganizes the packing of the ∞[4HP] chains from a nearly orthogonal arrangement into an ordered, nearly parallel alignment through strengthened directional hydrogen‐bonding interactions. Combined experimental and theoretical analyses further indicate that the aligned 4HP sublattice serves as the dominant source of optical anisotropy, whereas H2O2 primarily acts as a hydrogen‐bond‐directed structural modulator with a secondary direct optical contribution. These results demonstrate that peroxide‐assisted solvate engineering provides an effective approach for tuning crystal packing and optical anisotropy and offer a useful design principle for the development of ultraviolet birefringent materials.

Results and Discussion

Crystal Structure

4HPO2 crystallizes in the monoclinic space group P21/c (No. 14) with lattice parameters a = 6.9150(2) Å, b = 12.9235(3) Å, c = 7.5812(2) Å, and β = 116.9300(10)° (Tables S1–S6). In both pristine 4HP and the 4HPO2 solvate, the bifunctional hydrogen‐bonding 4HP moieties are connected via N–H···O hydrogen bonds, forming infinite ∞[4HP] single chains arranged in a head‐to‐head configuration (Figure 1a,b). However, in the 4HP (C2/c) structure, these chains adopt an unfavorable cross‐like arrangement, with a large dihedral angle of 89.37° between adjacent chains. Such a nearly orthogonal orientation significantly reduces the cooperative alignment of BAGs, accounting for the very small birefringence observed in 4HP (Figures 1c,d and S3).

(a) Molecular structure of 4HP; (b) neutral [4HP] infinity chains; (c) ball‐and‐stick model, and (d) topological representation of pristine 4HP; (e) molecular structure of H2O2; (f) ball‐and‐stick model of the 4HPO2 crystal structure; (g) ∞[(4HP·H2O2)2] pseudo‐layer motif; (h) topological representation of 4HPO2.

To address this limitation, H2O2 was introduced as a second bifunctional hydrogen‐bonding ligand to modulate the packing of the ∞[4HP] chains (Figure 1e,f). Two H2O2 solvent molecules, along with their hydrogen atoms, function as ‘claws’ that firmly bridge neighboring 4HP chains oriented in opposite directions through O–H···O hydrogen bonds, resulting in the formation of ∞[(4HP·H2O2)2] layers (Figure 1g). This interaction dramatically reorganizes the relative arrangement of adjacent chains, converting the original crossed configuration into an ordered, nearly parallel alignment. Consequently, the dihedral angle between coplanar 4HP groups is significantly reduced from 89.37° to 7.45° (Figure S4). These neutral ∞[(4HP·H2O2)2] layers further stack via π–π interactions, ultimately constructing the 3D structure of 4HPO2 (Figures 1f,h and S5; Table S6).

Evaluation of Hydrogen Bonding

Given the critical role of hydrogen bonding in regulating the assembly of the ∞[4HP] chains, we examined in detail the hydrogen‐bonding environment involving H2O2 in the optimized structure of 4HPO2. The electrostatic potential (ESP) maps (Figure 2a,b) reveal that both 4HP and H2O2 function as typical bifunctional hydrogen‐bonding units, enabling their cooperative assembly through directional hydrogen‐bond interactions (Figure 2c). The bond lengths of the key O–H···O hydrogen bonds (Figure 2d) provide initial evidence for their relative strengths: the H2O2–4HP interactions measure 1.676 and 1.768 Å, whereas the 4HP–4HP interaction is longer at 1.797 Å, suggesting stronger intermolecular interactions between H2O2 and 4HP. To further visualize and quantify these interactions, we performed an independent gradient model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH) analysis [46, 47, 48]. The resulting IGMH isosurfaces (Figures 2e and S6) reveal three distinct regions, with the darkest blue isosurface corresponding to the shortest and strongest H2O2–4HP hydrogen bond (1.676 Å). These findings are further supported by Hirshfeld surface analysis (Figures S7 and S8), where pronounced red regions around H2O2 and a sharp spike in the corresponding 2D fingerprint plots clearly indicate the presence of short, strong hydrogen bonds.

Multimodal characterization of hydrogen bonding. (a–c) Electrostatic potential (ESP) maps of 4HP, H2O2, and the [(4HP·H2O2)2] cluster. (d) Hydrogen‐bonding environment surrounding the H2O2 molecule. Text colors indicate: O···H distances (black); CVB indices (green), and hydrogen‐bond energies (red). (e) IGMH isosurface of the [(4HP 2·H2O2)2] cluster.

The strength of these hydrogen bonds was quantitatively assessed using core‐valence bifurcation (CVB) indices, wherein more negative values correspond to stronger interactions [49]. The calculated CVB indices of −0.077, −0.030, and −0.019 (Figure 2d) reveal a clear hierarchy of hydrogen‐bond strengths. In addition, hydrogen‐bond energy calculations yielded values of −11.11 kcal mol−1 for the strongest H2O2–4HP interaction and −7.53 kcal mol−1 for the weakest 4HP–4HP interaction. According to the classification established by Lu and co‐workers, the strongest interaction falls within the medium‐strength regime, whereas the others range from weak to medium [47, 48]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the stronger H2O2–4HP hydrogen bonds play a decisive role in reorganizing the ∞[4HP] chains into a more favorably aligned configuration.

Characterization

The infrared spectra of 4HPO2 and 4HP are highly similar, as shown in Figure S9. The UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectrum indicates that 4HPO2 exhibits a short solar‐blind UV cutoff edge at 278 nm, corresponding to an optical band gap of 4.19 eV (Figure 3a–b) [50]. In addition, thermogravimetric analysis indicates that 4HPO2 remains stable up to 343 K (Figure S10), suggesting reasonable thermal stability for optical applications, although further studies on long‐term environmental and photochemical stability are still required.

The well‐aligned arrangement of 4HP and H2O2 moieties within the 4HPO2 lattice, enabled by the aforementioned structural modulation, is expected to generate pronounced optical anisotropy. To verify this, experimental measurements were conducted on a transparent single crystal. Using the relationship R = Δn × d, the experimental birefringence was determined to be 0.609@546 nm (Figure 3c–f). This value is in good agreement with the calculated birefringence of 0.553@546 nm (Figure 3g), supporting both the reliability of the experimental measurements and the validity of the theoretical description of the optical anisotropy. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the largest experimentally measured birefringence among reported materials operating in the solar‐blind region and is also the highest value observed for a 6‐MR based solvate across the DUV−UV spectral range.

As illustrated in Figure 3h, the Pareto front (red dashed line) delineates the trade‐off between UV transparency and optical anisotropy among selected birefringent crystals (Table S7). 4HPO2 is positioned near the Pareto frontier and emerges as one of the outstanding performers that simultaneously combine a solar‐blind cutoff edge with giant experimental birefringence. Relative to pristine 4HP, the birefringence of 4HPO2 is enhanced by approximately 14‐fold, highlighting the profound impact of solvate‐induced packing reconstruction on macroscopic optical behavior.

Origin of the Giant Birefringence

A series of density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to gain deeper insight into the electronic structure and optical properties of 4HPO2. The compound is identified as an indirect‐band gap material with a calculated band gap of 3.56 eV (Figure S11) [51]. The valence‐band maximum and conduction‐band minimum are predominantly composed of orbitals associated with the 4HP units (Figure S12). The spatial distribution of the highest occupied and lowest unoccupied crystal orbitals (HOCO and LUCO), together with the electron localization function (ELF) map, further reveals that the 4HP moiety exhibits pronounced anisotropic electron density distribution, which is favorable for generating strong optical anisotropy (Figure S13a–d).

To probe the microcosmic origin of the birefringence, the Δα values of 4HP and H2O2 were calculated (Table S8) [52]. The 4HP moiety (45.04 a.u.) exhibits substantially larger intrinsic anisotropy than H2O2 (7.02 a.u.), indicating that it serves as the primary optically active component within the crystal. However, intrinsic anisotropy alone cannot fully account for the exceptionally large macroscopic birefringence observed in 4HPO2.

The relationship between the crystallographic axes and the optical principal axes of 4HPO2 is shown in Figure 4a. Notably, the optical principal Z‐axis is nearly parallel to the crystallographic b‐axis, indicating that the strongest optical response is closely associated with the direction of chain alignment in the crystal. This result is consistent with the structural analysis, which shows that the incorporation of H2O2 reorganizes the 4HP sublattice into a nearly parallel packing arrangement that is favorable for constructive optical anisotropy.

(b) Principal components of the Lorentz‐factor tensor (L ii) and the corresponding refractive indices (n i). Color code: 4HP (red) and H2O2 (pink). (c) Schematic deconvolution of the structural and optical contributions of the 4HP and H2O2 sublattices to the anisotropic local‐field environment and the resulting birefringence.

In addition to molecular alignment, local‐field effects also play an important role in amplifying the macroscopic birefringence [53, 54]. As shown in Figure 4b, the calculated Lorentz‐factor tensor deviates significantly from the isotropic value of 1/3, with principal components of L xx = 0.42, L yy = 0.36, and L zz = 0.22. This result indicates that the local dielectric environment in 4HPO2 is strongly anisotropic. Such an anisotropic local‐field environment enhances the intrinsic molecular Δα along favorable optical directions, thereby amplifying the overall birefringence.

To quantify this effect, we further analyzed the tensor contributions of the 4HP and H2O2 sublattices to the principal optical axes through Lorentz‐factor decomposition and full tensor projection (Figure 4c; Sections S3, S4). The results show that the 4HP sublattice dominates the tensor‐framework contribution, accounting for 81% of the total structural contribution, whereas H2O2 contributes the remaining 19%. Further local‐field‐integrated birefringence deconvolution indicates that 4HP contributes 94.11% of the total birefringence, while H2O2 contributes only 5.89% in a direct optical sense.

Accordingly, the giant birefringence of 4HPO2 can be attributed to three key factors: (i) the intrinsically anisotropic 4HP units, (ii) H2O2‐induced reorganization of the crystal packing into a nearly parallel arrangement, and (iii) local‐field amplification within the reordered lattice. Overall, these findings establish a clear structure–property relationship in 4HPO2, in which H2O2 primarily acts as a hydrogen‐bond‐directed structural modulator, while the aligned 4HP sublattice dominates the optical anisotropy, and the anisotropic local‐field environment further amplifies the resulting birefringence.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we demonstrate an effective solvate‐engineering strategy to enhance birefringence in a solar‐blind transparent crystal by incorporating H2O2 into the 4HP lattice. The resulting peroxide‐containing solvate, 4HPO2, exhibits a solar‐blind UV cutoff edge at 278 nm and a giant experimental birefringence of 0.609@546 nm, representing an approximately 14‐fold enhancement compared with pristine 4HP. Structural analysis reveals that the introduction of H2O2 reorganizes the packing of the ∞[4HP] chains from a nearly orthogonal arrangement into an ordered, nearly parallel alignment through strengthened directional hydrogen‐bonding interactions. This packing reconfiguration serves as the principal origin of the markedly enhanced optical anisotropy.

Combined experimental and theoretical analyses further indicate that the giant birefringence of 4HPO2 is dominated by the aligned 4HP sublattice, while H2O2 primarily serves as a hydrogen‐bond‐directed structural modulator with only a secondary direct optical contribution. The anisotropic local‐field environment generated by the reordered crystal packing further amplifies the intrinsic molecular Δα, ultimately leading to the observed macroscopic birefringence.

These results demonstrate that peroxide‐assisted solvate engineering provides an effective route for tuning molecular packing and optical anisotropy in birefringent crystals. More broadly, this work establishes a practical design principle for developing high‐performance ultraviolet birefringent materials that simultaneously achieve short cutoff wavelengths and giant birefringence.
