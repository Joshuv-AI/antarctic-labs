 1. INTRODUCTION
In advanced reactor (AR) designs, a common feature is continuous chemical processing and circulation
of the nuclear material. This work bridges a significant measurement gap in safeguarding reactors with
circulating fuel or continuous refueling by leveraging and building on the isotope ratio method first de-
veloped by our team under an FY21 Advanced Reactors International Safeguards Engagement (ARISE)
project (Uribe et al. 2021). In circulating fuel designs, the radioisotope inventory changes from traditional
effects (e.g., radioactive decay, fission) but also includes material transport due to pressure and temperature
gradients. Such designs may also require regular or continuous additions or removals during operation,
which significantly increases the rate of inventory change compared to traditional pressurized water reac-
tor (PWR)s. Thus, directly tracking the nuclear inventory is ineffective since the isotopes are continuously
added and removed. The isotope ratio method instead focuses on detecting changes to the input and out-
put flows of radioisotopes. Previous work showed that for well-chosen pairs of isotopes, the isotopic ratio
provides a sensitive and lasting indicator of deviation from normal conditions (e.g., startup, shutdown, di-
version).
The isotope ratio method is a process monitoring method with potential for application in for forward-
looking approaches to International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) safeguards. The original process mon-
itoring method was developed for a specific case—the decay tank of a thorium-fueled molten salt breeder
reactor. In this expanded work, we explored other types of reactors and processes with nonstationary (e.g.,
flowing) nuclear material, which are difficult to safeguard with traditional methods because of the transient
nature of the systems.
The goal of the present work is to generalize the isotope ratio method for use in processes with continu-
ously flowing nuclear material. All continuous processes have an average time for isotopes to be replaced
in the system. The isotope ratio method works by choosing radioisotopes with half-lives both above and
below the processing time. The present work seeks to explore which isotopes are suitable for the method
by simulating the nuclear inventory, radioisotope emissions, and detector responses for several classes of
advanced reactors. While the method can in principle be applied to other processes (e.g., enrichment or
reprocessing facilities), the present work limits scope to ARs with continuously flowing fuel.
Section 2 details the mathematics supporting the isotope ratio method, and Section 3 introduces the rep-
resentative ARs selected for this work. Section 4 discusses how each reactor was analyzed, and Section 5
showcases the results for each representative reactor. Finally, Section 6 provides concluding remarks and
suggests pathways for further analysis.

 2. ISOTOPE RATIO METHOD
The isotope ratio method provides a framework for monitoring for changes in an inventory. It is most use-
ful for inventories with continuously flowing material, for which traditional item counting methods are not
applicable. An inventory can be any defined volume for which the inputs and outputs to the volume are
readily quantified, such as the core of a reactor, a material balance area, or a tank or process unit. The iso-
tope ratio method is most effective when the interior of the volume is relatively homogeneous (i.e., a small
sample is representative of the whole or average volume). Of particular interest for this work are ARs with
continuously flowing fuel such as molten salt reactor (MSR) and pebble bed reactor (PBR) designs. This
description of the isotope ratio method has been adapted from another presentation, which includes more
details for the interested reader (Reed, Uribe, and Evans 2022).
As a simple example of the method, we examine a nuclear inventory (I) with a single input (A) and a sin-
gle output (B) as shown in Fig. 1. In general, the isotope ratio method works for continuous, periodic, or
irregular inputs and outputs; however, for simplicity, the following presentation considers only continuous
flows. The flowchart in Fig. 1 could represent an MSR with a feed of fresh fuel salt and removal of waste
or used salt. Often, such a system has a salt loop, which includes a recycle stream back to the reactor core;
in this case, the salt loop would be contained entirely within the nuclear inventory in Fig. 1. The nuclear
inventory is assumed to be well mixed (i.e., homogeneous), which is appropriate for most ARs with con-
tinuously flowing nuclear material. Most ARs use some sort of pump to induce material flow, which often
leads to turbulent flow, which itself promotes mixing. Therefore, the homogeneous assumption is applica-
ble to most ARs and is not significantly restrictive.

Any isotope with relatively slow rates compared to the effective system half-life requires many effective
half-lives to return to equilibrium values. Conversely, an isotope with relatively fast rates returns to equi-
librium inventory values in less than one system effective half-life. Previous work showed that the ratio
of a slow inventory to a fast inventory provided a sensitive and lasting indication of abnormal changes to
the inventory (Reed, Uribe, and Evans 2022). This work extends these previous efforts by finding fast and
slow isotopes for realistic AR systems.

per MW, which is equivalent to approximately 1.052 g of fissile isotopes (i.e., 235 U consumed per day per
MW). For a reactor to remain critical, the replacement rate must be at least enough to replace the fissioned

the effective half-life is unaffected; it already considers how the system dynamics (in this case, those of the
reactor core) change with removed material.

 3. NOMINAL AR MODELS
For this work, the AR design space was divided based on fuel type and spectral hardness. In particular,
only ARs with continuously flowing nuclear material were considered, narrowing the design space to pri-
marily MSRs and PBRs. Within these reactor categories, several design parameters may vary including
fuel type, moderation materials, reflector materials, conditioning processes (e.g., off-gas systems, fission
product removal), and online refueling. To further narrow the design space, several representative designs
were selected to cover the breadth of AR designs.
For MSRs, both fast and thermal designs were considered, and fuel types included LEU, high-assay low-
enriched uranium (HALEU), and thorium. Of the six possible combinations, fast LEU and fast thorium
are not practical for commercial applications and were not considered for this work. A thermal LEU MSR
design is presented in Section 3.1. Thermal HALEU and fast HALEU designs are discussed in Section 3.2
and Section 3.3, respectively. The final MSR design, a thermal thorium MSR, is presented in Section 3.4.
In addition to the four MSR designs, a thermal HALEU PBR design was considered and is presented in
Section 3.5.
Other types of MSRs are possible as well, but they are not considered in this work because of difficulties
in obtaining representative design details. In particular, an MSR can use plutonium as the fissile isotope, as
in mixed oxide (MOX) reactor designs. Companies proposing plutonium-fueled MSRs include Thorizen1 ,
Naarea2 , and Stellaria3 . Additionally, waste-burner reactors can use higher actinides and transuranic ra-
dioactive waste (TRU) fuel. These TRU-fueled designs include Saltfoss’s4 compact MSR, Canadian com-
pany Moltex Energy’s5 design, and the Copenhagen Atomics6 design.
The goal for modeling these reactors was to estimate the average fuel composition at a key measurement
point (KMP), which is a designated location for a radiation detector or other measurement instruments.
These KMPs are likely to be positioned on the return loop to the core, which is in a weaker radiation envi-
ronment compared to the core, making detector measurements easier. For MSRs, the fuel salt is typically
homogeneous because of its liquid state and rapid fuel cycling. Therefore, the estimated composition for
an MSR was assumed equivalent no matter the measurement point.
However, a PBR often includes burnup measurements for individual pebbles, which add a delay into the
process. For a PBR, a KMP is likely colocated with the burnup measurements or on the feed hopper. As
will be discussed in detail in Section 4, the PBR was handled differently from the MSRs because the com-
position of the pebbles is comparatively less homogeneous.
3.1 THERMAL SPECTRUM LEU-FUELED MSR
The thermal spectrum LEU-fueled design is based on the molten salt breeder reactor (MSBR) design pro-
duced by Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) during the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment (MSRE)
(Bettis, Alexander, and Watts 1972). The SCALE model used for this work was adapted from other work
(Radulescu et al. 2025) that presents the full details of the model. Figure 4 presents the core model. The
original MSBR was designed as a breeder reactor, but for this work, the salt is modified to reduce the
breeding ratio to better approximate designs closer to deployability. The MSBR design had a graphite-
moderated core and used a mixture of LiF and BeF2 salts combined with UF4 . The uranium for this model
is enriched to 2.3% and includes flow blocks that remove noble gases and metals during core depletion.

3.3 FAST SPECTRUM HALEU-FUELED MSR
Fast spectrum reactors do not include moderation to keep the neutron spectra hardened. Instead, they use
neutron reflectors to reduce leakage and improve neutron multiplication. A design similar to TerraPower’s11
molten chloride fast reactor (MCFR) was selected as a representative design for the fast spectrum HALEU-
fueled reactor. The model used for this work was adapted from other work (Hartanto et al. 2023). Figure 6
presents the core model.
The representative design includes noble gas and metal removal during operation. The nominal power is
120 MWth, and the reactor has a nominal core lifetime of 7 years. The initial fuel in the model is nomi-
nally enriched to 13.44 wt% 235 U. The model also features online refueling with salt that is enriched to
19.75 wt%. For this work, the fuel composition is calculated for the end of the core lifetime. The residence
time for the main salt loop, computed based on the reactor’s design parameters using Eq. (8), is approx-
imately 60 years. This half-life calculation assumes that all uranium fed into the core is fissioned, and
based on the calculation it is estimated that enough uranium is present at loading to provide power for the
lifetime of the reactor without refueling. The uranium residence time (and therefore the effective half-life
of the system) decreases significantly if the removed irradiated salt still contains appreciable 235 U.

3.4 THERMAL SPECTRUM THORIUM-FUELED MSR
The final MSR design considered was another thermal spectrum reactor that breeds 233 U from thorium
during operation. For this work, the original MSDR design was simulated based on available design spec-
ifications (ROBERTSON 1971). Figure 7 presents the core model. This reactor is similar to the represen-
tative reactor described in Section 3.1; however, the initial fuel composition is instead a 71.5–16.0–12.0–
0.5 mol% mixture of LiF–BeF2 –ThF4 –UF4 that is enriched to 93 wt% 235 U. The MSDR is another design
from the MSRE, and it was created as a practical pilot plant–sized MSR.
In this work, the simulation includes noble gas and metal removal but does not include any fuel additions.
The reactor power is nominally 500 MWth, and the core lifetime is approximately 11 years. The residence
time for the main salt loop, computed using Eq. (8), is approximately 30–60 days assuming that all tho-
rium fed into the reactor becomes 233 U. The chosen design is a single-fluid version, which differs from a
two-fluid design in which both uranium- and thorium-bearing salts are physically separated to enhance
the breeding ratio. The MSDR design is similar to the design of the Chinese TMSR-LF1 (thorium-based
molten salt reactor–liquid fuel).

3.5 PBR
The final reactor considered for this work was the PBR. A PBR has continuously flowing nuclear ma-

X-Energy’s12 Xe-100 high-temperature gas cooled PBR was used to generate a set of simulated pebble
compositions. The model was created using a modification of the SCALE leap-in method for cores at equi-
librium (SLICE) method. Details of the model and the method used to create it are presented elsewhere

check if maximum allowed burnup has been reached. Therefore, the effective residence time for a loop
is the time for a single pass plus the added pebble cooling time for burnup measurements. The residence
time is the average time a pebble takes to pass through the core, which is approximately 260 days for this
design (assuming no pebble cooling). From the residence time, the effective half-life is computed as ap-
proximately 180 days (assuming no pebble cooling).

 4. METHODOLOGY
For each of the reactors described in Section 3, the first step was to estimate the nuclear inventory under
normal operating conditions (i.e., steady-state, accident free operation). For this analysis, the SCALE code
suite (Wieselquist and Lefebvre 2024) was used to develop models and simulations of the representative
designs.
To compute the nuclear inventories, the SCALE TRITON module was used to create models for each rep-
resentative reactor; these models included geometry information and power history. At this stage, features
such as continuous refueling and noble gas/metal removal were added using the FLOW block in the TRI-
TON module. Each reactor was simulated for the core lifetime based on available operational parameters.

4.1 INVENTORY ESTIMATION
The aim for each model was to estimate the steady-state nuclear inventory (i.e., not considering start-up or
shutdown), which provides the best estimation of the isotopes present during normal operation. The nu-
clear inventories for the MSR designs were generally taken at the end of the simulation, which allowed
time for the simulated inventory to converge to equilibrium values. The exception was the thermal spec-
trum HALEU-fueled design discussed in Section 3.2, whose model already included the estimate for the
equilibrium composition. The inventories for the MSRs represent the composition of samples taken from
the main salt loop during normal operation. The SCALE ORIGEN module was used to simulate the com-
position after allowing the salt samples to cool for a fixed amount of time, which represented time needed
for measurement, external processing, and so forth and is further discussed later in this section.
The inventory for the PBR class was slightly more complicated than MSR inventories because the pebble
compositions needed to be combined in a reasonable way. First, the TRITON module was used to gener-
ate inventories for the individual pebbles based on their pathways and number of passes through the core.
Between passes, each pebble was cooled for a fixed amount of time, which represents the time needed for
measurements. After the individual inventories were computed, the SCALE ORIGEN module was used to
mix the compositions. Specifically, compositions from each zone and number of passes were combined,
simulating the average composition in the input hopper for the PBR. In other words, fresh compositions
were mixed with one-pass compositions, two-pass compositions, and so on up to six-pass compositions
using an even mixing strategy.
The fixed amount of time mentioned previously was fixed for each individual simulation. However, several
pebble cooling times were considered to understand how changing this time affected the simulated inven-
tories. Specifically, the times used were as follows:

 • 3s

 • 1h
 • 8h

These times were chosen to fill the design space for PBRs (i.e., cover the range of possible pebble cooling
times between passes). An inventory was computed for each reactor model and cooling time combination.

4.2 EMISSION ESTIMATION
The simulated inventories were then used to predict the gamma ray emissions from a 1 kg sample of the
material. For this step, gamma ray emission data from the International Atomic Energy Agency Nuclear
Data Services database, which has a Python API, were downloaded and used (International Atomic Energy
Association). A Python script was developed to combine the inventory mass fraction for each element with
the corresponding gamma rays and branching ratios. This script produced a list of the expected gamma
rays from the inventory samples along with the intensities and emission energies. Each reactor type and
cooling time combination produced a separate inventory and, therefore, a separate list of gamma ray emis-
sions.
A similar process could be used to estimate the neutron energy emissions; however, neutrons are less use-
ful compared to gamma rays for isotopic identification, which is required by the isotope ratio method.
Therefore, neutron emissions were ignored for this evaluation.

4.3 DETECTOR RESPONSE PREDICTION13
The first step for simulating the response of a hypothetical detector system to the irradiated fuel materi-
als at various ages was to develop the source term. Specifically, the isotopic inventory for each fuel at
each age was converted to a list of gamma ray emissions and their associated intensities, accounting for
each radioisotope as it decays in as a daughter product of other radioisotopes and decays out into its own
daughter. The resultant list of gamma rays is prohibitively long and complex, so two cuts were made to
reduce the complexity of the source. First, because fluorescence x-rays from actinides are expected to
be extremely intense and obscure other signatures at low energies, only gamma rays with energy greater
than 125 keV were considered. Second, because the simulations were limited to approximately 109 par-
ticles/run, gamma rays with emission intensities less than 10−6 as a fraction of the total intensity were re-
jected, such that every gamma ray line had at least 103 simulated particle emissions per simulation.
The resultant list of gamma energies and intensities was then used to create a source definition (SDEF)
card in Monte Carlo N-Particle (MCNP) (Rising et al. 2025). To represent a nominal gamma spectroscopy
measurement for irradiated fuel salt, a Hastelloy pipe was simulated (1 cm internal diameter, 1.5 cm outer
diameter, and 20 cm length); it contained a fuel salt with an elemental composition previously calculated
via burnup simulation. For the PBR fuel scenario, an 8 cm spherical pebble was simulated with a 6 cm in-
ner region containing the tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuel–graphite matrix surrounded by an outer layer
containing only graphite. An ORTEC Detective X high-purity germanium detector was simulated with
its front face at 10 cm from the center of the pipe or pebble. Although this would be too close to place a
detector to irradiated fuel in a real measurement scenario, the simulation results provide a reasonable un-
derstanding of the relative shape of the expected spectrum without adding further complications to the sim-
ulation. If an appropriate detector distance were used, the primary effect on the detector response would
be a lowered count rate, particularly for the low-energy gamma rays. However, a real measurement would
have additional complications, such as shielding material placement. In this case, typical variance reduc-
tion methods do not apply, because gamma rays that scatter in the salt or surrounding environment play an
important role in populating the underlying spectrum continuum.

The MCNPX-PoliMi code was used to simulate and reconstruct the detector response at the individual

with the correct radioisotope using the native isotopic library in InterSpec.
The detector response work produced peak spectra for all simulated materials—that is, spectra for all ma-
terial time and cooling time combinations. Then, the peaks were analyzed to identify which were present
for all cooling times of 8 h or less; these peaks corresponded to the detectable short-lived isotopes. Spec-
tral peaks present in the 90 day and 2 year cooling time spectra corresponded to the detectable long-lived
isotopes. A peak was considered detectable if the ratio of its area to its uncertainty was at least 25.

The analysis methodology worked well for each of the nuclear reactor types considered in this work. The
simulated detector spectra identified several characteristic gamma peaks that could be useful in applying
the isotope ratio method.
Although it is not necessary to target the same isotopes across multiple reactor designs, a few isotopes
were present across all five reactor types considered in this work. The short-lived isotopes meeting this
criterion included 239 Np, 243 Cm, 143 Ce, 140 La, and 135 I. The long-lived isotopes present in all reactor types
included only 144 Pr.
Because the reactors considered here were all fueled with uranium (either 233 U or 235 U), the fission prod-
uct distributions were similar. Thus, the reactors had similar sets of detectable isotopes, but each reactor
had a unique combination of isotopes. While not directly related to the isotope ratio method, this unique
combination of isotopes could be used as a safeguards-relevant signature to verify an MSR is operating
with the stated fuel and flux spectra. For such an application, a reactor would need to be analyzed similarly
to the method presented in this report.

 6. CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
In this work, the isotope ratio method was expanded to identify potentially useful isotopes in various ARs
with continuously flowing nuclear material. Following the approach in this work, multiple short- and long-
lived isotopes can be easily identified for a given reactor design. Depending on the refueling schedule for
a reactor, some of the identified isotopes may be inappropriate for the method because their half-lives are
too close to the system effective half-life. However, all reactor types had several isotopes, presented in the
tables in Section 5, that could work for applying the isotope ratio method as presented. This finding will
likely extend to designs not analyzed in this work especially if the reactor is based on fissioning uranium.
Although this work considered many categories of MSRs, some proposed AR designs fall outside the
scope of those considered in this work. Most notable among these designs are those fueled by MOX or
TRU isotopes instead of uranium. Based on the results presented in this report, we expect that these ad-
ditional designs will each have several detectable isotopes suitable for applying the isotope ratio method
because the fission products will be similar (though in different isotopic combinations compared to the
designs in this work). However, for completeness, analyses like those performed in this work should be
conducted for these additional designs to enable development of tailored detection equipment for these
reactors.
Furthermore, this work considered only irradiated nuclear material present in a reactor environment. The
nuclear material flowing through a fuel fabrication or enrichment plant falls outside this scope. The use of
passive nondestructive assay methods is unlikely to detect unirradiated uranium-based material based on
the isotopes identified in this work, which are all fission fragments. The use of a neutron source to induce
fission could enable the isotope ratio method to be applied within fuel fabrication or enrichment facilities.
However, doing so would require additional analysis, and the list of applicable isotopes is expected to be
significantly different than those presented in this report.
This work successfully identified isotopes suitable for applying the isotope ratio method to broad cate-
gories of reactor designs. The next step is to develop tailored detection systems for these isotopes. These
systems could then be used to help implement accounting and material tracking measures within nuclear
reactors with continuously flowing material.
