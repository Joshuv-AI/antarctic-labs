Decision-theoretic recruitment and validation planning for depression surveys: An illustrative Chinese application

Han Liu

ABSTRACT
Background: Survey planning must balance recruitment losses against uncertainty in screening yield, diagnostic accuracy and survey design. This study examined how these considerations determine invitation requirements and diagnostic-validation allocation. Methods: A Beta planning distribution used 170 PHQ-9-positive results among 1,045 Chinese community participants and an effective sample size of 100. Asymmetric recruitment loss selected conditional and joint-distribution invitation targets. A separate cost-plus-variance Bayes criterion jointly allocated survey clusters and independent reference-positive and reference-negative validation samples. Parameter uncertainty and future-data performance were evaluated by integration and simulation. Results: With an illustrative underplanning-to-overplanning loss ratio of 3, conditional planning selected a screening-positive proportion of 0.1862 and 4,794 screening invitations. Integrating design uncertainty increased this target to 5,346. Current-prevalence planning required 9,247 conditional invitations or 11,866 under joint parameter uncertainty when diagnostic accuracy was known within each scenario. The original validation sample imposed a reference-point half-width floor of 0.0292, exceeding the 0.020 target. Under specified relative costs, the joint allocation used 21,708 invitations and 240/4,538 reference-positive/reference-negative validation participants. Its prior-predictive width attainment was 53.15%, compared with 0.03% when nearly the same budget retained the original validation sample. Increasing the precision valuation fourfold raised attainment to 10,000/10,000 simulations, with 95.31% coverage. Uncorrected specificity shifts produced substantial bias despite larger samples. Conclusions: At k = 3, the loss-based rule selected a screening-positive planning proportion of 0.1862, reducing conditional invitation requirements by 39.38% relative to p = 0.5. Joint survey–validation allocation improved simulated precision at comparable assumed cost. These findings support allocating recruitment and diagnostic-validation resources jointly when planning current-prevalence surveys.

Introduction Huang et al. [ 1 ] reported a weighted 12-month prevalence of major depressive disorder (MDD) of 2.1% (95% CI [1.8%, 2.4%]) among community-dwelling adults aged 18 years or older in the China Mental Health Survey, conducted in 2013–2015. A subsequent analysis documented substantial impairment and low treatment use among people with 12-month depressive disorders [ 2 ]. These findings motivate careful survey planning, but a 12-month disorder estimate does not determine the yield of a screener with a two-week symptom window. The national estimates therefore provide background only; none enters the numerical analysis. For fixed precision, the usual sample-size formula for a proportion contains p ( 1 − p ) [ 3 ]. This term is maximised at p = 0.5 [ 3 ]. Thus, p = 0.5 gives the largest requirement over the full parameter space. The convention is appropriate when defensible prior information is unavailable or full-space protection is required. When expected positivity is low, however, it may increase recruitment without addressing misclassification, clustering or non-response. A lower planning value is defensible only when specified prospectively and evaluated under credible departures from its assumptions. Bayesian sample-size methods have used posterior precision averaged over possible samples [ 4 ]. Binomial-proportion methods include average-coverage, average-length and worst-outcome criteria for highest posterior density intervals [ 5 ]. Generalised average-length and average-coverage criteria extend this framework [ 6 ]. Variance- and risk-based criteria have also been developed for conjugate binomial models [ 7 ]. Mixed Bayesian–likelihood criteria address comparisons of two proportions [ 8 ]. A decision-theoretic criterion instead assigns losses to the consequences of alternative sample sizes [ 9 ]. These approaches distinguish a precision requirement from a decision about the consequences of meeting or missing it. Diagnostic uncertainty adds a variance contribution and an allocation decision. Bayesian prevalence planning has shown that uncertainty in misclassification rates can increase sample-size requirements and prevent a specified precision target from being reached [ 10 ]. Related methods address prevalence and diagnostic-test studies without a gold standard [ 11 ]. Testing strategies have also been compared by their accuracy and total cost [ 12 ]. The present application connects these established ideas to temporally aligned PHQ-9 evidence and clustered recruitment. It distinguishes conditional screening-loss decisions, decisions over the full invitation requirement, and allocation under a stated survey–validation cost-and-precision criterion. The primary aim was to determine how aligned screening evidence and specified losses guide recruitment and diagnostic-validation allocation under uncertain diagnostic accuracy, clustering and response. A secondary aim was to quantify these decisions for two-week PHQ-9 positivity and current MINI-defined depression prevalence in an illustrative Chinese application, including comparisons with matched p = 0.5 designs. Decision-theoretic selection of the planning screening-positive proportion The planning parameter was the future survey’s two-week PHQ-9 screening-positive proportion p s . A Beta distribution with shape parameters α > 0 and β > 0 represented planning uncertainty: p s ~ Beta ( α , β ) . Define T = p s ( 1 − p s ) , the component of binomial variance that depends on the proportion. Let T * ∈ [0, 0.25] be the value used for planning, c o the unit loss assigned to oversampling and c u the unit loss assigned to under-recruitment relative to the target half-width. Both unit losses are strictly positive. The asymmetric absolute loss is L ( T * , T ) = c o ( T * − T ) + + c u ( T − T * ) + , ( x ) + = max ( x , 0 ) . (2) The unrounded invitation requirement, with other inputs fixed, was proportional to T. The planning quantile was obtained by differentiating the expected loss in Equation (2) : for candidate action a, the derivative was (c o + c u )FT(a) − c u , where FT is the cumulative distribution function of T. Setting this derivative to zero gave: q = c u c o + c u = k 1 + k , k = c u c o . (3) The standard asymmetric linear-loss quantile rule was applied to the continuous variance term [ 15 ]. For t ∈ [0, 0.25], r(t) was defined as [1 − √(1 − 4t)]/2, and Fp s denoted the Beta cumulative distribution function. The distribution of T was evaluated as: F T ( t ) = F p s { r ( t ) } + 1 − F p s { 1 − r ( t ) } . (4) Brent’s method solved F T ( T * ) = q . The two roots of T* = p s *(1 − p s *) give the same variance and invitation requirement. The lower root was selected for reporting in this low-positivity application: p s * = 1 − 1 − 4 T * 2 . Discussion The calculations show how recruitment decisions change when uncertainty is integrated into the decision itself. At k = 3, the conditional screening-loss rule produced 4,794 screening invitations, whereas loss on the full requirement produced 5,346 after integrating clustering and response. The corresponding current-prevalence values were 9,247 and 11,866 under oracle diagnostic accuracy. Thus, the 39.38% difference from conditional p = 0.5 calculations describes a fixed-input comparison; it does not quantify the gain from a complete uncertain-input design. The decision-theoretic contribution is the explicit separation of objectives and action spaces. The quantile rule is established decision theory [ 15 ]. Applying it to the entire invitation requirement gives a Bayes recruitment decision under the specified joint distribution. It cannot assign costs to validation, and an infinite requirement with positive prior probability makes its expected shortfall loss infinite. The cost-plus-variance criterion supplies a finite, explicitly defined alternative that allocates survey clusters and both validation strata jointly. Its global optimum is proved for the stated first-order risk, rather than asserted for every design objective. Validation allocation had a larger effect than adding main-survey invitations in the evaluated budget comparison. At relative costs of 45,598 and 45,559 units, joint allocation achieved 53.15% prior-predictive width attainment, whereas retaining the original validation sample achieved 0.03% despite 40,334 invitations. This result follows from the three separate variance components: more survey clusters reduce only the survey component. The reference-point specificity contribution of 97.32% also depends on the validation allocation. With both strata free to vary, the optimum balances marginal precision per cost unit; it does not imply that specificity is universally the only validation priority. The allocation model uses independent external validation, a setting for which adjusted-prevalence intervals have been studied [ 19 ]. Validation nested within the main survey introduces a different sampling problem because phase-two selection enters prevalence estimation [ 20 ]. The budget comparison therefore addresses the external-validation design specified here. A Bayes optimum remains specific to its utility. The base allocation was calibrated to a continuous-design prior mean variance corresponding to d = 0.020, yet its predictive width attainment was 53.15%. Increasing λ to 4λ 0 produced 10,000/10,000 width successes and 95.31% coverage under the stated prior-predictive model. This improvement required approximately twice the assumed total cost. The comparison does not identify an empirically correct λ or k; a probability-of-success requirement should be declared as a separate design constraint when that is the commissioning objective. Diagnostic transportability changes the interpretation of additional precision. When target specificity fell from 0.86 to 0.80 but validation retained source accuracy, the large-precision-value design had zero coverage in 10,000 simulations despite universal width attainment. Local validation at target specificity restored coverage to 94.83% in that design. Published PHQ-9 analyses have also documented sensitivity bias associated with selective cutoff reporting [ 21 ]. These findings support validating the chosen threshold in the intended population before treating a narrower interval as improved inference. S1 Appendix retains the practical decision rules for field implementation. The informative-response simulation identified a separate failure mechanism: width attainment exceeded 99% while coverage fell to 35.30%–53.43%. Increasing sample size did not correct selection into the responding sample. This finding limits the interpretation of recruitment inflation and supports incorporating response mechanisms into survey analysis and subsequent design evaluation. Limitations The empirical basis limits transportability. One Shanghai community sample supplies the screening and validation inputs; discounting its effective sample size does not establish national representativeness. Accuracy against current MINI classification concerns an observed reference standard rather than error-free MDD. The source describes the order of PHQ-9 and MINI administration but does not report their exact elapsed interval. Neither analysed outcome estimates 12-month prevalence. Inconsistencies among the published diagnostic summaries prevent confirmation of an exact validation table. The numerical rounding range cannot provide a sampling confidence interval or quantify between-population accuracy variation. The design priors, cost ratios, k and λ were investigator-specified. No local pilot, stakeholder valuations, local case-ascertainment costs or nationally representative joint parameter data were available. Reference-positive and reference-negative unit costs must include the resources required to obtain eligible validation participants. The joint optimum therefore establishes a conditional design result, not an optimal national recruitment policy. Its prior-dependence and wider-accuracy scenarios examine selected alternatives rather than estimating transportability. The precision calculations depend on normal and first-order delta approximations, which may be inaccurate near parameter boundaries or with few diagnostic cases. The joint allocation fixes 67 invitations per cluster and assumes outcome-independent response and independent external validation conditional on the design parameters. It excludes informative cluster size, complex weights, threshold-selection uncertainty and correlated validation sampling. Dividing by RR adjusts expected recruitment yield and cannot remove response bias. The cluster simulations generated screening counts directly rather than individual paired screening and reference diagnoses; their known-accuracy analyses were separate from the finite-validation simulations. The simulations evaluate selected departures and do not validate national transportability or every complex survey design. Parameter assurance and prior-predictive width attainment remain distinct, and observed zero or universal event counts do not establish probabilities of exactly zero or one. Conclusion At k = 3, the loss-based rule selected a screening-positive planning proportion of 0.1862 and conditional invitation requirements 39.38% below matched p = 0.5 calculations. Integrating parameter uncertainty increased the screening and current-prevalence invitation targets to 5,346 and 11,866, respectively, with diagnostic accuracy fixed within each current-prevalence scenario. Joint survey–validation allocation increased prior-predictive width attainment from 0.03% to 53.15% at nearly equal assumed cost, and local diagnostic recalibration restored approximately 95% coverage after a specificity shift. Together, these findings show that aligned screening evidence and recruitment losses determine the planning target, while validation allocation and diagnostic calibration shape the precision and validity of current-prevalence estimates.
