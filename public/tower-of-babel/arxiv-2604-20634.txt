Distributional Statistical Models: Weak Moments, Cumulants, and a Central Limit Theorem
R. Labouriau

Abstract
Many important statistical models fall outside classical moment-based methods due to the non-existence of moments or moment generating functions. We propose a generalised probabilistic framework in which the classical requirement of a density with respect to a dominating measure is replaced by a pair ( T , φ ) (T,\varphi) , where T ∈ 𝒮 ′ ​ ( ℝ ) T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}) is a tempered distribution and φ ∈ 𝒮 ⁡ ( ℝ ) \varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}) is a Schwartz kernel. Expectations are defined via the action of distributions on regularised test functions, yielding well-defined weak moments , weak characteristic functions , and weak cumulants of all orders. These extend classical quantities and retain key algebraic properties such as additivity under independence and natural affine transformation rules. Despite dispensing with classical densities, the framework recovers ordinary event probabilities: the weak expectation determines a kernel-weighted distribution function, and under the uniqueness conditions developed here, the classical distribution function is fully recovered. The main results are: (i) a systematic algebra of weak cumulants; (ii) a weak moment problem where existence of all moments holds unconditionally and uniqueness depends on the kernel, with uniqueness results under Gaussian kernels (via Hermite completeness, including a multivariate extension to ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} ), positive Schwartz kernels with square-integrable densities (via a Carleman-type criterion), and kernels with exponential decay (via Denjoy–Carleman quasi-analyticity); (iii) a weak central limit theorem formulated as convergence of weak characteristic functions to a Gaussian limit, covering cases where the classical theorem fails; and (iv) a distributional CLT showing that, in the density case, the kernel-weighted measures converge in distribution to the standard normal—thereby bridging weak transform convergence and classical convergence in distribution. The framework is illustrated with Student’s t t , stable, and hyperbolic distributions. As a statistical consequence, the weak first moment yields a consistent estimator of the location parameter in the Cauchy model, where no classical moment-based estimator exists. A full statistical treatment is given in a companion paper [ 6 ] .



1
Introduction

Classical statistical theory is largely built upon probability measures that admit densities with respect to a reference measure. In this setting, expectations, moments, cumulants, and transform-based methods are defined via integration against such densities
[
1
,
16
]
. However, many important models—heavy-tailed distributions such as the Cauchy distribution, low-degree Student
t
distributions, and
\alpha
-stable laws—may fail to possess moments of sufficiently high order, thereby limiting the applicability of moment-based methods
[
3
,
14
]
.

We propose a framework in which densities are replaced by pairs
(T,\varphi)
where
T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
is a tempered distribution and
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
is a Schwartz kernel. Expectations are defined via the pairing
\mathbb{E}_{T,\varphi}[\psi]=\langle T,\psi\varphi\rangle
, and the closure of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
under multiplication by polynomials immediately gives that
weak moments
of all orders exist for every pair
(T,\varphi)
. When the ordinary moments of the underlying distribution exist, they coincide with the corresponding weak moments up to the kernel factor; when they do not (as in the Cauchy case), the weak moments are still well defined.

The classical approach to statistical inference rests on the existence of a probability density with respect to a dominating measure: one writes down a likelihood, derives moments and cumulants from the density, and builds estimators and tests on these quantities. When moments do not exist or the density is not available in closed form, the classical machinery breaks down. The framework proposed here represents a genuine change of paradigm: it replaces the density–dominating-measure pair with a distribution–kernel pair
(T,\varphi)
, and it replaces ordinary expectations with the distributional pairing
\langle T,\psi\varphi\rangle
. The crucial point is that
this substitution does not sacrifice probabilistic content
. In Section
2.1
we show that the weak expectation determines a kernel-weighted distribution function by approximating indicator functions with Schwartz functions (Proposition
2.9
), and that the ordinary distribution function is recovered whenever the underlying density is identifiable from the weak representation (Corollary
2.10
). The weak framework thus supports the same kinds of probabilistic statements—event probabilities, quantiles, confidence intervals—and even allows statistical inference, just as classical density-based methods do, while extending seamlessly to models where no classical moments exist or densities are not available. Moreover, in the classical density setting, the framework admits an explicit reconstruction interpretation: weak data determine a regularised version of the density, from which the original density can be recovered by stable inverse methods (see Appendix
C
).

The paper develops five blocks of results:

(i)

Algebra of weak cumulants
(Sections
3
–
4
): we show that weak characteristic functions factorise under independence, that weak cumulants are additive, and that affine transformation rules carry over from the classical theory.

(ii)

The weak moment problem
(Section
6
): existence of weak moments of all orders is automatic; uniqueness depends on the kernel. We establish three levels of uniqueness: (a) for any positive Schwartz kernel, among square-integrable densities via Carleman’s condition; (b) for kernels with exponential-type decay, in all of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
via the Denjoy–Carleman theorem; (c) for Gaussian kernels, via the completeness of Hermite functions in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
. The Gaussian uniqueness theorem is extended to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
using the multivariate Hermite basis.

(iii)

Weak central limit theorem
(Section
7
): we prove convergence of the weak characteristic function of normalised sums to a Gaussian limit, together with a quantitative (Berry–Esseen type) refinement.

(iv)

Distributional CLT
(Section
7.5
): in the density case, the weak CLT is shown to imply classical convergence in distribution of the kernel-weighted measures to the standard normal. This bridges the weak framework and classical probability, and connects to the Tikhonov reconstruction of Appendix
C
.

(v)

A statistical consequence
(Section
8
): a short illustration showing that even a single weak moment yields a consistent estimator for the location parameter of a Cauchy distribution, where no classical moment-based estimator exists.

Section
2
introduces the framework, basic definitions, and the recovery of event probabilities from weak expectations. Section
3
develops independence and additivity. Section
4
studies weak transforms and cumulants. Section
5
presents illustrative examples. Section
6
is the centrepiece: the weak moment problem and its uniqueness theorems. Section

establishes the weak CLT, its quantitative refinement, and the distributional CLT for kernel-weighted measures. Section

gives the statistical illustration. Section

presents a technical estimate used in the proof of Theorem

. Additional proofs omitted from the main text for brevity are collected in Appendix

. Appendix

Notation.

Throughout the paper, weak objects carry a superscript
\varphi
denoting the kernel in play:
{}^{(\varphi)}m_{n}
for weak moments,
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)
for the weak characteristic function,

2
Preliminaries and generalised probability framework

2.1
Distribution–kernel pairs and weak expectation

Let
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the Schwartz space of rapidly decreasing smooth functions, and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
its dual, the space of tempered distributions. We work primarily in the tempered setting, since it is stable under Fourier transform and accommodates the weak characteristic functions introduced below. The closure of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
under multiplication by polynomials—if
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
p
is a polynomial, then
p\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
—underlies the existence of weak moments of all orders.

Definition 2.1

(Distribution–kernel pair)
.

A
distribution–kernel pair
is a tuple
(T,\varphi)
with
T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The distribution
T
plays the role of a generalised density; the kernel
\varphi
regularises the pairing.

Remark 2.2
.

The definitions are stated for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to establish generality, but the detailed development is carried out for
d=1
. The multivariate extension is straightforward for the basic constructions; a systematic treatment is left for future work.

Given
(T,\varphi)
, define the
class of admissible functions
by
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}:=\{\psi:\psi\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
. All polynomials and all functions
x\mapsto e^{itx}
belong to
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
.

Definition 2.3

(Generalised expectation)
.

For
(T,\varphi)
and
\psi\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
, the
weak expectation
is

\mathbb{E}_{T,\varphi}[\psi]\;:=\;\langle T,\,\psi\varphi\rangle.

Definition 2.4

(Generalised probability measure)
.

A pair
(T,\varphi)
defines a
generalised probability measure
if
\langle T,\varphi\rangle=1
(normalisation) and
\langle T,\psi\varphi\rangle\geq 0
for all non-negative
\psi\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
(positivity).

Remark 2.5
.

The positivity condition is strong and essentially forces
T
to be a positive measure. For pairs
(T_{f},\varphi)
with
f\geq 0
a.e. and
\varphi>0
, it is automatic. However, the technical results of this paper—weak moments, characteristic functions, additivity, the CLT, and the uniqueness theorems—depend only on the normalisation condition and the structure of the pair, not on positivity. Positivity is thus a modelling axiom that restricts attention to pairs with a probabilistic interpretation.

This point of view leads naturally to a generalised notion of random variable.

Definition 2.6

(Generalised random variable)
.

A generalised random variable is an abstract object
X
whose distribution
is specified by a distribution–kernel pair
(T,\varphi)
, with

T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,
and whose expectations are defined by

E[X;\psi]:=E_{T,\varphi}[\psi]=\langle T,\psi\varphi\rangle,

for all admissible test functions
\psi
.

Remark 2.7
.

In this formulation, a generalised random variable is not defined as a
measurable function on an underlying probability space, but rather through
its expectation functional. This extends the classical notion of random
variable while retaining its probabilistic content. In the density case,

T=T_{f}
, this reduces to

E[X;\psi]=\int\psi(x)\varphi(x)f(x)\,dx,

which coincides with classical expectation in the limit
\varphi\to 1
.

For a classical density
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with associated distribution
T_{f}
, the weak expectation reduces to

\mathbb{E}_{T_{f},\varphi}[\psi]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\psi(x)\varphi(x)\,dx,

which recovers the ordinary expectation formally in the limit
\varphi\to 1
.

Remark 2.8
.

The construction
\mathbb{E}_{T,\varphi}[\psi]=\langle T,\psi\varphi\rangle
is related to, but distinct from, classical mollification
T*\rho_{\varepsilon}
. Mollification acts by
convolution
(global averaging), whereas the present framework acts by
pointwise multiplication
by the kernel
\varphi
, localising information to the region where
\varphi
is appreciably nonzero. This distinction becomes important in the reconstruction problem, where the forward operator is a multiplication rather than a convolution.

A natural question arises at this point: if expectations are defined via the distributional pairing
\langle T,\psi\varphi\rangle
rather than via integration against a density, can one still recover ordinary event probabilities? The answer is affirmative: the weak expectation determines a kernel-weighted distribution function, and under the uniqueness conditions of Section
6
, the classical distribution function is fully recovered.

Proposition 2.9

(Recovery of weighted distribution functions)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be a nonnegative density, let
T_{f}
be the induced
tempered distribution, and let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
with

\varphi\geq 0
. For each
a\in\mathbb{R}
, define

F_{\varphi}(a):=\int_{-\infty}^{a}f(x)\varphi(x)\,dx.

Then
F_{\varphi}(a)
is determined by the weak expectation

\mathbb{E}_{T_{f},\varphi}
in the following sense: if
(\psi_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies

0\leq\psi_{n}\leq 1,\qquad\psi_{n}(x)\to\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,a)}(x)\quad\text{pointwise},

then

\mathbb{E}_{T_{f},\varphi}[\psi_{n}]\longrightarrow F_{\varphi}(a).

In particular, the weak expectation determines the kernel-weighted cumulative
distribution function
F_{\varphi}
.

Proof.

Since
T_{f}
is induced by the density
f
, the weak expectation has the
integral form

\mathbb{E}_{T_{f},\varphi}[\psi_{n}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi_{n}(x)\varphi(x)f(x)\,dx.

By assumption,
0\leq\psi_{n}\leq 1
and
\psi_{n}(x)\to\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,a)}(x)

pointwise. Hence

0\leq\psi_{n}(x)\varphi(x)f(x)\leq\varphi(x)f(x),

and the dominating function
\varphi f
is integrable because

\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
is bounded and
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Therefore, by the dominated convergence theorem,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{T_{f},\varphi}[\psi_{n}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,a)}(x)\varphi(x)f(x)\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^{a}f(x)\varphi(x)\,dx=F_{\varphi}(a).

∎

Corollary 2.10

(Recovery of the classical distribution function)
.

Assume that the kernel
\varphi
is fixed and that the weak data
determine
f
uniquely—for example, under the uniqueness conditions developed
in Section
6
. Then the ordinary cumulative distribution function

F(a)=\int_{-\infty}^{a}f(x)\,dx

is recovered from the weak representation.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.9
, the weak expectation
determines
F_{\varphi}(a)=\int_{-\infty}^{a}f(x)\varphi(x)\,dx
for every

a
. The non-trivial step is the uniqueness of
f
from
F_{\varphi}
: under the
assumptions of Section
6
, the weak representation
determines
f
uniquely. Once
f
is known, the ordinary cumulative
distribution function
F(a)=\int_{-\infty}^{a}f(x)\,dx
is obtained by direct integration.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

Proposition
2.9
shows that the weak expectation
determines event probabilities only indirectly, through approximation by
admissible smooth functions. What is obtained directly is the
kernel-weighted distribution function
F_{\varphi}
. The ordinary
distribution function is then recovered whenever the underlying density is
identifiable from the weak representation. Thus, despite the absence of a classical density in the general setting, the weak framework does not sacrifice probabilistic content: it supports the same kinds of probabilistic statements—event probabilities, quantiles, tail probabilities—as classical density-based inference.

Remark 2.12

(Reconstruction from weak data)
.

In the classical density setting, the weak representation determines the
product
g(x)=\varphi(x)f(x)
. Recovering
f
from
g
is an inverse problem,
which is in general ill-posed due to the decay of
\varphi
at infinity.
However, stable reconstruction can be achieved by standard regularisation
methods. In Appendix
C
, we show that Tikhonov
regularisation yields a well-posed recovery procedure in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, with
explicit error bounds under noise.

2.2
Weak moments, transforms, and cumulants

Definition 2.13

(Weak moments)
.

For
n\geq 0
, the
n
-th
weak moment
is

{}^{(\varphi)}m_{n}\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{T,\varphi}[x^{n}]\;=\;\langle T,\,x^{n}\varphi(x)\rangle.

Proposition 2.14
.

All weak moments are well defined:
{}^{(\varphi)}m_{n}
exists for every
n\geq 0
and every pair
(T,\varphi)
.

Proof.

Since
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{S}
is closed under multiplication by polynomials, the product
x^{n}\varphi(x)
belongs to
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. As
T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the pairing
\langle T,x^{n}\varphi(x)\rangle
is well defined for every
n
.
∎

Remark 2.15
.

For a classical density
f
and pair
(T_{f},\varphi)
, the weak moment is
{}^{(\varphi)}m_{n}=\int x^{n}f(x)\varphi(x)\,dx
, which differs from the classical moment
m_{n}=\int x^{n}f(x)\,dx
unless
\varphi\equiv 1
. In the limit
\varphi\to 1
, the two coincide whenever
m_{n}
exists. For a general kernel,
{}^{(\varphi)}m_{n}
is a
regularised
version of
m_{n}
.

Definition 2.16

(Weak characteristic function)
.

The
weak characteristic function
of a generalised probability measure
(T,\varphi)
on
\mathbb{R}
is

{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{T,\varphi}[e^{itx}]\;=\;\langle T,\,e^{itx}\varphi(x)\rangle,\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Definition 2.17

(Weak cumulant generating function and weak cumulants)
.

Assume
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)\neq 0
in a neighbourhood of
t=0
. The
weak cumulant generating function
is
{}^{(\varphi)}K(t):=\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)
, and the
n
-th
weak cumulant
is

{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{n}\;:=\;\left.\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}{}^{(\varphi)}K(t)\right|_{t=0}.

Remark 2.18
.

Weak moments, weak characteristic functions, and weak cumulants all depend on the kernel
\varphi
, as the superscript notation makes explicit. They are properties of the pair
(T,\varphi)
, not of
T
alone. The kernel should therefore be viewed as part of the model specification.

2.3
The running Student’s
t
example

As a guiding example throughout the paper, we use the Student’s
t_{\nu}
family with density
f_{\nu}(x)=c_{\nu}(1+x^{2}/\nu)^{-(\nu+1)/2}
. Classically,
\mathbb{E}[|X|^{k}]<\infty
iff
k<\nu
; for
\nu=1
(Cauchy), no non-trivial ordinary moment exists.

For any
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, the pair
(T_{\nu},\varphi)
defines a generalised probability measure, and the weak moments
{}^{(\varphi)}m_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{T_{\nu},\varphi}[x^{k}]
are well defined for all
k\geq 0
, regardless of
\nu
. We shall illustrate the main results with this family as we proceed.

3
Independence and additivity of weak cumulants

3.1
Product representations and independence

Let
(T_{1},\varphi_{1})
and
(T_{2},\varphi_{2})
be generalised probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
. Their
product
is the pair
(T_{1}\otimes T_{2},\,\varphi_{1}\otimes\varphi_{2})
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
\otimes
denotes the tensor product.

Definition 3.1

(Independence)
.

Two generalised random variables represented by
(T_{1},\varphi_{1})
and
(T_{2},\varphi_{2})
are
independent
if their joint representation is the product pair
(T_{1}\otimes T_{2},\,\varphi_{1}\otimes\varphi_{2})
.

Remark 3.2
.

Independence depends on the chosen representation, since the kernel is part of the model specification. This is analogous to the role of a
\sigma
-algebra in conditional independence. In practice, the kernel is fixed at the modelling stage, and independence is well defined relative to that choice.

3.2
Weak characteristic functions of sums

Let
X
and
Y
be independent generalised random variables with pairs
(T_{1},\varphi_{1})
and
(T_{2},\varphi_{2})
, and set
Z=X+Y
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The weak characteristic function factorises:

{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z}(t)={}^{(\varphi_{1})}\phi_{1}(t)\,{}^{(\varphi_{2})}\phi_{2}(t).

Proof.

By the defining property of the tensor product,

\displaystyle{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle T_{1}\otimes T_{2},\,e^{it(x+y)}\varphi_{1}(x)\varphi_{2}(y)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle T_{1},\,e^{itx}\varphi_{1}(x)\rangle\;\langle T_{2},\,e^{ity}\varphi_{2}(y)\rangle={}^{(\varphi_{1})}\phi_{1}(t)\;{}^{(\varphi_{2})}\phi_{2}(t).

∎

3.3
Additivity of weak cumulants

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
X
and
Y
be independent generalised random variables. Then
{}^{(\varphi)}K_{X+Y}(t)={}^{(\varphi_{1})}K_{X}(t)+{}^{(\varphi_{2})}K_{Y}(t)
, whenever the logarithms are defined.

Proof.

From Proposition
3.3
,
\phi_{X+Y}(t)=\phi_{X}(t)\phi_{Y}(t)
; taking logarithms yields the result.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

If
{}^{(\varphi_{1})}\phi_{X}(0)\neq 0
and
{}^{(\varphi_{2})}\phi_{Y}(0)\neq 0
, then the weak cumulants are additive:
{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{n}(X+Y)={}^{(\varphi_{1})}\kappa_{n}(X)+{}^{(\varphi_{2})}\kappa_{n}(Y)
for all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.
See Appendix
B
.

3.4
Affine transformations

Proposition 3.6

(Translation)
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}
and define the translated pair
(T_{a},\varphi_{a})
by
\langle T_{a},\psi\rangle:=\langle T,\psi(\cdot+a)\rangle
and
\varphi_{a}(x):=\varphi(x-a)
. Then
{}^{(\varphi_{a})}\phi_{a}(t)=e^{ita}{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)
, and

{}^{(\varphi_{a})}\kappa_{1}={}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}+a,\qquad{}^{(\varphi_{a})}\kappa_{n}={}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{n}\quad(n\geq 2).

Proof.
See Appendix
B
.

Proposition 3.7

(Scaling)
.

Let
b\neq 0
and define
(T_{b},\varphi_{b})
by
\mathbb{E}_{T_{b},\varphi_{b}}[\psi]:=\mathbb{E}_{T,\varphi}[\psi(b\,\cdot)]
and
\varphi_{b}(x):=\varphi(bx)
. Then
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{b}(t)={}^{(\varphi)}\phi(bt)
and
{}^{(\varphi_{b})}\kappa_{n}=b^{n}{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{n}
.

Proof.
See Appendix
B
.

4
Weak transforms and weak cumulants

4.1
Smoothness of weak characteristic functions

Proposition 4.1
.

The weak characteristic function
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)
is infinitely differentiable in
t
, and for each
n\geq 0
,

({}^{(\varphi)}\phi)^{(n)}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{T,\varphi}[(ix)^{n}e^{itx}]=\langle T,(ix)^{n}e^{itx}\varphi(x)\rangle.

Proof.
See Appendix
B
.

Corollary 4.2
.

The derivatives at the origin encode the weak moments:

({}^{(\varphi)}\phi)^{(n)}(0)=i^{n}\,{}^{(\varphi)}m_{n}
.

Proof.
See Appendix
B
.

Remark 4.3
.

Thus
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi
is smooth and encodes all weak moments, even when the classical characteristic function is not differentiable at the origin (as for stable laws with
\alpha<2
).

4.2
Existence of weak cumulants and formal expansion

Proposition 4.4
.

If all weak moments exist and
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(0)\neq 0
, then all weak cumulants
{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{n}
exist.

Proof.

Since
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi
is smooth and non-vanishing near
0
, the function
\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi
is smooth. Its derivatives at the origin are finite combinations of weak moments.
∎

Whenever
{}^{(\varphi)}K(t)
is sufficiently regular at
t=0
, it admits the formal expansion

{}^{(\varphi)}K(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{n}\frac{(it)^{n}}{n!},\qquad{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)=\exp\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{n}\frac{(it)^{n}}{n!}\right\}.

This should be interpreted as a formal power series unless analyticity of
{}^{(\varphi)}K
near the origin is established. The second-order truncation,

\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(u)=i{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}u-\frac{{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}{2}u^{2}+o(u^{2}),\qquad u\to 0,

follows from smoothness and
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(0)=1
, and is the key ingredient in the proof of the weak CLT (Section
7
).

4.3
Relation to classical transforms

For a classical density
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and kernel
\varphi
approximating the constant function
1
,

{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}f(x)\varphi(x)\,dx\;\approx\;\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}f(x)\,dx,

recovering the classical characteristic function. Thus
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi
is a regularised version of the classical one, and weak cumulants converge to classical cumulants (when they exist) as
\varphi\to 1
.

5
Examples

5.1
Student’s
t
distributions: classical failure and weak recovery

The Student
t_{\nu}
family provides the canonical illustration: classically, no moment of order
\geq\nu
exists. In the weak framework, all moments
{}^{(\varphi)}m_{k}
exist for every
k
and every
\nu
.

For the standard Cauchy distribution (
\nu=1
) with Gaussian kernel
\varphi(x)=e^{-x^{2}/2}
, the weak moments are

{}^{(\varphi)}m_{n}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{\pi(1+x^{2})}\,e^{-x^{2}/2}\,dx.

All odd weak moments vanish by symmetry. The zeroth moment admits the closed form
{}^{(\varphi)}m_{0}=e^{1/2}\operatorname{erfc}(1/\sqrt{2})\approx 0.523
, and the second is
{}^{(\varphi)}m_{2}=\sqrt{2/\pi}-{}^{(\varphi)}m_{0}\approx 0.275
. These values are finite and explicitly computable despite the classical second moment not existing.

Remark 5.1
.

The normalisation
{}^{(\varphi)}m_{0}\neq 1
reflects the fact that
\varphi\neq 1
; one may work with the normalised pair
(T_{1},\varphi/{}^{(\varphi)}m_{0})
if desired. The numerical values of the weak moments depend on the kernel bandwidth, illustrating the regularisation-dependence emphasised throughout this paper.

5.2
Stable distributions and regularisation of transforms

Let
X
be an
\alpha
-stable random variable with
0<\alpha<2
. Moments of order
\geq\alpha
do not exist classically, and the classical characteristic function
\exp(i\mu t-c|t|^{\alpha})
is not infinitely differentiable at
t=0
when
\alpha
is not an even integer.

In the weak framework,
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)=\langle T,e^{itx}\varphi(x)\rangle
is smooth in
t
by construction, and all weak cumulants
{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{n}
exist. The kernel
\varphi
acts as a regularising factor, producing a smoothed transform from which derivatives at the origin can be computed. This provides a consistent way to extend cumulant-based methods to stable distributions.

5.3
Hyperbolic models

The normal inverse Gaussian distribution
\mathrm{NIG}(\alpha,\beta,\mu,\delta)
has density involving a modified Bessel function
K_{1}
and tails decaying as
|x|^{-3/2}\exp(-\alpha|x|)
—faster than polynomial but slower than Gaussian. In the classical setting, all moments exist. In the weak framework with a Gaussian kernel, the product
f(x)\varphi(x)
is super-exponentially integrable, weak moments converge rapidly, and the weak characteristic function has super-exponential decay in
t
, providing improved numerical stability for transform-based inference. As
\varphi\to 1
, the weak cumulants converge to their classical counterparts. More generally, the generalised hyperbolic family is accommodated by the same device.

Remark 5.2
.

The examples above span three regimes:
complete failure
of classical moments (Cauchy, general stable),
partial failure
(Student
t_{\nu}
with moderate
\nu
), and
no failure but improved numerics
(NIG, generalised hyperbolic). Further examples—elliptically contoured distributions and exponential dispersion models—fit naturally into the framework; we omit the details for brevity, noting only that the multivariate case requires tensor weak moments
m^{(\varphi)}\alpha=\langle T,x^{\alpha}\varphi(x)\rangle
indexed by multi-indices.

6
The weak moment problem

This section contains the main theoretical results of the paper. We show that the weak moment problem has a fundamentally different character from its classical counterpart: existence is unconditional, and uniqueness is governed entirely by the kernel. We establish a hierarchy of three uniqueness theorems—for positive Schwartz kernels via Carleman’s condition, for exponential-decay kernels via Denjoy–Carleman quasi-analyticity, and for Gaussian kernels via completeness of Hermite functions—and show that positivity of the kernel is both sufficient and necessary for uniqueness.

6.1
Formulation: existence vs uniqueness

Let
(T,\varphi)
be a probability pair with weak moments
{}^{(\varphi)}m_{n}=\langle T,x^{n}\varphi(x)\rangle
for
n\geq 0
. The
weak moment problem
asks: given a sequence
\{m_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, does there exist a pair
(T,\varphi)
with
{}^{(\varphi)}m_{n}=m_{n}
for all
n
? And if so, is the pair uniquely determined?

In contrast with the classical Hamburger moment problem
[
15
]
, existence in the weak setting is automatic: for any
T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
and any
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, all weak moments
{}^{(\varphi)}m_{n}
exist (Proposition
2.14
). Thus the weak moment problem shifts the focus entirely to
uniqueness
: can two different distributions
T_{1}\neq T_{2}
produce identical weak moment sequences with respect to the same kernel?

In general, uniqueness fails: both the distributional component and the kernel contribute to the moment sequence, and different pairs may produce the same moments. If, however, the kernel is
fixed a priori
, the question reduces to identifying
T
from the sequence
\{\langle T,x^{n}\varphi(x)\rangle\}_{n\geq 0}
. This is possible if and only if the family
\{x^{n}\varphi(x):n\geq 0\}
is
total
(i.e., has dense span) in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
.

The remainder of this section establishes totality, and hence uniqueness, under three progressively general conditions on the kernel.

6.2
Uniqueness for Gaussian kernels

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
be such that
\{x^{n}\varphi(x):n\geq 0\}
is total in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
. If
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
\langle T_{1},x^{n}\varphi\rangle=\langle T_{2},x^{n}\varphi\rangle
for all
n\geq 0
, then
T_{1}=T_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
S=T_{1}-T_{2}
. By assumption,
\langle S,x^{n}\varphi\rangle=0
for all
n
. Since
S
is continuous on
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
and vanishes on a dense subspace,
S=0
.
∎

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\varphi(x)=c\,e^{-ax^{2}}
with
c>0
and
a>0
. Then
\{x^{n}\varphi(x):n\geq 0\}
is total in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
. Consequently, for a fixed Gaussian kernel, the weak moment sequence
\{{}^{(\varphi)}m_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
uniquely determines
T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

By rescaling it suffices to treat
\varphi(x)=e^{-x^{2}/2}
. The Hermite functions
h_{n}(x)=H_{n}(x)\,e^{-x^{2}/2}
(
H_{n}
the physicist’s Hermite polynomial) form a Schauder basis for
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
: every
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
admits a unique expansion
\psi=\sum_{n}c_{n}h_{n}
converging in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
(see
[
11
]
; the result goes back to
[
13
]
). Since
H_{n}
is a polynomial of degree
n
with nonzero leading coefficient,

\operatorname{span}\{x^{k}e^{-x^{2}/2}:0\leq k\leq n\}=\operatorname{span}\{h_{0},\ldots,h_{n}\}

for every
n
. Hence
\operatorname{span}\{x^{n}e^{-x^{2}/2}:n\geq 0\}=\operatorname{span}\{h_{n}:n\geq 0\}
, which is dense in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
. The conclusion follows from Proposition
6.1
.
∎

6.3
Uniqueness for positive Schwartz kernels

The Gaussian case gives uniqueness in

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
. For statistical applications—where distributions
are typically induced by densities—a more general result holds.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
with
\varphi(x)>0
for all
x
, and let

f_{1},f_{2}
be measurable functions with

f_{1}-f_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\varphi(x)\,dx)
. If

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{n}f_{1}(x)\varphi(x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{n}f_{2}(x)\varphi(x)\,dx\qquad\text{for all }n\geq 0,

then
f_{1}=f_{2}
almost everywhere.

Proof.

Set
g=f_{1}-f_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\varphi\,dx)
. Then
\int x^{n}g(x)\varphi(x)\,dx=0
for all
n
. We show polynomials are dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\varphi\,dx)
via Carleman’s condition
[
15
]
:

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{2n}^{-1/(2n)}=\infty,\qquad\mu_{2n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2n}\varphi(x)\,dx.

(1)

Since
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
with
\varphi(x)\leq C_{\varepsilon}e^{-\varepsilon|x|}
. Therefore
\mu_{2n}\leq 2C_{\varepsilon}(2n)!/\varepsilon^{2n+1}
. By Stirling,
(2n)!^{1/(2n)}\sim 2n/e
, so
\mu_{2n}^{1/(2n)}=O(n)
and
\sum\mu_{2n}^{-1/(2n)}\geq c\sum n^{-1}=\infty
. Thus Carleman’s condition holds: polynomials are dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\varphi\,dx)
. Since
g
is orthogonal to every polynomial,
g=0
a.e.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

A notable feature: Carleman’s condition holds
automatically
for every positive
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, because Schwartz functions decay faster than any exponential. No special growth condition on
\varphi
is needed. The condition
f_{1}-f_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\varphi\,dx)
is mild: since
\varphi
is bounded, any
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
function qualifies, covering virtually all standard parametric families.

6.4
Uniqueness via Denjoy–Carleman theory

Theorem
6.3
gives uniqueness among square-integrable densities. Can uniqueness be extended to all of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
for kernels beyond the Gaussian? The answer is yes, under a decay condition on the kernel, via quasi-analytic classes.

Definition 6.5
.

Let
\beta\geq 1
. A function
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies an
exponential-type decay condition of order
\beta
if there exist
A>0
and, for each
m\geq 0
, a constant
C_{m}>0
such that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,(1+|x|)^{k}\,|\varphi^{(m)}(x)|\leq C_{m}\,A^{k}\,(k!)^{1/\beta}\qquad\text{for all }k,m\geq 0.

(2)

This is a Schwartz-space analogue of the Gevrey class
G^{\beta}(\mathbb{R})
.
Gaussian kernels satisfy it with
\beta=2
; more generally, any
super-Gaussian kernel
\varphi(x)=c\,e^{-a|x|^{\alpha}}
with

\alpha>1
satisfies it with
\beta=\alpha/(\alpha-1)
.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
with
\varphi(x)>0
for all
x
,
satisfying the exponential-type decay condition of order
\beta\geq 1
. If

T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy

\langle T_{1},x^{n}\varphi\rangle=\langle T_{2},x^{n}\varphi\rangle

for all
n\geq 0
, then
T_{1}=T_{2}
.

Proof.

Set
S=T_{1}-T_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, so that
\langle S,x^{n}\varphi\rangle=0
for all
n
. The proof proceeds in three steps.

Step 1: Derivative bounds for the weak characteristic function.

Define
\psi(t)=\langle S,e^{itx}\varphi(x)\rangle
. Since
e^{itx}\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
for every
t
and the map
t\mapsto e^{itx}\varphi(x)
is smooth into
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, the function
\psi
is smooth with

\psi^{(n)}(t)=\langle S,(ix)^{n}e^{itx}\varphi(x)\rangle.

In particular,
\psi^{(n)}(0)=i^{n}\langle S,x^{n}\varphi\rangle=0
for all
n
.

Since
S\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, there exist
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
C^{\prime}>0
such that
|\langle S,f\rangle|\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{|\alpha|+|\gamma|\leq N}\sup_{x}|x^{\alpha}f^{(\gamma)}(x)|
for all
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
. Applying this to
(ix)^{n}e^{itx}\varphi(x)
and using the decay condition (
2
) via a Leibniz-rule argument (see Appendix
A
for the detailed estimate), one obtains: for each
R>0
there exist
B,C^{\prime\prime}>0
such that

|\psi^{(n)}(t)|\leq C^{\prime\prime}(n+1)^{N}B^{n}(n!)^{1/\beta}\qquad\text{for all }|t|\leq R,\;n\geq 0.

(3)

Step 2: Quasi-analyticity via the Denjoy–Carleman theorem.

Define
M_{n}=B^{n}(n!)^{1/\beta}
. The polynomial prefactor
(n+1)^{N}
in (
3
) can be absorbed into a modified sequence
M_{n}^{\prime}=(n+1)^{N}M_{n}
without affecting the divergence of
\sum(M_{n}^{\prime})^{-1/n}
, since
(n+1)^{N/n}\to 1
. We verify the Denjoy–Carleman condition
[
5
, Thm. 1.3.8]
:

M_{n}^{-1/n}=B^{-1}(n!)^{-1/(\beta n)}.

By Stirling,
(n!)^{1/n}\sim n/e
, so
M_{n}^{-1/n}\sim(B^{-1}e^{1/\beta})n^{-1/\beta}
. Since
\beta\geq 1
, the series
\sum n^{-1/\beta}
diverges, so
\sum M_{n}^{-1/n}=\infty
and
C\{M_{n}\}
is quasi-analytic.

Since all derivatives of
\psi
vanish at
t=0
and
\psi\in C\{M_{n}\}
on
[-R,R]
, quasi-analyticity gives
\psi\equiv 0
on
[-R,R]
. As
R
is arbitrary,
\psi(t)=0
for all
t
.

Step 3: From
\psi\equiv 0
to
S=0
.

We have
\langle S,e^{itx}\varphi(x)\rangle=0
for all
t
, so the Fourier transform of
S\varphi
vanishes identically. By injectivity of the Fourier transform on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
,
S\varphi=0
. It remains to show
S\varphi=0\Rightarrow S=0
. For any
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})
, the quotient
\psi/\varphi
is smooth with compact support (since
\varphi>0
), so
\psi/\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
. Then
\langle S,\psi\rangle=\langle S,\varphi(\psi/\varphi)\rangle=\langle S\varphi,\psi/\varphi\rangle=0
. Since
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})
is dense in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
and
S
is continuous,
S=0
.
∎

6.5
Hierarchy of uniqueness results

Remark 6.7

(Hierarchy)
.

Theorems
6.3
,
6.6
, and
6.2
establish three levels of uniqueness for the weak moment problem:

1.

Any positive Schwartz kernel
(Thm.
6.3
):
the weak moments determine the density uniquely among square-integrable functions. This is sufficient for most parametric statistical models.

2.

Exponential-type decay with
\beta\geq 1
(Thm.
6.6
):
uniqueness in all of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, including singular distributions. The proof uses the Denjoy–Carleman theorem and is sharp in
\beta
(see below).

3.

Gaussian kernel
(Thm.
6.2
):
uniqueness in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
via the completeness of Hermite functions—a special case of (2) with
\beta=2
, but with a self-contained proof that illuminates the algebraic structure.

In all three cases, positivity of the kernel (
\varphi>0
everywhere) is essential.

Remark 6.8

(Sharpness and obstruction by zeros)
.

The condition
\beta\geq 1
is sharp: the Denjoy–Carleman class
C\{M_{n}\}
with
M_{n}=B^{n}(n!)^{1/\beta}
is quasi-analytic if and only if
\beta\geq 1
. When
\beta<1
(sub-exponential decay), quasi-analyticity fails and uniqueness is not guaranteed by this method. Separately, the condition
\varphi>0
is necessary for any uniqueness result. If
\varphi(x_{0})=0
for some
x_{0}
, then the Dirac distribution
\delta_{x_{0}}
satisfies
\langle\delta_{x_{0}},x^{n}\varphi\rangle=x_{0}^{n}\varphi(x_{0})=0
for all
n
, so
T
and
T+\delta_{x_{0}}
produce identical weak moment sequences. Zeros of the kernel thus create a fundamental obstruction to identifiability.

Remark 6.9

(The Gaussian kernel as natural default)
.

The Gaussian kernel occupies a distinguished position: it ensures rapid decay (well-defined weak moments of all orders), uniqueness (via Hermite completeness or the Denjoy–Carleman theorem with
\beta=2
), and natural compatibility with Fourier analysis. It provides a principled default choice when identifiability is required. Characterising which other positive Schwartz kernels yield totality of
\{x^{n}\varphi:n\geq 0\}
in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
is an open problem connected to the theory of orthogonal polynomials and weighted approximation.

6.6
Multivariate uniqueness for Gaussian kernels

The definitions in Section
2.1
were stated
for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and, in particular, weak moments indexed by multi-indices

\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
are well defined:

{}^{(\varphi)}m_{\alpha}\;=\;\langle T,\,x^{\alpha}\varphi(x)\rangle,\qquad x^{\alpha}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}.

We show that Theorem
6.2
extends to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a
multivariate Gaussian kernel, so that the full collection of weak moments
uniquely determines the distribution.

Proposition 6.10

(Multivariate totality implies uniqueness)
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that the family

\{x^{\alpha}\varphi(x):\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\}
is total in

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. If

T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfy

\langle T_{1},x^{\alpha}\varphi\rangle=\langle T_{2},x^{\alpha}\varphi\rangle

for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, then
T_{1}=T_{2}
.

Proof.

The argument is the same as for Proposition
6.1
:
S=T_{1}-T_{2}
vanishes on a dense subspace of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and is
continuous, hence
S=0
.
∎

Theorem 6.11

(Multivariate Gaussian uniqueness)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be symmetric positive definite,
c>0
, and

\varphi(x)=c\,\exp\!\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,x^{\!\top\!}Ax\bigr).

Then
\{x^{\alpha}\varphi(x):\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\}
is total in

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Consequently, for a fixed multivariate Gaussian
kernel, the weak moment array

\{{}^{(\varphi)}m_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}
uniquely determines

T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

Step 1. Reduction to the standard Gaussian kernel.

Let
L=A^{1/2}
be the positive definite square root of
A
. The linear
substitution
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
y=Lx
, induces a topological
isomorphism
\Phi^{*}:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
via

(\Phi^{*}\psi)(x)=\psi(Lx)
, and
\varphi=c\,(\Phi^{*}\tilde{\varphi})
with

\tilde{\varphi}(y)=e^{-|y|^{2}/2}
. Since
L
is invertible, the monomials

(L^{-1}y)^{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, span all polynomials in
y
.
Therefore, totality of
\{x^{\alpha}\varphi(x)\}
in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

is equivalent to totality of

\{p(y)\,e^{-|y|^{2}/2}:p\text{ polynomial}\}
in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
It thus suffices to prove totality for
\varphi(x)=e^{-|x|^{2}/2}
.

Step 2. Multivariate Hermite functions.

For each multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, define the multivariate Hermite
function

h_{\alpha}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}H_{\alpha_{j}}(x_{j})\,e^{-x_{j}^{2}/2},

where
H_{n}
is the
n
-th physicist’s Hermite polynomial. The family

\{h_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\}
is a Schauder basis for

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

[
11
]
: every

\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
admits a unique expansion
\psi=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}h_{\alpha}
converging in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Step 3. Triangularity and totality.

Since
H_{n}
is a polynomial of degree
n
with leading coefficient
2^{n}
,
the product
\prod_{j}H_{\alpha_{j}}(x_{j})
is a polynomial with leading
monomial
2^{|\alpha|}x^{\alpha}
(where
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}
)
plus monomials of lower degree. In particular, for every
N\geq 0
,

\operatorname{span}\{x^{\alpha}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}:|\alpha|\leq N\}\;=\;\operatorname{span}\{h_{\alpha}:|\alpha|\leq N\}.

(4)

This follows by upper triangularity: the change-of-basis matrix between the
monomial system
\{x^{\alpha}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\}
and
\{h_{\alpha}\}
, ordered by
any total ordering compatible with
|\alpha|
, is upper triangular with
nonzero diagonal entries
2^{|\alpha|}
. Since

\bigcup_{N\geq 0}\operatorname{span}\{h_{\alpha}:|\alpha|\leq N\}
is dense in

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, so is

\{x^{\alpha}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}:\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\}
.
The conclusion follows from Proposition
6.10
.
∎

Remark 6.12

(Scope of the multivariate extension)
.

The Carleman-type uniqueness (Theorem
6.3
)
also extends to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for any positive
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
among densities in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\varphi\,dx)
: the argument uses the fact
that polynomials are dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\varphi\,dx)
whenever

\varphi>0
satisfies a multivariate Carleman condition, which holds
automatically for Schwartz functions. The Denjoy–Carleman
extension (Theorem
6.6
) to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
requires additional
care—in particular, the notion of quasi-analyticity in several variables
is richer—and is left for future work.

The multivariate Gaussian uniqueness theorem is the building block needed
for extending the framework to standard multivariate statistical models
(location-scale families, elliptically contoured distributions, etc.),
where the kernel
\varphi(x)=c\exp(-\frac{1}{2}x^{\!\top\!}Ax)
arises
naturally through the quadratic form of the model.

7
Weak central limit theorem

7.1
Setup and normalisation

Let
\{(T_{i},\varphi_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. generalised random variables with common weak characteristic function
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)
and first two weak cumulants
{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
,
{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}>0
. Define
S_{n}=X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}
and the normalised sum

Z_{n}=\frac{S_{n}-n{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}}{\sqrt{n\,{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}}.

7.2
Convergence

By independence,
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{S_{n}}(t)={}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)^{n}
. For the normalised sum,

\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)=-it\frac{n{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}}{\sqrt{n{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}}+n\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}}\right).

Substituting the second-order expansion
\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(u)=i{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}u-({}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}/2)u^{2}+r(u)
with
r(u)=o(u^{2})
and
u=t/\sqrt{n{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}
gives
\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)=-t^{2}/2+n\,r(t/\sqrt{n{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}})
. Since
r(u)=o(u^{2})
, the remainder vanishes as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 7.1

(Weak Central Limit Theorem)
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}
be i.i.d. generalised random variables with
{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}>0
. Then

{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)\to\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\right)\qquad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

In particular,
Z_{n}
converges in the weak transform sense to a Gaussian generalised random variable.

Proof.
The calculation in Section
7.2
above provides the essential argument; a self-contained proof is given in Appendix
B
.

7.3
A quantitative refinement

The proof of the weak central limit theorem is based on a second-order
expansion of the weak cumulant generating function near the origin.
Under one additional order of regularity, this argument yields an explicit
rate of convergence on compact
t
-intervals.

Theorem 7.2

(Quantitative weak CLT)
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}
be i.i.d. generalised random variables with weak
characteristic function
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi
, and assume that

{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}>0
. Suppose that the weak cumulant generating
function

{}^{(\varphi)}K(t):=\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)

is
C^{3}
in a neighbourhood of
0
.

Let

Z_{n}=\frac{S_{n}-n\,{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}}{\sqrt{n\,{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}}.

Then for every
T>0
,

\sup_{|t|\leq T}\left|\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\right|=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

and consequently,

\sup_{|t|\leq T}\left|{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)-e^{-t^{2}/2}\right|=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Remark 7.3
.

Theorem
7.2
provides a quantitative refinement of the weak
central limit theorem in the topology of weak characteristic functions.
Unlike the classical Berry–Esseen theorem, the bound is expressed in
transform space rather than in terms of distribution functions. Extending
such bounds to metrics on (weighted or reconstructed) distribution
functions remains an open problem.

Proof.

By Taylor’s theorem with remainder,

{}^{(\varphi)}K(u)=i\,{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}u-\frac{{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}{2}u^{2}+R(u),

where

R(u)=\frac{{}^{(\varphi)}K^{(3)}(\xi_{u})}{6}\,u^{3}

for some
\xi_{u}
between
0
and
u
. Hence, for
|u|
small,

|R(u)|\leq C|u|^{3}

for some constant
C>0
.

Setting

u=\frac{t}{\sqrt{n\,{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}},

we obtain

\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)=-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+nR\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n\,{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}}\right).

Therefore, for
|t|\leq T
,

\left|\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\right|\leq\frac{C_{T}}{\sqrt{n}},

for some constant
C_{T}>0
.

The bound on the characteristic functions follows from the local Lipschitz
continuity of the exponential map on compact sets.
∎

Remark 7.4
.

An explicit bound can be obtained as follows:

\sup_{|t|\leq T}\left|\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\right|\leq\frac{M_{T}\,T^{3}}{6\,({}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2})^{3/2}\sqrt{n}},

where

M_{T}=\sup_{|u|\leq T/\sqrt{{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}}\left|{}^{(\varphi)}K^{(3)}(u)\right|.

7.4
Interpretation

The limiting transform
e^{-t^{2}/2}
is the classical characteristic function of the standard normal. The convergence takes place in the sense of
weak transforms
, not in the classical sense of convergence in distribution; the two notions do not in general coincide.

Remark 7.5

(The Cauchy case)
.

If
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. standard Cauchy, the arithmetic mean
\bar{X}
is again standard Cauchy (the distribution is
1
-stable), and
\sqrt{n}
-normalisation does not produce a Gaussian limit in the classical sense. Yet the weak CLT asserts
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)\to e^{-t^{2}/2}
.

There is no contradiction. First, the normalisation depends on the kernel:
{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}
and
{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}
change with
\varphi
. Second,
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)
is the
regularised
transform (not the classical characteristic function of
Z_{n}
), which suppresses the heavy tails via the kernel. Third,
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)\to e^{-t^{2}/2}
does not imply
Z_{n}
converges in distribution to a Gaussian; it says that the regularised transform of the normalised sum, viewed through the kernel window, becomes asymptotically Gaussian. The underlying distribution of
Z_{n}
remains heavy-tailed (a rescaled Cauchy), but the kernel reveals a Gaussian core.

7.5
Distributional convergence of the kernel-weighted measure

The weak CLT (Theorem
7.1
) establishes convergence of weak
characteristic functions. In the classical density case, a stronger
conclusion is available: the convergence of weak transforms implies
classical convergence in distribution of the kernel-weighted measures.
This result does not replace the weak CLT but complements it, providing
a bridge from the weak framework back to the classical one.

Theorem 7.6

(Distributional CLT for the kernel-weighted measure)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
7.1
, suppose additionally
that each
X_{i}
has a nonnegative density
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with

\varphi\geq 0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\varphi(x)\,dx=1
. Define the
kernel-weighted probability density
h:=\varphi f
. Then:

(a)

The function
h
is a probability density with finite moments of all
orders.

(b)

The mean and variance of
h
coincide with the first two weak
cumulants:

\mu_{h}={}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}
and
\sigma_{h}^{2}={}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}
.

(c)

The classical characteristic function of
h
is the weak
characteristic function:
\widehat{h}(t)={}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)
.

(d)

Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots
be i.i.d. with density
h
and set

\bar{Z}_{n}\;=\;\frac{Y_{1}+\cdots+Y_{n}-n\,{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}}{\sqrt{n\,{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}}.

Then
\bar{Z}_{n}
converges in distribution to
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
:
for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
,

P(\bar{Z}_{n}\leq a)\;\longrightarrow\;\Phi(a),

where
\Phi
is the standard normal CDF.

Proof.

(a) Since
f\geq 0
,
\varphi\geq 0
, and
\int h=1
, the function
h
is
a probability density. For every
k\geq 0
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{k}h(x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{k}\varphi(x)f(x)\,dx<\infty,

because
|x|^{k}\varphi(x)
is bounded (
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
implies

x^{k}\varphi
is bounded for every
k
) and
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

(b) The mean of
h
is

\mu_{h}=\int x\,h(x)\,dx=\int x\,\varphi(x)f(x)\,dx={}^{(\varphi)}m_{1}={}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}

(since
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(0)=1
). The variance
is
\sigma_{h}^{2}=\int(x-\mu_{h})^{2}h(x)\,dx={}^{(\varphi)}m_{2}-({}^{(\varphi)}m_{1})^{2}={}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}
.

(c)
\widehat{h}(t)=\int e^{itx}h(x)\,dx=\int e^{itx}\varphi(x)f(x)\,dx={}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)
.

(d) By (c), the classical characteristic function of the distribution
h

coincides with the weak characteristic function of the pair
(T_{f},\varphi)
.
Therefore the weak CLT (Theorem
7.1
) can be reinterpreted as
the convergence of classical characteristic functions of the standardised
sums of
h
-distributed random variables to
e^{-t^{2}/2}
. Since
h
has
all moments, Lévy’s continuity theorem
[
1
]
gives

\bar{Z}_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
∎

Remark 7.7

(Interpretation)
.

Theorem
7.6
reveals that the weak CLT is, in the
density case, the classical CLT applied to the kernel-weighted
distribution
h=\varphi f
. The kernel
\varphi
tames the tails of
f
:
even when
f
itself violates the conditions of the classical CLT (as for
Cauchy or stable densities, where no variance exists), the product

h=\varphi f
has all finite moments and therefore admits a classical
Gaussian limit theorem. The weak framework thus provides a canonical
regularisation that restores the CLT for heavy-tailed distributions.

In particular, the kernel-weighted CDF converges to the standard normal
CDF:

F_{\varphi}^{(n)}(a):=\int_{-\infty}^{a}\varphi(x)f_{n}(x)\,dx\;\longrightarrow\;\Phi(a),

where
f_{n}
is the density of
\bar{Z}_{n}
. Comparing with
Proposition
2.9
, which shows that the
kernel-weighted CDF is directly accessible from the weak framework, we
see that the object the weak framework computes—the kernel-weighted
distribution function—converges in the classical sense.

Remark 7.8

(Connection to reconstruction)
.

Combined with the Tikhonov reconstruction of
Appendix
C
, the distributional convergence provides
a pathway from weak transform convergence to density-level statements.
Since
h_{n}:=\varphi f_{n}\to\phi
in distribution (where

\phi(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}/2}
is the standard normal density),
one can apply the regularised inverse to the limiting density:

R_{\lambda}\phi=\frac{\varphi\phi}{\varphi^{2}+\lambda}
,
which as
\lambda\downarrow 0
approximates
\phi/\varphi
—the density
whose kernel-weighted version is Gaussian. This establishes a concrete
bridge: the weak CLT provides convergence of kernel-weighted measures;
the reconstruction machinery then translates this into density-level
approximations.

8
A motivating statistical consequence

The preceding sections develop the weak framework as a probabilistic-analytic object. We close with a brief illustration showing that it already has a tangible statistical consequence, even at the level of a single weak moment. A full statistical theory—including GMM estimation, transform-based estimators, regularised reconstruction, and robust estimation—is the subject of the companion paper Labouriau
[
6
]
.

8.1
Cauchy location

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Cauchy}(\mu,1)
with density
f(x;\mu)=\pi^{-1}(1+(x-\mu)^{2})^{-1}
. No classical moment exists, so the ordinary method of moments cannot estimate
\mu
. Fix a Gaussian kernel
\varphi_{\sigma}(x)=\exp(-x^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))
with
\sigma>0
. The weak first moment

{}^{(\varphi_{\sigma})}m_{1}(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,\varphi_{\sigma}(x)\,f(x;\mu)\,dx

is finite for every
\mu
and every
\sigma>0
, because
|x|\varphi_{\sigma}(x)
is bounded and
f(\cdot;\mu)
is integrable. By dominated convergence,
\mu\mapsto{}^{(\varphi_{\sigma})}m_{1}(\mu)
is smooth, and for
\sigma
of moderate size it is strictly monotone in a neighbourhood of any
\mu_{0}
, admitting local inversion.

8.2
A weak-moment estimator

Given i.i.d.
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\sim\mathrm{Cauchy}(\mu,1)
, define the empirical weak first moment
\widehat{m}_{1}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\varphi_{\sigma}(X_{i})
. Since
|x|\varphi_{\sigma}(x)
is bounded,
\widehat{m}_{1}
obeys the strong law of large numbers:
\widehat{m}_{1}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}{}^{(\varphi_{\sigma})}m_{1}(\mu)
. The estimator
\widehat{\mu}
is the solution of

\widehat{m}_{1}={}^{(\varphi_{\sigma})}m_{1}(\widehat{\mu}),

which exists and is unique on the monotonicity interval of
{}^{(\varphi_{\sigma})}m_{1}
. An implicit-function argument gives
\sqrt{n}
-consistency and asymptotic normality.

Remark 8.1

(What the illustration shows)
.

The point is not that
\widehat{\mu}
is the best estimator for Cauchy location—the sample median and the MLE are available and well understood—but that it is constructed exclusively from a first-moment condition in a model where the classical first moment does not exist. This is impossible within the classical method-of-moments framework, and it illustrates, in the simplest setting, the statistical payoff of the weak moment theory.

Remark 8.2

(Kernel as design parameter)
.

The estimator depends on
\sigma
, which trades off robustness to outliers against asymptotic variance. This trade-off, including its connection to classical robust statistics and a detailed comparison with median, MLE, Huber, and Tukey estimators, is developed in the companion paper.

9
Discussion and open problems

The framework developed in this paper rests on replacing classical densities
by distribution–kernel pairs and defining expectations through the
distributional pairing
\langle T,\psi\varphi\rangle
. From this single
device we obtain a coherent extension of moment-based probability:
weak moments of all orders exist unconditionally; the weak characteristic
function is smooth and encodes these moments; weak cumulants retain their
algebraic structure under independence and affine transformations; a weak
moment problem admits a hierarchy of uniqueness results; and a weak central
limit theorem holds at the level of regularised transforms.

These results show that the absence of classical moments does not prevent a
meaningful probabilistic calculus. Instead, the kernel
\varphi
provides a
controlled regularisation that restores finiteness and smoothness while
preserving the essential algebraic structure of moments and cumulants.

Several questions remain open and define a natural research programme.

Uniqueness beyond the hierarchy:

For which positive Schwartz kernels is the family

\{x^{n}\varphi:n\geq 0\}
total in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
?
The Gaussian kernel provides a canonical case, via the completeness of the
Hermite functions. More generally, this problem is closely related to
weighted polynomial approximation on the real line. For kernels of the form

\varphi(x)=e^{-Q(x)}
with
Q
even and sufficiently convex, one enters the
classical theory of Freud weights, where density and approximation
properties of polynomials in weighted function spaces have been extensively
studied
[
8
]
. In particular, for

\varphi(x)=e^{-|x|^{\alpha}}
with
\alpha>1
, results on rapid polynomial
approximation suggest that the Gaussian case
\alpha=2
is part of a broader
phenomenon, although a full characterisation of totality in

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
remains open. Complementary insight is provided by the
theory of de Branges, which characterises non-density of polynomials in
weighted spaces in terms of associated entire functions

[
2
]
. Extending such criteria to the Schwartz topology
appears to be the natural route to a complete solution.

Identifiability in parametric families:

While the weak moment sequence determines the underlying distribution under
conditions on the kernel, parametric identifiability requires that the map

\theta\mapsto\{{}^{(\varphi)}m_{n}(\theta)\}
be injective. This is a
model-dependent question that must be analysed case by case. In the context
of statistical inference, related local identifiability conditions arise
through rank assumptions on moment mappings, and are developed further in
the companion paper.

Rates in the weak CLT:

The weak central limit theorem established here is qualitative. A natural
refinement is to obtain quantitative bounds—in the spirit of
Berry–Esseen theorems—for the convergence of weak characteristic
functions. Section
7
provides a first step in this direction at the level of
transform convergence, but sharper bounds and extensions to distributional
metrics remain open.

Connections with other frameworks.

The weak-expectation framework is related in spirit to several extensions of
classical probability, but is distinct from each of them. In sublinear
expectation theory
[
10
]
, the expectation operator is nonlinear
and encodes model uncertainty, leading to objects such as the
G
-normal
distribution and nonlinear central limit theorems. In contrast, the present
framework retains linearity once the pair
(T,\varphi)
is fixed, and the
extension arises from regularisation rather than nonlinearity. It is closer
in spirit to the theory of generalised random variables and processes in the
sense of Gel’fand and Vilenkin
[
4
]
, where random objects are
defined as continuous linear functionals on spaces of test functions and
characterised by their characteristic functionals. The present approach may
be viewed as a scalar, regularised counterpart of this theory, focused on
moment, cumulant, and transform structures. Finally, there is a connection
to functional limit theorems for heavy-tailed processes
[
12
]
,
where partial sums converge to stable or self-similar limits. The weak CLT
developed here operates at the level of regularised transforms for scalar
sums; extending it to process-level limits in distribution or Skorokhod
spaces is a natural direction for future work.

Statistical inference.

The motivating consequence presented in
Section
8
is deliberately minimal. A full
development—including weak moment estimation, transform-based methods,
regularised reconstruction, optimal kernel selection, and robustness
analysis—is the subject of a companion paper (see
[
6
]
). In particular, the weak
framework naturally leads to redescending
M
-estimators (or inference functions) with bounded
influence functions, without the need for ad hoc truncation (see
[
7
]
).

These questions define a coherent programme growing out of the present
framework, linking distributional probability, weighted approximation
theory, and robust statistical inference.

Leibniz estimate for the proof of Theorem

We verify the bound (

) claimed in Step 1 of the proof of Theorem

. Recall that
\psi^{(n)}(t)=\langle S,f_{n}\rangle
where

S\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})

For each pair

Proof of Corollary
3.5

(Additivity of weak cumulants).

Proof.

By Theorem
3.4
, the weak cumulant generating
functions satisfy

{}^{(\varphi)}K_{X+Y}(t)={}^{(\varphi_{1})}K_{X}(t)+{}^{(\varphi_{2})}K_{Y}(t)

in a neighbourhood of
t=0
. Since
{}^{(\varphi_{1})}\phi_{X}(0)\neq 0
and

{}^{(\varphi_{2})}\phi_{Y}(0)\neq 0
, all three CGFs are smooth near the
origin. Differentiating
n
times with respect to
t
and evaluating

\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}{}^{(\varphi)}K_{X+Y}(t)\Big|_{t=0}=\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}{}^{(\varphi_{1})}K_{X}(t)\Big|_{t=0}+\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}{}^{(\varphi_{2})}K_{Y}(t)\Big|_{t=0},

which is
{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{n}(X+Y)={}^{(\varphi_{1})}\kappa_{n}(X)+{}^{(\varphi_{2})}\kappa_{n}(Y)
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.6

(Translation).

Proof.

Recall that the translated pair
(T_{a},\varphi_{a})
is defined by

\langle T_{a},\psi\rangle=\langle T,\psi(\cdot+a)\rangle
and

\varphi_{a}(x)=\varphi(x-a)
. Hence the weak characteristic function
of the translated pair is

\displaystyle{}^{(\varphi_{a})}\phi_{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle T_{a},\,e^{itx}\varphi_{a}(x)\rangle=\langle T,\,e^{it(x+a)}\varphi_{a}(x+a)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle T,\,e^{it(x+a)}\varphi((x+a)-a)\rangle=\langle T,\,e^{it(x+a)}\varphi(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{ita}\,\langle T,\,e^{itx}\varphi(x)\rangle=e^{ita}\,{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t).

Taking logarithms,
K_{a}(t)=ita+{}^{(\varphi)}K(t)
. Differentiating:

K_{a}^{\prime}(0)=ia+{}^{(\varphi\prime)}K(0)
, giving

{}^{(\varphi_{a})}\kappa_{1}=a+{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}
. For

,
the term

-th derivative, so

{}^{(\varphi_{a})}\kappa_{n}={}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{n}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition

(Scaling).

Proof.

The scaled pair
(T_{b},\varphi_{b})
is defined operationally by

\mathbb{E}_{T_{b},\varphi_{b}}[\psi]=\mathbb{E}_{T,\varphi}[\psi(b\,\cdot)]

{}^{(\varphi_{b})}\phi_{b}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{T_{b},\varphi_{b}}[e^{itx}]=\mathbb{E}_{T,\varphi}[e^{itbx}]={}^{(\varphi)}\phi(bt).

Proof of Proposition
4.1

(Smoothness of the weak characteristic function).

Proof.

We must show that
t\mapsto{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)=\langle T,e^{itx}\varphi(x)\rangle
is infinitely differentiable in
t
and
that derivatives pass through the distributional pairing.

Step 1. The map
t\mapsto g_{t}
is smooth from
\mathbb{R}
into

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
.

Define
g_{t}(x)=e^{itx}\varphi(x)
. For each fixed
t
,

g_{t}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
because
|e^{itx}|=1
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, and products with bounded smooth functions of
polynomial growth preserve the Schwartz class. The formal
t
-derivative
is
\partial_{t}g_{t}(x)=ix\,e^{itx}\varphi(x)
, and more generally

\partial_{t}^{n}g_{t}(x)=(ix)^{n}e^{itx}\varphi(x)
. Since

x^{n}\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
(the Schwartz space is closed
under polynomial multiplication), each
\partial_{t}^{n}g_{t}
belongs
to
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
.

Step 2. Convergence of difference quotients in

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
.

We verify that
h^{-1}(g_{t+h}-g_{t})\to\partial_{t}g_{t}
in the

. The difference is

h^{-1}(g_{t+h}(x)-g_{t}(x))=e^{itx}\varphi(x)\cdot\frac{e^{ihx}-1}{h}.

By the mean value theorem,

(e^{ihx}-1)/h=ix\,e^{i\theta hx}
for some

\theta\in(0,1)
depending on

and

h^{-1}(g_{t+h}(x)-g_{t}(x))-ix\,e^{itx}\varphi(x)=ix\,e^{itx}\varphi(x)(e^{i\theta hx}-1).

For any Schwartz seminorm

p_{\alpha,\beta}(f)=\sup_{x}|x^{\alpha}f^{(\beta)}(x)|
, the

-derivatives of

produces finitely many terms, each containing

|e^{i\theta hx}-1|\leq|\theta hx|\leq|hx|
times a Schwartz

. Since
|hx|\cdot|x^{m}\varphi^{(k)}(x)|\leq|h|\sup_{x}|x|^{m+1}|\varphi^{(k)}(x)|
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}

every seminorm tends to zero as

. The same argument applies
to higher-order difference quotients by induction.

Step 3. Conclusion.

Since
T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
is a continuous linear functional on

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, the composition
t\mapsto\langle T,g_{t}\rangle

is smooth and

\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}\langle T,g_{t}\rangle=\langle T,\partial_{t}^{n}g_{t}\rangle=\langle T,(ix)^{n}e^{itx}\varphi(x)\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{T,\varphi}[(ix)^{n}e^{itx}].\qed

Proof of Theorem
7.1

(Weak Central Limit Theorem).

Proof.

Let
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi
denote the common weak characteristic function
of the i.i.d. generalised random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
with
first two weak cumulants
{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}
and

{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}>0
.

Step 1. Weak CF of the normalised sum.

By the factorisation property (Proposition
3.3
)
applied to i.i.d. copies,

{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{S_{n}}(t)=({}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t))^{n}
. The
normalisation
Z_{n}=(S_{n}-n{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1})/\sqrt{n{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}

corresponds, via translation and scaling
(Propositions
3.6
and
3.7
), to the
logarithmic identity

\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)=-it\frac{n{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}}{\sqrt{n{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}}+n\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}}\right).

Step 2. Second-order expansion.

Since
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi
is smooth (Proposition
4.1
)
and
{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(0)=\langle T,\varphi\rangle\neq 0
, the function

\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi
is smooth near the origin with

\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(u)=i{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}u-\frac{{}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}}{2}u^{2}+r(u),\qquad r(u)=o(u^{2})\text{ as }u\to 0.

Step 3. Substitution and limit.

Write
\mu={}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{1}
and
\sigma^{2}={}^{(\varphi)}\kappa_{2}

for brevity and set
u=t/\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}}
:

\displaystyle\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{it\sqrt{n}\,\mu}{\sigma}+n\!\left[i\mu\frac{t}{\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}}}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\frac{t^{2}}{n\sigma^{2}}+r\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{it\sqrt{n}\,\mu}{\sigma}+\frac{it\sqrt{n}\,\mu}{\sigma}-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+n\,r\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+n\,r\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}}}\right).

For the remainder: since
r(u)=o(u^{2})
, for every
\varepsilon>0

there exists

such that
|r(u)|\leq\varepsilon|u|^{2}

whenever
|u|<\delta
. For

, hence

\left|n\,r\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}}}\right)\right|\leq n\,\varepsilon\,\frac{t^{2}}{n\sigma^{2}}=\frac{\varepsilon\,t^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\;\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}\;0.

Therefore
\log{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)\to-t^{2}/2
, and consequently

{}^{(\varphi)}\phi_{Z_{n}}(t)\to e^{-t^{2}/2}
for every

.

Appendix C
Reconstruction from weak data

We show that, in the classical density setting, the weak
representation admits a stable reconstruction via Tikhonov
regularisation. This provides an explicit inverse procedure complementing
the uniqueness results of Section
6
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
with
\varphi(x)>0
for all
x
, and let

f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. In the classical case
T=T_{f}
, the weak expectation
determines the product

g(x):=\varphi(x)f(x),

either directly (through admissible test functions) or indirectly via the
weak characteristic function

{}^{(\varphi)}\phi(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\varphi(x)f(x)\,dx=\widehat{g}(t).

Thus, reconstruction of
f
from weak data reduces to the inversion of the
multiplication operator

M_{\varphi}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\qquad(M_{\varphi}f)(x)=\varphi(x)f(x).

Since
\varphi(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
, the inverse problem

M_{\varphi}f=g
is ill-posed: small perturbations in
g
can lead to large
errors in
f
where
\varphi
is small. A natural remedy is Tikhonov
regularisation.

Theorem C.1

(Tikhonov reconstruction from weak data)
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
\varphi(x)>0
for all
x
, and
let
M_{\varphi}
be as above. Given data
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, define for

\lambda>0

R_{\lambda}g:=(M_{\varphi}^{*}M_{\varphi}+\lambda I)^{-1}M_{\varphi}^{*}g.

Then:

1.

R_{\lambda}g
is the unique minimiser of the functional

J_{\lambda}(h)=\|M_{\varphi}h-g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lambda\|h\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

2.

If
g=\varphi f
with
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then

R_{\lambda}g\to f\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\quad\text{as }\lambda\downarrow 0.

3.

If noisy data
g_{\delta}
satisfy

\|g_{\delta}-g\|_{L^{2}}\leq\delta,

then

\|R_{\lambda}g_{\delta}-f\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{\delta}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}+\left\|\frac{\lambda}{\varphi^{2}+\lambda}f\right\|_{L^{2}}.

In particular, if
\lambda=\lambda(\delta)\downarrow 0
and

\delta/\sqrt{\lambda(\delta)}\to 0
, then

R_{\lambda(\delta)}g_{\delta}\to f\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

Since
M_{\varphi}
is a bounded self-adjoint multiplication operator on

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

M_{\varphi}^{*}=M_{\varphi},\qquad M_{\varphi}^{*}M_{\varphi}=M_{\varphi^{2}}.

Hence

M_{\varphi}^{*}M_{\varphi}+\lambda I=M_{\varphi^{2}+\lambda},

which is invertible for all
\lambda>0
. Its inverse is again a
multiplication operator:

(M_{\varphi^{2}+\lambda})^{-1}=M_{(\varphi^{2}+\lambda)^{-1}}.

Therefore,

R_{\lambda}g=M_{(\varphi^{2}+\lambda)^{-1}}M_{\varphi}g=\frac{\varphi}{\varphi^{2}+\lambda}\,g.

(1) The minimiser of
J_{\lambda}
is characterised by the normal equation

(M_{\varphi}^{*}M_{\varphi}+\lambda I)h=M_{\varphi}^{*}g,

which yields the stated expression for
R_{\lambda}g
. Strict convexity of

J_{\lambda}
ensures uniqueness.

(2) If
g=\varphi f
, then

R_{\lambda}g=\frac{\varphi^{2}}{\varphi^{2}+\lambda}f,

and hence

R_{\lambda}g-f=-\frac{\lambda}{\varphi^{2}+\lambda}f.

Since
0\leq\frac{\lambda}{\varphi^{2}+\lambda}\leq 1
and this factor
converges pointwise to
0
, dominated convergence yields

\|R_{\lambda}g-f\|_{L^{2}}\to 0.

(3) For noisy data
g_{\delta}
, write

R_{\lambda}g_{\delta}-f=\frac{\varphi}{\varphi^{2}+\lambda}(g_{\delta}-g)-\frac{\lambda}{\varphi^{2}+\lambda}f.

Taking norms,

\|R_{\lambda}g_{\delta}-f\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left\|\frac{\varphi}{\varphi^{2}+\lambda}\right\|_{\infty}\|g_{\delta}-g\|_{L^{2}}+\left\|\frac{\lambda}{\varphi^{2}+\lambda}f\right\|_{L^{2}}.

The function
x\mapsto\frac{x}{x^{2}+\lambda}
attains its maximum at

x=\sqrt{\lambda}
with value
1/(2\sqrt{\lambda})
, hence

\left\|\frac{\varphi}{\varphi^{2}+\lambda}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}.

This yields the stated bound and the convergence condition.
∎

Remark C.2
.

The reconstruction operator
R_{\lambda}
regularises the ill-posed inversion
of
M_{\varphi}
by suppressing regions where
\varphi
is small. The
regularisation parameter
\lambda
controls the trade-off between bias and
stability. This illustrates that the weak representation, while losing
pointwise information through multiplication by
\varphi
, retains enough
structure to allow stable recovery of the underlying density under suitable
regularisation.

Remark C.3

(On the
L^{2}
assumption)
.

Theorem
C.1
assumes
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which is
slightly stronger than the
L^{1}
integrability assumed for densities
elsewhere in the paper. This is a mild condition: since
\varphi
is
bounded, every
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
density qualifies, and virtually all standard
parametric families (Gaussian, Student
t
, stable with index
\alpha>1/2
,
generalised hyperbolic, etc.) have
L^{2}
densities. The
L^{2}
setting is
natural for the Tikhonov framework because the normal equation

(M_{\varphi}^{*}M_{\varphi}+\lambda I)h=M_{\varphi}^{*}g
is formulated in
Hilbert space.
