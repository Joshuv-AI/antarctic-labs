Dual roles of polyphenols in modulating red wine aroma: From yeast-derived aroma accumulation to matrix effects on aroma release
Authors: Xiaoyu Xu, You Liu, Dongsheng Cui, Ruyi Li, Ruyi Zhang, Chifang Cheng, Zhenzhen Zhang, Mengyao Qi, Keji Yu, Changqing Duan, Yibin Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polyphenols are well recognized for their roles in wine color and mouthfeel, yet their comprehensive influence on aroma modulation remains poorly understood. Here, a dual-system approach was employed, combining a synthetic grape must fermentation model analyzed by headspace solid-phase microextraction gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME/GC–MS) with a wine matrix system analyzed by static headspace gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (SHS/GC–MS). During synthetic must fermentation, polyphenol supplementation enhanced fermentation kinetics without inhibiting yeast growth, and promoted the accumulation of fatty acid ethyl esters including ethyl hexanoate, ethyl octanoate, and ethyl decanoate. Within the wine matrix, polyphenols exerted structure-dependent effects on ester volatility, suppressing short-chain and acetate esters while enhancing the release of longer-chain ethyl esters. Sensory evaluation across both systems revealed a consistent shift toward a red wine-like aroma profile. These findings demonstrate that polyphenols dually modulate both biosynthetic accumulation and physicochemical release of fruity esters, collectively shaping red wine aroma typicity.

Introduction

Wine aroma is a central determinant of sensory quality and stylistic identity, strongly influencing consumer preference and product differentiation (He et al., 2023). In red wines, the overall aroma profile arises from the synergistic contribution of varietal, fermentative, and aging-derived volatile compounds, including higher alcohols, esters, fatty acids, terpenes, and norisoprenoids (Ubeda et al., 2019). Among these sources, alcoholic fermentation is widely regarded as the most critical stage for defining the aromatic character of young red wines, as it generates the bulk of fruity and floral volatiles through yeast metabolism (Massera et al., 2021). In particular, higher alcohol acetates (HAAs) and ethyl esters of fatty acids (EEFAs) are considered the principal drivers of fresh fruit perception in early-stage wines owing to their low sensory thresholds and pronounced sensory impact (Rigou et al., 2021; van Wyk et al., 2019).

Although the biosynthesis of these esters during alcoholic fermentation has been extensively characterized, their accumulation is strongly influenced by viticultural and enological factors, including grape maturity, fermentation temperature, maceration regime, and yeast strain selection (Zang et al., 2025). Of particular relevance, winemaking techniques that intensify the extraction of grape solids, including cold maceration, extended skin contact, and flash-détente processing, not only modify the phenolic composition of the resulting wine but are also accompanied by marked changes in ester accumulation and overall fruity aroma intensity (Setford et al., 2017; Tong et al., 2024). In a recent study on flash-détente processing, Tong et al. (2024) reported that this technique substantially elevated ester concentrations and enhanced fruit-driven sensory attributes, an effect that was attributable to the altered grape must matrix, particularly its enriched polyphenol content. This finding implies that changes in the phenolic environment during fermentation may modulate yeast secondary metabolism and thereby influence aroma compound biosynthesis, extending beyond the conventional understanding of polyphenols as non-volatile contributors to color and mouthfeel (Soares et al., 2017). Indeed, an expanding body of evidence supports the existence of significant interactions between phenolic compounds and yeast aroma metabolism. Specific grape phenolics and enological tannins have been reported to affect yeast viability, modulate intracellular redox balance, and alter carbon flux distribution, which collectively influence the biosynthesis of volatile metabolites (Jia et al., 2015; Li et al., 2023, Li et al., 2023; Mekoue Nguela et al., 2019). For instance, polyphenols may compete for key metabolic intermediates such as pyruvic acid and acetaldehyde through the formation of anthocyanin derivatives, potentially redirecting precursor availability and shifting the balance between acetate esters and ethyl esters (Ling et al., 2022). However, the majority of existing studies have focused on individual phenolic compounds or single tannin preparations. Given the potential for synergistic or antagonistic interactions among different phenolic classes, the yeast metabolic response to a composite mixture may differ fundamentally from the mere additive effects of isolated compounds. Consequently, despite their capacity to more accurately represent the authentic phenolic matrix encountered during red wine fermentation, the metabolic impact of complex polyphenol mixtures remains poorly understood.

Beyond their potential influence on yeast metabolism, phenolic compounds are also known to modulate aroma expression through physicochemical interactions in the finished wine matrix. The non-volatile phenolic fraction can regulate the partitioning, retention, and release of volatile compounds via noncovalent binding, hydrophobic interactions, and changes in solution viscosity (Muñoz-González et al., 2014; Pittari et al., 2021). Sáenz-Navajas et al. (2010) demonstrated that reconstituting a white wine volatile extract within a red wine non-volatile matrix shifted olfactory perception toward red and dark fruit descriptors, indicating that aroma typicity is not solely determined by volatile composition but is also contingent upon the surrounding phenolic environment. In addition, phenolic acids and condensed tannins have been shown to interact with specific odorants such as terpenes and esters, modifying their headspace concentration and perceived sensory intensity in a structure- and concentration-dependent manner (Wang et al., 2021, 2022, 2026). Nevertheless, how winemaking-induced changes in matrix composition, particularly the phenolic fraction, further influence aroma volatility and sensory perception under conditions that simulate real wine consumption remains insufficiently explored.

To systematically examine the roles of polyphenols in red wine aroma modulation, a dual-system experimental framework was established. A controlled synthetic grape must model was employed to evaluate the effects of polyphenol supplementation on yeast growth, fermentation kinetics, primary metabolite formation, and ester biosynthesis during alcoholic fermentation, thereby isolating potential metabolic effects. Complementarily, a commercial Chardonnay wine matrix was used to assess polyphenol-driven changes in aroma volatility and headspace release under static observation conditions, isolating physicochemical matrix effects. Through the integrated application of HS-SPME/GC–MS profiling, SHS/GC–MS analysis, multivariate statistics, and sensory evaluation, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of how polyphenols regulate wine aroma through both metabolic and matrix pathways, offering new insights into the formation of red wine aroma typicity.

Materials and methods

Reagents and standards

The standards of volatile compounds used for quantification were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Grape skin pigments were provided by Shanghai Dingtang International Trade Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), grape seed tannins by Aesica Co., Ltd. (Italy), and the other compounds, including myricetin (≥98.8%), gallic acid (≥97.5%), and caffeic acid (≥98.0%), by Prat Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. (Shaanxi, China). All HPLC-grade reagents, namely ethanol (≥99.8%), glycerol (≥99%), and acetic acid (≥99%), were supplied by Shanghai McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

The fermentation medium was synthetic must MS300, which was prepared according to the protocol outlined by Zhang et al. (2024). The initial sugar concentration was 200 g/L, and the pH was adjusted to 3.3. The medium was sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min. The dry white wine was sourced from the Chateau Yuanshi (Ningxia, China) (N38°34′ 23.23″ E106°01′ 54.88″), with the specific production parameters adhering to local winemaking standards. The wine contained 12.8% (v/v) alcohol, with a pH of 3.35, titratable acidity of 6.50 g/L (expressed as tartaric acid), and residual sugar content of 1.50 g/L, representing the typical physicochemical characteristics of a commercial dry Chardonnay wine.

Synthetic grape must fermentation system with exogenous phenolic addition

Fermentation experiments in synthetic grape must

This study was conducted using a synthetic grape must medium (MS300), to which different classes of phenolic compounds were exogenously added in order to investigate their effects on the fermentation kinetics of S. cerevisiae and the formation of volatile compounds. The target phenolic levels were selected to establish a controlled high-phenolic model representative of intensified extraction conditions, while remaining within concentration ranges reported for major phenolic classes in red wines. According to previous studies, including both FD treated and conventionally vinified wines, the major phenolic classes in red wines generally fall within the following concentration ranges: total monomeric anthocyanins are typically present at approximately 100–400 mg/L; flavonols occur at tens of mg/L; hydroxybenzoic and hydroxycinnamic acids are commonly detected at low-to mid-tens of mg/L; and condensed tannins/proanthocyanidins vary widely, ranging from several hundred mg/L to the g/L level, depending on grape variety, matrix composition, and extraction intensity (Tong et al., 2024; Waterhouse et al., 2024). Based on these reported ranges, the addition levels in this study were selected to establish a controlled high-phenolic model system representative of intensified extraction conditions, while ensuring reproducibility across fermentations. Specifically, grape skin pigments were added to achieve a final anthocyanin concentration of 340 mg/L, while grape seed tannins were supplemented to obtain a final flavanol concentration of 1160 mg/L. In addition, myricetin, gallic acid, and caffeic acid were individually added to reach final concentrations of 44 mg/L for flavonols, 70 mg/L for hydroxybenzoic acids, and 50 mg/L for hydroxycinnamic acids, respectively. The phenolic mixture was freshly prepared immediately before addition to the synthetic must. Depending on their solubility, the individual phenolic preparations were separately dissolved or dispersed in sterile water or ethanol to obtain concentrated stock solutions or suspensions. These stock preparations were protected from light and prepared on the day of fermentation. All preparation steps were carried out aseptically. Appropriate volumes of the individual stocks were then combined according to the predetermined proportions and added to MS300 synthetic must to reach the target final concentrations. The must was thoroughly homogenized before yeast inoculation. To avoid solvent-related effects, the control must received the same total volume of the corresponding solvents.

Fermentation experiments were conducted in synthetic must under two conditions: a blank control without phenolic supplementation (CK) and a phenolic-supplemented treatment (P). Both treatments were inoculated with commercial active dry Saccharomyces cerevisiae X16 (Laffort, France) at 25 g/hL. Plate counts performed immediately after inoculation showed that the initial viable yeast population was approximately 5.0 × 106 CFU/mL in both CK and P. All treatments were conducted in triplicate and subjected to alcoholic fermentation under static conditions at 18–20 °C. Fermentation was considered complete when the residual sugar concentration fell below 4 g/L.

During fermentation, weight loss of the fermentation vessels was recorded every 2 day as an indirect measure of carbon dioxide release to characterize fermentation kinetics. Samples were collected on days 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, and 15 after fermentation onset, with a sampling volume of 20 mL each time. Samples were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected and stored at −20 °C in the dark for subsequent physicochemical and volatile compound analyses.

Colony count and residual sugar determination

At time zero and each subsequent sampling point, fermentation samples were serially diluted and plated onto YPD agar plates. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 2–3 days, after which colonies were counted and the viable yeast population was expressed as CFU/mL. The reducing sugar content was determined using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method, with glucose as the standard (Sun et al., 2025).

Determination of primary fermentation products

The concentrations of glycerol, ethanol, and acetic acid were determined using an Agilent 1200 high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an Aminex HPX-87H ion-exchange column (300 mm × 7.8 mm; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) (Zhang et al., 2022a, Zhang et al., 2022b). Prior to analysis, fermentation samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter. The mobile phase consisted of 5 mM H2SO4, delivered at a constant flow rate of 0.6 mL/min under isocratic conditions.

Ethanol and glycerol were quantified using a refractive index detector at a column temperature of 45 °C, whereas acetic acid was analyzed using a UV detector at 214 nm with the column maintained at 60 °C. All samples were analyzed in triplicate. Identification was based on retention times of authentic standards, and quantification was performed using external calibration curves.

Analysis of volatile compounds by HS-SPME/GC–MS

Volatile compounds were extracted and quantified using HS-SPME/GC–MS as described by Ling et al. (2022). A 5 mL aliquot of each sample was transferred into a 20 mL headspace vial containing 1.5 g of NaCl and 10 μL of 4-methyl-2-pentanol (1.0 g/L, internal standard), and the vial was immediately sealed with a PTFE/silicone septum. The extraction procedure was performed using a CTC-CombiPAL autosampler (CTC Analytics, Zwingen, Switzerland). Following sample pre-equilibration for 30 min at 40 °C, a 2 cm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber (50/30 μm; Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) was exposed to the headspace for 30 min for volatile extraction. The fiber was then thermally desorbed in the GC injector at 250 °C for 8 min in a 5:1 split mode.

Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Agilent 7890B-5975C system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an HP-INNOWAX capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA). Helium (>99.999%) served as the carrier gas at a constant flow of 1 mL/min. The oven temperature was programmed as follows: 50 °C for 1 min, increased to 220 °C at a rate of 3 °C/min, and held for 5 min. The mass spectrometer was operated in electron impact (EI) mode at 70 eV, with the transfer line, ion source, and quadrupole temperatures maintained at 250 °C, 230 °C, and 150 °C, respectively. The mass scan range was m/z 29–350. Identification of volatile compounds was achieved by comparing the obtained mass spectra and calculated retention indices (RIs) with those of authentic standards, as well as with data from the NIST 14 mass spectral library. Quantification was conducted using the internal standard method based on calibration curves established in a synthetic wine matrix (12% v/v ethanol, 2 g/L glucose, and 7 g/L tartaric acid at pH 3.5). The same calibration matrix was used for all fermentation time points to maintain a consistent quantification framework across samples. Because ethanol and sugar concentrations changed during fermentation, the resulting concentrations should be interpreted primarily for comparisons between CK and P at the same sampling time and for relative temporal profiling, rather than as fully matrix-matched absolute concentrations across all fermentation stages. Detailed linear ranges and R2 values are summarized in Table S2. Odor activity values (OAVs) were calculated as the ratio of the measured concentration of each compound to its reported odor threshold value in wine or a wine-like matrix (OAV = concentration/threshold). An OAV >0.1 was used as an inclusive criterion for selecting potentially aroma-relevant compounds for temporal analysis, whereas compounds with OAV ≥1 were considered more likely to contribute directly at the individual-compound level. All samples were analyzed in technical duplicate.

Commercial Chardonnay wine system with exogenous phenolic addition

Polyphenol addition to commercial Chardonnay wine

Chardonnay dry white wine was used as the model wine matrix, with a control group (Wine-CK) and a polyphenol-supplemented group (Wine-P). Wine-CK consisted of the base wine without added phenolic compounds, whereas Wine-P was supplemented with the phenolic mixture at the same concentrations as those used in the fermentation experiments. After thorough mixing, the samples were transferred to sealed glass containers and maintained at 18–20 °C in the dark for six days. Based on preliminary observations, this duration was selected as a standardized time-course window for monitoring changes in volatile headspace abundance following polyphenol addition; it was not intended to represent an experimentally verified time required to reach thermodynamic equilibrium. Static headspace volatile compounds in both groups were analyzed once daily throughout the six-day observation period.

Impact of exogenous phenolic additives on aroma volatility in dry white wine studied by SHS/GC–MS

The effect of exogenously added phenolic compounds on the volatility of aroma constituents in dry white wine was investigated using SHS/GC–MS. Volatile compounds were extracted from the headspace using a PAL 3 autosampler equipped with a sealed syringe (CTC Analytics AG, Switzerland), with the syringe temperature maintained at 85 °C. Samples were equilibrated under static conditions at 35 °C for 30 min, after which 1 mL of headspace gas was withdrawn and injected into the GC–MS system. Injection was performed in pulsed, splitless mode with a pulse pressure of 25 psi. The GC–MS operating conditions were identical to those described in Section 2.2.4. Variations in the peak areas of aroma compounds were used to evaluate the effects of exogenous phenolic addition on the volatility of aroma compounds in dry white wine (Zhao et al., 2025). The calculation formula is as follows Eq. (1):(1)ΔS(%)=S1‐S2S2*100

ΔS represents the percentage change in peak area; S1 denotes the peak area of the corresponding ester compounds in dry white wine after the addition of phenolic compounds, and S2 denotes the peak area of the corresponding ester compounds in untreated dry white wine.

Sensory evaluation

All experimental procedures involving human participants were approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of China Agricultural University (Approval No. CAUHR-20231103). The sensory panel consisted of 18 trained assessors (8 males and 10 females, aged 25–38 years) from the Center for Viticulture and Enology (CFVE). All panelists had received systematic training in wine sensory analysis and demonstrated high proficiency in aroma recognition and wine style discrimination.

Sensory evaluations were conducted in a standardized sensory laboratory under controlled conditions, including a clean and odor-free environment, uniform lighting, and a constant temperature of 20 °C. Assessments were performed in individual booths. Samples (approximately 30 mL) were served in dark-colored (black) ISO standard wine-tasting glasses (ISO 3591:1977) to eliminate visual bias arising from color differences between treatments, and presented in a randomized order. Both synthetic wine samples with exogenous phenolic addition and commercial dry white wine samples were subjected to the same sensory evaluation procedures described below, and were assessed in independent sessions to avoid sensory interference between matrices.

Quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA)

Quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) was used to characterize the aroma profiles of the fermented synthetic must and commercial Chardonnay wine systems. A trained sensory panel was assembled, and the training protocol was conducted as described by Xia et al. (2025). During preliminary evaluation sessions and panel discussion, seven aroma descriptors were selected by consensus to represent the major olfactory differences among the samples: red fruit, black fruit, tropical fruit, citrus, peach, fruit candy, and chemical odor. The same set of descriptors was applied to both matrices to ensure consistency in descriptor use across the two evaluation systems. Panelists completed four 1-h training sessions before the formal evaluation to calibrate and standardize their perception of aroma intensity. The reference materials and their preparation are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Samples from each matrix were evaluated in separate sessions, with presentation order randomized within each session. Aroma intensity was rated using an 11-point scale ranging from 0 (“not perceived”) to 10 (“extremely intense”). Data were processed using PanelCheck software (version 1.4.2). For each descriptor, the two matrices were analyzed separately using two-way analysis of variance, with sample and panelist as factors. The fermented synthetic must samples (CK and P) and the Chardonnay wine samples (Wine-CK and Wine-P) were evaluated under otherwise identical sensory conditions.

Classification test

A classification test was employed to evaluate the influence of exogenous polyphenols on the aroma typicity of synthetic and dry white wine, as described by Xiang et al. (2025). Categories were predefined as “white wine style,” “red wine style,” or “uncertain.” Prior to the formal session, panelists underwent targeted training using archetypal reference standards to calibrate their perception of each style. During the assessment, panelists were first provided with two reference samples to reinforce the sensory benchmarks, followed by the categorization of experimental samples based on their overall olfactory impression. The frequency of each categorization was recorded to statistically determine the impact of polyphenols on aroma style recognition. The same classification procedure was independently applied to the fermented synthetic must and Chardonnay wine samples under an identical evaluation protocol. Statistical analyses were selected according to the specific inferential objective of each experimental system.

Statistical analysis

Physicochemical and volatile compound data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation of three independent fermentations. Differences among multiple samples or fermentation time points were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan's multiple range test. Pairwise comparisons between the control and polyphenol-supplemented groups at the same sampling point were performed using a two-tailed Student's t-test. Aroma-typicity classification data were evaluated using Pearson's chi-square test, chi-square goodness-of-fit test, or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. PLS-DA was performed using SIMCA 14.1 (Umetrics, Malmö, Sweden). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Unless otherwise specified, statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 3.4.2), and figures were generated using Origin 2024 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA).

Results and discussion

Alcoholic fermentation kinetics and primary metabolism in synthetic grape must

To assess the biological effects of exogenous polyphenols during alcoholic fermentation, yeast growth dynamics, fermentation kinetics, sugar consumption, and the formation of primary metabolites were systematically monitored throughout the fermentation process in synthetic grape must.

Yeast growth and fermentation kinetics

Yeast growth and fermentation performance of S. cerevisiae X16 in synthetic grape must were evaluated to assess the influence of exogenous polyphenol addition during alcoholic fermentation. Under both conditions, yeast populations followed a typical sigmoidal growth pattern, characterized by a rapid exponential phase during the first four days and a subsequent transition to stationary growth after day 4 (Fig. 1A). Comparable maximum cell densities were reached in both treatments, indicating that polyphenol supplementation did not impair yeast proliferation under the experimental conditions applied. In contrast to the similar biomass evolution, fermentation kinetics differed markedly between treatments. As reflected by cumulative CO2 release (Fig. 1B), the supplemented system exhibited a consistently higher fermentation rate from day 3 onward, with statistically significant differences observed at multiple time points. Despite this divergence in CO2 evolution dynamics, both fermentations reached completion within 15 days. The enhanced CO2 release observed in the polyphenol-treated system reflects an increase in fermentative metabolic activity rather than biomass accumulation. This observation suggests a possible change in intracellular carbon flux distribution. This shift may arise from polyphenol-mediated modulation of yeast physiological states, including membrane properties, redox balance, or stress-adaptive responses, which collectively govern the efficiency of fermentative metabolism (Li et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021).Fig. 1Effects of exogenous polyphenol addition on yeast growth, fermentation kinetics, and primary metabolite formation during alcoholic fermentation by Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Sugar consumption and formation of primary fermentation metabolites

Changes in sugar utilization and the formation of primary fermentation metabolites were subsequently examined to further elucidate the metabolic consequences of polyphenol supplementation. In both treatments, reducing sugar concentrations decreased progressively during fermentation, with a more rapid depletion observed in the polyphenol-supplemented group (Fig. 1C). This accelerated consumption coincided with elevated ethanol concentrations at corresponding fermentation stages, consistent with the enhanced CO2 evolution observed in Fig. 1B and indicative of increased glycolytic throughput.

Beyond ethanol formation, polyphenol addition exerted a pronounced influence on the accumulation of glycerol and acetic acid. As fermentation progressed, acetic acid concentrations increased steadily in both treatments, with the polyphenol-supplemented group displaying consistently higher levels from mid-fermentation onward (Fig. 1D). A similar pattern was observed for glycerol, whose production was significantly enhanced in the presence of polyphenols, particularly during the early and mid-fermentation phases (Fig. 1E). The concurrent increases in ethanol, glycerol, and acetic acid in polyphenol-supplemented fermentations suggest that exogenous polyphenols altered overall carbon flux distribution within central metabolism, rather than affecting a single metabolic branch (Yu et al., 2022). During alcoholic fermentation, glucose is primarily converted into pyruvate through glycolysis, generating NADH in the process. The regeneration of NAD+ is essential to sustain glycolytic flux. While most NADH is reoxidized during ethanol formation, excess reducing equivalents can be redirected toward glycerol synthesis via the glycerol-3-phosphate pathway (Spavieri et al., 2025). The elevated glycerol concentration in the polyphenol-supplemented system may reflect an increased requirement for NADH reoxidation associated with enhanced glycolytic activity. At the same time, glycerol production is also a well-established adaptive response of yeast to environmental stress, including phenolic stress. Thus, the observed increase may reflect both redox balancing and a mild adaptive response to the phenolic environment, although the relative contributions of these processes require further investigation. In parallel, the higher accumulation of acetic acid suggests enhanced flux through acetaldehyde and acetyl-CoA intermediates, which represent key metabolic nodes linking primary metabolism to aroma precursor formation (Moffett et al., 2020). Although phenolic compounds have been reported to produce variable effects on yeast metabolism, including growth inhibition and stress-induced glycerol overproduction (Bianchini et al., 2023; Fletcher and Baetz, 2020), the present results show that, under controlled synthetic must conditions, exogenous polyphenols stimulated fermentative metabolism overall rather than suppressing it.

Volatile composition of wines fermented in the presence of polyphenols

Overall differentiation of volatile profiles revealed by multivariate analysis

To comprehensively assess the temporal influence of exogenous polyphenol supplementation on wine volatile composition during alcoholic fermentation, partial least squares–discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed using the quantified volatile profiles of CK and P samples collected on days 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 15. The resulting two-component model explained 79.8% of the total variance (Fig. 2A). The model exhibited parameters of R2X (cum) of 0.932, R2Y (cum) of 0.899, and Q2 (cum) of 0.616, indicating good explanatory and predictive ability. As illustrated in the score plot, polyphenol-treated wines were clearly separated from the controls along the first latent variable, indicating clear differences in volatile profiles between treatments. This separation became evident from day 4 of fermentation onward, indicating that polyphenol-driven modulation of aroma metabolism emerged at an early fermentation stage and progressively intensified.Fig. 2Partial least squares–discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) of volatile aroma compounds in synthetic wines with and without polyphenol addition. (A) score plot; (B) loading plot.

Partial least squares–discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) of volatile aroma compounds in synthetic wines with and without polyphenol addition. (A) score plot; (B) loading plot.

The corresponding loading plot (Fig. 2B) further highlighted the key volatiles contributing to this separation. Several medium- and long-chain fatty acid ethyl esters, including ethyl hexanoate, ethyl octanoate, ethyl decanoate, and ethyl dodecanoate, were predominantly associated with the polyphenol-treated wines, whereas certain aldehydes and higher alcohols (e.g., nonanal) were more closely related to the CK wines. Beyond esters, polyphenol supplementation also affected the levels of other volatile classes, although to varying extents (Table S2). Among higher alcohols, phenylethyl alcohol and isopentanol were significantly elevated in the polyphenol-treated wines, with fold changes of 3.00 and 1.40, respectively, while isobutanol and 1-propanol remained largely unchanged. Linalool, the sole terpenoid quantified, increased 2.33-fold in the presence of polyphenols. In contrast, nonanal was markedly reduced (0.21-fold) in polyphenol-supplemented wines. Despite these changes, the most consistent and pronounced differences between treatments were observed among fatty acid ethyl esters, which exhibited systematic increases across multiple chain lengths and contributed prominently to group separation in the PLS-DA model. Given their well-established roles as key contributors to fruity aroma in wines and their dominant influence on the multivariate discrimination observed here, subsequent analyses focused specifically on ester compounds to elucidate the mechanisms underlying polyphenol-mediated aroma modulation. Importantly, many of these discriminant compounds exhibited odor activity values (OAVs) above 0.1 at the end of fermentation, underscoring their potential sensory relevance. The enrichment of fatty acid ethyl esters in polyphenol-supplemented wines suggests that phenolic compounds may promote ester formation by influencing yeast carbon metabolism during fermentation. In line with this, Ling et al. (2022) reported that defined oenological polyphenols significantly enhanced the accumulation of fatty acid ethyl esters in both industrial-scale and simulated fermentations. Their work proposed that polyphenol-induced shifts in anthocyanin derivative formation altered metabolic competition for intermediates such as pyruvic acid and acetaldehyde. This change may favor acetyl-CoA–related pathways toward ester production. In line with these findings, the elevated levels of medium- and long-chain ethyl esters observed here further support the notion that polyphenols can modulate aroma-related metabolic outcomes through indirect metabolic reprogramming rather than direct effects on redox balance. This positive modulation of aroma composition is in agreement with Chen et al. (2016), who reported that the pre-fermentative addition of oenological tannins significantly reshaped the volatile profile and improved the overall aromatic complexity of red wines. It should be noted, however, that yeast metabolic responses are highly dependent on the specific concentration and structure of the phenolics present. For instance, a recent transcriptomic study by Li et al. (2023) revealed that excessive exposure to tannic acid (1.6 g/L) induced severe environmental stress in S. cerevisiae, downregulating genes related to aroma biosynthesis and decreasing overall volatile production by over 80%. In contrast to such stress-inducing conditions, the specific polyphenol matrix employed in our study did not trigger metabolic repression. Instead, it was associated with changes in metabolic output that specifically redirected the intracellular carbon flux toward the accumulation of fatty acid ethyl esters without impairing yeast vitality. Consistent with the PLS-DA results, hierarchical clustering analysis based on volatile compounds at the end of fermentation revealed a clear segregation between control and polyphenol-supplemented wines, indicating a distinct remodeling of the final aroma composition induced by exogenous phenolics (Fig. S1).

Dynamic evolution of major esters during alcoholic fermentation

The temporal evolution of ester compounds during fermentation further elucidated the differential effects of polyphenol supplementation on aroma development (Fig. 3). Ester concentrations increased over time in both the control wines and the polyphenol-treated wines, but distinct accumulation patterns emerged between groups. For most short- and medium-chain fatty acid ethyl esters, markedly higher concentrations were detected in the polyphenol-supplemented group from mid-fermentation onward, with these differences becoming more pronounced at the fermentation endpoint. Specifically, ethyl hexanoate, ethyl octanoate, and ethyl decanoate exhibited 2.08-, 1.88-, and 2.13-fold increases, respectively, under polyphenol supplementation (Table S2). The increased accumulation of fatty acid ethyl esters in the polyphenol-supplemented group is consistent with evidence that phenolic compounds can influence ester formation during alcoholic fermentation (Leonard et al., 2021). Based on our findings, this enhancement can be directly linked to the accelerated primary metabolism. Specifically, the increased production of ethanol and acetic acid (a direct precursor to acetyl-CoA) likely provided an expanded substrate pool for ester biosynthesis.Fig. 3Changes in concentrations of ester compounds with odor activity values (OAVs) > 0.1 that differed at the end of alcoholic fermentation during fermentation. Differences between the control and polyphenol-supplemented groups were most pronounced for short- and medium-chain fatty acid ethyl esters, including ethyl hexanoate, ethyl octanoate, and ethyl decanoate, which showed clear divergence between days 6 and 8 of fermentation. Treatment-related differences in ethyl dodecanoate emerged later and were less substantial throughout the fermentation course. This delayed and smaller separation in concentration profiles suggests that the response of long-chain esters to polyphenol supplementation differs in both magnitude and timing from that of shorter-chain esters. Based solely on the observed concentration dynamics, the enhancement of long-chain ester levels appears to be associated primarily with cumulative effects during the later stages of fermentation rather than with a marked acceleration during early or mid-fermentation. Unlike short-chain esters, long-chain ethyl esters are less volatile and more hydrophobic, and their apparent accumulation in the fermenting matrix may be influenced not only by biosynthetic rates but also by physicochemical interactions with the surrounding medium (Liu et al., 2024).

Sensory perception of polyphenol addition in synthetic wine

Aroma profiles characterized by quantitative descriptive analysis

To characterize the sensory impact of polyphenol supplementation, quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) was performed using seven predefined aroma descriptors (Fig. 5A). The polyphenol-treated wines exhibited significantly higher intensities of red fruit, black fruit, peach, fruit candy, and tropical fruit compared with the control sample (P < 0.05). Among these descriptors, red fruit and black fruit showed the largest increases after polyphenol addition. In contrast, the control wine showed relatively higher intensity of the chemical odor descriptor, whereas citrus perception did not differ markedly between treatments. Overall, the sensory profile of the polyphenol-treated wines was characterized by stronger fruit-related attributes, particularly those associated with red and dark fruit notes. These sensory changes are consistent with the compositional differences observed in the volatile analysis. Polyphenol-treated fermentations contained higher concentrations of several fatty acid ethyl esters, including ethyl hexanoate, ethyl octanoate, and ethyl decanoate, which are known to contribute to fruity aroma impressions. The increased abundance of these esters may therefore contribute to the stronger fruit-related descriptors perceived in the QDA evaluation.

Aroma typicity assessed by classification test

To further evaluate whether the observed sensory differences influenced overall aroma style perception, a classification test was conducted in which panelists categorized the samples as “red wine–like,” “white wine–like,” or “uncertain.” For this system, the primary statistical objective was to determine whether the overall distribution of aroma-typicity classifications differed between CK and P; therefore, the two treatments were compared directly using a 2×3 contingency-table analysis. As shown in Fig. 5B and Table 1, a clear shift in perceived aroma typicity was observed after polyphenol supplementation. In the control condition, 72.22% of assessors (13 of 18) classified the sample as white wine–like, whereas only one assessor (5.56%) identified it as red wine–like and four assessors (22.22%) selected the uncertain category. After polyphenol addition, the sensory classification shifted strongly toward the red wine–like category, with 88.88% of assessors (16 of 18) assigning the treated sample to this group, while only one assessor selected white wine–like and one remained uncertain (Fig. 5B). The contingency table analysis confirmed that the distribution of classifications differed significantly between treatments (χ2 (2, N = 36) = 25.321, P < 0.001; Table 1). Adjusted residual analysis further indicated a strong association between the polyphenol-treated sample and the red wine–like category, whereas the control sample was strongly associated with the white wine–like category. Aroma release behavior in commercial Chardonnay wine in the presence of polyphenols

Building upon the metabolic changes observed in the synthetic must fermentation system, a commercial Chardonnay wine matrix was used to examine the time-dependent effects of exogenous polyphenols on ester headspace behavior independently of active fermentation. The headspace peak areas of major ester compounds were monitored under static conditions throughout the six-day observation period. As shown in Fig. 4, polyphenol supplementation caused an immediate and compound-dependent decrease in ester headspace abundance during the initial stage of observation (days 0–1), followed by distinct temporal patterns among compounds with different structures and chain lengths. For the majority of esters examined, including ethyl acetate, isobutyl acetate, ethyl butanoate, isoamyl acetate, ethyl hexanoate, hexyl acetate, ethyl octanoate, ethyl decanoate, and ethyl dodecanoate, peak areas in Wine-P were consistently lower than those in Wine-CK at the onset of observation. The lower headspace abundance observed in Wine-P is consistent with matrix-mediated retention of volatile compounds through non-covalent interactions, such as hydrophobic association and hydrogen bonding. As equilibration proceeded, the release behaviors of the esters varied depending on their chain length and structural properties. Short-chain and acetate esters, including ethyl acetate and isobutyl acetate, remained consistently suppressed throughout the entire observation period, with Wine-P exhibiting lower headspace concentrations than Wine-CK at all measured time points. By comparison, several medium- and long-chain fatty acid ethyl esters exhibited an initial suppression followed by a gradual recovery during the later stages of equilibration. Notably, ethyl decanoate and ethyl dodecanoate exhibited higher peak areas in Wine-P than in Wine-CK during the late equilibration phase (days 4–6), suggesting a delayed enhancement of volatility for longer-chain esters. Similar chain-length-dependent modulation of ester volatility by phenolic matrices has been reported in other wine systems, where hydrophobic interactions and matrix partitioning effects were shown to differentially affect short- and long-chain esters (Wang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022a, Zhang et al., 2022b). These time- and chain-length-dependent profiles indicate that polyphenol supplementation was associated with compound-specific changes in apparent headspace partitioning rather than with uniform suppression of all esters.

These chain-length-dependent headspace profiles may reflect differences in the balance of non-covalent interactions between the phenolic matrix and individual esters. Recent literature has extensively documented the matrix effects of various phenolic classes on aroma partitioning. For instance, phenolic acids, flavanols, and flavonols have been shown to form transient complexes with aroma compounds, significantly altering their volatility and headspace retention (Wang et al., 2021, 2026). Furthermore, specific interactions between hydroxycinnamic acids (e.g., caffeic acid) and esters have been reported to modify ester stability and matrix partitioning, directly influencing the overall aroma expression in red wines (Wu et al., 2025). In the present study, short-chain esters, owing to their higher polarity, may be preferentially and stably retained within the polyphenol-enriched matrix through hydrogen bonding. The partial recovery of some medium- and long-chain ethyl esters during the later observation period suggests that the relevant matrix interactions changed over time. However, the present data do not determine whether these temporal patterns resulted from changes in non-covalent association, reorganization of the phenolic matrix, oxidative transformation, or a combination of these processes (Li et al., 2025; Pittari et al., 2021).

Sensory effects of polyphenol addition in commercial Chardonnay wine

Changes in sensory aroma profiles of Chardonnay wine

Quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) was first conducted to determine how polyphenol addition modified the sensory aroma profile of commercial Chardonnay wine (Fig. 5A). The control wine (Wine-CK) was mainly characterized by citrus, tropical fruit, peach, and fruit candy attribute. After supplementation with polyphenols, significant increases (P < 0.05) in red fruit and black fruit descriptors were observed, whereas the intensities of citrus and tropical fruit attributes were reduced. Peach notes showed a slight decrease, although this change was not statistically significant. No additional descriptors outside the predefined lexicon were required after polyphenol addition; instead, the intensities of existing descriptors shifted. This indicates that polyphenols did not introduce additional odor qualities, but altered the sensory balance among pre-existing aroma attributes. These sensory changes are consistent with the ester release data obtained from the static headspace analysis. In particular, medium-chain fatty acid ethyl esters, including ethyl hexanoate, ethyl octanoate, and ethyl decanoate, exhibited an initial decrease followed by partial recovery during the later observation period. As these medium-chain ethyl esters are recognized contributors to berry-like and red fruit nuances (de-la-Fuente-Blanco et al., 2020; Lytra et al., 2016), the shift in ester release balance likely underlies the increased perception of red and black fruit attributes in Wine-P. Overall, QDA results demonstrate that polyphenol addition shifted the sensory profile of Chardonnay from a citrus- and tropical-fruit-dominant pattern toward a profile with stronger red-fruit-related characteristics.

Aroma typicity evaluated by classification test

Based on the differences observed in QDA, a classification test was subsequently performed to evaluate whether the sensory modifications translated into changes in perceived aroma typicity. For the Chardonnay wine system, the statistical analysis addressed both the classification tendency within each treatment and the difference between Wine-CK and Wine-P. Accordingly, goodness-of-fit tests were conducted separately for each treatment, followed by Fisher's exact test for the between-group comparison. For the control wine (Wine-CK), all assessors (18/18) classified the sample as white wine–like, confirming the stability and clarity of its baseline sensory identity. Following polyphenol addition (Wine-P), the distribution of classifications changed markedly. Twelve assessors (66.7%) categorized the wine as red wine–like, one assessor (5.6%) maintained a white wine–like classification, and five assessors (27.8%) selected the uncertain category (Fig. 5C; Table 2). Chi-square goodness-of-fit testing (against a uniform distribution) indicated that the classification distribution for Wine-P differed significantly from a uniform distribution (P = 0.006). In addition, Fisher's exact test confirmed a significant difference between Wine-CK and Wine-P in terms of typicity classification (P < 0.001). The shift from unanimous white wine–like classification to a majority red wine–like classification demonstrates that polyphenol addition substantially modified the overall aroma style perception of the Chardonnay wine. As non-volatile matrix constituents, polyphenols may modulate aroma perception by altering the headspace partitioning of key odorants through non-covalent interactions such as hydrophobic association and hydrogen bonding, thereby shifting their release profiles and perceived sensory intensity (Perez-Jiménez et al., 2019, 2020). Integrated interpretation of the dual roles of polyphenols in wine aroma formation and expression

Polyphenols are traditionally regarded as non-volatile matrix components that modulate wine aroma primarily through physicochemical interactions (Muñoz-González et al., 2014; Sherman et al., 2020). The present study demonstrates that polyphenols influence wine aroma through two distinct but complementary mechanisms: biological regulation of ester biosynthesis during fermentation, and physicochemical effects on ester release behavior in the finished wine matrix. By integrating results from synthetic must fermentation, static headspace analysis of ester release in commercial Chardonnay wine, and sensory evaluation, it becomes evident that these two modes of action operate at different stages yet jointly influence sensory perception and aroma typicity.

During alcoholic fermentation, exogenous polyphenols did not inhibit yeast growth but were associated with enhanced fermentative intensity and a redistribution of intracellular carbon fluxes, leading to increased formation of ethanol, glycerol, acetic acid, and, notably, medium- and long-chain fatty acid ethyl esters. These metabolic adjustments suggest that polyphenols function as indirect modulators of yeast physiological state, influencing redox balance and precursor allocation rather than eliciting classical stress responses. The aroma precursor pool entering the post-fermentation stage therefore contained higher relative levels of several ethyl esters associated with darker fruit notes. Polyphenols also modulated aroma expression through a distinct yet complementary pathway by regulating aroma release behavior within the wine matrix. Time-course observations under static storage conditions in commercial Chardonnay wine showed an initial suppression of ester volatility upon polyphenol addition, followed by compound- and chain-length-dependent divergence over time. Short-chain and acetate esters remained consistently suppressed, with medium- and long-chain ethyl esters displaying delayed volatilization and, in certain cases, enhanced headspace presence at later stages. This behaviour may be related to non-covalent interactions between polyphenols and volatile compounds (Liu et al., 2025; Shahidi and Dissanayaka, 2023).

Crucially, the final sensory perception resulted from the combined influence of these two processes rather than from either mechanism alone. Quantitative descriptive analysis and classification tests showed that polyphenol addition did not introduce new aroma descriptors but changed the relative intensity of existing sensory attributes. As medium-chain fatty acid ethyl esters, including ethyl hexanoate, ethyl octanoate, and ethyl decanoate, are recognized contributors to berry-like and red fruit nuances (de-la-Fuente-Blanco et al., 2020; Lytra et al., 2016), their partial recovery in headspace concentrations likely shifted the sensory balance toward red and dark fruit attributes during evaluation. Concurrently, the persistent suppression of short-chain and acetate esters diminished the citrus and tropical fruit attributes that characterize the typical aroma profile of white wines. Through these differential effects on volatile release, polyphenols altered sensory dominance patterns and shifted the overall aroma profile toward a red wine-like typicity. These findings support a mechanistic interpretation in which polyphenols play a dual regulatory role by modulating fermentative metabolism and modulating the physicochemical partitioning and release of volatile esters within the wine matrix, thereby affecting both the formation and release of aroma compounds and contributing to the observed sensory differences. Nevertheless, the reconstructed phenolic mixture represented a simplified high-phenolic model based on selected phenolic classes and did not reproduce the full qualitative and quantitative complexity of any individual red wine. The findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence obtained under controlled model conditions rather than as a direct simulation of a specific flash-détente-treated wine.

Conclusion

This study shows that polyphenols can influence wine aroma through complementary effects during fermentation and in the finished wine matrix. In synthetic must, polyphenol supplementation did not inhibit yeast growth but enhanced fermentation kinetics and increased the accumulation of several fatty acid ethyl esters, particularly ethyl hexanoate, ethyl octanoate, and ethyl decanoate. In Chardonnay wine, polyphenols altered ester headspace release in a compound- and chain-length-dependent manner, with persistent suppression of short-chain and acetate esters and partial recovery of some longer-chain ethyl esters during the later observation period. Across both systems, these compositional and release changes coincided with a sensory shift toward red wine-like aroma typicity. Although these model systems do not fully reproduce the complexity of commercial red-wine production, the findings suggest that management of the phenolic matrix may provide a practical means of modulating fruity aroma expression and wine sensory style.
