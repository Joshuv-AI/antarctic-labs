Therapeutic effect of mitochondrial transfer on bone tissue diseases: treatment strategy of mitochondrial transplantation and delivery technology
Authors: Zuping Wu, Yuzhe Guan, Ruifeng Song, Peng Chen, Xinyi Fang, Qian chen, Xiaoyan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Disruptions in bone tissue metabolic balance can lead to osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and bone tumors. This disruption typically manifests as reduced or abnormal bone mass, accompanied by pathological changes such as inflammation, fractures, and pain. Recent studies have revealed that mitochondrial dysfunction is prevalent in the aforementioned pathological processes, participating in the regulation of bone tissue cell function and intracellular immune function. Mitochondrial transfer, a newly discovered physiological phenomenon in recent years, can regulate mitochondrial function within recipient cells, thereby influencing metabolic activities, proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and immune function of various bone tissue cells. This article primarily reviews the regulatory effects of mitochondrial transfer associated with MSCs on various cells in bone and joint tissues. Given the role of mitochondrial transfer in regulating bone metabolism, we elucidate the application of mitochondrial transfer therapy in the treatment of osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and other bone tissue diseases.The Translational Potential of this Article: This review elaborates in detail on the therapeutic strategies provided by mitochondrial transplantation technology or targeted mitochondrial delivery systems for the treatment of bone tissue diseases, based on the occurrence of mitochondrial transfer in bone tissue tips and its therapeutic effects on related diseases such as OA, RA, osteoporosis.

Introduction

In recent years, the pathological progress of bone-related diseases represented by osteoarthritis (OA) and its treatment have been a popular research topic. Human bone metabolism is always in dynamic balance, which is maintained by osteoblast-mediated bone formation and osteoclast-led bone resorption, and the osteogenic/lipogenic differentiation activity of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) further influences this balance by regulating the bone remodeling microenvironment. In joint, the functional activity of chondrocytes and synoviocytes directly determines the structural and functional integrity of the joints, and their abnormal energy metabolism can lead to pathological changes such as cartilage matrix degradation and synovial inflammation.

There is now substantial evidence that mitochondrial biosynthesis and quality control are critical to the functional integrity of the bone and joint and the treatment of osteoarticular diseases. Mitochondria are an important site of cellular metabolism, and in the metabolic process of osteoarticular tissue, mitochondria take the role of acquiring energy and generating essential metabolites, and the mitochondrial membrane potential, respiratory enzyme complexes, oxygen consumption, and intracellular ATP content are reported elevated during osteogenesis and adipogenesis [[1], [2], [3]]. They also play an important role in reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, calcium homeostasis, and cell signaling [4]. Mitochondria regulate a range of cellular functions including apoptosis, autophagy, cell cycle regulation, differentiation and senescence [5]. In recent years,​ dysregulation of the mitochondrial quality control network has been shown to be an important causal factor in related diseases. Mitochondrial dysfunction usually occurs in conjunction with inflammatory factor aggregation, excessive accumulation of ROS, and failure of mitochondrial self-renewal. For example, mitochondrial DNA mutations in chondrocytes trigger excessive accumulation of ROS and aberrant secretion of inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α), resulting in a vicious circle of “mitochondrial damage-inflammation amplification” [6,7]. Recent studies have shown that mitochondrial transfer has become an important way to rescue mitochondrial dysfunction. And intercellular transfer of mitochondria or mitochondrial genome can rescue and re-establish the biological function of recipient cells. Koyanagi showed that mitochondria can be spontaneously transported from cardiomyocytes to endothelial cells via transient cytoplasmic bridges or nanotubular structures [8].Intercellular mitochondrial transfer, as a novel repair mechanism, can restore the metabolic activity of mitochondria to damaged cells via nanotubular or extracellular vesicle (EVs) delivery pathways. Donation of mitochondria from MSCs to chondrocytes significantly suppressed ROS levels and slowed down the progression of OA [9]. In the context of injury and inflammation, MSCs respond to ROS and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMP) by donating mitochondria to damaged cells [10,11]. In studies of disease progression in bone and its treatment, it has been shown that mitochondrial transfer protects chondrocyte function and relieves OA pain symptoms in OA [12]. In addition, mitochondrial transfer is associated with chemotherapy resistance in bone tissue tumors and proliferation and growth of tumor tissues.

This paper focus on mitochondrial function, mitochondrial transfer and its modalities, the role of mitochondrial transfer in bone and joint and related tissues and its therapeutic role in bone and joint diseases, and develop mitochondrial transplantation technology or nanocarrier-targeted delivery system based on MSCs to provide intervention strategy for the reversal of bone metabolic imbalance.

Mitochondria and mitochondrial transfer

Physiological changes in mitochondria

Mitochondria contain electron transport chain complexes that surround and are embedded within the cristae membrane. Mitochondrial cristae topology influences respiratory efficiency and thus energy metabolism by regulating respiratory complex function, nucleoid maintenance, iron-sulfur biogenesis, and protein synthesis and translocation [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Mitochondria also change shape (including cycles of mitochondrial fusion and division, motility and cristae remodeling) to meet cellular demands [17]. In undifferentiated MSCs from bone tissue, mitochondria are localized near the nucleus (perinuclear) and are broken and spherical [18,19]. As MSCs differentiate and mature a branching mitochondrial network is formed, accompanied by increased OXPHOS activity and higher ATP concentration, providing energy for matrix mineralization [19]. Morphological changes in mitochondria are often accompanied by changes in energy metabolism, which manifested as an increase in intracellular ATP concentration, which contributes to the provision of energy for cellular differentiation, accompanied by an increase in ROS concentration.

Mitochondrial transfer

Definition of mitochondrial transfer

Mitochondria in the human body may be damaged along with aging, inflammation, injury, genotoxicity and oxidative stress. At this time, in addition to mitochondrial autophagy and other ways of self-renewal, the horizontal transfer of mitochondria or mitochondrial genome between cells can save and rebuild the biological function of the recipient cell, mitochondrial transfer is an important mechanism for energy and information reprogramming through the horizontal transfer of functional mitochondria between cells. Mitochondrial transfer between cells is mainly divided into two categories, the first is vertical inheritance, the DNA of mitochondria can be passed with the division of the cell after fertilization, the mother cell contributes mitochondria to the daughter cell, this process is called vertical inheritance of mitochondria. The cell then undergoes mitochondrial biosynthesis to increase and retain mitochondrial biomass to support future cell divisions and their cellular functions. Additionally, mitochondria are transferred across cells via non-division pathways Mitochondria Mainly to be classified as: contact-dependent: tunneling nanotubes (TNTs), cellular synaptic junctions; contact-independent: EVs encapsulated or free mitochondria released.

Mitochondrial transfer pathway

Inter cellular mitochondrial transfer can be divided into two categories: contact dependent mitochondrial transfer and non-contact dependent mitochondrial transfer. Contact dependent mitochondrial transfer occurs when the donor and recipient need to physically contact each other in order for the transfer event to occur. The most commonly reported contact dependent mechanism is through TNTs; Cells can also make contact through dendritic structures to promote mitochondrial transfer, which we define as “dendritic structure mediated mitochondrial transfer”. Another contact dependent transfer mechanism involves Cx43 (connexin 43), which researchers define as “adhesion mediated mitochondrial transfer”. Cells do not require direct physical contact for mitochondrial transfer. On the contrary, donor cells can excrete mitochondria and produce mitochondria that can be captured by another cell type, such as extracellular vesicles with mitochondria. In addition, cells can release mitochondria that are not wrapped by EV membranes [20].A.TNT approach

A.TNT approach

TNT approach

TNTs were first proposed by Rustom, and researchers believe that TNTs are gap junction channels (GJCs) mediated by Cx43 formation [21]. TNTs have a diameter size of 50-200 nm, and consist of cytoskeletons such as F-actin and microtubules [21]. Its function is to communicate between two cells for unidirectional or bidirectional exchange of cytoplasmic and plasma membrane components, including, for example, mitochondria, lysosomes, endosomal vesicles, as well as plasma membrane components, lipid droplets, ions, proteins, miRNAs and pathogens [[21], [22], [23]]. Later studies have revealed that mitochondrial transfer is mainly mediated by two genetically encoded proteins that mediate the formation of TNT: (1) Miro1-Rho GTPase pathway: mitochondrial transfer triggered by the mitochondrial Rho GTPase Miro1-associated motor articulating protein complex [24]. Its pathway is structurally related to the cytoskeleton and mitochondria enter the cytosol of recipient cells along the actin-microtubule highway using the Rho-GTPase Miro1. Novel TNTs containing intermediate filaments and microtubules have also been shown to accomplish mitochondrial transfer [25].B.Cx43-mediated gap junctions

Cx43-mediated gap junctions

Cx43-mediated gap junction channels (GJCs) for mitochondrial transfer through intercellular gap junctions [27]. C.Extracellular vesicle approach

Extracellular vesicle approach

Three pathways for mitochondrial transfer:

(1) The nanotunnel tube (TNT) pathway, regulated by the Miro1-Rho GTPase pathway or gap junctions (GJCs) mediated by Cx43, controls TNT formation to facilitate mitochondrial transfer.

(2) Extracellular vesicle pathway: Receptor cells capture extracellular vesicles (EVs) containing mitochondria and their genetic material to complete mitochondrial transfer and integration of mitochondrial genetic material.

(3) Release and phagocytosis of free mitochondria: Receptor cells capture free mitochondria through endocytosis.

EVs are composed of lipid bilayers that carry components from the cell of origin [29]. Cells carry out intercellular mitochondrial transfer by releasing and capturing mitochondria-containing EVs [30]. EVs can be categorized into microvesicles and vesicles based on their size and their contents [31,32]. Mitochondria and mitochondrial genetic material contained in the contents of the EVs can be delivered to the recipient cell, altering the metabolism and mitochondrial function of the recipient cell. Microvesicles (100-1000 nm): directly encapsulate intact mitochondria, e.g. astrocytes deliver mitochondria to neurons via EVs to alleviate ischemic injury [31], Exosomes (30-150 nm): carry mitochondrial components (e.g. mtDNA). CD38+ osteoclasts release mitochondria via EVs to promote osteogenic progenitor cells' differentiation [30]. EV-encapsulated mitochondria The transfer process releases senescent mitochondria to renew its own mitochondrial state on one hand, and on the other hand, as a mitochondrial donor cell, it releases high-quality mitochondria through EVs to supply other cells to improve mitochondrial function. For example, platelets activated by thrombin release respiratory mitochondria in EVs (as well as free mitochondria). CD38 mediates the release of mitochondria from osteoblasts in EVs, which promotes the differentiation of bone progenitor cells into osteoblasts in a positive feedback loop, thereby promoting bone formation [33] (Fig. 1).D.Release and phagocytosis of free mitochondria

D.Release and phagocytosis of free mitochondria

Release and phagocytosis of free mitochondria

A third mechanism of intercellular mitochondrial transfer is the direct release of free mitochondria by donor cells, which are captured by recipient cells via acetylheparin sulfate-mediated endocytosis. Originally found in mouse blood platelets, free mitochondria are released, which are approximately 0.5-1 μm in diameter and contain the full length of the mtDNA genome. Researchers believe that the source of mitochondria in mouse blood is adipocytes in addition to platelets. These mitochondria lack EV markers such as CD9, CD63, and CD81 in their composition so the researchers ruled out the possibility of EV-delivered mitochondria [34]. Cells release free mitochondria in a manner dependent on mitochondrial division proteins such as dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) and mitochondrial fission 1 protein (FIS1). Acetylheparin sulfate from recipient cells is then involved in the capture of free mitochondria [35] (Fig. 1).

Mitochondrial transfer function and therapeutic implications

A.Regulates receptor cell metabolism

A.Regulates receptor cell metabolism

Regulates receptor cell metabolism

ρ0 cells (mitochondrial DNA-deficient cells induced by ethidium bromide) are incapable of generating ATP by oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), but can acquire intact mitochondria via the TNTs pathway when co-cultivated with healthy cells, restoring the activity of the respiratory chain driven by complex I (NDUFS4-dependent) to re-establish ATP synthesis capacity [[36], [37], [38]]. This phenomenon has been categorized by researchers as the “generator phenomenon”. Cells preferentially use their own mitochondria for nutrient metabolism but can integrate exogenous mitochondria to maintain their survival if they are in demand for metabolic emergencies. The use of exogenous mitochondria in ischemic stroke animal models can effectively reduce infarct size and restore local aerobic function [39]. Mitochondrial transfer in stroke animals can increase the local mitochondrial gene expression concentration to rescue the local ischemia and oxidative energy supply disorder [40]. Osteoblasts transfer mitochondria to osteoblast progenitors, which stimulate osteoblast progenitors to differentiate into mature osteoblasts and promote bone matrix formation [41].

Mitochondrial transfer does not just restore metabolically impaired disease, but may also promote cancer progression due to promoting levels of oxidative function in recipient cells. Tumor metabolic hijacking refers to the fact that ρ0 tumor cells are slow to divide but can acquire respiratory function by acquiring intact mitochondria and associated mitochondrial DNA from neighboring cells, thereby accelerating cell division and restoring their tumorigenic potential [41,42] Whereas tumor cells ingest free mitochondria in a manner that is predominantly TNT. Tumors uptake free mitochondria from cells such as mesenchymal stem cells/stromal cells [[43], [44], [45]], cancer-associated fibroblasts [46], T cells [47], macrophages [48], astrocytes cells [49] and other cells to take up mitochondria, and mitochondrial transfer promotes tumor pathology progression (Fig. 2).B.Mitochondrial transfer and intracellular mitochondrial autophagy form a synergistic quality control network:Fig. 2Functions of mitochondrial transfer:(1) Regulation of recipient cell metabolism: Restoring ρ0 cell mitochondrial function, reestablishing respiratory chain activity, and enhancing ATP synthesis capacity; mitochondrial transfer increases tumor cell proliferation activity.(2) Mitochondrial transfer and intracellular mitochondrial autophagy form a synergistic quality control network: Recipient cells update mitochondria through mitochondrial transfer; autophagy eliminates aged or abnormal mitochondria.(3) Mitochondrial transfer promotes wound healing and angiogenesis: mitochondrial transfer at fracture sites promotes osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells and osteocyte activity; at skin defect sites, mitochondrial transfer to neutrophils triggers an antimicrobial response to combat infection, while transfer to endothelial progenitor cells stimulates the secretion of angiogenic factors.(4) The double-edged sword effect of mitochondrial transfer in immune regulation: adipocytes transfer mitochondria to cardiomyocytes, promoting antioxidant response and reducing ischemia-reperfusion injury; Platelets activate neutrophil NETosis through the TLR9 MyD88 axis to resist infections caused by sepsis; The genetic information carried by mitochondrial transfer (CpG residues in acetylated N-terminal methionine peptides) is recognized by the immune system as damage related molecular patterns through Toll like receptor 9 (TLR9) and formyl peptide receptors 1-3 (FPR1-3), thereby driving pro-inflammatory immune responses.

Mitochondrial transfer also contributes to the improvement of mitochondrial health in recipient cells. This involves two aspects: 1) Recipient cells promote intracellular mitochondrial gene expression and compensate for their own mitochondrial dysfunction by acquiring free mitochondria and completing mitochondrial transfer, such as activating the PGC-1α pathway to promote mitochondrial biosynthesis at the same time [50]. 2) When intracellular mitochondrial dysfunction occurs, the cell can improve the quality of the remaining intracellular mitochondria by discharging the damaged mitochondria. For example, mouse and human cardiomyocytes with loss-of-function mutations in the lysosomal protein LAMP2 are unable to successfully recycle damaged mitochondria via autophagy and expel damaged mitochondria by releasing more EVs [51], which are subsequently captured and degraded by macrophages (Fig. 2).C.Wound healing and vascularization

C.Wound healing and vascularization

Wound healing and vascularization

In bone defects caused by fractures and osteoporosis, mitochondria are involved in mitochondrial transfer via TNT, restoring the mitochondrial function of MSCs, promoting osteogenic activity in bone defects and facilitating bone defect recovery [52]. Platelets are enriched at the damaged skin and promote coagulation, and their mitochondria move from the center of the platelet to the inner surface of the plasma membrane [34] and are discharged from the platelet in the form of EVM and free mitochondria. And these mitochondria play multiple roles through translocation: (1) platelet-derived mitochondria bind to the surface of neutrophils triggering neutrophil adhesion to the endothelial wall, driving neutrophils to produce an antimicrobial inflammatory response, eliciting a localized innate immune response to fight infection [34]. (2) Free mitochondria released after platelet activation are taken up by endothelial progenitor cells via sulfate-mediated endocytosis, which induces an increase in VEGF-A secretion to promote angiogenesis [53]. Endothelial progenitor cells provide mitochondria to mature endothelial cells in the brain to improve the blood-brain barrier and promote angiogenesis [54] (Fig. 2).D.The double-edged sword effect of immunomodulation

D.The double-edged sword effect of immunomodulation

The double-edged sword effect of immunomodulation

The role of mitochondrial transfer in the regulation of immune function is more complex. One view is that CpG residues and formylated n-terminal methionine peptides in mitochondrial genes are recognized by the immune system by Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) and formylated peptide receptors 1-3 (FPR1-3) as damage-associated molecular patterns and drive pro-inflammatory immune responses [55,56]. But it has also been shown that mitochondrial transfer can be anti-inflammatory. After mitochondrial transfer from MSCs to CD4+ T cells, reprogramming of aspartate metabolism contributed to an increase in the proportion of Treg cells, and IL-10 secretion [57,58] (Fig. 2). Activation of neutrophil NETosis by platelet-derived mitochondria via the TLR9-MyD88 axis leads to increased bacterial clearance efficiency in a sepsis model (Mitochondrial transfer mediates endothelial cell engraftment through mitophagy) [59]. Many metabolites, proteins, and signaling molecules are delivered by adipocytes during the transfer of mitochondria to other cells. These accompany the mitochondria in transmitting metabolic information to distant organs and form the regulatory axis of mitochondrial transfer. Mitochondria released by adipocytes are captured by cardiomyocytes, promoting an antioxidant response that conditions the heart's defense against metabolic stress and ischemia/reperfusion injury [60].

B.Synovial cells

Synovial cells

The synovial membrane, which covers the inner surface of the joint capsule, is a special loose connective tissue rich in a variety of synoviocytes. It is rich in a variety of synoviocytes, including macrophage-like synoviocytes, fibroblast-like synoviocytes and a small number of dendritic synoviocytes. The normal function of synoviocytes plays an important role in the joints. The mitochondrial transfer function of synovial MSCs (sMSCs) is impaired in rheumatoid arthritis (RA). After co-culturing human sMSCs from RA patients with Th17 cells, it was found that mitochondrial transfer was impaired and the inhibitory effect of IL17 expression was inhibited between RA-sMSCs and Th17 cells, compared to healthy BM-MSCs, suggesting that not only the mitochondrial mass of donor cells is impaired when in an inflammatory state, but their mitochondrial transfer to recipient cells Th17 mitochondria is also deficient [64]. MSC receiving mitochondrial transfer from different sources and states may produce different effects. mitochondrial transfer from M1-type macrophages to MSC resulted in the accumulation of ROS in MSC, an increase in extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), and a concomitant decrease in oxygen consumption and an increase in glycolysis. The TNF-α pathway and Toll-like pathway were activated in the MSC of osteoporotic mice; the abnormal accumulation of ROS in the MSC mitochondria led to metabolic remodeling, and the inhibition of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) activity triggered the accumulation of succinic acid, which activated the HIF-1α pathway and promoted the secretion of the inflammatory factor IL-1β to rise significantly. However, twice-weekly injections of linear quality granules through the tail vein activated osteogenic activity, as evidenced by a rise in OCN expression and a decrease in positive TRAP staining of osteoclasts, reversing the trend of bone loss in osteoporotic mice [52]. It is concluded that the transfer of “poor quality” mitochondria from polarized macrophages in osteoporosis impairs the function of MSCs and contributes to bone loss. Provision of exogenous mitochondria improves mitochondrial function in MSC, rescues bone loss, and removes the accumulation of oxidative stress products. Furthermore, in vitro cultured BMSCs tended to enter G2/M phase and initiate osteogenic differentiation more after receiving purified mitochondria from the same BMSC source. The regulation of immune cell function by mitochondrial transfer is complex, and a part of the research suggests that mitochondrial transfer promotes inflammation, but MSC-derived mitochondrial transfer participates in the regulation of immune cell function, such as T cells, which is important for the treatment of certain immune-type diseases of bone, such as RA. Co-culture of BMMSC with T cells revealed that MSC transferred mitochondria to primary CD4 (+) CCR6 (+) CD45RO (+) T helper 17 (Th17) cells. Mitochondrial transfer from BM-MSCs to Th17 cells via MitoEV (1) suppressed the expression level of pro-inflammatory cytokine (IL17) by a mechanism associated with the transition of Th17 cells to a Treg phenotype, and (2) increased the level of aerobic respiration in Th cells. Interestingly, however, the state of BMMSC as mitochondrial donor cells and their mitochondrial “quality” are critical for Th17 cells receiving mitochondria, and the inhibitory effect of isolated mitochondria from pro-inflammatory cytokine (TNFα and IFNγ)-treated BMMSC on IL-17 secretion by Th17 cells was diminished by the presence of the pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNFα and IFNγ) [64] (Fig. 3).

Regulatory network of mitochondrial transfer between osteoblasts

Osteoblasts are interconnected during bone formation through a highly developed dendritic network, which influences the balance between bone resorption and bone formation by communicating with osteoblasts and osteoclasts [40,68,69]. Mitochondrial transfer between dendritic networks established by osteoblasts is regulated by mitochondria-endoplasmic reticulum contacts (MERCs). It has been reported that mitochondria are tightly coupled and in extensive contact with the ER, and that the contact sites between the ER and mitochondria influence the mitochondrial diameter and thus regulate mitochondrial fission and migration (three-dimensional modeling shows that the endoplasmic reticulum wraps around the outer membrane of mitochondria, in which mitochondria are in a constricted state, with diameters of 138 nm and 146 nm, and their locomotion is activated). Mitochondrial division has been suggested as one of the potential mechanisms for mitochondrial transfer [70,71]. Mitochondria communicate with the endoplasmic reticulum with the help of two proteins, Mitofusin 2 (Mfn2) (a GTPase localized in the outer mitochondrial membrane that connects the endoplasmic reticulum to the mitochondria), and vesicle-associated membrane protein-associated protein B (VAPB). The endoplasmic reticulum alters mitochondrial distribution, morphology, and metastatic progression through binding protein complexes, including Mfn2 and VAPB, and is an important mediator of the mitochondrial contact site with the ER, which plays an important role in mitochondrial autophagy, mitochondrial motility, and mitochondrial connectivity with other organelles such as the ER [40].

In aging bone tissue, dissociation of the ATP synthase dimer lead to impaired osteoblast metabolism, ROS accumulation, and impaired ATP synthesis ADP levels. Aging bone cells release ADP into the extracellular space through damaged cell membranes, and ADP acts as a rescue signal that binds to P2Y2/P2Y6 receptors on the membrane surface of neighboring young osteoblasts and transmits the signal downstream, with a consequent elevation of Miro1 (the expression product of mitochondrial motility-associated genes) and Mfn2 protein expression in young osteoblasts. At this time, the distribution of mitochondria is more concentrated around the endoplasmic reticulum, i.e., the contact between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria is increased, and the contact between the mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum activates mitochondrial intercellular transfer from the dendritic network structure [72,73] (Fig. 4). The binding of mitochondria to the endoplasmic reticulum triggers mitochondrial transfer between young and aging osteoblasts, and the triggering mechanism is related to the P2Y family purinergic signaling pathway. However, the researchers did not elaborate on the detailed regulatory mechanisms of signaling, nor did they explore whether the cellular dendritic network, as a carrier of mitochondrial transfer, is one of the classical TNT, EVs, or direct-release phagocytosis modes of transfer.Fig. 4Mitochondrial transfer between young and aged osteoblastsIn aged osteoblasts, ADP levels rise and bind to P2Y2/P2Y6 receptors on the surface of young osteoblasts, activating the Miro1–Mnf2 regulatory axis. Therapeutic role of mitochondrial transfer in bone tissue diseases

Mitochondrial transfer has been implicated in a variety of disease processes, including inflammatory pain, ischemic stroke, hypoxia-induced pulmonary hypertension, acute lung injury repair, cancer cell growth, and allograft rejection [52]. Mitochondrial transfer has emerged as an emerging potential therapeutic approach that may become a salvage potential treatment for mitochondrial dysfunction by replenishing mitochondria to recipient cells with abnormal status, improving mitochondrial status and function, rescuing respiration and oxidative phosphorylation, and modulating immune function in the body to improve the functioning of various types of cells within bone tissue.

Osteoarthritis

OA is a common chronic degenerative bone disease characterized by cartilage degeneration, osteophyte formation, subchondral bone sclerosis, and synovitis, accompanied by joint pain and loss of function. It has been proven that chondrocytes in OA have mitochondrial dysfunction [61]. Compared with normal chondrocytes, the activity of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes II and III is lower in OA chondrocytes (complex II: succinate dehydrogenase; Complex III: Antimicrobial sensitive quinone cytochrome c reductase) [61,74]. The blockade of ATP synthesis and mitochondrial respiratory chain activity is accompanied by a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential [61,[75], [76], [77], [78], [79]]. Mitochondrial dysfunction ultimately leads to cartilage degeneration by promoting chondrocyte apoptosis, oxidative stress, cytokine induced chondrocyte inflammation, and cartilage matrix calcification [80,81].

In vitro studies have demonstrated that intercellular mitochondrial transfer, particularly between mesenchymal stem cells and other cell types, can enhance the mitochondrial function of recipient cells and alleviate oxidative stress, positioning it as a potential therapeutic strategy for bone tissue diseases. Mechanistically, MSCs package intact and functional mitochondria into extracellular vesicles for secretion. In the absence of direct cell–cell contact, chondrocytes with mitochondrial dysfunction take up these EVs to accomplish mitochondrial transfer, subsequently integrating the functional mitochondrial components derived from MSCs into their own mitochondrial networks. Through the secretion of EVs containing functional mitochondria, MSCs significantly increase the mitochondrial membrane potential, mitochondrial content, and overall mitochondrial function of osteoarthritic chondrocytes. Studies have reported the isolation of mitochondria from umbilical cord-derived MSCs (termed Mito MSCs), confirming their functional integrity and effective transfer to chondrocytes, synovial macrophages, and synovial fibroblasts [82]. In vitro co-culture of neonatal mouse tibial MSCs with aged mouse OA chondrocytes resulted in mitochondrial transfer from BMSCs to OA chondrocytes, leading to an increased mitochondrial membrane potential and a 60% reduction in chondrocyte apoptosis.

To further promote intercellular mitochondrial transfer for OA treatment, the expression of TNT- and EV-related factors during mitochondrial development can be enhanced. Both full-length Cx43 and its truncated internal translation isoform GJA1-20k have been implicated in mitochondrial transfer among highly oxidative cells. The Cx43-dependent tunneling nanotube pathway also mediates mitochondrial transfer from MSCs to chondrocytes, and under oxidative stress, the GJA1-20k heterodimer enhances mitochondrial transport efficiency by recruiting actin fibers. Importantly, injecting mitochondria purified from MSCs into OA joints has been shown to alleviate cartilage and joint degeneration [[83], [84], [85]]. In terms of mechanistic exploration, studies have revealed that EVs secreted by adipose-derived stromal/stem cells carry functional mitochondria that activate the MAPK/ERK1/2/FoxO1/autophagy pathway, leading to metabolic reprogramming of dendritic cells (DCs) and achieving therapeutic effects. In vivo experiments using a rat temporomandibular joint OA model further confirmed the therapeutic role of mitochondrial transfer in OA [86].

In other in vivo studies, intra-articular injection of MSC-derived mitochondria (MT) improved histological scores and bone mineral density in the affected joints of OA mouse models, indicating that MT transplantation exerts a protective effect against cartilage degeneration [87]. Vaamonde-Garcia et al. injected mitochondrial preparations into the knee joint and detected functional mitochondria within the extracellular matrix of cartilage, meniscus, and synovium 48 h post-injection [88]. Vega-Letter et al. injected Mito MSCs isolated from umbilical cord-derived MSCs into the joint cavity of mice, demonstrating that this approach promotes functional recovery and supports the use of MSC mitochondrial transplantation as a cell-free therapy for OA.

Compared with traditional endocytosis, the novel mitochondrial transplantation delivery technology offers significant advantages in terms of transfer success rate and efficiency. Studies have shown that the M-sec protein can interact with the Ral-mediated extracellular vesicle pathway to induce the formation of TNTs in stem cells. Based on this mechanism, researchers have developed a genetically engineered stem cell delivery system that overexpresses M-sec. This system enables the synergistic delivery of mitochondria from M-sec-overexpressing stem cells to damaged chondrocytes in OA, substantially enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of mitochondrial transfer for OA [89]. Additionally, injecting fusogenic mitochondrial capsules (FMCs) containing neutral lipids, cationic lipids, aromatic lipids (Liss Rhod PE), and three types of liposomes (FMC0, FMC1, FMC2) into the joints of OA mouse models effectively alleviates OA symptoms and promotes cartilage formation [90]. A genetically engineered chondrocyte-mimicking nanoplatform equipped with a nano-core (HKL-GECM@MPNPs) can carry mitochondria and fuse with the membranes of OA chondrocytes. The hK-MPnPs transfer IL-1R2 to the plasma membrane of OA chondrocytes via membrane fusion, after which the core (hKl MPnPs) is released into the cytoplasm to directly target mitochondria, achieving a synergistic effect of IL-1β blockade and mitochondrial SIRT3 restoration. The therapeutic efficacy of this approach has been validated in OA mice [87,91]. Furthermore, methods such as mitochondrial loading into erythrocytes to ameliorate inflammatory bone loss and recombinant adenoviral gene vectors have also been applied in mitochondrial transfer therapy, effectively promoting the transition of M1 to M2 macrophages and enhancing the targeting and uptake efficiency of recipient cells following mitochondrial transplantation [92,93].

Beyond improving mitochondrial function in chondrocytes, mitochondrial transfer also suppresses the expression of inflammatory factors in recipient cells and promotes extracellular matrix formation. When mitochondria encapsulated in fusogenic mitochondrial vesicles are delivered into chondrocytes via membrane fusion, recipient chondrocytes exhibit decreased MMP-13 expression and increased extracellular matrix secretion. Following intra-articular injection of these mitochondria-loaded fusogenic vesicles into a mouse model of arthritis, the in vivo experimental results demonstrated an 84% reduction in serum levels of IFN-γ, an indicator of inflammation. Micro CT analysis further revealed an 84% recovery in bone mineral density of both cartilage and subchondral bone in mice treated with mitochondrial transfer. Mesenchymal stem cell transplantation is also associated with pain management and a reduction in osteoarthritis-related pain biomarkers. In the context of alleviating OA pain symptoms, mitochondrial transfer from astrocytes to isolated neurons may play an important role in improving neuronal function in OA. Rheumatoid arthritis

RA is an autoimmune disease characterized by joint inflammation, joint pain, and bone destruction. T cells are the most abundant immune cell population in the synovial fluid rich in white blood cells of RA joints, and they are also the main source of TNF - α. Targeting CD4+T cells has become a novel treatment for RA caused by T cell dysfunction [96].

The application of mitochondrial transfer in RA treatment also involves intercellular mitochondrial transfer and mitochondrial transplantation. In vitro, healthy mesenchymal stem cells can improve the mitochondrial function of primary CD4(+) CCR6(+) CD45RO (+) Th17 cells through mitochondrial transfer, and the T cells that receive BMMSC-derived mitochondria show significantly reduced production of the cytokine IL-17 [64]. In addition, co-culturing human synovial mesenchymal stem cells from RA patients with Th17 cells revealed that mitochondrial transfer from RA sMSCs to Th17 cells is impaired compared to that from healthy BM-MSCs [97]. Researchers have noted that T cells can undergo metabolic “reconstruction” with exogenous mitochondria in vitro, and this form of mitochondrial transfer exhibits anti-inflammatory properties.

In vivo experimental results indicate that treating RA with mitochondria purified from T cells can limit the production of several pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-17A, and slow the pathological progression of RA. Corresponding in vivo experiments have also confirmed that the application of purified mitochondria significantly reduces the ability of Th17 cells to produce IL-17 [98]. The transplantation of isolated adipose-derived stem cell-derived mitochondria has also been shown to be effective in treating RA. Local injection of ADSC-derived mitochondria significantly improves the energy metabolism characteristics of macrophages in an animal model of RA and may promote barrier recovery through mitochondrial transfer [99].

In addition to the mouse RA model, the application of mitochondrial transplantation in human RA patients has also been reported. Wu et al. demonstrated that mitochondrial function is impaired in CD4(+) T cells of RA patients. Defects in mitochondrial aspartate production disrupt the metabolism and regeneration of the cofactor nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, leading to endoplasmic reticulum dysfunction (characterized by ER membrane dilation and promoting co-translational translocation) and increased TNF-α expression. Transferring purified mitochondria from naive CD4+ T cells of RA patients to RA CD4+ T cells rescued the abnormal intracellular metabolic state and replenished exogenous aspartic acid in the mitochondria. This intervention reversed ER dilation caused by mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced TNF-α and IL-2 levels, and alleviated RA inflammation. However, this study has not yet validated its therapeutic efficacy in clinical trials [56].

Mitochondrial transplantation technology has also made breakthrough progress in the treatment of RA, by transplanting mitochondria and gold nanoparticle bound mitochondria (GNP Mito) into collagen induced arthritis mice, resulting in a decrease in the number of autophagosomes and an increase in the number of autophagosomes; The Th17 cell population decreased, while the Treg cell population increased by 93%. In addition, dual effect mitochondrial renewal therapy has shown significant therapeutic potential. It can serve as a new platform for treating RA and other mitochondrial dysfunction. Combined with autophagy targeted chimeric 4 (AUTAC4) mitochondrial transplantation, it can selectively destroy dysfunctional mitochondria, while activating M1 macrophages targeted in RA joints to complete mitochondrial transfer by regulating abnormal CGAS-STING1 signaling, thereby improving the success rate of mitochondrial transfer. After mitochondrial transfer occurs, macrophage energy metabolism is restored and reaches a steady state [98,100] (Fig. 5).

Osteoporosis and fractures

Osteoporosis is a systemic metabolic disease characterized by the destruction of bone microstructure and increased bone fragility. Abnormal mitochondrial function in osteoporotic tissues leads to swelling of mitochondria in vascular endothelial cells within bone tissue, resulting in reduced blood flow perfusion in osteoporotic tissues. In addition, mesenchymal stem cells and osteoblasts have impaired aerobic respiration and energy metabolism due to mitochondrial dysfunction [101]. Mitochondrial transfer provides healthy mitochondria for mesenchymal stem cells to improve their quality, thereby rescuing cellular states, making mitochondrial transfer a potential treatment for osteoporosis and fractures.

The in vitro experimental results indicate that mitochondrial transfer from macrophages to MSCs allows metabolic crosstalk to regulate bone homeostasis [52]. Extracellular vesicles derived from brown adipose tissue regulate mitochondrial activity in liver cells; Reduced the levels of TG and FFA in liver cells, while enhancing the osteogenic activity of MC3T3-E1 cells through indirect co culture [102]. In terms of in vivo experiments, in the osteoporosis model of castrated mice, macrophages are mostly M1 macrophages, and the increase of inflammatory factors IL-1 and IL-6 in macrophages is accompanied by an increase in succinate. Under inflammatory conditions, administration of M1 macrophage mitochondria to a mouse model showed varying degrees of bone loss, manifested as reduced bone volume, trabecular volume, and trabecular quantity. At this point, the TNF - α pathway, NF - κ B pathway, and Toll like pathway in mesenchymal stem cells are activated, which are transmitted downstream to the HIF-1 α pathway and promote the secretion of inflammatory cytokine IL-1 β. Through purified mitochondrial therapy, osteoporotic mice showed a rebound in bone mass, manifested by an increase in OPG and osteocalcin content, and a decrease in TRAP positive staining. This indicates that mitochondrial transfer therapy has a clear promoting effect on osteogenesis in osteoporotic mice and inhibits osteoclastogenesis [52]. Similar to this conclusion, the research team successfully transferred mitochondria derived from stem cells into mononuclear macrophages, effectively reducing inflammatory cell infiltration, inhibiting osteoclast maturation, and showing higher relative bone volume in a mouse bone resorption model [103]. There are also studies indicating that osteoporosis is caused by glucocorticoids, and mitochondrial transfer from bone lineage cells to myeloid cells is involved in regulating glucocorticoid induced osteoporosis. Glutathione depletion can alleviate the progression of glucocorticoid induced osteoporosis [104]. In addition to osteoporosis, mitochondrial transfer has been shown to be closely involved in the recovery after traumatic injuries such as fractures and has therapeutic significance. M2 macrophages may reduce oxidative damage at the fracture site by providing mitochondrial sources, clearing reactive oxygen species from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, and inhibiting oxidative stress biological activity during the healing process [105]. In addition, in the skin near the fracture, platelets release extracellular vesicles containing mitochondrial genetic material during the coagulation inflammatory response stage. Mitochondria derived from platelets are captured by endothelial mesenchymal stem cells, leading to the secretion of angiogenic factors into the vascular microenvironment to promote wound healing response. Promote coagulation and early inflammation progression of fractures, creating an environment for microvascular formation [106].

At present, multiple studies have proposed new delivery methods for mitochondrial delivery systems in the treatment of osteoporosis: (1) In an elderly osteoporosis model, KGM-PEG-SPION functionalized scaffolds reshape the immune niche and promote bone formation by combining organelle quality control with environmentally adapted mitochondrial transfer [107]. (2) Artificial cell microspheres(Fmito@ACs)Containing fetal mouse mesenchymal stem cell mitochondria improves aging of elderly BMSCs and promotes osteogenesis by enhancing mitochondrial fusion and aerobic glycolysis. And its good biocompatibility was validated in a rat model of osteoporosis [108]. (3) After mouse fracture, GAD/Ag pIO hydrogel scaffold was used to play an immunomodulatory role, eliminate the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species in macrophages and promote the differentiation of M2 macrophages; When anti-inflammatory macrophages differentiate, GAD/Ag pIO further promotes mitochondrial transfer from M2 macrophages to the site of fracture injury. Mitochondria provided by M2 macrophages are transferred to BMSCs, promoting their osteogenic differentiation and increasing the expression of ALP, Runx2, OPN, and OCN; Accelerate fracture healing speed by 88% [105]. (4) Extracellular vesicles derived from stem cells promote the intercellular transfer of functional mitochondria to osteoblasts, resulting in three key therapeutic effects: restoration of oxidative phosphorylation ability; Upregulation of osteogenic transcription factors and osteocalcin; Enhance bone matrix deposition and mineralization [109,110] (Fig. 5).

Therapeutic role of mitochondrial transfer in multiple myeloma and osteosarcoma

Multiple myeloma is a hematologic malignancy with oxidative energy supply mainly in the form of glycolysis. When BMSC transfers mitochondria to myeloma cells, changes in mitochondrial composition and function alter the metabolic pattern of myeloma cells from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation. Mitochondrial transfer in myeloma can be accomplished by TNTs and partial cell fusion. In a recent study tumor cells transferred MSC-derived mitochondria into myeloma cells by means of TNT, and this process was closely linked to CD38, and the transfer of mitochondria to primary multiple myeloma was significantly reduced after the use of a CD38 blocking antibody [33]. The CD38 antibody Daratumumab has emerged as an anti-myeloma agent, the mechanism of which is, on the one hand, to attenuate myeloma cell proliferation and survival by inhibiting protective myeloma cell-stromal cell interactions, inhibiting the formation of nanotubes, and decreasing the transfer of mitochondria from stromal cells to myeloma cells. On the other hand promotes complement dependent cytotoxicity (CDC), antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) [111].

Platelet-derived mitochondrial transfer stimulates osteosarcoma metastasis to distant sites such as the lungs. Platelets in osteosarcoma undergo transfer of mitochondria to osteosarcoma cells, and tumor cells that acquire mitochondria are reprogrammed to a metastatic state, drive metabolic shifts in cancer cells and initiate oxidative glutathione accumulation, and regulate the GSH/GSSG ratio and reactive oxygen species (ROS) concentration in osteosarcoma cells. The PINK1/Parkin pathway is involved in regulating mitochondrial transfer between platelets and osteosarcoma cells. Activation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway promotes Mnf2 phospho-ubiquitination, resulting in dissociation of Mnf2 from the endoplasmic reticulum and ultimately in Mnf2 degradation. Mitochondrial transfer is inhibited at this point due to reduced levels of expression of proteins that mediate mitochondrial movement. Direct impairment of platelet mitochondrial function has been shown to be an effective way to impair metastasis, and from another perspective impairing mitochondrial metastatic pathways (e.g., disruption of Mnf2 protein or activation of the PINK/Parkin pathway) is one of the potential therapeutic options to inhibit osteosarcoma metastasis, which deserves to be explored and validated by more in-depth studies [112]. The strategy of mitochondrial transfer and mitochondrial transplantation technology system in the treatment of bone tissue diseases

As mentioned earlier, to restore mitochondrial dysfunction in bone tissue diseases, current experimental studies have explored mitochondrial transfer and mitochondrial transplantation treatment methods to restore the recipient's mitochondrial function. From in vitro cell studies to animal studies, there are numerous studies that can support the therapeutic effect of restoring mitochondrial function on bone diseases such as OA, RA, and osteoporosis. There are substantial differences between mitochondrial transfer and mitochondrial transplantation in terms of methodology, biocompatibility, ethical significance, and clinical translatability. Mitochondrial transfer refers to the endogenous intercellular movement of mitochondria through physiological channels (such as TNT, EV or cell fusion). Mitochondrial transplantation is a strategy of extracting functional mitochondria from normal cells, and exogenous delivery of mitochondria to target tissues by microinjection, nano carrier encapsulation or hydrogel-based loading. Overall, compared to traditional mitochondrial transfer methods, mitochondrial transplantation has better controllability, stability, and effectiveness. However, there are still many unresolved technical barriers in mitochondrial transplantation technology. Challenges and countermeasures in the separation, purification, storage, and transportation of mitochondria

Mitochondrial transplantation (MT) is a process primarily involving the separation, delivery, and internalization of mitochondria into target cells or tissues. In this context, the origin of mitochondria is the main concern. MSCs, as excellent candidates in the field of cell therapy, are a good choice for transferring functional mitochondria to myeloid and chondrocytes. However, even if MCS amplification is selected from individuals of the same race to extract mitochondria, there are still ethical and immune reactivity issues associated with allogeneity. In addition to the issue of mitochondrial origin, there are still many challenges that need to be discussed after mitochondrial purification: 1. Mitochondrial activity and dosage concentration; 2. The integrity of mitochondrial function and membrane structure, as well as immune response issues caused by membrane damage; 3. Weakened efficacy or even potential carcinogenic potential due to changes in mitochondrial epigenetics (mitochondrial preparations may lead to mtDNA mutations over time); 4. After the maturity of mitochondrial transplantation technology and industrialization of mitochondrial preparation, the difficulty in controlling the quality of mitochondrial products due to uneven donor sources; 5. The storage and transportation of mitochondrial products have adverse effects on the quality of mitochondrial preparations [116].

Centrifugal method is currently the most mainstream method, and differential centrifugation refers to first removing intact cells from tissues through low-speed centrifugation, and then performing high-speed centrifugation to concentrate mitochondria and isolate high-purity mitochondria [118]. Ultrafiltration also uses a similar principle, relying on multiple centrifugation steps to remove cell debris and purify mitochondria. Non synaptic and synaptic mitochondria can be separated by density gradient [119]. The centrifugal method, as an early applied approach, has the disadvantage of being time-consuming. Preble et al. proposed that by first passing the solution to be separated through a 5-40 μ m filtration device and then combining it with high-speed centrifugation to separate and purify mitochondria, the separation and purification time of centrifugation can be effectively reduced. Differential filtration significantly reduces the time cost of mitochondrial separation and purification, which is also beneficial for functional testing of mitochondrial preparations [118]. McCully et al. constructed an effective separation program by applying a filter for separating mitochondria and successfully commercialized it [120]. Adlimoghaddam et al. improved the time-consuming centrifugation step by rapidly separating and purifying mitochondria using three different direct filters combined with centrifugation [121]. In addition, physical stimuli such as magnetic stimulation have also been applied to mitochondrial isolation and have been commercialized [122]. It is worth noting that there are corresponding techniques for isolating MSCs to provide high-quality mitochondria. Yang et al. proposed using rapidly expanded clones (REC) [123] and screening ultrapure MSCs with two different antibodies, which have genetic stability, ultrapurity, and high uniformity [124,125]. Some studies have suggested that after isolating and purifying mitochondria, mtDNA content, Western blotting, ATP production, and OCR can be used to evaluate the purity and function of receptor tissues/cells [126,127].

The storage and transportation of separated mitochondrial products also have an impact on mitochondrial transfer therapy. Long term storage of mitochondrial products requires the long-term stability of mitochondrial preparations, and the current mainstream methods are refrigeration or freezing storage. Yamaguchi et al. [43]developed a method of storing isolated mitochondria in an improved buffer containing trehalose as a cryoprotectant. The mitochondria stored in this solution retained the integrity of the outer mitochondrial membrane [128], and compared to fresh mitochondria, frozen stored mitochondria effectively reduced energy consumption but still retained the ability to open the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) [129].

Appropriate mitochondrial purification methods and storage and transportation methods ensure that mitochondrial transplantation therapy can use high-purity and functionally feasible mitochondria, improving patient safety.

The delivery process of mitochondrial transplantation is crucial for the therapeutic effect

Exogenous and isolated mitochondria have been successfully delivered to recipient cells in various in vitro and in vivo models. The traditional method is to use autologous mitochondria, which are isolated from healthy cells and injected into somatic cells through microscopic manipulation by hiding mitochondria and their genetic material mtDNA in healthy cells as mitochondrial carriers. This method has a low fusion rate between mitochondria and their genetic material and receptor cells, and the therapeutic effect is not ideal [130]. Existing research has shown that local injection or microinjection has been applied in animal models such as rats and mice, but still faces the problem of low delivery efficiency [97,130]. The therapeutic effect of mitochondrial transplantation is influenced by several factors: 1. The functional integrity of mitochondrial preparations determines the quality of mitochondria, and even damaged mitochondria may cause immune reactions; The specific timing of mitochondrial transplantation is difficult to control in different pathological processes of diseases, and there is no consensus on its clinical application in bone tissue diseases; 3. The poor targeting of mitochondria after transplantation to the recipient tissue results in insufficient effective concentration of functional mitochondria in the damaged area; 4. When mitochondria fuse with target receptor cells, the receptor cells have insufficient ability to uptake mitochondria; 5. There may be mitochondrial escape or fusion failure during the process of mitochondrial fusion; 6. The microenvironment of aging receptor cells undergoes changes, and environmental factors become one of the important factors that potentially affect mitochondrial function [117].

In order to solve the difficulties of mitochondrial transplantation mentioned above, previous studies have shown that besides direct injection into local tissues, methods such as photothermal and magnetic transfer can also be used. The cell penetration peptide method can also promote the success rate of mitochondrial transplantation, but the results show that these methods are not satisfactory. On the one hand, invasive procedures cannot be accepted by patients, and on the other hand, there are still significant issues with the targeting of mitochondrial transplantation assisted by these methods [131,132].

Application of mitochondrial membrane proteins and mitochondrial transfer-related proteins in promoting mitochondrial transfer

Unlike various emerging technology-assisted mitochondrial transplantation delivery techniques, which primarily aim to ensure the stability and fusion efficiency of mitochondrial preparations, the regulation of mitochondrial membrane proteins and transfer-related proteins is intended to directly enhance the incidence of endogenous mitochondrial transfer.

The full-length Cx43 (encoded by GJA1) and its truncated internal translation isoform GJA1-20k have been implicated in mitochondrial transfer between highly oxidative cells. Cx43 is enriched at the tips of actin-based cellular extensions containing mitochondria in MSCs, a localization closely associated with the occurrence of mitochondrial transfer. Studies have shown that Cx43 knockout significantly reduces mitochondrial transfer, whereas overexpression of Cx43 markedly enhances it [94].

Cx43-mediated gap junction channels represent a primary mechanism of mitochondrial transfer in rapidly expanded clones. Through these two key mitochondrial transfer pathways, RECs can transfer a greater number of mitochondria to mitochondrial-deficient cells (ρ0 cells) and significantly restore mitochondrial functional parameters. Hypoxic preconditioning of human BMSCs upregulates Cx43 and Cx32 expression; these BMSCs can then form homologous Cx43 and Cx32 gap junctions with hepatocytes, thereby enhancing gap junction-mediated mitochondrial transfer and alleviating ischemia-reperfusion injury in liver grafts. Additionally, magnetic iron oxide nanoparticle-activated MSCs induce Cx43 expression, thereby promoting mitochondrial translocation.

Furthermore, the Miro1 protein has been demonstrated to play a critical regulatory role in mitochondrial transfer. Miro1 is a core protein involved in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis. Researchers have shown that deletion of Miro1 in bone lineage cells impairs mitochondrial transfer, leading to increased commitment of myeloid cells toward the osteoclast lineage and enhanced bone resorption. In addition, Zhang et al. found that Miro1 enhances mitochondrial transfer from BMSCs to mtCC1-2 cells, potentially by promoting tunneling nanotube formation. However, this study did not investigate the regulatory mechanism by which Miro1 controls TNT formation [149].

Compared with the delivery of mitochondrial transplantation assisted by carrier technology mentioned earlier, there are also reports of promoting mitochondrial transfer simply by regulating TNT EV and other factors. Cx43 GJCs are the main mechanism of mitochondrial transfer in RECs. Through these two key mitochondrial transfer pathways, RECs can transfer more mitochondria to mitochondrial defective (ρ (0)) cells and significantly restore mitochondrial functional parameters [150]. Low oxygen pretreatment of hBMSCs (hypoxia hBMSCs), upregulated Cx43 and Cx32 in hBMSCs can form homologous Cx43 GJs and Cx32 GJs with liver cells, thereby enhancing mitochondrial transfer through gap junctions to alleviate ischemia reperfusion injury in liver graf. Magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (IONP) activated MSCs can induce cx43 expression, thereby promoting mitochondrial transfer [151,152].

Conclusion

With advancements in scientific research, mitochondrial transfer, a recently discovered form of intercellular material exchange, has been found to play a significant role in regulating the oxidative respiratory function, immune function, and inflammatory factor expression of recipient cells, as well as in regulating cell proliferation and differentiation. In various inflammatory bone diseases, bone defects, and bone tumors, mitochondrial function is influenced by mitochondrial transfer. Through studying mitochondrial transfer, researchers have discovered that it may serve as a novel therapeutic approach for treating bone-related diseases. On one hand, intercellular mitochondrial transfer, as a novel repair mechanism, can deliver functional mitochondria to damaged cells via nanotubes or extracellular vesicles, restoring their metabolic activity or regulating the accumulation of oxidative stress products, thereby alleviating cell damage caused by inflammation or oxidative stress. Directly delivering mitochondria purified and differentiated from MSCs and other stem cells to recipient cells has also been shown to have the same therapeutic effect as intercellular mitochondrial transfer. Some teams have attempted to develop mitochondrial transplantation techniques based on MSCs or targeted delivery systems using nanocarriers, providing precise intervention strategies to reverse bone metabolic imbalance. On the other hand, inhibiting intercellular mitochondrial transfer in bone tumor cells using antibody agents to regulate tumor cell metastasis and suppress tumor cell drug resistance also points to a potential direction for future bone tumor disease treatment. Further investigation is needed into the mechanisms of intercellular mitochondrial transfer in bone tissue cells for treating bone tissue diseases, and clinical studies are required to validate their therapeutic efficacy.

Outlook

Mitochondrial transplantation technology, grounded in the principles of intercellular mitochondrial transfer, is advancing rapidly, driven by progress in various biotechnological fields. These developments have helped mitigate certain risks associated with treatment failure during mitochondrial transplantation. This review elaborates on the therapeutic applications of mitochondrial transfer and transplantation techniques in the context of bone tissue diseases. However, to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes, further in-depth investigation is required to elucidate how mitochondria are activated within recipient cells following successful intercellular transfer, and to identify the downstream mechanisms involved.Moreover, although this review briefly describes key molecules such as Cx43 and Miro1, our understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing the distinct intercellular mitochondrial transfer axes remains limited. While various technologies are currently available to assist in mitochondrial targeted delivery, research on the application in this domain is still in its infancy, and their mechanisms of action remain poorly defined.

Despite these challenges, research on mitochondrial transfer and targeted delivery strategies continues to flourish. The encouraging therapeutic effects observed in bone tissue provide new and promising avenues for the treatment of bone tissue diseases.
