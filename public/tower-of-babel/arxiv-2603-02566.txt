Modeling double bounded data based on correlated gamma random variables
Roberto Vila 1 , Felipe Quintino 1 and Marcelo Bourguignon 2 1 Department of Statistics, University of Brasilia, Brasilia, Brazil 2 Department of Statistics, Federal University of Rio Grande do Norte, Natal, RN, Brazil

Abstract
Many types of bounded data defined on the unit interval arise naturally as ratios of the form X / ( X + Y ) X/(X+Y) . In the existing literature, the main statistical models proposed for this type of bounded data typically based on the assumption that the random variables X X and Y Y are independent. However, this assumption is often unrealistic in practical applications, where X X and Y Y tend to be correlated due to shared underlying mechanisms or common sources of variability. In this paper, we overcome such limitations and propose a model in which the marginal distributions of the two components are linked by a copula, leading to a more flexible and realistic representation of unit-interval data. In particular, in the proposed model, X X and Y Y are correlated gamma random variables linked by the Farlie-Gumbel-Morgenstern (FGM) copula, allowing for positive and negative correlations between the components. The mathematical properties and practical applications are rigorously investigated. Depending on the choice of parameters, the new model is very versatile, showing symmetric or asymmetric behavior, as well as unimodality or bimodality. A Monte Carlo simulation study is carried out that shows the good performance of the maximum likelihood estimator in several scenarios of parameter choices. The potential and limitations of efficient likelihood-based computations are also discussed. We evaluate the effectiveness of the new model and its estimates in modeling real-world datasets related to economics.

The need for modeling and analyzing bounded data occurs in

We say that an absolutely continuous random variable

\displaystyle=(1+\rho)\,{z^{\alpha-1}(1-z)^{\beta-1}\over\textrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)}

\displaystyle-{2\rho\Gamma(2\alpha+\beta)\over\alpha\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\,{z^{2\alpha-1}(1-z)^{\beta-1}\over(1+z)^{2\alpha+\beta}}\,_{2}F_{1}\left(1,2\alpha+\beta;1+\alpha;{z\over 1+z}\right)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}}{\frac{x^{n}}{n!}}}

\displaystyle F_{2}(a,b_{1},b_{2};c_{1},c_{2};x,y)=\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}{(a)_{m+n}(b_{1})_{m}(b_{2})_{n}\over(c_{1})_{m}(c_{2})_{n}}\,{x^{m}\over m!}\,{y^{n}\over n!}

\displaystyle f_{{X\over X+Y}}(z)=(1+\rho)f_{1}(z)-\rho f_{2}(z)-\rho f_{3}(z)+\rho f_{4}(z).

\displaystyle f_{Z}(z;\bm{\theta})=f_{{X\over X+Y}}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\pi_{i}f_{i}(z),\quad 0<z<1,

\pi_{2}=\pi_{3}=-\rho

.
In other words, if

,

\displaystyle Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{X\over X+Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\mathds{1}_{\{B=i\}}Z_{i},

where

3.4
The cumulative distribution function

Using the stochastic representation of
Z\sim{\rm EBB}(\bm{\theta})
, given in (
6
), by the Law of total expectation we have (for
s=1/z-1
and
0<z<1
)

\displaystyle F_{Z}(z;\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{P}\left({X\over X+Y}\leqslant z\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(sX\leqslant Y\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\left(Y\leqslant sX\right)

\displaystyle=1-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y\leqslant sX|X=x\right)f_{X}(x){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=1-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{sx}f_{X,Y}(x,y){\rm d}y{\rm d}x.

(29)

Since
(X,Y)^{\top}
has Morgenstern’s bivariate distribution (
7
)-(

\displaystyle F_{Z}(z;\bm{\theta})=1-\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{X}(x)\left\{F_{Y}(sx)+\rho[2F_{X}(x)-1]\left[\int_{0}^{sx}2f_{Y}(y)F_{Y}(y){\rm d}y-F_{Y}(sx)\right]\right\}{\rm d}x.

(30)

As

Y~\sim{\rm Gamma}(\beta,\theta)
, from Proposition
A.1
in Appendix,
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{sx}2f_{Y}(y)F_{Y}(y){\rm d}y={2\theta^{\beta}\over\Gamma^{2}(\beta)}\int_{0}^{sx}y^{\beta-1}\exp(-\theta y)\gamma(\beta,\theta y){\rm d}y=2\,{\gamma(\beta,\theta sx)\over\Gamma(\beta)}-1.

(31)

As

and
Y~\sim{\rm Gamma}(\beta,\theta)

),
F_{Z}(z;\bm{\theta})
in (

\displaystyle F_{Z}(z;\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=1-{\theta^{\alpha}\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}\exp(-\theta x)\left[{\gamma(\beta,\theta sx)\over\Gamma(\beta)}+\rho\left\{2\,{\gamma(\alpha,\theta x)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}-1\right\}\left\{{\gamma(\beta,\theta sx)\over\Gamma(\beta)}-1\right\}\right]{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=1+{(\rho-1)\theta^{\alpha}\over\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}\exp(-\theta x){\gamma(\beta,\theta sx)}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle-{2\rho\theta^{\alpha}\over\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}\exp(-\theta x)\gamma(\alpha,\theta x)\gamma(\beta,\theta sx){\rm d}x

\displaystyle+{2\rho\theta^{\alpha}\over\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}\exp(-\theta x)\gamma(\alpha,\theta x){\rm d}x-{\rho\theta^{\alpha}\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}\exp(-\theta x){\rm d}x.

By using the identities in (

\displaystyle F_{Z}(z;\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=1+{\rho-1\over\beta\textrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)}\,{z^{\alpha}(1-z)^{\beta}}\,_{2}F_{1}\left(1,\alpha+\beta;1+\beta;1-z\right)

\displaystyle-{2\rho\Gamma(2\alpha+\beta)\over\alpha\beta\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\,{z^{2\alpha}(1-z)^{\beta}\over(1+z)^{2\alpha+\beta}}\,F_{2}\left(2\alpha+\beta,1,1;\alpha+1,\beta+1;{z\over 1+z},{1-z\over 1+z}\right).

By using the well-known identity

\displaystyle B_{x}(a,b)={x^{a}(1-x)^{b}\over a}\,_{2}F_{1}(1,a+b;1+a;x),

where

is the incomplete beta function,
we write

as follows (for

\displaystyle F_{Z}(z;\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=1+(\rho-1){I_{1-z}(\beta,\alpha)}

\displaystyle-{2\rho\Gamma(2\alpha+\beta)\over\alpha\beta\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\,{z^{2\alpha}(1-z)^{\beta}\over(1+z)^{2\alpha+\beta}}\,F_{2}\left(2\alpha+\beta,1,1;\alpha+1,\beta+1;{z\over 1+z},{1-z\over 1+z}\right),

(32)

where

.

When

we have (for

3.5
Moment-generating function and moments

Given that
Z\sim{\rm EBB}(\bm{\theta})
has unitary support, its moment-generating function (MGF),
M_{Z}(t)
, and raw moments,
\mu_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, exist and are finite, particularly within an open interval encompassing zero. Then
M_{Z}(t)
may be expanded in a
power series about
0
in the following form

\displaystyle M_{Z}(t)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mu_{n}\over n!}\,t^{n}.

In what follows we derive a closed-form expression for
\mu_{n}
. Indeed, by using the well-known formula for moments of positive order
\mathbb{E}(X^{p})=p\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(X>x){\rm d}x
of positive random variables, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{n}=n\int_{0}^{1}z^{n-1}[1-F_{Z}(z;\bm{\theta})]{\rm d}z,

where
F_{Z}(z;\bm{\theta})
is as in (
3.4
). So, from (
3.4
),

\displaystyle\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=n(1-\rho)\int_{0}^{1}z^{n-1}{I_{1-z}(\beta,\alpha)}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle+{2n\rho\Gamma(2\alpha+\beta)\over\alpha\beta\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\int_{0}^{1}{z^{n+2\alpha-1}(1-z)^{\beta}\over(1+z)^{2\alpha+\beta}}\,F_{2}\left(2\alpha+\beta,1,1;\alpha+1,\beta+1;{z\over 1+z},{1-z\over 1+z}\right){\rm d}z.

(33)

For the particular scenario where
\alpha=\beta
, we obtain
\mu_{1}=1/2
(see Subsection
3.1
). Due to the integral’s complexity in (
3.5
), numerical methods are requisite for its computation.

4
Estimation

The log-likelihood function for
\bm{\theta}=(\alpha,\beta,\rho)^{\top}
based on a sample of
n
independent
observations is given by

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\Bigg[(1+\rho)\frac{z_{i}^{\alpha-1}(1-z_{i})^{\beta-1}}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2\rho\Gamma(2\alpha+\beta)}{\alpha\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\frac{z_{i}^{2\alpha-1}(1-z_{i})^{\beta-1}}{(1+z_{i})^{2\alpha+\beta}}\,_{2}F_{1}\mskip-3.0mu\left(1,2\alpha+\beta;1+\alpha;\frac{z_{i}}{1+z_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2\rho\Gamma(\alpha+2\beta)}{\beta\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma^{2}(\beta)}\frac{z_{i}^{\alpha-1}(1-z_{i})^{2\beta-1}}{(2-z_{i})^{\alpha+2\beta}}\,_{2}F_{1}\mskip-3.0mu\left(1,\alpha+2\beta;1+\beta;\frac{1-z_{i}}{2-z_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{4^{1-\alpha-\beta}\rho\Gamma(2\alpha+2\beta)}{\alpha\beta\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)\Gamma^{2}(\beta)}z_{i}^{2\alpha-1}(1-z_{i})^{2\beta-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times F_{2}\mskip-3.0mu\left(2\alpha+2\beta,1,1;\alpha+1,\beta+1;\frac{z_{i}}{2},\frac{1-z_{i}}{2}\right)\Bigg].

(34)

The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE)

\bm{\widehat{\theta}}=(\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\beta},\widehat{\rho})^{\top}
of
\bm{\theta}=(\alpha,\beta,\rho)^{\top}

is obtained by the maximization of the log-likelihood function (
4
). However, it is
not possible to derive an analytical solution for the MLE

\bm{\widehat{\theta}}
; therefore, we must require
a numerical solution using some optimization algorithm such as Newton–Raphson
and quasi-Newton. But, computations of likelihood
\ell(\bm{\theta})
can be extremely costly due to the dependence of PDF (
2
) and CDF (
3.4
) on special functions such as the Gauss hypergeometric
{}_{2}F_{1}
and Appell
F_{2}
. Moreover, optimization methods may not converge properly.

Thus, our strategy is as follows: given a random sample from
(X,Y)
, we first fit the margins as
\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,1)
and
\operatorname{Gamma}(\beta,1)
, and then estimate the dependence parameter
\rho
in a second stage.
Thus, comparing
X
and
Y
through
Z=X/(X+Y)
, we obtain a natural estimator for the parameters of
Z
.
Naturally, we can first use the estimates from the
\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
model and then estimate
\rho
in a second stage, thus improving the initial Beta fit. The algorithm is summarized in the two steps below:

1.

Given a random sample
\mathbf{Z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
, use the ML estimators of the Beta model to obtain
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})=(\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{Z}),\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{Z}))
;

2.

estimate
\rho
using an appropriate optimization algorithm.

These steps are effective in scenarios where the Beta model provides a good initial fit to the data.

Taking into account the second stage, the estimation of
\rho
,

•

if
Z
is observed directly in
[0,1]
, we can use

\widehat{\rho}\in\arg\max_{\rho\in[0,1]}\ell(\rho),

(35)

where
\ell(\rho):=\ell(\bm{\theta})\mid_{(\alpha,\beta)=(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})}
;

•

if
Z=\frac{X}{X+Y}
, then, using Theorem 2 in
7
, we obtain the estimator

\widehat{\rho}=\frac{\widehat{\operatorname{corr}}(X,Y)\hat{\sigma}_{x}\hat{\sigma}_{y}}{\hat{\beta}_{x}\hat{\beta}_{y}},

(36)

where
\sigma_{x}=\operatorname{Var}X
,
\sigma_{y}=\operatorname{Var}Y
,
\beta_{x}=\int_{0}^{\infty}G(x;\alpha,1)\left[1-G(x;\alpha,1)\right]dx
,
and

\beta_{y}=\int_{0}^{\infty}G(x;\beta,1)\left[1-G(x;\beta,1)\right]dx,

in which
G(\cdot;\theta,1)
denotes the CDF of a Gamma model with shape
\theta
and scale 1.

5.1

Define the bivariate function
C:[0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
as

C(u_{1},u_{2})=u_{1}u_{2}\left[1+\rho(1-u_{1})(1-u_{2})\right],~~(u_{1},u_{2})\in[0,1]\times[0,1]~\mbox{and}~-1\leq\rho\leq 1.

(37)

Note that the bivariate distribution function
F_{X,Y}(x,y)
given in (

with margins Gamma CDFs
(F_{X}(x),F_{Y}(y))

X\sim Gamma(\alpha,\theta)
and

. That is,

F_{X,Y}(x,y)=C(F_{X}(x),F_{Y}(y)),~~x,y>0.

Aiming to simulate a random sample

from the extended Beta PDF (
2
), we use the Algorithm
1
, which simulates

Concluding remarks

In this paper, we introduced the EBB distribution, a new unit-supported model obtained from the ratio of two correlated Gamma random variables following a Morgenstern-type dependence structure. The additional dependence parameter extends the classical Beta distribution while preserving interpretability. As shown throughout the paper, this extra flexibility allows the EBB family to accommodate a wide range of shapes, including symmetry, U-and J-shapes, and bimodality, which are often observed in applied problems but poorly captured by traditional unit models.
Closed-form expressions were obtained for the probability density and cumulative distribution functions and moments, relying on tools from special-function theory. Estimation was addressed using likelihood-based and alternative methods, and a Monte Carlo study highlighted both the finite-sample behavior of the estimators and the computational challenges that may arise. Applications to real datasets demonstrated that the EBB distribution can provide improved fits compared to competing models, despite requiring only one additional parameter.
Several directions remain open. Extensions to regression frameworks for responses to
(0,1)
, Bayesian inference with informative priors, and multivariate generalizations are natural avenues for future research. Another promising line involves studying robustness properties and developing faster numerical strategies for likelihood maximization in large samples.
In general, the EBB model adds a versatile and analytically tractable option to the family of unit distributions, with potential for broad application in economics, reliability, hydrology, and related fields.
