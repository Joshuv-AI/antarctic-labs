Awareness and misconceptions of palliative care among paid, unpaid, and non-caregivers in the U.S.
Authors: Zhigang Xie, Young-Rock Hong, Jiaming Liang, Jumana Akter, Jiamin Hu, Carla Fisher, Diana Wilkie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPalliative care improves quality of life for individuals with serious illnesses; however, misconceptions about it remain widespread. Caregivers, both paid professionals and unpaid family members, serve as critical intermediaries in healthcare decision-making for these patients. Little is known about differences in understanding of palliative care among non-caregivers, paid caregivers, and unpaid informal caregivers.MethodsWe analyzed nationally representative data from the 2024 Health Information National Trends Survey. Survey design-adjusted Chi-square tests and multivariable logistic regression models were used to compare palliative care awareness and misconceptions across groups with different caregiving experiences.ResultsA total of 5,835 respondents (representing 217,985,076 U.S. adults) were included in the final analysis. Overall, 45.3% of non-caregivers, 55.9% of paid caregivers, and 61.9% of unpaid caregivers (p < .001) reported palliative care awareness. Awareness was highest among caregivers who cared for parents only (65.7%) or for patients with cancer (76.3%). In adjusted analyses, both paid (aOR, 2.88; 95% CI, 1.56–5.33) and unpaid (aOR, 2.34; 95% CI, 1.68–3.27) caregivers had significantly higher odds of awareness compared with non-caregivers. Among those aware of palliative care, paid caregivers were most likely to hold the misconception that palliative care requires stopping other treatments (48.3% vs. 20.8% unpaid vs. 20.1% non-caregivers; p = .041).ConclusionsCaregivers exhibit significantly greater palliative care awareness than non-caregivers, though paid caregivers report more misconceptions. Awareness also varies by caregiving relationship and disease type. These findings underscore the need for targeted educational strategies to enhance accurate understanding of palliative care across caregiving groups.Keywords: Palliative care, Awareness, Misconception, Caregiver

Introduction

Palliative care is a multidisciplinary approach that seeks to improve the quality of life for individuals with serious or life-limiting illnesses by addressing physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs [1]. Although palliative care is often associated with, or confused with, hospice and end-of-life care, it is appropriate throughout the course of illness and can be delivered concurrently with disease-directed treatmen [1]. National and international organizations, including the World Health Organization, the National Consensus Project for Quality Palliative Care and the European Association for Palliative Care, have emphasized integrating palliative services earlier in the disease trajectory for conditions such as cancer, dementia, and advanced heart failure [1–4]. Despite this growing recognition, public awareness and understanding of palliative care remain limited in the United States (U.S.) [5–8], with significant implications for the approximately 13 million adults living with serious illness and their caregivers [9].

The caregiver population has expanded dramatically, with current estimates indicating 63 million family caregivers in the US contributing approximately $600 billion in unpaid care annually [10–12]. Caregivers play a critical role in the healthcare decision-making and day-to-day management of patients with serious illness [13, 14]. Both paid caregivers (e.g., home health aides, nursing assistants) and unpaid informal caregivers (e.g., family members or friends) are often directly involved in care coordination, treatment adherence, and communication with healthcare providers [13, 14]. Because caregivers frequently serve as intermediaries between patients and the healthcare system, their understanding of available care options (including palliative care) is essential for informed decision-making [15]. However, whereas previous studies have focused on general public awareness of palliative care [5–8], few have addressed how such awareness varies by caregiving status or type of caregiver focusing on the population in U. S. [16, 17]. This gap is concerning, given that caregivers often make or influence critical healthcare decisions during patients’ most vulnerable periods.

Existing evidence suggests that caregiving experience may influence individuals’ exposure to and understanding of palliative care concepts [18]. Unpaid caregivers, often family members providing long-term support for aging relatives or those with chronic illness, may develop awareness through direct encounters with healthcare teams [18, 19]. Paid caregivers such as health aides, by contrast, may receive structured training but vary widely in their familiarity with the broader goals and philosophy of palliative care [20]. Importantly, misconceptions persist among both the general public and healthcare workers, particularly the false belief that palliative care is synonymous with hospice care or that accepting palliative care requires discontinuing curative treatment [8]. Such misconceptions can deter timely referrals and prevent patients from receiving supportive services that could improve their quality of life.

The Health Information National Trends Survey (HINTS) provides a unique opportunity to assess nationally representative, population-level awareness and attitudes toward palliative care. The 2018 HINTS (HINTS 5, Cycle 2) was the first wave in recent years to include questions on palliative care knowledge. Analyses of these data documented generally low awareness of palliative care among U.S. adults, with substantial variation across sociodemographic subgroups. For example, 71% of participants reported no knowledge of palliative care in 2018, while women, older adults, and non-Hispanic White individuals demonstrated significantly greater awareness compared with men, younger adults, and racial and ethnic minority groups, respectively [5–7]. Given the rapid expansion and integration of palliative care services in the U.S. over the past several years, along with the significant effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on end-of-life care discussions [21–23], an updated assessment using the most recent 2024 HINTS (Cycle 7) data is essential to understand the current state of public awareness. Building on prior work [5–7], this study assessed awareness and misconceptions of palliative care among non-caregivers, paid caregivers, and unpaid informal caregivers. We hypothesized that both paid and unpaid caregivers would exhibit higher awareness of palliative care compared with non-caregivers, and the misconception about palliative care replacing curative treatment would be more prevalent among non-caregivers. We further explored whether caregiving characteristics, including relationship to care recipient and type of illness cared for, were associated with variations in palliative care awareness.

Methods

Data source and study population

We analyzed data from the 2024 HINTS 7, a nationally representative cross-sectional survey of U.S. adults aged 18 years and older conducted by the National Cancer Institute [24]. HINTS is designed to assess the public’s knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors related to health communication, healthcare access, and awareness of emerging health issues [24]. Comprehensive details regarding the survey’s sampling design, weighting, and methodology have been published elsewhere [24]. Data for HINTS 7 were collected between March 25 and September 16, 2024 [24]. Participants completed the survey either online or by mail, with both modes available in English and Spanish (27.3% response rate) [24]. The analytic sample included individuals with complete responses to questions on palliative care awareness and caregiving experience. Respondents with missing information on other covariates were excluded using a listwise deletion approach. After exclusions, the final analytic sample comprised 5,835 respondents representing an estimated 217,985,076 U.S. adults. Because this study used publicly available, de-identified secondary data, the University of Florida Institutional Review Board determined that it was non-human subjects research and was therefore exempt from review; as such, human ethics and consent to participate declarations was not applicable. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of interest was awareness of palliative care, which was assessed using the following question: “How would you describe your level of knowledge about palliative care? I have never heard of it; I know a little bit about palliative care; I know what palliative care is and could explain it to someone else.” Consistent with prior studies, self-reported responses were dichotomized to indicate awareness if the participant selected either “I know a little bit about palliative care” or “I know what palliative care is and could explain it to someone else.” Respondents selecting “I have never heard of it” were classified as having no awareness, allowing us to distinguish respondents with any awareness or familiarity with palliative care from those reporting no awareness [5, 6]. The secondary outcome of interest was misconception about palliative care. Among respondents who reported awareness of palliative care, misconceptions were measured using the statement: “If you accept palliative care, you must stop other treatment, with response options of strongly agree, somewhat agree, somewhat disagree, strongly disagree, or I do not know.” Following prior HINTS analyses and conceptual frameworks of misconception endorsement, responses of “strongly agree” or “somewhat agree” were coded as presence of the misconception, and responses of “strongly disagree” or “somewhat disagree” were coded as no misconception. Responses of “I don’t know” were treated as reflecting uncertainty rather than endorsement or rejection of the misconception and were therefore excluded from the regression analyses [5, 6].

Independent variable

Caregiving experience was determined from a series of separate survey questions regarding whether respondents had ever provided care or assistance to a friend or family member with a health problem or disability, and HINTS further collected whether they were compensated for their caregiving work among those who self-identified as caregivers. Based on responses, participants were categorized as mutually exclusive non-caregivers (those without caregiving experience), unpaid caregivers (those who provided informal or family caregiving without pay), and paid caregivers (those who provided caregiving as part of their paid occupation [e.g., home health aides, nursing assistants]) regardless their relationships with the care recipients [25, 26]. To capture nuances in caregiving experiences, the caregiver’s relationship to the care recipient (e.g., parent, spouse or partner) and the care recipient’s primary health condition (e.g., cancer, multiple conditions) were included in secondary descriptive analyses.

Covariates

Caregiver sociodemographic and health-related characteristics were included as covariates based on prior literature [5–7]. These included age (years), sex (male, female), marital status (married/partnered, not married), race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non-Hispanic other), educational attainment (less than high school, high school, some college, college graduate or higher), annual household income (<$35k, $35k-<$75k, ≥$75k), Census region (Northeast, Midwest, South, West), metro area ( big metro, small metro, rural), and health insurance status(insured, uninsured). Health-related covariates included history of cancer diagnosis (yes, no), number of comorbid conditions (0, 1, 2–5), and self-rated health status (excellent/very good, good, fair/poor).

Statistical analysis

All analyses accounted for the complex sampling design and survey weights to produce nationally representative estimates of the U.S. adult population [24]. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc). Two-sided p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. First, we described respondents’ sociodemographic and health characteristics by caregiver status using chi-square tests. Next, we estimated the prevalence of palliative care awareness across caregiver groups and other subpopulations. Multivariable logistic regression models were then used to calculate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between caregiver status and palliative care awareness, controlling for sociodemographic and health-related covariates. The same analytic approach was applied to examine the prevalence of palliative care misconceptions and their associations with caregiver status, with responses of “unknown” treated as missing in the regression models. Finally, we estimated the prevalence of palliative care awareness stratified by the caregiver’s relationship to the care recipient and the care recipient’s primary health condition. Misconceptions were not further examined in this secondary analysis owing to small cell sizes.

Results

Among 5,835 respondents representing 217,985,076 U.S. adults, 3.1% identified as paid caregivers and 15.4% as unpaid caregivers. Participant characteristics differed significantly by caregiver status (Table 1). Paid caregivers were younger and more likely to be female, belong to racial/ethnic minority groups, be unmarried, had lower educational attainment, reported poorer health, and had more chronic conditions than non-caregivers or unpaid caregivers. Specifically, nearly three-quarters (74.5%) of paid caregivers were aged 18 to 49 years compared with 52.2% of non-caregivers and 53.4% of unpaid caregivers (p=.017). Women comprised 66.4% of paid caregivers and 60.0% of unpaid caregivers compared with 45.1% of non-caregivers (p<.001). Educational attainment was lowest among paid caregivers, with only 19.5% reporting a college degree or higher (p=.015). Paid caregivers were more often unmarried (56.7%) than unpaid caregivers (34.3%) or non-caregivers (43.6%; p = .006). Paid caregivers were less likely to have health insurance (82.3%; p=.050) and were less likely to report excellent or very good health (32.2% vs. 48.3% of non-caregivers; p=.020), although they had the highest percentage of multiple chronic conditions (42.9%; p=.030).

Table 2 presents the prevalence and predictors of palliative care awareness. Overall, 45.3% of non-caregivers, 55.9% of paid caregivers, and 61.9% of unpaid caregivers reported knowing at least a little about palliative care (p<.001). In adjusted models, both paid (aOR, 2.88; 95% CI, 1.56–5.33) and unpaid (aOR, 2.34; 95% CI, 1.68–3.27) caregivers had significantly higher odds of awareness compared with non-caregivers, with no significant difference between paid and unpaid caregivers (aOR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.43–1.54; data not shown). Greater awareness was also observed among adults aged 50–64 years compared with those aged 18–49 years (aOR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.07–1.75) and among females compared with males (aOR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.54–0.80). Awareness was lower among non-Hispanic Black (aOR, 0.54; 95% CI, 0.41–0.72), Hispanic (aOR, 0.44; 95% CI, 0.34–0.57), and other race/ethnicity groups (aOR, 0.40; 95% CI, 0.29–0.56) compared with non-Hispanic White adults. Individuals with a college degree or higher had substantially greater odds of awareness compared with those with less than a high school education (aOR, 6.24; 95% CI, 3.90–9.98). In contrast, respondents residing in rural areas (aOR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.51–0.95, vs. large metropolitan areas) and those reporting fair or poor health (aOR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.54–0.98, vs. excellent/very good health) demonstrated lower levels of awareness.

Prevalence and predictors of palliative care awareness among U.S. adults

Among respondents who were aware of palliative care, misconceptions about its meaning also varied by caregiver status (Table 3). Nearly half of paid caregivers (48.3%) believed that accepting palliative care meant discontinuing other treatments, compared with 20.8% of unpaid caregivers and 20.1% of non-caregivers (p=.004). In adjusted analyses, paid caregivers had significantly higher odds of endorsing this misconception compared with non-caregivers (aOR, 2.67; 95% CI, 1.09–6.50), whereas unpaid caregivers did not differ significantly from non-caregivers (aOR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.67–1.50). Misconceptions were less prevalent among individuals with higher education (college degree vs. < high school: aOR, 0.30; 95% CI, 0.13–0.71) and higher income (≥$75,000 vs. < $35,000: aOR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.30–0.89) when compared to their respective counterparts.

The highest awareness was observed among caregivers providing care to parents (65.7%), followed closely by those caring for a spouse or partner (65.3%) and those involved in multiple caregiving relationships (64.7%). Discussion

In this nationally representative sample of U.S. adults, 45.3% of non-caregivers, 55.9% of paid caregivers, and 61.9% of unpaid caregivers reported knowing at least a little about palliative care in 2024, representing a 15%–30% increase compared with estimates of the general US population from 2018 [5–7]. However, among those who self-reported awareness of palliative care, paid caregivers were most likely to endorse the misconception that accepting palliative care means stopping other treatments (48.3% vs. 20.8% among unpaid caregivers and 20.1% among non-caregivers). This finding contradicts our hypothesis and raises concern given the persistently high levels of both unawareness and misconceptions across all three groups. Furthermore, awareness varied across caregiving contexts, being highest among those caring for parents only (65.7%) and for patients with cancer (76.3%). As these findings are based on a nationally representative sample, these patterns have important clinical implications nationwide, highlighting opportunities to promote targeted educational interventions within clinical settings.

The greater awareness of palliative care in caregivers likely reflects caregivers’ greater exposure to healthcare systems and serious illness management, which provides opportunities to encounter palliative services and related discussions [13, 14]. Furthermore, a recent study shows providing end-of-life care support to relatives is associated with higher end-of-life health literacy [18]. Caregiving experiences therefore represent an important touchpoint for increasing palliative care literacy, offering opportunities to disseminate accurate information during routine healthcare interactions. Additionally, as palliative care becomes more integrated into primary care and home care settings [27, 28], primary care providers are increasingly expected to deliver palliative care services due to rising needs and a nationwide shortage of palliative care specialists [29, 30]. Strengthening primary care providers’ competencies in palliative care may also help disseminate accurate knowledge to the public [31, 32]. Studies testing the COMFORT model further show the important role of nurses in communicating with caregivers about palliative care early in the illness experience or treatment trajectory as well as in providing palliative care training to clinical teams [29]. Wittenberg and colleagues have shown that nurses play a lead role in promoting palliative care awareness and understanding and can promote families’ access to such services when they have key communication skills that help them engage the family caregiver in such discussions [33].

Despite increased awareness of palliative care over the past few years, misconceptions remain prevalent. This finding is consistent with prior research documenting ongoing conceptual gaps in public understanding between palliative and hospice care [17, 34]. Such misunderstandings may contribute to broader societal resistance to palliative care, as many individuals continue to associate it exclusively with imminent death or end-of-life care rather than supportive care focused on symptom management, quality of life, and care coordination across the disease trajectory. Within an increasingly fragmented healthcare system, these misconceptions may further contribute to delayed referrals or underutilization of palliative care services, limiting opportunities for timely symptom management, advance care planning, psychosocial support, and care coordination, thereby increasing preventable suffering for both patients and caregivers.

Notably, this study found that paid caregivers had a substantially higher misconception rate than both non-caregivers and unpaid caregivers, a finding not reported in previous studies. The higher prevalence of misconceptions among paid caregivers may reflect their comparatively lower educational attainment and income levels in this sample, which are associated with reduced health literacy, consistent with prior studies [20, 35]. Even more concerning, our data suggest that many paid caregivers hold “confident misconceptions”, as only 6% responded “I do not know” to the misconception item. Such misplaced confidence may lead to low quality of care provided to patients [31].

Additionally, the elevated rate of misconceptions among paid caregivers may reflect the environments in which they commonly work. Many paid caregivers operate in under-resourced settings where palliative care teams are introduced only late in the illness trajectory, often while curative treatments are being discontinued (e.g., hospice). As a result, their professional experiences may inadvertently reinforce the belief that palliative care is synonymous with end-of-life care. Workforce training limitations may further contribute to this issue, as many paid caregivers receive task-focused instruction with limited exposure to interdisciplinary care models or the broader philosophy of palliative care [36, 37]. In contrast, unpaid family caregivers might gain a more accurate understanding through direct engagement with healthcare teams [38]. Given the 2025 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule Final Rule, which establishes new coding and reimbursement for caregiver training services (including delivery via telehealth), these payment mechanisms offer a timely opportunity to integrate structured palliative care education into professional caregiver training [39]. Such efforts could meaningfully reduce misconceptions and better equip the caregiving workforce to support informed decision-making among patients and families.

Awareness of palliative care differed notably by the caregiver’s relationship to the care recipient and the recipient’s primary health condition. Caregivers providing care to parents demonstrated the highest level of awareness, followed by those caring for a spouse or partner, suggesting that generational caregiving roles may influence exposure to and understanding of palliative care. As caregivers participate in the decision-making with parents, a spouse, or a partner, they may have access to information often shared with the patient about the goals of palliative care or other care models. Consistent with prior research [6], this study also found that individuals aged 50–64 years reported greater awareness compared to both younger and older age groups. Middle aged caregivers supporting their parents may be more likely to engage with the healthcare system with well-established palliative care integration (e.g., hospice and skilled nursing facilities) on behalf of older parents with complex or life-limiting illnesses, leading to greater opportunities for palliative care discussions. Similarly, caregivers for patients with cancer reported the highest awareness, consistent with prior studies showing that cancer care settings have historically integrated palliative care earlier and more systematically than other specialties [40–42]. These findings underscore the importance of extending palliative care education and integration beyond oncology, particularly to conditions such as dementia or chronic mental illness, where palliative approaches remain underutilized [43, 44].

When further stratified by paid and unpaid caregiver status, differences in awareness emerged across disease contexts. Although no significant differences were observed between paid and unpaid caregivers within specific caregiver–patient relationship groups, paid caregivers demonstrated significantly higher awareness than unpaid caregivers when caring for patients with mental illnesses. This may reflect greater professional training or workplace exposure among paid caregivers working in institutional or behavioral health settings [45]. Conversely, unpaid caregivers reported significantly higher awareness than paid caregivers when caring for recipients with multiple chronic conditions, possibly due to their more sustained and family-centered involvement in navigating complex care needs [46, 47]. Together, these findings highlight that both relationship dynamics and care context shape caregivers’ familiarity with palliative care, suggesting that outreach and education strategies should be tailored to the unique informational needs of different caregiver populations.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, in the 2024 HINTS dataset, palliative care awareness was assessed using a single self-reported item, which may may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of the construct or the actual knowledge. Additionally, the 2024 survey included only one question addressing misconceptions, whereas the 2018 survey contained multiple items assessing understanding of palliative care goals and common misconceptions, allowing for a more comprehensive evaluation. Despite this limitation, the retained misconception item “accepting palliative care means stopping other treatments” represents one of the most critical misunderstandings influencing decisions to initiate palliative care. Prior research also indicates that related misconception domains are correlated, suggesting that the single item meaningfully captures the broader construct of palliative care awareness [48]. Second, due to small cell sizes, we were unable to estimate the prevalence of palliative care misconceptions stratified by the caregiver’s relationship to the care recipient and the recipient’s primary health condition. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples should explore these subgroup differences. Additionally, the small sample size of paid caregivers may have limited the statistical power for certain subgroup comparisons. Finally, potential misclassification between paid and unpaid caregiving status cannot be ruled out due to recall bias and overlapping caregiving roles.

Conclusions

Although awareness and misconceptions have improved modestly since 2018, both remain important areas for targeted education within the U.S. adult population. Both paid and unpaid caregivers exhibit significantly greater awareness of palliative care than non-caregivers, though paid caregivers report more misconceptions. Awareness also varies by caregiving relationship and disease type. These findings underscore the need for tailored educational strategies to enhance accurate understanding of palliative care across caregiving groups.
