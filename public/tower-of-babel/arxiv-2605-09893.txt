Pseudo-Deliberation in Language Models: When Reasoning Fails to Align Values and Actions
Sushrita Rakshit, Hanwen Zhang, Hua Shen

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are often evaluated based on their stated values, yet these do not reliably translate into their actions—a discrepancy termed value–action gap . In this work, we argue that this gap persists even under explicit reasoning, revealing a deeper failure mode we call “Pseudo-Deliberation”: the appearance of principled reasoning without corresponding behavioral alignment. To study this systematically, we introduce Valdi , a framework for measuring alignment between stated values and generated dialogue. Valdi includes 4,941 human-centered scenarios across five domains, three tasks that elicit value articulation, reasoning, and action, and five metrics for quantifying value adherence. Across both proprietary and open-source LLMs, we observe consistent misalignment between expressed values and downstream dialogues. To investigate intervention strategies, we propose ViValdi , a multi-agent value auditor that intervenes at different stages of generation. While interventions targeting intermediate reasoning aim to shape value formation, we find post-hoc auditing and rewriting of final dialogues yields more robust alignment gains. These findings suggest that value-action alignment in LLMs may not emerge from deliberative reasoning alone, and highlight the need for action-level alignment strategies at the point of generation 1 1 1 Codes and data will be released upon acceptance. .

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly entrusted with decisions that shape human lives – advising clinicians on diagnoses, guiding students through their education, and screening candidates in hiring pipelines

25

. As these systems migrate from controlled

Recent work has identified the “value–action gap
[
29

,” where LLMs’ stated value preferences diverge from the actions they recommend. However, this gap has primarily been evaluated in static multiple-choice settings
[
27
,
29

. In practice, LLMs act through situated dialogue, where advice is delivered conversationally and shaped through interaction. This raises a critical, underexplored
issue:
if a model endorses a value in principle but abandons it under generation pressure,
alignment evaluations based on stated preferences alone are dangerously misleading.

A natural hope is that
deliberative reasoning
closes this gap. Chain-of-thought and structured reasoning
[
33
]
have become the dominant levers for aligning LLM behavior, and the appeal of these methods rests on a tacit assumption: that explicit reasoning over values yields actions consistent with those values. However, we found that
LLMs frequently engage in
pseudo-deliberation
– the surface form of principled reasoning without the corresponding behavioral alignment. In this regime, reasoning traces faithfully invoke values the model endorses, yet the resulting action systematically violates them; worse, as we show,
deliberation can
amplify
rather than repair misalignment
.

To study pseudo-deliberation systematically, we introduce
Valdi
(

alue

Valdi
Framework: Measuring Situated Value-Action Gaps in Dialogues

The key components of our framework involve a value-driven dataset (§
2.1

2.2

2.1
DAISY Dataset Generation

We introduce DAISY (Decisions AI Steers for You), a dataset of 4,941 guidance-seeking dilemmas designed to evaluate value–action alignment in realistic decision-making contexts. The dataset spans diverse domains (e.g., social, ethical, and personal tradeoffs) and is constructed from a mixture of 6 social dialogue datasets
[
26
,
35
,
8
,
31
,
15
,
6
]
and controlled generation. A human annotator first filters scenarios according to predefined criteria. To increase value diversity of selected scenarios, we generate paraphrases using GPT-4o (
T=0.7
, max_tokens=500), producing multiple formulations of the same dilemma with varied surface realizations
[
21
]
. Crucially, all scenarios are rewritten to exclude explicit mentions of values, requiring models to implicitly infer and balance competing values during generation. This design enables a controlled evaluation of value–action gaps under naturalistic conditions, where value expression must emerge from behavior rather than prompting. Additional details regarding DAISY’s construction and content are provided in Appendix
D
.

2.2
Three Tasks for Stated Values and Situated Actions

We define three tasks to assess LLMs’ value expression across stages of generation: (1) stating value inclinations, (2) generating immediate dialogue, and (3) producing deliberative dialogue following structured reasoning, enabling evaluation of whether values expressed in isolation are preserved in downstream interaction. While we leverage Shalom Schwartz’s Theory of Basic Values to evaluate our model, our framework is extendable to other value orientations
[
28
]
.

Task 1: Stated Value Inclinations.

For each scenario, the LLM evaluates the extent to which each of the 10 Schwartz values should be encouraged when advising the user, using a 3-point Likert scale:
Encourage
,
Neutral
, or
Discourage
, which we map to
\{+1,0,-1\}
respectively. This yields a signed value vector representing the model’s stated value preferences.

Task 2: Fast Dialogue Generation.

Given the scenario and value definitions, the LLM generates a response directly to the user without explicit reasoning. This setting captures intuitive, immediate behavior and reflects standard prompting conditions.

Task 3: Slow (Deliberative) Dialogue Generation.

The LLM is prompted to produce a structured reasoning trace (four steps) prior to generating its final response. As in Task 2, we provide value definitions in the system prompt, but allow the model to implicitly select and express values during reasoning and in the final dialogue.

2.3
Detecting and Scoring Values in Text

Prior work has shown success in multi-stage prompting for complex LLM tasks
[
18
]
. We use a two-stage multiprompt pipeline to construct an LLM-based
ValueJudge
that extracts value signals from text. In the first stage, the model identifies which Schwartz values are present. In the second stage, it assigns a stance label to each detected value:
+1
(action endorsement for value),
-1
(action discouragement for value), or
0
(neutral discussion of value). Values not detected in the first stage are labeled as “irrelevant” and represented as
\varnothing
.

To assess the reliability of our annotation scheme, we report human–human agreement using Cohen’s
\kappa
for both value detection and stance scoring, following our two-pass
ValueJudge
setup. For detection, we compute binary agreement over value presence (
\varnothing
vs.
{-1,0,1}
), yielding
\kappa=0.7483
between two annotators. For stance scoring, we compute agreement over the full label space
{\varnothing,-1,0,+1}
, yielding
\kappa=0.8077
between two annotators. These results indicate substantial agreement, supporting reliable measurement of value identification and stance in text. In our final
ValueJudge
, we leverage a two-stage setup with GPT-4.1-mini for value detection and GPT-4.1 for stance scoring
[
22
]
, achieving Cohen’s
\kappa\approx 0.62
on a sample of
N=240
annotated examples, indicating substantial agreement with human annotators. Specific prompts and details on
ValueJudge
can be found in Appendix
F
.

2.4
Alignment Representations and Metrics

We represent each scenario using value vectors over the 10 Schwartz values and define metrics for measuring value–action gaps. Full formulas and implementation details are provided in Appendix
G
.

Formalization.

For each scenario
i
, we construct four value vectors given ten Schwartz values (
K=10
): stated values
V=[v_{i1},\dots,v_{iK}]
from Task 1, values expressed in fast dialogue
A^{\text{fast}}=[a^{\text{fast}}_{i1},\dots,a^{\text{fast}}_{iK}]
, values expressed in slow dialogue
A^{\text{slow}}=[a^{\text{slow}}_{i1},\dots,a^{\text{slow}}_{iK}]
, and values expressed in reasoning
R=[r_{i1},\dots,r_{iK}]
. Each entry corresponds to one value and takes a label from
\{\varnothing,-1,0,+1\}
, denoting absence, discouragement, neutrality, or encouragement. For multi-step reasoning traces, we aggregate annotations by taking the modal stance for each value, breaking ties by the most recent occurrence.
We use two label representations. For dialogue-level alignment metrics, we merge absence (
\varnothing
) with neutrality (
0
), yielding a 3-class space
\{-1,0,+1\}
. For reasoning-to-dialogue transition metrics, we retain
\varnothing
to explicitly track whether values appear, disappear, or emerge across stages.

Alignment Rate.

We measure how consistently models enact their stated values by extending
[
29
]
’s binary rate to the 3-class macro-F1 between
V_{i}
and the dialogue value vectors
A_{i}^{fast}
or
A_{i}^{slow}
. Higher alignment rate indicates stronger consistency between stated values and generated dialogue.

Alignment Distance.

To capture the magnitude of value drift defined in
[
29
]
, we compute the normalized Manhattan distance between stated and dialogue value vectors:

{\bar{D}_{i}=\frac{1}{2K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|v^{\prime}_{ik}-a^{\prime}_{ik}|,}

(1)

where
K=10
and
v^{\prime}_{ik},a^{\prime}_{ik}\in\{-1,0,+1\}
. Lower distance indicates less drift from the model’s stated value profile.

Value Survival, Suppression, and Emergence.

To analyze whether values expressed in reasoning are preserved in final dialogue, we define binary indicators
R_{i,v},S_{i,v}\in\{0,1\}
, where
R_{i,v}=\mathbb{I}[\mathbf{R}_{i}[v]\neq\varnothing]
and
S_{i,v}=\mathbb{I}[\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\text{slow}}[v]\neq\varnothing]
denote the presence of value
v
in reasoning and dialogue, respectively. We then compute:

\text{Survival}_{v}=\frac{\sum_{i}R_{i,v}S_{i,v}}{\sum_{i}R_{i,v}},\text{Suppression}_{v}=\frac{\sum_{i}R_{i,v}(1-S_{i,v})}{\sum_{i}R_{i,v}},\text{Emergence}_{v}=\frac{\sum_{i}(1-R_{i,v})S_{i,v}}{\sum_{i}(1-R_{i,v})}.

(2)

These metrics quantify value transitions from the latent reasoning stage to the manifested dialogue.

Survival
measures whether reasoned values persist into dialogue;
Suppression
captures values mentioned in reasoning but omitted in dialogue; and
Emergence
captures values introduced in dialogue without appearing in prior reasoning.

4.1
Value-Action Alignment Rates and Distances.

A natural hypothesis is that reasoning should improve value–action alignment, as step-by-step reasoning allows the model to reflect on its stated values before producing a response. Contrary to this expectation, we observe a consistent degradation in alignment under Slow Thinking. GPT-4o achieves an alignment rate of 0.4511 under Fast Thinking, which decreases to 0.4219 under Slow Thinking. This pattern is also reflected in alignment distance: Fast Thinking yields a lower distance (0.1685) compared to Slow Thinking (0.1785), indicating that responses generated without explicit reasoning more closely track the model’s stated value profile.

At the individual-value level, we observe substantial heterogeneity and a mismatch between value endorsement and execution. As shown in Figure
2
, highly endorsed values such as Self-Direction, Security, and Benevolence in the stated distribution (Figure
2(a)
) do not necessarily achieve high action alignment (Figure
2(b)
). For example, Benevolence and Self-Direction remain near the random baseline in F1 despite frequent endorsement, suggesting that these values are not reliably preserved in generated responses. Conversely, values such as Achievement and Tradition, which have more class-balanced or neutral stated distributions, achieve higher alignment. Thus, alignment is not simply driven by endorsement strength, but by whether values are consistently preserved during generation.

Across nearly all values, Slow Thinking further reduces alignment relative to Fast Thinking, reinforcing that deliberative reasoning does not improve, and may actively degrade, value–action consistency. These results reveal a systematic gap in which models fail to align with their own stated value commitments during response generation.

4.2
Reasoning Decomposition.

To identify where misalignment arises, we decompose Slow Thinking into intermediate reasoning and final dialogue, and analyze how value signals transition between two stages. As shown in Figure
3
, the model exhibits high value suppression for several self-oriented values such as Stimulation, Power and Achievement, where the model invokes these values in reasoning, but fails to carry a stance in dialogue. In contrast, values such as Security and Universalism display relatively higher survival rates, indicating they are more consistently carried into the final response. Emergence remains relatively low overall, but is more pronounced for values such as Self-Direction and Security, suggesting that certain value dimensions are more likely to be introduced during response generation. In contrast, other values exhibit near-zero emergence, indicating strong dependence on prior reasoning signals.

This asymmetry suggests that the model does not uniformly translate its reasoning into action, but instead selectively filters which values are expressed. In particular, values associated with individual preference or self-enhancement are more likely to be suppressed, while socially normative or safety-aligned values are more likely to persist. One plausible explanation for this suppression effect lies in post-training procedures such as instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), which shape how models express their outputs. While pretraining equips the model with the ability to recognize a broad range of value-relevant considerations, post-training may impose constraints that favor socially acceptable, safe, or normative responses.

ViValdi
: Gap Mitigation with the Multi-Agent Value Alignment System

Figure 4
:
Overview of
ViValdi
-D’s multi-agent dialogue auditor. We create a system with

to evaluate distorted values, rubricate them, and make a plan to correct the output.

ViValdi
-R&D performs iterative reasoning-level repair to improve alignment, and applies dialogue-level repair when alignment fails to propagate to the final output.

Motivated by the suppression of value signals between reasoning and final dialogue,
we developed
ViValdi
system with three agentic intervention strategies:

•

ViValdi
-R
: A reasoning-centric repair approach that iteratively identifies and corrects inconsistencies within intermediate reasoning, and generates the final dialogue from the revised reasoning.

•

ViValdi
-R&D
: A reasoning-centric repair approach that identifies and corrects inconsistencies at each reasoning step, and generates the final dialogue from the revised reasoning. When alignment fails to propagate, a final dialogue-level audit can be applied.

•

ViValdi
-D
: A dialogue-centric repair approach that applies corrections directly to the final response without modifying intermediate reasoning.

ViValdi
-R&D and
ViValdi
-R Architecture.

Our multi-agent architecture (Figure
4
) enforces value alignment through iterative audit and repair. Given a scenario, a generator first produces intermediate reasoning and a final dialogue. A
ValueExtractor
, implemented via our two-stage
ValueJudge
, identifies the presence and stance of each value. During the iterative phase, an auditor agent operates on the reasoning, comparing extracted values against a target value profile to identify discrepancies (e.g., missing, overexpressed, or conflicting values). A planning module translates these discrepancies into structured repair instructions, which are executed by a rewriting agent to produce revised reasoning. A new dialogue is then generated from the repaired reasoning and evaluated using a verifier based on exact-match alignment with a fixed threshold.

If alignment fails to meet this threshold after a fixed number of iterations, a final fallback stage directly audits and rewrites the dialogue. This design allows us to additionally consider
ViValdi
-R
, a variant that performs reasoning-level repair without the final dialogue-level fallback. This isolates the effect of reasoning-centric interventions on alignment. Additional details in Appendix
I
.

ViValdi
-D Architecture.

Our multi-agent system in Figure
4
performs value steering directly at the level of final dialogue, without modifying intermediate reasoning. Given an initial fast response, a value extractor first identifies the values expressed in the text using
ValueJudge
to produce
A_{i}^{\text{fast}}
. An auditor then compares these signals against the model’s stated value profile
V_{i}
, diagnosing discrepancies such as missing, overexpressed, or contradictory values. A planning module translates these discrepancies into targeted repair instructions, which are executed by a rewriting agent to produce a revised response. A verifier then re-evaluates the candidate using the value extractor and computes its alignment with the target profile. The rewrite is accepted if it improves or preserves the current best alignment score, enabling a greedy search over candidate responses. See details in Appendix
H
.

Related Work

Value–Action Alignment in LLMs.

The belief–behavior gap, long studied in social science
[
4
,
9
]
, has recently been examined in LLMs via frameworks such as
ValueActionLens

[
29
]
and
ValueBench

[
27
]
, which quantify discrepancies between expressed values and model decisions. However, their reliance on value-primed prompts and multiple-choice formats limits their ability to capture value–action gaps in open-ended dialogue. Additionally, the rise of post-training methods has made study of this gap increasingly important. Alignment approaches such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
[
24
]
and constitutional AI
[
2
]
steer outputs toward human preferences. However, they operate at the level of individual responses and do not assess consistency between reasoning and subsequent actions. A more holistic lens is needed to understand how LLMs socially reason and whether such methods contribute to value misalignment between assertions and behavior.

Reasoning and External Deliberation Mechanisms.

The dual-process model distinguishes fast, intuitive reasoning from slower, deliberative reasoning
[
11
]
, motivating methods such as chain-of-thought prompting
[
33
]
. Prior work in social reasoning emphasizes mental state inference
[
36
]
and interpersonal relationships
[
14
]
, rather than value propagation in dialogue. Additionally, recent work demonstrates that generated reasoning may not reflect underlying decision processes
[
32
]
. We leverage this paradigm to examine whether reasoning reduces the value–action gap. Beyond internal reasoning, ensemble methods such as multi-agent systems introduce collaboration, debate, and self-critique to improve reasoning
[
7
,
19
]
, with recent work extending these ideas to alignment by studying value propagation across agents
[
17
]
. In contrast, we propose a multi-agent auditing system that verifies and steers value consistency across both reasoning and dialogue.

8
Conclusion

We introduced
Valdi
, a framework for measuring value–action gaps in realistic dialogue settings. Across nearly 5,000 scenarios and multiple LLMs, we find that models’ stated values often fail to carry over into generated responses. More importantly, explicit chain-of-thought deliberation does not reliably close this gap and can even worsen it—a failure mode we term
pseudo-deliberation
.
Our analysis shows that models often recognize relevant values in reasoning but suppress or alter them in final dialogue, suggesting a failure of value expression rather than value recognition. To mitigate this, we proposed
ViValdi
, a multi-agent auditing framework that verifies and repairs outputs after generation. Dialogue-level repair improves alignment more reliably than reasoning-level intervention, indicating that value-consistent behavior must be checked and corrected at the level of action, not assumed to emerge from reasoning alone.
We also note the limitation of this work in Appendix
B
.

Appendix B
Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, our evaluation relies on automatic value extraction through
ValueJudge
. Although
ValueJudge
shows substantial agreement with human annotations, value interpretation is inherently subjective and context dependent. Implicit, culturally specific, or pragmatically conveyed values may therefore be missed or simplified by the annotation pipeline.
Second, the same underlying
value-extraction pipeline is used both inside the
ViValdi
repair loop, where we refer to it as

ValueJudge
, and in final post-hoc assessment, where we refer to it as
ValueEvaluator
. Although we include human evaluation as a complementary check, some observed improvements may partly reflect optimization toward evaluator-specific criteria rather than broader value alignment. Future work should test robustness with larger-scale human studies, independent evaluators, and alternative value-detection methods.
Third, DAISY focuses primarily on advice-giving scenarios. Value–action alignment may behave differently in other settings, such as collaborative problem solving, negotiation, education, healthcare, or long-term human–AI interaction.
Finally, we operationalize values using Schwartz’s 10-value framework. While this provides broad psychological coverage, it does not capture all dimensions of moral, cultural, or social reasoning. Future work should examine whether our findings generalize across domains, cultures, and value ontologies.

Appendix C
Broader Impacts

This work studies value–action misalignment in LLMs, with the goal of
improving the reliability of LLMs in advice-giving and other socially situated
dialogue settings. A positive societal impact of this work is that
Valdi
and

ViValdi
may help researchers and deployers identify cases where models state
socially desirable values but fail to preserve them in generated responses. This
could support more reliable auditing of LLMs used in domains such as education,
clinical triage, workplace support, and other settings where value-sensitive
advice may influence user decisions.

At the same time, the work has potential negative impacts. First, value alignment
is inherently normative and culturally dependent; systems that optimize toward a
fixed value ontology may overrepresent some moral or cultural perspectives while
underrepresenting others. Second, automated value auditing could create a false
sense of safety if used as a substitute for human oversight, especially in
high-stakes settings. Finally, because our
evaluation focuses on advice-giving scenarios, deployment in other domains may
produce harms not captured by our benchmark.

To mitigate these risks, we frame
Valdi
and
ViValdi
as research tools for auditing
and diagnosis rather than as sufficient safeguards for deployment. We recommend
using them alongside human evaluation, domain-specific review, alternative value
ontologies, and independent evaluators. We also encourage transparent reporting of
the target value framework, known failure cases, and the limits of automatic value
extraction when applying these tools in practice.

Appendix E
Prompt Variants and Optimizations

E.1
Task 1 Prompt Variants

Prompt Decomposition.

To systematically study how prompt design influences value articulation, we decompose our prompting strategy into three orthogonal components:
context framing
,
value definition
, and
response scale
. Rather than treating prompts as monolithic, this decomposition allows us to isolate how different semantic and structural choices affect the model’s stated value profile.

•

Context framing
controls the role and perspective of the model. We vary between:

–

Neutral
: a generic assistant with minimal framing,

–

Advisor
: an explicitly decision-support role,

–

First-person
: a more immersive framing where the model imagines itself in the scenario.

•

Value definition
determines how abstract values are presented:

–

Canonical
: textbook-style definitions of Schwartz values,

–

Actionary
: operationalized definitions grounded in observable behavior (e.g., “the advice reflects…”).

•

Response scale
specifies how value stance is expressed:

–

3-class
:
\{-1,0,+1\}
(discourage, neutral, encourage),

–

5-class
:
\{-2,-1,0,+1,+2\}
(allowing finer-grained intensity with "strongly agree").

These components are programmatically composed to generate prompt variants, resulting in a controlled grid of configurations (e.g.,
3\times 2\times 2
combinations of context, definition, and scale). Each prompt asks the model to map a given scenario to a structured JSON object over the ten Schwartz values. The implementation explicitly enforces output structure and deterministic decoding (temperature
=0
), ensuring consistency across runs.

Prompt Agreement and Scale Normalization.

To quantify the consistency of value elicitation across prompt variants, we introduce a
prompt agreement
metric. Because our prompt configurations use both 3-class and 5-class response scales, we first normalize all outputs into a shared semantic space before computing agreement.

Scale normalization.

We map all responses onto a unified 5-point ordinal scale:

\{0,1,2,3,4\}\equiv\{\text{strongly discourage},\text{discourage},\text{neutral},\text{encourage},\text{strongly encourage}\}.

Specifically, 3-class outputs
\{-1,0,+1\}
are mapped to
\{1,2,3\}
, while 5-class outputs
\{-2,-1,0,+1,+2\}
are mapped to
\{0,1,2,3,4\}
. We further collapse this representation into a shared 3-class scale:

\{0,1\}\rightarrow\text{discourage},\quad 2\rightarrow\text{neutral},\quad\{3,4\}\rightarrow\text{encourage},

enabling comparison at both coarse and fine granularities.

Prompt agreement metric.

For each scenario–value pair, we collect responses across all prompt variants and compute the proportion of responses that match the modal label. Formally, let
y_{i,j}^{(k)}
denote the label assigned to value
j
in scenario
i
under prompt variant
k
. The agreement for
(i,j)
is defined as:

\text{Agreement}_{i,j}=\frac{\max_{c}\sum_{k}\mathbf{1}\big[y_{i,j}^{(k)}=c\big]}{\sum_{k}\mathbf{1}\big[y_{i,j}^{(k)}\text{ is valid}\big]},

where
c
ranges over possible labels. The overall prompt agreement is then computed by aggregating counts across all scenario–value pairs:

\text{Agreement}=\frac{\sum_{i,j}\max_{c}\sum_{k}\mathbf{1}\big[y_{i,j}^{(k)}=c\big]}{\sum_{i,j}\sum_{k}\mathbf{1}\big[y_{i,j}^{(k)}\text{ is valid}\big]}.

E.2
Task 2 and 3 Prompt Optimization with DSPy (MIPROv2)

To optimize the fast-thinking policy (Task 2) and slow-thinking policy (Task 3), we use
DSPy
and its teleprompting module
MIPROv2
, which performs automated prompt search under a user-defined objective.

Setup.

We define a structured generation program that maps a scenario to action-oriented advice. The base prompt encodes (i) a value ontology (Schwartz’s ten values), (ii) behavioral constraints (decisive, non-neutral), and (iii) formatting requirements. This serves as the initial policy prior to optimization.

Objective.

We optimize prompts using a task-specific alignment metric. For each generated response, we apply
ValueJudge
to extract value stances and compare them to the model’s stated values. We compute macro-F1 over the label space
\{-1,0,+1\}
:

\text{Score}=\text{MacroF1}(V_{\text{stated}},A_{\text{dialogue}})

(3)

where
V_{\text{stated}}
denotes the stated value vector and
A_{\text{dialogue}}
denotes the values inferred from the generated advice. Missing values are mapped to
0
.

Optimization Procedure.

MIPROv2
performs iterative prompt search by:

1.

Sampling candidate prompt variants,

2.

Evaluating each candidate on a minibatch using the alignment metric,

3.

Selecting high-performing prompts and refining them over multiple trials.

Given
C
candidates and
T
trials, the procedure evaluates
\mathcal{O}(C\times T)
prompt variants and retains the best-performing program.

Training Protocol.

We use a held-out tuning setup with
N=25
examples for optimization and
N=25
for evaluation (nested prompt on DAISY with first seed=50 then seed = 42). No in-context demonstrations are used (i.e., zero-shot optimization), ensuring improvements arise solely from prompt search.

Implementation Details.

We use GPT-4o-mini as both the prompt and task model with temperature
0
. Optimization is run with 10 candidates and 12 trials, using minibatches of size 4.

Evaluation.

We compare the original hand-written prompt and the optimized prompt on held-out data using macro-F1. This isolates the effect of prompt optimization independent of downstream interventions such as MAS.

Prompt Optimization Results.

Based on the results in Tables
12
and
13
, we use the original prompts for both fast (Task 2) and slow (Task 3) generation in all subsequent experiments. For fast generation, the manually designed prompt clearly outperforms the DSPy-optimized variant, achieving higher Macro F1 (0.4273 vs. 0.3848) and lower alignment distance (0.180 vs. 0.194), indicating both stronger value alignment and reduced deviation from the target value profile. For slow generation, the DSPy-optimized prompt yields only a negligible improvement in Macro F1 (0.3762 vs. 0.3759), well within the observed variance, and does not provide a meaningful advantage in alignment quality. Taken together, these results suggest that automated prompt optimization does not reliably improve performance in this setting and, in the case of fast generation, can even degrade alignment. We therefore adopt the original prompts as a stable and stronger baseline for all downstream evaluations.

F.4
Final ValueJudger Evaluation

We conduct human evaluation on a fixed set of scenario-level model generations produced by GPT-4o-mini. These generations were produced by randomly sampling (n=50, seed=50) DAISY. We convert these outputs into a long-format annotation dataset consisting of fast responses, slow responses, and individual reasoning steps, yielding 160 annotation units. These units are randomly shuffled (seed = 42) prior to annotation.

Two human annotators jointly annotate each instance to produce a consensus ground truth. We then evaluate our ValueJudge pipeline, which uses GPT-4.1-mini for value detection and GPT-4.1 for stance scoring. All model parameters are deterministically set (temperature = 0, seed = 42). We use GPT-4.1-mini for value detection, as it achieves comparable performance to GPT-4.1 on this task, and GPT-4.1 for stance scoring, where we observe improved reliability. We obtain substantial agreement between the consensus human annotations and ValueJudge predictions (
\kappa=0.62
).

Appendix G
Metrics

We highlight the details and equations corresponding to our alignment metrics below.

Alignment Rate.

We measure alignment between stated values
V
and action values
A
using macro-F1 over stance labels
\{-1,0,+1\}
.

Let
V_{i},A_{i}\in\{-1,0,+1,\varnothing\}^{K}
denote the stated and action value vectors for scenario
i
. We first map absent values (
\varnothing
) to the neutral class (0), yielding
V^{\prime}_{i},A^{\prime}_{i}\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{K}
.

We then flatten these vectors across all scenarios and values to obtain
\mathbf{y}_{\text{true}}
and
\mathbf{y}_{\text{pred}}
, and compute macro-F1 across the three stance classes:

\text{AlignmentScore}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{c\in\{-1,0,+1\}}\frac{2\cdot\text{Precision}_{c}\cdot\text{Recall}_{c}}{\text{Precision}_{c}+\text{Recall}_{c}}.

Higher scores indicate stronger alignment between stated values and expressed behavior.

Alignment Distance.

We measure alignment between stated values
V_{i}
and action values
A_{i}
using a normalized L1 distance:

D_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}|v^{\prime}_{ik}-a^{\prime}_{ik}|,\quad\bar{D}_{i}=\frac{D_{i}}{2K},

where
K=10
is the number of values and
v^{\prime}_{ik},a^{\prime}_{ik}\in\{-1,0,1\}
after squashing absent values to 0.

Appendix H
ViValdi
-D Multi-Agent Auditing Architecture

H.1
Overview

We implement a dialogue-level multi-agent auditing system that treats value alignment as a post-hoc repair problem of generated dialogue. Given a scenario
s
, a model-generated response
a^{(0)}
, and a target value profile
v\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{K}
, the system iteratively refines the response to improve alignment.

The pipeline consists of four modular components:

\textbf{Extractor}\rightarrow\textbf{Auditor}\rightarrow\textbf{Planner}\rightarrow\textbf{Rewriter}.

(4)

Each iteration produces a candidate response
a^{(t)}
, which continues the optimization process if it does not decrease alignment under a greedy acceptance rule.

H.2
Value Representation

We operate over the Schwartz value inventory with
K=10
values:

V=(v_{1},\dots,v_{K}),\quad v_{k}\in\{-1,0,+1\}.

(5)

Here,
-1
denotes discouragement,
0
denotes neutrality or absence, and
+1
denotes encouragement towards the user taking an action on the value. Missing values are represented as
0
during evaluation.

H.3
Value Extraction (Extractor) and
ValueEvaluator

We use our few-shot ValueJudge module to extract value signals from text:

E(a)=\hat{V}=(\hat{v}_{1},\dots,\hat{v}_{K}).

(6)

In our multi-agent alignment system, we use
ValueJudge
to denote the in-loop extraction and auditing module used during iterative repair. When the same underlying pipeline is used for post-hoc alignment assessment, we refer to it as the
ValueEvaluator
to distinguish its role as an out-of-loop evaluation module.

H.4
Value Auditor

The auditor compares predicted values
\hat{V}
with target values
V
to identify misalignment:

\mathcal{I}=\{k\mid\hat{v}_{k}\neq v_{k}\}.

(7)

Each misaligned value is categorized into one of three issue types:

•

Missing:

v_{k}\neq 0,\hat{v}_{k}=0

•

Overexpressed:

v_{k}=0,\hat{v}_{k}\neq 0

•

Contradiction:

v_{k}\neq\hat{v}_{k},\;v_{k}\neq 0,\hat{v}_{k}\neq 0

The auditor outputs a structured issue set:

\mathcal{I}=\{(k,\text{type}_{k},\text{action}_{k})\},

(8)

where
\text{action}_{k}\in\{\text{add},\text{remove\_or\_soften},\text{flip}\}
.

H.5
Repair Planner

The planner generates targeted repair instructions conditioned on the scenario
s
, current response
a^{(t)}
, and detected issues
\mathcal{I}
:

\mathcal{R}=P(s,a^{(t)},\mathcal{I}).

(9)

The repair plan
\mathcal{R}
consists of natural language instructions specifying how to modify the response. Instructions are constrained to:

•

modify semantic content rather than surface wording,

•

directly affect the recommendation,

•

avoid explicitly naming values.

H.6
Response Rewriter

The rewriter applies the repair plan to produce a new candidate response:

a^{(t+1)}=R(s,a^{(t)},\mathcal{R}).

(10)

The rewritten response is required to be fluent, concise, and free of explicit reasoning or value labels.

H.7
Alignment Objective

We define alignment using the Exact Match Ratio (EMR):

\mathrm{EMR}(V,\hat{V})=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{v}_{k}=v_{k}\}.

(11)

This metric serves as the optimization objective during MAS. At each iteration, we compare the extracted value profile of the current response, obtained via ValueJudge, to the target value profile derived from Task 1 using the Exact Match Ratio (EMR).

H.8
Greedy Acceptance Rule

At each iteration, a candidate response is accepted only if:

\text{EMR}(V,E(a^{(t+1)}))\geq\text{EMR}(V,E(a^{(t)})).

(12)

Otherwise, the candidate is rejected and the process terminates. This ensures monotonic improvement and prevents degradation relative to the original response.

H.9
Iterative Optimization

The system performs up to
T
rewrite attempts:

a^{(0)}\rightarrow a^{(1)}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow a^{(T)}.

(13)

Early stopping occurs if:

•

no issues remain (
\mathcal{I}=\emptyset
), or

•

a candidate fails to improve EMR.

-R

\tau

If the generated response fails to satisfy the target alignment threshold, an additional dialogue-level repair pass is performed as a failsafe correction mechanism.

This additional stage is motivated by our observation that improved reasoning alignment does not necessarily guarantee alignment in the final generated response.

Appendix K
Human Validation of
ViValdi
Performance

We constructed the blind human evaluation set by uniformly sampling 60 scenarios (seed=123) from the
500-scenario evaluation subset used in
4
. For each scenario, we created
pairwise comparisons between each
ViValdi
variant and its corresponding baseline:

ViValdi
-D was compared against Fast, while
ViValdi
-R and
ViValdi
-R&D were compared
against Slow. For each comparison, the order of the two responses was randomly assigned
to Response A or Response B, and method identities were hidden from annotators. Each
annotation row contained the scenario, the target stated-value profile, two anonymized
responses, and an empty preference column. A private key stored the mapping from
Response A/B to the underlying systems and was used only after annotation to compute
win rates. The final annotation set was shuffled and split into two Google Sheets for
annotation.

To assess our multi-agent system from human standpoint, we provide further details on our human evaluation paradigm. Blind pairwise human evaluation was conducted using a Google Sheets annotation interface. Each row corresponded to one pairwise comparison and contained a pair identifier, the target stated-value profile, two anonymized model responses, and an annotator decision column. Responses were shown as
response_A
and
response_B
; model identities and method labels were hidden from annotators. Annotators were asked: “Which demonstrates the stated values better?” They selected
A
or
B
, with
C
used for ties. We instructed annotators to use the tie option sparingly, only when both responses preserved the stated values similarly well or when neither response was clearly preferable.

For blind pairwise human evaluation, annotators selected which of two anonymized
responses better preserved the target stated-value profile, with a tie option. For each comparison, we counted a win
if the selected response corresponded to the evaluated
ViValdi
method and a loss if
it corresponded to the baseline. Ties were excluded from win-rate computation but
reported separately as a tie rate. Thus, for each method, human win rate is computed
as

\text{WinRate}=\frac{\#\text{{{ViValdi}} wins}}{\#\text{{{ViValdi}} wins}+\#\text{baseline wins}}.

[Yes]

Guidelines:

•

The answer
[N/A]

The authors should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the implications would be.

The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.

The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach. For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle technical jargon.

The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms and how they scale with dataset size.

•

(b)

If the authors answer

•

Examples of negative societal impacts include potential malicious or unintended uses (e.g., disinformation, generating fake profiles, surveillance), fairness considerations (e.g., deployment of technologies that could make decisions that unfairly impact specific groups), privacy considerations, and security considerations.

•

The conference expects that many papers will be foundational research and not tied to particular applications, let alone deployments. However, if there is a direct path to any negative applications, the authors should point it out. For example, it is legitimate to point out that an improvement in the quality of generative models could be used to generate Deepfakes for disinformation. On the other hand, it is not needed to point out that a generic algorithm for optimizing neural networks could enable people to train models that generate Deepfakes faster.

•

The authors should consider possible harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended and functioning correctly, harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended but gives incorrect results, and harms following from (intentional or unintentional) misuse of the technology.

•

If there are negative societal impacts, the authors could also discuss possible mitigation strategies (e.g., gated release of models, providing defenses in addition to attacks, mechanisms for monitoring misuse, mechanisms to monitor how a system learns from feedback over time, improving the efficiency and accessibility of ML).
