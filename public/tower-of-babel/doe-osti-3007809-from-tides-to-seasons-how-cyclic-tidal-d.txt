Executive Summary

Coastal ecosystems are among the most biologically and biogeochemically active and diverse
systems on Earth. Because they act as important linkages between terrestrial ecosystems and
the open ocean, their incorporation in Earth system models (ESMs) is critical to predict coastal
and global responses to environmental changes. However, they vary greatly in the magnitude of
tides and the volume and timing of freshwater input from land, making it challenging to model
the major biogeochemical reactions that control productivity and greenhouse gas emissions
across coastal terrestrial aquatic interfaces (TAIs).

Our overall objective was to improve mechanistic process understanding and modeling of tidal
wetland hydro-biogeochemistry in coastal TAIs. We established a new flux tower site (Ameriflux
US-PLo) in the oligohaline part of the Parker River to continuously monitor ecosystem-scale
carbon fluxes under temporally varying salinity conditions. The site is co-located with long-term
monitoring plots of the Plum Island Ecosystems LTER project. We installed wells and redox
sensors in the marsh interior and creek bank, established biomass monitoring plots and
deployed novel optode sensors in both locations. We used this data to parameterize plant-
mediated transport in PFLOTRAN and tested the impact of soil heterogeneity on porewater
constituents and gas fluxes. We collected observations of root oxygen release with a novel
planar optode system in the field. Flux data collected during the measurement period
encompasses a large variation in salinity ranging from drought to record precipitation years. We
developed a method to extract functional relationships from the flux data using artificial neural
networks, identifying salinity thresholds for CH4 fluxes. Finally, we are using the coupled ELM-
PFLOTRAN model to test the impact of antecedent hydrological conditions on the salinity-CH4
flux relationship.

This grant contributed to the professional development of one postdoc, three research
assistants and one graduate student. The sensor data has been shared with external
collaborators.

Main Body

 1. Background

Coastal ecosystems are dynamic terrestrial-aquatic interfaces (TAIs) between land and ocean
and represent some of the most biologically and biogeochemically active and diverse systems
on Earth. Because they act as important linkages between terrestrial ecosystems and the open
ocean, their incorporation in Earth system models (ESMs) is critical to predict coastal and global
responses to environmental changes. They vary greatly in the magnitude of tides, the intensity
of seasonality, the species composition of plant communities, and the volume and timing of
freshwater input from land. This makes it particularly challenging to model the major
biogeochemical reactions that control productivity and greenhouse gas emissions across coastal
TAIs. Key characteristics that distinguish coastal wetlands, such as tidal oscillation, sulfur
biogeochemistry, and plant structural adaptations to anaerobic soil, have only very recently
been incorporated in land surface models such as ELM-PFLOTRAN and are largely untested.
To rigorously test a coupled model, observations on hydrological oscillations, porewater
constituents and responding carbon fluxes should be available from one site. To be able to do
that for a site with known seasonal and annually varying salinity levels, we combined new data
collection with known history of inter-annual salinity fluctuations.

 2. Research questions
Three questions, spanning small to ecosystem scales, and short to long-term drivers, guided our
approach:

 1. How do tidal water level oscillations, evapotranspiration-driven water level changes,
 and oxygen (O2) transport from roots into the rhizosphere control tidal marsh redox
 and sediment oxygen distribution?
 2. How do hydrology, biogeochemistry and plant biology interact on different
 timescales to influence energy and greenhouse gas fluxes?
 3. How will carbon sequestration and greenhouse gas fluxes respond to changes in
 climate and sea level?

We addressed these questions with a combination of discrete and continuous measurements at
two topographical units (marsh interior and creekbank) at our research site that helped inform
modeling on plot and ecosystem-scale.

 b. Hypothesis testing

Q1) Subsurface processes: How do tidal water level oscillations, evapotranspiration-driven
water level changes, and O2 transport from roots into the rhizosphere control tidal marsh
redox and sediment oxygen distribution?

H1: The extent of sediment oxygenation by roots increases from early spring into summer as
new plant roots and shoots grow for the year.

Planar optodes deployed at US-PLo revealed that pools of oxygen (O2) resulted from radial O2
loss (ROL) from roots. Major deployments were in Sept. 2022, August and Oct. 2023, and May,
June, and August 2024. The highest concentration oxygen pools were localized around actively
growing roots and rhizomes in early summer (June) (Fig. 2), though some ROL was detected
even as late as October in 2023. Pools of O2 extended several mm radially from root surfaces
and were dynamic, appearing and disappearing within hours, sometimes (but not always)
congruent with day/night cycles (Fig 2). Other live roots that were not growing did not tend to
have pools of O2 around them, e.g. vertical roots in lower portions images in Fig. 2. At other
times of year, pools of O2 resulting from root ROL were detectable only sporadically and tended
to be lower concentration (Fig. 3). Because the O2 pool size is influenced by both supply (O2
release by roots) and demand (microbial and abiotic rhizosphere biogeochemical demand for

O2), the lack of a rhizosphere O2 pool does not necessarily mean that O2 is not being released by
roots. Rather, it means that if O2 is being released, the rate of release is outstripped by the rate
of O2 utilization by rhizophere abiotic and biotic processes. Our field data repeatedly showed O 2
pools appearing and disappearing over time (e.g. Figs 2,3), suggesting a dynamic balance of
supply and demand in space and time, even on the time frame of hours (Cardon et al., in prep).

H2: Root O2 release is a more important driver of sediment aeration than ET-driven water
level changes during most of the growing season in the marsh interior, while drainage
dominantly controls O2 levels in the creek bank sediment.

In October 2023, we deployed optodes at both the creekbank and marsh interior at the Typha
marsh and obtained long time series of images in both locations. The optode deployed among
creekbank Typha plants clearly showed the effects of drainage and air intrusion into the

sediment, intrusion that was erased by the incoming tide (Fig. 3). This air intrusion introduced
localized, very high concentrations of O2 as deep as ~5 cm into the sediment (Fig. 3), whereas
ROL from roots, though detectable, resulted in only ~2-5% O2 rhizosphere saturation in the
otherwise anaerobic sediment.

Figure 3 Red fluorescence images taken during deployment of the field planar optode prototype ~4m from the edge of the marsh
platform, among tall Typha angustifolia in the creekbank zone of the Typha site. Darker grey indicates higher O 2 concentrations.
Images were taken in October 2023 over the course of 12 hours, every 5 minutes. The selected images illustrate both intrusion of
air down into the sediment as drainage occurs (black blobs – 100% O2 saturation - located at white arrows, center two images),
and faint ROL from horizontal roots approximately 8 cm down in the sediment. This ROL results in O2 pools at approximately 2-
5% O2 saturation along the root length. As noted for the marsh interior image series in Fig. 2, the O2 pool is dynamic around the
root; it persists for hours the evening of Oct. 15, then disappears before high tide, and reappears after high tide. The penetration
of air associated with sediment drainage also is highly dynamic and at times introduces locally very high O2 concentrations in
pockets to ~5cm depth.

Fig. 4 illustrates the contrasting pattern in the marsh interior. (The same contrast was seen
during other deployments.) At the same time points as shown for the creekbank optode in Fig.
3, the marsh interior optode shows no drainage and (black) air intrusion into the anaerobic
sediment. This pattern is consistent with solidly low Eh measured by the redox probes in the
marsh interior, and the contrasting porewater biogeochemistry with high methane and sulfide
concentrations in the marsh interior. The live roots and rhizome obvious in the panels in Fig. 4
are not surrounded by plumes of O2, either because rhizosphere demand is outstripping any
root supply, or because the rhizomes and roots have become suberized as they matured,
preventing O2 diffusion out into sediment. The one visible living O2 source in the upper right of
each image is a leaf that was mistakenly pulled down into the hole where the optode box was
deployed.

We should note that extensive air intrusion and sediment oxygenation can occur at the height
of vegetative biomass in mid-summer; redox probe data showed that even in the marsh interior,
evapotranspiration in July of 2022 drew down the water table by nearly 20 cm during drought.
We do not yet have optode data during such rare events. The drawdown started during neap
tides in the marsh interior, and Eh (measured by redox probes) began to rise shortly thereafter,

ultimately remaining high even for a full week. At the creekbank, drainage-related positive
excursions of Eh were more far more common.

H3: Incorporation of root oxygen release and related spatial heterogeneity of redox conditions
in PFLOTRAN will improve predictions of redox driven sediment processes over predictions
assuming only controls by water level, particularly in the interior marsh.

Root oxygen release is currently underrepresented in mechanistic wetland models, particularly
in large scale applications. Given the computational demand and uncertainties in
parametrization, these models typically assume anoxic conditions for inundated wetland soil
and horizontal homogeneity. Observations, including our own, however, have shown that ROL
can supply a substantial amount of oxygen into fully water-saturated soil and cause horizontal
heterogeneity of O2 distribution at the rooting zone. It remains unclear whether or how such
heterogeneity should be included in large scale models simulating greenhouse gas exchange.
We developed ROL simulation in a numerical model for coastal wetland biogeochemistry to
quantify the impacts of the ROL and spatial heterogeneity on model predictions of porewater
concentrations and CH4 emissions. We hypothesized that (1) addition of simulated ROL will
reduce porewater concentration of CH4 at the root tips with O2 input and reduce CH4 flux and
(2) simulated heterogeneous ROL will decrease CH4 and H2S concentrations and CH4 emissions
compared to homogeneous ROL simulation because oxidation rates locally (at the root scale)
are higher in the heterogeneous scenario. Three modes of ROL were tested, i.e., no ROL,
homogeneous ROL, and heterogeneous ROL. We coupled the reaction network with each of
these three ROL modes to model the soil porewater concentrations of redox sensitive species
and CH4 fluxes at steady state. From the heterogeneous ROL simulation, we obtained 18%
higher column averaged CH4 concentration at the rooting zone but 5% lower total CH4 flux
compared to simulations of the homogeneous ROL or without ROL. This difference is because
lower CH4 concentrations occurred in the same rhizosphere volume that was directly connected
with plant-mediated transport of CH4 from the rooting zone to the atmosphere. Sensitivity

 analysis indicated that the
 impacts of heterogeneous
 ROL on model predictions of
 porewater oxygen and sulfide
 concentrations will be more
 important under conditions
 of higher ROL fluxes or more
 heterogeneous root
 distribution (lower root
 densities). Despite the small
 impact on predicted CH4
 emissions, the simulated ROL
 drastically reduced porewater
 concentrations of sulfide, an
 effective phytotoxin,
 indicating that incorporating
 ROL combined with sulfur
 cycling into ecosystem
 models could potentially
 improve predictions of plant
 productivity in coastal
 wetland ecosystems.

Q2) Sediment-Plant-Atmosphere exchange over time: How do hydrology, biogeochemistry and
plant biology interact on different timescales (tidal, diel, neap-spring, seasonal, interannual)
to influence greenhouse gas fluxes?

Unexpectedly, we captured an extreme event in our first year of measurements (2022): An
extreme drought led to earlier and higher saltwater intrusion at the site than typically observed
in the LTER long-term record. It caused a strong reduction in CH4 fluxes that lasted until summer
2023. This event gave us the opportunity to gain a better understanding of how hydrology and
biogeochemical processes interact during these events, causing ecosystem-scale CH4 and CO2
fluxes to respond on different timescales. To further gain mechanistic insights into underlying

mechanisms of the observed flux response, separate funding was acquired (DE-SC0024270, PI:
Cardon).

H4: Plant biology drives methane fluxes on diel and seasonal timescales via photosynthetic
carbon and pressurized gas transport.

We have focused our flux analysis on the first two years of measurements, which covered the
extreme drought period and the recovery. We found that CH4 fluxes dropped immediately after
flooding with saltwater to detection limit and recovered only in the next summer, when our
porewater data indicated sulfate depletion in the topsoil (Forbrich & Sturtevant, submitted).
Contrary to our expectations, we did not find a strong influence on CH4 fluxes by gross primary
production or other variables associated with plant-mediated transport during these two years.
However, in the early months of 2022, atmospheric pressure variations are associated with
higher CH4 flux indicating ebullition dominating least temporarily ecosystem-scale gas transport
(data not shown). Thus, there is potential for other transport mechanisms dominating of plant-
mediated transport which we detected on smaller scales:

During 2022 and 2023, we developed a sampling technique with very thin 28 gauge needles
that allowed us to insert the needles parallel to and into obvious ridges on the outsides of lower
portions of Typha stems. Air withdrawn using a syringe was analyzed for CH4 and very often
tested notably above ambient. Though not quantitative, the technique could be used on green
plants as well as the very prevalent standing dead stems from the previous year’s production
and clearly illustrated methane buildup within plant stems 15-30 cm above the marsh surface.

We followed up with intact blocks of Typha marsh platform held in the greenhouse, using a
continuous flow LGR cavity ring-down analyzer to measure methane concentrations in air drawn
from around green growing leaves or cut dead stems, over several full daily cycles. CH4 was
clearly detected around leaves and over the cut ends of dead stems in notably higher
concentration than background atmosphere and the gas boundary layer of the marsh sediment
block. Interestingly, over the course of two days and two nights, CH4 emission from around the
top 10 cm of young leaves increased at night and plummeted at dawn; measurements taken
with the LiCor 6400 gas exchange system showed that older leaves had strong stomatal control,
opening at dawn and closing nearly completely at night. Our current hypothesis is that older
leaves’ stomata, when open, allow substantial methane flux to the atmosphere, depleting the
pool of methane within the plant; this depletion occurs because methane diffusion into the
plant’s roots cannot replenish the methane pool within the plant as quickly as efflux occurs via
the mature leaves. The young leaves are plumbed into that same methane pool within the
plant; when the larger leaves’ conductance to the atmosphere surges in the morning, the
availability of CH4 within the plant drops rapidly, and the efflux from the younger leaves drops
rapidly as the pool is depleted. When the mature leaves’ stomata close at the end of the
afternoon, the methane begins building up again in the plant body, and methane begins to be
detected around the young leaf surfaces. We are currently working with a protocol to measure
both stomatal and cuticular conductance of young and old leaves in the Typha greenhouse
plants.

H5: Increased sediment aeration during neap tides will decrease net methane fluxes.

In addition to not finding a strong influence of GPP on ecosystem-scale CH4 flux, we did not find
evidence for a spring-neap tide modulating CH4 fluxes during our study period, either. It could
be that in non-drought years, water level draw down is not strong enough to induce a
detectable shift in methane oxidation. The strongest water level drawdown during neap tide
periods occurred in July and August 2022. During these periods, rapid increases in redox
potential indicated oxygen diffusion into the soil, however, we did not detect a decrease in CH4
fluxes during that period but after. Flux reduction occurred after the saltwater intrusion, when
fluxes were non-detectable possibly because of anaerobic methane oxidation coupled to sulfate
reduction (Forbrich & Sturtevant, submitted, Q3 below).

Q3) Climate Change Scenarios: How will carbon sequestration and greenhouse gas fluxes
respond to changes in climate and sea level?

H6: Annual net CO2 uptake and net CH4 emissions are controlled by the timing and volume of
freshwater discharge and tidal flow that control salinity level and, together, determine plant
productivity and electron acceptor availability for sediment organic matter decomposition.

To address our third question, collaborator Sulman coupled the land surface model ELM with
the reaction transport model PFLOTRAN using hydrological, biogeochemical and flux data from a
tidal wetland in the Plum Island Ecosystems LTER domain. In addition, Sulman’s postdoc LaFond-
Hudson incorporated a GPP reduction due to tidal fluctuations in salinity and water level into
ELM. This model development focused on low marsh settings, where flooding with seawater
occurs twice daily and the vegetation is Spartina alterniflora, a C4 grass.

To use ELM-PFLOTRAN to simulate CH4 and CO2 fluxes across the flooding and salinity gradient
at Plum Island tidal wetlands, we needed to further modify the model. We adapted ELM-
PFLOTRAN to simulate tidal flooding during bi-weekly spring tides, while no flooding occurs
during neap tides (Fig.7). Furthermore, we parametrized the vegetation response to salinity
fluctuations specifically for C3 plant functional types using experimental data from our field site.

Figure 7 Water table depth based on observations and HL model output for two selected neap tide cycles in summer 2022
and summer 2023 together with precipitation and evapotranspiration. The adapted ELM-PFLOTRAN is able to simulate
water level draw down during these periods driven by evapotranspiration, which is the prerequisite to assess the impact
that antecedent hydrological conditions have on the reduction of CH4 fluxes after seawater intrusion.

We gap-filled long-term data of river water salinity (9 years) for three locations and 19 year of
meteorological data to force the model. We selected three hydrologically different years, which
are associated with different salinity input to predict marsh porewater chemistry and CH4 fluxes
for three different sites along the estuarine salinity gradient. While the prediction of both
variables matched seasonal dynamics across years and sites, the magnitude of simulated CH4
fluxes was large compared to observations initially. Since the prediction of GPP and NPP were
considerably larger than the observed fluxes as well, we further adapted the salinity response

function controlling GPP with preliminary data from a lab experiment led by Cardon’s postdoc
Stein for Typha angustifolia (C3). This subsequently reduced carbon fluxes to more reasonable
values for the brackish site. We then focused on this site, addressing more specific questions
derived from our analysis above (Forbrich, Sulman, in prep.): Do antecedent hydrological
conditions modulate the impact of saltwater intrusion on tidal wetland CH4 cycling and can
ELM-PFLOTRAN capture the difference? Since ELM-PFLOTRAN is able to simulate anaerobic
methane oxidation, we also address the question of how much this process influences fluxes. To
do so, we run the simulations with and without the process activated.

We then addressed our question by running 3 different simulations, for which we created
different antecedent hydrological conditions for the saltwater intrusion by swapping the
meteorological drivers for 2022 and 2023 (HL, LH and LL scenarios, where LL is a freshwater run
only). Since evapotranspiration is thus lower in the LH run, the water level draw down is not as
strong as for the HL run, during drought conditions. We find that initially, CH4 and SO4
concentrations are higher in the LH than the HL run, but quickly after the flooding with
saltwater do the concentrations decrease relatively. Interestingly, differences between
simulations with and without anaerobic methane oxidation are small but distinct, indicating
higher concentrations with AMO than without. While these differences are relatively small, they
do impact the emissions. We find that under elevated salinity, AMO can reduce fluxes by about
50%, while under freshwater conditions it plays a less important role (Fig. 8).

Figure 8 Simulated surface methane flux for HL, LH, and LL scenarios with or without anaerobic methane oxidation (AMO)
shown as weekly means. Left panel shows monthly average fluxes and right panel shows annual total fluxes. No AMO
simulations are run without anaerobic methane oxidation. In summer 2022, antecedent hydrological conditions modulate

methane fluxes, but the reduced flux magnitude compared to LL (low salinity input) is primarily due to increased methane
