Parasite based biotherapy for cancer therapeutic strategies and preclinical applications
Authors: Rahim Mehdioghli, Alireza Chavshin, Fatemeh Bakhshipour, Haleh Hanifian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer immunotherapy has transformed oncology by enabling durable responses in multiple malignancies; however, its clinical efficacy remains limited by immune evasion, therapeutic resistance, and treatment-related toxicity. In this context, parasites—long considered pathogenic organisms—have emerged as context-dependent immunomodulatory systems capable of reshaping host immunity in both beneficial and detrimental directions.Main BodyThis mini-review critically synthesizes preclinical evidence on parasite-based cancer biotherapy, focusing on protozoa (Plasmodium spp., Toxoplasma gondii, Leishmania spp.) and selected helminths (Trichinella spiralis, Echinococcus granulosus). Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy has fundamentally reshaped the therapeutic landscape of oncology, offering durable clinical responses across multiple malignancies through strategies that harness host immune surveillance. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, adoptive cell therapies, and therapeutic cancer vaccines have demonstrated significant success in melanoma, lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and hematological cancers [1, 2] However, a substantial proportion of patients either fail to respond or develop resistance due to tumor immune evasion, intratumoral heterogeneity, and the establishment of profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments [2, 3].These limitations continue to constrain the overall impact of current immunotherapeutic modalities and highlight the need for alternative or complementary biological approaches [4, 5].

In parallel with advances in immuno-oncology, increasing attention has been directed toward organisms capable of naturally modulating host immune responses. Among these, parasites represent a unique and underexplored class of biological entities that have co-evolved with the human immune system for long-term survival [6–8] Through sophisticated immune evasion strategies, parasites actively shape innate and adaptive immune pathways, including antigen presentation, cytokine networks, and T-cell polarization, resulting in sustained immunological remodeling of the host ([4–5, 9]).

Importantly, the relationship between parasitic infection and cancer is inherently bidirectional. Certain chronic parasitic infections are well-established etiological factors in carcinogenesis. For example, Schistosoma haematobium is strongly associated with bladder cancer, while liver flukes such as Opisthorchis viverrini and Clonorchis sinensis are linked to cholangiocarcinoma and other hepatobiliary malignancies. These associations are primarily driven by chronic inflammation, repeated epithelial injury, oxidative stress, and long-term genomic and epigenetic instability within infected tissues [10, 11].

Conversely, experimental evidence from preclinical models suggests that selected protozoan and helminth parasites, or their derived products, may exert immunomodulatory effects that suppress tumor growth under controlled conditions [7, 8]. Studies involving Plasmodium spp., Toxoplasma gondii, Leishmania spp., and selected helminths have demonstrated tumor growth inhibition associated with immune activation and modulation of tumor-associated immune compartments [4, 12–14]. In addition, parasite-derived components such as excretory–secretory products and extracellular vesicles have shown the ability to influence angiogenesis, immune cell polarization, and tumor cell survival pathways in experimental systems [8, 15, 16].

Despite these promising findings, the current evidence base remains largely confined to in vitro and animal studies, with minimal clinical translation [4, 5, 17]. Key barriers include biosafety concerns, complex parasite life cycles, limited controllability of live organisms, and the absence of standardized therapeutic platforms [6, 7]. These challenges underscore the need to shift the focus from live parasitic infections toward parasite-derived molecules as more feasible immunotherapeutic candidates [8, 18].

Accordingly, this mini-review aims to provide an integrated and critical synthesis of parasite–cancer interactions within an immuno-oncology framework. Specifically, it addresses: (i) the dual role of parasites in carcinogenesis and tumor suppression, (ii) preclinical evidence supporting parasite-based and parasite-derived anticancer strategies, and (iii) translational challenges and future perspectives for integration into modern cancer immunotherapy.

Parasites in cancer: a dual biological role

Parasitic infections represent a biologically complex and context-dependent component of host–pathogen interactions shaped by long-term co-evolution, resulting in profound immune remodeling. In cancer biology, these interactions are inherently bidirectional, as parasites may either promote or suppress tumor development depending on species, infection chronicity, host immune status, and tumor microenvironmental context [4, 6–8, 10].

Helminth-associated carcinogenesis

A substantial body of epidemiological and mechanistic evidence demonstrates that chronic helminth infections act as established carcinogenic drivers in humans. Schistosoma haematobium is strongly associated with bladder cancer and has also been implicated in hepatocellular carcinoma, while Schistosoma japonicum is linked to hepatocellular carcinoma and colorectal cancer. Similarly, liver flukes such as Opisthorchis viverrini and Clonorchis sinensis are well-established etiological agents of cholangiocarcinoma and other hepatobiliary malignancies [10, 11]. These parasites promote carcinogenesis through chronic inflammation, sustained cytokine release, repeated epithelial injury and repair, fibrosis, and long-term oxidative stress, ultimately leading to DNA damage, epigenetic alterations, and genomic instability.

In addition, helminth-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) further amplify genotoxic stress, while persistent Th2 polarization and expansion of regulatory immune pathways suppress effective tumor immune surveillance, thereby creating a tumor-permissive microenvironment that facilitates malignant transformation [6, 7, 10, 11].

Parasite-associated antitumor systems

In contrast, experimental and preclinical evidence indicates that selected parasitic systems may exert antitumor effects under controlled conditions. Protozoan parasites including Plasmodium spp. [16, 19–22], Toxoplasma gondii [23–28], and Leishmania spp. [14, 29, 30], as well as parasite-derived products, have demonstrated the ability to reprogram host immunity toward tumor-suppressive phenotypes. These effects are mainly associated with enhanced type 1 immune responses, increased cytotoxic CD8⁺ T-cell and NK cell activity, and remodeling of tumor-associated immune compartments [2, 6, 7].

Importantly, antitumor activity is not restricted to live infection. Parasite-derived molecules such as excretory–secretory products, surface antigens, and extracellular vesicles can reproduce key immunomodulatory effects observed in infection models. These factors regulate dendritic cell maturation, macrophage polarization, and T-cell activation, thereby indirectly influencing tumor progression dynamics [6–8, 25, 31].

Context-dependent immune outcome

The coexistence of tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive outcomes reflects a fundamental principle in parasite–host biology: immune responses are highly dependent on temporal and contextual factors rather than fixed pathogenic or therapeutic properties. Acute or controlled exposure may promote immune activation and tumor suppression, whereas chronic and unresolved infection favors immunopathology, immune exhaustion, and carcinogenesis [5–9, 19].

Translational implications

Despite this duality, current evidence remains largely confined to preclinical systems, and the determinants governing the switch between pro-tumorigenic and antitumor outcomes remain incompletely understood. This highlights the importance of clearly distinguishing between active parasitic infections and parasite-derived therapeutic platforms in oncology. In this context, parasite-derived molecules may represent a safer and more controllable immunotherapeutic strategy compared with live organisms, offering improved translational feasibility while minimizing risks associated with persistent infection and systemic parasitism [5–9, 19].

Examples of parasite-based cancer biotherapies

An increasing body of experimental evidence highlights the potential of specific protozoan and helminth parasites, as well as parasite-derived molecules, in modulating tumor growth in preclinical cancer models. These effects are heterogeneous and strongly dependent on parasite species, host immune status, and tumor microenvironmental context.

Plasmodium spp.

Plasmodium species, the causative agents of malaria, represent one of the most extensively studied protozoa in experimental cancer models. Plasmodium yoelii infection has been shown to suppress hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression in murine models, primarily through enhancement of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T-cell and NK cell infiltration, accompanied by increased IFN-γ and TNF-α production [12, 32].

Additional studies indicate that Plasmodium-mediated modulation of angiogenic pathways and tumor-associated macrophage activity contributes to reduced tumor vascularization and growth [19, 20]. In combination settings, Plasmodium infection has been reported to enhance the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents such as gemcitabine in lung cancer models, suggesting potential synergistic immunomodulatory effects [21]. Furthermore, experimental radiotherapy models have demonstrated improved survival outcomes following Plasmodium exposure, likely mediated by immune activation and increased tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes [22]. More recently, parasite-derived extracellular vesicles have been implicated in the regulation of endothelial VEGF signaling, further supporting indirect anti-angiogenic effects [16]. However, these findings remain strictly preclinical and require careful interpretation due to variability in infection models and tumor systems.

Toxoplasma gondii

Toxoplasma gondii has been widely investigated for its ability to induce strong Th1-skewed immune responses. Attenuated strains have demonstrated tumor growth inhibition in murine breast cancer models through activation of dendritic cells and enhancement of CD8⁺ T-cell responses [23]. In vitro studies further suggest that infection with both virulent and avirulent strains can suppress cancer cell proliferation and migration, associated with reduced expression of proliferation markers such as Ki-67 [24]. Mechanistically, these effects are linked to parasite effector proteins, including rhoptry (ROP) and dense granule (GRA) proteins, which modulate host signaling pathways and immune activation [25]. Despite these promising observations, safety concerns associated with latent infection remain a major barrier to clinical translation.

Helminths

Several helminthic parasites have also demonstrated immunomodulatory and antitumor effects in experimental models. Echinococcus granulosus antigens and hydatid cyst fluid have been shown to suppress tumor growth in breast and colon cancer models through induction of apoptosis, oxidative stress modulation, and inhibition of angiogenesis [33, 34]. In contrast, Taenia crassiceps infection has been reported to alter tumor progression in melanoma models by modulating Th1/Th2 balance and regulating macrophage activity [35]. Trichinella spiralis excretory–secretory products exhibit direct cytotoxic effects on hepatocellular carcinoma cells, primarily through apoptosis induction and immune-mediated mechanisms [31]. Importantly, these effects are largely dependent on antigenic exposure rather than active parasitic infection, suggesting that isolated parasite-derived components may be more suitable for translational development.

Other protozoans

Less extensively studied protozoa also demonstrate potential antitumor activity. Leishmania major infection has been shown to suppress fibrosarcoma growth in murine models through dendritic cell activation and IFN-γ-mediated immune responses [14]. Additionally, attenuated Leishmania tarentolae has been developed as a vaccine vector capable of inducing tumor antigen-specific CD8⁺ T-cell responses, resulting in reduced tumor burden in melanoma and colon cancer models [30].

Trypanosoma cruzi-derived molecules have also been explored for their anticancer properties. Recombinant proteins such as gp82 have been shown to induce apoptosis in melanoma cells [36], while the calcineurin B subunit (TcCaNB) exerts antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic effects in melanoma and cervical cancer models [37]. Other molecules, including calreticulin and P21, have demonstrated antiangiogenic and immunostimulatory effects [38, 39]. Moreover, cruzipain has been shown to enhance T-cell mediated immune responses in experimental vaccine platforms, suggesting a potential adjuvant role in cancer immunotherapy strategies [40].

Overall, while parasite-based interventions demonstrate compelling biological activity in experimental cancer models, their clinical applicability remains highly speculative and requires rigorous validation in standardized and ethically controlled systems.

Mechanisms of anticancer action

Parasite-associated antitumor effects arise from a coordinated network of immunological and molecular processes that collectively reshape tumor progression. Rather than a single dominant pathway, these effects reflect an integrated reprogramming of immune surveillance, tumor vascular biology, cellular survival signaling, and the tumor microenvironment (TME). Immune reprogramming and type 1 antitumor immunity (core axis)

The most prominent mechanism underlying parasite-associated tumor suppression is the induction of a type 1–skewed immune response [2]. Experimental models consistently demonstrate enhanced activation of dendritic cells, increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, and robust expansion of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells following exposure to selected protozoan and helminth systems [4, 19, 20, 25].

This immune shift promotes re-establishment of tumor immune surveillance, particularly in immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments where effector T-cell activity is otherwise suppressed. In parallel, modulation of antigen presentation pathways enhances tumor antigen recognition and cytotoxic clearance. Collectively, this axis represents the central immunological driver of parasite-mediated tumor control [2–5, 19, 20] (Fig. 1).

Regulation of tumor angiogenesis and hypoxia signaling

Parasite-induced modulation of tumor vascular biology represents a second major antitumor mechanism. Preclinical studies demonstrate downregulation of key angiogenic mediators, including VEGF, HIF-1α, Angiopoietin-2, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2/9), leading to reduced neovascularization and impaired tumor oxygen and nutrient supply [16–20].

This anti-angiogenic effect results in decreased tumor growth kinetics and limited metastatic dissemination. In several models, parasite exposure is associated with reduced microvessel density and disruption of hypoxia-driven signaling cascades that normally sustain aggressive tumor phenotypes [12, 19–21] (Fig. 2).

Suppression of EMT and metastatic potential

Another key mechanism involves regulation of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), a central process in tumor invasion and metastasis. Experimental models indicate that parasite-associated immune and molecular signaling can restore epithelial characteristics while suppressing mesenchymal markers such as vimentin and Snail [12, 19–21].

This shift is accompanied by increased expression of epithelial adhesion molecules such as E-cadherin, resulting in reduced migratory capacity and metastatic potential. These effects are often mediated through upstream modulation of PI3K/Akt, MAPK, and GSK-3β signaling pathways, although pathway specificity varies across parasite systems [41, 42].

Induction of tumor cell apoptosis and cell cycle arrest

Parasite-derived systems and infection models also exert direct effects on tumor cell survival. These effects are mediated through both intrinsic (mitochondrial) and extrinsic apoptotic pathways, often involving caspase activation, mitochondrial membrane disruption, and regulation of Bcl-2 family proteins [4, 34, 36, 43].

In addition, cell cycle arrest at G0/G1 or G2/M phases has been reported in several parasite-derived experimental systems, contributing to reduced tumor proliferation. Importantly, these effects are frequently enhanced by immune-mediated cytotoxicity rather than direct parasite–tumor interaction alone [44].

Tumor microenvironment remodeling and immune suppression reversal

Parasite exposure significantly alters the cellular composition and functional state of the tumor microenvironment. A consistent finding across models is the reduction of immunosuppressive populations, including regulatory T cells (Tregs) and M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), alongside increased infiltration of effector immune cells [45].

This remodeling shifts the TME from an immunologically “cold” to a “hot” state, enhancing responsiveness to immune effector mechanisms. Additionally, parasite-induced cytokine networks reshape stromal–immune interactions, further contributing to tumor suppression [2, 4, 46].

Emerging modulation of immune checkpoints (translational axis)

Although still incompletely defined, emerging evidence suggests that parasite-induced immune activation may intersect with immune checkpoint regulation pathways. Enhanced Th1 inflammatory signaling may influence PD-1/PD-L1 axis activity within the tumor microenvironment, potentially increasing tumor sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade [2, 4, 19].

However, chronic immune stimulation may also induce compensatory upregulation of inhibitory receptors, highlighting a dynamic balance between immune activation and exhaustion. This duality suggests a potential rationale for combining parasite-derived immunomodulatory strategies with checkpoint inhibitors in future translational approaches [47, 48].

Comparative perspective

Within the broader field of cancer immunotherapy, multiple biologically derived platforms have been explored, including bacteria-based therapies (e.g., Salmonella, Listeria), oncolytic viruses (e.g., herpes simplex virus, adenovirus), and emerging cell- and cytokine-based approaches.

These modalities share a common principle: the ability to remodel the tumor microenvironment and stimulate innate and adaptive immune responses. However, parasite-based immunotherapy represents a fundamentally distinct and less-developed paradigm characterized by both unique immunological properties and substantial translational constraints [49–51].

From a mechanistic standpoint, parasites induce prolonged and multilayered immune activation due to their complex antigenic structures and evolutionary adaptation to immune evasion. Unlike oncolytic viruses or engineered bacteria, which typically trigger acute and transient inflammatory responses, parasitic infections may generate sustained immune modulation involving chronic Th1/Th2 polarization shifts, regulatory T-cell modulation, and long-term reshaping of the tumor immune microenvironment. This prolonged immunological imprint may be advantageous for durable tumor control but simultaneously increases the risk of off-target immunopathology [49, 50].

Despite these potential advantages, parasite-based platforms face several critical disadvantages compared with bacterial or viral systems. First, parasites possess complex and often multi-stage life cycles, making controlled propagation, genetic manipulation, and standardization significantly more challenging than in bacterial or viral vectors. Second, large-scale manufacturing of parasite-derived therapeutic agents is less established, with limited Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compatible production pipelines. Third, safety concerns remain substantial, particularly regarding host tissue invasion, latent infection, and unpredictable immune activation. These issues are considerably more pronounced than those associated with engineered viruses or attenuated bacterial strains, which are typically designed with defined replication limits and safety switches.

Another major limitation is the lack of precise tumor targeting. While certain bacterial and viral platforms can be engineered for tumor tropism or selective replication within hypoxic tumor regions, parasites generally lack inherent tumor specificity, and their distribution within the host is largely determined by complex host–parasite interactions rather than programmable targeting mechanisms. This limits their direct applicability as live therapeutic agents in clinical oncology [6–8, 18, 49–51].

In addition, regulatory and ethical barriers represent a significant translational hurdle. Public acceptance of intentional parasite administration is expected to be low compared with well-characterized viral or bacterial therapies. Consequently, the most realistic translational trajectory for parasite-based approaches likely lies not in the use of live organisms, but in the isolation and engineering of parasite-derived molecules, including excretory–secretory products, immunomodulatory proteins, and extracellular vesicles [8, 52].

In contrast, parasite-derived biomolecules offer a more feasible and safer alternative, with improved controllability, reduced pathogenic risk, and greater compatibility with existing drug development pipelines. These derivatives can be integrated with current immunotherapeutic strategies, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors, to enhance antitumor immune responses while minimizing systemic toxicity [6–8, 48].

Overall, while parasite-based immunotherapy provides a conceptually novel addition to the cancer immunotherapy landscape, its clinical translation is currently constrained by biological complexity, safety concerns, and manufacturing limitations. In comparison, bacterial and viral platforms remain more advanced in terms of engineering feasibility, regulatory progress, and clinical implementation. Therefore, parasites are best considered as a source of immunologically active molecules rather than direct live therapeutic agents in contemporary oncological applications.

Current preclinical studies of parasite-based cancer therapies have predominantly relied on intratumoral, intraperitoneal, or localized delivery approaches, which may not be universally applicable across different tumor types and anatomical sites. Although these administration routes can enhance local immune activation and reduce systemic toxicity, their clinical feasibility remains limited for metastatic or deeply inaccessible tumors. Systemic delivery strategies, including intravenous administration, may offer broader tumor accessibility; however, they face substantial challenges such as rapid immune clearance, off-target tissue distribution, limited tumor tropism, and the potential induction of excessive inflammatory responses. Oral delivery may represent a safer and more practical alternative for selected parasite-derived molecules or extracellular vesicles, but issues related to gastrointestinal degradation, bioavailability, and pharmacokinetic stability remain largely unresolved. Consequently, the optimization of administration routes, delivery platforms, and tissue-targeting strategies will be essential for the successful clinical translation of parasite-based immunotherapies.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Parasites, which have traditionally been considered solely as human pathogens, have recently emerged as unconventional yet promising candidates for cancer biotherapy [9]. An expanding body of experimental and preclinical evidence supports the antitumor potential of protozoans such as Plasmodium spp., Toxoplasma gondii, Leishmania spp., and Trypanosoma cruzi, as well as several helminth species [4, 12, 14, 19, 36]. These organisms exert multifaceted antitumor effects through immune activation, inhibition of angiogenesis, suppression of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), induction of apoptosis, and modulation of the tumor microenvironment [4, 12, 15, 17, 19]. Additional mechanisms, including oxidative stress induction, autophagy modulation, and metabolic reprogramming, may further broaden their therapeutic relevance [8, 9].

Despite this promising landscape, translating parasite-based therapies into clinical oncology presents several major challenges [8, 9]. Most existing data derive from in vitro experiments and animal models, necessitating rigorous investigation into biosafety, host immune tolerance, and effective delivery systems [6, 7]. Safety profiling, including evaluation of toxicity, immunogenicity, and the potential risk of parasite persistence or reactivation, must be comprehensively addressed in preclinical studies before clinical translation can be considered [8, 18]. Furthermore, the immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment and substantial interpatient heterogeneity may limit therapeutic efficacy and reproducibility across clinical settings [2, 9].

To address these barriers, future research should prioritize the identification and characterization of parasite-derived molecules with anticancer activity, alongside the development of genetically attenuated strains and purified immunotherapeutic platforms [8, 25]. Integration of these approaches with established immunotherapies, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors, may enhance antitumor efficacy while improving therapeutic controllability and safety [2]. In parallel, parasite-based vaccine strategies and engineered immunomodulatory platforms may contribute to the development of more personalized and tumor-specific therapeutic modalities [18, 39].

In conclusion, although still in its infancy, parasite-based cancer biotherapy represents a compelling and innovative direction in oncology. The unique capacity of parasite-derived systems to reshape the tumor microenvironment, enhance antitumor immunity, and interfere with oncogenic signaling pathways highlights their potential as both complementary and alternative approaches within the expanding field of immuno-oncology. Continued mechanistic investigation and translational refinement will be essential to determine whether these unconventional biological systems can ultimately be transformed into safe, standardized, and clinically effective anticancer therapies.
