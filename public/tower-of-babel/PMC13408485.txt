Human CD24+ dental papilla cells are competent seed cells for dentin-pulp regeneration via BMP2/SIRT1 axis
Authors: Cheng Liang, Zhi Liu, Jie Li, Cong Han, Yu Gao, Bohuai Zhou, Qiuyu Chen, Fei Bi, Chengcheng Liao, Weimin Wu, Tiantian Hu, Jingyi Zhang, Ding Bai, Yiping Chen, Zhonghan Li, Weidong Tian, Tian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegeneration of the dentin-pulp complex is essential for tooth integrity and function. However, the inherent cell heterogeneity limits our understanding of lineage-specific subsets critical for efficient odontogenesis and regenerative outcomes. Here, we demonstrated that CD24+ human dental papilla cells (hDPCs) exhibit robust odontogenic differentiation capacity and drive coordinated regeneration of well-vascularized pulp and structurally integrated dentin tissues in both ectopic murine and preclinical in situ minipig models, significantly outperforming conventional dental pulp stem cells. Mechanistically, we delineate a BMP2/SIRT1 axis where elevated BMP signaling sustains SIRT1 expression and promotes mitochondrial metabolism and odontogenic capacity. Furthermore, BMP signaling induces VEGF expression, enhancing neovascularization via paracrine effects. CD24 is also a downstream marker of BMP signaling, though it does not directly mediate differentiation. Together, CD24+ hDPCs represent a regeneration-competent subpopulation that integrates mitochondrial metabolism and signaling crosstalk to enable coordinated dentin-pulp regeneration, representing a translationally relevant cell source for dental tissue engineering.Subject terms: Regeneration, Mesenchymal stem cells, Stem-cell niche

Introduction

The dentin-pulp complex constitutes a structurally and functionally integrated unit in which the dental pulp and surrounding dentin interact dynamically to maintain tooth vitality1. The pulp, a highly vascularized connective tissue, provides nutritional support and regulates dentinogenesis, particularly in response to external stimuli2. Characterized by its organized dentinal tubule architecture, the dentin functions as a mineralized barrier that enables bidirectional communication with the underlying pulp, thereby supporting effective injury responses and reparative processes3. However, pathological insults such as severe caries, trauma, or infection can disrupt this delicate balance, leading to inflammation, pulp necrosis, and progressive dentin degradation. Despite advances in elucidating these pathologies, the dentin-pulp complex exhibits limited regenerative capacity due to its anatomical complexity and structural constraints4. Consequently, conventional clinical interventions often fail to fully restore its physiological function or structural integrity, culminating in tooth loss5.

Regenerative medicine and tissue engineering hold considerable potential in restoring integrated pulp and dentin structures6,7. We previously developed a bioengineered tooth root utilizing autologous treated dentin matrix (TDM) in non-human primates, achieving sustained occlusal function over 2 years8. However, the long-term therapeutic outcome was limited by the absence of vascularized pulp, highlighting the necessity for simultaneous regeneration of both pulp and dentin components. Current regenerative strategies have primarily focused on dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs), stem cells from the apical papilla (SCAPs), and stem cells from human exfoliated deciduous teeth (SHED)9. While these cells rarely produce structurally organized dentinal tubules, the hallmark of functional dentin10. This limitation likely arises from the heterogeneity of dental stem cells, in which only select subsets may possess robust dentinogenic capacity11,12. Therefore, the identification of defined progenitor subpopulations represents a critical unmet need in achieving integrated dentin-pulp complex regeneration.

Given that both dentin and pulp originate from the dental papilla, which represents a developmentally appropriate source for ideal seed cell candidates12,13. Compared to mature pulp, the dental papilla retains less differentiated hDPCs with high proliferative capacity and multilineage differentiation potential, making them particularly amenable to regenerative applications14,15. Notably, our group identified Cd24a+ multipotent stem cells derived from murine dental papilla as a distinct population16. Lineage-tracing analyses further demonstrated that Cd24a+ cells contribute significantly to the dentin-pulp complex development in murine17,18. Nonetheless, the functional roles, odontogenic potential, and underlying regulatory mechanisms of their human counterparts remain poorly understood, representing critical knowledge gaps that must be addressed for clinical translation.

In this study, we characterize CD24+ hDPCs as a functionally distinct subpopulation that exhibits exceptional odontogenic differentiation potential and robust pro-angiogenic capacity. These cells are spatially enriched in the dentin formation region during development and also emerge beneath reparative dentin in response to deep carious lesions, implicating active participation in both developmental and injury-induced dentinogenesis. Under two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) culture conditions, CD24+ hDPCs display enhanced migratory behavior and lineage-specific differentiation capacities. When transplanted into ectopic murine and orthotopic minipig regenerative models, CD24+ hDPCs reliably reconstruct highly organized, vascularized dentin-pulp structures closely resembling native tissue architecture, demonstrating significantly improved regenerative outcomes compared to both CD24− hDPCs and unsorted, conventionally utilized human dental pulp stem cells (hDPSCs). At the mechanistic level, we reveal a signaling-metabolism axis whereby BMP2-driven activation of SIRT1 orchestrates mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolic reprogramming, critically supporting odontogenic differentiation. Overall, our findings identify a human lineage-specific progenitor population and delineate a signaling-metabolic regulatory pathway underpinning their enhanced regenerative functionality, thereby significantly advancing translational regenerative endodontic strategies.

Results

Human CD24+ mesenchymal cells define a lineage-specific progenitor subset restricted to dental papilla/pulp compartments

Previous studies, including ours, have identified Cd24a+ stem cells as key contributors to odontoblast generation during murine tooth development16–18. However, the distribution and regenerative potential of the human CD24+ subset remained poorly defined. To specify the distribution pattern of the human CD24+ mesenchymal cells, we performed an integrative single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of publicly available datasets from multiple human dental tissues, including dental papilla (GSM6122993)19, adult healthy and carious tooth pulp (GSM4998461, GSM7990494)20,21, periodontium (GSM4904135)20, and gingiva (GSM5005049)22 (Fig. 1a–c). Following batch correction23,24, we identified 12 transcriptionally distinct clusters (0–11), which were annotated into seven major cell types based on canonical lineage markers: dental mesenchymal cells (DMCs) (MSX1, BARX1, and PAX9), endothelial cells (CD34, VWF, and PECAM1), immune cells (PTPRC), pericytes (ACTA2, MCAM, and PDGFRB), neural cells (ALDH1A1, MPZ, and S100B), fibroblasts (COL6A2, COL6A1, and COL3A1), and epithelial cells (KRT17, KRT15, and KRT14)12,19,22,25 (Fig. 1d, e). Tissue origin mapping confirmed that DMCs were largely distributed in dental papilla and pulp tissues, whereas epithelial cells were enriched in gingival and periodontal tissues (Fig. 1f). Among these cell populations, CD24 expression was primarily observed in both KRT14+ epithelial cells (cluster 11) and MSX1+ DMCs (cluster 2) (Fig. 1g). Interestingly, within the mesenchymal compartment, CD24 expression was specifically enriched within the dental papilla and dental pulp, with minimal detection in the periodontium and gingiva (Fig. 1c, h). Moreover, it was predominantly confined to a distinct subset of MSX1+ cells, rather than being broadly distributed (Fig. 1h), implying that CD24 marks a distinct mesenchymal population.

To further characterize this subset, we extracted DMCs from the dental papilla, healthy tooth pulp, and carious tooth pulp and re-clustered them into 11 transcriptionally distinct subgroups (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Fig. 1a), which highly expressed DMC markers MSX1 and SOX9 (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed two differentiation continuums initiating from cluster 1 (Fig. 1j), in which CD24 is highly co-expressed with progenitor markers LHX6 and THY1 (Fig. 1k and Supplementary Fig. 1c). Furthermore, transcription factors SP7 (encoding OSX) and RUNX2 were widely expressed across mesenchymal clusters, while mineralization-related markers ALPL and BGLAP (encoding ALP and OCN) were enriched in CD24− clusters (2, 3, 8, 9, and 10) and remained low in CD24+ cluster 1 (Fig. 1k and Supplementary Fig. 1d). To further distinguish CD24+ from CD24− DMCs, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis between CD24+ (cluster 1) and CD24− DMCs (clusters 0, 3, 4). Cluster 2, despite of CD24−, was excluded due to its high expression of terminal differentiation markers (e.g., BGLAP) and low levels of progenitor markers (e.g., LHX6 and THY1), thereby avoiding confounding effects from differences in differentiation status (Fig. 1k and Supplementary Fig. 1c). CD24+ DMCs showed significant enrichment for genes related to “ossification,” “osteoblast differentiation,” “biomineral tissue development,” and “collagen-containing extracellular matrix” (Fig. 1l and Supplementary Fig. 1e, f), suggesting an odontogenic lineage bias during development. Notably, CD24+ DMCs were diverse from previously reported subpopulations such as FGF3+ (cluster 0), GLDN+ (clusters 2, 3, and 5), and IFITM5+ (cluster 0) cells12,26 (Supplementary Fig. 1g), supporting the interpretation that CD24+ DMCs constitute a unique progenitor-like population positioned toward an early, relatively undifferentiated state within the human dental mesenchymal hierarchy and linked to dental papilla and pulp development.

CD24+ DMCs contribute to both developmental and reparative dentinogenesis in vivo

To test the functional implication of CD24+ DMCs inferred from transcriptomic data, we started with the examination of their spatial localization at developmental stages of human tooth samples (Supplementary Fig. 2a). At the late bell stage, we found that CD24+ DMCs were densely concentrated in the odontoblast-forming region of the dental papilla (Fig. 1m), a pattern similar to that observed for murine Cd24a+ populations18. As development progressed into the root elongation stage, this type of cells became sparsely distributed and migrated apically, with only a small subset remaining within the central pulp (Fig. 1n, o), indicating a spatial correlation with active dentinogenesis. This developmental redistribution is consistent with a maturation-associated shift in the dental papilla/pulp mesenchymal composition, in which the CD24+ compartment becomes less prominent as dentinogenesis slows and tissues mature.

Beyond developmental contexts, dental pulp can generate reparative dentin when tooth caries occur27. To assess whether CD24+ DMCs participate in the reparative dentinogenesis, we examined human pulp tissues from healthy teeth and teeth with deep caries (Supplementary Fig. 2b). In carious tooth pulp, increased expression levels of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL1, IL6) confirmed the presence of an inflammatory microenvironment (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Although CD24+ DMCs were relatively scarce in healthy pulp (HP), a remarkable layer of CD24+ cells was detected accumulating beneath reparative dentin in carious tooth pulp (CP) (Fig. 1p). Flow cytometry and gene expression analyses validated a rise in both the proportion of CD24+ DMCs (from 2.68% to 4.68%) and CD24 transcript levels in CP (Fig. 1q–s). Taken together, these findings align with our single-cell analysis, demonstrating that CD24+ DMCs are not only enriched during tooth development in regions of active dentin formation but are also reactivated during reparative dentinogenesis in adulthood, suggesting their close association with both developmental and caries-induced odontogenic processes (Fig. 1t).

CD24+ hDPCs demonstrate enhanced proliferative, migratory, and odontogenic potential in vitro

We next evaluated the possibility of CD24+ DMCs as seed cells for dentin-pulp complex regeneration. To this end, we cultured hDPCs isolated from dental papilla at the late bell stage of developing human third molars. Flow cytometric analysis showed that approximately 25.1% of adherent hDPCs were CD24+, a proportion substantially higher than the 3.9% observed in mature hDPSCs (Fig. 2a, b). Phenotypic analysis confirmed that these cultured hDPCs displayed a mesenchymal profile, characterized by positive expression of CD90 and CD73, and absence of the hematopoietic and endothelial markers CD45 and CD31 (Supplementary Fig. 3a). To characterize CD24+ hDPCs in vitro, we assessed their dynamic expression during serial passaging. The proportion of CD24+ cells peaked at passage 3 (P3) and subsequently declined, whereas CD73 expression remained consistently high (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). Interestingly, gene expression analysis revealed that CD24 gene expression levels coincided with those of odontogenic transcription factors, including LHX6, PAX9, BMP2, and DMP1, suggesting a potential association between CD24 expression and odontogenic lineage priming (Supplementary Fig. 3d). Accordingly, CD24+ hDPCs were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) from P2 and P3 cultured hDPCs for subsequent functional evaluation and dentin-pulp complex regeneration assays (Fig. 2d). CD24+ hDPCs exhibited approximately 4-fold higher CD24 transcript levels (Fig. 2e), with morphologically indistinguishable from their CD24− counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 3e). Colony-forming assay demonstrated significantly enhanced colony formation efficiency in CD24+ hDPCs (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g). EdU staining showed that ~30% of cultured CD24+ hDPCs were EdU+, approximately two-fold higher than CD24− hDPCs, indicative of greater proliferative capacity (Supplementary Fig. 3h, i). Moreover, both scratch-wound and transwell assays showed increased migratory activity in CD24+ hDPCs relative to CD24− controls (Supplementary Fig. 3j–m).

To evaluate odontogenic differentiation under 2D culture conditions, CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs were subjected to odontogenic induction for 14 days. We found that CD24+ cells exhibited stronger Alizarin Red staining (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 3n) and higher expression levels of mineralization-related markers, including RUNX2, ALPL, and COL1A1 (Fig. 2g). Western blot analysis confirmed increased protein levels of odontogenic markers DSPP and DMP1 after induction, further supporting their enhanced odontogenic potential (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 3o).

To further investigate the functional characteristics of CD24+ hDPCs in a 3D environment that more closely recapitulates the physiological conditions in vivo, we established a collagen hydrogel-based 3D spheroid culture system (Fig. 2i). Under both suspension and attachment conditions, high-density spheroids (4 × 106 cells/mL) demonstrated pronounced contraction and peripheral migration of CD24+ spheroids relative to CD24− ones (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). To rule out potential confounding effects caused by excessive cell density-mediated mechanical contraction, we further employed a lower cell density (2 × 106 cells/mL), which exhibited minimal shrinkage throughout the 4-day observation period. Nevertheless, CD24+ spheroids consistently exhibited earlier initiation and more extensive outward cell migration than CD24− controls (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Corresponding live-cell fluorescent staining further confirmed the enhanced migratory behavior of CD24+ spheroids by day 4 (Fig. 2k, l and Supplementary Fig. 4d). Additionally, CD24+ spheroids exhibited higher proliferative activity than CD24− spheroids, while both groups maintained high viability (>95%) throughout the culture period (Supplementary Fig. 2e–g). Upon odontogenic induction for 7 days, CD24+ spheroids also showed significantly increased calcium nodule formation (Fig. 2m–o) and upregulated mineralization markers BGLAP and SPP1 (encoding OPN) (Supplementary Fig. 2h). Collectively, these results establish CD24+ hDPCs as a functionally defined subpopulation with enhanced proliferation, migratory, and odontogenic differentiation capacities in both 2D and 3D culture systems.

CD24+ hDPCs mediate organized dentin-pulp regeneration in ectopic and preclinical in situ models

To evaluate the in vivo regenerative capacity of CD24+ hDPCs for dentin-pulp complex restoration, we combined low-density CD24+ hDPCs with a collagen-based hydrogel to generate an injectable CD24+ hDPC-loaded collagen gel (CD24+ hDPC CG). This strategy is well-suited to the complex morphology of root canals by its injectable property. To assess regenerative efficacy, CD24+ or CD24− hDPC CG, along with unsorted hDPSCs as controls, were injected into swine TDM segments and subcutaneously implanted into immunodeficient mice for 4 weeks (Fig. 3a). Histologically, H&E, Masson’s trichrome staining, and Sirius red staining revealed that the CD24+ hDPC CG generated a well-organized dentin-pulp complex structure, characterized by newly deposited dentin and collagen matrix, polarized odontoblast-like cell layer, and abundant vasculature within pulp-like tissues (Fig. 3b–f and Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). In contrast, CD24− hDPC and hDPSC controls produced poorly organized tissues with sparse vasculature and lacked dentin formation. Furthermore, immunofluorescent staining showed strong expression of DSPP and DMP1 in the peripheral odontoblast-like layer of the CD24+ group, along with extensive CD31+ vessels and GFAP+ nerve fibers (Fig. 3g, h). Notably, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) visualized apparent dentinal tubules (red arrowheads) within the regenerated dentin (Fig. 3i). Human-specific NM95 staining confirmed that the regenerated odontoblast-like cells originated from transplanted CD24+ hDPCs (Supplementary Fig. 5d). These data suggest that the tissues regenerated by CD24+ hDPCs recurred the canonical zonal organization of native dental pulp, including regenerative dentin, an odontoblast-rich periphery, a cell-rich zone, and a central cell-poor zone, indicative of coordinated soft and hard tissue regeneration (Fig. 3j).

To further validate its translational potential in preclinical models, we established an in situ dentin-pulp regeneration model in a miniature pig, which recapitulates the anatomical and physiological properties of human dentition. Following standard root canal preparation and disinfection, immature premolars were filled with either CD24+ or CD24− hDPC CG and sealed for 4 weeks (Fig. 3k). Notably, radiographic imaging showed continued root elongation and apical foramen closure in the CD24+ group, which was absent in CD24− controls (Fig. 3l, m). Histological examination revealed dense cellular organization and formation of a continuous polarized odontoblast-like layer along dentin walls in the CD24+ group (Fig. 3n, o). In contrast, CD24− group failed to generate comparable reparative structures. Immunohistochemistry further demonstrated robust DSPP expression in the regenerated tissues of CD24+ group, indicative of active odontogenic differentiation (Fig. 3p). Extensive neovascularization was also evident, as shown by abundant blood vessels and CD31+ vascular lumens (Fig. 3q–s). Again, NM95 immunostaining confirmed the presence of human-derived cells within the regenerated tissue (Supplementary Fig. 5e), substantiating donor cell engraftment.

To evaluate biosafety, CD24+ and CD24− hDPC CG were injected intracutaneously into immunocompetent rats (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). At 1 day post-injection, histological analysis showed mild inflammatory cell infiltration in both CD24+ and CD24− groups compared with the sham control (Supplementary Fig. 6c), accompanied by increased local IL-1β and TNF-α signals (Supplementary Fig. 6d–f). Systemic assessments showed no significant differences among groups in complete blood count parameters, including WBC (white blood cells), LYMPH (lymphocytes), MON (monocytes), GRAN (granulocytes), RBC (red blood cells), HGB (hemoglobin), and PLT (platelets), or in serum biochemical indices of liver function (ALT, alanine aminotransferase; AST, aspartate aminotransferase; γ-GT, gamma-glutamyl transferase) and kidney function (Ca2+, Mg2+, urea, and creatinine) (Supplementary Fig. 6g). At 4 weeks post-transplantation, body weight remained comparable across groups (Supplementary Fig. 6h), and inspection of the injection sites revealed no evidence of tumor formation (Supplementary Fig. 6i). Histological examination of major organs and repeat hematology/biochemistry analyses at 4 weeks revealed no notable abnormalities (Supplementary Fig. 6j, k), indicating the absence of overt systemic toxicity under the tested conditions.

Mitochondrial metabolism governs the odontogenic differentiation of CD24+ hDPCs

Building upon the observed in vitro and in vivo dentin-pulp regenerative capacity of CD24+ hDPCs, we subsequently investigated the underlying molecular mechanisms regulating their lineage specification. For this purpose, bulk RNA-seq was performed on CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs prior to odontogenic induction (Fig. 4a). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct transcriptomic profiles between the two populations (Supplementary Fig. 7a), in which 375 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 233 upregulated and 142 downregulated genes in CD24+ hDPCs (Fig. 4b). GO enrichment analysis highlighted pathways associated with “positive regulation of cell migration,” “cadherin-mediated cell-cell adhesion,” and “collagen-containing extracellular matrix” (Fig. 4c, d and Supplementary Fig. 7b), consistent with the enhanced migratory and condensation phenotypes observed in CD24+ hDPCs (Fig. 2j, k). Several members of the cadherin and chemokine families, such as PCDH20, PCDH7, PCDHB2, CDH4, CDH13, and CCL7, were markedly increased, suggesting a potential regulatory role in these cellular behaviors (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Notably, terms related to “mitochondrial transport,” “regulation of mitochondrial membrane potential,” and “mitochondrion morphogenesis” were also significantly enriched (Fig. 4c, d), implicating the potential modulatory functions of mitochondrial metabolism in CD24+ hDPC differentiation.

Mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming has been shown to influence the differentiation of various stem cell types, including muscle satellite cells, intestinal stem cells, and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells28–30. Accordingly, to test if the robust odontogenic differentiation potential of CD24+ hDPC is controlled by mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming, we examined the spatial relationship between mitochondria and CD24+ DMCs in human teeth. We observed a prominent colocalization of CD24 expression with high mitochondrial density in both developing and carious tooth pulp tissues (Fig. 4e, f). Particularly, in carious tooth pulp, mitochondrial density and CD24 expression exhibited a clear spatial gradient, progressively decreasing from regions beneath the lesion toward the central pulp, implying a close correlation between mitochondrial enrichment in CD24+ DMCs and their odontogenic potential in response to carious stimuli.

To characterize mitochondrial function in vitro, we compared mitochondrial morphology and metabolic output in CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs. Prior to induction, mitochondrial number and length were comparable between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 7d–g). Upon odontogenic induction, mitochondrial fragmentation increased in both populations, yet CD24+ hDPCs exhibited nearly doubled mitochondrial fragment length and number compared to CD24− controls (Fig. 4g–k), indicating heightened mitochondrial remodeling. Functional metabolic assays using Seahorse analysis revealed significantly elevated mitochondrial respiration in CD24+ hDPCs, with increased basal oxygen consumption, maximal respiratory capacity, and ATP production (Fig. 4l–n). Immunoblotting and RT-qPCR confirmed higher expression levels of PGC1α and mitochondrial proteins and upregulation of mitochondrial biogenesis regulators (PPARGC1A, TFAM) and odontogenic markers (DSPP, DMP1) in CD24+ hDPCs (Fig. 4o, p). To probe the functional requirement of mitochondrial respiration, we perturbed electron transport chain activity using antimycin A and rotenone (AA/ROT, 1 μM). AA/ROT reduced ATP production both in CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs while maintaining high cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 7h–j). Notably, AA/ROT led to a robust suppression of odontogenic markers in CD24+ hDPCs, whereas CD24− cultures showed a slight response of DMP1 (Fig. 4q). This was accompanied by reduced PPARGC1A and TFAM expression (Supplementary Fig. 7k), consistent with compromised mitochondrial biogenesis. Collectively, these data indicate that mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming is required for the odontogenic differentiation of CD24+ hDPCs.

BMP2-activated BMP signaling promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and odontogenic differentiation in CD24+ hDPCs

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying odontogenic differentiation in CD24+ hDPCs, we performed pathway enrichment analysis on the transcriptome data. The analysis highlighted several critical signaling cascades potentially involved in odontogenic regulation, including “canonical Wnt signaling pathway” and “positive regulation of BMP signaling pathway” (Fig. 4c, d). Indeed, we found that CD24+ hDPCs exhibited increased nuclear-localized p-SMAD1/5/9 during odontogenic induction, indicating enhanced BMP signaling activity (Fig. 5a, b, and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c), whereas CD24− hDPCs showed elevated β-catenin and AXIN2 expression, consistent with predominant WNT signaling (Supplementary Fig. 8d, e).

Given the known roles of BMP and WNT signaling in dental cell-fate determination31, we next examined the extent to which these pathways regulate mitochondrial function and odontogenic differentiation in CD24+ hDPCs by genetic and pharmacological approaches. To determine the functional role of BMP signaling, we treated cells with the BMP pathway inhibitor LDN193189 (1 μM)32. This intervention effectively suppressed p-SMAD1/5/9 in both subsets (Fig. 5c, d, and Supplementary Fig. 8f). Of note, in CD24+ hDPCs, BMP inhibition led to significantly reduced mitochondrial number and decreased expression of TFAM, as well as downregulation of odontogenic markers (Fig. 5e–g and Supplementary Fig. 8g). Conversely, CD24− hDPCs exhibited only mild reductions in mitochondrial proteins, with no significant decrease in the expression of TFAM, OSX, and DMP1. Consistently, histological analysis verified reduced mitochondrial density in CD24+ hDPCs following BMP inhibition (Fig. 5h, i), accompanied by impaired ATP production (Fig. 5j). RT-qPCR analysis similarly demonstrated decreased expression of PPARGC1A, NRF1, TFAM, DSPP, and DMP1 upon BMP signaling blockade (Fig. 5k). To ensure that these effects were specifically attributed to BMP pathway inhibition rather than pharmacological off-target effects, we employed siRNA-mediated knockdown of the BMP receptor BMPR1A in CD24+ hDPCs. In addition to suppressed p-SMAD1/5/9 levels (Supplementary Fig. 8h, i), genetic inhibition of BMP signaling also resulted in markedly reduced mitochondrial and odontogenic protein expression in CD24+ hDPCs (Supplementary Fig. 8j, k). These results consistently demonstrated that BMP signaling specifically modulates mitochondrial metabolism and odontogenic differentiation in CD24+ hDPCs by ligand-receptor binding pathway. Interestingly, BMP signaling inhibition resulted in highly increased active β-catenin (Supplementary Fig. 8l, m), indicating reciprocal activation of the canonical WNT pathway in CD24+ hDPCs. This observation aligns with our single-cell RNA-seq data, which showed that CD24+ cells expressed high levels of BMP ligands (e.g., BMP2) and WNT antagonists (SFRP2, WIF1), but relatively low levels of the canonical WNT ligands (Fig. 5l, m, and Supplementary Fig. 8n).

To identify the source of BMP activation, we examined the expression of key BMP pathway components. BMPR1A expression was comparable between CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs (Fig. 5n), suggesting that the differential pathway activity is more likely attributable to ligand availability rather than receptor abundance. We therefore assessed BMP ligand expression and found that BMP2 and BMP6 were significantly upregulated in CD24+ hDPCs, with BMP2 showing dominant expression, whereas BMP7 and BMP9 did not differ significantly between the two populations (Fig. 5o–q and Supplementary Fig. 8o). Thus, the elevated BMP signaling in CD24+ hDPCs is most plausibly driven by endogenous BMP2.

To determine whether BMP2 functions upstream in this program, we performed loss- and gain-of-function experiments using siRNA-mediated BMP2 knockdown and exogenous BMP2 supplementation. Silencing BMP2 in CD24+ hDPCs markedly reduced the expression of PPARGC1A, TFAM, and DSPP (Fig. 5r). Conversely, exogenous BMP2 supplementation increased the expression of these genes in CD24+ hDPCs, while CD24− hDPCs showed minimal response under the same conditions (Fig. 5s and Supplementary Fig. 8p). Collectively, these results support a model in which BMP2-mediated activation of BMP signaling preferentially promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and odontogenic differentiation in CD24+ hDPCs.

SIRT1 links BMP signaling to redox balance and metabolic regulation in CD24+ hDPCs

Given our findings that BMP signaling enhances mitochondrial function and promotes odontogenic differentiation in CD24+ hDPCs, we next sought to elucidate how mitochondrial metabolic remodeling contributes to this process. We first characterized mitochondrial dynamics during odontogenic induction. Prior to induction, no apparent differences in mitochondrial fragmentation were observed between CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs (Fig. 6a, b). Upon odontogenic induction, however, mitochondrial biogenesis regulator PPARGC1A and fission-related genes (FIS1, DRP1) were significantly upregulated, whereas mitochondrial fusion genes (MFN1, MFN2) were downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 9a), indicating a close association of active mitochondrial remodeling with differentiation progression. Consistent with this notion, differentiated CD24+ hDPCs displayed more pronounced mitochondrial fragmentation and significantly higher expression of FIS1 and DRP1 compared to CD24− cells (Fig. 6a, c).

Mitochondrial oxidative capacity is a metabolic hallmark of osteogenic differentiation30,33. We then evaluated oxidative stress levels using MitoTracker and Mito SOX staining. The results indicated significantly enhanced oxidative stress levels in CD24+ hDPCs under inductive conditions (Fig. 6d, e). Despite this elevated oxidative state, mitochondrial integrity remained largely intact, correlating with higher expression of mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme glutathione reductase (Mito GR) (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 9b), indicative of sustained redox homeostasis. In parallel, CD24+ hDPCs displayed elevated autophagic activity following induction, as demonstrated by increased LC3+ puncta formation (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d). This observation is in line with established roles of autophagy in mitochondrial quality control during cellular differentiation34,35. Of note, autophagosome formation was more prominent in CD24+ cells compared to CD24− controls (Fig. 6g, h), suggesting greater mitochondrial clearance capacity. These data demonstrate that CD24+ hDPCs undergo strong and coordinated mitochondrial reprogramming to support odontogenic differentiation.

To ask how the mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming of CD24+ hDPCs is regulated by BMP signaling, we probed the effects of pathway inhibition using LDN193189, with a particular focus on antioxidant enzymes, by reasoning that the intracellular redox balance is primarily maintained by these systems36,37. We found that BMP inhibition resulted in potently reduced expression of Mito GR and SOD1 in CD24+ hDPCs, while SOD2 levels remained unaltered (Fig. 6i, j, and Supplementary Fig. 9e), suggesting that BMP signaling sustains oxidative homeostasis predominantly through SOD1. In agreement, immunofluorescent staining verified the reduction of SOD1 expression and autophagosome formation following BMP inhibition (Fig. 6k–p), accompanied by increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels detected via flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 9f, g).

SIRT1, a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)-dependent histone deacetylase, has been reported to act downstream of BMP signaling and play a crucial role in the regulation of mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative stress responses, and autophagy38–43. Accordingly, we investigated SIRT1 involvement in this regulatory axis. During odontogenic differentiation, SIRT1 was significantly upregulated in CD24+ hDPCs compared with CD24− counterparts (Fig. 6q, r). However, this induction was blunted by pharmacological BMP pathway inhibition as well as by BMP2 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 9h, i), accompanied by reduced expression levels of PGC1α, TFAM, and DMP1 (Figs. 5e, g, 6s, t). Consistently, exogenous BMP2 significantly increased SIRT1 expression in CD24+ hDPCs, whereas CD24− cells remained unresponsive, and BMP2 treatment was further associated with enhanced autophagosome formation in CD24+ hDPCs (Supplementary Fig. 9j–l). To further test SIRT1 function, we applied the SIRT1 inhibitor EX527 and siRNA-mediated SIRT1 knockdown. Silencing SIRT1 was associated with an apparent reduction in BMP2 expression, together with decreased expression of genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis and odontogenic differentiation (Fig. 6u). Likewise, EX527 treatment also resulted in downregulation of PPARGC1A and DSPP in CD24+ hDPCs (Fig. 6v). Together, these results identify SIRT1 as a key downstream effector of BMP2-associated signaling in CD24+ hDPCs.

CD24+ hDPCs promote vascularization of regenerated dental pulp via BMP/VEGF paracrine signaling cascade

Our in vivo transplantation data highlighted CD24+ hDPCs in generating highly vascularized pulp-like tissue. GO enrichment also revealed significant activation of angiogenesis-related processes, including “positive regulation of endothelial cell chemotaxis” and “positive regulation of angiogenesis” (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b), aligning with the observed vascular phenotype. To further investigate their pro-angiogenic role, we evaluated the effects of conditioned media (CM) derived from CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs on human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (Fig. 7a). Both scratch and transwell assays demonstrated that CD24+ CM significantly enhanced HUVEC migration (Fig. 7b–d), whereas no differences were observed in HUVEC proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 10c). Furthermore, the tube formation assay showed that CD24+ CM markedly promoted angiogenesis, evidenced by increased branching and node formation (Fig. 7e, f). Importantly, pretreatment of CD24+ hDPCs with GW4869, an extracellular vesicle (EV) secretion inhibitor, substantially reduced their pro-angiogenic effect on HUVECs (Fig. 7e, f), indicating an EV-dependent paracrine mechanism. To recapitulate in vivo-like angiogenesis, we employed the 3D culture model described above to assess endothelial sprouting (Fig. 7g). HUVECs exposed to CD24+ CM exhibited earlier and more extensive sprouting compared to those treated with CD24− CM (Fig. 7h), corroborating the angiogenic competence of CD24+ hDPCs.

Since vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) plays a central role in angiogenesis during tissue regeneration44, we assessed VEGF expression in CD24+ hDPCs. Consistent with the above results, CD24+ hDPCs expressed markedly higher VEGF protein levels compared to CD24− cells, as evidenced by Western blot and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Fig. 7i–k). CD24, a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored surface protein, has been widely implicated in stem cell maintenance and lineage specification across various tissues45,46. To test if CD24 expression reflects BMP activity and odontogenic potential in hDPCs, we first assessed the impacts of the BMP pathway and mitochondrial function inhibition on CD24 expression. Treatment with either LDN193189 or AA/ROT led to significant downregulation of CD24 expression (Fig. 8a), suggesting that CD24 is responsive to upstream regulatory cues associated with odontogenic regulation.

To further determine if CD24 modulates odontogenic differentiation, we subjected plated CD24+ hDPCs to siRNA-mediated reduction in CD24 expression (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Following odontogenic induction, we found that silencing CD24 neither affected SMAD1/5/9 phosphorylation (Fig. 8b, c), nor did it alter mitochondrial abundance or the expression of PGC1α, TFAM, RUNX2, COLI, BGLAP, and DSPP (Fig. 8d, e and Supplementary Fig. 11b–d), indicating that CD24 is dispensable for both mitochondrial function and odontogenic differentiation in CD24+ hDPCs. To validate these findings, we employed an in vitro organ culture system using E13.5 mouse mandibular molar germs treated with CD24-targeting siRNA for 7 days. No morphological differences were observed between the knockdown and control groups (Fig. 8f, g), suggesting that CD24 does not influence tooth development. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that while CD24 expression is positively regulated by odontogenic BMP signaling and mitochondrial activity, it does not directly contribute to dentin formation. Therefore, we concluded that CD24 functions as a downstream indicator that reflects, but does not mediate, the odontogenic potential of hDPCs.

Discussion

hDPCs, which developmentally give rise to both dentin and pulp tissues, represent a biologically relevant source for dentin-pulp complex regeneration. However, the absence of well-characterized progenitor subsets with validated regenerative potential has posed a major challenge to advancing clinical applications9. In this study, we identified CD24+ hDPCs as a regeneration-competent subset. CD24+ hDPCs are specifically localized to odontoblast-generating regions during tooth development, and dramatically reduce in mature pulp, but reappear beneath reparative dentin in response to caries stimuli, indicating their lineage-specific involvement in both physiological and reparative dentinogenesis. Functionally, these cells exhibit robust odontogenic differentiation capacity, as demonstrated in both ectopic and orthotopic regenerative models. The selective enrichment of CD24 expression in dental papilla and pulp, but not in mesenchymal lineages of the gingiva or periodontal ligament that do not contribute to dentin formation, further underscores its lineage specificity. In addition to odontogenic potential, our current studies reveal an enhanced pro-angiogenic effect of CD24+ hDPCs, essential for maintaining tissue viability and supporting regeneration13. Thus, our results uncover a role for CD24+ hDPCs in establishing a vascular niche, thereby reinforcing their application potential in coordinated dentin-pulp complex regeneration. Given their dual capacity for dentin formation and vascular support, CD24+ hDPCs offer an advantage in achieving coordinated tissue regeneration. However, further optimization of cell sourcing, expansion, delivery protocols, as well as rigorous long-term safety and efficacy evaluations, remains a critical step to translate these findings into clinical applications.

Accumulating evidence supports the presence of heterogeneous mesenchymal progenitor populations in adult dental pulp, each with distinct regenerative capacity; however, their developmental origins and functional roles remain poorly defined11,12. The present study offers insight into a precursor subtype within the human dental mesenchyme (a.k.a., CD24+ hDPCs) that originates from early developmental lineages and becomes selectively retained into adulthood, demonstrating a lineage-restricted stem cell pool capable of reinitiating odontogenic programs upon demand. Beyond CD24+ hDPCs, diverse precursor subsets likely contribute collectively to dentin and pulp formation. For instance, previous studies identified Fgf3+ cells in the apical papilla of murine molars as bipotent progenitors capable of generating both odontoblasts and pulp cells12. However, Fgf3+ cells are absent in the coronal pulp, suggesting that distinct subsets are anatomically restricted and may perform region-specific functions. Therefore, further studies should aim to systematically map the full spectrum of progenitor cells in human dental pulp, elucidating their precise lineage trajectories and defining their specific contributions during both physiological and reparative dentinogenesis. Such comprehensive characterization is a critical prerequisite for selecting and harnessing optimal progenitor populations to maximize clinical regenerative outcomes. Within this context, our findings raise the possibility that CD24+ hDPCs constitute a core progenitor subset contributing broadly across distinct phases of dentinogenesis.

Extensive studies have established that BMP signaling plays a decisive role in odontoblast differentiation. For example, BMP2 has been reported as a critical regulator of DPC odontogenic differentiation, promoting dentinal tubule formation and upregulating odontogenic markers such as DMP1 and DSPP47–49. Unlike previous observations from aggregates of mixed DPSC populations, our study presents subpopulation-level evidence of enriched BMP2 expression and elevated SMAD1/5/9 phosphorylation in CD24+ hDPCs, substantiating the direct involvement of BMP signaling in this type of cell. BMP signaling in CD24+ hDPCs enhances mitochondrial biogenesis, ATP production, fission-fusion remodeling, and redox homeostasis, thereby fulfilling the elevated metabolic demands associated with odontogenic differentiation and matrix mineralization33,50. Furthermore, our data reveal that BMP activity suppresses the canonical WNT signaling, possibly through transcriptional upregulation of its inhibitors SFRP2 and WIF1. This antagonistic interaction, reversed upon BMP inhibition, suggests that a BMP-on/WNT-off regulatory axis governs odontogenic specification and is consistent with mechanisms described in other developmental systems51–53. Complementing this pathway, we identified SIRT1 as a downstream effector of BMP signaling. SIRT1 is highly expressed in CD24+ hDPCs during differentiation but diminished following BMP pathway inhibition. Functionally, SIRT1 promotes mitochondrial biogenesis, antioxidant defense (such as SOD1), and autophagic turnover, thus maintaining metabolic homeostasis. Our findings demonstrate a BMP2/SIRT1 regulatory axis that orchestrates mitochondrial remodeling and sustains the metabolic requirements of the odontogenic program in CD24+ hDPCs.

Despite the advances presented in this study, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the long-term fate, persistence, and functional stability of transplanted CD24+ hDPCs after engraftment remain to be determined, and extended follow-up studies will be required to evaluate the durability of the regenerated tissues in vivo. Second, the upstream cues that initiate or modulate BMP2/SIRT1 signaling in CD24+ hDPCs are not yet defined. Mechanical signals, niche-derived factors, or epigenetic regulators may contribute to activation of this axis, and elucidating these upstream influences could not only deepen our understanding of progenitor activation but also inform strategies to enhance odontogenic potential in CD24− or bulk DPC populations. Third, although our data support a pro-angiogenic role for VEGF-enriched EVs, their molecular determinants and downstream responses in endothelial cells remain incompletely characterized. Identifying key cargo molecules and their functional targets could facilitate refinement of cell-free therapeutic strategies and vesicle engineering to optimize neovascularization. Finally, studies incorporating lineage-tracing or clonal tracking approaches would enable definitive mapping of donor versus host contributions and lineage trajectories within regenerated dentin-pulp tissues, improving mechanistic resolution of in vivo cell-fate decisions.

In conclusion, our current study defines CD24+ hDPCs as a compelling candidate of precursor cell source with strong regenerative attributes for dentin-pulp complex reconstruction. By linking odontogenic BMP signaling with SIRT1-dependent mitochondrial remodeling, we uncover regulatory pathways that shape their odontogenic differentiation and pro-angiogenic potentials (Fig. 8h). These insights advance our understanding of dental pulp cell heterogeneity and offer a mechanistic basis for developing biologically guided reparative and regenerative strategies in endodontics.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed to define and functionally validate a human progenitor population with high competence for dentin-pulp complex regeneration, addressing a long-standing barrier in dental tissue engineering. We focused on CD24+ hDPCs, a candidate subset emerging from our developmental biology work, and evaluated their regenerative performance relative to CD24− hDPCs. The experimental strategy integrated multi-tiered approaches: 2D and 3D culture systems for in vitro functional profiling; ectopic subcutaneous transplantation in immunodeficient mice for initial in vivo validation; and orthotopic dentin-pulp regeneration in minipigs to assess preclinical efficacy in a large-animal model. Regenerative outcomes were examined by histological and ultrastructural analysis, radiographic imaging, and vascularization assessment. Mechanistic interrogation combined human single-cell transcriptomic data mining, bulk RNA sequencing, pharmacologic perturbations, siRNA-mediated knockdown, and mitochondrial functional assays to delineate the BMP2/SIRT1 metabolic axis. All experiments included a minimum of three independent biological replicates, with random allocation to treatment groups and blinded outcome assessment. Human dental papilla tissues were obtained with informed consent from third molar donors, and all human and animal protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan University.

Human and animal specimens

Human teeth were obtained from donors aged between 11 and 30 years following informed consent at West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University (Chengdu, China). hDPCs and hDPSCs were isolated from the dental papilla tissue and dental pulp tissue of immature and mature mandibular third molars, respectively. The remaining tissue samples were destroyed after the completion of cell isolation and analysis. HUVECs were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Animal studies employed 5-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) for the ectopic pulp regeneration experiments. Mandibular molar tooth germs were isolated from embryos at embryonic day 13.5 (E13.5) from pregnant female C57BL/6 mice (Dashuo Experimental Animal Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China). Additionally, 4-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (Dashuo Experimental Animal Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China) were used in the intracutaneous injection model. For the preclinical in situ pulp regeneration model, a 13-month-old male Yucatan miniature pig (Dashuo Experimental Animal Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China) exhibiting immature premolars was employed. All animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions, with a 12-h light/dark cycle, and provided ad libitum access to food and water. All animal experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University (Approval Nos. WCHSIRB-D-2024-269 and WCHSIRB-D-2024-464).

Cell culture

The isolation of hDPCs and hDPSCs was performed as followed methods54. Briefly, fresh dental papilla tissue and dental pulp tissue were carefully dissected into approximately 1 mm3 fragments under sterile conditions. The tissue fragments were then placed in Modified Eagle Medium (MEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA), and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 until cells migrated out from the explants and reached sufficient confluence for further experiments. In subsequent culture, the medium was replaced every 3 days with MEM containing 10% FBS. Cells at P0-P7 were used for experiments.

HUVECs were cultured in endothelial cell medium (OriCell, China) supplemented with 10% FBS. HUVECs P3-P5 were utilized for in vitro angiogenic assays.

For preparation of CM, CD24− and CD24+ hDPCs were cultured in serum-free Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) for 48 h. The supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The clarified CM was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until use.

FACS-based cell isolation

hDPCs at P2/P3 were digested using TrypLE Express Enzyme (Gibco, USA) and incubated with CD24-FITC (BD, 555482) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. The stained cells were treated with Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, R37165) for 10 min to distinguish live cells. The cell suspension was filtered through a 40 μm nylon mesh strainer (Corning, USA) to ensure a single-cell suspension. Sorting was performed using a BD FACSAriaTM II cell sorter (BD Biosciences, USA) to isolate CD24+ and CD24− populations under flow cytometry gating strategy (Supplementary Fig. 12). The sorted cells were subsequently cultured under either 2D or 3D conditions for downstream assays, with passages limited to P7.

Flow cytometry

To confirm mesenchymal identity, hDPCs were washed with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS; Servicebio, China) and digested using TrypLE Express (Gibco, 12604013). Cells were incubated with the following fluorophore-conjugated antibodies: CD24-FITC (BD, 555427), CD90-FITC (BD, 555595), CD73-PE (BD, 550257), CD45-FITC (BD, 555482), and CD31-FITC (BD, 555445). Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a BD Accuri C6 system (BD, USA).

To assess the CD24+ cell population under pathological conditions, single-cell suspensions were prepared from healthy and carious tooth pulp tissues via enzymatic digestion and red blood cell lysis (Beyotime, China). Cells were stained with CD24-FITC (BD, 555427) and analyzed to determine the relative abundance of CD24+ subsets.

For each assay, approximately 3 × 105 cells were incubated with the indicated antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min. Mouse IgG (BD, USA) was used as an isotype control. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10).

Colony formation assay

CD24− and CD24+ hDPCs were seeded at a density of 1 × 103 cells per 10-cm culture dish and cultured for 10 days. Colonies were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Biosharp, China) for 30 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution (Beyotime) for 10 min at room temperature (RT). Colonies consisting of more than 50 cells were counted under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan) and recorded as colony-forming units (CFUs).

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed by an EdU-488 staining kit (Beyotime). CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs were seeded in 24-well plates at 2 × 104 cells per well and cultured to approximately 80% confluence. Cells were incubated with 10 μM EdU for 4 h, followed by fixation and EdU detection according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus). The percentage of EdU+ cells (EdU+/DAPI+) was quantified using ImageJ.

Migration assays

Cell migration was evaluated by scratch-wound and transwell assays. For the scratch-wound assay, CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs were seeded in 6-well plates at 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured to approximately 90% confluence. A uniform linear wound was generated using a sterile 1-mL pipette tip. Cells were washed with DPBS to remove debris, and fresh medium containing 2% FBS was added. Images were acquired at 0 and 16 h using a phase-contrast microscope (Olympus), and wound closure was quantified in ImageJ. For HUVEC scratch assays, cells were treated with conditioned medium (CM) derived from CD24+ or CD24− hDPCs using the same procedure, and images were collected at 0 and 18 h.

For the transwell migration assay, CD24+/CD24− hDPCs or HUVECs (2 × 104 cells in 200 μL medium) were seeded into the upper chamber of 24-well transwell inserts (8.0 μm pore size; Corning) in α-MEM (for hDPCs) or endothelial medium (for HUVECs) containing 1% FBS. The lower chamber was filled with 750 μL of α-MEM (for hDPCs migration) or CD24+/CD24− hDPC-derived CM (for HUVEC migration), supplemented with 5% FBS. After 24 h of incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were gently removed. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, stained with crystal violet for 10 min, and quantified in five random fields per insert using ImageJ.

Alizarin red staining

To assess odontogenic differentiation, CD24− and CD24+ hDPCs were cultured in pro-odontogenic induction medium containing 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (Sigma-Aldrich), 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich), and 10−8 M dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich), with 10% FBS supplementation. A total of 3 × 104 cells per well were seeded in 24-well plates and induced for 14 days, with medium changes every 2 days. After fixation with 4% PFA, cells were stained with Alizarin Red S (Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For quantification of mineralized nodules, bound dye was solubilized using 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (Sangon Biotech, China), and absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Tube formation assay

The angiogenic capacity of HUVECs was examined using a Matrigel-based tube formation assay. Briefly, a total of 10 µL Matrigel (Corning, USA) was added per well of a μ-slide angiogenesis plate (Ibidi, Germany) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. HUVECs at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well were seeded in 50 µL of CD24− and CD24+ CM (2% FBS). CD24+ hDPCs were pretreated with GW4869 (S7609; Selleck, USA), a non-competitive neutral sphingomyelinase (nSMase) inhibitor for inhibiting extracellular vesicle (EV) production, prior to CM collection. After 4 h of incubation, tube-like structures were imaged and quantified using ImageJ.

Spheroid culture

3D spheroid culture models were employed to investigate the behavior of CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs. Cells were suspended in collagen type I gel (Cellmatrix) to generate either high-density (4 × 106 cells/mL) or low-density (2 × 106 cells/mL) mixtures. After dispensing into plates, collagen was allowed to polymerize at 37 °C for 30 min. Formed spheroids were then either detached and transferred to ultra-low attachment 48-well plates for suspension culture (20 μL per well), or placed onto standard tissue-culture plates for adherent culture (2–4 μL per well in 6-well plates), as indicated for each experiment. Spheroid size and cell viability (Live/Dead staining; Beyotime) were assessed on days 1, 2, 4, and 7. Migration and viability-related measurements were quantified using ImageJ.

For odontogenic induction, low-density spheroids were seeded under adherent conditions in 48-well plates (8 μL/well) and cultured in odontogenic induction medium for 14 days. Mineralization was evaluated by Alizarin Red S staining (Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For fluorescence intensity profiling, a linear region of interest (ROI) was manually drawn across the indicated area in ImageJ, and fluorescence intensity values were extracted using the “Plot Profile” tool. For endothelial spheroid assays, low-density HUVEC spheroids (4 μL) were cultured in 48-well plates with CM derived from CD24+ or CD24− hDPCs for 24 h. Angiogenic responses were assessed by CD31 immunofluorescent staining.

Immunoblotting

Cells were lysed on ice in RIPA buffer (Beyotime) containing 1% phosphatase and protease inhibitors (Beyotime). Lysates were mixed with loading buffer (Beyotime), boiled, and 10–20 μg total protein per lane was resolved on SDS-PAGE (6%, 8%, or 12%). Subsequently, proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore), blocked in 5% milk or BSA (Sigma-Aldrich), and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: mouse anti-DSPP (LFMb-21, 1:500, sc-73632, Santa Cruz), RUNX2 (1:1000, ab76956, Abcam), Mitochondria (1:1000, ab92824, Abcam), Osterix (1:1000, ab209484, Abcam), β-catenin (1:1000, sc-59737, Santa Cruz), SOD2 (1:1000, sc-133254, Santa Cruz), GAPDH (1:1000, sc-47724, Santa Cruz), and rabbit anti-DMP1 (1:500, 3844-100, Biovision), PGC1α (1:1000, D262041, Sangon Biotech), p-SMAD1/5/9 (1:1000, #13820, Cell Signaling Technology), SMAD1 (1:1000, #6944, Cell Signaling Technology), TFAM (1:1000, R22594, Zenbio), active β-catenin (1:1000, #8814, Cell Signaling Technology), AXIN2 (1:1000, ab109307, Abcam), SIRT1 (1:1000, R25721, Zenbio), mito GR (1:1000, 384462, Zenbio), SOD1 (1:1000, ab51254, Abcam), VEGF (1:1000, ab46154, Abcam), and goat anti-BMP2 (1:1000, sc-6895, Santa Cruz). After TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse, anti-rabbit, or anti-goat) and visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (Tanon).

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells using the Total RNA Isolation Kit (RC112, Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA was synthesized using HiScript III RT SuperMix (R323, Vazyme). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q712, Vazyme) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies). Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Immunofluorescence for cultured cells

Cells on confocal dishes or spheroids were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min. For intracellular antigen detection, cells were permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 and blocked in 3% BSA at RT for 1 h. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Mitochondria (1:300, ab92824, Abcam), Phalloidin (1:50, #8953, Cell Signaling Technology), p-SMAD1/5/9 (1:200, #13820, Cell Signaling Technology), LC3 (1:300, 14600-1-AP, Proteintech), SOD1 (1:300, ab51254, Abcam), and CD31 (1:300, NB100-2284, Novus Bio). After DPBS washes, fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000, Invitrogen) were applied for 1 h at RT, followed by DAPI (Thermo Fisher) nuclear staining. Imaging was performed using an Olympus FV1000 confocal microscope.

For redox state analysis, live cells were stained with MitoTracker Green and Red CMXRos (Beyotime) after odontogenic induction for 7 days, followed by imaging via super-resolution structured illumination microscopy (Multi-SIM, Naxi Tech). ImageJ was used for quantification.

Histological staining

To prepare paraffin sections, tooth samples were fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight and decalcified in 15% EDTA (pH 7.0) for 2 months. Specimens were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, G1140 and G1100, Solarbio), Masson’s trichrome (G1346, Solarbio), and Sirius red (R21890, Saint-Bio) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Immunofluorescence for tissue sections

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in 0.1% trypsin (Solarbio, China). After blocking with donkey serum, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: CD24 (1:300, ab202073, Abcam), Mitochondria (1:300, ab92824, Abcam), DSPP (1:300, sc-73632, Santa Cruz), DMP1 (1:300, 3844-100, Biovision), CD31 (1:300, NB100-2284, Novus Bio), and GFAP (1:200, ab7260, Abcam). Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry procedures followed standard protocols (detailed above). Fluorescence imaging was conducted using either Olympus FV1000 or VS200 systems. Colocalization of CD24 and mitochondria was evaluated via fluorescence intensity profiles from ImageJ.

Immunohistochemistry for tissue sections

For immunohistochemical analysis, endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide. After blocking with goat serum, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against DSPP (1:200, sc-73632, Santa Cruz) and NM95 (1:200, ab190710, Abcam), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) detection kit (ZSGB Bio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained sections were imaged using a BX43F light microscope.

Ectopic pulp regeneration model in nude mice

To assess the pulp regeneration potential, injectable constructs were fabricated by combining low-density CD24+ hDPCs, CD24− hDPCs, or unsorted hDPSCs with a collagen-based hydrogel (CD24+, CD24− hDPC, and hDPSC CG injectants). A TDM transplantation model was established in nude mice as previously described54. Briefly, to prepare the TDM, miniature pig incisors were extracted, and the periodontal ligament, cementum, and dental pulp tissues were carefully removed. Root canals were sectioned into 4–6 mm segments and sequentially treated in an ultrasonic cleaner with 17%, 10%, and 5% EDTA solutions, followed by triple rinses in deionized water. The prepared TDM segments were incubated with penicillin (50 U/mL) and streptomycin (50 mg/mL) at 4 °C for 48 h. CD24+, CD24− hDPC, and hDPSC CG injectants were subsequently loaded into the TDM canals sealed apically with gutta-percha and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Constructs were subcutaneously transplanted into nude mice (n = 5 per group) and harvested after 4 weeks for histological evaluation.

In situ pulp regeneration model in minipig

To evaluate the in situ regenerative capacity in a large-animal model, pulp regeneration was performed in a minipig using constructs containing either CD24+ or CD24− hDPCs. Five immature teeth were allocated to each treatment group. Surgical procedures were conducted under general anesthesia (propofol and isoflurane) combined with local anesthesia (lidocaine).

Access cavities were prepared using a fissure bur and a round bur under appropriate moisture control. Pulp tissue was removed using an extended-length barbed broach, and root canals were shaped using stainless steel hand files and NiTi rotary instruments (Dentsply), with intermittent saline irrigation. After achieving hemostasis and drying the canals with paper points, the injectants were delivered into the root canals and allowed to gel undisturbed for 10 min.

Cavities were then sealed sequentially with iRoot BP Plus (Innovative BioCeramix Inc., Canada), chemically cured glass ionomer (GC, Japan), and light-cured composite resin (3 M, USA). After a 4-week transplantation period, the animal was euthanized by intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital. Mandibular specimens were dissected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and decalcified in 15% EDTA for 2 months before histological analysis. Periapical radiographs were taken pre- and post-transplantation to evaluate apical closure and root development.

Intracutaneous injection model in rats

To evaluate local biocompatibility and immunogenicity, 100 µL of CD24+ or CD24− hDPC CG was injected intradermally into immunocompetent rats (n = 3 per group). Injection-site reactions were monitored, including gross erythema at 1-h post-injection. Injection-site tissues were harvested at 1 day and 4 weeks. Local inflammatory responses were evaluated at 1 day by H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining, together with immunofluorescence staining for IL-1β (1:300, sc-52012, Santa Cruz) and TNF-α (1:300, sc-52746, Santa Cruz). For systemic safety evaluation, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were collected for H&E histology. Complete blood counts and serum biochemical analyses were performed at 1 day and 4 weeks using automated analyzers (BC-2800Vet and BS-460, Mindray).

Extracellular flux assay

Mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured using the Seahorse XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs were pre-induced with pro-odontogenic medium for 7 days and seeded in Seahorse 24-well microplates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. Prior to measurement, culture medium was replaced with XF assay medium containing 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine, and 10 mM glucose, and incubated at 37 °C without CO2 for 1 h. OCR was recorded at baseline and following sequential injections of oligomycin (1 mM), FCCP (2 mM), and Rot/AA (1 mM). Bioenergetic parameters were analyzed using Seahorse XF software and normalized to total protein content per well, quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime).

ATP quantification

ATP production was assessed in CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs after 7 days of odontogenic induction using an ATP assay kit (Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance readings from replicate wells were normalized to total protein content determined by BCA assay.

Electron microscopy

For SEM, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and freeze-dried. Representative images were captured at ×120 and ×2000 magnifications to assess dentinal tubule morphology in regenerated tissues.

For the transmission electron microscope (TEM), CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs were cultured with or without odontogenic induction for 7 days, dissociated into single cells, and pelleted by centrifugation at 2500 rpm for 10 min. Cell pellets were fixed overnight at 4 °C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (v/v) in DPBS (pH 7.4) and processed by CSG Experimental Testing Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Ultrathin sections were imaged at ×10,000 and ×30,000 magnifications. Mitochondria number and length were quantified from at least three fields, analyzing a minimum of 200 mitochondria per group.

Small-molecule inhibitors treatment

CD24− and CD24+ hDPCs were treated with the LDN193189 (1 μM, 7 days; S2618, Selleck), EX527 (1 μM, 7 days; S1541, Selleck), or AA/ROT (1 μM, 48 h; 103673-100, Agilent). For 7-day treatments, inhibitor-containing medium was refreshed every 3 days. Cells were subsequently collected for RT-qPCR, immunostaining, and immunoblotting analyses as described above. Vehicle controls received an equivalent final concentration of solvent used for each inhibitor, according to the manufacturer’s recommendations.

siRNA transfection

CD24+ hDPCs and mouse tooth germs were transfected with 50 nM siRNAs (Han Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) using LipofectamineTM 3000 (Invitrogen), with medium refreshed every 2 days according to the manufacturer’s instructions. siRNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

BMP2 induction assays

To assess BMP2-mediated effects, CD24− and CD24+ hDPCs were seeded in 6-well plates or glass-bottom confocal dishes and cultured to approximately 60% confluence. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium (α-MEM supplemented with 5% FBS) containing 100 ng/mL recombinant human BMP2 (Peprotech, Thermo). Medium was refreshed every 3 days. Cells were harvested for RT-qPCR and immunostaining analyses after 7 days of treatment.

In vitro tooth germ culture

To investigate the effect of Cd24 knockdown on tooth development, mandibular molar germs from E13.5 mouse embryos were cultured using a modified Trowell’s organ culture method55. Briefly, tooth germs were placed on 0.4 μm pore-size polycarbonate membranes (Millipore) supported by a metal grid with the membrane surface contacting DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS. Cultures were maintained for 7 days. Cd24 siRNAs were applied at the initiation of culture and refreshed every 2 days.

Bulk RNA sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from CD24+ and CD24− hDPCs before implantation and at 2 weeks post-implantation using the Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, China). RNA purity and concentration were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and integrity was verified with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. RNA libraries were established using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit and sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Low-quality reads were removed using fastp56, and clean reads were aligned to the human or mouse genome using HISAT257. Gene expression levels (FPKM) and read counts were calculated using HTSeq-count58. PCA analysis was performed using R (v 3.2.0), and differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq259. DEGs were defined by Q value < 0.05 and fold change (FC) > 1.5 or <0.5. GO enrichment analysis was performed using hypergeometric testing in R60.

scRNA-seq data analysis

Transcriptional datasets were obtained from GEO: GSM612299319, GSM499846120, GSM490413520, GSM799049421, GSM500504922. Data were processed in R using the Seurat v5.2.1 package for quality control, dimensionality reduction, and clustering23,24. Cells with <300 or >5000 genes, <1000 unique molecular identifiers (UMI) counts, or >10% mitochondrial gene content were excluded. Data were normalized using the “ScaleData” function, and the top 3000 variable genes were used for PCA. The top 30 principal components were used for clustering, and batch effects were corrected using Harmony61. The FindClusters function with default parameters and a resolution of 0.3 was used to identify clusters. The Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied for 2D visualization61.

Cell clusters were annotated based on canonical marker expression and prior literature. Dot Plots, bar plots, heatmaps, violin plots, and feature plots were generated to visualize marker distribution. Sub-clustering was performed for selected populations, followed by pseudotime analysis using Monocle 362,63. DEGs were identified using Seurat’s FindMarkers function with Bonferroni correction (P < 0.05; logFC > 0.25). GO enrichment analysis was conducted for significant DEGs.

Statistical analysis

All values are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc test was applied to assess the effects of two independent variables and their interaction on the dependent variable. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 software, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
