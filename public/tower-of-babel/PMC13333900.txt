Monitoring of the Cascade of Care in Patients With Chronic HBV in Primary Care Clinics in Australia
Authors: Dina Moussa, Jack Wallace, Jacqui Richmond, Joseph S. Doyle, Long Nguyen, Douglas Boyle, Mark A. Stoové, Jason Asselin, Victoria Polkinghorne, Marvad Ahad, Susanne Glasgow, Kate New, Lena Sanci, Matt Penn, John Furler, Alexander Thompson, Margaret Hellard, Jess Howell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo achieve hepatitis B virus (HBV) elimination in Australia, a shift from hospital to community‐based care is needed. This study aimed to describe the prevalence of chronic HBV and uptake of the cascade of care in primary care patients. This prospective cohort study was conducted in 76 urban and regional primary care clinics across Victoria, Australia between 1/7/2020 and 30/6/2023. Anonymised socio‐demographic, clinical and laboratory data from general practice (GP) clinics' electronic medical records (EMR) were extracted. Descriptive analysis of the cohort of clinic clients with HBV was performed. A total of 346,927 individuals attended appointments across the study period. Of these, 25,212 had records indicating HBV‐related testing. 491 (0.14%) individuals had evidence of current HBV infection defined as HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) positive and/or HBV DNA positive. 469 attended the clinics during the study period, among whom 239 (51%) were female. In the GP EMR, only 59 (13%) had evidence of at least one HBV DNA test and at least one ALT test ordered. Fourteen patients (3%) had record of being reviewed for HBV management by the clinic nurse or GP and 78 (17%) reviewed by a viral hepatitis specialist. Ninety‐two (20%) were on treatment for HBV. Of people receiving treatment, 23 (25%) had a record of linkage to specialist care. Our data show that significant gaps in the cascade of care remain for people with chronic HBV in primary care settings, with a low proportion of patients having evidence of participating in all stages of the HBV cascade of care.

Introduction

Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) affects over 220,000 Australians and without timely treatment can lead to death from liver cirrhosis and liver cancer [1, 2, 3]. Due to the high burden of HBV globally, the World Health Organisation (WHO) has established ambitious HBV elimination goals aiming for 90% of people with HBV to be diagnosed, 80% linked to care and 80% of eligible patients on treatment by 2030 [4, 5, 6]. These goals require a substantial scale‐up in linkage to HBV treatment, particularly within community and primary care settings. The Australian government has adopted these global elimination goals; however, Australia is not on track to achieve these by 2030: in 2023, an estimated 69% of people living with HBV were diagnosed, only 24.5% of people living with HBV were linked to care and 12.6% of eligible patients received guideline‐based treatment [1].

In Australia, most HBV clinical management, consisting of regular liver assessment and treatment when eligible, currently occurs in specialist hospital clinics. While authorised general practitioners (GPs) can prescribe HBV treatment, only an estimated 21.9% of people living with HBV that are linked to care receive their HBV care from GPs [1]. A low proportion of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people and people from culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) populations receive care in community settings, suggesting there are barriers in accessing HBV care for some people such as cultural beliefs, stigma, and communication [7]. To achieve elimination goals, clinical care must be decentralised and better integrated into primary care settings [8, 9, 10]. Primary care services, including general practice, are ideally placed to identify people at risk, initiate HBV testing and diagnosis, conduct routine monitoring, and coordinate care including treatment when indicated. However, little is known about the extent of implementation of HBV care in Australian primary care clinics [6]. Understanding the distribution, delivery, and uptake of guideline‐based HBV care in primary health settings is important if we are to develop appropriate strategies to achieve elimination targets.

Qualitative research with GPs has identified several barriers to managing HBV in primary care settings including the perceived complexity of HBV clinical guidelines, lack of specialist support, and lack of confidence and knowledge regarding HBV management. During the study period, the 2022 Australian consensus recommendations and the Third National Hepatitis B Strategy (2018–2022) mainly advocated testing individuals from HBV endemic countries and other high‐risk groups. Universal triple serology, which includes HBsAg, was not the standard national recommendation, which is important when interpreting the overall low HBsAg testing rate in this cohort [11, 12]. HBV treatment eligibility assessment follows an algorithm requiring the interpretation of multiple blood tests, and transient elastography, which are often difficult to access outside hospital settings. Moreover, there is a lack of consensus between international HBV management guidelines, further complicating HBV management [12, 13].

From the patient perspective, hospital‐based HBV care presents logistical barriers to linkage and retention in care [7, 14]. These challenges are evident among CALD communities and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people who experience linguistic and cultural barriers when navigating hospital systems. These factors can delay follow‐up after diagnosis, reduce adherence to ongoing monitoring and treatment, and ultimately contribute to poorer outcomes. On the other hand, community‐based care is potentially more accessible and familiar, which could help play a key role in elimination.

This study aimed to describe the prevalence and proportion positivity of HBV Surface Antigen (HBsAg) testing and HBV management practices in a large cohort of patients attending GP clinics in Victoria, Australia. Our objective was to describe the prevalence, demographic characteristics, and uptake of the HBV cascade of care to explore the role of primary care staff in HBV management, including testing, treatment initiation, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) surveillance.

Methods

Ethics

This study has received ethics approval by St Vincent's Hospital. All patient data were anonymised.

Study Design

Simply‐B was a prospective cohort study. Baseline primary outcome data was collected from the electronic medical records (EMR) of clinic chronic HBV patients, including the number of patients with HBV attending the clinic within the study period and the number (proportion) receiving guideline‐based care [12, 13], referred to specialist care, prescribed HBV treatment, and those who were eligible but not receiving HBV treatment, respectively.

Study Setting and Time Period

This study was conducted across 76 general practices in Victoria that have the Future Health Today (FHT) software embedded in their EMR [15]. FHT works in the background of GP electronic medical records to help identify and support management guidance for chronic diseases. The PATRON surveillance system software is connected to EMRs in participating clinics [16]. PATRON uses GHRANITE software to extract anonymised demographic (e.g., gender, age, etc.) and clinical data (e.g., HBsAg test results) from EMRs in participating primary care practices with the purpose of improving knowledge and delivery of care for chronic health conditions [15, 16]. GHRANITE enables multiple tests to be extracted, encrypted and tagged to an individual over time. The study period was from July 1, 2020 to June 30, 2023. The clinics were a combination of regional and urban clinic and were classified using the Modified Monash Model (MMM) [17]. The model is a validated method to define whether a location is metropolitan, rural, remote or very remote by measuring remoteness and population size on a scale of Modified Monash (MM) categories MM 1 to MM 7. MM 1 is a major city and MM 7 is very remote.

Study Populations

Active patients of participating practices who had chronic HBV were included in the analysis. An active patient in the dataset was defined as a patient that is active in the GP EMR as the patient is not deceased or inactive (archived) and attended the clinic during the study period. Chronic HBV for the purpose of this analysis was an active patient who had a record of attending the clinic during the study period (see below for further details) during the study period. We were provided with the absolute number of active patients within the PATRON dataset during the study period, which was used as the denominator to determine the proportion of patients across all clinics with a record of a HBsAg test and the proportion who tested HBsAg positive.

Eligibility

Eligibility criteria for the cohort study include individuals aged ≥ 18 years who had attended a participating primary care clinic in the 36 months prior to July 1, 2023 and were HBsAg positive. Individuals < 18 years of age were excluded.

Definitions

HBV infection (chronic) was defined as a person having a positive HBsAg test recorded in the primary care clinic patient EMR and/or a diagnosis of chronic HBV infection entered in the EMR, either as a free text entry or selected active or past health issue.

HBV management was defined as the patient having (1) liver function tests (including alanine aminotransferase (ALT)) and/or (2) HBV viral load (HBV DNA level) ordered by their GP at least once every 13 months in accordance with national clinical guidelines [1].

HBV specialist care was defined by evidence of (a) referral to specialist hepatology or gastroenterology or infectious diseases clinic care in the EMR, (b) letters received from the specialist stored in the EMR indicating ongoing HBV care.

HBV antiviral therapy was defined as evidence in the primary care clinic patient EMR of a prescription for HBV antiviral therapy (entecavir, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate or tenofovir alafenamide) prescribed by the GP or evidence of the person being on HBV antiviral therapy in their medication list on the EMR.

HBV endemic country was defined as being born in a country other than Australia where the 2022 HBV prevalence estimate is > 2% according to the Centre for Disease Analysis Foundation's Polaris Observatory [18].

Quantitative Variables

Demographic variables included age (5‐year category), gender, biological sex, postcode, clinic, need for interpreter, country of birth, identification as Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander, and socioeconomic status of residence as defined by Socio‐Economic Indexes for Areas (SEIFA) data. The age quintiles were in a 5‐year range given the need for anonymity, with more precise age data not provided through the surveillance system. SEIFA data is developed by the Australian Bureau of Statistics and ranks areas in Australia according to relative socio‐economic advantage and disadvantage based on information from 5‐yearly census data [19].

Extracted data included anonymised socio‐demographic information, clinical history, laboratory results (including HBsAg, HBV DNA, and ALT), imaging (liver ultrasound), prescriptions and documentation of specialist referrals and where data were available, cirrhosis, HCC, type 2 diabetes, smoking and alcohol consumption. Type 2 diabetes, smoking and alcohol consumption were extracted as they are established cofactors for liver disease progression in people with chronic HBV. These variables are recommended as part of comprehensive HBV management per national guidelines. HIV and HCV co‐infection were not extracted as they were not consistently available in the EMR data.

Statistical Analysis

Data were extracted, linked, and deidentified using GHRANITE, a secure data collection computer software, from GP clinic electronic medical records [16].

Descriptive statistics were used to examine the number of people tested for HBsAg, the number and proportion of people with chronic HBV infection who were HBsAg positive, uptake of key clinical actions (e.g., liver function testing, HBV DNA testing, antiviral therapy) and involvement of primary care providers in patient management. The distribution of socio‐demographic and clinical factors was described for the cohort. The number and proportion achieving each step in the cascade of care (testing HBsAg positive, accessing antiviral therapy and specialist referral) were described. Normally distributed data were described as means ± standard deviation and non‐parametric data as median (IQR) respectively. All analyses were completed using STATA version 17 [20].

Results

Of the 76 participating clinics in urban and regional Victoria Modified Monash (MM) categories, 49 were classified as MM 1, 3 clinics as MM 2, 6 clinics as MM 3, 9 clinics as MM 4 and 9 clinics as MM 5. A total of 346,927 individuals attended the participating general practice clinics between July 2020 and June 2023. Of these, 25,212 had HBV related testing (either a HBsAg test or HBV DNA). A subset of 20,056 of the 25,212 individuals had at least one HBsAg test (5.8%), of whom 246 were HBsAg positive, with a test yield of 1.2. A total of 491 (2%) individuals of 25,212 were identified as living with HBV, defined as evidence of a positive HBsAg test and/or HBV DNA (Figure 1). These patients were then included in the chronic HBV cohort for further analysis. Out of the 491 patients with HBsAg positive test results, 469 were defined as active patients in the clinics during the study period.

Flow chart of Cascade of HBV in cohort.

The remainder of the analysis is based on the 469 individuals with chronic HBV considered to be active patients at the health services participating in the study. Demographic data showed that 239 (51%) of the 469 active patients were female, and the median age was 39 (35–44 years; Table 1 and Figure 2). While half of the country of birth data was missing from the dataset, of the 235 (50%) people with available data, 153 (32.6%) were from countries recognised as HBV endemic countries (Figure 3, Table S1). Less than 1% of the 469 patients with HBV identified as Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander in the EMR. The SEIFA index used was the Index of Relative Socio‐economic Disadvantage (IRSD). Eighty‐five (18%) resided in an area with a decile score of 1, which is the most disadvantaged 10% of areas; 33 (7%) had a decile score of 10 which is the most advantaged 10% of areas; the remainder resided in areas with IRSD scores between 2 and 9 (Table 1).

Among the patients with HBV, seventy‐nine HBV DNA tests were performed during the study period on 59 individuals (13%). 25 had a single HBV DNA viral load performed during follow up, 40 had two HBV DNA load tests performed, six people had 3 performed and 8 people had 4 performed. 112 ALT tests had been performed in 59 individuals (13%). Twenty patients had more than one ALT test performed during follow up, whereas 39 only had one ALT performed. 144 individuals (29%) had blood test events (n = 4239) during the study period, with a median of 16 blood test events per person over time. Only 14 (3%) had a documented review by the GP or nurse for their management in primary care. Similarly, only 78 (17%) patients had evidence in the EMR of being referred to and/or reviewed by a liver specialist (Figure 4).

HBV cascade of care of active patients. Step 2 comprises 14 patients reviewed by GP/nurse and 78 reviewed by specialist. Step 2 and 3 represent independent counts from EMR and may not reflect the same individuals.

Ninety‐two (20%) patients with HBV had evidence of receiving hepatitis B treatment; 53 (58%) on entecavir and 37 (40%) on tenofovir (Figure 4). Two (2%) were on tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) which might have been due to HIV co‐infection however that data was not available to the study (see Methods). It should be noted that 92 patients document as engaged in HBV care (14 with GPs and 78 with specialists) and the 92 patients on antiviral therapy are independent counts from the EMR and should not be assumed to represent the same individuals. Any overlap between these groups could not be confirmed from the available data. Fifty‐five (12%) patients had evidence of liver ultrasound or Computed Tomography (CT) screening for HCC in primary care (all 469 active patients with HBV were considered eligible for 6 monthly HCC surveillance per the Australian Consensus Statement [21]), with only a subset having evidence of being co‐managed with a liver specialist. Only three patients (0.6%) were recorded as having cirrhosis and three (0.6%) were recorded as having HCC.

Lifestyle and comorbidity data were sparsely recorded in clinic EMRs, but provide some insight into the risk profile of this cohort. Less than a quarter of patients had an alcohol history available, of whom two individuals (< 1%) reported drinking alcohol more than four times per week, 22 patients (4.7%) reported drinking alcohol monthly or less and 79 patients (17%) stated that they never consumed alcohol (refer to Table 1). Smoking data was available for 370 (79%) of patients, with 283 patients (60%) being non‐smokers. Thirteen patients (2.8%) had a recorded diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus.

Discussion

Screening for HBV infection occurred at a low rate in our study primary care in Victoria, with only 20,056 (5.8%) of 346,927 of the entire cohort having any evidence of HBsAg or HBV DNA testing during the three‐year study period. Among those diagnosed with chronic hepatitis B, the available evidence suggested that there was infrequent monitoring and follow up through the care cascade delivered in primary care, with less than 15% having evidence of routine monitoring for hepatitis B and 20% receiving treatment. Essential investigations such as HBV DNA and ALT testing were rarely recorded in the EMRs. This is despite these tests being essential for disease staging and treatment eligibility assessment, as recommended in national hepatitis B consensus and requested on specialist clinic referral forms [22].

Due to the mechanism of data recording in EMRs included in this PATRON dataset, we cannot be certain if patients were or were not receiving hepatitis B care in tertiary hospitals or specialist settings. However, as outlined above, the absence of evidence of investigations within GP records required for specialist referral suggests that many patients with chronic hepatitis B are not undergoing the essential follow‐up required to determine disease phase or treatment eligibility in either the primary care or specialist/tertiary care setting. EMR systems, including pathology and radiology, are generally on separate systems with limited communication between primary care and specialist/tertiary settings. Without data linkage between these settings, the full continuum of HBV management remains unclear. Expansion and increased utilisation of My Health Record could help achieve this [23]. My Health Record is a secure online summary of key patient health information which healthcare providers can access and add information to. Records contain key health information like immunisations, pathology and diagnostic imaging reports, prescription and dispensing information, hospital discharge summaries and more. Integrating its use Australia‐wide might in the future improve visibility and integration of medical notes and investigations across services.

We do not have access to any past screening trends for this cohort but < 10% of patients being screened for hepatitis B is much lower than expected and the proportion positive is low given national estimates. Reasons for this could include that individuals classified as higher risk for hepatitis B who attended those services had been previously tested and diagnosed elsewhere. Even if there were diagnosed elsewhere, it would be expected to see follow up testing and monitoring for chronic hepatitis B within primary care services. Overall, the proportion of patients with chronic hepatitis B identified as being prescribed treatment was around 20%. Of note, only a quarter of those on treatment had a record in the EMR of linkage to specialist care with the available data not including information on whether treatment was prescribed by the GP or specialist, indicating further disconnection in recording treatment pathways. Furthermore, the proportion of patients undergoing HCC surveillance in primary care was low with only 58 (12%) having a record of receiving a liver ultrasound during the study period. This is concerning given that HCC often develops silently and is frequently diagnosed at advanced stages when treatment options are limited [21]. Again, whilst surveillance may have occurred in specialist care, the fact that the results do not appear in the GP EMR is a major concern. It suggests that GPs are unaware if screening is occurring or of the results.

Whilst the data from our study had gaps, there is minimal empiric data available in Australia that examines the chronic HBV cascade of care. Our findings are consistent with two mapping projects in Australia and globally that show substantial attrition across the care cascade, from diagnosis to regular monitoring and treatment of chronic HBV [1, 22]. Nationally, persistent gaps across the HBV cascade of care continue to be reported [24, 25]. Recent Victorian data show that HBV remains a major contributor to HCC, with 18% of incident HCC cases attributable to chronic HBV [25]. Alarmingly, 26% of HBV‐related HCC cases were first diagnosed with cirrhosis at the time of their cancer diagnosis, and 60% met criteria for antiviral therapy but more than a quarter were untreated at HCC diagnosis. Those referred from primary care were less likely to have received guideline‐based therapy, and surveillance uptake was much lower in non‐specialist settings (16%) compared with specialist care (85%) [25].

The data from our study suggested that only 14 (3%) of patients with hepatitis B had a documented review by the GP or nurse specifically for hepatitis B management in primary care. Practice nurses appear underutilised in HBV management, despite evidence that nurse‐led and shared‐care models can increase follow‐up, adherence to surveillance, and linkage to specialist care in other chronic disease settings [26]. In some clinics, there was evidence of the use of Medicare rebates for chronic disease management plans specifically tailored to HBV. This mechanism could provide an important avenue for improving funding, structured follow‐up and coordination of care between patients, nurses and GPs. Potential barriers include a lack of awareness, limited confidence among GPs in managing HBV, unclear referral pathways and time/resource constraints [27, 28]. Strategies such as targeted GP training and interoperable data flows between primary and specialist services (including expanded use of My Health Record) so that investigations and surveillance are visible across care settings could help bridge the care gap. It is also important to note that in Australia, patients usually access their GP when they have a health issue rather than for scheduled annual reviews. While the national HBV guidelines recommend at least annual review for people living with chronic HBV, there is no mandated visit frequency and there is no specific item under Medicare for reimbursement for GPs to manage HBV. That means that some patients in this cohort may have had infrequent GP appointments contributing to under‐documentation of HBV‐related care across the study period.

A limitation of this study is that only data captured within participating general practice clinics were available for analysis and implies that patients who received the majority or entirety of their HBV care within specialist services may not have their monitoring, treatment, or surveillance activities reflected in our dataset. As such, some individuals classified as not receiving HBV care in primary practice may be receiving guideline‐based management through tertiary centres, but this information was not shared or integrated back into the GP EMR. We therefore cannot comment on the overall proportion of people with HBV in this cohort who are receiving guideline‐based HBV care. Despite this, our findings clearly show that only a minority of patients with HBV are receiving care directly from their GP in the primary care setting. This highlights both the dependency on specialist‐led care and the missed opportunity to expand the role of general practice in the continuum of HBV management. A further limitation was lack of data completeness, with low levels of available data in EMR records for many variables which are important for guideline‐based HBV management, including country of birth, alcohol and smoking history, BMI and comorbidities such as type 2 diabetes that impact liver disease progression and management. However, this lack of data is in itself informative, as it highlights a lack of systematic approach to liver disease management where cofactors are not routinely screened for and managed appropriately. A further limitation was not receiving demographic data on the people who were tested but HBsAg negative, thus we could not describe who was tested for HBV and whether all who were eligible were tested. Moreover, our definition of specialist care refers to referrals and letters from specialists providing evidence that they were seeing the specialist documented in the EMR. It is possible that some of the gap between diagnosis and documented specialist care reflects barriers to accessing specialist care such as cost, wait times and health literacy rather than absence of GP recognition of the need for referral.

This study adds to the growing evidence that testing and diagnosis of people with HBV is low in community settings in Australia. Robust systems and support are essential to ensure that patients receive the full spectrum of care recommended by national and international clinical guidelines [4, 8]. Importantly, our findings provide novel insights into the real‐world delivery of HBV management in primary care, revealing a lack of liver disease assessment in particular, where targeted interventions may have the most impact. By characterising patterns of treatment uptake, referral and surveillance, this study identifies practical gaps that can be unclear in national‐level reporting. In doing so, it highlights opportunities to strengthen GP–specialist integration, develop decision‐support tools and prioritise education and resources for primary care providers. Ultimately, these insights contribute to more effective implementation of guideline‐based care and improved long‐term outcomes for people living with HBV in Australia.

The findings of this study directly reflect key priorities articulated in the Fourth National Hepatitis B Strategy 2023–2030, which positions decentralised testing, management and equitable access to care as central to Australia's elimination goal [29]. Our data show that while testing in primary care occurs, the subsequent management steps are infrequently documented.

Conclusion

GPs in Victoria play a critical role in the initial screening for HBV, yet significant gaps persist in the continuum of care for patients diagnosed with HBV. A minority of patients receive complete HBV clinical evaluation or follow‐up care in primary settings, and GP involvement in HBV management remains limited. To achieve HBV elimination targets, efforts must focus on strengthening primary care capabilities through training, decision‐support tools, and coordinated care models. Interventions that address both clinical capability and data systems are likely to have the greatest impact on closing the cascade of care.
