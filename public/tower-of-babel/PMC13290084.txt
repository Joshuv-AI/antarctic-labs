The current landscape and future directions of Lyme disease vaccines
Authors: Zhiqiang Chen, Ling He, Xun Huang, Xiao Liang, Aihua Liu, Fukai Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLyme disease (LD) represents a significant public health challenge in North America and Eurasia. The persistent increase in its incidence may create an opportunity for vaccines to gain broader acceptance. Recent years have seen notable advances in vaccine development. However, achieving a balance between broad-spectrum protection and durable immunity remains a critical bottleneck. This article is presented as a narrative review that systematically summarizes recent progress in Lyme disease vaccine research worldwide, with particular emphasis on the strengths and limitations of approved and investigational candidates. Unlike existing reviews, this study integrates recent clinical investigations of multivalent vaccine candidates with breakthrough advances across diverse vaccine platforms, yielding a comprehensive and current synthesis. We identify critical challenges in contemporary vaccine development and propose actionable solutions. Our forward-looking perspective centers on a triangular strategy that integrates antigen multivalency, platform personalization, and geographical customization, providing a conceptual framework to guide future LD immunoprophylaxis research.KEYWORDS: Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato, Lyme disease, public health, vaccine

Introduction

Lyme disease (LD) is a zoonotic infectious disease caused by the Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato complex, transmitted primarily through the bites of Ixodes ticks [1–3]. The disease is highly endemic in North America and temperate regions of Eurasia, with marked heterogeneity in incidence across geographic areas; now this disease has occurred in more than 70 countries and regions all over the world; approximately 700,000 cases annually are estimated to take place in its endemic areas [4–6]. Clinically, early-stage infection often presents with erythema migrans; however, in the absence of timely intervention, it may progress to severe complications such as arthritis, meningoradiculitis, or myocarditis [7]. In animals, particularly livestock, infection typically manifests as joint swelling, lameness, fever, abrupt declines or cessation in milk production, and reproductive dysfunction factors that significantly impair growth, productivity, and economic viability in the livestock industry [8].

Currently, antibiotics remain the primary therapeutic approach for Lyme disease [9]. Although regimens such as doxycycline, amoxicillin, or ceftriaxone are highly effective for early-stage cases, a subset of patients continue to experience adverse symptoms persisting for >6 months despite eradication of active infection following standard protocols. These symptoms-persistent fatigue, cognitive impairment, and arthralgia-are clinically defined as post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome (PTLDS) [10,11]. Notably, the causes of PTLDS remain incompletely understood and are the subject of ongoing investigation. Moreover, prolonged antibiotic therapy may lead to complications such as gastrointestinal disturbances, photosensitive dermatitis, and antimicrobial resistance [12–14].

Given these limitations, vaccination is regarded as the most promising approach to prevent infection and reduce the public health burden of Lyme disease. Among groups requiring protection, high-risk populations such as outdoor workers, children and the elderly residing in or traveling to endemic regions face elevated infection risks. However, the development of an effective human vaccine remains highly challenging due to the genomic complexity of Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato , its intricate life cycle, and considerable strain heterogeneity [15]. Although several Lyme disease vaccine candidates have entered clinical trials in recent years, concerns remain regarding their broad applicability and long-term efficacy. Nevertheless, advancements in vaccine technology have introduced new avenues for innovation in this field. This review is structured to first provide a comprehensive overview of the global research progress in Lyme disease vaccines, covering whole-cell, subunit, nucleic acid, vector, epitope-based, and anti-microbiota vaccine platforms in turn. The review then systematically analyzes the key scientific, technical, and societal challenges currently limiting vaccine development. Finally, it proposes forward-looking strategies and priorities to guide the design of next-generation broadly protective vaccines. By synthesizing up-to-date evidence and critical appraisal, this article aims to provide a solid foundation for the development of safe, effective, and broadly protective Lyme disease vaccines.

Whole-cell vaccines

The first attempt at Lyme disease prevention utilized whole-cell vaccines made up of either inactivated or live-attenuated bacterial preparations. Preclinical studies have shown that inactivated whole-cell vaccines can induce specific antibody responses and confer a degree of protective immunity [17,18]. Between 1992 and 1994, two U.S.-based companies received approval to market chemically inactivated whole-cell vaccines for veterinary use, particularly in dogs [19,20]. However, translating these vaccines for human use proved problematic. Studies have demonstrated that the p41 flagellin of Borrelia burgdorferi sensu stricto shares amino acid homology with periplasmic flagellar antigens of other spirochetes, notably Treponema pallidum [21,22], raising concerns that whole-cell vaccines might elicit cross-reactive antibodies capable of confounding the diagnostic serology and triggering off-target immune responses. Furthermore, sera from Lyme disease patients contain IgM antibodies reactive with neuronal antigens [23] and anti-flagellin monoclonal antibodies have been shown to alter neuritogenesis in neuroblastoma cells in vitro [24], indicating potential autoimmune mechanisms. Together, these cross-reactivity issues, compounded by the inability to serologically distinguish vaccine-induced immunity from natural infection, led to the discontinuation of whole-cell vaccines for human use.

In a notable study, Sadziene et al. [25] developed a nonpathogenic Borrelia burgdorferi sensu stricto mutant strain, rendered avirulent by the loss of flagella through serial in vitro passage. This strain was evaluated as a candidate for a live-attenuated vaccine. A single immunization with 105 colony-forming units (CFU) elicited a protective antibody response comparable to that induced by wild-type strains and significantly stronger than that observed with inactivated vaccines. Importantly, the effective dose was only 1/25 that of the inactivated formulation, indicating a promising efficiency advantage. These seemed very encouraging. Yet two important problems still had to be overcome. First, their complex antigen composition may provoke off-target or nonspecific immune responses. Second, the genetic stability of the attenuated strain in vivo remains uncertain, and the risk of reversion to virulence has not been fully excluded [26]. Notably, while live-attenuated platforms have been successfully deployed for other bacterial pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis (BCG) and Salmonella Typhi (Ty21a), where strict attenuation and genetic stability are rigorously validated [27,28], Borrelia’s complex lifecycle and antigenic diversity preclude direct extrapolation of such safety profiles. These safety concerns continue to limit their progression in clinical development.

Subunit vaccines

Subunit vaccines contain only specific antigenic components of Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato most commonly outer surface proteins that are sufficient to stimulate a protective immune response without introducing the entire pathogen [29,30]. Among these, outer surface protein A (OspA) has been the most extensively studied target for vaccine development against Lyme disease [31,32]. Preclinical studies have revealed that immunization with recombinant OspA (rOspA) can elicit high titers of specific antibodies in animal models [33]. These antibodies effectively block the transmission of Borrelia burgdorferi sensu stricto from the tick salivary glands to the mammalian host, enhance complement-mediated bactericidal activity, and significantly inhibit spirochete proliferation in vitro [34]. Encouraged by these findings, two independent clinical trials reported that rOspA-based vaccination achieved a protective efficacy of 76%–92% against symptomatic Lyme disease [35,36]. Therefore, the U.S. FDA approved the first human Lyme disease vaccine LYMErix in 1998. The vaccine’s active ingredient was recombinant OspA. However, LYMErix was voluntarily withdrawn from the market in 2002, only four years after its launch. From a scientific perspective, public and professional concerns centered on the “molecular mimicry” hypothesis. A segment of OspA (residues 165–173) was found to share partial sequence homology with human lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1). This raised theoretical concerns of autoimmune arthritis in genetically susceptible individuals [37]. From a regulatory evidence standpoint, extensive post-licensure epidemiologic investigations and FDA reviews did not establish a definitive causal link between vaccination and chronic autoimmune or arthritic outcomes [38]. Sociopolitical and commercial factors drove the manufacturer’s decision to discontinue the product. These factors included widespread public anxiety, negative media narratives, litigation, and declining uptake, rather than definitive safety signals [38,39]. Since then, no human Lyme disease vaccine has been commercially available.

Subsequently, researchers launched a re-engineering campaign focused on the rOspA protein to generate novel, safer vaccine candidates. Their idea was to adjust the sequence that might be involved in molecular mimicry with human LFA-1 while keeping its overall structure and immunogenicity unaltered [40,41]. Stony Brook University then licensed the underlying intellectual property to Baxter Healthcare Corporation; the IP covered the replacement of the C-terminal segment of full-length rOspA with the corresponding homologous sequences from Borrelia garinii and Borrelia afzelii. Using this license, Baxter’s team developed a hexavalent multivalent rOspA formulation. Phase I/II data released in 2013 showed the vaccine to be well tolerated and strongly immunogenic [42]. Building upon a strategy analogous to Baxter’s, Valneva and Pfizer jointly developed the VLA15, a vaccine incorporating the C-terminal domains of six OspA serotypes (1–6), thereby targeting the predominant Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato strains circulating in North America and Europe. To mitigate safety concerns, putative autoimmune epitopes (residues 163–175) were deleted [1]. In Phase II clinical trials, VLA15 elicited broad-spectrum antibody responses, with seroconversion rates exceeding 90% [43]. Although the VLA15 vaccine is currently among the most promising candidate for Lyme disease, recent clinical trials have also revealed common challenges associated with vaccination. These include the potential requirement for multiple booster doses and the occurrence of certain adverse reactions. Pfizer is conducting the pivotal phase 3 VALOR trial to evaluate efficacy and safety in endemic populations.Importantly, the trial has encountered operational challenges-including temporary site closures, recruitment delays, and the exclusion of participants from non-compliant clinical sites, that have introduced uncertainty into the clinical timeline [44]. From a regulatory standpoint, key remaining hurdles include confirming durable protection across age groups, validating long-term safety, and aligning with FDA expectations for vector-borne vaccine endpoints. The agency has maintained a rigorous, evidence-based stance toward multivalent OspA vaccines, emphasizing the need to distinguish scientific evidence from public perception and to address historical concerns regarding autoimmune safety [43,45]. VLA15 has been granted Fast Track designation by the U.S. FDA. Submission of a marketing authorization application is contingent upon generation of robust confirmatory Phase III efficacy data and subject to comprehensive regulatory review. Additionally, researchers are actively exploring and validating other candidate antigens, particularly those targeting the spirochete after it has established infection within the host.

Several other outer surface proteins have been explored as potential vaccine targets. These include OspC, DbpA/B (adhesion mediators), and BBK32 [46–49]. Among them, OspC is regarded as one of the most promising due to its unique expression kinetics, immunogenicity, and demonstrated protective potential [50]. However, extensive sequence and antigenic variability among strains severely limits broad cross-protection. Monovalent OspC vaccines elicit only type-specific immunity and fail to provide cross-serotype coverage [51]. To overcome this limitation, Immuno-AG (now Baxter, Austria) developed a pentavalent adjuvanted recombinant chimeric OspC vaccine in 1999 [52]. Phase I clinical trials showed that > 95% of participants developed protective antibody responses after three doses, with an acceptable safety profile. Building on these results, Baxter advanced a 14-valent OspC vaccine designed for wider serotype coverage. However, excessive local reactogenicity at injection sites limited its clinical progress, and Phase II safety and preliminary efficacy data remain unpublished [53].

Beyond single-antigen formulations, multi-target combination strategies have emerged as a promising direction in Lyme vaccine development. A representative example is VANGUARD®crLyme (developed by Zoetis), a USDA-approved recombinant subunit vaccine for canine use that employs a “dual-antigen synergy” design. In this formulation, recombinant non-lipidated OspA prevents Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato colonization within the tick midgut. Meanwhile, a chimeric epitope protein, Ch14, incorporates protective L5/H5 epitopes from 14 OspC serotypes, thereby overcoming OspC polymorphism. This two-pronged mechanism establishes a defense both within the vector and within the host: OspA antibodies eliminate spirochetes inside the tick, whereas Ch14-induced antibodies neutralize early infection after transmission to the host. Immunization with VANGUARD®crLyme elicits high-titer, cross-reactive antibodies against more than 25 OspC variants. Tick challenge studies demonstrated infection-blocking efficacy exceeding 93%, together with a favorable safety profile [54]. This approach highlights the potential of multi-target vaccine strategies and provides a conceptual framework for the design of next-generation human vaccines.

Despite these advances including technological refinements and integrated antigen strategies that have addressed several early limitations subunit vaccines still face critical challenges. Chief among these are antigenic variation and immune escape, durable immune memory induction, and heterogeneous responsiveness across human populations.

Nucleic acid vaccines

DNA vaccines

DNA vaccines, introduced in the early 21st century, represent a novel class of immunization platforms and have gained considerable attention in the context of Lyme disease research. These vaccines are based on circular plasmid DNA carrying antigen-encoding genes. Following inoculation, the DNA enters host cells, where it is transcribed and translated into antigenic proteins. These proteins are subsequently processed and presented on the cell surface via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) pathways, thereby activating both humoral and cellular immune responses [55]. Among outer surface proteins (Osps) of Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato, OspC has been a major focus in DNA vaccine research due to its critical involvement in transmission and virulence. Preclinical studies in animal models have consistently demonstrated strong immunogenicity and protective efficacy of DNA vaccines targeting Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato antigens: DNA vaccines encoding OspC have demonstrated strong immunogenicity and protection in animal models. For instance, tattoo-delivered OspC plasmid vaccination in mice elicited high-titer antigen-specific IgG and conferred nearly complete protection against tick-borne challenge, with sterilizing immunity observed in seven of eight animals. This protection matched that of recombinant protein vaccines and persisted even with an accelerated immunization schedule with a Th1-skewed immunity (indicated by a low IgG1/IgG2a ratio) supporting effective bacterial clearance [56].

OspA has also remained a key focus of DNA vaccine research. A synthetic OspA-based construct, pLD1, delivered using CELLECTRA® electroporation technology, elicited robust humoral and cellular immune responses in mice, with a single dose conferring complete protection against Borrelia burgdorferi sensu stricto challenge. In guinea pigs, antibody responses persisted for at least one year, with antibodies directed against protective OspA epitopes that competed with human protective monoclonal antibodies for binding [57]. In addition, a fusion DNA vaccine encoding both OspA and OspC was capable of inducing antibodies against both antigens. However, protection appeared to depend primarily on OspA-specific responses; OspC antibodies alone were insufficient to clear established infections. These findings highlight the importance of antigen complementarity and synergy in designing multi-component DNA vaccines [58].

But attempts to develop DNA vaccines targeting tick salivary proteins have been less successful. Proteins such as Salp15, tHRF, TSLPI, and Tix-5, owing to their roles in Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato transmission, were considered attractive targets. However, DNA vaccines encoding these proteins generated only modest IgG titers (Salp15 and Tix-5) or negligible responses (tHRF and TSLPI), and failed to provide protection against infection. These results suggest that DNA vaccination may be less suitable unsuitable for identifying or validating anti-tick antigens [59].

Despite promising preclinical efficacy in animal models, DNA vaccines face well-defined translational barriers that have constrained clinical development against Lyme disease and other pathogens. Historically, these have encompassed inefficient in vivo transfection of human cells, immunogenicity that is weak and variable in humans relative to animal models, and scalability challenges for delivery systems (e.g. electroporation) suited to mass immunization [60,61]. These factors have hindered the advancement of Lyme disease DNA vaccine candidates from preclinical studies to human clinical trials, even for the most promising Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato antigen targets such as OspA and OspC.

In summary, DNA vaccines targeting Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato antigens such as OspA and OspC have produced promising protective outcomes, whereas attempts to target tick salivary proteins have been far less effective and remain underdeveloped. Moving forward, research should prioritize overcoming strain diversity, optimizing delivery, and advancing candidates into clinical trials.

mRNA vaccines

mRNA vaccines represent a newer generation of vaccine technology, in which lipid-based delivery systems transport messenger RNA encoding Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato antigens into host cells. The host’s own cellular machinery then synthesizes the encoded proteins and presents them to the immune system, stimulating both antibody and T cell – mediated responses [62].

Across preclinical studies, mRNA-Lipid Nanoparticle (mRNA-LNP) platforms have in animal models consistently exhibited more favorable immunological profiles than conventional protein vaccines, with a trend toward greater potency. In murine models, LNP-encapsulated OspA mRNA (mRNA-OspA) elicited stronger OspA-specific IgG responses and more potent serum bactericidal activity compared with traditional protein-based vaccines such as OspA-ferritin constructs or the licensed canine product Recombitek Lyme. Notably, a construct lacking a secretion signal peptide conferred complete protection, entirely preventing tick-mediated transmission of Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato [63]. In a separate study, a nucleoside-modified OspA mRNA-LNP vaccine induced durable humoral and cellular immunity after a single immunization, including robust memory B cell and plasma cell responses. Antibody titers persisted for at least 24 weeks, with protection rates of approximately 80% against heterologous challenge with the Borrelia burgdorferi sensu stricto N40 strain, performance that appeared favorable relative to alum-adjuvanted protein vaccines in the same animal models [64]. Collectively, these preclinical findings reveal a consistent advantage of mRNA platforms: single‑dose immunogenicity, strong T follicular helper (Tfh) and germinal center (GC) responses, and trends toward improved durability relative to protein subunits as observed in rodents.

Beyond pathogen-directed approaches, mRNA vaccines encoding tick salivary proteins represent an early proof-of-concept strategy in preclinical models for potentially interrupting transmission at the vector-host interface. A representative example is the 19ISP vaccine, which encodes 19 salivary proteins from Ixodes scapularis [65]. In guinea pig models, immunization triggered erythema at tick bite sites within 18 hours an indication of rapid immune activation. This response impaired tick feeding, promoted premature detachment, and reduced tick engorgement, collectively suppressing Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato transmission. When ticks were manually removed upon erythema development, none of the immunized animals became infected, compared with a 46% infection rate in controls. Notably, guinea pigs are not natural hosts for Ixodes ticks, and these findings remain restricted to preclinical models; as such, this approach should be interpreted as preliminary proof-of-principle rather than a near-term clinical candidate. Further validation in natural host models and clinical studies will be required to assess its translatability for human use.

Summarizing previous research, the advantages of mRNA vaccines observed in animal studies are evident. For standard non-replicating mRNA vaccines, their manufacturing process does not involve infectious pathogens, thereby substantially reducing the risk of vaccine-derived infection or disease. Compared with protein subunit vaccines, mRNA vaccines direct antigen synthesis inside host cells, allowing proteins to undergo natural folding and post-translational modifications, thus avoiding the structural errors frequently seen in recombinant proteins produced in vitro. Compared with DNA vaccines, mRNA only remains transiently in the cytoplasm and does not enter the nucleus. This characteristic greatly lowers the theoretical risk of genomic integration, though rare innate immune DNA damage pathways remain under scientific evaluation. It also bypasses transcription and nuclear export, features that have supported rapid deployment during recent public health emergencies; however, direct extrapolation of regulatory or epidemiologic trajectories from COVID-19 or influenza to Lyme disease is not warranted given fundamental differences in transmission dynamics, host – pathogen interactions, and target populations [66–68].

Despite these preclinical advantages, mRNA-based platforms face substantial translational limitations in Lyme disease prevention. A key challenge is the intrinsic instability of mRNA, which imposes stringent cold-chain requirements for storage and distribution, complicating deployment in rural or resource-limited endemic areas [69]. Moreover, the need for lipid nanoparticle formulations and modified nucleoside manufacturing elevates production costs compared to conventional vaccines. Additionally, lipid nanoparticle carriers can elicit mild-to-moderate local and systemic reactogenicity. Finally, clinical data from mRNA vaccines targeting other pathogens indicate that protective antibody titers may wane rapidly, potentially necessitating booster doses to sustain long-term immunity [70,71].

Vector vaccines

Vector-based delivery systems integrate antigen stability, targeted delivery, and intrinsic adjuvanticity, making them versatile tools for Lyme disease vaccine development. Across diverse viral and non-viral platforms, studies consistently reveal: robust cellular immunity, favorable safety profiles in animal models, and protection breadth that varies with vector choice and antigen design.

Among viral vectors, Newcastle disease virus (NDV) was developed to carry genes for either Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato membrane protein A (BmpA) or OspC. Immunization of hamsters via intranasal, intramuscular, or intraperitoneal routes induced robust antigen-specific antibody responses. Recombinant NDV expressing BmpA markedly lowered spirochete loads in joints but failed to provide reliable protection in C3H and BALB/c mice, highlighting the influence of host background on immune efficacy [72]. An attenuated vaccinia virus (VV) vector carrying an OspC-A fusion gene conferred complete protection against homologous infection after two doses, preventing both myocarditis and lymphadenopathy. This vaccine induced a Th1-biased response dominated by IgG2a, promoted antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), and stimulated CD4+ T-cell production of IFN-γ. Even so, its breadth of protection and long-term safety remain uncertain and require further clinical evaluation [73]. A cross study comparison reveals that viral vectors excel at cellular immunity but suffer from host-specific efficacy and preexisting immunity risks, limiting broad clinical translatability.

Nanoparticle-based vectors have also shown considerable promise for antigen delivery. LNP-encapsulated OspC DNA vaccines, after two doses in mice, elicited high titers of bactericidal antibodies and completely blocked spirochete colonization in the bladder, heart, and other tissues, thereby preventing associated pathology [74]. mRNA-LNP formulations further enhanced immunogenicity in animal models: OspA-expressing constructs triggered strong T follicular helper (Tfh) cell and germinal center B-cell responses, as well as long-lived memory B cells and plasma cells after a single immunization. Antibody titers persisted for at least 24 weeks, and protection compared favorably with traditional alum-adjuvanted recombinant protein vaccines in murine studies [64]. In addition to the aforementioned LNP-based vaccines, another research effort has developed an OspA-ferritin nanoparticle vaccine utilizing Helicobacter pylori ferritin as a carrier. This vaccine incorporates lipid-free OspA proteins from seven major OspA serotypes. In mouse and rhesus macaque models, it induced OspA-specific antibody titers that were 4.4 to 200-fold higher than those elicited by the conventional Recombitek™ vaccine, and these elevated titers persisted for at least six months. Furthermore, no immune interference among serotypes was observed in the multivalent formulation, and no adverse effects were reported in animal studies. Importantly, protective efficacy and immunogenicity have been evaluated exclusively in rodent and non-human primate models, with observed variability across different animal systems underscores host-specific immune effects, and findings should not be directly extrapolated to humans without rigorous clinical validation. These findings collectively present an efficient strategy for preventing Lyme disease, with the potential to cover the major globally prevalent strains [42].

Outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) and virus-like particles (VLPs) represent emerging vector platforms. Genetically engineered Neisseria meningitidis OMVs displaying or chemically conjugated with OspA serve simultaneously as adjuvant and delivery systems. In murine studies, immunization with these constructs elicited OspA-specific antibodies and conferred partial protection through a Th1-skewed response dominated by IgG2a [75]. Similarly, VLPs derived from hepatitis B core (HBc) protein fused with Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato BBK32 fragments demonstrated efficient cellular entry via fibronectin-mediated caveolae/raft endocytosis, offering a novel framework for targeted antigen delivery [76].

Taken together, the various vector platforms offer distinct advantages: viral vectors drive potent cellular immunity; nanoparticle systems induce humoral responses and multivalency; OMVs and VLPs combine delivery with built-in adjuvanticity. However, host variability, scalability, and preexisting immunity remain shared challenges. Although vector-based approaches hold considerable promise for Lyme disease prevention, their successful clinical translation will depend on sustained optimization of safety, stability, and immunogenicity, together with rigorous validation in well-designed clinical trials.

Epitope vaccines

A major trend in current vaccine research is the application of computational modeling, bioinformatics, and advanced recombinant gene expression technologies to design synthetic antigens with both high immunogenicity and enhanced safety. This “reverse vaccinology” strategy enables the rational selection and engineering of protective epitopes [77,78].

Bioinformatics plays a important role in this process, facilitating structural and physicochemical characterization of antigens as well as prediction of T- and B-cell epitopes. By precisely designing and modifying protein structures, multiple immunodominant epitopes can be combined into a single construct capable of inducing stronger and longer-lasting immune responses, thereby improving protective efficacy [79]. Chen et al. [80] took this approach by analyzing the protein signal peptides, physicochemical properties, transmembrane domains, and secondary and tertiary structures, and then combining the results with predictions of both T- and B-cell epitopes and molecular docking to optimize vaccine design according to the research objectives. Using this approach, they successfully constructed a multi-epitope vaccine based on Borrelia burgdorferi sensu stricto OspC and BmpA. Experimental studies in mice confirmed that the vaccine exhibited strong immunogenicity, stimulating both humoral and cellular immune responses. In addition to the antigens described above, proteins such as OspA, TsaE, and FliP have also been applied to T- and B-cell epitope prediction in multi-epitope vaccine design, although their actual potential still awaits robust experimental confirmation [81,82].

Bioinformatics-driven strategies for multi-epitope vaccine development offer a promising new avenue for Lyme disease prevention. However, despite the advantages of computational approaches, reverse vaccinology and epitope-based methods face significant limitations. Epitope prediction algorithms exhibit non-negligible false-positive rates, and predicted epitopes often fail to recapitulate the expected immunogenicity experimentally due to differences in protein folding, post-translational modifications, and host MHC context [83,84]. In addition, both in vitro and in vivo validation remains laborious and low-throughput, and many computationally designed constructs lack confirmed protective efficacy in relevant infection models [85,86]. Translating such strategies into practical application requires establishing a comprehensive computational prediction – experimental validation cycle. Future research should focus on optimizing epitope combinations, enhancing structural and formulation stability, and systematically conducting more comprehensive in vitro and in vivo evaluations to accelerate the clinical translation of vaccine candidates.

Anti-microbiota vaccine

The tick microbiome is a central determinant of vector competence and pathogen colonization. In this context, anti-microbiota vaccines have emerged as a cutting-edge strategy to simultaneously control tick infestation and interrupt pathogen transmission [87,88]. Leveraging 16S rRNA gene sequencing and co-occurrence network analysis, studies have identified key bacterial groups, such as Escherichia-Shigella, Muribaculaceae, and Alistipes, which play a pivotal role in maintaining tick microbiome homeostasis [89,90]. The survival, colonization, and transmission of Lyme disease pathogens, typified by the Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato complex, are highly dependent on the microenvironmental homeostasis of their host’s microbiome. Recent research demonstrates that vaccines targeting core bacterial taxa such as Escherichia coli can elicit specific host IgM/IgG antibodies. When ingested by ticks during feeding, these antibodies significantly perturb the abundance of core bacterial communities, consequently reducing the infection burden of Borrelia afzelii [91]. This strategy provides an innovative intervention pathway to interrupt Lyme disease transmission by indirectly modulating tick microbiome structure via host immune responses.

Compared to conventional control methods, anti-microbiota vaccines offer distinctive advantages, including high host specificity, superior environmental compatibility, and low propensity for inducing drug resistance. These vaccines can be integrated synergistically with existing vaccine strategies to enhance overall prophylactic efficacy [88,92]. However, current research still faces several critical limitations. First, existing vaccine targets are predominantly focused on a limited subset of core microbiota, necessitating broader coverage for comprehensive protection. Furthermore, the persistence of microbiome perturbation and its generalizability across tick species remain to be systematically validated. Addressing these challenges will establish a theoretical foundation for the clinical development and large-scale application of this technology.

In addition, solving the above problems is still inseparable from attention to the hidden ecological safety risks and regulatory issues. Specifically, the ecological safety and long-term reversibility of microbiota perturbations remain inadequately characterized. Large-scale intervention may restructure the microbial mutualistic networks within tick populations, alter interspecific competitive dynamics, and exert unforeseen cascading effects on non-target arthropod communities. Furthermore, host-derived antibodies and immune effector molecules can enter ticks through the blood meal; however, whether they induce bidirectional perturbations in microbial interactions within the tick-host-environment ternary system remains systematically unevaluated [93]. From a regulatory perspective, such vaccines currently lack standardized frameworks for ecological risk assessment, clearly defined approval pathways, and long-term safety data. Therefore, future research should prioritize field-scale ecological risk assessment, validation of the temporal dynamics and reversibility of microbiota perturbations, and cross-ecosystem monitoring of non-target effects, thereby ensuring the safe and sustainable deployment of such novel vaccines.

Diversity of borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato strains

The extraordinary genetic and antigenic diversity of Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato strains represents the primary obstacle to developing broadly protective Lyme disease vaccines. At a global scale, distinct pathogenic genospecies predominate by region: Borrelia burgdorferi sensu stricto in North America, and B. afzelii and B. garinii in Europe and Asia [94,95]. This geographic divergence drives marked sequence variation in key antigens such as OspC (<80% identity across genospecies), complicating cross-regional vaccine design [96]. Compounding this challenge, global Borrelia surveillance networks remain fragmented, representing a critical yet underaddressed barrier to targeted vaccine development. Systematic genomic sequencing, real-time strain tracking, and standardized genotyping capacity are deficient across many endemic regions, further impeding the rational design of geographically tailored vaccines. The surveillance gap is especially acute in low-resource and emerging endemic settings, where regional strain diversity remains uncharacterized and vaccine-strain matching uncertain.

Within genospecies, extensive OspC polymorphism (at least 22 types, A – U) further limits cross-protection, with types A, B, I, K linked to invasive infection [51,97]. Full coverage of clinically important strains would require at least 34 different OspC domains in such a vaccine [98]. Compounding these challenges, Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato employs robust immune evasion via antigenic variation of the vlsE gene. Through recombination with multiple silent cassettes, vlsE continuously generates novel surface epitopes that rapidly escape vaccine-induced antibody recognition [99].

Together, geographic divergence, intraspecies polymorphism, and antigenic switching collectively render conserved epitope discovery the central challenge for next-generation vaccines. Moreover, vaccine performance is further modulated by tick vector competence, host exposure patterns, and climate-driven changes in tick distribution and seasonal activity, all of which shape real-world transmission dynamics independently of antigen composition.

Complex immune evasion mechanisms of borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato

Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato has evolved multiple immune evasion strategies that directly undermine vaccine-induced immunity [100,101]. Each mechanism directly constrains vaccine design, as outlined below.

First, Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato evades complement attack by recruiting host regulatory factors via CRASP family proteins (CspA, CspZ, ErpP), which bind factor H (FH) and FHL-1 to block pathway activation and MAC formation [102,103]. CspZ binds FH with sub-nanomolar affinity (KD = 210 pM), potently inhibiting C3b deposition and rendering complement-dependent cytotoxicity ineffective [104]. Therefore, vaccines relying solely on complement-fixing antibodies may fail to clear spirochetes, necessitating formulations that also elicit cellular immunity or opsonophagocytosis.

Second, the pathogen invades fibroblasts and macrophages to establish intracellular persistence, sequestering itself away from antibody-mediated clearance [101,105,106]. This necessitates vaccines that induce robust T-cell immunity-especially CD8+ cytotoxic T cells-to target intracellular reservoirs.

Third, vlsE-mediated antigenic variation enables rapid escape from adaptive immunity by continuously generating novel surface epitopes [99,107]. Variant selection occurs within two weeks in mice, rendering monovalent vaccines ineffective; consequently, candidates must target conserved, non-variable epitopes or employ multivalent designs.

Finally, Borrelia burgdorferi sensu stricto dampens host immunity through tick – pathogen interactions: Salp15 binds OspC and inhibits CD4+ T-cell activation, blunting vaccine-induced cellular responses [108,109]. This implies that effective Lyme disease vaccines may need to either avoid targets susceptible to Salp15-mediated inhibition or incorporate adjuvants capable of sustaining T-cell activation despite immunosuppressive conditions.

Therefore, advancing research into the molecular mechanisms of Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato invasion in mammalian hosts and its developmental processes in vivo, as well as identifying key antigens and targets directly or indirectly involved in immune evasion, is essential for the rational design of next-generation vaccines. Importantly, Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato can establish latent, immune-privileged niches in connective tissues, joints, and the central nervous system, evading both antibody and T-cell surveillance [110,111]. This persistence poses a critical translational limitation: even highly effective infection-blocking vaccines may fail to prevent persistent infection or PTLDS.

Limitations of adjuvants

An effective adjuvants should induce durable, broad systemic and mucosal immunity. However, currently available adjuvants present important limitations.

First, aluminum salts are safe and widely used, although they primarily induce Th2-type immune responses dominated by humoral immunity (consistent with observations in subunit and DNA vaccine platforms, Subunit vaccines-2.3), while providing limited stimulation of cellular immunity (Th1, CD8+ T cells) [112]. Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato is an extracellular bacterium, yet it can invade host tissues and establish persistent infection, likely requiring more robust cellular immune responses-particularly Th1 and CD8+ T-cell activity-for effective clearance. The restricted ability of aluminum adjuvants to drive such responses may therefore limit their protective efficacy.

Second, newly developed adjuvants such as Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists (e.g. MPL, CpG) can improve cellular immunity and promote Th1 polarization, but achieving a balance between safety and immunogenicity remains a challenge [113–115]. For instance, the TLR4 agonist MPL, when combined with aluminum salts in the AS04 formulation, enhances both humoral and cellular immune responses but increases manufacturing complexity and cost [116]. From regulatory and manufacturing perspectives, novel adjuvants often face prolonged approval timelines due to limited long-term safety data, while their specialized production processes hinder scalable, low-cost deployment in endemic regions.

Third, nanoparticle-based adjuvants (e.g. lipid nanoparticles, Matrix-M) and viral vector adjuvants have demonstrated excellent efficacy in COVID-19 vaccines, although their mechanisms are complex. A key strength of these delivery systems is their potent induction of Tfh cell and germinal center B-cell responses (Vector vaccines)-a property that also contributes to their high immunogenicity. However, this strong immunogenicity is associated with a risk of adverse reactions. Furthermore, preexisting immunity remains a concern. Collectively, these factors may compromise the feasibility of repeated vaccination and limit broad population coverage [117–120]. Furthermore, these advanced formulations frequently require strict cold-chain logistics and specialized manufacturing facilities, which further limits their accessibility in resource-limited settings and adds to the regulatory hurdles for global registration. Since Lyme disease vaccines must cover people from various age groups and various immune states, more work has yet to be done to improve adjuvant formulation of Lyme disease vaccines.

Notably, next-generation adjuvant and antigen design strategies are emerging and hold substantial promise for future Lyme disease vaccine development. These strategies mainly fall into three categories: First, structure-based self-adjuvanting antigen design can endow antigens with intrinsic adjuvant properties through multimerization, oligomerization, or structural stabilization [121,122]. The engineered ordered antigen arrays and conformational epitopes mimic the native structure of pathogens, enhancing B cell receptor clustering and germinal center reactions, thereby simultaneously improving the magnitude and durability of humoral immune responses with reduced or no requirement for exogenous adjuvants [123]. Second, the integrated design of mRNA platforms achieves synergistic enhancement through two complementary mechanisms: on one hand, the use of nucleoside modification and untranslated region (UTR) engineering suppresses excessive innate immune activation of the mRNA itself to ensure efficient antigen translation [124,125]; on the other hand, the endosomal sensing of ionizable lipids within the lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivery system provides a built-in adjuvant effect, driving germinal center reactions and potent humoral immunity [126]. This “low-immunogenicity mRNA plus immunostimulatory carrier” strategy simplifies formulation processes and induces durable protective immunity without the need for exogenous adjuvants, thereby providing a streamlined platform for Lyme disease vaccine development. Finally, mucosal adjuvants can induce local tissue-resident memory T cell and antibody responses at the tick – host interface, providing immediate protection at the site of pathogen transmission [127,128]. By improving frontline defense against pathogen transmission and complementing systemic immunity, this strategy holds promise for achieving earlier, stronger, and more durable protection against tick-borne pathogens.

In summary, current adjuvants are constrained not only by immunological gaps (e.g. weak Th1 polarization, poor germinal center support, limited CD8+ T-cell priming) but also by regulatory uncertainty, manufacturing complexity, and scalability limitations. Emerging strategies-including self-adjuvanting antigens, mRNA-intrinsic immune activation, and mucosal adjuvants-offer promising avenues to overcome these barriers and enable cost-effective, widely deployable Lyme disease vaccines for global endemic populations.

Pathogen co-infection

Ticks commonly carry several pathogens at once, including Anaplasma phagocytophilum, Babesia spp., and the tick-borne encephalitis virus [3,129]. As a result, patients often present with complex and overlapping clinical manifestations, making single infections more difficult to recognize and increasing the likelihood of treatment failure [130,131]. Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato co-infection with Babesia microti or Ap has been shown to exacerbate disease severity [129,132], whereas co-infection with tick-borne encephalitis virus may obscure the diagnosis and delay timely initiation of antibiotic therapy for Lyme borreliosis [131]. However, the prevalence of such co-infections and their influence on the epidemiology, diagnosis, clinical course, and treatment of Lyme disease remain poorly defined. Mixed infections therefore represent a major challenge for Lyme vaccine development and require careful evaluation.

Safety concerns and low public acceptance restricting vaccine uptake

The redevelopment of Lyme disease vaccines remains severely hindered by deep-rooted human factors, including persistent safety concerns, suboptimal public acceptance, historic distrust, and inadequate risk communication, which together represent one of the most formidable barriers to successful deployment.

First, public acceptance barriers stem directly from historical controversies surrounding the LYMErix® vaccine. Licensed in the late 1990s, this vaccine faced intense scrutiny over self-reported adverse events. These events included arthritic symptoms and persistent fatigue. Extensive epidemiologic investigations and formal FDA reviews concluded that no causal link to autoimmunity existed. Nevertheless, negative media narratives, high-profile litigation, and grassroots anxiety led to abrupt product withdrawal. This episode established legitimate historical concerns about safety communication, transparency, and post-marketing surveillance that continue to shape public perceptions today.

Second, online misinformation and unsubstantiated claims have further eroded confidence, including suggestions that Lyme disease vaccines may induce chronic Lyme-like syndromes. However, misinformation is only one contributing factor; low uptake also reflects genuine concerns arising from past experiences, low individual risk perception, inconsistent public health messaging, and limited community engagement in endemic regions. The discontinuation of LYMErix® has also reduced trust in industry and regulatory systems, fueling perceptions that development priorities may not fully align with long-term public safety needs [38,44].

Third, complex multi-dose regimens (e.g. three primary doses plus a booster in the VALOR trial) contribute to vaccine fatigue and poor adherence, particularly in non-clinical populations.

Therefore, sustained, bidirectional trust-building is essential. Success will require shared responsibilities among manufacturers, regulators, clinicians, and public health institutions-not merely one-way public education. Manufacturers must uphold transparent clinical trial design, proactive safety disclosure, and robust long-term pharmacovigilance. Regulators must maintain evidence-based oversight while communicating rationales clearly to the public. Clinicians and health authorities should engage endemic communities, acknowledge historical concerns respectfully, and provide consistent, culturally appropriate risk information.

While HPV and COVID-19 vaccine campaigns demonstrate that targeted communication and pharmacovigilance can improve acceptance [133,134], key contextual differences must be recognized. Lyme disease has distinct regional endemicity, variable individual risk perception, a unique history of vaccine withdrawal, and distinct stakeholder dynamics that preclude direct extrapolation. Even technically safe and effective next-generation vaccines such as VLA15 may face limited uptake without transparent governance, inclusive dialogue, and proactive, culturally sensitive engagement to address both historical concerns and contemporary uncertainties.

Conclusions and prospects

Collectively, this review offers a systematic roadmap of Lyme disease vaccine evolution – from first-generation monovalent OspA constructs to modern multivalent candidates – while dissecting the intricate interplay of antigen selection, platform engineering, and safety considerations that define contemporary design. Distinguishing itself from recent literature, it synthesizes cutting-edge 2025 findings: pivotal Phase II data for multivalent OspA vaccines, breakthroughs in mRNA-mediated antigen delivery, and a dual socio-scientific reappraisal of earlier vaccine withdrawals. This synthesis closes the timeliness and multidimensional-analysis gaps evident in current coverage.

To address the global heterogeneity of Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato, sophisticated immune evasion, and region-specific epidemiology, we propose a “triangular strategy” integrating antigen multivalency, platform personalization, and geographical tailoring to overcome current bottlenecks. Future vaccines must transcend antigen-centric design to embrace the full vector-host-pathogen triad, while addressing ecological constraints, immune privilege, post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome, and safety communication. Transparent risk communication and robust post-marketing surveillance are equally critical to rebuild public trust.

Looking ahead to the next decade, we identify four priority action areas that hold the potential to drive the translation of research outcomes into practical applications. First, advance multivalent and chimeric antigen candidate vaccines (e.g. VLA15 and next-generation OspC combination vaccines) through confirmatory Phase III efficacy trials, while optimizing regulatory review pathways, to obtain marketing authorization featuring broad-spectrum protective efficacy and regionally adaptive formulation configurations. Second, integrate cutting-edge vaccine technologies such as self-amplifying RNA (saRNA), vector-host interface vaccines, mucosal immunization strategies, and nanoparticle delivery systems to achieve durable immunity, simplified immunization regimens, reduced cold-chain dependence, and scalable manufacturing capacity in Lyme disease-endemic regions. Third, establish a global genomic surveillance consortium for Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato to continuously map strain diversity, antigenic drift, and emergent genospecies, thereby informing iterative vaccine strain selection and multivalent composition. Fourth, implement a prospective, community-engaged framework for risk communication and long-term pharmacovigilance from the earliest stages of clinical research, in order to rebuild public trust, ensure transparent safety governance, and maximize vaccination coverage among high-risk populations. Naturally, achieving these priorities will require sustained collaboration among academia, industry, regulatory agencies, and public health institutions. If successfully implemented, these efforts will provide a clearer pathway toward safe, effective, and widely accepted Lyme disease vaccines by 2035.
