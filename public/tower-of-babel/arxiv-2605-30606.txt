Transcendence and measures via the refined Diophantine exponent
Quang-Khai Nguyen

Abstract
. In 2007, Adamczewski and Bugeaud introduced the notion of the Diophantine exponent of an infinite word as a quantitative measure of repetition, leading to new transcendence results for real numbers whose expansions in an integer base are sufficiently simple. In the present article, we introduce the refined Diophantine exponent , which detects weaker forms of repetition while preserving the full strength of the classical approach. This new exponent applies in situations where repetition is partially obscured by some form of noise . Related ideas already appear in the work of Corvaja and Zannier in 2002 and, more recently, in the works of Kebis, Luca, Ouaknine, Scoones, and Worrell. Our approach provides a unified framework that recovers and extends these results, as well as those of Adamczewski and Bugeaud. We also develop quantitative refinements of this method, leading to results about transcendence measures. The recent breakthrough of Bell, Diller, and Jonsson in the context of algebraic dynamics is partly based on a similar idea, which also served as a motivation for the present work.



1.
Introduction

The study of the Diophantine properties of values of analytic functions

f_{\mathbf{a}}(z)=\sum_{i\geq 0}a_{i}z^{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[[z]]

at algebraic points is a central theme in transcendental number theory. A long-standing approach consists in exploiting combinatorial properties of the coefficients sequence
\mathbf{a}=a_{0}a_{1}\cdots
to investigate the transcendence of the values of
f_{\mathbf{a}}
, as well as more quantitative questions such as irrationality and transcendence measures. The underlying philosophy is that when the sequence
\mathbf{a}
is
sufficiently simple
, in the sense that it has a simple combinatorial or dynamical structure, it can often be well approximated by eventually periodic sequences. Consequently, the function
f_{\mathbf{a}}
can be well approximated by rational functions, which in turn implies that its values at algebraic points admit good algebraic approximations. From the perspective of Diophantine approximation, this typically leads to a
dichotomy
: such values are either transcendental, or algebraic of a very restricted form.

This circle of ideas goes back to the pioneering work of Liouville, who proved the transcendence of numbers such as

\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{1}{10^{i!}}\,\cdot

Later, deeper tools from Diophantine approximation led to further classes of transcendental numbers obtained in this way. Particularly significant are the works of Maillet
[
36
]
and Baker
[
12
]
on continued fractions, and, a few decades later, the work of Ferenczi and Mauduit
[
25
]
on Sturmian numbers. This approach acquired a new dimension in the works of Adamczewski and Bugeaud, and of Adamczewski, Bugeaud, and Luca, where the full strength of the
p
-adic Subspace Theorem
[
39
,
40
]
, which is a powerful extension of Schmidt’s Subspace Theorem
[
41
]
, was brought into the picture.

The general strategy was formalized by Adamczewski and Bugeaud in

[
2
]
through the introduction of the notion of the
Diophantine exponent
, which measures the periodicity of an infinite word.
The Diophantine exponent of a word
\mathbf{a}
, denoted
{\bf Dio}(\mathbf{a})
, is defined as the supremum of the real numbers
\rho
for which there exist three sequences of integers
(r_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(s_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, and
(t_{n})_{n\geq 0}
satisfying, for all
n\geq 0
,

(i)

-1\leq r_{n}<s_{n}<t_{n}
;

(ii)

the words
\mathbf{a}[r_{n}+1,\,r_{n}+t_{n}-s_{n}]
and
\mathbf{a}[s_{n}+1,\,t_{n}]
coincide;

(iii)

t_{n}\geq\rho\,s_{n}
;

(iv)

s_{n}-r_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

Here,
\mathbf{a}[r,s]
denotes the finite word
a_{r}a_{r+1}\cdots a_{s}
.
The main result of
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
asserts that if
\mathbf{a}
takes values in a finite set of integers and if
\beta
is an algebraic number with
|\beta|>1
such that

{\bf Dio}(\mathbf{a})>\frac{\log M(\beta)}{\log|\beta|}\,,

then the following dichotomy holds:

•

either
f_{\mathbf{a}}(1/\beta)
belongs to the number field
\mathbb{Q}(\beta)
, or it is transcendental.

Here
M(\beta)
denotes the Mahler measure of
\beta
. The original definition of the Diophantine exponent is slightly different, but easily seen to be equivalent to the one given above.
Setting
U_{n}=\mathbf{a}[0,r_{n}]
and
V_{n}=\mathbf{a}[r_{n}+1,s_{n}]
, Condition (i) implies that
\mathbf{a}
coincides with the eventually periodic sequence
U_{n}V_{n}^{\infty}
up to index
t_{n}
.
When
\beta
is an integer, or more generally a Pisot or a Salem number, the above result only requires that
{\bf Dio}(\mathbf{a})>1
. However, stronger repetitivity is needed to treat values of
f_{\mathbf{a}}
at more general algebraic points. In particular, in order to treat values at
all
algebraic numbers, one needs to assume that
{\bf Dio}(\mathbf{a})=\infty
, which is a very strong condition.

In retrospect, a way to overcome this difficulty takes its source in a previous work by Corvaja and Zannier
[
23
]
on lacunary numbers, by applying the Subspace Theorem in higher dimensions.
More recently, similar ideas have appeared, apparently independently, in several works

[
33
,
34
,
28
,
29
]
,
where new combinatorial transcendence criteria are established for some class of coefficents sequences called
echoing
and
stuterring
over arbitrary algebraic bases.
Such ideas also arise in the breakthrough work of Bell, Diller, and Jonsson
[
13
]
in the context of algebraic dynamics, where the authors construct the first example of a map with a transcendental dynamical degree.

The present paper is motivated partly by these recent developments.
We introduce a new exponent, called the
refined Diophantine exponent
and denoted by
\mathbf{Rdio}
, which is defined in the same spirit as the classical Diophantine exponent, but with Condition (ii) replaced by a weaker requirement (ii’): instead of asking the two words
\mathbf{a}[r_{n}+1,r_{n}+t_{n}-s_{n}]
and
\mathbf{a}[s_{n}+1,t_{n}]
to coincide, we only require them to be sufficiently close in a precise sense related to the Hamming distance as follows.

To define the refined Diophantine exponent, we need to introduce the notion of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-closedness.

Definition 1.1
.

For two finite words
U=u_{1}\cdots u_{L}
and
V=v_{1}\cdots v_{L}
of the same length
L>0
and for
\epsilon>0
and integer
\delta\geq 0
, we say that
U
and
V
are
(\epsilon,\delta)
-closed
if there exists
\delta
subintervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{\delta}
of
\{1,\ldots,L\}
such that

(i)

U
and
V
agree outside
\displaystyle\bigcup_{j=1}^{\delta}I_{j}
, more precisely,

\{1\leq i\leq L:u_{i}\neq v_{i}\}\subseteq\displaystyle\bigcup_{j=1}^{\delta}I_{j};

(ii)

\Bigg|\displaystyle\bigcup_{j=1}^{\delta}I_{j}\Bigg|\leq\epsilon L
.

Here, we note that the intervals
I_{j}
can be empty.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=a_{0}a_{2}\cdots
be an infinite word. Let

\rho\geq 1
be a real number. We say that
\mathbf{a}
satisfies Condition
(*)_{\rho}
if for every
\epsilon>0
, there exist three sequences of integers
(r_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(s_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, and
(t_{n})_{n\geq 0}
satisfying, for all
n\geq 0
,

(i)

-1\leq r_{n}<s_{n}<t_{n}
;

(ii’)

there exists
\delta\geq 0
such that for all
n
large enough,
\mathbf{a}[r_{n}+1,r_{n}+t_{n}-s_{n}]
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-closed to
\mathbf{a}[s_{n}+1,t_{n}]
;

(iii)

{t_{n}}\geq\rho{s_{n}}
;

(iv)

s_{n}-r_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

We define
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})
to be the supremum of the real numbers
\rho
for which
\mathbf{a}
satisfies
Condition
(*)_{\rho}
.

The mismatches between these two words may be viewed as a form of
noise
that partially obscures the periodicity of
{\bf a}
. It follows from this definition that
1\leq\mathbf{Dio}(\mathbf{a})\leq\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})\leq\infty
.

Our first main result, Theorem
A
, is the analogue, for this new exponent, of the main result of
[
2
]
.
It also allows for more general coefficient sequences and for the use of arbitrary absolute values.
In this sense, it expresses the fact that such noise can, to a certain extent, be ignored from the Diophantine point of view.
On the one hand, in all the aforementioned works

[
23
,
13
,
33
,
34
,
28
,
29
]
, we show that the corresponding refined Diophantine exponent is infinite, so Theorem
A
recovers all these results.
On the other hand, our theorem also applies to coefficient sequences whose refined Diophantine exponent may be finite, thereby recovering the results of

[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.

Theorem A
.

Let
K
be a number field, and
\mathsf{v}
be a place on
K
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be a finite set of places containing all archimedean places. Let
\beta\in K
such that
|\beta|_{\mathsf{v}}>1
. Let
\mathbf{a}=a_{0}a_{1}\cdots
be an infinite word over the ring of
\mathcal{S}
-integers
\mathcal{O}_{K,\mathcal{S}}
such that
{\mathrm{h}}(a_{i})=o(i)
and
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})>\dfrac{\log{\mathrm{H}}(\beta)}{\log|\beta|_{\mathsf{v}}}
. Then the number
\xi=\sum_{i\geq 0}{a_{i}}{\beta^{-i}}\in K_{\mathsf{v}}
either lies in
K
or is transcendental.

In this statement, we denote by
|\cdot|_{\mathsf{v}}
the normalized absolute value and by
K_{\mathsf{v}}
the completion of
K
with respect to
\mathsf{v}
. Our notion of convergence refers to
K_{\mathsf{v}}
. We write
{\mathrm{H}}(x)
for the absolute Weil height, and
{\mathrm{h}}(x)
for the absolute logarithmic Weil height (see §
3.1
for definitions).

The proof of Theorem
A
follows from the Subspace Theorem by taking into account the mismatches as new variables. This allows us to obtain linear relations between
\xi
, its rational approximations, and the mismatches. An
ad hoc
argument then yields that the corresponding coefficients of the mismatches must be zero, and the desired conclusion follows. The proof in fact shows that if
\xi\in K
, then
\xi
must be of a
very restricted
form.

Our second goal is to develop a quantitative version of our approach in order to obtain transcendence measures.
Adamczewski and Bugeaud observed that whenever the Subspace Theorem can be used to prove the transcendence of a number, it is in principle possible to derive a transcendence measure by means of the Quantitative Subspace Theorem due to Evertse
[
24
]
.
In particular, one may hope to show that such numbers are either
S
- or
T
-numbers, in the sense of Mahler’s classification, provided that the underlying approximations are
sufficiently dense
.
In the setting of the Diophantine exponent, this typically requires both that the sequence
(t_{n+1}/t_{n})
remains bounded and that the Diophantine exponent is finite.
This approach has been systematically developed in the seminal works

[
4
,
5
,
6
]
;
see also
[
30
,
18
,
19
]
for
p
-adic analogues. We implement this strategy in our setting, which leads to Theorem
B
. We need the following assumption.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=a_{0}a_{2}\cdots
be an infinite word, and

\rho\geq 1
be a real number. We say that
\mathbf{a}
satisfies Condition
(**)_{\rho}
if for every
\epsilon>0
, there exist sequences of integers
(r_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(s_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(t_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and an integer
\delta\geq 0
satisfying the conditions (i), (ii’), (iii), (iv) of
(*)_{\rho}
and

{r_{n}}\ll{s_{n}-r_{n}},\limsup\dfrac{t_{n}}{s_{n}}<\infty,\limsup\dfrac{t_{n+1}}{t_{n}}<\infty

where the implied constants do not depend on
n
.

With this condition, our second result is stated as follows.

Theorem B
.

Let
K
be a number field, and
\mathsf{v}
be a place on
K
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be a finite set of places containing all archimedean places. Let
\beta\in K
such that
|\beta|_{\mathsf{v}}>1
. Let
\mathbf{a}=a_{0}a_{1}\cdots
be an infinite word over
\mathcal{O}_{K,\mathcal{S}}
such that
{\mathrm{h}}(a_{i})=o(i)
and
\mathbf{a}
satisfies
(**)_{\rho}
for some
\rho>\dfrac{\log{\mathrm{H}}(\beta)}{\log|\beta|_{\mathsf{v}}}
. Then there exist constants
c,H>0
independent of
d
such that for every
d\geq 1
, the following holds: For all algebraic numbers
\alpha\not\in K
of degree at most
d
and of height
{\mathrm{H}}(\alpha)
at least
H
, we have

|\xi-\alpha|_{\mathsf{v}}\geq{\mathrm{H}}(\alpha)^{(2d)^{c(\log 4d)(\log\log 4d)}}.

Theorem
B
is proved by quantifying the proof of Theorem

, namely by replacing
\xi
with
\alpha
and applying the Quantitative Subspace Theorem.
The condition
\alpha\not\in K
cannot be ignored due to the existence of
exceptionally good approximations
of

by elements in
K
.

Theorem
B
yields a new trichotomy with respect to Mahler’s classification. Let us first recall this classification for complex and
p
-adic numbers, following
[
22
]
. In the following, we denote by
{\mathrm{H}}_{\mathrm{naive}}(P)
the naive height of the polynomial
P(X)\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
, that is, the maximum of the absolute values of its coefficients. For
x\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, we denote by
{\mathrm{H}}_{\mathrm{naive}}(x)
the naive height of
x
, which is the naive height of its minimal polynomial over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
(resp.
\xi\in K_{\mathsf{v}}
for a non-archimedean place
\mathsf{v}
on some number field

). For every positive integer

\omega_{d}(\xi)

of degree at most

. In addition, we set

\omega(\xi)=\limsup_{d\to\infty}\dfrac{\omega_{d}(\xi)}{d}.

It is known that
\omega_{d}(\xi)\in[0,\infty]
for all

and
\omega(\xi)\in[0,\infty]
. We say that
\xi
is a

•

(

;

•

(
p
-adic)

-number
if

;

•

(
p
-adic)

-number
if
\omega(\xi)=\infty

\omega_{d}(\xi)<\infty
for all positive integers

;

•

(
p
-adic)
U
-number
if
\omega(\xi)=\infty
and
\omega_{d}(\xi)=\infty
for some positive integer
d
.

The class of

-numbers can be divided into smaller classes as follows. Let

be a positive integer. We call
\xi
a

•

(
p
-adic)

The class of

[

For its

Using Mahler’s classification, Theorem

Under the assumptions of Theorem

At this level of generality, however, it remains difficult to obtain explicit transcendence measures, since it is notoriously hard to exclude the possibility that the number

Corvaja and Zannier
[

proved that lacunary numbers (see Definition

p
-adic)

Theorem C
.

Let
K
be a number field and
\mathsf{v}
be a place on
K
. Let
(u_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be an increasing sequence of non-negative integers such that
\liminf\dfrac{u_{i+1}}{u_{i}}>1.
Let
\beta\in K
such that
|\beta|_{\mathsf{v}}>1
, and non-zero elements
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots\in K
such that
{\mathrm{h}}(a_{i})=o(u_{i})
. We set

\xi=\sum_{i\geq 0}{a_{i}}{\beta^{-u_{i}}}\in K_{\mathsf{v}}.
The following dichotomy holds:

(i)

Either
\limsup\dfrac{u_{i+1}}{u_{i}}
is infinite and
\xi
is a (
p
-adic)
U_{d}
-number for some
d\leq[K:\mathbb{Q}]
;

(ii)

Or
\limsup\dfrac{u_{i+1}}{u_{i}}
is finite and there exists
c>0
independent of

such that
\omega_{d}(\xi)\leq(2d)^{c(\log\log 4d)}
for all

. In this case,
\xi
is a (
p
-adic)
S
- or
T
-number.

The main difficulty in Theorem

lies in excluding

Theorem

[

We conclude the article by establishing transcendence measures for numbers generated by
k
-bonacci words and Sturmian words. Recall that a Pisot (resp. Salem) number is a real algebraic integer greater than 1, whose Galois conjugates lie
inside the open unit disk (resp. inside the closed unit disk, with at least one of them
on the unit circle). Particularly, every integer larger than 1 is a Pisot number.

Theorem D
.

Let
\beta
be a Pisot number of degree at most 2. Then in any of the following cases of
\mathbf{a}
:

(i)

\mathbf{a}
is a
k
-bonacci word over
\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
with
k-1\leq\beta
;

(ii)

\mathbf{a}
is a Sturmian word over
\{0,1\}

the real number
\xi=\sum_{i\geq 0}a_{i}\beta^{-i}
is an
S
- or
T
-number.

The proof of Theorem

follows from the ideas of Baker
[

]
and Adamczewski and Cassaigne

]
in constructing

is neither a

. However, it remains unclear how to show that

-number for

Remark 1.5
.

We observe that if
\mathbf{a}
is a Sturmian word whose slope has unbounded partial quotients, then its Diophantine exponent is infinite by

. In this case, Proposition

implies that

Finally, we recall that there exists another classical general method to study the Diophantine properties of values of analytic functions at algebraic points.
It is based on the construction of Padé approximants and requires that the functions under consideration satisfy suitable
functional equations
.
This approach was first introduced in the context of transcendence in the celebrated memoir of Hermite
[
27
]
, where he established the transcendence of
e
. It has since been developed in various settings, notably for Siegel
E
-functions and Mahler
M
-functions (see, for instance,
[
8
]
).
One major advantage of this method is that it allows one to address questions of linear and algebraic independence, and typically yields stronger transcendence measures, as well as algebraic independence measures (see, for instance,
[
9
,
10
]
).
However, this comes at the cost of requiring the presence of functional equations, which is a very restrictive condition: such functions form at most a countable family and are not robust under perturbations. By contrast, the approach developed in the present article leads to weaker results, but applies to uncountable families of functions and remains effective in the presence of sufficiently moderate noise.

Remark 1.6
.

For some families of
\mathbf{a}
considered above, the corresponding generating series are related to Mahler functions. More precisely, when
\mathbf{a}
is a Sturmian word,
f
satisfies a chain of linear Mahler equations
[
21
]
. When
\mathbf{a}
is a lacunary sequence given by
u_{i}=d^{i}+r
where
d\geq 2
,
f
satisfies a linear Mahler equation
[
35
]
. In such cases, Mahler’s method
[
9
,
10
]
could be applied. Moreover, Mahler functions are known to have the unit circle as a natural boundary (hence transcendental) once they are not rational. The same property holds in our case: for any infinite word
\mathbf{a}
satisfying asymptotic conditions as in Theorem
A
, the generating series is either rational or has the unit circle as a natural boundary, as follows from a generalization of the Pólya-Carlson dichotomy
[
14
, Theorem 1.2]
. Furthermore, the generating series of an infinite word
\mathbf{a}
with
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})>1
is algebraic if and only if
\mathbf{a}
is eventually periodic. Similarly, the generating series of a lacunary sequence always has the unit circle as a natural boundary.

2.
Refined Diophantine exponent

In this section, we study some basic combinatorial properties of the refined Diophantine exponent. We then compute it for several recently introduced words.

2.1.
Refined Diophantine exponent

Recall that
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})\geq{\mathbf{Dio}}(\mathbf{a})
. In particular, if
{\mathbf{Dio}}(\mathbf{a})=\infty
, then
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})=\infty
. In contrast, there exist examples of infinite words—namely, lacunary sequences—whose Diophantine exponent is as small as possible, while their refined Diophantine exponents are infinite (see Proposition
2.9
below). However, we still have the following.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{a}
be an infinite word. Then
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})=1
if and only if
{\mathbf{Dio}}(\mathbf{a})=1
.

To prove this proposition, we introduce some notation. For two finite subsets
I
and
J
of
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, we write
I<J
if
\max\{i:i\in I\}<\min\{j:j\in J\}
and in this case, we denote
d(I,J)=\min\{j:j\in J\}-\max\{i:i\in I\}
for their distance. For a finite set, we use
|\cdot|
to denote its cardinality. We need the following refinement of the condition
(*)_{\rho}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=a_{0}a_{2}\cdots
be an infinite word. Let

\rho\geq 1
and
\epsilon>0
. We say that
\mathbf{a}
satisfies Condition
(*)_{\rho,\epsilon}
if there exist
(r_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(s_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(t_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
\delta
satisfying conditions (i), (ii’), (iii), and (iv) in Definition
1.2
.

The condition
(*)_{\rho,\epsilon}
ensures that for all sufficiently large
n
, the set of
mismatches

\{1\leq i\leq t_{n}-s_{n}:a_{i+r_{n}}\neq a_{i+s_{n}}\}

is contained in
\delta
intervals of total length at most
\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})
. These intervals, denoted by
I_{n,j}=[l_{n,j},r_{n,j}]
for
j=1,\ldots,\delta
, are ordered such that
I_{n,1}<\ldots<I_{n,\delta}
. Here,
l_{n,j}
(resp.,
r_{n,j}
) denotes the left (resp., right) endpoint of the interval
I_{n,j}
.

Remark 2.3
.

If
\mathbf{a}
is an infinite word and
\rho\geq 1
, then
\mathbf{a}
satisfies
(*)_{\rho}
if and only if
\mathbf{a}
satisfies
(*)_{\rho,\epsilon}
for all
\epsilon>0
. Further, if
\mathbf{a}
satisfies
(*)_{\rho,\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon>0
, then
\mathbf{a}
satisfies
(*)_{\rho^{\prime},\epsilon^{\prime}}
for all
\rho\geq\rho^{\prime}\geq 1
and all
\epsilon^{\prime}\geq\epsilon
.

The following lemma shows that, using a suitable modification of the intervals
I_{n,j}
, we can always assume that there is a large gap between any two consecutive intervals
I_{n,j}
and
I_{n,j+1}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\rho>1
be a real number. In the definition of
(*)_{\rho}
, we can modify the boxes
I_{n,j}
so that for all
n
large enough, the distances between two consecutive intervals are at least
\dfrac{\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})}{2+\delta}
.

Proof.

For any
\epsilon>0
, we set
\epsilon^{\prime}=\dfrac{\epsilon}{2+\delta}>0
and let
(r_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(s_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(t_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
\delta
be a data associated with
\epsilon^{\prime}
in the definition of
(*)_{\rho,\epsilon^{\prime}}
. Let
I_{n,1}^{\prime},\ldots,I_{n,\delta}^{\prime}
be the corresponding intervals. If
\delta=0
, we are done. If
\delta\geq 1
, we do the following modifications:

(1)

If
I_{n,j}^{\prime}
and
I_{n,j+1}^{\prime}
have distance
<{\epsilon^{\prime}}(t_{n}-s_{n})
for some
j
, then we merge them into a new box
I_{n,j}=[l_{n,j},r_{n,j+1}]
;

(2)

If
I_{n,j}^{\prime}
and
I_{n,j+1}^{\prime}
have distance
\geq{\epsilon^{\prime}}(t_{n}-s_{n})
for some
j
, then we do nothing.

For each
n
sufficiently large, we observe that after completing all the possible modifications, we obtain at most
\delta
boxes whose total length is
\leq(\delta-1)\epsilon^{\prime}{}(t_{n}-s_{n})+\epsilon^{\prime}(t_{n}-s_{n})\leq\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})
. Therefore, the new boxes satisfy our desired conditions.
∎

We can also obtain a large gap between
r_{n}
and the first mismatch as follows. Recall that
I_{n,1}=[l_{n,1},r_{n,1}]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\rho>1
be a real number. Let
\mathbf{a}
be an infinite word satisfying the condition
(*)_{\rho}
. Let
\epsilon>0
be sufficiently small, namely
\epsilon<\min\left\{\dfrac{1}{4},\dfrac{1}{2(\rho-1)}\right\}
. Then there exist

\epsilon^{\prime}\in(\epsilon,2\epsilon)\text{ and }\rho^{\prime}\in\left(\dfrac{\rho}{1+2\epsilon(\rho-1)},\rho\right)

such that
\mathbf{a}
satisfies the condition
(*)_{\rho^{\prime},\epsilon^{\prime}}
with a data
(r_{n}^{\prime})_{n\geq 0}
,
(s_{n}^{\prime})_{n\geq 0}
,
(t_{n}^{\prime})_{n\geq 0}
,
\delta^{\prime}
satisfying the following gap conditions with respect to
\rho^{\prime},\epsilon^{\prime}
and
\delta^{\prime}
: when
n
is large enough, the distances between two consecutive intervals are at least
\dfrac{\epsilon^{\prime}(t_{n}^{\prime}-s_{n}^{\prime})}{2(2+\delta^{\prime})}
, and the distance between
r_{n}^{\prime}
and the first mismatch is at least
\dfrac{\epsilon^{\prime}(t_{n}^{\prime}-s_{n}^{\prime})}{2(2+\delta^{\prime})}
, i.e,
l_{n,1}^{\prime}\geq\dfrac{\epsilon^{\prime}(t_{n}^{\prime}-s_{n}^{\prime})}{2(2+\delta^{\prime})}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4
, we can find a data for
(*)_{\rho,\epsilon}
such that for all
n
large enough, the distances between two consecutive intervals are at least
\dfrac{\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})}{2+\delta}
. In particular, we have
d(I_{n,1},I_{n,2})\geq\dfrac{\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})}{2+\delta}
. Since the total length of
\delta
intervals is at most
\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})
, we have
r_{n,1}-l_{n,1}\leq\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})
. If
l_{n,1}\geq\dfrac{\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})}{2+\delta}
, then we are done. Now, if
l_{n,1}<\dfrac{\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})}{2+\delta}
, then we do the following modification. We set
r_{n}^{\prime}=r_{n}+r_{n,1}
,
s_{n}^{\prime}=s_{n}+r_{n,1}
and
t_{n}^{\prime}=t_{n}
. Then, since
\epsilon<\dfrac{1}{4}
, we have

t_{n}-s_{n}>t_{n}^{\prime}-s_{n}^{\prime}=t_{n}-s_{n}-r_{n,1}>(t_{n}-s_{n})\Big(1-\epsilon-\dfrac{\epsilon}{2+\delta}\Big)>0

since
r_{n,1}<(t_{n}-s_{n})\Big(\epsilon+\dfrac{\epsilon}{2+\delta}\Big)
.
Since all the mismatches in the first interval now belong to
\mathbf{a}[r_{n}^{\prime}]
, we deduce that the total length of the remaining
\delta-1
intervals is at most
\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})\leq\epsilon^{\prime}(t_{n}-s_{n})
where
\epsilon^{\prime}=\dfrac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon-\frac{\epsilon}{2+\delta}}\in(\epsilon,2\epsilon]
since
\epsilon<\dfrac{1}{4}
. Thus the distances between two new consecutive intervals are
\geq\dfrac{\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})}{2+\delta}>\dfrac{\epsilon^{\prime}(t_{n}^{\prime}-s_{n}^{\prime})}{2(2+\delta)}
. In addition, the distance between
r_{n}^{\prime}
and the first new mismatch (between
\mathbf{a}[r_{n}^{\prime}+1,r_{n}^{\prime}+t_{n}^{\prime}-s_{n}^{\prime}]
and
\mathbf{a}[s_{n}^{\prime}+1,t_{n}^{\prime}]
) is
\geq\dfrac{\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})}{2+\delta}>\dfrac{\epsilon^{\prime}(t_{n}^{\prime}-s_{n}^{\prime})}{2(2+\delta)}
.
Moreover,
s_{n}^{\prime}-r_{n}^{\prime}=s_{n}-r_{n}
still tends to
\infty
. Further, there is
\rho^{\prime}<\rho
such that
\rho^{\prime}\dfrac{1-\epsilon-\frac{\epsilon}{2+\delta}}{1-\rho^{\prime}\epsilon-\rho^{\prime}\frac{\epsilon}{2+\delta}}=\rho
. Since
t_{n}\geq\rho s_{n}
, we have
t_{n}\Big(1-\rho^{\prime}\Big(\epsilon+\dfrac{\epsilon}{2+\delta}\Big)\Big)\geq\rho^{\prime}s_{n}\Big(1-\Big(\epsilon+\dfrac{\epsilon}{2+\delta}\Big)\Big),
i.e.,

t_{n}\geq\rho^{\prime}s_{n}+\rho^{\prime}(t_{n}-s_{n})\Big(\epsilon+\dfrac{\epsilon}{2+\delta}\Big),

which yields that
t_{n}^{\prime}\geq\rho^{\prime}s_{n}+\rho^{\prime}r_{n,1}=\rho^{\prime}s_{n}^{\prime}
, hence
t_{n}^{\prime}\geq\max\{1,\rho^{\prime}\}s_{n}^{\prime}
. We note also that since
\epsilon<\dfrac{1}{2(\rho-1)}
, we have
\rho^{\prime}>\dfrac{\rho}{1+2\epsilon(\rho-1)}
. Therefore, our new data satisfies the desired conditions.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

When
\epsilon
tends to
0
,
\rho^{\prime}
tends to
\rho
.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

We only need to show that if
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})>1
, then
{\mathbf{Dio}}(\mathbf{a})>1
. Let
\rho
be a real number such that
1<\rho<\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})
and let
\epsilon>0
be sufficiently small. In virtue of Lemma
2.5
, we can find a data
(r_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(s_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(t_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
\delta
for
(*)_{\rho,\epsilon}
such that the distance between
{r_{n}}
and the first mismatch
{r_{n}+l_{n,1}}
is at least
\dfrac{\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})}{2(2+\delta)}
. We set
U_{n}=a[0,r_{n}]
and
V_{n}=a[r_{n}+1,s_{n}]
, then
\mathbf{a}
coincides with the eventually periodic sequence
U_{n}V_{n}^{\infty}
up to the index
s_{n}+l_{n,1}
with

\dfrac{s_{n}+l_{n,1}}{s_{n}}\geq 1+\dfrac{\epsilon(\rho-1)}{2(2+\delta)}.

Therefore
{\mathbf{Dio}}(\mathbf{a})>1
.
∎

Using Theorem
A
, we see that there are many examples of infinite words over a finite alphabet whose refined Diophantine exponent is equal to 1.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
\beta
is a Pisot or Salem number. Let
x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}(\beta)
be an algebraic number. Let
0.a_{0}a_{1}\ldots
be its
\beta
-expansion. Then
\mathbf{Dio}(a_{0}a_{1}\cdots)=\mathbf{Rdio}(a_{0}a_{1}\cdots)=1
.

2.2.
Connections to other families of words

We show that for several families of infinite words, ranging from classical examples to more recently introduced ones, their refined Diophantine exponents are infinite.

2.2.1.
Lacunary sequences

We begin with lacunary sequences, which are known to be a quintessential case study in transcendental number theory, dating back to the work of Liouville
[
32
]
.

Definition 2.8
.

An infinite word
\mathbf{a}
over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is called a
lacunary
sequence if there exist
b\in\mathcal{A}
and a sequence of integers
0\leq u_{0}<u_{1}<\ldots
such that
\liminf\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_{n}}>1
and
\mathbf{a}
is of the form
a_{i}\neq b
when
i=u_{n}
for some
n
, and
a_{i}=b
otherwise. So we have

\mathbf{a}=b\cdots ba_{u_{0}}b\cdots ba_{u_{1}}b\cdots ba_{u_{j}}b\cdots

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\mathbf{a}
be a lacunary sequence. Then
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})=\infty
.

Proof.

We fix any
\rho\geq 1
. Let
\epsilon>0
, then there is some
n_{0}>0
such that
u_{n+n_{0}}\geq\rho u_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
. We set
r_{n}=u_{n}
,
s_{n}=u_{n+1}
,
t_{n}=u_{n+n_{0}}
. So we have
\mathbf{a}={b\cdots ba_{r_{n}}}b\cdots ba_{s_{n}}b\cdots ba_{t_{n}}b\cdots

Then the number of mismatches between

\mathbf{a}[r_{n}+1,r_{n}+t_{n}-s_{n}]=a_{u_{n}+1}\cdots a_{u_{n+1}}\cdots a_{u_{n}+u_{n+n_{0}}-u_{n+1}}

and

\mathbf{a}[s_{n}+1,t_{n}]=a_{u_{n+1}+1}\cdots a_{u_{n+n_{0}}}

are at most
\delta=2(n_{0}-1)
, and
t_{n}/s_{n}\geq\rho
. Therefore,
\mathbf{a}
satisfies the condition
(*)_{\rho}
for all
\rho
as desired.
∎

In fact, the proof of Proposition
2.9
yields that when
\limsup\dfrac{u_{i+1}}{u_{i}}
is finite, the lacunary sequence
\mathbf{a}
satisfies the condition
(**)_{\rho}
for all
\rho\geq 1
, so we can apply Theorem
B
to obtain a trichotomy for transcendence measures of lacunary numbers. Nevertheless, we even have a dichotomy (see Theorem
C
) based on the Diophantine exponent: for a lacunary sequence
\mathbf{a}
, we have
{\mathbf{Dio}}(\mathbf{a})=\limsup\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_{n}}
, see
[
6
, Théorème 6.1]
. In particular,
{\mathbf{Dio}}(\mathbf{a})
is finite if and only if
\limsup\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_{n}}
is finite.

Remark 2.10
.

In view of lacunary sequences, one can similarly construct other examples of words with infinite refined Diophantine exponents. For instance, for any integers
0\leq u_{0}<u_{1}<\ldots
such that
\liminf\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_{n}}>1
, we set
\mathbf{a}=0^{u_{1}}1^{u_{1}}0^{u_{2}}1^{u_{2}}\cdots\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
where
0^{u_{i}}
(resp.
1^{u_{i}}
) denotes the finite word defined by
u_{i}
occurrences of 0 (resp.
1
). One can verify that
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})=\infty
.

2.2.2.
Stuttering words

Next, we compute refined Diophantine exponents of stuttering words introduced in
[
34
]
. The motivation behind these words is the transcendence and linear independence of numbers arising from Sturmian words over any algebraic base. Over integer bases, their transcendence (resp. linear independence) was first proved by Ferenczi–Mauduit
[
25
]
(resp.
[
20
]
). However, going to any algebraic bases requires a more detailed analysis of the combinatorial nature of Sturmian words.

Definition 2.11
.

(
[
34
, page 3]
)
An infinite word over a finite alphabet set
\mathbf{a}
is called a
stuttering
word if for all
\rho>0
, there exist sequences of positive integers
(u_{n})_{n\geq 0},(v_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and an integer
d\geq 2
such that

(i)

u_{n}
is unbounded and
v_{n}\geq\rho u_{n}
for all
n
;

(ii)

for every
n
, there exist integers
i_{0}(n)=0<i_{1}(n)<\ldots<i_{d}(n)\leq i_{d+1}(n)=v_{n}
such that the words
a_{0}\cdots a_{v_{n}}
and
a_{u_{n}}\cdots a_{u_{n}+v_{n}}
differ at positions
\bigcup_{j=1}^{d}\{i_{j}(n),i_{j}(n)+1\}
;

(iii)

we have

i_{d}(n)-i_{1}(n)=\omega(\log u_{n})

and
i_{j+1}(n)-i_{j}(n)=\omega(1)
for all
j\in\{0,1,\dots,d\}
;

(iv)

we have
u_{i_{j}(n)}+u_{i_{j}(n)+1}=u_{i_{j}(n)+u_{n}}+u_{i_{j}(n)+u_{n}+1}
for all
n
and all
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

Examples of stuttering words include
Sturmian words
, which can be defined as follows. The
complexity function
of an infinite word
\mathbf{a}=a_{0}a_{1}\cdots
is the function
n\mapsto p(n,\mathbf{a})
defined by

p(n,\mathbf{a})=|\{a_{i}\cdots a_{i+n-1}:i\geq 0\}|.

An infinite word
\mathbf{a}
is then called Sturmian if
p(n,\mathbf{a})=n+1
for all
n
. A classic example of a Sturmian word is the Fibonacci word.

The proof of the following proposition will also be used in Theorem
D
to verify that Sturmian words satisfy condition
(**)_{\rho}
for all
\rho\geq 1
.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
\mathbf{a}
be a stuttering word, then
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})=\infty
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\geq 1
and
\epsilon>0
. With the data given by Definition
2.11
, we set
r_{n}=0,s_{n}=u_{n},t_{n}=u_{n}+v_{n}
for all
n
, and set
\delta=2d
. Since the number of mismatches between
a[r_{n}+1,r_{n}+t_{n}-s_{n}]=a_{1}\cdots a_{v_{n}}
and
a[s_{n}+1,t_{n}]=a_{u_{n}+1}\cdots a_{u_{n}+v_{n}}
is at most
\delta
, which is
\leq\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})
for
n
sufficiently large. Therefore
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})\geq\rho
for all
\rho\geq 1
, hence
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})=\infty
.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

Combining with Theorem
A
, we recover the dichotomy part of
[
34
, Theorem 5]
.

2.2.3.
Echoing words

We now study the refined Diophantine exponent of echoing words. The concept of echoing words was recently introduced in
[
29
]
to analyze certain Arnoux-Rauzy words, namely
k
-bonacci words.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=a_{0}a_{1}\cdots
be an infinite word over a finite alphabet. Then
\mathbf{a}
is said to be
echoing
if for every
\epsilon>0
,
there exist integers
0\leq r_{n}<s_{n}
and non-empty intervals
\{0\}=I_{n,0}<I_{n,1}<I_{n,2}<\cdots

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(i)

\{i\geq 1:a_{i+s_{n}}\neq a_{i+r_{n}}\}\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}I_{n,j}
;

(ii)

\mathrm{den}\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{\delta}I_{n,j}\right)\leq\epsilon

for all sufficiently large
\delta
and
n
;

(i)

s_{n}<s_{n+1}
for all
n
;

(i)

we have
s_{n}-r_{n}\gg s_{n}
,
and
d(I_{n,j},I_{n,j+1})\gg s_{n},

where the implied constants are independent of
n,j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Here, for a finite subset
I\neq\{0\}
of
\mathbb{N}
, we define its
density
as

\mathrm{den}(I)=\dfrac{|I|}{\max\{i:i\in I\}}.

Typical examples of echoing words are the
k
-bonacci words

[
29
, Theorem 12]
, which can be defined as follows. For
k\geq 2
, the
k
-bonacci word over the finite alphabet
\Sigma=\{b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{k-1}\}
is the infinite word given by the limit
\lim\varphi^{n}(0)
, where
\varphi
is the
k
-bonacci self-morphism on the free semigroup generated by
\Sigma
, defined by

\varphi(b_{0})=b_{0}b_{1},\varphi(b_{1})=b_{0}b_{2},\ldots,\varphi(b_{k-2})=b_{0}b_{k-1},\varphi(b_{k-1})=b_{0}.

The proof of the following result will also be used in establishing Theorem
D
to check that
k
-bonacci words satisfy condition
(**)_{\rho}
for all
\rho\geq 1
.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
\mathbf{a}
be an echoing word over a finite alphabet, then
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})=\infty
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\geq 1
and
0<\epsilon<1
. We use the data of Definition
2.14
with respect to
\epsilon
.
It follows that there exists
\delta\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n
, the right endpoint of
I_{n,\delta}
, say
t_{n}
, is
\geq\rho s_{n}
. We note that
\mathbf{a}[r_{n}+1,t_{n}-s_{n}]=a_{r_{n}+1}\cdots a_{r_{n}+t_{n}-s_{n}}
and
\mathbf{a}[s_{n}+1,t_{n}-s_{n}]=a_{s_{n}+1}\cdots a_{t_{n}}
. Thus the number of mismatches between
\mathbf{a}[r_{n}+1,t_{n}-s_{n}]
and
\mathbf{a}[s_{n}+1,t_{n}-s_{n}]
is equal to the cardinality of the set

\{1\leq i\leq t_{n}-s_{n}:a_{i+s_{n}}\neq a_{i+r_{n}}\},

which is
\leq\epsilon t_{n}-s_{n}\leq\epsilon(t_{n}-s_{n})
. Thus
\mathbf{a}[r_{n}+1,t_{n}-s_{n}]
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-closed to
\mathbf{a}[s_{n}+1,t_{n}-s_{n}]
. Therefore
\mathbf{Rdio}({\mathbf{a}})\geq\rho
for all
\rho\geq 1
as wanted.
∎

Remark 2.16
.

Combining with Theorem
A
, we recover
[
29
, Theorem 9]
. In addition, Proposition
2.15
also applies to
strongly echoing
words introduced in
[
29
, Definition 10]
.

2.2.4.
Algebraic dynamics

Next, we focus on words arising from algebraic dynamics–more precisely, words coming from coding of rotations by rational intervals. We will show that the refined Diophantine exponents of these words are always infinite. Before stating the result, let us provide some motivation from algebraic dynamics.

Algebraic dynamics is the study of self-maps of algebraic varieties. One of the most important birational invariants of such dynamical systems is the
dynamical degree
, an algebraic analogue of entropy in topological dynamics. In general, dynamical degrees are very difficult to compute. The only accessible cases include monomial maps, algebraically stable maps, and certain polynomial endomorphisms. In all these cases, the dynamical degrees are algebraic integers, and it had been conjectured that all dynamical degrees are algebraic numbers. It was therefore a major surprise when Bell, Diller, and Jonsson
[
13
]
constructed the first example of a rational map whose dynamical degree is transcendental. Their construction relies on toric geometry, and their transcendence proof relies on Diophantine approximation, motivated by the works
[
23
,
1
,
2
,
3
]
. For the geometric construction, we refer the reader to
[
13
, §2]
, which can be summarized as follows.

Let
\zeta\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathrm{i}]
be a Gaussian integer whose argument is incommensurable with
2\pi\mathbb{Q}
. Let
\theta=\dfrac{\arg(\zeta)}{2\pi}\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}
.
We set
\Gamma=\{0,-2,\pm 2\mathrm{i},1\pm 2\mathrm{i}\}
. Let
\mathbf{\gamma}=\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\cdots
be the word defined by

\gamma_{i}=\begin{cases}1-2\mathrm{i}&when $\{i\theta\}\in\Bigg(0,\dfrac{2}{8}\Bigg)$,\\
-2\mathrm{i}&when $\{i\theta\}\in\Bigg(\dfrac{2}{8},\dfrac{3}{8}\Bigg)$,\\
-2&when $\{i\theta\}\in\Bigg(\dfrac{3}{8},\dfrac{5}{8}\Bigg)$,\\
2\mathrm{i}&when $\{i\theta\}\in\Bigg(\dfrac{5}{8},\dfrac{6}{8}\Bigg)$,\\
1+2\mathrm{i}&when $\{i\theta\}\in\Bigg(\dfrac{6}{8},1\Bigg)$.\\
\end{cases}

Here,
\{\cdot\}
denotes the fraction part. We set
\Phi(z)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\gamma_{i}z^{i}
. Using geometric considerations, the authors of
[
13
]
show that one can associate
with such a
\zeta
a dominant rational map
f:\mathbb{P}^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
whose dynamical degree
{\lambda(f)}
is a solution of the following equation

{\operatorname{Re}\,}(\Phi({\lambda(f)}^{-1}{\zeta}))=1.

The transcendence of
\lambda(f)
is then obtained by proving that
{\operatorname{Re}\,}(\Phi(\alpha))
is transcendental for all algebraic numbers
\alpha
,
0<|\alpha|<1
. To this end, the authors measure the periodicity of the word
\gamma
. This allowed them to construct good rational approximations for
\Phi(z)
, and consequently for
{\operatorname{Re}\,}(\Phi(z))
, to which they applied tools from Diophantine approximation to deduce the final result. It turns out that such periodicity also implies that
\mathbf{Rdio}(\gamma)=\infty
, as we will prove next.

In fact, we will consider a more general class of words called
coding of rotations by rational
intervals
, which are defined as follows. Let
\theta\in(0,1)
be irrational. Let
s\geq 2
be an integer, and let
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s}\}
be a finite alphabet with at least two distinct letters (
\alpha_{i}
might equal
\alpha_{j}
for
i\neq j
). Then the word
\mathbf{a}=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
obtained by coding of a rotation
\theta
by rational intervals
\Bigg(\dfrac{j-1}{s},\dfrac{j}{s}\Bigg)
for
1\leq j\leq s
is defined by
a_{i}=\alpha_{j}
if
\{i\theta\}\in\Bigg(\dfrac{j-1}{s},\dfrac{j}{s}\Bigg)
.

Proposition 2.17
.

For any word
\mathbf{a}
given by coding of rotations by rational intervals, we have
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})=\infty
.

We will rely on the following well-known fact in the theory of continued fractions.

Lemma 2.18
.

(see e.g.
[
13
, Proposition 3.3]
)
Let
\theta
be an irrational number with continued fraction approximants
\dfrac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}
. The following assertions are equivalent.

(i)

There exists
\kappa>0
such that
|q_{n}\theta-p_{n}|\geq\dfrac{\kappa}{q_{n}}
for all
n
.

(ii)

There exists
\kappa>0
such that
|q\theta-p|\geq\dfrac{\kappa}{q}
for all
p,q
with
q>0
.

(iii)

There exists
A
such that
q_{n+1}\leq Aq_{n}
for all
n
.

(iv)

\theta
has bounded partial quotients.

Here, the
partial quotients
of
\theta
are the integers
w_{0},w_{1},\ldots
determined via the continued fraction
\theta=[w_{0}:w_{1}:w_{2}:\ldots]
. The
continued fraction approximants
of
\theta
are the sequences
\dfrac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}=[w_{0}:\ldots:w_{n}]
where
q_{n}
are strictly increasing and
p_{n}

is coprime to
q_{n}
.

Now, to prove Proposition
2.17
, we let
\theta
be the rotation defining
\mathbf{a}
and consider two cases of
\theta
depending on the boundedness of the partial quotients of
\theta
.

Lemma 2.19
.

Assume that
\theta
has unbounded partial quotients. Then for every
\rho>1
, there are infinitely many
q
such that
a_{i}=a_{i-q}
for all
i\in(q,\rho q]
. In particular,
{\mathbf{Dio}}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})=\infty
.

Proof.

We adapt the proof of
[
13
, Proposition 3.9]
. We take
\kappa=\dfrac{1}{2s(\rho+1)}
. By Lemma
2.18
, for
p=p_{n}
and
q=q_{n}
with
n
sufficiently large, we have
|q\theta-p|<\dfrac{\kappa}{q}
and
p
is coprime to
q
. We consider any
i\in(q,\rho q]
and assume that
a_{i}=\alpha_{j}
, i.e.,
\{\mathrm{i}\theta\}\in\Bigg(\dfrac{j-1}{s},\dfrac{j}{s}\Bigg)
or
\Bigg(\dfrac{j}{s},\dfrac{j+1}{s}\Bigg)
where
j
is the integer closest to
si\theta
. We have two cases.

(1)

If
\Bigg|{i}\theta-\dfrac{j}{s}\Bigg|\geq\dfrac{\kappa}{q}
, then either both
\{i\theta\}
and
\{(i-q)\theta\}
lie in
\Bigg(\dfrac{j-1}{s},\dfrac{j}{s}\Bigg)
or both lie in
\Bigg(\dfrac{j}{s},\dfrac{j+1}{s}\Bigg)
.

(2)

If
\Bigg|{i}\theta-\dfrac{j}{s}\Bigg|<\dfrac{\kappa}{q}
, then

|sip-jq|=s\Bigg|i(p-q\theta)+q\Bigg(i\theta-\dfrac{j}{s}\Bigg)\Bigg|<s\Bigg(\dfrac{i\kappa}{q}+\kappa\Bigg)\leq s\kappa(i+1)\leq\dfrac{1}{2}.
Thus
sip=jq
, whence there exists an integer
j^{\prime}
such that
i=\dfrac{j^{\prime}q}{s}
, hence
j=j^{\prime}p
. Therefore
j^{\prime}\in(s,\rho s]
. Further,

i\theta-\dfrac{j}{s}=\dfrac{j^{\prime}}{s}(q\theta-p),\text{ and }(i-q)\theta-\dfrac{j-sp}{s}=\dfrac{j^{\prime}-s}{s}(q\theta-p).

Since the absolute values of the right sides of these equalities are bounded above by
\dfrac{1}{2s}
, we conclude either both
\{i\theta\}
and
\{(i-q)\theta\}
lie in
\Bigg(\dfrac{j-1}{s},\dfrac{j}{s}\Bigg)
or both lie in
\Bigg(\dfrac{j}{s},\dfrac{j+1}{s}\Bigg)
as desired.

In either case, we always have
a_{i}=a_{i-q}
as desired.
The second assertion then follows.
∎

Lemma 2.20
.

Assume that
\theta
has bounded partial quotients. Then, there is a constant
\nu>0
such that for infinitely many
q
, the following holds: for any
\rho>1
, there are at most
\dfrac{\rho}{\nu}
indices
i\in(q,\rho q]
such that
a_{i}\neq a_{i-q}
. In particular,
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})=\infty
.

Proof.

We mimic the proof of
[
13
, Proposition 3.15]
. Since
\theta
has bounded partial quotients, there is
\kappa>0
such that
|q\theta-p|\geq\dfrac{\kappa}{q}
for any
p,q
with
q>0
. Now we take
p=p_{n},q=q_{n}
where
\dfrac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}
are continued fraction approximants of
\theta
, whence
|q\theta-p|<\dfrac{1}{q}
.

We set
\nu=\dfrac{\kappa}{2s^{2}}
. It suffices to show that
|i-i^{\prime}|\geq\nu q
for any
i>i^{\prime}>q
such that
a_{i}\neq a_{i-q}
and
a_{i^{\prime}}\neq a_{i^{\prime}-q}
. Let
j
be the integer closest to
si\theta
. If
\Bigg|i\theta-\dfrac{j}{s}\Bigg|\geq\dfrac{1}{q}
, then either both
\{i\theta\}
and
\{(i-q)\theta\}
lie in
\Bigg(\dfrac{j-1}{s},\dfrac{j}{s}\Bigg)
or both lie in
\Bigg(\dfrac{j}{s},\dfrac{j+1}{s}\Bigg)
, which is absurd. Thus
\Bigg|i\theta-\dfrac{j}{s}\Bigg|<\dfrac{1}{q}
. Similarly, we write
j^{\prime}
for the integer closest to
si^{\prime}\theta
, and we have
\Bigg|i^{\prime}\theta-\dfrac{j^{\prime}}{s}\Bigg|<\dfrac{1}{q}
. It follows that

\dfrac{\kappa}{s|i-i^{\prime}|}\leq|s(i-i^{\prime})\theta-(j-j^{\prime})|\leq s\Bigg|i\theta-\dfrac{j}{s}\Bigg|+s\Bigg|i^{\prime}\theta-\dfrac{j^{\prime}}{s}\Bigg|<\dfrac{2s}{q}.

Hence
|i-i^{\prime}|>\dfrac{\kappa q}{2s^{2}}=\nu q
and the first assertion follows.

For the second assertion, we set
r_{n}=-1,s_{n}=q_{n}-1,t_{n}=\lfloor\rho q_{n}\rfloor-1
and
\delta=\rho/\nu
(noting that there is a shift by 1 since in the definition of the refined Diophantine exponent, we start with
a_{0}
). We deduce that
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})\geq\rho
for all
\rho\geq 1
, hence
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a})=\infty
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.17
.

This follows from Lemmas
2.19
and
2.20
.
∎

Remark 2.22
.

The word
\mathbf{a}
given by coding of rotations by rational
intervals is not eventually periodic since
\{i\theta\}
is dense in
(0,1)
. Further,
\mathbf{a}
has sub-linear complexity. Indeed, for any
n\geq 0
, we consider the fractional parts
\Bigg\{\dfrac{j}{s}-i\theta\Bigg\}\in(0,1)
for

and

. Then these
sn

lies. Assume the contrary that there are

on some interval such that

or

and

2.2.5.
Further examples

Finally, we construct some infinite words whose Diophantine properties cannot be obtained directly by using known results in
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
6
,
34
,
29
]
. We begin with an observation that, given any infinite word, we can construct a new one by modifying it with a suitable lacunary sequence such that the Diophantine exponent of the new word is as small as possible, while its refined Diophantine exponent is at least that of the original word.

We consider the following condition which is stronger than
(*)_{\rho}
.

Definition 2.23
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=a_{0}a_{2}\cdots
be an infinite word, and

\rho\geq 1
a real number. We say that
\mathbf{a}
satisfies Condition
(***)_{\rho}
if for every
\epsilon>0
, there exist sequences of integers
(r_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(s_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(t_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and an integer
\delta\geq 0
satisfying the conditions of
(*)_{\rho}
and
\limsup\dfrac{t_{n}}{r_{n}}<\infty
.

Proposition 2.24
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=a_{0}a_{1}\cdots
be an infinite word over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, and let
b
be a letter not in
\mathcal{A}
. Let
0\leq u_{0}<u_{1}<\ldots
be an increasing sequence of integers such that
\liminf\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_{n}}>1
. Let
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
be the new word defined by
a_{i}^{\prime}=a_{i}
if
i\neq u_{n}
, and
a_{i}^{\prime}=b
if
i=u_{n}
for some
n
.

•

Then
{\mathbf{Dio}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})\leq\limsup\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_{n}}
, and
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
is not eventually periodic.

•

For any
\rho\geq 1
, if
\mathbf{a}
satisfies
(***)_{\rho}
, then
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
also satisfies
(***)_{\rho}
, in particular,
\mathbf{Rdio}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})\geq\rho
.

Proof.

The first assertion is clear from the definition of the Diophantine exponent and from the fact that lacunary sequences are not eventually periodic.

For the second assertion, since
\liminf\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_{n}}>1
, there exists
c_{0}>1
such that
u_{n+m}\geq c_{0}^{m}u_{n}
for all
n,m\geq 0
. The hypotheses yield that
t_{n}\ll r_{n}
, thus there are
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
c_{1}t_{n}\leq r_{n}
and
r_{n}+t_{n}-s_{n}\leq c_{2}t_{n}
. The numbers of occurrences of
b
in
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}[r_{n}+1,r_{n}+t_{n}-s_{n}]
is at most the numbers of
j
such that

(2.1)

c_{1}t_{n}\leq u_{j}\leq c_{2}t_{n}.

Assume that there are exactly
k
indices
m,m+1,\ldots,m+k-1
satisfying (
2.1
), i.e.,

u_{m-1}<c_{1}t_{n}\leq u_{m}<u_{m+1}<\ldots<u_{m+k-1}\leq c_{2}t_{n}<u_{m+k}.

It follows that

c_{0}^{k}\leq\dfrac{u_{m+k-1}}{u_{m-1}}<\dfrac{c_{2}}{c_{1}},

hence
k
is bounded. Thus, the number of occurrences of
b
in
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}[r_{n}+1,r_{n}+t_{n}-s_{n}]
is always bounded by some constant
k
. Similarly, the number of occurrences of
b
in
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}[s_{n}+1,t_{n}]
is also bounded by some constant
k
. Therefore, the number of mismatches between
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}[r_{n}+1,r_{n}+t_{n}-s_{n}]
and
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}[s_{n}+1,t_{n}]
is contained in a union of the set of such
2k
positions of
b
and
\delta
intervals of mismatches between
\mathbf{a}[r_{n}+1,r_{n}+t_{n}-s_{n}]
and
\mathbf{a}[s_{n}+1,t_{n}]
. By setting
r_{n}^{\prime}=r_{n},s_{n}^{\prime}=s_{n},t_{n}^{\prime}=t_{n}
and
\delta^{\prime}=\delta+2k
, we deduce that
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
satisfies
(***)_{\rho}
as desired.
∎

.

The new word

has sub-linear complexity. Therefore, we can not apply directly theorems in

Recall that the Thue-Morse word

given by the morphism

is overlap-free. Further, observe that for every

is a prefix of
\mathbf{t}
where

satisfies

Now, we consider any real number

3.
Transcendence result

In this section, we prove Theorem
A
. Before going to the proof, we recall the Subspace Theorem and some auxiliary results.

3.1.
Diophantine prerequisites

We start with some preparations.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
d_{0}
and
M_{K}
(resp.
M_{K}^{\infty}
and
M_{K}^{\mathrm{fin}}
) be its sets of all places (resp. all archimedean places and all non-archimedean places). We will use the following normalized absolute values. For
x\in K
and
\mathsf{v}\in M_{K}
, we set

(i)

|x|_{\mathsf{v}}=|\sigma(x)|^{1/d_{0}}
when
\mathsf{v}
corresponds to a real embedding
\sigma:K\xhookrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}
;

(ii)

|x|_{\mathsf{v}}=|\sigma(x)|^{2/d_{0}}
when
\mathsf{v}
corresponds to a complex embedding
\sigma:K\xhookrightarrow{}\mathbb{C}
;

(iii)

|x|_{\mathsf{v}}=(N\mathfrak{p})^{-\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)/d_{0}}
when
\mathsf{v}
corresponds to a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
of the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

We note that with these normalizations, the triangle inequality holds for
v
archimedean. The product formula reads as

\prod_{\mathsf{v}\in M_{K}}|x|_{\mathsf{v}}=1\text{ for }x\in K^{\times}.
For each
v
, we denote the local degree by
d_{\mathsf{v}}=[K_{\mathsf{v}}:\mathbb{Q}_{\mathsf{v}}].
We will write
|x|
for the usual absolute value on
\mathbb{C}
.

For
n\geq 2
, we use the following height functions associated with a point
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in K^{n}
. For each place
\mathsf{v}\in M_{K}
, we set

|\mathbf{x}|_{\mathsf{v}}=\max\{|x_{1}|_{\mathsf{v}},\cdots,|x_{n}|_{\mathsf{v}}\}.

The absolute Weil height
{\mathrm{H}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
is then defined as

{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{\mathsf{v}\in M_{K}}|\mathbf{x}|_{\mathsf{v}}
. For

we write
{\mathrm{H}}(x)={\mathrm{H}}(x,1)
. This notation will not cause any confusion. More generally, for
\mathbf{x}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
, we can find a number field

such that
\mathbf{x}\in K^{n}
, and one observes that these heights do not depend on the choice of
K
. Thanks to the product formula, we always have
{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{x})={\mathrm{H}}(\alpha\mathbf{x})
for all
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}
. We write
{\mathrm{h}}(x)=\log{\mathrm{H}}(x)
for the absolute logarithmic Weil height. For a polynomial
P
with algebraic coefficients, we define its height
{\mathrm{H}}(P)
to be the absolute Weil height of its coefficient vector.

Lemma 3.1
.

(The Northcott property)
For any positive real numbers
d
and
H
, the set

\{\mathbf{x}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}:\deg(\mathbf{x})\leq d,{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{x})\leq H\}

is finite. Here,
\deg(\mathbf{x})
denote the degree of the smallest number field
K
such that
\mathbf{x}\in K^{n}
.

In the following, we fix an extension
|\cdot|_{\mathsf{v}}
to
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
for each

.

Theorem S
.

(The Subspace Theorem
[
16
, Theorem 7.2.2]
)
Let

,

a number field, and
\mathcal{S}
a finite subset of

. For each

, let

is contained in finitely many proper subspaces of

.

The following height estimates will be useful for us.

Lemma 3.2
.

(
[
43

to be the sum of the absolute values of the coefficients of

In particular, we have

.

(
[

Lemma 3.4

4.1.1.
The Quantitative Subspace Theorem

To state the Quantitative Subspace Theorem, we need some invariants attached to linear forms. Let
L(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\xi_{i}X_{i}}
be a linear form in
n

\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})
with algebraic coefficients. We set
{\mathrm{H}}(L)={\mathrm{H}}(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n})
(which remains the same when scaling
L
) and
K(L)=K\Bigg(\dfrac{\xi_{1}}{\xi_{i}},\cdots,\dfrac{\xi_{n}}{\xi_{i}}\Bigg)
where
i
is any indice with
\xi_{i}\neq 0
..

In the following, we fix an extension
|\cdot|_{\mathsf{v}}
to
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
for each
\mathsf{v}
.

Theorem ES
.

(The Quantitative Subspace Theorem
[
24
]
)
Let
K
be a number field. Let
\mathcal{S}
be a finite subset of
M_{K}
of cardinality
s>0
. Let

,
H>0
, and
1>\epsilon>0
. For each
\mathsf{v}\in\mathcal{S}
, let
L_{1,\mathsf{v}},\dots,L_{n,\mathsf{v}}
be
n
linearly independent linear forms in
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
with algebraic coefficients such that for every
i=1,\cdots,n
and every
\mathsf{v}\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

[K(L_{i,\mathsf{v}}):K]\leq d\text{ and }{\mathrm{H}}(L_{i,\mathsf{v}})\leq H.

Then the set of non-zero solutions
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in K^{n}
of the inequality

\prod_{\mathsf{v}\in\mathcal{S}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{|L_{i,\mathsf{v}}(\mathbf{x})|_{\mathsf{v}}}{|\mathbf{x}|_{\mathsf{v}}}\leq\prod_{\mathsf{v}\in\mathcal{S}}|\det(L_{1,\mathsf{v}},\cdots,L_{n,\mathsf{v}})|_{\mathsf{v}}{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{x})^{-n-\epsilon}

with

is contained in at most

proper subspaces of

where

is a constant depending only on

and
\epsilon
.
