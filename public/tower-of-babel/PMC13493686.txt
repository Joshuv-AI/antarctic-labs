A Durability Index for Atopic Dermatitis: Indirect Comparison of Lebrikizumab, Dupilumab, and Tralokinumab in Maintaining Efficacy Under Variable Treatment Adherence
Authors: Jonathan I. Silverberg, Alan D. Irvine, Peter Foley, James Del Rosso, Luis Puig, Linda Stein Gold, Masahiro Kamata, Andreas Wollenberg, H. Chih-ho Hong, Chia-Yu Chu, Yousef Binamer, Martin Dossenbach, Marta Casillas, Gaia Gallo, Buelent Akmaz, Kim Rand, Raj Chovatiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionReal-world management of moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis (AD) may involve treatment interruptions. This indirect comparison evaluated the efficacy of lebrikizumab, dupilumab, and tralokinumab monotherapy in achieving week-52 response at variable adherence levels.MethodsThis indirect comparative analysis used data from the induction (week 0–16) and maintenance (week 16–52) phases of the ADvocate, SOLO, and ECZTRA monotherapy trials. A novel Durability Index (DI) was used to estimate week 52 response from week 0 at variable maintenance adherence levels. To estimate the impact of adherence, reported maintenance responses in the continuous treatment (100% adherence) and withdrawal (0% adherence) arms were weighted by the assumed proportion of patients remaining on treatment, with intermediate adherence levels estimated by linear interpolation. Responses included an Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) score of 0/1 or ≥ 75% improvement in Eczema Area Severity Index (EASI 75). Odds ratios (ORs) and risk differences (RD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated.ResultsLebrikizumab had significantly greater efficacy than dupilumab of achieving week 52 IGA 0/1 for adherence rates between 0% (OR 3.4, 95% CI 1.3–11.3) and 78% (OR 1.5, 1.0–2.5) and EASI 75 for adherence rates between 0% (OR 2.6, 1.4–5.0) and 63% (OR 1.4, 1.0–2.0). At higher adherence rates, lebrikizumab had comparable efficacy to dupilumab. Lebrikizumab had significantly better efficacy than tralokinumab of achieving week 52 responses for all adherence rates for IGA 0/1 (0%: OR 2.5, 1.1–5.9; 100%: OR 2.6, 1.6–4.2) and EASI 75 (0%: OR 5.3, 2.8–10.4; 100%: OR 3.2, 2.2–4.8). Dupilumab had significantly better efficacy than tralokinumab of achieving IGA 0/1 at adherences rates above 69% and EASI 75 across all adherence rates. RDs were consistent with differences observed for ORs.ConclusionsIn this indirect comparative analysis, lebrikizumab was associated with comparable or superior efficacy relative to dupilumab and superior efficacy relative to tralokinumab across all adherence rates in moderate-to-severe AD. As the DI is a novel metric, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and independent validation of the DI is needed to ensure its clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01791-1.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, inflammatory skin disease characterized by skin lesions and pruritus [1–4]. Treatment options for moderate-to-severe AD after inadequate response to topical therapy include the biologic therapies dupilumab, tralokinumab, and lebrikizumab [5]. Although all three biologics are approved for moderate-to-severe AD in adults and adolescents [6–11], there are no head-to-head clinical trials comparing these biologics.

Recent network meta-analyses of phase 3 trial data found that lebrikizumab had similar efficacy to dupilumab and superior efficacy to tralokinumab in achieving itch improvement and skin clearance after 16 weeks of induction treatment [12–15]. Although previous network meta-analyses assessed short-term efficacy, AD is a chronic condition that requires long-term treatment to maintain disease control. Maintenance phases of these trials were undertaken to evaluate efficacy for an additional 36 weeks of treatment with lebrikizumab, dupilumab, and tralokinumab [16–18]. In these trials, patients who achieved a clinical response at week 16 were re-randomized to receive active treatment or placebo (treatment withdrawal) from week 16 up to week 52. Notably, response rates at week 52 in the active treatment and treatment withdrawal arms differed for lebrikizumab, dupilumab, and tralokinumab. In particular, a numerically higher proportion of week 16 responders who withdrew lebrikizumab maintained their response at week 52 as compared to those who had dupilumab or tralokinumab withdrawn [16–18]. These findings suggest that biologics vary in their ability to maintain treatment response after treatment discontinuation or interruption.

To our knowledge, no study has evaluated whether off-drug durability of response affects overall drug performance. For a chronic disease such as AD, real-world management is subject to treatment interruptions [19–24], and the withdrawal arms in maintenance phase trials offer the opportunity to assess how the durability of response after stopping therapy contributes to week 52 outcomes. We therefore developed a novel Durability Index (DI) that combines on-drug maintenance efficacy and off-drug durability of response to estimate week 52 outcomes among all patients initiating treatment, across a range of maintenance treatment adherence rates from 100% to 0%. This study is the first application of the DI in an indirect comparative analysis.

Methods

Data Source

A population-adjusted indirect comparison was conducted of placebo-controlled phase 3 monotherapy trials with similar maintenance phase designs [16–18]. However, the populations in these trials differed. The ADvocate 1 and 2 trials of lebrikizumab included adults and adolescents [16], while the SOLO 1 and 2 and SOLO-CONTINUE trials of dupilumab [17] and ECZTRA 1 and 2 trials of tralokinumab included only adults [18]. To create comparable populations, our analysis used the modified intention-to-treat adult subsample in the ADvocate trials. Dosing regimens were selected based on approved product labeling for each medication [6, 9, 11]. All analyses included population-adjusted response data from the induction (week 0–16) and maintenance (week 16–52) phases (Table 1) [16–18, 25, 26]. ADvocate data were derived from the lebrikizumab 250 mg every 2 weeks (Q2W) induction arm and 250 mg every 4 weeks (Q4W) maintenance arm. SOLO data were derived from the dupilumab 300 mg Q2W induction arm and the combined every week/Q2W (QW) maintenance arm, because the approved Q2W dosing was not reported separately. ECZTRA data were derived from the tralokinumab 300 mg Q2W induction and maintenance arms. During induction treatment, patients received active treatment or placebo from week 0 to 16. Responses at week 16 were defined as an Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) score of 0 (clear) or 1 (almost clear), with a ≥ 2 point reduction from baseline, or ≥ 75% improvement in Eczema Area Severity Index (EASI 75). Week 16 responders were re-randomized to continue maintenance treatment or switch to treatment withdrawal (placebo) from week 16 to 52. During treatment, patients who used topical or systemic rescue medication after week 16 were considered week 52 non-responders. In our analysis, patients who used rescue medication were classified as non-responders using non-responder imputation. Differences in the maintenance of response between the active treatment and withdrawal arms at week 52 highlight the unique nature of the withdrawal arm. Unlike a traditional placebo, the withdrawal arm reflects the durability of the previous treatment’s effect (weeks 0–16). Although this makes the withdrawal arm an inappropriate comparator for between-trial comparisons of active treatment response rates from week 16 to 52, it can be used to estimate the off-drug efficacy of maintenance treatment. Durability Index (DI)

The DI combines the induction response rate with the maintenance and withdrawal response rates to provide an overall estimate of week 52 outcomes among all patients initiating treatment at week 0 across varying levels of maintenance treatment exposure. The DI can be interpreted at the individual patient level (reflecting adherence to the treatment schedule) or at the population level (reflecting the proportion of patients on treatment). For our analysis, the DI refers to the population-level interpretation.

The DI is calculated using the probability of patients achieving an outcome of interest at week 16 and the subsequent probability of maintaining the same outcome at week 52, depending on whether they remain on treatment or withdraw from treatment. On-treatment and off-treatment maintenance response rates are weighted to reflect different rates of treatment continuation in a population (e.g., if 75% of patients remain on treatment, on-treatment responses are weighted at 75% and off-treatment responses at 25%). The DI was derived from reported week 16 response rates [18, 25, 26] and from reported week 52 response rates in the active treatment (100% adherence) and treatment withdrawal (0% adherence) arms of maintenance trials [16–18]. After estimating week 52 maintenance response probabilities at 100% and 0% adherence, linear interpolation on a log odds scale was used to estimate maintenance response probabilities at adherence rates between 100% and 0%. Linear interpolation of intermediate adherence levels occurred between the two observed maintenance responses at week 52 in the treatment continuation arm (interpreted as 100% maintenance adherence) and the treatment withdrawal arm (interpreted as 0% maintenance adherence). For a given maintenance adherence rate, the DI is calculated as the product of the probability of achieving a week 16 response and the adherence-weighted probability of maintaining response at week 52. This yields an overall estimate of week 52 efficacy among all patients initiating treatment at baseline.

Expected Re-randomization of Non-responders

Populations in the maintenance trials for dupilumab and tralokinumab cannot be directly compared because of inconsistent reporting of baseline characteristics for re-randomized participants at week 16. However, comparable baseline characteristics data are available from induction trials at week 0. Consequently, to enable population-adjusted estimation of the DI, the analysis population included all patients who received active treatment at week 0 (baseline) in induction trials. This required reconstruction of maintenance populations from the baseline population based on expected re-randomization ratios. To ensure the re-randomized groups were unbiased estimates of the initial population (week 0, active treatment), non-responders at week 16 were included in the model using their expected re-randomization ratios, along with responders actually enrolled in the maintenance trials. This approach recreates unbiased distributions of baseline characteristics as they were in the active treatment induction populations at week 0. The resulting re-randomized populations are meaningful for analysis under the conditions that the outcome of interest is binary and that all non-responders at week 16 are handled as non-responders at week 52. Additional details can be found in the Supplemental Material.

Unanchored Simulated Treatment Comparison (STC) Analysis

Logistic regression models and STC were used to adjust outcome estimates for differences in baseline covariates across trials [27–29]. Equivalent STC models with an interaction term for the on-treatment rate assignment were used to predict week 52 maintenance of response conditional on having response at week 16. STC regresses outcomes on baseline covariates, treating them as prognostic factors and including interaction terms for effect modifiers. The pool of baseline covariates for re-weighting was limited to those available in the individual patient data (IPD) for the ADvocate trials and the aggregate data for the comparators of interest in the ECZTRA and SOLO trials. The primary STC analysis adjusted for both treatment effect modifiers and prognostic factors, including baseline EASI scores, percentage of affected body surface area (BSA), age, proportion of male participants, and proportion of white participants. These variables are key indicators of disease severity and patient demographics used in previous matching-adjusted indirect comparisons (MAICs) of biologics [30, 31]. In the primary STC analysis, only age was used as a treatment effect modifier, while other variables were used as prognostic factors. Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted by varying the target population with various combinations of variables to test the stability of the primary analysis. The pooled population of all three trials was used as the target population for comparisons to minimize bias associated with using only a single trial population in isolation. Two STC logistic regression models were generated: one for week 16 outcomes and one for week 52 outcomes. Uncertainty was handled using non-parametric bootstrapping, with 100,000 resamples drawn from the active induction treatment population [32]. The re-creation of baseline distributions was achieved by considering the predicted outcomes at week 16 and week 52 for each bootstrap subsample. Additional details can be found in the Supplemental Material.

Statistical Analysis

Odds ratios (ORs) and risk differences (RDs), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), for DIs were estimated using weighted logistic regression for maintenance adherence rates from 100% to 0%, and standard errors were adjusted using a heteroscedasticity-consistent sandwich estimator [33].

Results

Week 52 Outcomes for Lebrikizumab vs Dupilumab

At week 52, lebrikizumab had statistically significantly better efficacy than dupilumab of achieving IGA 0/1 for maintenance adherence rates between 0% (OR 3.4, 95% CI 1.3–11.3) and 78% (OR 1.5, 1.0–2.2) (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S1). Lebrikizumab had numerically better efficacy than dupilumab of achieving IGA 0/1 for all maintenance adherence rates above 78%. Lebrikizumab also had significantly better efficacy than dupilumab of achieving EASI 75 at week 52 for maintenance adherence rates between 0% (OR 2.6, 1.4–5.0) and 63% (OR 1.4, 1.0–2.0). Lebrikizumab had numerically better efficacy than dupilumab of achieving EASI 75 for maintenance adherence rates between 63% and 98% (OR 1.0, 95% CI 0.7–1.4), while dupilumab had numerically better efficacy of achieving EASI 75 at maintenance adherence rates above 98%. Sensitivity Analyses and Unadjusted Comparisons

All comparisons in the primary analysis remained consistent in sensitivity analyses when the target population was adjusted to match the SOLO or ECZTRA trials (Supplementary Tables S7–S8) and when covariates used for adjustment were varied. In addition, all comparisons in the primary analysis remained consistent when no covariate adjustments were made (Supplementary Table S9).

Discussion

In a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by periods of relapse and remission, long-term treatment is crucial to maintain disease control and improve quality of life. Traditionally, long-term treatment strategies involve continuous treatment regimens. Trial designs evaluating the long-term efficacy of lebrikizumab, dupilumab, and tralokinumab demonstrated that these biologics maintain long-term disease control on continuous therapy, but the durability of effect after treatment discontinuation markedly varies. There are currently no clinical trials directly comparing responses to continuous maintenance treatment versus intermittent maintenance treatment in moderate-to-severe AD. To our knowledge, this study is the first three-way comparison of all biologics approved for monotherapy in AD. A key aspect of this study is the introduction of the DI to assess drug performance in clinically relevant settings where maintenance treatment may be intermittent. At 100% maintenance adherence, lebrikizumab had comparable week 52 response rates to dupilumab. At lower adherence levels, lebrikizumab had significantly higher week 52 responses than dupilumab and was superior to tralokinumab across all adherence levels. These findings suggest that lebrikizumab may provide better outcomes in real-life settings where treatment interruptions may occur for various reasons, such as surgery, pregnancy, or a patient’s preference to temporarily pause treatment after achieving remission.

In real-world settings where treatment adherence is below 100%, the durability of response upon discontinuation is a crucial treatment attribute. Although treatment adherence to biologics is generally higher than that of oral drugs [34], the therapeutic potential of biologics in chronic inflammatory skin diseases is not always fully realized in clinical practice because of suboptimal compliance and persistence [35–38]. This may contribute to lower levels of treatment effectiveness compared with clinical trial results. For AD, discontinuation rates for dupilumab have been reported to range from approximately 7% to 23% within 1–2 years, with higher rates observed over longer periods [19, 21, 39]. A recent prospective multicenter cohort study found that dupilumab was discontinued in approximately 24% of patients after a median of 4.5 years of treatment, mainly due to adverse events or ineffectiveness [40]. Other reasons for discontinuing dupilumab may include pregnancy, remission, noncompliance, surgery, issues with insurance coverage, and patient preference [41]. Real-world data for lebrikizumab and tralokinumab adherence rates are lacking. Given the varying durability of response among biologics, future studies should evaluate their real-world discontinuation rates to better inform long-term management of AD.

In the absence of head-to-head trials, indirect comparative analyses have compared patients receiving active treatment (i.e., 100% adherence) in maintenance trials [16–18]. Placebo-anchored indirect comparative analyses of lebrikizumab vs dupilumab [42] and tralokinumab [43] used the treatment withdrawal arms in these studies as a common comparator to assess differences between active treatment arms. However, off-drug durability of response must be considered in any analyses of efficacy data from these maintenance trials. By ignoring differences in maintenance of response between the active treatment and withdrawal arms, these anchored MAICs resulted in misleading findings that favored drugs with worse post-withdrawal maintenance responses [44]. In cases where the comparator is not similar, such as the treatment withdrawals arms, an unanchored MAIC should be used [44]. In one unanchored MAIC, lebrikizumab 250 mg Q4W had equal or superior long-term maintenance of efficacy as measured by EASI 75 and IGA 0/1 compared to dupilumab 300 mg QW/Q2W [31]. Notably, in this study, treatment efficacy was assessed based on the proportion of patients who maintained response between week 16 and 52, whereas the DI estimates treatment efficacy based on the proportion of patients achieving response at week 16 and maintaining that response at week 52. Another unanchored MAIC found that lebrikizumab 250 mg Q4W was significantly more likely than tralokinumab 300 mg Q2W to both achieve IGA 0/1 and EASI 75 at week 16 and maintain those responses at week 52 [30]; this finding is consistent with our comparisons of lebrikizumab and tralokinumab at 100% maintenance treatment adherence. These results suggest that lebrikizumab may offer an equivalent or higher likelihood of both achieving and maintaining response with 100% adherence compared with dupilumab or tralokinumab. However, head-to-head trials are needed to address the limitations of indirect treatment comparisons, including reliance on cross-trial assumptions and, for matching-based approaches, the reduction in effective sample size that occurs after matching on baseline patient characteristics.

While data from long-term trials of Janus kinase inhibitors were not included in the DI analysis [45–47], a recent phase 2 trial of upadacitinib in patients with moderate-to-severe AD assessed the effect of treatment withdrawal on disease control [48]. Patients were treated for 16 weeks with upadacitinib at various doses and then re-randomized to receive the same dose or placebo (treatment withdrawal). Approximately 60% of patients who were treated with upadacitinib 30 mg once daily (QD) and re-randomized to treatment withdrawal experienced a ≥ 4-point worsening in their daily pruritus numerical rating score within 7 days of withdrawal. Moreover, in patients re-randomized at week 16 from upadacitinib 15 or 30 mg QD to treatment withdrawal, 78.9% required rescue treatment due to loss of response. Similarly, in the phase 3 JADE REGIMEN trial, 77.5% of patients with moderate-to-severe AD who achieved an induction response to abrocitinib 100 or 200 mg QD lost their response after withdrawing from treatment, primarily in the first month [49]. While upadacitinib and abrocitinib resulted in rapid itch improvement [50, 51], their short half-lives [52, 53] may reduce their durability of response and require continuous treatment to maintain efficacy.

Trial-specific factors are comparable across the lebrikizumab, tralokinumab, and dupilumab phase 3 trials, including a 16-week induction period, subsequent re-randomization of week 16 responders to active treatment or treatment-withdrawal arms, and a monotherapy design. Accordingly, differences in off-drug durability of response are unlikely to be attributable to differences in trial design. Rather, the efficacy of biologics may reflect a combination of on-drug maintenance efficacy and off-drug durability of response. Longer half-life, for example, may contribute to a more sustained effect even after treatment discontinuation. Lebrikizumab [11] and tralokinumab [6] have half-lives of approximately 3 weeks, whereas dupilumab has a shorter half-life of less than a week [54]. However, half-life alone may not explain lebrikizumab’s durability of response. Lebrikizumab was undetectable in serum 36 weeks after treatment withdrawal, even in patients who maintained stable EASI 90 responses [55]. It is therefore possible that the mechanism of action of each biologic may lead to unique therapeutic effects [56]. Cytokine binding with lebrikizumab may have a different long-term effect than receptor blocking with dupilumab. For example, in psoriasis, it is well documented that receptor blockade with brodalumab is associated with shorter times to relapse after drug discontinuation [57, 58]. Additionally, although both lebrikizumab and tralokinumab target interleukin (IL)-13, lebrikizumab binds IL-13 with higher affinity and slower dissociation and allows IL-13 clearance through the IL-13Rα2 decoy receptor, whereas tralokinumab-bound IL-13 does not [59–62]. Additionally, lebrikizumab reaches steady-state concentrations earlier than tralokinumab (approximately 4 vs 16 weeks) [63, 64], which may influence the depth and stability of IL-13 pathway suppression before treatment withdrawal. Another factor to consider is the potential for disease modification, where a therapeutic agent alters the disease course, leading to long-term disease remission. These drug-specific carryover effects could differentiate biologics and Janus kinase inhibitors and should be explored in future studies to determine whether off-drug durability of response can be enhanced.

Composite measures have previously been used to summarize real-world treatment performance by integrating efficacy and adherence. The LUNDEX is a composite measure that evaluates the real-world performance of tumor necrosis factor inhibitors and other biologics in inflammatory diseases [65–67]. The LUNDEX incorporates both clinical response rates and adherence rates at a given time into a composite value. While the LUNDEX is useful for assessing the impact of treatment adherence on response, it does not account for responses in patients not adhering to treatment. In contrast, the DI is a composite measure of both on-drug efficacy and off-drug durability of response. It estimates the probability that patients will achieve a response and maintain that response depending on the proportion of patients who continue or discontinue maintenance treatment.

A strength of this analysis is that all comparisons in the primary analysis remained consistent even without covariate adjustments. Another strength is the use of individual patient data from the ADvocate 1 and 2 trials to identify potential treatment effect modifiers and prognostic factors. Baseline prognostic factors included EASI, %BSA, sex, and race, with age as a treatment effect modifier for week 52 outcomes. By pooling data from the ADvocate, ECZTRA, and SOLO trials, this analysis allowed for a comprehensive three-way comparison that better reflected the general patient population. The use of STC models to simulate treatment outcomes in different patient populations, combined with bootstrapping techniques to test data stability, helped ensure our findings were robust and reliable by keeping the distribution of key covariates consistent [29, 32]. The STC approach has several limitations. It assumes that the trials being compared are similar in important ways, such as trial design, patient characteristics, and treatment protocols. It is also limited to the available covariates in both individual patient data and reported aggregate data from the trials. Additionally, the absence of reported correlations between covariates in comparator trials introduces uncertainty, and the impact of any non-normal distributions of covariates is unknown but assumed to be small. Despite these limitations, pooling data from the ADvocate, ECZTRA, and SOLO trials provided a more comprehensive reflection of the clinical population than any single trial alone.

A limitation of this analysis is that the DI is a novel, unvalidated composite metric introduced in this post hoc exploratory analysis. Our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, and independent validation in separate datasets is needed before the DI can be used to support clinical decision-making. Another limitation is the lack of detailed information on the timing of efficacy loss between weeks 16 and 52 for dupilumab and tralokinumab. Although intermediate maintenance efficacy timepoints between week 16 and 52 have been reported for lebrikizumab [16], such data have not been reported for dupilumab or tralokinumab. Future research could enhance the real-world representativeness of the DI if there are intermediate clinical trial timepoints available to assess how maintenance efficacy is impacted by the duration of treatment interruption. Additionally, the DI estimates the probability of both achieving response at week 16 and maintaining that response at week 52. If a patient did not achieve response at week 16, they are considered non-responders in this analysis. However, some week 16 non-responders may eventually achieve response between week 16 and 52 in the absence of further treatment. Additionally, non-responders at week 16 have been shown to achieve week 52 responses with continued lebrikizumab treatment [68]. The DI should therefore be interpreted as a measure of week 52 efficacy contingent on week 16 response. Furthermore, the DI requires an assumption about the relationship between maintenance adherence and week 52 response. To estimate intermediate adherence levels, a linear relationship was assumed between the two observed week 52 maintenance response rates: treatment continuation (100% adherence) and treatment withdrawal (0% adherence). However, the true relationship may be nonlinear and may depend on the pattern and timing of treatment discontinuation. As a result, the DI should be interpreted as an approximation of outcomes under simplified adherence patterns, and further research is needed to better characterize the relationship between real-world adherence patterns and maintenance of response. Finally, we also acknowledge that our analysis focuses on monotherapy trials, which allow for a clear comparison of the pure drug effect and the consequences of drug withdrawal across biologics, however, many patients in real-world practice use topical agents alongside biologics. Notably, the efficacy of biologics in combination with topical therapy has been evaluated in other comparative analyses; for example, a recent MAIC of the ADhere and CHRONOS trials found that lebrikizumab Q2W plus topical corticosteroids and dupilumab Q2W plus topical corticosteroids had similar efficacy through week 16 across multiple endpoints in adults with moderate-to-severe AD [69].

Conclusions

This indirect comparative analysis suggests that biologics differ in their long-term efficacy under varying treatment adherence scenarios. Lebrikizumab had significantly higher week 52 responses than dupilumab or tralokinumab at most adherence rates. These findings suggest that lebrikizumab may be an optimal treatment in clinical scenarios where treatment breaks are expected, given its efficacy under continuous maintenance treatment and ability to accommodate treatment interruptions. By introducing the DI as a novel approach to evaluating on-drug efficacy and off-drug durability of response during maintenance treatment, this study offers a framework for evaluating long-term treatment strategies for chronic diseases, such as AD, where treatment interruptions may occur in real-word conditions. However, because the DI is a novel, unvalidated metric, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as hypothesis-generating. Independent validation in external datasets is needed before these results can be used to guide clinical decision-making.
