Sexual dysfunction and effect on quality of life in chronic kidney disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Imuetinyan Rashida Edeki, Oluseyi Ademola Adejumo, Dapo Sunday Oyedepo, Adedayo Oluwadamilola Adesida, Olanrewaju Olamide Ige, Olawale Elijah Yisau, Godswill Thomas Eromosele, Olayinka Vincent Olalekan, Victor Ayodeji Ajiboye, Oluwatomisin Abiola Ijatuji, Inyeneabasi Udeme Ekrikpo, Udeme-Abasi Udoudo Nelson, Enajite Ibiene Okaka, Adekunle Oyeyemi Lawal, Dilaram Acharya, Udeme Ekpenyong Ekrikpo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSexual dysfunction in chronic kidney disease (CKD) is frequently underrecognized and underreported worldwide, despite its well-established association with depression and reduced quality of life (QoL). This study estimated the pooled global prevalence of sexual dysfunction among individuals with CKD and evaluated its impact on psychosocial well-being.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis of published studies examining sexual dysfunction in CKD was conducted following PRISMA 2020 guidelines (PROSPERO: CRD42024508777). Pooled prevalence estimates were calculated using a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model with Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation, and correlation coefficients were pooled following Fisher’s Z transformation.ResultsForty-four eligible studies, involving 10,672 participants from 18 countries, were included. The overall pooled prevalence of sexual dysfunction was 71.2% (95% CI: 65.9–76.3%, I²= 96.8%), with no significant difference across geographical regions (p = 0.87) or between males (71.5%, 95% CI: 65.5–77.3%) and females (67.3%, 95% CI: 52.6–80.5%) (p = 0.61). Prevalence was significantly higher among patients on hemodialysis (77.6%) and those in predialysis stage (76.7%) compared with post-kidney transplant recipients (56.9%), with a significant difference across patient groups (p = 0.01). Sexual dysfunction was significantly correlated with poorer mental (r ≈ 0.36, 95% CI: 0.27–0.45, p < 0.001) and physical (r ≈ 0.39, 95% CI: 0.29–0.48, p < 0.001) health-related quality of life, as well as greater depression severity as measured by the IIEF (r = -0.34, p < 0.001) and FSFI (r = -0.42, p < 0.001). No significant publication bias was observed on Begg’s test (p = 0.48) or Egger’s test (p = 0.16).ConclusionSexual dysfunction is highly prevalent among individuals with CKD, affecting more than two-thirds of patients and varying by treatment modality, with lower prevalence observed after kidney transplantation. It is significantly associated with poorer quality of life and depression severity. These findings underscore the need for routine screening and integrated psychological and sexual health interventions in CKD care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12882-026-05142-2.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects multiple systems in humans [1]. Sexual health plays an important role in both physical and mental health and is a basic aspect of overall well-being [2]. Compared to the general population, people with CKD experience sexual dysfunction more frequently and with greater burden [3]. Despite its substantial impact on general well-being, sexual dysfunction in CKD is usually overlooked because of stigma, sensitivity, and concerns about therapeutic safety [4].

Sexual dysfunction refers to a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized typically by significant disturbance in a person’s ability to respond sexually or to experience pleasure [5]. Sexual dysfunction in CKD results from a complex interplay of physiological and psychological alterations, adverse drug effects, and CKD-associated co-morbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, anemia, uremia, malnutrition, and calcium-phosphate abnormalities, leading to diminished quality of life [6].

The common features of male sexual dysfunction are erectile dysfunction (ED), premature ejaculation, male hypoactive sexual desire disorders, and other ejaculatory disorders (such as retrograde ejaculation, anejaculation, delayed ejaculation, and anorgasmia). The features of female sexual dysfunction include female orgasmic disorders, female sexual interest/arousal disorders, genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorders, and other specified/unspecified sexual disorders (sexual aversion disorder, medication/substance-induced sexual disorder, unspecified sexual dysfunction disorder) [7]. Male and female CKD patients are equally affected, with erectile dysfunction reported in 80–90% of men on hemodialysis [8, 9] and amenorrhea in up to 95% of women with end-stage disease [9].

Sexual dysfunction has a negative correlation with overall quality of life (QoL) and is associated with low self-esteem, melancholy, anxiety, frustration, infertility, diminished confidence, absenteeism, and decreased productivity [8, 10–13]. Therefore, routine screening in all stages of CKD, prompt identification, and effective treatment contribute significantly to improving the quality of life in patients with CKD. This review aimed to determine the global prevalence of sexual dysfunction in CKD patients and its effect on QoL and depression.

Methods

The systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted on published articles on sexual dysfunction in individuals with CKD and its effect on quality of life, anxiety, and depression. The review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guideline [14] and was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (registration number CRD42024508777) [15].

Literature search

A systematic literature search was conducted on articles about sexual dysfunction in individuals with CKD and its effect on QoL, anxiety, and depression. We conducted the literature search on PubMed and EMBASE as well as hand searches using terms related to sexual dysfunction in CKD and effect on quality of life such as ' sexual dysfunction’’, ‘’sexual abnormality’’, ‘’sexual disorder’’, ‘sexual disorientation’’, ‘’sexual disturbance’’, chronic kidney disease’’, ‘’ chronic renal failure’’, ‘’chronic renal insufficiency’’, ‘’chronic renal dysfunction’’, ‘’kidney failure’’, ‘’’predialysis chronic kidney disease’’, ‘’end stage renal disease’’, ‘’end stage renal failure’’, ‘’maintenance hemodialysis (HD)’’, ‘’peritoneal dialysis (PD)’’, ‘’quality of life’’, ‘’well-being’’, ‘’life quality’’, ‘’life satisfaction’’, ‘’quality of life index’’, ‘’way of life’’,‘’ life outcome’’, ‘’living condition’’, ‘’lifestyle’’, ‘’standard of living’’, ‘’level of comfort’’ and ‘’health-related quality of life’’ in conjunction with all the names of continents. The search strategy created in February 2024 is presented in Supplementary Table 1. There were no language limitations in the literature search, and reference lists of relevant articles were also manually searched to identify additional eligible studies.

Study selection

Inclusion criteria were cross-sectional and prospective studies reporting sexual dysfunction in patients with CKD, as well as its effect on QoL and/or depression across predialysis, dialysis, and kidney transplant adult populations in all the World Health Organization (WHO) regions up to February 2024. Studies without prevalence of sexual dysfunction in individuals with CKD, had sample sizes less than 100, or were available only as abstracts without full text articles on sexual dysfunction in CKD were excluded. Two investigators (IRE and OAA) independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility. Full text of eligible articles meeting the inclusion criteria were then retrieved and assessed for final inclusion by the same reviewers. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and a third investigator (DSO) adjudicated unresolved discrepancies to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Data extraction and management

The following variables were extracted: the last name of first author, year of publication, country and continent where study was carried out, study design, study sample size, study duration, mean age of study participants, stage of CKD, type of kidney replacement therapy, number with sexual dysfunction, male and female sexual function scale used, number with ED or female sexual dysfunction (FSD) with reduced QoL, number with ED and FSD having depression, scale used to assess QoL, anxiety and depression. Sexual dysfunction was defined according to the diagnostic criteria reported in the included studies, using established instrument-specific cut-offs. Where applicable, these included IIEF erectile-function domain score ≤ 25, IIEF-5 score ≤ 21, FSFI score ≤ 26.55, and ASEX score ≥ 19, with lower scores indicating worse sexual function for IIEF, IIEF-5, and FSFI, and higher scores indicating worse sexual function for ASEX.

The data extraction form was developed collaboratively with input from co-authors, including nephrology specialists. Data were independently extracted by two reviewers, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer. Study locations were categorized based on the continent, and the term “intercontinental” was used to connote studies done in countries that spanned at least two different continents. Study locations were categorized based on the continent. Studies were categorized by year of publication, severity of CKD, and type of kidney replacement therapy. For ease of statistical analysis, individual studies that involved different patient categories were entered separately.

Methodological quality

The Joanna-Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data was used to assess the methodological quality of the constituent studies [16]. Studies were assigned a score of 1 for each of the nine questions with a positive response. Studies with scores of 0–3 were regarded as poor quality, 4–6 as intermediate/medium quality, and 7–9 as high quality. Quality assessment was done by two different reviewers, and areas of conflict were resolved by consensus.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was not required, as the study used only previously published data. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024508777).

Statistical analysis

The study-specific estimates were pooled using Der-Simonian-Laird random effects model [17] to determine the pooled prevalence of SD in the CKD population and examine the correlation between sexual dysfunction, QOL, anxiety, and depression. To minimize the effect of extreme values, the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation [18] was used to stabilize the individual study variances before using the random effect model to obtain pooled prevalence estimates. For the main forest plot, we also calculated the 95% prediction interval to quantify the expected range of true prevalence estimates in comparable future studies and to reflect between-study heterogeneity beyond the pooled confidence interval. To compute the pooled correlation between sexual dysfunction and quality of life scores, correlation coefficients were extracted and Fisher’s Z-transformed prior to pooling to normalize the sampling distribution and stabilize the variance. Pooled Fisher’s Z estimate and 95% confidence intervals were back-transformed to Pearson’s r for interpretation. One study [19] was excluded from the correlation meta-analysis because it used a different quality of life measurement instrument, the Dartmouth Primary Care Cooperative Research Network and World Organization of National Colleges, Academies, and Academic Associations of General Practitioners/Family Physicians (COOP-WONCA), than the other studies, introducing clinical heterogeneity that would preclude the meaningful pooling of correlation coefficients across conceptually non-equivalent constructs. For the studies that reported correlation estimates between the Beck Depression Index (BDI) and male or female sexual dysfunction scores, pooled Fisher’s Z estimates were also back-transformed to pooled correlation coefficients.

Potential publication bias and small-study effects were assessed using visual inspection of funnel plots and formal statistical tests. Funnel plots were constructed by plotting the Freeman-Tukey double-arcsine-transformed prevalence estimates against their corresponding standard errors, with pseudo-95% confidence limits superimposed around the pooled random-effects estimate. Funnel plot asymmetry was formally evaluated using the Begg’s rank correlation test and Egger’s regression asymmetry test [20], with statistical significance defined a priori as a two-sided p value < 0.05.

A subgroup analysis of pooled prevalence by continent, predialysis versus HD versus PD versus kidney transplant (KT) population, male versus female, and among studies that used different SD scales. Subgroup analysis was performed using the Q test based on ANOVA. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using I2 statistic. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 19.5 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) [21].

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The systematic literature search identified 3,356 records, of which 177 were assessed in full text after removal of duplicates and title/abstract screening. Following eligibility assessment, 44 studies [19, 22–64] published between 2000 and 2023 were included in this systematic review (Fig. 1). The included studies comprised 10,672 patients with CKD from 18 countries. There were 646 participants (3 studies) from Africa [29, 30, 42], 4580 (20 studies) from Europe [19, 22, 23, 28, 33, 36–39, 41, 45, 50, 52, 53, 55, 57, 58, 60–62]; 1062 (5 studies) from South America [25–27, 32, 47]; 877 (4 studies) from North America [31, 35, 44, 51], 2189 (10 studies) from Asia [24, 34, 40, 43, 46, 48, 49, 59, 63, 64] and 1318 (2 studies) were intercontinental [54, 56].

The sample size of the component studies ranged from 100 [24, 30, 39] to 809 [64], with a mean female proportion of 40.1% and a median age of 48.3 years (IQR: 42.5–52.3). Twenty-two studies (50%) involved only hemodialysis patients [19, 23–26, 30, 32, 34, 35, 37, 40, 41, 43, 44, 46, 47, 49, 51, 53, 54, 56, 58], 2 (4.5%) studies focused on peritoneal dialysis [62, 63], two (4.5%) studies on predialysis [26, 57], and twelve (27.2%) in post kidney transplant patients [29, 31, 33, 36, 38, 39, 45, 50, 52, 59, 61, 64]. Eleven studies included only females [22, 24, 34, 38, 39, 44, 48, 53, 54, 56, 61]. The majority of the studies (29, 87.9%) assessing male sexual function used the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) scale in assessing male sexual function, while 8 studies (44.4%) assessing female sexual function used the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) scale (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

Most studies (68.2%, n = 30) were of high methodological quality, including 14 studies (46.7%) from Europe, 5 studies (11.4%) from Asia, 4 studies (11.4%) each from South America and North America, 2 (4.5%) from Africa, and 1 intercontinental study (Supplementary Table 2). Prevalence of sexual dysfunction in chronic kidney disease

The overall pooled prevalence of sexual dysfunction was 71.2% [95% CI: 65.9–76.3%, I2= 96.8%, p < 0.001] (Fig. 2). Asia showed the highest pooled prevalence of 76.5% [95% CI: 65.3–86.1%, I2 = 96.7%, p < 0.001], followed by North America at 75.8%. [95% CI: 57.3–90.4%, I2 = 97.1%, p < 0.001], Africa 72.3% [95% CI: 36.9–96.6%, I2 = 98.5%, p < 0.001], South America 71.5% [95% CI: 64.0-78.5%, I2 = 85.1%, p < 0.001] and Europe 69.0% [95% CI: 62.1–75.5%, I2 = 94.3%, p < 0.001]. There was no difference in pooled prevalence across the geographical regions (p = 0.87). The overall heterogeneity was τ² = 0.18, I² = 96.78%, H² = 31.03. The overall 95% prediction interval was 0.30–0.98, representing the range within which the true prevalence of sexual dysfunction in a future CKD population would be expected to fall.

The overall pooled prevalence of male and female sexual dysfunction was 71.5% [95% CI: 65.5–77.3%, I2 = 95.7%, p < 0.001] and 67.3% [95% CI: 52.6–80.5%, I2 = 97.9%, p < 0.001], respectively (p = 0.61) (Fig. 3). There was also no significant difference in the overall pooled prevalence of sexual dysfunction when using the various male and female sexual function scales (p = 0.52 and 0.15, respectively) (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

The prevalence of sexual dysfunction was 76.7% [95% CI: 65.5–86.3%, I2 = 89.5%, p < 0.001] in the predialysis group; 77.6% [95% CI: 70.4–84.0%, I2 = 96.9%, p < 0.001] in the hemodialysis group; 74.1% [95% CI: 60.4–85.8%, I2 = 93.4%, p < 0.001] in peritoneal dialysis and 56.9% [95% CI: 46.6–67.0%, I2 = 96.4%, p < 0.001] in the post kidney transplant group (Fig. 4), p = 0.01 for difference across the treatment groups. There was no difference in the pooled prevalence between sex in the hemodialysis subgroup (p = 0.96) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Sexual dysfunction in CKD and correlation with depression and quality of life

Six studies (n = 547) examined the correlation between sexual dysfunction scores and the mental component summary score of the SF-36, stratified by sex. The overall pooled Fisher’s Z was 0.38 (95% CI: 0.27, 0.48), corresponding to a back-transformed Pearson’s r of 0.36 (95% CI: 0.27–0.45), indicating a statistically significant positive correlation between greater sexual dysfunction and poorer mental health-related quality of life (z = 7.13, p < 0.001). Heterogeneity was low overall (I² = 28.74%), and no significant difference was found between sex subgroups (p = 0.30) (Fig. 5A).

The overall pooled Fisher’s Z was 0.41 (95% CI: 0.30, 0.52), corresponding to a back-transformed Pearson’s r of 0.39 (95% CI: 0.29–0.48) for the correlation between sexual dysfunction scores and the physical component summary score of SF-36, indicating a statistically significant positive correlation between greater sexual dysfunction and poorer physical health-related quality of life (z = 7.17, p < 0.001). Heterogeneity was low overall (I² = 39.15%), and no significant difference was observed between sex subgroups (p = 0.96) (Fig. 5B).

A significant negative pooled correlation was found between IIEF score and BDI scores (r = -0.34, 95% CI: -0.45, -0.21, z= -5.02, p < 0.001) and between FSFI scores and BDI scores (r = −0.42, 95% CI: -0.51, -0.33, z= -8.11, p < 0.001), indicating that greater depression severity was consistently associated with worse sexual function across both instruments. Heterogeneity was negligible in both analyses (I2 = 0.00% for both), suggesting highly consistent findings across studies (Figs. 6 A and B).

Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. 4) showed an overall symmetrical distribution of studies around the pooled prevalence estimates, with no clear evidence of directional asymmetry or selective absence of smaller studies. Consistent with this observation, Begg’s test did not demonstrate evidence of publication bias (Kendall’s τ p = 0.48), and Egger’s regression test showed a non-significant intercept (p = 0.16), indicating no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 44 studies involving 10,672 patients from 18 countries demonstrated that the overall pooled prevalence of sexual dysfunction among individuals with CKD was high at 71.2%. The burden of sexual dysfunction was consistently high across all CKD stages and geographic regions, with no significant differences by sex or region, although prevalence varied according to treatment modality. It was most common among patients in predialysis and hemodialysis groups, whereas kidney transplant recipients exhibited comparatively lower rates. In addition, sexual dysfunction was significantly correlated with poorer physical and mental health-related quality of life, as well as greater severity of depressive symptoms.

The overall pooled prevalence of sexual dysfunction in this study is consistent with reports of previous studies, such as those by Navaneethan et al. [65], who reported a 70% prevalence of erectile dysfunction, and Pyrgidis et al. [66], who identified a 74% prevalence of FSD in end-stage renal disease. There were no significant regional differences in our study in the prevalence of sexual dysfunction (p = 0.87), although estimates were generally higher in Asia, North America, South America, and Africa compared with Europe. The relatively lower prevalence observed in Europe may reflect better access to healthcare and higher quality of life compared with regions such as South America and Africa [67]. Although not statistically significant, sexual dysfunction appeared slightly lower in females than males (67.3% vs. 71.5%). This may reflect under-recognition and underreporting in women, partly due to challenges in assessing female sexual function and sociocultural barriers to discussing sexual health. However, our findings contrast with Navaneethan et al. [65], who reported higher erectile dysfunction rates using the 15-item IIEF compared with IIEF-5, suggesting that the shorter IIEF-5 may be sufficient for routine clinical assessment, particularly in resource-limited settings. The IIEF and IIEF-5 scales have similar sensitivity (0.98 vs. 0.97) and specificity (0.88 for both) [68, 69]. The IIEF-5 scale, however, has a lower specificity in males with premature ejaculation, which may be falsely diagnosed as erectile dysfunction. Nevertheless, compared to the IIEF-5 scale, which primarily addresses erectile dysfunction, the complete 15-item IIEF scale is considered a more comprehensive tool [69]. Given the global shortage of nephrology workforce, especially in low- and middle-income countries [70, 71], simpler tools may improve the feasibility of sexual dysfunction screening in CKD care.

Our review showed that Sexual dysfunction is highly prevalent in CKD, especially in hemodialysis (77.6%) and predialysis patients (76.7%), compared with transplant recipients (56.9%). The persistently high prevalence of sexual dysfunction in CKD likely reflects intrinsic disease-related mechanisms involving vascular, hormonal, neurological, and psychological pathways, including uremia-associated endothelial dysfunction, hypogonadism, hyperprolactinemia, autonomic neuropathy, anemia, and psychosocial factors such as depression and fatigue [4, 72, 73]. Kidney transplantation may improve metabolic and psychological health and thereby enhance sexual function and satisfaction [74], whereas ongoing uremia, anemia, mood disorders, and mineral metabolism disturbances likely contribute to the higher burden observed in predialysis and dialysis populations. However, recent reports estimate that sexual dysfunction affects 20% to 50% of kidney transplant recipients, indicating that although transplantation improves overall health, it does not fully resolve related complications. This persistence is likely due to ongoing vascular changes, psychological factors, and the long-term effects of immunosuppressive therapy [75–77].

We observed a significant association between sexual dysfunction and depression severity in individuals with CKD, suggesting a likely bidirectional relationship in which each condition may exacerbate the other. This is consistent with previous studies by Thakurdesai et al. [78] and Akpan et al. [79]. The high prevalence of sexual dysfunction in CKD may also contribute to the reported global depression prevalence of 26.5% in this population [80].

Interestingly, our analyses demonstrated that sexual dysfunction in patients with CKD was significantly associated with poorer quality of life and depression severity, consistent with prior reports showing that poorer sexual health is closely related to reduced overall well-being in the general population [81–83]. Although based on a limited number of studies, these findings highlight the need for early recognition and management of sexual dysfunction in CKD to reduce its impact on mental health, quality of life, and overall outcomes. There remains an opportunity for interventional studies on sexual dysfunction in CKD [84], given the substantial disease burden.

This review has several strengths, including a large pooled sample across multiple continents and subgroup analyses by CKD stage, sex, and treatment modality, which enhance clinical interpretability. The absence of significant publication bias further supports the reliability of the findings. However, some limitations should be considered. Substantial heterogeneity likely reflects differences in study design, populations, and assessment tools. Most included studies were cross-sectional, limiting causal inference, and variability in measurement instruments may have introduced inconsistency. In addition, few studies examined correlations with depression and quality of life, only one addressed anxiety, and there was limited representation of predialysis and peritoneal dialysis populations. Because our search was restricted to PubMed and EMBASE, regional literature (e.g., LILACS) and discipline-specific nursing and psychology studies (CINAHL, PsycINFO) may have been missed — a limitation given the psychosocial scope of this topic. Although stratification of SD prevalence by CKD stage was desirable, it was not feasible because most included studies did not report stage-specific data—a gap that future primary studies should address.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that sexual dysfunction is highly prevalent in chronic kidney disease, affecting more than two-thirds of patients worldwide with CKD, with the highest burden in predialysis and hemodialysis populations and lower rates after kidney transplantation. It is consistently correlated with poorer quality of life and higher depression severity, underscoring its important psychosocial impact. These findings highlight the need for routine screening and integrated management of sexual dysfunction in CKD care to improve overall patient well-being.
