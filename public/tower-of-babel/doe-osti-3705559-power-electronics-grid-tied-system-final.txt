 ABSTRACT

Across the country, electric utilities are grappling with the persistent hurdles of integrating Distributed
Energy Resources (DERs). Managing these assets safely and effectively is a complex endeavor,
complicated by varying ownership structures, management philosophies, and the diversity of the
technologies themselves. Consequently, the industry has seen a proliferation of bespoke system designs,
control strategies, and communication frameworks—forcing utilities to spend significant time and
resources developing one-off integration solutions. This project addressed these integration hurdles
through a scalable demonstration of intelligent devices designed to coordinate and control diverse
resources in low-voltage applications. This concept minimized the need for complex integration by
transforming the separate DERs into a dispatchable virtual power plant (VPP) with integrated resiliency
functions (called a Node). By collaborating with a utility partner, the project focused on developing
rapidly implementable use cases that bridged the gap between theoretical control and real-world
deployment.

 1. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

This project emphasized two critical pillars for modernizing the electric grid and overcoming DER
integration hurdles: (1) an integrated ecosystem of power electronics and intelligent controllers capable of
synchronizing functions to satisfy both economic and resiliency objectives, and (2) the validation of this
platform through deployment in a live, real-world environment. To meet these targets, four challenges
had to be overcome to ensure the proof of principle product was achieved: (1) the deployment of a
scalable demonstration platforms with real-world environments for newly developed grid technology
testing; (2) advanced components and new packaging and assembly of subsystems required for a heavy,
customer interactive grid, (3) system integration solutions for in-field demonstrations of prototypes to
accelerate the adoption of the technologies while minimizing customer risks associated with such early
prototype demonstrations, and (4) integration methods for bridging the gap between high-risk research
and development and technology adoption by utilities.

 2. BENEFITS TO THE FUNDING DOE OFFICE MISSION

This work directly aligned with the DOE Office of Electricity’s (OE) mission by accelerating the
deployment of grid modernization solutions—a primary objective of the Grid Modernization Laboratory
Consortium (GMLC). The project facilitated more efficient resource utilization while fostering the
development of shared knowledge networks. Furthermore, by championing regional perspectives and
strengthening ties between local stakeholders and industry partners, this initiative bolstered the
foundational relationships necessary to advance a secure, resilient, and modern electric grid.

 3. TECHNICAL DISCUSSION OF WORK PERFORMED BY ALL PARTIES
Together, Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) and Southern Company Services (SCS) defined use
cases for solid state power substations (SSPS) which incorporated typical scenarios (price responsive
systems and resilient power delivery). These use cases have been validated in a computer-hardware-in-
the-loop (CHIL) environment. For proof-of-principle, two different scenarios were combined into a single
operational run at the Grid Research Innovation and Development Center (GRID-C) shown in Figure 1.
This work leveraged a previous collaboration with SCS, where economic power dispatch optimization of
a Node was the main objective (buy energy at a low cost, store, and use during a high cost period). In
general, this capability provided a means for a utility to use price signals to incentivize Node control
response (i.e. utility issues a high price signal when utility peak generation and equipment challenges are
present enforcing a response by the Node to reduce net consumption or to export power in supporting

 7

utility needs). In this work, the Node was a DC-coupled system with photovoltaic (PV), energy storage
(ES), load, and grid connections.

 Figure 1: Economic power dispatch and resiliency use cases.
The design of this VPP or Node is multi-hierarchical: 1) a resource management controller (RMC) for use
case operations and energy management (uses real-time measurements, forecasts, and utility price signals
to generate optimized control and power dispatch targets from each resource) 2) resource integration
controllers (RIC) to intercouple power electronics to energy assets such as ES, PV, and loads (bundling
the physical resource and its power converter into a single, abstracted system), and 3) converter
controllers that perform fast control for switching of power electronic converters with state machines and
multiple modes of operations. Each layer was integrated to be plug-and-play providing a rapid setup for
deployment, flexibility, and integration.
As shown in Figure 1, resiliency is also an included function for a Node. In the presented use case, a loss
of the primary grid (shown by an intentional grid fault) resulted in seamless islanded operations of the
system (the grid connected converter trips but the load power is maintained via the ES and PV.) The
resource controls provided stable DC voltage allowing for a re-optimization and adjustments in real-time
operations for sustainable system performance. This re-scheduling for off-grid made optimal use of the
available solar energy by storing and using the energy to cover the load during the extended outage run.
Following a restoration of solar production, PV was automatically curtailed to limit over charging the ES.
Upon grid restoration, energy was discharged from the Node back to the grid to support the high price
period and utility needs (operational model assumed providing power to the grid was desired if excess
energy was available, but this can be adjusted in the optimization depending on the desired behavior.)

testbed comprised of integrated converters, resources, and the control hierarchy previously described
coupled with the actual resources. This testbed incorporated ES, PV, grid tie capabilities, and load
inverter test capabilities that aligned with the previously established use cases present in CHIL.
A single-line diagram for the testbed, shown in Figure 2, illustrates the overall system Node
interconnection. The design featured a DC-interconnected node where all 800 to 1500 volts direct current
(Vdc) points were tied to a common, fused box for added safety.

outside the front of the building. The power is routed through the green interface box which provided an

remote RICs were accessible via ethernet and could be completely managed from the RMC. The control

 Figure 3: Testbed with source, resource, and load connections.

The existing distribution panels were reconfigured to accommodate the grid-tied interconnection and the
critical load panel. However, these panels were supplied by two independent transformers, resulting in
two separate grounding references. This configuration presented a serious ground loop challenge for the
various integrated assets.
To resolve this issue, the testbed was designed with only one common ground reference for all alternating
current (AC) power. The chassis grounds for all equipment were provided by the grid connection ground
point, while each asset was galvanically isolated and had its own reference to ground per each asset
requirement. This design also provided operational flexibility: the neutral-to-ground bond on the critical
load panel was temporarily left in place (making it the single ground reference), which allowed the panel
to operate normally from its existing main feed while the rest of the isolated system was tested
independently.
Isolation was achieved using an isolation transformer at the grid-tie and dual-active-bridge (DAB)
converters for the ES and PV ports. The DABs provided isolation by converting direct current (DC) into
high-frequency AC, passing it through a small transformer, and converting it back to DC. This means that
assets with different grounding schemes (such as a grounded PV array) and assets that are floating (which
is desired for battery safety) can safely connect to the same common DC bus without creating ground
faults.
The AC load distribution panel was prepared with multiple protected disconnects to support a future load
inverter for powering the entire panel or other local loads. To create realistic test conditions, existing
resistive and inductive load banks from the building's infrastructure were relocated to this distribution

 10

panel. A large heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) unit was also added to the panel to
provide a real-world load for validation.
The overall testbed was successfully developed and validated by connecting physical converters to their
resources respectively and configuring them within their independent RICs. In addition, a load inverter
and its RIC were modified and tied into the load distribution panel. The first node test run consisted of the
PV, ES, and grid.

For comparison (CHIL versus testbed), a direct correlation between datasets is not possible since solar
production and ES performance deviate from actual models in CHIL. However, the integrated functions
and architecture of the system are consistent and provide a means to demonstrate a consistent set of use
cases. At the conclusion of this project phase, challenges in safety considerations have not been addressed
to perform 24-hour operations, which remain an ongoing effort to establish.
To validate the two primary use cases, the RMC and dashboards were launched with optimization and
energy management functions were visually proven as fully operational. The integration was verified
through a rigorous, multi-step testing procedure, which demonstrated that the optimization algorithms
dynamically adapted to the configuration of actively connected assets. While the system was designed to
auto-launch agents during the boot sequence for standard deployments, these agents were manually
managed during the demonstration to provide control and visibility.

 4. PLANS FOR FUTURE COLLABORATION
ORNL and SCS plan to explore a follow-on CRADA to advance the integration of RMC and RIC
technologies with the testbed developed under this project, enabling critical utility use-case testing and
validation of MVDC technologies.

 5. Conclusion
ORNL and SCS successfully proved the RMC to be effective for coordination of multiple assets (PV, ES,
and grid) by communicating with their independent RICs. As demonstrated, the RMC could automatically
adopt new assets as they came online and generated meaningful, real-time optimizations based on the
active resources. Thereby the demonstration for low voltage applications validated the testbed's overall
design, including both the physical electrical layout and the control architecture of the HUB concept.

SCS visited the GRID-C, specifically Building 3114, where the testbed was developed, and the system
was demonstrated to the satisfaction of SCS. The overall system with the physical implementation of
prototype converters was discussed in relation to this testbed, and future deployment at the SCS facility in
Irondale, Alabama, was also discussed, as shown in Figure 15: SCS and ORNL Technical Discussion
