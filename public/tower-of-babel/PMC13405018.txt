Generative AI-enhanced ensemble model for predicting adolescent obesity in India: a school-based study in two southern districts of Karnataka
Authors: Jayashankar Rakshitha, Doddaiah Sunil Kumar, M Santhosh Kumar, Vanishri Arun, Kamidi Venkatesh Krishnamurthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent obesity is a growing concern in India, shaped by sociodemographic, behavioural, familial, sleep and mental health factors. Validated AI-based prediction models for adolescents are scarce. Generative AI (GenAI) offers opportunities to improve accuracy through synthetic data augmentation.MethodsA case-control, school-based study included 1292 adolescents aged 10–16 years from Mysuru and Chamarajanagar, selected via stratified random sampling. Data on sociodemographic, lifestyle, sleep, mental health, familial and anthropometric measures were collected using validated tools. The dataset was split into training (80%), validation (10%) and testing (10%). Conditional tabular generative adversarial network (CTGAN) generated 1000 synthetic samples and a variational autoencoder (VAE) produced 500, expanding training to 2532 records. Fidelity was assessed using Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests, principal component analysis (PCA), Wasserstein distance and privacy risk checks. A stacking ensemble combining XGBoost, TabNet and TabTransformer with logistic regression stacking was trained using 10-fold cross-validation.ResultsThe final model achieved an accuracy of 0.977, sensitivity 0.97, specificity 0.985, F1 score 0.97 and AUC 0.994. Calibration was strong (Brier score 0.042; Hosmer–Lemeshow p>0.05). SHAP and TabNet analyses highlighted screen time, sleep duration, parental obesity, physical activity and diet as key predictors.ConclusionThe GenAI-enhanced stacking ensemble model accurately predicts adolescent obesity, with strong calibration and interpretability. It identifies behavioural and familial determinants, supporting school and community deployment for early detection and prevention.Keywords: Adolescent, Obesity, Body Mass Index, Risk Assessment

Introduction

Adolescent obesity has emerged as a major global health issue, with rates climbing steadily in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Recent global estimates from the WHO indicate that more than 390 million children and adolescents aged 5–19 years were overweight in 2022, including approximately 160 million living with obesity, highlighting the accelerating global burden of adolescent obesity.1 In India, recent studies indicate that adolescent overweight and obesity rates range from 12% to 24%, driven by rapid lifestyle changes, urbanisation and greater exposure to obesogenic environments.2 The impacts of adolescent obesity carry into adulthood, increasing risks of early diabetes, hypertension and cardiometabolic diseases—highlighting the importance of early detection as a public health priority.3

The causes of adolescent obesity are complex, involving behavioural, familial and environmental factors. Indian research consistently links unhealthy diets, excessive screen time, low physical activity and poor sleep with increased adiposity in adolescents.2,4 When these risk behaviours cluster, they further heighten the risk of obesity. A recent multicentre study showed that low physical activity, poor sleep and high screen exposure often co-occur, significantly raising the chances of being overweight or obese.4 Parental obesity and metabolic diseases also influence adolescent risk, emphasising the need for multifaceted predictive strategies.

Despite the rising challenge, predictive models for adolescent obesity are still underdeveloped in India. Current approaches mainly use traditional statistical or classical machine-learning methods, which face limitations such as small datasets, class imbalance, limited generalisability and inadequate integration of behavioural and familial factors.5 Many existing tools also lack explainability, restricting their use in clinical and community settings. A major obstacle is the shortage of high-quality, balanced datasets needed to train reliable models, especially since obesity is less common than the normal-weight or overweight categories in population samples.

Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) offers a promising approach to addressing these issues. Techniques such as conditional tabular generative adversarial network (CTGAN) and variational autoencoders (VAEs) can produce realistic, privacy-preserving synthetic data that improve the representation of minority groups while maintaining key statistical relationships.6 These models have been successfully used to augment electronic health records, enhance rare disease prediction and address data imbalance in medical AI applications.6,7 Recent evidence suggests that CTGAN-based and VAE-based synthetic data augmentation can improve model learning, reduce overfitting and preserve complex non-linear feature relationships in healthcare prediction modelling, particularly in tabular clinical datasets with limited sample sizes.8–10 However, their application in predicting adolescent obesity remains largely unexplored, especially in low- and middle-income countries. To date, no Indian study has used synthetic data augmentation to create an adolescent obesity risk prediction model that combines advanced deep-learning architectures with comprehensive behavioural and familial factors. Recent reviews also highlight the growing role of explainable AI and tabular deep-learning architectures in improving predictive modelling performance and interpretability in adolescent obesity research.11,12

This study addresses the existing gap by developing a GenAI-enhanced stacking ensemble model that combines CTGAN-generated and VAE-generated synthetic data augmentation with advanced machine learning frameworks, including XGBoost, TabNet and TabTransformer. The main objectives are to develop a reliable predictive model for adolescent obesity using both real and synthetic datasets; validate it through internal, external and cross-validation methods; and improve its interpretability via Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values and attention-based explainability methods. By merging synthetic data augmentation, ensemble techniques and explainability tools, this research aims to advance methodological developments in adolescent obesity prediction and lay a scalable foundation for digital health initiatives in India.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study used a case-control, school-based approach conducted in selected private schools across the Mysuru and Chamarajanagar districts of Karnataka, India, from June 2024 to April 2025. It gathered real-world sociodemographic, behavioural (diet, physical activity and sedentary lifestyles), familial, mental health and well-being and sleep factors, along with anthropometric data related to adolescent obesity, to develop, train and externally validate a GenAI-enhanced machine-learning model for predicting obesity. Schools were chosen through a stratified random sampling method to represent both urban and rural areas, and within each school, systematic random sampling was employed to enrol eligible participants.

The reporting of this study was guided by both the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline for case-control studies and the TRIPOD+AI reporting framework for artificial intelligence-based prediction models (online supplemental file 2)

Study population

A total of 1292 adolescents aged 10–16 years participated in the study, matched 1:1. Inclusion criteria for age, gender and class included being aged 10–16, having written parental consent, participant assent and securing institutional approval. Exclusion criteria encompassed chronic illnesses, medical conditions affecting weight or unwillingness to take part. Anthropometric data were collected in accordance with WHO guidelines, with height and weight measured using calibrated stadiometers and electronic scales. Obesity classification was performed using the WHO 2007 BMI-for-age growth reference for children and adolescents aged 5–19 years. Adolescents with BMI-for-age z-scores greater than +2 SD above the WHO reference median were classified as obese.

Adolescents were selected from participating schools using probability proportionate to size (PPS) sampling. Following anthropometric assessment and obesity classification based on WHO BMI-for-age criteria, adolescents with BMI-for-age >+2 SD were classified as cases (obese) and non-obese adolescents as controls. Obesity classification was performed using the WHO 2007 BMI-for-age growth reference for children and adolescents aged 5–19 years. Adolescents with BMI-for-age >+2 SD were classified as obese. A 1:1 case-control allocation strategy was adopted to maintain balanced representation of obese and non-obese participants for predictive model development. The distribution of controls across age, sex and school class categories was reviewed to ensure adequate representation within the study population. Participant enrolment and allocation were performed after anthropometric assessment and obesity classification.

All data were encrypted and stored securely in Firebase Firestore, with access controlled by Firebase Authentication to ensure data integrity.

During preprocessing, data inconsistencies in categorical labels were corrected using automated text-embedding-based standardisation. Missing values were imputed via hybrid methods: K-nearest neighbours for continuous variables and a generative routine for categorical data. Continuous features were normalised with min-max scaling.

To prevent data leakage, variables involving BMI or related measures were excluded from predictors. Synthetic samples from augmentation were restricted to the training set. Correlation checks verified no high pre-model correlation between predictors and the target (|r|<0.25).

Patient and public involvement

School authorities, parents and adolescents participated early in the study to evaluate the feasibility, clarity and acceptability of the questionnaire and digital data collection platform. Input from adolescents and school representatives helped improve the wording, sequence and usability of questions on behaviour and lifestyle, thereby enhancing understanding and encouraging participation. Additionally, teaching, non-teaching staff and school administrators offered feedback on the practicality of conducting anthropometric measurements and digital data collection in school environments.

Participants did not take part in developing the machine learning algorithms, the synthetic data augmentation framework or the statistical modelling procedures. No interventions were carried out during the study. The time and effort needed to participate were informally evaluated during pilot testing to confirm they were manageable within regular school hours.

Dataset splitting and synthetic data augmentation

The dataset (n=1292) was split into training (80%, n=1032), validation (10%, n=130) and internal testing (10%, n=130) groups based on obesity status. To overcome sample size constraints, especially for complex deep-learning models, two synthetic data augmentation methods were used: 1. CTGAN: produced 1000 synthetic records by learning the conditional distributions of numerical and categorical variables. 2. Variational autoencoder (VAE): created 500 synthetic samples by mapping real records into a latent space and reconstructing realistic variants. The final training set, after synthetic augmentation, included 2532 samples.

The original dataset was first divided into training, validation and testing subsets using stratified sampling. CTGAN and VAE models were then trained exclusively on the 80% training partition, and the generated synthetic samples were added only to the training dataset. The validation, testing and external validation datasets contained only real observations and were not used during synthetic data generation or model training. This sequence was followed to prevent data leakage and reduce the risk of optimistic bias in model performance estimates (figure 1).

Feature engineering and selection

Starting with 35 original variables, additional composite features were developed to improve the model’s performance. These included scores for diet quality, physical activity, sedentary behaviour, Mental Health and sleep quality. Features derived from deep learning included 10 latent features from a neural autoencoder and five interaction embeddings from TabNet. Four complementary feature selection methods were used: Pearson/point-biserial correlations, recursive feature elimination with XGBoost, TabNet attention ranking and mutual information thresholds. Features were retained only if they ranked well across at least two methods and demonstrated strong domain relevance, resulting in a final set of 15 predictors.

Model development

A stacking ensemble was built with three base learners—XGBoost, TabNet and TabTransformer—selected for their complementary strengths in learning from tabular data. A logistic regression model functioned as the meta-learner. Hyperparameters for all base models were tuned using Bayesian optimisation with 50 trials in Optuna. Training employed a 10-fold stratified cross-validation to enhance stability and robustness.

Final hyperparameter settings for all machine learning and synthetic data generation models are provided in online supplemental file 1.

External validation

To assess generalisability, an external dataset comprising 300 adolescents from two additional schools in Mysuru was gathered using the same procedures. No synthetic data was added to this dataset. The model’s performance was evaluated based on accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, negative predictive value, F1 score, area under the curve (AUC), calibration slope and intercept, Brier score and Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit statistics.

Explainability

To promote transparency and uphold ethical standards, we examined the model’s decision-making using SHAP for both overall and individual analyses. Global SHAP assessments measured the influence of each predictor, whereas local SHAP plots explained sample-specific risk factors. Additionally, TabNet attention masks provided further insights into how features were prioritised during decision steps.

Data analysis

Data analysis involved summarising the variables with means and SD or frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between obese and non-obese adolescents were made using χ2 tests. The dataset was divided into training (80%), validation (10%) and testing (10%) subsets via stratified sampling to maintain balanced class proportions. The training set was then augmented with CTGAN and VAE models, and the quality of the synthetic data was verified via distributional similarity checks prior to model development. Predictive performance was evaluated using metrics including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, Negative Predictive Value (NPV), F1-score and AUC, along with confusion matrices for each data split. Model robustness was assessed via 10-fold stratified cross-validation, yielding a mean accuracy of 0.984±0.005. Calibration assessments included Brier scores, calibration curves and observed-versus-predicted plots, while log-loss quantified prediction uncertainty. Model interpretability was explored using SHAP values for global and individual feature contributions, and TabNet attention masks were reviewed to visualise feature-focused patterns. All analyses and model development procedures were performed using Python (version 3.10). Major libraries included pandas and NumPy for data handling, scikit-learn for preprocessing and evaluation, XGBoost for gradient boosting, PyTorch and TensorFlow-based frameworks for deep learning architectures, CTGAN and SDV libraries for synthetic data generation, Optuna for Bayesian hyperparameter optimisation, SHAP for explainability analyses and Matplotlib/Seaborn for visualisation. Model training and validation were conducted in a reproducible computational environment using fixed random seeds where applicable. Additional technical details regarding the computational environment, analytical workflow, model implementation and optimisation procedures are provided in online supplemental file 1.

As the model was developed using a balanced case-control dataset with equal representation of obese and non-obese adolescents, prevalence-dependent measures should be interpreted cautiously. Discrimination-based metrics such as accuracy, AUC, sensitivity and specificity remain appropriate for evaluating the model’s ability to distinguish between obese and non-obese participants within the study setting. Calibration-related measures including Brier score, log-loss and observed-versus-predicted analyses were therefore interpreted within the sampled study distribution rather than as direct population-level risk estimates. Although the model demonstrated acceptable internal and external probabilistic consistency, predictive probabilities and calibration behaviour may differ in broader populations with varying obesity prevalence patterns. Accordingly, despite stable internal and external validation performance, further prospective population-based validation and recalibration may improve probability estimation and support wider public health deployment. As the dataset used an exact 1:1 balanced case-control distribution, Precision-Recall (PR) curves were not prioritised, as PR analysis is particularly informative in highly imbalanced classification settings where positive class prevalence is substantially lower than the negative class.

Results

Participant and dataset overview

The study included 1292 adolescents aged 10–16 years. The dataset was divided into training (1032 samples, expanded to 2532 after synthetic augmentation), validation (130 samples) and testing (130 samples) sets. The augmented training set used for model training consisted of 2532 samples—1266 obese and 1266 non-obese—generated using CTGAN (1000 samples) and VAE (500 samples), followed by quality filtering.

The study involved adolescents aged 10–16 years with an equal distribution of obese and non-obese participants across all categories. Among those aged 10–13 years, 290 were obese and 290 were non-obese, while in the 14–16 year-olds, there were 356 participants. in each group. Gender distribution was also balanced, with 315 obese and 315 non-obese males and 331 obese and 331 non-obese females. Similarly, 154 students in each of the fifth–seventh standards and 492 students in each of the eighth–tenth standards were classified as obese and non-obese. This ensured a proportionate and comparable sample for further analysis.

Calibration, log-loss and Brier score

To assess probabilistic calibration and overall predictive uncertainty, we calculated log-loss and Brier scores for the stacking ensemble. The log-loss values were 0.018 on training, 0.079 on validation and 0.062 on testing, reflecting low predictive entropy and strong generalisation. Brier scores were 0.012 (training), 0.048 (validation) and 0.039 (testing), indicating well-calibrated probability predictions.

Calibration plots (online supplemental file 1) with isotonic regression showed slight overconfidence in the highest-probability bins on the training set, while observed and predicted risks aligned well in validation and test sets. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test results were non-significant (validation: χ² = 6.12, df=8, p=0.63; testing: χ² = 7.05, df=8, p=0.53), supporting the absence of substantial miscalibration on unseen data. An observed-vs-predicted decile analysis further confirms reasonable calibration, with predicted probabilities within each decile closely matching the actual obesity rates, with differences of less than five percentage points across the validation and test sets.

Overall calibration seems adequate, with small differences between predicted and actual risks across deciles that are clinically acceptable. This confirms that the model’s probability estimates are reliable for making decisions.

Synthetic data validation (fidelity, diversity, privacy, bias)

Given the importance of synthetic augmentation in model training, we assessed synthetic data across four key aspects.

Fidelity

Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) tests comparing real and synthetic continuous features showed median KS statistics below 0.09 (range 0.02–0.09); no feature exceeded 0.12. χ² tests on categorical distributions resulted in p-values above 0.05 for most categories after Bonferroni correction, indicating no systematic distribution shifts.

Diversity

Wasserstein distances between real and synthetic marginal distributions ranged from 0.12 to 0.19, with lower values indicating higher similarity. PCA and UMAP visualisations showed that synthetic samples blended well with real samples in low-dimensional space, confirming that the generative models captured the data structure without forming separate clusters.

Privacy

Membership-inference (black-box attack simulations) yielded success rates close to random chance (≈52%), suggesting low memorisation of individual records. Nearest-neighbour distance ratios exceeding 1.2 across all tests supported that the synthetic data were not mere copies of real data.

Bias checks

Comparing distributions across groups (gender, class, etc) showed no evidence of increased disparities in the synthetic data. Differences in prevalence by gender and urban/rural status before and after augmentation were less than 1.5 percentage points.

External validation performance

External validation using an independent dataset of 300 participants demonstrated stable predictive performance. The model achieved an overall accuracy of 0.95, a sensitivity of 0.94 and a specificity of 0.95. Precision and NPV were 0.95 and 0.94, respectively, while the F1-score was 0.945. Discussion

This study created and externally tested a machine learning model enhanced with GenAI to forecast adolescent obesity among Indian schoolchildren. The model showed excellent discrimination, calibration and fairness. Its high internal accuracy (0.977–0.99) and solid external validation results (accuracy 0.95; AUC 0.97) suggest it is both reliable and broadly applicable. These results demonstrated in predicting adolescent obesity in India, where AI-based screening tools remain scarce.

The study reported that external validation in an independent cohort demonstrated stable predictive performance, with only minor variation compared with internal testing results. Such differences may reflect variability in behavioural patterns, sociodemographic composition, regional characteristics or measurement conditions across independent adolescent populations.

The recent national surveys reported adolescent obesity rates of 19–25% across various Indian states.13,14 Similar increasing trends in adolescent obesity are observed internationally, such as in the United States and South Korea, mainly due to lifestyle changes, sedentary habits and unhealthy diets.15,16 Our findings identified key behavioural factors—physical inactivity, extended screen time, limited sleep and frequent intake of energy-dense foods—as the main contributors, as revealed by SHAP analyses. These factors are consistent with earlier epidemiological research emphasising lifestyle patterns as the leading modifiable causes of childhood obesity.17–19

The use of advanced AI techniques, such as CTGAN, VAE, TabNet and the TabTransformer offered significant methodological benefits. Recent studies have similarly reported that synthetic data generation using GAN- and VAE-based frameworks may improve predictive modelling performance and reduce overfitting in healthcare datasets with limited sample sizes.20 Synthetic data augmentation increased sample diversity while maintaining distributional integrity, as shown by low KS statistics and strong overlap in PCA/UMAP analyses. This aligns with the findings of Xu et al, who found that CTGAN-generated data improves model performance in small health datasets.21 Additionally, the model’s high calibration, indicated by favourable Brier scores and non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow p-values, highlights its potential use in adolescent obesity screening—an uncommon achievement for predictive models targeting adolescents in low-resource environments.22

Few studies have integrated GenAI with ensemble learning for obesity prediction. Prior research using traditional machine learning models—such as Logistic Regression, Random Forest or SVM—reported moderate accuracies ranging from 0.72 to 0.88.3,23,24 Conversely, this study achieved significantly higher predictive metrics, demonstrating the advantages of multimodal generative augmentation combined with an ensemble of tabular deep learning models. Importantly, it addresses a critical challenge in digital health AI research: the absence of external validation. Our independent external evaluation confirmed the model’s generalisability, with performance comparable to that observed in internal testing. This distinguishes our work from previous Indian and international studies, many of which relied exclusively on internal validation.3,25

Fairness analyses revealed minimal differences in performance across sex, age groups and demographic divisions (ΔAUC<0.02), indicating that the model does not unfairly misclassify vulnerable subgroups. This is important since AI systems can sometimes mirror sociocultural biases if fairness is not actively assessed.26 The lack of detectable algorithmic bias further supports the tool’s public health relevance.

Explainability was another key strength. SHAP and TabNet attention maps clearly highlighted physical activity, screen time, sleep duration, junk food consumption and parental obesity as the main features shaping predictions. These results align with established behavioural science and clinical research27,28 and help build clinician trust and transparency—both vital for the ethical use of AI in children.

To our knowledge, this is among the first Indian school-based studies to integrate Generative AI-driven synthetic augmentation with externally validated ensemble deep learning models for adolescent obesity prediction, advancing digital adolescent health research. With rising rates of youth obesity and limited local predictive tools, this model suggests potential applicability for integration into school screening programmes, mobile health apps and targeted prevention efforts.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

This study highlights several key methodological and practical strengths. It uses a comprehensive generative-AI framework, integrating CTGAN and VAE to augment minority classes and enhance model generalisation—an approach rarely seen in adolescent health research. The multimodel architecture—merging TabNet, TabTransformer, XGBoost and stacking ensembles—delivers high predictive accuracy with robust calibration and minimal overfitting. Additionally, employing SHAP, attention maps and fairness metrics ensures the model is interpretable, trustworthy and unbiased across subgroups, aligning with international AI ethics standards. Notably, external validation using an independent dataset bolsters credibility and promotes potential applicability, addressing a significant gap in prior obesity prediction studies.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, the study has limitations. While synthetic data was rigorously validated, larger real-world multi-site datasets would further improve generalisability. Longitudinal validation is necessary to assess predictive stability over time. External validation was conducted at a single regional site; expanding to diverse geographic and sociocultural contexts across India would strengthen its national relevance. Additionally, although the model shows high fairness, ongoing bias monitoring is vital before integrating it into mobile or clinical decision systems. Future research should also consider federated learning to facilitate privacy-preserving multi-institutional data sharing.

The potential utility observed in this study should be interpreted cautiously and may partly reflect the balanced case-control design, enriched behavioural and engineered composite feature representation, advanced stacking ensemble learning architecture and synthetic augmentation applied exclusively within the training dataset. Synthetic data augmentation and feature engineering may have enhanced class separability and strengthened model learning, potentially contributing to optimistic performance estimates. However, synthetic augmentation was restricted to the training dataset, while independent validation, testing, cross-validation and external validation were conducted on real-world data. Although these validation approaches demonstrated stable predictive performance, some degree of performance inflation due to enriched feature representation and controlled sampling conditions cannot be entirely ruled out. Furthermore, predictive metrics and probability calibration may differ in broader real-world population settings with varying behavioural distributions and obesity prevalence patterns. Therefore, further confirmation in larger, purely real-world, population-based, multi-centre datasets without synthetic augmentation is warranted to assess model generalisability, transportability, calibration stability and real-world implementation performance.

Also, the study compared multiple advanced AI-based tabular learning architectures; comparative evaluation against conventional regression-based approaches was not performed and may be considered in future validation studies using larger real-world datasets.

Although synthetic data augmentation improved class balance and model training stability, the generative process may have inherited or potentially amplified underlying selection, measurement or sampling biases present in the original dataset. Therefore, residual bias cannot be completely excluded despite the fidelity, privacy and subgroup consistency assessments performed during synthetic data validation.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and externally validated a GenAI–powered stacking ensemble model to predict adolescent obesity in an Indian school population.

By combining CTGAN-based and VAE-based synthetic data augmentation with advanced deep learning models for tabular data, the model demonstrated utility for discrimination, calibration and fairness across internal, validation and external datasets. Explainability analyses showed that the model’s predictions matched known behavioural and familial factors associated with obesity, thereby improving transparency and clinical understanding. However, predictive performance across internal and external validation datasets may support school-based obesity risk stratification, provided further prospective population-based validation and recalibration are conducted.

The study employs GenAI, fairness checks and external validation for adolescent obesity prediction. This model offers a solid base for scalable digital screening in schools and community health programmes. The results emphasise the potential of GenAI-driven predictive tools for early risk detection and targeted prevention in low- and middle-income contexts.

Public health implications

This study emphasises the potential of AI-driven risk prediction to address India’s rising rates of adolescent obesity. It shows that a GenAI-enhanced model can achieve high accuracy and consistent calibration across the validation and testing phases, offering the opportunity to shift public health from reactive to proactive strategies. Early detection of at-risk adolescents allows for timely behavioural counselling, nutrition advice and family-centred interventions—most effective when introduced before obesity becomes deeply rooted.

Synthetic data generation has notable implications for public health in low- and middle-income countries. GenAI enables the development of high-quality predictive models even with limited, fragmented or geographically confined datasets—common characteristics of India’s school health data. This approach empowers regions with limited epidemiological resources to use advanced analytics for population monitoring, democratising access to precision public health tools.

From an implementation perspective, the model’s robust performance and explainability make it suitable for integration into school health platforms, digital adolescent health services and mobile screening apps. This can greatly reduce the screening workload for teachers and health workers while enhancing coverage and promptness. Additionally, personalised risk profiles facilitate tailored care pathways, enabling programmes to focus on high-risk students more efficiently.

At a policy level, these predictive tools support data-driven planning through risk stratification across geographic regions, socioeconomic groups and behavioural patterns. Such insights can inform resource distribution for nutrition initiatives, urban planning to encourage physical activity, targeted school interventions and the integration of digital health solutions into routine adolescent care. As obesity continues to rise in India, scalable, transparent AI-powered surveillance systems, like the one developed here, could contribute to shaping future adolescent health policies and prevention strategies.

Recommendations

Based on the findings, several recommendations are outlined. The state and district-level school health programmes should consider adopting AI-driven obesity prediction tools to facilitate early detection and targeted interventions. Future research should emphasise multi-site data collection, longitudinal studies and model adaptation across age groups to improve generalisability. Integrating the model into a mobile or web-based digital screening platform with behavioural nudges can help teachers, parents and health workers monitor adolescent health more effectively.

Although the study compared multiple advanced AI-based tabular learning architectures, additional comparisons with simpler conventional baseline models trained exclusively on real-world data may further clarify the trade-offs among predictive performance, computational complexity and deployment feasibility in resource-constrained public health settings.

Policymakers are advised to incorporate AI-based risk stratification into national adolescent health initiatives, such as Rashtriya Kishori Swasthya Karyakram, to optimise resource use. Regular fairness assessments and external audits are essential to ensure ethical implementation. Finally, using federated or privacy-preserving AI architectures can further enhance scalability across India.
