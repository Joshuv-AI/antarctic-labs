Advances and challenges in the management of haemoptysis in the emergency department: A narrative review
Authors: Prakash Ranjan Mishra, Khushboo Choudhury, Bharath Gopinath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHaemoptysis, defined as the expectoration of blood from the lower respiratory tract, can present to the emergency department (ED) with symptoms ranging from mild, self-limiting to massive haemorrhage. Severe presentations account for approximately 5% of cases, with mortality primarily because airway flooding with blood leading to hypoxia. This underscores the importance of early airway protection and structured resuscitation in emergency settings. This narrative review summarizes the current evidence to guide emergency physicians in the management of haemoptysis. Aetiologies vary geographically: pulmonary tuberculosis accounts for up to 40% of cases in low- and middle-income countries, whereas malignancy, cardiovascular disease, and bronchiectasis predominate in high-income regions. Management begins with differentiating haemoptysis from pseudohaemoptysis and gastrointestinal bleeding, followed by risk stratification and initial stabilization of the patient. CT angiography helps in localization of bleeding and underlying pathology, while flexible bronchoscopy is the gold standard for both diagnosis and therapeutic interventions. Nebulized tranexamic acid is emerging as a rapid, localized, and safe adjunct in emergency settings, reducing the need for invasive procedures. Haemostasis can be achieved by bronchial artery embolization in over 90% of cases, while surgery is reserved for refractory or localized disease. Despite these advances, challenges remain in optimizing the timing and integration of imaging, pharmacological adjuncts, and invasive procedures. A multidisciplinary approach involving emergency physicians, pulmonologists, interventional radiologists, and thoracic surgeons is crucial to improving outcomes in this complex, potentially life-threatening condition. Further studies are needed to standardize protocols and priorities for emergency management of massive haemoptysis.

INTRODUCTION

Haemoptysis is defined as the expectoration of blood originating from the lower respiratory tract. It is a potentially life-threatening clinical presentation that requires urgent evaluation in the emergency department. Its severity can range from mild, self-limited streaks to massive haemorrhage, which can rapidly cause airway obstruction, respiratory failure, and haemodynamic collapse. About 5% of haemoptysis cases are severe and need immediate intervention.[1] Mortality results from asphyxiation rather than from blood loss itself. This underscores the need for early airway protection and resuscitation using the ABC (airway, breathing, circulation) approach.[123]

The aetiologies of haemoptysis vary with geography. Globally, pulmonary infections are the most common cause, accounting for 40% in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC).[23] Most bleeding arises from hypertrophied bronchial arteries under systemic pressure, formed due to chronic inflammation, infection, or neoplastic invasion.[45] There have been recent advances in CT angiography, flexible bronchoscopy, pharmacological adjuncts such as nebulized tranexamic acid, and bronchial artery embolization in the management of haemoptysis. However, the optimal integration of these in an emergency setting remains uncertain. This review highlights the best practices and summarizes the current evidence to guide emergency physicians in the management of a patient with haemoptysis in the emergency room.

EPIDEMIOLOGY AND AETIOLOGY

Haemoptysis has a multifactorial aetiology with notable geographic variation. Infectious causes dominate globally, with pulmonary tuberculosis leading in LMICs, such as India, where it accounts for approximately 40% to 79% of all haemoptysis presentations.[67] In high-income countries, lung malignancies, particularly lung cancer, are a major contributor.[8] In sub-Saharan Africa, HIV-associated pulmonary infections, such as pneumonia and tuberculosis, are significant.[9]

Other causes include neoplastic metastases, cardiovascular conditions (mitral stenosis, heart failure, pulmonary embolism), and vascular abnormalities, such as arteriovenous malformations or Rasmussen’s aneurysms. Coagulopathies from anticoagulation or intrinsic disorders, inflammatory diseases like granulomatosis with polyangiitis, and less common factors, such as trauma, foreign body aspiration, and iatrogenic injury, also contribute to haemoptysis [Table 1].

PATHO-PHYSIOLOGY OF HAEMOPTYSIS

The lungs receive a dual blood supply. Pulmonary arteries provide approximately 99% of the total blood flow to the lungs, carrying deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle for gas exchange in the alveoli. In contrast, bronchial arteries account for about 1% of the lung’s blood supply, delivering oxygenated blood from the systemic circulation to nourish the airways, connective tissue, and supporting structures. While the pulmonary arteries are essential for gas exchange, the bronchial arteries play a critical role in maintaining airway integrity and are most often implicated in clinically significant haemoptysis due to their higher pressure and potential for hypertrophy and rupture in chronic lung diseases.

About 90% of clinically significant haemoptysis arises from rupture of the bronchial arteries, which supply the airways under systemic arterial pressure, making them inherently more fragile during chronic pulmonary inflammation, infection, or neoplastic growth.[10] Chronic diseases—including tuberculosis and bronchiectasis—cause persistent injury and irritation of airway and lung tissue, triggering local angiogenic mediators that promote hypertrophy, neovascularization, and dilatation of bronchial arteries. The resulting vessels are friable; they may rupture spontaneously or following small trauma, especially where cavitary lung lesions spill into adjacent hypertrophied arteries, producing abrupt and often massive bleeding. In bronchiectasis, repetitive cycles of infection and inflammation cause airway wall remodelling and abnormal vascular proliferation, perpetuating a cycle of bleeding and further tissue damage [Figure 1].

Pulmonary artery involvement accounts for only about 5% of cases of haemoptysis,[11] most commonly arising in the setting of pulmonary embolism, where arterial obstruction leads to parenchymal infarction and alveolar haemorrhage. Rasmussen’s aneurysm—a pseudoaneurysm of pulmonary artery branching next to tuberculous cavities—represents a classic, albeit rare, cause of catastrophic bleeding.[1213] Additional sources include non-bronchial systemic arteries (such as the subclavian, intercostal, and internal mammary), which collectively contribute a further 5% of haemoptysis cases but tend to be implicated most in advanced pulmonary pathology and post-surgical states.[14]

APPROACH TO PATIENT

History and physical examination

A quick history and thorough physical examination are essential for determining the cause and severity of haemoptysis. The duration and severity of symptoms help in narrowing down the potential causes. Acute haemoptysis (<2 weeks) is often associated with infections like pneumonia, bronchitis, or tuberculosis, whereas chronic haemoptysis is usually seen in malignancy, bronchiectasis, or vasculitis. Associated symptoms, such as fever, weight loss, night sweats, or recurrence of haemoptysis, suggest malignancy or tuberculosis.

A history of smoking, recent travel, prior infections, or anticoagulant use also needs to be elicited. A significant smoking history increases the risk of malignancy and chronic lung diseases, whereas recent travel may suggest endemic infections, such as tuberculosis or paragonimiasis. The use of anticoagulants or antiplatelet therapy can also cause haemoptysis.

Distinguishing true haemoptysis from pseudohaemoptysis or gastrointestinal bleeding is vital in guiding management. Haemoptysis originates from the lungs and appears typically bright red, frothy, and alkaline, whereas haematemesis from gastrointestinal sources is darker, mixed with food particles, and acidic. Pseudohaemoptysis (epistaxis or oral bleeding) usually arises from the upper airways and can be ruled out with oral cavity, nose, and throat examination.

Risk stratification

Severity classification of haemoptysis varies across the literature, with different amount of bleeding and terminology.[1516171819] To standardize and provide clarity for emergency care, this review adopts a consensus framework dividing haemoptysis into mild, moderate, and severe categories based on expectorated blood volume and clinical impact.

Some experts advocate for the use of the term ‘life-threatening’ haemoptysis instead of massive haemoptysis, for patient presenting with hypoxia, respiratory failure, or hypotension, regardless of the total volume of expectorated blood.[1920]

It is important to note that volume alone may underestimate the severity of the condition. In patients with limited pulmonary reserve, smaller quantities of bleeding can result in life-threatening airway compromise or asphyxiation. Therefore, clinical judgement must be based on patient’s comorbidities, respiratory status, and haemodynamic parameters along with the volume of bleeding.

EMERGENCY MANAGEMENT

The management of haemoptysis in the emergency department pivots on the ABC approach—securing the airway, maintaining adequate breathing, and stabilizing circulation [Figure 2].

Airway

Airway management is the foremost priority in patients presenting with massive haemoptysis. Airway obstruction can be caused by blood clots, active bleeding, or aspiration. Failure to secure the airway promptly can result in rapid respiratory failure and death. Patients with massive or persistent bleeding, ongoing respiratory distress, altered mental status, or haemodynamic instability require urgent airway interventions.

Breathing

After securing the airway, ensuring adequate oxygenation and ventilation is paramount to prevent hypoxic injury. Supplemental oxygen should be administered to maintain oxygen saturation (SpO2) above 92%, using oxygen delivery devices—ranging from nasal cannula to non-rebreather masks.[1]

Mechanical ventilation is required in patients with respiratory failure or significant respiratory distress. Lung-protective strategies similar to those used in ARDS are recommended.[29] Ventilation should be initiated with tidal volumes of 6–8 mL/kg ideal body weight to reduce barotrauma and avoid worsening bleeding from high airway pressures. FiO2 and PEEP should be titrated with starting PEEP of 5–8 cm H2O, to optimize alveolar recruitment and oxygenation while minimizing intrathoracic pressures that could aggravate haemorrhage.[30] Respiratory rate would be adjusted according to the tidal volume to maintain the minute ventilation of the patient.

Non-invasive ventilation (NIV) may be cautiously attempted in selected patients without airway compromise or active massive haemoptysis; however, because NIV does not provide airway protection against aspiration of blood and secretions, it risks delaying definitive airway management and is generally not favoured in haemorrhagic cases.[31] Early endotracheal intubation with secure airway control remains the preferred option for patients with ongoing or large-volume haemoptysis to facilitate suctioning and safe ventilation.

Circulation

Haemodynamic compromise, though uncommon in haemoptysis, should not be underestimated, especially in cases involving rapid or recurrent blood loss. Haemodynamic stabilization requires rapid assessment and intervention to prevent shock and organ hypoperfusion. Vital signs monitoring, assessment of capillary refill, urine output, and mental status should be conducted to identify signs of circulatory compromise. Establishment of at least two large-bore intravenous access sites is necessary for fluid resuscitation and administration of blood products or medications.

Systematic reviews of randomized trials support isotonic crystalloids as the first-line fluid for restoring volume and maintaining perfusion.[3233] Colloids, though historically used, show no proven benefit over crystalloids and carry risks, such as coagulopathy and renal impairment.[34] Blood transfusions with packed red blood cells are indicated when there is significant anaemia or ongoing bleeding compromising oxygen delivery. A Cochrane review for red blood cell transfusion recommends haemoglobin levels around 7–8 g/dL in stable patients to balance the risks of anaemia against transfusion-related complications. The American Association of Blood Banks recommends to consider the overall clinical context of the patient before making a transfusion decision.[34] A higher threshold can be considered and decisions should be individualized based on haemodynamic stability, ongoing bleeding, and patient comorbidities.[34]

Recognition and reversal of coagulopathy are vital, particularly in patients on anticoagulant therapy. For vitamin K antagonists (warfarin), reversal includes vitamin K administration and prothrombin complex concentrates (PCCs) for rapid correction. PCCs are better than FFP due to faster reversal, lower volume administration, and reduced risk of volume overload. For heparin, protamine sulphate remains the antidote. For direct-acting oral anticoagulants (DOACs), specific reversal agents are employed where available, such as idarucizumab for dabigatran and andexanet alfa for factor Xa inhibitors. In settings where these agents are unavailable, supportive care and PCC may be considered.[35]

Tranexamic acid in the management of haemoptysis: systemic and localized approaches

Tranexamic acid (TXA) is a synthetic lysine analogue that inhibits fibrinolysis by blocking the lysine-binding sites on plasminogen, thereby preventing plasmin formation and stabilizing the clot and reducing bleeding. Intravenous TXA has demonstrated efficacy in reducing bleeding duration and volume in haemoptysis. TXA decreases the frequency and quantity of sub-massive haemoptysis.[36] However, systemic administration carries a theoretical risk of thrombosis and requires intravenous access, which may not be feasible in all emergency scenarios.[37]

Nebulized TXA delivers the drug directly to the respiratory tract, achieving high local concentrations while minimizing systemic exposure. This targeted drug delivery reduces the risk of systemic thromboembolic complications, making it a safer and more effective option. Clinical studies, including randomized trials and meta-analyses, report early bleeding cessation, shorter hospital stays, and reduced need for invasive procedures.[3839] In one RCT, 96% of patients receiving nebulized TXA achieved complete resolution within five days, compared with 50% in the placebo group.[40]

A rare minor side effect reported with nebulized TXA is bronchospasm, which can be easily treated with a bronchodilator.[38] Typical dosing of the nebulized form is 500 mg in 5 mL normal saline, three to four times daily.[4142] Its ease of administration and effectiveness make it a practical, non-invasive option in emergency and critical care, especially for mild-to-moderate haemoptysis or as a temporizing measure before definitive therapy.

DEFINITIVE MANAGEMENT AND AETIOLOGY IDENTIFICATION

Imaging

Radiological imaging helps in localizing bleeding source, identifying underlying aetiologies, and guiding subsequent interventions. The choice of modality depends on haemodynamic stability, available resources, and the urgency of intervention.

Chest radiograph (CXR) is the first radiological imaging of choice due to its accessibility and rapidity. It can detect parenchymal infiltrates, cavitary lesions, or masses and help lateralize the bleeding side. However, its diagnostic yield is limited—sensitivity of 52% for localizing bleeding and 25% for identifying the aetiology.[4344] Consequently, a normal chest X-ray does not rule out significant pathology, and further imaging should be performed when clinical suspicion persists.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography with bronchial angiography (CTBA) is the imaging modality of choice in stable patients. It offers high diagnostic accuracy, with 90–98% sensitivity and nearly 100% specificity for bleeding source localization.[45] CT angiography provides precise localization and guides interventional procedures by visualization of bronchial and non-bronchial systemic arteries, collaterals, hypertrophied vessels, and pseudoaneurysms. Other causes of haemoptysis, such as neoplasms, inflammatory, or infectious lesions, can also be identified through CT imaging.

Flexible bronchoscopy allows direct visualization of the tracheobronchial tree, suctioning of clots, and localization of the active bleeding site. Sensitivity for detecting the bleeding source is approximately 70%, though its diagnostic accuracy for aetiology is lower (30–50%) and depends on timing.[4647] Bronchoscopy is especially beneficial when performed within 48 hours of the haemoptysis episode.[46] Early bronchoscopy improved bleeding source detection compared with delayed exam (site: 36% vs. 25%; lobe: 46% vs. 34%; lung: 51% vs. 38%), but did not significantly increase overall diagnostic yield (53% vs. 45%; P = 0.10).[46]

Topical measures, such as cold saline lavage and diluted epinephrine (1:10,000), provide immediate, though temporary, haemostasis through vasoconstriction. Caution is advised with epinephrine due to potential systemic absorption and arrhythmogenicity.[27] A double-blind randomized controlled trial comparing TXA and adrenaline for controlling iatrogenic bleeding during flexible bronchoscopy found no significant difference between the two agents.[48] These agents primarily serve as a bridge to definitive therapies like embolization or surgery.

Interventional radiology

Bronchial artery embolization (BAE) is the first-line definitive therapy for persistent, severe, or life-threatening haemoptysis once the patient is stabilized. It targets hypertrophied bronchial and non-bronchial systemic arteries responsible for most cases of significant bleeding. BAE is minimally invasive, rapidly effective, repeatable, and associated with shorter hospital stays and lower morbidity compared to surgical options. Technical success rates exceed 90%, with immediate bleeding control achieved in approximately 80–95% of cases.[495051]

Common complications include transient chest or back pain occurring in 34% to 91% of patients and dysphagia reported in up to 30%.[52] Serious but rare risks include spinal cord ischaemia (0.19%), and the use of microcatheters for super selective catheterization can markedly reduce this risk.[53] Long-term success ranges from 70% to 85%, with a recurrence rate of 23.7%.[495154] A meta-analysis, including 21 studies with 2,511 patients, reported a pooled recurrence-related mortality rate of 0.2%.[54] Recurrence of haemoptysis may result from incomplete occlusion, recanalization, collateral vessel formation, or progression of the underlying disease. Recurrence rates vary depending on the aetiology, with higher frequencies observed in patients with tuberculosis, aspergilloma, and cystic fibrosis.[54] Repeat embolization can be considered for recurrent bleeding.

Surgical considerations

Surgical intervention is reserved for patients with life-threatening haemoptysis unresponsive to embolization or with localized, resectable pathology, such as aspergilloma, malignancy, or destroyed lung.[55] Surgical mortality rates vary widely, ranging from 2.4% to 11.9%, depending on timing (emergency vs elective), extent of resection, and patient comorbidities.[5556] Elective limited resections and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) approaches have demonstrated lower mortality and morbidity. Emergency surgeries and extensive procedures, such as pneumonectomy, carry a higher risk.[55]

DISPOSITION

The disposition of patients presenting with haemoptysis should be based on the severity of bleeding and haemodynamic stability. Those with mild, self-limiting haemoptysis and stable vital signs may be safely discharged and advised appropriate outpatient follow-up. Moderate cases will require admission for close monitoring, etiological workup, and possible bronchoscopy or CT imaging. Severe or life-threatening haemoptysis will need prompt airway protection, haemodynamic monitoring, and multidisciplinary management involving emergency physicians, pulmonology, interventional radiology, intensive care, and thoracic surgery.

OUTCOMES AND PROGNOSIS

The prognosis of haemoptysis depends on the severity of bleeding, the underlying aetiology, and the timing of intervention. Mild haemoptysis in otherwise stable patients often resolves spontaneously, whereas severe or massive haemoptysis is associated with significant morbidity and mortality. In most fatal cases, death results from asphyxiation caused by flooding of the airways with blood, which underscores the importance of early airway protection and prompts haemorrhage control.[49]

GAPS IN EVIDENCE AND AREAS FOR FUTURE RESEARCH

Despite significant advances in imaging and therapeutic interventions, uncertainties persist regarding the optimal timing and sequencing of these procedures in the emergency management of haemoptysis. There is a lack of clear evidence on when to prioritize CT angiography versus bronchoscopy, or the most appropriate sequence of these diagnostic tools. Additionally, well-defined clinical pathways for escalating care from medical management to embolization or surgery are still not established. The optimal dosing of tranexamic acid dosing also remains unclear. Further studies are required to develop risk-based algorithms, investigate the role and optimal dosing of nebulized tranexamic acid, assess the utility of early bronchoscopy across different severity categories, and evaluate long-term outcomes associated with emerging therapies.

CONCLUSION

Haemoptysis is a life-threatening emergency that requires immediate intervention, with airway protection being the priority. Bronchial artery embolization remains the standard of care; however, nebulized tranexamic acid shows promise as an adjunctive therapy in the emergency department and may reduce the need for embolization. Future research should focus on standardizing emergency protocols, defining the optimal role and dosing of inhaled antifibrinolytics, and improving long-term management strategies to reduce recurrence and mortality.
