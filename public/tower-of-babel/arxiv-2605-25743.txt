Discriminants of derivatives and symmetric difference polynomials
Boris Shapiro

Abstract
. Let P P be a monic polynomial of degree n n with roots x 1 , … , x n x_{1},\ldots,x_{n} . We study the discriminants of the derivatives P ( k ) P^{(k)} as symmetric translation-invariant polynomials in the original roots. The general “square-graph cone” positivity problem was formulated by Alexandersson and Shapiro. The main result of this note proves this conjecture for the terminal cubic family k = n − 3 k=n-3 : we give an explicit positive square-graph expansion for disc ⁡ ( P ( n − 3 ) ) \operatorname{disc}(P^{(n-3)}) . We also record closed central-moment formulas for the terminal quadratic, cubic and quartic cases, introduce normalized terminal polynomials for all fixed terminal orders, and write down the next, quintic, terminal polynomial explicitly. These formulas turn the first open cases of the square-graph problem into concrete finite linear-algebraic certificate problems.

1.
Introduction

Let

P(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t-x_{i})=t^{n}+a_{1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{n}

be a monic polynomial. For
0\leq k\leq n-2
put

\mathcal{D}_{k}^{(n)}:=\operatorname{disc}(P^{(k)}),

where the discriminant is taken with the usual leading-coefficient
normalization.
Equivalently,

\mathcal{D}_{k}^{(n)}=(-1)^{m(m-1)/2}c^{-1}\operatorname{Res}(P^{(k)},P^{(k+1)}),\qquad m=n-k,

where
c
is the leading coefficient of
P^{(k)}
.

The polynomial
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{(n)}
is symmetric and translation-invariant in

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. Hence it lies in the algebra generated by the pairwise
root differences
x_{i}-x_{j}
. It is natural to ask whether it admits a
representation by manifestly nonnegative expressions in these differences.

The following strengthening of a question of Sottile and Mukhin was formulated
in
[
1
]
.

Conjecture 1

(square-graph cone conjecture)
.

For every
n\geq 2
and every
0\leq k\leq n-2
, the polynomial
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{(n)}

belongs to the convex cone generated by symmetrized graph monomials whose
edges all have even multiplicity.
Equivalently,
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{(n)}
is a positive linear combination of symmetric sums
of products

\prod_{(i,j)\in E(G)}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}.

For
k=0
this is the usual discriminant formula. For the first derivative,
the weaker assertion that
\operatorname{disc}(P^{\prime})
is a sum of squares in the differences of
the roots follows from Sanyal’s work on the entropic discriminant

[
5
, Corollary 14]
. The full square-graph cone conjecture remains a
more precise combinatorial positivity problem.

The purpose of the present note is to push this problem in the terminal
range, where the order

r=n-k

of the remaining derivative is fixed. The main contributions are the
following.

(a)

We give closed central-moment formulas for the terminal quadratic,
cubic and quartic discriminants.

(b)

We prove a positive square-graph expansion for the terminal cubic
family. Consequently Conjecture
1
holds for

\operatorname{disc}(P^{(n-3)})
for all
n\geq 3
.

(c)

We introduce normalized terminal polynomials
J_{n,r}
for arbitrary
fixed terminal order
r
and write down the quintic case
r=5
explicitly.
This makes the next cases of the conjecture finite and computationally
checkable in a uniform set of symmetric moment coordinates.

Thus the quadratic and cubic terminal cases are settled here, while the
quartic and quintic formulas below isolate the next concrete certificate
problems.

2.
Graph monomials and the ambient vector space

We recall the basic language. If
G
is a loopless multigraph with vertex set

\{1,\ldots,n\}
, define

M_{G}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\prod_{(i,j)\in E(G)}(x_{i}-x_{j}),

where an orientation may be chosen for each edge. Its symmetrization is

\widetilde{G}:=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}M_{G}(x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(n)}).

If every edge of
G
occurs with even multiplicity, then
\widetilde{G}
is a
sum of squares, since it is a sum of squares of monomials in differences.
Such graphs will be called
square graphs
.

Let
\mathrm{PST}_{n,d}
denote the vector space of homogeneous symmetric
translation-invariant polynomials of degree
d
in
n
variables. It is
classical that this space is spanned by symmetrized graph monomials with
d

edges; see
[
1
]
and the references therein. Since

\deg\mathcal{D}_{k}^{(n)}=(n-k)(n-k-1),

Conjecture
1
says that a distinguished element of

\mathrm{PST}_{n,(n-k)(n-k-1)}
lies in the square-graph cone.

3.
The quadratic terminal case

Let

\overline{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i},\qquad z_{i}=x_{i}-\overline{x},\qquad p_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}^{r}.

Thus
p_{1}=0
.

Proposition 2
.

For every
n\geq 2
,

\mathcal{D}_{n-2}^{(n)}=(n-1)((n-2)!)^{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}.

Equivalently,

\mathcal{D}_{n-2}^{(n)}=n(n-1)((n-2)!)^{2}p_{2}.

Proof.

The polynomial
P^{(n-2)}
is quadratic. Its coefficients are

P^{(n-2)}(t)=\frac{n!}{2}t^{2}+(n-1)!a_{1}t+(n-2)!a_{2}.

Therefore

\operatorname{disc}(P^{(n-2)})=((n-1)!a_{1})^{2}-2n!(n-2)!a_{2}.

Using
a_{1}=-\sum_{i}x_{i}
and
a_{2}=\sum_{i<j}x_{i}x_{j}
, this becomes

(n-1)((n-2)!)^{2}\left((n-1)\Big(\sum_{i}x_{i}\Big)^{2}-2n\sum_{i<j}x_{i}x_{j}\right).

The expression in parentheses is

\sum_{i<j}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}=np_{2}
, which proves the claim.
∎

This proves Conjecture
1
in the terminal quadratic case.
It is also the formula appearing as Example 2 in
[
1
]
, with the same
normalization of the discriminant.

4.
The cubic terminal case

The next terminal case is already more interesting. It gives a compact formula
in terms of the second and third central moments of the roots.

Theorem 3
.

For every
n\geq 3
,

\boxed{\mathcal{D}_{n-3}^{(n)}=\frac{n!((n-2)!)^{3}}{12}\left(p_{2}^{3}-\frac{n(n-1)}{(n-2)^{2}}p_{3}^{2}\right).}

In particular, if the roots
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are real, then

p_{2}^{3}\geq\frac{n(n-1)}{(n-2)^{2}}p_{3}^{2}.

Proof.

Translate the variable so that the roots become
z_{i}=x_{i}-\overline{x}
and

\sum_{i}z_{i}=0
. Translation does not change the discriminant.
The polynomial
P
then has the form

P(t)=t^{n}+e_{2}t^{n-2}-e_{3}t^{n-3}+\cdots,

where, by Newton’s identities,

e_{2}=-\frac{p_{2}}{2},\qquad e_{3}=\frac{p_{3}}{3}.

After differentiating
n-3
times, we obtain the depressed cubic

P^{(n-3)}(t)=\frac{n!}{6}t^{3}+(n-2)!e_{2}t-(n-3)!e_{3}.

For a depressed cubic
At^{3}+Bt+C
, the discriminant is

-4AB^{3}-27A^{2}C^{2}.

Substituting

A=\frac{n!}{6},\qquad B=-\frac{(n-2)!}{2}p_{2},\qquad C=-\frac{(n-3)!}{3}p_{3}

gives

\mathcal{D}_{n-3}^{(n)}=\frac{n!((n-2)!)^{3}}{12}p_{2}^{3}-\frac{(n!)^{2}((n-3)!)^{2}}{12}p_{3}^{2}.

Since

\frac{(n!)^{2}((n-3)!)^{2}}{n!((n-2)!)^{3}}=\frac{n(n-1)}{(n-2)^{2}},

the stated formula follows. If all
x_{i}
are real, then
P^{(n-3)}
is a real
cubic with three real roots by Rolle’s theorem, and hence its discriminant is
nonnegative. This gives the displayed moment inequality.
∎

Remark 4
.

The inequality in Theorem
3
is the sharp finite version of the
usual bound on skewness for a distribution supported on
n
equally weighted
points. The normalization here is especially natural because it is forced by
the discriminant of
P^{(n-3)}
.

Proposition 5
.

Assume
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
are not all equal. Equality in

p_{2}^{3}\geq\frac{n(n-1)}{(n-2)^{2}}p_{3}^{2}

holds if and only if, after a permutation, the centered roots have the form

z_{1}=\cdots=z_{n-1}=a,\qquad z_{n}=-(n-1)a

for some nonzero real number
a
, or the same configuration with
a
replaced
by
-a
.

Proof.

Equality is equivalent to
\mathcal{D}_{n-3}^{(n)}=0
, i.e. to
P^{(n-3)}
having a
multiple root. Since
P
has real roots,
P^{(n-3)}
has three real roots
counted with multiplicity. A depressed cubic with a multiple root has root
multiset
\{u,u,-2u\}
for some
u\in\mathbb{R}
.

Equivalently, the extremal problem for
p_{3}
under the constraints

\sum_{i}z_{i}=0
and
\sum_{i}z_{i}^{2}=p_{2}
has a Lagrange multiplier equation

3z_{i}^{2}=\lambda+2\mu z_{i}.

Thus all
z_{i}
assume at most two distinct values. If these values have
multiplicities
r
and
n-r
, the constraints give

|p_{3}|=\frac{|n-2r|}{\sqrt{r(n-r)n}}p_{2}^{3/2}.

This is maximal precisely for
r=1
or
r=n-1
, giving the stated equality
case.
∎

5.
A quartic terminal formula

The next case is still computable in closed form. It is useful because it
is the first terminal case involving the fourth central moment.

Theorem 6
.

Let
n\geq 4
. With the notation

p_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{r},\qquad\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}x_{i},

one has

\mathcal{D}_{n-4}^{(n)}=\operatorname{disc}\left(At^{4}+Bt^{2}+Ct+D\right),

where

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{24},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=-\frac{(n-2)!}{4}p_{2},

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=-\frac{(n-3)!}{3}p_{3},

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=(n-4)!\left(\frac{p_{2}^{2}}{8}-\frac{p_{4}}{4}\right).

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n-4}^{(n)}={}

\displaystyle 256A^{3}D^{3}-128A^{2}B^{2}D^{2}+144A^{2}BC^{2}D-27A^{2}C^{4}

\displaystyle+16AB^{4}D-4AB^{3}C^{2}.

In particular, for real roots
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
this gives the sharp
finite moment inequality

256A^{3}D^{3}-128A^{2}B^{2}D^{2}+144A^{2}BC^{2}D-27A^{2}C^{4}+16AB^{4}D-4AB^{3}C^{2}\geq 0.

Proof.

After translating the roots we may assume
\sum_{i}x_{i}=0
. Newton’s identities
give

e_{2}=-\frac{p_{2}}{2},\qquad e_{3}=\frac{p_{3}}{3},\qquad e_{4}=\frac{p_{2}^{2}}{8}-\frac{p_{4}}{4}.

Hence

P(t)=t^{n}+e_{2}t^{n-2}-e_{3}t^{n-3}+e_{4}t^{n-4}+\cdots.

Differentiating
n-4
times gives

P^{(n-4)}(t)=\frac{n!}{24}t^{4}+\frac{(n-2)!}{2}e_{2}t^{2}-(n-3)!e_{3}t+(n-4)!e_{4},

which is precisely the stated quartic. The discriminant formula for

At^{4}+Bt^{2}+Ct+D
is

256A^{3}D^{3}-128A^{2}B^{2}D^{2}+144A^{2}BC^{2}D-27A^{2}C^{4}+16AB^{4}D-4AB^{3}C^{2}.

Finally, if the
x_{i}
are real, then all roots of
P^{(n-4)}
are real by
Rolle’s theorem; therefore its discriminant is nonnegative.
∎

Corollary 7

(normalized quartic form)
.

Let

q_{n,4}(t)=t^{4}+\alpha t^{2}+\beta t+\gamma,

where

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=-\frac{6p_{2}}{n(n-1)},

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=-\frac{8p_{3}}{n(n-1)(n-2)},

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{3(p_{2}^{2}-2p_{4})}{n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)}.

Then, after centering the roots,

P^{(n-4)}(t)=\frac{n!}{24}\,q_{n,4}(t)

and hence

\mathcal{D}_{n-4}^{(n)}=\left(\frac{n!}{24}\right)^{6}\operatorname{disc}(q_{n,4}).

In particular, for real roots the depressed quartic
q_{n,4}
is hyperbolic
and

\displaystyle 0\leq\operatorname{disc}(q_{n,4})={}

\displaystyle 256\gamma^{3}-128\alpha^{2}\gamma^{2}+144\alpha\beta^{2}\gamma-27\beta^{4}

\displaystyle+16\alpha^{4}\gamma-4\alpha^{3}\beta^{2}.

If
p_{3}=0
, this specializes to the factorization

\operatorname{disc}(q_{n,4})=16\gamma(\alpha^{2}-4\gamma)^{2}.

Proof.

Divide the quartic in Theorem
6
by its leading coefficient

n!/24
. The formula for the discriminant follows from the homogeneity rule

\operatorname{disc}(cq)=c^{2r-2}\operatorname{disc}(q)
for a degree
r
polynomial. The final
factorization is the standard discriminant factorization for a biquadratic
quartic
t^{4}+\alpha t^{2}+\gamma
.
∎

Remark 8
.

The quartic formula gives a natural continuation of the cubic skewness
inequality. In the language of finite probability distributions with equal
weights it is an algebraic inequality among the variance, skewness, and fourth
central moment. Unlike the cubic case, the equality locus is no longer only a
two-point configuration; it corresponds to configurations for which

P^{(n-4)}
has a multiple root. Corollary
7
is a
convenient normalized form for a future square-graph certificate search.

6.
Universal terminal polynomials

For
m\geq j
write

(m)_{\downarrow j}=m(m-1)\cdots(m-j+1)

for the falling factorial. The preceding examples are instances of the
following normalized terminal principle.

Theorem 9

(normalized terminal polynomial)
.

Fix
r\geq 2
and
n\geq r
. After translating the roots so that

\sum_{i}x_{i}=0
, put

J_{n,r}(t)=\frac{r!}{n!}P^{(n-r)}(t).

Then

J_{n,r}(t)=t^{r}+\sum_{j=2}^{r}(-1)^{j}\frac{(r)_{\downarrow j}}{(n)_{\downarrow j}}e_{j}t^{r-j}.

Consequently
J_{n,r}
is a universal polynomial in

p_{2},p_{3},\ldots,p_{r}
, and

\mathcal{D}_{n-r}^{(n)}=\left(\frac{n!}{r!}\right)^{2r-2}\operatorname{disc}(J_{n,r}).

If the roots
x_{i}
are real, then
J_{n,r}
is real-rooted; hence

\operatorname{disc}(J_{n,r})\geq 0
.

Proof.

With
e_{1}=0
one has

P(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}e_{j}t^{n-j}.

Differentiating
n-r
times gives

P^{(n-r)}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}(-1)^{j}e_{j}\frac{(n-j)!}{(r-j)!}t^{r-j}.

Multiplication by
r!/n!
gives the displayed formula. Newton’s identities
express
e_{j}
as a polynomial in
p_{2},\ldots,p_{j}
because
p_{1}=e_{1}=0
.
The discriminant scaling formula for a degree
r
polynomial gives the
normalization of
\mathcal{D}_{n-r}^{(n)}
. Finally, if
P
has real roots, then every
derivative of
P
has real roots by Rolle’s theorem.
∎

The first three normalized polynomials are

J_{n,2}(t)=t^{2}-\frac{p_{2}}{n(n-1)},

J_{n,3}(t)=t^{3}-\frac{3p_{2}}{n(n-1)}t-\frac{2p_{3}}{n(n-1)(n-2)},

and
J_{n,4}=q_{n,4}
from Corollary
7
. The next
case is still short enough to be useful.

Proposition 10

(the quintic terminal polynomial)
.

For
n\geq 5
,

\displaystyle J_{n,5}(t)={}

\displaystyle t^{5}-\frac{10p_{2}}{n(n-1)}t^{3}-\frac{20p_{3}}{n(n-1)(n-2)}t^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{15p_{2}^{2}-30p_{4}}{n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)}t

\displaystyle+\frac{20p_{2}p_{3}-24p_{5}}{n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)(n-4)}.

Thus, for real roots, the discriminant of this quintic is a nonnegative
universal polynomial in
p_{2},p_{3},p_{4},p_{5}
.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
9
with
r=5
and use Newton’s
identities in the centered case:

e_{2}=-\frac{p_{2}}{2},\qquad e_{3}=\frac{p_{3}}{3},\qquad e_{4}=\frac{p_{2}^{2}}{8}-\frac{p_{4}}{4},\qquad e_{5}=\frac{p_{5}}{5}-\frac{p_{2}p_{3}}{6}.

Substitution gives the displayed expression.
∎

Problem 11
.

For fixed
r
, determine whether the universal polynomial

\operatorname{disc}(J_{n,r})
belongs, after the positive normalization above, to the
square-graph cone for all
n\geq r
.
The cases
r=2
and
r=3
are settled in this note. The case
r=4
is the
next concrete target; the case
r=5
is now reduced to the explicit quintic
in Proposition
10
.

7.
Terminal stability and a route to the main conjecture

The formulas above suggest the following strategy for the full
square-graph cone conjecture.

(1)

First prove the conjecture for the terminal family

\mathcal{D}_{n-r}^{(n)}
with
r
fixed. This reduces the problem to a finite
universal computation in
p_{2},\ldots,p_{r}
.

(2)

Next study how the cone certificate changes when
n
is increased.
The graph language suggests that stabilization phenomena should occur:
for fixed
r
, only finitely many graph types can appear before
symmetrization.

(3)

Finally use polarisation or graph-splitting identities to pass from
terminal derivatives to general derivatives. A promising identity is

(x_{i}-x_{j})(x_{j}-x_{k})=\frac{1}{2}\big((x_{i}-x_{k})^{2}-(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}-(x_{j}-x_{k})^{2}\big),

which converts pairs of odd edges into square edges at the cost of changing
the graph type.

This leads to the following more concrete conjectural strengthening.

Conjecture 12
.

For each fixed
r\geq 2
there exists a finite list of square multigraphs

G_{1},\ldots,G_{N}
, depending only on
r
and not on
n
, such that for all

n\geq r

\mathcal{D}_{n-r}^{(n)}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{N}c_{\nu}(n)\,\widetilde{G_{\nu}},\qquad c_{\nu}(n)\geq 0,

where the coefficients
c_{\nu}(n)
are rational functions, or equivalently
polynomials after a common positive normalization, in
n
.

Remark 13
.

Conjecture
12
is more concrete than the full
square-graph cone conjecture and is amenable to computation. It asks for a
uniform certificate in
n
for each fixed terminal order
r
. Even proving it
for
r=4
would add substantial evidence for the main conjecture.

8.
Relation with the square-graph cone

The formulas above already prove Conjecture
1
in the
terminal quadratic case. The cubic case is subtler: the formula of
Theorem
3
is not itself a square-graph decomposition, because it
contains the difference of two invariant terms. Thus the essential remaining
point is to prove that

p_{2}^{3}-\frac{n(n-1)}{(n-2)^{2}}p_{3}^{2}

belongs to the square-graph cone. The next section gives an explicit positive
certificate. This certificate is stronger than the corresponding moment
inequality, because every summand is a symmetrized monomial in squared root
differences.

9.
A positive square-graph expansion in the cubic terminal case

We now give the announced certificate. This proves
Conjecture
1
for the terminal cubic family

k=n-3
.

Continue to use the centered variables

z_{i}=x_{i}-\bar{x},\qquad\sum_{i}z_{i}=0,\qquad p_{r}=\sum_{i}z_{i}^{r}.

Introduce the following four symmetric sums of square-graph monomials:

\displaystyle T_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq n}(z_{i}-z_{j})^{2}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{2}(z_{k}-z_{i})^{2},

\displaystyle P_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k,l}^{*}(z_{i}-z_{j})^{2}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{2}(z_{k}-z_{l})^{2},

\displaystyle Q_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k,l}^{*}(z_{i}-z_{j})^{4}(z_{k}-z_{l})^{2},

\displaystyle R_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k,l,m,s}^{*}(z_{i}-z_{j})^{2}(z_{k}-z_{l})^{2}(z_{m}-z_{s})^{2}.

Here
\sum^{*}
means summation over ordered pairwise distinct indices. Thus

T_{n}
is the triangle square-graph sum,
P_{n}
is the ordered path sum,

Q_{n}
is the ordered sum of a double edge disjoint from a single edge, and

R_{n}
is the ordered matching sum. Each of these belongs to the square-graph
cone.

Lemma 14
.

For centered variables
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
one has

\displaystyle T_{n}={}

\displaystyle-p_{2}^{3}-np_{3}^{2}+np_{2}p_{4},

\displaystyle P_{n}={}

\displaystyle(4n-6)p_{2}^{3}-2(n-5)(n-2)p_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+(4n^{2}-24n+30)p_{2}p_{4}-2n(n-1)p_{6},

\displaystyle Q_{n}={}

\displaystyle 12(n-2)p_{2}^{3}+8(2n-5)p_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+(4n^{2}-36n+60)p_{2}p_{4}-4n(n-1)p_{6},

\displaystyle R_{n}={}

\displaystyle 8(n-2)(n^{2}-7n+15)p_{2}^{3}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle-16(3n^{2}-15n+20)p_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle-24(n-2)(n^{2}-5n+10)p_{2}p_{4}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+16n(n-1)(n-2)p_{6}.

Proof.

We record the diagonal-removal calculation. It is a useful check on the
normalization of the ordered sums. For nonnegative integers

\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
, put

S(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}}^{*}z_{i_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots z_{i_{m}}^{\alpha_{m}}.

By inclusion–exclusion, or equivalently by Möbius inversion on the lattice

\Pi_{m}
of set partitions, one has

(9.1)

\displaystyle S(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{m}}\mu(\pi)\prod_{B\in\pi}p_{\sum_{j\in B}\alpha_{j}},

\displaystyle\mu(\pi)

\displaystyle=\prod_{B\in\pi}(-1)^{|B|-1}(|B|-1)!.

Here
p_{0}=n
and
p_{1}=0
. Formula (
9.1
) is just the
standard principle that the unrestricted sum is decomposed according to the
partition of positions on which the indices coincide.

Expand each of the four square-graph products as a sum of monomials in the
corresponding ordered variables and apply (
9.1
). For the
triangle one first computes the ordered sum and divides by
6
, because each
unordered triple is counted six times; this gives

T_{n}=-p_{2}^{3}-np_{3}^{2}+np_{2}p_{4}.

The same expansion for the ordered path, the ordered disjoint double-edge plus
single-edge graph, and the ordered matching gives the displayed expressions for

P_{n},Q_{n}
and
R_{n}
. No specialization of the variables is used in this
calculation.
∎

Theorem 15
.

For every
n\geq 3
,

\displaystyle p_{2}^{3}-\frac{n(n-1)}{(n-2)^{2}}p_{3}^{2}={}

\displaystyle\frac{6}{n(n-2)}T_{n}+\frac{1}{2n(n-2)}P_{n}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4n(n-2)}Q_{n}+\frac{1}{8n(n-2)^{2}}R_{n}.

Consequently
\mathcal{D}_{n-3}^{(n)}
belongs to the square-graph cone for every

n\geq 3
.

Proof.

Substitute the four identities of Lemma
14
into the
right-hand side. The coefficients of
p_{2}p_{4}
and
p_{6}
cancel. The
coefficient of
p_{2}^{3}
is

-\frac{6}{n(n-2)}+\frac{4n-6}{2n(n-2)}+\frac{12(n-2)}{4n(n-2)}+\frac{8(n-2)(n^{2}-7n+15)}{8n(n-2)^{2}}=1,

and the coefficient of
p_{3}^{2}
is

-\frac{6}{n-2}-\frac{n-5}{n}+\frac{2(2n-5)}{n(n-2)}-\frac{2(3n^{2}-15n+20)}{n(n-2)^{2}}=-\frac{n(n-1)}{(n-2)^{2}}.

This proves the displayed identity. All four coefficients in the identity are
positive for
n\geq 3
, and
T_{n},P_{n},Q_{n},R_{n}
are square-graph sums. Combining
this identity with Theorem
3
proves the square-graph cone
conjecture for
k=n-3
.
∎

Corollary 16
.

For every
n\geq 3
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n-3}^{(n)}={}

\displaystyle\frac{n!((n-2)!)^{3}}{12}\left(\frac{6}{n(n-2)}T_{n}+\frac{1}{2n(n-2)}P_{n}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 76.82234pt+\frac{1}{4n(n-2)}Q_{n}+\frac{1}{8n(n-2)^{2}}R_{n}\right).
