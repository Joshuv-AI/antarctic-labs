Asymptotically Optimal Depth Fermionic Permutation on 2D Grid Quantum Architecture without Ancillas
Dantong Li, Shifan Xu, Yongshan Ding

Abstract
Simulating fermionic systems on qubit hardware involves many nonlocal interactions, and efficient routing of these interactions is critical to the overall cost of fermionic simulation algorithms. Recent works reduce this Jordan–Wigner routing overhead to polylogarithmic depth under all-to-all connectivity, but degrade to O ⁡ ( N ​ polylog ⁡ N ) O(\sqrt{N}\polylog N) for N N fermionic modes on 2D nearest-neighbor architectures. We present a fermionic permutation protocol tailored to 2D grid architectures that achieves the optimal O ⁡ ( N ) O(\sqrt{N}) depth with O ⁡ ( N ​ N ) O(N\sqrt{N}) nearest-neighbor gates and no ancilla qubits, mid-circuit measurements, or classical feedforward. This matches the Ω ⁡ ( N ) \Omega(\sqrt{N}) lower bound, which holds even when O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) ancillas and classical feedforward are permitted. We further construct an O ⁡ ( N ) O(\sqrt{N}) -depth transformation between the Jordan–Wigner, Bravyi–Kitaev, and Parity encodings on the 2D grid via a Hilbert-curve layout, extending our result to all three encodings. Benchmarks on the fermionic fast Fourier transform and Trotter simulation of sparse SYK model demonstrate consistent reduction in depth, spacetime volume, and infidelity for system sizes N ≳ 100 N\gtrsim 100 in the early fault-tolerant regime.

These systems pose a fundamental computational barrier for classical algorithms: the antisymmetry of fermionic wavefunctions induces the sign problem, which obstructs Monte Carlo methods and forces exponential cost for a broad class of physically relevant Hamiltonians. Quantum computers can in principle sidestep this barrier by coherently and efficiently encoding and simulating fermionic dynamics

The fundamental challenge stems from the fact that qubit operators follow a local tensor-product structure, while fermionic creation and annihilation operators obey nonlocal anticommutation relations. Bridging this gap requires a fermion-to-qubit mapping. The Jordan–Wigner (JW) transformation
25
serializes fermionic modes along a one-dimensional chain, representing each fermionic operator as a Pauli-
Z

O(N)

49

X

All three encodings fix a static labeling from fermionic modes to qubits, but no static labeling completely eliminates routing overhead for nonlocal interactions: in a simulation algorithm, the interaction graph often changes between simulation steps, and the active modes must be dynamically routed to maintain gate locality, via an operation called the
fermionic permutation
. Unlike ordinary qubit permutation, a fermionic permutation requires preserving anticommutation; in JW encoding, this requires phase corrections between all mode pairs whose ordering changes. As such, dynamic fermionic routing incurs significant cost in fermionic simulation algorithms, including simulating Trotter product formula for the Fermi–Hubbard and Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev (SYK) models, and the fermionic fast Fourier transform (FFFT) used throughout plane-wave electronic structure

II
Background

II.1
Majorana operators and the Jordan–Wigner mapping

Throughout this paper, we consider
N=L^{2}
fermionic modes mapped to an
L\times L
qubit grid with nearest-neighbor connectivity. We work primarily in the Majorana picture, since it gives a uniform language for the fermion-to-qubit encodings considered in Section
IV.1
, where each encoding corresponds to a ternary tree whose leaves carry Majorana operators. Each fermionic mode
j
(
0\leq j\leq N{-}1
) is associated with two self-adjoint
Majorana operators

\gamma_{2j}
and
\gamma_{2j+1}
, satisfying the Clifford algebra

\{\gamma_{i},\,\gamma_{k}\}\;=\;2\,\delta_{ik},\qquad\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{i}^{\dagger},

(1)

for
i,k\in\{0,\ldots,2N{-}1\}
. The standard fermionic creation and annihilation operators are recovered from the Majoranas as

a_{j}^{\dagger}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{2j}-i\gamma_{2j+1}),\qquad a_{j}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{2j}+i\gamma_{2j+1}),

(2)

and Eq. (
1
) translates into the canonical anticommutation relations
\{a_{j},a_{k}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{jk}
.

The JW transformation
25
maps the
2N
Majorana operators to qubit operators by

\gamma_{2j}\;\mapsto\;X_{j}\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}Z_{\ell},\qquad\gamma_{2j+1}\;\mapsto\;Y_{j}\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}Z_{\ell},

(3)

which combined with Eq. (
2
) reproduces the familiar form
a_{j}^{\dagger}\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(X_{j}-iY_{j})\prod_{\ell<j}Z_{\ell}
. The trailing Pauli-
Z
string is the qubit-side manifestation of fermionic anticommutation: any operator at site
j
inherits a parity over all sites
\ell<j
.

The JW transformation requires a fixed linear ordering of the
N
fermionic modes. On the
L\times L
grid, we adopt the row-major
snake
(boustrophedon) order shown in Figure
1
: even rows are traversed left-to-right and odd rows right-to-left, giving the JW index
\mathrm{jw}(r,c)=rL+c
for even
r
and
rL+(L{-}1{-}c)
for odd
r
. Under this ordering, every horizontal grid neighbor is JW-adjacent (indices differ by 1), but vertical neighbors are separated by up to
2L{-}1
sites along the chain. This vertical separation is the source of the long parity strings that fermionic permutation algorithms must address.

Figure 1
:
Snake JW order on an
L\times L
grid. Vertical swaps cross the row turn, making them nonlocal in JW order and need phase corrections along the chain.

With this fixed linear order in place, we can now state the central object of this paper. For a permutation
\pi\in S_{N}
, the corresponding
fermionic permutation
is the unitary

\mathcal{F}_{\pi}\;=\;\hat{P}_{\pi}\,\hat{V}_{\pi},\qquad\hat{V}_{\pi}\;=\;\prod_{(i,j)\in\Inv(\pi)}\text{CZ}_{i,j},

(4)

where
\hat{P}_{\pi}
physically permutes the qubits according to
\pi
, and
\hat{V}_{\pi}
applies a
CZ
gate on every pair
(i,j)
in the inversion set
\Inv(\pi)=\{(i,j):i<j\;\wedge\;\pi(i)>\pi(j)\}
—matching the antisymmetry built into Eq. (
3
). Equivalently,
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
is the unique unitary (up to global phase) that permutes the Majorana operators:
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}\,\gamma_{2j+\alpha}\,\mathcal{F}_{\pi}^{\dagger}=\gamma_{2\pi(j)+\alpha}
for all modes
j
and
\alpha\in\{0,1\}
.

II.2
Fermionic SWAP operations

The simplest fermionic permutation is the adjacent transposition
\mathcal{F}_{(j,\,j+1)}
, implemented by the fermionic SWAP (
FSWAP
) gate
27
. On two JW-adjacent qubits
j
and
j{+}1
,
FSWAP
acts as

\text{FSWAP}_{j,j+1}\ket{s_j\, s_{j+1}}\;=\;(-1)^{s_{j}\cdot s_{j+1}}\ket{s_{j+1}\, s_j}.

(5)

On hardware,
FSWAP
is locally equivalent to iSWAP and decomposes into 2
CNOTs
with single-qubit gates, strictly cheaper than implementing
\mathcal{F}_{(j,\,j+1)}
as a
SWAP
(3
CNOTs
) followed by a
CZ
(1
CNOT
)
44
.

The cost of
FSWAP
between two grid-adjacent qubits depends sharply on whether they are also JW-adjacent. For horizontal neighbors
(r,c)
and
(r,c{\pm}1)
, the JW indices differ by 1, so a single
FSWAP
gate suffices. For vertical neighbors
(r,c)
and
(r{+}1,c)
, however, the JW indices
j<k
can be much farther apart: the snake order forces the chain to pass through up to
2L{-}1
intermediate sites between them (specifically,
k-j=2|d|+1
, where
d
is the horizontal distance from
c
to the snake turning corner between rows
r
and
r{+}1
). The full fermionic
SWAP
between such vertical neighbors carries a parity correction over all intermediate sites:

F_{j,k}\ket{s}=(-1)^{s_{j}s_{k}\;+\;(s_{j}+s_{k})\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{k-1}s_{\ell}}\;\ket{s'},

(6)

where
s^{\prime}
has
s_{j}
and
s_{k}
exchanged. We refer to
P=\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{k-1}s_{\ell}
as the
parity string
; its nonlocality is the central difficulty addressed by this paper.

To distinguish the local hardware operation from its parity-corrected counterpart, we use two pieces of notation throughout. The
bare

FSWAP
applies the standard
FSWAP
gate (Eq. (
5
)) directly to two physically adjacent qubits, ignoring the parity string:

B_{j,k}\ket{s}=\text{FSWAP}_{j,k}\ket{s}=(-1)^{s_{j}s_{k}}\,\ket{s'}.

(7)

The
full

FSWAP

F_{j,k}
is the operator of Eq. (
6
), which additionally incorporates the parity correction
(-1)^{(s_{j}+s_{k})P}
. The bare
FSWAP
is what 2D nearest-neighbor hardware can execute natively; the full
FSWAP
is what fermionic correctness demands.

II.3
Odd–even transposition sort

The basic primitive for realizing an arbitrary permutation on a 1D line of
L
elements using only adjacent swaps is the
odd–even transposition
(OET) network
28
;
27
. As shown in Figure
2
, OET runs in
L
rounds, alternating between two layers: even-numbered rounds swap pairs
(0,1),(2,3),\ldots
and odd-numbered rounds swap pairs
(1,2),(3,4),\ldots
, where each “swap” applies the adjacent transposition only when the local order disagrees with the target. Within a round, all pairs are disjoint, so the round executes in a single layer of parallel two-qubit gates, and
L
rounds are sufficient to realize any permutation in
S_{L}
.

Applied to a 1D chain of
N
qubits under JW, OET realizes any fermionic permutation
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
in depth
2N
using local
FSWAPs
. The naive way to extend this to the 2D
L\times L
grid is to thread the qubits along the snake order and run the same 1D OET, which still has depth
2N=O(N)
and uses
O(N^{2})
gates—ignoring the second axis of the grid entirely.

III.1
Protocol overview

We present a protocol that implements an arbitrary fermionic permutation on an
L\times L

22L+O(1)=O(\sqrt{N})

O(N\sqrt{N})

The protocol has two ingredients. The first is a classical decomposition, which follows from Hall’s marriage theorem: any permutation on an

(Section
II.3

SWAP
carries a parity-string correction over up to
\Theta(L)
intermediate qubits, which the bare
FSWAP
does not supply.

III.2
Hall’s three-stage Row-Column-Row routing

Hall’s three-stage Row-Column-Row (RCR) routing
(
2
, Theorem 4)
decomposes any permutation
\pi
on an
L\times L
grid as
\pi=\pi^{\mathrm{rowB}}\circ\pi^{\mathrm{col}}\circ\pi^{\mathrm{rowA}}
(Figure
4
), where
\pi^{\mathrm{rowA}}
and
\pi^{\mathrm{rowB}}
are within-row permutations and
\pi^{\mathrm{col}}
is a within-column permutation. The decomposition is constructed by reduction to bipartite edge coloring: one builds an
L
-regular bipartite graph between source columns and destination rows, decomposes it into
L
perfect matchings via the edge-coloring theorem
29
, and reads off the three permutations from the matching assignment. Operationally, RowA shuffles items within source rows to intermediate columns, the column stage moves items to their destination rows, and RowB shuffles items within destination rows to final columns. We treat this decomposition (
Hall3StagePlan
) as a classical preprocessing step that runs in
O(N\log N)
time using standard edge-coloring algorithms
20
.

We choose Row-Col-Row rather than the equally valid Col-Row-Col because under the row-major snake JW order, horizontal neighbors are JW-adjacent, so row-stage
FSWAPs
are natively local; only the single column stage requires parity correction via
\Gamma
, giving two
\Gamma
applications total instead of four.

III.3
Row and column sorting stages

We realize all three stages by an OET network running in parallel across the active lines of the grid. In the two row stages, horizontal neighbors are JW-adjacent, so each swap is a local
FSWAP
requiring no parity correction.

The column stage is implemented identically as a depth-
2L
OET network, but the underlying gates are now
bare

FSWAPs
(Eq. (
7
)) on vertical grid neighbors with JW-non-adjacent indices. By themselves, these bare gates do not realize the correct fermionic transpositions, since they omit the parity-string corrections required by Eq. (
6
). The two surrounding
\Gamma
applications supply all of these corrections in a single sandwich (Section
III.4
), and so the bare column sort, when sandwiched, computes exactly the fermionic column permutation
\mathcal{F}_{\pi^{\mathrm{col}}}
.

III.4
The
\Gamma
operator

The role of
\Gamma
is to convert bare vertical
FSWAPs
into full fermionic
SWAPs
by conjugation alone. Concretely,
\Gamma
is a diagonal unitary

\Gamma\ket{s}\;=\;(-1)^{f(s)}\ket{s},

where
f:\mathrm{GF}(2)^{N}\to\mathrm{GF}(2)
is a degree-2 polynomial determined entirely by the snake JW geometry, independent of the permutation
\pi
being implemented. Because every eigenvalue is
\pm 1
,
\Gamma
is Hermitian and self-inverse:
\Gamma^{\dagger}=\Gamma=\Gamma^{-1}
. The remaining task is to choose
f
so that conjugation by
\Gamma
converts each bare vertical
FSWAP
into its full fermionic counterpart:

\Gamma\;B_{j,k}\;\Gamma\;=\;F_{j,k}.

(8)

To see what condition this places on
f
, write
\gamma_{s}=(-1)^{f(s)}
for the eigenvalue of
\Gamma
on
\ket{s}
, and let
\ket{s'}
denote the basis state obtained from
\ket{s}
by swapping the occupations
s_{j}
and
s_{k}
. A direct check on the four basis states of the
(j,k)
-subsystem reduces Eq. (
8
) to a single algebraic condition. When
s_{j}=s_{k}=0
, both
FSWAPs
act as the identity on
\ket{s}
, and the identity holds trivially. When
s_{j}=s_{k}=1
, neither
FSWAP
exchanges the basis state, but both contribute a phase of
-1
: the bare
FSWAP
via its
CZ
phase
(-1)^{s_{j}s_{k}}=-1
, and the full
FSWAP
via
(-1)^{1+2P}=-1
; combined with
\gamma_{s}^{2}=1
from the conjugation, the identity holds automatically. The non-trivial case is
\{s_{j},s_{k}\}=\{0,1\}
. Here
s_{j}s_{k}=0
, so the bare
FSWAP
exchanges with phase
+1
, giving
\Gamma\,B_{j,k}\,\Gamma\ket{s}=\gamma_{s}\gamma_{s^{\prime}}\ket{s'}
, while
F_{j,k}\ket{s}=(-1)^{P}\ket{s'}
with
P=\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{k-1}s_{\ell}
. Equating the two yields the
parity-encoding condition
: for every vertical grid-neighbor pair with JW indices
j<k
and every basis-state pair
\ket{s},\ket{s'}
differing only at sites
j,k
with
s_{j}+s_{k}=1
,

\gamma_{s}\cdot\gamma_{s^{\prime}}\;=\;(-1)^{\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{k-1}s_{\ell}}.

(9)

The construction in Section
III.5
produces an
f
satisfying this condition simultaneously for every vertical pair on the grid.

Eq. (
8
) is a per-pair statement, but it lifts to the entire column sort stage. Let
B_{t}
and
F_{t}
denote the products of parallel bare and full
FSWAPs
in OET round
t
. Within any single round, the
FSWAPs
have pairwise disjoint support, and conjugation by a diagonal operator distributes over disjoint-support products, so applying Eq. (
8
) to each pair gives
\Gamma\,B_{t}\,\Gamma=F_{t}
. Inserting
\Gamma^{2}=I
between consecutive rounds then telescopes the entire
L
-round column sort:

\Gamma\;B_{L}\cdots B_{1}\;\Gamma\;=\;F_{L}\cdots F_{1}.

(10)

A single
\Gamma
before and after the bare column sort therefore promotes the entire sort to its full fermionic counterpart—no per-round overhead. The identity holds as an operator equation for any input state, since Eq. (
9
) depends only on the grid layout and JW ordering, never on which mode currently occupies which site.

, using only

Algebraic restructuring to match the grid.

The polynomial

III.7
Asymptotic optimality

We now show that the depth and gate count of Algorithm
1
are both asymptotically optimal on the 2D
L\times L
grid. The depth bound is in fact optimal in a strong sense: it holds even against protocols that use
O(N)
ancillas, mid-circuit measurements, and arbitrarily fast classical feedforward.

The depth lower bound follows from a routing argument. Under any fixed JW ordering,
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
acts on single-excitation states as
\ket{e_j}\mapsto\pm\ket{e_{\pi(j)}}
, so any implementation must move quantum information from each source site to its destination on the grid. Devulapalli et al.
14
prove that in the LOCC model—which permits
O(N)
ancillas, mid-circuit measurements, and arbitrarily fast classical communication—the worst-case routing time on any graph
G
is bounded below by
\mathrm{rt}_{\mathrm{LOCC}}(G)\geq 2/c(G)-1
, where
c(G)
is the vertex expansion. The lattice isoperimetric inequality gives
c(G)=\Theta(1/L)
for the
L\times L
grid, yielding a depth lower bound of
\Omega(L)=\Omega(\sqrt{N})
. The gate-count lower bound is more elementary: the reversal permutation has total Manhattan displacement
\Theta(NL)
, and each nearest-neighbor entangling gate can decrease the displacement by
O(1)
, forcing
\Omega(NL)=\Omega(N\sqrt{N})
gates. Since our algorithm matches both bounds while using
zero
ancillas, no measurements, and no feedforward, it is optimal even against protocols with strictly more resources.

IV
Extensions to Bravyi–Kitaev and Parity Encodings

Our algorithm is formulated under the Jordan–Wigner encoding, but it extends to the Bravyi–Kitaev (BK)
8
and Parity
49
encodings via efficient basis transformations. The strategy is to first transform from BK or Parity to JW, apply our
O(\sqrt{N})
-depth fermionic permutation algorithm of Section
III
, and then transform back. The basis transformation itself can be performed in
O(\sqrt{N})
depth on the 2D grid by laying out qubits along a Hilbert space-filling curve, so the overall depth for fermionic permutations under BK or Parity remains
O(\sqrt{N})
.

IV.1
Ternary tree encodings

A broad class of fermion-to-qubit encodings, including JW, BK, and Parity, can be described uniformly by
ternary trees

50
;
36

9

N

N

2N{+}1

N
parent nodes by qubits, and a labeling of
2N
of the
2N{+}1
leaves by Majorana operators (one leaf is unused), as illustrated in Figure

\gamma_{j}
is then read off from its root-to-leaf path: at each parent node labeled by qubit

respectively. The fermionic creation operators are recovered from pairs of leaves via Eq. (

The JW, BK, and Parity encodings are all

Concretely, conversion between binary-shaped ternary tree encodings is implemented by
tree rotations

\text{CNOT}_{j,k}

(j,k)
in the tree
50

46

10

7

CNOT

IV.2
Fermionic permutation under BK and Parity

We now describe the basis transformation between encodings, then assemble the full BK/Parity fermionic permutation algorithm. Both BK-to-JW and Parity-to-BK conversions can be expressed as
O(\log N)
rounds of parallel tree rotations, each round consisting of disjoint
CNOT
gates
10
. Within each round, the gates satisfy two key properties:
(i) each qubit appears in at most one gate;
(ii) the inorder-traversal intervals spanned by different gates are non-overlapping, since each rotation by design acts within a subtree rooted at a distinct spine node.
The maximum interval length follows a geometric pattern across rounds: for BK
\to
JW it shrinks as
N/4,N/8,\ldots,1
; for Parity
\to
BK it grows symmetrically.

Standard
CNOT
-circuit synthesis on 2D nearest-neighbor connectivity routes these rounds in
O(N)
depth
30
;
11
. We obtain
O(\sqrt{N})
by aligning the qubit layout with the non-overlapping interval structure of the rounds.

To this end, we lay out qubits according to the
Hilbert space-filling curve
: inorder-traversal index
i
is mapped to grid cell
H(i)
. The Hilbert curve has the property that any contiguous interval of length
d
in the inorder traversal maps to a connected grid region of diameter
O(\sqrt{d})
. Combined with property (ii) above, this means the rotations within a single round fire on disjoint, spatially-separated grid regions, and each round can be routed in depth
O(\sqrt{d_{\max}})
where
d_{\max}
is the round’s maximum interval length (Figure
8
). A formal proof of disjointness via dyadic-rectangle bookkeeping is given in Appendix
D
.

Summing the per-round cost gives the total BK
\to
JW conversion depth:

\sum_{i=2}^{\lfloor\log_{2}N\rfloor}O\!\left(\sqrt{N/2^{i}}\right)\;=\;O(\sqrt{N})\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}})^{i}\;=\;O(\sqrt{N}).

The Parity
\to
BK sum is the same series in reverse, also
O(\sqrt{N})
.

We now assemble the four-step algorithm. Under the Bravyi–Kitaev or Parity encoding on an
L\times L
grid, any fermionic permutation can be implemented in depth
O(\sqrt{N})
with
O(N\sqrt{N})
nearest-neighbor gates and zero ancillas, as follows. (1) Convert from the source encoding to JW using the tree-rotation
CNOT
layers above on the Hilbert layout, in depth
O(\sqrt{N})
. (2) Re-route the qubits from the Hilbert layout to the snake layout. Since this is an ordinary qubit permutation with no fermionic phase corrections, it can be implemented in depth
O(\sqrt{N})
using standard 2D nearest-neighbor routing
2
. (3) Apply Algorithm
1
to implement the desired fermionic permutation on the snake-ordered JW grid, again in depth
O(\sqrt{N})
. (4) Reverse steps (2) and (1) to return to the original encoding, contributing another
O(\sqrt{N})
depth. The total depth remains
O(\sqrt{N})
.

A natural question is whether this
O(\sqrt{N})
bound extends beyond BK and Parity. Constantinides et al.
10
show that any product-preserving ternary tree encoding
9
can be converted to JW in
O(\log^{2}N)
depth under all-to-all connectivity. On 2D grids, our
O(\sqrt{N})
bound applies to the BK and Parity special cases, where the non-overlapping interval property holds by construction; whether the same depth is achievable for general ternary trees on 2D nearest-neighbor hardware remains an open question.

V.4
Sparse SYK simulation

The sparse Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev (SYK) model is a strongly interacting fermionic Hamiltonian

H=\sum_{\{i<j<k<l\}\in\mathcal{S}}J_{ijkl}\,\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l},

(15)

which has long-range four-body interactions and is believed to be dual to toy models of quantum gravity
38
;
43
;
48
.
Here
\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{2N-1}
are the Majorana operators of Section
II.1
,
\mathcal{S}
is a random sparse subset with each quartet included independently with probability
k\cdot 2N/\binom{2N}{4}
(yielding
2kN
terms in expectation), and couplings
J_{ijkl}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
are drawn with variance
\sigma^{2}=6/N^{3}
.
We fix sparsity
k=1
throughout, the canonical regime in which the model retains its characteristic chaotic dynamics while admitting linear-in-
N
Hamiltonian terms.

Construction.

We implement one first-order Trotter step.
Under the JW transformation, each term
e^{-iJ_{ijkl}\,\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l}\,\Delta t}
becomes a multi-qubit Pauli rotation realized by a
CNOT
staircase sandwiching a
Z
-rotation; the same primitive appears in block-encoding approaches to SYK simulation.
To exploit parallelism, we partition terms into non-conflicting groups via greedy graph coloring on the mode-conflict graph (two terms conflict if they share a fermionic mode).
For each color group
\alpha
, a packing permutation
\pi_{\alpha}
maps the active modes to consecutive JW positions; after
\mathcal{F}_{\pi_{\alpha}}
, every Pauli string in the group is local and the staircases execute in parallel.
A Trotter step thus consists of, for each color group, a forward FP, parallel local rotations, and an inverse FP.
The local-rotation cost is a constant depending only on
k
and is identical across all FP-based methods, so the only variable is the routing cost.

\sqrt{N}

N\approx 200

VI
Related Work

The textbook approach sorts modes along the snake JW chain using
FSWAP
networks
27
, yielding
O(N)
depth and
O(N^{2})
gates on an
L\times L
grid, leaving the grid’s second axis unused. Two recent works improve on this baseline by observing that any permutation on
N
elements admits an
O(\log N)
-layer decomposition into structured primitives. Maskara et al.
33
use merge-sort-style interleaves, achieving
O(\log N)
depth with
\Theta(N)
ancillas and mid-circuit measurement with classical feedforward. Constantinides et al.
10
instead use staircase permutations from recursive bisection, and further show that any product-preserving ternary-tree encoding (a class that includes JW, BK, and Parity) can be fermionically permuted in
O(\log^{2}N)
depth without ancillas. Neither decomposition, however, is tailored to 2D connectivity: both rely on all-to-all interaction, and on a physical 2D grid each layer inherits a
\Theta(\sqrt{N})
routing penalty
14
, pushing the effective depth to
O(\sqrt{N}\polylog N)
, a log factor above the
\Omega(\sqrt{N})
lower bound. Explicit 2D-NN compilations of both algorithms are derived in Appendices
B
and
C
.

Our natively 2D decomposition closes this gap with zero ancillas, and via a Hilbert-curve layout (Section
IV
) we match
10
for JW, BK, and Parity; whether the general product-preserving ternary-tree result extends to
O(\sqrt{N})
depth on the 2D grid remains an interesting open question.

The idea of a diagonal parity-correction unitary for vertical hops on the 2D grid was first introduced by Jiang et al.
24
, who use
\Theta(\sqrt{N})
traveling ancillas in an extra column adjacent to the grid to implement two specific subroutines, the 2D fermionic Fourier transform and Hubbard-model Trotter steps, in
O(\sqrt{N})
depth. Our
\Gamma
construction (Section
III.5
) recasts the unitary through a phase-polynomial lens, eliminating the ancillas, cutting the per-
\Gamma
depth constant from
13L
to
8L
(
{\sim}38\%
reduction), and generalizing from fixed subroutines to arbitrary fermionic permutations.

VII
Conclusion

Implementing arbitrary fermionic permutations on 2D nearest-neighbor hardware is the dominant routing bottleneck in fermionic simulation, yet no prior method simultaneously saturates the
\Omega(\sqrt{N})
lower bound and avoids ancillas. We close this gap via algorithm-architecture co-design: Hall’s Row-Column-Row decomposition tailors the routing to the grid, and a phase-polynomial
\Gamma
operator absorbs every vertical parity correction in a single fused sweep per row. The result is an ancilla-free protocol of asymptotically optimal depth even against LOCC protocols with
\Theta(N)
ancillas, and a Hilbert-curve layout lifts the same
O(\sqrt{N})
advantage to the Bravyi–Kitaev and Parity encodings. Across standalone permutation, the FFFT, and sparse SYK Trotter simulation, our method overtakes the strongest baselines near
N\approx 100
and reaches
50
–
60\%
depth reductions by
N\approx 900
, directly shrinking the logical budget for fermionic simulation on early fault-tolerant devices. Two questions remain open: whether the
O(\sqrt{N})
ancilla-free bound extends from JW, BK, and Parity to the full class of product-preserving ternary-tree encodings on the 2D grid, and whether the constant prefactor of
\Gamma
can be reduced further by jointly optimizing the row-column schedule. Both would tighten the practical resource estimate at the scales where fermionic simulation begins to outperform classical methods.

Appendix B
2D Nearest-Neighbor Compilation of the Reconfigurable Merge-Sort Permutation

This appendix presents the explicit 2D nearest-neighbor (2D-NN) compilation of the merge-sort fermionic permutation of Maskara et al.

Below we identify the four points at which the algorithm relies on the reconfigurable assumption and substitute, at each, the cheapest 2D-NN realization compatible with the rest of the pipeline; to the best of our knowledge the resulting compilation achieves the lowest 2D-NN depth currently known for this baseline. Mid-circuit measurement and classical feedforward are already compatible with 2D-NN connectivity, and we keep them as in the original.

Appendix C
2D Nearest-Neighbor Compilation of the All-to-All Ancilla-Free Staircase Permutation

Appendix D
Hilbert-Curve Disjointness for BK
\to
JW Rounds

This appendix proves the claim used in Section
IV.2
: in every round of the BK
\to
JW conversion, the
CNOT
intervals on the inorder line admit pairwise-disjoint dyadic containers of comparable length. Under the Hilbert layout, these containers become pairwise-disjoint axis-aligned rectangles of diameter
O(\sqrt{|J|})
, so all cascades in a round route in parallel and the total conversion depth is
O(\sqrt{N})
.

We prove this for
N=2^{k}-1
with
k\geq 2
, and index both qubits
i\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
and Hilbert cells
\{0,1,\dots,2^{k}-1\}
from zero. Qubit
i
is placed at cell
H_{k}(i)
, with cell
2^{k}-1
unused.

D.1
Dyadic intervals and the Hilbert layout

A
dyadic interval of order
q
is an interval

[a2^{q},\,(a+1)2^{q}-1]\subseteq[0,2^{k}-1];

it has length
2^{q}
. Two elementary facts about dyadic intervals underpin the entire argument: they are closed under aligned translations, and they map to rectangles under the Hilbert layout.

Lemma 5

(Translation invariance)
.

Let
D=[a2^{q},(a+1)2^{q}-1]
be a dyadic interval of order
q
, and let
t
be a nonnegative multiple of
2^{q}
. Then

t+D\;=\;[t+a2^{q},\,t+(a+1)2^{q}-1]

is a dyadic interval of order
q
.

Proof.

Writing
t=b\cdot 2^{q}
gives
t+D=[(a+b)2^{q},\,(a+b+1)2^{q}-1]
.
∎

Proposition 6

(Hilbert dyadic-rectangle property)
.

Under
H_{k}
, every dyadic interval of order
q
maps to an axis-aligned rectangle of area
2^{q}
with side lengths
2^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}
and
2^{\lceil q/2\rceil}
. Disjoint dyadic intervals map to non-overlapping rectangles.

Proof.

The finite Hilbert layout
H_{k}
fills a
2^{\lceil k/2\rceil}\times 2^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}
rectangle via four recursive
H_{k-2}
calls on the four quarter-rectangles
22
;
4
; in particular, any two consecutive subcalls together cover an axis-aligned half of their parent square.

Let
J=[a2^{q},(a+1)2^{q}-1]
. If
q\equiv k\pmod{2}
, then
J
is exactly the domain of a single recursive subcall, so
H_{k}(J)
is one subrectangle of area
2^{q}
. If
q\not\equiv k\pmod{2}
, write
J=J_{1}\sqcup J_{2}
as two consecutive dyadic intervals of order
q-1
; each
J_{t}
is the domain of a subcall, and the two subcalls are consecutive within a common parent call of area
2^{q+1}
. Their images therefore fill an axis-aligned half of that parent, so
H_{k}(J)
is again an axis-aligned rectangle of area
2^{q}
. The claimed side lengths follow in both cases, and disjointness is preserved because
H_{k}
is a bijection on cells.
∎

D.2
The local subproblem

We now isolate the combinatorial object at the heart of one round of
CNOTs
.

Definition 1

(Local-subproblem family
\mathcal{I}_{d,r}
)
.

For integers
d\geq 1
and
1\leq r\leq d
, define the collection of intervals
\mathcal{I}_{d,r}
in
[0,2^{d}-1]
recursively by

\mathcal{I}_{d,1}\;=\;\bigl\{[2^{d-1}-1,\,2^{d}-1]\bigr\},

and, for
r\geq 2
,

\mathcal{I}_{d,r}\;=\;\mathcal{I}_{d-1,r-1}\;\sqcup\;\bigl(2^{d-1}+\mathcal{I}_{d-1,r-1}\bigr),

where
t+[a,b]:=[t+a,t+b]
and
t+\mathcal{A}:=\{t+J:J\in\mathcal{A}\}
.

Why
\mathcal{I}_{d,r}
is the BK
\to
JW access pattern.

Consider a
depth-
d
local subproblem
: a balanced BK subtree occupying cells
0,\dots,2^{d}-2
together with an external parent at cell
2^{d}-1
. Its root sits at the median cell
2^{d-1}-1
, so sub-round
1
fires the single
CNOT
on endpoints
(2^{d-1}-1,\,2^{d}-1)
, matching
\mathcal{I}_{d,1}
. This rotation splits the remaining work into two depth-
(d-1)
local subproblems: the left child on cells
[0,2^{d-1}-2]
inherits cell
2^{d-1}-1
as its external parent, and the right child on cells
[2^{d-1},2^{d}-2]
retains the original external parent. After subtracting
2^{d-1}
from the right child’s cells, both children are identical copies of a depth-
(d-1)
local subproblem, and following the same logic, sub-round
r\geq 2
of the parent decomposes as sub-round
(r-1)
of the left child together with a
2^{d-1}
-translate of sub-round
(r-1)
of the right child—exactly the recursion defining
\mathcal{I}_{d,r}
. Hence
\mathcal{I}_{d,r}
enumerates the inorder intervals of the
CNOTs
fired in sub-round
r
of a depth-
d
local subproblem. We also verified this through extensive numerical simulations.

The crucial structural property of
\mathcal{I}_{d,r}
is that its intervals admit pairwise-disjoint dyadic containers.

Lemma 7

(Dyadic containers for
\mathcal{I}_{d,r}
)
.

For every
d\geq 1
and
1\leq r\leq d
, there is an assignment
J\mapsto D(J)
such that, for each
J\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}
,
D(J)\subseteq[0,2^{d}-1]
is a dyadic interval with

|J|\;\leq\;|D(J)|\;\leq\;2|J|,

and the family
\{D(J):J\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}\}
is pairwise disjoint.

Proof.

We induct on
r
. For
r=1
, the only interval is
J=[2^{d-1}-1,\,2^{d}-1]
; taking
D(J)=[0,2^{d}-1]
yields
|J|=2^{d-1}+1
and
|D(J)|=2^{d}
, satisfying the bounds.

For
r\geq 2
, write
\mathcal{I}_{d,r}=\mathcal{I}_{d-1,r-1}\sqcup(2^{d-1}+\mathcal{I}_{d-1,r-1})
. By induction, each
J\in\mathcal{I}_{d-1,r-1}
has a dyadic container
D(J)\subseteq[0,2^{d-1}-1]
with
|J|\leq|D(J)|\leq 2|J|
, and the left-copy containers are pairwise disjoint. Since every
|D(J)|
is a power of two at most
2^{d-1}
, the translation amount
2^{d-1}
is a multiple of
|D(J)|
; Lemma

then gives that

is dyadic of the same order, providing a valid container for
2^{d-1}+J\in 2^{d-1}+\mathcal{I}_{d-1,r-1}
with the same length bounds. Left-copy containers lie in
[0,2^{d-1}-1]
and right-copy containers lie in
[2^{d-1},2^{d}-1]
, so the combined family is pairwise disjoint.
∎

D.3

The full BK

Corollary 9

(BK
\to
JW conversion depth)
.

Under the Hilbert layout, the total BK
\to
JW conversion depth on an
L\times L
grid (

) is

.

Proof.

By Theorem
