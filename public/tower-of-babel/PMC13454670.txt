A rare right coronary artery arising from the left anterior descending artery with a critical left main lesion: a case report

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Coronary artery anomalies are uncommon, but certain variants carry important clinical implications and can influence risk assessment and revascularization planning.1 Among these, an anomalous right coronary artery (RCA) arising from the left anterior descending artery (LAD) is very rare and is most often reported in isolated case reports and small series.2,3 Contemporary descriptions still suggest that only a few dozen cases have been reported overall.4

Acute coronary syndromes (ACS) involving the left main (LM) are intrinsically high-risk because a large myocardial territory is jeopardized if LM flow is compromised. When an anomalous RCA originates from the LAD, a distal LM lesion located before the LAD/left circumflex artery (LCx) bifurcation and proximal to the anomalous RCA origin can jeopardize the LAD, LCx, and anomalous RCA territories, creating an expanded left-main jeopardy territory in which even transient LM compromise may be catastrophic. This anatomical–clinical combination adds complexity to revascularization decisions and procedural strategy in the ACS setting.5

Case presentation

A 40-year-old male, non-diabetic, heavy smoker presented with typical chest pain of 1-h duration. On examination, he was diaphoretic but haemodynamically stable (heart rate 90 b.p.m., blood pressure 135/85 mmHg). His lung fields were clear with no signs of heart failure (Killip class I). The electrocardiogram showed no significant ischaemic changes (Figure 1). High-sensitivity troponin (ng/L) rose from 300 at 0 h to 1700 at 2 h. Baseline laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, and coagulation profile, were within normal limits. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated mild anterior wall hypokinesia with preserved left ventricular systolic function. He received 300 mg of aspirin and was transferred to the catheterization laboratory for early invasive evaluation. Procedural unfractionated heparin was administered in the catheterization laboratory.

ECG at presentation showing no significant ischaemic changes.

Coronary angiography was undertaken for acute coronary ACS without ST-segment elevation via a right radial approach (6F). Several attempts to engage the RCA were unsuccessful; therefore, the left coronary system was assessed. Angiography demonstrated a severe lesion involving the distal LM and the ostial LAD, estimated at 80%–90%, An anomalous RCA arising from the proximal LAD was identified without significant stenosis, and no significant disease was noted in the LCx or other vessels (Figure 2), (see Supplementary material online, Video S1). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) showed plaque rupture with thrombus in the distal LM (see Supplementary material online, Video S2). An urgent Heart Team discussion recommended immediate coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). However, after detailed counselling, the patient categorically refused CABG and stated that he would accept percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) only. Given the unstable culprit lesion, with OCT-confirmed plaque rupture and thrombus in the distal LM, located proximal to both the LAD/LCx bifurcation and the anomalous RCA origin from the LAD abrupt occlusion was considered to carry a high risk of fatal outcome. In addition, the patient was young and non-diabetic, and the anatomical complexity was low with a calculated SYNTAX score of 13. Although the LM lesion was critical, TIMI 3 flow was preserved to all vessels, and the patient remained haemodynamically stable with preserved left ventricular systolic function. We therefore proceeded with OCT-guided PCI, with full mechanical circulatory support options (ECMO, Impella, and intra-aortic balloon pump) available on standby.

Coronary angiography demonstrates an anomalous right coronary artery arising from the left anterior descending artery and a critical distal left main lesion.

Percutaneous coronary intervention was performed via the right radial approach. A 6F JL 3.5 guiding catheter was used to engage the LM, and both the LAD and LCx were wired. Lesion preparation was performed cautiously and stepwise. Initial predilatation was performed using a 3.0 × 8 mm conventional balloon followed by a 4.0 mm non-compliant balloon. Because the lesion remained resistant with persistent focal under-expansion, a 3.5 × 10 mm cutting balloon was used focally, followed by a 3.5 × 12 mm non-compliant balloon. During lesion preparation, there was thrombus migration with pinching of the LCx ostium; therefore, kissing balloon inflation to the LAD/LCx was performed with a good immediate result. Given the focal nature of the distal LM/ostial LAD culprit lesion, a short drug-eluting stent was selected to provide adequate lesion coverage while avoiding unnecessary metal burden and extension into relatively healthy vessel segments. A 4.0 × 18 mm drug-eluting stent was deployed from the LM into the LAD, followed by post-dilatation with a 4.5 mm non-compliant balloon and proximal optimization technique using a 5.0 × 8 mm non-compliant balloon. Final angiography showed an excellent result with TIMI 3 flow (see Supplementary material online, Video S3). The procedure was OCT-guided, and the final OCT run confirmed a well-expanded, well-apposed stent without complications (see Supplementary material online, Video S4). Quantitative OCT assessment demonstrated a pre-PCI minimal lumen area of 3.57 mm2 in the distal LM and 5.01 mm2 in the proximal LAD. After stent implantation and optimization, the final minimal stent area was 20.35 mm2 in the LM and 13.06 mm2 in the proximal LAD (Figure 3).

Optical coherence tomography measurements before and after left main-left anterior descending artery percutaneous coronary intervention.

Throughout the procedure, the patient remained haemodynamically stable, and no pharmacological or mechanical circulatory support was required. He was kept in hospital for 2 days and treated with aspirin, ticagrelor, a statin, and a beta-blocker. His LDL cholesterol was 2.7 mmol/L and HbA1c was 4.5%. Pre-discharge transthoracic echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 60% with no regional wall motion abnormalities. He stopped smoking and is attending a smoking cessation clinic. At 8-month follow-up, the patient remained completely asymptomatic, was exercising regularly, and was compliant with his medications. ECG was normal and repeat transthoracic echocardiography was normal with an LVEF of 60%. CT coronary angiography confirmed an anomalous RCA arising from the proximal LAD with a prepulmonic/anterior course, without an inter-arterial course between the aorta and pulmonary artery, and demonstrated a patent LM stent (Figures 4 and 5).

Discussion

This case highlights a high-risk anatomical setting created by a rare coronary anomaly—an anomalous RCA arising from the LAD. In ACS, this configuration can markedly increase the myocardium at risk.2,3,4 In the present case, the culprit distal LM lesion was located proximal to both the LAD/LCx bifurcation and the anomalous RCA origin from the LAD. Therefore, compromise at this site could jeopardize the LAD, LCx, and anomalous RCA territories, creating an expanded left-main jeopardy territory.6

Despite the potential for global myocardial jeopardy in this anatomy, the patient presented with non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), no ischaemic ECG changes, and preserved left ventricular systolic function. This highlights that in LM-related ACS—particularly when the LM represents the sole proximal inflow—clinical stability and a non-diagnostic ECG do not exclude a high-risk culprit lesion, and early invasive assessment remains warranted.7 In our case, angiography identified severe distal LM/ostial LAD stenosis, and OCT confirmed plaque rupture with thrombus, establishing an unstable culprit mechanism and supporting urgent revascularization. An urgent Heart Team discussion recommended immediate CABG. However, after detailed counselling, the patient categorically refused surgery and stated that he would accept PCI only. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) was therefore pursued as an individualized strategy, supported by the patient’s young age, non-diabetic status, low anatomical complexity with a calculated SYNTAX score of 13, and the need to secure an unstable LM culprit lesion in a uniquely high-risk anatomical setting, within a shared decision-making framework.5,8

Intracoronary imaging was pivotal in this case. In a distal LM culprit lesion within an expanded left-main jeopardy territory, OCT provided high-resolution characterization of the culprit pathology (plaque rupture with superimposed thrombus) and enabled an imaging-guided PCI strategy with careful stent sizing and optimization, aiming to secure the LM–LAD segment and minimize procedural complications.9

Conclusion

This case highlights how very rare coronary anatomy can substantially increase the myocardium at risk in LM ACS and should influence early risk stratification and revascularization planning. Timely Heart Team discussion and patient-centred decision-making are crucial, particularly when surgical revascularization is declined. In such settings, early intracoronary imaging–guided PCI can achieve effective revascularization with preservation of myocardial function and a favourable 8-month clinical outcome.

Patient perspective

When bypass surgery was recommended, I felt anxious. I was also surprised to learn that my right heart artery was coming from the left side instead of its usual origin. After discussing the options with the doctors, I chose a less invasive stenting procedure. I was happy to recover quickly and have remained well and symptom free.
