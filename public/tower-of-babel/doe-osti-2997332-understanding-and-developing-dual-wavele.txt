ABSTRACT
This report details the goals, approach, results, and future needs of a three-year Laboratory Directed
Research and Development project funded by Sandia’s Materials Science Research Foundation. In
this project, we established basic principles and mechanistic understanding of orthogonal or
sequential chemistries for fabricating multi-material or robust materials. We developed numerous
additive manufacturing, or 3D-printing, techniques including volumetric solid-state printing, dual-
wavelength printing, and lithographic regulation of polymer crystallinity. We leveraged these new
printing motifs to expand upon what is possible with manufacturing to give bespoke optical and
mechanical performance. Moreover, we discovered new techniques for depolymerization enabling
recovery of pristine carbon-fiber and high value electronics under moderate conditions.

1. OVERARCHING GOALS
Stereolithography (SLA) AM has a greater capability for reproducibly manufacturing, high resolution
parts as with other additive manufacturing (AM) techniques, and, therefore, has found extensive use
in industry. Unfortunately, conventional SLA processes are slow (<40 mm/hr) and limited to radical
polymerizations, generally producing brittle, low strength materials. Innovative continuous-SLA (C-
SLA) processes have demonstrated printing rates of 2000 mm/hr by utilizing a thin liquid ‘dead zone’
adjacent to the projection window where polymerization does not occur to eliminate the sluggish
delamination step required in traditional SLA processes. Yet, C-SLA processes remain limited to the
manufacturing of weak materials through radical polymerizations.
In a brief FY21 exploratory project, we demonstrated the photo-initiation of ring-opening metathesis
polymerization (ROMP) to afford thermoset materials with improved thermo-mechanical properties
that can be printed using C-SLA at rates approaching industry-leading technologies. This proof-of-
concept work was restricted to a single resin system with limited printing rates, print geometries, and
final material properties. However, a broad range of monomers active towards ROMP exists or can
be readily synthesized to achieve tailored material properties. These material properties are not only
limited to thermomechanical response, but include properties such as degradability, biocompatibility,
and chemical/environmental stability.
Here, we ask; can a library of monomers be built to tailor manufacturing to performance and
application? Moreover, through AM, can the manufacturing process dictate material properties to
enable bespoke performance.
Despite the numerous examples of monomers for ROMP that exists in literature, to-date,
dicyclopentadiene (DCPD) has exclusively been used as monomer for bulk-phase, photo-initiated
ROMP due to its high ring strain and excellent mechanical properties upon polymerization. Thus,
while this study will be focused on printing of tailored components, this effort represents a first-of-
its-kind study of enormous influence to the burgening fields of photo-ROMP and lithography.
The outcomes of these studies will enable the rapid development of application-tailored components
for mission-relevant applications.

2. OVERVIEW OF APPROACH
SLA AM has been leveraged for the fabrication of products ranging from medical devices,[1, 2] to
made-to-order athletic wear,[3] to aerospace components.[4, 5] During SLA, a 3D object is produced
layer-wise through a series of cross-sectional curing steps using a photopolymerizable resin. However,
SLA and related methods rely, almost exclusively, on free radical polymerization chemistry, limiting
the diversity of available monomers (e.g., acrylates) and thus material properties.[6-11] An additional
limitation of SLA printing is the time-consuming delamination and recoating steps between each
printed layer, restricting printing speed to millimeters or centimeters per hour.[12] Continuous liquid
interface production (CLIP) has addressed this limitation by creating a layer of inhibited
polymerization within the photoresin adjacent to the projection window such that delamination and
 recoating is unnecessary.[12-14] Our work
 addressed the two largest limitations of
 traditional SLA AM, sluggish printing
 rates and restricted material properties.

While this three-month exploratory project was successful in terms of output and evaluation of

amenability of the C-SLA process to other monomer systems enabling application-driven material
selection . Moreover, while the method developed demonstrated the feasibility of C-SLA, the
irreversible release or ‘uncaging’ of amines resulted in accumulation of inhibition chemistries over
time and a decrease in printing resolution and rate. Fortunately, in the last month of the project, we
discovered a reversible radical generator, HABI, capable of rapidly inhibiting polymerization at a
selected wavelength while preventing accumulation of the inhibition agent due to its reversibility.[16,
17] However, the mechanism for catalyst deactivation and polymerization inhibition is unknown and
the optimization of C-SLA with this new system is unfinished – motivating further investigation.

3. KEY RESULTS

3.1. Published Work
Much of the key results of this project have already been published in peer-reviewed journals. These
publications are summarized here with a brief synopsis and figure capturing the work. This includes
two reviews, one invited, that summarize SNL leadership in the field.

3.1.1. PhotoROMP: The Future Is Bright [28]
Since the earliest investigations of olefin metathesis catalysis, light has been the choice for
controlling the catalyst activity on demand. From the perspective of energy efficiency, temporal and
spatial control, and selectivity, photochemistry is not only an attractive alternative to traditional
thermal manufacturing techniques but also arguably a superior manifold for advanced applications
like additive manufacturing (AM). In the last three decades, pioneering work in the field of ring-
opening metathesis polymerization (ROMP) has broadened the scope of material properties
achievable through AM, particularly using light as both an activating and deactivating stimulus. In
this Perspective, we explore trends in photocontrolled ROMP systems with an emphasis on
approaches to photoinduced activation and deactivation of metathesis catalysts. Recent work has
yielded a myriad of commercial and synthetically accessible photosensitive catalyst systems, although
comparatively little attention has been paid to achieving precise control over polymer morphology
using light. Metal-free, photophysical, and living ROMP systems have also been relatively
underexplored. To take fuller advantage of both the thermomechanical properties of ROMP
polymers and the operational simplicity of photocontrol, clear directions for the field are to improve
the reversibility of activation and deactivation strategies as well as to further develop
photocontrolled approaches to tuning cross-link density and polymer tacticity.

Vat photopolymerization (VP) has emerged as a promising additive manufacturing technique to
allow rapid light-based fabrication of 3D objects from a liquid resin. Research in the field of vat

applied chemistry and physics. This perspective brings together early-career researchers from various

outlook on next-generation polymer processing methods from synthesis of novel materials to
multimodality manufacturing and performance engineering. Further, this article combines the ideas
of many of these junior researchers to present a vision for the future of the field by highlighting the
challenges and opportunities that lie ahead.

3.1.3. Volumetric Additive Manufacturing of Dicyclopentadiene by Solid-State
 Photopolymerization[30]
Polymerization in the solid state is generally infeasible due to restrictions on mobility. However, in
this work, the solid-state photopolymerization of crystalline dicyclopentadiene is demonstrated via
photoinitiated ring-opening metathesis polymerization. The source of mobility in the solid state is
attributed to the plastic crystal nature of dicyclopentadiene, which yields local short-range mobility
due to orientational degrees of freedom. Polymerization in the solid state enables photopatterning,
volumetric additive manufacturing of free-standing structures, and fabrication with embedded
components. Solid-state photopolymerization of dicyclopentadiene offers a new paradigm for
advanced and freeform fabrication of high-performance thermosets.

3.2.1. Regenerative Digital Light Processing of Thermosets via Photoinitiated
 Ring-Opening Metathesis Polymerization

3.2.1.1.   Abstract
 Alternatives to radical-initiated acrylate resins are now being explored for vat
photopolymerization, enabling printing of thermoset materials with property and performance
benefits. However, as with many thermosets, these materials are often challenging to reprocess,
limiting their lifecycle circularity. Poly(dicyclopentadiene) (pDCPD) is one such material with excellent
properties that can be additively manufactured via photoinitiated ring-opening metathesis
polymerization. Introducing cleavable units into networks via comonomers is a common method for
enabling breakdown and reprocessing of thermosets. This method has also been used for regeneration
of pDCPD with good success and ability to maintain its desirable properties. Inspired by recent
literature, we use silyl ether comonomers that facilitate network deconstruction and demonstrate
compatibility with digital light processing (DLP). We characterize the material properties and
deconstruction behavior of printed components and further illustrate the feasibility of regenerative
DLP over multiple cycles by incorporating deconstructed fragments into the printing resins. As
opposed to prior work, our process exhibits minimal alterations in material properties and printing
parameters, even after two cycles of regeneration. This offers a promising strategy for the regenerative
printing of high-performance thermosets, contributing to the advancement of circular additive
manufacturing practices.

3.2.1.2.   Introduction
 The polymer industry plays a crucial role in providing a wide array of essential consumer
products. However, the escalating manufacturing demand for plastic feedstock necessitates enhanced
lifecycle of these materials.[31-33] Additive manufacturing (AM) offers a promising avenue for
improving the sustainability of polymeric materials by minimizing waste generation, allowing for the
precise deposition of required material and conserving excess.[34-37] Vat photopolymerization (VP)
is an emerging AM method excelling in printing rate, resolution, and geometric complexity.[29] To
date, VP is primarily used for the fabrication of thermoset materials, with a large portion utilizing
radical-initiated photopolymerization of acrylate-based resins.[38] Increasingly more work has focused
on alternative chemistries, unlocking new potential for achievable materials and their properties.[39-
41] However, thermosets are notoriously difficult to reprocess due to the presence of permanent
covalent crosslinks, limiting the achievable lifecycle circularity of these materials.
 Chemical strategies have been developed to address breakdown and regeneration of thermoset
polymers, primarily focusing on network adjustment to include dynamic, deconstructable, or
depolymerizable bonds.[42-48] To enhance the impact of these proposed recycling methods, focusing
on the circularity of commercially relevant materials is essential as the barriers to incentivizing
recycling remain substantial.[49] One promising candidate, made from inexpensive byproducts of the
oil and gas industry, is poly(dicyclopentadiene) (pDCPD), a high-performance material with industrial
significance due to its desirable properties such as impact resistance, high heat deflection temperature,
chemical corrosion resistance, and low shrinkage.[50, 51] Furthermore, pDCPD can be processed by

 15

ring-opening metathesis polymerization (ROMP) in the presence of a transition metal catalyst.[52, 53]
Recent advancements in the digital light processing (DLP), a VP AM method, of pDCPD through
photoinitiated ring-opening metathesis polymerization (photoROMP) have led to the development of
commercially available resins.[28, 54-57] Here, we are interested in combining latent ruthenium
catalysts with photosensitizers, a recently developed method that makes a large variety of latent
ruthenium catalysts accessible for photoROMP.[30, 58-60]
 Incorporating cleavable comonomers into dicyclopentadiene (DCPD) resins has been
established as a viable strategy for fabricating pDCPD materials that can be readily deconstructed.
One such comonomer, 2,3-dihydrofuran (DHF), has been utilized for VP, yet its compatibility with
photoROMP processes was suboptimal and not conducive to regenerative printing.[61] Indeed, the
inherent volatility and inhibitory nature of DHF resulted in components with poor resolution,
extended printing times, and required the periodic addition of fresh resin to replace the evaporated
comonomer. In contrast, the development of non-volatile and highly-reactive silyl ether comonomers
offers an exciting alternative for VP manufacturing, presenting opportunities for improved printing
capabilities and the deconstruction of networks.[24, 62-67] Furthermore, recent work on material
regeneration using silyl ether comonomer systems suggests a mechanism for addressing the end-of-
life challenges of additively manufactured thermosets.[24, 68-71]
 Here, we investigate the application of a silyl ether comonomer in the VP of pDCPD
copolymer thermosets. We demonstrate significant advancements over previous comonomer systems
regarding printing performance and report on the material properties achieved through photoROMP.
Additionally, we extend our research to encompass regenerative AM by reincorporating deconstructed
thermosets into printing resins. Notably, our findings indicate retention of rapid photopolymerization
and thermomechanical properties over multiple generations, underscoring the potential for circular
practices in the realm of thermoset AM.

3.2.1.3. Results and Discussion

3.2.1.3.1. DLP Printing with Cleavable Comonomers
 DLP is a VP additive manufacturing technique that constructs parts layer-by-layer from a
liquid monomer or oligomer mixture through projected patterns from a light engine (Figure 7A). This
method offers significant advantages, including enhanced geometric complexity, isotropic material
properties, and improved print rates and resolution. We leverage the recently identified synergy
between photosensitizers and latent ruthenium catalysts, in conjunction with the coinitiator ethyl 4-
(dimethylamino)benzoate (EDAB), to effectively polymerize resins containing DCPD and cleavable
comonomers via photoROMP (Figure 7B). Specifically, we utilize the ruthenium catalyst M220 and
the photosensitizer benzophenone, a formulation validated in previous research, and incorporate the
cleavable comonomers DHF and iPrSi8 at varying loadings (Table S1) to facilitate the subsequent
deconstruction of the thermoset in the presence of cleaving agents (Figure 7C).

 Previous studies utilizing DHF as a comonomer aimed to introduce cleavable segments into
the polymer network but required a catalyst concentration of 0.1 mol%, an order of magnitude higher
than the standard catalyst amount, for photoROMP of DCPD.[61] This elevated catalyst
concentration, combined with a lengthy exposure time of 60 seconds per 20 µm layer at 60 mW cm-2,
highlighted the sluggish polymerization rates. This inhibition was attributed to the formation of a
thermodynamically stable Fischer carbene upon ring-opening of the DHF comonomer. Additionally,
the inherent volatility of DHF necessitated frequent changes of the vat resin every 10 layers to
maintain its presence in the prints and enable deconstruction. Photopolymerizing DCPD with DHF
at reduced catalyst loadings (in this case 0.04 mol%) requires extended times to achieve crosslinking,
indicated by the modulus crossover via photorheology (Figure S1). As such, adding DHF increases
the dose (i.e., light intensity and exposure time) required to achieve appropriate cure depths for
printing compared to neat DCPD resins (Figure 7D). This formulation additionally results in
difficulties obtaining cohesive 3D objects, and the combination of high light intensities (385 nm at
120 mW cm-2) and prolonged exposure times (2-5 minutes) led to critical failures of the printer’s light

 17

engine. Consequently, we shift our approach to 2D photopatterning of a skeletal fish, which similarly
requires extended exposure times for crosslinking, and we note a deterioration in the resolution of the
photopattern with increasing DHF loading (Figure 7D). Ultimately, this experiment culminated in
another critical failure of the light engine. Previous work indicated that optimal deconstruction of
materials necessitated DHF loadings around 20 mol%, underscoring its inadequacy for DLP printing
due to significant reductions in photopolymerization rate, patterning resolution, and its volatility.
 In contrast, the silyl ether comonomer (iPrSi8) demonstrates superior performance for DLP
printing. We observe little impact on time to obtain crosslinked materials via photorheology (Figure
S2). At comparable molar loadings to DHF, iPrSi8 exhibits minimal impact on cure depth (Figure 7E)
and reaction rate as determined by real-time IR (Figure S3). We successfully achieve 3D printing with
exposure times of 30 seconds (385 nm at 120 mW cm-2) for 100 µm layers, a substantial improvement
over the previous DHF system. As we increase the loading of iPrSi8, we maintain consistent printing
conditions, resulting in parts with good resolution as exemplified by the printed chess pieces (Figure
1E). These findings highlight the advantages of silyl ether comonomers in retaining the efficiency and
effectiveness of DLP processes.

3.2.1.3.2. Properties and Deconstruction of Thermosets
 Following the printing process, we implement a post-curing procedure involving exposure to
UV light followed by thermal treatment as complete network formation via photoROMP is
challenging due to vitrification of the green body.[59, 60] To confirm the extent of curing in our
system, we analyze residual exotherms through differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) of uncured
liquid resins, materials after photocure, and materials after sequential photo and thermal cure (Figure
S4). Our results indicate a significant reduction in the residual exotherm after photocuring. However,
a small residual exotherm remains, suggesting incomplete conversion (~80% conversion, Table S2),
and underscoring the necessity for additional thermal curing (180 °C for 4 hours). To investigate the
role of degradation during the thermal postcure, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted on
photocured samples. Less than 5% weight loss was observed up to 200 °C (Figure S5) and no more
weight was lost during a prolonged 4 hour exposure (Figure S6).
 As previously established, the incorporation of cleavable comonomers, including iPrSi8, tends
to lower the glass transition temperature (Tg) of the polymer system as indicated by the shift in tan
delta peaks from dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) experiments (Figure 8A, S7-S11). The Tg values
we obtain are consistent with prior studies that employed thermal curing or frontal polymerization
with silyl ether comonomers.[24, 71] Notably, we observe a decrease in tensile properties, specifically
Young’s modulus and tensile strength, with increasing silyl ether loading(Figure 8B, S7-S11). We
additionally characterize the properties of materials that underwent photocuring without a subsequent
thermal treatment (Figure S7-S10), and observe similar trends to thermally treated materials (Figure
S7-S11). Gel fraction analyses further confirm the incorporation of the silyl ether monomer into the
network after photo and thermal cure (Figure S12). DMA experiments suggest enhanced network
homogeneity following thermal treatment (Figure S7-S10). Ultimately, we observe modulus values on
the order of 1 GPa at a 20 wt% iPrSi8 loading, aligning well with foundational work utilizing thermal
curing methods.[24]

 We next investigate the deconstruction of the printed thermosets using hydrochloric acid
(HCl) as a cleaving agent. Materials are cut and placed into a vial of 1M HCl in THF for 6 hours, after
which time the residual mass is removed and weighed (Figure 8C). We observe that full deconstruction
is not achieved, with residual mass plateauing at approximately 15% for the material containing 20
wt% iPrSi8 (Figure 2D). We hypothesize that this limitation is due to oxidative crosslinking occurring
on the surface of the deconstructed materials, a phenomenon exacerbated by the high-temperature
thermal treatment conducted in an air atmosphere. Previous work using iPrSi8 required a minimum
of 7.5 mol% loading to achieve complete deconstruction. Here, we observe a significant increase in
deconstruction efficiency between 10 wt% (6.4 mol%) and 20 wt% (13.3 mol%) iPrSi8 loading, in
good agreement with these previous results.
 Further analysis of the deconstruction time of materials with 20 wt% iPrSi8 reveals that the
lowest residual mass is achieved at the 6-hour mark within a 24-hour period (Figure S13). The slight
increase in residual mass observed at 9 and 24 hours may indicate ongoing oxidation processes
affecting the residual solids. To explore this phenomenon further, we also attempted to deconstruct
the 20 wt% iPrSi8 material with the inclusion of the antioxidant butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT).
This modification resulted in an even lower residual mass of 8%, nearly half that observed without
the antioxidant, suggesting that the residual mass primarily corresponds to material oxidized prior to
deconstruction or during the manufacturing process. Notably, pDCPD is known to form an oxidation
layer approximately 100-300 µm in thickness.[72] This layer offers advantages as it imparts a chemically
inert protective barrier without compromising the bulk properties of manufactured components.
Therefore, elimination of extensive oxidation was not further pursued.
 Soluble fragments of the deconstructed pDCPD material were recovered by precipitating the
filtered deconstruction solution in cold methanol as previously described (90% yield).[24]

 19

Interestingly, gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis of these fragments reveals negligible
differences in fragment size across samples with varying iPrSi8 loadings and deconstruction times
(Figure S14, Tables S3 and S4), which contrasts with previous findings that indicated a decrease in
fragment size with increasing iPrSi8 content. This observation, along with the tensile property results,
may suggest a fundamental difference in the network formation of materials produced via
photoROMP.

3.2.1.3.3. Regenerative DLP of Thermosets
 Previous research has demonstrated the feasibility of reincorporating fragments through
partial metathesis and utilizing them as filler content without compromising tensile performance.[24,
68, 69] In this study, we aim to enable regenerative printing by incorporating fragments across multiple
generations by including iPrSi8 in subsequent networks. We use 20 wt% iPrSi8 as our benchmark
material for these demonstrations. Fragments are incorporated at specific loadings as a replacement
of neat DCPD monomer (see Table S5 for formulations). Initial screening via real-time IR reveals that
the conversion rate for norbornene is similar up to 25 wt% iPrSi8 loading but experiences a significant
slowdown at 40 wt%, possibly due to the inhibition of the ruthenium catalyst by the hydroxyl end
groups present on the fragments (Figure S15). A 10 wt% fragment loading was used for subsequent
regenerative printing experiments to conserve both fragments and monomer.
 Notably, including fragments from the deconstructed, neat material (Gen 0) into the first
regeneration (Regen 1) had only a small effect on the cure depth measurements compared to the neat
resin without fragments (Figure S16). Furthermore, the rate of reaction is maintained for the second
regeneration (Regen 2) using fragments from deconstructed Regen 1 materials, as apparent by real-
time IR (Figure S17). We see little difference in the size of fragments from GPC experiments between
deconstructed Gen 0 and Regen 1 material (Figure S18 and Table S6). We additionally characterize
extent of reaction via DSC (Figure S19 and Table S7), thermomechanical properties (Figure S20 and
S21), and gel fractions (Figure S22) after photocure and sequential photo and thermal cure.
Regenerated materials have decreased gel fractions compared to neat materials, indicating that
fragments may primarily participate as filler materials. Regardless, materials properties after sequential
photo and thermal postcure remain largely unchanged.
 With successful regeneration of networks using silyl ether comonomers reported in the
literature,[24, 68, 71] we seek to introduce this same lifecycle circularity into DLP printing. Starting
with Gen 0 (i.e., original 20 wt% iPrSi8 materials with no fragments), we deconstruct an arbitrary print,
here demonstrated by the disappearance of a ghost (Figure 9A), to obtain fragments (Figure 9B) for
subsequent regenerative prints (Regen N). Here, we regenerate materials up to two times (i.e., three
total generations of printed material) and successfully print complex geometries, as demonstrated by
3DBenchy (Figure 9D). Moreover, thermomechanical properties are maintained across generations
(Figure 9E-F).

3.2.1.4.     Conclusion
In this study, we have successfully demonstrated the potential of silyl ether comonomers for
regenerative DLP of thermosets. Our findings indicate that the incorporation of iPrSi8 not only
improves the printing performance and resolution compared to previously explored dihydrofuran
(DHF) systems but also maintains critical material properties across multiple generations. The ability
to reincorporate deconstructed fragments into new printing resins represents a significant
advancement in the sustainability of thermoset additive manufacturing, addressing the pressing need
for circularity in polymer lifecycle management. The minimal impact on reaction kinetics and
thermomechanical properties, even after two cycles of regeneration, underscores the viability of this
approach additive manufacturing. Our results reveal that the deconstruction process allows for
effective recovery and reuse of thermoset fragments, thereby reducing waste and promoting resource
efficiency. Overall, this research contributes to the ongoing efforts to improve the lifecycle
management of thermosets, paving the way for more sustainable practices in the polymer industry.
Future work will focus on optimizing the deconstruction process, exploring additional comonomer
systems to further enhance the regenerative capabilities of DLP-printed thermosets, and reactivation
of fragments for better incorporation, ultimately advancing the field of additive manufacturing
towards a more circular economy.

3.2.1.5.     Methods

3.2.1.5.1.    Materials
 Dicyclopentadiene (DCPD) and 2,3-dihydrofuran (DHF) were purchased from Oakwood
Chemicals. 5-Ethylidene-2-norbornene (ENB), ethyl-4-dimethylamino benzoate (EDAB), and ethyl

 21

vinyl ether (EVE) were purchased from TCI. Dichloromethane (DCM), tetrahydrofuran (THF),
hydrochloric acid (HCl), methanol, benzophenone, and silyl ether-based ROMP monomer (iPrSi8)
were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

3.2.1.5.2. Resin Formulation and Preparation
 A monomer solution of 95:5 DCPD:ENB (wt:wt) was used for liquid-state processing of
DCPD monomer, which is otherwise crystalline at ambient conditions. DCPD was melted in an oven
at 40 °C, and ENB was quickly added at the designated ratio. Mixtures remained in the liquid state
after adding ENB. Cleavable comonomers, either DHF or iPrSi8, were added to the mixture at the
desired loading and used within 8 hours. For regenerated materials using deconstructed fragments at
the desired loading, the mixture was sonicated at room temperature for 15 minutes to dissolve the
solid fragments into the liquid monomeric mixture. To complete resin preparation, catalyst M220,
photosensitizer benzophenone, and coinitiator EDAB were measured into a container corresponding
to 0.04 mol% M220 loading and 1:4:8 M220:benzophenone:EDAB by weight. Minimal DCM (~1
wt%) was added to dissolve the solids before adding the liquid monomer solution and vortexing to
achieve solution homogeneity. Comonomer formulations can be found in Table S1, and regeneration
formulations can be found in Table S5.

3.2.1.5.3. Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

norbornene in real time during photopolymerization to 365 nm light at 60 mW cm-2. A Nicolet iS50

photopolymerization. Resin was casted between salt plates with 20 µm spacers and placed within the
fixture. Spectra were recorded for the material approximately every 0.5 seconds and for 30 total
seconds before initiating photopolymerization. Gaussian fitting was used to track the area under the
peak occurring at 3140 cm-1, corresponding to the norbornene C=C-H vibration. The relative
conversion of norbornene bonds was calculated via Equation (1).

3.2.1.5.4.   Rheology
 Rheology was used to determine the gelation behavior of resins during photopolymerization.
An ARES-G2 (TA Instruments) with photopolymerization assembly operating in small-amplitude
oscillatory shear mode was used with 20 mm diameter parallel plate assembly. A frequency of 2 Hz
and amplitude of 1% strain were used with a gap size of 0.5 mm between plates. Data was collected
for 30 seconds before photopolymerization with 365 nm light at 60 mW cm-2.

3.2.1.5.5.   Cure Depth Tests
 Cure depths of materials using cleavable comonomers DHF and iPrSi8 were determined using
a Micro printer (MicroSLA). Resin was cast between glass slides separated by 750 µm thick spacers.
The printer was used to supply 385 nm light at 120 mW cm-2, and rectangles were projected through
the resin at different exposure times. Residual resin was washed away using DCM, and the projected

 22

rectangles were further cured with 365 nm light at 8 mW cm-2 for 3 hours. Afterwards, calipers were
used to measure the cure depth of the rectangles. The dose was taken as the light intensity from the
printer multiplied by the exposure time.

3.2.1.5.6.    2D Photopatterning
 The Micro printer (MicroSLA) was also used to photopattern samples with comonomer DHF.
Resin was cast between glass slides separated by 750 µm thick spacers. The printer was used to supply
385 nm light at 120 mW cm-2, and a skeletal fish pattern was projected through the resin. Exposure
times were selected to give 100 µm thick samples for each DHF loading corresponding to the cure
depth experiments. Excess resin was washed away with DCM, and materials were post-cured with 365
nm light at 8 mW cm-2 for 1 hour.

3.2.1.5.7. DLP Printing and Post-Processing
 The Micro printer (385 nm light at 120 mW cm-2) was also used for DLP 3D printing of
materials. Resin was prepared and placed into a vat. Print files were prepared using MicroSLA’s slicing
software. 100 µm layers were used. For comonomer DHF, exposure times on the order of 2-5 minutes
were necessary to result in any material on the build plate and ultimately resulted in a critical light
engine failure, making prints unsuccessful. For comonomer iPrSi8, 30 s of exposure was used across
all loadings. After printing, samples were removed carefully from the build head, and residual resin
was washed away using DCM. The materials were then cured further to 365 nm light at 8 mW cm-2
for 3 hours. Thermally post-cured samples were further placed in an oven at 180 °C for 4 hours.

filled with approximately 20 mL of THF and 100 µL of EVE to prevent further metathesis
polymerization. The solutions were left at room temperature for 7 days, after which the remaining
solid material was removed and dried at room temperature under vacuum for 3 more days. The solids
were weighed before and after this process. Gel fractions were calculated via Equation (2).

3.2.1.5.10. Thermogravimetric Analysis
 Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was completed on samples after UV curing to determine
thermal stability during thermal post treatment using a Discovery SDT650 (TA Instruments).
Approximately 20 mg samples were loaded into alumina pans, and the temperature was ramped from
room temperature to 800 °C at 15 °C min-1. The mass was tracked during the ramp and plotted as a
percentage of the original mass versus temperature.

3.2.1.5.11. Tensile Testing
 Tensile testing was done using a Mark-10 tensile tester with a 1500 N load cell. Scaled-down
Type V dogbones (75% of the specified size in ASTM D638) were used to allow printing within the
build area of the Micro printer. The gauge of the dogbone samples was marked with white acrylic
marker, and samples were deformed at a rate of 10% min-1 of the original gauge length. The
deformation was tracked using a video extensometer (FLIR X-One), and the data was used to obtain
the strain of samples during deformation. The tensile tester data was used to determine the stress
applied to samples during deformation. Tensile testing data was done in triplicate. The Young’s
modulus was taken from the initial linear slope of the stress strain curve (from 0.2-0.8% strain). Tensile
strength was determined as the maximum stress samples reached on the stress-strain curve.

3.2.1.5.12. Dynamic Mechanical Analysis
 Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) was done using a Discovery DMA 850 (TA
Instruments). Testing samples were printed approximately 20 mm long, 5 mm wide, and 0.5 mm thick.
Samples were loaded into tensile clamps with a gauge length of 10 mm. Samples were oscillated at 1
Hz and 0.1% strain from room temperature to 200 °C at 5 °C min-1. The glass transition temperature
was taken as the temperature where the maximum of the tan delta peak occurred.

3.2.1.5.13. Deconstruction and Fragment Recovery
 Deconstruction was done using thermally post-cured samples. Samples were placed in a glass
container with 1 M HCl in THF at a ratio of 1 g sample per 40 mL solution. Solutions were stirred at
room temperature for the desired amount of time, 6 hours unless otherwise specified. The residual
solids were then filtered from the solution and dried to obtain their total mass. The filtered
deconstruction solution was then precipitated dropwise in cold methanol at a volumetric ratio of 1:5.
The precipitate was then vacuum filtered from the remaining solution and dried overnight under
vacuum. These fragments were stored in a vial in a freezer until ready for use in regenerative printing.

3.2.2. Continuous Dual-Wavelength ROMP 3D Printing Using Non-Planar
 Inhibition

3.2.2.1.   Abstract
Stereolithographic Additive Manufacturing (SLA-AM) offers significant advantages in print speed,
part fidelity, and surface finish. Recent advancements in photopolymerizable monomer systems,
particularly through the integration of Ring-Opening Metathesis Polymerization (ROMP), have
expanded the material selection for SLA. However, the transition from radical to ROMP chemistries
presents challenges in continuous printing methods, as traditional radical inhibition techniques are
ineffective. This study introduces a novel approach, SWOMP, utilizing a reversible inhibitor, o-Cl-
HABI, to facilitate continuous SLA printing of ROMP resins. By employing a dual-wavelength
irradiation strategy (365 nm and 475 nm), we achieved independent control over polymerization
initiation and inhibition. The photoinitiator system demonstrated effective inhibition of ROMP
kinetics without compromising mechanical properties. Experimental results, including Fourier
Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) and rheological assessments, confirmed the ability to toggle
polymerization on and off, allowing for precise control over print complexity and size. The
incorporation of o-Cl-HABI not only mitigated the limitations of irreversible inhibitors but also
provided a dynamic equilibrium between the lophyl radicals and the ruthenium catalyst, enhancing the
versatility of SLA-AM processes. This research highlights the potential of reversible inhibition
strategies in advancing SLA technologies, paving the way for more complex and larger-scale prints in
the field of additive manufacturing.

3.2.2.2.   Introduction
Stereolithographic Additive Manufacturing (SLA-AM) is advantageous for many reasons such as print
speed, part fidelity, and surface finish. Continuing development on printing methods and
photopolymerizable monomer systems has further expanded the potential of the technique. New
chemistries such as Ring-Opening Metathesis Polymerization (ROMP) have been adapted into
photopolymerizable resins and greatly increased freedom in material selection. Moving away from
radical photopolymerizations allows greater freedom but limits the use of previously discovered
techniques for SLA. Multiple methods have been developed for continuous printing in bottom-up
SLA using radical photopolymerizable resins by inhibiting polymerization at the window.[12, 14, 73]
Two of these methods, Continuous Liquid Interface Production and Dual-Wavelength initiation and
inhibition, rely on radical inhibition either through oxygen or a radical annihilator. ROMP chemistry
is not prone to the radical inhibition methods used in these previous methods thus new chemistries
need to be developed top continuously print these new chemistries and compete with radical resins.
One way to inhibit ROMP polymerizations is using a base to deprotonate the metallocyclobutane
intermediate and decompose the active catalyst. Thus, a simple method to print continuously was
developed, SWOMP, that uses a common photo-activated base, NPPOC-TMG, to spatially inhibit
polymerization.[58] Although successful, the irreversible nature of the photobase led to a gradual
buildup of base, a reduction in photopolymerization kinetics as the print continued and, ultimately,
limited the size and complexity of prints.
Herein, we describe the use of a reversible inhibitor in a ROMP photopolymerizable resin that affords
continuous SLA printing and nonplanar inhibition without affecting kinetics or mechanical properties.

3.2.2.3. Results and Discussion
Although hexaarylbimidazole (HABI) are known as capable photoinitiators in many free-radical-
initiated photoimaging systems when paired with a hydrogen-donating initiator[74], previous work

 35

with HABI suggests that the lophyl radicals produced through photoinduced cleavage of HABI can
interfere with propagating radicals in chain-growth polymerization through chain termination or
quenching of radicals generated from radical initiators.[12, 75] The mechanism of ring-opening
metathesis polymerization (ROMP) is distinct from radical chain growth polymerization but
interestingly, ROMP is traditionally inhibited using strong bases, including imidazoles.[76] Thus, it is
conceivable that HABI, which cleaves into imidazole radicals, could be used as a dual inhibitor for
both radical chain growth polymerization and ROMP. Given that the HABI photocleavage is
reversible, it has additional advantages over previous systems relying on irreversible photorelease of
base.[58]
Computational studies were done to further validate the possibility of HABI inhibition of ROMP.
First looking at the mechanism of photo-activated ROMP (photoROMP) using camphorquinone
(CQ) as a visible light initiator, ethyl 4-(dimethylamino)benzoate (EDAB) as a co-initiator, and
HeatMet as a ruthenium Grubbs type catalyst with o-Cl-HABI as the source of lophyl radicals.
Computational studies ruled out possible avenues such as direct redox activation from CQ to HeatMet
and suggested that the likely mechanism of activation is a reduction of HeatMet caused by a CQ anion
formed when EDAB is promoted to a higher energy state. If this is the primary activation mechanism,
then it is plausible that introducing a photoinduced lophyl radical, would interfere with the radical
intermediates in the activation mechanism or higher energy states needed for catalyst activation similar
to the inhibition of radical chain growth polymerization. Due to the similarity of the imidazole radical
to common ROMP inhibitors, it was also hypothesized that the lophyl radical could inhibit the
ruthenium catalyst. Computations and experimental CV ruled out lophyl radical oxidation of HeatMet
but chelation of the lophyl radical to the activated HeatMet appears to be a plausible pathway to
catalyst inhibition.
PhotoROMP of dicyclopentadiene (DCPD) based on previous photoROMP vat photopolymerization
studies[58-60] was used to experimentally observe the inhibition characteristics. CQ and EDAB were
used in combination as a visible light photoinitiating system and a ruthenium catalyst identified in a
previous photoROMP study.[59] Controlling the photoinitiation and photoinhibition independently
requires orthogonal absorbance spectra. Similar to previous studies using this combination of
photoinitiators and photoinhibitiors, we confirmed that o-Cl-HABI displays weak absorbance in the
visible region above 450 nm and this perfectly contrasts the absorbance spectra of CQ which displays
an absorption maximum at 470 nm but absorbs poorly around 360 nm where o-Cl-HABI displays a
strong absorbance (Fig 10 – UV-Vis). Thus, a combination of 470 nm and 365 nm irradiation affords
independent control over these separate photochemical processes simultaneously. Real-time FTIR
was used to confirm the inhibition characteristics of o-Cl-HABI in the photoROMP system using
DCPD and observing the norbornene double bond stretch at 3140 cm-1 during photoirradiation.
Irradiating a resin with 470 nm irradiation at 60 mW/cm2 irradiation resulted in norbornene double
bond conversion within 30 seconds. Even though HABI is a known photoinitiator, irradiation of 365
nm light resulted in no norbornene double bond conversion within four minutes, confirming the
inability of HABI to act as a photoinitiator in a photoROMP system. Combining both 475 nm and
365 nm wavelengths and irradiating simultaneously resulted in a much more inhibited polymerization
where norbornene conversion was not observed for 2 minutes at which time the polymerization does
start to overcome the inhibition. Even though the inhibition can be overcome after an amount of
time, this only occurs when both 475 nm and 365 nm wavelengths are continuously irradiated
simultaneously for many minutes, simply turning off the 475 nm wavelength or using higher
concentrations of HABI can reduce this effect.

Due to the living nature of ROMP chemistry, polymerization continues after light exposure more than
might be seen in other radical polymerizations making active photoinhibition control of conversion
even more useful. To illustrate this point, a typical DCPD resin was exposed to the 475 nm initiating
blue wavelength but only in 15 sec increments (Fig. 11A). Even though the light was only on for 15
seconds and then off for the next 15 s, the trend of the conversion is unaffected by toggling the light.
The inability to control the conversion using the single, initiating wavelength confirms the need for a
second wavelength to enable precise temporal control over conversion. Enabling a second
wavelength, 365 nm, to control photogeneration of lophyl radicals affords control over conversion
and allows abrupt cessation of polymerization and subsequent reinitiation during continuous
irradiation at the initiating wavelength (475 nm). Furthermore, the control of conversion can be
further tuned by modifying the UV light intensity (IUV) which likely controls the HABI photocleavage
leading to the generation of more lophyl radicals at higher IUV and more responsive temporal control
of norbornene conversion (Fig. 11B). Higher IUV also affects conversion after the UV light is turned
off likely due to residual lophyl radicals that haven’t recombined. As well as changing IUV, the
conversion at which polymerization is inhibited can be controlled by varying the time at which the
inhibiting wavelength is activated (Fig. 11C).
The rapid conversion control using photoinhibition compared to initiation control suggests that the
inhibition affects the catalyst leading to a halt in living polymerization in the absence of light. To fully
confirm the inhibition was affecting the catalyst and not just a radical termination of the
photosensitizers, the photosensitizers were completely removed from the resin and the DCPD and
HeatMet resin was thermally initiated (Fig. 11C). Without the photosensitizers the only interaction
would be between the lophyl radicals and the ruthenium catalyst. At 50°C on a photorheometer the
polymerization of DCPD was monitored by tracking the storage modulus of the resin, once the
polymerization reached a storage modulus indicating partial polymerization had occurred (~103 Pa),
the light source was manually switched on and an immediate plateau of storage modulus was observed
in response. However, when the thermal polymerization occurred in the absence of 365 nm light, a
continual gradual increase in storage modulus is observed as expected. After a few minutes the
inhibition at 50°C was overcome and polymerization resumed suggesting a dynamic equilibrium
between lophyl radicals and the ruthenium catalyst. This also suggests that neither the inhibition nor
reinitiation processes are primarily controlled through inhibition of species generated through the
photosensitization process but rather a direct interaction between lophyl radicals and the ruthenium
catalyst.

3.2.3. Crystallinity Regulation in Lithographic Additive Fabrication of
 Thermoplastics

3.2.3.1.   Abstract
Semicrystalline thermoplastic polymers dominate the polymer market with a desirable combination of
robust material properties and low manufacturing cost. Properties are tuned through the balance
between interconnected ordered crystalline and disordered amorphous regions. Traditionally,
crystallinity is encoded through polymer chain architecture and processing history, affording
macroscopically homogeneous products that are limited to a single set of crystalline parameters. A
straightforward method to define crystallinity at the micro-scale within a continuous volume has been
elusive. We present the discovery of a light-based approach for fabricating robust polyolefins with
spatiotemporal control over percent crystallinity. This technique enables precise manipulation of
multimaterial, three-dimensional architectures, conferring additional versatility and functionalities to
semicrystalline polymers, exemplified here through the design of materials with controlled optical
properties and complex mechanical behaviors.

3.2.3.2.   Introduction
Semicrystalline thermoplastic polymers are a significant portion of the commodity polymer market
due to their unique mechanical strength, optical performance, and long-term stability. In particular,
polyethylene (PE) constitutes approximately 30% of the market, largely owing to the ease at which
degree of crystallinity (Χc) can be tuned to afford a range of bulk chemical and mechanical
performances in the forms of high-density (HDPE), low-density (LDPE), medium-density, and linear
low-density polyethylene [77]. Although bulk control of crystalline properties using branching,
annealing [78], polymer blends [79], or inclusion of additives [80] is well established, a facile method
for spatially encoding crystallinity within a single material would transform the commodity polymer
industry. Seminal work by Page, Sottos, and coworkers have demonstrated patterning of crystallinity,
but are limited in spatial control, unable to resolve intermediate levels of crystallinity, and restricted in
geometric complexity [81-83]. Herein, we describe our discovery and development of a facile method
for fabricating three-dimensional thermoplastic architectures with voxel-encoded crystallinity through
grayscale stereolithographic additive manufacturing (AM).

 42

3.2.3.3. Results and Discussion
Despite the prevalence of semicrystalline polymers in industry, their application to lithographic AM
has been limited to a handful of reports [84-90]. This is primarily due to challenges in rapid,
photochemical conversion of monomer or oligomers into high molecular weight polymers and poor
adhesion of crystalline domains in the layer-by-layer deposition process. To overcome these
challenges, Commisso et al. employed cyclic allyl sulfide monomers that rapidly underwent radical
ring-opening to yield semicrystalline polymers. Critically, the allyl sulfide groups throughout the
polymer backbone were then available to participate in further addition–fragmentation chain transfer
events, resulting in polymer chains that traversed layers and reduced anisotropy [84]. Although this
previous work resulted in brittle materials, we recognized an opportunity to fabricate tough materials
akin to PE by employing cis-cyclooctene (COE) as a monomer in photo-initiated ring-opening
metathesis polymerization (photoROMP, Fig. 13A). COE, an inexpensive, low viscosity monomer
with rapid photopolymerization kinetics, readily undergoes chain transfer and secondary metathesis
during ROMP to form highly entangled polymers. The resulting materials have advantageous
mechanical, optical, and shape memory properties and have been commercialized since the 1980s [91-
94].
Using slight modifications to a previously established photoROMP resin formulation [59, 60, 95],
photopolymerization of COE was characterized using rheology with in situ irradiation (365 nm). This
formulation included a commercial ruthenium catalyst (HeatMet), a photosensitizer system consisting
of 2-isopropylthioxanthone (ITX) and ethyl-4-(dimethylamino) benzoate (EDAB), butylated
hydroxytoluene (BHT) as an antioxidant, and 1-methyl imidazole (MIM) as an inhibitor to maintain a
potlife of several hours (table S1, fig. S1). For COE reaction mixtures, solidification was observed
within seconds of irradiation, even at a moderate intensity of 5 mW/cm2 (Fig. 13B). Two plateau
moduli were observed, attributable to initial solidification and subsequent crystallization [84, 85]. As
anticipated, the adoption of this resin system for lithographic additive manufacturing resulted in tough
printed samples, exhibiting an elongation of over 600% and isotropic properties characteristic of a
highly entangled material (figs. S2-S3, table S2). Printing two halves of a dogbone on different days –
allowing for full crystallization of the initial half before subsequently printing the second – had no
obvious effect on mechanical properties, suggesting secondary metathesis can occur between
crystallized layers (fig. S4). To verify the exclusive formation of non-crosslinked polymer, samples
were dissolved in toluene and solvent cast into a new form (fig. S5).
Further exploration of this photopolymerization with varying irradiation intensity led to a
serendipitous discovery (Fig. 13A, fig. S6-S14, table S3-S4). The initial increase in modulus, attributed
to chain growth, was predictably more rapid with increasing light intensity (Fig. 13B). However, the
second increase in modulus, attributed to crystallization, varied inversely with light intensity such that
the highest light intensity (132 mW/cm2) afforded the softest material with the lowest final plateau
modulus (≈106). Furthermore, we observed that the optical transparency increased with the intensity
used for polymerization, suggesting lower crystallinity. Characterization of samples photopolymerized
under a range of intensities by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) revealed significant variation in
Χc that ranged from 25% to 60% crystallinity with peak melting temperatures from 40°C to 70°C (Fig.
1, table S3). Imaging with scanning electron microscopy further showed disparate fracture surfaces
for materials made with high (168 mW/cm2) and low (30 mW/cm2) intensity light (fig. S15).
Examination of the polycyclooctene (pCOE) backbone stereochemistry by 1H nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) spectroscopy showed an increase in the cis-content of the polyoctenamer with
increasing irradiation intensity during polymerization (table S3). This correlation was unsurprising, as
the stereochemistry of polyolefins is well known to affect crystallization properties, with high polymer
cis-content yielding more amorphous materials [81].

Mechanism:
A wide range of hypotheses for the underlying mechanism enabling light-encoded stereochemistry
and crystallinity were explored through density functional theory (DFT) transition state modeling and
appropriate experimental controls (figs. S16-S36, tables S4-S15, schemes S1-S7). Examples of these
proposed mechanisms included photoisomerization of monomer, competing radical polymerization,
and coordination of the photosensitizer species to yield bulky, cis-selective ruthenium complexes
(scheme S8). For brevity, we have included the discussion of these alternative postulated pathways in
the supplementary text, and here focus on the most probable mechanistic pathway: photochemical
decomposition of the catalyst (Fig. 14). Additionally, the supplementary text includes a discussion on
the importance of ITX for the mechanism.
Generally, ROMP of COE or cyclooctadiene (COD) follows a process of catalyst activation,
polymerization initiation, chain extension, and secondary metathesis (Fig. 14A) [18]. Previous work
on COE and COD using Grubbs second generation ruthenium catalyst (GC2) and other N-

 44

heterocyclic carbene substituted catalysts have shown polymer cis:trans ratios for initial polymerization
and chain extension are kinetically controlled and reflect the nature of the coordination sphere,
whereas secondary metathesis drives reaction mixtures into thermodynamic equilibrium favoring
predominantly trans-conformations [96-98]. As secondary metathesis is a relatively slow process,
decomposition or inhibition of the catalyst favors the kinetic products. Indeed, recent work by Lloyd
and Craig demonstrated that increasing the number of equivalents of a phosphite inhibitor to GC2
disfavored secondary metathesis, and thus, decreased the final trans-alkene content and Χc in a bulk
COD system [97].
Transition state calculations found a near-equivalent population of cis- and trans-alkenes during the
initial polymerization of HeatMet catalyzed COE mixtures, whereas subsequent secondary metathesis
was shown to have a strong probability of converting cis-isomer to trans-isomer over time (figs. S23 -
S25, scheme S4-S7). Periodic sampling and characterization by NMR spectroscopy of unirradiated
COE reaction mixtures (i.e., dark cure) showed an increase of the proportion of trans-alkene from an
initial ≈58% to a final ≈83% (fig. S26). Reaction mixtures that were continuously irradiated yielded
materials with varied final polymer trans-alkene content according to the intensity applied (Fig. 14B).
High irradiation intensity (170 mW/cm2) yielded lower trans-alkene content (70%) as compared with
low irradiation intensity (11 mW/cm2, 76%). Importantly, samples under continuous irradiation never
reached greater than 80% conversion (Fig. 14B, fig. S27). In contrast, samples irradiated with a single,
ten second pulse of light and subsequently left unirradiated, showed quantitative conversion over 1
hour. During this time, the trans-alkene content continued to increase even after quantitative
conversion was reached (Fig. 2C, fig. S28), yet, a significant difference in final polymer trans %
remained between samples pulsed with high or low intensity (72% and 80%, respectively). The
decrease in conversion and apparent inhibition of secondary metathesis observed for continuously
irradiated reaction mixtures was a strong indication of light-mediated catalyst decomposition.
To directly probe the occurrence of catalytic decomposition, various catalytic intermediates and
possible mechanistic pathways were explored through DFT modeling (Fig. 14A). Two possible
mechanisms were quickly identified: an oxygen-free pathway via triplet state energy transfer and a
mechanism mediated by oxygen (Fig. 14A). Indeed, oxygen mediated C–H activation in NHC-
containing complexes has been shown in previous studies and, notably, the resulting ‘decomposed’
catalysts have performed cross metathesis with moderate Z-selectivity [99-101]. Earlier work has
suggested the irreversible consumption of HeatMet after irradiation in the presence of a Type I radical
photoinitiator [102]. Similarly, for our HeatMet system sensitized by ITX and EDAB, NMR
spectroscopy confirmed the formation of new and distinct ruthenium complexes under inert and
ambient conditions in the absence of monomer (fig. S35). Interestingly, subsequent exposure of the
inert solution to air lead to further decomposition.
An effort to replicate the chemical environment of neat COE reactions for in situ NMR experiments
with the use of cyclooctane, unexpectedly resulted in the formation of a precipitate with surprisingly
high catalytic activity towards COE and little difference in cis- or trans-alkene formation (Fig 14D., fig.
S33). Elemental analysis was performed on the precipitate (table S13), and showed only minute
amounts of sulfur, indicating that the precipitated species contained trace quantities of ITX. The
formation of relatively low final trans-alkene (65%) content from the reaction of COE with the
precipitate, along with the near absence of HeatMet, suggests that the decomposed catalyst species,
while active in chain growth, has limited—if any—participation in secondary metathesis. Efforts to
isolate the new ruthenium complexes through controlled precipitation, recrystallization, or other
means of separation proved ineffective, and rather, verified the presence of several different
decomposed species. Interestingly, precipitates also formed in the presence of other photosensitizers,
albeit requiring larger doses of irradiation (fig S34).

 45

Finally, to emphasize that photochemical decomposition of the catalyst traps the system in a non-
equilibrium state, pCOE samples with relatively high cis-polyoctenamer content formed under high
intensity irradiation of HeatMet or using the precipitated catalyst mixture (76% and 65% trans
respectively) were subjected to an additional equivalent of HeatMet in the absence of irradiation. As
anticipated, isomerization via secondary metathesis occurred, yielding materials with high trans-
polymer content (81-85% trans) (Fig. 2D).

CRAFT Grayscale Printing Method:
We next sought to assess this technique’s capability for additive manufacturing on commercial 3D
printers by applying a grayscale modification to individual layer slices, or images [103-105]. Projection
of these grayscale slices by the printer results in layer-wise spatial variation of irradiation intensity and,
consequently, crystallinity (Fig. 15A). Herein, we refer to this method as CRAFT, or Crystallinity
Regulation in Lithographic Additive Fabrication of Thermoplastics. For CRAFT, grayscale values
ranged from G0 (black) to G255 (white) and corresponded proportionally to an intensity range of 0
mW/cm2 to 168 mW/cm2 (fig. S37). Initially, dogbone specimens were printed using several grayscale
values (i.e., irradiation intensities), subjected to uniaxial tensile testing, and compared with LDPE and
HDPE samples (Fig. 3B). Printing pCOE samples (two equivalents of ITX to catalyst) with low light
intensity (G17, 11 mW/cm2) resulted in highly crystalline materials with Young’s moduli (E) around
250 MPa, yield stress (σ) of 19 MPa, and an average strain at break (ε) over 700%. As light intensity is
increased, stiffness and strength decreased reaching values of E = 120 MPa, σ = 9 MPa, and ε = 630%
for G255. As anticipated, the application of an additional UV post cure (10 s of 100 mW/cm2 at 365
nm) or additional equivalents of ITX further lowered the final moduli, attributable to increased
catalytic decomposition (Fig. 15B, fig. S38, table S16). To evaluate the evolution of mechanical
properties, tensile specimens of G40 and post cured G255 were characterized periodically over a 12-
week period. For both sets of samples, E and σ increased over the first two days and showed negligible
increase thereafter (fig. S39-S40, table S17-S18).
As the opacity of semicrystalline materials correlates to the Χc due to scattering by the crystallites,
optical imaging could be leveraged for analysis of the prints. For example, spatial resolution of
crystalline domains using CRAFT was evaluated by printing a 1951 USAF standardized pattern and
measuring regions with disparate opacities. The optical resolution was demonstrated to be on the
order of hundreds of microns (fig. S41). Additionally, nanoindentation was used to evaluate the
contact modulus of an interface between regions of G59 and G17. These measurements showed a
relatively sharp transition, with over 90% of the modulus change occurring over a 300 µm span (Fig
15C.). Building upon these prints, squares with 18 distinct grayscale values ranging from G17 to G255
were printed to form multi-crystalline patterned quilts which were evaluated with optical imaging and
nanoindentation (Fig. 3D and fig. S42). As anticipated, an inverse relationship between modulus and
grayscale value was found, with G17, G59, and G255 having contact moduli values of ≈1050, ≈100,
and ≈50 MPa, respectively for a COE resin formulation with two equivalents of ITX to catalyst. This
range could also be readily shifted by increasing the equivalents of ITX and EDAB in the resin (fig.
S43, table S19).
Semi-quantitative analysis of grayscale designs and spatial resolution of crystallinity were conducted
by comparing the counts of individual grayscale values of initial designs and grayscale images of the
printed pCOE samples. Initially, a design consisting of a series of four grayscale nested hexagons was
printed with CRAFT to form a thin film (16 x 100 µm layers, fig. S44). Image analysis of the printed
sample showed the presence of five distinct sets of grayscale values corresponding to five disparate
crystalline regions. It is important to note differences between the observed and designed grayscale
value are expected due to the light engine pixel size and camera resolution. To illustrate this point, the
nested hexagon design was printed using office printers and compared against the original and polymer
printed image. Interestingly, the hexagons printed using office printers showed markedly less
distinction between grayscale domains. This patterning concept was further expanded to more
complex images with a continuous grayscale spectrum, including the Mona Lisa (Fig. 15E, fig. S45).
The remarkable fidelity of the grayscale histograms between designs and the corresponding pCOE
prints underscores the CRAFT method’s ability to tightly control and fine-tune Χc. Finally, to verify
patterning of percent crystallinity occurs within three-dimensions, a highly crystalline skull (G40) was

 47

printed within matrix of low crystallinity (G255) and cross-sectioned to reveal complex internal
features and bespoke patterning (Fig. 15F, fig. S46).
Encouraged by the resolution at which mechanical properties and Χc could be tuned with CRAFT,
we next evaluated the method’s ability to pattern a range of crystallinities in a single three-dimensional
print. A three-dimensional thunderbird with a square border (G40 and G140, respectively) was
patterned within a cube-matrix (G255) and cross-sectioned to reveal three semicrystalline regions of
varied Χc (Fig. 16A). In pursuit of greater complexity, we also explored representing three-dimensional
biological structures with the CRAFT method, starting with the intricate and captivating patterns of
the Burmese Star Tortoise (Geochelone platynota). (Fig. 16B). Along the same veins, we were inspired to
print a hand similar to the one found in seminal multimaterial work by Schwartz and Boydston (Fig.
16C and D, fig. S47) [106]. In addition to the bi-material bones and skin demonstrated in the previous
work, CRAFT permitted the introduction of additional materials – namely tendons and ligaments–
with corresponding changes in material stiffness.

Strain hardening is a common phenomenon in tough, highly entangled semicrystalline polymers
related to the structural reorganization within the crystalline and amorphous regions. We recognized
this inherent attribute of pCOE could be leveraged in conjunction with patterning of crystallinity to
afford sequential deformation of localized domains and tailored load profiles (fig. S48). This concept
was actualized using a rectangular bar (30x 100 µm layers, G40) patterned with four distinct crystalline
regions of varying stiffness (G115, G165, G205, G255) (Fig. 4E). For further visual effect, the
deformable areas were patterned internally (middle 5 layers) with a number corresponding to the order
at which yield was expected. Under uniaxial strain, yielding occurs in increasing order of grayscale
value (i.e., increasing Χc), with subsequent strain hardening increasing the overall stiffness of the region
(Fig. 16E and F). Critically, as the strain-hardening region attains a stiffness greater than that of the
sequentially patterned region, the sequential region yields, and so forth, resulting in a staircase-like
strain profile. This unique capability was further applied to various embedded lattice, or grid, structures
to demonstrate further complexity in programmed deformation and stress-strain behavior (figs. S49-
S50).
Beyond uniaxial tensile deformation of planar samples, we evaluated CRAFT as a means for
fabricating polymer-polymer composite architectures for energy dampening. A variety of 3D-
structures were designed and printed from pCOE with patterned crystallinity and characterized using
a custom-built dynamic loading fixture [107] with an applied vibrational sweep (Fig. 16G and H, and
fig. S51 – fig. S52). Structures examined included monolithic blocks (G40, G90, G140, G190, and
G255), blocks with layers of alternating stiffness (G40 and G255), and Bouligand structures inspired
by the impressive energy dissipation and damage-tolerance of the dactyl club of mantis shrimp [108-
112]. Bouligand structures are composed of chiral stacks of alternating unidirectional planks (G40 and
G255) rotated by a twist angle of 5° or 90° to create an embedded helix or log-pile pattern, respectively.
The structural natural frequencies can be observed as local maxima of vibration transmissivity (Tr),
typically appearing in low frequency ranges, below 1000 Hz. We observed that orientations of
patterned crystalline domains, as differentiated by different twist angles, have a profound effect on the
intrinsic frequencies that are transmitted through the sample without any changes in sample mass or
external geometry. As a notable example, contrasting responses – damped vibration or natural
frequency – were observed at frequencies of ≈ 800 and ≈ 1050 Hz for the two Bouligand structures.
This demonstrates that CRAFT has a unique capacity to tune dissipative behavior through simple
design changes that would be otherwise impractical for traditional polyolefin manufacturing
techniques.
This work describes CRAFT, a versatile system enabling the patterning of percent crystallinity in
thermoplastic polyolefins from a single feedstock without the need for physicochemical modifications
used with traditional semicrystalline thermoplastic production. In CRAFT, light functions as both an
initiating mechanism for photopolymerization and, through intensity variation, as a method of
regulating secondary metathesis to afford polymers with specified stereochemistry, and thus, Χc. The
facile manner in which mechanical and optical properties can be encoded within the volume of a three-
dimensional architecture will unlock numerous possibilities for multimaterial applications and
provides a transformative new tool for understanding semicrystalline materials and interfaces.

3.2.3.4.2. Experimental Methods:

3.2.3.4.2.1.   Resin Formulation:
COE monomer was mixed with an inhibitor, 1-methyl imidazole (0.2 mol equivalents to catalyst) to
increase potlife. This mixture was prepared by adding 10 µL of MIM to 6.9 g of COE as a stock
solution and then diluting this solution 100-fold with neat COE. Reactive resins were prepared by
mixing the solid components (HeatMet, ITX, EDAB, and BHT) and dissolving in the minimum
amount of DCM (approx. 0.1 mL) required for complete dissolution of catalyst. This catalyst mixture
was added to a given amount of the diluted COE/MIM solution to reach the desired catalyst loading
as noted below.
Initially, a formulation derived from previous photoROMP literature was used with a catalyst
concentration of 0.02 mol% in COE [59, 60]. This formulation was for initial tests on inhibitor
concentration, photosensitizer choice and concentration, and catalyst concentration before the resin
was optimized. For a 1 mg HeatMet basis, this resin formulation used 1 mg HeatMet, 0.5 mg ITX (1.3
mol eq to HeatMet), and 1 mg EDAB dissolved in DCM and added to 0.825 g of the COE/MIM
solution.
The optimized resin contained 0.01 mol% HeatMet catalyst and 2 molar equivalents of ITX. The
EDAB to ITX ratio was left unchanged from the initial formulation (EDAB:ITX = 2:1 wt:wt or 2.6:1
mol:mol). Thus, to 1 mg HeatMet was added 0.76 mg ITX, 1.52 mg EDAB, and 1 mg BHT prior to
dissolution in DCM and the addition of 1.65 g of the COE/MIM solution. This reaction solution is
referred to as the standard COE reaction mixture.
For COE reaction mixtures specified to have 4 or 8 molar equivalents of ITX, ITX and EDAB
amounts were changed in the above formulation to 1.51 mg ITX/3.02 mg EDAB and 3.0 mg ITX/6.0
mg EDAB, respectively.

3.2.3.4.2.2.   Lithographic 3D Printing:
Printing of polymer structures was executed on a Micro 3D-printer (microSLA, 385 nm). Layer slices
used for printing were created by inputting STL files into the kudo3D slicing software and generating
PNG file stacks. For initial work using un-patterned printing, a 1.3 ITX molar equivalent formulation
was printed at 275 µm at a light intensity of 30 mW/cm2 with a 1.7s exposure time.

100 µm layers, a layer exposure time of 1.4 s, and 2-3 burn in layers of 2.5 seconds. When printing
with formulations with 4 equivalents of ITX, 0.9 second layers with 2-3 burn-in layers of 2.5 seconds.
When printing with formulations with 8 equivalents of ITX, 0.6 second layers with 2-3 burn-in layers

3.2.3.4.2.4.   Photorheology:

Photorheological measurements were carried out on an Ares G2 rheometer at room temperature
using a Lumen Dynamics Omnicure 2000 mercury arc lamp light source equipped with a 365 nm
wavelength bandpass filter and a liquid light guide fed to a collimating lens held in the
photoaccessory of the Ares G2. COE resin was placed between a 20 mm aluminum bottom parallel
plate and a transparent acrylic 20 mm parallel plate with a gap size of 0.25 mm. Tests were
conducted at 2 Hz oscillation frequency and 1% strain for 10 min. Samples were held in the dark for
the first 60 s of data collection, before irradiating continuously at a specified intensity. After testing,
completed samples were left for 24 hrs in the dark and then subjected to differential scanning
calorimetry (below).

3.2.3.4.2.5. Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC):
DSC measurements were performed using a TA instruments Q200 equipped with a Liquid Nitrogen
Cooling System (LNCS) apparatus. 2-5 mg samples were loaded into hermetic aluminum pans. The
first heating cycles from -25°C to 100°C at 5°C/min were recorded. Degree of crystallinity (Χc) is
based on 100% crystalline polyoctenamer (ΔH100) at 216 J/g and calculated as ΔH/ΔH100*100% [113].

3.2.3.4.2.6.   Tensile testing:
The mechanical properties of printed ASTM D1708 dogbone samples were tested using a Mark-10
tensile tester at a strain rate of 100%/min. ASTM D1708 dogbones were printed from the standard
COE reaction mixture with a 100 µm layer height and 1.4 s of exposure per layer after 2-3 burn in
layers of 2.5 seconds. An ASTM D1708 dogbone die was used to trim excess material from overcure
(e.g., burn in layers). Unless otherwise specified, samples were allowed to crystallize over two days

 52

prior to tensile testing. Due to height (z-axis) limitations in print size, a smaller custom dogbone (30
mm in length x 12.5 mm in width x 1.5 mm thick with a gauge area of 15.8 mm x 7.6 mm) was created
to examine the relative strengths of printing in different orientations. These vertical and horizontal
dogbones were printed using layer heights of 275 µm at a light intensity of 30 mW/cm2 with a 1.7s
exposure time and were left to crystalize unirradiated at room temperature for 24 hours before any
testing.

3.2.3.4.2.7. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM):
SEM specimens were prepared by incubating printed samples in liquid nitrogen for 10 minutes,
removing and fracturing through the sample to reveal interior surfaces. These surfaces were sputter
coated with gold to a nominal thickness of 10nm (SPI sputter coater) and imaged using a FEI HELIOS
NanoLab 650 SEM at 5kV, 0.8nA.
Imaging: Resolution was carried out using a Keyence VHX-7000 digital microscope. Images of printed
parts were taken on the Keyence microscope or using a Canon EOS Rebel T3i camera with a Macro
lens. Gray scaling and contrast enhancement of images was performed in ImageJ by converting images
to 8-bit and using the ‘Brightness/Contrast’ tool. Image J analysis was used to provide counts of each
grayscale value in an image using the ‘Histogram’ analysis feature.

3.2.3.4.3. Computational Methods
All computations were performed using Orca 6.1 [114].

3.2.3.4.3.1.   Ruthenium Complexes:
All ruthenium complexes were optimized in DCM using wB97M-D4/ZORA-def2-TVZP with the
full electron basis SARC-def2-TZVP used for ruthenium with the conductor-like polarizable
continuum model (CPCM) solvent model. All calculations employed the zeroth-order regular
approximation (ZORA) to account for relativistic effects. Frequency calculations were performed at
the same level of theory. The RIJCOX approximations utilizing the SARC/J and autoaux/C auxiliary
basis sets were employed where applicable to save computational resources. Geometries were
confirmed to be at a minimum as evident by the lack of imaginary frequencies. Single point energies
were taken at the wB97M(2)/ ZORA ma-ZORA-def2-QVZPP with the full electron basis SARC-
def2-TZVPP used for Ruthenium with the Solvation Model Density (SMD) solvent model. Transition
states were obtained using CI-NEB followed by a geometry optimization. Transition states were
confirmed to contain a single imaginary frequency.
Configurational Active Space (CAS) calculations were performed by obtaining the initial guess orbitals
from an Atomic Valence Active Space procedure. Final CAS calculations utilized ZORA ZORA-def2-
TVZPP with SARC-def2-TZVPP for Ruthenium and the SMD solvent model.
NMR calculations were performed using TPSSh/ZORA ZORA-def2-QZVPP with the full electron
basis SARC-def2-TZVPP used for ruthenium, the CPCM solvent model, and GIAOs with numerical
1e integrals. A series of organic standards were run to construct a calibration curve of the calculated
shielding values vs experimental shielding values. All shielding constants are vs tetramethylsilane.

3.2.3.4.3.2.   Organic Molecules:
All photosensitizers and associated organic molecules were optimized in DCM using wB97M-
D4/ZORA ZORA-def2-TVZP with the CPCM solvent model. The RIJCOX approximations
utilizing the SARC/J and autoaux/C auxiliary basis sets were employed where applicable to save
computational resources. Frequency calculations were performed at the same level of theory.
Geometries were confirmed to be at a minimum as evident by the lack of imaginary frequencies. Single
point energies were taken at the wB97M(2)/ ZORA ma-ZORA-def2-QVZPP with the SMD solvent
model.
DLPNO-CCSD(T) calculations were performed using ZORA ZORA-def2-QVZPP with the SMD
solvent model along with tightPNO and further tightening TCutMKN to 1E-4. The SARC/J and
autoaux/C auxiliary basis sets were employed for the RIJ and DLPNO approximations.
The excited state dynamics (ESD) employed wB97X-D4/ma-def2-TZVP geometries for S1 and T1
states, and wB97X/ma-def2-TZVP geometries for all other excited states. Ground state energy (GSE)
of each geometry was taken from DLPNO-CCSD(T)/def2-TZVPP. Excited state energies (DE) were
taken from DLPNO-STEOM-CCSD/def2-TZVP. The GSE and DE were summed to get the final
excited state adiabatic energy which were input as the DELE in all calculations. The adiabatic hessian
was used for fluorescence, ISC, and phosphorescence. The adiabatic hessian after step approximation
was used for internal conversion which included 1 explicit DCM solvent molecule. Spin-orbit coupling

 54

(SOC) was calculated using the mean-field potential, 4 max centers, implementation of the RI
approximation for the coulomb term, full analytical calculation of the exchange term, and inclusion of
local DFT correlation.[115] Transition dipole moments were taken from the wB97X/ma-def2-TZVP
calculations. Final rates were calculated at line width of 35 cm-1, inhomogeneous line width of 175
cm-1, and 2097152 points.

increased probability of defects with the additional layers. Moreover, we anticipate effects of light
penetration depth are emphasized in samples printed vertically. Importantly, both sets of samples

3.2.3.5.10. Mechanistic Studies
The following section provides more detailed information of our investigation to understand the
mechanism by which stereochemistry and Χc are controlled by the intensity of light used for
photopolymerization. First, we applied density functional theory (DFT) transition state modeling to
better elucidate an activation mechanism for HeatMet with ITX and EDAB, which revealed possible
decomposition pathways. We next applied appropriate controls and testing to evaluate the suggested
catalyst decomposition mechanism as well as alternative mechanisms.

3.2.3.5.11. HeatMet Photosystem Activation
Heatmet is a latent catalyst with moderately long potlife due to its strongly chelated ligands which
prevent reaction with monomer under ambient conditions. Heatmet, and Grubbs’ catalyst in general,
may be activated by either light or heat to dissociate a ligand. Previous studies have examined photo-
induced ligand dissociation in ruthenium complexes and have concluded that dissociation is induced
by promotion of an electron into the ligand-Ru anti-bonding orbital. Examination via DFT of the
excited states of HeatMet predict that it will act congruently to earlier generations of Grubb’s catalyst
[118, 119]. The LUMO+3 was identified as a likely candidate orbital to promote the dissociation of
the chelating imine nitrogen and carboxyl oxygen. The percent contribution of this orbital for various

 67

excited states was monitored while scanning the relaxed potential energy surfaces (PES) of the Ru-N
bond dissociation (Table S5). The S3 and S5 excitations were found to contain the most significant
contributions to the population of the LUMO+3 at and near the ground state geometry. At
increasingly longer Ru-N distances, the promotion of an electron into the ground state LUMO+3
decreases in energy until becoming the S1 state around 3Å. The triplet states appear to similarly evolve
(Table S6), but in contrast to the singlets, show only minimal contribution to the LUMO+3 population
until an elongated Ru-N bond length of around 2.4Å and appear to reach a maximum at 2.7Å in the
T4 state. Based on these orbital contributions, it seems probable that the ligand dissociation is
facilitated by the S3 state rather than triplet excitations. A strong counterargument to this point would
be that ligand dissociation and thus catalyst activation should be possible without a sensitizer, which
is not observed. This can be explained by the low oscillator strength of the S3 and S5 excitations
shown in Table S7 and illustrated in Figure S16.

3.2.3.5.12. Catalyst Decomposition
Catalyst decomposition is intimately related to the isomer composition and thus crystallinity of the
ROMP products, by reducing the amount of secondary metathesis, and thus maintaining high cis
content. We have previously alluded to one possible decomposition mechanism in which HeatMet is
excited to its T4 state, resulting in the loss of chlorine. This excitation occurs through EnT processes
from ITX*, ITX3, and EDAB-H2*. Complexation with EDAB-H2 was also implied to result in a similar
decomposition mechanism. Therefore, catalyst decomposition is accelerated under higher light flux
and higher concentrations of these organic species, which is in agreement with experimental data (fig.
S8-S12).
The presence of oxygen presents another well-known decomposition pathway for Ruthenium catalysts
[121]. The typical oxidation process for a SIMes-Grubbs catalyst is shown in Figure 2A. This process
can be greatly accelerated in a photosystem due to the production of singlet oxygen from ITX*. While
HeatMet is predicted to be quite resistant to ground state triplet oxygen (54 kJ/mol), an exergonic
reaction is predicted for reaction with singlet oxygen (-80 kJ/mol). A vast plethora of decomposition
products are possible after the formation of this initial complex and NMR analysis of decomposed
catalyst confirmed numerous Ru-based compounds were generated in later sections.
Based on the above investigation of the photosystem and catalytic cycle in tandem with
comprehensive light, time, and photosensitizer concentration response testing, it is almost certain that
the trans/cis selectivity can be attributed to the delicate balance of catalyst kinetics between ROMP,
secondary metathesis, and catalyst degradation. We have shown that the ROMP is expected to result
in a near 1:1 trans:cis ratio, which is indeed very close to our experimental observation at short time
scales. Secondary metathesis was then shown, both computationally and experimentally, to convert
the cis-isomer to trans-isomer over time up to around a 9:1 trans:cis ratio (fig. S26). The addition of more
photosensitizer and/or light intensity, which we have shown to accelerate catalyst degradation,
resulted in a trans:cis ratio closer to the predicted 1:1, attributable to the decrease in secondary
metathesis (fig. S27-S28).

3.2.3.5.15. Alternative Mechanisms
 Examples of proposed alternative mechanisms included photoisomerization of monomer, competing
radical polymerization, and coordination of the photosensitizer species to yield a bulky, cis-selective
ruthenium complex. Each of these proposed mechanisms were considered unlikely due to the
computational and experimental arguments presented below (Scheme S8).

 87

 Scheme S8: Schematic of the alternative mechanisms examined as a part of this study.

3.2.3.5.15.1. Monomer Photoisomerization:
Cyclooctene (COE) almost exclusively exists as a cis-isomer but can be converted to the much more
reactive trans-isomer in the presence of light [122-124]. Based on STEOMCCSD calculations, COE is
expected to absorb around 170nm (fig. S26) with a T1 at 4.26eV. A lack of absorption above 300 nm
was confirmed experimentally (fig. 30) and is far below the 365 nm irradiation used in this work,
making two-photon absorption the only pathway to the high energy singlet. Furthermore, the T1 is
far above the triplets of the photosensitizers. Two photon absorption is consistent with experiments
illustrating increasing cis-content with higher light intensity; however this phenomenon would also be
independent of photosensitizer concentration which is not experimentally observed. Moreover,
irradiation of COE in 1H NMR showed no evidence of any trans-cyclooctene being formed during or
after irradiation in the presence of ITX and EDAB (fig. S31). Therefore, with a lack of evidence in 1H
NMR and a low likelihood of absorption at 365 nm irradiation, it is unlikely that we are generating
any substantial amount of trans-cyclooctene in situ.

3.2.3.5.15.2. Radical Polymerization:
Previous studies using dicyclopentadiene in similar photoROMP systems saw no evidence of
polymerization without the addition of a ruthenium catalyst [59]. NMR characterization before and
after irradiation of a standard COE resin absent the ruthenium catalyst showed no changes in spectra,
implying that the radicals generated from the photosensitizer system do not interact directly with the

3.2.3.5.15.3. Photosensitizer Coordination:
The dependence of the mechanism on thioxanthone-type photosensitizers (fig. S13 – S14) led to the
belief that a binding event of an activated thioxanthone-type photosensitizer with the activated catalyst
species was a possibility. The discovery of a precipitate during irradiation of ITX/EDAB/HeatMet in
cyclooctane led to the hypothesis that a new, bulkier catalytic species was formed and precipitating.
Bulky catalytic species are well-known to exhibit Z-selectivity [125]. Despite the absence of HeatMet
in the isolated precipitate (fig. S33), the precipitate retained catalytic activity towards COE in the
absence of light. To rule out residual HeatMet being the source of the catalytic activity, COE resins
were prepared with low concentrations of HeatMet, specifically targeting around 1% of the typical
concentration (i.e., 0.0001 mol% HeatMet). In the absence of light, this reaction mixture was limited
to 10% conversion after six days (table S12). Conversely, polymerization by the precipitate reached
over 90% conversion (Fig. 2D), indicating the formation of a new catalytic species. However,

 90

elemental analysis of this precipitate was conducted, and compared with an expected ITX-HeatMet
adduct. The analysis showed a much lower sulfur content and higher oxygen content than the
hypothesized ITX-HeatMet adduct (table S13). Notably, experimental values closely matched
HeatMet with two additional oxygen atoms. Further experimentation with other photosensitizers
found that precipitates could be formed using other photosensitizers and using ITX without the
EDAB coinitiator, albeit at longer irradiation times. While other photosensitizers formed ample
precipitates, multiple hours of high intensity irradiation of HeatMet only formed very little precipitate
in comparison and the NMR spectra of this precipitate closely resembled the purchased HeatMet
catalyst. This suggests that a photosensitizer is necessary for this intermediate to be formed but the
kinetics seem to vary depending on the choice of photosensitizer and coinitiator (fig. S34). In addition
to experimental data, the ligand binding affinities towards the ruthenium center were investigated
using previously mentioned DFT methodologies. According to the energies in Table S14, ITX has the
lowest binding energy, both through the oxygen and sulfur, of all organic components in solution.
Therefore, it is unlikely that ITX is able to form a solution stable complex with the ruthenium center
under the present conditions.

3.2.3.5.15.4. Accelerated Oxidation:
As described above, the presence of oxygen and a high energy photosystem likely contributes to
decomposition of the catalyst. To examine if oxygen was the sole contributor to varied percent
crystallinity, we examined the result of polymerization in the absence of oxygen (i.e., in an argon
atmosphere). Similar to previous experiments under ambient conditions, polymerization of COE
under argon resulted in disparate trans-polyoctenamer content depending on the irradiation intensity
used (table S15). Interestingly, NMR after irradiation of an ITX/EDAB/HeatMet mixture in C6D6 in
both the absence and presence of oxygen had differences in the resulting decomposition products
around 3.0 ppm. Furthermore, exposing the decomposed product formed under inert conditions to
oxygen led to further decomposition of the catalyst. This suggests that while oxygen decomposition
occurs under irradiation, it is not necessary for the observed variation in stereochemistry (fig. S35).

3.2.3.5.15.5. Temperature Control:
To rule out any effects from the extra thermal energy from the higher intensity light, photorheology
was conducted using a Peltier system to actively maintain temperature control while
photopolymerizing. The standard COE resin was photopolymerized under active temperature control
at 25°C and 5°C at high (130 mW/cm2) and low (10 mW/cm2) intensities. These samples were then
tested in 1H NMR for the trans-alkene content and in DSC for the crystallinity. Due to the complexity
in temperature likely affecting polymerization, crystallization, and decomposition rates,
photopolymerization at different temperatures does change the crystallization behavior. However, the
difference in crystallinity caused by different light intensities can be seen at different temperatures and
under active temperature control suggesting the difference isn’t a temperature effect. In fact, this data
suggests that there may be opportunities to improve crystallinity differences further through active
temperature control during printing.

4. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE NEEDS
In summary, this project has demonstrated several new additive manufacturing techniques and
shown the versatility of photoROMP. While we were unable to progress on machine learning thrusts
do to turnover in staff, the new materials we discovered more then make up for the lack of progress
in the one area and allowed us to reach our goal.
These recent advancements in Ring-Opening Metathesis Polymerization (ROMP) have significantly
enhanced the understanding and application of this versatile polymerization technique. Key
developments include the introduction of novel catalysts that exhibit improved activity and
selectivity, which not only provide insights into catalyst design but also open avenues for the
synthesis of high-performance polymers with tailored properties. Innovative approaches have been
explored for creating defined crystallinity in polymers with unique morphologies through ROMP.
The ability to control molecular weight and composition in these copolymers has implications for
applications in drug delivery systems and nanotechnology, showcasing the versatility of ROMP in
producing materials with specific functionalities.
The integration of ROMP with other polymerization techniques has demonstrated the potential for
creating hybrid materials with synergistic properties. This approach broadens the scope of ROMP
and enhances the performance of the resulting materials in various applications, including coatings
and adhesives. Understanding the mechanistic pathways of ROMP is crucial for optimizing
polymerization conditions and rationally designing catalysts. By elucidating these reaction
mechanisms, researchers can develop more efficient and sustainable processes, ultimately leading to
improved outcomes in polymer synthesis.
Additionally, the application of ROMP in the synthesis of stimuli-responsive polymers has garnered
attention. These materials exhibit changes in properties in response to external stimuli, paving the
way for advancements in smart materials and responsive systems.
Looking ahead, several promising directions emerge from the ongoing research in ROMP. First, the
development of more sustainable and environmentally friendly catalysts is essential. Future studies
could focus on utilizing biobased or recyclable materials in catalyst design, thereby minimizing the
environmental impact of polymer production.
Another exciting avenue is the investigation of ROMP in the context of self-healing materials.
Designing polymers that can autonomously repair themselves could significantly enhance the
longevity and durability of materials used across various industries.
