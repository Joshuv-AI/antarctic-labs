BrainHealth 360: cognitive intervention for older adults designed using a participatory framework
Authors: Jamie F Mayer, Ryanne C Anderson, Jamie Ward, Laura R Johnson, Makenna Green, Yessenia Chavez, Hongdao Meng, William Baker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesDespite increasing evidence of the critical impact of modifiable lifestyle factors and cognitive self-efficacy in healthy aging, programs to address these components have been limited for rural-dwelling adults. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and test BrainHealth 360, a co-created multi-component healthy aging protocol, via a community-based transcreation framework within a rural context.Research Design and MethodsFollowing iterative co-creation, implementation outcomes and preliminary efficacy data for BrainHealth 360 were gathered using a mixed-methods convergent design with 40 older adults.ResultsHigh levels of intervention acceptability and appropriateness were tied to themes (self-efficacy, social connection, and applicability) in the context of individualized instruction. Identified barriers included accessibility challenges and aging-related fear. Participants reported increased memory self-efficacy and implementation of selected lifestyle changes and cognitive strategies. No change was detected on an objective measure of cognitive function nor in participants’ self-perceptions of the aging process.Discussion and ImplicationsThese results provide preliminary support for the co-design approach to develop context-specific educational interventions for the promotion of brain health among older adults, with future testing warranted to confirm the efficacy of the resulting intervention.

We illustrate the use of a transcreation framework to develop and implement a multi-component brain health intervention for rural-dwelling older adults, emphasizing intervention co-design and evaluation in real-world settings to maximize relevance and impact in the setting of potential place-based health disparities. This work demonstrates the value of partnering directly with older adults at the community level for cognitive intervention design and implementation, particularly for those who may be experiencing structural inequities and thus increased vulnerability to cognitive decline.

Cognitive health in late life is closely intertwined with physical and psychological health as well as quality of life (Cohen-Mansfield et al., 2015); thus, maintaining cognitive function during the aging process is a critical public health concern. More than two-thirds of older adults experience cognitive changes during healthy aging (Hale et al., 2020), with up to 40% of older adults without dementia living with age-associated memory impairments (Small & Vorgan, 2012). A paradigm shift has taken place in the last decade, with a growing focus on identifying modifiable risk and protective factors relative to cognitive decline and dementia (Livingston et al., 2024). The result has been the creation of a more positive narrative: cognitive decline is not inevitable. However, the rapid gains in knowledge and resulting influx of public messaging has led to significant variability within the general population with respect to knowledge, beliefs, and actions related to cognitive aging and research-supported risk and protective factors (Niechcial et al., 2025). This is particularly problematic in rural areas within the US, where older adults tend to face a higher burden of modifiable dementia risk factors and structural inequities in access to care compared to other geographic locations (Cohen & Greaney, 2023; Xie et al., 2025). Moreover, even among adults who endorse a high awareness level of behaviors known to prevent or delay cognitive impairment, a much smaller percentage report making lifestyle changes accordingly (Nielsen et al., 2018). There is thus a need for increased reach of direct interventions that empower individuals to take concrete steps toward achieving and sustaining brain health, particularly in rural areas where older adults may be more vulnerable to cognitive decline.

Given the multifactorial nature of age-related cognitive decline and dementia neuropathology (Park & Festini, 2017), the fact that no singular cognitive intervention or educational protocol has been shown to definitively prevent or delay the onset of cognitive decline or dementia makes intuitive sense (Kane et al., 2017). Recent evidence suggests that multicomponent approaches aligned with a cognitive reserve framework may help to build cognitive plasticity, resilience, and resistance to age-related neuropathology (Basak et al., 2020; Denny et al., 2023). These comprehensive approaches typically include a combination of lifestyle education (e.g., optimal physical activity and sleep habits), individualized cognitive rehabilitation or metacognitive strategy training (Hertzog et al., 2021; Sella et al., 2023), and mental stimulation in the form of decontextualized cognitive training exercises (Cohen-Mansfield et al., 2015; Denny et al., 2023).

Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR; Israel et al., 2017), defined as the building of collaborative and equitable partnerships among academic researchers and stakeholders, has demonstrated high relevance to align behavioral change with scientific evidence for such change. With origins in public health, CBPR frameworks, and in particular, the transcreation framework (Nápoles & Stewart, 2018), have been suggested as a means to reach marginalized communities experiencing health disparities (Bogart & Uyeda, 2009). This framework shifts the typical implementation paradigm: Rather than adaptation of an existing evidence-based intervention to a new setting, new interventions are co-designed to best fit local needs, thus maximizing program viability, authenticity, and accessibility (Nápoles & Stewart, 2018). Despite these advantages, CBPR and the transcreation framework specifically have rarely been applied to cognitive health educational interventions (Middleton et al., 2026). Thus, the overarching aim of this study was to describe the utilization of a transcreation framework to co-design and formatively assess BrainHealth 360: a structured, multicomponent intervention program developed with and for older adults residing in a rural area of the US. Method

The transcreation framework consists of seven distinct steps (Nápoles & Stewart, 2018; see Supplementary Table S1, See online supplementary material). This study involved the application of transcreation Steps 1–5 to develop and test BrainHealth 360—so named to capture the multi-domain or comprehensive approach endorsed—with the primary research questions serving as a formative assessment of feasibility and preliminary efficacy following the third iterative cycle of intervention co-design. Each step as applied to the current study is described in detail below.

Transcreation Step 1: Identify community infrastructure and engage partners

Older adult participants

Residents of two senior living communities located within a rural county in the Midwest were invited to participate in BrainHealth 360 development and piloting. The senior living environment was selected to minimize transportation and mobility issues as potential participation barriers. Residents were, by definition, self-sufficient, but had opted to live in a senior living environment due to financial, safety, or other concerns. Additional inclusion criteria were: (1) age 65 or older; and (2) ability to engage meaningfully in group activities as reported by site staff. Given the significant heterogeneity often observed across samples of independently dwelling older adults (e.g., Schönstein, 2025) and to ensure maximal inclusion of the voices of adults within each senior living facility, no formal cognitive or sensory (e.g., visual or hearing loss) criteria were established for project inclusion/exclusion. Residents self-selected program enrollment following an informational session held at each site. Additional recruitment methods included fliers posted in common areas at each site and weekly verbal reminders provided by site staff during other activities (e.g., resident meetings).

A total of 40 older adults participated in this study. Participants ranged in age from 65 to 99 (mean = 84.7 years) and were primarily female (77%), widowed (82%), and Caucasian (all). Older adult participant characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Student participants

Twenty graduate and undergraduate students enrolled in health-related majors (e.g., psychology, speech-language pathology) at Northern Illinois University served as intervention co-facilitators over the course of this study. Seven students were recruited following brief in-class presentations from the research team; 13 were enrolled in an undergraduate seminar taught by the first author. Prior to their first intervention session alongside older adults, all student participants received an in-person or online training session (60 min) provided by the first author, during which common age-related cognitive and sensory challenges and corresponding communication tips were reviewed (Hallowell, 2023).

Facility staff

Three facility staff members (one Associate Director, two Activities Directors) observed portions of each of the intervention co-design and refinement cycle sessions. Although not directly involved in intervention implementation, each staff member provided feedback to the research team based on their observations and on informal discussions with participants outside of the sessions.

Ethical approval

All study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board at Northern Illinois University (Protocols no. HS24-0155 (older adult participants) and HS240100 (student participants)) and by the administration at the participating sites. Written informed consent was provided by each participant prior to each intervention/refinement cycle.

Transcreation Step 2: Specify theory

The theoretical basis for intervention development was drawn from Nielsen et al.’s (2018) “mechanisms of behavior change” framework, by which behavior change as a clinical endpoint is achieved via one of several overlapping target domains: (1) self-regulation, (2) stress resilience, and/or (3) interpersonal and social processes. While each domain is highly relevant to health behavior outcomes, we identified both self-regulation (i.e., health beliefs) and social processes (i.e., social isolation) as dual disparity determinants (Cohen & Greaney, 2023) and potential target mechanisms of change. Therefore, the overarching goals of our intervention were to (1) change participants’ health beliefs (i.e., empower participants to understand that cognitive decline is not inevitable; Livingston et al., 2024); (2) within a social, supportive context. Self-efficacy (i.e., confidence in one’s abilities to achieve behavioral objectives; Bandura, 1997) is closely tied to the concept of self-regulation (i.e., monitoring, controlling, and potentially adapting thoughts and behaviors for the purpose of achieving goals; Fan & Cui, 2024). That is, individuals with increased self-efficacy are more likely to engage in both proactive and adaptive self-regulation behaviors (Bandura, 1997). We hypothesized that increased self-efficacy in addition to interpersonal and social support (i.e., regular interactions throughout the intervention encouraging a sense of belonging and community among participants) would facilitate behavioral changes, including (a) increased health behavior engagement, (b) increased use of cognitive strategies for maximal cognitive engagement, and thus (c) increased cognitive performance.

Transcreation Steps 3 and 4: Identify multiple inputs and develop intervention

The transcreation framework gives weight to scientific evidence as well as community-level input during intervention development. Our research team (i.e., a trained speech-language pathologist with expertise in acquired neurogenic cognitive-communicative impairments and two student research assistants) thus began by developing elements of the BrainHealth 360 intervention prototype guided by experiential learning frameworks (Kolb, 2015) in conjunction with previously published multicomponent programs (Hafdi et al., 2021). Our prototype included three primary components: (1) psychoeducational and lifestyle-focused instruction, (2) cognitive strategy practice (e.g., deep processing; Craik, 2002), and (3) cognitive exercises. Based on our dual hypothesized mechanisms of change (self-efficacy and social support), the intervention was structured to promote both social interaction, as well as self-efficacy and a sense of control (Lee et al., 2023; Strickland-Hughes & West, 2022). Social components of each session included group-based game design elements, including small prizes distributed weekly (Chen et al., 2025); small-group conversational interactions; and positive feedback. Self-efficacy was targeted via a self-regulatory, learner-oriented focus. Brain health literacy components were reviewed during large-group didactic discussions, which focused on underlying mechanisms of cognition (e.g., how memory works; mechanisms by which physical activity or sleep hygiene directly affect cognitive health). Knowledge checks were included at the end of each session, with explicit discussions to encourage actionable goals and application of trained strategies to daily life. Self-efficacy was further addressed by provision of (a) enactive mastery experience (i.e., cognitive exercises, adapted from published cognitive rehabilitation workbooks (e.g., Small & Vorgan, 2012; Tomlin, 2007)), provided in group and individual activities to promote a sense of accomplishment and confidence, and (b) vicarious experience (peer-to-peer discussions with metacognitive reflection and sharing of experiences in practicing trained cognitive strategies; Hertzog et al., 2021; Sella et al., 2023).

BrainHealth 360 as a transcreated intervention required three cycles of community-based refinement. The first two cycles provided feedback loops, with grounded theory methodology (Charmaz, 2006) used to refine intervention components iteratively. Refinement cycle participants (n = 11 in Cycle 1 and n = 16 in Cycle 2; Table 1) met as a group with the research team on-site at their senior living community. Twenty sessions (10 per refinement cycle) took place for 60 min once per week over the course of an academic year, with a mid-year break following the university schedule. Reflective feedback was gathered during each session using two methods. First, within-session reflective feedback during large group discussions and 1:1 or small-group interactions was gathered directly from refinement cycle participants and transcribed verbatim in real time. Next, memos, or informal analytic notes (Charmaz, 2006), were recorded during sessions to document participant observations and interpretations of direct feedback. All data were reviewed weekly by the first three authors (J.F.M., R.C.A., and J.W.), with agreement on all feedback elements achieved through discussion among the research team. This consensus process served as a reflexive site, where the team interrogated initial assumptions about intervention topics and documented points of agreement to ensure that the analysis reflected collaborative negotiation rather than a single viewpoint. Open coding was used to identify themes characterizing participant experiences and feedback (Charmaz, 2006). Emergent themes were discussed with participants for accuracy and refined accordingly. The BrainHealth 360 curriculum was then adapted iteratively by considering each of these themes in turn. Transcreation Step 5: Design study strategies for data collection

Nápoles and Stewart (2018) specify that Step 5 of the transcreation framework typically involves planning for a summative intervention assessment (e.g., pilot RCT), based on results of Steps 1–4. We chose to utilize this step to plan a single-group, pre-post formative assessment of BrainHealth 360 during refinement Cycle 3, with results informing the finalized intervention protocol and future 2-group pilot RCT (Steps 6 and 7). Our formative assessment plan was guided by Proctor et al.’s (2011) conceptual outcomes framework, which specifies three key domains of interest, including the constructs of acceptability (satisfaction), appropriateness (perceived fit and relevance), and feasibility (resource requirements, barriers/facilitators). We additionally planned to examine preliminary efficacy with respect to: (1) hypothesized mechanisms of change (self-efficacy, social support) and (2) health behaviors (e.g., use of cognitive strategies). We chose a convergent mixed-methods approach to assess each construct (Aschbrenner et al., 2022), with data triangulation to inform comprehensive understanding of each outcome domain. In line with the core principles of the CBPR framework (Israel et al., 2017), a subset of older adult intervention participants (n = 3) worked collaboratively to review, interpret, and provide feedback on all data analysis procedures and results. BrainHealth 360 implementation outcomes: Acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility

Three interrelated tools were selected to provide quantitative assessment of stakeholder perspectives (older adults, students, and facility staff) on key outcome domains: the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM), and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) (Weiner et al., 2017). The AIM, IAM, and FIM each consist of four items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1: Completely Disagree to 5: Completely Agree). Additionally, recruitment, retention, session attendance, and program adherence (i.e., pre-post testing participation) were tracked for each participant as quantitative outcomes informing intervention feasibility.

To maximize our understanding of potential factors driving each key outcome domain, we chose to gather qualitative feedback from all stakeholders, including older adult participants, student volunteers, and staff. An author-designed written survey was distributed during the final session of Cycle 3 (see Supplementary Methods 1); all participants were encouraged to complete this anonymously for maximal candor. We gathered additional verbal feedback from a subgroup of older adult participants during a post-intervention focus group (8 weeks following the last session of Cycle 3) to contextualize written survey responses. All written feedback was analyzed in its original form; verbal feedback was recorded and transcribed offline by a student research assistant. All transcriptions were checked for accuracy by the first author (J.F.M.), with differences resolved through discussion.

BrainHealth 360 preliminary efficacy

We planned to assess the preliminary efficacy of BrainHealth 360 via both objective and participant-reported measures, administered prior to and immediately following Cycle 3.

Hypothesized mechanism of behavioral change I: self-efficacy

We chose the Multifactorial Memory Questionnaire (MMQ; Troyer & Rich, 2002) to measure potential changes in participants’ cognitive self-efficacy following intervention. The MMQ is a self-report tool that examines self-perceived memory (i.e., how confident a person is in his or her daily memory performance), in contrast to an objective, clinical memory test. The MMQ has strong psychometric properties for measuring meta-memory across both healthy older adults and adults with subjective memory complaints (α = 0.80–0.96; Troyer & Rich, 2002), and includes a “satisfaction” scale in addition to “ability” (frequency of memory mistakes) and “strategy” (frequency of using memory aids or strategies) sections.

Hypothesized mechanism of behavioral change II: Social support

We chose to use the Attitudes to Aging Questionnaire (AAQ; Laidlaw et al., 2007) to assess potential changes in aging-related quality of life as a proxy for reflecting participants’ feelings of social support, given the close ties between this construct and older adults’ well-being (Zanjari et al., 2022). The AAQ contains three subscales: physical change, psychosocial loss, and psychological growth; it has been shown to be a reliable and valid measure of attitudes toward aging and correlates with other measures of self-perceived health and well-being (α = 0.74–0.81; Laidlaw et al., 2007).

Behavioral targets I and II: Lifestyle changes and cognitive strategy use

An author-developed rating scale (see Supplementary Methods 1) was administered immediately post-intervention to track participants’ application of individual lifestyle changes and trained cognitive strategies to their everyday lives. These behavioral targets were selected based on the hypothesized mechanisms of self-efficacy and social support. Questionnaires were included to measure these variables to support sample size calculation for a fully powered efficacy trial.

Behavioral target III: Cognitive performance

The Brief Cognitive Assessment Tool (BCAT; Mansbach et al., 2012) was chosen as a potential indicator of cognitive performance. This assessment was chosen due to its sensitivity to multiple aspects of cognitive function across a variety of older adult populations (MacDougall et al., 2015) in conjunction with its ease of administration, which made it a practical option and avoided the potential burden of prolonged or more in-depth testing. The BCAT is scored from 0 to 50, with scores below 45 suggestive of cognitive impairment (MacDougall et al., 2015). We administered the BCAT prior to and immediately following Cycle 3 of BrainHealth 360.

Data analysis strategy

BrainHealth 360 implementation outcomes: Acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility

Cycle 3 recruitment, retention, attendance, testing participation rate, and results of the AIM, IAM, and FIM (Weiner et al., 2017) were examined via descriptive statistics. The planned analytic strategy for written and verbal feedback included initial inductive analysis followed by deductive coding to align data with the key outcome areas of interest (i.e., Table 3). This approach is advantageous for examining the acceptability and feasibility of complex behavioral interventions (Palsola et al., 2020), in which an iterative and reflexive process harnesses the benefits of both inductive and deductive methodology. The planned analysis followed a 4-step process (Braun & Clarke, 2006): (1) answers to open-ended survey questions and focus group transcripts were read through in entirety to ensure familiarization; (2) an open (inductive) coding approach was used to identify individual subtopics until thematic saturation (i.e., no new emergent codes) was achieved; (3) axial coding was used to align identified subtopics with outcome domains of interest; and (4) quotations were selected that captured each identified subtheme. At each step, active reflexivity and inter-rater reliability of coding were established by the first two authors (J.F.M. and R.C.A.) via joint discussion, challenging of potential individual biases, and consensus.

BrainHealth 360 preliminary efficacy

All pre-/post-quantitative data were analyzed using paired t-tests with significance set to p < .05. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s dz, which reflects that the units of analysis in a within-subject design are (individually correlated) change scores (Lakens, 2013). Participants’ self-reported lifestyle changes and trained strategy application were ranked from most to least often utilized based on average scores across the Cycle 3 cohort. Given the exploratory nature of this study, an a priori power analysis was not conducted.

Results

RQ 1. Implementation outcomes: acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility

Following BrainHealth 360’s third refinement cycle, 22 older adults (65% participation rate) in addition to three staff members and three student volunteers completed the AIM, IAM, and FIM. Figure 1A shows the distribution of answers on the three scales. Inductive thematic analysis of participants’ written and verbal feedback resulted in the identification of six overarching themes illustrating pragmatic aspects of program implementation and potential facilitators or barriers. In order from most to least often occurring, these included (a) increased self-efficacy via engagement in cognitively effortful activities, (b) enjoyment of social interactions, (c) appreciation for applicability and relevance of program content to everyday life, (d) the need for individualized assistance to maximize inclusion and facilitate differentiation, (e) accessibility challenges in the face of age-related cognitive and/or sensory changes, and (f) fear of uncovering or facing age-related cognitive deficits. Each theme was aligned with one of the three key outcome domains of interest via deductive analysis (axial coding; Figure 1B). Integration of qualitative and quantitative data for each outcome domain is described below.

Implementation outcomes (A) and axial coding of qualitative feedback (B) based on Proctor et al.’s (2011) conceptual framework. AIM = acceptability of intervention measure; FIM = feasibility of intervention measure; IAM = intervention appropriateness measure (Weiner et al., 2017).

Acceptability

The median AIM scale answer was 5 (“completely agree”), with a mean of 4.55 (SD =.66), indicating strong acceptability of BrainHealth 360 (Figure 1A). Two core themes were identified via axial coding as shaping this outcome: self-efficacy via cognitive effort and peer-to-peer and intergenerational social connections.

Self-efficacy via cognitive effort

The most common theme was participants’ sense of accomplishment during weekly cognitive challenges. As one participant shared: “We need more activities [here]… that stimulate the mind.” Another older adult added: “It helped me to challenge myself… I’m ready for more learning!” One participant, a woman in her 90s, commented: “It was hard! And the sessions kept getting harder… I didn’t think I could figure out the exercises. But then my son told me, ‘Don’t give up. Keep trying.’ And I did, and I’m so proud!” This sense of pride was additionally noted by facility staff; as one activities staff member commented: “They just loved showing us their homework.”

Social connections

Participants and staff consistently noted the group camaraderie and the program’s interactive environment. A staff member commented: “The amount of laughter coming from the group is amazing.” One older adult, who had recently moved into the facility, noted: “I met a lot of great friends here!” Participants additionally appreciated the opportunity to build ongoing intergenerational relationships. A retired professor commented: “I loved having the students to make it fun and challenging—Definitely keep bringing them!” Intergenerational social connections were often mutual; for example, one student who did not have surviving grandparents of her own became very close with one of the older adult participants, continuing to visit her during university breaks. The student observed that “our connection transcended our generational and racial differences … I found myself opening up about my challenges and goals.”

Appropriateness

The median IAM scale answer was 5 (“completely agree”), with a mean of 4.47 (SD =.68), indicating a strong perception of the appropriateness of BrainHealth 360 among participants (Figure 1A). Axial coding identified two themes shaping this outcome: applicability/relevance of program content and individualized instruction and differentiation.

Applicability

Participants highlighted the importance of relating session content to everyday life. Many reported independently applying learned cognitive strategies (e.g., spaced retrieval), as one participant noted: “I could have used these ideas earlier!.” Another commented: “I used the strategies for memory improvement–and it was successful!” Participants noted the value of integrating lifestyle changes into their daily activities (e.g., “I access the exercise equipment here more, now…”), although several also pointed out the challenges of incorporating what they’d learned in their community living setting; as illustrated by one participant’s comment that “I have a harder time changing nutrition because it [the menu] is limited, here… .”

Inclusion and differentiation

A major challenge to offering cognitive aging intervention programs is the wide range of functional capacity possible among program participants (Hale et al., 2020; Schönstein, 2025). Participants reinforced the importance of individualized pacing, repetition, and positive feedback. One participant, a woman who relied on student scaffolding to complete group-based cognitive exercises, noted: “When you goof up, they tell you it’s okay.” Acknowledging the wide inter-individual variability in cognitive and functional status among participants, several participants recommended further differentiation of program options; as one older adult put it, “Some [of us] had to help others with the homework…Maybe offer groups for more versus less challenging [activities].”

Feasibility

The median of the FIM was 4 (“agree”), with a mean of 4.3 (SD = 0.78). While indicating positive perceptions of feasibility from stakeholders, this was lower than the ratings for both acceptability and appropriateness (Figure 1A). Feasibility was shaped by attendance, adherence, and two themes derived via axial coding (Charmaz, 2006): accessibility and aging-associated fear/stigma.

Of the 43 older adults who initially enrolled in Cycle 3 of BrainHealth 360, nine declined to participate after attending the first session, resulting in a retention rate of 78%. Of the remaining 34 Cycle 3 participants, 21 (62%) met the 80% attendance benchmark (i.e., a minimum of 7 sessions attended), with a mean of six sessions attended across older adult participants (median = 7). Approximately half completed the optional pre- and post-testing, with 19 older adults completing the MMQ (55.9%), 17 completing the AAQ (50%), and 16 completing the BCAT (47.1%). Although these numbers did not meet pre-established feasibility benchmarks (Table 3), feedback from facility staff indicated that these trends were positive indicators for the facilities; as one activities staff member noted: “The program has had consistent resident turnout, which hasn’t been the norm with extended activities.”

Accessibility

Participants acknowledged that issues impacting sensory function, such as poor auditory discrimination in noise, negatively affected their ability to understand large-group discussions; as one participant commented: “There are probably no solutions, but I can’t concentrate with all the noise!” Others noted that this challenge was alleviated at least partially via the 1:1 and small-group support from student volunteers, as illustrated by another participant, who added: “I loved the individual help–otherwise I wouldn’t have been able to participate.”

Brain health through the lens of fear

Participants identified avoidance-based behaviors as a potential but critical barrier to intervention recruitment. As one older adult, a former occupational therapy assistant, noted: “There’s a lot of cognitive decline here… I see it. But they’re afraid to admit it. There’s many who just don’t want to know how they are doing.” Another participant, a retired professor, voiced this concern relative to intervention retention: “Drawing these things to personal attention has a downside… deficiencies may well increase rather than reduce.” Others, however, observed the value of vicarious experience via peer-to-peer discussion of cognitive aging, as summarized by a participant in her 70s: “My confidence has gone up. Because I was already having to use some of these [strategies], so I’m not that bad off. I had thought something was wrong with me.”

As shown in Figure 2, participants (n = 19) endorsed significant changes on two of the three MMQ subscales from pre- to post-intervention, including strategy use (t = 2.89, p = .001; dz = .66) and memory ability (t = 2.213, p = .04; dz = .51). Marginally significant change was found in the “satisfaction with memory” subscale (t = 1.95, p = .07, dz = .45). Participants’ self-reported lifestyle changes and trained cognitive strategy application as reported via post-intervention questionnaires (see Supplementary Methods 2) indicated that the most consistently utilized content from BrainHealth 360 (“often” to “very often”) included nutritional changes, implementation of physical exercise routines, and application of attentional strategies (i.e., reduction of multi-tasking). Discussion

As the population of older adults increases, there is a parallel need for increased reach of and access to evidence-based community-level interventions that encourage individuals to take concrete steps toward promoting brain health. This is particularly relevant for older adults residing in rural areas, who face unique challenges compared to those in more urban areas. Given the strong evidence supporting multicomponent interventions (e.g., Sella et al., 2023), this study thus explored the potential of a transcreation framework to maximize implementation outcomes and preliminary efficacy of a multicomponent program, BrainHealth 360, developed with and for older adults in a rural area of the US. Study results were consistent with high levels of intervention acceptability and appropriateness, tied to themes of maximizing self-efficacy via cognitive effort, facilitating peer-to-peer and intergenerational social connections, and providing applicable program content in the context of individualized differentiation, which was made possible by pairing older adult participants with student co-facilitators. Intervention feasibility was moderately high, with accessibility challenges and fear identified as barriers to recruitment and retention. Preliminary efficacy data were consistent with positive effects on participants’ cognitive self-efficacy (i.e., self-evaluative memory skills [MMQ]), and increased application of selected healthy lifestyle changes and cognitive strategies. No change was detected on an objective measure of cognitive function (BCAT) nor in participants’ self-perceptions of their quality of life during the aging process (AAQ).

The results of this study add to the existing literature supporting direct targeting of belief restructuring and metacognition within a social context to increase perceived control over memory and strategy use to maximize daily functioning (e.g., Denny et al., 2023; Hertzog et al., 2021; West et al., 2008). Our results extend this literature by demonstrating that this potential mechanism of change is highly relevant for adults residing in a rural environment. The multifactorial nature of BrainHealth 360 may have enhanced self-regulatory factors within a relatively short timeframe by encouraging participants to use newly acquired knowledge about the nature of memory to guide implementation of learned strategies in a flexible manner (Lineweaver et al., 2023; Strickland-Hughes & West, 2022; West et al., 2008). It is also possible that the incorporation of game design elements (e.g., rewards, challenges, collaboration) increased motivation and engagement, thereby promoting intervention adherence (Chen et al., 2025).

Lessons learned: Study challenges

Brain health intervention recruitment

Interventions to maximize cognitive health during aging are only as effective as the extent to which they are completed (Strickland-Hughes & West, 2022). In line with this challenge, a significant barrier identified during this study was the fear and stigma associated with participation in a cognitive health intervention that could potentially expose or uncover existing deficits. Future iterations of this and similarly motivated protocols will require application of the transcreation framework beyond crafting aspects of the intervention itself to address this stigma prior to the initial recruitment phase (Jacklin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). This is particularly relevant for interventions developed with and for rural-dwelling older adults, where resistance to recognizing cognitive decline may be more common (Cohen & Greaney, 2023).

Accordingly, although our results supported the utility of BrainHealth 360 for its intended purpose, it is possible that our choice of the senior living environment attracted participants who were more highly intrinsically motivated at baseline compared to the general older adult population (Strickland-Hughes & West, 2022; Vorwerk et al., 2022), including those facing a health disparity such as rurality. The social determinants of rurality are complex, and it may be that residents of senior living centers, which inherently involve collectivism, are a subgroup that is less likely to align with the emphasis on self-reliance often attributed to rurality (Cohen & Greaney, 2023) and thus more likely to embrace a community-level intervention. Future iterations of BrainHealth 360 will require an implementation assessment for rural-dwelling adults outside of the communal senior living setting.

Formative and summative intervention assessment

Our primary planned quantitative cognitive outcome measure, the BCAT, has been shown to be sensitive to both the presence and absence of cognitive impairments (MacDougall et al., 2015). However, the majority of BrainHealth 360 participants performed near ceiling level at baseline. This restricted range of scores may have reflected our collaborative approach to intervention development and implementation, in that participant pre-post cognitive testing was optional. The emphasis on an empowering, collaborative learning culture inherent in the transcreation process may conflict with the provision of mandatory, more challenging, or sensitive cognitive outcome measures, which risk lengthy testing sessions and possible participant fatigue, perception of a stigmatizing environment, or declines in cognitive self-efficacy (Strickland-Hughes & West, 2022; Vorwerk et al., 2022). It is possible that those who opted out of BCAT testing would have found the test more challenging. A potential solution for future studies of BrainHealth 360 and similarly motivated interventions is to focus on person-centered goals as the primary intervention outcome measure (e.g., goal attainment scaling) in conjunction with journaling or self-report of everyday memory failures (Hertzog et al., 2021) to reflect cognitive strategy application and everyday implications thereof.

Cognitive strategy training

Although study participants endorsed using trained cognitive strategies following intervention, task-specific tactics such as spaced retrieval and visual imagery were rated less often utilized compared to more general strategies (e.g., avoiding multitasking). This is in line with selective engagement theory (Hess, 2014) and may reflect decreased willingness to implement more effortful or cognitively demanding strategies (Lineweaver et al., 2023). It is likely that cognitive strategy application following training requires ongoing support and practice to achieve enduring benefits (Denny et al., 2023; Sella et al., 2023), supporting the need for long-term embedding of cognitive health programs into the fabric of community-based aging services (Blair & Minkler, 2009).

The value and complexity of co-design frameworks

Co-design has become increasingly more common across disciplines, reflecting the broader context of shifting healthcare priorities toward person-centered care and outcomes (Middleton et al., 2026; Ní Shé & Harrison, 2021). The transcreation framework provides a path for meaningfully addressing health-related concerns of older adults, particularly those facing challenges unique to rural areas, by actively involving them in the process of designing solutions (Blouin Gagnon et al., 2025; Nápoles & Stewart, 2018). Building collaborative and equitable partnerships among academic researchers and older adults can enhance intervention outcomes, particularly older adults’ self-efficacy about their ability to control current and prospective cognitive health. The iterative dialogue involved in BrainHealth 360 co-development allowed for the intervention to reflect participants’ strengths, challenges, and logistical realities, mirroring the results of co-design processes for cognitive health interventions with other vulnerable populations (e.g., Middleton et al., 2026).

It is important to contextualize the key strengths of CBPR by carefully examining potential drawbacks of co-design frameworks. A fundamental advantage of the transcreation process and a driver of this study is the concept of buy-in: Participants are more likely to engage with an intervention they helped create, resulting in higher levels of adherence and thus potential intervention effectiveness compared to a more “investigator-driven” approach. This enhanced contextual relevance comes at a potential cost, however, in that it can be difficult to separate evaluation of the intervention in and of itself from evaluation of the co-design process (Kirk et al., 2021; Ní Shé & Harrison, 2021). For example, despite our efforts to encourage critical and confidential feedback, the highly positive ratings of intervention acceptability and appropriateness by participants in this study, a portion of whom were involved in the first two cycles of intervention refinement, may have reflected enhanced ownership and commitment to the project rather than or in addition to key elements of the intervention itself. Moreover, the ability of the co-design process to result in highly contextualized intervention paradigms may limit ultimate scale and spread of the intervention (Kirk et al., 2021), with potential negative effects in terms of long-term health impacts (Ní Shé & Harrison, 2021).

Conclusion

As the population of older adults worldwide continues to grow, there is a need for direct, context-specific interventions that encourage individuals to take concrete steps toward achieving and sustaining behavioral changes and brain health. This study, as a single-group pre-post iterative design, highlighted intervention development in a participatory-based context and represents an initial step in evaluating the value of co-design for implementing a multi-component brain health program, BrainHealth 360, within a rural context. Our results lend support for continuing to involve key partners actively and early in the development process based on mutual, shared goals and a collaborative learning culture. While a strength of the study was the transparent and reproducible illustration of a co-design process in real time, future work should prioritize intervention sustainability, scale, and efficacy via a two-group pilot RCT within the rural community setting.
