Perioperative Dexamethasone Is Associated With a Reduced Risk of Periprosthetic Joint Infection Following Aseptic Revision Total Hip and Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Sahil S. Telang, Pranit Kumaran, Sagar Telang, Ian A. Jones, Julian Wier, Andrew G. Yun, Jay R. Lieberman, Nathanael D. Heckmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDexamethasone has been increasingly used perioperatively for arthroplasty procedures. However, there is limited evidence on its use and safety for revision total hip (THA) and knee arthroplasty (TKA). This study sought to assess the association between perioperative dexamethasone administration and postoperative infectious complications in patients undergoing aseptic revision THA and TKA.MethodsUsing a national healthcare database, we identified adult patients undergoing aseptic, both-component revision THA and TKA between 2016 and 2023. Patients were stratified into those treated by high- (DEX[+]) or low-use (DEX[−]) dexamethasone surgeons using k-means clustering. Mixed-effects logistic regression was used to compare the 90-day rates of periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) and composite infectious outcomes between the DEX(+) and DEX(−) cohorts.ResultsIn total, 35,222 patients were identified who underwent aseptic revision THA (n = 12,771) and TKA (n = 22,451). When accounting for surgeon clustering and comorbidities, DEX(+) demonstrated lower odds of PJI in revision THA (1.05% vs 3.42%, adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 0.72, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.53-0.978, P = .035) and revision TKA (0.74% vs 2.87%, aOR: 0.75, 95% CI: 0.56-0.99, P = .042) as well as composite infectious outcomes in the revision THA (2.60% vs 12.15%, aOR: 0.58, 95% CI: 0.48-0.71, P < .001) and revision TKA (1.66% vs 6.90%, aOR: 0.68, 95% CI: 0.56-0.83, P < .001) cohorts.ConclusionsPerioperative dexamethasone use was associated with reduced PJI and infectious risk following aseptic revision THA and TKA, supporting the routine dexamethasone administration in revision arthroplasty procedures.

Introduction

For patients undergoing total joint arthroplasty (TJA), emerging evidence has demonstrated the association between perioperative dexamethasone administration and multiple postoperative outcomes, including reduced postoperative nausea and vomiting, decreased risk of venous thromboembolism and acute kidney injury, as well as improved postoperative mobility and functional outcomes [[1], [2], [3]]. In spite of initial concerns regarding glucocorticoid-related infectious complications, recent investigations have shown that dexamethasone administration may in fact be associated with decreased rates of infection, including periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), following TJA [2,[4], [5], [6]].

Despite evidence supporting dexamethasone use in primary TJA, its role in revision TJA remains largely undescribed. As the volume of primary TJA continues to rise in tandem with an aging and increasingly comorbid population, the demand for revision TJA has substantially grown [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Revision TJA is associated with a higher rate of perioperative complications relative to primary TJA, owing to increased surgical complexity, prolonged operative times, and more traumatic tissue exposure [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. In the absence of dedicated investigations into revision TJA, findings from primary TJA cohorts are frequently extrapolated to revision populations, despite their inherently different risk profiles [2,[15], [16], [17], [18]]. As such, there is limited evidence to guide the use of dexamethasone specifically in revision TJA.

However, perioperative dexamethasone use exhibits significant heterogeneity among arthroplasty surgeons, driven largely by patient selection criteria, which obscures comparative analysis of outcomes [5]. This variability introduces substantial surgeon selection bias when comparing patients receiving dexamethasone to those who do not, as the decision to administer the drug may be influenced by patient-specific factors such as perceived risk of postoperative complications, diabetes status, or other comorbidities [15]. The selective administration of dexamethasone has been demonstrated to be largely influenced by clinical scenarios, potentially skewing estimates of outcome measures and undermining the validity of both prospective series and retrospective cohort studies, as unaccounted confounders may disproportionately affect results [17,19].

As such, the present study sought to evaluate the effect of dexamethasone administration on postoperative infectious complications following either revision total knee arthroplasty (TKA) or total hip arthroplasty (THA), while employing a model that accounts for surgeon-specific practice patterns and selection bias.

Material and methods

A retrospective cohort study was performed utilizing the Premier Healthcare Database (PHD) (Premier, Inc., North Carolina, USA), an all-payer database encompassing approximately 25% of inpatient admissions in the United States from approximately 1400 hospitals and healthcare systems [20]. The PHD includes over one billion encounters across inpatient and outpatient settings, as well as provides detailed billing and clinic information [20]. The PHD was queried to identify all adult patients (>18 years old) who underwent both-component, aseptic revision TKA and THA between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2023, using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 Procedure Coding System and Current Procedural Terminology codes. Patients undergoing single-component, or isolated liner exchange were excluded from this study as prior literature has demonstrated high levels of coding variability for these procedures [21,22]. For the final aseptic revision TKA and THA cohorts, any patients with an indication of prior prosthetic infection, identified using ICD, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes, were excluded. Administration of dexamethasone on the day of surgery was identified using PHD-specific billing codes that identify the medication, route, and dosage (Supplementary Table 1).

As all patient-level data were deidentified in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, this study was exempt from institutional review board approval.

Identification of study cohort

Patients were stratified based on type of surgery (eg, revision TKA vs THA) and then divided into high- and low-use surgeon groups based on the corresponding surgeon's rate of dexamethasone utilization in revision arthroplasty cases. Focusing on extreme clusters of dexamethasone use where exposure is driven primarily by surgeon preference rather than patient-specific factors minimizes confounding by indication and unmeasured bias that may arise from discretionary administration among moderate-use surgeons. As patient-specific decision-making and clinical judgment are not captured in administrative datasets, traditional covariate adjustments are limited by indication bias. Therefore, by restricting the analysis to surgeons with consistent prescribing behavior at the extremes of utilization (ie, almost never vs almost always administer dexamethasone), the present approach aims to reduce heterogeneity in treatment assignment and to better approximate exogenous variation in exposure. Furthermore, this approach improves the precision of effect estimates for rare outcomes by concentrating the analysis on stark contrasts in practice patterns while limiting the dilution of effects of selection bias from heterogeneous decision-making in moderate-use surgeons.

To determine the grouping of high- and low-use surgeons, dexamethasone utilization rates were calculated by identifying the percentage of revision TJA patients a surgeon administered dexamethasone on the day of surgery. Surgeons were then clustered based on their dexamethasone administration rates using k-means clustering [23,24]. Methodology used to perform k-means clustering can be found within the Appendix. Patients treated by surgeons in the highest dexamethasone use cluster (≥ 87.69% of revision THA and ≥ 89.09% of revision TKA cases) were defined as DEX(+), while patients treated by surgeons in the lowest dexamethasone use cluster (≤ 23.26% of revision THA and ≤23.26% of revision TKA cases) were defined as DEX(−).

Outcomes of interest

The primary outcome was the 90-day risk of PJI. Secondary study outcomes included 90-day rates of readmission, mortality, composite infectious complications, including PJI, sepsis, pneumonia, urinary tract infection, and “other unspecified postoperative infections,” a general “catch-all” ICD-10 code used to identify other infectious complications not captured by other disease-specific ICD-10 codes. Composite infectious complications were defined to capture both musculoskeletal and systemic infections, to determine if perioperative dexamethasone may influence susceptibility to a broader spectrum of infectious complications through its immunomodulatory effects. All outcomes were defined using ICD-10 diagnosis codes.

Statistical analysis

Patient demographics, including age, sex, and race; hospital characteristics; and medical comorbidities were collected and compared between the DEX(+) and DEX(−) cohorts. Chi-square and independent t-tests were used to assess categorical and continuous variables, respectively.

To account for potential confounders and account for the hierarchical structure of our dataset, we employed a mixed-effects logistic regression to compare outcomes across the DEX(+) and DEX(−) clusters, with cluster membership treated as a categorical fixed effect, patient-level covariates as additional fixed effects, and surgeon identification as the random intercept [[25], [26], [27]]. The mixed-effects approach models how patient outcomes vary across surgeons while accounting for both individual patient characteristics and surgeon-specific practices [28]. Model covariates were selected using clinical judgment, prioritizing established risk factors for postoperative complications in revision TJA, and statistical optimization, minimizing the Akaike Information Criterion and Bayesian Information Criterion to achieve optimal model fit and reduce overfitting. Patient covariates included patient demographics, hospital characteristics, patient comorbidities, perioperative administration of tranexamic acid, postoperative transfusion, and postoperative venous thromboembolism chemoprophylaxis agent. Model covariates are tabulated in Supplemental Table 2. Intraclass correlation coefficient values greater than 0.05 indicated non-negligible clustering and warranted the use of mixed-effects modeling over traditional logistic regression.

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata (version 18.0; StataCorp, College Station, TX). Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Results

In total, 37,222 patients who underwent both-component aseptic revision TJA and were treated by DEX(+) and DEX(−) surgeons were identified, of which 12,771 (39.7%) underwent revision THA and 22,451 (60.3%) underwent revision TKA. Importantly, patients were assigned to a group based on the prescribing pattern of their surgeon, rather than based on whether they received or did not receive dexamethasone. In the revision THA cohort, 9117 (61.72%) of patients were treated by 556 (44.80%) DEX(+) surgeons and 3654 (38.28%) were treated by 685 (55.20%) DEX(−) surgeons. For patients undergoing revision TKA, 16,711 (74.43%) patients were treated by 1001 (56.17%) DEX(+) surgeons, while 5740 (25.57%) were treated by 781 (43.83%) DEX(−) surgeons.

For revision THA, 94.34% of patients treated by DEX(+) surgeons received dexamethasone compared to 7.31% of patients treated by DEX(−) surgeons. Within the revision TKA cohort, 96.68% of patients treated by DEX(+) surgeons received dexamethasone compared to 9.49% of patients treated by DEX(−) surgeons. With regard to dosage, for patients in the revision THA cohort who received dexamethasone, the average dose was 9.97 ± 8.89 mg. For revision TKA patients, the average dose was 9.62 ± 8.38 mg.

Patient demographics and hospital characteristics

Among patients undergoing revision THA, DEX(+) patients were more likely to be younger in age (66.90 ± 11.84 years vs 67.78 ± 12.38 years, P < .001), Caucasian (86.95% vs 84.73%, P < .001), and married (55.76% vs 48.41%, P < .001). Moreover, DEX(+) revision THA patients were less likely to be treated at larger hospitals (bed size>500: 25.34% vs 44.03%, P < .001) and teaching hospitals (52.23% vs 56.27%, P < .001). For the revision TKA cohort, DEX(+) patients were more likely to be Caucasian (81.96% vs 77.47%, P < .001) and married (61.71% vs 56.45%, P < .001). In terms of hospital characteristics within the revision TKA cohort, DEX(+) patients were less likely to be treated at larger hospitals (bed size<500: 23.28% vs 33.22%, P < .001) and teaching hospitals (50.05% vs 51.74%, P < .001). Study outcomes

Patients within the DEX(+) cohort demonstrated reduced rates of PJI than patients within the DEX(−) cohort for both revision THA (1.05% vs 3.42%, adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 0.720, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.530-0.978, P = .035) and revision TKA (0.74% vs 2.87%, aOR: 0.746, 95% CI: 0.562-0.989, P = .042) (Tables 5 and 6). A similar trend was seen with respect to composite infection outcomes with DEX(+) patients having fewer complications than DEX(−) patients in both revision THA (2.60% vs 12.15%, aOR: 0.582, 95% CI: 0.475-0.713, P < .001) and revision TKA (1.66% vs 6.90%, aOR: 0.683, 95% CI: 0.563-0.830, P < .001) cohorts. Discussion

Analyzing over 35,200 patients, the present study found that treatment by high-dexamethasone-use surgeons, relative to low-use surgeons, was associated with a 0.72- and 0.75-fold decrease in adjusted odds of PJI following revision THA and TKA, respectively. Notably, we also identified a 0.58- and 0.68-fold reduction in adjusted odds of composite infectious complications in the revision THA and TKA cohorts. These findings highlight that dexamethasone may be administered without notably higher rates of infectious complications among revision TJA patients. Given the observed reduction in infectious complications, arthroplasty surgeons should consider the administration of perioperative dexamethasone in patients undergoing revision TJA, with individualized patient risk stratification remaining essential.

While previous arthroplasty literature has evaluated the safety of dexamethasone with respect to infectious complications, these analyses are predominantly derived from cohorts undergoing primary TJA [4,5,[29], [30], [31]]. However, in contrast to primary arthroplasty, revision procedures are associated with a heightened risk of perioperative morbidity, including a 2- to 5-fold increase in PJI risk [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36]]. Moreover, patients undergoing revision arthroplasty tend to be older and have a higher comorbidity burden, further compounding the risk of postoperative complications [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. Accordingly, the present study extends prior work by demonstrating that patients undergoing aseptic revision THA and TKA who are treated by surgeons that routinely administer dexamethasone were not associated with increased infectious complications. These findings provide reassurance regarding the use of perioperative dexamethasone in the revision arthroplasty setting and suggest that it may even afford a potential association with reduced PJI rates. Given its low cost and established benefits across multiple perioperative outcomes, dexamethasone may represent a cost-effective intervention among aseptic revision arthroplasty patients [3,[41], [42], [43]].

The underlying mechanism of the observed effects herein remains incompletely defined. It is plausible that the associated decrease in infectious complications with dexamethasone administration may be due to modulation of the early pro-inflammatory response that follows surgical trauma [4,44,45]. Mounting evidence has posited that in a subset of patients, the postoperative immune response to surgical trauma deviates from a self-limited, adaptive trajectory and instead transforms into a dysregulated state marked by a deleterious cascade of excessive cytokine release and impaired immune homeostasis, suggesting that there may be a responder subgroup of patients [46]. Glucocorticoids such as dexamethasone may alter this inflammatory trajectory by exerting a biphasic effect, initially “priming” the innate immune response, followed by subsequent resolution of exaggerated inflammation and restoration of immune homeostasis [[46], [47], [48], [49], [50]]. In the context of PJI risk, an excessive postoperative inflammatory milieu can impair local immune surveillance, promote tissue injury, and facilitate bacterial adhesion and colonization [4,51,52].

Studies investigating inflammatory and immune biomarkers following corticosteroid administration support this mechanism [6,47,50,[53], [54], [55]]. Among arthroplasty patients, dexamethasone has been shown to modulate postoperative inflammatory pathways [50,[53], [54], [55]]. Brekke et al. [55], in a cohort of primary TKA patients, noted reductions in C-reactive protein with dexamethasone administration. Notably, low-dose corticosteroid administration does not appear to affect neutrophil-mediated bacterial clearance [50]. Ganio et al. [50] randomized 63 patients to receive either placebo or a single preoperative dose of 125 mg methylprednisolone and found selective attenuation of adaptive immune signaling pathways, with preservation of innate immune activity (ie, neutrophil and macrophage activity) with corticosteroid administration. Accordingly, mitigation of early inflammatory cascade may create a more favorable environment for microbial clearance in the immediate postoperative period [4,[56], [57], [58], [59]].

Consistent with the hypothesized mechanism, the association between dexamethasone and reduced infectious complications was more pronounced in revision cases compared to primary procedures, as reflected by the larger observed effect size. Heckmann et al. [31] reported a 0.87- and 0.80-fold reduction in adjusted odds of PJI, respectively, with intraoperative dexamethasone use. In comparison, our findings in the revision setting demonstrated a markedly greater reduction. The observed differences in outcomes may be attributable to a heightened inflammatory burden from the increased invasiveness, surgical complexity, and surgical time inherent in revision arthroplasty surgery [[60], [61], [62], [63]]. Therefore, dexamethasone's anti-inflammatory effect may be more pronounced in the setting of the increased inflammatory environment found in revision procedures. However, further investigation is required to verify this hypothesis.

Our study, though, is not without limitation. As with any investigation relying on administrative databases, there is potential for misclassification and coding inaccuracies when utilizing ICD-10 and Current Procedural Terminology coding. Nonetheless, the large cohort size and the fact the PHD is used for insurer reimbursement, thereby incentivizing accurate coding, likely mitigates such discrepancies [[64], [65], [66]]. Additionally, the PHD is limited to inpatient hospitalization and subsequent admissions and may not fully capture outpatient encounters, limiting our ability to account for all complications. Furthermore, we did not account for the dose of dexamethasone provided to each patient in the present analysis. As we observed variability in the administered dexamethasone dose, it is plausible that there are differential risk profiles in infectious complications across dexamethasone dosing stratum that were not captured herein. Accordingly, future studies investigating the interaction between dexamethasone dose and postoperative infection risk are warranted. Finally, while defining cohorts according to surgeon prescribing patterns (ie, high- vs low-utilization surgeons) rather than individual medication administration may be viewed as a limitation, we view this approach as a strength. Grouping patients solely on dexamethasone administration introduces the potential for surgeon-selection bias because treatment decisions can be influenced by patient-specific factors that are not captured within administrative datasets. Instead, comparing outcomes among patients treated by surgeons who routinely prescribe dexamethasone with those treated by surgeons who rarely do reduces the influence of unmeasured surgeon-selection bias and obtains a more robust estimate of the association between dexamethasone and postoperative infection risk. Importantly, this approach is well-precedented in orthopaedic literature [[67], [68], [69]]. For example, Vertullo et al. [67] categorized surgeons by their patellar resurfacing preference into infrequent (<10%), selective (10%-90%), and routine (≥90%) cohorts in an analysis of 136,116 TKAs from the Australian registry. Similarly, Chan et al. [68] used surgeon-specific utilization rates to evaluate drain use in 105,116 shoulder arthroplasties. In the accompanying methodological commentary, Huang et al. [69] described surgeon preference-based grouping as superior to conventional multivariable regression when unmeasured confounders are present in administrative data. Accordingly, the use of dichotomous surgeon prescribing patterns, together with mixed-effects modeling, increased confidence that the observed differences are attributable to variation in dexamethasone utilization rather than differences in patient selection. However, we acknowledge that excluding surgeons with intermediate prescribing patterns may limit the generalizability of our findings.

Although these limitations warrant consideration, the present study also possesses several notable strengths. Foremost, to our knowledge, our study is the first to evaluate the outcomes of intraoperative dexamethasone administration in revision TJA. Additionally, the present study utilized a large cohort of 35,222 revision TJA patients through a large, national, all-payer database, offering sufficient statistical power to assess rare but clinically significant outcomes such as PJI. Given that an adequately powered prospective or institutional study for such endpoints would require thousands of patients per arm and be financially and logistically prohibitive, our design offers a pragmatic alternative to evaluate differences in postoperative infection outcomes and rare events [5]. Therefore, the most salient strength in our study lies in its methodological rigor. The approach applied offers advantages over traditional multivariable regression by comparing extreme-use cohorts to uncover specific subgroup effects, mitigating the effect of surgeon selection bias, and providing robust estimates of outcome differences in the presence or absence of dexamethasone administration.

Conclusions

For patients undergoing both-component aseptic revision TKA and THA, we observed a clear association between dexamethasone administration and a lower risk of PJI and infectious complications, demonstrating both the safety of using dexamethasone in revision arthroplasty patients and potential efficacy in preventing serious postoperative complications. Our analysis, which accounted for confounding from surgeon-specific practice patterns, provides evidence for the routine administration of dexamethasone for patients undergoing both-component aseptic revision total hip and knee arthroplasty.
