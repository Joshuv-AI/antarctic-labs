1.
Introduction

We consider the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SK) spin glass model at inverse temperature
\beta>0
and with zero external field.
For each positive integer
N
, we define the configuration space
\Sigma_{N}=\{-1,+1\}^{N}
.
For a configuration
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[N]}\in\Sigma_{N}
, the Hamiltonian of the SK model is given by

\displaystyle H_{N,\beta}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}g_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j},

where
g_{ij}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) standard Gaussian random variables.

We define the partition function, Gibbs measure, and free energy for the SK model, respectively, by

Z_{N}(\beta)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{N}}\exp({H_{N,\beta}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})})
,

G_{N,\beta}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\exp(H_{N,\beta}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))/Z_{N}(\beta)
, and

F_{N}(\beta)=\ln Z_{N}(\beta)
.

The Sherrington-Kirkpatrick spin glass model has been a subject of intense study since its introduction, with the primary goal of characterizing its limiting free energy. A landmark achievement in this field was the rigorous proof of the Parisi formula, which yields the free-energy limit at any temperature. This was first established by Talagrand
[
8
]
for the standard SK model and later extended to mixed
p
-spin models by Panchenko
[
7
]
.

While the Parisi formula describes the first-order limit of the free energy, the study of its fluctuations focuses on the second-order behavior. In the high-temperature regime
\beta<1
, the first rigorous proof of the central limit theorem (CLT) for the free energy was established by Aizenman, Lebowitz, and Ruelle
[
1
]
, who showed that for the entire regime
\beta<1
, the fluctuations of the free energy are Gaussian with
O(1)
variance. This foundational result was later re-established by Comets and Neveu
[
5
]
using an alternative martingale-based approach. In contrast, the low-temperature regime
\beta>1
exhibits much larger fluctuations. Chatterjee
[
3
]
provided a general upper bound in this region, proving that the variance of the free energy is at most of the order
N/\ln N
.

As the temperature approaches the critical value
\beta_{c}=1
, the fluctuations of the free energy are predicted to diverge. In the physics literature, it is widely believed that at
\beta=1
, the variance behaves as
\frac{1}{6}\ln N+O(1)
. However, establishing this scaling rigorously remains a formidable challenge. A significant step forward was made by Chen and Lam
[
4
]
, who established that the variance at the critical temperature is at most of the order
(\ln N)^{2}
.

For the
spherical
SK model, which is often more tractable due to its connections with random matrix theory, Baik and Lee
[
2
]
provided a complete characterization of the free energy fluctuations, identifying a transition to the Tracy–Widom distribution at low temperatures. Recently, Landon
[
6
]
characterized the near-critical fluctuations for the spherical model within a critical window of size
\sqrt{\ln N}\cdot N^{-1/3}
.

A central quantity in spin glass theory is the overlap between two configurations
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}
chosen independently from the Gibbs measure
G_{N,\beta}
, defined as

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\leqslant N}\sigma_{i}^{1}\sigma_{i}^{2}

In the standard SK model with Ising spins, the analysis is significantly more involved due to the discrete nature of the configuration space
\Sigma_{N}=\{-1,1\}^{N}
. The control of fluctuations in this case relies heavily on the concentration of the overlap
R(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2})
, for which Talagrand
[
10
]
established crucial bounds that become increasingly difficult to control as
\beta\uparrow 1
. Our work builds upon the variance representation via Gaussian interpolation introduced by Chatterjee
[
3
]
and employs Stein’s method to provide a refined CLT that remains valid as
\beta_{N}
approaches
1
at the scaling rate
N^{-1/3}
.

1.1.
Main Results

Our main objective is to characterize the fluctuation of the free energy near, but above the critical temperature
\beta_{c}=1
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that the inverse temperature sequence
(\beta_{N})_{N\geqslant 1}
satisfies
\lim_{N\to\infty}\beta_{N}=1
and

(1)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{1/3}(1-\beta_{N}^{2})=c\in(0,\infty)\cup\{\infty\}.

Define the function

\displaystyle\nu(\beta):=-\frac{1}{2}\ln(1-\beta^{2})-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}

for
\beta<1
.
Then, the variance satisfies

(2)

\displaystyle\left|\var(F_{N}(\beta_{N}))-\nu(\beta_{N})\right|\leqslant L\left(N^{1/3}(1-\beta_{N}^{2})\right)^{-3/2}.

Moreover, we have the following distributional convergence for the free energy

(3)

\displaystyle\dtv\left(\frac{F_{N}(\beta_{N})-\E F_{N}(\beta_{N})}{\sqrt{\nu(\beta_{N})}},g\right)\leqslant\frac{L}{\sqrt{\nu(\beta_{N})}}\left(N^{1/3}(1-\beta_{N}^{2})\right)^{-3/4}.

Here,
L
is a finite constant depending on the sequence
(\beta_{N})_{N\geqslant 1}
,
g\sim\N(0,1)
and
\dtv
is the total variation distance.

Corollary 1.2
.

In particular, when
\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{1/3}(1-\beta_{N}^{2})=c\in(0,\infty)
, we have
\var(F_{N}(\beta_{N}))=\frac{1}{6}\log N+\mathcal{O}(1)
as
N\to\infty
.

Remark 1
.

In the physics literature, it is conjectured that the variance of the free energy at the critical temperature
\beta_{c}=1
satisfies

\displaystyle\var(F_{N}(\beta_{c}))=\frac{1}{6}\ln N+\mathcal{O}(1).

To extend the methods developed in this paper to the critical case, one would require precise estimates of the overlap moments at
\beta_{c}
, similar to Lemma
1.4
. For instance, it is conjectured in
[
10
, Conjecture 11.7.5]
that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{2/3}\langle R^{2}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2})\rangle=a

for some constant
a>0
. Alternatively, one could attempt to compare the free energy at the scaling
\beta_{N}=1-cN^{-1/3}
with that at
\beta_{c}=1
using a martingale approach. Define the process

\displaystyle M_{N}(t):=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{N}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}B_{ij}(t)\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-\frac{(N-1)t}{4}\right)

where
((B_{ij}(t)))_{i<j}
are independent standard Brownian motions. By Itô’s formula, we have

\displaystyle\ln M_{N}(\beta_{c}^{2})-\ln M_{N}(\beta_{N}^{2})=\int_{\beta_{N}^{2}}^{\beta_{c}^{2}}\frac{1}{M_{N}(t)}dM_{N}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\beta_{N}^{2}}^{\beta_{c}^{2}}\frac{1}{M_{N}^{2}(t)}d\langle M_{N}\rangle_{t}.

If one can show that the term

\displaystyle\E\left[\int_{\beta_{N}^{2}}^{\beta_{c}^{2}}\frac{1}{M_{N}^{2}(t)}d\langle M_{N}\rangle_{t}\right]=\int_{\beta_{N}}^{\beta_{c}}\beta(N\E\langle R^{2}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2})\rangle-1)d\beta

is uniformly bounded in
N
, a CLT at the critical temperature could potentially be established. The aforementioned conjecture on the overlap scaling would provide a path to such a proof.

Remark 2
.

Our proof approach remains valid in both the high-temperature regime and the near-critical window. Specifically, we extend the classical results of
[
1
]
and
[
5
]
to sequences of inverse temperatures
(\beta_{N})
that approach the critical value
\beta_{c}=1
at the rate of
N^{-1/3}
.
By employing a sharper analysis, the variance bound can be improved further. Specifically, for any
\epsilon>0
, it can be shown that

\displaystyle\left|\var(F_{N}(\beta_{N}))-\nu(\beta_{N})\right|\leqslant L\left(N^{1/3}(1-\beta_{N}^{2})\right)^{-2+\epsilon}.

For any real-valued function
f
on
\Sigma_{N}^{n}
, we define its Gibbs average under the Gibbs measure
G_{N,\beta}
as

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{n}\in\Sigma_{N}}f(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{n})G_{N,\beta}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1})\cdots G_{N,\beta}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{n}).

The main technical tools used in our proof are the Gaussian interpolation method and Stein’s method for normal approximation.
In the following two sections, we briefly discuss these now-classical tools. Throughout the rest of the article, we write
H_{N}
,
Z_{N},G_{N}
, and
F_{N}
instead of
H_{N,\beta}
,
Z_{N}(\beta),G_{N,\beta}
, and
F_{N}(\beta)
, unless we need to explicitly indicate the dependence on
\beta
. Without loss of generality, we will also assume that
N^{1/3}(1-\beta_{N}^{2})\geqslant\varepsilon_{0}
for all
N\geqslant 1
and some
\varepsilon_{0}>0
.

1.2.
Coupled Hamiltonians and variance representation

To analyze the fluctuations, we employ a Gaussian interpolation method.
Let
y
be a centered Gaussian vector in
\mathds{R}^{n}
with covariance matrix
C=((C_{j,j^{\prime}}))_{1\leqslant j,j^{\prime}\leqslant n}
. Consider two independent copies
y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime}
of
y
. For
t\in[0,1]
, we define the interpolated vectors

\displaystyle y_{1}(t)=\sqrt{t}y+\sqrt{1-t}y^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{2}(t)=\sqrt{t}y+\sqrt{1-t}y^{\prime\prime}.

Let
A,B:\mathds{R}^{n}\to\mathds{R}
be absolutely continuous functions such that

\displaystyle\E\|\nabla A(y)\|_{2}^{2}<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\E\|\nabla B(y)\|_{2}^{2}<\infty.

Using Gaussian integration by parts, the derivative of the coupled expectation can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\E[A(y_{1}(t))B(y_{2}(t))]=\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{n}C_{j,j^{\prime}}\E[\partial_{j}A(y_{1}(t))\partial_{j^{\prime}}B(y_{2}(t))].

Applying the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and noting that at
t=1
the copies coincide (both equal to
y
) while at
t=0
they are independent (namely
y^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime\prime}
), we derive the covariance formula

(4)

\displaystyle\E[A(y)B(y)]-\E A(y)\cdot\E B(y)=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{n}C_{j,j^{\prime}}\E[\partial_{j}A(y_{1}(t))\partial_{j^{\prime}}B(y_{2}(t))]\,dt.

Specifically, when
A=B
, the matrix
C
is positive semi-definite, so the integrand on the right-hand side of (
4
) is non-negative. This shows the monotonicity of correlations along the interpolation.

We apply this to the SK model. Define two independent copies of the Hamiltonian
H_{N}^{1},H_{N}^{2}
using independent disorder
g^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime}
. For
t\in[0,1]
, define the interpolated Hamiltonians

\displaystyle H_{N,t}^{1}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{t}\cdot H_{N}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+\sqrt{1-t}\cdot H_{N}^{1}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\text{ and }H_{N,t}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau}):=\sqrt{t}\cdot H_{N}(\boldsymbol{\tau})+\sqrt{1-t}\cdot H_{N}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau})

for
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{N}
.
Let
\langle\cdot\rangle_{t}
denote the Gibbs average with respect to the product measure
\tilde{G}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})\propto\exp(H_{N,t}^{1}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+H_{N,t}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau}))
.
Define the function

\displaystyle\xi(x):=\xi_{N}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}\cdot\left(x^{2}-1/N\right).

A direct application of (
4
) to the function
A(g)=B(g)=F_{N}(\beta)
yields the following variance representation.

Lemma 1.3

(
[
3
, Theorem 3.8]
)
.

The variance of the free energy is given by

\displaystyle\var(F_{N}(\beta))=N\int_{0}^{1}\E\,\langle\xi(R(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}))\rangle_{t}\,dt.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
reduces to establishing the concentration of
N\langle R(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})^{2}\rangle_{t}
near
1/(1-\beta_{N}^{2}t)
and will be proved in Proposition
1.6
. The following lemma will play an important role in the proof, controlling the overlap near the critical temperature.

Lemma 1.4
.

(See
[
10
, Theorems 11.7.1 and 11.7.2]
.)

Suppose that (
1
) holds.
Then, for each
k\geqslant 1
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{N}\E\langle(N(1-\beta_{N}^{2})R(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2})^{2})^{k}\rangle<\infty.

However, Lemma
1.4
cannot be directly used to control moments of
N(1-\beta_{N}^{2}t)\langle R(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})^{2}\rangle_{t},t\in(0,1)
for the coupled Hamiltonian. We overcome this issue by a careful comparison between the moments under
\langle\cdot\rangle_{t}
and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{1}
, as presented in equation (
15
) and (
21
).

Next, we discuss the version of Stein’s method for normal approximation that will be useful for us.

1.3.
Stein’s method for normal approximation

To prove the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), we use Stein’s method.
Recall that the total variation distance between two random variables
X
and
Y
is given by

\displaystyle\dtv(X,Y):=\sup_{A}\left|\pr(X\in A)-\pr(Y\in A)\right|.

Stein’s method for normal approximation states that the total variation distance between a random variable
W
and a standard Gaussian random variable
g
is bounded by the Stein discrepancy

(5)

\displaystyle\dtv(W,g)\leqslant\sup\left\{\left|\E(W\psi(W)-\psi^{\prime}(W))\right|:\left\|\psi^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2\right\}.

Let us define

\displaystyle W_{N}:=\frac{F_{N}-\E F_{N}}{\sqrt{\nu_{N}}},

where
\nu_{N}:=\nu(\beta_{N})
.
Using the Gaussian integration by parts formula (
4
) with
A=W_{N}
and
B=\psi(W_{N})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\E[W_{N}\psi(W_{N})]=\frac{N}{\nu_{N}}\int_{0}^{1}\E\left[\psi^{\prime}(W_{N,t}^{1})\,\langle\xi(R(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}))\rangle_{t}\right]dt,

where
W^{1}_{N,t}
is defined by replacing
H_{N}
in
W_{N}
with
H^{1}_{N,t}
.
By evaluating the integral

\nu_{N}=\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1/(1-\beta_{N}^{2}t)-1)dt

and noting that
W_{N,t}^{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}W_{N}
, we obtain the following bound

\displaystyle\left|\E[W_{N}\psi(W_{N})]-\E\psi^{\prime}(W_{N})\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{2\nu_{N}}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\E\left[\psi^{\prime}(W^{1}_{N,t})\left(N\langle R(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})^{2}\rangle_{t}-\frac{1}{1-\beta_{N}^{2}t}\right)\right]dt\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}\left\|\psi^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}}{2\nu_{N}}\int_{0}^{1}\E\left|N\langle R(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})^{2}\rangle_{t}-\frac{1}{1-\beta_{N}^{2}t}\right|dt.

In particular, we have

(6)

\displaystyle\dtv(W_{N},g)\leqslant\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{\nu_{N}}\int_{0}^{1}\E\left|N\langle R(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})^{2}\rangle_{t}-\frac{1}{1-\beta_{N}^{2}t}\right|dt.

Finally, establishing the concentration of
N\langle R(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})^{2}\rangle_{t}
near
1/(1-\beta_{N}^{2}t)
completes the proof of the CLT. The concentration result is presented in Proposition
1.6
.

1.4.
Cavity method

To estimate the overlap moments, we employ the cavity method to decouple the
N
th spin.
We define the cavity Hamiltonian on
(N-1)
spins as

\displaystyle H_{N}^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}):=\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{1\leqslant i<j<N}g_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j},

and define
H_{N}^{1,-}
and
H_{N}^{2,-}
analogously by replacing
g_{ij}
with
g_{ij}^{\prime}
and
g_{ij}^{\prime\prime}
respectively.
We introduce a second interpolation parameter
s\in[0,1]
that scales the interaction between the first
(N-1)
spins and the
N
th spin. For
\boldsymbol{\rho}=(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in\Sigma_{N}^{2}
, define

\displaystyle H_{N,t,s}^{1}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}):=\sqrt{t}H_{N}^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+\sqrt{1-t}H_{N}^{1,-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+\sqrt{s}\cdot\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}}\sigma_{N}\sum_{i<N}(\sqrt{t}g_{iN}+\sqrt{1-t}g_{iN}^{\prime})\sigma_{i},

\displaystyle H_{N,t,s}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau}):=\sqrt{t}H_{N}^{-}(\boldsymbol{\tau})+\sqrt{1-t}H_{N}^{2,-}(\boldsymbol{\tau})+\sqrt{s}\cdot\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}}\tau_{N}\sum_{i<N}(\sqrt{t}g_{iN}+\sqrt{1-t}g_{iN}^{\prime\prime})\tau_{i}.

Let
\langle\cdot\rangle_{t,s}
denote the Gibbs average with respect to the product measure

\tilde{G}_{t,s}(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})\propto\exp(H_{N,t,s}^{1}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+H_{N,t,s}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau})).

By construction, the parameter
s=1
recovers the full
N
-spin system, while
s=0
decouples the
N
-th spin. This identification yields the relations

\langle\cdot\rangle_{t,1}=\langle\cdot\rangle_{t}
and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{1,1}=\langle\cdot\rangle_{1}=\langle\cdot\rangle.

For
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{N}
, define the overlap of the first
(N-1)
spins as

\displaystyle R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\leqslant N-1}\sigma_{i}^{1}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

For
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{l}=(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{l},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{l}),\boldsymbol{\rho}^{l^{\prime}}=(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{l^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{l^{\prime}})\in\Sigma_{N}^{2}
, we denote the covariance of the
s
-dependent part of the Hamiltonian
H_{N,t,s}^{1}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+H_{N,t,s}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
. Explicitly, this covariance is defined as

\displaystyle U(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{l},\boldsymbol{\rho}^{l^{\prime}})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\E\left[\left(\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}}\sigma_{N}^{l}\sum_{i<N}(\sqrt{t}g_{iN}+\sqrt{1-t}g_{iN}^{\prime})\sigma_{i}^{l}+\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}}\tau_{N}^{l}\sum_{i<N}(\sqrt{t}g_{iN}+\sqrt{1-t}g_{iN}^{\prime\prime})\tau_{i}^{l}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left.\left(\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}}\sigma_{N}^{l^{\prime}}\sum_{i<N}(\sqrt{t}g_{iN}+\sqrt{1-t}g_{iN}^{\prime})\sigma_{i}^{l^{\prime}}+\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}}\tau_{N}^{l^{\prime}}\sum_{i<N}(\sqrt{t}g_{iN}+\sqrt{1-t}g_{iN}^{\prime\prime})\tau_{i}^{l^{\prime}}\right)\right].

A straightforward computation shows that this expression reduces to

\displaystyle U(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{l},\boldsymbol{\rho}^{l^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(\sigma_{N}^{l}\sigma_{N}^{l^{\prime}}R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{l},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{l^{\prime}})+\tau_{N}^{l}\tau_{N}^{l^{\prime}}R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{l},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{l^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\beta^{2}t}{2}(\sigma_{N}^{l}\tau_{N}^{l^{\prime}}R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{l},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{l^{\prime}})+\tau_{N}^{l}\sigma_{N}^{l^{\prime}}R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{l},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{l^{\prime}})).

Using Gaussian integration by parts with respect to the coupling parameter
s
, we obtain the following useful derivative formula.

Lemma 1.5

(
[
9
, Eq. (1.90)]
)
.

For a function
f
on
(\Sigma_{N}^{2})^{n}
and
t,s\in[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\E\langle f\rangle_{t,s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}\leqslant n}\E\langle U(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{l},\boldsymbol{\rho}^{l^{\prime}})f\rangle_{t,s}-2n\sum_{l\leqslant n}\E\langle U(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{l},\boldsymbol{\rho}^{n+1})f\rangle_{t,s}

\displaystyle\quad-n\E\langle U(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\rho}^{n+1})f\rangle_{t,s}+n(n+1)\E\langle U(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\rho}^{n+2})f\rangle_{t,s}.

We proceed by Taylor expansion in
s
around
s=0
. For
\boldsymbol{\rho}=(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in\Sigma_{N}^{2}
, consider the function

f_{1}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\sigma_{N}\tau_{N}R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})
.
Since
\sigma_{N}
and
\tau_{N}
are independent and uniformly distributed on
\{-1,1\}
under the expectation
\E\langle\cdot\rangle_{t,0}
, all odd moments vanish by symmetry. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\E\langle f_{1}\rangle_{t,0}=0.

Calculating derivatives using Lemma
1.5
, we find

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\E\langle f_{1}\rangle_{t,s}\right|_{s=0}

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}t\E\langle R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})^{2}\rangle_{t,0}\text{ and }

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}\E\langle f_{1}\rangle_{t,s}\right|_{s=0}

\displaystyle=-4\beta^{4}t\E\langle R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{1})R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{1})R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2})\rangle_{t,0}.

Using Taylor’s theorem, we derive the identity

\displaystyle\E\langle R(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})^{2}\rangle_{t,1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}+\E\langle f_{1}\rangle_{t,1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}+\beta^{2}t\E\langle R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})^{2}\rangle_{t,0}-2\beta^{4}t\E\langle R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{1})R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{1})R^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2})\rangle_{t,0}

(7)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{6}\cdot\left.\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial s^{3}}\E\langle f_{1}\rangle_{t,s}\right|_{s=c}

for some
c\in[0,1]
.
The precise control of the error terms yields the following proposition, which is the technical core of this paper.

Proposition 1.6
.

Under condition (
1
), for all
t\in[0,1]
and
N\geqslant 1
, we have

(8)

\displaystyle\textsc{I. }\left|N\E\langle R(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})^{2}\rangle_{t}-\frac{1}{1-\beta_{N}^{2}t}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant L\cdot N^{-1/2}(1-\beta_{N}^{2})^{-1}(1-\beta_{N}^{2}t)^{-3/2},

\displaystyle\textsc{II. }\E\left|N\langle R(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})^{2}\rangle_{t}-\frac{1}{1-\beta_{N}^{2}t}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant L\cdot N^{-3/4}(1-\beta_{N}^{2})^{-53/24}(1-\beta_{N}^{2}t)^{-49/24}

(9)

\displaystyle\quad+L\cdot N^{-1/2}(1-\beta_{N}^{2})^{-4/3}(1-\beta_{N}^{2}t)^{-13/6}

where
L
is a constant depending on
(\beta_{N})_{N\geqslant 1}
.

2.
Proofs

In this section,
K
denotes a universal constant, and
L
denotes a constant depending on the sequence
(\beta_{N})_{N\geqslant 1}
, in particular it will depend on
\varepsilon_{0}:=\inf_{N}N^{1/3}(1-\beta_{N}^{2})>0
. The values of these constants may change from line to line. We prove Theorem
1.1
assuming the validity of Proposition
1.6
.

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Invoking the variance representation in Lemma
1.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\var(F_{N}(\beta))

\displaystyle=N\int_{0}^{1}\E\,\langle\xi(R(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}))\rangle_{t}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{2}N}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\E\,\langle R(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})^{2}\rangle_{t}\,dt-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}.

By integrating the estimate (
8

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant K(\E\langle R(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2})^{12}\rangle)^{1/2}.

In the third inequality, we used Lemma

.
Taylor’s theorem completes the proof.

\blacksquare
