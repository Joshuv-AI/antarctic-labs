Implementing a checklist to strengthen IMCI-based assessment of childhood illnesses in an urban primary care setting in Delhi: a quality improvement initiative
Authors: Shahana Singh, Adhish Kumar Sethi, Riya Biswas, Parshav Gilhotra, Pranav Sankar, Roshan Mariam Manu, Sandeep Das, Sumna Velarambath Manalil, Baridalyne Nongkynrih, Ravneet Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren presenting to the outpatient department (OPD) with common complaints may have other underlying conditions. The Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) provides a comprehensive assessment for this. This study aimed to strengthen IMCI-based assessment by using a checklist by doctors among children aged 2 months to 5 years attending an urban primary health centre in Delhi through a quality improvement initiative.MethodsA baseline assessment with process mapping and root cause analysis was conducted to identify gaps in the existing system. Age-specific child health assessment checklists were introduced to standardise evaluation of danger signs, common conditions and malnutrition. This was implemented through two 6-week Plan–Do–Study–Act (PDSA) cycles. In addition, a dedicated paediatric OPD room, improved anthropometry and availability of essential supplies were established.ResultsAfter the first PDSA cycle, complete assessment improved to 18.3%, and danger sign evaluation increased to 88.3%. By the second cycle, complete assessment reached 50%; danger sign assessment rose to 97.2%. Screening for cough improved from 86.8% to 100%, fever from 64.2% to 91.7%, diarrhoea from 13.2% to 94.5% and ear problems from 3.8% to 88.9%. Assessment for malnutrition increased from 23.1% to 94.4%, haemoglobin testing from 25% to 69.4%, and documentation of immunisation from 1.9% to 83.3%. However, there were concerns regarding sustainability, such as additional time required for consultation and irregular supplies.ConclusionThe intervention improved IMCI-based assessment, highlighting the value of simple, context-appropriate strategies while underscoring the requirement for continued implementation in resource-constrained primary care settings.Keywords: Child, Therapeutics, Low and Middle Income Countries, Health Policy

Introduction

Children under 5 years of age are vulnerable to a spectrum of illnesses, with acute respiratory illness and diarrhoea being leading causes of morbidity and frequently the primary reason for visits to outpatient care.1 Children brought to healthcare settings may be suffering from more than one health condition requiring careful diagnosis with a comprehensive management plan titrated to the needs of the individual child.2 Malnutrition often complicates diseases, with 19% of children in India suffering from wasting and 32% underweight. In addition, 67% of children of this age group are anaemic.1

The Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) guidelines by the WHO provide a template for comprehensive assessment and management of common illness in children under 5 years of age. These guidelines are used in resource-limited primary care settings, which is often the first point of contact for children.3 Implementation of this strategy has been shown to improve quality of care4–7 and prevent childhood mortality.8 9

However, despite established guidelines, there are shortcomings in the care being provided due to resource constraints, knowledge gaps among healthcare personnel and ineffective communication.10 In addition, although the IMCI guidelines are developed for use by doctors, nurses and field workers,2 most of the literature around implementation of this revolves around health workers.9 11–13

Primary healthcare services should ensure that all children coming in contact with the health system are assessed comprehensively, including assessment for danger signs, pneumonia, diarrhoea, fever, ear discharge or rash. It is also important to ensure that no child with anaemia, malnutrition or incomplete immunisation presenting to the outpatient department (OPD) in primary care settings is missed out.

Quality improvement (QI) initiative involves ongoing scrutiny for gaps between expected and actual healthcare processes, and making efforts to eliminate such gaps.14 Such initiatives for standardising clinical processes have been found to reduce disparities in provider practice and health outcomes in paediatrics.15 Hence, using the principles of QI, this study was planned to improve diagnosis of common health conditions as per IMCI guidelines for children under 5 years of age presenting to the OPD of an urban primary health centre in Delhi, over a 6-week time interval.

Methods

Context

This initiative was undertaken at an urban health centre (UHC) in Dakshinpuri extension, a resettlement colony in South-East Delhi. The centre, catering to a population of about 38 000, provides general outpatient services on 5 days a week between 10:00 and 12:30, in addition to biweekly antenatal and immunisation services. The average monthly outpatient attendance is 3000, of which 200 are children under 5 years of age. The primary health centre team consists of doctors, public health nurses, medical social service officers, pharmacists, laboratory technicians and health staff.

The medical doctors were pursuing postgraduation in Community Medicine. They provided care under the supervision of senior residents and faculty. Since these doctors were trained in different medical institutes of India during their undergraduate training, their baseline knowledge varied. Refresher training was provided to ensure that they all had a similar level of knowledge and skills required to manage childhood illnesses at the centre. The unique operational feature of the UHC is the rotational posting of resident doctors, with postings ranging from 6 months to 1.5 years. In addition, a new batch of residents join every 6 months, leading to a periodic turnover. However, they are supervised by permanent faculty and follow standard protocols for OPD management to ensure uniformity in management. At a time, six doctors attend to outpatient services. Each consultation lasts for about 6–8 min. All other health personnel were permanent employees working at the UHC. The healthcare providers were regular staff of the same institute as authors. The nearest secondary level hospital is located approximately 1 km away, while the closest tertiary care hospital is about 10 km from the colony, which serves as referral centres for the UHC.

Baseline assessment and identification of gaps

Doctors, public health nurses, medical social workers and health educators constituted the QI team and were involved in mapping current processes, identifying key gaps and brainstorming change ideas. A process flow chart was drawn by observation of the current processes from registration to dispensing of medication to children availing OPD services (figure 2).

Process flow diagram for care of under 5 children presenting to UHC OPD. OPD, outpatient department; UHC, urban health centre.

A baseline assessment was conducted over a 2-week period between 26 December 2022 and 7 January 2023. An audit of the OPD cards was done to determine the proportion of new patients under 5 years of age being assessed and managed as per WHO IMCI protocol. A ‘new patient’ was defined as a patient who had not visited the OPD in the prior 1 week. In addition, gaps were identified by brainstorming with the QI team. A root cause analysis of the identified problems was done (figure 3). Change was planned keeping these issues in mind and implemented in Plan–Do–Study–Act (PDSA) cycles.

Intervention

Gaps identified were prioritised and simple solutions were identified for standardising diagnosis for common childhood conditions. At the baseline, four cabins in the UHC functioned as a general clinic, attending to patient of all age groups (children, adults, geriatrics). As a part of the intervention, one cabin was allocated exclusively for paediatrics OPD and breastfeeding room. QI initiative was also undertaken to improve anthropometry of children, details of which are published elsewhere.16 Thermometers and essential drugs which were out of stock were indented.

PDSA cycle 1: development of checklist for assessment of under 5 children

The first PDSA cycle was conducted over 6 weeks (8 January to 20 February 2023). A new child health assessment checklist based on WHO-IMCI was developed separately for children under 2 months of age and for 2 months to 5 years of age (online supplemental material). The checklist was developed by QI team members in consultation with faculty from Community Medicine, by adapting standard WHO IMCI algorithms into a concise two-sided OPD card suitable for routine outpatient use by doctors at UHC. The checklist was reviewed for content and comprehensiveness by faculty experts before implementation.

All children presenting to the OPD were assessed using the checklist developed to ensure complete assessment. If a child presented again within 7 days, follow-up was done in routine blank OPD cards. Beyond 7 days, repeat assessment using the comprehensive checklist was done. This ensured that danger signs and symptoms of common conditions, that is, pneumonia, diarrhoea, fever, rash, ear problems, malnutrition, anaemia, jaundice, were asked irrespective of the primary complaint. Any child with severe disease was to be referred. Immunisation, deworming and vitamin A supplementation status was also assessed for all children. Only after complete assessment, diagnosis was made and a treatment, referral and follow-up plan was formulated. After consultation, medications were dispensed from the pharmacy. All the parameters measured during baseline were reassessed after a period of 2 weeks.

Clinical consultation was conducted by 8–10 resident doctors posted at the UHC during the study period. Baseline and endline assessment of the QI intervention was conducted by a team of five doctors on a rotational basis on days when they were not involved in routine clinical work. Data was collected using a standard assessment form.

PDSA cycle 2

The sustainability of the first PDSA cycle was checked in September 2024 to evaluate whether the checklist use has been integrated into routine clinical practice. The primary implementation team had been shifted out of the centre in March 2023 and was not involved in day-to-day activities of UHC. The checklist remained available at the centre. When the original implementation team was reposted to the UHC, two major barriers affecting checklist utilisation were identified. The first was irregular printing of the assessment form. Another was the lack of regular refresher training for doctors, particularly in the context of frequent rotational postings. Inadequate assessment for anaemia was also identified as part of the first PDSA and the main reason was mothers refusing invasive haemoglobin testing for their children.

Thus, a second PDSA cycle was planned and corrective measures were taken based on these gaps. The assessment form was formatted and distributed as the main OPD card for all children of the age group under study. An image of an infant/child was added to it for easy identification and distribution of OPD cards to the respective age groups by the registration staff. Regular printing of OPD cards was ensured. It was decided that refresher training would be conducted every 6 months for the doctors on IMCI. These were to be conducted by senior residents under the supervision of faculty in-charge of postgraduate teaching of Community Medicine, in a single day session lasting 2 hours. The training content included discussion of standardised assessment and management of childhood illnesses, along with practice scenarios based on WHO-IMCI modules. Besides this training, regular case-based discussions were held on a weekly basis. These changes were implemented and re-assessment was done after 2 weeks of implementation.

The team held informal discussions with doctors after completion of the second PDSA cycle. In these discussions, they talked of the latest findings, their experiences in implementing this initiative, and the prospects of its further continuation.

Measures and statistical analysis

Data were collected using Epicollect V.5 application and analysed using Stata V.15. Categorical data from baseline and endline assessment were presented as frequency and proportion. The primary outcome was the proportion of children with complete assessment in all IMCI domains. Secondary outcomes were domain-wise completeness of assessment. We initially performed analysis considering haemoglobin estimation as a mandatory part of assessment. However, in clinical practice, haemoglobin estimation adds to a useful assessment only for those patients who have not had their haemoglobin checked in the past 2 months. Hence, we also did an analysis of complete assessment of under five children with all components except haemoglobin estimation.

Results

The QI team discussed the issues concerning the assessment and management of common conditions in children under 5 years of age. The key issues identified were inadequate knowledge of assessment domains under IMCI, lack of a standard checklist for diagnosis, lack of routine assessment for malnutrition and anaemia, and lack of adequate follow-up (figure 3).

On baseline assessment, it was found that complete assessment based on IMCI guidelines was not done for any of the children. In both baseline and endline assessment, there were less than 10 children aged <2 months. Hence, the results are limited to findings for children aged between 2 months and 5 years.

After the implementation of the checklist, complete assessment of under 5 children improved to 18.3% at the end of first PDSA cycle and to 50% at the end of the second PDSA cycle (table 1). The parameter that was commonly missed was identified to be testing of haemoglobin for anaemia. On excluding this parameter, it was found that 77.8% of the children were assessed completely. Assessment of general danger signs by history inability to feed, repeated vomiting, convulsions, lethargy or unconsciousness, improved to 88.3% in the first PDSA cycle and to 97.2% in the second PDSA cycle. Assessment of children from malnutrition improved from 23.1% at baseline to only 41.7% during the first PDSA cycle. However, with improvement in anthropometry (study published elsewhere), assessment of malnutrition in the second PDSA cycle was already high at baseline (84.8%) which increased to 94.4%. Haemoglobin testing for children increased from 25% at baseline to 48.3% in the first PDSA cycle and to 69.4% in the second PDSA cycle. Immunisation status was recorded for only 1.9% at baseline which improved to 83.3% at the end of second cycle.

Assessment of child with cough/breathing difficulty

The assessment of a child for cough showed a minor improvement from 86.8% at baseline to 90%% at endline of first PDSA cycle and 51.5% to 100% during the second PDSA cycle. It was noted that most children present with this complaint which is reported upfront by their caregiver. A child presenting with a history of cough/breathing difficulty should be assessed for pneumonia by screening for fast breathing and chest indrawing, as well as for danger signs requiring referral to specialist. At the baseline stage (n=53), 45 children presented to the OPD with cough or breathing difficulty, but only 3 (6.7%) of them were assessed for pneumonia or danger signs of pneumonia. During the first PDSA cycle (n=60), 37 children presented with these symptoms, and the proportion assessed for pneumonia or danger signs improved to 23 (61.1%). By the second PDSA cycle (n=36), 21 children presented with cough or breathing difficulty, with the assessment rate increasing to 20 children (95.2%).

Assessment of child with diarrhoea

Assessment of diarrhoea improved from 13.2% at baseline to 76.7% during the first PDSA cycle and from 9.1% to 94.5% in the second PDSA cycle. When evaluating a child with diarrhoea, it is essential to check for signs of dehydration, such as lethargy/irritability, sunken eyes, thirst and skin pinch, as well as blood in stools. In this study, diarrhoea was the presenting complaint in only 1.9% at baseline, while 11.7% in the first PDSA and 2.8% in the second PDSA were found to have diarrhoea. For these children, assessment for dehydration was done both at baseline and endline. However, assessment for blood in stools was noted after the introduction of checklist.

Assessment of child with fever

Assessment for fever improved from 64.2% at baseline to 88.3% at the end of first PDSA and from 60.6% to 91.7% in the second. In a child with fever, it is essential to look for conditions which warrant antibiotics or referral, such as meningitis, urinary tract infection (UTI), septic arthritis, abscess, measles or malaria. For this, it is crucial to actively look for signs such as duration of fever >7 days, purpura/petechiae, skin swelling, bulging fontanelle, neck stiffness, jaundice, refusal to move limb, difficulty swallowing, rash, suprapubic pain, crying or irritability on passing urine or incontinence. At baseline (n=53), 18 children presented with fever, yet none (0.0%) were assessed for relevant complications. In the first PDSA cycle (n=60), 17 children presented with fever, and the assessment rate rose sharply to 14 (82.4%). During the second PDSA cycle (n=36), the number of children presenting with fever was 22, and the proportion assessed for all conditions warranting antibiotics or referral further improved to 20 (90.9%).

Assessment of child with ear problem

Assessment of ear problem improved from 3.8% at baseline to 63.3% in the first PDSA cycle and from 3% to 88.9% in the second. Ear pain or discharge was an uncommon condition with only 1.9% of baseline cases, 1.7% of cases during first PDSA and none from the second PDSA presenting with these complaints. The presence of tender swelling behind the ear suggesting mastoiditis was ruled out in the child presenting at baseline as well as for the one presenting during intervention.

After the first PDSA cycle was conducted in February 2023, the team reassessed the situation in September 2024. Although the checklist was being regularly used at the centre, however, this use was not documented formally during the period. After the second PDSA cycle, informal discussions were held with resident doctors regarding their viewpoints of checklist-based intervention for paediatric outpatient management. Doctors raised concerns that excessive reliance on checklist-based assessment of children in the outpatient care undermined clinical acumen of a treating doctor. However, this was the perception of newly joined doctors. The same was allayed during the training, and utility of checklist was emphasised. Some doctors felt that using an exhaustive checklist at every follow-up visit increased consultation time in a high patient load setting. It was suggested that the checklist could be completed by other trained healthcare staff, reducing the time constraints faced by doctors.

Discussion

This study highlights the outcomes of implementing a quality initiative to improve the diagnosis of common health conditions in children under 5 years at UHCs, in line with IMCI guidelines. Completeness of assessment improved from negligible levels at baseline to 50% overall. This study also resulted in marked improvement of previously poorly assessed domains such as danger signs, ear problems, nutrition status and immunisation. Presenting complaints are easily captured in outpatient settings, but negative history and ruling out subtle danger signs are often missed. This was the basis of development of IMCI guidelines.17 However, implementation of this has been poor due to challenges in resources and programme management.18 This checklist helped standardise care such that screening for conditions such as malnutrition and incomplete immunisation was not missed out. In addition, screening for danger signs is important for timely patient referral and prevention of severe morbidity or mortality.9

Despite overall improvement, screening for anaemia remained suboptimal and the primary barrier in improving complete assessment of child. This was primarily due to caregiver refusal due to the invasive nature of finger prick testing of haemoglobin levels. Even if complete assessment was defined without haemoglobin testing, the proportion plateaued at 78%. Informal discussion with treating doctors highlighted time constraints in high volume OPDs limiting complete filling of the checklist. Similar challenges have been reported in other primary care settings where some components of IMCI assessment are invariably missed out.19–21 This suggests that there may be a ceiling effect of utilisation of checklist-based tools in routine OPD care.

Most IMCI-based interventions are studied in health workers in the field, with limited evidence from doctors in outpatient settings.9 22 Improvements in clinical assessment and management have been seen consistently in health worker led interventions.9 18 22 Our study extends existing literature by showing improvement in assessment by implementing IMCI-based assessment in doctors in busy OPD settings. This study also highlights that in spite of incorporating a checklist, regular refresher training of doctors is essential to improve assessment. This can be seen by improvements in the second PDSA cycle where regular training was incorporated. This supports evidence that tools alone are insufficient for improving clinical care without adequate capacity building.6 22 23 This study used objective outpatient records for assessment of providers’ practices rather than self-reports. Also, consecutive sampling of under-5 children reduced the possibility of selection bias.

The major limitation of this intervention is the lack of assessment of long-term sustainability. This limits generalisability and external validity; however, the findings are highly relevant in our context and may be beneficial for other similar settings. There were found to be logistic concerns requiring repeated printing of forms. This highlights the importance of system-level administrative support for sustaining QI initiatives. Frequent turnover of resident doctors due to rotational postings and interrupted availability of printed forms adversely affected continuity of implementation. Due to the absence of a control group, we could not attribute the trends solely to our QI intervention. In addition, prescribing patterns or adherence to management guidelines was not assessed in this study and has been planned as a separate QI. Pulse oximetry in the assessment of pneumonia has been added to later guidelines of IMNCI in India.24 While it was being used in the OPD, it was not routinely added to the checklist. The possibility of Hawthorne effect with respect to checklist completion cannot be ruled out, as the study was conducted alongside routine OPD activities.

Globally, structured clinical tools are being widely used to aid clinicians’ judgement.25 26 In addition, using digital tools and simplified workflows are being explored to improve sustainability.27 There is also increasing task shifting of initial assessment, documentation, anthropometry and screening from doctors to nurses and allied health professionals.28 29 Our findings show that IMCI-based guidelines can be integrated into OPD systems to improve comprehensive assessment of children presenting to primary healthcare settings. However, scale up to other facilities would require integration into routine systems, logistical support including regular printing, periodic refresher training and adaptation to local workflow to improve sustainability of such an intervention. Task shifting with trained healthcare staff in OPD setups or developing team-based healthcare models may also improve feasibility. Further research is also required for digitalisation of such a tool.

Conclusion

While the QI initiative led to substantial improvements in the completeness of IMCI-based assessments for under-5 children, its long-term sustainability was limited. The continued requirement for printed age-specific forms and the additional time needed for their completion posed significant operational challenges in a high-volume urban healthcare setting. These resource and time constraints ultimately hindered routine adoption of the intervention, highlighting the need for more streamlined, resource-efficient approaches to sustain comprehensive IMCI implementation.
