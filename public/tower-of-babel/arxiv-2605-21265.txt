The non-Hermitian minor process


Abstract
. We show that the log-determinant of leading principal minors of large non-Hermitian random matrices converges in distribution to a 2+1 dimensional Gaussian field, which is logarithmically correlated for the parabolic distance, reminiscent to the Edwards-Wilkinson universality class.

Corollary 2.3

(Test functions with 3-dimensional support)
.

Let
X
and
\Omega
be as in Theorem
2.2
. For test functions
F_{1},F_{2}:[c_{0},1]\times\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{C}
assume that
F_{j}(x,\cdot)
,
j=1,2
, satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
2.2
uniformly in
x\in[c_{0},1]
. Assume additionally that
F_{1},F_{2}
are
\alpha
-Hölder continuous in the first variable for some fixed
\alpha>0
. Then we have

(2.8)

N^{-2}\mathrm{Cov}\left(L_{N}(F_{1}),L_{N}(F_{2})\right)=\int_{c_{0}}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\int_{c_{0}}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}C_{\beta}\Big(\big(x_{1},F_{1}(x_{1},\cdot)\big),\big(x_{2},F_{2}(x_{2},\cdot)\big)\Big)+\mathcal{O}(N^{-c}),

for some
N
-independent
c>0
. The implicit constant in
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
may depend only on
c_{0}
,
\sup_{x}\|F(x,\cdot)\|_{H^{2+\delta}(\Omega)}
, on the constant in the Hölder regularity condition and on model parameters from Assumption
2.1
. We also have that
N^{-1}L_{N}(F_{1})
and
N^{-1}L_{N}(F_{2})
are asymptotically jointly Gaussian distributed in the sense of (
2.6
).

To justify the
N^{-2}
scaling in the lhs. of (
2.8
) one needs to show that the integral in the rhs. of (
2.8
) does not vanish for
F_{1}=F_{2}
. This is done in the following remark.

Remark 2.4

(Positivity of
C_{\beta}
)
.

Let
F
be any test function satisfying the assumptions of Corollary
2.3
. Owing to
\mathrm{Var}[L_{N}(F)]\geq 0
, Corollary
2.3
applied for
F_{1}=F_{2}=F
yields that the sesquilinear form
C_{\beta}
is non-negative:

(2.9)

\int_{c_{0}}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\int_{c_{0}}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}C_{\beta}\Big(\big(x_{1},F(x_{1},\cdot)\big),\big(x_{2},F(x_{2},\cdot)\big)\Big)\geq 0.

The following argument shows that
C_{\beta}
is strictly positive at least when the support of
F
is mesoscopic or sufficiently small on the macroscopic scale. For
\ell>0
,
x_{0}\in(c_{0},1)
and
|z_{0}|<\sqrt{x_{0}}
define

(2.10)

F_{\ell}(x,z):=\ell^{-2}F\left(\ell^{-2}(x-x_{0}),\ell^{-1}(z-z_{0})\right).

We view
C_{\beta}
as a perturbation around its mesoscopic version (see (
2.21
) below), which is known to be strictly positive from
[
9
, Section 5.5]
. Thus, an elementary perturbative calculation gives that the lhs. of (
2.9
) evaluated at
F_{\ell}
is a positive quantity of order 1 for sufficiently small
\ell>0
(which may be chosen independently of
N
).

Now we present an explicit formula for
C_{\beta}
emerging in (
2.7
). First, for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in(0,1]
define

(2.11)

\begin{split}C^{(G)}\big((x_{1},f_{1}),(x_{2},f_{2})\big):=&-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbf{D}_{x_{1}}}\!\!\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}z_{1}\int_{\mathbf{D}_{x_{2}}}\!\!\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}z_{2}\,\overline{\partial_{z_{1}}f_{1}(z_{1})}\partial_{z_{2}}f_{2}(z_{2})\partial_{z_{1}}\partial_{\overline{z}_{2}}\log K\big((x_{1},z_{1}),(x_{2},z_{2})\big)\\
&+\frac{\min\{x_{1},x_{2}\}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{\partial\mathbf{D}_{x_{1}}\times\partial\mathbf{D}_{x_{2}}}\frac{\overline{f_{1}(z_{1})}f_{2}(z_{2})}{\left(\min\{x_{1},x_{2}\}-z_{1}\overline{z}_{2}\right)^{2}}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}\overline{z}_{2}.\end{split}

Here
\mathbf{D}_{r}:=\sqrt{r}\,\mathbf{D}
for
r>0
, and

K
is given by

(2.12)

K\big((x_{1},z_{1}),(x_{2},z_{2})\big):=|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}+|x_{1}-x_{2}|B,\quad B=B\big((x_{1},z_{1}),(x_{2},z_{2})\big):=\begin{cases}1-\frac{|z_{1}|^{2}}{x_{1}},&x_{1}\geq x_{2},\\
1-\frac{|z_{2}|^{2}}{x_{2}},&x_{1}<x_{2}.\end{cases}

Next, we set

(2.13)

\begin{split}&C^{(4)}\big((x_{1},f_{1}),(x_{2},f_{2})\big):=\\
&\frac{\min\{x_{1},x_{2}\}}{\max\{x_{1},x_{2}\}}\!\!\left(\!\frac{1}{\pi x_{1}}\int_{\mathbf{D}_{x_{1}}}\!\!\!\!\overline{f_{1}(z)}\mathrm{d}^{2}z-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\overline{f_{1}(\sqrt{x_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})}\mathrm{d}\theta\!\right)\!\!\!\left(\!\frac{1}{\pi x_{2}}\int_{\mathbf{D}_{x_{2}}}\!\!\!\!f_{2}(z)\mathrm{d}^{2}z-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!f_{2}(\sqrt{x_{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})\mathrm{d}\theta\!\right)\!.\end{split}

Finally, we define the bilinear forms
C_{\beta}
,
\beta=1,2
, determining the behavior of (
2.5
) in the real (
\beta=1
) and complex (
\beta=2
) case. Denote

(2.14)

\kappa_{4}:=\mathbf{E}|\chi|^{4}-1-2/\beta.

For
\beta=2
we set

(2.15)

C_{\beta=2}\big((x_{1},f_{1}),(x_{2},f_{2})\big):=C^{(G)}\big((x_{1},f_{1}),(x_{2},f_{2})\big)+\kappa_{4}C^{(4)}\big((x_{1},f_{1}),(x_{2},f_{2})\big).

For
\beta=1
we further define

(2.16)

C_{\beta=1}\big((x_{1},f_{1}),(x_{2},f_{2})\big):=2C^{(G)}\big((x_{1},P_{\mathrm{sym}}f_{1}),(x_{2},P_{\mathrm{sym}}f_{2})\big)+\kappa_{4}C^{(4)}\big((x_{1},f_{1}),(x_{2},f_{2})\big),

where the symmetrization operator is given by

(2.17)

\left(P_{\mathrm{sym}}f\right)(z):=\frac{f(z)+f(\overline{z})}{2},\quad\forall z\in\mathbf{C}.

The superscript in
C^{(G)}
refers to the fact that the corresponding terms in (
2.15
) and (
2.15
) are the only remaining ones when
X
is a Ginibre matrix, i.e. when the entries of
X
have Gaussian distribution. The quadratic form
C^{(4)}
is a correction to the Gaussian result and appears in (
2.15
), (
2.16
) multiplied by the fourth cumulant of the single-entry distribution. The appearance of symmetrization
P_{\mathrm{sym}}
in the real case is due to the fact that the spectrum of
X
(and of its minors) is symmetric with respect to the real axis.

All integrals in (
2.11
) are well-defined, we postpone a detailed discussion of this topic to Appendix
A.1
.

2.2.
Mesoscopic CLT

In the mesoscopic regime, we fix
a\in(0,1/2)
,
x_{0}\in(0,1)
,
|z_{0}|<\sqrt{x_{0}}
, all
N
-independent, and set

(2.18)

f_{j,z_{0},a}(z):=f_{j}\left(N^{a}(z-z_{0})\right),\quad z\in\mathbf{C},\qquad x_{j}:=x_{0}+N^{-2a}y_{j},\quad y_{j}\in\mathbf{R},

for
j\in[n]
. As Theorem
2.5
below shows, the limiting behavior of covariance (
2.5
) in this regime is simpler compared to the macroscopic regime. The contribution from
\kappa_{4}
is dominated by the Gaussian contribution, and the limit becomes universal; confirming the natural intuition that spectral statistics on smaller scales are "more universal". Moreover, since we rescale test functions around some point in the bulk, the edge contribution in the second line of (
2.11
) disappears. In such a way, we are left only with the analogue of the first term in the rhs. of (
2.11
). Define the local version of the kernel
K
by

(2.19)

K_{x_{0},z_{0}}\big((y_{1},z_{1}),(y_{2},z_{2})\big):=|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}+|y_{1}-y_{2}|\left(1-\frac{|z_{0}|^{2}}{x_{0}}\right).

The mesoscopic analogue of
C^{(G)}
is given by

(2.20)

C^{(G)}_{x_{0},z_{0}}\big((y_{1},f_{1}),\!(y_{2},f_{2})\big)\!:=\!-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\!\int_{\mathbf{D}_{x_{0}}}\!\!\!\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}z_{1}\!\!\int_{\mathbf{D}_{x_{0}}}\!\!\!\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}z_{2}\,\overline{\partial_{z_{1}}f_{1}(z_{1})}\partial_{z_{2}}f_{2}(z_{2})\partial_{z_{1}}\partial_{\overline{z}_{2}}\log K_{x_{0},z_{0}}\big((y_{1},z_{1}),\!(y_{2},z_{2})\big)\!.

We also define

(2.21)

C_{x_{0},z_{0},\beta=2}:=C_{x_{0},z_{0}}^{(G)},\quad C_{x_{0},z_{0},\beta=1}\big((y_{1},f_{1}),(y_{2},f_{2})\big):=2C_{x_{0},z_{0}}^{(G)}\big((y_{1},P_{\mathrm{sym}}f_{1}),(y_{2},P_{\mathrm{sym}}f_{2})\big).

We point out that an analogous kernel previously appeared in the context of dynamics on non-Hermitian matrices in
[
9
, Eq.(2.7)]
. Our second main result is as follows.

Theorem 2.5

(Mesoscopic CLT)
.

Let
X
be a complex or real
N\times N
i.i.d. matrix satisfying Assumption
2.1
. Fix (small)
c_{0},\tau>0
, an exponent
a\in(0,1/2)
and an open bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbf{C}
. For
x_{0}\in[c_{0},1)
and
z_{0}\in\mathbf{C}
with
|z_{0}|\leq(1-\tau)\sqrt{x_{0}}
, there exists the centered Gaussian process
L_{x_{0},z_{0}}(y,f)
indexed by
\mathbf{R}\times H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
with the covariance structure given by
C_{x_{0},z_{0},\beta}
defined in (
2.21
). The process of mesoscopically scaled centered linear statistics
L^{\lceil xN\rceil}(f_{z_{0},a})
with
x:=x_{0}+N^{-2a}y
converges to
L_{x_{0},z_{0}}(y,f)
in the sense of moments (
2.6
). The implicit constant in the error term may depend only on the moment degree,
c_{0},\tau,a
, model parameters from Assumption
2.1
and
H^{2}(\Omega)
norms of test functions.

Similarly to Corollary
2.3
, Theorem
2.5
can be also extended to test functions with 3-dimensional support.

3.
Proof strategy

The proof of Theorem
2.2
closely follows the strategy introduced in
[

and

and for any (possibly,
N
-dependent)
x_{j}:=k_{j}/N\in[c_{0},1]
,
j=1,2
, it holds that

(3.1)

\mathrm{Cov}\left(L^{(k_{1})}(f_{1}),L^{(k_{2})}(f_{2})\right)=C_{\beta}\left((x_{1},f_{1}),(x_{2},f_{2})\right)+\mathcal{O}(N^{-c}),

for some
N
-independent
c>0
. The derivation of (
2.6
) for higher order moments from the inputs presented below is standard and thus is postponed to Appendix
A.4
. The proof of Theorem
2.5
additionally requires only the inputs already available from
[
19
]

The proof of (
3.1
) consists of two steps outlined in Sections

and
3.2
. First, we apply the Girko’s Hermitization formula and remove the singular regimes from it. This is the content of Lemma

[
18
]
. In the second step we compute the covariance of two resolvent traces in Proposition

3.1.
The Girko’s Hermitization formula

As usual, for
k\in[N]
we set
x:=k/N
. For
z\in\mathbf{C}
and
w\in\mathbf{C}\setminus\mathbf{R}
denote

(3.2)

W^{(k)}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&X^{(k)}\\
\big(X^{(k)}\big)^{*}&0\end{pmatrix},\,\,Z^{(k)}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&z\\
\overline{z}&0\end{pmatrix},\,\,H^{(k),z}:=W^{(k)}-Z^{(k)},\,\,G^{(k),z}_{x}(w):=\big(H^{(k),z}-w\big)^{-1}.

All matrices defined in (
3.2
) have size
(2k)\times(2k)
. In
G^{(k),z}_{x}
the subscript indicates that the entries of
H^{(k),z}
have variance
N^{-1/2}=x^{1/2}k^{-1/2}
instead of the standard normalization
k^{-1/2}
. The same subscript could be included into the notations
W^{(k)}
and
H^{(k),z}
for the same reason, however we avoid this for brevity. We call
W^{(k)}
the
Hermitization
of
X^{(k)}
,
z
the
Hermitization parameter
, and
w
the
spectral parameter
. The Girko’s Hermitization formula
[
28
]
in the regularized form given in
[
42
]
states that

(3.3)

\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(\sigma_{i}^{(k)})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbf{C}}\Delta f(z)\log\left|\det(H^{(k),z}-\mathrm{i}T)\right|\mathrm{d}^{2}z-\frac{k}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathbf{C}}\Delta f(z)\mathrm{d}^{2}z\int_{0}^{T}\big\langle G^{(k),z}_{x}(\mathrm{i}\eta)\big\rangle\mathrm{d}\eta,

where we choose
T:=N^{100}
. For a small fixed
\delta_{c}>0
which will be chosen later we set

(3.4)

I_{c}^{(k)}(f):=-\frac{k}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathbf{C}}\Delta f(z)\mathrm{d}^{2}z\int_{\eta_{c}}^{T}\big\langle G^{(k),z}_{x}(\mathrm{i}\eta)\big\rangle\mathrm{d}\eta,\quad\eta_{c}:=N^{-1+\delta_{c}}.

Here we removed the small
\eta
regime from consideration that is shown to be negligible. Indeed, the following statement shows that the leading contribution to the lhs. of (
3.1
) comes from
I_{c}^{(k_{j})}(f_{j})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume the set-up and conditions of Theorem
2.2
. There exists a (small) fixed
\delta_{c}>0
such that

(3.5)

\mathrm{Cov}\left(L^{(k_{1})}(f_{1}),L^{(k_{2})}(f_{2})\right)=\mathrm{Cov}\left(I_{c}^{(k_{1})}(f_{1}),I_{c}^{(k_{2})}(f_{2})\right)+\mathcal{O}(N^{-c}),

for any
f_{j}\in H^{2+\delta}_{0}(\Omega)
and
k_{j}\in[N]
with
x_{j}:=k_{j}/N\in[c_{0},1]
,
j=1,2
. The constant
c>0
does not depend on
N
, and the implicit constant in
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
may depend on
\|\Delta f_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and
|\Omega|
.

The proof of Lemma
3.1
immediately follows from
[
18
, Lemma 4.1]
for
\beta=2
and
[

, Proposition 3.4]
for
\beta=1

3.2.
Covariance of two resolvent traces

Lemma
3.1
reduces the calculation of the lhs. of (
3.5
) to the calculation of covariance of two resolvent traces. The result of this calculation is presented in Proposition
3.3
below. Let us introduce the framework for this result. The
self-energy operator

\mathcal{S}^{(k)}_{x}
is given by

(3.6)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{S}^{(k)}_{x}[R]:=x\left(\big\langle RE_{+}^{(k)}\big\rangle E_{+}^{(k)}-\big\langle RE_{-}^{(k)}\big\rangle E_{-}^{(k)}\right),\quad E_{\pm}^{(k)}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\pm 1\end{pmatrix},\quad\forall R\in\mathbf{C}^{(2k)\times(2k)}.\end{split}

The blocks of
E^{(k)}_{\pm}
are of size
k\times k
. For
\beta=2
the rhs. of (
3.6
) actually coincides with
\mathbf{E}\big[W^{(k)}RW^{(k)}\big]
. For
\beta=1
this expectation slightly differs from the rhs. of (
3.6
), however we still use (
3.6
) as the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{x}^{(k)}
for technical convenience. For
z\in\mathbf{C}
and
w\in\mathbf{C}\setminus\mathbf{R}
, the
matrix Dyson equation
(MDE) is defined by

(3.7)

-M^{-1}=w-Z^{(k)}+\mathcal{S}^{(k)}_{x}[M],\quad\Im w\Im M>0,\quad M\in\mathbf{C}^{(2k)\times(2k)}.

It is known from
[
1
, Lemma 2.2]
that the solution to (
3.7
) exists and is unique (see also the abstract result in
[
32
]
). We denote this solution by
M:=M^{(k),z}_{x}(w)
. Typically, the equation (
3.7
) is considered in the literature for
x=1
, while the general case
x\in(0,1]
can be reduced to
x=1
by rescaling:

(3.8)

M_{x}^{(k),z}(w)=x^{-1/2}M_{1}^{(k),x^{-1/2}z}\left(x^{-1/2}w\right),\quad G_{x}^{(k),z}(w)=x^{-1/2}G_{1}^{(k),x^{-1/2}z}\left(x^{-1/2}w\right).

These relations allow us to transfer results stated for
x=1
to the general case
x\in(0,1]
, which we will implicitly use from now on. In particular, (
3.8
), in combination with
[
1
, Eq.(3.5)-(3.6)]
, implies that
M
has the following
2\times 2
block-constant structure with the blocks of size
k\times k
:

(3.9)

M=\begin{pmatrix}m&-zu\\
-\overline{z}u&m\end{pmatrix},\quad u=u^{z}_{x}(w):=\frac{m}{w+xm},

where
m=m^{z}_{x}(w)
solves the cubic equation

(3.10)

-\frac{1}{m}=w+xm-\frac{|z|^{2}}{w+xm},\quad\Im w\Im m>0.

By
[
18
, Eq.(3.3)]
it holds that

(3.11)

\|M\|+|m|+|u|\lesssim x^{-1}.

Finally, we denote

(3.12)

\rho=\rho^{z}_{x}(w):=\frac{1}{\pi}|\Im m^{z}_{x}(w)|.

The solution to (
3.7
) is called the
deterministic approximation
to
G^{(k),z}_{x}
, since this resolvent concentrates around
M^{(k),z}_{x}
in the sense of the following

X

dependence into the bound on the error term and getting that this bound improves as

increases. However we do not pursue this line of research here.

The key ingredient in the proof of Proposition

is the concentration bound for the product of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. Results of this type are commonly referred to as two-resolvent local laws. Since the sizes of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
do not match, we need to project

on the space of
(2k_{1})\times(2k_{1})
matrices. To this end, denote

(3.17)

\widetilde{G}_{2}:=P^{*}G_{2}P,\quad\text{where}\quad P:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\\
0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbf{C}^{(2k_{2})\times(2k_{1})}.

The matrix
P
is split into columns of width
k_{1},k_{1}
and the rows of height
k_{2}-k_{1},k_{1},k_{2}-k_{1},k_{1}
. It is defined in such a way that

(3.18)

W_{1}=P^{*}W_{2}P,\quad\text{where}\quad W_{j}:=W^{(k_{j})},\,\,j=1,2.

We show that the product of
G_{1}
and
\widetilde{G}_{2}
concentrates around its deterministic approximation, which is the content of Proposition
3.4
below.

Let

be a deterministic matrix, which we customarily call an
observable
. The deterministic approximation to
G_{1}B\widetilde{G}_{2}
is constructed from the deterministic approximations to
G_{1}
and
\widetilde{G}_{2}
, as we now explain. First, denote

(3.19)

M_{j}:=M^{(k_{j}),z_{j}}_{x_{j}}(w_{j}),\,\,j=1,2,\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{M}_{2}:=P^{*}M_{2}P.

Note that
\widetilde{M}_{2}
differs from
M_{2}
only by shrinking the size of blocks from
k_{2}\times k_{2}
to
k_{1}\times k_{1}
, while the constants inside the blocks remain intact. The
two-body stability operator
associated to
G_{1}
,
\widetilde{G}_{2}
is given by

(3.20)

\mathcal{B}_{1\widetilde{2}}[R]:=R-M_{1}\mathcal{S}^{(k_{1})}_{x_{1}}[R]\widetilde{M}_{2},\quad\forall R\in\mathbf{C}^{(2k_{1})\times(2k_{1})}.

Finally, the deterministic approximation to
G_{1}B\widetilde{G}_{2}
is defined by

(3.21)

M_{1\widetilde{2}}^{B}:=\mathcal{B}_{1\widetilde{2}}^{-1}[M_{1}B\widetilde{M}_{2}]\in\mathbf{C}^{(2k_{1})\times(2k_{1})}.

For further reference, we also define the
one-body stability operator
associated to
G_{j}
and the deterministic approximation to
G_{j}BG_{j}
:

(3.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{jj}[R]:=

\displaystyle R-M_{j}\mathcal{S}^{(k_{j})}_{x_{j}}[R]M_{j},\quad\forall R\in\mathbf{C}^{(2k_{j})\times(2k_{j})},

(3.23)

\displaystyle M_{jj}^{B}:=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{jj}^{-1}[M_{j}BM_{j}],\quad B\in\mathbf{C}^{(2k_{j})\times(2k_{j})},\,\,j=1,2.

We state the two-resolvent local law for the averaged quantities of the form
\langle G_{1}B_{1}\widetilde{G_{2}}B_{2}\rangle
in a narrow cone containing the imaginary axis. This means that the spectral parameters
w_{1}
,
w_{2}
of

and
\widetilde{G}_{2}
are not necessarily purely imaginary, but satisfy

in a sense explained in Proposition
3.4
. As at least one of the parameters

approaches the boundary of
\mathbf{D}_{x_{j}}
, the cone becomes more narrow. In fact, the restriction of

to a cone could be removed, however this is not needed for the proof of Proposition
3.3
, so we keep this restriction for simplicity.

Proposition 3.4

(Two-resolvent averaged local law)

Let

satisfy Assumption

uniformly in

The proof of Proposition

The main tool used in the proof of (

(also known as

(for earlier works, which did not operate with this formalism but followed a similar approach see e.g.

where

is an independent copy of

\widetilde{W}
. The name second order renormalization arises from the fact that the underline removes the second order terms in the cumulant expansion for

We start the proof of (

For the proof see
[

together with the rescaling (

is defined in (

) and its adjoint is taken with respect to the scalar product

In Appendix
A.2
we show that

(5.6)

\|A\|\lesssim\left|x_{1}-|z_{1}|^{2}\right|^{-1},

so the error term in the rhs. of (
5.5
) can be incorporated into the error term in the rhs. of (
5.1
) (recall that the latter one may deteriorate polynomially in
|x_{1}-|z_{1}|^{2}|
).

Next, we expand the underline on the second factor in the rhs. of (
5.5
) by the means of (
5.3
). To this end, let
\widetilde{W}_{1}
be an independent copy of
W_{1}
, and let
\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{1}
be the expectation wrt.
\widetilde{W}_{1}
. We have

(5.7)

\partial_{\widetilde{W}_{1}}\left(\langle G_{2}\rangle-\mathbf{E}\langle G_{2}\rangle\right)=-\langle G_{2}\partial_{\widetilde{W}_{1}}[W_{2}]G_{2}\rangle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2x_{2}N}\partial_{\eta_{2}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\partial_{\widetilde{W}_{1}}[W_{2}]G_{2}\right]=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2x_{2}N}\partial_{\eta_{2}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\widetilde{W}_{1}\widetilde{G}_{2}\right],

where in the last identity we used (
3.17
) and the fact that
W_{1}
is a minor of
W_{2}
. Observe that for any
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathbf{C}^{(2k_{1})\times(2k_{1})}
independent of
\widetilde{W}_{1}
it holds that

(5.8)

\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{1}\left[\mathrm{Tr}[\widetilde{W}_{1}B_{1}]\mathrm{Tr}[\widetilde{W}_{2}B_{2}]\right]=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\sigma\in\{\pm\}}\sigma\mathrm{Tr}\left[B_{1}E_{\sigma}^{(k_{1})}B_{2}E_{\sigma}^{(k_{1})}\right].

Combining (
5.5
)–(
5.8
) with (
5.3
), we obtain

(5.9)

\begin{split}\mathbf{E}\langle G_{1}-\mathbf{E}G_{1}\rangle\langle G_{2}-\mathbf{E}G_{2}\rangle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4x_{2}N^{2}}\sum_{\sigma\in\{\pm\}}\sigma\partial_{\eta_{2}}\left\langle G_{1}AE_{\sigma}^{(k_{1})}\widetilde{G}_{2}E_{\sigma}^{(k_{1})}\right\rangle-\Upsilon,\end{split}

where
\Upsilon
is the fully underlined term:

(5.10)

\Upsilon=\Upsilon\big((x_{1},\eta_{1},z_{1}),(x_{2},\eta_{2},z_{2})\big):=\underline{\langle W_{1}G_{1}A\rangle\left(\langle G_{2}\rangle-\mathbf{E}\langle G_{2}\rangle\right)}.

To finish the proof of (
5.1
) it remains to compute
\mathbf{E}\Upsilon
. Note that in the Gaussian case
\mathbf{E}\Upsilon=0
. We will perform a cumulant expansion and see that only cumulants of order at least three contribute to
\mathbf{E}\Upsilon
. Introduce the index set

(5.11)

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1}=&\left([(1-x_{1})N+1,N]\cup[(2-x_{1})N+1,2N]\right)^{2},\\
\mathcal{J}_{1}=&[(1-x_{1})N+1,N]\times[(2-x_{1})N+1,2N]\cup[(2-x_{1})N+1,2N]\times[(1-x_{1})N+1,N].\end{split}

We label the entries of
W_{1}
and
G_{1}
by
\mathcal{I}_{1}
. Then non-zero entries of
W_{1}
are enumerated by
\mathcal{J}_{1}
. For
l\in\mathbf{N}
and a collection of index pairs

(5.12)

\bm{\alpha}=\left(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}\right)\in\mathcal{I}_{1}^{l}

denote the normalized cumulant of the corresponding elements of
W_{1}
by

(5.13)

\kappa(\bm{\alpha})=\kappa(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}):=\kappa(\sqrt{N}w_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{N}w_{\alpha_{k}}).

Since
X
has i.i.d. entries,
\kappa(\bm{\alpha})
may not vanish only when
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\in\{ab,ba\}
for some
(a,b)\in\mathcal{J}_{1}
. We further denote
\partial_{\bm{\alpha}}:=\partial_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial_{\alpha_{k}}
, where
\partial_{\alpha}
denotes the directional derivative in the direction
w_{\alpha}
, for an index pair
\alpha
.

Performing the cumulant expansion
[
30
, Lemma 3.2]
(see also
[
13
, Eq.(7.14)]
,
[
31
, Lemma 3.1]
and
[
35
, Section II]
) in
\mathbf{E}\Upsilon
, we obtain

(5.14)

\mathbf{E}\Upsilon=\frac{1}{2x_{1}N}\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}\sum_{\ell=3}^{L}\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\{ab,ba\}^{\ell-1}}\frac{\kappa(ab,\bm{\alpha})}{N^{\ell/2}(\ell-1)!}\mathbf{E}\partial_{\bm{\alpha}}\left[\left(G_{1}A\right)_{ba}\left(\langle G_{2}\rangle-\mathbf{E}\langle G_{2}\rangle\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(N^{-D}),

for any fixed
D>0
and for some
L\in\mathbf{N}
which depends on
D
, but does not depend on
N
. Here we used that due to the definition of the underline (
5.3
), the second order cumulants are absent in the expansion. The analysis of (
5.14
) closely follows the one in
[
13
, Eq.(7.14)-(7.24)]
, so we only explain required adjustments. First,
[
13
, Eq.(7.14)-(7.24)]
concerns only the regime
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\lesssim 1
, however these estimates hold also for
\eta_{1}\lesssim 1
and
\eta_{2}\gtrsim 1
. In the regime
\eta_{1}\gtrsim 1
we need to use additionally that

(5.15)

\|M_{1}\|\lesssim\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}\quad\text{and}\quad\left|(G_{1}-M_{1})_{ab}\right|\prec\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\eta_{1}^{2}},

uniformly in
(a,b)\in\mathcal{I}_{x}
. The first bound in (
5.15
) directly follows from the MDE (
3.7
) for
M_{1}
, while the second one is the isotropic local law from
[
1
, Eq.(5.2)]
. Using these inputs, we get that the terms with
\ell\geq 5
and
\ell=3
contribute at most

(5.16)

\mathcal{E}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\frac{N^{\xi}}{N^{2}\eta_{1}\eta_{2}}

to the rhs. of (
5.14
). We further have that the
\ell=4
terms contributing to the leading order in the rhs. of (
5.14
) correspond to
\bm{\alpha}
which is a permutation of
(ab,ba,ba)
, and exactly one derivative
\partial_{ba}
hits the term
(G_{1}A)_{ba}
, while the remaining two hit
\langle G_{2}\rangle-\mathbf{E}\langle G_{2}\rangle
. The rest of the
\ell=4
terms can be incorporated into the error term (
5.16
). Thus, it holds that

(5.17)

\mathbf{E}\Upsilon=\frac{1}{2x_{1}N}\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}\frac{\kappa_{4}}{N^{2}}\mathbf{E}\partial_{ba}[(G_{1}A)_{ba}]\partial_{ab}\partial_{ba}\left[\langle G_{2}\rangle-\mathbf{E}\langle G_{2}\rangle\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathcal{E}\right).

Here we took into account that there are 3 permutations of
(ab,ba,ba)
and 2 ways to distribute derivatives over the two factors (due to the fact that there are two derivatives in
w_{ba}
). This canceled the factor
(\ell-1)!
for
\ell=4
in denominator in the rhs. of (
5.14
).

Next, we perform differentiation in (
5.17
) and replace each of the four obtained factors by its deterministic approximation using the isotropic single-resolvent local law (
3.14
). This yields an error term which can be again incorporated into the second term in the rhs. of (
5.17
), see
[
13
, Eq.(7.18)-(7.19)]
with the adjustments explained above. We get

(5.18)

\mathbf{E}\Upsilon=\frac{-1}{4x_{1}x_{2}N^{2}}\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}\frac{\kappa_{4}}{N^{2}}(M_{1})_{bb}(M_{1}A)_{aa}\!\!\left(\!(M_{2})_{aa}\big(M_{22}^{E_{+}}\big)_{bb}\!+\!\big(M_{22}^{E_{+}}\big)_{aa}(M_{2})_{bb}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)\!,

where
E_{+}=E_{+}^{(k_{2})}
and
M_{22}^{E_{+}}
is defined in (
3.23
). An explicit calculation based on (
3.7
) and (
3.22
) shows that

(5.19)

M_{22}^{E_{+}}=\mathcal{B}_{22}^{-1}[M_{2}^{2}]=-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\eta_{2}}M_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad M_{1}A=\frac{M_{1}^{2}}{1-x_{1}\langle M_{1}^{2}\rangle}=\mathcal{B}_{11}^{-1}[M_{1}^{2}]=-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\eta_{1}}M_{1},

compare with
[
13
, Eq.(7.21)]
stated for
x_{1}=x_{2}=1
. Since all diagonal entries of
M_{j}
equal to
m_{j}
, we conclude that

(5.20)

\begin{split}\mathbf{E}\Upsilon=&\frac{2}{4x_{1}x_{2}N^{2}}\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}\frac{\kappa_{4}}{N^{2}}m_{1}\partial_{\eta_{1}}[m_{1}]m_{2}\partial_{\eta_{2}}[m_{2}]+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)\\
=&\frac{2|\mathcal{J}_{1}|\kappa_{4}}{4x_{1}x_{2}N^{4}}\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\eta_{1}}[m_{1}^{2}]\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\eta_{2}}[m_{2}^{2}]+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{E})=-\frac{x^{2}_{1}\kappa_{4}}{2x_{1}x_{2}N^{2}}U_{1}U_{2}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{E}),\end{split}

where we computed that
|\mathcal{J}_{1}|=2x_{1}^{2}N^{2}
and recalled the definition of
U_{j}
from (
3.16
). This finishes the proof of (
5.1
).

5.2.
Computation of the deterministic approximation

In this section we consistently denote
E_{\sigma}:=E^{(k_{1})}_{\sigma}
, for
\sigma\in\{\pm\}
. We claim that

(5.21)

\left|\partial_{\eta_{2}}\left\langle\left(G_{1}AE_{\sigma}\widetilde{G}_{2}-M_{1\widetilde{2}}^{E_{\sigma}}\right)E_{\sigma}\right\rangle\right|\prec\frac{1}{N\eta_{1}\eta_{2}^{2}},

where the implicit constant in the rhs. of (
5.21
) may deteriorate polynomially in
||z_{j}|-\sqrt{x_{j}}|
,
j=1,2
. This immediately follows from the local law from Proposition
3.4
combined with the observation that the lhs. of (
5.21
) (without the absolute value) is an analytic function of
w_{2}
evaluated at
w_{2}=\mathrm{i}\eta_{2}
, so its derivative can be represented as a contour integral via Cauchy’s integral formula. For a similar argument see e.g.
[
13
, Eq.(7.11)]
. In particular, because of this step we formulated Proposition
3.4
not only on the imaginary axis, but for general spectral parameters.

From (
5.21
) we have that

(5.22)

\sum_{\sigma\in\{\pm\}}\sigma\partial_{\eta_{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\langle G_{1}AE_{\sigma}\widetilde{G}_{2}E_{\sigma}\rangle\right]=\partial_{\eta_{2}}\sum_{\sigma\in\{\pm\}}\sigma\left\langle M_{1\widetilde{2}}^{AE_{\sigma}}E_{\sigma}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}_{\theta}\left(\frac{N^{\xi}}{N\eta_{1}\eta_{2}^{2}}\right).

Here
\mathcal{O}_{\theta}(\cdot)
is understood in the same sense as in Proposition
3.3
. Computation of the sum in the rhs. of (
5.22
) closely follows the argument in
[
13
, Eq.(7.25)-(7.27)]
. First, we define
\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{2}1}
by interchanging
M_{1}
and
\widetilde{M}_{2}
in (
3.20
). Similarly,
M_{\widetilde{2}1}^{B}:=\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{2}1}^{-1}[\widetilde{M}_{2}BM_{1}]
for an
(2x_{1}N)\times(2x_{1}N)
observable
B
. An elementary calculation based on (
3.20
), (
3.21
), and on their analogues which we have just defined, shows that

(5.23)

\left\langle M_{1\widetilde{2}}^{AE_{\sigma}}E_{\sigma}\right\rangle=\left\langle M_{\widetilde{2}1}^{E_{\sigma}}AE_{\sigma}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{2}1}^{-1}[\widetilde{M}_{2}E_{\sigma}M_{1}]AE_{\sigma}\right\rangle,\quad\sigma\in\{\pm\}.

The exact choice of observables is not important, this identity holds with
AE_{\sigma}
and
E_{\sigma}
replaced by general
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathbf{C}^{(2k_{1})\times(2k_{1})}
. To compute the rhs. of (
5.23
), we denote for short
V_{\sigma}:=\widetilde{M}_{2}E_{\sigma}M_{1}
for
\sigma\in\{\pm\}
. By the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{2}1}
it holds that

(5.24)

\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{2}1}[V_{+}]\\
\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{2}1}[V_{-}]\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{P}\begin{pmatrix}V_{+}\\
V_{-}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{P}=\begin{pmatrix}1-x_{1}\langle V_{+}\rangle&x_{1}\langle V_{+}E_{-}\rangle\\
-x_{1}\langle V_{-}\rangle&1+x_{1}\langle V_{-}E_{-}\rangle\end{pmatrix}.

Thus we have

(5.25)

\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{B}^{-1}_{\widetilde{2}1}[V_{+}]\\
\mathcal{B}^{-1}_{\widetilde{2}1}[V_{-}]\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{P}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}V_{+}\\
V_{-}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{\det\mathcal{P}}\begin{pmatrix}\left(1+x_{1}\langle V_{-}E_{-}\rangle\right)V_{+}-x_{1}\langle V_{+}E_{-}\rangle V_{-}\\
x_{1}\langle V_{-}\rangle V_{+}+\left(1-x_{1}\langle V_{+}\rangle\right)V_{-}\end{pmatrix}.

Combining (
5.23
) with (
5.25
) and the second part of (
5.19
), we conclude that

(5.26)

\sum_{\sigma\in\{\pm\}}\sigma\left\langle M_{1\widetilde{2}}^{AE_{\sigma}}E_{\sigma}\right\rangle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{x_{1}}\partial_{\eta_{1}}\log\det\mathcal{P}.

An explicit calculation based on (
3.9
) and (
3.19
) shows that
\det\mathcal{P}
equals to the argument of logarithm in the last line of (
3.16
). Together with (
5.1
) and (
5.22
) this finishes the proof of Proposition
3.3
in the complex case.

5.3.
Real case

There are only three minor differences in the proof of Proposition
3.3
in the real case compared to the complex case. First, in the rhs. of (
5.4
) an additional term
N^{-1}\langle G_{1}^{\mathfrak{t}}A\rangle
arises. This term does not contribute to the leading order in (
5.5
) and can be incorporated into the error term similarly to the first term in the rhs. of (
5.4
). Second, the following new term arises in the rhs. of (
5.8
):

(5.27)

\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\sigma\in\{\pm\}}\sigma\mathrm{Tr}\left[B_{1}E_{\sigma}^{(k_{1})}B_{2}^{\mathfrak{t}}E_{\sigma}^{(k_{1})}\right].

As in the complex case, we take
B_{1}:=G_{1}A
and
B_{2}:=\widetilde{G}_{2}
. Since
W^{(k_{1})}
is a real symmetric matrix and
P
defined in (
3.17
) has real entries, we obtain

(5.28)

\widetilde{G}_{2}^{\mathfrak{t}}=P^{*}G_{2}^{\mathfrak{t}}P=P^{*}G^{(k_{2}),\overline{z}_{2}}(\mathrm{i}\eta_{2})P,

i.e. transpose on
\widetilde{G}_{2}
results in replacing

by
\overline{z}_{2}
. Thus, (
5.27
) contributes
V_{1\overline{2}}
to the rhs. of (

). Finally, in the analysis of the fully underlined term (

)–(

) the formula for

changes according to (
2.14
), while the entire analysis remains intact. This finishes the proof of Proposition
3.3
in the real case.

6.
Proof of the local law in Proposition
3.4

Recall the definition of the two-body stability operator
\mathcal{B}_{1\widetilde{2}}
from (
3.20
). One of the main ingredients in the proof of Proposition
3.4
is the upper bound on
\|\mathcal{B}_{1\widetilde{2}}^{-1}\|
, since this quantity controls the size of
M_{1\widetilde{2}}^{B}
via (
3.21
). Thus, we start with the discussion of spectral properties of
\mathcal{B}_{1\widetilde{2}}
.

Throughout this section we use the following short-hand notations. Index
j
is always meant to be from
\{1,2\}
. Denote
M_{j}:=M^{(k_{j}),z_{j}}_{x_{j}}(w_{j})
for
x_{j}:=k_{j}/N\in(0,1]
with
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
,
z_{j}\in\mathbf{C}
and
w_{j}\in\mathbf{C}\setminus\mathbf{R}
. The same convention is used for
m_{j},u_{j}
and
\rho_{j}
defined in (
3.9
) and (
3.12
). Denote additionally
\eta_{j}:=|\Im w_{j}|
. Whenever we refer to some properties of (
3.7
) known from the literature, we mean that they are known for
x=1
and for general
x\in(0,1]
one needs to apply the rescaling (
3.8
).

First, we have that
\mathcal{B}_{1\widetilde{2}}
has eigenvalue 1 of multiplicity
(2k_{1})^{2}-2
, and the remaining two eigenvalues are given by

(6.1)

\beta_{\pm}:=1-x_{1}\Re[z_{1}\overline{z}_{2}]u_{1}u_{2}\pm x_{1}\sqrt{m_{1}^{2}m_{2}^{2}-(\Im[z_{1}\overline{z}_{2}]u_{1}u_{2})^{2}}.

The proof of (
6.1
) immediately follows from the fact that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{B}_{1\widetilde{2}}
different from 1 are the eigenvalues of the
2\times 2
matrix
\mathcal{P}
defined in (
5.24
). For a similar argument see
[
18
, Eq.(6.3)-(6.4)]
. It further holds that

(6.2)

\|\mathcal{B}_{1\widetilde{2}}^{-1}[R]\|\lesssim\frac{\|R\|}{\beta_{*}},\quad\forall\,R\in\mathbf{C}^{(2k_{1})\times(2k_{1})},\quad\text{where}\quad\beta_{*}:=\min\{|\beta_{+}|,|\beta_{-}|\}.

The implicit constant in (
6.2
) depends only on
c_{0}>0
in the condition
x_{1},x_{2}\geq c_{0}
. Since
\mathcal{B}_{1\widetilde{2}}
is not Hermitian, (
6.2
) is not a direct consequence of (
6.1
). However, (
6.2
) follows by a explicit inversion of
\mathcal{B}_{1\widetilde{2}}
, see e.g.
[
13
, Lemma S2.8(1)]
.

To get an upper bound on the lhs. of (
6.2
) it suffices to get a lower bound on
\beta_{*}
. Since
|m_{j}|,|u_{j}|\lesssim 1
by
[
18
, Eq.(3.5)]
, we have
\beta_{*}\gtrsim|\beta_{+}\beta_{-}|
. We further focus on the analysis of the latter quantity, since it is more accessible than
\beta_{*}
. Let us immediately remove from consideration the regime when at least one of
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
is large. Indeed, by the definition of
u_{j}
(
3.9
),
|u_{j}|\lesssim\eta_{j}^{-1}
. Thus, there exists a (large) constant
C>0
such that if
\eta_{j}>C|z_{j}|
for at least one
j
, we have
\beta_{*}\sim 1
by (
6.1
).

Consider first the case when
w_{1},w_{2}
are purely imaginary.

Lemma 6.1

(
\beta_{+}\beta_{-}
on the imaginary axis)
.

Fix a (small)
c_{0}>0
and a (large)
L>0
. For
w_{j}=\pm\mathrm{i}\eta_{j}
it holds that

(6.3)

|\beta_{+}\beta_{-}|\gtrsim\rho_{2}^{2}|x_{2}-x_{1}|+|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}+\rho_{1}\eta_{1}+\rho_{2}\eta_{2},

uniformly in
x_{j}\in[c_{0},1]
,
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
,
|z_{j}|\leq L
and
0<\eta_{j}\leq L
. Additionally, in the regime
\sqrt{x_{j}}\leq|z_{1}|\leq L
,
j=1,2
, we have

(6.4)

|\beta_{+}\beta_{-}|\gtrsim\left(|z_{1}|^{2}-x_{1}\right)\left(|z_{2}|^{2}-x_{2}\right).

The proof of Lemma
6.1
is presented in Appendix
A.2
. In the case
x_{1}=x_{2}=1
, (
6.3
) was proven in
[
18
, Lemma 6.1]
in a slightly weaker form in terms of
\rho_{j}
. Compared to this result, (
6.3
) features a new effect, which is the dependence on
|x_{2}-x_{1}|
. In particular, for
|x_{2}-x_{1}|\sim 1
we have
\|\mathcal{B}_{1\widetilde{2}}^{-1}[R]\|\lesssim\|R\|
even for small
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
.

Recall that Proposition
3.4
is stated for
w_{1},w_{2}
not only on the imaginary axis, but in a narrow cone around it. Thus, we further consider the perturbative regime when
w_{1},w_{2}
are close to the imaginary axis.

Lemma 6.2

(
\beta_{+}\beta_{-}
in the perturbative regime)
.

Fix a (small)
c_{0}>0
and a (large)
L>0
. For
w_{j}=E_{j}\pm\mathrm{i}\eta_{j}
it holds that

(6.5)

\beta_{+}\beta_{-}(w_{1},w_{2})=\beta_{+}\beta_{-}(\mathrm{i}\Im w_{1},\mathrm{i}\Im w_{2})+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{|E_{1}|}{\eta_{1}}+\frac{|E_{2}|}{\eta_{2}}\right),

uniformly in
x_{1},x_{2}\in[c_{0},1]
,
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
,
|z_{j}|\leq L
and
w_{j}=E_{j}\pm\mathrm{i}\eta_{j}
with
-L\leq E_{j}\leq L
,
0<\eta_{j}\leq L
,
j=1,2
. Here by
\beta_{+}\beta_{-}(w_{1},w_{2})
we abbreviated the dependence of
\beta_{+}\beta_{-}
on
w_{1},w_{2}
, while the parameters
z_{1},z_{2}
are the same for both
\beta_{+}\beta_{-}
terms in (
6.5
).

The proof of Lemma
6.2
is presented in Appendix
A.2
. Now we are ready to proceed to the proof of the local law (
3.24
).

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

To prove the local law we rely on the zigzag strategy (see the references below Proposition
3.4
). Our proof is analogous to the proof of averaged two-resolvent averaged local laws
[
22
, Eq.(3.26)]
and
[
20
, Eq.(3.16)]
, with only minor modifications that we now explain. In fact, the current proof is simpler compared to the proof of
[
22
, Eq.(3.26)]
and
[
20
, Eq.(3.16)]
for two reasons. First, we now focus on the bulk of the spectrum. Though Proposition
3.4
also covers the complementary regime, it is not done optimally, as it is not needed for the proof of our main results. Second, we do not keep track of the additional smallness coming from the parameter
\gamma_{s}
, using the notation therein, which gives a smaller error when
z_{1},z_{2}
are away from each other. The parameter
\gamma_{s}
, which can be thought as an analog of the rhs. of (
6.3
) for
x=y=1
, determines an improved local law bound in terms of the decay
|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{-2}
(cf.
[
22
, Eq.(3.26)]
with (
3.24
)), i.e. further away
z_{1},z_{2}
give an improvement in the local law bound compared to the case
z_{1}=z_{2}
.

From now on we assume that
\theta\sim 1
in Proposition
3.4
, i.e. that
z_{i}
is either in the bulk regime or well outside of the non-Hermitian spectrum, for
i=1,2
. In the case when
\theta
vanishes with
N
, one can see that the estimates below deteriorate polynomially in
\theta
, which is affordable due to the
\theta
-dependence of the rhs. of (
3.24
). Recall the notations from Section
3.2
. When both parameters
z_{1},z_{2}
satisfy
|z_{i}|\geq\sqrt{x_{i}}+\theta
, then
|\beta_{+}\beta_{-}|\sim 1
by (
6.4
) for
w_{1},w_{2}
on the imaginary axis. Meanwhile, Lemma
6.2
extends this estimate to a narrow cone
|\Re w_{j}|\leq c\eta_{j}
,
j=1,2
, for some small
c>0
. Therefore,
\|\mathcal{B}_{1\widetilde{2}}^{-1}[R]\|\lesssim\|R\|
for any
R\in\mathbf{C}^{(2k_{1})\times(2k_{1})}
, and the result in (
3.24
) immediately follows by a global law as in
[
17
, Appendix B]
. In the case when
|z_{i}|\leq\sqrt{x_{i}}-\theta
and
|z_{j}|\geq\sqrt{x_{j}}+\theta
for
\{i,j\}=\{1,2\}
, the argument is similar and relies on (
6.3
) instead of (
6.4

for any fixed

and then used that
F_{1},F_{2}
and
\alpha
-Hölder regular in the first variable. Summing up (

A.2.
Two-body stability analysis

In this section we collect the proofs of Lemma

, Lemma

and (

). We often use the following asymptotics of

\rho^{z}_{x}(\mathrm{i}\eta)\sim\begin{cases}(x-|z|^{2})^{1/2}+\eta^{1/3},&|z|\leq\sqrt{x},\\

Proof of Lemma

.

Consider the product of

defined in (

x_{1}|\Im m_{2}|^{2}u_{1}\frac{1-x_{1}u_{2}}{u_{2}}=x_{1}|\Im m_{2}|^{2}u_{1}\frac{1-x_{2}u_{2}}{u_{2}}+x_{1}(x_{2}-x_{1})|\Im m_{2}|^{2}u_{1}\sim\rho_{2}\eta_{2}+\rho_{2}^{2}(x_{2}-x_{1}).

This finishes the proof of (
6.3
).

To prove (
6.4
), consider

for

. We estimate the second term in the rhs. of (

(1-x_{1}u_{1})(1-x_{1}u_{2})\geq(1-x_{1}u_{1})(1-x_{2}u_{2})\sim\frac{\eta_{1}\eta_{2}}{(\eta_{1}+\rho_{1})(\eta_{2}+\rho_{2})}\gtrsim\left(|z_{1}|^{2}-x_{1}\right)\left(|z_{2}|^{2}-x_{2}\right),

where in the last bound we used the second line of (

6.1

∎

Proof of Lemma

The explicit formula (

\left\lVert\widehat{M}_{j}-M_{j}\right\rVert\lesssim\frac{|E_{j}|}{\eta_{j}},\quad M_{j}:=M_{x_{j}}^{z_{j}}(\mathrm{i}\eta_{j}),\quad\widehat{M}_{j}:=M_{x_{j}}^{z_{j}}(E_{j}+\mathrm{i}\eta_{j}),

Proof of Lemma

For simplicity, we consider only the case

∎

Proof of Lemma

We start with the proof of (

\begin{split}x_{1}\left|\langle G_{1}B_{1}\widetilde{G}_{2}B_{2}\rangle\right|=&(2N)^{-1}\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left[G_{1}B_{1}P^{*}G_{2}PB_{2}\right]\right|\\

A.4.
Proof of Wick’s Theorem

In this section we prove Theorem
2.2
in full generality for any joint moments of centered linear statistics. Recall that in Section
4
we have already established this result for the second order moments. For simplicity of presentation we focus on the complex case. The real case requires only minor adjustments similar to the ones in Section
5.3
and thus is omitted.

From Proposition
3.3
we have that

(A.41)

\mathbf{E}\langle G_{1}-\mathbf{E}G_{1}\rangle\langle G_{2}-\mathbf{E}G_{2}\rangle=\mathcal{M}\big((x_{1},\eta_{1},z_{1}),(x_{2},\eta_{2},z_{2})\big)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{N^{\xi}}{\sqrt{N\eta_{*}}(N^{2}\eta_{1}\eta_{2})}\right),

where
\mathcal{M}
stands for the rhs. of (
3.15
) (without the error term):

(A.42)

\mathcal{M}\big((x_{1},\eta_{1},z_{1}),(x_{2},\eta_{2},z_{2})\big):=\frac{1}{2x_{1}x_{2}N^{2}}\left(V_{12}+\kappa_{4}x_{1}^{2}U_{1}U_{2}\right),\quad\text{for}\quad x_{1}\leq x_{2},

and for
x_{1}>x_{2}
the variables
(x_{1},\eta_{1},z_{1})
and
(x_{2},\eta_{2},z_{2})
in the rhs. of (
A.42
) are interchanged. We point out that the error term in (
3.15
) is actually better than the one in (
A.41
). Here, and throughout this section, we use this worse error for notational shortness and simplicity, as it will not affect our final result. We now show that the
\langle G_{i}-\mathbf{E}G_{i}\rangle
are asymptotically jointly Gaussian.

Proposition A.2

(Wick’s Theorem)
.

Assume the set-up and conditions of Proposition
3.3
with the only differences that we additionally fix
n\in\mathbf{N}
, consider
j\in[n]
instead of
j\in[2]
and do not assume that
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
. It holds that

(A.43)

\mathbf{E}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\langle G_{i}-\mathbf{E}G_{i}\rangle=\sum_{P\in\Pi_{n}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in P}\!\!\mathcal{M}_{ij}+\mathcal{O}_{\theta}\left(\frac{N^{\xi}}{\sqrt{N\eta_{*}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{N\eta_{i}}\right),\quad\mathcal{M}_{ij}:=\mathcal{M}\big((x_{i},\eta_{i},z_{i}),(x_{j},\eta_{j},z_{j})\big),

uniformly in the same sense as explained below (
3.15
). Here
\Pi_{n}
denotes the set of all pairings of
[n]
. We use the convention that
\Pi_{n}=\emptyset
if
n
is odd.

Using Proposition
A.2
we conclude the proof of Theorem
2.2
in the same way as (
3.1
) was derived from Proposition
3.3
in Section
4
. Thus, it remains to prove Proposition
A.2
.

Proof of Proposition
A.2
.

To keep the notation simpler, we assume that
\theta\sim 1
, which is equivalent to the assumption
||z_{i}|-\sqrt{x_{i}}|\gtrsim 1
,
i\in[n]
. It can be readily seen from the proof that all bounds will deteriorate only by a factor
\theta^{-D}
for some large fixed
D>0
, when considering
||z_{i}|-\sqrt{x_{i}}|\geq\theta
.

Applying (
5.4
) to
G_{i}
and using the single-resolvent local law (
3.13
) to estimate the first term in the rhs. of (
5.4
), we get

(A.44)

\langle G_{i}-\mathbf{E}G_{i}\rangle=-\langle\underline{W_{i}G_{i}}A_{i}\rangle+\mathbf{E}\langle\underline{W_{i}G_{i}}A_{i}\rangle+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{N^{\xi}}{(N\eta_{i})^{2}}\right),\qquad A_{i}:=\frac{M_{i}}{1-x_{i}\langle M_{i}^{2}\rangle}.

Here we additionally used that
\|A_{i}\|\lesssim 1
in our regime of interest by (
5.6
), and recalled the notation
W_{i}:=W^{(k_{i})}
. We now proceed by induction on
n
. For
n=1
(
A.43
) trivially follows from (
3.13
), while the case
n=2
is covered by Proposition
3.3
. Now, fix an even
n\in\mathbf{N}
and assume that (
A.43
) holds if the number of resolvents is at most
n-2
. Set
L\in\{n-1,n\}
and consider the product
\prod_{i\in[L]}\langle G_{i}-\mathbf{E}G_{i}\rangle
. We label the resolvents in such a way that
x_{1}
is the smallest of all
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
. Applying (
A.44
) to the first factor and performing the cumulant expansion in
\langle\underline{W_{1}G_{1}}A_{1}\rangle
similarly to (
5.14
), we get that this product equals to

(A.45)

\mathbf{E}\sum_{ab}\!\left(\!\!-\frac{1}{N}\langle\Delta^{ab}G_{1}A_{1}\rangle\partial_{ab}\!-\!\sum_{l=2}^{R}\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\{ab,ba\}^{l}}\!\frac{\kappa(ab,{\bm{\alpha}})}{l!N^{(l+1)/2}}\partial_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\langle\Delta^{ab}G_{1}A_{1}\rangle\,\cdot\,)+\mathbf{E}\langle\underline{W_{1}G_{1}}A_{1}\rangle\right)\!\!\prod_{i=2}^{L}\langle G_{i}-\mathbf{E}G_{i}\rangle,

up to the additive error term
\mathcal{O}(N^{-D})
, for any fixed
D>0
and for sufficiently large fixed
R\in\mathbf{N}
. In (
A.45
) the first summation runs over
a,b\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
defined in (
5.11
). Additionally, for any function
f(W)
in (
A.45
) we introduced the notation

\partial_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\langle\Delta^{ab}G_{1}A_{1}\rangle\,\cdot\,)f(W):=\partial_{{\bm{\alpha}}}\big(\langle\Delta^{ab}G_{1}A_{1}\rangle f(W)\big).

The first term in (
A.45
) comes from the second order cumulant contribution in the cumulant expansion. Note that the derivative
\partial_{ab}
hits only the product of
\langle G_{i}-\mathbf{E}G_{i}\rangle
,
i\in[2,L]
, but not
G_{1}
. Meanwhile, the terms in the
l
-summation come from higher order cumulants, and the derivatives there may hit any
G_{i}
including
G_{1}
. As we will shortly see, after all differentiations in (
A.45
) are performed, the second order cumulant term gives rise to the quantities of the form
V_{1j}
(see the second line of (
3.16
) with 2 replaced by
j
), and the fourth order terms (
l=3
) yield
U_{1}U_{j}
, the rest is negligible.

It can be easily seen (see e.g.
[
18
, Section 6]
) that all the terms in the
l
-summation in (
A.45
) with
l\neq 3
are bounded by

\mathcal{E}_{[L]}:=\frac{N^{\xi}}{\sqrt{N\eta_{*}}}\prod_{i=1}^{L}\frac{1}{N\eta_{i}},

and so negligible. This is a consequence of the fact that in all these terms it appears at least an off-diagonal term of the resolvent which is small by the isotropic local law in Theorem
3.2
. For the same reason the only term for
l=3
which is not negligible is when all the resolvent entries are diagonal. We are thus left with (see (
5.9
) for the first term below and (
5.17
) for the second)

(A.46)

\begin{split}\eqref{eq:cumexpw}=&-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4N^{2}}\mathbf{E}\sum_{p=2}^{L}\frac{1}{x_{p}}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\{\pm\}}\sigma\partial_{\eta_{p}}\left\langle G_{1}A_{1}E_{\sigma}^{(k_{1})}\widetilde{G}_{p}E_{\sigma}^{(k_{1})}\right\rangle\right)\mathbf{E}\prod_{i=2\atop i\neq p}^{L}\langle G_{i}-\mathbf{E}G_{i}\rangle\\
&-\frac{1}{2x_{1}N}\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}\frac{\kappa_{4}}{N^{2}}\mathbf{E}\partial_{ba}[(G_{1}A_{1})_{ba}]\partial_{ab}\partial_{ba}\left[\langle G_{p}\rangle-\mathbf{E}\langle G_{p}\rangle\right]\mathbf{E}\prod_{i=2\atop i\neq p}^{L}\langle G_{i}-\mathbf{E}G_{i}\rangle+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathcal{E}_{[L]}\right).\end{split}

Here we denoted
\widetilde{G}_{p}:=P_{p}^{*}G_{p}P_{p}
, where
P_{p}
is defined as in (
3.17
), but with
k_{2}
replaced by
k_{p}
. The index set
\mathcal{J}_{1}
is defined in (
5.11
). In (
A.46
) we also used that the term for
l=3
when all the three derivatives hit
\langle\Delta^{ab}G_{1}A_{1}\rangle
(which consists of all diagonal resolvent entries) is cancelled by the analogous term in cumulant expansion of
\mathbf{E}\langle\underline{W_{1}G_{1}}A_{1}\rangle
. In fact, all the terms when the derivatives hit only
\langle\Delta^{ab}G_{1}A_{1}\rangle
are cancelled by their counterpart in
\mathbf{E}\langle\underline{W_{1}G_{1}}A_{1}\rangle
.

Finally, proceeding analogously to the computations of Section
5
, we compute that the rhs. of (
A.46
) equals to

(A.47)

\sum_{p=2}^{n}\mathcal{M}_{1p}\,\mathbf{E}\prod_{i=2\atop i\neq p}^{L}\langle G_{i}-\mathbf{E}G_{i}\rangle+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathcal{E}_{[L]}\right).

Since the number of resolvents in (
A.47
) is at most
n-2
, we use the inductive hypothesis and finish the proof of (
A.43
).
∎
