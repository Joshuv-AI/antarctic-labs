Kac’s walk on rotation matrices mixes in n 2 ​ log ⁡ n n^{2}\log n steps
Natesh S. Pillai, Aaron Smith

Abstract
. Kac’s walk on SO ⁡ ( n ) \mathrm{SO}(n) , introduced in [ 12 ] , is an important high-dimensional Markov chain with applications in statistical physics, statistics, cryptography, and computational sciences. Despite its simple transition rules, determining its total-variation mixing time has remained a challenging problem for decades. A key obstacle is that the walk is not conjugation-invariant, placing it beyond the reach of classical Fourier-analytic techniques that apply to many related random walks on compact groups. We prove that Kac’s walk mixes in total variation in O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n^{2}\log n) steps, matching the conjectured mixing time up to constants. The proof is based on a refined two-stage coupling. Building on our earlier work [ 42 ] , the first stage contracts two copies of the chain to a small neighborhood via a Wasserstein coupling defined and analyzed in [ 38 ] . Our main contribution is a new framework for analyzing the second-stage coupling. It can be viewed as a discrete analogue of Malliavin calculus for Markov chains. We represent the law of the chain as the pushforward of high-dimensional noise and prove quantitative non-degeneracy of the associated linearization using matrix martingale methods. This yields an approximately Gaussian distribution in the Lie algebra with well-conditioned covariance, allowing small group translations to be absorbed at negligible cost in total variation. Our approach provides a new general framework for studying mixing in high dimensional Markov chains in continuous state space with singular transition kernels.

1.
Introduction

Introduced by Kac in the 1950s, Kac’s random walk on the sphere is one of the canonical stochastic models for the Boltzmann equation
[
23
,
34
]
. The analogous walk on the special orthogonal group
\mathrm{SO}(n)
was defined in the seminal paper introducing Markov chain Monte Carlo to statistics, where it was used as the most-developed sample algorithm
[
12
]
. Although the modern theory of mixing times had not been developed when
[
12
]
was written, the last sentence of that paper asks essentially the same question that we study in this paper: how dependent are widely-spaced samples from Kac’s walk, and how does this relate to computational efficiency?

Since 1970, Kac-type dynamics have become a natural test case for a basic question that appears across probability, statistics, and computation: how rapidly can a high-dimensional constrained system be randomized using only local low-dimensional updates?
The simplicity of the dynamics is an asset when using the walk as an algorithm: it makes both single-step computations (as in
[
12
]
) and downstream applications (as in
[
44
,
22
,
46
]
) faster. The same simplicity, however, makes the walk harder to analyze than superficially similar walks on the same group
[
14
,
39
,
43
]
. Kac-like dynamics have continued to be used in Bayesian statistics
[
3
]
, and are important in developing Gibbs samplers for distributions with hard constraints
[
38
,
42
]
and especially orthogonality constraints
[
19
]
. More recently, the same dynamics have been used as steps in other algorithms, including fast dimension reduction
[
1
,
22
]
, trapdoored pseudorandom matrices in cryptography
[
44
]
, parallel Kac-type constructions in quantum pseudorandomness
[
29
,
30
]
, other applications in machine learning
[
46
]
, and as a tool to prove stronger results in other areas of probability
[
37
]
.

Let
\mathrm{SO}(n)
denote the group of
n\times n
rotation matrices and let
N=\binom{n}{2}
be the dimension of
\mathrm{SO}(n)
. Fix an ordering of the
N
coordinate pairs

[n]_{\neq}=\{(a,b):1\leq a<b\leq n\}.

For
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, let
R(i,\theta)
denote the rotation by angle
\theta
in the coordinate plane associated with the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
element of
[n]_{\neq}
. The chain
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
evolves by

(1.1)

X_{t+1}=R(i_{t},\theta_{t})X_{t},

where
i_{t}
is uniform on
\{1,\dots,N\}
and
\theta_{t}
is uniform on
[0,2\pi)
. Thus each step acts in only one two-dimensional coordinate plane, while the stationary distribution is Haar measure on a manifold of dimension
N
. The closely-related walk on the sphere is obtained by taking the first column of
X_{t}
; this is also a Markov chain.

The total variation mixing time for Kac’s walk on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
has been a long standing open problem. The best bound to date,

\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}=O(n^{4}\log n),

was obtained in
[
42
, Theorem 1.1]
. Combined with the
\Omega(n^{2})
lower bound from
[
42
]
, this left a factor
n^{2}
gap in the main quantitative question for the chain. In this paper, we close this gap and prove an upper bound that is tight up to a factor of
O(\log(n))
:

Theorem 1
.

The total-variation mixing time of Kac’s walk on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
satisfies

\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}=O(n^{2}\log n).

A nearly-matching lower bound of
\Omega(n^{2})
can be obtained by dimension-counting:
\mathrm{SO}(n)
has dimension
N
, while each step is constrained to a union of 1-dimensional submanifolds. We conjecture that our upper bound of
O(n^{2}\log(n))
is tight.

1.1.
Our contribution and its connection to Malliavin calculus

There is a large literature on bounding the mixing times of Markov chains in many contexts. The theory is best developed for chains on discrete spaces
[
27
]
, while in continuous state spaces the best available results are, as noted in
[
26
]
, “very far from being as nice as in the finite case”; see also the discussions in
[
6
,
28
,
7
]
.

A key difficulty in continuous state spaces is the lack of a general mechanism for upgrading convergence in weaker metrics to total variation. In contrast, in discrete settings this upgrade typically follows from a spectral gap bound at logarithmic cost in the inverse of the smallest stationary probability. For Markov chains like Kac’s walk that make low-dimensional updates, it is well-known that knowledge of the highest eigenvalue “does not improve the mixing time” in general
[
40
]
. Previous work on these upgrades has either taken advantage of symmetries that apply to very narrow families of Markov chains
[
43
,
39
,
14
]
or has effectively sought a “warm start” (introduced in
[
31
]
) via truncation or perturbation estimates
[
21
,
42
]
.

The main difficulty in proving total-variation mixing for Kac’s walk is not the absence of
contraction. Wasserstein contraction is already known at the conjectural time scale by
Oliveira’s coupling
[
38
]
. The difficulty is that the transition kernel is singular.
At each step the chain moves only along a one-dimensional family of rotations, while the
state space
\mathrm{SO}(n)
has dimension
N=\binom{n}{2}
. Thus, for short and moderate times,
the usual smoothing mechanisms available for Markov chains with everywhere positive
transition densities are absent. One must instead understand how many singular, highly
structured updates combine to create a distribution that is spread in all tangent directions,
and one must do so quantitatively enough to compare nearby translates in total variation.

This problem is closely analogous to the classical problem of hypoellipticity.
Consider the Stratonovich stochastic differential equation

(1.2)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=V_{0}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{r}V_{\alpha}(X_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{\alpha},

on a smooth manifold
M
, where
V_{0},V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
are smooth vector
fields and
W^{\alpha}
are independent Brownian motions. Its generator is

\mathcal{L}=V_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{r}V_{\alpha}^{2}.

Even when the noise vector fields
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
fail to span the tangent space pointwise, their iterated Lie brackets can generate the missing directions. Hörmander’s theorem states that if, for every
x\in M
, the Lie algebra generated by
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
together with their commutators with the drift
V_{0}
spans the full tangent space
T_{x}M
, then the associated diffusion has smooth transition densities; equivalently,
\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L}
is hypoelliptic
[
16
,
11
]
.

Malliavin calculus gives a probabilistic route to obtaining this regularity

[
32
,
36
,
11
,
24
,
25
,
35
]
.
Differentiation of the solution map
X_{t}
with respect to the driving noise yields a
linearized flow. If
J_{s,t}:T_{X_{s}}M\to T_{X_{t}}M
denotes this flow, then, in
local coordinates,

\mathrm{d}J_{s,t}=DV_{0}(X_{t})J_{s,t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{r}DV_{\alpha}(X_{t})J_{s,t}\circ\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{\alpha},\qquad J_{s,s}=\mathrm{Id}.

The Malliavin derivative of the endpoint in the
\alpha
-th noise direction at
time
s
is

D_{s}^{\alpha}X_{t}=J_{s,t}V_{\alpha}(X_{s}),\qquad 0\leq s\leq t.

The associated Malliavin covariance matrix is

(1.3)

\mathcal{M}_{t}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{r}\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(J_{s,t}V_{\alpha}(X_{s})\bigr)\bigl(J_{s,t}V_{\alpha}(X_{s})\bigr)^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Non-degeneracy of
\mathcal{M}_{t}
means that the injected Brownian noise has
reached every tangent direction by time
t
. Under Hörmander’s condition, it turns
out that
\mathcal{M}_{t}
is invertible, and Malliavin integration by parts
then converts this covariance information into regularity of the law; see, for
example, Equation 3.4 of
[
11
]
. The classical scheme is
therefore to differentiate the endpoint with respect to the noise, establish
non-degeneracy of the resulting covariance, and then use integration by parts to
deduce regularity.

Our argument follows a similar overall pattern, but in a discrete, finite-dimensional setting tailored to Markov chains with singular transition kernels. In place of a Brownian path and an infinitesimal generator satisfying the celebrated Hörmander bracket condition, the randomness enters through the sequence of coordinate planes and rotation angles. We introduce a small Gaussian perturbation of the angles in a fresh block of updates, which plays the role of the driving noise in this setting.

More precisely, after a first-stage Wasserstein coupling due to Oliveira

[
38
]
, two copies of the walk can be brought within a very small
Hilbert–Schmidt distance after
O(N\log n)
steps, with high probability.
This produces closeness, but not coalescence. To upgrade this to total-variation
overlap, we expose a fresh block

A=((i_{1},\theta_{1}),\ldots,(i_{m},\theta_{m})),\qquad m=\lceil C_{1}N\log N\rceil,

and perturb all angles in this block by an independent Gaussian vector

\delta=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{m})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2}I_{m}).

Adding such a perturbation preserves the marginal law of Kac’s walk, because the
base angles are uniform modulo
2\pi
. Thus the perturbation gives us a source
of smoothing without changing the Markov chain.

The endpoint perturbation is encoded by

H_{A}(\delta)=L_{A}(\delta)L_{A}(0)^{-1},\qquad u_{A}(\delta)=\log H_{A}(\delta)\in\mathfrak{so}(n),

where
L_{A}(\delta)
is the product of the perturbed rotations. The map

u_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathfrak{so}(n)

is the finite-dimensional analogue of the endpoint map in Malliavin calculus.
Its derivative at the origin,

J_{A}=\mathrm{d}u_{A}(0),

plays the role of the Malliavin derivative, and

M_{A}=J_{A}J_{A}^{\top}

plays the role of the Malliavin covariance matrix.

The key estimate in the paper is a quantitative non-degeneracy bound for
M_{A}
.
The columns of
J_{A}
are transported coordinate-plane directions. Thus
M_{A}

is a sum of rank-one projections onto these transported directions, directly
mirroring the classical covariance formula
(
1.3
). The important new observation is that,
after conditioning on the future of the block and reversing time, these rank-one
terms have a matrix martingale difference structure. Tropp’s matrix Freedman
inequality then gives, with high probability,

\frac{m}{N}I_{N}\preceq M_{A}\preceq 3\frac{m}{N}I_{N}.

This is the discrete analogue of non-degeneracy of the Malliavin covariance:
although each update is one-dimensional, the transported update directions spread
across all
N=\dim\mathrm{SO}(n)
tangent directions over a block of length

O(N\log N)
.

Once this covariance estimate is available, the remainder of the second stage is
perturbative. On the scale of the Gaussian perturbation, the logarithmic map has
the expansion

u_{A}(\delta)=J_{A}\delta+r_{A}(\delta),

with a quadratic remainder. Hence the law of
u_{A}(\delta)
is close in total
variation to a non-degenerate Gaussian measure on
\mathfrak{so}(n)
with covariance

\sigma_{n}^{2}M_{A}
. Small right translations of the group then become, in
logarithmic coordinates, small shifts of this Gaussian together with a controlled
Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff error. Since the first-stage coupling has already
made the two chains
N^{-4}
-close, the resulting translation cost is
o(1)
.

This perspective also explains the improvement over our previous work

[
42
]
. There, the second stage perturbed only
N
carefully
chosen updates and required a delicate lower bound on the smallest singular value
of a square random Jacobian. Here we perturb all updates in a shorter block of
length
O(N\log N)
. The resulting Jacobian is rectangular, but its covariance
has a clean martingale structure, making the random-matrix analysis substantially
more tractable.

Although several estimates in the present paper use special features of Kac’s
walk, the general mechanism is more flexible; see Remark
1
.
Therefore we expect this point of view
to be useful for other high-dimensional Markov chains on continuous state spaces
with singular transition kernels.

Finally, our proof is conceptually related to the mechanism behind ergodicity for
stochastic systems with degenerate forcing, as in
[
15
]
. In
both settings, randomness is injected only in a restricted set of directions, and
one studies how the dynamics transports infinitesimal perturbations of the random
input. In
[
15
]
, this leads to asymptotic smoothing despite
a genuinely degenerate Malliavin matrix. In our finite-dimensional setting, the
same philosophy yields a non-degenerate Gaussian approximation in logarithmic
coordinates.

1.2.
Related Work

Much of the early work on mixing times of Markov chains focused on random walks on groups
[
9
,
2
]
. For conjugation-invariant random walks on compact groups, powerful Fourier-analytic methods are available, and in several cases they lead to sharp total-variation estimates and cutoff phenomena
[
8
]
, including for random walks on groups that are similar to Kac’s walk
[
43
,
39
,
14
]
. Kac’s walk on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
is different: its transition rule is tied to the ambient coordinate axes and is not conjugation invariant, so the classical representation-theoretic approach does not apply directly.

The first line of work on Kac’s walks studied their spectral gaps. For the walk on the sphere, Janvresse identified the spectral gap up to constants
[
17
]
. For the walk on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
, Diaconis and Saloff-Coste gave the first bounds that were polynomial in
n
using a comparison argument, and Janvresse showed that the spectral gap is of order
n^{-2}

[
10
,
18
]
. The exact spectral gap for both the sphere walk and the
\mathrm{SO}(n)
walk was then identified by
[
5
]
, and
[
33
]
computed the full spectrum;
[
4
]
later gave a streamlined and more general spectral-gap argument for Kac-type collision processes. Even with exact computations of the spectral gap and even the full spectrum, the lack of conjugacy-invariance meant that it was difficult to obtain bounds on the mixing time in total variation. As illustration, the first paper to obtain strong estimates of the spectral gap also gave the first bounds on the mixing time of the walk, but was unable to get a bound stronger than
e^{O(n^{2})}

[
10
]
. Despite the full characterization of the spectrum in the following decade, this estimate was not improved to a polynomial mixing bound until
[
21
]
.

In parallel to work on estimating the spectral gaps of Kac’s walks, there was work on estimating their mixing time in stronger metrics. On the sphere, the first polynomial upper bound on the mixing time was computed in
[
20
]
, and the correct mixing time was computed by the present authors in
[
41
]
. On
\mathrm{SO}(n)
, The first strong result was due to Pak and Sidenko, who proved an
O(n^{5/2}\log n)
weak-convergence bound
[
40
]
. Oliveira later showed that the walk mixes in Wasserstein distance in
O(n^{2}\log n)
steps, within a logarithmic factor of optimal
[
38
]
. The first polynomial upper bound on the mixing time was computed in
[
21
]
, and the present authors later improved this to
O(n^{4}\log n)

[
42
]
. The present note improves the estimate to
O(n^{2}\log n)
, which is conjectured to be the correct answer.

There is also substantial work on closely related random walks on compact groups. Rosenthal analyzed random fixed-angle rotations on
\mathrm{SO}(N)
using character theory, Porod studied the random-reflection walk, and Hough and Jiang proved cutoff for the uniform-plane Kac walk, a conjugation-invariant variant in which the rotation plane is sampled uniformly from the Grassmannian
[
43
,
39
,
14
]
.

Geometry

Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{m}
denote the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{m}

\mathrm{d}F(x)[u]

They form a basis of the tangent space

For

\mathrm{Ad}(g)u=gug^{-1}

\mathrm{SO}(n)

\mathfrak{so}(n)

Since the ambient Hilbert–Schmidt norm of a differentiable curve is bounded by its speed,

\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq D_{\mathrm{HS}}(x,y)

Lemma 1

There exist universal constants

3.
Two-stage coupling

3.1.
The first-stage coupling

The first stage of our two-stage coupling will be based on the following proposition, which is essentially an immediate corollary of the main result of
[
38
]
:

Proposition 1

(Contractive coupling)
.

Fix
B>0
.
There exists
C_{0}(B)<\infty
such that for every
x,y\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
one can couple two copies

(X_{t},Y_{t})
of Kac’s walk with
X_{0}=x
and
Y_{0}=y
so that, for all

(3.1)

t_{0}\geq C_{0}(B)\,N\log n,

we have

(3.2)

\mathbb{P}\!\bigl(D_{\mathrm{HS}}(X_{t_{0}},Y_{t_{0}})>N^{-B}\bigr)\leq N^{-2}.

Proof.

Theorem 1 of
[
38
]
states that, for all
\varepsilon>0
, all
t\geq\bigl\lceil n^{2}\log(\pi\sqrt{n}/\varepsilon)\bigr\rceil
and all
z\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
,

W_{D_{\mathrm{HS}},2}\bigl(K^{t}(z,\cdot),\mu\bigr)\leq\varepsilon.

Applying this with
\varepsilon=\tfrac{1}{2}N^{-B-1}
shows that there exists
C_{0}(B)
such that for
t_{0}
as in
(
3.1
) and all
z\in SO(n)

W_{D_{\mathrm{HS}},2}\bigl(K^{t_{0}}(z,\cdot),\mu\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{2}N^{-B-1}.

Therefore, for all
x,y\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
,

\displaystyle W_{D_{\mathrm{HS}},2}\bigl(K^{t_{0}}(x,\cdot),K^{t_{0}}(y,\cdot)\bigr)\leq W_{D_{\mathrm{HS}},2}\bigl(

\displaystyle K^{t_{0}}(x,\cdot),\mu\bigr)

\displaystyle+W_{D_{\mathrm{HS}},2}\bigl(\mu,K^{t_{0}}(y,\cdot)\bigr)\leq N^{-B-1}.

By the definition of Wasserstein distance, there exists a coupling of
(X_{t_{0}},Y_{t_{0}})
with

\mathbb{E}\bigl[D_{\mathrm{HS}}(X_{t_{0}},Y_{t_{0}})^{2}\bigr]\leq N^{-2B-2}.

Markov’s inequality then gives

\mathbb{P}\!\bigl(D_{\mathrm{HS}}(X_{t_{0}},Y_{t_{0}})>N^{-B}\bigr)\leq N^{2B}\mathbb{E}\bigl[D_{\mathrm{HS}}(X_{t_{0}},Y_{t_{0}})^{2}\bigr]\leq N^{-2},

which is (
3.2
) and we are done.
∎

3.2.
Second stage coupling

We now define our second stage of the coupling that enables the Wasserstein closeness from this first
stage to total variation.
The key idea is to represent the endpoint of the chain as a
perturbation of a fixed block of updates
, and to study this perturbation in logarithmic coordinates.

Definition 1
.

(Perturbation of Kac’s walk)
Fix
m=\lceil C_{1}N\log N\rceil
as in (
2.1
), and let

(3.3)

A=\bigl((i_{1},\theta_{1}),\dots,(i_{m},\theta_{m})\bigr)

4.
Proof sketch of the second stage coupling

We now summarize the mechanism that upgrades the Wasserstein contraction from
Proposition

to total-variation overlap. Starting from arbitrary

, Proposition
1
gives a
coupling such that after

steps,

with high probability. On this event, let

g=X_{t_{0}}^{-1}Y_{t_{0}}.

By bi-invariance of
D_{\mathrm{HS}}
and Lemma

, for all large
n
we
may write

g=\exp(h),\qquad\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq c_{\log}\omega_{n}.

Thus the second stage reduces to showing that a fresh block of updates can absorb
right multiplication by such a small element.

For the second stage, we expose an independent block

A=((i_{1},\theta_{1}),\dots,(i_{m},\theta_{m})),\qquad m=\lceil C_{1}N\log N\rceil,

and as described in Definition

, perturb all angles in the block by an independent Gaussian vector

\Delta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2}I_{m}).

Write

H_{A}(\delta)=L_{A}(\delta)L_{A}(0)^{-1},\qquad\nu_{A}=\mathcal{L}(H_{A}(\Delta)\mid A).

If

z=L_{A}(0)X_{t_{0}},

then Lemma
2
shows that the two second-stage conditional laws are

\mathcal{L}(L_{A}(\Delta)X_{t_{0}}\mid A)=(T_{z})_{\#}\nu_{A},\qquad\mathcal{L}(L_{A}(\Delta)Y_{t_{0}}\mid A)=(T_{zg})_{\#}\nu_{A}.

Since total variation is invariant under right translation and the adjoint action
preserves the Hilbert–Schmidt norm, it is enough to prove that

\|\nu_{A}-(T_{\exp(h)})_{\#}\nu_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=o(1)

uniformly for
\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq c_{\log}\omega_{n}
.

To study
\nu_{A}
, we pass to logarithmic coordinates. On the high-probability

gives the local expansion

with a quadratic remainder. Thus
U_{A}
is well approximated by the linear term

J_{A}\Delta
. The covariance of this linear approximation is

M_{A}=J_{A}J_{A}^{\top}.

Proposition

shows that with high probability over the block,

\frac{m}{N}I_{N}\preceq M_{A}\preceq 3\frac{m}{N}I_{N}.

\gamma_{A}=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{A}),\qquad\Sigma_{A}=\sigma_{n}^{2}M_{A},

is non-degenerate in every tangent direction. The matrix

thus plays the role of the Malliavin covariance matrix. Proposition
5

then shows that

\|\mathcal{L}(U_{A}\mid A,E)-\gamma_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=o(1).

The final step is to transfer this Gaussian approximation on
\mathfrak{so}(n)
back to the group.
Proposition

shows that if a law on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
is the
exponential pushforward of a measure on

that is close to a centered

Combining the above, Proposition

then yields

\|\nu_{A}-(T_{\exp(h)})_{\#}\nu_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=o(1).

5.
The perturbation block and its linearization

For
\delta=(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\cdots,\delta_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
L_{A}(\delta)
defined in (
3.4
),
define the associated
relative perturbation

(5.1)

H_{A}(\delta)=L_{A}(\delta)(L_{A}(0))^{-1}.

Thus
H_{A}(\delta)
isolates the effect of a small perturbation of the driving randomness.
Write

(5.2)

P_{t+1}=R(i_{m},\theta_{m})R(i_{m-1},\theta_{m-1})\cdots R(i_{t+1},\theta_{t+1}),\qquad P_{m+1}=\mathrm{Id},

and define the transported basis vectors

(5.3)

v_{t}=\mathrm{Ad}(P_{t+1})a_{i_{t}}\in\mathfrak{so}(n).

Lemma 3
.

We have

(5.4)

H_{A}(\delta)=\prod_{t=m}^{1}\exp\!\bigl(\sqrt{2}\,\delta_{t}v_{t}\bigr).

Proof.

Write
B_{t}=R(i_{t},\theta_{t})
and

E_{t}=\exp(\sqrt{2}\,\delta_{t}a_{i_{t}})
. We have

R(i_{t},\theta_{t}+\delta_{t})=E_{t}B_{t}
and thus

H_{A}(\delta)=E_{m}B_{m}E_{m-1}B_{m-1}\cdots E_{1}B_{1}B_{1}^{-1}\cdots B_{m}^{-1}.

Regrouping the factors gives

H_{A}(\delta)=\prod_{t=m}^{1}P_{t+1}E_{t}P_{t+1}^{-1},\qquad P_{t+1}=B_{m}\cdots B_{t+1}.

Since conjugation commutes with the exponential map,

P_{t+1}E_{t}P_{t+1}^{-1}=\exp\!\bigl(\sqrt{2}\,\delta_{t}\,\mathrm{Ad}(P_{t+1})a_{i_{t}}\bigr)=\exp\!\bigl(\sqrt{2}\,\delta_{t}v_{t}\bigr),

which proves (
5.4
).
∎

Define the (principal) logarithm
u_{A}(\delta)
of
H_{A}(\delta)
and its derivative:

(5.5)

u_{A}(\delta)=\log H_{A}(\delta),\qquad J_{A}=\mathrm{d}u_{A}(0):\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathfrak{so}(n).

Lemma 4
.

The derivative
J_{A}
satisfies

(5.6)

J_{A}e_{t}=\sqrt{2}\,v_{t}=\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{Ad}(P_{t+1})a_{i_{t}},

and therefore

(5.7)

J_{A}\delta=\sqrt{2}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\delta_{t}v_{t}.

Proof.

Fix
t
and set
\delta=se_{t}
.
All factors in (
5.4
) are equal to the identity except the
t^{\mathrm{th}}
factor, so

H_{A}(se_{t})=\exp\!\bigl(\sqrt{2}\,sv_{t}\bigr).

For
s
near
0
, the principal logarithm of the right-hand side is
\sqrt{2}\,sv_{t}
.
Differentiating at
s=0
gives (
5.6
), and summing over coordinates gives
(
5.7
).
∎

Next, we use Lemma
4
to obtain good control on
u_{A}(\delta)
for sufficiently small perturbations:

Proposition 2
.

There exist absolute constants
c_{0},C_{0}>0
such that if

(5.8)

\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{m}},

the principal logarithm
u_{A}(\delta)=\log H_{A}(\delta)
is well defined. Furthermore,
whenever (
5.8
) holds,
u_{A}(\delta)
admits the decomposition:

(5.9)

u_{A}(\delta):=J_{A}\delta+r_{A}(\delta)

with
\left\lVert r_{A}(\delta)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq C_{0}m\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
and

(5.10)

\left\lVert\mathrm{d}u_{A}(\delta)-J_{A}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{0}m\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert_{2}.

Proof.

For
1\leq t\leq m
, set

E_{t}(s)=\exp\!\bigl(\sqrt{2}\,sv_{t}\bigr),

so that

H_{A}(\delta)=E_{m}(\delta_{m})\cdots E_{1}(\delta_{1}).

We first verify that the principal logarithm of
H_{A}(\delta)
is well-defined. Since
\left\lVert v_{t}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}=1
and
\left\lVert v_{t}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
,

\sum_{t=1}^{m}\left\lVert\sqrt{2}\,\delta_{t}v_{t}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sqrt{2}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\left\lvert\delta_{t}\right\rvert\leq\sqrt{2m}\,\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert_{2}.

If (
5.8
) holds and
c_{0}
is small enough, then

\sum_{t}\left\lVert\sqrt{2}\,\delta_{t}v_{t}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1/8
.
Therefore

(5.11)

\left\lVert H_{A}(\delta)-\mathrm{Id}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\exp\!\Bigl(\sum_{t=1}^{m}\left\lVert\sqrt{2}\,\delta_{t}v_{t}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\Bigr)-1\leq\frac{1}{4}.

Hence the principal logarithm is well defined; see
[
13
, Chapter 1]
.

We next bound the first and second derivatives of
H_{A}
.
Differentiating (
5.4
) gives

\partial_{j}H_{A}(\delta)=E_{m}(\delta_{m})\cdots E_{j+1}(\delta_{j+1})\,E_{j}^{\prime}(\delta_{j})\,E_{j-1}(\delta_{j-1})\cdots E_{1}(\delta_{1}).

Applying (
5.8
) and the fact that
\left\lVert E_{j}^{\prime}(s)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sqrt{2}\,e^{\sqrt{2}\left\lvert s\right\rvert}
, there exists a universal constant
C
such that for all
j

\left\lVert\partial_{j}H_{A}(\delta)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C.

Consequently, for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\mathrm{d}H_{A}(\delta)[\eta]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\eta_{j}\,\partial_{j}H_{A}(\delta),

so

\left\lVert\mathrm{d}H_{A}(\delta)[\eta]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lvert\eta_{j}\right\rvert\leq C\sqrt{m}\,\left\lVert\eta\right\rVert_{2}.

Therefore

(5.12)

\left\lVert\mathrm{d}H_{A}(\delta)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C\sqrt{m}.

Differentiating (
5.4
) once more, if
k\neq j
then
\partial_{k}\partial_{j}H_{A}(\delta)
is a product of the factors
E_{\ell}(\delta_{\ell})
in which the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
factor is replaced by
E_{j}^{\prime}(\delta_{j})
and the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
factor is replaced by
E_{k}^{\prime}(\delta_{k})
, while if
k=j
then the
j
th factor is replaced by
E_{j}^{\prime\prime}(\delta_{j})
. Since
\left\lVert E_{\ell}^{\prime}(s)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C
and
\left\lVert E_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(s)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C^{\prime}
on the ball defined by (
5.8
), there exists a universal constant
C^{\prime\prime}
such that, for all
j,k
,

\left\lVert\partial_{k}\partial_{j}H_{A}(\delta)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C^{\prime\prime}.

Therefore, for all
\eta,\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\left\lVert\mathrm{d}^{2}H_{A}(\delta)[\eta,\zeta]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C^{\prime\prime}\sum_{j,k=1}^{m}\left\lvert\eta_{j}\right\rvert\left\lvert\zeta_{k}\right\rvert\leq C^{\prime\prime}m\,\left\lVert\eta\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\zeta\right\rVert_{2}.

Hence

(5.13)

\left\lVert\mathrm{d}^{2}H_{A}(\delta)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C^{\prime\prime}m.

Let

\mathcal{U}=\{M\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R}):\left\lVert M-\mathrm{Id}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1/2\}.

The matrix logarithm is smooth on
\mathcal{U}
, so its first and second derivatives are bounded in
\mathcal{U}

by an absolute constant.
Combining this with (
5.11
), (
5.12
), and (
5.13
),
the chain rule yields

(5.14)

\sup_{\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert_{2}\leq c_{0}/\sqrt{m}}\left\lVert\mathrm{d}^{2}u_{A}(\delta)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{0}m

for some absolute constant
C_{0}
.

Recall that
u_{A}(0)=0
, and that by Lemma
4

\mathrm{d}u_{A}(0)=J_{A}
.
Taylor’s theorem gives

u_{A}(\delta)=J_{A}\delta+\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}u_{A}(s\delta)[\delta,\delta]\,\mathrm{d}s,

which together with (
5.14
) proves (
5.9
).
Bound (
5.14
) and the mean value theorem give

\left\lVert\mathrm{d}u_{A}(\delta)-J_{A}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sup_{\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{2}\leq c_{0}/\sqrt{m}}\left\lVert\mathrm{d}^{2}u_{A}(\xi)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\,\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert_{2}\leq C_{0}m\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert_{2},

which is (
5.10
) and the proof is finished.
∎

6.
Covariance and the random-matrix input

In Proposition
2
of Section
5
, we showed that the logarithmic map

u_{A}(\delta)=\log H_{A}(\delta)

admits the linear approximation

u_{A}(\delta)\approx J_{A}\delta,\quad J_{A}=\mathrm{d}u_{A}(0)

for small deterministic
\delta
. Define the matrix:

(6.1)

M_{A}=J_{A}J_{A}^{\top}=2\sum_{t=1}^{m}v_{t}v_{t}^{\top},\qquad v_{t}=\mathrm{Ad}(P_{t+1})a_{i_{t}}

where
P_{t}
is as defined in (
5.2
).
We show that
M_{A}
is well-conditioned with high probability by exploiting a conditional isotropy property: conditional on the updates after time
t
in the block, the randomness of
i_{t}
implies that
v_{t}v_{t}^{\top}
has conditional expectation
(1/N)I
. This yields a matrix martingale difference decomposition, to which we apply Tropp’s matrix Freedman inequality
[
45
, Theorem 1.2]
to control the spectrum of
M_{A}
.

Proposition 3
.

The event

(6.2)

\mathcal{G}_{A}=\left\{\frac{m}{N}\leq\lambda_{\min}(M_{A})\leq\lambda_{\max}(M_{A})\leq 3\frac{m}{N}\right\}

satisfies

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{A}^{c})=o(1),

where the probability is over the randomness of the update block

A=((i_{t},\theta_{t}))_{t=1}^{m}.

Proof.

We identify
\mathfrak{so}(n)
with
\mathbb{R}^{N}
through the ordered orthonormal basis

(a_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
. Recall from (
5.3
) that

v_{t}=\mathrm{Ad}(P_{t+1})a_{i_{t}}\in\mathfrak{so}(n),

and define

(6.3)

C_{A}=\sum_{t=1}^{m}v_{t}v_{t}^{\top}.

By Lemma
4
, we have

J_{A}e_{t}=\sqrt{2}\,v_{t},

and therefore

(6.4)

M_{A}=J_{A}J_{A}^{\top}=2\sum_{t=1}^{m}v_{t}v_{t}^{\top}=2C_{A}.

We now introduce the backwards filtration

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\bigl(\{(i_{s},\theta_{s})\}_{s\geq t}\bigr),\qquad 1\leq t\leq m+1.

Since
P_{t+1}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t+1}
-measurable and
i_{t}
is uniform on

\{1,\dots,N\}
independently of
\mathcal{F}_{t+1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[v_{t}v_{t}^{\top}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t+1}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ad}(P_{t+1})\Bigl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}a_{i}^{\top}\Bigr)\mathrm{Ad}(P_{t+1})^{\top}

(6.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}I_{N}.

Define

(6.6)

Y_{t}=v_{t}v_{t}^{\top}-\frac{1}{N}I_{N}.

Then

\mathbb{E}(Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t+1})=0,

so, after reversing time,
(Y_{t})
is a self-adjoint matrix martingale
difference sequence.

Since
v_{t}v_{t}^{\top}
is a rank-one orthogonal projection, its eigenvalues are

1,0,\dots,0
.
Consequently,

(6.7)

\|Y_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=1-\frac{1}{N}\leq 1.

Next, using
(v_{t}v_{t}^{\top})^{2}=v_{t}v_{t}^{\top}
, we compute

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(v_{t}v_{t}^{\top}-\frac{1}{N}I_{N}\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(1-\frac{2}{N}\Bigr)v_{t}v_{t}^{\top}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}I_{N}.

Taking conditional expectation and using
(
6.5
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y_{t}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\Bigl(1-\frac{2}{N}\Bigr)\frac{1}{N}I_{N}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}I_{N}

(6.8)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\frac{1}{N}-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Bigr)I_{N}\preceq\frac{1}{N}I_{N}.

Hence the predictable quadratic variation satisfies

(6.9)

W_{m}:=\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}(Y_{t}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t+1})\preceq\frac{m}{N}I_{N}.

We now apply Tropp’s matrix Freedman inequality
[
45
, Theorem 1.2]
.
Using (
6.7
) and (
6.9
), for every

s>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\lambda_{\max}\Bigl(\sum_{t=1}^{m}Y_{t}\Bigr)\geq s\right)\leq N\exp\!\left(-\frac{s^{2}/2}{m/N+s/3}\right).

Applying the same bound to
-Y_{t}
yields

(6.10)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{m}Y_{t}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq s\right)\leq 2N\exp\!\left(-\frac{s^{2}/2}{m/N+s/3}\right).

From (
6.3
) and (
6.6
),

C_{A}=\sum_{t=1}^{m}v_{t}v_{t}^{\top}=\frac{m}{N}I_{N}+\sum_{t=1}^{m}Y_{t}.

Choose
s=\frac{1}{2}\frac{m}{N}
so that

(6.11)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\|C_{A}-\frac{m}{N}I_{N}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{m}{N}\right)\leq 2N\exp\!\left(-\frac{3}{28}\frac{m}{N}\right).

Define the event

\mathcal{E}:=\left\{\left\|C_{A}-\frac{m}{N}I_{N}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\frac{1}{2}\frac{m}{N}\right\}.

Then by (
6.11
),

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{c})\leq 2N\exp\!\left(-\frac{3}{28}\frac{m}{N}\right).

On the event
\mathcal{E}
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\frac{m}{N}I_{N}\preceq C_{A}\preceq\frac{3}{2}\frac{m}{N}I_{N}.

Using (
6.4
), this implies

\frac{m}{N}I_{N}\preceq M_{A}\preceq 3\frac{m}{N}I_{N}.

Equivalently,

\frac{m}{N}\leq\lambda_{\min}(M_{A})\leq\lambda_{\max}(M_{A})\leq 3\frac{m}{N},

so that
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{A}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{A}^{c})\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{c})\leq 2N\exp\!\left(-\frac{3}{28}\frac{m}{N}\right).

Since
m\geq C_{1}N\log N
and
C_{1}>10>\frac{28}{3}
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{A}^{c})\leq 2N^{\,1-\frac{3}{28}C_{1}}=o(1)

and the proof is finished.
∎

Remark 1
.

The key input in Proposition
3
is the conditional isotropy identity (
6.5
),

\mathbb{E}\bigl[v_{t}v_{t}^{\top}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t+1}\bigr]=\frac{1}{N}I,

which follows from two structural features of Kac’s walk: the uniform choice of the coordinate direction
i_{t}
, and the fact that the adjoint action
\mathrm{Ad}(g)
is orthogonal on
\mathfrak{so}(n)
. This construction suggests a general approach for proving mixing bounds for high-dimensional local-update Markov chains: if the update directions are sufficiently randomized and their transport does not distort directions too severely, one can analyze the spread of the chain via the covariance of transported directions and control it using matrix concentration.

7.
Gaussian approximation of the perturbation block

We now show that the perturbation block is quite close to an appropriate Gaussian. Recall from Section
3.2
that we fix a fresh block of updates

A=((i_{1},\theta_{1}),\dots,(i_{m},\theta_{m})),

and introduce an independent Gaussian perturbation

\Delta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2}I_{m}).

The endpoint of the perturbed block is described by

H_{A}(\Delta)=L_{A}(\Delta)L_{A}(0)^{-1},\qquad U_{A}:=u_{A}(\Delta)=\log H_{A}(\Delta)\in\mathfrak{so}(n).

By Lemma
2
,
H_{A}(\Delta)
has the correct marginal law for the
m
-step update. By Lemma
1
, the logarithm is a bijection on a reasonably large ball, so (after a small truncation) it turns out to suffice to analyze the distribution of
U_{A}
. Throughout this section, we condition on the block
A
and study the law of
U_{A}
with respect to the Gaussian randomness in
\Delta
.

From Section
5
, we have the expansion

u_{A}(\delta)=J_{A}\delta+r_{A}(\delta),

valid for
\delta
in a small neighborhood of the origin, while Section
6
shows that the covariance

M_{A}=J_{A}J_{A}^{\top}

is well-conditioned with high probability over
A
. Together, these results suggest that
U_{A}
should be well approximated by the Gaussian random variable

J_{A}\Delta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2}M_{A}).

To make this precise, we restrict to the high-probability event

(7.1)

E=\Big\{\|\Delta\|_{2}\leq 2\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m}\Big\}.

Under our choice of parameters from (
2.1
), we have

2\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m}=2N^{-3}\sqrt{m}\ll m^{-1/2},

so the radius condition (
5.8
) holds on
E
for all sufficiently large
n
. In particular,
U_{A}
is well-defined on
E
, and the linear approximation above is valid on
E
.
Set

(7.2)

\Sigma_{A}:=\sigma_{n}^{2}M_{A},\qquad\gamma_{A}:=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{A}).

We will compare the conditional law
\mathcal{L}(U_{A}\mid A;E)
to the Gaussian measure

\gamma_{A}
and show that they are close in total variation; see Proposition
5
. We commit a small abuse of notation when describing the conditional distribution
\mathcal{L}(U_{A}\mid A;E)
: by conditioning on the random variable
A
we are conditioning on the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
A
, while by conditioning on
E
we are conditioning on the event
E
happening (rather than conditioning on the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the
E
). This causes no real difficulties as
\sigma(E),\sigma(A)
are independent.

We begin with a Gaussian stability estimate for small perturbations of the
identity.

Proposition 4

(Gaussian stability under small perturbations)
.

Let
\gamma_{d}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
. Let
R:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
C^{1}
map satisfying

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|R(x)\|_{2}\leq\alpha,\qquad\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\mathrm{d}R(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\beta

with
0<\beta\leq 1/2
. Set
T=\mathrm{Id}+R
. Then
T
is a global
C^{1}

diffeomorphism, and there exists a universal constant
C_{\mathrm{NI}}>0
such
that

(7.3)

\mathrm{KL}(T_{\#}\gamma_{d}\,\|\,\gamma_{d})\leq C_{\mathrm{NI}}\bigl(\alpha^{2}+d\beta^{2}\bigr),

and

(7.4)

\|T_{\#}\gamma_{d}-\gamma_{d}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C_{\mathrm{NI}}\bigl(\alpha+\sqrt{d}\,\beta\bigr).

Proof.

First we show that
T
is a global diffeomorphism. For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\|T(x)-T(y)\|_{2}\geq\|x-y\|_{2}-\|R(x)-R(y)\|_{2}\geq(1-\beta)\|x-y\|_{2}.

Thus
T
is injective. For surjectivity, fix
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and consider

\Phi_{z}(x)=z-R(x).

Since
\Phi_{z}
is a contraction with Lipschitz constant at most
\beta<1
, it has
a unique fixed point. This fixed point satisfies
T(x)=z
. Hence
T
is bijective.
Moreover,

\mathrm{d}T(x)=I+\mathrm{d}R(x)

is invertible for every
x
, so the inverse function theorem implies that
T
is
a global
C^{1}
diffeomorphism.

Let
X\sim\gamma_{d}
. By the change-of-variables formula,

\frac{\mathrm{d}(T_{\#}\gamma_{d})}{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{d}}(T(x))=\frac{\exp(-\|x\|_{2}^{2}/2)}{\exp(-\|T(x)\|_{2}^{2}/2)\det\mathrm{d}T(x)}.

Therefore

(7.5)

\mathrm{KL}(T_{\#}\gamma_{d}\,\|\,\gamma_{d})=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|T(X)\|_{2}^{2}-\|X\|_{2}^{2}}{2}-\log\det\mathrm{d}T(X)\right].

Set
B(x)=\mathrm{d}R(x)
. Since
\|B(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1/2
, the series

\log\det(I+B)=\mathrm{Tr}\log(I+B)=\mathrm{Tr}B+\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\mathrm{Tr}(B^{k})

converges absolutely. We now claim that there is a universal constant
C_{\log}
such that

(7.6)

-\log\det(I+B)+\mathrm{Tr}B\leq C_{\log}\|B\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}.

To see this, note that

|\mathrm{Tr}(B^{k})|\leq\|B\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{k-2},\qquad k\geq 2,

and the geometric series is bounded uniformly when
\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1/2
.
Using
T(X)=X+R(X)
in (
7.5
), so

\mathrm{KL}(T_{\#}\gamma_{d}\,\|\,\gamma_{d})\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\langle X,R(X)\rangle-\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{d}R(X))\bigr]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\|R(X)\|_{2}^{2}+C_{\log}\mathbb{E}\|\mathrm{d}R(X)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}.

The first term vanishes by Gaussian integration by parts:

\mathbb{E}\langle X,R(X)\rangle=\mathbb{E}\operatorname{div}R(X)=\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{d}R(X)).

Hence

\mathrm{KL}(T_{\#}\gamma_{d}\,\|\,\gamma_{d})\leq\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}+C_{\log}d\beta^{2}.

Choose

C_{\mathrm{NI}}\geq\max\{1/2,C_{\log}\}.

This proves (
7.3
).

Pinsker’s inequality gives

\|T_{\#}\gamma_{d}-\gamma_{d}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}C_{\mathrm{NI}}\bigl(\alpha^{2}+d\beta^{2}\bigr)}\leq C_{\mathrm{NI}}\bigl(\alpha+\sqrt{d}\,\beta\bigr),

after increasing
C_{\mathrm{NI}}
once and for all. This proves
(
7.4
) and we are done.
∎

We will use the following functions to smoothly truncate our distributions:

Lemma 5

(Radial cutoff)
.

There is an absolute constant
C_{\mathrm{cut}}<\infty
such that for every

d\geq 1
and every
R>0
, there exists a smooth function

\Psi_{d,R}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
satisfying

\Psi_{d,R}(x)=1\quad\text{if }\|x\|_{2}\leq 2R,\qquad\Psi_{d,R}(x)=0\quad\text{if }\|x\|_{2}\geq 3R,

and

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\nabla\Psi_{d,R}(x)\|_{2}\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{cut}}}{R}.

Proof.

Choose a smooth function
\eta:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
such that

\eta(s)=1\quad\text{for }s\leq 4,\qquad\eta(s)=0\quad\text{for }s\geq 9.

Set

\Psi_{d,R}(x)=\eta\!\left(\frac{\|x\|_{2}^{2}}{R^{2}}\right).

Then

\nabla\Psi_{d,R}(x)=\eta^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{\|x\|_{2}^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)\frac{2x}{R^{2}}.

The derivative can be nonzero only when
2R\leq\|x\|_{2}\leq 3R
, so

\|\nabla\Psi_{d,R}(x)\|_{2}\leq\frac{6\|\eta^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}{R}.

Thus the claim holds with
C_{\mathrm{cut}}=6\|\eta^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
.
∎

We now use Proposition
4
to analyze our second stage coupling. Recall the Gaussian measure
\gamma_{A}
from Equation (
7.2
) and the event
\mathcal{G}_{A}
defined in (
6.2
).

Proposition 5

(Gaussian approximation for the perturbation block)
.

There exist absolute constants
c_{\mathrm{GA}},C_{\mathrm{GA}}>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
n
, the following holds uniformly over all
A\in\mathcal{G}_{A}
:

\|\mathcal{L}(U_{A}\mid A;E)-\gamma_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C_{\mathrm{GA}}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}}+e^{-c_{\mathrm{GA}}m}.

In particular, under the scale choice (
2.1
), the right-hand side is
o(1)
.

Proof.

Let
Z=\sigma_{n}^{-1}\Delta
so that
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m})
. From (
7.1
), recall the event

E=\{\|\Delta\|_{2}\leq 2\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m}\}=\{\|Z\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{m}\}.

By Gaussian concentration, there exists an absolute constant

c_{\mathrm{tail}}>0
such that

(7.7)

\mathbb{P}(E^{c})\leq e^{-c_{\mathrm{tail}}m}.

We introduce a cutoff so that the logarithmic expansion from
Proposition
2
is used only inside its domain. Using the construction in
Lemma
5
, choose

\chi=\Psi_{m,\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m}}.

Then

\chi(\delta)=1\quad\text{if }\|\delta\|_{2}\leq 2\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m},\qquad\chi(\delta)=0\quad\text{if }\|\delta\|_{2}\geq 3\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m},

and

(7.8)

\sup_{\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\|\nabla\chi(\delta)\|_{2}\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{cut}}}{\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m}}.

Define

(7.9)

\widetilde{r}_{A}(\delta):=\chi(\delta)r_{A}(\delta),\qquad\widetilde{u}_{A}(\delta):=J_{A}\delta+\widetilde{r}_{A}(\delta),\qquad\widetilde{U}_{A}:=\widetilde{u}_{A}(\Delta).

Since
\chi\equiv 1
on
E
,

(7.10)

\widetilde{U}_{A}=U_{A}\qquad\text{on }E.

We next record the bounds on the truncated remainder. By
Proposition
2
, there is an absolute constant

C_{0}>0
such that, whenever

\|\delta\|_{2}\leq c_{0}/\sqrt{m},

we have

\|r_{A}(\delta)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq C_{0}m\|\delta\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\|\mathrm{d}r_{A}(\delta)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{0}m\|\delta\|_{2}.

Under (
2.1
),

3\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m}\leq c_{0}/\sqrt{m}

for all sufficiently large
n
. Hence the preceding bounds apply on

\mathrm{supp}(\chi)
. We now claim that there exist absolute constants

C_{r},C_{dr}>0
such that

(7.11)

\sup_{\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\|\widetilde{r}_{A}(\delta)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq C_{r}\sigma_{n}^{2}m^{2},

and

(7.12)

\sup_{\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\|\mathrm{d}\widetilde{r}_{A}(\delta)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{dr}\sigma_{n}m^{3/2}.

To see this, on
\mathrm{supp}(\chi)
,

\|r_{A}(\delta)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\lesssim\sigma_{n}^{2}m^{2},\qquad\|\mathrm{d}r_{A}(\delta)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\lesssim\sigma_{n}m^{3/2}.

Moreover,

\mathrm{d}\widetilde{r}_{A}=\chi\,\mathrm{d}r_{A}+(\nabla\chi)\otimes r_{A}.

The term
\chi\,\mathrm{d}r_{A}
is controlled by the bound on
\mathrm{d}r_{A}
, while
(\nabla\chi)\otimes r_{A}
is controlled by (
7.8
) together with the bound on
r_{A}

Fix now
A\in\mathcal{G}_{A}
. By the definition of the good set
\mathcal{G}_{A}
,

\lambda_{\min}(M_{A})\geq\frac{m}{N}.

Let

\rho_{A}:=s_{\min}(J_{A})=\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(M_{A})}.

Thus

(7.13)

\rho_{A}\geq\sqrt{m/N}.

Take a singular value decomposition

J_{A}=Q[\Lambda\ \ 0]W^{\top},

where
Q\in O(N)
,
W\in O(m)
, and

\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(s_{1},\dots,s_{N}),\qquad s_{i}\geq\rho_{A}.

Since
W^{\top}Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m})
, write

W^{\top}Z=(X,Y),\qquad X\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m-N},

where
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{N})
and
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m-N})
are independent.
Since
\Delta=\sigma_{n}Z=\sigma_{n}W(X,Y)
,

(7.14)

J_{A}\Delta=\sigma_{n}Q\Lambda X,\qquad\mathcal{L}(J_{A}\Delta)=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{A})=\gamma_{A}.

For fixed
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m-N}
, define

T_{A,y}(x):=x+(\sigma_{n}Q\Lambda)^{-1}\widetilde{r}_{A}\bigl(\sigma_{n}W(x,y)\bigr),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

where we identify
\mathfrak{so}(n)
with
\mathbb{R}^{N}
via the fixed orthonormal basis
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
.
Then

(7.15)

\widetilde{U}_{A}=\sigma_{n}Q\Lambda\,T_{A,Y}(X).

We now verify that
T_{A,y}
is a small perturbation of the identity, uniformly
in
y
. By (
7.11
) and (
7.13
),

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\|T_{A,y}(x)-x\|_{2}\leq\frac{C_{r}\sigma_{n}^{2}m^{2}}{\sigma_{n}\rho_{A}}\leq C_{\alpha}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}},

where
C_{\alpha}
is an absolute constant. Similarly, by
(
7.12
) and (
7.13
),

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\|\mathrm{d}T_{A,y}(x)-I\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{C_{dr}\sigma_{n}m^{3/2}}{\rho_{A}}\leq C_{\beta}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{3/2}}{\sqrt{m/N}},

where
C_{\beta}
is an absolute constant.

Set

\alpha_{A}:=C_{\alpha}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}},\qquad\beta_{A}:=C_{\beta}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{3/2}}{\sqrt{m/N}}.

Since

\beta_{A}=C_{\beta}\sigma_{n}m\sqrt{N}\to 0,

we have
\beta_{A}\leq 1/2
for all sufficiently large
n
.

Applying Proposition
4
in dimension
N
, with

\gamma_{N}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{N})
, gives, for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m-N}
,

\|(T_{A,y})_{\#}\gamma_{N}-\gamma_{N}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C_{\mathrm{NI}}\bigl(\alpha_{A}+\sqrt{N}\,\beta_{A}\bigr).

Since
m\geq N
,

\sqrt{N}\,\beta_{A}=C_{\beta}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{3/2}\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{m/N}}\leq C_{\beta}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}}.

Therefore, for an absolute constant
C_{\mathrm{tr}}
,

(7.16)

\|(T_{A,y})_{\#}\gamma_{N}-\gamma_{N}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C_{\mathrm{tr}}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}}\qquad\text{for every }y\in\mathbb{R}^{m-N}.

By (
7.14
), (
7.15
),
and invariance of total variation under the invertible linear map
x\mapsto\sigma_{n}Q\Lambda x
,

\|\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{U}_{A}\mid A,Y=y)-\gamma_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C_{\mathrm{tr}}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}}.

Averaging over
Y
gives

(7.17)

\|\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{U}_{A}\mid A)-\gamma_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C_{\mathrm{tr}}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}}.

It remains to remove the cutoff. Since
\widetilde{U}_{A}=U_{A}
on
E
,
there exists a probability measure
\eta_{A}
such that

\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{U}_{A}\mid A)=\mathbb{P}(E)\mathcal{L}(U_{A}\mid A;E)+\mathbb{P}(E^{c})\eta_{A}.

Hence

\|\mathcal{L}(U_{A}\mid A;E)-\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{U}_{A}\mid A)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq 2\mathbb{P}(E^{c})\leq 2e^{-c_{\mathrm{tail}}m}.

Combining this with (
7.17
) gives

\|\mathcal{L}(U_{A}\mid A;E)-\gamma_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C_{\mathrm{tr}}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}}+2e^{-c_{\mathrm{tail}}m}.

Now set

C_{\mathrm{GA}}:=C_{\mathrm{tr}},\qquad c_{\mathrm{GA}}:=\frac{1}{2}c_{\mathrm{tail}}.

Since
m\to\infty
, for all sufficiently large
n
,

2e^{-c_{\mathrm{tail}}m}\leq e^{-c_{\mathrm{GA}}m}.

Therefore

\|\mathcal{L}(U_{A}\mid A;E)-\gamma_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C_{\mathrm{GA}}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}}+e^{-c_{\mathrm{GA}}m}.

Finally, under (
2.1
),

\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}}=o(1),

so the right-hand side above is
o(1)
and this proves the proposition.
∎

8.
The transport step on the group

In the previous section, we proved a Gaussian approximation for
U_{A}
; see Proposition
5
. In this section, we transfer that logarithmic approximation to a total-variation bound for the group-valued law of
H_{A}(\Delta)
; see Proposition
7
.

The following bound is well-known:

Lemma 6
.

If
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
\Sigma
positive definite, then for every

h\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

(8.1)

\left\lVert\mathcal{L}(G+h)-\mathcal{L}(G)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1/2}h\right\rVert_{2}.

Proof.

The Kullback–Leibler divergence between two Gaussians with the same covariance is

\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\mathcal{N}(h,\Sigma)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}h^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}h=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1/2}h\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Pinsker’s inequality then gives (
8.1
).
∎

We next estimate the nonlinear correction created by passing from addition in the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}(n)

to right multiplication in the group
\mathrm{SO}(n)
.

Lemma 7
.

There exist constants
r_{\ast},C_{\ast}>0
independent of
n
such that the map

\Phi(\xi,h)\equiv\log\!\bigl(\exp(\xi)\exp(h)\bigr)

is smooth on

\{(\xi,h)\in\mathfrak{so}(n)\times\mathfrak{so}(n):\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq r_{\ast},\ \left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq r_{\ast}\},

and the remainder

(8.2)

\beta_{h}(\xi)=\Phi(\xi,h)-\xi-h

satisfies

(8.3)

\left\lVert\beta_{h}(\xi)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq C_{\ast}\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}},\qquad\left\lVert\mathrm{d}_{\xi}\beta_{h}(\xi)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{\ast}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}

whenever
\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}},\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq r_{\ast}
.

Proof.

Choose
r_{\ast}>0
small enough that

e^{2r_{\ast}}-1\leq\frac{1}{2}.

If
\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}},\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq r_{\ast}
, then
\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}},\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq r_{\ast}
, and therefore

\displaystyle\left\lVert\exp(\xi)\exp(h)-\mathrm{Id}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\exp(\xi)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\,\left\lVert\exp(h)-\mathrm{Id}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}+\left\lVert\exp(\xi)-\mathrm{Id}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}\bigl(e^{\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}-1\bigr)+\bigl(e^{\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}-1\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq e^{2r_{\ast}}-1\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Hence
\exp(\xi)\exp(h)
lies in the fixed operator-norm neighborhood

\mathcal{U}=\{M\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R}):\left\lVert M-\mathrm{Id}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1/2\},

on which the principal logarithm is analytic; see
[
13
, Chapter 1]
. The matrix exponential, matrix multiplication, and principal logarithm all have first and second Fréchet derivatives bounded by absolute constants on these fixed neighborhoods (with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt norm). Consequently
\Phi
is smooth on the stated product ball, and its mixed second derivatives are bounded there by an absolute constant.

Define
\beta(\xi,h)=\Phi(\xi,h)-\xi-h
.
Because
\Phi(\xi,0)=\xi
and
\Phi(0,h)=h
, we have

\beta(\xi,0)=0,\qquad\beta(0,h)=0.

Therefore

\beta(\xi,h)=\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{1}\partial_{2}\beta(s\xi,th)[\xi,h]\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t,

so the fact that the mixed second-derivatives are bounded gives, for some
C_{\ast}
, the inequality

\left\lVert\beta_{h}(\xi)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq C_{\ast}\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}.

Also
\mathrm{d}_{\xi}\beta(\xi,0)=0
for all
\xi
, hence

\mathrm{d}_{\xi}\beta_{h}(\xi)=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{2}\mathrm{d}_{\xi}\beta(\xi,th)[h]\,\mathrm{d}t,

and the same bound yields

\left\lVert\mathrm{d}_{\xi}\beta_{h}(\xi)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{\ast}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}

and the proof is finished.
∎

For
g\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
, write

T_{g}(x)=xg.

The next proposition relates translations in the tangent space
\mathfrak{so}(n)
to translation in the group
\mathrm{SO}(n)
: if a law on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
is the exponential pushforward of a measure on

\mathfrak{so}(n)
that is close to a centered Gaussian, then small right translation on
the group has small total-variation cost.

Proposition 6

(Gaussian overlap transfer on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathfrak{so}(n)
supported on

(8.4)

\{\xi\in\mathfrak{so}(n):\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq r_{\ast}/4\},

where
r_{\ast}
is the radius from Lemma
7
,
and let
\nu=\exp_{\#}\mu.

Let
\gamma=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)

be a centered Gaussian law on
\mathfrak{so}(n)
, where
\Sigma
is positive definite, and
assume

(8.5)

\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{r_{\ast}}{16\sqrt{N}}.

Set
\kappa(\Sigma)=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)}{\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)}
.
There exist absolute constants
c_{\mathrm{GT}},c_{B},C_{B}>0
with the following
property: for every
h\in\mathfrak{so}(n)
satisfying

(8.6)

\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq c_{\mathrm{GT}}\,\kappa(\Sigma)^{-1/2},

and with
g=\exp(h)
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nu-(T_{g})_{\#}\nu\right\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq

\displaystyle 2\left\lVert\mu-\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}+C_{B}\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1/2}h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}

(8.7)

\displaystyle+C_{B}\sqrt{N}\,\kappa(\Sigma)^{1/2}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}+e^{-c_{B}N}.

Proof.

Let
r_{\ast}
and
C_{\ast}
be the constants from
Lemma
7
. Define

c_{\mathrm{GT}}:=\min\left\{r_{\ast},\,\frac{1}{2C_{\ast}(1+3C_{\mathrm{cut}})}\right\}.

Since
\kappa(\Sigma)\geq 1
, the assumption

\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq c_{\mathrm{GT}}\kappa(\Sigma)^{-1/2}

implies

\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq c_{\mathrm{GT}}\leq r_{\ast}.

Choose a measurable extension

B_{h}:\mathfrak{so}(n)\to\mathfrak{so}(n)

of the local map

\xi\mapsto\log(\exp(\xi)\exp(h))

from the ball
\{\|\xi\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq r_{\ast}/4\}
to all of
\mathfrak{so}(n)
.
Since
\mu
is supported in this ball, we have, for
\xi\in\mathrm{supp}\,\mu
,

B_{h}(\xi)=\log(\exp(\xi)\exp(h)).

Thus

T_{g}(\exp(\xi))=\exp(\xi)\exp(h)=\exp(B_{h}(\xi)),

and hence

\nu=\exp_{\#}\mu,\qquad(T_{g})_{\#}\nu=\exp_{\#}(B_{h})_{\#}\mu.

Since total variation cannot increase under pushforward,

\|\nu-(T_{g})_{\#}\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\|\mu-(B_{h})_{\#}\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

Inserting
\gamma
and
(B_{h})_{\#}\gamma
, and then using contraction of
total variation under the pushforward by the measurable map
B_{h}
, gives

\displaystyle\|\mu-(B_{h})_{\#}\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu-\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+\|\gamma-(B_{h})_{\#}\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+\|(B_{h})_{\#}\gamma-(B_{h})_{\#}\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu-\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+\|\gamma-(B_{h})_{\#}\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+\|\gamma-\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

(8.8)

\displaystyle=2\|\mu-\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+\|\gamma-(B_{h})_{\#}\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

It remains to control the second term. We split it into a mean shift and a nonlinear Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff correction; the latter is localized and whitened so that Proposition
4
can be applied globally. Let

\tau_{h}(\xi)=\xi+h.

We have

(8.9)

\|\gamma-(B_{h})_{\#}\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\|\gamma-(\tau_{h})_{\#}\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+\|(\tau_{h})_{\#}\gamma-(B_{h})_{\#}\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

By Lemma
6
,

(8.10)

\|\gamma-(\tau_{h})_{\#}\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\Sigma^{-1/2}h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.

This controls the first term in (
8.9
). We now bound the nonlinear term. Set

\kappa:=\kappa(\Sigma)=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)}{\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)}.

Write

\Xi=\Sigma^{1/2}X,\qquad X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{N}),

so that
\Xi\sim\gamma
. Let

F=\{\|X\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{N}\}.

A standard Chernoff bound gives

(8.11)

\mathbb{P}(F^{c})\leq e^{-2c_{B}N},

where

c_{B}:=\frac{3-\log 4}{4}.

Using the construction and notation from the statement of Lemma
5
, choose

\Psi=\Psi_{N,\sqrt{N}}.

Then

\Psi(x)=1\quad\text{if }\|x\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{N},\qquad\Psi(x)=0\quad\text{if }\|x\|_{2}\geq 3\sqrt{N},

and

(8.12)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\|\nabla\Psi(x)\|_{2}\leq C_{\mathrm{cut}}N^{-1/2}.

Recall that
r_{\ast}
is the radius from Lemma
7
. We first check that the whitened variable stays in this logarithmic chart on the ball that contains the support of the cutoff.
By the chart assumption (
8.5
), if

\|x\|_{2}\leq 4\sqrt{N}
, then

\|\Sigma^{1/2}x\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq\|\Sigma^{1/2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|x\|_{2}\leq\frac{r_{\ast}}{4}.

Thus, on this ball, the map

x\mapsto\log\!\bigl(\exp(\Sigma^{1/2}x)\exp(h)\bigr)

is well-defined and smooth.

We next write the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff remainder in whitened coordinates and then use the cutoff function to obtain the global bounds required by Proposition
4
.
Define the local whitened error

b_{h}(x):=\Sigma^{-1/2}\beta_{h}(\Sigma^{1/2}x),\qquad\|x\|_{2}\leq 4\sqrt{N},

where

\beta_{h}(\xi)=\log(\exp(\xi)\exp(h))-\xi-h

is the local remainder from Lemma
7
.
By the chain rule,

\mathrm{d}b_{h}(x)=\Sigma^{-1/2}\circ\mathrm{d}_{\xi}\beta_{h}(\Sigma^{1/2}x)\circ\Sigma^{1/2}.

Since

\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\Sigma^{1/2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\sqrt{\kappa},

Lemma
7
gives, for
\|x\|_{2}\leq 4\sqrt{N}
,

(8.13)

\|b_{h}(x)\|_{2}\leq C_{\ast}\sqrt{\kappa}\,\|x\|_{2}\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}},

and

(8.14)

\|\mathrm{d}b_{h}(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{\ast}\sqrt{\kappa}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.

Define the global cutoff error

R_{h}(x):=\begin{cases}\Psi(x)b_{h}(x),&\|x\|_{2}\leq 4\sqrt{N},\\
0,&\|x\|_{2}>4\sqrt{N},\end{cases}\qquad S_{h}(x):=x+R_{h}(x).

S_{h}
is well-defined and
C^{1}
, because
\Psi(x)=0
when

\|x\|_{2}\geq 3\sqrt{N}
. Since
0\leq\Psi\leq 1
and
\Psi
is supported in

\{\|x\|_{2}\leq 3\sqrt{N}\}
, (
8.13
) gives

(8.15)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\|R_{h}(x)\|_{2}\leq 3C_{\ast}\sqrt{\kappa N}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.

We next bound the derivative. On the support of
\Psi
,

\mathrm{d}R_{h}(x)=\Psi(x)\mathrm{d}b_{h}(x)+(\nabla\Psi(x))\otimes b_{h}(x),

and
\mathrm{d}R_{h}(x)=0
outside this support. The first term is controlled by
(
8.14
):

\|\Psi(x)\mathrm{d}b_{h}(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{\ast}\sqrt{\kappa}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.

For the second term, on the support of
\nabla\Psi
we have

\|x\|_{2}\leq 3\sqrt{N}
. Therefore (
8.12
) and
(
8.13
) imply

\|(\nabla\Psi(x))\otimes b_{h}(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{\mathrm{cut}}N^{-1/2}\cdot 3C_{\ast}\sqrt{\kappa N}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.

Combining the two estimates gives

(8.16)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\|\mathrm{d}R_{h}(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{\ast}(1+3C_{\mathrm{cut}})\sqrt{\kappa}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.

By the definition of
c_{\mathrm{GT}}
, the assumption on
h
implies

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\|\mathrm{d}R_{h}(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore Proposition
4
applies to

S_{h}=\mathrm{Id}+R_{h}.

With

\alpha=3C_{\ast}\sqrt{\kappa N}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}},\qquad\beta=C_{\ast}(1+3C_{\mathrm{cut}})\sqrt{\kappa}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}},

it yields

(8.17)

\|(S_{h})_{\#}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{N})-\mathcal{N}(0,I_{N})\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C_{\mathrm{NI}}C_{\ast}(4+3C_{\mathrm{cut}})\sqrt{\kappa N}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.

We have now controlled the whitened cutoff map
S_{h}=\mathrm{Id}+R_{h}
. It remains to
compare this cutoff model with the original map
B_{h}
. Define

\widetilde{B}_{h}(x)=\Sigma^{-1/2}B_{h}(\Sigma^{1/2}x),\qquad u=\Sigma^{-1/2}h.

On the event
F
, the cutoff is inactive:
\Psi(X)=1
. Moreover,

\|\Sigma^{1/2}X\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq r_{\ast}/4
, so
B_{h}
agrees with the local
logarithmic map at
\Sigma^{1/2}X
. Hence, on
F
,

B_{h}(\Sigma^{1/2}X)=\log\!\bigl(\exp(\Sigma^{1/2}X)\exp(h)\bigr)=\Sigma^{1/2}X+h+\beta_{h}(\Sigma^{1/2}X),

and therefore

\widetilde{B}_{h}(X)=X+u+b_{h}(X)=\tau_{u}(S_{h}(X)),\qquad\tau_{u}(x)=x+u.

Thus the coupling inequality gives

\|\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{B}_{h}(X))-\mathcal{L}(\tau_{u}(S_{h}(X)))\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\mathbb{P}(F^{c}).

By the triangle inequality and translation invariance of total variation,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{B}_{h}(X))-\mathcal{L}(\tau_{u}(X))\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(F^{c})+\|\mathcal{L}(\tau_{u}(S_{h}(X)))-\mathcal{L}(\tau_{u}(X))\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(F^{c})+\|(S_{h})_{\#}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{N})-\mathcal{N}(0,I_{N})\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

Using (
8.11
) in the form

\mathbb{P}(F^{c})\leq e^{-2c_{B}N}
, together with
(
8.17
), gives

(8.18)

\displaystyle\|(\widetilde{B}_{h})_{\#}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{N})-(\tau_{u})_{\#}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{N})\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{NI}}C_{\ast}(4+3C_{\mathrm{cut}})\sqrt{\kappa N}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}+e^{-2c_{B}N}.

Finally, applying the invertible linear map
\Sigma^{1/2}
to both measures and
using

B_{h}\circ\Sigma^{1/2}=\Sigma^{1/2}\circ\widetilde{B}_{h},\qquad\tau_{h}\circ\Sigma^{1/2}=\Sigma^{1/2}\circ\tau_{u},

turns (
8.18
) into

(8.19)

\|(B_{h})_{\#}\gamma-(\tau_{h})_{\#}\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C_{\mathrm{NI}}C_{\ast}(4+3C_{\mathrm{cut}})\sqrt{\kappa N}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}+e^{-2c_{B}N}.

Finally set

C_{B}:=\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},C_{\mathrm{NI}}C_{\ast}(4+3C_{\mathrm{cut}})\right\}.

Combining (
8.8
), (
8.9
),
(
8.10
), and (
8.19
), we get

\|\nu-(T_{g})_{\#}\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq 2\|\mu-\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+C_{B}\|\Sigma^{-1/2}h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}+C_{B}\sqrt{\kappa N}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}+e^{-c_{B}N}.

Since
\kappa=\kappa(\Sigma)
, this is exactly (
8.7
) and the proof is finished.
∎

Denote the “block law” by

(8.20)

\nu_{A}=\mathcal{L}(H_{A}(\Delta)\mid A).

We now apply Proposition
6
to compare
\nu_{A}
and
(T_{g})_{\#}\nu_{A}
.

Proposition 7
.

Let
c_{\log}
be the constant from Lemma
1
. Let
h\in\mathfrak{so}(n)
and let
g=\exp(h)
. If
\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq c_{\log}\omega_{n}
, then
for all sufficiently large
n
and for all
A\in\mathcal{G}_{A}
,

(8.21)

\left\lVert\nu_{A}-(T_{g})_{\#}\nu_{A}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}=o(1).

Proof.

Fix a realization
A\in\mathcal{G}_{A}
. All probabilities and conditional laws
below are taken with this
A
fixed.

Recall that

E=\{\|\Delta\|_{2}\leq 2\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m}\}

is the typical-size event for the Gaussian perturbation, and write

\mu:=\mu_{A}^{E}=\mathcal{L}(U_{A}\mid A;E),\qquad\nu:=\nu_{A}^{E}=\mathcal{L}(H_{A}(\Delta)\mid A;E).

Also recall that

\gamma:=\gamma_{A}=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{A}),\qquad\Sigma_{A}:=\sigma_{n}^{2}M_{A}.

Since
E
depends only on
\Delta
, which is independent of
A
, we may write

\nu_{A}=\mathbb{P}(E)\nu+\mathbb{P}(E^{c})\nu_{A}^{E^{c}}

for some probability measure
\nu_{A}^{E^{c}}
. Hence

(8.22)

\|\nu_{A}-(T_{g})_{\#}\nu_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\|\nu-(T_{g})_{\#}\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+2\mathbb{P}(E^{c}).

We now wish to apply Proposition
6
with

\mu=\mu_{A}^{E},\qquad\nu=\nu_{A}^{E},\qquad\gamma=\gamma_{A},\qquad\Sigma=\Sigma_{A}.

To do so, we verify the three assumptions of Proposition
6
:
\mu
is supported in the logarithmic chart, the chart-size bound on
\Sigma_{A}
, and the smallness condition on
h
.

(i) Support in the logarithmic chart.

Let
r_{\ast}
be the chart radius from Proposition
6
, and let

C_{0}
be the constant from Proposition
2
. Proposition
6
requires that
\mu
be supported in a set of the form (
8.4
). We now check that this “support” hypothesis holds.

On
E
,

\|\Delta\|_{2}\leq 2\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m}.

Under the scale choice (
2.1
),

2\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m}=o(m^{-1/2}),

so for all sufficiently large
n
the radius condition in
Proposition
2
holds on
E
. Therefore

U_{A}=J_{A}\Delta+r_{A}(\Delta).

On
\mathcal{G}_{A}
,

\|J_{A}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(M_{A})}\leq\sqrt{3m/N}.

Hence, on
E
,

\|U_{A}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq\|J_{A}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\Delta\|_{2}+\|r_{A}(\Delta)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq 2\sqrt{3}\,\sigma_{n}\frac{m}{\sqrt{N}}+4C_{0}\sigma_{n}^{2}m^{2}.

The right-hand side is
o(1)
under (
2.1
). Since
r_{\ast}>0
is fixed,
it follows that for all sufficiently large
n
,

\|U_{A}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq\frac{r_{\ast}}{4}\qquad\text{on }E.

Thus
\mu
is supported in

\{\xi\in\mathfrak{so}(n):\|\xi\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq r_{\ast}/4\}.

Moreover, on
E
we have
H_{A}(\Delta)=\exp(U_{A})
, and therefore

\nu=\exp_{\#}\mu.

This verifies the “support” hypothesis of Proposition
6
.

(ii) Chart-size condition for the Gaussian covariance.

On
\mathcal{G}_{A}
,

\frac{m}{N}I_{N}\preceq M_{A}\preceq 3\frac{m}{N}I_{N},

so

\sigma_{n}^{2}\frac{m}{N}I_{N}\preceq\Sigma_{A}\preceq 3\sigma_{n}^{2}\frac{m}{N}I_{N}.

In particular,

\|\Sigma_{A}^{1/2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sqrt{3}\,\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m/N}.

Therefore

16\sqrt{N}\,\|\Sigma_{A}^{1/2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 16\sqrt{3}\,\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m}=o(1),

so for all sufficiently large
n
,

\|\Sigma_{A}^{1/2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{r_{\ast}}{16\sqrt{N}}.

Thus the chart-size hypothesis (
8.5
) of
Proposition
6
holds.

(iii) Smallness condition on
h
.

Let
c_{\mathrm{GT}},C_{B},c_{B}
be the constants from
Proposition
6
. On
\mathcal{G}_{A}
,

\kappa(\Sigma_{A})=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{A})}{\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{A})}\leq 3.

Since
\kappa(\Sigma_{A})\leq 3
,
the smallness hypothesis (
8.6
) from
Proposition
6
,

\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq c_{\mathrm{GT}}\kappa(\Sigma_{A})^{-1/2},

is implied by

\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq\frac{c_{\mathrm{GT}}}{\sqrt{3}}.

But by assumption,

\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq c_{\log}\omega_{n}=c_{\log}N^{-4}\to 0,

so the bound
|h|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq c_{\mathrm{GT}}/\sqrt{3}
holds for all sufficiently large
n
. Therefore the smallness hypothesis of Proposition
6
is satisfied.

Having verified the hypotheses, we may apply
Proposition
6
with

\mu=\mu_{A}^{E},\qquad\nu=\nu_{A}^{E},\qquad\gamma=\gamma_{A},\qquad\Sigma=\Sigma_{A}.

This yields

\displaystyle\|\nu_{A}^{E}-(T_{g})_{\#}\nu_{A}^{E}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq 2\|\mu_{A}^{E}-\gamma_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle+C_{B}\|\Sigma_{A}^{-1/2}h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle+C_{B}\sqrt{N}\,\kappa(\Sigma_{A})^{1/2}\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}+e^{-c_{B}N}.

Using again that on
\mathcal{G}_{A}
,

\kappa(\Sigma_{A})\leq 3,\qquad\|\Sigma_{A}^{-1/2}h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq\frac{\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}}{\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m/N}},

we obtain

(8.23)

\|\nu-(T_{g})_{\#}\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq 2\|\mu-\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+C_{B}\frac{\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}}{\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m/N}}+\sqrt{3}\,C_{B}\sqrt{N}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}+e^{-c_{B}N}.

Next, Proposition
5
gives

\|\mu-\gamma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C_{\mathrm{GA}}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}}+e^{-c_{\mathrm{GA}}m},

where
c_{\mathrm{GA}},C_{\mathrm{GA}}>0
are the constants from that proposition.
Substituting this into (
8.23
), we get

\displaystyle\|\nu-(T_{g})_{\#}\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\;

\displaystyle 2C_{\mathrm{GA}}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}}+C_{B}\frac{\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}}{\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m/N}}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{3}\,C_{B}\sqrt{N}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}+2e^{-c_{\mathrm{GA}}m}+e^{-c_{B}N}.

Finally, since
\Delta=\sigma_{n}Z
with
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m})
, a standard
Gaussian tail bound gives an absolute constant
c_{E}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(E^{c})\leq e^{-c_{E}m}.

Combining this with (
8.22
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\|\nu_{A}-(T_{g})_{\#}\nu_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\;

\displaystyle 2C_{\mathrm{GA}}\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}}+C_{B}\frac{\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}}{\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m/N}}

(8.24)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{3}\,C_{B}\sqrt{N}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}+2e^{-c_{\mathrm{GA}}m}+e^{-c_{B}N}+2e^{-c_{E}m}.

It remains to prove the estimate (
8.21
). Assume

\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq c_{\log}\omega_{n}=c_{\log}N^{-4}.

Under (
2.1
),

\frac{\sigma_{n}m^{2}}{\sqrt{m/N}}\asymp\frac{\log^{3/2}N}{N}=o(1),

\frac{\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}}{\sigma_{n}\sqrt{m/N}}\leq\frac{c_{\log}N^{-4}}{N^{-3}\sqrt{m/N}}=\frac{c_{\log}}{N\sqrt{m/N}}=o(1),

and

\sqrt{N}\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq c_{\log}N^{-7/2}=o(1).

The exponential terms are also
o(1)
. Therefore, by (
8
),

\|\nu_{A}-(T_{g})_{\#}\nu_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=o(1),

which proves (
8.21
) and the proof is finished.
∎

9.
Proof of the main theorem

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Fix arbitrary
x,y\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
. For the first stage, apply Proposition
1
with
B=4
.
Thus there exists

t_{0}=O(N\log n)

and a coupling

(\widetilde{X}_{t},\widetilde{Y}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq t_{0}}

of two copies of Kac’s walk with

\widetilde{X}_{0}=x,\qquad\widetilde{Y}_{0}=y,

such that, with

\mathcal{E}_{0}:=\{D_{\mathrm{HS}}(\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}},\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}})\leq\omega_{n}\},\qquad\omega_{n}=N^{-4},

we have

(9.1)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c})=o(1).

We now define the second stage. Sample an independent fresh block

A=((i_{1},\theta_{1}),\dots,(i_{m},\theta_{m}))

as in Definition
1
, and condition on

(\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}},\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}},A).

Set

g:=\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}}^{-1}\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}},\qquad z:=L_{A}(0)\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}}.

Since
\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}}=\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}}g
and

L_{A}(\delta)=H_{A}(\delta)L_{A}(0)
, we have

L_{A}(\delta)\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}}=H_{A}(\delta)z,\qquad L_{A}(\delta)\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}}=H_{A}(\delta)zg.

Next, as in Definition
2
, let
\nu_{A}
be the block law from
(
8.20
):

\nu_{A}=\mathcal{L}(H_{A}(\Delta)\mid A),\qquad\Delta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2}I_{m}),

with
\Delta
independent of everything else. By the definition of
\nu_{A}
and

L_{A}(\delta)=H_{A}(\delta)L_{A}(0)
, conditionally on

(\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}},\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}},A)
,

\mathcal{L}(L_{A}(\Delta)\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}})=(T_{z})_{\#}\nu_{A},\qquad\mathcal{L}(L_{A}(\Delta)\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}})=(T_{zg})_{\#}\nu_{A}.

Now define

(X_{t_{0}+m},Y_{t_{0}+m})

conditionally on
(\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}},\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}},A)
to be a

maximal coupling
of these two conditional laws. Then

\mathcal{L}(X_{t_{0}+m}\mid\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}},\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}},A)=(T_{z})_{\#}\nu_{A},

\mathcal{L}(Y_{t_{0}+m}\mid\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}},\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}},A)=(T_{zg})_{\#}\nu_{A},

and

(9.2)

\mathbb{P}\!\bigl(X_{t_{0}+m}\neq Y_{t_{0}+m}\mid\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}},\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}},A\bigr)=\|(T_{z})_{\#}\nu_{A}-(T_{zg})_{\#}\nu_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

Averaging over the independent block
A
, Lemma
2
gives

\mathcal{L}(X_{t_{0}+m}\mid\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}},\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}})=K^{m}(\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}},\cdot),\qquad\mathcal{L}(Y_{t_{0}+m}\mid\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}},\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}})=K^{m}(\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}},\cdot).

We next estimate this mismatch probability on the good event

\mathcal{E}_{0}\cap\mathcal{G}_{A}.

On
\mathcal{E}_{0}
, bi-invariance of
D_{\mathrm{HS}}
gives

D_{\mathrm{HS}}(\mathrm{Id},g)=D_{\mathrm{HS}}(\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}},\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}})\leq\omega_{n}.

For all sufficiently large
n
, Lemma
1
applies, so

g=\exp(h)\qquad\text{with}\qquad\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq c_{\log}\omega_{n}.

Set

\widehat{g}:=zgz^{-1}.

Since
g=\exp(h)
, we have

\widehat{g}=\exp(\widehat{h}),\qquad\widehat{h}:=\mathrm{Ad}(z)h.

Since the adjoint action is orthogonal on
\mathfrak{so}(n)
,

\|\widehat{h}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\|h\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq c_{\log}\omega_{n}.

Now
T_{z^{-1}}
is a measurable bijection of
\mathrm{SO}(n)
, so total variation is
invariant under pushforward by
T_{z^{-1}}
. Hence

\|(T_{z})_{\#}\nu_{A}-(T_{zg})_{\#}\nu_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\|\nu_{A}-(T_{\widehat{g}})_{\#}\nu_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

A\in\mathcal{G}_{A}
on the good event, Proposition
7
,
applied with
h=\widehat{h}
, yields

\|\nu_{A}-(T_{\widehat{g}})_{\#}\nu_{A}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=o(1)

uniformly on
\mathcal{E}_{0}\cap\mathcal{G}_{A}
.

9.2
),

\mathbb{P}\!\bigl(X_{t_{0}+m}\neq Y_{t_{0}+m}\mid\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}},\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}},A\bigr)=o(1)\qquad\text{on }\mathcal{E}_{0}\cap\mathcal{G}_{A}.

Taking expectations and splitting according to
\mathcal{E}_{0}\cap\mathcal{G}_{A}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{0}+m}\neq Y_{t_{0}+m})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{A}^{c})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}\cap\mathcal{G}_{A}}\mathbb{P}\!\bigl(X_{t_{0}+m}\neq Y_{t_{0}+m}\mid\widetilde{X}_{t_{0}},\widetilde{Y}_{t_{0}},A\bigr)\right]=o(1),

where we used (
9.1
), Proposition
3
,
and the preceding uniform bound.

Since
X_{t_{0}+m}\sim K^{t_{0}+m}(x,\cdot)
and

, the coupling inequality gives

\|K^{t_{0}+m}(x,\cdot)-K^{t_{0}+m}(y,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{0}+m}\neq Y_{t_{0}+m})=o(1),

uniformly in

and using stationarity of Haar measure,

\mu=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}K^{t_{0}+m}(y,\cdot)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}y),

we obtain

\sup_{x\in\mathrm{SO}(n)}\|K^{t_{0}+m}(x,\cdot)-\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=o(1).

Finally,

t_{0}+m=O(N\log n)+O(N\log N)=O(n^{2}\log n),

because

and
\log N\asymp\log n
. This proves the theorem.
