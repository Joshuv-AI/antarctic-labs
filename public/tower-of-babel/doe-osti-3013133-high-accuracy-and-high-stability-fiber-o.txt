 Executive Summary
This report summarizes the technical findings and achievements in the project titled “High-
Accuracy and High-Stability Fiber-Optic Temperature Sensors for Coal Fired Advanced Energy
Systems”, funded by National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL), US Department of Energy
under award number DE-FE0031899 and performed by the research group in the Department of
Electrical and Computer Engineering at Michigan State University led by Principal Investigator
Dr. Ming Han. The overall objective of this project is to develop a gas-based fiber-optic
temperature sensor technology with the required accuracy and long-term stability for temperature
control and condition monitoring of the next generation of coal-fired power generation systems.
The sensor performance is not limited by the material properties of solids, and it can achieve
absolute and accurate measurement of high temperature with no drift and no cross-sensitivity to
strain and pressure.
Traditional fiber-optic sensors, such as those based on Fiber Bragg Gratings (FBG) and standard
Fabry–Pérot (FP) cavities, fundamentally rely on measuring temperature by detecting changes in
the fiber's refractive index or cavity length. This mechanism results in significant technical
liabilities such as temperature-strain cross-sensitivity and structural drift at high temperatures.
Since both temperature and mechanical strain cause a shift in the optical signal, the sensor output
contains mixed information, making it challenging to accurately separate temperature from strain.
Compensation methods add complexity, cost, and size. Additionally, at elevated temperatures, the
fiber's material properties (silica, dopants) can degrade or change, leading to long-term drift and
requiring frequent recalibration.
This report details breakthrough research demonstrating a shift from conventional fiber-optic
temperature sensors to drift-free, gas-based thermometry, and addresses and overcomes the two
aforementioned shortcomings of previous-generation optical fiber temperature sensors.
This research overcomes these issues by using gas (air or argon) filled within the FP cavity as the
sensing medium. Since the measurement relies on the gas's temperature-dependent refractive index
and pressure, it is rendered immune to the thermal expansion or mechanical stress of the
surrounding fiber structure.
The first section of this report demonstrates the gas-based sensing principle, which entirely
decouples the temperature reading from the fiber structure's physical changes, eliminating the
fundamental cross-sensitivity issue.
The second section presents dual-cavity design, which eliminated the need for external, bulky
pressure reference gauges, or the inherent knowledge of the optical properties of the gas used,
tackling the complexity and cost associated with system integration.
The third section discusses the research adopting the fundamentals of Refractive-Index Gas
Thermometry (RIGT) with argon and the use of the accurate virial-coefficient model for the
working equation. This provides a path for a practical fiber-optic primary thermometer for
industrial use, which offers foundational stability and accuracy (within 1.0 K of reference
standards) that conventional sensors cannot achieve due to their inherent structural limitations.

1.0 Fiber Optic Temperature Sensor System Using Air-Filled Fabry–
 Pérot Cavity with Variable Pressure
1.1 Introduction
Fiber optic sensors have gained remarkable popularity due to their many advantages, such as- light
weight, small size, immunity to electromagnetic interference, harsh environment compatibility,
and multiplexed sensing capability. Therefore, these sensors have been extensively studied for
measurement of a wide range of physical and chemical parameters including temperature,
pressure, strain, displacement, salinity, corrosion in various applications [1]–[4].
Temperature is a key parameter that needs to be monitored and controlled precisely in a wide range
of applications including industrial, chemical, structural, biomedical, and environmental aspects.
For the last three decades, various techniques have been developed regarding fiber optic
temperature sensors such as fiber-Bragg grating (FBG) based sensors [5], tapered fiber [6],
waveguide coupling devices using surface plasmon resonance [7], fiber ring laser demodulation
[8], modified fibers (panda fibers [9], multicore fiber [10]), and interferometer-based sensor [11–
26]. Among these, temperature sensors based on Fabry-Pérot (FP) interferometers have proved to
be an attractive option because of their additional advantages of simple structure and easy
fabrication. They utilize the Fresnel reflections from two parallel reflectors that form the cavity
that interfere due to different optical path lengths, nL, where n and L are the refractive index of the
material and the cavity length, respectively, both of which are dependent on temperature.
Nowadays, different types of FP interferometers have been deployed in terms of composition and
fabrication process. For example, interferometers can be made of (a) fiber-tip: SMF-microfiber
[11], SMF-polyvinyl alcohol [12], (b) with diaphragm: FBG-graphene [13], [14], MMF-silicon
[15], FBG-fused silica [16], (c) without diaphragm [17], (d) polymer materials [18], (e) inline
microcavities [19], [20], (e) multiplexed sensors [21], (f) microcantilever [22], polished materials
[27]. However, each type of sensor has some drawbacks, which limit their applications. For
example, sensors based on FBGs are prone to strain cross-sensitivity [5]. To overcome this
drawback, some studies implement techniques for simultaneous measurement of temperature and
pressure or other perturbations [13], [20], [23] . But these sensors mostly require calibration due
to their inherent FP cavity length change during interrogation. Also, for multiple parameter
measurement, many of these works utilize an FBG along with FP Interferometer sensor, where the
lower sensitivity of FBG to temperature (~10 pm/ºC) affects the accuracy at high temperature
measurement [13], [23]. Many researchers have used cavities formed by a tip composed of
graphene or other materials such as diaphragm or cantilever. These materials have large thermal
expansion coefficients (TEC) and thermo-optic coefficient (TOC) compared to their glass material
counterparts, resulting in higher temperature resolution. But the fabrication process often requires
complex MEMS techniques [15], [22], chemical processing [12]–[14], or high-power laser
ablation [16], which is expensive, time consuming, and lacking in reproducibility. Also, these
sensors show nonlinearity at high temperature because the composite structure’s TOC becomes a
complex function of temperature, resulting in a limited operation range [18]. Researchers have
also studied various methods for demodulation of sensor’s spectra. These FP interferometer
sensors [24]–[26] are mainly based on dual cavity, where the composite wavelength signal is
demodulated by using FFT with various algorithms such as white light interferometry [28], wavelet
phase extraction [29], wavelet transform and polarized low coherence interferometry [30], coarse
spectrum [31]. Most of them provide ~10 nm accuracy in cavity length measurement, which can

 6

result in ±5℃ error in temperature measurement at around 800℃. Also, these methods have
drawbacks, e.g., limited cavity length range, susceptibility to noise, slow demodulation speed.
 To overcome these drawbacks, we proposed and demonstrated a fiber optic temperature sensor
based on air filled FP cavity with variable and controlled pressure [32]. To perform the
measurement, the FP reflection spectrum was recorded when the air pressure was varied. Utilizing
the fact that the difference of air refractive index and unity is linearly proportional to the pressure
and inversely proportional to the absolute temperature, absolute temperature can be extracted from
the slope of the spectral shift vs. pressure. The measurement principle dictates that the sensor has
zero strain cross-sensitivity over a broad temperature range and the use of air as the sensing
material eliminates drifts from material degradation often encountered by solid materials at high
temperature. The system demonstrated in our previous work, however, has a few drawbacks that
limit its usefulness in practical applications. To achieve temperature reading, the slope of the
spectral shift vs. pressure was obtained using curve fitting by measuring reflection spectrum at
multiple air pressure levels over a large pressure range of 1500 psi using a manual air pressure
pump. Generating such a high air pressure level is not a trivial task and often requires expensive
and bulky instrumentation as well as long pumping time.
In this section, we have reported our theoretical and experimental work for further development
of the fiber-optic temperature sensor toward practical applications. The sensor head is fabricated
by splicing a fused-silica capillary tube with a single mode fiber (SMF) on one end and a side-hole
fiber on the other end. The pressure of the cavity is changed by passing air through the holes of
the side-hole fiber. Instead of using very high pressure and bulky instruments as in our previous
work [32], we used miniaturized instruments with lower pressure (100 psig). Each temperature
reading is obtained by recording the spectrum at two pressure levels. This makes the sensor
acquisition system compact, portable, fast, and electronically controlled. We developed a model
and theoretically analyzed the effect of key system parameters, including the wavelength
resolution in the spectral shift and the pressure stability on system performance in terms of
temperature measurement resolution and stability. In the experiment, our sensor shows high
wavelength resolution (< 0.2 pm), linear response, reduced strain cross-sensitivity, and high
accuracy with repeatability for a broad operation range (room temperature to over 800℃).
1.2 Sensor Fabrication and Theoretical Analysis
1.2.1 Sensor fabrication
Figure 1.1(a) shows the schematic of the sensor head, which consists of a single mode fiber (SMF),
a fused-silica capillary tube, and a side-hole fiber of identical diameter (125 µm). The silica tube
has a hollow of 75 µm diameter inside it. The side hole fiber has two holes of ~20 µm diameter
along the cladding. Figures 1.1(b) and 1.1(c) show their cross-sectional view of the silica tube and
side-hole fiber, respectively. Some deformity is seen in the contact edge of cleaver blade. For
fabrication, we spliced the SMF with silica tube by a fusion splicer (Sumitomo Type-36) using
optimized splicing parameter. We cleaved the end of the silica tube and spliced it again with the
side-hole fiber. The length of the silica tube is kept ~95 µm, which acts as the enclosed FP cavity.
Finally, we cleave the side-hole fiber end with 8° angle keeping appropriate length. The holes in
the side-hole fiber are employed as venting holes for pressurizing the FP cavity. The 8° end face
of the side-hole fiber effectively eliminates the back reflection from that end face to the SMF.
Figure 1.1(d) shows the longitudinal view of the fabricated sensor. The reflection spectrum of the
sensor at ambient temperature and pressure is shown in Figure 1.1(e). The spectrum shows that the
sensor has a good visibility of >18 dB and the full spectral range (FSR) is around 12.5 nm,

corresponding to a cavity length of ~96 µm, consistent with the silica tube length obtained from
the microscope image of the sensor.

1.3 Experimental Setup and Sensor Characterization
1.3.1 Experimental setup
We have developed a computer-controlled sensor acquisition system with portable and
miniaturized instruments. Figure 1.2 shows the schematic of the system. To measure the sensor’s
wavelength spectrum, we used a fiber optic sensor interrogator (Hyperion si-155) with 1 pm
accuracy. We used a thermal furnace to increase the temperature for sensor characterization. The
pressure in the FP cavity is changed by a miniaturized electronic pressure pump (model: H1R-
080P24HV-02). For our experiment we chose two different pressure levels at 0 psig and 100 psig
(or 690 kPa) as 𝑃𝑃1 and 𝑃𝑃2 , respectively. We used an electrical shut-off valve to stabilize the
pressure fluctuations while taking the readings. To read the pressure, we used a precision pressure
transducer (CPT6020) with 0.020% full scale accuracy. The pump, valve, and pressure transducer
are miniaturized in size (longest dimension is < 4.3 inch) and electronically controlled. We used a
programmable power supply to run these instruments. The whole setup is programmed in
MATLAB code, and the wavelength and pressure data are stored simultaneously in the control
unit (computer). It takes less than one minute to pressurize the chamber from 0 psig to 100 psig and
the pressure gets stable very fast after shutting the valve off. We used an alumina ceramic tube to
house the sensor in a pneumatic setup to prevent any air leak. Also, ceramic tubes have high
 10

melting temperatures (over 2500℃) and flexural strength (~54000 psi), which makes it suitable
for our high temperature application. As described in the theory section, temperature can be
measured by taking the data of two pressure levels 𝑃𝑃1 and 𝑃𝑃2 , their corresponding wavelength 𝜆𝜆1
and 𝜆𝜆2 . Figure 1.3 shows the flowchart of the integrated computer-controlled temperature
measurement algorithm.

1.3.3 Sensor’s wavelength resolution
Wavelength resolution is defined as the variation of fringe wavelength over a small period. It is
calculated by taking the standard deviation of the moving average of multiple valley wavelengths.
For this, we have recorded the reflection spectrum from the fiber optic sensor interrogator for 10
seconds of duration at 1 kHz scanning rate. Instead of using only one fringe valley as our previous
work [32], we have taken the arithmetic average of the first seven valleys in the range of 1500-

1600 nm to find the parameter 𝜆𝜆̅ for a specific temperature and pressure. This method eliminates
the wavelength noise caused by spurious jumps of any specific valley by a factor of √𝑀𝑀, where M
is the number of valleys (or peaks) used to calculate average wavelength [34]. Since we recorded
data for 10 s of duration for each measurement, the moving average of 𝜆𝜆̅ is taken to calculate the
wavelength 𝜆𝜆 to be used in Equation (1.6). Figure 1.6 shows that the sensor resolution at ambient

1.4 Discussion
Fiber-optic FP temperature sensors have become a widely explored research topic for the last few
decades. Most of the sensors use solid material (e.g., glasses, polymers, silicon, etc.) as the sensing
element and they have inherent limitations for high-temperature measurement. Many solid
materials have a low temperature capability. Their optical properties and sensors structures could
also experience permanent changes at high temperature that lead to sensor signal drift. The
uniqueness of the fiber optic temperature sensor studied here is the usage of air as the sensing
material and a sensing mechanism, which leads to high stability at high temperature. The sensor
consists of an FP cavity filled with air whose temperature can be controlled. It utilizes the fact that
the difference of air refractive index and unity is proportional to the air pressure and inversely
proportional to the air absolute temperature. The optical property of air is stable at high
temperatures. The absolute temperature is obtained by measuring the spectra of the FP reflection
fringes at two different pressure levels, which is independent on the cavity length. Therefore, even
the enclosure (a glass tube in this case) of the air in the FP cavity could change its length or have
permanent deformations at high temperature, it does not affect the accuracy of the sensor. An
assumption we need to make for the sensor to work is that the temperature and the cavity length
remain unchanged when the pressure is changed. Therefore, in practical applications, it is critical
to ensure that the sensor spectra are measured at two pressure levels within a short time over which
the ambient temperature and the cavity length have negligible changes. In practical applications,
the pressure can reach equilibrium within a few second and high spectrometers can be used for
 15

spectrum measurement, which means that the temperature and the FP cavity need to be stable over
a period of a few seconds.

The use of the gas pressure system increases the size of the sensor system. It also constrains the
distance between the sensor head and the pressure devices. In this work, we developed an
electronically controlled system with miniaturized and precision devices, which makes the
measurement more practical and ensures good accuracy and stability. This method can be used in
many applications that require a stable and accurate temperature sensor, for example, temperature
measurement in coal-gasified power plants.

We also analyzed the contribution of these parameters in temperature measurement deviation.
Specifically, we have found that the relative wavelength resolution in determining the wavelength
shift is the main noise source of the system and its noise contribution is also a function of the
temperature itself. This analysis is important for the system design to achieve optimized system
performance.

1.5 Conclusion
In this section, we have demonstrated a FP interferometer temperature sensor system based on the
spectral shift of fringe wavelength due to the pressure change in FP cavity. Sensor fabrication
process is very straightforward, which involves splicing a fused-silica tube with a regular SMF
and a side-hole fiber. Air is passed through the holes of the side-hole fiber to change the cavity
pressure up to 100 psi by using a miniaturized pump. The whole pressure calibration and sensor
acquisition system is electronically controlled, miniaturized and fast. In addition, we have
developed a model to analyze the impact of the system key parameters, such as sensor wavelength
resolution and pressure fluctuation on temperature measurement. In the experiment, the sensor has
been characterized from room temperature to 800℃ with our setup. The sensor’s wavelength
resolution is found to be less than 0.2 pm with a high pressure stability (~0.015 kPa). According
to our model, this resolution and pressure fluctuation can contribute to a temperature measurement
error of only 0.32℃ in room temperature, which is excellent compared to other fiber optic sensors
especially for this broad range of temperature. The repeatability of the sensor is tested by
undergoing it through several thermal cycles from room temperature to 800℃ and comparing the
measurement to a high accuracy reference thermometer. The comparison demonstrates high
accuracy of the sensor even after multiple cycles.

2.0 Fiber Optic Temperature Sensor Using Dual Air-filled Fabry-
Pérot Cavities with Variable Pressure
2.1 Introduction
High temperature measurement with accuracy is a prerequisite for operation of many modern
industries and fiber optic interferometric sensors have gained popularity in temperature
measurement due to their unique advantages such as high sensitivity, small size, light weight,
immunity to electromagnetic interference, harsh environment compatibility.
 In the last three decades, various techniques have been explored including fiber Bragg gratings
(FBG) [35], [36], ring resonators [37], Fabry-Pérot (FP) interferometers [13],[38]–[39]. Typically,
the operation of these sensors relies on spectral modulation resulting from the dependency of their
optical cavity length on temperature. More specifically, sensing material’s refractive index n, and
the characteristic length l (e.g., grating period for FBG, cavity length for FPI) are dependent on
temperature, due to the thermo-optic effect and the thermal expansion, respectively. While this
dependency allows for temperature measurement within a specific range, it is susceptible to
interference from cross-sensitivity to other perturbations, such as strain and pressure can introduce
measurement errors. To overcome this issue, composite sensor structures such as those with
multiple FP cavities and combining FBG and FP [40], [41], have been developed. Besides the
increased complexity of sensor structure, the operation also requires complicated calibration and
signal processing to separate the temperature information. Another issue of high-temperature
sensors is long-term drift. Most of the sensors use some solid materials such as the fiber itself as
the sensing elements. The sensor structure and the thermo-optic properties of the materials may
show complex temperature dependency at elevated temperatures, leading to sensor drift or
hysteresis. Mechanisms responsible for sensor drift under long-term exposure to high temperature
include fiber dopant diffusion [42], induced residual stress for frozen-in viscoelasticity [43],
devitrification (crystallization) of the glass [44]. Complicated annealing process may increase the
stability of sensor; however, it is often accompanied by a substantial decline in mechanical strength
of the sensors.
To overcome these drawbacks, we demonstrated a method that uses gas (e.g. air) as the material
for the sensing element [32], [45]. It has a structure of a FP interferometer, whose cavity is filled
with gas whose pressure can be controlled and varied. For ideal gases, the difference between gas
refractive index and unity (n-1) is linearly proportional to pressure and inversely proportional to
absolute temperature with a proportion coefficient ɑ. As the gas pressure varies, the FP fringes
shift linearly with pressure. The slope of spectral shift of the FP is proportional to temperature and
can be used to deduce temperature using coefficient ɑ. This method eliminates the cross sensitivity
to strain as the strain does not affect the slope of the spectral shift vs. pressure curve. Many gases
have a stable optical property at high temperatures, and using gas as the material for the sensing
element renders good long-term stability and no hysteresis. Nevertheless, the accuracy in
temperature measurement is largely dependent on the pressure stability and pressure reading
accuracy as well as the accuracy in determining coefficient ɑ. In this section, building upon the
previous work, we present a new approach for high temperature measurement that require neither
pressure readings (although a trivial low-temperature sensor is needed) nor the information of ɑ,
eliminating the need for a bulky pressure transducer and optical characterization of the gas,
resulting in simpler and cost-effective instrumentation.

2.5 Conclusion
We have presented a novel approach to measure high temperature using a measuring FP and a
reference FP with one of them having a known temperature in the cold zone. Both FP cavities are
filled with air of identical pressure. The air pressure can be varied, and the temperature of the
measuring FP can be deduced by the spectral fringe shift vs. pressure of the two FPs. This method
 23

does not require measurement of the pressure or the knowledge of the optical properties of the gas.
Experimental results showed that the sensor is capable of measuring temperature up to 800 ℃ with
good linearity. We also studied the noise performance which reveals a high resolution of ±0.52℃
at 800 ℃. Furthermore, a long-term test conducted at 800 ℃ exhibited the stability of the sensor
with fluctuations of ≤0.3% over a duration exceeding 100 hours. In the future, we plan to study
the spatial resolution of the sensor, which is mostly determined by the thermal spatial profile of
the air in the tube at thermal equilibrium. This method can be used in applications where accurate
measurement of high temperature is needed such as power plants, aerospace, and metallurgical
processes.

3.0 Toward a Practical Fiber-Optic Primary Thermometer For
Industrial Applications
3.1 Introduction
Accurate temperature measurement is fundamental to a wide range of industrial processes, from
material processing to energy generation. Secondary thermometers, such as thermocouples,
resistance temperature detectors (RTDs), and fiber Bragg gratings (FBGs), are typically used in
these settings. Unfortunately, these sensors may experience significant drift in harsh environments.
For example, FBGs may experience severe drift, sometime exceeding 1,000 ̊C, in high-
temperature or high-radiation environments [46, 47]. Primary thermometers [48], which measure
temperature based on fundamental physical constants rather than empirical calibrations, are
inherently driftless and can achieve sub-millikelvin accuracy. Among various primary
thermometry methods, refractive-index gas thermometry (RIGT) [49] has gained traction since the
definition of kelvin was revised in 2019, which is now linked to the fixed value of the Boltzmann’s
constant 𝑘𝑘𝐵𝐵 [50]. RIGT determines temperature by measuring the refractive index of a gas, such
as helium, nitrogen, or argon (Ar), using ab initio quantum mechanical calculations of virial
coefficients, without requiring empirical calibration. However, primary thermometry methods,
including RIGT, rely on complex, bulky apparatus and are sensitive to environmental conditions,
which has historically confined their use to controlled metrology laboratory settings and limited
practical industrial adoption.
In this section, we present our work toward a practical fiber-optic primary thermometer for
industrial applications. The device is based on a fiber-optic Fabry-Pérot (FP) interferometer filled
with Ar, whose pressure can be adjusted. The principle-of-operation is similar to RIGT. However,
rather than use a laser at fixed wavelength, we deduce temperature by the wavelength positions of
the FP fringes at two different pressure levels. The concept was previously demonstrated using
ambient air as working gas and assuming ideal gas law [32, 45]. However, the virial coefficients
for air are unavailable, and the ideal gas assumption may introduce large measurement errors. In
this work, using Ar as the working gas and a working equation that incorporates higher-order virial
coefficients, we demonstrate absolute temperature measurements over a wide range from ambient
conditions up to 800 °C. Although the accuracy and precision of our system do not match those of
metrological-grade primary thermometers, its compact form factor, robustness, and operational
versatility make it well-suited for many industrial applications that do not require extreme accuracy
but benefit from easily accessible and reliable calibration.

3.3 Experiment and Results
3.3.1 Experimental Setup
Experiment was carried out to study the proposed fiber-optic thermometer using the set up
schematically shown in Figure 3.4(a). We used ultra-high purity Ar (5N purity) as working gas. A
pneumatic pump was used to draw the air out and ensure that the system was only filled with Ar
only. The pressure was adjusted using a two-step pressure regulator and measured using an
absolute pressure gauge (Model: DPG210-330A, Omega) with accuracy of ±1 kPa. The FP was
fabricated by splicing a regular optical fiber and a side-hole fiber onto both ends of a fused silica
tube. The two gas/silica boundaries yield a reflectivity of ~4% and function as the reflectors of the
FP. The side-hole fiber was angle cleaved at the end to eliminate the reflection from the far end of
the fiber. The sensor was sealed in a stainless-steel tube connected to the pressure system and the
reflection spectrum of the sensor was recorded using a commercial optical sensor interrogator

3.4 Conclusion
In conclusion, we demonstrated a potentially practical fiber-optic primary thermometer based on
RIGT and using Ar as the working gas. The thermometer is a FP cavity at the tip of a single-mode
optical fiber filled with the working gas with variable pressure. We developed a virial-coefficient
model and the working equation to obtain the absolute temperature from the wavelength positions
of the FP at two different pressure levels along with the gas coefficients of Ar. We demonstrated
absolute temperature measurement from room temperature to ~1073 K. The measurement agrees
well with a reference thermocouple with discrepancies <1.0 K in the entire temperature range. The
standard deviation exhibits a trend of increase with temperature, from ~0.2 K at 373 K or lower to
>1.0 K as temperature approached 1073 K. Measuring uncertainties are likely dominated by the
uncertainties in the wavelength determination of the FP fringes. The FP in this work utilizes the
inherent ~4% Fresnel reflection at the silica/Ar interface. This results in a low-finesse cavity with
a weak signal amplitude. The associated uncertainties could be reduced by implementing a high-
finesse FP design, for example, one employing collimated beams and high-reflectivity mirror
coatings. It is worth noting that, for true primary RIGT, both pressure and wavelength sensors must
be primary standards or directly traceable to them. While a secondary pressure gauge was used in
this work, the pressure levels (a few hundred kPa) are within the range of primary piston gauges.
For wavelength determination, acetylene absorption lines in the 1550 nm window can serve as a
good wavelength reference traceable to primary standards.

4.0 Future Work
This project successfully demonstrated the feasibility of a practical, fiber-optic primary
thermometer based on RIGT. To advance this technology from a laboratory proof-of-concept to a
robust, field-deployable instrument, several key research and development pathways are identified.

Dual-Mode Thermometer System

An immediate and compelling research direction is the development of a dual-mode operational
system. The proposed device would intelligently switch between primary (absolute) and secondary
(relative) measurement modes. This hybrid approach would retain the long-term stability and
traceability of a primary thermometer while gaining the high-speed, continuous monitoring
capability of a secondary thermometer. Such a system would be uniquely versatile for applications
requiring both periodic calibration checks and real-time process control.

Enhanced Sensor Fabrication & Performance

The current prototype utilized a low-finesse Fabry-Perot (FP) sensor on an acrylate-coated fiber.
Future work will focus on material and fabrication advances to meet practical application demands:

 • Finesse Improvement: Implementing high-finesse FP cavities will significantly reduce
 optical wavelength uncertainty, thereby enhancing the fundamental resolution and accuracy
 of the RIGT measurement.
 • High-Temperature Materials: The acrylate coating's temperature limitation (~100°C) was
 a constraint in testing. For long-term operation in industrial or energy environments, fibers
 with metallic coatings (e.g., copper, gold) rated up to 800°C will be evaluated. For extreme
 temperatures exceeding 1500°C, single-crystal sapphire fibers present a promising pathway.
 • Integrated Sensor Redesign: We plan to investigate advanced fabrication techniques, such
 as femtosecond laser micromachining, to inscribe a robust, inline Fabry-Perot interferometer
 directly into the core of a single-mode optical fiber. This monolithic design would improve
 mechanical stability, reduce packaging complexity, and enhance interference signal quality.
System Integration and Application-Specific Development

Parallel to sensor advancement, work will focus on creating a complete, user-friendly measurement
system. This includes the development of dedicated optoelectronics for signal processing, intuitive
software for data analysis and mode-switching logic, and the design of sensor packaging suitable
for targeted applications in energy generation, advanced manufacturing, or aerospace testing.
