Electromagnetic Characterization of magnetic ring: Case of square cross section shape
Taha El Hajji*, Lars Sjöberg

Abstract
This paper presents a comprehensive 2D analytical model of a toroidal magnetic ring with a square cross-section, subjected to sinusoidal excitation. By applying Maxwell’s equations in local Cartesian coordinates and utilizing a complex permeability framework, the exact analytical expressions for the internal magnetic field, flux, complex impedance, and losses are derived. The model rigorously separates eddy current losses, hysteresis losses, and winding losses, explicitly accounting for the skin effect and complex permeability within the conductive core using separation of variables and hyperbolic functions. Furthermore, parameter for apparent permeability is expressed to map the core behavior onto simplified linear material models. The derivations establish a mathematical foundation highly suitable for standardized material characterizations, such as Brockhaus and Iwatsu ring measurements, by avoiding the heavy computational cost of 2D and 3D Finite Element Analysis.

Introduction

Accurate characterization of soft magnetic materials is vital for optimizing high-efficiency electromagnetic devices. Traditional measurement techniques, such as those performed using Brockhaus testers or Iwatsu systems, rely on extracting material properties by winding the specimen as a toroidal ring and measuring its voltage-current responses. However, as industry demands push excitation frequencies from 50Hz into the MHz range, the standardization of these measurement frameworks faces significant challenges regarding accuracy.
At high frequencies, the classical assumption of a uniform magnetic flux distribution across the core cross-section becomes invalid. The skin effect, driven by eddy currents within the conductive magnetic core, forces the flux toward the surface. In a core with a square cross-section, this effect leads to significant flux crowding at the edges and corners, creating a highly non-uniform field profile that empirical models fail to capture
[
6
]
. Furthermore, the material exhibits hysteresis, which introduces a phase lag between the applied field and the resulting flux. Modern characterization now necessitates a complex permeability framework to accurately map these frequency-dependent energy storage and loss mechanisms
[
5
]
.
Historically, engineers relied on empirical fits like the Steinmetz equation
[
1
]
, which offer no physical insight into internal spatial fields or the shielding effects caused by the core geometry. While Finite Element Analysis (FEA) can model these distributions, the computational cost for iterative material characterization is often prohibitive. Consequently, there is a renewed focus on developing exact analytical solutions for toroidal geometries to calculate eddy current distributions and volume-averaged losses
[
4
,
3
]
.
The motivation of this work is to provide a rigorous 2D analytical model of the complex impedance that accounts for the simultaneous impact of eddy currents and hysteresis in the case of ring with square cross section. By solving Maxwell’s equations in a 2D local Cartesian coordinate system applied to the toroid’s square cross-section, this paper provides exact analytical solutions for the fields, flux, impedance, and losses. Utilizing a complex permeability model allows for the explicit mathematical separation of hysteresis and eddy current losses, a distinction critical for advanced core loss predictions and standardized material testing.

4
Modeling and Mathematical Proof

4.1
Fundamental Maxwell’s Equations

The derivation of the internal fields begins with the generalized Maxwell’s equations:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle=\rho_{v}\quad\text{(Gauss's Law for Electricity)}

(3)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{(Gauss's Law for Magnetism)}

(4)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}\quad\text{(Faraday's Law of Induction)}

(5)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{J}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\quad\text{(Ampère's Circuital Law)}

(6)

In this model, since the quasi-static approximation is highly valid for the operating frequencies under consideration, the displacement current density is neglected. Thus, the analysis will primarily rely on Faraday’s Law and the simplified Ampère’s Law (
\nabla\times\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{J}
). Furthermore, all mathematical modeling and calculations in this study are performed at a no-load condition (the secondary circuit is an open circuit).

4.2
Assumptions and Operational Constraints

The analytical framework operates under the following key assumptions:

•

OD/ID\leq 1.2
: This assumption guarantees a uniform magnetic field in the DC case and at low frequency.

•

D_{m}>10.a
: this assumption is to ensure the equivalence between the 2D and 3D model.

•

The core material features a uniform complex permeability
\mu_{c}=|\mu_{c}|e^{-j\theta}
and a constant electrical conductivity
\sigma
.

•

The core remains in the Rayleigh region, representing the low-magnetic-field operational domain where permeability is relatively constant.

•

The secondary current is zero.

4.3
Maxwell’s Equations in 2D Plane

We establish a local Cartesian coordinate system
(x,y,z)
within the cross-section of the toroid. The origin
(x=0,y=0)
is the center of the square cross-section. The
z
-axis of this local system runs tangentially along the mean circumference of the toroid.

Due to the problem’s symmetry and the primary winding configuration, the magnetic field is purely axial (in the local
z
-direction), allowing
\mathbf{H}=H_{z}(x,y)\hat{\mathbf{z}}
. The induced electric field is purely in the
x-y
plane. Assuming time-harmonic fields (
e^{j\omega t}
), Maxwell’s equations inside the conductive core become:

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=\sigma\mathbf{E}

(7)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle=-j\omega\mathbf{B}

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}\mathbf{H}

(9)

Taking the curl of Ampère’s law and substituting Faraday’s law yields the vector Helmholtz equation:

\displaystyle\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle=\sigma(\nabla\times\mathbf{E})

(10)

\displaystyle\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle=-j\omega\mu_{c}\sigma\mathbf{H}

(11)

Using the vector identity
\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{H})=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{H})-\nabla^{2}\mathbf{H}
and knowing
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{H}=0
, we obtain the 2D diffusion equation:

\nabla^{2}_{xy}H_{z}-j\omega\mu_{c}\sigma H_{z}=0

(12)

By defining the complex propagation constant squared as
\gamma^{2}=j\omega\mu_{c}\sigma
, the differential equation becomes:

\frac{\partial^{2}H_{z}}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}H_{z}}{\partial y^{2}}=\gamma^{2}H_{z}

(13)

4.4
Expressions for B and H

To solve the partial differential equation, we use the separation of variables ansatz:

H_{z}(x,y)=X(x)Y(y)

(14)

Substituting this into the diffusion equation and dividing by
X(x)Y(y)
yields:

\frac{X^{\prime\prime}(x)}{X(x)}+\frac{Y^{\prime\prime}(y)}{Y(y)}=\gamma^{2}

(15)

Since the square symmetry of the cross-section forces identical behavior along both the
x
and
y
axes, each term must equal a constant
k^{2}
such that:

2k^{2}=\gamma^{2}

(16)

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}}

(17)

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{j\omega\mu_{c}\sigma}{2}}

(18)

The ordinary differential equations have general solutions built from hyperbolic functions. Constrained by the even symmetry of the core (the field must be symmetric about the
x
and
y
axes), the sinusoidal terms vanish, resulting in:

H_{z}(x,y)=H_{0}\cosh(kx)\cosh(ky)

(19)

Applying Ampère’s circuital law along the mean magnetic path
l_{m}=\pi D_{m}
, the field at the surface boundaries (
x=\pm a/2
or
y=\pm a/2
) is established by the primary current:

H_{surf}=\frac{N_{1}I}{l_{m}}

(20)

By evaluating the field at the center of a face, for instance at
(a/2,0)
, we find the amplitude constant
H_{0}
:

\displaystyle H_{0}\cosh(ka/2)\cosh(0)

\displaystyle=H_{surf}

(21)

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{H_{surf}}{\cosh(ka/2)}

(22)

This gives the exact analytical expressions for
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{B}
:

\boxed{H_{z}(x,y)=\frac{N_{1}I}{l_{m}}\frac{\cosh(kx)\cosh(ky)}{\cosh(ka/2)}}

(23)

\displaystyle B_{z}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}H_{z}(x,y)

(24)

\displaystyle B_{z}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}\frac{N_{1}I}{l_{m}}\frac{\cosh(kx)\cosh(ky)}{\cosh(ka/2)}

(25)

\boxed{B_{z}(x,y)=\mu_{c}\frac{N_{1}I}{l_{m}}\frac{\cosh(kx)\cosh(ky)}{\cosh(ka/2)}}

(26)

4.5
Flux Expression

The total magnetic flux
\Phi
passing through the square cross-section is the surface integral of
B_{z}(x,y)
:

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle=\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}B_{z}(x,y)\,dx\,dy

(27)

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}\frac{H_{surf}}{\cosh(ka/2)}\left(\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}\cosh(kx)\,dx\right)\left(\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}\cosh(ky)\,dy\right)

(28)

Evaluating the integrals yields:

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}\frac{H_{surf}}{\cosh(ka/2)}\left(\frac{2}{k}\sinh(ka/2)\right)^{2}

(29)

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}H_{surf}\frac{4}{k^{2}}\frac{\sinh^{2}(ka/2)}{\cosh(ka/2)}

(30)

By expanding
H_{surf}
, the final flux expression is framed as:

\boxed{\Phi=\mu_{c}\frac{N_{1}I}{l_{m}}\frac{4}{k^{2}}\frac{\sinh^{2}(ka/2)}{\cosh(ka/2)}}

(31)

4.6
Apparent Permeability

If the core were a linear, non-conductive material without skin effect, the flux would be defined as
\Phi_{ideal}=\mu_{app}\frac{N_{1}IA}{l_{m}}=\mu_{app}.H_{surf}.A
. Comparing this to the actual flux derived above, we define the complex apparent permeability under eddy current conditions:

\displaystyle\mu_{app}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi/a^{2}}{H_{surf}}

(32)

\displaystyle\mu_{app}

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}\frac{4}{(ka)^{2}}\frac{\sinh^{2}(ka/2)}{\cosh(ka/2)}

(33)

\boxed{\mu_{app}=\mu_{c}\frac{4}{(ka)^{2}}\frac{\sinh^{2}(ka/2)}{\cosh(ka/2)}}

(34)

4.7
Primary Complex Impedance Z

By Faraday’s law, the induced electromotive force in the primary is defined as
E_{1}=j\omega N_{1}\Phi
. The impedance of the core
Z_{core}=\frac{E_{1}}{I}
:

\displaystyle Z_{core}

\displaystyle=j\omega N_{1}\frac{\Phi}{I}

(35)

\displaystyle Z_{core}

\displaystyle=j\omega\mu_{c}\frac{N_{1}^{2}}{l_{m}}\frac{4}{k^{2}}\frac{\sinh^{2}(ka/2)}{\cosh(ka/2)}

(36)

By recalling the definition of the separation constant
k^{2}=\frac{j\omega\mu_{c}\sigma}{2}
, we can substitute
\frac{j\omega\mu_{c}}{k^{2}}=\frac{2}{\sigma}
. This simplifies the core impedance to:

\boxed{Z_{core}=\frac{8N_{1}^{2}}{\sigma l_{m}}\frac{\sinh^{2}(ka/2)}{\cosh(ka/2)}}

(37)

The primary winding wire also suffers from the skin effect. Let the wire have radius
r_{w}
, conductivity
\sigma_{w}
, and permeability
\mu_{w}
. The AC resistance
R_{winding}
for circular wires is defined using Kelvin functions as described in
[
2
]
:

R_{winding}=R_{DC}\frac{\xi}{2}\left[\frac{\text{ber}(\xi)\text{bei}^{\prime}(\xi)-\text{bei}(\xi)\text{ber}^{\prime}(\xi)}{\text{ber}^{\prime 2}(\xi)+\text{bei}^{\prime 2}(\xi)}\right]

(38)

where
\xi=\sqrt{2}r_{w}/\delta_{w}
. Including the leakage inductance
L_{leak}
, the total primary complex impedance is:

\boxed{Z=R_{winding}+j\omega L_{leak}+\frac{8N_{1}^{2}}{\sigma l_{m}}\frac{\sinh^{2}(ka/2)}{\cosh(ka/2)}}

(39)

4.8
Losses Expression

The losses can be explicitly separated into Winding, Eddy Current, and Hysteresis losses using an admittance-based approach.

1. Winding Loss:

\boxed{P_{w}=I_{rms}^{2}R_{winding}}

(40)

2. Core Losses Separation:

The core’s admittance is computed as
Y_{core}=\frac{1}{Z_{core}}=G_{core}-jB_{core}
. The real part
G_{core}
dictates the total active power lost in the core. To isolate the hysteresis loss, we analyze the hypothetical condition where the core’s conductivity approaches zero (
\sigma\to 0
):

\displaystyle Z_{h}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\sigma\to 0}Z_{core}

(41)

\displaystyle Z_{h}

\displaystyle=j\omega\frac{N_{1}^{2}A}{l_{m}}\mu_{c}

(42)

The equivalent conductance responsible solely for intrinsic hysteresis is:

G_{h}=\text{Re}\left(\frac{1}{Z_{h}}\right)

(43)

The additional loss imposed by macroscopic induced currents characterizes the eddy current conductance:

G_{e}=G_{core}-G_{h}

(44)

Therefore, the explicitly separated core power losses become:

Hysteresis Loss (
P_{h}
):

\boxed{P_{h}=|E_{1,rms}|^{2}G_{h}}

(45)

Eddy Current Loss (
P_{e}
):

\boxed{P_{e}=|E_{1,rms}|^{2}G_{e}}

(46)

where
E_{1,rms}=|Z_{core}|I_{rms}
.

4.9
Apparent Permeability

To isolate the influence of eddy currents on the energy-storage capability of the core, the apparent permeability
\mu_{app}
can be derived directly from the reactive component of the circuit.

If we consider an equivalent idealized core where the macroscopic eddy currents are neglected and the inductive behavior is defined strictly by a real apparent permeability
\mu_{app}
, the purely inductive impedance is
Z_{ideal}=j\omega L_{app}
. Using the cross-sectional area
A=a^{2}
, the imaginary part of this idealized impedance is expressed as:

\text{Im}(Z_{ideal})=\omega\frac{N_{1}^{2}a^{2}}{l_{m}}\mu_{app}

(47)

Conversely, the exact analytical complex impedance of the core, which simultaneously accounts for the skin effect, eddy currents, and complex permeability, was derived as:

Z_{core}=\frac{8N_{1}^{2}}{\sigma l_{m}}\frac{\sinh^{2}(ka/2)}{\cosh(ka/2)}

(48)

The imaginary part of this full complex impedance represents the true inductive reactance of the physical core:

\text{Im}(Z_{core})=\text{Im}\left(\frac{8N_{1}^{2}}{\sigma l_{m}}\frac{\sinh^{2}(ka/2)}{\cosh(ka/2)}\right)

(49)

By forcing the equivalent inductive model to match the true inductive behavior of the core, we equate the two imaginary components:

\omega\frac{N_{1}^{2}a^{2}}{l_{m}}\mu_{app}=\text{Im}\left(\frac{8N_{1}^{2}}{\sigma l_{m}}\frac{\sinh^{2}(ka/2)}{\cosh(ka/2)}\right)

(50)

Solving for
\mu_{app}
, the geometric and winding parameters
l_{m}
and
N_{1}^{2}
elegantly cancel out:

\displaystyle\mu_{app}

\displaystyle=\frac{l_{m}}{\omega N_{1}^{2}a^{2}}\cdot\text{Im}\left(\frac{8N_{1}^{2}}{\sigma l_{m}}\frac{\sinh^{2}(ka/2)}{\cosh(ka/2)}\right)

(51)

\displaystyle\mu_{app}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega a^{2}}\text{Im}\left(\frac{8}{\sigma}\frac{\sinh^{2}(ka/2)}{\cosh(ka/2)}\right)

(52)

Since the angular frequency
\omega
, the dimension
a
, and the conductivity
\sigma
are purely real and positive scalars, they can be consolidated inside the imaginary operator. This yields the final expression for the apparent permeability:

\boxed{\mu_{app}=\text{Im}\left(\frac{8}{\omega\sigma a^{2}}\frac{\sinh^{2}(ka/2)}{\cosh(ka/2)}\right)}

(53)

At low frequency (100 Hz), the electromagnetic skin depth is significantly larger than the dimension
a
, resulting in a nearly uniform flux density of 0.339 T across the entire domain. As frequency increases to 100 kHz, the counter-magnetomotive force from induced eddy currents begins to repel the flux from the volumetric center, where
B
drops to 0.295 T. At the 1 MHz limit, the skin effect is extreme; the magnetic field is virtually expelled from the core’s interior, leaving a ”dead zone” at the center (
B=0.057
T) and confining the flux to a thin peripheral layer.

The macroscopic impact of the skin effect is captured by the apparent relative permeability
\mu_{app}/\mu_{0}
(Fig.

P_{e}

In this case, the primary current is increased at higher frequencies to compensate for the flux reduction induced by the skin effect and internal magnetic shielding.
