Model Checking Matrix Product States against Linear Chain Logic
Ming Xu, Yihao Chen, Ji Guan

Abstract
Matrix product states (MPS) are a standard tensor-network representation for ground states of one-dimensional quantum many-body systems, and they underpin widely used simulation tools such as DMRG. However, while quantum model checking has been developed mainly for quantum programs and communication protocols (with properties expressed along a time axis), there is still no comparable framework for systematically verifying spatial and size-dependent properties of physical many-body states, where the key parameter is the system size. This paper takes a step toward bridging the gap. We propose Linear Chain Logic (LCL), a spatial logic designed to specify physically meaningful properties of periodic MPS families as the system size grows, such as nontriviality on rings and large-size asymptotic patterns. Our approach builds on a simple but powerful connection: every periodic MPS naturally induces a completely positive map (a quantum operation) on its virtual space, so many quantitative features of the MPS can be analysed through the repeated application of the operation. Using this perspective, we derive an effective procedure to compute the inner products of an MPS at a given size and to support richer LCL specifications, without relying on brute-force state expansion. We then develop approximate model-checking algorithms that combine sound bounding with asymptotic structural analysis, enabling scalable reasoning about large system sizes. Experiments on representative MPS families illustrate that our method can automatically verify nontriviality and detect asymptotic spatial regimes in a way that complements traditional numerical techniques.

A representative model is the

Many important quantum systems are not naturally described as a time-evolving protocol,

The viewpoint is central in quantum simulation and device-level validation,

From a verification perspective, the core challenge is therefore

It calls for a model-checking perspective that reasons over the index

MPS as a scalable system model.

For 1D local quantum systems,

From a verification perspective,

Thus, MPS provides a natural

\{|\psi_{N}\rangle\}_{N\geq 1}

|\psi_{N}\rangle=\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{N}=1}^{d}\mathrm{tr}(A_{k_{1}}\cdots A_{k_{N}})\;|k_{1}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|k_{N}\rangle.

(1)

The gap: model checking for spatial, quantitative properties of MPS families.

Despite substantial progress on temporal logics and QMC-style models,
systematic verification of
spatial
properties of MPS families remains largely unexplored.
Yet these properties are exactly what one needs to study and validate quantum many-body behavior:
Is the periodic MPS nontrivial for all sufficiently large size parameter
N
?
Do key observables eventually lie in a prescribed range?
Do behaviors become ultimately periodic or converge to a limiting regime?
Answering such questions requires: i) a logic whose semantics ranges over the size parameter
N
,
and ii) algorithms that exploit the algebraic structure of the MPS tensors.

Our approach.

To fill this gap,
we propose a spatial logic,
Linear Chain Logic
(LCL),
and approximate model-checking algorithms for periodic MPS.
The key technical bridge is that
a fixed MPS tensor family
\{A_{k}\}
induces a completely positive (CP) map

\mathcal{E}(X)=\sum_{k}A_{k}XA_{k}^{\dagger}
on the bond space (the space of
A_{k}
),
together with the (Liouville) matrix representation
M_{\mathcal{E}}=\sum_{k}A_{k}^{*}\otimes A_{k}
.
The transfer-operator view turns many MPS quantities into trace expressions of matrix powers.
Particularly,
\|\psi_{N}\|^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(M_{\mathcal{E}}^{\,N})
,
so fundamental questions are reduced to reasoning about the scalar sequence

\{\mathrm{tr}(M_{\mathcal{E}}^{\,N})\}_{N\geq 1}
.
Furthermore,
structural results for irreducible CP maps constrain the peripheral spectrum to be cyclic (roots of unity),
yielding eventual periodic regimes that our algorithms can exploit
without requiring an explicit full-spectrum computation.

Logic and algorithms.

LCL is LTL-like in syntax, but interpreted over the size index
N
.
Value expressions
\Gamma(N)
are extracted from the MPS contractions,
interval predicates
\in\mathcal{I}
give the ranges,
atomic formulas combine them as
\Gamma(N)\in\mathcal{I}
,
and general formulas admit Boolean connectives and spatial operators
such as “eventually” and “infinitely often”
along
N
.
Our approximate model-checking pipeline:
i) computes sound bounds for
\Gamma(N)
using the transfer-operator structure,
ii) derives semilinear over-/under-approximations of the set of sizes satisfying each atomic formula,
and iii) propagates these approximations bottom-up to decide the input formula.
In sum, this paper makes three main contributions:

1.

Specification
— We introduce the
linear chain logic
(LCL)
to express size-indexed, spatial properties of periodic MPS families
in many-body systems.

2.

Technique
— We develop the transfer-operator-based model checking,
leveraging CP-map view, irreducible decomposition,
and peripheral cyclicity to compute semilinear over-/under-approximations efficiently.

3.

Validation
— We evaluate the checker on representative MPS families and quantitative properties,
demonstrating scalable certification of nontriviality and detection of asymptotic spatial regimes.

1.1
Related work

(1) Chain logic vs. temporal logics:

Classical model checking is tightly coupled with temporal logics,
where the system is observed along a time axis.
Linear-time logics such as LTL specify properties over single executions,
while branching-time logics such as CTL/CTL
∗
quantify over computation trees and enable richer path quantification
(e. g., reachability)
[
10
,
4
]
.
The logics and their model-checking pipelines are now standard for software/hardware verification.
Our setting differs in what the “axis” ranges over:
rather than time steps of a program,
we consider the spatial index of a family of periodic MPS
\{|\psi_{N}\rangle\}_{N\geq 1}
,
and the target properties are quantitative and asymptotic in the chain length
N
.
It motivates our LCL to allow LTL-like connectives/operators,
but interpreted over the size parameter
N
and grounded in MPS contractions
(e. g., norms/correlators) as value expressions.
Thus, LCL fills a gap not covered by existing temporal logics:
it provides a systematic specification language for
spatial
size-indexed properties of
tensor-network state families, together with a dedicated checking pipeline.

(2) From quantum computing and communication models to physical many-body systems.

Most quantum model-checking work has been developed for computational and communication models,
where evolution is naturally temporal and often formalised
via quantum automata and quantum Markov chains (QMC)
driven by quantum operations and channels
[
14
,
40
,
39
]
.
In parallel,
recent years have seen a growing interplay between formal-language or automata viewpoints and quantum physics,
including explicit “language-theoretic” representations of quantum many-body states
(e. g. regular language quantum states)
[
15
]
.
These developments highlight an important frontier:
bringing verification ideas beyond program-like temporal evolution to
many-body state families

where the central object is a ground state ansatz
and the key questions are quantitative properties across system sizes
(nontriviality on rings, asymptotic regimes, thresholds, eventual periodicity).
Our paper contributes to this broadening by
i) taking
periodic MPS
as the system model for 1D many-body physics,
and ii) exploiting the “highway” connection from MPS tensors to CP maps (transfer operators),
so that MPS verification reduces to reasoning about algebraic objects
(CP maps and channels) familiar from QMC theory,
while the specification layer is redesigned to be spatial rather than temporal.

(3) Traditional approaches for analysing or certifying MPS.

MPS are the backbone of 1D many-body numerics and theory:
DMRG and MPS methods and tensor-network tooling provide efficient computation of energies and local observables,
and the transfer-matrix formalism enables scalable evaluation of expectation values
and correlations
[
37
,
31
,
33
,
12
]
.
Canonical forms, injectivity and parent-Hamiltonian techniques,
and spectral properties of transfer operators underpin much of the existing “verification” practiced in physics:
one typically checks consistency by computing energies, correlation sizes, or matching known invariants,
often for fixed sizes or in the thermodynamic limit under additional assumptions.
The gap is that these toolchains do not provide a
logic-based
,
property-driven model-checking interface for
size-indexed quantitative specifications

over the entire family
\{|\psi_{N}\rangle\}_{N\geq 1}
,
nor do they directly target decision problems
like eventual nonzeroness or dichotomy under periodic boundary conditions.
Our contribution is complementary:
we build a verification layer on top of the transfer-operator and CP-map structure,
developing approximate model checking that returns semilinear over/under-approximations of satisfying sizes
and systematically composes such results according to an input spatial formula (LCL),
enabling scalable certification of nontriviality and asymptotic spatial regimes beyond ad-hoc observable computations.

Preliminaries

This section recalls quantum states and Hamiltonian-governed evolution,
and then explains why ground states of 1D local many-body Hamiltonians are commonly represented
by matrix product states (MPS).
We finally define the
periodic
MPS studied in this paper and state the nonzeroness verification problem
that motivates our logic-based analysis.
For more background, see
[
30
,
9
]
.

2.1
Quantum states

State space
\mathcal{H}
.

A single quantum particle (or local degree of freedom) is described
by a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\cong\mathbb{C}^{d}
, where
d=\dim(\mathcal{H})
.
The notion
|\psi\rangle
denotes a (column) vector in
\mathcal{H}
,
which is not necessarily unit considered in this paper.
Only pure (quantum) states are referred to unit vectors
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
.
Fix an orthonormal computational basis
\{|1\rangle,\ldots,|d\rangle\}
of
\mathcal{H}
.
Then any pure state
|\psi\rangle
admits the expansion

|\psi\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}|k\rangle\quad\text{with}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{d}|a_{k}|^{2}=1.

(2)

We use Dirac notation (standard in quantum computing) throughout:

1.

\langle\psi|\coloneqq|\psi\rangle^{\dagger}
(conjugate transpose);

2.

\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle\coloneqq\langle\psi_{1}||\psi_{2}\rangle
(inner product);

3.

|\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\rangle\coloneqq|\psi_{1}\rangle\otimes|\psi_{2}\rangle
(tensor product).

For a linear operator (matrix)
X
, we write
X^{\dagger}
for the conjugate transpose
and
\mathrm{tr}(X)
for the trace (sum of diagonal entries).
For example, in a 1-qubit circuit model
[
30
]
,
the state space is 2-dimensional,
i. e.,
\mathcal{H}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
with computational basis
\{|1\rangle,|2\rangle\}
,
where
|1\rangle=(1,0)^{\mathsf{T}}
and
|2\rangle=(0,1)^{\mathsf{T}}
.
A 1-qubit pure state has the form

|\psi\rangle=\alpha|1\rangle+\beta|2\rangle
with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
,
satisfying
|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1
.

Tensor-product state space.

Consider a collection of
N
quantum particles,
each with local Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\cong\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
The global state space is the tensor power
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{all}}=\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}
,
whose dimension is
d^{N}
and thus grows exponentially with
N
.
A pure state on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{all}}
can be expanded in the computational basis

\Big\{|i_{1}\rangle\otimes|i_{2}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|i_{N}\rangle\ \Big|\ i_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}\Big\},

(3)

highlighting the intrinsic challenge of representing
and reasoning about large many-body (multiple-particle) quantum states.

2.2
Hamiltonian evolution and ground states

Hamiltonian evolution.

The evolution of an isolated quantum system is governed by a
Hamiltonian

H=H^{\dagger}
acting on
\mathcal{H}
.
The time evolution of a pure state follows Schrödinger’s differential equation

i\,\frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle=H|\psi(t)\rangle
,
whose solution is unitary:

|\psi(t)\rangle=U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle
with
U(t)=e^{-itH}
.
Some popular 1-qubit (2-dimensional) Hamiltonian (matrices) are the Pauli matrices

I=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad X=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad Y=\begin{bmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad Z=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix},

and the Hadamard matrix
\mathbf{H}=(X+Z)/\sqrt{2}
.
Additionally, the spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
matrices are
S^{x}=\tfrac{1}{2}X
,

S^{y}=\tfrac{1}{2}Y
and
S^{z}=\tfrac{1}{2}Z
.
They will be used to composite complicated Hamiltonian in our experiments.

Local Hamiltonians and ground states (1D chains).

A one-dimensional (1D) quantum many-body system consists of many (typically
N\gg 1
)
interacting quantum degrees of freedom (e. g., spins, atoms, or electrons arranged on a line),
whose joint state space is
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}
.
We focus on finite-range, translationally invariant 1D Hamiltonians of the form

H_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i,n}
,
where each local term
h_{i,n}
is centered at site
i
and acts nontrivially only on the
(n-1)
nearest sites
(interaction range
n
).
Let
E_{0}(N)=\min\mathrm{spec}(H_{N})
be the ground energy.
A normalised pure state
|\psi\rangle
is a
ground state
if
H_{N}|\psi\rangle=E_{0}(N)|\psi\rangle
.
Ground states play a central role in many-body physics: at low temperature,
equilibrium behavior is dominated by the ground space and the lowest-energy excitations,
and macroscopic order and correlations are largely reflected in ground-state structure.

2.3
Matrix product state representation

Why MPS for 1D ground states.

Although
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}
has exponential dimension,
1D ground states of local Hamiltonians often admit compact descriptions due to limited entanglement.
In particular, for broad classes of gapped 1D local Hamiltonians,
ground states satisfy an entanglement area law
[
23
]
,
which implies they can be approximated to arbitrary accuracy by MPS with sufficiently large bond dimension
D
,
with quantitative trade-offs between the bond dimension and the target error
[
3
]
.
Importantly, the required
D
is not determined solely by the interaction range:
it is governed by the amount of entanglement across bipartitions,
where for an exact MPS one has the entropy bound
S\leq\log D
.

Definition 1
(Periodic matrix product state)

Let
\{|1\rangle,\ldots,|d\rangle\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}\cong\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
Given a set of matrices
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}\subset\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}
,
the
periodic matrix product state
(MPS) on
N
sites is

|\psi_{N}\rangle\coloneqq\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{N}=1}^{d}\mathrm{tr}(A_{k_{1}}\cdots A_{k_{N}})\,|k_{1}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|k_{N}\rangle.

(4)

Here,
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
are called the tensors of the MPS,
and the virtual space that the matrices
A_{k}
act on is the bond space,
whose dimension
D
is independent of
N
.

With the tensors, we can define a family of MPS
\{|\psi_{N}\rangle\}_{N\geq 1}
,
which is determined by
d\cdot D^{2}
parameters.

The trace in (
4
)
identifies the two boundary virtual indices and closes the tensor network into a ring,
i. e., it imposes
periodic boundary conditions
.
Without that trace, it is led to
the boundary conditions

\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{N}}A_{k_{1}}\cdots A_{k_{N}}\,|k_{1}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|k_{N}\rangle
.
Throughout this paper we focus on
periodic
matrix product states;
for brevity, we will refer to them simply as matrix product states (MPS).

Ring-contraction view.

Equation (
4
) can be seen as a closed tensor-network contraction on an
N
-site ring,
as shown in Fig.
1
.
Accordingly,
|\psi_{N}\rangle\neq 0
means that this closed contraction does not vanish identically,
i. e., the local tensors
\{A_{k}\}
define a nontrivial many-body wavefunction on a ring of length
N
.

Problem 1
(MPS Nonzeroness Dichotomy)

Given a set of matrices
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}\subset\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}

defining
|\psi_{N}\rangle
in (
4
)
and an integer
J\geq 1
, decide:

•

(Always-nonzero after
J
) Does
|\psi_{N}\rangle\neq 0
hold for all
N\geq J
?

•

(Ultimately nonzero) Does there exist
N_{0}\geq J
such that
|\psi_{N}\rangle\neq 0
holds for all
N\geq N_{0}
?

Physical meaning.

Problem
1
asks
whether a fixed local tensor
\{A_{k}\}
yields a valid periodic many-body state on all sufficiently large rings,
or whether exact cancellations can force the periodic contraction to vanish for infinitely many system sizes.
This “eventual nontriviality” property is a natural target for
spatial
verification over the size axis
N
,
and will be expressed and checked using Linear Chain Logic proposed in Section
4
.

Example 1

We consider a 1D translation-invariant MPS with local dimension
d=3
and bond dimension
D=4

specified by the matrices
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\in\mathbb{C}^{4\times 4}
:

A_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{2}\\[4.0pt]
0&0&\alpha_{2}&\alpha_{1}\\[4.0pt]
\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{2}&0&0\\[4.0pt]
\alpha_{2}&\alpha_{1}&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad A_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\alpha_{3}&\alpha_{4}\\[4.0pt]
0&0&\alpha_{4}&\alpha_{3}\\[4.0pt]
\alpha_{3}&\alpha_{4}&0&0\\[4.0pt]
\alpha_{4}&\alpha_{3}&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad A_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{5}&\alpha_{6}&0&0\\[4.0pt]
\alpha_{6}&\alpha_{5}&0&0\\[4.0pt]
0&0&\alpha_{6}&\alpha_{5}\\[4.0pt]
0&0&\alpha_{5}&\alpha_{6}\end{bmatrix},

where
\alpha_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}
,

\alpha_{2}=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}
,

\alpha_{3}=\frac{\sqrt{3}\varpi}{6}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}
,

\alpha_{4}=-\frac{\sqrt{3}\varpi}{6}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}
,

\alpha_{5}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}

and
\alpha_{6}=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}
(
\varpi
denotes the 8th root of unity).
Then we get the famliy of MPS
\{|\psi_{N}\rangle\}_{N\geq 1}
,
in which
|\psi_{1}\rangle=0
,

|\psi_{2}\rangle=\tfrac{5}{3}|1,1\rangle-\tfrac{1+2i}{3}|2,2\rangle+\tfrac{2}{3}|3,3\rangle+(1+\tfrac{2}{3}\varpi)(|1,2\rangle+|2,1\rangle)
,
and so on.
The Dichotomy problem concerns
whether the MPS sequence
|\psi_{N}\rangle
is (ultimately) nonzero after a threshold
J
. ∎

2.4
Connection to quantum channel

A key bridge between MPS and our verification procedure is the
transition super-operator

induced by the local MPS tensors.
Given an MPS specified by matrices
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}\subset\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}
,
we define a linear map
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}\to\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}

as in
[
27
]
:

\mathcal{E}(X)\ \coloneqq\ \sum_{k=1}^{d}A_{k}\,X\,A_{k}^{\dagger}.

(5)

By construction,
\mathcal{E}
is completely positive (CP),
since it admits a Kraus form as in the Kraus representation theorem
[
7
]
.
The matrices
\{A_{k}\}
are called the Kraus operators of
\mathcal{E}
.
Moreover, if the tensors satisfy the
trace-preserving
condition
\sum_{k=1}^{d}A_{k}^{\dagger}A_{k}=I_{D}
,

\mathcal{E}
is a
quantum channel
(CPTP map).
In this case,
\mathcal{E}^{N}
corresponds to
N
repeated steps of the same process.
Equivalently,
(\mathbb{C}^{D},\mathcal{E})
can be viewed as
a
quantum Markov chain

[
14
]
with transition super-operator
\mathcal{E}
.
Finally, the periodic contraction of the MPS admits a convenient expression through this transfer map:
many scalar observables relevant to our logic (e. g., norms and correlation functions)
reduce to quantities of the form
\mathrm{tr}(M^{N})
,
where
M
is the matrix representation of
\mathcal{E}
,
which is detailed in Section
3
.
The reduction enables spatial verification of MPS by analysing the iterates of
\mathcal{E}
.

3
Inner Product of MPS

In this section, as a first step toward solving the dichotomy problem (Problem
1
),
we study the inner product of MPS
|\psi_{N}\rangle
for a given
N
.
It also serves as a fundamental subroutine for our model-checking procedures in Section
5
,
where we lift single-size reasoning to
asymptotic
guarantee, including the dichotomy problem and
other physically meaningful properties expressible in our logic.

Firstly, we reduce the nonzeroness of a periodic MPS to a scalar quantity derived directly from its local tensors.
Since
|\psi_{N}\rangle=0
if and only if its squared norm
\|\psi_{N}\|^{2}=\langle\psi_{N}|\psi_{N}\rangle
is zero,
our nonzeroness questions boil down to reasoning about a scalar sequence computed from the MPS data.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the CP map induced by the MPS tensors
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
.
Then, the Liouville matrix representation
[
39
]
is

M_{\mathcal{E}}\ \coloneqq\ \sum_{k=1}^{d}A_{k}^{*}\otimes A_{k}\quad\text{for}\quad\mathcal{E}(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}A_{k}\,X\,A_{k}^{\dagger},

(6)

where
A^{*}
denotes entry-wise complex conjugation.
A direct tensor-network contraction (or a trace manipulation) yields:

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{N}|\psi_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\dotsc,k_{N}=1}^{d}\mathrm{tr}(A_{k_{N}}^{\dagger}\cdots A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger})\;\mathrm{tr}(A_{k_{1}}\cdots A_{k_{N}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\dotsc,k_{N}=1}^{d}\mathrm{tr}(A_{k_{N}}^{*}\cdots A_{k_{1}}^{*})\;\mathrm{tr}(A_{k_{1}}\cdots A_{k_{N}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\dotsc,k_{N}=1}^{d}\mathrm{tr}\Big((A_{k_{N}}^{*}\cdots A_{k_{1}}^{*})\otimes(A_{k_{1}}\cdots A_{k_{N}})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\dotsc,k_{N}=1}^{d}\mathrm{tr}\Big((A_{k_{1}}^{*}\!\otimes\!A_{k_{1}})\cdots(A_{k_{N}}^{*}\!\otimes\!A_{k_{N}})\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\Big(\big[\sum_{k=1}^{d}A_{k}^{*}\!\otimes\!A_{k}\big]^{N}\Big)=\mathrm{tr}\big(M_{\mathcal{E}}^{\,N}\big).

Therefore, the squared norms of the MPS family
\{|\psi_{N}\rangle\}_{N\geq 1}
form the scalar sequence

\{\mathrm{tr}(M_{\mathcal{E}}^{\,N})\}_{N\geq 1}
.
Equivalently, they are determined by the discrete-time quantum evolution on the bond space

(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bond}},\mathcal{E})
with
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bond}}\cong\mathbb{C}^{D}
.
This reduction allows us to explore
\|\psi_{N}\|
symbolically via linear-algebraic and spectral properties of

M_{\mathcal{E}}
(or
\mathcal{E}
), and will be the basis of our model-checking algorithms.

To understand the long-run behavior of
\mathrm{tr}(M_{\mathcal{E}}^{\,N})
and hence the asymptotic validity
and periodic phenomena of
|\psi_{N}\rangle
,
we analyse the spectral structure of
M_{\mathcal{E}}

(or equivalently of
\mathcal{E}
under vectorization).

Peripheral eigenvalues.

By standard linear algebra
[
24
, Sect. 7.3]
, we have that
every (square) matrix
X
is unitarily similar to a Jordan canonical form
with all eigenvalues (counted with algebraic multiplicity) of
X
as diagonal entries,
and thereby the trace of
X
amounts to the sum of all eigenvalues.
Writing the eigenvalues of
M_{\mathcal{E}}

as
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{D^{2}}
yields

\mathrm{tr}(M_{\mathcal{E}}^{\,N})=\sum_{i=1}^{D^{2}}\lambda_{i}^{N}.

(7)

Eigenvalues of maximal modulus
\rho(M_{\mathcal{E}})
are called
peripheral
.
When
N
is large, the terms

dominate the sum,
and their arguments determine whether

exhibits oscillations or periodic patterns.
However, clean structural characterizations of the peripheral spectrum are available primarily for

CP maps. We therefore reduce

to an irreducible form while preserving

.

Invariant subspaces and block reduction.

Let
\mathcal{H}\cong\mathbb{C}^{D}
be the bond space.
A subspace

is

for
\mathcal{E}

if

for all

.
Then, we have:

Lemma 1
(Refinement)

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
be an invariant subspace for
\mathcal{E}
,
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
the orthogonal complement.
Then the MPS defined by the Kraus operators of
\mathcal{E}
can be decomposed
into the two MPS defined by the Kraus operators restricted to
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and to
\mathcal{H}_{2}
, respectively.

Irreducible decomposition.

By iterating the refinement with Lemma

we obtain an orthogonal decomposition

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{m}\mathcal{H}_{m}

such that each
\mathcal{H}_{m}
is invariant for
\mathcal{E}
and the restriction

\mathcal{E}_{m}\coloneqq\mathcal{E}\!\mid_{\mathcal{H}_{m}}

there is no nontrivial projector

on
\mathcal{H}_{m}
with

,
where

means the set of linear operators on
\mathcal{H}_{m}
.
Let

denote the projector onto

We provide the construction steps for analysing the complexity of our method afterwards.
This reduction isolates the components that govern the asymptotic behavior and enables a sharp

4
Formal Logic Specification

Matrix Product State (MPS) is a popular formalism for representing quantum states in many-body systems.
Traditional temporal logics such as Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)
have been adapted to reason about properties of MPS
by interpreting the chain length as a temporal index.
However, this is a conceptual mismatch:
in MPS, we reason about spatial properties along a one-dimensional chain, not temporal evolution.
This motivates the introduction of
Linear Chain Logic (LCL)
:
a logic designed specifically to express and verify properties of quantum states along spatial chains.

4.1
Syntax and semantics of LCL

Let
\text{val}_{N}
denote a value (to be determined in detail below) extracted from
the MPS
|\psi_{N}\rangle
at chain length
N
.
We start with basic properties of MPS that should be admitted by the logic:

•

[\text{val}_{N}>\varepsilon]
: the value at the current MPS exceeds a threshold,

•

[|\text{val}_{N}-\text{val}_{1}|<\delta]
: correlation with the initial MPS is small,

•

[\frac{\text{val}_{N+1}}{\text{val}_{N}}<\gamma]
: exponential decay rate.

The first property usually needs to decide
whether the current energy
\|\psi_{N}\|^{2}\linebreak=\langle\psi_{N}|\psi_{N}\rangle
of MPS
|\psi_{N}\rangle

meets an interval predicate
\in\mathcal{I}
;
if true, a corresponding label should be assigned to the current MPS
|\psi_{N}\rangle
.
For this end, we introduce the
value expression

\langle\psi_{N}|\psi_{N}\rangle

and the
atomic formula

\langle\psi_{N}|\psi_{N}\rangle\in\mathcal{I}

as the continuous-time linear logic (CLL)
[
19
]
.
The second property compares the current MPS
|\psi_{N}\rangle
with the past one
|\psi_{1}\rangle

in terms of energy.
So we need to pre-compute the constant value
\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{1}\rangle
for the use afterwards.
The last property proposes the comparison
between an expression over the energies of the MPS and a threshold,
thus the value expression should be correspondingly generalised to admit:
i) a linear expression and even a ratio of them;
ii) the energies of the current MPS, the next ones,
and the initial ones involved as the variables in those expressions.
Unlike classic first-order logics where predicate and atomic formula are the same thing,
we design them in two layers —

interval predicates
are the preparatory construct prior to
atomic formulas
in LCL.
Such a syntactical separation allows us to specify the properties of MPS precisely.

Example 4

For the MPS
|\psi_{N}\rangle
defined in Example
1
,
we consider the quantitative characterisation of its squared norm,
namely
\Gamma_{\textup{sqr}}(N)=\langle\psi_{N}|\psi_{N}\rangle
,
which is a
value expression
introduced here.
Other value expressions like
\Gamma_{\textup{dif}}(N)=\langle\psi_{N}|\psi_{N}\rangle-\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{1}\rangle

and
\Gamma_{\textup{rat}}(N)=\langle\psi_{N+1}|\psi_{N+1}\rangle/\langle\psi_{N}|\psi_{N}\rangle

are introduced similarly.
Let
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{pos}}
denote the positive interval
(0,\infty)
.
We introduce the label
\ell_{\textup{sqr,pos}}
to represent the
atomic formula

that the current value expression falls into the positive interval,
such that the label
\ell_{\textup{sqr,pos}}
is assigned to the MPS
|\psi_{N}\rangle

if and only if
\Gamma_{\textup{sqr}}(N)\in\mathcal{I}_{\textup{pos}}
. ∎

To specify the essential properties mentioned above,
we propose the formal definition of LCL as follows.

Definition 2
(Syntax)

An LCL formula
\phi
is finitely generated by the syntax:

\displaystyle\Phi\ \Coloneqq\

\displaystyle\text{true}\mid\ell\mid\neg\Phi\mid\Phi_{1}\wedge\Phi_{2}\mid\mathsf{X}\,\Phi\mid\mathsf{E}\,\Phi\mid\mathsf{G}\,\Phi

where
\ell
is a label representing some atomic formula over site-indexed observables or quantities,
and
\mathsf{X}
(next),
\mathsf{E}
(eventually),
\mathsf{G}
(globally) are
the spatial operator to be interpreted along the chain.

Here we do not include the until formula
\Phi_{1}\,\mathsf{U}\,\Phi_{2}
as the standard LTL formulas,
because of simplification, no technical hardness.
In contrast to the until formula,
as the MPS are usually investigated in the limit behavior
N\to\infty
,
we should pay less attention to those non-ultimate states.
Hence the composite formula
\mathsf{E}\,\mathsf{G}\,\Phi

would be of particular interest in verifying MPS.

As our intention that the LCL is devised for MPS,
we need a transformer from atomic formulas to labels,
which is constructed as follows.
For the current MPS
|\psi_{N}\rangle
,
if a value expression
\Gamma_{s}
on
|\psi_{N}\rangle
falls into a range (interval)
\mathcal{I}_{t}
,
i. e.
\Gamma_{s}(N)\in\mathcal{I}_{t}
,
a corresponding label
\ell_{s,t}
would be assigned to this
|\psi_{N}\rangle
.
Namely, we interpret LCL on the chain model
\mathcal{C}=(S,\mathcal{V},L)
of MPS, where:

•

S=\{|\psi_{N}\rangle\}
is a (possibly infinite) sequence of chain lengths,

•

\mathcal{V}
is the set of all labels
\ell

used to represent distinct value expressions falling into distinct intervals,

•

L:S\to\mathcal{V}
is a labeling function that maps each chain length to labels.

In this model,
(\mathcal{C},N)
denotes the MPS
|\psi_{N}\rangle
of length
N
in
S
.
Then, we have:

Definition 3
(Semantics)

Under the chain model
\mathcal{C}=(S,\mathcal{V},L)
,
the semantics of LCL is given by the satisfaction relation
(\mathcal{C},N)\models\Phi
as follows:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{C},N)

\displaystyle\models\text{true}

always holds

\displaystyle(\mathcal{C},N)

\displaystyle\models\ell

\displaystyle\text{if }\ell\in L(|\psi_{N}\rangle)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{C},N)

\displaystyle\models\neg\Phi

\displaystyle\text{if }(\mathcal{C},N)\not\models\Phi

\displaystyle(\mathcal{C},N)

\displaystyle\models\Phi_{1}\wedge\Phi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{if }(\mathcal{C},N)\models\Phi_{1}\text{ and }(\mathcal{C},N)\models\Phi_{2}

\displaystyle(\mathcal{C},N)

\displaystyle\models\mathsf{X}\,\Phi

\displaystyle\text{if }(\mathcal{C},N+1)\models\Phi

\displaystyle(\mathcal{C},N)

\displaystyle\models\mathsf{E}\,\Phi

\displaystyle\text{if }\exists\,j\geq N,(\mathcal{C},j)\models\Phi

\displaystyle(\mathcal{C},N)

\displaystyle\models\mathsf{G}\,\Phi

\displaystyle\text{if }\forall\,j\geq N,(\mathcal{C},j)\models\Phi.

Consider the sequence

of MPS

as well as

Actually, we notice that
the truth of LCL formulas relies on the notion:

Definition 4
(Evidence Set)

Under a chain model
\mathcal{C}
,
the evidence set of an LCL formula
\Phi
is the set
\{\,N:(\mathcal{C},N)\models\Phi\,\}
.

Once we obtain those evidence sets, deciding LCL formulas can be reduced to set operation,
which will be developed in the next section.

4.2
Expressivity of LCL: more physically meaningful properties

Using LCL, we can express a rich class of physically meaningful properties as follows,
which will be composed to complicated ones for checking in Section
6
.

1.

State validity:

\mathsf{G}\,(\text{val}_{N}>0)

with
\text{val}_{N}
denoting the energy
\langle\psi_{N}|\psi_{N}\rangle
(and the same below).
It exactly addresses the dichotomy problem (Problem
1
).

2.

Energy bounds:

\mathsf{G}\,(\text{val}_{N}\in(1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon))
,
where
\varepsilon
is the perturbation tolerance.
It concerns the stability of the CPTP map induced by some local MPS tensors.

3.

Finite correlation size:

\mathsf{E}\,\mathsf{G}\,(|\text{corr}_{1,N}|<\varepsilon)

with
\text{corr}_{1,N}
denoting the difference
\text{val}_{N}-\text{val}_{1}
.
It asks whether the energy of the MPS
would have a difference less than
\varepsilon
with the initial energy eventually always.

4.

Ratio decay:

\mathsf{G}\,(|\text{val}_{N+1}/\text{val}_{N}|<\gamma)
.
It asks whether the energy of the MPS would always decay exponentially at a rate less than
\gamma
.

5.

Oscillatory correlation:

\mathsf{G}\,\big(\text{sign}(\text{corr}_{1,N})\neq\text{sign}(\text{corr}_{1,N+1})\big)
.
It asks if the energy of the MPS would always fluctuate around the initial energy.

6.

Periodicity:

\mathsf{G}\,(\text{val}_{N}=\text{val}_{N+k})
,
where
k\in\mathbb{N}
is a fixed period.
It expects that the energy of the MPS would have a fixed period of
k
.

7.

Phase transition indicator:

\mathsf{E}\,\mathsf{G}\,(|\text{corr}_{i,j}|>\delta)
,
where
\text{corr}_{i,j}
is a selected two-size correlation function.
It expects that the energy difference of two MPS with size gap
|j-i|
would exceed a threshold
\delta
eventually always.

The properties considered in Problem
1
can be specified by the LCL formulas:

\mathsf{X}^{J-1}\,\mathsf{G}\,\ell_{\textup{sqr,pos}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{X}^{J-1}\,\mathsf{E}\,\mathsf{G}\,\ell_{\textup{sqr,pos}}.

LCL captures spatial reasoning along quantum chains,
aligning better with physical intuition and tensor network semantics than traditional temporal logics.
It provides a formal language to verify decay properties, symmetry, validity, and large-size order in MPS.

5
Model Checking Algorithms

In this section we present the algorithm for checking LCL formulas for MPS.
It attempts to compute the evidence set of a given LCL formula,
following a topdown fashion,
which in the end is reduced to the ground of computing the evidence sets of atomic formulas.
Mathematically, it amounts to the sign determination of exponential polynomials,
closely similar to the Skolem problem
[
21
]
and its variants
— the positivity and the ultimate positivity problems.
However, due to the hardness of the Skolem problems,
we approximate the evidence set of an atomic formula from above and from below using semilinear sets.
It further yields an over-approximation and an under-approximation of the evidence set of the given LCL formula.

From intuition, the proof idea can be explained as follows.

Whether its elements falling into a given interval

Extending the sieve method,

i) the sign of each arithmetic progression is exponentially stable in the
ultimate

ii) there exists a
computable

after which the sign of the arithmetic progression is exponentially stable.
If so, the elements after that threshold are sign-invariant;
if they fulfill the sign condition,
all of them are contributed to some arithmetic progression

the elements before that threshold need to be checked one by one,
which are contributed to the finite set
\Lambda_{0}
.
Otherwise, the arithmetic progression has complicated sign condition,
which is
indecisive
.
We include it by the over-approximation but exclude by the under-approximation.

We have seen that
the evidence set of an LCL formula can be approximated by the structure of semilinear sets,
since the family of semilinear sets is closed

7
Conclusion

We broadened quantum model checking from programs and protocols to 1D many-body physics,
where properties were naturally indexed by the chain length.
Our key bridge was the transfer-operator view: a periodic MPS induced a CP map,
turning MPS contractions into traces of matrix powers and reducing nonzeroness to a tractable scalar sequence.
On top of this, we introduced LCL for specifying size-indexed spatial properties
of MPS families and gave approximate model-checking algorithms with sound over/under-approximations.
Our experiments covered two sets of models:
i) scalable synthetic channel families to stress-test large bond dimensions,
and ii) ground-state MPS from standard spin-chains to reflect realistic physical instances.
Experimental results showed that the checker could efficiently certify many properties in gapped and mixing regimes,
and suggested that logic-based verification would be a practical complement
to traditional tensor-network analysis for many-body physics, like PEPS and MERA
[
9
]
.

0.A.1
Details on model preparation

We consider two families of models:
(A)
synthetic-but-structured
scalable channel families, and
(B)
physical
spin-chain families represented by their translation-invariant ground-state MPS tensors.
The two families serve distinct roles in our evaluation:
synthetic families are used to stress-test scalability up to large bond dimensions (
D=128
),
while physical spin-chain families are evaluated at moderate bond dimensions (
D\leq 32
),
reflecting realistic limits of infinite-system tensor-network solvers near criticality.

(A) Synthetic scalable channel families.

For each model family, we begin with a fixed set of
2\times 2
Kraus operators

\{K_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
defining a CPTP map.

•

AKLT-like

is a gapped, fast-mixing proxy inspired by valence-bond constructions (finite correlation size).
We use three Kraus operators

K_{+}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}\sigma^{+},\qquad K_{0}=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}Z,\qquad K_{-}=-\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}\sigma^{-},

where
\sigma^{\pm}=\tfrac{1}{2}(X\pm iY)
.

•

Cluster-like

is a stabilizer/Clifford-like proxy exhibiting structured mixing
(useful for bounded observables and oscillation tests):

K_{1}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbf{H},\qquad K_{2}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbf{H}Z,

where
\mathbf{H}
is the Hadamard matrix
(X+Z)/{\sqrt{2}}
.

•

Random-gapped

is a controllable gapped family defined by Pauli noise:

K_{0}=\sqrt{1-2\epsilon_{5}}\,I,\qquad K_{1}=\sqrt{\epsilon}_{5}\,X,\qquad K_{2}=\sqrt{\epsilon}_{5}\,Z,

with a fixed
\epsilon_{5}=0.05
.

•

Near-critical

is a slow-mixing proxy with a small effective gap (hard instances; large unknown zones):

K_{1}=\sqrt{1-\epsilon_{1}}\,\mathrm{diag}(1,e^{i\theta}),\qquad K_{2}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}}\,Z,

with
\theta=0.98\pi
and
\epsilon_{1}=0.01
.

•

Periodic

is a non-primitive periodic transfer structure inducing oscillatory correlations:

U=\mathrm{diag}(1,i),\quad K_{1}=\sqrt{1-\epsilon_{2}}\,U,\quad K_{2}=\sqrt{\tfrac{\epsilon_{2}}{2}}\,X,\quad K_{3}=\sqrt{\tfrac{\epsilon_{2}}{2}}\,Y,

with
\epsilon_{2}=0.02
.

To scale from the base
2\times 2
channel to bond dimension
D=2^{t}
,
we take the self-tensor power of the Kraus operators:

K_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{t})}\coloneqq K_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes K_{i_{t}}.

(B) Physical spin-chain families.

We additionally include three standard many-body models
and use their translation-invariant ground states represented as MPS tensors.
For each physical model,
we choose bond dimensions
D\in\{16,32\}
,
which are representative of physically meaningful
and computationally feasible truncation levels for infinite-system tensor-network solvers.
The MPS tensors
\{A_{1},A_{2}\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}

are obtained via a standard infinite-system TN routine (e. g., iDMRG/VUMPS)
at representative parameter regimes (gapped and near-critical where applicable),
and then they are gauge-fixed to a stable canonical form.

•

TFIM

is a transverse-field Ising model
H=-J\sum_{i}Z_{i}Z_{i+1}-h\sum_{i}X_{i}
,
exhibiting gapped and critical phases depending on
h/J
,
where the subscript
i
labels the lattice site for which the Pauli operators
X,Z
act on.

•

XXZ

is a spin-chain

H=\sum_{i}(S_{i}^{x}S_{i+1}^{x}+S_{i}^{y}S_{i+1}^{y}+\Delta S_{i}^{z}S_{i+1}^{z})
,
with both gapped and critical regimes,
where
S_{i}^{x,y,z}
denote the spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
operators acting on site
i
.

•

Kitaev chain

is a 1D topological superconducting chain with gapped and near-critical parameter regimes,
in which oscillatory correlations may arise.

0.A.2
Physical meaning of LCL Properties

This subsection provides the rigorous physical justification for the LCL properties raised in Subsections
4.2
and
6.2

Proof of Lemma
1

As
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}
,
we can obtain the two Hermitian projectors
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
,
satisfying
P_{1}\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
P_{2}\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{2}
and
P_{1}+P_{2}=I
.
In a basis adapted to this decomposition, each Kraus operator
A_{k}
of
\mathcal{E}
has an upper block-triangular form

A_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{k,11}&A_{k,12}\\
0&A_{k,22}\end{bmatrix}.

Define the block-diagonal part

B_{k}\coloneqq P_{1}A_{k}P_{1}+P_{2}A_{k}P_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{k,11}&0\\
0&A_{k,22}\end{bmatrix}.

Since
A_{k_{1}}\cdots A_{k_{N}}
remains upper block-triangular and the trace depends only on the diagonal blocks,
we have that for every word
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{N})
,

\mathrm{tr}(A_{k_{1}}\cdots A_{k_{N}})=\mathrm{tr}(B_{k_{1}}\cdots B_{k_{N}}).

Consequently, the MPS generated by
\{A_{k}\}
coincides with the one generated by
\{B_{k}\}
,
and in particular their norms satisfy
\mathrm{tr}(M_{\mathcal{E}}^{\,N})=\mathrm{tr}(M_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}^{\,N})
for all
N
,
where
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(X)=\sum_{k}B_{k}XB_{k}^{\dagger}
.

Proof of Lemma
3

It suffices to consider how to compare the value expression
\Gamma
on
|\psi_{N}\rangle

with any given constant (taken from the endpoints of
\mathcal{I}
),
when the parameter
N
varies.
For simplification, we assume that the value expression
\Gamma(N)
is exactly
\langle\psi_{N}|\psi_{N}\rangle
;
otherwise the employed approach works similarly.
Mathematically, it is exactly the sign determination problem

\underbrace{c_{1,1}\lambda_{1,1}^{N}+\cdots+c_{1,d_{1}}\lambda_{1,d_{1}}^{N}}_{\text{largest modulus}}\ +\ \underbrace{c_{2,1}\lambda_{2,1}^{N}+\cdots+c_{2,d_{2}}\lambda_{2,d_{2}}^{N}}_{\text{second largest modulus}}\ +\ \cdots,

where
c_{i,j}
are
\mathbb{C}
-coefficients
and
\lambda_{i,j}
are
\mathbb{C}
-bases from the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{E}
, sorted by their modulus.
Note that the sum of all terms of same modulus is always a real number
[
21
]
.
We assume w. l. o. g. that the eigenvalues of the largest modulus are unit,
since otherwise they can be normalised to be unit.
Then:

1.

By Lemma
2
, all terms of the largest modulus are periodic (with
N
),
as well as their sum.
If the sum hits zero periodically,
we need the terms of the second largest modulus to determine the sign.
Otherwise the sign determination is plainly completed.
(There is a computable threshold
N_{1}
,
after which the sum of all terms of the largest modulus are exponentially dominating the rest.)

2.

At those MPS in which the sum of all terms of the largest modulus hits zero,
if the sum of all terms of the second largest modulus is also periodic,
the case is reduced to the above;
or if the sum is strictly definite, the case is solved.
(There is a computable threshold
N_{2}
,
after which the sum of all terms of the second largest modulus are exponentially dominating the rest.)

3.

Otherwise, either the sum crosses zero infinitely often,
which is not exponentially dominating in the ultimate;
or the sum approaches zero arbitrarily close,
which is not ensure the computability of the threshold in general,
as the
effective
ultimate positivity problem is still open
[
32
]
.
