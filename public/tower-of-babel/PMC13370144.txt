Testing the applicability of the Parenting Occupations and Purposes Framework in an Irish context
Authors: Emma Dempsey, Laura Manley, Anne Honey, Yu Zhuang Germaine Lim, Yu‐Wei Chen, Margaret McGrath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUnderstanding the form, function, and meaning of occupations is essential for effective occupational therapy practice. Parenting is widely recognised as a meaningful and valued occupation. The Parenting Occupations and Purposes (POP) Framework conceptualises parenting occupations and their underlying purposes, but its validity in real‐world contexts has not been tested. This study aimed to evaluate the applicability of the POP Framework among parents in Ireland.MethodsAn exploratory descriptive design using Experience Sampling Methodology captured real‐time data on parenting occupations and purposes via the Participation In Everyday Life mobile application. Data were analysed using descriptive and inferential statistics as well as deductive and inductive content analysis to assess the framework's applicability to the sample.Consumer and Community InvolvementCollaboration with the POP Framework developers ensured accurate application of its concepts. Partnership with the developer of the Participation In Everyday Life mobile application supported correct setup and usability.ResultsForty‐four parents completed 1150 surveys over 7 days. All categories in the POP Framework were represented. The most frequent occupation was attending to the child's basic needs (223 occurrences) while seeking help appeared only once. Among purposes, having the child's basic needs met dominated (571 occurrences), whereas purposes related to continuous parental development were least frequent. New examples of occupations emerged, including faith‐based activities, purchasing discretionary goods, and technology‐mediated monitoring (e.g., texting a spouse to check on a child), suggesting areas for framework expansion.ConclusionFindings support the validity of the POP Framework in a real‐world context while highlighting opportunities for refinement to capture cultural and technological dimensions of parenting. Enhancing the framework may improve understanding of parenting occupations and inform more comprehensive occupational therapy services for parents.

INTRODUCTION

Occupational therapy practice is centred around occupation (American Occupational Therapy Association [AOTA], 2020; Kielhofner, 2009). The ways in which occupational therapists understand and use the concept of occupation shape practice, guiding what is done, which occupations are considered, and how they are assessed (Laliberte Rudman et al., 2022). However, the complexities of occupation are not universally understood, which can limit its potential to be used therapeutically (Wilcock & Hocking, 2015). A thorough understanding of the form, function, and meaning of occupations is therefore necessary to accurately inform practice (Hocking & Wright‐St Clair, 2011).

Parenting is identified in occupational therapy literature as an important occupation (Lampe et al., 2019; Pituch et al., 2024), falling under the category of ‘instrumental activities of daily living’ as classified by the AOTA (2020). Parenting is broadly described as supporting the developmental needs of a child (AOTA, 2020). Parenting is a valued role which informs parents' daily routines and responsibilities and is often central to their identity (Lampe et al., 2019; Pituch et al., 2024). Despite this, McGrath et al. (2024) highlight an inadequate understanding of parenting within occupational therapy literature. Although there is a wealth of occupational therapy research into child‐related outcomes of parenting (Bourke‐Taylor, 2017; Hanna & Rodger, 2002), there is a lack of consideration of parenting from a ‘parent‐centric’ perspective, which carries the risk of overlooking parent's experiences of parenting (McGrath et al., 2024).

This gap in literature is mirrored in clinical practice, where parenting is frequently neglected (Honey et al., 2024, 2025)—even in the presence of evident concerns—due to knowledge gaps and inadequate professional resources (Lampe et al., 2019; Lim et al., 2022). Existing assessment methods often fail to capture the full scope of parenting tasks and challenges, thereby limiting clinicians' ability to identify and address support needs effectively (Pastor‐Bédard et al., 2022).

This is a critical issue because many parents, particularly those living with varying challenges, such as physical disabilities, cognitive impairments, and neurological disorders, have unmet needs in relation to their parenting role (Pituch et al., 2024). Occupational therapists are well positioned to support parents in navigating these challenges (McGrath et al., 2024); however, these services are often underdeveloped or non‐existent (Pituch et al., 2024). While occupational therapy has the potential to support parents who may be experiencing barriers to fulfilling their parenting role (Lampe et al., 2019; McGrath et al., 2024; Pituch et al., 2024), meaningful support can only be provided if research and literature provide a thorough understanding of parenting and the associated values, needs, and challenges (Lim et al., 2022; McGrath et al., 2024).

In light of this gap in the understanding of parenting as an occupation, (Lim et al., 2022) developed the Parenting Occupations and Purposes [POP] Framework (see Figure 1), which conceptualises the occupations that parents do and their purposes, based on existing occupational therapy literature. The framework has two sections: the occupations frame and the purposes frame. The occupations frame classifies parenting occupations into 10 different categories, namely, (i) attending to the child's basic needs, (ii) moving the child, (iii) providing materially for the child, (iv) managing the child's health and protecting from harm, (v) communicating with the child, (vi) playing and engaging in child‐focused leisure, (vii) providing the child with emotional support and stability, (viii) teaching and facilitating the child's learning, (ix) creating a supportive environment for the child, and (x) managing the child's social networks (Lim et al., 2022). It is also identified that when performing parenting occupations, parents are constantly ‘looking in’ to monitor the child's needs and ‘looking out’ to monitor the child's surroundings. The purposes frame outlines the main purposes of parenting occupations, situating these occupations within their contexts. It specifies the main purposes of these occupations, under the categories of fulfilling children's basic, developmental, and social needs, as well as improving one's own parenting capacity through continuous parental development. Four additional occupation categories for the purpose of continuous parental development are also identified, namely, (i) seeking help, (ii) improving skills and knowledge, (iii) setting up for success, and (iv) managing resources. These occupations serve to strengthen parents' capacity to effectively engage in the occupations defined by the occupations frame.

While the POP Framework offers a first step towards providing a holistic understanding of the occupations of parenting, which may ultimately contribute to enhanced interventions and support systems for parents (Lim et al., 2022; McGrath et al., 2024), its ecological validity remains untested. This is particularly important because the framework was based entirely on existing occupational therapy literature and 61% originated from the United States (Lim et al., 2022). This may present a narrow perspective of parenting as an occupation, given that parenting practices and experiences of parenting can vary across different countries, societies, and cultures (Murray, 2011; Prevoo & Tamis‐LeMonda, 2017). Furthermore, while the materials which informed the POP Framework were published between 1981 and 2020, 55% of the included texts (n = 75) were published more than 10 years ago, raising questions about their current relevance and applicability.

Thus, this paper aims to broaden and validate the understanding of parenting outlined in the POP Framework by testing its capacity to capture an occupational perspective of contemporary parenting practices. Specifically, we sought to investigate the occupations that parents engage in over the course of a week and the extent to which these align with the POP Framework.

METHODS

Research design

This study employed an exploratory, descriptive approach to collect data on the occupations of parenting engaged in by parents in Ireland and their associated purposes. To gather this data, Experience Sampling Methodology (ESM) was employed (Hektner et al., 2007). ESM has been found to be a valid and reliable method of collecting real‐time data on the variability of people's engagement in occupations in their natural environment (Chen et al., 2017; Liddle et al., 2017; Wilson et al., 2020). ESM is particularly appropriate for this study as it collects data in the moment, thus providing an accurate, ecologically valid representation of the complexity and variability of parenting occupations, supporting the identification of patterns and routines that may not be easily captured through retrospective methods. This study obtained ethical approval from the School of Clinical Therapies, University College Cork, Social Research Ethics Committee (2024–2025).

Positionality statement

The research team consists of the three authors of the POP Framework (authors 3, 4, and 6), one of the developers of the ESM application used in this study (author 5), and two final year occupational therapy honours students (authors 1 and 2). The students were supervised by author 6 and were involved in the project as part of their final year research project.

The involvement of authors in developing the POP Framework informed the conceptual orientation of the study, sensitising the research team to issues of participation, context, and parenting as a meaningful life role. To mitigate the risk of privileging the framework over participants' accounts, data analysis prioritises inductive coding and remained grounded in parents' responses, with analytic decisions discussed across the team. The contribution of an author involved in the development of the ESM application shaped methodological choices related to data collection, including the feasibility and design of momentary data capture within everyday parenting contexts.

The inclusion of student researchers influenced both methodological rigour and interpretation. Their position as learners encouraged reflexive questioning of assumptions, close attention to participants' language, and transparency in analytic decision making. Reflexivity was further supported through regular supervisory and team meetings, during which researchers explicitly considered how their professional roles, theoretical commitments, and experiences shaped interpretation. These diverse perspectives were treated as analytic resources, strengthening the credibility and transparency of the study.

Data collection tools

ESM surveys

After completing a brief demographic questionnaire, participants completed a series of ESM surveys. ESM surveys were delivered using the Participation In Everyday Life [PIEL] Survey application, a mobile application developed to enable real‐time responses to custom survey questions (Jessup et al., 2012). Participants entered their available hours, identifying a timeframe in which survey prompts would appear. Survey promoters were randomly scheduled within these availability windows to reduce systematic bias and ensure responses were distributed across different times of day. Participants were then randomly signalled through an audio and pop‐up alert to respond to an ESM survey five times daily for 7 consecutive days, enabling the capture of the variety of occupations parents may participate in across different days of the week. Surveys expired after 20 min to ensure ecological validity and prevent retrospective bias and reconstructed data (Van Berkel et al., 2017).

Each survey included two or three questions:What are you doing right now? (free text)Does this activity contribute to your role as a parent? (Yes/No)(If yes), how does this activity contribute to your role as a parent? (free text)

A minimum response threshold of 33% was applied to ensure that included data reflected participants' everyday lives rather than sporadic engagement. This criterion aligns with recommendations in ESM literature for maintaining ecological validity and reducing retrospective bias (Van Berkel et al., 2017). Upon receipt, participants' data were transferred securely from the PIEL application to a UCC OneDrive with any identifying information removed and instantly deleted from the application.A pilot study with five participants was conducted to test the clarity, accessibility, and usability of the survey. Feedback from this phase informed minor adjustments to the wording of the survey questions and an extension of the expiry window from 5 to 20 min. Data from the pilot study were not included in the main analysis.

Demographic questionnaire

On commencement of the study, participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire via Qualtrics. This information provided a more contextualised understanding of occupational engagement and parenting roles among participants.

Study procedure

Participation was voluntary, and all participants were provided with a detailed participant information statement and had the opportunity to discuss participation with the researchers, prior to providing informed consent.

Once enrolled, participants completed the demographic questionnaire and submitted an email address to receive study materials. Participants were then emailed with written instructions on how to download and set up the PIEL application on their mobile device and how to complete the surveys. Contact details for the research team were provided in case any questions or technical issues arose.

Participants were asked to use the application for 7 days. They were encouraged to complete surveys immediately upon receiving notifications and to answer questions based on what they were doing at that moment in time.

Sample

There are currently no large‐scale studies of parenting occupations and purposes, meaning it was not possible to conduct a formal calculation for the sample size for this study. However, previous research utilising ESM with adults suggests that a sample size of between 29 and 45 is appropriate for studies of this nature (Chen et al., 2017; Liddle et al., 2017; Wilson et al., 2020). It was acknowledged that a high dropout rate is likely with this method (Van Berkel et al., 2017); therefore, the target sample size was adjusted to 80 to account for this.

Participants were recruited using cross‐sectional convenience sampling via radio advertisements and social media advertisements, including Instagram, Facebook, X, and LinkedIn, as well as snowball sampling. Additionally, advertisement posters were positioned in areas where parents would frequently visit, such as schools, childcare facilities, supermarkets, and community centres. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) permanent residency in Ireland, (ii) over the age of 18, (iii) parental responsibility of a child under the age of 18, (iv) proficient English, (v) access to a smartphone, and (vi) willing to download the PIEL application and fill out the ESM surveys. For the purpose of the study, the term parent was used to reflect participants' self‐identification and to foreground parenting as a relational and identity‐based role, rather than caregiving as a set of tasks.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (version 30). To explore possible differential non‐completion, a combination of chi‐square and independent sample t‐tests was used. These compared those who enrolled for the study and completed the ESM surveys with those who enrolled for the study but did not complete the ESM surveys, based on the demographic information provided upon enrolment. Significance was set at p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics, including measures of dispersion, central tendency, and frequency distribution, were used to provide an overview of the final sample. No formal statistical adjustment for variations in response timing was applied, as the primary aim was descriptive rather than predictive; however, random scheduling of prompts was used to minimise systematic bias.

Parents' descriptions of parenting occupations and their purposes were then coded using deductive content analysis (Pandey, 2018), according to their alignment with the POP Framework. This was ascertained by reference to the coding table provided as supplementary material in Lim et al.'s (2022) paper, which lists the specific occupations identified in the scoping review as belonging to each category. Each individual response from the ESM surveys was reviewed and categorised into one or more of the 10 categories of parenting occupations or the four categories of continuous parental development. The stated purposes behind each occupation were then analysed and coded, according to the four purposes categories outlined in the framework. Responses that contained content relevant to multiple occupation or purpose categories were coded more than once. All data were independently coded by authors 1 and 2 and initial codes compared, and any discrepancies were collectively discussed with reference to the original text. This was followed by discussion with authors 3, 4, and 6 to verify and finalise the interpretation of categorisation. A decision through consensus was then reached. The frequency of responses within each category of occupation or purpose were calculated to identify the most commonly occurring categories.

To identify whether and how parenting occupations are influenced by parents' demographic characteristics, a series of 10 multiple linear regression analyses were completed. The following parent and child characteristics were included as independent variables: (i) parent's age, (ii) age of youngest child, (iii) location, (iv) income, (v) employment/education status, and (vi) number of children. The dependent variable was the frequency of each of the 10 categories of parenting activity. Prior to conducting the analyses, all variables were checked for linearity, multicollinearity, and normality of residuals. Adjusted R2 was used to assess model fit. To account for the increased risk of type I error, a post hoc Bonferroni correction was applied to all models, and as such, significance was set as p < 0.003. Given the relatively small sample size (n = 44) and the number of predictors included in each model, these analyses were likely underpowered to detect small‐to‐moderate effects. Therefore, findings from the regression analysis should be interpreted with caution.

RESULTS

Participants

A total of 131 parents consented to participate in the study, all providing demographic information. Of those who consented to participate in the study, 34.4% went on to complete the ESM surveys, providing data for 45 participants. Comparative analysis of those who returned ESM data versus those who consented to participate in the study but did not return ESM data showed no significant difference in terms of gender, age, number of children, age of youngest child, location, level of education, education/employment status, or income. One participant failed to meet the minimum individual response threshold of 33% and was consequently excluded from further analysis, giving a final included sample of 44 participants. These participants provided a total of 1105 completed ESM surveys with a mean response rate of 71.36% (SD 11.56%). A total of 10 multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine whether age of parent, age of youngest child, location, education/employment status, income, or number of children predicted participants' performance of each of the 10 categories of parenting occupations. None of the models were statistically significant. Across each of the models, no individual predictors reached statistical significance at the p < 0.003 level, and the adjusted R2 values were low or negative, suggesting poor model fit.

A number of occupations reported by parents—including faith‐based activities, purchasing discretionary goods for the child, waiting for the child, preparing for child‐related activities, voluntary work for the benefit of the child, and texting a spouse to check in on the child—did not align neatly contributing codes presented in the POP Framework coding table (Lim et al., 2022), yet they did fit within the broader occupation categories in the framework. These occupations were incorporated as new examples of existing categories. Faith‐based activities were categorised under ‘teaching and facilitating the child's learning’. Purchasing discretionary goods for the child was categorised under ‘providing materially for the child’. The categories for the occupations of ‘waiting’, ‘preparing for child‐related activities’ and ‘voluntary work for the benefit of the child’ varied based on the other occupations they were attached to. For example, waiting for the child while they were at the orthodontist was categorised under ‘managing the child's health and protecting from harm’; however, waiting for the child at a piano exam fit under the category of ‘teaching and facilitating the child's learning’. Volunteering, which may have an altruistic component related to parenting, was also seen as contributing to the child's well‐being indirectly. Therefore, volunteering with healthcare services with which the child is involved was categorised under ‘managing the child's health and protecting from harm’, while volunteering with the child's local sports team was categorised under ‘teaching and facilitating the child's learning’. It was concluded that texting a spouse to check in on the child aligned with the concept of ‘looking in’, that is, monitoring the child's needs, which is described in the framework as something that occurs throughout the entire parenting role.

Similarly, a number of purposes reported by parents—including cultural celebrations, religious practice, birthday celebrations, and improving facilities and services for the child through volunteer work—did not neatly align with contributing codes for any purpose category in the framework. Following discussion, the purposes of cultural celebrations, religious practice, and birthday celebrations were incorporated into the category of ‘having the child's social needs met’, as they reflect essential aspects of the child's broader social environment, including social interaction, connection to the community, and a sense of belonging. The identified purpose of improving facilities and services for the child through volunteer work remained a unique case that did not fit within the existing framework.

DISCUSSION

This study sought to test the validity of the POP Framework (Lim et al., 2022) in a real‐world context. Overall study findings suggest the POP Framework is congruent with parents' experiences, albeit with some minor adjustments. Notably, all categories outlined by Lim et al. (2022) in the POP Framework were represented in the data, and additional concepts described in the framework, such as the ongoing process of ‘looking in’ to monitor the child's needs, also arose in participant responses. These findings highlight the applicability of the POP Framework in a real‐world context. As the first study to explore the validity of the POP Framework using lived experiences of parents, this research provides important evidence that the framework extends beyond theoretical conceptualisations of parenting developed by occupational therapists and occupational scientists. A small number of occupations and purposes reported by participants did not align neatly with contributing codes as presented in the POP Framework coding table; however, upon additional analysis, these were able to be incorporated into the framework, thus adding breadth and richness to the existing categories. This indicates that although the framework is reasonably robust, there is potential for further development of the codes to capture the full complexity of parenting occupations and purposes.

Although location (rural vs. urban) was included as a predictor in the regression models, no statistically significant associations were identified. Nevertheless, geographical context plausibly shapes the expression of parenting occupations, and the descriptive patterns observed suggest that parenting occupations may carry different temporal, logistical, and emotional demands in rural contexts compared to urban settings, even when categorised similarly within the framework. For example, rural settings may entail greater transport demands, longer travel times to services, and stronger reliance on community organisations, whereas urban environments may involve denser social networks, public transport use, and higher exposure to structured extracurricular activities. These contextual factors may influence the relative frequency of categories such as moving the child, managing the child's social networks, or creating a supportive environment. Future research should be powered to explicitly examine these contrasts, using stratified sampling and, where feasible, multilevel modelling to separate person‐level from context‐level effects.

The occupation category of ‘attending to the child's basic needs’ and the purpose category of ‘having the child's basic needs met’ appeared as the most dominant in the data. This reflects long‐established theories, such as Maslow's hierarchy of needs, which maintains that meeting fundamental physiological needs, such as nourishment and hygiene, form the foundational level of human development and must be fulfilled before addressing other needs (Maslow, 1943). Conversely, the occupation category of ‘seeking help’ only arose in the data once. This may reflect the context of the study with previous research suggesting that parents in Ireland might be less inclined to seek external support, whether for their parenting role or their own mental health, due to stigma, limited‐service access, or social norms around self‐reliance (Hickey & Leckey, 2021; Mulligan et al., 2021).

While no significant correlations were found between demographic variables and the frequency of parenting occupation categories, several descriptive tendencies were observable, which may guide future research. Parents with younger children appeared to report proportionally more occupations related to attending to the child's basic needs and play/child‐focused leisure, whereas parents of older children more often reported teaching/facilitating learning and managing social networks. Similarly, employed parents seemed to reference more setting up for success activities, and higher‐income households provided more examples of providing materially for children. These patterns did not meet the corrected threshold for significance and therefore should be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the modest sample size and its skew towards families with younger children. Further research is needed to explore these relationships more fully, with a particular need to include parents of adolescents.

In relation to the development of the framework, several participant responses suggested that, while the reported occupations generally aligned with the framework's existing structure, additional layers could be incorporated to deepen our understanding of parenting. For instance, the concept of ‘looking in’ to monitor the child's needs acquired new dimensions when considered in the context of remote interaction, specifically, checking in on children via technology, an element not explicitly addressed in the framework. This highlights the evolving nature of occupation in an increasingly technological world (Karanasios et al., 2021), emphasising how digital connectivity can shape parental roles and occupations.

Furthermore, although newly identified examples of occupations were categorised within the existing framework, they had not originally been identified as parenting occupations in the previous literature from which the framework was constructed. Thus, these new examples, as seen in Table 4, serve to broaden the scope of pre‐existing categories.

While the emergence of occupations such as waiting for the child and faith‐based activities could be interpreted as warranting the creation of new occupational categories, these were conceptualised as extending the scope of existing categories rather than fragmenting the framework further. This decision was guided by a desire to preserve the conceptual coherence and usability of the POP Framework while allowing sufficient flexibility to accommodate evolving and contextually embedded parenting occupations. Retaining a parsimonious category structure also supports the framework's application across diverse settings, where the form of parenting occupations may vary while their underlying purposes remain constant.

These insights point to the need for continued refinement and elaboration of the framework's occupation categories to reflect the complexity and diversity of parental occupations across different cultural and societal contexts while retaining a coherent overarching structure. Additionally, the variation in how some newly identified examples were categorised, depending on the other occupations they were attached to, aligns with the theory that occupations are uniquely experienced by individuals, making their meaning subjective and varying from person to person (Pierce, 2001). The overlap of certain occupations across multiple categories, along with the fact that many served multiple purposes, highlights the complex nature of such occupational experiences. This aligns with the findings of Lim et al. (2022) who identify that occupations and their purposes are often multi‐faceted.

Another significant finding from the data analysis was the concept of supporting activities that facilitate other occupations. For instance, packing bags for child‐related events, preparing for child‐related endeavours, and waiting for the child all highlight the supporting activities that enable broader, primary occupations. This concept is well established in occupational therapy literature, with activities being acknowledged as essential components that facilitate larger occupational roles (AOTA, 2020), yet it is not overtly acknowledged in the POP Framework. Taking these supporting activities into account is vital to understanding the parenting role and the full scope of its demands.

Similar to the expansion of the occupation categories, the findings also highlight a need to refine and extend the purpose categories identified in the POP Framework. For example, it may be valuable to expand the category ‘have the child's social needs met’ to more explicitly encompass cultural, religious, heritage‐based and celebratory occupations by renaming it to ‘have the child's social and cultural needs met’. Events such as Saint Patrick's Day celebrations, for example, were frequently referenced by participants and represent culturally specific forms of social participation that are not captured in the original framework. This highlights the importance of recognising the influence of cultural identity and tradition in shaping parenting occupations and further supports the value of adapting the framework across diverse cultural settings.

Similar patterns have been reported in international occupational therapy literature, where parenting is conceptualised as a complex and contextually embedded occupation shaped by cultural norms, service structures, and social expectations (Codd et al., 2023; McGrath et al., 2024; Slootjes et al., 2016). Studies conducted in non‐Irish contexts likewise emphasised the role of environmental constraints, supporting activities, and socio‐cultural values in shaping parenting occupations (Glazer et al., 2023; Honey et al., 2024), suggesting that while the form of parenting occupations may vary across countries, their underlying purposes and meanings show considerable consistency.

The relative socio‐economic and cultural homogeneity of the sample may have influenced which parenting occupations were most visible within the data. Factors such as employment conditions, access to childcare and support services, housing stability, and broader work–family policies likely shaped how parenting occupations were enacted and prioritised. As such, the occupations identified should be interpreted within this contextual frame, reinforcing the need for future research to examine how socio‐cultural and economic conditions shape parenting occupations across more diverse populations.

Additionally, findings point to the relevance of introducing a new purpose category focused on environmental needs, which would include occupations related to volunteering and advocacy for the improvement of services and facilities that serve the child. These activities—such as contributing to local schools, sports clubs, and volunteering for healthcare services—not only reflect active parental engagement but also demonstrate efforts to enrich the broader environment in which the child is raised. By supporting and enhancing community resources, these occupations play a vital role in shaping a supportive and inclusive context for child development (Williams et al., 2009), underscoring their significance within the parenting role.

The results of this study highlight the dynamic nature of parenting practices, thus building on the POP Framework. This is consistent with existing parenting‐related literature, which suggests that parenting practices are deeply complex and are influenced by cultural and contextual factors (Lampe et al., 2019; Murray, 2011). Understanding these complexities is essential in occupational therapy, as effective practice should be guided by a clear understanding of the form, function, and meaning of occupation (Hocking & Wright‐St Clair, 2011). This is important in this context as it has been identified that many parents have unmet occupational therapy needs (Lampe et al., 2019; McGrath et al., 2024; Pituch et al., 2024). The additions and refinements proposed to the POP Framework offer valuable insights for occupational therapists who may work with parents, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the diverse demands placed on this cohort. For example, recognising underpinning activities that enable primary parenting occupations may be particularly crucial when conducting activity analysis with parents experiencing disability, as the demand of parenting occupations may be heightened for this group (Pituch et al., 2024). This makes it essential to consider all supporting activities required for effective engagement. Thus, these additions to the framework could help to guide occupational therapy practice with parents.

Taken together, these findings suggest that while the POP Framework demonstrates strong conceptual robustness, the expression and organisation of parenting occupations are shaped by intersecting geographical, developmental, cultural, and socio‐economic contexts, underscoring the importance of ongoing empirical refinement.

LIMITATIONS AND IMPLICATIONS FOR FUTURE RESEARCH

One notable limitation of this study was the low representation of fathers and individuals from varied ethnic backgrounds, despite the advertisement of the study across multiple diverse locations. Similarly, there was an under‐representation of parents with older children, which may have skewed the data towards the experiences of those parenting younger children, which is often considered the most demanding stage of parenthood (Nelson et al., 2014). These somewhat disproportionate sample characteristics limited the statistical power needed to meaningfully understand the influence of demographic characteristics on occupational engagement. In addition, although the small sample size was consistent with previous ESM research and adequate for descriptive aims, it was likely underpowered for the multiple regression analyses conducted. The lack of significant associations may therefore reflect limited statistical power rather than a true absence of relationships. Future research should aim for a larger and more diverse sample to enable robust inferential analyses and improve the ability to detect small‐to‐moderate effects reliably.

The POP Framework was initially developed from occupational therapy research primarily based in America. While validating the POP Framework in the Irish context enhances its cultural relevance, to ensure global relevance and inclusivity, this validation process should be replicated across a range of countries and cultural contexts. The findings of this study highlight the importance of ongoing evaluation and refinement of theoretical models, ensuring they remain responsive to the evolving and diverse realities of parenting. Such efforts are essential for enabling frameworks to support practitioners and researchers in understanding the full scope of parenting across varied global settings.

Remote data collection may have introduced challenges related to participants' interpretation of questions (Schober et al., 2018), despite efforts to mitigate this through piloting in a preliminary study. Nevertheless, it highlights the value of future qualitative research to explore parents' experiences of their occupational role in greater depth. Furthermore, the temporal nature of data collection—coinciding with seasonal or cultural events such as lighting the fire, Pancake Tuesday and Saint Patrick's Day—may have influenced the types of occupations reported. This underscores the importance of longitudinal research to better capture the scope of parenting occupations across different times of the year.

CONCLUSION

This study set out to test the validity of the POP Framework using real‐time data gathered through ESM. The findings provide strong support for the framework's applicability in a real‐world context, as all existing occupation and purpose categories were represented in participants' lived experiences. The identification of additional occupations and purposes not captured by the contributing codes for categories in the framework highlights the need for ongoing refinement and expansion of the framework to provide a more enriched understanding of the complex and cultural‐specific realities of parenting. Further and more substantial additions are likely when the framework is tested in a diverse range of cultural contexts, expanding the conceptual depth of the POP Framework, enhancing its practical utility for occupational therapy and enabling more holistic and culturally responsive interventions. As the needs of parents remain under‐recognised in occupational therapy literature and practice, this contribution to the research is a crucial step towards enabling more informed and effective occupational therapy support for parents.
