Clinical Correlates of Hospital Admission in Adult SLE Patients Presenting to the Emergency Department
Authors: Neil Manadan, Burak Celik, Isadora Small, Shilpa Arora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Among 376,776 SLE related ED visits in 2022, 37.7% required hospital admission underlying high requirement of acute care in SLE.•Severe infections (particularly sepsis and central line infections), cardiovascular events (myocardial infarction, hypertensive emergencies), and renal complications (acute kidney injury, combined cardiorenal disease) were the leading indications of admission, with odds ratios ranging from ∼9 to over 500.•Increased age and Asian/Pacific Islander race were independently linked to higher admission rates, while female sex was associated with reduced odds; income did not predict admission after adjustment, though most visits came from lower-income quartiles, reflecting disparities in healthcare access.•Awareness of diagnosis-specific admission likelihoods can help emergency physicians prioritize high-risk SLE patients, potentially reducing ED wait times.Keywords: Emergency department, Emergency service, Hospital admission, Systemic lupus erythematosus

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex, multisystem autoimmune disease characterized by periods of flares and remission, often requiring emergency care for disease-related or comorbid complications.1, 2, 3 Patients with SLE are at increased risk for severe infections, thromboembolic events, and cardiovascular and renal dysfunction, all of which may necessitate hospitalization.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 While prior studies have focused on inpatient outcomes such as complications, length of stay, readmissions or mortality in SLE, far less is known about the factors that lead to hospital admission from the emergency department (ED), where initial triage decisions occur.5,6,9, 10, 11 Understanding the determinants and patterns of ED-to-inpatient transition in this population may guide earlier intervention and improve care delivery. In this study, we used a large national database to characterize demographic and clinical factors associated with hospital admission in adult SLE patients presenting to the ED and to identify the most common admitting diagnoses that prompt hospital admission.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective analysis of all SLE visits in those ≥18 years of age recorded in the 2022 Nationwide Emergency Department Database Sample (NEDS) (http://www.hcup-us.ahrq.gov). The NEDS is the largest all-payer ED database in the United States and contains data for ED visits from 993 hospitals across 41 States and the District of Columbia. The NEDS is a 20% stratified sample of U.S. hospital-owned EDs, and, after weighting, yields national estimates of hospital-owned ED visits. We conducted the analysis at the visit level because patient-level data was not available in the NEDS database. SLE ED visits were identified by International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes: M32.10, M32.11, M32.12, M32.13, M32.14, M32.15, M32.19, M32.8, and M32.9.

Potential Factors/Independent Variables

Potential factors considered for inclusion in the multivariable models included sex, race/ethnicity, median household income by ZIP code, and primary ED billing diagnosis. Income was categorized into quartiles based on the NEDS-provided national ZIP-code income distribution (Q1: $1-55,999; Q2: $56,000-70,999; Q3: $71,000-93,999; Q4: ≥$94,000).

Objectives

(1) Identification of demographic and clinical factors associated with ED-to-hospital admission in adult SLE patients.

(2) Evaluation of the top 25 primary billing diagnoses contributing to ED-to-hospital admission in adult SLE patients.

Outcome

Hospital admission, defined as admission to the same hospital or transfer to another acute-care facility, served as the outcome of interest in our logistic regression analyses. All other ED dispositions listed in Table 1 were grouped together as the comparison category, as they did not constitute an acute-care hospital admission.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted using STATA version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). The STATA rank command was employed to identify the 25 most common primary billing admission diagnoses for patients with SLE presenting to the ED. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics of SLE hospital admissions compared to ED discharges. All analyses were conducted using survey commands, incorporating discharge-level weights, hospital-level clustering, and stratification to account for the complex sampling design of the database. Weighted logistic regression was performed using the svy command, and all descriptive statistics are presented as weighted percentages. Logistic regression analyses were used to identify factors associated with ED-to-hospital admission in adult SLE patients. Univariable logistic regression was completed to estimate unadjusted odds ratios (OR) for hospital admission. Variables with P-values ≤ .2 were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. Statistical significance in the multivariable analysis was defined as P ≤ .05, and results were presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORadj) along with corresponding 95% CIs (CI).

Results

A weighted total of 376,776 adult SLE-related ED visits were identified, of which 141,863 (37.7%) resulted in hospital admission and 221,396 (58.8%) were discharged from the ED (Table 1). Among the top 25 ED primary billing diagnoses resulting in hospital admission in adults with SLE, infections, cardiovascular, and renal complications were the leading causes (Table 2). Sepsis, acute kidney failure, heart failure and myocardial infarction had the highest admission percentages, with over 80% of these ED visits resulting in hospitalization. Diagnoses of glomerular disease in SLE or other organ involvement, cerebral infarction and respiratory failure also had high admission percentages. In contrast, visits for nonspecific chest pain, urinary tract infections (UTI), and noninfective gastroenteritis were more commonly discharged from the ED. COVID-19 ED visits were numerically the second most common reason for inpatient admission but overall, the majority SLE patients with COVID-19 were discharged home.

Demographics

The demographics of the weighted sample of SLE patients admitted to the hospital compared to those discharged from the ED showed differences in age (median 56 vs 47 years; P < .001), sex (Females 88.3% vs 91.7%; P < .001), and racial/ethnic distribution, including in White (47.6% vs 44.2%; P < .001), African American (29.5% vs 31.6%; P = .033), Hispanic (15.0% vs 17.3%; P = .003), and Asian/Pacific Islander (Asian/PI) (2.5% vs 1.5%; P < .001) populations (Table 3). Patients in the lowest income quartile were more frequently discharged from the ED (33.8% vs 31.5%; P = .015), while distributions across other income quartiles were similar. The majority of ED visits occurred in patients from the lowest 2 income quartiles.

Factors Associated With Hospital Admission: Univariable and Multivariable Analysis

In adjusted analysis, several demographic and clinical variables were independently associated with hospital admission among SLE patients presenting to the ED (Table 4). Increasing age was associated with higher odds (ORadj 1.03; 95% CI: 1.023-1.027), while female sex was associated with lower odds (ORadj 0.78; 95% CI: 0.729-0.830) of admission. Asian/PI patients had significantly higher odds of admission (ORadj 1.77; 95% CI: 1.496-2.098), while other racial and income categories were not significantly associated.

Several clinical diagnoses were strongly associated with hospital admission. The most impressive were bloodstream infection due to central venous catheter (ORadj 532.10; 95% CI: 73.591-3847.345), other specified sepsis (ORadj 94.65; 95% CI: 49.126-182.340), and unspecified sepsis (ORadj 46.48; 95% CI: 32.787-65.902), followed by non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (ORadj 18.27; 95% CI: 13.017-25.630), hypertensive emergency (ORadj 14.33; 95% CI: 8.994-22.827), and combined hypertensive heart and kidney disease with heart failure and stage 1-4 CKD (ORadj 14.26; 95% CI: 10.761-18.901). Other conditions such as acute respiratory failure, cerebral infarction, glomerular disease in SLE, and acute kidney failure were also highly associated with hospital admission. In contrast, patients presenting with UTI (ORadj 0.63; 95% CI: 0.537-0.738), nonspecific chest pain (ORadj 0.19; 95% CI: 0.158-0.233) and unspecified SLE (ORadj 0.70; 95% CI: 0.615-0.803) had significantly lower odds of hospital admission.

Discussion

In this nationwide analysis of ED visits for adult patients with SLE, we found that nearly 2 in 5 encounters resulted in hospital admission. Higher odds of hospital admission were observed in older patients, males, Asian/PI individuals, and those presenting with high-acuity infectious, cardiovascular, or renal conditions. Notably, diagnoses such as sepsis, acute kidney injury, myocardial infarction, and hypertensive emergencies were among the conditions most strongly associated with hospital admission. These findings underscore the high acuity and medical complexity of SLE patients who are ultimately hospitalized from the ED.

These observations align with and expand on prior studies that have identified clinical predictors of hospitalization in SLE populations. Petri et al., in the Hopkins Lupus Cohort, identified active SLE (35%), infection (14%), and medical complications of SLE (13%) as the leading causes of hospitalization.12 Edwards et al. similarly found that lupus flares (58%) and infection (37%) were most common diagnoses categories for hospitalization.5 Lee et al. reported that the most common reasons for hospitalization in their Canadian SLE Cohort included disease flare (17.5%) and infection (16.2%).13 Dhital et al. reported infection (15.80%), cardiac (7.03%) and renal manifestations (4.91%) were the most common reasons for hospitalizations.6 Other landmark studies have shown that SLE patients experience a high overall burden of cardiovascular disease and serious infections, which aligns with the high‑acuity presentations observed in our ED cohort. It should be emphasized that none of these aforementioned studies reported on ED visits.

Gu et al. studied hospitalizations and ED visits in SLE patients in Canadian cohort.11 They reported clinical drivers of hospitalization including flare activity, infection and long-term disease damage.11 These are valuable pieces of information, but our study gives additional data by identifying the most common diagnosis-specific reasons for hospital admission from the ED, offering practical information for a triaging ED physician. Their study was single-center design and had a relatively small sample size which limits generalizability. In contrast, our study was a national analysis of SLE ED visits and admissions across diverse hospital types, geographic regions, and patient populations. Taken together, both studies provide complementary insights which have the potential to streamline SLE ED care.

In addition to clinical predictors, demographic factors were also associated with differences in admission status. Although female patients comprised the majority of all ED visits, they had lower odds of hospital admission compared with male patients. This finding may be explained with current medical literature suggesting men with SLE may have worse prognosis compared to their female counterparts.14 Asian/PI individuals had significantly higher odds of admission, suggesting possible differences in disease severity or healthcare access. Asian/PI have been reported to have higher rates of lupus nephritis and end organ damage.15 This higher burden of disease may explain the elevated admission percentage in this ethnic group.

In a related study, Dhital et al. examined SLE ED visits in the NEDS 2019 but their methodology was vastly different from our study design.10 Our study was restricted to SLE ED visits and examined the top 25 most common SLE ED-to-admission diagnoses and the demographic factors associated with admission in the most recent NEDS database. On the other hand, they compared SLE vs non-SLE ED visits. Our analysis adds to this previous SLE NEDs analysis by providing diagnosis-specific risk quantification. Both studies found infection to be a leading cause of hospital admission. Dhital et al. described socioeconomic differences.10 In our study, household income levels were not associated with admission risk after multivariable adjustment, although descriptive data showed modest differences in discharge patterns. Specifically, the majority of adult SLE-related ED visits, whether resulting in hospital admission or discharge, were from patients in the 2 lowest income quartiles. This trend suggests a socioeconomic disparity in SLE ED utilization and is consistent with existing SLE disparities studies.16 Importantly, several conditions commonly seen in outpatient SLE care such as nonspecific chest pain and UTI were associated with lower odds of admission. We hypothesize that some SLE patients with limited healthcare access use the ED for lower acuity conditions and were subsequently discharged from the ED.

Our study builds on prior literature by offering a diagnosis-specific view of admission risk in a nationally representative SLE population. Our data can shape future triage algorithms and early management protocols aimed at streamlining ED SLE care.

Our study has several limitations including a retrospective design, reliance on ICD 10 codes, lack of medication data, laboratory results, and inability to account for SLE disease activity/duration/severity. As NEDS captures encounters rather than individuals and lacks unique patient identifiers, multiple visits by the same patient cannot be distinguished, and the total number of unique patients is unknown. Consequently, some individuals may contribute more than one encounter, an inherent limitation of encounter-level administrative databases. However, our objective was to characterize factors associated with hospital admission at the ED-visit level, reflecting real-world triage decisions made per encounter rather than longitudinal patient-level risk. In this context, each ED presentation represents a clinically distinct decision point, even when multiple visits originate from the same individual, and therefore our findings should be interpreted as visit-based associations rather than patient-level predictions. Another limitation was that we only conducted complete-case analysis; however, the proportion of missingness was low. Sepsis due to Escherichia coli was excluded from regression modeling, as the 100% admission of all affected patients precluded statistical estimation. Finally, the model includes hospital admission diagnoses (e.g., sepsis, myocardial infarction, renal failure) as independent variables. This introduces a reverse causality issue and renders the model descriptive rather than predictive in nature.

Conclusion

In this national analysis of over 376,000 adult SLE ED visits, we found 37.7% were admitted to an acute care hospital. Hospital admission was strongly associated with high-acuity conditions, particularly sepsis, central line infection, acute kidney injury, heart failure, myocardial infarction, respiratory failure, stroke, and cardiorenal syndromes. These diagnoses demonstrated the highest odds of admission, often exceeding tenfold increases, underscoring their clinical severity and the need for inpatient care. In contrast, conditions like UTI and nonspecific chest pain were associated with significantly lower admission proportions, suggesting that a subset of SLE patients may present to the ED with lower-acuity complaints that can be managed on an outpatient basis.

In addition, demographic analysis revealed that increasing age and Asian/PI race were independently associated with higher odds of admission, while female sex was associated with reduced odds. Although income quartile did not predict admission in adjusted models, majority of all ED visits occurred in patients from the lowest 2 income quartiles, pointing to disparities in healthcare access. These findings in combination with bedside examination, can help physicians make decisions on the appropriateness of hospital admission vs routine ED discharge. Additionally, these quantitative findings provide a structured dataset that can be readily incorporated into future artificial intelligence models for predicting SLE ED-to-hospital admission.
