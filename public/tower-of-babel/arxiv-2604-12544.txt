Chiral electron-fluxon superconductivity in circuit quantum magnetostatics
Adel Ali, Alexey Belyanin

Abstract
We investigate electron paring in two-dimensional electron systems mediated by the vacuum fluctuations of a quantized magnetic flux generated by the inductor of an LC resonator. The interaction induces long-range attractive interactions between angular momentum states which lead to pairing in a broad class of materials with critical temperatures of few Kelvin or even higher, depending on the field-covered area. The induced state is a pair-density wave topological chiral superconductor. The proposed platform in circuit QED environment offers a tunable promising tool for engineering electron interactions in two-dimensional systems to create new quantum phases of matter.



I
Introduction

The interaction between quantized electromagnetic fields and electronic degrees of freedom has been widely studied in cavity quantum electrodynamics (QED) as a route to modifying and inducing quantum phases of matter. In that setting, cavity modes can strongly affect the electronic structure of materials and generate cavity-induced many-body phenomena
Schlawin et al. 2022
;
Garcia-Vidal et al. 2021
;
Lu et al. 2025
. By contrast, circuit QED has been developed primarily for quantum-information applications. A lumped-element LC circuit, however, also functions as a cavity and offers a particularly versatile platform for engineering light–matter interactions
Frisk Kockum et al. 2019
. Its capacitance, inductance, and, when present, Josephson energies can be tuned far more readily than in conventional cavity-QED architectures, providing a level of control with no direct analogue in standard cavity settings.

Here we consider charged particles coupled to the quantized magnetic flux of such a circuit cavity, namely an LC resonator. In this setting, particles exchange angular momentum through a quantized gauge field, opening a route to engineered many-body interactions and, in particular, cavity-mediated superconductivity. A key advantage of LC resonators is their extremely small effective mode volume, which yields large zero-point flux (ZPF) fluctuations and makes circuit-QED platforms especially attractive for engineering correlated electronic states. It was shown previously
Ali and Belyanin 2025
;
Ali and Belyanin 2026
that quantized magnetic fields can induce such unusual interactions between charged particles; in the present work, we focus on their superconducting consequences.

Unlike conventional cavity-QED settings, the matter sector considered here lies beyond the dipole approximation, allowing orbital angular-momentum exchange between electrons and the gauge field. Because the vector potential is itself engineerable, the induced interaction can be tailored to favor specific many-body phases, including superconducting and topological states. Moreover, since the gauge field is generated by closed current loops, its spatial profile naturally acquires nontrivial angular structure. Through the Biot–Savart law, the Fourier transform of the vector potential is directly linked to the Fourier content of the underlying current distribution, so fabrication can be used to selectively enhance or suppress specific momentum components and thereby engineer interaction channels in momentum space.

Photon-mediated attractive interactions leading to superconductivity were proposed in Ref.
Schlawin et al. 2019a
, where Amperean pairing between comoving electrons was predicted for a two-dimensional electron gas dispersively coupled to a nanoplasmonic cavity. However, Ref.
Andolina et al. 2024
showed that nanoplasmonic cavities do not generically provide sufficiently strong transverse current–current coupling. In the deep subwavelength regime, the interaction is instead dominated by quasi-electrostatic density–density terms, so the anticipated mode-volume compression does not generally drive the system into the Amperean-pairing regime.

Motivated by this limitation, we propose a circuit quantum magnetostatics (QMS) platform in which particles couple dispersively to a structured quantized vector potential. In contrast to conventional cavity QED, where the dominant electric-dipole coupling mediates the exchange of linear momentum, the present mechanism enables the exchange of orbital angular momentum. The resulting effective electron–electron interaction takes the form
\propto-L_{i}L_{j}
. For electrons near the Fermi surface, its magnitude scales as
v_{F}^{2}\times
area enclosed by the field profile, with an upper bound set by the spatial region over which the field is supported. To distinguish this mechanism from conventional Amperean pairing, we refer to the resulting instability as ”flux pairing”. This geometric scaling provides a route to enhancing the interaction strength and accordingly the superconducting critical temperature. The ring-cavity geometry permits multiple (
N
) resonators to couple to the same two-dimensional electron gas, thereby enhancing the field coverage of the material and effective interaction strength, with further enhancement possible through mutual inductive coupling that is beyond the scope of this work.

In contrast to Ref.
Schlawin et al. 2019a
, the present coupling is magnetostatic from the outset, so the system remains in the transverse-coupling regime even at strong coupling and the induced current–current interaction remains well defined. Unlike a static electric field, which can be represented by a scalar potential
\Phi_{\mathrm{static}}(\mathbf{r})
, a static magnetic field enters through
\nabla\times\mathbf{A}
and naturally mediates momentum exchange and hence current–current interactions
Ali and Belyanin 2025
. In addition, magnetostatic coupling is largely insensitive to the dielectric spacer separating the superconducting ring(s) layer from the two-dimensional material; see Fig. 1.

Figure 1
:
A sketch of an LC cavity made of a single superconducting (SC) loop (a) or an array of loops (b) which induces pairing in a 2D electron system (in this case graphene) mediated by quantum fluctuations of a quantized magnetic flux.

In this system, electrons interact through virtual fluxon excitations, i.e., vacuum fluctuations of the quantized gauge field. This architecture offers broad tunability of the resonator parameters
L
and
C
. If desired, a straightforward capacitive or inductive coupling between multiple cavities, and nonlinearities controlled by the Josephson parameters
E_{J}
and
E_{C}
could be introduced. We show that this tunability, particularly in the inductive sector, can drive the system into a superconducting regime.

In the dispersive regime, where the LC frequency is detuned from the relevant electronic energy scales, the cavity degree of freedom can be integrated out, yielding an emergent global interaction of the form
(\sum_{i}\hat{O}_{i})^{2}
, where
\hat{O}_{i}
is a electronic single-particle operator, for example, electron’s angular momentum in the simplest case,
\hat{O}_{i}=\hat{L}_{z}^{(i)}
. To promote pairing, however, this operator must not be diagonal in the relevant single-particle basis. This can be achieved either by engineering an electronic band structure that breaks the symmetry associated with
\hat{O}
, for example through lattice geometry, external potentials, stacking, boundaries, or finite-size effects, or by introducing additional spatial structure into the vector potential itself, thereby generating nondiagonal matrix elements in the desired pairing channel.

Cavity materials engineering and superconductivity.

Cavity quantum electrodynamics (cQED) with solids has recently evolved into the broader notion of “cavity materials engineering,” in which the quantized electromagnetic environment acts as a control parameter for correlated phases in quantum matter
Schlawin et al. 2022
;
Garcia-Vidal et al. 2021
;
Hübener et al. 2024
;
Lu et al. 2025
.
Many-body and
ab initio
approaches that extend Pauli–Fierz QED to periodic solids, most notably quantum-electrodynamical density-functional theory (QEDFT) and cavity Eliashberg-type treatments, have predicted that coupling superconductors to cavity modes can renormalize electron-phonon interactions, collective modes, and even effective electron-electron interactions
Sentef et al. 2018
;
Schlawin et al. 2019b
;
Curtis et al. 2019
;
Lu et al. 2024
.
Model calculations show that cavity polaritons can enhance or suppress conventional phonon-mediated pairing depending on cavity frequency, polarization, and geometry, and may even favor unconventional pairing channels, while first-principles QEDFT simulations for MgB
2
find an equilibrium enhancement of
T_{c}
of the order of ten percent in realistic cavity setups
Sentef et al. 2018
;
Lu et al. 2024
.
Experimentally, vibrational strong coupling of molecular superconductors and cuprates to surface-plasmon or microcavity modes has been used to modify
T_{c}
in both directions, consistent with an underlying cavity-controlled electron–phonon coupling
Thomas et al. 2025
;
Garcia-Vidal et al. 2021
;
Keren et al. 2026
.
A recent perspective emphasizes that these developments illustrate how vacuum fluctuations inside cavities can reshape superconductivity in bulk materials, and frames cavity materials engineering as a route to equilibrium, light-controllable superconducting phases
Nova and Caviglia 2025
;
Lu et al. 2025
.
Taken together, these works establish cavity QED as a viable platform for engineering superconducting materials.

In a separate development on the materials side, chiral superconductors, i.e., unconventional superconductors whose order parameter has a complex phase winding in momentum space and breaks time-reversal symmetry, have emerged as a central platform for exploring topological superconductivity and Majorana excitations
Kallin and Berlinsky 2016
;
Wysokiński 2019
. The layered perovskite Sr
2
RuO
4
has long been a leading candidate, with early experiments indicating unconventional pairing and spontaneous time-reversal-symmetry breaking
Maeno et al. 1994
;
Luke et al. 1998
;
Xia et al. 2006
, although more recent measurements have challenged the simplest chiral
p
-wave scenario and stimulated intense debate about its actual pairing symmetry. In parallel, the heavy-fermion compound UPt
3
provides compelling evidence for a bulk time-reversal-symmetry-breaking state with multiple superconducting phases and possible chiral order
Avers et al. 2020
, while the locally noncentrosymmetric pnictide SrPtAs shows signatures consistent with chiral
d
-wave superconductivity
Biswas et al. 2013
. On the theoretical side, chiral
d+id
states have been predicted in doped graphene near Van Hove filling and, more generally, on hexagonal lattices
Nandkishore et al. 2012
;
Black-Schaffer and Honerkamp 2014
, and a growing body of work has identified a wide family of multicomponent and multiband superconductors in which frustration between competing pairing channels naturally generates time-reversal-symmetry-breaking condensates
Kallin and Berlinsky 2016
;
Wysokiński 2019
.

Our proposal offers a promising platform for unconventional chiral superconductivity in “conventional” two-dimensional (2D) electron systems. While an applied magnetic field explicitly breaks time-reversal symmetry (TRS), the cavity vacuum has zero average field; nevertheless, cavity-mediated interactions are expected to spontaneously break TRS and yield a chiral superconducting phase.
Depending on the microscopic regime, the structured gauge-field-mediated interaction may support either a chiral pair-density-wave state at finite momentum or a more conventional chiral superconducting state in the zero-momentum channel. Owing to the tunability of the interaction profile and lattice-scale form factors, the proposed setting may offer a controlled route for exploring the competition between these distinct superconductivity orders.

In Section II we describe the model and flux coupling. In Section III we study electron pairing on a square lattice in a uniform magnetic field over the finite area. In Section IV we generalize the treatment to a nonuniform field. We find that in all geometries the interaction favors a chiral superconducting gap function. In Sec. V, we present estimates of the critical temperature and discuss ways of increasing it. We also highlight the advantages of our platform relative to standard cavity QED and discuss its potential as a route toward elevated, and possibly high-temperature, superconductivity. In Section VI we show how Zeeman coupling to electron spins favors spin-triplet, chiral state in the superconducting phase. Appendix contains technical details of the derivation of some results in the main text.

II
Quantized vector potential of a superconducting loop

We model a single superconducting loop as a lumped linear LC mode with canonical flux–charge pair
(\hat{\Phi},\hat{Q})
obeying
[\hat{\Phi},\hat{Q}]=i\hbar
. Quantization yields the harmonic-oscillator Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\frac{\hat{Q}^{2}}{2C}+\frac{\hat{\Phi}^{2}}{2L}=\hbar\omega\!\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\tfrac{1}{2}\right),\qquad\omega=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}},

(1)

with ladder-operator representation

\hat{\Phi}=\Phi_{\mathrm{zpf}}\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right),\quad\hat{Q}=i\,Q_{\mathrm{zpf}}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\hat{a}\right),

(2)

where zero-point fluctuation amplitudes are

\Phi_{\mathrm{zpf}}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar Z}{2}},\ \ Q_{\mathrm{zpf}}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2Z}},

and
Z=\sqrt{L/C}
is the characteristic impedance.

For a
linear
loop (no Josephson element), the loop current operator is
\hat{I}=\hat{\Phi}/L
, so the current zero-point amplitude is

I_{\mathrm{zpf}}=\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{zpf}}}{L}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega}{2L}}.

Let
\mathbf{A}_{I}(\mathbf{r})
be the
vector potential per unit current
, defined in Coulomb gauge by the Biot–Savart line integral over the wire path
C
,

\mathbf{A}_{I}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\oint_{C}\frac{d\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|},\qquad\mathbf{B}_{I}(\mathbf{r})=\nabla\times\mathbf{A}_{I}(\mathbf{r}),

so that for a static current
I
one has
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r})=I\,\mathbf{A}_{I}(\mathbf{r})
and
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r})=I\,\mathbf{B}_{I}(\mathbf{r})
. The quantized vector-potential operator of the LC mode is therefore

\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{r})=I_{\mathrm{zpf}}\!\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)\,\mathbf{A}_{I}(\mathbf{r}),\;\hat{\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{r})=I_{\mathrm{zpf}}\!\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)\,\mathbf{B}_{I}(\mathbf{r}).

(3)

The flux linkage through the loop satisfies

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}_{\rm loop}=\oint_{C}\hat{\mathbf{A}}\,\cdot\,d\bm{\ell}=\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)I_{\mathrm{zpf}}\oint_{C}\hat{\mathbf{A}_{I}}\,\cdot\,d\bm{\ell}=L\,\hat{I}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\mathrm{zpf}}\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right),

since
\oint_{C}\mathbf{A}_{I}\!\cdot d\bm{\ell}=L
by definition of the self-inductance.
The magnetic energy equals the inductive energy,

\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\int d^{3}r\,|\hat{\mathbf{B}}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}L\,\hat{I}^{\,2},

so in the vacuum,
\langle 0|\tfrac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\!\int|\hat{\mathbf{B}}|^{2}|0\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}LI_{\mathrm{zpf}}^{2}=\hbar\omega/4
, i.e., half the zero-point energy.

Cavity-QED normalization.

It is convenient to introduce a magnetoquasistatic mode function

\mathbf{u}_{B}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{\mathbf{A}_{I}(\mathbf{r})}{\sqrt{L}},\qquad\int\!\frac{d^{3}r}{\mu_{0}}\,|\nabla\times\mathbf{u}_{B}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}=1,

(4)

(as we will see later the effective interaction will inversely depend on
L
so cavity compression is just reducing L)
which yields the cavity-like expressions

\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{r})=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}}\,\mathbf{u}_{B}(\mathbf{r})\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right),

(5)

\hat{\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{r})=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}}\,\left[\nabla\times\mathbf{u}_{B}(\mathbf{r})\right]\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right).

These mirror the usual single-mode cavity-QED formulas in the Coulomb gauge,

\hat{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r})=i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega}{2\epsilon_{0}}}\,\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})\left(\hat{a}-\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right),\,\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{r})=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2\epsilon_{0}\omega}}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)

with the electric-field normalization
\int\epsilon_{0}|\mathbf{u}|^{2}=1
replaced by the inductive normalization in Eq. (
4
).

Minimal coupling to matter.

For charges
q_{i}
and masses
m_{i}
at positions
\mathbf{r}_{i}
, the coupling is

\hat{H}_{\rm int}=-\sum_{i}\frac{q_{i}}{m_{i}}\,\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{i}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{r}_{i})+\sum_{i}\frac{q_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}\,\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{2}(\mathbf{r}_{i}),

(6)

with
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
from Eqs. (
3
) or (
5
). Gauge-invariant treatments retain both the paramagnetic
\propto\hat{\mathbf{p}}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf{A}}
and diamagnetic
\propto\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
terms.

III
Square lattice

We consider spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
fermions on a square lattice with nearest-neighbor hopping
t
and chemical potential
\mu
.
Minimal coupling to a (quantized) vector potential is implemented by the Peierls substitution,

\hat{H}=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}\Big(e^{i\hat{\theta}_{ij}}\,\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j\sigma}+\text{H.c.}\Big)-\mu\sum_{i}\hat{n}_{i}+\hbar\omega\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},

where the link phase operator is

\hat{\theta}_{ij}\equiv\frac{e}{\hbar}\int_{\bm{r}_{i}}^{\bm{r}_{j}}\!\bm{A}(\bm{r})\cdot d\bm{\ell},\quad\bm{A}(\bm{r})=A_{\phi}(r)\,\hat{\bm{\phi}}\,\hat{X},

with

A_{\phi}(r)=A_{0}f(r),\qquad\hat{X}\equiv\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}.

Here
\hat{\bm{\phi}}
is the azimuthal unit vector and

\hat{n}_{i}=\sum_{\sigma}\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i\sigma},

while
f(r)
is the radial spatial profile.

Since
\bm{A}(\bm{r})\propto\hat{X}
commutes with fermionic bilinears, the phase factor may be written as

\hat{\theta}_{ij}=\alpha_{ij}\,\hat{X},\;\alpha_{ij}\equiv\frac{eA_{0}}{\hbar}\int_{\bm{r}_{i}}^{\bm{r}_{j}}\!f(r)\,\hat{\bm{\phi}}\cdot d\bm{\ell},\;\alpha_{ji}=-\alpha_{ij}.

Expanding to linear order in
\bm{A}
(i.e., keeping only the paramagnetic term),

e^{i\alpha_{ij}\hat{X}}\simeq 1+i\alpha_{ij}\hat{X},

we obtain

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\rm el}+\hbar\omega\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-\hat{X}\,\hat{J},

with

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm el}

\displaystyle=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}\big(\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j\sigma}+\text{H.c.}\big)-\mu\sum_{i}\hat{n}_{i},

\displaystyle\hat{J}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\alpha_{ij}\,\hat{j}_{ij},\;\hat{j}_{ij}\equiv it\sum_{\sigma}\big(\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j\sigma}-\hat{c}_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i\sigma}\big),

where

\hat{j}_{ij}=\hat{j}_{ij}^{\dagger}
is the bond-current operator and
\hat{J}=\hat{J}^{\dagger}
.

Mode representation.

In an arbitrary single-particle basis
\{\varphi_{\alpha}(i)\}
,

\hat{c}_{i\sigma}=\sum_{\alpha}\varphi_{\alpha}(i)\,\hat{c}_{\alpha\sigma},

the coupling takes the bilinear form

\hat{J}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\sigma}J_{\alpha\beta}\,\hat{c}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\beta\sigma},

with

J_{\alpha\beta}=it\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\alpha_{ij}\Big[\varphi_{\alpha}^{\ast}(i)\varphi_{\beta}(j)-\varphi_{\alpha}^{\ast}(j)\varphi_{\beta}(i)\Big].

Because the flux couples only to the orbital current,
J_{\alpha\beta}
is spin independent, within this approximation see below for spin coupling to the QMF, and satisfies

J_{\alpha\beta}=J_{\beta\alpha}^{\ast}.

Dispersive elimination of the cavity.

Consider

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\rm el}+\hbar\omega\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\hat{J}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}),

where
\hat{J}=\hat{J}^{\dagger}
acts only on the electronic sector. A purely electronic effective Hamiltonian follows directly from projection onto the displaced-oscillator ground manifold only when

[\hat{H}_{\rm el},\hat{J}]=0
. More generally, a Schrieffer–Wolff (polaron) transformation generates additional retardation/high-frequency corrections, and the projected electronic Hamiltonian becomes

\hat{H}_{\rm eff}=\hat{H}_{\rm el}-\frac{\hat{J}^{2}}{\hbar\omega}+\frac{1}{2(\hbar\omega)^{2}}[\hat{J},[\hat{H}_{\rm el},\hat{J}]]+O(\omega^{-3}).

To isolate the two-body term, we normal order
\hat{J}^{2}
:

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{\,2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}J_{\alpha\beta}J_{\gamma\delta}\,\hat{c}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\beta\sigma}\hat{c}_{\gamma\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\delta\sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\delta,\sigma}(J^{2})_{\alpha\delta}\,\hat{c}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\delta\sigma}-\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}J_{\alpha\beta}J_{\gamma\delta}\,\hat{c}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\gamma\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\beta\sigma}\hat{c}_{\delta\sigma^{\prime}}.

The first term on the right-hand side is a one-body renormalization and may be absorbed into
\hat{H}_{\rm el}
.

In the spin triplet state, as one can see in the section below, the interaction should be written in explicitly antisymmetrized form. Expanding gives

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{2{\rm b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\hbar\omega}\sum_{\alpha\gamma}\sum_{\beta\delta}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\Big[J_{\alpha\beta}J_{\gamma\delta}\,\hat{c}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\gamma\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\delta\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{\beta\sigma}

\displaystyle-J_{\alpha\delta}J_{\gamma\beta}\,\hat{c}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\gamma\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\delta\sigma}\hat{c}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\Big].

This is the induced genuine two-body interaction, which is explicitly invariant under spin rotations; the spin circuit effects are discussed below. In what follows, we consider two representative radial profiles of the vector potential: a uniform magnetic profile, for which the induced interaction is highly long-ranged and nonlocal, and a Gaussian profile, for which the predominantly transverse/chiral scattering of electronic states can be demonstrated more transparently.

III.1
Uniform magnetic field

Finite-
\bm{Q}
spin triplet.

We consider a single equal-spin component of a triplet state, so that the orbital
pairing problem is effectively spinless and the gap function satisfies

\Delta_{\bm{Q}}(-\bm{k})=-\Delta_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k}),

(7)

where
\bm{Q}
is center-of-mass pair momentum.
After dispersive elimination of the cavity mode, the effective Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}_{\rm eff}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\xi_{\bm{k}}\,\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}-\frac{\hat{J}^{2}}{\hbar\omega},\;\xi_{\bm{k}}=-2t(\cos k_{x}a+\cos k_{y}a)-\mu,

with

\hat{J}=\kappa_{\Phi}\sum_{i}(x_{i}\hat{j}_{i}^{y}-y_{i}\hat{j}_{i}^{x}),\quad\kappa_{\Phi}=\frac{ea}{2\hbar}\frac{\Phi}{\mathcal{A}}.

Because
\hat{J}
contains
x_{i},y_{i}
, translational invariance is broken in a finite
sample, and the pair momentum
\bm{Q}
is only approximately conserved.

Writing

\hat{J}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}J_{\alpha\beta}\,\hat{c}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\beta},

fermionic normal ordering gives

\displaystyle-\frac{\hat{J}^{2}}{\hbar\omega}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\sum_{\alpha\delta}(J^{2})_{\alpha\delta}\,\hat{c}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\delta}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}V_{\alpha\beta;\gamma\delta}\,\hat{c}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\delta}\hat{c}_{\gamma},

with the antisymmetrized two-body vertex

V_{\alpha\beta;\gamma\delta}=-\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\left(J_{\alpha\gamma}J_{\beta\delta}-J_{\alpha\delta}J_{\beta\gamma}\right).

The bilinear term renormalizes the one-body spectrum and is absorbed into

\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{k}}
below. See Appendix
A
for the exact disscusion.

The exact finite-sample instability is obtained in the eigenbasis of the renormalized
one-body Hamiltonian,

\Delta_{nm}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{rs}V_{nm;rs}\,\frac{1-f(\varepsilon_{r})-f(\varepsilon_{s})}{\varepsilon_{r}+\varepsilon_{s}}\,\Delta_{rs},\,\Delta_{mn}=-\Delta_{nm}.

(8)

To expose the leading weak-coupling structure, we project onto local plane-wave states
near a Fermi-surface patch and resolve the pairing kernel in an approximate finite
center-of-mass channel
\bm{Q}
:

\bm{k}_{\pm}=\frac{\bm{Q}}{2}\pm\bm{k},\qquad\bm{p}_{\pm}=\frac{\bm{Q}}{2}\pm\bm{p}.

Here
\bm{k}_{\pm}
and
\bm{p}_{\pm}
are electron momenta of a pair on the Fermi surface before and after the scattering, respectively.
The projected BCS Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm BCS}^{(\bm{Q})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k}}\bigl(\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{k}_{+}}\hat{n}_{\bm{k}_{+}}+\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{k}_{-}}\hat{n}_{\bm{k}_{-}}\bigr)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\bm{k},\bm{p}}V_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k},\bm{p})\,\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_{+}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_{-}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\bm{p}_{-}}\hat{c}_{\bm{p}_{+}},

where

\displaystyle V_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k},\bm{p})

\displaystyle\equiv V_{\bm{k}_{+},\bm{k}_{-};\bm{p}_{+},\bm{p}_{-}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\left[J_{\bm{k}_{+},\bm{p}_{+}}J_{\bm{k}_{-},\bm{p}_{-}}-J_{\bm{k}_{+},\bm{p}_{-}}J_{\bm{k}_{-},\bm{p}_{+}}\right].

Because the vertex is already antisymmetrized,

V_{\bm{Q}}(-\bm{k},\bm{p})=-V_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k},\bm{p}),\;V_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k},-\bm{p})=-V_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k},\bm{p}),

so the gap equation closes automatically in the odd-parity sector
(
7
).

Defining

\Delta_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k})=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{p}}V_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k},\bm{p})\,\langle\hat{c}_{\bm{p}_{-}}\hat{c}_{\bm{p}_{+}}\rangle,

(9)

the mean-field Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm MF}^{(\bm{Q})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k}}\Big[\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{k}_{+}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_{+}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_{+}}+\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{k}_{-}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_{-}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_{-}}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt+\Delta_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k})\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_{+}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_{-}}^{\dagger}+\text{H.c.}\Big].

(10)

With

\bar{\xi}_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k})=\frac{\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{k}_{+}}+\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{k}_{-}}}{2},\quad\eta_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k})=\frac{\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{k}_{+}}-\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{k}_{-}}}{2},

the Bogoliubov branches are

E_{\bm{Q},\pm}(\bm{k})=\eta_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k})\pm\mathcal{E}_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k}),\;\mathcal{E}_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k})=\sqrt{\bar{\xi}_{\bm{Q}}^{2}(\bm{k})+|\Delta_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k})|^{2}}.

The anomalous average is

\langle\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_{-}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_{+}}\rangle=-\frac{\Delta_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k})}{2\mathcal{E}_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k})}\Big[1-f(E_{\bm{Q},+})-f(E_{\bm{Q},-})\Big],

which yields

\Delta_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{p}}V_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k},\bm{p})\,\frac{\Delta_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{p})}{2\mathcal{E}_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{p})}\Big[1-f(E_{\bm{Q},+})-f(E_{\bm{Q},-})\Big].

(11)

At
T=T_{c}
,
\Delta_{\bm{Q}}\to 0
, and one obtains

\Delta_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{p}}V_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k},\bm{p})\,\chi_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{p};T_{c})\,\Delta_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{p}),

with

\chi_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{p};T)=\frac{1-f(\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{p}_{+}})-f(\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{p}_{-}})}{\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{p}_{+}}+\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{p}_{-}}}.

(13)

Equation (

) is an eigenvalue problem for

V_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k},\bm{p})\chi_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{p};T)
; the leading instability is the largest
eigenmode, optimized over
\bm{Q}
.
To determine the preferred direction of
\bm{Q}
, we set

\bm{Q}=2\bm{K},

(14)

where
\bm{K}
lies on the Fermi surface, and expand the dispersion near the corresponding patch in local normal and tangential coordinates
(q_{\perp},q_{\parallel})
,

\displaystyle\xi_{\bm{K}+\bm{q}}

\displaystyle=v_{F}q_{\perp}+\frac{\kappa_{F}}{2}q_{\parallel}^{2}+\frac{q_{\perp}^{2}}{2m_{\perp}}+\cdots,

\displaystyle\xi_{\bm{K}-\bm{q}}

\displaystyle=-\,v_{F}q_{\perp}+\frac{\kappa_{F}}{2}q_{\parallel}^{2}+\frac{q_{\perp}^{2}}{2m_{\perp}}+\cdots.

(15)

Their sum is therefore

\xi_{\bm{K}+\bm{q}}+\xi_{\bm{K}-\bm{q}}=\kappa_{F}q_{\parallel}^{2}+\frac{q_{\perp}^{2}}{m_{\perp}}+\cdots,

and the pair susceptibility becomes sharply peaked along the tangential ridge
q_{\perp}=0
,

\chi_{2\bm{K}}(\bm{q};T_{c})\sim\frac{1-f(\xi_{\bm{K}+\bm{q}})-f(\xi_{\bm{K}-\bm{q}})}{\kappa_{F}q_{\parallel}^{2}+q_{\perp}^{2}/m_{\perp}}.

(16)

The available pairing phase space is thus largest where the local Fermi-surface curvature
|\kappa_{F}|
is smallest. For the square-lattice band well inside the band, the flattest segments are generically the antinodal ones, so the dominant finite-
\bm{Q}
instability is expected to occur along the axes of the lattice,

\bm{Q}_{\star}\in\left\{(\pm Q_{0},0),(0,\pm Q_{0})\right\},

(17)

rather than along the Brillouin-zone diagonals. Equation (
17
) should be understood as the leading weak-coupling result within the patch approximation; the exact finite-sample kernel preserves the full
C_{4}
symmetry.

The structure of the eigenfunction follows from Eq. (
7
) together with the ridge in Eq. (
16
). Since the pairing weight is concentrated near
q_{\perp}=0
, an odd function proportional to
q_{\perp}
is suppressed because it vanishes on the ridge, whereas the leading odd function supported by the ridge is odd in the tangential coordinate,

\phi_{2\bm{K}}(\bm{q})\propto q_{\parallel}F(\bm{q}),\qquad F(-\bm{q})=F(\bm{q}).

(18)

Thus the leading spinless finite-
\bm{Q}
state has tangential
p
-wave character around the paired patch. In lattice language, if
\bm{Q}_{\star}=(Q_{0},0)
, the paired patches are centered on an antinodal region whose tangent is approximately parallel to
\hat{y}
, and a natural global continuation is

\phi_{(Q_{0},0)}(\bm{k})\sim g_{Q_{0}}(\bm{k})\sin(k_y a),

(19)

where
g_{Q_{0}}(\bm{k})
is an even envelope localized near the paired patches. Likewise, for
\bm{Q}_{\star}=(0,Q_{0})
one obtains

\phi_{(0,Q_{0})}(\bm{k})\sim g_{Q_{0}}(\bm{k})\sin(k_x a).

(20)

The two axial directions are degenerate at quadratic order by
C_{4}
symmetry; the eventual selection between a single-
\bm{Q}
stripe state and multi-
\bm{Q}
combinations requires quartic terms beyond the linearized gap equation.

Finally, if the pairing kernel is projected onto its dominant odd separable channel,

V_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k},\bm{p})\simeq-\lambda_{\bm{Q}}\,\phi_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k})\phi_{\bm{Q}}^{\ast}(\bm{p}),

(21)

then Eq. (
12
) admits the analytic solution

\Delta_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k})=\Delta_{\bm{Q}}\phi_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{k}),

(22)

with scalar instability condition

1=\lambda_{\bm{Q}}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{p}}|\phi_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{p})|^{2}\chi_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{p};T_{c}).

(23)

The favored ordering vector is therefore maximizing the weighted susceptibility in Eq. (
23
); for the square-lattice Fermi surface deep inside the band, this selects the axial directions in Eq. (
17
) with tangential odd form factors given locally by Eq. (
18
) and globally by Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
).

To find the ground state within this degenerate manifold, one can construct the Ginzburg-Landau (GL) free energy and evaluate the quartic (fourth-order) terms that dictate how these two modes interact.
The resulting ground state is expected to be a chiral combination of Qs. We discuss this in more detail in the appendix
C
.

IV
Gaussian profile nonuniform field

To build intuition for the nature of the interaction arising in this system, we first study a structured vector-potential profile with a Gaussian envelope. This choice provides a simple and analytically tractable model. The vector potential corresponding to a more realistic loop geometry at a finite height from the 2D plane is given in the Appendix
B
.

Square-lattice derivation from Peierls substitution.

Consider electrons on a square lattice with lattice constant
a
,
nearest-neighbor hopping
t
, and a single cavity quadrature

\hat{X}\equiv\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}
. This time we consider a more realistic field distribution with the cavity vector potential

\hat{\bm{A}}(\bm{r})=\bm{A}(\bm{r})\,\hat{X},\qquad\bm{A}(\bm{r})=A_{0}\,(\hat{\bm{z}}\times\bm{r})\,e^{-r^{2}/(2\ell^{2})},

(25)

which is smooth on the scale of the lattice and decays at infinity.

The electronic Hamiltonian is introduced by Peierls substitution on each bond,

\hat{H}=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}\left[e^{-i\hat{X}\,\Phi_{ij}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{c}_{j\sigma}+\mathrm{H.c.}\right]-\mu\sum_{i,\sigma}\hat{n}_{i\sigma}+\hbar\omega\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},

(26)

where

\Phi_{ij}\equiv\frac{e}{\hbar}\int_{\bm{r}_{i}}^{\bm{r}_{j}}\bm{A}(\bm{r})\cdot d\bm{l}

is the dimensionless Peierls phase associated with bond
\langle ij\rangle
and
\sigma
is spin index.

Expanding Eq. (
26
) to linear order in the cavity field gives

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hbar\omega\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-\hat{X}\,\hat{J}+\hat{H}_{A^{2}},

with

\hat{H}_{0}=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{c}_{j\sigma}+\mathrm{H.c.}\right)-\mu\sum_{i,\sigma}\hat{n}_{i\sigma},

and the paramagnetic coupling operator

\hat{J}=it\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}\Phi_{ij}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{c}_{j\sigma}-\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}\hat{c}_{i\sigma}\right).

(27)

Here
\hat{H}_{A^{2}}
denotes the diamagnetic term, which we suppress in the following
since our focus is on the induced two-body interaction from the paramagnetic part.

Bonds in the nonuniform cavity.

For nearest-neighbor bonds it is convenient to introduce directed bond variables

\bm{\delta}\in\{a\hat{\bm{x}},a\hat{\bm{y}}\}
, and bond-center coordinates

\bm{R}=\bm{r}_{i}+\bm{\delta}/2
. Since the field varies on the scale
\ell
, for

\ell\gg a
the Peierls phase may be evaluated in the midpoint approximation:

\Phi_{i,i+\delta}=\frac{e}{\hbar}\int_{\bm{r}_{i}}^{\bm{r}_{i}+\bm{\delta}}\bm{A}(\bm{r})\cdot d\bm{l}\simeq\frac{ea}{\hbar}\,\bm{A}(\bm{R})\cdot\hat{\bm{\delta}}.

(28)

For the profile in Eq. (
25
), this gives

\displaystyle\Phi_{x}(\bm{R})

\displaystyle\equiv\Phi_{i,i+a\hat{x}}\simeq-\Phi_{0}\,R_{y}\,e^{-R^{2}/(2\ell^{2})},

\displaystyle\Phi_{y}(\bm{R})

\displaystyle\equiv\Phi_{i,i+a\hat{y}}\simeq+\Phi_{0}\,R_{x}\,e^{-R^{2}/(2\ell^{2})},

(29)

where

\Phi_{0}\equiv\frac{eaA_{0}}{\hbar}.

Thus the lattice cavity field imprints an azimuthal bond phase with a Gaussian envelope.

Fourier transform of the paramagnetic operator.

We now Fourier-transform the lattice fermions,

\hat{c}_{i\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma},

(30)

where
N
is the number of lattice sites. Using Eq. (
27
), and summing
over directed bonds
\bm{\delta}=a\hat{x},a\hat{y}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{J}

\displaystyle=it\sum_{i,\sigma}\sum_{\bm{\delta}}\Phi_{\delta}(\bm{r}_{i}+\bm{\delta}/2)\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{c}_{i+\delta,\sigma}-\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i+\delta,\sigma}\hat{c}_{i\sigma}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k},\bm{q},\sigma}g^{\rm lat}_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}\,\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma},

(31)

with lattice vertex

g^{\rm lat}_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}=-2t\sum_{\bm{\delta}=a\hat{x},a\hat{y}}\Phi_{\delta}(\bm{q})\,\sin\!\left[\left(\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2}\right)\cdot\bm{\delta}\right].

(32)

Here

\Phi_{\delta}(\bm{q})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{R}}e^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{R}}\,\Phi_{\delta}(\bm{R})

is the bond-center Fourier transform of the Peierls phase.

For the Gaussian profile in Eq. (
29
), the Fourier transforms are odd in

\bm{q}
and purely imaginary,

\displaystyle\Phi_{x}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=+i\,\Phi_{\ast}\,q_{y}\,e^{-q^{2}\ell^{2}/2},

\displaystyle\Phi_{y}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=-i\,\Phi_{\ast}\,q_{x}\,e^{-q^{2}\ell^{2}/2},

(33)

where
\Phi_{\ast}
is a real constant that depends on normalization conventions and is
proportional to
\Phi_{0}\ell^{4}
. Substituting Eq. (
33
) into
Eq. (
32
) yields

\displaystyle g^{\rm lat}_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}=g_{\ast}^{\rm lat}\left[q_{y}\sin\!\left(k_{x}a+\frac{q_{x}a}{2}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-q_{x}\sin\!\left(k_{y}a+\frac{q_{y}a}{2}\right)\right]e^{-q^{2}\ell^{2}/2},

(34)

where
g_{\ast}^{\rm lat}
is purely imaginary. Equation (
34
)
is the lattice analogue of the continuum form factor:
it is odd in momentum transfer, transverse in structure, and exponentially suppressed for

|\bm{q}|\gtrsim\ell^{-1}
.

In the long-wavelength limit
qa\ll 1
, Eq. (
34
) reduces to

\displaystyle g^{\rm lat}_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}\simeq\tilde{g}_{\ast}\,(\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}\times\bm{q})_{z}\,e^{-q^{2}\ell^{2}/2},

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}=\nabla_{\bm{k}}\xi_{\bm{k}}=2ta(\sin k_{x}a,\sin k_{y}a),

Thus the continuum factor
(\bm{k}\times\bm{q})_{z}
is replaced on the lattice by the
periodic band-velocity structure
(\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}\times\bm{q})_{z}
.

Cavity-mediated interaction.

Adiabatically eliminating the cavity quadrature
\hat{X}
gives the effective interaction

\hat{H}_{\rm int}=-\frac{\hat{J}^{2}}{\hbar\omega}.

Substituting Eq. (
31
), and normal-ordering the fermions, we obtain the
two-body part

\hat{H}_{2{\rm b}}=-\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\sum_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q}}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}g^{\rm lat}_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}\,g^{\rm lat}_{\bm{k}^{\prime},-\bm{q}}\,\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q},\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma},

(35)

and suppress one-body terms that merely renormalize the dispersion.

Zero-momentum Cooper channel.

if spin singlet states are allowed (see below for spin discussion), we project Eq. (
35
) onto the singlet
Q=0
Cooper channel,

(\bm{k}\uparrow,-\bm{k}\downarrow)\to(\bm{p}\uparrow,-\bm{p}\downarrow)
, for which

\bm{q}=\bm{p}-\bm{k}
, where
Q
is center of mass momentum.

The corresponding BCS kernel is

V^{\rm BCS}_{\bm{k},\bm{p}}=-\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\,g^{\rm lat}_{\bm{k},\bm{p}-\bm{k}}\,g^{\rm lat}_{-\bm{k},\bm{k}-\bm{p}}.

(36)

Using Eq. (
34
), one finds

\displaystyle V^{\rm BCS}_{\bm{k},\bm{p}}

\displaystyle=V_{0}^{\rm lat}\,e^{-|\bm{p}-\bm{k}|^{2}\ell^{2}}\Big[(p_{y}-k_{y})\sin\tfrac{(k_{x}+p_{x})a}{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-(p_{x}-k_{x})\sin\tfrac{(k_{y}+p_{y})a}{2}\Big]^{2}\times e^{-|\bm{p}-\bm{k}|^{2}\ell^{2}}.

(37)

where

V_{0}^{\rm lat}\equiv-\frac{(g_{\ast}^{\rm lat})^{2}}{\hbar\omega}>0,

since
g_{\ast}^{\rm lat}
is purely imaginary. The interaction is therefore positive
semidefinite as a kernel, but enters the gap equation with an overall minus sign.

The linearized gap equation is

\Delta_{\bm{k}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{p}}V^{\rm BCS}_{\bm{k},\bm{p}}\,\frac{\tanh(\xi_{\bm p}/2T)}{2\xi_{\bm{p}}}\,\Delta_{\bm{p}}.

(38)

Because
V^{\rm BCS}_{\bm{k},\bm{p}}\geq 0
, the leading pairing instability must lie in a

sign-changing
even-parity representation of the square-lattice point group.
The natural basis functions are

\displaystyle\eta_{A_{1g}}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle\sim 1,\ \cos k_{x}a+\cos k_{y}a,

\displaystyle\eta_{B_{1g}}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\cos k_{x}a-\cos k_{y}a,

(39)

\displaystyle\eta_{B_{2g}}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=2\sin k_{x}a\sin k_{y}a.

The
A_{1g}
(
s
-wave-like) sector is disfavored, while the sign-changing even-parity
channels
B_{1g}
and
B_{2g}
are naturally attractive.

This conclusion becomes explicit in the long-wavelength limit, where

\sin k_{\alpha}a\simeq k_{\alpha}a
, and Eq. (
37
) reduces to

V^{\rm BCS}_{\bm{k},\bm{p}}\simeq V_{0}\,(\bm{k}\times\bm{p})_{z}^{2}\,e^{-|\bm{p}-\bm{k}|^{2}\ell^{2}},

(40)

with the standard decomposition

\displaystyle(\bm{k}\times\bm{p})_{z}^{2}=(k_{x}p_{y}-k_{y}p_{x})^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})(p_{x}^{2}+p_{y}^{2})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(k_{x}^{2}-k_{y}^{2})(p_{x}^{2}-p_{y}^{2})-\frac{1}{2}(2k_{x}k_{y})(2p_{x}p_{y}).

(41)

Equation (
41
) shows directly that the
s
-wave projection is
repulsive, while the
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
and
d_{xy}
projections are attractive.
On the square lattice, these correspond precisely to the
B_{1g}
and
B_{2g}

representations in Eq. (
39
).

Finally, the Gaussian factor in Eq. (
37
) controls the phase space for
pairing. For
k_{F}\ell\lesssim 1
, large-angle scattering is substantial and the

d
-wave projections remain effective. By contrast, for
k_{F}\ell\gg 1
the interaction
becomes strongly forward focused,
|\bm{p}-\bm{k}|\lesssim\ell^{-1}\ll k_{F}
, and the
conventional
Q=0
Cooper instability is weakened, opening the possibility that
finite-momentum pairing channels (or pair density wave (PDW)) may become competitive.

In this regime, the conventional
Q=0
Cooper instability is
substantially weakened, and finite-momentum pairing channels may become
competitive.

We now consider pairing in a finite center-of-mass channel,
\mathbf{Q}\neq 0,

and focus on the spin-triplet sector.
Because the spin
wave function is symmetric the Fermi statistics require the orbital form factor
to be odd,

\Delta_{\mathbf{Q},i}(-\mathbf{k})=-\Delta_{\mathbf{Q},i}(\mathbf{k}).

(42)

We project the interaction (
35
) into the finite-
\mathbf{Q}

pairing channel

\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{2}+\mathbf{k},\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{2}-\mathbf{k}\right)\rightarrow\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{2}+\mathbf{p},\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{2}-\mathbf{p}\right),

(43)

for which the direct momentum transfer is

\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}.

(44)

The corresponding direct Cooper kernel is

V^{\rm dir}_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})=-\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\,g_{\mathbf{Q}/2+\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}}\,g_{\mathbf{Q}/2-\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p}}.

(45)

Using the continuum form factor for simplicity

g_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}=g_{\ast}\,(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{q})_{z}\,e^{-q^{2}\ell^{2}/2},

(46)

we obtain

\displaystyle g_{\mathbf{Q}/2+\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=g_{\ast}\left[(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{p})_{z}+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbf{Q}\times(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})\bigr)_{z}\right]e^{-|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}\ell^{2}/2},

\displaystyle g_{\mathbf{Q}/2-\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle=g_{\ast}\left[(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{p})_{z}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbf{Q}\times(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})\bigr)_{z}\right]e^{-|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}\ell^{2}/2}.

It follows that

V^{\rm dir}_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})=V_{0}\left[(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{p})_{z}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\bigl(\mathbf{Q}\times(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})\bigr)_{z}^{2}\right]e^{-|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}\ell^{2}},

(47)

where

V_{0}\equiv-\frac{g_{\ast}^{2}}{\hbar\omega}>0.

(48)

Compared with the
\mathbf{Q}=0
channel, Eq. (
47
)
contains the new term

-\frac{1}{4}(\mathbf{Q}\times(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}))_{z}^{2}
, which is manifestly
negative and therefore provides a direct source of attraction in the
finite-momentum channel.

For spin-triplet pairing which is stabilized by the ferromagnetic spin collective coupling (see Sec. VI), the orbital kernel must be antisymmetrized.
The exchange contribution is

V^{\rm ex}_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})=-\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\,g_{\mathbf{Q}/2+\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}}\,g_{\mathbf{Q}/2-\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{k}},

(49)

which evaluates to

V^{\rm ex}_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})=V_{0}\left[(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{p})_{z}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\bigl(\mathbf{Q}\times(\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{k})\bigr)_{z}^{2}\right]e^{-|\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{k}|^{2}\ell^{2}}.

The full triplet pairing kernel is therefore

V^{(t)}_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})=V^{\rm dir}_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})-V^{\rm ex}_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p}),

(50)

Equation (
50
) makes it clear why a finite-
\mathbf{Q}

instability is natural in the present problem. The Gaussian factor favors
small momentum transfer,
|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|\lesssim\ell^{-1}
, while the
transverse structure suppresses strictly forward scattering. At the same
time, the term proportional to
(\mathbf{Q}\times\mathbf{q})_{z}^{2}
generates an
explicitly attractive contribution that is absent for
\mathbf{Q}=0
. This
combination is precisely the structure expected for an Amperean or
pair-density-wave-like instability.

To explore the leading odd-parity structure, it is convenient to work in a
local patch description of the Fermi surface. Let
k_{\parallel}
denote the
component parallel to
\mathbf{Q}
and
k_{\perp}
the component transverse to

\mathbf{Q}
. For same-patch pairing one has
k_{\parallel},p_{\parallel}\ll k_{F}
,
and the dominant dependence arises from the transverse components. In this
limit,

\bigl(\mathbf{Q}\times(\mathbf{p}\pm\mathbf{k})\bigr)_{z}^{2}\simeq Q^{2}(p_{\perp}\pm k_{\perp})^{2},

(51)

while the
(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{p})_{z}^{2}
term is subleading. The triplet
kernel then reduces approximately to

\displaystyle V^{(t)}_{\mathbf{Q}}(k_{\perp},p_{\perp})\simeq-\frac{V_{0}Q^{2}}{4}\Big[

\displaystyle(p_{\perp}-k_{\perp})^{2}e^{-(p_{\perp}-k_{\perp})^{2}\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle-(p_{\perp}+k_{\perp})^{2}e^{-(p_{\perp}+k_{\perp})^{2}\ell^{2}}\Big].

(52)

Acting with Eq. (
52
) on the odd basis function

\phi_{p}(k_{\perp})=k_{\perp}
yields a negative eigenvalue, indicating that the
leading finite-
\mathbf{Q}
triplet solution has
p
-wave character within
each patch. In continuum notation, the corresponding form factor may be
written as

\Delta_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{k})\propto(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\times\hat{\mathbf{Q}})\cdot\mathbf{k}.

(53)

Thus, for
\mathbf{Q}\parallel\hat{x}
one finds

\Delta_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{k})\propto k_{y}
, while for

\mathbf{Q}\parallel\hat{y}
one has

\Delta_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{k})\propto-k_{x}
.

The linearized gap equation in the triplet channel is

\Delta_{\mathbf{Q},i}(\mathbf{k})=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{p}}V^{(t)}_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})\,\chi_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{p};T)\,\Delta_{\mathbf{Q},i}(\mathbf{p}),

(54)

with pair susceptibility

\chi_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{p};T)=\frac{1-f(\xi_{\mathbf{Q}/2+\mathbf{p}})-f(\xi_{\mathbf{Q}/2-\mathbf{p}})}{\xi_{\mathbf{Q}/2+\mathbf{p}}+\xi_{\mathbf{Q}/2-\mathbf{p}}}.

(55)

The preferred ordering wave vector
\mathbf{Q}
is determined by maximizing
the leading eigenvalue of the kernel

-V^{(t)}_{\mathbf{Q}}\chi_{\mathbf{Q}}
.

For the square-lattice dispersion, the optimal
\mathbf{Q}
is selected by the geometry of the Fermi surface.
Symmetry-related candidates include the axial wave vectors

\mathbf{Q}=(\pm Q_{0},0),\qquad(0,\pm Q_{0}),

(56)

and, depending on filling, possibly diagonal vectors

(\pm Q_{0},\pm Q_{0})
. For the axial states, the corresponding odd lattice
harmonics are naturally

\Delta_{(Q_{0},0)}(\mathbf{k})\sim\sin(k_y a),\qquad\Delta_{(0,Q_{0})}(\mathbf{k})\sim\sin(k_x a),

(57)

which are the lattice analogs of the continuum
p
-wave form
in Eq. (
53
).

We therefore conclude that the transverse interaction
naturally supports a finite-momentum, spin-triplet instability with

p
-wave structure within each
\mathbf{Q}
patch, i.e., a triplet
pair-density-wave or Amperean-like superconducting state.

IV.1
Continuum limit

Momentum-space vertex for the Gaussian vortex profile.

To understand the structure of the interaction it is insightful to investigate the continuum limit where it is more analytically tractable.
Using
\hat{\psi}(\bm{r})=\int_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}
with
\int_{\bm{k}}\equiv\int d^{2}k/(2\pi)^{2}
,
Eq. (
27
) becomes

Cooper channel and
p

From

Using Eq. (

\ell

L_{z}

The weak-coupling BCS relation for the critical temperature reads

2\Delta(0)\approx 3.53\,k_{B}T_{c}.

Investigating the solution of Eq. (

This mechanism may offer a promising route towards enhanced superconducting critical temperatures and even high temperature SC . Because the pairing interaction is mediated by quantized magnetic field, extending its spatial coverage over a larger portion of the two-dimensional material can strengthen the superconducting instability, as one can see that the interaction energy scales with the magnetic flux. In contrast to conventional cQED platforms, which are typically limited to a single mode whose field amplitude diminishes with increasing mode volume, the present geometry can be scaled by using multiple loops to cover a larger area, as shown in Fig. 1(b). This form of geometric extensibility is difficult to realize in standard cQED setups.

Expanding the area over which the magnetic field is supported increases the interaction energy until the coupling becomes sufficiently strong to excite the circuit and drive the system out of the dispersive regime. In this strong-coupling limit, the approximation of an instantaneous interaction ceases to be valid, and the effective coupling is mediated by real circuit excitations.

in the presented model,the circuit is a dark state, vacuum
|n\rangle=0
, and the fluctuations of the vacuum flux state mediate the interactions. Higher
|n\rangle
Fock states that are easier to implement in the circuit QED setting than in the cQED will enhance the interaction
Chakraborty et al. 2025

Compared with atomic cQED, circuit QED offers a broader and more engineerable route to the preparation of large Fock states. Since

Experimentally, this phase may be identified through its magneto-optical response, which provides a direct probe of time-reversal-symmetry breaking. Moreover, if the superconducting state is indeed triplet, the emergence of half-quantum vortices is expected.

VI
Zeeman coupling to spin

Graphene is known for having small spin-orbit coupling. However, as we show in this section, the cavity can mediate total spin-orbit coupling or locking. Also the cavity can induce ferromagnetic interactions for the spins.

We now include spin degrees of freedom and let the quantized magnetic mode
couple both to the orbital motion and to the spin. The electron spin couples to the same quantized magnetic field
via a Zeeman term

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{spin}}=-\mu_{B}B_{0}\big(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\big)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z},

(63)

where
\mu_{B}
is the Bohr magneton (we absorb the
g
-factor into
\mu_{B}
).

It is convenient to separate purely electronic and bosonic parts. We introduce the orbital and spin operators

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{\mathrm{orb}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{qB_{0}}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{L}_{z}^{(i)},

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{\mathrm{spin}}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{B}B_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}^{(i)}_{z}.

The electron–mode coupling then takes the compact form

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})\,\hat{X},\quad\hat{X}\equiv\hat{F}_{\mathrm{orb}}+\hat{F}_{\mathrm{spin}},

so that the full Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{e}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{mode}}+(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})\hat{X}.

In the dispersive regime, where the field mode is far detuned from electronic
excitations and remains close to its ground state,

\|\hat{X}\|\ll\hbar\Omega,

we can integrate out the bosonic degree of freedom perturbatively (e.g.,
via a Schrieffer–Wolff transformation). To second order in
\hat{X}
one
obtains an effective purely electronic Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\hat{H}_{e}-\frac{1}{\hbar\Omega}\,\hat{X}^{2}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\hat{X}^{3}}{(\hbar\Omega)^{2}}\right),

up to an additive constant. Using
\hat{X}=\hat{F}_{\mathrm{orb}}+\hat{F}_{\mathrm{spin}}
,
we have

\hat{X}^{2}=\hat{F}_{\mathrm{orb}}^{2}+\hat{F}_{\mathrm{spin}}^{2}+2\,\hat{F}_{\mathrm{orb}}\hat{F}_{\mathrm{spin}},

so that

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\hat{H}_{e}-\frac{1}{\hbar\Omega}\hat{F}_{\mathrm{orb}}^{2}-\frac{1}{\hbar\Omega}\hat{F}_{\mathrm{spin}}^{2}-\frac{2}{\hbar\Omega}\hat{F}_{\mathrm{orb}}\hat{F}_{\mathrm{spin}}.

The first interaction term is orbit-orbit coupling seen before,

-\frac{1}{\hbar\Omega}\hat{F}_{\mathrm{orb}}^{2}=-\frac{q^{2}B_{0}^{2}}{m^{2}\hbar\Omega}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{L}_{z}^{(i)}\right)^{2},

which is a current–current interaction in the orbital sector. In momentum space and
projected to the Fermi surface it generates a separable attractive kernel whose leading eigenfunction
is odd under
k_{y}\to-k_{y}
. i.e., a superconducting instability in the
p
-wave orbital channel.

The second term,

-\frac{1}{\hbar\Omega}\hat{F}_{\mathrm{spin}}^{2}=-\frac{\mu_{B}^{2}B_{0}^{2}}{\hbar\Omega}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}\right)^{2},

describes an infinite-range Ising interaction between spins. Expanding

\left(\sum_{i}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}\right)^{2}=N+2\sum_{i<j}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{z}
,
we see that the nontrivial part is a ferromagnetic coupling

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{spin\text{-}eff}}=-J_{s}\sum_{i<j}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{z},\quad J_{s}=\frac{2\mu_{B}^{2}B_{0}^{2}}{\hbar\Omega}>0,

which tends to polarize the electronic spins along the
z
-axis. In the
spin-polarized regime the superconducting instability is necessarily

triplet
, involving equal-spin pairs (e.g.,
\uparrow\uparrow
).

Finally, the cross term

-\frac{2}{\hbar\Omega}\hat{F}_{\mathrm{orb}}\hat{F}_{\mathrm{spin}}=-\frac{2q\mu_{B}B_{0}^{2}}{m\hbar\Omega}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{L}_{z}^{(i)}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{z}\right)

provides a magneto-orbital coupling that locks the direction of the orbital
current to the spin polarization. In a mean-field description, a finite

\langle\sum_{j}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{z}\rangle
acts as an effective field favoring a
particular sign of
\langle\sum_{i}\hat{x}^{(i)}\hat{p}_{y}^{(i)}\rangle
, thereby
selecting the chirality or orientation of the chiral state and stabilizing the spin triplet.

In summary, integrating out the magnetic mode generates (i) an orbital
current–current interaction that drives a
p
-wave superconducting instability,
(ii) a ferromagnetic Ising interaction that spin-polarizes the electrons,
and (iii) a cross term that couples spin polarization to orbital chirality.
The resulting superconducting phase is naturally a
spin-triplet,
chiral
state, built from equal-spin Cooper pairs on a polarized Fermi
surface.

VII
conclusion

In conclusion, our results identify the proposed platform as a promising and potentially realizable route to different unconventional superconductivity phases of matter. Depending on the microscopic regime, the same structured gauge-field-mediated interaction can favor either chiral pair-density-wave order at finite momentum or a uniform chiral superconducting state in the zero-momentum channel. More broadly, the high degree of tunability in the interaction profile and pairing structure makes this setting a compelling arena for engineering and probing competing chiral superconducting phases in a controlled manner.

Chiral superconductivity was recently observed in rhombohedral graphene. Our results could provide an explanation if at least one of the layers contain magnetic impurities that could facilitate a similar type of angular momentum pairing.

Our results highlight a qualitative distinction from standard cavity QED: the relevant coupling here is not electric dipole, but to the orbital motion, thereby enabling angular-momentum exchange between electrons. The induced interaction, proportional to
-L_{i}L_{j}
, grows for Fermi-surface electrons as
v_{F}^{2}
times the field-covered area. Increasing the spatial extent of the supported magnetic field can therefore enhance the interaction energy scale and, in turn, provide a favorable route toward higher critical temperatures.

Appendix A
Finite-sample interaction matrix elements for uniform magnetic field

We consider spinless fermions on a finite
L\times L
square lattice with open boundary conditions,

\hat{H}_{e}=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\left(\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j}+\mathrm{H.c.}\right)-\mu\sum_{i}\hat{n}_{i},

(64)

and the cavity-induced effective Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{\rm eff}=\hat{H}_{e}-\frac{\hat{J}^{2}}{\hbar\omega}.

(65)

For a uniform magnetic field in symmetric gauge,

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{B}{2}(-y,x,0)\,\hat{X},\qquad\hat{X}\equiv\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger},

(66)

the electronic operator coupled to the cavity is

\hat{J}=\kappa_{\Phi}\left[\sum_{x=1}^{L}\sum_{y=1}^{L-1}X_{x}\,\hat{j}_{y}(x,y)-\sum_{x=1}^{L-1}\sum_{y=1}^{L}Y_{y}\,\hat{j}_{x}(x,y)\right],

where

\kappa_{\Phi}=\frac{ea}{2\hbar}\frac{\Phi}{\mathcal{A}},

(67)

and we measure position from the sample center,

X_{x}=a\left(x-\frac{L+1}{2}\right),\qquad Y_{y}=a\left(y-\frac{L+1}{2}\right).

(68)

The bond-current operators are

\displaystyle\hat{j}_{x}(x,y)

\displaystyle=it\left(\hat{c}_{x+1,y}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{x,y}-\hat{c}_{x,y}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{x+1,y}\right),

(69)

\displaystyle\hat{j}_{y}(x,y)

\displaystyle=it\left(\hat{c}_{x,y+1}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{x,y}-\hat{c}_{x,y}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{x,y+1}\right).

(70)

Let
\{\phi_{\alpha}(i)\}
denote the normalized single-particle eigenstates of

\hat{H}_{e}
,

\sum_{j}h_{ij}\phi_{\alpha}(j)=\varepsilon_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}(i),\qquad\hat{c}_{i}=\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}(i)\hat{c}_{\alpha},

(71)

so that

\hat{H}_{e}=\sum_{\alpha}\varepsilon_{\alpha}\hat{c}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\alpha}.

(72)

In this basis,

\hat{J}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}J_{\alpha\beta}\,\hat{c}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\beta},

(73)

with matrix elements

\displaystyle J_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=it\,\kappa_{\Phi}\Bigg[\sum_{x=1}^{L}\sum_{y=1}^{L-1}X_{x}\,\Lambda^{(y)}_{\alpha\beta}(x,y)-\sum_{x=1}^{L-1}\sum_{y=1}^{L}Y_{y}\,\Lambda^{(x)}_{\alpha\beta}(x,y)\Bigg],

(74)

where

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(y)}_{\alpha\beta}(x,y)

\displaystyle\equiv\phi_{\alpha}^{*}(x,y+1)\phi_{\beta}(x,y)-\phi_{\alpha}^{*}(x,y)\phi_{\beta}(x,y+1),

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(x)}_{\alpha\beta}(x,y)

\displaystyle\equiv\phi_{\alpha}^{*}(x+1,y)\phi_{\beta}(x,y)-\phi_{\alpha}^{*}(x,y)\phi_{\beta}(x+1,y).

This is the exact finite-sample matrix element of the angular-momentum . Because
X_{x}
and
Y_{y}
appear explicitly, translational invariance is lost, and crystal momentum is no longer a good quantum number.

Fermionic normal ordering of
-\hat{J}^{2}/(\hbar\omega)
yields

\displaystyle-\frac{\hat{J}^{2}}{\hbar\omega}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\sum_{\alpha\delta}(J^{2})_{\alpha\delta}\,\hat{c}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\delta}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}V_{\alpha\beta;\gamma\delta}\,\hat{c}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\delta}\hat{c}_{\gamma},

where

V_{\alpha\beta;\gamma\delta}=-\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\left(J_{\alpha\gamma}J_{\beta\delta}-J_{\alpha\delta}J_{\beta\gamma}\right)

(75)

is the antisymmetrized two-body vertex. The effective one-body sector is therefore

\hat{H}_{\rm 1b}^{\rm eff}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}h^{\rm eff}_{\alpha\beta}\,\hat{c}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\beta},\quad h^{\rm eff}_{\alpha\beta}=\varepsilon_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{(J^{2})_{\alpha\beta}}{\hbar\omega}.

In a finite sample,
(J^{2})_{\alpha\beta}
is not generally diagonal in the eigenbasis of
\hat{H}_{e}
. Thus, the cavity-induced bilinear term cannot in general be absorbed into a simple scalar shift of
\varepsilon_{\alpha}
; rather, one must first diagonalize the full matrix
h^{\rm eff}_{\alpha\beta}
.

For the
L\times L
square with open boundaries, the eigenstates of
\hat{H}_{e}

\phi_{\mathbf{n}}(x,y)=u_{n_{x}}(x)u_{n_{y}}(y),u_{n}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L+1}}\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi nx}{L+1}\right),

with
\mathbf{n}=(n_{x},n_{y})
and eigenvalues

\varepsilon_{\mathbf{n}}=-2t\left[\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi n_{x}}{L+1}\right)+\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi n_{y}}{L+1}\right)\right]-\mu.

In this basis, Eq. (
74
) reduces to

J_{\mathbf{n}\mathbf{m}}=it\,\kappa_{\Phi}\left[X_{n_{x}m_{x}}\,D_{n_{y}m_{y}}-D_{n_{x}m_{x}}\,X_{n_{y}m_{y}}\right],

(76)

where

\displaystyle X_{nm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=1}^{L}X_{x}\,u_{n}(x)u_{m}(x),

\displaystyle D_{nm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=1}^{L-1}\left[u_{n}(x+1)u_{m}(x)-u_{n}(x)u_{m}(x+1)\right].

These matrix elements obey the parity selection rule
X_{nm}=D_{nm}=0
for
n+m

n+m

\displaystyle D_{nm}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{L+1}\frac{\sin k_{n}\,\sin k_{m}}{\cos k_{n}-\cos k_{m}},

(77)

\displaystyle X_{nm}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2a}{L+1}\frac{\sin k_{n}\,\sin k_{m}}{(\cos k_{n}-\cos k_{m})^{2}},

(78)

with

(79)

The full cavity-mediated interaction in the finite-sample eigenbasis is therefore completely specified by Eqs. (
75

76
). Unlike in the translationally invariant limit, the resulting two-body vertex does not conserve pair momentum exactly; any center-of-mass momentum labeling of the Cooper channel is therefore only approximate and becomes asymptotically accurate only for states localized far from the sample boundaries or in the large-

\displaystyle=+i\,\Phi_{R}\,e^{-qh}\,\frac{J_{1}(qR)}{q^{2}}\,q_{y},

\displaystyle\Phi_{y}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=-i\,\Phi_{R}\,e^{-qh}\,\frac{J_{1}(qR)}{q^{2}}\,q_{x},

(85)

is real. The vertex is therefore again purely imaginary and transverse, but it
now inherits the exact nonlocal momentum structure of the loop.

In contrast to the Gaussian case, the loop profile
does not impose a Gaussian small-
q
cutoff; instead, the relevant scale is set
by
R
and the vertical separation
h
.

Cavity-mediated interaction and Cooper kernel.

Adiabatically eliminating the cavity quadrature again gives

\hat{H}_{\rm int}=-\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\,\hat{J}^{2},

(86)

so the two-body interaction retains the same operator structure as before,
with the replacement

g^{\rm lat}_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}\to g^{\rm loop}_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}
.
Projecting onto the
Q=0
singlet Cooper channel,

(\bm{k}\uparrow,-\bm{k}\downarrow)\to(\bm{p}\uparrow,-\bm{p}\downarrow)
,
with
\bm{q}=\bm{p}-\bm{k}
, we obtain

V^{\rm BCS}_{\bm{k},\bm{p}}=-\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\,g^{\rm loop}_{\bm{k},\bm{p}-\bm{k}}\,g^{\rm loop}_{-\bm{k},\bm{k}-\bm{p}}.

(87)

Using Eq. (
84
), this becomes

\displaystyle V^{\rm BCS}_{\bm{k},\bm{p}}

\displaystyle=V_{R}\,e^{-2|\bm{p}-\bm{k}|h}\,\frac{J_{1}^{2}(|\bm{p}-\bm{k}|R)}{|\bm{p}-\bm{k}|^{4}}\Bigg[(p_{y}-k_{y})\sin\!\frac{(k_{x}+p_{x})a}{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-(p_{x}-k_{x})\sin\!\frac{(k_{y}+p_{y})a}{2}\Bigg]^{2},

where

V_{R}\equiv\frac{g_{R}^{2}}{\hbar\omega}>0.

The kernel is therefore again positive semidefinite as a quadratic form, while
entering the gap equation with the usual overall minus sign.

Comparison to a Gaussian profile.

Relative to the Gaussian ansatz, the circular-loop geometry replaces the
short-range factor
e^{-q^{2}\ell^{2}/2}
by the exact magnetostatic form

e^{-qh}J_{1}(qR)/q^{2}
. The scale
h
is typically few nanometers of for the separation of the loop and the 2DEG so it does not suppress large momentum transfer
through the evanescent factor
e^{-qh}
, while
R
imprints the oscillatory
Bessel structure associated with the loop circumference.

Appendix C
chiral ground state from PDW component components

We consider the two symmetry-related PDW ordering vectors

\mathbf{Q}_{x}=(Q,0),\qquad\mathbf{Q}_{y}=(0,Q),

(88)

together with their opposite-momentum partners.
To describe the standing-wave sector, we restrict to the reduced manifold in which the
\pm\mathbf{Q}_{\nu}
components within each direction are locked with equal magnitude. The gap function can then be written as

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\Big[\eta_{x}\,\phi_{x}(\mathbf{k})\cos(\mathbf Q_x\!\cdot\!\mathbf r)+\eta_{y}\,\phi_{y}(\mathbf{k})\cos(\mathbf Q_y\!\cdot\!\mathbf r)\Big],

(89)

where
\eta_{x}
and
\eta_{y}
are complex amplitudes. For the odd-parity triplet channel selected by the transverse interaction on the square lattice, the leading basis functions are

\phi_{x}(\mathbf{k})=\sin(k_y a),\qquad\phi_{y}(\mathbf{k})=-\sin(k_x a),

(90)

which are exchanged by the
C_{4}
symmetry of the lattice.

The quadratic Ginzburg–Landau functional is fixed by symmetry to be

F_{2}=r\left(|\eta_{x}|^{2}+|\eta_{y}|^{2}\right),

(91)

so that the two components become critical simultaneously at
r=0
.
To determine the structure of the ordered state, one must therefore retain the quartic terms.

A controlled derivation follows from integrating out the fermions in the presence of the pairing field. Writing the Nambu action in the standard form, the free energy reads

F[\Delta]=\frac{1}{g}\int d^{2}r\sum_{\mathbf{k}}|\Delta(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})|^{2}-T\,\Tr\ln\!\left(1-G_{0}\Sigma\right),

where
G_{0}
is the normal-state Green’s function and
\Sigma
is the anomalous self-energy generated by
\Delta
.
Expanding the logarithm,

-\,T\,\Tr\ln(1-G_0\Sigma)=T\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m}\Tr(G_0\Sigma)^{m},

(92)

and the quartic contribution is

F_{4}=\frac{T}{4}\Tr(G_0\Sigma)^{4}.

(93)

Projecting Eq. (
93
) onto the two-component manifold
(
89
) yields the general quartic form

F_{4}=u\left(|\eta_{x}|^{2}+|\eta_{y}|^{2}\right)^{2}+v\left(|\eta_{x}|^{2}-|\eta_{y}|^{2}\right)^{2}+\beta_{2}\,\bigl|\eta_{x}^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}\bigr|^{2},

where
u
and
v
are real coefficients and
\beta_{2}
is the phase-locking coefficient.
The last term is the only independent quartic invariant in the reduced basis that depends on the relative phase between
\eta_{x}
and
\eta_{y}
.

We now derive
\beta_{2}
microscopically.
The mixed quartic term arises from the box contribution in Eq. (
93
) containing two insertions of the
x
-component and two insertions of the
y
-component.
Its order-parameter structure is

F_{4}^{xy}=\beta_{2}\Big[2|\eta_{x}|^{2}|\eta_{y}|^{2}+\eta_{x}^{2}\eta_{y}^{\ast 2}+\eta_{x}^{\ast 2}\eta_{y}^{2}\Big],

(94)

which is equivalent to the invariant
|\eta_{x}^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}|^{2}
up to terms already absorbed into
u
.
For real basis functions
\phi_{x},\phi_{y}
, one finds

\beta_{2}=C\,T\sum_{\omega_{n},\mathbf{k}}\phi_{x}^{2}(\mathbf{k})\phi_{y}^{2}(\mathbf{k})\,K_{x}(\mathbf{k},\omega_{n})\,K_{y}(\mathbf{k},\omega_{n}),

(95)

where
C>0
is a positive combinatorial constant and

K_{\nu}(\mathbf{k},\omega_{n})=G\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_{\nu}}{2}+\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n}\right)G\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_{\nu}}{2}-\mathbf{k},-i\omega_{n}\right),\qquad\nu=x,y.

(96)

To proceed, we assume the weak-coupling symmetric-patch reduction appropriate to the LO state, namely that the dispersion in each PDW channel is inversion-symmetric about the pair center-of-mass momentum:

\xi\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_{\nu}}{2}+\mathbf{k}\right)=\xi\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_{\nu}}{2}-\mathbf{k}\right)\equiv\xi_{\nu}(\mathbf{k}).

(97)

Then the normal-state Green’s function

G(\mathbf{p},i\omega_{n})=\frac{1}{i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\mathbf{p}}}

(98)

gives

\displaystyle K_{\nu}(\mathbf{k},\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bigl(i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\nu}(\mathbf{k})\bigr)\bigl(-i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\nu}(\mathbf{k})\bigr)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{\nu}^{2}(\mathbf{k})}.

(99)

Equation (
95
) therefore reduces to

\beta_{2}=C\,T\sum_{\omega_{n},\mathbf{k}}\frac{\phi_{x}^{2}(\mathbf{k})\phi_{y}^{2}(\mathbf{k})}{\left[\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{x}^{2}(\mathbf{k})\right]\left[\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{y}^{2}(\mathbf{k})\right]}.

(100)

Since
C>0
,
\phi_{x}^{2}\phi_{y}^{2}\geq 0
, and each denominator is strictly positive, every term in the sum is nonnegative. Moreover, because
\phi_{x}(\mathbf{k})\phi_{y}(\mathbf{k})\neq 0
on a finite region of the Brillouin zone, the sum is strictly positive:

\beta_{2}>0.

(101)

Thus, within the weak-coupling symmetric-patch reduction, the sign of the phase-locking invariant is fixed microscopically. Equation (
101
) relies on the symmetric-patch condition
(
97
). Without that reduction, the mixed quartic coefficient is still obtained from the fermionic box diagram, but its sign is not generically fixed by symmetry alone and must be computed from the full momentum-resolved loop.

It is now convenient to parametrize

\eta_{x}=\sqrt{\rho}\,\cos\theta\,e^{i\varphi_{x}},\qquad\eta_{y}=\sqrt{\rho}\,\sin\theta\,e^{i\varphi_{y}},

(102)

with relative phase

\delta=\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{x}.

(103)

Using the identity

\bigl|\eta_{x}^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}\bigr|^{2}=\rho^{2}\Bigl[1-\sin^{2}(2\theta)\sin^{2}\delta\Bigr],

(104)

the quartic free energy becomes

F_{4}=u\rho^{2}+v\rho^{2}\cos^{2}(2\theta)+\beta_{2}\rho^{2}\Bigl[1-\sin^{2}(2\theta)\sin^{2}\delta\Bigr].

(105)

Because
\beta_{2}>0
, the relative phase is minimized by maximizing
\sin^{2}\delta
, hence

\delta=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}.

(106)

Substituting this into Eq. (
105
) gives

F_{4}=u\rho^{2}+\left(v+\beta_{2}\right)\rho^{2}\cos^{2}(2\theta).

(107)

Therefore, the coexistence of the two symmetry-related PDW components is favored when

v+\beta_{2}>0,

(108)

in which case the minimum occurs at equal amplitudes,

|\eta_{x}|=|\eta_{y}|,\qquad\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}.

(109)

Combining Eqs. (
106
) and
(
109
), the two minima are

(\eta_{x},\eta_{y})\propto(1,\pm i).

(110)

The resulting gap function is

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{\rm TRSB}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\propto\bigl[i(\mathbf{d}\!\cdot\!\bm{\sigma})\sigma^{y}\bigr]\Big[\phi_{x}(\mathbf{k})\cos(Qx)

\displaystyle\pm i\,\phi_{y}(\mathbf{k})\cos(Qy)\Big].

(111)

This state is a bidirectional PDW with a relative phase
\pm\pi/2
, and hence it spontaneously breaks time-reversal symmetry.

The chirality is characterized by the Ising order parameter

\chi=i\left(\eta_{x}\eta_{y}^{\ast}-\eta_{y}\eta_{x}^{\ast}\right)=2\,\imaginary\!\left(\eta_{x}\eta_{y}^{\ast}\right),

(112)

which is nonzero for the two minima (
110
).
Under time reversal,

\mathcal{T}:\ (\eta_{x},\eta_{y})\mapsto(\eta_{x}^{\ast},\eta_{y}^{\ast}),

(113)

so the two states
(1,+i)
and
(1,-i)
are exchanged.
We therefore conclude that, within the reduced LO manifold and for
v+\beta_{2}>0
, the weak-coupling theory selects a time-reversal-breaking bidirectional PDW ground state.

C.1
Reduction to the chiral sector

In this Appendix we formulate the quartic Ginzburg–Landau theory for the four
axial PDW components

\Delta_{x\pm}\equiv\Delta_{\pm\bm{Q}_{x}},\qquad\Delta_{y\pm}\equiv\Delta_{\pm\bm{Q}_{y}},\bm{Q}_{x}=Q_{0}\hat{x},\bm{Q}_{y}=Q_{0}\hat{y},

and show how the reduced two-component LO theory discussed in the main text
emerges as a controlled submanifold.

We take the pairing Hamiltonian with
the odd-parity basis functions that satisfy

\phi_{\mu}(-\bm{k})=-\phi_{\mu}(\bm{k}).

(114)

For the leading axial triplet harmonics on the square lattice one may choose

\phi_{x}(\bm{k})=\sin(k_y a),\qquad\phi_{y}(\bm{k})=-\sin(k_x a),

(115)

although the derivation below does not require this specific form until the
weak-coupling reduction is invoked.

The free-energy functional obtained after integrating out the fermions is

F[\Delta]=F_{N}+\sum_{\alpha}\frac{|\Delta_{\alpha}|^{2}}{\lambda_{\alpha}}-T\,\Tr\ln\!\left(1-\hat{G}_{0}\hat{\Sigma}_{\Delta}\right),

(116)

with
\alpha\in\{x+,x-,y+,y-\}
and

G_{0}(\bm{p},i\omega_{n})=\frac{1}{i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\bm{p}}}.

(117)

Expanding the logarithm,

-\,T\,\Tr\ln\!\left(1-\hat{G}_{0}\hat{\Sigma}_{\Delta}\right)=T\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m}\Tr\!\left(\hat{G}_{0}\hat{\Sigma}_{\Delta}\right)^{m},

(118)

so that the quartic contribution is

F_{4}=\frac{T}{4}\Tr\!\left(\hat{G}_{0}\hat{\Sigma}_{\Delta}\right)^{4}.

(119)

C.2
Symmetry-allowed quartic invariants

Translation symmetry requires every quartic monomial to carry zero total
crystal momentum. For the four axial components
\Delta_{x\pm}
and

\Delta_{y\pm}
, the quartic free energy therefore contains the generic
invariants

\displaystyle F_{4}=

\displaystyle\;u_{1}\sum_{\mu=x,y}\sum_{s=\pm}|\Delta_{\mu s}|^{4}+u_{2}\sum_{\mu=x,y}|\Delta_{\mu+}|^{2}|\Delta_{\mu-}|^{2}

\displaystyle+u_{3}\left(|\Delta_{x+}|^{2}+|\Delta_{x-}|^{2}\right)\left(|\Delta_{y+}|^{2}+|\Delta_{y-}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+u_{4}\Bigl(\Delta_{x+}\Delta_{x-}\Delta_{y+}^{\ast}\Delta_{y-}^{\ast}+\mathrm{c.c.}\Bigr)+\cdots,

(120)

where the last term denotes additional symmetry-allowed quartic terms that are
not phase sensitive in the reduced LO sector. The key phase-locking invariant is
the last term in Eq. (
120
). It is allowed because

\bm{Q}_{x}+(-\bm{Q}_{x})-\bm{Q}_{y}-(-\bm{Q}_{y})=0.

(121)

C.3
Exact microscopic definition of
u_{4}

The coefficient
u_{4}
is defined microscopically by projecting the quartic
trace (
119
) onto the monomial

\Delta_{x+}\Delta_{x-}\Delta_{y+}^{\ast}\Delta_{y-}^{\ast}
:

u_{4}=\frac{T}{4}\left.\frac{\partial^{4}}{\partial\Delta_{x+}\,\partial\Delta_{x-}\,\partial\Delta_{y+}^{\ast}\,\partial\Delta_{y-}^{\ast}}\Tr\!\left(\hat{G}_{0}\hat{\Sigma}_{\Delta}\right)^{4}\right|_{\Delta=0}.

Equivalently,

u_{4}=T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{k}}\,\mathcal{B}_{xy}(\bm{k},i\omega_{n}),

(122)

where
\mathcal{B}_{xy}
is the properly momentum-routed fermionic box kernel,
including the sum over inequivalent cyclic orderings of the four pairing
vertices that contribute to the monomial

\Delta_{x+}\Delta_{x-}\Delta_{y+}^{\ast}\Delta_{y-}^{\ast}
.

At the full lattice level,
\mathcal{B}_{xy}
depends on shifted internal
momenta and on form factors evaluated at different arguments. In particular,
the exact box does
not
generically collapse to a single common-momentum
product of the form

\phi_{x}^{2}(\bm{k})\phi_{y}^{2}(\bm{k})\,G_{x+}\bar{G}_{x-}G_{y+}\bar{G}_{y-},

(123)

and the sign of
u_{4}
is therefore not fixed by symmetry alone in the full
lattice theory. Away from the controlled weak-coupling reduction discussed
below,
u_{4}
must be obtained by evaluating the full box kernel

\mathcal{B}_{xy}
.

C.4
Weak-coupling symmetric-patch reduction

A transparent analytic result follows in the weak-coupling regime, where each
PDW component is concentrated near a symmetry-related patch and the relevant
pairing momenta satisfy

\bm{Q}_{x}=2\bm{K}_{x},\qquad\bm{Q}_{y}=2\bm{K}_{y}.

(124)

Writing the internal momentum relative to the corresponding patch center as

\bm{q}
, we assume that the dispersion is inversion symmetric within each
paired patch:

\xi\!\left(\frac{\bm{Q}_{\nu}}{2}+\bm{q}\right)=\xi\!\left(\frac{\bm{Q}_{\nu}}{2}-\bm{q}\right)\equiv\varepsilon_{\nu}(\bm{q}),\qquad\nu=x,y.

(125)

In the same approximation the form factors may be treated as smooth patch
functions
\phi_{\nu}(\bm{q})
, and the inequivalent box routings reduce to the
same positive kernel up to an overall combinatorial constant
C>0
. One then
obtains

u_{4}\approx C\,T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{q}}\frac{\phi_{x}^{2}(\bm{q})\phi_{y}^{2}(\bm{q})}{\bigl(\omega_{n}^{2}+\varepsilon_{x}^{2}(\bm{q})\bigr)\bigl(\omega_{n}^{2}+\varepsilon_{y}^{2}(\bm{q})\bigr)}.

(126)

For the axial triplet lattice harmonics,

\phi_{x}(\bm{q})\sim\sin(k_y a),\qquad\phi_{y}(\bm{q})\sim\sin(k_x a),

(127)

but the sign argument below requires only that
\phi_{x}
and
\phi_{y}
be
real, so that
\phi_{x}^{2}\phi_{y}^{2}\geq 0
.

The Matsubara sum is elementary:

\displaystyle T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{(\omega_{n}^{2}+\varepsilon_{x}^{2})(\omega_{n}^{2}+\varepsilon_{y}^{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{y}^{2}-\varepsilon_{x}^{2}}\left[\frac{\tanh(\varepsilon_x/2T)}{2\varepsilon_{x}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{\tanh(\varepsilon_y/2T)}{2\varepsilon_{y}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{f(\varepsilon_{x})-f(\varepsilon_{y})}{2(\varepsilon_{y}^{2}-\varepsilon_{x}^{2})},

(128)

where

f(\varepsilon)\equiv\frac{\tanh(\varepsilon/2T)}{\varepsilon}.

(129)

Since
f(\varepsilon)
is strictly decreasing for
\varepsilon>0
, the
right-hand side of Eq. (
128
) is nonnegative.
Therefore every term in Eq. (
126
) is nonnegative, and one
finds

u_{4}>0

(130)

within the weak-coupling symmetric-patch approximation.

C.5
Phase locking and reduction to the LO sector

Writing

\Delta_{\mu\pm}=|\Delta_{\mu\pm}|e^{i\theta_{\mu\pm}},

(131)

the phase-sensitive quartic contribution is

F_{u_{4}}=2u_{4}\,|\Delta_{x+}\Delta_{x-}\Delta_{y+}\Delta_{y-}|\cos\Phi,\Phi\equiv\theta_{x+}+\theta_{x-}-\theta_{y+}-\theta_{y-}.

(132)

Hence, if
u_{4}>0
, the minimum occurs at

\Phi=\pi\qquad(\mathrm{mod}\;2\pi).

(133)

We now restrict to the LO submanifold, in which the
\pm\bm{Q}_{\nu}
components
within each direction are locked with equal magnitude and equal phase:

\Delta_{x+}=\Delta_{x-}\equiv\frac{\eta_{x}}{2},\qquad\Delta_{y+}=\Delta_{y-}\equiv\frac{\eta_{y}}{2}.

(134)

The real-space gap function then becomes

\Delta(\bm{r},\bm{k})=\eta_{x}\,\phi_{x}(\bm{k})\cos(\bm Q_x\!\cdot\!\bm r)+\eta_{y}\,\phi_{y}(\bm{k})\cos(\bm Q_y\!\cdot\!\bm r).

(135)

Substituting Eq. (
134
) into
Eq. (
132
) gives

F_{u_{4}}^{\rm LO}=\frac{u_{4}}{8}\,|\eta_{x}|^{2}|\eta_{y}|^{2}\cos\!\bigl[2(\varphi_{x}-\varphi_{y})\bigr],

(136)

where
\eta_{\nu}=|\eta_{\nu}|e^{i\varphi_{\nu}}
.
For
u_{4}>0
, Eq. (
136
) is minimized by

\varphi_{y}-\varphi_{x}=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}.

(137)

Thus the four-component quartic invariant reduces in the LO sector to the
time-reversal-breaking chiral combination

(\eta_{x},\eta_{y})\propto(1,\pm i).

(138)

Up to a trivial normalization factor, the phase-locking coefficient in the
reduced two-component LO theory is therefore

\beta_{2}=\frac{u_{4}}{8},

(139)

so that the positivity of
u_{4}
in the weak-coupling symmetric-patch
approximation is equivalent to the positivity of
\beta_{2}
in the main text.
