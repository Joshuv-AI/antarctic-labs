Shockwave‐Induced Myoblast Exosome‐Like Vesicles: Emerging Drivers of Muscle Regeneration
Authors: Larisa Ryskalin, Federica Fulceri, Lorenzo Germelli, Maria Cristina D'Agostino, Paola Soldani, Eleonora Da Pozzo, Marco Gesi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExosomes, a subset of nanosized extracellular vesicles, are key mediators of intracellular communication that show growing therapeutic potential in skeletal muscle regeneration. By shuttling bioactive cargoes between cells, they modulate recipient cell behavior, influencing proliferation, differentiation, and tissue remodeling. Previous studies demonstrated that different tensile load regimes can modulate both the production and regenerative capacity of myoblast‐derived exosomes, positioning mechanically induced vesicle release as a potential driver of morpho‐functional adaptations required for myoblast differentiation and myotube formation. Within this framework, shockwave (SW) therapy is gaining increasing attention as a non‐invasive mechanical stimulus in regenerative medicine; however, the precise cellular mechanisms linking SW stimulation and tissue repair remain poorly understood. Preclinical evidence shows that extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from SW‐treated cardiomyocytes possess pro‐angiogenic and regenerative properties, suggesting that SW‐induced paracrine signaling may be conserved across tissues. In this study, we investigated whether in vitro SW treatment affects myogenic exosome release, contributing to SW‐mediated muscle regeneration. Tunable Resistive Pore Sensing analysis revealed a significant increase in EV concentration at 6 h post‐treatment, with no detectable differences between treated cells and controls at 12 h, indicating a rapid and transient secretory response. Importantly, vesicle size distribution remained unchanged across conditions, suggesting that SW primarily modulates EV production dynamics rather than vesicle morphology. Ultrastructural analyses further confirmed this transient activation, showing vesicular trafficking and exosome‐like EV secretion in treated myoblasts. Further mechanistic insights into SW‐induced exosome release may uncover novel pathways, contributing to SW regenerative effects supporting its translational application to muscle injuries and disorders.Keywords: cell communication, extracorporeal shockwave therapy, muscles, secretory vesicles, skeletal myoblasts, treatment outcome

After development, skeletal muscle retains a significant, albeit limited, capacity for self‐repair mediated by resident stem cells and their progeny, including proliferating myoblasts and circulating cell populations [1]. Following injury, a finely tuned cascade of cellular and molecular events orchestrates muscle repair and recovery, involving cell activation and differentiation, release of extracellular secreted factors, and dynamic cell‐to‐extracellular matrix interactions [1, 2].

However, incomplete or impaired muscle healing is frequently observed in traumatic injuries and pathological conditions, highlighting the need for novel therapeutic strategies to enhance self‐repair and improve functional outcomes [3]. While minor lesions can generally undergo complete healing, severe or extensive injuries can overwhelm the intrinsic regenerative capacity of skeletal muscle, leading to fibrosis and long‐term functional deficits. In such cases, particularly in large‐volume muscle loss, intrinsic repair mechanisms often require interventional support [1, 4, 5].

Over the past decades, considerable efforts have been devoted to developing therapeutic strategies to improve muscle regeneration, including biomaterials and tissue engineering approaches, cell‐based therapies, physical rehabilitation treatments, and surgical interventions [1]. Nonetheless, current therapeutic strategies remain insufficient to fully restore muscle architecture and function in such conditions. Early evidence suggests that mechanical stimulation may represent a simple, non‐invasive, and effective strategy to enhance muscle regeneration, while limiting fibrosis [6, 7, 8, 9]. In this context, extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) has gained growing attention in soft tissue regenerative medicine [10].

By delivering acoustic waves (shockwaves, SW) to biological tissues, ESWT acts as a mechanical stimulus that activates mechanotransduction pathways, triggering a complex interplay of repair and remodeling processes. These include improved blood flow and perfusion, enhanced collagen synthesis, stem cell activation, and growth factor release, all of which contribute to tissue recovery [11]. However, the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of shockwaves remain poorly understood. A few current studies on cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells suggest that the beneficial effects of SW are achieved, at least in part, by the enhanced secretion of paracrine extracellular vesicles (EVs), including exosomes [12, 13, 14].

Exosomes are nano‐sized (30–150 nm in diameter), lipid membrane‐enclosed vesicles derived from the release of intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) upon fusion of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) with the plasma membrane [15]. Once released into the extracellular milieu, exosomes can deliver bioactive molecules that modulate recipient cell behavior [15]. Acting as pivotal mediators of intercellular communication, their role in skeletal muscle regeneration has been increasingly recognized, with several studies demonstrating that skeletal muscle cell‐derived exosomes promote satellite cell differentiation and upregulate myogenic gene expression in injured skeletal muscle [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Research by Forterre et al. [20] demonstrated that muscle‐derived exosomes enhance myogenesis by delivering miRNAs between proliferating myoblasts and maturing myotubes. Furthermore, in vivo studies in a muscle laceration mouse model showed that exosomes from differentiating human skeletal myoblasts can reduce the fibrotic area and increase the number of regenerated myofibers in the injury site, resulting in significant improvement of skeletal muscle regeneration [21].

Based on these observations, we employed an in vitro C2C12 culture system to determine whether short‐term SW stimulation could induce exosome release in myoblasts. Clarifying the extent to which SW influences vesicle biogenesis and trafficking in muscle cells may help advance SW‐based strategies for regenerative medicine and muscle repair.

Experiments were conducted using murine C2C12 myoblasts purchased from the Istituto Zooprofilattico Sperimentale of Lombardia and Emilia Romagna. Upon reaching 70% confluence, cells were exposed to SW using a clinical device (DUOLITH, Storz Medical AG) in a water‐bath setup, as previously described by Holfeld et al. [22]. SW parameters were selected based on our preliminary studies (EFD 0.1 mJ/mm2, 3 Hz, 500 impulses) [23]. Untreated cells served as controls. To exclude any cytotoxic effects following SW stimulation, cell viability and morphology were evaluated at both time points (6 h, 12 h). Cell morphology was observed under the bright‐field microscope, showing intact cell morphology and adherence (Figure S1A). Furthermore, cell viability was measured using the LUNA‐III Automated Cell Counter, which showed no significant differences in cell viability between control and treated cells (Figure S1B). Following stimulation, cells were maintained in medium supplemented with exosome‐depleted FBS to avoid contamination from serum‐derived vesicles. At 6 and 12 h post‐treatment, EVs were isolated from culture media by sequential differential centrifugation, using a Lynx 6000 Superspeed centrifuge equipped with a T29 fixed‐angle rotor (ThermoFisher). The medium was first centrifuged at 2000× g for 10 min at room temperature to remove cells and debris. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 16 000× g for 30 min at 4°C to eliminate large extracellular vesicles. Small extracellular vesicles were pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 100 000× g for 2 h at 4°C. The resulting pellet was washed in 3 mL of PBS, subjected to a second ultracentrifugation step at 100 000× g for 3 h at 4°C to increase purity, and then the EV pellet was resuspended in 300 μL of PBS. EVs were analyzed using the Exoid instrument (Izon Science), which operates through Tunable Resistive Pulse Sensing (TRPS) to measure particle‐by‐particle size and concentration. Measurements were performed using NP150 nanopores, suitable for the dimensional range of small extracellular vesicles (70–300 nm). Before analysis, EV samples were diluted 1:10 in PBS to ensure an optimal particle event rate. For each measurement, data acquisition proceeded until reaching a blockade count of 500 events, ensuring robust statistical accuracy. Quantification of particle concentration was performed using CPC100 calibration particles at a known concentration. Both calibration standards and EV samples were analyzed in triplicate, each measured at three different applied pressures, as required for accurate TRPS‐based concentration calculations. Size and concentration outputs were generated using the Exoid software after applying calibration curves derived from CPC100 standards.

Ultrathin sections of fixed C2C12 cells were examined by plain transmission electron microscopy (TEM). In detail, C2C12 cells were fixed in 2.0% paraformaldehyde/0.1% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M PBS for 90 min at 4°C, scraped, pelleted, and post‐fixed in 1% OsO4 for 2 h at 4°C. Samples were dehydrated through graded ethanols, transitioned in propylene oxide, and embedded in Epon‐Araldite resin (Sigma‐Aldrich) at 60°C for 72 h. Ultrathin sections (90 nm) were cut on an ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems), collected on 200‐mesh copper grids, contrasted with lead citrate and uranyl acetate, and examined using a Jeol JEM SX100 electron microscope.

TRPS measurements of EVs revealed that SW treatment, at the tested parameters, triggered a significant vesicle release compared to controls (p < 0.05) (Figure 1). Quantitative analysis showed a marked increase in vesicle concentration in stimulated cells at 6 h post‐treatment (Figure 1A), whereas no significant differences were detected at 12 h (Figure 1B). In contrast, vesicle size distribution remained unchanged between treated and control groups, with particle diameters consistently falling within the typical exosomal size range (30–150 nm).

TEM observations of ultrathin cellular sections further supported these findings, revealing the occurrence of MVBs in the perimembraneous region (Figure 2A), in close apposition to or fusing with the plasma membrane (Figure 2B,C), suggesting active vesicle trafficking and release from SW‐treated cells (Figure 2D). Moreover, vesicles displaying morphological features and dimensions consistent with exosome‐like EVs were clearly detected in progressive outward budding from the plasma membrane or completely released within the extracellular space, compatible with an active secretory process of myoblasts in response to SW stimulation. Of note, MVBs were identified as membrane‐bound endosomal structures containing multiple intraluminal vesicles (ILVs), typically measuring 200–600 nm in diameter and enclosed within a single limiting membrane. Exosome‐like vesicles were recognized as round, single‐membrane vesicles within the 30–50 nm size range, observed either inside MVBs or in the extracellular space after their release [24, 25]. Furthermore, TEM‐based morphometric assessment of total MVBs showed a significant increase in their number at 6 h post‐treatment compared with control cells (Figure S2).

Representative EM micrographs illustrating the progression of exosome secretion from a C2C12 cell following SW treatment. A multivesicular body (MVB) is observed as a membranous structure in close proximity to the plasma membrane (PM), characterized by a single limiting membrane enclosing exosomes that appear as intraluminal vesicles (ILVs, arrows) (A). MVB migrates and fuses with the plasma membrane (B), leading to the exocytic release of exosomes into the extracellular space (C). The fusion profile between the MVB and the plasma membrane can be observed in proximity to the released exosomes (D). M: mitochondrion. Scale bar: A–D = 175 nm; B–C = 350 nm.

In the current study, we adopted an experimental approach similar to that described by Holfeld et al. [22] to investigate the effects of SW stimulation on exosome release from C2C12 myoblasts and the associated ultrastructural changes in EV trafficking. Using this setting, our data suggest that SW induces a rapid and transient increase in the release of small EVs with sizes compatible with exosome‐like vesicles from myoblasts compared to controls. Notably, the absence of significant differences in vesicle size distribution between SW‐treated and control cells suggests that SW may affect vesicle production and/or release dynamics, rather than altering the overall EV morphology. These observations align with previous evidence indicating that mechanical cues can influence vesicular trafficking and secretory activity in muscle cells, reinforcing the concept that biomechanical stimulation may potentially serve as a cell‐free strategy to influence regenerative processes through the modulation of EV‐mediated intercellular communication [16, 21, 26]. However, further studies are required to establish the role of SW in muscle tissue regeneration.

Ultrastructural TEM analysis, which stands out as the gold standard for high‐resolution EV visualization [27], supported TRPS findings by revealing features compatible with enhanced vesicular trafficking and secretory activity in myoblasts following SW stimulation, as evidenced by the combined presence of peripherally localized MVBs together with EVs displaying morphology and dimensions consistent with exosome‐like vesicles. Remarkably, the transient nature of the observed response may reflect an early adaptive mechanism triggered by SW stimulation, although this interpretation remains preliminary. While these findings provide insights into additional mechanisms through which SW could influence vesicular trafficking, dedicated studies will be required to clarify to which extent these processes contribute to regenerative outcomes in muscle tissue.

Taken together, our data suggest that SW may modulate intracellular vesicle biogenesis and trafficking pathways; however, the precise molecular mechanisms involved remain to be fully elucidated and warrant further investigation.

Some limitations should be noted. Although TRPS and TEM analyses provide valuable information on vesicle size, concentration, and morphology, additional EV‐marker profiling would be required to classify these vesicles as genuine exosomes. Moreover, functional assays are needed to confirm the biological relevance of SW‐induced vesicle release. Finally, all the experiments were performed in vitro, limiting the translation of these findings to in vivo muscle regeneration.

In conclusion, our findings extend previous observations obtained in other cell types to skeletal muscle cells [12, 13, 14], thereby providing further rationale for the clinical application of SW in muscle injuries and other diseases. Moreover, these results contribute to the growing field of SW basic research and highlight the need to further elucidate how SW mechanical stimulation affects membrane remodeling, endosomal trafficking, and vesicle sorting machinery. To address these questions, we are currently designing studies aimed at characterizing the molecular cargoes of these myoblast‐derived vesicles and clarifying their potential role in mediating SW regenerative effects. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms may open new translational perspectives for optimizing regenerative therapies targeting skeletal muscle disorders and injury repair.
