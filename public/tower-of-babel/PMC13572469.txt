Metal oxides-based catalytic pyrolysis of lignocellulosic biomasses for bio-oil development: a comparative assessment on process optimization and characterization
Authors: P. Jennita Jacqueline, V. R. Pranav Raj, S. Vijayatharishan, J. Ranjitha, Nanthagopal Kasianantham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the conventional fixed-bed catalytic pyrolysis using metal oxides (CaO, Fe₂O₃, and TiO₂) to enhance bio-oil yield from three lignocellulosic biomasses: safflower press cake (SPC), coconut press cake (CPC), and coconut shell (CS). The primary characterization, using ultimate, proximate, and compositional analyses, as well as TGA-DTG-DSC, was carried out in the experiment. SPC biomass had a high hemicellulose content (50.8 ± 0.11%), which is suited for bio-oil applications. An initial non-catalytic pyrolysis experiment was conducted at 550 °C with a heating rate of 25 °C/min for 30 min; the bio-oil yields were 34% (SPC), 29% (CPC), and 26% (CS), respectively. During the catalytic pyrolysis, TiO₂ showed its strongest catalytic activity, primarily combined with SPC biomass. Under optimized conditions 550 °C, 25 °C/min heating rate, and a 30-minute reaction time SPC with TiO₂ yielded a maximum bio-oil output of 71.75 ± 0.16%. In contrast, under the same conditions without a catalyst, SPC produced 34 ± 0.16% bio-oil, indicating a significant 37.5% improvement with the addition of TiO₂. Among the three biomasses, SPC showed the greatest responsiveness to catalytic enhancement, while CPC offered a better balance between oil quality and char usability. The GC-MS analysis of TiO₂-catalyzed SPC bio-oil confirmed TiO₂’s selective catalytic influence on bio-oil compound distribution by revealing a predominance of phenolic compounds, such as Guaiacol (19.8%), 2,4-Dimethoxyphenol (31.12%), and hydroxy ketones such as -Hydroxy-2-butanone (29.49%) and 1-Hydroxypropan-2-one (26.36%), confirming the superior Lewis acid catalytic mechanism of TiO₂ for the production of phenolic compounds and ketone – rich bio-oil from lignocellulosic agro-industrial wastes.

Introduction

With rising energy demand and the rapid depletion of fossil fuel reserves, the shift toward sustainable, renewable energy sources has emerged as a global priority. Fossil fuels remain the dominant energy resource; however, their non-renewable nature and finite supply pose long-term challenges1. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), global oil demand grew by 0.8% (830 kb/d) in 2024, reaching 193 EJ. Transport’s share of demand growth has declined to 5% since 2022, while petrochemical feedstocks now account for most of the increase. Oil’s share in total energy demand fell below 30% for the first time, reflecting broader economic and industrial trends2. This increasing reliance on fossil fuels has severe repercussions, particularly for the environment, including greenhouse gas (GHG) and CO2 emissions. In response to these concerns, biomass has emerged as a promising alternative energy source.

Biomass, which includes organic materials such as municipal/industrial waste, aquatic waste, woody biomass, and animal manure, is composed primarily of carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, and other inorganic species3. Lignocellulosic biomass is a plant-based material composed mainly of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. It includes agricultural residues, forest waste, and energy crops, offering a renewable source for biofuel production4. India produces approximately 990 million metric tons of agricultural biomass annually, with surplus biomass availability estimated at around 230 million metric tons per year, a significant portion of which is lignocellulosic5. In agricultural economies like India, biomass remains widely available, with lignocellulosic biomass constituting a substantial fraction. For instance, India ranks as the third-largest producer of coconuts globally, with over 11.2 million tonnes produced annually. This results in approximately 4 million tonnes of lignocellulosic waste, like husks, shells, and coir dust. These residues, with a carbon content of around 45–55%, are thermally stable and can generate bio-oil and biochar6.

Biomass is a plentiful, affordable, renewable, and ecologically sustainable energy source. The two primary methods for producing biofuel from second-generation resources are biochemical and thermochemical. The thermochemical pathways encompass a broad range of processes, such as hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL)7, gasification8, pyrolysis9, and combustion10. Among the various biomass conversion technologies, thermochemical processes such as pyrolysis are gaining considerable attention due to their ability to produce multiple valuable products, including solid biochar, liquid bio-oil, and gaseous fuels11. The pyrolysis process, which involves the thermal degradation of biomass at temperatures ranging from 400 to 600 °C in an inert atmosphere, is particularly attractive due to its efficiency in converting lignocellulosic biomass into bio-oil12,13. Bio-oil produced from biomass pyrolysis typically contains 35–40 wt% oxygen on a dry basis. Bio-oil has several advantages over raw biomass, including higher energy density, favorable storage and transportation properties, and a relatively clean and homogeneous feedstock13. However, its direct application as a conventional fuel is restricted due to its high oxygen content, instability, corrosiveness, and low heating value4. Furthermore, the utilization of biomass as an energy source contributes to climate change mitigation and energy security while promoting waste valorization. In the context of carbon neutrality and the global push toward sustainable energy, the development of bio-based fuels remains a crucial area of research. Considering these factors, integrating pyrolysis technology with agricultural waste management offers a promising approach to generating renewable biofuels while addressing environmental and energy-related challenges2.

Pyrolysis encompasses several types, including slow pyrolysis, fast pyrolysis, flash pyrolysis, and microwave-assisted pyrolysis, each distinguished by its heating rate, residence time, and target product yield14. Among these, conventional pyrolysis has attracted particular attention for its simplicity, low operational cost, and ability to produce relatively high bio-oil yields when optimized with suitable feedstocks15. Siu Hua Chang (2020) investigated rice husk as a potential feedstock for bio-oil production; however, its unfavorable properties, particularly high ash content (15–20%) and low energy density, reduce the bio-oil. These characteristics negatively affect both the yield and quality of bio-oil produced via fast pyrolysis, making rice husk a less desirable option16. Similarly, safflower press cake, a by-product from oil extraction, is often discarded or underutilized despite its high carbon and hydrogen content. It exhibits a high volatile matter content, making it suitable for catalytic pyrolysis to produce bio-oil. Recent agro-waste utilization reports indicate that significant volumes of safflower residue are generated annually by oil mills, especially in regions where safflower is cultivated as a drought-resistant crop; 22,900 metric tonnes were produced in the Indian state of Maharashtra alone in the fiscal year 2022–2317. Harnessing these biomass sources through pyrolysis could provide a sustainable pathway for waste valorization while tapping into their inherent energy potential. Several researchers have explored the use of press cakes in slow catalytic pyrolysis, demonstrating its ability to enhance not just the yield but also the quality of the resulting bio-oil.

Catalytic pyrolysis integrates solid catalysts into the thermal degradation process, promoting deoxygenation, cracking of large molecules, and selective formation of desirable compounds18. Catalysts such as zeolites, clays, and nano-metal oxides have been widely studied, with particular focus on enhancing bio-oil stability and calorific value. Among the different catalyst categories, acid-based metal oxide catalysts have shown superior performance due to their high acidity, thermal resilience, and ability to facilitate secondary cracking reactions19. Jennita Jacqueline et al. (2022) demonstrated that CaO, a basic oxide, significantly enhances both the yield and quality of bio-oil during catalytic co-pyrolysis, raising yields to 51.6% while reducing oxygenates and acidity, highlighting its dual role in promoting hydrocarbon formation and stabilizing pyrolysis vapors20. Likewise, TiO₂ has demonstrated catalytic efficiency in enhancing aromatic hydrocarbon content, while Fe₂O₃ plays a notable role in redox-driven cracking of heavy volatiles21. Almalki et al. (2024) showed that using a green TiO₂/AlSBA-15 nanocomposite catalyst in biomass pyrolysis significantly enhanced bio-oil production, increasing the yield to 67.1%, reducing the oxygen content to 24.5%, and improving the higher heating value to 31.5 MJ/kg, indicating superior fuel quality22. From an economic standpoint, the use of such catalysts in slow pyrolysis remains viable, particularly when the catalysts are locally sourced or recyclable23. Encouragingly, government-backed initiatives and bioenergy missions have begun supporting pilot-scale trials and feasibility studies incorporating catalytic slow pyrolysis for rural biomass valorization, reinforcing the pathway toward sustainable, decentralized bio-oil production from agro-industrial residues.

The present study focused on the pyrolysis of agro-lignocellulosic residues from safflower press cake (SPC), coconut press cake (CPC), and coconut shell (CS) to produce bio-oil. The catalytic pyrolysis of three different metal oxides, such as CaO, Fe₂O₃, and TiO₂, was utilized to produce bio-oil, enabling selective deoxygenation, suppressing char formation, and increasing bio-oil yield via Lewis-acid-catalyzed cracking and redox-driven breakdown pathways24. The previous study (Jacqueline et al., 2021) was limited to a single catalyst, CaO, and microwave pretreatment was not statistically significant at the pilot scale and was not economically viable. The present study focused on high-pressure batch-reactor pretreatment, which offers a more economically viable and scalable alternative to microwave pretreatment. The research gap/novelty addressed in the present study is (i) the unexplored combinations of these biomasses SPC, CPC and CS with metal oxides such as CaO, Fe₂O₃, and TiO₂, to determine the bio-oil production, (ii) the influence of temperature and heating rate, along with TGA- DSC/DTG analysis to identify the mechanism of catalytic pyrolysis to enhance the bio-oil production. The following aims were undertaken in the current endeavour to solve the objectives: (1) The comprehensive physiochemical characterization study on individual biomass using proximate, ultimate, compositional analysis, TGA – DTG/DSC thermal curve analysis was carried out, (ii) the investigation of the influences of three metal oxides with three biomasses on the bio-oil yield and product distribution, (iii) the process parameter of temperature of pyrolysis process to determine the optimized operating conditions, and (iv) Bio-oil composition of GC-MS bio-oil classification and XRD spent catalyst analysis was investigated in this study.

Materials and methods

Feedstocks preparation and pretreatment

In the present study, the lignocellulosic agro-residues of safflower press cake (SPC), coconut press cake (CPC), and coconut shell (CS) were collected in quantities of 3–5 kg from local markets in Vellore, Tamil Nadu, India. The biomass was dried in sunlight for 3 days, followed by controlled oven drying at 60 °C for 24 h. The dried biomass was shredded to a uniform particle size of 0.18 mm using an electric motor to optimize particle-size distribution and mass-transfer efficiency during pyrolysis. The biomass was stored in air-sealed containers, which help to prevent moisture content and can be used for all further experiments.

A systematic pretreatment procedure was developed through extensive trial and error to determine the optimal biomass-to-catalyst ratio to improve pyrolysis performance. In this study, the metal oxide catalysts (CaO, Fe₂O₃, and TiO₂) were commercially purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (India) with a minimum purity of 99% and were utilized without further purification or modification. A biomass-to-catalyst ratio of 21:1 (approximately 4.67%) was adopted throughout the experiment based on preliminary trials. The preliminary stage employed a biomass-to-catalyst ratio of 30:1, as reported in previous studies20 and was optimized to 21:1 of 150:7, based on the optimum bio-oil yield given in the experimental data. Using a high-pressure batch reactor, the biomass-catalyst mixture was heated for 5 min at 105 °C and 10 bar as part of the pretreatment procedure10. This temperature and time combination was chosen to achieve sufficient catalyst activation while avoiding thermal degradation of the biomass structure. This combination of temperature and time was chosen to achieve sufficient catalyst activation while avoiding thermal degradation of the biomass structure25. The pretreatment conditions were closely monitored to ensure uniform activation and consistent heat distribution across all samples.

Instrumental analysis

Ultimate and proximate analysis

The analytical procedures were carried out comprehensively at the analytical facilities of VIT University, Vellore, India, to determine the elemental composition and proximate characteristics of the biomass feedstocks used. Ultimate and proximate analyses were conducted to identify the fundamental chemical composition and thermal characteristics of biomass feedstocks. Proximate analysis, following standard ASTM methods, included measuring the levels of moisture, volatile substances, fixed carbon, and ash. Ultimate Analysis utilized elemental analysis to quantify the levels of carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, sulfur, and oxygen.

Using a DM 143 SC double-pin platinum electrode (Mettler Toledo) mounted on a coulometric titrator, the water content of the bio-oil and biochar samples was determined by coulometric Karl-Fischer titration. Specimens weighing 0.10–0.15 g were placed into a drying oven connected to the titration cell via a dropper. The titration procedure used a 60 s mixing time (from sample insertion to the start of electrolysis), a 900 s electrolysis interval, and a 60 s delay time (from detection of the first endpoint to titration termination). This helps measure water content, ranging from trace levels (0.0001 wt%) to 100 wt%, with high precision.

The PerkinElmer 2400 Series II CHNS/O elemental analyzer was used to conduct a combustion-based ultimate analysis. The elemental composition (elements such as C, H, N, S, and O) of the raw biomasses has been measured through this analyzer. This system operates on the Pregl-Dumas principle by combusting 2–3 mg samples in pure oxygen at high temperature. Gases such as CO₂, H₂O, N₂, and SO₂ are produced and separated via frontal chromatography, then detected by a thermal conductivity detector. H/C and O/C molar ratios were figured using data on C, H, N, S, and O content, and the Dulong equation was applied to estimate higher heating values26.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis was conducted to evaluate the thermal decomposition behavior of the feedstocks under controlled heating conditions. Thermogravimetric analysis and Derivative Thermogravimetry were performed using the TA Instruments SDT Q600 analyzer. It combines TGA and DTG functions in a single instrument that simultaneously operates as a differential thermal analyzer and a thermogravimetric analyzer. The SDT Q600 includes a high-precision microbalance with a furnace assembly containing a quartz sample chamber, which is surrounded by an electric resistance heater within a water-cooled furnace housing. The Platinel II thermocouple is positioned just above the sample pan and has precise temperature control. The TGA experiments were carried out in a nitrogen atmosphere within monitored heating conditions. For each biomass type, approximately 2.25 mg of samples were taken and examined over a temperature range of 30–800 °C. The heating rate was maintained at 20 °C/min, with a continuous supply of nitrogen gas at 100 mL/min to maintain the inert atmosphere required for the analysis.

XRD analysis

X-ray diffraction analysis was conducted to identify the crystal structures and phase compositions of the biomass feedstocks and catalyst materials used. The Bruker D8 Advance (Germany) and Panalytical X’Pert 3 (Netherlands) systems at the Center for Disaster Mitigation and Management (CDMM) in VIT University were used to perform XRD analysis. With its two-circle goniometer, independent stepper motors, and optical encoders, the Bruker D8 Advance offers unmatched accuracy and precision for powder diffraction. The LynxEye and Scintillation Counter detectors enhance signal detection capabilities. The system runs on a Cu Kα radiation source at 40 kV and 30 mA. To conduct the powder diffraction analysis, the samples were first finely powdered, and 1.0 g of each sample was taken. By applying standardized scanning techniques, XRD measurements were performed throughout the angle range of 10–90° (2θ) with a step size of 0.02° and a step time of 1 s. The crystallinity index, which provides a quantitative measure of the relative amounts of organized (crystalline) versus disordered (amorphous) regions in the biomass, was then determined via the Segal technique on these processed patterns27.

Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS)

The chemical composition of pyrolysis oil was analyzed using Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry at the Sophisticated Analytical Instrument Facility (SAIF), IIT Madras, utilizing the Agilent Model 8890 GC System equipped with a Single Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer (5977B MSD). This advanced GC-MS setup, featuring split/split-less injectors and a range of capillary columns (30 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm), enabled high-precision separation and identification of thermally stable volatile compounds. The facility’s system, complemented by an auto liquid sampler and access to the NIST 2017 spectral library, ensured robust compound identification and reproducibility. All chromatographic and spectral data were processed using OpenLab CDS software, and the instrument’s stringent specifications, such as excellent area and retention-time repeatability, provided confidence in the qualitative and quantitative results obtained from complex biomass-derived samples.

Sample preparation for GC-MS analysis involved proper dilution and filtration procedures to ensure accurate identification and quantification of the organic compounds present in the bio-oil. The sample was diluted with 10 mg/mL of dichloromethane (DCM) and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter prior to injection. The PTFE syringe filter was utilized. The 1 µL sample was injected into a capillary column using helium as the carrier gas at 1.2 mL/min in split mode at a 5:1 ratio. The analytical methodology enabled comprehensive characterization of the pyrolysis products, providing essential data for product quality assessment and process optimization.

Experimental setup and procedure

Based on the procedures reported by Jennita Jacqueline et al. (2022), the experimental methodology was established using a conventional fixed-bed catalytic pyrolysis system20. The uniform mixture of biomass to catalyst was prepared and introduced into a fixed-bed quartz reactor (internal diameter: 50 mm; length: 400 mm) as a pyrolysis reactor. A type-K thermocouple (Omega Engineering) was installed in the reactor chamber, along with a PID controller (Eurotherm 3216). The thermocouple ensures accurate thermal control, while the PID controller executes a predefined heating program. The system, initially held at room temperature, was heated at 20 °C/min to 550 °C, followed by a 30-minute isothermal hold. The experimental setup for the fixed-bed catalytic pyrolysis process is shown in Fig. 1. To maintain an inert environment, nitrogen gas was fed at a flow rate of 120 mL/min throughout the experiment, regulated using a mass flow controller (Model 5850E, Brooks Instrument). The pyrolysis vapors were guided through a dual-stage condensation system. The first stage had a 5-meter-long coiled stainless-steel condenser, which was water-cooled and used to collect the heavier bio-oil fractions. The second stage consists of a cold trap, in which a dry ice–ethanol mixture is used to condense the lighter volatile compounds. The remaining non-condensable gases were collected in a 5-liter Tedlar gas sampling bag for subsequent analysis via gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS).

The reactor pressure was maintained at atmospheric pressure (1 atm) during each trial. The bio-oil yields were measured gravimetrically after solvent-aided extraction from the condenser’s surface, while the residual solid char immediately after cooling was weighed28. To ensure that the collected data was accurate and the results were reproducible, the experimental setup was conducted with precise temperature control, residence-time management, and product-collection systems. Across trials, all experimental parameters were kept constant to ensure reliable results, in accordance with established protocols for biomass pyrolysis experiments24.

Results and discussion

Lignocellulosic biomass analysis

In the current study, the lignocellulosic biomass samples of safflower press cake (SPC), coconut press cake (CPC), and coconut shell (CS) were characterized by proximate and ultimate compositional analyses and higher heating value (HHV), as shown in Table 1. The current study measured the moisture content of SPC (13.4%), CPC (12.4%), and CS (11.1%) after sun drying, prior to pretreatment experiments. It has been determined that biomass’s inherent moisture characteristics imitate their natural hygroscopic properties, which may improve the HHV content and combustion rate29,30. It is an acceptable range for catalytic pyrolysis and confirming the influences product distribution and bio-oil quality. The proximate analysis showed that the three biomass samples had moisture content, volatile matter, fixed carbon, and ash content ranging from 10 to 13%, 55–75%, 11–25%, and 3–6% by weight, respectively. Biomass with higher ash content absorbs heat that would otherwise be used to burn fuel. The presence of less ash and more volatile materials during pyrolysis indicates higher fuel ignition. This reduces the energy available for conversion, thereby lowering the fuel’s total heating value and combustion efficiency. Even high ash content is undesirable because it hinders the gasification and combustion processes rather than promoting energy production31. The current study’s biomass contains SPC (6.5%), CPC (3.1%), and CS (3.2%), thereby increasing combustion efficiency and improving the quality of condensed bio-oil. Even in pyrolysis, an ash content of 5–10% is an acceptable limit for thermochemical conversion and confirms the suitability of the three biomasses for bio-oil production32,33.

Physicochemical compositional analysis of biomass.

The elevated volatile matter characteristics of the three biomasses, which are essential for maximizing the formation of liquid products during pyrolysis, are confirmed by the volatile matter content of SPC (57%), CPC (66.9%), and CS (74.5%). It increases bio-oil production by enhancing devolatilization during thermal degradation and releasing condensable volatiles during pyrolysis34. Since bio-oil tends to rely on volatile matter, biomass with a higher volatile matter content is better suited for pyrolysis processes35. Compared with safflower press cake, coconut press cake, and shell contain more volatile substances36. The biomass’s higher heating value of 20–30 MJ/kg, combined with its high oxygen content, suggests that it could be an adequate feedstock for catalytic reforming to produce bio-oil using an alcohol-and-oxygenated-aryl conversion approach37. Table 1 shows the results of the chemical composition analysis of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. The primary components of cellulose and hemicellulose are found in the range of 17–32 wt% and 48–56 wt%, respectively. The current study, with its high cellulose and hemicellulose content, is suitable for bio-oil production. High bio-oil yields require a high hemicellulose content, which is more volatile than cellulose, a commonly used energy source29. The higher bio-oil yield from SPC is attributed to its high hemicellulose content (50.8%), as hemicellulose readily depolymerizes into volatile compounds during pyrolysis. In contrast, CS with the highest lignin content (26.28%) produced a lesser bio-oil yield, consistent with lignin’s tendency to favor biochar formation during thermal degradation. A high lignin content typically results in high biochar production. Because a higher lignin percentage often results in greater biochar output, the hemicellulose has a higher lignin content31.

Carthamus tinctorius seeds are used to extract oil, and the resulting SPC contains low levels of lignin (17–20 wt%), cellulose (30–35 wt%), and residual proteins. When pyrolyzed, the lignin fraction, characterized by complex aromatic structures, helps produce phenolic compounds. CPC and CS, formed from the residual coconut flesh after oil extraction, are rich in cellulose (~ 35–40 wt%), hemicellulose (~ 25–30 wt%), and lignin content (~ 20 wt%)38. A choice of biomasses is the balanced mixture of holocellulose (cellulose + hemicellulose), which is provided by the SPC, CPC, and CS, during thermal breakdown for the formation of light oxygenates and furans35.

Thermal behavior of biomass matrix

As seen in Fig. 2(i and ii), the thermal breakdown of weight loss characteristics is represented by TGA/DTG and DSC curves as a function of temperature for the four stages provided in the biomass samples29. Peak displacement can be ascribed to heat transfer and biomass degradation at varying heating rates. As the rate of heating increases, the four stages, the drying stage’s thermal breakdown, the pyrolysis’s active stage (which involves the degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose), and the pyrolysis stage’s extractive stage gradually rise to the peak. In contrast, the degradation stage drops to the residue38. The DTG and DSC results were obtained at a heating rate of 20℃/min. The removal of moisture content indicates that the first stage of water loss takes place at the three biomass temperatures of SPC (113.2 °C), CPC (108 °C), and CS (106.2 °C), respectively (Fig. 2(i)).

In thermogravimetric analysis, lignocellulosic biomass containing bound and free water exhibits reduced binding and vaporizes during the initial heating phase39. The DTG curve’s weaker peak indicates bound free water and less volatile molecules in the biomass38. For the three biomass samples, the initial mass loss rates are 3.73%, 3.16%, and 4.52%, respectively. The DSC curve shows the second zone of the active pyrolysis stage at 20℃/min for three biomasses: SPC (330℃), CPC (346.9℃), and CS (355.8℃).

The peaks in the TGA-DTG and DSC curves were primarily observed between 270 and 600 °C, corresponding to the temperature range of the biomass during the active pyrolysis phase. When comparing the TG curves of the three biomasses (SPC, CPC, and CS), it was found that CPC and CS were either marginally or precisely in the same temperature range as SPC. The DSC curve has also shown a similar tendency20. The second zone of biomass samples consumed during the active pyrolysis stage showed mass losses of 53.81%, 29.55%, and 25.49% for SPC, CPC, and CS, respectively. At the same heating rate, thermal degradation increases the temperature range to 350–585 °C. As shown in Fig. 2(ii), the maximum mass loss rate at SPC biomass is near 373.9℃, and it occurs at close to the maximum fluidity temperature, with a mass loss rate of 63.02%, respectively. The raw sample of thermal stability stimulates mass loss ratio, implying that increased heating value has an inhibiting effect on decomposition during the third zone of high amounts of extractives40. The last zone of residual loss corresponds to the explicit devolatilization of lignin decomposition at 796.5℃, driven by hydroxyl phenolic groups that improve lignin’s thermal resilience, as confirmed in the DSC curve analysis. There was no further weight loss in the passive zone, but carbon and ash were present in solid form (inorganic residue).

The TGA data were used to calculate the thermal heating range of activation energy, and the projected Ea ranges from 75.95 to 84.91 kJ/mol using the Arrhenius equation, indicating whether a catalyst is required for pyrolysis. It serves as a baseline description of the heating rate 20℃/min for SPC, CPC, and CS biomasses. The relatively high activation energy of raw biomass feedstocks indicates that substantial thermal energy is required to initiate and sustain pyrolysis. This implies that a catalyst is required to reduce the energy barrier and improve the efficiency of thermochemical conversion. Metal oxide catalysts (CaO, Fe₂O₃, and TiO₂) were strategically employed in the present study to reduce activation energy, facilitate selective cracking and deoxygenation, and significantly improve bio-oil yield and quality of biomass feedstocks41,42. It may be concluded that the extractives’ thermal degradation activation energy is comparatively lower than that of the other SPC constituents43. Overall, the activation energy was in the following descending order: coconut shell < coconut press cake < safflower press cake.

Catalytic pyrolysis of biomass with metal oxides

Using metal catalysts CaO, Fe₂O₃, and TiO₂, the pyrolysis experiment was conducted on individual biomass samples of SPC, CPC, and CS to determine the highest bio-oil yield. The pyrolysis results in oil rate are primarily determined by heating rate and temperature, biomass characterization and intrinsic properties. The initial experiment used a primary temperature of 550℃, a heating rate of 25℃/min, and a 30-minute run without a catalyst to determine the bio-oil’s initial concentration. The bio-oil yields were SPC (34%), CPC (29%), and CS (26%), and the biochar yields were SPC (27%), CPC (33%), and CS (32%), respectively (Fig. 3(i)). The non-catalytic bio-oil yields serve as a baseline for catalytic pyrolysis experiments using CaO, Fe₂O₃, and TiO₂ catalysts. It revealed that the individual biomass bio-oil yield depends on the raw biomass’s inherent characteristics and is also influenced by its oxygen content. This is shown in the ultimate analysis of biomass in Table 1. In contrast to bio-oils derived from three biomass sources, SPC bio-oils had a lower concentration of aromatic hydrocarbons and a higher concentration of polar organic molecules and aromatic rings44. CS contains around 36.65% extractives, with cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin serving as its primary structural components. Bio-oil from CPC and CS had a higher 30% concentration due to the biomass’s higher oxygen content, which may improve energy density and compatibility during pyrolysis38. Three catalysts were used in the pyrolysis studies to enhance bio-oil yield (Table 2). During pyrolysis, the choice of metal oxide catalysts in this research, such as CaO (basic)45,46, Fe2O3 (redox-active)47, and TiO₂ (amphoteric, high surface area)48,49, enables selective cracking, deoxygenation, and aromatization. These catalysts improve thermal degradation pathways while also reducing the levels of acids and oxygenates in the bio-oil, thereby increasing its stability and calorific value. The synergistic relationship between lignocellulosic content and catalyst type determines conversion efficiency and product selectivity. The effects of catalyst loading, catalytic mechanisms, and greater yield are described in detail below.

Pyrolysis of CaO catalyst

Metal oxides have become prevalent in catalytic pyrolysis due to their simplicity and affordability. Additionally, it was found that metal oxides were effective in removing oxygen. CaO, a typical basic oxide, has been studied as a catalyst for bio-oil enhancement due to its accessibility and environmental neutrality50. The CaO catalyst’s basicity and cracking capabilities enable the catalytic conversion of biomass components into hydrocarbons through neutralization and catalyst-mediated cracking of the pyrolytic products51. The yield data from the biomass reaction with the CaO catalyst are shown in Fig. 3 (ii) and indicate the product distribution of bio-oil, biochar, residue/ash, and water. Under CaO catalysis, SPC produced the highest bio-oil production of 52.75% among the three feedstocks, followed by CPC (44.67%) and CS (39.33%). There was no statistically significant difference in the bio-oil production between CPC and CS, suggesting that these two feedstocks have similar catalytic performance. Additionally, the CaO catalyst improved the overall fuel quality of the bio-oil produced by increasing ketone selectivity and reducing its acid content52. Similarly, Vichaphund et al. (2017) examined Jatropha residue using a CaO metal-oxide catalyst, which eliminated corrosive carboxylic acids and increased hydrocarbon production to 37.3%. It also showed that the formation of Fe/CaO and Ni/CaO decreased aldehyde levels compared to a CaO ratio of 1:5, increasing hydrocarbon production to 47.5%45. In comparison with the present study, base-catalyzed cracking increased hydrocarbon production to 52.57%. The chemical adsorption of CaO on water and the potential interaction between water and bio-oil particles reduced the yield of pyrolysis water from 8% to 6%. Even Zhang et al. (2014) reported that the CaO catalyst reduced oxygenated compounds, including aldehydes and ketones, and demonstrated that Fe(III)/CaO eliminated acids during fast pyrolysis46.

Meanwhile, the SPC biomass promoted the breakdown of oxygen-containing components such as carbonyl and phenolic compounds, resulting in a 29.6% increase in biochar yield. Nevertheless, the pyrolysis biochar decreased by 3% when non-catalytic biomass components were compared with catalytic CS, CPC, and SPC biomass. It is evident that CaO ensnared the reforming reactor of the aldol condensation of linear carbonyl compounds and the ketonization of acids50. The water vapor absorbed and used by CaO during pyrolysis was further stimulated by the reforming action, which was fully released at 500℃, producing high-quality bio-oil. Concurrently, CS and CPC biomass influenced the CaO catalyst system during pyrolysis, resulting in a final residue of 15–18%, respectively. CPC is appropriate for catalytic upgrading due to its homogeneous particle size and fibrous structure, which provide advantageous thermal sensitivity and biomass-catalyst contact. TiO2 and Fe2O3 were used as catalysts in the pyrolysis of three biomass sources to improve bio-oil quality and reduce alkadiene content.

Pyrolysis of Fe2O3 catalyst

The catalytic pyrolysis of SPC, CPC, and CS biomass using Fe2O3 as a catalyst to produce bio-oil compared to TiO2 and CaO catalysts. When the redox-driven Fe2O3 catalyst was added, the bio-oil output increased drastically by 34–47% (Fig. 3(iii)). This is a large-scale distribution of bio-oil from the pyrolysis of CPC, SPC, and CS biomass at 550 °C with a heating rate of 25 °C/min. The bio-oil yield is unaffected by the reaction temperature when it is raised to 510–570 °C. According to Huang et al. (2018), during Fe-embedded sewage sludge pyrolysis, the bio-oil yield rises from 32.54 wt% to 38.74 wt%, while bio-char falls from 58.77 wt% to 49.64 wt% in a fixed-bed reactor with an Fe2O3 catalyst added at 5% relative to the dried sludge47. In comparison, the present study’s Fe2O3 catalyst is deactivated, and residue forms as the temperature rises because the high bonding energy in the char residue begins to break down, as a consistent redox mechanism confirms. Even though the activation energy decreased when a catalyst was added, residue formation (18%) was higher during the decline phase than with other catalysts because Fe2O3 exhibited a larger reduction in the breakdown of oxygen-containing functional groups, which occurs more readily8. Consequently, the biochar yielded SPC (31%), CPC (29%), and CS (33%). The proportions of bio-oil and biochar production are nearly equal. Then, Li et al. (2023) showed that during catalytic pyrolysis, the Fe2O3/Al2O3 catalyst enhanced hydrocarbon production via a redox-active mechanism53.

The catalyst produced with three biomasses exhibits a greater binding energy, which may indicate a higher surface concentration of carbon and iron in the biomass54. The primary breakdown of cellulose and the secondary breakdown of biochar both contribute to the increase in biochar with rising pyrolysis temperature. The composition of bio-oil remains complex, as the liquid products (bio-oil) produced by pyrolysis of SPC and CPC, after processing, primarily contain aliphatic hydrocarbons with trace amounts of aromatic hydrocarbons and phenols.

As the reaction is faster, the char readily becomes residue. It’s interesting to note that the assumption of a higher CO2 yield suggests decarboxylation as the primary deoxygenation pathway. This pathway is chosen because it eliminates a higher O content while causing less loss of C atoms44,55. Catalytic thermal degradation of biomass promotes char breakdown, increasing liquid yield, whereas the press cake with an Fe2O3 catalyst decreases liquid yield and is accompanied by a parallel increase in biochar. It is observed that the water content of SPC, CPC, and CS around (10–15%) produced the catalytic decomposition of redox- driven catalyst, with oxidation of adsorbed molecules by -OH species, the water content produced moderately56. Metallic iron is less reactive in the breakdown of the press cake than the CaO catalyst.

Pyrolysis of TiO2 catalyst

In a fixed-bed pyrolysis reactor, the catalytic behavior of TiO2 metal oxide with three biomasses (CPC, SPC, and CS) was examined at 550 °C and a heating rate of 25 °C/min for a 30-minute reaction. A large amount of hydroxyphenols was produced, generating significant organic bio-oil and aliphatic hydrocarbons; hence, their removal reduced the bio-oil yield. As a result of increased coke formation and decreased H2 availability, mainly C was converted to an undesired product55. The potential of the dehydration reaction to produce bio-oil yields from three biomasses of SPC (71.75%), CPC (63%), and CS (42%), corresponding to the biomass transformation rate of the TiO2 catalyst during the pyrolysis process (Fig. 3(iv)). After pyrolysis vapor condensation, SPC collected approximately 81% of the total condensate, yielding 71.75% bio-oil after separating the aqueous phase (~ 10% water). It is widely understood that the catalyst’s acidity determines the outcome of a dehydration mechanism. It has been observed that the number of Lewis acid sites on the catalyst can be increased by adding TiO2 to the support, thereby improving the effectiveness of catalytic pyrolysis57. According to Dong et al. (2019), pyrolysis of maize straw to phenol-rich oil was catalyzed by TiO2, which improved volatile repolymerization and stimulated light-gas development, thereby boosting phenol synthesis. In a fixed-bed reactor, guaiacol selectivity increased as TiO2 reacted with biomass, increasing total phenolic compounds by up to 86%, yielding 23.64 mg/g of maize straw48. Similarly, Lu et al. (2010) increased the hydrocarbon yield by up to 25.6% using fast pyrolysis with a TiO2-based catalyst. It also encouraged the cracking of non-volatile oligomers into monomeric, volatile molecules, thereby increasing the yield of volatile products49.

In the same way, the present study shows the TiO2 peak area as a function of biomass and identifies molecules that fall into four groups: hydrocarbons, phenols, ketones, and acids. It exhibits diols, carboxylic acids, and aliphatic and aromatic hydrocarbons. The output of aromatic hydrocarbons increased by 27% and aliphatic hydrocarbons by 4% and 24%, respectively. On the TiO2 catalyst, decarboxylation is a side reaction that occurs at other active sites. The fact that only the TiO2 catalyst produces the pentadecane from the side reaction lends credence to this. More effectively, deoxygenation was coupled with biomass, as the bio-oil derived from the biomass had a significant oxygen content, which constrained the production of water (> 10%). As a result, the TiO2-catalytic pyrolysis of CPC, SPC, and SP biomass that yields biochar has a yield that is comparable to or lower than that of the other two catalysts of Fe2O3 and CaO (> 25%). In either case, the CPC and SPC produced larger quantities of bio-oil because the carbon content of the press cakes was higher in the safflower (68.76%) and coconut (64.23%) press cakes. Cellulose and hemicellulose have a major impact on bio-oil yield during pyrolysis. Compared with other catalysts, the metal oxide TiO2 hindered the formation of oxime and promoted the formation of aromatic compounds in variable quantities. This behavior implies that phenols can undergo demethoxylation and deoxygenation via synergistic interactions with the TiO2 catalyst, converting them to deoxy aromatics58.

Optimization of pyrolysis temperature study

In this experiment, the effect of temperature on the yield and composition of bio-oil from different biomass types, using a TiO2 catalyst, was examined at a constant heating rate. The TiO2 catalyst has yielded the best results among the aforementioned metal oxides; thus, an optimization study of the effects of various temperatures was conducted (Fig. 4(i-iii)). Based on bio-oil output and product composition, three biomass samples treated with a TiO2 catalyst were categorized and examined for temperature effects. As shown in Fig. 4(i), the bio-oil yield from SPC pyrolysis increased gradually with temperature, then slightly decreased after 560 °C. Using TGA data, bio-oil was collected from three biomass samples, with yields ranging from 41% to 71.75% over the temperature range of 500–575 °C. Among these, press cake pyrolysis oil has a higher carbon content, accounting for 65–71% of the bio-oil. As the temperature rose, the volatile matter released from the press cake was transformed into lower-carbon bio-oil59. The press cake decreased with increasing temperature due to thermal decomposition of the biomass, which transformed it into lightweight bio-oil. The yield was enhanced at low temperatures (530–540 °C) without disruption of surface functional groups (H and O) or aliphatic fractions.

The influence of temperature on the three biomasses, with higher yields at SPC (550℃), CPC (530℃), and CS (560℃), respectively, is due to their natural hygroscopicity and inherent properties. TiO2 reacts with species to reduce deoxygenation, but carbon and volatile content remain the main priorities for bio-oil production. In catalytic pyrolysis, the presence of a TiO2 catalyst is beneficial for mass reduction in CPC and SPC because it promotes biomass cracking via dehydration. Since combustion efficiency enhances the interaction between biomass and catalyst during thermal dehydration, a modest increase in the heating rate to 10–25 °C/min may be necessary to achieve low water content in the resulting preferred condensed bio-oil. The TiO₂-catalyzed bio-oils’ Higher Heating Value (HHV) was found to be SPC (27.5 MJ/kg), CPC (24.3 MJ/kg), and CS (25.6 MJ/kg), indicating acceptable energy content and their potential as a feasible biomass. For comparison, the temperature-evolved ratios of pyrolysis products derived from SPC, CPC, and CS feedstock differed. It displays the proportions of phenols, ketones, diols, and aromatic hydrocarbons. Compared to other components, the ratio of methoxy (m/z = 109) corresponds to Phenol, 2-methoxy – (Guaiacol), and benzoyl (m/z = 79) corresponds to phenolic ring derivatives (2,4-Dimethoxyphenol) identified in the GC-MS analysis24. The two processes by which benzoyl and methoxy are formed are the primary cracking of biomass structure, heating rate at the beginning of pyrolysis, and the breakdown of persistent volatile compounds in the residue at higher temperatures. The evolution of phenol ratios changes with the addition of TiO261. A low-temperature, biomass bed is formed into an elevated temperature carbon material bed during the solidification process, and the occurrence of molecular replenishing in the carbon framework during the softening process and the incorporation of liquid mass during the thermolysis stage are the chemical changes that accompany the physicochemical modification of biomass structure phenomenon62. These cleaving bonds are reduced when the TiO2 catalyst is included during the pyrolysis stage, thereby lowering the number of aliphatic and aromatic free radicals. Conversely, the cleavage bond forms during the biomass heating stage via TiO2 pyrolysis, thereby increasing the length of the aliphatic chain, aromatic interfacial spacing, and hydrocarbon-producing activity of the carbon-lattice bio-oil.

Bio-oil composition analysis

Figure 5 displays the results of an investigation into the XRD spectra of spent TiO2 catalyst after pyrolysis of three biomasses. TiO2 nanoparticles exhibited broad peaks at 2θ = 21.8° and 25.3°, confirming the anatase phase and indicating the stability of the spent catalyst’s crystalline phase after the reaction. At 2 Theta values of 48.1˚, 54.0˚, and 62.7˚, three diffraction peaks of TiO2 were detected; these peaks are significant because they indicate a phase change from anatase to rutile as the calcination temperature increases. Major changes in the biomass product were noted with the TiO2 catalyst. For example, the characteristic diffraction peaks of SPC, CPC, and CS were found at 2θ = (25.3, 37.9, 48.1, 54.0, 55.1, and 62.74), which correspond to the reflection planes (101), (112), (200), (105), (211), and (204). The main peaks of 2θ = (21.8 and 38.4) corresponded to the planes of (002) and (001), respectively, and the rutile phase was discovered after integrating the spent catalyst of TiO2. The crystalline phase stability of the spent TiO₂ catalyst was evaluated by comparing its XRD pattern (Fig. 5) with the standard reference data, indicating that the anatase phase remained stable after the reaction. It correlates with TiO2, implying that, as the anatase phase is unstable in air, oxygen may oxidize a portion of it during pyrolysis63. All diffraction angles were accurately indexed to the anatase and rutile structures, consistent with JCPDS nos. 21–1276 and 21–1272, respectively. During pyrolysis depolymerization, the crystallinity indices were calculated as SPC (58.5%), CPC (42.1%), and CS (36.4%). Furthermore, GC analysis indicates that TiO2 exhibits strong catalytic activity during pyrolysis. Catalytic pyrolysis provides low-intensity peaks that will aid in the transformation of heavy bio-oil. The peaks of the XRD patterns are consistent with earlier observations in the literature and confirm the presence of pyrolyzed anatase-phase TiO2 nanoparticles43.

Future perspectives and limitations

The encouraging findings of this study highlight several key avenues for advancing catalytic pyrolysis of lignocellulosic biomass into a sustainable, scalable method for producing bio-oil. A shift from batch laboratory experiments to continuous pilot- or full-scale operations is necessary to achieve industrial viability. This necessitates the creation of scalable reactor systems with effective vapor recovery, thermal control, and catalyst-handling systems that preserve product uniformity as feedstock inputs vary. Continuous-feed system integration has the potential to greatly improve operational efficiency and lower energy losses, especially in microwave-assisted pyrolysis. Additionally, innovations in catalyst design remain a crucial area of focus. Advanced materials such as doped metal oxides, biogenic-supported catalysts, and metal-functionalized porous structures that offer enhanced deoxygenation, coke resistance, and hydrocarbon selectivity can be explored, even though CaO, Fe₂O₃, and TiO₂ have proven effective. Furthermore, over several cycles, these catalysts must exhibit resistance to deactivation processes such as fouling and sintering.

Crude bio-oil can be converted into transport-grade fuels using downstream upgrading techniques such as steam reforming and hydrodeoxygenation. At the same time, there is a revolutionary potential in using machine learning models for process optimization and real-time prediction. AI-driven systems can dynamically modify pyrolysis parameters to increase yield, energy efficiency, and process stability by utilizing reaction kinetics and experimental data. These developments have the potential to facilitate sustainable fuel production and encourage the use of biomass in rural areas when incorporated into a decentralized biorefinery model. Notwithstanding the positive results, several limitations need to be noted.

The complexity of industrial-scale continuous systems, where control over parameters such as residence time, catalyst regeneration, and feedstock consistency is crucial, is not adequately captured by the batch-mode setup used in this study. Even though catalysis improved the bio-oil, it still contains a high level of oxygenates, which make it unstable, reduce its calorific value, and necessitate additional upgrading steps, thereby raising processing and infrastructure costs. Furthermore, the catalysts’ long-term stability, recyclability, and regeneration potential were not evaluated, which may affect their economic viability in continuous operations. Although SPC, CPC, and CS are regionally abundant, the study examines only a small number of biomass types; for broader applicability, additional validation across a wider range of biomass types is required. When designing scalable systems, seasonal supply chains and feedstock variability must also be taken into account. Finally, a thorough techno-economic and environmental analysis is necessary to bolster the feasibility of the suggested pyrolysis pathway. For the process to be both economically competitive and environmentally sustainable, future research must assess metrics such as life-cycle emissions, net energy gain, catalyst cost, and integration with current energy systems.

Conclusion

The study focused on the thermochemical conversion of decomposition of lignocellulosic biomass via pyrolysis of highly lignified biomass to produce bio-oil, making it a waste-to-energy conversion strategy. In this investigation, metal oxides (CaO, Fe₂O₃, and TiO₂) were used as catalysts to produce bio-oil via pyrolysis of SPC, CPC, and CS biomass at 500–575℃. In the conventional fixed-bed catalytic pyrolysis process under the optimized conditions of 550–560 °C, 25 °C/min heating rate, and a 30-minute reaction time, the highest bio-oil yield was SPC (71.75%), CPC (66%), and CS (50%) in the presence of the TiO2 catalyst, respectively. X-Ray Diffraction confirmed TiO₂’s structural stability post-reaction, 2θ = (21.8 and 38.4). The crystallinity index was calculated to be SPC (58.5%), CPC (42.1%), and CS (36.4%), respectively. The peak temperature at which the main products form is centered at 545℃, with the following order: ketones > phenols > hydroxy ketones > furans. The study indicates that catalytic pyrolysis technology has the potential to produce high-yield bio-oil, which, with further upgrading, might serve as a sustainable and economically feasible feedstock for future blending with vehicle fuels.
