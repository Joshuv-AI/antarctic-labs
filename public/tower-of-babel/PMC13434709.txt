The clinical utility of carrier screening
Authors: Mia J. Gruzin, Leslie Burnett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carrier screening aims to identify couples at risk of having children with serious genetic disorders. However, screening panels vary markedly in size, gene content and price, with little guidance to assess clinical utility or value. We analysed 89 carrier screening panels from 30 global providers, modelling test performance using pathogenic variants from gnomAD v4.1.0 and ClinVar across ten ancestry groups and two synthetic populations (representing the United States and Australia). We evaluated how panel size, content, price and clinical utility interrelate. We also compared value per dollar spent to guide clinical decision-making and fair pricing. Clinical utility showed no consistent relationship with panel size or price. Instead, mid-sized, pan-ancestry “Goldilocks” panels delivered the greatest utility, outperforming both smaller and larger panels, while delivering more equitable outcomes. We developed a visual “clinical utility meter” to compare relative test performance and an “efficiency frontier” framework to identify the best value tests for a given price. This data-driven framework and visual tool can support rational test design and selection for value-based laboratory medicine.

Introduction

Carrier screening (CS) aims to identify couples planning or undergoing pregnancy who are at risk of having children with severe autosomal recessive (AR) or X-linked (XL) genetic conditions. Advances in next-generation sequencing have transformed CS from limited, ancestry-specific programmes (such as Tay-Sachs disease in Ashkenazi Jewish populations)1 to broad multi-gene pan-ancestry panels2–7. Some countries have now included CS within national screening programmes8.

Professional organisations, including the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG), American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), Canadian College of Medical Geneticists (CCMG), European Society of Human Genetics (ESHG), Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RANZCOG), Royal College of Pathologists of Australasia (RCPA) and the Human Genetics Society of Australasia (HGSA), endorse CS for its ability to inform reproductive decision-making and reduce disease burden. However, their recommendations vary markedly9–16, and there are no universal criteria for carrier panel design. As a result, commercial panels diverge widely: panel sizes span three orders of magnitude, prices vary widely, and transparency around gene inclusion is limited17,18. Large panels are often marketed as maximally inclusive, and clinicians and patients may assume that larger panels and “more genes” equates to greater benefit.

A recent development is that of “Goldilocks” panels, which consist of gene lists ranked in ancestry-agnostic order based on at-risk couple yield at defined clinically-relevant decision thresholds; this emerging evidence suggests that utility depends more on which genes are included, rather than the sheer number of genes in the panel19.

Given that the primary goal of CS is to inform at-risk couples, its clinical utility lies in its capacity to accurately quantify reproductive risk information. For AR conditions, risk arises when both reproductive partners are heterozygous “carriers” of pathogenic variants in the same gene (25% chance of affected offspring); for XL conditions, when the XX partner is a carrier (50% chance of affected XY offspring). Because each disorder being tested maps to specific causative genes, disease incidence can be estimated from population-level frequencies of pathogenic variants, assuming the penetrance of such variants. This relationship enables test sensitivity to be derived directly from the test itself.

CS also provides an opportunity to quantify value, by balancing clinical utility against cost20,21. Although economic evaluations suggest that expanded CS is broadly cost-effective, they do not specify which CS test delivers greatest value in practice22–25. No previous study has comprehensively evaluated how such panels perform across ancestry groups, nor how these differences translate into value.

Here, we model the yield of 89 commercially available CS panels using population-level allele frequency data (gnomAD v4.1.0) and curated pathogenic variants (ClinVar) across 10 diverse ancestries and two synthetic populations. We then develop intuitive visual tools and a value-based framework to guide test selection that maximises clinical utility, incorporates equity, and aligns price with performance. Carrier screening thus provides a paradigm for quantifying value in genomic pathology and laboratory medicine.

Results

Gene content and overlap

We identified 89 panels from 30 international CS providers (27 commercial laboratories and three hospital-based services), covering 2632 unique genes (Tables S1 and S7). Panel sizes ranged from 2 to 2360 genes (mean, 325; mode, 3). Price data were available for 83 panels from 27 providers, with a mean cost per couple of US Dollars (USD) $1,339 (range, $398– $5,443).

Panel composition varied markedly worldwide. Only one gene (CFTR) appeared in every provider’s largest panel; one third of genes (34.2%; 899) were present in only one or two panels, and nearly half (48.8%; 1283) in six or fewer panels (Table S2).

Selecting the best value

Discussion

Healthcare delivery is increasingly shifting from volume-based to value-based models, in which value is defined as health outcomes achieved per dollar spent. In laboratory medicine, value is often equated with analytical performance, but its downstream contribution—how testing informs clinical decisions and improves outcomes—is rarely measured directly27–31.

Perceptions of value also vary by stakeholder: patients may prioritise comprehensive testing; payers cost-effectiveness; providers, revenue; and public health programmes, population equity and disease prevention. Universal health systems, such as those in Europe and Australia, emphasise equitable access and cost-effectiveness, whereas market-driven systems, such as those of the United States, embed value within fragmented insurance frameworks, often generating inefficiency and inequity. A comparison of ten high-income countries ranked the United States lowest in outcomes, preventable mortality, access, and equity, despite the highest per-capita healthcare expenditure32.

CS highlights these challenges and provides an opportunity to address them. In this study, we have conducted the most comprehensive comparative analysis to date of available reproductive CS panels, evaluating more than 80 panels from 30 providers worldwide, across ten gnomAD ancestry groups and two admixed synthetic populations (United States and Australia). We found that neither the number of genes (panel size) nor price reliably predicts clinical utility—whether measured as the proportion of reproductive risk detected (positive yield) or excluded (NPV). Larger or more expensive panels did not necessarily improve clinical utility, and in several instances, the highest-priced tests underperformed relative to less costly alternatives, underscoring that price is a poor proxy for performance. Instead, utility is determined primarily by which genes were included, challenging the assumption that simply adding additional genes will improve panel performance, and emphasising the need for evidence-based and transparent panel design criteria.

Another major finding was the substantial variation in performance of panels across ancestries. While some panels performed consistently across ancestries, others varied markedly (Fig. 2 and Tables S3 and S4), demonstrating that differences in gene inclusion and variant representation can directly lead to inequitable outcomes. Higher positive yields among individuals of European ancestry reflect a well-recognised systemic bias in genomic reference databases, which remain disproportionately weighted towards European-ancestry derived data and underrepresent many non-European populations33. Expanding representation of under-represented and diverse populations in genomic databases is essential to achieve equity in genomic testing. Notably, mid-sized ancestry-agnostic “Goldilocks” panels19 consistently outperformed both smaller targeted and very large panels, delivering both high utility and ancestry-equitable performance across all populations studied, suggesting a promising panel design strategy.

Only one larger panel [5a; 1704 genes; $1,742] outperformed the mid-sized Goldilocks panels in positive yield, owing to its near-total inclusion of the 1350 genes in our model. However, this marginal gain did not translate into a meaningfully higher NPV (both ~99.99%, to four significant figures) and, because of its size and price, this panel did not fall within the top tier of best-value test (Fig. 4). Beyond a certain point, continuing expansion of panel size adds cost without meaningfully improving clinical benefit9,10.

To support clinical and policy decision-making, we developed clinical utility meters—visual tools that display for each panel the predicted positive yield and variation across ancestries. These facilitate transparent and data-driven comparison of test performance for clinicians, patients, laboratories, payers and policymakers. Importantly, the meters reflect population-level risk, rather than other metrics like severity or onset, and are therefore sensitive to the inclusion of genes with founder variants, which can markedly increase yield in specific ancestries.

Additionally, the efficiency frontier provides an objective framework linking test performance with price, enabling both providers and payers to define a fair price for a given level of utility, set reimbursement thresholds, or prioritise tests that deliver the highest utility per dollar within a given budget.

Our study has some limitations. Not all providers disclosed list pricing, particularly in the United States, and list prices do not necessarily capture the effects of insurance coverage, public funding, or bundled services such as genetic counselling; convenience and access may also be influenced by factors not captured in pricing. Prices are dynamic, and our analysis represents a cross-sectional snapshot of current offerings, with access potentially restricted based on regional or jurisdictional boundaries. We did not model additional factors such as CNV detection or analytical sensitivity for technically challenging genes; our modelling also applied a consistent variant interpretation policy across all panels and did not take into account laboratory-specific variant interpretation policies, any of which may affect real-world performance. We also did not apply correction factors to reward or penalise for the inclusion or exclusion of potential confounding factors, such as technical difficulty in analysis, population-specific frequency (other than ancestry), gene size or variant type. Our methodology required that variants in gnomAD are only included if they have been curated in ClinVar and thus would not include novel loss-of- function variants in gnomAD without corresponding ClinVar entries. Estimates of utility were based on known pathogenic allele frequencies and did not account for gene-specific analytical sensitivity or variations in gene penetrance. Although we modelled outcomes for both ancestry-specific and pan-ancestry populations, diversity within each group is complex and not fully captured by synthetic groupings constructed using Census weightings and gnomAD data (Supplementary Information)34,35. Certain groups, including First Nations peoples, and Central or South-East Asian populations, remain under-represented in gnomAD and therefore could not be modelled, underscoring the ongoing need for diversification of future genomic reference databases to ensure equity in test performance.

Carrier screening serves as a powerful model for laboratory medicine: value can be quantified, optimised and made transparent through data-driven approaches. Our findings show that clinical utility—not panel size or price—can anchor test design, performance and reimbursement. Such a framework enables clinicians to select tests with appropriate clinical utility, payers to set rational reimbursement, and laboratory providers to benchmark new tests against objective utility thresholds. In doing so, they advance the goals of value-based healthcare— improving outcomes while containing costs—and challenging the prevailing assumption that in genomic medicine, as elsewhere, “larger or more expensive is always better”.

Methods

Carrier screening providers and tests

We identified carrier screening providers through an internet search conducted between October 2024 and July 202517,18. For each test, we recorded panel size, gene content, list price, test methodology (targeted genotyping or next-generation sequencing), turnaround time (TAT) and inclusion of copy number variants (CNV) detection. List prices reflected out-of-pocket patient costs without insurance coverage and were standardised to USD (July 2025). Where pricing was not publicly available, prices were obtained from intermediary providers. We excluded single-gene tests, custom panels, outsourced (“white-boxed”) assays, laboratories lacking clinical accreditation, and laboratories that did not respond after three contact attempts.

Gene and variant curation

We compiled a reference set of 1350 genes associated with severe childhood-onset AR or XL conditions, relevant for reproductive decision-making19,36. This “starting point” reference set included comprehensive lists of genes selected from two perspectives: genes known to be life-limiting or disabling, with childhood onset and likely to be ones that couples would take steps to avoid having an affected child37, as well as lists of genes suitable for population-based carrier screening selected largely on the basis of their carrier frequency9,10.

Pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants (collectively referred to as “pathogenic”) were extracted from ClinVar (July 2025) and linked to allele frequency data from gnomAD v4.1.0 exomes and genomes (total: 807,162 individuals) as described previously19,38. Variants with conflicting annotations, low penetrance, or poor-quality metrics were excluded. Details of variant curation are provided in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Methods; Table S5). Variant types for each gene after filtering and curation are also shown in Supplementary Information (Table S6).

Ancestry groups and synthetic populations

Analyses were performed on the global gnomAD dataset (ALL), and the ten principal component analysis (PCA)-defined ancestry groups, representing biogeographically diverse populations: African/African American (AFR), Admixed American (AMR), Amish (AMI), Ashkenazi Jewish (ASJ), East Asian (EAS), Finnish European (FIN), Middle Eastern (MID), Non-Finnish European (NFE), South Asian (SAS) and Remaining Individuals (RMN). To mimic real-world testing populations, where ancestry is often admixed, we also generated two synthetic populations (USA and AUS), representing the U.S. and Australia, respectively, by weighting ancestry groups according to national Census data and literature estimates. Data sources and population estimates are provided in Table S8. Several Australian Census ancestry responses represented composite ancestry groups (including New Zealander, South African and Australian categories), requiring further disaggregation into gnomAD PCA ancestry groups (Table S8C–E). Where estimates were difficult to obtain, sensitivity analyses of different ancestry weightings were performed to determine whether these impacted the top gene carrier rates for each ancestry (derived from ref. 19; data not shown).

Clinical utility and value modelling

We calculated gene carrier rates (GCR) and couple carrier rates (GCCR) from population allele frequencies and published literature (Supplementary Information), as described previously19,38. Statistical analysis

We evaluated the relationships among panel size, price and clinical utility using Pearson’s r and Spearman’s rank ⍴ correlation coefficients39, focussing on the largest panel offered by each provider. To compare the clinical utility of different panels and to identify panels with optimal value, we generated graphical “meter” visualisations (see Supplementary Information for details).

Ethics

This study used only publicly available, de-identified datasets and did not involve human participants; institutional review board approval was not required.
