Geometry- and topology-controlled synchronization phase transition on manifolds Preprint: APS/123-QED
Yang Tian

Abstract
In this work, we explore how the geometry and topology of the underlying manifold shape the synchronization phase transition of a system. To do so, we extend the Kuramoto-Sakaguchi model from spheres to compact, connected, orientable, and homogeneous Riemannian manifolds of arbitrary dimension. Starting from the mean-field kinetic equation on the manifold, we derive a local response equation for the order parameter near the incoherent state and separate the geometric and topological contributions to the phase transition out of the incoherent state. The manifold geometry determines a coefficient κ ⁡ ( M ) \kappa\left(M\right) to control the critical coupling for the linear loss of stability of the incoherent state. The manifold topology constrains the cubic term of the response equation through the Euler characteristic χ ⁡ ( M ) \chi\left(M\right) . Under a local sign condition on the cubic term, topology does not allow a generic continuous or tricritical synchronization phase transition to occur when χ ⁡ ( M ) ≠ 0 \chi\left(M\right)\neq 0 , and it imposes a non-zero net defect charge on the incipient ordered texture. When an additional local stabilization condition holds in that nonzero-Euler class, topology further selects a discontinuous phase transition. When χ ⁡ ( M ) = 0 \chi\left(M\right)=0 , topology does not impose that obstruction, so continuous, discontinuous, and tricritical local branches are all allowed. We verify these findings on representative families including hyperspheres, equal even-sphere products, complex Grassmannians, complex projective spaces, flat tori, real Stiefel manifolds, rotation groups, and unitary groups. Our framework recovers the classical hyperspherical parity law and extends it to a broad class of non-spherical state spaces. It therefore identifies the geometric origin of the linear instability threshold and the topological origin of the accessible phase transition scenarios in diverse synchronization phenomena.

II.2
General model in discrete form

Based on the definitions presented above, we first introduce our general model in its discrete form. The Kuramoto–Sakaguchi model on the manifold is written as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\sigma_{i}=V_{i}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)+\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma\left(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j};\alpha\right),

(3)

where
K
denotes the coupling strength,
N
is the number of oscillators, and
\alpha
denotes the phase-lag effect. Each
V_{i}
is a smooth tangent vector field of oscillator
i
that satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla_{M}\cdot V_{i}=0,

(4)

which is the natural condition ensuring that the spatially uniform incoherent state used below remains stationary under the intrinsic drift. We define the function
\Gamma
as

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j};\alpha\right)=\mathsf{P}_{\sigma_{i}}^{\perp}\left[\left(I-\alpha A\right)F\left(\sigma_{j}\right)\right],

(5)

in which
\mathsf{P}_{\sigma_{i}}^{\perp}
maps any vector in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{D_{a}}
to the tangent space of
M
with respect to
\sigma_{i}
. The function
F
isometrically embeds oscillator states on
M
into the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{D_{a}}
to support the synchronization analysis in an Euclidean space. The anti-symmetric operator
A
is used to describe the non-reciprocal coupling caused by phase lags, whose strength is measured by
\alpha
. Specifically,
I-\alpha A
is the first order approximation of the rotation operator
\exp\left(\alpha A\right)
. This operator creates local rotations to break inversion symmetry when
|\alpha|>0
, which models
phase frustration in analogy with the classic Kuramoto-Sakaguchi coupling on the circle
67
.

In Eq. (
5
),
\mathsf{P}_{\sigma_{i}}^{\perp}F\left(\sigma_{j}\right)
enforces synchronization among oscillators while
-\alpha\mathsf{P}_{\sigma_{i}}^{\perp}AF\left(\sigma_{j}\right)
imposes asynchronization. The action of
\mathsf{P}_{\sigma_{i}}^{\perp}
ensures that the change in oscillator state
\sigma_{i}
remains confined to the tangent space, irrespective of whether the evolution of
\sigma_{i}
is governed by synchronizing or desynchronizing interactions. To analyze the global state of the system, the order parameter of the system is defined as

\displaystyle r\left(t\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}F\left(\sigma_{i}\right),

(6)

which is the empirical mean of the embedded states
F\left(\sigma_{i}\right)
in the ambient Euclidean space
41
;
67
;
63
;
62
. The particular choice of embedding
F

determines which geometric features of the manifold are probed by the
order parameter. In the classical examples,
F
is an isometric
embedding into the unit sphere, so that
|r\left(t\right)|\in\left[0,1\right]
quantifies
the degree of synchronization. As
|r|
approaches
0
, the system is in an asynchronization phase. As
|r|
approaches
1
, the system is in a synchronization phase.

Connection to existing models

For better conceptual clarity, we now explain the connection between the abstract model presented above and existing concrete models.

In a special case where
\left(M,g\right)
reduces to a standard sphere

which contains only synchronization forces. The gradient flow structure under this condition is elaborated in Appendix

General model in continuous form

Building on the established correspondence with previous models, we proceed to develop the continuous counterpart of our discrete formulation. The continuum formulation arises in the mean-field limit

\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V
, where
\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)
denotes the conditional density of oscillators at time

, in state
\sigma\in M
, for a fixed intrinsic field
V

\displaystyle\int_{M}\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)=1

(9)

for every fixed
V

is the tangent bundle of the manifold (i.e., the union of all the tangent spaces),

denotes a volume measure, and
\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)=\sqrt{\det g}\mathsf{d}^{D}x

In the continuous setting, the order parameter of the system can be expressed as

\displaystyle r\left(t\right)=\int_{TM}\int_{M}F\left(\sigma\right)\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V,

which is the expectation of

with respect to the probability measure
\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}V
(here

is the probability density of

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\left(\sigma,V,t\right)=V\left(\sigma\right)+K\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left(r\left(t\right)-\alpha Ar\left(t\right)\right).

(11)

In this equation,
V\left(\sigma\right)
describes the intrinsic velocity that depends on the local position
\sigma
and reflects heterogeneity. For simplicity, we assume
\mathbb{E}\left[V\left(\sigma\right)\right]=0
and
\mathbb{E}\left[V\left(\sigma\right)\otimes V\left(\sigma\right)\right]=\Delta^{2}\mathbf{I}
, where
\Delta
measures the variance of intrinsic velocity and
\mathbf{I}
is the identity tensor on the tangent space.
K\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}r\left(t\right)
drives the system toward synchronization given the feedback effects of the order parameter. The non-reciprocal coupling term
-K\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\alpha Ar\left(t\right)
models the reaction delays inherent in real systems and disrupts synchronization.

The structure of the velocity field in Eq. (
11
) is consistent with the mean-field velocity obtained in the continuum limit. In Appendix
C
we show that, at the level of the limiting
density, the drift term entering the continuity equation is given by
an intrinsic part
V\left(\sigma\right)
plus an interaction part
obtained by averaging the discrete coupling

\Gamma\left(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j};\alpha\right)
over the
distribution of oscillator states. When the interaction is expressed
in terms of the order parameter
r\left(t\right)
as in

\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left(r\left(t\right)-\alpha Ar\left(t\right)\right)
in

The velocity field defined above effectively transports the probability density over the manifold to create the flow of a “probability fluid”. This probability flow describes how the statistical distribution of the oscillator system evolves over time in the phase space. In general, the evolution equation of this flow is written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho+\nabla_{M}\cdot\mathbf{J}=0,

(12)

where
\nabla_{M}
is the previously defined covariant divergence operator and
\mathbf{J}=\rho\mathbf{v}
is a probability density flow tensor
43
. For any
\sigma\in M
,
\mathbf{J}\left(\sigma\right)
indicates the direction of probability flow and
|\mathbf{J}\left(\sigma\right)|
quantifies the flow rate. The divergence
\nabla_{M}\cdot\mathbf{J}
measures the net outflow per unit time and unit volume. There is a net outflow of probability density from the local volume element when
\nabla_{M}\cdot\mathbf{J}>0
and there is a net inflow of probability density when
\nabla_{M}\cdot\mathbf{J}<0
. More precisely, the above equation can be rewritten in an explicit form under a local coordinate chart
\{x^{1},\ldots,x^{D}\}
by explictly calculating the covariant divergence

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{k}}\left(\sqrt{\det g}\rho v^{k}\right)=0,

(13)

in which
v^{k}
is the contravariant component of the velocity field (i.e.,
\mathbf{v}=v^{k}\partial_{k}
) and
\rho v^{k}=\mathbf{J}^{k}
is the contravariant component of the probability flow density. In Eq. (
13
),
\sqrt{\det g}\rho v^{k}
measures the invariant probability flux density through a coordinate surface (i.e., the amount of probability crossing a unit coordinate area per unit time).
\sqrt{\det g}
encodes the influence of the manifold curvature on the probability flow since
\det g
varies sharply in regions of high curvature. When
\sqrt{\det g}
enlarges, probability flow deceleration and density dilution occur, making curvature equivalent to a repulsive potential. When
\sqrt{\det g}
reduces, probability flow accelerates and probability density concentrates, making curvature equivalent to an attractive potential. The normalization coefficient
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}
normalizes this flux variation to unit volume.

Eqs. (
12
-
13
) interpret the probability flow as an incompressible fluid over the manifold and describe its local conservation law for every fixed intrinsic field
V
. The corresponding global conservation law is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{TM}\int_{M}\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V=0.

(14)

The corresponding boundary condition is either that the manifold
M
is compact, or that a zero-flux condition (i.e., the Neumann-type condition) is imposed at the boundary. Subject to these constraints, the total probability density is conserved over time, with only local flows occurring.

III
Geometry- and topology-governed phase transition

III.1
Analysis framework

After extending the Kuramoto-Sakaguchi model to compact, connected, orientable, and homogeneous
D
-dimensional Riemannian manifolds, we present the derivation of our main theorem in this section. The core result of our work has two complementary parts. For the chosen embedding and order-parameter sector, the averaged tangent projection determines the coefficient
\kappa\left(M\right)
, and this coefficient fixes the critical coupling for the linear loss of stability of the incoherent state. The manifold topology constrains the cubic term of the response equation through the Euler characteristic. Within the one-mode local reduction of Appendices
D
and
E
, topology does not allow generic continuous or tricritical synchronization transitions to occur when
\chi\left(M\right)\neq 0
. If the additional stabilization condition
\Lambda_{5}>0
also holds in that nonzero-Euler class, topology further selects the discontinuous side. When
\chi\left(M\right)=0
, topology does not impose that obstruction, so continuous, discontinuous, and tricritical local branches all remain allowed. Below, we use the continuous form of the generalized model to elaborate our derivation. Our basic strategy is to formulate the response equation of the order parameter under perturbations and establish its connection with the geometric and topological characteristics of the manifold.

To implement this analysis, we consider a situation in which the system is initialized at a ground state

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\left(\sigma,V\right)=\frac{1}{\textsf{Vol}\left(M\right)}

(15)

for every fixed
V
(i.e., the spatial distribution is uniform over the manifold conditional on each intrinsic drift field, here
\textsf{Vol}\left(M\right)
measures the total volume of
M
). This uniform distribution lies far from the synchronized state, leading to a near-zero value of the order parameter
r_{0}\approx 0
. We add small perturbations on
\rho_{0}
to affect
r_{0}

\displaystyle\rho=\rho_{0}+\delta_{\rho},\;\;\;\;\;r=r_{0}+\delta_{r}.

(16)

These small perturbations can be used to model the influence of interactions between oscillators on the probability flow described in Eq. (
12
). Note that
\rho_{0}
is a constant at every point on the manifold for each fixed
V
.

Under small perturbations, we assume that the response of the order parameter can be described by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}r=\mathcal{L}\left(r\right)+\mathcal{N}\left(r\right),

(17)

where
\mathcal{L}\left(r\right)
is the linear term that we can obtain through linear perturbation analysis and
\mathcal{N}\left(r\right)
is the non-linear term. In the following analysis, our task is to explicitly determine the forms of these terms and to relate them to the topology of the manifold.

stability, and the real growth rate, are

therefore fixed by the chosen embedding

geometry.

III.2
Linear response term of the order-parameter response equation

Divergence formulation of the linear response term.-

We first deal with the linear term
\mathcal{L}\left(r\right)
. Based on linear perturbation analysis, the velocity field of the oscillator states after perturbations changes according to
\mathbf{v}\left(\sigma,V,t\right)=\mathbf{v}_{0}+\delta_{\mathbf{v}}
, where we know
\mathbf{v}_{0}=V\left(\sigma\right)
because each oscillator evolves according to its intrinsic velocity at the moment of system initialization. By combining Eq. (
11
) and Eq. (
16
), we can calculate
\delta_{\mathbf{v}}
as

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathbf{v}}=K\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left(\delta_{r}-\alpha A\delta_{r}\right),

(18)

which characterizes how the interactions between oscillators affect the dynamical evolution of the system after initialization.

Meanwhile, by inserting perturbations on the probability density and the velocity field into Eq. (
12
), we can obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\rho_{0}+\delta_{\rho}\right)+\nabla_{M}\cdot\left[\left(\rho_{0}+\delta_{\rho}\right)\left(\mathbf{v}_{0}+\delta_{\mathbf{v}}\right)\right]=0.

(19)

By omitting the second-order infinitesimal
\delta_{\rho}\delta_{\mathbf{v}}\approx 0
in the linear perturbation analysis, we can simplify Eq. (
19
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\rho_{0}+\delta_{\rho}\right)+\nabla_{M}\cdot\left(\rho_{0}\delta_{\mathbf{v}}+\delta_{\rho}\mathbf{v}_{0}+\rho_{0}\mathbf{v}_{0}\right)=0.

(20)

Given the stability,
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{0}=0
, and equilibrium nature,
\nabla_{M}\cdot\left(\rho_{0}\mathbf{v}_{0}\right)=0
, of the ground state, we can reformulate Eq. (
20
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\delta_{\rho}+\nabla_{M}\cdot\left(\rho_{0}\delta_{\mathbf{v}}\right)+\nabla_{M}\cdot\left(\delta_{\rho}\mathbf{v}_{0}\right)=0,

(21)

where
\nabla_{M}\cdot\left(\rho_{0}\delta_{\mathbf{v}}\right)
describes the coupling-induced probability flow (i.e., collective dynamics driven by order parameter perturbations), which serves as the positive feedback mechanism of the order parameter (i.e.,
\delta r\uparrow\;\Rightarrow\delta\mathbf{v}\uparrow\;\Rightarrow\nabla_{M}\cdot\left(\rho_{0}\delta_{\mathbf{v}}\right)<0\;\Rightarrow\delta_{\rho}\uparrow
). Moreover,
\nabla_{M}\cdot\left(\delta_{\rho}\mathbf{v}_{0}\right)
describes the joint effects of the intrinsic velocity and the probability density perturbations on the probability flow, which serves as the negative feedback mechanism of the order parameter (i.e.,
\nabla_{M}\cdot\left(\delta_{\rho}\mathbf{v}_{0}\right)<0\;\Rightarrow\delta_{\rho}\downarrow
).

Moreover, we can follow Eq. (
10
) to define
\delta_{r}
as
\delta_{r}=\int_{TM}\int_{M}F\left(\sigma\right)\delta_{\rho}\left(\sigma,V,t\right)h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V
. Then we compute the derivative of this equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\delta_{r}=\int_{TM}\int_{M}F\left(\sigma\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\delta_{\rho}h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V.

(22)

By inserting the expression of
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\delta_{\rho}
in Eq. (
21
) into Eq. (
22
), we can obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\delta_{r}=

\displaystyle-\int_{TM}\int_{M}F\left(\sigma\right)\nabla_{M}\cdot\left(\rho_{0}\delta_{\mathbf{v}}\right)h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V

\displaystyle-\int_{TM}\int_{M}F\left(\sigma\right)\nabla_{M}\cdot\left(\delta_{\rho}\mathbf{v}_{0}\right)h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V.

(23)

We use the divergence theorem
43
and the differential of the embedding to reformulate these divergence terms in Eq. (
23
). Specifically, for the manifold without boundary we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{M}F\left(\sigma\right)\nabla_{M}\cdot\mathbf{X}\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)=-\int_{M}\mathsf{d}F_{\sigma}\left[\mathbf{X}\left(\sigma\right)\right]\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right),

(24)

where
\mathbf{X}
is a tangent vector field and
\mathsf{d}F_{\sigma}:T_{\sigma}M\to\mathbb{R}^{D_{a}}
denotes the differential of the embedding. Applying Eq. (
24
) on the divergence terms in Eq. (
23
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\delta_{r}=

\displaystyle\int_{TM}\int_{M}\mathsf{d}F_{\sigma}\left[\rho_{0}\delta_{\mathbf{v}}\right]h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V

\displaystyle+\int_{TM}\int_{M}\mathsf{d}F_{\sigma}\left[\delta_{\rho}\mathbf{v}_{0}\right]h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V.

(25)

Then, our central target is to investigate the topological mechanisms underlying these two divergence terms.

The first divergence term.-

For the first divergence term, we can interchange the order of integration and insert Eq. (
18
) into the formula to write this divergence as

\displaystyle\int_{TM}\int_{M}\mathsf{d}F_{\sigma}\left[\rho_{0}\delta_{\mathbf{v}}\right]h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K\rho_{0}\int_{M}\mathsf{d}F_{\sigma}\left[\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\mathbf{Y}\right]\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\int_{TM}h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}V,

(26)

where we mark
\mathbf{Y}=\delta_{r}-\alpha A\delta_{r}
. Meanwhile, we notice that
\int_{TM}h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}V=1
as required by the definition of a probability distribution and
\rho_{0}=1/\textsf{Vol}\left(M\right)
. Since the embedding identifies
T_{\sigma}M
with a tangent subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{D_{a}}
, we have
\mathsf{d}F_{\sigma}\left[\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\mathbf{Y}\right]=\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\mathbf{Y}
. Therefore Eq. (
26
) reduces to

\displaystyle\int_{TM}\int_{M}\mathsf{d}F_{\sigma}\left[\rho_{0}\delta_{\mathbf{v}}\right]h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{K}{\textsf{Vol}\left(M\right)}\int_{M}\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\mathbf{Y}\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right),

(27)

Up to this point, no homogeneity assumption has been used. The integral form above is valid for any compact orientable manifold equipped with a smooth embedding. To obtain the scalar coefficient used in the subsequent bifurcation analysis, we now invoke the homogeneity of
M
together with the isotropy of the embedding sector probed by the order parameter. Under this symmetry reduction, the averaged tangent projection acts as a scalar multiple of the identity on the relevant ambient subspace, so that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\textsf{Vol}\left(M\right)}\int_{M}\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\mathbf{Y}\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)=\kappa\left(M\right)\mathbf{Y},

(28)

Therefore the first divergence term becomes

\displaystyle\int_{TM}\int_{M}\mathsf{d}F_{\sigma}\left[\rho_{0}\delta_{\mathbf{v}}\right]h\left(V\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V=K\kappa\left(M\right)\mathbf{Y},

(29)

where
\kappa\left(M\right)
is a geometric factor determined by the averaged tangent projection of the embedding. For the standard sphere
S^{D-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
, one has
\kappa\left(S^{D-1}\right)=\frac{D-1}{D}
.

The second divergence term.-

Then, we deal with the second divergence term in Eq. (
25
), i.e., the contribution involving the intrinsic velocity field

\mathbf{v}_{0}
and the perturbation of the density

\delta_{\rho}
.

In contrast to the first divergence term in Eq. (
25
),
which is purely geometric and linear in
\delta_{r}
through the projection of
F\left(\sigma\right)
, the second term encodes how the intrinsic velocity
\mathbf{v}_{0}
redistributes the perturbation of the density on
M
. To make this contribution precise, we introduce the transport operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{V}\left[\varphi\right]=-\nabla_{M}\cdot\left(\varphi V\right),

(30)

which is the linearized advection generated by the intrinsic drift. Because
\nabla_{M}\cdot V=0
, integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\int_{M}f\,\mathcal{T}_{V}\left[g\right]\mathsf{d}\mu=-\int_{M}g\,\mathcal{T}_{V}\left[f\right]\mathsf{d}\mu,

(31)

for all smooth scalar functions
f
and
g
on
M
. Therefore the transport operator is skew-adjoint on
L^{2}\left(M,\mu\right)
and satisfies

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\int_{M}\overline{\varphi}\,\mathcal{T}_{V}\left[\varphi\right]\mathsf{d}\mu=0,

(32)

for every smooth test function
\varphi
. Consequently, the intrinsic drift contributes only to the antisymmetric part of the linearized generator. In particular, it redistributes phases and frequencies but does not change the real growth rate that determines the onset of synchronization. We therefore identify the geometric contribution to the linear term
\mathcal{L}\left(r\right)
in Eq. (
17
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{geo}}\left(\delta_{r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{\textsf{Vol}\left(M\right)}\int_{M}\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left(\delta_{r}-\alpha A\delta_{r}\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right),

(33)

which reduces, in the homogeneous-isotropic case, to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{geo}}\left(\delta_{r}\right)=K\kappa\left(M\right)\left(\delta_{r}-\alpha A\delta_{r}\right).

(34)

Here
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{geo}}
denotes the part of
\mathcal{L}
that is
generated by the coupling geometry after the antisymmetric transport part has
been separated. The threshold means the critical coupling
K_{c}
at which the incoherent
state first loses linear stability. Eq. (
34
) shows that

K_{c}
depends on the manifold only through the geometric factor

\kappa\left(M\right)
extracted from Eq. (
29
), because this
factor multiplies the real linear growth rate of the order-parameter
perturbation. For the standard sphere, Eq. (
34
) reproduces the
familiar coefficient
K\frac{D-1}{D}
in the linear response. We record this
sphere value as

\displaystyle\kappa\left(S^{D-1}\right)=\frac{D-1}{D}.

(35)

III.3
Non-linear response term of the order-parameter response equation

In the previous section, we have obtained the explicit form of the linear
response operator and identified the geometric factor
\kappa\left(M\right)
that
controls the deterministic linear growth rate [see Eqs. (
26
-
29
)].
We now focus on the nonlinear coefficient that determines the local
bifurcation type near the incoherent state. The technical reduction is
collected in Appendix
D
; here we retain only the ingredients
needed for the topological conclusion. The assumptions introduced below are
standard in a local bifurcation analysis near onset
15
;
33
. These assumptions also
match the physical picture of synchronization onset in a homogeneous mean-field
system. A simple critical eigenvalue selects the critical collective mode. The
transversality condition states that this mode changes stability at a definite
critical coupling. The condition that the quadratic coefficient vanishes rules
out a preferred sign of the order parameter at the incoherent state. Many
symmetric couplings satisfy this condition automatically. Appendix

D
gives the precise form used here.

Reduced amplitude equation.-

We assume that the steady response
problem admits a smooth local reduction near the critical coupling

K=K_{c}
. We assume that the critical eigenvalue of the linearized
order-parameter operator is simple. We assume that this eigenvalue crosses
zero transversely. We also assume that the quadratic normal-form coefficient
vanishes. Appendix
D
then yields a one-dimensional scalar
amplitude equation for the critical collective coordinate. This equation is
the one-mode reduction of the full response equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}r=\mathcal{L}\left(r\right)+\mathcal{N}\left(r\right)

in Eq. (
17
). Its linear term is the reduced action of

\mathcal{L}
near
K=K_{c}
, whereas its cubic term is the leading nonlinear
contribution generated by
\mathcal{N}
. The reduced equation reads

\displaystyle\Psi\left(a;K\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(K\right)a+\Lambda_{3}a^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(a^{4}\right)+O\left(\left|K-K_{c}\right|a^{2}\right)=0,

(36)

together with the critical response branch

r\left(a\right)=a\,r_{c}+O\left(a^{2}\right)
, where
r_{c}
is the critical
order-parameter direction. The coefficient
\Lambda_{3}
admits the explicit
formula derived in Appendix
D
. This reduction does not change
the model. It only separates the critical collective coordinate from the
stable modes that are determined by it near onset.

Topological input.-

To connect
\Lambda_{3}
with the topology of

M
, we associate with
r_{c}
the induced critical tangent field

\displaystyle u_{c}\left(\sigma\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left(r_{c}-\alpha Ar_{c}\right).

(37)

This field is the natural geometric object in our model. The microscopic
transport law in Eq. (
11
) depends on the order parameter through
the projected tangent field

\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left(r-\alpha Ar\right)
. For this reason, the
topological information enters the nonlinear response through
u_{c}
. It does
not enter through
r_{c}
alone.
Under the locality and symmetry conditions stated in Appendix

D
, the cubic coefficient can be written as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}=\int_{M}T\left(\sigma\right)\!\left[u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right)\right]\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right),

(38)

where
T\left(\sigma\right)
is a smooth symmetric trilinear tensor field.
These conditions reflect locality and symmetry that are already built into the
model. The coupling term acts pointwise on the manifold. The homogeneous
structure organizes the relevant tensor fields into symmetry sectors. Appendix

D
shows that these properties are preserved when the stable
modes are eliminated.
The Poincaré-Hopf theorem
56
then implies that, when the Euler characteristic

\chi\left(M\right)
of
M
is non-zero,
the field
u_{c}
must have zeros. This topologically forced zero set does not
by itself determine the sign of
\Lambda_{3}
. To obtain a definite sign
statement, we impose the sign condition stated in Appendix
E
.
This condition concerns the local cubic density

q\left(\sigma\right)=T\left(\sigma\right)\!\left[u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right)\right]
. It requires the contribution from each isolated zero of
u_{c}
to
be
-\gamma_{\ell}\operatorname{ind}_{p_{\ell}}\left(u_{c}\right)
at leading
order with
\gamma_{\ell}>0
, and it also requires the regular contribution
away from the zero set not to overwhelm these indexed contributions.
Under that condition,
\chi\left(M\right)\neq 0
implies

\Lambda_{3}<0
, whereas
\chi\left(M\right)=0
leaves the sign of
\Lambda_{3}

to be decided by the analytic structure of the reduced nonlinearity.

Bifurcation consequence.-

Appendix
D
also gives

\lambda\left(K\right)=\lambda^{\prime}\!\left(K_{c}\right)\left(K-K_{c}\right)+O\left(\left(K-K_{c}\right)^{2}\right)
with

\lambda^{\prime}\!\left(K_{c}\right)\neq 0
, such that any nontrivial
small-amplitude branch with
\Lambda_{3}\neq 0
satisfies

\displaystyle\left|a\right|\sim\sqrt{-\frac{\lambda\left(K\right)}{\Lambda_{3}}}.

(39)

Therefore, if
\lambda^{\prime}\!\left(K_{c}\right)>0
, then

\Lambda_{3}>0
yields a supercritical continuous branch on the linearly
unstable side, while
\Lambda_{3}<0
yields a subcritical branch on the
linearly stable side and therefore a discontinuous onset. Combining this
analytic bifurcation criterion with the topological sign statement from
Appendix
E
, we conclude that non-zero Euler characteristic
forbids a supercritical small-amplitude synchronization branch, whereas zero
Euler characteristic leaves open both continuous and discontinuous transitions.
The condition
\Lambda_{3}\neq 0
is also standard. This condition only states
that the cubic term is the first nonlinear term that controls the local branch.
When symmetry leaves a higher-dimensional critical eigenspace, the same local
conclusions apply branch by branch after one selects a symmetry-broken critical
direction. The present one-mode reduction should be read in that branchwise
sense for such cases.

III.4
Critical defect structure of the onset mode

The topological obstruction described above also has a direct spatial
interpretation. The induced tangent field

u_{c}\left(\sigma\right)=\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left(r_{c}-\alpha Ar_{c}\right)
gives the local direction in which the
synchronized component first develops on
M
. In the language of statistical
physics, one may regard
u_{c}
as the ordered texture selected at onset

68
;
65
;
64
. An
isolated zero of
u_{c}
is then a topological defect of that texture, and the
zero itself is the defect core. At such a point, the local ordered direction
cannot be extended smoothly, because the critical mode does not select any
tangent direction there.

Assume that all zeros of
u_{c}
are isolated. Then the Poincaré-Hopf theorem
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{\mathrm{def}}}\operatorname{ind}_{p_{\ell}}\left(u_{c}\right)=\chi\left(M\right),

(40)

where
N_{\mathrm{def}}
denotes the number of defect cores in the onset
texture. Under the generic nondegeneracy condition stated in Appendix

H
, each isolated zero has index
\pm 1
. Therefore

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{def}}\geq\left|\chi\left(M\right)\right|.

(41)

The integer
\operatorname{ind}_{p_{\ell}}\left(u_{c}\right)
is the
topological charge carried by the
\ell
th defect core. Eq.
(
40
) states that the net defect charge of the onset texture is
fixed by the Euler characteristic of
M
.

This conclusion sharpens the topological statement beyond the sign of

\Lambda_{3}
. A non-zero Euler characteristic does not merely forbid a smooth
defect-free onset texture. In the generic case, it forces the ordered texture
that first appears at threshold to contain at least

\left|\chi\left(M\right)\right|
simple defect cores. The onset pattern
therefore carries a finite background topological charge from the start.

When
\chi\left(M\right)=0
, topology allows a smooth defect-free onset
texture. In that sense, topology no longer blocks a continuous branch on
defect-counting grounds. If critical defects are present and all of them are
simple, then their positive and negative topological charges must balance. The
onset texture may still contain local defects, but topology does not impose a
nonzero background charge. Appendix
H
records this counting
argument in a precise form.

III.5
Topology-conditioned onset scenarios

Within the local reductions of Appendices
F
and

G
, the cubic and quintic coefficients determine the local
statistical-physics scenario at onset. The important point is not that topology
changes the numerical values of the generic mean-field exponents in this
one-dimensional local theory. The important point is that topology determines
which branch carrying those scalings can actually appear near threshold.

If
\chi\left(M\right)\neq 0
and the sign condition in Appendix

E
holds, then
\Lambda_{3}<0
. The generic supercritical
continuous branch in Appendix
F
is therefore not allowed to
occur by topology. The tricritical continuous branch discussed in Appendix

G
is also not allowed to occur by topology, because it would
require
\Lambda_{3}=0
. Under the additional analytic condition

\Lambda_{5}>0
, the onset is locally discontinuous, and the local hysteresis
width and jump amplitude are then determined by Appendix
G
.

If
\chi\left(M\right)=0
, topology imposes no sign constraint on

\Lambda_{3}
. Topology therefore removes the obstruction to a continuous
onset. A generic continuous branch is allowed when
\Lambda_{3}>0
. The same
zero-Euler condition also allows a discontinuous branch, which is allowed when

\Lambda_{3}<0<\Lambda_{5}
. It also allows a tricritical
continuous branch, which is allowed after additional tuning to
\Lambda_{3}=0

with
\Lambda_{5}>0
. In this sense, topology acts as a selection rule for
onset scenarios: a non-zero Euler characteristic does not allow certain
branches to occur by topology, whereas a zero Euler characteristic permits them
without selecting which one is actually realized. The local exponents are still
fixed by the reduced normal form itself.

IV.1
Hypersphere case

We begin with the standard hypersphere

. The hypersphere provides the basic reference
case because the generalized Kuramoto model on the hypersphere is already
known to display an even/odd dimensional dichotomy. The present framework
reproduces that result and also separates the geometric and topological
contributions.

As we show in Appendix

, the geometric average is

\overline{\mathsf{P}}=\frac{D-1}{D}I_{D}
, so the linear coefficient in
Subsec.
III.2

\kappa\left(S^{D-1}\right)=\frac{D-1}{D}
. Hence the deterministic linear
threshold is fixed by the same geometric coefficient that appears in the usual
hyperspherical theory.

The hypersphere case can be compared directly with the generalized hyperspherical
Kuramoto model studied in Refs.
13
;
46
;
51
;
75
. That work found a
discontinuous onset for odd ambient dimension
D
and a continuous onset for
even ambient dimension
D
. The present theory recovers the odd-

half of
that result as a topology-forced statement, and it identifies the vanishing of

\chi\left(S^{D-1}\right)
as the reason why even
D

continuous branch first becomes allowed by topology. The stronger statement
that the even-

an additional analytic property of that specific model.

As we show in Appendix

K
, the Euler characteristic is

\chi\left(S^{D-1}\right)=1+(-1)^{D-1}
. When

\Lambda_{3}<0
. The onset texture must then

When

not exclude the discontinuous branch of Appendix

recovers the classical parity law as a direct consequence of the Euler

IV.9
Summary of the case studies

The case studies all support the same split between geometry and topology.
In every model, the chosen embedding and order-parameter sector determine the
coefficient
\kappa\left(M\right)
through the averaged tangent projection.
This coefficient multiplies the linear growth term of the order-parameter
perturbation near the incoherent state. More precisely,

\kappa\left(M\right)
fixes the critical coupling
K_{c}
at which the
incoherent state first loses linear stability, but it does not decide which
onset scenario is allowed.

The nonzero-Euler cases identify the regime in which topology has direct
predictive power. In the odd
hypersphere sector, in the equal even-sphere product family, in the complex
Grassmannian family, and in the complex projective family, topology fixes the
sign-accessibility of the cubic term in the reduced response equation through
\chi\left(M\right)\neq 0
,
the locality reduction of Appendix
D
, and the sign condition of
Appendix
E
. As a result, topology imposes a nonzero net defect
charge on the onset texture, gives the lower bound

N_{\mathrm{def}}\geq|\chi\left(M\right)|
on generic simple-defect branches,
and does not allow generic continuous or tricritical onset to occur. When

\Lambda_{5}>0
, topology therefore selects a discontinuous onset.

The zero-Euler cases show the complementary role of topology. In the even
hypersphere sector, in the torus family, in the real Stiefel family, in the
rotation group, and in the unitary group, topology removes the obstruction to
continuous onset and allows a defect-free texture. At the same time, the same
zero-Euler condition also allows discontinuous and tricritical local branches.
In that class, topology acts as a permission rule rather than a selection
rule. The actually realized onset scenario is then decided by the analytic
coefficients of the reduced normal form.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we extended the Kuramoto-Sakaguchi framework from spheres to
compact, connected, orientable, and homogeneous manifolds of arbitrary
dimension. We derived the continuum equation on the manifold, identified the
linear and nonlinear terms in the order-parameter response equation near the
incoherent state, and connected those terms with the geometry and topology of
the state manifold. For the chosen embedding and order-parameter sector, the
averaged tangent projection determines the coefficient

\kappa\left(M\right)
. This
coefficient multiplies the linear growth term of the order-parameter
perturbation and therefore fixes the critical coupling
K_{c}
at which the
incoherent state first loses linear stability. The manifold topology
constrains the cubic term of the reduced response equation through the Euler
characteristic and the corresponding cubic coefficient. Within the one-mode local reduction of Appendices

D
and
E
, topology does not allow generic
continuous or tricritical onset to occur when
\chi\left(M\right)\neq 0
. It
also imposes a non-zero net defect charge on the onset texture. If the
additional stabilization condition
\Lambda_{5}>0
also holds in that
nonzero-Euler class, topology further selects a discontinuous onset. When

\chi\left(M\right)=0
, topology does not impose that obstruction, so
continuous, discontinuous, and tricritical local branches all remain allowed.
In that class, topology does not select the branch by itself.

We then tested the theory on representative homogeneous manifolds. The
hypersphere case recovers the classical parity law and shows how the odd and
even sectors fall on the two topological sides of the theory. The family

S^{2m}\times S^{2m}
demonstrates that the same topological selection mechanism
extends beyond a single sphere and survives while the geometric threshold
factor varies. The complex Grassmannian and complex projective families show
that the nonzero-Euler mechanism also persists on genuinely non-spherical
complex homogeneous spaces. The torus, real Stiefel, rotation-group, and
unitary cases show the complementary zero-Euler side: topology allows
continuous onset and defect-free textures, but it does not rule out
discontinuous or tricritical local branches. Collectively, the case studies
support the main message of our theory: the averaged tangent projection of the
chosen embedding sector fixes the critical coupling for the linear loss of
stability of the incoherent state, while topology
constrains which synchronization-onset scenarios can occur.

Appendix C
Continuum limit and kinetic equation

In this appendix, we provide a formal derivation of the continuum limit for the general manifold-based Kuramoto-Sakaguchi model in Eqs. (
3
) and (
5
). The strategy follows the standard mean-field limit for the classic Kuramoto model on
S^{1}
and its generalizations
70
;
24
, which can be adapted to the present geometric setting.

To keep the derivation compact, we work in this appendix with the joint density on
TM
. The main-text notation is recovered by the factorization
\varrho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)=\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)h\left(V\right)
, where
\rho
is the conditional spatial density for a fixed intrinsic drift field and
h\left(V\right)
is the time-independent distribution of
V
.

Empirical measure and weak formulation

For a fixed number
N
of oscillators, the dynamics is defined by Eq. (
3
), which we recall here for convenience

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}\sigma_{i}}{\mathsf{d}t}

\displaystyle=V\left(\sigma_{i}\right)+\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma\left(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j};\alpha\right),

(62)

where
\sigma_{i}\left(t\right)\in M
denotes the oscillator state. The coupling is

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j};\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}^{\perp}_{\sigma_{i}}\left[\left(I-\alpha A\right)F\left(\sigma_{j}\right)\right],

(63)

where
F:M\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D_{a}}
is a fixed smooth embedding,

\mathsf{P}^{\perp}_{\sigma_{i}}:\mathbb{R}^{D_{a}}\to T_{\sigma_{i}}M

is the orthogonal projection,
A\in\mathfrak{so}\left(D_{a}\right)
is a
skew-symmetric matrix, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is the phase-lag parameter.

The empirical measure on
TM
associated with the configuration

\left\{\left(\sigma_{i}\left(t\right),V_{i}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}

is defined by

\displaystyle\nu_{t}^{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\left(\sigma_{i}\left(t\right),V_{i}\right)},

(64)

where
\delta_{\left(\sigma,V\right)}
is the Dirac mass on
TM
. By its construction,
\nu_{t}^{N}
is a probability measure on
TM
. The corresponding
configuration-space empirical measure on
M
is the marginal
\mu_{t}^{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\sigma_{i}\left(t\right)}
, which is obtained from
\nu_{t}^{N}
by integrating out
V
.

To derive an evolution equation for
\nu_{t}^{N}
, we consider it in a weak form. Given a smooth function

\Phi\in C^{\infty}\left(TM\right)
, we define

\displaystyle\left\langle\Phi,\nu_{t}^{N}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{TM}\Phi\left(\sigma,V\right)\,\nu_{t}^{N}\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma\mathsf{d}V\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Phi\left(\sigma_{i}\left(t\right),V_{i}\right)

(65)

Differentiating Eq. (
65
) with respect to
t
and using the chain rule on the manifold
M
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\left\langle\Phi,\nu_{t}^{N}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle\nabla_{M}\Phi\left(\sigma_{i},V_{i}\right),\frac{\mathsf{d}\sigma_{i}}{\mathsf{d}t}\right\rangle_{g},

(66)

where
\nabla_{M}\Phi
denotes the Riemannian gradient of

\Phi
and

\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{g}
is the Riemannian
inner product on
T_{\sigma}M
induced by the metric
g
.

Substituting Eq. (
62
) into Eq. (
66
) and separating
intrinsic and coupling contributions, we can obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\left\langle\Phi,\nu_{t}^{N}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle\nabla_{M}\Phi\left(\sigma_{i},V_{i}\right),V\left(\sigma_{i}\right)\right\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle+\frac{K}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle\nabla_{M}\Phi\left(\sigma_{i},V_{i}\right),\Gamma\left(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j};\alpha\right)\right\rangle_{g}.

(67)

Based on the empirical measure defined in Eq. (
64
), the
right side of Eq. (
67
) can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\left\langle\Phi,\nu_{t}^{N}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{TM}\left\langle\nabla_{M}\Phi\left(\sigma,V\right),V\left(\sigma\right)\right\rangle_{g}\nu_{t}^{N}\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma\mathsf{d}V\right)

\displaystyle\quad+K\int_{TM}\int_{TM}\left\langle\nabla_{M}\Phi\left(\sigma,V\right),\Gamma\left(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\alpha\right)\right\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\nu_{t}^{N}\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma\mathsf{d}V\right)\,\nu_{t}^{N}\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma^{\prime}\mathsf{d}V^{\prime}\right).

(68)

Eq. (
68
) is the weak formulation of the finite-
N
dynamics in terms of the empirical measure
\nu_{t}^{N}
.

Mean-field limit and continuity equation

We now formally take the mean-field limit
N\to\infty
. We assume that the empirical measures

\nu_{t}^{N}
converge weakly to a Borel probability measure, i.e,

\nu_{t}
on
M
for each
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\nu_{t}^{N}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\ \mathrm{weakly}\ }\nu_{t}.

(69)

Eq. (
69
) holds in the sense that

\left\langle\Phi,\nu_{t}^{N}\right\rangle\to\left\langle\Phi,\nu_{t}\right\rangle
for all

\Phi\in C^{\infty}\left(TM\right)
. Based on Eq. (
69
), we can formally obtain the limit in Eq. (
68
)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\left\langle\Phi,\nu_{t}\right\rangle=\int_{TM}\left\langle\nabla_{M}\Phi\left(\sigma,V\right),V\left(\sigma\right)\right\rangle_{g}\nu_{t}\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma\mathsf{d}V\right)

\displaystyle+K\int_{TM}\int_{TM}\left\langle\nabla_{M}\Phi\left(\sigma,V\right),\Gamma\left(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\alpha\right)\right\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\nu_{t}\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma\mathsf{d}V\right)\nu_{t}\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma^{\prime}\mathsf{d}V^{\prime}\right).

(70)

This is the weak formulation of the mean-field equation for the limit measure
\mu_{t}
.

On the tangent bundle
TM
, it is natural to assume that
\nu_{t}
admits a density
\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)
with respect to the
product of the Riemannian volume measure

\mu\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma\right)
on
M
and a reference measure

\mathsf{d}V
on each fiber
T_{\sigma}M
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\nu_{t}\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma\mathsf{d}V\right)

\displaystyle=\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)\,\mu\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V,

(71)

with the normalization

\displaystyle\int_{TM}\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)\,\mu\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V

\displaystyle=1.

(72)

In terms of this density, the continuum order parameter takes the form

\displaystyle r\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{TM}\int_{M}F\left(\sigma^{\prime}\right)\,\rho\left(\sigma^{\prime},V^{\prime},t\right)\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma^{\prime}\right)\mathsf{d}V^{\prime}.

(73)

Using the explicit form of the coupling in Eq. (
63
), the interaction contribution in Eq. (
70
) can be re-written as

\displaystyle\int_{TM}\int_{TM}\left\langle\nabla_{M}\Phi\left(\sigma,V\right),\Gamma\left(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\alpha\right)\right\rangle_{g}\nu_{t}\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma\mathsf{d}V\right)\,\nu_{t}\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma^{\prime}\mathsf{d}V^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{TM}\left\langle\nabla_{M}\Phi\left(\sigma,V\right),\int_{TM}\Gamma\left(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\alpha\right)\,\nu_{t}\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma^{\prime}\mathsf{d}V^{\prime}\right)\right\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{TM}\left\langle\nabla_{M}\Phi\left(\sigma,V\right),\int_{TM}\Gamma\left(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\alpha\right)\,\rho\left(\sigma^{\prime},V^{\prime},t\right)\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma^{\prime}\right)\mathsf{d}V^{\prime}\right\rangle_{g}\mathsf{d}\nu_{t}\left(\sigma,V\right),

(74)

where we have used Eq. (
71
) in the last line.

By substituting Eq. (
63
) into the inner integral in
Eq. (
74
) and using the linearity of

\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}
and
\left(I-\alpha A\right)
with
respect to
F\left(\sigma^{\prime}\right)
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\int_{TM}\Gamma\left(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\alpha\right)\,\rho\left(\sigma^{\prime},V^{\prime},t\right)\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma^{\prime}\right)\mathsf{d}V^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{TM}\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left[\left(I-\alpha A\right)F\left(\sigma^{\prime}\right)\right]\rho\left(\sigma^{\prime},V^{\prime},t\right)\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma^{\prime}\right)\mathsf{d}V^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left[\left(I-\alpha A\right)\int_{TM}F\left(\sigma^{\prime}\right)\,\rho\left(\sigma^{\prime},V^{\prime},t\right)\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma^{\prime}\right)\mathsf{d}V^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left(r\left(t\right)-\alpha Ar\left(t\right)\right),

(75)

where we have used the definition of the
order parameter in Eq. (
73
) in the last step. This shows explicitly that, in the mean–field
limit, the averaged coupling can be expressed in terms of the
order parameter as the projected field

\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left(r\left(t\right)-\alpha Ar\left(t\right)\right)
,
which is precisely the coupling structure used in Eq. (
11
) in our main text.

After substituting Eq. (
71
) and Eqs. (
74
-
75
) into Eq. (
70
) and integrating
by parts on
M
with respect to the Riemannian volume

\mu\left(\mathsf{d}\sigma\right)
(based on that
M
is compact and
without boundary), we can obtain the weak formulation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\int_{TM}\Phi\left(\sigma,V\right)\,\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{TM}\Phi\left(\sigma,V\right)\,\nabla_{M}\cdot\left(\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)\,\mathbf{v}\left(\sigma,V,t\right)\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V,

(76)

where the mean–field velocity field on
TM
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\left(\sigma,V,t\right)

\displaystyle=V\left(\sigma\right)+K\,\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left(r\left(t\right)-\alpha Ar\left(t\right)\right)

(77)

according to our analysis of the averaged coupling.

Because Eq. (
76
) holds for all smooth functions

\Phi\in C^{\infty}\left(TM\right)
, it is equivalent to the continuity equation on
TM

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)+\nabla_{M}\cdot\left(\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)\,\mathbf{v}\left(\sigma,V,t\right)\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(78)

where
\mathbf{v}\left(\sigma,V,t\right)
is given by Eq. (
77
). Eq. (
78
) serves as the kinetic
equation underlying the macroscopic model
Eqs. (
12
-
14
). The associated normalization
\int_{TM}\rho\left(\sigma,V,t\right)\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}V=1

Appendix D
Local bifurcation reduction of the order-parameter response equation

In this appendix, we provide the technical reduction used in
Subsec.
III.3
. This
appendix has two purposes. The first purpose is to derive the scalar amplitude
equation near the incoherent state. The second purpose is to state explicit
conditions under which the cubic coefficient becomes a local integral over

M
. We assume that the steady order-parameter response near the incoherent
state is described by the equation

\displaystyle G\left(K,r\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(K\right)r+\mathcal{N}\left(K,r\right)=0,

(79)

on a Banach space
X
of admissible order-parameter perturbations, with

G\in C^{4}
in a neighborhood of
\left(K_{c},0\right)
and

G\left(K,0\right)=0
. Let

\displaystyle L_{c}=\mathrm{D}_{r}G\left(K_{c},0\right),

(80)

and assume that
L_{c}
is Fredholm of index
0
. We assume that

\ker L_{c}=\operatorname{span}\left\{r_{c}\right\}
is one-dimensional. We
assume that the adjoint kernel is generated by
r_{c}^{\dagger}
. We normalize
the dual pairing by
\langle r_{c}^{\dagger},r_{c}\rangle=1
. We also assume
that the corresponding simple eigenvalue satisfies the transversality condition

\displaystyle\lambda\left(K_{c}\right)=0,\qquad\lambda^{\prime}\!\left(K_{c}\right)\neq 0.

(81)

These assumptions describe a standard codimension-one instability. The
critical mode
r_{c}
is the only neutral collective direction at
K_{c}
. The
remaining directions are determined by this mode near onset. The transversality
condition states that the instability is crossed at a nonzero rate when
K

passes through
K_{c}
.
We introduce the spectral projectors

P\left[f\right]=r_{c}\langle r_{c}^{\dagger},f\rangle
and
Q=I-P
, and decompose

\displaystyle r=a\,r_{c}+w,\qquad\langle r_{c}^{\dagger},w\rangle=0.

(82)

Expanding the nonlinear part at
K=K_{c}
gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\left(K_{c},r\right)=\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(r,r\right)+\mathcal{N}_{3}\left(r,r,r\right)+O\left(\|r\|_{X}^{4}\right),

(83)

where
\mathcal{N}_{2}
and
\mathcal{N}_{3}
are the symmetric multilinear
Taylor coefficients. To exclude a transcritical-type correction in the scalar
normal form, we further impose the condition

\displaystyle\Big\langle r_{c}^{\dagger},\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(r_{c},r_{c}\right)\Big\rangle=0.

(84)

Eq. (
84
) is automatic whenever the steady response equation is odd
under
r\mapsto-r
. In more general settings, we impose Eq. (
84
) as
an additional condition. Eq. (
84
) states that the incoherent state has
no preferred sign along the critical mode at quadratic order. The range
equation then yields

\displaystyle w\left(a;K\right)=

\displaystyle a^{2}w_{2}+O\left(a^{3}\right)+O\left(\left|K-K_{c}\right|a\right),

\displaystyle w_{2}=

\displaystyle-\left(QL_{c}Q\right)^{-1}Q\,\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(r_{c},r_{c}\right).

(85)

The correction
w_{2}
is the stable component generated by the quadratic
self-interaction of the critical mode. Eq. (
85
) shows that
w_{2}
is
not an independent degree of freedom. The stable part is determined by the
critical amplitude. Eq. (
85
) then enters the kernel equation and yields
the reduced amplitude equation

\displaystyle\Psi\left(a;K\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(K\right)a+\Lambda_{3}a^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(a^{4}\right)+O\left(\left|K-K_{c}\right|a^{2}\right)=0,

(86)

with

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}=\Big\langle r_{c}^{\dagger},\,\mathcal{N}_{3}\left(r_{c},r_{c},r_{c}\right)+2\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(r_{c},w_{2}\right)\Big\rangle.

(87)

Eq. (
86
) is the technical statement used in
Subsec.
III.3
. Eq. (
87
)
shows that the cubic coefficient contains a direct cubic term and a mixed term
produced by the stable correction.
Moreover, Eq. (
81
) implies the local expansion

\displaystyle\lambda\left(K\right)=\lambda^{\prime}\!\left(K_{c}\right)\left(K-K_{c}\right)+O\left(\left(K-K_{c}\right)^{2}\right),

(88)

so every nontrivial small-amplitude branch with
\Lambda_{3}\neq 0
satisfies

\displaystyle\left|a\right|\sim\sqrt{-\frac{\lambda\left(K\right)}{\Lambda_{3}}}=\sqrt{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}\!\left(K_{c}\right)\left(K-K_{c}\right)}{\Lambda_{3}}}.

(89)

Eq. (
89
) is the usual square-root scaling near a cubic bifurcation.

We next connect Eq. (
87
) with the topology of
M
. We introduce the induced
critical tangent field

\displaystyle u_{c}\left(\sigma\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left(r_{c}-\alpha Ar_{c}\right).

(90)

This field is the relevant geometric object for the nonlinear transport. The
microscopic velocity in Eq. (
11
) depends on the order parameter
through the projected field

\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left(r-\alpha Ar\right)
. For this reason, the
topological content of the critical response is carried by
u_{c}
.
For any stable correction
w\in QX
, we also define the induced tangent field

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}_{w}\left(\sigma\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\left(w-\alpha Aw\right).

(91)

We now give a sufficient set of conditions under which the cubic coefficient
becomes pointwise local.

Locality and symmetry conditions.-

Let

\mathcal{S}_{2}\left(u_{c}\right)\subset QX
denote the set of stable
corrections

with the following property. The induced tangent field

\widetilde{u}_{w}\left(\sigma\right)
can be written pointwise as

S\left(\sigma\right)\left[u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right)\right]

for some
S\in\Gamma\left(\operatorname{Sym}^{2}T^{\ast}M\otimes TM\right)
.
This set collects the local quadratic tensor sector generated by the critical
field. This set is natural in our model. The microscopic transport acts
locally on
M
. The homogeneous structure organizes tensor fields by symmetry
type.
We first require the direct cubic contribution to be local in the induced
tangent field. This requirement states that the direct cubic response is
assembled from a pointwise cubic density on
M
. Concretely, we assume that
there exists a smooth symmetric trilinear tensor field
T_{3,0}\left(\sigma\right)

such that

\displaystyle\Big\langle r_{c}^{\dagger},\mathcal{N}_{3}\left(r_{c},r_{c},r_{c}\right)\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{M}T_{3,0}\left(\sigma\right)\!\left[u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right)\right]\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right),

(92)

We next require the quadratic source to stay inside the local quadratic sector.
We also require the stable inverse to preserve that sector:

\displaystyle Q\,\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(r_{c},r_{c}\right)\in\mathcal{S}_{2}\left(u_{c}\right).

(93)

\displaystyle\left(QL_{c}Q\right)^{-1}\mathcal{S}_{2}\left(u_{c}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{2}\left(u_{c}\right).

(94)

These conditions imply that the stable correction produced by the quadratic
self-interaction remains local and quadratic in
u_{c}
. Therefore there
exists a smooth symmetric bilinear bundle map
S_{2}\left(\sigma\right)
. This
map satisfies

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}_{w_{2}}\left(\sigma\right)

\displaystyle=S_{2}\left(\sigma\right)\left[u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right)\right].

(95)

We finally require the mixed quadratic contribution to be local in the pair

\left(u_{c},\widetilde{u}_{w}\right)
. This requirement states that the
interaction between the critical mode and the stable correction is still
evaluated pointwise on
M
. We therefore assume that there exists a smooth
bilinear form
B\left(\sigma\right)
. This form satisfies the following
relation for every
w\in\mathcal{S}_{2}\left(u_{c}\right)
:

\displaystyle\Big\langle r_{c}^{\dagger},\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(r_{c},w\right)\Big\rangle=\int_{M}B\left(\sigma\right)\!\left[u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),\widetilde{u}_{w}\left(\sigma\right)\right]\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right).

(96)

Eq. (
95
) turns Eq. (
96
) into

\displaystyle\Big\langle r_{c}^{\dagger},\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(r_{c},w_{2}\right)\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{M}T_{3,1}\left(\sigma\right)\!\left[u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right),

(97)

The tensor field
T_{3,1}\left(\sigma\right)
is obtained by symmetrizing the
contraction of
B\left(\sigma\right)
with
S_{2}\left(\sigma\right)
. Eqs.
(
87
), (
92
), and (
97
) then give

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}T\left(\sigma\right)\!\left[u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right)\right]\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right),

(98)

with
T=T_{3,0}+2T_{3,1}
. This is precisely the local tensor
representation used in Subsec.
III.3
. The three conditions above state that the
direct cubic term, the quadratic correction, and their mixed contribution all
preserve the local tensor structure selected by the projected tangent field.

A convenient sufficient criterion for Eq. (
94
) comes from symmetry.
We assume that the quadratic source generated by
u_{c}\otimes u_{c}
belongs
to a finite-dimensional
G
-invariant harmonic sector for the transitive
isometry group
G
of
M
. We also assume that
QL_{c}Q
is
G
-equivariant on
that sector. If that sector is irreducible, Schur’s lemma
32
implies that
QL_{c}Q

acts there as a scalar multiple of the identity. The inverse then preserves the
local tensor structure, and Eq. (
95
) follows automatically. This
criterion is natural on homogeneous manifolds. The linearized transport
operator and the tensor decomposition are both controlled by the same symmetry
group. Under these locality and symmetry conditions,
Subsec.
III.3
reduces the
global bifurcation problem to the topology of the induced tangent field, while
Appendix
E
supplies the additional sign condition needed to pass
from the forced zeros of
u_{c}
to the definite sign of
\Lambda_{3}
.

Appendix E
Sign condition for the cubic coefficient

In this appendix, we state the additional sign condition used in
Subsec.
III.3
.
Appendix
D
shows that the cubic coefficient can be written as a
local integral over
M
. We now explain how the zeros of
u_{c}
can force the
sign of that integral. We write the cubic density in Eq. (
38
) as

\displaystyle q\left(\sigma\right)

\displaystyle=T\left(\sigma\right)\left[u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right),u_{c}\left(\sigma\right)\right],

(99)

The density
q\left(\sigma\right)
measures the local contribution of the
critical tangent field to the cubic coefficient. The coefficient
\Lambda_{3}

is the integral of this density:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}=\int_{M}q\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right).

(100)

We assume that the zero set of
u_{c}
consists of isolated points

\left\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\right\}
. This situation is generic for a smooth
vector field after a small perturbation. For each zero
p_{\ell}
, we choose a
geodesic ball
B_{\varepsilon}\left(p_{\ell}\right)

43
. This ball contains no
other zeros. As
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, we require that the local
contribution to the cubic integral around each zero satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\varepsilon}\left(p_{\ell}\right)}q\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)=-\gamma_{\ell}\operatorname{ind}_{p_{\ell}}\left(u_{c}\right)+o\left(1\right).

(101)

The coefficient
\gamma_{\ell}>0
is independent of
\varepsilon
. Eq.
(
101
) states that the leading contribution near each zero is
proportional to the local index. The coefficient
\gamma_{\ell}
measures the
strength of this contribution. The sign is fixed by the chosen orientation
convention.
We denote the complement of the zero neighborhoods by

M_{\varepsilon}=M\setminus\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m}B_{\varepsilon}\left(p_{\ell}\right)
.
We also require that the regular contribution on
M_{\varepsilon}
is
controlled. More precisely, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for
every
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{M_{\varepsilon}}q\left(\sigma\right)\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right)\right|<\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\gamma_{\ell}.

(102)

Eq. (
102
) states that the regular contribution away from the zeros does
not overwhelm the indexed contributions created near the zeros.
Under these conditions, Eq. (
100
) can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\gamma_{\ell}\operatorname{ind}_{p_{\ell}}\left(u_{c}\right)+\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon},

(103)

The remainder
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}
collects the
o\left(1\right)
terms
from Eq. (
101
) and the regular remainder from Eq. (
102
). The
Poincaré-Hopf theorem
56
gives

\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\operatorname{ind}_{p_{\ell}}\left(u_{c}\right)=\chi\left(M\right)
.
Therefore, if
\chi\left(M\right)\neq 0
and the chosen orientation convention
makes

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\gamma_{\ell}\operatorname{ind}_{p_{\ell}}\left(u_{c}\right)>0,

(104)

then Eq. (
103
) yields
\Lambda_{3}<0
. This is the additional sign
condition used in the main text when we pass from the topological obstruction
created by the zeros of
u_{c}
to a definite subcritical sign of the cubic
normal-form coefficient.

When
\chi\left(M\right)=0
, the above sign conclusion is unavailable. In that
case the sign of
\Lambda_{3}
must be determined from the analytic structure of

T\left(\sigma\right)
and from the detailed nonlinear reduction.

Appendix F
Dynamical normal form and weak-field scaling

In this appendix, we record two direct consequences of the reduction derived in
Appendix
D
. The first consequence is a scalar dynamical normal
form near the critical coupling. The second consequence is the standard
weak-field scaling law near a continuous branch. These statements do not add a
new topological mechanism. They use the same one-dimensional reduction that
already underlies Subsec.
III.3
.

Dynamical normal form.-

We assume that the local order-parameter
dynamics near the incoherent state is generated by the same response map as in
Appendix
D
. More precisely, we assume that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}r=-G\left(K,r\right),

(105)

where
G\left(K,r\right)
is defined by Eq. (
79
). We also assume that
Eq. (
105
) admits a
C^{3}
local center manifold
33
at

\left(K_{c},0\right)
. The center manifold is tangent to

\operatorname{span}\left\{r_{c}\right\}
and uses the same critical mode as
the steady reduction in Appendix
D
. Therefore the reduced center
variable
a
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}a

\displaystyle=-\Psi\left(a;K\right)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\left(K\right)a-\Lambda_{3}a^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(a^{4}\right)+O\left(\left|K-K_{c}\right|a^{2}\right),

(106)

where
\Psi\left(a;K\right)
is the steady reduced equation in
Eq. (
86
). Hence the equilibria of Eq. (
106
) are exactly the
small-amplitude steady branches already studied in
Subsec.
III.3
.

Let
a_{\ast}\left(K\right)
be a stable equilibrium of Eq. (
106
).
Linearizing Eq. (
106
) around
a_{\ast}\left(K\right)
gives the local
relaxation rate

\displaystyle\tau^{-1}\left(K\right)=\left|\partial_{a}\Psi\left(a_{\ast}\left(K\right);K\right)\right|.

(107)

For the incoherent branch
a_{\ast}=0
, Eq. (
107
) gives

\displaystyle\tau^{-1}\left(K\right)=\left|\lambda\left(K\right)\right|+o\left(\left|K-K_{c}\right|\right),

(108)

and therefore

\displaystyle\tau\left(K\right)\sim\left|\lambda\left(K\right)\right|^{-1}\sim\left|K-K_{c}\right|^{-1}.

(109)

This is the usual critical slowing-down law. When a continuous branch exists,
Eq. (
89
) gives

a_{\ast}^{2}\left(K\right)=-\lambda\left(K\right)/\Lambda_{3}+o\left(\lambda\right)
.
Substituting this relation into Eq. (
107
) yields

\displaystyle\tau^{-1}\left(K\right)

\displaystyle=\left|\lambda\left(K\right)+3\Lambda_{3}a_{\ast}^{2}\left(K\right)\right|

\displaystyle=2\left|\lambda\left(K\right)\right|+o\left(\left|K-K_{c}\right|\right),

(110)

so the same scaling
\tau\sim\left|K-K_{c}\right|^{-1}
holds on the
continuous branch.

Weak-field scaling.-

We next add a weak external bias in the
critical direction. We assume that the full response equation takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}r=-G\left(K,r\right)+h\,\eta,

(111)

where
\eta\in X
is a fixed bias direction with

\langle r_{c}^{\dagger},\eta\rangle\neq 0
. After rescaling the field
amplitude
h
, the reduced steady equation on the center manifold becomes

\displaystyle\Psi_{h}\left(a;K\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(K\right)a+\Lambda_{3}a^{3}-h

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(a^{4}\right)+O\left(\left|K-K_{c}\right|a^{2}\right)+O\left(\left|h\right|\left|a\right|\right)

\displaystyle=0.

(112)

At the critical coupling
K=K_{c}
, Eq. (
112
) gives

\displaystyle\left|a\right|\sim\left|\frac{h}{\Lambda_{3}}\right|^{1/3},

(113)

provided
\Lambda_{3}\neq 0
. This is the standard mean-field field-response
law, so the exponent is
\delta=3
.

When
h=0
and a continuous branch exists, Eq. (
89
) already gives

\displaystyle\left|a_{\ast}\left(K\right)\right|\sim\left|K-K_{c}\right|^{1/2},

(114)

so the order-parameter exponent is
\beta=1/2
. To obtain the differential
susceptibility, we differentiate Eq. (
112
) with respect to
h
along a
steady branch. This gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial h}a_{\ast}=\left(\lambda\left(K\right)+3\Lambda_{3}a_{\ast}^{2}\left(K\right)\right)^{-1}+o\left(\left|K-K_{c}\right|^{-1}\right).

(115)

On the incoherent side, where
a_{\ast}=0
, Eq. (
115
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial h}a_{\ast}\sim\lambda\left(K\right)^{-1}.

(116)

On a continuous branch, Eq. (
89
) and Eq. (
115
) give

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial h}a_{\ast}\sim\left(-2\lambda\left(K\right)\right)^{-1}.

(117)

Therefore, on both sides of a continuous transition,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial h}a_{\ast}\right|\sim\left|K-K_{c}\right|^{-1},

(118)

which is the standard mean-field susceptibility law
\gamma=1
.

The present one-dimensional reduction therefore gives the usual mean-field
triplet

\beta=\frac{1}{2}
,
\gamma=1
, and
\delta=3
whenever a continuous branch
exists. In the present theory, topology does not change these exponents at the
generic codimension-one level. Instead, topology acts by constraining the sign
of
\Lambda_{3}
and therefore by constraining whether the continuous branch
described by Eqs. (
113
)-(
118
) can actually occur. Under the sign
condition in Appendix
E
, a manifold with

\chi\left(M\right)\neq 0
does not allow that continuous branch to occur by
topology. A manifold with
\chi\left(M\right)=0
allows it.

Appendix G
Quintic normal form and hysteresis scales

In this appendix, we extend the local reduction to quintic order.
This extension is useful when the cubic coefficient is negative and the
continuous branch is therefore not allowed to occur by topology. The cubic
normal form then does not
determine the stable large-amplitude branch by itself. The hysteresis analysis
only needs one additional property beyond Appendix
D
. The
reduced scalar equation must be odd up to quintic order:

\displaystyle\Psi\left(-a;K\right)=-\Psi\left(a;K\right)+O\left(a^{7}\right)+O\left(\left|K-K_{c}\right|a^{3}\right).

(119)

This condition is natural when the reduced order parameter has no preferred
sign and no external bias is present. The amplitudes
a
and
-a
describe the
same critical mode with opposite sign. In the absence of an external field or
built-in chirality, the reduced dynamics should not distinguish them.

A weaker sufficient condition at the model level is a local

\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry of the response map near the incoherent state.
Assume that there exists a bounded linear involution

\mathcal{R}:X\to X
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{2}=I,\qquad G\left(K,\mathcal{R}r\right)=\mathcal{R}G\left(K,r\right),\qquad\mathcal{R}r_{c}=-r_{c}.

Then
L_{c}
commutes with
\mathcal{R}
. The local center manifold can be
chosen
\mathcal{R}
-invariant. The amplitude coordinate changes from
a
to

-a
. The reduced scalar equation is therefore odd. Eq. (
119
) follows.
This condition is weaker than
G\left(K,-r\right)=-G\left(K,r\right)
. This
condition is also weaker than assuming a specific involutive isometry of
M
.
A manifold isometry is only one concrete realization of this local

\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry.

If Eq. (
119
) fails, the reduced scalar equation contains even terms.
The two signs of
a
are then not equivalent. The local bifurcation becomes
imperfect or asymmetric. The symmetric branch formulas derived below do not
hold in the same form.

Eq. (
119
) is enough for the quintic normal form and for the hysteresis
scalings derived below. We now keep the general Taylor expansion of

\mathcal{N}\left(K_{c},r\right)
up to quintic order. We also assume that

G\in C^{7}
in a neighborhood of
\left(K_{c},0\right)
, so the reduced
equation admits a controlled quintic expansion. The range correction then has
the form

\displaystyle w\left(a;K\right)

\displaystyle=a^{2}w_{2}+a^{3}w_{3}+a^{4}w_{4}

\displaystyle\qquad+O\left(a^{5}\right)+O\left(\left|K-K_{c}\right|a\right).

(120)

Here
w_{2}
is given by Eq. (
85
). The coefficients

w_{3},w_{4}\in QX
satisfy

\displaystyle w_{3}=-\left(QL_{c}Q\right)^{-1}Q\left(2\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(r_{c},w_{2}\right)+\mathcal{N}_{3}\left(r_{c},r_{c},r_{c}\right)\right),

(121)

and

\displaystyle w_{4}

\displaystyle=-\left(QL_{c}Q\right)^{-1}Q\Big(\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(w_{2},w_{2}\right)+2\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(r_{c},w_{3}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+3\mathcal{N}_{3}\left(r_{c},r_{c},w_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathcal{N}_{4}\left(r_{c},r_{c},r_{c},r_{c}\right)\Big).

(122)

Substituting Eqs. (
120
)-(
122
) into the kernel equation yields the
quintic normal form

\displaystyle\Psi_{5}\left(a;K\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(K\right)a+\Lambda_{3}a^{3}+\Lambda_{5}a^{5}

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(a^{7}\right)+O\left(\left|K-K_{c}\right|a^{3}\right)=0,

(123)

where
\Lambda_{3}
is still given by Eq. (
87
), and

\displaystyle\Lambda_{5}

\displaystyle=\Big\langle r_{c}^{\dagger},\mathcal{N}_{5}\left(r_{c},r_{c},r_{c},r_{c},r_{c}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+4\mathcal{N}_{4}\left(r_{c},r_{c},r_{c},w_{2}\right)+3\mathcal{N}_{3}\left(r_{c},r_{c},w_{3}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+3\mathcal{N}_{3}\left(r_{c},w_{2},w_{2}\right)+2\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(r_{c},w_{4}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(w_{2},w_{3}\right)\Big\rangle.

(124)

Eq. (
119
) removes the quadratic and quartic terms from the reduced
scalar equation. Eq. (
119
) does not require
w_{2}
or
w_{4}
to
vanish. Therefore the hysteresis analysis only needs Eq. (
123
) together
with the sign condition
\Lambda_{5}>0
. The explicit form of
\Lambda_{5}

changes, but the later formulas do not.

Eq. (
124
) also shows why a universal topological sign rule for

\Lambda_{5}
is unavailable in the present framework. Unlike
\Lambda_{3}
,
the quintic coefficient depends on the slaved corrections
w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}

and on the higher multilinear forms
\mathcal{N}_{2}
-
\mathcal{N}_{5}
. These
objects are fixed by the analytic structure of the transport law. They are not
fixed by the zero set of
u_{c}
alone. One may, for example, add a local
quintic perturbation proportional to

\langle r_{c}^{\dagger},r\rangle^{5}r_{c}
. Such a perturbation changes

\Lambda_{5}
. It does not change
L_{c}
, the topology of
M
, or the cubic
sign statement in Appendix
E
. Therefore topology alone does not
determine the sign of the general coefficient
\Lambda_{5}
.

To obtain a shorter coefficient formula, we now add one more simplification. We
assume that the nonlinear Taylor expansion along the critical branch contains
no even terms up to quintic order:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\left(K_{c},r\right)=\mathcal{N}_{3}\left(r,r,r\right)+\mathcal{N}_{5}\left(r,r,r,r,r\right)+O\left(\|r\|_{X}^{7}\right).

(125)

Under Eq. (
125
), Eq. (
87
) simplifies because
w_{2}=0
. Eqs.
(
120
)-(
122
) then reduce to

\displaystyle w\left(a;K\right)=a^{3}w_{3}+O\left(a^{5}\right)+O\left(\left|K-K_{c}\right|a\right),

\displaystyle w_{3}=-\left(QL_{c}Q\right)^{-1}Q\,\mathcal{N}_{3}\left(r_{c},r_{c},r_{c}\right).

In that case, Eq. (
124
) becomes

\displaystyle\Lambda_{5}=\Big\langle r_{c}^{\dagger},\mathcal{N}_{5}\left(r_{c},r_{c},r_{c},r_{c},r_{c}\right)+3\mathcal{N}_{3}\left(r_{c},r_{c},w_{3}\right)\Big\rangle.

(126)

Eq. (
123
) is the quintic extension of Eq. (
86
). Eq.
(
126
) is only a compact special case of the general coefficient
formula in Eq. (
124
). Eq. (
123
) is the appropriate local normal
form when the cubic term is not sufficient to describe the stable
large-amplitude branch.

We now assume
\lambda^{\prime}\!\left(K_{c}\right)>0
and

\Lambda_{3}<0<\Lambda_{5}
. This is the subcritical situation selected by
Appendix
E
when
\chi\left(M\right)\neq 0
and the quintic term
stabilizes the large-amplitude branch. For nontrivial equilibria, Eq.
(
123
) reduces to

\displaystyle\lambda\left(K\right)+\Lambda_{3}x+\Lambda_{5}x^{2}=0,\qquad x:=a^{2}>0.

(127)

Therefore the two nonzero branches are

\displaystyle x_{\pm}\left(K\right)=\frac{-\Lambda_{3}\pm\sqrt{\Lambda_{3}^{2}-4\Lambda_{5}\lambda\left(K\right)}}{2\Lambda_{5}}.

(128)

The reduced dynamics in Appendix
F
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}a=-\Psi_{5}\left(a;K\right)+O\left(a^{7}\right)+O\left(\left|K-K_{c}\right|a^{3}\right).

(129)

Linearizing Eq. (
129
) along a nonzero branch gives

\displaystyle\partial_{a}\Psi_{5}\left(a;K\right)=2a^{2}\left(\Lambda_{3}+2\Lambda_{5}a^{2}\right)+O\left(a^{6}\right).

(130)

Hence the branch
x_{+}\left(K\right)
is locally stable and the branch

x_{-}\left(K\right)
is locally unstable at leading order.

The saddle-node point is determined by

\Lambda_{3}+2\Lambda_{5}a^{2}=0
. Therefore

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{sn}}=-\frac{\Lambda_{3}}{2\Lambda_{5}},\qquad\lambda_{\mathrm{sn}}=\frac{\Lambda_{3}^{2}}{4\Lambda_{5}}.

(131)

Using Eq. (
88
), we obtain the leading-order spinodal location

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{sn}}-K_{c}\sim\frac{\Lambda_{3}^{2}}{4\lambda^{\prime}\!\left(K_{c}\right)\Lambda_{5}}.

(132)

This quantity is the local hysteresis width:

\displaystyle\Delta K_{\mathrm{hys}}:=K_{\mathrm{sn}}-K_{c}\sim\frac{\Lambda_{3}^{2}}{4\lambda^{\prime}\!\left(K_{c}\right)\Lambda_{5}}.

(133)

At
K=K_{c}
, the stable large-amplitude branch satisfies

\displaystyle\left|a_{\mathrm{jump}}\right|=\sqrt{-\frac{\Lambda_{3}}{\Lambda_{5}}}.

(134)

This is the jump amplitude when the incoherent branch loses stability at

K=K_{c}
. At the saddle-node point, the synchronized branch disappears with
the smaller amplitude

\displaystyle\left|a_{\mathrm{sn}}\right|=\sqrt{-\frac{\Lambda_{3}}{2\Lambda_{5}}}.

(135)

Therefore, under the additional condition
\Lambda_{5}>0
, the topological sign
statement for
\Lambda_{3}
obtained in Appendix
E
extends to a
local description of the discontinuous regime: a non-zero Euler characteristic
selects
\Lambda_{3}<0
, while the positive quintic coefficient fixes the
width of the hysteresis window and the size of the order-parameter jump.

The same quintic normal form also refines the continuous regime. If

\Lambda_{3}>0
and
\lambda\left(K\right)<0
, the small supercritical branch
of Eq. (
127
) satisfies

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{cont}}\left(K\right)=-\frac{\lambda\left(K\right)}{\Lambda_{3}}-\frac{\Lambda_{5}}{\Lambda_{3}^{3}}\lambda\left(K\right)^{2}+O\left(\lambda\left(K\right)^{3}\right).

(136)

Therefore
\Lambda_{5}
gives the first higher-order correction to the
continuous branch. It does not change the generic mean-field exponents derived
in Appendix
F
. Those exponents are still controlled by the cubic
term. The role of
\Lambda_{5}
becomes leading only when
\Lambda_{3}=0
.
If that codimension-two situation occurs and
\Lambda_{5}>0
, the transition
is still continuous, but the scaling changes to

\displaystyle\left|a_{\ast}\right|\sim\left(-\frac{\lambda\left(K\right)}{\Lambda_{5}}\right)^{1/4},\qquad\left|a\right|\sim\left|\frac{h}{\Lambda_{5}}\right|^{1/5}.

(137)

This is the usual tricritical scaling. In the present theory, topology does
not force this tricritical point. Such a point can only arise when the cubic
coefficient is not already fixed to a definite nonzero sign.

Appendix H
Critical defect count and charge balance

In this appendix, we record the counting argument used in
Subsec.
III.4
. In the main text, the field

u_{c}
is read as the onset texture, its isolated zeros are the defect cores,
and the local index is the corresponding defect charge. Let

p_{1},\ldots,p_{N_{\mathrm{def}}}
be the isolated zeros of the critical
tangent field
u_{c}
. The Poincaré-Hopf theorem
56
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{\mathrm{def}}}\operatorname{ind}_{p_{\ell}}\left(u_{c}\right)=\chi\left(M\right).

(138)

Assume that each defect core is nondegenerate. Then each local charge is equal
to
+1
or
-1
. Let
N_{+}
and
N_{-}
denote the numbers of positive and
negative defect cores. Eq. (
138
) then becomes

\displaystyle N_{+}-N_{-}=\chi\left(M\right).

(139)

Therefore the total number of simple critical defects satisfies

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{def}}=N_{+}+N_{-}\geq\left|\chi\left(M\right)\right|.

(140)

This is the generic counting statement used in the main text.

When
\chi\left(M\right)=0
, Eq. (
139
) gives
N_{+}=N_{-}
. Simple
critical defects must therefore be charge neutral as a set. Under smooth
parameter variation, the total index remains fixed, so simple defects can only
be created or annihilated through events with zero net topological charge.
When
\chi\left(M\right)\neq 0
, Eq. (
139
) shows that a nonzero
background charge remains in the onset texture. That net charge cannot be
removed by local charge-neutral defect events.

Appendix I
Topology-conditioned accessibility of local onset scenarios

In this appendix, we collect the logical consequences of
Appendices
E
-
G
. The statements below concern only
the local onset scenarios captured by the one-mode cubic and quintic
reductions stated there. They do not claim a classification of the full global
dynamics away from threshold. When symmetry produces a multidimensional
critical space, these statements apply after selecting an individual
symmetry-broken critical branch. The generic continuous branch in Appendix

F
requires

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}>0.

(141)

The tricritical continuous branch in Appendix
G
requires

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}=0,\qquad\Lambda_{5}>0.

(142)

The discontinuous branch with local hysteresis requires

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}<0,\qquad\Lambda_{5}>0.

(143)

Suppose that the sign condition of Appendix
E
holds. If

\chi\left(M\right)\neq 0
, then Appendix
E
gives

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}<0.

(144)

Eq. (
144
) does not allow Eq. (
141
) or Eq. (
142
) to occur
by topology. Therefore a non-zero Euler characteristic does not allow either
the generic continuous or the tricritical continuous onset scenario in the
present framework. Under the
additional analytic condition
\Lambda_{5}>0
, Eq. (
143
) remains as the
local scenario selected near onset.

If
\chi\left(M\right)=0
, the sign of
\Lambda_{3}
is not fixed by topology.
In that case topology allows Eq. (
141
), so a continuous onset is no
longer forbidden by topology. The same zero-Euler condition also allows Eq. (
142
) and
Eq. (
143
), so topology does not by itself select which local branch is
realized. Their actual realization is then decided by the analytic
coefficients of the reduced equation. This is the sense in which the topology
of
M
acts as an accessibility constraint on local onset scenarios.

Appendix J
Geometric averages for the case studies

In this appendix, we compute the averaged tangent projector used in
Sec.
IV
. These calculations verify the explicit values of

\kappa\left(M\right)
quoted there.

Hypersphere.

Let
M=S^{D-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
with the standard
embedding. The tangent projector at
\sigma\in S^{D-1}
is

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}=I_{D}-\sigma\sigma^{\top}.

(145)

Its average over the sphere is

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{S^{D-1}}:=\frac{1}{\textsf{Vol}\left(S^{D-1}\right)}\int_{S^{D-1}}\mathsf{P}_{\sigma}^{\perp}\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(\sigma\right).

(146)

Rotational invariance implies

\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{S^{D-1}}=c\,I_{D}
for some scalar
c
. Taking the trace
of Eq. (
146
) gives

\displaystyle cD=\operatorname{tr}\left(\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{S^{D-1}}\right)=D-1,

(147)

so

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{S^{D-1}}=\frac{D-1}{D}I_{D},\qquad\kappa\left(S^{D-1}\right)=\frac{D-1}{D}.

(148)

Flat torus.

Let

M=\mathbb{T}^{d}=\left(S^{1}\right)^{d}
with the standard product embedding

\displaystyle F\left(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d}\right)=\left(\cos\theta_{1},\sin\theta_{1},\ldots,\cos\theta_{d},\sin\theta_{d}\right).

(149)

The tangent projector is block diagonal,

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{\left(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d}\right)}^{\perp}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\mathsf{P}\left(\theta_{1}\right),\ldots,\mathsf{P}\left(\theta_{d}\right)\right).

(150)

where

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}\left(\theta\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\sin^{2}\theta&-\sin\theta\cos\theta\\
-\sin\theta\cos\theta&\cos^{2}\theta\end{pmatrix}.

(151)

Averaging Eq. (
151
) over one circle gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathsf{P}\left(\theta\right)\,\mathsf{d}\theta=\frac{1}{2}I_{2}.

(152)

Therefore

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}=\frac{1}{2}I_{2d},\qquad\kappa\left(\mathbb{T}^{d}\right)=\frac{1}{2}.

(153)

Real Stiefel manifold.

Let

M=\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
with

2\leq p<n
, equipped with the canonical Frobenius metric. For

X\in\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)
and
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, the
orthogonal projection onto

T_{X}\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)=\left\{\Xi:X^{\mathsf{T}}\Xi+\Xi^{\mathsf{T}}X=0\right\}
is

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{X}^{\perp}\left(Z\right)=Z-X\,\operatorname{sym}\left(X^{\mathsf{T}}Z\right)=Z-\frac{1}{2}X\left(X^{\mathsf{T}}Z+Z^{\mathsf{T}}X\right).

(154)

Let

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)}:=\frac{1}{\textsf{Vol}\left(\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)\right)}\int_{\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)}\mathsf{P}_{X}^{\perp}\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(X\right).

(155)

The canonical left-right action of

\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)\times\mathrm{SO}\left(p\right)
on

\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
implies

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)}\left(Q_{1}ZR\right)=Q_{1}\,\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)}\left(Z\right)\,R

(156)

for all
Q_{1}\in\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)
and

R\in\mathrm{SO}\left(p\right)
. Therefore

\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)}
commutes with the standard
tensor-product action of
\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)\times\mathrm{SO}\left(p\right)

on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
. By Schur’s lemma, it is therefore a scalar
multiple of the identity on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
. Since each tangent projector has
trace

np-\frac{p\left(p+1\right)}{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)}=\frac{2n-p-1}{2n}I_{np},\qquad\kappa\left(\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)\right)=\frac{2n-p-1}{2n}.

(157)

Rotation group.

Let
M=\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)
with the
canonical embedding in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
equipped with the Frobenius
inner product. For
R\in\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)
and

Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the orthogonal projection onto

T_{R}\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)=R\mathfrak{so}\left(n\right)
is

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{R}^{\perp}\left(Z\right)=R\,\operatorname{skew}\left(R^{\mathsf{T}}Z\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(Z-RZ^{\mathsf{T}}R\right).

(158)

Let

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)}:=\frac{1}{\textsf{Vol}\left(\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)\right)}\int_{\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)}\mathsf{P}_{R}^{\perp}\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(R\right).

(159)

Bi-invariance of the Haar measure gives

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)}\left(Q_{1}ZQ_{2}\right)=Q_{1}\,\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)}\left(Z\right)\,Q_{2}

(160)

for all
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)
. Therefore

\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)}
commutes with the left-right
action of
\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)\times\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)
on

\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. By the real form of Schur’s lemma,

\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)}
is therefore a scalar
multiple of the identity on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Since each tangent projector has trace

n\left(n-1\right)/2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)}=\frac{n-1}{2n}I_{n^{2}},\qquad\kappa\left(\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)\right)=\frac{n-1}{2n}.

(161)

Unitary group.

Let
M=\mathrm{U}\left(d\right)
with the canonical
embedding in
\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2d^{2}}
, equipped with
the real Frobenius inner product

\langle X,Y\rangle=\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{tr}\left(X^{\dagger}Y\right)
.
For
U\in\mathrm{U}\left(d\right)
and
Z\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, the
orthogonal projection onto

T_{U}\mathrm{U}\left(d\right)=U\mathfrak{u}\left(d\right)
is

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{U}^{\perp}\left(Z\right)=U\,\operatorname{skew}\left(U^{\dagger}Z\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(Z-UZ^{\dagger}U\right).

(162)

Let

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{U}\left(d\right)}:=\frac{1}{\textsf{Vol}\left(\mathrm{U}\left(d\right)\right)}\int_{\mathrm{U}\left(d\right)}\mathsf{P}_{U}^{\perp}\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(U\right).

(163)

For any phase
\lambda\in\mathrm{U}\left(1\right)\subset\mathrm{U}\left(d\right)
,
left invariance of the Haar measure gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{U}\left(d\right)}UZ^{\dagger}U\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(U\right)=\lambda^{2}\int_{\mathrm{U}\left(d\right)}UZ^{\dagger}U\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(U\right).

(164)

Choosing
\lambda
with
\lambda^{2}\neq 1
shows that the integral in
Eq. (
164
) vanishes. Therefore

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{U}\left(d\right)}=\frac{1}{2}I_{2d^{2}},\qquad\kappa\left(\mathrm{U}\left(d\right)\right)=\frac{1}{2}.

(165)

Special unitary group.

For
M=\mathrm{SU}\left(d\right)
, the
tangent space consists of traceless skew-Hermitian directions. The canonical
projection is therefore obtained from Eq. (
162
) by removing the trace
component of
U^{\dagger}Z
before applying the left translation by
U
. In the
canonical embedding into
\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, the averaged tangent
projector then separates the trace direction from its orthogonal complement.
For this reason, the linear response is described by a sector-dependent
operator rather than by a single scalar coefficient on the full ambient space.
The special case
\mathrm{SU}\left(2\right)\cong S^{3}
is already covered by
the hypersphere case.

Complex Grassmannian.

Let

M=\mathrm{Gr}_{k}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
with
1\leq k\leq n-1
. We
represent a point of
M
by the rank-
k
Hermitian projector
P
onto the
corresponding
k
-plane, and we use the centered projector embedding

F\left(P\right)=P-\frac{k}{n}I_{n}
into the real vector space of traceless
Hermitian matrices. For a traceless Hermitian matrix
Z
, the orthogonal
projection onto the tangent space of the conjugacy orbit through
P
is

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{P}^{\perp}\left(Z\right)=PZ+ZP-2PZP.

(166)

Let

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{Gr}_{k}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)}:=\frac{1}{\textsf{Vol}\left(\mathrm{Gr}_{k}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)\right)}\int_{\mathrm{Gr}_{k}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)}\mathsf{P}_{P}^{\perp}\,\mathsf{d}\mu\left(P\right).

(167)

The
\mathrm{U}\left(n\right)
-invariance of the Haar measure implies that

\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{Gr}_{k}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)}
commutes
with conjugation on the traceless Hermitian sector. Therefore it is a scalar
multiple of the identity on that sector. Since the real dimension of

\mathrm{Gr}_{k}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
is
2k\left(n-k\right)
and the
ambient real dimension is
n^{2}-1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathrm{Gr}_{k}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)}=\frac{2k\left(n-k\right)}{n^{2}-1}I_{n^{2}-1},\qquad\kappa\left(\mathrm{Gr}_{k}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)\right)=\frac{2k\left(n-k\right)}{n^{2}-1}.

(168)

Complex projective space.

Since

\mathbb{CP}^{m}=\mathrm{Gr}_{1}\left(\mathbb{C}^{m+1}\right)
, Eq.
(
168
) gives

\displaystyle\kappa\left(\mathbb{CP}^{m}\right)=\frac{2}{m+2}.

(169)

Equal-sphere product.

Let

M=\left(S^{m}\right)^{q}\subset\left(\mathbb{R}^{m+1}\right)^{q}=\mathbb{R}^{q\left(m+1\right)}
with the product embedding

F\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}\right)=\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}\right)
. The tangent
projector at
\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}\right)
is

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}\right)}^{\perp}=\operatorname{diag}\left(I_{m+1}-x_{1}x_{1}^{\top},\ldots,I_{m+1}-x_{q}x_{q}^{\top}\right).

(170)

Using the sphere computation from Eqs. (
145
)-(
148
) on each factor,
we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\left(S^{m}\right)^{q}}=\frac{m}{m+1}I_{q\left(m+1\right)},

(171)

and therefore

\displaystyle\kappa\left(\left(S^{m}\right)^{q}\right)=\frac{m}{m+1}.

(172)

In particular,

\displaystyle\kappa\left(S^{2m}\times S^{2m}\right)=\frac{2m}{2m+1}.

(173)

Appendix K
Topological data and onset-texture consequences for the case studies

In this appendix, we collect the Euler characteristics and the corresponding
defect-counting consequences used in Sec.
IV
. These
statements combine the standard Euler-characteristic identities with the
counting result of Appendix
H

56
;
35
;
34
.

Hypersphere.

For
M=S^{D-1}
, one has
35

\displaystyle\chi\left(S^{D-1}\right)=1+(-1)^{D-1}.

(174)

Hence

\displaystyle\chi\left(S^{D-1}\right)=\begin{cases}2,&D\;\text{odd},\\
0,&D\;\text{even}.\end{cases}

(175)

When
D
is odd, Appendix
H
gives net defect charge
2
and

N_{\mathrm{def}}\geq 2
on a generic branch. When
D
is even, a smooth
defect-free onset texture is topologically allowed, and any simple defects must
be charge neutral as a set.

Flat torus.

For
M=\mathbb{T}^{d}=\left(S^{1}\right)^{d}
, one has
35

\displaystyle\chi\left(\mathbb{T}^{d}\right)=\chi\left(S^{1}\right)^{d}=0.

(176)

Therefore a smooth defect-free onset texture is topologically allowed. If
simple defects are present, Appendix
H
gives zero net charge.

Real Stiefel manifold.

For

M=\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)
with
2\leq p<n
, the projection onto the first
vector gives a fiber bundle
35
;
18

\displaystyle\mathrm{St}\left(p-1,n-1\right)\longrightarrow\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)\longrightarrow S^{n-1}.

(177)

The multiplicativity of the Euler characteristic for compact fiber bundles
35

therefore gives

\displaystyle\chi\left(\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)\right)=\prod_{j=0}^{p-1}\left(1+(-1)^{n-1-j}\right).

(178)

For every
2\leq p<n
, this product contains a zero factor. Hence

\displaystyle\chi\left(\mathrm{St}\left(p,n\right)\right)=0,\qquad 2\leq p<n.

(179)

Therefore a smooth defect-free onset texture is topologically allowed on every
such real Stiefel manifold. If simple defects are present, Appendix

H
gives zero net charge.

Rotation and unitary groups.

Every positive-dimensional compact
connected Lie group admits a nowhere-vanishing left-invariant vector field.
Therefore Poincaré-Hopf gives
32
;
56

\displaystyle\chi\left(\mathrm{SO}\left(n\right)\right)=0,\qquad\chi\left(\mathrm{U}\left(d\right)\right)=0,\qquad\chi\left(\mathrm{SU}\left(d\right)\right)=0.

(180)

Hence both the rotation-group and unitary-group cases allow a smooth
defect-free onset texture. The same is true for
\mathrm{SU}\left(d\right)
.
If simple defects are present, Appendix
H
gives zero net charge
in all three cases.

Equal-sphere product.

For
M=\left(S^{m}\right)^{q}
, one has
35

\displaystyle\chi\left(\left(S^{m}\right)^{q}\right)=\left(1+(-1)^{m}\right)^{q}.

(181)

If
m
is odd, then
\chi\left(\left(S^{m}\right)^{q}\right)=0
. A smooth
defect-free onset texture is then topologically allowed, and any simple
defects must be charge neutral as a set. If
m
is even, then

\chi\left(\left(S^{m}\right)^{q}\right)=2^{q}
. Appendix
H

then gives

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{def}}\geq 2^{q}.

(182)

In particular, every manifold of the form
S^{2m}\times S^{2m}
has
\chi=4

and therefore carries at least four simple defect cores on a generic branch.

Complex Grassmannian.

The complex Grassmannian

\mathrm{Gr}_{k}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
admits a Schubert-cell
decomposition with one cell for each
k
-element subset of
34

\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
. Every Schubert cell has even real dimension.
Therefore the Euler characteristic equals the number of Schubert cells:

\displaystyle\chi\left(\mathrm{Gr}_{k}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)\right)=\binom{n}{k}.

(183)

Appendix
H
then gives

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{def}}\geq\binom{n}{k}.

(184)

Hence the onset texture on
\mathrm{Gr}_{k}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
must
carry net defect charge
\binom{n}{k}
and, on a generic branch, at least

\binom{n}{k}
simple defect cores.

Complex projective space.

Since
34

\mathbb{CP}^{m}=\mathrm{Gr}_{1}\left(\mathbb{C}^{m+1}\right)
, Eq.
(
183
) gives

\displaystyle\chi\left(\mathbb{CP}^{m}\right)=m+1.

(185)

Appendix
H
then gives

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{def}}\geq m+1.

(186)

Hence the onset texture on
\mathbb{CP}^{m}
must carry net defect charge

m+1
and, on a generic branch, at least
m+1
simple defect cores.
