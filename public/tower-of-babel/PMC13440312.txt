Parasites constrain within-population diversification while accelerating between-population divergence in an RNA replication system
Authors: Yuki Kanai, Norikazu Ichihashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiversification of replicators from simple self-replicating RNA molecules is a major challenge in prebiotic evolution. In our previous evolution experiment using an RNA replication system derived from the Qβ phage, a single RNA species spontaneously diversified into multiple coexisting host and parasite RNA lineages through arms race dynamics, suggesting that host–parasite interactions may promote diversification in molecular replicator ecosystems. To test this idea more directly, we evolved six independent RNA populations under high-parasite and low-parasite conditions. High-parasite populations showed accelerated mutation fixation and greater sequence divergence between populations. However, contrary to expectation, host diversification within populations was limited under high-parasite conditions, whereas transient diversification into at most three host lineages occurred under low-parasite conditions. Sequence and biochemical analyses indicate that this diversification was consistent with relaxed selection among host RNAs that achieved similar replication yields under compartmentalized replication. In contrast, coexistence with parasites helped maintain high replication capacities by continuously imposing selection for improved replication. These results suggest that antagonistic host–parasite interactions help maintain functional activity and promote divergence between populations while constraining diversification within populations. Such opposing effects may have shaped the balance between functional optimization and diversification during early molecular evolution.

Introduction

A central question in the origin of life is how simple self-replicating molecules capable of Darwinian evolution, likely RNA, accumulated information and ultimately gave rise to cellular life (Gilbert 1986; Szathmáry and Smith 1995; Joyce 2002; Higgs and Lehman 2015; Adamski et al. 2020). It has been proposed that, beginning from a minimal replicator, an intermediate stage consisting of an ecosystem of cooperating replicators emerged, eventually enabling the formation of multigene genomes (Eigen and Schuster 1977; Szathmáry and Smith 1995). Such ecosystems likely involved the diversification and coexistence of multiple replicator lineages, which together can maintain and propagate more information than any single molecule. Numerous theoretical studies have addressed how information might accumulate and be maintained in such molecular ecosystems (Eigen et al. 1980; Szathmáry and Demeter 1987; Kaneko and Yomo 2002; Takeuchi and Hogeweg 2012), yet direct experimental validation of these hypotheses remains limited.

A potential driver of increasing complexity is the coevolution between active replicators and their parasitic derivatives, which arise spontaneously in self-replicating systems (Takeuchi and Hogeweg 2008; Colizzi and Hogeweg 2016; Hickinbotham et al. 2021). These parasitic molecules lack catalytic activity but replicate efficiently by exploiting enzymes encoded by active replicators (Mills et al. 1967; Bansho et al. 2016; Matsumura et al. 2016; Koonin et al. 2017). For consistency with our previous work (Bansho et al. 2016), we use the terms “host” for enzyme-encoding active RNA replicators and “parasite” for RNA replicators that exploit host enzymes; these labels reflect functional ecological roles and carry no implication of organismal or cellular biology. In well-mixed environments, parasites typically outcompete hosts, leading systems to collapse. However, when dispersal is limited, such as on mineral surfaces or within compartments, parasite spread can be locally constrained (Takeuchi and Hogeweg 2009; Mizuuchi and Ichihashi 2021). Under such spatial structures, hosts and parasites can coexist. Parasites can impose directional selection for resistance, potentially accelerating mutation fixation through antagonistic “arms-race” dynamics (Furubayashi et al. 2020). In addition, parasites can impose negative frequency-dependent selection, and resistance to parasites can create new ecological niches, allowing multiple host lineages to coexist that might otherwise be competitively excluded (Takeuchi and Hogeweg 2008; Hickinbotham et al. 2021; Buckingham and Ashby 2022; Kamiura et al. 2022).

Although much of our current understanding derives from theory, experimental RNA replication systems exhibiting host–parasite coevolution have been established (Bansho et al. 2016; Matsumura et al. 2016). Qβ replicase-based systems have long served as experimentally tractable models for studying RNA replication and coevolution among RNA species, although they depend on protein-based replication rather than ribozyme catalysis (Mills et al. 1967; Matsumura et al. 2016; Mizuuchi et al. 2022). In our previous work, we sustained host–parasite coevolution in RNA derived from Qβ phage replicating within water-in-oil emulsions (Bansho et al. 2016; Furubayashi et al. 2020). This system ultimately transitioned from a single-gene replicator to a coexistence of three host and two parasite lineages (Mizuuchi et al. 2022), providing an experimental model to investigate diversification in molecular replicator ecosystems. However, whether parasite-driven coevolution promotes host lineage diversification remains unresolved.

Here, we experimentally dissect the role of parasites in host diversification by evolving self-replicating RNA under two ecological regimes. One condition reproduced the previously studied high-parasite condition (Mizuuchi et al. 2022), whereas the other imposed a low-parasite regime. Starting from the same ancestral RNA, we propagated three independent populations per condition for 200 transfer rounds and quantified diversification at the sequence level throughout the evolution experiment.

High-parasite populations exhibited faster mutation fixation, consistent with stronger antagonistic selection. Because arms-race dynamics led to the sequential emergence of new host sequences in our previous study (Furubayashi et al. 2020), we expected high-parasite conditions to accumulate higher diversity. Unexpectedly, greater within-population sequence diversification emerged in low-parasite populations, indicating that stronger interactions with parasites do not necessarily promote diversification and may instead limit diversification in early molecular replicator systems.

Results

RNA replication system with host–parasite interactions

The replication system used in this study is based on an RNA derived from the genome of Qβ phage with minimal components required for replication in a reconstituted translation system (Shimizu et al. 2001; Ichihashi et al. 2013) (Fig. 1a, left). The RNA replicates via the replicase it encodes, and replication introduces mutations, generating genetic variants that compete under selection. Recombination events also produce shorter RNA molecules that lack the replicase gene but retain the sequences required for replication by replicase translated from intact RNAs. We refer to these shorter, replication-competent but nonencoding molecules as parasites, and to the replicase-encoding RNAs as hosts.

Evolution of self-replicating RNA under high- and low-parasite conditions. a) Overview of the experimental system. The ancestral host RNA is replicated by the Qβ replicase that it encodes. Replication generates mutants, including shorter RNAs that lack the replicase gene and act as parasites, exploiting the enzymes encoded by the host RNA. The reaction system is compartmentalized into water-in-oil droplets, incubated, diluted, and mixed with new oil and translation system towards the next round of incubation. b) Expected population dynamics under the two conditions. Under high-parasite conditions (constant dilution), populations exhibit Lotka–Volterra-like oscillatory dynamics. Under low-parasite conditions, strong dilution reduces co-encapsulation of hosts and parasites, limiting parasite proliferation. H and P denote host and parasite RNAs, respectively. Thickness of letters (H and P) indicates replication efficiency: thin black (no replication), thin colored (weak replication), and thick colored (efficient replication).

Because parasitic RNAs are shorter, they replicate more efficiently and rapidly dominate bulk reactions, eventually leading to host extinction through the depletion of replicase, but spatial structure can prevent this collapse (Bansho et al. 2016) (Fig. 1a, right). In each round, 10 μL of reaction mixture was emulsified in 1 mL of buffer-saturated oil, producing approximately 109 droplets with ∼1 µm diameters (Bansho et al. 2016). With this size, approximately one molecule is distributed for every 1 nM of RNA. After 5 h of incubation, a fraction of the emulsion was diluted with fresh translation system and re-emulsified by vigorous homogenization, initiating the next round.

Under the previously used 5-fold dilution scheme, where we achieved diversification into five species (Mizuuchi et al. 2022), host and parasite populations exhibit oscillatory dynamics resembling Lotka–Volterra interactions (Bansho et al. 2016) (Fig. 1b, left): (i) Hosts initially replicate in parasite-free compartments; (ii) as host concentration increases, hosts and parasites increasingly co-occupy droplets, allowing parasite amplification; (iii) parasites subsequently dominate replication due to their shorter length and efficient replication, suppressing host growth; (iv) reduced host abundance then limits parasite replication, returning the system to a low-host, low-parasite state and completing the cycle.

Evolution under high-parasite and low-parasite conditions

Starting from a homogeneous N96 host RNA population previously adapted to the translation system (Ichihashi et al. 2013), we evolved six independent populations under two dilution regimes designed to generate high-parasite and low-parasite ecological conditions (Table 1).

As a comparison, we propagated three independent RNA populations under low-parasite conditions (named L1 to L3 for “Low”) by increasing the dilution rate to constrain the system to initiate replication from a low-host, low-parasite state (1) (Fig. 1b, right). In this state, individual host molecules are largely segregated into distinct compartments, reducing the probability of parasites being replicated by replicases translated from co-encapsulated hosts. To keep this state, RNA concentrations were quantified after every three transfers by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR). Based on the RNA concentration, dilution rates were adjusted, aiming for sub-0.1 nM concentrations at the start of incubation. Dilution factors were initially ∼1,000-fold and eventually stabilized around 100-fold (Fig. 2, Figure S1c).

To verify reduced parasite proliferation, RNA was extracted from emulsified droplets and analyzed by native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Figure S3). Clear parasite-derived bands were detected at multiple time points in high-parasite populations (H1 to H3), whereas in low-parasite populations (L1 to L3), such bands were observed only once (in L3 at round 190), indicating that parasite abundance was substantially reduced under the low-parasite regime.

Because dilution was stronger in the low-parasite condition, hosts underwent more replications per round (Fig. 2). Based on concentration increases during incubation, hosts experienced an estimated mean of 1,339 generations under low-parasite conditions compared with 461 generations under high-parasite conditions (2.9-fold difference).

Independent evolutionary trajectories across replicate populations

To characterize evolutionary dynamics, RNA populations from each evolutionary line were reverse-transcribed and sequenced using long-read nanopore sequencing. Sampling spanned the full 200 rounds of transfer (Table S1). Because shorter RNAs amplify more efficiently during PCR, host and parasitic RNAs were size-selected by gel extraction prior to sequencing. To reduce errors introduced during reverse transcription and long-read sequencing, reads were clustered to construct consensus haplotypes, and haplotype frequencies were estimated based on the clustering (Figures S4 to S7). In addition, mutational analyses were restricted to variations reaching ≥10% frequency in at least one sampled round. Under this criterion, 66 and 95 host mutations were identified in high-parasite and low-parasite conditions, respectively, and 89 and 90 parasite mutations were identified in the two conditions (Figures S8 and S9). Two-dimensional sequence maps and phylogenetic trees derived from consensus haplotypes are shown in Figs. 3, S11, and S12.

Each population accumulated largely distinct sets of host mutations over time (Fig. 3a; Figures S11, S13 to S15), indicating independent evolutionary trajectories rather than convergence to a single dominant lineage. Nevertheless, some mutations were shared across conditions (Figures S14a and S15a) and populations (Figures S14b and S15b), suggesting the action of positive selection.

Host diversity was higher under the low-parasite condition

Next, we compared the sequence diversity in each population to assess how parasite richness influences RNA diversity. Unexpectedly, the mean nucleotide diversity (π) between rounds 100 and 200 was 3.3 × 10−3 ± 6.3 × 10−4 in low-parasite populations, approximately double the value (1.8 × 10−3 ± 6.7 × 10−4 [mean ± SE]) in high-parasite populations, implying higher within-population diversification under low-parasite conditions (Figure S16).

While π captures overall sequence variation, it does not distinguish between populations harboring multiple distinct lineages and those with variation diffusing around a single genotype. To further confirm the unexpected finding, we aimed to resolve how many coexisting lineages contributed to this diversity. This was estimated using a threshold extension of the Hill number framework widely used in community ecology (Ricotta and Feoli 2024) (Fig. 4a). We focused primarily on order 0, corresponding to species richness (see Methods). In this analysis, two sequences were considered distinct species if they differed by at least a threshold distance d (here, 5 bp differences). Raw species number estimates were obtained as the mean of bootstrap subsamples from frequency trajectories smoothed to account for transient losses of haplotypes (see Methods). To further reduce noise, diversity was assessed as the median of three consecutive estimates calculated at 10-round intervals (Fig. 4b and c; raw values in Figure S17a). This approach reproduced the previously reported coexistence of three host and two parasite species around round 200 (Mizuuchi et al. 2022), supporting the validity of the metric (Fig. 4b). Although absolute species number estimates varied across methods, relative relationships between conditions and between host and parasite species numbers were preserved across alternative sampling schemes (Figure S17b), distance thresholds (Figure S18), higher-order Hill numbers (Figure S19), and sequence-based metrics (Figure S20).

Applying this metric to our data also supported that the host species number reached higher values under low-parasite conditions than under high-parasite conditions (Fig. 4c and d). Under high-parasite conditions, host diversity remained limited throughout the experiment: median species numbers stayed near 1 in population H2, and only transient two-species coexistence was observed in H1 (around round 150) and H3 (after round 180). In contrast, all three low-parasite populations sustained coexistence of approximately two or more host species for at least 50 rounds, with population L2 exceeding three species between rounds 140 and 160. However, this diversity eventually collapsed, and all low-parasite populations ultimately converged to a single dominant host genotype.

Because species number estimates fluctuated during evolution, we summarized diversity using median species numbers between rounds 100 and 200, when regime-specific dynamics had likely emerged (Fig. 4d). Low-parasite populations consistently maintained higher host diversity (medians of 1.7, 2.3, and 1.8 for L1, L2, and L3, respectively) than high-parasite populations (1.6, 1.0, and 1.2 for H1, H2, and H3, respectively). For comparison, our previous study under high-parasite conditions achieved a median of 2.6 host species, exceeding any population observed here (Fig. 4d).

The higher dilution rate in low-parasite conditions resulted in approximately 3-fold more host generations, providing more opportunities for diversification. However, elevated host species number was already evident within the first 70 rounds in populations L1 and L2, exceeding levels observed at any point during 200 rounds under high-parasite conditions.

Changes in host and parasite species numbers were linked. Median species numbers generally followed a clockwise trajectory in species-number space, with increases in host numbers occurring only when parasite diversity was low, followed by subsequent increases in parasite diversity (Fig. 4c). A similar trajectory was observed in our previous study, where host diversification also occurred (Fig. 4b). In addition, to examine the relationship between maximal host and parasite species numbers, we compared the 90th percentile species numbers across populations (Fig. 4e). Within low-parasite conditions, host and parasite species numbers were positively associated. In contrast, under high-parasite conditions, parasite species number was negatively associated with host species number.

Mutation accumulation and substitution patterns

To investigate mutational processes underlying the unexpectedly higher host diversity under low-parasite conditions, we analyzed mutation accumulation rates and substitution patterns in the evolving populations. Analyses were restricted to samples collected between rounds 100 and 200, when regime-specific evolutionary dynamics had likely emerged.

First, to compare the speed of mutation accumulation, we examined the total number of mutations per host read. At the end of evolution, this was similar between conditions (mean of rounds 180 to 200; High: 12.6 ± 2.8 vs. Low: 10.4 ± 1.1, mean ± SE). Here, mutations per read represent mutations weighted by their relative abundance in the population. However, because host RNAs underwent approximately 3-fold more replication events under low-parasite conditions, normalizing per host replication revealed a significantly higher mutation accumulation rate in high-parasite populations (1.3 × 10−5 ± 2.2 × 10−6 per base pair per host replication) than in low-parasite populations (3.7 × 10−6 ± 3.1 × 10−7; Fig. 5a; Figure S21a), corresponding to an approximately 3-fold increase.

Next, to assess differences in selection pressures, we compared patterns of nonsynonymous and synonymous substitutions. Despite comparable mutation counts per read, hosts in high-parasite conditions accumulated fewer nonsynonymous mutations (Figure S13b); notably, in population H2 no nonsynonymous mutations were detected after round 62 (Figure S8). Consistent with this pattern, mean dN/dS values during rounds 100 to 200 were lower under high-parasite conditions (0.06 ± 0.04) than under low-parasite conditions (0.15 ± 0.01) (Fig. 5b; Figure S21b).

Together, it appears that high-parasite populations experienced more rapid sequence fixation per replication while maintaining stronger purifying selection on protein sequence, whereas low-parasite populations accumulated mutations more slowly but experienced relaxed selection pressure.

In our previous study (Mizuuchi et al. 2022), mutations accumulated faster, with a median rate of 2.3 × 10−5 per base pair per host replication between rounds 100 and 200, indicating faster fixation of mutations, and the population-averaged dN/dS values had a substantially higher median over the same period (0.76), indicating qualitatively different evolutionary dynamics despite identical experimental design (Fig. 5; Figure S21).

Compartment-mediated coexistence under low-parasite conditions

These patterns suggest a scenario in which compartment-mediated host segregation promotes coexistence of multiple host species within a single population (Fig. 6a). Under low dilution (high-parasite conditions), multiple host genotypes are often encapsulated within the same compartments, where they directly compete for resources and replicase. Such direct competition likely favors higher replication rates, imposes stronger purifying selection, and limits the stable coexistence of different hosts (Fig. 6a, left).

Significance: Linear mixed-effects model, P = 0.034. c) The same RNAs were incubated in a nonemulsified reaction mixture, and their replication rates were measured over the first 30 min of incubation. In the low-parasite condition, host molecules were effectively segregated into separate compartments (Fig. 6a, right). In the absence of competition within compartments, selection acts primarily on total yield within the incubation period rather than on relative growth rate. If replication rates already suffice to approach carrying capacity within the incubation time, further increases in replication speed confer little advantage, relaxing selection. Mutations with small fitness effects may then behave neutrally, permitting the coexistence of multiple host species.

This model makes several predictions: (i) yields of species that evolved under low-parasite conditions, when assayed independently, should converge across coexisting species and populations, as they were constrained by the carrying capacity of compartments; (ii) in contrast, yields of species that evolved under high-parasite conditions, when assayed independently, need not be similarly constrained; and (iii) under low-parasite conditions, this constraint should allow coexisting host RNAs to exhibit different replication rates.

To test the first two predictions, we synthesized representative sequences, including those from rounds showing peak diversity in the low-parasite condition (consensus sequences marked in Figure S12), by in vitro transcription. To analyze the yield starting from one molecule per compartment, reaction mixtures with 0.1 nM of each RNA were emulsified and incubated for 5 h (Fig. 6b). At this concentration, RNA molecules are expected to segregate into independent compartments. Under low-parasite conditions, no significant differences in final yield were detected among coexisting host genotypes, consistent with convergence of replication yield under compartmental constraints (i). In contrast, such convergence was not observed under high-parasite conditions (ii).

To test the third prediction regarding replication rates, we aimed to measure the initial replication speed. Because emulsification introduced substantial noise, RNA was incubated without emulsification for 30 min, starting from 5 nM of each RNA (Fig. 6c). In lines L1 and L3, the faster sequence replicated more than 1.5-fold faster than the slower sequence, consistent with the coexistence of lineages with distinct intrinsic replication rates. The addition of 5 nM parasite RNA to the same reaction mixtures did not produce a clear effect on replication rates (Figure S22).

Collectively, these results support the idea that relaxed selection under host segregation permits the neutral coexistence of host lineages with distinct replication properties.

Parasite suppression led to decreased replication yield during evolution

In the absence of parasites, host RNAs would be expected to experience direct selection for higher replication yield. Contrary to this expectation, under low-parasite conditions, the yields of evolved host RNAs were approximately 10-fold lower than those of high-parasite populations and the ancestral RNA (Fig. 6b). We hypothesized that incomplete mixing between rounds increased the effective initial copy number per occupied compartment before incubation (n0), as supported by simulation (Figure S23), limiting amplification to approximately K/n0 rather than the full carrying capacity K.

However, direct experimental estimation and control of n0 in this system are challenging. Incomplete mixing in this emulsion system has been experimentally confirmed using fluorescence measurements (Ledoux et al. 2026), but these measurements do not provide a direct estimate of n0. Although n0 could in principle be modulated by adjusting the intensity and duration of mixing, such changes would also alter the size and number of compartments, making direct experimental tests of evolutionary outcomes under different n0 values infeasible.

Thus, to evaluate our hypothesis, we computationally simulated the evolution of host replication rates under incomplete mixing. Starting from a population of host RNAs with identical replication rate (r0), RNA molecules were distributed across compartments with carrying capacity K = 1,000 such that each occupied compartment initially contained approximately n0 molecules. Molecules were then allowed to replicate, with mutants of different replication rates arising during replication. Assuming n0=20 and high starting replication rate r0=2.0, mean replication rates declined over time due to the accumulation of slower replicators (Fig. 6d), resembling the decline in fold amplification from ∼1,000-fold in early rounds to ∼100-fold in later rounds in our experiment (Fig. 2). Simulations initiated from different r0 values converged toward similar values (Fig. 6e), consistent with selection being relaxed once amplification became limited by compartment carrying capacity.

To simulate the biochemical assay, we calculated the yield after 5 h of incubation starting from 0.1 nM of the dominant RNA genotype obtained from the in-silico evolution (Fig. 6f). The predicted yield was ∼100 molecules per compartment—approximately one-tenth of the carrying capacity (K)—closely matching the experimental measurements (Fig. 6b). In contrast, under complete mixing (Figure S24), dominant genotypes reached yields approaching the carrying capacity (∼750 molecules per compartment). These results suggest that incomplete redistribution during evolution, which increases the effective initial copy number per compartment (n0), likely constrained amplification and drove convergence of replication yields below the carrying capacity.

Discussion

Diversification of self-replicators is considered a major step in the origin of life (Szathmáry and Smith 1995). In this study, we asked whether stronger host–parasite interactions promote diversification in an experimental RNA replication system (Figs. 1 and 2). High-parasite populations, which led to diversification in our previous study (Mizuuchi et al. 2022), exhibited accelerated fixation of mutations (Fig. 5a). However, unexpectedly, host diversification was greater under low-parasite conditions (Fig. 4c to e), as quantified using a threshold-based species counting metric (Fig. 4a). This diversification is consistent with neutral coexistence among multiple lineages under relaxed selection on replication yield (Fig. 6). These findings indicate that stronger antagonistic interactions do not necessarily promote diversification in early replicator systems.

The unexpected increase in host diversity under low-parasite conditions can be explained by coexistence under relaxed purifying selection (Fig. 6a). The ancestral host sequence evolved under a similar protocol but with 1-h transfers (Ichihashi et al. 2013), whereas in the present study, incubation was extended to 5 h, allowing replication to approach saturation under well-mixed conditions (Fig. 6b; Figure S25). Relaxed competition, consistent with the slightly elevated dN/dS values (Fig. 5b), likely permitted the accumulation of weakly deleterious mutations. Together with incomplete mixing between rounds (Fig. 6d and e), this process eventually produced host lineages with approximately 10-fold lower replication capacity (Fig. 6b). Although the lack of direct competition within compartments would itself slow selective sweeps and prolong coexistence, this alone does not account for the convergence of replication yields across lineages (Fig. 6b). Instead, these results indicate that compartment-imposed yield caps contributed to both the convergence of replication yields and the maintenance of coexistence.

These results suggest an alternative mode of coexistence despite high dilution rates. In our previous study, higher dilution rates disrupted the coexistence of multiple host species (Yukawa et al. 2026). Faster dilution intensified oscillations in Lotka–Volterra dynamics, increasing the likelihood that minor host populations went extinct during the low-abundance phase of the cycle. In contrast, the present study shows that maintaining segregation of host molecules can allow coexistence even under strong dilution.

In contrast, high-parasite conditions were characterized by accelerated mutation accumulation (Fig. 5a) together with lower dN/dS values (Fig. 5b). This pattern indicates rapid fixation of synonymous substitutions while nonsynonymous substitutions remained selectively constrained. Three factors may account for this. First, because this is an RNA-based system, selection may act at the level of RNA structure or translational regulation, consistent with the recurrent fixation of identical synonymous substitutions across independent populations (Figure S13). Second, prior adaptation for efficient host replication (Ichihashi et al. 2013) may have limited the scope for further amino acid-level improvement. Third, we cannot exclude that replicase fidelity itself differed between conditions, which would affect mutation supply independently of selection.

We did not observe host diversification in high-parasite populations, despite using conditions intended to replicate our previous study, which reported reproducible diversification (Mizuuchi et al. 2022). In that study, three replicates were initiated from a shared droplet mixture at round 74. This raises the possibility that genetic variants capable of later diversification were already present at low frequency prior to round 74. Consistently, the phylogenetic trees of those “independent” lines were more similar to one another than the truly independent replicates analyzed here, suggesting that early shared variation may have influenced their subsequent trajectories.

Consistently, sequence evolution also differed qualitatively between the two studies (Fig. 5; Figure S21). The previous experiment showed higher mutation accumulation rates and elevated dN/dS values, consistent with a stronger host–parasite arms race. These contrasts suggest that diversification in the earlier experiment may have depended on specific historical or genetic contexts not reproduced here, emphasizing the role of contingency in replicator diversification. Extended evolution under high-parasite conditions or identification of the genetic changes associated with diversification in the previous study may clarify the conditions under which diversification occurs.

Another unexpected result was that high-parasite populations retained higher replication yields after evolution compared to the low-parasite populations experiencing direct selection for yield (Fig. 6b). This pattern is consistent with stronger competition under high-parasite conditions (Fig. 6a). Although incomplete mixing likely reduced yields under both regimes (Fig. 6d to f), competition among RNA molecules, including competition with parasites, appears to have maintained selection for replication capacity. This indicates that antagonistic interactions can, at times, promote community functions.

Accelerated mutation accumulation under high-parasite conditions did not lead to increased host diversity within populations. Instead, parasite diversification appears to have restricted the coexistence of multiple host lineages, possibly by reducing the parasite-resistant sequence space and thereby limiting the emergence of new host lineages. Consistent with this interpretation, host species numbers under high-parasite conditions were higher in populations that retained lower parasite diversity (Fig. 4e). In contrast, under low-parasite conditions, maximal host and parasite species numbers were positively associated (Fig. 4e). However, species-count trajectories indicated that host diversification occurred preferentially when parasite species numbers were low (Fig. 4b and c). This likely reflects that, under the low-parasite conditions, host diversification proceeded largely independently of parasite dynamics, with parasite lineages subsequently adapting to different host lineages. Together, these observations suggest that parasite diversity does not necessarily promote the coexistence of multiple host lineages, contrary to our previous proposal (Kamiura et al. 2022; Mizuuchi et al. 2022). Similar phenomena have been observed in multi-phage–bacteria systems, where phage diversity drives selective sweeps that promote divergence among populations while limiting standing diversity within them (Betts et al. 2018).

Environmental conditions that promote host diversification are central to understanding how complexity may emerge from simple RNA replicators (Szilágyi et al. 2017). Each independently evolving population in our experiment can be viewed as a spatial patch containing many compartments within which hosts and parasites coevolve locally. If mixing between patches occurs intermittently, independently evolved lineages may encounter one another, creating opportunities for interaction or integration. From this perspective, parasite richness may enhance diversity by increasing divergence among patches, while episodic mixing could allow the integration of independently evolved variants. In contrast, strong segregation that permits neutral coexistence within patches may limit large-scale diversification if divergence between patches remains modest. Thus, our results suggest that the balance between within-patch segregation and between-patch mixing may shape how independently evolved replicators diversify and interact.

The low-parasite regime was introduced as a control to reduce parasite abundance rather than to mimic a specific natural environment (Fig. 1b). In this regime, host molecules remained segregated across compartments, but such segregation is unlikely to be stable under natural conditions. With constant dilution, segregation can be maintained only when net amplification per round approximately balances dilution: lower amplification leads to loss, whereas higher amplification allows genotypes to spread across compartments and enter direct competition.

In an origin-of-life context, however, sustained segregation might arise naturally through infection-like processes in protocell populations, as proposed in virus-first scenarios for the origin of cells (Forterre 2006; Koonin et al. 2006). If viruses are diluted or degraded more rapidly than protocells divide, coinfection events would be rare, and each infection would allow viral replication to approach saturation within an individual protocell. Under such conditions, multiple viral lineages with different replication kinetics could persist (Fig. 6c), maintained by the finite carrying capacity of individual protocells rather than by direct competitive exclusion (Fig. 6a).

Relatedly, although our experiment was designed as a model of RNA-protein world evolution, the dynamics observed here are conceptually related to coevolution between infectious viruses and their defective interfering particles (DIPs) (Vignuzzi and López 2019). DIPs rely on active viruses for replication, analogous to the parasites in our system, though viral systems involve additional complexities absent here, including immune responses, latent infection, and transmission dynamics among host organisms. Nevertheless, clear parallels exist: DIPs and active viruses generate oscillatory changes in their relative titers (Muthupandiyan and Yin 2026), as we observed between hosts and parasites in our system. Active viruses can evolve resistance to DIPs (DePolo et al. 1987), as in our system (Furubayashi et al. 2020; Yukawa et al. 2026). The von Magnus effect—the progressive loss of infectious viral yield during serial transfer at high viral concentrations (von Magnus 1951)—is similar to how uncompartmentalized passage leads to extinction in our system (Bansho et al. 2016).

A key determinant of DIP coevolution is the multiplicity of infection (MOI)—the average number of viral particles introduced per cell—because defective genomes must coinfect cells with active virus to replicate (Vignuzzi and López 2019). The corresponding quantity in our system is the number of RNA molecules introduced into each compartment, and how they were controlled was a major difference between the two conditions. In the low-parasite condition, we aimed for a multiplicity of 0.1 to minimize the probability that multiple active RNAs were co-encapsulated; however, incomplete mixing instead yielded a multiplicity of approximately 20. In the high-parasite condition, multiplicity was not controlled and likely fluctuated substantially as host and parasite concentrations oscillated (Fig. 2). Because parasites can reach an order of ∼10,000 molecules per compartment (Bansho et al. 2016), the multiplicity at its peak after 5-fold dilution likely reached ∼1,000, while falling to values similar to the low-parasite condition when parasite abundance was low.

Whereas most studies of DIPs have emphasized their effects on reducing viral yield or pathogenesis, our results highlight two potential evolutionary consequences: selection imposed by DIPs may maintain replication activity of active species, while also shaping their diversification through arms-race dynamics. First, mapping our results to viral systems and treating infected cells as analogous to compartments, if replication within cells is resource-limited such that efficiency differences are effectively neutral when the MOI is low, and co-infection with DIPs is rare, DIPs may paradoxically maintain selection for replication activity by imposing parasite pressure through co-infection. This is, however, unlikely in lytic systems, where faster replication up to the carrying capacity leads to faster transmission. Second, arms-race dynamics driven by parasite pressure may reduce diversity within individual infected hosts by driving fixation of the locally fittest active variant, while simultaneously promoting divergence of active species among host populations (Fig. 7). Whether defective replicators act not only as suppressors of replication but also as modulators of evolutionary adaptation and diversification remains an exciting open question.

Several limitations should be considered.

First, parasite concentration was reduced indirectly by altering the dilution rates rather than by directly manipulating the abundance of parasites. This was because parasites arise spontaneously at high frequency in this system, making their appearance difficult to control experimentally. This feature may also be ecologically relevant, given the prevalence of DIPs in natural viral populations. Nevertheless, dilution can affect not only parasite abundance and initial molecular occupancy, but also the time required for compartments to approach saturation, the number of replication events per cycle, and other within-compartment dynamics. Thus, the observed differences cannot be attributed exclusively to parasite abundance or to the number of molecules present before incubation. Future experiments using a transient compartmentalization scheme (Matsumura et al. 2016), in which host and parasite RNAs are separately purified, mixed at defined concentrations, and encapsulated into droplets of fixed size, would help decouple parasite abundance from the dilution regime and more directly test the causal role of parasites, although such experiments would be substantially more laborious.

Second, each ecological condition was represented by three independent populations. While qualitative patterns were consistent across replicates, the statistical power to detect subtle quantitative differences is inherently limited. Additional independent lines and extended rounds of transfers would improve the resolution of the differences.

Third, the phenotypic effects of individual mutations were not analyzed. Nonsynonymous mutations in the replicase-coding region may have altered replicase activity, specificity, or interactions with host and parasite RNAs; even synonymous mutations or mutations outside the coding region may have affected fitness through changes in RNA structure or template activity. With a single-gene genome, standard summary statistics such as dN/dS provide limited resolution for distinguishing adaptive from neutral evolution at the mechanistic level. Future genetic and biochemical analyses may identify the molecular basis of diversification; in particular, comparing mutations accumulated here with those from our previous study (Mizuuchi et al. 2022) may reveal the genetic changes specifically responsible.

Finally, although our system provides an experimentally tractable model of RNA replicator ecology, it relies on a cell-free system with high-fidelity ribosomes and protein-based replicase activity, rather than ribozyme-based replication thought to have operated in the RNA world (Higgs and Lehman 2015). Early replicator systems likely operated under higher mutation rates and may have relied on ribozyme-based catalysis. The balance between mutation, selection, and ecological structure may therefore differ in prebiotic contexts, and extrapolation to early evolution should be made with care.

Overall, antagonistic host–parasite interactions helped maintain replication capacity and accelerated mutation fixation, promoting divergence between populations but not diversification within populations (Fig. 7). In contrast, segregation under low-parasite conditions permitted the transient coexistence of multiple host lineages under relaxed purifying selection. Parasites may thus play a dual role: they accelerate divergence among populations while constraining diversity within them.

Genetic parasites were likely unavoidable in early replicator systems, particularly before stable cellular boundaries emerged and horizontal transfer between protocells was frequent (Iranzo et al. 2016). Interactions between replicators and their parasitic derivatives have been proposed as important drivers of molecular innovation, contributing to processes ranging from transposon-mediated gene reorganization (Kanai et al. 2022, 2023) to the emergence of ribosomal complexes from parasitic proto-ribosomal components (Lynch and Ellington 2026). Our results add an ecological dimension to this picture: the balance between diversity maintained within populations and divergence generated between populations depends critically on compartment structure, mixing, and dilution regimes. Understanding how ecological conditions modulate this balance—and how transient diversity can be converted into lasting functional innovation—will be central to understanding how parasite–replicator interactions shaped the path from simple replicators to complex genomes.

Methods

Evolution experiments of Qβ replicase

Evolution experiments were initiated from a clonal RNA population previously evolved for high replication efficiency (Ichihashi et al. 2013). This RNA is identical to the ancestral sequence used in our earlier evolution experiments (Mizuuchi et al. 2022). RNA was evolved largely as described previously (Ichihashi et al. 2013; Mizuuchi et al. 2022).

In the first round, 1 nM of ancestral N96 RNA was added to 10 µL of a customized translation system (Ichihashi et al. 2013) and emulsified with 1 mL of buffer-saturated oil (Ichihashi et al. 2013) in Nunc Biobanking and Cell Culture Cryogenic Tubes (Thermo Scientific) by homogenization at 16,000 rpm for 1 min on ice using a POLYTRON PT-1300D with a plastic shaft (KINEMATICA). The emulsion was then incubated for 5 h at 37 °C. In subsequent rounds, RNA from the previous emulsion was transferred into freshly prepared reaction mixture and oil to achieve the desired dilution factor.

The high-parasite condition was maintained at a 5-fold dilution factor, as previously (Mizuuchi et al. 2022). In each transfer, 200 µL of water-in-oil emulsion from the previous round was added to 8 µL of fresh translation mixture and 800 µL of buffer-saturated oil.

For low-parasite conditions, the concentration of host RNA after incubation was measured approximately every three rounds using RT-qPCR with host RNA-specific primers (Table S2), One Step TB Green PrimeScript PLUS RT-PCR Kit (Takara), and QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Scientific), as previously (Mizuuchi et al. 2022). The system was then diluted such that the RNA concentration after dilution was well below 1 nM (concentration corresponding to one molecule per compartment), approximately 0.01 nM. For example, when the measured concentration after incubation was 1 nM, a 100-fold dilution was achieved by transferring 10 µL of the RNA mixture into a mixture of 10 µL of fresh translation system and 1 mL of buffer-saturated oil.

Sequencing analysis

RNA samples were collected every ten rounds under low-parasite conditions and from representative rounds exhibiting high host RNA concentrations, as in our previous study (Mizuuchi et al. 2022), under high-parasite conditions using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Collected RNAs were analyzed by 8% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis in 1× TBE buffer, stained with SYBR Green II (Takara), and visualized using FUSION-SL4 (Vilber-Lourmat) as described previously (Mizuuchi et al. 2022).

To determine genotypes, the extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara) and subsequently amplified by PCR with KOD FX (Toyobo), as previously described (Mizuuchi et al. 2022). Over eight sampling rounds per line were selected to cover major changes throughout evolution (Table S1). After PCR, because shorter RNAs are preferentially amplified and sequenced, PCR products were size-selected by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis: approximately 2,000 bp for host RNAs and 500 to 700 bp for parasite RNAs. Extracted gel bands were purified using the Gel/PCR Extraction Kit (FastGene). Unique barcode tags were added by a second PCR, and the resulting amplicons were subjected to long-read sequencing using Oxford Nanopore Technologies with a Flongle flow cell (FLO-FLG114) and the Ligation Sequencing Kit (SQK-114). Basecalling and demultiplexing were performed using Dorado in super-accurate mode.

Construction of consensus haplotypes

To mask the effect of errors due to reverse transcription, PCR, and sequencing, we clustered reads based on similarity, and the consensus haplotype sequences recovered from the reads were used in the downstream analysis. First, for each sample, up to 1,000 reads were mapped to the evolvable region of the N96 genome using minimap2 (v2.28) with the options “-ax splice –cs -O5,32” (Li 2018). Alleles were then extracted from the alignments using paftools and bcftools (Danecek et al. 2021) (v1.21).

Towards clustering the sequences into haplotypes, first, we identified major substitution alleles present in at least 10% of reads in at least one round, to distinguish true variants from sequencing artifacts arising from reverse transcription, PCR, or sequencing. Because including more sampling rounds increases the number of detectable alleles, to avoid bias due to uneven sampling among lines, reads were subsampled evenly across eight rounds plus the final round for each molecule type and evolutionary line. Only samples with more than 100 valid reads were included, except for host molecules from line H2 at round 197 (90 valid reads), which were retained to capture mutations at the end of evolution (Table S1). Variants observed in more than 5% of reads when sequencing the ancestral sequence were excluded as likely artifacts. Notably, more than half of these apparent errors were single-base deletions within tandem repeats, which are prone to amplification and sequencing errors. In addition, any mutation occurring within 10 bp of an alignment gap was masked at this initial stage, as identifying mutations around the boundary of alignments tends to be ambiguous.

Using the subsampled dataset, major haplotypes were defined independently for each line and molecule type. Within each round and molecule type, haplotypes were ranked by read count, and the top three haplotypes supported by multiple reads were selected. In cases where multiple haplotypes tied for third place, one was selected at random. On average, 25% of reads per evolutionary line exactly matched one of the top three haplotypes.

To detect additional major haplotypes not captured among the top three, we clustered reads with at least three major alleles different from all top haplotypes of the same population. These reads were converted into binary presence–absence vectors for all major alleles and clustered using Ward's method with Euclidean distance using R (v4.3.1). The number of clusters was set to one-twentieth of the retained reads, yielding approximately 20 reads per cluster. Cluster genotypes were defined using the medians of the binary vectors. To validate these candidate haplotypes, the cluster medians were merged with the previously identified top haplotypes, and distances to 3,000 reads selected randomly across samples from different rounds were calculated. Cluster medians supported by at least 10 reads as the closest match and within two major mutation differences were retained as additional major haplotypes.

All reads were then assigned to major haplotypes using principal component analysis (PCA) on the binary allele vectors using MultivariateStats.jl (v0.10.3) of Julia (v1.9 or later). PCA was performed using the covariance matrix without variance scaling, retaining the top five principal components. Pairs of haplotypes with distances below 0.001 in PCA space were considered redundant, and only the first was retained. Each read was assigned to the nearest haplotype if it was within a Euclidean distance of 1 and a Hamming distance of 10. On average, 97.5% of reads were assigned to a major haplotype; the remaining reads were classified as noise and excluded from subsequent analyses.

Because the above analyses relied on only major alleles, mutations specific to individual haplotypes but not common at the population level may have been missed. To recover such within-haplotype mutations, consensus sequences were reconstructed for each major haplotype using up to 200 randomly selected supporting reads. This reconstruction was performed in three steps: gap identification, variant identification with phasing, and polishing.

Step 1: Gap identification

To facilitate polishing of the reference sequence, particularly for parasite RNAs with large alignment gaps, a draft reference was first generated by introducing gaps into the ancestral sequence at positions where read alignments failed. Reads were mapped to the evolvable region using minimap2 with options ‘-ax splice –cs -O5,3’, and per-base depth was calculated using samtools depth. Regions supported by mapped reads were then identified using a custom Python script. Bases with less than 50% read support were flagged, and consecutive flagged bases were grouped. Clusters of 10 bp or fewer were masked, whereas groups longer than 10 bp were retained as gaps. This procedure yielded a draft reference retaining only regions supported by mapped reads.

Step 2: Variant identification and phasing

Because reads within the same principal component space cluster may represent mixtures of distinct haplotypes, variants were called using Clair3 (v1.1.0), which supports haplotype phasing (Zheng et al. 2022). Reads were remapped to the gap-containing reference using minimap2, and variants were identified using Clair3 with the following parameters: –enable_variant_calling_at_sequence_head_and_tail –include_all_ctgs –chunk_size=100000 –use_whatshap_for_final_output_haplotagging –use_whatshap_for_final_output_phasing –var_pct_full=1 –ref_pct_full=1 –var_pct_phasing=1 –snp_min_af = 0.1 –indel_min_af = 0.3 –longphase_for_phasing.

Variants were applied to the draft sequences using bcftools consensus. Phasing was accepted when (i) more than two alleles differed between phases, (ii) at least 10 reads supported the minor phase, and (iii) the minor phase constituted at least 20% of reads within the cluster. All reads were then mapped to the phase-specific FASTA sequences using minimap2, and each read was assigned to the most likely haplotype.

Step 3: Polishing

To identify potential mutations that Clair3 may have missed, sequences were polished using medaka_consensus command. The quality of the consensus sequences was manually assessed by mapping the raw reads back to the consensus.

Finally, these raw haplotypes were clustered based on sequence similarity to obtain the haplotypes used in downstream analyses. First, pairwise distances between haplotypes were calculated as the number of differing bases, excluding mismatches at alignment gaps. Haplotypes with zero distance were clustered, and the haplotype with the highest maximum frequency across rounds was selected as the representative. Second, major mutations were redefined as those supported by more than 10% of reads assigned to a haplotype in any of the eight rounds used for the initial clustering (Figures S8 and S9). Pairwise distances between representatives were computed using Hamming distance based on the presence or absence of the new major mutations, excluding sites within large alignment gaps. Representatives with zero distance were merged, and a new representative was selected using the same maximum-frequency criterion. The frequencies of each representative haplotype were calculated based on the sum of all raw haplotypes that were clustered into each representative haplotype (Figures S6 and S7).

Phylogenetic trees were constructed with the R package ape (Paradis and Schliep 2019), from representative sequences using the Neighbor-Joining method with the large-gap ignored Hamming distance used as the distance metric.

Counting species numbers

In our previous work, individual lineages often persisted at low but undetectable frequencies and reappeared at later rounds (Mizuuchi et al. 2022). To account for the apparent loss of species due to transient drops below the detection threshold, we smoothed frequency trajectories using an Epanechnikov kernel.

Here, pi,r and Ri,r denote, respectively, the smoothed frequency and the set of rounds in which the ith haplotype was observed within a a-round window centered at round r. With this kernel, with a=25, rounds separated by five and ten rounds receive relative weights of 0.45 and 0.15, respectively, and any round further than 12.5 receives zero weight. The smoothed frequencies used to assess coexistence are plotted in Fig. 4b and Figure S12.

Using these smoothed frequencies, species numbers were quantified using Hill numbers, with a modification to account for genetic similarity among haplotypes. Specifically, haplotypes within a threshold genetic distance d of one another were treated as belonging to the same species, to reduce overcounting caused by sequencing noise or minor variants in quasispecies-like distributions.

For orders 1 and 2—corresponding to the exponential of Shannon entropy and the inverse Simpson index, respectively—we incorporated both pi, the frequency of haplotype i, and pi,d′, defined as the summed frequencies of all haplotypes within genetic distance d of haplotype i.

Because pi≤pi,d′ for all i, the thresholded metric is always less than or equal to the conventional Hill number, reducing overcounting due to sequencing noise or minor variants.

For order 0—representing species richness—we employed a bootstrap-based procedure (Fig. 4a). Ten variants were sampled according to their smoothed frequencies. Starting from the first sampled variant, each subsequent variant was evaluated to determine whether it lies within the threshold distance d of any previously sampled variant. If so, it was not counted as a new species; otherwise, it was classified as distinct. This procedure was repeated 100 times, and the average number of distinct variants was taken as the effective species number. Limiting the initial sampling to ten variants effectively excludes rare clusters with frequencies below approximately 0.1.

Replication assay

For biochemical analyses, positive-strand RNA of each consensus sequence was prepared as follows. DNA templates with the consensus sequence placed downstream of a T7 promoter and bearing a 3′ SmaI site were chemically synthesized (Twist Bioscience). Templates were digested with SmaI (Takara), purified (FastGene), transcribed with T7 RNA polymerase (Takara), and purified again (RNeasy Mini Kit, Qiagen). Residual DNA was removed by DpnI digestion (Takara), the RNA was purified again, and the concentration was adjusted to 100 nM with nuclease-free water. Synthesized sequences are provided as Source Data.

To measure the initial replication speed, 5 nM of the in vitro transcribed RNA was added to the reconstituted translation system described previously (Mizuuchi et al. 2022), mixed by pipetting, incubated at 37 °C, and 1 µL of the reaction mixture was sampled at 30 min and diluted 104-fold into 1 mM EDTA to stop the reaction. For two measurements of every triplicate, the 30 min time point was accidentally missed, and sampling was performed at 35 min instead; an additional sample was collected at 1 h, and the 30 min value was estimated by extrapolation assuming exponential replication. RNA concentration was determined by RT-qPCR using a standard curve generated from the corresponding in vitro transcribed positive-strand RNA. Separate primer sets were used for host and parasite RNAs, respectively (Table S2).

To measure yield, 0.1 nM of the in vitro transcribed RNA was mixed with 10 µL of the customized translation system and emulsified with 1 mL of buffer-saturated oil as in the evolution experiment. Aliquots of 50 µL were transferred to PCR tubes and incubated in a thermal cycler at 37 °C for 5 h, after which the reaction was stopped by cooling to 4 °C. After prolonged incubation, the emulsion showed signs of phase separation. To ensure uniform sampling, the mixture was pipetted vigorously, and RNA was collected from the intermediate height of the liquid, diluted 100-fold into 1 mM EDTA, and quantified by RT-qPCR. Replicates yielding below 3 nM were considered failed emulsifications and excluded from analysis, as replication consistently exceeded 30-fold amplification under low-parasite conditions during the evolution experiment (Fig. 2).
