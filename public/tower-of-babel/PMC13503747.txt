Acidophilic fungi as possible candidates for living forms in the Venus clouds
Authors: Anna Olewicz, Grzegorz P. Słowik, Miroslav Kolařik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Present-day Venus, similar to Earth in terms of relative radius, acceleration of gravity and mean density, is characterised by a very strong greenhouse effect and full cloud cover. Numerical simulations and 3D-climate models suggest that Venus may have had a hospitable climate and surface water in the past. Therefore, potential Venusian life could have migrated to the clouds, which provide conditions that might support life. Many fungi have been identified as polyextremophiles, successfully coping with multiple stressors, like irradiation, extremes of temperatures and pH, low water availability. Acidophilic fungi could thus be candidates for potential Venus cloud inhabitants. We have analyzed the UV-Vis spectra of the spores of three acidophilic fungal species, Acidiella bohemica, Acidomyces acidophilus, and Acidomyces acidothermus. The UV-Vis spectrum of A. acidothermus showed three peaks of increased absorbance at 243 nm, 275 nm, and 323 nm, whereas in the case of A. bohemica and A. acidomyces one peak of maximal absorption was observed, at 265 nm and 258 nm, respectively. This partially overlaps with the wavelength range of the Venus “mysterious” UV absorber. Until future Venus missions provide more data, fungi with extremophilic properties should not be overlooked when considering possibility of life in the Venus clouds.

Introduction

The need of exploration is an intrinsic part of human nature, extending far beyond our own planet, the Earth. The Copernican principle asserts that the Earth occupies no special position in the universe and thus offers a robust support for the search for extraterrestial life1, called sometimes the next Copernican revolution. So far, Earth is the only planet known to support life, and one of the key questions in recent years has been whether Earth’s closest neighbour, Venus, could also sustain life. According to the Venus Life Equation, the odds of life emerging and surviving on Venus are considered to be nonzero2.

As the nearest planet to the Earth, Venus has been the subject of research for decades, but many Venusian aspects remain unexplored. Although Venus is similar to Earth in terms of relative radius, acceleration of gravity and mean density3, it is characterised by a very strong greenhouse effect and is fully covered by clouds. The clouds hide one of the Venusian mysteries, the ultraviolet absorbers, which nature and unknown identity inspires Venusian researchers for decades4,5. Gaseous SO2 was found to be responsible for some absorption in the atmosphere of the planet, with peak below 300 nm5. Many other molecules also absorb in that region, inter alia, OCS6, CS2, CS, SO37, and O38,9. Nevertheless, spectral behaviour of the albedo of Venus in the 300–400 nm region requires presence of additional absorbers, which are likely to be particulate rather than gaseous species4. The obscure nature of the unknown UV absorber led to speculations about life in Venus clouds and biomolecules or living microorganisms as UV absorbers10. The discussion on biosignatures resulted in a targeted search for phosphine in Venusian atmosphere and its presence was reported by Greaves et al.11, however it was not confirmed by the others12–14 and remains debated. The key factors for habitability in Venus’s cloud include water activity, the presence of energy source, specifically for fungi, the potential availability of organic carbon. On the one hand, models described by Gillmann et al. and recent study by Constantinou et al. suggest that Venus was desiccated at the onset of its evolution and has never been liquid-water habitable15,16. On the other hand, numerical simulations and 3D-climate models indicate that Venus may have had a hospitable climate and ocean of water on its surface for about three billion years until as little as 700 Mya17. The presence of water on Venus was previously confirmed by Donahue et al. using the deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio as an indicator for past water loss18, and recent research not only verified previous observations but also found that this ratio is 1.500 times higher than on Earth19. In situ measurements from Vega probes estimated average water abundances of 1000 ppm at 50–60 km with a decrease to 150 ppm at 25–30 km20. However, the average value of the volume mixing ratio of H2O based on the spectrometric measurements reported by Pérez-Hoyos et al. varied with altitude, from 1 ppm above 70 km to 30 ppm below 45 km, with values interpolated linearly between 45 and 70 km21. As a consequence of catastrophic climate change, due to convection and other processes, potential Venusian life may have migrated from the inhospitable surface to the plausibly habitable zone in the lower clouds layer (approximately 50–60 km above the planet surface), which is characterised by the conditions that could support life, like moderate temperature and pressure, the presence of elements C, H, N, O, P, and S and solvents, despite being extreme in terms of low pH and a sulfuric environment22. By definition, extreme environments can be characterised by the presence of at least one physicochemical parameter, such as temperature, pH, radiation, that reach values significantly exceeding those considered normal and compatible with human life23. However, diverse microorganisms has been found in every extreme natural ecosystem across the Earth23. In line with this, extremophilic and extreme-tolerant life forms are capable of living in conditions that would kill other microbial life, including those relating to Venus clouds, that is dehydration, acidity, heat, and irradiation. Until recently the researchers seeking potential Venusian life focused on acidophilic prokaryotes as for example Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans24. Acidophilicity typically describes microorganism that optimally grow at pH values below 325, while extremely acidophilic microorganisms can grow at pH 1. Eukaryotes with their more complex cell structure were long considered more vulnerable than archaea and bacteria to stressors connected with extreme conditions. However, some eukaryotes like black fungi, characterised by strong melanization and adaptation to growth under oligotrophic and highly stressful conditions, proved to be exceptionally capable of living in extremely inhospitable environments26. These fungi have been identified as polyextremophiles, successfully coping with multiple stressors simultaneously, including irradiation, extreme temperature and pH, high salt and ion concentrations, low water availability27, growing in Earth’s acidic environments like volcanic areas and acidic lakes, which in addition are frequently accompanied with high temperatures and elevated levels of toxic metals28. Fungal radioresistance is mainly due to the presence of melanins29. Whereas solar radiation exposure is a potent environmental factor influencing both microbial survival and diurnal bacterial occurrence in air30, it is not considered to be a limiting element for radioresistant species. In addition, the majority of fungi can form spores, which are designed for aerial dispersion. Therefore, melanized, acidophlic fungi as polyextremophiles could be considered candidates for potential Venus cloud inhabitants.

Based on the one of the first reports of fungal isolates from highly acidic soils31, we selected for the analysis of UV-Vis spectra spores of three acidophilic or acidotolerant melanized fungi, Acidomyces acidophilus, Acidomyces acidothermus, and Acidiella bohemica. The morphology of these three species is quite similar, therefore molecular tools were used to taxonomically define these fungi as belonging to Teratosphaeriaceae family31. To date, only two species of Acidomyces have been described.

Results

The results of the spectrophotometric UV-Vis analysis of A. bohemica and A. acidomyces spores showed the maximal absorption at 265 nm, 258 nm, respectively. The UV-Vis spectrum of A. acidothermus spores showed 3 peaks of increased absorbance, at 243 nm, 275 nm, and 323 nm. In order to obtain repeatable results spore suspensions used for analysis were comparable to McFarland standard 1, estimating that spore suspension generated at McFarland 1.0 represents approximately 10⁷–10⁸ fungal propagules per mL. The UV-Vis spectra and transmittances for A. bohemica, A. acidophilus, and A. acidothermus are shown on Figs. 1 and 2, and 3, respectively. The results were compared to the transmittance of iron-sulfur (Fe-S) protein from A. ferrooxidans32.

Discussion

Since many aspects of Venusian clouds are being studied using different models and await future Venus missions, Earth’s atmosphere is still an important source of information regarding potential Venus aerobiosphere. The Earth’s aeromicrobiome consists of countless microorganisms, including thousands of bacterial and fungal species33,34. Different research approaches unambiguously indicated that bacteria and fungi are the most abundant cellular microorganisms within the atmospheric boundary layer35. Primary biological aerosol is estimated to represent up to 25% of all particles larger than 200 nm36. Fungal spores are released into the atmosphere and contribute 23% of total primary emissions of organic aerosol, 7% of the fine particulate matter (PM 2.5), and approximately 5% of PM10 mass37,38. Fungal propagules emitted into the atmosphere have been shown to participate in the climate system by serving, among others, as cloud condensation nuclei and solar radiations absorbers39.

Acidiella bohemica, also known by its synonym Fodinomyces uranophilus, is a cosmopolitan fungus, typically inhabiting extremely acidic soil (pH < 3), capable of growing at pH 2 and tolerating high levels of radioactivity31,40–42. Moreover, the results of transcriptomic analysis showed that A. bohemica expresses genes related to its metabolic pathways of C, N, S, and Fe as well as its adaptation mechanisms, such as response to acid stress and the resistance to heavy metals43. Furthermore, A. bohemica might have the potential for biologically induced mineralization. The results of a recent study showed that during the growth of A. bohemica, yellow precipitates containing high amount of Fe, S, and K elements were formed [43]. These elements are components of jarosite, a common mineral in acid mine drainage environment43. Therefore fungi like A. bohemica or other Acidiella species, namely A. americana and A. polonica, could be of special interest not only in case of Venus studies, but also when considering potential existence of life on other extraterrestrial bodies, for instance Mars, where jarosite is a common sulfate44. The second of the investigated fungi, A. acidophilus was first described in 1943 as “Fungus D” and was found to grow at pH 045. In 1973 “Fungus D” was isolated from acidic soil from a field adjacent to a sulfur stockpile from a natural gas purification plant and was proposed as a new species Scytalidium acidophilum46. In 1983 Ivarson and Morita studying bioconversion of waste paper to single-cell protein at pH < 1 reported good growth of S. acidophilum at the pH to 0.547. Acidomyces acidophilus was also found in extremely acidic, sulfate containing industrial water and later isolated from a sulfur-containing soil at a pH of 1.148. Interestingly, Boonen et al. identified and sequenced the multicopper oxidase gene in A. acidophilus, which encoded an enzyme of ferroxidase activity49. In the recent study, A. acidophilus demonstrated arsenic removal through biosorption onto its biomass and the biotransformation of arsenate into arsenite, followed by potential biomethylation50. However, A. acidophilus cannot grow above 37 °C31.

The last-named specie, A. acidothermus, was isolated from microbial mats and biofilms collected at an extremely acidic and high temperature Sainokawara hot spring51. All isolated strains of A. acidothermus grew well even in extremely acidic conditions at pH 1 and were able to grow at elevated temperatures (up to 45 °C)51. The genome of A. richmondensis (the synonym of A. acidothermus) FRIK2901 has been sequenced, revealing a wide set of enzymes presumably of peptidase activity and carbohydrate-active enzymes52. The study also unveiled several lineage-specific genes with domains potentially involved in acid tolerance and heavy metal tolerance52. The results of the spectrophotometric UV-Vis analysis of the three fungi sharing similar extremophilic traits as was described above, showed maximal absorption at the wavelength range between 243 and 323 nm, which partly covers the wavelength range of the Venus “mysterious” UV absorber4. When compared to the transmittance of Fe-S protein from A. ferrooxidans32, which has a UV spectrum that is very similar to that of Venus10, certain similarity could be a result of the presence of iron-based cofactors. Moreover, the UV–Vis spectra of oxidized and reduced forms for the recombinant Fe–S cluster-containing protein of A. ferrooxidans showed major absorption peaks at 330 nm and 410 nm53.

The major limitations for fungi presence in Venusian clouds seem to be the low water activity, low molecular oxygen, and uncertain presence of organic carbon in Venusian atmosphere. The water activity is a thermodynamic parameter used to quantify the availability of water, and is defined as the ratio between the water vapour pressures of the solution and of pure water under the same conditions. The extreme dryness of Venus has been underpinned for many years54. In the article by Hallsworth et al., the authors derived the water activity levels in Venus’ clouds from direct observations and determined the sulfuric acid concentration corresponding to this water activity according to the solution chemistry55. They found the water activity in the droplets extremely low, below 0.00455. However, recent re-analysis of data acquired by the Pioneer Venus Large Probe shed new light on the composition of aerosols from Venus’ middle and lower clouds, indicating that water, ferric sulfate, and sulfuric acid are major components and the amount of water is sufficient to yield hydrated ferric sulfates, potentially hydrated magnesium sulfate, and other uncharacterised hydrates56. It was shown that even the thin layer of liquid water that occurs at the surface of bioaerosols under subsaturated conditions might be sufficient to preserve microbial activity57.

All three acidophilic fungi studied here require molecular oxygen for growth, although A. bohemica can grow for some time in anaerobic conditions. In situ detections of dioxygen in the Venusian clouds have been reported58,59. The analysis of the data collected by Venera 14 lander determined the oxygen abundance in the range of 18 ± 4 ppm at heights of 58 to 35 km59. Furthermore, in a recent re-analysis of data acquired by the Large Probe Neutral Mass Spectrometer (LNMS) and Large Probe Gas Chromatograph (LGC) Mogul et al. reported that at 52 km the congruent LNMS and LGC xO2 were obtained before aerosol capture (or the LNMS clog) indicating that the middle clouds could possess atmospheric O2 and/or unidentified and non-clog inducing atmospheric compounds that released O2 immediately after collection56.

Fungi require organic sources of carbon and energy60. Over the past decades literature disregard organic carbon in relation to Venus atmosphere61. Even until recently, the models responding to the findings in the Venus atmosphere were based on inorganic chemistry62. However, organic material were to be that delivered to Venus atmosphere by cosmic dust63,64, and meteorites from space65. Moreover, recently a model of organic carbon cycle in the atmosphere of Venus was proposed by Spacek66.

To conclude the potential of acidophilic melanized fungi as inhabitants of Venusian clouds, due to their stunning capacity to tolerate, persist, and reproduce under conditions incompatible with life in the human sense, they appear as good candidates. Acidophiles are members of microbial communities inhabiting extremely acidic, metal-rich environments, characterised by very severe limitation of bioavailable organic carbon31,42,67. However, many aspect regarding not only Earth’s “sister” Venus, but also fungi, remain unexplored. In the optimistic scenario, as discussed above, Venus cloud environment could ensure access to organic carbon as an energy source for fungi, along with molecular oxygen and water availability. An important factor are spores of fungi, which can act as cloud condensation nuclei, are resistant to dessication and are light enough to travel with vertical winds and avoid downward sedimentation. However, life cycle of fungi as we know it is generally significantly more complex and longer than that of prokaryotes, such as acidophilic and sulfur-metabolizing bacterium A. ferrooxidans having UV spectrum similar to that of Venus10. Hence, a question arises whether fungi could reside enough time in the middle and lower cloud to reproduce. On the other hand, due to enormous abilities to adapt potential Venusian fungi could have evolved new unknown mechanisms enabling long lasting survival in the habitable zone.

Moreover, besides trying to discover the true nature of the mysterious Venusian UV absorber, future studies on potential Venusian life should focus on fungi also for another reason. It cannot be excluded that future missions to Venus would discover life which originated from the Earth. Beginning with the Venera 3 Soviet probe launched on November 16, 1965, until now 13 other probes reached the atmosphere of Venus68. There is evidence, that majority if not all attempts to completely sterilize space craft have failed, and that some of the microorganisms which survived the sterilization procedure are known to endure long lasting exposure to space conditions69. Furthermore, except for the sterilization procedure, the space vehicle is exposed to the environment rich in diverse microorganisms. Analysis of the samples taken from the surface of constructions of Mir Space Station after 13 years of operation revealed the ubiquitous presence of many microorganisms, including twelve fungal species with the predominance of Penicillium chrysogenum70,71. The primary contamination of space craft materials may occur while manufacturing and assembling, or during transportation to the orbit, and secondary contamination during regular use of the spacecraft by the crew. Frequently observed in prokaryotes horizontal gene transfer, defined as the exchange and stable integration of genetic material between different strains or species, was also reported in fungi72. The exchange of genes between fungi species or even between fungi and bacteria73, could have significant impacts on niche specification or shift in metabolic capabilities, especially important for evolution in extreme environments. Therefore, looking among fungi as potential Venusian microorganisms does not render an unreasonable idea, while waiting for future Venus missions to shed light on this conundrum.

To sum up, the fungi with their extremophilic properties should not be neglected when considering possibility of life in Venus clouds. The issue of absorber fitting the spectral slope between 0.4 μm and 0.5 μm still remains to be solved and waits for the future Venus missions, but analogs of acidophilic fungi might be in part responsible for the unknown absorber phenomenon.

Methods

The cultures of the investigated fungi were maintained at the Culture Collection of Fungi (Charles University, Prague, Czech Republic) on MEA (Difco, pH 6.5) agar slants, in 5 °C, with periodic transfers. Before the experiment, the cultures were grown on Petri dishes with the same medium (MEA, pH 6.5) and incubated for 21 days at 24 °C.

Preparation of spore suspension of the examined fungi species was performed in laminar flow hood. First, 10 mL of sterile demineralized water was poured on a plate containing a fungal culture. Then, the mycelium was scraped with a plate spreader for 5 min and the obtained suspension was then pipetted into a Falcon tube and vortexed with a speed of 3000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. The suspension was subsequently filtered through a paper filter (Whatman No. 43, pore size of 16 μm) into a glass flask and the filtrate was pipetted into quartz cuvettes and sealed with a Parafilm (Fig. 4). UV-Vis absorbance of each sample was measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader equipped with mounted Take3 plate holder for cuvettes in the wavelength 200–700 nm (repeatability of absorbance ± 3%). To correct for baseline drift all measurements were done after regular calibration using standard reference materials in stable environmental conditions. Sterile demineralized water was used as a blank.
