As the picture gets prettier the bar must rise higher
Authors: Khurram Nasir
Published: 2026

Coronary computed tomography for cardiovascular risk stratification is enjoying a remarkable moment. Anyone who spent time at the 2026 Society of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography (SCCT) Annual Scientific Meeting a few weeks ago will testify that the excitement for plaque phenotyping, artificial intelligence, and integrated anatomic and physiologic assessment, was unmistakable.

That excitement is not misplaced. Cardiovascular risk stratification is moving beyond indirect estimates such as the pooled cohort equations and PREVENT toward direct measurement of disease. Risk scores estimate probability. Atherosclerosis reflects the disease process itself, moving treatment decisions closer to biology, which is exactly the right direction [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. The conversation has also shifted decisively from whether we can see plaque to how much more we can see, and how fast.

CAC testing for asymptomatic individuals: foundational CT modality

Fortunately, we already have a powerful CT-based tool for atherosclerosis assessment: non-contrast coronary artery calcium (CAC) testing. It is favored in the real world because it is low-cost, easy to perform, and widely accessible. But more importantly, it earned that place the slow way, through more than two decades of reproducible data that held up across populations [[5], [6], [7], [8]].

The 2026 cholesterol management guidelines rightfully granted CAC testing multiple Class I indications for doing four things exceptionally well [3]. First, among proposed novel markers, CAC shows the largest improvement in risk discrimination. Second, and perhaps its greatest contribution, is the power of zero support for deferring pharmacotherapy in favor of lifestyle-first management. Third, it is the most valued decision aid for initiating preventive therapy. Fourth, it has become a dosing instrument, scaling LDL-lowering intensity and targets directly to CAC burden.

The pull toward computed tomography angiography (CCTA)

In this background, the appeal of CCTA or more precise risk stratification in asymptomatic individuals is genuine. It offers a richness of plaque characterization that calcium scoring, by design, cannot, and that richness dominated recent conversation in a way CAC, by comparison, rarely does anymore.

As we are all vowed by the amazing growth in CCTA capabilities, the prevention community needs to take the lead in asking what added information changes care sufficiently beyond CAC to justify its added cost, complexity, contrast exposure, and downstream consequences.

The question is, how should we think about "added value" in this context?

Naming the standard: the airport ride principle

Consider the choice most of us make without thinking at the end of a long meeting: how to get back to the airport. A standard ride and a premium sport utility vehicle may both be waiting at the curb. What would you choose? The premium vehicle may provide more space, additional sensors, and a more impressive experience, but several times the cost. Luxury is not wrong. Yet when the standard ride already reaches the same destination safely and efficiently, shouldn't the burden rest on the premium option to prove meaningful added value before it becomes the default?

For this conversation, while the analogy is imperfect; the principle is not. This is exactly how CCTA should be evaluated as a tool for measuring coronary atherosclerosis and guiding management, set against an established, inexpensive, effective CAC testing, already in guideline-directed use.

State it as a simple ratio: incremental clinical value, divided by incremental burden (cost, access, technical complexity, downstream testing).

That threshold suggests that for CCTA vs CAC, we will require substantial incremental risk stratification capable of producing substantial changes in management, sufficient to outweigh those trade-offs. Call it the incumbent's burden of proof, a standard we should apply to CCTA, to AI-derived plaque scores, and to whatever emerging tool comes next.

Testing the standard against what we actually know

How does CCTA fare against that bar? The historical signal is weak. In asymptomatic individuals, and even in symptomatic patients where CCTA is rightly a Class I test for diagnosing coronary disease, the incremental prognostic signal is thin once CAC, and especially the power of zero, is accounted for [9,10].

The recent Swedish CardioPulmonary BioImage Study (SCAPIS) analysis provides the strongest contemporary evidence [11]. The investigators are to be commended for studying 24,791 adults aged 50 to 64 without prior cardiovascular disease, with a median follow-up of 7.8 years and 304 first coronary events [11]. Adding CCTA to traditional risk factors and CAC improved model discrimination from an AUC of 0.764 to 0.779 (p = 0.004), statistically real, but in plain terms a small step, not a leap [11].

Looking under the hood: power of CAC zero vs. power of CCTA no plaque

This is not a number most readers will likely see from the SCAPIS recent publication, because it lies deep in the supplement tables. Since much of the skepticism toward CAC's power of zero rests on the concern that it misses too many people with noncalcified plaque, and with it, meaningful future risk; I think it’s prudent to ask how does a CAC zero fares to no plaque on CCTA?

The difference is rather underwhelming. Looking under the hood of the SCAPIS publication, among participants with CAC = 0, approximately 0.505% experienced an event over 8 years, compared with 0.451% among those with a normal CCTA [11]. That calls for some soul-searching: does a roughly 0.054% absolute difference over 8 years, justify routine CCTA in a population already stratified as low-risk by a $100 scan?

This is why the familiar decade-old narrative of "CAC = 0 is not zero risk", should be retired as an argument for routine CCTA screening. CAC = 0 is not literally zero risk, and neither is a normal CCTA. Nearly 20% of all SCAPIS events occurred in this extremely low-risk group. We have to come to terms that minimal residual risk will always exist. It cannot be a mandate for universal imaging or universal treatment. Otherwise, we will simply replace one narrative with another such as “no plaque is not zero risk.” In doing so, while the goalposts will shift, however those who engage in clinical decision-making will become even more uncertain.

A cost opportunity?

I also strongly believe that the current discussion cannot remain isolated from cost, and it should not be a taboo word in how we evaluate new cardiovascular technology. As an intentionally simple thought experiment ($100 for a CAC scan, $400 for CCTA, without even accounting for AI-based plaque analysis), the back-of-the-napkin math suggests that chasing with broad CCTA costs roughly $24,500 more per additional event identified, and nearly $595,000 in extra imaging cost vs CAC per single additional event found. Those same funds would cover almost 6000 CAC scans, capable of identifying roughly 69 more people at risk.

These calculations are not a formal cost-effectiveness analysis, and they should not be interpreted as one. At AJPC, we encourage investigators to pursue more formal investigations into this specific query. Yet I feel most can appreciate the underlying point, that every premium strategy carries an opportunity cost, and every imaging dollar spent in one place is unavailable for detection, navigation, or treatment elsewhere.

There is a path forward

Ongoing randomized trials such as TRANSFORM, DANE-HEART and SCOT-HEART 2 deserve strong support. They ask whether CCTA-guided prevention improves outcomes compared with standard or risk-score-guided care. But they do not answer whether CCTA adds enough value beyond a contemporary CAC-guided strategy. That is the harder comparison, but also the one that our CV prevention community ultimately needs.

Fortunately, the future need not be winner-take-all.

SCAPIS itself suggests a more rational pathway: among participants with CAC present, those with noncalcified plaque had meaningfully higher event rates. Under the same cost assumptions, the price of finding one noncalcified-plaque-positive person who subsequently experienced an event was roughly $70,000 among those with CAC 1–100, and closer to $22,000 among those with CAC >100.

CCTA's sweet spot may be precisely where CAC has already enriched the population. The logical trial design follows: begin with CAC; avoid routine CCTA at CAC = 0; enroll CAC-positive participants, enriched at higher burdens; and randomize to CAC-guided or CCTA-guided management, with prespecified treatment algorithms. Outcomes should include therapy initiation and intensification, achieved LDL-C, adherence, downstream testing, cost-effectiveness, and possibly improvement in outcomes. While some may argue that this would slow innovation. Many among us believe this would identify where innovation actually matters.

Concluding thoughts

Despite all skepticism, CAC has stood the test of time and remains the foundational CT-based risk stratification tool for asymptomatic individuals. The evidence is mature, reproducible, and increasingly integrated into guideline-directed prevention. Given the trade-offs, especially costs, CCTA has a real but narrower role than current enthusiasm suggests. Insights from SCAPIS study suggest likely its role is weakest as a universal front-line screening test and especially among those with Power of zero, and potentially of value after CAC has already identified disease and additional plaque characterization has a credible pathway to change management.

This is an exciting time for the coronary CT field with a rapid shift in preventive cardiology towards a philosophy of seeing the disease, treat the disease proposition. However, this is also a time for us to pause and reflect on our collective responsibility across the prevention ecosystem on the added impact of “seeing more”. Investigators and funders owe the field to the enrichment trial that will answer the real question. As clinicians, we owe our patients' resistance to both image or treat everyone proposition. Also, journals and societies need a more stringent standard to focus on added value over mere novelty.

In the end, while Carl Sagan's reminder that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence wasn't specifically about coronary imaging, it fits our situation well. The bar should keep rising with every year of CAC data, not fall simply because the newer pictures are prettier.
