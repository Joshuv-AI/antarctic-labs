Effect of post-discharge transitional care in patients with COPD: a multicenter single-blind randomised controlled trial
Authors: Yukyung Park, Deog Kyeom Kim, Woo Jin Kim, Seon Sook Han, Yeon Jeong Heo, Da Hye Moon, Oh Beom Kwon, Myung Goo Lee, Ji Young Hong, Jung-Kyu Lee, Eun Young Heo, Hyun Woo Lee, Yu Seong Hwang, Su Kyoung Kim, Chang Youl Lee, Heui Sug Jo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) significantly impairs patient quality of life and places a substantial burden on healthcare systems. However, the effectiveness of transitional care in reducing healthcare utilisation and improving symptom control remains uncertain. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of an evidence-informed transitional care program for hospitalised patients with COPD.MethodsIn this randomised controlled trial, 465 hospitalised patients with COPD from three South Korean university hospitals were block-randomised to intervention and control groups based on severity of airflow limitation (FEV1). The intervention involved a personalised care plan, including comprehensive assessment, patient education and training, telephone consultations, linkage to community services, home visits, and multidisciplinary case conferences. Participants were followed for 3 months after hospital discharge. Outcomes were analysed using an intention-to-treat approach. The primary outcome measures were readmission, respiratory symptoms, and treatment adherence. Mixed-effects models were used for repeated measures, and regression models were adjusted for baseline covariates.ResultsAt 3 months post-discharge, all-cause readmission was numerically lower in the intervention group than in the control group, but the difference was not statistically significant (adjusted OR 0.84; 95% CI 0.48–1.47). In exploratory cause-specific analyses, COPD-related readmissions were numerically fewer in the TCG than in the UCG (2 vs. 9 patients), although the small event counts limit interpretation. The intervention group showed significantly greater improvement in respiratory symptoms compared with the control group (modified Medical Research Council scale scores: adjusted mean difference − 0.41, p < 0.001; chronic obstructive pulmonary disease assessment test scores: − 3.38, p < 0.001). Treatment adherence also improved in the intervention group at 1 month, although this difference was not statistically significant at 3 months.ConclusionsPost-discharge transitional care may improve short-term symptom control and early inhaler adherence in patients with COPD, while its effect on readmission remains uncertain and warrants evaluation in adequately powered studies with longer follow-up.Trial registrationClinical Research Information Service (CRIS), No. KCT0007937, registered 24 November 2022-retrospectively registered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04407-8.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is characterised by persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow limitation [1]. Its 2010 global prevalence was 11.7%, while it reached 12.9% between 2015 and 2019 in South Korea [2, 3]. COPD causes approximately 3 million deaths annually and is a leading contributor to disability-adjusted life years [4]. Further, with disease progression, dyspnoea leads to functional decline and diminished quality of life, placing substantial caregiving and financial burden on families. According to the 2024 Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease report, optimal COPD management involves appropriate pharmacologic therapy; oxygen therapy; and non-pharmacologic interventions, including smoking cessation, physical activity, immunisations, pulmonary rehabilitation, and enhanced self-management through education and regular monitoring. Patients hospitalised owing to acute exacerbations have a high 90-day risk of readmission or death. Accordingly, implementing structured care bundles is recommended during transitional periods [1, 5].

Transitional care services (TCS) aim to ensure continuity of care during critical changes in care environments, including hospital discharge [6], and Hirschman et al. outlined nine key components of such models, ranging from screening and staffing to self-management, collaboration, and coordination [7]. Even in countries with robust national health coverage such as South Korea, education and capacity-building components remain underutilised [8], although building self-management capacity is central to effective COPD care. While multiple studies have shown that TCS improves healthcare utilisation and quality of life, personalised, bundle-based TCS models for COPD have received less attention than pulmonary rehabilitation or early follow-up, and effectiveness likely varies with intervention components and setting. Among systematic reviews of randomised controlled trials evaluating TCS effectiveness in COPD, two offer important insights. Ridwan et al. synthesised 13 trials and reported significant reductions in COPD-related and all-cause readmissions but no effect on all-cause mortality with greater benefits observed for interventions of longer duration, those involving diverse providers, or those incorporating telephonic follow-ups [9]. Park et al. reported significantly lower relative risk for readmission (0.68) [10], although the St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire scores did not differ significantly, and physical capacity showed only marginal improvement [11]. Effective transitional care bundles typically include initial inpatient assessments; structured education and training delivered by trained professionals; individualised care planning centred on patient motivation; and continued telephonic, home visits, or follow-up consultations [10–14]. However, evidence remains heterogeneous, underscoring the need for well-designed trials of clearly specified, bundle-based transitional care that rigorously evaluate COPD-related healthcare utilisation. Three specific gaps emerge from this evidence base. First, intervention components, intensity, and duration vary substantially across trials [9–14], and even nationally promoted discharge bundles show inconsistent real-world uptake owing to communication gaps between hospital and primary care, resource constraints, and variable protocol adherence [15], making it difficult to identify which standardised, bundle-based configurations are reproducibly effective. Second, evidence from Asian populations is underrepresented — only 3 of 13 trials in Ridwan et al. [9] and 2 of 9 in Park et al. [10] — yet TCS effectiveness depends on surrounding factors such as length of stay, primary-care follow-up density, home-visit nursing, and post-discharge coverage that differ markedly between Asian and Western health systems. Third, few trials simultaneously evaluate a clearly specified bundle against both readmission and the proximal treatment-management outcomes (respiratory symptoms, inhaler adherence) that plausibly mediate utilisation effects.

In a previous interim analysis of this randomised trial, we reported early improvements in respiratory symptoms and patient-centered outcomes using data from a subset of participants [16]. Because the sample size at that stage was limited, we were unable to evaluate readmission outcomes with sufficient statistical power. Using the full randomised trial dataset with 1- and 3-month follow-up assessments, the present study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the effects of a protocol-driven TCS program on healthcare utilisation and key treatment-management outcomes closely linked to utilisation, namely respiratory symptoms and inhaler treatment adherence in patients with COPD. To this end, we developed and implemented a hospital-to-home transitional care program informed by previous evidence [17, 18]. The intervention components and trial procedures have been described in the published protocol [19].

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This multicenter, single-blind randomised controlled trial was conducted in three university hospitals in South Korea between November 2022 and December 2024.

Potentially eligible patients were screened within 72 h of admission through review of electronic medical records (EMRs). Patients aged ≥ 18 years were eligible if ICD-10 codes J44.0 or J44.9 were recorded as either the principal or secondary diagnosis during the index hospitalisation and if spirometry showed FEV1/FVC < 0.70. In practice, all randomised participants were aged ≥ 40 years, and only 11 patients (2.4%) were between 40 and 59 years of age, indicating that the study population predominantly comprised older adults with COPD. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) receiving active treatment for cancer; (2) unable to participate due to mental or behavioural disorders; and (3) having cognitive, hearing, or speech impairments that would limit informed consent or questionnaire completion. Final eligibility was confirmed by pulmonologists based on EMRs. The participants were randomly assigned to either a transitional care group (TCG) or usual care group (UCG). Eligible patients were stratified into mild-to-moderate (FEV1 ≥ 50% predicted) and severe (FEV1 < 50% predicted) COPD subgroups using the most recent pre-randomisation FEV1 value available in the electronic medical record. Within each FEV1 stratum, participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the TCG or UCG using permuted block randomisation with a block size of 4. The random allocation sequence was generated in Microsoft Excel by an independent researcher who had no direct contact with participants and was not involved in intervention delivery or outcome assessment. To maintain allocation concealment, the randomisation list was held only by the independent researcher and was not accessible to study coordinators or clinical staff. Before randomisation, the treating physician provided eligible patients with brief oral information about the study and asked whether they were willing to participate. Patients who declined at this stage were not randomised. For patients who agreed to participate, randomisation was performed, after which the study coordinator visited the patient and obtained written informed consent using the IRB-approved information sheet and consent form specific to the assigned group. This group-specific consent procedure was used to support participant blinding by avoiding disclosure of the alternative study arm and the specific study hypotheses before intervention delivery and study-specific data collection. This was a participant-level single-blind trial. Group-specific information and consent procedures, reviewed and approved by the institutional review boards, were used to support participant blinding. Participants were informed only about the procedures relevant to their assigned group and were not informed of the alternative study arm or the specific study hypotheses. When feasible, participants assigned to different groups were not placed in the same hospital room to minimise contamination and support participant blinding. Intervention providers and treating physicians could not be blinded because of the nature of the transitional care intervention.

The first participant was enrolled in November 2022, and the trial was registered with the Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS; KCT0007937) on 24 November 2022; it is therefore reported as retrospectively registered. The institutional review boards of all participating hospitals approved the study, which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Interventions

The participants in the TCG received structured TCS (for more detail intervention, see Supplementary File 1): (1) individualised discharge planning and two in-hospital education sessions, (2) two home visits (within 48 h and at 1 month), and (3) four weekly follow-up calls plus a final 3-month visit. Education was based on the Living Well with COPD curriculum [20], and the transitional care service was delivered by the trained coordinators in collaboration with multidisciplinary team members including pulmonologists. Multidisciplinary coordination meetings were held regularly. UCG patients received standard inpatient care and discharge instructions only, without any structured transitional care components.

Outcome measures

The protocol-defined primary outcomes were readmission, respiratory symptoms, and inhaler adherence. All outcomes were assessed at baseline (during hospitalisation) and at 1- and 3-month follow-up assessments after discharge. Readmission events were ascertained through structured participant interviews capturing the principal reason for each readmission; COPD-related readmissions were additionally confirmed by review of electronic medical records (primary ICD-10 diagnosis code J44.0 or J44.9). Respiratory-related readmissions were defined as those due to respiratory diseases other than COPD, and other-cause readmissions as those due to non-respiratory conditions. All-cause readmission during the 3-month follow-up period was prespecified as the primary readmission measure. Cause-specific readmissions (COPD-related, respiratory-related, and other-cause) were analysed as exploratory post hoc subcategories. Emergency department visits and COPD exacerbations were analysed as additional healthcare utilisation outcomes; exacerbations were defined as symptomatic worsening exceeding daily variability and requiring treatment escalation. Healthcare utilisation variables were assessed using study-specific questionnaire items developed for this study (see Supplementary File 2). Respiratory symptoms were measured using the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnoea scale and the COPD Assessment Test (CAT) [21]. Inhaler adherence was measured using the 10-item Test of Adherence to Inhalers (TAI) [22], with scores ranging from 10 to 50 (higher scores indicating better adherence).

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation assumed an expected reduction in 1-month readmission from 26.2% (following prior institutional data) to 15.6% (a meta-analytical effect size) [9], one-sided Z-test applied by assuming no increase in readmission risk following intervention, power of 80%, and significance level of 0.05. Accordingly, the target sample size was calculated as 362 (n = 460, accounting for 20% attrition).

All primary analyses were conducted according to the intention-to-treat principle, including all randomised participants in the groups to which they were assigned. For repeated continuous outcomes (mMRC, CAT, and TAI), linear mixed-effects models with participant-specific random intercepts were fitted to estimate between-group differences over time. The models included fixed effects for group, time, group-by-time interaction, and baseline covariates. These models incorporated all available observations, and participants with incomplete follow-up data contributed outcome data up to the point of withdrawal, loss to follow-up, or death.

Healthcare utilisation outcomes were analysed separately for the first month after discharge and for the interval between 1 and 3 months after discharge. Binary outcomes were analysed using multivariable logistic regression models, and count outcomes were analysed using negative binomial regression models when model estimation was stable. Negative binomial estimates were not reported for outcomes with sparse event counts that resulted in non-convergence or unstable estimates. Exploratory post hoc analyses of cause-specific readmissions and additional healthcare utilisation-related outcomes were performed to examine the distribution of outcome categories but were not intended as confirmatory evidence of intervention effects. Additionally, a post hoc composite endpoint analysis was conducted by combining all-cause readmission and death within each follow-up window.

Adjusted models included age group, smoking status, and prior COPD exacerbation-related hospitalisation within the previous year as covariates, based on clinical relevance and observed baseline imbalance between groups. Given the presence of multiple outcomes, findings were interpreted cautiously with attention to consistency across outcomes rather than reliance on individual tests alone. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 14.2 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 465 eligible patients were recruited and randomly assigned to either the TCG (n = 233) or UCG (n = 232) (Fig. 1). At the index hospitalisation, the proportion of participants with COPD recorded as the principal diagnosis was similar between the TCG and UCG (56/233 [24.0%] vs. 59/232 [25.4%]); most participants in both groups had COPD recorded as a secondary diagnosis (177/233 [76.0%] vs. 173/232 [74.6%]). Follow-up completion and losses to follow-up, including deaths and withdrawals, are detailed in Fig. 1. All 465 randomised participants were included in the intention-to-treat analysis set, and available outcome data were used for each analysis. Table 1 shows the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. The UCG included more participants aged ≥ 80 years (126 [54.3%] vs. 101 [43.4%]), living alone (68 [29.3%] vs. 49 [21.0%]), and hospitalised for COPD exacerbation within the previous year (20 [8.6%] vs. 10 [4.3%]) than the TCG.

Readmissions and additional healthcare utilisation outcomes

All-cause readmission, the primary readmission outcome, was numerically lower in the TCG than in the UCG at both 1 month (19 [8.8%] vs. 23 [10.7%]) and 3 months (28 [13.3%] vs. 35 [16.9%]) after discharge. In the adjusted models, the odds of all-cause readmission did not differ significantly between groups at 1 month (OR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.45–1.69) or 3 months (OR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.48–1.47).

In a post hoc composite endpoint analysis combining all-cause readmission and death, the direction of effect remained similar, but the between-group differences were not statistically significant. The composite outcome was numerically less frequent in the TCG than in the UCG at 1 month (20 [8.6%] vs. 29 [12.5%]; adjusted OR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.38–1.32) and at 3 months (34 [14.6%] vs. 42 [18.1%]; adjusted OR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.51–1.43).

In exploratory analyses of cause-specific readmissions, COPD-related readmission was numerically less frequent in the TCG than in the UCG at both 1 month and 3 months. Although the unadjusted OR for COPD-related readmission at 3 months reached conventional statistical significance, the adjusted OR was no longer statistically significant (OR, 0.24; 95% CI, 0.05–1.20). Respiratory-related and other-cause readmissions did not show statistically significant between-group differences.

COPD exacerbations and emergency department visits were analysed as additional healthcare utilisation-related outcomes. COPD exacerbations were numerically less frequent in the TCG than in the UCG at both 1 month and 3 months, but the adjusted models did not show statistically significant between-group differences. Emergency department visits showed no statistically significant between-group differences during either follow-up interval (Table 2).

Discussion

Effectiveness of TCS for improving clinical outcomes

This multicenter, single-blind randomised controlled trial evaluated the effects of a hospital-to-home TCS bundle for patients with COPD within a 3-month post-discharge follow-up period. This manuscript focuses on the protocol-defined primary outcomes; protocol-specified secondary outcomes, including patient-centred outcomes and outcomes related to the nursing intervention process, are beyond the scope of the present manuscript and will be reported separately. The primary outcome of all-cause readmission was numerically lower in the TCG than in the UCG at both 1 month (8.8% vs. 10.7%; adjusted OR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.45–1.69) and 3 months (13.3% vs. 16.9%; adjusted OR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.48–1.47), but these differences were not statistically significant and the confidence intervals were wide. The post hoc composite endpoint analysis incorporating death showed non-significant trends in the same direction (adjusted OR, 0.71 at 1 month; 0.86 at 3 months). In exploratory analyses of cause-specific readmissions, COPD-related readmission was less frequent in the TCG than in the UCG, but this estimate was based on small event counts (TCG n = 2 vs. UCG n = 9), with a wide confidence interval (adjusted OR, 0.24; 95% CI, 0.05–1.20) that crossed the null after covariate adjustment. This interpretation should also consider that most participants had COPD recorded as a secondary rather than principal diagnosis during the index hospitalisation, although the distribution of principal and secondary COPD diagnoses was similar between groups. Given the small number of cause-specific events and the resulting imprecision, this finding should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. COPD exacerbations showed a numerical trend favouring the TCG, whereas emergency department visits showed no consistent directional difference between groups. Despite the baseline imbalance in prior exacerbation history, the direction of effect on readmission was preserved after covariate adjustment for age, smoking status, and prior-year exacerbations; however, the trial was not powered to detect modest differences in low-frequency events, and the magnitude and certainty of any reduction cannot be reliably estimated from these data. The direction of effect is broadly consistent with that reported in recent meta-analyses, including the analysis by Park et al., which showed similar reductions in COPD-specific readmission [10]. Meanwhile, Ridwan et al. demonstrated broader effects including all-cause readmissions, particularly with longer-duration and more intensive interventions [9]. By implementing a standardised transitional care bundle across three university hospitals in South Korea and assessing both COPD-specific and all-cause healthcare utilisation in a real-world context, our trial extends the existing evidence base.

In contrast to the readmission findings, the strongest and most consistent effects of the intervention were observed in patient-reported respiratory symptoms. Adjusted mean mMRC and CAT scores improved more in the TCG than in the UCG at 1 and 3 months after discharge. At 3 months, the adjusted between-group difference in mean change from baseline was − 0.41 (95% CI, − 0.56 to − 0.26; p < 0.001) for mMRC and − 3.38 (95% CI, − 4.49 to − 2.28; p < 0.001) for CAT, with the CAT difference exceeding the 2-point threshold for clinically meaningful change [23]. These improvements were maintained through 3 months, despite the active intervention being concentrated in the first month. This pattern suggests that comprehensive disease education during hospitalisation and intensive post-discharge follow-up may have contributed to early symptom stabilisation. By targeting modifiable behaviours and self-management skills, the TCS may provide additional support beyond usual clinical recovery, although the present study cannot disentangle the effect of individual intervention components. In our earlier interim report from this trial, changes in respiratory symptom scores were primarily analysed using pre–post comparisons [16]. In the present analysis, we extended this approach by using linear mixed-effects models to simultaneously account for repeated measurements at baseline, 1 month and 3 months, and to explicitly estimate group-by-time interactions. This allowed a more comprehensive assessment of symptom trajectories over time and the persistence of intervention effects beyond the initial post-discharge period.

The TCG also showed a statistically significant improvement in inhaler adherence at 1 month (TAI, adjusted between-group difference 2.26; 95% CI, 0.76–3.75; p = 0.003). At 3 months, the between-group difference attenuated and did not retain significance (1.40; 95% CI, − 0.12 to 2.91; p = 0.072), although TAI scores in the TCG remained numerically higher than in the UCG. This trajectory—early gains during the intensive intervention period that partially attenuated as direct contact decreased—suggests that repeated education, skills training and reinforcement during the early vulnerable post-discharge phase can change inhaler use behaviours, but that longer-term support may be needed to fully stabilise these behaviours as routine. This is because patient behaviour is influenced by multiple factors beyond knowledge, including routinisation, experience of treatment effects, and treatment beliefs [24].

Previous studies have attributed respiratory symptom improvements to dyspnoea management, psychological coping strategies, and self-management education [12, 25]. Unlike other studies, the concurrent improvement in TAI scores suggests that improved inhaler adherence may be one plausible pathway through which the intervention supported symptom improvement, although this mechanism was not directly tested. Meanwhile, other studies showed varied readmission outcomes despite CAT score improvements—some attributed increased readmissions to better symptom recognition, whereas others attributed them to improved early response through action plans [12, 26]. Although difficult to fully explain, healthcare utilisation outcomes, including readmissions, are determined not only by patients’ objective symptom deterioration but also by multidimensional factors such as health literacy, physical and economic accessibility, and institutional context; this may lead to contradictory results [10]. These complexities may partly explain the variable intervention effects observed across trials and may help explain the more uncertain readmission findings in our study.

Implications for practice

Clinical guidelines advocate for care bundle implementation in high-risk patients with COPD, but TCS bundles may sometimes be unavailable depending on institutional circumstances. South Korea’s social insurance-based healthcare system makes non-reimbursed services less accessible. While pulmonary rehabilitation services are covered by insurance, utilisation rates remain low due to several barriers including high costs and time requirements [27]. Indeed, no study participants received formal pulmonary rehabilitation during the study period. In this context, multidisciplinary transitional care may represent a pragmatic and scalable strategy to extend support beyond discharge, strengthen continuity between hospital-based and home-based care and reinforce guideline-based management without requiring major structural changes. As evidence accumulates regarding clinically meaningful improvements in respiratory symptoms and inhaler adherence, alongside favourable trends in readmission outcomes, such services may provide a strong basis for future reimbursement and routine provision even in countries where TCS is not yet integrated into formal payment systems.

Study strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. The collaborative nature of the intervention meant that only single blinding was feasible. Although participant blinding was supported through IRB-approved group-specific information and consent procedures and efforts to minimize contamination between groups, intervention providers and treating physicians could not be blinded. In addition, allocation-specific information provided after randomisation may have introduced bias by influencing participants’ expectations, engagement, or continuation in the study. We did not formally assess whether participants became aware of their allocation; therefore, possible unblinding due to the additional attention received by the TCG cannot be excluded. Statistical power was limited for low-frequency events such as cause-specific readmissions, ED visits, and exacerbations, for which findings should be interpreted as numerical trends and treated as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Even for the all-cause readmission outcome, the observed effect sizes were smaller than commonly assumed in prior trials, leaving precision of the estimate insufficient to support definitive inference. Despite covariate adjustment for age, smoking status, and prior-year exacerbations, residual confounding from the baseline imbalance in prior exacerbation history (8.6% UCG vs. 4.3% TCG) cannot be completely excluded, and this imbalance contributed to the loss of statistical significance for the unadjusted COPD-related readmission estimate after adjustment. The follow-up period was limited to 3 months after discharge; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as short-term effects and cannot determine whether the observed improvements are sustained over a longer period. The attenuation of the between-group difference in TAI scores between 1 and 3 months suggests that further follow-up and possibly extended reinforcement strategies are warranted in future trials. We could not disentangle the effects of individual components within the bundled intervention. Finally, the trial was conducted in three university hospitals in South Korea, which may limit the generalisability of our findings to other healthcare systems or community-based settings.

Conversely, this trial also has important strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first RCT to evaluate a TCS bundle targeting discharged patients with COPD in South Korea. The intervention incorporated evidence-based items while considering realistic contexts, with the 1-month intensive intervention showing higher practical applicability. By assessing both healthcare utilisation and patient-reported outcomes—including respiratory symptoms and inhaler adherence—we were able to examine both clinical and potential treatment-management pathways.

Conclusions

In conclusion, a hospital-to-home transitional care bundle for patients with COPD was associated with clinically meaningful improvements in respiratory symptoms (mMRC, CAT) at 1 and 3 months after discharge and an early improvement in inhaler adherence (TAI) at 1 month, which attenuated by 3 months. Readmission outcomes, including all-cause readmission, the post hoc composite endpoint analyses, and exploratory cause-specific analyses, all showed numerical but non-significant trends towards lower risk. The strongest evidence from this trial therefore supports the value of structured transitional care for improving patient-reported symptom control and treatment-management behaviours in the early post-discharge period, while the magnitude and durability of any effect on readmission require confirmation in adequately powered trials with longer follow-up. These findings suggest that structured transitional care may support safer transitions from hospital to home for patients with COPD, particularly in health systems where continuity of care after discharge is suboptimal. Recognising TCS as an essential component of comprehensive COPD management and integrating multidisciplinary, hospital-to-home care pathways may represent an important strategy for improving short-term outcomes in this high-risk population.
