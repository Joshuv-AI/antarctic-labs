Effects of humate and Yucca schidigera supplementation on slaughter weight, carcass performance, intestinal morphology, ileal digestibility and antioxidant capacity in broilers
Authors: Selim Mert, Figen Kirkpinar, Zümrüt Açikgöz, Helin Atan Çirpici, Özgün Işik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined the effects of humate (H; Farmagülatör DRY™), Yucca schidigera (Y; Vizyonmix BF2338 Biopowder), and their combination (HY) on slaughter weight, carcass characteristics, intestinal morphology, ileal digestibility, and antioxidant capacity in broiler chickens. A total of 240 Ross 308 broilers were allocated to four dietary groups: control (C), H (300 mg/kg humate), Y (100 mg/kg Yucca schidigera), and HY (both additives). After a 42-day feeding trial, the HY group demonstrated the highest slaughter weight (2967.65 g), carcass weight (2049.21 g), breast weight (885.40 g), and thigh weight (710.10 g; P=0.001). It was determined that the highest carcass (69.00%), breast (29.80%), and thigh (23.90%) yields were obtained in the HY group, followed by the Y, H, and C groups, respectively (P<0.05). At day 42, among the intestinal morphology parameters (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum), villus height and width increased in the groups supplemented with feed additives compared with the C group, whereas crypt depth decreased (P<0.001). The highest villus height and width, as well as the lowest crypt depth, were observed in the HY group. The HY group showed the highest ileal digestibility of crude protein (87.11%), crude fat (88.08%), and organic matter (80.92%) on day 21 (P<0.001). In terms of antioxidant parameters, both HY and Y groups exhibited higher levels of superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase, total antioxidant status, and high density lipoprotein, along with lower levels of triglycerides, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, cholesterol, total oxidant status, lactate dehydrogenase, and malondialdehyde (P<0.05). These findings suggest that combining humate and Yucca schidigera has synergistic effects, improving slaughter weight, carcass characteristics, intestinal health, and antioxidant defense, thereby highlighting their potential as effective feed additives in broiler production.Keywords: Antioxidant capacity, Broiler, Carcass performance, Humate, Intestinal morphology, Yucca schidigera

Introduction

Since the mid-20th century, a variety of feed additives have been incorporated into poultry diets to enhance immune function, improve nutrient digestibility, maximize production efficiency, and optimize product quality in broiler genotypes commonly used in conventional chicken meat production (Ben Lagha et al., 2017; Sevillano et al., 2026). In recent years, growing consumer awareness of food safety has led to increased interest in natural alternatives among feed additives (Atan Çırpıcı & Kırkpınar, 2025). Given the strong interrelationship between performance, gut health, and immune status in poultry production, supplementation with feed additives such as enzymes, probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, organic acids, and plant-derived compounds has been widely recommended. In particular, phytogenic feed additives have attracted considerable attention due to their antioxidant, antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and growth-promoting properties.

Yucca schidigera (Y. Schidigera) is a desert plant containing phenolic compounds with ammonia-binding properties and steroidal saponins that help retain water and nutrients, thereby mitigating environmental stress (Khaskheli et al., 2020). Its saponin fraction exhibits multiple beneficial effects, including ammonia reduction, urease inhibition, decreased intestinal surface tension, and antimicrobial, antiprotozoal, antifungal, and antioxidant activities (Fidan & Dündar, 2007). Due to their amphiphilic nature, saponins enhance micelle formation during lipid digestion, improve nutrient absorption, and exert bactericidal effects through interactions with microbial membranes (McAllister et al., 2001), while also supporting intestinal villus development and growth performance (Su et al., 2016). These properties have increased interest in Y. schidigera extract in broiler nutrition, with studies reporting improvements in gut health, immunity, antioxidant status, and feed efficiency; for instance, Ayoub et al. (2019) observed reductions in litter ammonia and microbial load alongside enhanced immune and antioxidant parameters, while Sun et al. (2017) reported dose-dependent improvements in antioxidant enzyme activity and later-stage growth performance, with higher doses improving oxidative stability. In addition, Alghirani et al. (2021) investigated the effects of 25, 50, 75, and 100 mg/kg Y. schidigera supplementation in broilers raised under tropical conditions and reported that the 100 mg/kg group showed decreased cholesterol (CHOL), triglycerides (TG), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), along with increased high-density lipoprotein (HDL). Moreover, Y. schidigera has demonstrated synergistic effects when combined with caprylic acid, or curcumin, improving immune responses, growth performance, and gut microbiota balance (Begum et al., 2015; Galli et al., 2020).

Humates are heterogeneous organic compounds formed during humification through the biochemical and chemical transformation of plant and microbial residues, and they mainly consist of humic and fulvic acids (Marcin et al., 2022). The formation of these structures involves organic constituents such as lignin, polysaccharides, proteins, lipids, melanin, and nucleic acids (Bezuglova, 2019). It has been reported that fulvic acid, a component of humates, enhances nutrient absorption, supports immune function, exhibits antimicrobial activity, and reduces inflammation (Gao et al., 2017; Lieke et al., 2021; Feng et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024). Humic acid, on the other hand, is widely used in poultry nutrition as an alternative to antibiotic growth promoters and has been shown to improve growth performance, feed efficiency, and nutrient digestibility. In addition, it contributes to the regulation of gut microbiota, increases antioxidant capacity, improves carcass traits, and enhances meat quality (Arafat et al., 2017; El-Naggar & El-Kelawy, 2018; Korsakov et al., 2019; Domínguez-Negrete et al., 2021; Ghalwash & ELnaggar, 2022; Ahfeethah et al., 2023). It has also been reported that humic acid may improve hematological parameters due to its mycotoxin-binding properties. In broiler chickens, studies on humate supplementation have shown beneficial effects on body weight gain, feed conversion ratio, carcass characteristics, and intestinal microbial balance (Hassan et al., 2011). Similarly, Marcin et al. (2022) reported that different humate preparations can influence serum triglyceride and cholesterol levels in broilers, with a notable reduction in cholesterol concentrations. Some aspects were studied previously, but the literature about the use of humates is still limited.

Yucca schidigera extract (Ayoub et al., 2019; Galli et al., 2020; Dai et al., 2023) and humate (Marcin et al., 2022) are multifunctional natural products known to support animal health, improve performance, and enhance both the quality and safety of poultry products. Previous studies have mainly focused on the individual use of Y. schidigera or humate. Yucca schidigera, through its saponin content and ammonia-reducing activity, may help maintain a healthier intestinal environment, whereas humate may contribute to improving intestinal integrity and nutrient utilization. Therefore, supplementation of these additives either individually or in combination may provide beneficial effects on gut health, antioxidant status, and overall broiler performance, with combined supplementation potentially resulting in greater benefits than their separate use.

The aim of this study was to determine the effects of Y. schidigera and humate, individually or in combination, when added to a basal diet in broiler chickens, on slaughter weight, carcass traits, intestinal morphology, ileal digestibility, and antioxidant capacity.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments of the Faculty of Agriculture, Ege University (Approval No: 2023/002).

Experimental design and treatments

In this experiment, 240 one-day-old Ross 308 broiler chicks with an average initial body weight of 38.38 ± 0.44 g were randomly assigned to four dietary treatments. Each treatment included five replicate pens, with 12 chicks per pen. The experimental groups were as follows: Control group (C) receiving only the basal diet; H group, receiving the basal diet supplemented with 300 mg/kg humate; Y group, receiving the basal diet supplemented with 100 mg/kg Y. Schidigera; and HY group, receiving the basal diet supplemented with both 300 mg/kg humate and 100 mg/kg Y. Schidigera. All birds had free access to feed and water throughout the 42-day trial period. The diets were provided in two phases: the starter diet from days 1 to 21 and the grower-finisher diet from days 22 to 42 (Table 1). All feeds were formulated by thoroughly blending powdered ingredients and supplements in a mixing unit. Environmental conditions were standardized throughout the experiment. The initial environmental temperature was maintained at 32 ± 1 °C and gradually reduced to 24 ± 1 °C as the chicks aged. A lighting schedule of 23 h of light and 1 h of darkness per day was applied throughout the experiment. Relative humidity levels were kept at approximately 65%. Throughout the trial, environmental temperature and relative humidity were maintained according to the management recommendations for Ross 308 broilers (Aviagen, 2019). The recorded temperature–humidity (THI) conditions remained within the thermoneutral comfort zone reported for poultry, indicating that THI values were not at levels likely to impair bird welfare during the experimental period. Each pen (1.4 m × 1.2 m) was equipped with wood shavings as bedding, a circular feeder, and four to five nipple drinkers to ensure sufficient access to feed and water. Broilers were vaccinated against Gumboro disease on day 9 and Newcastle disease on day 11 via drinking water according to the company’s recommendations.

Data collection and carcass parameter

On the 21st and 42nd days of the experiment, animals were individually weighed using scales with 1 g precision. Subsequently, twelve animals per group (48 animals in total) with body weights representative of each group were selected, and 48 animals were slaughtered on the 21st day and 48 animals on the 42nd day of the experiment. After an 8 h feed and water withdrawal, the birds were humanely euthanized by severing the jugular vein without prior anesthesia. The procedure was performed by a faculty member of the Department of Animal Science who holds a certified license for laboratory animal experiments, in accordance with the approved animal care and use protocol. On the 42nd the carcass components, including the whole carcass, breast and thighs, were separated and individually weighed. Intestinal morphology

On day 42, twelve birds from each treatment group were euthanized to evaluate intestinal morphology. Approximately 5 cm segments of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were excised from each bird, rinsed with 0.9% saline, and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. The samples were processed using standard histological techniques: paraffin embedding, sectioning at 4 µm with a rotary microtome (Leica RM2155), and mounting on glass slides (Thermo Scientific, USA). Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under a light microscope (Zeiss Scope A1). Digital images were obtained with a ZEISS AxioCam 208. For each group, villus height (VH), villus width (VW), and crypt depth (CD) were measured on 20 randomly selected, well-oriented, and intact villi obtained from histological sections prepared from 12 samples.

Ileal digestibility

The nutrient composition of the experimental diets and ileal digesta (collected on day 21) was analyzed using the Weende analysis method (AOAC, 2006) at the Chemical Analysis Laboratory of E.Ü. Z.F., Department of Animal Science. Dry matter content was determined by oven drying at 105 °C, crude ash by combustion in a muffle furnace at 550 °C, crude protein by the Kjeldahl method (N × 6.25), and ether extract by Soxhlet extraction. Titanium dioxide (TiO₂) was included in all diets at 5 g/kg as an indigestible marker (Kandel et al., 2026). On days 21, twelve birds per group were slaughtered to determine ileal digestibility of dry matter, protein, and fat. TiO₂ concentrations in feed and ileal samples were measured spectrophotometrically, and digestibility coefficients were calculated using the regression equation described by Morgan et al. (2014).

Collection of blood samples for serum biochemical analyses

Twelve animals from each group were fasted, with feed and water access withdrawn 8 h prior to slaughter. At slaughter (12 samples from each group), approximately 10 mL of blood was collected from the vena jugularis and transferred into heparinized tubes (Greiner Bio-One, Les Ulis, France). Samples were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 10 °C. Serum concentrations of superoxide dismutase (SOD; Otto Scientific, kit no: Otto3047, Ankara, Türkiye), catalase (CAT; Elabscience, kit no: E-BC-K031-S, Wuhan, China), TG (Otto Scientific, kit no: OttoBC155, Ankara, Türkiye), glutathione peroxidase (GPx; Otto Scientific, kit no: Otto2085, Ankara, Türkiye), aspartate aminotransferase (AST; Otto Scientific, kit no: OttoBC127, Ankara, Türkiye), alanine aminotransferase (ALT; Otto Scientific, kit no: OttoBC128, Ankara, Türkiye), CHOL (Otto Scientific, kit no: OttoBC135, Ankara, Türkiye), total oxidant status (TOS; REL ASSAY, kit no: RL0024, Gaziantep, Türkiye), total antioxidant status (TAS; REL ASSAY, kit no: RL0017, Gaziantep, Türkiye), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; Otto Scientific, kit no: OttoBC129, Ankara, Türkiye), HDL (Otto Scientific, kit no: OttoBC144, Ankara, Türkiye), and malondialdehyde (MDA; Otto Scientific, kit no: Otto1001, Ankara, Türkiye) were measured using a MINDRAY-BS400 autoanalyzer (Maharashtra, India) with the colorimetric method.

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7, indicating that a minimum of 60 animals per group (totaling 240 animals) was required to achieve a statistical power greater than 80%, with a 95% confidence interval, a significance level of 0.05, and a medium effect size. For all analyses, the pen was considered the experimental unit. Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. Since the data met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances, they were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Results

Growth performance parameters obtained during the first phase of this project were previously reported by Mert (2025). Therefore, only slaughter weight data relevant to the objectives of the present study are presented herein.

Slaughter weight and carcass traits

The effects of Y. Schidigera and humate supplementation, either alone or in combination, to the basal diet on slaughter weight and carcass traits in broiler chickens were presented in Table 2. Statistically significant differences were observed among the experimental groups in terms of slaughter, carcass, breast, and thigh values (P < 0.001). The highest slaughter weight was recorded in the HY (2967.65 g) group. However, the HY, Y(2934.69 g), and H (2851.03 g), groups were found to be similar to each other, while the lowest slaughter weight was determined in the C (2628.32 g) group. The slaughter weight of the HY group was approximately 42.23% higher than that of the C group. The highest carcass weight was observed in the HY group (2049.21 g), followed by the Y (1992.4 g), H (1900.12 g), and C (1722.00 g) groups, respectively (P < 0.001). Compared to the HY C group, carcass weight increased by approximately 16%. The HY group achieved the highest breast weight (885.40 g), followed by Y (859.65 g), H (823.99 g), and C (725.17 g). The HY and Y groups showed significantly higher breast weights than the C and H groups, with no significant difference between HY and Y. The highest thigh weight was observed in the HY group (710.10 g), followed by the Y (690.06 g), and H groups (659.19 g), which were found to be similar to each other. The lowest thigh weight was recorded in the C group (555.79 g). Significant differences were also observed in carcass yield among the dietary groups (P < 0.001). The HY group showed the highest carcass yield (69.0%), followed by Y (68.14%), H (66.87%), and C (65.06%). The highest breast yield was observed in the HY (29.80%) and Y (29.40%) groups, followed by the H (29.00%) group, while the lowest value was recorded in the C (27.40%) group (P < 0.05). The highest thigh yield was observed in the HY (23.90%) and Y (23.60%) groups, followed by the H (23.20%) group, while the lowest value was recorded in the C (21.02%) group (P < 0.001).

Intestinal morphology

The effects of Y. Schidigera and humate supplementation, either alone or in combination, to the basal diet on intestinal morphology in broiler chickens were shown in Table 3. Statistically significant differences were observed among the experimental groups in terms of VH, VW, and CD of the duodenum (P < 0.001). In the duodenum, the HY group exhibited the highest VH (1237.24 µm), followed by the Y (1227.36 µm), H (1207.06 µm), and C (1102.29 µm) groups. Compared to the C group, the HY group showed an approximate 12.25% increase. The highest VW in the duodenum was observed in the HY (161.76 µm) group, followed sequentially by the Y (159.95 µm), H (155.78 µm), and C (142.71 µm) groups. Regarding CD, the C (152.70 µm) and H (152.30 µm) groups had the deepest crypts, followed by Y (150.65 µm) and HY (141.18 µm). In the jejunum, all measured parameters differed significantly among groups (P < 0.001). The highest VH in the jejunum was observed in the HY (768.31 µm) group, followed sequentially by the Y (740.96 µm), H (700.85 µm), and C (670.83 µm) groups. Jejunal VH in the HY group increased by approximately 14.53% compared to the C group. For VW, HY (124.40 µm) recorded the largest value, followed by Y (122.10 µm), H (118.83 µm), and C (100.95 µm) groups. The highest jejunal CD was observed in the C (134.85 µm) group, followed sequentially by the H (133.05 µm), Y (131.88 µm), and HY (130.33 µm) groups. In the ileum, significant differences were observed across all measurements (P < 0.001). The HY group had the highest VH (640.96 µm), followed by Y (624.70 µm), H (600.78 µm), and C (560.65 µm) groups. The highest ileal VW was observed in the HY (94.50 µm) group, followed sequentially by the Y (93.33 µm), H (90.16 µm), and C (88.23 µm) groups. For CD, the C group had the deepest crypts (101.61 µm), followed by H (100.25 µm), Y (97.31 µm), and HY (95.85 µm) groups.

Statistically significant differences were observed among groups in crude fat, crude protein, and organic matter digestibility values (P < 0.001). For crude fat digestibility, the HY group achieved the highest value at 88.08%, followed by Y (87.26%) and H (86.46%), with the C group lowest (85.81%) Relative to the C, HY increased crude fat digestibility by 4.3%, while both H and Y improved it by 1.3%. Crude protein digestibility was highest in the HY (87.11%) group, followed by the Y(86.55%) group, and lowest in both the H (84.81%) and C (84.13%) groups. The highest organic matter digestibility was observed in the HY (80.92%) group, followed sequentially by the Y (80.20%), H (79.75%), and C (78.43%) groups.

Blood parameters

The effects of Y. Schidigera and humate supplementation, either alone or in combination, added to the basal diet on selected blood parameters in broiler chickens at day 42 were shown in Table 5. The groups were found to have statistically significant differences in blood serum SOD, CAT, TG, and GPX levels (P < 0.001). The highest SOD value was observed in the HY (1350.36 U/ml) group, followed by the Y (1340.24 U/ml), H (1320.48 U/ml), and C (1301.04 U/ml) groups. The highest CAT values were found in the HY (124.23 U/ml) and Y (120.06 U/ml) groups, which were followed by the H (102.56 U/ml) and C (82.59 U/ml) groups, respectively. The highest blood serum TG value was observed in the C (35.12 mg/dl) group, followed by the H (33.42 mg/dl), Y (32.53 mg/dl), and HY (32.01 mg/dl) groups, which show similar values to each other. The TG value in the HY group decreased by approximately 8.86% compared with the C group. The HY (100.28 U/L), Y (100.02 U/L), and H (98.23 U/L) groups had higher GPX activity than the C group (77.43 U/L). The highest AST value was observed in the C (228.26 U/L) group, followed by the H (216.51 U/L), Y (213.43 U/L), and HY (212.27 U/L) groups (P < 0.05), respectively, and these three groups were found to be similar to each other. The groups were found to have statistically significant differences in blood serum ALT, CHOL, TAS, and TOS levels (P < 0.001). The lowest ALT values were observed in the HY (13.01 U/L) and Y 13.81 U/L groups, followed by the H (15.51 U/L) group, while the highest value was recorded in the C (17.20 U/L) group. The highest CHOL value was observed C (118.22 mg/dl), followed by H (114.51 mg/dl), Y (113.84 mg/dl), and HY (112.49 mg/dl). The lowest TAS value was recorded in the C (2.24 mmol/L) group, followed by the H (2.35 mmol/L), Y (2.37 mmol/L) and HY (2.38 mmol/L) groups, which were found to be similar to each other. The C group had the highest TOS level (8.02 µmol/L), followed by H (7.58 µmol/L), Y (7.44 µmol/L), and HY (7.31 µmol/L) which were found to be similar to each other. LDH levels varied significantly among the groups (P < 0.05). The highest LDH level was detected in C (685.42 U/L), followed by H (621.28 U/L), Y (602.97 U/L), and HY (598.93 U/L). HDL levels differed significantly among the dietary groups (p < 0.001). The HY (80.02 U/L), Y (79.22 U/L), and H (78.51 U/L) groups had higher HDL levels compared to C (69.42 U/L). Discussion

In the broiler production industry, carcass yield and the body weights and proportions of edible parts, particularly breast and thigh meat, are considered important indicators of meat production efficiency. In evaluating slaughter performance, not only body weight but also carcass yield and the development of valuable carcass parts are considered (Feng et al., 2022). In the present study, supplementation of the basal diet with Y. Schidigera and humate, either separately or in combination, improved slaughter weight, carcass weight, and the weights and yields of breast and thigh meat. The positive effects of Y. schidigera may be attributed to its steroidal saponin content, which has been reported to increase intestinal surface area, stimulate digestive enzyme activity, and positively modulate gut microflora (Francis et al., 2002; Alghirani et al., 2021). Alghirani et al. (2021) also reported that Y. Schidigera supplementation increased feed efficiency at 42 days of age, attributing this improvement to the growth-promoting effects of Y. schidigera, including the activation of digestive enzymes and increased VH, which positively influence digestive function. These effects can improve nutrient utilisation and digestion efficiency, thereby promoting growth performance and carcass development. Additionally, humates are recognised for their ability to support intestinal integrity, bind toxic compounds in the digestive tract, and promote mineral absorption (Islam et al., 2005). Similarly, Hrnčár et al. (2018) demonstrated that humate, either alone or in combination with probiotics, increased final body weight and improved carcass yield, possibly due to synergistic effects on the intestinal microflora. The impact of humate on gut morphology is also noteworthy. Eivollahi et al. (2019) found that sodium humate supplementation increased VH and the villus-to-crypt ratio in the small intestine, thereby enhancing nutrient absorption capacity. Improved nutrient utilisation resulting from these effects may contribute to higher body weight gain and better carcass characteristics. On the other hand, Feng et al. (2022) reported that dietary supplementation with fulvic acid alone did not significantly affect carcass yield. This finding suggests that supplementation with humate, rather than fulvic acid alone, may be a more effective approach to enhancing carcass characteristics and overall broiler performance. Previous studies have also reported beneficial effects of Y. schidigera and humate supplementation on carcass traits in broilers. Patoary et al. (2020) demonstrated that Y. schidigera supplementation enhanced breast muscle development and improved carcass yield. Similarly, humate supplementation has been associated with improvements in gut health, growth performance, and slaughter characteristics. The findings of the present study are in agreement with these earlier reports. Moreover, the greater improvements observed in the combined Y. schidigera and humate group suggest that these feed additives may exert complementary or synergistic effects on broiler performance and carcass quality.

Intestinal morphology is considered an important indicator of digestive health and nutrient utilization in broilers, and it can be markedly influenced by dietary composition (Shi et al., 2020). In the current study, supplementation with Y. schidigera increased VH and VW, while CD decreased. These findings indicate a positive effect on intestinal development and absorptive capacity. Comparable results have previously been reported in broilers fed diets containing Y. schidigera (Sun et al., 2017; Bafundo et al., 2021). Alghirani et al. (2021) reported that increasing dietary levels of Y. schidigera increased VH in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. The authors attributed these effects to the steroidal saponins present in Y. schidigera, which may enhance digestive activity and intestinal health. In the present study, the higher VH and VW values observed in Y. schidigera-supplemented groups support these findings and suggest improved absorptive capacity of the intestine. Crypt depth was also reduced in broilers receiving Y. schidigera. Lower CD is generally linked to slower epithelial turnover and reduced energy demand for intestinal tissue renewal (Chung et al., 2019). As a result, a greater proportion of dietary nutrients may be directed toward growth rather than maintenance functions. This may partly explain the improvements observed in growth performance and carcass characteristics in the Y. schidigera-supplemented groups. Humate supplementation similarly improved intestinal parameters. Eivollahi et al. (2019) reported that sodium humate increased VH and the VH:CD, indicating enhanced absorptive capacity. In addition, humic substances have been shown to support intestinal barrier integrity and reduce mucosal damage under inflammatory conditions (He et al., 2023; Lv et al., 2025). These effects may help maintain a healthier intestinal environment and improve digestive efficiency. The combined use of Y. schidigera and humate appeared to provide additional benefits for intestinal development. Y. schidigera -derived saponins may primarily support digestive activity and villus development, whereas humates may contribute to intestinal stability and epithelial integrity. Therefore, the simultaneous improvement in villus structure and reduction in crypt depth observed in the present study suggest that these feed additives may work together to support intestinal function and nutrient utilization in broilers.

Nutrient digestibility is an important factor affecting growth performance and feed utilization in broiler chickens. In the present study, supplementation of the basal diet with humate and Y. schidigera, either separately or in combination, improved crude protein, crude fat, and organic matter digestibility on day 21. The positive effects of Y. schidigera may be associated with the steroidal saponins naturally present in its structure, which have been reported to improve intestinal conditions and nutrient absorption by modulating microbial activity and supporting digestive function (Cheeke, 2000; Patoary et al., 2020). In addition, the emulsifying properties of saponins may contribute to improved lipid digestion, which could partly explain the increase in crude fat digestibility observed in the present study. Humate supplementation also positively affected nutrient digestibility, although its effects appeared less pronounced than those observed with Y. schidigera. Humic substances are known to support intestinal integrity and help maintain microbial balance within the digestive tract (Hrnčár et al., 2018; Islam et al., 2005). Similarly, Alfaro et al. (2007) suggested that the beneficial effects of Y. schidigera on protein digestibility may be related to its regulatory influence on intestinal microflora and nutrient absorption. In the current study, the combined supplementation group showed higher digestibility values than the individual treatments, which may indicate complementary effects of humate and Y. schidigera on digestive processes. Although some previous studies have reported improved nutrient utilization following the combined use of humic substances with enzymes or probiotics (Disetlhe et al., 2018; Hrnčár et al., 2018), limited information is available regarding the combined effects of humate and Y. schidigera on ileal nutrient digestibility in broiler chickens. Therefore, direct comparisons with previous studies investigating a similar combination are limited.

Commercial poultry production is frequently challenged by multiple stressors that adversely affect performance, reproduction, and general health (Surai et al., 2019). Recent studies have identified oxidative stress, marked by excessive free radical production and compromised antioxidant defense systems, as a key mediator of stress-related dysfunctions in poultry (Surai et al., 2019). In this context, dietary interventions aimed at enhancing antioxidant capacity have garnered significant attention. Humate has been shown to exhibit potent antioxidant activity by inducing the activity of key antioxidant enzymes, including CAT and glutathione reductase, thereby protecting cells from oxidative stress and reducing MDA levels. Similarly, supplementation with 2 g/kg sodium humate significantly elevated serum total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD) and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), while reducing MDA levels in laying hen (Çetin et al., 2006). In another study, sodium humate significantly increased serum GSH-Px, T-AOC, and T-SOD activities, while concurrently decreasing MDA concentrations in yellow-feathered broilers. These effects are likely attributed to the functional groups in HA, such as carboxyl, hydroxyl, carbonyl, and quinone, which are capable of neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and maintaining intestinal barrier integrity by attenuating oxidative stress (de Melo et al., 2016). The current study corroborates these findings, demonstrating similar enhancements in antioxidant markers in broilers supplemented with H.

Superoxide anion (O₂⁻) and hydroxyl radical (·OH), which are commonly generated during normal physiological processes, are known to cause extensive damage to biological macromolecules including carbohydrates, proteins, and nucleic acids, eventually leading to cellular dysfunction (Sun et al., 2017). T-AOC reflects the cumulative action of all antioxidants present in plasma, while MDA, a by-product of lipid peroxidation, serves as a reliable biomarker of oxidative damage (Kadiiska et al., 2005). GSH-Px, one of the most crucial antioxidant enzymes, protects cellular components by reducing hydrogen peroxide and lipid hydroperoxides in response to excessive ROS production. Consistent with the present findings, previous research has shown that Y. Schidigera supplementation reduces serum MDA levels and enhances serum GSH-Px activity in calves (Liu et al., 2021). In aquatic species, dietary Quillaja saponaria (QS) extract reduced MDA levels in Nile tilapia (Elkaradawy et al., 2021). In broilers, supplementation with YSa, YS, or QS similarly increased T-AOC and GSH-Px activity while lowering MDA levels, without significant differences between the three treatments. These antioxidant effects may be partially mediated by the activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway by saponins, which upregulates the expression of endogenous antioxidant enzymes such as GSH-Px (Gao et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2020). In the present study, the most pronounced improvements in antioxidant status were observed in the group receiving both Y and H, suggesting a synergistic effect. The combination appeared to offer more robust protection against oxidative stress than either additive alone, likely due to complementary mechanisms of action, with H providing functional radical-scavenging groups, and Y enhancing enzymatic antioxidant pathways. The liver plays a central role in cholesterol homeostasis, and disturbances in bile acid synthesis can result in elevated serum cholesterol, which may exacerbate hepatic injury (Tabas, 2002). Previous studies have shown that humate inclusion significantly reduces serum total cholesterol without affecting total protein levels, suggesting a potential hepatoprotective role. This is consistent with the present findings, in which both H and Y supplementation lowered serum cholesterol levels. Such lipid-lowering effects may support liver function, enhance nutrient absorption, and ultimately improve overall performance. In addition, serum LDH, a nonspecific marker of tissue damage, and HDL levels were positively influenced by the dietary treatments. The reduction in LDH and modulation of HDL levels further support the notion that YS and HA supplementation can contribute to systemic metabolic stability and cellular protection. Collectively, the findings suggest that both Y. Schidigera and humate, whether individually or in combination, can enhance antioxidant defenses, protect against lipid peroxidation, and support hepatic and metabolic functions in broiler chickens. These improvements may translate into better health status and performance, particularly under oxidative or environmental stress.

Conclusion

Dietary supplementation with humate and Y. Schidigera, individually and especially in combination, significantly enhances slaughter weight, carcass traits, intestinal morphology, nutrient digestibility, and antioxidant status in broiler chickens. The synergistic effects observed suggest that these additives improve gut health, nutrient utilization, and oxidative balance while mitigating environmental stressors. Thus, humate and Y. Schidigera represent promising, sustainable alternatives to antibiotic growth promoters in poultry production. However, the present study was limited to a 42-day experimental period conducted under controlled conditions. In addition, further studies conducted under different dietary inclusion levels and environmental stress conditions, particularly heat stress, are needed to better clarify the potential synergistic effects between Y. schidigera and humate.
