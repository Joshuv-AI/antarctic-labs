Effects of melatonin and vitamin E on bone healing following alloplastic grafting in osteoporotic cranial bone defects: an experimental study
Authors: Nur Efşan Aydın, Sedat Çetiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBone healing in osteoporotic bone represents a significant clinical challenge in oral and maxillofacial surgery. The aim of this experimental study was to evaluate the effects of melatonin and vitamin E supplementation on bone healing following alloplastic graft application in cranial bone defects created under osteoporotic conditions.MethodsA total of 35 male Wistar rats were randomly allocated into five groups. Experimental osteoporosis was induced by retinoic acid administration. Following osteoporosis induction, critical-sized cranial bone defects were created in the parietal bone. Except for the negative control group, all defects were filled with an alloplastic bone graft. Melatonin and/or vitamin E were administered systemically to the experimental groups. At the end of the experimental period, bone healing was evaluated histologically.ResultsNo bone healing was observed in the negative control group, whereas mild to moderate new bone formation was detected in the graft control group. The melatonin-treated group demonstrated pronounced and high-grade bone formation. In contrast, the vitamin E-treated group did not show a significant improvement in bone healing compared to the graft control group.ConclusionsThe findings of this experimental study suggest that melatonin may have a supportive effect on bone healing following alloplastic grafting in osteoporotic bone defects. Vitamin E, either alone or in combination with melatonin, did not provide an additional benefit in terms of bone regeneration. These results provide preclinical evidence to guide the selection of biological adjunctive agents in osteoporotic bone healing.

Introductıon

Bone tissue is a metabolically active structure that undergoes continuous remodeling throughout life, in addition to providing mechanical support and maintaining mineral homeostasis [1]. The physiological balance between bone formation and bone resorption plays a fundamental role in preserving bone integrity and function. Disruption of this balance results in decreased bone mineral density, deterioration of bone microarchitecture, and an increased risk of fractures [2]. Osteoporosis is a common metabolic bone disease characterized by bone resorption exceeding bone formation and is associated with significant morbidity, particularly in elderly individuals [3]. In osteoporosis, not only a reduction in bone mass but also marked alterations in trabecular and cortical bone microarchitecture are observed [4]. These structural changes negatively affect the regenerative capacity of bone tissue and impair the healing of bone defects [5].

In oral and maxillofacial surgery, bone defects frequently occur following tooth extractions, cystic lesions, tumor resections, and trauma [6]. Repair of bone defects in osteoporotic bone represents a greater clinical challenge compared with normal bone. Critical-size bone defects are defined as defects that cannot heal spontaneously and typically result in fibrous tissue formation. Bone grafting procedures are therefore widely used in the management of such defects [7]. Autogenous bone grafts are considered the gold standard for bone defect reconstruction due to their osteogenic, osteoinductive, and osteoconductive properties [8]. However, donor-site morbidity, limited availability, and the need for additional surgical intervention have increased interest in alternative graft materials. Alloplastic bone grafts are widely used in clinical practice owing to their high biocompatibility, unlimited availability, and osteoconductive properties [9]. Nevertheless, the limited osteogenic potential of alloplastic grafts necessitates adjunctive biological approaches to enhance bone healing, particularly in osteoporotic conditions.

Melatonin is an indolamine secreted by the pineal gland and is known for its potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, as well as its regulatory effects on bone metabolism [10, 11]. Previous studies have demonstrated that melatonin promotes osteoblast proliferation and differentiation while inhibiting osteoclast activity [12, 13]. Moreover, melatonin has been shown to enhance angiogenesis during bone healing, thereby supporting the regenerative response [14]. In addition, melatonin has been reported to improve bone structure and reduce bone resorption in osteoporotic conditions, suggesting its potential therapeutic role in enhancing bone healing in compromised bone environments [10, 14].

Vitamin E is a lipophilic antioxidant; however, its effects on bone tissue remain controversial, with heterogeneous findings reported in the literature [15]. While some studies suggest that vitamin E may positively influence bone biomechanics and osteoblast activity, its role in osteoporotic bone healing has not yet been clearly established [16, 17].

Based on these considerations, evaluating the contribution of melatonin and vitamin E to bone healing following alloplastic grafting in osteoporotic bone defects is of particular interest. The aim of this study was to histologically investigate the effects of melatonin and vitamin E on bone healing in alloplast-grafted cranial bone defects in an experimentally induced osteoporosis model.

Materıals and methods

Ethical approval and study design

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Gazi University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (GUHADYEK) (Approval No: E-66332047–604.01–910304). The study was supported by the Scientific Research Projects Unit of Gazi University under project number TDH-2024–9577.

The study was conducted in two stages: an experimental phase and a histological evaluation phase. The experimental procedures were carried out at the Gazi University Laboratory Animal Breeding and Experimental Research Center (GÜDAM), while histological analyses were performed at the BIONIX Histology Laboratory.

Experimental animals and group allocation

The experimental animals were obtained from the Gazi University Laboratory Animal Breeding and Experimental Research Center (GÜDAM), where they were housed and maintained under standard laboratory conditions.

A total of 35 male Wistar albino rats, aged 10–12 weeks and weighing between 250 and 300 g, were included in the study. The animals were randomly allocated into five groups, each consisting of seven rats.

All animals were housed under conditions in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health. Rats were maintained at a temperature of 21–24 °C, with 40–45% relative humidity and a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. Standard pellet food and water were provided ad libitum. A two-week acclimatization period was allowed before initiating the experimental procedures.

Experimental osteoporosis model

The experimental osteoporosis model was established based on the retinoic acid–induced osteoporosis model described by Wu et al. To confirm the applicability and histopathological validity of the model, a pilot study was conducted on two rats prior to the main experiment. Histomorphological comparison of parietal bones from retinoic acid–treated rats and control samples revealed a reduction in trabecular and cortical bone volume, along with enlargement of bone marrow spaces. As the pilot results were consistent with the literature, the main experimental protocol was initiated.

In the main experiment, retinoic acid (Retinol Acetate, Stara Prorizna, Ukraine) was administered intragastrically at a dose of 70 mg/kg once daily for 14 consecutive days using a stainless steel gavage cannula. Retinoic acid was diluted with distilled water and prepared in a total volume of 2 mL for each rat.

Surgical procedure

Prior to surgery, the calvarial region was shaved and disinfected using a povidone–iodine antiseptic solution. Under aseptic conditions, a midline skin incision approximately 20 mm in length was made to expose the parietal bone. Using the bregma as a reference point, a critical-sized cranial bone defect measuring 8 mm in diameter was created with a trephine bur. Continuous irrigation with sterile saline solution was performed throughout the procedure. Except for the negative control group, all defect sites were filled with a particulate alloplastic bone graft material (Ceraball® MCB®, Micro-Chambered-Beads®). The surgical site was closed primarily by suturing the muscle, subcutaneous tissue, and skin layers separately using 5–0 polyglactin 910 sutures.

Experimental treatments

Negative control group

No additional treatment was applied after defect creation.

Graft control group

Only alloplastic bone graft material was applied to the defect area.

Vitamin E group

Following grafting, vitamin E (dl-alpha-tocopheryl acetate) was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 20 mg/kg daily for 28 days.

Melatonin group

Following grafting, melatonin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) dissolved in isotonic solution was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 10 mg/kg daily for 28 days.

Vitamin E + melatonin group

Following grafting, vitamin E (20 mg/kg) and melatonin (10 mg/kg) were administered intraperitoneally once daily for 28 days. The selected dosage of melatonin (10 mg/kg) was based on previously published experimental studies investigating its effects on bone metabolism and regeneration in rat models [18, 19].

Postoperative care and sacrifice

Postoperative analgesia was provided by intramuscular administration of carprofen at a dose of 4 mg/kg. On day 56 of the experiment, all rats were sacrificed under deep anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (45 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg). The parietal bones, including the defect area, were harvested for histological evaluation.

Histological evaluation

Bone specimens were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and subsequently processed routinely and embedded in paraffin (Leica ASP300). Sections of 3 µm thickness were obtained from paraffin blocks and stained with hematoxylin–eosin and Masson’s trichrome (Leica 2125RT). Histological evaluation was performed to assess bone healing, osteoblast and osteoclast presence, and fibrosis. A semi-quantitative scoring system was used. Bone formation was scored semi-quantitatively based on the proportion of newly formed bone within the defect area (0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = extensive). Osteoblast and osteoclast presence were evaluated according to cellular density (0 = none, 1 = low, 2 = moderate, 3 = high). Fibrosis was assessed based on the extent of fibrous tissue formation (0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = extensive). The scoring system was adapted from previously described histological evaluation methods [20, 21]. All specimens were examined using a light microscope equipped with a digital imaging system (CX41, Olympus, Japan) by two blinded histologists.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows, version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data were expressed as numbers and percentages. Relationships between categorical variables, including bone formation, osteoblast and osteoclast presence, and fibrosis levels, were analyzed using the chi-square test. When the assumptions of the chi-square test were not met (i.e., when more than 20% of the expected cell frequencies were less than 5), the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test was applied. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical findings

During the experimental period, all rats generally tolerated the surgical procedures well. One rat from the negative control group, one from the graft control group, and one from the melatonin group were lost during the study period. Apart from these losses, no feeding difficulties, surgery-related infections, or systemic complications were observed. The general health status of the remaining animals remained stable throughout the experiment. Accordingly, the final number of animals included in the analysis was 32, with 6 animals in the negative control, graft control, and melatonin groups, and 7 animals in the vitamin E and melatonin + vitamin E groups.

Dıscussıon

Bone tissue plays a crucial role not only in providing mechanical support and serving as a structural framework for the musculoskeletal system, but also in endocrine functions such as calcium homeostasis [22]. Although bone undergoes continuous remodeling under physiological conditions, large defects resulting from tumors, trauma, or metabolic diseases may fail to heal spontaneously and often require surgical intervention [23]. In the oral and maxillofacial region, bone defects that occur following tooth extraction, periodontitis, or cyst and tumor surgeries are particularly challenging, as the localization, three-dimensional configuration, and volume of the defect are key factors determining both treatment selection and procedural complexity. In contemporary approaches, grafts and biomaterials, as well as stem cells and growth factors, are increasingly employed to promote bone regeneration [24]. However, autogenous grafts are associated with disadvantages such as donor site morbidity and the need for additional surgical procedures, while alternative graft materials may present limitations related to insufficient regenerative capacity [25]. Consequently, interest has grown in novel materials and biological agents that may enhance the effectiveness of bone repair.

Although in vitro studies provide valuable preliminary information regarding biomaterials, their clinical relevance cannot always be directly extrapolated due to the absence of immune and inflammatory responses and the limited lifespan of cell cultures [26]. For this reason, animal models are widely used in bone regeneration research [27, 28]. When selecting an appropriate experimental model, factors such as physiological similarity to humans, material compatibility, number of animals, experimental duration, tolerance, and maintenance costs should be evaluated in accordance with international standards. Considering the frequent use of Wistar rats in calvarial defect studies and the potential influence of hormonal variability related to the estrous cycle in female rats, male Wistar rats weighing 250–300 g were used in the present study.

Osteoporosis is a common metabolic disease associated with increased morbidity and mortality, characterized by an imbalance between bone formation and resorption in favor of resorption [29]. It may also affect the oral region by causing microstructural deterioration of alveolar bone, trabecular thinning, and loss of mineral density. Although bisphosphonates used in osteoporosis treatment can increase jawbone mineral density, serious complications such as medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw have been reported [30]. Studies demonstrating reduced mandibular bone density in osteoporotic individuals, evidence highlighting the association between bone density and implant stability and success, experimental data indicating impaired post-extraction healing, and reports of decreased Hounsfield unit values at maxillofacial osteotomy sites collectively emphasize the importance of osteoporosis in oral and maxillofacial treatment planning [31, 32].

Experimental osteoporosis models can be established using surgical (ovariectomy) or non-surgical approaches. Among non-surgical methods, osteoporosis induction using glucocorticoids, immunosuppressive agents, and vitamin A metabolites has been described. The retinoic acid–induced osteoporosis model, first described by Wu et al., has been shown in multiple studies to produce outcomes consistent with osteoporotic pathology [33]. In the present study, a retinoic acid–based model was selected due to its relatively low cost, short experimental duration, minimal invasiveness, and documented reliability in the literature. Osteoporosis was induced via oral gavage administration of retinoic acid for 14 days.

Craniofacial bone defects may arise from surgical procedures, cyst and tumor resections, trauma, or osteonecrosis. Defects that cannot heal spontaneously are defined as “critical-sized defects,” and the creation of such defects is preferred in experimental models to accurately evaluate treatment efficacy [34]. The calvarial defect model is commonly employed due to its relatively low morbidity [35, 36]. Based on literature reports identifying an 8-mm defect in the rat calvarium as critical-sized, an 8-mm defect was created in the parietal bone in this study [36–38].

Bone graft materials used in defect reconstruction are generally classified as autogenous, allogeneic, xenogeneic, or alloplastic. The reparative process is influenced by the osteogenic, osteoinductive, and osteoconductive properties of these materials. Although autogenous grafts remain the gold standard, they are associated with donor site morbidity and increased complication risk. Alloplastic grafts, on the other hand, are widely used clinically due to their biocompatibility and osteoconductive scaffold properties; however, in some cases, they may not provide sufficient bone regeneration when used alone and may require adjunctive techniques [39]. Ryu et al. evaluated customized alloplastic grafts and xenografts in a beagle mandibular model and reported no significant differences in radiographic or histological outcomes at 6 and 12 weeks [40]. In contrast, Costa et al. demonstrated that granular alloplastic grafts yielded superior results compared with paste-form alloplastic materials in a rabbit maxillary sinus augmentation model [41]. In the present study, autogenous grafts were not preferred in order to avoid donor site morbidity and because of their potentially reduced success in osteoporotic conditions. Instead, a granular alloplastic graft was selected in accordance with the literature. The significant difference in new bone formation between the negative control and graft control groups suggests that the alloplastic graft enhanced the baseline reparative response within the defect area.

Melatonin is an endogenous hormone primarily known for its role in regulating circadian rhythm, but it has also been implicated in bone metabolism. Decreased melatonin levels with aging have been suggested to contribute to age-related bone loss and osteoporosis [42]. Zheng et al. reported increased alkaline phosphatase activity in mesenchymal stem cells treated with melatonin and demonstrated enhanced bone formation and mineralization around bone defects in animal models receiving low- and high-dose melatonin supplementation [43]. Similarly, Xie et al. showed that melatonin promotes osteogenesis in mesenchymal stem cells and significantly improves trabecular bone structure based on histological and micro-CT analyses [44]. Moreover, in experimental periodontitis models associated with circadian rhythm disruption, melatonin supplementation has been shown to reduce inflammation and alveolar bone loss [45]. Reviews evaluating clinical and preclinical studies have suggested that melatonin may serve as a potential supportive agent to slow bone loss and increase bone mass, particularly in high-risk populations such as postmenopausal women and shift workers [46]. Collectively, these findings indicate that melatonin may support the repair process in osteoporotic bone defects.

Vitamin E is a potent antioxidant, and numerous preclinical and clinical studies have investigated the relationship between α- and γ-tocopherol and bone metabolism, yielding conflicting results [47]. While some studies have suggested that vitamin E supplementation may reduce bone loss or influence resorption markers [48, 49], others have reported increased osteoclast activity and reduced bone mass [50]. Additionally, negative associations between serum tocopherol levels and bone mineral density have been documented [47, 51]. Therefore, the effects of vitamin E on bone healing appear to be dependent on factors such as dosage, duration, route of administration, and experimental model.

In this study, the effects of intraperitoneally administered melatonin (10 mg/kg), vitamin E (20 mg/kg), and their combination on bone healing were evaluated over a 28-day period in osteoporotic rat calvarial defects. While no bone formation was observed in the negative control group, mild to moderate bone formation was noted in the graft control and vitamin E groups. In contrast, moderate to high levels of bone formation were observed in the melatonin and melatonin plus vitamin E groups, with high-grade bone formation detected exclusively in these two groups. These findings suggest that melatonin may provide a substantial contribution to osteoporotic defect healing, and that combined treatment may also be associated with enhanced bone formation. Although no statistically significant difference was observed between the vitamin E group and the graft control group, suggesting that vitamin E alone did not result in a statistically significant improvement in bone healing under the present experimental conditions, the combination group (vitamin E + melatonin) demonstrated comparable outcomes to the melatonin group.

This finding suggests that vitamin E does not antagonize the effect of melatonin, but neither does it confer additional benefit beyond melatonin alone. Furthermore, the absence of a detectable difference may also be related to the semi-quantitative nature of the histological scoring system, which could limit the sensitivity to detect subtle variations between groups. The conflicting findings reported in the literature regarding vitamin E [48, 51] may provide a biological explanation for this observation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The relatively small number of animals per group and the loss of some specimens during the experimental period may affect the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the evaluation was based on histological and semi-quantitative scoring methods; the inclusion of quantitative imaging techniques such as micro-CT or additional biochemical and immunohistochemical markers could further strengthen the interpretation of the findings. Nevertheless, the present results indicate that melatonin-based approaches warrant further investigation in the context of osteoporotic bone defect healing.

Conclusıon

In this experimental study, the effects of alloplastic grafting, melatonin, and vitamin E administration on bone healing were evaluated in critical-size calvarial bone defects created in osteoporotic rats. Throughout the experimental period, the surgical procedures were well tolerated by the animals, and no major postoperative complications were observed. Histological findings demonstrated that the alloplastic bone graft supported bone healing and enhanced the basic reparative response within the defect area. Melatonin supplementation showed a marked positive effect on bone regeneration, with moderate to high levels of new bone formation being more prominent in the melatonin-treated groups. In contrast, vitamin E administered alone did not provide an additional benefit in bone healing when compared with the graft control group. Although the present study is limited by its histological and semi-quantitative evaluation, the findings suggest that melatonin-based approaches may represent a promising strategy for enhancing bone regeneration in osteoporotic defects. Future studies incorporating quantitative imaging techniques such as micro-CT and well-designed clinical investigations are warranted to further clarify these effects.
