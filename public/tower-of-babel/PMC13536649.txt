Belowground clonal expansion and biomass allocation of Phragmites australis along a sand burial gradient in mobile dunes
Authors: Chaoqun Ba, Ruochen Jia, Zhimin Liu, Jianqiang Qian, Yue Yang, Zhiming Xin, Bo Liu, Quanlai Zhou, Yuan Liu, Zhongcai Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsSand burial is a dominant physical disturbance in arid dune ecosystems, profoundly altering soil moisture, temperature regimes and mechanical resistance, and thereby constraining belowground plant growth. Rhizomatous clonal plants are particularly dependent on belowground strategies to persist and expand under such conditions, yet how sand burial regulates rhizome expansion and biomass allocation remains poorly understood.MethodsWe combined two complementary field components in an active dune system in the Ulan Buh Desert, northwestern China. First, rhizome extension of Phragmites australis was monitored descriptively across three burial-depth categories. Second, rhizome traits, ramet recruitment and biomass allocation were examined across three dune microhabitats: interdune lowland, transition zone and windward slope.ResultsRhizomes in deeper burial categories showed greater extension rate and total expansion length than those in shallow burial. Rhizome morphology exhibited strong habitat-dependent variation: internode length and cross-sectional area were greater on windward slopes and transition zones, while rhizomes in interdune lowlands displayed higher length-to-width ratios. Ramet recruitment pathways also shifted spatially, with rhizome-derived ramets dominating in lowlands and tiller-derived ramets prevailing on windward slopes. Biomass allocation further reflected this gradient, with greater vertical investment in lowlands and increasing horizontal allocation toward windward slopes over the growing season.ConclusionThese results show that P. australis exhibits substantial variation in rhizome expansion, clonal traits and biomass allocation across heterogeneous dune environments. Descriptive observations suggest contrasting rhizome expansion under different burial conditions, while habitat-scale patterns reveal variation in clonal organization and biomass allocation. Introduction

In arid and semi-arid dune ecosystems, plant growth is strongly constrained by physical soil disturbances, among which sand burial and wind erosion are particularly pervasive [1, 2]. Sand burial alters multiple belowground conditions simultaneously, including soil moisture availability, temperature stability and mechanical resistance, thereby exerting direct controls on root and rhizome growth [3–5]. While shallow burial often provides limited insulation from drought stress and temperature extremes, deeper burial layers may provide buffered microenvironments with higher moisture retention and reduced thermal fluctuation [6, 7]. By reshaping the vertical and horizontal distribution of moisture, temperature buffering, and mechanical resistance, sand burial creates a heterogeneous belowground environment in which plant performance depends strongly on how effectively belowground organs exploit and tolerate local conditions. Understanding how plants respond to these contrasting belowground conditions is central to predicting vegetation persistence and dune stabilization processes.

Clonal plants, especially rhizomatous grasses, are widespread and often dominant in mobile dune systems. Their success largely depends on belowground clonal expansion, which allows individuals to escape locally unfavorable conditions, exploit spatial heterogeneity in soil resources, and maintain physiological integration among ramets [6, 8, 9]. Rhizomes function not only in horizontal spread, but also in resource storage, transport, and bud deployment, linking resource-rich patches with stressful microsites and thereby serving as the primary organs through which rhizomatous plants respond to heterogeneous sand burial. Consequently, rhizome traits such as extension rate, internode length and cross-sectional morphology are highly sensitive to soil physical environments and play a decisive role in clonal foraging efficiency [5, 10, 11].

Sand dunes typically exhibit pronounced microtopographic heterogeneity, forming gradients from relatively stable interdune lowlands to highly disturbed windward slopes. Along this gradient, sand burial depth, soil moisture and erosion intensity vary substantially, creating a natural experiment to examine how belowground processes respond to physical disturbance [12]. Previous studies have shown that burial depth can modify clonal growth patterns and induce trade-offs between horizontal rhizome proliferation and vertical shoot development [5, 6]. According to optimal partitioning theory, plants are expected to allocate biomass toward structures that alleviate the most limiting resource [13]. However, most empirical work has focused on root -shoot allocation [14, 15], whereas how rhizomatous clonal plants balance horizontal (rhizome) versus vertical (ramet) biomass under heterogeneous sand burial remains insufficiently explored.

Phragmites australis (Cav.) Trin. ex Steud. (syn. Phragmites communis Trin.) is a rhizomatous grass species widely distributed in China [16]. In the active dune system, it mainly occurs in interdune lowlands and transitional habitats, while clonal spread could extend into other dune microsites [17]. Its extensive rhizome network enables rapid lateral expansion and persistence under sand burial and wind erosion [7, 17]. Owing to its ecological significance in dune habitats and its distinctive clonal structure, P. australis provides a suitable model species for examining how sand burial regulates belowground expansion and biomass allocation. Using P. australis as a model system, we addressed the following questions: (1) how does sand burial depth affect rhizome expansion dynamics; (2) how do rhizome morphological traits and ramet recruitment pathways vary across dune microhabitats; and (3) how does biomass allocation between belowground (horizontal) and aboveground (vertical) components shift in response to dune heterogeneity? We hypothesized that deeper burial would favor rhizome expansion, that rhizome traits, ramet recruitment and biomass allocation would vary among dune microhabitats. Linking sand burial to belowground traits and allocation strategies may provide ecological insights into how clonal plants persist in mobile dunes and may inform vegetation restoration and dune stabilization in aeolian landscapes.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted on the northeastern margin of the Ulan Buh Desert (107°10′-109°09′E, 40°09′-40°55′N) in Inner Mongolia, China. The region experiences a temperate continental climate, with mean annual temperature of 7.6–8.5 °C and annual precipitation of approximately 140 mm, of which nearly 80% occurs between June and August. Aeolian sandy soil and gray-brown desert soil prevail, and wind erosion and sand burial are frequent during the growing season [18]. The landscape is dominated by mobile, semi-fixed, and fixed sand dunes. Among these dune types, mobile dunes experience the strongest aeolian disturbance, which directly affects the physical environment of belowground organs. Therefore, they provide an appropriate system for examining rhizome expansion and biomass allocation in clonal plants.

Plant identification and permissions

Phragmites australis was identified by Quanlai Zhou following standard morphological characters. A voucher specimen has been deposited in the Northeast Biological Specimen Museum, Institute of Applied Ecology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Herbarium code: IFP) (voucher no. IFP0250507). Genotype or haplotype identification was not conducted in this study. The studied population was identified as P. australis based on morphological characteristics and regional occurrence. Field sampling and plant collection were conducted with permission from the Experimental Centre of Desert Forest, Chinese Academy of Forestry (Inner Mongolia, China).

Experimental design and sampling

To address the three research questions, we conducted two complementary but analytically independent experiments at the individual and population scales.

Experiment 1. rhizome expansion responses to burial depth

In May 2021, newly emerged ramets were identified from naturally established wild populations within the study area on the northeastern margin of the Ulan Buh Desert. For each ramet, the attached rhizome tip was carefully excavated to preserve belowground integrity. To compare rhizome expansion under contrasting field burial conditions, a total of 18 intact rhizomes were identified and classified into three burial depth categories, with six rhizomes in each category: shallow (0–20 cm), intermediate (20–40 cm), and deep (40–60 cm). The horizontal extension length of each rhizome was measured at 15-day intervals over a four-month period. Experiment 2. clonal trait and biomass allocation responses across dune microhabitats

In April 2021, a representative active sand dune on the northeastern margin of the Ulan Buh Desert was selected, and nine linear transects were established perpendicular to the prevailing wind direction, spaced 3 m apart. Six permanent quadrats (1 m × 1 m) were positioned along each transect to represent three microhabitats: interdune lowland, transition zone, and windward slope. Field sampling was conducted at three stages: early growing season (April 10), mid-season (June 10), and late season (September 10). During each sampling period, three previously unsampled transects were randomly selected for destructive harvest, and all P. australis individuals within each quadrat were harvested for trait and biomass measurements.

Trait and biomass measurements

Belowground rhizomes were excavated, cleaned and measured for total length, node number and mean internode length (MIL). Rhizome cross-sectional width and height were measured using calipers to calculate cross-sectional area (CSA) and length-to-width ratio (L/W). Aboveground ramets were classified as rhizome-derived ramets and tiller-derived ramets. Biomass was separated into horizontal (rhizome) biomass (HB) and vertical (ramet) biomass (VB) components, oven-dried at 80 °C for 48 h and weighed (Fig. 1). The horizontal-to-vertical biomass ratio (H/V) was used as an index of biomass allocation.

Statistical analysis

For Experiment 2, statistical analyses were designed to address two specific objectives: to examine spatial differences among dune positions within each sampling period, and to assess temporal variation within each dune position. Accordingly, linear mixed-effect models (implemented using the lme4 package in R) [19] were employed to test the effects of time and dune position on rhizome and ramet traits. Spatial variation was tested by treating dune position as a fixed effect within each sampling time, whereas temporal variation was tested by treating sampling time as a fixed effect within each dune position. In all models, transect was included as a random intercept. Count data were modeled using Poisson distribution with a log link function, and ratio data was analyzed as a continuous response variable. Residual diagnostics were performed to assess normality and homoscedasticity of the fitted models. When fixed effects were statistically significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using estimated marginal means (emmeans) with Tukey adjustment for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.1 [20].

Results

Effects of burial depth on rhizome expansion

Because the rhizome-tracking component was based on a limited number of sampled rhizomes (n = 6 per burial category), the following results are presented as descriptive observations rather than formal statistical comparisons. Rhizomes expansion length and expansion rate tended to increase with burial depth. Rhizomes buried at 40–60 cm exhibited the greatest total extension length and highest expansion rates throughout the growing season, whereas shallowly buried rhizomes (0–20 cm) showed limited extension and suffered complete mortality by late July. Expansion rates peaked during early summer and declined thereafter, but remained consistently higher under deep burial compared with shallow and intermediate burial depths. Notably, rhizomes under the shallowest burial depth (0–20 cm) died in late July in our observation (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Rhizome expansion responses to contrasting burial conditions

Sand burial is a dominant physical disturbance in mobile dune ecosystems and is widely recognized to influence belowground conditions relevant to clonal growth. Our results showed greater rhizome expansion under deep sand burial (40–60 cm), whereas shallow burial was associated with limited extension and mortality during the peak growing season. The observed pattern suggests that burial does not uniformly constrain clonal growth and that contrasting burial conditions may differentially influence belowground performance [21].

In mobile sand dunes, the loose surface layers typically have low water-holding capacity and experience strong diurnal temperature fluctuations [22], which may create stressful conditions for shallowly buried rhizomes. In contrast, deeper burial may provide relatively buffered microenvironments with more stable moisture and thermal conditions, potentially favoring belowground persistence and extension. At the same time, deeper burial may impose greater mechanical resistance and constrain vertical emergence. One plausible interpretation is that rhizomatous growth under deeper burial may favor lateral extension under conditions where near-surface persistence is less favorable.

Taken together, the observed differences in rhizome expansion are consistent with the idea that burial depth may alter the balance of belowground constraints and opportunities in mobile dunes. While the specific mechanisms remain unresolved because soil moisture, temperature and mechanical resistance were not directly quantified, the observed patterns align with broader theoretical expectations that clonal plants adjust spatial growth in response to local environmental constraints.

Rhizome morphological variation in response to heterogeneous dune environments

Rhizomatous clonal plants rely on flexible belowground architecture to explore heterogeneous environments [6, 23]. Our study shows that P. australis exhibits marked variation in key rhizome traits along the dune gradient. Mean internode length, cross-sectional area and the cross-sectional length to width ratio all varied among microhabitats and seasons, indicating fine-scale coordination of mechanical support, transport capacity and ramet placement [24].

During the mid-growing season, internodes were significantly longer on windward slopes than in interdune lowlands or transition zones. Longer internodes increase the distance between successive nodes, enabling clonal fragments to bypass small unfavourable patches and rapidly reach more suitable microsites [25]. This pattern may indicate a more exploratory clonal growth form under high disturbance and is broadly consistent with a guerrilla-like growth pattern, in which widely spaced ramets reduce local competition and facilitate exploratory spread into sparsely vegetated slope habitats.

Cross-sectional area of rhizomes was larger in transition zones and windward slopes during early and mid-season, suggesting reinforced structural development under more exposed conditions [26]. Thicker rhizomes with larger cross-sections may represent a morphological response associated with greater structural support and resource transfer under exposed habitat conditions. In contrast, rhizomes in interdune lowlands exhibited higher length to width ratios, forming more slender, elongated profiles. Such geometry may reduce frontal area and friction during penetration through compact or moist substrates while maintaining sufficient mechanical stability.

The convergence of rhizome traits across habitats by late season suggests that initial structural differentiation diminishes as dune conditions become less extreme or as rhizomes age. Overall, the observed variation in rhizome morphology suggests habitat-associated differences in clonal foraging, with P. australis exhibiting longer internodes on slopes, more structurally reinforced rhizomes in exposed habitats, and slender forms in relatively stable lowlands.

Shifts in ramet recruitment pathways across the heterogeneous dune habitats

Successful colonization of mobile dunes depends not only on rhizome spread but also on the timing and mode of ramet production [27]. Our results reveal a clear spatial and temporal shift between rhizome-derived and tiller-derived ramets along the dune gradient.

Interdune lowlands hosted the highest densities of total and tiller-derived ramets in the mid-season. This pattern suggests relatively favorable conditions for dense shoot recruitment and local canopy development, consistent with the potential role of lowlands as important source areas for propagule production. Rhizome-derived ramets also peaked in lowlands, mirroring the concentration of rhizome nodes in this microhabitat.

Towards windward slopes, total ramet number and tiller density declined sharply in mid-season but increased again by late season, driven mainly by tiller-derived ramets. In contrast, rhizome-derived ramets showed a persistent decline from lowlands to slopes. This decoupling indicates that exposed slopes may rely more on secondary tillering from established ramets than on direct emergence from rhizome nodes. One possible explanation is that conditions on windward slopes may constrain successful emergence of rhizome-derived ramets while favoring compensatory tillering of surviving shoots.

These findings suggest that ramet recruitment pathways vary across contrasting dune microhabitats. Under conditions favorable for bud emergence, rhizome derived ramets may contribute more strongly to clonal expansion, whereas tiller derived ramets may become relatively more important where local establishment is constrained. The observed spatiotemporal shift from rhizome- to tiller-dominated recruitment along the lowland to slope gradient therefore indicates variation in clonal regeneration patterns under heterogeneous aeolian environments [28].

Biomass allocation (horizontal- vs. vertical) patterns in response to dune heterogeneity

Biomass allocation patterns integrate the cumulative effects of morphological and demographic responses and provide insight into how clonal plants balance resource capture under heterogeneous stress [11, 29]. Our study shows that P. australis exhibits habitat-associated variation in the balance between horizontal (rhizome) and vertical (shoot) biomass across interdune lowlands, transition zones and windward slopes.

Early in the growing season, horizontal biomass declined from lowlands to slopes, while vertical biomass did not yet differ significantly across habitats. This indicates that clonal investment in rhizomes was concentrated in lowlands, potentially supporting rhizome storage and network maintenance [30]. By late season, vertical biomass in lowlands and transition zones surpassed that on windward slopes, whereas horizontal biomass continued to decline along the gradient towards the slope. These temporal shifts are reflected in decreasing horizontal-to-vertical ratios in lowlands and transition zones but relatively high ratios on slopes.

On windward slopes, vertical growth remained relatively limited, whereas the horizontal-to-vertical biomass ratio was consistently high. One possible explanation is that plants in these more exposed habitats maintain proportionally greater investment in rhizomes, which may contribute to anchorage and persistence under disturbed dune conditions [31]. In contrast, plants in interdune lowlands showed stronger vertical investment as the season progressed. This pattern may reflect more favorable growth conditions in lowlands and a greater tendency to invest in aboveground structures for canopy development and carbon gain.

Taken together, P. australis exhibited clear spatial variation in biomass allocation across dune microhabitats, with greater vertical biomass accumulation in lowlands and relatively higher horizontal investment on slopes [32]. This spatial partitioning of horizontal versus vertical biomass mirrors the division of labour in clonal plants, allowing the population to simultaneously exploit favourable patches and probe into more stressful habitats.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Genotype or haplotype identification of the studied P. australis population was not conducted. Because different lineages of P. australis may vary in growth dynamics, rhizome traits and stress tolerance, the broader applicability of these ecological interpretations should be considered with caution. The rhizome-tracking experiment was based on a relatively small number of sampled rhizomes, and the associated burial-depth patterns should therefore be viewed with caution. In addition, the quadrat survey did not directly quantify burial depth or other environmental variables, including soil moisture and temperature, so the mechanisms discussed here remain inferential. More broadly, because all field observations were made on a single active dune, the extent to which these patterns apply to other dunes or dune systems remains uncertain. Even so, the two complementary experiments provide a useful framework for understanding variation in rhizome growth, clonal traits and biomass allocation in P. australis under heterogeneous dune conditions.

Conclusion

Sand burial is an important component of dune disturbance and may influence belowground clonal growth of P. australis in mobile dunes. Descriptive observations suggested greater rhizome extension under deeper burial, whereas shallow burial was associated with restricted rhizome growth and higher mortality. Second, rhizome traits and ramet recruitment pathways varied among dune microhabitats, indicating that clonal growth responses are closely associated with habitat heterogeneity in active dunes. Third, biomass allocation shifted between horizontal and vertical structures across dune microhabitats, with relatively greater horizontal biomass on windward slopes and greater vertical biomass accumulation in lowlands and transition zones.

Together, these findings suggest that P. australis exhibits coordinated variation in belowground expansion, clonal structure, and biomass allocation that may enhance persistence in mobile dunes. As a dominant rhizomatous species in active dunes, P. australis may contribute to dune stabilization and vegetation development in disturbed sandy habitats. Therefore, understanding its belowground trait variation and biomass allocation patterns may provide useful ecological insights for vegetation restoration and management in aeolian landscapes.
