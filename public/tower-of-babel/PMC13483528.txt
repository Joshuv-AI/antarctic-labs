Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals immune-peritubular myoid cell crosstalk driving testicular interstitial fibrosis in idiopathic non-obstructive azoospermia
Authors: Xiaofeng Tan, Junlong Zhang, Qiyun Yang, Kaiyu Zhu, Yuyao Hou, Zexin Guo, Xinyu Li, Huazong Deng, Chong Chen, Chunhua Deng, Xiangzhou Sun, Yu Chen, Zhihong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfertility is a major global health challenge, and non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) represents the most severe form of male infertility. While a small proportion of NOA cases are caused by congenital factors, the majority arise from unknown causes and are therefore classified as idiopathic non-obstructive azoospermia (iNOA). Understanding the pathogenesis of iNOA is essential for developing effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. In this study, we analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from testicular tissues of patients with iNOA and healthy controls, and validated the findings using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Masson’s trichrome (MT) staining, and Multiplex Immunofluorescence of patient testicular sections. Our results identified peritubular myoid cells (PTMs) as the primary contributors to testicular interstitial fibrosis. Cell–cell communication analysis revealed that macrophage-derived PDGF signaling is significantly upregulated in iNOA and promotes PTMs fibrosis. Fibrotic PTMs were associated with changes in the immune microenvironment, including pro-inflammatory activation of macrophages, T cells, and mast cells, consistent with a potential fibrosis-inflammation interaction. Furthermore, through integrative network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses, we identified regorafenib as a potential therapeutic agent that targets PTM fibrosis. These findings offer new insights into the diagnosis and treatment of iNOA.Graphical abstractSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-026-01600-4.

Introduction

Infertility is a disorder of the male or female reproductive system, defined as the failure to achieve pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular unprotected sexual intercourse [1]. It can result from male, female, or unexplained causes, and it significantly impacts the well-being and harmony of affected families worldwide. According to the World Health Organization, approximately one in six individuals of reproductive age will experience infertility at some point in their lives. Male factors contribute to 30–50% of infertility cases [1–3], often with limited treatment options. Non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) is the most severe form of male infertility, accounting for 10–15% of cases [4]. In addition to known genetic causes of NOA, including Klinefelter syndrome (KS), Y chromosome AZF microdeletions (AZF_Del), and mutations in spermatogenesis-related genes such as TEX11, approximately 70% of patients present with idiopathic non-obstructive azoospermia (iNOA) [5–7], where the etiology remains unknown. Thus, unraveling the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms is critical to advancing diagnosis and treatment.

Recent studies at molecular and cellular levels have begun to shed light on impaired spermatogenesis in iNOA. For instance, dysregulated expression of key transcription factors, such as the upregulation of HES7 and the downregulation of SOX5, has been linked to disruptions in spermatogenic regulatory networks [8]. Mutations in genes critical for meiosis, such as MEIOB, USP26, and TEX11, have also been reported to cause meiotic arrest in iNOA patients [9]. In addition to intrinsic germ cell abnormalities, disrupted cell–cell communication between germ cells and somatic cells, leading to immune microenvironment dysregulation, has emerged as a potential mechanism. For example, elevated numbers of proinflammatory macrophages and increased expression of cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α have been observed in iNOA testes, promoting chronic inflammation unfavorable to spermatogenesis [10]. Moreover, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and upregulation of IL-1β, IL-18, and AIM2 have been implicated in germ cell apoptosis [11].

Despite these advances, the role of testicular interstitial fibrosis in iNOA pathogenesis has been largely overlooked. Excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and tissue stiffening can disrupt the testicular microenvironment and impair spermatogenesis, suggesting fibrosis may be an important, yet underexplored, contributor to iNOA. A recent study indicated that mast cell activation and degranulation may trigger fibrosis in cryptorchidism, leading to spermatogenic failure [12]. Similar fibrotic mechanisms may also exist in iNOA. Although some studies have investigated the roles of Sertoli cells and Leydig cells in male infertility [13, 14], few have addressed the involvement of peritubular myoid cells (PTMs) in the development of testicular interstitial fibrosis during the progression of iNOA. PTMs are known to secrete a variety of key signaling molecules involved in regulation of stem cell regulation, immune activation, inflammatory responses, and other critical cellular processes [15–19]. Moreover, they may modulate the intercellular communication between somatic and germ cells by secreting components of the ECM [15, 20]. Thus, understanding the fibrotic transformation of PTMs is crucial for elucidating the altered testicular microenvironment in iNOA.

Given the high cellular heterogeneity of the testis, conventional transcriptomic approaches lack resolution to characterize individual cell types. The advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and associated analytical tools has enabled detailed exploration of disease mechanisms at single-cell resolution. In this study, a total of 43,911 testicular cells from 3 iNOA patients and 5 healthy controls were analyzed. Our study revealed that testicular interstitial fibrosis is primarily caused by the upregulated PDGF signaling from macrophages, which induces fibrotic transformation of PTMs. Our study investigated the association between PTMs and immune microenvironment remodeling, revealing that fibrotic PTMs upregulate COLLAGEN signaling and highly express APP and collagen ligands, which can bind to CD74 and CD44 receptors on immune cells and contribute to inflammatory activation of immune cells. This mutual regulation between PTMs and immune cells suggests a potential fibrosis-inflammation interaction network. In addition, by performing PTM subpopulation analysis, we identified distinct subtypes associated with fibrosis and mitochondrial function. Furthermore, we screened potential therapeutic agents and identified regorafenib as a promising candidate for targeting PTM-mediated fibrosis in iNOA. These findings provide new insights into the diagnosis and treatment of iNOA.

Materials and methods

Ethics and human testis tissue

In this study, testicular tissues were collected from three patients with iNOA, and three individuals in the healthy control group. The control testicular tissues were obtained from prostate cancer patients who had not undergone androgen deprivation therapy. All donors had confirmed offspring, indicating normal reproductive function during their youth. Testicular samples were collected from areas distant from the tumor site, and pathological reports confirmed the absence of malignancy in the submitted testicular and epididymal tissues. In the iNOA group, histological sections from all three patients showed an absence of mature spermatozoa within the seminiferous tubules. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University.

Histological analysis

Hematoxylin and eosin staining was used to assess histological differences between the iNOA and healthy control groups. Masson's trichrome staining was performed to evaluate testicular fibrosis, while Periodic Acid Schiff (PAS) staining was used to assess the expression of glycogen and polysaccharide substances. Formalin-fixed tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 4 µm. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated in graded ethanol (100%, 95%, 85%, 75%), and subjected to standard H&E and Masson's trichrome staining protocols. Images were acquired using a Leica DMi8 microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). For each Masson-stained section, identical high-power fields were selected, and fibrosis was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) as follows: Relative collagen content (%) = collagen-positive area / total tissue area. PAS stain kit was applied and performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the relative PAS content of each section was subsequently quantified.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry

To explore the expression and distribution of PDGFRA, Collagen I, α-SMA, CD68, Tryptase, and CD8 in iNOA, testicular tissue sections from iNOA patient and healthy control were subjected to multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) using the PANO Multiplex IHC kit (Panovue, Cat. No. 10234100100) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, testicular sections were incubated in a hot-air oven at 65 °C overnight, deparaffinized in fresh xylene for 10 min three times, rehydrated in graded ethanol (100–95–70%), and washed three times with PBS. Antigen retrieval was performed using a microwave heating method and then cooled in an ice-water bath for at least 15 min. After blocking in blocking solution (Panovue, Cat. No. 0018001120) for 15 min at room temperature, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies for 30 min, secondary antibodies for 10 min, and TSA Opal fluorophores for 10 min. Antigen retrieval, blocking, primary antibody incubation, and TSA Opal fluorophore staining were repeated for each marker. Finally, the sections were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. D9542) for 5 min and mounted. Whole-section imaging was performed using the panoVIEW VS200 (China). The following primary antibodies were used: PDGFRA (AiFang Biological, Cat. No. AFRM0324, 1:1000), CD68 (AiFang Biological, Cat. No. AFRM0021, 1:1000), Collagen I (AiFang Biological, Cat. No. AFRP0017, 1:800), α-SMA (AiFang Biological, Cat. No. AFMM0002, 1:1000), Tryptase (AiFang Biological, Cat. No. AF20308, 1:1000), and CD8 (AiFang Biological, Cat. No. AFRM0004, 1:1000).

Data sources and processing

The single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE149512, which includes testicular tissue samples from three iNOA patients, three KS patients, and five healthy adult controls, was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/) [13]. A Seurat object was constructed using Seurat functions, and data quality control was performed based on the following criteria: nFeature_RNA > 500 & nFeature_RNA < 9000; mt_percent < 25; and nCount_RNA < quantile (nCount_RNA, 0.97) & nCount_RNA < 35,000. Following the standard preprocessing workflow recommended on the Seurat official website, normalization and scaling were performed. Batch effect correction and integration of the eight samples were conducted using the Harmony algorithm [21].

Cell identification and clustering analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the integrated Seurat object, and the top 20 principal components (PCs) were selected to construct a K-nearest neighbor (KNN) graph. The edge weights between cells were defined to enable subsequent UMAP dimensionality reduction and clustering. The resolution parameter of the FindClusters function was set to 0.1. Clusters were annotated based on the expression of well-established marker genes, resulting in the identification of 12 distinct cell populations.

GO enrichment analysis and fibrosis score

Based on normalized UMI counts, the top 500 upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package [22]. DEGs specific to PTMs (upregulated and downregulated) and upregulated genes in immune cells were identified using the FindMarkers function (logfc.threshold = 0.5, min.pct = 0.25) and filtered with pct.1 > 0.25, adjusted p-value < 0.05, and absolute avg_log2FC > 0.5. GO enrichment analysis was repeated on these subsets. Seven fibrosis-related gene sets were retrieved from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/) [23]. The AddModuleScore function was used to calculate fibrosis scores across the 12 identified cell types. The four cell types with the highest fibrosis contribution were selected, and fibrosis scores were calculated separately in the iNOA and control groups.

Cell communication analysis

To investigate intercellular communication networks, we applied the CellChat package (version 1.6.1) [24]. Dominant signaling cell groups and their roles in the communication network were identified using the netAnalysis_signalingRole_scatter function. Target signaling pathways were prioritized using rankNet and netVisual_heatmap functions. The contribution of each ligand-receptor pair was evaluated using the netAnalysis_contribution and netAnalysis_signalingRole_network functions, identifying cell-type-specific roles. Interactions between PTMs and immune cells were further visualized using the netVisual_bubble function.

Reclustering of cell subtypes and pseudotime trajectory analysis

To investigate PTMs heterogeneity, a KNN graph was constructed using the top 10 principal components of the PTMs subset. PTMs were reclustered into four subpopulations using the FindClusters function (resolution = 0.1). Fibrosis-, proliferation-, and mitochondria-related gene sets were obtained from the MSigDB database. Gene set scores were calculated for each subpopulation using the irGSEA.score function, and subclusters were annotated accordingly. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted using the monocle package (version 2.24.1). The newCellDataSet function was used to create a cellData object. After filtering lowly expressed genes, highly variable genes selected by monocle were used as ordering genes. Dimensionality reduction was performed using the DDRTree algorithm. Cells were ordered along the trajectory based on gene expression trends.

HdWGCNA Analysis

To identify key genes involved in PTMs fibrosis, high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) [25, 26] was performed. PTMs were extracted from the scRNA-seq dataset, and low-expressing genes were removed using the SetupForWGCNA function. Metacells were constructed via the MetacellsByGroups function. After selecting an appropriate soft threshold, a co-expression network was built, and module eigengenes and intramodular connectivity were calculated. A bubble plot was generated to visualize module expression across the four PTM subclusters. DEGs with avg_log2FC > 1.2 were defined as PTMDEGs. The intersection of PTMDEGs, module genes, and fibrosis-related genes was visualized with a Venn diagram. A total of 25 genes were identified as core fibrosis hub genes in PTMs.

Drug target analysis

To explore potential therapeutic targets, fibrosis hub genes were analyzed using the “DGIdb Drug Targets 2024” database available on the Enrichr platform (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/). Molecular docking of the top 10 FDA-approved candidate drugs was performed using Dockeasy platform (https://www.dockeasy.cn), AutoDock Tools, AutoGrid, and AutoDock Vina (version 1.2.0), and the results were visualized with PyMOL. Among these, regorafenib was identified as a promising small-molecule inhibitor targeting PTM fibrosis-related genes.

Results

Establishment of a single-cell transcriptomic landscape of human iNOA testis

To investigate the cellular landscape of the testes in iNOA patients, we performed a comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic analysis using testicular samples from five healthy adult males and three patients with idiopathic non-obstructive azoospermia. After stringent quality control, a total of 43,911 cells were retained for downstream analysis. Based on the expression of established marker genes, twelve distinct cell types were identified (Fig. 1A and C), including spermatogonia (SPG), characterized by the expression of UTF1, FGFR3, MAGEA4, and UCHL1; spermatocytes, expressing SYCP3, SYCP1, and PIWIL1; round spermatids, marked by ZPBP and TSSK4; elongating spermatids, expressing TNP2, PRM2, and PRM1; vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMs), characterized by FAM129A and NOTCH3; endothelial cells, expressing VWF, CD34, and PECAM1; Leydig cells, characterized by STAR, CYP11A1, IGF1, INHBA, and INSL3; peritubular myoid cells, expressing ACTA2 and MYH11; Sertoli cells, marked by SOX9 and WT1; macrophages, expressing CD86, CD14, CD163, and LYZ; CD8⁺ T cells, characterized by CD8A, CD3D, and CD3E; and mast cells, marked by KIT and CPA3. To further explore the transcriptional and functional specificity of each cell type, Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was performed. The enrichment results were consistent with the known biological functions of the corresponding cell types (Fig. 1D). Notably, PTMs were significantly enriched in fibrosis-related pathways such as extracellular structure organization, extracellular matrix organization, and TGF-beta signaling. In contrast, macrophages, CD8⁺ T cells, and mast cells were significantly enriched in immune activation pathways including regulation of inflammatory response, leukocyte-mediated immunity, and mast cell-mediated immunity, respectively. These findings point to a potential interplay between fibrosis and immune microenvironment remodeling in the pathogenesis of iNOA.

PTMs are the primary contributors to testicular fibrosis

To examine whether testicular interstitial fibrosis is abnormally activated in iNOA patients, we performed histological evaluation on testicular sections from three healthy controls and three iNOA patients using hematoxylin and eosin staining and Masson's trichrome staining (Fig. 2A). In the control group, seminiferous tubules exhibited normal architecture, with the presence of Sertoli cells, multiple stages of germ cells, and mature spermatozoa. The surrounding interstitial cells showed no evidence of hyperplasia. In contrast, testicular sections from iNOA patients revealed seminiferous tubules containing only Sertoli cells and lacking mature germ cells, accompanied by a notable proliferation of interstitial cells. Quantification of relative collagen content demonstrated a significant increase in collagen and myofiber deposition in the iNOA group compared to controls, indicating pronounced interstitial fibrosis (Fig. 2B). To identify the specific cell types contributing to this fibrotic phenotype, we applied a fibrosis scoring system to each cell type using seven fibrosis-related gene sets from the MSigDB database (Fig. 2E). We found that vascular smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, leydig cells, and peritubular myoid cells were the main contributors to testicular fibrosis, with the peritubular myoid cells displaying the highest fibrotic activity. Further stratification of these four cell types into iNOA and control groups revealed that PTMs from healthy controls had very low expression of fibrosis-associated genes, while PTMs from iNOA patients exhibited the highest expression among all cell types (Fig. 2F). Interestingly, we observed substantial transcriptional similarities between Leydig cells and PTMs, both in marker gene expression and fibrosis scoring. Literature suggests that Leydig cells and PTMs may originate from a common progenitor population that persists through prepuberty before diverging into distinct lineages [27]. This may explain their overlapping gene expression profiles.

To further explore the unique biological functions associated with PTM-specific gene expression changes, we performed Gene Ontology enrichment analyses separately for upregulated and downregulated genes in PTMs (Fig. 2H). Upregulated genes were enriched in classical fibrosis-related pathways, including the WNT signaling pathway, TGF-β response, and fibroblast proliferation. In contrast, downregulated genes were predominantly associated with mitochondrial function, including mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled electron transport, oxidative phosphorylation, and aerobic electron transport chain processes. Subsequently, we selected twelve key genes from four major fibrosis-related pathways and visualized their expression differences between iNOA and control groups using violin plots (Fig. 2G). Recent studies have shown that PTMs can secrete a variety of signaling molecules involved in key pathways, such as GDNF, PDGFRA, EGFR, PTCH1, and PPARA [17, 18, 28]. For example, the proliferation of spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs) is regulated by GDNF secreted from PTMs [15]. Furthermore, PTMs modulate the secretion of extracellular matrix components, thereby regulating cell–cell communication between somatic and germ cells [20].

In summary, PTMs not only serve as a major contributor to testicular interstitial fibrosis but also act as a critical interface in the testicular microenvironment, mediating the interactions between structural and germ cells. Thus, investigating the PTM-associated cell communication network is essential for understanding the pathogenesis of iNOA.

Next, we quantified the number and overall strength of signaling pathways (Figure S1B), and observed that both metrics were significantly elevated in the iNOA group compared to healthy controls. By comparing the strength of each signaling pathway between the two groups (Fig. 3B), we identified three fibrosis-related signaling pathways that were significantly upregulated in iNOA patients: PDGF, NOTCH, and WNT pathways. To determine the primary signaling pathway mediating PTM-associated fibrosis, we generated a pathway-specific heatmap to illustrate the interaction strength between PTMs and other cell types for each fibrosis signaling pathway (Figure S1C & S1D). Notably, PDGF signaling from macrophages to PTMs was significantly enhanced in the iNOA group compared to controls. Based on this finding, we reconstructed the regulatory network of the PDGF signaling pathway (Fig. 3C & D). In healthy controls, VSMs acted as the primary signal senders, while Leydig cells, PTMs, and VSMs served as the main receivers. However, in the iNOA group, macrophages replaced VSMs as the dominant signal-sending population, while Leydig cells and PTMs remained the principal receivers. It is noteworthy that VSMs and Leydig cells were involved in regulating PDGF signaling in both groups. A ligand-receptor pair contribution analysis further revealed a shift in dominant ligand-receptor interactions: in controls, PDGFA-PDGFRB was the main contributing pair, whereas in iNOA patients, PDGFB-PDGFRA became the predominant signaling axis (Fig. 3E).

This shift was supported by increased PDGFB expression in macrophages and concurrent upregulation of PDGFRA in PTMs in the iNOA group (Fig. 3H & I), suggesting that PDGFB-PDGFRA constitutes the major signaling pair driving PDGF pathway activation in the fibrotic microenvironment of iNOA testes. The increased expression of α-SMA in the testes of the iNOA group indicated an elevated number of PTMs and aggravated fibrosis in iNOA patients. Subsequently, PTMs exhibited increased expression of Collagen I, and the PDGFRA signaling pathway, which is closely associated with the fibrotic process of PTMs, was significantly enhanced in the testes of the iNOA group (Fig. 3F & G).

To further explore signaling interactions associated with germ cell niches, we analyzed intercellular communication targeting Scytes. Global interaction analysis showed that PTMs, Leydig cells, and Sertoli cells displayed relatively higher interaction counts and weights toward Scytes compared with other cell types (Figure S2A). These three somatic cell populations were therefore selected as major signaling senders for subsequent analysis. Pathway enrichment revealed that PTN, CADM, GRN, and MK signaling were detected in the HC group, whereas MK, APP, NEGR, and CD99 pathways were identified in iNOA group (Figure S2B). Previous studies indicated that PTN plays a key role in spermatogenesis, and downregulation of PTN signaling pathway was linked to age-related reductions in male fertility. We compared the PTN signaling network between HC and iNOA groups, which showed stronger communication from Leydig cells to Scytes in HC group, whereas this interaction was reduced in iNOA group (Figure S2C).

To further investigate the role of the PDGF signaling pathway in another form of NOA, we performed a comprehensive transcriptomic analysis of the cellular landscape in testicular tissues from three patients with Klinefelter syndrome and five healthy adult males. A total of 12 cell types were identified (Figure S3A). Using seven fibrosis-related gene sets obtained from the MSigDB database, we conducted systematic fibrosis scoring across all cell types and subsequently performed comparative analyses focusing on the four cell populations exhibiting the most pronounced fibrotic signatures, namely VSMs, endothelial cells, Leydig cells, and PTMs. The results showed that fibrosis-related gene expression remained at low levels in PTMs from healthy controls, whereas PTMs from KS patients exhibited markedly elevated fibrosis-associated gene expression (Figure S3B). To elucidate the mechanisms underlying PTMs fibrosis in KS, we conducted intercellular communication analysis using the CellChat algorithm. Consistent with the mechanism observed in iNOA, PTMs in KS patients underwent fibrotic remodeling in association with upregulated macrophage-derived PDGF signaling (Figure S3C). In control patients, VSMs were the predominant source of outgoing signals, whereas Leydig cells, PTMs, and VSMs represented the major signal-receiving populations. In contrast, in KS samples, macrophages became the dominant signaling source, replacing VSMs, while Leydig cells and PTMs remained the principal receivers (Figure S3D). A ligand-receptor pair contribution analysis further demonstrated a shift in the dominant signaling axis: PDGFA-PDGFRB was the principal interacting pair in controls, whereas PDGFB-PDGFRA became the predominant pathway in KS patients (Figure S3E).

PTMs remodel the immune microenvironment and induce inflammatory activation of immune cells

Having investigated the role of PTMs as signal receivers in testicular interstitial fibrosis in iNOA, we next aimed to explore the impact of PTMs as signal senders on the immune microenvironment of the testis. We first compared the proportions of immune cells among somatic cells and found that macrophages, T cells, and mast cells were significantly increased in the iNOA group compared to the HC group (Fig. 4A). Moreover, multiplex immunofluorescence validation showed that the numbers of CD8⁺ T cells, CD68⁺ macrophages, and Tryptase⁺ mast cells in the testicular interstitium of iNOA patients were significantly higher than those in healthy controls (Fig. 4B & C). To further investigate the functional alterations of immune cells, we performed GO enrichment analysis of the upregulated genes in the three immune cell types in iNOA. Due to the limited number of mast cells in the control group, GO analysis was only feasible for macrophages and CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 4E). The results revealed that both macrophages and CD8⁺ T cells were enriched in inflammation-related activation pathways. For instance, the upregulated genes in macrophages were significantly enriched in the “response to lipopolysaccharide” pathway [29], which activates innate immune responses and induces the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IFN-β [30]. Similarly, CD8⁺ T cells were enriched in the “response to GM-CSF” pathway [31], which promotes macrophage proliferation, T cell activation, and enhances phagocytic and antigen-presenting functions of macrophages [32–35]. We next used bubble plots to display all significantly interacting ligand-receptor pairs between PTMs and immune cells, alongside the expression differences of the corresponding genes (Fig. 4F & G & H). In the iNOA group, PTMs were found to predominantly interact with macrophages, T cells, and mast cells by secreting ligand proteins such as APP and collagen-related proteins including COL1A1. These ligands primarily target CD44 and CD74 receptors expressed on immune cells. CD44 is a cell surface receptor for hyaluronan, a key structural component of the extracellular matrix, and plays a critical role in various inflammatory diseases [36]. CD74, the receptor for macrophage migration inhibitory factor, is involved in antigen presentation, inflammatory responses, and immune cell recruitment [37]. Previous studies have shown that CD44 can recruit and regulate the function of macrophages and T cells in diseases such as atherosclerosis [38] and asthma [39]. Additionally, CD44 contributes to the proliferation of mast cells during terminal differentiation in skin tissues [40]. Similarly, CD74 expressed on the surface of alveolar macrophages has been shown to activate the p44/p42 MAPK signaling pathway, leading to the production of proinflammatory chemokines such as MIP-2 and KC [41]. CD74 is also implicated in the regulation of T cell activation under inflammatory conditions [42].

Based on these findings, we propose a feedback model in which macrophages promote PTM fibrosis via PDGF signaling, and the Fibrotic PTMs further remodel the immune microenvironment by upregulating COLLAGEN signaling and overexpressing APP and collagen-related ligands. This correlates with pro-inflammatory activation of T cells, macrophages, and mast cells, supporting the hypothesis of a fibrosis-inflammation positive feedback interaction.

Based on the functional enrichment of upregulated and downregulated genes, we performed fibrosis and mitochondrial function scoring for each cluster and named the four subpopulations accordingly: Fibro + PTM, Mt + PTM, PTM1, and PTM2 (Figure S3A & B & C). These four subpopulations were then subjected to trajectory analysis to infer their developmental progression (Fig. 5A & B). The trajectory assigned PTMs into three distinct states. Based on the differential expression of cell cycle-related genes, PTM1 and Mt + PTM (corresponding to state 2) were placed at the beginning of the pseudotime trajectory (Figure S3D & E), while Fibro + PTM represented the terminal state (Fig. 5D). Pseudotime heatmaps and gene expression plots revealed the dynamic functional transitions across PTM subpopulations during pseudotime progression (Fig. 5C & E). At the beginning of the trajectory, PTMs were enriched in mitochondrial-related pathways, while toward the end, their functions were predominantly associated with fibrosis-related pathways. Discussion

Idiopathic non-obstructive azoospermia is one of the most severe forms of male infertility. It is characterized by impaired spermatogenesis in the absence of identifiable genetic, endocrine, structural, or infectious causes, resulting in a complete lack of sperm in the ejaculate. Currently, there is no effective treatment for iNOA. This may be due to the fact that most patients are diagnosed at reproductive age, by which time irreversible damage to spermatogenesis has already occurred. In this study, we constructed a comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic atlas of the testes from iNOA patients and deepened our understanding of the spermatogenic microenvironment from the perspective of PTMs fibrosis. Our findings may provide new insights into the diagnosis and treatment of iNOA.

Recent studies have increasingly emphasized the critical role of the testicular microenvironment in maintaining normal spermatogenesis. PTMs, as an essential component, are contractile cells located on the outer surface of the seminiferous tubule basement membrane. They are involved not only in maintaining the structural integrity and transport of tubular contents, but also in supporting the spermatogonial stem cell niche and mediating interactions between somatic and germ cells through paracrine signaling and extracellular matrix remodeling [15, 20, 48]. Thus, PTMs act as a crucial communication hub between somatic and germ cells.

Testicular interstitial fibrosis has been recognized as a hallmark of testicular dysfunction [49–52]. In iNOA patients, peritubular fibrosis is frequently observed in testicular biopsies. Fibrosis in the peritubular region is characterized by abnormal ECM deposition, fibrotic phenotypic transformation of PTMs, and changes in their functional status. These alterations lead to thickening of the basement membrane and impaired signaling between PTMs and Sertoli cells, ultimately disrupting the microenvironment required for SSC survival and differentiation. For example, testosterone has been shown to upregulate GDNF expression in PTMs via activation of the androgen receptor [53], thereby promoting SSC self-renewal and proliferation. PTM fibrosis may impair their responsiveness to testosterone, resulting in reduced GDNF secretion and SSC niche dysfunction. Moreover, PTMs secrete PEDF [54], a potent anti-angiogenic factor that maintains the avascularity of seminiferous tubules [55]. PEDF is also known for its anti-fibrotic properties, and its expression is negatively correlated with the degree of fibrosis, highlighting the important role of PTMs in regulating testicular fibrosis. Given their central role in supporting spermatogenesis, elucidating the upstream inducers and mechanisms of PTM fibrotic transformation is critical for understanding the pathogenesis of iNOA.

Histological analysis using hematoxylin–eosin staining, Masson's trichrome staining, and multiplex immunofluorescence revealed significant fibrosis in the PTM region of iNOA testes. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis further demonstrated that PTMs were the primary contributors to testicular interstitial fibrosis in iNOA patients, whereas they exhibited minimal fibrotic features in healthy controls. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we analyzed intercellular communication among somatic cells and found that macrophages significantly activated the PDGF signaling pathway in PTMs, promoting their fibrotic transformation. This finding was further validated in KS patients, confirming the critical role of the PDGF signaling pathway.

Consistent with the communication patterns identified in the present study, PTN signaling has been reported to play an important role in spermatogenesis and in supporting the germ cell microenvironment, and was detected in the HC group in our study. In contrast, signaling interactions targeting Scytes in iNOA group were primarily represented by MK, APP, NEGR, and CD99 pathways. These pathways are generally associated with cell adhesion or broad intercellular communication rather than established regulators of spermatogenesis. The absence of PTN-mediated niche signaling together with the predominance of adhesion-related pathways suggests that the signaling environment surrounding germ cells differs between HC and iNOA groups.

On the other hand, fibrotic PTMs in turn upregulated COLLAGEN signaling and secreted APP and collagen-related ligands, which targeted CD44 and CD74 receptors on immune cells. Recent research has revealed that CD44 and CD74 play key roles in inflammation and immune regulation [37, 42]. For instance, CD44 is a surface receptor for hyaluronan, a major ECM component, and is involved in several inflammatory diseases. Anti-CD44 antibodies have been shown to suppress inflammation in mouse models of colitis, arthritis, dermatitis, experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, and IL-2-induced vascular leak syndrome [56–59]. CD44 also regulates macrophage and T cell recruitment and function in atherosclerosis [38] and asthma [39], and mediates mast cell invasion [60] and terminal differentiation [40]. CD74, the receptor for macrophage migration inhibitory factor, contributes to antigen presentation, inflammatory responses, and immune cell recruitment. In alveolar macrophages, CD74 activation induces proinflammatory chemokines such as MIP-2 and KC via the p44/p42 MAPK pathway [41], and it also regulates T cell activation [42]. These findings underscore the central roles of CD44 and CD74 in inflammation and immune modulation. GO enrichment analysis of immune cells in our study confirmed that they were in an activated proinflammatory state, suggesting a fibrosis-inflammation positive feedback loop between PTMs and immune cells.

After characterizing intercellular communication, we further investigated the intrinsic molecular mechanisms of PTM fibrosis by identifying PTM subpopulations and performing pseudotime trajectory analysis. Early in the trajectory, PTMs highly expressed mitochondria-related genes, whereas fibrotic markers were upregulated in the later stages. Our results suggest a progressive PTM remodeling process characterized by mitochondrial functional decline followed by activation of fibrotic transcriptional programs, ultimately leading to heterogeneous fibrotic PTM substates. Mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in fibrosis in various organs. In patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic reprogramming have been observed in alveolar epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages. These cells exhibit reduced mitochondrial numbers, impaired biosynthesis, and decreased turnover, which collectively contribute to tissue injury and fibrosis in the lung [61]. Moreover, deficiency of PINK1, a key regulator of mitophagy, impairs mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby promoting apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells and contributing to the progression of pulmonary fibrosis [62]. Correlation analysis in our data revealed a significant negative association between mitochondrial function and fibrotic gene expression in PTMs, suggesting the presence of distinct functional PTM subtypes. Thus, targeting the Fibro + PTM subtype specifically may be a promising therapeutic strategy.

We applied hdWGCNA to identify fibrosis-related gene modules in Fibro + PTMs and intersected module genes, differentially expressed genes, and a curated fibrosis gene set to define hub genes. Drug target prediction based on these hub genes identified PDGFRA, DDR2, and PTEN as potential therapeutic targets, along with regorafenib as a candidate drug. PDGFRA is a key receptor in the PDGF pathway, which we found to be essential for PTM fibrotic transformation. Previous studies have shown that PDGFRA activates the PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways to promote fibrosis [63, 64]. DDR2 activation enhances MMP-2 expression and activity, contributing to the progression of liver fibrosis [65, 66].

Regorafenib is an administered multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor that has been approved for the treatment of several cancers. In recent years, studies have demonstrated the antifibrotic potential of regorafenib in various disease models. For example, in gastric cancer, regorafenib combined with immunotherapy has been shown to reduce tumor-associated fibrosis [43]. In HepG2 and SMMC-7721 HCC cell lines, multiple studies have demonstrated that regorafenib obstructs Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) through the inhibition of hepatocyte growth factor mediated Snail upregulation and the suppression of the STAT3 and ERK pathway [44]. In canine models, it reduces collagen deposition and fibroblast activation in the subconjunctival tissue, effectively preventing postoperative scar formation [45]. Additionally, regorafenib has exhibited significant efficacy in attenuating pulmonary fibrosis [46, 47]. Based on these findings, we speculate that regorafenib may inhibit the fibrosis-inflammation network in iNOA by targeting PDGFRA and other fibrosis-associated genes, representing a novel therapeutic strategy for this disease.

We acknowledge several limitations in the current study. Due to the inherent challenges in obtaining human testicular tissue, the sample size was limited. Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, it is difficult to establish a direct causal relationship between PTM fibrosis and impaired spermatogenesis. In addition, multiplex immunohistochemistry provided direct evidence within the native tissue context of PDGF signaling activation, suggesting a potential link to PTM fibrotic features. Future studies, including gene overexpression or knockdown experiments in animal models and analyses of larger cohorts, will be important to further explore and validate these observations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study constructed a comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic map of iNOA testes and identified the macrophage derived PDGF signaling pathway as a major driver of PTM fibrosis. We observed associations between Fibro + PTMs and immune cells that are consistent with a potential fibrosis-inflammation interaction, providing new insights into the testicular microenvironment. By identifying Fibro + PTM subtypes and key hub genes, we proposed potential drug targets and candidate therapeutics such as regorafenib, which may offer new avenues for the diagnosis and treatment of iNOA.
