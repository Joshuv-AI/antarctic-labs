Embedding ℓ 2 \ell_{2} and J J into subspaces of J ​ T JT and J ​ T ∗ JT^{*} Thanks: 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary 46B03, 46B20.
Spiros A. Argyros, Manuel González, Pavlos Motakis

Abstract
. In the first part of the paper we show that every closed subspace of J ​ T JT or J ​ T ∗ JT^{*} contains ℓ 2 \ell_{2} complemented in J ​ T JT or J ​ T ∗ JT^{*} respectively, and J ​ T JT contains uncomplemented copies of ℓ 2 \ell_{2} . As a result, the predual ℬ \mathcal{B} of J ​ T JT , as well as the spaces J ​ T JT and J ​ T ∗ JT^{*} , are subprojective and superprojective. In the second part, we prove that every weakly Cauchy sequence that is not weakly convergent in J ​ T JT has a subsequence equivalent to the basis of J J . Hence, every non-reflexive subspace of J ​ T JT contains an isomorphic copy of J J , and every Schauder basic sequence in J ​ T JT has a subsequence which is equivalent either to the basis of ℓ 2 \ell_{2} or to the basis of J J . Moreover these subspaces may be selected to be complemented in J ​ T JT .



1.
Introduction

The James tree space
JT
, introduced more than half a century ago by R.C. James
[
16
]
, has become a central example for understanding the structure of Banach spaces beyond the classical ones. Its fundamental properties include that
JT
is separable, it contains no copies of
\ell_{1}
and
JT^{*}
is non-separable, as proved by James. Furthermore,
JT
is the dual of a Banach space
\mathcal{B}
, and the quotient
JT^{*}/\mathcal{B}
is isometric to
\ell_{2}(2^{\mathbb{N}})
(due to J. Lindenstrauss and C. Stegall
[
18
]
), which yields the decomposition
JT^{**}=JT\oplus\ell_{2}(2^{\mathbb{N}})
. Finally, every seminormalized weakly null sequence in
JT
has a subsequence equivalent to
\ell_{2}
, a result established by I. Amemiya and I. Ito
[
2
]
.

The norm of
JT
is defined on the space of real-valued functions on the dyadic tree
\mathcal{T}
with finite support as follows:

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}=\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}}\left(\sum_{\alpha\in S}x(\alpha)\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

where the supremum is taken over all families
\mathcal{P}
of pairwise disjoint segments in
\mathcal{T}
.

The space
JT
is defined as the completion of the space
(c_{00}(\mathcal{T}),\|\cdot\|_{JT})
. Notably, for every branch
\sigma
of
\mathcal{T}
, the sequence
(e_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\sigma}
is isometrically equivalent to the basis of the James quasi-reflexive space
J

[
15
]
. Consequently,
(e_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\sigma}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}e_{\sigma}^{**}
, and the family
(e_{\sigma}^{**})_{\sigma\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}}
is isometrically equivalent to the standard basis of
\ell_{2}(2^{\mathbb{N}})
. Furthermore, every sequence
(e_{\alpha_{n}})_{n}
with
(\alpha_{n})_{n}
a sequence of pairwise incomparable nodes is isometrically equivalent to the
\ell_{2}
basis.

These properties are not merely specific to
JT
; rather, they reflect the underlying structure of the broader class of separable Banach spaces that do not contain
\ell_{1}
and possess a non-separable dual, as indicated by the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1

(Argyros, Dodos & Kanellopoulos
[
5
]
)
.

Let
X
be a separable Banach space containing no copies of
\ell_{1}
with
X^{*}
non-separable. Then there exists a tree-basis
(e_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{T}}
in
X
satisfying the following properties:

(1)

For every branch
\sigma
of
\mathcal{T}
, we have
(e_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\sigma}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}x_{\sigma}^{**}\neq 0
.

(2)

For every sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n}
of pairwise incomparable nodes, we have
e_{\alpha_{n}}\xrightarrow{w}0
.

(3)

The family
(x_{\sigma}^{**})_{\sigma\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}}
is
w^{*}
-accumulated to
0
and it is unconditional.

At the boundaries of this class, one encounters exotic separable spaces, such as W.T. Gowers’ tree space
GT

[
11
]
, such that every infinite-dimensional closed subspace contains a tree-basis satisfying the aforementioned properties. This actually yields that
GT
does not contain
\ell_{1}
and has non-separable dual. For a comprehensive study of such spaces, we refer the reader to
[
4
]
.

In the present paper, we complete the study of the isomorphic structure of the subspaces of
JT
, providing a further understanding of the isomorphic structure of its natural relatives
\mathcal{B}
,
JT^{*}
and
JT^{**}
. As a consequence, we establish the subprojectivity and superprojectivity of
\mathcal{B}
,
JT
, and
JT^{*}
. In particular, we prove that every infinite-dimensional closed subspace of
JT
or
JT^{*}
contains an isomorphic copy of
\ell_{2}
that is complemented in the respective ambient space. Furthermore, every non-reflexive subspace of
JT
contains an isomorphic copy of the James space
J
which is also complemented in
JT
. We also recall that, as shown in
[
3
]
, every subspace of
JT
with a non-separable dual contains a subspace isomorphic to
JT
and complemented in
JT
.

The following theorem encapsulates these results and provides a complete description of the isomorphic structure of the subspaces of
JT
:

Theorem 1.2
.

The following facts hold:

(1)

Every infinite-dimensional closed subspace of
JT
or
JT^{*}
contains a further subspace isomorphic to
\ell_{2}
that is complemented in
JT
or
JT^{*}
, respectively.

(2)

Every closed non-reflexive subspace of
JT
contains an isomorphic copy of
J
which is complemented in
JT
.

(3)

Every closed subspace of
JT
with a non-separable dual contains a subspace
Y
isomorphic to
JT
that is complemented in
JT
.

The embeddability of
\ell_{2}
into the subspaces of
JT
was first established by James
[
16
]
, and Amemiya and Ito later extended this by showing that every seminormalized weakly null sequence in
JT
admits a subsequence equivalent to the basis of
\ell_{2}
. The following result serves as a further extension of the Amemiya-Ito theorem:

Theorem 1.3
.

Every seminormalized basic sequence in
JT
has a subsequence equivalent to the standard basis of either
\ell_{2}
or
J
, and the closed subspace it generates is complemented in
JT
.

We also prove the following projectivity properties (see Definition
4.1
):

Theorem 1.4
.

The spaces
\mathcal{B}
,
JT
,
JT^{*}
, and all of their higher-order dual spaces are both subprojective and superprojective.

The paper is organized as follows. After the preliminaries in Section 2, Section 3 presents a revisited approach to the basic properties of
JT
. Our proofs are based on the Amemiya-Ito theorem and a result implicitly contained in
[
18
]
:

Proposition 1.5
.

Every seminormalized block sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
in
JT
has a subsequence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
such that
\lim_{k}\sigma^{*}(x_{n_{k}})=0
, with the exception of countably many branches of
\mathcal{T}
.

This result, coupled with the Amemiya-Ito theorem, easily yields that
\ell_{1}
is not contained in
JT
, and the remaining basic properties of
JT
emerge as straightforward consequences. Section 3 also includes the following proposition, which is critical for proving that
JT^{*}
is superprojective:

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
JT^{**}=JT\oplus\ell_{2}(2^{\mathbb{N}})
be the natural decomposition. Then, for every separable subspace
Y
of
\ell_{2}(2^{\mathbb{N}})
,
Y_{\perp}
is a complemented subspace of
JT^{*}
.

In the first part of Section 4, we prove that the spaces
JT
and
JT^{*}
are
\ell_{2}
-complementably saturated, which yields that the spaces
\mathcal{B}
,
JT
, and
JT^{*}
are subprojective. In the second part, we prove the superprojectivity of these spaces.

In the final section, we first prove that non-trivial weakly Cauchy sequences in the James space
J
possess a subsequence equivalent to the basis of
J
, and the generated subspace is complemented in
J
. The second part of the section is devoted to proving the analogous result for sequences in
JT
.

2.
Preliminaries

We use standard Banach space notation. For a Banach space
X
,
B_{X}
and
S_{X}
denote the closed unit ball and the unit sphere of
X
. The closed subspace generated by a sequence
(x_{n})\subset X
is denoted by
[x_{n}]
. A sequence
(x_{n})
in
X
is seminormalized if
0<\inf_{n}\|x_{n}\|
and
\sup_{n}\|x_{n}\|<\infty
.

Given non-empty subsets
A\subset X
and
G\subset X^{*}
, we denote

A^{\perp}=\{f\in X^{*}:f(a)=0\;\textrm{ for all }a\in A\}
and
G_{\perp}=\{x\in X:g(x)=0\;\textrm{ for all }g\in G\}
.

If
X
and
Y
are Banach spaces, we write
X\simeq Y
if they are isomorphic, and
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
denotes the set of all (bounded) operators from
X
into
Y
.

We say that a sequence
(x_{n})
in
X

admits an upper
\ell_{2}
-estimate
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\|x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}\|\leq C(\|x_{1}\|^{2}+\cdots+\|x_{k}\|^{2})^{1/2}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
The sequence
(x_{n})

admits a lower
\ell_{2}
-estimate
if there exists a constant
D>0
such that
\|x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}\|\geq D(\|x_{1}\|^{2}+\cdots+\|x_{k}\|^{2})^{1/2}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Given a sequence
(X_{n})
of Banach spaces, we denote

\ell_{2}(X_{n})=\{(x_{n}):x_{n}\in X_{n}\textrm{ and }(\|x_{n}\|)\in\ell_{2}\}.

Endowed with the norm
\|(x_{n})\|_{2}=(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|x_{n}\|^{2})^{1/2}
,
\ell_{2}(X_{n})
is a Banach space.

2.1.
The dyadic tree

Recall that the dyadic tree
T
is a countable tree with a unique root, and every
\alpha\in T
has two immediate successors. Traditionally,
T
is represented as
T=\{(n,i):0\leq n<\infty,0\leq i<2^{n}\}
with the usual partial order in which each
(n,i)\in T
has two successors
(n+1,2i)
and
(n+1,2i+1)
.
The
height of

\alpha=(n,i)\in T
is
|\alpha|=n
.

Working in Banach spaces related to the dyadic tree, it is convenient to identify
T
with
(\mathbb{N},\preceq)
where
\preceq
is compatible with the natural order of
\mathbb{N}
in the sense that
n\preceq m
implies
n<m
. For example, the function
V:T\to\mathbb{N}
defined by the rule
V((n,i))=2^{n}+i
identifies
T
with
(\mathbb{N},\preceq)
.

A segment of
T
is any set
s
such that for every
n,m\in s
we have
n\preceq m
, and for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\preceq k\preceq m
we have
k\in s
. We shall denote the segments by the letters
s,t
.
An initial segment is a segment having the root as its first element.
If
s
is a segment and
I
is an interval of
\mathbb{N}
, the restriction of
s
to
I
is the segment
s_{|I}=s\cap I
. This follows from the compatibility of
\preceq
and
\leq
.

A branch is a maximal segment. We shall denote branches by the letters
\sigma,\tau
.
If
\sigma
is a branch and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\sigma_{>n}
the set
\sigma_{>n}=\{k\in\sigma:k>n\}
. For a branch
\sigma
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we shall call the set
\sigma_{>n}
a final segment.

Also, by
\sigma_{<n}
we shall denote the initial segment
\sigma_{<n}=\{k\in\sigma:k<n\}
. In a similar manner, we define the initial segment
\sigma_{\leq n}
and the final segment
\sigma_{\geq n}
.

Note that every infinite segment is contained in a unique branch. Actually, it is a final segment of a branch
\sigma
.
Two final segments
\sigma_{1,>n_{1}}
and
\sigma_{2,>n_{2}}
are said to be incomparable if their first elements are incomparable.
We denote by
\Gamma
the set of all branches in
T
. Note that the cardinality of
\Gamma
is
\mathfrak{c}
.

Let us note that:

a)

if
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{l}
are distinct branches, then there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that the final segments
\sigma_{1,>k},\dots,\sigma_{l,>k}
are incomparable.

b)

if
(\sigma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of initial segments, there exist a subsequence
(\sigma_{n})_{n\in L}
and an initial segment or branch
\sigma
such that
\sigma_{n}\xrightarrow[n\in L]{}\sigma
in the pointwise topology.

3.
James Tree and its basic properties

In this section, we define the norm of the space
JT
and we present some of its basic properties. Namely, we prove that
\ell_{1}
is not contained in
JT
and that the quotient
JT^{*}/\mathcal{B}
is isometric to
\ell_{2}(\Gamma^{*})
and their consequences. Our approach is based on the Amemiya-Ito theorem
[
2
]
. In particular, we explain how, using the Amemiya-Ito Theorem (Theorem
3.3
), we derive an alternative approach to the proof of the basic properties of
JT
and
JT^{*}
(Theorems
3.7
,
3.12
). For additional information on these spaces, we refer to
[
10
, Chapter 3]
or
[
9
, Chapter VIII]
.

3.1.
The norm of
JT

Let
\mathcal{T}
denote the dyadic tree. We start with the definition of the norm of
JT
.

For
x=(x_{i})_{i}\in c_{0}
, we denote

(3.1)

\|x\|_{JT}=\sup\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(\sum_{i\in s_{j}}x_{i}\big)^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

where the
sup
is taken over all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}
pairwise disjoint segments in
T
.

The space
JT
is defined as

JT=\{x=(x_{i})_{i}\in c_{0}:\|x\|_{JT}<\infty\},

and endowed with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{JT}
is a Banach space.

Let
F
be an initial segment of
\mathbb{N}
. A family
P=\{s_{i}\}_{i\in F}
of pairwise disjoint segments of
\mathcal{T}
is called a partition of
\mathcal{T}
and for
x\in JT
and
P
a partition we set

(3.2)

\|x\|_{P}=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(\sum_{i\in s_{j}}x_{i}\big)^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq\|x\|_{JT}

An
x
-norming partition is a partition
P
which satisfies
\|x\|_{JT}=\|x\|_{P}
. It is not difficult to see that every
x\in JT
admits an
x
-norming partition (see
[
6
]
).

We set
(e_{n})_{n}
the natural basis of
c_{0}
which is a normalized bimonotone boundedly complete basis of
JT
.
We denote by
\{e^{*}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset JT^{*}
the sequence of biorthogonal functionals.
Every finite segment
s
of
T
defines a norm-one functional
s^{*}\in JT^{*}
by the rule
s^{*}(x)=\sum_{n\in s}x(n)
. This is extended to the infinite segments and hence to the branches. We set
\Gamma^{*}=\{\sigma^{*}:\sigma\in\Gamma\}
. It is easy to see that for
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Gamma
not equal,
\|\sigma_{1}^{*}-\sigma_{2}^{*}\|>1
; hence we have:

Proposition 3.1
.

The space
JT^{*}
is non-separable.

The following result is well-known.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
A\subset\mathcal{T}
such that for each segment
s
in
\mathcal{T}
the intersection
s\cap A
is again a segment. Then
P_{A}(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}e_{k})=\sum_{k=1;k\in A}^{\infty}a_{k}e_{k}
defines a norm-one projection
P_{A}:JT\to JT
.

Proof.

Observe that
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(\sum_{i\in s_{j}}(P_{A}x)\big)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(\sum_{t_{i}\in s_{j}\cap A}x_{i}\big)^{2}
.
∎

We will need the following fundamental result of Amemiya and Ito
(
[
2
, Theorem]
)
.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
(x_{n})_{n}
is a seminormalized block sequence in
JT
such that
\sigma^{*}(x_{n})\to 0
for every
\sigma\in\Gamma
, then there exists a subsequence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
of
(x_{n})_{n}
equivalent to the unit vector basis of
\ell_{2}
. In particular,
(x_{n})_{n}
is a weakly null sequence.

This result is a key ingredient for understanding the structure of
JT
. Its proof is based on the Ramsey Theorem for two-point sets and an iteration.

Next, we show that it also provides short and clear proofs of the basic properties of
JT
and
JT^{*}
. See
[
7
]
for variants of Theorem
3.3
.

The following definition and proposition are implicitly included in
[
18
]
.

Definition 3.4
.

A bounded sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
in
JT
admits a non-zero
\ell_{2}
-vector if there exists sequences
(\alpha_{i})_{i\in F}
,
(\sigma_{i})_{i\in F}
with either
F=\mathbb{N}
or
F=\{1,\dots k\}
such that
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
,
\sigma_{i}^{*}(x_{n})\to\alpha_{i}
and for every
\sigma\neq\sigma_{i}
for
i\in F
we have
\sigma^{*}(x_{n})\to 0
.

We denote by
\#G
the cardinal of the set
G
. The following fact is easily proved.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume that
(x_{n})_{n}
is a normalized block sequence in
JT
. For
\epsilon>0
we set
G_{\epsilon}=\{\sigma:\lim_{n}|\sigma^{*}(x_{n})|\geq\epsilon\}
.
Then
\#G_{\epsilon}\leq 1/\epsilon^{2}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n}
be a seminormalized block sequence in
JT
. Then one of the following two alternatives hold:

a)

There exists a weakly null subsequence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
of
(x_{n})_{n}
.

b)

(i) There exists a subsequence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
admitting a non-zero
\ell_{2}
-vector.

(ii) If
(\alpha_{i})_{i\in F}
,
(\sigma_{i})_{i\in F}
determine the
\ell_{2}
-vector, then
\liminf_{k}\|x_{n_{k}}\|\geq\|(\alpha_{i})\|_{2}
.

Proof.

Assume that (a) does not hold. Then using Lemma
3.5
and iteration we choose
(L_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
a decreasing sequence of infinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
and
(G_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
an increasing sequence of finite subsets of
\Gamma
such that the following hold:

(1) For every
\sigma\in G_{j}
, we have
\lim_{n\in L_{j}}\sigma^{*}(x_{n})=\alpha_{\sigma}
and
|\alpha_{\sigma}|\geq\frac{1}{j}
.

(2) For every
\sigma\notin G_{j}
,
\liminf_{n\in L_{j}}|\sigma(x_{n})|<\frac{1}{j}
.

Choose an infinite
L
almost contained in each
L_{j}
and set
G=\cup_{j}G_{j}
. The set
G
is countable and it is easy to see that for
\sigma\in G
,
\lim_{n\in L}\sigma^{*}(x_{n})=\alpha_{\sigma}\neq 0
while for
\sigma\notin G
,
\lim_{n\in L}\sigma^{*}(x_{n})=0
.

Lemma
3.5
easily yields the last part of the statement, ending the proof.
∎

Proposition
3.6
and Theorem
3.3
yield the following basic property of
JT
proved by James in
[
15
]
.

Theorem 3.7
.

The space
JT
does not contain an isomorphic copy of
\ell_{1}
.

Proof.

Since the basis of
JT
is boundedly complete, if
\ell_{1}
is embedded into
JT
then there exists a seminormalized block sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
equivalent to the
\ell_{1}
basis. Then Proposition
3.6
implies that there exists a subsequence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
of
(x_{n})_{n}
admitting a non-zero
\ell_{2}
-vector. Setting
y_{k}=x_{n_{2k+1}}-x_{n_{2k}}
, we have
\sigma^{*}(y_{k})\to 0
for every
\sigma\in\Gamma
. Theorem
3.3
yields that
(y_{k})_{k}
is a weakly null sequence, a contradiction.
∎

3.2.
The spaces
\mathcal{B}
,
JT^{*}

We denote
\mathcal{B}=[e^{*}_{k}]
, the closed subspace of
JT^{*}
generated by the sequence
(e^{*}_{k})
, and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{B}}
the norm on
\mathcal{B}
. Note that
\{e^{*}_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a shrinking basis of
\mathcal{B}
. Therefore
\mathcal{B}^{*}=JT
and, by
[
19
, Proposition 1.b.2]
, we have

(3.3)

JT^{*}=\Big\{x=(x_{t})_{t\in T}\subset\mathbb{R}:\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{t_{i}}e^{*}_{t_{i}}\Big\|_{\mathcal{B}}<\infty\Big\}.

Proposition 3.8
.

The family
D=\{e_{n}^{*}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{\sigma^{*}:\sigma\in\Gamma\}
norm-generates
JT^{*}
.

Proof.

Assume that the conclusion fails. Then there exists an
x^{**}\in JT^{**}
such that
x^{**}\neq 0
and
D\subset\ker(x^{**})
.
Since
\ell_{1}
is not isomorphic to a subspace of
JT
, there exists a bounded sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
in
JT
such that
x_{n}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}x^{**}
(
[
20
]
). Since
e_{k}^{*}(x_{n})\to 0
we may assume that
\{x_{n}\}_{n}
is a block sequence
[
1
, Proposition 1.3.10]
. Then for every
\sigma\in\Gamma
, we have that
\sigma^{*}(x_{n})\to 0
and Theorem
3.3
yields that
x_{n}\xrightarrow{w}0
, a contradiction.
∎

The following is also well-known.

Lemma 3.9
.

The set

(3.4)

W=\Bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}s_{i}^{*}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq 1\text{ and }\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\text{ pairwise disjoint segments}\Bigr\}

is a subset of
B_{JT^{*}}
which is norming for
JT
.

Proof.

Let
x^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}s_{i}^{*}\in W
and
x\in J
with
\|x\|=1
. Then Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields

|x^{*}(x)|=\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}s_{i}^{*}(x)\Big|\leq\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{*}(x)^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq 1,

which shows that
W\subset B_{JT^{*}}
.

Also for
x\in c_{00}
with
\|x\|=1
, choose
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
pairwise disjoint segments such that

\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{*}(x)^{2}\Big)^{1/2}=1.

Then
x^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{*}(x)s_{i}^{*}\in W
and
x^{*}(x)=1
. This completes the proof.
∎

The next lemma is implicitly contained in
[

]
and will be used for the description of the norm of

.

Lemma 3.10
.

a)

Let
\{s_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a disjoint family of segments of
\mathcal{T}
. Assume also that there exists a family
\{n_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
of incomparable nodes of
\mathcal{T}
such that
n_{i}\in s_{i}
,
i=1\dots k
. Then for
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
we have

\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}s_{i}^{*}\Big\|=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

b)

In particular, every sequence
\{s_{i}^{*}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of disjoint infinite segments in
JT^{*}
is isometric to the usual basis of
\ell_{2}
.

Proof.

a) Let
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be such that
(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{2})^{1/2}=1
. Then
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}s_{i}^{*}\in W
, hence
\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}s_{i}^{*}\|\leq 1
. On the opposite, let
\{n_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be incomparable nodes with
n_{i}\in s_{i}
,
i=1\dots k
. The definition of the norm of
JT
yields
\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}e_{n_{i}}\|=1
. Hence

\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}s_{i}^{*}\Big\|\geq\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}s_{i}^{*}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}e_{n_{j}}\Big)\Big|=1.

This ends the proof of the first part.

b) For each

, there exists a unique branch
\sigma_{i}
such that
s_{i}
is a final segment of
\sigma_{i}
. Thus, for every finite subset

\mathbb{N}
, there exists

\mathcal{T}
with

is isometric to the usual basis of
\ell_{2}^{\#F}

∎

4.

In this section, we prove that every infinite-dimensional closed subspace of

contains a complemented subspace isomorphic to

Given

Definition 4.1
.

Let

be a Banach space.

(1)

The space

is subprojective if every infinite-dimensional closed subspace
Y
contains an infinite-dimensional closed subspace complemented in
X
.

(2)

The space
X
is superprojective if every closed subspace
Y
of infinite codimension is contained into a subspace
Z
of infinite codimension and complemented in
X
.

Subprojective and superprojective spaces were introduced in
[

]
. We refer to
[

]
and

]
for examples of these spaces.

4.1.
The spaces
\mathcal{B}
,

and

are subprojective

Definition 4.2
.

We say that a sequence
(x_{k})
of non-zero vectors in
\mathcal{B}
or

is a

if
u(x_{k})<l(x_{k+1})
for each

The following result is a direct consequence of the definition of the norm

Lemma 4.3
.

Every level-block sequence
(x_{k})
in
JT
admits a lower
\ell_{2}
-estimate. In fact,
\|x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}\|^{2}_{JT}\geq\|x_{1}\|^{2}_{JT}+\cdots+\|x_{k}\|^{2}_{JT}

Proof.

For every

Corollary 4.4
.

Every normalized level-block sequence
(v_{k})
in
\mathcal{B}
admits an upper
\ell_{2}
-estimate.

Proof.

Let
(a_{k})\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}v_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
and let
x\in JT
be a norm-one vector norming
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}v_{i}
.
By Proposition
3.2
, we can assume that there exists a level-block sequence
(x_{k})
in
JT
such that
l(v_{k})\leq l(x_{k})
and
u(x_{k})\leq u(v_{k})
for each

and

\langle v_{k},x_{k}\rangle=\langle v_{k},x\rangle
for each

. Then

\displaystyle\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}v_{i}\Big\|_{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}\langle v_{i},x\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}\langle v_{i},x_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|a_{i}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|\langle v_{i},x_{i}\rangle|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|a_{i}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|x_{i}\|_{JT}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|a_{i}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\|x\|_{JT}=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|a_{i}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

where the last inequality is a consequence of Lemma

∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Every normalized level-block sequence
(v_{k})
in
\mathcal{B}
has a subsequence equivalent to the unit vector basis of

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2

Note that

M^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{B}
finite-dimensional and

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
(z_{k})
be a seminormalized sequence in
JT^{*}
.
Suppose that every segment

in
\mathcal{T}
intersects at most once
\supp(z_{k})
and

is always a segment. Then
(z_{k})
is equivalent to the unit vector basis of
\ell_{2}

Proof.

Let
(x_{k})
in
JT
with
\supp(x_{k})\subset\supp(z_{k})
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
. From the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{JT}
it follows that
\|x_{1}+\cdots+x_{m}\|_{JT}^{2}=\|x_{1}\|_{JT}^{2}+\cdots+\|x_{m}\|_{JT}^{2}
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\varepsilon>0
.
For each
k=1,\ldots,n

x_{k}\in JT
such that
\|x_{k}\|_{JT}=1

\supp(x_{k})\subset\supp(z_{k})

\|z_{k}\|^{2}\leq\langle x_{k},z_{k}\rangle^{2}+\varepsilon/n

If
x=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle x_{k},z_{k}\rangle x_{k}

\|x\|_{JT}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle x_{k},z_{k}\rangle^{2}
. Therefore

\|x\|_{JT}^{2}\,\Big\|\sum_{l=1}^{n}z_{l}\Big\|^{2}\geq\Big|\Big\langle x,\sum_{l=1}^{n}z_{l}\Big\rangle\Big|^{2}=\Big|\Big\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle x_{k},z_{k}\rangle x_{k},\sum_{l=1}^{n}z_{l}\Big\rangle\Big|^{2}.

Since
\langle x_{k},z_{l}\rangle=0
for

\|x\|_{JT}^{2}\,\Big\|\sum_{l=1}^{n}z_{l}\Big\|^{2}\geq\Big|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle x_{k},z_{k}\rangle^{2}\Big|^{2}\geq\|x\|_{JT}^{2}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|z_{k}\|^{2}-\varepsilon\Big).

Hence
\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}\|^{2}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|z_{k}\|^{2}

For the converse inequality, let

and let us denote

is the projection considered in Proposition

\displaystyle\Big\langle x,\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle x,z_{k}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle x_{k},z_{k}\rangle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|x_{k}\|_{JT}\,\|z_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|x_{k}\|_{JT}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|z_{k}\|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq\|x\|_{JT}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|z_{k}\|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Hence

4.3.
Uncomplemented copies of

For

Proposition 4.18
.

[
10

Let
(n_{i})
be a sequence in
\mathbb{N}
. Then

.

If
X
and
Y
are isomorphic Banach spaces, the
Banach-Mazur distance between
X
and
Y
is defined by

d_{BM}(X,Y)=\inf\{\|T^{-1}\|:T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)\textrm{ bijective and }\|T\|=1\}.

For a finite-dimensional space
Y
, a Banach space
X
, and a constant
C\geq 1
, the
C
-projection constant of
Y
in
X
is

A_{C}(Y,X)=\inf\{\|P\|:P\textrm{ projection on $X$ onto $N$ with }d_{BM}(Y,N)\leq C\}.

A Banach space

has the
Dunford-Pettis property
if every weakly compact operator
T:X\to Y
takes weakly convergent sequences to convergent sequences.

If
X
has the Dunford-Pettis property and

is a projection onto a reflexive subspace
M
of
X
, then
P
is weakly compact. Hence every bounded sequence
(m_{k})
in
M
has a weakly convergent subsequence
(m_{k_{i}})
. Since
Pm_{k_{i}}=m_{k_{i}}
,
(m_{k_{i}})
is convergent. Thus
M
is finite-dimensional.

5.

In this section, we will show that every non-reflexive subspace of

contains a copy of the James space
J
. For this, we need a refinement of the corresponding result for
J
.

5.1.
Non-Trivial Weakly Cauchy Sequences in
J

A sequence of real numbers
x=(x(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is in
J
when

(5.1)

\|x\|=\sup\biggl\{\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big|\sum_{k\in I_{i}}x(k)\Big|^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}:\;\{I_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\text{ disjoint intervals of }\mathbb{N}\biggr\}<\infty.

The basis
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
c_{00}
defines a boundedly complete basis of
J
. Further, every interval
I
of
\mathbb{N}
defines by the rule
I^{*}(x)=\sum_{n\in I}x(n)
a norm-one functional
I^{*}\in J^{*}
. We denote by
I_{\infty}^{*}
the functional corresponding to
I=\mathbb{N}
.
Notice that the basis of
J
is isometric to the sequence
(e_{n})_{n\in\sigma}
in
JT
for every
\sigma\in\Gamma
.

Definition 5.1
.

A sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
in a Banach space
X
is called non-trivial weakly Cauchy if
x_{n}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}x^{**}\in X^{**}\setminus X
.

We also need the following definition and result from
[
6
]
.

Definition 5.2

(
[
6
]
, Definition 3.1)
.

Let
x\in J
. A family
\{I_{i}\}_{i\in F}
with
F
either an initial interval of
\mathbb{N}
or
F=\mathbb{N}
is said to be an
x
-norming partition if

\|x\|=\Big(\sum_{i\in F}\Big(\sum_{k\in I_{i}}x(k)\Big)^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

Proposition 5.3

(
[
6
]
, Corollary 3.4)
.

Every
x\in J
admits an
x
-norming partition.

The next result is known. We give a proof for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a block sequence in
J
and
\epsilon>0
. Assume that
I_{\infty}^{*}(x_{n})=0
and there exists an
\alpha>0
such that
\big|\|x_{n}\|-\alpha\big|<\alpha\epsilon
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is equivalent to the usual basis of
\ell_{2}
. More precisely,

(5.2)

(1-\epsilon)\alpha\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq\Big\|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}x_{n}\Big\|\leq\sqrt{2}(1+\epsilon)\alpha\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Proof.

For the first inequality, we choose a partition
\{I_{i}\}_{i\in F}
such that for every
i\in F
there exists a (unique)
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
I_{i}\subset ran(x_{n})
and also for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
the family
\{I_{i}:I_{i}\subset\ran(x_{n})\}
is an
x_{n}
-norming partition. Then for
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}
we have

(5.3)

\Big\|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}x_{n}\Big\|\geq\Big(\sum_{i\in F}I_{i}^{*}\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}x_{n}\Big)^{2}\Big)^{1/2}>(1-\epsilon)\alpha\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

We pass to the second inequality. Since
I_{\infty}^{*}(x_{n})=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, given
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and an interval
I
,

(5.4)

\Big(I^{*}\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}x_{n}\Big)\Big)^{2}\leq 2\Big(\lambda_{n_{1}}^{2}I_{1}^{*}(x_{n_{1}})^{2}+\lambda_{n_{2}}^{2}I_{2}^{*}(x_{n_{2}})^{2}\Big),

where
n_{1}=\min\{n:\supp(x_{n})\cap I\neq\emptyset\}
,
n_{2}=\max\{n:\supp(x_{n})\cap I\neq\emptyset\}
,
I_{1}=\ran(x_{n_{1}})\cap I
and
I_{2}=\ran(x_{n_{2}})\cap I
.

Let
x=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}x_{n}
and let
\{I_{i}\}_{i\in F}
be an
x
-norming partition. By the previous observation, for every
I_{i}
intersecting the support of more than one
x_{n}
, there are
I_{i}^{1},I_{i}^{2}
such that
I_{i},I_{i}^{1},I_{i}^{2}
satisfy inequality (
5.4
) and each
I_{i}^{1},I_{i}^{2}
intersects the support of only one
x_{n}
.
Therefore there exists a partition
\{J_{j}\}_{j\in G}
such that either
J_{j}=I_{i}
for some
i\in F
or
J_{j}=I_{i}^{1}
or
J_{j}=I_{i}^{2}
for some
i\in F
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

G_{n}=\{j\in G:J_{j}\cap\supp(x_{n})\neq\emptyset\}

Clearly,
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are pairwise disjoint and from
5.4
we have:

(5.5)

\|x\|^{2}=\sum_{i\in F}\Big(\sum_{k\in I_{i}}x(k)\Big)^{2}\leq 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j\in G_{n}}\lambda_{n}^{2}(J_{j}^{*}(x_{n}))^{2}\leq 2(1+\epsilon)^{2}\alpha^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{2}.

Inequalities
5.3
and
5.5
complete the proof.
∎

Applying a sliding hump argument and Lemma
5.4
, we derive the following result.

Proposition 5.5
.

Every seminormalized sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
in
J
such that
I_{\infty}^{*}(x_{n})\to 0
and
x_{n}(k)\to 0
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
has a subsequence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
equivalent to the basis of
\ell_{2}
.

In particular, every weakly null
(x_{n})_{n}
in
J
with
\lim_{n}\|x_{n}\|=\alpha>0
has an
\ell_{2}
-subsequence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
, and if for some
0<\epsilon<1
, we have

(5.6)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Big|I_{\infty}^{*}(x_{n_{k}})\Big|<\alpha\frac{\epsilon}{4}\quad\text{and}\quad\Big|\|x_{n_{k}}\|-\alpha\Big|<\alpha\frac{\epsilon}{4}

then

(5.7)

(1-\epsilon)\alpha\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}x_{n_{k}}\Big\|\leq(1+\epsilon)\sqrt{2}\alpha\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Proof.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}

m_{k}\in\supp(x_{n_{k}})

I_{\infty}^{*}(y_{k})=0\quad\lim_{k}\|y_{k}\|=\alpha\quad\text{and}\quad\big|\|y_{k}\|-\alpha\big|<\alpha\frac{\epsilon}{2}

(5.8)

(1-\frac{\epsilon}{2})\alpha\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}y_{k}\Big\|\leq\sqrt{2}\Big(1+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\Big)\alpha\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

Since

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|x_{n_{k}}-y_{k}\|<\epsilon/4
,
the triangle inequality gives

(5.9)

(1-\epsilon)\alpha\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}x_{n_{k}}\Big\|\leq\sqrt{2}(1+\epsilon)\alpha\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

We pass to study the non-trivial weakly Cauchy sequences in
J
. First we show that each of these sequences has a subsequence equivalent to the basis of

.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n}
be a block sequence in
B_{J}
such that

\lim_{n}I_{\infty}^{*}(x_{n})=\alpha\neq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{n}\|x_{n}\|=\beta\geq 0,

(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
such that for every

in

(5.10)

(1-\epsilon)|\alpha|\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}e_{k}\Big\|\leq\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}x_{n_{k}}\Big\|\leq 3(1+\epsilon)(|\alpha|+\beta)\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}e_{k}\Big\|.

Hence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
is equivalent to the basis
(e_{k})_{k}
of

.

Proof.

We assume that
(x_{n})_{n}
is a skipped block sequence, and for every

with
\supp(x_{n})<m(n)<\supp(x_{n+1})

\lim_{n}\|v_{n}\|=\gamma\leq|\alpha|+\beta

We will show that a subsequence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
such that

(5.11)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Big|I_{\infty}^{*}(x_{n_{k}})-\alpha\Big|<|\alpha|\frac{\epsilon}{4}\quad\text{and}\quad\Big|\|v_{n_{k}}\|-\gamma\Big|\leq\gamma\frac{\epsilon}{4}\leq(|\alpha|+\beta)\frac{\epsilon}{4}

satisfies inequality (

Let
(\lambda_{k})_{k}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{R}
and let

be an interval of
\mathbb{N}

(5.12)

\Big|\sum_{k\in I}\lambda_{k}\Big|\leq 1.

We set

as the minimum interval such that

\Big|I_{\infty}^{*}\Big(\sum_{k\in L}\lambda_{k}x_{n_{k}}\Big)\Big|\geq\Big|I_{\infty}^{*}\Big(\sum_{k\in L}\lambda_{k}\alpha e_{m(n_{k})}\Big)\Big|-\Big|I_{\infty}^{*}\Big(\sum_{k\in L}\lambda_{k}(x_{n_{k}}-v_{n_{k}})\Big)\Big|,

and using (

\Big|I_{\infty}^{*}\Big(\sum_{k\in L}\lambda_{k}x_{n_{k}}\Big)\Big|\geq(1-\epsilon)|\alpha|\Big|\sum_{k\in L}\lambda_{k}\Big|.

This inequality and the definition of the norm of

(1-\epsilon)|\alpha|\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}e_{k}\Big\|\leq\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}x_{n_{k}}\Big\|.

We pass to the second part of inequality (

We first observe that the sequence
(v_{n_{k}})_{k}
satisfies the assumptions of Proposition

(5.13)

\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}v_{n_{k}}\Big\|\leq(1+\epsilon)\sqrt{2}\gamma\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq(1+\epsilon)\sqrt{2}(|\alpha|+\beta)\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Moreover,

(5.14)

\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\alpha e_{m(n_{k})}\Big\|=|\alpha|\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}e_{k}\Big\|.

Since
x_{n_{k}}=v_{n_{k}}+\alpha_{n_{k}}e_{m(n_{k})}
, inequalities (

completing the proof of the second part of inequality (

For the general result we need the following fact.

Proposition 5.7

Let

(z_{n})_{n}
be a sequence in

(z_{n_{k}})_{k}
of

equivalent to a block sequence of the basis

Moreover, if every subsequence
(y_{k})_{k\in L}
generates a complemented subspace of

(z_{n_{k}})_{k}
can be chosen to satisfy the same property.

Proof.

Denoting by
(e_{n}^{*})_{n}
the sequence in

X_{*}=[(e_{n}^{*})_{n}]
is the predual of

is boundedly complete.
This yields that

(y_{k})_{k}
, which ends the proof.

If
y\neq 0
then we set
x_{n}=z_{n}-y
and observe that
\lim_{n}e_{m}^{*}(x_{n})=0
for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence there is a subsequence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
which is a small permutation of a block sequence
(y_{k})_{k}
and clearly the two sequences are equivalent. Also
y_{k}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}y^{**}
. We will show that there exists a
k_{0}
such that
(z_{n_{k}})_{k\geq k_{0}}
is equivalent to
(y_{k})_{k\geq k_{0}}
, which ends the proof.

Indeed, choose
x^{*}\in X_{*}
a finite linear combination of
(e_{n}^{*})_{n}
with
x^{*}(y)=1
, and
y^{*}\in B_{X^{*}}
such that
y^{**}(y^{*})>\alpha>0
. With no loss of generality, we can assume that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have
y^{*}(y_{k})>\alpha
. Finally, denote

k_{0}=\min\big\{k:\max\,\supp(x^{*})<\min\supp(y_{k})\big\}.

Claim
.

The sequences
(y_{k})_{k\geq k_{0}}
and
(y_{k}+y)_{k\geq k_{0}}
are equivalent.

Since
X=[\{y\}]\oplus\ker x^{*}
, there are numbers
c,C>0
such that for
x=\lambda y+z

c\max\{|\lambda|\|y\|,\|z\|\}\leq\|x\|=\|\lambda y+z\|\leq C\max\{|\lambda|\|y\|,\|z\|\}.

Then
v=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{m}\lambda_{k}y_{k}
and
w=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{m}\lambda_{k}(y_{k}+y)
satisfy
c\|v\|\leq\|w\|
.

In the opposite direction,

\|w\|\leq C\max\Big\{\Big|\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{m}\lambda_{k}\Big|\|y\|,\|v\|\Big\}.

Since
\|v\|\geq|y^{*}(v)|\geq|\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{m}\lambda_{k}|\alpha
, we conclude that there exists a
C_{1}>0
such that
\|w\|\leq C_{1}\|v\|
.
This completes the proof of the claim.

Since
(y_{k})_{k\geq k_{0}}
is a small permutation of
(x_{n_{k}})_{k\geq k_{0}}
,
(y_{k}+y)_{k\geq k_{0}}
is a small permutation of
(z_{n_{k}})_{k\geq k_{0}}
, and the claim implies the equivalence of
(y_{k})_{k\geq k_{0}}
and
(z_{n_{k}})_{k\geq k_{0}}
.

Next we prove the second part of the statement of the proposition. As before,
z^{**}=y+y^{**}
. If we assume that
y=0
, as in the first part of the proof, there is a subsequence
(z_{n_{k}})_{k}
which is a small permutation of a block sequence
(y_{k})_{k}
and if every subsequence of
(y_{k})_{k}
generates a complemented subspace of
X
then Proposition 1.a.9 in
[
19
]
completes the proof.

Assume next that
y\neq 0
. Let
x^{*}
and
k_{0}
as before with
x^{*}(z)=1
,
x^{*}\in[(e_{l}^{*})_{l\leq{m}}]
and
m<\min\supp y_{k}
for
k>k_{0}
.

Claim
.

For every subsequence of
(y_{k})_{k>k_{0}}
the space generated by
(y+y_{k})_{k\in L}

is complemented in
X
.

Indeed, set
W=\ker x^{*}
. Clearly
(y_{k})_{k\in L}\subset W
and let
P_{W,L}
be the restriction of the projection
P_{L}:X\to[(y_{k})_{k\in L}]
to
W
. Then it is easy to see that the operator

Q:X=<y>\oplus Kerx^{*}\to[(y+y_{k})_{k\in L}]

defined by
Q(x_{1}+x_{2})=x^{*}(x_{1})y+P_{W,L}(x_{2})
is a bounded projection.

Since
\|z_{n_{k}}-(y+y_{k})\|\to 0
, Proposition 1.a.9 in
[
19
]
yields the result and the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n}
be a block sequence in
J
equivalent to the basis of
J
.
Then the space generated by
(x_{n})_{n}
is complemented in
J
.

Proof.

Since
(x_{n})_{n}
is equivalent to the basis of
J
we may assume that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

I_{n}^{*}(x_{n})=1
, where
I_{n}^{*}=\ran x_{n}
is the smallest interval containing
\supp x_{n}
. We consider a complete partition
(J_{n})_{n}
of
\mathbb{N}

(i.e.
\mathbb{N}=\cup_{n}J_{n}
) into segments such that
I_{n}\subset J_{n}
.

Claim
.

The projection
P(x)=\sum_{n}J_{n}^{*}(x)x_{n}
is bounded.

Let
x\in J
then there exists
C>0
such that

(5.15)

\|P(x)\|=\|\sum_{n}J_{n}^{*}(x)x_{n}\|\leq C\|\sum_{n}J_{n}^{*}(x)e_{n}\|.

We set
y=\sum_{n}J_{n}^{*}(x)e_{n}
and we choose
Q=\{L_{i}\}_{i\in F}
an
y
-norming partition. Let
Q_{1}=\{M_{i}\}_{i\in F}
with
M_{i}=\cup_{n\in{L_{i}}}J_{n}
. Then

\|P(x)\|\leq C\|y\|=C\|y\|_{Q}\quad\text{and }\quad\|y\|_{Q}=\|x\|_{Q_{1}}\leq\|x\|

We recall that
\|y\|_{Q}
denotes the
Q
norm of
y
as it is defined in (
3.2
) and the same for

\|x\|_{Q_{1}}
.

Hence
\|P(x)\|\leq C\|x\|
.
This ends the proof of the claim and completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

5.2.
Non-Trivial Weakly Cauchy Sequences in
JT

Here we study the embedding of
J
into non-reflexive subspaces of
JT
.

We recall (Definition
3.4
, Proposition
3.6
) that every seminormalized block sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
which is not weakly null has a subsequence that admits a non-zero
\ell_{2}
vector determined by a sequence of reals
(a_{i})_{i\in F}
and a sequence of branches
(\sigma_{i})_{i\in F}
. In the sequel, we will assume that
F=\mathbb{N}
and the sequence
(|a_{i}|)_{i}
is non-increasing.

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n}
be a bounded block sequence in
JT
admitting a non-zero
\ell_{2}
vector determined by the sequences
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.

a)
x_{n}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}x^{**}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}\sigma_{i}^{**}
and
\|x^{**}\|=\big(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}\big)^{1/2}
.

b) There exists a further block sequence
(z_{n})_{n}
consisting of successive convex combinations of
(x_{n})_{n}
such that
z_{n}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}x^{**}
and
\|z_{n}\|\to\|x^{**}\|
.

Proof.

a) If
x^{*}
denotes either some
e_{n}^{*}
or some
\sigma^{*}
, it is easy to see that
x^{*}(x_{n})\to x^{**}(x^{*})
. Then the first part of (a) follows from Proposition
3.8
.
The second part of (a) is explained in subsection 3.3.

b) This is well-known and its proof goes as follows. Assume that
\|x^{**}\|=\alpha
. Then there exists a sequence
(y_{n})_{n}
in
\alpha B_{JT}
with
y_{n}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}x^{**}
. Observe that
x_{n}-y_{n}
is weakly null; hence there exists a sequence
(z_{n})_{n}
of successive convex combinations of
(x_{n})_{n}
and a corresponding sequence
(v_{n})_{n}
of
(y_{n})_{n}
such that
\|z_{n}-v_{n}\|\to 0
. Since
\|v_{n}\|\to\alpha
, the result follows.
∎

Notation 5.13
.

For
A=\{\sigma_{i,>m}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, we say that
A
is incomparable if the corresponding final segments
\{\sigma_{i,>m}\}_{i=1}^{k}
are incomparable. For
A
incomparable, we denote by
P_{A}
the projection defined as the restriction of any
x\in JT
to
\cup_{\sigma_{>m}\in A}\sigma_{>m}
. As it is shown in Proposition
3.2
,
\|P_{A}\|=1
.

The following is an easy consequence of the definition of the norm of
JT
and the property that on the branches of
\mathcal{T}
the
JT
-norm coincides with the
J
-norm.

Lemma 5.14
.

Suppose that
A=\{\sigma_{i,>m}:i\in F\}
is incomparable. Then for each
x\in JT
,

\|P_{A}(x)\|=\Big(\sum_{\sigma\in A}\|P_{\sigma_{>m}}(x)\|_{J}^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{J}
denotes the norm of the James space
J
.

Remark 5.15
.

Let
x^{**}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}\sigma_{i}^{*}
with
\|x^{**}\|=1
, and let
(x_{n})_{n}
be a block sequence such that
x_{n}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}x^{**}
. In the next proposition, we will assume that
(x_{n})_{n}
satisfies the following additional properties:

i)

\lim_{n}\|x_{n}\|=b\geq 1
.

ii)

For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lim_{n}\|P_{\sigma_{i}}(x_{n})\|=b_{i}\geq|a_{i}|
.

Clearly, every
(x_{n})_{n}
with
x_{n}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}x^{**}
has a subsequence satisfying (i) and (ii). Moreover, it is easy to check that
(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}b_{i}^{2})^{1/2}\leq b
.

Proposition 5.16
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n}
be a block sequence in
JT
such that

x_{n}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}x^{**}
with
x^{**}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}\sigma_{i}^{*}
and
\|x^{**}\|=1
.
Then
(x_{n})_{n}
has a subsequence equivalent to the basis of
J
generating a complemented subspace of
JT
.

Proof.

As we have noticed, we can assume that
(x_{n})_{n}
satisfies (i) and (ii) in Remark
5.15
. For
0<\epsilon<1
, we choose a sequence
(\delta_{k})_{k}
of positive reals such that

(5.16)

\delta_{1}=b\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\delta_{k}<(1+\epsilon)b.

Next we partition
\mathbb{N}
into successive disjoint intervals
(F_{k})_{k}
satisfying for
k\geq 2
:

(5.17)

3(1+\epsilon)\Big(\sum_{i\in F_{k}}(|a_{i}|+b_{i})^{2}\Big)^{1/2}<\delta_{k},

and we choose an increasing sequence of naturals
(m_{k})_{k}
such that the set

A_{k}=\Big\{\sigma_{i,>m_{k}}:i\in F_{k}\Big\}

consists of incomparable final segments. Proposition
3.2
yields that
\|P_{A_{k}}\|=1
.
By induction, we define a decreasing sequence
(L_{k})_{k}
of infinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
such that the following facts are satisfied:

(5.18)

\begin{split}(i)\quad&\forall n\in L_{k},\text{ we have }m_{k}<\supp(x_{n})\text{ and }\forall i\in F_{k},\quad\Big|\sigma_{i}^{*}(x_{n})-a_{i}\Big|<\epsilon|a_{i}|\text{ and }\\
&\quad\Big|\|P_{\sigma_{i}}(x_{n})\|-b_{i}\Big|<\epsilon b_{i}.\\
(ii)\quad&\forall i\in F_{k},\\
&\quad(1-\epsilon)|a_{i}|\Big\|\sum_{n\in L_{k}}\lambda_{n}e_{n}\Big\|\leq\Big\|\sum_{n\in L_{k}}\lambda_{n}P_{\sigma_{i}}(x_{n})\Big\|\leq 3(1+\epsilon)(|a_{i}|+b_{i})\Big\|\sum_{n\in L_{k}}\lambda_{n}e_{n}\Big\|.\\
(iii)\quad&\text{For }k\geq 2,\quad\Big\|\sum_{n\in L_{k}}\lambda_{n}P_{A_{k}}(x_{n})\Big\|\leq 3(1+\epsilon)\Big(\sum_{i\in F_{k}}(|a_{i}|+b_{i})^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big\|\sum_{n\in L_{k}}\lambda_{n}e_{n}\Big\|\leq\\
&\quad\leq\delta_{k}\Big\|\sum_{n\in L_{k}}\lambda_{n}e_{n}\Big\|.\end{split}

The inductive definition of
L_{k}
satisfying (i) follows from the fact that
(x_{n})_{n}
is a block sequence,
\|P_{\sigma_{i}}(x_{n})\|\to b_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}^{*}(x_{n})\to a_{i}
.

Property (ii) follows from Proposition
5.6
and Property (iii) follows from (ii), Lemma
5.14
and the properties of
(\delta_{k})_{k}
(
5.17
).

This completes the inductive definition of the sequence
(L_{k})_{k}
.

Choose an increasing sequence
(n_{k})_{k}
with
n_{k}\in L_{k}
and set

v_{k}=P_{\cup_{p\leq k}A_{p}}(x_{n_{k}})\quad\text{and}\quad w_{k}=x_{n_{k}}-v_{k}.

We will show the following facts:

1) The block sequence
(v_{k})_{k}
is dominated by the basis of
J
.

2) The sequence
(w_{k})_{k}
is either norm null or has a subsequence equivalent to the
\ell_{2}
basis.

Notice that if (1) and (2) hold, then there exists a subsequence of
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
which is equivalent to the basis of
J
. Indeed, assume for simplicity that
(w_{k})
is equivalent to the
\ell_{2}
basis. Then there exist
c_{1},c_{2}
positive constants such that

(5.19)

\begin{split}\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}x_{n_{k}}\Big\|&\leq\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}v_{k}\Big\|+\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}w_{k}\Big\|\\
&\leq c_{1}\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}e_{k}\Big\|_{J}+c_{2}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq(c_{1}+c_{2})\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}e_{k}\Big\|_{J}.\end{split}

The last inequality follows from the fact that the
\ell_{2}
norm is dominated by the
J
norm in the basis
(e_{k})_{k}
of
J
. If a subsequence of
(w_{k})_{k}
is equivalent to the
\ell_{2}
basis, then (
5.19
) remains valid for this subsequence. Also, if
(w_{k})_{k}
is norm null, then (1) and a permutation argument yield a subsequence of
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
dominated by the basis of
J
.

For the opposite inequality, namely the basis of
J
is dominated by
(x_{n_{k}})_{n}
, we notice that
G=\{n_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset L_{1}
. Hence (
5.18
) (ii) gives:

(1-\epsilon)|a_{1}|\Big\|\sum_{n\in G}\lambda_{n}e_{n}\Big\|_{J}\leq\Big\|\sum_{n\in G}\lambda_{n}P_{\sigma_{1}}(x_{n})\Big\|\leq\Big\|\sum_{n\in G}\lambda_{n}x_{n}\Big\|.

This ends the proof of the equivalence. It remains to prove (1) and (2).

To prove (1), let
(\lambda_{k})_{k\leq m}\subset\mathbb{R}
. By the definition of
(v_{k})_{k}
we have

(5.20)

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}v_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}P_{A_{1}}(v_{k})+\sum_{k=2}^{m}\lambda_{k}P_{A_{2}}(v_{k})+\dots+\lambda_{m}P_{A_{m}}(v_{m}).

Since for every
p\geq k
we have
P_{A_{k}}(v_{p})=P_{A_{k}}(x_{n_{p}})
and
(n_{p})_{p\geq k}\subset L_{k}
for
1<k\leq m
, from the bimonotonicity of the basis of
J
and (
5.18
) (iii) we derive:

(5.21)

\Big\|\sum_{p=k}^{m}\lambda_{p}P_{A_{k}}(v_{p})\Big\|\leq\delta_{k}\Big\|\sum_{p=k}^{m}\lambda_{p}e_{p}\Big\|_{J}\leq\delta_{k}\Big\|\sum_{p=1}^{m}\lambda_{p}e_{p}\Big\|_{J}.

For
k=1
we have

(5.22)

\Big\|\sum_{p=1}^{m}\lambda_{p}P_{A_{1}}(v_{p})\Big\|\leq 3\Big(\sum_{i\in F_{1}}(|a_{i}|+b_{i})^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big\|\sum_{p=1}^{m}\lambda_{p}e_{p}\Big\|_{J}\leq 3\sqrt{2}(1+\epsilon)b\Big\|\sum_{p=1}^{m}\lambda_{p}e_{p}\Big\|_{J}.

Using the triangle inequality, (
5.16
) and (
5.22
) we get

(5.23)

\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}v_{k}\Big\|<6(1+\epsilon)b\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}e_{k}\Big\|_{J}.

This completes the proof of (1).

To prove (2), we show that
v_{k}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}x^{**}
. Indeed, if
\sigma=\sigma_{i}
, then
\sigma_{i}^{*}(v_{k})\to a_{i}=x^{**}(\sigma_{i}^{*})
. Assume
\sigma\neq\sigma_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
We assert that
\lim_{k}\sigma^{*}(v_{k})=0
. Observe from (
5.18
) (i) we have that for every
\delta>0
there exists a finite initial segment
F
of
\mathbb{N}
and
k_{0}
such that for
k>k_{0}
and
\sigma\neq\sigma_{i}
for
i\in F
we have
\|P_{\sigma}(v_{k})\|<\delta
.

Therefore the assertion is true and
v_{k}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}x^{**}
.
Since
x_{n_{k}}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}x^{**}
and
w_{k}=x_{n_{k}}-v_{k}
, we conclude that
w_{k}\xrightarrow{w}0
. This and Theorem
3.3
proves (2) and completes the proof that the subsequence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
is equivalent to the basis of
J
.

For the complementation of
[(x_{n_{k}})_{k}]
we observe that
P_{\sigma_{1}}
(defined above) is an isomorphism on
[(x_{n_{k}})_{k}]
. So, Lemma
5.8
yields the property.
∎
