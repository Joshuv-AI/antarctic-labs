Abstract

 In this project, we developed a transformational Ti-13X sorbent by employing molecular layer
deposition (MLD) or atomic layer deposition (ALD) technology to passivate the strong adsorption
sites of conventional 13X zeolite. This treatment increased the CO2 working capacity and CO2/N2
separation selectivity, while also reducing the CO2 heat of adsorption and improving tolerance to
water vapor and flue gas contaminants. Meanwhile, we also developed a proprietary pressure
swing adsorption (PSA) cycle schedule for CO2 capture from flue gas. This process requires far
fewer number of adsorption beds (relative to conventional adsorption processes), thereby reducing
the CO2 capture cost. By combining the advantages of the Ti-13X sorbent and the proprietary PSA
cycle, a bench-scale skid was designed and constructed. The skid was extensively tested at
University of South Carolina (USC) including comparison between 13X and Ti-13X and effects
of water vapor on CO2 capture performance.
 The skid was moved to the National Carbon Capture Center (NCCC) for tests with actual coal
flue gas or actual natural gas boiler flue gas modified to replicate the CO2 concentrations found in
coal flue gas, natural gas combined cycle (NGCC) and industrial flue gases. A good long-term and
dynamic stability was demonstrated over a 1,000-h test, with a CO2 dry-basis purity of 96.3 vol%
and a CO2 capture efficiency of 92.6%. Systematic testing results indicate the Ti-13X/PSA
technology is promising for CO2 capture at coal-fired power or industrial plants where the CO2
concentrations are higher than 10 vol%. The Ti-13X/PSA bench-scale skid was tested for over
eight months under multiple test campaigns at NCCC with different types of flue gases. During
these tests, the Ti-13X adsorbent did not undergo regeneration, demonstrating good stability. This
good stability enables continuous, reliable PSA operation while reducing energy consumption,
maintenance and operational costs.
 Trimeric Corporation conducted techno-economic analysis (TEA) to evaluate the Ti-13X
sorbent utilized in PSA for post-combustion CO2 capture from flue gas. A sensitivity study on
desiccant wheel costs and PSA sorbent costs was also conducted.

Executive Summary

 This report summarizes the carbon capture research and development conducted by The State
University of New York at Buffalo (UB), University of South Carolina (USC), GTI Energy (GTI),
and Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute (RPI) for U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) project DE-
FE0031730 titled “Transformational Molecular Layer Deposition Tailor-made Size-Sieving
Sorbents for Post-Combustion CO2 capture.” The objective of this project was to develop a
transformational molecular layer deposition (MLD) tailor-made size-sieving sorbent integrated
with a pressure swing adsorption (PSA) cycle schedule that can be installed in new or retrofitted
into existing pulverized coal (PC) power plants for CO2 capture with a cost of electricity at least
30% lower than a supercritical PC power with CO2 capture, or approximately $30 per tonne of
CO2 captured, and with it being ready for demonstration by 2030.
 In this project, UB developed a transformational Ti-13X sorbent by employing MLD or
atomic layer deposition (ALD) technology to passivate the strong adsorption sites of conventional
13X zeolite. This treatment increased the CO2 working capacity by approximately 15% and almost
doubled CO2/N2 separation selectivity, while also reducing the CO2 heat of adsorption to 35 kJ/mol
and improving tolerance to water vapor and flue gas contaminants. Meanwhile, USC developed a
proprietary PSA cycle schedule for CO2 capture from flue gas. This process requires far fewer
number of adsorption beds (relative to conventional adsorption processes), thereby reducing the
CO2 capture cost. By combining the advantages of the Ti-13X sorbent and the proprietary PSA
cycle, a bench-scale skid was designed and constructed. The skid was extensively tested at USC
including comparison between 13X and Ti-13X and effects of water vapor on CO2 capture
performance. During these tests, greater than 90% CO2 capture efficiency with >95% CO2 dry-
basis purity was achieved for the Ti-13X adsorbent. The skid was then moved to NCCC for tests
with actual coal flue gas or actual natural gas boiler flue gas modified to replicate the CO 2
concentrations found in coal flue gas, natural gas combined cycle (NGCC) and industrial flue
gases. GTI led the field tests at the national carbon capture center (NCCC).
 A three-bed PSA system loaded with approximately 2 kg of Ti-13X sorbent was installed in
NCCC’s laboratory-scale test unit, where testing was first conducted using simulated coal-fired
flue gas (15.7 vol% CO2, wet basis). Under a feed flow rate of 13 standard liter per minute (SLPM)
and a vacuum pressure of 5 kPa, the system achieved a CO2 dry-basis purity of 96.3 vol% and a
CO2 capture efficiency of 92.6%. Good long-term and dynamic stabilities were demonstrated over
a 1,000-h test. Parametric tests indicated: 1) the CO2 dry-basis purity remained constant while CO2
capture efficiency decreased with increasing vacuum pressure; 2) the H2O vapor concentration had
minimal impact on CO2 capture performance; and 3) higher feed flow rates and CO2 concentrations
resulted in slightly higher CO2 dry-basis purity but lower capture efficiency. When testing
simulated NGCC flue gas (5 vol% CO2, wet basis), under a vacuum pressure of 15 kPa and a feed
flow rate of 18 SLPM, the system achieved a CO2 dry-basis purity of 85.6 vol% and a CO2 capture
efficiency of 31.1%. Tests were then conducted for simulated industrial flue gas (24 vol% CO2,
wet basis). Under a mild vacuum pressure of 20 kPa and a feed flow rate of 8 SLPM, the system
achieved a CO2 dry-basis purity of 96.4 vol% and a CO2 capture efficiency of 70.5%. The
performance also remained highly stable during the 200-h test. Testing results indicate the Ti-
13X/PSA technology is promising for CO2 capture at coal-fired power or industrial plants where
the CO2 concentrations are higher than 10 vol%.
 Trimeric Corporation conducted the techno-economic analysis (TEA) to evaluate the Ti-13X

 6

sorbent utilized in PSA for post-combustion capture from flue gas produced in a supercritical PC
power plant. The cost of CO2 capture, $48.69/tonne, was estimated using the capital and operating
and maintenance costs for PSA process technology. A sensitivity study on desiccant wheel costs
and PSA sorbent costs was also conducted, suggesting a CO2 capture cost range of $48.69-
51.99/tonne.
 Following these successful tests at NCCC, a future plan after the current project is to design
and build an engineering-scale Ti-13X/PSA CO2 capture system, conduct tests at coal-fired power
or industrial plants, and demonstrate a continuous, steady-state operation for a minimum of two
months.

1. Introduction

1.1. Zeolite-based Sorbents for CO2 Capture and PSA
 As a promising adsorbent material for post combustion CO2 capture, zeolites, such as 13X,
have been widely investigated in PSA [1]. 13X is particularly attractive due to its high CO2
adsorption capacity, low cost, and commercial availability as a low attrition formed material [2].
Gas adsorption in 13X zeolite is inherently heterogeneous, involving both physisorption and
chemisorption sites that are thermodynamically distinct [3-4]. Physical adsorbed CO2 can be readily
regenerated under mild pressure swing conditions, whereas chemisorbed CO2 requires much more
energy for regeneration. Moreover, the chemisorption sites occupy pore volume within the zeolite
cages and induce strong polarization of extra-framework cations, which in turn suppresses the
physisorption of CO2 [5]. Therefore, suppressing CO2 chemisorption in 13X zeolite while
preserving or enhancing physisorption is critical to lowering the CO2 capture cost in the PSA-
based CO2 capture process.
 ALD/MLD techniques have been applied to tailor the gas separation properties for zeolites [6-
8]
  . The MLD modified sorbents represent a completely new class of composite porous materials
and are distinctly different from traditional sorbents. Current microporous crystalline materials,
including zeolites, MOFs and ZIFs, have uniform pores in the molecular size range. However, it
is very challenging to precisely design pores of these materials to distinguish molecules with ~
0.01 nm size difference. In contrast, the MLD modified sorbents can be prepared by design and
can provide precise pore mouth size control in the range of 0.01 nm. Considering the very small
size difference between CO2 (0.33 nm) and N2 (0.364 nm), precise design is crucial to achieve
size-sieving/highly selective separation of CO2 from N2.

MLD cycles will modify the internal cavity/surface of the 13X and particularly may “passivate”
strong adsorption sites for H2O and CO2, such as Na+ and polar sites in 13X. To permanently
weaken the chemisorption strength of 13X zeolite, we also employed ALD [10] to passivate the
chemisorption sites, thereby producing a novel adsorbent, Ti-13X. In the ALD/MLD process,
vapor-phase precursors undergo self-limiting surface reactions, resulting in the deposition of
conformal, nanometer-scale coatings. The optimized sorbent also showed doubled CO2/N2
selectivity and improved CO2 working capacity.
 We also developed a proprietary PSA process for CO2 capture from flue gas. This new PSA
process requires far fewer numbers of adsorbent beds, thereby reducing the CO2 capture cost [11].
By combining the advantages of the MLD/ALD-based sorbents and the proprietary PSA process,
it allows the PSA system to be designed with far fewer number of beds than would otherwise be
required because the bed height can be modestly increased without a pressure drop penalty during
the feed step, thereby reducing CO2 capture costs.
1.2. Project Objectives and Team
 The objective of this project is to develop a transformational tailor-made sorbent/PSA process
that can be installed in new or retrofitted into existing PC power plants for CO2 capture with 95%
CO2 purity and a cost of approximately $30 per tonne of CO2 captured; it will be ready for
demonstration by 2030.
 The project team is comprised of UB, USC, GTI, RPI, Trimeric, and NCCC. The proposed
program uses each team member’s unique expertise (RPI and UB: simulation and sorbent
development; USC: innovational PSA process integration with sorbents; GTI: field testing
processing and engineering; Trimeric: TEA) which is critical to developing the transformational,
and revolutionary MLD/ALD tailor-made size-sieving sorbent/PSA technology for bench-scale
testing, and to achieve DOE cost and performance targets.

2. Experimental Methods, Assumptions, and Procedures

2.1. Computer and Simulation Geometry Setup
The computer cluster containing 8-node and with a total of 96 CPUs was used for the primary
computational screening, because it enables extensive parallelization that allows simulations of
larger systems over a longer time. A new dual quad-core desktop computer was used for shorter
validation simulations and for data analysis and visualization. The simulations were done using
GROMACS, which we have used previously for simulations of membranes. The visualization was
done using Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD).
We have performed the initial simulation setup analysis, and a schematic of the setup is shown in
Figure 2. We use periodic boundary conditions in order to approximate larger sized systems than
are possible to simulate directly. A slab geometry of the underlying sorbent is used (shown in
grey). In order to accelerate the simulations, we coated MLD layers (shown in blue) on both the
top and bottom surfaces. This allows us to simulate essentially two systems/surfaces at the same
time. A gas mixture of CO2 and N2 are both above the top surface and below the bottom surface,
and these gas regions are connected by the periodic boundary conditions. Holes are created in the
MLD coating that leads to a mismatch with the pores of the sorbent.

 Figure 2. Schematic of the simulation geometry to produce sorbent/coating/gas interfaces with periodic
boundary conditions in all three dimensions. The sorbent is shown in grey and the MLD layers are shown
 in blue.
Sorbent Material Setup
In order to test the sorbent/MLD materials, we created the geometry with all atoms that is imported
into the GROMACS program. We wrote a custom program that takes the unit-cell of the base
sorbent, repeats it to create the crystal structure, and places the MLD layer on the top and bottom.
This allows us to rapidly create a number of different composite materials. As an example, our
new program has been used to create the setup of a 5A zeolite base material with MLD coating.
This is shown in Figure 3. The unit cell of the 5A zeolite structure is shown in Figure 3a, and our
new program automatically repeated this unit cell to create the base material, as shown in grey in
Figure 3b. The MLD layer on the top and bottom is shown in cyan. We have determined that the
best way to model the pore-mismatch properties of this layer is to use a layer composed of spheres.
The removal of spheres from the layer creates the pores in the MLD layer. We varied the thickness
of the MLD layer, along with the number and size of the pores, to design and screen the new
sorbent materials.

2.2. PSA Process Modeling
A vacuum swing adsorption (VSA) process that uses commercial (i.e., non-modified) 13X zeolite
beads is being designed to handle a very large, full-scale, water-free, flow rate corresponding to
the flue gas produced from a 550 MW coal fired power plant. The goal is to design a VSA process
that reaches the target VSA performance while minimizing the number of total beds required in
the VSA system by operating one or more identical VSA units in parallel. Each of these VSA units
contains a minimum number of identical beds. Previous work by the USC team has indicated an
80-unit 3-bed VSA system (i.e., 240 beds total) using a structured adsorbent achieved the target
CO2 purity and CO2 recovery in the heavy product.

The VSA cycle step sequence includes a feed (F) step at the feed high pressure, a cocurrent Heavy
Reflux (HR) step at the feed high pressure that receives all the heavy product coming from all the
Light Reflux (LR) steps, a cocurrent Equalization Down (EqD) step, a Countercurrent
Depressurization (CnD) steps in sequence targeting the low pressure, a countercurrent LR step at
the low pressure receiving as feed a fraction of the light product produced during the F step, a
countercurrent Equalization Up (EqU) step that receives all the gas leaving the EqD step, and a
Light Product Pressurization (LPP) step that receives light product produced during the F step to
bring the pressure of the bed back to 120 kPa. The fraction of the light product leaving the F step
used in the LR step is referred to as the Light Reflux Ratio (LRR). The feed flow rate into the VSA
unit corresponds to the total feed flow rate to be processed divided by the number of VSA units
required that are operating in parallel.

Figure 5. Schematic of a bed interconnect diagram for the nF+nLR+3-bed nF+nLR+5-step VSA process,
showing where the influent and effluent gases come from for each. All the LR gas for each LR bed comes
 from F1. The cycle schedules with more feed or light reflux beds simply have additional beds and

The reason this design uses multiple feed beds and especially multiple LR beds in the cycle
schedule is due to axial pressure drop along the bed. It is possible to significantly reduce the total
number of beds and the number of units running in parallel by using multiple feed beds and
multiple LR beds in essentially the same cycle step sequence. To prove this concept, a process
intensification scaling formulation was developed to size the full-scale beds according to certain
restrictions on velocity and pressure drop when scaled up from bench scale experimental results
that met the CO2 capture performance criteria. This process intensification scaling formulation is

then followed by full-scale simulations of some of the more optimal designs and extensions thereof
using the USC DAPS. A brief description of the process intensification scaling procedure is given
below.
Process Intensification Scaling Formulation: Preliminary VSA Unit Design
A desirable preliminary full-scale bed size in a VSA unit was obtained in MS Excel initially by
satisfying the bed pressure drop conditions for both the F and LR steps using Ergun’s equation. It
is assumed that there are a total number of identical large scale VSA units equal to nu, where each
contain nF beds for the F step and nLR beds for the LR step. Each of the beds in these units is also
identical with length L and diameter D that is defined by a given aspect ratio RLD (i.e, D = L/RLD).
The large-scale bed size is determined by scaling it up from a small bench scale experimental
system by relating the total flow rate FT (i.e., FT = 25,156,286 SLPM) to the bench scale flow rate
FBS of a single bed in a bench scale experimental PSA system of length LBS = 0.508 m (20 inch)
and diameter DBS = 0.0508 m (2 inch) with a verified good performance. LRR, Dp and RLD are input
parameters, with nu, nF and nLR as outputs, and while minimizing the amount of metal surface area
of each cylindrical bed S, where S is considered to be proportional to the capital cost.
The scaling analysis algorithm developed to find the optimum VSA design with minimized capital
cost is shown in Figure 6. For a given LRR, Dp and RLD, the procedure begins with nLR = 2 and nF
= 2. Then nU is increased from 1 until the F and LR pressure drop limitations are reached. S is then
evaluated. Next, the previous step is repeated by setting nF = nF + 1 (nF ≤ 6 in this work). The
more optimal VSA design is determined by the nF that gives the lowest S with nLR = 2. Then, the
entire procedure is repeated by setting nlR = nLR + 1. The optimum VSA design is selected from
the nF-nLR pair matrix that gives the lowest S.

ii) Dryer Wheels
The flue gas leaving the DCC needs to be dried further before reaching the PSA. The PSA has a
maximum water tolerance of 1.0 mol% per experimental testing conducted by the project team;
0.4 mol% was chosen as a conservative value to use for this study. To reach this spec, USC
suggested the use of desiccant wheels to remove this water and avoid the use of thermal input for
regeneration. Desiccant wheels can operate using a concentration swing as opposed to temperature
swing to remove water vapor from a gas stream. The flue gas flows through a portion of the rotating
wheel where water is adsorbed using the desiccant. Then, bone dry purge gas from the PSA and
supplemental air flow through the remaining portion of the rotating wheel to remove adsorbed
water vapor and regenerate the desiccant. The wet exhaust gas leaving the wheel is vented to
atmosphere. This process requires thirty-five (35) industrially sized desiccant wheels operating in
parallel (each with a capacity of 42,000 scfm) to dry the flue gas to the required water spec entering
the PSA. A reputable commercial vendor provided a quotation and sizing for the desiccant wheels,

 27

estimating their performance based on flow conditions from the process simulation and desired
outlet water content.
iii) Pressure Swing Adsorption
The PSA unit takes the dry flue gas exiting the dryer wheels and processes it through a twelve-bed
system to produce a purified CO2 stream; a detailed explanation of the PSA technology is included
in the final report. Three external streams exit the PSA process block – a light reflux stream, a
purge stream, and a product CO2 stream.
The vacuum light reflux stream is compressed and recycled to the PSA inlet. The dry purge gas is
sent to the dryer wheels for desiccant regeneration. The vacuum CO2 product stream leaving the
PSA is sent to the product vacuum compression train for further processing.
iv) Product Vacuum Compression
The CO2-rich stream leaving the PSA feeds the product vacuum compression unit. The vapor
enters a three-stage, centrifugal turbo blower to compress the gas from 25 kPa(a) to roughly
atmospheric conditions (103 kPa(a)) with intercooling between each stage. After the third stage of
compression, the vapor passes through an aftercooler and liquid knockout before being sent to the
CO2 compression train.
v) Light Reflux Recycle Compression
A separate light reflux stream leaving the PSA feeds the recycle compression unit. The vapor
enters a three-stage, centrifugal turbo blower to compress the gas from 25 kPa(a) to roughly
atmospheric conditions (108 kPa(a)) with intercooling between each stage. After the third stage of
compression, the reflux passes through an aftercooler and liquid knockout before being sent back
to the PSA as a sweep gas to purge the sorbent.
vi) CO2 Compression, Oxygen Removal, and Dehydration Area
The outlet from the product vacuum compression unit feeds the CO2 compression train. CO2-rich
gas is compressed using a seven-stage centrifugal compressor with intercooling and liquid
knockout between each stage. CO2 is compressed from 0.10 MPa to 15.3 MPa to produce a CO2
product stream at pipeline conditions.
After the fifth stage of compression, an Oxygen Removal system is used to remove most of the
residual oxygen from the CO2. The system consists of a fixed bed of proprietary platinum catalyst
over an alumina substrate. Hydrogen is introduced upstream of the fixed bed and serves as a
reducing agent. Hydrogen and oxygen react on the catalyst to form water vapor; an excess of
hydrogen is required to drive the reaction to completion. This process is used in the oil and gas
industry but has yet to be demonstrated commercially for CO2 applications.
After oxygen removal, a Packaged TEG Dehydration System is used to remove most of the residual
water from the product CO2. The dehydration system is a requirement for all CO2 capture systems,
because if it were it not included, the compressed gas leaving the compression system would be
saturated (or nearly saturated) with water. Because the CO2 may have to be transferred a significant
distance via pipeline, cooling of the CO2 is likely to occur. Condensation of water and formation
of a free-water phase in high-pressure CO2 can result in a corrosive environment, which could
preclude the use of carbon steel as a material of construction for the pipeline. The dehydration unit
is a conventional TEG system, which is a liquid desiccant commonly used to dehydrate natural gas
and CO2 streams. After the fifth stage of compression (4,089 kPa(a)), a significant percentage

(~91%) of the water entering the compressor has already been removed due to compression of the
gas.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1. Computational Screening of MLD Modified Sorbents
3.1.1. Sorbent Material Screening
i) 5A zeolite as the base material
Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations quantified the behavior of the 5A/MLD material by
placing it in a gas environment of CO2 or N2 and quantifying the dynamics of penetration of the
molecules into the material. The number of molecules in each location was measured using the
average density across the x and y directions at different z locations within the sorbent and within
the gas phase. The average density within the central region is used to quantify the kinetics and
preferences of the molecules for the sorbent material. We can manipulate and improve the
preference for CO2 by shifting the MLD layer relative to the 5A zeolite. As an example, this type
of quantification is shown in Figure 18. The open symbols correspond to a shift of the MLD layer
by 0.3 nm. The CO2 was barely affected by the shift, while the amount of N2 was suppressed. This
shifted case is a promising new material found through this computational screening.

Overall, these full-scale simulation results show that the MLD sorbent performs as well as or even
better than pristine 13X and has the potential to reduce the number of beds to as low as 20 in a 2-
unit VSA process. This is a remarkable result by MLD sorbent. Hence, these results show the
potential superior performance of the MLD sorbent over that of pristine 13X, especially when
considering it appears to be more water tolerant. Thus, the simulation part of Milestone 5.1 was
accomplished, i.e., follow-on DAPS simulations validated that the required beds can be reduced
to ≤ 48 for a 550 MWe (net) power plant.
Overall, these new VSA system designs are proving to be significantly better than the VSA system
design USC developed with previous DOE funding. In that project, an 80-unit 3-bed VSA system
was developed (i.e., 240 beds). In contrast, the new VSA cycle concept reduces the number of
beds required for a 550 MWe (net) power plant from 240 to 104 with 3 mm commercial 13X
zeolite beads, from 240 to 24 with 9 mm 13X zeolite beads, and from 240 to just 20 with 9 mm
MLD zeolite beads. So far, five unique VSA cycle schedules were developed that achieve all the

3.2.2. Further DAPS Simulation for Improved PSA System Design

3.5. ALD Modified 13X Zeolite: CO2 Capture Performance Evaluation
Due to its large pore size of 0.74 nm, 13X zeolite permits TiCl4 (molecular size: 0.5 nm) to
access its internal micropores and interact with hydroxyl defects throughout the framework,
instead of reacting solely to the crystal exterior. Since hydroxyl defects occupy the same
framework sites as chemisorbed CO32- species [16-17], reaction of TiCl4 with these defects is
expected to inhibit the formation of CO32-, as shown in Figure 44. ALD modification could
polarize Na+ cations and/or create steric hinderance that limit interactions between Na+ and
physiosorbed CO2/N2, thereby decreasing quadrupole interactions and lowering adsorption
enthalpy and affinity.

 Figure 44. Schematic of the 13X zeolite functional group modification via ALD.
3.5.1. CO2 Chemisorption Inhibition via Pre-loading of H2O
To assess the effect of selective passivation of strong adsorption sites in 13X on the CO2 and
N2 adsorption properties, we pre-loaded H2O in 13X zeolite and collected CO2 and N2
isotherms at 30oC. H2O pre-loading was accomplished via H2O vapor saturation followed
by degassing from 100-300oC, causing the weakly adsorbed H2O to be liberated and removed
while the strongly adsorbed H2O remained trapped in the 13X framework. After H2O pre-

 61

loading, the 13X zeolite sample was characterized via 30oC N2 isotherms, followed by
evacuation at 30oC and collection of 30oC CO2 isotherms with the isotherms shown in Figure
45A and B.

Figure 45. (A) CO2 and (B) N2 isotherms at 30oC of pristine 13X preloaded with various
amounts of H2O. The corresponding desorption temperatures for pre-loaded H2O are listed
in (C) and (D). (C) CO2 and N2 amount adsorbed at 30oC and 15 and 85 kPa, respectively.
(D) CO2 working capacity (5-15 kPa) and CO2/N2 separation factor (5-15 kPa CO2; 1-85 kPa
N2) versus H2O desorption temperature for H2O pre-loaded 13X.
There was a clear trend of declining CO2 capacity, N2 capacity, and CO2 adsorption affinity
as the amount of pre-loaded H2O increased. To quantify this behavior as relevant to PCC,
QCO2 and QN2 at PCC adsorption conditions (15 kPa CO2; 85 kPa N2) were calculated and
shown in FigureC to illustrate the declining CO2 and N2 capacity with increasing H2O
loading. Likewise, the WCCO2 (5-15 kPa) and the SFCO2/N2 (5-15 kPa CO2; 1-85 kPa N2) were
calculated and shown in FigureD. Remarkably, the WCCO2 increased by 12.7% at the
optimal H2O loading (175oC desorption temperature) despite a 22% decline in QCO2 (15 kPa).
This was a consequence of a dramatic reduction in CO2 adsorption affinity as indicated by
the decreasing initial slope of CO2 isotherms with the increasing H2O loading (FigureA). In
effect, the CO2 capacity below 5 kPa, and especially <1 kPa, declined while the WCCO2
between 5-15 kPa increased with H2O pre-loading up to 175oC desorption temperature.
Meanwhile, the N2 isotherms were almost entirely linear (FigureB), and the QN2 was
proportional to the WCN2 independent of the chosen pressure interval at a given H2O loading
so both decreased with increasing H2O loading. The combination of maintained WCCO2 with
rapidly declining QN2 resulted in a substantial increase in the SFCO2/N2 with increasing H2O
loading. The improvements in WCCO2 and SFCO2/N2 indicate that the CO2 and N2 adsorption
thermodynamics were significantly altered by the presence of adsorbed H2O. Furthermore,
 62

CO32- chemisorption inhibition alone cannot explain the changes in N2 adsorption behavior.
Instead, we speculate that hydroxyl defects generated by pre-adsorbed H2O and/or free H2O
molecules may polarize Na+ or sterically hinder interactions between Na+ and N2 gas.
Overall, these results are consistent with the hypothesis that preferential passivation of the
highest strength adsorption sites in 13X may result in more desirable properties for PCC via
P/VSA. However, the preparation of H2O pre-loaded 13X is highly impractical at scale
because the H2O Henry’s constant is high at temperatures lower than 300oC, resulting in
slow desorption kinetics. Furthermore, the H2O pre-loading is highly unstable when exposed
to ambient air as atmospheric H2O will quickly saturate 13X, necessitating a reproduction
of the degassing procedure. Instead, permanent chemical modification of strong adsorption
sites in 13X should result in a more stable and scalable adsorbent that emulates the desirable
properties of H2O pre-loaded 13X. We employed ALD, via TiCl4/H2O vapor deposition, as a
facile and scalable technique to permanently passivate strong adsorption behavior and
improve the properties of 13X zeolite for PCC.

Considering that the Ti-13X samples were degassed at 350oC before adsorption isotherm
measurements, adsorbed free H2O was not present. Instead, the changes in CO2 and N2 adsorption
properties must be a result of ALD modification. We hypothesize that two phenomena are
responsible for the thermodynamic changes in CO2 and N2 adsorption properties. First, CO32-
chemisorption formation may be inhibited via reaction between TiCl4 and hydroxyl defects in the
13X micropores, as depicted in Error! Reference source not found. 44. However, N2 does not
undergo chemisorption in 13X zeolite, so the inhibition of chemisorption cannot explain the
suppressed N2 adsorption displayed by both Ti-13X and H2O pre-loaded 13X. We hypothesize
that ALD modification, or hydroxyl defects in the case of H2O pre-loaded 13X, may polarize Na+
cations and/or impose steric hinderance between Na+ cations and physisorbing CO2/N2 gases,
resulting in weaker interactions with their quadrupoles and a reduction in adsorption enthalpy and
affinity.
To further elucidate the change in CO2 adsorption thermodynamics during fabrication of 1-Ti-
100C, microcalorimetry experiments were conducted via CO2 adsorption in a TGA/DSC to
observe the average CO2 heat of adsorption at several CO2 loadings in both pristine 13X and 1-Ti-
100C. The average CO2 heat of adsorption was also calculated from the isotherm model (Table
32). The average CO2 heat of adsorption for the isotherm model was shown alongside the CO2
microcalorimetry experiments and plotted in Figure 47B for both pristine 13X and 1-Ti-100C. For
pristine 13X, the average heat of adsorption declined significantly with CO2 loading as observed
in both the isotherm model and the CO2 microcalorimetry data. Similar behavior has been
observed by other researchers conducting CO2 microcalorimetry experiments on 13X. This is
consistent with the known CO2 adsorption behavior in 13X. Namely, the CO2 adsorption behavior
is highly heterogeneous, with both high and low adsorption strength sites existing in 13X. In
contrast, 1-Ti-100C demonstrated almost no change in CO2 heat of adsorption according to both
the isotherm model and the microcalorimetry experiments, and the heat of adsorption was lower
than pristine 13X. These results are consistent with the hypothesis that the ALD modification
techniques employed during fabrication of 1-Ti-100C resulted in the selective passivation of high
enthalpy CO2 adsorption sites in 13X.
To conclusively demonstrate the inhibition of high enthalpy CO2 adsorption in Ti-13X, CO2 TPD
was conducted on pristine 13X and 1-Ti-100C. CO2 TPD was collected under 4.7 kPa CO2 with
N2 balance at 8oC/min ramp rate, and the DTG plot was shown in Figure 47C. 1-Ti-100C was
found to have a reduced amount of CO2 adsorbed at all temperatures, which was consistent with
the CO2 isotherm data. Pristine 13X demonstrated a local minimum in the DTG CO2 desorption
spectrum at about 240oC with a subsequent high temperature CO2 desorption zone. The high
temperature (240-400oC) CO2 DTG peak shown in this work has also been observed in several
other CO2 TPD experiments on 13X in literature. This high temperature CO2 desorption is
typically cited as chemisorption, and the desorption energy is estimated to be >60 kJ/mol. In
contrast, the CO2 DTG desorption profile decreased monotonically for 1-Ti-100C, indicating that
the highest enthalpy CO2 adsorption sites typically present in pristine 13X have been passivated.
This further supports the hypothesis that the changes in CO2 and N2 adsorption behavior from ALD
fabrication were due to selective passivation of the high enthalpy CO2 adsorption sites. Overall,
1-Ti-100C demonstrates highly desirable CO2 capture properties with improved WCCO2 and
dramatically improved SFCO2/N2 compared to pristine 13X. Furthermore, the combined findings

 66

of the CO2 isotherm model, CO2 microcalorimetry, and CO2 TPD support the hypothesis that the
change in CO2 adsorption behavior was due to the selective passivation of high enthalpy CO2
adsorption sites.
3.5.4 Comparison of Ti-13X to promising MOFs
Without conducting P/VSA process simulations, there is widely accepted consensus that there is
no fool-proof evaluation method to determine the adsorbent with the lowest energy requirements
and CO2 capture cost for a given P/VSA process. However, researchers have developed
performance metrics that may be calculated from isotherm properties of the adsorbents for general
comparison. These performance metrics are generally formulated from isotherm properties,
including working capacities and adsorption enthalpies of both CO2 and N2.
We selected two performance metrics found in literature. The first performance metric selected
was GEM. GEM was specifically developed for evaluation of adsorbents for PCC via P/VSA.
SFCO2/N2 was selected as the second performance metric. SFCO2/N2 is frequently used in a variety
of separation applications and has wide-spread applicability across many separation technologies.
Two MOFs, UTSA-16 and Mg-MOF-74 were selected for comparison to Ti-13X and pristine 13X.
UTSA-16 and Mg-MOF-74 are frequently chosen in comparison studies, as they are both
promising adsorbents for CO2 capture but demonstrate significant differences in their overall
CO2/N2 adsorption behavior. Namely, UTSA-16 has a relatively low affinity for CO2 and N2,
while Mg-MOF-74 has a relatively high CO2 and N2 affinity. The SFCO2/N2 and GEM of 1-Ti-
100C, pristine-13X, UTSA-16, and Mg-MOF-74 were calculated from 30 to 70oC using DSL
model parameters and plotted in Figure 48.

 Figure 48. (A) GEM and (B) SFCO2/N2 versus adsorption temperature for 1-Ti-100C, UTSA-16, Pristine
                                      13X, and Mg-MOF-74.
The model parameters for UTSA-16[18] and Mg-MOF-74[19] were obtained from literature. We
selected the pressure intervals of 15-9 kPa for CO2 and 85-1 kPa for N2. The adsorption pressures
reflect the feed conditions of coal flue gas for PCC. The desorption pressures would depend on

 67

the process design of the P/VSA system, so we opted to use the desorption conditions used during
development of the GEM of 9 kPa CO2 and 1 kPa N2. The pressure interval selection of N2 has
almost no influence on the comparison of these adsorbents. This was because the N2 isotherms
are almost entirely linear between 0 and 85 kPa for all four adsorbents. 1-Ti-100C demonstrated
significantly increased GEM and SF compared to pristine 13X or Mg-MOF-74 at all temperatures.
1-Ti-100C was slightly outperformed by UTSA-16 at low temperatures (30-50oC). However, 1-
Ti-100C outscored UTSA-16 beyond ~60oC and ~50oC for GEM and SF, respectively.
Considering that coal-fired flue gas is typically delivered from 50 to 60oC in PCC processes, both
performance metrics suggest there may be significant potential for Ti-13X to improve the process
level performance of PCC via P/VSA systems. However, full process level simulations and P/VSA
testing are required to determine if the adsorption property improvements present in 1-Ti-100C
may manifest in improved process level performance.
In summary, Ti-13X scored higher than pristine 13X and Mg-MOF-74 and scored similarly to
UTSA-16. Overall, Ti-13X presents as a highly scalable and low-cost physical adsorbent with
properties on par with promising MOFs for PCC via P/VSA, meriting further investigation into
the potential of Ti-13X via adsorbent bed testing and flue gas contaminant investigation. In
addition, in ALD process, only TiCl4 and water are needed, which simplifies sorbent preparation
process. Therefore, we selected Ti-13X for further contaminant testing and scaleup.

Run 1 served as the dry baseline test, in which the feed contained no moisture. Under these

95.5 vol%. This run established a reference point for evaluating the effect of moisture and provided
a benchmark for assessing sorbent recovery after regeneration.
Run 2-4 introduced moisture levels of 1%, 0.42%, and 0.05% H2O vapor, respectively, and each
run was operated continuously for 24 hours. Compared with the dry baseline, the presence of
moisture led to a modest decline in CO2 capture efficiency, decreasing from 89.4% in Run 1 to
88.7-86% across the moist runs. This trend reflects competitive adsorption between H2O and CO2
on the sorbent surface. Despite the reduction in capture efficiency, the CO2 dry-basis purity

underwent regeneration at 200ºC under vacuum.

notably higher than the dry baseline in Run 1. Although the CO2 dry-basis purity decreased slightly
to 93.6 vol%, the significant recovery and even improvement of capture efficiency indicates that
regeneration at 200ºC is sufficient to fully regenerate the sorbents, the residual moisture or
adsorbed intermediates accumulated during the 24-hour tests were effectively removed during
regeneration.
Overall, these results show that moderate moisture levels in the feed lead to a small but consistent
reduction in CO2 capture efficiency, while the CO2 dry-basis purity remains essentially unaffected
by the presence of water under the applied vacuum desorption conditions. In addition, the post-
regeneration performance indicates that heating the sorbent to 200ºC under vacuum is sufficient
to fully regenerate the sorbents.

3.7.6.1 Parametric testing
Figure 62 shows the effect of vacuum level on CO2 capture performance using simulated industrial
flue gas. The feed gas flow rate was maintained at 8 SLPM, with a composition of 24.0 vol% CO2,
10.3 vol% O2, 1.0 vol% H2O, and balanced N2. The operating temperature and feed pressure were
fixed at 35ºC and 1.06 bara, respectively. As the vacuum pressure increased from 15 kPa to 20 kPa
and then to 25 kPa, the CO2 dry-basis purity of the product slightly decreased from 97.2 vol% to
96.1 vol% and further to 92.5 vol%. The CO2 capture efficiency decreased from 84.4% to 70.3%
and then to 60%. Correspondingly, the CO2 capture rate decreased from 1.96 to 1.82 and finally
to 1.55 kg CO2/kg sorbent/day as the vacuum level increased.

 Figure 62. Effect of vacuum level on industrial flue gas CO2 capture performance.
3.7.6.2 Long-term testing
 78

A 200-h continuous CO2 capture test using simulated industrial flue gas was conducted to
investigate the long-term stability. The system was operated under the following conditions: a feed
gas composition of 24.0 vol% CO2, 10.3 vol% O2, and 1.0 vol% H2O, balanced N2, a feed flow
rate of 8 SLPM, feed pressure of 1.06 bara, temperature of 35ºC, and vacuum pressure of 20 kPa.
The system achieved a CO2 dry-basis purity of 96.4 vol% with a CO2 capture efficiency of 70.5%,
indicating promising capture performance. Throughout the 200-hour test, both the CO2 dry-basis
purity and CO2 capture efficiency remained stable, as shown in Figure 63. These results show the
robust long-term stability of the sorbent under industrial flue gas conditions. It is also worth noting
that since the test campaign began at NCCC in April 2025 and continued through December 2025,
no sorbent regeneration was carried out. After each test campaign, the system was simply shut
down and later restarted for the next campaign, without any special reactivation procedures applied
to the sorbent during the shutdown periods. This clearly indicates the robustness and long-term
durability of the sorbent under realistic operating conditions.

3.8.2. Energy Performance Evaluation of RPI PSA Technology
Power Plant Derating and Parasitic Power Demand
The energy performance for the novel process technology was evaluated considering the following
major energy requirements:
 Incremental power requirements (cooling water pumps and cooling tower fans) for the
 increased cooling tower load due to the CO2 capture process cooling needs. Cooling duties for
 80

 the capture process were derived from Trimeric’s simulation of process technology using
 inputs from USC for the dryer wheels and PSA.
 Power requirements for all rotating equipment within the process technology.
The parasitic energy demand for the process was estimated and compared directly to DOE Case
B12B.90 in the Baseline Report pulverized coal power plant with a net electrical capacity of 650
MWe.An advantage of the novel PSA process technology is that there is no parasitic demand on
the power plant for steam usage. Since the PSA does not require external heat input, no steam is
needed. The only additional parasitic demands on the power plant from the novel case are the
compression power and cooling water duty. Although it may not show up in the economics of this
TEA, eliminating the need for steam extraction from the base power plant to the capture system
will reduce construction costs and operational complexity of the overall system.
Overall, the total parasitic demand for the PSA sorbent process technology process is nearly
identical to Case B12B.90. The parasitic load for capture and compression is approximately 2
MWe less when compared to the base case, but as noted previously the cooling tower demand for
the vacuum condenser is higher compared to Case B12B.90. Overall, the base power plant sizes
are essentially the same for both cases, and both technologies have an energy requirement of 1.10
GJ/tonne CO2 captured.
3.8.3. Equipment Sizing and Capital Cost Evaluation
The stream tables serve as the basis for equipment sizing and economic estimates. The economic
evaluation utilizes cost estimates for the purchased equipment costs of individual equipment or
equipment packages associated with the PSA process technology, combined with appropriate
scaling factors to develop a total plant cost estimate for the capture plant that can be compared to
the NETL Rev 4a Baseline Case B12B.90. The Case B12B.90 baseline does not include itemized
equipment costs for the capture system, so the aggregate plant-level economics are the appropriate
level of comparison.

The general approach used for preliminary equipment sizing and associated cost estimation can be
summarized as follows:
 Determine equipment type and estimate sizes of equipment for all process technology
 equipment in the capture process. Trimeric used standard engineering sizing methods, scaling
 from previous projects/internal sources, and bottom-up sizing to generate characteristic
 equipment sizing for all equipment. This approach was confined primarily to the packed
 column for the direct contact cooler and the packed vessels associated with the PSA system.
 All remaining equipment were sized and costed as skidded equipment packages – desiccant
 wheels, light reflux compression, product CO2 compression, CO2 oxygen removal, and CO2
 dehydration. For these systems, Trimeric used vendor quotes obtained specifically for this
 project (or from past projects of a similar nature and scaled based upon power and/or flow
 requirements) to estimate skidded equipment costs.
Identify a reference or baseline equipment cost with an associated equipment sizing metric and
cost scaling exponents to allow estimation of the cost of the equipment in the PSA process

4. Summary and Future Steps

UB developed a transformational Ti-13X sorbent to passivate the strong adsorption sites of
conventional 13X zeolite. Compared to the pristine 13X zeolite, Ti-13X exhibited improved CO2
capture performance, including higher CO2 working capacity, increased CO2/N2 selectivity,
improved regenerability, and greater tolerance to water and contaminants. USC developed a
proprietary PSA cycle schedule for CO2 capture from flue gas. This process requires far fewer
number of adsorption beds (relative to conventional adsorption processes), thereby reducing the
CO2 capture cost.
A bench-scale PSA skid loaded with the Ti-13X sorbent and incorporating the proprietary PSA
cycle schedule was designed and constructed. The skid was extensively tested at USC including
comparison between 13X and Ti-13X and effects of water vapor on CO2 capture performance.
During these tests, greater than 90% CO2 capture efficiency with >95% CO2 dry-basis purity was
achieved for the Ti-13X adsorbent. The skid was then moved to NCCC for tests with actual coal
flue gas or actual natural gas boiler flue gas modified to replicate the CO2 concentrations found in
coal flue gas, NGCC and industrial flue gases. GTI Energy led field testing at the NCCC. For
simulated coal fired flue gas, the system achieved a CO2 dry-basis purity of 96.3 vol% and a CO2
capture efficiency of 92.6%, with stable operation over 1,000 h. For simulated industrial flue gas,
the system achieved a CO2 dry-basis purity of 96.4 vol% and a CO2 capture efficiency of 70.5%,
with stable operation over 200 h.
Trimeric Corporation conducted TEA to evaluate the Ti-13X sorbent utilized in PSA for post-
combustion capture from flue gas produced in a supercritical PC power plant. The cost of CO 2
capture, $48.69/tonne, was estimated using the capital and operating and maintenance costs for
PSA process technology. A sensitivity study on desiccant wheel costs and PSA sorbent costs was
also conducted, suggesting a CO2 capture cost range of $48.69-51.99/tonne.
Future steps would include the following:
 • Design and build an engineering-scale Ti-13X/PSA CO2 capture system; and
 • Conduct tests at coal-fired power or industrial plants, and demonstrate a continuous,
 steady-state operation for a minimum of two months.
