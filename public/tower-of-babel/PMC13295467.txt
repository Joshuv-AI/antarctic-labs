CRRT-miECCO2R: a step toward integrated multiorgan support
Authors: Faeq Husain-Syed, Vitalii Kryvenko, István Vadász
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We thank Gasca-Aldama and colleagues for their thoughtful comments regarding our study evaluating minimally invasive extracorporeal carbon dioxide removal (miECCO2R) using a continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) platform [1]. We appreciate their discussion of several key aspects of ECCO2R, including extracorporeal blood flow, pump technology, anticoagulation strategies, and future trial design. We agree that the ultimate value of this approach can only be established through adequately powered multicenter randomized controlled trials. Future studies should therefore assess not only ventilator-free days but also broader patient-centered outcomes, such as non-respiratory organ failure-free days [2].

Patients with hypercapnic respiratory failure may benefit from miECCO2R in two ways: first, by enabling a reduction in ventilatory intensity and, second, by facilitating the achievement of (near)-normocapnia, as both excessive ventilator settings and elevated PaCO2 levels are independently associated with worse outcomes in this patient population [3]. In response to the authors’ concern that low-flow systems may provide insufficient CO2 clearance, we observed clinically meaningful reductions in PaCO2, normalization of pH, and significant decreases in driving pressure were achieved despite very low (100–400 mL/min) blood flow rates. Possible explanations may include the large surface area of the gas exchanger, the continuous nature of treatment, and the combined CRRT-miECCO2R approach, in which extracorporeal CO2 removal is accompanied by continuous management of acid–base homeostasis through RRT. In addition, regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) may have contributed to stable circuit performance and treatment continuity. Continuous operation over several days may compensate, at least in part, for lower instantaneous CO2 removal rates by providing sustained CO2 clearance and facilitating gradual ventilatory de-escalation. Whether higher CO2 removal rates translate into improved clinical outcomes remains to be demonstrated. Indeed, rapid correction of hypercapnia may result in cerebral vasoconstriction and ischemia, increasing the risk of neurological complications, including seizures, ischemic stroke, and intracranial hemorrhage [4]. Such complications may have contributed to the unfavorable outcomes observed in previous ECCO2R trials, although intracranial hemorrhage was likely multifactorial and may have also been related to therapeutic anticoagulation and centrifugal pump-associated hemolysis [5].

Regarding the authors’ concern that prolonged operation of peristaltic pumps may increase mechanical blood trauma, peristaltic pumps have been safely used in extracorporeal therapies for several decades and constitute the standard pumping technology in intermittent hemodialysis and CRRT. Moreover, they are routinely employed in continuous extracorporeal therapies where uninterrupted operation over several days is common. In clinical practice, complications during RRT are more frequently related to vascular access, anticoagulation, and circuit management rather than pump technology itself. In our study, no clinically relevant hemolysis or major bleeding events were observed.

Importantly, many current CRRT platforms incorporate predefined operational limits designed to maintain circuit integrity during prolonged continuous use. In our system, these include a maximum filter runtime of 72 h and a maximum processed blood volume of 1250 L. These safeguards are intended to preserve the mechanical integrity and compliance of the tubing segment subjected to continuous pump compression and may help limit excessive mechanical stress on blood components during prolonged operation. Once either threshold is reached, the system prompts circuit replacement. At blood flows of approximately 200 mL/min during CRRT-miECCO2R, the 72-hour maximum filter runtime is typically reached before the processed blood volume limit. In contrast, at higher blood flow rates, such as approximately 400 mL/min used in standalone miECCO2R, the processed blood-volume limit of 1250 L would be reached after approximately 52 h, necessitating earlier circuit replacement [6]. Whether operation at higher blood flow rates results in improved patient-centered outcomes or different safety profiles compared with established CRRT-based platforms remains to be demonstrated.

Beyond CO2 removal, CRRT platforms offer additional functionalities that may become increasingly relevant as extracorporeal support evolves. Integrated temperature management, for example, can help counteract heat loss associated with extracorporeal circulation, which may become more pronounced when high sweep gas flows are used during standalone miECCO2R. These features highlight the broader concept of integrated multiorgan support and suggest that future extracorporeal platforms should be evaluated not solely according to their CO2 removal capacity but also according to their ability to safely support multiple physiological functions simultaneously.

With regard to vascular access, the higher blood flow rates used in our study were achieved using dual-lumen venous catheters of 13.5 Fr. Although somewhat larger than catheters commonly used for conventional CRRT (approximately 12 Fr), these sizes are routinely used in intensive care practice and remain substantially smaller than the cannulas typically required for higher-flow ECCO2R or ECMO systems (usually ~ 20–30 Fr). Thus, clinically meaningful increases in extracorporeal blood flow can be achieved while preserving the minimally invasive nature of the approach.

Regarding the authors’ concern that RCA becomes increasingly challenging at higher blood flow rates, we agree that currently available CRRT-based RCA protocols have limitations at extracorporeal blood flow rates exceeding 200 mL/min. However, RCA remains the preferred anticoagulation strategy in CRRT because it prolongs circuit lifespan while reducing bleeding complications [7]. Recent work suggests that RCA in ECCO2R can be maintained even under substantially increased blood flow conditions, at least for shorter treatment durations [8]. Accordingly, further technological development may enable safe implementation of higher-flow miECCO2R while retaining the advantages of RCA. Such developments may include extracorporeal blood purification platforms with improved anticoagulation protocols, enhanced calcium management, and integrated acid–base control [9]. From this perspective, the key question may therefore not be whether RCA is feasible at higher blood flow rates, but rather how anticoagulation strategies and extracorporeal platforms should evolve to support them safely and effectively.

The evolution of CRRT platforms over recent decades has progressively expanded their role from isolated renal support to integrated extracorporeal support systems capable of managing fluid balance, electrolyte disorders, acid–base disturbances, elimination of hepatic toxins and, more recently, CO2 removal. In this context, CRRT-miECCO2R should be viewed as a further step toward integrated multiorgan support rather than merely as another approach to extracorporeal CO2 removal. From this perspective, future developments should be evaluated according to their ability to safely integrate complementary organ-support functions and, ultimately, improve patient-centered outcomes.
