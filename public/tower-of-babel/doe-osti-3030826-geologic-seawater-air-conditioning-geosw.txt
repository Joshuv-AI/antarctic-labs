Executive Summary
Puerto Rico’s year-round high temperatures and humidity create a persistent and growing
demand for cooling, especially in residential, commercial, and institutional buildings. This
demand significantly contributes to peak electricity loads, primarily driven by widespread
reliance on air-conditioning systems that operate inefficiently in many buildings that rely on
electricity intensive vapor-compression cooling. The island’s dependence on an aging, import-
fueled energy grid makes supplying electricity for cooling expensive and unreliable and
increases the island’s vulnerability to disruptions, particularly during extreme weather events like
hurricanes.

As a potential solution to reduce these vulnerabilities, this study evaluates a novel seawater air
conditioning (SWAC) system hydrogeologically connected to cold seawater via inland wells as
an alternative to conventional air conditioning. The proposed system, called geologic seawater
air conditioning (GeoSWAC), leverages inland wells hydrologically connected to deep, cold
seawater through permeable subsurface formations, eliminating a refrigeration cycle to produce
cooling. While conventional SWAC systems require long offshore pipelines in coastal
environments, GeoSWAC configuration reduces infrastructure costs, minimizes environmental
impacts, and improves resilience by reducing dependence on grid-supplied electricity. The
SWAC systems simplify the cooling process by avoiding energy-intensive refrigeration cycles
and using minimal moving parts and no refrigerants. Electricity consumption of 75%–90% can
be reduced the SWAC systems compared to chiller-based cooling systems (TEC Inc 2009; Lilley
et al. 2015). The system offers substantial operational cost savings and improved resilience
during power outages, as the system can run on backup generators or batteries. While the SWAC
system has been extensively developed and deployed worldwide (e.g., Sanjivy et al. 2023), the
hydrogeologic behavior and techno-economic performance of GeoSWAC have not been
systematically evaluated motivating the present study.

To evaluate the economic value of GeoSWAC system, a case study was conducted at a coastal
university campus in San Juan—the Universidad de Puerto Rico Río Piedras. Surface and
subsurface characteristics were investigated through building energy modeling, a site visit, and
extensive literature review. While in-depth modeling of the hydraulic connection between inland
wells and deep, cold seawater in the GeoSWAC system remains for future study, economic
metrics were estimated and compared with those of a water-cooled chiller system at the campus.
Results demonstrated that GeoSWAC system has significantly lower levelized costs of cooling
and operational costs, along with water conservation through the elimination of evaporative and
blowdown losses from cooling tower operation. Key advantages of GeoSWAC systems can
include access to a constant cold heat sink from deep seawater, minimal pumping requirements
due to favorable hydraulic gradients, and increased resilience during grid failures in areas like
Puerto Rico, the Caribbean, and coastal Florida. However, the system’s performance is
contingent on local geologic conditions, particularly the hydraulic connection between inland
wells and deep cold seawater through a permeable formation. Uncertainties in the deep
geological condition and aquifer characteristics in the San Juan Metropolitan Area must be
addressed through detailed geologic and hydrogeologic surveys and site-specific performance
modeling.

1 Introduction
Puerto Rico, a U.S. territory situated roughly 1,600 km (1,000 miles) southeast of Miami, sits in
the direct path of powerful Atlantic hurricanes. The island’s exposure to warm ocean waters,
high humidity, and prevailing wind patterns makes it especially susceptible to tropical storms
that damage critical infrastructure.

This vulnerability is compounded by a heavy reliance on expensive imported energy. The
devastating impact of Hurricane Maria in 2017 underscored these risks. Making landfall as a
Category 4 storm and briefly reaching Category 5 intensity, Maria triggered the longest blackout
in U.S. history, lasting nearly a year for some communities in Puerto Rico, and was linked to
thousands of fatalities (Santos-Burgoa et al. 2018; Kishore et al. 2018).

Puerto Rico experiences consistently high temperatures and humidity throughout the year,
creating a strong and growing demand for cooling, particularly for air conditioning in residential,
commercial, and public buildings. Peak electricity demand is heavily influenced by cooling
needs, especially during the hot summer months and periods of heat waves. Most buildings on
the island are not designed with passive cooling in mind and rely heavily on air-conditioning
systems. This has led to a significant increase in energy consumption, especially during midday
and early evening hours.

The island’s aging electrical grid and dependence on expensive imported fuels makes meeting
increased electricity demand costly and leaves the island vulnerable to power disruptions,
especially during hurricanes and other extreme weather events (Pokhrel et al. 2019; Baggu et al.
2024). Moreover, rising urbanization is expected to significantly increase cooling demand in the
coming decades, placing additional strain on existing infrastructure. The adoption of more
efficient cooling technologies is essential to address these challenges. Ongoing efforts are
focused on improving energy efficiency and promoting alternative energy sources for cooling,
aligning with Puerto Rico’s broader goals for energy flexibility.

This study explores a novel concept termed geologic seawater air conditioning (GeoSWAC) (Oh
et al. 2025a). The concept is inspired by seawater air conditioning (SWAC), which has emerged
as a potential alternative for reducing reliance on conventional, electricity-intensive cooling
systems in tropical coastal regions (Makai Ocean Engineering 2008; Lilley et al. 2015; Hunt et
al. 2019, 2022). While SWAC systems have been deployed and studied worldwide (e.g., Sanjivy
et al. 2023), the GeoSWAC concept—defined here as the use of inland wells hydraulically
connected to deep, cold seawater cold seawater through permeable geologic formations for
building cooling—has not been previously deployed or examined in the peer-reviewed literature.

In this study researchers assessed the subsurface, developed a conceptual geothermal model, and
evaluated the techno-economic feasibility of applying a GeoSWAC system at the University of
Puerto Rico at Río Piedras (UPR-RP), focusing on cooling loads for four representative campus
buildings. Modeled hourly loads were developed using EnergyPlus® and benchmarked against
existing system capacity, and economic performance was assessed using levelized cost of
cooling (LCOC) metrics to compare GeoSWAC with the current chilled water plant.

1.1 Conceptual District Cooling System—GeoSWAC
SWAC systems have gained attention as an alternative to electricity-intensive conventional air
conditioning in tropical coastal regions (Coony and Johnson 2024). These systems utilize cold
seawater, typically drawn from depths of 500 to 1,000 m where temperatures range 4°C–6°C, to
supply cooling demand in buildings (Figure 1a) (Hunt et al. 2019, 2022). The cold seawater is
pumped using intake pipelines, often extending along the seabed one to several kilometers
offshore, and circulated through heat exchangers that transfer the cold energy to a freshwater
distribution loop connected to buildings. Because the SWAC system relies primarily on intensive
pumping energy and does not require refrigeration cycles, electricity consumption can be
reduced by as much as 75%–90% compared to traditional air conditioning (TEC Inc 2009; Lilley
et al. 2015). Over the long term, this reduction in electricity consumption leads to substantial
operational savings, up to 82% relative to conventional air conditioning (Makai Ocean
Engineering 2008). However, the implementation of SWAC systems remains limited due to their
high capital costs and the technical and regulatory challenges associated with installing long
offshore intake pipelines in coastal environments.

To address the limitations associated with conventional SWAC systems, this study proposes a
novel adaptation: GeoSWAC. The GeoSWAC concept harnesses the virtually inexhaustible
energy of cold, deep ocean water, typically found at depths of around 1,000 m, for building
cooling. Rather than relying on long offshore pipelines and intensive pumping energy like
traditional SWAC systems, the GeoSWAC concept employs inland wells that are hydrologically
connected to deep seawater through permeable geologic formations (Figure 1b). This inland
configuration may eliminate the need for extensive marine infrastructure and offers a more
feasible solution in regions where offshore construction is technically, environmentally, or
economically challenging. Owing to the natural hydraulic pressure gradient, GeoSWAC systems
require pumping only to overcome the small hydraulic head difference between seawater and the
elevation of the end use. As a result, they provide a near continuous supply of cooling with only
minimal pumping energy. The concept leverages established drilling technologies that have been
developed over decades in the oil, gas, and geothermal industries, including directional drilling.
Although still in the early stages of conceptualization and development, GeoSWAC offers
streamlined system architecture, featuring minimal moving parts, no refrigerants, and
significantly lower electricity consumption compared to conventional vapor-compression
systems, which suggests strong potential for reliable and resilient operation in regions subject to
grid instability or extreme weather events.

1.2 San Juan Metropolitan Area
San Juan, the capital and most populous municipality of Puerto Rico, has a tropical rainforest
climate with persistently high temperatures and humidity throughout the year. Typical
meteorological year 3 (TMY3) data collected at the San Juan–Luis Muñoz Marín International
Airport from 1973 to 2005 show that ambient temperatures range from 20.6°C to 35°C (Figure
2) while relative humidity varies between 40% and 100% (Bianchi and Fontanini 2021). These
climatic conditions result in substantial and year-round cooling demands, making air
conditioning an essential and energy-intensive requirement for buildings across the region.

To meet cooling demand, UPR-RP, one of the largest universities in Puerto Rico, operates eight
chilled water plants consisting of electric-driven water-cooled vapor-compression chillers,
circulating pumps, and distribution loops. While the chilled water plants enable efficient
centralized cooling and operational coordination, their reliance on grid electricity emphasizes the
importance of enhancing energy resilience, particularly during extreme weather events that can
disrupt the power supply.

2 Geothermal Resource Assessment
2.1 Data Compilation—Subsurface
Between July and September 2024, the research team compiled more than 80 references relevant
to Puerto Rico’s data. The references were divided into two categories: subsurface and surface
datasets.

Regional geology and hydrogeology were primarily sourced from U.S. Geological Survey
(USGS) reports and scientific journals, which documented deep, medium, and shallow wells
across the north and south coasts. Detailed data were sparse in local publications. The team
assessed relevance in real time while reviewing references and excluded broader Caribbean
studies unless they contained data specific to Puerto Rico. The researchers conducted technical
visits to local universities and agencies in San Juan to gather additional information on local
studies that contained rock and soil properties.

2.1.1 Regional Subsurface Review
The island of Puerto Rico lies at the tectonically complex boundary between the Caribbean and
North American plates, forming part of the Puerto Rico–Hispaniola microplate. The tectonic
uplift associated with the subduction of the North American plate beneath the Caribbean plate
exposed the island’s arc basement (Van Gestel et al. 1999). The northern margin of the island is
bordered by the Puerto Rico Trench, which accommodates mainly left-lateral strike-slip motion
with a component of subduction (Hayes et al. 1986), while the Muertos thrust belt to the south
reflects compressional forces in the opposite direction (Van Gestel et al. 1999). Sedimentary
sequences composed of carbonate and terrigenous deposits accumulated in the northern forearc
basin from the Oligocene onward, following a long period of volcanic activity during the early
Cretaceous to mid-Miocene (Hayes et al. 1986). These sequences dip gently northward due to
regional Pliocene-Holocene tectonic tilting and tend to be thinner toward the eastern part of the
basin, where the San Juan structural arch limits their thickness to less than 100 m in some areas
(Renken et al. 2002).

The tectonic evolution can be summarized as follows: Phase 1 Cretaceous-Eocene: formation
and sedimentary infilling from a southern source. Phase 2 Oligocene-Pliocene: development of
the Puerto Rico–Virgin Islands platform (~1,600 m thick), mainly carbonate rocks, thickening
northward. Phase 3 Pliocene-Holocene: northward tilting of the Puerto Rico–Virgin Island
platform, submerging the northern edge to ~4 km depth and raising the southern edge to several
hundred meters above sea level (Figure 3).

The North Coast Province spans approximately 2,100 km² and is underlain by the North Coast
Limestone aquifer system, the most productive and extensive aquifer system in Puerto Rico. The
North Coast Limestone and South Coastal Alluvial Plain aquifer systems account for nearly 70%
of all groundwater withdrawals on the island. The North Coast Limestone aquifer system covers
roughly 2,000 km², with the most intensive groundwater development occurring within an 800
km² area between the Río Grande de Arecibo and the Río de la Plata. This region is of particular
concern for the use of groundwater, which serves as a critical source of water for both domestic
and industrial purposes. In 1985, groundwater withdrawals in this area were estimated at 280,000
cubic meters per day (m³/d), supplying 100% of the water used by self-supplied industries and
approximately 85% of public water supplies. By 2005, withdrawals had declined to 150,000
m³/d, reflecting changes in demand, resource availability, or water management practices
(Gómez-Gómez et al. 2014).

The North Coast Groundwater Province is divided into three limestone aquifer systems: (1) from
the West Coast to the Río Grande de Arecibo area, (2) from Río Grande de Arecibo to Río de la
Plata, and (3) from Río de la Plata to Río Espíritu Santo, the latter of which lies beneath San
Juan. Generalized geology and cross sections from the San Juan and Barceloneta areas show that
the surface is primarily composed of unconsolidated Quaternary alluvial and other surficial
deposits, overlying older Cretaceous-Tertiary limestone and terrigenous clastic rocks. Several
deep exploration wells were analyzed across the North Coast of Puerto Rico, including 15 North
Coast wells, an Interaquifer System well, hydrocarbon Commonwealth of Puerto Rico (CPR)
CPR-4 well, and the Toa Baja Scientific Drillhole (Figure 5).

Thermal studies, including the Toa Baja Scientific Drillhole analysis (Anderson and Larue,
1991), estimated heat flow between 23 and 37 milliwatts per square meter (mW/m²) from depths
of 800 to 2,500 m, with a peak of 90 mW/m² observed at a mineralized zone beneath a volcanic
sill or flow, before decreasing to 50 mW/m². Additionally, a thermal gradient of 18.5°C/km was
recorded in Eocene volcaniclastics at the base of CPR-4, located about 40 km west of San Juan.

2.1.2 Geology and Hydrogeology, San Juan Metropolitan Area
After millions of years of volcanic activity (early Cretaceous to mid-Miocene), layers of
sediments, mainly carbonates, formed in the northern basin during the Oligocene. These layers
later tilted northward due to more recent tectonics and thinned toward the east, where the San
Juan structural arch limited thickness to less than 100 m.

In the San Juan Metropolitan Area (SJMA), sedimentary units are thinner, more variable, and
more heavily influenced by structural and depositional transitions compared to the broader North
Coast basin. The area is predominantly covered by Quaternary surficial deposits, with limited
exposures of tertiary formations such as the Aymamón and Aguada limestones, which are more
intact further south near Guaynabo and Bayamón (Kaye 1959). The aquifer system in the SJMA
is distinct due to its geologic complexity, along with anthropogenic factors like urbanization and
historical groundwater use. The upper aquifer is often poorly developed due to erosion and
karstification and is typically brackish or saline (Anderson 1976). The middle confining unit,
consisting of clay-rich facies of the Cibao Formation, is locally leaky, allowing some vertical
water movement (Renken et al. 2002). The lower aquifer, primarily composed of the
Mucarabones Sand and lower Cibao Formation, offers variable but generally modest yields of
groundwater. Salinity increases eastward beyond Río Piedras, and recharge occurs mostly near
outcrop zones. The aquifer’s productivity is influenced by both lithological and structural
heterogeneity (Anderson 1976; Renken et al. 2002).

Much of the land surface of the SJMA is covered by younger deposits, whereas older limestone
formations appear more consistently farther south, near towns such as Guaynabo and Bayamón.
This stratigraphic variability, combined with extensive urban development and historical

 9

groundwater withdrawals, has resulted in aquifer systems with distinctive characteristics and
significant saltwater intrusion (Giusti 1978; Gómez-Gómez 1987; Rodríguez-Martínez 1995).

Surficial and Quaternary Deposits
The Quaternary deposits consist of alluvium, terrace deposits, blanket sands, beach sands, and
dune deposits. Their thickness varies considerably, reaching up to 300 feet (ft) within major
stream valleys but thinning to 100 ft or less elsewhere. These deposits can locally provide fresh
water, particularly in coastal areas east of San Juan, where surficial lenses of freshwater float
above denser salt water. However, these freshwater bodies are typically thin (≤100 ft), restricting
development to small domestic supplies (Gómez-Gómez 1987).

Stratigraphic Framework
 • Aymamón Limestone: In this area, the unit contains mostly salt water due to its
 proximity to the coast and influence from seawater intrusion (Gómez-Gómez 1987).
 • Aguada Limestone: In the SJMA, this formation contains a higher proportion of sand
 and clay compared to its occurrence in the western North Coast Province. It intercalates
 with chalky beds of the Cibao Formation and becomes indistinguishable from it about 0.5
 miles east of the Río de la Plata (~15 km west of San Juan).
 • Cibao Formation: Composed mainly of limestone, sand, and clay. In this area, it
 intercalates and grades laterally eastward with the Mucarabones Sand. Importantly, it is
 the principal freshwater-bearing unit in the SJMA, not because of high yields, but
 because it is the thickest unit (~200 ft) containing fresh water (Giusti 1978; Gómez-
       Gómez 1987).
 • Mucarabones Sand: Characterized by cross-bedded, grayish-orange to yellow, fine- to
 medium-grained sand. Near the base, it becomes coarser, containing gravel and lenses of
 greenish-gray sandy clay. The unit extends from Río Grande de Manatí to San Juan and
 reaches its maximum thickness (~300 ft) between Río de la Plata and Río Bayamón (~10
 km west of San Juan). Few wells draw exclusively from this unit, leaving its full
 freshwater supply potential uncertain (Rodríguez-Martínez 1995).

Hydrostratigraphic Units of the North Coast Limestone Aquifer System
Hydrogeologic investigations (Giusti 1978; Gómez-Gómez 1987; Rodríguez-Martínez 1995)
show that the SJMA forms part of the North Coast Limestone aquifer system, which consists of
three major hydrostratigraphic units:

 • Upper Aquifer: Composed primarily of the Aymamón Limestone and the Aguada
 Limestone and locally includes the upper Cibao Formation and surficial alluvium. It
 contains water under water-table conditions, except in coastal areas where fine-grained
 sediments locally confine the system. A saltwater zone underlies the freshwater zone in
 the coastal region. The Aymamón Limestone is the most permeable unit, with hydraulic
 conductivities ranging from 57 to 570 ft/d, although values diminish with depth. Minor
 water-bearing units in the SJMA show transmissivity values of 200–500 ft²/d.
 • Middle Confining Unit: Consists of low-permeability rocks, including the upper Cibao
 Formation, Quebrada Arenas Limestone, Río Indio Limestone, and mudstone. It
 generally restricts vertical flow, but test holes (NC-15 at 1,268 ft and NC-1 at 635 ft)

 10

 suggest it may be leaky, allowing water from the lower aquifer to move upward into the
 upper aquifer in the SJMA.
 • Lower Aquifer: Composed of the Lares Limestone, the Montebello Limestone Member
 of the Cibao Formation, and shallow facies of the Mucarabones and San Sebastián
 formations. It is thickest and most transmissive in the north-central part of the basin; its
 extent west of Río Grande de Arecibo is uncertain. The Montebello Limestone Member
 shows transmissivity values ranging from 625 to 3,600 ft²/d, with the highest values
 between Río Grande de Arecibo and Río Grande de Manatí (~30 km west of San Juan).
 Additionally, the Mucarabones Sand aquifer within the SJMA has estimated
 transmissivity values of 850–1,000 ft²/d, making it a significant but underutilized water-
 bearing unit (Gómez-Gómez 1987; Rodríguez-Martínez 1995).

Aquifer System Characteristics
The aquifer system in the SJMA is stratified and influenced by both geology and human activity.
The shallow aquifer is generally weakly developed, often eroded or dissolved by groundwater
circulation, and commonly contains brackish or saline water. A clay-rich layer below the shallow
aquifer serves as a semi-confining unit but allows some leakage between aquifer levels. The
deeper aquifer system, composed mainly of the Cibao Formation and Mucarabones Sand, yields
varying amounts of groundwater, although water quality decreases east of Río Piedras due to
increased salinity. Recharge occurs primarily where aquifer units crop out; however, urban
development has greatly restricted recharge in the SJMA (Giusti 1978; Gómez-Gómez 1987).

Groundwater Development and Use
Groundwater in the SJMA was among the first to be developed in Puerto Rico’s North Coast
Province. However, widespread saltwater intrusion has dramatically reduced its viability. Most
wells in the area have been abandoned or destroyed, particularly in central and eastern SJMA,
where intrusion is most severe. Limited groundwater use persists in the western SJMA, where
reduced withdrawals have slowed saltwater encroachment. The natural groundwater flow regime
has been significantly altered by urbanization, historical over-pumping, and the destruction of
multi-aquifer wells. Present groundwater withdrawals are modest, estimated at less than 10
million gallons per day (Gómez-Gómez 1987; Rodríguez-Martínez 1995).

In a comprehensive study of groundwater along Puerto Rico’s North Coast, including the SJMA,
Anderson (1976) analyzed the region’s geology, hydrology, and water quality chemistry. One
key observation was that groundwater became progressively less mineralized moving inland
from the coast. Higher median concentrations of dissolved solids, hardness, and bicarbonate
were found in the Aguada and Aymamón limestones, which are closest to the sea. In contrast,
lower concentrations were observed in the San Sebastián and Cibao formations, located farther
inland. Additionally, unconfined aquifers near the coast, particularly in the SJMA, were
identified as vulnerable to seawater intrusion, with evidence or potential signs of encroachment
in coastal areas (Figure 7).

2.2 Geothermal Conceptual Model
This section examines the subsurface conditions of the SJMA by developing a 3D geologic
model alongside a geothermal conceptual model, utilizing a broad range of published data and
literature. The 3D model was employed to evaluate potential geothermal cooling scenarios for
the UPR-RP campus.

The modeling process began with the collection and organization of data, following
methodologies established in similar regional studies (Cumming 2009). Insights from previous

specialized 3D modeling software package, Leapfrog Geo2, to construct the initial geologic
framework. The overall project workflow, from literature review to data collection and model
construction, is summarized in Figure 8.

This model was subsequently refined by integrating additional geologic information related to
various units. This comprehensive approach facilitated the identification of promising sites for
detailed investigation, assessing the geologic suitability of preselected locations, and the
initiation of techno-economic analyses for specific geothermal cooling technologies.

Modeling progressed stratigraphically, starting from the oldest (deepest) geologic unit and
building upward. Contacts between units were digitized from cross sections, contour maps, and
geologic maps. Then contacts were used to automatically generate surface boundaries. Once a
surface was created, the corresponding 3D volume for that geologic unit was created, either

above or below the surface depending on the unit’s classification (e.g., deposit, erosion, or
intrusion).

All geologic units were modeled as deposits, apart from surficial deposits, which were modeled
as erosional features to allow them to cut into the underlying stratigraphy to better reflect their
geomorphic character. After generating each surface and corresponding volume, a basic quality
control process was applied using criteria as the fits with the input data and geologic response.
As part of the model quality control, adjustments were made either by modifying software
parameters or through minor manual editing of the surfaces.

The finalized 3D geologic model, along with accompanying hydrogeologic property maps,
provides a comprehensive synthesis of the available geologic and hydrological data for the
SJMA. While simplified, the model offers a representative visualization of the region’s
subsurface geology and supports the interpretation of its geologic evolution. At the base of the
model lie highly deformed, undifferentiated volcaniclastic and igneous basement rocks, formed
in a subduction-related volcanic arc. These basement units represent the island’s geologic core.
After the cessation of volcanic activity, thick sequences of siliciclastic and carbonate sediments
were deposited in shallow marine environments along Puerto Rico’s northern and southern
coasts. Subsequent northward tilting uplifted these sedimentary units above sea level along the
North Coast, resulting in a gentle dip of approximately 4°–5° to the north and an overall east-
west regional strike. Figure 10 shows an oblique, southwest-facing view of the complete 3D
model, illustrating the spatial relationships among the major geologic units.
Hydrogeologically, the SJMA features are both confined; these units are overlain by the clay-rich
Cibao Formation, which acts as an effective confining layer. At the surface, surficial deposits
form the uppermost 10–30 m of the subsurface. These deposits exhibit high spatial variability in
both composition and water-bearing capacity across the SJMA, influencing groundwater
recharge and flow dynamics.

2.2.2 Local Geothermal Conceptual Cross-Section
The thermal properties of Puerto Rico have been investigated using both global and site-specific
datasets, providing insights into geothermal gradients across the island. Li et al. (2017)
developed a global Curie-point depth model through a robust inversion algorithm of magnetic
anomaly data based on fractal magnetization, with the dataset made available upon request to the
authors.3

Using the Curie-point depth method and the global Curie-point depth EMAG2 database (Li et al.
2017), researchers estimated a regional geothermal gradient of 30°C/km–32°C/km for Puerto
Rico. However, local well data indicates lower gradients. Based on geophysical logs from the
Toa Baja well, Anderson (1991) interpreted that reburial and deposition of Oligocene-Miocene
limestones produced a present-day gradient of about 15°C/km, corresponding to a heat flow of

3
 https://www.nature.com/articles/srep45129#Sec12

 17

30–50 mW/m². Similarly, direct measurements from well CPR-4 reported a geothermal gradient
of 18.5°C/km (Anderson 1991). Together, these findings suggest that while regional models
point to elevated geothermal potential, localized lithological and depositional processes and
groundwater effects may have resulted in lower gradients in certain areas of Puerto Rico. As a
reference for geothermal gradient anomalies in island settings, Wilmarth et al. (2017) reported
that geothermal gradients on Tutuila Island are lower than expected for a young volcanic
province. They suggested this may result from several factors: the lack of significant magma
storage in the shallow crust, the insulating effect of more than 3,000 m of Pleistocene and older
shield-volcano basalts that make up much of the island, and the island’s proximity to the near-
freezing abyssal plains of the Pacific Ocean only a few kilometers offshore. Together, these
conditions help maintain unusually low subsurface temperatures.

The geothermal energy investigation of Puerto Rico has been guided by the integration of
temperature data with regional geologic and hydrostratigraphic characteristics. Using geothermal
gradient estimates derived from Curie-point depth modeling (Li et al. 2017; Lin et al. 2017) and
local well measurements (Anderson 1991), combined with a detailed 3D geologic model of the
SJMA, researchers developed a conceptual cross section with isotherms extending from south to
north near UPR-RP (Figure 11). This conceptual model incorporates the stratigraphic framework
of the SJMA, where Quaternary deposits, Oligocene-Miocene limestones, and deeper units such
as the Cibao Formation and Mucarabones Sand define a structurally complex aquifer system
influenced by tectonic tilting, karstification, and saltwater intrusion. The model emphasizes the
interplay of heat flow with hydrostratigraphic units, including the shallow surficial aquifer, the
semi-confining clay-rich Cibao facies, and the deeper Mucarabones Sand and lower aquifer
system, which together control groundwater movement and thermal distribution. As in Wilmarth
(2017) where conceptual cross sections were used to visualize geothermal system components,
our Puerto Rico model highlights the influence of lithology and groundwater flow on spatial
distribution. While subsurface features such as fault-controlled permeability pathways and the
lateral extent of aquifers remain uncertain, the integration of thermal gradients and geologic
structure provides a framework for assessing the potential for cooling geothermal resources in
the SJMA.

One of the key wellbores in the hydrogeologic investigation within the SJMA was the drilling of
test hole NC-3 in 1986 located on the UPR-RP campus (Figure 5a, Figure 12). This well reached
a depth of 375 feet (114.3 m) and encountered, from top to bottom, surficial Quaternary deposits,
the Cibao Formation, the Mucarabones Sand, and undifferentiated volcanic basement rocks of
early Tertiary or Late Cretaceous age (Rodríguez-Martínez et al. 1991). The Cibao Formation
displayed significant heterogeneity, including packstones, claystones, and quartz-rich sandstones
with fossiliferous components (Rodríguez-Martínez et al. 1991). The Mucarabones Sand,
consisting of conglomeratic quartz sandstone and claystone, reflects the fluvial-deltaic
sedimentation that once dominated this area during the early Miocene (Monroe 1980; Scharlach
1990). Seven artesian zones were identified within NC-3, located across the surficial deposits,
Cibao Formation, and Mucarabones Sand, producing brackish water with specific conductance
between 610 and 900 microsiemens per centimeter. Yields ranged from 20 to 90 gallons per
minute (GPM), with the most productive intervals in the mid-depth Cibao Formation
(Rodríguez-Martínez et al. 1991). The measured water temperature in the well was 76°F
(24.5°C). These results emphasize the hydrogeologic importance of the Mucarabones Sand and
Cibao Formation as confined, artesian aquifers in the structurally uplifted eastern portion of the
North Coast basin.

During the development of the conceptual geothermal model, several data gaps were identified
that limit the accuracy and reliability of the assessment. Most notably, there is a lack of local
hydraulic properties of the lower aquifer and deeper (>500 m) such as permeability, porosity, and
transmissivity along with groundwater flow rate data. Islandic deep subsurface studies (Wilmarth
et al. 2017) indicate that although tropical oceans exhibit warm surface temperatures, the global
thermocline declines steeply beneath the shallow epipelagic zone, reaching <10°C at
approximately 500 m depth and <5°C by 1,000 m. Consequently, an effectively limitless
reservoir of very cold, highly permeable seawater can envelop the submerged volcanic edifices
of deep-sea islands and extend beneath the freshwater lens onshore. Addressing these data gaps
with advanced exploration campaigns would be essential for refining the model and increasing
confidence in future analyses and decision-making.

3 Infrastructure Analysis
3.1 Data Compilation – Surface
The surface data search targeted databases containing ambient conditions, energy demand and
cost data, and infrastructure characteristics. Most data originated from government agency
compilations, public web resources, and select journal articles. However, detailed local
information on energy demand was limited.

During a site visit to Puerto Rico, researchers met with multiple agencies, universities, and
facilities management teams. The team at UPR-RP provided comprehensive data on building-
level energy demand and existing system configurations. The modeling conducted in this study is
based on those configurations.

3.2 System Configurations and Building Characteristics
3.2.1 Central Chilled Water Plant at the University of Puerto Rico at Río Piedras
The UPR-RP campus has one of the largest heating, ventilating, and air-conditioning systems in
Puerto Rico. Almost 7,430 tons of refrigeration (26 megawatts thermal) are required to provide
air conditioning to the facilities. An increment of 3,000 tons for new buildings has been
estimated.

UPR-RP operates eight chilled water plants across campus, including a central chilled water
plant, with a combined maximum installed cooling capacity of approximately 10,600 tons of
refrigeration. The central plant distributes chilled water to about 30 buildings via three main
distribution lines that serve the Facundo Bueso building, general academic buildings, and the
Sports Complex (Figure 13). The plant includes key mechanical components, including water-
cooled vapor-compression chillers, evaporative cooling towers, circulation pumps, chemical
treatment systems, and supply and return piping loops, thus forming a distribution system for
campus-wide space cooling.

The campus building stock served by the central plant is functionally diverse, ranging from
general-purpose academic buildings to specialized facilities, such as medical buildings, theaters,
and athletic complexes. Each building has distinct operational schedules, internal load patterns,
and cooling requirements, resulting in highly variable and often complex load profiles. This

3.3 Building Energy Modeling
To address the data gap and support techno-economic analysis, this study employed a
simulation-based approach to generate time-resolved cooling load profiles. Given the limited
availability of detailed data on occupancy, internal loads, and envelope characteristics, a
prototype college building model developed by Oak Ridge National Laboratory (Bae et al. 2022)
was used with EnergyPlus®, a whole-building energy simulation tool, rather than developing a
custom model for a specific campus building. The prototype model representing typical
characteristics of U.S. higher education buildings as classified in the 2012 Commercial Buildings
Energy Consumption Survey (U.S. Energy Information Administration 2016), includes a
functional mix of spaces like classrooms, laboratories, faculty offices, and support areas, each
with distinct internal loads and occupancy schedules. While the original prototype complied with
ASHRAE Standard 90.1, including mass wall construction, built-up roofing, and metal-framed
windows (Bae et al. 2022), this study modified the building envelope to reflect pre-1980
construction standards (Deru et al. 2011), better matching the age and thermal characteristics of
the UPR-RP campus. The heating, ventilating, and air-conditioning system was modeled as a
variable air volume system served by a central gas boiler and electrically-driven water-cooled
vapor-compression chillers, aligned with the existing chilled water infrastructure at UPR-RP. For
climate input, TMY3 data at San Juan International Airport in San Juan (World Meteorological
Organization # 785260) were used to represent local climatic conditions. These modifications
and modeling inputs establish a realistic basis for generating hourly cooling load profiles for a
representative campus building, which are used in the subsequent techno-economic analysis.

Figure 14a shows the modeled hourly cooling loads generated for the prototype college building
over the first 30 days of the year, and Figure 14b presents daily aggregated loads across the full
annual simulation period. The hourly load profile showed strong weekday-weekend variation as
well as strong diurnal variation. On weekdays (Monday through Friday), hourly peak loads
reached approximately 1,200 kW during daytime, corresponding to active classroom and
laboratory use. In contrast, weekend daytime hourly peaks were significantly lower, around 400
kW, reflecting reduced occupancy and minimal instructional activity. Similarly, the daily cooling
energy profile in Figure 14b showed seasonal variability alongside the weekday-weekend trends.
Despite a constant cooling set point throughout the simulation, the annual trend in cooling
demand followed a similar pattern to the ambient temperature profile shown in Figure 14b. This
pattern implies that ambient temperature contributed to seasonal variation, as maintaining a fixed
indoor temperature required more energy on hotter days. However, the relatively small variation
in moving average temperature (approximately 2°C–3°C) indicates that occupancy-driven
internal heat played a more significant role. Therefore, the seasonal patterns in cooling demand

 22

were more closely tied to academic schedules and space usage. During the spring months
(February and March), maximum daily cooling energy was slightly lower, around 12–13 MWh,
likely due to milder temperatures and reduced cooling demand. From April through mid-June,
maximum daily cooling energy increased to approximately 16–17 MWh, and during the summer,
maximum daily cooling energy slightly decreased to around 15 MWh. The maximum daily
cooling energy rose again to 16–17 MWh during the fall semester from September through mid-
December. These patterns align with occupancy schedules developed by Bae et al. (2022), based
on actual class reservation data collected from multiple U.S. universities, representing typical
academic-year operations with reduced activity during weekends and summer months.

To align the simulated cooling load profile with actual campus infrastructure, the Facundo Bueso
building was selected as a representative case study. Functionally similar to the prototype model
(e.g., classroom, laboratory, and administrative spaces), the modeled load profile provides a
reasonable basis for scaling. In addition to the functional similarity, the prototype building’s
maximum instantaneous cooling load was approximately 341.3 tons (1,200 kW), closely
matching the Facundo Bueso building’s design maximum capacity of 350 tons and average
operational load of 223.8 tons, obtained from the site visit. Using a scaling factor of 1.03, the
prototype load profile was adjusted to reflect Facundo Bueso’s load magnitude. To evaluate
system performance and cost-effectiveness at a district scale, the adjusted profile was further
multiplied by four, representing a cluster of similar buildings (Figure 14c). This approach offers
a practical and scalable method for comparing the existing chilled water plant with the proposed
GeoSWAC concept under realistic, aggregated demand conditions.

4.1 Central Chilled Water Plant
This section presents a preliminary assessment of the capital and operational costs, along with
the LCOC, for the central chilled water system at UPR-RP. The analysis boundary encompassed
chiller units, cooling towers, circulating pumps, and connecting piping between chiller units and
cooling towers, as well as between chillers and the campus loop. Capital and operational costs
associated with the broader distribution network and in-building systems (e.g., air-handling
units) were excluded to reduce uncertainty and maintain focus on core system components.
These air-side components were assumed to remain unchanged across both the chiller-based and
GeoSWAC scenarios and thus do not affect the comparative assessment of the central chilled
water system.

4.1.1 Capital Cost
Capital cost estimates for the key components were developed using the EnergyPlus® modeling
results (scaled to represent campus-level loads) and cost correlations obtained from the literature,
market research, and the information collected from the site visit:

 • Chiller Units: Two cost scenarios were evaluated for the range of installed chiller costs
 based on data collected from the site visit: $1,071/ton and $1,686/ton. For the modeled
 peak cooling load of 1,400 tons (4.9 MW in Figure 14c), the estimated total capital costs
 for chiller units ranged from approximately $1.5 million to $2.36 million.
 • Circulating Pumps: Capital cost for pumps was estimated using a correlation of $1,000
 per kW of pump power (Oh et al. 2025b). The required pump capacity, based on the
 modeled peak power consumption of 52.7 kW, resulted in a total capital cost of $52,700.
 • Cooling Towers: A cost of $300/ton was used based on current market estimates for
 water-cooled cooling towers with similar thermal and flow specifications of a case study
 (P2S Engineering 2011). For a maximum rejected thermal load of 1,646.4 tons through
 cooling towers, the capital cost of the cooling tower was estimated at $493,920.
 • Piping Infrastructure: Piping costs were estimated using reference values reported in
 the Los Angeles Valley College Central Plant case study (P2S Engineering 2011). For a
 representative 500-ft length of 18-inch-diameter pipe (sized to accommodate flow rates
 exceeding 5,250 GPM while maintaining velocity below 2.5 m/s), the installed cost was
 estimated at $699/ft, resulting in a total piping cost of $349,500. This estimate includes
 labor, material, and insulation.
 • Initial Water Fill: Initial water cost was estimated based on the cooling towers’ peak
 flow rate (4,083 GPM). Assuming a conservative fill duration of 5 hours, representing the
 time required to fully charge the loop volume at peak flow, the total water volume was
 estimated at approximately 1.22 million gallons (4,637 m³). Using the bulk water rate of
 $3.56/m³ set by the Puerto Rico Aqueduct and Sewer Authority, the total one-time capital
 water cost was estimated at approximately $16,507.

The estimated capital and operational costs were used to calculate the LCOC, a standardized

4.2 GeoSWAC System
Although the NC-3 borehole drilled on the UPR-RP campus encountered undifferentiated
igneous basement at depths of 300–375 ft (91–114 m), this classification, common for Upper
Cretaceous to Lower Tertiary basement units in northeastern Puerto Rico, does not preclude the
presence of interbedded sedimentary formations such as limestone or sandstone. For instance,
Lower Tertiary formations in other regions of the island, including the Cibao and San Sebastián
formations, contain significant carbonate sequences. Given this regional geologic variability, and
in the absence of deep site-specific data, it would be unjustified to assume a uniformly crystalline
or impermeable basement at depths approaching 1 km beneath the SJMA.

Based on this broader geologic context, the preliminary GeoSWAC assessment in this study
assumes the potential presence of permeable sedimentary units, such as sandstone or fractured
limestone, at the target depth of 1 km. While this preliminary study assumed an idealized case in
which a vertical well intersects a permeable, cold-water-bearing formation at that depth, the
subsurface isotherms shown in Figure 11 suggest that angled or directional drilling may be
required to reach the target temperatures. These discrepancies reflect substantial uncertainty in
the subsurface temperature structure, which would need to be confirmed or revised through
additional exploratory geoscientific investigations. The requirement for vertical versus
directional drilling ultimately depends on local geologic structure, bathymetry, and the hydraulic
connection between the inland well and deep cold seawater. The overall viability of this concept

 28

hinges on establishing hydraulic connectivity to cold seawater in the adjacent ocean basin at
depth. Site-specific investigations, including deep exploratory geophysical surveys and drilling,
will be necessary to verify subsurface conditions and assess the feasibility of long-term
GeoSWAC operation. For this preliminary analysis, however, economic metrics were estimated
under the simplifying assumption that hydraulically connected, permeable formations containing
4°C–5°C water exist at a depth of roughly 1 km, regardless of the schematic isotherms in Figure
11.

4.2.1 Economic Metrics
The conceptual GeoSWAC system consists of heat exchangers, circulating pumps, and surface
piping connected to the campus distribution loop. Instead of chillers and cooling towers in the
central chilled water plant, GeoSWAC relies on two inland wells: a deep production well (~1
km) to access cold water and a shallower injection well (~200 m) for discharge. This
asymmetrical well configuration generally follows the well-established SWAC systems, where
cold seawater is extracted from great depths and discharged at shallower levels to reduce
infrastructure costs and environmental impact (Figure 1a). This approach also helps lower capital
cost by reducing deep drilling requirements and simplifies subsurface engineering in this
preliminary GeoSWAC design. Meanwhile, this configuration raises important questions, such
as maintaining hydraulic balance, managing thermal dispersion, and evaluating potential thermal
plume migration, especially in heterogeneous coastal aquifers. These subsurface dynamics need
to be resolved through comprehensive future hydrogeologic modeling; this study presents a best-
case conceptual scenario. In this scenario, it was assumed that 4°C cold water is continuously
available at a depth of approximately 1 km, based on a simplified hydraulic equilibrium model in
which the inland production well is statically connected to seawater at or near sea level. This
connection implies that cold water at depth is under sufficient hydrostatic pressure to rise within
the well column (the production well), enabling pumping from a shallower depth, assumed in
this study as 100 m, instead of lifting cold water from the full 1 km. This assumption
significantly reduces energy demand for pumping and associated operational costs. In addition,
the water is assumed to have sufficiently low total dissolved solids that no additional filtering or
pre-treatment is required before circulation through the heat exchangers (i.e., no water
conditioning cost). This assumption is intended for first-order techno-economic evaluation and
does not represent a site-specific water chemistry determination. Groundwater total dissolved
solids, salinity, and fouling potential may vary spatially and would need to be characterized in
detail for site-specific feasibility and system design. As the water is circulated through heat
exchangers and returned to the subsurface without net water consumption, water costs do not
factor into the capital or operational estimates. Instead, water savings associated with the avoided
evaporative losses in conventional cooling towers were incorporated as revenue.

4.2.4 Levelized Cost of Cooling
The LCOC of GeoSWAC with the two configurations was calculated using the following
parameters: a 20-year system lifetime, 5% discount rate, and an annual cooling energy output of
19.1 GWh. Capital costs were estimated at $2.1 million for the single-doublet system and $2.9
million for the two-doublet system (Table 3). Annual operational costs were $52,702 and
$26,361, respectively, which were significantly lower than $390,946 for the chiller-based
scenario. The resulting LCOC values for GeoSWAC were $11.1/MWh (single) and $13.1/MWh
(double), GeoSWAC achieved a lower LCOC than chiller-based systems ($30.2/MWh compared
to $33.6/MWh), primarily due to much lower electricity consumption and the elimination of
water losses. While the capital cost for GeoSWAC was 11%–14% lower than that of chiller-
based systems, the operational cost of GeoSWAC was approximately 90% lower on average,
resulting in an overall LCOC reduction of 62%. When the avoided cooling tower water cost of
$241,193 per year is considered as revenue, the net operational cost becomes negative
(−$188,492 and −$214,832, respectively), and the LCOC drops below $0.50/MWh. These results
highlight the strong economic potential of GeoSWAC, even without considering revenue
benefits. However, further analysis is required to address uncertainties related to subsurface
connectivity, long-term performance, and site-specific feasibility. For example, Seawater can
induce corrosion, biofouling, and scaling, due to its elevated chloride and sulfate content,
dissolved oxygen, and biological activity, which collectively accelerate material degradation and
mineral deposition. The GeoSWAC system was assumed in this preliminary analysis to employ
corrosion- and fouling-resistant materials, such as high-density polyethylene, fiber-reinforced
polymer, or coated-steel piping, and titanium or coated-steel heat exchangers, ensuring long-term
reliability.

Conclusions
The GeoSWAC concept benefits from two key assumptions: 1) access to an effectively infinite
heat sink provided by deep seawater, and 2) a favorable natural hydraulic gradient, which
enables cold seawater to rise to sea level with minimal pumping. Pumping is only required from
sea level to the building infrastructure, further reducing energy demand. Functionally similar to
conventional SWAC, GeoSWAC reconfigures the system vertically using inland wells instead of
horizontal seabed pipelines. This design shifts constraints from ocean bathymetry to subsurface
hydraulic connectivity offering potential advantages such as greater geographic flexibility and
reduced sensitivity to weather during construction. However, these benefits come with
uncertainties, particularly related to subsurface conditions and potential flow rate limitations due
to borehole diameter constraints. Future research should focus on validating key assumptions
through site-specific performance modeling, geological surveys, and feasibility-level techno-
economic analysis to quantify LCOC sensitivity to these variables.

This study demonstrates the potential of the GeoSWAC system as a resilient, economic, and
energy-efficient alternative to electricity-intensive chiller-based cooling at a tropical, coastal
university campus in Puerto Rico, based on a set of simplifying assumptions and a conceptual
analysis. GeoSWAC is a concept to produce cooling through inland wells in a permeable
formation hydraulically connected to deep, cold seawater.

While in-depth modeling of the hydraulic connection between inland wells and deep, cold
seawater in the GeoSWAC system remains for future study, economic metrics were estimated
and compared with those of a water-cooled chiller system at the campus.

GeoSWAC achieved substantially lower operational costs (up to 93.3% compared to the chiller-
based system) and lower LCOC (up to 63.2% compared to the chiller-based system), driven by
reduced electricity consumption and elimination of evaporative and blowdown water losses.
Although GeoSWAC still requires electricity for pumping, its lower energy demand enhances
system resilience, as the system can be sustained for longer periods with backup power sources,
such as diesel generators or battery systems, offering continued cooling capacity during grid
outages. The economic assessment of GeoSWAC systems relative to conventional chiller-based
systems showed substantial reductions in both capital (10.6% to 13.6%) and operational costs
(86.5% to 93.3%). These results indicate the economic potential of GeoSWAC technology based
on initial assumptions and conceptual analysis, while acknowledging limitations such as
operational and maintenance costs related to water chemistry effects, scaling, or corrosion and
site-specific constraints, which should be addressed in future validation studies.

The GeoSWAC concept benefits from two key assumptions: (1) access to an effectively infinite
heat sink provided by deep seawater and (2) a favorable natural hydraulic gradient, which
enables cold seawater to rise to sea level with minimal pumping. Pumping is only required from
sea level to the building infrastructure, further reducing energy demand. Functionally similar to
conventional SWAC, GeoSWAC reconfigures the system vertically (or with greater geometric
freedom using directional drilling techniques) using inland wells instead of seabed pipelines.
This design shifts constraints from ocean bathymetry to subsurface hydraulic connectivity
offering potential advantages such as greater geographic flexibility and reduced sensitivity to
weather during construction.

 32

However, these benefits come with uncertainties, particularly related to subsurface conditions
and potential flow rate limitations due to borehole diameter constraints. Overall, GeoSWAC
represents a promising cooling solution for regions prone to power disruptions, where long-term
resilience, water conservation, and reduced dependence on electricity are critical. Continued
exploration of site feasibility, geologic suitability, and operational performance will be essential
to unlocking its full potential.

Future research could focus on validating key assumptions through site-specific performance
modeling, deep geologic studies (e.g., exploratory drilling), and feasibility-level techno-
economic analysis to quantify LCOC sensitivity to these variables. Research addressing these
data gaps with advance exploration campaigns will be essential for refining the model and
increasing confidence in future analyses and decision-making.
