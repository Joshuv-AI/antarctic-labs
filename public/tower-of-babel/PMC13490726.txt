Resect first in resectable colorectal liver metastases: Recalibrating the default
Authors: Rathin Gosavi, Mithra Sritharan, Stephen Bell, Paul McMurrick, William Teoh, Vignesh Narasimhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Perioperative chemotherapy is frequently used for resectable colorectal liver metastases (CRLM), despite persistent uncertainty regarding the extent of its survival benefit. This perspective revisits the evidence supporting this practice and asks whether upfront liver-directed treatment should be more strongly considered in selected patients. A central paradox underpins this debate: although adjuvant chemotherapy improves OS in stage III colon cancer, randomized trials in resectable CRLM have consistently failed to demonstrate an OS advantage for perioperative or adjuvant systemic therapy. Instead, chemotherapy primarily appears to delay recurrence while exposing patients to potential harms. These harms include treatment-related hepatic injury, increased perioperative morbidity, and, in some cases, progression during induction therapy that may preclude curative resection. Several explanations may account for this discrepancy, including clonal selection of resistant disease, the influence of the hepatic metastatic microenvironment, and the availability of salvage local therapy at recurrence. Nevertheless, systemic therapy remains relevant in selected patients with technically or biologically complex disease, such as those requiring staged liver surgery, downsizing for resectability, or treatment sequencing in synchronous primary and liver disease. Additionally, thermal ablation is emerging as a valid early liver-directed option for carefully selected small-volume metastases. Looking forward, circulating tumor DNA and other molecular tools may better identify the subgroup most likely to benefit from perioperative treatment. Treatment sequencing in resectable CRLM should therefore be guided by anatomy, tumour biology, and emerging molecular markers of residual risk, with systemic therapy used selectively rather than as a universal default.

INTRODUCTION

Liver metastases represent the most common site of distant spread in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer [1]. Among patients with liver-limited disease, surgical resection offers the only potential for cure, with 5-year overall survival (OS) approaching 40%–60% [1]. Despite this, variation in management persists, particularly regarding the sequencing of systemic therapy and surgery for patients with resectable colorectal liver metastases (CRLM). Systemic chemotherapy is often administered routinely before surgery, reflecting an entrenched paradigm that metastatic disease mandates upfront systemic therapy. This approach is frequently justified on theoretical grounds, including its potential to address micrometastatic disease, provide indirect information about tumour behaviour and biology, and facilitate parenchymal-sparing resection through downsizing.

However, Level I evidence does not support the routine use of neoadjuvant or perioperative chemotherapy in patients with resectable CRLM [1]. Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) [2-4] demonstrate no OS advantage and identify potential harms associated with chemotherapy-first strategies. These harms include treatment-related hepatic injury, increased perioperative morbidity, and the risk of disease progression during induction therapy, which may preclude resection. A striking paradox emerges when these data are considered alongside outcomes in non-metastatic disease. In stage III colon cancer, adjuvant fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy with oxaliplatin confers a clear OS benefit, with absolute gains varying by regimen and risk group [5,6].

By contrast, in stage IV disease rendered resectable through surgery, the addition of systemic therapy has consistently failed to improve OS despite reducing the risk of recurrence.

Contemporary guidelines [7,8] therefore support both surgery alone and perioperative chemotherapy in selected patients. Treatment sequencing is shaped by disease burden, synchrony, molecular risk, technical complexity, and multidisciplinary judgment. Against this background, we revisit the evidence underpinning routine induction chemotherapy in resectable CRLM and ask whether upfront, liver-directed treatment may be underused in selected low-risk patients.

RANDOMISED EVIDENCE: NO SURVIVAL BENEFIT FOR ROUTINE INDUCTION THERAPY

Three RCTs inform current practice in the perioperative management of resectable CRLM. The EORTC 40983 (EPOC) trial [2] randomized 364 patients with one to four resectable liver metastases to either surgery alone or perioperative FOLFOX (six cycles pre- and post-operatively).

While perioperative chemotherapy was associated with a modest improvement in progression-free survival (PFS) (hazard ratio [HR] 0.77, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.60–1.00; p = 0.041), OS was similar between groups (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.68–1.14; p = 0.34). Notably, perioperative chemotherapy was associated with a higher rate of postoperative complications compared with surgery alone (25% vs. 16%, p = 0.04).

The New EPOC trial [3] evaluated the addition of cetuximab to perioperative chemotherapy in patients with KRAS wild-type, resectable CRLM. Surprisingly, the inclusion of cetuximab resulted in significantly worse PFS and OS, leading to the recommendation that epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitors should not be used in the perioperative setting for resectable disease. This trial highlighted the risk of extrapolating biologic efficacy from the metastatic setting to the curative-intent perioperative context without robust supporting data.

The JCOG0603 trial [4] examined the role of adjuvant chemotherapy after curative-intent hepatectomy in patients with limited CRLM. Participants were randomised to adjuvant mFOLFOX6 vs. surgery alone. Although disease-free survival improved with chemotherapy, there was no corresponding OS benefit, (71% vs. 83%, p > 0.05). This raises concerns about overtreatment in biologically indolent disease. Across these trials, perioperative or adjuvant chemotherapy appears to delay recurrence without clearly prolonging survival. These foundational studies have limited applicability to contemporary practice. They were conducted in earlier eras of systemic therapy, enrolled heterogeneous populations, and were not designed to define subgroups based on synchrony, tumor burden, or modern molecular risk. More recent pooled individual patient data suggest that chemotherapy’s effect may not be uniform across all patients with resectable CRLM. While a modest OS benefit exists, it is more pronounced in cases of synchronous metastases [9]. Therefore, the randomized evidence may argue less against chemotherapy itself than against its routine, undifferentiated use.

OBSERVATIONAL DATA AND PROGRESSION RISK

High-quality observational studies, including multicentre cohort analyses and propensity-matched comparisons, broadly support the randomised data in suggesting that routine neoadjuvant chemotherapy does not clearly improve long-term outcomes in patients with clearly resectable disease [10-13]. However, these comparisons are inherently vulnerable to selection bias, as patients selected for upfront surgery often differ from those selected for preoperative chemotherapy in disease burden, synchrony, biology, and technical complexity. Several studies have also reported higher perioperative morbidity following preoperative chemotherapy, reflecting treatment-related hepatic injury [14-16].

A key concern is the potential for disease progression during systemic therapy. Between 5% and 10% of patients with technically resectable CRLM may experience progression while on induction chemotherapy, thereby losing the window for curative resection [17]. This risk, while numerically modest, is clinically significant and undermines a central goal of early-stage metastatic management: to deliver potentially curative interventions when available. Contemporary meta-analyses [18] comparing neoadjuvant chemotherapy with upfront surgery in resectable CRLM similarly demonstrate no OS advantage for chemotherapy-first strategies, reinforcing the conclusions drawn from randomised trials.

CHEMOTHERAPY-ASSOCIATED HEPATIC INJURY

Oxaliplatin and irinotecan, backbone agents in metastatic colorectal cancer, are associated with clinically relevant hepatotoxicity. Oxaliplatin can induce sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, which increases intraoperative bleeding risk and postoperative morbidity. Irinotecan, on the other hand, has been linked to steatohepatitis and, in some series, higher postoperative mortality, particularly in patients with obesity or diabetes [15,19].

These injuries are cumulative and may be particularly consequential in major hepatectomy or staged resections. Bevacizumab may attenuate sinusoidal injury, but it introduces wound-healing concerns and may delay surgery [16]. Chemotherapy may also distort radiological appearances; sinusoidal injury and pseudolesions can complicate the assessment of disease extent and operative planning [20].

WHY DOES CHEMOTHERAPY FAIL TO IMPROVE OVERALL SURVIVAL IN RESECTABLE CRLM?

The absence of an OS benefit is likely multifactorial. One plausible explanation is that perioperative chemotherapy suppresses chemosensitive disease and delays recurrence without eradicating resistant subclones. These clones may later re-expand, driving relapse. Tumour heterogeneity and clonal evolution are well recognised in metastatic colorectal cancer and may limit the curative impact of early systemic therapy [21]. A second possibility is that once tumor cells seed the liver, they enter a distinct organ microenvironment characterized by immune tolerance and metastatic niche support. This environment could render residual disease less responsive to cytotoxic therapy [22]. These mechanisms may help explain why recurrence-free endpoints improve while OS does not [23]. In addition, recurrence after hepatectomy frequently includes a liver-limited component, and a meaningful proportion of patients remain candidates for salvage local therapy, which may further attenuate any OS separation between upfront strategies [24,25].

SELECTIVE INDICATIONS FOR NEOADJUVANT THERAPY

Systemic therapy remains relevant in selected patients with technically or biologically complex resectable CRLM. Patients best suited for upfront liver-directed therapy are those with low-volume, clearly resectable, liver-limited disease, an adequate future liver remnant, and no major adverse biological features. Preoperative systemic therapy remains a reasonable option for patients with a marginal future liver remnant, bilobar disease requiring staged or two-stage hepatectomy, or lesions abutting major vascular structures where downsizing may facilitate a safer or parenchymal-sparing approach. It is also suitable for patients with biologically higher-risk disease, characterized by a high tumour burden, short disease-free interval, elevated carcinoembryonic antigen, node-positive primary disease, or adverse molecular features such as RAS or BRAF mutations [26,27]. In these settings, chemotherapy may help preserve resectability, provide interval disease control, and inform subsequent multidisciplinary planning. For selected patients with small-volume disease, particularly lesions ≤ 3 cm, early liver-directed treatment may also include thermal ablation. The recent COLLISION trial [28] suggests a high likelihood of non-inferior OS and local control with fewer adverse events than resection, and large contemporary surgical microwave ablation series [29] support the feasibility of a predominantly laparoscopic approach with a median hospital stay of 1 day. Outside these scenarios, however, current evidence does not support routine preoperative systemic therapy in clearly resectable disease, and it may expose patients to unnecessary harm.

SYNCHRONOUS PRIMARY AND LIVER DISEASE: WHEN SEQUENCING MATTERS

In patients with synchronous colorectal primary and resectable liver metastases, the question is often not whether chemotherapy should precede surgery, but how best to sequence the operative strategy [30]. Simultaneous resection may be appropriate in selected patients with limited hepatic disease and a straightforward colorectal resection, particularly where both procedures can be performed safely without excessive physiological burden [31]. By contrast, a staged approach is often more appropriate when a major hepatectomy is required, when the primary tumour is rectal, or when obstruction, sepsis, recent surgery, or a hostile operative field makes combined or early repeat intervention undesirable [30,32]. In such settings, a short course of systemic therapy may pragmatically maintain disease control while planned local treatment is sequenced. Framed this way, chemotherapy for synchronous disease is best understood as one component of individualized, multidisciplinary planning rather than evidence for a universal “chemotherapy-first” strategy [30,31].

CIRCULATING TUMOUR DNA (ctDNA) AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

ctDNA is emerging as a prognostic and potentially predictive biomarker in CRLM. Multiple studies have demonstrated that ctDNA positivity following curative resection is associated with early recurrence and inferior survival, while ctDNA negativity identifies patients with more indolent disease biology [33,34].

Prospective trials [35,36] are now exploring the role of ctDNA-guided adjuvant therapy. In practical terms, ctDNA offers a route beyond broad clinicopathologic surrogates toward molecular residual disease stratification. This would involve enriching postoperative systemic therapy for patients with persistent ctDNA positivity while identifying lower-risk patients in whom treatment could potentially be de-escalated or withheld. Therefore, ctDNA may help define which patients are most likely to benefit from perioperative chemotherapy. Integrating ctDNA with clinicopathologic and molecular risk factors may further refine individualized treatment sequencing in the future.

CONCLUSION

In resectable CRLM, the routine use of perioperative chemotherapy reflects a strong biological rationale, but its benefit is unlikely to be uniform across all patients. Patients with clearly resectable, low-volume, and biologically favourable disease may be well served by upfront liver-directed treatment, particularly when timely local therapy can be delivered without compromising future options. By contrast, perioperative systemic therapy remains appropriate in selected patients with synchronous presentation, technically complex disease, or adverse biological features. The challenge is to move away from a single default approach and instead align the treatment sequence with anatomy, tumour biology, and emerging molecular markers of residual risk.
