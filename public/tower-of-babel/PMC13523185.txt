Associations between maternal and child anemia and development outcomes: evidence from a large cross-sectional population-based study
Authors: Hermano Alexandre Lima Rocha, Sabrina Gabriele Maia Oliveira Rocha, Marcia Maria Tavares Machado, Anamaria Cavalcante e Silva, Jocileide Sales Campos, Álvaro Jorge Madeiro Leite, Luciano Lima Correia, Christopher Robert Sudfeld
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis cross-sectional study investigated the association between maternal and child anemia and development in 2,473 children up to 72 months old in Ceará, Brazil. Hemoglobin was measured using HemoCue Hb 201, and child development was assessed via the Ages and Stages Questionnaires-Brazil (ASQ-BR). In multivariate analyses, neither maternal anemia nor child anemia (overall) was associated with continuous domain z-scores or developmental delays (all p > 0.05). However, when stratified by severity, mild child anemia was associated with a higher prevalence of fine motor delay (Prevalence Ratio = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.18–2.31), while moderate-to-severe child anemia was associated with a higher prevalence of gross motor delay (PR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.06–2.75). Exploratory analyses revealed effect modification by age; in children aged 49–72 months, mild child anemia was associated with lower gross motor (Standardized Mean Difference = -0.52), fine motor (SMD = -0.91), and problem-solving scores (SMD = -0.33), while moderate-to-severe child anemia was associated with lower gross motor scores (SMD = -0.83). Our findings suggest that the severity and timing of child anemia are associated with specific developmental outcomes, reinforcing the need for targeted interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-27887-z.Keywords: Anaemia, Child development, Cross-sectional studies, Maternal and child health, Epidemiology

Introduction

In 2019, it was estimated that the global prevalence of anemia among children aged 6–59 months was 39.8%, which equates to approximately 269 million children [1]. In Brazil, the prevalence of childhood anemia has shown a decline, with the 2019 National Survey on Child Nutrition (ENANI-2019) reporting a national prevalence of 10% in children aged 6–59 months. However, significant regional disparities persist, with prevalence reaching 17% in the North and 30% among children aged 6–23 months in that region, indicating that anemia remains a significant public health issue in more vulnerable populations [2]. Maternal anemia is also a persistent challenge in Brazil. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis estimated a pooled prevalence of 23% among pregnant women, with the Northeast region, where our study is located, showing a higher burden of 26% [3–7].

It is also estimated that more than 250 million children under the age of 5 years in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) may not reach their full developmental potential [8]. A large body of literature has identified many factors associated with suboptimal child development outcomes, including poverty [9], low maternal education, toxic stress, and poor health and nutrition. Anemia in childhood has been associated with suboptimal child development outcomes [10]. Iron deficiency anemia, in particular, has been linked to cognitive and motor impairments in children, through different mechanisms including dopamine metabolism, myelination, and hippocampal structure and function [11]. Despite anemia among children being prevalent in Brazil, few studies have examined the relationship to day, particularly at the population-level.

The relationship between maternal and child anemia and developmental outcomes is complex. Maternal anemia during pregnancy can impact fetal brain development directly [12], yet recent meta-analytic work suggests short-term effects on neonatal neurodevelopment may not be apparent [13]. Furthermore, maternal anemia can be a strong predictor of subsequent anemia in her child, suggesting an indirect pathway where maternal health influences child health, which in turn affects development [14]. This highlights the importance of considering both the direct effects of child anemia and the potential influence of maternal anemia, as well as the timing of these exposures, on a child’s developmental trajectory [15].

Maternal anemia may also be related to child development outcomes. The prevalence of anemia among women of reproductive age globally is around 33%, affecting approximately 40% of pregnant women and over 20% of non-pregnant women [16]. This highlights the substantial impact anemia has on women’s health that may affect their offspring [17]. In pregnancy, it can substantially impact fetal brain development by restricting oxygen delivery to the fetus, and studies have shown that maternal anemia is associated with smaller brain volumes in neonates, indicating potential differences in cerebral development that may translate to long-term neurocognitive impairments [18]. In addition, during the postpartum period, maternal anemia may exacerbate maternal stress and fatigue, which can negatively affect maternal-infant interaction and bonding, further influencing the child’s developmental trajectory [19]. Nevertheless, few studies have examined the relationship between maternal anemia during early childhood with child development outcomes.

To address this evidence gap, we conducted a statewide representative study in Ceará, Brazil, to evaluate the association of child and maternal anemia with child development. This information is intended to inform the need for anemia prevention and treatment interventions among mothers and children and their potential to impact child development outcomes.

Methods

Study design and population

This investigation utilized data from the seventh wave of the Pesquisa de Saúde Materno Infantil no Ceará (PESMIC), a series of population-based, cross-sectional studies focusing on maternal and child health among preschool children up to 72 months of age in Ceará, northeastern Brazil. The broader PESMIC initiative provides a robust framework for understanding child health in this region. For the present analysis, data were specifically drawn from the 2023 survey, which was conducted between November 2022 and March 2023, and the final analytical sample was restricted to 2,473 mother-child dyads who had complete data for both maternal and child hemoglobin concentrations and the Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ-BR) assessment. Comprehensive details of the overarching PESMIC study design and methodology have been described previously [20].

The sample size for the PESMIC survey was calculated to be representative of children under 72 months in the state of Ceará, stratified by urban (Fortaleza) and rural settings. The calculation was based on an estimated prevalence of developmental delay of 15% based in our prior research [21], a margin of error of 3% points, a 95% confidence level, and a design effect of 1.5 to account for the cluster sampling design. This resulted in a target sample size of approximately 2,400 children, which our final analytical sample of 2,473 achieves, ensuring adequate statistical power and representativeness for the state.

The study was situated in Ceará, a state in northeastern Brazil characterized by significant socioeconomic challenges and a semiarid climate, with a population of approximately 9 million. The state capital, Fortaleza, with 2.7 million inhabitants, serves as the primary urban and commercial hub. The study’s geographical scope also encompassed the rural areas of Ceará, where subsistence agriculture is the predominant livelihood [22].

The PESMIC studies employed a multi-stage cluster sampling strategy, designed to provide a representative sample of the target population. The sampling strategy was stratified by geographic setting to ensure representation from both the state’s primary urban center, the capital city of Fortaleza, and the combined rural areas throughout the rest of Ceará. The primary sampling units were census tracts, as defined by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE). A total of 160 such census tracts were selected for inclusion. The selection process was designed to ensure representativeness through a combination of random and systematic techniques. Initially, simple random sampling was used to determine the starting point for selection, followed by systematic sampling for the selection of subsequent cities and census tracts. Within each selected census tract, a cluster of 20 households was identified for investigation. The specific starting household for each cluster was randomly chosen using ArcGIS® software (GIS Inc.), and subsequent households were then visited consecutively in a counterclockwise direction [23].

In each participating household, data were collected for one child aged up to 72 months. Information regarding the child was obtained via report from the mother or primary caregiver. Anthropometric measurements for the children were conducted by experienced field staff who had undergone a specific 40-hour training program delivered by pediatricians and epidemiologists.

Hemoglobin measurements

For assessing hemoglobin, we collected capillary blood samples from mothers and children, and hemoglobin concentrations were quantified with the HemoCue Hb 201 System, with a related correlation of 0.99 when compared with the reference method to measure hemoglobin concentrations [24]. An accuracy study specifically conducted in children suggested that the HemoCue might reliably be used as an emergent device in areas with limited resources [25]. Anemia was defined using WHO guidelines based on hemoglobin concentrations measured in g/dL. The cutoffs were as follows:

For children 6–59 months of age: non-anemic (≥ 11.0 g/dL), mild anemia (10.0–10.9 g/dL), moderate anemia (7.0–9.9 g/dL), and severe anemia (< 7.0 g/dL).

For children 5–6 years of age: non-anemic (≥ 11.5 g/dL), mild anemia (11.0–11.4 g/dL), moderate anemia (8.0–10.9 g/dL), and severe anemia (< 8.0 g/dL).

For non-pregnant women (≥ 15 years): non-anemic (≥ 12.0 g/dL), mild anemia (11.0–11.9 g/dL), moderate anemia (8.0–10.9 g/dL), and severe anemia (< 8.0 g/dL) [26].

Child development assessment

Child development was assessed using the Ages and Stages Questionnaire, version 3 [27], validated in Brazil (ASQ-BR). The ASQ-BR is the Brazilian Portuguese adaptation of the ASQ-3 and has demonstrated good psychometric properties, including high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.80 for all domains) and test-retest reliability. It is widely used in Brazil for both population-level epidemiological research and clinical screening in primary care settings to identify children at risk for developmental delays [28]. The ASQ-BR assesses five distinct domains of child development: communication, gross motor skills (referred to as broad-motor coordination in the original text), fine motor skills, problem-solving abilities, and personal-social skills. The administration of the ASQ-BR was conducted by study interviewers who received extensive training specifically for this instrument. This training encompassed 80 h and was facilitated by medical professionals, in addition to the general training provided for the broader research project. The ASQ-BR was administered via a structured interview with the child’s mother or primary caregiver, who reported on the child’s ability to perform specific age-appropriate tasks [27]. Standardized procedures, as outlined in the ASQ-3 manual, were followed for scoring the ASQ-BR. If a child had more than two items skipped within a particular developmental domain, the score for that domain was considered missing and excluded from the analysis. In instances where one or two items within a domain were skipped, an adjusted score was calculated. This adjustment involved determining the average score of the completed items within that domain and assigning this average value to the skipped item(s). This protocol for managing missing item data ensures consistency in scoring and maintains the integrity of the domain scores by adhering to the developer’s guidelines. Furthermore, a crucial adjustment was made for children younger than 24 months of age who were born preterm [27]. For the binary outcome analyses, developmental delay in a specific domain was defined as a standardized z-score of less than − 2 standard deviations relative to the brazilian reference population.

Other variables

A structured questionnaire was administered to the primary caregiver to gather comprehensive information on household socioeconomic status and the child’s morbidity history. The socioeconomic data collected encompassed several key indicators, including maternal age, level of education, and occupation; the total number of individuals residing in the household; household participation in governmental cash transfer programs; and an assessment of household wealth. For the assessment of food insecurity, we used EBIA, a Brazilian-validated questionnaire for assessing food insecurity based on the American Scale of Perception and Experience of Hunger that was first used by the US Department of Agriculture (USDA) [29]. Based on guidelines described by Gibson [30], we developed a 24-h recall tailored to the unique cultural and dietary characteristics of Brazil.

Statistical analysis

The primary developmental outcomes analyzed were the domain scores from the ASQ-BR. These scores were standardized for both age and sex to allow for meaningful comparisons across children. A specific approach to standardization was adopted: for children aged five to 66 months, Brazilian reference standards were utilized. However, for infants younger than five months, standardization was based on United States reference standards. For the continuous ASQ-BR domain z-scores, we used generalized linear models (GLMs) with an identity link function and Gaussian distribution, which is equivalent to linear regression. For the binary outcome of developmental delay, we used log-linear models, a form of GLM with a log link function, to estimate prevalence ratios (PRs). For the analysis of developmental delay in each of the assessed domains, loglinear models were applied. In the multivariable analyses, a comprehensive set of covariates was included to control for potential confounding factors. These covariates were: child’s age and sex, urban versus rural residence, quintiles of monthly household income, the number of people residing in the household, household participation in cash transfer programs, household food security status, maternal age, maternal education level, and an indicator for the interviewer who conducted the assessment. The selection of these covariates was not arbitrary but was guided by the theoretical framework of the World Health Organization’s Nurturing Care Framework [31] and by findings from prior research conducted by the study group. The ‘interviewer’ variable was included as a categorical covariate in the adjusted models to account for potential systematic differences in data collection between interviewers. This is a standard practice in multi-interviewer survey research to control for the interviewer effect as a source of variance, thereby improving the precision of the estimates for the variables of interest. To further explore the nuances of the relationship between anemia and child development, the potential for effect modification by child’s age was assessed. This was achieved by testing for an interaction term between age and anemia status within the sample-adjusted generalized linear models. All study data were entered twice using EpiInfo 2000 software, and these double entries were subsequently compared for concordance to identify and correct data entry errors. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Version 23 (SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0. IBM Inc.).

Ethics

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Informed consent to participate was also obtained from the parents or legal guardians of any participant under the age of 18. The PESMICs survey was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Unimed Fortaleza in Brazil, under the protocol number 5.614.102.

Results

The study population included 2,473 children, with a majority residing in urban areas (67.6%). Table 1 presents a description of the children and their mothers in the sample. The mean household monthly income was 1,563.95 reais (SD = 1,243.21). The children in the study had an average age of 36.7 months (SD = 22.9), with 48.1% being female. A significant portion of the sample lived in food-insecure households (79.1%), and a substantial number of families (76.4%) participated in conditional cash transfer programs. The average maternal age was 30 years (SD = 7), with 32.4% having completed secondary education and 56.3% possessing advanced educational qualifications. Maternal anemia was prevalent in 43.9% of cases, with varying degrees of severity. Child anemia was observed in 35.2% of the sample. Developmental assessments revealed varying degrees of delay across different domains, with communication and gross motor delays observed in 5.0% of the children, fine motor in 7.1%, problem-solving in 5.3%, and personal-social in 5.4%.

In analyses examining anemia severity, some associations were identified (Table 2). Mild anemia was associated with a significantly higher risk of fine motor delays (aPR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.18–2.31, p = 0.004), while moderate to severe anemia was associated with an increased risk of gross motor delays (aPR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.06–2.75, p = 0.03).

Supplementary Table 1 displays the multivariable associations between maternal anemia and child developmental outcomes as measured by the ASQ adjusted standardized mean differences (SMDs). Overall, when comparing children of anemic versus non-anemic mothers, the SMDs were nearly null across domains, with communication scores showing an SMD of 0.04 (95% CI: -0.07 to 0.14, p = 0.49) and gross motor skills an SMD of 0 (95% CI: -0.12 to 0.12, p = 0.97), while fine motor, problem-solving, and personal-social domains exhibited SMDs of -0.09 (95% CI: -0.25 to 0.06, p = 0.23), 0.01 (95% CI: -0.09 to 0.10, p = 0.91), and − 0.06 (95% CI: -0.15 to 0.04, p = 0.23), respectively. Additionally, when stratified by anemia severity, both mild and moderate/severe maternal anemia were associated with SMD values close to zero—for example, mild anemia ranged from 0.03 in communication to -0.09 in fine motor skills and moderate/severe anemia ranged from 0.04 in communication to -0.10 in fine motor (supplementary Table 2).

Our study further explored the effect modification of age on the association between child anemia and developmental outcomes (Table 3). We observed that the association of anemia on developmental domains varied across different age groups. In children aged 6–24 months, neither mild nor moderate/severe anemia significantly influenced development across all domains, as indicated by small standardized mean differences (SMDs) and non-significant confidence intervals. However, among children aged 49–60 months, mild anemia was associated with notable delays in gross motor (SMD = -0.52, 95% CI: -1.02 to -0.02) and fine motor development (SMD = -0.91, 95% CI: -1.45 to -0.38), with significant delays also observed in problem-solving abilities (SMD = -0.33, 95% CI: -0.56 to -0.11). Moderate/severe anemia in this age group showed a substantial negative effect on gross motor skills (SMD = -0.83, 95% CI: -1.58 to -0.07), but no significant association was detected on other domains.

The p-values for effect modification indicated that the interaction between age and anemia was statistically significant for fine motor and problem-solving abilities (p = 0.01 for both). All associations can be seen in Table 3 (Table 3). It is important to note that the p-value for effect modification is a formal statistical test of the interaction term, assessing whether the association between anemia and development significantly differs across the age strata. A significant p-value (e.g., p = 0.01 for fine motor) indicates that the effect of anemia is not uniform across age groups. The confidence intervals, in contrast, refer to the specific point estimates within each age stratum. The significant interaction for the fine motor and problem-solving domains is driven by the large, statistically significant negative association observed in the oldest age group (49–72 months) compared to the null associations in the younger two groups. We also tested the effect modification with categorical outcomes, but no statistical significance was found (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we identified an association between childhood mild and severe anemia and a higher prevalence of developmental delays in the fine motor and gross motor domains. Furthermore, we identified that the age of children modified the association in the gross motor, fine motor and problem-solving domains, affecting older children with greater magnitude.

Although in our study we found no association between maternal anemia and child development, other studies show that when caregivers, especially mothers, are anemic, they may lack the energy, motivation, or cognitive capacity to engage in responsive caregiving behaviors, such as playing, talking, and interacting with their children, which are essential for stimulating the child’s development [32, 33]. Caregivers who are anemic may struggle to provide adequate stimulation to their children, leading to a lack of enriching experiences that are vital for optimal child development. This deficiency in stimulation can result in developmental delays, as children may not receive the necessary cognitive and emotional input required for healthy growth and learning [34].

It was seen in the results of the study that the motor development of children is affected by anemia, both gross and fine motor skills. When children suffer from anemia, their bodies lack an adequate supply of oxygen to support various physiological processes, including muscle function and coordination, which are important for motor development [35]. Research has highlighted that anemia in children can lead to growth restriction and impaired motor development, indicating the detrimental impact of insufficient oxygen supply on physical growth and motor skills [36]. Moreover, studies have shown that children with anemia may experience delays in motor development, affecting their ability to achieve age-appropriate milestones and engage in physical activities. The insufficient oxygen supply resulting from anemia can compromise the body’s ability to support optimal motor development, affecting a child’s physical abilities and coordination [37]. These findings reinforce the findings found in our sample that children with anemia may have worse motor development.

We also identified that older children, aged five and over, showed a greater effect of anemia on child development, in motor domains and beyond that in the problem-solving domain. Older children are more affected by anemia due to various factors that influence their susceptibility to this condition. As children grow older, their bodies require more nutrients, including iron, to support their rapid physical development and increased activity levels [38]. At this stage, if children are iron deficient, they may have even greater difficulty performing these more laborious activities. Additionally, older children may experience growth spurts, further increasing their need for iron to support optimal growth and development [39]. Moreover, older age groups may face unique challenges that increase their vulnerability to anemia. Factors such as dietary preferences, limited access to nutrient-dense foods, and poor eating habits can impact the nutritional status of older children, potentially leading to deficiencies [40]. Likewise. older children may also be more exposed to environmental factors that can contribute to anemia and poor child development, such as infections, chronic diseases, or inadequate healthcare access, further heightening their risk of developing this condition [41].

The absence of an association between maternal anemia and child development outcomes in our study, while seemingly counterintuitive, aligns with recent meta-analytic evidence from Wang et al. (2025) which found no significant short-term effects of maternal anemia on neonatal neurodevelopment [12]. A crucial factor may be the timing of exposure. Our cross-sectional design assessed maternal anemia concurrently with child development, whereas the most critical period of vulnerability may be in utero. Indeed, longitudinal research by Ringshaw et al. (2025) has shown that antenatal maternal anemia is associated with persistent alterations in child brain structure years later, whereas postnatal child anemia was not [13]. It is therefore plausible that the effects of maternal anemia are most profound during fetal brain development, and our measurement outside this critical window may explain the null association observed.

The effect identified on problem-solving domains of older children can be explained by some mechanisms. As children grow older, their cognitive demands increase, requiring optimal brain function and development. Anemia can have a significant impact on cognitive function, affecting memory, attention, and overall cognitive abilities, which are essential for intellectual activities and learning [42]. The brain requires a sufficient supply of oxygen to support cognitive processes, and anemia, by reducing oxygen delivery to the brain, can impair cognitive function, potentially hindering intellectual activities in older children. Moreover, brain development continues throughout childhood and adolescence, with significant changes occurring in neural connections and cognitive abilities. Anemia can interfere with this critical period of brain development, affecting neural pathways and cognitive processes necessary for intellectual activities [43].

While our multivariate models adjusted for a range of key socioeconomic and demographic factors, we must acknowledge the potential for residual confounding from unmeasured variables. For instance, the quality of the home learning environment and the degree of environmental stimulation, which are strong predictors of cognitive development, were not captured in our survey. It is likely that more stimulating environments are also associated with better nutritional practices, which could confound the observed associations. Similarly, we did not have data on the burden of infectious diseases, such as parasitic infections, which are known to be a significant cause of anemia in many regions of Brazil and can independently impact a child’s development and well-being. Finally, while we accounted for maternal anemia, other aspects of maternal nutritional status and mental health could also act as confounders.

This study has some noteworthy limitations. This study has some noteworthy limitations. Foremost, its cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causality. We are unable to determine the temporal sequence between the onset of anemia and the manifestation of developmental delays. It is plausible that developmental delays could lead to feeding difficulties and subsequent nutritional deficiencies like anemia, representing a case of reverse causality. Longitudinal studies that follow children from birth are necessary to untangle the precise causal direction of this association. Furthermore, although the ASQ is a widely validated instrument, including in Brazil, for assessing child development, it is not a diagnostic tool for delays in development. However, for population-based research, the use of clinical scales is practically unfeasible. Furthermore, not all children had their hemoglobin levels measured. However, our analyses show that the difference between children who had the measurement and those who did not was statistically significant for any of the variables of interest.

Conclusion

We conclude from this population-based cross-sectional study that anemia in children is associated with a higher prevalence of delay in gross and fine motor development and that this association is greater in older children aged five years and older, in whom anemia also leads to worse cognitive development as measured by the problem-solving domain. Furthermore, the prevalence of anemia in the population evaluated, which represents a large part of Brazil, is high. Interventions aimed at reducing anemia can lead to better child development, and children over 48 months of age may particularly benefit. Our findings have direct policy implications. The high prevalence of anemia, particularly among older preschool children who demonstrated the greatest developmental deficits, suggests a need to strengthen and potentially extend existing public health programs. Feasible actions could include: (1) Improving the coverage and adherence to Brazil’s National Iron Supplementation Program, particularly for children beyond infancy; (2) Reinforcing food fortification policies and monitoring their impact; and (3) Integrating developmental screening with routine nutritional assessments at primary healthcare facilities. This would allow for the early identification of both anemia and developmental delay, enabling targeted interventions for high-risk children, especially those over 48 months of age, who may benefit most.
