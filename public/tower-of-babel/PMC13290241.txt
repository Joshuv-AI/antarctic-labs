Incidence, Risk Factors, Management, and Nonunion Rates of Pediatric Scaphoid Fractures: A National Database Study
Authors: Katie M. Zehner, Joshua G. Sanchez, Julian Smith-Voudouris, David L. Colen, Andrea Halim, Jonathan N. Grauer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:Scaphoid fractures are often complicated by nonunion, but incidence, risk factors, and management have not been well-characterized for pediatric patients.Methods:Patients aged <18 years with scaphoid fracture were identified in the 2015 to 2023 PearlDiver M170 database. Patient and fracture characteristics, imaging, and surgeries were extracted. Predictive factors for nonunion were characterized with multivariable analyses. Among patients with nonunion, characteristics of those receiving surgical and nonsurgical management were compared with multivariable analyses.Results:Of 26,364 pediatric scaphoid fractures, 64.5% were male, with an average age of 13.54 years. Of fractures with displacement and location specified, 22.8% were displaced and distal, middle, and proximal third fractures constituted 45.4%, 45.7%, and 8.9%, respectively. MRI was used for 11.5% and CT for 8.6%. Nonunion occurred in 1102 patients (4.2%). Nonunion was independently associated with clinical characteristics (older age [OR = 1.57 per year], male sex [OR = 2.11], and overweight/obesity [OR = 1.52]), nonclinical characteristics (relative to Northeast, Midwest region OR = 1.45), and fracture characteristics (displacement [OR = 2.45] and position [relative to distal, middle OR = 2.18 and proximal one third location OR = 4.74]) (P < 0.0003 for all). Of nonunions, 73% were diagnosed within 3 months of first fracture diagnosis. Of patients with nonunion, 784 (71.3%) had surgery. Surgery was independently associated with older age (OR = 1.15 per year) and Midwest region (relative to West, OR = 1.96) (P < 0.0063 for all).Conclusion:Pediatric scaphoid fracture patient and fracture characteristics roughly mirrored adult populations. The occurrence of most nonunion diagnoses shortly after initial fracture diagnosis suggests that many patients with nonunion presented late to care.

In adults, scaphoid fractures comprise 60 to 70% of carpal fractures and injury characteristics,1 risk factors,2 and management1,3-6 are well characterized. Nonunions occur frequently, affecting 3.5 to 15.5% of all fractures,5,6 and risk factors for nonunion are also well established.4,7-11 However, these factors are less well characterized in the pediatric population.

A limitation of studying pediatric scaphoid fractures is their incidence, accounting for 0.45% of pediatric upper extremity fractures and 3% of all hand and wrist fractures.12 Peak injury incidence occurs between ages 12 and 15 years,13 although few epidemiologic studies exist. A single institution study of 56 scaphoid fractures in patients younger than 13 years found boys to be more commonly affected than girls, with most fractures occurring at the distal pole,14 whereas a retrospective analysis of 351 pediatric patients with scaphoid fracture found most fractures to occur at the scaphoid waist.15 Although management is less well defined than for adults, most guidelines recommend initial management as weeks to months of casting in a short arm or thumb spica splint, with consideration of open reduction and internal fixation of acute displaced fractures.12,13,15-20

The risk factors for the development of scaphoid nonunion and optimal management when it does occur are also not well described in pediatric patients. Estimates of scaphoid nonunion rate in children range from 0 to >5%,17,21,22 with rates as low as 0.8% in patients managed promptly with adequate immobilization.17 However, approximately one third of pediatric scaphoid fractures present late to care,15 which has been consistently associated with nonunion.15,16,23 A retrospective study of 384 pediatric scaphoid fractures identified proximal and displaced fractures, increased patient age, and casting delay to be associated with nonunion.15 When nonunion occurs, optimal management is not well-defined, and standard of care varies among institutions. Although some reviews recommend an initial course of immobilization even in later presentation of scaphoid fracture,16,21 open reduction and internal fixation is recommended for displaced nonunion.21 In the largest study of pediatric fracture nonunions in general, Zura et al22 identified 237,033 fractures of any bone and studied risk factors associated with nonunion, finding that scaphoid fractures comprised a small fraction of identified fractures but had the highest nonunion rate of any bone. Increasing age, severe fracture (i.e. open fracture, multiple fractures), high BMI, male sex, and smoking were associated with nonunion of any bone, although this was not characterized with respect to the scaphoid specifically.

Understanding limitations of prior studies, the current study leveraged the power of a national database to better characterize pediatric scaphoid fracture incidence, risk factors, and associated imaging. Furthermore, risk factors for the development of nonunion and subsequent surgical management were characterized.

Methods

Patient Population

This study used data from the October 2015 to April 2023 PearlDiver M170 Mariner Patient Claims Database (PearlDiver Technologies, Colorado Springs, CO). The PearlDiver database is a multi-insurance administrative database, including 170 million covered patient lives in the United States and has been well established in the pediatric orthopaedic and hand/upper extremity literature.10,24-28 As all output from PearlDiver is aggregated and deidentified, our institutional review board has deemed studies using the dataset exempt from review.

Pediatric patients (age <18 years) with acute scaphoid fracture were identified by International Classification of Diseases 10 (ICD-10) coding in the S62.0XX family (fracture of the navicular [scaphoid] bone of the wrist, initial encounter). These ICD-10 fracture codes stratify for fracture displacement, laterality (right, left or unspecified), open/closed fracture, and location (proximal one third, middle one third, distal one third, or unspecified). As the International Classification of Diseases 9 (ICD-9) coding system used before 2015 lacks the granularity to describe fracture location and displacement, patients with injuries before the implementation of the ICD-10 system were not included.

Codes referring to initial encounter for open or closed fracture were included, whereas those referring to “subsequent encounter for routine healing,” “subsequent encounter with delayed healing,” or “sequela” were not included as acute scaphoid fractures. The presence of additional ipsilateral carpal or metacarpal fractures on the same day as initial presentation of scaphoid fracture was identified by ICD-10 codes and considered as an additional variable of “other ipsilateral hand or wrist fracture.”

Exclusion criteria were patients 18 years of age or older and those not active in the PearlDiver database for 1 year following initial injury diagnosis, to ensure that all nonunions were captured.

Patient Characteristics

Patient demographics, including age, sex, and Elixhauser comorbidity index (ECI) were abstracted from the database. ECI is a measure of overall comorbidity burden based on ICD-10 codes.29,30 Insurance coverage (private, Medicaid, Medicare, government, or other) and geographic region of residence (Northeast, South, Midwest, or West) were also extracted.

Medical comorbidities abstracted included asthma, diabetes mellitus, obesity, and substance use and were extracted using ICD-10 codes. To account for the changing BMI levels associated with overweight or obesity in children of different ages, the code Z68.54 was used (pediatric BMI > 95th percentile), as well as E66.811, E66.812, and E66.813 (obesity class 1, 2, and 3).

Fracture Management

Advanced imaging was identified by the presence of a Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code for hand, wrist, forearm or general upper extremity CT, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or bone scan in the three months before or one year after first fracture diagnosis code. A combined category of “any advanced imaging” was also determined to indicate the use of any of CT, MRI, or bone scan. Initial surgical management was identified by CPT codes 25628 (open treatment of carpal scaphoid navicular fracture, with or without external fixation) or CPT 25430 (insertion vascular pedicle into carpal bone) within one year of first fracture presentation, either before or in the absence of a code for nonunion.

Nonunions and Their Management

Nonunion was identified among those in the scaphoid fracture cohort by the presence of an ICD-10 code for “subsequent encounter for fracture with nonunion” or CPT code 25440 indicating nonunion repair, within one year of the initial fracture code. All nonunions were classified by occurrence after nonsurgical management (before any surgical CPT codes) or after surgical management (first instance after a surgical CPT code). Surgical interventions for nonunions initially managed nonsurgically were identified using CPT codes 25628, 25440, or CPT 25430. Among the nonunion cohort, late presentation to care was classified as fewer than 3 months elapsed time between first presentation of fracture and first diagnosis of nonunion. It was assumed that this would be too short an interval to diagnose a nonunion if the patient had promptly presented at the time of injury and that this cohort would include those who may have presented in a delayed manner or had an initial diagnosis missed but still presented to care before the development of a nonunion.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 for all analyses, with Bonferroni corrections applied for each separate analysis to account for multiple comparisons. Approximate annual fracture incidence, reported as number of fractures per 10,000 pediatric patients per year, was calculated by dividing the total number of fractures over the course of the study by the number of covered patient-years, based on annual database pediatric patient coverage and number of months of available data each year. Univariable analysis was conducted to compare demographic information, fracture characteristics, and medical comorbidities of patients who progressed to nonunion as compared with those with routine healing. Differences in categorical variables were compared with chi square tests and continuous variables were compared using Student t-tests. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify characteristics independently associated with nonunion.

Among patients with nonunion initially managed nonsurgically, univariable analysis was conducted to compare characteristics of patients receiving surgical treatment with those managed nonsurgically. As above, differences in categorical variables were compared using chi square tests, and continuous variables were compared with Student t-tests. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify characteristics independently associated with surgical management.

Results

Patient/Fracture Characteristics

A total of 26,364 diagnosed acute pediatric scaphoid fractures were identified, representing an annual incidence of 24 fractures per 10,000 covered pediatric patients. Of all identified patients, 64.5% were male, with an average age of 13.54 years (Table 1). Distal one-third fractures constituted 17.9% of all fractures, whereas middle third fractures constituted 18.0% and proximal third fractures constituted 3.5%. The remaining patients had location unspecified. Of those with presence or absence of displacement specified, 22.8% had displacement whereas 77.2% were nondisplaced. In addition, 805 patients (3.1%) presented with at least one additional carpal or metacarpal fracture.

Factors Associated with Nonunions

Of pediatric patients diagnosed with scaphoid fracture, nonunion was subsequently noted for 1,102 patients (4.2%). Age (P < 0.0001), sex (P < 0.0001), and region (P = 0.0005) were markedly different among groups, whereas ECI and insurance were not. By multivariable analysis, nonunion was independently associated with older age (OR = 1.57 per year, P < 0.0001), male sex (OR = 2.11, P < 0.0001), and Midwest region (relative to Northeast, OR = 1.45, P = 0.0002).

Factors Associated with Nonunion Surgery

Of pediatric patients initially treated nonsurgically and diagnosed with scaphoid nonunion, surgery was performed for 784 (71.3%; Tables 6 and 7). Age (P = 0.0039), insurance provider (P = 0.0002), and region (P = 0.0038) were markedly different among patients managed surgically or nonsurgically by univariable analysis. By multivariable analysis, surgery for nonunion was independently associated with older age (OR = 1.15 per year, P = 0.0032) and Midwest region (relative to West, OR = 1.96, P = 0.0017). Of the 801 patients with nonunion classified as having late presentation to care, 75.2% were managed surgically, as opposed to only 60.5% of those 299 patients who did not present late to care.

Discussion

Although the incidence of pediatric scaphoid fractures is relatively low, this study leveraged a national database to identify a large cohort of 26,364 fractures, allowing for subsequent analyses. This cohort was majority male, with an average age of 13.54 years, with predominantly closed, isolated, nondisplaced, distal or middle one third fractures. These findings are largely consistent with existing pediatric literature describing scaphoid fracture patient demographics.13,14 Although adult studies report waist fractures most common,31,32 pediatric studies have variably found distal pole fractures33,34 or waist fractures to be most common.13,15 We report nearly equivalent rates, with 45.7% of fractures with location specified occurring at the waist and 45.4% occurring at the distal pole. A large percentage of fracture locations and displacements were unspecified, suggesting that providers may default to using less-specific codes for these injuries. Only 3.1% of patients presented with concomitant fracture, lower than the 11%35 and 14.5%15 previously reported in adults and children, respectively, although these studies investigated more extensive sets of injuries.15 Furthermore, only those concomitant fractures identified on the same day as first scaphoid fracture presentation were presently assessed, excluding those that may have been identified with later advanced imaging.

Advanced imaging modalities were used in the workup of 18.6% of identified fractures. This was most commonly MRI (11.5% of the cases), CT (8.6% of the cases), and rarely bone scan (<0.1% of the cases). The rate of advanced imaging utilization in scaphoid fractures is not well characterized in the adult or pediatric populations, but recent guidelines recommend prompt CT evaluation in adult patients for whom scaphoid fracture is clinically suspected but not visible on initial radiograph, suggesting that this rate would be higher.20 Utilization of advanced imaging may be lower in the pediatric population due to a desire to minimize radiation exposure or potential need for procedural sedation, although Gholson et al.15 report a much higher advanced imaging rate of 64% at a single pediatric tertiary care center, suggesting that imaging decisions may be influenced by clinical setting and resources.

A nonunion rate of 4.2% was observed, falling at the lower end of the reported adult range and higher end of the previously reported pediatric range.5,6,17,21 However, as only patients with a prior diagnosis of acute scaphoid fracture were included in this study, this figure represents the rate of nonunion among patients presenting to care for their fracture before development of nonunion and not the overall rate of nonunion in the population, which would include those patients who did not present to care until after developing nonunion or never presented to care. This is important to note, as it has been estimated that half of pediatric scaphoid fracture nonunions occur in patients with a missed initial injury.21 By multivariable analysis, nonunion was associated with older age, male sex, and region (Midwest relative to Northeast). That male sex and older age were associated with nonunion is consistent with findings prior studies of all pediatric nonunions.22 Markedly higher rates of nonunion in the Midwest relative to the Northeast suggests potential regional differences in management patterns, or that limited timely access to subspecialty care, as has been previously noted for hand conditions in rural areas,36,37 may lead to delays in proper management and worsened outcomes.

When comparing medical comorbidities in patients with and without nonunion, substance use differed by univariable analysis but not multivariable analysis, whereas overweight/obesity was only notable by multivariable analysis. Substance use is likely highly colinear with age, which may better account for differences in nonunion rate. Furthermore, although substance use has been associated with nonunion in the adult population,9,10 pediatric patients with shorter histories of substance use lack the cumulative effects of many years of use. A database study previously found obesity to be associated with pediatric fracture nonunion,22 whereas a smaller prospective study of long bone fractures found no association between obesity and time to fracture recovery, when stratified by injury mechanism.38 Obesity may be associated with scaphoid nonunion due to difficulty achieving adequate immobilization in patients with increased adiposity or due to poor nutrition contributing to delays in bony healing.

When assessing fracture characteristics, displacement and more proximal location were associated with the highest odds of nonunion. This is consistent with prior adult and pediatric literature4,11,15,34 and expected given the limited vascularity of the proximal pole and the tendency of displacement to introduce mechanical instability.

Of those patients with nonunion, 71.3% were managed surgically. This suggests that providers may be willing to trial nonsurgical management even after nonunion, as suggested by recent studies,16,21 and that this paradigm can be successful, given that 28.7% of patients had no surgery in 1 year of follow-up. In particular, this treatment approach may be appropriate in the many patients diagnosed with nonunion within 12 weeks of first presentation to care, who have not yet had a full trial of immobilization. Odds of surgical management in the Midwest were nearly two times greater than the West, suggesting regional differences in standards of practice and underscoring the need for improved evidence-based clinical guidelines for pediatric scaphoid nonunion management. Increasing age was also associated with surgical management, likely due to a recognition that fractures in older teenagers may behave more similarly to adult nonunions and be less likely to resolve without surgical intervention.

To roughly approximate late presentation to care, late fracture diagnosis was defined as nonunion diagnosis or surgery within 3 months of initial fracture diagnosis. Although recognizing insurance databases are imperfect tool to assess delays in patient presentation to care, in the setting of well-established associations between delays in care and development of nonunion,15,16,23 this secondary analysis was included. By this definition, late fracture diagnosis was markedly associated with surgical management. Although this group is likely heterogenous, encompassing late presentations to care, missed initial injuries, and early nonunion diagnoses due to limited bony healing, many diagnoses and surgeries occurred within only days to weeks of first fracture diagnosis, including 43% within one month of fracture diagnosis, suggesting truly late presentation. This association suggests providers may be more likely to operate in patients who missed an initial window for proper immobilization, consistent with a prior study noting that presentation >21 days after injury is associated with failure to unite after casting.39 Although delayed presentation is an established a risk factor for nonunion, management guidelines for nonunion based on initial presentation time are still not well established. Further research incorporating radiologic data and more granular clinical detail is necessary to understand the relative contributions of treatment delays and diagnosis of impending nonunion to this high rate of early nonunion diagnoses.

This study has limitations. Fracture location and displacement were only able to be assessed for approximately 40% of patients, as the remaining patients had location and displacement unspecified. Because of the nature of the PearlDiver database, patient skeletal maturity could not be assessed, and thus, pediatric patients were defined based on age alone. As only patients with an initial code for acute fracture before a code for nonunion were included, patients first presenting to care with nonunion are missed, limiting the understanding of the epidemiology of scaphoid nonunion in the population at large. By defining our nonunion cohort by diagnosis codes or procedural codes for nonunion surgery, we also likely miss a population with delayed healing or nascent nonunion, who were then converted from nonsurgical to surgical management before a true diagnosis of nonunion was made, and such have different procedural coding.

Conclusion

In summary, the current study reports patient demographics and factors associated with nonunions and their management in large, nationally representative cohort to be largely similar to that of the adult population, albeit with lower rates of nonunion. Regional variations in nonunion rate and management highlight a need for improved evidence-based clinical guidelines.
