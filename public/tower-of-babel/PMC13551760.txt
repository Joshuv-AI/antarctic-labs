Aging self-stereotypes and associated factors among elderly patients with multimorbidity undergoing elective surgery: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Qianling Li, Yushan Tan, Chuanyan Hu, Dongxue Li, Wei Luo, Zhi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aging self-stereotypes are increasingly recognized as a significant psychosocial issue affecting older adults with multimorbidity, particularly those undergoing elective surgery. The aging self-stereotypes can exacerbate postoperative recovery and reduce quality of life. However, factors influencing aging self-stereotypes in this vulnerable population remain underexplored. This study aimed to investigate the level of aging self-stereotypes among elderly patients with multimorbidity undergoing elective surgery and to analyze relevant influencing factors with the goal of laying a foundation for the development of targeted intervention strategies. The findings will help medical staff identify high-risk patients and inform the development of targeted perioperative interventions to improve recovery outcomes and quality of life.MethodsThis study, which featured a cross-sectional survey design, focused on a total of 188 elderly patients with multimorbidity who underwent elective surgeries at a tertiary A-grade hospital in Chongqing and were recruited through convenience sampling between July 2025 and December 2025. General information questionnaires, the Barthel index, the Cumulative Illness Rating Scale, the Aging Self-Stereotypes Scale, the Fried Frailty Phenotype, and the Control Preference Scale were used in this research. Multiple linear regression was used to identify associated factors. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.ResultsThe aging self-stereotypes scores obtained by elderly patients with multimorbidity who underwent elective surgery were 27.697 ± 1.953 points. The results of multiple linear regression analysis identified first-time surgery ( β = 0.143, CI (-0.008, 1.072), P = 0.034), Cumulative Illness Rating Scale score ( β = 0.177, CI (0.230,1.741), P = 0.011), and active decision-making (β = -0.161, CI (-1.900, -0.134), P = 0.024) as influencing factors pertaining to aging self-stereotypes among elderly patients with multimorbidity who underwent elective surgeries, which altogether explained 50.1% of the total variance.ConclusionsElderly patients with multimorbidity who underwent elective surgeries have high aging self‑stereotype scores, which are independently associated with first‑time surgery, multimorbidity burden, and decision‑making preference. A management model that combines multidisciplinary collaboration with family support should be established to reduce negative stereotypes and improve quality of life among these patients.

Background

According to a report issued by the National Bureau of Statistics of China, by the end of 2024, the population aged 60 years or older in China reached 310.31 million, accounting for 22.0% of the national population [1]. In this context, chronic noncommunicable diseases have become the most serious disease burden worldwide [2]. Notably, the coexistence of various chronic diseases is becoming increasingly common in the elderly population [3]. The concept of “comorbidity” was proposed by Professor Feinstein [4] in 1970. Professor Brandlmeier [5] subsequently materialized this concept and proposed the notion of “multimorbidity” in 1976. In 2008, the World Health Organization defined “multimorbidity” as the simultaneous presence of two or more chronic diseases in an individual [6]. Many epidemiological surveys have shown that the incidence of multimorbidity in surgical patients is approximately 50%-70% [7]. Among elderly individuals in China, the number of patients with chronic diseases is very large, approximately 180 million, of which more than one-third are patients with comorbidities [8]. Multimorbidity is not additive effect of multiple individual disease; however, its clinical complexity poses a significant challenge to traditional diagnosis and treatment models based on the management of a single disease. In addition, multimorbidity is more likely to lead to increased difficulty in clinical diagnosis and treatment, reduced functional status, and increased risk of death among elderly individuals [9, 10].

Levy et al. [11] argue that people hold specific beliefs and expectations toward elderly adults as a social group, referred to as aging stereotypes. Starting from the age of 4–5, children gradually internalize the aging stereotypes of their culture, which is continuously reinforced through family, media, and social interactions [12]. As individuals age, aging stereotypes may gradually become internalized into their self-perceptions of aging, a process referred to as aging self-stereotypes [13]. Specifically, when individuals gradually absorb and incorporate the general beliefs and expectations about older age groups in society into their self-concept, the resulting self-perception and evaluation of themselves as members of this age group constitute the aging self-stereotype. Stereotype embodiment theory [11] suggests that this internalization process is often unconscious, accompanies individuals throughout life, and may even show significant changes over a relatively short period of time. Moreover, the theory further suggests that individuals establish negative perceptions about aging early in life; with increasing age, these negative perceptions, namely, aging self-stereotypes, further contaminate and permeate the self. Studies [13] have shown that aging self-stereotypes have the following characteristics: (1) Continuity: they first appear in childhood as aging stereotypes and continue to be strengthened into adulthood; (2) Duality of consciousness: they exhibit both externalization and internalization, with implicit-level influences being more covert and enduring; (3)Identity association: when an individual’s age identity shifts from a “non-older adult group” to an “older adult group” aging stereotypes originally directed toward others are transformed into aging self-stereotypes. As a core structure of the self-concept of older adults, aging self-stereotypes increase the likelihood of individuals identifying with group stereotypes and engaging in stereotype-related behaviors. Consequently, they shape elder adults’ expectations, goals, and actions related to aging, and serve as the key pathway through which societal aging stereotypes affect psychological well-being and even physiological health [14, 15]. Aging self-stereotypes are particularly relevant and clinically important for elderly patients with multimorbidity undergoing elective surgery. This is because this population faces significant challenges from aging, multimorbidity, role conflict, and surgical stress. Aging self‑stereotypes may be associated with negative self‑evaluation of one’s own abilities, potentially leading to decreased treatment compliance and impaired psychological function [16]. These issues may in turn prolong hospitalization and increase mortality risk [17]. Key influencing factors such as multimorbidity burden, functional status, social support, and decision-making preferences may directly or indirectly affect patients’ physical recovery, quality of life, and clinical disease indicators.

However, important gaps remain in existing research on multimorbidity and aging self-stereotypes. On the one hand, most studies have focused on nonsurgical populations and failed to fully consider the potential amplification effect of surgical stress on psychological internalizing mechanisms; on the other hand, previous intervention strategies have emphasized the regulation of physiological indicators and paid insufficient attention to modifiable psychological factors such as aging self-stereotypes. In addition, the question of how situational variables such as disease severity and clinical decision mode regulate the formation and effect of self-stereotypes remains unanswered. Therefore, this study was designed as an exploratory cross-sectional analysis to identify factors associated with aging self-stereotypes. Based on stereotype embodiment theory and existing literature, we expected that greater multimorbidity burden, first-time surgery, frailty, and passive decision-making might be associated with higher aging self-stereotypes scores. Accordingly, this study aimed to: (1) describe the level of aging self-stereotypes in this population; (2) identify factors associated with aging self-stereotypes, with the goal of lay a foundation for developing targeted psychological interventions.

Methods

Design

This study featured a cross-sectional descriptive study design.

Recruitment of participants, inclusion and exclusion criteria

Using a convenience sampling method, 188 patients who underwent elective surgery under general anesthesia in a tertiary A-grade hospital in Chongqing between July 2025 and December 2025 were selected as respondents for the survey. In this study, the infinite population sample size calculation formula n = Zα2 × σ 2/δ 2 was used [18]. According to the pilot results, the standard deviation was 1.97 points, a two-sided test was needed, α was 0.05, and the allowable error was 0.3 points. Given a 5% invalid questionnaire rate, a sample size of at least 174 cases was needed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: ① age ≥ 60 years; ② elective surgery with a procedure classification of Grade III or IV; ③ the types of surgery include general surgery and orthopedics; ④ a Cumulative Illness Rating Scale score ≥ 1 point; and ⑤ informed consent and voluntary participation in this study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: ① patients with communication disorders; ② patients with a pure tone hearing threshold > 55 decibels; ③ patients who were unconscious or had mental disorders; and ④ patients who had recently experienced other major traumatic events.

Survey instruments

General information questionnaire

The data were compiled by the investigator on the basis of clinical experience and the relevant literature and included age, sex, educational level, marital status, living method, preoperative length of hospital stay, occupation, and type of medical insurance.

Barthel index

The Barthel index [19] is a standardized tool that is widely used to assess patients’ ability in the context of activities of daily living (ADL). By quantifying the basic self-care abilities of individuals, this scale effectively reflects the functional status and degree of disability. The assessment covered 10 dimensions: eating, bathing, grooming, dressing, stool continence, urination control, using the toilet, transferring to a bed and chair, and walking both on level ground and up and down stairs. The total score was 100 points, and higher scores indicated better self-care ability and a milder degree of disability. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale in this study was 0.876.

Cumulative Illness Rating Scale (CIRS)

This scale covers 14 organ systems, including the heart, kidney, liver, blood vessels, upper gastrointestinal tract, lower gastrointestinal tract, respiration, blood, musculoskeletal, nerve, eye, otolaryngology, genitourinary, endocrine metabolism, and psychology [20]. Each system was scored according to the severity of the disease: 0 points indicated no damage, 1 point represented mild damage, 2 points represented moderate damage, 3 points represented severe damage, and 4 points represented potentially life-threatening damage. The total score ranged from 0 to 56 points, and higher scores indicated more severe conditions. Various classification methods can be used for CIRS. (1) Severity index: In view of the possible limitations of using the CIRS total score alone as an indicator of comorbidity in middle-aged and elderly patients, Salvi et al. proposed using this measure in combination with the severity index for assessment. The index was calculated as the average value of the scores of the remaining thirteen systems after the mental and psychological systems were excluded (severity index = total score of 14 systems/14). (2) Comorbidity index: This index reflects the number and severity of involved organ systems in a patient and is defined as the total number of items with a score ≥ 2 points (i.e., moderate damage or higher) in the 14 systems (for example, if there are five systemic scores ≥ 2 points, the comorbidity index is 5). In this study, the CIRS scores of the patients were classified into mild as well as moderate and severe cases, in which context of those with ≤ 14 points were included in the mild group and those with > 14 points were included in the moderate and severe group. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale in this study was 0.759.

Aging self-stereotypes scale

The aging self-stereotypes scale for elder adults was adapted from the Fraboni Scale of Ageism developed by Rupp, Vodanovich and Credé et al. [21], and translated and revised by the Chinese scholar Xu [22]. This scale was associated with a total of 10 items, which were selected from the items with relatively high factor loadings and the most representative items with respect to the elderly group on which the original scale focused. The translation and back-translation of the scale were performed by a team of graduate students majoring in social psychology, and the item validity was evaluated. The questionnaire used a 4-point scoring system (1 = “strongly disagree” to 4 = “strongly agree”), and higher total scores indicated stronger aging self-stereotypes. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.881.

Fried Frailty Phenotype (FFP)

The FFP scale was proposed by Fried et al. (2001) [23]. This assessment included five items, including weight loss, walking time, grip strength, physical activity, and fatigue. The total score ranged from 0 to 5 points, with 0 indicating no weakness, 1–2 points indicating early-stage frailty, and ≥ 3 points indicating frailty. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale in this study was 0.740.

Control Preference Scale (CPS)

This scale was developed by Degner [24] in 1997 and adapted by Nolan in 2005 [25]. The adapted scale can assess the degree of patient participation in decision-making. The scale contains 5 cards, that include five options ranging from A to E as follows: A, the doctor made a complete decision; B, the doctor made a decision after fully considering the opinions of the patient and his family; C, the doctor and the patient made a decision after weighing the matter jointly; D, the patient/family made a decision after fully considering the doctor’s recommendation; E, the patient/family member made their own decision after learning about the various medical options. Here, options A and B represent the active type of patient participation in decision-making, option C represents the cooperative type of patient participation in decision-making, and options D and E represent the passive type of patient participation in decision-making. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale in this study was 0.881.

Methods of data collection

Before formally initiating the research process, the researchers first screened 30 elderly patients who met the inclusion criteria and carried out the preliminary pre-survey. The aim was to comprehensively evaluate the applicability of the scales and questionnaires used in this study, including the clarity with which elderly patients understood the questionnaire content and the reasonableness of the estimated completion time. During the preparatory phase, we also invited nursing experts from relevant fields to provide guidance and actively incorporated their comments and suggestions to refine the general information questionnaire.

The researcher informed participants of the study purpose, procedures, and relevant precautions. For survey respondents who were unable to answer on their own or those with low education levels, the researchers repeated the questions one by one, filled in the responses on the basis of the answers of the survey respondents, and verified the questions once again. After the questionnaires were collected on-site, the investigators carefully checked whether any missing or incorrect items were present in the questionnaire and preliminarily reviewed the reliability of the questionnaire. In cases involving missing or wrong items, the survey respondents were promptly asked to make additions or corrections. Disease information was assessed and supplied by the investigator by querying the hospital information system.

Statistical analysis

All questionnaires were double-entered and analyzed using SPSS 25.0 software. Descriptive statistics were performed to summarize patients’ demographic characteristics and aging self-stereotypes scores. Measurement data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and count data were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The effects of demographic characteristics on aging self-stereotypes scores were analyzed using t tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Multiple linear regression was conducted to identify factors influencing aging self-stereotypes among elderly patients with multimorbidity undergoing elective surgery, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Only variables significant in univariate analysis were entered multiple linear regression, while non-significant variables were retained in the final table as covariates. The aging self-stereotypes score was included as the dependent variable, and variables that showed statistical significance in univariate analyses were entered as independent variables. Prior to multiple linear regression analysis, multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The VIF quantifies the degree of linear dependence between independent variables. A VIF of 1 indicates no multicollinearity; 1 ≤ VIF < 5 suggests mild multicollinearity; VIF ≥ 5 indicates high multicollinearity; and VIF ≥ 10 indicates severe multicollinearity that may destabilize the regression model.

Ethical considerations

This cross-sectional study was approved by the Ethics Committee of a tertiary, university-affiliated teaching hospital in Chongqing, China (Trial No.2024 − 290) in 2024. All survey respondents provided informed consent and participated in this study voluntarily.

Results

Common method bias test

As some of the variable data in this study were derived from participants’ self-reports, there may be a potential risk of common method bias. Therefore, the following measures were taken to mitigate this: firstly, procedural controls were implemented, emphasising anonymity, confidentiality and the restriction of data use to academic research during the data collection process; secondly, the Harman one-way test was employed to conduct exploratory factor analysis on the items of all self-report scales. The results indicated that, prior to rotation, there were 4 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1; the first principal component explained 17.48% of the total variance, which was below the critical threshold of 40%, suggesting that there was no common method bias in this study.

General information of the survey respondents

A total of 197 questionnaires were distributed, and 188 valid questionnaires were collected. The recovery rate of valid questionnaires was 95.43%. Among the 188 older participants, the mean age was 67.14 ± 4.72 years; patients aged 60–65, 66–70, and 71–75 years accounted for 35.11% (n = 66), 37.23% (n = 70), and 27.66% (n = 52) of the study population, respectively. Results of the univariate analysis of aging self-stereotypes among elderly patients with multimorbidity who underwent elective surgery

The univariate analysis revealed that age, the preoperative length of hospital stay, educational level, first-time surgery, Barthel index, CIRS score, FFP, and CPS significantly influenced the aging self-stereotypes of elderly patients who underwent elective surgery (all P < 0.05, Table 3). Among these significant factors, further analyses identified first-time surgery, CIRS score, and CPS as the variables most strongly associated with aging self-stereotypes. Patients undergoing surgery for the first-time had significantly higher aging self-stereotypes scores (27.97 ± 1.89), than those with previous surgery (27.34 ± 1.97) (t = 0.083, P = 0.028). Aging self-stereotypes scores increased with higher CIRS scores. Specifically, patients with a CIRS score > 14 had a significantly higher mean score (28.59 ± 2.27) than those with a CIRS score ≤ 14 (27.54 ± 1.86) (t = 0.014, P = 0.010). Furthermore, patients with an active decision-making had a lower mean aging self-stereotypes score (26.90 ± 1.83) compared with those who preferred a cooperative (27.10 ± 2.11) or passive (27.93 ± 1.89) preference (F = 4.173, P = 0.017).

Results of multiple linear regression analysis of aging self-stereotypes among elderly patients with multimorbidity who underwent elective surgery

The total aging self-stereotypes score of elderly patients with multimorbidity who underwent elective surgeries was used as a dependent variable, and the variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis were used as independent variables; entry criterion was set to 0.05, and the exclusion criterion was set to 0.10. The assignments of the independent variables for the linear regression analysis are listed in Table 4. A multiple linear regression analysis identified the first-time surgery, CIRS score, and active decision-making as relevant factors associated with aging self-stereotypes among elderly patients with multimorbidity who underwent elective surgery. Elder patients undergoing first‑time surgery had a 0.143 point increase in aging self‑stereotype scores (β = 0.143, 95% CI = -0.008 to 1.072, P = 0.034). Elder patients with a higher CIRS score, which means greater multimorbidity burden, had a 0.177 point increase in aging self-stereotype scores (β = 0.177, 95% CI = 0.230 to 1.741, P = 0.011). In addition, the patients with active decision-making had a 0.161 point decrease in aging self-stereotype scores (β = -0.161, 95% CI = -1.900 to -0.134, P = 0.024). The results of this study revealed that the VIFs of all variants were < 5, thus suggesting the absence of multicollinearity. The regression results are presented in Table 5.

Discussion

The level of aging self-stereotypes among elderly patients with multimorbidity who underwent elective surgery was high

The results of this study revealed that the aging self-stereotypes scores of elderly patients with multimorbidity during elective surgery were 27.697 ± 1.953, which were relatively high; in particular, these values were higher than those reported in previous studies [26]. Several factors may be associated with this finding. ① Age, as an uncontrollable objective factor, can lead to a decrease in physical function. In addition, elderly patients may have a reduced ability to actively learn and absorb new knowledge, which may be related to dual physiological and psychological challenges. ② The surgical operation itself is traumatic. Postoperative body function is limited, and patients may be unable to meet their own needs in daily life, including in terms of bathing, toileting, and cleaning, which may contribute to psychological fluctuations. ③ The coexistence of multiple diseases may increase the risk of perioperative complications [27]. The prolongation of hospitalization may be related to negative emotions such as anxiety and loneliness in patients [28], which may be associated with increased negative self-evaluations in terms of physical and mental aspects. ④ Medical conditions and social security vary across regions, and patients cannot receive timely family and social support, which may aggravate the physical and mental burdens faced by patients [29]. From a clinical perspective, this finding suggests that it may be beneficial to routinely assess aging self-stereotypes in elderly patients before elective surgery. Medical staff can identify patients at high aging self-stereotypes risk early and provide targeted health education and psychological support, which is helpful to improve patients’ adaptation to surgery and perioperative quality of life.

In this study, the item“Most old people can be irritating because they tell the same stories over and over again.” scored the highest, and the item “Most elderly people cannot properly take care of their infants.” scored the lowest. The item with the highest score may reflect the elderly patients are most concerned not about physical decline, but about being perceived as a social burden. In other words, they worry that others will perceive them as boring, repetitive, and burdensome. Conversely, the lowest-scoring item may reflect the culturally valued role of grandparents as caregivers in Chinese families. Therefore, interventions targeting aging self-stereotypes should focus on enhancing patients’ perceived social value and communication skills, rather than only emphasizing physical function. During the perioperative period, medical staff could organize experience-sharing support groups in which patients are encouraged to share a meaningful personal experience rather than routine or repetitive topics. Positive feedback can be provided by medical staff or peers. At the same time, patients can be taught communication skills to reduce verbal repetition and enhance social confidence.

The aging self-stereotypes of elderly patients with multimorbidity who have undergone elective surgery are affected by various factors

Elderly patients with multimorbidity who underwent their first elective surgery obtained higher aging self-stereotypes scores

This study revealed that elderly patients with multimorbidity who underwent surgery for the first time had higher self-stereotypes scores (P < 0.05). The potential reasons for this finding may be as follows. First, elderly patients undergoing surgery for the first time may experience role conflict and role stress. In the traditional Chinese family structure, elderly people are generally responsible for caring for their families and children. Given that surgical procedures usually require hospitalization and postoperative trips to the hospital for follow-up care, such as dressing changes and physical therapy, the role of the patient may conflict with other roles. Second, fear of the unknown may increase perceived uncertainty. Although surgery is effective for treating and diagnosing diseases, it may be associated with increased psychological stress in patients [30]. For patients undergoing surgery for the first time, in particular, the lack of prior experience may be associated with psychological stress responses to unknown events, including surgical procedures, postoperative discomfort, and rehabilitation outcomes. In addition, the preoperative waiting period for elective surgery may be related to repeated rumination on adverse consequences, which may contribute to the formation of negative self-labels. Moreover, when performing medical activities, medical staff unconsciously emphasize the risks entailed by age and multimorbidity, thus forcing elderly patients to face their own aging and to develop the subconscious belief that “I am an elderly person in poor health and therefore I need surgery”, thus triggering the patient’s fear and negative evaluation of aging itself.

In clinical practice, first-time surgery may serve as a simple and practical indicator for screening high-risk patients. To improve postoperative rehabilitation outcomes and psychological adaptability in elderly patients, medical staff could focus on the following interventions. First, coordinated education should be provided to patients and their primary caregivers, and patients should be encouraged to actively accept role change and to participate in postoperative rehabilitation activities. Second, preoperative education should be enhanced. During the preoperative visit, medical staff should explain the surgical procedure, anesthesia method, pain management, and expected recovery in clear, easy-to-understand language to reduce fear of the unknown. In the digital era, accessible educational materials are particularly important. For elder patients with lower educational levels, easy-to-understand electronic handbooks or instructional videos may be developed to enhance comprehension. Third, perioperative psychological care should be integrated into routine clinical practice. On the day of surgery, medical staff should proactively communicate with patients upon arrival in the operating room and explain the purpose of the procedure to enhance their sense of safety. After surgery, peer support and experience sharing may be facilitated by inviting patients who have undergone similar procedures. These individuals can provide practical information about surgery, useful resources, appropriate postoperative rehabilitation methods, and detailed perioperative precautions, including preparation of surgery-related items, suitable types and duration of exercise, and dietary plans. These suggestions may effectively reduce patients’ level of aging self-stereotypes.

The higher the CIRS score is, the higher the level of aging self-stereotypes among elderly patients with multimorbidity who underwent elective surgery

The results of this study revealed a significant positive correlation between the CIRS score and the level of aging self-stereotypes among elderly patients with multimorbidity who underwent elective surgery. This finding was consistent with the conclusions of previous studies [31, 32]. In other words, a greater number of multimorbidity is associated with cumulative physical impairments and may contribute to a vicious cycle of negative self-cognition through complex treatment requirements and functional limitations [33]. The intrinsic mechanism may involve the following multiple pathways. First, cumulative physical distress and loss of control. The degree of disease accumulation, as an objective indicator of the severity of the condition, directly reflects the pathophysiological load of the patient. With an increasing number of comorbidities, patients often face more complex combination treatment regimens and longer disease course management cycles, and the risk of adverse reactions and challenges with treatment compliance caused by drug interactions can significantly increase the severity of disease in patients. These factors may be associated with greater uncertainty and a reduced sense of control, which may be related to strengthened negative self-perceptions such as “old age = frail and sickly” [34]. Second, social role loss and diminished social value. Severe multimorbidity limits patients’ participation in family and social activities, weakening their sense of social value and accelerating the formation of aging self-stereotypes [35]. Physical function limitations not only reduce the quality of life of patients but also impair their self-awareness of health and restrict social engagement. When the social connections are reduced, patients cannot receive sufficient health resources and emotional support; this situation not only causes health conditions but also weakens their sense of trust and belonging to the family and the outside world. When patients perceive a gap between their own condition and societal expectations of “healthy aging,” they may internalize this age-based stigma, resulting in intensified self-stereotyping.

Therefore, the following measures are recommended. In the early stage, the screening of patients with moderate to severe multimorbidity should be strengthened, and an interdisciplinary integrated team should be established. This team comprises surgeons, anesthesiologists, geriatricians, clinical pharmacists, dietitians, rehabilitation therapists, and psychological counselors, who provide collaborative assessment and integrated management. A comprehensive perioperative assessment should be performed, including physical function, the burden of comorbidities, and psychological status, to identify aging self-stereotypes risks. Second, a multidisciplinary team should jointly develop a personalized treatment plan, optimize the surgical plan to minimize complications, and provide targeted nutritional support and physical rehabilitation. Third, regular multidisciplinary consultations should be conducted to dynamically adjust intervention according to changes in the patients’ physiological and psychological condition. Finally, comprehensive psychological support should be provided throughout the perioperative period to alleviate negative emotions and break the vicious cycle between physical comorbidities and aging self-stereotypes associated with aging, thereby ultimately improving physical resilience and mental health.

Patients who engaged in active medical decision-making exhibited lower levels of aging self-stereotypes

The results of this study revealed that patients’ medical decision-making expectations were negatively associated with aging self-stereotypes among elderly patients with multimorbidity undergoing elective surgery. The medical decision expectation type refers to the decision preference type exhibited by patients when they are faced with surgical decision-making. The degree of autonomy can be divided into three types: active decision-making, shared decision-making, and passive decision-making. In this context, active decision-making patients exhibited a clear trait advantage. Specifically, neuroimaging studies have revealed that such patients have reduced activation of brain regions such as the insula and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex during the decision-making process [36]. This pattern may be related to differences in individuals’ cognitive and emotional processing of events, which may be associated with more positive emotional experiences and lower levels of negative stereotypes. Studies have shown that patients engaged in active decision-making often actively participate in surgical and medical decision-making [37]. After patients receive positive feedback from medical staff or experiencing successful verification of decision-making ability, their sense of self-efficacy significantly increases [38]. Higher self-efficacy may be related to behavioral confirmation and a positive cycle of effective decision-making, as well as reduced excessive risk perception and avoidance behavior, which are associated with the promotion of active aging in surgical patients.

However, in reality, as a result of limitations pertaining to their health literacy levels and understanding of the effect of treatment, elderly patients may face a magnified sense of disease fragility, difficulty in the integration of treatment information, and obstacles to understanding medical plans in the clinical decision-making process, resulting in reducing medical decision-making efficiency [39]. One study [40]revealed that among the annual deaths in the United States, approximately two-thirds of elderly people did not choose the best treatment option before death because of their lack of good medical decision-making ability. Numerous studies have confirmed that behavioral decision-making is an important influencing factor and predictor variable of aging self-stereotypes [41]. Specifically, more risky decisions made by the target individual may be associated with the activation of negative stereotypes [42]. Conversely, the activation of negative stereotypes may also trigger individuals to exhibit higher loss aversion and greater risk-averse behaviors in decision-making [43]. In clinical practice, health education should be strengthened. Decision support tools for surgical procedures and a digital platform for shared decision-making should be established to effectively promote patient participation in clinical decisions. For example, perioperative booklets, illustrations, and videos can be developed using easy-to-understand language and accessible materials to deliver clear information, enhance medical staff and patients communication, and facilitate high-quality clinical decision-making. Additionally, web-based and mobile applications can serve as effective decision-support tools, such as professional educational websites and programs for predicting postoperative survival. Moreover, medical staff should try their best to ensure that the information provided to patients is objective. In terms of fairness, timely assessment and counseling can reduce the influence of adverse medical experience on medical decision-making and improve the quality of decision-making, which may help reduce aging self-stereotypes.

Limitations and recommendations for future study

However, the present study has several limitations. First, the study population was recruited using convenience sampling at a single tertiary hospital in Chongqing, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the results. This single-center design not only restricts sample representativeness and geographic coverage but also reduces the transferability of findings to other health care settings, especially those with different levels of medical resources (e.g., primary care institutions, non-tertiary hospitals) and distinct regional characteristics. Moreover, the concentration of participants in one region may fail to capture cultural differences and health care system variations across in China, which could further weaken the external validity of the results. Second, aging self-stereotypes were assessed using only a single scale, which may introduce measurement bias and limit the comprehensiveness of the evaluation. Third, several potential variables, such as social support, family care, and other psychosocial and environmental factors, were not included in the analysis. These factors may be associated with the psychological state and aging self-stereotypes of elder patients, and their omission may limit a full and in-depth interpretation of the psychosocial findings. Fourth, this study employed a cross-sectional design, which only permits the identification of correlational relationships rather than causal inferences. Because aging self-stereotypes represent a complex psychological construct, interpretation based solely on cross-sectional data may be overly simplistic. In addition, the cross-sectional design captured levels of aging self-stereotypes only at a single time point and could not reflect dynamic changes over time. To address these limitations, future studies should adopt multicenter sampling with more diverse populations to improve representativeness and generalizability. Using multiple scales or mixed-methods approaches to measure aging self-stereotypes may help reduce measurement bias. Furthermore, including potential variables such as social support and family care in future analyses will help clarify the true relationships among variables. Longitudinal or interventional designs are also recommended to explore the dynamic trajectory, potential causal pathways, and underlying mechanisms of aging self-stereotypes among elder patients with multimorbidity undergoing elective surgery.

Conclusions

Aging self-stereotypes are important factors that affect the psychosocial and health of elderly people. Aging self-stereotypes can induce higher levels of depression and anxiety and lower subjective well-being while accelerating cognitive decline and increasing the physiological load. This study confirmed that elderly patients undergoing surgery generally exhibit a tendency toward aging self-stereotypes and highlighted the combined effects of multimorbidity, surgical stress and behavioral decision-making on mental health, thus providing a clinical basis for understanding the mechanism underlying the formation of aging self-stereotypes. On the basis of the findings presented above, overcoming the traditional single-disease management framework in clinical care is recommended to establish a multidisciplinary collaborative comorbidity management model and an empowerment mechanism that can enable family caregivers to form a hospital-family linkage system with the goal of systematically promoting the quality of life of elderly patients with multimorbidity undergoing surgery.
