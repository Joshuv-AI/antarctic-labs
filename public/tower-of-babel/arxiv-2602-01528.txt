Making Bias Non-Predictive: Training Robust LLM Judges via Reinforcement Learning
Qian Wang, Xuandong Zhao, Zirui Zhang, Zhanzhi Lou, Nuo Chen, Dawn Song, Bingsheng He

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) increasingly serve as automated judges, yet they remain susceptible to cognitive biases—often altering their reasoning when faced with spurious prompt-level cues such as consensus claims or authority appeals. Existing mitigations via prompting or supervised fine-tuning fail to generalize, as they modify surface behavior without changing the optimization objective that makes bias cues predictive. To address this gap, we propose Epistemic Independence Training (EIT), a reinforcement learning framework grounded in a key principle: to learn independence, bias cues must be made non-predictive of reward. EIT operationalizes this through a balanced conflict strategy where bias signals are equally likely to support correct and incorrect answers, combined with a reward design that penalizes bias-following without rewarding bias agreement. Experiments on Qwen3-4B demonstrate that EIT improves both accuracy and robustness under adversarial biases, while preserving performance when bias aligns with truth. Notably, models trained only on bandwagon bias generalize to unseen bias types such as authority and distraction, indicating that EIT induces transferable epistemic independence rather than bias-specific heuristics. Code and data are available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/bias-mitigation-with-rl-BC47 .

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as automated evaluators across a wide range of tasks, a paradigm commonly referred to as LLM-as-a-Judge
(
9
;
19
)
. Recent advances in large reasoning models (LRMs), such as OpenAI-o1
(
25
)
and DeepSeek-R1
(
10
)
, further strengthen this paradigm by equipping models with explicit reasoning mechanisms, including Chain-of-Thought
(
46
)
and self-reflection
(
23
)
. These models achieve strong performance on complex domains ranging from mathematics to code generation
(
14
;
44
)
.

Despite these reasoning capabilities, LLM judges remain highly sensitive to prompt-level cues unrelated to task correctness. Prior work documents such cognitive biases in LLM judgments, including bandwagon bias and authority bias
(
16
;
55
)
. Figure
1
illustrates this vulnerability: when asked whether the Great Wall of China is visible from space, an advanced reasoning model, OpenAI-o1, arrives at the correct answer under a clean prompt but reverses its decision when presented with a fabricated consensus—even though the added information is non-causal and irrelevant to the underlying question.

Figure 1:
The fragility of LLM judgment under bandwagon bias.
Left:
In a clean setting, OpenAI-o1 correctly identifies that the Great Wall of China is not visible from space with the naked eye.
Right:
When exposed to
bandwagon bias
(a fabricated consensus claiming visibility), the same model succumbs to social pressure and reverses its correct judgment.

These cognitive biases mirror well-studied phenomena in human decision-making, where social signals systematically influence judgments
(
16
)
. Critically, the issue is not that LLMs lack reasoning ability—the model in Figure
1
demonstrates sound reasoning under clean conditions. Rather, the problem is that prompt-level cues can systematically alter the model’s reasoning trajectory and final decision. Motivated by the concept of epistemic dependence in social epistemology
(
29
)
, we characterize this as a failure of
epistemic independence
: the ability to make judgments invariant to spurious prompt-level signals, relying instead on task-relevant reasoning.

This dependence arises because, during pretraining and alignment, cues such as consensus statements or authoritative language often correlate with positive feedback, making them attractive shortcuts
(
22
;
43
)
. Existing approaches, such as prompting
(
55
;
40
)
and supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
(
50
)
, leave this dependency unaddressed as they do not fundamentally alter the optimization objective that incentivizes reliance on these cues.

To address this gap, we introduce Epistemic Independence Training (EIT), a reinforcement learning framework that operationalizes this insight through two components. First, a
balanced conflict strategy
: each bias signal supports the correct answer in 50% of samples and the incorrect answer in the remaining 50%, rendering the cue-correctness association statistically neutral. Second, a
reward design that combines accuracy with a bias penalty
: the model is rewarded for correct answers and penalized for following bias to wrong answers, while receiving no extra credit when bias happens to align with the truth. We optimize the model using Group Relative Policy Optimization
(
31
)
, reinforcing responses that demonstrate both correctness and independence.

We train EIT on Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B using MMLU-Pro
(
42
)
with only bandwagon bias, evaluating on both in-domain (bandwagon) and out-of-domain bias types (authority, distraction, position). Experiments show that EIT improves adversarial-bias accuracy by 13.2% (from 70.1% to 83.3%) and robustness by 16.4% on Qwen3-4B, while maintaining strong performance when bias aligns with the correct answer. EIT also generalizes to unseen bias types—distraction robustness improves by 39.1% despite never being seen during training. Notably, EIT-trained Qwen3-4B outperforms larger untrained models (Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B) on bias resistance, demonstrating that targeted training is more effective than model scaling alone. Further analysis of reasoning traces reveals that this performance is not merely superficial: while standard SFT often leads to “performative independence”—mimicking refusal language without underlying computation—EIT induces a qualitative shift toward substantive reasoning, characterized by explicit domain engagement and logical verification prior to overriding bias.

Contributions.
First, we formulate epistemic independence as a reinforcement learning problem in which spurious cues must be rendered non-predictive of reward, providing a principled framework for bias mitigation in LLM judges. Second, we propose a balanced conflict strategy and a bias-penalizing reward design that jointly prevent shortcut learning and force reliance on task-relevant reasoning. Third, we demonstrate that EIT improves both accuracy and robustness, generalizes to unseen bias types, outperforms larger untrained models on bias resistance, and produces genuine epistemic independence rather than surface-level imitation through comprehensive experiments.

Related Work

Due to page constraints, we present only the most relevant prior work here. Additional related work are in Appendix
A
.

Cognitive Biases in LLM-as-a-Judge.

LLM-as-a-Judge has emerged as a scalable alternative to human evaluation
(
59
)
, yet its reliability is undermined by cognitive biases: systematic deviations where models alter judgments based on external cues rather than factual reasoning
(
16
)
. These biases include social influence biases such as bandwagon bias (conforming to majority opinion) and authority bias (deferring to perceived experts)
(
27
)
, as well as process biases related to superficial features like position or length
(
41
;
58
)
. Critically, standard RLHF training often exacerbates these vulnerabilities by incentivizing sycophancy—the tendency to prioritize user agreement over epistemic truth
(
32
)
.

Inference-Time Bias Mitigation Approaches.

Existing inference-time approaches attempt to mitigate bias without modifying model weights.
Prompt engineering
instructs models to ignore external cues, but fails against strong bias injections because models lack intrinsic incentive to follow such instructions
(
59
)
.
Activation steering

(
63
)
manipulates latent representations to encourage truthfulness, but requires identifying appropriate steering vectors for each bias type and does not generalize to unseen biases.
Modular detectors

(
50
)
identify biased evaluations externally and prompt self-correction, but add inference overhead and depend on detector quality rather than improving the judge model itself. These approaches share a common limitation: they treat symptoms rather than causes, leaving the model’s underlying susceptibility unchanged.

Training-Time Bias Mitigation Approaches.

Training-based approaches aim to produce inherently robust models. SFT on debiasing datasets
(
47
)
teaches models to produce unbiased outputs, but tends to learn surface patterns rather than genuine reasoning, leading to poor generalization on unseen bias types
(
12
)
. Our EIT leverages reinforcement learning with balanced conflict data and bias-penalizing reward design to make bias signals non-predictive of reward. A detailed comparison of existing approaches and EIT is in Table
5
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the
first work to utilize reinforcement learning to mitigate cognitive biases in LLM judges, explicitly optimizing for epistemic independence rather than surface-level imitation.

3.1
Design Rationale

Cognitive biases arise because LLMs learn to exploit spurious correlations—such as “agreeing with the prompt”—rather than engaging in intrinsic reasoning
(
32
)
. To dismantle this dependency, we introduce a
balanced conflict environment
where the injected bias cue supports the correct answer in exactly 50% of cases and an incorrect answer in the other 50%. In this setting, the bias signal becomes statistically orthogonal to the truth: any policy that relies on the cue will fail to maximize expected return, forcing the model to abandon the shortcut and revert to intrinsic reasoning.

We employ RL to solve this optimization problem. Unlike SFT, which often encourages models to mimic the
surface form
of independence (e.g., memorizing refusal phrases), RL directly optimizes for the
functional outcome
of accuracy. This allows the model to explore and discover robust reasoning strategies that persist even under pressure.

3.2
Bias Injection

We inject biases into MMLU-Pro
(
42
)
prompts, training on one bias type and evaluating generalization across an OOD spectrum.
Bandwagon bias
serves as our training signal, simulating social consensus (e.g., “90% of people choose X”) with the conflict strategy. For evaluation, we test three additional biases:
authority bias
adds expert claims (“Research shows X is correct”),
distraction bias
introduces irrelevant information, and
position bias
swaps option order. The first two are semantic biases related to social influence; position bias is structural, testing whether transfer extends beyond semantic cues. See Appendix
B
for full examples.

3.3
EIT Algorithm Overview

Figure
2
illustrates the EIT framework. During training, we inject bandwagon bias using the conflict strategy (50% correct-bias, 50% wrong-bias). The policy model
\pi_{\theta}
generates multiple candidate responses, which are evaluated by our hierarchical reward function combining
\mathcal{R}_{\text{acc}}
,
\mathcal{R}_{\text{struct}}
, and the asymmetric
\mathcal{R}_{\text{ind}}
. GRPO updates the policy to maximize expected reward. At test time, the trained model
\pi^{*}
is evaluated on both in-domain (bandwagon) and out-of-domain biases (authority, position, distraction) to assess generalization of epistemic independence.

3.4
Epistemic Reward Shaping

We design a hierarchical reward
R=\mathcal{R}_{\text{struct}}+\mathcal{R}_{\text{acc}}+\mathcal{R}_{\text{ind}}
where each component prevents a specific failure mode: structural constraint blocks gaming via unparsable outputs, accuracy prevents random contrarianism, and independence prevents the “always follow or reject bias” shortcut. Hyperparameter are in Appendix
C
.

Structural Constraint.

The model receives
\mathcal{R}_{\text{struct}}=\alpha
only if the response follows Chain-of-Thought format:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{struct}}(y)=\alpha\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\text{CoT}}(y)

(1)

This grounds exploration by ensuring parsable reasoning paths before evaluating accuracy.

Factual Accuracy.

We assign a positive reward for correct answers:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{acc}}(y,y^{*})=\mathbb{I}(y=y^{*})

(2)

This prevents “independence” from degenerating into random contrarianism—the model must know what is true to be meaningfully independent.

Independence Incentive.

This component decouples reasoning from social influence via context-dependent incentives. In
adversarial context
(
b\neq y^{*}
), when bias contradicts truth:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{ind}}^{\text{adv}}(y,y^{*},b)=\begin{cases}+\gamma_{1},&\text{if }y=y^{*}~~\text{(Robustness Bonus)}\\
-\gamma_{1},&\text{if }y=b\quad\text{(Bias Penalty)}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(3)

The explicit penalty makes bias-following a high-cost action. In
supportive context
(
b=y^{*}
), when bias aligns with truth:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{ind}}^{\text{supp}}(y,y^{*},b)=\begin{cases}-\gamma_{2},&\text{if }y\neq y^{*}\quad\text{(Contrarian Penalty)}\\
0,&\text{if }y=y^{*}\quad\text{(Zero Marginal Gain)}\end{cases}

(4)

Crucially, there is no bonus for being correct when bias is also correct. If we rewarded agreement here, the model could learn “always follow bias”. By providing zero marginal gain in supportive contexts and penalties in adversarial contexts, the optimal policy must ignore
b
entirely.

3.5
Optimization Objective

We optimize the EIT framework using GRPO. For each input
x
, we sample a group of
G
outputs
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{G}\}
from the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
. The optimization objective is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y\sim\pi_{\theta}}\bigg[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}

\displaystyle\left(R(y_{i})-\bar{R}_{G}\right)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}|x)

\displaystyle-\beta\text{KL}(\pi_{\theta}||\pi_{\text{ref}})\bigg],

(5)

where
\bar{R}_{G}
is the group average reward used as a dynamic baseline to reduce variance. This objective encourages the policy to shift probability mass toward responses that demonstrate both high accuracy and epistemic independence relative to the group mean.

4
Experiments

We address four key questions: (1) Does EIT improve both accuracy and robustness across diverse bias types? (2) Does resistance learned from one bias type transfer to unseen biases? (3) How does EIT compare against prompting-based and supervised fine-tuning approaches? (4) Can model scaling alone achieve similar robustness, or is targeted training necessary? We also conduct ablation studies to validate our conflict data strategy and reward design.

Evaluation Protocol.
All evaluations use a pairwise choice setting with two options (one correct, one incorrect). For each sample, we generate both a clean version and a bias-injected version, enabling direct comparison.

Metrics.
We report two metrics. Accuracy measures the fraction of correct answers:

\text{Accuracy}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\left[\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}\right],

where

\text{Robustness Rate}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\left[\hat{y}^{\text{clean}}_{i}=\hat{y}^{\text{bias}}_{i}\right],

(7)

where
\hat{y}^{\text{clean}}_{i}
and
\hat{y}^{\text{bias}}_{i}
denote choices under clean and bias-injected prompts, respectively.

and

present results on four bias types: bandwagon (in-domain), authority, distraction, and position (OOD). For bandwagon, authority, and distraction, we evaluate both correct-bias (C: bias points to correct answer) and wrong-bias (W: bias points to wrong answer) scenarios. For position bias, we evaluate when the correct answer appears in first (A) or second (B) position. We report accuracy (Acc) and robustness rate (RR), with key findings below.

EIT improves both accuracy and robustness simultaneously.
EIT achieves the best or second-best performance in the majority of metrics across both model sizes. The improvements are particularly striking on the Qwen3-4B test set: for Distraction Bias, accuracy under wrong-bias (Acc
W
) jumps from 45.9% to 85.0%, and robustness (RR
W
) improves from 40.6% to 79.7%. Similarly, for the in-domain Bandwagon Bias (test set), Acc
W
improves from 70.1% to 83.3%, with robustness rising from 63.6% to 80.0%. These gains suggest that resisting bias is fundamentally about grounding decisions in factual reasoning, which benefits both accuracy and robustness.

Bias resistance transfers to semantic biases but not structural biases.
Although EIT is trained solely on Bandwagon Bias, robustness improvements generalize effectively to other semantic biases on the Qwen3-4B test set. Distraction Bias shows the strongest transfer, with wrong-bias robustness (RR
W
) jumping from 40.6% to 79.7%, while Authority Bias robustness improves from 68.5% to 72.9%. In contrast, the structural Position Bias shows minimal transfer: on the test set, robustness for second-position answers (Position-B) slightly decreases from 38.6% to 36.4%. This distinction suggests that EIT teaches models to selectively discount misleading semantic cues, while leaving structural vulnerabilities like position bias unaddressed.

EIT outperforms both prompting and supervised approaches.
Prompt-mitigation often hurts accuracy: Qwen3-4B clean accuracy on validation drops from 66.2% to 40.9% on validation set. SFT is inconsistent across model sizes: Qwen3-1.7B clean accuracy on test even drops from 84.3% to 52.1%. Critically, SFT fails to generalize to unseen biases: on the Qwen3-4B, SFT’s robustness against authority bias (under wrong-bias conditions) stagnates at 68.8% (vs. Baseline 68.5%) on the test set, whereas EIT improves it to 72.9%. This contrast highlights that SFT overfits to the surface patterns of the training bias (Bandwagon), while EIT learns a generalized independence capability also effective for other biases.

4.4
Comparison with Larger Models

A natural question is whether epistemic independence can be achieved simply by scaling model size. We compare EIT-trained Qwen3-4B against larger Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B
(
37
)
without EIT training on the test set.

Figure 4:
Comparison of Qwen3-4B+EIT against larger Qwen3 models under bias conditions on the MMLU-Pro test set. Despite having fewer parameters, EIT-trained Qwen3-4B outperforms both Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B under wrong-bias settings, demonstrating that targeted training is more effective than model scaling for bias robustness.

Figure
4
shows that model scaling alone does not solve the bias susceptibility problem. While larger models achieve moderately higher clean accuracy, their accuracy under adversarial bias remains limited. Under wrong-bias bandwagon, Qwen3-8B achieves 72.5% and Qwen3-14B achieves 75.2%, substantially below EIT-trained Qwen3-4B at 83.3%. The pattern also holds for authority bias.

4.5
Ablation Studies

We conduct ablation studies to validate two key design choices: (1) the conflict data strategy (Figure
5
); (2) the reward design (Figure
6
). We have following findings:

Figure 5:
Impact of Training Data Composition. Comparison of our balanced
Conflict
strategy (50/50 correct/wrong bias) versus a
Wrong-Only
strategy (training solely on samples where bias predicts the wrong answer). While Wrong-Only artificially boosts performance when the bias is misleading (Wrong-Bias), it causes a catastrophic drop when the bias happens to be correct (Correct-Bias), indicating the model learned a simple “reverse-bias” shortcut. In contrast, EIT’s Conflict strategy maintains high robustness across both scenarios.

Figure 6:
Impact of Reward Components. We evaluate the impact of each component by removing the Structure Reward and the Independence Reward individually. Additionally, we assess a baseline trained solely on the Accuracy Reward.

Balanced conflict data prevents the model from just “reversing” the bias.

As shown in Figure
5
, we compare EIT with a “Wrong-Only” baseline. The Wrong-Only model achieves high robustness when the bias is misleading. However, its performance on Correct-Bias data drops by about 20% compared to the baseline. This “inverted” pattern suggests the model learns a simple shortcut: it just opposes whatever the social signal says. In contrast, our 50/50 Conflict strategy forces the model to ignore the bias direction effectively. This leads to balanced performance, maintaining high accuracy regardless of whether the bias is right or wrong.

The Independence Reward is key to success.

In Figure
6
, we analyze the impact of different reward terms. Removing the Independence Reward causes robustness against wrong-bias samples to fall by roughly 15%. This confirms that standard accuracy rewards are not enough; the model needs explicit negative feedback when it blindly follows external cues. The Structure Reward also provides a moderate improvement, showing that encouraging a clear reasoning format helps stability.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced Epistemic Independence Training (EIT), a reinforcement learning framework designed to mitigate cognitive biases in LLM judges. By leveraging a balanced conflict strategy and a bias-penalizing reward structure, EIT fundamentally alters the optimization landscape, rendering spurious social signals
non-predictive
of reward. Our experiments on Qwen3 models demonstrate that EIT significantly improves accuracy and robustness against both in-domain bandwagon bias and out-of-domain biases like authority and distraction, often outperforming larger untrained models. Crucially, qualitative analysis reveals that EIT transcends the
performative independence
observed in SFT baselines, instead cultivating
substantive reasoning
behaviors where models explicitly verify facts before rejecting bias. Future work could extend EIT to more complex, open-ended generation tasks and explore its potential in mitigating subtler forms of implicit bias in other reasoning domains.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work aimed at improving the reliability and robustness of LLM-based evaluation systems. We see several positive societal implications:

Positive Impacts.
As LLMs are increasingly deployed as automated judges and evaluators, their susceptibility to cognitive biases poses risks of unfair or manipulated assessments. Our work contributes to making these systems more trustworthy by training models to resist social influence cues (consensus claims, authority appeals) that could be exploited by adversarial actors. More robust LLM judges benefit applications ranging from content moderation to educational assessment.

Potential Concerns.
We do not foresee significant negative applications of this work. The techniques we develop are defensive in nature—they make models harder to manipulate rather than easier. Understanding how biases operate (as revealed in our qualitative analysis) could theoretically inform adversarial strategies, but this knowledge is already well-documented in the cognitive bias literature.

Limitations.
Our method was evaluated on English-language benchmarks with specific bias types. Broader deployment would require validation across languages, cultures, and bias categories to ensure the approach generalizes without unintended consequences.

E.9
Summary of Reasoning Patterns

These eight case studies reveal three distinct cognitive patterns under bias pressure:

SFT Pattern: Performative Independence

Signature:
Independence language
\rightarrow
no actual reasoning
\rightarrow
wrong answer

Example markers:
“I should not let popular opinion influence my judgment” [followed by zero domain analysis], “After careful consideration” [with no calculation shown]

Root cause:
SFT learns
what independence looks like
(the language, the structure) from demonstrations, but because its training signal is imitation rather than outcome-based, it learns to
mimic appearance
without developing underlying capability.

EIT Pattern: Genuine Epistemic Independence

Signature:
Domain knowledge retrieval
\rightarrow
explicit verification
\rightarrow
bias acknowledged but rejected based on reasoning

Example markers:
“However, according to my reasoning…”, “The physics clearly indicates…”, “Let me verify: [calculation]…”, “The mention of [distraction] is irrelevant…”

Root cause:
Conflict training makes bias signals non-predictive of reward. The only path to reward is genuine reasoning, forcing development of actual epistemic independence rather than surface-level heuristics.

Key Insight:
The SFT “performative independence” pattern is particularly instructive for understanding the difference between imitation learning and reinforcement learning. SFT models learn from demonstrations that show
what independent reasoning looks like
, but because their training signal rewards matching the demonstration rather than reaching correct conclusions, they can perfectly reproduce the
form
of independence while lacking its
substance
. EIT’s conflict strategy prevents this failure mode by ensuring that form without substance yields no reward.
