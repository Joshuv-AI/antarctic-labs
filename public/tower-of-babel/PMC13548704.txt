Prognostic nutritional index as a predictor of ursodeoxycholic acid response in primary biliary cholangitis: a retrospective study
Authors: Mengyao Zheng, Hongtao Lei, Haiyu He, Yaqin Huang, Huiling Zhu, Huiying Lin, Wen Fu, Wenlin Tai, Yan Wang, Jinhui Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC) is an autoimmune hepatic disorder characterized by the progressive destruction of intrahepatic bile ducts. Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) remains the primary treatment modality; however, a subset of patients exhibits non-responsiveness to UDCA therapy. The objective of this study is to investigate the association between the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) and UDCA treatment non-responsiveness in individuals diagnosed with PBC.MethodsThis retrospective study encompassed PBC patients who received UDCA therapy (13–15 mg/kg) from June 1, 2014, to December 31, 2021. The criterion for UDCA non-response was defined as an alkaline phosphatase (ALP) level exceeding 1.67 times the upper limit of normal (ULN) after 12 months of UDCA therapy. Logistic regression analysis was utilized to examine the relationship between baseline PNI and response to UDCA. The stableness of the findings was assessed through both unadjusted and adjusted models.ResultsThe study included 241 patients with PBC (mean age 55.80 ± 11.694 years; 210 (87.1%) females). During the 12-month clinical follow-up, 83 patients exhibited non-responsiveness to UDCA, corresponding to a non-response rate of 34.44%. Univariate binary logistic regression analysis indicated that factors such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), total bile acid, total cholesterol, neutrophils, and PNI were associated with UDCA non-responsiveness (p < 0.05). Multivariate regression analysis showed that lower baseline PNI was associated with UDCA non-response (odds ratio [OR]: 0.882, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.825–0.943, p < 0.001). PNI showed modest discriminatory ability for UDCA non-response, with an AUROC of 0.664 in the overall cohort and 0.676 in non-cirrhotic patients. Based on the Youden index, the optimal PNI cut-off value was 49.03.ConclusionsLower baseline PNI was associated with inadequate biochemical response to UDCA in patients with PBC and may serve as a supportive risk stratification marker. However, its modest discriminatory performance and retrospective design limit its standalone clinical application. Further prospective validation is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05040-9.

Background

Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC) is a chronic intrahepatic cholestatic condition characterized by progressive, non-suppurative, and destructive inflammation of the intrahepatic bile ducts. This inflammation can lead to liver fibrosis, potentially culminating in cirrhosis. Clinical presentations often include fatigue, pruritus, and asymptomatic elevations in serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT). Anti-mitochondrial antibodies (AMA), particularly the M2 subtype (AMA-M2), are highly sensitive and specific for PBC [1, 2]. Currently, Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) serves as the primary first-line treatment for PBC. The global incidence and prevalence of PBC are 17.6 per million persons per year and 146 per million people, respectively [3]. However, approximately 30–40% of patients with PBC show an inadequate biochemical response to UDCA, which is known to affect clinical outcomes [4, 5]. Therefore, predicting the biochemical response to UDCA is vital for managing PBC and improving patient outcomes. Ongoing research efforts are directed toward identifying new biomarkers that can predict responsiveness to UDCA [6, 7]. Identifying specific and predictive biomarkers is essential for the tailored selection of treatment based on responsiveness to UDCA, aiming to halt disease progression and avert adverse outcomes.

Extensive research has established a correlation between nutritional status and the prognosis of chronic liver disease [8, 9]. The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), which utilises serum albumin levels and peripheral blood lymphocyte counts [10], is employed to assess the nutritional and immune status of patients with chronic liver diseases [11]. In cirrhotic patients, PNI reflects overall health status and can predict disease progression [12]. For liver cancer patients, PNI is used to evaluate surgical tolerance, treatment response, and prognosis [13, 14]. Similarly, in liver transplantation, PNI serves as an indicator for assessing surgical risks and patient prognosis [14]. Considering that PBC is an autoimmune liver disease, the immune function of patients is critical for the response to UDCA. Lymphocyte levels are a key indicator of immune function, with studies affirming their association with UDCA response [15–17].

Notably, research exploring the relationship between PNI levels and UDCA response in PBC patients is lacking. Hence, this study is designed to investigate the predictive value of PNI for UDCA response in PBC patients. The findings of this study may aid in the development of new prognostic indicators for UDCA responsiveness in PBC patients, thereby offering insights into optimising treatment strategies for this condition.

Methods

Between June 1, 2014 and December 31, 2021, patients diagnosed with PBC at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University were retrospectively enrolled in this study. Data including patients’ general information, comorbidities, liver cirrhosis, blood cell counts, immunological tests, liver function, blood lipids, and other laboratory results were retrospectively collated. The comorbidities include metabolic associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), type 2 diabetes(T2DM), hypertension, Sjögren’s syndrome, thyroid disorders and dyslipidemia. Among them, MAFLD is characterized by imaging findings (including ultrasound, CT, MRI, etc.) suggesting hepatic steatosis, with exclusion of other diseases causing fatty liver such as alcoholic liver disease, drug-induced liver injury, viral hepatitis, etc. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients lacking initial diagnosis data; (2) Patients with hepatitis B or C virus, primary sclerosing cholangitis, Budd-Chiari syndrome, or autoimmune hepatitis; (3) Patients who did not consistently take UDCA at a dosage of 13–15 mg/kg/day; (4) Patients with incomplete data during a minimum of 12 months follow-up. (5) Patients with significant alcohol consumption (> 40 g/day for men or > 20 g/day for women) or those with diagnosed alcoholic liver disease. The primary outcome of this retrospective cohort study was the biochemical response to UDCA.

The methodologies implemented in this study comply with the ethical standards outlined in the Helsinki Declaration and have received ethical clearance from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (YJ-2023-96). As this study involves the routine collection of existing clinical data, the requirement for informed consent from patients was deemed unnecessary. This retrospective cohort study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for observational studies.

PBC and UDCA response

For the diagnosis of PBC and the assessment of UDCA response, the study adheres to the criteria set by the Asia-Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver (APASL) clinical practice guidelines [1]. A diagnosis of PBC is established when two or more of the following criteria are met: (1) Elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) levels without extrahepatic biliary obstruction; (2) Positive anti-mitochondrial antibodies (AMA) or other PBC-specific antinuclear antibodies (ANAs), such as anti-sp100 or anti-gp210; (3) Liver histology confirming nonsuppurative destructive cholangitis, primarily involving interlobular bile ducts.

The standard for UDCA response is defined as an ALP level less than or equal to 1.67 times the upper limit of normal (ULN) after 12 months of UDCA treatment [18].

PNI

The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) is calculated using serum albumin levels and peripheral blood lymphocyte count. The formula for PNI involves adding the albumin value to five times the lymphocyte count.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 software, with the significance level established at 0.05. Variables conforming to a normal distribution are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and those deviating from a normal distribution are expressed as the median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical qualitative variables are represented by frequencies and percentages. For data that were normally distributed and homoscedastic, group comparisons were undertaken using the independent samples t-test. In contrast, groups with non-normally distributed data were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Comparative analyses of categorical variables were performed using the Chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Logistic regression analysis was employed to assess the specific relationship between PNI and the outcome (UDCA response), utilizing odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) to evaluate the associated risk. Model A was adjusted for sex, age, and BMI. Model B was adjusted for sex, age, BMI, MAFLD, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, Sjogren’s syndrome, thyroid disease, liver cirrhosis, pruritus and fatigue. Model C was adjusted for sex, age, BMI, MAFLD, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, Sjogren’s syndrome, thyroid disease, liver cirrhosis, pruritus and fatigue, sp100, gp210, ALP, GLB, ALT, AST, GGT, total bile acid, total bilirubin, triglycerides, total cholesterol, neutrophils and PNI. Given the limited number of UDCA non-responders and the number of covariates included, Model C was considered an exploratory fully adjusted model, and its results were interpreted with caution because of the potential risk of overfitting and model instability. We utilized the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve (AUC) to assess the performance of the PNI in predicting the non-response of PBC patients to UDCA treatment.

Results

A total of 860 patients were evaluated for eligibility within the study timeframe (Fig. 1). Of these, 63 patients lacking initial diagnosis data were excluded. Furthermore, 499 patients were excluded due to hepatitis B or C virus, primary sclerosing cholangitis, Budd-Chiari syndrome, or autoimmune hepatitis. Additionally, 8 patients who did not regularly take UDCA at a dosage of 13–15 mg/kg/day were excluded, along with 49 patients who had incomplete data for a minimum of 12 months of follow-up.

Within the cohort of 241 patients, 210 (87.1%) were female, with an average age of 55.80 ± 11.694 years. The median BMI was 22.27 kg/m^2 (Interquartile Range [IQR] 20.38–24.20). The study identified several comorbidities, including fatty liver in 15 patients (6.2%), type 2 diabetes in 16 patients (6.6%), hypertension in 26 patients (10.8%), Sjogren’s syndrome in 19 patients (7.9%), thyroid disease in 49 patients (20.3%), liver cirrhosis in 77 patients (32.0%) and dyslipidemia in 100 patients (41.8%). A notable finding was that 18 patients (7.5%) reported experiencing pruritus, while 35 patients (14.5%) reported experiencing fatigue. Additionally, 23 patients (9.5%) were positive for sp100, and 59 patients (24.5%) tested positive for gp210 (Table 1).

Of the total cohort, 158 patients demonstrated a positive response to UDCA, whereas 83 did not respond. Among those who did not respond to UDCA, a lower BMI (21.64 versus 22.66) and a lower incidence of thyroid disease (13.3% versus 24.1%) were observed compared to responders. Non-responders, compared to responders, also exhibited elevated levels of ALP, ALT, AST, GGT, total bile acid, total bilirubin, total cholesterol, and neutrophils, and decreased levels of albumin, lymphocytes, and PNI (Table 1).

Univariate binary logistic regression analysis revealed significant associations between various factors and UDCA nonresponse (p < 0.05), including ALP, GGT, total bile acid, total cholesterol, neutrophils, and PNI (Table 2).

In the multivariate binary logistic regression analysis, three models were formulated, each adjusting for a distinct set of covariates. Model A, adjusted for sex, age, and BMI, identified lower PNI as an independent predictor of UDCA nonresponse (OR: 0.923, 95% CI: 0.890–0.956, P < 0.001). Model B, further adjusted for MAFLD, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, Sjogren’s syndrome, thyroid disease, liver cirrhosis, pruritus and fatigue, corroborated the significance of lower PNI in predicting nonresponse to UDCA (OR: 0.917, 95% CI: 0.880–0.955, P < 0.001). In the exploratory fully adjusted Model C, including a comprehensive range of covariates such as anti-sp100, anti-gp210, ALP, GLB, ALT, AST, GGT, total bile acid, total bilirubin, triglycerides, total cholesterol, neutrophils, and PNI, lower PNI remained associated with UDCA non-response (OR: 0.882, 95% CI: 0.825–0.943, P < 0.001), although this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the relatively large number of covariates included in the model and the limited number of non-response events (Table 2).

Albumin and PNI showed limited-to-moderate discriminatory ability for differentiating UDCA responders from non-responders in patients with PBC. In the overall cohort, the AUROC was 0.678(95% CI: 0.555–0.806) for albumin and 0.664 for PNI (95% CI: 0.5956–0.7389) (Fig. 2A). Based on the Youden index, the optimal cut-off value of PNI for identifying UDCA non-response was 49.03. To further explore whether the discriminatory performance of PNI differed according to cirrhosis status, subgroup ROC analyses were performed according to cirrhosis status (Fig. 2B and C). Among patients with cirrhosis, albumin showed a higher AUROC 0.674 than PNI 0.624, although the discriminatory performance of both markers remained limited (Fig. 2B). Among patients without cirrhosis, PNI showed a modest AUROC of 0.676, with no statistically significant difference compared with albumin (Fig. 2C). Overall, these findings suggest that lower PNI may be associated with inadequate UDCA response, particularly in non-cirrhotic patients. However, the modest AUROC values indicate that PNI alone is insufficient for clinical decision-making and should be interpreted as a supportive risk stratification marker rather than a standalone predictive tool.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the influence of the PNI on the response of patients with PBC to UDCA. Our results indicate that a lower baseline PNI is independently associated with a suboptimal response to UDCA. This investigation is the first to document the correlation between a low PNI at the onset of treatment and an increased risk of inadequate response to UDCA.

Currently, UDCA remains the sole first-line pharmacological option for treating PBC. However, a subset of patients demonstrates limited responsiveness, significantly impacting clinical outcomes. Early identification of PBC patients who do not respond to UDCA, and timely switching to the second-line medication, Obeticholic acid, may potentially improve the prognosis of some UDCA non-responders with PBC [1, 19]. The determinants of response to UDCA in PBC patients, however, are not fully understood. Various factors have been identified as influencing UDCA responsiveness, including parameters such as sex, ALT, AST, GGT, ALP, albumin, bilirubin levels, and IgG [20–23].Research has found that patients who do not respond to UDCA exhibit the higher levels of alkaline phosphatase, elevated total bilirubin, lower levels of transaminases, younger age, treatment time delay, and worsening alkaline phosphatase, and these indicators have been used to establish a predictive model; some researchers have suggested that the best-fit model includes baseline albumin and platelet count, and bilirubin, aminotransferase, and alkaline phosphatase after 12 months of UDCA treatment [24, 25]. While some of these indicators have been recognized as criteria for defining incomplete responsiveness to UDCA treatment, the practical clinical application of these biomarkers has yielded inconsistent results [6].

The PNI is calculated using the formula: 5 × lymphocyte count (10^9/L) + serum albumin (g/L). This index integrates both albumin and lymphocyte count as markers of nutritional and immune status, respectively. In patients with chronic liver disease, nutritional issues are prevalent, largely due to the liver’s pivotal role in nutrient digestion, absorption, storage, synthesis, and metabolism [26]. Malnutrition is associated with increased risks of complications such as hepatic encephalopathy, ascites, infections, and liver failure. The critical importance of nutrition in liver disease contexts is well-recognized [27]. Serum albumin, produced by the liver, can diminish in conditions where liver functionality is impaired. In PBC, a chronic liver disease marked by bile duct inflammation and damage, liver function may decline, potentially impacting serum albumin levels. Furthermore, the autoimmune response is vital in PBC pathogenesis. Lymphocytes are closely related to the primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) disease since it is one kind of autoimmune disease where destruction to the bile ducts is evident, mainly by the abnormal activity of lymphocytes. Recent studies have demonstrated that autoreactive T lymphocytes may be at the center of the pathogenic process seen in PBC. Further progress in this field of research is essential for understanding the precise mechanisms of lymphocyte participation and for the development of targeted therapies to modulate their activity in the context of PBC. T lymphocytes, a type of immune cell, can be classified into several subtypes based on the receptors they express on their surface and their functions. These subtypes include CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, regulatory T cells (Treg cells), memory T cells, and NKT cells. These T cell subsets interact with each other during immune responses, collectively coordinating and regulating the functions of the immune system. In early PBC stages, CD3+, CD4+, and CD8 + lymphocytes, especially CD8 + T cells, are significantly involved [28–31]. CD8 + T cells are notably abundant around damaged interlobular bile ducts during PBC’s initial phases [32]. Recent evidence underscores the role of various T cell subpopulations, including Th1, Th17, Tregs, Tfh cells, and Tfr cells, in PBC pathogenesis [33, 34]. Thus, the inclusion of both albumin and lymphocyte counts in the PNI provides a combined reflection of an individual’s overall health status. Histologically, PBC is traditionally categorized into four stages: portal inflammation (Stage I), periportal lesions extending into hepatic parenchyma (Stage II), hepatic architecture distortion with fibrous septa (Stage III), and cirrhosis with regenerative nodules (Stage IV) [35]. Patients with incomplete UDCA response often exhibit more advanced histological stages [36] and a higher tendency for histological progression [37]. UDCA responsiveness is a determinant of long-term prognosis and survival in PBC patients. The efficacy of UDCA response is partly dependent on the nutritional and immune status of the patients. To our knowledge, this study provides preliminary evidence that baseline PNI is associated with biochemical response to UDCA therapy in patients with PBC. Our ROC analysis showed that PNI had modest discriminatory ability for identifying patients at risk of inadequate UDCA response, suggesting that it may provide supportive information for risk stratification rather than serve as a standalone predictive tool. It is important to emphasize that this direct comparison was conducted precisely to determine whether the composite PNI index offers a predictive advantage over its individual component, albumin, which is a well-established conventional marker. In contrast, PNI combines albumin and lymphocyte levels and may provide a broader reflection of nutritional and immune-inflammatory status. This improvement in stability is substantiated by the differential AUROC outcomes observed between non-cirrhotic and cirrhotic patient cohorts. Moreover, albumin levels are significantly influenced by liver function alterations, whereas lymphocyte counts do not directly correlate with liver health, which underlines the potential value of combining these disparate indicators into PNI. Integrating albumin and lymphocyte count may reduce reliance on a single laboratory parameter, although its incremental predictive value over albumin alone remains uncertain. This multidimensional approach may help explain the observed association between lower PNI and inadequate UDCA response, although its incremental clinical value requires further validation. The ease of obtaining PNI from routine laboratory parameters supports its potential use as a convenient supportive marker [38]. Consequently, monitoring PNI, along with other clinical parameters, could offer useful information into the nutritional and immune status of PBC patients. Whether nutritional or immune-related interventions can improve UDCA responsiveness in patients with low baseline PNI requires further investigation. Although stratification by cirrhosis severity (e.g., Child-Pugh classes A/B/C) could offer more granular insights, the limited number of cirrhotic patients (n = 77) in our cohort precluded meaningful sub-analyses. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate our findings across different cirrhosis severity stages. Several validated models, such as the UK-PBC risk score [39] and the GLOBE Score [40], have been developed to predict long-term outcomes and treatment response in PBC using a combination of baseline and post-treatment biochemical variables. While these models are powerful, they primarily rely on liver-specific parameters and do not routinely incorporate comprehensive indicators of systemic nutritional and immune status. Our findings suggest that PNI, a simple composite marker reflecting both nutrition (albumin) and immunity (lymphocytes), provides independent prognostic information. This implies that the pathophysiological processes captured by PNI—malnutrition and immune dysregulation—may not be fully represented in existing models. Therefore, while our study does not propose a new clinical prediction model, it identifies PNI as a potential marker for future model enhancement. Furthermore, future studies should focus on integrating the Prognostic Nutritional Index into existing, validated prediction models to determine whether it provides incremental prognostic value and improves their accuracy in identifying high-risk patients.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design may introduce selection and measurement bias, and may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, although multivariable models were used, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as nutritional intake, inflammatory conditions, treatment adherence, and other disease-related variables, cannot be excluded. Third, the number of UDCA non-responders was limited, which may affect the precision and stability of the estimates and increase the risk of overfitting in the fully adjusted model. In addition, the ROC analysis showed only modest discriminatory performance, and calibration analysis, decision curve analysis, and external validation were not performed. These limitations indicate that the current findings should be regarded as exploratory and require confirmation in larger prospective multicenter cohorts.

Conclusions

In this retrospective observational study, lower baseline PNI was associated with inadequate biochemical response to UDCA in patients with PBC. These findings suggest that PNI may provide clinically relevant information for identifying patients at increased risk of suboptimal UDCA response. As a composite index derived from serum albumin and lymphocyte count, PNI may reflect the combined influence of hepatic synthetic function, nutritional status, and immune-inflammatory status. However, the discriminatory performance of PNI was modest, and the retrospective single-center design limits causal inference and generalizability. Therefore, PNI should be interpreted as a supportive biomarker for risk stratification rather than a standalone clinical decision-making tool. Further prospective, multicenter studies are needed to validate its incremental predictive value beyond established clinical and biochemical markers.
