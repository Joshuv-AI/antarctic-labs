Isolation and antimicrobial profiling of Salmonella in apparently healthy slaughtered cattle and abattoir personnel at Debre Birhan municipal abattoir, Ethiopia
Authors: Tadiwos Asfaw Dessie, Kassa Demissie Abdi, Yeshimebet Chanyalew Getahun, Enyiew Alemnew Alamerew, Abdi Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFoodborne diseases caused by Salmonella remain a major public health concern, often linked to contaminated animal products. Asymptomatic cattle can transmit the pathogen during slaughter and meat handling, while rising antimicrobial resistance further complicates treatment. However, limited data in Debre Birhan hinder effective control measures. Therefore, a cross-sectional study was conducted at the Debre Birhan municipal abattoir from October 2024 to January 2025 to estimate the prevalence of Salmonella in apparently healthy slaughtered cattle and abattoir personnel, assess associated risk factors, and determine antimicrobial resistance patterns of the isolates.MethodsA total of 452 samples were collected, with equal representation (25% (113/452) each) from cecal contents, knife swabs, carcass swabs, and hand swabs, and examined for Salmonella following standard procedures recommended by the International Organization for Standardization. Additionally, semi-structured questionnaires were administered to abattoir workers to assess personal hygiene practices.ResultOut of the 452 samples examined, 29 (6.42%; 95% CI: 4.34–9.09) were culture-positive for Salmonella, with the highest proportion from carcass swabs (12/113; 10.62%; 95% CI: 5.61–17.82), followed by cecal contents (9/113; 7.96%; 95% CI: 3.70–14.58), knife swabs (6/113; 5.31%; 95% CI: 1.97–11.20), and hand swabs (2/113; 1.77%; 95% CI: 0.22–6.25). There was a statistically significant association between Salmonella detection in cecal contents and carcass contamination at the individual cattle level. Cattle carrying Salmonella in their cecal contents were observed to have higher odds of contaminated carcasses compared with non-carrier animals (OR = 4.8; 95% CI: 1.01–22.75). However, this relationship should be interpreted as an association between samples collected from the same animal rather than as evidence of an independent risk factor. All isolates were tested for susceptibility to a panel of 13 selected antimicrobials, and most showed resistance to one or more agents. Multidrug resistance (MDR) was observed in the majority of resistant isolates. The highest resistance rates were recorded for ampicillin (100%), followed by ceftazidime (93.1%) and azithromycin (93.1%).ConclusionThe findings indicate the presence of Salmonella contamination in slaughtered cattle and abattoir environments, with a high level of multidrug resistance among isolates. This highlights the need for improved hygienic practices in abattoirs and prudent use of antimicrobials to reduce public health risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05294-z.Keywords: Foodborne pathogens, Carcass contamination, Drug resistance, Hygienic practices, Public health

Introduction

Foodborne bacterial diseases are a major public health concern worldwide, with Salmonella species being among the most important pathogens affecting both humans and animals. Salmonellosis is a leading cause of gastroenteritis, resulting in millions of illnesses and thousands of deaths annually [1, 2]. Annually, approximately 93.8 million cases of illness and 155,000 deaths are attributed to gastroenteritis caused by Salmonella [3–5]. All Salmonella strains are considered pathogenic to humans, and the infection can range from mild diarrhea to severe systemic illness.

Salmonellosis is broadly classified into typhoidal and nontyphoidal forms. Typhoidal salmonellosis, mainly caused by Salmonella Typhi, is common in developing countries and is typically transmitted through contaminated food or water. Nontyphoidal salmonellosis, caused by a variety of Salmonella serotypes, often results in foodborne illness characterized by diarrhea, abdominal cramps, and fever, and is commonly linked to consumption of contaminated animal products [3, 6].

Animals serve as a major reservoir for Salmonella, with the intestinal tract as the primary habitat. Infected livestock, including cattle, may appear clinically healthy but can shed the bacteria, contaminating the environment and meat products. Cross-contamination can occur in abattoirs through contaminated tools, floors, and personnel, and may continue during meat handling, processing, and preparation [4, 7].

The economic impact of salmonellosis includes treatment costs, productivity losses, regulatory compliance, product recalls, and trade restrictions, making it a significant concern for both public health and the livestock industry [5, 8].

A growing concern is the emergence of MDR Salmonella, which complicates treatment and poses a serious public health threat. Antimicrobial resistance in Salmonella is influenced by the misuse and overuse of antibiotics in human and veterinary medicine, as well as subtherapeutic use in livestock [2, 9]. In Ethiopia, several studies have reported the occurrence of Salmonella and high levels of antimicrobial resistance in livestock and livestock products [3, 4, 10–12]. Similar challenges have also been widely reported in East Africa and other low- and middle-income countries, where comparable slaughterhouse hygiene conditions, antimicrobial usage patterns, and food safety constraints persist. Despite this broader regional relevance, evidence on the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance, and associated risk factors of Salmonella in apparently healthy slaughtered cattle and abattoir workers remains limited in Debre Birhan and comparable settings. Therefore, this study aimed to estimate the prevalence of Salmonella, assess associated risk factors, determine antimicrobial resistance patterns, and evaluate personal hygiene practices among abattoir workers at the Debre Birhan Municipal Abattoir, Northeast Ethiopia. The findings are expected to contribute to both local evidence for policy and intervention in Ethiopia and broader insights for improving abattoir hygiene and food safety systems in similar contexts.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The study was conducted at the Debre Birhan Municipal Abattoir in North Shoa Zone, Amhara Region, central Ethiopia, from October 2024 to January 2025. The abattoir is located in the northwestern part of Debre Birhan town and operates with 20 workers, one meat inspector, and two guards. Debre Birhan lies approximately 130 km northeast of Addis Ababa, at 9°41′N latitude and 39°32′E longitude, with an elevation of 2,840 m above sea level. The town experiences an average daytime and nighttime temperature of 17.8 °C and 8.83 °C, respectively, with annual rainfall of about 920 mm [13].

Study population

The study was conducted on apparently healthy slaughtered feedlot cattle at Debre Birhan Municipal Abattoir. The cattle included both local and crossbred animals sourced from different agro-ecological zones and management systems. Most animals originated from household fattening farms in Debre Birhan and surrounding towns, including Enewari, Deneba, Chacha, Mendida, Keyit, and Ankober. The feedlot system in the study area was mainly intensive and semi-intensive, where cattle were fed concentrate and hay. Before slaughter, animals were transported either by truck or on foot in small groups from local markets to the abattoir. Upon arrival, they were kept in concrete-floored, open-air holding pens for approximately 3–5 h without access to feed or water before slaughter. In addition, the study population included abattoir personnel with prior work experience, who were involved in slaughtering and related handling activities.

Study design

The study was a cross-sectional observational study involving cattle slaughtered between the months of October 2024 and January 2025 at Debre Birhan Municipal Abattoir.

Sample size determination

The sample size for this study was calculated using a single population proportion formula [14] with a 95% confidence level and 5% absolute precision. Due to the absence of previous studies or pilot data on Salmonella prevalence in the study area, an expected prevalence of 8% reported from the Dessie Municipal Abattoir, Northeast Ethiopia [15] was used. The selection of this estimate was considered appropriate because the two study areas are geographically proximate and share similar agroecological conditions, cattle production systems, breed composition, and socioeconomic practices related to livestock production and marketing.

Based on this calculation, the minimum required sample size was 113. However, to increase the reliability of comparisons across different sample types (carcass swab, cecal content, knife swab, and hand swab), the sample size was expanded to 452, with equal allocation across the four sample categories. This balanced allocation was used to ensure adequate and comparable representation of each sample type for statistical comparison rather than for estimation of a single population prevalence. Apparently healthy slaughtered cattle were selected using systematic random sampling based on slaughter order. Specifically, animals were selected at regular intervals from the slaughter line until the required sample size was achieved. In addition, all 20 abattoir workers at Debre Birhan Municipal Abattoir were included to assess hygienic practices and working conditions.

Sampling procedure and sample collections

The study was conducted over four consecutive months, with sampling carried out three days per week. Apparently healthy slaughtered cattle were selected using a systematic random sampling approach based on slaughter order. From each selected animal, matched samples were collected from both animal and environmental sources, resulting in a total of 452 samples. Animal-derived samples included carcass swabs (n = 113) and cecal contents (n = 113), while environmental samples consisted of hand swabs from abattoir workers (n = 113) and knife swabs from slaughtering equipment (n = 113). In addition, all 20 workers at Debre Birhan Municipal Abattoir were eligible to participate in an interview to assess hygiene practices and working conditions.

Cecal content samples were collected directly from the cecum into sterile universal bottles using a scalpel blade disinfected with 70% ethanol. Carcass swab samples were obtained from four anatomical sites (neck, ribs, flank, and rump) on one side of the carcass using sterile swabs moistened with approximately 10 mL of buffered peptone water (BPW) (Hi-Media Laboratories, India). Each swab covered an area of approximately 10 cm × 10 cm while wearing sterile gloves, and samples were placed into sterile tubes. Carcass swabbing was performed at the end of the slaughter process after the carcasses had been washed with water. All samples were transported to the laboratory in ice-cooled boxes and processed within two hours of collection; when immediate processing was not possible, samples were stored at 4 °C for up to three days before analysis [16, 17].

Detailed individual animal-level data, including exact origin, age, and lairage duration, were not systematically recorded. Accurate determination of animal origin was not feasible because cattle were supplied through traders sourcing from multiple locations. In addition, most slaughtered animals belonged to similar adult age groups and were processed within a relatively uniform slaughtering schedule, limiting variability in these factors. Consequently, the analysis focused primarily on sample type and Salmonella occurrence.

Sample processing

In the laboratory, 25 g of cecal content was aseptically weighed on sterile aluminum foil and transferred into sterile stomacher bags. Subsequently, 225 mL of buffered peptone water (BPW) was added, and the mixture was manually agitated to ensure proper dispersion of the sample [17]. Carcass swab, hand swab, and knife swab samples were similarly processed by manually agitating each swab in its original sterile universal bottle containing 10 mL of BPW to facilitate the release of microorganisms into the medium [16, 17].

Isolation and identification of Salmonella

Salmonella was isolated and identified according to standard techniques [16, 17]. The detection of Salmonella necessitates different successive stages, which are described below. The bacteriological media used in different stages were prepared according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. All the samples were processed and analyzed separately.

Pre-enrichment

Processed samples in an appropriate amount of BPW (1:9) were incubated for 16 to 20 h at 37 0 C. Cecal content samples were incubated while they were in stomacher bags, whereas all the swab samples were incubated in their original plastic bags [16, 17].

Selective enrichment

Modified Rappaport-Vassiliadis Medium (MRVM) (AccumixR, Microxpress, Tulip Diagnostics, India) and selenite Cysteine F broth (SB) (ThermoFisher Scientific, Oxoid, UK) media were used for selective enrichment. One ml of incubated pre-enrichment broth was transferred aseptically into 10 ml of SB and was incubated at 37 °C for 18 to 24 h. Another 0.1 ml of the pre-enrichment broth culture was transferred into 10 ml of MRVM broth and incubated at 42 °C for 18 to 24 h [16–18].

Selective plating and identification

A loopful of inoculum from each MRVM and SB cultures were streaked onto xylose lysine deoxycholate (XLD) agar (HIMEDIA Laboratories, MIDC, India) plates and MacConkey agar (HIMEDIA Laboratories, M1702, India). The inoculated plates were incubated at 37 °C for 20 to 24 h. After incubation, the plates were examined for the presence of presumptive Salmonella colonies. Typical colonies of Salmonella grown on XLD medium appear as pink colonies with or without black center. However, many cultures of Salmonella on XLD medium may produce colonies with large, glossy black centers or may appear as almost completely black colonies, while on MacConkey agar colonies appear pale yellow or colorless, and are non-lactose fermenting. This is because Salmonella cannot ferment lactose, a sugar present in MacConkey agar, and thus does not produce acid, which would cause the agar to turn pink or red in lactose-fermenting bacteria [16–18].

Biochemical tests

For confirmation, up to five presumptive (typical or suspected) Salmonella colonies were picked from each selective plating medium. When the suspected colonies on each plate were fewer than five, all the colonies were picked. Colonies were picked by lightly touching the very center of the colony with sterile inoculating needle.

Triple sugar iron agar (TSI) (AccumixR, Microxpress, Tulip Diagnostics, India) slants were inoculated by streaking the slant and stabbing the butt without flaming. Lysine Iron Agar (LIA) (AccumixR, Microxpress, Tulip Diagnostics, India) slants were inoculated by stabbing the butt twice and then streaking the slant. TSI and LIA slants were incubated at 37 °C for 24 ± 2 h with tubes capped loosely to maintain aerobic conditions while incubating slants to prevent excessive H2S production. Isolates, which appear contaminated on TSI slant, were streaked onto XLD agar for purification. Pure isolates were inoculated on urea agar slants (CHRISTENSENS UREA AGAR BASE, Greenstar Impex, India), indole test using SIM (AccumixR, Microxpress, Tulip Diagnostics, India), and citrate utilization test using Simmon’s citrate agar (SIGMA-ALDRICH, Switzerland) plates for further characterization. Agar plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h [16, 17]. Acid production and acetoin production (Voges-Proskauer reaction) test was performed on MR-VP Medium (Sisco Research Laboratories, New Mumbai, India). Isolates presumptive of Salmonella on the biochemical tests were cultured on brain heart infusion agar (Difco, Becton Dickinson, Claix, France).

Resistance to antimicrobial agents

The Salmonella isolates were tested for susceptibility to 13 selected commercial antimicrobial drugs (Table 1). The tests were carried out at the Microbiology Laboratory, Animal Health Laboratory, Debre Birhan Agricultural Research Center (DBARC), Ethiopia. The antimicrobial resistance tests of Salmonella strains were carried out using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion susceptibility test protocol [19, 20].

The standards and reference strains described by committee for Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) for antimicrobial susceptibility tests of bacteria isolated from animals and humans were followed throughout the study, including the use of Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 as a quality control strain [20]. Quality control results were verified to fall within the CLSI-recommended zone diameter ranges for E. coli ATCC 25,922 before interpretation of test results. All antimicrobial susceptibility tests were performed under standardized laboratory conditions in accordance with CLSI guidelines. Briefly, the strains were grown to a 0.5 McFarland density in Mueller-Hinton (MH) agar (HIMEDIA Laboratories, MIDC, India). Isolates that were resistant to one or more antimicrobials were considered resistant strains, and isolates that were resistant to two or more antimicrobial classes were considered MDR strains. Antimicrobial susceptibility results were interpreted and categorized as susceptible (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R) according to CLSI inhibition zone [19, 20].

Questionnaire survey and hygiene assessment of abattoir workers

A semi-structured questionnaire was designed to assess the awareness, hygiene practices, and meat handling behaviors of abattoir workers. The questionnaire was pretested to ensure clarity and avoid confusion, and it was originally prepared in English and translated into the local language, Amharic, to facilitate understanding. The survey captured sociodemographic information, including educational level, work experience, job responsibilities, and source of abattoir skills (Supplementary file 1). It also assessed hygiene practices such as handwashing, knife and equipment cleaning, use of protective clothing, footwear, hair covering, and personal habits that may influence meat contamination, including jewelry use, fingernail maintenance, smoking, recent illness, and recent antimicrobial use (Supplementary file 2). In addition to the questionnaire, direct observations of meat handling and hygiene practices were conducted to verify reported behaviors and assess compliance with recommended sanitation standards. Data from the survey and observations were recorded and later analyzed to identify gaps in hygiene and potential risk factors for Salmonella contamination in the abattoir.

Data management and analysis

Data from the abattoir survey and laboratory analyses were entered into Microsoft Excel 2011, checked for completeness, consistency, and accuracy, and then coded before being exported to STATA Version 17 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Salmonella prevalence at sample and animal levels was calculated as the proportion of positive samples or animals among those examined and presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Animal-level prevalence was based on 113 slaughtered cattle, and an animal was considered positive if Salmonella was detected in at least one animal-derived sample (cecal content or carcass swab).

Associations between sample type and Salmonella occurrence were assessed using univariable logistic regression, with results expressed as odds ratios (OR) and 95% CI. This analysis compared occurrence across sample types rather than identifying independent animal- or worker-level risk factors. Because samples were collected within a single slaughter context and detailed individual-level variables were not systematically collected, potential confounding factors could not be assessed, and multivariable logistic regression was not performed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, as the analysis was exploratory and intended to identify potential associations rather than infer causality.

Results

Prevalence

A total of 452 samples were collected and examined, of which 29 were positive for Salmonella, resulting in an overall sample-level prevalence of 6.42% (29/452; 95% CI: 4.34–9.09). Among the 113 slaughtered cattle examined, 12 animals tested positive for Salmonella in at least one of the two animal-derived samples (cecal content or carcass swab), giving an animal-level prevalence of 10.62% (12/113; 95% CI: 5.61–17.82).

Univariable logistic regression analysis revealed significant variation in Salmonella occurrence among sample types. Carcass swab samples showed the highest prevalence (12/113; 10.62%; 95% CI: 5.61–17.82) and were significantly associated with Salmonella detection compared with hand swab samples (2/113; 1.77%; 95% CI: 0.22–6.25), the reference category (OR = 6.59; 95% CI: 1.44–30.18; p = 0.015). Cecal content samples also showed a significant association with Salmonella occurrence, with a prevalence of 7.96% (9/113; 95% CI: 3.70–14.58) (OR = 4.80; 95% CI: 1.01–22.75; p = 0.048). In contrast, knife swab samples had a prevalence of 5.31% (6/113; 95% CI: 1.97–11.20) and were not significantly associated with Salmonella detection (OR = 3.11; 95% CI: 0.62–15.76; p = 0.170).

Host-derived samples had a significantly higher prevalence of Salmonella (21/226; 9.29%; 95% CI: 5.84–13.85) than environmental samples (8/226; 3.54%; 95% CI: 1.54–6.86), with increased odds of detection (OR = 2.79; 95% CI: 1.21–6.44; p = 0.016) (Table 2). However, this result should be interpreted as an association between paired samples collected from the same animal or slaughter context rather than evidence of an independent risk factor. Similarly, the association between cecal content and carcass contamination reflects a within-animal sample relationship rather than a causal or independent risk effect, as both samples were obtained from the same animal. The wide confidence intervals observed for some estimates indicate limited statistical precision due to the small number of positive observations in certain categories.

Antibiotic susceptibility test

The current antibiotic susceptibility test showed that the majority of antimicrobial tests (72.7%) indicated resistance. The isolates exhibited particularly high resistance to ampicillin (100%), azithromycin (93.1%), cefotaxime (82.8%), nitrofurantoin (86.2%), gentamicin (72.4%), nalidixic acid (89.7%), tetracycline (89.7%), streptomycin (89.7%), doxycycline (89.7%), and ceftazidime (93.1%) (Table 3). Moreover, most isolates exhibited multidrug resistance. The most frequently observed resistance pattern involved resistance to three antibiotics (24.1%), followed by resistance to nine antibiotics (13.8%) and to six and eleven antibiotics (10.3% each). Resistance to four, five, seven, eight, and ten antibiotics was observed in 6.9% of the isolates for each pattern (Table 4).

Hygiene of abattoir workers and abattoir

The questionnaire revealed that most abattoir workers had basic education, with 65% completing up to grade 8 and only 5% holding college-level qualifications. The majority (80%) were carcass processors, and 60% had 2–5 years of work experience. Regarding work practices, 60% acquired skills through observation, while only 15% received formal training; all reported some prior job-related training. Knife cleaning was mainly performed at the end of work (65%) or before starting (20%), predominantly using water only (95%). About half (55%) washed their hands after handling intestinal contents, and floor cleaning was primarily done between work tasks (55%) or at the end of work (20%). These findings indicate that while basic hygiene practices are partially followed, formal training and consistent adherence to sanitation remain limited among abattoir workers (Table 5).

The assessment of hygiene and sanitation practices among abattoir workers showed partial adherence to recommended standards. Most workers reported washing their hands after using the bathroom (75%) and before slaughtering (70%); however, the use of soap was limited (20% and 5%, respectively, with many relying on water only). Although most workers reported wearing protective clothing (90%), consistent use was lower (55%). Hair covering was largely neglected, as 70% of workers did not use it. Boots were commonly worn (80%). Risky personal practices were also observed, including jewelry use (30%), untrimmed or unpolished fingernails (75%), and smoking during work (10%). A small proportion of workers reported recent episodes of diarrhea (10%) and antimicrobial use within the past 15 days (5%). Overall, these findings indicate suboptimal hygiene practices, particularly in handwashing technique, consistent use of protective equipment, and personal sanitation behaviors (Table 6).

Discussion

Prevalence

Salmonella contamination in slaughtered cattle and abattoir environments poses a significant public health concern due to its potential transmission to humans through contaminated meat and handling practices. It may also negatively affect animal health and farm productivity. Therefore, monitoring its prevalence, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and hygiene practices among abattoir workers is essential to support evidence-based interventions, improve food safety, and reduce the spread of resistant strains. In line with this public health importance, this study assessed the occurrence of Salmonella in samples collected from different sources, including host and abattoir environments, and evaluated antimicrobial resistance profiles.

The current result indicated an overall prevalence of 6.42% (95% CI: 4.34–9.09%), which is comparable with previous reports from Ethiopia, including 6.04% [21] and 6.08% [3]. Higher prevalence values have been reported in other regions, such as 9.5% [22], 11.4% [23], and 12.9% [24], with even higher rates documented in certain areas, including 12.5% [11] and 17.45% [25]. Conversely, lower prevalence rates, such as 3.7% [26], 2.5% [27], and 2% [28], have also been reported, reflecting variations in study design, sample types, and study duration. Several factors may have contributed to the relatively lower prevalence observed in this study. The study period (October 2024–January 2025) coincided with abundant feed and water availability; cattle were sourced from nearby household fattening farms, reducing transport-related stress, and intensive veterinary care, including antibiotic use, could have contributed to reduced Salmonella carriage and fecal shedding [2, 29]. Additionally, the short lairage time (3–5 h) without feed or water may have minimized stress, cross-contamination, and conditions favorable for pathogen shedding. These findings highlight the ongoing risk of Salmonella in apparently healthy cattle and abattoir environments and underscore the importance of continued surveillance, antimicrobial resistance monitoring, and hygiene interventions to protect public health and ensure food safety.

The prevalence of Salmonella in carcass swabs observed in the current finding (10.2%; 95% CI: 5.61–17.82%) is consistent with previous reports of 12.5% [11], 11.3% [22], 8.6% and 8.7% [27, 30], 12.7% [31], and 8.0% [15]. However, it is lower than 18.5% [32] and higher than 3.7% [21], 2.5% [26], and 0.42% [33]. The higher prevalence observed in the current finding may be associated with both cattle-related factors and environmental contamination. At Municipal Abattoir, evisceration, flaying, carcass cutting, and loading were all carried out in the same room, which may facilitate cross-contamination and increase the risk of Salmonella presence on carcasses.

The prevalence of Salmonella in the cecal content of slaughtered cattle at Debre Birhan Municipal Abattoir was found to be 7.96% (95%CI = 3.70-14.58), which is in agreement with 8.6% prevalence of faecal samples collected from dairy farms in Adama town, Oromia, Ethiopia [21] and 7.32% prevalence reported from Bishoftu city, central Ethiopia [28]. But it is higher than 3% isolation rate of cecal content samples collected from the occurrence of Salmonella in the Cattle Production in France [34] and 0.99% prevalence in cecal content samples collected from assessment of the contamination of beef with Salmonella along the beef supply chain in Dukem town, Ethiopia [31]. However, it was lower than 70.9% prevalence in faeces collected from a longitudinal study on the dynamics of Salmonella enterica prevalence and serovar composition in beef cattle faeces, lymph nodes, and potential contributing sources from the feedlot environment [35]. The shedding of Salmonella in faeces contributes to the contamination of water, feed, soil, and other animals, which are potential sources of infection [2].

The current result indicated that Salmonella contamination in knife swabs (5.31%; 95% CI: 1.97–11.20%) and host-related samples (9.29%; 95% CI: 5.84–13.85%) may be influenced by slaughterhouse practices. The knife swab prevalence is lower than 20% reported in Adama town [21] but comparable to 11.3% from Addis Ababa Abattoir Enterprise [23], while the host-related prevalence aligns with 11.5% in Gondar dairy farms [24] and is higher than 4.1% reported in Addis Ababa [26]. These variations suggest that hygiene and equipment handling are key determinants of contamination. At the abattoir, knives and other slaughter tools are repeatedly used for multiple animals without sterilization, and procedures such as throat cutting, skinning, and evisceration are performed with the same knives. Standard decontamination practices, such as immersing knives in hot water at 82 °C for two minutes, were not followed due to the unavailability of hot water; instead, knives were wiped with cloths that were not soaked, which may have facilitated cross-contamination [36, 37]. Slaughter equipment is also frequently placed on unclean surfaces, further increasing the risk of pathogen transfer to carcasses. Worker-related factors may compound these risks. In this study, 65% of abattoir workers had only primary school education and acquired their skills through observation rather than formal training. Similar findings were reported in eastern Ethiopia, where limited education and lack of job-related training contributed to poor sanitation and unsafe handling practices [37]. Inadequate knowledge of hygiene and contamination risks can lead to cross-contamination and compromise meat safety. Therefore, targeted training programs to improve personal hygiene, food handling knowledge, and operational practices are critical to reducing Salmonella contamination and ensuring safer products for consumers [37].

Antimicrobial resistance test

The emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is primarily driven by improper use of antimicrobials in both animals and humans, facilitating the transfer of resistance genes and bacteria across animals, humans, food products, and the environment, which poses a significant public health risk [2, 27, 30, 38]. The consumption of MDR Salmonella, particularly through raw or undercooked meat, further exacerbates the global burden of AMR [3]. The current result indicated considerable variation in antimicrobial susceptibility among Salmonella isolates, ranging from complete resistance to high susceptibility (0–89.7%). This pattern reflects the presence of resistant strains and might be associated with the widespread and uncontrolled use of antibiotics in livestock production [4, 15]. Similar trends have been reported in other regions of Ethiopia, indicating that resistance is an ongoing and regionally widespread problem [4, 12, 24, 39]. However, because molecular characterization of antimicrobial resistance genes was not conducted, the mechanisms underlying the observed resistance could not be confirmed. In particular, resistance patterns observed in this study may be consistent with possible mechanisms such as chromosomal mutations and plasmid-mediated resistance, including extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production; however, these mechanisms were not investigated. These observations underscore the need for rational antimicrobial use, continuous surveillance of resistance patterns, and implementation of stewardship programs to mitigate the risk of resistant pathogens spreading to humans and the broader environment [2].

Most isolates in this study were 89.7% and 75.9% susceptible to ciprofloxacin and chloramphenicol, respectively. This finding is consistent with previous reports, which recorded susceptibility rates of 87.5% for ciprofloxacin [11] and 72.7% for chloramphenicol [21]. On the other hand, the current finding is in contrast with the reports of 27.3% [21], 35.7% [26], and 15.53% [40] susceptibility for ciprofloxacin, as well as 14.3% [11, 39] and 24% [25] susceptibility for chloramphenicol.

Among all isolates, resistance was highest for ampicillin (100%), followed by tetracycline (89.7%), streptomycin (89.7%), and ceftazidime (93.1%). This finding is consistent with previous reports, which recorded the highest resistance rates for ampicillin (100%), tetracycline (96.7%) in Gondar town, northwest of Ethiopia [24], and also other result indicated that lowest susceptibility rate (0%) for ampicillin [21] and (10.7%) for tetracycline [11]. The complete resistance to ampicillin is particularly concerning and may reflect its extensive and often indiscriminate use in both human and veterinary medicine in Ethiopia. Similarly high levels of resistance have been widely reported in Ethiopia and other low- and middle-income countries [2, 23, 41], and are consistent with global surveillance reports indicating increasing resistance of Salmonella spp. to commonly used β-lactam antibiotics, including ampicillin [1, 2, 42]. From a public health perspective, ampicillin remains one of the most widely used and affordable antibiotics in low-resource settings and is included in the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines due to its accessibility and clinical importance [2, 8]. Therefore, high resistance levels may limit treatment options for human salmonellosis and increase reliance on more expensive or last-resort antimicrobials. This is consistent with global evidence showing that antimicrobial resistance reduces treatment effectiveness, increases clinical complexity, and contributes to higher healthcare costs and poorer. Consequently, strengthening antimicrobial stewardship and surveillance systems is essential to preserve antibiotic effectiveness and guide rational use in both human and animal health sectors [2, 8].

The antimicrobial resistance results of 89.3% [26], 85.72% [3], and 80.7% [43] for streptomycin are closer to the current finding. The antimicrobial resistance results of 83.9% [11], 96.5% [40], 96.7% [24], and 93.0% [42] for tetracycline are closer to this study. The antimicrobial resistance results of 100% [25], 100% [44], 92.4% [42], and 100% [24] for ampicillin, the present finding is closer to these results. The findings of this study disagree with the report of 39.3% [26], 15.0% [4], 28.1% [41], and 50% [28] for tetracycline; 45.5% [22] for streptomycin; 10% [41] for ampicillin, and 14.3% [26] for ceftazidime. The resistance rate of Salmonella isolates to azithromycin in this study was 93.1%, which is lower than 99.4% [42] but it is higher than 40% [4], 42.9% [39], and 34.6% [43]. The resistance level of nalidixic acid was 89.7% in the current study. This result is lower than 98.3% [40], but it is higher than 39.3% [11], 0% [21], 7.1% [26], and 12.5% [41].

Limitations of the study

The current study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. It was conducted in a single municipal abattoir, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other slaughter facilities and broader cattle production systems with different hygiene practices and operational conditions. In addition, the sample size was calculated using a single-population proportion formula without accounting for clustering effects arising from multiple sample types collected from the same animal, which may have slightly affected the precision of the prevalence estimates. The study design was mainly descriptive, and detailed animal- and worker-level risk factors were not comprehensively collected, limiting the ability to assess potential confounding factors through multivariable analysis. Information on the exact origin of slaughtered cattle was not available, as animals were supplied through traders sourcing from multiple locations, and most cattle belonged to similar adult age groups, reducing variability for age-related comparisons. Moreover, slaughtering activities occurred within a limited time period, restricting the assessment of temporal factors such as slaughter timing and lairage duration. Because multiple sample types (cecal content, carcass swab, hand swab, and knife swab) were collected from the same animals and processing environment, the observed associations reflect within-animal or within-process relationships rather than independent risk factors, and therefore should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than causal effects. The study also did not include molecular characterization or serotyping of Salmonella isolates, limiting understanding of strain diversity and genetic relatedness; due to due to resource and budget limitations in our setting. Furthermore, the questionnaire-based assessment of hygiene practices involved a small number of respondents and was descriptive in nature, and therefore could not be statistically linked with Salmonella isolation results; in addition, self-reported responses may be subject to recall bias. Finally, although strict aseptic procedures were followed during sampling and laboratory analysis, the possibility of cross-contamination between samples cannot be entirely excluded. Despite the presence of these limitations, the current study addresses an important public health gap by providing baseline evidence on the occurrence and antimicrobial resistance of Salmonella in slaughtered cattle and abattoir environments in the study area, and it provides relevant information for public and animal health due to its implications for food safety, antimicrobial resistance monitoring, and hygiene improvement in abattoir practices.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study confirmed the presence of Salmonella in apparently healthy slaughtered cattle and the abattoir environment, with animal-derived samples possibly representing a potential source of carcass contamination. The detection of MDR Salmonella highlights a significant public health concern and underscores the potential risk associated with consuming contaminated meat. Slaughterhouse practices and worker behaviors, including reuse of knives without proper sterilization, handling multiple animals with the same tools, and partial adherence to hygiene protocols, were identified as key factors facilitating cross-contamination. These findings emphasize the urgent need for improved hygienic slaughtering practices, enhanced abattoir sanitation, targeted training for workers, and prudent use of antimicrobials to reduce Salmonella transmission and protect public health. Implement mandatory sterilization protocols for all abattoir tools by ensuring a reliable supply of hot water, along with the provision of soap and accessible hand-washing stations.
