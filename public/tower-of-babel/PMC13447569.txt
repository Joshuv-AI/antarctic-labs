Pediatric Lemierre syndrome of rhinosinusogenic origin complicated by bilateral cavernous sinus thrombosis: A case report
Authors: Adam El Hassouni, Hicham Mimouni, Houssam Eddine El Failali, Ilham Rkain, Rajae Borki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLemierre syndrome is a rare but potentially life-threatening septic thrombophlebitis classically following an oropharyngeal infection, most often associated with Fusobacterium necrophorum. Atypical head and neck sources and unusual patterns of venous thrombosis have increasingly been reported.Case presentationWe report the case of a 13-year-old boy admitted with fever, meningeal syndrome, left cervical swelling, ptosis, anisocoria, and altered consciousness following a short history of dental pain and fever. Contrast-enhanced cerebral and cervicothoracic computed tomography established the diagnosis of Lemierre syndrome of rhinosinusogenic origin, revealing left maxillary sinusitis complicated by a left medial pterygoid muscle abscess, thrombophlebitis of the left internal jugular vein and its tributaries, bilateral cavernous sinus thrombosis, left superior ophthalmic vein thrombosis, diencephalic ischemic stroke, and bilateral pulmonary septic embolic lesions. Blood cultures obtained before antibiotic therapy remained negative, and no focal microbiological specimen was available because no surgical drainage was performed. The patient received intravenous ceftriaxone, metronidazole, and an aminoglycoside, followed by low-molecular-weight heparin because of extensive intracranial venous thrombosis. Corticosteroids were also administered for severe orbital involvement. The clinical course was favorable, with complete clinical and radiological recovery after six weeks of antibiotic therapy.ConclusionThis case illustrates that complicated pediatric rhinosinusitis may present as a multidirectional Lemierre-like septic thrombophlebitis involving cervicofacial, orbital, intracranial, and pulmonary territories, even when cultures are negative. It also highlights that favorable outcome may be achieved without surgical drainage when no accessible collection is identified, provided that broad antimicrobial therapy, selected anticoagulation, and close multidisciplinary monitoring are rapidly implemented.

Introduction

Lemierre syndrome was first described by André Lemierre in 1936 as anaerobic post-anginal septicemia associated with septic thrombophlebitis, classically involving the internal jugular vein after an oropharyngeal infection [1]. The syndrome is most commonly associated with Fusobacterium necrophorum, an anaerobic Gram-negative bacillus belonging to the normal flora of the oropharynx, gastrointestinal tract, and female genital tract [2], [3].

The classical presentation combines a recent tonsillopharyngeal infection, septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, bacteremia, and metastatic septic emboli, most frequently involving the lungs [3], [4]. However, the clinical picture is often incomplete, which may delay diagnosis and treatment. In addition, the definition of Lemierre syndrome has progressively expanded to include atypical forms arising from other head and neck infectious sources, including sinusitis, otitis, otomastoiditis, mastoiditis, dental infections, and parapharyngeal or deep cervical infections [3], [5], [6], [7].

Intracranial venous extension, cavernous sinus thrombosis, and superior ophthalmic vein involvement remain uncommon but severe complications. The interest of the present case does not lie in pediatric Lemierre syndrome or rhinosinusogenic origin alone, as both have been previously reported. Rather, the case is distinguished by the combination of a presumed maxillary sinus source, medial pterygoid muscle abscess, extension toward the masticator space, left internal jugular vein thrombophlebitis, bilateral cavernous sinus thrombosis, left superior ophthalmic vein thrombosis, pulmonary septic embolic lesions, diencephalic ischemic stroke, negative blood cultures, and favorable outcome without surgical drainage.

Case presentation

A 13-year-old boy with no significant medical or surgical history was admitted for febrile altered consciousness. He was not known to have any congenital hemoglobinopathy or immunodeficiency. One week before admission, he developed fever, reaching 39°C, associated with dental pain. He received symptomatic treatment with paracetamol and ibuprofen. Because of progressive clinical worsening, his family brought him to the emergency department.

At initial presentation, the patient weighed 40 kg and was febrile. He had a complete meningeal syndrome, including severe periorbital headache, photophobia, phonophobia, vomiting, and neck stiffness. He also had left-sided cervical swelling, torticollis, left ptosis, and anisocoria. No focal sensory or motor deficit was noted. The initial level of consciousness was impaired, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score reported as severely decreased at first evaluation, followed by partial improvement after admission to intensive care. Cardiovascular examination was stable, without signs of shock. The cervical examination revealed a painful, warm, erythematous left laterocervical swelling. There were no clinical signs of otitis or pharyngitis.

Laboratory tests showed marked inflammation, with C-reactive protein at 259 mg/L, leukocytosis at 12,970/mm³ with neutrophil predominance, mild anemia at 11.2 g/dL, and normal platelet count at 224,000/mm³ . Hyponatremia was also present, with serum sodium at 127 mmol/L. Lumbar puncture was not performed because of anisocoria and concern for intracranial complications. Blood cultures were obtained before antibiotic therapy but remained negative. No causative organism was identified.

The patient was admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit. Empirical intravenous antibiotic therapy was started with ceftriaxone, metronidazole, and an aminoglycoside, together with supportive care, rehydration, correction of hyponatremia, analgesic treatment, and antipyretics.

Contrast-enhanced cerebral and cervicothoracic computed tomography established the diagnosis, revealing left maxillary sinus opacification, an abscess of the left medial pterygoid muscle, and multiple septic venous thromboses involving the left internal jugular vein and its tributaries, both cavernous sinuses, and the left superior ophthalmic vein. Complementary contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance angiography further confirmed the cervicofacial infectious extension and the intracranial venous thromboses, including extension toward the left masticator space and bilateral cavernous sinus involvement (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Imaging also showed a diencephalic ischemic stroke, interpreted as secondary to infectious vascular involvement. During hospitalization, the patient remained febrile and developed worsening left eyelid edema. Ophthalmological examination showed left ptosis, exophthalmos, complete ophthalmoplegia, and a non-reactive semi-mydriatic pupil, without chemosis. The neurological examination remained otherwise stable, without focal sensory or motor deficit.

Because of the extensive venous thrombosis involving the cavernous sinuses and the superior ophthalmic vein, therapeutic anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin was initiated at 100 IU anti-Xa/kg every 12 h subcutaneously, after assessment of the bleeding risk. Methylprednisolone at 2 mg/kg/day was also administered because of severe orbital inflammatory involvement. Antibiotic therapy with ceftriaxone, metronidazole, and an aminoglycoside was continued. No surgical drainage was performed, as otolaryngological assessment found no drainable cervical collection and the patient improved under medical treatment.

The clinical course was favorable. General condition improved within five days, respiratory status normalized rapidly, and the inflammatory syndrome decreased progressively, with C-reactive protein decreasing to 37 mg/L after seven days of antibiotic therapy and to 12.5 mg/L after 15 days. The patient was discharged after three weeks of hospitalization. He completed a total of six weeks of antibiotic therapy, including approximately three weeks of intravenous treatment.

Follow-up neurological and otolaryngological examinations were normal. Follow-up magnetic resonance imaging at three and six months after completion of treatment showed no residual neurological, otolaryngological, or radiological abnormality.

Discussion

Lemierre syndrome is a rare septic condition whose incidence markedly decreased after the introduction of antibiotics, although it remains a severe and potentially fatal disease when diagnosis and treatment are delayed [1], [3], [4]. It typically affects adolescents and young adults, which is consistent with the age of our patient [3], [8]. The classical syndrome follows an oropharyngeal infection and is characterized by septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, bacteremia, and metastatic septic emboli, most frequently involving the lungs. However, the clinical spectrum is broader than the historical post-anginal form, and atypical presentations are increasingly recognized.

The incremental contribution of the present case is not pediatric Lemierre syndrome or rhinosinusogenic origin alone. Rather, its value lies in the unusual combination of a presumed maxillary sinus source, medial pterygoid muscle abscess, extension into the deep facial spaces, left internal jugular vein thrombophlebitis, bilateral cavernous sinus thrombosis, left superior ophthalmic vein thrombosis, diencephalic ischemic stroke, pulmonary septic embolic lesions, negative blood cultures, and favorable outcome without surgical drainage. This combination illustrates a severe form of cervicocranial septic thrombophlebitis and expands the spectrum of atypical Lemierre syndrome arising from non-oropharyngeal head and neck infections [5], [6], [7], [9].

The anatomical pattern of thrombosis in this case is noteworthy. Rather than a purely linear extension from a tonsillopharyngeal infection to the internal jugular vein, the association of maxillary sinusitis, medial pterygoid muscle abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis, and superior ophthalmic vein thrombosis suggests possible multidirectional spread through valveless venous networks of the face and skull base. Atypical thrombophlebitis patterns in head and neck infections have been described, including retrograde or cephalad propagation through the pterygoid venous plexus, cavernous sinus, ophthalmic venous system, and other interconnected venous pathways [7]. This mechanism may explain the coexistence of cervical swelling, orbital signs, cavernous sinus involvement, pulmonary septic emboli, and ischemic cerebral involvement in the same patient. The present case therefore supports the concept that Lemierre syndrome may present not only as a post-anginal internal jugular vein process, but also as septic thrombophlebitis spreading through interconnected cervicofacial and skull-base venous pathways.

The diagnosis of Lemierre syndrome is often difficult because the initial presentation may mimic meningitis, pneumonia, sepsis of unknown origin, complicated sinusitis, orbital cellulitis, or deep neck infection. In our case, the patient initially presented with fever, meningeal syndrome, anisocoria, altered consciousness, cervical swelling, and orbital signs. Lumbar puncture was appropriately deferred because of anisocoria and the risk of intracranial complications. Contrast-enhanced cervicothoracic computed tomography was decisive, allowing simultaneous identification of the infectious source, cervicocranial venous thromboses, intracranial complications, and pulmonary septic lesions. Magnetic resonance imaging and venography were useful to further assess cavernous sinus involvement, superior ophthalmic vein thrombosis, ischemic complications, and follow-up evolution. This case supports the use of early contrast-enhanced imaging that includes the head, neck, and thorax when severe rhinosinusogenic infection is associated with neurological, orbital, cervical, or pulmonary manifestations.

Microbiological confirmation may be difficult in Lemierre syndrome. Although Fusobacterium necrophorum remains the main pathogen, other organisms have been reported, including streptococci, Staphylococcus aureus, Eikenella corrodens, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and other Gram-negative bacilli [2], [3], [10], [11]. Therefore, the absence of Fusobacterium isolation should not exclude the diagnosis when the clinical and radiological pattern is strongly suggestive. In addition, negative cultures do not rule out Lemierre syndrome. In the meta-analysis by Gore, negative cultures were reported in 29 patients, corresponding to 7.4% of cases [12]. Possible explanations include the fastidious nature of anaerobic organisms, delayed growth, suboptimal anaerobic culture conditions, low circulating bacterial load, previous or partial antibiotic exposure, and absence of drainage of the infected focus [13]. In the present case, blood cultures were obtained before antibiotic therapy but remained negative, and no surgical drainage was performed; consequently, no focal specimen was available for culture or molecular testing. Adjunctive microbiological methods such as broad-range polymerase chain reaction or 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid sequencing could be considered when drained material or tissue samples are available, particularly in culture-negative cases [7], [13].

The empirical antibiotic regimen used in this patient was selected to cover the most likely pathogens in severe complicated rhinosinusogenic infection with septic thrombophlebitis. Ceftriaxone provided broad coverage against common upper-airway and sinus pathogens, including streptococci and many Gram-negative bacilli. Metronidazole was added to ensure anaerobic coverage, particularly against Fusobacterium species and other anaerobes involved in head and neck deep-space infections. The aminoglycoside was included in the initial regimen because of the severity of the septic presentation, the presence of intracranial and pulmonary complications, and the absence of microbiological documentation at admission. This approach is consistent with the principle that treatment of Lemierre syndrome should include urgent broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics with reliable anaerobic coverage [3], [4], [13]. The total duration of antibiotic therapy is usually prolonged, often four to six weeks, with an initial intravenous phase followed by adaptation according to clinical response, microbiological results, and radiological evolution [3], [4]. In our patient, six weeks of antibiotic therapy were administered, including approximately three weeks of intravenous treatment, with favorable clinical and radiological outcome.

The role of anticoagulation in Lemierre syndrome remains controversial. No randomized controlled trial is available, and retrospective studies and meta-analyses have not demonstrated a clear statistically significant benefit on mortality or vessel recanalization [12], [14]. However, anticoagulation is frequently considered in selected high-risk situations, including intracranial venous extension, cavernous sinus thrombosis, superior ophthalmic vein thrombosis, thrombus progression, extensive thrombosis, persistent septic embolization, or lack of clinical improvement despite appropriate antibiotic therapy. In our patient, the presence of bilateral cavernous sinus thrombosis, superior ophthalmic vein thrombosis, and ischemic cerebral involvement supported the decision to initiate therapeutic anticoagulation after careful evaluation of hemorrhagic risk. The favorable outcome in this case cannot be attributed to anticoagulation alone, but it supports individualized multidisciplinary decision-making in extensive intracranial venous thrombosis.

Surgery is not systematic in Lemierre syndrome and should be tailored to the infectious source, the presence of drainable collections, and the clinical course. Drainage of abscesses, sinus surgery, mastoidectomy, or cervical drainage may be required when there is a surgically accessible collection, clinical deterioration, airway compromise, or insufficient response to medical therapy [3], [5]. Ligation or excision of the internal jugular vein is now exceptional and reserved for selected refractory cases. In the present case, no surgical drainage was performed because otolaryngological assessment found no accessible drainable cervical collection and the patient improved rapidly under medical treatment. This management point is important: the presence of deep facial space infection does not automatically mandate surgery if source control is not technically accessible or if the patient shows early and sustained clinical improvement under appropriate antimicrobial therapy.

Comparison with previous reports helps clarify the incremental value of this case. Superior ophthalmic vein thrombosis and intracranial complications have been described after rhinosinusitis, supporting the potential for severe orbital and intracranial spread from paranasal sinus infections [15], [16]. Septic pulmonary emboli are a classical metastatic manifestation of Lemierre syndrome and have also been reported in atypical otogenic or mastoid presentations [17], [18]. Epidemiological data on Fusobacterium bacteremia and radiological reports emphasizing the usefulness of computed tomography for detecting extensive occult thrombophlebitis further support early broad imaging when Lemierre syndrome is suspected [19], [20]. Compared with these reports, the present case is distinguished by the simultaneous involvement of multiple venous territories and organs: the internal jugular vein and its tributaries, both cavernous sinuses, the superior ophthalmic vein, the brain, and the lungs. This constellation supports the concept of Lemierre syndrome as potentially multidirectional septic thrombophlebitis rather than a disease limited to a single anatomical pathway.

The main lesson from this case is therefore not simply that Lemierre syndrome should be considered in severe head and neck infection, which is already established. Rather, this case shows that complicated pediatric rhinosinusitis may produce severe Lemierre-like septic thrombophlebitis with deep facial space extension, bilateral cavernous sinus thrombosis, orbital venous involvement, ischemic stroke, and pulmonary septic emboli, even when blood cultures are negative. It also illustrates that favorable outcome is possible without surgical drainage when no accessible collection is identified, provided that broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy, selected anticoagulation for extensive intracranial venous thrombosis, corticosteroids for severe orbital inflammation, and close multidisciplinary monitoring are rapidly implemented.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a severe pediatric presentation of Lemierre syndrome of rhinosinusogenic origin complicated by multiple cervicocranial venous thromboses, orbital involvement, pulmonary septic emboli, and ischemic stroke. Its main contribution is to show that complicated rhinosinusitis may generate a multidirectional pattern of septic thrombophlebitis involving cervicofacial, orbital, intracranial, and pulmonary territories. Culture negativity should not exclude the diagnosis when the clinical and radiological pattern is characteristic. Early contrast-enhanced imaging, prompt broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy with anaerobic coverage, individualized anticoagulation for extensive intracranial venous thrombosis, and multidisciplinary monitoring are essential to prevent morbidity and mortality.
