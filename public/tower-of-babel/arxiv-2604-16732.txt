Dynamics of spinor Bose-Einstein condensates close to spin-spatial resonances
W. Wills, D. Blume, Q. Guan

Abstract
We develop a coupled-channel framework to describe the dynamics of spinor Bose-Einstein condensates (BECs), with particular emphasis on the behavior near resonances between spin dynamics and spatial excitations. Taking advantage of the disparity between the spin-dependent and spin-independent scattering lengths in typical spinor BECs, the Bogoliubov modes of the spin-independent part of the full system Hamiltonian provide an efficient set of basis functions for describing the system dynamics in a coupled-channel framework. For quadratic Zeeman shifts far from any resonance, the system can be described by a single spatial wavefunction during the spin dynamics, i.e., the so-called single-mode approximation holds. By tuning the quadratic Zeeman shift, we find resonant excitations of the Bogoliubov modes, which can be classified into two categories: those with particle–hole correlations and those without particle-hole correlations. We show that the beyond-quadratic-order terms that are neglected in standard Bogoliubov theories become increasingly important for capturing the long-time dynamics of the system near resonances. The coupled-channel framework is benchmarked against results from 1D Gross–Pitaevskii equation simulations. The framework developed in this work not only provides a numerically efficient tool for describing spinor BEC dynamics governed by different length scales, but also provides a clean physical interpretation of resonance phenomena in spinor BECs. Applications of this approach to other systems and extensions to the beyond-mean-field regime are also discussed.



I
Introduction

Spinor Bose–Einstein condensates (BECs), i.e., BECs with multiple internal spin
degrees of freedom, are unique platforms due to the presence of intrinsic
elastic and inelastic spin collisions, which, for example, are crucial for
generating spin entanglement and realizing SU(1,1)
interferometers
25
;
43
. Spinor BECs are
frequently realized in
23
Na and
87
Rb quantum
gases
43
, for which the spin-dependent scattering length
is much smaller than the spin-independent one. The disparity between the two
associated energy scales implies that the spatial part of the wavefunction is
essentially frozen even when the spin degrees of freedom are in highly
non-equilibrium states, providing a clean platform for studying pure spin
physics
50
. Within the single-mode approximation (SMA),
the spin dynamics are governed by the spin-interaction energy and the quadratic
Zeeman shift
21
;
8
;
52
. With
magnetic fields and microwave dressing
16
;
53
, the latter can be tuned over a wide range, which further
enhances the system’s tunability and enables the study of diverse quantum
phenomena, including equilibrium and non-equilibrium quantum phase
transitions
38
;
5
;
33
;
11
;
49
;
9
;
54
;
3
;
45
;
17
,
quantum state engineering
10
;
35
;
36
, and quantum metrology
48
;
36
;
32
;
34
;
12
.

Recent work by two of us and collaborators shows that the SMA can be extended
to scenarios where the spin components share a single common time-dependent (as
opposed to time-independent) spatial wavefunction that may undergo surprisingly
strong dynamics over timescales that are long compared to the coherent spin
evolution
18
. In this dynamical single-mode
approximation (dSMA), the spin and spatial degrees of freedom are
asymmetrically coupled: the spin dynamics depend on the spatial part of the
wavefunction, while the spatial part of the wavefunction remains largely
independent of the spin dynamics. Exploiting the dynamical spatial wavefunction
as an additional control knob, we used the
23
Na spinor BEC as a quantum
simulator to study Landau–Zener tunneling during Bloch
oscillations
19
and to engineer the dynamical
phase diagram under periodic modulation of the trap
frequency
2
.

Similar to the SMA, the validity of the dSMA stems from the energy scale
separation between the spin-dependent and spin-independent parts of the spinor
BEC Hamiltonian. A natural follow-up question is whether the spatial dynamics
can be affected by the non-equilibrium nature of the spin degrees of freedom.
Indeed, this occurs in multiple scenarios. An iconic example is the formation
of spin domains induced by quenching a parameter of the spinor BEC across a
quantum phase transition, where the condensate size is much larger than the
spin healing length
44
;
24
;
46
;
47
. In such large spinor BECs,
recent work has also demonstrated that spin chaos enhances the turbulent
behavior of the spatial dynamics
26
;
29
. For trapped spinor BECs whose size
is much smaller than the spin healing length, spatial excitations can be, as
has been confirmed experimentally, resonantly triggered by the spin dynamics
when the quadratic Zeeman shift is close to certain characteristic
values
39
;
40
;
27
;
23
.

The mean-field Gross–Pitaevskii equation and Bogoliubov theory are powerful
theoretical tools for understanding non-equilibrium phenomena in spinor BECs,
such as dynamical instabilities
25
. Several works,
including those by two of us and our collaborators, have shown that the
resonant value of the quadratic Zeeman shift for trapped spinor BECs can be
predicted using the spectrum of a particle confined in an effective potential
formed by the external trap and the background atomic density
distribution
27
;
22
. This paper extends previous
work by developing a Bogoliubov-theory-based framework that captures both the
resonance conditions and the long-time dynamics near such resonances. Going
beyond the effective potential picture, which corresponds to excitations with
no particle–hole correlations, our theory also predicts a new class of
resonances that exhibit particle-hole correlations. By studying the long-time
dynamics of the system near resonances, we identify the contributions of
higher-order terms, which are neglected in standard Bogoliubov theory and
represent interactions between quasi-particles. These effective
excitation-excitation interactions have previously been found to be important
for describing different damping mechanisms such as Beliaev damping in spinor
BECs
37
.

In general, Bogoliubov theory is particularly useful for describing collective
excitations on top of an unperturbed many-body background. In this work, we
consider spinor BECs in which the spin degrees of freedom are in
far-from-equilibrium states, while the spatial degrees of freedom are
approximately frozen, except near resonances. We find that the Bogoliubov
eigenmodes of the spin-independent part of the full many-body Hamiltonian
provide a convenient basis. One challenge in using Bogoliubov eigenmodes,
particularly those with particle–hole correlations, is that the particle number
is not conserved. This is the case because standard Bogoliubov theory is
non-Hermitian and breaks the
U(1)
symmetry
6
. These features make
it challenging to describe the long-time dynamics of systems with such a basis.
Several approaches to overcome this challenge have been discussed in the
literature, such as consistently including the back-action of the excitations
on the background
7
;
4
;
41
or
introducing an additional mode that corresponds to phase fluctuations of the
background
31
. A modified version of standard
Bogoliubov theory, which preserves the
U(1)
symmetry, has also been developed
in the literature
6
;
14
. The framework developed in
our work builds on the
U(1)
-symmetry-preserving Bogoliubov theory of
Refs.
6
;
14
. While our paper focuses on the
mean-field regime, extensions to the beyond-mean-field regime are expected to
follow an analogous derivation.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II introduces the
system Hamiltonian, outlines the main logic behind the derivation of the
coupled-channel model developed in this work, and places our key results into
context. Section III reviews the Bogoliubov theory and presents the derivation
of the coupled-channel model and the resonance conditions. Finally, Section IV
presents conclusions and an outlook. Numerical details are relegated to
Appendix
A
.

II
System overview and summary of key results

II.1
Mean-field theory for spin-1 BECs

The mean-field energy functional for spin-1 BECs described by the spinor wave
function
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=\left[\psi_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t),\psi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t),\psi_{-1}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\right]^{T}
is

H=H_{\text{scalar}}+H_{s},

(1)

where the spin-independent energy functional
H_{\text{scalar}}
and the spin-dependent
energy functional
H_{s}
are

\displaystyle H_{\text{scalar}}={}

\displaystyle\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\times

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}+V(\bm{\mathbf{r}})+\frac{g_{n}}{2}\left\lvert\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\right\rvert^{2}\right]\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)

(2)

and

H_{s}=\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\left\{q\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{z}^{2}\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\right.\\
\left.+\frac{g_{s}}{2}\sum_{j=x,y,z}\left[\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\right]^{2}\right\},

(3)

respectively. Here,
V(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
denotes an external single-particle potential, the
spin-1 Pauli matrices
\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}
take the forms

\begin{gathered}\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&-i&0\\
i&0&-i\\
0&i&0\end{pmatrix},\\
\quad\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&-1\end{pmatrix},\end{gathered}

(4)

and
m
,
\hbar
, and
q
denote the atomic mass, the reduced Planck constant,
and the quadratic Zeeman shift, respectively. Using the normalization

\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=1,

(5)

the nonlinear coupling constants
g_{n}
and
g_{s}
for a 3D spinor BEC are
related to the spin-independent scattering length
a_{n}
, the spin-dependent
scattering length
a_{s}
, and the total particle number
N
via

g_{n/s}=\frac{4\pi\hbar^{2}a_{n/s}(N-1)}{m}.

(6)

For typical spinor BECs, the value of
g_{s}
is much smaller than that of
g_{n}

(e.g.,
g_{s}/g_{n}\approx 1/28.10
for
23
Na
28
), which
leads to a clear scale separation between the dynamics of the spin degrees of
freedom and those of the spatial components. This scale separation underlies
the widely-used SMA treatment of spinor BECs
21
;
8
;
52
.

At the mean-field level, the equation of motion for
\psi_{j}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
is
determined by varying the mean-field energy functional
H
[see
Eq. (
1
)] with respect to
\psi^{*}_{j}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
, yielding the
spinor Gross-Pitaevskii equation (SGPE)
25

i\hbar\frac{\partial\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)}{\partial t}=\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{SGP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t),

(7)

with

\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{SGP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=\mathcal{H}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)+\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{s}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t),

(8)

\mathcal{H}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=-\frac{\hbar^{2}\nabla^{2}}{2m}+V(\bm{\mathbf{r}})+g_{n}\left\lvert\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\right\rvert^{2},

(9)

and

\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{s}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=q\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{z}^{2}+g_{s}\sum_{j=x,y,z}\left[\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\right]\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}.

(10)

The SMA assumes that a common spatial wavefunction
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
is shared
by all spin components, i.e.,

\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\approx\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t),

(11)

where
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
denotes the spin wavefunction and
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
is normalized
to
1
:

\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})|^{2}=1.

(12)

The spatial wavefunction
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
can be determined by minimizing the
scalar energy functional
H_{\text{scalar}}
.

We use
\mathcal{H}_{\text{scalar}}^{\text{SMA}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
and
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
to denote the
mean-field Hamiltonians
\mathcal{H}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
and
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{s}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
under the SMA:

\mathcal{H}_{\text{scalar}}^{\text{SMA}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
and
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
are obtained by
replacing
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
in Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
) with

\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
. After this replacement, the only dynamical variable left
is the spin vector
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
, for which the SMA equation of motion reads

i\hbar\frac{\partial\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)}{\partial t}=[\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)+\mu_{n}]\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t),

(13)

where

\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)=q\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{z}^{2}+c_{s}\sum_{j=x,y,z}\left[\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)\right]^{2},

(14)

\mu_{n}=\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\phi_{0}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\mathcal{H}^{\text{SMA}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}),

(15)

and

c_{s}=g_{s}\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})|^{4}.

(16)

II.2
Dynamics near resonances

Because of the separation of energy scales between the spin and spatial degrees
of freedom, one can expect that the spatial dynamics are typically only weakly
coupled to the spin. This means that if one starts from an initial state that
satisfies the SMA assumption in Eq. (
11
), the state retains,
to a good approximation, the spin-spatial-decoupled form throughout the
far-from-equilibrium spin dynamics. It turns out, however, that the SMA is not
always valid, even if a scale separation between the spatial and spin degrees
of freedom exists. For instance, as shown in Refs.
22
;
23
,
significant spatial dynamics can arise at certain resonant values of the
quadratic Zeeman shift
q
, thereby breaking the SMA assumption.

In this section, we aim, through SGPE simulations, to identify the parameter
regimes in which the spinor BEC exhibits substantial spatial dynamics.
Throughout, the initial state is prepared to be of the SMA form given in
Eq. (
11
), with
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
being the ground state of

\mathcal{H}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
. The system starts in a non-equilibrium spin state and
subsequently undergoes spin dynamics. We probe the spatial dynamics by
examining the density of a single hyperfine component as well as the total
density of the condensate. For illustrative purposes, we perform SGPE
simulations for 1D systems. The SGPE simulations reveal several resonances; the
underlying nature of these resonances is described in
Sec.
III
.

indicates that Bogoliubov eigenmodes provide an effective basis for a
coupled-channel description of the resonance dynamics. These observations form
the backbone of the theory framework developed in this paper.

II.3
Framing of our results

This paper develops a coupled-channel framework based on a
particle-number-preserving Bogoliubov theory to describe the dynamics of spinor
BECs. Our framework is designed to accurately capture resonances between spin
and spatial degrees of freedom. Aligned with our goal of describing spatial
excitations atop the SMA solutions of spinor BECs, our Bogoliubov theory is
formulated around a vacuum state that is expressed as a direct product of a
spatial wavefunction and a spin vector, where the former is given by the
spatial part of the scalar mean-field ground state and the latter is determined
on the fly [see the discussion later in this section, especially
Eq. (
23
)]. The channel functions are constructed from the
corresponding Bogoliubov eigenmodes. We find that the Bogoliubov spectrum can
be divided into two uncoupled sectors: one with spin parallel to the background
and the other with spin perpendicular to the background. The former corresponds
to excitations with particle–hole correlations, while the latter corresponds to
excitations without such correlations. These findings uncover the nature of the
two types of resonances observed in Figs.
1
(a) and

1
(c).

To overcome the non-hermiticity issue in the standard Bogoliubov theory that
leads to non-conserved normalization of the wavefunction and makes the
coupled-channel treatment diverge in the long-time limit, this work develops a
modified Bogoliubov theory. Our Bogoliubov Hamiltonian
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)

(see Sec.
III
C for the full mathematical form) is related to
the standard Bogoliubov Hamiltonian
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{scalar}}^{\text{std}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
via

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)=

\displaystyle\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime\prime}\begin{bmatrix}Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime\prime})&0\\
0&Q^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime\prime})\end{bmatrix}\times

(19)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{scalar}}^{\text{std}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime\prime},t)\begin{bmatrix}Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime\prime},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})&0\\
0&Q^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime\prime},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\end{bmatrix},

where the spatial projection operator is

Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\delta(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})-\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\phi_{0}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}).

(20)

We show that this construction yields equations of motion for the excitation
modes or perturbations that are only dependent on the populations of the
background state and independent of the background phase. The relative phase
between the perturbation and the background determines the current that governs
the transfer of population from the background state to the perturbations. In
standard Bogoliubov theory, this current must be treated self-consistently
order by order, together with the dynamics of the background state, when
truncating the number of channels to ensure that the normalization of the
wavefunction does not diverge. Our construction avoids this complication and
enables us to treat the dynamics of the excitation modes without explicitly
solving for the dynamics of the background phase and occupation.

It can be shown that the spectrum of
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)
is identical to
that of
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{scalar}}^{\text{std}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
, with eigenmodes that are related to each
other by the projection operator
Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
. For modes without
particle–hole correlations, the spatial wavefunction of the standard Bogoliubov
mode is “automatically” orthogonal to the background state; in this case,
both Bogoliubov theories predict the same spatial parts of the modes. For modes
with particle–hole correlations, however, the eigenmodes of our modified
Bogoliubov theory differ from those of the standard Bogoliubov theory.
Figure
3
compares the spatial part of the
particle-hole-correlated resonant mode, obtained from the FFT of the SGPE
results [also shown in Fig.
2
(f)], with the spatial part of the
corresponding modes obtained from our modified Bogoliubov theory and the
standard Bogoliubov linearization of
\mathcal{H}_{\text{scalar}}
for the resonance near

q=h\times 171
Hz. It can be seen clearly that the modified Bogoliubov
theory agrees with the FFT results significantly better than the standard
Bogoliubov theory.

(f). As in

(f), the solid blue and orange dashed lines correspond to

The green dash-dotted and red dotted lines correspond to the spatial part of

symmetry.
The
U(1)
-symmetry–conserving theory provides a significantly better

The extension developed in this work is related to the existing

U(1)
-symmetry–preserving Bogoliubov theory developed in Refs.
6
;
14
. In particular, the projection operator
Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

bears a nearly identical form to that used in Ref.
6
. Making an
analogy to the approach developed in Ref.
6
, their projection
operator for the spinor system reads

\bm{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)=\delta(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})-\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t).

(21)

The fundamental difference is that the time-dependent projection operator in
Ref.
6
projects out the time-dependent background wavefunction

\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
, which describes the mean-field dynamics of the full system and
thus requires the full SGPE solution. Instead, our time-independent projector

Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
projects out a time-independent spatial wavefunction, which is
determined
a priori
as the time-independent solution of the scalar part
of the full spinor BEC Hamiltonian. By looking at excitations orthogonal to a

specified
time-independent spatial background wavefunction, we are able
to study how the common spatial wavefunction of the spin components changes the
time evolution, thereby facilitating beyond-SMA corrections to be accounted for
systematically and efficiently.

Since the operator
Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
in this work acts only on the spatial
degrees of freedom, we treat the partition of the spatial Hilbert space
separately from that of the spin space. We define the time-dependent spin
projection operator as

\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{s}(t)=\mathbb{I}-\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}^{\dagger}(t),

(22)

where
\mathbb{I}
is the
3\times 3
identity matrix. Akin to the resulting
expression from the SMA ansatz in Eq. (
11
), the background
spin
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
is obtained via

\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{N_{0}(t)}\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\,\phi_{0}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t),

(23)

with the background population
N_{0}(t)
given by

N_{0}(t)=\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\,\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\phi_{0}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t).

(24)

Importantly, the background spin
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
, and correspondingly the spin
projection operator, is
not
specified
a priori
but is, instead,
treated as a dynamical variable that is determined self-consistently by the
coupling between the spin and spatial degrees of freedom. Our approach is
motivated by the observation that the spin degrees of freedom undergo
non-equilibrium dynamics in which spin excitations can become resonant with
spatial excitations, leading to significant modifications of the time evolution
of the spin dynamics compared to a predetermined spin trajectory. The
time-varying coupling between the background spin and the perturbation is
therefore central to our analysis.

Our approach of dividing the Hilbert space and treating the spatial and spin
degrees of freedom separately exploits the scale separation in spinor BECs and
provides deep insights into the physics near resonances. Within our approach,
the scalar Bogoliubov Hamiltonian is time-dependent due to the time-evolving
background spin state. By rotating away this time dependence and neglecting
contributions from the spin-dependent Bogoliubov Hamiltonian, we can derive
explicit resonance conditions (see Sec.
III.4
). Comparing with the
full time evolution, our treatment enables us to see the contributions of the
terms neglected in deriving the resonance conditions. The approximation used to
derive those conditions relies on the scale separation between the
spin-dependent Bogoliubov Hamiltonian and the energy spacing of the scalar
Bogoliubov Hamiltonian. For “weak” confinement (see Sec.
III

for details), this scale separation may not hold. Our SGPE simulations
indicate, that the breakdown of scale separation can lead to dynamical
instabilities.

Section
III
outlines our Bogoliubov theory and the resulting
“
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}
-model”, namely, a coupled-channel model that provides a

U(1)
-symmetric particle-conserving description of the dynamics near
resonances without the need of solving the full SGPE. Even though the exact
equations of motion for the mode amplitudes contain a multitude of terms due to
the nonlinearities of the system, the model is highly versatile in that it can
be truncated at various orders to examine the significance of different classes
of terms. For example, despite being motivated by Bogoliubov theory, the

\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}
-model includes terms that go beyond standard Bogoliubov theory due to the
Bogoliubov modes serving as a basis. In Section
III.4
, we show
that a truncation of the
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}
-model to an order comparable to what is
typically done in standard Bogoliubov theory, while retaining the coupling
between the background spin and the spatial excitation modes, explains the
resonance conditions of the SGPE. The ability to systematically vary the
truncation order of both the modes included and the terms retained in the
equations of motion provides a powerful tool for understanding the nature of
the excitations in the system and the significance of beyond-Bogoliubov effects
in the long-time dynamics. In addition, although our framework is applied to
the mean-field regime of the system, extending this approach beyond the
mean-field regime is conceptually straightforward.

III
Bogoliubov theory and the
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}
-model

III.1
General formalism

This section seeks to elucidate the origin of the modes discussed in
Sec.
II.2
for a spinor Bose–Einstein condensate using a Bogoliubov
theory-based framework. To introduce perturbations about the background spatial
wavefunction
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
, we write the mean-field spinor wavefunction as

\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=a_{0}(t)\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)+\delta\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t),

(25)

where the complex coefficient
a_{0}(t)
describes the time evolution of the
population and phase of atoms occupying the spatial wavefunction
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
,

\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
describes the internal spin dynamics, and
\delta\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
accounts
for the dynamics of the perturbations. Importantly, the background spin

\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
is not, in general, taken to be the SMA solution, i.e., it is not
predetermined, but instead must be determined alongside the perturbation

\delta\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
. If
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
was taken to be the SMA solution alongside a
predetermined
a_{0}(t)
, then Eq. (
25
) would serve as the
starting point for developing what we are referring to as standard Bogoliubov
theory throughout this paper. We impose that
\delta\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
is spatially
orthogonal to
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
for each spin component, i.e.,

\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\,\phi_{0}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\delta\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=\bm{\mathbf{0}}.

(26)

With this choice,
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
fully captures the internal spin dynamics
associated with the spatial wavefunction
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
, i.e., there is no
ambiguity in distinguishing the internal spin dynamics of the background state
from that of the excited spatial modes.

In what follows, we introduce separate projection operators for the spatial and
spin degrees of freedom. We define the spatial projection operator

Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
via Eq. (
20
). Using
Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
, the
perturbed and background components of the spinor wavefunction can be written
as

\delta\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\,Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)

(27)

and

a_{0}(t)\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)=\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\,\left[\delta(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})-Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\right]\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t),

(28)

respectively. This shows that the operator
Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
partitions the
spatial Hilbert space into the subspace spanned by
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
and the
subspace spanned by spatial functions orthogonal to
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
.

We normalize
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
such that

\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)=1.

(29)

There is a gauge freedom in choosing the overall phase of
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
. It is
straightforward to verify that Eq. (
29
) implies

\dot{\bm{\mathbf{s}}}_{0}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)+\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}^{\dagger}(t)\dot{\bm{\mathbf{s}}}_{0}(t)=0
, where
the dot above the background spin
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
denotes the time derivative. This
implies that
\dot{\bm{\mathbf{s}}}_{0}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
is purely imaginary. We
therefore impose the gauge condition

\dot{\bm{\mathbf{s}}}_{0}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)=0.

(30)

This choice implies that
a_{0}(t)
describes the overall phase and the
population fraction of the background state
a_{0}(t)\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
,
while
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
describes the internal spin structure within this background
state.

The dynamics of
\delta\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
are closely tied to the Bogoliubov spectrum of
perturbations about the background state
a_{0}(t)\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
. A major
challenge in the derivation is that this background state depends non-trivially
on time, which reduces the direct usefulness of the Bogoliubov spectrum for our
purposes. In a scalar system with a well-defined stationary background state,
the only time dependence of the background arises from the phase evolution that
is governed by the
predetermined
chemical potential, which can be gauged
away. In contrast, in our treatment, as discussed above, neither the time
evolution of
a_{0}(t)
nor that of
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
are known
a priori
;
instead, they are part of the solution to be determined. This makes it
impossible to fully rotate away the time dependence of the Bogoliubov
Hamiltonian.

To simplify the theory and establish a connection to Bogoliubov theory, we
leverage the characteristic time scales of
a_{0}(t)
and
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
. Since the
phase evolution of the background state is fully captured by
a_{0}(t)
, it is
natural to expect that
a_{0}(t)
evolves on timescales that are set by the
scalar Hamiltonian, such as the timescale associated with the chemical
potential or the spin-independent mean-field energy. These timescales are much
shorter than the characteristic timescales of
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
, which are set by the
spin-dependent Hamiltonian. These considerations suggest that the dynamics of

\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
are “more adiabatic” than that of
a_{0}(t)
. Motivated by this
observation, we define the perturbation
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
as

\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=a_{0}^{*}(t)\delta\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t).

(31)

In terms of
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
, the expansion of
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
becomes

\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=a_{0}(t)\left[\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)+\frac{1}{N_{0}(t)}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\right],

(32)

where
N_{0}(t)=|a_{0}(t)|^{2}
is the population of the background state. The key
benefit of working with
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
as opposed to
\delta\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
is that the
redefinition of the perturbation [Eq. (31)] enables, as will be shown below,
the time-dependent phase of
a_{0}(t)
be removed from the equations of motion.
Equation (
32
) also clearly separates the phase dynamics
and spin dynamics of the background state, with the phase dynamics captured by

a_{0}(t)
being much faster than the spin dynamics captured by
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
. The
perturbation
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
then is only sensitive to the slower time scales
that are associated with the population and spin structure of the background
state. As mentioned in Sec.
II.3
, our definition of

\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
bears close similarity to the perturbations introduced in
Refs.
6
;
14
and, to leading order, the Bogoliubov
Hamiltonian for
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
has an analogous form as the Bogoliubov
Hamiltonian obtained in particle–number–conserving Bogoliubov theory.

With these definitions in place, the main objective is to derive equations of
motion for
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
,
a_{0}(t)
, and
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
. One approach is to treat

a_{0}(t)
,
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
, and
\delta\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
as independent dynamical variables
and to express the full Hamiltonian
H
in terms of these dynamical variables.
From this representation, the equations of motion for
a_{0}(t)
,
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
, and

\delta\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
can be derived. The equation of motion for
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)

subsequently follows from Eq. (
31
) together with the identity

\frac{\partial\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{a_{0}^{*}(t)}\frac{\partial a^{*}_{0}(t)}{\partial t}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)+a_{0}^{*}(t)\frac{\partial\delta\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)}{\partial t}.

(33)

For the gauge convention defined in Eq. (
30
), we find that the
equations of motion for
a_{0}(t)
,
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
, and
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
are

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{\partial a_{0}(t)}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle a_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}^{\dagger}(t)\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\phi^{*}_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{SGP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\times

(34)

\displaystyle\left[\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)+\frac{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)}{N_{0}(t)}\right],

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{\partial\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{s}(t)\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\phi^{*}_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{SGP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\times

(35)

\displaystyle\left[\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)+\frac{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)}{N_{0}(t)}\right],

and

i\hbar\frac{\partial\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)}{\partial t}=\\
\begin{aligned} &N_{0}(t)\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{SGP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)\\
+&\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{SGP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\\
-&\left[\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\dagger}_{0}(t)\phi^{*}_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{SGP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)\right]\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\\
-&\left[\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{SGP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)\right]\frac{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)}{N_{0}(t)}.\end{aligned}

(36)

In Eqs. (
34
)–(
36
), it is implied that the
wavefunction
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
that is contained in
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{SGP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
[see
Eqs. (
8
)-(
10
)] is given by
Eq. (
32
).
Equations (
34
)–(
36
) form a closed set of coupled
differential equations for the three dynamical variables.

The first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
36
), which is
independent of
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
, acts as a seeding term for the growth of

\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
. This term is nonzero provided
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
is, at
fixed time
t
, not an eigenstate of
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{SGP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
and the spatial integral
does not vanish accidentally. Since
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
is, for
far-from-equilibrium states, not an eigenstate of
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{SGP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
, coupling to
excited modes is, in general, present if the spatial degrees of freedom, the
spin degrees of freedom, or both are out of equilibrium.

III.2
Discussion on
U(1)
symmetry conservation

By discretizing the perturbation
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
on a spatial grid, the coupled
differential equations can be solved numerically, yielding results that are
fully equivalent to those obtained by solving the SGPE. One advantage of the
formalism summarized in Eqs. (
34
)–(
36
) is that it
allows us to treat the background and the excitations separately, thereby
facilitating a clear conceptual understanding of the nature of spin-spatial
resonances and providing a route to engineer resonance conditions. Another
technical advantage is that it allows the perturbation
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
to be
expanded in a set of well-defined basis functions, namely the Bogoliubov modes,
with the potential to capture the main features of the dynamics using a
relatively small number of modes. In standard Bogoliubov theory, the
basis-expansion approach breaks the system’s
U(1)
symmetry and therefore
suffers from the inability to preserve wavefunction normalization during the
time evolution. As discussed below, the formalism developed here resolves this
issue.

First, it can be shown that Eq. (
35
) preserves both the
normalization of
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
and the gauge convention defined in
Eq. (
30
), provided that the spin projection operator
\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{s}(t)

is retained on the right-hand side of the equation. Second,
Eq. (
36
) can be shown to preserve the orthogonality between

\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
and
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
, provided the spatial projection operator

Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
is retained in the first two terms on the right-hand side of
the equation and
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{SGP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
is Hermitian. Third, since
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{SGP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)

depends on
N_{0}(t)
rather than
a_{0}(t)
, the equations of motion for

\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
and
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
[Eqs. (
35
) and (
36
)]
depend on
N_{0}(t)
but not on the phase of
a_{0}(t)
, i.e., the phase of the
background state. This
U(1)
-symmetry–conserving property of the theory
implies that the normalization of the wavefunction
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
is conserved
even if
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
is expanded in terms of a finite number of modes (an
incomplete basis) as opposed to a complete basis.

Using conservation of the normalization of
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
,

N_{0}(t)+\frac{1}{N_{0}(t)}\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=1,

(37)

N_{0}(t)
can be eliminated from Eqs. (
35
)
and (
36
) by solving Eq. (
37
), assuming

N_{0}(t)>1/2
, for
N_{0}(t)
,

N_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left[1-4\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\right]^{1/2}.

(38)

Therefore, the dynamics of
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
and
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
can be determined
without solving for
a_{0}(t)
. Given
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
,
N_{0}(t)
can be obtained
from Eq. (
38
). Since
|a_{0}(t)|=\sqrt{N_{0}(t)}
, it follows that

a_{0}(t)
is determined up to an arbitrary phase. Since this overall phase does
not enter into any physical observable, it is irrelevant for our purposes. It
follows that there is no need to solve for
a_{0}(t)
order by order, consistent
with the approximations used for determining
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
and
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
.
Instead, the
U(1)
symmetry guarantees that determining
|a_{0}(t)|
from the
normalization condition yields a solution that is automatically accurate to the
same order as
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
and
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
. In the following analysis, we
therefore focus on the equations of motion for
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
and
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
.

III.3
Connection to “standard” Bogoliubov theory

To make the connection to standard Bogoliubov theory explicit, we Taylor expand
the right-hand side of Eq. (
35
) up to linear order in
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
. This yields

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{\partial\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\approx\left[\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)-\mu_{s}(t)\right]\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)+g_{s}\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{s}(t)\times

(39)

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{j=x,y,z}\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}\right]\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\phi_{0}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\phi^{2}_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t),

where the time-dependent spin chemical potential
\mu_{s}(t)
is given by

\mu_{s}(t)=\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t).

(40)

Doing the same for Eq. (
36
), we find

i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\\
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\end{bmatrix}\approx\\
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)+\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{GP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\\
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\end{bmatrix},

(41)

where the driving term
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
is given by

\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=\left[\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)-\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)\right]\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)\\
-\phi^{*}_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{*}_{0}(t)\end{bmatrix}.

(42)

The full Bogoliubov Hamiltonian
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{GP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)
contains the scalar part

\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)
and the spin part
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{S}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)
,
i.e.,
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{GP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)=\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)+\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{S}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)
,
where

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)=

(43)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{scalar}}^{\text{SMA}}-\mu_{n}\right]\delta(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})+g_{n}\left(Q\left||\phi_{0}|^{2}\right|Q\right)\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\dagger}_{0}(t)&g_{n}\left(Q\left|\phi^{2}_{0}\right|Q^{*}\right)\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{T}_{0}(t)\\
-g_{n}\left(Q^{*}\left|\phi^{*2}_{0}\right|Q\right)\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{*}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\dagger}_{0}(t)&-\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{scalar}}^{\text{SMA}}-\mu_{n}\right]\delta(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})-g_{n}\left(Q^{*}\left||\phi_{0}|^{2}\right|Q^{*}\right)\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\dagger}_{0}(t)\end{bmatrix}

and

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{s}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\left(Q\left|\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)\right|Q\right)-\mu_{s}(t)&0\\
0&-\left(Q^{*}\left|\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)\right|Q^{*}\right)+\mu_{s}(t)\end{bmatrix}\delta(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

(44)

\displaystyle+g_{s}\begin{bmatrix}\left(Q\left||\phi_{0}|^{2}\right|Q\right)\sum_{j=x,y,z}\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\dagger}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}&\left(Q\left|\phi_{0}^{2}\right|Q^{*}\right)\sum_{j=x,y,z}\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{T}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}^{T}\\
-\left(Q^{*}\left|\phi_{0}^{*2}\right|Q\right)\sum_{j=x,y,z}\bm{\mathbf{F}}^{T}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{*}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\dagger}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}&-\left(Q^{*}\left||\phi_{0}|^{2}\right|Q^{*}\right)\sum_{j=x,y,z}\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\dagger}_{0}(t)\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}\end{bmatrix}.

Here, expressions of the form
\left(\cdots\left|\cdots\right|\cdots\right)

should be understood as the
(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
matrix element of the triple matrix
product; e.g.,
(Q\left||\phi_{0}|^{2}\right|Q)=\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime\prime}Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime\prime})|\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime\prime})|^{2}Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime\prime},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
. Both
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)

and
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{s}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)
are time dependent due to the time dependence of

\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
.

To get rid of the time dependence of
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)
, we move
to the rotating frame of
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
. Note that the Taylor-expanded equation of
motion for
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
[Eq. (
39
)] is governed by two
terms of different orders in
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
, with the leading- and
sub-leading-order terms proportional to
\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)-\mu_{s}(t)
[this term is
proportional to
g_{s}
and independent of
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
] and
g_{s}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
,
respectively. Since both
g_{s}
and
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
are small, we neglect the

g_{s}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
term (for our current purposes, we are counting it as being
effectively of quadratic order) and find approximately

\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)=\bm{\mathbf{U}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0),

(45)

where

\bm{\mathbf{U}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)=\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{t}\left[\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t^{\prime})-\mu_{s}(t^{\prime})\right]dt^{\prime}/\hbar\right),

(46)

i.e., the time evolution is, at this order, governed by the SMA solution to

\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
. In the rotating frame of
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
, we define the rotated
perturbation
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
via

\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=\left[\bm{\mathbf{U}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)\right]^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t).

(47)

The corresponding equation of motion up to linear order in
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
is

i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\\
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\end{bmatrix}\approx\\
\begin{aligned} \tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)-\left[\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)-\mu_{s}(t)\right]&\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\\
-\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\end{bmatrix}\\
+\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{\text{GP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)&\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\\
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\end{bmatrix},\end{aligned}

(48)

where the rotated driving term is given by

\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathscr{H}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)\\
-\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathscr{H}}}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\phi_{0}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}^{*}(0)\end{bmatrix}

(49)

with

\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathscr{H}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=\left[\bm{\mathbf{U}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)\right]^{-1}\left[\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)-\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)\right]\times\\
\bm{\mathbf{U}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t).

(50)

The rotated full Bogoliubov Hamiltonian consists of the rotated scalar part and
the rotated spin part, i.e.,

\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{\text{GP}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)=\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})+\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{s}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)
. The rotated
scalar part
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
has the same form as
Eq. (
43
) with
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)
representing the spin state at

t=0
. The rotated spin part
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{s}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)
is obtained using the same
strategy as the one we used to obtain Eq. (
44
). In addition,

\mu_{s}(t)
is replaced by
\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)
. A key observation is that

\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{H}}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)-\bm{\mathbf{h}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)
is independent of single-particle terms such
as the quadratic Zeeman shift term. Because of this, the contribution to the
equation of motion of
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
from the rotated spin Bogoliubov
Hamiltonian is of the order
g_{s}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
, which can be—consistent with
our earlier counting of orders—neglected. With these approximations, the
structure of
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
is determined by the time-independent Bogoliubov
Hamiltonian
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
with the time-dependent driving term

\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
, i.e.,

i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\\
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\end{bmatrix}\approx\\
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)+\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\\
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\end{bmatrix}.

(51)

The eigenmodes of
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
are obtained by solving

\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}_{k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\\
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\end{bmatrix}=E_{k}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}_{k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\\
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\end{bmatrix},

(52)

where
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
and
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
are spin-spatial wavefunctions that
represent, respectively, the Bogoliubov particle-component and Bogoliubov
hole-component. Note that
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
depends on the initial
background spin state
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)
through the spin operator

\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}^{\dagger}(0)
; off-diagonal elements of
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

vanish for a perturbation that is proportional to a spinor that is orthogonal
to
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)
. The Bogoliubov eigenmodes for spin-perpendicular excitations
therefore satisfy
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})=0
. The presence of the spin operator suggests a
decomposition of the Bogoliubov spectrum into two subspaces. This motivates us
to decompose
[\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t),\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)]^{T}
via

\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\\
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}_{\perp}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\\
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}_{\perp}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}_{\parallel}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\\
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}_{\parallel}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\end{bmatrix},

(53)

where the perpendicular and parallel excitations can be further expanded into
the two types of Bogoliubov eigenmodes via

\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}_{\perp}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\\
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}_{\perp}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{k>0}\begin{bmatrix}u_{\perp k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{s}}}_{k}(t)\\
0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
u_{\perp k}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{s}}}^{*}_{k}(t)\end{bmatrix}

(54)

and

\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}_{\parallel}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\\
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}_{\parallel}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{k>0}b_{k}(t)\begin{bmatrix}u_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)\\
v_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{*}_{0}(0)\end{bmatrix}+b^{*}_{k}(t)\begin{bmatrix}v^{*}_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)\\
u^{*}_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{*}_{0}(0)\end{bmatrix},

(55)

where we have, by definition,

\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{s}}}_{k>0}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)=0.

(56)

Note that
u_{\perp k>0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
,
u_{\parallel k>0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
, and

v_{\parallel k>0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
are scalar functions that satisfy

\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\perp}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\begin{bmatrix}u_{\perp k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\\
0\end{bmatrix}=E_{\perp k}\begin{bmatrix}u_{\perp k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\\
0\end{bmatrix}

(57)

and

\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\parallel}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\begin{bmatrix}u_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\\
v_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\end{bmatrix}=E_{\parallel k}\begin{bmatrix}u_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\\
v_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\end{bmatrix},

(58)

with the block
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\perp}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
and the block

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\parallel}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
of
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
for perpendicular and parallel
spin states, respectively, given by

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\perp}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\\
\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{H}^{\text{SMA}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})-\mu_{n}&0\\
0&-\left[\mathcal{H}^{\text{SMA}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})-\mu_{n}\right]\end{bmatrix}\delta(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

(59)

and

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\parallel}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\begin{bmatrix}\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{scalar}}^{\text{SMA}}-\mu_{n}\right]\delta(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})+g_{n}\left(Q\left||\phi_{0}|^{2}\right|Q\right)&g_{n}\left(Q\left|\phi^{2}_{0}\right|Q^{*}\right)\\
-g_{n}\left(Q^{*}\left|\phi^{*2}_{0}\right|Q\right)&-\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{scalar}}^{\text{SMA}}-\mu_{n}\right]\delta(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})-g_{n}\left(Q^{*}\left||\phi_{0}|^{2}\right|Q^{*}\right)\end{bmatrix}.

(60)

The index
k
in Eqs. (
54
)
and (
55
) runs only over positive values because
contributions from the positive-energy and negative-energy eigenmodes are
included explicitly in each term of the sum.

In Eq. (
56
), we choose the normalization

\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\,\left\lvert u_{\perp k>0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\right\rvert^{2}=1

(61)

and an unnormalized time-dependent spin part
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{s}}}_{k}(t)
with the quantity

\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{s}}}^{\dagger}_{k>0}(t)\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{s}}}_{k>0}(t)/N_{0}(t)
representing the occupation
fraction of the excited state labeled by
k
. Since there exist two spin
projections for a spin-1 system that are orthogonal to
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)
, there exist
two degenerate modes with different spins that are associated with each spatial
wavefunction
u_{\perp k>0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
. With time evolution,
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{s}}}_{k>0}(t)

may precess in the spin plane that is orthogonal to
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{s}}}_{0}(0)
, hence the
explicit time dependence.

In contrast, in Eq. (
55
), we choose the typical
normalization

\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\left[|u_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})|^{2}-|v_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})|^{2}\right]=1

(62)

with
b_{k}(t)
and
b^{*}_{k}(t)
being the complex amplitudes corresponding to the

k
-th pair of Bogoliubov eigenmodes. Since the excitations
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}_{\parallel}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)

are, in spin space, parallel to
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)
, these excitations exhibit
scalar-BEC-like behavior including the particle-hole-coupled structure
characterized by the wavefunctions
u_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
and
v_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
.

Note that
u_{\perp k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
can be understood as an excitation in the
effective potential
V(\bm{\mathbf{r}})+g_{n}|\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})|^{2}-\mu_{n}
, consistent with the
effective potential picture introduced in Ref.
22
. The pair

[u_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}),v_{\parallel k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})]^{T}
, in contrast, corresponds to
excitations of a scalar BEC obtained using
U(1)
-symmetry-preserving
Bogoliubov theory
6
. Because of the projection operators

Q(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
and
Q^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
that appear in each element of

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\parallel}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
, the spatial wavefunctions of the particle-hole coupled
modes are orthogonal to the background spatial wavefunction, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\phi_{0}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})u_{\parallel k>0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle=0,

(63)

\displaystyle\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})v_{\parallel k>0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle=0.

(64)

This is in contrast to standard Bogoliubov theory
30
,
where such orthogonality does not, in general, exist. Since the scalar
wavefunctions
\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
and
u_{\perp k}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
are non-degenerate
eigenfunctions of the same scalar single-particle Hamiltonian, they must all be
orthogonal to each other as well, and thus
Eqs. (
53
)-(
55
) satisfy
the orthogonality conditions set by Eq. (
26
),
namely,
\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\phi_{0}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)=0
for all excitations in the
system. The energy-space results shown in Fig.
3
confirm that
these spatial amplitudes that obey the orthogonality condition just discussed
better match the numerics than those without it, suggesting that our extension
of the standard Bogoliubov theory better captures the properties of the
particle-hole-correlated excitations.

A complication arises in these modes in that the bases formed by them are not
orthogonal, and thus spatial overlaps between the modes of the
different

excitation types do not vanish:

\displaystyle\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}u_{\perp k>0}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})u_{\parallel k^{\prime}>0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle\neq 0,

(65)

\displaystyle\qquad\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}u_{\perp k>0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})v_{\parallel k^{\prime}>0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle\neq 0.

(66)

However, since by definition the Bogoliubov expansion diagonalizes the dominant
Bogoliubov Hamiltonian
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
, the coupling between the states
of different bases only arises from the excitations of the low-energy spin
Bogoliubov Hamiltonian
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{s}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
as well as terms of higher order in

\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
that only become important at later times when the depletion of
the background state is appreciable.

III.4
Resonance conditions

To derive explicit conditions for where spin-spatial resonances occur, we
analyze the time-dependent driving term
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
. Resonant coupling to
an eigenmode of
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}}_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
is triggered when the frequency of

\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
matches the corresponding eigenenergy of the eigenmode. In what
follows, we restrict ourselves to the “far Zeeman regime,” i.e., the regime
where the quadratic Zeeman shift dominates (
\lvert q\rvert\gg c_{s}
). In this regime,
according to Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
), we approximate
the time evolution operator in Eq. (
46
) by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{U}}_{s}^{\text{SMA}}(t)\approx\exp\left[-i\frac{\left(q\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{z}^{2}+\mu_{n}\right)}{\hbar}t\right].

(67)

Using this approximation, we find

\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\approx\left[g_{s}|\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})|^{2}-c_{s}\right]\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{M}}}(t)\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)\\
-\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{M}}}^{*}(t)\phi^{*}_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{*}_{0}(0)\end{bmatrix}\\
+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{C}}}\phi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)\\
-\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{C}}}^{*}\phi^{*}_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\},

(68)

where the matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{M}}}(t)
has the matrix elements

\displaystyle\left[\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{M}}}(t)\right]_{\alpha\beta}=

\displaystyle\left[\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{x}\right]_{\alpha\beta}\left[\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)\right]_{4-\beta}\left[\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{*}_{0}(0)\right]_{4-\alpha}\cos\left(\frac{2qt}{\hbar}\right)+

(69)

\displaystyle\left[\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{y}\right]_{\alpha\beta}\left[\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)\right]_{4-\beta}\left[\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{*}_{0}(0)\right]_{4-\alpha}\sin\left(\frac{2qt}{\hbar}\right)

and the time-independent matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{C}}}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{C}}}=\sum_{j=x,y,z}\left[\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\dagger}_{0}(0)\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(0)\right]\bm{\mathbf{F}}_{j}-\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{M}}}(0).

(70)

This analysis shows that the matrix elements of
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
contain terms
that oscillate at the frequency
2q/\hbar
, resulting in the resonance
conditions

2\left\lvert q_{\text{res}}\right\rvert=E_{\perp k}

(71)

and

2\left\lvert q_{\text{res}}\right\rvert=E_{\parallel k},

(72)

where
E_{\perp k}
and
E_{\parallel k}
are the eigenenergies defined in
Eqs.
57
and (
58
), respectively.

Our derivation relies on three approximations: i) terms that are quadratic or
of higher order in
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
are dropped, ii) linear terms that are
proportional to
g_{s}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
are also neglected since they are effectively
counted as being of quadratic order (see our discussion above), and iii) the
resonance occurs in the large quadratic Zeeman shift regime,
\lvert q\rvert\gg c_{s}
.
Condition i) holds until the excitation modes have a significant fraction of
the population. This can occur if the system is exactly on resonance over very
long time scales. Importantly, signatures of the resonance should emerge before
condition i) is violated. Condition ii) is almost always satisfied when
condition i) is satisfied provided
g_{s}
is much smaller than the other energy
scales of the system. Recall that this scale separation is a critical
ingredient of our theory. Condition iii) tends to be satisfied in the
reasonably strong trap regime (see below for additional discussion).

The neglected nonlinear terms
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
in the equations of motion for

\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
and
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
introduce frequencies that are integer multiples
of the fundamental frequency [see Eqs. (
71
)
and (
72
)]. Therefore, the more general resonance
conditions are

2n\left\lvert q_{\text{res}}\right\rvert=E_{\perp k},\quad 2n\left\lvert q_{\text{res}}\right\rvert=E_{\parallel k},\quad(n=1,2,3,\dots)

(73)

where
n=1
corresponds to the fundamental resonance frequencies derived
above, while
n>1
corresponds to higher-order parametric resonance
frequencies. Figure
1
(b), e.g., corresponds to a parametric
resonance with
n=2
.

The analysis presented in the previous section shows that the resonances
associated with the
u_{\parallel k>0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
and
v_{\parallel k>0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})

particle-hole-correlated spatial wavefunctions correspond to a spin structure

\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{0}(t)
that is parallel to the background spin state, whereas those
associated with the
u_{\perp k>0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
spatial wavefunctions correspond to
a spin structure
\bm{\mathbf{s}}_{k>0}(t)
that is perpendicular to the background spin
state. The former implies that different spin components share the same spatial
wavefunction to leading order near the resonances and that the resulting
dynamics resemble an overall density oscillation, whereas the latter implies
that the resonances involve changing spin structures, leading to spin-dependent
spatial wavefunctions. We use the state fidelity

\mathcal{F}_{\pm}(t)=\frac{\left|\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\,\psi_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\,\psi_{\pm 1}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)\right|}{\sqrt{\rho_{0}(t)\rho_{\pm 1}(t)}}

(74)

to identify resonances that are associated with the spin-perpendicular modes
and the integrated time difference in the total single-particle density

\mathcal{D}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\,\left|n_{\text{tot}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)-n_{\text{tot}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},0)\right|

(75)

to identify resonances that are associated with the particle-hole-coupled modes
(the factor of
1/2
ensures a maximum value of unity).

From the discussions above, it is clear that the nonlinear nature of the system
obfuscates the role of the various terms in the equations of motion. Despite of
this, insights into the dynamics can be gained from simplified and truncated
treatments of the system. The fundamental resonance positions can be well
predicted by the Bogoliubov theory, with the quadratic Bogoliubov-like terms in
the equations of motion defining the structure of the resonance modes and the
resonant parameter regimes. The terms independent of
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}
in the equations of
motion for the resonant mode amplitudes seed occupation of the resonant modes
due to nonequilibrium spin dynamics, while the nonlinear terms in
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}
further
enhance the resonance by allowing for transitions to higher energy modes and
therefore stronger fluctuations in the mean-field interaction energies to drive
parametric excitations. The resonances can be made (artificially) weaker by
either removing the higher-energy modes, equivalently truncating the nonlinear
terms (which therefore reduces the number of modes that can be excited), or by
detuning the system from the resonant regimes such that the nonlinear terms and
higher-energy mode occupations are less significant in driving the dynamics.

IV
Conclusion and outlook

In this work, we developed a general coupled-channel framework to describe
resonant spatial excitations induced by the spin dynamics of a spinor
Bose–Einstein condensate. Taking advantage of the scale separation, our theory
is closely related to the particle-number–conserving Bogoliubov theory for the
spin independent part of the full spinor Hamiltonian, in which the
eigenenergies and eigenstates determine the resonance conditions and the
corresponding resonant spatial wavefunctions. Depending on the nature of the
spatial excitations at resonance, two types of resonances are identified: one
involving particle–hole correlations and the other without such particle-hole
correlations. Our theory reveals that these two types of excitations are
associated with distinct spin structures: the former corresponds to a spin
structure parallel to the background spin, whereas the latter corresponds to a
spin structure perpendicular to the background spin. For resonances involving
perpendicular spin excitations, spin-dependent spatial structures emerge,
indicating a breakdown of the single-mode approximation. In contrast,
resonances involving parallel spin excitations behave more like total-density
excitations with different spin components sharing common spatial orbitals.
Owing to the nonlinear nature of the system, higher-order parametric resonances
are observed for both types of resonances.

The dynamics near resonances were studied in both the strong-trap and weak-trap
regimes. Sharp resonances could be identified only in the strong-trap regime,
where the energy spacing in the Bogoliubov spectrum is larger than the
spin-changing collision energy. In the weak-trap regime, many spatial
wavefunctions are involved, leading to more complicated spatial structures such
as the development of spin domains. The behavior in the weak-trap regime more
closely resembles that of homogeneous systems where the Bogoliubov theory of
the spin-dependent part of the full spinor Hamiltonian plays a role equally
important as that of the spin-independent part, and is known to support
dynamical instabilities in certain parameter regimes
25
.
Our theory smoothly connects the sharp-resonance regime, which requires
comparatively large trapping frequencies, and the regime where dynamical
instabilities dominate, which requires comparatively small trapping
frequencies.

The framework presented in this paper focuses on the mean-field limit and
reproduces results obtained by solving the spinor Gross–Pitaevskii equation.
From a technical perspective, the developed coupled-channel framework may
enhance the efficiency of simulating higher-dimensional systems near resonances
compared with full Gross–Pitaevskii simulations, since a fairly small number of
spatial orbitals is expected to capture the resonant dynamics. In this work, we
focused—for illustrative purposes—on the resonant dynamics in a
one-dimensional system. We found that the number of orbitals required to reach
convergence depends on several factors, including the detuning from the
resonance, the relevant time scales, and the coupling strength to the resonant
modes. A detailed investigation of higher-dimensional systems is an interesting
direction for future work.

Extending our framework to include quantum fluctuations and quantum
correlations should be fairly straightforward. Such an extension would involve
deriving equations of motion for correlation functions of the excitations order
by order in terms of the second-quantized operator-equivalent of
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathbf{r}},t)
.
Extensions of our framework along these lines are closely related to the
Zaremba–Nikuni–Griffin formalism
51
, which is useful for
describing the dynamics of a condensate coupled to a thermal ensemble at finite
temperature. In this context, our framework can be generalized to capture both
single-particle–type excitations and excitations involving particle–hole
correlations in the thermal bath. A natural and interesting question for future
work is to investigate the robustness of the resonances against quantum and
thermal fluctuations.

Furthermore, this work opens broader avenues for using spinor BECs as quantum
simulators. For example, within the single-mode approximation, the spin
dynamics of a spin-1 system is known to be integrable
52
.
When multiple modes become involved near resonance, the integrability of the
spin Hamiltonian is broken, leading to chaotic
dynamics
13
and enabling the exploration of richer
nonequilibrium quantum physics. By carefully engineering the coupling between
spin and spatial excitations, a trapped background condensate can mimic the
role of a cavity in which the spin is coupled to tunable cavity modes,
providing potential applications for simulating various iconic spin–photon
models. In addition, given the sensitivity of the spin dynamics near resonance
and the scale separation of the system, the spin degrees of freedom may be used
to probe quantum phenomena in spatial excitations, such as Beliaev damping
induced by phonon–phonon collisions
37
or Landau damping, which
arises from collisions between phonons and thermal atoms
42
.

The current framework can also be applied to other cold-atom quantum
simulators, including those based on Floquet engineering, synthetic spin–orbit
coupling, or momentum-space lattices, where the pseudo-spin is encoded in
various ways and a simple frozen spatial wavefunction is typically
assumed
15
;
1
. The framework can be
directly used to verify the validity of these assumptions and to provide
guidance on the tunability and stability of those systems.
