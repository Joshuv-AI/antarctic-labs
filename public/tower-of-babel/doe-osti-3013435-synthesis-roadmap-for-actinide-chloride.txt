 1. Introduction
 Molten salt reactors (MSRs) are among the main advanced nuclear reactor types at the forefront of
development by industry, with the support of the US government, for the next fleet of nuclear reactors to
support the demand for energy in the coming decades. MSRs are highly unique because they are cooled
and typically also fueled by molten salt. This quality brings about safety benefits such as low-pressure
operation and self-stabilization of the neutron flux, operational benefits such as high-temperature
operation and the ability for online refueling, and fuel and waste management benefits thanks to the
flexibility of post-processing of molten salts and reprocessing options that involve removal of fission
products and actinide separation. In fact, domestic deployment of molten salt (or molten salt–cooled)
reactors is an approaching reality: a handful of molten salt reactor developers are planning to operate
demonstration-scale reactors, as a step toward commercial-scale power reactors, within the decade. For
example, Natura Resources received a construction permit in September 2024 for the deployment of
MSR-1, which is a graphite-moderated thermal spectrum reactor, at Abilene Christian University. Also,
TerraPower, in a collaborative effort with Southern Company and Idaho National Laboratory (INL),
received approval from DOE in 2023 to proceed with the construction of the Molten Chloride Reactor
Experiment (MCRE), a homogeneous chloride fast reactor at INL, and has begun assembly of system
components. There are several other examples of developers at different stages of development of their
own unique MSR designs (Jenet et al., 2025).
 As developers are in the process of obtaining approvals for their designs, deploying demonstration-
scale reactors, and planning for their commercial-scale power reactors, there is a critical supply chain
need for the synthesis of fuel for these reactors that must be addressed. The challenge generally is three-
fold: (1) the quantity of fueled salt needed for these reactors is extraordinarily high (>100s of kilograms),
but demonstrations of scaled-up techniques for fueled salt synthesis are significantly more limited than
demonstrations of lab-scale syntheses; (2) each developer has a unique reactor design, which means
different actinide halide elements in different carrier salts must be synthesized; and (3) there is a need for
particularly high-purity salt so as to ensure the long-term operability of these reactors with minimal
degradation to salt-wetted components, which necessitates synthesis techniques with high levels of
quality control, repeatability, and well-characterized precursor and reactant materials. It is crucial that this
supply chain challenge be addressed by demonstrating synthesis techniques that are scalable, de-risked,
and well-documented so that these technologies may be adopted and utilized by industry to support the
fueling needs of MSRs that are to come online within the next 10 years.
 With this supply chain challenge clearly defined for the developing MSR industry, it is important to
consider that addressing such a challenge is oftentimes complex and context-dependent. There is not
necessarily a single synthesis technique that can be scaled up and adopted to address the needs of all MSR
developers; the synthesis approach that may be viable for producing a desired fuel salt will be entirely
dependent on the exact salt composition needed, the quantity needed, purity needed, the refueling and
waste plans, and the availability of a carrier salt for the fuel. Therefore, it is important to consider more
broadly what synthesis techniques are available and have been demonstrated to understand the benefits,
challenges, and general nuances that should be considered when evaluating a particular route for efficient
production of high-purity fuel salts at scale.
 In this report, a roadmap evaluating synthesis methods for actinide chloride salts is described; these
particular salt compounds discussed herein are of key interest for chloride fast reactor developers. This

 1

roadmap is intended to provide a summary of previously reported synthesis techniques to provide a
general understanding of scalability, purity, and relevance to specific compounds for each approach. The
main compounds of focus in this roadmap are UCl3, UCl4, PuCl3, and ThCl4, as they represent fissile,
fissionable, or fertile compounds that may exist within the fueled salt of a chloride MSR. The synthesis
methods evaluated here include synthesis of these compounds in their pure form and synthesis as a
constituent of a multi-component salt mixture. In addition, purification techniques are also explored in
this roadmap, as certain synthesis techniques may not yield a desirable level of purity in the product salt
and additional purification efforts may be required after production. This roadmap can inform future
research efforts to develop and demonstrate specific synthesis or purification techniques that are deemed
strategically relevant for fuel production for one or more MSR developers and ensure that the technology
readiness is sufficient for industrial adoption to meet front-end fuel cycle needs for chloride-based MSRs.

 2. Synthesis Routes
 2.1 Gaseous Routes
 This section provides an overview of the various chlorination methods in which the chlorinating agent
is a gas that can convert actinide oxides or metals to chlorides. A number of these methods have been
demonstrated at the kilogram scale (namely, for production of PuCl3 and ThCl4). One major benefit to
these methods is that, particularly when metals are the precursor, a highly pure product (free from oxides
or oxychlorides) can generally be achieved, so long as the reactant gas stream is sufficiently free from
oxygen or moisture. In addition, these methods can produce solvent-free products, meaning the desired
actinide chloride can be produced without being dissolved in a solution with other alkali or alkaline earth
chlorides. However, there are challenges with this chlorination route, including the handling of highly
toxic and reactive gases that require equipment, materials, and monitoring systems to ensure that gases
are appropriately contained and vented. These reactions also generally require high temperatures and
careful control of temperature; careful control is especially necessary with synthesizing uranium chloride
compounds, a process in which the temperature plays a key role in the oxidation state of the product.

2.1.1 Carbochlorination with CCl4
 Carbochlorination involves the reaction of a precursor actinide compound with CCl4 as a gas in order
to chlorinate the actinide to the desired compound. This process has been demonstrated with oxides as
precursors for the synthesis of both UCl4 and PuCl3. Synthesis of UCl4 through carbochlorination has
been performed with UO2 (Kuznetsov et al., 2005; Yoshimura et al., 1971) and U3O8 (Jangg et al., 1961;
Kitawaki et al., 2013) as precursor compounds. Synthesis of PuCl3 by carbochlorination has been
performed with PuO2 (Cleveland et al., 1972; Harder et al., 1958; Rasmussen, 1959). In addition, it is
worth mentioning that UO3 has been used as a precursor for synthesizing UCl5 (Johnson et al., 1944).
Using a chemically stable oxide such as UO2 or U3O8 is advantageous because it avoids the handling risks
(e.g., oxidation) associated with actinide metals.
 Carbochlorination has been demonstrated at the gram to multi-gram scale to synthesize UCl4.
Generally, one challenge with the use of UO2 as a precursor is that the reaction kinetics are slow, leading
to an incomplete reaction and generation of intermediate oxychlorides (Yoshimura et al., 1971). This
formation of intermediate compounds has also been reported when U3O8 is used as a precursor in a
mechanochemical reaction process (Kitawaki et al., 2013). In general, carbochlorination may be more
appropriate for lab-scale activities as opposed to large-scale production, because of the long reaction
times required to convert small amounts of oxide precursors and the need to separate out crystallized salt
products from unconverted or intermediate actinide compounds. Furthermore, carbochlorination may be
less attractive for uranium fuel cycles because the chlorinated product is UCl4, which must be reduced to
UCl3 before use as a fuel salt constituent.
 The carbochlorination process may be more useful for large-scale Pu chlorination because the
chlorinated product is trivalent, making it immediately useful as a fuel salt constituent for MSRs. Also,
the reported successful syntheses of PuCl3 do not indicate that intermediate compounds, such as
plutonium oxychlorides, are present in significant quantities in the solid product. However, the scale of
production has been limited to the multi-gram scale, and an attempt to perform carbochlorination
synthesis of PuCl3 at larger scales formed by-products that were challenging to handle in the off-gas
(Rasmussen, 1959). Furthermore, the reaction kinetics of PuCl3 carbochlorination were slow (Harder et
al., 1958).

 3

 It is important to note that COCl2 has been analyzed as a singular carbochlorinating agent, as an
alternative to CCl4 (Ferran & West, 1993; Rasmussen & Hopkins Jr., 1961). While CCl4 has been used
more broadly, COCl2 has reported benefits such as a lower chlorinating temperature and a higher reaction
rate in comparison to CCl4.

2.1.2 Chlorination or Carbochlorination with Cl2
 Direct chlorination (i.e. reaction of the actinide precursor with Cl2 gas), has been demonstrated with
various oxide precursors to form UCl4 (Canning, 1957, Roderburg, 1913). This process required very high
temperatures to be successful because the thermodynamics of this conversion are generally unfavorable
and additional steps were required for improving the purity of the end product. Also, challenges have
been reported in obtaining the desired valence state of the final chlorinated product (Canning, 1957).
Chlorination with UAl3 as the precursor resulted in the production of UCl3 and UCl4 at much more
modest temperatures, however AlCl3 formed as a byproduct and require removal via sublimation
(Cassayre et al., 2011). Direct chlorination has not been reported for chlorination of Pu or Th.
 Carbon has been used as an additional reducing agent to encourage the chlorination of actinide oxides
to actinide chlorides; this has been demonstrated for U chlorination (Haas et al., 1991; Hussonnois et al.,
1981; Johnson et al., 1944; Shin et al., 1998; Yang et al., 1997), Th chlorination (Hussonnois et al., 1981;
Peterson et al., 1964), and Pu (Vigier et al., 2018). Mixed chlorination with Cl2 and carbon falls under the
umbrella term of carbochlorination andproduces CO and/or CO2 as byproducts, depending on the oxide
being chlorinated. The addition of carbon to the direct chlorination process allows the reaction to become
thermodynamically favorable. Additionally, the reaction rate tends to increase when carbon is present,
such that the entirety of the reaction may occur in a couple of hours. Of course, it is crucial that the
temperature and carbon-to-oxide ratio are chosen carefully to yield the desired product with the
appropriate valence. This is most clearly a challenge in the generation of UCl4, where formation of the
highly volatile UCl5 and UCl6 is challenging to avoid (Shin et al., 1998).
 Like with carbochlorination where only CCl4 is used, direct chlorination produces a tetravalent
product if uranium oxides are used as the precursor. This is also true when ThO2 is chlorinated in this
manner (Dean & Chandler, 1957). However, for chlorination of PuO2, though PuCl4 may be immediately
formed, it will spontaneously reduce to PuCl3 due to chemical instability (Vigier et al., 2018).

2.1.3 Hydrochlorination
 Hydrochlorination involves the reaction of a precursor actinide compound with gaseous HCl to
chlorinate it to the desired compound. It has been shown that uranium metal alloys and hydrides can be
chlorinated by HCl (Hamilton & Simpson, 2021; Meier et al., 2012), although it is crucial that the
reaction temperatures and stoichiometry are optimized to get the desired valence of the product. It may be
possible to produce pure UCl3 using hydrochlorination as the sole reaction pathway, but production of
pure UCl3 is challenging to achieve because UCl4 generation is thermodynamically favorable over a wide
range of temperatures and HCl ratios (Meier et al., 2018). It is also possible to use pure U metal as the
precursor. Both methods inhibit the production of UCl4 by creating a heavily reducing environment. This
has been achieved within a salt solution that acts as a carrier to the uranium (Perhach et al., 2022). It is
important to note that hydrochlorination reactions with uranium alloys tend to require relatively modest
conditions (< 400 °C), although the reaction kinetics may not be as fast as direct chlorination based on the

 4

reported reaction times by Meier et al. (Meier et al., 2012). Scale-up beyond multi-gram batches of the
chlorinated product has not been reported and would require demonstration to ensure that product purity
is achievable at scale.
 Hydrochlorination with UO2 as a precursor is also possible and has been reported within a molten salt
bath of alkali and alkaline-earth chlorides (Lin et al., 2019; Volkovich et al., 2007). In this approach,
gaseous HCl was bubbled into a salt containing UO2, allowing for the conversion to UCl4. The challenge
with this approach is that oxychlorides may form, and the cationic radii of the carrier salt can impact the
final oxidation state of the U in the melt. Oxidation states >4 were typical if the cationic radius of the
carrier salt was too low (Volkovich et al., 2007). As such, hydrochlorination may be more ideal for
chlorination of U metal or a U alloy as opposed to an oxide; in some cases, hydrochlorination of the oxide
may be sufficient if a high purity product is not required.
 Hydrochlorination of Pu metal or a Pu metal alloy is preferred over hydrochlorination of a Pu oxide.
Hydrochlorination of PuO2 resulted in formation of plutonium oxychlorides when attempted on a multi-
gram scale (Rasmussen, 1959). Hydrochlorination of PuAl2 was reported as particularly successful for
production of PuCl3, with no reported formation of higher valence states (Meier et al., 2018). The gaseous
approach with HCl may not be necessary if it is desirable to perform this reaction at ambient
temperatures; aqueous hydrochlorination has been reported with Pu metal and the PuO2 can be effectively
removed with nitric acid as a dissolution medium (Hanson et al., 2023). Like with uranium, the scalability
of plutonium hydrochlorination has not yet been demonstrated, as the reported syntheses are at the multi-
gram scale or smaller.

2.1.4 Other Combined Gaseous Approaches
 The previous sections within Section 2.1 described approaches in which only one gaseous
chlorination agent was used. Utilization of a single chlorination agent simplifies the engineering of a
chlorination system and may limit the number of hazards requiring management. However, achieving the
desired salt purity and oxidation state of the product salt may be difficult with a single chlorinating agent.
As such, some gaseous approaches can be strategically combined to take advantage of staged reaction
pathways and enable the production of a higher quality fuel product.
 One example of a combined gaseous process is performing direct chlorination with Cl2 with CO as an
additional reducing agent to ensure that the chlorination reaction is thermodynamically favorable. This
reaction mechanism is similar to using solid carbon, but utilized a gaseous reactact. This particular
approach has been demonstrated for chlorination of actinide oxides to UCl4 (Haas et al., 1991), ThCl4
(Dean & Chandler, 1957), and PuCl3 (Rasmussen, 1959). The results of those chlorinations were mixed:
while UCl4 and ThCl4 synthesis efforts had poor conversion efficiencies, PuCl3 synthesis resulted in an
apparently pure product. Regarding UCl4 synthesis specifically, oxychloride formation was noted as one
particular issue with CO as a reductant (Haas et al., 1991).
 A combination of Cl2 and CCl4 can also be used for chlorination. This combined Cl2 + CCl4 approach
has been demonstrated for chlorination of various oxides of U, Pu, and Th. This approach is advantageous
(as opposed to carbochlorination with CCl4 alone) because the additional Cl2 in the gaseous reactant
mixture can remove potential carbon impurities (West et al., 1988). For uranium, this particular
chlorination approach has generally been used to synthesize the higher valence states (UCl5, UCl6)
(Johnson et al., 1944; Warren & Ferris, 1966), and is similar to previously discussed approaches
involving Cl2 gas where very careful control of synthesis parameters is required if UCl4 is desired without
generating significant quantities of higher-volatility higher oxidation states because of the highly-

 5

oxidizing environment created by the Cl2. This is not a concern for the synthesis of PuCl3 for reasons
discussed previously, and this approach may be scalable based on scalability studies that showed similar
conversion efficiencies over a range of 10 to 300 g (West et al., 1988). For synthesis of ThCl4, this
approach can result in high yields when the appropriate temperatures are used and the reaction is
performed for a sufficient period of time (Dean & Chandler, 1957).
 Another combined gaseous approach is hydrochlorination with the addition of H2. This approach has
been performed with U metal to produce UCl3 (Hamilton & Simpson, 2021; Johnson et al., 1944). Two
reaction mechanisms are possible: the U metal can be converted to a hydride first by reaction with H2 and
then converted to UCl3 after reaction with HCl; or U metal can be chlorinated by reaction with HCl to
form UCl4 and then reduced by reaction with H2 to form UCl3. Regardless of the order of reactions, this
combined HCl + H2 approach may be the best gaseous chlorination route for synthesis of pure UCl3.

 2.2 Metal Chloride Routes
 As highlighted in Section 2.1, several methods to produce dry uranium chlorides have been identified
that involve the management of corrosive gases and volatile products. Additionally, these methods often
require finely powdered actinide feedstocks with high surface areas for complete conversion, which can
be difficult because of the high sensitivity of uranium chlorides for oxygen and water. Therefore, other
synthesis methods that use various metal chlorides to oxidize actinide metals have been explored. Some
advantages to using metal chloride reactants include the relative ease of handling the reagents (i.e., no
need for corrosive gas infrastructure), the direct synthesis of UCl3 in the working salt (such as LiCl-KCl
eutectic (LKE)), and the protection of the dissolved UCl3 from oxygen/moisture.

2.2.1 Lead and Cadmium Chlorides
 The oxidation of uranium metal with a metal chloride in salt was demonstrated with lead chloride
according to the reaction (Inman 1954):
                                     2 U0 + 3 PbCl2  2 UCl3 + Pb0

For processing of used nuclear fuel (UNF), the use of PbCl2 has some advantages, including (Zaikov, 2020):

  Fission products (FP) do not react with the lead chloride during the chlorination process.
  Metallic lead that accumulated during the reaction is immiscible with the LiCl-KCl melt.
  FP can collect in the Pb0 phase and allow for efficient separation.

 Historically, cadmium chloride has been used for large-scale synthesis following a similar reaction
mechanism (Miller & Tomczuk, 2004; Poa et al., 1988; Woo et al., 2009). Miller and colleagues
demonstrated 250 kg synthesis of UCl3 through the in situ generation of CdCl2, which was formed by
injecting Cl2 into a Cd0 layer below the LiCl-KCl salt (Miller & Tomczuk, 2004). The less-dense CdCl2
then migrated to LiCl-KCl to oxidize the uranium metal, forming metallic cadmium that returned to the
lower layer. However, 0.4 wt% cadmium remained in the salt despite separation of the two layers and
further distillation steps to remove the cadmium. The toxicity of lead and cadmium metals along with the
challenges in separation and disposal have since led to the exploration of alternative metal chlorides for
this process.

2.2.2 Aluminum and Zirconium Chlorides
 Dai et al. investigated the chlorination of UO2 in molten CaCl2, MgCl2, and AlCl3 melts (Dai et al.,
1996). The AlCl3 melt was the most efficient in dissolving the uranium oxide to generate UCl4. In a
similar manner, UCl4 was synthesized via AlCl3 in LKE (Y. Liu et al., 2018; Y. L. Liu et al., 2015; Xu et
al., 2022) following the equation:
                                      3 UO2 + 4 AlCl3  2 Al2O3 + 3 UCl4

 Additionally, chlorination using AlCl3 was accomplished outside of eutectic salt melts via chemical
vapor transport (CVT) at the gram scale (Rudel et al., 2018; Rudel & Kraus, 2017). This transformation
was performed in a glass ampoule under vacuum that was placed in a temperature gradient. Impurities
from this method included unreacted AlCl3 and AlOCl.

 8

 Syntheses with AlCl3 are not limited to the production of UCl4, as UCl3 was obtained with AlCl3
in LKE when metallic aluminum was added (Samanta, Maji, et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2022):

                               3 UO2 + 4 AlCl3 + Al  3 UCl4 + 2 Al2O3

                                     3 UCl4 + Al  3 UCl3 + AlCl3

 Overall Reaction: 3UO2 + 3AlCl3 + Al  3UCl3 + 2Al2O3

 This reaction mechanism is analogous to other UCl3 synthesis techniques that require a metal
reductant to be present to product of UCl3. Employing a combination of Al and AlCl3, Samanta and
colleagues achieved an approximate 93% conversion efficiency at a multi-gram scale (Samanta, Kumar,
et al., 2022). Although AlCl3 is an effective chlorinating agent of both uranium metal and oxides, the
corrosive nature of AlCl3 and the formation of Al2O3 as a by-product must be considered.
 Similar to AlCl3, ZrCl4 is a highly reactive chlorinating agent for chlorination of uranium and
plutonium oxides (Chamberlain & Simpson, 2022; Sakamura et al., 2005; Westphal & Mariani, 2013;
Yokoo & Inoue, 2012). One method generated ZrCl4 in situ via a reaction with NiCl2 and Zr(0) in LiCl-
KCl for subsequent chlorination of UO2. However, this method resulted in incomplete conversion as
unreacted UO2 was encapsulated by ZrO2 formed during the chlorination step (Chamberlain & Simpson,
2022). Complete conversion was achieved through modification of the experimental configuration in
which pre-vaporized ZrCl4 was introduced to the salt (Chamberlain & Simpson, 2022). However, ZrCl4
presents some disadvantages that include being corrosive to refractory metals such as steel and low
solubility in LiCl-KCl.
2.2.3 Other Metal Chlorides
 A variety of metal chlorides have been surveyed as chlorination agents following similar reaction
pathways as discussed above. As in the case of CdCl2, both BiCl3 (Lambert et al., 2018) and ZnCl2 (Lee et
al., 2019) generated immiscible liquid metal layers below the salt after chlorination of uranium metal.
Bismuth trichloride has a higher vapor pressure than CdCl2, making it easier to volatize for separation
from the salt after the chlorination step. However, this high volatility can also result in low actinide
chloride yield. Chlorination with zinc dichloride resulted in 3.5 wt% Zn metal contamination in the salt.
Separation of Zn metal from the salt was only achieved by rough mechanical separation techniques.
Attempting to boil the zinc by-product at 907 °C could result in co-vaporization of the salt.
 Westphal and colleagues explored alternatives to CdCl2 through the survey of CuCl2, MnCl2, and
ZrCl4 for chlorination of uranium metal in LKE at the kilogram scale (Westphal et al., 2011). Uranium
tetrachloride was generated as an intermediate product, especially during chlorination by CuCl2.
Additionally, separating the metallic copper particles from the salt was challenging. Conversely,
chlorination using MnCl2 resulted in negligible Mn0 contamination.
 Simpson and colleagues investigated FeCl2 as the chlorinating agent (Yankey et al., 2023; H. Zhang
et al., 2022). Uranium trichloride was synthesized at 71% yield in NaCl with FeCl2 at 850 °C (H. Zhang
et al., 2022). This yield improved to 97% in equimolar NaCl-CaCl2 with FeCl2 at 600 °C (H. Zhang et al.,
2022). The separation of the metallic iron from the salt was achieved by using an AlNiCo magnet to
retain the iron while the product salt was poured into a stainless-steel pan. This procedure was
subsequently modified to remove the need for magnetic separation, which may be difficult to implement
on an industrial scale. The modified approach removed the Fe(0) by-product through electrodeposition
onto the stainless-steel basket containing the uranium metal precursor (Yankey et al., 2023). This gram-
scale demonstration resulted in ~90% UCl3 yield in NaCl-MgCl2 at 550 °C and LiCl-KCl at 450 °C. The

 9

residual iron concentration in the salt after chlorination and separation was 0.1–0.12 wt%. Choi and
colleagues synthesized UCl3 without the introduction of an external chlorination agent by using a
stainless-steel basket electrode in which the FeCl2 and CrCl3 oxidants were derived from corrosion
processes (Choi et al., 2024).
 Further techniques using metal chlorides outside of molten salt environments were evaluated at

and resulted in a high chlorination rate, which may make chlorination with MoCl5 advantageous.
However, significant work would be needed to demonstrate the process at large scale.

 2.3 Ammonium Chloride Routes
 As highlighted in the previous section, one major consideration for the employment of metal
chlorides toward the synthesis of actinide chlorides is the difficulty in separating metallic or oxide by-
products from the actinide chloride. While gaseous routes largely avoid contaminating by-products,
further development and scale-up of these routes requires careful handling and storage of compressed
corrosive gases. Ammonium chloride has been investigated as a possible alternative for chlorination of
actinide metals and oxides by generating the HCl (g) in situ from decomposition of NH4Cl. This
decomposition of NH4Cl occurs between 310 and 338 °C (Yoon et al., 2022) as represented by the
equation:
                                       NH4Cl  HCl (g) + NH3 (g)

At an elevated temperature of approximately 450 °C (Yoon et al., 2022), the generated NH3 can further
decompose into N2 and H2 gases.

                                        2NH3 (g)  N2(g) + 3H2(g)

2.3.1 Metal Precursor
 The chlorination of uranium metal using HCl derived from NH4Cl decomposition follows the equation:

                                   3 NH4Cl + U  UCl3 + 1.5 N2 + 6H2

Eun et al. accomplished the chlorination of uranium metal by heating the metal/NH4Cl mixture in a quartz
tube (Eun et al., 2017). Both UCl3 and UCl4 were formed; however, unreacted uranium remained. One
solution to this challenge is to first synthesize uranium hydride from uranium metal as described by
Barnes et al. (2025). During the hydriding pre-treatment, a solid uranium pellet can be transformed into a
fine powder through the difference in densities for the metal and hydride solid phases (Banos et al., 2018).
Decreasing particle size and thereby increasing the surface area is essential for complete conversion (i.e.,
unreacted metal retained in the center of large particles) from gas–solid reactions.

 Generation of pure UCl3 is strongly dependent on parameters such as the ratio of reactants and
temperature control, as UCl4 can be generated following the equation:

                                    4 NH4Cl + U  UCl4 + 8 H2 + 2 N2

Undesired UCl4 can then be reduced to UCl3 with the presence of uranium metal or hydrogen gas.

                                           3 UCl4 + U  4 UCl3

                                        UCl4 + ½ H2  UCl3 + HCl

Yoon and colleagues employed a sealed reaction vessel for a multi-gram synthesis of UCl3 from both
U(0) and UH3 as the starting reagent (Yoon et al., 2022). The chlorination reaction temperature was
maintained above 800 K to facilitate the reduction of generated UCl4 with H2 gas. The purity of the UCl3
obtained exceeded 92.5 %. Impurities detected included iron and chromium chlorides that derived from
the reaction of NH4Cl with the stainless-steel vessel.

 The synthesis of UCl3 in alkali–metal chloride media was investigated by Herrmann et al. (Herrmann
et al., 2022). Uranium trichloride was successfully detected in both LKE and NaCl. However, the

 13

synthesis in LKE resulted in some unidentified impurities that may suggest the generation of tetravalent
uranium. The impurities were not observed in the case of NaCl. Similarly, Rood and colleagues
investigated synthesis of UCl3 in LKE, with NH4Cl vapor injected into the molten salt (Rood et al., 2024).
Uranium metal chlorination was achieved with up to 91.5% conversion. The rate of chlorination using
NH4Cl was found to be significantly faster than pure HCl, attributed to a change in solubility of HCl with
the presence of NH3.

 Hames et al. described a two-step procedure for the synthesis of UCl3 using NH4Cl (Hames, 2017) at
lower temperatures (573–623 K) than Yoon (693–893 K), Rood (723–773 K), and Herrmann (773–
923 K). The first step generated UCl4 through the reaction of NH4Cl and uranium metal under vacuum to
produce an intermediate (NH4)2UCl6, which was then decomposed under vacuum at an elevated
temperature to generate UCl4. Uranium metal was subsequently introduced to reduce the UCl4 to UCl3.

 The chlorination of plutonium metal was also accomplished using NH4Cl, following similar reaction
mechanisms as described with uranium (Karlsson et al., 2023; Lichtenstein et al., 2022). Lichtenstein and
colleagues first reduced PuO2 to plutonium metal through a reaction with metallic calcium. The metal was
then chlorinated with excess NH4Cl in a stainless-steel vessel via a two-step heat treatment. Pretreatment
of plutonium metal with hydriding/de-hydriding cycles can also be used to further decrease the particle
size of the metal and increase the surface area of the powder (Karlsson et al., 2023). The synthesis of
PuCl3 with this hydride pre-treatment was demonstrated at a multi-gram scale with a purity of 99.7%
(Karlsson et al., 2023).

2.3.2 Oxide Precursor
 The chlorination of actinide oxides with NH4Cl has been investigated to a lesser extent than their
metal analogs. Partial chlorination of U3O8 to UO2Cl2 has been achieved in molten LKE in the presence of
O2 (Wu et al., 2024):

                        2 U3O8 + 12 NH4Cl + O2  6 UO2Cl2 + 12 NH3 + 6 H2O

Wu and colleagues utilized this chlorination to then recover UO2 from UO2Cl2 via electrodeposition,
achieving a recovery efficiency of 97%. Using a similar reaction environment, Liu and colleagues
dissolved UO2 in LKE through chlorination to UO2Cl2, along with other rare earth oxides that were
simultaneously chlorinated (Y. L. Liu et al., 2018). The oxychloride was targeted for this synthesis
because it is both soluble in LKE and is more stable in comparison to uranium chlorides.

 The chlorination of UO2 to UCl3 using ammonium chloride and zirconium metal was demonstrated at
the gram scale, described by the equation (Eun et al., 2018)

                  UO2 + Zr + 3 NH4Cl  UCl3 + 0.5 Zr2O + 1.5 N2 + 1.5 H2O + 4.5 H2

Following the reaction at 320 °C, UCl3 was identified as a product along with by-products including
Zr2O, ZrO2, and ZrCl2. The authors note that further optimization of this setup could decrease the amount
of unreacted UO2 and Zr through the addition of a mixing device and adjustments in molar ratio of NH4Cl
and Zr injected to increase the contact efficiency of the solid materials.

 2.4 Other Chemical Methods
 In addition to gaseous and molten salt routes, the synthesis of uranium and plutonium chlorides has
also been explored through the use of organic chlorinating agents. Hexachloropropene (HCP, C3Cl6) has
been identified as a possible reagent for the chlorination of uranium oxides, oxychlorides, and nitrates
(Patel et al., 2015). This reaction generated UCl4 at high yields (92-100%), with the synthesized chloride
easily isolated via filtration and washing. Additionally, HCP can be recycled by distillation with no
reduction in yields during subsequent reactions (Patel et al., 2015). Harder and colleagues demonstrated
this reaction with plutonium oxalate to synthesize PuCl3 with a purity of 99.7% (Harder et al., 1958).
However, this vigorous exothermic reaction requires expert attention and specialty equipment (Patel et al.,
2015). The actinide precursor quality was found to greatly impact the efficiency of the reaction (Patel et
al., 2015).

 The synthesis of additional trivalent uranium halides was further explored by Fetrow et al. via
Schlenk chemistry techniques (Fetrow, 2021). Fetrow used HCP to synthesize UCl4, which then served as
the starting reagent for generation of UCl3(THF)2. A variety of reactions can be employed for the
transformation to UCl3(THF)2 or other trivalent adducts using reductants such as NaH (Clark et al., 1989),
Na2C2 (Avens et al., 1994), Mg0 (Clark & Sattelberger, 1997), KC8 (Fetrow et al., 2021) and KH (Fetrow
et al., 2021). Furthermore, the UCl3(THF)2 compounds could be transformed into bromide and iodide
analogs via halide exchange using Me3SiX (X = Br, I) (Fetrow et al., 2021). Although these pathways are
effective synthetic routes for uranium compounds, the demonstrated scale has been limited to sub-
gram/gram, and significant R&D would be required to determine the scalability of chlorination by HCP.

 2.5 Sequential Chlorination
 Sequential chlorination with a variety of chlorinating agents has been investigated for partitioning of
elements in used nuclear fuel. Hur et al. conducted extensive thermodynamic simulations to assess
individual and sequential chlorination of a representative used light-water reactor fuel vector using
MgCl2, NH4Cl and Cl2, with the aim of identifying pretreatment pathways that would decrease the amount
of material requiring pyroprocessing (Hur et al., 2023). Thermodynamic calculations reacting used fuel in
molten MgCl2 showed that alkaline earth metal oxides would be completely converted to chlorides, while
the remaining actinide oxides and other metals would partially chlorinate. Single chlorination with NH4Cl
would also chlorinate the alkaline earth metal oxides and achieve a greater degree of chlorination for the
actinide oxides. Use of Cl2 would result in complete chlorination of alkaline earth metal oxides and all
metals. The calculations showed that overall chlorination of the spent fuel was greater with Cl2; however,
NH4Cl was the most effective for the actinide oxides. Hur et al. also performed calculations for sequential
chlorination with MgCl2 followed by NH4Cl. The calculated total chloride amount surpassed the totals for
each of the single chlorination steps. Additionally, sequential chlorination resulted in the synthesis of
UCl3 and UCl4, which were not obtained in the single chlorination calculations. Sequential chlorination
was further explored with the addition of Cl2 following the NH4Cl step. As expected, this progressive
chlorination exceeded the two-step and single chlorination techniques, and would result in UCl4, UCl5,
and UCl6 uranium species. This study suggests that there may be an advantage in using sequential
chlorination techniques for enhanced selectivity towards synthesis from complex actinide feedstocks.

 3. Purification Options
 Following uranium chloride synthesis, several types of impurities may be present depending on the
synthesis method selected. These impurities may include other metallic species, oxides, and moisture, and
may pose operational threats such as hydrolysis and corrosion. Such impurities require different treatment
methods and environments. Several purification methods have been previously investigated and can be
categorized into thermal, chemical, and electrolytic treatments, with some proposed processes utilizing a
combination of these methods. This section reviews each of these methods, along with benefits and
drawbacks of each, to aid in the purification method selection process based on desired purity, acceptable
impurity types, and available resources.

 3.1 Thermal Purification
 Thermal purification treats the salt species using only temperature to drive off any lingering moisture,
which is typically present in the form of hydrated salt species. These hydrated species may contain
different numbers of bound water molecules, ranging from one bound water molecule (monohydrate) to
ten or more bound water molecules. This method subjects the salt system to a high temperature, or a
series of different temperatures, without the use of any chemical agents.

 A study by Burak found that while a thermal treatment is effective at reducing the number of bound
water molecules, this reduction is often limited to achieving the monohydrate form of a salt (Burak &
Raiman, 2025). Further dehydration requires additional purification methods, such as carbochlorination,
electrochemical refining, or zone refining. Additionally, the unbound moisture is expected to hydrolyze
into corrosive species that persist in the salt once it becomes molten, posing additional challenges such as
the need for corrosive or hazardous off-gas handling. This hydrolysis may proceed by either of the
following equations resulting in a hydroxide or oxide species, where M represents the metal cation:

 MCl + H2O  MOH + HCl

                                    MCl + H2O  2MO + HCl + ½H2

 3.2 Metallic and Metallothermic Purification
 Metallothermic purification incorporates a reductant species that reacts with oxygen-bearing
impurities in the molten salt and precipitates an insoluble product that can be filtered or removed
mechanically after solidification. This purification method is straightforward and avoids the need for
hazardous gas handling. The active metal reductant can be any metal that preferentially binds with oxygen
compared to the molten salt species. Using a metallic form of one of the major cation components of the
molten salt is recommended to minimize the possibility of further contamination.

 In the metallothermic purification process performed by Burak, excess magnesium (a major
component of the MgCl2-NaCl-KCl salt) in the form of solid pieces (0.25 wt% of total mixture) was
added to the solid salt (Burak & Raiman, 2025). Thermocouples and quartz sparge gas (4.7 cm3/sec) lines
were lowered into the solid salt, while a quartz purge gas line (16.5 cm3/sec) was positioned away from
the salt to evacuate additional volatile species and maintain an inert environment. The ultra-high purity
argon sparge/purge gas allowed for the partitioning and evacuation of volatile species including H2O and

 18

HCl. Following the purification stage, acidic gaseous species such as HCl should be neutralized by
bubbling through a basic solution such as KOH or NaOH. The salt was heated above the melting point
while the sparge gas flowed continuously. After three hours of bubbling, the sparge lines were removed
and the salt was allowed to resolidify. Solid salt ingots were removed from the quartz containment
crucible. Remaining metallic magnesium pieces were chipped away, and the slag layer containing
insoluble oxides was removed.

 Post-analysis was conducted via combustion analysis to measure oxygen content of the salt before
and after purification. As-received salt contained 3466 ppm oxygen. Melting alone decreased the oxygen
content to 889 ppm (74.4% reduction). Three hours of metallothermic purification further reduced oxygen
content to 442 ppm (87.2% reduction), while six hours of metallothermic purification resulted in 143 ppm
oxygen (95.9% reduction overall). Processing time proved to have the largest impact on oxygen removal,
while sparge and purge gas flow rates had little effect.

 Zhao performed a similar procedure involving elemental magnesium chips (≤ 0.25 wt%) with
nitrogen sparge and cover gas in a quartz containment (Zhao, 2020). In addition to the hydrolysis
reactions listed above, Zhao observed an intermediate compound, magnesium hydroxychloride, produced
by the following reaction:

 MgCl2 + H2O  MgOHCl + HCl

This intermediate compound can be thermally decomposed above 500 °C following:
 MgOHCl  MgO + HCl

However, incorporating elemental magnesium can also decompose the hydroxychloride while avoiding
the generation of corrosive HCl, following:

 2MgOHCl + Mg  2MgO + MgCl2 + H2

 Increasing the purification temperature above 650 °C (the melting point of magnesium) accelerates
the decomposition of MgOHCl by eliminating the MgO passivation of the solid Mg surface (Zhao, 2020).
However, it was noted that because vapor pressure of the molten salt increases exponentially with
temperature, excessive evaporation and redeposition can result in clogging of the purification apparatus.
Therefore, a temperature of 670 °C was chosen for the purification.

 The solid magnesium content of 0.25 wt% was chosen to balance the reduction of MgOHCl while
minimizing the excess magnesium following purification. Based on the above equation for magnesium
reduction, 0.457 wt% magnesium is required to consume the MgOHCl. Considering other mechanisms
for moisture release and thermal decomposition, however, lowers this required magnesium content to
0.102 wt% (Zhao, 2020). Additional magnesium is recommended when considering the possible
reduction of other impurity cation species such as iron and to compensate for magnesium lost to reaction
with HCl and/or moisture.

 Observation of the pH change during purification resulted in other important conclusions. The
constant pH below 400 °C is indicative of the effectiveness of the pre-drying process at <300 °C to
remove most of the moisture via dehydration and most HCl via hydrolysis. Above 400oC, decreasing pH
indicates further evolution and release of HCl due to the dehydration of additional MgCl2 hydrates that
are not accessible below 400 °C. Only the monohydrate form has a reported dehydration temperature in

 19

this range. This conclusion is in agreement with the observation by Burak noted above. Furthermore, the
molten state of the salt may facilitate the mass transfer necessary to release the liberated hydrate (Zhao,
2020).

 Robb investigated the application of metallic and metallothermic purification to large-scale batches
(Robb et al., 2022). It was noted that an additional benefit of adding excess magnesium is maintaining a
low redox potential in the salt. The metallic purification route via contact with solid magnesium used in
that work was selected with input from a consortium of US national labs, including Oak Ridge National
Laboratory, Savannah River National Laboratory, National Renewable Energy Laboratory (now National
Laboratory of the Rockies), and Argonne National Laboratory. Recommendations resulting from this
study include careful consideration of the amount and timing of excess magnesium additions that lead to
the release of H2 or HCl species and proper design of the effluent vapor stream to eliminate condensation
and clogging.

 While these studies are related to alkali and alkaline earth metal cations and the application to
actinide chlorides is not discussed, it is expected that the wide range of oxidation states available to
actinides makes the above findings similarly applicable, perhaps with some key differences in the number
of hydrates, intermediate reaction products, and especially required temperatures for dehydration and
hydrolysis. However, the mechanistic descriptions of dehydration and hydrolysis are likely consistent for
any cation species.

 3.3 Inert Gas Sparging
 Kashani-Nejad proposed that mass transfer of the purification reaction product, HCl, is likely the rate-
limiting step of the MgOHCl thermal decomposition (Kashani-Nejad et al., 2005). Therefore, inert gas
sparging without the use of a reducing agent was investigated by Zhao. No noticeable difference was
reported in post-purification of salts treated with no magnesium addition and with 0.1 and 0.04 wt%
magnesium additions with respect to HCl release time, final MgOHCl content, sludge weight, and weight
loss during purification—as long as the inert gas sparging rate remained consistent (Zhao, 2020).
However, the inclusion of solid magnesium as a reducing agent may be important when considering
downstream effects such as corrosion mitigation. Additionally, the inclusion of solid magnesium will
reduce other impurities such as iron, sulfur, or silicon and shift these impurities to the sludge phase.

 Ultimately, HCl release during the high-temperature purification (with or without excess magnesium)
was ~1.5–2.5 times higher than the pre-drying step. There is a strong correlation between HCl release
time (purification time needed) and sparging gas flow rate, while the amount of excess magnesium does
not play a significant role (Zhao, 2020). There is a diminishing return on decreasing purification time as
the sparge gas flow rate is increased.

 3.4 Electrolytic Purification
 Salt purification via electrolysis is a useful technique for transforming the redox state of dissolved
impurities such as oxides, hydroxides, or cation impurities such as undesirable metals and corrosion
products. One particular benefit of electrolytic purification is the ability to maintain tight control over the
redox potential of the molten salt system, allowing highly selective reactions and the possibility of
recovering specific impurities. However, there is a possibility of oxide buildup on the electrode surface or

 20

formation of a passivation layer, leading to decreased electrolytic efficiency. An example of the
reduction-oxidation reactions occurring at the electrode surfaces is given by:

 Cathode (Reduction): MOH+ + e- ↔ MO(s) + H2(g)

 Anode (Oxidation): M(s) ↔ M+ + e-
 Total Reaction: MOH+ + M(s) ↔ M+ + MO(s) + H2(g)

 In this example, the insoluble metal oxide either remains at the cathode or precipitates into the molten
salt. Cleaning of the cathode surface (electrochemically or mechanically) and filtration or other separation
of the slag oxide phase may be required.

 Ding and colleagues used electrolytic purification to remove MgO impurities from a molten MgCl2-
NaCl-KCl salt combined with the use of cyclic voltammetry (CV) to monitor the MgO concentration in
the salt (Ding et al., 2019). In the work by Ding, a significant peak was observed in the initial CVs
corresponding to the cathode reaction given above, indicating an MOH+ concentration of 16713 ± 3325
ppm O mass fraction in the salt prior to purification. Electrodes for electrolysis were immersed in the salt
for ~30 mins to activate the surfaces, and then a potential of –1.25 V vs. a W quasi-reference electrode
was applied to the cathode while the molten salt was at 500 °C. The electrolysis current decreased to
<10% of the initial value after 3 minutes due to cathode passivation, and a significant lump was observed
on the cathode surface after electrolysis. EDS analysis indicated the lump was primarily composed of
magnesium (>55 wt%) and oxygen (>37 wt%), corresponding to the deposition of oxide (Ding 2019).
Applying a pulsed potential to the cathode (30 sec pulse with 90 sec interval) significantly decreased the
cathode passivation from the deposited oxide. It was suspected that this behavior was caused by shedding
the oxide layer during the interval period. These pulse and interval times can be further optimized for
given salt/impurity system. After 9 mins of electrochemical purification using the pulsed potential, the
CV peak current corresponding to hydroxide impurity was decreased by a factor of 6, indicating that the
concentration of MOH+ impurity in the bulk salt was reduced by 93% from 16713 ± 3325 ppm O to 1184
 ± 223 ppm O (Ding et al., 2019).

 Ultimately, the work by Ding concluded that electroactive impurities in the molten chloride salt can
be significantly reduced by electrochemical purification (Ding et al., 2019). Further analysis showed the
corrosion rate of the purified salt was reduced to ~20% of the unpurified salt at 700oC, resulting in a
significant decrease in chromium and iron species present in the bulk salt.

 In an effort to modify the above procedure into a continuous process, a later work by Ding examined
the possibility of periodically switching the applied potential between two identical electrodes, effectively
alternating which electrode serves as the anode and which is the cathode. The goal of this work was to
avoid electrode passivation and extend the lifetime of the purification electrodes. The electrodes of
interest were composed of a metallic species representing the dominant cation in the molten salt system to
minimize contamination of the salt with foreign species. The redox reactions described previously remain
applicable in this altered system. In this new configuration, the metal oxide again deposits on the cathode
surface resulting in passivation. However, switching the potential allows oxidation of the underlying
electrode surface, shedding the deposited oxide and effectively cleaning the electrode surface. A range of
conditions with different potential magnitudes and time intervals were tested. These parameters must be
carefully controlled to avoid unexpected side reactions resulting in irreversible electrode passivation. A

 21

test with a voltage magnitude of 0.8 V, time interval of 2 sec, and a 1 sec pause between voltage switches
to avoid current overload due to fast voltage changes (Ding et al., 2021) showed a strong decrease in
current in the first 20 mins due to the fast decrease of impurity concentration, owing to the high current
and high rate of salt purification. After this initial stage, the decrease in current slowed due to lower
impurity concentration and/or partial electrode passivation (Ding et al., 2021). The slower rate of
purification continued for the remaining 100 mins of testing. This represents a marked improvement from
the initial non-switching procedure described in the 2019 study, which ceased electrolysis within 5 mins
due to complete passivation of the cathode surface (Ding et al., 2021). XRD analysis indicated the loosely
adhered residues on the electrode surfaces were composed primarily of the metal oxide electrolysis
product, confirming the expected passivation during the cathodic reaction at the respective electrode.

 Although the dual-electrode construction greatly improved the electrolysis duration compared to the
initial design, it ultimately resulted in less effective purification. Post-analysis performed via cyclic
voltammetry indicated that the MOH+ concentration in the bulk salt was reduced by ~50%, compared to
~93% observed in the initial configuration. Further optimization of the electrolysis rate and duration of
this new configuration as well as the electrode geometry (i.e., a larger active surface) or mechanical
cleaning of the electrodes is needed to improve the purification efficiency at increasing scales.

 3.5 Chemical Purification
 Chemical purification reagents such as HCl or NH4Cl are currently used to process chloride salt
feedstock that will be used for metal production via electrolysis (Z. Zhang et al., 2012, 2015).
Carbochlorination is a well-known process for removing oxide impurities from metals (Sun et al., 1991).

 Several carbochlorination methods have been utilized in the literature, including C+Cl2, CO+Cl2,
COCl2, and CCl4, and these reactions are listed below where M represents a cation impurity (Kurley et al.,
2019). Chen demonstrated that CCl4 removes trace oxides as efficiently as phosgene (COCl2) with safer
handling protocols (Chen et al., 1993). A reaction vessel utilized by Kurley incorporated a quartz sparge
tube with several 1 mm holes to diffuse the gas into the molten salt. Graphite was initially used but was
replaced after wear resulted in significant breakage (Kurley et al., 2019). This sparge tube was initially
positioned above the salt to purge the reaction vessel with argon, then lowered into the molten salt and
CCl4 was flowed for 3 hours followed by an argon sparge for 30 mins. An alternative procedure sparged
with CCl4 for ~60 hours followed by 30 mins argon, ~60 hours argon with 4% hydrogen, and then
finished with 30 mins argon. A final sparging step consisting of 55 hours CCl4 and 55 hours Ar/4%H2
reduced the oxide impurities to trace amounts (Kurley et al., 2019).

 The efficacy of each reagent was assessed via thermodynamic favorability. A low Gibbs free energy (
Δ𝐺) for HCl with a positive Δ𝐺 above ~525 °C indicates that HCl is not favorable for removing MO but
was included in the study for comparison to CCl4. Based on the thermodynamic assessment, it was
determined that CCl4 becomes the favored purifying reagent for removing metal oxide impurities above
~525 °C (Kurley, 2019). The decomposition of CCl4 results in formation of CO and CO2, driving

 22

deoxygenation of the salt. It is important to filter the precipitated oxide species or otherwise maintain the
oxide below the solubility limit so as to prevent continuous dissolution into the molten salt during
purification.

 The as-received “anhydrous” chloride salt used by Kurley contained 0.39–0.40% moisture. Formation
of oxide species upon heating resulted in 10–30 mmol/kg O. A moderate purity was achieved via sparging
with CCl4 only, resulting in 915 µmol/kg O. The high-purity salt was achieved following the 55 hours
CCl4 followed by 55 hours Ar/4%H2, resulting in 42 µmol/kg O (Kurley et al., 2019). Further details
regarding the sparging and oxide determination procedures were reported by Mayes (Mayes et al., 2018).

 An alternative approach to chemical purification considered reaction with thionyl chloride (SOCl2) to
remove oxygen, oxygenated compounds, and hydroxides (McFarlane et al., 2022). Thionyl chloride was
vaporized into a stream of argon and passed through the chloride salt both above and below the melting
point to react with oxygenated impurities. Although the procedure was effective at reducing the oxygen
content in the salt, undesirable ternary compounds formed in the process.

 3.6 Distillation
 Halstenberg developed a multi-step distillation approach for the purification of molten salt systems
(Halstenberg, 2023). This method utilizes the high vapor pressure of molten chloride salts relative to their
oxide contaminants. Prior to distillation, a fused silica filtering apparatus was employed to remove oxides
and other solid contaminants present above the solubility limit, ultimately reducing the amount of HCl
required for thorough dehydration. This HCl gas was bubbled through a solution of pure H2SO4 to filter
any contaminants before passing to the filtration chamber and through a quartz filter and was finally
neutralized in a NaOH bath after flowing through the purification vessel. Other possible chlorinating
agents include carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) and hexachloropropene (C3Cl6). Following chlorination, a dry
oxygen-containing gas was bubbled through the molten chloride solution to remove carbon impurities (as
CO, CO2, etc.) and convert trace metal impurities in the salt to their oxide forms. These oxides were later
separated by the distillation process. Furthermore, the dry sparge gas may remove adsorbed moisture as
effluent, though a previously cited study indicates that a sufficiently high temperature is required to
release the monohydrate form (Burak & Raiman, 2025).

 Previous studies investigating the distillation method employed a single-use device where the molten
salt is heated under vacuum and the vapor is passed to a condensation zone at the top of the distillation
column. The salt vapor quickly deposits in the cold zone, and after sufficient salt has migrated, the
column is taken to an inert glove box and broken to recover the distilled salt. Halstenberg investigated a
modified approach to reduce cost and time required. This staged horizontal column design eliminated the
transfer tube between hot and cold zones, eliminating the clogging issue. A removable cap at one end
allowed vacuum connection and simple access to the final distillation stage to facilitate salt recovery
without breaking the column.

 3.7 Purification Summary
 The reported literature indicates that different methods of purification are beneficial at different stages
of salt processing and should be selected based upon the desired results. For example, if the goal is to

 23

reduce moisture content of the salt, this can be achieved simply and economically through a staged
heating approach to liberate the majority of the adsorbed hydrates. However, if a completely moisture-
free salt is desired, simple thermal processing is likely insufficient. Furthermore, thermal processing may
result in undesirable reaction intermediaries that require further processing to eliminate. One approach is
to purposefully create insoluble oxide particulates that can be mechanically filtered or separated in a slag
phase. This approach has the benefit of lowering oxide concentration at or below the solubility limit of the
salt, at which point more involved purification methods such as chemical or electrolytic purification can
be used to remove the remaining oxide without concern of reintroducing precipitated impurities. With
chemical purification methods such as bubbling chlorinating gases like CCl4 or adding a reducing agent
like magnesium chips (for magnesium salts), the concentration of corrosive impurities can be reduced to a
very low level, <5 ppm O (Kurley et al., 2019). However, this approach requires safe handling of
potentially corrosive and hazardous byproducts such as HCl. Advantages and disadvantages of these
purification methods are reviewed in Table 9 below.

 Certainly, the selection of a particular purification strategy must balance the desired salt purity with
economic concerns and time constraints. For the purpose of this review, salt purity is the most important
consideration. In this scenario, the recommended approach is to begin with a simple thermal treatment to
remove as much moisture as possible. Agitating the salt in the molten state via bubbling an inert gas
provides an accessible route for generated vapor species to exhaust from the salt system. Care must be
taken to prevent clogging in the exhaust stream from vapor condensation, as well as safe handling of
corrosive or toxic species via neutralization and filtering. Beyond the thermal decomposition of
impurities, further purification can be achieved chemically with reagents such as HCl, COCl2, or CCl4.
The previous studies indicated that the most effective process utilizes a vapor stream of CCl4, followed by
Ar/4%H2. Both steps should be performed while the salt is molten, and the reagent streams should be
maintained continuously for more than 2 days each. It is important to preheat the vapor stream to prevent
salt freezing and clogging upon introduction to the molten salt. The effectiveness of these purification
steps may be jeopardized by high initial impurity content leading to reduced kinetic efficiency or
generation of additional byproducts.

 4. Conclusions
 With a broad spectrum of MSR technologies in development, a diverse array of synthesis and
purification techniques will be necessary to address the unique needs and challenges of each reactor
design. Fuel salt synthesis methods that can produce hundreds of kilograms of fuel salt with impurity
levels that meet the reactor design specifications unique to each developer must be developed and
demonstrated to support deployment of these reactors. Furthermore, the synthesis techniques must be
operable with high levels of quality control and repeatability. This roadmap review reported avenues for
the synthesis of actinide chloride salts along with possible purification methods for different stages of salt
processing. Synthesis routes were evaluated based on the type of chlorinating agent, the actinide
precursor, the purity of the actinide product, whether the reaction was performed in a carrier molten salt,
and the scale at which the reactions have been reported. Overall, chlorination using gaseous agents (i.e.,
Cl2, HCl) and metal chlorides (i.e., AlCl3, CdCl2) have been reported at the largest scale in the literature.
However, careful control over the reaction environment is required when using gaseous chlorinating
agents to generate uranium chlorides to maintain the desired uranium valence state in the product, and
synthesis using metal chlorides may generate impurities that require an additional step to remove. Other
chlorinating agents such as ammonium chloride may provide alternative routes to generate gaseous
chlorinating agents in situ and better control the reaction environment but require additional effort to
demonstrate at scale.

 The choice of purification route will depend on the desired impurity content and the need to remove
or dispose of separated impurities (e.g., precipitated oxides). Thermal dehydration is a useful first step,
but complete removal of moisture impurities through this method is difficult and requires additional
processing to fully dehydrate the salt. Purification via electrolysis provides the greatest level of control
over the purification process, as well as the possibility for highly selective species separation and
recovery, but may be best suited for low impurity levels because of the reaction times required. Chemical
purification routes including HCl, COCl2, or CCl4 are highly effective but provide less control compared
to electrochemical methods generate gaseous waste products that should be captured or scrubbed. Other
methods such as metallic reduction and distillation may be effective depending on the properties of the
salt system undergoing purification. Many purification methods may require further processing to extract
precipitated contaminant forms such as oxides. Selection of the purification process must consider desired
purity and acceptable contaminants and economics for chemical feedstock and equipment and should be
developed in parallel with the chlorination process.

 Selection of the best synthesis and purification route for a given MSR system will require careful
consideration of factors such as the actinide or halide elements involved, the purity of the salt needed, as
well as the scale and economic feasibility to address the supply chain challenge. All the chlorination and
purification methods evaluated in this roadmap will require further effort to develop and demonstrate
actinide chloride synthesis at engineering scale to retire technical risks and provide necessary information
for industrial deployment. Metal chloride and gaseous chlorinating agents are the most mature
technologies for implementation on the industrial scale and should be further demonstrated at scale to
identify and retire technical gaps. Less mature technologies such as ammonium chloride chlorination may
offer benefits such as superior reaction control and should be evaluated to determine the actinide chloride
yield and purity. Additionally, purification technologies that complement the chlorination technologies
such as thermal treatment, electrolysis, and the addition of metallic reductants should be evaluated and

 26

implemented during chlorination process development to ensure that the resulting actinide chloride salt
will meet MSR fuel specifications. Future development of chlorination and purification technology with a
focus on scalability and purity will be instrumental in the transition of molten salt fuel cycle technologies
to industrial application and MSR adoption.
