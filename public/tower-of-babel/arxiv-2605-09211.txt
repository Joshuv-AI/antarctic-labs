A Variational Equation and Lower Bound for the Linear Least-Squares Backward Error
Eric Hallman

Abstract




Abstract
: This paper derives a new variational equation for the linear least-squares backward error by expressing the backward error in terms of a generalized eigenvalue problem and using results from indefinite linear algebra. For problems with multiple right-hand sides, the variational equation also shows that the backward error can be decomposed as a sum of smaller backward error problems. Applications to stopping criteria for iterative methods are considered, and a new sketching-based lower bound is proposed which is provably of quality comparable to the sketched Karlson-Waldén estimate.

Keywords
: Linear least squares, backward error, generalized eigenvalue problem, stopping criteria, iterative methods, LSQR, LSMR, Karlson-Waldén estimate

MSC Classification
: 15A06, 15A18, 15A22, 15A42, 65F10

1
Introduction

Given an approximate solution
X
to the linear least-squares problem

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}}\|AX-B\|_{F},

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
, this paper considers the problem of efficiently estimating the
backward error

\mu\equiv\min_{E,F}\left\{\|[E,\theta F]\|_{F}\,:\,(A+E)^{T}(A+E)X=(A+E)^{T}(B+F)\right\}.

(1)

Here,
E
and
F
are perturbations such that
X
is an exact solution to the perturbed least-squares problem, and
\theta\in(0,\infty]
is a weighting parameter. In principle, the backward error can be used in stopping criteria for iterative least-squares solvers, where the solver halts once
\mu
falls below a user-defined tolerance. In practice, computing
\mu
directly requires solving a large eigenvalue problem and so is prohibitively expensive in this context. It is therefore desirable to find estimates that are both cheap and reliable; see
[
14
,
13
,
7
,
10
,
2
]
for some prior work on this topic.

A key term appearing in expressions for the backward error is

R_{\theta}\equiv R\begin{bmatrix}X\\
-\theta^{-1}I\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger},\quad\text{where}\quad R\equiv B-AX,

where
A^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore-Penrose inverse of
A
. It represents the optimal backward error for the
consistent
problem,

R_{\theta}=\argmin_{[E,\theta F]}\left\{\|[E,\theta F]\|_{F}\,:\,(A+E)X=B+F\right\},

provided a valid perturbation exists. This requires the following condition:

Condition 1
.

The approximate solution
X
satisfies

\mathcal{R}(R^{T})\subseteq\mathcal{R}([X^{T},\theta^{-1}I])
.

It is sufficient to have
\theta<\infty
, or for
X
to have full column rank. Under this condition, the backward error may be expressed as

\mu=\mu(A,R_{\theta})\equiv\min_{Y}\left(\|YY^{\dagger}A\|_{F}^{2}+\|(I-YY^{\dagger})R_{\theta}\|_{F}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(2)

If
1
is not satisfied, the analysis is slightly more complicated
[
19
, Thm. 5.1]
but still requires solving a problem of the same type as
Eq.

2
. We find it useful to study the binary function
\mu(A,R_{\theta})
as opposed to the definition
Eq.

1
from which it was derived, and so will use the term “backward error” to refer to
\mu(A,R_{\theta})
for the remainder of the paper.

Remark 1
.

It can be seen from
Eq.

2
that
\mu(A,R_{\theta})
is invariant under right rotations of its inputs. We can therefore assume without loss of generality that
A
and
R_{\theta}
both have full column rank. Accordingly, sometimes
R(\theta^{-2}I+X^{T}X)^{-1/2}
is used in place of
R_{\theta}
in the literature, or
\theta r/\sqrt{1+\theta^{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}}
for problems with a single right-hand side.

The main contribution of this work is the following theorem, which elegantly decomposes the backward error as a sum of smaller backward error problems:

Theorem 1
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
R_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
. Then with
\mu(A,R_{\theta})
defined as in
Eq.

2
and
k\equiv\min(n,d)
,

\mu(A,R_{\theta})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}P^{T}P=I_{k}\\
Q^{T}Q=I_{k}\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu^{2}(Ap_{i},R_{\theta}q_{i})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(3)

where
P=[p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and
Q=[q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
.

Each summand can be evaluated cheaply and stably;
Theorem

3
gives an exact expression.

We also consider some potential applications of
Theorem

1
to stopping criteria for iterative methods. The main point of comparison in this regard is the
sketched Karlson-Waldén estimate

[
2
]
, which uses subspace embedding techniques to estimate the highly accurate Karlson-Waldén estimate
[
14
,
6
]
. The room for improvement is admittedly limited, as the sketched Karlson-Waldén estimate was found in
[
2
]
to be highly accurate in practice. Still, there is one notable difference: any estimate derived from
Eq.

3
is a lower bound on the backward error, while the sketched Karlson-Waldén estimate is not necessarily an upper or lower bound.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows:
Section

2
provides background including notations and some lemmas.
Section

3
proves an intermediate result that involves expressing the backward error in terms of a generalized eigenvalue problem.
Section

4
gives the proof of the main result, along with some results for problems with a single right-hand side.
Section

5
considers applications to error bounds and stopping criteria.
Section

6
includes the results of some numerical experiments, and
Section

7
offers our concluding remarks.

2
Background

It was shown in
[
19
, Thm. 4.2]
that the backward error can be expressed as

\mu(A,R_{\theta})=\left(\|R_{\theta}\|_{F}^{2}+\operatorname{tr}_{-}(AA^{T}-R_{\theta}R_{\theta}^{T})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(4)

where
\operatorname{tr}_{-}(X)
denotes the sum of the negative eigenvalues of
X
. This value can be attained in
Eq.

2
by having
YY^{\dagger}
project onto the negative eigenspace of
AA^{T}-R_{\theta}R_{\theta}^{T}
. This matrix has rank at most
n+d
. If
m\gg n\gg d
then the cost of computing
\mu(A,R_{\theta})
can be reduced using a QR factorization or SVD of
A

[
20
,
14
]
, but this is generally not practical in the context of iterative solvers.

Three simple upper bounds on
\mu(A,R_{\theta})
are obtained by evaluating
Eq.

2
for
Y=0
,
Y=A
, and
Y=R_{\theta}
. The first choice yields
\mu\leq\|R_{\theta}\|_{F}
, but this is not an especially useful upper bound since the residual does not necessarily converge to zero. The second is too costly to compute to be used for stopping rules, although efforts to estimate it are made in
[
13
]
. The third equals
\|A^{T}r\|_{2}/\|r\|_{2}
for problems with a single right-hand side, and it is used in stopping rules for iterative solvers LSQR
[
18
]
and LSMR
[
3
]
. However, it is possible for these three bounds to simultaneously overestimate
\mu
by a factor depending on the condition number of
A

[
7
]
.

The proofs in this work are often simplified by assuming that
[A,R_{\theta}]
has full column rank, then invoking a continuity argument. This argument can be made precise via the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

The backward error as defined in
Eq.

2
satisfies

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\mu\left(\begin{bmatrix}A\\
\epsilon I\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}R_{\theta}\\
0\\
\epsilon I\end{bmatrix}\right)=\mu(A,R_{\theta}).

Proof.

Use
Eq.

4
and the fact that eigenvalues are continuous.
∎

2.1
The Karlson-Waldén estimate

The Karlson-Waldén estimate was derived in
[
14
, (2.6)]
as the solution to a maximization problem

\displaystyle\nu(A,r_{\theta})

\displaystyle\equiv\max_{\|p\|_{2}=1}\frac{|p^{T}A^{T}r_{\theta}|}{(\|Ap\|_{2}^{2}+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2})^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=\|(A^{T}A+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}I)^{-1/2}A^{T}r_{\theta}\|_{2},

where the maximum is attained by
p\propto(A^{T}A+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}I)^{-1}A^{T}r_{\theta}
. For multiple right-hand sides, the estimate can be defined additively: if
W=[w_{1},\ldots,w_{d}]
is a set of right singular vectors for
R_{\theta}
, then

\nu(A,R_{\theta})\equiv\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\nu^{2}(A,R_{\theta}w_{i})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(5)

It can alternately be defined in terms of a minimal perturbation
[
10
, (14)]

\nu(A,R_{\theta})=\min_{E,F}\{\|[E,F]\|_{F}\ :\ A^{T}F+E^{T}R_{\theta}=-A^{T}R_{\theta}\},

which shows that
\nu(A,R_{\theta})
, like
\mu(A,R_{\theta})
, is symmetric in its inputs and invariant under right rotations of the inputs.

The Karlson-Waldén estimate is always accurate, satisfying
[
10
, Thm. 4.8]

1\leq\frac{\mu(A,R_{\theta})}{\nu(A,R_{\theta})}\leq\sqrt{1+\|AA^{\dagger}R_{\theta}R_{\theta}^{\dagger}\|_{2}}\leq\sqrt{2}.

The
\sqrt{2}
bound cannot be improved in general
[
6
]
. For the single right-hand side case the asymptotic accuracy of
\nu(A,r_{\theta})
was proved in
[
8
, Thm. 4.8]
, and
[
6
, Cor. 3.2]
gives an asymptotic bound that is tighter than the one stated above.

2.1.1
The sketched Karlson-Waldén estimate

The Karlson-Waldén estimate is still too expensive to use directly for stopping rules, though some efforts to efficiently compute or estimate it have been made in
[
13
,
9
,
4
]
. One practical approach, recently proposed in
[
2
]
, is to use a
sketching matrix
: a matrix
S\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{sketch}}\times m}
with the property that
\|SAy\|_{2}\approx\|Ay\|_{2}
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with high probability. One option for
S
is a Gaussian embedding, whose entries are independent
\mathcal{N}(0,n_{\text{sketch}}^{-1})
random variables. Other options are discussed in
[
17
]
; the authors in
[
2
]
recommend using a class known as sparse sign embeddings.

Given a sketching matrix
S\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{sketch}}\times m}
(
n_{\text{sketch}}\geq n
), the
sketched Karlson-Waldén estimate
is defined as

\widetilde{\nu}(A,r_{\theta};S)\equiv\|((SA)^{T}(SA)+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}I)^{-1/2}A^{T}r_{\theta}\|_{2}

(6)

for the single right-hand-side case; the definition can be naturally extended to the multiple right-hand-side case as with
Eq.

5
. This estimate can be practical for iterative methods because the SVD of
SA
can be precomputed, after which computing
\widetilde{\nu}
requires only computing
A^{T}r_{\theta}
plus
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
operations. It was observed in
[
2
]
that the sketched estimate is extremely reliable in practice, although the authors note that it is difficult to determine at runtime the precise level of distortion incurred by the sketch.

2.2
Tools from indefinite linear algebra

The key technique in this work is to transform the eigenvalue problem from
Eq.

4
into a generalized eigenvalue problem. We first introduce a standard result for the generalized eigenvalue problem, essentially
[
5
, Cor. 8.7.2]
with minor modifications.

Lemma 2
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric positive definite and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
symmetric and nonsingular. Then there exists a nonsingular
V=[v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that

V^{T}AV=\Diag(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\quad\text{and}\quad V^{T}BV=\Diag(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}).

Moreover,
Av_{i}=\lambda_{i}Bv_{i}
, where
\lambda_{i}=a_{i}/b_{i}
.

The columns of
V
are known as
generalized eigenvectors of the pencil
(A,B)
, and the
\lambda_{i}
are the
generalized eigenvalues
.

Next, define

J_{n,d}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}&0\\
0&-I_{d}\end{bmatrix}.

The second lemma does the heavy lifting, and is a special case of a result by Kovač-Striko and Veselić
[
16
, Thm. 3.1]
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+d)\times(n+d)}
be symmetric positive semidefinite. Then

\min_{X^{T}J_{n,d}X=-I_{d}}\operatorname{tr}(X^{T}AX)\geq-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{-},

where

\lambda_{n}^{+}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{1}^{+}\geq\lambda_{1}^{-}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}^{-}

are the generalized eigenvalues of
(A,J_{n,d})
. If there exists a feasible matrix
X_{*}
whose columns are generalized eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda_{1}^{-},\ldots,\lambda_{d}^{-}
, then equality is attained at
X_{*}
.

Finally, any matrix
X
satisfying
X^{T}J_{n,d}X=-I_{d}
has a
hyperbolic CS decomposition
, an indefinite analogue of the CS decomposition for unitary matrices
[
5
, Thm. 2.5.3]
. The lemma below is adapted from
[
12
, Thm. 3.2]
.

Lemma 4
.

Assume that
n\geq d
. If
X\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+d)\times d}
satisfies
X^{T}J_{n,d}X=-I_{d}
, then there exist orthogonal matrices
Q,Z\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and a matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with orthonormal columns such that

X=\begin{bmatrix}P&0\\
0&Q\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}S\\
C\end{bmatrix}Z^{T},

where
S=\Diag(s_{i})
and
C=\Diag(c_{i})
satisfy
{C^{2}-S^{2}=I_{d}}
. Conversely, any
X
having this factorization satisfies
X^{T}J_{n,d}X=-I_{d}
.

3
Optimization over an indefinite manifold

This section presents and proves an intermediate result that admits a lower bound on the backward error. As with the expression in
Eq.

4
it can suffer from stability issues if evaluated directly, but it is useful in proving the main result in
Theorem

1
.

Lemma 5
.

For any
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
R_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
,

\mu(A,R_{\theta})=\sup_{X^{T}J_{n,d}X=-I_{d}}\left(\|R_{\theta}\|_{F}^{2}-\|[A,R_{\theta}]X\|_{F}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(7)

Proof.

Assume to start that
[A,R_{\theta}]
has full column rank, so that
[A,R_{\theta}]^{T}[A,R_{\theta}]
is symmetric positive definite, and consider the pencil
([A,R_{\theta}]^{T}[A,R_{\theta}],J_{n,d})
. By
Lemma

2
, there exists a nonsingular
V\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+d)\times(n+d)}
such that
V^{T}J_{n,d}V=J_{n,d}
1
1

1

Without loss of generality, the columns of
V
can be permuted and scaled and so that the diagonal entries of
V^{T}J_{n,d}V
are
\pm 1
. Preservation of inertia implies that there are exactly
n
positive and
d
negative eigenvalues.
and

[A,R_{\theta}]^{T}[A,R_{\theta}]V=J_{n,d}V\Lambda,\quad\Lambda\equiv\Diag(\lambda_{n}^{+},\ldots,\lambda_{1}^{+},\lambda_{1}^{-},\ldots,\lambda_{d}^{-}).

(8)

The generalized eigenvalues are ordered so that

\lambda_{n}^{+}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{1}^{+}>0>\lambda_{1}^{-}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}^{-}.

Applying
Lemma

3
to the positive definite matrix
[A,R_{\theta}]^{T}[A,R_{\theta}]
then implies that

\min_{X^{T}J_{n,d}X=-I_{d}}\|[A,R_{\theta}]X\|_{F}^{2}=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{-},

(9)

where equality is attained by setting the columns of
X
to be the generalized eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda_{1}^{-},\ldots,\lambda_{d}^{-}
.

Finally, the generalized eigenvalues of
([A,R_{\theta}]^{T}[A,R_{\theta}],J_{n,d})
are precisely the nonzero eigenvalues of
AA^{T}-R_{\theta}R_{\theta}^{T}
. To see this, left-multiply both sides of
Eq.

8
by
[A,R_{\theta}]J_{n,d}
to get

(AA^{T}-R_{\theta}R_{\theta}^{T})U=U\Lambda,\quad U\equiv[A,R_{\theta}]V,

(10)

where the columns of
U
are linearly independent since
[A,R_{\theta}]
has full column rank. Thus, the
n+d
columns of
U
account for all nonzero eigenvectors of the rank-(
n+d
) matrix
AA^{T}-R_{\theta}R_{\theta}^{T}
.

Starting from
Eq.

4
, we thus find that

\displaystyle\mu^{2}(A,R_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\|R_{\theta}\|_{F}^{2}+\operatorname{tr}_{-}\left(AA^{T}-R_{\theta}R_{\theta}^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\|R_{\theta}\|_{F}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{-}

\displaystyle=\max_{X^{T}J_{n,d}X=-I_{d}}\|R_{\theta}\|_{F}^{2}-\|[A,R_{\theta}]X\|_{F}^{2}.

The case where
[A,R_{\theta}]
does not have full column rank is proved by making a continuity argument as in
Lemma

1
.
∎

Equality in
Eq.

7
is not necessarily attainable when
[A,R_{\theta}]
does not have full column rank because the set of matrices
X
satisfying
X^{T}J_{n,d}X=-I_{d}
is not bounded.

Example 1
.

Let
A=R_{\theta}=[1]
,
in which case
\mu(A,R_{\theta})=1
. Equality cannot be attained for any
X
, but we can come arbitrarily close by taking
X=[s,-\sqrt{1+s^{2}}]^{T}
as
s\rightarrow\infty
.

Remark 2
.

The eigenvalue relations for the matrix
AA^{T}-R_{\theta}R_{\theta}^{T}
and the pencil
([A,R_{\theta}]^{T}[A,R_{\theta}],J_{n,d})
can be expressed simultaneously by the indefinite generalized eigenvalue problem

\begin{bmatrix}0&[A,R_{\theta}]^{T}\\
[A,R_{\theta}]&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}v\\
u\end{bmatrix}=\xi\begin{bmatrix}J_{n,d}&0\\
0&I_{m}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}v\\
u\end{bmatrix}.

The generalized eigenvalues
\xi
satisfy
\xi^{2}=\lambda
, and so are either real or purely imaginary.

3.1
Eigenvalue expressions

For problems with a single right-hand side, it is known
[
20
, (2.15)]
that the backward error can be expressed in terms of a singular value problem:

\mu(A,r_{\theta})=\min\left\{\|r_{\theta}\|_{2},\sigma_{\min}\left(\left[A,\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}(I-r_{\theta}r_{\theta}^{\dagger})\right]\right)\right\}.

Although this expression avoids forming
A^{T}A
or
AA^{T}
explicitly, it overstates the size of the problem if considered naively: the matrix whose smallest singular value is to be computed is
m\times(n+m)
. But since the backward error is rotation-invariant, the QR factorization of
[A,r_{\theta}]
can be used to reduce the size to
(n+1)\times(2n+1)
.

The true size of the problem is better captured by the generalized eigenvalue problem
Eq.

8
. For problems with a single right-hand side, it may be formulated as follows:

Theorem 2
.

For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
r_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\mu(A,r_{\theta})=\lambda_{\min}\left(\begin{bmatrix}A^{T}A+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}I&A^{T}r_{\theta}\\
r_{\theta}^{T}A&0\end{bmatrix},J_{n,1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof.

When
[A,r_{\theta}]
has full column rank, take the eigenvalue relations in the proof of
Lemma

5
and shift by
\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}J_{n,1}
. When
[A,r_{\theta}]
does not have full column rank, use a continuity argument as in
Lemma

1
.
∎

Since the generalized eigenvalues in
Theorem

2
are solutions to the equation

\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}A^{T}A+(\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}-\mu^{2})I&A^{T}r_{\theta}\\
r_{\theta}^{T}A&\mu^{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)=0,

we can recover an expression for the backward error that appears in
[
6
, (3.13)]
.

Corollary 1
.

For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
r_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mu(A,r_{\theta})
is the smallest nonnegative number solving

\mu^{2}=r_{\theta}^{T}A(A^{T}A+(\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}-\mu^{2})I)^{-1}A^{T}r_{\theta}.

Considered as a function of
\mu^{2}
, the right-hand side is convex and monotonically increasing. The Karlson-Waldén estimate is the square root of the value of this function at zero. Given the right singular vectors of
A
, the equation can be solved using fixed-point iteration or Newton’s method, and so the backward error can be computed to a high degree of accuracy.

4
Main results

From looking at
Lemma

5
, it might be tempting to split the expression in
Eq.

7
columnwise, with each column of
X
being one generalized eigenvector. As it turns out, it is more natural to consider the hyperbolic CS decomposition of
X
. The proof of
Theorem

1
does exactly that, and as a result decomposes the backward error into a sum of smaller backward error problems.

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

Since
Eq.

2
implies that
\mu(A,R_{\theta})=\mu(R_{\theta},A)
, we may assume without loss of generality that
n\geq d
. Proceeding from
Lemma

5
, consider the hyperbolic CS decomposition of any feasible
X
. By
Lemma

4
, optimizing over
X
is equivalent to optimizing over its constituent parts
P,Q,S,C,Z
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mu^{2}(A,R_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sup_{X^{T}J_{n,d}X=-I_{d}}\|R_{\theta}\|_{F}^{2}-\|[A,R_{\theta}]X\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{P,Q,S,C,Z}\|R_{\theta}\|_{F}^{2}-\left\|[AP,R_{\theta}Q]\begin{bmatrix}S\\
C\end{bmatrix}Z^{T}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{P,Q,S,C}\|R_{\theta}Q\|_{F}^{2}-\left\|[AP,R_{\theta}Q]\begin{bmatrix}S\\
C\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{P,Q,S,C}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\|R_{\theta}q_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|[Ap_{i},R_{\theta}q_{i}]\begin{bmatrix}s_{i}\\
c_{i}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{P,Q}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sup_{c_{i}^{2}-s_{i}^{2}=1}\|R_{\theta}q_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|[Ap_{i},R_{\theta}q_{i}]\begin{bmatrix}s_{i}\\
c_{i}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{P,Q}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu^{2}(Ap_{i},R_{\theta}q_{i}),

where the final step uses
Lemma

5
. Note that the maximum is attainable because the set of feasible
\{P,Q\}
(both matrices with orthonormal columns) is compact.
∎

A secondary result presents a clean expression for each summand in
Eq.

3
.

Theorem 3
.

For
a\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
r\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, at least one of which is nonzero,

\mu(a,r)=\frac{2|a^{T}r|}{\|a+r\|_{2}+\|a-r\|_{2}},

where
\mu(a,r)
is as defined in
Eq.

2
.

Proof.

By
Corollary

1
,
\mu(a,r)
is the smallest nonnegative solution to the equation

\mu^{2}=\frac{(a^{T}r)^{2}}{\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\|r\|_{2}^{2}-\mu^{2}}.

Solving the resulting quadratic equation yields

\displaystyle\mu(a,r)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\|r\|_{2}^{2}-\sqrt{(\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\|r\|_{2}^{2})^{2}-4(a^{T}r)^{2}}}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\|r\|_{2}^{2}-\|a+r\|_{2}\|a-r\|_{2}}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\|a+r\|_{2}^{2}+\|a-r\|_{2}^{2}-2\|a+r\|_{2}\|a-r\|_{2}}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\|a+r\|_{2}-\|a-r\|_{2}|}{2}.

Although elegant, this formula is unstable. The stable formulation is

\mu=\frac{|\|a+r\|_{2}-\|a-r\|_{2}|}{2}\cdot\frac{\|a+r\|_{2}+\|a-r\|_{2}}{\|a+r\|_{2}+\|a-r\|_{2}}=\frac{2|a^{T}r|}{\|a+r\|_{2}+\|a-r\|_{2}},

which completes the proof.
∎

For problems with a single right-hand side,
Theorem

1
means that the backward error can be “explained” by a single direction
Ap
. The next theorem gives an expression for the optimal
p
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
r_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. If
\mu(A,r_{\theta})>0
, then

\mu(A,r_{\theta})=\max_{\|p\|_{2}=1}\frac{2|p^{T}A^{T}r_{\theta}|}{\|Ap+r_{\theta}\|_{2}+\|Ap-r_{\theta}\|_{2}},

(11)

and the maximum is attained by
p\propto\left(A^{T}A+(\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}-\mu^{2})I\right)^{-1}A^{T}r_{\theta}
.

Proof.

The expression
Eq.

11
is simply the result of combining
Theorem

1
with
Theorem

3
. As for the optimal value of
p
, it can be checked that

v=\begin{bmatrix}(A^{T}A+(\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}-\mu^{2})I)^{-1}A^{T}r_{\theta}\\
-1\end{bmatrix}

is a generalized eigenvector of the pencil
([A,r_{\theta}]^{T}[A,r_{\theta}],J_{n,1})
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{-}=\mu^{2}-\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}
. By
Lemma

3
and its application to
Theorem

1
, the maximum of
\mu(Ap,r_{\theta})
is attained precisely by taking the CS decomposition of
v
(i.e., normalizing the vector in the first entry to get
p
).
∎

5
Application to error bounds

This section will focus on problems with a single right-hand side; the more general case will be left to future work.

5.1
Lower bound

Given some estimate
\mu_{\text{est}}
for the backward error, one may use a sketch
SA
to compute

\widetilde{p}\equiv\left((SA)^{T}(SA)+(\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}-\mu_{\text{est}}^{2})I\right)^{-1}A^{T}r_{\theta},

(12)

then normalize
\widetilde{p}
and apply
Theorem

4
to obtain a true lower bound. As compared with computing
\widetilde{\nu}
as in
Eq.

6
it requires one additional matvec with
A
to compute
Ap
. The choice
\mu_{\est}=0
will generally be sufficient, as the quality of the sketch is far more likely to be factor limiting the accuracy of any subsequent backward error estimate.

The following theorem gives bounds on the error of this estimator; the proof is given in
Appendix

A
of the appendix.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\widetilde{p}
be as in
Eq.

12
, with
\mu_{\text{est}}=0
and where the sketch
S
satisfies
(1-\eta)\|Ay\|_{2}\leq\|S(Ay)\|_{2}\leq(1+\eta)\|Ay\|_{2}
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\frac{1-\eta^{2}}{1+\eta^{2}}\nu(A,r_{\theta})\leq\mu(A\widetilde{p}/\|\widetilde{p}\|_{2},r_{\theta})\leq\mu(A,r_{\theta}).

5.1.1
Iterative refinement

The lower bound may be improved via iterative refinement. Given
\widetilde{p}
and
\mu_{\text{est}}
as in
Eq.

12
, one can compute

\widetilde{p}_{\text{next}}=\widetilde{p}+\left((SA)^{T}(SA)+(\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}-\mu_{\text{est}}^{2})I\right)^{-1}(A^{T}(r_{\theta}-A\widetilde{p})-(\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}-\mu_{\text{est}}^{2})\widetilde{p}).

(13)

This will improve the estimate if the sketch
SA
is of sufficient quality. An iterative method could potentially use
\widetilde{p}
as a starting guess to compute
\widetilde{p}_{\text{next}}
on the subsequent iteration, but the added cost per iteration is non-negligible.

5.1.2
Estimate recycling

Once
Ap
is computed, its value may be reused for other choices of
r_{\theta}
, including for subsequent iterations of an iterative method. For fixed
Ap
,
Eq.

11
can be computed with just access to
r_{\theta}
, and it is not necessary to find
A^{T}r_{\theta}
. The vector
p
may be recomputed whenever the estimated error drops below a user-defined threshold.

5.2
Upper bound

With
\widetilde{p}
as in
Eq.

12
, one can interpret
[\widetilde{p}^{T},-1]^{T}
as an approximate generalized eigenvector (see
Theorem

4
). From
Eq.

10
it follows that
\widetilde{u}\equiv A\widetilde{p}-r_{\theta}
is an approximate eigenvector for
AA^{T}-r_{\theta}r_{\theta}^{T}
, and so in principle we can use
Eq.

2
to bound the backward error as

\mu(A,r_{\theta})\leq\left(\|\widetilde{u}\widetilde{u}^{\dagger}A\|_{2}^{2}+\|(I-\widetilde{u}\widetilde{u}^{\dagger})r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

More generously, we could compute an orthonormal basis
U
for
[A\widetilde{p},r_{\theta}]
and directly solve the right-hand side of

\mu(A,r_{\theta})\leq\mu(U^{T}A,U^{T}r_{\theta}).

(14)

These upper bounds require additional matvecs with
A
beyond what is needed to compute the lower bound.

7
Conclusion

The primary contribution of this work is a theoretical one: a decomposition of the backward error into a sum of smaller terms, which uses the fact that the backward error can be expressed naturally in terms of a generalized eigenvalue problem. This decomposition allows for the simple computation of lower bounds on the backward error, and in particular can be used to convert a sketch-based estimate of the backward error into a lower bound of comparable quality. Efforts to produce upper bounds in a similar manner were generally unsuccessful.

For a potential application to iterative methods, one can compute a single test vector
Ap
, which may be reused over multiple iterations to cheaply find lower bounds on the backward error. If the residual does not change too drastically from one iteration to the next then the bounds may be of sufficient quality to use in place of more expensive estimates.

One potential avenue for future work is to develop procedures for finding lower bounds when the problem has multiple right-hand sides. Whereas the single right-hand side case involves estimating a single vector
p
, the general case involves two matrices
P
and
Q
, constrained to have orthonormal columns. It would, of course, be valuable to find a method of producing accurate upper bounds; failing that, future work could explain why finding a good upper bound appears to be more difficult than finding a good lower bound.

Appendix A
Proof of
Theorem

5

The goal is to prove that

\frac{1-\eta^{2}}{1+\eta^{2}}\nu(A,r_{\theta})\leq\mu(A\widetilde{p}/\|\widetilde{p}\|_{2},r_{\theta})\leq\mu(A,r_{\theta}).

The right-hand inequality follows directly from
Theorem

1
. As for the left-hand inequality,

\displaystyle\mu^{2}(A\widetilde{p}/\|\widetilde{p}\|_{2},r_{\theta})

\displaystyle\geq\nu^{2}(A\widetilde{p}/\|\widetilde{p}\|_{2},r_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\widetilde{p}^{T}A^{T}r_{\theta})^{2}/\|\widetilde{p}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|A\widetilde{p}\|_{2}^{2}/\|\widetilde{p}\|_{2}^{2}+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{p}^{T}A^{T}r_{\theta}r_{\theta}^{T}A\widetilde{p}}{\widetilde{p}^{T}(A^{T}A+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}I)\widetilde{p}}.

This Rayleigh quotient is maximized at
p\equiv(A^{T}A+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}I)^{-1}A^{T}r_{\theta}
. To compare the value in the above expression to the maximum attainable value, make the substitutions

\displaystyle A^{T}r_{\theta}

\displaystyle=(A^{T}A+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}I)p,

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}

\displaystyle=((SA)^{T}(SA)+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}I)^{-1}(A^{T}A+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}I)p.

To simplify the expressions, define
\hat{A}\equiv[A^{T},\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}I]^{T}
and take the Cholesky factorization
((SA)^{T}(SA)+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}I)^{-1}=\hat{R}^{T}\hat{R}
. Proceeding, we find that

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{p}^{T}A^{T}r_{\theta}r_{\theta}^{T}A\widetilde{p}}{\widetilde{p}^{T}(A^{T}A+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}I)\widetilde{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(p^{T}(\hat{A}^{T}\hat{A})(\hat{R}^{T}\hat{R})^{-1}(\hat{A}^{T}\hat{A})p\right)^{2}}{p^{T}(\hat{A}^{T}\hat{A})(\hat{R}^{T}\hat{R})^{-1}(\hat{A}^{T}\hat{A})(\hat{R}^{T}\hat{R})^{-1}(\hat{A}^{T}\hat{A})p}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(p^{T}\hat{A}^{T}(\hat{A}\hat{R}^{-1})(\hat{R}^{-T}\hat{A}^{T})\hat{A}p\right)^{2}}{p^{T}\hat{A}^{T}(\hat{A}\hat{R}^{-1})(\hat{R}^{-T}\hat{A}^{T})(\hat{A}\hat{R}^{-1})(\hat{R}^{-T}\hat{A}^{T})\hat{A}p}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(p^{T}\hat{A}^{T}H\hat{A}p\right)^{2}}{p^{T}\hat{A}^{T}H^{2}\hat{A}p},

where
H\equiv(\hat{A}\hat{R}^{-1})(\hat{R}^{-T}\hat{A}^{T})
.

To bound this expression, we use the Kantorovich inequality
[
11
]
, which (when put in terms of matrix algebra) states that for a symmetric positive definite matrix
M
and vector
x
,

(x^{T}Mx)(x^{T}M^{-1}x)\leq\frac{(\lambda_{\max}(M)+\lambda_{\min}(M))^{2}}{4\lambda_{\max}(M)\lambda_{\min}(M)}(x^{T}x)^{2}=\frac{(1+\kappa(M))^{2}}{4\kappa(M)}(x^{T}x)^{2},

where
\kappa(M)=\lambda_{\max}(M)/\lambda_{\min}(M)
. Applying this inequality with
x=H^{1/2}\hat{A}p
and
M=H
, then rearranging, yields

\displaystyle\frac{\left(p^{T}\hat{A}^{T}H\hat{A}p\right)^{2}}{p^{T}\hat{A}^{T}H^{2}\hat{A}p}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{4\kappa(H)}{(1+\kappa(H))^{2}}\|\hat{A}p\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\kappa(H)}{(1+\kappa(H))^{2}}r_{\theta}^{T}A(A^{T}A+\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}I)^{-1}A^{T}r_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\kappa(H)}{(1+\kappa(H))^{2}}\nu^{2}(A,r_{\theta}),

where
\kappa(H)=\sigma_{\max}^{2}(\hat{A}\hat{R}^{-1})/\sigma_{\min}^{2}(\hat{A}\hat{R}^{-1})
.
3
3

3

Note that although
H
is not necessarily positive definite,
\hat{A}p
is in the column space of
H
. Thus only the nonzero singular values of
H
are considered.

Finally, from the sketching bounds
(1-\eta)\|Ay\|_{2}\leq\|S(Ay)\|_{2}\leq(1+\eta)\|Ay\|_{2}
we can find that

(1-\eta)\|\hat{A}y\|_{2}\leq\|\hat{R}y\|_{2}\leq(1+\eta)\|\hat{A}y\|_{2}

holds as well; i.e., the regularizing term
\|r_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}I
can only reduce the distortion of the sketch. From
[
2
, Fact 2.2]
it follows that the condition number of
\hat{A}\hat{R}^{-1}
is bounded above by
(1+\eta)/(1-\eta)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mu(A\widetilde{p}/\|\widetilde{p}\|_{2},r_{\theta})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2\sqrt{\kappa(H)}}{1+\kappa(H)}\nu(A,r_{\theta})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2\frac{1+\eta}{1-\eta}}{1+\frac{(1+\eta)^{2}}{(1-\eta)^{2}}}\nu(A,r_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{2(1+\eta)(1-\eta)}{(1-\eta)^{2}+(1+\eta)^{2}}\nu(A,r_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\eta^{2}}{1+\eta^{2}}\nu(A,r_{\theta}).
