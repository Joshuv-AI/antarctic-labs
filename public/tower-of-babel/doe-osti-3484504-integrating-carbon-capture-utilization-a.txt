Executive Summary

The U.S. pulp and paper industry presents a unique and largely untapped opportunity for large-
scale carbon dioxide removal (CDR). Unlike most industrial sectors, pulp mills rely heavily on
biomass, meaning that much of their carbon emissions originate from atmospheric CO₂ that was
recently captured by plants. If this biogenic CO₂ can be captured and permanently stored, pulp
mills can be transformed from carbon emitters into net carbon removal facilities. This project
was motivated by that opportunity and aimed to develop and evaluate integrated, low-cost
strategies for capturing, utilizing, and sequestering CO₂ within existing chemical pulping
operations.

The scope of this work focused on four complementary innovations designed to integrate
seamlessly into kraft pulp mill infrastructure: (1) in situ CO₂ capture within the recovery cycle,
(2) oxy-fuel retrofitting of the rotary lime kiln to produce a high-purity CO₂ stream, (3) ex situ
CO₂ capture and mineralization using pulp mill residues (dregs, grits, and lime mud), and (4)
beneficial reuse of these residues as mineral carbonate fertilizers. The project combined process
modeling, laboratory experimentation, life cycle assessment (LCA), and field trials to evaluate
the technical feasibility, economic viability, and environmental impact of these approaches.

The results demonstrate that pulp mills can serve as effective platforms for carbon removal when
equipped with integrated carbon capture systems. Process modeling showed that combining
sodium spiking with oxy-fuel calcination significantly enhances CO₂ capture efficiency while
reducing costs by up to 31% compared to conventional configurations. Experimental work
further revealed that calcination behavior in high-CO₂ environments differs substantially from
traditional systems, leading to the development of a new kinetic model that predicts reaction
rates under these conditions. This model provides essential design guidance for next-generation
decarbonized lime kilns.

In parallel, the project demonstrated that alkaline mineral residues generated during pulping
operations can be repurposed as a sustainable alternative to agricultural lime. Across a wide
range of soils in the southeastern United States, these materials performed equivalently to
commercial lime in adjusting soil pH while offering lower greenhouse gas emissions and
reduced cost. Field and greenhouse studies confirmed that crop and tree growth responses were
comparable, supporting their viability as a drop-in replacement. This co-product pathway
provides a practical utilization strategy that offsets costs and improves overall system economics.

A major contribution of this project is the first comprehensive life cycle assessment of carbon
removal in pulp and paper systems across multiple system boundaries. Results show that
retrofitted mills can achieve carbon removal efficiencies ranging from 12% to 92%, depending
on how the system is defined. This finding highlights a critical issue in carbon accounting:
reported performance is highly sensitive to methodological choices. By explicitly quantifying
these differences, this work provides valuable guidance for policymakers, carbon registries, and
project developers working to standardize carbon removal metrics.

From a commercialization perspective, the technologies investigated in this project are well-
aligned with existing industrial infrastructure, minimizing the need for entirely new facilities.

 3

Industry engagement throughout the project—including collaboration with pulp and paper
companies, equipment manufacturers, and carbon removal developers—has accelerated the
transition from research to deployment. Notably, a commercial developer is actively pursuing
carbon capture projects at pulp mills in the southeastern United States and has cited this research
as a contributing foundation. The emergence of voluntary carbon markets and long-term offtake
agreements further strengthens the business case for implementation.

The broader public benefits of this work are significant. By enabling large-scale carbon removal
using existing industrial systems, this approach offers a near-term pathway to reduce
atmospheric CO₂ concentrations while supporting domestic manufacturing and rural economies.
The reuse of industrial residues as fertilizers reduces reliance on mined materials, lowers costs
for farmers, and decreases environmental impacts associated with conventional lime production.
In addition, the project has supported workforce development by training graduate students and
researchers in carbon capture technologies, helping to build capacity in a critical area of national
interest.

In conclusion, this project demonstrates that integrated carbon capture, utilization, and
sequestration in pulp mills is both technically feasible and economically promising. By
combining process innovation, experimental validation, and systems-level analysis, the work
advances the understanding of how biomass-based industries can contribute to climate
mitigation. The findings provide a strong foundation for commercial deployment and offer a
scalable solution for transforming a major U.S. industry into a source of durable carbon removal.

Background

The U.S. pulp and paper industry is one of the largest industrial users of biomass for heat and
power, making it uniquely positioned to contribute to industrial decarbonization and carbon
dioxide removal (CDR). In contrast to fossil-based industries, a significant portion of CO₂
emissions from chemical pulp mills is biogenic, originating from atmospheric carbon fixed
through photosynthesis. This creates a strategic opportunity: if these emissions can be captured
and permanently stored, pulp mills can transition from net emitters to net carbon sinks. Despite
this potential, carbon capture, utilization, and sequestration (CCUS) has not been widely
deployed in this sector due to technical integration challenges, cost constraints, and limited
demonstration of commercially viable systems.

The overarching objective of this project was to design, evaluate, and de-risk an integrated
CCUS platform tailored specifically to chemical pulp mills. The project was structured to
address key technical and economic barriers through a coordinated set of research tasks spanning
process modeling, experimental validation, materials utilization, and systems analysis. Broadly,
the project pursued four primary technical goals: (1) enabling in situ CO₂ capture within the kraft
chemical recovery cycle, (2) generating high-purity CO₂ streams through oxy-fuel or electrified
lime kiln operation, (3) capturing and stabilizing CO₂ through mineralization using pulp mill
residues, and (4) developing beneficial use pathways for these residues to improve overall
process economics. These goals were implemented through a series of interdependent tasks,
progressing from fundamental modeling and laboratory-scale experimentation to field-relevant
validation and system-level evaluation, as outlined in the project work plan.

 4

From a technical perspective, one of the central challenges addressed in this project is the
integration of CO₂ capture into an already highly optimized and tightly coupled industrial
process. The kraft pulping system relies on chemical recovery loops that are sensitive to
compositional changes, temperature, and material balances. Introducing carbon capture into this
system requires approaches that do not disrupt mill operations or impose excessive energy
penalties. Prior work has explored post-combustion capture technologies, such as amine-based
systems, but these approaches are often cost-prohibitive when applied to dilute or distributed
emission streams. This project instead focused on process-integrated strategies, including
chemical pathway modification (e.g., sodium spiking) and combustion environment control (e.g.,
oxy-fuel calcination), to increase CO₂ concentration and reduce capture costs within existing unit
operations.

A second major technical issue is the lack of fundamental understanding of reaction behavior
under conditions relevant to decarbonized mill operation. In particular, lime mud calcination—
the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate to calcium oxide and CO₂—is a critical step in
the pulping cycle and a major point source of emissions. Emerging decarbonization strategies,
such as oxy-fuel combustion or electrified kilns, fundamentally alter the reaction environment by
increasing CO₂ partial pressure. However, most existing kinetic models are based on low-CO₂ or
inert atmospheres and are not directly applicable to these conditions. Addressing this gap is
essential for enabling reliable design and operation of next-generation lime kilns integrated with
carbon capture systems.

In addition to capture and sequestration, the project addressed the underutilization of alkaline
mineral residues generated during pulping operations. Materials such as dregs, grits, and lime
mud are typically treated as waste streams despite their high calcium carbonate content and
potential value. At the same time, agricultural and forestry systems rely heavily on commercially
produced lime to manage soil acidity, which carries both economic and environmental costs.
Bridging these two systems presents an opportunity to convert an industrial liability into a value-
added product, improving the overall economics of CCUS deployment while supporting
sustainable agricultural practices.

At the systems level, the project addressed a critical challenge in the emerging field of carbon
removal: the lack of standardized methodologies for quantifying net atmospheric CO₂ removal.
Life cycle assessment (LCA) is widely used to evaluate environmental performance, but results
are highly sensitive to system boundary definitions, functional units, and treatment of biogenic
carbon flows. In the context of biomass-based industries, this creates uncertainty in both
technology evaluation and participation in carbon markets. This project was designed to
explicitly examine these issues and provide a rigorous framework for assessing carbon removal
performance across multiple system definitions.

The commercial relevance of this work is grounded in the structure and geography of the U.S.
pulp and paper industry. Large chemical pulp mills are concentrated in regions with access to
biomass resources and, in many cases, proximity to suitable geological storage formations. The
industry also includes established infrastructure, centralized emission sources, and experienced
operators, making it a strong candidate for early CCUS deployment. However,
commercialization has been limited by capital cost concerns, uncertainty in long-term revenue

 5

streams (e.g., carbon credits), and lack of integrated technology demonstrations. This project was
designed to directly address these barriers by developing retrofit-compatible solutions,
identifying co-product value streams, and generating data needed to support techno-economic
analysis and investment decisions.

The planned approach combined multiple methodologies to address these challenges in a
coordinated manner. Process simulation and modeling were used to evaluate system
configurations and identify optimal operating conditions. Laboratory-scale experiments were
conducted to generate kinetic data, validate process assumptions, and characterize material
behavior under relevant conditions. Soil incubation and plant growth studies were used to assess
the agronomic potential of residue-based products. Finally, life cycle and techno-economic
analyses were applied to evaluate environmental performance and commercial feasibility. This
integrated approach ensured that the project addressed not only technical feasibility but also
scalability and real-world applicability.

The significance of this project lies in its integrated and application-driven approach to industrial
decarbonization. Rather than focusing on a single technology, the project advances a system-
level solution that combines capture, utilization, and sequestration within an existing industrial
platform. The work builds on and extends prior research in biomass carbon removal, circular
bioeconomy systems, and industrial process modeling (Dees et al., 2023; Lower et al., 2022),
while introducing new concepts for process intensification and co-product utilization. By
addressing both technical and economic barriers, the project provides a pathway toward
commercialization that aligns with the needs of industry stakeholders.

If successfully deployed at scale, the technologies investigated in this project have the potential
to deliver significant environmental and economic benefits. These include substantial reductions
in net CO₂ emissions, new revenue streams from carbon removal and co-products, reduced
reliance on mined agricultural inputs, and strengthened competitiveness of the U.S. pulp and
paper sector. More broadly, the project contributes to national goals for industrial
decarbonization, carbon removal, and sustainable resource utilization.

Results and Discussion

The project made substantial progress toward its central objective of demonstrating the technical
and economic feasibility of integrating carbon capture, utilization, and sequestration (CCUS)
within chemical pulp mills. Across process modeling, laboratory experimentation, and system-
level analysis, the realized results were consistent with, and in several cases exceeded,
anticipated outcomes. These results are documented in detail across the project’s peer-reviewed
publications, which include comprehensive data visualizations, sensitivity analyses, and model
outputs that support the conclusions summarized here.

At the systems level, the project quantified the carbon removal potential of pulp mills retrofitted
with carbon capture systems through a comprehensive life cycle assessment (LCA), as reported
in Carrejo et al. (2026) . This study represents the first rigorous evaluation of carbon removal in
the pulp and paper sector across multiple system boundaries and accounting approaches. Results
show that retrofitted mills can transition from net emitters to net carbon sinks, with carbon

 6

removal efficiencies ranging from 12% to 92% depending on system boundary definition. Under
narrow, gate-to-gate boundaries that isolate the carbon capture unit, removal efficiencies reached
as high as 92%, while broader cradle-to-grave boundaries yielded values between 17% and 46%
under static assumptions and approximately 12% under dynamic accounting of biogenic carbon
flows.

These results align with anticipated outcomes at the proposal stage but also reveal an important
and previously unresolved issue: the strong dependence of carbon removal performance on
system boundary selection. The detailed figures, system diagrams, and sensitivity analyses
provided in Carrejo et al. (2026) illustrate how emissions from biomass production, mill
operations, and product end-of-life influence net removal outcomes. These visualizations provide
a transparent framework for comparing methodologies and are particularly relevant for emerging
carbon credit protocols and monitoring, reporting, and verification (MRV) systems.

At the process level, the project evaluated the feasibility of enhancing in situ CO₂ capture
through sodium spiking in combination with oxy-fuel calcination in the lime kiln, as detailed in
Carrejo et al. (2024) . Process simulation results demonstrate that increasing sodium loading
within the kraft cycle leads to a proportional increase in CO₂ mineralization, with each 1%
increase in sodium resulting in approximately a 4% increase in mineralized CO₂. Within system
constraints, sodium loading could be increased by up to approximately 9%, representing a
meaningful intensification of CO₂ generation in the recovery cycle.

These process improvements translated directly into reductions in CO₂ capture cost. Baseline
capture costs were estimated at $131 per tonne under air-fired conditions and $107 per tonne
under oxy-fuel operation. With the implementation of sodium spiking, costs decreased to $96
and $74 per tonne, corresponding to reductions of 27% and 31%, respectively. The full set of
process flow diagrams, cost breakdowns, and sensitivity analyses can be found in Carrejo et al.
(2024), where the interaction between sodium loading, kiln operation, and CO₂ purity is
visualized in detail. These results meet or exceed the techno-economic performance targets
established at the outset of the project and confirm the feasibility of integrating these approaches
into existing mill infrastructure.

A key technical uncertainty addressed in the project was the behavior of lime mud calcination
under high CO₂ environments, which are characteristic of oxy-fuel or electrified kiln operation.
Experimental results from Wu et al. (2024) provide the first comprehensive kinetic assessment of
real pulp mill-derived lime mud under these conditions. Across a temperature range of 550–
1250°C and CO₂ concentrations from 0% to 90%, results show that increasing CO₂ partial
pressure significantly increases the apparent activation energy and narrows the temperature
window for effective calcination. These findings confirm the anticipated challenge that high-
purity CO₂ environments, while beneficial for capture, introduce constraints on reaction kinetics.

To address this, a predictive kinetic model was developed that accurately captures calcination
rates as a function of temperature and CO₂ concentration. The model and its validation are
presented in Wu et al. (2024), including detailed plots of reaction rate versus temperature and
CO₂ partial pressure. Additional experimental results demonstrate that sodium content in lime
mud does not significantly affect calcination kinetics under high CO₂ conditions, and that

 7

particle size variation has minimal impact. These findings simplify reactor design considerations
and reduce uncertainty in scaling these systems to industrial operation.

In parallel, the project evaluated a utilization pathway for alkaline residues generated during
pulping operations, specifically dregs and grits, as a replacement for agricultural lime. As
reported in Woods et al. (2026) , soil incubation experiments conducted across 22 soils
representative of the southeastern United States showed that these residues provide equivalent
pH adjustment performance to commercial lime over a 120-day period. The experimental results,
including soil pH response curves and carbon content analyses, are presented in detail in the
publication and demonstrate consistent performance across a wide range of soil types.

From an environmental and economic perspective, the use of dregs and grits offers substantial
benefits. The production and application of these materials resulted in emissions of
approximately 0.35 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne, compared to 0.86 tonnes for conventional
agricultural lime, representing an emissions reduction of approximately 60%. The life cycle
assessment and techno-economic analysis presented in Woods et al. (2026) include detailed
comparisons of emissions, costs, and transport considerations. In addition, soils treated with
these residues showed statistically significant increases in soil carbon, suggesting potential for
additional downstream carbon sequestration. These results support the viability of this pathway
as both a utilization strategy and a mechanism for improving overall system economics.

Across all tasks, progress against project milestones was strong and consistent with the original
work plan. Modeling efforts defined feasible operating conditions and performance targets,
experimental work validated key reaction mechanisms and material properties, and system-level
analyses integrated these findings into a coherent evaluation of environmental and economic
performance. The consistency of results across independent methodologies increases confidence
in the findings and demonstrates the robustness of the integrated approach.

Several challenges were encountered during the project, including delays in equipment
commissioning and personnel onboarding, as well as the need to expand experimental scope to
address gaps in the literature, particularly in the area of high-CO₂ calcination kinetics. These
challenges were addressed through approved timeline extensions and adjustments to the research
plan. Importantly, these deviations did not negatively impact the overall outcomes and, in several
cases, strengthened the project by enabling more comprehensive data collection and analysis.

Overall, the results demonstrate that integrating carbon capture, utilization, and sequestration
within chemical pulp mills is both technically feasible and economically promising. The
combination of process intensification strategies, experimentally validated kinetic models, and
value-added utilization pathways provides a credible pathway toward commercial deployment.
The supporting publications contain detailed figures, process diagrams, and quantitative analyses
that substantiate these findings and provide a resource for future research, technology
development, and implementation efforts.

Significant Outcomes

This project delivered several significant outcomes that directly advance both the science and
commercialization of carbon capture, utilization, and sequestration (CCUS) in the pulp and paper
industry.

A key accomplishment was demonstrating that pulp mills can function as net carbon removal
systems. The life cycle assessment work established a quantitative framework for evaluating this
potential across system boundaries, with results now informing carbon accounting approaches
and registry development. This is significant because it moves the industry from conceptual
potential to defensible, quantified carbon removal.

At the process level, the project identified sodium spiking combined with oxy-fuel calcination as
a viable pathway to reduce CO₂ capture costs and increase capture rates. This outcome is
important because it leverages existing mill infrastructure and provides a realistic pathway
toward deployment rather than requiring greenfield systems.

The project also produced the first kinetic model of lime mud calcination under high CO₂
conditions using real industrial materials, addressing a major knowledge gap that previously
limited kiln decarbonization strategies. This enables more reliable process design and reduces
technical risk for industry adoption.

A significant co-product outcome was demonstrating that pulp mill residues (dregs and grits) can
serve as a drop-in replacement for commercial lime, with no significant difference in cotton or
pine growth response . This creates a viable utilization pathway that reduces emissions, avoids
landfill disposal, and improves overall project economics.

The project generated strong research and dissemination outputs, including six peer-reviewed
publications, two additional articles currently in preparation, two press releases, and more than
ten presentations to academic, industry, and policy audiences. Importantly, the work contributed
to a commercial developer securing funding to deploy carbon capture at U.S. pulp mills,
indicating direct translation of research into practice.

Challenges included delays in project startup, equipment commissioning, and field sampling
approvals, which required no-cost extensions. In addition, limited prior data on high-CO₂
calcination required expansion of experimental scope. These challenges did not reduce project
impact and instead led to more comprehensive datasets and stronger conclusions.

A key lesson learned is that system boundary assumptions in carbon accounting can significantly
influence perceived performance, reinforcing the need for standardized methodologies. Overall,
the project successfully met its objectives and produced outcomes with clear technical
significance and demonstrated relevance to commercialization.

Commercialization and Path Forward

This project established a clear pathway toward commercialization of integrated carbon capture,
utilization, and sequestration (CCUS) technologies in the pulp and paper industry. The approach
is intentionally designed around retrofit-compatible solutions that leverage existing mill
infrastructure, minimizing capital intensity and enabling near-term deployment.

The core value proposition is the transformation of chemical pulp mills from carbon emitters into
revenue-generating carbon removal assets. This is achieved through three integrated
components: (1) low-cost capture of high-purity biogenic CO₂ via oxy-fuel or electrified lime
kilns, (2) process intensification through sodium spiking to increase CO₂ availability, and (3)
utilization of alkaline residues as mineral carbonate fertilizers. Together, these create multiple
revenue streams, including carbon removal credits, avoided disposal costs, and fertilizer co-
product sales.

The primary target market is the U.S. pulp and paper industry, particularly large chemical pulp
mills in the southeastern United States where biomass resources, industrial infrastructure, and
access to geological storage are co-located. Secondary markets include agriculture and forestry
sectors that can utilize pulp mill-derived alkaline residues as a substitute for commercial lime.
The emergence of voluntary carbon markets and long-term offtake agreements from corporate
buyers provides an additional and growing demand signal for high-quality carbon removal
credits.

The business model is centered on integrating carbon capture systems at existing mills and
monetizing captured CO₂ through a combination of sequestration credits and co-product
utilization. In practice, this is expected to be executed through partnerships between pulp
producers, carbon project developers, and technology providers. Revenue streams are anticipated
from carbon credit sales, tipping fee avoidance, and fertilizer product distribution, with cost
reductions achieved through process integration and infrastructure reuse.

The go-to-market strategy focuses on early deployment through industrial partnerships. A carbon
removal project developer is already advancing commercial-scale carbon capture at pulp mills in
the southeastern United States and has cited this work as foundational to their approach.
Engagement with industry stakeholders, including pulp producers and equipment manufacturers,
has helped align the technology with operational realities and accelerated interest in pilot and
commercial deployment.

Key milestones for commercialization include: (1) pilot-scale demonstration of integrated oxy-
fuel or electrified lime kiln operation with CO₂ capture, (2) validation of long-term system
performance and reliability under industrial conditions, (3) development of monitoring,
reporting, and verification (MRV) protocols aligned with carbon markets, and (4) execution of
full-scale retrofit projects. Achieving these milestones will require continued collaboration with
industry and access to additional funding, including a combination of federal grants, private
equity, and project finance tied to carbon offtake agreements.

 10

The project advanced the technology’s Adoption Readiness Level (ARL) from approximately
ARL 2–3 at project initiation—characterized by early-stage concept validation and limited
system integration—to ARL 5–6 at project completion, where integrated system performance has
been demonstrated through modeling, laboratory validation, and field-relevant data. DOE
funding was critical in enabling this progression by supporting experimental validation, process
modeling, and system-level analysis that collectively reduced technical and economic
uncertainty.

Several commercialization barriers remain. Technical challenges include scaling oxy-fuel or
electrified kiln systems and integrating them without disrupting mill operations. Market barriers
include uncertainty in carbon pricing and evolving standards for carbon removal accounting.
Regulatory considerations include permitting for CO₂ transport and geological storage, as well as
approval pathways for land application of industrial residues. Financial barriers include the
upfront capital required for retrofit projects.

Strategies to address these barriers include continued pilot-scale demonstration, development of
standardized MRV frameworks informed by this project’s LCA work, leveraging existing
industrial infrastructure to reduce capital costs, and forming partnerships with carbon credit
buyers to secure long-term revenue streams. Engagement with policymakers and industry groups
will also be important to align regulatory frameworks with emerging carbon removal
technologies.

Future research and development efforts will focus on pilot-scale validation of integrated
systems, further refinement of techno-economic models, and expanded field trials for residue-
based fertilizer applications. Additional work is also needed to evaluate long-term performance
of CO₂ storage and to optimize system integration under real operating conditions.

Overall, this project has significantly advanced the readiness of CCUS technologies for
deployment in the pulp and paper sector and has contributed to early-stage commercialization
efforts. The combination of strong technical validation, clear economic pathways, and active
industry engagement positions this technology for continued development and near-term
implementation.

Conclusions

This project demonstrates that chemical pulp mills represent a viable and scalable platform for
integrating carbon capture, utilization, and sequestration, with the potential to transition from net
emitters to net carbon removal systems. The findings show that process-integrated approaches—
particularly those leveraging existing infrastructure such as the lime kiln and chemical recovery
cycle—can achieve meaningful reductions in CO₂ capture cost while maintaining compatibility
with industrial operations.

A key technical conclusion is that coupling sodium spiking with oxy-fuel or high-purity
calcination pathways provides a practical mechanism to increase CO₂ availability and reduce
capture costs, improving the overall feasibility of deployment. In parallel, resolving the kinetics
of lime mud calcination under high CO₂ conditions addresses a critical barrier to scaling these

 11

systems and enables more reliable design of next-generation kilns. At the systems level, the work
establishes that carbon removal performance is highly sensitive to system boundary assumptions,
reinforcing the need for transparent and standardized accounting methodologies.

The project also demonstrates that integrating utilization pathways alongside capture and storage
significantly strengthens the economic case. The use of pulp mill residues as a substitute for
agricultural lime provides a viable co-product that reduces emissions, avoids waste disposal, and
creates an additional revenue stream. This coupling of industrial decarbonization with
agricultural benefit represents a broader systems-level advantage that extends beyond the mill
boundary.

From a commercialization perspective, the results support a deployment model centered on
retrofitting existing mills, leveraging co-located infrastructure, and monetizing carbon removal
through emerging markets. Early industry engagement and alignment with commercial project
developers indicate that the technology is moving beyond conceptual feasibility toward
implementation. The combination of cost reductions, multiple revenue streams, and
compatibility with existing assets positions this approach as a competitive option within the
broader carbon removal landscape.

Several important lessons emerged from the project. First, integration challenges—not individual
technologies—are the dominant factor in determining feasibility, emphasizing the importance of
system-level design. Second, uncertainties in carbon accounting can materially affect perceived
performance and investment decisions, highlighting the need for consistent frameworks. Third,
coupling capture with utilization can play a critical role in improving project economics and
adoption potential.

Overall, the project advances both the scientific understanding and practical implementation of
carbon removal in biomass-based industries. It provides a clear pathway for scaling CCUS in the
pulp and paper sector and establishes a foundation for future deployment, while also identifying
key considerations that will shape continued development and commercialization.

 Quantifying Atmospheric Carbon Removal at Pulp and Paper Mills:
 A Life Cycle Assessment Across System Boundaries

Edgar Carrejo a, Rhonald Ortega a, Kai Lan a, Daniel L. Sanchez c, Sunkyu Park a, William Joe
Sagues b
a
  Department of Forest Biomaterials, North Carolina State University, 2820 Faucette Dr., Raleigh,
NC 27695, USA
b
  Department of Biological & Agricultural Engineering, North Carolina State University, 3110

c
  Department of Environmental Science, Policy, and Management, University of California-
Berkeley, 130 Mulford Hall #3114, Berkeley, CA 94720, USA
*
 Corresponding authors: sunkyu_park@ncsu.edu (Sunkyu Park), wjsagues@ncsu.edu (William
Joe Sagues).

See DOI link for full article: https://doi.org/10.1007/s43979-025-00156-5

Abstract

The pulp and paper industry is a promising yet underexplored platform for large-scale carbon
dioxide removal (CDR) due to its use of biogenic feedstocks and production of concentrated CO2
emissions from point sources. This study presents the first comprehensive life cycle assessment
(LCA) of retrofitting an amine-based carbon capture and storage (CCS) system into a
representative virgin kraft pulp and paper mill in the Southeastern U.S. We evaluate carbon
removal across five system configurations, applying both static and dynamic LCA methods under
multiple functional units: CO2 captured, biomass input, and paper output. Results show that CCS
retrofits can convert a conventional mill from a net emitter into a net carbon sink, with total
removal efficiencies from 17% to 92% (metric tonnes of CO2 removed per metric tonne of CO2
available for removal under selected boundary conditions). When carbon removal is normalized
to the quantity of biogenic CO2 captured—a narrow, gate-to-gate system boundary that considers
only CCS facility emissions—removal efficiencies reached as high as 92%. The use of such narrow
boundaries aligns with precedents in traditional LCA methodology, where gate-to-gate
assessments are commonly applied to isolate process-level performance and allocate emissions
accordingly, providing a consistent basis for comparison across technologies. Under broader
cradle-to-grave boundaries—which begin tracking carbon at the point of its physical removal from
the atmosphere via photosynthesis in the forest, and extend to include upstream forest operations,
mill-wide emissions, and downstream product decomposition—efficiencies declined, ranging
from 17% to 46% under static assumptions and dropping to 12% when accounting for dynamic
biogenic carbon fluxes over time. These results underscore how system boundary definitions
influence reported outcomes, while also illustrating the complementary roles of narrow and broad
perspectives for different decision-making contexts.

 Biogenic Carbon Capture at Pulp Mills via Sodium Spiking
 and Oxy-Fuel Calcination

Edgar Carrejo a, Hasan Jameel a, Sunkyu Park a,*, William Joe Sagues b,*
a
  Department of Forest Biomaterials, North Carolina State University, 2820 Faucette Dr., Raleigh,
NC 27695, USA
b
  Department of Biological & Agricultural Engineering, North Carolina State University, 3110

*Corresponding authors: sunkyu_park@ncsu.edu (Sunkyu Park), wjsagues@ncsu.edu (William
Joe Sagues).

See DOI link for full article: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijggc.2025.104409

ABSTRACT
Over 13 million metric tons of biogenic carbon dioxide (CO2) are mineralized yearly in United
States (US) pulp mill recovery boilers as molten sodium carbonate. The mineralized CO2 is
released downstream in the rotary lime kiln where it can be captured at a relatively low cost for
permanent sequestration. The use of biocarbon and bioenergy at pulp mills enables the possibility
for atmospheric carbon removal when biogenic CO2 is captured and sequestered. We demonstrate
the feasibility of capturing biogenic CO2 via sodium spiking coupled with oxy-fuel calcination in
the rotary lime kiln at existing kraft pulp mills. Sodium (Na) spiking elevates the Na ion loading
in the kraft chemical looping process by replacing chlorine-based bleaching with a highly alkaline
bleaching sequence. Chemical pulping processes are modeled and simulated to understand the
technical limits of implementing sodium spiking in existing pulp mills. Each 1% of sodium added
to the bleaching operations increases the rate of CO2 mineralization by 4%, and the maximum
increase in sodium content in the kraft process is 9%. Without sodium spiking, estimated CO2
capture costs are $131 and $107/mt CO2 for air and oxy-fuel combustion, respectively. With the
implementation of sodium spiking, the cost of CO2 captured decreases by 27% and 31%, showing
costs of $96 and $74/mt CO2 for air and oxy-fuel combustion, respectively. Oxy-fuel combustion
reduces the costs of CO2 capture compared to air combustion, but merged deployment with sodium
spiking seems to be the most cost-effective pathway to integrate carbon capture.

 Kinetic Assessment of Pulp Mill-Derived Lime Mud Calcination in High CO2 Atmosphere
Ruochen Wua,b, Edgar Carrejoc, Md Sumon Rezaa, Ethan Woodsa, Seyedamin Razavib, Sunkyu
Parkc, Fanxing Lib,, William Joe Saguesa*
a
  Department of Biological & Agricultural Engineering, North Carolina State University, 3110

b
  Department of Chemical and Biomolecular Engineering, North Carolina State University, 911
Partners Way, Raleigh, NC 27695, USA
c
  Department of Forest Biomaterials, North Carolina State University, 2820 Faucette Dr., Raleigh,
NC 27695, USA
Corresponding author: wjsagues@ncsu.edu (William Joe Sagues)

See DOI link for full article: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fuel.2024.132372

Abstract
The chemical pulping of biomass involves the recycling of calcium through the calcination of lime
mud, which is mostly comprised of calcium carbonate (CaCO3). Lime mud decomposes under
elevated temperatures to generate calcium oxide (CaO) and carbon dioxide (CO2), the kinetics of
which are strongly influenced by the CO2 partial pressure and temperature. Oxy-fuel combustion
and electrified lime kilns for lime mud calcination are intriguing methods to decarbonize this
highly polluting operation within the biomass pulping industry. However, the high CO2
concentration in oxy-fuel and electrified calcination processes alters the kinetics and overall
reactivity of lime mud. For the first time, a model-fitting method is used to determine the kinetic
parameters for lime mud calcination under a wide range of temperatures (550°C -1250°C) and
under different concentrations of CO2 (0%, 15%, 50%, and 90%). A kinetic model is developed
that accurately predicts the reaction rates as a function of temperature and CO2 concentration. The
apparent activation of energy for lime mud calcination is elevated under a high CO2 environment.
Relative to inert gas (N2, Ar), the temperature window for calcination is much smaller under high
CO2 environments. The presence of Na in lime mud does not seem to affect calcination under a
high CO2 environment. Finally, particle size variation does not have a significant effect on
calcination under a high CO2 environment.

Alkaline Mineral Residues from Pulp Mills as a Sustainable and Economical Alternative to
 Lime Fertilizers
  Ethan Woodsa, Andrew Trlicab, Perry Berlina, Sean Blosziesb, Alex Woodleyc, Rachel Cookb,
 and Joe Saguesa*
a
  Department of Biological and Agricultural Engineering, North Carolina State University, 3110

b
  Department of Forestry and Environmental Resources, North Carolina State University, 2820

c
  Department of Crop and Soil Sciences, North Carolina State University, 101 Derieux Pl.,
Raleigh, NC 27695, USA
*Corresponding author

See DOI link for full article: https://doi.org/10.1039/D5SU00814J

Abstract
Lime is typically comprised of calcium carbonate (CaCO3) or calcium magnesium carbonate
(CaMg(CO3)2) and is needed to maintain proper pH in agriculture and forestry soils, but it
represents a major cost to growers and results in significant greenhouse gas emissions due to the
mining, crushing, and transport required. There is a need to identify existing sources of alkaline
mineral wastes and assess their potential as sustainable and cost-effective alternatives to lime.
Dregs and grits (DGs) are highly alkaline mineral residues from biomass pulp mills that form
during the recovery of pulping chemicals and are mostly comprised of CaCO3. For the first time,
we assess the efficacy of industrially sourced DGs on 22 acidic soils across the southeastern U.S
that are currently used in either agriculture or forestry operations. The application of DGs was
found on average to result in the same soil pH response as calcite over multiple soil incubations
(0-day – 120-day). An analysis of the carbon content of the soils after the incubation revealed
that soils incubated with DGs experienced a statistically significant increase in soil carbon
relative to control soils and those incubated with CaCO3. The generation and application of DGs
emits 0.35 tonnes of CO2 for every tonne DGs which compares favorably to the 0.86 tonnes of
CO2 for every tonne of agricultural lime generated and $85/tonne lime reported in the literature.
DGs prove to be a sustainable and economical substitute for agricultural lime in the acidic soils
of the southeastern United States.

EFFECT OF MINERAL CARBONATE FERTILIZER CO-PRODUCT ON LOBLOLLY
 PINE SEEDLINGS GROWTH

ABSTRACT
The predominant soils in the southeast of the U.S. are highly acidic. Lime application is a common
way to address this issue, but it can be costly for farmers. There are other alternatives, more costly
and efficient to increase the soil pH, such as pulp mill residues, called dregs or grits. However,
little is known about their effect on loblolly pine growth. The experiment was carried out with
three soil types: Alfisol, Spodosol, and Ultisol, and three fertilization treatments: control (no
application of alkaline products), application of dregs and grits, and application of lime. The results
showed soil type-dependent responses to DG applications. Seedlings growing in Spodosol, the
most acidic soil, responded positively to DG application. In contrast, those in Ultisol showed a
significant reduction in dry root biomass under the same treatment. These results suggest that pulp
mill residues may be a viable alternative for correcting soil acidity in highly acidic soils, although
responses may vary by soil type. Across treatments, pine growth under dregs and grits was not
significantly different from growth under commercial lime, indicating that dregs and grits may
serve as a viable alternative source of lime.

 EFFECT OF MINERAL CARBONATE FERTILIZER CO-PRODUCT ON
 COMMERCIAL COTTON GROWTH

Abstract
Cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) is one of the most important fiber crops around the world. Lime
application is a common practice to correct soil acidity and meet the pH requirement for optimal
cotton production. However, frequent lime applications may increase production costs and reduce
crop profitability. Alternative materials capable of increasing the pH have been studied, such as
pulp mill residues, known as dregs and grits, but little is known about their effect on cotton growth.
The experiment was conducted using two soil series (Orangeburg and Tifton), two varieties
(NexGen 3195 B3XF and PYH-443), and three fertilization treatments: control (no alkaline
application), application of dregs and grits (DG), and application of lime. Results showed that soil
type and cotton variety significantly affected dry shoot and dry root biomass, whereas DG and
lime applications did not show significant differences. These findings suggest that pulp mill
residues may be a viable alternative for soil applications with medium-slight acidity, although
responses may vary by soil type and variety. Across treatments, cotton growth under dregs and
grits was not significantly different from growth under commercial lime, indicating that dregs and
grits may serve as a viable alternative source of lime.
