Use of volumetric analysis to predict pathologic response in esophageal cancer: a single-institution cohort study
Authors: Eric Klipsch, Saylor Hardin, Kylie Duckworth, Kelly Sokevitz, Jeffrey Rodgers, David Mann, Barry C Gibney, Kathryn E Engelhardt, Dhiraj Baruah, Ian C Bostock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPathologic complete response (pCR) after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CRT) is associated with improved survival in esophageal cancer. We used volumetric analysis of hypermetabolic esophageal tissue to assess if a decrease in volume following CRT was associated with pCR in esophageal cancer patients who underwent neoadjuvant therapy and esophagectomy. We aimed to determine if a decrease in the hypermetabolic esophageal volume could be a potential marker for better response to neoadjuvant CRT.MethodsPatients who received CRT followed by esophagectomy between 2013 and 2023 at a single institution were retrospectively reviewed. Patients were grouped by pathologic response grade. The hypermetabolic tumor volume, tumor size, and maximum standardized uptake value (mSUV) pre- and post-CRT were compared.ResultsOf 309 patients, 69 had positron emission tomography scans available. Most patients were White (88.4%), but Black patients were more likely to have pCR on univariate analysis (50% vs. 24.6%, P=0.04). Similarly, most patients had adenocarcinoma (81.2%), but pCR was more likely in squamous cell carcinoma (72.7% vs. 17.9%, P=0.002). The pCR group had a significantly larger decrease in tumor size (5.6 vs. 3.2 vs. 2.9 vs. 2.8 cm, P=0.01) and esophageal volume (34.1 vs. 8.0 vs. 29.7 vs. 27.1 cm3, P=0.02) compared to the grade 1, grade 2, and grade 3 response groups, respectively. On multivariable logistic regression, change in tumor size (odds ratio 0.67, 95% confidence interval: 0.52–0.88, P=0.003) was a significant predictor of better pathologic response, with every 1 cm decrease in tumor size representing a 33% increase in the odds of pCR, while change in esophageal volume was not.ConclusionsPatients with pCR had a significantly larger decrease in tumor size and esophageal volume compared to other grade responders on univariate analysis, but on multivariable analysis only change in tumor size remained as an independent predictor of pCR. In this limited cohort, these findings encourage further investigation of volumetric analysis as a tool to predict pathologic response and indicate that it may potentially be a useful adjunct when counseling high-risk surgical patients deciding between resection and active surveillance.Keywords: Esophageal cancer (EC), pathologic complete response (pCR), volumetric analysis

Highlight box

Key findings

• The present study found that patients who experienced pathologic complete response (pCR) had a significantly greater decrease in the volume of positron emission tomography (PET) avid esophageal tissue following chemoradiotherapy (CRT) compared to other grade responders on univariate analysis.

What is known and what is new?

• Prior studies have assessed the use of radiographic measurements to predict pCR in esophageal cancer. Namely, a decrease in standardized uptake value (SUV) on PET scans following CRT has been shown to be a useful metric to help determine treatment response. The use of volumetric analysis has not been shown to be predictive of pCR in esophageal cancer, but the literature is limited.

• The present study contains the largest cohort to date that includes both squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma histologic subtypes, and it is the first to demonstrate an association between pCR following CRT and a decrease in PET-avid esophageal tissue.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The findings of this study, although underpowered due to low cohort size, demonstrate that the use of volumetric analysis may potentially aid in predicting the extent of pathologic response in esophageal cancer patients following CRT. This tool may be useful when counseling high-risk surgical patients who are deciding between operative intervention and active surveillance. Although limited by sample size, these results encourage further investigation of volumetric analysis in esophageal cancer.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) is the sixth deadliest cancer worldwide, accounting for 5.3% of cancer deaths, and current treatment paradigms include the use of chemoradiation followed by surgery (1,2). However, esophagectomies are associated with significant morbidity and mortality, and the risk is substantially increased in older patients with comorbid conditions and poor performance statuses (3). For this reason, high-risk surgical patients may be more suitable for active surveillance. There are ongoing trials that are investigating the use of active surveillance in patients with EC who have had a clinically complete response on post-treatment biopsies to evaluate if it is an acceptable treatment strategy (4). To this end, the use of radiographic changes following chemoradiation may aid in predicting a patient’s response to therapy.

The degree of pathologic response observed in post-esophagectomy specimens is directly associated with overall and disease-free survival (5). Prior research has examined the utility of radiographic measurements from positron emission tomography (PET) scans as indicators of pathologic response for the identification of treatment response across several types of solid organ tumors (6-21). Metrics such as maximum standardized uptake values (mSUV) and hypermetabolic tumor volume (HTV) have been used to evaluate pre-surgical treatment responses in patients undergoing neoadjuvant therapy (6-21). Increased HTV was found to be an independent predictor of incomplete response in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma and rectal carcinoma. It was even statistically superior to mSUV in predicting pathologic response (6-9). In EC, multiple previous studies have demonstrated the utility of mSUV to predict pathologic response and overall survival (10-14). Additionally, there are data that suggests HTV measured prior to neoadjuvant therapy may be a useful predictor of overall survival following esophagectomy (15,16). Furthermore, a recent study has shown that lower HTV prior to chemoradiotherapy (CRT) is associated with improved response seen on post-CRT PET scans in EC patients (17). However, a previous study of EC did not find HTV to be a useful radiographic measurement to predict pathologic complete response (pCR), determined by surgical pathology, following CRT, but the study only included squamous cell histology and had a limited sample size (18). At this time, the current literature is inconclusive, but these prior studies encourage further investigation of HTV as a predictor of pCR following esophagectomy.

Due to the high morbidity and mortality associated with esophagectomy, high-risk patients may benefit from active surveillance if they have pCR (4,5). We have performed the largest known analysis of the use of HTV in EC to date with hopes of demonstrating its feasibility and utility in the prediction of pCR following CRT. We grouped patients by grade of pathologic response as determined by final postoperative pathology report and retrospectively performed volumetric analysis of PET scans pre- and post-CRT to determine if there was a significant decrease in HTV following CRT in each group. The primary endpoints of the study were change in HTV, change in mSUV, and change in tumor size pre- and post-induction. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1103/rc).

Methods

Study design

We performed a retrospective cohort analysis of patients at the Medical University of South Carolina in the United States who underwent neoadjuvant CRT and esophagectomy for EC between 2013 and 2023. All patients with pre- and post-induction therapy PET scans available in the electronic medical record were included in the study. Patients received up to four cycles of CRT prior to obtaining a restaging PET scan. Patients were not excluded if they did not receive all four cycles of induction therapy. Patients were grouped based on the degree of pathologic response. Pathologic response was categorized as pCR, grade 1 response, grade 2 response, and grade 3 response based on the criteria seen in Table 1 as determined by an attending pathologist examining the post-esophagectomy surgical specimen (22). Our outcomes of interest included radiographic measurements obtained from PET scans pre- and post-CRT, including HTV and mSUV. HTV was measured by trained research team members, and mSUV was pulled from radiology reports. Additionally, we assessed change in tumor size pre- and post-induction. Pre-CRT, esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) measurements were utilized to determine tumor size. The tumor size measured on the surgical specimen was used to determine the post-treatment tumor size. For patients who had a pCR with no residual tumor on final pathology, the tumor size was designated as 0 cm. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Medical University of South Carolina determined this retrospective study to be exempt from human subjects regulations and granted waivers of informed consent and of HIPAA authorization (protocol Pro00125994, approved February 1, 2023). All data were extracted from the institutional electronic medical record and de-identified prior to analysis; no identifiable personal information was included.

Assessment of HTV on PET scans

To determine the total volume of the hypermetabolic esophageal lesions, reviewers used the Manual Volume feature of the SECTRA IDS7 software. Under the guidance of a cardiothoracic radiologist, reviewers outlined the hypermetabolic area in the esophagus in two different planes. The imaging tool would then automatically outline the entire volume of the tumor. Manual adjustments could be made if areas with lower standardized uptake value (SUV) were included that did not correspond to the known esophageal lesion as referenced on available EGD reports (Figure 1A,1B). In these cases, all discrepancies were reviewed and resolved directly by the cardiothoracic radiologist included in this study. Measurements were made both pre- and post-CRT. Any hypermetabolic areas outside of the esophagus, such as lymph nodes, were not included in the volume measurement. Additionally, when post-CRT PET scan reports indicated resolution of hypermetabolic lesion or a significant decrease in mSUV, volume was reported as zero as determined by the cardiothoracic radiologist, which occurred in five of the patients in the cohort (Figure 2A,2B). In the case of circumferential lesions, patients were excluded from analysis when there was a large volume esophageal lumen due to limitations in the software’s ability to accurately exclude the lumen (Figure 3). Notably, reviewers were not blinded to the pathologic response of patients when performing the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to characterize demographic, clinical, surgical, and radiologic features of patients stratified by pathologic response (Grade 0 to Grade 3). Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations and compared across response groups using one-way analysis of variance. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Variables with statistically significant or near-significant associations (P<0.10) in univariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. Multivariable logistic regression was performed using the Polytomous Universal Model procedure in SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.0). Pathologic response was the dependent variable, treated as a binary outcome (Grade 0 vs. Grades 1, 2, and 3). Independent variables included histology (adenocarcinoma vs. squamous cell carcinoma), race (White vs. Black), clinical N stage (N0, N1, N2, N3), and continuous radiologic parameters: change in tumor size, change in mSUV, percent decrease in PET-avid volume, and change in esophageal volume.

Results

Out of 309 patients with EC who underwent neoadjuvant treatment and esophagectomy between 2013 and 2023, a total of 69 were included in the study. As detailed in Figure 4, of the 309 patients, 231 were excluded because their PET scans were unavailable for analysis. Of the 78 remaining patients, 4 were excluded due to the presence of circumferential esophageal lesions that precluded accurate measurements, and 5 were excluded due to post-radiation esophagitis that obscured the actual tumor dimensions. Table 2 provides a comparison of baseline demographics, clinical stage, histology, and pathologic response between the included and excluded patients. Excluded patients were younger than included patients (63.6 vs. 66.8 years, P=0.01), but all other variables were similar between the two groups. There were 19 patients in the pCR group (27.5%), 18 in the grade 1 group (26.1%), 27 in the grade 2 group (39.1%), and 5 in the grade 3 group (7.2%).

As seen in Table 5, on multivariable logistic regression analysis, comparing pCR patients to all partial responders, change in tumor size following CRT was a significant independent predictor of pCR [odds ratio (OR) 0.67, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.52–0.88, P=0.003]. This OR of less than one signifies that a greater decrease in size represents increased odds of pCR, with each 1 cm decrease (greater shrinking) corresponding to an approximately 33% increase in the odds of pCR. In contrast, change in esophageal volume was not significantly associated with pCR (OR 1.01, 95% CI: 0.99–1.03, P=0.35). Additionally, age was not associated with pCR (OR 0.97, 95% CI: 0.89–1.05, P=0.41). Notably, race and histology were removed from the logistic regression model due to the low number of Black patients and squamous cell carcinoma patients included in the cohort. When included, these variables made the model unstable.

Discussion

Esophagectomy for the treatment of EC can be associated with high morbidity and mortality in high-risk surgical patients, making an active surveillance strategy an attractive option in this patient population if they achieve pCR. The present study assessed the feasibility and utility of volumetric analysis of HTV on PET scans pre- and post-CRT to determine if it could aid in the prediction of pCR in EC, comparing it to other objective metrics such as change in tumor size and mSUV, which have been studied previously (10-14). In this cohort of 69 patients, those with pCR had significantly larger changes in tumor size and HTV following CRT as compared to those with an incomplete pathologic response. Additionally, patients with pCR had significantly smaller post-CRT tumor size and HTV measurements as compared to other grade responders. In comparison, changes in mSUV were not significantly different between groups. These findings are limited by sample size, but they demonstrate that use of radiographic measurements such as HTV could potentially aid in prediction of pCR in EC, warranting further investigation. Looking ahead, these radiographic changes could even be used together in a single algorithm to better predict response to treatment.

The results of this study show the successful application of volumetric analysis to quantify HTV utilizing imaging assessment tools. On univariate analysis, the change in HTV pre- and post-CRT was significantly greater in the pCR as compared to other incomplete responders. Although neither change in HTV nor percent decrease in HTV were found to be significant predictors of pCR on multivariable logistic regression, these results are still promising that HTV may be used as a marker for better response to CRT in EC, especially given the limited sample size. These results contrast those of a prior study on squamous cell EC, which found that HTV was not predictive of pCR; however, it also had a limited sample size with only 27 patients included in the study (18). Additionally, this current study differs from the prior in that adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma were grouped together to increase the size of the cohort. The current literature shows that these two histologies have distinct genomic compositions as well as clinical behavior (24,25). Future studies should attempt to assess the two histologies independently provided sufficient sample sizes. The findings on univariate analysis that squamous cell carcinoma patients had higher pCR rates could be due to squamous cell carcinoma’s sensitivity to CRT as compared to adenocarcinoma (26). These results further underscore the need to assess use of HTV as a predictive tool in the two histologies separately, especially given the limited number of squamous cell patients included in this study. In contrast to previous literature, the present study found that SUV, specifically use of mSUV, was not a useful adjunct in predicting pathologic response. However, these studies included larger cohorts and focused on overall and disease-free survival rather than looking at prediction of pCR (10-14). With the limited cohort size of this study, it is prone to type II errors, and although it was not statistically significant, the change in mSUV was greater in the pCR group compared to grades 1, 2, and 3. Change in tumor size was the only significant predictor of pCR on multivariable logistic regression. This metric is useful, but it can be difficult to assess tumor size accurately based on imaging studies alone, especially in post-CRT EC patients where radiographic changes may still be present despite a good response to treatment. For accurate tumor size measurements, invasive studies, such as EGD, may be required, making this metric more difficult to obtain. Of note, this study did not address the emerging use of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to assess treatment response in EC. Although the current literature is sparse, early investigation into the use of MRI for staging and monitoring disease recurrence in EC is promising due to its ability to accurately distinguish soft tissue and delineate the extent of tumor invasion (27). Future studies should include volumetric analysis by PET and MRI to determine which of the two modalities is best suited for measuring response to CRT.

Currently, several barriers exist to the widespread implementation of these tools. Firstly, obtaining PET scan images from hospitals outside of the tertiary care center’s umbrella can prove difficult at times, especially when specific imaging software is required to perform certain analyses. Secondly, current methodologies to obtain HTV require a trained cardiothoracic radiologist to manually delineate regions of interest for the assessment of metabolic volume and heterogeneity, a process that is both time-consuming and impractical for widespread clinical implementation (6,7,9,19,20). Considering the above challenges, it may be beneficial to investigate automated approaches for integrating volumetric analysis into clinical practice. Automating volumetric analysis would make HTV an easily accessible data point across hospital systems and clinical settings, much like SUV is today. In the future, HTV may prove useful as a single piece of a larger algorithm that incorporates several data points to predict pathologic response but based on the results of the present study its utility is still uncertain, and further investigation is needed to determine if HTV should be incorporated into such an algorithm. Previous studies utilizing machine-learning algorithms, known as radiomics, have demonstrated potential in predicting survival risk and stratifying prognosis for patients with non-small cell lung cancer and gastric cancer (28,29). Radiomics has even been employed to discern non-responders to CRT based on gross tumor volume and tumor regression markers (30).

There are multiple limitations of the present study. First and foremost, not all patients who underwent CRT prior to esophagectomy could be included given the absence of PET scans either pre- or post-induction, leading to a small and non-standardized sample size. As seen in Table 2, the two groups did not vary significantly except for age, but nonetheless excluding a large portion of patients within the study period subjects the findings to selection bias, limiting the generalizability of the study. Additionally, since PET scans were performed at multiple facilities using different machines, the technique used to obtain the scan may have varied from hospital to hospital. Further standardization of pre- and post-CRT PET scans and improvement in hospital image sharing would allow for larger sample sizes and help eliminate selection bias in future studies. Given the small sample size, some of the results should also be interpreted with caution. For example, the finding that Black patients were more likely to have pCR is significantly limited by the fact that there were only eight Black patients included in the analysis compared to 61 White patients. Studies with larger sample sizes and a more equally distributed cohort are needed to verify these results. The present study also grouped adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma together, but current literature shows that these two histologies behave differently and respond differently to CRT (24-26). Grouping them allowed for a larger cohort size, but in the future the use of HTV should be assessed in adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma separately, especially as the treatment algorithms of these two histologies begin to diverge. It is possible that HTV is a useful tool in one but not the other, but investigation of this question was beyond the scope of this study. In addition to small sample size, there are still limitations to the technology used in this study, namely the manual volume tool of SECTRA IDS7. To use it properly, it requires dedicated training with a cardiothoracic radiologist to identify and analyze the lesions accurately. This process can be time-consuming and represents a major limitation of its implementation. On average, it requires approximately 10 minutes to perform the analysis of each scan with some cases taking up to 20 minutes to resolve, not including time spent with the cardiothoracic radiologist to resolve any discrepancies. Additionally, there is an inherent degree of variability in the assessment, depending on the individual reviewer performing the analysis. Notably, a major limitation of this study was that there was no formal intra- or inter-reviewer analysis performed, which is in part due to the small size of the study team and the time required to perform each analysis. To address intra-reviewer variability, there was a period of training under the direction of the cardiothoracic radiologist to ensure each reviewer was competent prior to proceeding with formal analysis. To further inform the results of the volumetric analysis given the subjective nature of the manual adjustments, the authors had two reviewers analyze pre- and post-CRT PET scans in five patients, and the differences in percent decrease in HTV between the two had a range of 1–31%. Refinement and automation of this tool would make implementation of this metric more feasible in clinical practice by decreasing the amount of time required to perform each analysis as well as standardize the measurements to eliminate the concern of inter-reviewer variability, better informing the reliability of the results and the utility of this metric in patient decision-making. As mentioned previously, there are also certain lesions that are difficult to analyze, such as circumferential lesions with large, dilated esophageal lumens, and in some instances, background esophagitis following induction makes analysis of post-treatment lesions more difficult. This study also did not account for the HTV of lymph nodes in patients with clinically positive nodes, so it remains unknown if including these volumes would provide additional information regarding the utility of this metric in predicting pathologic response. Additionally, given that active surveillance is infrequently employed in EC, we did not include non-surgical patients within the study. As active surveillance becomes more common, future studies should include these patients to assess the use of HTV in this unique cohort. Lastly, in future studies, reviewers should be blinded to the pathologic response of patients prior to performing the analysis to eliminate any confirmation bias.

Conclusions

The present study shows the successful application of volumetric analysis in EC, and to date, it represents the largest analysis of HTV in EC patients who have undergone CRT followed by esophagectomy. Although limited by a small sample size, univariate analysis demonstrated that patients with pCR had significantly larger changes in tumor size and HTV pre- to post-CRT as compared to incomplete responders. Change in tumor size remained an independent predictor of pCR on multivariable logistic regression, and even though change in HTV did not, these results are still encouraging and should motivate future research into HTV as a prognostic indicator in EC patients. With larger sample sizes and further refinement and automation of volumetric analysis, HTV may prove to be a useful metric in EC, aiding in clinical decision-making for high-risk surgical candidates. Future studies should focus on systematic assessment of PET scans pre- and post-CRT to increase sample size and allow for the possibility of prospective analysis. Additionally, future research should attempt to implement automated tools to provide a streamlined, standardized method to quantify HTV, decrease the time required for analysis, and eliminate inter-reviewer variability.
