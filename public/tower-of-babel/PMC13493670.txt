Letter to the Editor Regarding “Anchored Matching-Adjusted Indirect Comparison of the Long-Term Maintenance of Efficacy of Tralokinumab and Lebrikizumab in Patients with Moderate-to-Severe Atopic Dermatitis”
Authors: Kim Rand, Kurt Gebauer, Yousef Binamer, Yung-Tsu Cho, Manny Papadimitropoulos, Chao Yang, Helena Agell, Bülent Akmaz, Lucia Seminario-Vidal
Published: 2026

Dear editor,

We read with great interest the article by Augustin et al. [1] reporting the results of an anchored matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) of tralokinumab and lebrikizumab in maintaining 16-week efficacy outcomes at week 52 for patients with moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis (AD).

The authors conducted a maintenance phase analysis (week 52 outcomes) using individual patient data from the ECZTRA 1 and 2 tralokinumab trials and aggregate data from the ADvocate 1 and 2 lebrikizumab trials, with individual patient data weighted to match ADvocate 1 and 2 baseline and week-16 characteristics. The authors concluded that tralokinumab and lebrikizumab had comparable maintenance of efficacy at 52 weeks among patients meeting response criteria at week 16.

While the intent to compare long-term efficacy between these two biologics is commendable, both the selective patient population analyzed and limitations of the anchored MAIC approach when comparing data from maintenance periods, limit the robustness of the conclusions.

The analysis examined a subset of patients who achieved week-16 responses. However, response rates differed markedly between treatments (52% for lebrikizumab vs 28% for tralokinumab) [1], creating an inherent imbalance in the populations being compared. The disparity in initial response rate is clinically meaningful because it indicates that fundamentally different proportions of the original patient cohorts qualified for maintenance therapy, potentially introducing selection bias into the long-term comparison. While the authors adjusted for measured baseline and week-16 characteristics through MAIC, this statistical approach cannot account for the differential enrichment of the maintenance populations themselves. Indeed, the tralokinumab cohort represents a more highly selected subset of "best responders", which may differ in unmeasured ways (such as treatment sensitivity) that covariate adjustment cannot address.

Anchored MAIC, when applied correctly, can compare treatments from different trials. It relies on the assumption that the treatments share a true common comparator arm, such as a placebo, that represents the same clinical condition across studies. However, in the context of the maintenance-phase analysis performed by Augustin et al. [1], this assumption does not hold.

The ECZTRA 1 and 2 [2] and ADvocate 1 and 2 [3] trials included a 16-week induction phase followed by a 36-week maintenance phase. The withdrawal arms of both trials comprised patients who were week-16 per-protocol responders and were re-randomized to placebo during maintenance. Patients re-randomized to placebo had thus already received 16 weeks of active tralokinumab (ECZTRA) or lebrikizumab (ADvocate) treatment before treatment withdrawal. Because patients in the withdrawal arms received different treatments for 16 weeks, the withdrawal arms cannot be considered comparable.

Tralokinumab and lebrikizumab target the same cytokine, interleukin (IL)-13, but differ in their mechanisms of action, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics. Lebrikizumab binds to the IL-13 cytokine at an epitope that overlaps with the binding site of the IL-4Rα subunit, and tralokinumab binds to the IL-13 cytokine at an epitope that overlaps with the binding site of the IL-13Rα1/α2 subunit [4, 5]. Since lebrikizumab binds to IL-13 at an epitope that does not interact with the IL-13Rα1 and IL-13Rα2 receptor, lebrikizumab-bound IL-13 can still bind to the IL-13Rα2 receptor (decoy receptor). However, tralokinumab-bound IL-13, is not able to bind to IL-13Rα2 [5]. IL-13Rα2 has an extremely high binding affinity for IL-13 and plays a role in the natural clearance of IL-13 [6]. The binding affinity of lebrikizumab for IL-13 is approximately 140-fold higher than the binding affinity of tralokinumab for IL-13 [7], and its dissociation rate is slower [7]. These key mechanistic differences should be considered when evaluating comparative data between the two agents.

Lebrikizumab has a mean half-life of 24.5 days and an absolute bioavailability of 86% [8]; tralokinumab has a mean half-life of approximately 21 days and an absolute bioavailability of 76% [9]. The lower absolute bioavailability of tralokinumab indicates that a smaller proportion of the administered dose reaches systemic circulation, which may result in slower drug accumulation and greater inter-patient variability in exposure. While exposure to monoclonal antibodies is known to be body weight-dependent, differences in pharmacology and/or pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic relationships between tralokinumab and lebrikizumab may manifest in a more sensitive body-weight effect for tralokinumab that ultimately requires a different dosing regimen to maintain adequate exposures for people above a given body weight threshold (≥ 100 kg) [9].

Furthermore, when approved dosing regimens are followed, lebrikizumab reaches steady-state concentrations by week 4 [8], whereas tralokinumab achieves steady-state concentrations around week 16 [9]. The differences in both time to steady state and absolute bioavailability may contribute to earlier clinical responses and faster stabilization of disease activity in patients treated with lebrikizumab.

The combination of a 140-fold greater binding affinity, slower dissociation rate, higher absolute bioavailability, and faster achievement of steady-state concentrations may result in a prolonged pharmacodynamic activity for lebrikizumab and explain its off-drug treatment effect compared to tralokinumab. Thus, making withdrawal arms not suitable as common comparators.

Indeed, the maintenance outcomes (non-comparative raw data) reported for the withdrawal arms of the ECZTRA and ADvocate trials demonstrate clear differences in off-drug durability. After week-16 treatment withdrawal, 26% of tralokinumab-treated week-16 responders maintained a 75% reduction in the Eczema Area Severity Index (EASI 75), and 34% maintained an Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) of 0 or 1 at week 52, based on non-responder imputation analysis [10]. For lebrikizumab, non-responder imputation data from pooled ADvocate 1 and 2 studies show that 56% of week-16 responders maintained EASI 75, and 40% maintained IGA 0/1 at week 52 [3], suggesting longer off-drug durability of responses for lebrikizumab than tralokinumab. Notably, the proportion of lebrikizumab-treated responders who maintained EASI 75 after treatment withdrawal was more than double that observed with tralokinumab. For lebrikizumab, a subgroup analysis in withdrawal arm patients on monotherapy with stable 90% reduction in the EASI (EASI 90) response for 36 weeks (between week 16 and 52) showed negligible lebrikizumab serum concentrations, supporting long-term off-drug durability of deep responses but also suggesting that drug-free remission with lebrikizumab is possible [11].

Using an anchored MAIC to compare studies with withdrawal arms such as these penalizes the drug with a longer off-drug effect even if both drugs have equivalent on-drug effects. Specifically, the difference between the active treatment and withdrawal arms for lebrikizumab will be smaller than that for tralokinumab, making lebrikizumab seem less effective than tralokinumab. This creates a methodological paradox: the greater durability of response after lebrikizumab withdrawal—an inherently favorable treatment attribute—results in smaller differences between its active and withdrawal arms. Thus, when the withdrawal arm is incorrectly used as a common comparator, the analysis paradoxically suggests lower maintenance of response for lebrikizumab, despite its stronger off-drug durability. Therefore, the use of the withdrawal arms as a common comparator renders an anchored MAIC inappropriate for this between-trial comparison.

When a true common comparator is unavailable, a population-adjusted unanchored comparison is generally considered to be a more suitable approach for cross-trial comparisons [12–14]. An unanchored MAIC re-weights individual patient data from the active treatment arm of one trial to match the characteristics of patients in the active treatment arm of a comparator trial. Although an unanchored MAIC relies on stronger assumptions (all key prognostic factors and effect modifiers must be accounted for) than an anchored one, it is more appropriate when no appropriate common comparator exists.

Conclusion

The authors attempt to address a meaningful clinical question, which is commendable. However, the methodological approach, specifically using the withdrawal arm as a common comparator during the maintenance period, is insufficient to adequately address the research question posed. Consequently, the comparative maintenance efficacy conclusions drawn from this analysis are not valid and should not inform clinical practice.
