Interactive effects of field pea genotypes and Rhizobium Leguminosarum strains on nodulation, yield, and nitrogen fixation in Sinana district, Ethiopia
Authors: Tesfahun Kassahun, Habtamu Admas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractField pea (Pisum sativum L.) is one of the most important food legumes in the highlands of Ethiopia, but its productivity in Sinana District is limited by poor soil fertility and inefficient biological nitrogen fixation (BNF). This study examined the joint effects of field pea genotypes and Rhizobium leguminosarum strains on nodulation, yield, nutrient uptake, and nitrogen fixation in greenhouse and field experiments. Two strains (EAL 300 and EAL 302), their mixture, and a control were compared for three varieties (local, ‘Wayitu’, and ‘Dadimos’) in a factorial randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications. Greenhouse experiments showed that EAL 302 had better survival ability on seeds, competitiveness, and symbiotic efficiency than EAL 300. In the field experiment, significant interactions between genotypes and strains were found for nodulation and straw yield. Inoculation with EAL 302 increased nodule dry weight, plant dry matter, grain yield (2900 kg ha⁻¹), straw yield (5633 kg ha⁻¹), total Nitrogen (TN) uptake (169.7 kg N ha⁻¹), and total nitrogen fixed (242 kg N ha⁻¹) significantly compared with the uninoculated control. Among varieties, ‘Wayitu’ had the highest grain yield (2876 kg ha⁻¹) and better nodulation and nutrient uptake. The combination of ‘Wayitu’ and EAL 302 was found to be the most effective symbiotic combination. The results clearly show that genotype-strain interaction is an important factor in improving BNF and yield. Inoculation with EAL 302, especially ‘Wayitu’, is recommended as a promising inoculation strategy to improve field pea yield, nitrogen fixation, and soil nitrogen status in Sinana District.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09271-6.Keywords: Field pea, Pisum sativum, Rhizobium leguminosarum, Biological nitrogen fixation, Nodulation

Introduction

Background

One of the most popular and affordable protein sources, field peas provides a sizable household income for farmers. In addition, it is an important break crop in cereal-based agricultural systems, especially in the Bale zone [2]. Due to its widespread use and significance, this crop ranks first in the Bale zone and second nationally among food legume crops [10]. However, inadequate production plans and poor soil physical and chemical characteristics are the main causes of low yield, which is typically unsatisfactory [21].

It is obviously understood that commercial fertilizers have helped to improve yields of some pulse crops. However, farmers are reluctant to use sources of inorganic N fertilizers because of the associated management problems and the higher costs compared with organic N fertilizers. Although the use of mineral N fertilizer in production has increased substantially, its (agronomic) use efficiency is less than 50%. More than one-half of the applied N is lost through various processes, resulting in higher production costs and considerable environmental disturbances [43]. Thus, farmers are now looking for alternatives to these fertilizers.

One way to lessen the need for industrially fixed nitrogen is to take advantage of symbiotic nitrogen fixation. Economic and environmental challenges force the reduction of fertilizer use and the maximization of in-situ biological nitrogen fixation, even though intensive agricultural systems are often maintained by the extensive use of industrially fixed nitrogen. The process by which some rhizobium bacteria and legume crops cooperate to extract nitrogen from the soil air surrounding the plant’s roots is known as symbiotic nitrogen fixation. About 78% of the nitrogen in the air contained in the soil’s tiny pores is gaseous (N2). The N cannot be utilized by plants in this form. Nonetheless, gaseous nitrogen can be fixed by rhizobia in the root nodules so that plants can use it. A significant amount of energy is needed for this procedure. The rhizobium in the nodules receives energy, nutrients, and water from the plant and the rhizobium returns the favor by giving the plant nitrogen. The pulse crop, its dry matter production, the amount of accessible soil N, and other environmental factors all affect how much N2 is fixed [11].

As long as Rhizobium leguminosarum, an N-fixing bacteria, has colonized the roots, field pea is a legume that can meet some of its N needs through biological processes. According to production guidelines, biological fixation can provide between 30% and 80% of the N required for field peas [19].

Creating low-input systems that are both sustainable and profitable is now the main obstacle facing research on boosting food production in tropical Africa. Legumes’ primary benefit in this regard is their ability to increase soil organic nitrogen levels to a point where a sufficient quantity is available for crops in the future [6]. Therefore, there are many benefits to inoculating with a persistent and effective strain of rhizobium, including the avoidance of the need to apply nitrogen fertilizers repeatedly [37].

Therefore, when it comes to biological nitrogen fixation, matching host genotypes and rhizobial strains is the most relevant criterion. According to an ICARDA study, strain-cultivar interactions have been noted in faba beans and chickpeas on plant response measures such herbage yield, nodule number, nodule mass, and plant vigour, but they seem to be negligible in lentil, lathyrus, and vetch [4].

Effective inoculation is necessary to sustain a high number of viable bacteria until they are able to nodulate the roots, which is necessary for good nodulation to occur. However, rhizobium strains that can nodulate the host legume must be present in the soil for a symbiosis to establish. Effective rhizobial strains, on the other hand, must be made available to farmers as seed inoculants because they are frequently either absent from soils or present in very little amounts [12]. There is currently relatively little data about BNF research in Ethiopia [25].

Nevertheless the significance of field pea production in Sinana District, there has been limited scientific investigation on the interactive effects of field pea genotypes and Rhizobium leguminosarum strains in the area’s agro-ecological conditions. In most previous investigations, the focus was on the evaluation of varieties or inoculation effects, without considering the compatibility of strains and genotypes and its effects on the efficiency of nitrogen fixation. As such, there are no location-specific recommendations on inoculants for the area. This investigation is the first of its kind in Sinana District, aimed at investigating the interactive effects of various field pea genotypes and Rhizobium leguminosarum strains on nodulation, nitrogen fixation, and yield performance.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

Location

The experiment was conducted on a silty-loam soil at a farmer’s field located approximately 3 km from Sinana Agricultural Research Center during the 2024/2025 ‘Bona’ cropping season. The field had previously been cultivated with wheat under a continuous cereal-based cropping system. It is located at latitudes of 07007′N and 40010′E longitudes with an elevation of 2400 m.a.s.l. referenced from the research station (Fig. 1). The major research station is situated 463 km southeast of the capital, Addis Ababa. The major crops grown in the area are cereals such as wheat, emmer wheat, and barley; pulses such as field pea, faba bean, and lentil, and some oil crops are also grown. However, the natural vegetation cover and the overall ecosystem disturbances have greatly expanded due to mismanagement and illegal exploitation of the resource base [15].

Climate

The area is typically characterized by bimodal rainfall having two distinct growing season viz., ‘Ganna’ (March-July) and ‘Bona’ (August–December). To minimize the effect of moisture stress, supplemental manual irrigation was uniformly applied to all plots at equal intervals and in equal amounts until the pod filling stage, ensuring that no treatment received differential water application.

Methods

Source of inoculants

In order to investigate the interaction impact with field pea varieties, carrier peat-based inoculants of Rhizobium leguminosarum strains EAL 300 and EAL 302 were acquired from the National Soil Research Center (NSRC), Addis Ababa’s Soil Microbiology Laboratory. In order to assess the strains’ resistance to antibiotics, pure cultures of the same strains were also gathered.

Source of plant seeds

The field pea (Pisum sativum L.) genotypes used in this study were obtained from the Sinana Agricultural Research Center (SARC), which operates under the Oromia Agricultural Research Institute, Ethiopia. The seeds were officially released and maintained varieties used for research purposes. The Rhizobium leguminosarum strains were also sourced from the Soil Microbiology Laboratory of SARC. All plant materials were cultivated genotypes; no wild plant samples were collected for this study. Therefore, no special permits were required for plant collection. The experiment was conducted in compliance with institutional research guidelines and national agricultural research regulations of Ethiopia. The identity and purity of the genotypes were confirmed by pulse crop breeders at Sinana Agricultural Research Center prior to planting. The experiment was conducted in accordance with institutional research guidelines and national agricultural research regulations of Ethiopia.

Experimental details

Experiments on the intrinsic antibiotic resistance of rhizobial strains, rhizobia survival on inoculated seeds of various pea varieties, efficacy in greenhouse soil conditions, and a field trial to examine the interaction effect between rhizobial strains and pea genotypes were carried out.

Survival of rhizobia on seeds

The ability of two Rhizobium leguminosarum strains EAL 300, EAL 302, and EAL 300 + EAL 302 as well as three field pea varieties local, “Wayitu,” and “Dadimos” to survive on seeds was examined. Equal amounts (7 g/kg seed) of inoculant containing various strains of Rhizobium leguminosarum and their combination were added to the seeds of the three types of field pea. For ten days, the inoculated seeds were incubated at 30 °C. By serially diluting the inoculated seeds and plating 1 ml of the diluted sample on Yeast Extract Mannitol Agar (YEMA), a standard medium used for culturing rhizobia, at intervals of 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, and 10 days, the population of Rhizobium leguminosarum strains was ascertained. The rhizobia count on seeds was expressed as cfu/seed.

The combined inoculation treatment (EAL 300 + EAL 302) was prepared by mixing the two peat-based inoculants in equal proportions (1:1 ratio by weight) before seed application at the recommended total rate of 7 g kg⁻¹ seed.

Intrinsic antibiotic resistance (IAR) pattern of rhizobial species

In order to conduct an intrinsic antibiotic resistance (IAR) investigation, stock solutions of the antibiotics (10 mg/ml) were prepared in the proper solvent (Table 1), filter-sterilized, and kept at 4 °C. 50 ml of YEMA media was dispensed into 100 ml conical flasks, and it was autoclaved for 30 min at 15 lbs pressure. After cooling the medium to between 50 and 60 degrees Celsius, the right amount of antibiotic was applied. While tetracycline was evaluated at 5, 10, 15, and 25 µg/ml, ampicillin and vancomycin were added at doses of 100, 125, 150, and 200 µg/ml. For each concentration, two plates were filled with the melted medium with the corresponding antibiotic concentrations, which were then left to harden. Each strain’s pure cultures were streaked on both the antibiotic-treated and antibiotic-free control plates. At 30 degrees Celsius, the inoculation plates were incubated for 48 to 72 h. Following incubation, control and antibiotic plate growth were compared. For every concentration, growth was recorded as either positive (+++, ++, +) or negative (-). The resistance limit was taken into consideration up to the concentration at which no growth was observed.

Antibiotics and their solvents

Ten nodules were randomly chosen from each treatment, and isolations were made from each to determine the intrinsic antibiotic resistance of the rhizobia isolated from plant root nodules from treatments in which both strains had been utilized. As previously mentioned, each isolate’s pure cultures were examined for antibiotic resistance. The antibiogram (the concentration of each antibiotic at which growth of a rhizobial strain/isolate did not occur) of the pure strain and that of isolates from nodules from each treatment were compared in order to calculate the percentage nodule occupancy. The number of isolates from each treatment’s nodules that matched the antiobiogram of either of the two strains was calculated as the number of nodules out of the ten total nodules that that strain occupied. As a result, each strain’s % nodule occupancy was determined.

Evaluation of effectiveness of rhizobial strains under greenhouse conditions

For the purpose of conducting pot trials in the greenhouse under both sterilized and unsterilized soil conditions, the necessary quantity of a bulk soil sample was gathered from the experimental site’s field. For three days in a row, the soil was autoclaved for two hours at 121 degrees Celsius. After cleaning their surfaces with alcohol, the sterilised soil was put into individual plastic pots at a rate of 4 kg per pot.

Field pea seeds of the native cultivars “Wayitu” and “Dadimos” were surface sterilized by applying 0.1% HgCl2 solution and 100% alcohol in succession, followed by four to five rinses with sterile water. Different strains and their combination (7 g/kg seed) were inoculated onto surface-sterilized seeds. Under both sterilized and unsterilized soil conditions, the pots were set up in a fully randomized block design with three replications for every treatment. Each pot received a starter dosage of fertilizer consisting of 20 kg N/ha of urea and 46 kg P2O5/ha of TSP. For each replication, three plants were kept in each pot. Six weeks after planting, the plants were carefully removed and their dry matter and nodulation characteristics assessed.

Inoculant strains and pea varieties

At planting in the seed row, the treatments received uniform applications of triple super phosphate and urea at rates of 46 kg P2O5/ha and 20 kg N/ha, respectively. Plots were 3 by 4 m in size, with 1 and 1.5 m separating them from blocks, respectively.

Seed inoculation

Rhizobium leguminosarum strains were injected into seeds at a rate of 7 g/kg using a 1:1 slurry of peat-based inoculants and cool, clean water. To ensure cell viability, all inoculants were sprayed right before planting in a shaded area. Before being planted, the seeds were given a few minutes to air dry.

Sowing

On August 30, 2024, ten rows of field pea varieties were planted in each plot. 30 cm between rows and 10 cm between plants within rows were used for seeding. The noninoculated plots were planted initially in order to reduce contamination. In order to reduce the possibility of unintentionally contaminating the treatments, all treatments containing the same rhizobia strain or strains were planted one after the other. In order to lessen the spread of bacteria across plots, ridges were also constructed between each plot.

Agronomic practices

To get rid of any outside competition, the experimental field was weeded twice while it was growing. After 40 days of planting, the first weeding was done, and a month later, the second weeding was completed. To control aphids and ball worms, respectively, the pesticide Karate was sprayed at a rate of 0.4 lit/ha before flowering and at the time when the crop was beginning to form pods.

Observation at mid flowering

Nodulation

Excavating the roots of five randomly selected plants from each plot’s two border rows, leaving the central six rows in the mid-flowering stage of field peas, was how the nodulation sampling was carried out. An undisturbed soil core including the entire root system was collected using a spade. It measured about 20 cm deep and had a radius of about 12 cm extending out from the center stem. After being wrapped in plastic, the undisturbed soil samples were brought to the lab, where tap water was used to gently rinse the soil away from the roots. To guarantee that the roots and nodules were collected undamaged, care was taken. Later, nodules from the crown region and lateral roots were extracted independently of the roots. Nodules were counted overall, and the average number of nodules per plant among five plants was noted.

The gathered nodules were submerged in a measuring cylinder filled with water that had already been measured. Nodule volume (ml) was defined as the amount of water that nodules displaced. The collected nodules were dried in an oven to a consistent weight after the nodule volume was determined. This allowed for the measurement of the nodule dry weight per plant. The nodule dry weight per plant was calculated as the average of five plants. The greenhouse investigation used a similar method to determine the quantity and dry weight of nodules.

Plant dry matter

Plant dry matter was measured from plants sampled for nodulation at the crop’s mid-flowering stage. To ascertain the plant dry matter, the nodules from the roots were removed, and the plant samples were then put in labeled perforated paper bags and oven-dried to a constant weight. To calculate the dry weight per plant, the average dry weight of five plants was measured. Similar approach was utilized for the greenhouse investigation for the assessment of plant dry matter.

Agronomic data

Plant height

For the purpose of measuring plant height at physiological maturity, five plants were chosen at random from the central rows. Plant height was measured by measuring the distance between the apical bud and the soil’s surface. Five plants were selected from each plot, and their average height was measured and reported in centimetres.

Number of pods per plant

Five plants were chosen at random from each plot’s center rows. Each plant’s pods were gathered individually, and the average number of pods per plant was calculated.

Number of grains per pod

The amount of grains per plant was determined by separating the grains from the pods after each of the five randomly chosen non-border plants had had its pods counted. By dividing the total number of grains per plant by the number of pods per plant, the number of grains per pod for each plant was determined. Lastly, the quantity of grains per pod was calculated as the average of five plants.

Biomass and grain yield and thousand-grain weight

Plants from the central six rows of a net plot measuring 1.8 × 4 m (7.2 m2) were manually harvested near the ground when they reached physiological maturity. The above-ground plant biomass yield was calculated by weighing the collected plants after they had been sun-dried outside. Following threshing at the proper moisture level of 10.5%, the grain yield of each plot was calculated. Ultimately, the yield per plot was translated to the yield per hectare. 1000 grain-weight was given as the weight of a random sample of 1000 grains in grams.

Straw yield

Grain yield was subtracted from the matching total above-ground biomass yield to determine straw yield.

Soil and plant sample collection and processing

Before planting, ten sampling locations throughout the entire experimental site were used to gather composite surface soil samples (0–30 cm depth) in order to determine the physico-chemical characteristics of the soil. Similarly, five samples were taken at a depth of 0–30 cm from each plot, and they were then composited replication-wise. Following collection, the samples were powdered to fit through a 2 mm sieve and allowed to air dry at ambient temperature.

Two rows of non-border plants were harvested at physiological maturity and divided into grain and straw. After being air-dried and oven-dried at 70 °C to a consistent weight, the grain and straw sample materials were crushed to fit through a 1 mm filter and stored for tissue analysis.

Physico-chemical analysis of soil

Prior to planting, the typical bulk soil sample was examined for the following parameters: base saturation, total nitrogen, accessible P, cation exchange capacity, organic carbon, bulk density, particle size distribution, pH, and exchangeable bases (Na, K, Ca, and Mg). After harvest, total N was measured for every treatment in every replication using the usual protocol.

At a soil: water ratio of 1:2.5, the bulk density of the soil was calculated using the core method, and the pH was determined using the potentiometric approach [36]. The 1 M ammonium acetate method at pH 7 was used to determine cation exchange capacity [8], while the dichromate oxidation method [38] and the micro kjeldhal method [17] were used to determine organic carbon and total nitrogen. The Olsen method [28] was used to analyse available P, and the ascorbic acid-molybdate blue method [39]. Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer readings were used to determine the Ca2+ and Mg2+ values, while flame photometers were used to determine the Na+ and K+ values. The hydrometer method was used to determine the size distribution of soil particles [5].

Determination of N uptake in grain and straw

A Kjeldahl technique was used to quantitatively measure the total N in grain and straw sub-samples [26]. Grain uptake of nitrogen is calculated by multiplying the grain’s nitrogen content by its yield. In a similar vein, straw nitrogen uptake is calculated by multiplying straw yield by nitrogen content. Grain and straw nitrogen uptake were added together to determine total nitrogen.

Determination of P uptake in grain and straw

The meta vanadate method [27] was used to determine the phosphorus content of the grain and straw sub-samples. The samples were calcined for 24 h at 450 °C, and the ash was dissolved in 20% nitric acid (HNO3) to liberate organic phosphorus. The phosphorus in solution was measured colorimetrically using molybdate and metavanadate for colour development, and the phosphorus reading was taken at 460 nm in a spectrophotometer. The phosphorus uptake by grain and straw was calculated by multiplying the phosphorus content of the corresponding parts by the grain yield and the straw yield, respectively. The total P uptake was then calculated as the sum of the phosphorus uptake in the grain and straw.

Measurement of biological nitrogen fixation

N-difference method

N2 fixation was estimated in the field by measuring the total amount of N in the legume crop and the nitrogen content in a non-fixing reference crop (barley). The TN yield was estimated using Kjeldahl analysis for the N content of plant dry matter, and the amount of nitrogen that remained in the soil after harvest from the legume field and reference crop field was also examined to determine the amount of nitrogen fixed [4].

Results and discussion

The current study included a number of experiments, such as laboratory tests to ascertain whether rhizobia could survive on seeds of various field pea varieties, tests to ascertain whether pure cultures were resistant to antibiotics, and a greenhouse study in both sterilised and unsterilized soil conditions to assess the fixing ability of rhizobial strains.

During the 2024/2025 growing season, rainfall was substantially below the long-term average, creating severe moisture stress that threatened crop establishment and survival. To prevent complete crop failure and ensure the evaluation of genotype-rhizobium interactions, supplemental irrigation was applied uniformly to all experimental plots. Because all treatments received the same amount and timing of irrigation, treatment comparisons remained unaffected. However, the results should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to strictly rainfed farming conditions, and further evaluation under normal rainfall conditions is recommended.

Furthermore, a field experiment was carried out during the main cropping season of ‘Bona’ (Aug-Dec, 2024) to assess the impact of the interaction between field pea genotypes and Rhizobium leguminosarum strains on nitrogen fixation, nodulation, and grain production. Pure culture and peat-based rhizobium strains were acquired from the National Soil Research Center’s (NSRC) Soil Microbiology Laboratory in Addis Ababa. Survival of rhizobial strains on seeds

One crucial factor in choosing an efficient commercial strain or strains may be the injected rhizobia’s ability to survive on seed. In his most recent study [40], briefly reported that rhizobial inoculation by seed coating approach more than doubled the establishment rate and frequently greatly boosted seedling growth rates. Thus, rhizobia survival is a crucial factor limiting legume nodulation.

In the current investigation, viable live cells on inoculation seeds were measured at different intervals of days. Appendix Tables 2 and Figs. 4 and 5, and 6 present the experiment’s findings. Rhizobia survival on field pea variety seeds revealed differences in the number of rhizobia among the inoculated seeds at various time intervals. On the first day after inoculation, the seeds of the “Wayitu” variety and the local variety showed the greatest number of cells. Variety “Dadimos” was found to be less successful in preserving a higher number of viable cells on seed. Strain EAL 302 inoculated on pea varieties, ‘Wayitu’ and local, recorded the maximum number of rhizobial cells (7 × 104 cfu/seed) immediately after inoculation. On the other hand, combination of strain (EAL 300 + 302) showed maximum number of cell colony (6 × 104 cfu/seed) on seeds of variety ‘Wayitu’. The number of rhizobia on seeds started declining after first day of inoculation. However, it was interesting to note that enough population of strain EAL 302 was maintained on seeds of varieties ‘Wayitu’ and local at the end of 8 days, whereas rhizobia on seeds of ‘Dadimos’ could not be detected after 6th day of incubation. The aforementioned results were consistent with [1], who reported that the safest quality standards needed for inoculated seeds were 105 rhizobia per large seed (soybean, lupin), 104 rhizobia per medium seed (mungbean, pigeonpea), and 103 rhizobia per small seed (clover, sirato) right after inoculation. Thus, all inocula resulted in the safest quality standards required for inoculated seeds. However, the presence of enough number of viable cells for a longer period after inoculation on the seeds of variety like ‘Wayitu’ will ensure better establishment of rhizobia once the seeds are sown.

Properties of soil of the experimental site

Some of the selected physico-chemical properties of the experimental site are shown in Table 11 below. Effect on nodulation of field pea

The response of field pea to inoculation in terms of nodulation was, in general, poor probably due to the insufficient moisture prevailing in the cropping season in the area [23]. According to reports, fixation is highly susceptible to moisture stress, which might impact the growth of nodules that were started with a sufficient water supply. Both inoculated and uninoculated plants had pink nodules, which suggested that the soil had beneficial native rhizobia. However, the native population declines to the point that it can no longer compete with the introduced strains due to poor soil conditions brought on by improper land usage for crop cultivation [30].

According to the material currently in publication, rhizobia alone is not always enough. In order to fix a sufficient amount of nitrogen, it needs also be successful in colonizing the rhizosphere. As a result, a number of factors, such as the host cultivar and rhizobial strain matching, affect the legumes’ ability to succeed. Therefore, the significance of the host legume and cropping history in sustaining the rhizobia population is relevant as a fundamental paradigm [24].

Conclusion

The present study investigated the interaction effects of field pea genotypes and Rhizobium leguminosarum strains on nodulation, growth performance, yield, and biological nitrogen fixation based on the agro-ecological conditions of Sinana District, Ethiopia. The results obtained showed that the interaction effects of genotype-strain combinations significantly affect the efficiency of biological nitrogen fixation and crop productivity. Laboratory and greenhouse studies showed that there were significant variations among rhizobial strains with respect to survival on seeds, competitiveness, and nodulation efficiency. Strain EAL 302 was found to have better survival on inoculated seeds, higher inherent competitiveness, and higher occupancy than EAL 300. In both sterilized and unsterilized soils, EAL 302 had higher nodule number, nodule dry weight, and plant dry matter, showing its higher symbiotic efficiency. The field study also supported these observations. Significant interaction effects between pea genotypes and rhizobial strains were found for nodulation parameters and straw yield. Inoculation with EAL 302 showed a significant increase in nodule dry weight, nodule volume, plant dry matter, grain yield, straw yield, and nitrogen uptake over the control and EAL 300. Inoculation with EAL 302 significantly increased grain yield compared with the uninoculated control. However, moisture stress during the growing season may have limited the full expression of the yield response. Among the genotypes used for testing, the improved variety ‘Wayitu’ was found to be the best performer for nodulation, plant dry matter, grain yield, and nitrogen uptake. The interaction of ‘Wayitu’ with EAL 302 was found to be the most effective symbiotic treatment, which increased nodulation and straw yield. This result emphasizes the significance of identifying compatible host-strain interactions instead of depending upon improved varieties or inoculation alone. Despite the important findings of this study, some limitations should be acknowledged. The field experiment was carried out during a single cropping season and at one location, which may limit the wider applicability of the results across different environments and years. The occurrence of low rainfall and moisture stress during the cropping season may also have affected nodulation, nitrogen fixation, and yield performance of field pea. Therefore, further multi-location and multi-season studies are recommended to validate the present findings.
