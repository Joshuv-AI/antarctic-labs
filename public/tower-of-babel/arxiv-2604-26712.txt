Abstract
. Core-nilpotent endomorphisms over an arbitrary vector space form the largest subset of the ring of endomorphisms over that arbitrary vector space which admit a decomposition as sum of two endomorphisms satisfying the analogous properties as the well known core-nilpotent decomposition of matrices. In this paper we present a new description of core-nilpotent endomorphisms using the k ⁡ [ x ] − k[x]- module structure they define in the base vector space. Moreover, our approach provides us with a “new” generalized inverse that restricts to the well known Drazin inverse under certain conditions. Similarly, we present a generalized core-nilpotent decomposition for endomorphisms over arbitrary vector spaces.

1.
Introduction.

Let us consider a square matrix
A
with entries in an arbitrary field
k.
If we denote by
\mathrm{rk(A)}
the rank of the matrix
A,
the index
A,
which is denoted by
i(A);
is the smallest integer satisfying that
\mathrm{rk(A^{i(A)})}=\mathrm{rk(A^{i(A)+1})}.
In
[
6
, Theorem 2.2.21]
, it was shown that any square matrix
A
can be written as the sum of two other matrices which are usually denoted as
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
such that:

•

i(A_{1})\leq 1;

•

A_{2}
is nilpotent;

•

A_{1}\cdot A_{2}=0=A_{2}\cdot A_{1}.

This decomposition is unique and it is named as the “core-nilpotent” decomposition of matrix
A.
Matrices
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are called the core and nilpotent parts of
A
respectively. Applications of the core-nilpotent decomposition vary from solving differential-algebraic equations, to the study of generalized inverses and matrix partial orders, analysing matrices associated to graphs (like the adjacency matrix of a tree) and there are even generalizations of this decomposition such as the generalized core-nilpotent decomposition. See, among others,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
and
[
9
]
.

In 2021,
[
8
]
, a natural problem was studied related to this decomposition. Precisely, a notion of index that generalizes the index of a finite square matrix was introduced to define core-nilpotent endomorphisms on arbitrary vector spaces. These are the largest class of endomorphisms that admit a decomposition which is equivalent to the homonym one for the case of square matrices. In particular, all endomorphisms that have Drazin inverse were characterized.

In this paper, we propose a new algebraic formulation of this problem, based on localization of
k[x]
-modules, which naturally leads to the notion of core-nilpotent endomorphism and, in fact, to the slightly more general notion of
pointwise core-nilpotent endomorphism
. Our approach clarifies the exact role that each hypothesis plays in the theory as far as existence and uniqueness of the decomposition are concerned. In the process, we also introduce a generalization of the Drazin inverse whose existence characterizes pointwise core-nilpotent endomorphisms and that restricts to the ordinary Drazin inverse in the core-nilpotent case. Both of these “pointwise” notions relax the nilpotency conditions seen in the classical literature, whicn allows us to treat well known operators —such as the formal derivative operator for polynomials— within the boundaries of our theory.

Preliminaries

This section is included for the sake of completeness.

Let
k
be a field, let
V
be an arbitrary
k-

\varphi\colon V\to V
be an endomorphism.

Definition 2.1
.

[
8
, Definition 3.1]

An endomorphism
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}_{k}(V)
has index

when it is an automorphism,
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}_{k}(V).

Definition 2.2
.

[
8

We will call index of an endomorphism

to the smallest positive integer

\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi^{m})=\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi^{m+1})
and
\operatorname{Im}(\varphi^{m})=\operatorname{Im}(\varphi^{m+1}).

Definition 2.3
.

[
8
, Definition 3.4]

We say that an endomorphism
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}_{k}(V)
is core-nilpotent (or it is a CN-endomorphism) when there exists two endomorphisms
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\operatorname{End}_{k}(V)
such that:

•

\varphi=\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2};

•

i(\varphi_{1})\leq 1;

•

\varphi_{2}
is nilpotent;

•

\varphi_{1}\circ\varphi_{2}=0=\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1}.

Theorem 2.4
.

[
8
, Theorem 3.6]

If
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}_{k}(V)
then, the following conditions are equivalent:

•

\varphi
is a CN-endomorphism.

•

\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi^{m})=\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi^{m+1})
and
\operatorname{Im}(\varphi^{m})=\operatorname{Im}(\varphi^{m+1})
for certain
m\in\mathbb{N}.

•

V=\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi^{m})\oplus\operatorname{Im}(\varphi^{m})
for a certain
m\in\mathbb{N}.

•

There exists a unique decomposition
V=W_{\varphi}\oplus U_{\varphi},
where
W_{\varphi}
and
U_{\varphi}
are
\varphi
-invariant subspaces of
V,

\varphi_{{}_{|W_{\varphi}}}\in\operatorname{Aut}_{k}(W_{\varphi})
and
\varphi_{|U_{\varphi}}
is nilpotent.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
8
, Lemma 3.13]

The index of an endomorphism
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}_{k}(V)
exists if and only if
\varphi
is a CN-endomorphism.

Given a matrix

M.P Drazin proved, in
[

]
, the existence of an unique matrix that he denoted

which later was named in his honour as Drazin inverse, satisfying the following equations:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}A^{D}\cdot A\cdot A^{D}&=A^{D};\\
A^{D}\cdot A&=A\cdot A^{D};\\
A^{m+1}\cdot A^{D}&=A^{m},\text{ for }m=i(A).\end{split}

S.L. Campbell, in 1976, approached firstly the study of the Drazin inverse for “infinite-dimensional” complex matrices in
[

]
. Considering an arbitrary
k-

[

. Moreover, the following result was proven:

.

[

An endomorphism
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}_{k}(V)

\varphi

3.
k[x]-modules and core-nilpotent endomorphisms

Let
V
denote an arbitrary
k-
vector space. For every linear operator
\varphi\colon V\to V
one has the following two chains of subspaces (which are non-isomorphic):

(3.1)

\displaystyle 0\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi)\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi^{2})\subseteq...\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi^{m})\subseteq...,

which, in virtue of the First Isomorphism Theorem, is equivalent to

\displaystyle V\overset{\varphi}{\to}\operatorname{Im}(\varphi)\overset{\varphi}{\to}\operatorname{Im}(\varphi^{2})\overset{\varphi}{\to}...\overset{\varphi}{\to}\operatorname{Im}(\varphi^{m})\overset{\varphi}{\to}...;

and on the other hand

(3.2)

\displaystyle...\subseteq\operatorname{Im}(\varphi^{m})\subseteq...\subseteq\operatorname{Im}(\varphi^{2})\subseteq\operatorname{Im}(\varphi)\subseteq V,

which is equivalent to the following chain of quotients (by definition of cokernels):

\displaystyle...\to\operatorname{Coker}(\varphi^{m})\to...\to\operatorname{Coker}(\varphi^{2})\to\operatorname{Coker}(\varphi)\to 0.

Remark 3.1
.

We shall remark that the chains presented in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), necessarily stabilize when the vector space is of finite dimension. On the contrary, when the vector space is infinite dimensional, these chains may not stabilize, or in fact, one may stabilize and the other may not. Let us illustrate this fact with the following examples:

Example 1
.

Let us consider the following vector space with infinite countable dimension
V=\underset{i\in\mathbb{N}}{\bigoplus}\langle v_{i}\rangle
over an arbitrary vector space
k,
and let us define
\varphi\colon V\to V
by linearity from the expression:

\varphi(v_{i})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}0&\text{ if }&i=1\\
v_{i-1}&\text{ if }&i\geq 2\end{array}\right..

Note that
\operatorname{Im}(\varphi^{m})=V
and
\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi^{m})=\langle v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{m}\rangle
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}.
Therefore, the chain formed by the inclusions of the images, (
3.2
), stabilizes for
m=1
and the one formed by the kernels do not stabilize.

Furthermore, this example highlights a notable difference between linear algebra over infinite dimensional vector spaces and linear algebra over finite dimensional vector spaces (matrix theory), there exists endomorphisms that are surjective and are not injective (one can also find examples for the opposite).

Example 2
.

Let us now include an example of an endomorphism over a
k-
vector space in which the chain presented in (
3.1
) stabilizes and another one in which it does not. Let

\begin{array}[]{rccl}\varphi_{1}\colon&k[x]\oplus k[x]/x^{n}&\rightarrow&k[x]\oplus k[x]/x^{n}\\
&(p(x),\overline{q(x)})&\mapsto&(x\cdot p(x),\overline{x\cdot q(x)}).\end{array}

Note that:

•

\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi_{1})=0\oplus\langle\overline{x^{n-1}}\rangle\subseteq k[x]\oplus k[x]/x^{n};

•

\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi_{1}^{2})=0\oplus\langle\overline{x^{n-2}},\overline{x^{n-1}}\rangle\subseteq k[x]\oplus k[x]/x^{n};

⋮

•

\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi_{1}^{n})=0\oplus\langle k[x]/x^{n}\rangle\subseteq k[x]\oplus k[x]/x^{n};

and therefore the chain stabilizes and:

(3.3)

\underset{i=1}{\bigcup^{n}}\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi_{1}^{i})=0\oplus k[x]/x^{n}.

On the other hand, let us consider the following endomorphism:

h_{x}\colon\underset{n\geq 1}{\bigoplus^{\infty}}(k[x]/x^{n})\to\underset{n\geq 1}{\bigoplus^{\infty}}(k[x]/x^{n}),

again, defined as the homothecy given by multiplication by
x
at each component. A straightforward calculation shows that, setting
x^{0}=1
and
x^{j}=0
for any
j<0
:

•

\operatorname{Ker}(h_{x})=\bigoplus_{m\geq 0}\langle\overline{x^{m}}\rangle\subseteq\bigoplus_{n\geq 1}k[x]/x^{n};

•

\operatorname{Ker}(h_{x}^{2})=\bigoplus_{m\geq 0}\langle\overline{x^{m}},\overline{x^{m-1}}\rangle\subseteq\bigoplus_{n\geq 1}k[x]/x^{n};

⋮

•

\operatorname{Ker}(h_{x}^{i})=\bigoplus_{m\geq 0}\langle\overline{x^{m}},\overline{x^{m-1}},...,\overline{x^{m-i}}\rangle\subseteq\bigoplus_{n\geq 1}k[x]/x^{n};

⋮

where
\overline{x}
denotes the class of
x
in the corresponding quotient. It is clear that the chain of inclusions

\operatorname{Ker}(h_{x})\subset\operatorname{Ker}(h_{x}^{2})\subset\dots\subset\operatorname{Ker}(h_{x}^{i})\subset\dots

does not stabilize and, in fact,

\displaystyle\underset{i=1}{\bigcup^{\infty}}\operatorname{Ker}(h_{x}^{i})=\underset{n\geq 1}{\bigoplus^{\infty}}(k[x]/x^{n})

This states that every vector is anihilated by some power of
h_{x}
, but note that
h_{x}
is not nilpotent.

3.1.
On chain conditions.

Before continuing with the theory of modules, we shall point out something. The reader may have noticed that the third item presented in the characterization of Theorem
2.4
bears certain resemblance to the decomposition expressed in the well known Fitting Decomposition Theorem. To wit:

Theorem 3.2
.

(
Fitting Decomposition Theorem
.)
Let
R
be a ring and
M
a finite-length module over
R.
Then, for any
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}(M),
the endomorphism ring of
M,
there is a positive integer
m
such that

M=\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi^{m})\oplus\operatorname{Im}(\varphi^{m}).

It is well known that considering an endomorphism
\varphi\colon V\to V
is equivalent to fix a
k[x]
-module structure in
V
compatible with the vector space structure. This is, giving a morphism of
k
-algebras
\Phi\colon k[x]\to\operatorname{End}_{k}(V)
where:

x\cdot v\equiv\Phi(x)(v):=\varphi(v),

for any
v\in V.

Furthermore, it is also a classical result that a module is of finite length if and only if it is Artinian and Noetherian. In the case of a module over a field, a vector space, it is equivalent to be finite dimensional as a vector space than to be a module of finite length (see
[
2
, Proposition 6.10]
).

Moreover, in the proof of Fitting Decomposition Theorem, the hypothesis of being Artinian and Noetherian are used to ensure that the chains presented in (
3.1
) (Noetherian) and (
3.2
) (Artinian) stabilize respectively. We shall devote the rest of the section to show that these conditions can be relaxed. Roughly speaking, we do not need to take control over all of the chains of submodules of our module to ensure that we have the decomposition and neither we shall impose any condition which is relative to the dimension of
V
as a vector space. Notice that core-nilpotent endomorphisms were introduced for arbitrary vector spaces and do have the aforementioned decomposition. This is why the theory related to the decomposition presented in Theorem
3.2
for the case of the module of endomorphisms over an arbitrary vector space shall be approached with other tools that enable us to establish a sharpened characterization for its existence in the language of
k[x]-
modules.

Given an arbitrary
k-
vector space
V,
and an endomorphism
\varphi\colon V\to V,
the
k[x]-
module structure enables us to consider the localization in the multiplicative system
S_{x}=\{1,x,x^{2},x^{3},\dots,x^{n},\dots\}
that henceforth will be denoted as

V_{x}:={\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{(V\times S_{x})}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.83437pt}{$\displaystyle{\sim}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{(V\times S_{x})}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.83437pt}{$\textstyle{\sim}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{(V\times S_{x})}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.34427pt}{$\scriptstyle{\sim}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{(V\times S_{x})}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-0.99101pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\sim}$}}},

where
(v,x^{n})\sim(v^{\prime},x^{m})
if there exists an
x^{j}\in S_{x}
such that
x^{j}(x^{m}\cdot v-x^{n}\cdot v^{\prime})=0.
Therefore, considering the localization morphism
\phi\colon V\to V_{x}
, one has the following exact sequence of
k[x]-
modules, and, in particular, of
k-
vector spaces:

(3.4)

0\to\operatorname{Ker}(\phi)\to V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi}}{{\to}}V_{x}.

As short exact sequences of vector spaces split, or in other words, as any vector subspace of a given vector space admits a supplementary subspace, there exists an isomorphism of
k-
vector spaces

(3.5)

V\simeq\operatorname{Ker}(\phi)\oplus\operatorname{Im}(\phi).

The following section that contains the main body of this article, is dedicated to answer two problems related to this isomorphism of
k-
vector spaces.

•

Giving a description of both,
\operatorname{Ker}(\phi)
and
\operatorname{Im}(\phi)
in terms of subspaces associated to the fixed endomorphism
\varphi\colon V\to V.

•

Studying the conditions under which the isomorphism of vector spaces presented in (
3.5
) can be chosen to be an isomorphism of
k[x]-
modules.

Before continuing and maintaining the notations, recall that in general,
\operatorname{Im}(\phi)
is not a sub–
k[x]
-module of
V
but of
V_{x}.
Therefore, we shall point out that the second statement is directly linked to realize
\operatorname{Im}(\phi)
as a sub–
k[x]
-module of
V.

3.2.
Study of the localization morphism

Let
V
be an arbitrary

.

Let
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}_{k}(V).
Let
\phi\colon V\to V_{x}
be the localization morphism in the multiplicative system
S_{x}

I.)

\operatorname{Ker}(\phi)=\underset{n\geq 0}{\bigcup}\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi^{n})

II.)

III.)

\varphi

Let us begin by proving

By construction of the localization, a fraction (i.e. an element of the localization)

Statement

0
in
V_{x}
and the kernels of the powers of
\varphi.
The third statement is obvious.
∎

We shall highlight that

of the previous proposition occurs, for example, if
\varphi
is a nilpotent endomorphism.

.

(
Properties and uniqueness of the splitting
)
.

If the morphism
\phi

-modules, then there is a decomposition
V=U\oplus W

Additionally,

is an isomorphism (
\varphi_{|W}
is an isomorphism) if and only if
\phi\colon V\to V_{x}
is surjective and, in this case, the decomposition is unique and the converse holds: if
V=U\oplus W
with

being
\varphi
-invariant subspaces,

and

\phi
admits a (unique) section of

Let
\sigma\colon\operatorname{Im}(\phi)\to V

-modules of

and

and, since multiplication by

4.
The “pointwise Drazin inverse”

If the exact sequence of
k[x]
-modules
0\to\operatorname{Ker}(\phi)\to V\to V_{x}\to 0
(in the hypothesis of Theorem
3.8
) splits, we have a definition for a generalized inverse of
\varphi
. Later, we will see that, when
\varphi
is a core-nilpotent endomorphism, this natural inverse will coincide with the Drazin inverse of
\varphi
.

The construction goes as follows: consider the commutative diagram

\textstyle{V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\phi}
\textstyle{V_{x}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\varphi_{x}}
\textstyle{0}
\textstyle{V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi}
\textstyle{V_{x}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0.}

Since
\varphi_{x}
is an isomorphism and
\sigma\colon V_{x}\to V
is a section, we can define

(4.1)

\displaystyle\varphi^{d}:=\sigma\circ\varphi_{x}^{-1}\circ\phi.

Note that the fact that
\sigma
is a section of
k[x]
-modules implies that

\varphi\circ\sigma=\sigma\circ\varphi_{x}.

Lemma 4.1
.

The endomorphism
\varphi^{d}
satisfies the following properties:

•

\varphi^{d}\circ\varphi\circ\varphi^{d}=\varphi^{d}
;

•

\varphi^{d}\circ\varphi=\varphi\circ\varphi^{d}
is the projector onto
W
induced by the section
\sigma;

•

\phi\circ\varphi\circ\varphi^{d}=\phi
.

Proof.

For the first property, write

\displaystyle\varphi^{d}\circ

\displaystyle\varphi\circ\varphi^{d}=\sigma\circ\varphi_{x}^{-1}\circ\phi\circ\varphi\circ\sigma\circ\varphi_{x}^{-1}\circ\phi=

\displaystyle=\sigma\circ\varphi_{x}^{-1}\circ\phi\circ\sigma\circ\varphi_{x}\circ\varphi_{x}^{-1}\circ\phi=\sigma\circ\varphi_{x}^{-1}\phi=\varphi^{d};

where in the last step we have used that
\phi\circ\sigma=\operatorname{Id}_{V_{x}}
. The second property follows from the fact that all morphisms in the definition of
\varphi^{d}
are of
k[x]
-modules and that
\varphi\circ\varphi^{d}=\varphi\circ\sigma\circ\varphi_{x}^{-1}\circ\phi=\sigma\circ\varphi_{x}\circ\varphi_{x}^{-1}\circ\phi=\sigma\circ\phi
, which is exactly the projector associated to
\sigma
. As for the third property

\phi\circ\varphi\circ\varphi^{d}=\phi\circ\varphi\circ\sigma\circ\varphi_{x}^{-1}\circ\phi=\phi\circ\sigma\circ\varphi_{x}\circ\varphi_{x}^{-1}\circ\phi=\phi,

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Explicitly, the third condition of the Lemma says that, for every
v\in V
, there exists some
m\geq 0
dependent on

such that
\varphi^{d}(\varphi^{m+1}(v))=\varphi^{m}(v)
.

Existence of such an “inverse” characterizes when the localization sequence splits.

Lemma 4.3
.

[
8
, Definition 3.1]

If
f,g
are endomorphisms such that
f=f\circ g\circ f
and so that
f\circ g=g\circ f
, then

.

Theorem 4.4

(
Existence of the splitting
)
.

Let
\varphi
be an endomorphism. The sequence

0\to\operatorname{Ker}(\phi)\to V\overset{\phi}{\to}V_{x}\to 0

is split exact if and only if there exists an endomorphism
\alpha\colon V\to V
such that

•

\alpha\circ\varphi\circ\alpha=\alpha
;

•

\alpha\circ\varphi=\varphi\circ\alpha
;

•

\phi\circ\varphi\circ\alpha=\phi
.

Furthermore, this endomorphism
\alpha
is unique.

Proof.

There only remains to prove the “if” part. By Corollary
3.9
, it suffices to check that
\varphi
is pointwise core-nilpotent and, to be in the hypothesis of the aforementioned Corollary, to see that
\phi\colon V\to V_{x}
is surjective. First, note that, by the first condition,
\alpha\circ\varphi=(\alpha\circ\varphi)^{2}
, so
\alpha\circ\varphi
is a projector. By the third condition

\phi(\mathrm{Id}-\varphi\circ\alpha)=0,

hence
\operatorname{Im}(\mathrm{Id}-\varphi\circ\alpha)=\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha\circ\varphi)\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(\phi)
. But we actually have an equality, because
v\in\operatorname{Ker}(\phi)
if and only if
\varphi^{m}(v)=0
for some
m\geq 0
(dependent on
v
), and then

(\alpha\circ\varphi)(v)=(\alpha\circ\varphi)^{m}(v)=(\alpha^{m}\circ\varphi^{m})(v)=0.

Now, since we have shown that
\alpha\circ\varphi
is a projector and have computed its kernel, we have an isomorphism of
k[x]
-modules

\beta=(\mathrm{Id}-\alpha\circ\varphi,\alpha\circ\varphi)\colon V\to U\oplus W

with
W=\operatorname{Im}(\alpha\circ\varphi)=\operatorname{Ker}(\mathrm{Id}-\alpha\circ\varphi)
and
U=\operatorname{Ker}(\phi)=\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha\circ\varphi)
. This allows us to write
\varphi=\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}
with
\varphi_{1}
being the extension by zero over
U
of
\varphi_{|W}
and
\varphi_{2}
being the extension by zero over
W
of
\varphi_{|U}
. This is the pointwise core-nilpotent decomposition of
\varphi
because, on the one hand,
i(\varphi_{|W})\leq 1
because
\varphi_{1}=\varphi\circ\alpha\circ\varphi
by the projector condition and definition of
W
and by Lemma
4.3
together with the fact that

(\varphi\circ\alpha\circ\varphi)\circ\alpha\circ(\varphi\circ\alpha\circ\varphi)=\varphi\circ\alpha\circ\varphi\circ\alpha\circ\varphi=\varphi\circ\alpha\circ\varphi.

On the other hand,
\varphi_{2}
is pointwise nilpotent because the elements of
U
are exactly those anihilated by some power of
\varphi
that depends on the element. Finally, note that the condition

\phi(\mathrm{Id}-\varphi\circ\alpha)=(\mathrm{Id}-\varphi_{x}\circ\alpha_{x})\phi=0

(where
\varphi_{x},\alpha_{x}\colon V_{x}\to V_{x}
are the induced morphisms) implies that

\operatorname{Im}(\phi)\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(\mathrm{Id}-\varphi_{x}\circ\alpha_{x})=\operatorname{Im}(\varphi_{x}\circ\alpha_{x})\simeq(\operatorname{Im}(\varphi\circ\alpha))_{x}=W_{x}

(localization commutes with taking image because both spaces consist of vectors of the form
\phi(\alpha(v))/1
for
v\in V
), so we have a natural morphism

\operatorname{Im}(\phi)\hookrightarrow W_{x}\overset{\sim}{\leftarrow}W,

where the second isomorphism comes from the fact that
\varphi_{|W}
is an isomorphism because
i(\varphi_{1})\leq 1
(otherwise
W=0
). We have a commutative diagram

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi\circ\alpha)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\varphi\circ\alpha}
\textstyle{W\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0}
\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Ker}(\phi)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Im}(\phi)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0;}

which, together with the fact that vertical inclusion on the left hand side is actually
\operatorname{Ker}(\phi)=\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi\circ\alpha)
and by the Fifth Lemma, is enough to obtain that
\operatorname{Im}(\phi)\simeq W
. Since the top exact sequence is split exact of
k[x]
-modules (with the section of
\varphi\circ\alpha
provided by the natural inclusion), the bottom one is also split exact and
\operatorname{Im}(\phi)
is indeed identified with
W
via the section
\operatorname{Im}(\phi)\simeq W\subseteq V
. Again, since
\varphi_{1}
is of index
\leq 1
, we have that
\varphi_{|W}
is an isomorphism (otherwise

, this shows that

must be surjective. Uniqueness of

∎

[
