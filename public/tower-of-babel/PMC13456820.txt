Mccune–Albright syndrome managed with multiple osteotomies, massive curettage, intramedullary nailing, and bone grafting: a case report
Authors: Yuni Artha Prabowo Putro, Ahmad Syaukat, Rahadyan Magetsari, I Made Dolly, Muhammad Phetrus Johan, Mohammad Asri Abidin

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:McCune–Albright syndrome (MAS) is a rare, benign mosaic syndrome. Limb length discrepancy and deformity are common, and the new bone formed following corrective osteotomy is dysplastic, indicating that recurrence of deformity should be anticipated.Presentation of case:A 15-year-old boy presented with left leg bowing following a previously healed femoral fracture with a persistent deformity. His parents reported early pubic and axillary hair development at the age of eight and a rapid growth spurt by the age of 12. Radiographs showed a 42.6° lateral bowing of the left femur, suggestive of fibrous dysplasia. Multiple osteotomies, intramedullary nail fixation, and synthetic bone grafting were performed, resulting in improved lower extremity function based on Lower Extremity Functional Scale scores.Clinical discussion:The diagnosis of MAS requires fibrous dysplasia, café-au-lait macules, and precocious puberty. Surgical management of limb deformity and discrepancy in polyostotic fibrous dysplasia is challenging, and not all procedures provide optimal results. Intramedullary nailing of the femur or tibia is considered the preferred option.Conclusion:In cases of MAS, multiple osteotomies to correct deformity, followed by massive curettage to remove the fibrous lesion, intramedullary nailing, and bone grafting, resulted in favorable clinical and functional outcomes in this patient.

Introduction and importance

McCune–Albright syndrome (MAS) is a rare disorder, signified by the triad of fibrous dysplasia, café-au-lait macules, and precocious puberty[1,2]. Although both MAS and fibrous dysplasia arise from the mutation of GNAS, causing impaired osteoblast differentiation and subsequent impaired mineral deposition[3,4], the syndromic presentation of MAS suggests a higher degree of cells involved[2]. MAS is a rare condition with no reliable prevalence estimates[5,6]. MAS, particularly those cases existing with fibrous dysplasia, results in significant morbidities, with more than half of fibrous dysplasia cases experiencing at least one long bone fracture in their lifetime[8]. Fibrous dysplasia in MAS may present as monostotic or polyostotic lesions[2,9]. We present a case of proximal femoral fibrous dysplasia in MAS with fracture and deformity, treated with multiple osteotomies, intramedullary nailing, and synthetic bone grafting. This report has been prepared in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria[10].

Presentation of case

A 15-year-old male presented with pain and lateral bowing of the left femur. Seven months earlier, he had sustained a femoral fracture after falling in a rice field and underwent fixation with a broad plate at a private hospital. Intraoperatively, cortical thinning and slight bowing were noted. Follow-up radiographs taken 3 months later showed fracture healing with progressive bowing. The patient also had a similar fracture at age seven, which was treated conservatively. His parents reported early pubic and axillary hair development at age eight and a rapid growth spurt by age twelve.

The physical examination revealed left lateral bowing, tenderness, and a restricted range of motion. The right lower limb was shortened by approximately 2 cm. Plain radiographs showed 42.6° lateral bowing of the left femur with loss of trabecular pattern and multiple intramedullary ground-glass radiolucencies, suggestive of fibrous dysplasia. A bone survey was conducted and revealed fibrous dysplasia in the bilateral intramedullary femur with bowing of the left femur (Fig. 1). Hematological investigations were performed to rule out endocrinopathies. The patient was found to have a deranged thyroid profile. His T3 and T4 levels were high, while thyroid-stimulating hormone levels were low. The presence of hyperthyroidism, polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, together with precocious puberty, reinforced the MAS diagnosis. We then removed the implant, conducted multiple osteotomies to correct the deformity and to remove the massive dysplastic lesion, followed by the re-implantation of a new intramedullary nail and bone graft to achieve bone formation. The patient was placed in the right lateral decubitus position, and the previous lateral femoral incision was used. The implant (broad plate non-locking nine-hole with eight screws) was identified and removed, followed by multiple osteotomies and excision of fibrous tissue from the intramedullary cavity (Fig. 2). After reaming, a long femoral interlocking nail (Φ11 mm, L 340 mm, Synthes Expert A2FN, Switzerland) was inserted with standard interlocking. Histopathological examination confirmed fibrous dysplasia without evidence of malignancy. Immediate postoperative radiographs were obtained (Fig. 3). Partial weight-bearing was permitted for the patient right after the surgery. There were no infections, thromboembolism, or other notable complications. Clinical discussion

MAS is a rare, benign mosaic syndrome characterized by the triad of fibrous dysplasia, café-au-lait macules, and precocious puberty[1,2]. The mutation of GNAS is the cause of MAS due to impaired cellular differentiation during the embryonic development process[3,4]. Somatic GNAS mutations, particularly those involving exon 1 or exon 1 silencing affecting GSα and/or XLαs, result in abnormal osteoblast differentiation due to increased cellular signaling[11]. A study in mice found that the homozygous deletion of GNAS reduced trabecular number (by 10%) and thickness (by 15%). GNAS knockdown in mice increases osteoclast number, resulting in lower overall bone density[12]. These studies provide insights into the pathophysiological mechanism of fibrous dysplasia in MAS.

The majority of fibrous dysplasia mutations are related to R201H (80.5%) and R201C (19.5%)[12]. The heterogeneous presentation of fibrous dysplasia suggests that mosaicism may occur during the embryonic phase or as a de novo mutation after zygotic meiosis[13,14]. Indeed, a study found that the progression of fibrous dysplasia of the proximal femur is limited, with 81% of the cases showing progression-free status after 5 years of follow-up[15]. We did not perform genetic tests due to cost constraints; thus, we were unable to identify the culprit mutation in this patient.

MAS shows myriad extra-skeletal signs. Fibrous dysplasia is the main presentation of MAS, discovered in 98% of patients[6]. Other extra-skeletal signs of MAS include café-au-lait spots (66%), gonadal abnormalities (70% in males), hyperthyroidism (28%), phosphate wasting (43%), excess growth hormones (21%), and Cushing’s syndrome[2,6,9]. Although we discovered no café-au-lait spots in our patient, the concurrent existence of precocious puberty and hyperthyroidism confirmed the presence of MAS in our patient.

Fibrous dysplasia patients (59%) experienced at least one long-bone fracture, corresponding to 0.05 fractures per patient-year, peaking at 6–10 years of age. Furthermore, 51% of the fractures occurred in the femur, followed by the humerus (22%) and tibia (16%)[8]. In our case, the first fracture occurred at the age of 7 years. A younger age at the time of the first fracture is related to a higher risk of subsequent re-fractures over the lifetime of the patient[8]. The progression rate for fibrous dysplasia occurring in patients under 17 years old was 39%, with predisposing factors for fibrous dysplasia progression including pain (36%), limping (67%), and microfractures (50%)[15].

Management options for femoral fibrous dysplasia include intramedullary femur stabilization to address fractures and prevent re-fractures. Based on clinical symptoms, long intramedullary nails may help prevent the loss of neck-shaft angle and improve subsequent mobility[16]. We chose to pursue a wedge osteotomy to remove the dysplastic lesion, followed by a bone graft, and fixated it with intramedullary nailing. Monostotic lesions, such as in our case, have a higher potential to be satisfactorily treated with resection and bone grafting in comparison to polyostotic lesions[16], and this became the procedure of choice in our case. Large grafts may result in resorption and subsequent return to fibrous dysplasia phenotypes, particularly in polyostotic lesions; thus, care should be taken to ensure proper healing proceeds after the resection[17,18]. Increased bone resorption caused by abnormal osteoclast activity and reduced osteoblast density has led to the use of antiresorptive agents, such as bisphosphonates and denosumab. However, these treatments are not yet approved for fibrous dysplasia and require further studies, especially in MAS cases[9,19].

Managing precocious puberty in male patients may involve the utilization of androgen receptor blockers (spironolactone, flutamide, or cyproterone acetate) and aromatase inhibitors. Due to the fact that the majority of precocious puberty cases in male MAS are relatively benign and require no intervention, we decided to proceed with the observation approach. Hyperthyroidism in MAS should be managed through thionamides or thyroid ablation[2,6]. We consulted the Pediatric Endocrinology Department for further management of hyperthyroidism in our case.

Conclusion

MAS is a rare, benign somatic mutation and mosaicism with a clinical spectrum of fibrous dysplasia, café-au-lait spots, and endocrinopathies, resulting in hyperthyroidism and precocious puberty. Management of fibrous dysplasia in MAS with multiple osteotomies, intramedullary nailing, and bone grafting resulted in favorable clinical outcomes in our case.
