A novel Carica papaya fruit peel peroxidase: kinetics, stability, and dye degradation
Authors: Ozoemena E Eje, Rachael C Chinweuba, Okpanachi N Oyibo, Immaculata A Eze, Arinze L Ezugwu, Ferdinand C Chilaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDyes in industrial effluents constitute a major source of environmental pollution, posing a threat to human life. The use of enzymes for bioremediation is a greener, more efficient approach than conventional methods. This study reported partial purification of a novel peroxidase (POD) from Carica papaya (pawpaw) peels using a two-step approach: 80% ammonium sulfate precipitation followed by gel filtration chromatography on Sephadex G-200. The enzyme was purified up to 24-fold with 23% yield and a specific activity of 6020.9 U/mg proteins. The molecular weight of the enzyme, determined by gel filtration, was 54.82 kDa, and the pH and temperature optima were 5.5 and 40 °C, respectively. The apparent Michaelis-Menten constants (Km) for hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and o-dianisidine were 0.026 and 0.93 mM, respectively. Metal ions: Co3+, K+, Ca2+, Na+, Fe2+, and Mn2+ inhibited activity, while Cu2+ was an activator in a concentration-dependent manner; 20 mM Zn2+ activated the enzyme. The enzyme retained 98.2% of its original activity after incubation at pH 7 and 40 °C for 30 min, while 49.8% was retained after 90 min. Peroxidase decolorized malachite green (up to 88.09%), eriochrome black T, local, methyl orange, methylene blue, and geni dyes. Hence, Carica papaya fruit peel peroxidase exhibited good stability after heat treatment. Meanwhile, the pronounced dye-decolorizing ability and the sustainable source of this peroxidase make it robust and cost-effective for biotechnological applications.Keywords: Carica papaya, Peroxidase; dye-decolorization; kinetics, pH & thermal stability, Industrial dyes

Introduction

Environmental pollution and degradation resulting from increased effluent releases from industries, especially the textile, cosmetics, and oil and gas sectors, are a global concern, as the safety of the environment and its inhabitants is not guaranteed [1]. These effluents, usually released into water bodies, contain many hazardous dyes. Some known endocrine disruptors, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), pose a threat to human health and the entire ecosystem [2, 3]. For example, crude oil exploration in the Niger Delta region of Nigeria leads to oil spills and subsequent environmental pollution, releasing PAHs, phenolics, and azo dyes into water bodies and soils. Moreover, the continuous application of pest-herbicides to revolutionize farming systems and the excessive use of industrial dyes in the textile industry pose hazardous environmental challenges [4, 5]. The textile industry is a major source of industrial dyes, which are used daily to color clothes, plastic packaging for cosmetics, and printing inks [6]. The textile industry uses a vast amount of dyes, thereby representing a major source of wastewater contaminated with dyes. It has been estimated that up to 280,000 tons of dyes are discharged each year. This could be due to inefficient dyeing, leading to dye loss resulting from ineffective binding of the dyes to the fiber. Several dyes are characterized by their high stability and resistance to degradation, hence negatively impacting aquatic and human life [7].

For the past 20 years, scientists have focused on improving wastewater treatment technology to achieve more efficient, sustainable removal of ecotoxicological threats [7]. Unfortunately, in Nigeria, there are few water treatment plants, and even the few available ones are poorly managed. The consequence is that partially treated and untreated effluents are released into the environment via dumping sites and water bodies [8, 9]. Most dyes, even at their lowest concentration, can pose a threat to both aquatic and terrestrial organisms. The significant environmental problem posed by untreated or poorly treated dyes necessitates the development of innovative treatment methods before discharging them into the environment [10]. Several conventional approaches, such as ozonation, coagulation, precipitation, adsorption, and chemical degradation, have been used to remediate the environment from these contaminants [11]. Some of these strategies are cost-intensive and relatively inefficient, which could result in products that are more harmful than the parent pollutant [11]. Enzyme-assisted approaches for the remediation of environmental pollutants are much greener and cost-effective [12, 13]. Treating pollutants with dye-degrading enzymes—such as peroxidase, tyrosinase, phenol oxidase, laccase, and azoreductase-offers a more efficient alternative to conventional methods. Due to their high specificity and ability to operate under mild environmental conditions, these enzymatic approaches eliminate the need for hazardous chemical agents typical of traditional physicochemical treatments [7]. Some microorganisms can also metabolize most industrial dyes into non-toxic products by secreting oxidoreductases such as laccase, oxygenases, and peroxidases. These enzymes possess high catalytic potential for effective transformation of a wide range of organic and inorganic pollutants [13, 14]. The extraction of these enzymes for direct use in dye biodegradation has become widely accepted, revolutionizing the environmental industry. Unlike conventional physicochemical treatments, enzymatic methods do not require harsh chemicals to reduce secondary pollutants or generate waste.

Peroxidases (EC 1.11.1.7) are a class of oxidoreductases that catalyze the reduction of H2O2 and the oxidation of various compounds, including dyes and other organic and inorganic substances, transforming them into non-toxic products [15]. The active site of peroxidase comprises a folded pocket, with essential amino acids that maintain the structure, bind the substrate, and catalyze its conversion into a product. Such catalytic residues include histidine, aspartate, and glutamate, forming catalytic triads. Peroxidases may contain metals such as iron, manganese, and copper at their active sites, depending on their source and the level of glycosylation, which varies from 0 to 25% of the glycan moieties [14]. Peroxidases have found applications in biotechnology and biomedical sectors such as food preservation [16], biosensors [17], cancer therapeutics design, and the design of an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit [18]. This group of enzymes are found in animals, microorganisms, and plants. However, plant peroxidases appear more stable and easier to isolate. It is worthy of note that peroxidases have been isolated from many plant sources such as horseradish, cauliflower, radish, spinach, broccoli, moringa leaves, oranges, papaya, cabbage, potato, turnip, oil palm, black gram, vanilla bean, royal palm, date palm, olives, strawberries, soybean, and bitter gourd [19–22]. The use of edible plant materials for peroxidase isolation could strain the food supply; therefore, it is necessary to source peroxidase from underutilized plant materials. It has been reported that global food losses from fruits and vegetables amount to 60% daily due to poor storage facilities, especially in countries like Nigeria [23]. These wastes constitute a nuisance to the environment [24]; therefore, harnessing them to produce high-value products, such as enzymes, is invaluable. This waste conversion is a very promising pathway for bioremediation and enzyme-assisted processes due to its cost-effectiveness, its lack of threat to food security, and its minimization of environmental pollution [12–14, 23, 24].

Carica papaya is a member of the Caricaceae family widely grown in tropical regions, including Nigeria (Fig. 1). While various parts of the plant are used for their medicinal properties [25], the fruit peels are typically discarded as agricultural waste (Fig. 1). This study explores the potential of using these peels, an abundant agricultural waste product, as a sustainable, cost-effective source for peroxidase isolation. The novelty of this work is underscored by the comprehensive biochemical characterization of a peroxidase from this underutilized source, especially the peels, which are a considerable waste, revealing a unique combination of high thermal and pH stability, coupled with robust dye-decolorizing potential. This distinct profile positions it as a promising candidate for industrial applications, particularly in bioremediation, offering a greener alternative to conventional methods and addressing the limitations of peroxidases derived from edible plants [24, 25]. Although enzyme applications are often limited by instability under extreme conditions [26, 27], the peroxidase isolated in this study was kinetically characterized to demonstrate its robustness across various environments, offering an eco-friendly alternative for treating industrial sites contaminated with dyes.

Materials and methods

Materials

Mature, fresh green, and unripe Carica papaya fruits were purchased from the University of Nigeria, Nsukka, Agricultural Farm.

Chemicals and reagents

Six dyes: methylene blue (MB), malachite green (MG), methyl orange (MO), and erichrome black T (EBT), were purchased from Molychem, India. local dye (LOD) and geni (GED) were obtained from Prof. F. C. Chilaka and were of analytical grade (97.5% purity). The chromogenic substrate, o-dianisidine (99.9% purity), hydrogen peroxide (30% purity), Sephadex G-200, and Sephadex G-100 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. Other chemicals: Folin-Ciocalteau, bovine serum albumin (BSA), Tris-HCl, copper sulfate, sodium trioxocarbonate (IV), disodium hydrogen phosphate, sodium dihydrogen phosphate, ammonium sulfate, potassium-sodium tartarate, sodium acetate salt, acetic acid, hydrochloric acid, and phosphoric acid were obtained from Lobachemie and were of analytical grade.

Extraction of crude Cpp peroxidase

Carica papaya fruit peels (300 g) were used for the isolation of crude peroxidase following the method of Eze [28] with minor modifications. To homogenize the sample, 500 ml of cold 0.1 M acetate buffer (pH 5.5) was used as the extraction buffer, mixed with the sample in an electric blender for 15 to 20 min. The mixture was filtered through a 4-layered cheesecloth, and the filtrate was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min using a Model 800 centrifuge (China) to obtain the clear supernatant as the crude peroxidase.

Peroxidase assay

The peroxidase assay was carried out according to the method of Eze [28]. The mixture comprised 0.2 ml of 0.1% of o-dianisidine (3,3-dimethoxybenzidine), 0.3% of H2O2, and 2.5 ml of 0.1 M acetate buffer (pH 5.5). The reaction was initiated by adding 0.1 ml of the crude extract. The increase in absorbance at 460 nm was monitored using a Spectrumlab 23 A spectrophotometer (England). One unit (U) of peroxidase activity is defined as the amount of enzyme that catalyzes the oxidation of µmol of o-dianisidine per minute under the specified assay conditions. Protein content

The crude protein content was determined using the Folin-phenol method of Lowry et al. (1951). A standard curve was prepared using bovine serum albumin (BSA) at a stock concentration of 2 mg/mL. This reaction involves two steps: the Biuret assay and the redox reaction. Potassium tartrate chelates the cupric ion (Cu2+) to stabilize it, and the reaction between Cu2+ and a peptide bond always occurs in an alkaline medium to form a cupric-peptide complex. The addition of Folin reagent oxidizes Cu+ back to Cu2+, forming molybdenum blue, which is measured at 750 nm.

Purification of Cpp peroxidase

Salting-out and gel-filtration chromatography

The crude enzyme was precipitated using 80% saturation of (NH4)2SO4, following established protocols for peroxidase concentration (Adiguzel et al., 2025). The precipitated enzyme was desalted on a Sephadex G-200 column (65 × 1.8 cm). The column was pre-equilibrated with 0.1 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.5). Fractions were eluted at a flow rate of 5 ml/10 min, and activity and protein content were determined as described in Sect. “Peroxidase assay” and “Protein content”.

Biochemical characterization of Cpp peroxidase

pH influence on Cpp peroxidase activity

pH effect on peroxidase activity was carried out following the method of Kesavan et al. [29] and Eze [28]. An aliquot of 0.1 ml was pre-incubated for 5 min in 2.5 ml of various buffer systems: sodium acetate (pH 3.0-5.5), sodium phosphate (pH 6.0-7.5), and Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0–9.0). Following this pre-incubation, the activity assay was conducted as detailed in Sect. “Protein content”, with each respective buffer serving as the binding buffer for the reaction. The pH corresponding to the maximal activity, as identified from the activity versus pH profile, was designated as the enzyme’s pH optimum.

Temperature influence on Cpp peroxidase activity

The temperature effect on Cpp peroxidase activity was determined at different temperatures from 30 to 90 °, using a temperature-controlled water bath at the optimum pH of 5.5 [29].

Substrate influence on Cpp peroxidase activity

Different concentrations of H2O2 (0.0584–0.6037 mM) and o-dianisidine (0.0086 mM – 0.0889 mM) were prepared, and activity was monitored at a fixed concentration of each substrate, already saturating. The Vmax and Km were obtained for each substrate from a double-reciprocal Lineweaver-Burk plot of initial velocity data at varying substrate concentrations. While Lineweaver-Burk plots are commonly used for initial kinetic parameter estimation, it is acknowledged that non-linear regression methods can provide more accurate and robust estimations, especially when dealing with experimental noise. For this study, the Lineweaver-Burk method provided a sufficient estimation for comparative purposes.

Determination of Cpp peroxidase molecular weight

Gel filtration chromatography was used to determine molecular weight according to Whitaker [30]. A 10 ml aliquot of the partially purified Cpp peroxidase was eluted from a tightly packed Sephadex G-200 column (65 cm x 1.8 cm) using neutralized phosphate buffer. An appropriate protein marker, BSA, was used to calibrate the column and to determine the elution volume of the unknown; the standard was recorded. Determination of metal ion effect on Cpp peroxidase activity

The effect of metal ions on Cpp peroxidase was determined using the method of Kesavan et al. [29]. Briefly, various concentrations (10, 20, 30, 40, & 50 mM) of the chlorides of salts of Na+, Co3+, Cu2+, Zn2+, Fe2+, K+, Mn2+, and Ca2+ were prepared and incubated with Cpp peroxidase, and an activity assay was conducted after 10 min of incubation at 460 nm using a spectrophotometer at optimum pH. The relative activity was monitored compared to the control, which had no metal ion.

Heat stability studies on Cpp peroxidase

Stability properties of Cpp peroxidase after heat treatment were monitored according to the method of Saqib and Siddiqui [31]. Buffer: enzyme was added to test tubes at a 1:1 ratio and incubated for 2 h at temperatures ranging from 40 to 80 °C. A 0.2 ml aliquot was withdrawn every 15 min, and after cooling on ice for 3 h, an activity assay was performed, and residual activity was calculated relative to the native enzyme.

Stability properties of Cpp peroxidase on different pH environments

The pH stability of Cpp peroxidase in different pH environments (pH 4.5-7.0) was conducted according to the method of Kesavan et al. [29]. Equal volumes of the enzyme and buffer were incubated in freshly prepared buffer for 0.5, 1.5, and 2 h, and the residual activity was measured under optimal conditions.

Evaluation of Cpp peroxidase dye decolorization potential

In order to evaluate the efficiency of dye decolorization of Cpp peroxidase, 0.4 ml of 0.1% dye(s) as a co-substrate in 0.2 ml of 0.3% H2O2, 2.2 ml of acetate buffer (pH 5.5, 0.1 M), and 0.2 ml of the Cpp peroxidase were incubated for 24 h in the dark. Control experiments included enzyme-free reactions (dye + H2O2 + buffer) to account for non-enzymatic degradation. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 24 h, and the final absorbance was measured without enzyme inactivation, reflecting the cumulative degradation by the active enzyme. Absorbance of methylene blue (630 nm), malachite green (617 nm), methyl orange (457 nm), local dye (450 nm), eriochrome black T (566 nm), and gen dye (620 nm) was recorded before and after the addition of the enzyme. Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software, version 20. One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was used to determine significant differences among experimental groups, followed by Duncan’s Multiple Range Test as a post hoc test, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Graphical representations were generated using SigmaPlot 15.

Results and discussions

Peroxidase from Carica papaya fruit peel was isolated: the crude extract was precipitated with 80% (NH4)2SO4, followed by desalting on a Sephadex G-200 column. The Cpp peroxidase-specific activity at each purification step increased to 6020.9 U/mg protein after gel filtration, with a 24-fold increase and 23% recovery (Table 1; Fig. 2). Successful purification at each step of protein isolation is indicated by increased enzyme-specific activity and decreased protein content compared to crude POD. In terms of salting out, Carica papaya fruit peel peroxidase required a high concentration of (NH4)2SO4 to get precipitated, similar to the 85, 80, 70, and 70% saturation reported for the peroxidase from apple, orange seeds, cabbage leaves, Cucumis sativus, Litchi pericarp and sorghum, respectively Zia et al. [33]; Kharatmol and Pandit [34]; Battistuzz et al. [35]; and Eze et al. [36]. It has been reported that the addition of (NH4)2SO4 causes protein aggregation and precipitation because proteins in aqueous solution are highly hydrated, with their hydrophilic groups interacting with water molecules. However, these water molecules attached to the protein molecules become more attracted to the (NH4)2SO4 salt, triggering a competition for hydration, which favors the salt. The salt subsequently sequesters most of the water molecules from the protein, resulting in a conformational change in which the protein’s hydrophobic moieties intercalate, leading to aggregation due to strong protein-protein interactions and, finally, precipitation or salting-out [37]. The differences in the amount of salt suitable for the precipitation of peroxidase could be attributed to the source and degree of glycosylation. This Carica papaya fruit peel peroxidase showed greater hydrophilicity, as it required a higher concentration of ammonium sulfate for precipitation.

The Cpp peroxidase enzyme was biochemically characterized with respect to pH, temperature, substrate concentration, and metal-ion effects, as well as its stability at pH and temperature. Cpp peroxidase exhibited maximum activity at pH 5.5, indicating pH 5.5 as the optimal pH (Fig. 3a). Previous studies reported that peroxidases have optimum pHs between 5 and 7.5, depending on their sources [38, 39]. Diao et al. [40] similarly reported pH values of 4.0, 5.0, 5.5, and 6.0 for peroxidases from Sorghum bicolor, Raphanus sativus, Allium sativum, and Ipomoea batatas, respectively, using guaiacol. An optimum peroxidase activity of pH 4.0, 6.5–7.5, 5.0, 6.0, and 4.5 -7.0, from Raphanus sativus, different kinds of vegetables, oil palm trees, strawberries, and Solanum sp, respectively, were reported [41, 42]. Interestingly, this study is consistent with the findings reported by Diao et al. [40], Joel et al. [43], Ortutu and Aremu [44] on peroxidase isolated from Ipomoea batatas, Brassica oleracea capitata L, and Mangifera indica, respectively. Each enzyme has an optimal pH value depending on its source and the substrate used in the assay. The amino acids at the enzyme’s active site of proteins are affected by the pH of the medium, making the enzyme more or less active depending on the ionization state in that environment. The 3-D structure of an enzyme is of great importance to its activity and function. pH affects the enzyme’s active site conformation through interactions between the charges of neighboring amino acids on the polypeptide chains, particularly hydrogen bonding, thereby altering the ionization states of the side chains of active-site amino acid residues. Also, the haem-binding instability of peroxidase at extreme pH values can lead to denaturation, ionic shifts in the haem group, and subsequent loss of activity [45].

The pH optimum (a) and temperature optimum ( b) of the C. papaya peroxidase

In this study, partially purified Cpp peroxidase showed that an increase in temperature from 30 to 40 °C led to a subsequent rise in activity, beyond which the peroxidase activity dropped rapidly, making 40 °C the optimum temperature (Fig. 3b). Similarly, Eze et al. [36] and Omeje et al. [42] reported 40 °C from sorghum-infected Solanum sp peroxidase. A 30 °C was reported from Brassica oleracea capitata L peroxidase, Turkish black radish, and Moringa oleifera peroxidase [43, 46–47]. Peroxidase isolated from runner bean and oil palm trees exhibited an optimum temperature of 50 °C [48, 49]; however, de Almeida et al. [50] found a higher optimum temperature of 70 °C for Moringa oleifera peroxidase when guaiacol was used as the co-substrate. Temperature is a critical factor in enzyme-catalyzed reactions; as temperature increases, the reacting molecules gain kinetic energy, leading to more effective collisions between the reactants. Similarly, enzyme-catalyzed reaction rate usually increases with temperature until the maximum point, called the optimum temperature, at which the enzyme exhibits maximum activity and catalysis. However, at high temperatures above this threshold, enzyme activity decreases due to protein denaturation, which alters the active-site amino acid residues [51].

Overall, pH 5.5 and 40 °C are suitable conditions, particularly relevant for various industrial processes. For instance, many bioremediation applications, especially those involving wastewater treatments, often operate under slightly acidic to neutral conditions [1, 2]. The optimal pH of 5.5 for Cpp-POD suggests its suitability for such environments, minimizing the need for extensive pH adjustment, which can add to operational costs. Compared to other plant peroxidases, such as those from horseradish (HRP), which typically have an optimal pH range of 5.0–7.0, and some fungal peroxidases that can operate at more extreme pH values, Cpp-POD falls within a commonly encountered range for industrial biocatalysis [3, 4]. Meanwhile, the enzyme’s optimal temperature of 40 °C is also advantageous in that, while some industrial processes might require higher temperatures, many enzymatic reactions are conducted at moderate temperatures to preserve enzyme activity and reduce energy consumption. For example, dye decolorization processes can often be effectively carried out at mesophilic temperatures [5].

The kinetic constants Km and Vmax were estimated from initial velocity plots at different substrate concentrations (4a, 4b; 5a, 5b). The substrate Km parameter allows comparison of enzymes; a low Km value indicates a high affinity of the enzyme for its substrate, while Vmax provides information on the rate of catalysis, which is related to the enzyme’s turnover number [52]. The Carica papaya fruit peel peroxidase showed Km values of 0.93 and 0.026 mM, with catalytic rates of 76.92 and 287.71 U for H2O2 and o-dianisidine, respectively, indicating a higher affinity for o-dianisidine than for H2O2 (Figs. 4 and 5). The Km values obtained for Pusa majesty fruits peroxidase with o-dianisidine, guaiacol, and ascorbic acid were 0.125, 0.8, and 5.2 mM, respectively [53]. A Km of 0.25 mM was also reported from unripe Carica papaya var., and Nouren and Bhatti [54] observed a similar trend, with Km values of 0.66 and 1.428 mM and Vmax values of 380 and 625 U for guaiacol and H2O2, respectively, in Citrus reticulata var. Kinnow peroxidase. Peroxidases possess varying kinetic constants, Km and Vmax, which depend solely on the enzyme source and the co-substrate they are oxidizing. Sisecioglu et al. [46]. reported Km values of 0.036 and 0.0084 mM for guaiacol and H2O2, respectively, with catalytic rates of 38,728 and 35,122 U for Turkish Black radish peroxidase. The Km for H2O2 (0.026 mM) is particularly low, suggesting that Cpp-POD can efficiently utilize even low concentrations of H2O2, which is often a limiting factor in peroxidase-catalyzed reactions. This high affinity for H2O2 is comparable to, or even better than, that of some reported peroxidases, such as those from Brassica oleracea (Km for H2O2 was 0.05–0.1 mM) [12]. The Km for o-dianisidine (0.93 mM) indicates a reasonable affinity for this phenolic substrate. These kinetic characteristics imply a high catalytic efficiency, translating into better process productivity in applications such as dye decolorization, where rapid substrate conversion is desired. The ability to function effectively with low substrate concentrations can reduce the overall cost of reagents in industrial processes.

It has been proposed that papaya peroxidase belongs to a multigene family of peroxidases [53]. Several peroxidases showed varying molecular weights, ranging from 30 to 150 kDa [42, 47, 55–58]. The molecular weight of the partially purified Cpp peroxidase was estimated at 54.82 kDa using gel filtration chromatography on Sephadex G-200 (Fig. 6). This value is comparable to other plant peroxidases, such as those from horseradish (44 kDa) and Arabidopsis thaliana (35–50 kDa); it also appears close to the soluble and bound ripening papaya fruit peroxidase reported as 41 and 54 kDa, respectively [59]. Pandey et al. [53] reported that papaya fruit peroxidase was a heterotrimeric enzyme consisting of two subunits of 70 and 85 kDa. Just a year before that, Pandey and Dwivedi [60] found that Leucaena leucocephala peroxidase was also a heterotrimer of 200 kDa containing two subunits of 66 kDa and 58 kDa. L. esculentum peroxidase contained 63 and 57 kDa subunits with an average molecular weight of 142 kDa [61]. Due to current facility limitations, SDS-PAGE analysis, which provides information on subunit composition and purity, was not performed. Similarly, more advanced techniques such as MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry and peptide sequencing were beyond the scope of this study due to funding and infrastructure constraints. Nevertheless, the significant increase in specific activity (24-fold purification) and the clear elution profile from gel filtration suggest a high degree of enrichment for the target enzyme.

The molecular weight determination profile of Cpp peroxidase

Cu2+ activated Carica papaya fruit peel peroxidase activity in a dose-dependent manner from 10 to 50 mM, and at 20 mM of Zn2+, Cpp peroxidase activity was enhanced. However, the study showed that Co3+, K+, Ca2+, Na+, Fe2+, and Mn2+ inhibited the activity of the enzyme (Fig. 7). Cu2+- activated Ficus carica latex peroxidase, similar to the present study [62]. Omeje et al. [42] revealed that Cu2+ was also an activator of Solanum sp peroxidase as well as Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Mn2+, and Al3+. Co2+ has been shown to inhibit Solanum sp peroxidase, while Mg2+ and Mn2+ did not influence the activity of Moringa oleifera peroxidase [50]. K+, Zn2+, and Ba2+ activated peroxidase activity in jackfruit bulbs, but Cu2+ and Ca2+ were inhibitors. Zeyadi and his colleague [45] reported that Cu2+ enhanced Z. jujuba peroxidase activity, findings similar to those of the present study. In another study, L. leucocephala peroxidase showed increased activity at lower concentrations of Ca2 + and Mn2+, but decreased activity at higher concentrations [60]. Most peroxidases are metalloproteins, in which metal ions help stabilize the 3-D structure, while others destabilize the functional structure, thereby influencing the enzyme’s activity [60]. The influence of metal ions on peroxidase activity is diverse and highly dependent on the specific enzyme and its active site characteristics [10]. The activation by Cu2+ and Zn2+ is noteworthy, as these ions are sometimes present in industrial effluents or can be used as cofactors to enhance enzyme performance in certain applications. This property could be exploited to fine-tune the enzyme’s activity in specific industrial contexts. For example, some peroxidases require metal ions for optimal activity or stability, and their presence can be critical for maintaining catalytic efficiency [11].

Effect of metal ions on partially purified peroxidase activity

Thermostability indicates an enzyme’s resistance to thermal unfolding [26, 63, 64], and heat-stability studies provide insight into its potential for biotechnological applications. In this study, Carica papaya fruit peel peroxidase retained 68.8% of its activity after incubation at 60 °C for 75 min (Fig. 8), indicating high stability and resistance to heat treatment. Several plant peroxidases from Azadirachta indica, Jatropha curcas, Umbu, broccoli, carrot, and Solanaceae species (eggfruit, tomato, and potato) exhibited good thermostability [65–69]. As aforementioned, most plant peroxidases are glycosylated, with up to 25% of the protein mass composed of glycan moieties, which contribute to the protein’s thermal resistance [70]. The observed thermal stability, with 68.8% residual activity after incubation at 60 °C for 75 min (Fig. 8), indicates a robust enzyme capable of withstanding moderate heat stress. This is a significant functional advantage, as thermal stability is a key factor for enzyme longevity and reusability in industrial settings [6]. While some variability in stability data is expected given the nature of enzymatic assays, the overall trend indicates a significant functional advantage for industrial applications. This level of thermal stability, combined with the enzyme’s high affinity for H2O2 and its potent dye decolorization capabilities, distinguishes it from many previously reported papaya peroxidases, which often exhibit lower stability or have not been as comprehensively characterized for these specific biotechnological applications. Many plant peroxidases show good thermostability, often attributed to glycosylation [7]. For instance, peroxidases from Ziziphus jujuba and Lagenaria siceraria have shown similar thermal profiles, retaining significant activity after heat treatments [8, 9].

Cpp peroxidase was used to decolorize six different industrial dyes, which serve as co-substrates with H2O2 for biotechnological applications. The decolorization mechanism employed by Cpp peroxidase involves oxidation. Electrons are abstracted from the dyes by enzyme molecules, resulting in the formation of reactive intermediates, such as quinones, which undergo further polymerization, cleavage, or degradation [72–73]. Dye decolorization potential of the partially purified Carica papaya fruit peels peroxidase on different industrial dyes after 24 h of incubation is shown in Fig. 10a. The highest decolorizing power of 88.09% on malachite green (MG) was achieved, while methylene blue (MB) exhibited the least decolorizing power of 5.97% compared to eriochrome black T (EBT), local dye (LOD), and methyl orange (MO). However, geni dye (GED) degradation could have resulted in the product(s) with higher absorption at visible region (Fig. 10a). The dye decolorization potential increased with time, indicating it is time-dependent (Fig. 10b); while the absorption spectra of the six dyes (MB, MG, MO, LOD, EBT, & GED) before and after incubation for 24 h showed significant degradation (Fig. 11). The variation in decolorization efficiency could be attributed to differences in dye structure and the enzyme’s specificity in utilizing them as efficient substrates for effective degradation [13].

Compared to other reported peroxidases, Cpp-POD shows competitive performance. For instance, literature reports indicate that HRP, a widely studied and commercially available peroxidase, decolorizes various dyes with efficiencies typically ranging from 40 to 60% [14, 15]. Methyl orange and azo dyes were effectively degraded by soluble and immobilized HRP peroxidase [74]. Peroxidases from horseradish have been reported to decolorized Indigo Carmine [75], acid blue 25 (AB-25) [76], Acid Orange 7 [77] and RB-19 [48, 76, 78]. This highlights the efficacy of Cpp-POD, although HRP itself was not utilized in the present study. Some fungal peroxidases also exhibit high decolorization rates, but their production can be more complex than that of plant-derived enzymes [16]. The ability of Cpp-POD to effectively decolorize malachite green, a recalcitrant triphenylmethane dye, positions it as a promising candidate for treating specific industrial effluents. The time-dependent nature of decolorization (Fig. 10b) and the influence of H2O2 concentration (Table 3) further provide insights into optimizing its application. Bleaching of textile and non-textile dyes, including Trypan Blue, was reported from T. dioica and soybean peroxidases [79]. Peroxidase immobilized on an alginate matrix showed lower decolorization efficiency for Congo Red, trypan blue, and MO dyes than chitosan-immobilized peroxidase [69]. Decolorization of crystal violet and aniline blue, bromophenol blue and MO; indigo carmine, and trypan blue with soluble and free peroxidase have been reported, while the removal of phenol and p-chlorophenol has also been shown by immobilized peroxidase [80]. Detoxification of contaminated wastewater containing phenols, cresols, and chlorinated phenols from synthetic model effluents and real industrial effluents has also been documented as an application niche for peroxidases [81]. The H2O2 concentration has been reported to enhance the decolorization of dyes such as triphenylmethane dyes, such as methyl violet 2B [82]. Similar to this study, H2O2 concentration maximally influences the degradation of MG and MB, with 102.78% and 14.78% degradation, respectively (Tables 3 and 4). Almaz and Agircelik [83] revealed that azo dye decolorization was highest in the presence of 0.6 mM H2O2, and remained almost unchanged at 0.8 mM. Peroxidase from fungi has been reported to degrade mycotoxins (aflatoxins) with high efficiency due to their high redox potential, which enables them to act on a broad spectrum of substrates and reactions [84]. Thus, Cpp peroxidase is robust and promising for applications in the textile industry and environmental remediation due to its high stability at extreme temperatures and pH values. Dye decolorization studies using peroxidase investigated the effects of pH, enzyme amount, H2O2 concentration, and contact time, showing effective degradation of Congo Red and Reactive Black 5 Dyes [85]. This study has demonstrated that Cpp-POD exhibited significant dye-decolorization capabilities, with the highest efficiency observed for malachite green (88.09%) and varying degrees of efficiency for other industrial dyes (Fig. 10a). This highlights its potential in the bioremediation of textile wastewater. The mechanism of dye degradation by peroxidases typically involves the enzyme catalyzing the oxidation of various organic substrates, including dyes, in the presence of H2O2. This process generates highly reactive free radicals from the dyes, which then undergo further non-enzymatic reactions such as polymerization, cleavage, or degradation into smaller, less toxic molecules [86]. The observed variation in decolorization efficiency among different dyes can be attributed to their distinct chemical structures and the enzyme’s specific catalytic preferences, a phenomenon commonly observed with peroxidases [13].

In summary, the optimal pH and temperature, coupled with good thermal stability and favorable kinetic parameters, suggest that the peroxidase from C. papaya fruit peels possesses characteristics that make it a robust and efficient biocatalyst. Its ability to effectively decolorize various industrial dyes, particularly MG, further underscores its practical relevance for biotechnological applications, especially in environmental remediation. The use of an agricultural waste product as a source also contributes to the sustainability and cost-effectiveness of this enzyme, offering a distinct advantage over peroxidases derived from more resource-intensive sources or those requiring complex purification protocols.

Conclusion

Our study revealed successfully isolation and partially purification of a novel peroxidase (Cpp-POD) from Carica papaya fruit peels, an abundant agricultural waste product. The enzyme demonstrated significant stability across a broad pH and temperature range, with optimal activity at pH 5.5 and 40 °C. Cu2+ and Zn2+ improved the enzyme catalytic activity. Interestingly, Cpp-POD exhibited high affinity for H2O2 (Km = 0.026 mM) and remarkable dye decolorization capabilities, achieving up to 88.09% degradation of MG. These characteristics, coupled with its sustainable source, position Cpp-POD as a robust, cost-effective biocatalyst with considerable potential for various biotechnological applications, particularly for the bioremediation of industrial wastewater contaminated with recalcitrant dyes. Future research will focus on complete purification, structural elucidation, immobilization, and in-situ application studies to further validate its industrial utility.
